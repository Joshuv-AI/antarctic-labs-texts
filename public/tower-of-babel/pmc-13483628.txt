Characterizing SMN1 hybrid and deletion alleles using large-scale SNP array-based SMA carrier screening
Authors: Noemi Vidal-Folch, Christian Stout, Jennifer Winters, Devin Oglesbee, Linda Hasadsri, Sara L. Cook
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMost spinal muscular atrophy (SMA) carrier screening assays quantify SMN1 exon 7 copy number, providing limited information about the prevalence or structure of SMN1–SMN2 hybrid alleles generated through gene conversion. As long-read sequencing (LRS) studies begin to resolve these alleles at high resolution, typically in modest cohorts, large population-scale data remain essential for defining the broader allele-frequency context in which such structural signatures occur.MethodsWe evaluated SMN1 exon 7 and exon 8 copy number in 18,015 individuals undergoing expanded carrier screening using a SNP-based array. Carriers were classified by deletion pattern, and representative cases underwent droplet digital PCR (ddPCR) to distinguish contiguous deletions from gene-conversion–derived hybrid alleles based on coordinated SMN1/SMN2 exon and intron copy number profiles.ResultsAmong 457 carriers (2.5%), exon 7-only deletions accounted for ~ 53%, while exon 7 + 8 deletions comprised ~ 47%. ddPCR showed that exon 7-only deletions predominantly represented SMN1–SMN2 hybrid alleles, whereas exon 7 + 8 deletions reflected contiguous loss of both exons. Isolated exon 8 deletions were rare (~ 0.05%) and likewise exhibited hybrid architecture. This distribution contrasts with earlier population studies reporting a predominance of exon 7 + 8 deletions and indicates that hybrid alleles constitute a substantial proportion of SMN1 deletion alleles in the general population.ConclusionsWhile hybrid resolution is not required for routine SMA carrier detection, the unexpectedly high prevalence of hybrid alleles highlights an important category of SMN1 structural variation that merits deeper investigation. These population-scale data provide a framework to guide and contextualize future LRS-based analyses aimed at resolving SMN1 gene-conversion tracts, allele structures, and potential modifier variants relevant to SMA pathogenesis and therapeutic response. Together, these findings support a more comprehensive understanding of SMN1 allele diversity as genomic technologies advance toward high-resolution characterization of the SMN locus.Trial RegistrationClinical trial number: not applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12920-026-02406-6.

Background

Spinal muscular atrophy (SMA) is an autosomal recessive neurodegenerative disease characterized by degeneration of the alpha motor neurons of the spinal cord leading to progressive muscle weakness [1, 2]. SMA is clinically heterogeneous and traditionally classified into four types, with progression occurring in all four types if disease-modifying therapies are not initiated [1, 2]. Type I, the most severe, presents before 6 months of age; affected infants never achieve independent sitting and typically progress to respiratory failure and death before age two [1]. Type II manifests between 6 and 18 months with significant motor delays; patients never become ambulatory and often develop progressive weakness, scoliosis, and restrictive lung disease [1]. Type III often presents in early childhood; patients are initially ambulatory but frequently use wheelchairs by adolescence [1]. Type IV, the mildest form, presents in adulthood with slowly progressive proximal limb weakness [1].

SMA is caused by pathogenic variants in the survival motor neuron 1 (SMN1) gene. The SMN locus also contains the survival motor neuron 2 (SMN2) gene, which differs from SMN1 by five nucleotides, one of which is a cytosine to thymine variation in exon 7 [3, 4]. This variation affects the mRNA splicing such that 90% of transcripts are missing exon 7, leading to an alternative transcript and SMN protein that is rapidly degraded [5–10]. Accordingly, there is an inverse relationship between SMN2 copy number and disease severity. In general, Type I SMA patients have two copies of SMN2, Type II SMA patients have three copies of SMN2, Type III SMA patients have three or four copies of SMN2, and Type IV SMA patients have four or greater copies of SMN2 [11, 12].

Approximately 95% of affected SMA patients have a homozygous deletion of SMN1 exon 7 or SMN1 exon 7 and 8, and most of the remaining cases have a deletion on one allele and a more subtle pathogenic variant in the other allele [13, 14]. The genomic region encompassing SMN1 and SMN2 is highly unstable due to large segmental duplications, predisposing to two major mechanisms of structural variation: (1) contiguous deletions involving exons 7 and 8, and (2) gene conversion events that generate SMN1–SMN2 hybrid alleles [13]. These hybrid alleles complicate carrier detection [15, 16] and may influence clinical phenotype [17, 18], yet their prevalence and structural diversity remain poorly defined.

Molecular analysis for carrier screening and diagnosis consists of deletion/duplication testing which utilizes techniques to quantify SMN1 exon 7. Such techniques include multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification (MLPA), quantitative PCR (qPCR), or droplet digital PCR (ddPCR). Most carrier screening assays interrogate only exon 7, leaving exon 8 and hybrid configurations largely unexplored.

Previous population-based studies have reported exon 7 and 8 deletions as predominant among carriers (~ 80–93%), with exon 7-only deletions less frequent (~ 6–19%) and isolated exon 8 deletions rare (0.0–0.5%) [19–22]. However, these studies did not systematically characterize hybrid alleles, and data from diverse populations remain limited. Understanding the distribution and structure of these alleles is critical for improving diagnostic accuracy, refining carrier risk assessment, investigating genotype–phenotype correlations, and potentially informing therapeutic strategies.

In this study, we leveraged a clinically validated SNP‑based platform that interrogates SMN1 exons 7 and 8 across 18,015 individuals to (i) quantify the population distribution of deletion patterns; (ii) use ddPCR to differentiate contiguous deletions from gene‑conversion–derived hybrid alleles; and (iii) provide population‑scale context that will guide and interpret long‑read sequencing (LRS) studies of SMN locus architecture.

Our findings suggest that hybrid alleles are more prevalent among carriers than previously appreciated, with exon 7-only (hybrid-associated) deletions occurring at frequencies comparable to exon 7 + 8 contiguous deletions, and rare exon 8-only events consistently exhibiting hybrid architecture. These results help identify the allele categories most likely to yield informative structural insights with long-read sequencing.

Methods

Study cohort

Retrospective clinical data was obtained from the first two years of expanded carrier screening (ECS) at Mayo Clinic Laboratories (MCL), a national reference laboratory that provides genetic testing for healthcare providers across the United States and internationally (August 2022 – September 2024). This study included a total of 18,015 individuals referred for carrier screening. Patient demographics, including age and gender, were collected at time of testing (Supplementary Table 1); ancestry and clinical indications were not routinely available.

SMA carrier targeted genotyping array testing

Carrier screening was performed using the CarrierScan 1 S Assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific), a clinically validated high-density SNP microarray designed for comprehensive detection of inherited genetic conditions. This commercially available platform enables simultaneous detection of single-nucleotide variants (SNVs), small insertions and deletions (indels), and copy number variants (CNVs), with high analytical sensitivity and specificity.

Clinical testing integrated the CarrierScan 1 S platform into a CLIA-certified workflow. All samples were processed according to standard operating procedures, with DNA extracted from peripheral blood and hybridized to the array following the manufacturer’s protocol. Data analysis was performed using Thermo Fisher’s Axiom Analysis Suite. The assay has demonstrated robust performance characteristics suitable for clinical use, including reproducibility and accuracy across diverse populations.

CNV thresholds were defined as follows: Exon 7-only deletion: SMN1 exon 7 CNV between 0.5 and 1.5 and exon 8 CNV > 1.5; Exon 7 and 8 deletion: Both SMN1 exons 7 and 8 CNV between 0.5 and 1.5; Exon 8-only deletion: SMN1 exon 8 CNV between 0.5 and 1.5 and exon 7 CNV > 1.5. CNV thresholds were defined empirically based on assay performance characteristics and visual inspection of copy-number distributions, consistent with clinical carrier screening practice. The distribution of SMN1 exon 7 and exon 8 CNV values across the cohort and the placement of thresholds are shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. Because SMN CNV calling relies on run-specific normalization using internal controls and a proprietary algorithm, formal threshold optimization analyses were not performed. All cases flagged as carriers were confirmed by droplet digital PCR (ddPCR) using SMA Deletion/Duplication testing (described below).

Confirmatory ddPCR and allele characterization

Representative cases from each deletion category were analyzed by both clinically validated SMA Deletion/Duplication Testing and additional Intron 1 and Exon 8 Copy Number Testing. Additional ddPCR testing was not performed on all identified carriers due to feasibility constraints.

SMA deletion/duplication testing

Deletion duplication testing for exon 7 of SMN1 and SMN2 was performed using a clinically validated multiplex droplet digital PCR (ddPCR) assay. This assay simultaneously quantifies CNV of SMN1 exon 7 and SMN2 exon 7, along with an internal control gene (RPP30), using a partition-based amplification and fluorescence detection system (Bio-Rad AutoDG and QX200). The method enables absolute quantification of target DNA without the need for standard curves. Absolute copy numbers for SMN1 exon 7 and SMN2 exon 7 were calculated by QuantaSoft Analysis Pro software using Poisson statistics based on the fraction of positive droplets, normalized to the reference target.

The assay was analytically and clinically validated in a cohort of 1,530 newborn DBS samples and 12 SMA-positive cases, demonstrating 100% sensitivity and specificity for SMN1 exon 7 deletions. Precision studies showed intraassay and interassay coefficients of variation (CVs) ranging from 0% to 7.1% depending on copy number. All results were interpreted using QuantaSoft Analysis Pro software, with thresholds established for CNV classification based on validated performance metrics. This ddPCR-based approach is suitable for both diagnostic and carrier screening applications and has been previously published [23].

Intron 1 and exon 8 copy number testing

ddPCR assays were applied to assess CNV in SMN1 and SMN2 at intron 1 and exon 8 using target-specific primers and probes designed to distinguish SMN1 and SMN2 based on single nucleotide differences at exon 8 (c.1155G > A) and a shared sequence in intron 1. Each reaction included EcoRI-digested genomic DNA, target-specific primer/probe mixes, and a reference assay for RPP30 (RNase P), which served as an internal control for normalization.

The combined CNV of SMN1 and SMN2 intron 1 was determined using oligonucleotides previously described [24]. Fluorescent signals were analyzed using QuantaSoft Analysis Pro software, with CNV calculated by comparing target signal to reference (RPP30) signal as described above. This ddPCR approach enabled quantification of SMN1 and SMN2 CNVs and facilitated detection of gene conversion events and partial deletions by comparing CNV profiles across intron 1 and exon 8.

Data analysis

Carrier frequency and deletion type distribution were calculated as proportions of the total screened population. ddPCR results were interpreted to distinguish contiguous deletions from hybrid alleles based on CNV patterns across exons and introns.

Results

A total of 18,015 individuals underwent carrier screening, among whom 457 SMA carriers were identified, corresponding to a carrier frequency of 2.5%, consistent with prior reports. Carrier frequencies stratified by sex are summarized in Supplementary Table 2. Of these carriers, 216 (47.3%) harbored heterozygous deletions of SMN1 exons 7 and 8, while 241 (52.7%) had heterozygous deletions of exon 7 alone (Table 1). Rare, isolated exon 8 deletions were observed in approximately 0.5% of individuals (90 cases), confirming their low prevalence (Table 1).

These findings indicate that exon 7 and 8 deletions are common among carriers and likely represent a deletion event resulting in a contiguous deletion allele involving both exon 7 and 8. Furthermore, among carriers, exon 7-only deletions have comparable frequencies as exon 7 and 8 deletions and likely represent gene conversion events that result in hybrid alleles. Lastly, exon 8-only deletions are rare, and their hybrid nature further underscore the complexity of SMN locus rearrangements.

Discussion

Our study provides new insights into the structural diversity of SMN1 deletion alleles and their relative frequencies in a large U.S. clinical cohort. Among 457 carriers identified (2.5% carrier frequency), exon 7-only deletions accounted for 52%, while combined exon 7 and 8 deletions comprised 48%. Rare, isolated exon 8 deletions occurred in approximately 0.05% of individuals.

Our observed distribution of deletion types differs from prior large-scale population studies. Although few studies have interrogated both exon 7 and exon 8, those that have consistently report combined exon 7 and 8 deletions as predominant (approximately 80–93%), with exon 7-only deletions occurring less frequently (approximately 6–19%) [19–22] (Table 2). In contrast, our cohort demonstrated a more balanced distribution, perhaps suggesting potential population-specific variation or methodological differences.

Analyses of SMA patient alleles similarly indicate that combined exon 7 and 8 deletions predominate (72–95%) [18, 20, 25–33], though a few reports describe comparable frequencies of exon 7-only and combined deletions, consistent with our observations [15, 34]. These discrepancies may reflect differences in population structure, screening approaches, or underlying mechanisms of SMN1 allele formation, underscoring the need for further investigation into genetic architecture across diverse populations.

ddPCR characterization revealed that exon 7-only and exon 8-only deletions typically exist as SMN1–SMN2 hybrid alleles, consistent with gene conversion events, whereas combined exon 7 and 8 deletions represent contiguous loss of exons. This distinction underscores two major mechanisms shaping the SMN locus: absolute deletion and gene conversion. Our observations indicate that hybrid alleles may constitute a substantial proportion of carrier alleles suggesting that gene conversion may play a more significant role in SMA genetics than previously appreciated.

In this study, isolated SMN1 exon 7 deletions detected by SNP array analysis were interpreted as consistent with SMN1–SMN2 hybrid alleles, based on ddPCR CNV analysis. However, confirmatory molecular testing was not performed for every individual carrier identified, and thus the underlying molecular mechanism for each exon 7–only deletion was not directly established. As a result, the reported prevalence of partial gene conversion events relies on the assumption that comparable CNV patterns reflect similar structural rearrangements across individuals. This assumption is reasonable given existing validation data but nevertheless introduces uncertainty and should be considered when interpreting the observed frequency of hybrid alleles in this cohort.

Understanding hybrid alleles generated through gene conversion events may have important clinical implications. Some reports suggest these alleles correlate with milder phenotypes [13, 14], although other studies have found no consistent relationship, reflecting substantial structural complexity and variability [12, 15]. The hybrid alleles identified in this study exhibit structural features similar to those previously described in SMA patients [12, 13]. Notably, a recent study using long-read sequencing to characterize hybrid alleles revealed frequent SMN1-to-SMN2 gene conversion and demonstrated comparable frequencies of hybrid vs. non-hybrid alleles [35], aligning with our observations. These findings underscore the need for comprehensive structural characterization to clarify genotype–phenotype correlations.

Hybrid alleles introduce complexity in carrier detection and molecular diagnosis. Standard assays such as MLPA or qPCR have failed to distinguish hybrid structures, leading to ambiguous or false-negative results [15, 16]. Accurate identification of these alleles is critical for reliable carrier screening, risk assessment, and genetic counseling.

Notably, we observed rare occurrences of isolated exon 8 deletions in our cohort. While SMA is most often caused by homozygous deletions of SMN1 exon 7 or exons 7 and 8, and carriers typically harbor heterozygous deletions of these regions, the clinical significance of isolated exon 8 loss remains uncertain. Because exon 8 encodes the 3′ untranslated region, a homozygous deletion, particularly if present as a hybrid allele continuous with SMN2 exon 8, could theoretically produce fully functional SMN protein. However, several reports describe SMA cases caused by homozygous exon 8 deletions, including severe phenotypes with bilateral optic atrophy [30, 36–39].

Conversely, unaffected individuals with isolated homozygous exon 8 deletions have also been reported [11]. These discrepancies may reflect misclassification of exon boundaries, confusion between SMN1 and SMN2 exon 8 due to high sequence homology, or additional pathogenic variants in SMN1. Consequently, whether a homozygous exon 8 deletion alone constitutes a disease-causing allele remains unresolved. Clarifying this question will require high-resolution sequencing and comprehensive clinical correlation.

Long-read sequencing technologies offer the resolution needed to fully delineate SMN locus architecture, detect hybrid alleles, and uncover subtle rearrangements. Approaches utilizing long-read technology can successfully determine copy number variation, detect phased variants, and identify SMN1 and SMN2 single-nucleotide variants, small insertions, and deletions [16, 40–43], and initial studies have led to the discovery of SNVs specific to SMN1/SMN2 gene conversion events, which have provided direct evidence of SMN1-toSMN2 conversion as a common genetic event leading to SMA [35].

The unexpectedly balanced distribution of exon 7‑only (hybrid‑associated) and exon 7 + 8 (contiguous) deletions indicates that hybrid alleles are far more common than previously appreciated. Because current LRS studies often enroll dozens to low hundreds of samples, population‑scale CNV datasets like ours are complementary: they define the expected frequencies of hybrid and contiguous mechanisms and help prioritize which CNV patterns (e.g., exon 7‑only) are most likely to reveal informative conversion tracts or subtle rearrangements when deeply sequenced. Thus, our results may serve as a roadmap for targeted LRS of the SMN locus. Leveraging our findings for long-read sequencing studies will clarify the full spectrum of gene conversion events and their contribution to SMA pathogenesis.

Although clinical carrier screening detects reduced SMN1 copy number regardless of architecture, the structural diversity observed here has implications for SMA biology. Hybrid alleles contain mixed SMN1/SMN2 sequence elements that may influence exon 7 splicing, transcript stability, or other regulatory features relevant to disease variability or response to SMN2‑targeted therapies. Of the three disease-modifying therapies available for SMA, two target SMN2 splicing to enhance SMN expression, and not all patients respond equally to these treatments [44–46]. Improved characterization of these alleles may ultimately inform personalized therapeutic strategies.

This retrospective analysis used a SNP‑based array subject to probe cross‑hybridization in the SMN1/SMN2 region. Determining SMN1 and SMN2 copy number using SNP-based microarray platforms is inherently challenging due to the high sequence homology between these two genes. This similarity can lead to probe cross-hybridization and inaccurate CNV calls, particularly when distinguishing between SMN1 and SMN2 or assessing higher copy numbers. While the assay used in this study is clinically validated and highly sensitive for detecting a single copy of SMN1, making it reliable for carrier screening, accuracy decreases in cases involving elevated copy numbers of SMN1 or SMN2. As with all array-based CNV analyses, copy-number calls near classification thresholds should be interpreted cautiously; however, the primary conclusions of this study rely on clear separation of major copy-number clusters rather than marginal values.

In addition, although SNP array analysis and intron 1 ddPCR enabled detection of exon 7 and exon 8 copy number changes and potential allele configurations, other exons within SMN1 and SMN2 were not directly interrogated. As a result, contiguous deletions or more complex intragenic rearrangements involving additional exons cannot be excluded; in particular, cases interpreted as combined exon 7 and 8 deletions may represent larger deletion events extending beyond these regions. More comprehensive exon-level copy number assessment, such as multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification across the full SMN gene or long-read sequencing, would be required to fully resolve these possibilities.

A key limitation of this study is the lack of detailed demographic and ancestry information, which precludes assessment of whether population-specific factors may have contributed to the higher proportion of isolated exon 7 deletions observed in this cohort. While representative cases were analyzed by ddPCR, this analysis testing was not performed for all carriers, and we did not perform LRS in this cohort; therefore, hybrid designation is inferred from exon‑level CNV patterns and orthogonal ddPCR rather than full structural resolution. Prospective studies incorporating phased LRS across diverse populations will be required to map conversion tracts and identify small variants that may modify phenotype or treatment response.

Conclusions

In a large clinical cohort, exon 7-only (hybrid-associated) and exon 7 + 8 (contiguous) deletions occurred at comparable frequencies, and rare exon 8-only events were consistently hybrid. These findings suggest hybrid alleles may be more common among carriers than earlier population studies suggested and are consistent with a dual-mechanism framework, gene conversion and contiguous deletion, describing SMN1 allele formation. Although hybrid resolution is not required for routine carrier detection, the population-scale patterns described here may help contextualize long-read sequencing efforts aimed at resolving SMN1 allele structure and exploring potential modifier variants and therapeutic response variability.
