Orthodontic treatment need according to the IOTN-DHC and AC indices: a cross-sectional study among adolescents aged 12–15 from different ethnic groups in Kosovo
Authors: Albena Reshitaj, Nora Aliu, Krenare Berisha, Liza Musliu, Venera Bimbashi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMalocclusion affects oral function, self-esteem, and social integration, particularly in underserved ethnic communities. Despite its clinical and social impact, malocclusion remains insufficiently investigated in Kosovo and surrounding regions. This study assessed malocclusion among 12–15-year-old adolescents from different ethnic groups in Kosovo using the Dental Health Component (DHC) and Aesthetic Component (AC) of the Index of Orthodontic Treatment Need (IOTN), and examined potential interethnic differences.MethodsA cross-sectional observational study was conducted among 454 adolescents aged 12–15 years, representing Albanian, Bosnian, Turkish, Serbian, and Roma/Ashkali/Egyptian (RAE) ethnic groups. Sociodemographic data (age, sex, ethnicity, residence, parental education) were collected using a structured questionnaire, while clinical assessments employed validated IOTN-DHC and IOTN-AC indices.ResultsThe mean age of participants was 12.7 ± 0.8 years; 47.1% were male and 52.9% female. Ethnic distribution was Albanian (56.5%), RAE (14%), Turkish (12%), Bosnian (10%), and Serbian (8%). Significant interethnic differences were observed for residence, income level, and parental education (p ≤ 0.001). No significant differences were noted in IOTN-DHC grades 1–4 across ethnicities; however, Grade 5 showed a statistically significant difference across ethnic groups (χ² = 13.21, p = 0.010). The Kruskal–Wallis test showed significant variation in overall IOTN-DHC scores (χ² = 11.35, df = 4, p = 0.023), with Turkish adolescents exhibiting the highest treatment-need rank (254.1), followed by Bosnian (228.5), Serbian (210.4), Albanian (200.6), and RAE (174.7) adolescents. Pairwise Mann–Whitney U testing revealed significant differences between Albanian vs. Turkish (p = 0.001), Turkish vs. RAE (p = 0.048), and Turkish vs. Serbian (p = 0.041). After Bonferroni correction (p < 0.0056), only the Albanian vs. Turkish comparison remained significant (Z = − 3.175, p = 0.009), indicating a higher treatment need among Turkish adolescents. No significant interethnic differences were found in IOTN-AC scores (F = 1.54, p = 0.177).ConclusionSignificant ethnic disparities in orthodontic treatment needs were identified among adolescents in Kosovo, with Turkish adolescents showing the highest burden. These findings highlight the need for public health strategies focused on early detection, prevention, culturally sensitive education, and equitable orthodontic care to reduce oral health inequalities.Keywords: Orthodontic treatment need, IOTN-DHC, IOTN-AC, Malocclusion, Ethnicity, Adolescent

Introduction

Malocclusion, defined as a deviation from ideal occlusion, is one of the most common oral anomalies and has significant functional, aesthetic, and psychosocial consequences for individuals [1, 2]. In addition to impairing oral function, malocclusion negatively impacts psychological well-being, happiness, and quality of life, particularly during adolescence, when self-esteem and body image are especially sensitive issues [2, 3]. Studies have shown that individuals with moderate to severe malocclusion frequently experience aesthetic dissatisfaction and psychosocial restrictions, making orthodontic treatment not only a functional necessity but also a means of enhancing overall well-being [3, 4]. The severity of malocclusion plays a crucial role in determining the need for orthodontic treatment, as more severe cases are often associated with functional impairments and increased psychosocial distress [5]. Indices designed to evaluate the need for orthodontic treatment serve as important tools to determine both the necessity and priority of orthodontic interventions. Among the most widely used indices are the Index of Orthodontic Treatment Need (IOTN), which comprises two distinct components: The Dental Health Component (DHC), which objectively evaluates the severity of malocclusion, and the Aesthetic Component (AC), which reflects the subjective perception of dental appearance. Both components are assessed independently to provide a comprehensive analysis of treatment necessity [6]. The IOTN has been extensively validated and is considered a reliable instrument for both epidemiological and clinical assessment of malocclusion, assisting in the efficient allocation of healthcare resources [7, 8].

In recent years, increasing attention has been given within the orthodontic literature to the role of ethnic and cultural factors in assessing the need for orthodontic treatment. Craniofacial characteristics, aesthetic perceptions, and socioeconomic and lifestyle-related variables significantly influence how malocclusions are identified and managed clinically across different ethnic groups residing within the same country or region.

Within the European context, significant differences in malocclusion prevalence between Roma and majority children in Romania highlight the importance of ethnicity in dental care planning and the need for ethnically informed orthodontic research [9]. A study conducted in Sweden identified significant differences in orthodontic treatment needs and malocclusion patterns between native Swedish and immigrant adolescents, highlighting the importance of considering ethnic and cultural factors in orthodontic assessments within multiethnic populations [10]. Furthermore, a London-based study revealed that while objective orthodontic treatment needs showed minimal variation across ethnic groups, the aesthetic perception of malocclusion differed markedly according to cultural background. These findings underscore the importance of culturally sensitive approaches in orthodontic evaluation and clinical decision-making [11].

Given that the Western Balkans is a region characterized by a highly diverse ethnic composition and a complex history of both internal and external migration, these factors must be explored within this geographical context as well. Such investigations would contribute to a more accurate understanding of population-specific treatment needs and support the development of more personalized and effective orthodontic care strategies.

In Kosovo, however, the data remain limited. The first study among children aged 11–13 reported a very high prevalence of orthodontic treatment according to the IOTN, with 66.5% of children categorized as having a great or very great need for treatment on the basis of the DHC and over 80% according to the dichotomous classification [4]. This rate is significantly higher than that in neighboring countries and underscores the need for public health interventions and strategic planning. Other studies from the region (e.g., Albania, Bosnia and Hercegovina, Serbia, Greece) have also reported high prevalence rates, yet comparative data across ethnic groups are lacking [12–15].

Considering the ethnic diversity of Kosovo’s population, the limited availability of comprehensive data, and the clinical and psychosocial implications of malocclusion, there is a clear need to assess and compare the prevalence and severity of malocclusion among different ethnic groups. Specifically, evaluating orthodontic treatment needs via the IOTN-DHC and AC is crucial among adolescents from various ethnic backgrounds in Kosovo. To the best of our knowledge, no previous studies have assessed orthodontic treatment needs on the basis of ethnicity using the IOTN in Kosovo or, more broadly, in the Southeast Balkan region.

This study aimed to evaluate malocclusion among 12 to 15 year-old adolescents from diverse ethnic groups in Kosovo, using both the DHC and AC components of the IOTN, and to compare interethnic differences. By addressing the lack of ethnicity-based data, the findings can guide evidence-based planning and support the development of targeted preventive and therapeutic orthodontic treatments in the country.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This epidemiological cross-sectional study was conducted in seven municipalities in Kosovo, and the target group included adolescents aged 12 to15 years who were enrolled in public secondary schools across urban and rural regions; both genders were included. Since the aim was to assess and compare orthodontic treatment needs across different ethnic groups, only schools with multiethnic student populations were included. Among 454 high school students aged 12 to 15 years, participants were stratified by ethnicity (Albanian, Bosnian, Turkish, Serbian, and RAE -Roma, Ashkali, Egyptian) to ensure representativeness). School selection was carried out in collaboration with the respective Municipal Directorates of Education, which provided the most accurate data regarding the ethnic composition of students, and 12 schools with diverse ethnic compositions were selected. Students with a history of orthodontic treatment or craniofacial anomalies, a history of trauma affecting occlusion or incomplete dental records were excluded.

Sample size

According to the Kosovo Agency of Statistics (Education Statistics in Kosovo 2021/2022), 120,398 students aged 12 to 15 years were enrolled in primary and lower secondary schools (grades 5–9) [16], providing a comprehensive context for assessing adolescent oral health needs. All the data were collected between May and June 2025. The sample size was calculated via Open Epi software on the basis of a 95% confidence level, 5% margin of error, a population size of 120,398 schoolchildren, and an assumed prevalence of malocclusion of 50%. The sample was proportionally distributed according to the number of students in each municipality. Based on a priori power analysis α = 0.05, statistical power = 0.80, and expected effect size, the minimum required sample size was estimated to be 384 participants. Our final dataset met and exceeded this threshold, thereby ensuring adequate statistical power and robustness of the analyses.

Ethical consideration

Participation was entirely voluntary, with assurance of confidentiality and anonymity. Informed consent was obtained from all parents/guardians, and assent was obtained from all the students in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All personal data were handled with strict confidentiality, in accordance with established ethical principles and data protection standards.

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of the Dental Chamber of Kosovo (Reference number 58/1).

Clinical examination procedures

Clinical examinations were carried out in school settings under natural light using disposable instruments. Clinical examinations were performed by trained professionals experienced in the application of orthodontic applied validated indices, including the IOTN-DHC, which assesses occlusal traits across five grades from no need (Grade 1) to very severe cases requiring urgent care (Grade 5) based on malocclusion severity and its impact on oral health [7]. The IOTN-AC evaluation was also applied, using the official 10-point photographic scale that categorizes patients into no/slight need, borderline/moderate need, and definite/clear need for orthodontic treatment [17]. These indices are well validated and widely applied in both epidemiological and clinical studies, enabling direct comparisons with regional and international data.

The instruments used included dental mirrors, millimeter calipers, and digital calipers. All examiners adhered to infection control protocols and hygiene standards and wore gloves and face masks throughout the examinations. Sociodemographic data (age, sex, ethnicity, place of residence, parental level of education) were obtained via a structured questionnaire form developed to record demographic information, orthodontic dental history and clinical examination findings.

Examiners were trained based on WHO recommendations and took part in calibration sessions with standardized criteria and pilot assessments to ensure consistency. About 15% of the sample, 68 out of 454 participants, were re-examined after a two-week period. Cohen’s Kappa (κ) was used for categorical variables, while Intraclass Correlation Coefficients (ICC) were used for continuous variables, with 95% confidence intervals reported. Intra- and inter-examiner reliability showed substantial to excellent agreement for categorical variables (κ > 0.82) and good reliability for continuous variables (ICC > 0.89), confirming that clinical measurements could be reproduced reliably.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics (frequencies and percentages) were calculated for all sociodemographic variables and for IOTN-DHC and IOTN-AC grades across ethnic groups. Chi-square tests were used to assess distributional differences in sociodemographic characteristics and orthodontic treatment needs between ethnic groups. To assess the normality of the data, the Shapiro–Wilk test was used, which indicated that the data were not normally distributed (p < 0.05). Owing to the nonparametric nature of the data, differences in the median and IQR of the IOTN-DHC component were analyzed via the Kruskal–Wallis test.

When statistically significant results in Kruskal-Wallis test were found, Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc comparisons were performed to identify specific intergroup differences. We applied multiple Mann–Whitney U tests, to conduct pairwise comparisons between specific ethnic groups within the IOTN‑DHC, as this test is designed to evaluate differences between two independent groups at a time.

For the IOTN-AC component, a one-way ANOVA was conducted to evaluate the mean differences in aesthetic scores across ethnic groups. Prior to conducting one‑way ANOVA, variance homogeneity across groups was assessed using Levene’s test which showed that the data meet the assumption of homogeneity (p < 0.5).

All the data were collected via questionnaires and analyzed via IBM SPSS® Statistics version 23, with the significance level set at p < 0.05.

Results

A total of 454 participants in the adolescent age group aged 12–15 years were included in the study. The ethnic composition was predominantly Albanian (56.5%), followed by Roma/Ashkali/Egyptian (RAE) (14%), Turkish (12%), Bosnian (10%), and Serbian (8%), with a mean age of 12.7 ± 0.8 years and a balanced gender distribution, with 47.1% males and 52.9% females. Most participants resided in urban areas 69%, whereas 31% lived in rural settings. With respect to household income, 81.3% participants were classified as middle income, 12.8% as low income, and 5.9% as high income. Maternal education levels were reported as elementary 29%, secondary 44%, and higher education 27%. The fathers’ education levels were 18.7% elementary, 52.2% secondary, and 29.1% higher (Table 1).

The distribution of orthodontic treatment needs varies across ethnic groups. Grade 4 irregularities (severe irregularities, treatment required) were the most prevalent category in almost all groups, particularly among Bosnians (57.8%), Serbs (47.2%), and Albanians (44.8%). The percentage of Grade 5 (extreme irregularities) adolescents was markedly greater among Turkish adolescents (23.2%) than among all other groups (< 13%). Minor and moderate irregularities (grades 2–3) were most common among the Serbian, Albanian, and RAE groups, whereas the percentage of adolescents without abnormalities (grade 1) remained low overall, ranging from 0% to 8.3%. According to the IOTN-DHC, the majority of adolescents across ethnic groups demonstrated a clear need for orthodontic treatment, with the Bosnian and Turkish groups showing the highest prevalence of severe or extreme malocclusions.

According to the chi-square (χ²) test, there was no statistically significant difference in the prevalence of Grade 1, Grade 2, Grade 3 or Grade 4 of IOTN-DHC across ethnic groups. The Chi-square test in extreme irregularities Grade 5 yielded a p -value of 0.010, indicating statistical significance at the 95% confidence level.

A statistically significant difference in the prevalence of Grade 5 malocclusions across ethnic groups revealed that Turkish adolescents exhibited a markedly greater proportion of extreme irregularities (23.2%) than did all other groups, which remained below 13% (Table 3).

The distribution of orthodontic treatment needs, as assessed by the IOTN-AC, revealed distinct patterns across ethnic groups. Bosnian adolescents exhibited the most favorable outcomes, with 91.1% classified as having no need for orthodontic intervention. In contrast, Turkish adolescents presented the highest treatment burden, with over one-third requiring either borderline (21.4%) or definite (12.5%) orthodontic care. The Albanian, RAE, and Serbian adolescents presented intermediate profiles. Approximately three quarters of the adolescents in these groups fell within the no-need category, whereas 7–9% were identified as having a definite need for treatment. However, statistical analysis via the Chi-square test indicated that these differences were not significant at the 95% confidence level. Specifically, no statistically significant variation was found across ethnic groups for Grade 1–3 (p = 0.208), Grade 4–7 (p = 0.151), or Grade 8–10 (p = 0.573).

While descriptive disparities are evident, they do not reflect statistically meaningful differences in treatment need distribution among the ethnic subgroups (Table 4). The Kruskal–Wallis test, utilized for nonparametric data, revealed a statistically significant difference in IOTN-DHC scores among different ethnic groups (χ²= 11.35; df = 4; p = 0.023). The average rank values suggested that Turkish adolescents (Mean Rank = 254.1) exhibited the greatest treatment need, followed by Bosnian (228.5), Serbian (210.4), and Albanian (200.6), while RAE adolescents showed the least (174.7). Despite the statistical significance of the Kruskal–Wallis test, the effect size was quite small (η² = 0.015; 95% CI [0.00–0.05]). The broad confidence intervals and minimal variance explained indicate that these findings have limited clinical significance (Table 5).

The Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare IOTN-DHC grades between ethnic groups. Statistically significant differences were found in the following pairwise comparisons: Albanian vs. Turkish (Z = − 3.175; p = 0.001), Turkish vs. RAE (Z = − 1.975; p = 0.048), and Turkish vs. Serbian (Z = − 2.048; p = 0.041). No significant differences were observed in the remaining group comparisons. Pairwise post-hoc comparisons using the Mann–Whitney U test were conducted to assess differences in IOTN-DHC grades between specific ethnic groups. Nine comparisons were performed, with Bonferroni correction applied to account for multiple testing (adjusted significance threshold: p < 0.0056). After correction, only the comparison between Albanian vs. Turkish adolescents remained statistically significant (Z = − 3.175; p = 0.009), suggesting greater orthodontic treatment need among Turkish adolescents. Other comparisons, such as Turkish vs. RAE (p = 0.432) and Turkish vs. Serbian (p = 0.369), did not reach statistical significance. These results indicate that the most pronounced difference in IOTN-DHC scores was observed between Albanian and Turkish groups. These effect sizes indicate small to moderate variations in rank distributions, suggesting that while statistically significant, and the magnitude of these differences remains limited. For all other group comparisons, no significant differences were observed, and the corresponding effect sizes were insignificant.

One-way ANOVA was used for parametric variables, which revealed no statistically significant differences in Aesthetic Component (AC) scores across the studied ethnic groups (F = 1.54; p = 0.177; η²=0.017; CI-0.00-0.04) (Table 6). The effect size was very small, clarifying only a minimal proportion of the variance. The wide confidence intervals further limit the precision of these estimates, reducing the clinical significance of the findings.

Discussion

This study examined orthodontic treatment needs among adolescents from different ethnic minority groups in Kosovo via the IOTN-DHC and AC components. Our findings revealed significant differences in malocclusion severity and prevalence across ethnic groups, highlighting important clinical and sociodemographic disparities. The analysis revealed statistically significant differences in key sociodemographic variables, including place of residence, household income, and parental education, across ethnic groups (p ≤ 0.001). The inclusion of sociodemographic variables such as gender, place of residence, household income, and parental education was essential for understanding the broader context influencing orthodontic treatment needs.

These findings suggest that ethnicity is thoroughly related with broader social determinants of health slightly than substitute as an independent causal factor, and may effect access to orthodontic care and clinical outcomes. Grade 4 malocclusions (severe irregularities) were prevalent among nearly all groups, particularly among Bosnian (57.8%), Serbian (47.2%), and Albanian (44.8%) adolescents, whereas Grade 5 malocclusions (very severe/extreme irregularities) were disproportionately higher among Turkish adolescents (23.2%) compared with the other groups (< 13%).

This uneven distribution of severe malocclusions signals potential barriers to early intervention and preventive orthodontic care within specific ethnic populations. Pairwise analyses established significant differences, mainly between Albanian and Turkish adolescents, highlighting variation in clinical need across ethnic groups that may be prejudiced by a combination of unmeasured genetic, environmental, and socioeconomic factors rather than ethnicity itself.

Despite pronounced differences in clinical severity, the AC of the IOTN did not show statistically significant variation in subjective treatment need perception across ethnic groups (F = 1.54; p = 0.177). Consistent with our findings, a study conducted among ethnic groups in London reported that the perceived orthodontic treatment need based on the AC component was very low, with minimal variation in overall AC scores among children from ethnic minority backgrounds [11]. Although Turkish and Albanian adolescents descriptively expressed greater aesthetic concerns, these differences lacked statistical robustness. This finding aligns with prior research indicating that aesthetic self-perception is influenced more by individual psychological, cultural, and environmental factors than by ethnicity alone. This underscores the importance of integrating both objective clinical assessments and subjective psychosocial evaluations in orthodontic treatment planning, as motivation for care often relates more to perceived appearance than to clinical severity.

Our findings correspond with and extend regional and international evidence on orthodontic treatment needs disparities.

Laganà et al. reported a lower prevalence of Grade 4 and Grade 5 malocclusions in Tirana, Albania (37.3% and 3.9%, respectively) [12]. In contrast, the present study showed a substantially higher prevalence of these malocclusion grades among adolescents in Kosovo. Specifically, Grade 4 malocclusions were more frequent among Bosnian (57.8%), Serbian (47.2%), and Albanian (44.8%) adolescents, whereas Grade 5 malocclusions were particularly pronounced among Turkish (23.2%) and Roma (12.3%) adolescents. Džemidžić et al. (2012) studied Bosnian adolescents in Bosnia and reported comparable treatment needs, with 53.6% of adolescents classified as Grade 4–5, but with low aesthetic concern, reflecting patterns similar to those observed in our study [18]. Data from previous studies (Janosevic et al., 2015) [14] for Serbian adolescents outside Kosovo showed that only 27.4% were classified as Grade 4–5, which is notably lower than the 47.2% observed among Serbian adolescents in Kosovo in the present study. Conversely, moderate cases (Grade 3) were more frequent in the external Serbian population (41.0%) compared with 19.4% in our sample [12, 13, 18].

In Greece, treatment needs among adolescents (38%) are similar to those seen in Kosovo’s Bosnian and Turkish groups [15]. Turkish studies (Yılmaz et al., 2017) also report high normative treatment needs (39%), slightly lower than the Grade 5 prevalence we found among Turkish adolescents in Kosovo, but contrast in subjective need patterns, as they note greater aesthetic concern among younger children, an aspect not evident in our uniform AC scores [19]. Research further highlights the impact of orthodontic need on oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL), indicating an important avenue for future Kosovar studies [20].

Our study also confirms international observations that ethnicity and socioeconomic factors substantially influence malocclusion prevalence and treatment demand [21–23]. The overall higher prevalence of severe malocclusions in Kosovo, especially among Bosnian and Turkish adolescents, likely reflects multifactorial influences, including genetics, access disparities and socioeconomic inequalities.

Compared with developed countries, the prevalence of orthodontic treatment need in our study appears higher. In France, DHC Grades 4–5 were reported at 21.3% and AC at 7% [24]. In Italy, Grade 5 DHC was 37% and Grade 4 DHC 22.5%, with AC at 5.4% [25]. In Spain, although reported DHC and AC percentages separately by sex, the overall trend indicates a lower treatment need compared with our findings [26].

In developing countries, treatment need is also substantial. In Ethiopia, approximately 30% of children had DHC Grades 4–5 and 15.3% had a clear aesthetic need according to AC, in Nigeria19.5% of adolescents fell into DHC Grades 4–5 and only 1% into AC Grades 8–10 [27, 28].

Our findings indicate that, compared with many developed countries where orthodontic treatment need is generally lower, adolescents in our study population exhibit comparatively greater treatment needs, resembling or exceeding levels reported in several developing populations. Recent studies have similarly demonstrated that orthodontic treatment need may vary substantially across ethnic and regional groups, with certain populations bearing disproportionately higher burdens of severe malocclusion [1]. These disparities are not solely clinical but may also have important psychosocial and public health implications [3]. Furthermore, differences in healthcare infrastructure and equitable access to orthodontic services may further exacerbate these inequalities [29]. Together, these findings underscore the importance of incorporating ethnicity, regional context, and socio-cultural determinants into orthodontic prevention and treatment planning.

Similar results have been reported in other populations. A study conducted in Tehran reported a high overall prevalence of malocclusion (83.7%), with the majority of cases classified as mild to moderate and only a small proportion presenting severe (5.2%) or extreme (7.4%) treatment need. In disparity, our findings indicate a higher proportion of severe malocclusions (Grade 4–5) in certain ethnic groups. These discrepancies may reflect differences in study design, age distribution, population characteristics, and access to orthodontic care, thereby limiting direct comparability between populations [30].

More broadly, comparisons with international studies should be interpreted with caution due to methodological heterogeneity. Differences in age groups, sample size, sampling strategies, diagnostic criteria, examiner calibration, and the use of varying indices or thresholds to define orthodontic treatment need could substantially impact reported prevalence. Moreover, discrepancies in healthcare systems and access to orthodontic services further limit direct comparability across populations.

Malocclusion showed a significant association with ethnic variances; however, this variance should not be set in causal relation with the ethnicity itself, which is a multifactorial entity having genetic, environmental, and socioeconomic factors. These factors are not analyzed separately with each other. Likewise confounding factors such as dietary habits, oral hygiene awareness, availability of dental care facilities, and socioeconomic factors have not been adjusted. Therefore, the variances should not be interpreted as co-relation rather than causation.

This research has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the cross-sectional design precludes any fundamental inferences. Second, potential sources of bias, including selection bias and information bias, may have prejudiced the results. The inclusion of only school-attending adolescents may limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader adolescent population. Moreover, although the IOTN is a widely validated index, its aesthetic component contains a degree of subjectivity that may present measurement error. Finally, even with standardized assessment procedures, some inconsistency between examiners may still exist.

Conclusion

This study found that orthodontic treatment needs vary among adolescents in Kosovo from different ethnic groups, with Turkish adolescents facing the greatest clinical challenges. Still, these findings should be viewed with caution, as they might be influenced by genetic, environmental, or socioeconomic factors beyond ethnicity. The results suggest that orthodontic care should be included in national public health plans, with a focus on early detection, prevention, culturally sensitive education, and equitable access to treatment. These steps could help reduce oral health disparities, improve long-term outcomes, and support the World Health Organization’s goals for inclusive and resilient health systems in Kosovo.
