Coordinate recognition: General theory, Groups, and other surprises
Farah, I, Gannon, K, Touchard, P

Abstract
. A class of structures recognizes coordinates if any reduced product of structures from said class witnesses a certain kind of rigidity phenomena. We provide several equivalent characterizations of this property. This property has (at least) two remarkable consequences, one set-theoretic and one model-theoretic, for reduced products of structures of the said class. First, under appropriate set-theoretic assumptions every isomorphism between such reduced products associated with the Fréchet ideal lifts (modulo a finite change) to an isomorphism between products of the original structures. Second, with an additional mild assumption, it implies a strong quantifier elimination result. Of note, we show that a class recognizes coordinates if and only if an individual formula witnesses a certain syntactic property. We also consider many concrete classes of structures and determine whether or not they recognize coordinates. We place heavy emphasis on well known classes of groups, but we also discuss other classes of structures.

Introduction

This work is motivated by problems in both set theory and model theory. With regards to set theory, the central motivation arises from problems involving rigidity of quotient structures.
In 1979 Shelah described a forcing extension of the universe where all automorphisms of the Boolean algebra
\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{N}})/\Fin
are induced by a bijection
f_{\Phi}
between two cofinite subsets of
{\mathbb{N}}
(
[
45
]

of

such that the following diagram commutes (vertical arrows correspond to quotient maps).

\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{N}})
\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{N}})
\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{N}})/\Fin
\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{N}})/\Fin
\Phi_{*}
\Phi

In other words, in Shelah’s model every automorphism
\Phi
of

can be lifted by an endomorphism
\Phi_{*}
of
\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{N}})

Such automorphisms are called

Shelah’s conclusion held in a very specific forcing extension of the universe (the oracle-cc forcing was invented for the purpose of finding this extension!), but it was soon proved to follow from forcing axioms (
[
47

51

Analogous lifting results from forcing axioms were proven for quotient algebras of the form

New impetus to the study of set-theoretic rigidity theory was given by the solution to a prominent 1977 problem in the theory of operator algebras (
[
4

) asking whether the Calkin algebra has outer automorphisms. The Calkin algebra is the quotient of the algebra

of all bounded linear operators on the separable, infinite-dimensional, complex Hilbert space modulo the ideal of compact operators, and it is generally considered to be the noncommutative analog of

[
42

respectively, surprisingly showing that the answer to the Brown–Douglas–Fillmore question is independent from ZFC. (The fact that the proof that all automorphisms of the Calkin algebra are inner owes a lot to the ideas presented in

may be even more surprising.) A far-reaching generalization of

showing rigidity of coronas of other separable

-algebras was obtained in

The algebra

is isomorphic to the reduced product of two-element Boolean algebras modulo

it was generally believed by the experts that the chances of a sweeping extension of Shelah’s rigidity result to reduced products of other structures were slim. However in

-saturated, giving the analog of Rudin’s result. For state of the art of rigidity of quotient structures see

In the present paper we continue the investigation of set-theoretic rigidity.
Fundamental questions from this perspective include the following:

(1)

How does the isomorphism type of a reduced product

and the ideal

Analogous rigidity questions for reduced products of groups were studied in

Both of the above questions admit partial, yet substantive answers when considering whether or not our reduced product is composed of structures from a class which
recognizes coordinates

We continue the study of this property and our results proceed in two directions: general theory and examples with an emphasis on groups. 2.1.
Reduced products

A class of

-structures will often be denoted by

A\simeq B\ \Leftrightarrow\ A\vartriangle B\in\mathcal{I}.

If

Definition 2.1

(Reduced product)

Let

-structures. We let

(a_{i})_{i}\simeq(b_{i})_{i}\ \Leftrightarrow\{i\in\mathbb{I}\mid a_{i}\neq b_{i}\}\in\mathcal{I}.

Formally, we let
[(a_{i})]_{\mathcal{I}}
denote the equivalence class of
(a_{i})_{i}
. This quotient is naturally equipped with an
\mathcal{L}
-structure. For any
n

R\in\mathcal{L}
and

\models R([(a^{1}_{i})]_{\mathcal{I}},...,[(a^{n}_{i})]_{\mathcal{I}})~\Leftrightarrow~\{i\in\mathbb{I}\mid\mathcal{M}_{i}\models\neg R(a^{1}_{i},...,a^{n}_{i})\}\in\mathcal{I},

and

The structure

We often abuse notation and identify elements in

If all

Definition 2.2

An ideal

For all structures

,

Claim 2.20

2.5.
A folklore reformulation of Feferman–Vaught

As the title of this subsection suggests, we discuss a folklore variant of the Feferman–Vaught theorem in the context of reduced products. In broad strokes, this theorem explains how to understand the theory of the reduced product by understanding the theory of the indexed models along with the quotient Boolean algebra. As usual, we let
\mathbb{I}
be an indexing set,
\mathcal{I}
be an ideal on
\mathbb{I}
, and
(\mathcal{M}_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{I}}
be an indexed family of
\mathcal{L}
-structures. We denote by
\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{Bool}}
the language of Boolean algebra:

\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{Bool}}:=(\cap,\cup,0,1,^{\complement}).

Definition 2.21
.

Let
\phi(\bar{x})
be a formula with free variables
\bar{x}
and let
\bar{a}\in(\prod_{\mathcal{I}}\mathcal{M}_{i})^{|\bar{x}|}
. The
\phi
-support of
\bar{a}
, denoted by
[\![\bar{a}]\!]_{\phi(\bar{x})}
or
\supp_{\phi(\bar{x})}(\bar{a})
is
the element,

[\{i\in\mathbb{I}\mid\mathcal{M}_{i}\models\phi(\bar{a}_{i})\}]_{\mathcal{I}}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{I})/\mathcal{I}.

We call the function,

\supp_{\phi(\bar{x})}:\left(\prod_{\mathcal{I}}\mathcal{M}_{i}\right)^{|\bar{x}|}\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{I})/\mathcal{I},\text{ via }(\bar{a}_{i})\mapsto[\![\bar{a}]\!]_{\phi(x)},

the
\phi
-support function
of
\prod_{\mathcal{I}}\mathcal{M}_{i}
.
If
\theta
is an
\mathcal{L}
-sentence, we denote by
c_{\theta}
the following element of
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{I})/\mathcal{I}
:

c_{\theta}:=[\{i\in\mathbb{I}\mid\mathcal{M}_{i}\models\theta\}]_{\mathcal{I}}.

The theorem of Feferman–Vaught can be adapted to reduced products (see e.g.
[
5
, Theorem 6.3.2]
). We give below a folklore reformulation of this theorem in terms of relative quantifier elimination. We refer to
[
44
, Appendix A]
for definitions of relative quantifiers elimination and related concepts.

Proposition 2.22

(Feferman–Vaught for reduced products)
.

The reduced product
\mathcal{M}\coloneqq\prod_{\mathcal{I}}\mathcal{M}_{i}
eliminates quantifiers relative to
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{I})/\mathcal{I}
in the following two-sorted language
\mathcal{L}^{+}
:

\mathcal{L}\cup\left\{\supp_{\phi(\bar{x})}:\phi(\bar{x})\text{ an }\mathcal{L}\text{-formula}\right\}\cup\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{Bool}}\cup\{c_{\theta}:\theta\text{ an }\mathcal{L}\text{-sentence}\}.

The Feferman–Vaught theorem also asserts that there is an algorithmic procedure for quantifier elimination in this setting. Since we are not concerned with decidability, we will never (ever) mention this algorithm again.

Proof.

We first observe that the sort,

B:=(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{I})/\mathcal{I};\cap,\cup,0,1,\{c_{\theta}:\theta\text{ an }\mathcal{L}\text{- sentence}\}),

is
closed
in the following sense: in the language, there are neither functions from
B
to the main sort
\mathcal{M}
, nor predicates in
B^{n}\times\mathcal{M}^{m}
for strictly positive integers
n,m
. Any (parameter-free)
\mathcal{L}^{+}
-formula is equivalent, in the theory of the reduced product, to a Boolean combination of formulas of the form:

•

\psi_{B}(\supp_{\phi}(\bar{x}))

•

\phi(\bar{x})

where
\phi
is an
\mathcal{L}
-formula, and
\psi_{B}
is a formula in the language
\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{Bool}}\cup\{c_{\theta}:\theta\text{ an }\mathcal{L}\text{-sentence}\}
.
By
[
5
, Theorem 6.3.2]
, a formula of the second kind can be expressed as a formula of the first kind. Therefore, we have eliminated all quantifiers in the main sort
\prod_{\mathcal{I}}\mathcal{M}_{i}
. Since the sort is closed, relative quantifier elimination follows by
[
44
, Remark A.8]
. ∎

This language for quantifier elimination can sometimes be optimized by using the notion of a
fundamental
set of formula.

Definition 2.23
.

We call a set
\Phi
of

and

-formula is equivalent, in

.

The following (easy) proposition implies that we may obtain quantifier elimination in a fragment of

which contains the support functions

where

.

Proposition 2.24
.

Let
\phi(\bar{x})
and

be two
\mathcal{L}
-formula. Then every

in

satisfies the following:

\displaystyle\supp_{\neg\phi}(\bar{a})

\displaystyle=\supp_{\phi}(\bar{a})^{\complement},

\displaystyle\supp_{\phi\wedge\psi}(\bar{a})

\displaystyle=\supp_{\phi}(\bar{a})\cap\supp_{\psi}(\bar{a}),

\displaystyle\supp_{\phi\vee\psi}(\bar{a})

\displaystyle=\supp_{\phi}(\bar{a})\cup\supp_{\psi}(\bar{a}).

Corollary 2.25
.

If
\Phi
is a fundamental set of
\mathcal{L}
-formulas for the pair
(\mathcal{M}_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{I}}
and
\mathcal{I}
then

\mathcal{M}\coloneqq\prod_{\mathcal{I}}\mathcal{M}_{i}
eliminates quantifiers relative to
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{I})/\mathcal{I}
in the following two-sorted language

:

\mathcal{L}\cup\left\{\supp_{\phi(\bar{x})}:\phi(\bar{x})\in\Phi\right\}\cup\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{Bool}}\cup\{c_{\theta}:\theta\in\Phi\text{ is a sentence}\}.

Proof.

It is enough to show that every function of the form

, where

-formula, is equivalent to a quantifier-free

Fix an

is equivalent to a Boolean combination of formulas from

\phi_{1}(\bar{x}),\dots,\phi_{k}(\bar{x})
. By

is the same Boolean combination of

. Therefore, it is expressed without quantifiers in the language

∎

3.1.
Interpreting supports implies recognizing coordinates

We show in this subsection that if all reduced products from a class of structures interpret the relative support function, then said class recognizes coordinates. The proof breaks nicely into two steps: (1) if a reduced product
\prod_{\mathcal{I}}\mathcal{M}_{i}
interprets the relative support function, then it also (uniformly) interprets the quotient structures

for any

and (2) if all reduced products from a class of structures interpret the appropriate Boolean algebra and quotients (coherently), then the class recognizes coordinates. We prove Step (1) and then Step (2).

Consider a reduced product
\mathcal{M}:=\prod_{\mathcal{I}}\mathcal{M}_{i}
and nonzero

induces the ideal

We first show Step (1), i.e., interpreting the relative support function implies that the relevant family of substructures is also interpretable.

3.2.
Recognizing coordinates implies interpretability of the support

In this section, we prove the main implication (

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
{\mathcal{L}}
be a countable language and let

\mathfrak{C}
be a full class of
\mathcal{L}
-structures. If
\mathfrak{C}
recognizes coordinates in
{\mathbb{N}}

defines the relative support function.

The proof of this proposition requires two lemmas. In one of the lemmas, we use a Keisler–Shelah style argument to compare isomorphic ultrapowers.
Since there is a forcing extension in which two elementarily equivalent countable structures have no isomorphic ultrapower associated with an ultrafilter on
{\mathbb{N}}
(
[
46
]
), our proof involves set theory. More precisely, we need a slight modification of a standard theorem, first proven in
[

, asserting that if
\varphi

\varphi
is provable in ZFC.
We say that a class
\mathfrak{C}
of structures of the same language is
closed under elementary equivalence
of
\mathcal{M}\in\mathfrak{C}
and
\mathcal{N}\equiv\mathcal{M}
implies
\mathcal{N}\in\mathfrak{C}
.
Such class is coded by a set of (a priori unrelated) theories
\mathcal{T}
, as the union of classes of all models of each theory in
\mathcal{T}
.

For a reduced product

4.
Groups recognizing coordinates

In this section, we shift our attention to classes of groups. We prove that a variety of different class of groups recognize coordinates. We first prove some basic preparatory results to work in this setting. We then show that a large variety of familiar classes of groups recognize coordinates. In later sections, we will consider classes of groups which do not recognize coordinates.

4.3.
Perfect groups

We remark that up to this point in the paper, it is not immediately obvious if there exists groups with non-trivial centers which recognize coordinates. We will see in a later section that if a group admits a
non-trivial homomorphism to its center
, then it cannot recognize coordinates. Yet, not every group with a nontrivial center admits a nontrivial homomorphism into its center. An example is provided by the so-called
perfect
groups. These are the groups
G
with commutator subgroup
[G,G]
equal to
G
itself. A homomorphism from
G
into some group has an abelian range if and only if its kernel includes the commutator subgroup. In particular, since for
H\triangleleft G
we have that
G/H
is abelian if and only if
H\supseteq G^{\prime}
, a group is perfect if and only if it has no nontrivial abelian quotients. Hence a perfect group
G
with nontrivial center does not admit any nontrivial homomorphism into its center.

The
commutator width
of a perfect group is the minimal
n
such that every element of
G
is the product of
n
commutators. There are perfect groups with arbitrarily large commutator width, see
[
35
, §2]
.

5.

5.1.
General criteria for not recognizing coordinates

Theorem 5.1
.

Suppose that
\mathfrak{C}
is a class of groups that satisfies one of the following two conditions.

(1)

It contains groups
G
and
H
and a nontrivial homomorphism
f\colon G\to Z(H)
.

(2)

It contains a group that is decomposable as a direct product of nontrivial groups.

Then
\mathfrak{C}
does not recognize coordinates

The converse of this theorem does not hold. Namely, there is a family
\mathfrak{D}
of groups that fails both (
1
) and (
2
) of Theorem
5.1
but it does not recognize coordinates (see Theorem
7.1
).
The sufficiency of the second condition is obvious, and the
proof of the sufficiency of the first uses the following obvious lemma.

Lemma 5.2
.

Suppose that
T
recognizes coordinates. Consider
\theta:\prod\mathcal{M}_{i}\to\prod\mathcal{N}_{i}
an isomorphism between (non-reduced) products. If
|\supp(a)|
is finite, then
|\supp(\theta(a))|=|\supp(a)|
.

Proof.

By
Theorem

2.16
, the support map is interpretable. In particular, the set
\{a\mid|\supp(a)|=n\}
is definable and thus preserved by isomorphisms.
∎

Proof of Theorem
5.1
.

Here, we simply quotient out by the trivial ideal. Consider the map
\tau_{f}:G\times H\to G\times H
defined via
\tau_{f}((a,b))=(a,f(a)b)
. Then
\tau_{f}
is an automorphism of
G\times H
.

(1)

Homomorphism: Notice that

\displaystyle\tau_{f}((a,b)(c,d))

\displaystyle=\tau_{f}((ac,bd))=(ac,f(ac)bd)

\displaystyle=(ac,f(a)bf(c)d)=(a,f(a)b)(c,f(c)d)

\displaystyle=\tau_{f}((a,b))\tau_{f}((c,d)).

(2)

Injective: Suppose that
\tau_{f}((a,b))=\bar{e}
. Then
(a,af(b))=(e,e)
. Hence
a=e
and so
e=f(a)b=f(e)b=b
. It follows that
\tau_{f}
is injective.

(3)

Surjective: Fix
(a,b)\in G\times G
. Then

\tau_{f}(a,f(a)^{-1}b)=(a,b).

In particular, this allows us to build an automorphism

7.

In this section we collect results that together suggest that the problem of characterizing classes of groups that recognize coordinates is nontrivial.

7.1.

We have already seen that each one of

7.2.

8.2.
Isomorphisms between (non-reduced) products

Here we make some connections between recognizing coordinates and recognizing coordinates in products and direct sums. The following is the analog of Definition
2.5
in the context of products.

Definition 8.2
.

An isomorphism
\Phi
between products
\mathcal{M}=\prod_{i}\mathcal{M}_{i}
and
\mathcal{N}=\prod_{i}\mathcal{N}_{j}
is
isomorphically coordinate respecting
if there is a bijection
\pi\colon\mathbb{J}\to\mathbb{I}
such that for all
j\in\mathbb{J}
there is an isomorphism
\varphi_{j}\colon\mathcal{M}_{\pi(j)}\to\mathcal{N}_{j}
such that

\Phi((a_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{I}})=(\varphi_{i}(a_{\pi(i)})),

for all
(a_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{I}}
in
\mathcal{M}
.

A first-order theory
T
is said to
recognize coordinates in products
if every isomorphism between products of models of
T
is isomorphically coordinate respecting.

More generally, if
\mathfrak{C}
is a class of structures of the same language (not necessarily axiomatizable), then
\mathfrak{C}
is said to
recognize coordinates
if for every isomorphism between products of structures from
\mathfrak{C}
is isomorphically coordinate respecting.

We remark that because products are a kind of reduced product, the following theorem holds trivially.

Theorem 8.3
.

Every class of structures that recognizes coordinates, in particular every class of groups listed in Theorem
2
recognizes coordinates in products.

Recognizing coordiantes in products is closely related to the classical Renek–Krull–Schmidt–Azumaya theorem that we now discuss, following
[
30
]
. In the original context of groups (or groups with operators—that is, groups with additional operations) this theorem asserts that if
G
is a group which has a (finite) principal series of normal subgroups, then any two decompositions of
G
into direct product of indecomposable factors are centrally isomorphic (
[
30
, p. 120]
; see also its strengthening ‘The Fundamental Theorem’
[
30
, p. 114]
).
6
6

6

Note that the assumptions are stated in terms of the product group, and not in terms of the indecomposable factors as in our case.

In other words, if
G=\prod_{i<m}G_{i}
has principal series and
\Phi\colon\prod_{i<m}G_{i}\to\prod_{j<n}H_{j}
is an isomorphism where all
G_{i}
and all
H_{j}
are indecomposable, then
m=n
, there is a bijection
\pi\colon n\to m
, and there are isomorphisms

such that

defines an isomorphism which satisfies that
\Phi(a)\Psi(a)^{-1}
is in

for all

.

Our requirements on the factor groups are more stringent, since our assumptions on classes of groups in Theorem
2
imply that no nontrivial homomorphism from
G
into its center exists. On the other hand, to the best of our knowledge, our result is the first extension of the
Renek–Krull-Schmidt–Azumaya theorem to arbitrary infinite products. Along these lines, Azumaya’s theorem (see e.g.,
[
13
]
) and the results of
[
8
]
are about infinite direct

of modules and arbitrary algebraic structures, respectively.

It is curious that admitting nontrivial homomorphisms into the center gives a limiting example in this type of theorem. In
[
30
, p. 81]
Kurosh gives an example of indecomposable groups

,
B
,
C
and
D
such that

but neither

nor
B
is isomorphic to
C
or to
D
. Each one of these groups has the center isomorphic to
{\mathbb{Z}}
(actually,
D
is isomorphic to
{\mathbb{Z}}
) and admits a homomorphism onto
{\mathbb{Z}}
. An even more interesting class of examples, showing that even the number of indecomposable factors is not an isomorphism invariant, is given in
[
1
]
.

Remark 8.4
.

The main theorem of
[
2
]
records a version of recognizing coordinates with respect to
finite products
of finite groups. More explicitly, if
\mathfrak{C}=\{G_{1},...,G_{n}\}
such that

(1)

For
i\leq n
, each
G_{i}
is a finite group.

(2)

For

(3)

Then the class

Finally, we provide a example which demonstrates how the collection of automorphisms in the context of direct sums can appear wildly different than the collection of automorphisms in the context of reduced products.
