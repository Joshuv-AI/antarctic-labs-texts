Outcomes of surgical fixation of osteochondral fracture: a systematic review
Authors: Alexander Shao-Rong Pang, Ramesh Oviya, Jarell Jie-Rae Tan, Kean Seng Andrew Lim, James Hoi Po Hui, Si Heng Sharon Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDealing with knee osteochondral defects presents a substantial clinical challenge due to the variable nature of repair outcomes and intricate biomechanical complexities inherent to the knee joint. Numerous surgical alternatives exist for addressing knee osteochondral lesions, yet there is limited evidence available for comparing their respective outcomes.MethodsAccording to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) guidelines, a systematic literature search was conducted to identify publications from inception to September 2023 on PubMed, Cochrane, and Medline academic search engines with the MeSH terms “osteochondral” AND (“patella” OR “patellar” OR “patellofemoral” OR “femoral condyle lesions” OR “trochlear lesions”). Inclusion criteria were clinical human studies, English language, subjects who underwent surgical management (fixation, reconstruction, or debridement) for knee osteochondral lesions with reported treatment outcomes, and a minimum sample size of ten patients. The methodological index for non-randomized studies (MINORS) was used to evaluate the non-randomized studies’ methodological quality. Main outcome measurements Pediatric International Knee Documentation Committee (Pedi IKDC) scale, Tegner–Lysholm Scoring Scale, Tegner Activity Score, Visual Analog Scale, Knee Society Score (KSS) Score, Kujala Score, and Fulkerson score were collected.ResultsIn all, 25 studies were included with 1093 patients in total. The analysis revealed that patellar lesions had similar outcomes to femoral lesions. Surgical fixation demonstrated superior outcomes compared with debridement, with improvements observed across multiple validated outcome measures. Both fixation and reconstruction resulted in significant improvement in outcomes. Osteochondral fragment size, age, and sex did not influence postoperative outcomes.ConclusionsWhile surgical management of knee osteochondral defects consistently yields significant functional improvement, the current evidence favors anatomic restoration via fixation or reconstruction over debridement. Despite the variability in patient demographics and reported outcomes, these findings suggest that treatment selection should be prioritized on the basis of lesion characteristics and specific surgical technique rather than age alone. However, given the significant heterogeneity, wide age range, and missing demographic data across the literature, these findings should be interpreted as observed associations rather than definitive evidence of clinical superiority. They underscore the need for tailored treatment strategies and highlight the requirement for high-level, standardized research with consistent use of validated outcome measures and established minimal clinically important difference (MCID) thresholds to further clarify optimal surgical interventions.Level of Evidence4.Trial registration: The protocol of this review was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) international prospective register of systematic reviews database (CRD42024596365).

Introduction

Osteochondral defects of the knee occur when articular cartilage is removed from the joint surface, either partially or entirely, along with underlying subchondral bone. These may arise from biological or mechanical reasons (such as recurrent microtrauma from trauma or overuse) [1]. The knee joint is the most commonly affected site, with patellar instability representing a frequent mechanism of injury [2]. Approximately 70% of patellar dislocations result in osteochondral fractures, as shearing stresses act on the lateral femoral condyle and medial patellar facet during lateral patellar displacement [3]. While osteochondral fragments can develop in any age group, children and young adolescents are the most susceptible to developing such injuries [4, 5].

The morphological classification of osteochondral fractures requires further elaboration, as different fragment types may influence treatment decisions [6]. Management of osteochondral fragments in the knee is complex and dependent on multiple factors, including lesion size, chronicity, and patient characteristics. Orthopedic surgeons face significant challenges when treating large osteochondral lesions of the knee [7]. There is a variety of methods for treating these osteochondral lesions. Available surgical treatment modalities include screw insertion, autologous chondrocyte implantation (ACI), osteochondral autograft transfer systems (OATS), osteochondral allograft (OCA) transplantation, pull-through sutures, scaffolds with or without regenerative cells, and bioabsorbable pins [8, 9].

The indications for surgical intervention in pediatric patellar osteochondral fractures remain unclear, and the long-term outcomes of these procedures are uncertain. The long-term outcomes and effectiveness of such surgeries are still uncertain. Surgical fixation has demonstrated effectiveness, particularly in acute settings. Bone marrow stimulation techniques, including drilling, microfracture (MFx), and abrasion arthroplasty, promote fibrocartilage regeneration. ACI represents a newer approach that uses a chondrocyte scaffold to generate hyaline-like cartilage. Systematic reviews demonstrate that ACI is a safe and effective across various age groups, particularly for treating large cartilage lesions in adolescents due to its regenerative potential [10–12]. OCA transplantation effectively restores mature hyaline cartilage without donor site morbidity, addressing delamination issues associated with ACI [13]. This technique is indicated for large, full-thickness cartilage defects (> 3 cm2) resulting from trauma, osteochondritis dissecans, osteonecrosis, or failed prior treatments such as MFx or ACI, especially when subchondral bone damage is present [14]. Techniques include the dowel method for most lesions and the shell method for irregularly shaped defects [14, 15]. Mosaicplasty treats chondral defects using autografts or cadaveric allografts, though graft mismatching limits its use to larger defects [10, 12]. When combined with open reduction and internal fixation for full-thickness chondral lesions, mosaicplasty has demonstrated excellent radiologic and clinical outcomes [16].

Despite the clinical importance of these treatment options, comprehensive data on how osteochondral fragment characteristics influence surgical outcomes remain limited. For the purposes of this review, “osteochondral fracture” refers to the acute traumatic event, while “lesion” and “defect” describe the localized area of joint surface damage. This systematic review aims to provide a comprehensive evaluation of surgical fixation outcomes for osteochondral fragments of the knee, establishing consensus on morphological classification systems, treatment modalities, surgical indications, expected patient outcomes, and complications associated with knee osteochondral lesions and their management.

Material and methods

Search methodology

The systematic review was performed in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook and reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [17] (Fig. 1).Fig. 1PRISMA flow diagram depicting the selection process for the systematic review

PRISMA flow diagram depicting the selection process for the systematic review

This study provides Level 4 evidence. The protocol of this review was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) international prospective register of systematic reviews database (CRD42024596365).

Selection criteria

Three authors conducted a comprehensive literature search across PubMed, MEDLINE, and The Cochrane Library from database inception up to 15 September 2023. In accordance with PRISMA guidelines, the specific search string with the medical subject headings (MeSH) and Boolean operators used was “osteochondral" AND (“patella” OR “patellar” OR “patellofemoral” OR “femoral condyle lesions” OR “trochlear lesions”). The eligibility criteria for the inclusion of articles included (1) articles with original clinical human studies; (2) publication in the English language; (3) subjects who underwent any surgical management (including surgical fixation, reconstruction, or debridement) for knee osteochondral lesions with reported treatment outcomes; and (4) a minimum sample size of ten patients. By including a broader range of surgical interventions in the initial selection, we were able to perform a comparative analysis between fixation and other surgical management modalities. Exclusion criteria included duplicates, non-English publications; studies that had no original data on clinical human studies, had no knee osteochondral lesions, only reported isolated chondral defects, only reported lesions due to osteochondritis dissecans, or had a case series of sample size less than 10 patients; and case studies or reports. The search, using the aforementioned eligibility requirements and procedures, yielded shortlisted abstracts, which were assessed for eligibility via full-text screening.

Data extraction

The study titles and abstracts were screened by three independent researchers in accordance with the inclusion criteria. The full text of the studies potentially meeting the eligibility criteria were retrieved for a detailed perusal to make a final decision regarding inclusion. Data for each study were retrieved individually.

The following data were extracted: (1) identification (first author; publication year); (2) demographics (number of patients, number of males and females, average age during operation); (3) pre-operative factors (presence of predisposing conditions to patellar instability, mechanism of injury); (4) injury type (injury location, weight-bearing [WB] or non-weight-bearing [NWB], chondral or osteochondral injury, associated injuries, size of lesion); (5) operative management (time from injury to surgery, fixation or reconstruction, type of fixation or reconstruction, concomitant procedures performed); (6) postoperative status (Kyle classification; Pediatric International Knee Documentation Committee [Pedi IKDC] score, a validated knee-specific outcome measure ranging from 0 to 100 with higher scores indicating better function; Tegner–Lysholm Scoring Scale, a 100-point scale assessing knee function, with higher scores representing better outcomes; Tegner Activity Score, ranging from 0 (disability) to 10 (competitive sports at the highest level); postoperative Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) score or pain, ranging from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst pain imaginable); Kujala Score, a patellofemoral-specific outcome measure ranging from 0 to 100 with higher scores indicating less pain and better function; Fulkerson Score, a 100-point scale with higher scores indicating better function and less pain; Knee Society Score (KSS), comprising separate knee (0–100) and function (0–100) subscales with higher scores indicating better outcomes); and (7) complications (presence of complications, type of complications, revision surgery).

In case of disputes, a fourth senior reviewer was involved in the discussion to arrive at a consensus.

Assessment of study quality

The methodological index for non-randomized studies (MINORS) was used to evaluate the non-randomized studies’ methodological quality. In all, 12 items for comparative non-randomized studies and 8 items for noncomparative studies make up the MINORS tool [18]. Scores for the items are as follows: 0 for not reported, 1 for reported but inadequate, and 2 for reported and adequate. The global ideal score was 24. Two authors (A.S.-R.P. and R.O.) independently examined this, and any differences between them were discussed or appealed to a third author (J.T.J.-R.). Data and statistical analysis

Outcomes were assessed according to the change in respective outcome measurement scores postoperatively. Subgroup analysis was performed on the basis of factors such as injury location (on the patella and femur), types of management (fixation, reconstruction or debridement), method of fixation (arthroscopic versus open), size of osteochondral fragment, and patient age.

Results

The review search yielded a total of 2309 articles, of which 25 met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis, with 1093 patients in total (Table 2). Regarding demographic data, sex distribution was available only for 696 of the 1093 patients (63.7%). Of these, 513 were male, and 183 were female. Sex information was unavailable for 397 patients (36.3%) across seven studies. The mean age of the patients in 24 studies was 28.4 years (minimum = 9, maximum = 59). The ages of the patients in the study by Krishnan et al. were not included in the sample size calculation as the age distribution was not reported. In all, 464, 565, and 55 knees had osteochondral lesions that were located on the patella, femur, and tibia, respectively. Ten knees in Schneider et al.’s study reported lesions on multiple locations. Of the knees, 215 underwent fixation, with 88 undergoing open fixation and 127 undergoing arthroscopic fixations. In all, 828 knees underwent reconstruction, and 50 knees underwent debridement. Outcomes based on osteochondral fragment location

All 25 studies included patients with patellar lesions (n = 464), but only 18 included patients with femoral lesions (n = 565), with 31 patients having both patellar and femoral lesions [19–22]. Of the studies, 12 (645 knees) directly compared the outcomes based on their respective locations on the patella versus femur. Out of these, nine studies (372 knees) reported no significant difference between in outcomes based on osteochondral fragment location based on the International Knee Documentation Committee (IKDC), VAS, Tegner–Lysholm Scoring Scale, Knee Society Score, Modified Hospital for Special Knee Surgery, Cincinnati Knee Rating System, Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS) scoring systems [15, 20, 21, 23–31]. Two studies (74 knees) reported that lesions on the femur yielded superior outcomes compared with patellar lesions based on the KOOS and IKDC score [20, 30], but the difference reported was lower than the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) [32]. Another study (199 knees) reported better outcomes in lesions occurring on the patella compared with the femur based on the Cincinnati Knee Rating System score [28], but this similarly did not achieve the MCID [33].

Outcomes based on type of surgical management—fixation, reconstruction, or debridement

Surgical fixation was performed in 11 studies (330 knees), reconstruction was performed in 15 studies (716 knees), and debridement was performed in 2 studies (47 knees). Fixation resulted in a significant improvement in outcomes and function measured based on the Tegner–Lysholm Scoring Scale (four studies), Kujala Score (three studies), VAS (three studies), the Tegner Activity Score (two studies), KOOS (three studies), IKDC (five studies) and Modified Hospital for Special Knee Surgery (one study) scores. Likewise, all studies that carried out reconstruction also found a significant improvement in outcomes across Tegner–Lysholm Scoring Scale (two studies), Kujala Score (three studies), Fulkerson score (1 study), VAS (two studies), Tegner Activity Score (four studies), KOOS (two studies), IKDC (four studies), Knee Society Score (one study), Cincinnati Knee Rating System (three studies), and Oxford (1 study) scores. Only one study (30 knees) performed a direct comparison of outcomes between fixation (16 knees) versus reconstruction (14 knees) of osteochondral lesions, which reported that outcomes between both groups were not significantly different consistently across Tegner Activity Score, Kujala Score, IKDC, and KOOS scores [19]. Two studies (95 knees) compared the fixation versus debridement of osteochondral lesions, with both studies reporting that fixation had significantly better outcomes compared with debridement across KOOS, IKDC, and Tegner–Lysholm Scoring Scale scores [34]. Furthermore, the rate of reintervention was significantly lower in those who underwent fixation compared with debridement.

Outcomes based on sex

Among five studies (301 knees) that reported outcomes according to patient sex, all showed that patient sex had no bearing on patient outcomes. These findings were consistently seen in the studies that reported IKDC (three studies), VAS (two studies), Tegner Activity Score (one study), and Tegner–Lysholm Scoring Scale (one study) scores [20, 21, 26–28].

Outcomes based on the size of the osteochondral fragment

Across seven studies (404 knees), osteochondral fragment size did not affect postoperative outcomes measured using IKDC (two studies), Tegner Activity Score (one study), Tegner–Lysholm Scoring Scale (one study), Knee Society (one study), Cincinnati Knee Rating System (one study), or VAS (one study) scores [15, 21, 23, 25, 26, 28, 31]. Further sensitivity analysis showed that this finding remained consistent across both fixation (two studies) and reconstruction (five studies) of osteochondral fragments.

Outcomes based on age

Four studies (203 knees) reported patient age at the time of intervention was not a significant factor affecting postoperative outcomes in patients undergoing either surgical fixation or reconstruction [24, 25, 27]. One study (13 knees) reported that, although slower recovery times were observed in older patients, this did not result in any significant difference in long term outcomes and function based on the IKDC score [26]. Although one study (36 knees) reported inferior outcomes among older patients based on the Cincinnati Knee Rating System score [28], this did not achieve the MCID established in previous literature [35].

Discussion

The management of knee osteochondral defects presents a substantial clinical challenge due to the joint’s complex biomechanics and the variable nature of repair outcomes. This review analyzed data from 1093 patients across 25 studies, evaluating outcomes from a range of patient demographics and various surgical interventions for patellar and femoral osteochondral lesions. The analysis revealed that patellar lesions had similar outcomes to femoral lesions overall. Fixation techniques demonstrated superior outcomes compared with debridement with improvements observed across multiple validated outcome measures, but did not show significant difference in outcomes when compared with reconstruction in the single study (30 knees) that directly compared these treatment modalities. Osteochondral fragment size had no significant effect on postoperative outcomes. Patient sex had no effect on patient outcomes. Additionally, age did not have a consistent impact on outcomes across most studies.

A comparative analysis of fixation, reconstruction, and debridement techniques showed notable differences in clinical outcomes. Based on the Tegner Activity Score, Kujala Score, IKDC, and KOOS scores, fixation and reconstruction exhibited no significant differences. Fixation showed significantly better outcomes than debridement based on KOOS, IKDC, and Tegner–Lysholm Scoring Scale scores, as well as lower reintervention rates. Fixation (330 knees) and reconstruction (716 knees) both respectively showed significant improvement across multiple functional scores.

These findings are strongly supported by contemporary literature on osteochondral fragment management. A direct comparison study of open flake refixation versus ACI following acute patella dislocation with osteochondral flake fractures demonstrated no significant difference between both groups across Tegner Activity Score (5.1 ± 1.8 versus 5.1 ± 1.4), Kujala Score (86.1 ± 12.6 versus 84.9 ± 9.1), IKDC (83.8 ± 15.0 versus 83.6 ± 11.3), and KOOS (83.3 ± 14.0 versus 83.6 ± 12.0) scores at a mean 51–57 months’ follow-up, with excellent clinical and radiological outcomes in both groups [19]. This equivalence between fixation and reconstruction is further corroborated by a meta-analysis of level 1 studies (12 randomized controlled trials [RCTs], 765 patients) which found no significant difference between marrow stimulation, ACI, and osteochondral autograft transfer (OAT) in improving function and pain at intermediate-term follow-up [36]. A comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis of 47 studies (1993 patients) demonstrated that all cartilage reconstruction procedures—including ACI, matrix-induced ACI (MACI), OAT, and OCA—result in significant improvements in knee function and pain, with all four procedures meeting the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for IKDC, Tegner–Lysholm Scoring Scale, Tegner Activity Score, and VAS scores [37]. A recent systematic review of primary fixation of isolated chondral fragments (93 patients, mean follow-up 42.4 months) reported intact fixation in 85% of patients, successful return to sport/activity in 90% of patients, and revision surgery directly associated with the chondral fragment in only 10.8% of patients [38]. These findings are corroborated by existing literature demonstrating that surgical fixation preserves the joint surface, maintaining better joint congruity and proper load distribution across the knee joint. Fragment preservation through fixation restores the native articular surface with hyaline cartilage, which has superior biomechanical properties compared with the fibrocartilage produced by debridement and bone marrow stimulation techniques [39–41].

In contrast, debridement can exacerbate mechanical imbalances in the knee, especially if a significant portion of the cartilage or bone is removed, which can result in a further deterioration of the joint surfaces and accelerated progression to early osteoarthritis [42]. The evidence for debridement as a standalone treatment for osteochondral lesions is considerably weaker. A randomized controlled trial (ChAMP Trial) comparing debridement versus observation of unstable chondral lesions during partial meniscectomy found no significant difference in outcomes at 5-year follow-up, with no benefit demonstrated for arthroscopic debridement of chondral lesions across the Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) Pain Scale, KOOS, VAS, or 36-Item Short Form Survey (SF-36) scores [43].

This review did not find a significant correlation between a patient’s age at time of surgery and surgical outcomes in the majority of included studies. Although, Krishnan et al. reported inferior outcomes among older patients based on the Cincinnati Knee Rating System score, the differences are lower than the MCID established in previous literature [35]. These findings are partially consistent with, but also contrast with, contemporary literature on age-related differences in cartilage healing and repair. Multiple recent studies have demonstrated that, when outcomes are standardized according to age-appropriate normative data, older patients (> 40 years) achieve comparable functional improvement relative to their baseline and age-matched healthy populations. Filardo et al. showed that, while older patients achieved lower absolute IKDC scores at 10-year follow-up (65.8 ± 24.3 versus 77.2 ± 20.4, p = 0.007), standardized results revealed no significant differences when accounting for age-expected functional levels. Similarly, recent registry data from 217 patients aged 50–69 years demonstrated comparable Tegner–Lysholm Scoring Scale scores (p = 0.232) and VAS pain scores (p = 0.299) to younger cohorts following autologous matrix-induced chondrogenesis (AMIC), with a mean follow-up of 6.8 years. Furthermore, a 2023 systematic review of 184 studies (8905 procedures) found that patient age had only a fair negative association with outcomes (p = 0.03–0.04) [44–46].

However, the effect of age appears to be technique-dependent and may be more pronounced with certain surgical approaches. For microfracture specifically, Kreuz et al. demonstrated that patients aged > 40 years experienced significant deterioration in International Cartilage Repair Society (ICRS) scores between 18 and 36 months postoperatively (p < 0.05), while younger patients maintained stable outcomes. Conversely, more advanced techniques such as collagen-augmented chondrogenesis have shown superior cartilage repair quality compared with microfracture regardless of patient age, with no significant differences in VAS or WOMAC scores between patients aged ≥ 50 years and < 50 years at 2-year follow-up [47, 48]. For OAT/mosaicplasty, age has been identified as a significant prognostic factor in multiple studies. A systematic review of 610 patients with mean 10.2-year follow-up found that increased age positively correlated with failure rate. Solheim et al. reported that patients ≥ 40 years had a 59% poor outcome rate compared with lower rates in younger patients, with a mean Tegner–Lysholm Scoring Scale score of 72 at 10–14 years. Similarly, Emre et al.’s regression analysis of 152 patients demonstrated that age was an independent predictor of final Tegner–Lysholm Scoring Scale score, with the equation showing a 0.2-point decrease for each year of age [49–51]. This indicates the necessity of further investigation into the relationship between patient age and surgical outcomes for osteochondral lesions. The limited and inconsistent data on age effects across studies may reflect inadequate statistical power or confounding by lesion characteristics and surgical technique selection.

Limitations and future work

There were several limitations to this study. Firstly, the inclusion of level III and IV studies could have introduced potential confounders that were not controlled for in the original studies. Secondly, the use of many different scoring systems to compare the improvement in outcomes postoperatively could also contribute to some inconsistencies in the interpretation of the results, since each individual scoring system comprises different components and weighting of outcomes. The lack of standardized outcome measure reporting across studies, including inconsistent use of validated scoring systems and variable definitions of clinical success, limited the ability to perform robust meta-analyses and draw definitive conclusions. This also contributed to certain categories drawing inconclusive results, due to the difficulties in drawing a fair comparison across the different types of scoring systems used. For use of this review’s findings in clinical practice, it may be useful to compare the criteria of each scoring system and use the scoring system that prioritizes the important factors on a case-by-case basis for each patient. Additionally, the significant proportion of missing demographic data (36.3% of patients with unreported sex) and the wide age range (9–59 years) across studies introduced substantial heterogeneity that may have obscured clinically meaningful subgroup differences. The predominance of male patients (74% of those with reported sex) may limit generalizability to female populations, particularly given potential sex-based differences in cartilage biology and healing capacity.

As for future work, it would be advantageous to be able to access the specific scoring data for each scoring system used, so the outcomes can be further stratified more accurately and provide greater comparison and results. Long-term follow-up studies would also add value, as they would assess the durability and sustained efficacy of different treatment approaches. Prospective studies with standardized outcome measure reporting, including consistent use of validated patient‐reported outcome measures (PROMs) with established MCID values, would strengthen the evidence base. Future research should also address the impact of patient-specific factors such as sex, body mass index, activity level, and lesion chronicity on surgical outcomes, as these variables were inconsistently reported across the included studies.

Conclusions

While surgical management of knee osteochondral defects consistently yields significant functional improvement, the current evidence favors anatomic restoration via fixation or reconstruction over debridement. Despite the variability in patient demographics and reported outcomes, these findings suggest that treatment selection should be prioritized on the basis of lesion characteristics and specific surgical technique rather than age alone. However, given the significant heterogeneity, wide age range, and missing demographic data across the literature, these findings should be interpreted as observed associations rather than definitive evidence of clinical superiority. They underscore the need for tailored treatment strategies and highlight the requirement for high-level, standardized research with consistent use of validated outcome measures and established MCID thresholds to further clarify optimal surgical interventions.
