Ostrich-derived Bacillus safensis O-6 enhances broiler growth performance by modulating gut microbiota and protein digestibility☆
Authors: Gege Wang, Yingying Chen, Yuncai Xiao, Mengxin Sun, Xiang Li, Muhammad Akhtar, Yingting You, Xinxin Zhou, Deshi Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In broiler production, the inefficient utilization of dietary protein limits growth performance and wastes resources. Inspired by the high feed conversion efficiency of ostriches, which may host beneficial digestive microbes, this study investigated whether fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) from ostriches could confer advantageous traits to broilers, and further sought to isolate and evaluate potential probiotic. In an initial FMT trial involving 54 one-day-old broilers, the FMT group exhibited significantly higher average daily gain (ADG) at 7 and 14 days of age compared to the Ctrl group (P < 0.05). From the fecal samples of ostrich donors, a bacterial strain, Bacillus safensis O-6 (O-6), was isolated and identified as a high producer of both protease and cellulase. In a subsequent feeding trial with 72 one-day-old broilers, supplementation with O-6 (4.74 × 10⁷ CFU/mL/day in drinking water) increased the ADG by 7.16% by day 35 (P <0.01). The treatment also significantly enhanced the activities of protease, amylase, and lipase in the small intestine (P < 0.05), elevated serum immunoglobulins A and M (IgA and IgM) (P < 0.01), and improved immune organ indices (Spleen index and Bursa of Fabricius index, P < 0.05). Furthermore, O-6 supplementation significantly enhanced the alpha and beta diversity of the cecal microbiota (P < 0.05). This was accompanied by an enrichment of beneficial bacterial taxa, including Rikenellaceae and Alistipes, alongside a substantial reduction in cecal protein content by 28.23%, indicating improved protein digestibility. These results demonstrate that O-6 is a promising probiotic that enhances broiler growth by improving digestive enzyme activity, immune function, and gut microbiota structure. This study demonstrates that beneficial traits of ostriches can be transferred to broiler chickens through their gut microbiota (probiotics), offering a new avenue for the exploration of avian-derived probiotics.

Introduction

In intensive broiler farming, corn-soybean meal diets, which are rich in protein, are commonly used, with soybean meal being the main source of protein (Kandel et al., 2025). However, compared to other livestock such as pigs, cattle, and sheep, chickens possess a shorter and more limited digestive tract, resulting in insufficient nutrient digestion and relatively low feed utilization efficiency. This inefficiency ultimately leads to the wastage of protein resources (Matton et al., 2025). Enhancing protein utilization efficiency, minimizing resource waste, and mitigating nitrogen emissions are pressing issues that require attention. The exogenous supplementation of digestive enzymes (e.g., proteases, amylases, cellulases) has been shown to significantly enhance protein utilization, improve growth performance, and reduce nitrogen emissions in livestock (Chen et al., 2024; Simon et al., 2024). Nevertheless, the high costs associated with direct exogenous enzyme supplementation restrict its widespread application in animal production. Studies have indicated that certain probiotics can produce digestive enzymes in vivo. For instance, Bacillus safensis isolated from the intestine of tilapia has been demonstrated to produce proteases, amylases, and cellulases, significantly increasing daily weight gain, growth rate, and cumulative survival rate in tilapia (Wu et al., 2021). Therefore, exploring beneficial microorganisms with robust enzyme-producing capabilities has emerged as a promising solution to this challenge.

As one of the largest extant bird species, ostriches possess a well-developed cecum colonized by a rich microbial community capable of efficiently decomposing and utilizing dietary proteins, cellulose, and other nutrients(Videvall et al., 2019). This species exhibits desirable traits such as strong reproductive capacity, rapid growth, disease resistance, high feed conversion efficiency, and robust environmental adaptability (Akram et al., 2025). These superior production traits are regulated not only by the host’s genetic background but might also be closely associated with its extensive intestinal microbial community. Research has demonstrated that the structure and function of the gut microbiota dynamically change across different developmental stages of the host and profoundly influence host health, phenotype, and disease susceptibility. Thus, the gut microbiota is regarded as a virtual metabolic organ within the host (Chen et al., 2022). Based on this, we hypothesize that the intestinal microbiota of ostriches harbors beneficial microorganisms with potent enzyme-producing capabilities, which enhance the host’s efficiency in utilizing and absorbing various nutrients, thereby promoting growth.

As an effective approach to remodeling gut microbiota, fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) has been widely used to treat microbiota-related disorders (Lou et al., 2022). In livestock production, it has been demonstrated to reshape the gut microbiota and improve intestinal morphology in piglets, thereby enhancing feed utilization, increasing average daily gain (ADG), and ultimately boosting overall growth performance (Hu et al., 2017; Gu et al., 2021). However, research on cross-species FMT, particularly the transplantation of fecal microbiota from large-bodied birds with superior traits (such as ostriches) to broilers, remains limited (Bornbusch et al., 2024). In this study, we innovatively established an ostrich-broiler cross-species FMT model from the perspective of modern animal microecology, which views gut microorganisms as the host’s second genome. Using this model, we first aim to investigate the potential growth-promoting effects of fecal microbiota transferred from ostrich to broilers. Building on these findings, we then sought to identify specific microbial strains responsible for the observed effects. By targeting protease-producing activity as the primary screening criterion, we isolated probiotic strains from ostrich feces. This approach addresses the issue of inadequate protein digestion in broilers, offering a novel strategy to enhance dietary protein utilization and promote green and healthy farming practices. Moreover, it provides a foundation for developing high-performance probiotic preparations.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics

All the experimental procedures applied in this study were reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Huazhong Agricultural University (Approval number: HZAUCH-2023-0031).

Cross-species fecal microbiota transplantation

Adult healthy ostriches housed at the Wuhan Jiufeng Forest Wildlife Ecological Viewing Park (n = 4, 3 males and 1 female, aged 2–3 years) were selected as donors. Fresh fecal samples were collected directly from each ostrich and pooled immediately. The fecal microbiota solution was prepared as follows: 20 g of pooled fresh feces was mixed with 200 mL of sterile saline solution (0.9% NaCl) and homogenized using a vortex mixer for 10 min. The mixture was then filtered through four layers of sterile gauze to remove large particles, and the filtrate was centrifuged at 800 × g for 5 min to collect the supernatant. The resulting fecal suspension was adjusted to a final bacterial concentration of approximately 1 × 109 CFU/mL (determined by plate counting on nutrient agar under anaerobic conditions. One-day-old yellow-feathered broilers (crossing the white-feathered broiler and Sanyang chicken) served as recipients. A total of 54 recipient broilers were randomly divided into two groups: the control group (Ctrl group) and the fecal microbiota transplantation group (FMT group). Each group had 3 replicates with 9 broilers per replicate, and all birds were raised at the Huazhong Agricultural University chicken farm. The FMT group received 1 mL of the ostrich-derived fecal microbiota solution daily by gastric gavage, while the Ctrl group received 1 mL of sterile saline solution. Throughout the trial, broilers had free access to feed and water, and their body weights were recorded weekly.

Isolation and screening of proteolytic bacteria

Protease screening medium (0.5% beef extract, 1% peptone, 0.1% NaCl, 1% skim milk powder, 1.5% agar powder) was used as the isolation medium. Ostrich feces were diluted with sterile physiological saline. A 100 μL inoculum of the bacterial suspension was spread onto prepared protease screening medium and incubated at 37°C for 24 hours. Isolate single colonies exhibiting clear zones around them. Streak each colony 2-3 times onto new plates until pure colonies are obtained. Reculture these colonies on protease screening medium and select strains with strong protease production (designated O-1 - O-14) for subsequent experiments.

Identification of strain species

Genetic identification and confirmation of the isolated strains were performed via PCR and 16S rDNA sequencing. PCR primers 27F (5′-AGAGTTTGATCCTGGCTCAG-3′) and 1492R (5′-GGTTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3′) were used to amplify the 16S rDNA gene. Following amplification, the PCR products were electrophoresed in a 1% agarose gel containing ethidium bromide and photographed using a gel imaging system. The anticipated length of the amplified fragments was 1400bp. The amplified positive PCR products were sent to Shanghai Sangong Bioengineering Co., Ltd. for sequencing and analysis. The 16S rDNA sequences were submitted to NCBI for BLAST comparison, and the top 15 homologues were selected for the 16S rDNA gene sequence analysis. The neighbor-joining method was employed to construct a bacterial phylogenetic tree using MEGA7.0 software, and the species of the strains were determined by comparing the sequences of the top 15 strains with the highest homology, which were selected for download.

Protease production capacity assay for bacterial strains

A 1 µL aliquot of the test bacterial suspension was inoculated onto a protease identification medium. The plates were incubated upside down at 37°C for 24 hours. The formation of a clear hydrolysis zone around the bacterial colony was recorded as a positive indicator of protease production. The diameters of the clear zone and the bacterial colony were measured, and the adjusted zone-to-colony ratio was calculated by dividing the former by the latter.

Cellulase production capacity assay for bacterial strains

A 1 µL aliquot of the test bacterial suspension was streaked onto plates containing sodium carboxymethylcellulose (2% CMC-Na; Sigma-Aldrich, USA) as the sole carbon source. The plates were incubated at 37°C for 24 hours. Subsequently, they were stained with a 0.1% (w/v) Congo red solution (Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 15-30 minutes and then destained with 1 M NaCl. The formation of a pale yellow hydrolysis zone around the colonies was observed and recorded, indicating cellulase secretion. The corrected zone-to-colony ratio was calculated as the diameter of the hydrolysis zone divided by the diameter of the bacterial colony.(Yang et al., 2022).

Amylase production capacity assay for bacterial strains

The test bacterial suspension (1 µL) was streaked onto an Luria-Bertani agar plate containing 1% soluble starch (Macklin, Shanghai, China). After incubation at 37°C for 24 hours, the plate was flooded with 2 mL of iodine solution (Solarbio, Beijing, China), gently swirled for even coverage, and allowed to stand for 10 minutes. A clear (decolorized) zone formed around the bacterial colony against the blue background of the starch-iodine complex was recorded as a positive indicator of amylase production. The diameters of the clear zone and the bacterial colony were measured to calculate the corrected zone-to-colony ratio.

In vitro bacteriostatic assay

The antibacterial activity of the isolated strains was determined by the agar well diffusion method, and the diameter of the inhibition zone was measured (Yadav and Tiwari, 2023). Indicator bacteria included Escherichia coli K88ac, Salmonella C78-1, and Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25923 at a concentration of 1.0 × 10⁷ CFU/mL. In a laminar flow hood, 100 µL of each indicator bacterial suspension was evenly spread onto LB agar plates. Sterilized Oxford cups were then placed on the LB plates, ensuring seamless contact with the agar surface. Each Oxford cup was filled with 200 µL of test bacterial suspension. After overnight incubation, the size of the inhibition zones was measured using a Vernier caliper.

Acid, bile salt, and high temperature resistance tests

Acid and Bile Salt Resistance Test: Bacterial cultures in the logarithmic growth phase (4.74 × 10⁸ CFU/mL) were inoculated at a 10% (v/v) ratio into fresh LB medium adjusted to pH 3.0 and LB medium supplemented with 0.3% bile salts. Viable cell counts were determined immediately after inoculation (0 h) and after 2 hours of incubation to assess acid and bile salt tolerance.

Thermotolerance Test: Bacterial cultures in the logarithmic growth phase (4.74 × 10⁸ CFU/mL) was placed in metal water baths at 40°C, 50°C, and 60°C for 5 minutes. Samples were then collected, and viable bacteria were counted.

Survival rate calculation: Survival rate = [C/C₀] × 100%. Here, C and C₀ represent the colony counts in the experimental group and Ctrl group, respectively.

Antibiotic susceptibility testing

The agar disk diffusion method was used to assess the susceptibility of bacterial strains to antibiotics (Mansour-Ghanaei et al., 2022). 12 antibiotic susceptibility discs (Meierdi, Wuhan, China.) were used, with test criteria adhering to the latest version of the NCCLS standards provided by the World Health Organization (WHO).

Virulence gene detection

Bacillus cereus AH7-7, harboring non-hemolytic enterotoxin Nhe genes (nheA, nheB, nheC) and enterotoxin FM gene (entFM), was used as a positive control strain for detecting virulence genes in target strains. Using the previously prepared target strain genomes as templates, PCR amplification was performed with specific primers for each virulence gene. Primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. Amplification conditions were as follows: 95°C or 10 min; 95°C for 15 seconds, 55°C for 15 seconds, 72°C for 40 seconds, repeated for 35 cycles; 72°C or 5 min. The primer sequences for virulence genes and the expected PCR product sizes are shown in Table S1.

Broiler feeding trial

Isolated strains (O-6) were streaked on agar plates and incubated at 37°C for 24 h. Single colonies were inoculated into 3 mL de Man, Rogosa and Sharpe broth and cultured at 37°C with shaking at 200 rpm for 12 h to obtain seed cultures, which were then transferred at 1% (v/v) into fresh de Man, Rogosa and Sharpe broth and incubated under the same conditions for 12 h to produce the fermentation broth. Cultures were centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 15 min, the supernatant discarded, and the cell pellets resuspended in PBS to prepare the bacterial suspension for broiler administration, with a viable cell density of 4.74 × 108 CFU/mL.

The feeding trial was conducted under controlled conditions at the experimental chicken farm of Huazhong Agricultural University (Wuhan, China). A total of 72 healthy one-day-old yellow-feathered broilers were randomly assigned to one of two groups using a completely randomized design, with 4 replicates of 9 birds per group. Growth performance and carcass traits

Daily feed intake of broilers was recorded throughout the trial period. Broilers were weighed at 1, 7, 14, 21, 28, and 35 days of age. ADG, average daily feed intake (ADFI), and feed conversion ratio (FCR) were calculated using the following formulas: ADG = (Final Weight - Initial Weight) / Number of Trial Days; ADFI = Feed Intake / Number of Trial Days; FCR = Total Feed Consumed (g) / Total Weight Gain (g).

At 35 days of age, remove the heaviest and lightest individuals from each group to eliminate individual variation. Randomly select 15 chickens from the remaining individuals in each group. Slaughter the broilers by cervical dislocation. Then weigh the liver, abdominal fat, breast muscle, and leg muscle. Calculate their indices using the following formulas: Hepatosomatic Index (%) = (Liver Weight / Body Weight) × 100%; Abdominal Fat Percentage (%) = (Abdominal fat weight / Body weight) × 100%; Pectoral Muscle Yield (%) = (Bilateral pectoral muscle weight / Body weight) × 100%; Thigh muscle Yield (%) = (Bilateral thigh muscle weight / Body weight) × 100%.

Immune organ index

Following dissection, the spleen, thymus, and bursa of Fabricius were weighed. The immune organ index was determined and expressed as a percentage of pre-slaughter live weight using the following formula: Immune Organ Index (%) = (Weight of Immune Organs / Pre-slaughter Live Weight) × 100%.

ELISA assay for serum immunoglobulin content and intestinal enzyme activity

ELISA assays were conducted to determine serum immunoglobulin content and intestinal digestive enzyme activities. Specifically, serum samples from slaughtered broilers were analyzed for serum immunoglobulins A, M and Y (IgA, IgM, and IgY) levels using commercial kits (Meimian, Jiangsu, China) following the manufacturer's instructions. Additionally, the activities of trypsin, α-amylase, and lipase, as well as total protein content, were quantified in duodenal, jejunal, and ileal contents using ELISA kits (ELK, Wuhan, China) according to the manufacturers' guidelines.

16S rDNA sequencing of cecal microbiota

Cecal contents (approximately 2 mL) were aseptically collected from slaughtered broilers into sterile cryogenic tubes. The samples were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80°C until DNA extraction. Microbial community analysis was performed by high-throughput sequencing of the V3-V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene on an Illumina MiSeq platform (Majorbio, Shanghai, China)

Protein content detection in cecal feces

Following collection from 35-day-old broilers, cecal content samples were processed, and their total protein content was measured using the Coomassie Brilliant Blue-based Bradford assay.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS software (version 26). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and potential outliers were identified using the Tukey method through boxplots. Data points exceeding 1.5 times the interquartile range (IQR) above the third quartile or below the first quartile were classified as outliers. Following validation, outliers were either retained or excluded based on their impact on the analysis. Results are presented as mean ± SD unless otherwise specified. For comparisons between two groups, Student’s t-test was employed for normally distributed data, while the Mann-Whitney test was utilized for non-normally distributed data. Statistical significance is indicated as follows: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001.

Detection results of virulence genes

The PCR products were electrophoresed on a 1.0% agarose gel containing ethidium bromide and visualized under a gel imaging system. The positive control (Bacillus cereus) showed clear amplification of the four target virulence genes (nheA, nheB, nheC, and entFM), with amplicon sizes matching the predicted values. In contrast, none of these enterotoxin genes were detected in strain O-6 (Fig. S2). Combined with its antibiotic susceptibility profile, these results confirm the safety of Bacillus safensis O-6 and support its potential for practical application.

The results showed that the ADFI of broilers in the O-6 group was significantly higher than that in the Ctrl group at all time points (1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 weeks of age; Fig. 2B, P < 0.05). Discussion

Large-bodied birds, such as ostriches, exhibit remarkable traits, including a rapid growth performance and strong disease resistance, which are regulated not only by their genetic makeup but are also closely associated with their gut microbiota (Videvall et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024). This study involved the transplantation of ostrich gut microbiota to broilers via FMT, resulting in a significant increase in both ADG and ADFI in the recipient broilers. These findings indicate that the superior growth performance of ostriches can be transferred across species to broilers through FMT. Based on this discovery, the present study aimed to isolate and screen probiotic strains with protease-producing capability from ostrich feces, to investigate whether their supplementation to broilers could improve protein utilization and elucidate the underlying probiotic mechanisms.

Using a protease screening medium, we initially isolated 16 protease-producing Bacillus strains from ostrich and swan feces, followed by a comprehensive evaluation of their digestive enzyme production, antimicrobial activity, and stress resistance (tolerance to acid, bile salts, and high temperatures), as well as antibiotic susceptibility and the presence of virulence genes. We ultimately selected a high-performing strain from ostrich feces, identified as Bacillus safensis O-6. This strain produces both protease and cellulase, demonstrates susceptibility to most antibiotics, lacks common Bacillus virulence genes, and exhibits robust stress resistance, thus fulfilling the prerequisite criteria for probiotic application (O’Sullivan, 2001).

The growth-promoting effect of O-6 was evidenced by significant increases in ADG (7.16%) and ADFI (25.34%) in 5-week-old broilers. This improvement in performance is likely attributed to enhanced digestive capacity(Zhang et al., 2020). In support of this hypothesis, O-6 supplementation significantly elevated the activities of protease, amylase, and lipase in the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum. Furthermore, a concurrent 28.23% reduction in cecal protein content suggests that O-6, possibly through its promotion of in-situ protease secretion, facilitates more efficient protein degradation and absorption in the upper gastrointestinal tract, thus improving feed nutrient utilization (Gong et al., 2018). However, despite these improvements in enzyme activities, FCR in the O-6 group was transiently higher at 2 and 3 weeks of age. This apparent paradox may be explained by the disproportionate increase in feed intake relative to the maturation of digestive functions. During the 2-3 weeks period, ADFI in the O-6 group increased substantially more than the concomitant enhancement in digestive and absorptive capacity. In young broilers, intestinal maturation may not occur rapidly enough to accommodate a sharp, probiotic-induced increase in feed intake (Ravindran and Abdollahi, 2021). As a result, some nutrients may pass through the gut without complete absorption, leading to a temporary increase in FCR. By weeks 4 and 5, as the gut matured and digestive enzyme activities reached higher levels, the O-6 group effectively converted the increased feed intake into weight gain, resulting in a significant ADG advantage without a sustained FCR penalty. This interpretation is supported by the finding that, over the entire 5-week period, there was no significant difference in overall FCR between groups, indicating that the early inefficiency was a transient adaptation rather than a persistent digestive impairment. This finding is consistent with previous reports on other Bacillus strains, such as B. amyloliquefaciens CECT 5940, which significantly increased amylase, lipase, and chymotrypsin activities in the jejunum (Sun et al., 2021), and Bacillus licheniformis BCG, which increased the activities of α-amylase, maltase, and sucrase in the jejunum and ileum (Han et al., 2023). Collectively, these findings suggest that the augmentation of host digestive enzyme activities by exogenous probiotics is a key mechanism underlying improved growth performance in poultry, although transient mismatches between feed intake and digestive maturation should be considered when evaluating early‑phase FCR.

The cecal content of broilers harbors a rich and complex microbial community, which plays a crucial role in maintaining intestinal homeostasis and promoting host growth (Star-Shirko et al., 2025). Probiotic interventions are known to modulate host health by reshaping the gut microbial community (Schepper et al., 2019; Navale et al., 2025). In this study, O-6 supplementation did not alter the alpha diversity indices of richness (Ace, Chao) but significantly reduced the Simpson index while increasing the Shannoneven index, indicating a more even and less dominant community structure. Microbial ecosystems with high evenness are generally considered more stable and resilient against perturbations, such as pathogen invasion or dietary shifts, thereby contributing to more consistent host digestive and metabolic functions (Ha et al., 2014). Beta diversity analysis further confirmed a distinct clustering of the cecal microbiota in the O-6 group, demonstrating a significant shift in overall community composition. At the phylum level, Firmicutes and Bacteroidota were the dominant phyla in the cecal microbiota, both closely associated with energy harvest and nutrient metabolism (Pinnell et al., 2022). O-6 treatment increased the relative abundance of Firmicutes (+3.89%) and decreased Bacteroidota (-4.93%). This shift was largely driven by changes at the genus level, particularly the enrichment of beneficial taxa such as Lactobacillus and Faecalibacterium in the O-6 group. The increase in Faecalibacterium, a keystone butyrate producer, is particularly noteworthy(Machiels et al., 2014). It offers a direct mechanistic link to the elevated SCFA concentrations observed, as butyrate and other SCFAs are crucial for maintaining intestinal barrier function and providing energy to colonocytes (Salvi and Cowles, 2021). Furthermore, SCFAs, particularly butyrate, are known to stimulate appetite, which may contribute to increased feed intake. Studies have shown that SCFAs, especially butyrate, can enhance the secretion of hunger-related hormones and improve overall gut motility, which could, in turn, lead to an increase in ADFI in broilers (Wang et al., 2026). This aligns with the findings of the current study, where the ADFI of broilers in the O-6 group was consistently significantly higher than that of the Ctrl group. Additionally, O-6 significantly increased the relative abundances of beneficial bacteria such as Rikenellaceae and Alistipes, which may play key roles in promoting nutrient absorption, disease prevention, and host health maintenance (Bhogoju and Nahashon, 2022). Although O-6 itself does not produce amylase or lipase, it can enhance the activities of these enzymes in vivo by modulating the gut microbiota. Specifically, the increased abundance of Lactobacillus, a genus known to possess both amylase and lipase activities, contributes to the elevated enzymatic activities observed (Abdul Hakim et al., 2023). In addition, other unclassified amylase- and lipase-producing bacteria may also be enriched following O-6 administration, collectively leading to improved starch and lipid digestion.

The thymus, bursa of Fabricius, and spleen are essential immune organs in poultry, and their indices serve as indicators of the host’s immune status (Kumar, 2021). IgY, IgM, and IgA are the primary antibodies responsible for mediating humoral immunity, and their serum levels reflected the capacity for disease resistance (Han et al., 2017).Analysis of immune parameters revealed that O-6 supplementation significantly increased the indices of the spleen and bursa of Fabricius, as well as elevated serum IgM and IgA levels in 35-day-old broilers. These findings indicate that O-6 activates the humoral immune response and enhances overall immune function in broilers. This immunomodulatory effect is likely linked to the shifts in gut microbiota composition discussed above. The proliferation of beneficial microbes, potentially including the enriched Lactobacillus and Faecalibacterium populations, can provide continuous antigenic stimulation to the gut-associated lymphoid tissue, thereby promoting the development and maturation of systemic immune organs (Rawal and Ali, 2023). This crosstalk between the remodeled gut microbiota and the host immune system underscores the holistic benefits of O-6 supplementation.

In summary, Bacillus safensis O-6, isolated from ostrich feces, functions as a multi-faceted probiotic for broilers. Its primary mode of action involves direct protease secretion, which augments host digestive enzyme activity, leading to improved protein utilization and enhanced growth performance. Concurrently, O-6 beneficially modulates the cecal microbiota by increasing community evenness and enriching populations of SCFA-producing genera such as Faecalibacterium, which is associated with elevated SCFA levels and enhanced systemic immunity. While these findings highlight the potential of O-6 for improving poultry health and growth, its low heat resistance (survival rate of only 2.56% at 70°C for 5 min) limits its application in standard pelleted feed processing, representing an important consideration for commercial development. However, this limitation may be addressed by exploring the probiotic effects of its heat-inactivated cells, which belong to the category of postbiotics. We intend to further investigate the heat-inactivated form of strain O-6 in future studies to compensate for the heat sensitivity of its live cells. In conclusion, this study provides evidence that cross-species transfer of beneficial traits from ostriches to broilers via probiotic intervention is feasible, thus opening new avenues for the development of novel and sustainable feed additives.
