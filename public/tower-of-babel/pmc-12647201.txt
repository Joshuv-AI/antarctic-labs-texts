Editorial: Cerebrospinal fluid flow imaging in Chiari Malformation type 1
Authors: Victor E Staartjes, Erik Öhlén, Anders Lilja, Erik Edström, Adrian Elmi-Terander
Published: 2025

In their recent study “Prognostic value of the preoperative study of cerebrospinal fluid dynamics in Chiari malformations: a pilot study”, Carlier and colleagues [3] report on their experiences with phase-contrast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) for characterization of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) dynamics among adult patients who improved and those who did not improve in terms of headache after posterior fossa decompression.

With their work, the authors touch upon a clinically relevant topic in the treatment of adult Chiari Malformation Type 1 (CM1): which patients are likely to profit from decompression? As with any kind of pain surgery, there is a disconnect between the indication for surgery and the chances of successful symptom relief with surgery. Posterior fossa decompression (PFD) is indicated and generally efficacious in symptomatic patients, but not all patients improve after decompression [8, 18]. Between 57 and 93% of symptomatic patients report a favourable outcome after decompression, either in reduced headache severity or frequency, or according to validated disease-specific metrics [8, 10, 18].

Patients with classical CM1 symptoms tend to respond well to PFD. These symptoms include typical headaches, which according to the International Headache Society are distinguished by brief and severe episodes of often occipital headaches, triggered by Valsalva or effort [6, 8, 13]. Clinical decision making is much more complicated in patients with radiological CM1-like crowding in the foramen magnum but atypical headaches or neurological symptoms. How can one objectively inform this patient population about their chances of success?

This is the question that Carlier et al. [3] aim to tackle with imaging: objectively rating disease severity based on CSF flow alterations. It has long been known that patients with CM1 exhibit higher CSF flow velocities through the foramen-magnum than control subjects [7]. On the other hand, we also know that there are barely any differences in these foramen magnum flows between symptomatic and asymptomatic CM1 patients [11]. Flow studies have proven ineffective in measuring disease severity in CM1. Nonetheless, it has been shown that increased CSF stroke volumes over the aqueduct correlate with good outcomes after surgery [4, 14].

Carlier et al. [3] apply phase-contrast MRI, which has been commonly available since the 1990s. Moving CSF interacts differently with bipolar gradients than stationary tissue, producing a phase shift that varies through the cardiac cycle and can be turned into velocity and flow waveforms. After subtracting the phases to cancel out the stationary tissue signal, the remaining phase shows how fast the CSF is moving. With cardiac gating and a slice placed perpendicular to e.g. the aqueduct, one can assess stroke volumes and flow velocities during the cardiac cycle (mean/peak systolic–diastolic flow/velocity).

As subsets of phase-contrast imaging, several other adaptations of the technique exist, such as 4D, pencil beam imaging (PBI), and displacement encoding with stimulated echoes (DENSE) imaging. 4D flow MRI extends phase-contrast into a full 3D + time acquisition, allowing visualization of complex CSF jets and recirculation across the foramen magnum and aqueduct, albeit with longer scans and heavier post-processing [15]. PBI focuses a narrow excitation along the cranio-cervical canal to capture CSF velocities with exceptional temporal resolution (but a smaller surface area captured), making it uniquely sensitive to transient phenomena such as cough, valsalva, and respiration, though at the cost of limited spatial context [2]. DENSE complements flow imaging by encoding very tiny hindbrain and tonsillar motions into phase, yielding quantitative maps of tissue displacement and strain that reflect the biomechanical consequences of altered CSF dynamics, but does not image CSF flow directly [5].

Carlier et al. [3] state that phase-contrast MRI is the only non-invasive way to show CSF dynamics, but this is not entirely true. Another method with several advantages over phase contrast imaging, namely CSF time-spatial labelling inversion pulse MRI (T-SLIP MRI), has been successfully used to achieve the same goal [20]. Phase-contrast MRI, while established, can have a low signal-to-noise ratio and often needs averaging and careful parameter tuning, which limits routine use. In contrast, T-SLIP MRI provides a higher intrinsic signal-to-noise ratio and robust qualitative visualization of CSF flow. The intuition behind T-SLIP is that a bolus of CSF is tagged (spin-labelled) and watched over a few seconds. This allows visualization of how CSF moves between compartments, including linear and turbulent components, independent of the heartbeat. Technically, T-SLIP tags a chosen CSF region with an inversion pulse, akin to arterial spin labelling.

There are only a few studies such as the one by Carlier et al. that evaluate the predictive value of preoperative imaging. Papaker et al. [14] assessed changes before and after surgery in CSF flow volumes over the aqueduct and found that postoperative increased volumes correlated to better outcomes. However, they did not analyze whether preoperative measurements per se were predictive of outcome, making their findings useless in surgical decision-making. To their benefit, Carlier et al. [3] consider other regions for measurement than the more commonly investigated aqueduct or foramen magnum. A classic study by Armonda et al. [1] from 1994 did consider measurements of peak flow velocities at the C2 level, but did not look at outcome prediction. To the best of our knowledge, McGirt et al. [12] is the only other study that systematically used phase-contrast MRI to look at predictive effects of preoperative flow measurements. They measured at the aqueduct, fourth ventricle, foramen magnum, and the upper cervical canal and classified CSF flow as abnormal if biphasic flow was absent at any position, finding that a normal CSF flow was independently predictive of poor outcome, and consequently that an abnormal CSF flow was predictive of good surgical outcome.

Carlier et al. [3] include 41 patients, of which 50% were treated with PFD and 50% received PFD plus duraplasty. Of these, 27 patients presented with typical headaches, while 14 patients exhibited atypical headaches. They did not focus on peak flow velocities as most studies do, but instead on cardiac-gated stroke volumes, corresponding to the integral of the flow velocity curves. The stroke volumes were measured at five different positions: the mesencephalic aqueduct, the cervical subarachnoid spaces at the C2-C3 level, the prepontine cistern, the foramen magnum, and the cerebellar tonsils adjacent to the foramen magnum. They compare stroke volumes for each position in patients who improved in terms of headache severity. Their findings indicate that an increased preoperative stroke volume across the aqueduct and a decreased C2-C3-level perimedullary stroke volume are observed in patients reporting improvements in headache severity. Regarding increased stroke volumes through the aqueduct the authors speculate on the recruitment of ventricular compliance to compensate for inhibited hindbrain CSF flow. Furthermore, the decreased C2-C3-level stroke volume is suggested to reflect decreased trans-foramen flow and decreased recruitment of compliance of extracranial CSF spaces. The authors also note that, as shown before, morphological factors such as aqueductal area and Evans index have no predictive value.

While the results appear plausible, they represent few data points and an effect size that is barely significant. As such, the findings cannot support the routine use of flow imaging studies for surgical decision-making in CM1. Moreover, there are important limitations that limit generalizability of the findings. These limitations include patient selection, defining the outcome predictor and the validity of the flow measures.

Regarding patient selection, the cohort included is a mix of typical and atypical CM1-headache patients. Outcomes after PFD are excellent when the indication is effort induced headaches, which are typical for symptomatic CM1 [8, 18]. Conversely, PFD should not be performed for other headaches since the outcomes are poor. As the authors themselves state, atypical headaches are an independent predictor of poor outcome, and in this study 33% of patients in the improved group had atypical headaches versus 65% in the not-improved group. In addition, headaches are not assessed using a validated instrument.

Regarding the definition of the predictive value itself, the authors report their results differently from what is expected for predictive tests. They compare stroke volumes for two classes of outcome rather than comparing outcomes for two classes of stroke volumes. Ordinarily, for a binary outcome, one would search for an optimal cutoff value to dichotomize the predictor, which in this case is stroke volume [17]. For example, the authors could have chosen a stroke volume across the aqueduct exceeding 100 µL per cardiac cycle as relevantly increased. Then, a binary outcome such as a validated minimum clinically important difference (MCID) for a headache inventory or for the Chicago Chiari Outcome Scale (CCOS) would need to be reported and compared considering the chosen cutoff. There are several ways to choose an optimal cutoff, depending on the clinical situation [17]. That discussion however, is outside the scope of this text. Moreover, standard reporting of predictive ability using a minimum of area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, and specificity should be considered.

Finally, the literature disagrees on whether these flow imaging techniques and the associated measurements themselves are reproducible and accurate [16, 19]. Thus, the use of multiple raters at multiple centers could improve the generalizability of the findings. Even a simple study on inter-rater agreement of these measurements could be highly important to validate the use of flow imaging techniques in this context. Since it has been shown that not only velocities and stroke volumes, but also the presence of more turbulent and heterogenous flow, are important, the inclusion of other techniques such as 4D phase contrast MRI could increase the predictive value [9].

We congratulate our colleagues on rekindling the interest in this potentially useful imaging technique. The presented limitations are certainly all addressable in future studies. However, since more than 90% of patients with classical symptoms and radiological findings benefit from PDF [10], a future role for flow imaging studies may be to identify patients otherwise denied surgical treatment. Prospective studies on larger populations with well-defined preoperative clinical parameters and postoperative outcome metrics are needed to elucidate the role of flow imaging techniques in CM1 decision-making.
