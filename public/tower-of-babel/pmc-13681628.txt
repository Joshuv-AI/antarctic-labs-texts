Vitamin D deficiency and oral health in the Middle East and North Africa (MENA): A public health perspective
Authors: Najat Alyafei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTo examine evidence regarding the relationship between vitamin D deficiency and oral health in Middle East and North Africa (MENA) populations, where vitamin D deficiency and oral diseases remain important public health challenges, emphasizing biological mechanisms, dental caries, periodontal diseases, other oral outcomes, and public health implications.MethodsThis narrative review synthesizes systematic reviews, meta-analyses, randomized controlled trials, observational studies, and regional public health reports identified in electronic databases and through reference screening, focusing on vitamin D deficiency, oral health outcomes, biological mechanisms, and preventive strategies relevant to MENA populations.ResultsEvidence suggests that vitamin D contributes to oral health through tooth mineralization, calcium and phosphate homeostasis, immune regulation, antimicrobial defense, inflammation control, and alveolar bone maintenance. Low vitamin D status has been associated with dental caries, periodontal diseases, developmental enamel defects, alveolar bone loss, impaired wound healing, and oral mucosal conditions. Evidence regarding oral cancer remains insufficient to establish a relationship. Periodontal disease shares inflammatory and metabolic pathways with systemic conditions, particularly diabetes, and emerging evidence suggests a possible association with cognitive decline and dementia. However, evidence remains heterogeneous and predominantly observational; therefore, causality has not been established. The coexistence of vitamin D deficiency and a high oral disease burden in MENA supports integrated public health strategies based on shared risk factors and the Common Risk Factor Approach.ConclusionVitamin D deficiency should be considered a potentially modifiable component of oral health promotion rather than a standalone cause or treatment of oral diseases. Integrating nutritional strategies with established oral disease prevention programs may strengthen public health efforts throughout the MENA. Longitudinal studies and randomized controlled trials are needed to clarify causal relationships and guide evidence-based policy and clinical practice.

Introduction

Vitamin D is a fat-soluble secosteroid hormone with fundamental roles in calcium and phosphate homeostasis, skeletal development, immune regulation, and overall health. The primary source of vitamin D is via synthesis in the skin following exposure to ultraviolet B (UVB) radiation from sunlight, after which it undergoes sequential hydroxylation in the liver and kidneys to form the biologically active metabolite. Cutaneous synthesis depends on adequate UVB exposure, and thus factors such as limited outdoor activity, extensive skin covering, darker skin pigmentation, aging, obesity, and environmental conditions may substantially reduce vitamin D production despite abundant sunshine.1,2

These factors are particularly important in the Middle East and North Africa (MENA), where vitamin D deficiency remains highly prevalent despite year-round sunlight. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis of 594,712 individuals reported substantial between-country variability in serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25(OH)D]) concentrations, ranging from 9 ± 7 to 88 ± 76 nmol/L, with a pooled mean concentration of 46 nmol/L (95% confidence interval (CI): 40–52 nmol/L), which is below the commonly accepted sufficiency threshold of 50 nmol/L (20 ng/mL). The analysis also demonstrated that the mean serum 25(OH)D concentrations were approximately 4 nmol/L lower in women than men.3 The coexistence of abundant solar radiation and widespread vitamin D deficiency represents an important public health paradox that warrants particular attention.

Vitamin D exerts its biological effects through the vitamin D receptor, which is expressed in osteoblasts, odontoblasts, periodontal ligament cells, epithelial tissues, and immune cells. Consequently, vitamin D contributes to tooth mineralization, alveolar bone remodeling, immune regulation, antimicrobial defense, and maintenance of oral tissue homeostasis1,4 Therefore, it has been proposed that vitamin D deficiency is a potentially modifiable factor influencing susceptibility to several oral diseases.

Similarly, oral diseases remain a major public health challenge throughout the MENA region. Regional reviews have highlighted the substantial and persistent burden of oral diseases among Arab, African, and Middle Eastern populations, including dental caries, periodontal diseases, tooth loss, and other oral conditions. Important inequalities in disease burden and access to oral healthcare have also been reported, but reliable epidemiological data remain limited in several countries throughout the region.5,6

High proportions of children experience dental caries throughout the region. In Qatar, analysis of weighted data from the 2017 Qatar Child Oral Health Survey showed that 69.3% of children aged 4–8 years had experienced dental caries in the primary dentition (95% CI: 63.4–74.5), with a mean caries experience index of 3.8 dmft (95% CI: 3.3–4.2).7 A similarly high prevalence has also been reported among young children in the United Arab Emirates and KSA.8,9

Periodontal diseases are also highly prevalent and contribute substantially to tooth loss, impaired quality of life, and increased healthcare burden, while sharing important risk factors with several chronic systemic diseases, including diabetes and cardiovascular disease.1

Growing evidence suggests an association between vitamin D deficiency and oral health outcomes, including dental caries, periodontal diseases, alveolar bone loss, impaired wound healing, and developmental enamel defects. However, current evidence remains inconsistent because many studies are observational and they differ in terms of the study design, population characteristics, adjustment for confounding variables, and methods used to assess vitamin D status.10,11 Furthermore, specific evidence for MENA populations remains relatively limited despite the coexistence of widespread vitamin D deficiency and a substantial burden of oral diseases.

To the best of our knowledge, this narrative review is among the first to specifically examine vitamin D deficiency and oral health from a public health perspective within the MENA region. In contrast to previous reviews that primarily focused on biological mechanisms or global evidence, this review emphasizes the unique epidemiological context of MENA populations, shared risk factors, biological pathways, oral health outcomes, and opportunities for integrated prevention, health promotion, and policy development.

Materials and Methods

This narrative review was developed based on a targeted literature search of peer-reviewed publications and relevant public health reports. Evidence was identified in major electronic databases, including PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar, as well as reports from international health organizations. The search terms included combinations of “vitamin D deficiency,” “oral health,” “dental caries,” “periodontal disease,” “matrix metalloproteinases,” “oral mucosal diseases,” “oral cancer,” “diabetes,” “dementia,” “cognitive decline,” “Middle East,” “Gulf Cooperation Council,” and “MENA.” Priority was given to systematic reviews, meta-analyses, randomized controlled trials, observational studies, and regional public health publications. Additional references were identified by manually screening reference lists. The aims of this review were to synthesize current evidence regarding biological mechanisms, oral health outcomes, and public health implications relevant to MENA populations.

Burden and epidemiology

Vitamin D deficiency in the Middle East

Vitamin D deficiency is one of the most prevalent nutritional disorders across the MENA, affecting children, adolescents, adults, pregnant women, and older adults. Despite considerable geographic and socioeconomic diversity, systematic evidence demonstrates that vitamin D deficiency remains highly prevalent throughout the region, although its prevalence varies among countries and population groups.3

Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) countries consistently report a high prevalence of vitamin D deficiency, with substantial variation among countries and population groups.3,12,13 Similar patterns have also been reported in Levant countries and across North Africa, demonstrating that vitamin D deficiency is a regional public health concern rather than an isolated national problem.3,14

In addition to its established effects on skeletal health, vitamin D deficiency has been associated with osteoporosis, metabolic disorders, cardiovascular disease, immune dysfunction, adverse pregnancy outcomes, and potentially several oral diseases, including dental caries, periodontal diseases, impaired wound healing, and alveolar bone loss.1,11

Contributing factors

The persistently high prevalence of vitamin D deficiency in MENA populations reflects interactions among environmental, behavioral, cultural, and biological factors rather than inadequate availability of sunlight alone. The region receives abundant solar radiation throughout the year, but opportunities for effective cutaneous vitamin D synthesis are often reduced by limited outdoor activities, predominantly indoor occupations, urban lifestyles, and avoidance of direct sunlight because of extreme climatic conditions.3

Cultural clothing practices that limit skin exposure may further reduce UVB-induced vitamin D synthesis, particularly among women, who consistently exhibit lower serum vitamin D concentrations than men in many MENA populations.3 In addition, darker skin pigmentation reduces the efficiency of vitamin D production because a higher melanin content decreases the penetration of UVB into the skin.

Dietary intake also contributes to deficiency because relatively few foods naturally contain vitamin D and food-fortification policies differ considerably among countries. Rising obesity rates further aggravate deficiency because vitamin D becomes sequestered within adipose tissue, reducing its bioavailability.3 Other high-risk groups include pregnant women, older adults, and individuals with chronic diseases, who may have increased physiological requirements or a reduced capacity for vitamin D synthesis.

Together, these interacting factors explain why vitamin D deficiency remains highly prevalent throughout the MENA region despite year-round sunshine, as well as highlighting the need for coordinated prevention strategies. Biological role of vitamin D in oral health

Vitamin D influences oral health through multiple interconnected biological pathways involving tooth development, mineral metabolism, immune regulation, antimicrobial activity, inflammation control, and alveolar bone remodeling. Tooth development and mineralization

Vitamin D is essential for normal tooth development because it regulates the availability of calcium and phosphate required for enamel and dentin mineralization, while also supporting the functions of ameloblasts and odontoblasts.4 Deficiency during fetal development or childhood may impair mineralization, resulting in enamel defects, dentin abnormalities, delayed tooth eruption, and increased susceptibility to dental caries.1 Maternal vitamin D deficiency has also been associated with developmental defects that affect the primary dentition and an increased risk of early childhood caries.1

Immune regulation and oral inflammation

Vitamin D contributes to oral immune homeostasis by stimulating antimicrobial peptides, including cathelicidin and β-defensins, as well as modulating inflammatory responses involved in periodontal tissue integrity.4 It suppresses pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6, and promotes anti-inflammatory pathways that support tissue repair.1 Consequently, an inadequate vitamin D status may impair host defense mechanisms and increase susceptibility to periodontal inflammation. Several studies have reported poorer periodontal status and higher inflammatory marker levels among individuals with lower serum vitamin D concentrations.10

Alveolar bone remodeling

Adequate vitamin D status supports alveolar bone remodeling through its effects on calcium homeostasis, osteoblast function, and bone turnover.1 Vitamin D deficiency has been associated with reduced bone density, greater alveolar bone loss, and periodontal destruction.4 Lower circulating vitamin D concentrations have also been reported among individuals with more severe periodontal breakdown.10 Furthermore, adequate vitamin D may contribute to improved healing following periodontal therapy, tooth extraction, and implant placement by supporting bone regeneration and regulating inflammatory responses.1

These biological mechanisms provide biologically plausible explanations for the observed associations between vitamin D deficiency and oral diseases. However, these mechanisms alone do not establish causality, and clinical outcomes are influenced by numerous additional biological, behavioral, and environmental factors.10,11

Vitamin D and dental caries

Current evidence

Dental caries remains the most prevalent oral disease in children and is influenced by multiple biological, behavioral, environmental, and socioeconomic determinants. Established risk factors including frequent sugar consumption, inadequate fluoride exposure, poor oral hygiene, and limited access to preventive care remain the principal drivers of disease, but increasing attention has been focused on the potential contribution of vitamin D deficiency.1,11

Vitamin D may influence the susceptibility to caries by supporting enamel and dentin mineralization during tooth development, maintaining the calcium and phosphate balance, and enhancing innate immune defense through the production of antimicrobial peptides.1,4 These biological mechanisms provide plausible explanations for the reported association between inadequate vitamin D status and increased caries risk.

Several observational studies have demonstrated higher DMFT/dmft scores among individuals with lower serum vitamin D concentrations.11 Maternal vitamin D deficiency has also been associated with developmental enamel defects and an increased risk of early childhood caries in offspring.15 However, the available evidence remains inconsistent. In many studies, the observed associations were substantially attenuated after adjusting for dietary habits, fluoride exposure, oral hygiene practices, socioeconomic status, parental education, and access to dental care. Furthermore, the predominance of cross-sectional study designs limits causal inference.

Historical evidence for a possible role of vitamin D in dental caries predates much of the contemporary observational literature. Studies conducted during the early twentieth century investigated vitamin D supplementation and UVB exposure in relation to caries prevention. A systematic review and meta-analysis of controlled clinical trials found that vitamin D supplementation was associated with reduced caries incidence, although the trials included were conducted several decades ago and varied considerably in terms of their design and methodological quality. A review of historical epidemiological and experimental evidence similarly suggested that greater solar UVB exposure and vitamin D availability may be associated with lower caries risk. These early findings provide a historical context for contemporary research but should be interpreted cautiously in light of modern standards for clinical trial design and the multifactorial etiology of dental caries.16,17

Accordingly, current evidence suggests that vitamin D deficiency should be regarded as a potentially modifiable contributor rather than an independent cause of dental caries. Maintaining an adequate vitamin D status may complement but cannot replace established preventive measures, such as fluoride exposure, sugar reduction, effective oral hygiene, and regular dental care.

Public health implications for the Middle East

The coexistence of widespread vitamin D deficiency and a high prevalence of childhood dental caries in MENA countries presents an opportunity for integrated public health interventions. Instead of considering these conditions independently, prevention strategies should address their shared determinants through coordinated nutrition and oral health promotion initiatives.

Maternal and child health services can incorporate education regarding vitamin D, nutrition, and oral health, while school-based programs provide opportunities to promote healthy dietary habits, outdoor physical activity, oral hygiene, and appropriate fluoride use. Primary healthcare systems may further strengthen prevention through nutritional assessment, oral health counseling, and risk-based preventive services.

Importantly, vitamin D deficiency and dental caries are influenced by several overlapping behavioral, nutritional, and socioeconomic determinants. Addressing these shared determinants through integrated prevention strategies is consistent with the Common Risk Factor Approach, which emphasizes targeting risk factors that are common to oral and other chronic diseases.18

Vitamin D and periodontal diseases

Current clinical evidence

Periodontal diseases are chronic inflammatory conditions characterized by progressive destruction of the supporting tissues of the teeth, and they remain a major cause of tooth loss worldwide. Vitamin D has recognized roles in immune regulation, inflammatory modulation, and bone metabolism, so considerable attention has been focused on its potential effects on periodontal health.1,4

Observational studies have consistently reported lower serum vitamin D concentrations among individuals with periodontitis compared with periodontally healthy controls.10 Lower vitamin D status has also been associated with greater probing pocket depth, increased clinical attachment loss, bleeding on probing, and radiographic evidence of alveolar bone loss. These findings suggest that inadequate vitamin D status may be associated with more severe periodontal disease.

Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) also play important roles in periodontal tissue destruction. In particular, MMP-8, MMP-9, and MMP-13 are involved in extracellular matrix degradation and collagen breakdown, and have been implicated in periodontal and peri-implant pathological processes. Their activities reflect the complex interactions between microbial challenge and the host inflammatory response. These pathways provide additional biological context for understanding periodontal tissue destruction and the potential relevance of factors, including vitamin D, which modulate host immune and inflammatory responses.19

However, periodontal disease is multifactorial, where its development and progression are influenced by microbial biofilm, smoking, diabetes mellitus, aging, oral hygiene practices, obesity, genetic susceptibility, socioeconomic conditions, and access to dental care. Consequently, vitamin D deficiency should be considered as one of several potentially modifiable factors rather than as an independent determinant of periodontitis.10,11

Vitamin D supplementation and periodontal therapy

Several clinical studies have investigated whether vitamin D supplementation improves periodontal treatment outcomes. Randomized and controlled studies suggest that supplementation may reduce selected inflammatory markers and provide modest improvements in some periodontal clinical parameters, particularly among individuals with documented vitamin D deficiency.20,21

However, current evidence remains limited by relatively small sample sizes, heterogeneous study designs, differences in supplementation protocols, and variations in outcome measures. Therefore, vitamin D supplementation cannot currently be recommended as a standalone therapy for periodontal disease, but instead maintaining an adequate vitamin D status should be considered a supportive component of comprehensive periodontal care alongside established mechanical and preventive therapies.

Public health implications

From a public health perspective, the coexistence of widespread vitamin D deficiency and periodontal diseases in MENA countries supports the integration of nutritional and oral health strategies within primary healthcare systems. Risk assessment, nutritional counseling, oral health education, and preventive dental services may be delivered in a coordinated manner, particularly for high-risk populations such as individuals with diabetes, older adults, and people with obesity.

Vitamin D deficiency and periodontal diseases share several common determinants, including unhealthy lifestyles, obesity, diabetes, socioeconomic disadvantages, and limited health literacy, so integrated prevention strategies may help address overlapping risks. Vitamin D and other oral health outcomes

Dental implant outcomes

Successful implant therapy depends on adequate osseointegration, where this process is influenced by bone metabolism and host immune responses. Vitamin D contributes to bone remodeling and inflammatory regulation, so its potential role in implant outcomes has received increasing attention.1,4 Experimental studies suggest that vitamin D may support osseointegration, and several clinical studies have reported higher rates of early implant failure among individuals with severe vitamin D deficiency.1 However, the findings remain inconsistent, and current evidence is insufficient to recommend routine vitamin D supplementation solely to improve implant success.

Oral wound healing

Vitamin D may contribute to oral wound healing by regulating inflammatory responses, promoting epithelial regeneration, supporting collagen synthesis, and enhancing antimicrobial defense mechanisms.4 Vitamin D deficiency has been associated with delayed healing following tooth extraction, periodontal surgery, and implant placement. However, the available evidence remains limited and largely observational, highlighting the need for well-designed prospective and interventional studies.

Oral mucosal diseases

Vitamin D has also been investigated in several oral mucosal disorders because of its immunomodulatory properties. Lower serum vitamin D concentrations have been reported among patients with oral lichen planus, and some randomized clinical studies suggest that vitamin D supplementation may improve clinical symptoms when used as an adjunct to conventional therapy.22 Emerging evidence also suggests a possible association between vitamin D deficiency and oral candidiasis, although additional research is required before definitive conclusions can be made.23

Developmental enamel defects

Vitamin D plays an important role during prenatal and early childhood tooth development. Maternal vitamin D deficiency has been associated with developmental enamel defects, including enamel hypoplasia and hypomineralization, which may increase the susceptibility to dental caries.1,15 Primary tooth mineralization begins during fetal development, so maintaining an adequate maternal vitamin D status may contribute to optimal dental development. However, the current evidence remains heterogeneous, and further longitudinal studies are needed to clarify this relationship.

Vitamin D and oral cancer

Vitamin D has also been investigated in relation to oral carcinogenesis due to its roles in immune regulation, inflammation, cellular differentiation, proliferation, and apoptosis. However, evidence specifically linking vitamin D deficiency to oral cancer remains limited and substantially less developed than the evidence relating vitamin D to dental caries or periodontal disease. A recent systematic review evaluated vitamin D deficiency in relation to dental caries, periodontitis, and oral cancer, and found insufficient evidence to establish a clear relationship between vitamin D deficiency and oral cancer, highlighting the limited number of available studies. Therefore, vitamin D deficiency should not currently be considered as an established causal or independent risk factor for oral cancer. Further longitudinal and mechanistic studies, including research in MENA populations, are needed to clarify whether vitamin D status has a clinically meaningful role in oral carcinogenesis.24

Integration of oral and systemic health

Vitamin D deficiency and oral diseases occur within a broader network of chronic conditions and shared biological and behavioral risk factors. Diabetes mellitus is particularly relevant because periodontal disease is a common oral complication associated with diabetes, while poor glycemic control may increase the susceptibility to periodontal inflammation and tissue destruction. Other oral manifestations reported among individuals with diabetes include xerostomia, dental caries, oral infections, delayed wound healing, and mucosal changes.25

Vitamin D deficiency has also been associated with several chronic conditions that are prevalent in MENA populations, including obesity, cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and osteoporosis. These conditions share inflammatory, immune, metabolic, nutritional, and lifestyle pathways with adverse oral health outcomes. Therefore, vitamin D may represent one component within a broader network connecting nutritional status, chronic disease, and oral health rather than being an isolated determinant of oral disease. This perspective supports integrated approaches to prevention that are consistent with the Common Risk Factor Approach.

Emerging evidence also suggests an association between periodontal disease and cognitive decline or dementia. The proposed mechanisms include systemic inflammation and the dissemination of periodontal pathogens or their products beyond the oral cavity. However, causality remains uncertain because of heterogeneity among studies, residual confounding, and the possibility of reverse causation, as cognitive impairment itself may adversely affect oral hygiene and access to dental care. Evidence addressing this relationship specifically in Middle Eastern populations remains limited, and thus this is an important area for future regional research.26

Why MENA is a unique context

The MENA region is a unique epidemiological setting where widespread vitamin D deficiency coexists with a substantial burden of oral diseases despite abundant year-round sunlight. This paradox reflects the interactions among the environmental, behavioral, cultural, nutritional, and socioeconomic factors discussed in this review. Understanding these regional characteristics is essential for developing culturally appropriate prevention strategies and for generating region-specific evidence to guide future research and public health policy.

Public health implications for the MENA

Vitamin D deficiency and oral diseases represent significant public health challenges throughout the MENA.3,14,27 The coexistence of these conditions presents an opportunity to integrate nutritional and oral health strategies within existing healthcare systems. A causal relationship between vitamin D deficiency and oral diseases has not been established, but maintaining an adequate vitamin D status may contribute to improved oral health as part of a comprehensive prevention strategy.1,4,11

Given the high prevalence of vitamin D deficiency throughout the MENA region, public health initiatives should promote safe sunlight exposure, balanced nutrition, appropriate vitamin D supplementation for individuals at risk of deficiency, and healthy lifestyle behaviors.3,14 These measures should complement established oral disease prevention strategies, including twice-daily toothbrushing with fluoride toothpaste, dietary sugar reduction, regular dental examinations, and community-based preventive programs. Primary healthcare systems provide an important platform for integrating nutritional assessment with oral health promotion. Healthcare professionals should be aware of the potential relationship between vitamin D status and oral health when providing comprehensive preventive care, particularly for high-risk groups such as children, pregnant women, older adults, and individuals with chronic conditions, including diabetes and obesity.1,4,11 Strengthening collaboration between physicians, dentists, dietitians, and other healthcare professionals may further support integrated preventive care.

School-based oral health programs also provide valuable opportunities for integrating oral health education with broader health promotion initiatives. Educational interventions that encourage healthy nutrition, outdoor physical activity, appropriate oral hygiene practices, and preventive dental care may contribute to improvements in both the general and oral health of children.

Despite growing interest in the relationship between vitamin D and oral health, important evidence gaps remain, particularly within MENA populations. Most of the available studies are observational and considerably heterogeneous in terms of the study design, population characteristics, definitions of vitamin D deficiency, and oral health outcomes.1,11

Future research should prioritize well-designed prospective cohort studies and randomized controlled trials to clarify causal relationships, evaluate the effectiveness of vitamin D interventions, and generate high-quality evidence from MENA populations. This evidence will be essential for informing future clinical recommendations, strengthening preventive strategies, and supporting evidence-based public health policies throughout the region.

Research gaps and future directions

Despite growing evidence linking vitamin D deficiency with oral health outcomes, important knowledge gaps remain. The available evidence is largely based on observational studies and limited by methodological heterogeneity, insufficient longitudinal and interventional research, and underrepresentation of Middle Eastern populations. Thus, further region-specific research is needed to clarify the nature and public health significance of these associations.

A major limitation is the scarcity of studies conducted in Middle Eastern and Gulf populations. Future research should prioritize GCC countries, Levant populations, North African communities, underserved groups, and migrant populations to obtain culturally relevant evidence and support targeted public health interventions.

Most of the existing studies are cross-sectional, and thus causality cannot be established. Longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether vitamin D deficiency precedes oral disease development, identify critical periods of susceptibility, and evaluate long-term oral health consequences in different age groups.

High-quality randomized controlled trials are also required to assess whether correcting vitamin D deficiency improves oral health outcomes. The priority areas include childhood caries prevention, maternal vitamin D supplementation, periodontal treatment outcomes, alveolar bone preservation, implant osseointegration, postoperative healing, and oral surgical outcomes.

Future research should promote the standardization of vitamin D assessment methods, reporting procedures, and diagnostic thresholds to facilitate comparability among studies. Better control of confounding factors is also essential, including age, sex, socioeconomic status, diet, fluoride exposure, oral hygiene practices, smoking, obesity, diabetes, sun exposure, and access to dental care.

Maternal and child oral health research remains a priority, particularly regarding the effects of prenatal vitamin D status on tooth development, enamel defects, and childhood caries. In addition, further evaluations are needed of integrated public health interventions, including school-based programs, community initiatives, health literacy strategies, and primary healthcare integration.

Given the shared pathways linking vitamin D deficiency, obesity, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, osteoporosis, and periodontal diseases, future studies should increasingly adopt integrated approaches that are consistent with the Common Risk Factor Approach. Finally, policy-oriented research should support surveillance systems, food fortification initiatives, health promotion campaigns, school health programs, and primary healthcare models that are capable of informing evidence-based public health policies within Middle Eastern populations.

Proposed research agenda for MENA countries

Future research in MENA populations should extend beyond documenting associations and focus on generating evidence that is capable of informing policy and practice. Priority areas include longitudinal cohort studies, randomized controlled trials to evaluate vitamin D supplementation and oral health outcomes, maternal and child oral health research, school-based prevention programs, and integrated primary healthcare interventions. Establishing standardized regional surveillance systems for both vitamin D status and oral diseases may also facilitate comparisons among countries and support evidence-based public health planning. These efforts could contribute to the development of region-specific recommendations to address both nutritional and oral health challenges within MENA populations.

Limitations of this review

This review has several limitations. In particular, as a narrative review, it does not follow the formal methodology of a systematic review, and thus may be subject to selection bias. The studies included varied considerably in terms of their design, population characteristics, vitamin D assessment methods, oral health outcomes, and adjustment for confounding factors. Furthermore, much of the available evidence was derived from observational studies, limiting causal inference. Emphasis was placed on Middle Eastern and North African populations but evidence remains limited from some countries in the region. Consequently, the findings should be interpreted as a synthesis of current evidence rather than definitive proof of causality.

Conclusion

Vitamin D deficiency is highly prevalent throughout the MENA and is an important public health concern. Emerging evidence suggests that inadequate vitamin D status may be associated with several oral health conditions, including dental caries, periodontal diseases, developmental enamel defects, alveolar bone loss, impaired wound healing, and selected oral mucosal conditions. Evidence regarding oral cancer remains limited and is insufficient to establish a clear relationship. However, the overall body of evidence is predominantly observational, and thus a causal relationship cannot be established.

This review highlights the biological possibility of vitamin D maintaining oral health through its roles in mineral homeostasis, immune regulation, and bone metabolism, as well as emphasizing that oral diseases are multifactorial conditions influenced by complex interactions among biological, behavioral, environmental, and socioeconomic determinants. Accordingly, vitamin D should be considered as one component of a comprehensive approach to oral disease prevention rather than as a standalone preventive or therapeutic strategy.

Given the exceptionally high prevalence of vitamin D deficiency in MENA populations, integrating nutritional health with established oral disease prevention programs may provide meaningful public health benefits. However, further high-quality prospective cohort studies and randomized controlled trials should be conducted within MENA populations to clarify causal relationships, evaluate the effectiveness of vitamin D interventions, and generate robust evidence to inform future clinical practice and evidence-based public health policies.
