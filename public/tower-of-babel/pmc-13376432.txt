Illicit Bitcoin transaction detection via feature-gated temporal graph learning
Authors: Na Han, Ruixian Zhang, Xiaoyun Liu, Haining Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Illicit transaction detection is a key task in blockchain anti-money laundering, yet it remains challenging due to temporal graph evolution, severe class imbalance, and the difficulty of preserving informative transaction attributes during representation learning. Existing graph-based approaches have demonstrated the importance of temporal modeling on Bitcoin transaction networks, but their performance may still degrade when structural evidence becomes unstable or when minority illicit nodes are weakly supported by local graph context. To address these issues, we propose FG-EGCN, a feature-gated temporal graph model for illicit Bitcoin transaction detection. The proposed method combines temporal graph encoding with a residual feature branch and an adaptive gating mechanism, enabling the model to balance temporal-relational evidence and transaction-level attribute information in a node-aware manner. In addition, focal-loss-based optimization is adopted to improve sensitivity to rare illicit samples under highly imbalanced label distributions. We evaluate FG-EGCN on the Elliptic Bitcoin transaction dataset under a chronological train-test split. Experimental results show that FG-EGCN achieves strong performance among the compared graph neural models and delivers more robust illicit transaction detection under temporal distribution shift. Timestep-level analysis further demonstrates improved temporal stability, while ablation studies confirm the contribution of temporal evolution, feature preservation, adaptive gating, and imbalance-aware optimization. Moreover, representation visualization and gate analysis show that the proposed model produces a more structured and interpretable embedding space, in which illicit nodes are more compact and better separated from the dominant node population. These results indicate that combining temporal graph learning with feature preservation and adaptive fusion provides an effective and robust solution for illicit Bitcoin transaction detection.

Introduction

Blockchain-based cryptocurrencies enable transparent and immutable value transfer without centralized intermediaries, but the same properties also create favorable conditions for money laundering, ransomware payments, darknet settlements, and other illicit financial activities. Illicit transaction detection has therefore become a central problem in blockchain anti-money laundering (AML). Existing studies on graph anomaly detection and graph-imbalanced learning show that effective detection in real transaction networks requires models that can cope with evolving graph structure, highly skewed class distributions, and limited anomaly labels1,2.

The Elliptic benchmark remains one of the most important public testbeds for illicit Bitcoin transaction detection because it formulates AML as node classification on a temporal transaction graph. Earlier work on this benchmark demonstrated that graph-based learning is more effective than flat classification, that skip-connected graph convolution improves over plain graph convolution, and that temporal modeling further benefits illicit transaction detection. At the same time, it also revealed two persistent difficulties that remain highly relevant today: original transaction descriptors retain strong predictive value, and model performance can deteriorate markedly after major temporal shifts in the transaction ecosystem. More recent Bitcoin AML studies have continued to improve graph-based detection with convolutional and attention-based designs, indicating that reliable detection under evolving transaction patterns is still an open problem3–5.

A related line of work has shown that effective fraud and anomaly detection in graph data depends not only on topology modeling, but also on how attribute information, relation patterns, and class skew are handled. Studies on malicious crypto-address discovery, tail-effect mitigation in fraud graphs, risk-aware graph representation, and disentangled modeling of homophily and heterophily all suggest that strong attribute signals should be preserved rather than being excessively diluted during graph propagation. Recent sparse graph structure learning studies further show that relational learning can be weakened when graph connectivity is treated as dense, static, or uniformly reliable, especially in non-stationary spatio-temporal systems6–8. This insight is also important for Bitcoin transaction networks, where observed transaction edges are sparse and temporally evolving, and where relational evidence may vary substantially in reliability across nodes and time steps. In parallel, graph anomaly detection research has emphasized the importance of residual design and the reconciliation of structural and attribute anomalies, especially when abnormal samples are rare and difficult to separate from the majority class9–14.

These observations are highly relevant to illicit Bitcoin transaction detection. Blockchain AML is inherently temporal, but temporal graph evolution alone does not fully address the challenge if the highly informative handcrafted transaction features are weakened during representation learning. The problem becomes even harder under severe class imbalance, where illicit samples are rare and supervision is limited. Related progress on learning efficiency under scarce supervision further suggests that stable optimization becomes especially important when only a small amount of informative supervision is available. This motivates a model design that can preserve strong feature cues, exploit temporal graph dynamics, and remain sensitive to rare illicit behavior within a unified framework15.

It is important to note that the role of gated fusion in this task is different from a generic feature-combination operation. In temporal Bitcoin transaction graphs, the reliability of graph-based evidence is both node-dependent and time-dependent. Some illicit transactions are supported by informative neighborhoods and benefit from temporal-relational propagation, whereas others appear in sparse, noisy, or temporally shifted local structures, where excessive graph propagation may dilute discriminative transaction attributes. Meanwhile, the handcrafted local transaction descriptors in the Elliptic dataset remain highly predictive, but they should not dominate all nodes uniformly because some suspicious behaviors are only revealed through relational context. Therefore, a fixed fusion rule is insufficient for this task. A node-wise gate is needed to adaptively estimate the relative reliability of temporal-relational evidence and preserved transaction-level feature evidence for each transaction at each time step.

To this end, we propose FG-EGCN, a feature-gated temporal graph model for illicit Bitcoin transaction detection. The specific novelty of FG-EGCN lies in its task-oriented fusion design for temporal AML graphs, rather than in simply stacking an additional gating block on a temporal GCN. FG-EGCN treats the temporal graph representation and the original transaction-feature representation as two evidence sources with different and time-varying reliability. It explicitly retains transaction attributes through a residual feature pathway and uses a learned node-wise gate to decide how strongly temporal-relational evidence should correct the preserved feature representation. This design is particularly suitable for the Elliptic setting, where temporal graph structure evolves, illicit nodes are rare, and handcrafted transaction descriptors remain highly informative. To further improve sensitivity to rare illicit cases, focal-loss-based optimization is employed for imbalance-aware training. In addition to aggregate classification metrics, we evaluate FG-EGCN through timestep-level performance analysis, gate-value analysis, and representation-space visualization, thereby examining temporal robustness, adaptive evidence fusion, and class separability under distribution shift. Overall, FG-EGCN provides a compact but task-consistent architecture for more reliable illicit transaction detection in evolving Bitcoin transaction graphs.

The design directly addresses the two main empirical signals exposed by the Elliptic setting, namely, the importance of temporal adaptation and the continued usefulness of strong transaction attributes, while aligning with more recent graph fraud and anomaly detection insights on attribute preservation, imbalance handling, and stable learning1–3,13–16.

Related work

Illicit transaction detection in blockchain networks

Illicit transaction detection has become one of the most active research directions in blockchain analytics, especially in the context of anti-money laundering for Bitcoin and fraud detection for Ethereum. The Elliptic benchmark established a temporal node-classification setting for illicit Bitcoin transaction detection and showed that graph-based learning is more suitable than purely tabular modeling for capturing relational transaction patterns3. Building on this benchmark, subsequent studies explored different modeling granularities and learning paradigms. Inspection-L introduced self-supervised graph representation learning for Bitcoin AML and demonstrated that pretraining can improve downstream illicit transaction detection when labeled samples are limited17. Elliptic++ further expanded the original transaction-centric setting by incorporating wallet addresses, user entities, and multiple graph views, making it possible to jointly study fraudulent transactions and illicit actors in a richer forensic environment18. More recently, Elliptic2 argued that money laundering often exhibits subgraph-level patterns rather than isolated node-level signatures, and proposed a large-scale benchmark for suspicious subgraph representation learning on blockchain data19.

Related efforts on Ethereum have shown that temporal behavior and sequential transaction semantics can be equally important. BERT4ETH employed a pre-trained Transformer to model dynamic transaction sequences and demonstrated that sequence-aware representation learning can substantially improve fraud detection performance beyond conventional graph-only baselines20. Recent Bitcoin AML studies have also continued to improve illicit transaction detection with graph convolutional and graph-attention-based architectures4,5. Taken together, these studies indicate that blockchain fraud detection has moved from static shallow classification toward temporal, graph-structured, and multi-view representation learning.

Despite this progress, a practical gap remains for node-level illicit Bitcoin transaction detection. Existing methods either emphasize temporal graph propagation, self-supervised representation learning, heterogeneous graph expansion, or subgraph abstraction, but few methods explicitly address the tension between two facts repeatedly observed in the Elliptic setting: temporal dynamics matter, yet original transaction features remain highly informative. This gap is central to our work. FG-EGCN is designed to preserve and adaptively reuse strong transaction descriptors while still learning from evolving graph structure, which makes it particularly suitable for the Elliptic-style AML setting.

Temporal graph representation learning

Temporal graph learning has become an important foundation for modeling evolving relational systems. EvolveGCN proposed to capture graph dynamics by evolving graph convolution parameters over time, avoiding the need to maintain fixed node embeddings across all time steps and making the framework suitable for dynamic graphs with changing node sets21. Temporal Graph Attention (TGAT) introduced functional time encoding and self-attention-based temporal neighborhood aggregation, showing that node representations on evolving graphs can be treated as time-dependent functions22. Temporal Graph Networks (TGN) further generalized dynamic graph learning with memory modules and event-based updates, providing a flexible continuous-time framework for representation learning on temporal interaction graphs23.

These methods have strongly influenced dynamic graph learning, but their design priorities are largely general-purpose. In illicit Bitcoin transaction detection, temporal evolution is only one side of the problem. The other side is that handcrafted transaction features, such as statistical and local structural descriptors, often remain highly discriminative for separating licit and illicit behavior. As a result, directly applying a generic temporal graph model may not be sufficient if feature information is gradually weakened during message passing or parameter evolution. This issue is particularly important in the Elliptic benchmark, where the graph evolves over discrete time steps and the test distribution may shift substantially after major market events3.

FG-EGCN is closely related to this line of work, but differs in its design focus. Instead of pursuing a more complex temporal architecture, our method emphasizes a task-oriented balance between temporal graph evolution and raw feature preservation. By introducing a residual feature pathway together with feature-gated fusion, FG-EGCN is able to maintain strong attribute signals while still benefiting from dynamic graph reasoning. This makes the proposed model more aligned with the empirical characteristics of illicit Bitcoin transaction detection than a purely topology-driven temporal encoder.

Sparse graph structure learning

Recent studies on sparse graph structure learning provide another important perspective for modeling evolving relational systems. Instead of assuming that all pairwise relations are equally useful, this line of work emphasizes that graph sparsity, noisy connections, and non-stationary dependencies should be explicitly considered during representation learning. For example, Miao et al6. proposed a sparse graph generation strategy with non-stationary temporal processing for wind turbine condition monitoring, showing that dynamically constructed sparse dependencies can improve the modeling of complex spatio-temporal systems. In multisensor anomaly detection, Miao et al7. further pointed out that the commonly used fully connected graph assumption may contradict real-world sparse connectivity, and introduced a long- and short-term sparse graph attention network to generate sparse inter-sensor relations while capturing temporal dependencies. From a different perspective, Wu et al8. proposed dynamic sparse training for resource-constrained spatio-temporal forecasting, where data-level node or region selection is learned dynamically to reduce redundant observations and improve efficiency.

These works are highly relevant to the present study because Bitcoin transaction networks are also sparse, dynamic, and potentially noisy. They suggest that temporal graph learning should not simply aggregate all available relational information in a uniform manner. However, the setting of illicit Bitcoin transaction detection differs from the above sensor-based or forecasting-oriented tasks in two important aspects. First, the Elliptic dataset provides an observed transaction topology rather than a latent sensor-dependency graph to be inferred from multivariate signals. Second, the main difficulty is not only sparse structure modeling, but also the coexistence of evolving transaction topology, highly informative handcrafted transaction attributes, and severe illicit-class imbalance. Therefore, FG-EGCN does not aim to replace the observed transaction graph with a newly generated sparse graph. Instead, it uses normalized temporal graph propagation to exploit the available sparse topology, while introducing a residual feature branch and a node-wise feature gate to prevent unreliable or insufficient relational evidence from overwhelming strong transaction-level descriptors. In this sense, sparse graph learning further strengthens the motivation of FG-EGCN: robust blockchain AML requires not only temporal graph modeling, but also adaptive control over how sparse structural evidence and feature evidence are fused.

Imbalance-aware and anomaly-aware graph learning

Another closely related direction concerns graph anomaly detection and graph learning under severe class imbalance. Recent surveys have shown that anomaly detection on graphs depends on the joint modeling of node attributes, structural context, objective design, and robustness under scarce supervision1. Class imbalance is especially problematic in graph classification and node classification tasks because minority nodes are not only few in number, but may also be topologically marginalized or surrounded by majority-class neighborhoods2. GraphSMOTE extended synthetic oversampling to graph-structured data and demonstrated that imbalance-aware representation augmentation can improve minority-class node classification24. In addition, focal loss has been widely adopted as an effective way to emphasize hard and minority examples by down-weighting easy negatives during optimization25.

Within graph anomaly detection itself, several studies have further clarified the role of feature-structure coupling. Rethinking unsupervised graph anomaly detection with deep learning showed that residual design and objective selection are crucial to stable anomaly modeling13. Reconciling attribute and structural anomalies demonstrated that anomaly detection improves when discrepancies between node attributes and graph structure are modeled explicitly rather than implicitly ignored14. Related fraud-oriented work has also shown that preserving strong attribute cues, mitigating long-tail effects, and distinguishing heterogeneous relation patterns can substantially improve graph-based fraud detection10–12. Furthermore, progress in learning efficiency under scarce supervision suggests that stable optimization is particularly important when informative labeled examples are limited15.

These observations are directly relevant to illicit Bitcoin transaction detection, where illicit samples are rare, temporal variation is significant, and the mismatch between strong transaction descriptors and evolving relational patterns is non-negligible. FG-EGCN addresses this combination of challenges in a unified manner. The residual feature branch is introduced to preserve high-signal transaction attributes, the feature-gating mechanism adaptively reconciles feature evidence with temporal graph representations, and focal-loss-based optimization improves sensitivity to rare illicit transactions. Compared with prior work, the main novelty of FG-EGCN lies not in introducing a heavier temporal backbone, but in designing a more task-consistent framework for imbalanced temporal graph learning in blockchain AML.

In summary, prior studies have established the value of graph learning, temporal modeling, self-supervised representation learning, and imbalance-aware optimization for blockchain fraud detection. However, the literature still lacks a compact model specifically tailored to the Elliptic-style setting, where temporal graph evolution, strong handcrafted transaction features, and rare illicit samples must be handled simultaneously. FG-EGCN is proposed to fill this gap by combining feature preservation, adaptive temporal graph fusion, and imbalance-aware training in a single framework.

Although gated and cross-gated fusion mechanisms have been widely used in other domains, directly transferring such designs to illicit Bitcoin transaction detection does not by itself solve the task-specific difficulty of temporal AML graphs. The key issue in this setting is not only how to combine two representations, but how to avoid over-reliance on temporally unstable graph propagation while preserving highly predictive transaction descriptors. FG-EGCN therefore uses gated fusion as a node-wise reliability control mechanism between temporal-relational evidence and residual feature evidence. This distinguishes it from generic gated fusion designs whose main goal is multi-modal or multi-branch feature combination, and makes the fusion mechanism explicitly tied to temporal graph drift, feature dilution, and minority illicit-node detection.

Feature-gated temporal graph learning

The proposed FG-EGCN contains two representation branches and one adaptive fusion module. The first branch is a temporal graph encoder, which models evolving structural dependencies across time. The second branch is a residual feature projector, which preserves transaction-level attribute information in the hidden space. The two branches are then integrated by a node-wise gating mechanism. Compared with a static GCN, this recurrent parameter-evolution design allows the propagation operator itself to adapt to non-stationary transaction patterns.

Its purpose is not merely to concatenate or reweight two hidden vectors, but to estimate the node-wise reliability of two heterogeneous evidence sources. In the Elliptic transaction graph, temporal-relational evidence may be reliable for nodes whose suspiciousness is supported by neighboring transaction patterns, but it may become less reliable for nodes located in sparse, noisy, or temporally shifted neighborhoods. Conversely, raw transaction descriptors may provide strong local evidence, but they cannot fully capture relational laundering patterns. The classifier then operates on the fused representation:15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} P_t=\textrm{softmax}(H_tW_c+b_c), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_t\in \mathbb {R}^{N_t\times C}$$\end{document} is the class probability matrix and C is the number of classes.

Equation (14) is the core design of FG-EGCN. It allows the model to determine, for each node and each time step, whether the decision should rely more on temporal graph evidence or on preserved feature evidence. As a result, FG-EGCN does not assume that one source of information dominates globally. Instead, it learns a data-dependent balance between structural evolution and node attributes.

The adaptive fusion in Eq. (14) can be rewritten as16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} H_t=S_t+\Gamma _t\odot (Z_t-S_t). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}This form reveals that FG-EGCN can be interpreted as a residual correction mechanism. The feature branch \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_t$$\end{document} acts as a stable reference representation, while the temporal branch contributes an adaptive correction term \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Gamma _t\odot (Z_t-S_t)$$\end{document}. Therefore, the gate does not merely interpolate between two embeddings. It controls how much temporal structural evidence should modify the preserved feature representation.

This interpretation provides two useful consequences. First, since each element of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Gamma _t$$\end{document} lies in (0, 1), the correction magnitude is explicitly bounded. From Eq. (16), we obtain17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \Vert H_t-S_t\Vert _F = \Vert \Gamma _t\odot (Z_t-S_t)\Vert _F \le \Vert \Gamma _t\Vert _{\infty }\Vert Z_t-S_t\Vert _F \le \Vert Z_t-S_t\Vert _F, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}and similarly,18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \Vert H_t-Z_t\Vert _F = \Vert (1-\Gamma _t)\odot (S_t-Z_t)\Vert _F \le \Vert S_t-Z_t\Vert _F. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Equations (17) and (18) show that the fused representation remains controlled by the discrepancy between the two branches, rather than deviating arbitrarily from both. This property is useful under temporal drift, where direct reliance on the temporal branch may amplify unstable graph evidence.

Second, the gate is most sensitive when the two branches disagree strongly. By differentiating Eq. (14) with respect to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Gamma _t$$\end{document}, we have19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial H_t}{\partial \Gamma _t}=Z_t-S_t. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Equation (19) indicates that the gating update is driven by the discrepancy between temporal graph evidence and feature evidence. When the two branches are already consistent, the gradient with respect to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Gamma _t$$\end{document} becomes small, and the fusion remains stable. When they differ substantially, the gate receives a stronger optimization signal and learns to reweight the two sources accordingly. This behavior is particularly desirable in illicit transaction detection, where some nodes benefit from graph context while others are better characterized by their original transaction descriptors.

A perturbation analysis further clarifies the role of the gate. Suppose the temporal branch is perturbed by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta _t$$\end{document}, yielding \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{Z}_t=Z_t+\Delta _t$$\end{document}, while \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_t$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Gamma _t$$\end{document} remain fixed. The resulting fused representation becomes \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{H}_t=\Gamma _t\odot \tilde{Z}_t+(1-\Gamma _t)\odot S_t$$\end{document}, and thus20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \Vert \tilde{H}_t-H_t\Vert _F = \Vert \Gamma _t\odot \Delta _t\Vert _F \le \Vert \Gamma _t\Vert _{\infty }\Vert \Delta _t\Vert _F \le \Vert \Delta _t\Vert _F. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Equation (20) shows that the gate can attenuate perturbations propagated from the temporal graph branch. When the graph evidence becomes noisy or unstable, smaller gate values naturally suppress the corresponding disturbance in the fused embedding. This provides an additional explanation for why feature-gated fusion is well suited to temporal Bitcoin AML, where transaction graphs may change abruptly across time.

Imbalance-aware temporal objective

The temporal graph sequence is optimized over labeled nodes only. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {V}_t^{L}$$\end{document} denote the labeled node set at time step t, and let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_{i,y_i}^{(t)}$$\end{document} denote the predicted probability assigned to the true class \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y_i$$\end{document} of node i. Since illicit nodes are rare, a standard objective is often dominated by the majority class. To alleviate this problem, we use focal-loss-based optimization25 over the temporal training window:21\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {L} = \frac{1}{\sum _{t=1}^{T_{\textrm{tr}}}|\mathcal {V}_t^{L}|} \sum _{t=1}^{T_{\textrm{tr}}} \sum _{i\in \mathcal {V}_t^{L}} -\alpha _{y_i} \left( 1-p_{i,y_i}^{(t)}\right) ^{\gamma } \log p_{i,y_i}^{(t)}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_{\textrm{tr}}$$\end{document} is the number of training time steps, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha _{y_i}$$\end{document} is the class-specific weight, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma$$\end{document} is the focusing parameter. In our implementation, the focusing parameter is set to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma =2.0$$\end{document}, following the standard focal-loss setting for emphasizing hard examples25. The class-weight vector is set to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha =[0.70,0.29,0.01]$$\end{document} for the licit, illicit, and unknown classes, respectively. Since only labeled nodes are used for supervised optimization, the unknown class receives a very small weight and mainly remains in the graph to preserve relational structure. When \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma =0$$\end{document}, Eq. (21) reduces to weighted cross-entropy.

The objective in Eq. (21) has a clear role in our setting. The modulating term \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left( 1-p_{i,y_i}^{(t)}\right) ^{\gamma }$$\end{document} suppresses easy majority-class samples and increases the contribution of hard minority samples. This is particularly important in the Elliptic scenario, where illicit nodes are not only scarce but may also appear in graph regions with weak structural regularity. Therefore, the objective complements the architecture of FG-EGCN: the feature-gated fusion improves representation quality, while the focal-loss-based objective increases the effective learning pressure on rare illicit transactions.

Experiments

In this section, the proposed FG-EGCN is evaluated on the benchmark task of illicit Bitcoin transaction detection. The experiments are designed to examine whether feature-gated temporal fusion improves classification performance over the temporal graph baseline under realistic anti-money laundering settings. In addition to overall classification results, timestep-level evaluation, ablation studies, and representation-space visualization are further conducted to assess the robustness, interpretability, and design effectiveness of the proposed model.

Datasets

All experiments are conducted on the Elliptic Bitcoin transaction dataset3, which is one of the most widely used public benchmarks for blockchain anti-money laundering. The dataset contains 203,769 transaction nodes and 234,355 directed edges, organized into 49 temporal steps. Each node corresponds to a Bitcoin transaction, and each edge represents a directed transaction flow between two transactions. The nodes are associated with three label states, namely illicit, licit, and unknown. During training and evaluation, only the labeled nodes are used for supervised classification, while the unlabeled nodes are retained in the graph to preserve the temporal relational structure.

For each transaction, the original dataset provides 166 attributes, including 94 local features and 72 aggregated features. In our implementation, we use the 94 local features as the default node attributes. This setting is consistent with the motivation of FG-EGCN, since the proposed model explicitly preserves transaction-level descriptors through a residual feature branch and adaptively fuses them with temporal graph representations. Using local features therefore allows us to evaluate more directly whether the model can benefit from preserving strong raw transaction evidence during temporal graph learning.

Following the standard temporal evaluation protocol, the transaction graphs are ordered chronologically and divided into training and testing periods according to time steps. Specifically, the first 34 time steps are used for training and the remaining 15 time steps are used for testing, corresponding to an approximate 70%/30% chronological split3. This setting avoids information leakage from future transaction behavior and provides a realistic evaluation of the model under temporal distribution shift.

Implementation details

The proposed FG-EGCN is implemented in PyTorch 2.3.0, and all experiments are conducted on NVIDIA A100-PCIE-40GB GPUs. Unless otherwise specified, all trainable models are optimized using Adam with an initial learning rate of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1\times 10^{-3}$$\end{document}. The maximum number of training epochs is set to 500. Instead of using a fixed patience-based early-stopping rule, we retain the checkpoint with the best validation illicit F1 during training and use this checkpoint for final testing. This strategy is adopted because the illicit class is highly imbalanced and the best epoch may differ from the epoch with the lowest aggregate loss.

The temporal graph encoder contains two temporal graph convolution layers. The hidden dimension is set to 64, and the feature projection branch also maps the original transaction attributes into a 64-dimensional hidden space. The temporal encoder follows the EvolveGCN-H-style recurrent parameter evolution described in Section "Feature-gated temporal graph learning". In the implemented architecture, the graph representation dimension follows the path \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$94\rightarrow 64\rightarrow C$$\end{document}, where 94 is the number of local transaction features used as node attributes and C is the number of output classes. The adaptive gating module uses a sigmoid activation to generate node-wise fusion weights between the temporal graph representation and the projected feature representation.

For regularization and stable optimization, dropout is applied after the activation function in each temporal graph layer, with the dropout rate set to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_{\textrm{drop}}=0.5$$\end{document}. This value is consistent with common dropout settings used in Bitcoin fraud detection and graph-based transaction analysis. In addition, residual connections and LayerNorm are used inside the temporal encoder. The adjacency matrix is symmetrically normalized according to Eq. (4) after adding self-loops. No additional weight decay is used unless otherwise stated.

During training, focal loss is adopted as the default optimization objective to improve sensitivity to rare illicit transactions. The focusing parameter is set to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma =2.0$$\end{document}, and the class-weight vector is set to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha =[0.70,0.29,0.01]$$\end{document} for the licit, illicit, and unknown classes, respectively. All experiments use a fixed random seed of 42 for data loading, parameter initialization, and stochastic training operations. All compared graph neural models are evaluated under the same chronological data split and a consistent optimization setting whenever possible, so that the reported performance differences mainly reflect the effectiveness of the model design rather than discrepancies in the experimental protocol.

Following the standard temporal evaluation protocol of the Elliptic benchmark, the transaction graphs are ordered chronologically. The first 34 time steps are used for training and model selection, and the remaining 15 time steps are used for testing. During supervised optimization, only labeled licit and illicit nodes contribute to the loss, while unlabeled nodes are retained in the graph to preserve temporal relational structure. For evaluation, we report illicit Precision, illicit Recall, illicit F1, and MicroAVG F1, with particular emphasis on illicit F1 because the main practical objective is to identify rare illicit transactions.

Overall performance

Table 1 reports the overall illicit transaction classification results on the Elliptic dataset. To address the need for more up-to-date comparisons, we additionally reimplemented four recent anomaly detection methods under the same chronological split and labeled-node evaluation protocol. Since these methods were originally developed for network traffic anomaly detection rather than Bitcoin transaction graphs, they are adapted here to the Elliptic node-feature setting using the same transaction attributes as input. Therefore, they provide useful recent non-graph anomaly detection references, while GCN, Skip-GCN, EvolveGCN, and FG-EGCN constitute the temporal graph learning comparison group.

Among the compared temporal graph learning models, FG-EGCN achieves the best performance on all reported metrics. Specifically, FG-EGCN obtains an illicit Precision of 0.834, an illicit Recall of 0.722, an illicit F1 of 0.774, and a MicroAVG F1 of 0.971. Relative to EvolveGCN, the proposed model improves illicit Precision by 0.019, illicit Recall by 0.124, illicit F1 by 0.082, and MicroAVG F1 by 0.004. Relative to GCN and Skip-GCN, FG-EGCN improves illicit F1 by 0.146 and 0.069, respectively. These results show that the proposed feature-gated temporal graph learning strategy provides a more effective temporal graph representation for illicit transaction detection, especially by improving the recovery of rare illicit nodes.

The newly added recent anomaly detection baselines also achieve competitive results after adaptation to the Elliptic node-feature setting. In particular, MFR obtains an illicit F1 of 0.793, which is slightly higher than FG-EGCN, indicating that reconstruction-oriented anomaly objectives can be effective when strong transaction attributes are available. UnDiff, ConMD, and Contrastive obtain illicit F1 scores of 0.762, 0.748, and 0.718, respectively, while FG-EGCN remains competitive and achieves the highest illicit Precision among these recent adapted anomaly detection baselines. These results suggest that recent anomaly detection objectives provide useful feature-level detection ability, but they do not explicitly exploit the evolving Bitcoin transaction topology26.

Notably, Random Forest with AF+NE still achieves the highest overall illicit F1 among all compared methods. This observation is consistent with previous findings on the Elliptic benchmark and suggests that handcrafted transaction descriptors carry strong predictive information. However, FG-EGCN substantially narrows the gap while remaining the strongest method within the end-to-end temporal graph learning family. This result supports the design motivation of FG-EGCN: instead of relying solely on graph propagation, the model preserves transaction-level feature evidence and adaptively fuses it with temporal-relational information. The performance gain of FG-EGCN is particularly meaningful when viewed together with the temporal label distribution shown in Fig. 2. The upper panel shows that the ratio between illicit and licit nodes varies substantially across time, with several pronounced peaks and troughs. The lower panel further shows that the total number of nodes is also highly non-stationary, while unknown nodes consistently dominate the graph. This means that the model is trained and evaluated under a challenging temporal anti-money laundering scenario, where the effective class prior changes over time and the relational context around labeled illicit nodes is often incomplete. Under such conditions, the improvement from FG-EGCN cannot be explained by model capacity alone. Instead, it reflects a more suitable treatment of three task-specific factors, namely temporal evolution, feature preservation, and class imbalance.

Another important finding is that the gain of FG-EGCN is driven mainly by the increase in illicit Recall. Compared with the compared temporal graph model, the recall improvement reaches 0.124, which is much larger than the corresponding precision gain. This behavior is consistent with the design of the model. The residual feature branch preserves strong transaction-level descriptors, the adaptive gating mechanism prevents unreliable temporal propagation from dominating all nodes uniformly, and the focal-loss-based objective increases the learning pressure on hard minority samples. As a result, FG-EGCN is better able to recover illicit nodes that may otherwise be missed by the baseline temporal encoder.

The result is also consistent with the trend observed in the timestep-level analysis. According to the experimental record associated with Table 1, the curve of FG-EGCN remains above those of GCN and Skip-GCN over most test timesteps, and its aggregate illicit F1 of 0.774 lies within the expected range indicated by the per-timestep evaluation. This suggests that the gain is not caused by isolated improvements at only a few time steps, but instead reflects a more stable advantage across the testing period.

Overall, the first experiment demonstrates that FG-EGCN provides a stronger and more stable illicit transaction detector than the original temporal graph baseline under the quantified temporal distribution shift observed in the Elliptic test period. The result supports the central hypothesis of this work: in evolving Bitcoin transaction graphs, illicit transaction detection should not rely solely on temporal graph propagation, but should instead combine temporal relational evidence with preserved transaction attributes in an adaptive and imbalance-aware manner.

Efficiency comparison

In addition to classification accuracy, computational efficiency is also important for practical blockchain anti-money laundering systems31. Therefore, we further compare FG-EGCN with representative baselines in terms of model parameters, training time per epoch, inference time per graph snapshot, and illicit-class F1 score. The comparison is conducted under the same Elliptic data setting and model configurations used in the main experiments. For neural models, the parameter count refers to trainable weights. For Random Forest, the reported model scale is treated as an effective parameter count based on split thresholds and leaf distributions, since tree ensembles do not have differentiable trainable weights in the same sense as neural networks.

Temporal distribution shift quantification

The previous results indicate that FG-EGCN performs better than the temporal graph baseline under the chronological split. However, the chronological split alone does not directly quantify temporal distribution shift. To provide more explicit evidence, we further analyze the Elliptic temporal snapshots from two complementary perspectives: feature-level distribution discrepancy and performance-level concept drift.

For feature-level shift, we compute Wasserstein distance (WD) and maximum mean discrepancy (MMD) between consecutive temporal snapshots. To obtain a stable one-dimensional marginal summary for WD, each node feature vector is projected by mean pooling over the feature dimension. The WD between two consecutive snapshots is computed as22\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} D_{\textrm{WD}}(t,t+1) = \int _0^1 \left| Q_t(u)-Q_{t+1}(u) \right| \,du, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Q_t(u)$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Q_{t+1}(u)$$\end{document} are the quantile functions of the projected feature distributions at time steps t and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t+1$$\end{document}, respectively.

We also compute MMD with a Gaussian RBF kernel to capture kernel-level distribution discrepancy. Given samples \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{x_i\}_{i=1}^{m}$$\end{document} from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_t$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{y_i\}_{i=1}^{m}$$\end{document} from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_{t+1}$$\end{document}, the squared MMD is computed as23\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \begin{aligned} D_{\textrm{MMD}}^2(t,t+1) =&\frac{1}{m(m-1)} \sum _{i\ne j} k(x_i,x_j) + \frac{1}{m(m-1)} \sum _{i\ne j} k(y_i,y_j) \\&- \frac{2}{m^2} \sum _{i=1}^{m} \sum _{j=1}^{m} k(x_i,y_j), \end{aligned} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k(x,y)=\exp (-\Vert x-y\Vert ^2/(2\sigma ^2))$$\end{document}. For performance-level drift, we further apply the Page–Hinkley Test (PHT) to the timestep-level illicit F1 sequence over the test period. PHT monitors cumulative deviations from the running mean and flags a change point when the deviation exceeds a preset threshold. In addition, we use an ADWIN-style smoothed F1 curve with a short sliding window to reveal the underlying performance trend and reduce local fluctuation.

Figure 4 reports the resulting drift analysis. In Fig. 4(a), both WD and MMD show a clear spike around the train–test boundary. The WD score increases sharply between time steps 34 and 35, reaching the largest value of approximately 0.257. The MMD curve also peaks near the same boundary, with a maximum value of approximately 0.185. This confirms that the first test snapshot differs substantially from the preceding training-period snapshots at the feature-distribution level. Moreover, the elevated WD values persist through the early-to-middle test period, suggesting that the shift is not a single isolated fluctuation but a sustained distributional change.

Figure 4(b) further shows the performance-level consequence of this shift. The timestep-level illicit F1 remains relatively high in the early test period, but a clear downward trend appears in the later test snapshots. The PHT flags multiple change points from the middle-to-late test period, and the ADWIN-style smoothed F1 curve also decreases after the early test stage. This pattern suggests that feature-level shift appears first near the train–test boundary, while performance degradation becomes more evident after several subsequent snapshots.

This analysis provides quantitative support for the temporal distribution shift assumption used in this work. More importantly, it helps explain why feature-gated fusion is useful. Under temporal shift, graph-derived relational evidence may become less stable because transaction neighborhoods evolve over time. The residual feature branch preserves transaction-level descriptors, and the learned gate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Gamma _t$$\end{document} adaptively balances feature evidence and temporal-relational evidence. Therefore, the improved timestep-level stability of FG-EGCN is consistent with its design motivation: the model does not rely solely on temporal graph propagation, but retains a feature-preserving path that can help mitigate the influence of unstable temporal graph context.

By contrast, the residual feature branch in FG-EGCN provides a direct and stable source of transaction-level information, while the adaptive gate determines how strongly the final prediction should depend on temporal graph evidence. Therefore, when the graph context becomes less reliable, the model can rely more on preserved feature descriptors, which in turn stabilizes the final prediction. This interpretation is consistent with the intended role of Fig. 5 in the experiment design, where the fused model is expected to remain more stable than the baseline under sudden increases or decreases in the number of illicit samples.

Overall, the timestep-level evaluation demonstrates that the proposed method does not gain its advantage from only a few isolated timesteps. Instead, it exhibits a persistent and more stable superiority over the temporal test period, especially in the later stage where distribution shift is more likely to occur. These results complement the aggregate results in Table 1 and further confirm that the combination of temporal graph learning, residual feature preservation, and adaptive fusion is well suited to illicit Bitcoin transaction detection.

Effect of unlabeled nodes and label sparsity

The Elliptic dataset contains a large proportion of unlabeled transaction nodes. This is a realistic setting in blockchain anti-money laundering because only a small subset of transactions can be confidently annotated as licit or illicit. However, unlabeled nodes may also introduce potential bias in semi-supervised graph learning. Although they do not directly provide supervised labels, they can still affect node representations through message passing. Therefore, we further analyze how unlabeled nodes influence FG-EGCN.

In our implementation, unlabeled nodes are retained in the temporal transaction graph to preserve the observed transaction topology, but they are not treated as licit or illicit supervision. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {V}_t^{L}$$\end{document} denote the labeled node set at time step t, including only licit and illicit nodes. The supervised focal loss is computed only over \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {V}_t^{L}$$\end{document}:24\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {L} = -\frac{1}{\sum _{t=1}^{T_{\textrm{tr}}}|\mathcal {V}_{t}^{L}|} \sum _{t=1}^{T_{\textrm{tr}}} \sum _{i\in \mathcal {V}_{t}^{L}} \alpha _{y_i} (1-p_{i,y_i})^{\gamma } \log p_{i,y_i}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Thus, unlabeled nodes are used as structural context during temporal graph propagation, rather than as pseudo-labeled training samples. This design avoids directly converting unknown transactions into negative samples, which would otherwise introduce label-noise bias.

In the labeled-only subgraph, all unlabeled nodes and their incident edges are removed. In the unlabeled-edge-masked graph, unlabeled nodes are retained but their message-passing contribution is partially suppressed. In the full-graph setting, unlabeled nodes are fully retained for graph propagation but are excluded from the supervised loss. Since the graph sequence is chronologically split into training and testing periods, the model must generalize to future transaction patterns rather than only fitting a static graph snapshot. The poor performance of the static variant confirms that temporal evolution is a necessary foundation for robust illicit transaction detection.

Taken together, the ablation results reveal a clear hierarchy of contribution. Temporal evolution and the feature branch provide the major structural gains, while adaptive gating and focal-loss-based optimization further refine the model and improve minority-class detection. This pattern is also consistent with the design logic of FG-EGCN: the evolving graph encoder captures dynamic relational context, the feature branch preserves strong transaction descriptors, the adaptive gate reconciles the two sources of information at the node level, and focal loss improves learning on rare illicit transactions.

To better understand why adaptive fusion is effective, we further analyze the learned gate values in Figure 6. The gate analysis provides a mechanistic explanation for the ablation results. In particular, illicit nodes tend to receive smaller gate values, which means that the model places relatively more weight on preserved feature information for difficult minority samples, whereas unknown nodes rely more strongly on graph structural evidence. This class-specific behavior explains why removing either the feature branch or the adaptive gate leads to a substantial decline in illicit Recall and F1.

Discussion

The findings of this study indicate that illicit Bitcoin transaction detection should be viewed as a temporal and imbalanced graph-learning problem in which transaction attributes and evolving relational structure play complementary roles32. In the Elliptic setting, the transaction graph changes over time, the minority illicit class is sparse, and the local graph context of suspicious nodes is often incomplete or unstable33. Under such conditions, methods that rely only on temporal graph propagation may fail to preserve highly informative transaction descriptors, whereas methods that rely only on raw features may overlook the temporal dependencies embedded in the evolving transaction network34. The proposed FG-EGCN addresses this tension by explicitly integrating an evolving graph encoder with a residual feature branch and a feature-gated fusion mechanism. This design allows the model to adaptively determine how much each node should rely on transaction-level evidence or temporal-relational evidence. The experimental results support this interpretation from multiple perspectives. The overall comparison shows that FG-EGCN improves illicit transaction detection over the temporal graph baseline, the timestep-level analysis shows that the gain remains stable under temporal shift, the ablation study confirms that temporal evolution, feature preservation, adaptive gating, and focal-loss-based optimization all contribute positively, and the visualization results show that the learned embedding space becomes more structured and more interpretable. Taken together, these observations suggest that the effectiveness of FG-EGCN does not arise from increased architectural complexity alone, but from a more suitable treatment of the actual characteristics of blockchain anti-money laundering35–39.

At the same time, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the present study is conducted on the Elliptic benchmark, which remains an important public dataset but does not fully capture the diversity of real-world blockchain anti-money laundering scenarios, such as address-level risk assessment, entity-level detection, or suspicious subgraph analysis. Second, the current framework focuses on transaction-level node classification and does not explicitly model long-range laundering semantics, cross-chain behavior, or richer heterogeneous interaction patterns40,41. Third, although the gating analysis improves interpretability at the representation level, the model still does not provide fully human-readable forensic explanations42. These limitations point to several directions for future work. A natural extension is to evaluate the feature-gated design on richer blockchain settings, including address graphs, entity graphs, and suspicious subgraph benchmarks. Another promising direction is to combine feature-gated temporal graph learning with self-supervised pretraining or other label-efficient learning strategies, which may further improve robustness when illicit labels are scarce43. In addition, integrating more explicit explainability techniques with the proposed gating mechanism may help bridge the gap between predictive performance and analyst-oriented interpretability. More broadly, the proposed design is not restricted to Bitcoin anti-money laundering alone, and may also be useful for other dynamic graph tasks where node attributes and evolving structure must be balanced adaptively. Future work may further explore online updating, self-supervised pretraining, and deployment-oriented optimization for dynamic blockchain AML systems44–46.

Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed FG-EGCN for illicit Bitcoin transaction detection and investigated how temporal graph learning, feature preservation, and adaptive fusion can be integrated within a unified framework for blockchain anti-money laundering. The proposed model was designed to address three practical challenges in this task, namely evolving transaction structure, severe class imbalance, and the difficulty of retaining informative transaction descriptors during representation learning. To this end, FG-EGCN combines temporal graph encoding with a residual feature branch and a feature-gated fusion mechanism, and further introduces focal-loss-based optimization to improve sensitivity to rare illicit transactions. Experimental results on the Elliptic dataset showed that FG-EGCN achieves strong illicit transaction detection performance, remains more stable under quantitatively measured temporal feature shift and performance-level concept drift, and produces a more structured and interpretable embedding space. The timestep-level evaluation, ablation study, and visualization analysis consistently support the effectiveness of the proposed design and show that temporal evolution, feature preservation, adaptive fusion, and imbalance-aware optimization all contribute positively to the final model. Overall, these results indicate that illicit Bitcoin transaction detection should be treated as a temporal and imbalanced graph-learning problem in which the relative importance of transaction attributes and relational context changes across node types and time periods. FG-EGCN provides an effective solution to this problem and offers a practical direction for developing more robust and interpretable blockchain anti-money laundering models, while also suggesting that feature-gated temporal graph learning may be valuable for other dynamic graph tasks with scarce anomalies and evolving structures.
