Systematic review of buried flap reconstructions after nipple-sparing mastectomy
Authors: Jennifer Main, Mehul Thakkar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough autologous reconstruction after nipple-sparing mastectomy (NSM) is well established, the subset of buried-free flap reconstructions remains less well characterised. With evolving surgical techniques understanding the safety, efficacy, and outcomes of buried-free flap reconstructions is increasingly important.AimTo review recent literature regarding autologous buried-free flap reconstructions post mastectomy, including demographics, indications, outcomes and complications.MethodsA search of PubMed and Medline databases was performed using the keywords “deep inferior epigastric perforator flap”, “DIEP”, “deep inferior epigastric flap”, “free flap”, “transverse upper gracilis”, “TUG”, “bury”, “buried”, “burying”, “autologous breast reconstruction”, “autologous reconstruction”, “breast reconstruction”, and “nipple-sparing mastectomy” using a 3-component search along with the Boolean operators 'AND' and 'OR'.ResultsSeventy-five studies were retrieved with 12 meeting the inclusion criteria. Four studies directly compared buried and non-buried flaps. A total of 385 patients underwent 585 buried-free flap reconstructions. These were predominantly DIEP (70.8%), immediate (76.8%) and post-NSM (72.6%). Flap loss was similar (1.5%vs 1.8%; partial 0.3% vs 0.9%). Return-to-theatre rates were 7.5% for buried and 9.1% for non-buried flaps. Anastomotic complications were similar (2.8%vs 2.6%). Buried flaps demonstrated higher rates of fat necrosis (4.1%vs 2.1%) and infection (4.5%vs 1.5%), while non-buried flaps had higher wound dehiscence rates (5.9%vs 4.6%). Revision procedures were more common in buried flaps (59.9%vs 49.2%).ConclusionsBuried-free flap reconstruction following NSM is safe and technically feasible, with comparable complication rates compared to conventional techniques. Standardised monitoring and patient-reported outcomes are needed to optimise selection and wider adoption.

Introduction

NSM has emerged as a preferred surgical option for selected patients with breast cancer, offering improved aesthetic and psychological outcomes compared to traditional mastectomy techniques. NSM involves removal of the breast glandular tissue while preserving the native skin envelope and the native nipple-areolar complex (NAC), thereby maintaining breast contour, body image, and identity.1,2

Preservation of the NAC has gained widespread surgical acceptance, as accumulating evidence indicates that, in carefully selected patients, it carries a low risk of disease recurrence and is oncologically safe.3,4 NSM is generally regarded as safe in women with small, peripherally located tumours without multicentric disease, in the absence of clinical or radiological nipple involvement, and in those undergoing prophylactic mastectomy5,6 Prophylactic NSM is increasingly performed in high-risk individuals, particularly carriers of genetic variants such as BRCA1 and BRCA2 mutations, as well as in young women with strong familial predisposition to breast cancer. In this population, preservation of the NAC is associated with meaningful psychosocial benefits, including higher levels of breast satisfaction and BREAST-Q Psychosocial Well-Being and Sexual Well-Being scores when compared to skin-sparing mastectomy (SSM).7,8

The principal technical challenge of NSM is achieving adequate oncological clearance while maintaining nipple viability. Complete excision of retro-areolar glandular tissue must be balanced against preservation of vascular supply to the NAC. Following mastectomy, the intrinsic perforating vessels from the internal thoracic and lateral thoracic systems are disrupted, rendering nipple perfusion dependent predominantly on the subdermal plexus of the mastectomy skin flaps. This reliance increases susceptibility to ischaemic complications, particularly in patients with impaired microvascular circulation, such as smokers or those with prior chest wall irradiation.9,10

NSM is contraindicated in patients with Paget’s disease, pathological nipple discharge, or imaging evidence of nipple involvement, and should be undertaken with caution in individuals at increased risk of wound-healing complications.10

UK guidance, including the Association of Breast Surgery (ABS) Oncoplastic Breast Reconstruction: Guidelines for Best Practice, recommends routine intraoperative assessment of the retro-areolar margin. A separate subareolar tissue specimen is excised and submitted for histopathological evaluation to exclude occult malignancy. In therapeutic cases, frozen section analysis may be considered if results would influence immediate surgical management; however, routine intraoperative frozen section is not supported in risk-reducing mastectomy.10

Although implant-based reconstruction remains common after NSM, autologous reconstruction offers superior long-term aesthetic outcomes, lower capsular contracture rates, and improved patient satisfaction.11

Reconstruction following NSM remains complex, particularly when autologous tissue is used. In recent years, “buried free flap” reconstructions - where the transferred tissue is inset beneath the mastectomy skin envelope without an external skin paddle - have become increasingly common.12,13 The absence of an external skin paddle presents challenges for intraoperative and postoperative monitoring. Without the traditional visual cues of skin colour, turgor, or capillary refill, surgeons must rely on adjunctive technologies such as implantable Doppler probes, near-infrared spectroscopy, or indocyanine green (ICG) angiography to assess flap perfusion.14,15

Although autologous reconstruction after NSM is well established, the subset of buried-free flap reconstructions (without an external skin paddle) remains less well characterised. Early series have demonstrated that buried free-flap reconstruction after NSM is technically feasible, with flap loss rates as low as 2% and favourable aesthetic outcomes.16 More recent comparative studies and systematic reviews confirm that buried autologous flaps can achieve equivalent safety profiles to non-buried reconstructions when appropriate monitoring is used.17, 18, 19

Among autologous options, the deep inferior epigastric perforator (DIEP) flap is widely regarded as the gold standard in breast reconstruction and is the most commonly used.20 It provides sufficient soft tissue for shaping the breast mound while preserving abdominal wall musculature, reducing donor site morbidity compared to previous techniques such as the transverse rectus abdominis myocutaneous (TRAM) flap.21 The DIEP flap also lends itself particularly well to buried reconstruction due to its pliability and volume, allowing surgeons to fill the mastectomy envelope without requiring external skin paddles.11,17

As reconstructive strategies and microsurgical techniques continue to evolve, understanding the safety, efficacy, outcomes and patient satisfaction of buried free flap reconstructions has become increasingly important. This systematic review aims to critically evaluate the existing literature on buried autologous flap reconstruction following NSM, focusing on surgical techniques, postoperative monitoring methods, complication profiles, and patient-reported outcomes. By synthesising the current evidence, this review seeks to inform best practice and identify when buried flap reconstruction can be safely offered, addressing the key question of whether its success rates are comparable to routine non-buried DIEP reconstructions, thereby expanding the reconstructive repertoire of the microsurgeon in appropriately selected patients.

Methods

The review was conducted in line with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA).22

A search was carried out using the PubMed and Medline databases in November 2025. The keywords (“deep inferior epigastric perforator flap”, “DIEP”, “deep inferior epigastric flap”, “free flap”, “transverse upper gracilis”, “TUG”) AND (“bury”, “buried”, “burying”) AND (“autologous breast reconstruction”, “autologous reconstruction”, “breast reconstruction”, “nipple-sparing mastectomy”, “nipple sparing mastectomy”) were used in a Boolean search strategy using the operators “OR” and “AND”.

The abstracts retrieved from the search were imported into Covidence (https://www.covidence.org/), an online systematic review management platform. Two reviewers independently screened the abstracts for relevance and subsequently reviewed the full texts for eligibility based on the following inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Studies were included if they detailed operative techniques describing buried free autologous breast reconstructions following mastectomy, with patient populations greater than five. Studies were excluded if they were non-clinical papers, inconsistent or sparse operative details, incomplete data, letters and non-English publications.

Data extraction and analysis

Data were extracted after full-text review. Where no outcome measures were reported, no result (NR) was recorded. Data extracted from the studies included country, year, study type, patient demographics, flap types, flap indication, flap complications, flap failure rate, study aim, study protocol and key results. Extracted data were pooled as raw totals, means, or weighted averages. Levels of evidence were assigned to each study according to the Oxford Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine (OCEBM) Levels of Evidence.23

Statistical analysis

No formal statistical analysis was performed on the selected studies.

Search return

The search performed in November 2025 yielded a total of 75 studies. Fifteen full-text studies were assessed for eligibility, and 12 studies met the inclusion criteria (Fig. 1). Flap and surgical characteristics.

Complications

Total flap loss occurred in 1.5% of buried reconstructions, with partial flap loss reported in 0.3%. The overall return-to-theatre (RTT) rate was 7.5%. Other reported complications included fat necrosis in 4.1% of flaps, haematoma in 5.0%, seroma in 3.1%, infection in 4.5%, and wound dehiscence in 4.6%. Nipple necrosis occurred in 2.5% of buried flap reconstructions. There were no reports of tumour recurrence post-flap across any of the studies included.

Revision surgery

Revision surgery was common, with 49.2% of buried flaps undergoing at least one secondary procedure. Reported revision procedures most frequently included fat grafting, flap contour adjustments, and nipple reconstruction. Revision surgery was reported inconsistently across studies, with the majority reporting revision procedures at the flap level rather than per patient. To maximise data capture and comparability, revision data were therefore extracted and analysed at the flap level where reported.

Non-buried flaps

Across the same 12 studies, 468 patients underwent 612 non-buried autologous flap reconstructions. The mean patient age was 49.4 years, with an average BMI of 28.5 kg/m² (Table 2). Current smokers comprised 1.5% of patients. Unilateral reconstruction was performed in 50.7% of cases. Prior radiotherapy was more common in the non-buried cohort, reported in 32.6% of flaps. Reconstructions were predominantly immediate in 52.5% of cases, with 31.0% performed following nipple-sparing mastectomy. The mean follow-up duration was 32.5 months.

The DIEP flap was again the most frequently utilised flap (84.8%), followed by PAP flaps (6.9%) and muscle-sparing TRAM (MS-TRAM) flaps (3.9%) (Table 3).

Total flap loss occurred in 1.8% of non-buried reconstructions, with partial flap loss reported in 0.9%. The return-to-theatre rate was 9.1%. Anastomotic complications were reported in 2.6% of cases, comprising venous compromise in 2.0% and arterial compromise in 0.6% (Table 4)

Discussion

This systematic review demonstrates that buried autologous free-flap reconstruction following nipple-sparing mastectomy can be performed safely, with complication and flap survival rates comparable to non-buried reconstructions

Oncologic safety

Low rates of nipple necrosis and total flap loss across included studies support the oncologic and reconstructive safety of preserving the NAC when buried flap techniques are used in appropriately selected patients.

Preservation of the nipple-areolar complex did not appear to compromise reconstructive outcomes or increase complication rates. The findings support the oncologic safety of nipple preservation when buried flap techniques are used in appropriately selected patients.35 There were no reports of tumour recurrence post-flap across any of the studies included.

Flap monitoring

Despite the absence of an external skin paddle, buried flap reconstructions did not demonstrate increased rates of flap loss or unplanned return to theatre, suggesting that contemporary adjunctive monitoring strategies are effective when applied within structured postoperative protocols.

A range of monitoring techniques were employed across studies, most commonly implantable Doppler probes and venous flow couplers, with occasional use of sentinel skin paddles. However, there was no consistent evidence that any single modality conferred superior outcomes in terms of flap salvage or complication rates. Instead, differences in study design, flap composition, and reporting standards introduce substantial heterogeneity, limiting direct comparison between techniques and precluding identification of an optimal monitoring strategy.

Near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRO200NX) has also been proposed as an adjunct for buried flap monitoring. Although Tanaka et al. (2024) was excluded from quantitative analysis due to lack of patient-level data and its focus on monitoring methodology, it reported reliable detection of perfusion changes via tissue oxygenation indices.36 However, current evidence remains insufficient to determine its comparative effectiveness against established implantable monitoring techniques.

Comparison with non-buried reconstruction

Comparative data demonstrate similar rates of flap loss, vascular complications, and nipple necrosis between buried and non-buried autologous reconstructions, indicating that buried techniques do not confer additional reconstructive risk.

Differences observed in secondary outcomes, such as fat necrosis, infection, wound dehiscence, likely reflect variation in patient selection, reconstructive timing, and follow-up duration, rather than intrinsic differences in technique safety.

Fat necrosis

Buried flaps were associated with a higher reported rate of fat necrosis (4.1%vs 2.1%). However, this finding should be interpreted with caution because fat necrosis is frequently attributed to the flap, although it may also arise from residual native breast tissue or other surgical factors. Evidence from oncoplastic breast surgery demonstrates that fat necrosis is a multifactorial complication rather than one unique to flap tissue.37

Flap selection and reconstructive strategy

The predominance of DIEP flaps in buried reconstructions reflects their suitability for NSM, offering sufficient volume and pliability to recreate a natural breast mound without the need for an external skin paddle. Their ability to provide well-vascularised soft tissue while preserving abdominal musculature further supports their role as the preferred autologous option in this setting.

However, alternative perforator flaps, such as the PAP flap, are increasingly utilised, particularly in patients who are not ideal candidates for abdominal-based reconstruction. PAP flaps offer reliable vascular anatomy and are especially valuable in patients with limited abdominal tissue or prior abdominal surgery.38 This option is seen to be particularly advantageous in patients undergoing prophylactic mastectomies, such as those with BRCA mutations, who are often younger, slimmer, and may lack adequate abdominal donor tissue.39

Despite the expanding use of non-abdominal perforator flaps, there remains a relative paucity of high-quality comparative data evaluating outcomes between DIEP and alternative flaps in the context of buried reconstruction following NSM. Further prospective studies are needed to better define optimal flap selection and refine reconstructive strategies in this evolving field.

Advances in preoperative imaging have also contributed to the evolution of autologous breast reconstruction. Preoperative perforator mapping using computed tomography angiography (CTA), and less commonly magnetic resonance angiography (MRA), enables detailed visualisation of perforator anatomy, facilitating perforator selection and flap planning. Systematic reviews and clinical studies have demonstrated that CTA reduces operative time by improving the efficiency of perforator identification and dissection while enhancing preoperative surgical planning.40–41 Although preoperative imaging was not evaluated as an outcome in the studies included in this review, it has become an important adjunct to breast surgery and may further support the technical feasibility of both buried and non-buried free-flap reconstruction.

Aesthetic outcomes and PROMs

Although buried flap reconstruction is often advocated for aesthetic reasons, meaningful comparison of aesthetic outcomes is limited by sparse and inconsistent reporting of validated patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) such as BREAST-Q. Future research should prioritise the integration of standardised PROMs, such as BREAST-Q, into study outcomes to enable reliable comparisons and better inform clinical decision-making.

Sensation

In addition to oncological safety and reconstructive outcomes, sensory recovery represents an increasingly important yet underreported outcome. A pilot study by LuWang et al. (2023), excluded during screening, demonstrated earlier sensory recovery in neurotised DIEP flaps with externally inset skin paddles compared with buried DIEP flaps.42 This difference may reflect both direct nerve coaptation and more efficient peripheral nerve regeneration.43 However, sensory recovery following nipple-sparing mastectomy is complex. Anatomical studies have shown that the preserved breast skin envelope and NAC may retain or regain sensation through preservation of native intercostal nerve branches and spontaneous reinnervation, meaning postoperative sensation is not solely dependent on flap reinnervation. Consequently, differences in sensory recovery between buried and non-buried reconstructions likely reflect contributions from both flap innervation and recovery of the preserved native skin.44 Although the LuWang study was excluded from this review because it focused primarily on sensory outcomes rather than the predefined endpoints, its findings provide useful contextual insight into an important functional outcome.

Limitations

Interpretation of the current evidence is limited by predominantly retrospective study designs, heterogeneous monitoring techniques, and underreporting of oncologic and patient-reported outcomes. Retrospective design introduces selection bias and limits causal inference. There is significant heterogeneity in monitoring techniques and outcome reporting, with modalities inconsistently described, which limits meaningful comparisons and prevents identification of optimal postoperative surveillance protocols. Oncologic outcomes and long-term aesthetic satisfaction remain underreported; although complications and nipple necrosis rates are frequently described, long-term endpoints such as local recurrence and disease-free survival are rarely addressed, yet these are essential for informed patient decision-making. Additionally, PROMs such as BREAST-Q are infrequently reported, precluding robust assessment of aesthetic results and patient satisfaction. These limitations restrict definitive conclusions regarding optimal monitoring strategies and patient-centred outcomes.

Future directions

Prospective studies with standardised monitoring protocols and validated patient-reported outcome measures are required to better define best practice and refine patient selection for buried flap reconstruction

Conclusion

This systematic review demonstrates that buried autologous free-flap reconstruction following NSM is a safe and reliable reconstructive option in appropriately selected patients. Across the available literature, buried flap reconstructions achieved low total flap loss and vascular complication rates comparable to non-buried autologous reconstructions, supporting their technical feasibility and reconstructive safety. Importantly, rates of nipple necrosis and return to theatre were similarly low, reinforcing the reconstructive safety of preserving the NAC when buried flap techniques are employed within established selection criteria.

Despite the absence of an external skin paddle, monitoring strategies including implantable Doppler probes, venous flow couplers, and sentinel skin paddles appear sufficient for early detection of flap compromise, although heterogeneity in monitoring highlights the need for standardised postoperative protocols.

While aesthetic outcomes are often cited as a rationale for buried flap reconstruction, reporting of validated patient-reported outcome measures such as BREAST-Q is limited and inconsistent, preventing definitive conclusions regarding patient satisfaction.
