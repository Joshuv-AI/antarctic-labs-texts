Piezoelectric heterojunction/polyetherketoneketone composite with enhanced sonodynamic efficacy and nanozyme activities for anti-infection and encouraging neurovascular ossification
Authors: Shangyu Xie, Bowen Wang, Yang Zhou, Susu Ma, Zhengmao Guan, Jie Wei, Dejian Li, Yunfei Niu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTreatment of infected bone faces dual challenges of biofilm removal and pro-osteogenesis. Herein, a piezoelectric heterojunction of bismuth (Bi)-doped sodium niobate and oxygen-vacancy cerium oxide was constructed, and a heterojunction/polyetherketoneketone composite was prepared as a bone implanted material. The doping of Bi induced the lattice distortion and bandgap reduction of sodium niobate that remarkably enhanced piezoelectricity and sonodynamic efficiency, while oxygen-vacancies caused lattice distortion of cerium oxide that improved nanozyme activities. Under radiation of ultrasound (US), the piezoelectric effect of the composite enhanced sonodynamic performance and oxidant nanozyme activities that generated abundant reactive oxygen species (ROS) for eradicating bacteria and eliminating biofilm, and controlling infection, both in vitro and in vivo. Moreover, the piezoelectric effect not only enhanced the anti-oxidant nanozyme activities that scavenged excessive ROS for alleviating oxidative stress, but also stimulated M2 macrophage polarization that created an anti-inflammatory microenvironment for osteogenesis. Furthermore, the piezoelectric stimulation of composite boosted cellular responses (osteoblasts differentiation, angiogenesis and neurogenesis) in vitro, and promoted neuro-vascularized bone regeneration in vivo. Consequently, US-triggered piezoelectric effect of the composite displays promising potential for treatment of infected bone. Introduction

Piezoelectric materials can generate polarization charges by mechanical stress due to their non-centrosymmetric structures [1, 2]. The production of electrical stimulation by piezoelectric materials not only regulates cell behaviors and functions (e.g., differentiation, intracellular pathways activation, gene expression and protein secretion) but also promotes tissue regeneration, which exhibits great potential for promoting bone repair [1]. In recent decades, a growing number of piezoelectric materials (e.g., lithium niobate, barium titanate, potassium sodium niobate and zinc oxide) have been developed as promising candidates for bone implants [1, 3]. Bone regeneration is a complicated process that involves angiogenesis, neurogenesis and osteogenesis [4]. The lack of blood vessels limits oxygen and nutrition supply at the early stage of bone healing, thereby attenuating cell proliferation and differentiation as well as bone formation [4]. Concomitantly, nerve fibers precede blood vessels during growth, and influence bone cells by secreting neurotransmitters to stimulate osteogenesis [4]. Accordingly, in biomaterial design, the reconstruction of the neurovascular network should not be ignored, which can accelerate bone regeneration and avoid the delayed or incomplete bone healing.

Implant-related infection is a calamitous complication after orthopedic surgeries that leads to the failure of operations, joint deformity and even amputation [5]. Currently, the antibiotic therapy is far from satisfactory, and treatment of implant-related infections is still a challenge [5, 6]. Sonodynamic therapy (SDT) is regarded as a potential alternative strategy to prevent bacterial infection due to the high controllability and biosafety deep tissue penetration ability [5]. The efficiency of SDT mainly depends on the activation of sonosensitizers by ultrasound (US), which generates ROS through a series of redox reactions to kill bacteria [5]. However, the current sonosensitizers exhibit low efficiency of SDT owing to the inadequate ROS generation [6, 7].

Piezoelectric materials, as a new generation of sonosensitizers, have attracted considerable interest in improving the efficiency of ROS production for SDT [2, 8]. In response to the US, the piezoelectric materials generate a built-in electric field that significantly promotes charge carrier transfer and induces valence band bending for remarkably improving the efficiency of ROS production [6, 8]. The produced ROS interferes with bacterial activity, leading to destruction of proteins and lipids, and ultimately causing bacterial death [2, 8]. Accordingly, piezoelectric SDT is a promising strategy to treat bone tissue infection. However, the heterogeneity and adaptability of bacterial infections in bone tissue cause hurdles in SDT, making it is difficult to completely eradicate bacterial infections by SDT alone [6, 7]. Combination of SDT with other therapy technology to synergistically eliminate bacteria would be an effective strategy for improving the overall efficacy of infection control.

Nanozymes are nanomaterials with enzyme-like activities similarity to natural enzymes that have attracted much attention for biomedical applications, especially, it is a promising alternative therapy for various diseases (e.g., anti-tumor, anti-infection) [9]. Over the past 20 years, many nanozymes have been discovered to possess enzyme-like activities, which mainly include metal-based, carbon-based and metal sulfide/oxide-based materials [9]. The majority of nanozymes have ROS-production or ROS-scavenging capability originating from their intrinsic peroxidase (POD), oxidase (OXD), catalase (CAT) and superoxide dismutase (SOD) as well as glutathione peroxidase (GPx)-like activities [9, 10]. The nanozymes with ROS-production ability can kill bacteria for infection treatment, while the nanozymes with ROS-scavenger ability can alleviate oxidative stress and reduce inflammation for avoiding related diseases [9, 10]. Unlike traditional antibiotics, the nanozymes are less likely to cause bacterial resistance, and have significant potential for preventing infection [9]. Nevertheless, the nanozyme activities remain substantially much lower than those of natural enzymes, making it challenging to meet the requirements for controlling bacterial infection and boosting tissue regeneration in vivo [11].

Cerium (Ce) is a rare earth element that presents the valency states of Ce3+ and Ce4+ in its chemical compound [12]. The Ce-based materials are found to have enzyme-like activities, such as POD-like and CAT-like as well as superoxide SOD-like, which have been exploited for biomedical applications [12]. The reduced state of Ce3+ and oxidation state of Ce4+ can be converted to each other, and thereby leading to the strong oxidation–reduction ability [12]. For example, in acidic microenvironment, the Ce3+ can be oxidized to Ce4+, acting as POD-like nanozyme for converting hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) into hydroxyl radicals (·OH) [12]. However, in alkaline microenvironment, the Ce4+ can be reduced to Ce3+, acting as CAT-like nanozyme for converting H2O2 into oxygen (O2) [12]. Oxygen vacancies (Ov) can be created in oxygen-containing compounds through the detachment of oxygen atoms from the crystal lattice, and construction of oxygen vacancy is a common method to improve the nanozyme activity [13].

Sodium niobate (NaNbO₃, NNO) is a piezoelectric material with good biocompatibility that has been investigated for bone repair [14–16]. However, the low piezoelectricity and wide band gap (~ 3.4 eV) of NNO limit its application for SDT [14]. The doping of metal ions into piezoelectric material not only improve piezoelectricity but also enhance SDT [17]. Bismuth (Bi) is recognized as a "green" metal with a century-long history of clinical application, such as bismuth-based agents for treating gastric diseases, demonstrating good biosafety [18]. The Bi ions with a unique 6s2 lone pair of electrons induces distortion of NNO, thereby generating a significant intrinsic dipole moment [19]. Therefore, it is expected that doping of Bi ions into NNO improve the piezoelectricity and SDT. Moreover, the construction of heterojunctions is also an important strategy to improve the piezoelectricity and piezoelectric-enhanced SDT [17]. The formation of heterojunction creates built-in electric field that remarkably promotes separation and migration of electron–hole (e−-h+) pairs, and thereby improving efficacy of SDT [17]. As a high-performance polymer, polyetherketoneketone (PEKK) is applied to develop orthopedic implants for spinal fusion, joint replacements, and cranio-maxillofacial procedures due to its good biocompatibility, biostability and high mechanical strength as well as its elastic modulus is the same as of human bone [20, 21].

Herein, Bi-doped sodium niobate (BNO) with improved piezoelectricity, and oxygen vacancy cerium oxide (CeO2-x) with enhanced enzyme-like activities were firstly synthesized, respectively. Secondly, a heterojunction of BNO/CeO2-x (BN@CO) was fabricated for preventing infection by piezoelectric-enhanced both SDT and enzyme-like activities as well as promoting osteogenesis by production of electric signals. Finally, BN@CO was incorporated into polyetherketoneketone (PEKK) to prepare PEKK/BN@CO composite (PBCC) for controlling infection and accelerating bone repair (Scheme 1).

Results and discussion

Characterization of BN@CO

SEM (Fig. 1a, b, c, Fig S1a, b), TEM (Fig. 1d, e, f, Fig S1c, d) and DLS (Fig. S2a, b, c, d, Fig. 1l) display that CeO2-x with the particle size ranging from 25 to 50 nm exhibits an irregular spherical morphology, while BNO with the particle size ranging from 230–650 nm displays rectangular morphology. The CeO2-x particles are deposited on the BNO surface, forming the heterojunction of BN@CO with the particle size ranging from 300 to 730 nm. There are no obvious differences in morphology and particle size of CeO2-x and BNO compared to CeO2 and NNO. HRTEM images (Fig. S1e, f, Fig. 1g, h) show that compared to NNO (0.391 nm), the (110) interplanar spacing of BNO increases to 0.398 nm due to the substitution of Na+ (102 pm) by Bi3+ (103 pm) ions according to the similar-radius substitution principle, thereby causing the lattice distortion [22, 23]. Compared with the interplanar spacings (220) and (200) for CeO2 (0.191 nm and 0.271 nm), CeO2-x (0.195 nm and 0.274 nm) exhibits increase due to lattice distortion caused by oxygen vacancy (Ov) [24, 25]. Figure 1i reveals the distinct grain boundaries (yellow dashed lines) between BNO and CeO2-x in BN@CO. Amplified HRTEM images (Fig. 1i1, i2) reveal that the interplanar spacing (220 and 200) for CeO2-x, and (110) for BNO exhibit no changes in BN@CO. EDS mappings (Fig. 1j) display that the Ce, Bi, O, Na and Nb elements are distributed on BN@CO.

EPR (Fig. 1k) shows that compared with CeO2, CeO2-x exhibits a signal peak of Ov at g = 2.003, indicating formation of Ov [24]. High-resolution XPS of O 1 s of CeO2-x (Fig. S3) displays that the peaks at 529.42 eV, 531.53 eV, and 533.29 eV are assigned to the lattice oxygen, defect oxygen and adsorbed oxygen, and the content of Ov for CeO2-x is 31.52% calculated by the peak area of XPS [24, 26].

XRD (Fig. 1m) shows that the peaks at 22.68° (110), 32.46°, 46.35°, 46.79°, 52.60° and 57.96° in NNO (PDF #73–0803) move to 22.52°, 32.27°, 46.16°, 46.55°, 52.36° and 57.76° in BNO due to doping of Bi inducing lattice distortion [27], which exhibit no obvious change in BN@CO. No characteristic peaks disappear, and no new peaks emerge in BNO, indicating no change in phase structure. The peaks at 28.54°, 33.07° (200), 47.47° (220), and 56.33 in CeO2 (PDF #81–0792) shift to 28.35°, 32.84°, 47.20° and 56.21° in CeO2-x due to the presence of Ov [24], which exhibit no obvious change in BN@CO, indicating that the heterojunction does not alter the phase structures of BNO and CeO2-x. Magnified XRD (Fig. 1m) reveals that the full width at half maximum of peak (110) for BNO (0.38°) is wider than that of NNO (0.26°), and the peak (200) of CeO2-x (0.40°) is wider than that of CeO2 (0.30°). Fig. S4 demonstrates that the grain sizes of NNO, BNO, CeO2, CeO2-x are 32.75 nm, 25.78 nm, 31.25 nm and 23.37 nm. Figure 1n shows the lattice distortion rate for MNO and CeO2-x of 0.16% and 0.10%.

XPS (Fig. 1o) demonstrates that the elements for CeO2-x (Ce and O) and BNO (Bi, O, Nb and Na) are observed in BN@CO. Figure 1p indicates that the binding energies of Ce3+ (903.83 eV and 885.52 eV) and Ce4+ (900.73 eV and 881.91 eV) in CeO2 shift to low values at Ce3+ (903.39 eV and 884.85 eV) and Ce 4+ (900.34 eV and 881.62 eV) in CeO2-x [28, 29]. The contents of Ce3+ (from 10.91% to 19.13%) and Ce4⁺ (from 89.09% to 80.87%) exhibit an obvious change due to presence of Ov [24]. After formation of BN@CO, the binding energies of Ce3+ (902.77 eV and 884.01 eV) and Ce4+ (899.49 eV and 881.06 eV) further shift to low values, which causes the contents of Ce3+ (26.13%) increase while Ce4+ (73.87%) decrease [30, 31], indicating an electron gained by CeO2-x in BN@CO. The change in content of multivalent ionic for Ce provides the potential for enzyme-like activities of BN@CO due to the electrons gained by CeO2-x [30].

Figure 1q demonstrates that the binding energies of Bi3+ (163.25 eV and 157.95 eV) in BNO shift to high values (164.36 eV and 158.99 eV) in BN@CO [30, 32]. High resolution XPS of Na 1s (Fig. 1r) reveal that the binding energies of Na⁺ (1070.90 eV) in NNO shifts to high value (1071.45 eV) in BNO [34, 35]. Compared with NNO, the peak intensity and area of BNO decrease, indicating that Bi ions occupy the Na sites [36]. After the formation of BN@CO, the binding energies of Na⁺ move to high value at 1072.17 eV in BN@CO. Figure 1s demonstrates that the binding energies of Nb5+ (207.06 eV and 209.77 eV) in NNO shift to high values (207.31 eV and 210.02 eV) in BNO [33, 34]. Compared with NNO, the peak intensity and area of BNO show no significant changes, indicating that Bi ions do not occupy the Nb sites. After formation of BN@CO, the binding energies of Nb5+ in BNO shift to high values (207.67 eV and 210.44 eV) [30]. XPS fitting calculations (Table S1) indicate that the doping content of Bi in BNO is 2.03%.

Compared to NNO (17.68%), the content of Na in BNO decreases to 15.48%, while the contents of Nb and O exhibit no change. Compared to BNO, the binding energies of Bi 4f, Na 1 s, and Nb 3d in BN@CO shift toward the high region, indicating the electron loss occurs in the BNO phase of BN@CO [32]. After formation of the heterojunction, the valence states of Bi3+, Na+ and Nb5+ ions do not change, while the binding energies for different elements exhibit slight shifts, revealing the strong interactions between CeO2-x and BNO that promotes the generation of e−-h+ pairs.

Figure 1t, u demonstrate that the work function of BNO (3.882 eV) is lower than that of CeO2-x (4.508 eV). Accordingly, in the BN@CO, the electrons in BNO tend to spontaneously move toward CeO2-x until the Fermi level equilibrates [37]. The valence band (VB) and conduction band (CB) of BNO bend upward because of charge depletion, while the VB and CB of CeO2-x bend downward due to charge accumulation, which generates an internal electric field (IEF) for accelerating the separation of carriers [32]. DFT simulations (Fig. 1v) reveal the simulated differential charge distribution for BN@CO. Upon contact, the electrons of BNO are attracted to CeO2-x due to the strong electron affinity, leading to the accumulation of more electrons (yellow) on CeO2-x side [49].

Piezoelectricity and piezoelectric-enhanced sonodynamic performance

Figure 2a, d, g reveal that compared with NNO, the contrast between bright and dark regions of BN@CO is significantly enhanced, followed by BNO, indicating gradual improvement of piezoelectricity [38]. Figure 2b, e, h and Fig. 2c, f, i reveal approximately 180° phase reversal when the direction of the applied voltage changes, confirming the piezoelectricity of all samples [38]. Compared to NNO (28.93 pm/V), the piezoelectric coefficient (d33) of BNO (72.94 pm/V) significantly increases due to the lattice distortion induced by doping of Bi ions that enhances the non-centrosymmetric structure, which affects the local electric dipole moment and macroscopic polarization [39]. Compared with BNO, the increase of d33 for BN@CO (127.34 pm/V) is attributed to the construction of heterojunction that breaks the central symmetry and induces polar symmetry [40].

Piezoelectricity and sonodynamic performance: (a, d, g) PFM images of amplitude and phase mappings, and (b, e, h) phase hysteresis and (c, f, i) butterfly loops curves of various samples; (j) EIS plots and (k) ultrasonic current density; UV–Vis-NIR absorption spectra of (l) MB and (m) DPBF, US parameters: 1.5W/cm2, 1 MHz, 50% duty cycle; (n) Mechanism of piezoelectric-enhanced SDP of BN@CO

Figure S5a, b, c, d, e reveals that compared to NNO (3.24 eV), the band gap (Eg) for BNO (2.89 eV) reduces due to doping of Bi ions, while compared to CeO2 (2.81 eV), the Eg for CeO2-x (2.28 eV) reduces due to the introduction of Ov. Compared to BNO and CeO2-x, the reduced Eg for BN@CO (2.65 eV) implies that the energy barrier of the heterojunction decreases, which facilitates acoustic-electronic excitation [39]. Fig. S5f, g, h, i demonstrates that the valence band (EVB) of NNO, CeO2, BNO and CeO2-x are 2.50 eV, 2.38 eV, 2.27 eV and 3.14 eV, respectively. Based on the formula (EVB = Eg + ECB) [41], the conduction band (ECB) of BNO and CeO2-x are -0.62 eV and 0.86 eV, respectively.

Separation and migration of e−-h+ pairs are the decisive factors for improving sonodynamic performance (SDP) [41]. Figure 2j shows that compared with NNO and CeO2, the semicircle radius for BNO and CeO2-x decreases significantly, indicating the reduction of resistance to charge transfer due to doping of Bi ions and introduction of Ov [41]. The arc radius for BN@CO is the smallest, indicating the lowest resistance of charge transfer, thereby accelerating e−-h+ separation. Figure 2k shows that with US, the ultrasonic current density of BNO (0.95 μA/cm2) and CeO2-x (1.10 μA/cm2) significantly increases compared with NNO (0.41μA/cm2) and CeO2 (0.76 μA/cm2), indicating the doping of Bi ion and introduction of Ov improve the ability to separation and migration of e−-h+ pairs [41]. Further, the ultrasonic current density of BN@CO (1.66 μA/cm2) obviously increases, indicating the significant improvement of migration efficiency of e−-h+.

Figure 2l, m show that with US, compared to NNO and CeO2, the absorbance of MB (production of ·OH) and DPBF (production of·O2−) for BNO, CeO2-x and BN@CO obviously decrease, and BN@CO exhibits the lowest, indicating the significant improvement of ·OH and ·O2− production. ESR further confirms that with US, compared with NNO and CeO2, the peaks of production of ·OH (Fig. S6a) and production of·O2− (Fig. S6b) for BNO, CeO2-x and BN@CO markedly increase, and BN@CO is the highest, indicating the remarkable improvement of SDP. Fig. S6c shows that the absorbance of Rh B for BN@CO gradually decreases, indicating that ROS production increases with the US intensity increasing. With US (0.5 W/cm2), BN@CO exhibits very low ROS production, while with US (1.5 W/cm2), BN@CO exhibits high ROS production. Fig. S6d demonstrates that the absorbance of Rh B for BN@CO gradually decreases with the increase of US irradiation time (1.5 W/cm2), demonstrating that the ROS generated by BN@CO is time-dependent.

Figure 2n demonstrates the mechanism of piezoelectric-enhanced SDP of BN@CO. With US, BN@CO generates a polarization electric field (PEF) that accelerates the electron transfer at the interface of heterojunction, and promotes separation of e− and h+ [42]. With the combined effect of IEF and PEF, the e− in the CB of CeO2-x rapidly transfer to the VB of BNO and recombine with the h+ of BNO [32, 36]. While the h+ in the VB of CeO2-x and the e− in the CB of BNO participate in the redox reactions, and thereby generating ·OH from H2O, and·O2− from O2, respectively [42].

Figure 3a demonstrates that compared with CeO2, the absorbance of oxidized TMB (production of.OH) for CeO2-x increases, indicating the enhancement of POD-like activity. Compared with CeO2-x, the POD-like activity of BN@CO further increases. With US (1.5 W/cm2), the POD-like activity for BN@CO significantly improves because the piezoelectric effect accelerates electron transfer from Ce3+ to Ce4+(1) [43].

Figure S7a, b show that at pH = 5.5, the Km (0.52 mM) decreases, while Vm (16.6 × 10–8 M/s) of BN@CO + US increases compared with BN@CO (0.87 mM and 9.09 × 10–8 M/s), indicating an enhanced affinity for the substrate (TMB). At pH = 5.5, the Km (18.27 mM) decrease, while Vm (17.24 × 10–8 M/s) for BN@CO + US increase compared with BN@CO (24.26 mM, 11.24 × 10–8 M/s), indicating improvement of affinity to the substrate (H2O2). The improvements of affinity for BN@CO to the substrates are ascribed to the piezoelectric effect, which accelerates electron transfer, thereby facilitating valence state transition of Ce ions and reduces the binding energy between nanozymes and substrates [44].

Figure 3b reveals that the production of.OH for BN@CO decreases with the pH increasing, indicating that the POD-like activity is pH-sensitive. At pH = 7.4, the POD-like activity for BN@CO is very low, even no obvious change with US (0.5 W/cm2). Figure 3c indicates that compared with CeO2, production of ·O2− for CeO2-x increases, and further increase for BN@CO, demonstrating OXD-like activity (2). With US (1.5 W/cm2), production of ·O2− for BN@CO significantly improves, indicating piezoelectric-enhanced OXD-like activity. Figure S7c demonstrates that at pH = 7.4, the OXD-like activity for BN@CO is very low, with no significant change with US (0.5 W/cm2) [43].

Figure 3d shows that compared with CeO2, the O2 concentration of CeO2-x improves, indicating the enhancement of CAT-like activity. Compared to CeO2-x, the O2 concentration for BN@CO slightly decreases due to the low content of Ce4+ (3) [43]. With US (0.5 W/cm2), the O2 concentration for BN@CO further increases, indicating piezoelectric-enhanced CAT-like activity. Figure S7d shows that The Km (33.75 mM) decrease, while Vm (0.75 mg mL−1 min−1) for BN@CO + US increase compared with BN@CO (49.32 mM, 0.51 mg mL−1 min−1), indicating improvement of affinity to the substrate (H2O2).

Figure 3e reveals that the O2 concentration for BN@CO increases with the increase of pH, indicating that the CAT-like activity is pH-sensitive. At pH = 5.5, with US (1.5 W/cm2), the O2 concentration for BN@CO obviously increases. Figure 3f shows that compared with CeO2, the absorbance of GSH for CeO2-x significantly decreases, indicating the enhancement of GPx-like activity (4). The GPx-like activity of BN@CO is lower than that of CeO2-x, while with US (0.5 W/cm2), the GPx-like activity for BN@CO significantly enhances, indicating piezoelectric-enhanced GPx-like activity [45].

Figure 3g demonstrates that the absorbance of GSH for BN@CO gradually decrease with pH increasing, indicating the GPx-like activity is pH-sensitive. At pH = 5.5, the GPx-like activity is low, and slightly enhances with US (1.5 W/cm2). Figure 3h shows that compared with CeO2, the formazan concentration for CeO2-x decreases, indicating the enhancement of SOD-like activity (5 and 6). Compared with CeO2-x, the SOD-like activity for BN@CO further enhances due to improvement of Ce3+/Ce4+ ratio [12]. With US (0.5W/cm2), the SOD-like activity of BN@CO remarkably improves compared with BN@CO. Moreover, H₂O₂ generated by SOD-like activity serves as the substrate for CAT/GPx-like, thereby formation of the SOD-CAT/GPx cascade reaction for enhancement of ROS elimination [45].

Figure S7e shows that at pH = 5.5, the SOD-like activity for BN@CO is very low with US (1.5 W/cm2). Figure 3i demonstrates the mechanism of piezoelectric-enhanced enzyme-like activity: At pH = 5.5 (bacterial infection microenvironment), with US (1.5 W/cm2), piezoelectric-enhanced POD-like and OXD-like activities through promoting the electron transfer from Ce3+ to Ce4+, leading to increase of ROS (·OH,·O2−) production [46]. Moreover, the piezoelectric-enhanced CAT-like activity (promotion of electron transfer from Ce4+ to Ce3+) provides O₂ for SDP. However, at pH = 7.4 (bone regenerated microenvironment), with US (0.5 W/cm2), piezoelectric-enhanced CAT/GPx/SOD-like through promoting the electron transfer between Ce4+ and Ce3+, resulting in improvement of ROS elimination and O₂ generation [47].

Characterization of PBCC

Figure 3j, k, l, m shows the surface morphology of samples. Compared to PEKK with a smooth surface, PBNC, PCOC and PBCC exhibit roughened surfaces, with many particles (e.g., BNO, CeO2-x and BN@CO). Fig. S8a shows that Na, Nb, O, Bi and Ce elements are distributed on PBCC. Fig. S8b reveals that the characteristic peaks of dibenzone group (1650 cm−1, 1490 cm−1 and 920 cm−1) and C = C stretching vibration of benzene ring (1586 cm−1) of PEKK appear in all samples [48]. The characteristic peaks of Nb–O bond (717 cm−1) of BNO appear in both PBNC and PBCC [49]. The characteristic peak at 488 cm−1 for Ce–O bond of CeO2-x is not obviously observed in PCOC and PBCC due to the masking effect of the characteristic peaks of PEKK [50].

Figure S8c shows that the characteristic peaks at 18.02°, 19.27°, 22.56°, 28.29° of PEKK almost disappear in both PBNC and PBCC due to the high/strong peaks of BNO and BN@CO. The characteristic peaks of BNO are observed in PBNC (22.56°, 32.34°, etc.) and PBCC (22.59°, 32.28°, etc.), and the characteristic peaks of CeO2-x is observed in PCOC (28.38°, 47.27°, 56.30°, etc.) and PBCC (28.39°, 47.32°, 56.17°, etc.). Figure 3n shows that the introductions of BNO, CeO2-x and BN@CO significantly enhance the compressive strength of PBNC (161.00 ± 7.00 MPa), PCOC (156.67 ± 8.62 MPa) and PBCC (172.00 ± 6.49 MPa) compared to PEKK (136.67 ± 8.24 MPa). The compressive strengths of PBCC falls within the range of human cortical bone (100 ~ 180 MPa), suggesting that PBCC can provide sufficient mechanical support and exhibit biomechanical compatibility with human bone [21, 51].

Figure 3o demonstrates that the d33 values for PBNC and PBCC are 2.75 ± 0.20 pC/N and 4.10 ± 0.30 pC/N, indicating good piezoelectricity. Figure 3p, q reveals that with US (0.5 W/cm2), the open-circuit voltage and short-circuit current for PBCC (0.62 V and 595 nA) are obviously higher PBNC (0.33 V and 353 nA), indicating the improved piezoelectricity of PBCC.

The piezoelectric coefficient (d33) of natural bone typically ranges from 0.7 to 10 pC/N [1], and the d33 of PBCC (4.10 pC/N) falls within this range, which can provide a suitable electrophysiological microenvironment for bone repair. Moreover, the piezoelectric materials with piezoelectric voltage (0.1–10 μA) and current (40–500 mV) effectively stimulate cellular responses [1, 52], herein, PBCC with appropriate piezoelectricity (open-circuit voltage and short-circuit current) also basically falls within this range, thereby promoting cellular responses. Fig. S8d shows that PBCC consistently achieves a significant reduction in Rh B absorbance over 5 consecutive cycles, demonstrating its stability under the US intensity of 1.5 W/cm2. Fig. S8e, f illustrates that the open circuit-voltage and short-circuit current remain stable throughout 5 cycles, confirming the durability of PBCC under the US intensity of 0.5 W/cm2. The dual stability ensures that the performance of PBCC will not undergo significant attenuation during long-term antibacterial and bone regeneration applications.

Antibacterial properties in vitro

Figure 4a, S9a and Fig. 4f, S9d show that many S. aureus and E. coli are observed for PEKK, PBNC, PCOC, and PBCC, while a significant reduction in bacterial counts for PBCC + H2O2 and PBCC + US. With US and H2O2, the bacterial reduction rates of PBCC are the highest (S. aureus: 95.20 ± 1.94%, and E. coli: 97.73 ± 1.36%), indicating excellent antibacterial effect. SEM images of S. aureus (Fig. 4b) and E. coli (Fig. S9b) demonstrate that the bacterial structure for PEKK, PBNC, PCOC, and PBCC remains intact. With H2O2 or US alone, some bacteria are damaged for PBCC. With US and H2O2, most bacteria are damaged for PBCC. In summary, the US-driven piezoelectric effect of PBCC enhances the SOD/CAT/GPx-like activities for eliminating ROS, alleviating hypoxia, and improving MMP, thereby avoiding mitochondrial damage for ATP synthesis.

Our results demonstrate that in the bacterial infection/acidic microenvironment, the US-driven piezoelectric effect of PBCC significantly improves both SDP and oxidative enzyme-like activities that produce abundant ROS for synergistical controlling infection. In the alkaline microenvironment, the piezoelectric effect of PBCC not only enhances antioxidant enzyme-like activities to eliminate ROS for alleviating oxidative stress, but also produces electrical signals for promoting neovascularized bone regeneration. Although PBCC exhibits significant potential for infected bone repair, further exploration is necessary: the dense samples of PBCC were applied to repair bone defects, the porous scaffolds for bone ingrowth to match the bone repair in three dimensions needs to be studied. Moreover, the femoral condylar defects of healthy rat models were utilized in this study, which cannot reflect general clinical conditions (such as diabetes mellitus and osteoporosis). Consequently, the therapeutic efficacy in the pathological microenvironments should be systematic evaluated. In the future study, the large bone defect in large animal models (such as sheep and dog) should be used to further assess regenerative capacity of PBCC in vivo, which can promote the clinical translation of this composite.

Conclusion

We developed a piezoelectric heterojunction/PEKK composite with enzyme-like activities for neuro-vascularized bone regeneration. Under the acidic microenvironment of bacterial infection, the US-triggered piezoelectric effect of the composite elevated both sonodynamic efficiency and cascade reaction of CAT/POD/OXD-like activities that produced a large amount of ROS for synergistically killing bacteria/eliminating biofilms both in vitro and in vivo. After infection controlling, the piezoelectric effect of the composite enhanced the cascade reaction of SOD/CAT/GPx-like activities that effectively scavenges ROS for alleviation of oxidative stress. Further, the piezoelectric stimulation of the composite not only facilitated M2 macrophage polarization that created an anti-inflammatory microenvironment for osteogenesis, but also promoted osteoblastic differentiation as well as coupling of osteogenesis, angiogenesis and neurogenesis, and thereby accelerating neurovascularized bone formation in vivo. This study provides a new perspective of piezoelectric materials with enzyme-like activities for treatment of infected bone, and it is expected to promote the application of piezoelectric biomaterials and offer new insights for orthopedic medicine.

Materials and methods

Reagents and chemicals

Niobium oxide (Nb2O5) and cerium(IV) oxide (CeO2) were purchased from Shanghai Xiaohuang Nanotechnology Co., Ltd. polyetherketoneketone (PEKK) powder was purchased from Arkema. Bismuth(III) nitrate pentahydrate (Bi(NO3)3·5H2O), cerium(IV) nitrate (Ce(NO3)4), sodium borohydride (NaBH4) and sodium hydroxide (NaOH), rhodamine B (Rh B), methylthionine chloride (MB), 1,3-diphenylisobenzofuran (DPBF), cobalt(II) chloride (CoCl2) were from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. 5,5-Dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-oxide (DMPO), 5,5’-dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB), tris(4,7-diphenyl-1,10-phenanthroline)ruthenium(II) dichloride ([Ru(dpp)3]Cl2) were from Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. 3,3’,5,5’-Tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) was purchased from Shanghai Zhonghe Chemical Technology Co., Ltd. Glutathione (GSH) was purchased from Shanghai Kaiwei Chemical Technology Co., Ltd. Bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (BMSC) cells, rat schwann cells 96 (RSC96) cells, pheochromocytoma cells 12 (PC-12) cells and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVEC) cells were from Wuhan procell Life Technology Co., Ltd. Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus, ATCC 29213) and Escherichia coli (E. coli, ATCC 25922) were from American Type Culture Collection. Ultrasound diagnostic instrument (50% duty cycle, 1 MHz) was from Shenzhen Welld Medical Electronics Co., Ltd.

Fabrication of samples

Bi-doped NaNbO3 (BNO) was prepared by adding Nb2O5 (1.0 g) and Bi(NO3)3·5H2O (0.089 g) into 50 mL NaOH solution (8 mol/L) and stirred for 2 h. Thereafter, the solution was hydrothermally reacted in a stainless steel reactor at 160 ℃ for 12 h. Subsequently, the product was centrifuged (8000 r, 5 min), washed 3 times with distilled water and ethanol, and then dried for 12 h at 60°C. NaNbO3 (NNO) was prepared by the same method as a control. Cerium oxide with oxygen vacancies (CeO2-x) was prepared by mixing 1 g CeO2 and NaBH4, and calcined at 400 °C (in a tube furnace) under argon atmosphere for 1 h. The product was added to a beaker with 100 mL of deionized water and stirred overnight to remove unreacted NaBH4. The suspension was centrifugally (8000 r) for 5 min and washed 3 times with distilled water, and dried for12 h (at 60 ℃).

Heterojunction of BNO and CeO2-x (BN@CO) was prepared by mixing BNO (0.30 g) and CeO2-x (0.10 g) in a mortar, and the mixture was ground in the presence of anhydrous ethanol for 2 h, and calcined in a tube furnace at 400 °C under an argon atmosphere for 1 h. Before use, the samples were polarized by corona polarization. PBCC was prepared by mixing BN@CO powder (1.972 g) and PEKK powder (0.84 g), and pressed into pellets (Φ10 mm × 2 mm) and columnar composites (Φ10 mm × 12 mm and Φ2 mm × 5 mm). The composites were then calcined at 345 °C for 2 h in an argon atmosphere within a tube furnace. After sintering, the sample surfaces were treated (grinding) with sandpaper for exposure of BN@CO particles. Subsequently, the samples were rinsed with deionized water and ethanol, and dried at 60 °C for 12 h. PEKK, PEKK/BNO composite (PBNC) and PEKK/CeO2-x composite (PCOC) were prepared using the same method. Before use, the samples were polarized by corona polarization.

Characterization of samples

Physicochemical properties

Morphology, particle size, elemental distribution, interplanar spacing, chemical composition, valence band and compressive strength were analyzed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Hitachi S-4800, Japan) with energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS), transmission electron microscope (TEM, JEOL JEM-2100F, Japan), zeta potential analyzer (Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS90, UK), X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (XPS, Thermo Scientific K-alpha,USA), Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (FTIR, Nicolet is50, USA) and Universal testing machine (E44.034, MTS, USA). The crystal structures of samples were analyzed by X-ray diffractometer (XRD, D/max2550VB/PC, Japan), and grain size and the lattice distortion rate was determined using the Scherrer equation and Williamson-Hall analysis [65]. ESR spectra of free radicals was analyzed by electron paramagnetic resonance spectrometer (ESR, EMX-8/2.7, USA). Band gap of samples was calculated according to the Kubelka–Munk (K–M) equation based on UV–vis-DRS spectra (Lambda 950, USA) [66]. The work function and charge density difference plot were obtained from density functional theory (DFT) calculations. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) and transient ultrasonic response (I-t) curves of samples (US of 1.5 W/cm2, 1 MHz 50% duty cycle) were characterized by electrochemical workstation (CHI660E, China). Butterfly loop, phase hysteresis curve, and corresponding phase and amplitude maps were measured using piezoelectric response force microscope (PFM, NTEGRA, Russia) with applied voltage ranging from -10 V to + 10 V. The piezoelectric coefficient (d33) values of samples were calculated by using the corresponding slope of butterfly loop [67]. The d33 of PEKK based composites were tested by quasi-static d33 meter (ZJ-3A + , IACAS, China). Open-circuit voltage and short-circuit current of samples (US of 0.5 W/cm2, 1 MHz 50% duty cycle) were detected by oscilloscope (TBS1102C, Tektronix, US) and amplifier (SR570, Sranford, US).

Detection of ROS and enzyme-like activities

For detection of ROS and enzyme-like activities, the concentrations of BNO, CeO2-x, NNO and BN@CO were 3 mg/10 mL, 1 mg/10 mL mg, 3 mg/10 mL mg and 4 mg/10 mL, respectively. US parameter was 0.5/1.5 W/cm2, 1 MHz 50% duty cycle. ROS generation: Samples were dispersed in the solution of 10 mL MB (·OH, 5 mg/L) or DPBF (·O₂⁻, 5 mg/L), or 10 mL RhB (10 mg/L) solution. After US irradiation (1.5 W/cm2, 10 min), the supernatant was analyzed using UV–Vis-NIR spectrometer (Lambda 950, USA), and signals of ·OH and ·O₂⁻ were captured by ESR using DMPO solution (1 M).

POD-like or OXD-like activities: Samples were dispersed in 10 mL TMB solution (1.2 mM) with various pH (4.5, 5.5, 6.5, 7.4) with/without 30 mM hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). After reacting for 10 min with/without US irradiation, and the supernatant was analyzed using UV–vis-NIR spectrometer. Vm and Km were obtained by varying the substrate concentrations (TMB and H2O2) and calculated according to the Lineweaver–Burk double-reciprocal equation [68]. CAT-like activity: Samples were dispersed in 50 mL H2O2 solution (100 mM) with various pH (4.5, 5.5, 6.5 and 7.4). Dissolved oxygen content was recorded every 10 min with/without US irradiation using the dissolved oxygen analyzer (JPB-607A, China) for 60 min. Vm and Km were obtained by varying the concentration of H2O2. GPx-like activity: Samples were dispersed in 10 mL GSH solution (100 μM) with various pH (4.5, 5.5, 6.5, 7.4). After reacting for 3 h with/without US irradiation (10 min of US/60 min), the solution was centrifuged. Then DTNB solution (3 mg/mL) was added to the supernatant to react for 1 min. The supernatant was analyzed using UV–vis-NIR spectrometer. By using total SOD activity assay kit (WST-8 method) (Beyotime, China), SOD-like activity was determined. Samples were dispersed in 10 mL SOD solution (pH = 5.5, 7.4; US irradiation for 5 min).

Antibacterial properties in vitro

Bacterial suspensions of S. aureus and E. coli were adjusted to 1 × 108 CFU/mL. The samples were co-cultured with the bacteria for 24 h. Then, for H2O2-related groups, H2O2 (1 mM) was co-cultured with bacteria for 4 h. For US-related groups, US parameters were 1.5 W/cm2, 1 MHz, 50% duty cycle, 15 min. After different treatment, bacteria were further co-cultured with samples for 6 h. Bacterial suspension from each group were collected for 1 × 104-fold dilution, 10 μL of bacterial solution was uniformly applied to the luria–bertani (LB) agar plats. After incubation for 24 h at 37 ℃, the agar dish was photographed using digital camera. The bacterial colonies were quantified using ImageJ, and antibacterial rate was calculated [64]. Bacteria were fixed with 4% glutaraldehyde overnight, rinsed 3 times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and then gradient ethanol (10, 30, 50, 70, 90 and 100%) was used for dehydration, and observed by SEM. Bacterial supernatant were collected for each group by centrifugation (5 min, 12,000 r,) and incubated for 30 min using BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, China), and the concentration of protein was calculated. The bacteria were stained 30 min by utilizing 2’,7’-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) probe (Beyotime, China), and then treated with US/H2O2. The intra-bacterial ROS fluorescence was observed with confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM, LEICA TCS SP8, Germany).

Bacterial suspensions (1 × 108 CFU) were placed in 24-well plates (1 mL/well), incubated statically for 48 h at 37 ℃ incubator to form bacterial biofilms. After different treatment, 0.1% crystal violet (Solarbio, China) was added to each well for 30 min, and washed 3 times with PBS, and photographed using digital camera. Subsequently, 33% glacial acetic acid was used to dissolve the crystal violet, and the OD value at 590 nm was analyzed using microplate reader to calculate the residual biomass of the biofilm [69]. The bacterial biofilms were further stained with SYTO9 probe (Fushen, China) for 30 min, and then observed by CLSM.

Cellular responses in vitro

BMSC, HUVEC, RSC96 and PC-12 cells were cultured in alpha minimal essential medium /dulbecco’s modified eagle’s medium (αMEM/DMEM) medium (10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin), and incubated in a cell culture incubator (37 °C, 5% CO2), and the medium was changed every 2 d. Cells (1 × 104 cells/well) were seeded into 24-well plates until cell attachment. For H2O2-related groups, H2O2 (100 μM) was added to the medium and co-cultured with cells for 12 h. Then samples were added to the medium, co-cultured and given US irradiation (0.5 W/cm2, 1 min, 1 MHz, 50% duty cycle, 3 times/d).

Cytotoxicity assay and antioxidant properties

Four kinds of cells were seeded into 96-well plates (1 × 104 cells/well). After cell attachment, the original medium was replaced by the extracts of PEKK, PBNC, PCOC and PBCC (extraction ratio: 0.2 g/mL, extraction time: 24 h). After co-culturing for 24 h, the cell viability was calculated according to the instruction of cell counting kit-8 (Beyotime, China). Intracellular ROS level and mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP): After co-cultured and different treatment for 24 h, the cells were stained with DCFH-DA probe and JC-1 staining (Beyotime, China) for 30 min to determine the ROS level and MMP, respectively, and observed with CLSM. Intracellular O2 production: After cell attachment, CoCl2 (500 μM) was added to induce hypoxia. After co-cultured and different treatment for 24 h, cells were stained with [Ru(DPP)3]Cl2 probe for 45 min and observed with CLSM.

Cellular response to electrical stimulation

Cell migration: BMSC and HUVEC cells were seeded into 6-well plates (1 × 105 cells/well), and a wound healing assay was performed after cell attachment. After co-cultured for 24 h and US treatment, cell migration was observed by CLSM, and migration rate was calculated by ImageJ [64]. RSC96 cells (2 × 104 cells/well) were seeded in the upper chamber, and the samples were added to the lower chamber with US irradiation. After incubation for 24 h, the cells were stained with cell migration and invasion stain kit (Solarbio, China) and photographed by light microscope, and cell migration number was calculated by ImageJ. Cell proliferation: After co-cultured and US treatment for 1 d and 3 d, BMSC, HUVEC, RSC96 cells viability and proliferation were quantified according to the instruction of cell counting kit-8 (Beyotime, China). After co-culturing for 3 d, by using 4% glutaraldehyde, the cells were fixed, and dehydrated with gradient ethanol (10, 30, 50, 70, 90 and 100%), and observed with SEM. The cells were stained using 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole/fluorescein isothiocyanate (DAPI/FITC) probe (Biosharp, China) and observed by CLSM.

Cell differentiation: BMSC cells were co-cultured with samples and treated with US with osteogenic differentiation induction medium for 7 d and 14 d. At 7 d, the cells were stained by BCIP/NBT Alkaline Phosphatase Color Development Kit (Beyotime, China) and photographed under an inverted light microscope. Then ALP activity was quantified with alkaline phosphatase assay kit (Beyotime, China). The cells were fixed using 4% glutaraldehyde, and then stained with osteopontin (OPN)/FITC/DAPI and osteocalcin (OCN)/FITC/DAPI staining (Proteintech, China), and observed by CLSM. At 14 d, cells were stained using Alizarin Red S Staining Kit for osteogenesis (Beyotime, China) and photographed using inverted light microscope. Then mineralized nodules were dissolved in 10% cetylpyridinium chloride solution for 1 h, and microplate reader was used to determine the absorbance at 620 nm.

Angiogenesis: 12-well plate was filled with 150 uL Matrigel, and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h before solidification. Subsequently, HUVEC cells were seeded onto Matrigel (5 × 104 cells/well), and co-cultured with the samples for 4 h with US treatment. Blood vessel formation was observed using CLSM, and ImageJ was used to calculate number of master junctions and master segments, total master segments length, and number of meshes. Angiogenesis-related proteins: After co-cultured and US treatment for 3 d, HUVEC cells were stained with cluster of differentiation 31(CD31)/DAPI and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)/DAPI staining (Proteintech, China) after cells fixation using 4% glutaraldehyde, and observed using CLSM. Neurite outgrowth evaluation: After co-cultured and US treatment for 3 d, PC-12 cells were stained with class III beta-tubulin (TUJ1)/DAPI and growth associated protein 43 (GAP43)/DAPI staining (Proteintech, China). After fixation using 4% glutaraldehyde, the cells were observed using CLSM, and the length of neurite was quantified using ImageJ.

RT-qPCR: The total RNA of BMSC induced for 14 d, PC-12 cells cultured for 7 d, and HUVEC cells cultured for 3 d were extracted using the cell/tissue miRNA extraction kit (Yeasen, China), respectively, and then reverse transcribed into cDNA using PrimeScript (RR036A-1, Takara, Japan). Gene expression was performed using ABI SyBr Green and RT-qPCR system (QuantStudio 6 Flex, Thermo Fisher, USA), and calculations were performed using the 2−ΔΔCt method. The primers used were shown in Table S2 and Table S3. RNA sequencing: After 7 d of BMSC osteogenic induction and PC-12 neuronal induction, cells were collected and lysed to extract RNA for gene transcriptome sequencing. RNA sequencing was performed by Servicebio (Wuhan, China). Calcium imaging: BMSC and PC-12 cells were seeded into confocal dishes (1 × 104 cells/well). After cell attachment, samples were added and co-cultured for 7 d. The cells were stained with Fluo-4 probe (Beyotime, China) for 30 min, and observe with CLSM. Adenosine triphosphate (ATP) content was analyzed using ATP Assay Kit (Beyotime, China).

Osteogenesis in vivo

The animal experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee in East China University of Science and Technology (Ethics No.: ECUST-2024–149). The 60 male Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats (eight-week-old) were divided into four groups to model femoral condylar defects. the rats were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg). On the femoral condyle, the bone defects (2 mm in diameter and 5 mm in depth) were prepared using a microdrill, and the samples (Φ2 mm × 5 mm) impregnated with S. aureus (1 × 108 CFU/mL) were implanted into the defects. After suturing, the surgical sites were irradiated with an ultrasound exposure device (US: 0–7 d: 1.5 W/cm2, 5 min/2 d; 7–21 d: 0.5 W/cm2, 5 min/2 d; 1 MHz, 50% duty cycle). At 7 d after implantation, the infected samples were extracted, and bacteria were isolated by ultrasonication. 10 μL bacterial suspension was spread on LB agar plates and incubated for 24 h. Bacterial colonies were photographed by digital camera and counted by ImageJ.

At 1 d, 7 d, 21 d, and 8 w after implantation, the femoral condyles were harvested and put into paraffin sections or resin sections. The sections were stained with dihydroethidium (DHE) staining (1 d and 21 d), Giemsa immunohistochemistry (7 d), CD86/206 staining (21 d), hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Masson staining (8 w), CD31 and neurofilament 200 (NF200) immunofluorescence staining (8 w), which were observed by CLSM or optical microscope. The sections of heart, liver, spleen, lungs and kidneys were stained with H&E staining (8 w) and observed by optical microscope. At 21 d after implantation, rat blood was centrifuged to extract the supernatant, and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-a), and interleukin-4 (IL-4) cytokines were detected by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) test kit (Thermo Fisher, USA). At 8 w after implantation, the specimens of femoral condyle were separated and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and scanned with Micro-CT scanner (Perkin Elmer Quantum GX2, USA). The 3D reconstructed images were made using CTvox software, and bone volume/total volume (BV/TV), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), bone mineral density (BMD), and trabecular separation (Tb. Sp) were calculated by CTan software.

Statistical analysis

Relative fluorescence intensity of CLSM images was quantitative analyzed by ImageJ. All data was analyzed by two-way ANOVA or one-way ANOVA using GraphPad Prism8 software and Origin 2018, and expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was defined as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001.
