HAUS6 as a potential prognostic and immunological biomarker: validation from pan-cancer analysis to hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Haixiang Xie, Zuyin Wan, Xianwei Lu, Kejian Yang, Chongjiu Qin, Yu Chen, Chenlu Lan, Kai Peng, Wei Qin, Shutian Mo, Xin Zhou, Chengkun Yang, Xiwen Liao, Tao Peng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe HAUS augmin like complex subunit 6 (HAUS6) is associated with cytokinesis.HAUS6 has only been reported in a few cancers. To date, there are no published studies about the potential biological features and clinical meaning of HAUS6 in pan-cancer.MethodsWe used TCGA, GTEx and UALCAN databases to explore HAUS6 expression and to analyze its associated prognosis in pan-cancer. Mutation, m6A and DNA methylation analyses were performed by databases of cBioPortal, MethSurv, UALCAN, and SRAMP. We identified genes interacting and associated with HAUS6 by GeneMania and GEPIA2, respectively. We used the ESTIMATE package, TISIDB, TIDE, and TIMSO to investigate the correlation between HAUS6 and immune infiltration and immunotherapy. We investigated the relationship of HAUS6 with tumor heterogeneity, stemness and drug sensitivity. In addition, we constructed a nomograph and performed differential analyses based on HAUS6 in HCC, and RT-qPCR and immunohistochemistry were performed to validate the expression in HCC and normal tissues. Finally, the proliferative, migratory and invasive capacities of HAUS6 in HCC were verified using CCK-8, colony formation, Transwell array and wound healing assays.ResultsAfter pan-cancer analysis, HAUS6 showed different expression levels in tumor tissues and normal tissues, and correlated with prognosis, indicating its potential as a diagnostic and prognostic biomarker. HAUS6 mutations were identified in 19 of 27 tumor types. Notably, HAUS6 expression was closely associated with m6A, DNA methylation, tumor heterogeneity, stemness, immune cell infiltration and immune checkpoints. Furthermore, HAUS6 showed significant predictive value for immunotherapy outcomes and was potentially associated with multiple drugs. In HCC, HAUS6 was identified as an independent risk factor; functional enrichment analyses highlighted that increased HAUS6 expression is related to the cell cycle and multiple cancer signaling pathways. Experimental validation shows that the mRNA (P = 0.028) and protein (P < 0.001) levels of HAUS6 in HCC tissues are highly consistent with the TCGA prediction trend. In vitro experiments further demonstrated that HAUS6 silencing inhibited HCC cell proliferation, migration and invasion.ConclusionsThis study highlights the importance of HAUS6 in cancer biology. HAUS6 may be a biomarker for diagnosis and prediction of prognosis as well as a target for tumor immunotherapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12935-025-04081-6.Keywords: HAUS6, Pan-cancer, HCC, Prognosis prediction, Immune analysis

Introduction

Cancer continues to impose a substantial global health burden, with neoplastic diseases emerging as the Level 2 causes of the largest increase in disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) worldwide [1, 2]. Encouragingly, sustained reductions in cancer mortality have been observed, attributable to exploration of the mechanisms of HCC, early detection for specific cancers and therapeutic advancements including recent developments in targeted therapies and immunotherapies [3–7]. However, the potential biomarkers and key mechanistic drivers for many cancers remain incompletely understood. Pan-cancer analyses serve as powerful tools for elucidating both commonalities and distinctions among various malignancies, proving instrumental in deciphering tumorigenesis dynamics and informing diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic strategies [8].

The HAUS augmin like complex comprises eight subunits: HAUS1, HAUS2, HAUS3, HAUS4, HAUS5, HAUS6, HAUS7, and HAUS8 [9]. Members of the HAUS family are critically involved in tumor progression across diverse human malignancies, functioning as key regulators in oncogenic processes [9–11]. HAUS6, the HAUS augmin like complex subunit 6, localized to chromosome 9p22.1, serves as a recruitment subunit within the Augmin complex [12]. By interacting with NEDD1, HAUS6 facilitates microtubule-dependent amplification and modulates NEDD1 redistribution between centrosomes and spindles [13]. This subunit is essential for spindle assembly, directly influencing chromosomal segregation during mitosis [14]. HAUS6’s effectiveness as a biomarker for molecular subtyping in breast cancer has been confirmed [15] and implicated as a shared locus between type 2 diabetes and prostate cancer through genome-wide association studies (GWAS) [12]. These findings collectively suggest HAUS6 as a potential pan-cancer oncogenic driver.

So far, HAUS6 has only been reported in a few cancers, but these studies have mainly focused on studies limited to a single cancer. A systematic exploration of its pan-cancer mechanisms and clinical implications remains conspicuously absent. In this study, we employed multi-omics databases to dissect HAUS6 expression patterns, prognostic significance, and associations with immune infiltration landscapes and critical biological processes. Furthermore, we conducted a systematic functional characterization of HAUS6 in hepatocellular carcinoma, elucidating its regulatory effects on proliferation, migration, and invasiveness. The results of this study provide new insights for precise diagnosis and targeted therapy of various cancers, and HAUS6 may be a potential biomarker in pan-cancer.

Materials and methods

Expression and diagnostic value of HAUS6

We leveraged multiple databases and resources for HAUS6 expression data in normal and tumor tissues. Transcriptome data were sourced from the TCGA database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/), enabling us to investigate HAUS6 mRNA levels in both tumor and normal tissues, followed by an analysis of HAUS6 expression at different pathological stages. A comprehensive pan-cancer dataset, known as the PANCAN cohort, was acquired from UCSC (https://xenabrowser.net/), incorporating TCGA, TARGET, and GTEx for further analysis [16]. For assessing HAUS6’s value in diagnosis, Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves were developed using a PANCAN group from TCGA [17]. Gene expression data in normal and tumor cell lines were downloaded from the HPA database (https://www.proteinatlas.org/). We employed the UALCAN database’s CPTAC module (https://ualcan.path.uab.edu/index.html) to examine HAUS6 protein level [18]. Spatial transcriptomics data were obtained from Sparkle (https://grswsci.top/) and were used for HAUS6 expression in hepatocellular carcinoma and paracancerous tissues.

Prognostic value of HAUS6

The “survival” package was utilized for conducting proportional risk hypothesis testing and fitting survival regressions, while the “survminer” package and the “ggplot” package were employed for visualizing the outcomes. Based on the median HAUS6 expression [19], the patients were divided into the low-expression group and the high-expression group, and for cancers with P < 0.05, Kaplan-Meier curves were plotted. Three clinical outcomes, namely overall survival (OS), disease-specific survival (DSS) and progression-free interval (PFI), were used to assess the prognostic value of HAUS6. In addition, we investigated the relationship between HAUS6 and clinical characteristics in the TCGA-LIHC cohort, such as Albumin, BMI, gender, Prothrombin time, Residual tumor, Pathology stage and Age. Based on clinicopathological features, we carried out a prognostic stratification analysis. We performed univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses to assess the impact of clinicopathological variables on the prognostic analysis of HAUS6. We developed a nomogram incorporating HAUS6 expression and clinical-pathological characteristics to predict survival probabilities over 1, 3, and 5 years. The predictive precision of the nomogram was evaluated through the use of a calibration graph.

Mutations in HAUS6

We used the cBioPortal database to analyze mutations in HAUS6 (https://www.cbioportal.org/) [20]. For the purpose of depicting the HAUS6 protein’s architecture, we acquired its three-dimensional form (UniProtKB: Q7Z4H7) from the Protein Data Bank (http://www.rcsb.org/) [21]. Somatic mutation landscape and the correlation between HAUS6 expression and the Top 20 most frequently mutated genes in HCC were conducted on the Sangerbox platform.

DNA methylation and HAUS6 mRNA’s m6A modification

To predict the DNA methylation level of the HAUS6 gene in individual cancers, we examined the location, type, extent and prognostic significance of DNA methylation of the HAUS6 using the UALCAN portal. The prognostic significance of HAUS6 methylation levels was assessed using the MethSurv website [22]. We used the SRAMP website to make predictions of the HAUS6 m6A modification sites (http://www.cuilab.cn/sramp)[23].

Interacting genes and differentially expressed genes

The HAUS6 gene interaction network was analyzed using the GeneMania database (https://genemania.org/) [24]. We explored the correlation between interacting genes and HAUS6 using the GEPIA2 database (http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn/) and plotted OS and RFS-related prognostic heatmaps [25]. Based on the genes interacting with HAUS6 or differentially expressed genes (P.adj < 0.05 and a |log2 (FoldChange)| > 1.5), we performed Gene Ontology (GO) analysis, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) pathway enrichment analysis using the “clusterProfiler” software package [26–29]. A value of FDR < 0.1 and P. adj < 0.05 is considered to indicate statistically significant differences.

Immune infiltration, tumor heterogeneity and stemness

We searched 18 different tumor datasets using the Tumour Immunology Single Cell Centre (TISCH, http://tisch.comp-genomics.org) to compare the expression levels of HAUS6 in different cell types [30]. A range of algorithms (TIMER, CIBERSORT, quanTIseq, xCell, ssGSEA, IPS, MCP-counter, and EPIC) were employed to thoroughly evaluate the link between HAUS6 and immune cell infiltration. Additionally, the evaluation of immune, stromal, and estimation scores was conducted using the “ESTIMATE” package [31]. In addition, to examine the relationship between HAUS6 expression and tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), immunoinhibitors, immunostimulators, MHC molecules, chemokines, and receptors, the TISIDB website (http://cis.hku.hk/TISIDB/) was utilized [32].

Tumor heterogeneity and stemness

Using the “maftools” package, we determined both the tumor mutation burden (TMB) and the mutant-allele tumor heterogeneity (MATH). Additional factors linked to tumor diversity were derived from earlier research, encompassing microsatellite instability (MSI), neoantigenic load (NEO), the purity of the tumor, ploidy, homologous recombination defects (HRD), and heterozygous omission (LOH) [33, 34]. Additionally, scores related to stemness (RNAss, EREG.EXPss, DNAss, DMPss, ENHss, and EREG-METHss) were derived from earlier research [34].

Role of HAUS6 in immunotherapy cohorts

Within the TIDE database (http://tide.dfci.harvard.edu/), the efficacy of HAUS6 and other documented biomarkers in predicting results across various immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) treatment groups was evaluated. Furthermore, we examined the correlations between HAUS6 and T-cell dysfunction metrics, along with the differences in immunotherapies across various cohorts [35, 36].

We used the TIMSO database (http://tismo.cistrome.org/) to compare HAUS6 expression before and after ICB treatment and between responders and non-responders in different tumor models. In addition, we investigated the effects of different cytokine interventions on HAUS6 expression in different cell lines [37].

Interacting chemicals and HAUS6, and drug sensitivity analysis

The CTD database (http://ctdbase.org/) is a website that studies chemical-gene interactions, based on which highly similar genes can be queried [38].

GSCALite (http://bioinfo.life.hust.edu.cn/web/GSCALite/) serves as an online platform for analyzing correlations between drug sensitivity and the expression of targeted genes [39]. It includes Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) and Cancer Therapeutics Response Portal (CTRP).

Patients and clinical specimens

Between July 2024 and December 2024, we collected paired HCC tissues and normal liver tissues from 20 HCC patients for PT-qPCR. Between October 2023 and May 2024, we collected paired HCC tissues and normal liver tissues from 40 HCC patients for immunohistochemistry. The study obtained approval from the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University (Approval Number: 2025-E0268). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients before the procedure.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was isolated and extracted using the Total RNA Kit I (R6834-01, Omega, China), and PrimeScripttm RT reagent Kit (Takara, RR047A, China) was used to reverse transcribe RNA into cDNA. The qPCR assay was conducted on QuantStudio 6 Flex using GAPDH as an internal control. As follows were the primers used in the procedure: GAPDH-F: CAGGAGGCATTGCTGATGAT, GAPDH-R: GAAGGCTGGGGCTCATTT; HAUS6-F: CTTTCGAGAAGGAGCATCTCTG, HAUS6-R: GATCTTTCCGCAGGCAATGGT. The results of mRNA expression were calculated by using the 2−ΔΔCt method [19].

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemical steps were performed as in the previous study [40, 41]. The sections were treated with HAUS6 (1:400, GTX118732, GeneTex, USA) overnight at 4 °C. The H-score was used to evaluate the immunohistochemical staining, using the formula: H-score = (1×percentage of weak staining) + (2×percentage of moderate staining) + (3×percentage of strong staining).

Cell culture

Human HCC cell lines (Huh7 and HepG2) were purchased from Shanghai Zhong Qiao Xin Zhou Biotechnology Co., Ltd (www.zqxzbio.com). Cells were maintained in DMEM/MEM containing 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

Transfection

The si-HAUS6-NC, si-HAUS6-1, si-HAUS6-2, and si-HAUS6-3 were transfected into Huh7 and HepG2 cells by RNATransMate (E607402-0500, Sangon Biotech, China) [42]. HAUS6-related siRNA sequences are as follows: si-1: AGAUGAUCUCACAACUAUA; si-2: CAGUUAUUCAGUUAUUAAA; si-3༚GAGUGAACAUGUUUGACAA; si-NC: UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT.

Western blotting

Proteins were isolated from cells using RIPA buffer containing a common protease inhibitor cocktail (RR20032, Proteintech, China). The proteins, once prepared, were introduced into a 10% SDS/PAGE grid setup, where they were uniformly electrolyzed at a steady pressure (200 V) before being moved onto a PVDF membrane. The membrane was sealed using 5% skim milk powder for an hour, then left to incubate at 4 °C overnight with primary antibodies, and subsequently, a 1.5-hour incubation with secondary antibodies. Anti-HAUS6 (1:1000, GTX118732, GeneTex, USA) and anti-GAPDH (1:50000, 60004-1-Ig, Proteintech, China) antibodies were applied to the blots [19, 43].

CCK8

The cells underwent total digestion and were then placed in 96-well culture plates, maintaining a concentration of 2000 cells in each well, and were incubated at a temperature of 37 °C. The CCK-8 kit (C6005M, UElandy, China) was employed daily to assess absorbance over a span of 5 days of incubation. The aforementioned method was identified at a light wavelength measuring 450 nm [44].

Colony formation assay

In total, 2000 cells were transferred to new 6-well plates and incubated for 14 days as outlined in the aforementioned detailed procedure, with the medium being replaced every 4 days. The colonies formed were fixed in formaldehyde and stained with crystal violet (E607309-0500, Sangon Biotech, China) after a predetermined time, then counted and photographed [44].

Transwell assays

Migration assays were carried out in Transwell chambers 8 mm in diameter (356234; Corning, USA). For the invasion assay, the Basement Membrane (356234; Corning, USA) was precoated on the Transwell chambers 8 mm in diameter (353097, FALCON, USA) before the migration assays. The upper chamber received a mixture containing 80,000 HCC cells in 200µL of serum-free DMEM/MEM medium, and the bottom chamber was supplemented with DMEM/MEM medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum. Following a 24-hour growth period, the chamber’s cells were stabilized using 4% paraformaldehyde fixative (BL539A, Biosharp, China) for an hour and then colored with 0.1% crystal violet. Under an inverted light microscope, we observed cells in three randomly selected viewing areas that had either migrated or penetrated to the lower surface [44].

Wound healing assay

HCC cells were trypsinized and seeded in each well of a 6-well plate. After overnight incubation, the wells were scraped with a 200-µL sterile pipette tip. The initial gap width and the gap width after 24 h of scraping were taken [44].

Statistical analysis

The Mann-Whitney test was employed to compare continuous variables across two groups, while the Kruskal-Wallis test was utilized for comparing multiple groups. The Kaplan-Meier method and Cox regression analysis were employed to contrast survival traits. The Spearman/Person test was employed to evaluate the relationships between ongoing variables across both groups. Every statistical evaluation was conducted utilizing R software or digital tools. P < 0.05 (*), P < 0.01 (**), P < 0.001 (***), and P < 0.0001 (****) were considered significant. For pan-cancer analysis, the Benjamini-Hochberg method was used for correction, with FDR < 0.1 considered significant. All experiments were repeated three times.

Result

HAUS6 expression in pan-cancer

Initially, the analysis from the HPA database showed that HAUS6 mRNA expression was highest in testis and lowest in choroid plexus compared to other organs in normal tissues (Figure S1A). Compared to other cell lines, HAUS6 mRNA expression was highest in Lymphoma and lowest in Adrenocortical cancer (Figure S1B). In the TCGA database, the results demonstrated that HAUS6 was upregulated in various malignant tumors, but downregulated in THCA(Fig. 1A). By analyzing paired cancer and paracancerous tissues in TCGA, the results indicated that HAUS6 expression was upregulated in multiple cancers, but downregulated in THCA (Fig. 1B).

HAUS6 expression in normal tissue and cancer in pan-cancer. (A) Differential pan-cancer expression of HAUS6 in tumors and normal tissues from the TCGA database; (B) Differential pan-cancer expression of HAUS6 in paired tumors and adjacent normal tissues from the TCGA database; (C) Diagnostic value of HAUS6 from the TCGA database. ns, no significance; *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01;***p < 0.001 and ****p < 0.0001

We also analyzed and found a significant association between HAUS6 expression and pathologic stage in 7 cancers, especially in ACC, KICH and OV (Figure S1C). To assess the diagnostic value of HAUS6, we calculated area under the curve (AUC) values using ROC curves to differentiate between normal and tumor samples. In CHOL, COAD, ESCA, SARC, STAD, HAUS6 showed high diagnostic accuracy with an AUC value greater than 0.9; meanwhile, it was observed that the AUC values of CESC, GBM, HNSC, KIRC, KIRP, LIHC, LUSC, PAAD, PCPG, READ, and SKCM were in the range of 0.7–0.9, highlighting the excellent diagnostic potential(Fig. 1C).

Furthermore, our analysis of protein levels in both normal and tumor tissues, utilizing the CPTAC database, revealed elevated HAUS6 protein levels in clear cell RCC, glioblastoma multiforme, head and neck squamous, lung adenocarcinoma, lung squamous cell, hepatocellular carcinoma, pancreatic adenocarcinoma, and UCEC, with reduced expression noted in breast and ovarian cancer (Figure S2).

Prognostic value of HAUS6

Evaluating HAUS6’s predictive significance across various cancers involved extracting survival statistics from the TCGA database and conducting univariate Cox regression along with Kaplan-Meier analyses. Based on the median expression value of HAUS6, the patients were divided into the low-expression group and the high-expression group. OS analyses showed that HAUS6 was identified as a risk factor for KICH, LIHC, and SARC, and it was a protective factor for patients with COAD, LGG, and THYM (Fig. 2A, D). PFI analyses showed that HAUS6 was identified as a LIHC risk factor, while it was a protective factor in ACC, COAD, and GBM patients (Fig. 2B, E). In the DSS analysis, HAUS6 was a risk factor for CESC, LIHC, and SARC, while it appeared to be a protective factor for patients with COAD and LGG (Fig. 2C, F).

Mutation of HAUS6

The mutation condition of HAUS6 was analyzed using the TCGA pan-cancer data sourced from the cBioPortal database. The study revealed that Bladder Cancer exhibited the most frequent mutations, at a rate of 17.39%. In the majority of tumors, amplification emerged as the predominant mutation type, predominantly in cancers such as Bladder Cancer, Head and Neck Cancer, Lung Cancer, Endometrial Cancer, Pancreatic Cancer, Breast Cancer, Bone Cancer, Ovarian Cancer, Myelodysplastic/Myeloproliferative Neoplasms, Prostate Cancer, Mature B-cell lymphoma, Hepatobiliary Cancer (Fig. 3A). The 3D structure of the HAUS6 protein was constructed by the Protein Data Bank (Fig. 3B). The cBioPortal database revealed 16 sites of somatic mutations, with missense mutations being the most prevalent type (Fig. 3C). Comparative analysis of HAUS6 expression across CNA groups revealed a statistically significant difference (P < 0.05), notably demonstrating the highest expression levels in the amplification group (Fig. 3D).

(D) HAUS6 expression difference among different copy-number alterations; (E) The top ten genes with significant difference in mutation rate between HAUS6 altered and unaltered groups; (F) The correlation between HAUS6 expression and the Top 20 most frequently mutated genes in HCC. *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; ****P < 0.0001

In addition, the HAUS6-altered group had higher mutation frequencies in PLIN2, SCARNA8, RRAGA, SAXO1, DENND4C, RPS6, SLC24A2, ACER2, ADAMTSL1, and MLLT3 compared to the unaltered group (Fig. 3E). Particularly in HCC, the top 20 genes with the highest mutation frequency in HCC were significantly different in the HAUS6 high- and low-expression groups (median expression level as the threshold), and HCC patients with high HAUS6 expression showed higher TP53 mutation frequency (P = 3.6e-4). In addition, the HAUS6 was strongly associated with APOB, RB1, KMT2D, LAMA1, ZFHX4, RP1, CFAP47, MDN1, PLA2G4A, POTEH, FAM135B, MYO1E, APBA2, ADGRB1, CHST3, PRRC2B, IDH1, BOD1L1, PLCG2 (Fig. 3F).

mRNA’s m6A modification and DNA methylation in pan-cancer

m6A modification can influence the folding, stability, breakdown, and cell interactions of altered RNAs, enabling their involvement in activities like splicing, translation, exportation, and degradation [45]. m6A modification sites of HAUS6 were examined by the SRAMP portal. m6A is likely to modify 29 functional regions of HAUS6 (Fig. 4A). The five functional regions with the highest confidence are shown in Fig. 4B-G. Finally, we further analyzed the m6A-modified genes of HAUS6 mRNA using the TCGA database (Fig. 4H). These findings imply that m6A modifications may regulate HAUS6 expression in pan-cancer, which in turn regulates cellular functions.

HAUS6 mRNA’s m6A modification. (A) The m6A alteration sites of HAUS6 from the SRAMP database; (B-F)The five functional regions of HAUS6 mRNA’s m6A modification with the greatest confidence from the SRAMP database. (G) The relationship between m6A modification genes and HAUS6 based on TCGA

DNA methylation is a key component of gene regulation and cancer development [46]. We used the UALCAN portal to examine the DNA methylation status of the promoter region of HAUS6. Compared to normal tissues, Methylation levels of HAUS6 in SARC, COAD, KIRC, and PAAD were significantly higher, while methylation levels of HAUS6 in BLCA, HNSC, UCEC, READ, TGCT, BRCA, and PRAD were much lower (Fig. 5). In addition, prognostic analyses from the MethSurv database found that the methylation levels of multiple CpG islands correlated with OS in pan-cancer (Figure S3).

Interacting genes of HAUS6 and its pathway

To elucidate the interacting genes associated with HAUS6, we used the GeneMania database to construct a network containing 20 genes closely associated with HAUS6 (Fig. 6A). Subsequently, we identified the top 10 genes (FBXO5, GINS3, HAUS1, HAUS2, HAUS3, HAUS4, HAUS5, NEDD1, RBM7, XPO1) that had the highest correlation with HAUS6 in all tumor samples using the GEPIA2 database (Fig. 6B). We subjected HAUS6 and 20 genes interacting with HAUS6 to GO and KEGG enrichment analysis. The results indicated that HAUS6 may be involved in spindle assembly, spindle organization, centrosome cycle, cell cycle, DNA replication, mitotic spindle microtubule, spindle microtubule, DNA replication preinitiation complex, tubulin binding, microtubule minus-end binding, microtubule binding, and Influenza A (Fig. 6C). In addition, we analyzed the prognostic value of 20 HAUS6-interacting genes in pan-cancer by the GEPIA2 database. In LIHC, high expression of NPAS1, FBXO5, XPO1, GINS3, NEDD1, HAUS5, TUBG2, HAUS1 and HAUS2 was significantly associated with worse OS (Fig. 6D); SPIRE2, FBXO5, GINS3, HAUS5, HAUS8, TUBG2, HAUS7, HAUS1 and HAUS2 high expression was significantly associated with worse RFS (Figure S4).

Association of HAUS6 with tumor heterogeneity and stemness

Tumor heterogeneity is a characteristic feature of malignant tumors and includes various aspects such as growth rate, invasiveness, drug sensitivity and prognosis, which are all affected by genomic changes [47, 48]. HAUS6 expression was positively correlated with TMB in LUAD, KIPAN, STAD, KIRC, ACC, and KICH (Figure S5A). It was also positively correlated with MATH in LUAD, ESCA, STES, LUSC, and BLCA, but negatively in KIPAN and KIRC (Figure S5B). HAUS6 expression showed a positive correlation with MSI in CESC, STAD, KIRC, and ACC, and a negative correlation in PRAD, HNSC, and DLBC (Figure S5C). In ACC, HAUS6 expression was positively correlated with NEO (Figure S5D). HAUS6 expression was positively correlated with purity in GBM, GBMLGG, LGG, BRCA, ESCA, STES, SARC, KIRP, STAD, LUSC, and SKCM, and negatively in LAML, KIPAN, and READ (Figure S5E). A positive correlation with ploidy was observed in LUAD, LUSC, and BLCA, and a negative correlation in THYM and THCA (Figure S5F). HAUS6 expression was positively correlated with LOH in LUAD, BRCA, ESCA, STES, SARC, KIRP, PRAD, LUSC, LIHC, UVM, and BLCA, and negatively in KIPAN, KIRC, THCA, and MESO (Figure S5G). Finally, HAUS6 expression showed a positive correlation with HRD in LUAD, BRCA, SARC, KIPAN, PRAD, KIRC, LUSC, LIHC, and BLCA, and a negative correlation in THYM (Figure S5H).

In addition, cancer stemness is considered to be a key component of tumor progression [49]. HAUS6 expression was positively correlated with DNAss in LUAD, STES, LUSC, TGCT, and SKCM, and negatively correlated in HNSC, THYM, and MESO (Figure S6A). It was positively correlated with EREG-METHHss in LUAD, STES, TGCT, and SKCM, and negatively correlated with HNSC, THYM, and MESO (Figure S6B). HAUS6 expression was positively correlated with DMPss in LUAD, LUSC, TGCT, and SKCM, and negatively correlated in THYM (Figure S6C). In TGCT and SKCM, HAUS6 expression was positively correlated with ENHss and negatively correlated with THYM (Figure S6D). HAUS6 expression was positively correlated with RNAss in GBM, GBMLGG, LGG, LUAD, COAD, COADREAD, LAML, BRCA, ESCA, STES, SARC, STAD, PRAD, UCEC, HNSC, LUSC, OV, TGCT, PCPG, and BLCA, and negatively correlated in KIPAN, THCA, and PAAD (Figure S6E). HAUS6 expression showed a positive correlation with EREG.EXPss in GBMLGG, LGG, COAD, COADREAD, LAML, BRCA, STES, STAD, PRAD, LUSC, THCA, and ACC, and a negative correlation in SARC, THYM, and LIHC (Figure S6F).

Immune infiltration and immunomodulators related to HAUS6

The tumor’s immune microenvironment (TIME) is crucial in the evolution and advancement of cancer [50]. We explored HAUS6 expression in different cell types in TIME using single-cell data from the TISCH database. HAUS6 was found to be significantly upregulated in specific immune cell types, suggesting a potential role for HAUS6 in influencing the function of these immune cells (Fig. 7).

Single-cell analysis of HAUS6 expression signature in pan-cancer using the TISCH database. (A-R) For different cancer types, UMAP plots show the heterogeneity of cell distribution and HAUS6 expression in different tumours, while violin plots reflect HAUS6 expression in various cell types

We calculated the ESTIMATE, stromal, and immune scores for each patient in each tumor type using the R package “ESTIMATE (version 1.0.13).” HAUS6 expression showed a significant correlation with the ESTIMATE immune infiltration score in 18 cancer types: 3 were positively correlated (KIPAN, KIRC, PAAD) and 15 were negatively correlated (GBM, GBMLGG, UCEC, BRCA, CESC, ESCA, STES, SARC, KIRP, STAD, LUSC, SKCM, OV, TGCT, PCPG) (Fig. 8A). Similarly, HAUS6 expression correlated significantly with stromal immune infiltration scores in 18 cancer types: 4 positively (KIPAN, KIRC, PAAD, LAML) and 14 negatively (GBM, GBMLGG, UCEC, BRCA, CESC, STES, SARC, KIRP, STAD, LUSC, SKCM, BLCA, OV, TGCT) (Fig. 8B). Additionally, HAUS6 expression was significantly correlated with immune dip scores in 21 cancer types: 3 positively (KIPAN, PAAD, DLBC) and 18 negatively (GBM, GBMLGG, UCEC, BRCA, CESC, ESCA, STES, SARC, KIRP, STAD, LUSC, SKCM, THCA, OV, TGCT, LAML, PCPG, ACC) (Fig. 8C).

The relationship between HAUS6 and ESTIMATE (A), stromal(B), and immune(C) scores based on TCGA

We integrated four well-established algorithms—MCP-counter, quanTIseq, TIMER, and EPIC—to evaluate the correlation between HAUS6 expression and immune cell infiltration. In MCP-counter, quanTIseq, and TIMER, HAUS6 expression was significantly associated with immune cell infiltration in most cancer types, with neutrophils showing the strongest correlation. In PRAD, LIHC, and PAAD, HAUS6 expression was positively correlated with specific immune cell infiltrations (Fig. 9A-C). In the quanTIseq algorithm, the infiltration level of Tregs is most closely related to HAUS6 in LAML, showing a significant positive correlation (r = 0.49, P < 0.0001, Fig. 9C). In the EPIC algorithm, CD4 + T cells were positively correlated with HAUS6 expression in most cancers(Fig. 9D), UVM (r = 0.56, P < 0.0001) and THCA (r = 0.51, P < 0.0001); Meanwhile, CD8 + T cells were positively correlated with HAUS6 expression in most cancers(Fig. 9D), UVM (r = 0.62, P < 0.0001) and PRAD (r = 0.53, P < 0.0001). Additionally, we confirmed a significant association between HAUS6 and immune cell infiltration using the CIBERSORT, ssGSEA, xCELL, and IPS algorithms (Figure S7).

Subsequently, our study concentrated on exploring the relationship between HAUS6 and tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, along with immunomodulators, chemokines, and receptors. HAUS6 showed an inverse relationship with the prevalence of numerous immune cells across various cancers, with the exception of Th2, activated CD4 (+) T cells, memory B cells, and TCM CD4, especially in COAD, GBM, PCPG, SARC, and TGCT (Figure S8A). Most genes found in HNSC, KIRC, PAAD, THCA, and UVM showed a direct relationship with HAUS6 (Figure S8B-D). Ultimately, our investigation focused on the connections among chemokines, receptors, and HAUS6. Findings showed a notable positive relationship between HAUS6 and chemokine receptors, especially in THCA, PAAD, KIRC, and SARC (Figure S8E-F).

Bioinformatics role of HAUS6 in HCC and expression validation

Based on HAUS6’s median expression level, the TCGA-LIHC group was categorized into groups with high and low expression, and this was followed by a differential analysis to pinpoint several genes with varying expression levels. Genes with a P.adj < 0.05 and a |log2 (FoldChange)| > 1.5 were classified as having differential expression. Between the two groups, 1302 genes showed varied expression, with 1098 genes experiencing an increase and 204 genes a decrease in expression (Fig. 13A). Based on log2 (FoldChange), the heatmap showed the correlation between HAUS6 and the most significantly up- and down-regulated mRNAs (Fig. 13B). GO pathway enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes showed that HAUS6 was involved in phagocytosis-engulfment, phagocytosis-recognition, complement activation classical pathway, apical part of cell, immunoglobulin complex, immunoglobulin complex-circulating, serine hydrolase activity, antigen binding, and immunoglobulin receptor binding. The KEGG study indicated a potential role of HAUS6 in the interaction between Neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction, Bile secretion, and Nicotine addiction (Fig. 13C). In addition, by GSEA analysis, we found that HAUS6 was significantly associated with cell cycle, T Cell Receptor signaling pathways, Signaling by the B Cell Receptor BCR, and several tumor-regulating signaling pathways (Fig. 13D). Finally, we collected 20 HCC patients and examined the expression of HAUS6 in HCC tissues and adjacent normal liver tissues by RT-qPCR, which suggested that HAUS6 was significantly up-regulated in HCC compared to normal liver tissues (Fig. 13E). We also collected HCC tissues and normal liver tissues from 40 HCC patients for IHC, and the results similarly suggested that the expression of HAUS6 was higher in HCC tissues compared with normal liver tissues (Fig. 13F-G). Finally, the spatial transcriptome dataset was used to further validate that the expression of HAUS6 was higher in HCC tissues than in normal liver tissues (Fig. 13H-I).

Role of HAUS6 on proliferation, migration and invasion of HCC cells

Based on the above studies that HAUS6 may be a prognostic biomarker for HCC and is involved in the regulation of several biological functions and signaling pathways in HCC, we decided to conduct further experiments to explore its function in HCC.

Owing to the notably elevated levels of HAUS6 in Huh7 and HepG2 cells, these two cell lines were chosen for further experiments (Fig. 14A). A trio of HAUS6 siRNAs (si-1, si-2, si-3, and si-NC) underwent transfection into Huh7 and HepG2 cells. Subsequently, the effectiveness of the transfection was confirmed using RT-qPCR and Western blotting. siRNA-1 knockdown was the most efficient (Fig. 14B-D), so siRNA-1 was used for the next study. HAUS6’s efficacy in influencing HCC cell proliferation was verified through the CCK8 assay (Fig. 14E) and colony formation assay (Fig. 14F), revealing that reducing HAUS6 levels markedly decreased their proliferation ability relative to the negative control. Following this, transwell assays revealed a significant decrease in the migration and invasion capacities of HCC cells upon reducing HAUS6 levels (Fig. 14G-H). Furthermore, a wound healing test was conducted, revealing that the HAUS6 knockdown group exhibited markedly reduced healing abilities compared to the control group (Fig. 14I-J). The results indicate that HAUS6 has a substantial impact on the proliferation, migration, and infiltration of HCC cells.

Discussion

In this study, we leverage multi-omics data from TCGA and other public repositories to perform a comprehensive pan-cancer analysis of HAUS6. Inspired by prior TCGA-based investigations into signaling pathways and biomarker discovery [51, 52], we integrate computational mining with experimental validation to anchor our findings biologically. We examine HAUS6 expression, mutation profiles, survival associations, immune infiltration patterns, and functional pathways across multiple cancer types, with subsequent experimental focus on HCC to assess its role in malignant phenotypes.

We acknowledge that bulk transcriptomic analyses, including those derived from TCGA, are subject to technical and biological biases such as tumor purity, sample heterogeneity, and batch effects [53, 54]. While these limitations preclude definitive mechanistic inference from expression data alone, our combined bioinformatic and functional approach offers a robust framework for identifying HAUS6 as a candidate oncogenic driver and immunological biomarker with pan-cancer relevance.

Our study is the first comprehensive study of the tumor-promoting or inhibitory effects of HAUS6 in pan-cancer. Earlier research indicated a high expression of HAUS6 in glioma and squamous cell carcinoma, closely linked to poorer overall survival, with HAUS6’s possible cancer-causing effects first shown [9, 55]. Our results indicated that HAUS6 was highly expressed in most tumors and lowly expressed only in THCA. OS, PFI, and DSS-related prognostic analyses demonstrated that high HAUS6 expression was significantly associated with worse prognosis in HCC patients, suggesting that HAUS6 may be an important prognostic biomarker for HCC.

Mutations in HAUS6 were identified in 19 of the 27 tumor types analyzed, encompassing various alteration types including point mutations, amplifications, and deep deletions. Gene amplification was the predominant alteration observed in 16 tumor types. Notably, in several cancers—including Bladder Cancer, Endometrial Cancer, and Prostate Cancer—amplification represented the sole type of HAUS6 genetic alteration. As the amplification of oncogenes is a known driver of genetic heterogeneity, tumor progression, and poor prognosis, these findings underscore the potential role of HAUS6 amplification in cancer pathogenesis [56, 57].

In our study, TP53 mutations were found to occur in 48.3% of HCC cases, and HAUS6 overexpression was significantly associated with TP53 mutations in HCC. The tumor suppressor gene TP53 is a commonly mutated gene in HCC that initiates cell cycle arrest, apoptosis and senescence in the presence of DNA damage, oncogene activation and hypoxia to maintain genomic integrity [58]. Loss of p53 promotes PD-L1 expression and reduces CD8 + T cell infiltration in HCC, thereby facilitating immune escape [59]. Furthermore, through the positive feedback loop between HIF1α/USP22 and TP53, it can promote the development of HCC stemness under hypoxic conditions.

In our analysis, DNA methylation levels of HAUS6 differed significantly in multiple cancers compared to normal tissue. Differences in methylation levels in the promoter region of genes affect gene expression and genomic stability, leading to cancer development [60]. We also found that methylation levels of five methylation sites in HAUS6 (cg07988015, cg10456705, cg13739345, cg14087061 and cg22920498) were associated with patient prognosis. The results highlight the significance of examining these particular methylation sites in upcoming research.

In specific cancers, we observed that HAUS6 exhibited correlations with TMB, MSI, tumor purity, ploidy, NEO, MATH, LOH, and HRD; furthermore, HAUS6 showed significant correlations with RNA-based stemness scores (RNAss and EGER.EXPss), and DNA-based stemness scores (DNAss, DMPss, ENHss, and EGER- METHss) were also significantly correlated. Within this group, genomic markers such as microsatellite instability (MSI) and tumor mutational load (TMB) serve to pinpoint individuals who might gain from immune checkpoint inhibitors [61]. In a comprehensive review of 2,661 patients across 8 studies, it was observed that individuals with high TMB experienced improved progression-free survival (PFS) with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, as opposed to those with low TMB [62]. Stemness is positively correlated with intra-tumor heterogeneity [63]. There is growing evidence of a significant association between tumor stemness and cancer immune evasion and drug resistance, demonstrating good performance in predicting response to immunotherapy in a wide range of cancer types [64].

Immunoassays have shown that HAUS6 expression is negatively correlated with ESTIMATE, stromal and immune scores in most cancers. These three scoring algorithms are widely used in cancer-related immune infiltration analysis [65, 66]. It has been found that stromal scores are more predictive of outcomes than immune scores in most cancer types [67].HAUS6 expression is significantly correlated with the level of immune cell infiltration in most cancer types, with neutrophils being the immune cell type most closely associated with HAUS6 expression. Neutrophils play an important role in antigen presentation in humans, further triggering antigen-specific and non-antigen-dependent T-cell responses [68]. In the present study, HAUS6 was found to be significantly correlated with the currently listed pan-cancer immunosuppressive/immunostimulatory genes and positively correlated with most of the immune checkpoint genes, especially CD274. The expression level of HAUS6 in the samples of the response group was significantly reduced after anti-PD-L1 treatment. Programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1), encoded by the CD274 gene, is up-regulated in various types of cancers and induces immune escape through PD-L1/PD-1 signaling [69]. PD-1/PD-L1 interacts with each other to inhibit T-cell activation and proliferation, to enhance regulatory T-cells (Tregs) and to prevent apoptosis of the tumor cells themselves [70]. Blockade of PD-1/PD-L1 by neutralizing antibodies restores T-cell activity and enhances anti-tumor immunity, with remarkable success in cancer therapy [71]. The close association of HAUS6 with CD274 in pan-cancer suggests that it may be a potential target for cancer therapy.

In this study, we demonstrated that HAUS6 significantly affects the proliferation, migration and invasion of hepatocellular carcinoma cells by in vitro experiments. By bioinformatics analysis based on the TCGA-LIHC cohort, HAUS6 was involved in the cell cycle and regulation of several cancer signaling pathways. Previous studies have also found that HAUS6 is highly expressed in colorectal cancer tissues, and high HAUS6 expression correlates with shorter overall survival of patients, and HAUS6 can promote colorectal cancer cell proliferation through inhibition of the p53/p21 pathway [19].

While this study provides a comprehensive multi-omics portrait of HAUS6 across cancers, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, our findings are primarily derived from secondary analysis of public datasets such as TCGA. Although we have supplemented bioinformatic predictions with initial experimental validation in HCC cell lines, more in-depth functional and mechanistic studies are warranted to fully elucidate the role of HAUS6 in tumor immune regulation and therapeutic response. We recognize that resource and time constraints often shape the scope of computational research, as noted in the broader context of systems oncology [72]. Nevertheless, to strengthen the translational relevance of our findings, future work will include expanded validation in larger clinical cohorts, as well as more functional assays to dissect HAUS6’s role in immune checkpoint regulation and drug response. Such efforts would help bridge the gap between computational discovery and clinical application.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study highlights the importance of HAUS6 in cancer biology research. The varied expression of HAUS6 in various cancerous growths may indicate patient survival outcomes. There was a notable link between HAUS6 and immune cell infiltration and immune checkpoint genes, indicating its suitability as a target for cancer immunotherapy. The results of this study could have significant implications for the application in diagnosing and treating tumors.
