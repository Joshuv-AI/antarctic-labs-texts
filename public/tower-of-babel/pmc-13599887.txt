Prevalence of aortic findings among patients with multifocal fibromuscular dysplasia (FMD) seen at a specialised referral centre
Authors: Karen Ji, Dina Dejanovic, Jennifer E Murphy, Heather L Gornik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFibromuscular dysplasia (FMD) is a non-atherosclerotic vascular disease that affects medium-sized arteries with multifocal (beaded) or focal stenotic lesions, aneurysms, dissections and tortuosity. The extent of aortic involvement in FMD is uncertain. This study evaluates the prevalence and characteristics of aortic findings in patients with multifocal FMD.MethodsSingle-centre, retrospective chart review of patients with multifocal FMD seen from January 2019 to July 2024. Patients ≥18 years with complete aortic cross-sectional imaging were included. Demographic, medical history, family history and vascular phenotype were collected. Aortic pathologies were characterised: aneurysms (thoracic diameter ≥4 cm, abdominal diameter ≥3 cm), ectasia, dissection, stenosis and anatomical anomalies.ResultsOf 360 patients (353 female, 98.1%), aortic involvement was identified in 36 patients (10.0%; 95% CI 7.3% to 13.5%): three acute aortic syndromes (<1%), eight aneurysms (2.2%), 17 ectasias (4.7%) and nine congenital anomalies (2.5%). Among those with aortic involvement (n=36), three (8.3%) were male, four (11.1%) had a family history of aneurysm, two (5.6%) had a family history of dissection and nine underwent clinical genetic testing for aortopathies, all of which were negative. All aortic aneurysms and ectasias were in the proximal thoracic aorta (eight aortic root, 17 ascending aorta). No aortic stenosis was found. Of the nine congenital anomalies, seven had an aberrant right subclavian artery, one had ductus diverticulum and one had coarctation. Compared with patients without aortic involvement, those with aortic involvement were more likely to be male and have non-aortic arterial aneurysms.ConclusionAortic findings, predominantly presenting as thoracic ectasia or aneurysm, were identified in 10% of patients with multifocal FMD and complete aortic imaging seen at a single specialised referral centre. These findings support recommendations for head-to-pelvis imaging for patients with FMD and highlight the need for further research into the genetic and systemic aspects of this disease.

Background

Fibromuscular dysplasia (FMD) is a non-atherosclerotic vascular disease of uncertain pathogenesis. While FMD most classically presents as multifocal (beaded) or focal stenotic arterial lesions, its clinical spectrum also includes aneurysm formation, arterial dissection and tortuosity.1 These manifestations predominantly affect medium-sized arteries (eg, renal, extracranial carotid, vertebral), though other arterial beds may be involved.1

There is emerging recognition of an association between FMD and aortic pathology. Case reports have described aortic FMD through histopathological examination of surgical specimens from patients undergoing interventions for abdominal aortic aneurysms or dissections.2–4 Early data from the North American (formerly USA) Registry for FMD reported aortic aneurysms in 3.4% of the first 447 patients enrolled.5 A subsequent analysis of 921 patients from the same registry showed that, among 200 patients with aneurysms and 237 with dissections, 10% had aortic aneurysms and 3.4% had aortic dissections.6 Imaging studies have also identified increased aortic diameters across multiple segments in patients with FMD.7 8 While the majority of patients in the North American Registry have multifocal FMD, middle aortic syndrome (aortic stenotic lesions) has been reported in the setting of the focal type of FMD, particularly among paediatric patients.9

Despite emerging evidence that FMD affects the aorta, the prevalence, anatomical characteristics and clinical implications of aortic pathologies in multifocal FMD have not been well-described. This study addresses this gap by evaluating the prevalence and features of aortic pathology in a cohort of patients with multifocal FMD seen at a single referral centre.

Methods

Study cohort

We report a single-centre, retrospective chart review of patients with FMD seen in the FMD and Arterial Dissection Program at University Hospitals Harrington Heart & Vascular Institute in Cleveland, Ohio, USA between 1 January 2019 and 8 July 2024. This is a specialised programme that receives referrals from the local University Hospitals health system, the surrounding region and other states for rare disease care. Potential eligible patients were identified from the local registry list of the North American Registry for FMD, and additional chart and imaging reviews were conducted. All patients had a specialist-confirmed diagnosis of multifocal FMD, were ≥18 years and had complete aortic imaging—from the aortic root to the iliac bifurcation—with adequate documentation of findings. Acceptable imaging studies included MR and contrast-enhanced CT (CT angiography, CT angiography for pulmonary embolism protocol or CT with contrast). Non-contrast CT scans (eg, coronary calcium scoring), abdominal ultrasound (with or without duplex), echocardiography, aortography, coronary angiography and coronary CT angiography were excluded due to inadequate or incomplete aortic visualisation. Complete aortic imaging could originate from a single study or multiple studies conducted over time at University Hospitals or outside institutions, provided the complete aorta was visualised with documentation of findings.

Data collection

We systematically reviewed electronic medical records, including office notes and imaging reports, to capture demographic data, medical history, medications, family history of FMD and aortic disease (in first-degree or second-degree relatives) and detailed FMD vascular phenotypes. History of hypertension, diabetes and hyperlipidaemia were defined as a diagnosis requiring pharmacologic treatment. Current medications were recorded from the most recent clinic visit, including virtual appointments.

Data were captured on the vascular phenotype or extent of vascular involvement of FMD, including: location of arterial bed involvement, prior FMD-related vascular events (eg, stroke, myocardial infarction), presence/history and location of dissections, aneurysms and history of FMD-related vascular procedures. In our study, a high-risk vascular phenotype was defined as one or more non-aortic aneurysms and/or one or more non-aortic dissections, a modification of the definition proposed by O’Connor et al, who defined high-risk status as ≥1 arterial dissection and/or ≥2 aortic or arterial aneurysms.10 Multivessel involvement was defined as involvement of ≥2 arterial beds, excluding the aorta. ‘Definite’ FMD-related arterial bed involvement was defined as diagnostic imaging demonstrating multifocal beading, ectasia, aneurysm or dissection, with classic multifocal FMD (beaded lesions) in at least one other arterial bed.1 Cases with suboptimal imaging (eg, poor contrast timing, imaging artefacts) or diagnostic ambiguity (eg, difficulty distinguishing FMD-related beading from atherosclerosis, imaging artefacts or mild ectasia/tortuosity or mild arterial irregularity without classic FMD features) were classified as ‘possible/uncertain’. For all other data fields, ‘possible/uncertain’ was recorded when complete chart review could not exclude or definitively confirm the finding.

Aortic findings—including ectasia, aneurysm, acute aortic syndromes (dissection, penetrating ulcers, intramural haematomas), stenotic lesions and congenital anomalies—were categorised by location and size. Significant thoracic aortic aneurysm was defined as a maximal diameter ≥4.0 cm from the aortic root to the diaphragm.11 Significant abdominal aortic aneurysm was defined as a maximal diameter ≥3.0 cm from the diaphragm to the aortic/iliac bifurcation.11 Aortic aneurysms were further classified by maximal diameter and specific anatomical location within the thoracic (root, ascending, arch, descending) or abdominal (suprarenal, juxtarenal, infrarenal) aorta. Significant aortic ectasia was recorded when explicitly noted in imaging reports or clinical notes but measured below the diameter thresholds for aneurysm (<4.0 cm thoracic and <3.0 cm abdominal), and these ectasias were often monitored with follow-up imaging.

Aortic dissections were classified using the Stanford system as Type A or Type B.11 Common aortic anatomical variants such as bovine arch and anomalous left vertebral artery arising from the aortic arch were not considered significant congenital anomalies.12 Genetic testing for arteriopathies was documented when available.

Patients were defined as having aortic involvement if they met at least one of the following criteria: significant congenital anomaly (eg, aberrant right subclavian artery, Kommerell’s diverticulum, ductus diverticulum or aortic coarctation), acute aortic syndrome, significant aortic aneurysm (thoracic or abdominal) or documented significant aortic ectasia below the threshold for aneurysm.

Referral indications, reasons for obtaining complete rather than partial aortic imaging and timing of events relative to FMD diagnosis were not systematically captured in the dataset.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive univariate analyses were performed to assess the prevalence and anatomical distribution of aortic pathologies, as well as to compare characteristics between patients in the single-centre cohort with and without aortic involvement. Categorical variables are presented as counts (frequencies), and continuous variables are summarised as means±SD.

Comparisons between the definite aortic involvement and no aortic involvement cohorts were compared using χ2 tests (or Fisher’s exact test for small sample sizes) for categorical data and independent two-sample t-tests for continuous variables. For all variables (eg, hypertension, diabetes, etc) other than aortic involvement, values classified as possible/uncertain were treated as absence of the characteristic. Prevalence estimates and corresponding 95% CIs were calculated using score CIs for binomial proportions.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the impact of classification uncertainty by reassigning patients labelled possible/uncertain for aortic involvement under two alternative schemes: (1) no aortic involvement versus possible/uncertain or definite aortic involvement (online supplemental table S1) and (2) no or possible/uncertain aortic involvement versus definite aortic involvement (online supplemental table S2). A significance level of 0.05 was used for all statistical tests.

Results

A total of 478 patients seen at University Hospitals were enrolled in the local site of the North American Registry for FMD between 1 January 2019 and 8 July 2024. Patients with focal FMD (n=9), mixed multifocal and focal FMD (n=12) or an FMD-like phenotype (n=1) were excluded. From the remaining 456 patients with multifocal FMD, only those with complete aortic imaging and adequate chart documentation were included in the final study population (n=360), as shown in figure 1 with clinical characteristics in table 1. The overall cohort was predominantly female (98.1%) with a mean age at diagnosis of 54.9±11.1 years. Extracranial carotid, renal and vertebral arteries were the most common sites of arterial bed involvement, and a majority of patients had multivessel involvement (75.3%).

Among the 360 patients with multifocal FMD and complete aortic imaging, 36 (10.0%; 95% CI 7.3% to 13.5%) had definite aortic involvement (figure 2). Within this subgroup, 9/36 patients (25.0%; 95% CI 13.8% to 41.1%) presented with congenital anomalies, most commonly aberrant right subclavian artery, including two cases associated with Kommerell’s diverticulum. Aneurysms were identified in 8/36 patients (22.2%; 95% CI 11.7% to 38.1%), with a mean diameter of 4.2 cm, and all were located in the proximal thoracic aorta. Aortic ectasia was the most frequent finding, occurring in 17/36 patients (47.2%; 95% CI 32% to 63%). There were no cases of abdominal aneurysm or ectasia. Acute aortic syndromes were identified in 3/36 patients (8.3%; 95% CI 2.9% to 21.8%): one Stanford Type A dissection requiring extensive surgery (including elephant trunk procedure and thoracic endovascular repair), one Stanford Type B intramural haematoma and one Stanford Type A dissection with coronary and aortic root involvement requiring ascending aortic replacement and coronary artery bypass grafting. No traumatic or iatrogenic precipitants were documented for the first two cases. The third case was considered possibly iatrogenic as it was identified at the time of coronary angiography for acute coronary syndrome. This patient also had an aberrant right subclavian artery and spontaneous coronary artery dissection with negative genetic testing for arteriopathies.

There were an additional 29/360 patients with complete aortic imaging classified as having possible/uncertain aortic involvement after review of medical records and imaging studies. Of these, 12 had possible thoracic ectasia that was not felt to be of clinical significance and not monitored, one had possible abdominal ectasia, one had possible abdominal aortic hypoplasia, 14 had abdominal tortuosity and one had thoracic tortuosity. As these findings did not meet the predefined criteria of aneurysm, clinically significant ectasia, dissection or congenital anomaly, we could not definitively classify these patients as having or not having aortic involvement.

There were two patients with bicuspid aortic valves in the overall cohort who did not have aortic findings. No patients in the definite or possible/uncertain aortic involvement groups were found to have bicuspid aortic valve.

Patients with possible/uncertain aortic involvement (n=29) were excluded from this comparison. Those with aortic involvement were more often male and had a higher prevalence of hypertension. They were also more likely to be on beta-blockers and ACE inhibitors/angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs). There was no significant difference in smoking history between patients with and without definite aortic involvement.

Family history

Family history of aortic aneurysm, aortic dissection and FMD were reported in 13.8%, 3.9% and 8.4% of the overall cohort, respectively, and this did not differ significantly between patients with or without aortic involvement. Across the 49 patients with a reported family history of aortic aneurysm, 29 (59.2%) had a family history of abdominal aortic aneurysm, seven (14.3%) had family history thoracic aortic aneurysm, eight (16.3%) had an unspecified aneurysm type and five (10.2%) reported family history of both abdominal and thoracic aneurysms. Family history of genetic arteriopathy was reported in only 4/356 (1.1%), but it was significantly more common in those with definite aortic involvement (5.6% vs 0.3%; p=0.033). Notably, three patients with aortic involvement (definite and possible) had a family history of neurofibromatosis: one patient with aortic coarctation, one with ductus diverticulum of the aorta and another with possible thoracic ectasia. None of these three patients had clinical features of neurofibromatosis and none underwent genetic testing.

Vascular bed involvement

Patients with definite aortic involvement exhibited significantly higher rates of mesenteric, renal and iliac arterial bed involvement with FMD. Conversely, extracranial carotid artery involvement was less prevalent among those with aortic involvement. Rates of multivessel involvement did not differ significantly between the groups.

The prevalence of non-aortic aneurysms was higher in the definite aortic involvement group. Other complications, including myocardial infarction, stroke and non-aortic arterial dissections, showed no significant differences between groups. The high-risk phenotype was more commonly observed in patients with aortic involvement (77.8% vs 54.6%; p=0.008). Of note, including the 29 cases with possible/uncertain aortic involvement as having definite aortic involvement did not alter the key statistical differences observed between groups. The definite aortic involvement group, regardless of the inclusion of possible/uncertain cases, remained significantly associated with male sex, hypertension, mesenteric and iliac artery involvement, presence of non-aortic aneurysms and high-risk vascular phenotype (online supplemental tables S1 and S2).

Genetic testing

Of the 47 patients in the entire cohort tested for genetic arteriopathies that have an overlapping phenotype with FMD, none had pathogenic mutations. A total of 18 variants of uncertain significance were identified across 14 genes, including TGFBR1, PLOD1, ZNF469, PLOD3, LTBP3, COL3A1 (n=3), COL4A1, COL5A1 (n=2), COL5A2, MYLK (n=2), MYH11 (n=2), PRKG1, NOTCH1, and FLNA. None of the nine patients with definite aortic involvement who underwent genetic testing had a confirmed pathogenic mutation or variant of uncertain significance identified.

Discussion

The present findings support the evolving understanding that FMD is a systemic arteriopathy that can extend beyond medium-sized arteries to involve the aorta. In our single-centre cohort of patients with multifocal FMD and complete aortic imaging, 10% demonstrated aortic involvement, manifesting as aneurysms or significant ectasia, acute aortic syndromes and congenital anomalies, with predominant thoracic aortic involvement.

The overall prevalence of aortic aneurysms in this cohort of 2.2% (8/360) is consistent with previous reports of 2–4%.5 6 A recent investigation of 94 women with multifocal FMD documented increases in aortic diameters across all anatomical landmarks compared with age-matched healthy female controls, even in the absence of aneurysm or dissection.7 In our cohort, ectasia was the most frequent aortic pathology, with nearly half of those with aortic involvement (47.2%) being monitored clinically for subaneurysmal aortic enlargement. The natural history of aortic ectasia among patients with FMD remains uncertain, and longitudinal studies are needed to clarify whether these changes confer an elevated risk of progression. Longitudinal series of thoracic aortic ectasia monitoring is limited; however, it has been reported that aneurysmal ascending aortas grow at an average rate of 0.2 cm/year, with faster growth in larger aneurysms.13

All cases of aortic aneurysm or significant ectasia in this cohort were confined to the thoracic region. In contrast, other studies have reported both increased abdominal aortic diameters and abdominal aneurysms among patients with FMD, although at lower frequencies than thoracic involvement.5–7 This discrepancy may reflect differences in populations or imaging protocols but underscores the importance of complete imaging of both thoracic and abdominal segments to fully characterise aortic involvement. Historically, abdominal imaging among patients with FMD was common given proximity to the renal arteries; however, the concept of thoracic aortic imaging for patients with FMD is emerging.

Congenital aortic anomalies accounted for 25% of aortic involvement in our cohort. Without an age-matched and sex-matched comparison group, we cannot determine whether these anomalies are more common in FMD or related to the disease itself. Nevertheless, given the limited prior descriptions of congenital aortic pathology in FMD, we included these findings to more fully characterise the spectrum of aortic pathology. Their occurrence should therefore be considered descriptive, but their presence may point to the possibility of shared factors between FMD and aortic development.

Patients in this cohort with definite aortic involvement exhibited several distinct clinical features. They were more often male, more frequently hypertensive and exhibited significantly higher rates of renal, mesenteric and iliac involvement, along with more frequent non-aortic aneurysms. Conversely, extracranial carotid disease was less common in this subgroup, which may reflect distinct phenotypic expressions—one favouring thoracoabdominal manifestations and aneurysms and the other more cerebrovascular. The North American Registry for FMD previously reported that men, although a minority among FMD patients, often have a more severe presentation, including higher rates of aneurysms, dissections and renal artery complications.14 Our present data also suggest that male sex may also be linked to aortic involvement in multifocal FMD. At this time, these associations cannot, however, be interpreted as independent predictors of a distinct high-risk phenotype. While exploratory, the observed patterns remain clinically relevant and warrant further evaluation in larger cohorts to improve phenotypic classification and risk stratification in FMD.

The heritability of FMD remains an area of active investigation. The North American Registry reported increased prevalence of stroke (53.5%), aneurysms (23.5%) and sudden death (19.8%) among first and second-degree relatives of FMD patients.5 Katz et al further identified a sex-specific pattern, with female relatives having an elevated risk of FMD and male relatives having an elevated risk of abdominal aortic aneurysms.15 Additionally, UBR4 has been recently implicated as a key driver gene for FMD, with knockout mouse models demonstrating vascular phenotypes of aortic dilation and aneurysm formation.16 In our cohort, all observed aortic aneurysms and ectasias were thoracic, yet among family members of patients, abdominal involvement was predominant. This finding aligns with Katz et al regarding the distribution of vascular lesions in family members of patients with FMD, though our dataset did not capture data on family members’ sex in relation to aneurysm location. Collectively, these findings support the notion that FMD is a systemic, potentially heritable arteriopathy affecting multiple arterial beds, including the aorta.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-centre, retrospective study at a specialised referral centre and included only patients with complete aortic imaging, which may have resulted in over-representation of patients with more complex disease. Baseline characteristics of excluded patients and indications for imaging were not consistently available, which limited our ability to assess for potential selection/indication bias and the overall generalisability of our findings. Second, although standardised diameter thresholds were used to define aneurysms, significant ectasias were defined using the treating clinician’s interpretation rather than a uniform diameter threshold. Consequently, the reported frequency of ectasia may be subject to interobserver variability and borderline cases may have been misclassified. Finally, the cross-sectional design and variable timing of aortic imaging limited the assessment of the temporal relationship between FMD diagnosis and aortic abnormalities as well as how these aortic abnormalities progress over time. Future studies in multicentre FMD populations using standardised criteria for ectasia across anatomic locations and longitudinal follow-up are warranted to better define the prevalence, natural progression and clinical significance of aortic abnormalities. Further genetic and molecular research may also help stratify high-risk individuals who could benefit from more aggressive surveillance or earlier intervention.

Despite these limitations, the study documents the spectrum and anatomical distribution of aortic findings in a relatively large rare-disease cohort and supports the consensus-based recommendation for comprehensive head to pelvis vascular imaging to identify areas of vascular pathology.1

Conclusion

We report predefined aortic findings in 10% of patients with multifocal FMD seen at a specialised referral centre who underwent complete aortic imaging, predominantly involving the thoracic aorta with aneurysm, ectasia and aortic arch anomalies. These findings contribute to the expanding understanding of aortic pathology as a potential manifestation of this multivessel arteriopathy and underscore the importance of complete aortic imaging in patients with multifocal FMD. They also suggest consideration of underlying FMD as a differential among female patients presenting with thoracic aortic aneurysm, especially in the presence of other clinical manifestations of FMD (hypertension, pulsatile tinnitus). Prompt detection and appropriate management of aortic involvement may mitigate severe complications and improve long-term outcomes, although studies with standardised imaging criteria, appropriate comparator populations and longitudinal follow-up are needed to further clarify the clinical significance and identify specific patients who may benefit from closer surveillance.
