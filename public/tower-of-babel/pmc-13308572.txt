Abdominal Desmoplastic Small Round Cell Tumour: Two Clinical Cases
Authors: Miguel Catalo, Catarina Macedo, Cátia Sousa, João Silva, Mariana Afonso, Cristiana Couto, Sofia Conde
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Desmoplastic small round cell tumour (DSRCT) is a rare and highly aggressive neoplasm, typically diagnosed in adolescents and young adults. With fewer than 500 cases described worldwide, treatment is multimodal and challenging, involving intensive chemotherapy, cytoreductive surgery, and radiotherapy. We present two clinical cases of adolescents diagnosed with advanced abdominal DSRCT, exhibiting distinct clinical courses. Emphasis is placed on the role of whole abdominal radiotherapy in locoregional control within a multidisciplinary approach. Therapeutic options, associated complications, and clinical management challenges are discussed.

Introduction

Desmoplastic small round cell tumour (DSRCT) was first described in 1989 by Gerald and Rosai. It is a high-grade mesenchymal neoplasm that predominantly affects adolescents and young adults, with a male preponderance. Histologically, it is characterised by small round blue cells with prominent desmoplastic stroma and a specific immunohistochemical profile, typically showing co-expression of epithelial, neuronal, and muscular markers, along with cytoplasmic-pattern WT1 positivity. Genetically, most cases harbour the t(11;22)(p13;q12) translocation, resulting in an EWSR1-WT1 gene fusion. This rearrangement is specific and diagnostic of the entity. DSRCT typically presents with large abdominal masses, often with diffuse peritoneal involvement and early metastasis, particularly to lymph nodes and liver [1,2].

Treatment is complex, lacking robust evidence due to the disease's rarity, but generally includes intensive chemotherapy (regimens based on those used for Ewing sarcoma), cytoreductive surgery, and radiotherapy. The use of whole abdominal radiotherapy (WART) at doses of 24-30 Gy has gained prominence for achieving good locoregional control, particularly following cytoreductive surgery [3-5].

Modern techniques, such as intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) and volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT), enable more homogeneous dose distribution and reduced toxicity to organs at risk [2,6]. Despite the aggressive approach, the five-year survival rate remains low, in the range of 5-20% [7-9].

We present two illustrative clinical cases demonstrating the distinct clinical evolution in two adolescents diagnosed with DSRCT, both presenting with peritoneal involvement. The first patient achieved a significant response to multimodal therapy, including WART; the second exhibited an aggressive course with multiple treatment-related complications, albeit with good control of disease in the irradiated region.

Case presentation

Case 1

A 15-year-old boy with a history of attention deficit disorder presented to the emergency department with recurrent abdominal pain and distension that woke him at night, anorexia, vomiting, constipation, right testicular pain, low back pain, asthenia, and unquantified weight loss evolving over approximately three weeks. Physical examination revealed a distended abdomen with a palpable mass of ill-defined contours in the right quadrants, painful on palpation, and a positive fluid wave sign. Abdominal ultrasound showed peritoneal effusion with heterogeneous content. Abdominopelvic computed tomography (CT) (Figure 1) demonstrated a large intraperitoneal mass measuring 23.6 × 9.2 × 14.9 cm, with lobulated contours, a contrast-enhancing solid superior component, and cystic-necrotic hypodense areas inferiorly, consistent with a malignant peritoneal lesion, along with small nodules in the right iliac fossa suggestive of peritoneal carcinomatosis.

Low-attenuation ascites is observed, without evidence of haemoperitoneum.

The patient was transferred to our institution for further investigation and treatment. Ultrasound-guided biopsy of the abdominal mass confirmed desmoplastic small round cell tumour (DSRCT), with immunohistochemical positivity for desmin (Figure 2) and CD99, and evidence of WT1 gene rearrangement. Staging with PET-CT (Figure 3) and thoracic CT revealed peritoneal implants, ascites, and a right supraclavicular lymph node, which also proved to be metastatic.

Voluminous lesion/conglomerate (arrows) occupying much of the abdominopelvic cavity, with extensive hypodense and hypometabolic components (predominantly in lower quadrants) and areas of marked hypermetabolism (apparently associated with solid components), particularly in the mesogastrium (SUVmax = 18.4) and the transition between the right flank and iliac fossa.

Tumour markers (AFP and hCG) were negative. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) identified an ARID1A mutation with potential therapeutic implications. Chemotherapy was initiated according to the EURO EWING 2012 protocol, alternating VDC (vincristine, doxorubicin, cyclophosphamide) and IE (ifosfamide, etoposide) regimens [10]. After four cycles of chemotherapy (two VDC and two IE), reassessment with CT and PET scan (Figure 4) showed a significant reduction in the pelvic mass (from approximately 20 cm to 15 cm in the longest axis) and decreased metabolic activity. During this phase, multifactorial acute kidney injury with bilateral hydronephrosis occurred, necessitating bilateral double-J stent placement, subsequently removed with clinical and laboratory improvement, along with transient iatrogenic hepatic cytolysis (negative serological and virological studies).

Axial (A), coronal (B), and sagittal (C) slices of 18F-FDG PET performed after four cycles of chemotherapy.

Persistent voluminous abdominopelvic mass (arrows). Compared with the previous study, marked dimensional regression and reduced metabolic activity of the lesion are noted (previous SUVmax = 6.73 → current = 3.37).

After six cycles of chemotherapy, the patient underwent cytoreductive surgery. Median laparotomy was performed with resection of a pelvic lesion adherent to the omentum, appendicectomy, peritoneal biopsies, and excisional biopsy of a left supraclavicular lymph node. Histology confirmed DSRCT with extensive necrosis (>50%), lymphovascular invasion, and residual viable tumour cells in <30% in the tumour bed. Surgical margins were <0.1 cm from the lesion, and epiploic nodules contained residual tumour foci. The excised lymph node showed partial tumour response.

Adjuvant whole abdominal radiotherapy was delivered at a total dose of 30 Gy in 20 fractions using volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT) with 6 MV photons (Figure 5). Treatment was poorly tolerated overall, with mild gastrointestinal toxicity and significant haematological toxicity, including grade 2 anaemia, grade 3 neutropenia, and grade 4 thrombocytopenia, requiring a temporary treatment interruption of approximately two weeks. Radiotherapy was subsequently completed.

Whole abdominal radiotherapy dose distribution plan in “colour wash” display, shown in axial (A), coronal (B), and sagittal (C) slices.

Following completion of intensive chemotherapy (14 cycles in total), complementary right supraclavicular radiotherapy was delivered at a total dose of 50.4 Gy in 28 fractions using VMAT with 6 MV photons. This treatment was well tolerated, with only grade 2 radiation dermatitis and grade 1 dysphagia. Subsequent imaging evaluations with PET and CT performed at intervals over the following months (Figure 6) showed no evidence of tumour recurrence or increased glycolytic metabolism consistent with malignant lesions.

Axial (A), coronal (B), and sagittal (C) slices of 18F-FDG PET, performed during follow-up, after completion of multimodal treatment.

No evidence of lesions with FDG hypermetabolism.

Maintenance therapy was initiated with intravenous vinorelbine on days 1, 8, and 15 of each 28-day cycle, combined with daily oral cyclophosphamide. Due to haematological toxicity, multiple dose reductions were required.

During follow-up over several months, the patient remained clinically stable, without clinical or imaging evidence of recurrence. Physical examinations showed good general condition, absence of lymphadenopathy, a painless abdomen with a well-healed surgical scar, and no palpable masses. Toxicities associated with maintenance therapy were mild to moderate.

The most recent imaging evaluations confirmed the absence of tumour metabolic activity and no signs of recurrence. The patient continues on maintenance therapy under close surveillance.

Case 2

A 14-year-old male adolescent with a history of attention deficit disorder treated with lisdexamfetamine presented to the emergency department with constipation and abdominal distension evolving over approximately two months. Abdominopelvic CT and PET scan (Figures 7-8) revealed multiple heterogeneous abdominal masses with central necrosis, bulky thoracic lymphadenopathy, and hepatic metastases.

Multiple heterogeneous abdominal masses with central necrosis (arrow).

Axial (A), coronal (B), and sagittal (C) slices of initial 18F-FDG PET at initial diagnosis.

Elevated metabolism in multiple voluminous abdominopelvic masses extending from the axial level of D1 to the rectal region, inseparable from adjacent visceral structures and central hypometabolic areas consistent with tumour necrosis (arrows). Similarly elevated FDG uptake in multiple lymph nodes, particularly retroperitoneal. Elevated FDG metabolism in a hepatic lesion (segment VI) and multiple foci/areas of abnormal hyperuptake in juxtadiaphragmatic, perihepatic/subcapsular hepatic, ligamentum falciforme, perisplenic, peri-intestinal, and anterior abdominopelvic topography, suggestive of malignant peritoneal involvement.

The patient was transferred to our institution for investigation and management. CT-guided biopsy confirmed desmoplastic small round cell tumour (DSRCT) (Figure 9). MYC and FOXO1 markers were negative. EWSR1 (22q12) and WT1 (11p13) rearrangements were positive.

Histology of the abdominal mass biopsy. H&E-stained slides.

A) Fragments of malignant mesenchymal neoplasm with cells showing rounded hyperchromatic nuclei and scant cytoplasm, arranged in sheets or small aggregates within dense stroma (20× magnification). B) Mild nuclear pleomorphism with identifiable mitoses (40× magnification).

Neoadjuvant first-line chemotherapy was administered according to the EURO EWING 2012 protocol (five VDC cycles and three IE cycles) [10], with documented partial imaging response.

The patient subsequently underwent radical cytoreductive surgery, including segmental enterectomy (15 cm ileum), atypical hepatectomy (segment VI), omentectomy, excision of multiple peritoneal implants, pelvic lymph node dissection, and liver biopsy. Histology revealed persistent viable tumour cells in more than 50% of the tumour bed, with hepatic, nodal, and peritoneal metastases. Ascitic fluid cytology was negative for malignancy.

Three additional adjuvant chemotherapy cycles were completed. Reassessment with CT, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and 18F-FDG PET (Figure 10) showed significant reduction in hepatic and pelvic lesions, with persistent hypermetabolic pararectal uptake.

Axial (A), coronal (B), and sagittal (C) slices of 18F-FDG PET performed after completion of chemotherapy and cytoreductive surgery.

Focus of intense metabolic activity (SUVmax 8.2) in left pararectal topography, apparently corresponding to densification overlying mesorectal fat and extending into the rectovesical pouch (arrows). Hypodense formations with faint FDG uptake are seen presacrally, right of the midline (SUVmax 2.9), and in ipsilateral ilio-obturator topography (SUVmax 2.1), representing tumour implants with low metabolic activity. Focal intense FDG hyperuptake (SUVmax 6.1) in the caudal planes of the laparotomy scar, possibly reflecting inflammatory/infectious complication.

Adjuvant radiotherapy was delivered using VMAT, comprising whole abdominal irradiation (30 Gy in 20 fractions) with an integrated boost to the pelvic region (40 Gy in 20 fractions) (Figure 11), concomitantly with weekly vinorelbine, which was discontinued after the first cycle due to grade 4 thrombocytopenia. Radiotherapy proceeded without interruptions, with mild gastrointestinal toxicity.

Whole abdominal radiotherapy dose distribution plan in “colour wash” display, shown in axial (A, B), coronal (C), and sagittal (D) slices.

Subsequent imaging revealed thoracic disease progression, with new supraclavicular, mediastinal, and bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy, alongside persistent pathological pelvic uptake. Second-line chemotherapy with trabectedin and irinotecan was initiated. Irinotecan was discontinued early due to severe diarrhoea. Treatment was complicated by significant haematological and hepatic toxicity. Large-volume abdominopelvic ascites was later documented on imaging (Figure 12).

Axial (A), coronal (B), and sagittal (C) slices of 18F-FDG PET performed after second-line systemic therapy.

Significant uptake in bilateral pararectal pelvic densifications - arrows (left SUVmax previous study = 7.44; current = 4.53). The remainder of the study also showed persistent supradiaphragmatic (supraclavicular, mediastinal, internal mammary) metastatic involvement with imaging/metabolic improvement compared with the prior study. However, voluminous abdominopelvic ascites without evident 18F-FDG hyperfixation but with marked volume increase.

The patient was later admitted with a subocclusive intestinal episode, interpreted as a consequence of prior extensive abdominal surgery and, predominantly, radiation-induced enteritis. Medical management included corticosteroid therapy and hyperbaric oxygen therapy; however, due to a lack of clinical improvement and inability to tolerate oral intake, an ileostomy was performed. The patient subsequently developed short bowel syndrome and became dependent on long-term parenteral nutrition.

Despite further surgical interventions and systemic therapy, the patient experienced progressive supradiaphragmatic disease with mediastinal vascular compression, requiring palliative thoracic radiotherapy (8 Gy in 1 fraction). His clinical condition continued to deteriorate, with persistent dependence on parenteral nutrition and recurrent infectious complications.

The patient died in the context of thoracic oncological progression and progressive multiorgan failure.

Discussion

The two presented cases illustrate the variability in clinical evolution and therapeutic response of abdominal DSRCT, even under similar treatment regimens. This heterogeneity reflects the disease's intrinsic aggressiveness, high initial tumour burden, and possibly the influence of poorly understood molecular factors.

The first case demonstrated a favourable response to multimodal treatment, including complete cytoreduction, whole abdominal radiotherapy, and maintenance therapy. Such therapeutic success may be related to good chemotherapy tolerance, absence of extensive hepatic metastases, and effective locoregional control with surgery and WART.

Whole abdominal radiotherapy (WART), particularly when delivered using modern techniques, has proven to be a safe and effective tool for locoregional control of DSRCT. A retrospective study from the MD Anderson Cancer Center reported experience with eight patients treated with WAP-IMRT (30 Gy/20 fractions) following cytoreductive surgery and chemotherapy. Notable findings included low gastrointestinal and haematological toxicity even with concomitant chemotherapy, approximately 25% reduction in radiation doses to bony structures (vertebrae and pelvis) compared with 3D conformal radiotherapy, median time to local or distant progression of 8.7 months, and complete remission at 20 months post-RT in one patient [6].

In contrast, the second patient, despite intensive therapy aligned with current standards--including induction chemotherapy, cytoreductive surgery, and adjuvant radiotherapy with VMAT--experienced severe gastrointestinal toxicity, short bowel syndrome, recurrent infections, and systemic progression. Whole abdominal radiotherapy with an integrated pelvic boost was delivered, with good initial tolerance and no documented progression within the irradiated abdominal and pelvic volumes in subsequent examinations. Disease progression initially occurred in the thoracic territory, with mediastinal, supraclavicular, and bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy, eventually leading to mediastinal vascular compression.

Gastrointestinal toxicity associated with radiotherapy, manifesting as extensive enteritis and significant intestinal losses, was likely multifactorial, related to prior extensive bowel surgery and reduced functional bowel reserve. The use of an integrated pelvic boost up to 40 Gy may also have contributed to the increased gastrointestinal toxicity and higher rate of complications observed in this patient. These factors culminated in the need for ileostomy, prolonged dependence on parenteral nutrition, and recurrent infectious complications.

The unfavourable outcome highlights the limitations of aggressive multimodal therapy in highly invasive neoplasms with limited sustained chemosensitivity. Persistence of viable disease after extensive surgery and rapid extra-abdominal progression suggests intrinsic biological resistance to conventional treatment.

This case underscores the importance of careful patient selection and close monitoring for gastrointestinal toxicity in patients undergoing whole abdominal radiotherapy, even with advanced techniques such as IMRT/VMAT. Preoperative assessment of bowel anatomy and function, judicious radiation planning, and proactive supportive care remain essential to minimise treatment-related morbidity.

Furthermore, integration of targeted therapies and participation in international clinical trials may expand therapeutic options [2,9,11].

Conclusions

DSRCT remains one of the greatest challenges in paediatric oncology due to its biological aggressiveness, low survival rates, and scarcity of robust data for treatment standardisation. Intensive multimodal therapy, including high-intensity chemotherapy, cytoreductive surgery, and whole abdominal radiotherapy, can yield good outcomes in selected patients, as demonstrated in the first presented case. Additionally, whole abdominal radiotherapy appears to be associated with effective local control. However, associated risks should not be underestimated, as illustrated by the second case. Cumulative toxicity, particularly haematological and gastrointestinal, and the potential for progression must be considered in therapeutic decision-making. Integration of experienced multidisciplinary teams and personalisation of treatment plans based on emerging molecular biomarkers will be crucial for improving future prognoses.
