Predicting stroke and myocardial infarction risk in Takayasu arteritis with automated machine learning models
Authors: Yi-Ting Lu, Zeng-Lei Zhang, Xing-Yu Zhou, Di Zhang, Tao Tian, Peng Fan, Ying Zhang, Xian-Liang Zhou
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SummaryFew models exist for predicting severe ischemic complications (SIC) in patients with Takayasu arteritis (TA). We conducted a retrospective analysis of 703 patients with TA from January 2010 to December 2019 to establish an SIC prediction model for TA. SIC was defined as ischemic stroke and myocardial infarction. SIC was present in 97 of 703 (13.8%) patients with TA. Common iliac artery, coronary artery, internal carotid artery, subclavian artery, vertebral artery, renal artery involvement, chest pain, hyperlipidemia, absent pulse, higher BMI, vascular occlusion, asymmetric blood pressure in both upper limbs, visual disturbance, and older age were selected as predictive risk factors. Considering both discrimination and calibration performance, the Weighted Subspace Random Forest model was the most optimal model, boasting an area under the curve of 0.773 (95% confidence interval [0.652, 0.894]) in the validation cohort. Effective models for predicting SIC in TA may help clinicians identify high-risk patients and make targeted interventions.

Introduction

Takayasu arteritis (TA) is a rare chronic granulomatous large-vessel vasculitis with unknown etiology.1 It primarily manifests in Asian females between the ages of 10 and 40, although individuals of any race, age or gender can be affected.2 TA tends to affect the aorta and its major branches. The arterial wall inflammation can progressively lead to stenosis, occlusion, dilation, or aneurysm formation, subsequently causing ischemic symptoms such as claudication, decreased or absent brachial artery pulse and asymmetric blood pressure in the upper limbs.3

Patients with TA present with a notably elevated risk of severe ischemic complications (SIC), such as stroke and myocardial infarction (MI).4 Cavalli et al. reported that TA causes up to 10% of acute ischemic heart disease cases in female patients younger than 40 years.5 Moreover, Huo et al. found that 5.9% of patients with TA also had myocardial ischemia and neurologic symptoms6 and Mirouse et al. reported that 63 of 320 (20%) patients with TA experience cerebrovascular ischemic events.7 When SIC occurs, it is associated with an increased risk of disability and life-threatening conditions.8 Therefore, to avoid catastrophic outcomes, we urgently need to identify predictors associated with stroke and MI in patients with TA. However, there are currently no effective models for predicting SIC in the Chinese TA population.

Machine-learning tools are an emerging technology with great potential to improve diagnostic accuracy and patient risk stratification, and predict outcomes, by analyzing the structure and patterns of medical data.9 In this study, we aimed to describe the prevalence and characteristics of patients with TA with SIC in a large sample size of cohort and develop machine-learning prediction models of SIC risk to assist with careful monitoring and proactive management of patients with TA.

Results

A total of 703 patients with TA were enrolled in this study, of whom 17 had MI, 73 had ischemic stroke and 7 had both MI and ischemic stroke (Figure 1). Therefore, the prevalence of MI and ischemic stroke in the TA cohort was 3.4% and 11.4%, respectively, and the total prevalence of SIC was 13.8% (97/703). Discussion

In our single-center, large cohort study, we analyzed the clinical characteristics of a large cohort of patients with TA and SIC and found that the prevalence of SIC in TA was approximately 13.8%. Patients with SIC were older at presentation and had more cardiovascular risk factors (including higher BMI, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and diabetes) than patients without SIC. A higher urine microalbumin level and more subclavian artery, internal carotid artery, renal artery, common iliac artery, and coronary artery lesion involvement were observed in patients with SIC. Moreover, using automated machine-learning algorithms, we developed well-performing prediction models for the occurrence of SIC, including ischemic stroke and MI, in patients with TA.

Coronary arteries and intracranial arteries are commonly involved in TA and inflammation in these arteries is a major cause of morbidity and mortality in patients with TA. As previously reported, the proportion of coronary artery and intracranial artery involvement in patients with TA is 7.7%–53.2% and 13.4%–41.1%, respectively.10,11,12,13,14 Due to the chronic occlusive inflammatory nature of TA, previous studies have consistently demonstrated a significant increase in the frequency of severe ischemic events, such as MI and stroke, during disease progression.15,16,17 Accelerated atherosclerosis and the microembolic phenomenon caused by direct, persistent vascular inflammation may play a vital role in the pathogenesis of SIC in patients with TA.18,19 In our cohort, the prevalence of SIC in patients with TA was 13.8%, which included 11.4% of patients with ischemic stroke and 3.4% of patients with MI. These findings aligned with prior research, which reported that 8.9%–15.8% of patients with TA suffered from stroke and 3.4%–22.2% of patients with TA experienced MI.4,8,20,21,22,23,24,25

In our study, patients with TA combined with SIC were older than those without SIC.17,26 This is consistent with a recent South Korean study of 1065 patients, which used time-dependent Cox regression analysis and concluded that age was the only factor associated with increased risk of stroke in patients with TA.17 Liu et al. also found that advancing age was significantly associated with increased risk of cardiovascular disease in patients with TA.27 Moreover, in patients with SIC, more abnormal radiologic findings were identified in a number of arteries, including vertebral,28 subclavian, internal carotid,8,28 coronary, common iliac and renal artery.22 Furthermore, patients with SIC often present with more symptoms related to stroke or MI, such as chest pain and visual disturbance.24,26,28

While there is a clear link between SIC and cardiovascular disease in patients with TA, the cardiovascular risk factors for SIC in TA remain controversial. In our cohort, cardiovascular risk factors, including higher BMI, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and diabetes were more frequent in the SIC group, compared with the non-SIC group. In a previous study, Comarmond et al. assessed the prevalence of myocardial ischemic disease in 27 patients with TA using late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) on cardiac MRI and found a higher proportion of renovascular hypertension in the LGE (+) group.22 By comparing patients with TA with and without severe ischemic events, Yu et al. also found that patients with a severe ischemic event were more likely to have traditional cardiovascular risk factors.24 Kong et al. analyzed 122 patients with TA and reported that hyperlipidemia is an independent risk factor for cerebral infarction in TA.28 In contrast, Couture et al. conducted a multicenter case-controlled study including 17 TA patients with stroke and 17 matched patients with TA without neurological involvement and found that both groups had comparable cardiovascular risk factors.8 We speculate that these conflicting results may be due to differences in the number of patients enrolled and their ethnic backgrounds in each study.

Although many risk factors associated with SIC have been explored in patients with TA, no reliable predictive model has been established thus far. In this study, we used logistic regression in combination with LASSO regression analysis and best subset analysis to select 14 risk factors for SIC in patients with TA. We explored 96 machine-learning models using 10-fold cross-validation on a derivation cohort, tested these on a validation cohort. As shown in Table 2, the baseline data of the validation set and the derivation set were not completely consistent, which suggested that the establishment of the model had a certain degree of extrapolation rationality. Moreover, we applied the SHAP method to visualize chosen variables and interpret the model.

In terms of the model performances, we performed multi-directional evaluations based on discrimination, calibration ability, and DCA values. The top five models with the highest AUC in the validation data were bagFDAGCV (AUC: 0.773 [95% CI] [0.652, 0.894]), bagFDA (0.771 [0.652, 0.891]), wsrf (0.770 [0.656, 0.884]), pda2 (0.762 [0.615, 0.908]) and rf (0.761 [0.640, 0.882]), with no statistical differences among them. Regarding calibration ability, we also evaluated these five models and found that the best Brier score from lowest to highest was wsrf (Brier score, 95%CI: 0.097 [0.062, 0.133]), bagFDAGCV (0.098 [0.064, 0.135]), rf (0.098 [0.059, 0.141]) and bagFDA (0.100 [0.067, 0.137]), However, pda2 was excluded due to its high Brier score. Moreover, the DCA curve showed that the pda2 model performed best in the validation cohort, followed by wsrf and rf, bagFDA, and bagFDAGCV. Thus, combining all the above aspects of the analysis, wsrf was the optimal model to predict SIC in patients with TA.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, although the cohort was large, inevitable bias existed due to its single-center, retrospective, and cross-sectional nature. In this study, we are unable to thoroughly explore the relationships between disease progression or medication usage, and the occurrence of SIC due to the inherent limitation of not being able to address the temporal association between exposures and outcomes. Second, because some patients were transferred to our hospital for treatment, detailed imaging information of intracranial artery involvement was not shown in this study. Third, we need to acknowledge the lack of a rigorous external validation dataset for established models. Therefore, multicenter, large-scale, longitudinal, or prospective studies are required to further improve the prediction model and explain the relationship between risk factors and SIC.

Conclusions

SIC are not infrequent throughout the progressive course of TA. It is crucial to underscore the need for careful monitoring and proactive management, given the substantial burden of SIC in TA. Developing effective prediction models can help clinicians identify patients at a higher risk for SIC and prioritize resources and targeted interventions, leading to fewer catastrophic outcomes and more efficient and targeted healthcare delivery.

Experimental model and study participant details

We conducted a retrospective study, enrolling 703 patients of East Asian ancestry who were diagnosed with TA between January 2010 and December 2019 at Fuwai Hospital in Beijing, China. SIC was defined as ischemic stroke and MI in this study. The diagnosis of ischemic stroke was based on neurologic signs/symptoms and cranial Computer tomography or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) results. MI was diagnosed by clinical symptoms and electrocardiography, echocardiography, laboratory investigations and/or coronary angiography. All patients fulfilled the 1990 American College of Rheumatology (ACR) guidelines for the classification of TA.29 Patients who fulfilled at least 3 of the 6 following criteria were considered as TA: age at disease onset <40 years, claudication of an extremity, a decreased brachial artery pulse, systolic blood pressure difference >10 mmHg between arms, bruits over the subclavian arteries or the aorta, and abnormal arteriographic evidence of the entire aorta, its primary branches, or large arteries in the proximal upper and lower extremities. Only patients in whom SIC occurred at diagnosis or during the evolution of TA were considered to have an event, as assessed by two experienced senior specialists.8

Patients meeting any of the following conditions were excluded from the analysis: (1) comorbidities involving other vasculitis, such as giant cell arteritis, Kawasaki disease, (2) potential cardioembolic sources, such as atrial fibrillation, mitral stenosis, a prosthetic valve, dilated cardiomyopathy, sick sinus syndrome, acute bacterial endocarditis, or patent foramen ovale, and (3) active infections, hematologic diseases, tumors, or other autoimmune diseases.12,20 All enrolled patients were divided into derivation and validation cohorts, according to the date of admission, with 578 patients admitted from January 2010 through December 2017 included in the derivation cohort and 125 patients admitted from January 2018 through December 2019 in the validation cohort. The age, sex distribution of the cohorts was presented in Table 1. This study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fuwai Hospital. All patients provided written informed consent in advance.

Method details

Collection of clinical data

Comprehensive cross-sectional medical data, encompassing demographic parameters such as age, gender, age at disease onset, and clinical manifestations (including fever, fatigue, amaurosis, dizziness, syncope and so on), as well as biological and imaging results (including white blood cell count, platelet count, alanine aminotransferase, creatinine and so on), vascular lesion involvement, and prior medical histories of SIC, were collected retrospectively.

Selection of predictors

Initially, a preliminary screening of baseline variables was conducted. Variables with more than 5% missing values were removed, while variables with less than 5% missing values were imputed using multiple imputation. Variables that had only one factor level, zero variance or near-zero variance were deleted using the nearZeroVar function from the caret package. Logistic regression was employed for univariate analysis and variables with a p-value less than 0.05 were retained. These variables were further subjected to the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression analysis. After incorporating age, LASSO regression was performed with λ value as a reference to identify variables that achieved the minimum target parameter mean. In addition, we performed the best subset analysis. Finally, the candidate variables were obtained by synthesizing the above three methods.

Establishment of machine learning-based models

The machine-learning models used in this study were sourced from the caret package, an integrated platform for machine learning in R software. The package consists of 238 available machine-learning models, of which 191 are suitable for classification prediction models. For this study, a total of 96 machine-learning models from the caret package were extracted and used as the model library for subsequent analysis. These models were trained using 10-fold cross-validation on the derivation cohort to optimize the hyperparameters and the trained models were then tested on the validation cohort.

Evaluation of model performance

All models were identified that were applicable to the study data. The performance of the models in the validation cohort was evaluated based on discrimination and calibration. The area under curve of the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) was calculated to assess discrimination or accuracy of predictive models. After calculating the mean AUROC, the top five best models were chosen for further calibration evaluation by calibration plots and Brier scores. Moreover, clinical decision curve analysis (DCA) was also employed to assess the clinical utility of the models.

Visualization of predictors

One of the most optimal models, in conjunction with Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP), was employed to visualize the significance of model parameters. This was achieved by calculating the contribution of each feature to the predicted outcomes, thus enhancing the clinical interpretability of the model.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Continuous variables with normal distribution are presented as means and standard deviations, while continuous variables with non-normal distribution are presented as medians (Q1, Q3). Categorical variables are presented as absolute and relative frequencies. Differences between quantitative variables were analyzed using the nonparametric paired Wilcoxon test or Student’s t-test and Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. R (version 4.3.0) software was used for statistical analysis.
