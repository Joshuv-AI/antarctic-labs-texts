Spherical and Semibrick Classifications


Abstract
Zusammenfassung This article provides an overview of the techniques related to classification of spherical and more general objects within triangulated categories, and its relationship with algebraic geometry, representation theory and symplectic geometry. The primary focus are the techniques within the geometric but ‘finite’ setting of [ HW1 ] , but other approaches including to more general settings by [ IU , BDL , SW , KS , S2 ] are also surveyed, in varying levels of detail. Various explicit examples are provided.

1.1
Introduction

Through time, various phenomena in mathematics become naturally linked. Motivated by ideas and approaches to Broué’s conjecture in modular representation theory in the 1990s, particularly Okuyama’s method, in
[
R
, 5.1]
Rickard characterised ‘simple-like’ objects in the derived category of a finite dimensional symmetric algebra. The key observation was that if some collection of generating objects looks ‘a bit like simples’, they are simples, after passing through some shiny new derived equivalence. A follow-up paper of
[
AlN
]
refined these ideas further, generalising in two directions: first by dropping the symmetric assumption, then second by replacing the derived equivalence with a more general statement on t-structures.

These generating collections of ‘simple-like’ objects were called
cohomologically Schurian sets of generators
, and perhaps mercifully, through time this language has softened to the linguistically easier
simple-minded collections
. From the viewpoint of what follows, we pause here to dwell slightly on the loss of the word ‘generator’ from the lexicon, since this key technical point is perhaps the singlemost distinguishing factor that is lacking in all the geometric phenomena below.

Around the same time, but instead motivated by mirror symmetry considerations, spherical objects entered the fray
[
ST
]
. For a given
k
-linear triangulated category
\mathcal{T}
, these are by definition objects
x\in\mathcal{T}
which share the same cohomology as a sphere, in the sense that

\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{T}}(x,x[t])\cong\begin{cases}k&\mbox{if }t=0,d\\
0&\mbox{else,}\end{cases}

together with some additional compatibility with the Serre functor. Modulo the usual caveats regarding suitable enhancements, the key property of spherical objects
x
is that they generate an equivalence
T_{S}\colon\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{T}
, called the spherical twist.

Delaying slightly the big reveal, there are multiple reasons why spherical objects are important, many of which we will not list here. The classification of spherical objects became a desirable theme, partly since in the setting of Fukaya categories various remarkable topological consequences immediately follow. Similarly, in various algebraic-geometric settings there are immediate applications to the structure of the autoequivalence group, and to the connectedness of the stability manifold.

By definition spherical objects are ‘a bit like a sphere’, however as observed by many authors, they too have the habit of behaving ‘a bit like a simple’. From this, the obvious analogy is born. Much like the very best analogies, from the outset this is completely ridiculous: spherical objects are
single
objects, and whilst Rickard demonstrated that having a generating set of simples is desirable, it was well-known that having a single simple object is generally as good as useless.

The above point makes the need to restrict the setting obvious. Below we survey various categories in which both the classification of single simple objects
and
of generating sets of simple objects is possible, but in the interest of full disclosure, we need to be clear that there is currently no single abstract framework in which all these settings naturally belong. The techniques surveyed do transfer between the settings, after some reinterpretation, but work is required to do this.

Although the axiomatics currently escapes us, we do contest that all the categories
\mathcal{C}
considered in this survey should be viewed as
Iyama dream categories
in a manner that will one day be axiomatically defined. The main point is that all the categories
\mathcal{C}
considered below turn out to admit the dream level of completions: in particular, all simple objects can be completed to a simple-minded collection, and so the difference between single simple/spherical objects and generating collections melts away. The broader reason why the categories
\mathcal{C}
should satisfy this property remains a mystery; currently our techniques only tell us that it is true, they do not give much of a hint of why or when we should expect this behaviour more generally.

The remainder of this article overviews and compares approaches to the classification of spherical objects, in various contexts. Of course, given the above, we in fact survey more general classification results too, and the differing algorithms used in the various cases.

As one piece of language, many of the categories below will have a naturally associated hyperplane arrangement. In the setting that this arrangement is finite, we will denote the category
\mathcal{C}
and refer to it as a ‘finite’ setting, and in the case when the arrangement is infinite, we will denote the category
\mathcal{D}
, and refer to it as the ‘affine’ setting. Given the fact that Iyama dream categories are not yet defined, there are of course some categories considered below (such as
\mathop{\mathrm{D}^{\mathrm{b}}}(\operatorname{mod}A)
in §
1.6
) which strictly speaking fall into neither setting, but their simple-like objects can still be understood.

1.2
Definitions

In a triangulated category
\mathcal{T}
the notion of a
brick
, recalled below, is a replacement for the notion of a simple module. Indeed, the vanishing of the negative Ext groups mimicks the behaviour of an object in the heart of a t-structure, whilst the condition that the Hom-space is one-dimensional mimicks Schur’s lemma.

Definition 1.2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be a triangulated category over
\mathbb{C}
.

(1)

x\in\mathcal{T}
is a
brick

if
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{T}}(x,x[i])=0
for all
i<0
and
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{T}}(x,x)=\mathbb{C}
.

(2)

A
semibrick
is an object of the form
y=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}
where each
x_{i}
is a brick, and further
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{T}}(x_{i},x_{j}[k])=0
for all
i\neq j
and
k\leq 0
.

(3)

A
simple-minded collection (smc)
in
\mathcal{T}
is a collection of objects
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
such that
y=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}
is a semibrick, and further
y
is a classical generator of
\mathcal{T}
, in the sense that
\mathcal{T}
is the smallest triangulated subcategory of
\mathcal{T}
containing
y
that is closed under taking direct summands.

By abuse of notation, given an smc
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
,
we also call the direct sum
y=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}
an smc of
\mathcal{T}
.

Definition 1.2.2
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be a triangulated category over
\mathbb{C}
,
and
P\in\mathcal{T}
an object.

(1)

P
is called
presilting
if
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{T}}(P,P[i])=0
for all
i>0
.

(2)

P
is called
silting
if
P
is presilting, and a classical generator of
\mathcal{T}
.

Remark 1.2.3
.

For a finite dimensional
\mathbb{C}
-algebra
A
,
the full collection of simple
A
-modules is an example of a simple-minded collection in
\mathop{\mathrm{D}^{\mathrm{b}}}(\operatorname{mod}A)
, and
A
itself is an example of a silting object in
\operatorname{K^{b}}(\operatorname{proj}A)
.
Note that, the notions of smc and silting are in some sense dual to each other, but they exist in different categories.

1.3
Summary of Common Strategy

The aim of this section is to give an overview of the common strategy to most approaches to the classification of spherical (and others) objects. Details appear later, in future sections. The most common strategy is the following:

(1)

Fix a category
\mathcal{T}
, and some subset of objects defined by some chosen homological property.

(2)

Consider some invariant
\ell(x)\in\mathbb{R}
of objects
x
in
\mathcal{T}
.

(3)

Classify all objects
x
in
\mathcal{T}
, which

(a)

have small
\ell(x)
, and

(b)

satisfy the homological property in (1).

(4)

Find an induction step that makes the invariant
\ell(x)
smaller, by e.g. applying functors such as spherical twists, or by varying t-structures.

There are really four choices: the category, the homological property, the invariant, and the induction step. In some sense it has to be taken as given that the classification in (3) is possible, since otherwise the wider problem is hopeless. Combined, the choices above should be viewed as a choice of an algorithm that has as its outcome the desired classification of objects satisfying the homological condition in (1).

We remark that step (4) can often (but not always, see
1.6.8
) be thought of as a choice of a set of autoequivalences
\nabla
. From that viewpoint, the existence of an improvement of
\ell(x)
is equivalent to establishing the non-emptiness of the set

\left\{\,\Phi\in\nabla\mid\ell(\Phi x)<\ell(x)\,\right\}.

Depending on the choices made in (1)–(3), there is sometimes advantage in having
\nabla
to be a large set, and sometimes there is advantage in having
\nabla
to be small. In the works by Ishii-Uehara
[
IU
]
and Bapat-Deopurkar-Licata
[
BDL
]
surveyed below,
\nabla
consists of (single) spherical twists along spherical sheaves. On the other hand, in §
1.4

\nabla
is taken to be compositions of such functors, albeit in a controlled way.

In what follows, we survey the main approaches in settings from algebraic geometry, representation theory and symplectic geometry. In each case, the four choices above are all subtly different, mainly since they strongly depend on the precise category being considered, and on what precise objects are being classified.

1.4
Geometry Settings and HomMMP

This section is concerned with the following three geometric settings.

Setup 1.4.1

(Geometric setup).

Let
f\colon X\to\operatorname{Spec}R
be one of the following.

(1)

The minimal resolution
f\colon\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}/G
, where
G\leq\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})
is finite.

(2)

A partial crepant resolution
f\colon\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}/G
, where
G\leq\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})
is finite.
In other words,
f
is a crepant birational morphism.

(3)

A threefold flopping contraction
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to\operatorname{Spec}\mathcal{R}

such that
\mathcal{R}
is an isolated complete local cDV singularity.

In (1)
\mathcal{Z}
is smooth, in (2)
\mathcal{Y}
has at worst Kleinian singularities, and in (3)
\mathcal{X}
has at worst isolated cDV singularities. Of course, (1) is really just a special case of (2).

We will consider the
null category

\mathcal{C}\coloneq\{a\in\mathop{\mathrm{D}^{\mathrm{b}}}(\operatorname{coh}X)\mid\mathbf{R}f_{*}a=0\},

where
X
is either
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}
or
\mathcal{Z}
above. Later we will also consider the category

\mathcal{D}\coloneq\{a\in\mathop{\mathrm{D}^{\mathrm{b}}}(\operatorname{coh}X)\mid\operatorname{Supp}a\subseteq\mathtt{C}\},

where
\mathtt{C}
is the exceptional locus, but our primary focus will be on the category
\mathcal{C}
.

1.4.1
Hyperplane Arrangements

For each
X
as above, we first associate a marked Dynkin diagram
(\Delta,\mathcal{J})
as follows.

(1)

For the minimal resolution
\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}/G
, the dual graph of the exceptional curve
\mathtt{C}_{\mathrm{red}}
is a Dynkin diagram
\Delta
of ADE type. We associate to
\mathcal{Z}
the pair
(\Delta,\emptyset)
.

(2)

If
\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}/G
is a partial crepant resolution,
then consider the minimal resolution
\mathcal{Z}\to\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}/G
,
and let
\Delta
be the ADE Dynkin diagram associated to
\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}/G
.
We associate to
\mathcal{Y}
the pair
(\Delta,\mathcal{J})
, where
\mathcal{J}\subset\Delta
is the set of vertices that correspond to exceptional curves
\mathtt{C}_{i}\subset\mathcal{Z}
contracted by
\mathcal{Z}\to\mathcal{Y}
.

(3)

If
\mathcal{X}\to\operatorname{Spec}\mathcal{R}
is a threefold flopping contraction,
then for a general element
g\in\mathcal{R}
, the quotient
\mathcal{R}/g
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{C}[\![x,y]\!]^{G}
for some finite
G\leq\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})

\mathcal{X}\otimes_{\mathcal{R}}\mathcal{R}/(g)\to\operatorname{Spec}\mathcal{R}/(g)
is a partial crepant resolution of the form in (2). Through this, we associate to
\mathcal{X}
the corresponding
(\Delta,\mathcal{J})
in (2).

Given the data
(\Delta,\mathcal{J})
, it is possible to construct a finite hyperplane arrangement
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{J}}
. It is classical that
\Delta
has an associated root system
\mathfrak{h}=\bigoplus_{i\in\Delta}\mathbb{R}\upalpha_{i}
, where
\upalpha_{i}
are the simple roots. Let
\mathfrak{h}_{\mathcal{J}}\coloneq{\mathfrak{h}}/{\bigoplus_{i\in\mathcal{J}}\upalpha_{i}}
be the quotient space corresponding to the marking
\mathcal{J}\subset\Delta
, with the natural projection

\uppi_{\mathcal{J}}\colon\mathfrak{h}\to\mathfrak{h}_{\mathcal{J}}.

Note that the space
\mathfrak{h}_{\mathcal{J}}
has basis
\{\uppi_{\mathcal{J}}(\upalpha_{j})\mid j\in\Delta\setminus\mathcal{J}\}
.

Definition 1.4.2
.

A
restricted positive root
in
\mathfrak{h}_{\mathcal{J}}
is a non-zero element
\uppi_{\mathcal{J}}(\upalpha)\in\mathfrak{h}_{\mathcal{J}}
for some positive root
\upalpha\in\mathfrak{h}
.
A restricted positive root is
primitive
if it is not a multiple of another positive restricted root.

Example 1.4.3
.

Consider the Dynkin data
, where
\mathcal{J}
is the set of black nodes. Projecting all positive roots of
E_{6}
via
\uppi_{\mathcal{J}}
, and discounting the zeros, gives the set

\{10,01,02,11,12,13\}

where for example

(1,1)
in
\mathfrak{h}_{\mathcal{J}}
under the prescribed basis above. These are the restricted positive roots. The primitive restricted roots are
\{10,01,11,12,13\}
.

Definition 1.4.4
.

Let
(\Delta,\mathcal{J})
be a marked Dynkin diagram,
and
\mathfrak{h}_{\mathcal{J}}
the associated space.
Set
\Theta_{\mathcal{J}}\coloneq(\mathfrak{h}_{\mathcal{J}})^{\vee}
, and for each restricted positive root
\upbeta=\uppi_{\mathcal{J}}(\upalpha)

\mathsf{H}_{\upbeta}\coloneq\{(\upvartheta_{i})\in\Theta_{\mathcal{J}}\mid\sum\upbeta_{i}\upvartheta_{i}=0\}\subseteq\Theta_{\mathcal{J}}.

1.4.2
Functors

Let
f\colon X\to\operatorname{Spec}R
be one of the options in Setup
1.4.1
.
Van den Bergh
[
VdB
]
proves the existence of a vector bundle
\mathcal{O}\oplus\mathcal{N}
on
X
, and a derived equivalence

\Psi_{X}\colon\mathop{\mathrm{D}^{\mathrm{b}}}(\operatorname{coh}X)\xrightarrow{\operatorname{{\mathbf{R}}Hom}_{X}(\mathcal{O}\oplus\mathcal{N},-)}\mathop{\mathrm{D}^{\mathrm{b}}}(\operatorname{mod}\Lambda),

where
\Lambda\coloneq\operatorname{End}_{X}(\mathcal{O}\oplus\mathcal{N})
.

Remark 1.4.6
.

When
\dim X=2
, the noncommutative algebra
\Lambda
can be described in terms of representation theory.

(1)

If
X=\mathcal{Z}
is the minimal resolution, then
\Lambda
is isomorphic to the preprojective algebra
\Uppi
of the corresponding extended Dynkin diagram.

(2)

If
X=\mathcal{Y}
is a partial crepant resolution of a Kleinian singularity,
let
(\Delta,\mathcal{J})
be the associated marked Dynkin diagram.
Let
\Uppi
be as in (1), and
e_{0},e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\in\Uppi
the idempotents,
where
0
corresponds to the extended vertex.
Then
\Lambda
is isomorphic to the contracted affine preprojective algebra
\upvarepsilon_{\mathcal{J}}\Uppi\upvarepsilon_{\mathcal{J}}
, where
\upvarepsilon_{\mathcal{J}}=1-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{J}}e_{i}
.

In each of the cases of Setup
1.4.1
, we next associate categorical information to the hyperplane arrangement
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{J}}
.

Summary 1.4.7
.

Given a fixed
f\colon X\to\operatorname{Spec}R
as in Setup
1.4.1
, consider the associated
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{J}}
. To this, we associate the following categorical data:

—

to every chamber
\mathrm{C}
, a category
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{C}}
,

—

to every wall-crossing
s_{i}\colon\mathrm{C}\to\mathrm{D}
, an equivalence
\Phi_{i}\colon\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{C}}\to\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{D}}
,

in the following way, depending on the type of
f\colon X\to\operatorname{Spec}R
.

(1)

For the minimal resolution
f\colon\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}/G
, with associated
(\Delta,\emptyset)
, the hyperplane arrangement is the classical reflection arrangement of the Weyl group.
To every chamber
\mathrm{C}
we associate the same category, namely

\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{C}}\coloneq\{a\in\mathop{\mathrm{D}^{\mathrm{b}}}(\operatorname{coh}\mathcal{Z})\mid Rf_{*}a=0\},

and to each simple wall crossing
s_{i}\colon\mathrm{C}\to\mathrm{D}
with
i=1,\ldots,n
, is assigned the spherical twist along
\mathcal{O}_{\mathtt{C}_{i}}(-1)
,
where
\mathtt{C}_{i}
is the irreducible component corresponding to the index
i
.

(2)

For a partial crepant resolution
\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}/G
, with associated
(\Delta,\mathcal{J})
, we give an algebraic reinterpretation and generalisation of the above.
Now chambers in
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{J}}
are indexed by certain pairs
(x,\mathcal{I})
where
x
belongs to the Weyl group of
\Updelta
, and
\mathcal{I}\subset\Updelta

[
IW2
, 1.12]
.
For a chamber
\mathrm{C}=(x,\mathcal{I})
, set
\Lambda_{\mathrm{C}}\coloneq\upvarepsilon_{\mathcal{I}}\Uppi\upvarepsilon_{\mathcal{I}}
and associate the category

\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{C}}\coloneq\{a\in\mathop{\mathrm{D}^{\mathrm{b}}}(\operatorname{mod}\upvarepsilon_{\mathcal{I}}\Uppi\upvarepsilon_{\mathcal{I}})\mid e_{0}\,\mathrm{H}^{*}(a)=0\}.

where
e_{0}
is the idempotent corresponding to the extended vertex. These categories now typically vary, depending on the chamber. To each simple wall crossing
\upomega_{i}\colon\mathrm{C}\to\mathrm{D}
is an associated mutation functor
\Phi_{i}
, defined generally in
[
IW1
, §6]
, and in the setting here in
[
IW2
, §5.6]
.

(3)

For a flopping contraction
\mathcal{X}\to\operatorname{Spec}\mathcal{R}
, by
[
W
]
there is a bijection between chambers of
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{J}}
and the movable cone relative to

. Thus, to every chamber
\mathrm{C}
is an associated

, and we thus associate to
\mathrm{C}
the category

\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{C}}\coloneq\{a\in\mathop{\mathrm{D}^{\mathrm{b}}}(\operatorname{coh}\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{C}})\mid R(f_{\mathrm{C}})_{*}a=0\}.

To each simple wall crossing
s_{i}\colon\mathrm{C}\to\mathrm{D}
,
there is a corresponding curve

[
B1
,
C
]
.

Equivalently, but more algebraically, by
[
W
,
IW2
]
there is a bijection
\mathrm{C}\mapsto M_{\mathrm{C}}
between chambers in
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{J}}
and certain rigid objects in the category of Cohen–Macaulay modules
\operatorname{CM}\mathcal{R}

Necessarily
M_{\mathrm{C}}
has
\mathcal{R}
as a summand.
Thus to a chamber
\mathrm{C}
set
\Lambda_{\mathrm{C}}\coloneq\operatorname{End}_{\mathcal{R}}(M_{\mathrm{C}})
, and assign the subcategory

To each simple wall crossing

The noncommutative algebra

Definition 1.4.8
.

Given a chamber

(1)

If

(2)

1.4.3
The Classification Result

With the above in place, we now state the main classification result.

Theorem 1.4.11
.

Let
f\colon X\to\operatorname{Spec}R
be as in Setup
1.4.1
,

\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{J}}
the associated hyperplane arrangement, and
\mathrm{C}

(1)

An object

An object

\operatorname{Hom}(x,x[i])=0
for all

\mathrm{D}

The proof deploys the common strategy, using the following invariant

.

Definition 1.4.12
.

Consider the category
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{C}}
associated to the chamber
\mathrm{C}
, with standard heart
\operatorname{mod}\Lambda_{\mathrm{C},\operatorname{con}}
, and write
\mathrm{H}^{*}\colon\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{C}}\to\operatorname{mod}\Lambda_{\mathrm{C},\operatorname{con}}
for the cohomology functor associated with this t-structure. Then, for an object
x\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{C}}
and integers

(1)

x\in[a,b]
indicates that
\mathrm{H}^{i}(x)=0

.

(2)

x\in[\![a,b]\!]

x\in[a,b]
and
\mathrm{H}^{i}(x)\neq 0
for
i=a,b
.

If an object
x\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{C}}
satisfies

An object
x\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{C}}
satisfies

if and only if
x
belongs to a shift of the standard heart.
An important step for proving Theorem

is to guarantee that one can make

smaller by applying a composition of mutation functors.
To show this, one may want to test
\ell(\Phi_{i}x)
among all single mutations
\Phi_{i}
, however this naive strategy does not work well.

The correct strategy is to test
\ell(\Phi_{\upalpha}x)
among all
atoms

\upalpha
.
Atoms can be characterized using the standard hearts as follows,
and can be seen as a natural class of functors that contains the identity and all single mutation functors
\Phi_{i}
.

Lemma 1.4.13
.

Let
f\colon X\to\operatorname{Spec}R
be as in Setup
1.4.1
,
with corresponding hyperplane arrangement
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{J}}
.

(1)

A composition of mutation functors
\Phi_{\upalpha}\colon\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{C}}\to\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{D}}

\Phi_{\upalpha}(y)\in[0,1]
for all

(2)

For any two chambers
\mathrm{C}
and
\mathrm{D}
, there exists a unique atom

\Phi_{\upalpha}\colon\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{C}}\to\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{D}}
.
In particular, since there are finitely many chambers,
the set of all atoms is finite.

The set of atoms admits a natural preorder, which plays an important role.

Definition 1.4.14
.

Consider two atoms
\Phi_{\upalpha_{i}}\colon\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{C}_{i}}\to\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{D}_{i}}
for
i=1,2
.
Then
\Phi_{\upalpha_{1}}>\Phi_{\upalpha_{2}}
indicates that

(1)

\mathrm{C}_{1}=\mathrm{C}_{2}
, and

(2)

\Phi_{\upalpha_{1}}\circ\Phi_{\upalpha_{2}}^{-1}\colon\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{D}_{2}}\to\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{D}_{1}}
is an atom.

Remark 1.4.15
.

The notion of atoms and their preorder can be more clearly explained using the language of the Deligne groupoid; for details see e.g.
[
HW1
]
.

The following is the key proposition, which provides the induction step.

Proposition 1.4.16
.

Let
f\colon X\to\operatorname{Spec}R
be as in Setup
1.4.1
,
and
x\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{C}}

.
Suppose that

for

(1)

If

(2)

If

Remark 1.4.17
.

It is important to remark that

contains a non-identity element.
Indeed, if

for

,
there exists at least one

Since the identity

(

Note that Proposition

If this atom

\ell(x)\coloneq\upphi_{+}(x)-\upphi_{-}(x)

of phases by applying spherical twists.

From now on, consider the null subcategory
\mathcal{C}
of the minimal resolution
f\colon\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}/G
for some finite subgroup
G\leq\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})
.
Consider the standard heart
\operatorname{mod}\Lambda_{\operatorname{con}}\subset\mathcal{C}
,
and let
\sigma=(Z,\operatorname{mod}\Lambda_{\operatorname{con}})
be a generic Bridgeland stability condition on
\mathcal{C}
.
In this setup, if the spread
\ell(x)
of phases attains the minimal value, i.e. if
\ell(x)=0
, then
x
is a
\sigma
-stable object.
Such an object can be classified as in
1.4.18
.
Namely, if
x
is as above, then there are simple modules
s_{0},s_{1},\ldots,s_{t}
in
\operatorname{mod}\Lambda_{\operatorname{con}}
such that

\displaystyle x\simeq T_{s_{t}}\circ\ldots T_{s_{1}}(s_{0}).

(1.5.1)

Note that each
s_{i}
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{O}_{\mathtt{C}_{j_{i}}}(-1)
for some
irreducible component
\mathtt{C}_{j_{i}}
.

The step that makes
\ell(x)
smaller is the following.

Theorem 1.5.2
.

In the setup as above,
let
x\in\mathcal{C}
be a spherical object with
\ell(x)>0
,
with HN filtration given by

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0=x_{0}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces x_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\ldots}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces x_{n-1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces x_{n}=x,}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces z_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\ldots}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces z_{n}}

If
y_{1}
and
y_{n}
are
\sigma
-stable spherical objects such that

z_{i}\simeq y_{i}^{\oplus m_{i}}
, then

(1)

\ell(T_{y_{1}}(x))<\ell(x)
.

(2)

\ell(T_{y_{n}}^{-1}(x))<\ell(x)
.

For the null subcategory
\mathcal{C}
and any positive real number
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
,
the set

\{\ell(x^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}\mid\text{$x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}$ is spherical and $\ell(x^{\prime})\leq\varepsilon$}\}\subset\mathbb{R}

is a finite set. (Note that this fact does not hold for the category
\mathcal{D}
of complexes with compact support!) Therefore, applying the theorem repeatedly and then using (
1.5.1
) gives the main result.

Corollary 1.5.3

(
[
BDL
, 1.1]
)
.

For any spherical object
x\in\mathcal{C}
, there is

\Psi\in B^{\prime}=\langle T_{s}\mid\text{$s\in\operatorname{mod}\Lambda_{\operatorname{con}}$ is a $\sigma$-stable spherical object}\rangle\subset\operatorname{Auteq}\mathcal{C}

such that
\Psi(x)\simeq\mathcal{O}_{\mathtt{C}_{j}}(-1)

for some irreducible curve
\mathtt{C}_{j}
.

Note that (
1.5.1
) also implies that

B^{\prime}=\left\langle T_{s_{j}}\mid\text{$s_{j}\coloneq\mathcal{O}_{\mathtt{C}_{j}}(-1)$ for an irreducible curve $\mathtt{C}_{j}\subset\mathtt{C}_{\mathrm{red}}$}\right\rangle.

Remark 1.5.4
.

Note that
1.5.2
requires
x\in\mathcal{C}
to be spherical,
whilst
1.4.11
(
2
) holds for weaker objects
x\in\mathcal{C}
such that
\operatorname{Hom}(x,x[i])=0
for all
i<0
and
\operatorname{Hom}(x,x)=\mathbb{C}
(i.e.
x
is a brick). It is remarkable that the two results have different assumptions on

, but they have the same classification.

1.6.1
Silting Complexes and the Köning–Yang Bijection

As is clear from the definition in
1.2.2
, the notion of silting complexes is a generalization of tilting complexes, and hence of projective generators.
Note that, in contrast to tilting complexes, a silting complex is allowed to have negative self-extensions.

A key property of silting complexes is that they admit a natural preorder.

Definition 1.6.1
.

[
KY
]

Let
A
be a finite dimensional algebra over
\mathbb{C}
.

(1)

For silting complexes
P,Q\in\operatorname{K^{b}}(\operatorname{proj}A)
,
the notation
P\geq Q
indicates that
\operatorname{Hom}(P,Q[i])=0
for all
i>0
.

(2)

For smcs
x,y\in\mathop{\mathrm{D}^{\mathrm{b}}}(\operatorname{mod}A)
,
the notation
x\geq y
indicates that
\operatorname{Hom}(y,x[i])=0
for all
i<0
.

Write
\operatorname{\mathrm{silt}}A
for the set of silting complexes in
\operatorname{K^{b}}(\operatorname{proj}A)
, up to isomorphism, and
\operatorname{\mathrm{smc}}A
for the set of simple minded collections in
\mathop{\mathrm{D}^{\mathrm{b}}}(\operatorname{mod}A)
. The above relations
\geq
define preorders on both
\operatorname{\mathrm{silt}}A
and
\operatorname{\mathrm{smc}}A
.

Theorem 1.6.2

(The König-Yang Bijection
[
KY
]
)
.

Let
A
be a finite dimensional algebra over
\mathbb{C}
.
Then there are order-preserving bijections between the following sets.

(1)

\operatorname{\mathrm{silt}}A

(2)

\operatorname{\mathrm{smc}}A

(3)

The set of bounded
t
-structures on
\mathop{\mathrm{D}^{\mathrm{b}}}(\operatorname{mod}A)
with algebraic hearts.

Let
S=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
be an smc, and
P_{S}
and
\mathcal{A}_{S}
the corresponding silting complex and algebraic heart.
Then
\mathcal{A}_{S}\simeq\operatorname{mod}\operatorname{End}(P_{S})
and
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
gives the full collection of simple
\operatorname{End}(P_{S})
-modules.

The above theorem roughly says that silting complexes, smcs, and bounded algebraic hearts classify each other.
Note that the König-Yang bijection does not give any control for general presilting complexes, semibricks, and bounded
t
-structures.

Definition 1.6.3

(
[
AM

A finite dimensional

(2)

For any

Remark 1.6.8
.

The proof of step (a) is very similar to the proof of

Definition 1.6.9
.

Let

\mathcal{S}=\{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}\}
an smc in
\mathop{\mathrm{D}^{\mathrm{b}}}(\operatorname{mod}A)

For a chosen

(1)

.

(2)

, then let

be the minimal left approximation in the extension closure of

,
and put
x_{j}^{\prime}\coloneq\mathrm{Cone}(x_{j}[-1]\to x_{ij})

The collection
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
is again an smc
[
KY
, 7.6]
,
called the
(left) mutation
of
\mathcal{S}
at

,
and is denoted
\upnu_{i}\mathcal{S}

Notions of mutations for silting complexes and
t
-structures also exist
[
AI
]
, and all are compatible under the König-Yang bijection
1.6.2
.
In order to distinguish them, mutations of smcs are sometimes called simple-minded mutations.

The first step for proving

(
2

is to show that a given semibrick is contained in an algebraic heart.
For this, let us introduce the following notation, which is analogous to
1.4.12

Definition 1.6.10

For each smc
\mathcal{S}
in
\mathop{\mathrm{D}^{\mathrm{b}}}(\operatorname{mod}A)
, where

(1)

(1)

Corollary 1.6.12

The following quirk in the representation theory of
\Lambda_{\operatorname{con}}=\mathcal{J}\mathrm{ac}(Q,\mathbb{W}_{k})

Proposition 1.7.1
.

Consider
f_{k}\colon\mathcal{X}_{k}\to\operatorname{Spec}\mathcal{R}_{k}
, with associated
\Lambda_{\operatorname{con}}
.
If
M,N\in\operatorname{mod}\Lambda_{\operatorname{con}}
with
\operatorname{Hom}(M,N)=0
, then either
M
or
N
is filtered by a unique simple.

Given an object
x
with
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}(x,x[i])=0
for all
i<0

[\![a,b]\!]
with
a<b
, a spectral sequence shows that
\operatorname{Hom}(\mathrm{H}^{b}x,\mathrm{H}^{a}x)=0
. By
1.7.1
there are four options, and below each option we assign the following functor.

\begin{array}[]{rcccc}&\mathrm{H}^{a}x&\mathrm{H}^{a}x&\mathrm{H}^{b}x&\mathrm{H}^{b}x\\
\mbox{filtered by:}&\mathcal{S}_{1}&\mathcal{S}_{2}&\mathcal{S}_{1}&\mathcal{S}_{2}\\[5.69054pt]
\mbox{functor:}&\Phi_{1}&\Phi_{2}&\Phi_{1}^{-1}&\Phi_{2}^{-1}\\
\end{array}

The following asserts that the above assignment always improves the homological spread, and so gives a very direct induction step to the classification of objects
x
satisfying
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}(x,x[i])=0
for all
i<0
. This should be contrasted with §
1.4.3
where in general testing against compositions of positive mutation functors is required. Of course, the point is that whilst testing against atoms in general is required, in some situations (like here) more direct approaches can work.

Theorem 1.7.2
.

Suppose that
x\in\mathcal{C}_{k}
satisfies
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}(x,x[i])=0
for all
i<0
, and

with
\ell(x)=b-a>0
. Then the following statements hold.

(1)

Either
\mathrm{H}^{a}x
or
\mathrm{H}^{b}x
is filtered by a unique simple module
\mathcal{S}_{i}
, with
i\in\{1,2\}
.

(2)

When
\mathrm{H}^{a}(\Psi a)
is filtered only by
\mathcal{S}_{i}
, applying
\Phi_{i}
decreases the length
\ell
.

(3)

When
\mathrm{H}^{b}(\Psi a)
is filtered only by
\mathcal{S}_{i}
, applying
\Phi_{i}^{-1}
decreases the length
\ell
.

The above result provides part of the induction step which leads to the classification of various classes of objects, exactly as in
1.4.11
. There are various symplectic corollaries, including the following.

Corollary 1.7.3
.

Let
p=1
or
p>2
be prime. If
L\subset W_{p}
is a closed exact Lagrangian submanifold with vanishing Maslov class, then
\pm[L]\in\{(1,0),(0,1),(1,\pm 1)\}\subset\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}
.

1.8
‘Affine’ Geometric Setting (surfaces) à la Ishii–Uehara

Here, consider the minimal resolution
f\colon\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}/G
,
where
G\subset\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})
is a cyclic subgroup; in other words,
\mathbb{C}^{2}/G
is a Type
A
Kleinian singularity. As before, let
\mathtt{C}=f^{-1}(0)
be the scheme-theoretic fiber above the unique singular point, and now consider the ‘affine’ category

\mathcal{D}=\{a\in\mathop{\mathrm{D}^{\mathrm{b}}}(\operatorname{coh}\mathcal{Z})\mid\operatorname{Supp}a\subseteq\mathtt{C}\}.

Write
\mathcal{H}_{\operatorname{coh}}\colon\mathcal{D}\to\operatorname{coh}_{\mathtt{C}}(Z)
for the cohomology functor with respect to the heart
\operatorname{coh}_{\mathtt{C}}(Z)\subset\mathcal{D}
. The calligraphic font on
\mathcal{H}_{\operatorname{coh}}
reminds us that the output is now a sheaf, not a module.

This setting is significantly harder than the categories
\mathcal{C}
considered before. One of the main observations in
[
IU
]
is the following, which asserts that coherent cohomology of spherical objects is well behaved.

Proposition 1.8.1

(
[
IU
, 4.10, 6.1]
)
.

Let
x\in\mathcal{D}
be a spherical object.
Then the following statements hold.

(1)

\bigoplus_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{H}_{\operatorname{coh}}^{i}(x)
is a rigid
\mathcal{O}_{\mathtt{C}}
-module, pure of dimension one.

(2)

Furthermore, each indecomposable summand of
\mathcal{H}_{\operatorname{coh}}^{i}(x)

is a line bundle on a connected pure one-dimensional subscheme of
\mathtt{C}
.

Note that (1) applies to an arbitrarily finite subgroup
G\leq\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})
, however (2) heavily relies on the assumption that the singularity of
\mathbb{C}^{2}/G
is of Type
A
.

Let
\mathtt{C}_{1},\mathtt{C}_{2},\ldots,\mathtt{C}_{n}
be the irreducible components of
\mathtt{C}_{\mathrm{red}}
such that
\mathtt{C}_{\mathrm{red}}=\bigcup\mathtt{C}_{i}
,
and
\eta_{i}\in\mathtt{C}_{i}
the generic point.
For a spherical object
x\in\mathcal{D}
, the key invariant here is

\ell(x)\coloneq\sum_{i,p}\mathrm{length}_{\mathcal{O}_{\mathtt{C}_{i},\eta_{i}}}\mathcal{H}^{p}_{\operatorname{coh}}(x)_{\eta_{i}}.

Notice that if
\ell(x)=1
, then
x\simeq\mathcal{O}_{\mathtt{C}_{i}}(a)[k]
for some
i,a,k
.
The following is then the induction step, which implies the classification of spherical objects.

Theorem 1.8.2

(
[
IU
]
)
.

For any spherical object
x\in\mathcal{D}
with
\ell(x)>1
,
there exists an element

\Phi\in B\coloneq\langle T_{\mathcal{O}_{\mathtt{C}_{i}}(-1)},T_{\omega_{\mathtt{C}}}\mid\mathtt{C}_{i}\subset\mathtt{C}\rangle\subset\operatorname{Auteq}\mathcal{D}

such that
\ell(\Phi x)<\ell(x)
. Consequently, there is
\Psi\in B
such that
\Psi(x)\simeq\mathcal{O}_{\mathtt{C}_{i}}(a)[k]
for some
i,a,k
.

It seems difficult to generalize the method in
[
IU
]
to arbitrary Kleinian singularities, partly since
1.8.1
(
2
) so heavily relies on the Type
A
assumption (c.f.
[
K1
]
).
Furthermore, within
[
IU
]
is a key spectral sequence which heavily relies on two facts: the first is that the dimension is two, and the second that the objects
y
satisfy
\operatorname{Hom}(y,y[1])=0
. As such, the spectral sequence cannot be applied to classify more general objects
y
which only satisfy
\operatorname{Hom}(y,y[i])=0
for all
i<0
, nor can it be applied to the 3-fold flops setting.

At the time of writing, there are very few works that directly apply to the ‘affine’ category
\mathcal{D}
of compactly support complexes. In addition to
[
IU
]
are the papers
[
KS
,
S1
,
S2
]
, which are also surveyed below.

Note that the induction step in
[
IU
]
directly descends to the induction in the null subcategory
\mathcal{C}
. In particular, the proof of
1.8.2
contains the proof of the following, which was first explicitly stated in
[
IUU
]
.

Corollary 1.8.3

(
[
IUU
]
)
.

For any spherical object
x\in\mathcal{C}
,
there exists an element

\Phi\in B^{\prime}\coloneq\langle T_{\mathcal{O}_{\mathtt{C}_{i}}(-1)}\mid\mathtt{C}_{i}\subset\mathtt{C}\rangle\subset\operatorname{Auteq}\mathcal{C}

such that
\ell(\Phi x)<\ell(x)
. Consequently, there is
\Psi\in B^{\prime}
such that
\Psi(x)\simeq\mathcal{O}_{\mathtt{C}_{i}}(-1)
for some
i
.

As in
1.5.3
, this result
1.8.3
is now superseded by
1.4.11
, but again the proofs are very different.

Remark 1.8.4
.

Let
f\colon\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}/G
be the minimal resolution of a Type A Kleinian singularity.
As a consequence of the classification result
1.8.2
of spherical objects, it follows that

\langle T_{\mathcal{S}}\mid\text{$\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{D}$ is spherical}\rangle=B\coloneq\langle T_{\mathcal{O}_{\mathtt{C}_{i}}(-1)},T_{\omega_{\mathtt{C}}}\mid\mathtt{C}_{i}\subset\mathtt{C}\rangle\subset\operatorname{Auteq}\mathcal{D}.

As a corollary, it is shown in
[
IU
, 1.3]
(see also
[
IUU
, Appendix A]
) that

\operatorname{Auteq}\mathcal{D}=(\langle B,\operatorname{Pic}X\rangle\rtimes\operatorname{Aut}X)\times\mathbb{Z}.

Remark 1.8.5
.

The classification of spherical objects and the resulting description of
\operatorname{Auteq}\mathcal{D}
in
1.8.4
is related to the question of surjectivity of the homomorphism from the (affine) braid group to the subgroup of the autoequivalence group generated by spherical twists. The question of injectivity is also interesting, and difficult in general. For the minimal resolution of a Type A Kleinian singularity, injectivity has been proved in
[
IUU
, Cor. 37]
,
and hence the group
B
above is isomorphic to the affine braid group of Type A.
It is remarkable that the proof in
[
IUU
]
is achieved by reduction to characteristic two, a technique which is not possible in the
3
-fold setting, where the defining equations do not in general have integer coefficients. We remark that injectivity in the ‘finite’ settings of the null category
\mathcal{C}
is easier, and is contained within
[
BT
,
HW2
]
.

1.9
‘Affine’ Setting (3-folds) à la Keating–Smith and Shimpi

The setting of this section is
3
-fold flopping contractions
\mathcal{X}\to\operatorname{Spec}\mathcal{R}
for which the exceptional curve
\mathtt{C}
, given reduced scheme structure, is isomorphic to
\mathbb{P}^{1}
. These are known as single-curve flops, and they form a large, in fact uncountable, class of examples, even if we impose the additional assumption that
\mathcal{X}
is smooth. As in the previous section, consider the category of compactly supported objects

\mathcal{D}=\{a\in\mathop{\mathrm{D}^{\mathrm{b}}}(\operatorname{coh}\mathcal{X})\mid\operatorname{Supp}a\subseteq\mathtt{C}\}.

The simplest case is that of the Atiyah flop, where
\mathcal{R}=\mathbb{C}[\![u,v,x,y]\!]/(uv-xy)
, and
\mathcal{X}
is obtained by blowing up
\operatorname{Spec}\mathcal{R}
at either the ideal
(u,x)
or
(u,y)
.

1.9.1
Keating–Smith

The classification of spherical objects in
\mathcal{D}
for the Atiyah flop is due to Keating and Smith
[
KS
]
. Their paper is remarkable since it was the very first 3-fold ‘affine’ example, which overcame many of the issues with generalising
[
IU
]
as explained above, and furthermore their proof technique does not follow the common strategy of §
1.3
.

We very briefly overview the strategy in
[
KS
]
here. The main idea is to turn everything on its head, and to conclude properties of objects as a consequence of establishing recognition theorems for group elements. The common strategy in §
1.3
is, in some sense, the opposite direction to this.

For the Atiyah flop, the category
\mathcal{D}
has some standard spherical objects
\mathcal{S}_{i}=\mathcal{O}_{\mathtt{C}}(i)
. Now, given an arbitrary spherical object
S\in\mathcal{D}
, crucially there is an associated spherical twist
F\coloneq T_{S}
. Since this acts trivially on K-theory, the functor
F
belongs to the subgroup of autoequivalences that we already know
[
KS
, 4.11]
(namely, spherical twists in the
\mathcal{S}_{i}
, and even shifts) and so the trick is to use this information to deduce that
S
is a spherical object that we already know.

The proof goes via dynamics. Via the defining exact triangle of the spherical twist
F=T_{S}
, it follows that the Floer cohomology
\mathrm{HF}(\mathcal{S}_{i},F^{n}\mathcal{S}_{j};\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})
grows at most linearly with
n
. Then the main result
[
KS
, 6.18]
, which uses the Nielsen–Thurston theorem, asserts that up to shift
F=T_{S}
is a power of a spherical twist in an element of
\mathcal{S}
, which by definition is the set containing all images of all
\mathcal{S}_{i}
under the action of the group generated by the spherical twists in all the
\mathcal{S}_{j}
. In other words,
T_{S}
is, up to shift, a power of a spherical twist that we already know.

From there, the hard work has been done, and some standard reduction steps (involving e.g. tensoring by line bundles) reduces the problem to when
S
belongs to the null category
\mathcal{C}
. But that situation is known by previous sections, and thus the classification of spherical objects in
\mathcal{D}
follows; the precise statement of the result can be found as the
\ell=1
case of
1.9.3
below.

Remark 1.9.1
.

From the viewpoint of more general objects, including those considered in §
1.9.2
directly below, the main limitation of the above technique is found in the very first step, where the arbitrary spherical object
S
gives a group element
T_{S}
. Of course, objects do not generate autoequivalences (and thus group elements) in general. Regardless, it is intriguing whether or not there are replacements to the recognition theorem
[
KS
, 6.18]
in the more general settings below, and this question seems to be intertwined with various open questions on big mapping class groups.

1.9.2
Shimpi

Returning to the general setup of a single-curve flop
\mathcal{X}\to\operatorname{Spec}\mathcal{R}
, where
\mathcal{X}
may have Gorenstein terminal singularities, recently Shimpi
[
S1
,
S2
]
has classified spherical (and brick) complexes within the category
\mathcal{D}
of compactly supported objects on
\mathcal{X}
.

To set notation and language, to such a flopping contraction is associated Kollár’s length invariant
\ell

[
K3
]
, which is an integer satisfying
1\leq\ell\leq 6
. Roughly speaking this measures the complexity; the Atiyah flop has
\ell=1
. Given this number, there are natural sheaves

\mathcal{O}_{\mathtt{C}},\mathcal{O}_{2\mathtt{C}},\ldots,\mathcal{O}_{\ell\mathtt{C}},

which encode the successive thickenings of the curve
\mathtt{C}\cong\mathbb{P}^{1}
within
\mathcal{X}
. When
\ell\geq 5
, by
[
DW5
]
there is an additional sheaf, given as the unique non-split extension

0\to\mathcal{O}_{3\mathtt{C}}\to\mathcal{Z}\to\mathcal{O}_{2\mathtt{C}}\to 0.

Remark 1.9.2
.

Here it becomes important to be able to classify more general objects. Each of the
\mathcal{O}_{i\mathtt{C}}
turns out to induce an autoequivalence, after noncommutative deformation
[
DW5
]
. But, as explained in
[
DW5
, 7.5]
, very few are spherical. Indeed, when e.g.
\ell=4
only
\mathcal{O}_{4\mathtt{C}}
is a spherical object.

With the language set, the following is the main result of
[
S2
]
, which recovers the classification result (albeit not the results used in the proof) in §
1.9.1
as the case
\ell=1
.

Theorem 1.9.3

(
[
S2
]
)
.

If
x\in\mathcal{D}
is a brick, then up shifts, and iterated applications of mutation functors (which includes line bundle twists) and their inverses,
x
is either

(1)

one of the sheaves
\mathcal{O}_{\mathtt{C}},\mathcal{O}_{2\mathtt{C}},\ldots,\mathcal{O}_{\ell\mathtt{C}}
, or possibly
\mathcal{Z}
when
\ell\geq 5
, or

(2)

the Grothendieck dual of one of the sheaves in (
1
), or

(3)

a skyscraper sheaf at a closed point on
\mathcal{X}
, or on the flop
\mathcal{X}^{+}
.

A classification of all objects which satisfy
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{D}}(x,x[i])=0
for all
i<0
also exists
[
S2
, Thm A]
, but we refrain from stating that here.

We briefly sketch the strategy behind
1.9.3
, which is broadly similar to that of the category
\mathcal{C}
outlined in §
1.4.3
, but with significantly greater challenges. The induction is again on the homological spread with respect to the standard heart induced by the noncommutative resolution. The key is that now, on this larger category
\mathcal{D}
, the induction step becomes much more complicated, in part because the object
x
must be attacked using both positive atoms ‘from below’ and negative atoms ‘from above’, but also simply because the category
\mathcal{D}
contains more things, so fundamentally there are more options for what can happen.

The very rough overview is that the induction step works since Shimpi understands all intermediate t-structures, given that was the main result of his previous paper
[
S1
]
. In fact, the paper
[
S1
]
works for all
3
-fold flops, and the point is that passing from
\mathcal{C}
to
\mathcal{D}
means replacing the
\tau
-tilting finite and silting-discrete technology of the previous sections (which assert that there are finitely many intermediate t-structures in
\mathcal{C}
, all of which are algebraic) by the description of intermediate t-structures in
\mathcal{D}
in
[
S1
]
. However, this replacement comes at a cost, since
\mathcal{D}
contains many intermediate hearts that are not algebraic, and so care has to be taken if we land in one of these hearts, since in particular it is not so clear how the induction proceeds. On the positive side, from
[
S1
]
it turns out that the non-algebraic hearts only come in two basic flavours: straight-up coherent sheaves, and perverse sheaves on partial contractions (known as
mixed hearts
). In the case of single-curve flops, the mixed hearts do not exist, making the analysis easier, and thus making it possible to establish the induction step. Thus,
1.9.3
so far works only in the single curve setting.

Remark 1.9.4
.

Given that
[
S1
]
works in full generality, it seems promising to hope for a more general version of
1.9.3
which classifies all brick objects for all multi-curve
3
-fold flops, using the same broad strategy. That said, some of the mixed hearts remain stubbornly problematic, and their existence currently interferes with some of the more subtle support arguments made in
[
S2
]
.
