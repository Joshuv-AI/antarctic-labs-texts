Parental neglect and short video addiction among college students: a moderated mediation model
Authors: Jiansheng Li, Haiqing Wang, Li Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWith the popularity of short video platforms, Short Video Addiction has become a growing mental health concern. From a cognitive-emotional perspective, this study examined the association between Parental Neglect and Short Video Addiction among college students, focusing on the mediating role of Thought Suppression and the moderating effect of Thought Control Ability. A cross-sectional survey was conducted among 1,337 college students using the Parental Neglect Scale, Short Video Addiction Scale, White Bear Suppression Inventory, and Thought Control Ability Questionnaire. The results showed that Parental Neglect, Thought Suppression, and Short Video Addiction were all significantly and positively correlated with one another, while Thought Control Ability was significantly and negatively correlated with these three variables. Thought Suppression partially mediated the relationship between Parental Neglect and Short Video Addiction, and Thought Control Ability moderated the path from Thought Suppression to Short Video Addiction: for individuals with lower Thought Control Ability, Thought Suppression was a stronger positive predictor of Short Video Addiction; whereas for those with higher Thought Control Ability, this positive association was significantly attenuated. These findings suggest that enhancing individual Thought Control Ability may be associated with reduced negative influence of Thought Suppression on Short Video Addiction. This provides a theoretical and practical basis for cognitive-level interventions to help prevent excessive use of short video platforms among young adults.Keywords: Parental neglect, Short video addiction, Thought suppression, Thought control abilitySubject terms: Health care, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

The rapid advancement of digital technology has profoundly reshaped modern lifestyles, giving rise to short video social platforms such as Douyin (TikTok) and Kuaishou. Short videos, characterized by their conciseness, accessibility, and highly entertaining nature, have quickly attracted a massive user base1. According to the latest figures from the China Internet Network Information Center (2025), by June 2025, the number of short video users in China reached 1.068 billion, accounting for 95.1% of all internet users, highlighting the widespread popularity of this format. However, this convenience also entails potential risks: the algorithmic recommendation systems and instant feedback mechanisms of short video platforms easily trap users in continuous watching patterns, leading to attention deficits and loss of behavioral control, and subsequently resulting in the emergence of Short Video Addiction2. Research has shown that short video addiction not only impairs individuals’ learning motivation and perceived well-being3, but also may result in reduced sleep quality4, emotional regulation failures5, and exacerbated depressive symptoms6, posing severe threats to adolescent mental health. Therefore, it is of urgent theoretical and practical significance to explore the psychological mechanisms underlying short video addiction to formulate effective intervention strategies and safeguard individuals’ physical and mental health.

Among the many factors influencing short video addiction, the family environment acts as a microsystem for individual development and plays a critical role. According to Ecological Systems Theory7, the family is the most direct and influential environment, and dysfunction within it significantly increases the risk of problem behaviors. Parental neglect, as a common family risk factor, refers to parents’ failure to meet their children’s basic needs (such as emotional, physical, and educational needs), resulting in harm to their physical and mental well-being8. Unlike active abuse, parental neglect is often manifested as lack of parental care, such as lack of emotional response or insufficient supervision9. The Compensatory Internet Use Theory10 proposes that individuals deprived of real-life emotional resources rely on online activities to satisfy unmet needs; parental neglect may drive individuals to seek emotional compensation on short video platforms to fill an emotional void in reality11. Empirical studies have demonstrated a close relationship between parental neglect and internet addiction12,13. According to Attachment Theory14, parental neglect may weaken the parent-child bond, prompting individuals to seek emotional support in the virtual world15. In addition, General Strain Theory16 highlights that negative life events (such as parental neglect) elicit negative emotions, thus motivating individuals to adopt avoidance strategies, such as excessive internet use, to alleviate discomfort17.

Importantly, these theoretical perspectives converge on a developmental insight: the impact of parental neglect may extend far beyond its immediate occurrence, shaping individuals’ psychological functioning long into adulthood. Developmentally, early adverse experiences are known to become internalized, shaping cognitive and emotional patterns that persist over time18,19. Once formed during critical developmental periods, these patterns, including maladaptive strategies such as thought suppression, may operate automatically and outside conscious awareness in adulthood, influencing how individuals cope with stress and regulate emotions20. With this in mind, the present study focuses on individuals’ recollections of parental neglect experienced during childhood and adolescence (before age 16) rather than their current perceptions of parental neglect. This approach allows us to capture not merely the contemporaneous effects of family dysfunction, but rather its enduring developmental legacy: how early experiences of neglect may shape cognitive-emotional regulation strategies that, in turn, increase vulnerability to behavioral addictions such as short video overuse in adulthood.

Nevertheless, existing research predominantly explains this relationship from a behavioral perspective (e.g., lack of supervision or avoidance motivation), with insufficient attention to internal cognitive mechanisms, especially regarding emotional regulation strategies. Addressing this gap is essential, as cognitive mechanisms may serve as more proximal predictors of addictive behaviors and represent key targets for intervention. Therefore, investigating thought suppression as a potential mediator in the relationship between parental neglect and short video addiction contributes to the literature in several important ways. First, it extends existing behavioral explanations by elucidating the internal psychological processes through which early adverse experiences may lead to addictive behaviors. Second, it provides a more comprehensive understanding of how maladaptive cognitive-emotional regulation strategies, triggered by parental neglect, can perpetuate the cycle of addiction. Third, and most importantly, this cognitive perspective informs the development of targeted interventions that address the underlying cognitive mechanisms rather than merely the behavioral manifestations of addiction, thereby offering more effective strategies for prevention and treatment.

Thought suppression, as a core cognitive-emotional regulation strategy, may play a mediating role in this process. When individuals experience negative events such as parental neglect, they often actively attempt to suppress painful memories and emotions to alleviate psychological distress19. Thought suppression is defined as the conscious effort to eliminate unwanted thoughts from awareness21, such as trying to forget instances of parental criticism. However, the Ironic Process Theory indicates that suppression attempts may have paradoxical effects: unconscious monitoring processes may render suppressed thoughts more easily activated, resulting in a “rebound effect”22, where the suppressed thoughts intrude even more frequently, intensifying emotional distress. Studies have shown that thought suppression is associated with various addictive behaviors (such as Internet Gaming Disorder and substance abuse)23,24, but its mediating role between parental neglect and short video addiction remains underexplored.

On one hand, early parental neglect impairs the acquisition of adaptive emotion regulation strategies, leading individuals to rely on avoidance-oriented approaches such as thought suppression to manage distress18,19. On the other hand, the “rebound effect” intensifies the very distress individuals seek to escape, creating a psychological discomfort that demands immediate relief. Short video platforms, with their algorithm-driven, instantly gratifying content, offer a readily accessible means of distraction and emotional regulation. Thus, thought suppression, adopted as a coping strategy in response to parental neglect, paradoxically fosters dependence on short videos, thereby contributing to addictive patterns20,23.

Furthermore, the effectiveness of thought suppression may be moderated by individual differences in cognitive resources. Self-control Depletion Theory25 underscores that psychological resources are limited, and prolonged suppression exhausts self-control capacity, increasing the risk of failure. Thought Control Ability, as a key executive function, reflects the individual’s capacity to inhibit unwanted thoughts26, and the efficiency of executive control mechanisms, particularly those involving the prefrontal cortex27. Individuals with high thought control ability tend to manage intrusive thoughts more effectively and are less reliant on avoidance strategies (such as short video use)28. In contrast, those with low thought control ability experience more pronounced rebound effects, as the monitoring process dominates when cognitive resources for the operating process are insufficient. Therefore, thought control ability may buffer the negative association of thought suppression on addictive behaviors: even when experiencing parental neglect and a tendency towards suppression, individuals with high thought control ability are at lower risk of addiction; conversely, those with low ability are more likely to fall into an addictive cycle25. Existing research has mainly focused on single variables, with insufficient integration of such moderating mechanisms and a lack of empirical testing based on moderated mediation models.

In sum, the cognitive pathways linking parental neglect to short video addiction remain unclear, and the mediating role of thought suppression is in need of further investigation; likewise, the moderating effect of thought control ability as a protective factor warrants exploration. Accordingly, this study aims to construct a moderated mediation model to examine whether parental neglect predicts short video addiction through thought suppression, and to test the moderating role of thought control ability. Therefore, the following hypotheses are proposed:

Hypothesis 1

Parental neglect positively predicts thought suppression, and thought suppression positively predicts short video addiction. Thought suppression mediates the relationship between parental neglect and short video addiction.

Hypothesis 2

Thought control ability moderates the relationship between thought suppression and short video addiction, such that higher levels of thought control ability weaken this positive association. Moreover, thought control ability also moderates the mediating role of thought suppression in the link between parental neglect and short video addiction, with higher levels of thought control ability attenuating this indirect effect.

Method

Participants

To ensure sufficient statistical power for model testing, a priori sample size analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1. Based on a linear multiple regression framework with four predictors (Parental Neglect, Thought Suppression, Thought Control Ability, and their interaction term), the parameters were set as follows: medium effect size (f = 0.15), α level = 0.01, and statistical power = 0.95. The analysis indicated that a minimum of 178 participants would be required for adequate power. Participants were recruited online from universities in multiple regions, and all participants were at least 18 years of age and voluntarily participated after reading the informed consent form. The survey was administered anonymously, and participants could withdraw at any time. A total of 1,337 valid questionnaires were collected, with an average participant age of 21.330 years (SD = 2.038). Of the sample, 660 were male (49.364%), and 677 were female (50.636%); 536 were only children (40.090%), and 801 were non-only children (59.910%); 651 were urban residents (48.691%), and 686 were rural residents (51.309%).

Measures

Child neglect scale

The Child Neglect Scale (CNS) developed by Yang29 was used to assess experiences of neglect before age 16. The scale contains 38 items across four dimensions: Safety Neglect, Communication Neglect, Physical Neglect, and Emotional Neglect. Items are rated on a 4-point scale (0 = “Never” to 3 = “Always”), with higher total scores indicating greater neglect severity. In the present study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.943, indicating excellent reliability.

Short video addiction scale among college students

The Short Video Addiction Scale among College Students (SVAS), developed by Qin et al.30, was used to assess the degree of short video addiction. The scale consists of 14 items, divided into four dimensions: Withdrawal (5 items), Escape (3 items), Loss of Control (4 items), and Inefficiency (2 items). Responses are rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = “Completely Disagree” to 5 = “Completely Agree”), with higher scores indicating a stronger tendency toward addiction. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.897, demonstrating good reliability.

White bear suppression inventory

The Chinese version of the White Bear Suppression Inventory (WBSI), originally developed by Wegner and Zanakos31 and revised by Zhou et al.32, was employed to measure thought suppression tendencies. This inventory comprises 15 items, rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = “Strongly Disagree” to 5 = “Strongly Agree”), with higher scores indicating a stronger tendency to suppress negative thoughts. The Cronbach’s α coefficient for this sample was 0.903, demonstrating good reliability.

Thought control ability questionnaire

The Thought Control Ability Questionnaire (TCAQ), developed by Luciano et al.26 and revised by Yuan33, was used to assess the ability to suppress unwanted memories. The questionnaire consists of 7 items in a single dimension, rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = “Completely Disagree” to 5 = “Completely Agree”). Higher total scores reflect stronger thought control ability. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.665, which is slightly low but acceptable according to psychometric standards.

Statistical analysis

Data analyses were conducted using SPSS 26.0. Independent samples t-tests were employed to examine differences in study variables by place of origin and family structure. Pearson’s correlation analysis was used to explore relationships among the main variables. The mediation and moderation effects of thought suppression and thought control ability in the relationship between parental neglect and short video addiction were examined using Hayes’ PROCESS macro-4.1.

To account for potential confounding effects, age, gender, place of origin (urban/rural), and only child status were included as covariates in all analyses. This decision was informed by previous research documenting associations between these demographic variables and the key constructs. Specifically, gender, age, place of origin, and only child status have been linked to differences in child neglect experiences34,35. Gender differences have been observed in emotion regulation strategies36. Moreover, the use of these strategies varies with age and differs by gender37.

Results

Common method bias test

In this study, data for Parental Neglect, Short Video Addiction, Thought Suppression, and Thought Control Ability were all collected via self-report scales, which may raise concerns regarding common method bias and its potential influence on the results. To assess this risk, Harman’s single-factor test was conducted. The results showed that the variance explained by the first factor was 31.933% under unrotated conditions, which is below the critical value of 40%. This indicates that there was no serious common method bias in the data.

Group difference analysis

Independent samples t-tests were conducted to examine differences in Parental Neglect, Thought Suppression, Thought Control Ability, and Short Video Addiction across demographic variables (see Table 1). The results indicated no significant differences by gender for any of the variables (ps > 0.05). Significant differences were found by place of origin (urban/rural) in Parental Neglect, Thought Suppression, and Short Video Addiction (t = 4.430, p < 0.001; t = 3.438, p < 0.001; t = 3.268, p < 0.001). Thought Control Ability did not differ significantly between urban and rural participants (t = 0.568, p > 0.05). Significant differences were found in Thought Control Ability and Short Video Addiction between only children and non-only children (t = −2.049, p < 0.05; t = 1.995, p < 0.05), while there were no significant differences in Parental Neglect or Thought Suppression (t = 1.534, p > 0.05; t = 1.881, p > 0.05).

Due to high correlations among some core variables, multicollinearity diagnostics were conducted. All Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) values for predictors (including interaction terms) in the regression model were below 3.550, far lower than the conventional threshold of 10, indicating no serious collinearity issues.

These correlation patterns also provided empirical support for our covariate selection. Consistent with previous research, age was significantly correlated with all key variables (ps < 0.001), and place of origin and only child status showed significant correlations with several core variables (ps < 0.05). Although gender was not significantly correlated with the core variables in our sample, it was retained as a covariate in subsequent analyses based on its theoretical relevance and documented associations in prior research. Including these covariates helps isolate the unique effects of parental neglect on short video addiction from potential demographic influences.

Discussion

This study explored the mechanisms through which Parental Neglect is associated with Short Video Addiction among college students, with a focus on the mediating role of Thought Suppression and the moderating role of Thought Control Ability. Analysis of questionnaire data from 1,337 college students supported all hypotheses: Parental Neglect not only directly and positively predicts Short Video Addiction, but also indirectly predicts addictive behaviors via the mediating effect of Thought Suppression. Furthermore, Thought Control Ability moderated the association between Thought Suppression and Short Video Addiction, such that the positive relationship between Thought Suppression and addiction was attenuated with higher Thought Control Ability.

Parental neglect and short video addiction

The results showed that Parental Neglect significantly and positively predicted Short Video Addiction among college students, consistent with previous studies12,13. According to the Compensatory Internet Use Theory10, individuals whose emotional needs are not fulfilled in real life turn to the online world for alternative gratification. Parental Neglect, as a typical emotional deprivation environmental factor, results in a lack of necessary emotional support, sense of security, and belonging during development15. This emotional void makes individuals more prone to compensate for their emptiness through instant-feedback, high-engagement platforms such as short videos11. Short video platforms provide immediate feedback, entertainment, and virtual social interaction, quickly satisfying psychological needs and forming a compensatory mechanism.

According to Attachment Theory, parental neglect hinders the formation of secure attachment, fostering insecure attachment patterns that predispose individuals to seek emotional compensation in virtual environments14,38. Ecological Systems Theory7 also supports this view, suggesting that dysfunction in the family, the most immediate microsystem can significantly impact behavioral adaptation. Our findings further confirm that Parental Neglect is a significant risk factor for Short Video Addiction. In addition, General Strain Theory16 offers further theoretical support, proposing that negative life events (such as Parental Neglect) induce negative emotions that individuals must find ways to cope with. Short Video Addiction may represent such a coping strategy, whereby individuals seek to escape real-life distress through immersion in short videos17.

The mediating role of thought suppression

Mediation analyses indicated that Thought Suppression plays a significant mediating role between Parental Neglect and Short Video Addiction. This supports our hypotheses and aligns with the ironic process theory31 and research on the rebound effect22. Parental Neglect is often accompanied by the accumulation of negative emotions and distressing memories18. To alleviate such discomfort, individuals may employ Thought Suppression strategies to control or exclude these unwanted thoughts. According to Wegner’s Ironic Process Theory, thought suppression involves both a conscious operating process and an unconscious monitoring process. This cognitive mechanism explains why attempts to suppress unwanted thoughts often make them more frequent and intense.

The operating process involves actively seeking distraction to shift attention away from distressing experiences, precisely what is measured by the White Bear Suppression Inventory. Items such as “I often try to put problems out of my mind” directly reflect conscious efforts to avoid recalling experiences of neglect, often via engaging in short videos or similar distractions. The monitoring process, meanwhile, is an unconscious, automatic vigilance that keeps the suppressed targets highly accessible, so despite conscious efforts to banish these thoughts, they intrude more frequently and with greater intensity. Statistically, the positive association between Parental Neglect and Thought Suppression confirms that the monitoring process maintains the suppressed material’s high accessibility. The rebound effect is manifested in the increased reliance on short videos for instant gratification and emotional regulation following intrusive thoughts, ultimately leading to addictive behavior. Our findings confirm that Parental Neglect triggers negative emotions and memories, leading individuals to adopt Thought Suppression strategies, but these strategies paradoxically make negative thoughts more accessible and emotionally intense, resulting in more frequent intrusive thinking20. The resulting psychological discomfort drives individuals to seek rapid and intense external regulation (such as short videos) to distract or satisfy themselves instantly, fostering addictive behavior23,24. This finding is consistent with research in both substance and behavioral addictions, suggesting that Thought Suppression may have transdiagnostic significance in addictive behaviors39.

These results extend our understanding of the cognitive-emotional mechanisms underlying the impact of Parental Neglect on addictive behaviors, highlighting the central role of cognitive-emotional regulation strategies. The findings corroborate General Strain Theory’s16 assertion that negative affect drives avoidant behavior and offer new perspectives for understanding the cognitive motivations behind Short Video Addiction.

The moderating role of thought control ability

The moderated mediation analyses revealed that Thought Control Ability significantly moderates the relationship between Thought Suppression and Short Video Addiction. Simple slope analyses showed that for individuals with higher Thought Control Ability, even high levels of Thought Suppression were associated with lower levels of Short Video Addiction. In contrast, for those with lower Thought Control Ability, the predictive effect of Thought Suppression on addiction was much stronger. This supports the Ironic Process Theory, which suggests that when cognitive resources are insufficient or when the operating process is impaired, the monitoring process renders suppressed thoughts more salient, intensifying the rebound effect31. Low Thought Control Ability (as measured by low TCAQ scores) indicates limited cognitive resources for the operating process, resulting in a dominant monitoring process and more pronounced rebound effects, accounting for the moderation effect found in this study. This result also aligns with executive function theory28 and models of self-regulation25, which propose that higher Thought Control Ability aids in successfully managing intrusive thoughts, reducing dependence on avoidance strategies such as excessive use of short videos.

Furthermore, our study found that Thought Control Ability buffers the indirect pathway from Parental Neglect to addiction via Thought Suppression. This suggests that enhancing individual cognitive control capacity may be an effective intervention for Short Video Addiction, especially for those with histories of Parental Neglect in childhood, as improvement in Thought Control Ability may lower addiction risk. Neurocognitive evidence indicates that thought suppression activates general executive control mechanisms in the lateral prefrontal cortex27, and individuals with high Thought Control Ability may more efficiently recruit these resources to inhibit intrusions of negative thoughts40. Thus, Thought Control Ability functions as a protective factor that attenuates the adverse effects of Thought Suppression and reduces the occurrence of addictive behaviors.

Implications

This study integrates family systems theory, cognitive-emotional regulation theory, and motivational models of addiction to better understand how parental neglect is associated with short video addiction among college students. By identifying the mediating role of thought suppression and the moderating role of thought control ability, our findings highlight cognitive pathways that may underlie this relationship. These observations suggest that addressing maladaptive cognitive-emotional regulation strategies could be a valuable focus for prevention and intervention efforts.

Given the cross-sectional nature of our data, any practical implications should be considered preliminary. With that caution in mind, several tentative suggestions emerge. First, the association between parental neglect and short video addiction suggests that family-based interventions, such as parenting education or communication training, might help reduce neglect and strengthen emotional support at home. Second, for individuals with a history of neglect, cognitive-behavioral therapy or mindfulness training may offer ways to reduce reliance on thought suppression and enhance cognitive control. Mindfulness, for instance, encourages present-moment awareness and may help weaken the rebound effect41. Third, college mental health education could incorporate discussions about healthy screen use and self-monitoring of short video habits. Schools and community organizations might also consider programs aimed at improving emotion regulation and cognitive control, given that these factors were associated with lower addiction levels in our sample.

These possibilities, however, are grounded in correlational evidence rather than proven causal relationships. Future longitudinal or experimental research is needed to determine whether enhancing thought control ability directly reduces addiction risk and whether such effects persist over time. Additionally, as our sample consisted exclusively of Chinese college students, the applicability of these implications to other populations remains an open question requiring further empirical testing.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design makes it difficult to draw causal conclusions. Future research could adopt longitudinal or experimental designs to further test the proposed model. Second, the retrospective measurement of parental neglect may introduce recall bias, as memories of childhood experiences can be shaped by current emotional states. Prospective designs or multi-informant approaches could help improve accuracy in future studies. Furthermore, although the Child Neglect Scale demonstrated good internal consistency reliability in our sample, it has not been formally psychometrically validated in general university student populations. Future research would benefit from formally validating the CNS in diverse university samples or employing alternative measures that have been validated specifically for this population, so as to further enhance the reliability of findings. Third, all participants were college students living away from home, which may influence both how they recall neglect and their current short video use. Whether these findings hold for young adults who live with their parents remains an open question. Fourth, the sample consisted exclusively of Chinese college students, so the findings may be shaped by group-specific or cultural factors, and their generalizability should be considered with caution. For example, family dynamics, parenting practices, and cultural values in China may shape how parental neglect is experienced and interpreted. Future research should take these factors into account and pursue cross-cultural comparisons. Fifth, the Thought Control Ability Questionnaire showed relatively low reliability (α = 0.665), suggesting that future research should refine this measure or employ multi-method assessments. Finally, other moderators, such as social support or personality traits like neuroticism and impulsivity also warrant further investigation.

Conclusion

This study revealed that Parental Neglect is associated with Short Video Addiction among college students through the mediating role of Thought Suppression, and that this process is moderated by individual Thought Control Ability. As an early risk factor, Parental Neglect indirectly increases the likelihood of addiction via enhanced Thought Suppression; however, high Thought Control Ability may attenuate the strength of this pathway. Cognitive control interventions that strengthen Thought Control Ability may effectively reduce addiction risk and promote psychological well-being and behavioral adjustment among college students.
