Cutaneous Manifestations in Psoriatic Arthritis: Phenotypes, Clinical Burden, and Therapeutic Implications
Authors: Konstantinos Tsafis, Vasileios Skepastianos, Elpida Skouvaklidou, Dimitrios Deligeorgakis, Maria Boutel, Aikaterini Kontonikola, Nikolaos Papazoglou, George E. Fragoulis, Nikolaos Kougkas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cutaneous disease is a central component of psoriatic arthritis (PsA), contributing substantially to patient disease burden and influencing therapeutic choices. Beyond plaque psoriasis, specific phenotypes, including nail, scalp, palmoplantar, and inverse involvement, are relatively common in PsA and are frequently associated with greater functional limitation, impaired quality of life, and discordant responses to systemic therapy. Accurate identification of these manifestations is critical for diagnosis, risk stratification, and treatment optimization. This review provides a clinically oriented overview of the dermatologic spectrum of PsA, outlining epidemiology, pathogenic mechanisms linking the skin–joint axis, and the impact of skin disease on outcomes. We summarize evidence for topical therapies, conventional systemic agents, biologics targeting tumour necrosis factor alpha (TNFα), interleukin-17 (IL-17), and interleukin-23 (IL-23), targeted synthetic disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drugs (DMARDs), and emerging treatments, with attention to phenotype-specific considerations and multidisciplinary management. The implications of persistent skin activity in complex-to-manage PsA are also discussed. A domain-based, integrated approach to skin and joint care is required to achieve effective control both of musculoskeletal and cutaneous symptoms. Systematic evaluation and treatment of skin involvement should be considered a core component of PsA management rather than an ancillary concern.

Key Summary Points

Introduction

Psoriatic arthritis (PsA) is a chronic, immune-mediated, inflammatory condition characterized by the hallmark of psoriasis and inflammatory arthritis, with the prominent characteristics of enthesitis and new bone formation, in the absence of autoantibodies, such as rheumatoid factor (RF) or anti-citrullinated protein antibodies (ACPA) [1]. The clinical spectrum of the disease, the extent and severity of the affected joint and skin involvement can vary widely, including peripheral arthritis, axial disease, enthesitis, and dactylitis, nail and scalp involvement [2]. Extra-musculoskeletal features such as inflammatory bowel disease or uveitis may also occur. Cutaneous involvement and articular symptoms greatly affect the patients’ quality of life, and subsequently impact the physician’s therapeutic approach. Specific psoriasis phenotypes, particularly nail, scalp, and inverse involvement, are associated with an increased risk of PsA development and greater disease severity, and may significantly influence therapeutic decision-making [2]. Moreover, treatment responses may be disproportionate between skin and joints in patients with PsA, so better understanding of the dermatologic manifestations of the psoriatic disease is essential for rheumatologists to optimize outcomes in both skin and joint domains.

This review focuses on the spectrum of cutaneous manifestations in PsA and current therapeutic approaches. Relevant literature was identified through a structured search conducted as part of a narrative review in PubMed and Embase for manuscripts published from January 1999 to January 2026. Article selection was guided by relevance to the scope of the review, with emphasis on studies addressing cutaneous phenotypes in psoriatic arthritis, their clinical implications, and therapeutic considerations. Search terms included combinations of the keywords “psoriatic arthritis”, “psoriasis”, “cutaneous”, “skin”, “dermatologic”, “phenotype”, “burden”, “treatment”, and “management”. Given the narrative nature of this review, no formal inclusion or exclusion criteria were applied; instead, studies were selected on the basis of relevance to the clinical scope, with priority given to clinical trials, observational studies, systematic reviews, and international recommendations. This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Epidemiology

Psoriatic arthritis (PsA) has an equal distribution between sexes, with an annual incidence of 6 per 100,000 population and a prevalence of approximately 100–120 per 100,000 in the general population [3]. The prevalence of PsA among individuals with psoriasis is estimated to be greater than 13% and almost as high as 30%, depending on differences in classification criteria, whether patients were diagnosed by a rheumatologist, and geographical factors [4, 5]. History of psoriasis precedes arthritis in 60–70% of the patients, while in about 15–20% of cases, arthritis is the first manifestation with skin lesions developing later in the course of the disease [6]. Recognizing this temporal relationship is important, as lack of psoriasis does not rule out PsA entirely and early detection of musculoskeletal involvement in patients with psoriasis allows timely intervention.

Pathophysiology

In PsA, excess skin inflammation is linked to both innate and adaptive immune system dysfunction. The immunopathology of psoriasis reflects a complex interplay of genetic susceptibility, environmental triggers, and immune dysregulation. Microtrauma or infection can trigger the release of pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), which, together with peptides, cytokines, and immune cell activation, promote keratinocyte proliferation and abnormal differentiation, leading to psoriatic plaque formation [7].

The initiating event likely involves secretion of antimicrobial peptides such as LL37, defensins, S100 proteins, and cathelicidins by keratinocytes following microinjury. These complexes activate plasmacytoid dendritic cells, leading to the production of type I interferons, predominantly interferon-α (IFNα), which in turn promote maturation and activation of myeloid dendritic cells [8]. Activated myeloid dendritic cells subsequently induce increased production of tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNFα), interleukin (IL)-23, and IL-12.

IL-23 promotes the differentiation of naive T cells into Th17/Tc17 cells and activates innate lymphoid cells type 3 (ILC3); these cell populations produce IL-17 [9]. Another important cell population in psoriasis is resident memory T cells (TRMs) which also produce IL-17. These aggregate at sites of healed psoriatic plaques and can be activated with external stimuli, such as scratches or the Koebner phenomenon [10].

IL-23 mediates these actions through receptor (IL23R) signaling which further conveys the message depending on Janus kinase (JAK)/tyrosine kinase (TYK) molecules [10]. IL-17, a major cytokine in the pathogenesis of psoriasis, promotes the hyperproliferation of keratinocytes and together with TNFα induces the transcription of genes of various inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, and TNFα [11, 12]. These interleukins recruit other inflammatory cells like neutrophils and gamma delta (γδ) T cells, exacerbating the inflammatory environment [10]. TNFα is produced by multiple cell lineages and acts on various cell types (T lymphocytes, Th1, Th17) mainly by upregulating adhesion molecules. Through these effects, TNFα enhances the production of downstream cytokines such as IL-17 and IL-22 and promotes recruitment and activation of inflammatory cells, thereby sustaining a self-perpetuating inflammatory loop, reinforcing the IL-23/IL-17 axis [13]. This axis has emerged as the central pathogenic pathway linking cutaneous and musculoskeletal disease in PsA.

Phenotypic heterogeneity in PsA likely reflects differences in the relative contribution of cytokine networks, tissue-resident cells, and genetic susceptibility. In addition to keratinocyte-driven inflammation, cytokines such as TNF, IL-17, IL-23, IL-22, and IL-36 activate intracellular pathways including mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) and nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) signaling, contributing to tissue-specific inflammatory responses across the skin, nails, entheses, and synovium [10–12]. In this context, stromal populations such as dermal fibroblasts and synovial/entheseal fibroblast-like cells further contribute to disease propagation through cytokine and chemokine production, matrix remodeling, and amplification of IL-17 and TNF-mediated signaling. These cytokine-driven signaling pathways represent a key mechanistic bridge linking epidermal inflammation with synovial and entheseal responses, thereby supporting the concept of a unified skin–joint axis in PsA [10]. Genetic factors may further influence these processes and contribute to variability in clinical presentation and disease expression [13].

Clinical Aspects of Psoriasis and Skin Phenotypes in PsA

Psoriasis presents with a broad clinical spectrum, and several cutaneous phenotypes hold diagnostic and prognostic relevance for PsA (Table 1). Recognizing these patterns allows rheumatologists to anticipate disease course, interpret symptom distribution, and tailor therapy accordingly. Specific skin localizations such as the nails, scalp, and intertriginous regions have been associated with an increased likelihood of musculoskeletal involvement and greater functional impact [14, 15]. Nail Psoriasis

Psoriatic nail abnormalities, such as subungual hyperkeratosis (scaling beneath the nail), onycholysis (separation of the nail from the nail bed), pitting (partial loss of cells from the surface of the nail plate), and the oil-drop sign (translucent discoloration in the nail bed), may occur in patients with any subtype of psoriasis. Fingernail changes occur in about 60% of patients with psoriasis, while toenail abnormalities occur in about 35% of cases. Nail involvement is particularly common in PsA and may be present in approximately 80% of patients [16]. Nail disease correlates with distal interphalangeal (DIP) arthritis and enthesitis and is commonly regarded as a clinical marker associated with underlying musculoskeletal disease [16]. The anatomic proximity of the nail matrix to the DIP enthesis provides a structural basis linking nail pathology with joint inflammation [17]. Its recognition should heighten suspicion of PsA in patients with psoriasis and inform therapeutic selection toward agents effective in both nail and joint domains.

Scalp Psoriasis

Scalp psoriasis is among the most prevalent localizations, affecting up to 80% of patients with psoriasis [18]. It is characterized by the clearly defined erythematosquamous lesions with silver-white scaling. Lesions frequently extend beyond the hairline to the face or retroauricular region. Scalp psoriasis is not typically associated with hair loss. Nonetheless, it is suggested that hair loss occurs frequently at psoriatic lesions on the scalp. Hair plucks from psoriatic lesions used in trichograms show evidence of telogen effluvium. Psoriasis-related scarring alopecia has been reported by a number of clinical cases and prolonged psoriatic plaques may result in alopecia scarring [19]. Scalp psoriasis is highly noticeable, can occasionally spread to the face, and is considered a major determinant of psychosocial burden.

Plaque Psoriasis

Plaque psoriasis is the most common form of psoriasis associated with PsA. The prevalence of PsA varies widely, ranging from 2% to almost 26% in patients with plaque psoriasis, depending on population characteristics, diagnostic criteria, and disease duration [20]. It is usually symmetrically distributed, presenting at various sites of the body, commonly affecting extensor surfaces of the extremities, with a predilection for periarticular areas around large and small joints, such as the elbows and knees. It can also affect the trunk, facial areas, ears, and genitalia. The classic appearance of psoriatic lesions consists of erythematous plaques with adherent white-silver scale which can be gently scraped to reveal a moist, inflamed surface [21].

The clinical assessment of psoriasis is based on the severity of the psoriatic rash, the presence of erythema, the degree of induration and desquamation, and the percentage of the impacted areas, calculated with body surface area (BSA). Severity is further quantified using indices such as the Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI), which integrates lesion severity (erythema, induration, and scaling). The symptoms caused by the plaques can vary with mild discomfort, pruritus, pain or even ulcerations, accompanied by bleeding spots, especially in hands and feet [22]. The skin in PsA can be more prone to inflammation due to chronic barrier disruption and mechanical stress, leading to the creation of ulcerated areas, which can be further complicated by infection.

Pustular Psoriasis

Pustular psoriasis is a variant of psoriasis characterized by sterile, non-infectious pustules on a background of erythematous and inflamed skin [23]. It is generally categorized into two principal forms: the localized form, most often referred to as palmoplantar pustulosis (PPP), and the generalized form (GPP), including von Zumbusch psoriasis and acrodermatitis continua of Hallopeau. The prevalence of PsA in patients with pustular psoriasis varies according to the subtype; one single-center retrospective study reported PsA in approximately 8% of individuals with GPP and up to 28% in those with PPP [23, 24]. Neutrophil accumulation in the epidermis is responsible for pustule formation and contributes to the clinical severity of these variants. Acute GPP may present with extensive pustulation, fever, chills, and signs of systemic toxicity, and can progress to secondary infection, sepsis, or multiorgan involvement [24]. This form requires urgent assessment and, in severe cases, hospitalization.

Palmoplantar Pustulosis

Palmoplantar pustulosis or palmoplantar psoriasis (PPP) refers to psoriatic involvement of the palms and soles and represents a localized pustular phenotype. This subtype occurs more frequently in women between 30 and 50 years of age and is strongly associated with smoking [25]. Common triggers include trauma, drugs, smoking, and irritant exposures. PPP exhibits a range of morphologic patterns, from thick, scaly, hyperkeratotic plaques to primarily pustular or mixed presentations. It accounts for approximately 3–4% of all psoriasis cases, yet its functional impact is disproportionately high, as pain and fissuring can severely impair daily activities despite limited BSA involvement. PPP may lead to marked impairment of quality of life, especially when painful pustules rupture or when onycholysis is present. Paradoxical induction or exacerbation of palmoplantar pustulosis has been reported in patients receiving biologic therapy, particularly TNFα inhibitors, but has also been reported with other biologic DMARDs (bDMARDs) used in psoriasis and PsA, such as IL-17 inhibitors [26, 27]. These reactions may occur despite improvement in joint manifestations of PsA and are thought to reflect an immune shift involving type I interferon pathways and altered neutrophil recruitment mechanisms [28]. Recognition of this phenomenon is important in PsA, as persistent palmoplantar involvement can significantly impair function despite adequate control of musculoskeletal disease and may require topical therapy, dose adjustment, or switching to a bDMARD from a different class.

Inverse Psoriasis

Inverse (flexural) psoriasis presents with erythematous, sometimes slightly erosive plaques and patches localized to intertriginous areas such as the axillae, inframammary folds, perianal region, umbilicus, retroauricular skin, and external genitalia [29]. High moisture and friction in these areas promote irritation and secondary infection. Inverse psoriasis is common in children, including newborns, in whom it may appear as “napkin psoriasis”, and is frequently observed in individuals with elevated body mass index (BMI). Sweating and physical activity often worsen symptoms, which can significantly affect quality of life, sexual function, and emotional well-being [30]. Patients with inverse psoriasis have greater susceptibility to fungal infections in affected areas. In addition, this phenotype has been linked to an increased risk of developing PsA [31]. In adults, sudden onset inverse psoriasis may be associated with underlying HIV infection [32]. Reported prevalence of inverse psoriasis varies widely, ranging from 3% to 36% [33].

Erythrodermic Psoriasis

Erythrodermic psoriasis is a rare, severe inflammatory variant defined by extensive erythema affecting most of the BSA, often estimated at 80–90%. It may be accompanied by systemic symptoms such as fever, chills, malaise, and increased risk of secondary infection due to compromise of the skin barrier [34]. Patients may exhibit dehydration, tachycardia, thermoregulatory dysfunction, generalized pruritus, and skin exfoliation [35]. This presentation can be life-threatening, with potential complications including hypotension and shock. Hospitalization is often required for stabilization, intravenous fluid administration, antibiotics in cases of suspected infection or sepsis, and systemic immunomodulatory therapy [36]. Triggers include infections, abrupt withdrawal of systemic immunosuppressants, particularly ciclosporin or biologic agents in patients who had previously responded well, and certain medications [35]. Although the clinical presentation of psoriatic erythroderma may resemble erythroderma of other etiologies, its management and prognosis are determined by the underlying psoriatic disease.

Guttate Psoriasis

Guttate psoriasis is a common variant of psoriasis presenting predominantly in children and young adults, characterized by numerous, small, round “drop-like” papules distributed mainly on the trunk and extremities [37]. This phenotype is often self-limited and may resolve spontaneously without specific intervention, though in a proportion of cases it may evolve into chronic plaque psoriasis or coexist with other psoriatic morphologies [35, 38]. Triggers include upper respiratory infections, particularly streptococcal pharyngitis, mechanical stress, and cutaneous trauma. Approximately 10% of patients with psoriasis report a past episode of guttate psoriasis, although fewer than 2% present with active lesions at the time of diagnosis [38, 39]. Unlike nail or scalp disease, its role as a risk predictor for PsA is neither well established nor proven in cohort studies.

Cutaneous Manifestations Other Than Psoriasis in Psoriatic Arthritis

In addition to classical psoriatic lesions, several non-psoriatic inflammatory skin diseases may occur in patients with PsA. These conditions, many of which fall within the spectrum of neutrophilic dermatoses, share overlapping pathogenic pathways with PsA, particularly dysregulation of the IL-17, TNFα, and innate immune axes, and may complicate clinical assessment and therapeutic decision-making.

Hidradenitis Suppurativa (HS)

Hidradenitis suppurativa is a chronic inflammatory disorder of the follicular epithelium, typically involving axillary, inguinal, and anogenital regions and characterized by painful nodules, abscesses, and sinus tracts. A recent meta-analysis indicates a higher prevalence of HS in patients with PsA (0.3%) compared with the general population (0.05–0.25%), and this coexistence appears to correlate with more severe HS disease [40]. Shared pathogenic mechanisms, including IL-17 and TNFα-driven inflammation (with recruitment of neutrophils and macrophages), indicate the biological overlap between HS and PsA.

This common immunologic background is further reflected in therapeutic overlap; agents such as TNFα and IL-17 (A and A/F) inhibitors are effective in both diseases [41].

Erythema Nodosum (EN)

Erythema nodosum is frequently diagnosed along with systemic inflammatory conditions, including rheumatic diseases and inflammatory bowel disease [42]. Its specific frequency in PsA remains uncertain and uncommon, but association is well recognized in the literature [42]. Clinically, EN is a form of panniculitis, characterized by tender, warm, erythematous nodules on the anterior lower legs, often accompanied by systemic symptoms. Lesions may resolve spontaneously or require treatment such as glucocorticoids, colchicine, methotrexate, cyclosporine, azathioprine, or TNFα inhibitors [43]. Histopathology typically reveals septal panniculitis with neutrophilic infiltrates and granulomatous features [42]. In PsA, EN, although uncommon, may signal heightened systemic inflammation, underlying infection, associated comorbidities, or other autoimmune conditions including inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) and sarcoidosis, further complicating diagnostic approach and therapeutic strategy.

Pyoderma Gangrenosum (PG)

Pyoderma gangrenosum is a neutrophilic dermatosis, rarely associated with PsA. In a case-report based systematic review, PsA accounted for 8.5% of PG cases, with higher rates in rheumatoid arthritis and IBD-associated arthritis [44]. PG is characterized by rapidly developing ulcers with necrolytic, undermined violaceous borders. Proposed mechanisms include heightened IL-1β, TNFα, IL-8, and IL-17 activity, consistent with an autoinflammatory phenotype [42–45]. First-line treatment typically includes systemic glucocorticoids or ciclosporin, while conventional synthetic DMARDs (csDMARDs), intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), and bDMARDs, particularly TNFα inhibitors and anakinra, are effective as steroid-sparing agents or in refractory cases [42–44].

Although manifestations such as HS, EN, and PG are relatively uncommon in PsA, their presence may complicate diagnosis and influence therapeutic options.

Treatment of Skin Manifestations in Patients with PsA

Management of psoriasis in patients with PsA requires coordinated therapeutic planning, as skin involvement not only contributes to disease burden but also affects drug selection and survival, and long-term outcomes. Psoriasis phenotypes frequently seen in PsA—nail, scalp, and inverse disease—often respond differently to treatment compared with classic plaque psoriasis. Moreover, the approach to treating skin manifestations in psoriatic disease may differ depending on whether care is primarily directed by a rheumatologist or a dermatologist, highlighting the importance of integrated, multidisciplinary management [46].

Topical Therapies

Topical therapies remain appropriate for patients with PsA who have mild, localized psoriasis or for those using them in combination with systemic agents for joint disease. Although topical therapies do not target musculoskeletal inflammation, they help control residual plaques and psoriasis in sensitive sites, which often remain symptomatic despite effective systemic therapy.

Topical corticosteroids, often combined with vitamin D analogues, remain the mainstay treatment for localized plaque psoriasis and provide rapid symptomatic improvement through anti-inflammatory and anti-proliferative effects [47, 48]. Vitamin D analogues, such as calcipotriol or calcitriol, promote keratinocyte differentiation and can be used safely for long-term maintenance [48]. Calcineurin inhibitors (CNis) such as tacrolimus and pimecrolimus, which inhibit T cell activation and suppress IL-2 and IFNγ production, are valuable in intertriginous and facial psoriasis [49]. Adverse events include local skin irritation, especially in highly inflamed lesions.

Keratolytic agents, including salicylic acid and tazarotene, are useful in scalp and palmoplantar psoriasis [50]. They promote desquamation of psoriatic plaques, reducing pruritus and irritation. Combining them with topical corticosteroids can help minimize local adverse effects such as burning and irritation [50].

Targeted phototherapy, particularly narrowband UV-B, can benefit localized thick plaques or scalp disease and may be used adjunctively in patients with PsA receiving systemic therapy. It delivers therapeutic wavelengths, while minimizing exposure to carcinogenic spectra. Phototherapy can lead to clinical improvement after approximately 2 months of treatment. Reported adverse effects include erythema, burning sensations, and blistering [51].

GRAPPA 2021 treatment recommendations support the adjunctive use of topical therapy for cutaneous manifestations in PsA [52].

Treatments for Moderate to Severe Psoriasis

There is no consensus on the definition of moderate psoriasis. Some clinicians define moderate psoriasis affecting from 3–5% to 10% of the BSA. Severe psoriasis is typically characterized by a BSA coverage of 10% or more. Systemic treatments are the primary approach for patients with moderate to severe psoriasis. Both the US and European recommendations recommend biologics, oral agents, and phototherapy, alone or in combination, for these patients [53, 54].

Phototherapy for More Extensive Skin Disease

More extensive phototherapeutic modalities remain effective options for moderate psoriasis and may be used in patients with PsA with substantial skin involvement. Narrowband UV-B is preferred for its efficacy, longer duration of remission, and reduced carcinogenic risk compared with broadband UV-B [51]. Psoralen plus ultraviolet A (PUVA) retains utility for severe or refractory palmoplantar psoriasis—an impactful phenotype in PsA—but long-term use is limited by the increased risk of cutaneous malignancy [55].

Oral Systemic Therapies and csDMARDs, Including Apremilast

csDMARDs continue to play a role in PsA regarding skin disease, particularly when cost, accessibility, or comorbidities limit biologic use [52–54, 56]. Methotrexate is the most commonly used csDMARD in PsA and improves both plaque psoriasis and nail disease, while also addressing peripheral arthritis [57]. Cyclosporine may be used in severe cutaneous flares, such as erythrodermic psoriasis, but is limited by safety concerns; it has no disease-modifying effect on axial disease and is not generally recommended for peripheral joint disease control [52]. Apremilast, a targeted synthetic DMARD, offers benefit for both psoriasis and peripheral PsA with a favorable safety profile, though its efficacy on skin disease is moderate [49]. Methotrexate and apremilast are both considered viable options for patients presenting with concomitant joint and skin disease [53, 54]. Acitretin, useful for hyperkeratotic or palmoplantar disease, may be combined with phototherapy but does not affect musculoskeletal manifestations.

Biologic Therapies for PsA with Skin Involvement

Biologic therapies are central to managing PsA, when clinically significant skin involvement is present, as they target immunologic pathways shared by psoriasis and PsA and provide coordinated control of both manifestations. Selecting an appropriate agent depends not only on musculoskeletal manifestations but also on cutaneous phenotype, comorbidities, and prior treatment history.

In clinical practice, treatment selection for patients with PsA should reflect the active disease domains and phenotype. In patients with extensive psoriasis or difficult-to-treat cutaneous involvement—particularly scalp, nail, palmoplantar, or inverse disease—agents with stronger cutaneous efficacy may be preferred, whereas therapies with established efficacy in extra-musculoskeletal manifestations such as uveitis or inflammatory bowel disease may be preferentially selected in the presence of such involvement (Table 2). TNFα Inhibitors

TNFα inhibitors (monoclonal antibodies and the fusion protein etanercept) are the longest lived among the approved biologic agents for psoriasis and PsA and can provide substantial improvement in plaque, scalp, and nail psoriasis, all of which are highly relevant phenotypes in PsA [58]. They also offer advantages in patients with concomitant inflammatory bowel disease or uveitis and have robust long-term safety data [59–61]. However, TNFα inhibitors have a well-described class-effect risk of paradoxical psoriasiform reactions, most frequently manifesting as palmoplantar pustulosis, which may appear despite adequate control of musculoskeletal disease [62]. Recognition of this pattern is important, as persistent palmoplantar disease can be disabling and frequently requires switching to another biologic class. Infections, such as tuberculosis, hepatitis B and C reactivation, and other adverse events, such as drug-induced lupus and demyelinating central nervous system disorders, have also been reported with the use of TNF inhibitors in psoriasis and PsA [63].

IL-12/23 Inhibitors

Ustekinumab, targeting the shared p40 subunit of IL-12 and IL-23, improves plaque psoriasis and has established efficacy for peripheral PsA, and concurrent IBD [64]. Although skin responses are generally favorable, they tend to be slower and sometimes less complete than with newer IL-17 or IL-23 inhibitors [65]. Its safety profile remains excellent, with low rates of serious infections or malignancy [66].

IL-23 Inhibitors (p19 Blockers)

Selective IL-23 inhibitors, including guselkumab and risankizumab, are proven, effective therapies for cutaneous disease in PsA, providing durable and often complete skin clearance in patients with common PsA phenotypes [56, 67]. These agents are associated with a favorable safety profile, low immunogenicity, and sustained skin responses, supporting long-term disease control. While their musculoskeletal efficacy is somewhat less rapid than IL-17 inhibitors, IL-23 blockers show sustained benefit in peripheral arthritis and enthesitis [68]. In parallel, icotrokinra, a novel oral IL-23 receptor inhibitor approved for moderate-to-severe plaque psoriasis, is currently under clinical investigation for psoriatic arthritis, further expanding the therapeutic landscape of IL-23-targeted interventions, aiming to determine whether oral IL-23 inhibition can achieve comparable durability and musculoskeletal efficacy to injectable biologics in PsA [69]. Emerging observational and mechanistic data suggest that IL-23 blockade may influence immune pathways relevant to the evolution of psoriatic disease, raising interest in its potential role in disease interception through modification of the skin–joint axis and prevention of PsA; however, current evidence remains largely indirect and hypothesis-generating, and this concept is not yet established in clinical practice and remains under investigation [70, 71].

IL-17 Inhibitors

IL-17 inhibitors, including secukinumab and ixekizumab (IL-17A inhibitors), brodalumab (IL-17A receptor inhibitor), and bimekizumab (dual IL-17A/F blockade), are among the most potent skin-directed therapies available and provide rapid and substantial clearing of psoriasis [56]. IL-17 inhibitors also demonstrate strong efficacy across PsA domains, including enthesitis, dactylitis, and axial involvement [72–75]. Bimekizumab, through dual inhibition of IL-17A and IL-17F, has demonstrated high rates of skin clearance and resolution of arthritis in PsA, with a safety profile consistent with IL-17 pathway blockade, while emerging pooled data report that the incidence of uveitis and IBD remains low in patients with PsA treated with bimekizumab and is under further evaluation [76, 77]. IL-17 inhibitors are typically avoided in patients with active IBD because of the risk of disease exacerbation or emergence of drug-induced colitis [78, 79]. Secukinumab and bimekizumab have also demonstrated clinical efficacy in HS, supporting their role as therapeutic options in moderate-to-severe HS [80].

Targeted Synthetic Therapies (JAK Inhibitors)

JAK inhibitors, including tofacitinib and upadacitinib, show efficacy in peripheral and axial arthritis and provide improvement in cutaneous disease. However, they are not approved for the treatment of psoriasis and are primarily used for their musculoskeletal efficacy in PsA [56]. Their oral administration, rapid onset of action, and efficacy in arthritis and inflammatory bowel disease distinguish them as useful options [81]. Safety considerations include an increased risk of infections (including herpes zoster), malignancy, and cardiovascular and thromboembolic events, warranting careful patient selection and monitoring during therapy [82]. Deucravacitinib, a selective TYK2 inhibitor, has demonstrated high efficacy in plaque psoriasis, and is approved for PsO; it may become relevant for PsA management as indications expand [83].

Skin-Oriented Targeted Therapies in Psoriatic Arthritis (Interleukin-36, RORγt, and ROCK2 Inhibitors)

IL-36, a member of the IL-1 cytokine family, signals through binding to the IL-36 receptor, leading to activation of NF-κB and MAPK pathways via the MyD88/IRAK signaling complex [84]. Dysregulated IL-36 signaling is strongly implicated in pustular forms of psoriasis, particularly GPP, which is associated with loss-of-function mutations in the IL-36 receptor antagonist (IL-36Ra), a critical negative regulator of this pathway [85]. Spesolimab, a monoclonal antibody directed against the IL-36 receptor, demonstrated a clinical breakthrough with rapid disease control in early-phase studies and has since shown efficacy in phase II and III trials for GPP [86]. While its role in psoriatic arthritis is not established, IL-36 inhibition may be clinically relevant in patients with PsA who develop pustular flares or paradoxical pustulosis during biologic therapy.

Retinoic acid receptor-related orphan receptor-γt (RORγt) inhibitors target a transcription factor critical for Th17 differentiation and IL-17 production, offering a mechanistically novel approach to Th17-driven skin inflammation. Oral agents such as VTP-43742 have shown clinical efficacy against plaque psoriasis in phase II studies with associated reductions in IL-17A and IL-17F, consistent with Th17 axis modulation [87]. Further evaluation in larger and advanced-phase studies is needed to clarify their efficacy and safety profiles, and to determine any role in psoriatic arthritis.

Rho-associated protein kinase 2 (ROCK2) inhibitors have emerged as potential oral therapies for psoriasis by selectively modulating Th17-driven immune responses. ROCK2 regulates transcriptional programs involved in IL-17 and IL-23 expression, and its inhibition attenuates pro-inflammatory cytokine production while preserving other immune pathways. A proof-of-concept trial supports mechanistic relevance in skin disease; however, ROCK2 inhibitors are not currently approved for psoriasis or PsA, and no data on musculoskeletal efficacy are currently available, necessitating further investigation [88].

Emerging Targeted Therapies

Beyond current approved treatments, the therapeutic landscape is shifting toward smaller molecular formats and multispecific targets, with potential benefits against both skin and joint disease. Sonelokimab, a trivalent IL-17A/IL-17F-inhibiting nanobody designed for enhanced tissue penetration and simultaneous blockade of key IL-17 cytokines, demonstrated significant improvements in joint endpoints and skin clearance in a randomized phase 2 trial of active PsA [89]. Another novel IL-17 pathway agent, izokibep, showed promising efficacy and safety in a phase 2 study of active PsA, further supporting the relevance of alternative IL-17-targeted approaches [90]. Furthermore, combination biologic or targeted approaches may provide enhanced control in highly refractory disease, particularly when a single agent fails to adequately control both musculoskeletal and extra-musculoskeletal manifestations. Early evidence suggests potential benefit in selected patients, although data remain limited and are largely derived from observational studies and case-based reports [91]. These approaches remain investigational and are not currently established in routine clinical practice, but may address an unmet need in selected refractory cases.

Health-Related Quality of Life and Complex-to-Manage Psoriasis in Patients with PsA

Psoriasis remains challenging in PsA management. In a multinational cohort of 1286 patients, 80% reported skin or nail symptoms over the past 12 months, and a substantial proportion of patients receiving treatment for PsA still had active skin involvement. Furthermore, the majority of patients with PsA reported skin-related discomfort (55%) [92]. Skin manifestations have a deleterious effect on health-related quality of life (HRQoL). In a retrospective analysis of a multinational PsA program, physician global assessment scores did not significantly differ between patients with and without active skin involvement; however, patient-reported outcomes were worse among those with active skin disease [93]. Additionally, post hoc analyses of phase 3 ixekizumab trials (SPIRIT-P1 and SPIRIT-P2) further supported that improvements in HRQoL depended on amelioration of both skin and joint symptoms [94].

Complex-to-manage (C2M) PsA, as defined by the recent GRAPPA consensus, refers to persistent symptoms and/or inflammation despite at least one trial of a biologic or targeted synthetic DMARD, with consideration of comorbidities, treatment-related challenges, and patient-reported burden, extending beyond classical treatment resistance [95, 96]. Skin disease is a key contributor to the C2M phenotype, as persistent or extensive psoriasis may exacerbate patient-reported symptoms, impair quality of life, and increase overall disease burden. Discordant responses between cutaneous and musculoskeletal domains may drive treatment escalation or switching, complicating management, even when musculoskeletal symptoms are adequately controlled. Data from a Greek PsA registry identified associations between C2M features and female sex, smoking, higher BMI, unemployment, axial involvement, nail disease, and concomitant inflammatory bowel disease [97]. Extensive psoriasis (BSA > 3%) in the Greek cohort, as well as higher BSA in an Italian cohort, were associated with a C2M disease profile [97, 98]. In a French retrospective cohort, patients meeting C2M were more likely to discontinue a prescribed bDMARD as a result of inadequate skin disease control compared with non-C2M patients, prompting therapeutic adjustments [99].

Conclusion

Psoriatic arthritis is a multifaceted disease in which skin manifestations are central to patient burden and management. The type, distribution, and severity of psoriasis influence quality of life, treatment selection, and overall disease outcomes. Advances in targeted therapies have enhanced control of both joint and skin disease; however, nail, scalp, and intertriginous involvement remains challenging, while persistent skin activity contributes to C2M-PsA. Optimal management requires integrated multidisciplinary approach involving rheumatology and dermatology expertise, and systematic assessment and management of comorbidities. Future progress will depend on earlier recognition, novel therapies, and prioritization of patient-reported outcomes to achieve comprehensive disease control.
