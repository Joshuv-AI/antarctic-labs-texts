Childhood vaccination coverage and determinants in Jordan: a population-based study
Authors: Basant Motawi, Eric Ribaira, Samer S. El-Kamary
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGlobally, concerns about vaccine efficacy, safety, and long-term health impacts continue to increase among parents, resulting in a decline in the percentage of children receiving full vaccination. Within Jordan, several parental-related factors have previously been implicated in vaccine uptake. The current study aimed to investigate whether vaccination rates are changing in Jordan and to explore further the factors that influence parental vaccine decisions.MethodsThe current study analyzed data from the survey responses of mothers of 1,426 mother-child pairs (ages 12–23 months) sampled in the Jordan Population and Family Health Survey (JPFHS) between January and June 2023. The associations between sociodemographic and socioeconomic factors (including age, education, and employment), healthcare access, and childhood vaccination outcomes were explored. Healthcare access was measured through self-report of distance to a healthcare facility, while healthcare utilization was measured through the number of antenatal care visits. Whether the child’s mother had health insurance coverage was also included in the analysis model. Key confounding variables were included in the adjusted logistic regression model. The outcome variable was full vaccination coverage (defined in the national immunization schedule of Jordan).ResultsAmong the participants, 79% (n = 1,132) reported full vaccination coverage. 57% of mothers had a secondary or higher education, and this group was more likely to report full vaccination, higher education (AOR: 3.86, 95% CI: 1.14–13.05) and secondary education (AOR: 3.11, CI: 1.07–9.08). Most participants live in urban areas, with mothers living in rural areas less likely to report full child vaccination (AOR: 0.50, CI: 0.29–0.85).ConclusionThe results of the current study demonstrate that the percentage of children who received all basic vaccinations between 2023 and 2024 has declined since 2017–2018 in Jordan. Furthermore, maternal education is a key driving force behind achieving full vaccine uptake. Living in a rural area appears to reduce the likelihood of a child receiving full vaccination, while distance to a healthcare facility does not. These results require further investigation to inform the development of interventions aimed at slowing declining vaccine rates.

Background

The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that between 3.5 and 5 million deaths are prevented each year by vaccines [1]. Vaccines offer a cost-effective and efficient way to use the body’s immune system to prevent, or reduce the health impact of, approximately 30 diseases and conditions, including polio, tuberculosis, measles, HPV, and tetanus [2, 3]. Among children, a 2024 study reported that, globally, vaccinations for diphtheria-tetanus-pertussis, measles, rotavirus, and Haemophilus influenzae type b had prevented 86.9 million deaths between 1990 and 2019 [2]. A second study sought to model survival and health since the introduction of WHO’s Expanded Program on Immunization (EPI) in 1974 [4]. The authors estimated that 146 million deaths were averted among children under five, with 101 million among those < 1 year of age, since the introduction of the program. This achievement is due to greater access to vaccinations since EPI began, increasing from < 5% in 1974 to approximately 84% currently [5]. Finally, on a global level, vaccines have played a key role in eradicating some viruses in human populations, including small pox and polio types 2 and 3 [6].

The overarching focus of the current study is childhood vaccination uptake in Jordan and whether rates are changing (declining) as they are in other regions of the world [7]. Healthcare provision in Jordan is provided through three insurance systems – public, private, and refugee [8]. Since 1979, routine immunization for children has been mandatory in Jordan, with the immunization program (titled the National Expanded Programme on Immunization (EPI) led by the Ministry of Health [8, 9]. Every child in the country can receive vaccinations at a health center affiliated with the Ministry of Health. As a result, there is a high uptake of immunization for preventable diseases, and the program is considered a health success story in the region [8]. Jordan currently reports few cases of vaccine-preventable diseases. For example, the WHO Dashboard for Jordan reported < 20 cases each for rubella, tetanus, measles, mumps, diphtheria, pertussis, and yellow fever in 2024 [10].

Globally, vaccine hesitancy began increasing before the COVID-19 pandemic [7, 11]. It then increased precipitously amid the rapid rollout of the new mRNA vaccine for SARS-CoV-2, due to increased public health misinformation which, in turn, contributed to skepticism and hesitancy towards all vaccines [12, 13]. There were also disruptions in immunization scheduling (including pediatric) and declines in utilization due to lockdowns and COVID-19 fear that also reduced vaccine uptake [14]. Global studies report ongoing vaccine misinformation and corresponding parental hesitancy [15, 16]. A potential consequence of this is reduced uptake of childhood vaccinations below pre-pandemic rates [17]. For highly infectious diseases that require immunization rates ~ 95% for herd immunity, lower vaccination rates have caused significant outbreaks and an increasing number of deaths [7, 18].

During the pandemic in Jordan, drops of 14–16% for children aged < 12 months and 6–7% for those aged 1–2 years, respectively, were reported for recommended vaccines [19]. At the same time, whether vaccine uptake has experienced a rebound in the country, post-pandemic, is less well understood. Within Jordan, vaccine hesitancy related to the COVID-19 vaccines has been reported to stem from parental concerns and worries about vaccine safety, efficacy, and potential long-term health effects. For example, a 2023 study found that approximately 86% of the Jordanian parents in their sample had either resistance or hesitancy towards the measles rubella (MR) vaccine [12]. This result is of considerable importance considering that the government had implemented an MR catch-up vaccination campaign in 2023 to increase confidence and uptake of these vaccines following an outbreak of measles. A second study published in 2024 found that a complex interplay of socioeconomic and demographic factors, culture, and individual-level variables, as well as proximity and familiarity with the healthcare system, could positively or negatively influence parental decisions regarding vaccination in Jordan [20]. For example, fathers were more likely to be vaccine hesitant than mothers, as were divorced parents, while those with higher incomes or who worked in healthcare were less likely to be so.

The current study utilizes data from the Jordan Population and Family Health Survey (JPFHS) to explore the associations between sociodemographic and socioeconomic factors, healthcare access, and childhood vaccination outcomes. Healthcare access was measured through self-report of distance to a healthcare facility, the number of antenatal care visits, and whether a child’s mother had health insurance coverage. Within previous studies, including those that have utilized data from JPFHS, these are considered issues that generate barriers to, or facilitate, accessing healthcare [21]. The outcome variable was full vaccination coverage.

Methods

Design, setting, and context

This cross-sectional analysis draws on data from the most recent JPFHS - a nationally representative household survey conducted by the Department of Statistics (DoS) in Jordan [21]. The JPFHS is part of the Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) Program, which provides data for monitoring population and health trends. Data for the JPFHS were collected between January and June 2023 and made available for analysis by DoS in 2024 [21]. The data used within the current study was downloaded on March 15, 2024 from the DHS Program website (https://dhsprogram.com) [22] The survey offers up-to-date estimates on key sociodemographic, socioeconomic, and health indicators, including fertility, family planning, maternal and child health, and nutrition. The JPFHS includes data collected at the national level, with representation across urban and rural areas, governorates, and major nationality groups (Jordanian, Syrian, and other).

Sample sampling technique

The 2023 JPFHS employed a stratified two-stage cluster sampling design to obtain a nationally representative sample of households. In the first stage, the country was stratified into governorates. Primary sampling units (PSUs) were selected within each governorate with probability proportional to size (PPS), based on the most recent population census. These PSUs typically consisted of enumeration areas (EAs). The second stage involved a systematic random sampling of households within each selected PSU. This approach ensures adequate representation of different geographical regions and population subgroups while accounting for variations in population density. The survey aimed to interview all eligible women aged 15–49 and men aged 15–59 residing in the selected households. Sampling weights were calculated to adjust for unequal selection probabilities at each stage and to ensure that the final sample accurately reflects the age and regional distribution of the Jordanian population. These weights were applied in all analyses to provide nationally representative estimates. The survey also included specific protocols to ensure the inclusion of refugee populations residing within sampled households, contributing to a comprehensive understanding of health and demographic indicators across different population groups in Jordan.

Sample size

The sample for this study was restricted to survey data collected from mothers of children aged 12–23 months, the standard age range for assessing full vaccination coverage. Of the original dataset, 7,441 observations were excluded due to being outside this age range and 20 observations were also excluded for missing information on vaccination status. The final sample size for the analysis was 1,665 children.

Outcome variable: full vaccination coverage

In the current study, “full vaccination coverage” followed the Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) and WHO/UNICEF standard definition [23], this ensured comparability with national reports and international literature. A child aged 12–23 months was considered fully vaccinated if they had received: Bacille Calmette–Guérin (BCG) at birth, three doses of DPT-HepB-Hib (Pentavalent), three doses of polio, and one dose of measles vaccine, administered by 12 months of age. Documentation of vaccination was based on: [1] a written date recorded on the child’s vaccination card; [2] a tick mark on the card; or [3] maternal recall, consistent with DHS methodology.

In Jordan, the national immunization schedule also includes pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV) at 2, 4, and 6 months, measles-mumps-rubella (MMR) at 12 months, and booster doses of DTP, polio, and MMR at 18 months. However, these later doses were not included in our analytical definition of “full vaccination,” in line with DHS conventions.

A note on the inclusion of Pentavelant vaccines in the current study. We acknowledge that Jordan’s national immunization program currently uses the hexavalent vaccine (covering DTP, HepB, Hib, and IPV) rather than the pentavalent formulation. However, in the DHS dataset the vaccine is coded and labeled as “DPT/Pentavalent”, in line with the DHS global recode system. To maintain comparability with previous Jordan DHS reports and international DHS-based analyses, we used the DHS terminology in our study. For the polio vaccine, coverage in this analysis refers to the DHS-coded polio variable, which denotes receipt of oral polio vaccine (OPV) doses; in Jordan, the polio antigen is also provided through the hexavalent vaccine that includes IPV.

Key explanatory variables selected for the analysis

Explanatory variables were selected for analysis based on a literature search of published peer-reviewed evidence [25–28]. As previously mentioned, health coverage and access to healthcare were measured by self-reported perceived distance to a health facility (binary variable: a big problem, not a big problem) [21, 29]. The number of antenatal care visits was used as an indicator of health care utilization. Whether the mother had any health insurance coverage was also included in the analysis.

To isolate the effect of healthcare access on vaccination outcomes, the following variables were selected for descriptive analysis and inferential tests:

Height-for-age, weight-for-age, weight-for-height, and BMI-for-age z-scores (HAZ, WAZ, WHZ, and BAZ, respectively) were calculated based on WHO standards. Stunting and underweight were defined as HAZ and WAZ scores below − 2 standard deviations, respectively.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to describe and summarize the data in terms of numbers and weighted percentages. Survey-adjusted likelihood ratio was used instead of the traditional chi-square test statistic (χ2) to cross-tabulate vaccination coverage status by categorical covariates. This approach was utilized because the Jordan DHS employs a complex survey design (two-stage stratified cluster sampling with sampling weights, strata, and primary sampling units), traditional Pearson chi-square tests are not valid; they assume simple random sampling and independent observations. Using the survey-adjusted likelihood ratio accounts for the stratification, clustering, and weighting, thereby producing correct variance estimates and valid p-values under the DHS survey design. An independent sample t-test was used to compare continuous variables. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to determine the association between full vaccination coverage and the covariates. Using logistic regression, we examined sociodemographic and healthcare-related predictors of full vaccination coverage.

Key variables included: [1] maternal education (categorical: no education, primary, secondary, higher); [2] urban residence (binary: yes/no) [3], region (categorical, with Amman as reference); [4] wealth quintile; and [5] husband/partner occupation (categorical: white collar, blue collar, not working, unknown. Healthcare factors included ANC visits, contraceptive intention, and delivery mode (C-section vs. vaginal). To explore access to healthcare issues further, distance to health facilities was included in our model. We excluded age, birth order, insurance, and child health indicators, as they showed no association in bivariate analyses. Variables were selected based on P < 0.2 or theoretical importance, with categorical variables reference-coded and binary splits applied where appropriate. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. A formal regression diagnostic was performed, including variance inflation factor (VIF). Due to the disproportionate allocation of the sample across different areas and variation in response rates, sample weights were incorporated into all analyses as recommended. All analyses were performed using Stata 18th edition.

Results for full vaccination

The results presented in Tables 1 and 3 show that education level exhibited a notable trend: mothers with higher education were more likely to have fully vaccinated children (37% vs. 28% in the unvaccinated group). Within the logistic regression model, higher maternal education was strongly associated with increased vaccination likelihood, with secondary (AOR = 3.1, 95% CI: 1.0–9.0, P = 0.03) and higher education (OR = 3.8, 95% CI: 1.1–13.0, P = 0.03) demonstrating significant positive effects compared to no education.

Maternal and husband age showed no significant differences between vaccinated and unvaccinated groups. However, a higher percentage of children in the poorest quintile versus the richest (36% vs. 28%, respectively) were unvaccinated – but the regression analysis suggests that this was not a statistically significant difference.

As shown in Tables 2 and 3, healthcare access variables, including distance to health facilities and insurance coverage, did not exhibit statistically significant associations with vaccination status. Antenatal care visits demonstrated a trend: mothers with four or more visits had higher vaccination rates (93% vs. 87%), but this difference was not statistically significant within the regression analysis. Compared with urban dwellers, children living in rural areas were significantly less likely to be vaccinated (AOR: 0.50, CI: 0.30–0.85) – Table 3. There were inconsistent results by region, but they were suggestive of the presence of regional disparities. For example, Ajloun (OR = 0.47, 95% CI: 0.23–0.93, P = 0.03) and Ma’an (OR = 0.31, 95% CI: 0.11–0.88, P = 0.02) showed significantly lower vaccination rates compared to Amman (reference category).

No statistically significant associations were found for quintile wealth, antenatal care visits, contraceptive intention, delivery mode, the husband’s occupation, or access to a health facility. Finally, child-related factors, including sex, stunting, and underweight status, did not demonstrate a significant influence on vaccination coverage.

Discussion

This paper is the first to describe the prevalence and determinants of full vaccination coverage in a nationally representative sample of Jordanian children. Further, we report vaccination uptake for key vaccines separately. These results indicate a high level of vaccination coverage, which is likely contributing to the low rates of preventable diseases and conditions in Jordan [10]. Concurrently, we identified vaccination rates below 90% for the vaccines under-investigation in our current study (full vaccination (BCG + DPT/Pentavalent3 + Polio3 + Measles) and core vaccines individually), which may not be sufficient to reach herd immunity. Below, we discuss the results of our analysis and how Jordanian EPI could be improved to increase uptake.

A longstanding hypothesis in the literature suggests that maternal education is positively associated with child health and a reduction in child mortality [30, 31]. This is partly explained by increased vaccine uptake among the children of more educated mothers [31, 32]. The results of our study support this hypothesis, finding that mothers with either a secondary or higher education were significantly more likely to vaccinate their children compared with those with no education. We did not find significant associations between the education of the partner (male parent). There is currently a paucity of evidence in this area of investigation – most studies focus on the education of the mother. This area could and should be explored in future studies of diverse populations.

The results of our study also support those of earlier investigations, which found that children living in rural areas globally are less likely to be fully vaccinated against a range of conditions and diseases [33]. We also found statistically significant differences for key regions within Jordan. The reasons for such differences in vaccine uptake may be related to the geographic (in)accessibility of healthcare within specific areas, reduced travel infrastructure that creates challenges to accessing services, socioeconomic and cultural factors [34]. Addressing these disparities through targeted rural vaccination campaigns, including those provided online or by telephone, as well as improved healthcare access, could enhance overall coverage [35].

We did not find associations for a host of variables, including age and wealth. These results may be related to the relatively small sample size (1,665 children) utilized within our analysis and require further exploration in studies that can utilize a larger sample size. This study is also limited by its cross-sectional design, which precludes the detection of a causal relationship between covariates and full vaccine coverage. A further limitation relates to our operational definition of “full vaccination.” We applied the WHO/UNICEF and DHS standard definition (BCG, three doses of DPT/Pentavalent, three doses of polio, and one dose of measles by 12 months of age to enable comparability with prior Jordan DHS reports and with international literature. However, the national immunization program in Jordan also includes pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV) at 2, 4, and 6 months, measles-mumps-rubella (MMR) at 12 months, and booster doses of DTP, polio, and MMR at 18 months. These were not captured in our definition, which may underestimate vaccination coverage when compared with the full national EPI schedule. Future studies could examine adherence to the complete Jordan schedule, including 12- and 18-month doses, to provide a more comprehensive picture. The key strength of the study was our ability to utilize data from a nationally representative sample of those currently residing in Jordan.

While this study is focused upon individual-level factors related to vaccine uptake, it is also important to consider the wider societal context that individuals inhabit. There are several challenges to the vaccine program in Jordan, these include issues with trust and governance, such as populations with low confidence in health authorities, often compounded by misinformation and skepticism towards vaccines, as well as equity and access concerns. For example, barriers for refugees and rural populations include financial, geographical, travel capacity, and documentation-related issues. As well as delays caused by computer systems and registration issues [36].

Our study reports declines in full vaccine uptake for children (aged 12–23 months) from 86% between 2017 and 2018, as reported in the Population and Family Health Survey, to our figure of 79% [37]. This drop suggests that the declines in vaccination seen globally are being mirrored within Jordan, likely driven, in part, by misinformation [12, 17, 20, 38]. While we cannot be totally confident that the drop in vaccine uptake in Jordan is caused by the same factors causing declining uptake globally, the literature does support this, and we therefore hypothesize that the causes are likely driven by similar issues. This calls for an immediate multi-level and multi-pronged strategy that addresses the aforementioned barriers to vaccine uptake. To be effective, the strategy will likely need to include public awareness and health education to address misinformation, skepticism, and hesitancy provided by healthcare workers who are fully trained and able to provide a suite of support services.

The differences in uptake between regions found within our study suggest that culturally appropriate awareness campaigns, in regions with lower uptake, in Jordan are needed to provide services that effectively meet the population’s needs. Further, Jordanian EPI will also need to increase the convenience and accessibility of childhood vaccinations so that barriers related to distance and living location are reduced. This could be achieved by extending the program through integration into other services (vaccines alongside maternal and child health services, including antenatal care), extending opening hours to outside of working hours, providing a dynamic vaccination program to underserved areas via mobile vaccination units, and delivering vaccines through schools. Nationally, EPI should utilize the advances in data systems, electronic health records, and mobile apps to enable healthcare professionals and families to better manage vaccine appointments.

In the future, technology provides national EPI the opportunity to create digital immunization registries and health records, send text message reminders, and use data to identify harder to reach communities and individuals. Mobile phone use in Jordan is almost universal [39], this provides an opportunity for this technology to go some way to reducing the differences in vaccine uptake between communities, as well as those with differing educational levels, seen within our study. For this to occur education related to the use of mobile health applications and technology is likely required. Demand-side policies could include financial incentives (for example, cash payments or vouchers), while greater access to education for all (with specific education for expectant parents), and supply chain consistency are national policies that could be considered.

The results of this study enhance our understanding of the factors that influence whether a child receives vaccinations and whether they receive full vaccination coverage. Given the global shift towards reduced vaccination rates, this study helps us better understand the elements of healthcare, individual sociodemographic and socioeconomic factors, environment (rural versus urban), and behavior (for example, contraceptive use) that influence vaccination behavior among parents or caregivers. Given the growing influence of misinformation, it is important that parents are enabled to understand the benefits and risks of vaccination – such information needs to be tailored to the cultural, language and wider needs of the Jordanian population including the refugee population. Concurrently, the programs that provide vaccinations can also be adapted to more effectively meet the needs of children and their families – for example by introducing a multi-pronged strategy that amalgamates vaccination programs with other health services and extends opening hours, as well as mobile health units to increase access and convenience. Technology should also be utilized across the health sector to increase uptake of vaccines, improve data and record keeping and increase awareness of available services. Demand side policies (such as financial incentives) could also be explored.

Finally, the increasing population of refugees in Jordan requires services that can meet their healthcare needs, such as those that are culturally and linguistically tailored. Within Jordan, migrants and refugees are included in the national EPI – the work of UNICEF and the Ministry of Health ensures that all children receive free vaccinations [40, 41]. While there is a growing body of evidence exploring vaccine hesitancy among refugees and migrants, which demonstrates some evidence of high uptake and knowledgeable populations [42, 43], there is also some evidence of lower uptake [40]. This heterogeneity in rates suggests that refugee and migrant populations experience diverse barriers to vaccine uptake. These will need to be fully researched and tackled by the national EPI within Jordan to reach the whole population.

Conclusion

The current study highlights the importance of parental education, in conjunction with vaccination programs, in decisions regarding childhood vaccination utilization. Further, living in a rural area appears to reduce the likelihood of a child receiving full vaccination. These barriers require a dynamic strategy within the vaccination program that not only tackles vaccine hesitancy but also utilizes technology to increase uptake, while reducing inequalities between regions, and improves services through novel provision patterns and national policy. Improving EPI has the potential to reverse the declines in vaccine uptake seen not only in Jordan but across the globe. The results of this study require further investigation in diverse settings, as well as through studies that utilize larger sample sizes.
