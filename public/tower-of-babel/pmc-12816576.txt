Morphology analysis of the reconstruction of nano-filaments during aborted reset operation in Cu-based ReRAM devices
Authors: Shehla Yasmeen, Aaron DiFilippo, Marius Orlowski
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This paper presents a detailed physical analysis of the counterintuitive strengthening effect observed in Cu/TaOₓ/Pt-based resistive random-access memory (ReRAM) devices during an aborted reset operation. While our prior work [26] announced this phenomenon in a short letter, here, we develop a comprehensive electro-thermal model that links the observed resistance reduction directly to an atomic reconstruction of the conductive filament (CF). We demonstrate that the transformation from a truncated conical filament to a lower-resistance, hourglass-shaped structure is driven by the synergistic action of intense electric fields and localized Joule heating, which are both highly confined to the filament’s tip. This reconstruction of CF is governed by the fundamental physics of Cu atom diffusivity and ion electromigration within the TaOₓ matrix. Our model, constrained by volume conservation and supported by electrostatic and thermal simulations, successfully optimizes the filament geometry to predict a maximum resistance reduction factor of ~ 11, which aligns precisely with experimental data for high-resistance filaments. Furthermore, we identify a critical two-phase reset process: a reinforcement phase dominated by electromigration and a final rupture phase. The experimental dependence on voltage ramp rate is quantitatively explained by calculating the timescales required for Cu ion mobility, conclusively showing that slower ramps allow sufficient time for atomic rearrangement. This detailed analysis resolves the controversy surrounding filament morphology and provides profound insights into CF dynamics that are critical for advancing the reliability and design of ReRAM devices, particularly for neuromorphic computing applications.

Introduction

Resistive Random Access Memory (ReRAM) has emerged as a pivotal technology for next-generation non-volatile memory and neuromorphic computing, offering excellent scalability, a simple metal–insulator-metal (MIM) structure, and low power consumption1–3. Resistive RAM technologies are primarily categorized by their physical switching mechanism. The two main classes of ReRAM are filamentary and non-filamentary (or interfacial). Filamentary types are further divided in: (i) Electrochemical Metallization (ECM) Memory; this type relies on an electrochemically active electrode (e.g., Cu, Ag) that provides metal cations to form a conductive filament within an insulating solid electrolyte (e.g., TaOₓ, SiO₂). The switching is bipolar and the filament’s formation (SET) and dissolution (RESET) are governed by electrochemical processes. In this work our analysis is based on Cu/TaOx/Pt devices belonging to the ECM category.(ii) The Valence Change Mechanism (VCM) type Memory uses an electrochemically active electrode (e.g., TiN, Pt) and a metal oxide where oxygen vacancies are the mobile species (e.g., HfO₂, TaOₓ). The applied field drives the migration of oxygen anions/vacancies, changing the local stoichiometry and creating a highly conductive, oxygen-deficient filament. This is also a bipolar switching mechanism. An example of non-filamentary memory is the Phase-Change Memory (PCM) which operates on a fundamentally different principle. It uses a chalcogenide material (e.g., Ge₂Sb₂Te₅) that is switched between a crystalline (low-resistance) and an amorphous (high-resistance) state through the application of Joule heat (a melt-quench process for RESET, and an anneal for SET), not through the formation of a nanoscale metallic filament. Other notable types include Thermochemical Memory (TCM), driven purely by Joule heating, and non-filamentary ReRAM, where the resistance change occurs uniformly across an interface rather than being localized to a single filament.

In filamentary-type ReRAM cells, such as the Cu/TaOₓ/Pt system, the fundamental switching mechanism involves the formation and rupture of a conductive nanofilament (CF) via electrochemical metallization and thermochemical processes4–6. The SET operation (transition to a Low-Resistance State, LRS) is characterized by the field-driven growth of a metallic filament, while the RESET operation (transition to a High-Resistance State, HRS) is dominated by Joule-heat-induced dissolution of the filament at a critical current, Ireset7–11.

While numerous excellent studies have elucidated the mechanisms of filament rupture12–25, the intricate morphological evolution of the filament during the aborted reset operation—particularly the counterintuitive phenomenon of resistive strengthening—has remained unexplored. This effect is most manifest during an aborted reset operation 26. The preliminary reinforcement phase actively preconditions the final morphology of the ruptured filament, dictating the geometry of the gap. This, in turn, establishes the initial conditions for the subsequent SET operation, inevitably influencing the stochasticity and energy requirements of the next filament formation cycle. This is particularly true for incomplete or aborted reset operations26, where the applied voltage bias is halted near, but before reaching, the full rupture threshold. Such operations can lead to counterintuitive phenomena, including a robust reduction in device resistance, that challenge the conventional binary view of filament dynamics.

The ability to precisely control and modulate the conductive filament’s resistance is not merely an academic curiosity; it is the very foundation of ReRAM’s potential in neuromorphic computing27–29. In neuromorphic systems, devices must act as artificial synapses, capable of exhibiting a continuous range of stable conductance states—or “weights”—that can be incrementally potentiated or depressed. Standard ReRAM switching, however, often tends towards binary, stochastic behavior, which is a significant hurdle for reliable analog computing.

This work moves beyond the initial announcement of the resistive strengthening effect26 to provide a detailed physical and mathematical framework explaining the fundamental mechanisms behind this phenomenon during aborted reset operations in Cu/TaOₓ/Pt devices. We establish a comprehensive model that successfully bridges the gap between experimental electrical characteristics and the nanoscale electro-thermal atomic processes governing filament dynamics. We demonstrate that the observed reduction in resistance is a deterministic outcome of a fundamental morphological reconstruction of the filament, driven by the confluence of extreme electric fields and localized Joule heating at the filament tip.

A critical insight from our study is the introduction of a two-phase mechanism for the reset operation: an initial reinforcement phase governed by electromigration and atomic diffusivity, followed by the final rupture phase. We show experimentally and theoretically that the voltage ramp rate is the decisive parameter controlling which phase dominates, thus determining the final filament morphology and device resistance. This new paradigm resolves long-standing debates about filament evolution and reveals that the reset outcome is a complex competition between reinforcement and rupture. Our findings have significant implications for device engineering, providing a novel methodology to control filament morphology through operational parameters. Most importantly, the ability to intentionally strengthen a filament presents a intriguing novel functionality that could be directly exploited in multi-state memory and neuromorphic computing systems, where precise and stable control of synaptic weights is paramount.

Experimental

Cu/TaOₓ/Pt resistive random-access memory (ReRAM) cell arrays were fabricated in a crossbar configuration on thermally oxidized silicon substrates. The structure began with a 300 nm SiO₂ layer grown on a silicon wafer 2, followed by the sequential electron-beam physical vapor deposition (PVD) of Ti (20 nm), Pt (50 nm), and Cu (150 nm) thin films. The Pt electrode lines were patterned using a lift-off process with a 2 μm-thick photoresist layer to achieve gently sloped sidewall profiles. Between the definition of Pt and Cu electrodes, a 25 nm-thick, oxygen-deficient TaOₓ layer was deposited in a blanket fashion by evaporating TaOₓ pellets without introducing oxygen gas into the PVD chamber. Further fabrication and electrical characterization details are available in references30–32.

The current–voltage (I-V) characteristics were measured at room temperature using a Keithley 4200-SCS parameter analyzer. Conventional models attribute the reset operation to a combination of Joule-heating-assisted Cu atom thermodiffusion and electromigration of Cu ions, leading to filament rupture at a constriction point at critical Vreset and Ireset values. This rupture creates a nanoscale gap, causing the current to collapse to a nA-range values.

The observed reinforcement during an aborted reset has been hypothesized to result from an atomic reconstruction of the filament, where high Cu atom diffusivity and strong electromigration under high electric fields alter its structure13,15. The magnitude of this effect depends on the compliance current Icc(-), Vstop, and voltage ramp rate used during the aborted operation. Electrostatic simulations suggest that the high electric fields present in high-resistance filaments redistribute copper atoms, broadening the filament’s tip at the Cu electrode interface and transforming its shape from a cone to a more stable, low-resistance hourglass structure. This reconstruction reduces the local electric field, halting the process.

It is worth noting that the observed strengthening behavior is a local filamentary effect and is therefore independent of the device’s lateral dimensions. This is because the nanoscale conductive filament, whose morphology dictates the resistance, is several orders of magnitude smaller than the overall cell area. We have experimentally confirmed this by obtaining consistent results across devices with vastly different electrode dimensions, including 5 × 5 µm2 cells, 10 × 10 µm2 cells, and 35 × 35 µm2 cells.

These findings provide new insights into filament morphology and reveal a complex dynamic where the reset outcome is determined by a competition between filament reinforcement and rupture. This paper analyzes the mechanisms and effects of this atomic reconstruction in high-resistance filaments, comparing results with experimental data and linking them to the fundamental processes of Cu diffusion and electromigration in dielectric materials. In summary, the phenomenon reported here is specific to filamentary ReRAM cells and is not applicable to non-filamentary resistive switching devices, such as phase-change memory (PCM). We have experimentally demonstrated this effect in a Cu/TaOₓ/Pt cell as a representative example of an Electrochemical Metallization (ECM) device. Furthermore, we are confident that the underlying mechanism likely extends to vacancy-controlled filaments in Valence Change Mechanism (VCM)-type ReRAM. Therefore, we propose that this effect is a general characteristic of filamentary resistive switching in metal-oxide systems.

Theoretical models

The reconfiguration of the filament is governed by two key elements: the specific distribution of electric field and temperature within and around a highly resistive filament. This filament is geometrically represented as a truncated cone34 with a height, H, equal to the thickness of the TaOₓ layer, a base radius R, and a much smaller top radius r (r < < R).

The resistance of the conductive filament is intrinsically linked to its truncated cone geometry. As defined by Eq. (7), the resistance is proportional to the filament’s height (H) and inversely proportional to its top (r) and bottom (R) radii. Using an established Cu filament resistivity of 4 × 10⁻4 Ω·cm35 and holding the bottom radius and height constant at R = 30 nm and H = 25 nm, we can calculate how the top radius alone dictates the resistance. A moderately narrow cone with top radius r = 5 nm yields a resistance of ~ 666 Ω, a narrow cone with r = 1 nm yields ~ 3332 Ω, and a wide cone or a cylindrical filament where r = R = 30 nm yields ~ 111 Ω. This calculated range from 111 to 3332 Ω, achieved by varying only the top radius, aligns precisely with the experimental values reported in Table 1, validating the model’s geometric basis.

The tip of this cone is a critical region where the electric fields are most intense. Figure 3 shows explicit MATLAB simulations of the potential and electric field distributions for this geometry. For an applied voltage of 1 V—which is within the typical range of observed Vreset values—the maximum electric field near the cone’s tip reaches approximately 1.6 × 10⁶ V/cm. To facilitate further analysis, we propose a simplified model to approximate the potential drop and electric field between the conical filament tip and the Cu electrode. The potential distribution along the cone can be derived from the voltage division principle, where the potential at a given height z is equal to the resistance of the cone segment at z divided by the total cone resistance, multiplied by the applied voltage. This yields the function V(z), where the coordinate z = 0 is defined at the cone’s tip. A transformation from a truncated cone into a cylinder would require that all Cu atoms in the filament are free to move via electromigration throughout the TaOx matrix. However, as established in earlier discussions, the electric field is strong only in the top part of the frustum (see Figs. 2and 6). Simultaneously, the temperature due to Joule heating is also high only in this same top segment of the cone. Therefore, it is clear that the bulk lower part of the truncated cone will remain unaffected by the reconstruction process. We can thus assume that only the upper part undergoes reconstruction. From our calculations of the electric field and temperature distributions, we estimate that only a small upper part of the cone, with a height of approximately 0.15H, is affected by the reconstruction, while the bulk lower part of height 0.85H remains unchanged.

Thus, we see that the reset operation can, in principle, occur in two phases. The first phase of filament reinforcement happens when the current is near but below the Ireset value. During this phase, the temperature and electric fields is high enough to cause Cu atom displacement, leading to filament reconfiguration if sufficient time is allowed. The second phase is the rupture itself, which occurs when the temperature is so high that Cu atoms diffuse out of the filament at the hottest spot (i.e., near the constriction), creating a permanent gap.

The first phase can be probed experimentally with an aborted reset operation at a high Icc(-) < Ireset , e.g. around 0.8 Ireset. If the voltage ramp rate is slow (rr = 0.03 V/s, as used for the experiments in Table 1), then the time near Icc(-) = 0.8Ireset is sufficient for Cu ions to electromigrate and effect filament reconfiguration. Here, Vstop can be slightly larger than Vreset. However, if the same operation is performed with a fast ramp rate (rr = 2.0 V/s), there is insufficient time for significant Cu ion displacement, and reconfiguration does not occur. This is borne out by the results of experiments shown in Table 2 where the strengthening effect is shown as a function of ramp rate.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this work provides a detailed physical and mathematical framework to explain the fundamental mechanisms behind the resistive strengthening phenomenon observed in Cu/TaOₓ/Pt ReRAM devices during aborted reset operations. Moving beyond the initial announcement of the effect, we have established a comprehensive model that successfully bridges the gap between experimental electrical characteristics and the nanoscale electro-thermal atomic processes governing filament dynamics.

Our analysis demonstrates that the counterintuitive reduction in resistance, where Ron(ar) can be significantly smaller than Ron, on(+) on(-), is not a measurement artifact but a direct result of a deterministic atomic reconstruction of the conductive filament. This transformation is driven by the confluence of two key factors localized exclusively at the filament’s tip: extremely high electric fields, which can exceed 106 V/cm, and localized Joule heating, which elevates temperatures sufficiently to enable rapid Cu atom diffusion and ion mobility. The model reveals that under these conditions, a fragile, high-resistance truncated cone evolves into a more stable, lower-resistance double-cone or hourglass structure.

A critical insight from this study is the introduction of a two-phase mechanism for the reset operation. The first, reinforcement phase, occurs at high currents near but below Ireset and is governed by electromigration and diffusivity, allowing the filament to reconfigure if sufficient time (ramp rate) is provided. The subsequent rupture phase occurs at a critical current and temperature, leading to the final local dissolution of the filament. The experimental validation using different voltage ramp rates is a key finding; it quantitatively proves that the timescale for ion mobility—calculated from fundamental diffusivity data and the Einstein relation—is the decisive factor controlling which phase dominates. Slow ramp rates (~ 0.03 V/s) provide a ~ 33 s window for complete reconstruction, while fast rates (~ 2.0 V/s) bypass it entirely, leading to immediate rupture.

Furthermore, our geometrical optimization, accurately predicts the maximum possible reinforcement factor (~ 11) and its strong dependence on the initial filament’s tip sharpness. The excellent agreement between this theoretical limit and the experimental data range (1 to 11.3) provides compelling evidence for the model’s validity.

This deeper understanding has significant implications. It resolves long-standing debates about filament morphology and reveals that the reset outcome is not a simple process but a complex competition between reinforcement and rupture. This new paradigm is crucial for device engineering. The ability to control filament morphology through operational parameters like ramp rate and compliance current opens new avenues for enhancing device endurance and tailoring switching characteristics. Finally, the ability to intentionally strengthen a filament, as demonstrated here, presents a fascinating novel functionality that could be exploited in multi-state memory or neuromorphic computing systems, where precise and stable control of synaptic weights is paramount.29

In neuromorphic computing, the resistance of a memristive device represents the synaptic weight—the strength of a connection between artificial neurons—where a key challenge is updating this weight in a precise, analog manner28,29,38. The discovered “strengthening reset” effect, wherein a compliance-current-limited reset operation reinforces a filament instead of rupturing it, provides a unique and elegant solution. This phenomenon fundamentally transforms the reset operation from a simple, binary ‘erase’ function into a sophisticated ‘sculpting’ tool. Traditionally, memristive synapses have been hampered by asymmetric weight updates: a SET pulse (positive bias) increases the weight, while a RESET pulse (negative bias) decreases it. Now, with the strengthening (aborted) reset operation (but not RESET), we have two methods for potentiation. The first is the standard potentiation via SET, using a positive voltage to form or grow the filament. The second, and more novel, is potentiation via a constrained RESET operation, which applies a negative voltage with a specific, low compliance current to reconstruct and strengthen a weak filament. This dual approach allows for a more symmetric and potentially linear tuning of the synaptic weight, as both voltage polarities can be leveraged for strengthening, depending on the Icc(-) parameter. Furthermore, this versatility directly enables the implementation of sophisticated learning rules like Spike-Timing-Dependent Plasticity (STDP), where a Long-Term Potentiation (LTP) event could be triggered by either a standard SET pulse or a carefully calibrated strengthening reset pulse, giving designers more tools to fine-tune the learning process. The effect’s pronounced nature in high-resistance filaments makes it particularly valuable for making fine, incremental adjustments to weak synapses, which is often the most challenging regime. Beyond weight updates, the strengthening reset can enhance stability and retention for faint memories; weak filaments are often unstable and prone to decay, but by periodically applying a mild, constrained reset operation pulse as a reinforcement mechanism, a neuromorphic system can actively stabilize these synaptic structures against forgetting. In summary, this effect provides neuromorphic circuit designers with an additional, highly controllable knob to finely craft and maintain synaptic weights, paving the way for more stable, linear, and biologically plausible neural networks.
