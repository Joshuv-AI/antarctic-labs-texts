Global research trends in the relationship between influenza and CD4+ T/CD8+ T cells: A bibliometric analysis
Authors: Yanping Zong, Hui Li, Yonglong Chang, Jiajie Li, Lei He, Weibing Shi, Jinchen Guo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTInfluenza pathogens cause many illnesses and deaths yearly, posing a serious threat to global public health. As a result, most studies are increasingly focusing on the role of specific CD4+ T/CD8+ T cells in combating influenza. This study examines the key themes and trends in this field using bibliometric analysis. Literature on influenza and CD4+ T/CD8+ T cells were searched (from 1985 to 2023) in the Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC) database. Eligible articles were screened according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria for bibliometric analysis using VOSviewer, CiteSpace, and the R package “bibliometrix.” A total of 1,071 publications from 47 countries or regions and 1,148 institutions associated with 5,728 authors in the disciplines of immunology, virology, biochemistry, and molecular biology were included. The findings indicate a yearly increase in publications related to influenza and CD4+ T/CD8+ T cells, with the United States, Australia, and China leading in publication volume. The University of Melbourne had the highest volume of publications. Only a few researchers collaborated, and the collaborations were mostly concentrated in the same countries/regions. Professor Katherine Kedzierska, associated with The Peter Doherty Institute for Infection and Immunity, was the most productive academic in this field. According to the analysis of highly cited literature and keywords, the application of cellular immunity in formulating pioneering influenza vaccines is a key direction for future research.The role of CD4+ T/CD8+ T cells in combating the influenza virus has emerged as a significant focus within influenza research literature. This article summarizes the research institutions, authors, journals, hotspots, and application trends of CD4+ T/CD8+ T cells in influenza.KEYWORDS: Bibliometric analysis, Influenza, CD4+ T/CD8+ T cells, research trends, Citespace, VOSviewer

Introduction

Influenza is an acute respiratory illness caused by influenza viruses.1,2 The influenza virus is classified within the Orthomyxoviridae family and is a multi-segmented, single-stranded, negative-sense RNA virus. The incubation period of influenza infection is usually 18 to 72 hours,3 indicating a latency period between viral infection and the commencement of observable clinical manifestations. Influenza viruses are primarily classified into types A, B, C, and D according to their genetic compositions. Influenza A viruses, being highly mutable, play a key role in epidemics. The World Health Organization has shown that influenza causes many severe illnesses and deaths yearly, presenting a significant threat to human health and the global health system.4,5 Influenza viruses are highly contagious and rapidly spread, causing pandemics worldwide. Particularly, influenza A (H1N1) viruses can infect immunocompromised hosts, leading to severe clinical manifestations and increased mortality rates.6 Influenza is mainly controlled and prevented via vaccination and antiviral treatment.7 Vaccination strengthens the body’s immune system against the influenza virus, reducing the incidence of infection and illness. Meanwhile, antiviral drugs can reduce symptoms and shorten the duration of influenza infections.

CD4+ T/CD8+ T cells are expressed by mature T cells. Influenza virus infection mobilizes cellular and humoral immunity of the body, the severity and regression of the disease are significantly influenced by this pivotal factor.8 The body’s immune system is mainly composed of T and B lymphocytes. The body fights against viral infections mainly through specific T lymphocyte immune responses. Furthermore, various subpopulations of T lymphocytes regulate and maintain the stability of the body’s internal immune environment.9 T-lymphocyte subgroups include CD4+ T cells and CD8+ T cells. CD4+ T cells mainly aid the antibody response, while CD8+ T cells act as the primary effector cells in combating the influenza virus through cellular immunity. Cytokine generation by CD4+ T cells and CD8+ T cells regulates inflammatory changes in the lungs at the initial stage of influenza virus infection, highlighting their crucial function in this viral disease. Currently, immunization remains the most effective method for preventing and controlling influenza. However, the need for annual production of new vaccines poses a significant challenge due to the virus’s high rate of antigenic mutation. While humoral immunity offers strain-specific protection, cellular immunity, which targets different serotypes or antigenic variants, is gaining increased attention. In particular, CD4+ T and CD8+ T cells, which recognize conserved viral epitopes, are emerging as key focus areas for influenza vaccine design. Thus, a deeper understanding of the role of CD4+ T and CD8+ T cells in influenza is crucial for improving pandemic preparedness and guiding future vaccine development strategies.

Bibliometrics, as an interdisciplinary field, are increasingly used in medicine.10 Through bibliometric methods, researchers are not only able to analyze academic literature quantitatively and qualitatively, but also assess the present condition of studies and developing patterns in a particular subject area and promote a deeper understanding of the development of the field.11 In recent years, research on the role of CD4+ T and CD8+ T cells in influenza has grown significantly, yielding important advancements. However, despite its high research value, a comprehensive bibliometric analysis of CD4+ T and CD8+ T cells in the context of influenza has not yet been conducted. To address this gap, our study systematically analyzed the relevant literature using bibliometric methods to identify key countries/regions, institutions, journals, and authors contributing to this field. The results highlight emerging research trends and provide a valuable overview of the field’s progress. This analysis offers more in-depth and objective insights into the application of CD4+ T and CD8+ T cell immune mechanisms in influenza diagnosis and treatment, offering crucial direction and reference for future clinical studies.

Methods

Data collection

A literature search was conducted on April 26, 2024, using the Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC) database to collect research data. The search covered the period from January 1, 1985, to December 31, 2023. The following keywords were used: TS= (“Influenza” OR “Flu”) AND TS= (“CD4+ T cell” OR “CD8+ T cell”). A total of 2,317 studies were obtained after the search. Two reviewers filtered the studies based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria (Figure 1). Discrepancies were resolved through consensus with a third author. Finally, 1,071 valid publications were included. These articles were then saved in plain text format for further analysis.

Flowchart showing the filtering process.

Data analysis

In this study, bibliographic analysis was conducted using VOSviewer (1.6.18), CiteSpace (6.3.R1), and R package bibliometrix (4.1.1) to visualize and analyze bibliometric and citation data in the scholarly literature. VOSviewer employs a probability-based data normalization method and provides multiple visualization options, including Network, Overlay, and Density visualizations. These graphical representations effectively highlight key elements like keywords, collaborating institutions, and coauthors, offering intuitive mapping and visually appealing graphics.12 In the co-citation diagram, each component is represented by a node, with nodes of the same color indicating a certain level of association. The size of the node reflects the number of relevant publications or citations, with larger nodes signifying greater importance. The distance between two nodes represents the strength of their relationship, where shorter distances indicate stronger connections, and longer distances suggest weaker associations.13 CiteSpace uses a set theory-based data normalization method to evaluate the similarity between knowledge units. A similarity algorithm generates Timezone and Timeline views at specific intervals, effectively illustrating the evolution of knowledge and the historical scope of literature within a cluster. This approach provides deeper insights into the field’s development and highlights emerging trends.14 In the co-occurrence analysis map, node size represents the number of publications, while the connections between nodes indicate collaboration. The thickness of the links reflects the frequency of these collaborations. A higher level of co-occurrence or co-citation is indicated by more interconnected nodes and thicker connections.15 R package bibliometrix provided quantity statistics of basic information. The quantitative statistics of basic information, such as authors, institutions, countries, regions, and keywords, were obtained from “bibliometrix.” The quantitative statistics were quantitatively analyzed and visually plotted using Microsoft Office Excel 2021 and R package tidyverse (ggplot2). VOSviewer was used to visualize and analyze the collaboration between country regions, institutions, and authors (collaboration map) and keyword clustering (co-occurrence map). In addition, citation bursts, keyword time series, and burst citations were analyzed using CiteSpace, providing a time-series analysis of the literature data and demonstrating the evolutionary trend of citations and keywords.

The studies were associated with 5,492 scholars from 1,148 institutions in 47 nations or territories across 191 periodicals. The earliest publication, entitled “Murine CD4+ T cell clones vary in function in vitro and in influenza infection in vivo” was made in 1990 in the journal International Immunology by Taylor P M, a British scholar. In that publication, multiple CD4 Th1 variants, aimed at either influenza hemagglutinin or nucleosides, were administered to homozygous mice to probe whether these variants, similar to CD8 cytotoxic T cells, can improve viral eradication in vivo. The publication aimed to identify modifications in the functional characteristics of CD4 variants in vivo and their unique effects on the advancement of infection in vivo.16 However, the publications of related studies only showed a substantial increasing trend from 2003, reaching 74 in 2016 (average annual growth rate; 12.45%). The co-cited references and citation bursts

Co-citation is an important concept in scientific research. Co-citation refers to literature that is simultaneously cited by two or more academic publications and is regarded as one of the indicators of the importance of a specific area of research, as it shows the influence and acceptance of a particular article in that field. The article published by Sridhar S et al. in 2013 (the correlation between cellular immunity and safeguarding against influenza viruses) in Nature Medicine ranked first with 118 citations (Figure 6a). The investigation monitored 342 healthy individuals in the United Kingdom during an influenza outbreak, and the researchers correlated the T-lymphocyte reactions to the pH1N1 influenza virus with their influenza infections in the clinical setting. The results indicated that protection against symptomatic influenza is correlated with specific CD8+ T cell reactions toward conserved viral epitopes in the nonexistence of cross-reactive neutralizing antibodies. This implies that particular reactions of distinct T cells can offer a level of defense even without the presence of antibodies. This discovery can guide the creation of a comprehensive influenza vaccine.23 The detailed information on the top 10 co-referenced publications, including their citation count, is shown in Figure 6a. Discussion

Bibliometrics, as an emerging methodology, assesses the present condition and trajectories in the research field by quantitatively analyzing the scientific literature. The amount of literature on CD4+ T/CD8+ T cells related to influenza has surged in recent years owing to the growing interest. However, no bibliometric analysis has been conducted in this field. As a result, the overall research trends and priorities in this field are unclear. This systematic assessment of the research literature provides a thorough grasp of the present research landscape in this field, pinpointing potential research directions and priorities. This study also offers an in-depth analysis of trends and changes within the domain and comprehensively integrates quantitative and qualitative assessments. Overall, this investigation may offer crucial references and direction for subsequent advancements in the CD4+ T/CD8+ T cells and influenza-associated research. The subsequent sections offer a deeper insight into the research dynamics and future trajectories within the field.

Knowledge base

Analyzing the extensively cited literature offers a thorough comprehension of the responses of CD4+ T/CD8+ T cells in influenza.

Influenza, a respiratory illness caused by influenza viruses, is common during winter. These pathogens, belonging to the Orthomyxoviridae lineage, have single-stranded RNA genomes, which are divided into eight segments. Influenza viruses are categorized into categories A, B, C, and D based on the type of surface glycoproteins of the hemagglutination inhibition antigen (H) and the neuraminidase inhibition antigen (N).24,25 Immune system, comprising innate and adaptive immunity, is the body’s main defense against influenza viruses. Innate immunity identifies and eliminates pathogens through nonspecific methods, while acquired immunity relies on the actions of specific immune cells and molecules. Major histocompatibility complexes (MHC) within the immune system are highly diverse molecules pivotal in presentation of antigens and T-cell detection. MHC molecules mainly aid in antigen presentation to T-cells, triggering the immune response.26 Researchers (1974) found that MHC restricts the killing function of T cells, laying the groundwork for understanding MHC’s pivotal role in T cell antigen recognition.27 The interplay between the T-cell receptor (TCR) and antigenic peptide-major histocompatibility complex (pMHC) assemblies is crucial in T-cell functional maturation and activation.28 About 90–95% of particular T cells experience spontaneous apoptosis after the initial influenza infection. Furthermore, the residual cells transform into memory T cells, which are essential for immune memory functions.29 Recollection T cells are essential in the immune system due to their significant proliferative capacity, prolonged survival time, and self-renew ability over the long term.30 Recollection T cells undergo swift clonal amplification and transformation into subsequent effector T cells upon re-infection with the influenza virus. This process enables quicker infection control, resulting in a faster immune response and enhanced protection.31

In 1983, McMichael et al. showed that CD8+ T cells are essential in viral clearance. They also validated the capacity of CD8+ T cells for cross-reactivity, indicating that the cells can recognize and counteract diverse strains of influenza virus.32 In 1986, Townsend A.R.M. et al. discovered that a subgroup of cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs), stimulated by influenza infection, could recognize viral nuclear proteins displayed by target cells. They found that CTLs identified short overlapping regions of nuclear protein fragments produced in transfected L cells, recognizing these epitopes in association with class I major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules in both mice and humans. These epitopes could be further characterized using short synthetic peptides derived from nuclear protein sequences.33 In 1992, Bender B.S. et al. explored the role of CD8+ T cells in recovering from influenza pneumonia using β2-microglobulin (β2-M)-deficient transgenic mice. They observed a significant delay in viral clearance from lung tissue and increased mortality rates in mice exposed to pathogenic influenza strains, highlighting the critical role of CD8+ T cells in recovery from severe influenza infections.34 In 1998, Flynn K.J. et al. used tetramer staining and peptide activation to evaluate primary and secondary CD8+ CTL responses in mice with influenza pneumonia. They found a substantial accumulation of virus-specific CD8+ T lymphocytes in inflamed, non-lymphatic tissues, emphasizing the importance of these cells in both primary and secondary responses, as well as the persistence and limitations of CD8+ T cell memory.35 In 2000, Belz G.T. et al. infected H-2B mice with H3N2 influenza A viruses, inducing acute pneumonia with significant CD8+ T lymphocyte infiltration. Upon subsequent H3N2 to H1N1 challenge, this response persisted but was notably reduced during secondary activation, suggesting that the H-2 DB-restricted peptide plays a diminished role in secondary CD8+ T cell responses to influenza.36 In 2008, Lee et al. examined CD4+ and CD8+ memory T cell responses to human (H3N2) and avian (H5N1) influenza in vitro, highlighting the importance of internal protein regions for cross-protective memory T cells. Their findings suggest that cross-reactive CD4+ and CD8+ memory T cells enhance the development of vaccines targeting conserved internal protein regions, providing broad protection.37 In 2013, Sridhar S et al. showed that CD4+ T and CD8+ T cells triggered through seasonal influenza contagion also display intracellular proteins that can cross-recognize pH1N1,23 suggesting that they have some degree of cross-protective properties. Although cross-responsive T cells may not completely hinder the establishment of infection, they can identify and target epitopes conserved among viral subtypes,38 thereby potentially mediating heterosubtypic immune responses, facilitating viral elimination, and diminishing illness intensity. In addition, the literature also points out the significance of CD8+ T cells in influenza management. Operational CD8+ T cells decrease influenza-like symptoms in an a/H1N1-pdm-09 virus-affected group.23 Subsequent research has indicated that individuals possessing cross-reactive memory CD8+ T cells experience quicker recovery than those lacking such memory T cells.39,40 Furthermore, the long lifespan of influenza-specific memory CD8+ T cells suggests their persistence in the immune response.41 Therefore, future influenza vaccines should be developed using CD8+ T cells.42,43

These studies indicate that a vaccine stimulating cross-reactive T cells can offer wider and more efficient effects against potential pandemic variants. However, current investigations only focused on T-cell cultures expanded in laboratory settings, with various limitations impeding a precise assessment of comprehensive influenza-targeted memory T-cell reactions and the magnitude of cross-responsiveness toward diverse strains. Specifically, there are difficulties and constraints regarding the scope, intensity, and dominant order of responses at the population level. Therefore, these issues should be addressed through in-depth research and methodological improvements to advance the development of novel vaccine formulations targeting the stimulation of cross-protective T cells.

Emerging topics

The examination of extensively referenced literature and terms in influenza studies showed that the focus and trend of using cellular immunity as the research and development of novel influenza vaccines is a popular direction in this field. Specifically, targeting and inducing CD4+ T/CD8+ T cells to identify preserved viral epitopes is one of the main directions in designing influenza vaccines.

The presently utilized seasonal influenza immunizations comprise deactivated vaccines cultivated in chicken embryos, recombinant hemagglutinin (HA) proteins manufactured in insect cells, or cold-adapted live weakened vaccines administered via the nasal route.44 These vaccines primarily target the HA viral glycoproteins of influenza viruses, which are the major antigens of influenza viruses.45 One major challenge with antibody-centered influenza vaccines is that the HA glycoproteins on the influenza virus are prone to mutations. These mutations can render the previous year’s vaccine ineffective against the currently dominant strain, potentially leaving people vulnerable to infection. This is because neutralizing antibodies against previously dominant strains cannot kill antigenically distinct strains, which reduces the effectiveness of cross-protection.46 Therefore, to manage the changing influenza strains, influenza immunizations need to be reformulated on yearly basis to align with the guidelines of the World Health Organization, ensuring maximal safeguarding.5

Considering the significant antigenic shift of seasonal influenza viruses, researchers are developing strategies for inducing the body to generate new seasonal influenza vaccines with strong cross-protective efficacy against influenza to T-cell immunity.47,48 For instance, stimulating CD4+ T/CD8+ T cells to identify preserved viral antigenic sites has become an effective approach for formulating new influenza immunizations. Currently, There are two primary approaches employed in designing vaccines: one involves the use of various vectors to delivery vaccines, such as the internal proteins of nuclear protein (NP) and M1, which are processed by the major histocompatibility complex (MHC) and displayed on T cells. Uddbäck et al. used adenovirus as a vector to express the NP protein of influenza virus which led to lung accumulation of influenza virus-specific CD8+ T cells for up to 1 year.49 Another study stimulated cytotoxic CD8+ T lymphocytes and resident memory (TRM) CD8+ T cells to express influenza A virus NP sequences fused with OVX313‘s heptameric structural domain. This resulted in persistent immunity against various influenza viruses.50 In contrast, a vaccine developed from adenoviral vector articulating the influenza B NP protein efficiently stimulated a CD8+ T-cell response and offered broad protection against two separate strains of influenza B virus in mice, regardless of differences in amino acid makeup.51 These findings provide valuable insights for future design of effective influenza vaccines.

One promising strategy for influenza vaccines is the design of multi-epitope immunogens. These immunogens act like training tools for the immune system. They contain multiple epitopes from different influenza virus proteins. By presenting these epitopes together, the vaccine stimulates the body to generate a broader immune response. This encompasses a broader array of antibodies and T-cell responses. A key benefit of this approach is its ability to target conserved epitopes, which are protein segments that remain relatively unchanged across different influenza strains. This allows the vaccine to offer broader protection and potentially higher efficacy against various influenza viruses. This vaccination approach has been investigated in diverse platforms and protein combinations. For instance, the ChAdOx1 NP+M1 and cHA vaccine utilizes a combined immunization strategy, consisting of preserved linear epitopes from the NP, M1, and HA proteins of influenza A viruses. The blending of these elements stimulates an immune reaction targeting diverse proteins, which amplifies the vaccine’s immunogenicity. Biondvax, an Israel-based company, has engineered Multimeric-001 (referred to as M-001), containing preserved linear epitopes from the influenza A virus’ NP, M1, and HA proteins. The design concept of this vaccine is similar to that of the ChAdOx1 NP+M1 and cHA vaccines, both of these were formed using several epitopes to broaden the range and efficacy of the immune response. Building on the concept of multi-epitope immunogens, Bianchi et al. focused on a specific target: the highly conserved cleavage site of the influenza B virus HA protein in its precursor form (HA0). Their study in mice showed that a vaccine containing peptides corresponding to this cleavage site triggered a broad immune response against both strains of influenza B virus. Importantly, the vaccine fully protected the mice from lethal influenza B infection. These results provide strong evidence for the feasibility and potential of multi-epitope immunogen design in developing broadly protective influenza vaccines. Beyond developing new, innovative influenza vaccines, research is also focusing on how to improve the vaccines themselves. One approach is to use vaccine adjuvants. These are adjuncts incorporated into vaccines to boost the immune reaction. Research has indicated that a particular variety of enhancer, an acrylic adjuvant that targets TLR receptors, is particularly effective in stimulating the body to produce CD8+ T cells in response to both H1N1 and H5N1 influenza A viruses (IAVs).52 MacKerracher et al. utilized polylactic acid-hydroxyacetic acid copolymers as nano-adjuvant particles to induce T-cell formation in the lungs of mice. MacKerracher et al. used polylactic acid-hydroxyacetic acid copolymers as nanoadjuvant particles to induce T cell formation in the pulmonary system of mice. They reported that such nanoadjuvants served as effective nanoadjuvants for novel influenza vaccines.53 Notably, influenza immunization utilizing the nanoparticle pH response has shown good efficacy in triggering CD8+ T cells targeting influenza virus-specific antigens in both the upper respiratory tract and lungs, protecting mice from lethal amounts of the influenza virus.54 Therefore, researchers should not only investigate the role of T lymphocytes in the cross-reactivity against diverse strains of influenza viruses, but also the mechanisms underlying T cell generation in tissues and their perstistance for long durations. This will lead to the formulation of novel vaccines that can provide long-term protection to the body, while also taking into account the complementary role of vaccine adjuvants and new materials.

In summary, Influenza viruses pose a major threat to human health due to their high mutation rates, driven by segmented genes and polymerases that lack proofreading functions. This leads to frequent antigenic drift and shift during infection, underscoring the need for a universal vaccine to address the limited protection offered by traditional vaccines against new influenza strains. In addition to conventional approaches such as inactivated whole-virus vaccines, live attenuated vaccines, and subunit vaccines, new platforms – such as vector vaccines, nucleic acid vaccines, and virus-like particles (VLPs) – are actively being developed. However, despite numerous candidates, none have successfully passed phase III clinical trials. Current vaccine development is focused on targeting conserved regions of the influenza virus and delivering antigens in ways that trigger a broad immune response. CD4+ T and CD8+ T cells that recognize conserved viral epitopes have become promising targets for designing influenza vaccines. These studies offer new strategies for developing broader-spectrum and more effective vaccines. By incorporating multi-epitope immunogen designs, researchers aim to better manage influenza virus variability, improving vaccine coverage and long-term protection.55 Although this method can achieve better protection than previous conventional vaccines, it still faces many new problems and challenges. For example, the variations in sequences among target proteins across various isoforms and the rivalry among distinct epitopes. Therefore, researchers should explore the appropriate antigenic epitopes, immunodominant region, and the correct delivery method, etc., to obtain ideal vaccines with the optimal immune response.56 In addition, external protein targets can completely protect against homologous viruses, but a certain broad spectrum will be lost; internal proteins can promote virus clearance through T-cell response, thus obtaining a wider range of action, but their immunogenicity is poor, often requiring optimized delivery conditions or multi-targets to work together. Therefore, in future studies, in addition to further investigating the effective targets of influenza virus, researchers will also explore various aspects of the immune pathway, immunization procedures, and novel adjuvants to further improve the vaccine effect. In terms of vaccine evaluation, the establishment of preclinical animal models, the determination of endpoints in clinical trials, and the comparison and assessment of clinical outcomes are also urgent issues.

Limitation and outlook

The information source for this research is the Web of Science (Core Collection), which numerous scholars acknowledge as a reputable repository for digital literature. This study regarded it as the most appropriate database for bibliometric assessment.57 Visualization and analysis software like CiteSpace and VOSviewer not only unveil the alterations in the configuration and patterns of CD4+ T/CD8+ T cells response in influenza, but also help to identify key nodes, research hotspots and academic collaboration networks in the research field, providing important clues for further research. This investigation has certain constraints. One key limitation is the scope of the data sources. The study only uses WoSCC, which excludes other relevant databases like PubMed and Scopus. This could limit the comprehensiveness of the analysis, particularly for regions or disciplines less represented in WoSCC. Also, the analysis is constrained by the availability of literatureindexed in the Web of Science Core Collection, potentially excluding relevant studies published in other databases or languages. In addition, while quantitative analysis tools can provide a large amount of data, the interpretation of these data requires in-depth skills to prevent subjective bias. Lastly, while the study focuses on publications from 1985 to 2023, it may overlook emerging trends and recent advancements in T cell research related to influenza that are still unfolding.

Despite these limitations, continued research on CD4+ T/CD8+ T cells responses in influenza is expected to provide more comprehensive data through collaborative work of scholars in related fields. This will expand our objective understanding of the field, minimize subjective bias, and promote the development of research in this area.

Conclusion

This comprehensive analysis indicates that CD4+ T/CD8+ T cells play an critical contribution in the development of influenza vaccines. Publications related to CD4+ T/CD8+ T cells and influenza are increasing annually. Among the leading contributors to this research domain are primarily the United States, Australia, and China. The University of Melbourne leads the way in research on T cell-based influenza vaccines, with the highest number of publications in this field. Professor Katherine Kedzierska from The Peter Doherty Institute for Infection and Immunity is a prominent figure in this research area. In future, researchers should explore how to induce cellular immunity to fight the influenza virus.
