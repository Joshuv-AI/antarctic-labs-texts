Machine Learning-Based Assessment of Long-Term Groundwater Level Dynamics in the Nakhon Luang Aquifer from 1978 to 2023
Authors: Kanyapak Jankate, Srilert Chotpantarat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Groundwater is a critical resource supporting economic and social development in Thailand, particularly within the lower Chao Phraya Basin, where rapid urbanization and industrial expansion have led to intensive groundwater exploitation. This study applied a random forest (RF) machine learning model to investigate groundwater level (GWL) dynamics in the Nakhon Luang aquifer, which is one of the most productive aquifers due to its high yield and relatively good water quality, using long-term data from 1978 to 2023. The seven data sets include groundwater levels, groundwater pumping, rainfall, groundwater recharge, ground surface elevation, geological information, and lag-time features incorporated to represent delayed aquifer responses. The results demonstrate strong predictive performance, with R 2 = 0.851 for the testing data set. Cross-validation results further indicated stable model performance (R 2 = 0.833 ± 0.018). Feature importance analysis revealed that groundwater pumping and recharge lag variables were the most influential factors controlling groundwater level variations. In addition, residual analysis and spatial error mapping were conducted to evaluate model uncertainty, while SHAP analysis was used to interpret the influence of input variables on groundwater level predictions. This study provides new insights into the dynamics of confined aquifer systems using machine-learning techniques and offers valuable information to support sustainable groundwater management in the study area.

Introduction

Groundwater is a fundamental natural resource that plays a critical role in economic and social development. It is widely used for domestic consumption, agriculture, and industrial activities. In many parts of the world, groundwater resources are increasingly under pressure due to population growth, rapid urbanization, and the impacts of climate change, which have led to a continuous rise in groundwater demand.

The Lower Chao Phraya Basin (LCPB), located in the central region of Thailand, is one of the most economically important areas in the country. The basin covers several provinces, including Chainat, Sing Buri, Nakhon Nayok, Ang Thong, Suphan Buri, Phra Nakhon Si Ayutthaya, Pathum Thani, Nakhon Pathom, Nonthaburi, Samut Sakhon, Samut Prakan, Samut Songkhram, and Bangkok, as well as parts of Kanchanaburi, Nakhon Sawan, Ratchaburi, Prachinburi, Chachoengsao, Lopburi, Saraburi, and Uthai Thani. The basin contains high-potential groundwater aquifers composed mainly of gravel and sand layers. Eight major aquifer systems are present in the basin, including the Bangkok Aquifer (0–50 m depth), Phra Pradaeng Aquifer (50–100 m), Nakhon Luang Aquifer (100–150 m), Nonthaburi Aquifer (150–200 m), Sam Khok Aquifer (200–300 m), Phayathai Aquifer (300–350 m), Thonburi Aquifer (350–450 m), and Pak Nam Aquifer (450–550 m). , Currently, groundwater is primarily extracted from depths of approximately 150–300 m, with the Nakhon Luang aquifer being one of the most productive aquifers due to its high yield (100–300 m3/h) and relatively good water quality. As the basin includes major economic and industrial centers of Thailand, groundwater demand in this region has increased significantly over the past several decades.

Historically, intensive groundwater extraction in Bangkok and surrounding areas led to significant groundwater level decline and land subsidence. , Studies conducted during 1978–1982 reported rapid decreases in groundwater levels due to excessive pumping. In response, the Department of Groundwater Resources (DGR) designated Bangkok and nearby provinces as groundwater control areas and implemented regulatory measures to control groundwater extraction. Observed wells indicate that groundwater levels in the Nakhon Luang aquifer declined by approximately 38 m in central Bangkok and up to 60 m in eastern suburban areas during the period 1959–1982. After groundwater control policies were introduced in 1985, groundwater levels gradually recovered in some areas. Currently, the DGR maintains an extensive monitoring network of observation wells to track groundwater levels and groundwater quality across the basin. Recent observations indicate that groundwater levels in several aquifers, including the Bangkok, Phra Pradaeng, Nakhon Luang, and Nonthaburi aquifers, are rising, while some deeper aquifers continue to experience declining levels.

Traditionally, groundwater management in this region has relied on numerical groundwater models to estimate safe yield and groundwater balance. These models require relatively high-quality of hydrogeological parameters and often involve complex calibration procedures. For example, a study conducted by Chulalongkorn University during 1999–2000 estimated that groundwater recharge in Bangkok and surrounding areas was approximately 1.25 MCM per day, and groundwater levels were managed at a controlled depth of approximately 30 m. Although such approaches provide valuable insights into groundwater system behavior, they are often time-consuming and require large amounts of hydrogeological data.

In recent years, machine learning (ML) techniques have been increasingly applied in hydrological and hydrogeological studies, particularly in groundwater research, with a notable rise in applications after 2019 as environmental data sets have become more widely available and data-driven modeling approaches have advanced. Compared with traditional numerical groundwater models, ML approaches can efficiently analyze large data sets and identify hidden patterns without requiring detailed assumptions about physical processes. Various ML algorithms, including random forest (RF), artificial neural networks (ANNs), support vector machines (SVMs), and deep learning models, have been widely used to predict groundwater levels and analyze groundwater system dynamics. ,

Among these techniques, RF has received considerable attention in environmental modeling because it combines multiple decision trees to improve the prediction accuracy and reduce overfitting. This method is particularly effective for handling complex environmental data sets with nonlinear relationships among variables. , Several studies have demonstrated that RF models can achieve strong predictive performance in groundwater level forecasting and water resource management applications. ,

Despite the growing use of machine learning in groundwater studies, the application of ML techniques to investigate groundwater-level dynamics in LCPBs remains limited. Many previous studies have focused on short-term data sets or have been conducted in semiarid regions outside Southeast Asia. Furthermore, limited research has investigated groundwater dynamics in confined aquifer systems using long-term hydrogeological data sets that integrate both spatial and temporal variables. Therefore, applying machine learning techniques to long-term groundwater data sets in this region provides an opportunity to improve predictive capability while also enhancing understanding of groundwater system behavior.

Therefore, this study aims to investigate groundwater level dynamics in the Nakhon Luang aquifer of LCPB using a machine learning approach. Specifically, the objectives of this study are (1) to develop an RF regression model for predicting groundwater levels using long-term spatial and temporal data sets, including groundwater pumping, rainfall, recharge, elevation, and geological information; (2) to evaluate the predictive performance and robustness of the model using statistical indicators such as R 2, RMSE, and MAE; and (3) to interpret the relative importance of controlling factors influencing groundwater level variations using SHAP analysis. The findings of this study provide new insights into groundwater system dynamics and offer a data-driven framework that can support sustainable groundwater resource management in the study area.

Data Collection

Study Area and Description

The study area (Figure ) focuses on the Nakhon Luang aquifer of the Lower Chao Phraya basin, Thailand, covering the areas of Chainat, Sing Buri, Nakhon Nayok, Ang Thong, Suphan Buri, Phra Nakhon Si Ayutthaya, Pathum Thani, Nakhon Pathom, Nonthaburi, Samut Sakhon, Samut Prakan, Samut Songkhram and Bangkok, as well as parts of Kanchanaburi, Nakhon Sawan, Ratchaburi, Prachinburi, Chachoengsao, Lopburi, Saraburi, and Uthai Thani. The area is approximately 43317 km2, with an elevation ranging from 0 to 1875 m above mean sea level (m AMSL), located between latitudes 99° 04′39″ to 101°36′39″ E and longitudes 13°17′02″ to 16°00′44″ N. The Lower Chao Phraya Basin is located in the central region of Thailand. The central part of the basin is dominated by plains, while gently undulating terrain occurs around the basin margins. Steep mountainous terrain is located along the western and eastern boundaries. The western region extends down to the Ratchaburi Province from Nakhon Sawan, Uthai Thani, and Kanchanaburi Provinces. The eastern region extends from Lopburi, Saraburi, and Nakhon Nayok Provinces down to Prachinburi Province. There are 5 rivers in the area, including the Chao Phraya River, Tha Chin River, Mae Klong River, Pa Sak River, and Bang Pakong River. The flow direction of the rivers is from the north heading into the Gulf of Thailand. These rivers have continuously transported sediments derived from erosion and weathering, resulting in the long term accumulation of deposits that formed the extensive alluvial plains of the basin. Consequently, the geology of the study area is predominantly covered by Quaternary sediments (Figure ). The major surficial deposits include alluvium (Qa), consisting of gravel, sand, silt, and clay deposited along the major river systems. In the central part of the basin, the Bangkok Clay Formation extensively overlies the surface, extending from the Gulf of Thailand northward to parts of Ang Thong Province. This marine clay was deposited under tidal influences and is characterized by soft gray, brownish-gray, to dark gray clay, with a thickness reaching approximately 30 m. In addition, alluvial fan deposits occur along the western foothills of Kanchanaburi Province. The groundwater system of the study area is hosted within thick gravel and sand aquifers. According to the Japan International Cooperation Agency (JICA) in 1995, the groundwater system comprises eight principal aquifer units , (Figure ). These aquifers consist of laterally extensive gravel and sand layers trending predominantly in the north–south direction and are separated by clay layers. The Nakhon Luang aquifer is located at depths of approximately 100–150 m, underlying the Phra Pradaeng aquifer and overlying the Nonthaburi aquifer. At present, it is the most intensively exploited aquifer in the basin because of its high groundwater yield and good water quality.

Data Sources and Input Parameters

This study utilizes secondary data collected from multiple governmental agencies to characterize hydrogeological conditions, groundwater dynamics, and climatic influences within the study area, as shown in Figure . These data sets were compiled to support data analysis, model development, and interpretation of groundwater level variations in the Nakhon Luang aquifer.

Location of groundwater observation wells, groundwater pumping wells, and rainfall stations in the Nakhon Luang aquifer within the Lower Chao Phraya Basin.

The model input parameters include groundwater level data obtained from the DGR, collected from 234 groundwater observation wells distributed across the study area. Groundwater pumping data were obtained from both governmental and private sectors, comprising a total of 7468 pumping wells, as provided by the DGR. Rainfall data from 34 monitoring stations were obtained from the Meteorological Department (TMD) and Royal Irrigation Department (RID). Groundwater recharge was estimated based on the relationship between rainfall and area-specific infiltration ratios. Ground surface elevation data were derived from the Royal Thai Survey Department (RTSD). Geological data were obtained from the Department of Mineral Resources (DMR), while hydrogeological data were obtained from the DGR.

Before model development, all data sets were harmonized both temporally and spatially to ensure consistency among the different data sources. Groundwater level, rainfall, groundwater recharge, and groundwater pumping data were standardized to a monthly temporal resolution, covering the period from 1978 to 2023. Subsequently, all data sets, including both dynamic variables (rainfall, groundwater recharge, and groundwater pumping) and static variables (elevation, geology, and hydrogeology), were spatially processed, and the corresponding values were extracted at the groundwater observation well locations. This process generated a unified tabular data set for subsequent machine-learning analysis.

Machine Learning Model and Tools

Python is widely used in data science and machine learning due to its simplicity and extensive libraries, such as Scikit-learn, Pandas, NumPy, and Matplotlib. In this study, the machine-learning model was developed using Python in the Visual Studio Code environment. The model was trained using a selected data set that included groundwater level, rainfall, groundwater recharge, groundwater pumping, geological data, and hydrogeological data in the study area to predict groundwater levels.

In this study, a supervised machine-learning algorithm was applied to model the relationship between input and output variables for groundwater level prediction. Supervised learning algorithms can learn patterns from labeled data sets and can be applied to time-series forecasting problems when appropriate input features are provided. The selected algorithm in this study was the RF model, which was implemented using the Scikit-learn machine learning library in Python.

Random forest is an ensemble learning method that constructs multiple decision trees using bootstrap sampling and random feature selection and combines their predictions to improve model accuracy and generalization performance. The final prediction of the RF model is typically obtained by averaging the outputs of individual trees in regression tasks.

The RF model is well-known for its ability to model complex nonlinear relationships and capture interactions among predictor variables without requiring explicit model specifications. In addition, RF performs well when handling large data sets and multidimensional environmental data, including spatial and temporal variables, making it suitable for applications involving complex environmental and geospatial processes. ,

The collected data were processed through data preprocessing steps including data cleansing, data reduction, and data transformation. During the data cleansing process, all data sets were inspected for missing values, duplicate records, invalid values, and temporal consistency. Date formats were standardized to ensure temporal consistency across all data sets, while extreme values were retained because they may represent actual hydrogeological conditions. Data reduction was performed by removing variables that are not essential to model development, such as location data and the name of the owner of the pumping station. Data transformation included the derivation of groundwater recharge from rainfall by using area-specific infiltration ratios. These preprocessing procedures were conducted to improve data quality, ensure data set consistency, and prepare the data for machine learning analysis.

Missing temporal observations were first completed using linear interpolation based on adjacent monthly values to ensure a continuous time series. After completing the temporal records, spatial interpolation using the Inverse Distance Weighting (IDW) method was applied to estimate spatial variability between monitoring locations. The IDW method estimates unknown values based on the distance-weighted influence of surrounding observations, where nearby points exert greater influence than distant ones. This technique has been widely applied in environmental and hydrological studies to estimate spatially distributed variables. − The interpolated spatial data sets were then extracted at the groundwater observation wells to obtain corresponding values of rainfall, recharge, and pumping for each well location and time step. This process allowed the integration of spatially distributed environmental variables with groundwater level observations for subsequent machine learning modeling.

Furthermore, lag time features were incorporated for selected variables, particularly groundwater recharge and groundwater pumping, to capture the delayed response of groundwater systems to hydrological processes. In natural aquifer systems, the effects of rainfall infiltration and recharge do not occur instantaneously but may influence groundwater levels after several weeks or months. Therefore, historical values of these variables were included as lagged explanatory features to represent the temporal dependencies between past conditions and current groundwater levels. The use of lag time variables is commonly applied in hydrological and groundwater modeling to improve predictive performance and better represent delayed aquifer responses. ,, Similar approaches have also been implemented in machine learning models, including RF, where lagged predictors are introduced to incorporate temporal information that is not explicitly modeled by the algorithm.

In addition, some machine-learning models, such as RF or regression models, cannot directly process categorical features. Therefore, one-hot encoding was applied to the geology and hydrogeology data sets. This technique converts categorical variables into binary numerical variables (0/1), allowing machine learning algorithms to properly interpret and utilize these features during model training. ,

Model Training and Evaluation

The model training process is important in developing machine learning models to learn from data sets to improve model parameters. During learning, optimization is performed to optimize the model’s hyperparameters by finding the parameter values that make the model predict the outcome more accurately.

The data set was organized in CSV format and consisted of 45 874 rows. The data were randomly divided into training and testing subsets with a ratio of 70:30. The model was trained using the training data set and subsequently validated using an independent testing data set that had not been previously seen by the model. All computational procedures were implemented using the Python programming language and employing the scikit-learn library, which is an open-source Python framework that provides efficient and user-friendly tools for data mining and machine learning.

During model development, two data-splitting strategies were examined: a random split and a time-based split. To improve model performance, hyperparameter tuning was conducted to identify the optimal parameter configurations. In this study, hyperparameter optimization was performed using the Optuna framework, which is designed to efficiently search for optimal parameter combinations through automated optimization strategies. The K-fold cross-validation technique was applied in conjunction with the tuning process to enhance the robustness and stability of the model, reduce bias, and minimize the risk of overfitting. , In the random forest model, the training process required specifying the number of decision trees as well as the number of variables considered at each split, as these parameters influence model stability and predictive accuracy. In addition, lag-time features were incorporated for selected input variables to allow the model to capture delayed responses inherent in natural processes, particularly those affecting groundwater system dynamics within the study area.

Model performance was evaluated using standard statistical indices, including the coefficient of determination (R 2), root-mean-square error (RMSE), and mean absolute error (MAE), which are widely used to assess the predictive accuracy of machine learning and environmental models. ,, 1 R2=1−1−∑(Yi−Ŷi)21−∑(Yi−Y̅i)2 2 RMSE=1n∑(Yi−Ŷi)2 3 MAE=1n∑|Yi−Ŷi| In addition, scatter plots were used to compare observed and predicted groundwater levels, and SHAP analysis was applied to interpret the influence of individual variables on model predictions.

SHAP Analysis

SHAP (Shapley Additive exPlanations) is a technique used to interpret the predictions of machine learning models based on the concept of Shapley values derived from game theory. It explains the contribution of each feature to the model output by fairly distributing the prediction among the input variables. SHAP provides both the magnitude and direction of each feature’s influence on prediction and can be used to interpret model behavior at both global and individual prediction levels. ,

To improve model interpretability, SHAP analysis was applied to quantify the contribution of each predictor variable to the model output and to facilitate the interpretation of the trained machine learning model.

Preliminary results indicated that the random-split approach yielded higher R 2 values than the time-based split. However, the initial model performance was still moderate. This difference can be attributed to the fact that RF is not inherently a time series model and does not account for temporal dependencies unless lag features are explicitly included. In a random split, the training and testing data sets share similar data distributions, leading to higher predictive performance. Therefore, a 12 month recharge lag variable was introduced to better capture the physical time delay processes in groundwater response. Hyperparameter optimization was subsequently performed using Optuna, combined with K-fold cross-validation (K = 3). The data set was randomly partitioned into three subsets. In each iteration, two subsets were used for model training while the remaining subset was used for validation. This process was repeated three times so that each subset served as the validation set once.

The optimal hyperparameters of the RF model were determined through a tuning process to improve the predictive performance and model stability. The number of trees in the ensemble (n_estimators) was set to 764 to ensure sufficient model diversity and to reduce variance in the predictions. The maximum depth of each decision tree (max_depth) was limited to 30 to prevent excessive tree growth and reduce the risk of overfitting. In addition, the max_features parameter was set to none, allowing the model to consider all available predictor variables when determining the best split at each node. These parameter settings help balance model complexity and predictive accuracy, which are important for modeling complex environmental systems with nonlinear relationships among variables. ,

The trained model achieved an R 2 of 0.91 on the training data set, indicating that the model effectively captured the relationships among the input variables. When evaluated on the independent test data set, which was not used during training, the model achieved an R 2 of 0.85. The relatively small difference between training and testing performances suggests good generalization capability and indicates no significant evidence of overfitting. The performance of the developed RF model is also comparable to that reported in the previous groundwater prediction studies. Bilali et al. in 2021 reported R 2 values ranging between 0.82 and 0.89 for RF and artificial neural network (ANN) models applied to groundwater level forecasting. Similarly, Yonekura et al. in 2025 demonstrated that machine learning models, including RF, can effectively predict groundwater level dynamics using meteorological and hydrological variables. These findings indicate that the predictive accuracy obtained in this study is consistent with previous machine learning applications in hydrogeology.

Furthermore, cross-validation results (Table ) demonstrated improved model stability and performance. The average validation performance (mean ± standard deviation) was R 2 = 0.833 ± 0.018, indicating consistent predictive capability across different folds and low variability in model performance. The relatively small standard deviation suggests that the model predictions remained stable across the validation subsets. The strong predictive performance of the Random Forest model observed in this study is consistent with previous findings reported in recent reviews of machine learning applications in groundwater modeling. ,

Higher prediction errors are mainly concentrated in the lower part of the basin, which correspond to major urban and industrial areas. These larger errors may be associated with highly variable groundwater pumping caused by industrial activities, factories, and large commercial buildings with diverse water use patterns. Another important factor contributing to higher prediction errors is the data limitation. Wells exhibiting errors of >10 m generally contain incomplete or discontinuous records. In several cases, these wells are relatively old, damaged, or abandoned, resulting in insufficient observations for a reliable model training and evaluation. Such data scarcity can reduce the ability of the model to accurately capture groundwater-level variations at those locations.

Feature Importance

The SHAP feature importance results are shown in Figure and demonstrate that groundwater pumping is the most influential predictor of groundwater level variations. It exhibited the highest mean absolute SHAP value among all predictor variables, demonstrating a strong contribution to predicted groundwater levels. This indicates that anthropogenic groundwater abstraction plays a dominant role in controlling the groundwater dynamics in the Nakhon Luang aquifer. The average magnitude of influence, represented by the mean absolute SHAP value, was the highest for groundwater pumping (mean |SHAP| = 5.33) among all predictor variables, emphasizing the significant role of human groundwater extraction in shaping groundwater dynamics. Similar findings were reported by Bilali et al. in 2021, who demonstrated that anthropogenic pumping strongly controls groundwater fluctuations in aquifer systems. The dominance of groundwater pumping identified in this study highlights the important role of anthropogenic activities in influencing groundwater-level variations across the study area. This interpretation is consistent with long-term observations in the Bangkok metropolitan region, where intensive groundwater pumping has historically been associated with groundwater-level decline and land subsidence. Secondary influencing factors included elevation and geological conditions. This suggests that spatial variations in the physical characteristics of the aquifer system also contribute to the groundwater level variations across the study area. In addition, recharge lag variables, especially lags 4 to 8, had a greater influence than the current recharge variable. This supports the idea that groundwater systems do not respond immediately to recharge but instead show a delayed reaction over time, suggesting a delayed aquifer response to recharge processes. This behavior has been widely documented in confined aquifer systems where groundwater response occurs several months after rainfall events. Furthermore, groundwater recharge represents the fraction of precipitation that effectively infiltrates into the subsurface and replenishes the aquifer, making it a more physically meaningful indicator of groundwater replenishment than rainfall alone. Although recharge exhibited greater importance than rainfall in the SHAP analysis, recharge was estimated from rainfall using area-specific infiltration ratios. Therefore, recharge inherently contains rainfall information, and the two variables are not independent predictors. Nevertheless, groundwater recharge remains an important variable because it represents rainfall that infiltrates into the groundwater aquifer.

Model Limitations and Uncertainty

Although the RF model achieved high predictive accuracy, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, RF is not inherently a time-series algorithm and does not explicitly account for temporal dependencies unless lag features are incorporated. However, RF has been widely applied in groundwater studies because of its ability to model complex nonlinear relationships, handle large data sets, and maintain strong predictive performance without strict assumptions about data distribution. ,, In this study, lag-time variables were introduced to partially capture delayed aquifer responses and temporal effects within the groundwater system. Second, groundwater recharge was estimated indirectly from rainfall data, which may introduce uncertainty because of simplifications in the recharge estimation process. Despite these limitations, the model demonstrates a strong predictive capability and provides valuable insights into groundwater system dynamics. Future studies may explore advanced time-series algorithms, such as long short-term memory (LSTM) networks, which are designed to capture sequential temporal dependencies more explicitly, to further improve predictive performance.

Conclusion

This study employed a Random Forest (RF) machine learning model to investigate groundwater level dynamics in the Nakhon Luang aquifer of the Lower Chao Phraya Basin. The model was developed using long-term hydrogeological, meteorological, groundwater pumping, geological, and groundwater recharge data sets covering the period from 1978 to 2023. The incorporation of lag-time recharge features improved the model’s predictive performance and helped represent the delayed response of the groundwater system to recharge processes.

The optimized model achieved strong predictive accuracy and showed low variability during cross-validation, indicating a good generalization capability and stable model performance. Groundwater pumping and recharge variability are the dominant factors controlling groundwater level changes, while the elevation and geological conditions contribute to spatial variability within the aquifer system.

These findings demonstrate that machine learning techniques can effectively capture the complex and nonlinear behavior of groundwater systems. The developed modeling framework provides useful insights into groundwater management and can support sustainable groundwater utilization in the Lower Chao Phraya Basin. Furthermore, the approach can be applied to other regions with similar hydrogeological conditions. Future research may explore advanced time-series models and scenario simulations to further improve groundwater prediction and assess the potential impacts of climate variability and groundwater pumping on aquifer systems. The integration of spatial error analysis and SHAP-based interpretation also improved our understanding of model reliability and the relative importance of hydrogeological and anthropogenic factors controlling groundwater dynamics.
