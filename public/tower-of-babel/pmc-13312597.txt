Validation of the short-form quality care questionnaire – palliative care in Brazilian patients with heart failure eligible for palliative care
Authors: José Kléber de Figueiredo, Aldair Darlan Santos-de-Araújo, Patrícia Martins Santos, Mariana Campos Maia, Victória Pereira Frutuoso, Daniel Santos Rocha, Renan Shida Marinho, Ellen de Souza Pereira, Audrey Borghi-Silva, Paulo Ícaro Paixão Silva, Almir Vieira Dibai-Filho, Daniela Bassi-Dibai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Brazilian version of the Quality Care Questionnaire – Palliative Care (QCQ-PC) is an instrument developed to assess the quality of care provided in palliative care.ObjectiveTo structurally validate the Brazilian version of the QCQ-PC for use in individuals with HF eligible for palliative care.MethodologyThis study aimed to structurally validate the QCQ-PC, following the Consensus-Based Standards for the Selection of Health Measurement Instruments (COSMIN). Data were collected using the QCQ-PC, 36-Item Short Form Health Survey Instrument (SF-36), and Minnesota Living with Heart Failure Questionnaire (MLHFQ). Internal consistency was calculated using Cronbach’s alpha and, structural validity was assessed using confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). Construct validity was assessed by correlations between QCQ-PC and SF-36.ResultsThe study sample (n = 113) consisted predominantly of middle-aged to older adults mainly classified as NYHA class I (36.3%) or III (32.7%). The structural validity of the two-domain of the QCQ-PC was confirmed by adequate fit indices: χ²/df = 1.46; CFI = 0.979; TLI = 0.971; RMSEA = 0.064; SRMR = 0.067; and with factor loadings ≥ 0.30. Construct validity was demonstrated by correlations of less than 0.30 between the QCQ-PC and SF-36 domains. Reliability was adequate, with ICC values ​​above 0.80 for both domains, measurement error less than 10%, and Cronbach’s alpha greater than 0.70.ConclusionThe internal structure with two domains is the most suitable for use in individuals with FH eligible for palliative care through the QCQ-PC. The constructs are valid and reliable, with adequate internal consistency.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12904-026-02131-y.

Introduction

The multifaceted and complex nature of HF results from a range of risk factors associated with cardiovascular dysfunction, unhealthy lifestyle habits, and poor disease management [1, 2]. Its dynamic character, marked by heterogeneous clinical trajectories, diverse symptomatology, and varied therapeutic response, poses significant challenges to risk stratification, care planning, and the definition of individualized, long-term treatment strategies [3–5].

Many patients also experience non-cardiac manifestations, such as chronic pain, excessive sleepiness, and high levels of physical, emotional, and spiritual distress, which are often neglected or underestimated [6–8]. In this context, palliative care emerges as an effective strategy, not limited to the final stages of the disease, but integrated from the early phases of follow-up. Its main objective is to promote holistic care that complements the benefits of pharmacological therapies, focusing on symptom relief, emotional and spiritual support, and improvement of the patient’s quality of life [9–13].

However, patients’ limited understanding of their prognosis and their need for clearer information about their condition and future perspectives reveal significant gaps in care, reflecting poor communication characterized by insufficient dialogue, lack of transparency, and the absence of a trusting relationship to support informed decision-making [9, 14]. In the context of palliative care, the assessment of care should not be limited to the identification of clinical outcomes or unmet care needs, understood as situations in which the necessary care is not fully provided, such as insufficient information, inadequate symptom control, or lack of emotional support [9, 10, 14–18]. Although identifying these needs helps to recognize specific gaps in care, it does not fully capture how care is experienced by patients. In this sense, quality of care refers to how care is delivered in practice, particularly regarding communication, support provided, and patient involvement in decision-making [10, 15, 16]. This distinction carries important clinical implications, as the identification of unmet care needs and the assessment of quality of care address different dimensions of care and may contribute in a complementary way to understanding the patient experience and improving care practices [10, 15, 16].

In light of these aspects, the Quality Care Questionnaire – Palliative Care (QCQ-PC) was developed and previously validated with the aim of assessing the quality of care in palliative contexts more comprehensively [19]. The QCQ-PC was developed in South Korea with 32 items distributed across 4 domains [19]. However, due to its length and multidimensional structure, the original version may require longer administration time and greater cognitive effort from respondents, which can be particularly challenging in patients with advanced disease and limited tolerance for lengthy assessments [19]. The initial validation of the QCQ-PC for Brazil identified problematic internal structure (with inadequate fit indices and very high correlations between domains) using confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). Therefore, the authors used exploratory factor analysis with parallel analysis and identified two domains comprising 12 items [20]. This shorter version presents positive characteristics compared to the long version, such as being faster to complete, lower chance of completion errors, and lower probability of missing items [20].

Despite these advances, this instrument has not yet been validated for patients with HF, which limits its direct applicability in clinical contexts specific to this condition. Therefore, we aim to validate the short-form QCQ-PC in Brazilian patients with HF, under the hypothesis that the questionnaire will demonstrate adequate psychometric properties and will be able to capture essential, yet often neglected, aspects of quality of care.

Methodology

Study design

This was a questionnaire validation study conducted in accordance with the Consensus-based Standards for the Selection of Health Measurement Instruments (COSMIN) guidelines [21]. The short-form QCQ-PC had been previously translated, cross-culturally adapted, and validated for oncology [19, 20] and diabetics patients [22]. The research was carried out at the Center for Medium and High Complexity Exams (PAM Diamante) and at Primary, Secondary, and Tertiary Health Care Units located in the city of São Luís (Maranhão), Brazil, all of which are public health services. Data were also collected at the Laboratory of Cardiopulmonary Physiotherapy (LACAP), São Carlos (São Paulo), Brazil. The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Ceuma University under protocol number 6.159.628. All volunteers validated their participation by signing the informed consent form and the study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Participants

The sample size was estimated based on the COSMIN recommendations [21], which advised that the minimum number of participants should be at least seven times the number of items in the questionnaire, with an absolute minimum of 100 individuals. The study included men and women aged 40 years or older with a confirmed clinical diagnosis of chronic HF, regardless of ejection fraction or functional class, persistence of symptoms that have not improved satisfactorily after optimized treatment of the underlying condition, according to clinical information and who had been followed for at least six months in public healthcare services, either in outpatient or hospital settings.

Participants were also required to be eligible for palliative care according to the criteria established by the Supportive and Palliative Care Indicators Tool (SPICT-BR™) [23, 24] to Brazilian population, considering general and specific health indicators.

Participants were required to have adequate clinical and cognitive conditions to understand and respond to the research instruments. Individuals diagnosed with severe psychiatric disorders and/or significant cognitive impairments that prevented understanding or full completion of the questionnaire were excluded. Furthermore, it was considered essential that only participants capable of providing free and informed consent were included; therefore, refusal or inability to provide informed consent also constituted exclusion criteria.

Clinical and sociodemographic characteristics and anthropometric measures

Data regarding hospitalized patients were obtained from their medical records, including information on clinical diagnoses, comorbidities, age (years), sex, weight in kilograms (kg), heigh in meter (m), body mass index (kg/m²), smoking status, and marital status (single, married, divorced, and widowed). For outpatients, clinical and sociodemographic information was collected by the researchers through interviews using a previously designed assessment form. Anthropometric measurements were obtained using the InBody 120 bioelectrical impedance analyzer and a stadiometer. Body weight (kg), height (m), and body mass index (kg/m²) were recorded. This approach allowed for a comprehensive assessment of both clinical history and physical status across different care settings.

Functional classification – New York Heart Association

The New York Heart Association (NYHA) Functional Classification is a tool used to assess the impact of heart failure on functional capacity. It is divided into four classes: Class I – no limitation of physical activity; Class II – slight limitation; comfortable at rest, but ordinary activity results in fatigue, palpitation, or dyspnea. Class III – marked limitation; comfortable at rest, but less-than-ordinary activity causes symptoms. Class IV – unable to carry out any physical activity without discomfort; symptoms may be present even at rest [25].

Minnesota Living with Heart Failure Questionnaire (MLHFQ)

The MLHFQ was developed specifically for heart failure, making it more suitable for this type of patient, and consists of 21 questions related to limitations that are frequently associated with how much heart failure prevents patients from living as they would like [26, 27]. The last month should be considered when answering the questions. The response scale for each question ranges from 0 (no) to 5 (too much), where 0 represents no limitations and 5 represents maximum limitation. These questions involve a physical dimension (1 to 7, 12 and 13) that are highly interrelated with dyspnea and fatigue, an emotional dimension (17 to 21), and other questions (numbers 8, 9, 10, 11, 14, 15 and 16) which, added to the previous dimensions, form the total score .

Transthoracic echocardiogram

The transthoracic echocardiogram was performed by a cardiologist using an ultrasound device equipped with a 3 MHz transducer (Phillips, HD11 XE, Bothell, Washington, United States), in accordance with established recommendations [28]. The left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF%) was calculated using the Simpson biplane method.

Short-form Quality Care Questionnaire–Palliative Care (QCQ-PC)

The Brazilian version of the short-form QCQ-PC, translated, adapted, and validated for cancer patients in palliative care, was used [20]. This instrument consisted of two domains (“Communication with healthcare professionals” and “Care and assistance provided by healthcare professionals”) and 12 items with 4 response options (“strongly agree” = 4; “agree” = 3; “disagree” = 2; “strongly disagree” = 1). The total score ranged from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating better perceived quality of care. The following equation was used to calculate the score for each domain: the score of all items in the domain was added, 6 was subtracted from the value, and it was divided by 0.18. The brazilianshort-term QCQ-PC questionnaire is available as Supplementary Material in both English and Portuguese versions.

36-item short form health survey instrument

To assess quality of life, a 36-item instrument was used, encompassing eight domains: physical functioning, role limitations due to physical health, role limitations due to emotional problems, energy/fatigue, mental health, social functioning, pain, general health. Each domain produced a score ranging from 0 to 100, where 0 indicated the poorest health status and 100 represented the best possible health status. This instrument has undergone cross-cultural adaptation and validation for use in Brazil [29].

Statistical analyses

The variables were described as mean and standard deviation or as absolute values and percentages, when appropriate. To examine the correlations between instruments, data normality was first assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, followed by the use of either Spearman correlation coefficients to determine the strength of the correlations [30]. The interpretation of correlation magnitude followed these criteria: correlations with instruments measuring similar constructs were expected to be ≥ 0.50; correlations with instruments measuring related but different constructs were expected to range from 0.30 to 0.50; and correlations with instruments measuring unrelated constructs were expected to be < 0.30. The hypothesis of this study was that the QCQ-PC score would correlate with a magnitude < 0.30 with the 36-item short form health survey instrument.

Internal consistency was calculated using Cronbach’s alpha, with values above 0.70 considered acceptable [31]. Reliability was assessed through a test-retest model, with a 7-day interval between assessments. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC2,1), 95% confidence interval (CI) for the ICC, standard error of measurement (SEM), and minimal detectable difference (MDD) were used. An ICC value greater than 0.75 was considered acceptable [32]. A minimum subsample of 30 participants was used for the analysis of reliability and internal consistency. Internal consistency, reliability, and correlations were processed using the statistical software SPSS, version 17.0 (Chicago, IL, USA), and a significance level of 5% was adopted.

For the structural validity of the questionnaires, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was performed using R Studio software (Boston, MA, USA), with the lavaan and semPlot packages. The CFA was conducted using a polychoric correlation matrix and the robust diagonally weighted least squares extraction method. Model fit was assessed using the following indices: root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) with 90% confidence interval, comparative fit index (CFI), Tucker-Lewis index (TLI), standardized root mean square residual (SRMR), and chi-square/degrees of freedom (χ²/df). In the present study, values greater than 0.90 were considered acceptable for CFI and TLI, and values less than 0.08 were considered acceptable for RMSEA and SRMR, χ²/df values below 3.00 were considered indicative of a good model fit [33].

Results

Sample characterization

The main sociodemographic, anthropometric, and clinical characteristics of the participants (n = 113) are presented in Table 1. The study sample consisted predominantly of middle-aged to older adults, with an average age in the early 60s and a body mass index within the overweight range. Both sexes were represented, with a slightly higher proportion of males. The majority of participants were categorized as NYHA class I (36.30%) or III (32.70%), reflecting either no limitation or moderate limitation and moderate quality of life according to the MLHFQ score (37.93 ± 21.39). The LVEF% showed a mean value of 38.39 ± 13.98. Regarding lifestyle factors, over half of the sample reported never having smoked (54.00%), while a substantial portion were former smokers (44.20%); current smokers comprised a small minority (1.80%). Physical inactivity was prevalent, with approximately two-thirds of participants not engaging in regular physical activity (68.10%). Concerning clinical history, a slight majority had experienced recent hospitalization (51.30%). Structural validity

We tested the structure of the QCQ-PC with two domains and identified adequate fit indices: chi-square/df = 1.46; CFI = 0.979; TLI = 0.971; RMSEA (90% CI) = 0.064 (0.027 to 0.095); SRMR = 0.067. Furthermore, as shown in Fig. 1, the factor loadings were adequate, with values ≥ 0.30.

Construct validity

Regarding construct validity, as shown in Table 3, the hypothesis of this study was confirmed by identifying correlation magnitudes between the QCQ-PC domains and the SF-36 domains below 0.30 (since these are unrelated domains). Thus, we highlight the highest correlation magnitudes found: emotional role (rho = 0.265), vitality (rho = 0.226), mental health (rho = 0.292), bodily pain (rho = 0.221), and general health (rho = 0.377). Therefore, the higher the quality of life, the higher the perceived quality of care.

Floor and ceiling effects

No participant reached the minimum score in either domain. For the domain of communication with healthcare professionals, 3 (5.8%) patients reached the maximum score (100); for the domain of care and assistance provided by healthcare professionals, 1 (1.9%) patient reached the maximum score (100). Thus, no floor or ceiling effects were identified for the QCQ-PC domains.

Discussion

In the context of HF, the QCQ-PC proved to be a psychometrically robust instrument, with evidence of reliability, internal consistency, and construct validity. Moreover, it was well understood by patients, with no floor or ceiling effects observed. Additionally, this is the first Brazilian study to validate a specific questionnaire for assessing the quality of care provided to patients with heart failure.

The experience that HF imposes on affected individuals represents a significant burden, generating physical, emotional, and social suffering that is often overlooked in clinical assessments [34, 35]. Symptoms such as extreme fatigue, dyspnea, and edema are frequently accompanied by feelings of distress, fear, insecurity, and loss of autonomy [36]. However, these subjective dimensions of illness tend to be neglected, largely due to communication failures between healthcare professionals and patients. The lack of active listening and dialogue centered on the individual’s needs and perceptions compromises the comprehensiveness of care, limiting the understanding of the real impact of the disease on daily life. This communication gap not only hinders the identification of relevant aspects for therapeutic planning but also contributes to unrecognized and inadequately managed suffering [37].

In this context, when dealing with chronic and progressive conditions such as HF, the need for an approach that goes beyond clinical symptom control becomes even more evident. In recent years, there has been a significant increase in evidence highlighting the complexity of the suffering experienced by these patients, often characterized by non-cardiac symptoms that are difficult to manage and frequently overlooked [38, 39]. In response to this reality, international consensuses have been developed to guide the early integration of palliative care for this population, emphasizing the importance of effective communication, active listening, and shared decision-making regarding treatment goals [13, 40].

It is common for patients with heart failure to have limited understanding of their prognosis, which may lead them to overestimate their survival and neglect treatment due to a lack of awareness about the disease and its clinical course [41]. The combination of limited disease understanding by the patient and ineffective communication with healthcare professionals has been identified as a significant barrier to adherence to the proposed treatment [42, 43]. Given this concern, several studies have been conducted to investigate the impact of the patient–healthcare professional relationship, showing that this interaction has a significant influence on medication adherence in this population [44]. In patients with advanced heart failure, for example, by prioritizing their preferences and promoting communication about prognosis, these preferences have become a guide for healthcare professionals to appropriately plan and initiate a treatment aligned with the patients’ expectations and needs [45].

Previous results have shown that, in addition to being a reliable measure with acceptable psychometric properties, the QCQ-PC can be a valid option for assessing patient satisfaction with the quality of care among Brazilian cancer patients [20]. Similar findings have also been observed in individuals with type 2 diabetes mellitus [22]. Interestingly, it was observed that hospitalized patients with heart failure (HF) who received palliative care during their hospital stay had lower hospital readmission rates and a reduced need for mechanical ventilation [46].

The integration of findings on unmet needs is fundamental, as the QCQ-PC was structured to capture essential dimensions of the patient experience, such as the need for transparent communication and holistic support, which are intrinsically aligned with the concept of unmet care needs. By addressing frequently neglected clinical gaps and non-cardiac manifestations [47], the instrument reinforces its construct validity and clinical relevance. This approach allows for a deeper interpretation of the results by highlighting that communication failures and a lack of active listening contribute to unrecognized suffering [48, 49], demonstrating the real impact of the disease and the applicability of the QCQ-PC to identify and mitigate these deficiencies in care.

Clinical impact

Considering the nature of the questions addressed in the questionnaire, which essentially aims to assess the patient’s perception regarding communication with healthcare professionals and the quality of care and assistance received, a patient-centered approach focused on individual needs is evident. Its concise format, ease of application, and focus on essential components of care — such as interpersonal communication, emotional and spiritual support, and team-based care — make it especially relevant for oncology and palliative care contexts. These dimensions play a relevant role in the context of continuous and palliative care, as they reflect not only technical competence but also the sensitivity and humanization of the multidisciplinary team throughout the course of the illness.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. The absence of left ventricular ejection fraction measurement in part of the sample is due to incomplete medical records. Regarding the NYHA < 3 scale, many individuals in this sample could not differentiate the scale pattern, which was also an initial limitation for the direct application of the SPICT criteria. Regarding the NYHA scale, in the present study, the NYHA functional classification provided by the medical team may not reflect the patient’s actual condition. However, strategies were adopted to mitigate bias. Disease severity was assessed using validated clinical proxies, such as the presence of multiple indicators of deterioration (recurrent hospitalizations and quality of life). This approach, while not replacing the precision of objective methods, allowed the inclusion of patients with a clear need for palliative care based on the clinical phenotype of frailty and disease progression.

Conclusion

The internal structure with two domains (communication with healthcare professionals and quality of care) is the most suitable for use in individuals with HF eligible for palliative care through the QCQ-PC. The constructs are valid and reliable, with adequate internal consistency.
