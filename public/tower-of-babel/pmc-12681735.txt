Host TGF-β signalling facilitates Vi-capsule mediated adaptive immune evasion and vertical transmission of Salmonella Typhi in Caenorhabditis elegans
Authors: Bynedi Seshadhri Chinna Mounish, Ravichandran Monisha, Krishnaswamy Balamurugan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Bacterial infections trigger robust host immune responses, while pathogens concurrently adapt to enhance survival within the host. In our previous study, we observed 24 h of Salmonella Typhi infection in Caenorhabditis elegans colonized the body of the host and also vertically transmitted to their F1 offspring generation. This study investigates host interaction-mediated modulation in S. Typhi physiology and virulence, focusing on host-derived strains from initial infection (CeP0-ST) and vertically transmitted F1 progeny (CeF1-ST), compared to the wild-type unexposed strain (WT-ST). We employed C. elegans lifespan assays, bacterial colonization, morphology, motility, gene expression, ROS estimation, and infection models using immune pathway mutants. CeP0-ST and CeF1-ST exhibited increased infectivity, faster mortality, and enhanced colonization, accompanied by reduced cell size, motility, and lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-mediated immunogenicity. This was evidenced by downregulation of fliC, ompC, ompF, and SPI-1 encoded Type III Secretion System (T3SS-1) genes (sipA, avrA, sopE). Upregulation of Vi-capsular antigen genes (tviD, tviA, viaB) suggested immune evasion, which was supported by improved host survival upon Vi-negative Ty21a strain infection. Among various C. elegans immune pathway mutants, the TGF-β pathway mutant [sma-6(−)] showed altered pathogen modulation, with reduced Vi expression and increased T3SS gene expression in derived strains. Suppressed host immune gene expression during WT-derived infections, but increased expression during sma-6 (−)-derived infections, suggests a role for TGF-β signalling in driving pathogen adaptation. These findings highlight that host interaction promotes S. Typhi immune evasion via Vi-antigen expression, potentially regulated by host TGF-β signalling, and that vertical transmission of adapted strains facilitates long-term persistence.

Introduction

Host-pathogen interactions represent a dynamic interplay between the invading pathogen and the resisting host. The host responds to the infection by employing various defence mechanisms, which include pathogen recognition receptors (PRRs), activation of immune signalling pathways, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation to eliminate the invading pathogens (Akira et al., 2006; Medzhitov, 2007; Manoharan et al., 2024). The pathogen, in turn, will try to respond to the host response using highly adaptive mechanisms to evade immune detection and establish persistent infections (Cai et al., 2025; Finlay and McFadden, 2006). The co-evolutionary strategy induces genetic and phenotypic alterations among the interacting partners, which might lead to a rise in new pathogenic traits in the bacterium and adapted responses in the host. The pathogen uses several strategies to circumvent the host response, namely, attachment to body surfaces, recognition by surface immune receptors, secretion of antimicrobial effector molecules, internalization and degradation by phagocytes and activation of the humoral as well as the cellular immune systems (Hornef et al., 2002). The whooping cough-causing pathogen Bordetella pertussis paralyzes the respiratory tract's ciliary clearance function by releasing components of its cell wall that produce nitric oxide-mediated ciliostasis (Flak and Goldman, 1999). Helicobacter pylori, which causes chronic infection in the gastric epithelial surface, produces several virulent determinants such as adhesins, urease enzymes, cytotoxins and effector proteins in response to the host to enhance colonization and disease induction (Mohammadzadeh et al., 2023). Also, during cystic fibrosis, Pseudomonas aeruginosa modulates into mucoid, alginate-overproducing and small-colony variants to ensure enhanced biofilm production, further ensuring its persistence inside the host tissue (Koska et al., 2022). Pathogen such as Mycobacterium tuberculosis generates alterations in their epigenetic and phase transition mechanisms to their surface proteins, which help in immune evasion (Bhaskar et al., 2020).

Salmonella enterica Serovar Typhi is a gram-negative pathogen which is characterized by causing deadly typhoid fever. The pathogen possesses several virulence factors, such as the Type III Secretion System (T3SS), which is encoded by Salmonella Pathogenicity Islands (SPIs), which is involved in bacterial uptake, adhesion and invasion into the host cells. Endotoxins, which include Lipopolysaccharide (O antigen) reportedly involved in inducing inflammation during infections. The Vi capsular antigen is involved in immune evasion and survival in the host by masking the antigenic factors. The flagellar H antigen is essential for bacterial motility and invasion inside the host cells. During infection, these surface pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) are recognized by PRRs such as Toll-like receptors (TLRs) and NOD-like receptors (NLRs) in of host cell, which leads to the initiation of an active immune response (Yamamoto and Takeda, 2010). Upon detection and ligand binding, the TLRs initiate downstream signalling, which produces inflammatory factors such as interleukins and cytokines via recruitment of C-terminal domain adaptors. This causes a channelled response of neutrophils to the site of infection, producing an inflammatory response. Parallelly, Salmonella can also respond via SPI-7 encoded regulatory protein TviA, which can induce modulatory capsule expression and flagellar movement and ensure reduced detection by TLR-5 and subsequent inflammatory responses. Capsules are polysaccharide layers related to the Vi antigen in Salmonella, which are wrapped around membrane recognition PAMPs, masking their expression during host interaction (Raffatellu et al., 2006; Wilson et al., 2008). Recently, Vi capsular antigen has also been reported to alter autophagy of macrophages to ensure its enhanced survival in host cells by decreasing the expression of Nod2 and Galectin-8 (Zhang et al., 2025). In our lab, using the Caenorhabditis elegans model system, we have reported host modulations in the aspect of immune response at proteomic, transcriptomic and metabolomic levels upon S. Typhi infection, where we have observed an activation of immune regulatory pathways in C. elegans upon infection (Sivamaruthi and Balamurugan, 2014; Balasubramaniam et al., 2020; Muthubharathi et al., 2023).

Also, we have reported that infection of S. Typhi in C. elegans can induce transgenerational adaptive modulations in the host to generate resistance against the infection (Mounish et al., 2025). Also, in the same study, we have observed vertical transmission of the pathogen S. Typhi from parent to offspring of the host. The persistence and the transmission of the pathogen might be due to the physiological and molecular modulations undertaken within the pathogen in consequence of the host response. Hence, we sought to answer the following questions: How does the pathogen respond to the host response upon interaction? What is the regulatory mechanism that ensures the persistence of the pathogen in the host and its transmission across a generation? Also, we wanted to elucidate the role of the interacting host in the possible bacterial modulation. Through the present study, we observed a host immune response specific adaptive modulation in S. Typhi upon interaction with C. elegans appears to be mediated by enhanced Vi antigen response. This modulation might help the pathogen to exhibit immune evasion by suppressing the expression of immunogenic players, thereby assisting in the persistence inside the host and also subsequent vertical transmission in the host.

Materials and methods

Maintenance of C. elegans and Bacterial cultures: The N2 Bristol wild type strain of C. elegans and mutant strains BQ1 (mg306 V), CZ5730 (ju476 I) and PJ1190 (e1482 II), were obtained from Caenorhabditis Genetics Center (CGC), University of Minnesota, USA. Escherichia coli OP50 was used as a laboratory food source for the nematodes, and they were grown under standard conditions in Nematode Growth Media (NGM) at 20 °C. Synchronized L4 stage worms were used for all experiments. The synchronisation was done by lysing the gravid adult worms with alkaline sodium hypochlorite solution containing 5 M potassium hydroxide (KOH) in the ratio of 1:1 (Sivamaruthi and Balamurugan, 2014). Salmonella enterica Serovar Typhi (MTCC 733) (ST) was obtained from MTCC (Microbial Type Culture Collection, IMTECH, Chandigarh, India) and was maintained in LB medium at 37 °C. For the experiments, ∼0.2 OD at 600 nm of bacterial culture was used.

Isolation of interacted and vertically inherited S. Typhi: P0 parent L4 stage C. elegans were washed with M9 buffer, and infection was induced by adding them to a WT-ST lawn plate and incubating them for 24 h at 20 °C. The bacterial lawn plates were prepared by spreading the ∼0.2 OD bacterial culture over an NGM plate and incubating them at room temperature for overnight to obtain a uniform growth across the plate. After incubation, the worms were then washed thrice with M9 buffer, and ∼ 10 worms were lysed and plated on Salmonella Shigella agar to obtain the ST, which was accumulated in the body of P0 parent C. elegans (CeP0-ST) by following the procedure as mentioned in the Bacterial colonization assay section. The remaining WT-ST infected P0 worms were allowed to lay eggs naturally to obtain the F1 generation offspring worms, which were grown on E. coli OP50 seeded plates. Once the F1 offspring worms were grown upto the adult stage, ∼10 worms were washed with M9 buffer and lysed and plated on Salmonella Shigella agar to obtain the ST, which was transmitted from the P0 parental infection (CeF1-ST).

Survival assay: A liquid medium-based survival assay was used to assess the survivability of C. elegans. ∼10 number of L4 stage worms were added to each group in a 24-well plate consisting of 20 % bacterial culture and M9 buffer. To inhibit the production of progeny during the assay, 50 μM 5-Fluoro-2-deoxyuridine (FuDR) was added. The number of worms surviving was recorded at regular intervals. The survival rates in E. coli OP50 are considered as control lifespan. The experiments were conducted in biological triplicates (n = ∼ 90 worms for the assay), and the data were plotted using a Kaplan-Meier plot, and statistical analyses were carried out using the Log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test (Balasubramaniam et al., 2020).

Brood size assay: To assess the reproductive fitness of C. elegans, the number of progenies generated was counted. The L4 stage worms were washed, and 5 worms were added per plate for a group in triplicate on bacterial lawn NGM plates. The worms were transferred every 24 h to a new lawn plate of the same bacterium. The number of hatched worms was counted on the next day (VenkataKrishna et al., 2024). The experiments were done in six biological replicates, each consisting of 3 worms for an experimental replicate (n = ∼ 18 worms for the assay), and the mean value was taken for plotting the plot.

ROS Analysis through 2′-7′-Dichlorofluorescein Estimation: After 24 h of infection in the bacterial lawn, the worms were washed with M9 buffer. 50 μL of 100 μM/mL DCFH-DA [2′-7′ dichlorofluorescin diacetate (DCF)] was added to 500 μL of the worm sample and incubated for 20 min in dark conditions. Post incubation, the worms were washed with 1× PBS buffer and then with M9 buffer. The worms were then paralyzed by using a 1:100 ratio of 100 mM Sodium Azide and visualized under a fluorescence microscope (Nikon Eclipse TS2R-C-AL, Tokyo, Japan (Pooranachithra et al., 2019). The fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software, and values were used for plotting a graph (n = 3 worms/ group).

Bacterial cells preparation for SEM analysis: The Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) sample preparation for bacteria was assessed as per (Minuti et al., 2022) with slight modifications. The bacterial culture was added to a sterile glass slide and air-dried. The slide was washed with 1× PBS thrice, followed by a wash with 0.25 % glutaraldehyde dissolved in sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.5) and incubated at room temperature for overnight. Post incubation, the slides were washed with sodium phosphate buffer thrice, followed by dehydration using increasing concentrations of ethanol (30 %, 50 %, 70 %, 80 %, and 90 %) for a period of 10 min. Further, the bacterial glass slides were incubated in 100 % ethanol for 1 h at room temperature, and then they were air-dried. The slides were then taken for SEM analysis. The length and width of the cells were measured using ImageJ software and plotted as a graph (n = 9 cells/group).

Swimming and Swarming assay: The motility-associated swimming and swarming behaviour of the bacteria was assessed as per (Pradhan and Negi, 2019) with slight modifications. Briefly, 5 μL of an overnight-grown bacterial culture was inoculated in the centre of the freshly prepared LB agar plates (0.25 % for swimming and 0.5 % for swarming). The plates were allowed to dry in the hood for 10 min and then subjected to incubation at 37 °C. The area covered by the bacterial growth was assessed post-incubation. The diameters of swimming and swarming were measured individually and plotted as a graph (n = 6).

Bacterial colonization assay: To quantify the pathogen load inside the body of C. elegans, a colonization assay was performed. Briefly, the worms were washed thrice with M9 buffer after WT-ST, CeP0-ST and CeF1-ST infection and then treated with 100 μg/mL Tetracycline to remove the bacteria adhered to their surface (Balasubramaniam et al., 2020). ∼10 worms were added to 100 μL of 1× PBS and were lysed for 30 s (10–10 − 10 s) with a mini handheld homogenizer (Moxcare MT-13 K, Haryana, India). The homogenate was diluted and spread across Salmonella Shigella agar. The number of colonies was counted manually after 16 h of incubation at 37 °C, and CFU was calculated. The experiments were carried out in biological replicates, and a graph was plotted on a log scale.

RNA Fluorescence in situ Hybridization (FISH): RNA Fluorescence in situ Hybridization was performed for visualization of the bacterial colonization inside the body of C. elegans by following the protocol mentioned in (Rivera et al., 2022) with minor modifications. Briefly, the worms were collected in a sterile tube and washed thrice with 1× PBS + 0.1 % Tween 20 (PBS-T) buffer by spinning the worms at 500 x g for 1 min. Then, the worms were fixed using 4 % Paraformaldehyde (PFA) by adding 33 μL of already prepared 16 % PFA to 100 μL of PBS-T buffer containing worms and incubated for 45 min at room temperature. After incubation, PFA was aspirated, and the worms were incubated for overnight at 4 °C in 70 % ethanol. Then, ethanol was removed, and the fixed worms were gently washed to avoid breakage using PBS-T thrice by centrifugation. The worms were then washed once with the freshly prepared hybridization buffer (HB) (900 mM NaCl, 20 mM Tris, pH 7.5, 0.01 % SDS). After centrifugation, the buffer was removed, leaving 100 μL of HB with worms to which the fluorophore-labelled FISH probes were added to attain a final concentration of 10 ng/μL. The samples were incubated in the dark for 16 h at 48 °C in a water bath by gently mixing by tapping every hour. After incubation, the HB containing FISH probes was removed, and the worms were washed with freshly prepared wash buffer (WB) (900 mM NaCl, 20 mM Tris pH 7.5, 5 mM EDTA, 0.01 % SDS) and incubated at 51 °C for 1 h by gently inverting the tube every 15–20 min. Then, the WB was aspirated, and the worms were stored in PBS-T and then subjected to imaging using a Confocal Laser Scanning Microscope (LSM 710, Carl Zeiss, Germany). The FISH probe used in the study was Sal3 (5’-AATCACTTCACCTACGTG-3′), labelled with fluorophore Cyanine 3 (Cy3) at its 5′ end, which binds specifically to 23S rRNA of Salmonella species (Mounish et al., 2025). The gain of 720 ms and digital gain of 5.6 were set for all fluorescent images.

Isolation of Lipopolysaccharide from bacteria: LPS isolation was carried out as mentioned in (Kesika et al., 2015). Briefly, 200 mL 0.2 OD of S. Typhi cultures (WT-ST, CeP0-ST and CeF1-ST) were grown at 37 °C, and the cell pellet was obtained by centrifugation at 6000 rpm for 10 min. The pellet was resuspended in 100 mL of TRIzol™ reagent (RNA X Press reagent, Himedia) and stored at −20 °C for overnight. The samples were then thawed, and 25 mL of ice-cold chloroform was added and vortexed to induce phase separation. The samples were incubated at room temperature for 20 min, followed by centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 20 min. The aqueous phase was collected into a fresh tube, and 50 mL of water was added to the organic phase to further extract the LPS. The obtained crude LPS was then precipitated using the ethanol‑magnesium method and incubated at −20 °C overnight. Post incubation, the samples were centrifuged at 12,000 xg for 15 min to obtain the crude LPS pellet, which was further resuspended with MilliQ water and used for experimentation.

Multichannel chemotaxis assay using microchip platform: A microchip-based chemotaxis assay was performed as mentioned in (Muthubharathi et al., 2022). Briefly, the eight-lane microchip was prepared by pouring the NGM media on the Teflon mould. The media was dried, removed and gently placed on a sterile petri dish. For the chemotaxis assay, ∼15 numbers of worms were added to the centre, and 2 μL of ∼0.2 OD bacterial cultures (E. coli OP50 and WT-ST/CeP0-ST/CeF1-ST) was seeded on the lanes as shown in Supplementary Fig. 1A. The preferential behaviour towards each bacterial lane was calculated using the preferential index towards the lane.Preferential Index=No.of wormsatthe bacterial spotTotalNo.of worms assayed

Capsular staining: To visualize the capsule of the bacterium, capsular staining using crystal violet and copper sulphate was carried out. Briefly, a loop of ∼0.2 OD bacterial culture was smeared over a sterile glass slide and dried. The smear was then flooded with 0.5 % crystal violet solution and incubated for one minute. The slide was then washed with 20 % copper sulphate solution and immediately processed for imaging. The capsule remains unstained and appears as a transparent region around the stained cell, which is observed using a brightfield microscope under 100× magnification using oil immersion.

In vitro and in vivo passaging: For obtaining in vivo passaged strains, the CeP0-ST obtained after the first interaction with C. elegans was again subjected to interaction with naive C. elegans for the same duration of 24 h. The worms were lysed and plated in SS agar as mentioned in the bacterial colonization assay to obtain in vivo passage 2 strain. Similarly, strains were obtained up to 3 in vivo passages. For obtaining in vitro passaged strains, the CeP0-ST obtained from the colonization assay was grown on nutrient broth medium for 12 h to obtain in vitro passage strain 1. The passage strain 1 was further inoculated into fresh nutrient broth at 1 % inoculum and grown similarly to obtain in vitro passage strain 2. Similarly, strains were obtained up to 3 in vitro passages.

RNA isolation from bacterial cells: RNA isolation from bacteria was carried out as described in (Muthubharathi et al., 2022) with slight modifications. Briefly, ∼0.2 OD of overnight-grown bacterial cells were harvested by centrifuging the samples at 10,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was discarded and the pellet was washed with saline buffer (pH 8) and resuspended in 1 mL of TRIzol reagent and stored in −80 °C for overnight. The samples were then thawed at 60 °C for 1 min and vortexed vigorously for 1 min. The samples were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was transferred to a fresh RNase-free tube. An equal volume of ice-cold chloroform was added, followed by a gentle vortex for 15 s, and incubated in ice for 5 min. The aqueous phase was collected upon centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 50 min at 4 °C, and 500 μL of ice-cold isopropanol was added. The samples were gently mixed by inverting the tube and incubated at room temperature for 15 min. The pellet was collected by centrifuging the samples at 10,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The pellet was washed with 75 % ethanol and centrifuged at 7500 rpm for 5 min. The obtained pellet was air-dried and resuspended with 25 μL of DEPC water.

RNA isolation from C. elegans: The sample groups were washed with M9 buffer, followed by a final DEPC wash individually. 1 mL of TRIzol™ reagent (RNA X Press reagent, Himedia) was added to the worms, and the samples were frozen in −80 °C for 24 h. The samples were then thawed and vortexed gently before RNA extraction. The samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C, where the obtained supernatant was collected and transferred to a fresh RNase-free tube. To the supernatant, 500 μL of ice-cold chloroform was added to induce phase separation. The samples were vortexed briefly and incubated on ice for five minutes, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The aqueous phase was collected into a fresh tube, and 500 μL of ice-cold isopropanol was added and mixed gently. The samples were incubated at RT for 15 min, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The obtained pellet was washed using 100 μL of 75 % ethanol and centrifuged at 7500 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. The RNA pellet was air-dried and resuspended in DEPC water. The isolated RNA was then reverse transcribed using oligo dT primer and MultiScribe™ Reverse Transcriptase (Applied Biosystems) enzyme following the guidelines provided by the manufacturer and as described earlier (Muthubharathi et al., 2021).

Gene expression studies using Transcriptomic analysis: For quantitative PCR studies, Applied Biosystems SYBR Green Mastermix was used to assess the expression of candidate genes. For the expression of S. Typhi genes, 16S rRNA of S. Typhi was used as a housekeeping control and for the expression of C. elegans genes, act-2 was used as a housekeeping control. The sequence of the primers used in the study is provided in Table 1. The primers were designed using the Prime3Plus online tool, and the experiments were carried out in triplicate. Polymerization and quantification were performed using a real-time PCR instrument (QuantStudio™ 5 Real-Time PCR System). To quantify the fold change, the double delta Ct method was used (Prasanth et al., 2019). All gene expression analyses were carried out in biological triplicates consisting of duplicate technical replicates each (n = 6 technical replicates). Statistical analyses: All the experiments were performed in independent triplicates. The experimental mean value was taken, the standard deviation was calculated, and the graph was plotted using GraphPad Prism 8.0. For survival analyses, Kaplan-Meier plots were generated, and statistical analyses were carried out using the Log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test between the sample groups. The box plots were generated using individual data points corresponding to the replicates, and one-way ANOVA (Tukey's multiple comparisons test) was used for calculating significance between sample groups. The significance of p ≤ 0.05 was represented as *, p ≤ 0.005 as **, p ≤ 0.0005 as ***, and p ≤ 0.0001 as **** in this study.

Results

S. Typhi obtained after in vivo interaction with C. elegans exhibited enhanced infectivity and colonization potential

S. Typhi can colonize the body of C. elegans upon infection, causing deterioration in the health span parameters of the host. In our previous study, we observed that upon 24 h of infection in C. elegans with Salmonella Typhi, the pathogen colonizes the body of the host. Also, we observed vertical transmission of the pathogen from the parent to the F1 offspring worms (Mounish et al., 2025). In the current study, the pathogen was obtained from the body of C elegans after 24 h of interaction by homogenization and plating on selective media, which was termed as CeP0-ST strain. Similarly, a vertically transmitted pathogen was isolated from the adult F1 offspring worms of the infected parents, which was termed as CeF1-ST strain (Fig. 1A). When the interacted (CeP0-ST) and vertically transmitted (CeF1-ST) S. Typhi were checked for their killing ability using naive C. elegans, we observed a reduction in the worm's survival after CeP0-ST and CeF1-ST when compared with the wildtype S. Typhi (WT-ST). In control E. coli OP50, the C. elegans survived up to 20 days. In WT-ST, complete mortality of C. elegans was observed after ∼12 days. In CeP0-ST and CeF1-ST, the worms survived up to ∼19 days, which was 23.07 % lower than the survivability in WT-ST (Fig. 1B). In addition, the egg-laying capability was assessed using brood size assay, where WT-ST, CeP0-ST and CeF1-ST exhibited ∼97, ∼125 and ∼ 148 eggs, respectively, which were significantly lower than the E. coli OP50 fed control C. elegans, which laid ∼279 eggs (Fig. 1C). Further to analyse the colonization of the bacterial strains, a CFU assay was carried out where CeP0-ST and CeF1-ST infected C. elegans exhibited significantly higher pathogen load of 3.68*104 and 4.07*104 CFU/worm, respectively, when compared to WT-ST infected C. elegans showed 2.19*104 CFU/worm (Fig. 1D). Also, to visualize the in vivo colonization of the pathogen inside the body of the worm, RNA Fluorescence in situ hybridization was performed by hybridizing the worms with a fluorescent tag labelled Sal3 probe specific to 23S rRNA of S. Typhi. The data showed higher colonization inside the body of the worm, supporting the CFU results (Fig. 1E). Learnt pathogenic avoidance is a trait exhibited by C. elegans as an adaptive response to escape from the disease-causing pathogens. Infection of C. elegans with S. Typhi was found to learnt pathogenic avoidance, which was also found to be transgenerationally inherited (Mounish et al., 2025). To check the preferential choice of the worms among all the strains (E. coli OP50, WT-ST, CeP0-ST and CeF1-ST), a multichannel chemotaxis assay using a microchip was carried out post infection (Supplementary Fig. 1A). The worms fed with E. coli OP50 preferred WT-ST among all the strains tested, while the WT-ST infected worms preferred E. coli OP50, corresponding to the previous result of exhibition of learnt avoidance towards S. Typhi upon infection (Supplementary Figs. 1B and 1C). Interestingly, the worms infected with CeP0-ST and CeF1-ST showed altered responses, as CeP0-ST infected worms were attracted towards WT-ST and CeP0-ST and the worms infected with CeF1-ST showed uniform preference towards all the bacterial strains assayed (Supplementary Figs. 1D and 1E). S. Typhi displayed alterations in morphological, behavioural and virulence-associated traits upon host interaction

In continuation with the above-mentioned modulation with respect higher infectivity nature of CeP0-ST and CeF1-ST, experiments were done to check if the modulation was caused by any alteration in the pathogen. To assess the morphological changes, SEM analysis was carried which suggested a marginal reduction in the size of bacterial cells in CeP0-ST and CeF1-ST strains from ∼1.877 ± 0.26 μm in WT-ST to ∼1.63 ± 0.24 μm and ∼ 1.47 ± 0.23 μm in CeP0-ST and CeF1-ST, respectively (Fig. 2A, B and Table 2). Also, the cell width was also observed to be slightly reduced from ∼0.51 ± 0.06 μm in WT-ST to ∼0.50 ± 0.06 μm and ∼ 0.43 ± 0.07 μm in CeP0-ST and CeF1-ST, respectively (Fig. 2C and Table 2). In bacterial pathogens, motility is one of the features contributing to the invasiveness inside the host body. Hence, the swimming and swarming behaviour of the CeP0-ST and CeF1-ST was assessed by measuring the diameter of the spreading. Interestingly, both CeP0-ST and CeF1-ST exhibited reduced swimming and swarming when compared to WT-ST, suggesting a reduction in motility (Fig. 3A, B, D and E). In supportive of the finding, the expression of fliC, which produces flagellin protein involved in bacterial motility, was quantified using qPCR analysis. Our observations suggested a downregulation in the expression of fliC in both CeP0-ST and CeF1-ST when compared to WT-ST (Fig. 3C). Lipopolysaccharide (LPS), also known as endotoxin, is also a major factor associated with virulence in S. Typhi. Previous studies from our lab have reported the alteration in LPS upon host interaction (Vigneshkumar et al., 2012; Sivamaruthi et al., 2015). Hence, we extracted LPS from the bacterium and checked for the survivability of C. elegans upon supplementation with various concentrations. The survivability of C. elegans (n = ∼ 90 worms for the assay) gradually decreased with ∼17 days at 1 μg/mL, ∼16 days at 10 μg/mL, ∼14 days at 20 and 30 μg/mL and ∼ 12 days at 40 and 50 μg/mL of LPS extracted from WT-ST (Fig. 4A). But the survival of C. elegans upon supplementation of LPS extracted from CeP0-ST and CeF1-ST was observed to be higher. The survival of C. elegans ranged from 14 to 15 days and 14–16 days upon LPS supplementation from CeP0-ST and CeF1-ST, respectively, at the mentioned concentrations (Fig. 4B and C). This was in contrast with the prior bacterial survival, as CeP0-ST and CeF1-ST infection showed faster killing, but the LPS extracted from respective bacteria did not exhibit a similar effect. Further, the expression of ompF and ompC, which encode major outer membrane porins embedded in the bacterial outer membrane and coexist with LPS, was quantified. Altered Vi capsular antigen might render immune evasiveness upon host interaction, ensuring enhanced killing

As we observed contrasting findings with bacterial infectivity and their innate physiological alterations with respect to motility and LPS, we then assessed the expression of major virulence systems in S. Typhi, such as the Salmonella Pathogenicity Island SPI-1 encoded Type 3 Secretion System-1 (T3SS-1) and the capsule composing of Vi antigen. Similar to the previous findings, we observed downregulation in the expression of T3SS effector genes such as sipA, avrA and sopE (Fig. 5A, B and C). But interestingly, the expression of tviD (Vi antigen O-acetyltransferase), tviA (transcriptional regulator of Vi antigen expression) and viaB, which are responsible for biosynthesis and regulation of Vi capsular polysaccharide (Vi antigen), was found to be upregulated in CeP0-ST and CeF1-ST when compared with WT-ST (Fig. 5D, E and F). The Vi capsule has been reported to be involved in shielding the immunogenic surface molecules from host defence mechanisms responsible for pathogen detection and initiation of immune response inside the host by enabling the bacterium to evade host response and phagocytosis upon interaction (Raffatellu et al., 2005; Wilson et al., 2008; Hart et al., 2016; Wilson et al., 2011). Our findings suggest that upon interacting with C. elegans, S. Typhi may have undergone adaptive modifications in response to the host, potentially by upregulating Vi antigen expression. The observed increase in the expression of the Vi antigen could have facilitated immune evasion, thereby enhancing the virulence of the pathogen obtained after interaction (CeP0-ST) and subsequent vertical transmission (CeF1-ST). To validate the observation, we used the Ty21a strain, which is a live, attenuated vaccine strain of S. Typhi used as a vaccine to prevent typhoid fever. Due to the mutation in it viaB locus, the Ty21a strain exhibits reduced expression of Vi antigen (Cryz Jr et al., 1989; Guzman et al., 2006). The vertical transmission of the bacterium was assessed transgenerationally upon feeding the P0 parental generation C. elegans with Ty21a using the CFU assay. The transmission of Ty21a was observed up to two subsequent offspring generations (F1 and F2), which was in contrast with S. Typhi infection, where transmission of the pathogen was observed only till the F1 generation (Fig. 6A). The obtained bacterial strains were labelled as CeP0-Ty21a, CeF1-Ty21a and CeF2-Ty21a and were assessed for their effect on the naive C. elegans survival. While the C. elegans survived ∼13 days in Ty21a, their survival did not indicate a significant change in CeP0-Ty21a, CeF1-Ty21a and CeF2-Ty21a strains as they exhibited ∼13, ∼14 and ∼ 13, respectively (Fig. 6B). In contrast to the CeP0-ST and CeF1-ST of S. Typhi, the CeP0-Ty21a, CeF1-Ty21a, and CeF2-Ty21a strains did not show enhanced killing, which could suggest that alteration of the active Vi capsular antigen might be one of the important mechanisms for the bacterium to generate evasion against host response. Parallelly, the observed enhancement in Vi antigen regulation was also assessed to determine if the trait remains the same upon in vitro passaging using laboratory media along with multiple rounds of in vivo interactions in C. elegans. The findings suggested that the expression of tviA and viaB was downregulated upon in vitro passaging using nutrient broth (Fig. 7A and B). But interestingly, the enhanced expression was observed upon in vivo interactions upon two passages, and the levels dropped during the third interaction (Fig. 7C and D). The adaptive changes in Vi antigen expression may be influenced by the host's TGF-β immune signalling

Since the above-mentioned bacterial modulations are obtained after host interaction, we then wanted to assess the contribution of the host in rendering bacterial adaptations. During infection of C. elegans, the host responds to the invading pathogen by generating an immune response through immune regulatory pathways such as the insulin signalling pathway, TGF-β signalling pathway, and mTOR pathway (Marudhupandiyan and Balamurugan, 2017; Gowripriya et al., 2024; VenkataKrishna et al., 2024). Hence, we used mutant strains with the deletion of specific genes involved in the above-mentioned immune pathways for the current study. S. Typhi infection was tested in BQ1 (mg306 V), which is a loss of function mutant of akt-1 involved in insulin signalling, CZ5730 (ju476 I), which is a loss of function mutant of dlk-1 involved in mTOR signalling pathway and PJ1190 (e1482 II), which is a loss of function mutant of sma-6 involved in TGF- β signalling pathway. Vertical transmission of the pathogen was observed upto one offspring F1 generation across the mutant strains. akt-1 (−) worms showed a colonization of 3.77*104 CFU/worm [akt-1 (−) CeP0-ST] and 9.50*102 CFU/ worm [akt-1 (−) CeF1-ST] of vertically transmitted S. Typhi cells respectively (Fig. 8A). dlk-1 (−) worms showed a colonization of 3.74*104 CFU/worm [dlk-1 (−) CeP0-ST] and 8.50*102 CFU/ worm [dkl-1 (−) CeF1-ST] of vertically transmitted S. Typhi cells, respectively (Fig. 8B). Similarly, sma-6 (−) worms showed a colonization of 2.93*104 CFU/worm [sma-6 (−) CeP0-ST] and 5.50*102 CFU/ worm [sma-6 (−) CeF1-ST] of vertically transmitted S. Typhi cells, respectively (Fig. 8C). Further, the obtained S. Typhi cultures were assessed for their infectivity in their corresponding mutant C. elegans. akt-1 (−) worms exhibited a survival of ∼19 days in E. coli OP50, ∼11 days in S. Typhi, ∼8 days in akt-1 (−) CeP0-ST and akt-1 (−) CeF1-ST respectively (Fig. 8D). dlk-1 (−) worms exhibited a survival of ∼15 days in E. coli OP50, ∼12 days in S. Typhi, ∼9 days in dlk-1 (−) CeP0-ST and ∼ 9 days in dlk-1 (−) CeF1-ST respectively (Fig. 8E). Strikingly, sma-6 (−) worms exhibited a survival of ∼15 days in all tested groups, E. coli OP50, S. Typhi, sma-6 (−) CeP0-ST and sma-6 (−) CeF1-ST (Fig. 8F). While CeP0-ST and CeF1-ST of akt-1 (−) and dlk-1 (−) worms showed faster killing, similar to the wild type C. elegans counterpart, sma-6 (−) obtained pathogen did not exhibit a significant effect, suggesting that the strains might be devoid of higher infectivity and that the sma-6 (−) C. elegans exhibited enhanced resistance. Further, Vi capsular antigen-associated gene expression of the sma-6 (−) CeP0-ST and sma-6 (−) CeF1-ST S. Altered pathogen recognition in the host upon infection with wild-type and sma-6 (−) C. elegans obtained CeP0-ST and CeF1-ST

Vi antigen in S. Typhi plays an important role in mediating immune evasion during the pathogen's interaction with the host, enabling its persistence inside the body. The Vi capsule masks the highly immunogenic OMPs and LPS and prevents the host system from recognizing the pathogen (Wilson et al., 2008). Hence, the expression of pathogen recognition genes such as tol-1 (Toll-like Receptor in C. elegans), clec-67 (C-Type Lectin involved infection response), fshr-1 (GPCR protein involved in sensing bacterial toxins and metabolites and activating insulin and p38 MAP Kinase signalling pathways) and npr-1 (neuropeptide receptor which senses bacterial cues from environment to generate altered behavioural response) (Kamaladevi and Balamurugan, 2015; Xu et al., 2013; Madhu et al., 2023; Radeke and Herman, 2020; Yamamoto and Takeda, 2010) were assessed in wild type WT CeP0-ST and WT CeF1-ST and sma-6 (−) CeP0-ST and sma-6 (−) CeF1-ST infected C. elegans as compared to the WT-ST infected C. elegans. The expression of tol-1, clec-67, fshr-1 and npr-1 was observed to be significantly downregulated in WT CeP0-ST and WT CeF1-ST infected C. elegans whereas all the genes were significantly upregulated in sma-6 (−) CeP0-ST infected C. elegans and a mild upregulation of clec-67, fshr-1 and npr-1 was observed in sma-6 (−) CeF1-ST infected C. elegans (Fig. 11A, B, C and D). This suggests that upon infection of naive C. elegans with WT CeP0-ST and WT CeF1-ST, which have enhanced Vi antigen expression, the host could not effectively recognize the pathogen, which could possibly be the reason for its enhanced killing ability. CeP0-ST and CeF1-ST's adaptive upregulation of Vi antigen expression suppresses the host immune response, promoting increased infectivity

To check the possible implications of modulated pathogen recognition among WT CeP0-ST, WT CeF1-ST, and sma-6 (−) obtained sma-6 (−) CeP0-ST and sma-6 (−) CeF1-ST, Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) estimation using DCF-DA was carried out. The results indicated a reduction in fluorescence intensity (reduced ROS level) in WT CeP0-ST, WT CeF1-ST infected C. elegans when compared to the WT-ST infected worms, whereas in sma-6 (−) CeP0-ST and sma-6 (−) CeF1-ST infected C. elegans, the observed fluorescence was higher (Fig. 12A and B). ROS can be generated during stress conditions, which act as signalling players to activate the immune response during bacterial infections (Chávez et al., 2009). This was further validated by assessing the expression of sod-1, which encodes superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD-1) that is essential for protecting cells from oxidative damage during pathogenic infections. The expression of sod-1 was observed to be downregulated in WT CeP0-ST and WT CeF1-ST infected C. elegans. In contrast, the expression appeared to be upregulated in sma-6 (−) CeP0-ST and sma-6 (−) CeF1-ST infected C. elegans (Fig. 12C). Further, survival analysis was carried out for all the four strains where the worms survived for ∼9 days in WT CeP0-ST and WT CeF1-ST and ∼ 14 days in sma-6 (−) CeP0-ST and sma-6 (−) CeF1-ST when compared to WT-ST, where the survival was noted till ∼13 days (Fig. 12D). PMK-1 (p38 MAP Kinase-1) is an ortholog of p38 MAP Kinase, which is part of the conserved MAPK cascade in C. elegans. It is involved in the activation of immune response across tissues such as the intestine and hypodermis and controls the expression of C-type Lectin genes along with the activation of transcription factors involved in immune response (Kamaladevi and Balamurugan, 2015). Discussion

Bacterial infections can alter homeostatic physiological functioning of the host to respond to the invading pathogen by generating enhanced immunity through regulatory pathways (Barton et al., 2021; Hornef et al., 2002). Parallelly, the pathogen could also undergo modulations in response to the host's fightback (Vigneshkumar et al., 2014; Sivamaruthi et al., 2015; Kamaladevi and Balamurugan, 2015). In our recent study, we reported that when C. elegans were infected with S. Typhi for 24 h, the pathogen not just colonized the host but was also vertically transmitted to its F1 offspring generation (Mounish et al., 2025). Similar effect was also reported during Staphylococcus aureus infection from our lab (Prithika and Balamurugan, 2021). Our study provides evidence that S. Typhi undergoes such adaptive modulation following interaction with C. elegans. Hence, in the current study, we wanted to assess the modulations in S. Typhi which was obtained after interaction with C. elegans (CeP0-ST) for 24 h and also the vertically transmitted bacterium obtained from the F1 offspring worms (CeF1-ST). The recovered bacteria displayed increased virulence and colonization potential, suggesting that exposure to host immunity induces stable changes that favour pathogen survival. Similar findings in other pathogens support this observation; for instance, S. epidermidis strains producing biofilm exopolysaccharide (EPS) via the icaADBC locus have been reported to exhibit enhanced intestinal colonization and nematode killing, while mutants deficient in biofilm formation lost this advantage (Begun et al., 2007). Such parallels highlight that pathogen adaptation within the host often enhances persistence and virulence, reinforcing the notion that S. Typhi modulations observed here represent a conserved survival strategy.

As CeP0-ST and CeF1-ST appeared to be highly infective, we further wanted to study the modulations in the pathogen. SEM analysis revealed a reduction in bacterial size relative to WT-ST. Although motility typically underlies pathogenic spread (Zhang et al., 2022), CeP0-ST and CeF1-ST exhibited both diminished swimming/swarming activity and reduced expression of fliC, the gene encoding the flagellar filament (Baker et al., 2007). Similarly, LPS, which is a major component of the outer membrane composed of several antigenic structures, such as Lipid A and O antigen, which plays a major role in immune modulation (Kintz et al., 2017). Host survival improved following crude LPS supplementation, aligning with the observed downregulation of ompC and ompF. As the findings were in contrast with the increased killing ability, we further assessed the regulation of virulence systems such as T3SS-1 and Vi antigen system. The T3SS-1, which is encoded by Salmonella Pathogenicity Islands (SPI-1), is involved in facilitating the delivery of virulence factors into the host using effectors (Lou et al., 2019). While effector genes of T3SS-1, such as sipA, avrA and sopE, were also downregulated, we noted a significant upregulation in the expression of Vi capsular antigen biosynthesis genes such as tviD, tviA and viaB. The Vi antigen has been reported to be involved in assisting immune evasion from the host by masking antigenic outer membrane proteins and LPS (Wilson et al., 2008; Nagy and Pál, 2008). As, mutation in LPS and reduced flagellar expression is correlated with Vi antigen mediated inflammation suppression, the increase in the Vi antigen expression and parallel downregulation of flagella and LPS related genes suggest upon interaction with C. elegans, the pathogen might have enhanced its Vi antigen expression, thereby minimizing the immune detection of the LPS and other immunogenic molecules such as flagella (Crawford et al., 2013; Winter et al., 2015). Hence, the modulation in CeP0-ST and CeF1-ST probably facilitated the immune evasion, allowing the persistence of the pathogen inside the body of the host and enhancing the pathogenic potential. Consistent with this, recent work has shown that the Vi capsule actively suppresses autophagy in macrophages, thereby enhancing intracellular survival. Mutants lacking tviA or vexE exhibited increased LC3-II accumulation and p62 degradation, indicating higher autophagic flux, along with upregulation of Nod2 and Galectin-8, which are critical mediators of antibacterial autophagy (Zhang et al., 2025). This highlights that beyond masking outer membrane antigens, the Vi antigen manipulates host immune pathways to facilitate persistence.

Further, we studied the hypothesis using the S. Typhi Ty21a strain, which lacks a functional Vi antigen (Cryz Jr et al., 1989; Guzman et al., 2006; Sivamaruthi and Balamurugan, 2014). The Ty21a strains obtained post host interaction (CeP0-Ty21a, CeF1-Ty21a and CeF2-Ty21a) did not exhibit enhanced killing of C. elegans, which further supports and provides evidence of the bacterial adaptation and immune evasion aspect of the Vi antigen. However, the Ty21a strain also consists of additional mutations in addition to lacking the functional Vi antigen (Xu et al., 2013). Hence, further analyses using isogenic Vi capsule-deficient mutants would provide a detailed understanding into the mechanistic role of Vi capsule antigen in regulating the observed bacterial adaptation.

The adaptive bacterial modulation sustained upon two interaction passages with C. elegans and then lost when passaged further with growth media, we wanted to check if the modulation was dependent on the host's immune response. Hence, we have used three loss-of-function mutant strains pertaining to major immune regulatory pathways such as insulin signalling [akt-1 (−)], mTOR signalling [dlk-1 (−)] and TGF-β signalling pathway [sma-6 (−)]. CeP0-ST and CeF1-ST were obtained post S. Typhi infection of all the C. elegans mutant strains. Pathogens recovered from sma-6 mutants, defective in TGF-β signalling, showed attenuated infectivity and reduced Vi expression, indicating that signals from this pathway may serve as triggers for bacterial remodelling. The TGF-β signalling pathway is a conserved immune response mechanism that is involved in antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) and generating adaptive host response during bacterial infections in C. elegans (Madhu et al., 2023). This suggested that the enhanced adaptive Vi antigen expression might be a learning mechanism developed against the activated immune TGF-β signalling pathway and pathogen obtained after interaction with a host that lacks functional TGF-β signalling, might be less immune evasive and more immunogenic.

Enhanced Vi antigen modulation has been reported with masking of immunogenic OMPs and LPS, thereby ensuring evasion from the host immune response. As sma-6 (−) CeP0-ST and sma-6 (−) CeF1-ST were observed to be more immunogenic when compared with the wild type C. elegans interacted WT CeP0-ST and WT CeF1-ST, we sought to understand the role of pathogen detection factors in C. elegans, which are responsible for the first line of activation of an immune response. The expression of pathogen recognition genes in C. elegans, such as tol-1, clec-67, fshr-1 and npr-1 (Kamaladevi and Balamurugan, 2015; Radeke and Herman, 2020; Miller et al., 2015; Singh and Aballay, 2020; Yamamoto and Takeda, 2010), was assessed upon CeP0-ST and CeF1-ST infection of naïve C. elegans. The expression of the genes was significantly downregulated in WT CeP0-ST and WT CeF1-ST infected C. elegans, suggesting impaired immune detection, while their expression was comparatively higher in sma-6 (−) CeP0-ST and sma-6 (−) CeF1-ST infected C. elegans. Also, ROS is generated as a stress response mechanism to activate the downstream immune response to fight against the infection (Chávez et al., 2009). We observed reduced ROS levels and downregulation of sod-1 in WT CeP0-ST and WT CeF1-ST infected C. elegans, while they were observed to be higher in sma-6 (−) CeP0-ST and sma-6 (−) CeF1-ST infected C. elegans. Finally, we quantified the expression of immune regulatory players such as skn-1, sgk-1 and pmk-1, which were also downregulated in WT CeP0-ST and WT CeF1-ST infected C. elegans and expressed comparatively higher in sma-6 (−) CeP0-ST and sma-6 (−) CeF1-ST infected C. elegans.

Together, the findings indicate that enhanced Vi antigen expression in WT CeP0-ST and WT CeF1-ST could possibly assist the pathogen in evading the host recognition factors by masking their immunogenic elements. This evasion by the Vi antigen enhanced S. Typhi could further suppress the activation of the oxidative stress response, thereby inhibiting the activation of the host immune response, which assists the persistence of the pathogen, ensuring higher infectivity. This adaptive pathogenic mechanism appeared to be absent upon interaction with TGF-β mutant C. elegans, suggesting that the adaptive modulation in the pathogen might be dependent on host immune response. The ability of the pathogen to further vertically transmit across generations is a survival strategy, possibly contributing to the persistence in the host and possess an evolutionary advantage during host-pathogen interactions. Further studies are required to explore the role of molecular players involved in regulating the bidirectional communication between the host's TGF-β pathway and the pathogen's Vi capsular antigen machinery. This would help in understanding the intricate signals from the host, which could be taken up by the S. Typhi to alter its metabolism to generate an adaptive response inside the host.
