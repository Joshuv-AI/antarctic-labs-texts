Integrating political prioritisation into national surgical planning: a scoping review of surgical, obstetric and anaesthesia care in Cameroon
Authors: Lauren Agoubi, Melissa Carvalho, Sara Fewer, Rasheedat Oke, Brice Fabo, Leonid Daya, Fiona Obiezu, Janet Adeola, Boris A K Nteungue, Yannick Ekane, Alain Mballa Etoundi, Catherine Juillard
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSurgical diseases contribute substantially to death and disability in Cameroon. Strategic planning for surgical, obstetric and anaesthesia (SOA) care in low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs) requires consideration of the policy environment in addition to the issue severity. We aimed at the current landscape of SOA care in Cameroon, incorporating a framework for political prioritisation.MethodsA scoping review of published and grey literature was performed. Literature specific to Cameroon, published between 2010 and 2020 and written in either English or French, was included. Abstracts and full texts were screened for discussion of SOA policy context, care and delivery conditions, and issue characteristics. Data extraction and analysis were performed using the Shiffman and Smith framework for political prioritisation accounting for actors, ideas, political context and issue characteristics.Results121 articles were included. By specialty, 83 articles were specific to surgery, 45 to obstetrics and 6 to anaesthesia. Policy environment was discussed by 20% (n=25) articles; 30% (n=37) discussed actor power; 22% (n=27) discussed ideas in SOA care and 93% discussed issue characteristics. Core challenges to political prioritisation of SOA care in Cameroon are limited actor support, a lack of consensus definitions, gaps in capacity and a need for systematic data collection on surgical diseases. Policy opportunities include leveraging existing multilateral partnerships to unify SOA actors, conducting national assessments of SOA care capacity, formalisation of task shifting to build capacity, defining essential SOA procedures, including surgical care in future health coverage, and defining and including SOA benchmarks in strategic planning.ConclusionsIntegrating a framework for political prioritisation into a situational analysis of SOA care is critical to understanding an LMIC’s policy context and actors, in addition to issue severity. Such an approach can serve as a baseline for analysis in evidence-informed policy-making for SOA care, even in the absence of centralised, country-wide data.Keywords: Surgery, Health policy, Health systems

Introduction

Surgical system strengthening has emerged as a global health priority since 2015, with the passage of the World Health Assembly resolution 68.15 acknowledging surgical care as a critical public health investment.1 In the same year, the Lancet Commission on Global Surgery (LCoGS) published its landmark report calling for the development of National Surgical, Obstetric and Anaesthesia Plans (NSOAPs) as a means of improving surgical capacity and access in low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs).2 This report called attention to the fact that one-third of all global deaths are attributable to surgically treated diseases, yet safe, timely and affordable surgical care remains out of reach for over 93% of LMIC populations.3 4 Strengthening Surgical, Obstetric and Anaesthesia (SOA) care is increasingly a regional priority in Africa, highlighted by the Dakar declaration on equitable access to surgical care by 2030 and its regional action plan.5 NSOAPs are policy instruments that serve to formalise strategic planning, which can guide evidence-informed policy-making.6 To date, seven sub-Saharan African countries have either published or developed NSOAPs; however, adopting, financing and implementing these plans remains challenging in LMICs due to resource limitations for addressing existing burdens of disease as well as new outbreaks, as seen with the COVID-19 pandemic.7,9 Addressing barriers to NSOAP development requires that initial situational analyses assess country-specific capacity to prioritise adoption of surgical planning evidence into policy.8

In Cameroon, a central African country with a population of over 27 million, community-level data suggest a significant impact of surgical disease.10,12 Injuries and non-communicable diseases account for 11% and 35% of the country’s mortality, respectively.13 Cameroon is also estimated by the WHO to have a burden of trauma comparable to that of malaria.14 15 As such, assuring the provision of adequate emergency and essential surgical services is critically important to the Cameroonian Ministry of Public Health.16 17 Since 2011, there have been ongoing analyses of patterns of injury and violence requiring surgical intervention; however, despite national efforts to address SOA care, there remains a need for systematic assessment and development of surgical infrastructure.18,22 In order to inform strategic planning and identify priority areas for the development of an NSOAP, a situational analysis of Cameroon’s national surgical system is needed.

As a first step towards this goal, we seek to understand the current landscape of SOA care in Cameroon, as framed by its policy context. Using the Shiffman and Smith framework for political prioritisation, this scoping literature review examines published information on the organisation and delivery of SOA care in Cameroon, with respect to (1) the actors involved, (2) the perception and portrayal of SOA care, (3) the national policies shaping SOA care and (4) and the severity of the surgical disease burden.23 Together, these four elements provide evidence of the obstacles to advancing the national priority for SOA care in Cameroon, as well as highlight gaps in knowledge on crucial domains of the country’s surgical system. To date, situational analyses guiding national surgical planning have excluded indicators of political prioritisation and focused solely on severity within the LCoGS template.24,26 This review will not only help guide national surgical planning efforts and inform priority setting and stakeholder engagement for the development of an NSOAP for Cameroon, but also provide a generalisable model for other countries seeking to incorporate measures of political priority into situational analyses for strategic planning in SOA care.

Methods

A scoping literature review was performed targeting published scientific literature, grey literature and health policies in Cameroon contextualising SOA care. The review was conducted between June 2018 and June 2020 using the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines and principles (figure 1).27

Search strategy and screening

The search aimed to capture existing literature relating to Cameroon and SOA specialties or health policy creation. A full list of search terms for each database is included in online supplemental table 1. Searches were conducted in PubMed, OvidMEDLINE, CINAHL, EBSCO Global Health, EBSCO Africa-Wide Information, POPLine, EBSCO Health Policy Reference Center, PAIS International, World Bank e-Library Working Papers and Scopus. These databases were chosen in partnership with an institutional information scientist and purposefully included search engines with databases specific to African LMICs, such as EBSCO Africa-Wide Information and PAIS International.

Data collection and eligibility

Following the initial search, the titles were screened for duplicates using automation tools in Mendeley and Rayyan QCRI and manual review by one author (LA). The resulting titles and abstracts were then screened for eligibility by two authors (LA and MC). Full-text screening prior to analysis was completed by five authors (LA, MC, RO, FO and and JA). All full texts were reviewed by at least two authors for inclusion. All data extraction decisions were blinded between reviewers. Conflicts in data extraction were resolved by discussion between the relevant reviewers after the data extraction process was completed.

Included in the title screening were all articles in English and French published between 2010 and 2020. Abstracts and full-text documents were screened for discussion of (a) policy context, to include discussions of the political priority, health agenda, governance and financing contextualising SOA care

Articles were excluded if they were inaccessible across reviewers, did not discuss SOA care, did not include information specific to Cameroon or contained clinical findings without discussion of the broader context of SOA care delivery.

Analysis

Data collection for included articles was conducted simultaneous to full-text review, using a customised RedCap survey (see online supplemental file 2).

Data were both extracted and merged for analysis using the Shiffman and Smith framework. This framework was published in 2007 and posits that different types of power combine to influence collective action in a given context, particularly with respect to health policy. Since its publication, this framework has been used to analyse political prioritisation for maternal mortality, newborn survival, mental health and global surgery.23 Specifically, this framework accounts for the power of:

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct or reporting plans of this review.

Results

Our search produced 2850 unique sources. Title and abstract screening identified 402 sources for retrieval, 24 of which could not be accessed across reviewers (figure 1). Full-text screening yielded 121 articles for extraction and analysis (online supplemental table 2). 72 articles were based on data collected at the local level, 76% of which originated from the two largest cities in Cameroon (Yaoundé and Douala). Otherwise, 12 articles collected regional data and 22 collected district level data.

Data extraction revealed 83 articles relevant to surgery, 45 to obstetrics and 6 to anaesthesia. Some of these articles were pertinent to multiple categories across areas of extraction. Of the included studies, 55% focused on the prevention, diagnosis or management of a surgical disease process (n=67). Systems-level domains such as (1) care organisation, capacity and delivery or (2) training and education were discussed by 27% (n=33) and 5% (n=6) of articles, respectively. Health policy in SOA care was discussed explicitly by 9% (n=11) of studies reviewed. With respect to the Schiffman and Smith domains of political prioritisation in global health initiatives, 20% (n=25) of articles explored elements of policy environment, 30% (n=37) looked into aspects of actor power and 22% (n=27) characterised ideas of SOA care. Issue characteristics, such as strengths and gaps in SOA care, were addressed by 93% of included publications (n=114).

Policy environment

Although no singular event has galvanised political will around the prioritisation of surgical care in Cameroon, several examples of policy moments have emerged that have led to the prioritisation of disease-specific management strategies emerged (table 1). Multiple studies broadly cited the Millennium and Sustainable Development Goals as leading to the prioritisation of obstetric initiatives.28,32 Declines in several of Cameroon’s development indicators, such as its fall from 97th to 153rd on the Human Development Index over 1980–2014, as well as its stagnation in maternal mortality and skilled birth attendance in 2015 were noted as significant drivers in the implementation of performance-based financing (PBF) in 2011, where service-specific bonuses were awarded with a focus on maternal and child healthcare delivery.33 The high priority given to reducing maternal mortality within Cameroon’s health agenda was further highlighted in discussion of the regional implementation of obstetric kits in 2014.32 34 Two articles outlined the impact of the WHO 1998 Yamoussoukro Declaration on Buruli Ulcer (BU), which led to the creation of treatment centres in 2002. These centres were initially supported by international partnerships, but their effectiveness ultimately led to the development of the National Buruli Ulcer Control Program by the Ministry of Health (MoH) in 2004, demonstrating the critical link between using a policy window to facilitate national programme creation.35 36

Actor power

Institutions and organisations involved in SOA care, both internal and external, are summarised in table 2. Five studies explicitly examined the cohesion of the actors involved, including partnerships between professional societies, the government, aid agencies and academic institutions. The signing of an Memorandum of Understanding (MOU) between the MoH and Society of Gynecologists and Obstetricians in 2010 to facilitate programmatic training to boost provider competency in outlying regions served as an example of the success of collaborative priority setting.31 37 In the implementation of obstetric kits in Adamawa, Cacko et al found that integration of actors at every step of the policy process is crucial, when the implementation of obstetric kits met significant delays due to a lack of provider understanding, as they were not involved in the strategy development.32 An example of cohesion between international aid agencies and national actors was highlighted by Koki et al in their discussion of the partnership between the World Diabetes Foundation and the MoH National programme to fight blindness. The programme’s creation in 2007 increased the priority of eye care in Cameroon, which in turn led to increased funding from the WDF to provide diagnostic and treatment equipment to help fulfil the goals of the programme.38

Ideas

With respect to internal ideas and definitions, no source pointed to a single definition of SOA care in Cameroon. Internal definitions of essential, major and minor procedures varied in the literature.39,41 Chichom-Mefire et al provide a list of 15 surgical procedures which have been included in the competencies for all Cameroonian medical school graduates since 2014, though meeting these competencies is an ongoing effort; this paper is the only one reporting efforts made in Cameroon to systematically address task sharing and shifting in SOA care.42 Several studies pointed to internal agreement on certain aspects of surgical care, such as referral patterns and to the use of external guidelines for clinical care, such as the WHO’s guidelines for care of the injured and classification of BU.35 43 However, multiple authors called for the development of national guidelines adaptive to the Cameroonian circumstance in trauma, emergency general surgery, burn surgery and perioperative infection control.44,48

External ideas of SOA care in Cameroon were explored predominantly from the patient perspective. Authors discussed delays in clinical presentation as a function of varying acceptability of SOA care in Cameroon, tied to perceptions of the need for care and its cost.49,54 Kenu et al found that patient uncertainty of medical benefit and cultural beliefs can lead to decreased acceptability of surgical care in the case of early infant male circumcision.32 37 Alternatively, in their evaluation of factors associated with delayed surgical presentation in Mbingo Baptist Hospital, Long et al found that no patients reported shame, stigma, or religious beliefs as delays to seeking care.55 A later, interview-based study of patients seeking surgical consultation in the same healthcare facility found that of the 19.4% of patients who declined a recommended surgery, 76% of them cited cost as the most important factor, highlighting the nuanced nature of acceptability and its influences.56

Issue characteristics

Issue characteristics are detailed in table 3 according to the NSOAP strategy template proposed by the LCoGS. With respect to surgical infrastructure, no publicly available Cameroonian health facility registry was identified in the literature; the MoH reports 4034 health facilities in 2014, but does not specify which facilities have surgical capacity. Multiple studies provided examples of specialty-specific gaps in facility readiness, including lack of medications, diagnostic and therapeutic equipment, blood product access and advanced testing.3944 50 57,62 Themes of care access, delays and referral systems were prevalent. Though authors agreed on there being a well-defined referral system to guide care escalation, they noted a lack of reliable prehospital management, safe roads and equipped transport.40 48 58 63 64

Personnel shortages were described across the literature; however, the World Bank Specialist Surgical Workforce density in Cameroon has not been updated since 2013.65 Internal approaches to addressing personnel shortages with task-shifting were explored in the literature with respect to formalising metrics for adequate surgical training and exposure in medical school.40 42 66 While many studies explored surgical service delivery in Cameroon, none provided a comprehensive national assessment of surgical volume or perioperative outcomes. World Bank estimations of the number of surgical procedures performed in Cameroon were not available. Several local estimates were extracted from the literature (table 3).39 41 65 Institutional-level quality improvement and outcomes monitoring using hospital-based data were prevalent in the literature for specific populations such as obstetrics, maternal mortality and traumatic injury.28,3044 67 However, multiple authors note these sources likely result in substantial under-reporting and bias.22 Several studies found high rates of incomplete or missing data from medical records, limiting their interpretability.19 39 44 50 59 64 71

With respect to financing, World Bank estimates suggest that 57% and 39% of Cameroonians are at risk for catastrophic and impoverishing expenditure, respectively.65 The overwhelming theme in the literature is that surgical care is financed by patients directly and in cash, with limited insurance available.3237 38 40 41 43 50 54,56 61 62 71 Alternative financing structures were explored both at the administrative level with PBF and at the patient level with health vouchers or obstetric kits for pregnancy and childbirth, though these were not implemented at nationwide and were found to have mixed results.33 72

Discussion

This scoping review considered both the political prioritisation of SOA care within Cameroon, as well as indicators of issue severity. By combining the Shiffman and Smith framework with the LcoGS NSOAP template to guide data extraction, we found that it was feasible to derive substantial contextual information on SOA Care in Cameroon. The results of our analysis, while specific to Cameroon, demonstrate how a scoping review can be an invaluable tool for understanding an LMIC’s historical and current policy context, actors and issue severity, even in the absence of centralised, country-wide data. This strategy may be particularly relevant to researchers supporting local partners requesting resources for SOA care policy development. Ultimately, this review identified key challenges and opportunities for SOA care in Cameroon, which will further guide stakeholder assessments and priority setting (table 4).

Challenges

The political prioritisation of SOA care in Cameroon faces several challenges. Actor power within SOA care in Cameroon has the same limitations of global surgery actors in general—a heterogeneous group of actors, each with limited support and a fragmented grassroots network to build from.73 With respect to ideas, no consensus definitions of SOA care or essential procedures emerged from the literature, the importance of which has been demonstrated in positioning issues to command political support.7 23 Additionally, patient acceptability of SOA care, and particularly surgical care remains low, with multifactorial reasons.32 37 55 56 In terms of the political context, in the absence of a clear political window for SOA care as a whole in Cameroon, prioritisation of SOA care has lacked horizontal reach. Instead, the political prioritisation of SOA care mirrors vertical programmes by focusing on disease-specific strategies (ie, maternal health, blindness, early infant male circumcision, BU).31,34 With respect to issue characteristics, the gaps in infrastructure, workforce and capacity are profound and were discussed by 93% of the included articles (table 3). These gaps are heightened by a paucity of country-wide systematic data collection to prioritise and support evidence-informed interventions. Finally, the barriers to surgical care affordability were a recurring theme across the literature, profoundly impacting patient acceptance of and access to SOA care in Cameroon.

Opportunities

Despite these challenges, there is reason for optimism. While the identified actors represent a heterogeneous group, they also have a range of experiences with both national and multilateral partnerships (table 2). Additionally, though there was no consensus definition of SOA care or essential surgery, there was agreement throughout the literature on referral patterns and the need to increase surgical capacity at the district level, as well as broad acceptance of the utility of task-shifting to address workforce shortages.42 43 Finally, the financial toxicity of SOA care in Cameroon was the single most highlighted policy and access barrier discussed in the literature, with authors consistently advocating for the development of a public health insurance scheme that would include essential SOA care. A National Technical Working Group was created in 2015 to initiate discussions around the development of universal health coverage in Cameroon by 2030. Initial implementation of a reduced benefit package is planned that would include a subset of SOA care (obstetric, gynaecological, oncological and dialysis care).74 This represents a rare policy window to define and prioritise essential SOA services for provision under universal health coverage.

Our analysis has several limitations. Academic writing has an inherent publication bias, favouring topics that are perceived to be of greater interest to a western audience, the experiences of academic and tertiary centres, or areas well connected to NGOs. As such, a literature review has the potential to underrepresent issues of primary importance to certain Cameroonian contexts. Furthermore, our review of the literature included information from 2010 to the end of 2019, some of which may have been since updated. This time period also excludes the COVID-19 pandemic, which we expect profoundly impacted the systems providing SOA care in Cameroon. Finally, the data represented here reflect academic and grey literature, serving as a foundational step in strategy development; however, a full contextual understanding of SOA Care requires pairing with in-depth interviews and/or focus groups with Cameroonian stakeholders, which represent the next steps of this partnership.

Conclusions

This review provides a broad analysis of the political prioritisation of SOA care within a national system. Our approach expands on previous situational analyses using the LCoGS NSOAP template by accounting for contextual elements embedded in the Shiffman and Smith framework, in particular actor power, policy environment and ideas. Our findings illustrate the richness of published information available on an LMIC’s guiding institutions, internal definitions and historical experiences in SOA Care, serving as a baseline for analysis in evidence-informed policy-making. Further work will involve qualitative analysis of in-depth stakeholder interviews to provide a comprehensive situational analysis of Cameroonian SOA care.
