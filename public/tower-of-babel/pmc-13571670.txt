Sepsis, Patient Monitoring and Nursing Research
Authors: Sue Jordan
Published: 2026

A paper in this edition of the Journal of Multidisciplinary Healthcare1 warrants recognition by the clinical and research communities. This paper reports a successful nurse-led initiative to address an international problem: death from sepsis. Patients receiving the intervention were less likely to be admitted to intensive care or to die. A door-to needle time of under an hour was achieved for antibiotic administration, saving lives: every hour of delay increments septic shock mortality by 6–10%.2–4 As in other areas of patient monitoring, nurses were key to the success of the intervention.5

Despite its potential to improve care and save lives, as here, clinical nursing research is marginalised from many research funding schemes: for example, in the UK, clinical research funding is distributed via a Medical Research Council (MRC), but there is no equivalent for allied professions.6 One consequence has been that patient monitoring systems (National Early Warning Scores [NEWS/NEWS2] were introduced without evidence from randomised controlled trials (RCTs).7 Nurse-led RCTs identified that EWS improved recording of signs of clinical deterioration, but not nurses’ responses.8,9 Subsequently, NEWS/NEWS2 have been reported to be of limited value in managing patients with sepsis or suspected sepsis.10,11 Although NEWS may be the best available option,11 Xu et al1 needed additional instruments.

Xu et al (2026)1 should be supported to take their research forward, but will there be funding for clinical nursing research? If the initiative is adopted without the firm evidence of a RCT, success might be attributed to the enthusiasm of early adopters, and deemed unachievable in routine practice. Adoption without evidence from RCTs may highlight inadequacies, such as nurses’ knowledge or responses,12 much later, as with NEWS.

A barrier to be overcome is the physician dominance of clinical research.13 Biomedical research has delivered therapeutic advances, but patient monitoring is a lower priority.5 The current model of research has proved costly: opinion leaders estimate that ~85% of expenditure on biomedical research is wasted, due to non-completion or failure to publish in an accessible and useable form.14–17 Meanwhile, hospital admissions, mortality and litigation costs emanating from sepsis have increased.10,18 Is it time to divert research funding to nurse-led interventions that reduce delays in recognition and response to physiological disturbance?
