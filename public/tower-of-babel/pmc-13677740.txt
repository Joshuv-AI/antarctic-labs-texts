Learning stochasticity via a nonparametric approach to state-dependent noise estimation
Authors: G. Pillonetto, A. Giaretta, M. Bisiacco
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Modeling stochastic dynamical systems remains a central challenge across disciplines. Incomplete knowledge of nonlinear interactions and state-dependent fluctuations often renders bottom-up approaches ineffective, motivating methods that infer governing equations directly from data. However, parametric models struggle in the absence of strong prior assumptions, particularly when the intensity of the process noise depends on the system state. Here we introduce Trine (Three-phase Regression for INferred noisE), a nonparametric, kernel-based framework for inferring state-dependent noise from time-series data. The approach naturally extends to supervised learning settings with heteroskedastic noise. Trine employs a three-stage algorithm combining analytically solvable subproblems with a structured kernel architecture capable of capturing both abrupt stochastic fluctuations and smooth variations in variance. We validate Trine on biological and ecological systems, where it uncovers hidden dynamics without predefined parametric assumptions. Across benchmark systems, Trine achieves near-oracle performance, matching an idealized observer with direct access to the stochastic input realizations. On live-cell RNA transcription trajectories, Trine reveals localized regimes of elevated stochastic activity, demonstrating how effective state-dependent diffusion models can uncover biologically meaningful hidden variability directly from experimental observations. The Trine framework thus opens new avenues for quantifying how process noise shapes the behavior of complex dynamical systems.

Introduction

Many dynamical systems across science are inherently stochastic1. Random effects arising from several microscopic interactions or environmental variability are often represented in aggregated form as process noise within state-space models. Such descriptions play a central role in climate modeling2,3, financial volatility modeling4, and robotic state estimation5. In biological systems, stochasticity arises from biochemical reactions and is often referred to as intrinsic noise6–8, whose macroscopic effects are captured by the process noise. Fluctuations in gene expression generate phenotypic variability among genetically identical cells7,8. This variability contributes to differentiation and tissue homeostasis9,10, immune activation11, microbial survival strategies12, and drug resistance13. It also shapes population dynamics14,15 and spike-timing variability in excitable systems, such as neurons16,17.

Despite its ubiquity, process noise—especially when state-dependent—is notoriously difficult to quantify experimentally and computationally. In biological contexts, for example, biochemical networks are often expressed as continuous-time Markov processes governed by the chemical master equation18. While these descriptions offer mechanistic insight, they typically require detailed knowledge of interaction structures, reaction pathways, and noise parametrizations that are rarely accessible in practice. Consequently, data-driven system identification methods19–21 aim to reconstruct governing equations directly from time-series observations. However, most existing approaches rely on parametric assumptions, for example, based on Bayesian state-space frameworks and diffusion estimation methods for stochastic differential equations (SDEs)22,23. A limitation of these approaches is that they may struggle to capture complex nonlinear stochastic dynamics—particularly when the intensity of the process noise depends on the system state—without strong prior structural knowledge. In addition, some of these methods rely on computationally intensive inference procedures, such as Markov chain Monte Carlo or particle filtering24,25. This challenge is especially pronounced in biological systems, where stochasticity is often a critical component of function rather than a mere nuisance. The combination of ill-posedness, limited data, and the need for nonparametric methods renders model inference difficult—yet all the more important.

The problem is closely related to heteroskedastic noise modeling in machine learning26,27. In this field, common noise inference strategies assume that the log-variance of stochastic effects is an unknown function of the system state. This function is typically estimated either by parameterizing it with a neural network or by modeling its smooth profile as a realization of a Gaussian process 28–31. The latter approach, related to kernel-based methods, usually relies on approximate inference techniques to achieve computational tractability32–36. These nonparametric heteroskedastic models often do not explicitly distinguish measurement noise—i.e., errors introduced by the experimental procedures used to observe the system—from the intrinsic stochasticity generated by the system’s internal dynamics. While such models can, in principle, incorporate additional measurement noise terms, their robustness to measurement noise has not been extensively investigated. In particular, when flexible variance models—such as log-normal Gaussian-process priors—are adopted, the resulting variance estimates may become sensitive to observational noise and difficult to disentangle from measurement effects. This motivates the need for methods that leverage the flexibility of nonparametric inference while simultaneously providing robust and interpretable estimates of process noise.

Here, we present Trine (Three-phase Regression for INferred noisE), a framework capable of nonparametrically recovering state-dependent process noise from time-series data. In contrast to approaches based on parametric state-space models, the proposed framework directly estimates state-dependent noise from data without requiring predefined functional forms, complex stochastic simulation schemes, or high-dimensional nonconvex optimization problems. Trine is based on a modular, three-stage regression algorithm based on structured kernel estimators. Each stage uses linear estimators with analytical solutions, favoring parsimonious functional forms in cases where unique solutions do not exist37,38. A key component is a custom-designed kernel that captures the dependence of noise statistics on the system state, as well as the irregularities and discontinuities characteristic of stochastic fluctuations in dynamical systems. This is made possible by an initial step that estimates the signs of effective process-noise increments, enabling the use of a structured kernel in the subsequent regression stage. Intuitively, conditioning on the signs allows the underlying state-dependent variability to be more effectively exploited, as correlations across nearby states can then be captured through kernel regression.

We demonstrate Trine on several case studies—including ecological population dynamics39, gene regulatory networks15,40, and spiking neurons modeled by the FitzHugh–Nagumo system16—showing that it accurately reconstructs state-dependent stochastic structure without requiring parametric assumptions or prior system knowledge. We further apply Trine to live-cell RNA transcription trajectories, where the inferred diffusion structure reveals localized regimes of elevated stochastic activity directly from experimental observations, providing an effective coarse-grained representation of hidden transcriptional variability. We show that Trine not only outperforms established learning approaches, but also achieves performance comparable to oracle estimators that have access to the true realizations of process noise. Such information is fundamentally unavailable in realistic experimental settings. Beyond dynamical systems, the framework naturally extends to general supervised learning problems with input-dependent (heteroskedastic) noise, where the noise variance depends on the input locations at which the unknown function is observed. This is demonstrated on the real-world Abalone age prediction problem41,42, where Trine delivers accurate predictions together with well-calibrated uncertainty. Overall, these results highlight the method’s ability to recover latent stochastic structure directly from observational data, effectively bridging the gap between theoretical stochastic modeling and experimental observability.

The two central functions to be estimated from a finite set of noisy data are the deterministic drift f and the noise strength g. This estimation task is challenging due to the nonlinear structure of these functions. Additionally, g appears within a stochastic term in the dynamics, further complicating the problem. As a result, the problem is fundamentally ill-posed: infinitely many combinations of f and g can explain the observed data equally well, particularly in noisy settings. A common strategy for regularization is to adopt a parametric formulation, reducing the problem to estimating a finite number of parameters within predefined functional families. While this improves tractability, it imposes strong prior assumptions on the model structure, which are often difficult to justify in settings with poorly understood or highly nonlinear mechanisms.

The Trine algorithm

To estimate the components of this system from data, we adopt a nonparametric approach based on kernel methods—a flexible and principled tool that regularizes the estimation problem without imposing explicit functional forms29. This means that pairs of states that are “close” according to some metric tend to have similar values of the unknown function, i.e., f(xa) ≈ f(xb). For example, if \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\mathcal{K}}}$$\end{document}K is continuous, any function drawn from the associated model will also be continuous. Defining a kernel implicitly determines a potentially infinite set of basis functions—specifically, the kernel sections—that span the function space used for estimation. This construction not only provides great flexibility in approximating complex functions but also induces an implicit regularization on the expansion coefficients. This can be interpreted probabilistically: the kernel defines the covariance of a Gaussian process prior over functions, favoring those with higher prior probability (e.g., smoother if the kernel is regular)28. This naturally promotes simpler functional forms when multiple solutions fit the data equally well, thus helping to avoid overfitting without explicitly restricting the model class.

A widely used kernel to encode smoothness is the Gaussian kernel, defined as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\mathcal{K}}}({x}_{a},{x}_{b})=\lambda \exp \left(-\frac{\parallel {x}_{a}-{x}_{b}{\parallel }^{2}}{2\ell }\right),$$\end{document}K(xa,xb)=λexp−∥xa−xb∥22ℓ, where λ > 0 and ℓ > 0 are hyperparameters controlling the amplitude and smoothness of the estimate. These parameters are typically learned from data. Gaussian kernels are universal, in the sense that they can approximate any continuous function arbitrarily well43, and are particularly effective when the target function is expected to be smooth. In the Trine method, the Gaussian kernel is employed directly in some stages and also serves as a foundation for designing custom kernels in others, as described below.

Trine is a three-stage estimator designed to reconstruct not only the drift f and the noise variance defined by g, but also the process noise realizations g ⊙ w, which are recovered in the middle stage of the algorithm. Crucially, this intermediate quantity—the process noise—is not merely a byproduct of the model: it is a central component of the algorithm. From a modeling perspective, it provides direct insight into the stochastic mechanisms driving the system, which is key in domains where noise itself plays a functional role. From a computational perspective, estimating g ⊙ w is instrumental, as it enables the subsequent inference of the state-dependent variance.

The algorithm is graphically depicted in Fig. 1, which illustrates how this three-phase framework enables the separate and interpretable modeling of the structure and scale of the process noise. The modular steps of Trine can be briefly described as follows. First, the drift function f is estimated using Gaussian process regression (GPR) with a Gaussian kernel (see the upper blue box and smooth kernel curve in Fig. 1). A simplified process noise model with constant variance is assumed at this stage, and subsequently refined in the following steps. From the residuals of this step, the signs of the effective process-noise increments are inferred, capturing their directional component (see the red/blue sign estimates below the upper blue box in Fig. 1). Second, a specialized GPR model with a newly introduced structured, discontinuous kernel reconstructs the process noise realizations (see the middle orange box and discontinuous kernel curve in Fig. 1). This step leverages the sign estimates while preserving the smooth structure of the variance. Third, the absolute residuals from the previous step are appropriately scaled and used as input to a final GPR with a Gaussian kernel to estimate the state-dependent process noise standard deviation (SD) (see the bottom green box and smooth kernel curve above the final noise variance profile in Fig. 1). Results

Population dynamics: Ricker model with Allee effect

The Ricker model is a classic discrete-time population model developed for fish stock dynamics39. Like the logistic map, it describes density-dependent growth, but with an exponentially decreasing per-capita rate, making it suitable for species with strong overcompensation, where populations drop sharply after exceeding carrying capacity. Its rich nonlinear behavior has made it a standard tool for studying chaos, bifurcations, and noise effects in ecological systems44. Recent developments include stochastic competition models45, stock-recruitment models with environmental covariates46, and theoretical analyses47.

In this discrete-time setting, the function f represents the deterministic population update rather than a continuous-time drift. In our example, this function also incorporates an Allee effect: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f({x}_{k})={x}_{k}^{2}\,{e}^{r(1-{x}_{k})},$$\end{document}f(xk)=xk2er(1−xk), which introduces a positive relationship between population size and growth at low densities. Populations below a critical threshold then face negative growth, increasing extinction risk15. Incorporating the Allee effect enables the study of bistability between extinction and survival, especially under stochastic influences.

FitzHugh–Nagumo model with stochastic noise

The FitzHugh–Nagumo (FHN) model is a paradigmatic reduction of the Hodgkin–Huxley equations49, designed to capture the essential dynamical features of excitable systems, such as neurons and cardiac cells. By simplifying the high-dimensional biophysical description into a two-dimensional system, the FHN model provides a tractable framework for studying excitability, oscillations, and wave propagation in both single cells and extended media16,50. Its minimal formulation, consisting of a fast activator variable V(t) and a slower recovery variable W(t), has made it one of the most widely used models for theoretical and computational investigations of excitable dynamics. In a neuronal context, V corresponds to the membrane potential, which exhibits rapid, nonlinear excursions during action potentials, while W accounts for slower recovery mechanisms, such as sodium channel inactivation and potassium activation, that return the system to rest.

Abalone age prediction with uncertainty quantification

We now consider a real-data application involving abalone age prediction, a problem of major relevance for fisheries and aquaculture management42. Reliable age estimation is important for assessing growth dynamics, harvest timing, and population structure, making this setting a useful test for evaluating the ability of the proposed framework to recover meaningful heteroscedastic structure and calibrated uncertainty from real biological data.

Abalone growth modeling has been widely studied using statistical and machine learning approaches, including ensemble methods for aquaculture systems56 and Bayesian kernel regression models42, primarily aiming to improve growth prediction and management strategies.

In practice, biological age is inferred from the number of shell growth rings (Rings), obtained through shell sectioning and manual inspection. Because this procedure is destructive, labor-intensive, and affected by measurement variability, predictive models based on routinely collected morphometric measurements have received considerable attention.

We consider the UCI Abalone dataset41, a benchmark for supervised regression, where the objective is to predict Rings from physical measurements alone. Several studies have addressed this task using machine learning techniques, including artificial neural networks57,58, primarily focusing on point prediction accuracy. Here, we extend this setting by jointly modeling the conditional mean and the input-dependent variance of age, thereby explicitly capturing heteroscedastic uncertainty. Beyond predictive accuracy, we assess the statistical reliability of prediction intervals through repeated Monte Carlo validation, evaluating out-of-sample calibration and verifying whether nominal coverage levels are achieved in practice.

The dataset comprises N = 4177 independent observations. Let x denote the input vector containing the morphometric measurements Length, Diameter, Height, WholeWeight, ShuckedWeight, VisceraWeight, and ShellWeight. The response variable z is Rings, which serves as a proxy for biological age (approximately Rings + 1.5 years) and ranges from 1 to 29 in the dataset. Although several weight-related variables are included, no deterministic relationship links them; for example, WholeWeight is not the exact sum of the other weight components. The predictors, therefore, provide partially overlapping but non-redundant information about external morphology.

The observations are not temporally ordered and exhibit no dynamic dependence; the dataset consists of independent supervised pairs (xk, zk). This setting poses no restriction for Trine, which applies to general supervised learning problems and naturally accommodates heteroscedastic regression models (see “Methods”). We therefore jointly estimate the conditional mean function f(x) and the input-dependent standard deviation function g(x) from the observed morphometric–age pairs (xk, zk). We adopt the heteroscedastic regression model 5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${z}_{k}=f({x}_{k})+g({x}_{k}){w}_{k},$$\end{document}zk=f(xk)+g(xk)wk, where wk is white Gaussian noise of unit variance. This formulation separates deterministic growth dynamics, captured by f, from state-dependent stochastic variability, modeled by g. In particular, it enables a direct characterization of how biological variability changes across the input space, which cannot be captured by standard homoscedastic models. Biologically, this reflects the intuition that variability increases with age: older abalones, typically associated with larger morphometric measurements, exhibit greater dispersion due to cumulative environmental exposure and inter-individual heterogeneity.

Gene regulatory networks

Self promoter

The self-promoter is a canonical stochastic gene regulatory model in systems biology, where a gene activates its own transcription through positive feedback. This simple architecture can generate bistability, hysteresis, and noise-induced switching, providing a minimal framework to study how intrinsic noise shapes phenotypic variability59–61. Positive autoregulation is widespread: it drives the lysis-lysogeny decision in bacteriophage λ62, has been engineered in synthetic circuits to demonstrate robust bistable switching in both E. coli40 and yeast63, and is a recurrent strategy in virology. For instance, the HIV Tat loop produces bimodal dynamics that control latency versus replication64, while HPV early genes sustain their own promoter activity to stabilize early genes programs65,66. Beyond viruses, self-promoting loops also appear in mammalian regulation, e.g., in c-Myc and p53 feedback, reinforcing proliferation or stabilizing cell-fate decisions. Thus, the self-promoter model provides a minimal yet generalizable framework to explore how feedback and noise jointly govern cellular decision-making.

Mutual repressor: toggle switch

The mutual repressor, or genetic toggle switch (Fig. 6a), is a minimal circuit where two genes inhibit each other, generating bistability and mutually exclusive expression states. Its first synthetic implementation in E. coli67 was a milestone in synthetic biology, later formalized in the stochastic setting to show how process noise drives switching60. Since then, the toggle switch has become a paradigmatic system biology motif for studying stochastic switching, hysteresis, multistability, and nonlinear regulation59,68,69. Natural analogues include the PU.1-GATA1 antagonism in hematopoietic lineage choice70 and the miR-200/ZEB feedback in EMT71. Recent studies have extended this picture: synthetic toggles have been used to probe spatiotemporal dynamics in E. coli72, while Jagged-Delta asymmetry in notch signaling generates hybrid sender/receiver states through toggle-like dynamics73. More broadly, mutual repression motifs are increasingly viewed as modular building blocks for complex architectures, capable of producing oscillations, multistability, and spatial patterning74,75. Inference of coarse-grained stochastic dynamics from live-cell RNA trajectories

We next consider a real biological application involving experimentally measured RNA transcription dynamics in living cells. Specifically, we analyze 400 temporal trajectories from the study of ref. 77, obtained through live-cell single-molecule imaging experiments tracking transcriptional bursting and stochastic splice-site selection in human cells. The measured time series quantify the temporal evolution of RNA production in individual cells. They consist of short, highly heterogeneous, and strongly stochastic trajectories containing, on average, almost 370 samples (Fig. 1C in ref. 77).

These data define a particularly challenging identification problem because the observed RNA trajectories provide access to only a partial observation of the underlying biological dynamics, while the system itself is governed by multiple latent molecular processes, including promoter switching, transcriptional bursting, spliceosome dynamics, and recursive splicing. To explain these phenomena77, introduced a coarse-grained mechanistic hidden-state framework based on stochastic transitions among latent transcriptional and splicing states. Their approach further integrates multiple complementary molecular assays, including smFISH, PAR-CLIP, lariat sequencing, and nascent RNA sequencing, in order to constrain the underlying latent architectures. However, explicitly reconstructing such hidden switching mechanisms only from short noisy trajectories remains intrinsically difficult and potentially weakly identifiable, since distinct latent dynamical organizations may generate very similar observable statistics.

Rather than attempting to recover the full hidden microscopic architecture, we therefore adopt a complementary perspective and directly infer an effective stochastic representation of the observable dynamics from the measured trajectories alone. The key idea is that, although the underlying molecular switching mechanisms are not explicitly reconstructed, their aggregate dynamical effect can still emerge as a state-dependent stochastic process acting on the observed RNA signal. From this viewpoint, Trine can be interpreted as a data-driven coarse-graining framework that learns effective mesoscopic dynamics directly from partially observed biological trajectories.

Comparison with other approaches in simulated studies

To benchmark Trine, we compared it with three alternative estimators using extensive Monte Carlo simulations across four systems presented earlier.

The first comparator is an Oracle estimator. In statistics, an “oracle” refers to a method that has access to privileged information unavailable in practice, providing a theoretical performance upper bound. Here, we define the oracle as having access to the true realizations of the process noise. In biological terms, this corresponds to an idealized observer that can perfectly track the realizations of the coarse-grained intrinsic noise driving the system dynamics, originating from random fluctuations in molecular concentrations or reaction events and not directly observable in real experiments. The oracle thus obtains noiseless measurements of g(x)w(t) in Eq. 1 and estimates the noise profile using Trine’s third stage, providing a strong operational upper bound on the performance achievable by any data-driven method.

The second comparator is a classical literature method based on a log-normal Gaussian process prior, commonly used to model state-dependent variance30 and previously applied to the Abalone dataset. Specifically, we again consider Most Likely Heteroscedastic Gaussian Process regression (MLH-GP)33, which iteratively estimates the mean function (deterministic system part) and the state-dependent variance profile. This serves as a well-established baseline for nonparametric modeling of state-dependent noise, as demonstrated by its continued study and applications in recent years79–82. However, like other heteroscedastic noise methods, it does not explicitly account for observational noise. We therefore implemented an extended version incorporating known measurement noise variances during mean and variance estimation.

The third comparator is denoted by Trineu, a Trine variant where the third stage—estimating the process noise variance—relies directly on process noise estimates from the GP model in the first stage, bypassing the structured kernel of the second stage (the superscript u stands for unstructured). This comparison highlights the value of the custom-designed kernel and quantifies the resulting performance gains.

For each of the four systems, we performed a Monte Carlo study with 2000 runs. In each run, the measurement noise e in Eq. 2 varied, covering scenarios from very low to high noise. In these experiments, the variance of the measurement noise was assumed to be known and provided to all estimators. Trine robustness and limitations

To assess the robustness of the proposed framework, we performed additional Monte Carlo experiments focusing on the toggle switch system, which represents the most challenging scenario considered in this work due to its multidimensional state, bistability, and highly structured state-dependent diffusion. Extensive simulations performed on the remaining systems (Ricker, self-promoter, and FitzHugh-Nagumo) led to qualitatively identical conclusions.

Reduced data regime

In this experiment, the number of available observations was halved, reducing the dataset from N = 1000 to N = 500 samples while keeping all other simulation settings unchanged, including knowledge of measurement noise statistics. We then repeated the Monte Carlo study using the same four estimators considered in the main comparison: Oracle, Trine, Trineu, and MLH-GP. Each result reported below is obtained from 2000 independent Monte Carlo runs. Table 2b summarizes the resulting Fit scores. To provide a comprehensive characterization of performance under limited data, we report the mean, the median and the interquartile range (25% and 75% quantiles). The mean captures overall estimation accuracy, while the median and quantiles provide robust indicators that are less sensitive to occasional estimation failures, which become more frequent in low-sample regimes.

Reducing the dataset to N = 500 decreases the coverage of the two-dimensional state-space where the term regulating the diffusion must be estimated, making the identification problem significantly more challenging. Despite this reduced coverage, Trine remains highly stable. In particular, it achieves a mean Fit of 83.1% and a median Fit of 87.6%, remaining relatively close to the Oracle benchmark (mean 90.1%, median 90.5%). By contrast, both MLH-GP and the unstructured Trineu variant exhibit a marked degradation in performance, with a substantial number of low-Fit realizations and substantially lower median values. Additional dataset sizes were also investigated in order to assess the behavior of the estimators under progressively more severe data limitations. For brevity, only Trine and the Oracle benchmark are reported, with the corresponding mean fit values summarized in Table 2c. As expected, performance decreases as the amount of available data is reduced. Nevertheless, Trine remains relatively close to the Oracle benchmark.

These results highlight that, in low-data regimes, the structured kernel introduced in the second stage of Trine provides an effective form of regularization that compensates for the reduced exploration of the state-space. As a consequence, Trine maintains reliable estimates even when the available trajectories do not densely cover the domain where the state-dependent variance must be learned.

Increased sampling step

We next examine the effect of increasing the observation sampling interval Δt. In the simulations described in the Methods section, trajectories of the stochastic system are generated using a fine Euler–Maruyama integration step and subsequently sampled at the observation interval Δt. In the main experiments, the Toggle switch system was analyzed using Δt = 0.01. Increasing Δt, therefore, corresponds to observing the system at progressively lower temporal resolution.

When Δt becomes large, the system may traverse regions of the state-space where the statistics of the stochastic forcing vary substantially, potentially violating the local stochastic approximation underlying the identification procedure. To assess whether a given sampling interval remains compatible with this approximation, we introduce a diagnostic quantity measuring the variation of the diffusion structure along the system evolution.

Considering the stochastic system Eq. 1, the associated deterministic dynamics are obtained by removing the stochastic forcing and solving \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\dot{x}=f(x)$$\end{document}x°=f(x). Let Qij(x) denote the (i, j)-th entry of the local covariance matrix of the process noise. We then define, for each state component i, the dimensionless diffusion–variation index 11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\Lambda }_{i}(x,{\Delta }_{t})=\frac{\left\vert {Q}_{ii}\left({f}_{{\Delta }_{t}}(x)\right)-{Q}_{ii}(x)\right\vert }{{Q}_{ii}(x)},$$\end{document}Λi(x,Δt)=QiifΔt(x)−Qii(x)Qii(x), provided that Qii(x) > 0. Small values indicate that the diffusion structure remains approximately constant, whereas values of order one indicate that the system traverses regions in which the noise statistics vary markedly within a single observation interval.

When evaluated along stochastic trajectories, Λi(xt, Δt) becomes a random variable. We therefore estimated its expectation by means of a Monte Carlo study of the Toggle switch system, averaging realizations of Λ1 along simulated trajectories after the dynamics had reached stationarity. Setting the sampling interval to Δt = 0.1 yields a mean value of about 0.1, with a 95% quantile around 0.6. For Δt = 0.2, the mean increases to nearly 0.3, while the 95% quantile rises to about 1.5, indicating that the system begins to traverse regions where the noise statistics change appreciably within a single observation step. This suggests that such a regime lies close to the limit of validity of the local stochastic approximation.

Based on this diagnostic, we repeated the Monte Carlo identification study for the Toggle Switch system using Δt = 0.2, in place of Δt = 0.01, with N = 1000 observations. In this setting, the oracle residuals correspond to the stochastic contribution accumulated between t and t + Δt, computed as the difference between the true state at t + Δt and the deterministic coarse-step prediction obtained by evolving the deterministic system from the true state at time t. The oracle, therefore, represents the best achievable performance under this coarse-grained stochastic description. The resulting Fit statistics, obtained from 2000 independent Monte Carlo runs, are reported in Table 2d. Despite the substantially coarser temporal resolution, Trine remains essentially indistinguishable from the oracle benchmark, whereas MLH-GP exhibits a more pronounced degradation in performance, and Trineu remains consistently below the structured Trine estimator.

Unknown measurement noise variance

We next evaluate robustness when the measurement noise variance is unknown and must be estimated from data. In this more realistic setting, measurement noise is assumed state-dependent and proportional to the magnitude of the observed state. Accordingly, the measurement noise covariance is modeled as a diagonal matrix with entries \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\xi {y}_{k}^{2}$$\end{document}ξyk2, i.e., proportional to the squared state measurements through a single unknown scaling factor. This setting is naturally accommodated within Trine, where parameters of the structured kernel describing the process noise realizations can be estimated jointly with ξ (see “Methods”). For comparison, we also implemented variants of Trineu and MLH-GP that address this setting by treating ξ as an additional hyperparameter to be estimated within the optimization subproblems defining their respective architectures. The oracle baseline follows the same implementation as in the previous setting, as it operates in an ideal regime where measurement noise is absent, and the realizations of the process noise are directly observable.

We performed Monte Carlo studies on the toggle switch system using the full dataset (N = 1000), with 2000 independent runs in each case. Table 2e reports the resulting fit statistics. Compared with Table 2a, Trine maintains high accuracy even when the measurement noise variance is estimated rather than assumed known, outperforming both Trineu and MLH-GP.

Increasing measurement noise levels

We build upon the Monte Carlo study presented in the main comparison analysis by concentrating on the toggle switch system and disaggregating the corresponding fit values reported in Fig. 8 according to the measurement-to-process noise ratio. While the previous analysis considered noise ratios up to 0.4, the present study extends this range to substantially higher levels and examines performance explicitly as a function of increasing measurement noise intensity. The goal of this experiment is to identify the operating limits of the proposed method under progressively degraded signal-to-noise conditions.

We generate datasets with N = 1000 observations. In each Monte Carlo run, independent realizations of both process and measurement noise are generated. The measurement noise standard deviation is set as a varying fraction of the true state magnitude, thereby producing runs with different effective signal-to-noise ratios. The measurement noise variance is assumed known, as in the main comparison study. For each run, we compute the ratio between the norm of the measurement noise realization and the norm of the process noise realization. This ratio provides an empirical proxy for the effective signal-to-noise ratio governing the identifiability of the diffusion component. Runs are grouped into bins according to this ratio, and within each bin, we compute the average Fit between the estimated and true process noise standard deviation profiles. Results are obtained from 10,000 independent Monte Carlo runs, with at least 1000 runs contributing to each bin.

Table 2f summarizes the results. When measurement noise remains moderate relative to process noise fluctuations, Trine achieves performance close to the Oracle benchmark and markedly outperforms the unstructured variant Trineu. In this regime, the sign information estimated in the first stage remains statistically reliable, allowing the structured kernel in the second stage to effectively regularize and recover the state-dependent diffusion profile. As measurement noise increases and progressively masks stochastic fluctuations, performance degrades. This behavior reflects an identifiability limitation: when measurement noise becomes comparable to or larger than the process noise, recovering process noise realizations from observations becomes statistically unstable. In this regime, the sign estimates exploited by the structured kernel lose reliability, reducing the benefit of second-stage regularization. Consequently, the unstructured variant Trineu, which does not rely on sign information, can become comparatively more stable at very high noise levels. This transition occurs gradually with the deterioration of the effective signal-to-noise ratio. In practice, its onset can be detected by comparing the process noise variance estimated by Trine in the first stage with the measurement noise variance. When these quantities become comparable, the structured estimator approaches its reliability threshold, providing a simple diagnostic criterion for assessing whether the structured kernel is expected to operate within its identifiable regime.

Overall, these results delineate the operating conditions of the proposed framework. Trine performs near-oracle when process noise remains sufficiently observable relative to measurement noise. In regimes where intrinsic variability is largely obscured by observational noise, reliable separation of these two components becomes statistically challenging without stronger structural assumptions.

Discussion

Trine provides a nonparametric framework for disentangling process noise in stochastic dynamical systems. By integrating nonparametric kernel methods within a modular three-phase architecture, Trine addresses the core challenge of estimating state-dependent noise profiles without imposing restrictive parametric assumptions, while remaining robust under limited or noisy data. Beyond accurate estimation, Trine provides a clear and interpretable representation of the stochastic mechanisms shaping system dynamics. This aspect is particularly relevant in biological and ecological contexts, where noise is not merely a perturbation but often a functional component of the system. In this sense, Trine is not only a predictive tool but also a methodological instrument for discovery, revealing which dynamical variables modulate stochasticity, and uncovering symmetries or nonlinear dependencies that would otherwise remain hidden beneath measurement noise.

More broadly, the problem addressed by Trine lies at the intersection of statistics and machine learning, where heteroscedastic noise modeling arises in numerous scientific applications. Several approaches have been proposed to estimate input-dependent variance functions. They include parametric models and moment-based methods, often developed in the context of SDEs for diffusion estimation, including cases with measurement noise22,23,83,84. A large class of alternative methods, including Trine, rely on the Gaussian process framework. In this work, we focused on comparison with Most Likely Heteroscedastic Gaussian Process regression (MLH-GP)33, extended to account for measurement noise, as it constitutes a strong and well-established baseline with comparable computational complexity. Like Trine, MLH-GP decomposes the inference problem into kernel-based subproblems governed by a limited number of hyperparameters learned from data.

Other nonparametric approaches could, in principle, be considered. From a theoretical perspective, a fully Bayesian formulation solved via stochastic simulation, e.g., MCMC and particle filters24,25, would, in principle, provide fully Bayesian posterior estimates of both mean and variance functions. Variational Bayesian alternatives could also be employed to approximate the posterior distribution23,85. However, these methods operate at a fundamentally different computational scale. MCMC implementations require carefully designed Markov chains whose convergence to the target posterior can be challenging to ensure and difficult to diagnose. Variational approaches in our nonparametric setting introduce a number of latent variables that typically scale with the dataset size32, leading to more complex optimization problems. Moreover, the extensive Monte Carlo studies conducted in this work—comprising tens of thousands of runs—would be computationally infeasible under such inference schemes. For these reasons, rather than introducing a computationally prohibitive comparator, we adopted instead an oracle benchmark, which serves as a theoretical upper bound on attainable performance.

The oracle estimator can be interpreted as an idealized observer capable of directly measuring process noise realizations without error, thereby accessing information that is fundamentally unavailable in real experiments. Despite being entirely data-driven and lacking any privileged information, Trine achieves performance close to this oracle across a wide range of scenarios. This result narrows the gap between theoretical stochastic modeling and experimental observability. The structured kernel introduced in Trine’s second phase is essential for this performance, as it simultaneously enforces smooth state-dependent structure in the variance profile while allowing discontinuities in noise realizations.

The strong performance of Trine when measurement noise does not dominate process noise is not accidental but rooted in a precise statistical mechanism. The sign-estimation step in the first phase plays a crucial role. Importantly, this phase only needs to infer the signs of the process noise realizations, which is statistically much easier than estimating their amplitudes. In simulations with moderate measurement noise, the proportion of correctly estimated signs is high. As discussed in the Supplementary Material, in a classical supervised-learning setting with heteroskedastic noise—such as the Abalone data-set example—conditioning on correct sign information allows the second stage to recover the best possible joint linear estimate of the drift and process noise realizations under the assumed smoothness prior. This property is not shared by MLH-GP, which alternates between mean and variance profile updates without jointly reconstructing the underlying deterministic and stochastic structure, nor by the unstructured Trine variant denoted by Trineu. The main limitation of the framework emerges when measurement noise becomes comparable to or larger than process noise. In this regime, measurement noise begins to mask stochastic fluctuations, degrading the reliability of sign estimation in the first phase. As a consequence, the benefits of the structured second-stage regularization diminish and estimation accuracy deteriorates, as observed in the high-noise Monte Carlo experiments.

Across benchmark systems, Trine consistently uncovered biologically meaningful stochastic structures. In the genetic toggle switch, it identified regions of the state-space most sensitive to noise-driven transitions, illustrating how stochastic fluctuations shape the landscape of cell-fate decisions. Similar insights were observed in the self-promoter, FitzHugh-Nagumo, and Ricker models, where Trine successfully recovered characteristic variance profiles despite strong nonlinearities. Collectively, these paradigmatic systems span key classes of noisy biological dynamics—feedback regulation, bistability/excitability, oscillations, and stochastic population growth—highlighting both the biological relevance and the broad applicability of the framework. Trine’s scope further extends beyond dynamical systems. In the Abalone dataset, consisting of independent observations with no temporal structure, it accurately captures input-dependent variability and provides well-calibrated predictive uncertainty. This result shows that the method naturally applies to general regression settings with heteroscedastic noise, yielding a consistent decomposition of deterministic and stochastic components outside an SDE framework.

From an interpretative perspective, estimating state-dependent variability connects statistical inference to biological insight. While stochasticity in biological systems is often described as intrinsic noise at the level of individual components, within the SDE framework, it is represented in an aggregated, coarse-grained form through the diffusion term. In this sense, Trine estimates effective process noise, capturing how microscopic interactions shape variability at larger scales and highlighting regions of high sensitivity to fluctuations. The Smoluchowski construction, illustrated in the toggle switch example, provides a particularly informative test case. It defines a model with homogeneous (state-independent) noise that shares the same stationary distribution as the original system but differs in its dynamical properties. The ability of Trine to distinguish between these two scenarios from trajectory data shows that the inferred diffusion is not an artifact of the diffusion approximation, but reflects genuine dynamical structure beyond equilibrium statistics.

This interpretation also highlights a modeling limitation: in genetic networks, the diffusion models considered here should be viewed as approximations of underlying biochemical processes. This means that the diffusion term reconstructed by Trine represents an effective, state-dependent stochasticity at the level of the observations, rather than a direct characterization of the underlying microscopic noise mechanisms. The complexity of the estimated noise covariance may thus reflect the parametrization induced by such approximations, and its adequacy must be assessed in relation to the application and data resolution. In particular, the SDE description is expected to be most accurate in regimes where molecular copy numbers are sufficiently large or promoter switching occurs on relatively fast timescales60. Interestingly, the RNA transcription experiments considered here suggest that effective diffusion models may remain informative even in regimes influenced by transcriptional bursting and latent switching phenomena83,86–88, where gene expression is often described through explicit switching models. In this setting, Trine may still provide predictive models with well-calibrated uncertainty estimates, indicating that effective coarse-grained stochastic representations can capture relevant aspects of the observable dynamics even without explicit reconstruction of hidden transcriptional states. At the same time, these results may admit further refinement through explicit latent switching representations. A natural direction for future work is therefore to combine the nonparametric regression structure of Trine with switching dynamical systems, bridging effective stochastic coarse-graining with mechanistic hidden-state models.

Several avenues for further development remain. One promising direction is integrating Trine with experimental design strategies to optimize data collection for noise estimation, enhancing both accuracy and efficiency. Additionally, coupling Trine with real-time inference or adaptive control methods could open new possibilities for monitoring and steering stochastic systems in both experimental and applied settings. More broadly, any domain where state-dependent noise is relevant—including financial modeling and complex engineering systems—can benefit from this framework.

Trine algorithm

To introduce Trine, it is first useful to establish some preliminary notation. Although the algorithm is presented here for a temporal setting, Trine applies more generally to supervised learning problems. As illustrated by the Abalone example, when the data do not arise from a dynamical system, it suffices to provide standard input–output training pairs.

For clarity of exposition, we first present the algorithm for a scalar state variable. The extension to multidimensional systems is discussed below and follows straightforwardly. As explained in detail in Supplementary Material, each stage of Trine estimates the unknown functions F, G and the process noise realizations within the subspace spanned by the kernel sections \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{ \mathcal K }}({y}_{i},\cdot )$$\end{document}K(yi,⋅), where yi are the observed (noisy) states. This construction, grounded in the representer theorem37, reflects the nonparametric nature of the method, as the number of basis functions grows with the data and can become infinite under dense sampling. The associated expansion coefficients are computed by solving regularized quadratic optimization problems, where the regularization is implicitly defined by the choice of kernel29.

The initial part of the pseudo-code specifies the hyperparameters estimated at each stage of the algorithm. These parameters are essential for regularization, as they govern the trade-off between fidelity to the empirical data and adherence to the structural priors encoded in the kernel. A distinctive feature of the Trine methodology is its systematic formulation of linear estimation subproblems. This formulation permits the closed-form evaluation of model selection criteria, such as the prediction error sum of squares (PRESS) or the generalized cross-validation (GCV) score90, thereby enabling computationally efficient and theoretically principled hyperparameter optimization. In the present work, we adopt empirical Bayes methods21, with hyperparameters consistently estimated at each stage via maximization of a closed-form approximation of the marginal likelihood (Bayesian evidence), a widely adopted approach in probabilistic modeling (see Supplementary Material for details). This criterion quantifies the explanatory adequacy of the model with respect to the observed data while naturally incorporating Occam’s razor principle91.

The parameter β is used in specific steps of the algorithm to define certain matrices and vectors. The value \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sqrt{2/\pi }$$\end{document}2/π is calibrated for the case of Gaussian process noise. As detailed in the Supplementary Material, the constant can be modified if the noise is known to follow different statistical distributions, such as Laplacian or Student’s t-distributions.

The final stage of the algorithm produces an unconstrained estimate of the noise standard deviation. In our experiments, we did not impose any additional constraints. However, since the optimization problem in Step 3 is quadratic, the framework can easily be extended to include structural constraints on the variance profile, such as nonnegativity, concavity, or monotonicity. These properties can be enforced by applying inequality constraints to discrete-time derivatives of any order, resulting in convex optimization problems that standard numerical solvers can efficiently handle.

The Supplementary Material provides a detailed step-by-step derivation of the algorithm, along with further theoretical insights into its performance and practical aspects.
