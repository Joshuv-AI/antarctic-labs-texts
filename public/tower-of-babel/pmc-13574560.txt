Home-Based Cardiac Rehabilitation Using a Smart Ring and Telephone Counselling: A Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Chul Kim, So Myong Jun, Jeong Yeol Yoo, Ha Young Cho, Dong Woon Jeon, Seung Jin Oh, Se-Jung Yoon, Jong-Kwan Park, Hancheol Lee, Ji-Yong Jang, Hyeongsoo Kim, Jang Woo Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHome-based cardiac rehabilitation (HB-CR) is a promising alternative to center-based cardiac rehabilitation as it can address several barriers impeding CR implementation because of its enhanced accessibility. However, to date, HB-CR has not garnered adequate attention due to a lack of communication between patients and medical staff. In this study, we aimed to investigate the effectiveness of HB-CR by monitoring the pulse rate (PR) using a smart ring equipped with photoplethysmography.MethodsPatients with cardiovascular disease were educated on structured HB-CR methods following a symptom-limited cardiopulmonary exercise test (CPET), and were instructed to wear the device during the exercise session. The recorded PR was uploaded to a website via a smartphone and monitored by medical staff. Patients were randomly assigned to one of two groups: intervention or control group. The intervention group received weekly telephone counselling and was encouraged to maintain optimal levels of physical activity. In contrast, the control group only recorded their exercise data without additional interventions. After 3 months of training, the CPET was performed.ResultsAmong the 64 randomized patients (32 in each group), an intention-to-treat analysis showed that peak oxygen consumption increased over time in both the groups, with a significantly greater improvement in the intervention group. The group-by-time interaction for peak oxygen consumption was statistically significant (estimated mean difference, 2.0 mL/kg/min; 95% confidence interval, 0.7–3.3; P = 0.004).ConclusionHB-CR using a smart device was effective in improving oxygen consumption. However, appropriate monitoring and timely counselling by medical professionals are important for improved outcomes.Trial RegistrationClinical Research Information Service Identifier: KCT0005893Keywords: Cardiac Rehabilitation, Smartphone, Photoplethysmography, Teleconsultation

INTRODUCTION

Cardiac rehabilitation (CR), as a secondary prevention program for patients with cardiovascular disease (CVD), has been shown to significantly improve medical, physical, and psychological outcomes significantly.1 Although there is substantial evidence supporting the benefits of CR, participation rates and adherence to CR remain suboptimal.2 Several factors contribute to this underutilization, including limited awareness among both physicians and patients, comorbid conditions, insufficient medical resources, transportation difficulties, financial burden, and work or time constraints.3,4 As many of these structural problems cannot be addressed at the individual level, various strategies and policy initiatives aimed at improving participation in CR have been proposed across countries.

Home-based cardiac rehabilitation (HB-CR) facilitates better accessibility for patients and is as effective as center-based cardiac rehabilitation (CB-CR), positioning it as a promising alternative for overcoming various barriers to CR implementation.5,6 However, HB-CR has not garnered enough attention owing to limited communication with medical staff, concerns regarding adverse events caused by unsupervised exercises, reduced intensive exercise, lack of insurance support, and poor patient motivation.7

To resolve these limitations, we used a smart ring equipped with photoplethysmography for monitoring the pulse rate (PR). The recorded data were stored on a website allowing researchers to remotely access and review the uploaded measurements. In this study, we aimed to assess the clinical effects of HB-CR with weekly telephone counselling tailored to each patient’s physical activity data obtained from the device.

METHODS

Recruitment of participants

This study included patients who visited an outpatient CR clinic within 1 year following coronary revascularization for coronary artery obstructive disease and had undergone their first symptom-limited cardiopulmonary exercise test (CPET). The following inclusion criteria were considered: 1) adults aged ≥ 19 years; 2) patients who declined participation in CB-CR for financial or time-related reasons; 3) patients classified as having low to moderate risk during exercise, based on the risk stratification criteria proposed by the American Association of Cardiovascular and Pulmonary Rehabilitation (AACVPR). Patients with a left ventricular ejection fraction < 35%, generally categorized as the high-risk group, were also included if they demonstrated stable clinical indicators and adequate responses in symptom-limited CPET8; 4) patients who were able to communicate fluently in Korean; and 5) patients without musculoskeletal or neurological conditions that would restrict independent exercise.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: 1) contraindications to exercise testing and training as outlined by the AACVPR guidelines; 2) patients unfamiliar with the use of smart devices; 3) patients exhibiting abnormal findings during CPET, such as chest pain, ST-segment changes, or a decrease in systolic blood pressure; 4) patients who performed the symptom-limited CPET using a stationary bicycle; 5) patients with arrhythmias (e.g., atrial fibrillation) in which PR did not proportionally increase with exercise intensity; 6) patients unable to participate in self-directed exercise programs due to musculoskeletal or neurological problems, and 7) patients unwilling or unmotivated to participate in the study.

Smart ring specifications and recording of PR

The device used in this study was a ring-shaped smart device with a light pulse wave sensor (CART-I®, Sky Labs, Seongnam, Korea), capable of continuously recording PR while being worn on the finger (Fig. 1). An application linked to the smart ring was installed on each patient’s smartphone. Participants were instructed to start the exercise session by pressing the “Start Exercise” button and end it with the “End Exercise” button upon completion (Fig. 2). Pulse data from the smart ring were recorded on a smartphone using the application and automatically uploaded to a secure web server for remote monitoring. PR was highlighted in green during the exercise session, and in blue at other times (Fig. 3). Participants could view their PR in real-time and access exercise records at any time.

Research protocols

This was a comparative study between two groups. In the intervention group, exercise records were monitored using a smart ring, and medical staff provided weekly telephone counselling based on the recorded data. The control group was provided with an identical smart ring for exercise monitoring but did not receive any guidance regarding exercise. All participants were able to view their own PR data, recorded during the exercise session, using a smartphone application. The medical staff consisted of one medical doctor specializing in CR and two CR physiotherapists who met weekly to review the exercise records and discuss the contents of the feedback. Based on these meetings, the CR physiotherapists provided weekly telephone counselling to the intervention group, which primarily focused on evaluating whether the frequency, intensity, and duration of the CR exercise were appropriate. In addition, comprehensive advice was provided to address any difficulties or questions encountered by the participants during the exercise session. Participants were allowed to withdraw from the study at any time and for any reason. In total, 64 participants were recruited for this study. The sample size was calculated based on the following: the primary endpoint was peak oxygen consumption (VO2) after 3 months. At the time of recruitment, mean peak VO2 of the participants was estimated to be 27 mL/kg/min with a standard deviation of 5.5 mL/kg/min.9 Referring to previous HB-CR interventions that reported approximately 10–20% improvements in peak VO2, a 15% difference of peak VO2 between the intervention and control groups was assumed for calculating the sample size.10,11,12 If a type 1 error rate of 5% and a statistical power of 80% were achieved, the required sample size was calculated as 25 participants per group. Accounting for an anticipated dropout rate of 20%, the final required sample size was calculated as approximately 32 per group. Block randomization was performed to ensure that an equal number of patients were assigned to each group. The block size was set to four and each block was designated using a random number table.

Exercise prescription

All participants received a thorough explanation regarding the effectiveness and necessity of CR before participating in the program. The methods, principles, and general precautions to be followed were also explained. Smart rings were provided to all participants with the instruction to wear them during the exercise session to ensure that all exercise data were recorded on their smartphones.

After performing symptom-limited CPET, the exercise regimen was prescribed according to the Frequency, Intensity, Time, and Type (FITT) principle. The type of exercise, such as jogging, treadmill use, or indoor stationary cycling, was recommended was based on the patient’s age and environment. Each session lasted 50–60 minutes, including 5–10 minutes for warm-up and cool-down. The recommended frequency was 3–5 sessions per week. The exercise intensity was prescribed using the Karvonen formula. The heart rate (HR) reserve (maximal HR-resting HR) measured in CPET was obtained, and participants were trained to maintain exercise intensity at 60% or more of their HR reserve. Although exercise intensity was primarily based on HR, patients were also educated on the Borg scale to adjust the exercise intensity according to their perceived difficulty. The prescribed exercises were identical for both the intervention and control groups.

Outcome parameters

The results of CPET at 3 months of study registration were set as the primary outcome. Differences in peak VO2 changes within each group before and after the study were compared between each group. Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) were used as the secondary outcome. These included the amount of physical activity, functional performance scale score, quality of life, and depression index. Physical activity was measured by calculating the weekly min spent on high- and moderate-intensity exercises. High-intensity physical activity was assumed to be ≥ 8.0 metabolic equivalents (METs), and moderate-intensity physical activity was assumed to be ≥ 4.0 METs. The total weekly physical activity (METs·min/week) was calculated by multiplying the number of times per week and the duration (minutes) of each physical activity.13 Functional performance scale was measured using the Korean version of Activity Scale/Index, and the quality of life was evaluated using the Korean version of the EuroQoL 5-Dimension 5-Level questionnaire. For the depression index, the Korean version of Patient Health Questionnaire-9 was used.

Statistical analysis

The baseline characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables are presented as means ± standard deviation, and categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. To evaluate the intervention effect over time, while accounting for missing data, an intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis was performed using a linear mixed model (LMM). The model included fixed effects for group, time, and their interaction (group × time), with age and sex as covariates and a random intercept for participants to account for within-subject correlations. As a sensitivity analysis, per-protocol (PP) analysis was also performed in participants who completed the follow-up assessments. SAS 9.4 software (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) was used for the ITT analysis, while SPSS version 23 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) was used for descriptive statistics and PP analysis.

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the National Health Insurance Service Ilsan Hospital (IRB No. NHIMC 2020-12-009-014). Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their participation. The study was registered with the Clinical Research Information Service (http://cris.nih.go.kr; Registration No. KCT0005893).

RESULTS

Participant enrolment and baseline comparisons

Of the 111 patients screened between April 28, 2021 and April 1, 2022, 64 patients were enrolled and 54 of them completed the 3-month CPET follow-up: 26 in the intervention group and 28 in the control group (Fig. 4). All participants who discontinued did so for reasons unrelated to the study. Throughout the study period, no research-related serious adverse events were observed in either the intervention or control groups.

At the time of study enrolment, the average age of the study participants was 59.8 ± 9.0 years, and 53 of the 64 participants (82.8%) were men. Regarding diagnoses, 22 patients (34.4%) had stable angina, 9 (14.1%) had unstable angina, 18 (28.1%) had non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction (MI), and 15 (23.4%) had ST-elevation MI. The peak VO2 was 27.9 ± 5.5 mL/kg/min, on average, showing 8.0 ± 1.6 METs at baseline CPET. The VO2 at anaerobic threshold (VO2 AT) was 23.7 ± 5.1 mL/kg/min, whereas the respiratory exchange ratio (RER) averaged 1.08 ± 0.07, and the maximal HR was 148.8 ± 17.9 beats/min, reaching 93.0 ± 10.8% of the age-predicted maximal HR. There were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of demographic characteristics and baseline exercise parameters (Table 1).

Primary outcomes

Using an LMM under an ITT framework, model-based estimated marginal means showed that peak VO2 increased from 28.0 (standard error [SE], 1.0) to 31.2 (SE, 1.0) mL/kg/min in the intervention group (estimated mean difference, 3.2 mL/kg/min; 95% confidence interval [CI], 2.3–4.2) and from 27.9 (SE, 1.0) to 29.1 (SE, 1.0) mL/kg/min in the control group (estimated mean difference, 1.2 mL/kg/min; 95% CI, 0.3–2.1). The improvement was greater in the intervention group, as reflected by a significant group-by-time interaction (estimated mean difference, 2.0 mL/kg/min; 95% CI, 0.7–3.3; P = 0.004). VO2 AT also increased over time in both the groups, with an estimated mean difference of 4.1 mL/kg/min (95% CI, 2.6–5.7) in the intervention group, and 2.1 mL/kg/min (95% CI, 0.6–3.6) in the control group. However, the between-group difference in change did not reach statistical significance (estimated mean difference, 2.0 mL/kg/min; 95% CI, −0.1–4.1; P = 0.059). No discernible changes were observed over time within either group or significant group-by-time interactions for other CPET parameters, including RER, resting HR, maximal HR, and percentage of age-predicted maximal HR (Table 2). The PP analysis results are consistent with those of the ITT analysis. Peak VO2 increased in both the groups, and a greater improvement was observed in the intervention group. VO2 AT improved significantly in both the groups, but the between-group difference in the change was not significant (Supplementary Table 1).

Secondary outcomes

The amount of self-reported physical activity increased over time in both the groups, with estimated mean increase of 1,373 METs·min/week (95% CI, 730–2,016) and 776 METs·min/week (95% CI, 152–1,400) in the intervention and control groups, respectively. Although the increase was numerically greater in the intervention group, the group-by-time interaction did not indicate a clear between-group difference in change (estimated mean difference, 597 METs·min/week; 95% CI, −299–1,493; P = 0.187). The other PROM parameters showed no meaningful changes over time within either group and no group-by-time interactions (Table 3). The results of PP analysis were similar to those of ITT analysis. Self-reported physical activity significantly increased in both groups, without a significant between-group difference in change. The EQ Visual Analog Scale score improved in the intervention group; however, the between-group comparison did not reach statistical significance (Supplementary Table 2).

DISCUSSION

This study demonstrated that monitored HB-CR effectively increased both peak VO2 and physical activity levels. Furthermore, direct communication between the patients and healthcare providers led to better CR outcomes. Studies on the effectiveness of HB-CR using digital devices have been ongoing for several years. In 2000, Ades et al.14 achieved real-time electrocardiogram (ECG) monitoring during home exercises via a modem connection, and their findings indicated no significant difference in quality of life improvement between the HB-CR and CB-CR groups. As clinical studies on HB-CR using digital devices have increased, several meta-analyses have been conducted. In 2022, Ramachandran et al.15 conducted a meta-analysis of 14 studies and reported that digital HB-CR significantly improved functional capacity compared to usual care and demonstrated comparable effectiveness to CB-CR. More recently, Pagliari et al.16 reported that cardiac telerehabilitation delivered through digital platforms or wearable devices was effective in enhancing exercise capacity relative to conventional CR. Similarly, Harbi et al.17 found that digital health interventions were associated with significant improvements in functional capacity compared to CB-CR or usual-care groups. Digital CR has more advantages compared to traditional CR in terms of quality of life owing to its improved accessibility, enhanced patient engagement, and greater flexibility in scheduling.18 Previous studies on digital CR have demonstrated considerable methodological heterogeneity in terms of monitoring approaches and feedback delivery, compared to either usual-care or CB-CR groups.

The most distinctive feature of the present study compared to previous research is that both groups were provided the same digital device and received identical exercise prescriptions. The only difference between the two patient groups was the addition of direct telephone consultations with CR staff. The control group did not receive any consultations from medical staff; however, the patients were able to access their exercise records through the smartphone application. The use of photoplethysmography for PR monitoring during exercise is not technically demanding, and weekly phone calls are relatively simple to implement. PR can be monitored remotely and in real time not only through ring-type devices but also via various commercialized wearables, such as smartwatches, chest belts, and chest-worn patch.19 Numerous clinical studies have employed digital tools in CR, demonstrating the feasibility of applying information and communication technology-based approaches. However, despite sufficient technological readiness, actual clinical implementation remains limited. Legal concerns regarding exercise-induced complications and the lack of insurance reimbursement appear to be major barriers. In this study, patients at a high risk of exercise-induced cardiac events were excluded through comprehensive pre-assessments, including symptom-limited CPET, enabling safe implementation of the program without significant adverse events. Therefore, safety concerns regarding digital CR appear manageable through appropriate screening and supervision.

HB-CR, or a combined model of HB- and CB-CR, is considered a promising model for increasing participation in CR as it can overcome various barriers, such as transportation, time flexibility, cost, and individual patient preferences.5 However, HB-CR has the inherent risk of patients not achieving the desired exercise intensity.7 In this study, self-reported physical activity did not differ between the two groups, despite a significant difference in peak VO2 improvement. Notably, even the participants in the control group were able to access their exercise records on their smartphones. This finding indicates that the participants may have reported an exercise volume greater than what was objectively achieved. Indeed, Groothuis et al.20 previously reported that patients tend to overreport their physical activity compared to objectively tracked data. Structured guidance and professional supervision, beyond monitored exercise alone, are necessary to achieve meaningful improvements in exercise capacity.

Peak VO2 is an index closely related to the prognosis of patients with CVD and is the most important index for patient follow-up.21,22 Because improvements in peak VO2 are primarily determined by exercise volume,23 maintaining adequate physical activity is one of the most important goals of secondary prevention. However, peak VO2 enhancement cannot be achieved within a short period, and sustained lifestyle modification and motivation are essential. Simple feedback on exercise volume alone may be insufficient to maintain regular exercise behavior. Therefore, a personalized approach that considers individual characteristics and challenges is required. In our study, participants were provided comprehensive counselling regarding musculoskeletal problems and cardiac and non-cardiac symptoms that may occur during exercise, appropriate exercise types, and individualized practical tips. We believe that this holistic and personalized guidance played a key role in significantly improving peak VO2 in our study. A wide variety of CR methods have been explored; however, it is essential to provide personalized and specific treatments with practical feedback, as patient preferences, environments, and medical comorbidities vary significantly.

The rapid expansion of digital healthcare, significantly accelerated by the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, is transforming the global medical landscape, driving innovation, and reshaping care delivery.24 It can now be asserted that the paradigm has changed from whether to use digital devices in rehabilitation to how best to implement them. Digital devices are more likely to be used in CR than in any other medical field. PR is the most widely used objective parameter for prescribing exercise intensity in CR, and its measurement using smart devices is highly accurate and reproducible. Currently, only PR can be used in digital CR; however, technological advancements and the accumulation of clinical information may enable the integration of ECG, blood pressure, and oxygen saturation. Furthermore, based on the timing of monitoring, telerehabilitation can be categorized into synchronous and asynchronous modalities.25 Furthermore, the concept of artificial intelligence-enhanced CR has recently emerged.26

Previous studies demonstrating significant improvements in quality of life generally incorporated comprehensive and multidisciplinary CR programs that included psychological counselling, patient education, and social support, in addition to exercise training.27 In this study, albeit limited, the intervention group showed a slight improvement in their self-perceived health status. However, the present digital intervention primarily focused on exercise guidance and weekly feedback, which may have been insufficient to elicit measurable psychosocial benefits. This represents a common limitation observed across digital CR studies that primarily aim to enhance physical activity.28 As the use of digital therapeutics in CR continues to expand, tailored strategies may be required to effectively improve the quality of life.

One notable limitation of this study was the inability to record physical activity when the smart ring was not worn. Although the duration, frequency, and intensity of the exercises could be recorded, it was difficult to determine the type of exercise. Furthermore, long-term data are required to assess the impact of CR via digital devices on clinical outcomes.

In conclusion, HB-CR using a smart device proved effective in improving peak oxygen consumption in patients with coronary disease. However, for greater effectiveness, proper monitoring and timely counselling by medical experts are essential.
