Scoring architecture and transparency in healthcare accreditation: A scoping review of measurement design and signalling effects
Authors: Arjavi Patel, Bhavinkumar Parmar, Sankalp Sagar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHealthcare accreditation is widely adopted to improve quality and strengthen institutional credibility. Although research has examined its associations with organisational processes and clinical outcomes, the internal structure of accreditation decisions remains poorly understood. Specifically, the scoring, weighting, and aggregation processes that transform standards compliance into certification results have received limited analytical attention. This scoping review maps the extent to which the published literature addresses accreditation scoring architecture, transparency, and signalling functions in hospital and health service accreditation systems.MethodsScoping review was performed in line with PRISMA-ScR. The searches were conducted in PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science, and CINAHL using the English-language publications published between January 2000 and March 2026. Sources that were considered eligible were empirical studies, reviews, governance analysis, theoretical papers, and accreditation program documents. The data were obtained through a systematic framework and synthesized in three domains i.e., scoring and weighting mechanisms, transparency and methodological validity as well as market signalling.ResultsOf 2,968 records identified, 27 sources met the eligibility criteria. The majority (85%) defined accreditation as a standards-based compliance intervention focused on organisational outcomes. Ninety-three percent acknowledged methodological limitations in causal inference. Among the included sources, no published evidence was identified that directly examined weighting schemes, score aggregation, threshold logic, or composite score construction. Only six studies addressed transparency beyond binary certification status, and three considered equity implications. Despite 70% of sources describing accreditation as a reputational or legitimacy signal, empirical analysis of the underlying measurement architecture was absent across the entire corpus.ConclusionsAccreditation research has emphasised outcome measurement while neglecting the internal scoring and measurement systems that generate certification decisions. Within the published literature reviewed, the weighting and aggregation mechanisms that shape institutional incentives have received limited analytical attention and remain largely undisclosed in publicly accessible sources. Greater transparency in scoring design and weighting logic is essential, particularly as accreditation becomes more deeply embedded in regulatory, reimbursement, and public reporting frameworks.

Introduction

Healthcare accreditation has emerged as a prominent mechanism for quality assurance and institutional accountability across health systems worldwide. Originally rooted in early 20th-century hospital standardisation initiatives and subsequently institutionalised by national and international accreditation agencies, accreditation programs now operate across high-, middle-, and low-income contexts and are embedded in regulatory controls, insurer empanelment criteria, public reporting mechanisms, and reimbursement eligibility frameworks.1–4 The central premise of accreditation is that systematic assessment of compliance with predefined standards drives measurable improvements in healthcare quality and safety. However, while considerable research has examined whether accreditation is associated with improved organisational and clinical outcomes, a foundational and largely unexamined question remains: how are accreditation decisions actually made? Specifically, how are individual standards compliance assessments weighted, aggregated, and converted into final certification outcomes? For the purposes of this review, this process is referred to as scoring architecture, defined as the set of design decisions and operational mechanisms — including standard weighting, domain aggregation logic, threshold rules, and composite index construction — through which individual standards compliance assessments are converted into final accreditation certification decisions. As the result of early 20th-century hospital standardization initiatives and later institutionalized by national and international accreditation agencies, modern accreditation programs currently act in environments of high-, middle-, and low-income.1–4 They are entrenched in regulatory controls, empaneling of insurers, public reporting mechanisms and more reforms of reimbursement eligibility and universal coverage of health.5–7 There has been a considerable amount of literature on whether accreditation is linked to the enhancement of the organizational processes, safety culture and the chosen clinical outcomes.2,8–12 The majority of the research views accreditation as a compliance intervention based on the standards and assesses areas like documentation compliance, infection control measures, medication safety measures and governance reforms.9,13 The most common types of designs are observational designs, cross-sectional surveys, registry-based analyses and quasi-experimental studies.10,11,14 Randomised controlled evidence remains scarce 15 and most research findings admit limitations associated with causal inference and heterogeneity in the outcome measures. Although this is a comprehensive review of the results, there is still a vital aspect of the accreditation systems that has not been critically addressed and that is the internal process by which the certification decisions are formed. In the literature, there is no direct discussion of the weighting schemes, aggregation reasoning or composite score forming that supports the results of accreditation.2,8,15 The procedures that lead to weighting of individual standards and their conversion into final certification status are still largely uninvestigated even in program manuals that describe large groups of standards or tiers of compliance.

This gap has important implications. Accreditation systems commonly encompass hundreds of structural and procedural standards, yet the reasoning by which compliance with those standards is integrated into certification outcomes remains largely undisclosed.2,15 The existing literature engages minimally with the theoretical basis of scoring design, focusing instead on whether accreditation is associated with outcomes rather than how those outcomes are defined or measured. As a result, accreditation functions as a black box: it is studied as an intervention, but the mechanisms by which scores are generated and combined are not examined.

This limitation is further enhanced by the fact that binary accreditation status is the dominant one. The majority of the empirical studies define accreditation as a dichotomous exposure (accredited vs. non-accredited).10,11,14 Although these models can set minimum compliance levels, they may not be able to distinguish better performing institutions and those which are meeting minimum levels. 15 Very few studies cite graded accreditation models, and in such instances as well, the weighting structures and threshold logic have not been studied.4,6 Public reporting brings about other complications. The accreditation is often discussed as a way to increase the institutional legitimacy, facilitate the connections with the insurers and consolidate the regulations.16–18 Nevertheless, there is still the lack of transparency because the reporting is usually limited to the binary certifications results. There is some evidence as to the impact of accreditation status on patient choice or quantifiable quality improvements but that is restricted.11,19 In this regard, accreditation can be an important reputational indicator but not a performance metric differentiation.

A further paradox concerns patient experience. Some of the studies indicate that the accreditation status is not always correlated with patient satisfaction even though it is used as a quality indicator.11,20 This is an indication that the existing scoring systems might have a bias on the structural and administrative compliance areas that may not be as much visible to patients. Devoid of the correspondence between scoring architecture and perceived quality by patients, accreditation can strengthen internal governance standards but have a low relation to consumer experience.4,6 Equity considerations have not been given much attention as well. There are very few studies that study the distributional implications of accreditation, such as obstacles to smaller or resource-constrained facilities. Without clarity on the internal measurement design, it remains uncertain whether accreditation reduces quality variability or inadvertently reinforces structural inequities.

Moreover, the design and scoring schemes of accreditation are frequently incorporated in proprietary documents either in forms of subscriptions or documents.15,21 This constrains transparency and it also limits the transferability of design principles across jurisdictions. In the case of the emerging accreditation systems and especially those in resource constrained environments, the absence of publicly available evidence on scoring architecture can result in duplication of effort and inconsistent design strategies. Combined, the literature shows that much focus has been given to the question of whether accreditation can enhance outcomes, but scant attention has been given on how accreditation decisions are formulated and communicated. The accreditation systems are also a system of governance and measurement but their internal processes are not well studied empirically.

Four conceptually distinct functions attributed to accreditation in the literature warrant differentiation: (1) Institutional signalling — the use of accreditation status to communicate quality compliance to external stakeholders; (2) Legitimacy — the conferral of normative acceptance and social licence through accreditation, drawing on institutional theory; (3) Market competitiveness — the use of accreditation as a competitive differentiator in healthcare markets; and (4) Reputational indicator — the use of accreditation status as a proxy for quality in the absence of granular performance data. In line with this, this scoping review would map and critique the coverage of the accreditation literature on the topic of scoring architecture, weighting mechanisms, transparency, and signalling functions of hospital and health service accreditation systems. Systematic identification of what is known (and what has not been studied) is done by means of this review to enhance conceptual clarity and guide future research and policy formulation.

Methods

Protocol and reporting framework

The scoping review was done according to the Preferred Reporting Items Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension to Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR). The purpose of the review was to map the scope, nature and analysis emphasis of the literature investigating the scoring architecture, weighting mechanisms, transparency and signalling functions of hospital and health service accreditation systems.

Population–concept–context (PCC) framework for eligibility criteria.

The sources that were eligible included empirical quantitative or qualitative studies, mixed-method studies, systematic or narrative reviews, governance or policy studies, theoretical frameworks, and official documents on accreditation programs that offered structural understanding of accreditation systems. Limitations to publications were English-language publications that were published between January 2000 and March 2026. The exclusion criteria included studies that were not in health care accreditation settings, dealt with quality improvement efforts that were not formally accredited, referred to accreditation in a peripheral way (without analytical interaction), were commentary/editorial articles, or referenced accreditation only peripherally (defined as fewer than two substantive paragraphs engaging with accreditation systems, outcomes, or governance), or were commentary/editorial articles without original empirical or analytical content.

Information sources and search strategy

A structured search was conducted in PubMed/MEDLINE using a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms related to healthcare accreditation, scoring and composite indices, weighting and aggregation, transparency and public reporting, consumer behaviour, and signalling. The full electronic search strategy is provided in Supplementary Appendix 1. March 2026 was the date of the final search. In addition to electronic database searching, a targeted grey literature search was conducted to identify official accreditation program documents that may not be indexed in academic databases. The websites of major international and national accreditation bodies were systematically searched, including the Joint Commission International (JCI), Accreditation Canada, the Australian Council on Healthcare Standards (ACHS), the International Society for Quality in Health Care (ISQua), the National Accreditation Board for Hospitals and Healthcare Providers (NABH, India), and the relevant government health portals. This search identified three documents that met the eligibility criteria and provided structural insight into accreditation scoring architecture not available in the peer-reviewed literature: an Accreditation Canada organisational report, a NABH program brochure, and a Gujarat government health framework document. Comparable publicly accessible structural documents from JCI, ACHS, and ISQua were sought but were not identified within the search timeframe. Study Selection

All the records that were discovered by means of database search were then exported into the reference management software and duplicated records were eliminated before the screening process. The search retrieved 2,968 records, of which 108 were identified as duplicates, yielding 2,860 unique records for title and abstract screening. Of these, 2,391 were excluded at the title and abstract stage on the basis of predetermined eligibility criteria, leaving 469 records for full-text assessment.

Title and abstract screening was conducted independently by two reviewers. Disagreements were resolved through discussion, with a third reviewer consulted where consensus could not be reached. The same process was applied at full-text screening stage. Reasons for exclusion were systematically documented for all records excluded at full-text stage to ensure transparency and reproducibility.

Full-text assessment was conducted for 469 records (Figure 1). Of these, 442 were excluded for the following reasons: no substantive engagement with accreditation scoring architecture, weighting mechanisms, transparency, or signalling functions despite a healthcare accreditation setting (n = 297); irrelevance to hospital or health service accreditation (n = 128); inadequate methodological reporting precluding data extraction (n = 11); commentary or editorial content only, without original analytical contribution (n = 4); and duplicate data (n = 2). The sum of exclusion reasons (297 + 128 + 11 + 4 + 2 = 442) reconciles exactly with the total excluded at full-text stage. The remaining 27 studies were included in the final review.Figure 1.Prisma flow diagram of study selection (n = 27).

In total, 27 sources were incorporated into the final synthesis that met the inclusion criteria and consisted of 25 peer-reviewed articles and two official reports on accreditation, which provided structural perspective on the architecture of scoring and shape the design of governance.

Data charting and synthesis

A structured charting form was developed a priori and pilot-tested on five randomly selected studies. A priori coding scheme was developed based on three areas of analysis such as internal scoring mechanics, transparency and methodological validity and market signalling. Both the studies were coded by two reviewers and differences settled by consensus. Overall, pre-reconciliation domains were agreed to more than 85 percent. Since scoping reviews are exploratory, no formal statistics of inter-rater reliability (e.g., the kappa of Cohen) were computed. Synthesizing data descriptively and thematically was done by use of a predetermined three-point coding scale in the domains of analysis. The coding scheme revealed the absence of engagement, incomplete engagement and overt analytical attention. The calculation of frequency distributions to identify thematic concentrations and gaps in the evidence base in terms of structure identified them.

A three-point coding scale was applied, operationally defined as follows.0 = No engagement: the study makes no substantive reference to scoring architecture, weighting mechanisms, aggregation logic, or transparency of accreditation results;1 = Partial or descriptive engagement: the study acknowledges or incidentally refers to scoring or transparency elements without analytical scrutiny;2 = Analytical engagement: the study explicitly examines, critiques, or empirically investigates aspects of scoring design, weighting structures, aggregation logic, or transparency mechanisms.

Simple proportions were used in calculating frequency distributions according to the number of studies included (n = 27). Pre-reconciliation inter-reviewer agreement was calculated across all coded items (three domains x 27 studies = 81 coding decisions), with 70 items coded identically before reconciliation, yielding an agreement rate of 86.4%. Formal inter-rater reliability statistics (e.g., Cohen’s kappa) were not computed, consistent with common scoping review practice; this is acknowledged as a methodological limitation. No inferential statistical tests were performed, consistent with scoping review methodology.

Results

Study characteristics

The number of sources that were included in the final synthesis was twenty-seven sources that satisfied the inclusion criteria. The evidence base included 25 peer-reviewed journal articles and two official accreditation reports or framework documents which gave structural understanding of accreditation architecture. The types of study designs were heterogeneous (n = 6) systematic reviews, (n = 4) narrative or hybrid literature syntheses, (n = 5) retrospective longitudinal studies or registry-based analyses, (n = 5) cross-sectional survey studies, (n = 3) quasi-experimental or interrupted time-series designs, (n = 2) governance or policy analyses. Observational designs were the most common ones, and one randomized controlled trial was found in the included systematic reviews. Studies were conducted across high-, middle-, and low-income settings, spanning North America, Europe, the Middle East, South and Southeast Asia, sub-Saharan Africa, Latin America, and China. A number of multinational reviews combined the evidence on a number of accreditation systems. The accreditation organizations that were regularly investigated were associated with Joint Commission, national accreditation board, including NABH25,26 and country-specific regulatory frameworks. The scheme of voluntary and mandatory accreditation was both presented, such as binary certification models. In the majority of the empirical researches accreditation was operationalized as a dichotomous exposure (accredited vs. non-accredited). Outcomes were measured at organisational, process, and clinical levels, including safety culture, documentation compliance, mortality, infection rates, and length of stay. Most of the designs were observational; mostly in cross-sectional and before-after comparisons. Registry based analyses used quasi-experimental designs like interrupted time-series and adjusted regression models but were observational in nature.10,11,14

Although the scope of this review is comprehensive, within the included literature, no published evidence was identified that specifically examined accreditation weighting algorithms, composite score aggregation, or scoring transparency mechanisms.

Thematic distribution of accreditation research

The 27 included sources are summarised in Table 2. There was a marked disproportion of analytical attention across the literature: most sources concentrated on standards compliance and organisational outcomes, with little engagement with the internal scoring architecture and public reporting mechanisms through which accreditation decisions are generated.

Standardisation Examined; Weighting Ignored

Eighty-five percent (23 out of 27) studies conceptualized accreditation as a standards-based compliance intervention. These studies examined compliance with structural, procedural, and governance standards, assessing downstream effects on organisational performance, safety culture, and selected clinical outcomes.

Within the included literature, no published evidence was identified that directly tested a weighting scheme, score aggregation strategy, threshold logic, or composite index construction. Even though there are studies mentioning the tiered standards or summary outcomes, mathematical reasoning of how the standards were added to the certification decisions was not examined.

Accreditation was thus often considered a measurable intervention, and the mechanisms of quantification were not investigated.

Methodological reflexivity without architectural transparency

Limitations of method were also generally recognized. A total of 27 studies 28 claimed limitations to do with either causal inference, outcome attribution, or metric validity. They were consistently noted to be dominated by observational designs, heterogeneous outcome measures, and limited randomised evidence.

However, this methodological reflexivity was not extended to examination of accreditation scoring architecture. Impact evaluation was mostly criticized instead of scoring system transparency or internal validity. As a result, accreditation has been critically assessed as an intervention but not as a measurement system.

Binary Disclosure and Limited Public Interpretability

There was a lack of public transparency of accreditation results. There were only six studies (22%) that looked at reporting outside binary certification status. In the majority of instances, accreditation results were reported on a dichotomous basis, without the provision of domain level scores, composite weighting schemes, performance standards and inter-rater reliability indicators.

Granular reporting was not present even where there were composite scores or dashboards mentioned internally. As a result, accreditation was more of a status signal than a differentiated performance indicator.

Accreditation as institutional signal

The majority (70% or 19 out of 27) studies referred to accreditation as increasing institutional legitimacy, facilitating insurer relationships, enhancing regulatory positioning, and market competitiveness, such as in medical tourism.7,8,12 There was also the intensification of performance cycle as a cycle, which implied that accreditation could serve as a performance stimulus cycle.22,28

Nevertheless, there was still no empirical research on consumer behaviour, competition in the market and patient decision-making. The accreditation was often described as a reputational indicator but it was hardly studied as a quantifiable market process.

Equity considerations

Among 27 studies, only three studies (11%) directly studied the distributional implications of accreditation. This is a substantially underexplored dimension. Opaque threshold logic and weighting structures may differentially disadvantage facilities with limited quality infrastructure, since institutions cannot identify which standards carry the greatest weight in certification decisions. This concern is particularly acute in low- and middle-income health systems where accreditation is increasingly mandated by regulation or tied to reimbursement eligibility, such as differences in participation by smaller or resource-constrained facilities. Although resource implications and sustainability issues were reported in some studies, there is scanty systematic assessment of whether accreditation reduces or increases inequities.

Synthesis of findings

The results reveal a consistent structural pattern across the included literature. Accreditation research has focused predominantly on process compliance, organisational change, and safety indicators, with minimal engagement with the measurement design features that structure these outcomes. Across all three analytical domains — scoring mechanics, transparency, and signalling — the absence of architectural scrutiny is not incidental but systematic. This suggests that the field has adopted a conceptual framing of accreditation as a binary intervention rather than as a measurement system with design properties that can be empirically investigated, critiqued, and improved.

Across the included studies, virtually no engagement with weighting logic, composite score construction, or public reporting architecture was identified. Accreditation is therefore predominantly conceptualised as a dichotomous intervention rather than as a performance measurement system with behavioural, distributive, and signalling dimensions.

This imbalance implies that the literature has prioritised impact evaluation over measurement system analysis. As accreditation becomes more deeply embedded in reimbursement mechanisms, regulatory frameworks, and public reporting regimes, the absence of transparency in scoring and aggregation logic represents a substantive governance accountability gap. 29

The model is novel in that it explicitly positions the scoring architecture layer — comprising standard weighting, aggregation logic, and threshold design — as the foundational but analytically neglected stratum between standards compliance assessment and certification outcomes. Existing conceptual frameworks in the accreditation literature (e.g., Greenfield and Braithwaite, 2008; Nicklin and Dickson, 2009) have focused on the relationship between accreditation and organisational or clinical outcomes, treating accreditation primarily as a standards-based intervention.2,7 Figure 2 extends these frameworks by making the measurement architecture of accreditation explicit — the layer through which standards are converted into certification decisions. This reframing positions accreditation not only as a governance mechanism but as a performance measurement system with design properties that can be empirically investigated, critiqued, and improved. Discussion

This scoping review identifies a consistent structural imbalance in the accreditation literature. Across 27 included sources, the existing body of research has examined the relationship between accreditation and improvements in organisational processes, safety culture, and selected clinical outcomes — consistent with prior systematic reviews by Hinchcliff et al. (2012) and Hussein et al. (2021).1,8 The primary contribution of this review is not to reiterate that the evidence on accreditation is mixed, but to demonstrate that accreditation is a governance tool whose internal scoring structure is predominantly opaque. To contextualise these findings, the authors propose a conceptual framework depicting accreditation as a layered measurement–governance system (Figure 2).

This gap in publicly available information about scoring design has direct policy implications. As accreditation becomes increasingly embedded in reimbursement systems, regulatory regimes, and public reporting frameworks, the limited availability of publicly accessible scoring logic constrains the accountability functions that accreditation is designed to serve. When certification decisions are based on weighting schemes and aggregation rules that are not publicly disclosed, institutions, policymakers, and patients cannot assess whether accreditation status genuinely reflects quality performance. This tendency is indicative of a larger conceptual framing in the accreditation scholarship. According to Saut and Berssaneti, 3 the literature has mostly emphasized the standardization and compliance procedures but not on the mechanics of performance quantification. It is considered to be a standards-based programme but the process of converting standards into certification results, by way of weighting, threshold requirements, and aggregate strategies, has not been studied systematically.

This opaqueness is more amplified in light of accreditation gaining more governance functions other than quality improvement. A number of studies accentuate its connection with insurance empanelment, regulatory recognition, and institutional legitimacy.7,8 The accreditation in such settings has a direct impact on the financial flows and positioning in the market. Nonetheless, binary certification is usually the only thing that is publicly disclosed and little of the domain-level scores or weighting structures are reported.16–18,27 This represents a tension within the accreditation model: while accreditation is positioned as a mechanism for quality accountability, the criteria according to which certification decisions are made are not consistently open to public scrutiny — at least as reflected in the published literature reviewed.

The incentive structures created by binary versus graded certification models are also underexplored. Binary accreditation status provides minimal incentive differentiation: an institution narrowly meeting the accreditation threshold receives the same certification signal as one substantially exceeding it. It is also consistently observed in the literature that accreditation is a reputational or legitimacy indicator. 15 The accredited institutions are usually related to a greater credibility, better relationship with insurers, and better regulatory position.7,12 Nonetheless, patient choice and market behavior have not been empirically modeled. Consequently, accreditation operates more as an institutional indicator as opposed to a granular comparison measure. This limitation may be due to the fact that the majority of binary certification models presuppose minimal compliance without distinguishing between the performance levels among institutions. There is a lack of equity evidence. Few studies have documented resource requirements and administrative burdens of accreditation participation,1,8 and distributional effects have not been systematically assessed. It is hypothesised that when graded accreditation models are implemented, particularly in low- and middle-income settings, the absence of clear weighting and threshold logic may make it difficult to determine whether accreditation reduces quality variation or inadvertently reinforces structural inequities.21,25,26 These equity concerns warrant further empirical investigation and should not be treated as established findings on the basis of the current evidence base. Collectively, these results indicate that research on accreditation emphasizes the outcome assessment and ignores the measurement measurement structure to a considerable extent. Prior reviews have established that evidence on clinical outcomes is heterogeneous and context-dependent.8,15 This review extends that body of work by demonstrating that the internal scoring systems underpinning accreditation decisions have received almost no analytical scrutiny. As accreditation becomes more deeply integrated into reimbursement systems, regulatory regimes, and public reporting frameworks, greater analytic attention to weighting logic and aggregation transparency is essential to strengthen governance accountability.

Limitations

This review has a number of limitations that must be considered when interpreting the findings. First, only English-language publications were searched. Given that accreditation systems are widely used in non-English-speaking settings, analyses of national accreditation models and scoring designs from these contexts may not be fully represented, which may constrain the breadth of the evidence base.

Second, in line with the scoping review methodology, the goal was to map and describe the existing literature instead of attempting to estimate the effect sizes or make formal risk-of-bias evaluations. Quantitative synthesis and methodological quality appraisal were not done. In this regard, the review does not make conclusions about the size or the confidence of the effect of accreditation on clinical or organizational outcomes but rather on the manner in which accreditation systems are conceptually and analytically discussed.

Third, the search strategy employed terminology specific to measurement design and scoring architecture (e.g., “scoring architecture,” “aggregation logic,” “composite score construction”). Accreditation researchers may address related concepts using different terminology, such as standards prioritisation, survey methodology, scoring frameworks, or grading systems. It is therefore possible that some relevant literature was not captured, and that the apparent absence of engagement with scoring architecture in the published literature partly reflects a conceptual framing effect rather than a complete absence of the topic. Future reviews using broader or alternative terminology may yield different results. Fourth, the findings on the absence of explicit studies on weighting, aggregation, and composite score construction are based on publicly accessible publications and reports. Accreditation agencies may apply internal scoring algorithms that are not publicly disclosed. The findings therefore reflect a lack of transparency in the published literature and should not be interpreted as conclusive evidence that such mechanisms do not exist.

Fifth, there was interpretive judgement in thematic coding based on a predefined three-point framework. Despite the systematic application of coding criteria, qualitative categorisation involves analytic discretion, and marginal cases may have been classified differently by other reviewers.

Sixth, the review focused specifically on hospital and health service accreditation systems. The findings may not generalise to accreditation in other sectors such as higher education, laboratory certification, or professional licensure, where scoring systems and reporting practices may differ considerably.

Regardless of these limitations, this review offers an organized and transparent summary of the involvement of accreditation research in scoring architecture, transparency, and governance roles and reveals a systematic gap in analysis that should be explored further through empirical research.

Conclusion

This scoping review has examined the coverage of the accreditation literature on scoring architecture, weighting mechanisms, and public reporting in hospital and health service accreditation systems. Among the 27 sources included in this review, no eligible publication identified in this review explicitly and substantively examined scoring architecture, weighting mechanisms, and public reporting in hospital and health service accreditation systems. In the 27 sources found, no published evidence was identified in this review that directly examined weighting algorithm, aggregation logic, or composite score construction as primary research questions. Although the concept of accreditation has been thoroughly reviewed as the standards-based intervention, the performance measurement systems through which standards are converted into certifying outcomes have not been studied in depth. The results indicate that accreditation research has focused predominantly on outcome measurement while largely neglecting the measurement architecture that generates those outcomes. Even though it is generally accepted that methodological constraints of impact evaluation represent a major issue, there is limited scrutiny of the scoring transparency and internal design. This gap also takes on a more policy-relevant aspect as accreditation becomes more and more connected to reimbursement eligibility, regulatory recognition and institutional positioning. Future studies ought to go beyond the binary accreditation classifications to investigate the role of weighting structures, threshold models and aggregation rules on organizational behaviour and distributive outcomes. Higher disclosure of scoring design that involves better expression of weighting structures would enhance interpretability, accountability and conformity to public reporting systems. To promote the research on accreditation, it is desirable that we do not consider whether accreditation works but rather how certification decisions are built and how their structure influences the result of governance.
