Quantum Merlin-Arthur with an internally separable proof
Roozbeh Bassirian, Bill Fefferman, Itai Leigh, Kunal Marwaha, Pei Wu

Abstract
We find a modification to 𝖰𝖬𝖠 {\mathsf{QMA}} where having one quantum proof is strictly less powerful than having two unentangled proofs, assuming 𝖤𝖷𝖯 ≠ 𝖭𝖤𝖷𝖯 {\mathsf{EXP}}\neq{\mathsf{NEXP}} . This gives a new route to prove 𝖰𝖬𝖠 ⁡ ( 2 ) = 𝖭𝖤𝖷𝖯 {\mathsf{QMA}}(2)={\mathsf{NEXP}} that overcomes the primary drawback of a recent approach [ JW23 , BFM23 ] (QIP 2024). Our modification endows each proof with a form of multipartite unentanglement: after tracing out one register, a small number of qubits are separable from the rest of the state.

Introduction

Entanglement is a fundamental property of quantum mechanics. One approach to understand this phenomenon is through the lens of computational complexity.
For example, characterizing the power of
{\mathsf{QMA}}(2)
is now one of the central questions in quantum complexity theory, and has been unresolved for two decades (e.g.
[
KMY03
,
ABDFS09
,
HM13
]
).
We make progress on this question by finding a nearby model of computation where one quantum proof is strictly less powerful than two
unentangled
quantum proofs, unless
{\mathsf{EXP}}={\mathsf{NEXP}}
.

{\mathsf{QMA}}
— a quantum variant of
{\mathsf{NP}}
(see
[
Gha23
]
for a complete review) — is the class of decision problems that can be decided with access to a quantum proof.
Here, a quantum computer (“Arthur”) communicates with a dishonest but all-powerful machine (“Merlin”).
Merlin sends a quantum state (“proof”) that is most likely to convince Arthur of a statement’s veracity.
If the statement is true, there is a proof that convinces Arthur to accept with high probability; if it is false, all proofs make Arthur reject with high probability.

In
{\mathsf{QMA}}(2)
,
the quantum proof is guaranteed to be

not
entangled across a fixed bipartition —
equivalently, the two parts are given by two
unentangled
Merlins.
It is natural to wonder what additional power comes from this entanglement structure.
Although there is evidence that
{\mathsf{QMA}}(2)
is more powerful than
{\mathsf{QMA}}

[
BT12
,
CD10
,
Per12
,
GSSSY18
]
, we still
only know the trivial bounds
{\mathsf{QMA}}\subseteq{\mathsf{QMA}}(2)\subseteq{\mathsf{NEXP}}
.

What is the power of the complexity class
{\mathsf{QMA}}(2)
?

In a recent attempt to answer this question,
[
JW23
]
proposed a modification to
{\mathsf{QMA}}
they named
{\mathsf{QMA}}^{+}
, where the proof is required to have non-negative amplitudes. They used this model to exacerbate the difference between
{\mathsf{QMA}}
and
{\mathsf{QMA}}(2)
, showing that
{\mathsf{QMA}}^{+}(2)
with a constant completeness-soundness gap equals
{\mathsf{NEXP}}
. This result is particularly interesting because
{\mathsf{QMA}}^{+}(k)
with a large enough gap is equal to
{\mathsf{QMA}}(k)
; therefore, if
{\mathsf{QMA}}^{+}(2)
admits gap amplification, then
{\mathsf{QMA}}(2)={\mathsf{NEXP}}
.
However, more recently it was discovered
[
BFM23
]

that
{\mathsf{QMA}}^{+}
at some gap equals
{\mathsf{NEXP}}
as well.
Thus, if there exists a procedure to gap amplify
{\mathsf{QMA}}^{+}(2)
, it must crucially use unentanglement.

In this work, we propose a different strategy to highlight the difference between

We match the main results of the previous attempt while overcoming the primary drawback, giving a new approach to prove

1.2
Other implications

A complexity-theoretic framework to study multipartite entanglement.

Despite much attention given to
{\mathsf{QMA}}(2)
, entanglement can be much richer than simply across a bipartition.
For example, the W state
\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\left(\ket{001}+\ket{010}+\ket{100}\right)
and GHZ state
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{000}+\ket{111}\right)
are entangled in
inequivalent
ways:
both states are entangled across every bipartition, yet the states are not equivalent under LOCC
[
DVC00
]
.
The same paper observes that
tracing out a qubit from the W state gives an entangled state, but the same action on the GHZ state destroys all entanglement.

{\mathsf{QMA}}_{\mathsf{IS}}
allows us to probe one type of multipartite (un)entanglement from the lens of computational complexity, defined as
partial semi-separability
in
[
BM01
]
.
It is intuitive to study the power of
{\mathsf{QMA}}
with other kinds of multipartite entanglement guarantees. As a first step, we show that restricting to quantum proofs that are pure and
multiseparable
3
3

3

See
Appendix

E
for a definition of multiseparability.

[
Tha99
]

can also decide
{\mathsf{NEXP}}
:

Corollary 1.6
.

There exist constants
1>c>s>0
where
{\mathsf{QMA}}^{c,s}
restricted to pure, multiseparable quantum proofs can decide
{\mathsf{NEXP}}
.

These results motivate a general question about the power of multipartite entanglement:

Question 1.7
.

Which notions of
multipartite unentanglement
are computationally strong? Why?

A unifying perspective:
{\mathsf{QMA}}(2)
and purity testing.

Many works
[
LCV07
,
HM13
,
BKS17
,
YSNG22
]
use the fact that a protocol over separable states is equivalently a protocol over states with a purity constraint.
This suggests that
purity testing
is at the heart of
{\mathsf{QMA}}(2)
’s power, even if it seems like a weaker primitive.
In our case,
{\mathsf{QMA}}_{\mathsf{IS}}(2)
is no more powerful than
{\mathsf{QMA}}_{\mathsf{IS}}
with a purity test.
The proof of
Theorem

1.2
only uses the two unentangled quantum proofs to implement a SWAP test, ensuring that Merlin sends a
pure
quantum state.
We show that optimizing over
purifications
of separable states is enough to decide
{\mathsf{NEXP}}
, and even hard to approximate:

Corollary 1.8
.

Given any
M\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, consider the optimization problem

\displaystyle\max_{\rho\in{\mathcal{W}_{\ket{\mathsf{IS}}}}}\,\Tr[M\rho]\,,

where
{\mathcal{W}_{\ket{\mathsf{IS}}}}
is the set of
n

pure
states that the prover in
{\mathsf{QMA}}_{\mathsf{IS}}
can send.
Then there exists a constant
0<\alpha<1
for which it is

{\mathsf{NP}}
-hard to compute an
\alpha
-approximation of this problem.

By contrast, optimizing over separable states (of
\textnormal{poly}(n)
dimension) is known to be
{\mathsf{NP}}
-hard, but with gap decreasing with system size
[
Gur03
,
ABDFS09
,
Gha10
,
BT12
,
Per12
,
GNN12
]
.
If the gap in these problems could be made constant, then
{\mathsf{QMA}}(2)={\mathsf{NEXP}}
.
4
4

4

One must also assume that the problems can be made succinct.

More generally, with mild conditions on a set of quantum states
\mathcal{W}
, we show
how a
{\mathsf{QMA}}(2)
protocol with each proof restricted to
\mathcal{W}
can implement a
{\mathsf{QMA}}
protocol with a proof restricted to
pure states
in
\mathcal{W}
.
We use this as a recipe
to generate other computational models where one quantum proof is provably weaker than two
unentangled
quantum proofs, up to standard complexity-theoretic assumptions.
We give two examples in

{\mathsf{QMA}}^{+}
and

of

Each set represents the

\mathcal{W}
.
Then,
{\mathsf{QMA}}_{\mathcal{W}}
can be decided with a semidefinite program, but
{\mathsf{QMA}}_{\mathcal{W}}(2)
can restrict to the more capable pure states.
This technique can likely generate other examples that demonstrate the power of unentanglement.

1.3
Techniques

To show
Theorem

1.1
, we rely on a well-known duality of membership testing of a convex set and optimizing a convex function over this set. We use this duality to reduce any
{\mathsf{QMA}}_{\mathsf{IS}}
problem to membership testing of the set of internally separable quantum states
{\mathcal{W}_{\mathsf{IS}}}
.
In order to use tools from convex optimization, we represent quantum states as vectors in a generalized Bloch sphere. We use a non-ellipsoidal reduction that permits an error analysis matching our setup
[
Liu07
,
Gha10
]
.

We then show how to test membership of
{\mathcal{W}_{\mathsf{IS}}}
in
{\mathsf{EXP}}
.
It is essential that only
O(1)
qubits are unentangled from the rest of the subsystem.
Our algorithm tests membership of two convex sets whose intersection forms
{\mathcal{W}_{\mathsf{IS}}}
; note that this requires testers with inverse exponential precision.
The first set represents all Bloch vectors of quantum states, which can be tested using exponentially many explicit conditions (e.g.
[
Kim03
]
).
The second set represents all vectors whose reduced density matrix on the subsystem is separable.
This can be tested by solving the Doherty-Parrilo-Spedalieri SDP
[
DPS02
,
DPS04
,
NOP09
]
which decides if a state has a
symmetric extension
. A careful error analysis shows that separability of a bipartite state
\rho
with local dimensions
M,N
can be
tested
to error
\delta
in time
\tilde{O}((2/\delta)^{9N}\textnormal{poly}(M,N))

[
Ioa07
]
. When
N
is a fixed constant, this SDP runs in exponential time.

To prove
Theorem

1.2
, we consider
{\mathsf{QMA}}_{\ket{\mathsf{IS}}}
, a variant of
{\mathsf{QMA}}_{\mathsf{IS}}
where the proof must also be a
pure state
. Note that unlike
{\mathsf{QMA}}
and
{\mathsf{QMA}}(2)
, restricting
{\mathsf{QMA}}_{\mathsf{IS}}
to pure states can change the power of the complexity class, as an internally separable ensemble of pure states may not decompose into an
ensemble
of internally separable pure states.
5
5

5

For example, the same is true in
{\mathsf{QMA}}^{+}
: this is the difference between doubly non-negative matrices and completely positive matrices.

We then develop a
{\mathsf{QMA}}_{\ket{\mathsf{IS}}}
protocol for an
{\mathsf{NEXP}}
-complete problem, building on
the proof of
{\mathsf{QMA}}^{+}={\mathsf{NEXP}}

[
BT12
,
JW23
,
BFM23
]
.

As in
[
JW23
,
BFM23
]
, we consider a succinct constraint satisfaction problem (CSP) with constant promise gap, which is
{\mathsf{NEXP}}
-hard by the PCP theorem
[
AS92
,
ALMSS98
,
Har04
]
. In the
{\mathsf{QMA}}^{+}
protocol, one enforces a
rigidity
property of the quantum proof: the state must be close to
\frac{1}{\sqrt{R}}\sum_{j\in[R]}\ket{j}\ket{a_j}
.
It is easy to ensure that all constraints
j\in[R]
are included in the state (“density”), but the positive-amplitude guarantee is used to ensure there is at most one assignment
a_{j}
per constraint (“quasirigidity”).
We find a way to instead enforce quasirigidity using both internal separability
and
purity.

Lemma 1.9

(informal)
.

There is a test
A
and a fixed quantum circuit
C
such that for any internally separable
\ket{\psi}
, if
A(\ket{\psi})\geq 1-\epsilon
, then
C\ket{\psi}
has
1-O(\epsilon)
squared overlap with a
quasirigid
state.

In our
{\mathsf{QMA}}_{\ket{\mathsf{IS}}}
protocol, the verifier enforces tripartite proofs of the form
\sum_{j,a}z_{ja}\ket{j}\ket{a,j}\ket{a}
.
This can be done by measuring all registers and accepting iff we see two copies of the same constraint
j
, and two copies of the same assignment
a
.
We then observe that having multiple assignments per constraint adds entanglement to the second and third registers, even after tracing out the first register. Thus, if the state is
internally separable
, it has high overlap with a state with one assignment
a
per constraint
j
.
Notably, this test is inspired by recasting the
{\mathsf{QMA}}^{+}
protocol in the language of “superposition detection” and non-collapsing measurement
[
BM24
,
AGIMR24
]
,
an unphysical
operation related to a problem of Aaronson
[
Aar23
]
.

We then prove that
{\mathsf{QMA}}_{\ket{\mathsf{IS}}}\subseteq{\mathsf{QMA}}_{\mathsf{IS}}(2)
.
Without loss of generality, the best proof Merlin can send in a
{\mathsf{QMA}}_{\mathsf{IS}}(2)
protocol is a product state
\rho_{1}\otimes\rho_{2}
, since separable states form the convex hull of the set of product states.
The SWAP test accepts a product state with probability
\frac{1}{2}(1+\Tr[\rho_{1}\rho_{2}])
: this is
1
only if
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}
are
pure
.
The
{\mathsf{QMA}}_{\mathsf{IS}}(2)
simulator thus applies a SWAP test with some probability, and otherwise applies the
{\mathsf{QMA}}_{\ket{\mathsf{IS}}}
circuit.

In the area of convex optimization, a convex set

\displaystyle\rho=\frac{\mathbb{I}}{M}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{M^{2}-1}\vec{r}_{i}\sigma_{i}\,.

We only consider problems where

We conclude this section with a few facts about Bloch vector representations of quantum states. To start, the norm of the Bloch vector is always bounded by a constant:

Claim 2.5

(Folklore)
.

Every Bloch vector has norm at most
R=\sqrt{2}
.

Proof.

Fix any quantum state
\rho
, and let
\vec{r}
be the associated Bloch vector. Then

\displaystyle 1\geq\Tr[\rho^{2}]=\frac{1}{M}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{M^{2}-1}\vec{r}_{i}~^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}\|\vec{r}\,\|^{2}\,.

∎

Moreover, Bloch vectors with small enough norm are separable across every bipartition:

Fact 2.6

(
[
GB02
]
)
.

Fix any quantum state
\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{C}^{M})
, and let
\vec{r}
be the associated Bloch vector. If
\|\vec{r}\,\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{M^{2}}
, then
\rho
is separable across every bipartition.

Corollary 2.7
.

The set of Bloch vectors representing separable states in
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{C}^{M},\mathbb{C}^{N})
is well-bounded
\vec{0}
-centered with
r=\frac{1}{M^{2}N^{2}}
and
R=\sqrt{2}
.

Finally, the Euclidean distance of two vectors is upper-bounded by a constant times the trace distance of their associated matrices:

Claim 2.8
.

Consider two vectors
\vec{r},\vec{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{M^{2}-1}
, and let
\rho
and
\tau
be the associated

matrices according to
Eq.

2.1
. Then
\|\vec{r}-\vec{t}\,\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{2}\|\rho-\tau\|_{1}
.

Proof.

Note that
\|\rho-\tau\|_{1}\geq\|\rho-\tau\|_{2}
by monotonicity of Schatten norms. Moreover,

\displaystyle\|\rho-\tau\|_{2}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i,j=1}^{M^{2}-1}(\vec{r}_{i}-\vec{t}_{i})(\vec{r}_{j}-\vec{t}_{j})\Tr[\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}]}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{M^{2}-1}(\vec{r}_{i}-\vec{t}_{i})^{2}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\|\vec{r}-\vec{t}\|_{2}\,.

∎

3.1
Showing that

We first verify that

Claim 3.1
.

{\mathcal{W}_{\mathsf{IS}}}
is a convex, well-bounded
\vec{0}
-centered set with radii
R=\sqrt{2},r=\frac{1}{4^{p(n)}}

Proof.

We first check convexity. Consider
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\in{\mathcal{W}_{\mathsf{IS}}}

and
\Tr_{A}[\rho_{2}]

{\mathcal{W}_{\mathsf{IS}}}
is convex when viewed as density matrices. Since the transformation from density matrix to Bloch vector is linear,
{\mathcal{W}_{\mathsf{IS}}}
is also convex when viewed as Bloch vectors.

By

, all Bloch vectors have norm at most

. Moreover, by

and are thus internally separable. So
{\mathcal{W}_{\mathsf{IS}}}
is well-bounded
\vec{0}
-centered with

.
∎

It will be useful to represent
{\mathcal{W}_{\mathsf{IS}}}
as the intersection of two sets
{\mathcal{W}_{\mathsf{IS}}}=K_{1}\cap K_{2}

Remark 3.2
.

When the Bloch vector encoding uses the generators

associated with Pauli words, the Bloch vector of a

is a subset of the coordinates of the original Bloch vector, multiplied to a global normalization.

11

11

The normalizing factor is exactly sqrt of the dimension of register

\Tr[\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}]=2\delta_{ij}

In particular,
\vec{r}_{i}=\Tr[\rho\sigma_{i}]
corresponds to an element of the Bloch vector of the subsystem if and only if

is

Claim 3.3
.

K_{1}

are convex, compact, well-bounded

-centered sets, with associated radii

Proof.

We start with convexity. The set of all

-qubit states is convex; since the transformation from density matrix to Bloch vector is linear,

is also convex. The set of separable states is convex, by the same argument, so are the associated Bloch vectors.
K_{2}
is the set of all vectors which match a separable Bloch vector on a fixed set of coordinates, intersected with the solid hypercube
[-2,2]^{4^{p(n)}-1}
. Both sets are convex (and
K_{2}
is non-empty), so
K_{2}
is convex.

All Bloch vectors have norm at most
\sqrt{2}
. Vectors in
K_{2}
have norm at most
\sqrt{2\cdot(4^{p(n)}-1)}
. Since we have
{\mathcal{W}_{\mathsf{IS}}}\subseteq K_{1}
and
{\mathcal{W}_{\mathsf{IS}}}\subseteq K_{2}
, both
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
contain a
\vec{0}
-centered ball of radius
r=\frac{1}{4^{p(n)}}
by
Claim

3.1
.

The set of all density matrices is compact (e.g.
[
ŻB17
]
).
Since the transformation from density matrix to Bloch vectors is linear,
K_{1}
is also compact.
By Heine-Borel,

is compact iff it is closed and bounded. It remains to show closure. The set of Bloch vectors representing separable states is closed (e.g.
[

is the Cartesian product of this set with the closed set

is closed.
∎

Claim 3.4
.

{\mathcal{W}_{\mathsf{IS}}}
is compact.

Proof.

By Heine-Borel, we can prove compactness by showing that

is closed and bounded.
{\mathcal{W}_{\mathsf{IS}}}
is bounded because the norm is always at most

.
{\mathcal{W}_{\mathsf{IS}}}
is closed because it is the intersection of two closed sets

∎

3.2
Proving the reduction

The first step is straightforward. Recall the definition of
WVAL
from
Section

2
. Observe that the maximum acceptance probability of any
{\mathsf{QMA}}_{\mathsf{IS}}
protocol with verifier circuit

can be written as
\max_{\rho\in{\mathcal{W}_{\mathsf{IS}}}}\Tr[V\rho]
.
Thus, we can decide this problem if we can decide the value
\max_{\rho\in{\mathcal{W}_{\mathsf{IS}}}}\Tr[V\rho]
with good enough precision.

Claim 3.5
.

Every problem in
{\mathsf{QMA}}_{\mathsf{IS}}
with completeness-soundness gap
\Delta
has an exponential-time reduction to
\textnormal{WVAL}_{\epsilon}({\mathcal{W}_{\mathsf{IS}}})
, where
\epsilon=\Omega(\frac{\Delta}{2^{\textnormal{poly}(n)}})

Proof.

Suppose the verification circuit is
V
, and let the proof be a state on
p(n)
qubits.
We represent
V
as a vector
\vec{c}
, so the acceptance probability of
V
on
\rho
is affine to the inner product of
\vec{c}
with the Bloch vector representation of
\rho
.
The quantity
\Tr[V\rho]
can be decomposed via

2.1

\displaystyle\Tr[V\rho]=\frac{1}{2^{p(n)}}\Tr[V]+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{4^{p(n)}-1}\vec{r}_{i}\cdot\Tr[V\sigma_{i}]\,,

where
\vec{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{4^{p(n)}-1}
is the Bloch vector representation of
\rho

Let
\vec{d}
be the vector where
\vec{d}_{i}=\Tr[V\sigma_{i}]

\vec{c}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}\frac{\vec{d}}{\|\vec{d}\,\|}

with

Since
V

Suppose the

\displaystyle\vec{c}\cdot\vec{r}=\frac{2}{\|\vec{d}\,\|}\left(\Tr[V\rho]-\frac{1}{2^{p(n)}}\Tr[V]\right)\leq\frac{2}{\|\vec{d}\,\|}\left(s-\frac{1}{2^{p(n)}}\Tr[V]\right)\,.

(3.1)

The
{\mathsf{QMA}}_{\mathsf{IS}}

\displaystyle\vec{c}\cdot\vec{r}\geq\frac{2}{\|\vec{d}\,\|}\left(s-\frac{1}{2^{p(n)}}\Tr[V]\right)+\frac{2}{\|\vec{d}\,\|}\Delta\,.

(3.2)

Define the parameter

\gamma\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}\frac{2}{\|\vec{d}\,\|}\left(s-\frac{1}{2^{p(n)}}\Tr[V]\right)+\frac{1}{\|\vec{d}\,\|}\Delta.

We verify this problem can be solved by the

\textnormal{WVAL}_{\epsilon/4^{p(n)+1}}({\mathcal{W}_{\mathsf{IS}}})
for

\displaystyle\epsilon\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}\min\left(\frac{1}{2\cdot 4^{p(n)}},\frac{\Delta}{2\|\vec{d}\,\|}\right)\,.

•

In the soundness case, consider
\vec{y}\in S({\mathcal{W}_{\mathsf{IS}}},\epsilon)

So by Cauchy-Schwarz, for unit vector

\displaystyle\vec{c}\cdot\vec{y}=\vec{c}\cdot\vec{x}+\vec{c}\cdot\left(\vec{y}-\vec{x}\right)\leq\vec{c}\cdot\vec{x}+\|\vec{c}\,\|\|\vec{y}-\vec{x}\|\leq\vec{c}\cdot\vec{x}+\epsilon\,.

Since for any

In completeness, there is an

. We split into two cases, depending on the norm of

–

If

This is because by

Otherwise, define

\displaystyle\vec{c}\cdot\vec{z}=\vec{c}\cdot\vec{r}+\vec{c}\cdot(\vec{z}-\vec{r})\geq\vec{c}\cdot\vec{r}-\epsilon\geq\gamma+\epsilon\,.

Finally, by a geometric argument

,

B_{\ell}(\vec{z})\subseteq{\mathcal{W}_{\mathsf{IS}}}

Theorem 3.8

4
Purity and internal separability:
{\mathsf{QMA}}_{\ket{\mathsf{IS}}}={\mathsf{NEXP}}

In this section we prove there exist constants
c,s
representing completeness and soundness for which
{\mathsf{QMA}}_{\ket{\mathsf{IS}}}^{c,s}
(the variant of
{\mathsf{QMA}}_{\mathsf{IS}}^{c,s}
restricted to pure quantum proofs) can decide
{\mathsf{NEXP}}
.

The starting point of our approach is the Blier-Tapp protocol to decide the
{\mathsf{NP}}
-hard
3-COLORING

graph problem
[
BT12
]
in
{\mathsf{QMA}}_{\log}(2)
, i.e.
{\mathsf{QMA}}(2)
with two unentangled quantum proofs each on
O(\log n)
qubits.
In their protocol, the classical
{\mathsf{NP}}
proof is encoded in
O(\log n)
-qubit quantum states of the form
\ket{\psi}=\sum_{v\in V}\ket{v}\ket{c_v}
, where
V
is the set of vertices of the graph and
c_{v}
is a color assigned to vertex
v
. So
\ket{\psi}
encodes a coloring to the graph.
With two copies of a quantum proof in this form, the verifier can measure both in the computational basis, obtaining two random vertices
v,w
with their assigned colors
c_{v},c_{w}
.
If the two measured vertices are neighbors, one can check whether or not the color constraint is satisfied on this edge.
When the two proofs are
unentangled
, Arthur can verify that the proofs have the “correct” form: (i) each vertex is assigned exactly one color, and (ii) the coloring is consistent across the two proofs.

The catch is that the protocol decides
{\mathsf{NP}}
with an
inverse polynomial
completeness-soundness gap. Thus, a straightforward application of the same protocol (for a succinct version of 3-colorability
[
PY86
]
) decides
{\mathsf{NEXP}}
with
inverse exponential
completeness-soundness gap. This is too small a gap for
{\mathsf{QMA}}(2)
.
13
13

13

Intuitively, Arthur most often sees two vertices which are not connected by an edge and so he cannot check the validity of the coloring. In the
O(\log n)
-qubit version he saw adjacent vertices with an inverse-polynomial probability, but now this occurs with an inverse-exponential probability.

What causes this small gap in the Blier-Tapp protocol?
The first source is that when the graph is not 3-colorable, there may be only
one
edge that violates the coloring, so it is very difficult to sample this edge. This can be overcome by taking the PCP version of
3-coloring
.
The second source is that for
sparse
graphs, the probability of that two vertices chosen at random are adjacent is inverse polynomial in the number of vertices.
One may try to fix this with a
dense
graph; however, since one can efficiently approximate
3-coloring
on
dense
graphs
[
AKK99
]
, we can no longer use a PCP to fix the first issue.

To simultaneously overcome both issues in the Blier-Tapp protocol and appropriately scale up to
{\mathsf{NEXP}}
, Jeronimo and Wu
[
JW23
]
consider a different succinct gapped constraint satisfaction problem (CSP).
Their protocol follows a similar outline.
14
14

14

Each quantum proof, instead of encoding each vertex and a chosen color, now encodes a each constraint and a list of assigned values to its corresponding variables.
For ease of discussion, we continue to call the registers “vertex” and “color” registers.
This is just a difference in notation, as each constraint only uses a constant number of variables.

A key part of the protocol in
[
JW23
]
is to enforce the same “correct” form of each quantum proof, as used in the Blier-Tapp protocol. This form was later named
rigid
in
[
BFM23
]
:

Definition 4.1

(Rigid and quasirigid states)
.

A bipartite state
\ket{\psi}\in\mathbb{C}^{R}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{\kappa}
is called quasirigid if it is of the form
\ket{\psi}=\sum_{v\in[R]}\alpha_{v}\ket{v}\ket{c_v}
for some complex numbers
\{\alpha_{v}\}_{v\in[R]}
and indices
\{c_{v}\in[\kappa]\}_{v\in[R]}
, and moreover called
rigid
if all
\alpha_{v}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{R}}
.

However,
[
JW23
]
only managed to enforce the
rigidity
of each quantum proof by requiring additional structure, i.e.
the proofs are unentangled,
and
have non-negative amplitudes in the computational basis.
Bassirian, Fefferman, and Marwaha
[
BFM23
]
show how to use only the non-negative amplitudes requirement (i.e. without imposing unentanglement) to get the same result.
This is done using two distinct tests: (i) check that each vertex is assigned exactly one color (i.e.
quasirigid
or
valid
), and (ii) check that every vertex is represented in the proof with nontrivial weight.

In this section we go the other direction: we enforce that a quantum proof is
rigid
for pure states with our specific entanglement structure of
internal separability
.
We closely follow the recipe of
[
BFM23
,
BM24
]
, adjusting some of their claims (especially
[
BFM23
, Lemma 10]
) to our scenario.

4.1
A warm-up with the computational basis

In
[
BM24
]
, Bassirian and Marwaha create a framework to unify the previous results. They observed that a variant of
{\mathsf{QMA}}
, where Arthur is equipped with a fantastical operation they call a “superposition detector”, can decide
{\mathsf{NEXP}}
. This operation inputs a quantum state, and decides whether the quantum state is a computational basis element, or
\epsilon
-far from any such element.
It empowers Arthur to enforce condition (i) (quasirigidity) — i.e. ensuring the proof has one color per vertex.
For condition (ii), Arthur needn’t any special equipment.
He tests that all vertices are in the proof with similar weight by projecting to the uniform superposition state
\ket{+}
. While this test is simple, it comes at a price: the honest proof state passes it only with probability
1/\kappa
, strictly less than
1
.
This creates some difficulty when choosing the probabilities of
running each test in a way that decides the problem, but it typically can be overcome.

superposition detector can be easily implemented given two copies of
\ket{\psi}
: measure each copy in the computational basis, and accept iff the outcomes agree.
But in
[
BM24
]
, this operation is applied to a single quantum proof after a partial measurement in the computational basis.
Nonetheless,
[
BM24
]
shows how to efficiently implement this operation if the proof has a particular form, which includes states with non-negative amplitudes (showing the main result of
[
BFM23
]
within the framework).

Here, we warm up by considering two entanglement guarantees that are stronger than
{\mathcal{W}_{\ket{\mathsf{IS}}}}
.
We show how to decide
{\mathsf{NEXP}}
with each entanglement guarantee using the framework of
[
BM24
]
.
We progressively relax the guarantees, eventually working with
{\mathcal{W}_{\ket{\mathsf{IS}}}}
in the next subsection.

We start with a set of tripartite proofs — where the first register may contain
O(\textnormal{poly}(n))
qubits, and the other two registers are of constant size — that roughly corresponds to having “two copies” of the color register. Precisely, the set is
{\mathcal{W}_{\ket{\textnormal{dup}}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}\{\ket{\psi}\ |\ \ket{\psi}=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}\ket{i}\ket{\phi_i}\ket{\phi_i}\}
.
Thus, after measuring any outcome
\ket{i}
, the reduced state is some
\ket{\phi_i}\ket{\phi_i}
.
Since the reduced state has two copies, we can implement a superposition detector and enforce condition (i).
Thus, a quantum proof from this state must be close to
\sum_{v}\alpha_{v}\ket{v}\ket{c_v}\ket{c_v}
(where
\ket{c_v}
is a computational basis state).
We may convert such a state to a
quasirigid
state by applying a
CNOT
on the last two registers. From here, the framework of
[
BM24
]
shows how to choose the test probabilities to enforce a rigid state, and thus decide
{\mathsf{NEXP}}
.

We now slightly relax the guarantee by considering the set of computational basis purifications of separable states:
{\mathcal{W}_{\ket{\textnormal{comp}}}}=\{\ket{\psi}\ |\ \ket{\psi}=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}\ket{i}\ket{\mu_i}\ket{\nu_i}\}
. The difference between
{\mathcal{W}_{\ket{\textnormal{dup}}}}
and
{\mathcal{W}_{\ket{\textnormal{comp}}}}
is that
{\mathcal{W}_{\ket{\textnormal{comp}}}}
contains states where
\ket{\mu_i}
and
\ket{\nu_i}
are different. We claim that a similar argument as before shows
{\mathsf{QMA}}_{\ket{\textnormal{comp}}}={\mathsf{NEXP}}
.

Claim 4.2

(Inspired by
[
BM24
, Claim 21]
)
.

Consider a quantum state of the form
\ket{\mu}\ket{\nu}
, where each register has
k
qubits. For
0<\epsilon\leq 1/2
, there is an efficient quantum protocol that decides if (YES)
\ket{\mu}\ket{\nu}
is two copies of a computational basis element
\ket{e}\ket{e}
, or (NO)
\epsilon
-far in trace distance from any such basis element, with completeness and soundness gap
2x(1-x)
for
x=\min(\epsilon,\sqrt{1-\epsilon},\sqrt{\frac{1-\epsilon}{2^{k}}})
.

Proof.

Let
\ket{\mu}=\sum_{j}\beta_{j}\ket{j}
and
\ket{\nu}=\sum_{j}\delta_{j}\ket{j}
, where
\sum_{j}|\beta_{j}|^{2}=\sum_{j}|\delta_{j}|^{2}=1
. The protocol measures each state in the computational basis and accepts iff they agree. This succeeds with probability

\displaystyle\sum_{j}|\beta_{j}\delta_{j}|^{2}\,.

In the YES case,
\ket{\mu}=\ket{\nu}
both are equal to some computational basis element
\ket{j}
, and so the protocol succeeds with probability
1
.

In the NO case,
|\beta_{j}\delta_{j}|^{2}=|\bra{\mu,\nu}\ket{j,j}|^{2}\leq 1-\epsilon
for every computational basis element
\ket{j}
. We suppose the probability of success is at least
1-2\epsilon
, since otherwise there is a completeness-soundness gap of
2\epsilon
. Let
j^{*}
be a value that maximizes
|\beta_{j}\delta_{j}|^{2}
(break ties arbitrarily). Then
|\beta_{j^{*}}\delta_{j^{*}}|^{2}\geq\frac{1-2\epsilon}{2^{k}}
. So the probability of success is

\displaystyle\sum_{j}|\beta_{j}\delta_{j}|^{2}

\displaystyle=|\beta_{j^{*}}\delta_{j^{*}}|^{2}+\sum_{j\neq j^{*}}|\beta_{j}\delta_{j}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq|\beta_{j^{*}}\delta_{j^{*}}|^{2}+\big(\sum_{j\neq j^{*}}|\beta_{j}|\cdot|\delta_{j}|\big)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq|\beta_{j^{*}}\delta_{j^{*}}|^{2}+\big(\sum_{j\neq j^{*}}|\beta_{j}|^{2}\big)\cdot\big(\sum_{j\neq j^{*}}|\delta_{j}|^{2}\big)

\displaystyle\leq|\beta_{j^{*}}\delta_{j^{*}}|^{2}+(1-|\beta_{j^{*}}|^{2})\cdot(1-|\delta_{j^{*}}|^{2})\,.

The second inequality follows by Cauchy-Schwarz. We next invoke the AM-GM inequality: for non-negative
x,y
, we have
2\sqrt{xy}\leq x+y
. This implies

\displaystyle(1-x)(1-y)=1-x-y+xy\leq 1-2\sqrt{xy}+xy={(1-\sqrt{xy})}^{2}\,.

So the probability of acceptance is at most
|\beta_{j^{*}}\delta_{j^{*}}|^{2}+(1-|\beta_{j^{*}}\delta_{j^{*}}|)^{2}=1-2|\beta_{j^{*}}\delta_{j^{*}}|\cdot(1-|\beta_{j^{*}}\delta_{j^{*}}|)
.
This expression is maximized at the smallest and largest values of
|\beta_{j^{*}}\delta_{j^{*}}|
. By definition of the NO case,
|\beta_{j^{*}}\delta_{j^{*}}|\leq\sqrt{1-\epsilon}
, and by assumption,
|\beta_{j^{*}}\delta_{j^{*}}|\geq\sqrt{\frac{1-\epsilon}{2^{k}}}
.∎

Since we can still implement the superposition detector with a single proof from
{\mathcal{W}_{\ket{\textnormal{comp}}}}
, we may use the framework of
[
BM24
]
to enforce rigidity of our proof (up to a

gate), and so
{\mathsf{QMA}}_{\ket{\textnormal{comp}}}={\mathsf{NEXP}}
.

4.2
Handling purifications in an arbitrary basis

In
{\mathcal{W}_{\ket{\textnormal{comp}}}}
, after a computational basis measurement in the first register, the post-measurement state is
always
separable. We took advantage of this structure to implement a superposition detector in
Claim

4.2
.
Unfortunately, showing that
{\mathsf{QMA}}_{\ket{\mathsf{IS}}}={\mathsf{NEXP}}
is not quite as simple.
The main difference between
{\mathcal{W}_{\ket{\mathsf{IS}}}}
and
{\mathcal{W}_{\ket{\textnormal{comp}}}}
is that in
{\mathcal{W}_{\ket{\mathsf{IS}}}}
, the purification register can be oriented in any basis.
For example, the below state belongs to
{\mathcal{W}_{\ket{\mathsf{IS}}}}
, but not
{\mathcal{W}_{\ket{\textnormal{comp}}}}
:

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{+}\ket{0}\ket{0}+\ket{-}\ket{1}\ket{1})=\tfrac{1}{2}(\ket{0}(\ket{00}+\ket{11})+\ket{1}(\ket{00}-\ket{11}))\,.

To amend this issue, we add data to the second register to mimic the behavior of states in
{\mathcal{W}_{\ket{\textnormal{comp}}}}
.

Consider states on four registers
\mathbb{C}^{R}\otimes(\mathbb{C}^{\kappa}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{R})\otimes\mathbb{C}^{\kappa}
, where
R=2^{\textnormal{poly}(n)}
and
\kappa
is some constant. Let
p_{1},p_{2}
be probabilities that we set later. Our protocol is as follows:

•

Enforce “quasirigidity”.
With probability
p_{1}
run
\mathsf{MatchCheck}
— measure all the registers in the computational basis, and make sure it is of the form
\ket{v}\ket{c}\ket{v}\ket{c}
(i.e. the first pair of outcomes
matches
the second pair of outcomes).

•

Enforce “density”.
With probability
p_{2}
run
\text{CNOT}_{1,3}
(i.e. applied to the first and third register), then
\text{CNOT}_{2,4}
, and check
\mathsf{Density}
— measure the first two registers in the Hadamard (
X
) basis and accept if all the qubits are
\ket{+}
.

•

Estimate CSP value.
Otherwise run
\text{CNOT}_{1,3}
and
\text{CNOT}_{2,4}
, and then run the constraint tests of
[
BFM23
]
on the first two registers, which check that the encoded classical assignment satisfies the CSP.

Why does this work? It turns out that
\mathsf{MatchCheck}
combined with the internal separability guarantee enforces the state (after applying CNOTs) to be close to
quasirigid
. Adding the
\mathsf{Density}
check (after CNOTs) enforces it to be close to
rigid
. However, to show this, we must reprove adjusted versions of lemmas from
[

,
BM24
]
.

Consider an arbitrary tripartite state

\displaystyle\ket{\psi}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}\sum_{v,c,w,d}a_{vc,wd}\ket{v}_{A}\ket{c,w}_{B}\ket{d}_{C}\,,

(4.1)

and let
\rho_{B,C}=\tr_{A}(\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi})
be the reduced density matrix after tracing out register
A
. Then the success probability of
\mathsf{MatchCheck}
is exactly
\sum_{vc}|a_{vc,vc}|^{2}
.

Now consider
\ket{\psi}
after applying
\text{CNOT}_{1,3}
and
\text{CNOT}_{2,4}
:

\displaystyle\text{CNOT}_{1,3}\text{CNOT}_{2,4}\ket{\psi}=\sum_{vc,wd}a_{vc,wd}\ket{v}\ket{c}\ket{w \oplus v}\ket{d \oplus c}\,.

The last two registers are
\ket{0}\ket{0}
exactly when
(v,c)=(w,d)
, which happens with
exactly
the probability that
\mathsf{MatchCheck}
succeeds. So conditioned on the last two registers being
\ket{0}\ket{0}
, the new state is proportional to

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{vc}a_{vc,vc}\ket{v}\ket{c}\right)\ket{0}\ket{0}\,.

It remains to show that the new state is quasirigid.
Suppose
\mathsf{MatchCheck}
passes with high probability; then for a typical
\ket{v}_{A}
in state
Eq.

4.1

\ket{cc}
for allowed colors

Since we have that state
\rho_{B,C}
is

\ket{\psi}
must be close to quasirigid.

To prove this claim formally, we require a technical lemma:

Lemma 4.3
.

Consider a state
\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{C}^{m},\mathbb{C}^{n})

\displaystyle|\bra{wx}\rho\ket{yz}|\leq\sqrt{\min(\bra{wx}\rho\ket{wx},\bra{wz}\rho\ket{wz},\bra{yx}\rho\ket{yx},\bra{yz}\rho\ket{yz})}\,,

and moreover,

\displaystyle|\bra{wx}\rho\ket{yz}|\leq\frac{1}{2}\cdot\min(\bra{wx}\rho\ket{wx}+\bra{yz}\rho\ket{yz},\bra{wz}\rho\ket{wz}+\bra{yx}\rho\ket{yx})\,.

Proof.

Fix a separable decomposition of

\displaystyle\left|\bra{wx}\rho\ket{yz}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\sum_{\iota}p_{\iota}\bra{w}M_{\iota}\ket{y}\cdot\bra{x}N_{\iota}\ket{z}\right|\leq\sum_{\iota}p_{\iota}\left|\bra{w}M_{\iota}\ket{y}\right|\cdot\left|\bra{x}N_{\iota}\ket{z}\right|\,.

Recall that PSD matrices

, and so by Cauchy-Schwarz,

\displaystyle|\bra{i}A\ket{k}|^{2}=\left|(B\ket{i})^{\dagger}(B\ket{k})\right|^{2}\leq\bra{i}B^{\dagger}B\ket{i}\cdot\bra{k}B^{\dagger}B\ket{k}=\bra{i}A\ket{i}\cdot\bra{k}A\ket{k}\,,

So then
\left|\bra{wx}\rho\ket{yz}\right|\leq\sum_{\iota}p_{\iota}\sqrt{\bra{w}M_{\iota}\ket{w}\cdot\bra{y}M_{\iota}\ket{y}\cdot\bra{x}N_{\iota}\ket{x}\cdot\bra{z}N_{\iota}\ket{z}}

For any PSD matrix

, we have
0\leq\bra{\psi}A\ket{\psi}\leq\Tr(A)
. All

are trace-1, so

\displaystyle\left|\bra{wx}\rho\ket{yz}\right|\leq\sum_{\iota}p_{\iota}\sqrt{\bra{w}M_{\iota}\ket{w}\cdot\bra{z}N_{\iota}\ket{z}}\leq\sqrt{\sum_{\iota}p_{\iota}\bra{w}M_{\iota}\ket{w}\cdot\bra{z}N_{\iota}\ket{z}}=\sqrt{\bra{wz}\rho\ket{wz}}\,,

where the second inequality is Jensen’s.
The other bounds follow mutatis mutandis.

Moreover, we can use the AM-GM inequality; i.e. for non-negative

\displaystyle\left|\bra{wx}\rho\ket{yz}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\iota}p_{\iota}\left(\bra{w}M_{\iota}\ket{w}\cdot\bra{x}N_{\iota}\ket{x}+\bra{y}M_{\iota}\ket{y}\cdot\bra{z}N_{\iota}\ket{z}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\bra{wx}\rho\ket{wx}+\bra{yz}\rho\ket{yz}}{2}\,.

Similarly,

is
(\lambda_{1},\ket{\psi})
.
The fidelity of
\rho
and
\ket{\psi}
is exactly
\bra{\psi}\rho\ket{\psi}=\lambda_{1}
.
Since fidelity is non-decreasing under CPTP maps, the fidelity of
\Tr_{A}[\rho]
and
\Tr_{A}[\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}]
is at least
\lambda_{1}
.
Since

, Uhlmann’s theorem implies the existence of a purification

of

that maintains fidelity, i.e.

.

Note that by construction,

. Moreover,

\displaystyle\bra{\chi_{\rho}}\rho\ket{\chi_{\rho}}\geq\lambda_{1}\bra{\chi_{\rho}}\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}\ket{\chi_{\rho}}\geq\lambda_{1}^{2}\,.

We verify that
{\mathcal{W}_{\mathsf{IS}}}
has the continuity condition with
f(x)=2x
. Suppose
\lambda_{1}=1-x
; then the squared overlap with

is at least
(1-x)^{2}=1-(2x-x^{2})\geq 1-2x
.
Lemma

5.2
then implies that
{\mathsf{NEXP}}\subseteq{\mathsf{QMA}}_{{\mathcal{W}_{\mathsf{IS}}}^{\star}}(2)

{\mathsf{QMA}}_{{\mathcal{W}_{\mathsf{IS}}}}(2)={\mathsf{QMA}}_{\mathsf{IS}}(2)
.
∎

We show in

that
{\mathsf{QMA}}_{\mathsf{IS}}(2)
at large enough completeness-soundness gap can be simulated by
{\mathsf{QMA}}(2)
. As a consequence:

Corollary 5.5
.

Gap amplification of
{\mathsf{QMA}}_{\mathsf{IS}}(2)
implies
{\mathsf{QMA}}(2)={\mathsf{NEXP}}
.

However, by

, the single-copy variant
{\mathsf{QMA}}_{\mathsf{IS}}\subseteq{\mathsf{EXP}}
at all completeness-soundness gaps at least inverse exponential in input size. Unlike
{\mathsf{QMA}}^{+}

does not obviously imply any complexity collapse.
In other words,
{\mathsf{QMA}}_{\mathsf{IS}}(2)
has exactly the desired features of

[

]

Appendix A
Characterizing
{\mathsf{QMA}}(2)
as a purity test

The proof of
{\mathsf{QMA}}_{\mathsf{IS}}(2)={\mathsf{NEXP}}
uses the two unentangled proofs
only
to implement a purity test. We suggest this is not coincidence: many works
[
HM13
,
BKS17
,
YSWNG21
,
YSNG22
]
take advantage of the fact that every protocol over separable states is equivalently a protocol over states with a pure subsystem.
We formalize this equivalence in a self-contained way.

The optimal success probability of a
{\mathsf{QMA}}(2)
verifier
V
is
h_{sep}(V)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}\max_{\rho\in\mathsf{SEP}(X,Y)}\Tr[V\rho]

[
HM13
]
.
By convexity, we only need to optimize over pure product states
\rho=\outerproduct{\psi_X}{\psi_X}\otimes\outerproduct{\psi_Y}{\psi_Y}
. In fact, we only need to optimize over
\rho\in\mathcal{D}(X,Y)
such that
\Tr_{Y}[\rho]
is pure, since these states contain all pure product states:

Claim A.1
.

Consider a state
\rho\in\mathcal{D}(X,Y)
where
\Tr_{Y}[\rho]
is pure. Then
\rho
is a product state.

Proof.

Diagonalize
\rho=\sum_{i}p_{i}\outerproduct{\phi_i}{\phi_i}
. Since
\Tr_{Y}[\rho]=\sum_{i}p_{i}\Tr_{Y}[\outerproduct{\phi_i}{\phi_i}]
is some pure state
\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}
, all
\Tr_{Y}[\outerproduct{\phi_i}{\phi_i}]
must be proportional to
\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}
. A pure state has a pure subsystem if and only if it is separable, so
\ket{\phi_i}=\ket{\psi}\ket{\chi_i}
for all
i
. So
\rho=\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}\otimes\sum_{i}p_{i}\outerproduct{\chi_i}{\chi_i}
is a product state.
∎

Fact A.2
.

Consider a state
\rho=\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}\otimes\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi}
. Then its reduced density matrix
\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi}
is pure.

So
h_{sep}(V)
is equivalently an optimization over all states
\rho\in\mathcal{D}(X,Y)
where
\Tr_{Y}[\rho]
is pure. In other words,
h_{sep}(V)
is exactly

\displaystyle\max_{\rho\in\mathcal{D}(X,Y)}\Tr[V\rho]

such that

\displaystyle rank(\Tr_{Y}[\rho])=1\,.

Thus, the optimal acceptance probability of a
{\mathsf{QMA}}(2)
protocol can be written as an SDP with a rank-1 constraint (i.e. pure
subsystem
).

At the same time, any SDP with a rank-1 constraint can be converted to an SDP over separable states. For example, we may rewrite the above optimization as

\displaystyle\max_{\rho\in\mathsf{SEP}(XY,Y^{\prime})}\Tr[V\rho]

such that

\displaystyle\Tr_{X}[\rho]\in Sym(Y,Y^{\prime})\,.

(A.1)

Eq.

A.1
specifies the SWAP test on
(Y,Y^{\prime})
to accept with probability
1
.
This is because optimizing over separable states is equivalent to optimizing over product states, and for product states, the SWAP test passes with probability
1
iff the states are rank-1 (i.e. pure).

One can incorporate
Eq.

A.1
into a new verifier
V^{\prime}
, which with probability
p
applies the SWAP test on
(Y,Y^{\prime})
, and otherwise applies
V
. Then
\max_{\rho\in\mathsf{SEP}(XY,Y^{\prime})}\Tr[V^{\prime}\rho]
is affine to
h_{sep}(V)
, with precision depending on
p
. This is essentially the content of the product test of
[
HM13
]
.

We also describe how
{\mathsf{QMA}}(2)
essentially applies “purity testing” to extendable states.
Extendable states can be thought of as “weakly” separable.

Definition A.3

(
k
-extendable bipartite state)
.

Fix

. A state

-extendable with respect to

\sigma\in\mathcal{D}(A,B_{1},\dots,B_{k})
that is invariant on permutation of the subsystems

and satisfies

Fact A.4
.

Every separable state is
k
-extendable for all
k>1

Proof.

Any separable state

\sigma=\sum_{i}p_{i}\rho_{A,i}\otimes\rho_{B,i}^{\otimes k}

∎

In fact, a state is separable
if and only if

k
-extendable for all
k

[
DPS04
]

For

k
-extendable states for any

Fact A.5
.

Any

bipartite state
\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{D}(A,B)
is 2-extendable with respect to
B

Proof.

The backwards direction follows from
Fact

A.4
, so we focus on the forwards direction. Consider the 2-extension

\sigma=\sum_{i}p_{i}\outerproduct{\phi_i}{\phi_i}
. Note that

is pure; thus,

\Tr_{B_{1}}[\sigma]=\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}

must be separable.

density matrices
. Here, we know that two unentangled proofs are more powerful than one:
{\mathsf{QMA}}_{\mathcal{W}_{+}}\neq{\mathsf{QMA}}_{\mathcal{W}_{+}}(2)
unless
{\mathsf{EXP}}={\mathsf{NEXP}}
. This directly stems from the SWAP test (i.e. purity test) in
Lemma

5.2
. Thus, the power of
{\mathsf{QMA}}^{+}
comes not from restricting quantum proofs to non-negative elements, but from
further
restricting
\mathcal{W}_{+}
to
pure states
. Because of this,
{\mathsf{QMA}}^{+}
cannot have gap amplification (unless
{\mathsf{QMA}}={\mathsf{NEXP}}
), but
{\mathsf{QMA}}_{\mathcal{W}_{+}}
plausibly can.

D.2
Proper states

Proper states
are states of the form
\ket{\psi}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\ket{i}
.
It was shown in
[
ABDFS09
,
Bei08
]
that optimizing over proper states of polynomial dimension is
{\mathsf{NP}}
-hard with a constant gap.
We show how this can be used to build a computational model where having one quantum proof is strictly weaker than having two quantum proofs, assuming
{\mathsf{P}}\neq{\mathsf{NP}}
.

Consider the set of real density matrices with equal values on the diagonal; i.e.

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{\textnormal{proper}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}\{\rho\in\mathbb{R}\ |\ (\forall i,j)\ \rho_{ii}=\rho_{jj}\}\,.

Consider a variant of
{\mathsf{QMA}}
allowing only
O(\log n)
space, and the quantum proof restricted to states in
\mathcal{W}_{\textnormal{proper}}
. Then this class can be decided in
{\mathsf{P}}
, since there is an efficient semidefinite program to calculate the maximum acceptance probability.

On the other hand, note that the pure states of
\mathcal{W}_{\textnormal{proper}}
are exactly the proper states of
[
ABDFS09
,
Bei08
]
. By
Lemma

5.2
, the associated variant of
{\mathsf{QMA}}(2)
can simulate
{\mathsf{NP}}
, assuming that
\mathcal{W}_{\textnormal{proper}}
satisfies the continuity condition. We verify this now:

Claim D.3
.

\mathcal{W}_{\textnormal{proper}}
has the continuity condition.

Proof.

Consider any element
\rho\in\mathcal{W}_{\textnormal{proper}}
. Since
\rho
is real, we can always choose the maximum eigenvector to be real-valued; let it be
\ket{\psi}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}\sum_{i}a_{i}\ket{i}
. Let the maximum eigenvalue is
\lambda_{1}
; then
\rho-\lambda_{1}\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}
is a PSD matrix, and so all diagonal values are non-negative. Thus,

\displaystyle\sum_{i}|1/n-\lambda_{1}a_{i}^{2}|=\sum_{i}1/n-\lambda_{1}a_{i}^{2}=\Tr[\rho-\lambda_{1}\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}]=1-\lambda_{1}\,.

Let
X
be the random variable with value
n\lambda_{1}a_{i}^{2}
. Then
\mathbb{E}[X]=\lambda_{1}\sum_{i}a_{i}^{2}=\lambda_{1}
. Since
0\leq X\leq 1
,
Var(X)\leq\lambda_{1}(1-\lambda_{1})
. By Chebyshev,
\Pr[X\leq\lambda_{1}-k\sqrt{\lambda_{1}(1-\lambda_{1})}]\leq\Pr[X\leq\lambda_{1}-k\sigma]\leq\frac{1}{k^{2}}
.

We investigate the squared overlap of
\ket{\psi}
with any proper state, which is maximized at value
\frac{1}{n}(\sum_{i}|a_{i}|)^{2}
. We lower-bound this quantity by ignoring the small terms from Chebyshev’s inequality:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\left(n\cdot\left(1-\frac{1}{k^{2}}\right)\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{1}-k\sqrt{\lambda_{1}(1-\lambda_{1})}}{n\cdot\lambda_{1}}}\right)^{2}=\left(1-\frac{1}{k^{2}}\right)^{2}\cdot\left(1-k\sqrt{\frac{1-\lambda_{1}}{\lambda_{1}}}\right)\,.

Let
c=\frac{1-\lambda_{1}}{\lambda_{1}}
. Then choose
k=c^{-1/6}
.
The squared overlap value is then
(1-c^{1/3})^{3}
.
So,
Lemma

5.2
holds with any polynomial dominating
g(x)=1-(1-(\frac{x}{1-x})^{1/3})^{3}
. For example, we may use the Taylor polynomial of
g
around
0
, or
f(x)=4x^{1/4}
for
x\in[0,2^{-8}]
.
∎

In fact, assuming there is a PCP to scale up the
{\mathsf{NP}}
-hardness result of
[
ABDFS09
,
Bei08
]
to
{\mathsf{NEXP}}
-hardness, we have that
{\mathsf{QMA}}_{\mathcal{W}_{\textnormal{proper}}}\subseteq{\mathsf{EXP}}
, yet
{\mathsf{QMA}}_{\mathcal{W}_{\textnormal{proper}}}(2)={\mathsf{NEXP}}
.
Just as before, we can view the power of proper states as arising from restricting
\mathcal{W}_{\textnormal{proper}}
to pure states.

However, this class does not provide a good approach to prove
{\mathsf{QMA}}(2)={\mathsf{NEXP}}
. Unlike
{\mathsf{QMA}}^{+}
and
{\mathsf{QMA}}_{\mathsf{IS}}
, it is not clear how to simulate
{\mathsf{QMA}}_{\mathcal{W}_{\textnormal{proper}}}
in
{\mathsf{QMA}}
for any completeness-soundness gap. For example, any computational basis state in
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
has only
\frac{1}{d}
squared overlap with every proper state.

Appendix E
{\mathsf{QMA}}
with a multiseparable quantum proof

We investigate the power of quantum proofs with another notion of multipartite (un)entanglement, called
multiseparability
by
[
Tha99
]
. These are tripartite states where after tracing
any
register, the reduced density matrix is separable. Label the registers
A,B,C
as before. Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{\ket{\textnormal{multisep}}}=\{\ket{\psi}\ |\ \Tr_{A}[\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}]\in\mathsf{SEP}(B,C),\Tr_{B}[\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}]\in\mathsf{SEP}(A,C),\Tr_{C}[\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}]\in\mathsf{SEP}(A,B)\}.

Let
{\mathsf{QMA}}_{\ket{\textnormal{multisep}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}{\mathsf{QMA}}_{\mathcal{W}_{\ket{\textnormal{multisep}}}}
. Then this class can also decide
{\mathsf{NEXP}}
at some gap:

Theorem E.1
.

There exist constants
1>c>s>0
where
{\mathsf{QMA}}_{\ket{\textnormal{multisep}}}^{c,s}={\mathsf{NEXP}}
.

Proof sketch.

The proof is almost identical to the proof of
Theorem

1.2
.

We consider states on
five
registers
\mathbb{C}^{R}\otimes(\mathbb{C}^{R}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{\kappa})\otimes(\mathbb{C}^{R}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{\kappa})
, where
R=2^{\textnormal{poly}(n)}
and
\kappa
is some constant. Our protocol is as follows:

•

With probability
p_{1}
, measure all the registers in the computational basis, and make sure it is of the form
\ket{v}\ket{v}\ket{c}\ket{v}\ket{c}
(i.e. the vertices match and the colors match).

•

With probability
p_{2}
, run
\text{CNOT}_{1,2}
(i.e. applied to the first and third register), then
\text{CNOT}_{1,4}
, then
\text{CNOT}_{3,5}
, and check
\mathsf{Density}
on the first and third registers.

•

Otherwise, run
\text{CNOT}_{1,2}
and
\text{CNOT}_{1,4}
and
\text{CNOT}_{3,5}
, and then run the constraint tests of
[
BFM23
]
on the first and third registers.

Our quasirigid state before applying the
CNOT
gates is of the form
\sum_{v}a_{v}\ket{v}\ket{v,\sigma(v)}\ket{v,\sigma(v)}
. We remark that these states are in fact
multiseparable
. Since each basis element is uniquely determined by the value in any one of the registers, tracing out any one register gives a separable state. The rest of the proof goes through virtually unchanged.
∎

Remarkably, pure states that are multiseparable can be characterized in another way.

Definition E.2
.

A tripartite state
\ket{\psi}
has a
generalized
Schmidt decomposition if it can be written in the form

\displaystyle\ket{\psi}=\sum_{i}\sqrt{p_{i}}\ket{a_i}\ket{b_i}\ket{c_i}\,,

where each set
\{\ket{a_i}\},\{\ket{b_i}\},\{\ket{c_i}\}
is an orthonormal set.

Note that every tripartite state can be recursively Schmidt-decomposed, but this does not imply that every set
\{\ket{a_i}\},\{\ket{b_i}\},\{\ket{c_i}\}
is simultaneously an orthonormal set.

Fact E.3

(
[
Tha99
]
)
.

A state
\ket{\psi}
has a generalized Schmidt decomposition iff
\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{W}_{\ket{\textnormal{multisep}}}
.

Corollary E.4
.

There exists a constant
\alpha<1
above which it is
{\mathsf{NP}}
-hard to
\alpha
-approximately optimize linear functions of
\textnormal{poly}(n)
-dimension states with generalized Schmidt decompositions.

It is fruitful to further understand the connection between
Corollary

E.4
and other
{\mathsf{NP}}
-hardness results about testing properties of tensors.

One may also investigate the power of
mixed
multiseparable states
\mathcal{W}_{{\textnormal{multisep}}}
.
It is likely that
Lemma

5.2
can be used to show
{\mathsf{QMA}}_{{\textnormal{multisep}}}(2)={\mathsf{NEXP}}
. The one copy-variant
{\mathsf{QMA}}_{{\textnormal{multisep}}}
can simulate
{\mathsf{QMA}}(2)
by ignoring any one register of the proof, but its exact power remains unclear.

Appendix F
Computing probabilities of the
{\mathsf{QMA}}_{\ket{\mathsf{IS}}}
protocol for
{\mathsf{NEXP}}

Here, we set the probability of running each test in the protocol of
Section

4
. This largely follows
[
BM24
, Section 3.3]
although with slightly different constants.
We rely on the following claim, paraphrased from
[
BFM23
]
:

Lemma F.1

(
[
BFM23
]
)
.

There exist constants
0<\xi\leq\textnormal{C}_{\textnormal{YES}}<1
and a
{\mathsf{QMA}}
protocol
\mathsf{ConstraintCheck}
with completeness
\textnormal{C}_{\textnormal{YES}}
and soundness
\textnormal{C}_{\textnormal{YES}}-\xi
that decides
{\mathsf{NEXP}}
,
assuming
the quantum proof is a
rigid
state.

Lemma

F.1
implies the existence of absolute constants
\textnormal{C}_{\textnormal{YES}},\xi,\kappa
. We choose
distance thresholds

\nu_{\text{low}},\nu_{\text{high}}
to be small positive constants that satisfy the following conditions:

\displaystyle 0\leq\nu_{\text{high}}\leq\frac{\nu_{\text{low}}}{\kappa}\,,

\displaystyle\frac{\nu_{\text{high}}}{\nu_{\text{low}}}\leq\frac{\xi}{6(1-\textnormal{C}_{\textnormal{YES}})}\,,

\displaystyle\left(\kappa\nu_{\text{low}}+(\kappa+1)\sqrt{(\kappa+1)\nu_{\text{low}}}\right)^{1/2}\leq\frac{\xi}{2}\,.

This can be done by first making both constants equal and small enough to satisfy the last inequality, then reducing
\nu_{\text{high}}
to satisfy the first two inequalities.

Now we choose the probabilities
p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}
. Let

\displaystyle p_{1}=\frac{1}{Z}

\displaystyle p_{2}=\frac{(\nu_{\text{low}}+\nu_{\text{high}})}{\nu_{\text{high}}^{2}Z}

\displaystyle p_{3}=\frac{\nu_{\text{low}}}{2(1-\textnormal{C}_{\textnormal{YES}})Z}\,,

where
Z\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}1+\frac{(\nu_{\text{low}}+\nu_{\text{high}})}{\nu_{\text{high}}^{2}}+\frac{\nu_{\text{low}}}{2(1-\textnormal{C}_{\textnormal{YES}})}
, so that the probabilities sum to
1
.

Given a quantum proof
\ket{\psi}
, the verifier accepts with probability

\displaystyle p_{1}\cdot\Pr[\mathsf{Density}\text{ succeeds}]+p_{2}\Pr[\mathsf{MatchCheck}\text{ succeeds}]+p_{3}\cdot\Pr[\mathsf{ConstraintCheck}\text{ succeeds}]\,.

In completeness, we consider the quantum proof guaranteed by
Lemma

F.1
. Since this state is rigid, the verifier accepts with probability at least
\textnormal{P}_{\textnormal{YES}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}p_{1}\cdot\frac{1}{\kappa}+p_{2}\cdot 1+p_{3}\cdot\textnormal{C}_{\textnormal{YES}}
. For soundness, we analyze four separate cases:

1.

In the first case, the proof doesn’t test well enough on
\mathsf{Density}
. If
\Pr[\mathsf{Density}\text{ succeeds}]=\frac{1}{\kappa}-d
for
d\geq\nu_{\text{low}}
, then the verifier accepts with probability at most

\displaystyle\textnormal{P}_{\textnormal{NO}}\leq p_{1}\cdot(\frac{1}{\kappa}-d)+p_{2}\cdot 1+p_{3}\leq\textnormal{P}_{\textnormal{YES}}-p_{1}\cdot\nu_{\text{low}}+p_{3}\cdot(1-\textnormal{C}_{\textnormal{YES}})=\textnormal{P}_{\textnormal{YES}}-\frac{\nu_{\text{low}}}{2Z}\,.

2.

In the second case, the proof tests too well on
\mathsf{Density}
, and so cannot test well enough on
\mathsf{MatchCheck}
. If
\Pr[\mathsf{Density}\text{ succeeds}]=\frac{1}{\kappa}+d
for
d\geq\nu_{\text{high}}
, then by
Lemma

4.7
we can upper bound the success probability of
\mathsf{MatchCheck}
as
\Pr[\mathsf{MatchCheck}\text{ succeeds}]\leq\frac{k+1-d^{2}}{k+1}
. Then

\displaystyle\textnormal{P}_{\textnormal{NO}}\leq p_{1}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\kappa}+d\right)+p_{2}\cdot\frac{\kappa+1-d^{2}}{\kappa+1}+p_{3}\leq\textnormal{P}_{\textnormal{YES}}+p_{1}\cdot d-p_{2}\cdot\frac{d^{2}}{\kappa+1}+p_{3}\cdot(1-\textnormal{C}_{\textnormal{YES}})\,.

The right-most expression depends on
d
through the term
p_{1}\cdot d-p_{2}\cdot\frac{d^{2}}{\kappa+1}=p_{1}\left(d-\frac{(\nu_{\text{low}}+\nu_{\text{high}})d^{2}}{(\kappa+1)\nu_{\text{high}}^{2}}\right)
.
This term is decreasing with
d
when
d>\frac{\kappa+1}{2}\cdot\frac{\nu_{\text{high}}}{1+\nu_{\text{low}}/\nu_{\text{high}}}\geq\frac{\nu_{\text{high}}}{2}
. So the expression is maximized at
d=\nu_{\text{high}}
, and then

\displaystyle\textnormal{P}_{\textnormal{NO}}\leq\textnormal{P}_{\textnormal{YES}}+p_{1}\left(\nu_{\text{high}}-\frac{(\nu_{\text{low}}+\nu_{\text{high}})\nu_{\text{high}}^{2}}{\nu_{\text{high}}^{2}}\right)+p_{3}\cdot(1-\textnormal{C}_{\textnormal{YES}})=\textnormal{P}_{\textnormal{YES}}-\frac{\nu_{\text{low}}}{2Z}\,.

3.

In the third case, the proof doesn’t test well enough on
\mathsf{MatchCheck}
, and the acceptance probability of
\mathsf{Density}
is not too high. If
\Pr[\mathsf{Density}\text{ succeeds}]=\frac{1}{\kappa}+d
for
-\nu_{\text{low}}\leq d\leq\nu_{\text{high}}
and
\Pr[\mathsf{MatchCheck}\text{ succeeds}]\leq 1-\nu_{\text{low}}
, then

\displaystyle\textnormal{P}_{\textnormal{NO}}

\displaystyle\leq p_{1}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\kappa}+d\right)+p_{2}\cdot\left(1-\nu_{\text{low}}\right)+p_{3}\leq\textnormal{P}_{\textnormal{YES}}+p_{1}\cdot d-p_{2}\cdot\nu_{\text{low}}+p_{3}\cdot(1-\textnormal{C}_{\textnormal{YES}})\,.

By our choice of constants,
d\leq\nu_{\text{high}}\leq\nu_{\text{low}}
. Moreover, one can verify that
p_{2}\geq 2\cdot p_{1}
whenever
\nu_{\text{high}}\leq 1
. So
p_{1}\cdot d-p_{2}\cdot\nu_{\text{low}}\leq-p_{1}\cdot\nu_{\text{low}}
, and
\textnormal{P}_{\textnormal{NO}}\leq\textnormal{P}_{\textnormal{YES}}-p_{1}\cdot\nu_{\text{low}}+p_{3}\cdot(1-\textnormal{C}_{\textnormal{YES}})=\textnormal{P}_{\textnormal{YES}}-\frac{\nu_{\text{low}}}{2Z}
.

4.

In the fourth case, the proof tests well on
\mathsf{MatchCheck}
, and the acceptance probability of
\mathsf{Density}
is not too low. So the proof must be close to
rigid
, and do worse on
\mathsf{ConstraintCheck}
. If
\Pr[\mathsf{Density}\text{ succeeds}]=\frac{1}{\kappa}+d
for
-\nu_{\text{low}}\leq d\leq\nu_{\text{high}}
and
\Pr[\mathsf{MatchCheck}\text{ succeeds}]\geq 1-\nu_{\text{low}}
, then by
Claim

4.6
, there is a rigid state
\ket{\chi}
such that

\displaystyle|\innerproduct{\chi}{\psi}|^{2}\geq 1-\kappa d-(\kappa+1)\sqrt{(\kappa+1)\nu_{\text{low}}}\,.

We use
Fact

4.5
with
\Pi
equal to the accepting projector of
\mathsf{ConstraintCheck}
; then we have
|\Pr[\mathsf{ConstraintCheck}\text{ succeeds}]-\Pr[\mathsf{ConstraintCheck}\text{ succeeds}]|\leq\left(\kappa d+(\kappa+1)\sqrt{(\kappa+1)\nu_{\text{low}}}\right)^{1/2}
. Putting this all together,

\displaystyle\textnormal{P}_{\textnormal{NO}}

\displaystyle\leq p_{1}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\kappa}+d\right)+p_{2}\cdot 1+p_{3}\cdot\left(\textnormal{C}_{\textnormal{YES}}-\xi+\left(\kappa d+(\kappa+1)\sqrt{(\kappa+1)\nu_{\text{low}}}\right)^{1/2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\textnormal{P}_{\textnormal{YES}}+p_{1}\cdot d+p_{3}\cdot\left(-\xi+\left(\kappa d+(\kappa+1)\sqrt{(\kappa+1)\nu_{\text{low}}}\right)^{1/2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\textnormal{P}_{\textnormal{YES}}+p_{1}\cdot\nu_{\text{high}}-p_{3}\cdot\frac{\xi}{2}

\displaystyle=\textnormal{P}_{\textnormal{YES}}+\frac{1}{Z}\left(\nu_{\text{high}}-\frac{\nu_{\text{low}}\cdot\xi}{4(1-\textnormal{C}_{\textnormal{YES}})}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\textnormal{P}_{\textnormal{YES}}-\frac{\nu_{\text{high}}}{2Z}\,.

So, this protocol succeeds with completeness
\textnormal{P}_{\textnormal{YES}}
and soundness at most
\textnormal{P}_{\textnormal{YES}}-\frac{\nu_{\text{high}}}{2Z}
.

Appendix G
Padding argument for the proof of
{\mathsf{QMA}}_{\mathsf{IS}}(2)={\mathsf{NEXP}}

Here, we verify that the protocol in
Section

4
goes through even when the quantum proofs are from
\textnormal{rank1}({\mathcal{W}_{\mathsf{IS}}}^{\star})
; i.e. states in
{\mathcal{W}_{\ket{\mathsf{IS}}}}
obeying the register size inequality in the proof of
Claim

5.4
.
We modify the protocol as follows. We prepend a scratch qudit (using index variable
z
) to the first register to satisfy the register size inequality.
In both
\mathsf{Density}
and
\mathsf{MatchCheck}
, we also measure the scratch qudit in the standard basis, and reject if it does not output
0_{z}
.
In the other test, we ignore the scratch qudit.

For this modified protocol, we prove that
Lemma

4.4
,
Claim

4.6
, and
Lemma

4.7
hold with the same parameters.
We may then choose the test probabilities exactly as in
Appendix

F
, as the analysis only depends on these three statements.

Lemma G.1

(Padded version of
Lemma

4.4
)
.

Suppose
\mathsf{MatchCheck}
succeeds with probability
1-\epsilon
, and
\rho_{B,C}
is separable. Then there is a state
\ket{0_z}\ket{\phi}
that is
quasirigid
after applying
\text{CNOT}_{1,3}\text{CNOT}_{2,4}
such that
|\bra{0_z,\phi}\ket{\psi}|^{2}\geq 1-(\kappa+1)\epsilon
.

Proof.

Label the computational basis elements of
\ket{\psi}
as
a_{zvc,wd}\ket{z}\ket{v}\ket{c,w}\ket{d}
.
Let
\sigma:[R]\to[\kappa]
be a function that picks out the largest amplitude per vertex when the scratch qudit
z=0
, i.e. where
|a_{0_{z},v\sigma(v),v\sigma(v)}|^{2}\geq|a_{0_{z},vd,vd}|^{2}
for all
d\neq\sigma(v)
. For convenience, denote
\sigma_{v}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}\sigma(v)
.

Consider the matrix elements of the reduced density matrix of
\ket{\psi}
after tracing out the first register:

\displaystyle\bra{c, v, d}\rho_{B,C}\ket{e, w, f}=\sum_{z}\sum_{x}a_{z,xc,vd}a_{z,xe,wf}^{\dagger}\,.

We use this expression to upper-bound the weight on colors not chosen by
\sigma
:

\displaystyle\sum_{v}\sum_{d;d\neq\sigma_{v}}|a_{0_{z},vd,vd}|^{2}\leq\sum_{v}\sum_{d;d\neq\sigma_{v}}|a_{0_{z},v\sigma_{v},v\sigma_{v}}|\cdot|a_{0_{z},vd,vd}^{\dagger}|=\sum_{v}\sum_{d;d\neq\sigma_{v}}|a_{0_{z},v\sigma_{v},v\sigma_{v}}\cdot a_{0_{z},vd,vd}^{\dagger}|\,.

We break this upper bound into three parts, by rewriting
|a_{0_{z},v\sigma_{v},v\sigma_{v}}\cdot a_{0_{z},vd,vd}^{\dagger}|
as

\displaystyle|\bra{\sigma_v, v, \sigma_v}\rho_{B,C}\ket{d, v, d}-\sum_{z\neq 0}\sum_{x}a_{z,x\sigma_{v},v\sigma_{v}}\cdot a_{z,xd,vd}-\sum_{x;x\neq v}a_{0_{z},x\sigma_{v},v\sigma_{v}}\cdot a_{0_{z},xd,vd}^{\dagger}|

\displaystyle\leq\

\displaystyle|\bra{\sigma_v, v, \sigma_v}\rho_{B,C}\ket{d, v, d}|+\sum_{z\neq 0}\sum_{x}|a_{z,x\sigma_{v},v\sigma_{v}}|\cdot|a_{z,xd,vd}^{\dagger}|+\sum_{x;x\neq v}|a_{0_{z},x\sigma_{v},v\sigma_{v}}|\cdot|a_{0_{z},xd,vd}^{\dagger}|\,.

1.

For the first term, since
\rho_{B,C}
is separable, we use one of the bounds proven in
Lemma

4.3
:

\displaystyle|\bra{\sigma_v, v, \sigma_v}\rho_{B,C}\ket{d, v, d}|\leq\frac{1}{2}\cdot\big(\bra{\sigma_v, v, d}\rho_{B,C}\ket{\sigma_v, v, d}+\bra{d, v, \sigma_v}\rho_{B,C}\ket{d, v, \sigma_v}\big)\,.

So then

\displaystyle\sum_{v}\sum_{d;d\neq\sigma_{v}}|\bra{\sigma_v, v, \sigma_v}\rho_{B,C}\ket{d, v, d}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{v}\sum_{d;d\neq\sigma_{v}}\bra{\sigma_v, v, d}\rho_{B,C}\ket{\sigma_v, v, d}+\bra{d, v, \sigma_v}\rho_{B,C}\ket{d, v, \sigma_v}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{v}\sum_{d;d\neq\sigma_{v}}\sum_{z}\sum_{x}|a_{z,x\sigma_{v},vd}|^{2}+|a_{z,xd,v\sigma_{v}}|^{2}\right)\,.

2.

The second term appears because of the scratch qudit. This term is small because
z\neq 0
:

\displaystyle\sum_{v}\sum_{d;d\neq\sigma_{v}}\sum_{z\neq 0}\sum_{x}|a_{z,x\sigma_{v},v\sigma_{v}}|\cdot|a_{z,xd,vd}^{\dagger}|\leq\sum_{z\neq 0}\sum_{v,x,d}|a_{z,xd,vd}|^{2}

The sum of the first and second term are upper-bounded by
\sum_{(z,x,c)\neq(0,v,d)}|a_{z,xc,vd}|^{2}=\epsilon
.

3.

For the third term, we use the AM-GM inequality:

\displaystyle|a_{0_{z},x\sigma_{v},v\sigma_{v}}|\cdot|a_{0_{z},xd,vd}^{\dagger}|\leq\frac{1}{2}\cdot\left(|a_{0_{z},x\sigma_{v},v\sigma_{v}}|^{2}+|a_{0_{z},xd,vd}^{\dagger}|^{2}\right)\,.

Taking
|a_{0_{z},xd,vd}|^{2}
over all three sums
(v,d,x)
is at most
\sum_{(z,x,c)\neq(0,v,d)}|a_{z,xc,vd}|^{2}=1-(1-\epsilon)=\epsilon
.
Similarly, summing
|a_{0_{z},x\sigma_{v},v\sigma_{v}}|^{2}
over sums
(v,x)
contributes at most
\epsilon
, so at most
(\kappa-1)\epsilon
over sums
(v,d\neq\sigma_{v},x)
.
In total, this term is at most
\frac{\kappa}{2}\epsilon
.

Combining each of the three terms, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{v}\sum_{d;d\neq\sigma_{v}}|a_{0_{z},vd,vd}|^{2}\leq\epsilon+\frac{\kappa}{2}\epsilon=\frac{\kappa+2}{2}\epsilon\leq\kappa\epsilon\,,

where the last inequality holds because
\kappa\geq 2
(i.e. we need at least two colors).

Consider the state
\ket{\phi}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\sum_{v}a_{0_{z},v\sigma_{v},v\sigma_{v}}\ket{v}\ket{\sigma_v}\ket{v}\ket{\sigma_v}
, where
\gamma=\sum_{v}|a_{0_{z},v\sigma_{v},v\sigma_{v}}|^{2}
is chosen so that
\innerproduct{\phi}{\phi}=1
. Note that
\ket{\phi}
is quasirigid after applying
\text{CNOT}_{1,3}
and
\text{CNOT}_{2,4}
. Moreover,
|\innerproduct{0_z,\phi}{\psi}|^{2}=\gamma
. Putting everything together,

\displaystyle\gamma=\sum_{v}|a_{0_{z},v\sigma_{v},v\sigma_{v}}|^{2}=\Pr[\mathsf{MatchCheck}\text{ succeeds}]-\sum_{v}\sum_{d;d\neq\sigma_{v}}|a_{0_{z},vd,vd}|^{2}\geq 1-\epsilon-\kappa\epsilon\,.

∎

Claim G.2

(Padded version of
Claim

4.6
)
.

Suppose
\mathsf{Density}
and
\mathsf{MatchCheck}
succeed on
\ket{\psi}
with probability
\frac{1}{\kappa}-d_{\mathsf{D}}
and
1-d_{\mathsf{M}}
, respectively. Then there is a state
\ket{0_z}\ket{\chi}
that is rigid after applying
\text{CNOT}_{1,3}\text{CNOT}_{2,4}
such that

\displaystyle|\innerproduct{0_z,\chi}{\psi}|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq 1-\kappa d_{\mathsf{D}}-(\kappa+1)\sqrt{(\kappa+1)d_{\mathsf{M}}}\,.

Proof.

By
Lemma

G.1
, there exists a state
\ket{\phi}=\sum_{z,v}\alpha_{z,v}\ket{z,v}\ket{c_{z,v},v}\ket{c_{z,v}}
such that

\displaystyle|\innerproduct{\psi}{\phi}|^{2}\geq 1-(\kappa+1)d_{\mathsf{M}}\,.

By
Fact

4.5
,
for any unit vector
\ket{\mu}
,
we have

\left||\innerproduct{\mu}{\psi}|^{2}-|\innerproduct{\mu}{\phi}|^{2}\right|\leq\sqrt{(\kappa+1)d_{\mathsf{M}}}
.
We use this in two places.
Let
\ket{+'}=\ket{0_z}\otimes\left(\text{CNOT}_{1,3}\text{CNOT}_{2,4}\ket{+}\ket{0}\ket{0}\right)
. First, since
|\innerproduct{+'}{\psi}|^{2}\geq\frac{1}{\kappa}-d_{\mathsf{D}}
, applying
Fact

4.5
with
\ket{\mu}=\ket{+'}
implies
|\innerproduct{+'}{\phi}|^{2}\geq\frac{1}{\kappa}-d_{\mathsf{D}}-\sqrt{(\kappa+1)d_{\mathsf{M}}}
.
Now let
\ket{\chi}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{R}}\sum_{v}\ket{v}\ket{c_{0_z,v},v}\ket{c_{0_z,v}}
be such that
\ket{0_z}\ket{\chi}
has exactly the shared computational basis elements of
\ket{+'}
and
\ket{\phi}
. Then

\displaystyle|\innerproduct{0_z,\chi}{\phi}|^{2}=\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{R}}\sum_{v\in[R]}\alpha_{0_{z},v}\right|^{2}=\kappa\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa R}}\sum_{v\in[R]}^{R}\alpha_{0_{z},v}\right|^{2}=\kappa|\innerproduct{+'}{\phi}|^{2}\geq 1-\kappa\left(d_{\mathsf{D}}+\sqrt{(\kappa+1)d_{\mathsf{M}}}\right)\,.

Applying
Fact

4.5
using
\ket{\mu}=\ket{0_z}\ket{\chi}
then implies

\displaystyle|\innerproduct{0_z,\chi}{\psi}|^{2}\geq 1-\kappa\left(d_{\mathsf{D}}+\sqrt{(\kappa+1)d_{\mathsf{M}}}\right)-\sqrt{(\kappa+1)d_{\mathsf{M}}}=1-\kappa d_{\mathsf{D}}-(\kappa+1)\sqrt{(\kappa+1)d_{\mathsf{M}}}\,.

∎

Lemma G.3

(Padded version of
Lemma

4.7
)
.

Suppose
\mathsf{Density}
and
\mathsf{MatchCheck}
succeed on state
\ket{\psi}
with probability
w_{\mathsf{D}}\geq\frac{1}{\kappa}
and
w_{\mathsf{M}}
, respectively. Then
(w_{\mathsf{D}}-\frac{1}{\kappa})^{2}+(\kappa+1)w_{\mathsf{M}}\leq\kappa+1
.

Proof.

By
Lemma

G.1
, there exists a
\ket{\phi}=\sum_{z,v}\alpha_{z,v}\ket{z,v}\ket{c_{z,v},v}\ket{c_{z,v}}
such that

\displaystyle|\innerproduct{\psi}{\phi}|^{2}\geq 1-(\kappa+1)(1-w_{\mathsf{M}})\,.

Using
Fact

4.5
, we see that

\left||\innerproduct{+'}{\psi}|^{2}-|\innerproduct{+'}{\phi}|^{2}\right|
is at most
\sqrt{(\kappa+1)(1-w_{\mathsf{M}})}
. Note that by assumption,
|\innerproduct{+'}{\psi}|^{2}=w_{\mathsf{D}}\geq\frac{1}{\kappa}
, and by Cauchy-Schwarz,
|\innerproduct{+'}{\phi}|^{2}=\frac{1}{R\cdot\kappa}\left|\sum_{v\in[R]}\alpha_{0_{z},v}\right|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\kappa}\sum_{v\in[R]}|\alpha_{0_{z},v}|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\kappa}
. Combining these claims, we have

\displaystyle w_{\mathsf{D}}-\frac{1}{\kappa}\leq|\innerproduct{+'}{\psi}|^{2}-|\innerproduct{+'}{\phi}|^{2}\leq\sqrt{(\kappa+1)(1-w_{\mathsf{M}})}\,.

The lemma follows after rearranging the terms.
∎
