Moments of Representation Numbers
Naomi Bazlov

Abstract
A representation number is a function which expresses the number of ways an integer can be written as a sum of elements of chosen sets. One of the oldest number-theoretic results on representation numbers is Fermat’s theorem which says that an odd prime can be written as a sum of two squares in exactly 0 0 or 2 2 ways (if order of summands is important). In this dissertation, we discuss a selection of methods from modern analytic number theory and apply them to study asymptotics of certain representation numbers. In particular, we work through an argument in the recent paper [ 7 ] by Granville, Sabuncu and Sedunova to obtain upper bounds on higher moments of the number of ways of writing n n as the sum of a square and a square of a prime. We then use the method from the paper to obtain new results on moments of representation numbers where “prime” is replaced by “sum of two squares”.

Convention

Definition

(

Let

•

•

We say

Definition
.

(Representation numbers

)

The

.

.

•

•

The

is defined by
{(f*g)(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}f(d)g(\frac{n}{d})}
, where

runs over all divisors of

. Note that if

and
g
are multiplicative arithmetic functions, then

is also multiplicative.

Definition
.

(Mobius function, von Mangoldt function, divisor functions)

•

The

The

2.2
Basic Results

In this section we present some properties of arithmetic functions which will be used in the proofs of specialised results later on. These proofs are standard and can be found in many textbooks, including Hardy’s book
[
9
]
which we reference here.

Note that the set of all arithmetic functions is a ring with respect to pointwise addition and Dirichlet’s convolution
*
. The arithmetic function

I(n)=\mathbbm{1}_{\{n=1\}}
, taking value
1
at
1
and
0
elsewhere, is easily seen to be the multiplicative identity of this ring. It is well known that

\mu*\mathbbm{1}=I
, or in other words, that
\mu
is the multiplicative inverse of the constant function
\mathbbm{1}
in this ring.
From this we obtain Mobius inversion:
g=f*\mathbbm{1}\iff f=\mu*g
. It immediately follows from commutativity and associativity of the unital ring operation
*
and using
\mu*\mathbbm{1}=I
.

Lemma 2.2.1
.

\log n=\sum_{k\mid n}\Lambda(k)
.

Proof.

Let
n=\prod_{i=1}^{s}p_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}
be the prime factorisation of
n
. The only factors of
n
which give nonzero contributions to
\sum_{k\mid n}\Lambda(k)
are pure powers of primes, so

\smash{\sum_{k\mid n}\Lambda(k)=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\sum_{j=1}^{\alpha_{i}}\Lambda(p_{i}^{j})=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\sum_{j=1}^{\alpha_{i}}\log p_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\alpha_{i}\log p_{i}=\log\bigl(\prod_{i=1}^{s}p_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}\bigr)=\log n.}\qed

Lemma 2.2.2
.

For any
\epsilon>0
, we have
\tau(n)\ll_{\epsilon}n^{\epsilon}
.

Proof.

Refer to the second proof of Theorem 315 in
[
9
]
.

Let
\epsilon>0
be arbitary, and let
n=p_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\dots p_{k}^{\alpha_{k}}
. We can see that
\tau(n)=\prod_{i=1}^{k}(\alpha_{i}+1)
. Hence

\smash[t]{\frac{\tau(n)}{n^{\epsilon}}=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\alpha_{i}+1}{p_{i}^{\epsilon\alpha_{i}}}.}

Now, for a fixed
p_{i}
, consider the term
(\alpha_{i}+1)p_{i}^{-\epsilon\alpha_{i}}
. We have two cases, depending on if
p<2^{\frac{1}{\epsilon}}
or not. If
p\geq 2^{\frac{1}{\epsilon}}
then
\displaystyle\dfrac{\alpha_{i}+1}{p_{i}^{\epsilon\alpha_{i}}}\leq\frac{\alpha_{i}+1}{2^{\alpha_{i}}}\leq 1
.

In the remaining case, we will make use of the fact that
p_{i}^{\epsilon\alpha_{i}}=\exp(\epsilon\alpha_{i}\log p_{i})\geq\epsilon\alpha_{i}\log p_{i}\geq\epsilon\alpha_{i}\log 2
. We know that
e^{x}\geq x
by looking at the Taylor series, or derivative, or other methods; in fact,
e^{x}\geq 1+x
for
x\geq 0
. Hence

\frac{\alpha_{i}+1}{p_{i}^{\epsilon\alpha_{i}}}\leq\frac{\alpha_{i}+1}{\epsilon\alpha_{i}\log 2}\leq 1+\frac{1}{\epsilon\log 2}\leq\exp\Bigl(\frac{1}{\epsilon\log 2}\Bigr).

Thus

\smash[t]{\frac{\tau(n)}{n^{\epsilon}}=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\alpha_{i}+1}{p_{i}^{\epsilon\alpha_{i}}}\leq\prod_{p\leq 2^{\frac{1}{\epsilon}}}\exp\Bigl(\frac{1}{\epsilon\log 2}\Bigr)\leq\exp\Bigl(\frac{2^{\frac{1}{\epsilon}}}{\epsilon\log 2}\Bigr)=O(1).}

(2.1)

Then
\tau(n)=O(n^{\epsilon})
, which is equivalent to our original statement.
∎

Lemma 2.2.3
.

\forall\epsilon>0:\exists n_{0}
s.t.
\log(\tau(n))\leq\dfrac{(1+\epsilon)\log 2\log n}{\log\log n}
for any
n\geq n_{0}
.

Proof.

We improve the bound on
\tau(n)
using Lemma
2.2.2
, following the method in
[
9
]
, and filling in the missing steps. We replace
\epsilon
with the expression
\alpha=\dfrac{(1+\frac{1}{2}\epsilon)\log 2}{\log\log n}
. In the above proof, we did not use that
\epsilon
was a constant until the very last step of asserting
\exp\bigl(\frac{2^{1/\epsilon}}{\epsilon\log 2}\bigr)=O(1)
. Hence, as in (
2.1
), we have
\frac{\tau(n)}{n^{\alpha}}\leq\exp\bigl(\frac{2^{1/\alpha}}{\alpha\log 2}\bigr)
. Note that
\frac{1}{\alpha}=\frac{\log\log n}{(1+\frac{1}{2}\epsilon)\log 2}
, so
2^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}=\exp\bigl(\frac{\log\log n}{(1+\frac{1}{2}\epsilon)}\bigr)=(\log n)^{1/(1+\frac{1}{2}\epsilon)}
. Thus

\log\Bigl(\frac{\tau(n)}{n^{\alpha}}\Bigr)\leq\frac{2^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}{\alpha\log 2}=\frac{(\log n)^{1/(1+\frac{1}{2}\epsilon)}\log\log n}{(1+\frac{1}{2}\epsilon)(\log 2)^{2}}.

As
n\to\infty
, we have
\log\log n=o((\log n)^{\delta})
for any
\delta
, i.e.
\log\log n
is small compared to any power of
\log n
. In fact, this holds for all powers of
\log\log n
, so for large enough
n
(
n\geq n_{0}(\epsilon)
, some constant
n_{0}(\epsilon)
) we can write

\log\Bigl(\frac{\tau(n)}{n^{\alpha}}\Bigr)\leq\frac{(\log n)^{1/(1+\frac{1}{2}\epsilon)}\log\log n}{(1+\frac{1}{2}\epsilon)(\log 2)^{2}}\leq\frac{\epsilon\log 2\log n}{2\log\log n}.

But
\log\bigl(\frac{\tau(n)}{n^{\alpha}}\bigr)=\log(\tau(n))-\alpha\log n
. Hence

\log(\tau(n))\leq\frac{\frac{1}{2}\epsilon\log 2\log n}{\log\log n}+\frac{(1+\frac{1}{2}\epsilon)\log 2\log n}{\log\log n}=\frac{(1+\epsilon)\log 2\log n}{\log\log n}.\qed

In particular, either of the above results shows that
\frac{\log(\tau(n))}{\log n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, so
\frac{\log(\tau(n))}{\log n}=o(1)
. Since we can write
\tau(n)=n^{\log(\tau(n))/\log n}
, we have

\tau(n)=n^{o(1)}.

(2.2)

We give the following result for our future reference:

Theorem 2.2.4
.

\omega(n)\ll\dfrac{\log n}{\log\log n}
.

Proof.

By exponentiating both sides in Lemma
2.2.3
, we see that
\forall\epsilon>0
we eventually have
\tau(n)\leq 2^{(1+\epsilon)\log n/\log\log n}
. Moreover, by considering the prime factorisation
n=p_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\dots p_{k}^{\alpha_{k}}
, where
\forall i\colon\alpha_{i}\geq 1
, we compare
k=\omega(n)
and
\prod_{i=1}^{k}(\alpha_{i}+1)\geq 2^{k}
to get
2^{\omega(n)}\ll\tau(n)
. Hence, picking a particular constant
\epsilon
(e.g.
\epsilon=1
) we get
2^{\omega(n)}\ll 2^{2\log n/\log\log n}\implies\omega(n)\ll\frac{2\log n}{\log\log n}\ll\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}
, as required.
∎

2.3
PNT with error term

The asymptotic behaviour of the prime counting function is well-known:

\pi(x)\sim\frac{x}{\log x}
. Given a zero-free region for the Riemann zeta function, we can find an error term for the prime counting function
\pi
. Standard techniques such as Perron’s Formula and complex contours are often used in the proof. Perron’s Formula relates Chebyshev’s function
\psi
to an integral of
\frac{-\zeta^{\prime}(s)}{\zeta(s)}\cdot\frac{x^{s}}{s}
, and we then change the contour of integration using our knowledge of the poles of
\frac{\zeta^{\prime}}{\zeta}
and complex analysis. The stronger the known zero-free region, the better the error term; thus, proving the Riemann Hypothesis would greatly improve the error term estimate for
\pi
, as the zero-free region would extend all the way to the line
\Re(s)=\frac{1}{2}
. Unfortunately, even the strongest known zero-free regions are nowhere near to this.

The following result is equivalent to the Prime Number Theorem with weak error term
O(x\exp(-c\sqrt[10]{\log x}))
.

Lemma
.

\psi(x)=x+O(x\exp(-c\sqrt[10]{\log x}))
, for some real
c>0
, and
x\geq 2
.

The classical zero-free region for
\zeta
is
\sigma\geq 1-\frac{c}{\log(|t|+2)}
, giving the following result:

Lemma
.

\psi(x)=x+O(x\exp(-c\sqrt{\log x}))
, for some real
c>0
.

Corollary

(Prime Number Theorem with classical error term)
.

\pi(x)=\dfrac{x}{\log x}+O(x\exp(-c\sqrt{\log x})),\text{ for some real }c>0.

Remark
.

Although the logarithmic integral
\text{Li}(x)
is the same as
\frac{x}{\log x}
modulo the error term we can prove without assuming results such as the Riemann Hypothesis, in practice we can observe that
\pi(x)
is closer to the behaviour of
\text{Li}(x)
than
\frac{x}{\log x}
, which motivates its use in the following.

Lemma
.

\pi(x)=\mathrm{Li}(x)+O(x\exp(-c\sqrt{\log x}))
, for some real
c>0
, where the logarithmic integral
\mathrm{Li}(x)
is defined as
\displaystyle\int_{2}^{x}\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{\log t}
.

This can be done as
\text{Li}(x)-\frac{x}{\log x}
is small, in fact of order
\frac{x}{\log^{2}x}
– smaller than the error term in the result.

3.1
Moments of
r_{0}

The representation number
r_{0}
has been widely studied, for example in relation to the Gauss circle problem which counts the number of lattice points in a circle of given radius; this relates to the first moment of the function. Since
r_{0}(n)
is the number of ways to represent
n
as
x^{2}+y^{2}
, its moments can also be studied as part of the topic of quadratic forms. The Gauss circle problem has many generalisations in modern mathematics in areas including number theory and ergodic theory.

Lemma 3.1.1

(First moment of
r_{0}
)
.

\displaystyle\smash{\sum_{n\leq x}r_{0}(n)\sim\frac{\pi}{4}x.}

Note that

\sum_{n\leq x}r_{0}(n)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m,k\leq\sqrt{x}:\\
m^{2}+k^{2}\leq x\\
0\leq m,1\leq k\end{subarray}}1\asymp\frac{1}{4}\#\{\text{Lattice points in a circle of radius }\sqrt{x}\}.

(It is a quarter of the number of nonzero lattice points.)

Here,
r_{0}(n)=\frac{1}{4}\#\{\text{Lattice points on a circle of radius }\sqrt{n}\}
for all
n\geq 1
; we start by finding an expression for
r_{0}(n)
. We will think of all circles as circles drawn in the complex plane
\mathbb{C}
. The lattice points of
\mathbb{C}
are
\mathbb{Z}[i]
, the Gaussian integers.

Consider prime factorisation in
\mathbb{Z}[i]
. Primes that are
1\pmod{4}
factor into exactly
2
Gaussian primes. Primes that are
3\pmod{4}
are prime in
\mathbb{Z}[i]
as well. (
2
is special as it is a square up to associates.)

Let the prime factorisation of
n
over the integers be
n=p_{1}^{a_{1}}p_{2}^{a_{2}}\dots p_{k}^{a_{k}}
. If
p_{j}\equiv 1\pmod{4}
, then let
A_{j}
and
B_{j}
be the complex conjugate Gaussian primes whose product is
p_{j}
; if
p_{j}\equiv 3\pmod{4}
, then
p_{j}
is a Gaussian prime; if
p_{j}=2
, then
p_{j}=(1+i)(1-i)
.

Suppose
\alpha+\beta i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}[i]
lies on the circle of radius
\sqrt{n}
. Then
(\alpha+\beta i)(\alpha-\beta i)=n
, so
\alpha+\beta i
is a product of some Gaussian prime factors of
n
, and
\alpha-\beta i
is the product of their complex conjugates. So to count the number of lattice points on the circle, we count the number of ways to split the prime factors of
n
into conjugate pairs.

If
p_{j}=A_{j}B_{j}\equiv 1\pmod{4}
, there are
a_{j}+1
choices for the power of
A_{j}
dividing
\alpha+\beta i
. If
p_{j}\equiv 3\pmod{4}
, and
a_{j}
is odd, then there are actually no lattice points; if
a_{j}
is even, then
p_{j}^{a_{j}/2}
divides
\alpha+\beta i
. Finally, if
p_{j}=2
, then
(1+i)^{a_{j}}
divides
\alpha+\beta i
.

Now, consider the non-principal Dirichlet character modulo
4
, called
\chi
. It is periodic modulo
4
, with
\chi(2k)=0,\chi(4k\pm 1)=\pm 1
. Then
r_{0}(n)=\prod_{i=1}^{k}(\chi(1)+\chi(p_{i})+\dots+\chi(p_{i}^{a_{i}}))
; any power of
2
gives a contribution of
1
, powers of primes
3
mod
4
give
1
in the even power case and
0
else, and if
p_{i}\equiv 1\pmod{4}
then we get a factor of
a_{i}+1
as needed. But
\chi
is multiplicative, so

r_{0}(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}\chi(d).

(3.1)

Proof of Lemma
3.1.1
.

Substituting the above,
\sum_{n\leq x}r_{0}(n)=\sum_{n\leq x}\sum_{d\mid n}\chi(d)=\sum_{d\leq x}\lfloor\frac{x}{d}\rfloor\chi(d)\sim x(1-\frac{1}{3}+\frac{1}{5}-\frac{1}{7}+\dots)\sim\frac{\pi}{4}x
(by the arctan identity.)
∎

Note that this method does not give any estimate on the error term, but we can provide a crude estimate. We centre a square of side
1
around each lattice point in a circle of radius
\sqrt{x}
. These squares do not overlap, and the border of the shape covered by them lies fully inside the annulus
\sqrt{x}-1\leq r\leq\sqrt{x}+1
. Thus, the error on the number of lattice points in the circle is
O((\sqrt{x}+1)^{2}-(\sqrt{x}-1)^{2})=O(\sqrt{x})
. Hence

\sum_{n\leq x}r_{0}(n)=\frac{\pi}{4}x+O(\sqrt{x}).

(3.2)

The error of the first moment of
r_{0}
can be greatly refined, for example:

Lemma 3.1.2
.

\displaystyle\smash{\sum_{n\leq x}r_{0}(n)=\frac{\pi}{4}x+O(x^{\alpha})}
, some
\alpha<\frac{1}{3}
.

Proof.

See
[
11
]
. Huxley proves for various
\beta<\frac{2}{3}
that for a closed curve
C
of area
A
, drawing it with a scale factor of
M
gives
AM^{2}+O(M^{\beta})
integer points inside it. In our case, we take a unit circle which has area
\pi
, and enlarge it by a scale factor of
\sqrt{x}
to get
\pi\cdot x+O(x^{\beta/2})
integer lattice points inside a circle of radius
\sqrt{x}
.

But now,
\sum_{n\leq x}r_{0}(n)
is the number of ways to write any integer
n\leq x
as the sum of squares of a positive and non-negative integer, so we are only interested in lattice points in the first quadrant of the circle of radius
\sqrt{x}
, with the convention of counting lattice points of the form
(0,a)
but not
(b,0)
. Hence we get the result.
∎

Estimates for
\alpha
can be obtained by methods in analytic number theory, such as the exponent pairs method. Currently the best known bound of
\alpha\leq\frac{131}{416}+\epsilon
is due to Huxley’s third paper on the topic in 2000. One can show that
\alpha<\frac{1}{4}
is impossible, and the conjecture that
\alpha=\frac{1}{4}+\epsilon
remains open, after an attempted proof by Cappell and Shaneson in 2007 was retracted.

Higher moments of
r_{0}
have been studied through the lens of binary quadratic forms, and good estimates are shown in Blomer and Granville’s paper
[
2
, Theorem 1]
. Below we state a Lemma showing the asymptotic behaviour of the second moment.

Lemma 3.1.3

(Second moment of
r_{0}
)
.

There exists a real constant
H
such that

\smash{\sum_{n\leq x}r_{0}^{2}(n)=\frac{x\log x}{4}+Hx+O(x^{\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon}).}

Proof.

As
x^{2}+y^{2}
is a quadratic form,
r_{0}
is a special case of
r_{f}
as named in the paper
[
2
]
; we can apply Theorem
1
with
\beta=2
.
∎

3.2
First moments of representation numbers

In this section we provide asymptotics for the first moments of
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
. The Prime Number Theorem and its consequences form an important part of the argument, as the distribution of solutions to
a^{2}+p^{2}\leq x
or
p^{2}+q^{2}\leq x
is closely related to the distribution of primes.

We begin with asymptotics of the first moment of
r_{1}
. The error term appears to be the best currently known.
We will split the range of summation into two important parts that are estimated in different ways. The method is based on the Prime Number Theorem, however, the error term need not be strong.

Lemma 3.2.1

(The first moment of
r_{1}
)
.

\sum_{n\leq x}r_{1}(n)=\frac{\pi}{2}\frac{x}{\log x}\left(1+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{\log x}\Bigr)\right).

Proof.

We use ideas from the part of the proof of
[
7
, Theorem 2]
which deals with the first moment of
r_{1}
, and Daniel’s proof in
[
3
, Section 4]
.
We may write

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq x}r_{1}(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a^{2}+p^{2}\leq x}1=\sum_{a\leq\sqrt{\frac{x}{2}}}\sum_{p\leq\sqrt{x-a^{2}}}1+\sum_{p\leq\sqrt{\frac{x}{2}}}\sum_{a\leq\sqrt{x-p^{2}}}1-\sum_{a\leq\sqrt{\frac{x}{2}}}\sum_{p\leq\sqrt{\frac{x}{2}}}1

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\leq\sqrt{\frac{x}{2}}}\pi(\sqrt{x-a^{2}})+\sum_{p\leq\sqrt{\frac{x}{2}}}\lfloor\sqrt{x-p^{2}}\rfloor-\sqrt{x/2}\cdot\pi(\sqrt{x/2}).

(3.3)

Note that the weak error term in the PNT is still smaller than
O(\frac{x}{\log^{2}x})
, as
\exp(\sqrt[10]{\log x})
grows faster than any power of
\log x
. Hence
\pi(t)=\frac{t}{\log t}(1+O(\frac{1}{\log t}))
.

We deal with the components in (
3.3
). For the first one, since we have specified
a\leq\sqrt{x/2}
, we have

\log x\geq\log(x-a^{2})\geq\log(x/2)=\log x-\log 2
, so

\frac{2\sqrt{x-a^{2}}}{\log x}\leq\frac{2\sqrt{x-a^{2}}}{\log(x-a^{2})}\leq\frac{2\sqrt{x-a^{2}}}{\log x-\log 2}.

Since

(\log x-\log 2)^{-1}=\log^{-1}(x)(1+O(\frac{1}{\log x}))
we get

\pi(\sqrt{x-a^{2}})=\frac{2\sqrt{x-a^{2}}}{\log x}\left(1+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{\log x}\Bigr)\right).

We approximate
\sum_{a\leq\sqrt{x/2}}\sqrt{x-a^{2}}=\int_{0}^{\sqrt{x/2}}\sqrt{x-a^{2}}\mathrm{d}a+O(\sqrt{x})
. Now, we calculate

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\sqrt{x/2}}\!\!\sqrt{x-a^{2}}\ \mathrm{d}a+O(\sqrt{x})=\sqrt{x}\int_{0}^{\sqrt{1/2}}\!\!\sqrt{x}\sqrt{1-u^{2}}\ \mathrm{d}u+O(\sqrt{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{x}{2}\left[\arcsin(u)+u\sqrt{1-u^{2}}\right]_{0}^{\sqrt{1/2}}+O(\sqrt{x})=\frac{x}{2}\Bigl(\frac{\pi}{4}+\frac{1}{2}\Bigr)+O(\sqrt{x}),

and thus

\displaystyle\sum_{a\leq\sqrt{\frac{x}{2}}}\pi(\sqrt{x-a^{2}})

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\log x}\sum_{a\leq\sqrt{\frac{x}{2}}}\sqrt{x-a^{2}}\left(1+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{\log x}\Bigr)\right)=\frac{2}{\log x}\cdot\frac{\pi+2}{8}x\left(1+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{\log x}\Bigr)\right)

\displaystyle=\smash[b]{\frac{\pi+2}{4}\frac{x}{\log x}}\bigl(1+O((\log x)^{-1})\bigr).

We now deal with the second component of (
3.3
).

By partial summation, letting
a_{n}=\mathbbm{1}_{n\text{ is prime}}
and
f(y)=\sqrt{x-y^{2}}
we have

\smash[b]{\sum_{p\leq\sqrt{\frac{x}{2}}}}(\sqrt{x-p^{2}}+O(1))=O(\sqrt{x})+\pi(\sqrt{x/2})\sqrt{x/2}+\smash[t]{\int_{1}^{\sqrt{x/2}}\pi(t)t(x-t^{2})^{-1/2}\mathrm{d}t}.

The
\pi(\sqrt{x/2})\sqrt{x/2}
term cancels with the third component of (
3.3
), and
O(\sqrt{x})
can be absorbed into the error term of the first component as
O(\sqrt{x})=O(\frac{x}{\log^{2}x})
. Hence (noting that
\pi(t)=0
for
t<2
) we have

\sum_{n\leq x}r_{1}(n)=\frac{\pi+2}{4}\frac{x}{\log x}\left(1+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{\log x}\Bigr)\right)+\int_{2}^{\sqrt{x/2}}\pi(t)t(x-t^{2})^{-1/2}\mathrm{d}t.

(3.4)

It remains to estimate the integral.
We substitute
t=u\sqrt{x}
to get

\int_{2}^{\sqrt{x/2}}\frac{t}{\log t}\frac{t}{\sqrt{x-t^{2}}}\mathrm{d}t=\sqrt{x}\int_{2/\sqrt{x}}^{\sqrt{1/2}}\frac{xu^{2}}{\sqrt{x}\sqrt{1-u^{2}}}\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\log(u\sqrt{x})},

and use integration by parts, noting that
\int\frac{u^{2}}{\sqrt{1-u^{2}}}\mathrm{d}u=\frac{1}{2}(\arcsin(u)-u\sqrt{1-u^{2}})+c
to get

\displaystyle\frac{x}{2}\left[\frac{\arcsin(u)-u\sqrt{1-u^{2}}}{\log(u\sqrt{x})}\right]_{2/\sqrt{x}}^{\sqrt{1/2}}+\frac{x}{2}\int_{2/\sqrt{x}}^{\sqrt{1/2}}\frac{(\arcsin(u)-u\sqrt{1-u^{2}})\mathrm{d}u}{u\log^{2}(u\sqrt{x})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{x(\frac{\pi}{4}-\frac{1}{2})}{2\log(\sqrt{x/2})}+O(\sqrt{x})+\frac{x}{2}\int_{2/\sqrt{x}}^{\sqrt{1/2}}\Bigl(\frac{\arcsin(u)}{u}-\sqrt{1-u^{2}}\Bigr)\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\log^{2}(u\sqrt{x})}.

(3.5)

Now the last integral is written as

\displaystyle\int_{2/\sqrt{x}}^{1/\sqrt[4]{x}}\Bigl(\frac{\arcsin(u)}{u}-\sqrt{1-u^{2}}\Bigr)\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\log^{2}(u\sqrt{x})}+\int_{1/\sqrt[4]{x}}^{\sqrt{1/2}}\Bigl(\frac{\arcsin(u)}{u}-\sqrt{1-u^{2}}\Bigr)\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\log^{2}(u\sqrt{x})}.

In the first component, we use the Taylor series of
\arcsin
and
\sqrt{1-x}
to get
\frac{\arcsin(u)}{u}-\sqrt{1-u^{2}}=\frac{2}{3}u^{2}+O(u^{4})
, and bound
\log^{2}(u\sqrt{x})
from below by
\log^{2}2
. In the second, we bound
\frac{\arcsin(u)}{u}-\sqrt{1-u^{2}}
by a constant, and bound
\log^{2}(u\sqrt{x})
from below by
\log^{2}(\sqrt[4]{x})
. Hence

\displaystyle\int_{2/\sqrt{x}}^{\sqrt{1/2}}\Bigl(\frac{\arcsin(u)}{u}-\sqrt{1-u^{2}}\Bigr)\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\log^{2}(u\sqrt{x})}

\displaystyle=O\left(\int_{2/\sqrt{x}}^{1/\sqrt[4]{x}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}}{\mathrm{d}u}\right)+O\left(\int_{1/\sqrt[4]{x}}^{\sqrt{1/2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\log^{2}(\sqrt[4]{x})}\right)

\displaystyle=O(x^{-1/4})+O(\log^{-2}(x)).

(3.6)

We recall that
\pi(t)=O(\frac{t}{\log t})
. Hence substituting (
3.6
) into (
3.5
) gives

\smash{\int_{2}^{\sqrt{x/2}}\pi(t)t(x-t^{2})^{-1/2}\mathrm{d}t=\frac{x}{\log x}\Bigl(\frac{\pi}{4}-\frac{1}{2}\Bigr)+O\Bigl(\frac{x}{\log^{2}x}\Bigr).}

Substituting this into (
3.4
) gives the required main term of order
\frac{x}{\log x}
with coefficient
\frac{\pi+2}{4}+\frac{\pi-2}{4}=\frac{\pi}{2}
, and correct error term
O(\frac{x}{\log^{2}x})
.
∎

We will now use a similar method to obtain asymptotics of the first moment of
r_{2}
. Note that the error term obtained in this method is better than the error term cited in Sabuncu’s recent paper
[
17
]
, by a factor of
\log\log x
. A proof of the result with weaker error term can be found in
[
14
]
, for example.

Lemma 3.2.2

(The first moment of
r_{2}
)
.

\sum_{n\leq x}r_{2}(n)=\pi\frac{x}{\log^{2}x}\left(1+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{\log x}\Bigr)\right).

Proof.

We proceed as in Lemma
3.2.1
.
We may write

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq x}r_{2}(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p^{2}+q^{2}\leq x}1=\sum_{p\leq\sqrt{\frac{x}{2}}}\sum_{q\leq\sqrt{x-p^{2}}}1+\sum_{q\leq\sqrt{\frac{x}{2}}}\sum_{p\leq\sqrt{x-q^{2}}}1-\sum_{p\leq\sqrt{\frac{x}{2}}}\sum_{q\leq\sqrt{\frac{x}{2}}}1

\displaystyle=2\sum_{p\leq\sqrt{\frac{x}{2}}}\pi(\sqrt{x-p^{2}})-(\pi(\sqrt{x/2}))^{2}.

(3.7)

As previously,
\pi(t)=\frac{t}{\log t}(1+O(\frac{1}{\log t}))
. Since
t\leq\sqrt{x/2}
we may write
\pi(\sqrt{x-t^{2}})=\frac{2\sqrt{x-t^{2}}}{\log(x-t^{2})}(1+O(\frac{1}{\log x}))
, so
\sum_{p\leq\sqrt{\frac{x}{2}}}\pi(\sqrt{x-p^{2}})=\sum_{p\leq\sqrt{\frac{x}{2}}}\frac{2\sqrt{x-t^{2}}}{\log(x-t^{2})}(1+O(\frac{1}{\log x}))
.

By partial summation, letting
a_{n}=\mathbbm{1}_{n\text{ is prime}}
and
f(y)=\frac{2\sqrt{x-y^{2}}}{\log(x-y^{2})}
we have

\smash[b]{\sum_{p\leq\sqrt{\frac{x}{2}}}}\pi(\sqrt{x-p^{2}})=\left((\pi(\sqrt{x/2}))^{2}-\smash[t]{\int_{1}^{\sqrt{x/2}}\pi(t)f^{\prime}(t)\mathrm{d}t}\right)\Bigl(1+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{\log x}\Bigr)\Bigr).

(3.8)

We calculate
f^{\prime}(t)
:

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\Bigl(\frac{2\sqrt{x-t^{2}}}{\log(x-t^{2})}\Bigr)=-2t\frac{\log(x-t^{2})-2}{\sqrt{x-t^{2}}\log^{2}(x-t^{2})}.

The
(\pi(\sqrt{x/2}))^{2}
term thus has overall coefficient
1
in (
3.7
).

We use
\pi(t)=0
for
t\leq 2
.
Substitute
y=\sqrt{x-t^{2}}
,
u\sqrt{x}=y
to get

\displaystyle\int_{2}^{\sqrt{x/2}}\frac{t}{\log t}\frac{t}{\sqrt{x-t^{2}}\log(x-t^{2})}\mathrm{d}t=\int_{\sqrt{x/2}}^{\sqrt{x-4}}\frac{\sqrt{x-y^{2}}}{\log(\sqrt{x-y^{2}})\log(y^{2})}\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\sqrt{x/2}}^{\sqrt{x-4}}\frac{\sqrt{x-y^{2}}}{\log(x-y^{2})\log y}\mathrm{d}y=\sqrt{x}\int_{\sqrt{1/2}}^{\sqrt{1-4/x}}\frac{\sqrt{x}\sqrt{1-u^{2}}}{\log(x-xu^{2})\log(u\sqrt{x})}\mathrm{d}u.

(3.9)

Now, note that
\log(x-xu^{2})\log(u\sqrt{x})=(\log x+\log(1-u^{2}))(\log u+\frac{1}{2}\log x)=\frac{1}{2}\log^{2}x+\log x(\log(u\sqrt{1-u^{2}}))+\log u\log(1-u^{2})
. Since
u\sqrt{1-u^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{2}
and
\log u\log(1-u^{2})=O(1)
for
u\in[\sqrt{1/2},\sqrt{1-4/x}]
we have

\displaystyle\log(x-xu^{2})\log(u\sqrt{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log^{2}x+O(\log x)=\frac{1}{2}\log^{2}x\Bigl(1+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{\log x}\Bigr)\Bigr)

\displaystyle\implies\frac{\sqrt{x}\sqrt{1-u^{2}}}{\log(x-xu^{2})\log(u\sqrt{x})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{x}\sqrt{1-u^{2}}}{\frac{1}{2}\log^{2}x}\Bigl(1+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{\log x}\Bigr)\Bigr).

(3.10)

Since
\int\sqrt{1-u^{2}}\mathrm{d}u=\frac{1}{2}(\arcsin(u)+u\sqrt{1-u^{2}})+c
we get

\displaystyle\sqrt{x}\int_{\sqrt{1/2}}^{\sqrt{1-4/x}}\frac{\sqrt{x}\sqrt{1-u^{2}}}{\frac{1}{2}\log^{2}x}\mathrm{d}u=\frac{2x}{\log^{2}x}\Bigl[\frac{1}{2}(\arcsin(u)+u\sqrt{1-u^{2}})\Bigr]_{\sqrt{1/2}}^{\sqrt{1-4/x}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{x}{\log^{2}x}\left(\arcsin(\sqrt{1-4/x})+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}}\Bigr)-\frac{\pi}{4}-\frac{1}{2}\right)=\frac{x}{\log^{2}x}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}}\Bigr)-\frac{\pi}{4}-\frac{1}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{x}{\log^{2}x}\frac{\pi-2}{4}\Bigl(1+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}}\Bigr)\Bigr)=\frac{x}{\log^{2}x}\frac{\pi-2}{4}\Bigl(1+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{\log x}\Bigr)\Bigr).

(3.11)

We now wish to substitute (
3.11
) into (
3.8
). Indeed,

\displaystyle-\smash[t]{\int_{1}^{\sqrt{x/2}}\pi(t)f^{\prime}(t)\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{1}^{\sqrt{x/2}}\frac{t}{\log t}\Bigl(1+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{\log t}\Bigr)\Bigr)(-2t)\frac{\log(x-t^{2})-2}{\sqrt{x-t^{2}}\log^{2}(x-t^{2})}\Bigl(1+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{\log x}\Bigr)\Bigr)\mathrm{d}t

which by (
3.11
) is equal to

\displaystyle 2\Bigl(1+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{\log x}\Bigr)\Bigr)\frac{x}{\log^{2}x}\frac{\pi-2}{4}\Bigl(1+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{\log x}\Bigr)\Bigr)

\displaystyle+\int_{1}^{\sqrt{x/2}}O\Bigl(\frac{t}{\log^{2}t}\Bigr)(-2t)\frac{\log(x-t^{2})}{\sqrt{x-t^{2}}\log^{2}(x-t^{2})}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle+\int_{1}^{\sqrt{x/2}}\frac{t}{\log t}\Bigl(1+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{\log t}\Bigr)\Bigr)(-2t)\frac{-2}{\sqrt{x-t^{2}}\log^{2}(x-t^{2})}\mathrm{d}t.

(3.12)

The second component of the sum above can be estimated using the same substitutions as in (
3.9
). Proceeding as in (
3.10
) we get that the integrand is

O\left(\frac{\sqrt{x}\sqrt{1-u^{2}}}{\frac{1}{2}\log^{3}x}\Bigl(1+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{\log x}\Bigr)\Bigr)\right),

where
u\sqrt{x}=\sqrt{x-t^{2}}
, and hence this component is
O(\frac{x}{\log^{3}x})
.

Similarly, replacing
\frac{t}{\log t}\bigl(1+O(\frac{1}{\log t})\bigr)
with
O(\frac{t}{\log t})
and using the substitution
u\sqrt{x}=\sqrt{x-t^{2}}
gives that the integrand in the third component is

O\left(\frac{\sqrt{x}\sqrt{1-u^{2}}}{\frac{1}{4}\log^{3}x}\Bigl(1+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{\log x}\Bigr)\Bigr)\right),

so this component is also
O(\frac{x}{\log^{3}x})
.

We now substitute (
3.12
) into (
3.8
) to get

\smash[b]{\sum_{p\leq\sqrt{\frac{x}{2}}}}\pi(\sqrt{x-p^{2}})=(\pi(\sqrt{x/2}))^{2}+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{\log x}\Bigr)+2\frac{x}{\log^{2}x}\frac{\pi-2}{4}\Bigl(1+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{\log x}\Bigr)\Bigr)^{3},

and substituting this into (
3.7
) gives

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq x}r_{2}(n)

\displaystyle=(\pi(\sqrt{x/2}))^{2}+4\frac{x}{\log^{2}x}\frac{\pi-2}{4}\Bigl(1+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{\log x}\Bigr)\Bigr)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{x/2}{(\frac{1}{2}\log x)^{2}}\Bigl(1+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{\log x}\Bigr)\Bigr)+\frac{x}{\log^{2}x}({\pi-2})\Bigl(1+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{\log x}\Bigr)\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\pi\frac{x}{\log^{2}x}\Bigl(1+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{\log x}\Bigr)\Bigr).\qed

3.3
Zeroth moments

In this section we discuss the zeroth moments of
r_{0}
,
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
:

Definition

(Zeroth moments of
r_{i}
)
.

We define
M_{i}(x)=\#\{n\leq x:\ r_{i}(n)\geq 1\}
.

We begin by quoting a result of Landau about the zeroth moment of
r_{0}
.

Lemma 3.3.1
.

\displaystyle\smash{M_{0}(x)=\Bigl(2\prod_{p\equiv 3\mod 4}\bigl(1-\tfrac{1}{p^{2}}\bigr)\Bigr)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\frac{x}{\sqrt{\log x}}\bigl(1+O((\log x)^{-1})\bigr)}.

Proof.

In
[
13
, §176]
, the main term is shown subject to some constant
b
. The value of
b
is later shown in
[
13
, §183]
to be the left hand bracketed expression.
∎

The following Lemma relates the zeroth, first and second moments of each of the
r_{i}
. It is a version of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.

Lemma 3.3.2
.

\displaystyle\smash[b]{\Bigl(\sum_{n\leq x}r_{i}(n)\Bigr)^{2}\Bigl(\sum_{n\leq x}r_{i}^{2}(n)\Bigr)^{-1}\leq M_{i}(x)\leq\sum_{n\leq x}r_{i}(n)}
.

Proof.

For the left-hand inequality, we need an application of Cauchy-Schwarz:
\left(\sum_{n\leq x}a_{n}b_{n}\right)^{2}\leq(\sum_{n\leq x}a_{n}^{2})(\sum_{n\leq x}b_{n}^{2})
. Set
a_{n}=r_{i}(n)
and
b_{n}=~\mathbbm{1}_{\{r_{i}(n)>0\}}
; then

\displaystyle\smash[t]{\Bigl(\sum_{n\leq x}r_{i}(n)\Bigr)^{2}=\Bigl(\sum_{n\leq x}a_{n}b_{n}\Bigr)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\Bigl(\sum_{n\leq x}a_{n}^{2}\Bigr)\Bigl(\sum_{n\leq x}b_{n}^{2}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\smash[b]{\Bigl(\sum_{n\leq x}r_{i}^{2}(n)\Bigr)\Bigl(\sum_{n\leq x}\mathbbm{1}_{\{r_{i}(n)>0\}}\Bigr)=\Bigl(\sum_{n\leq x}r_{i}^{2}(n)\Bigr)M_{i}(x).}

Multiplying both sides by
\left(\sum_{n\leq x}r_{i}^{2}(n)\right)^{-1}
gives the inequality.
The right-hand inequality is satisfied as
M_{i}(x)=\#\{n\leq x:\ r_{i}(n)\geq 1\}=\sum_{n\leq x}\mathbbm{1}_{\{r_{i}(n)>0\}}\leq\sum_{n\leq x}r_{i}(n)\mathbbm{1}_{\{r_{i}(n)>0\}}=\sum_{n\leq x}r_{i}(n)
.
∎

Remark 3.1
.

Unless
n
is a perfect square, we must have
r_{2}(n)=0
or
r_{2}(n)\geq 2
. Hence
M_{2}(x)\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n\leq x}r_{2}(n)+O(x^{\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon})
.

To estimate the zeroth moment of
r_{2}
, we will make use of estimates on the first and second moment of
r_{2}
; Lemma
3.4.1
which describes the behaviour of the second moment of
r_{2}
can be found in the subsequent section.

Corollary 3.3.3

(The zeroth moment of
r_{2}
)
.

M_{2}(x)=\dfrac{\pi}{2}\dfrac{x}{\log^{2}x}\left(1+O\Bigl(\frac{L(x)}{\log x}\Bigr)\right)
.

Proof.

We have
M_{2}(x)\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n\leq x}r_{2}(n)+O(x^{\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon})
. So by Lemma
3.2.2
, we get that
M_{2}(x)
is at most the RHS of the above equation.

Using Lemma
3.2.2
and Lemma
3.4.1
and noting that
O(\frac{x}{\log^{3}x})=O\bigl(\frac{xL(x)}{\log^{3}x}\bigr)
we have

\displaystyle\smash{\sum_{n\leq x}r_{2}(n)^{2}=2\sum_{n\leq x}r_{2}(n)+O\Bigl(\frac{x}{\log^{3}x}\Bigr)}

\displaystyle=2\frac{\pi x}{\log^{2}x}\Bigl(1+O\Bigl(\frac{L(x)}{\log x}\Bigr)\Bigr).

From Lemma
3.3.2
,
M_{2}(x)\geq(\sum_{n\leq x}r_{2}(n))^{2}(\sum_{n\leq x}r_{2}(n)^{2})^{-1}
. Hence

\displaystyle M_{2}(x)

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{\pi x}{\log^{2}x}\left(1+O\Bigl(\frac{L(x)}{\log x}\Bigr)\right)\right)^{2}\left(2\frac{\pi x}{\log^{2}x}\left(1+O\Bigl(\frac{L(x)}{\log x}\Bigr)\right)\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=2\frac{\pi x}{\log^{2}x}\left(1+O\Bigl(\frac{L(x)}{\log x}\Bigr)\right).

We have equality due to the inequalities holding in both directions.
∎

Finally, we can bound the zeroth moment of
r_{1}
through Lemma
3.3.2
. Indeed, we substitute our known estimate of the first moment of
r_{1}
from Lemma
3.2.1
; an asymptotic formula for the second moment was proved by Daniel in
[
3
]
and we state the result in Lemma
6.4.2
. We obtain

\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}\frac{x^{2}}{\log^{2}x}\frac{4}{2\pi+9}\frac{\log x}{x}\left(1+O\Bigl(\frac{(\log\log x)^{2}}{\log x}\Bigr)\right)\leq M_{1}(x)\leq\frac{\pi}{2}\frac{x}{\log x}\left(1+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{\log x}\Bigr)\right),

where the left hand leading term simplifies to
\frac{\pi^{2}}{2\pi+9}\frac{x}{\log x}
. The lower and upper bounds have the same order of leading term, and moreover Stephan Daniel
[
3
]
and Granville, Sabuncu, Sedunova
[
7
]
show that the upper bound is the most correct estimate. The more recent paper provides a more refined error term in
[
7
, Theorem 1]
:

Theorem 3.3.4
.

\displaystyle\smash[b]{M_{1}(x)=\frac{\pi}{2}\frac{x}{\log x}-\frac{x(\log\log x)^{O(1)}}{(\log x)^{1+\delta}}}
, where
\delta=1-\frac{1+\log\log 2}{\log 2}
.

3.4
Second and higher moments of
r_{2}

We finish this chapter with a discussion about moments of
r_{2}
. For a given
n
, the quantity
r_{2}^{k}(n)
counts the number of
k
-tuples of pairs of primes that sum to
n
, i.e.
r_{2}^{k}(n)=\#\{(\textbf{p, q})\in\mathcal{P}^{k}\times\mathcal{P}^{k}:\forall i\leq k,p_{i}^{2}+q_{i}^{2}=n\}
. Here, and only in this section:

Notation
.

\mathcal{P}
is the set of all primes.

This set of tuples can be partitioned according to how many of the pairs
p_{i},q_{i}
are in fact the same pair. We write the following definition:

Definition

(Non-diagonal solutions of level
k
)
.

\mathcal{D}_{k}(x):=\#\{(\textbf{p, q})\in(\mathcal{P}\cap[0,\sqrt{x}])^{2k}:p_{i}^{2}+q_{i}^{2}=p_{j}^{2}+q_{j}^{2},\{p_{i},q_{i}\}\neq\{p_{j},q_{j}\},\forall i\neq j\}.

The
k
-th moment of
r_{2}
can be written as a linear combination of non-diagonal solutions of levels from
1
to
k
. In particular,
\mathcal{D}_{1}(x)
, the first moment of
r_{2}
, appears with coefficient
2^{k-1}
in the
k
-th moment as for a fixed pair of primes
p,q
with
p^{2}+q^{2}=n
, there are
2^{k-1}
possible vectors
(\textbf{p, q})\in\mathcal{P}^{k}\times\mathcal{P}^{k}
with
p_{1}=p
,
q_{1}=q
and
\{p_{i},q_{i}\}=\{p,q\}
for all
i>1
. (We can ignore the case
n=2p^{2}
, which is rare.)

We already know the asymptotics of the first moment of
r_{2}
, and Lemma
3.4.1
below tells us the behaviour of the second moment of
r_{2}
. The error term comes from the work of Rieger: his result
[
16
, Satz 3]
is in fact showing that
\mathcal{D}_{2}(x)\ll\frac{x}{\log^{3}x}
. As explained above,
\mathcal{D}_{2}(x)
is number of distinct pairs of primes
(p_{1},q_{1})
,
(p_{2},q_{2})
such that
p_{1}^{2}+q_{1}^{2}=p_{2}^{2}+q_{2}^{2}\leq x
, and
\sum_{n\leq x}r_{2}(n)^{2}=2\sum_{n\leq x}r_{2}(n)+\mathcal{D}_{2}(x)
.

Lemma 3.4.1
.

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq x}r_{2}(n)^{2}=2\sum_{n\leq x}r_{2}(n)+O\left(\frac{x}{\log^{3}x}\right)
.

We state Rieger’s theorem from
[
16
]
and explain why this shows
{\mathcal{D}_{2}(x)\ll\frac{x}{\log^{3}x}}
.

Theorem 3.4.2
.

Let
T(y)
be the number of tuples
(d,t,n,m)\in\mathbb{N}^{4}
such that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} dn\leq y,\ tn\leq y,\ m<n,\ d\neq t;\ tn\pm dm,\ tm\pm dn\in\mathcal{P}.\end{aligned}

Then
T(y)=O(y^{2}(\log y)^{-3})
.

Proof that
{\mathcal{D}_{2}(x)\ll\frac{x}{\log^{3}x}}
assuming Theorem
3.4.2
.

Let
x>0
and
y=\sqrt{x}
. Suppose we have two distinct pairs of primes
(p_{1},q_{1})
and
(p_{2},q_{2})
such that
p_{1}^{2}+q_{1}^{2}=p_{2}^{2}+q_{2}^{2}=n
, some
n\leq x
. Note that it is impossible for just one of the
p_{i}
,
q_{i}
to be
2
, so we can assume that all
p_{i}
and
q_{i}
are odd. Thus,
c_{1}=\frac{1}{2}(p_{2}-p_{1}),c_{2}=\frac{1}{2}(q_{2}-q_{1})\in\mathbb{Z}
. Let
d=\gcd(c_{1},c_{2})
and let the integers
m,n
be defined by
m=\frac{c_{1}}{d}
and
n=\frac{c_{2}}{d}
.

Note that by definition of the
p_{i}
and
q_{i}
, and factorising difference of two squares, we can write
c_{1}(p_{2}+p_{1})+c_{2}(q_{2}+q_{1})=0
. But
p_{1}+c_{1}=\frac{1}{2}(p_{2}+p_{1})
and
q_{1}+c_{2}=\frac{1}{2}(q_{2}+q_{1})
, by definition of the
c_{i}
. Hence
c_{1}(p_{1}+c_{1})+c_{2}(q_{1}+c_{2})=0
. Dividing by
d
gives
m(p_{1}+c_{1})+n(q_{1}+c_{2})=0
. In particular,
m(p_{1}+c_{1})
is a multiple of
n
. But
m,n
are coprime by definition, so
n\mid(p_{1}+c_{1})
.

Let
t=\frac{p_{1}+c_{1}}{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then
tn=p_{1}+c_{1}
, so
p_{1}=tn-dm
. Also,
p_{2}=p_{1}+2c_{1}=tn+c_{1}=tn+dm
.

Now
tmn+n(q_{1}+c_{2})=0
, so
q_{1}+c_{2}=tm
and hence
q_{1}=tm-c_{2}=tm-dn
. Finally
q_{2}=q_{1}+2c_{2}=tm+dn
.

By swapping some signs and ordering of the prime pairs we can guarantee
d,t,n,m
are as claimed in Theorem
3.4.2
; since
q_{1},q_{2}\leq\sqrt{x}
we have
dn=|c_{2}|\leq\sqrt{x}=y
. Similarly
tn=\frac{1}{2}(p_{2}+p_{1})\leq\sqrt{x}=y
.

Hence
\mathcal{D}_{2}(x)\leq T(y)=O(y^{2}(\log y)^{-3})=O(x(\frac{1}{2}\log x)^{-3})\ll\frac{x}{\log^{3}x}
.
∎

We omit the proof of Theorem
3.4.2
; it relies mainly on sieve methods. We will exhibit other proofs based on the sieve approach in Chapters
6
and
7
.

Now we turn to third and higher moments of
r_{2}
. Blomer and Brüdern showed in
[
1
]
that
\mathcal{D}_{3}(x)\ll x(\log\log x)^{6}(\log x)^{-3}
; this bound was more recently improved by Sabuncu in his paper
[
17
]
. Sabuncu also gave a general formula for upper bounds on moments of
r_{2}
, as well as lower bounds conditional on a conjecture about the size of a particular set. The conjecture would rely on a generalisation of the Green-Tao theorem; however, any weaker lower bound for this set that can be obtained would also give lower bounds on higher moments of
r_{2}
.

We define some additional useful notation below:

Definition

(
R_{2}
, Stirling numbers,
\mathcal{S}_{\ell}
)
.

•

R_{2}(n):=\#\{(p,q)\in\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{P}:p<q,\ p^{2}+q^{2}=n\}
. Since order matters when counting the pairs of primes for
r_{2}
, we have
r_{2}(n)=2R_{2}(n)+\mathbbm{1}_{n\in 2\mathcal{P}^{2}}
(
R_{2}
skips the possible case of
n
being double a prime square.)

•

\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k}{\ell}
is a Stirling number of the second kind, and is the number of ways to partition
k
distinct objects into
\ell
nonempty sets.

•

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\ell}:=\ell!\sum_{n\leq x}\binom{R_{2}(n)}{\ell}
.

We can express moments of
r_{2}
in terms of
R_{2}
as follows:

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq x}r_{2}^{k}(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\leq x}(2R_{2}(n)+\mathbbm{1}_{n\in 2\mathcal{P}^{2}})^{k}=2^{k}\sum_{n\leq x}\left(R_{2}^{k}(n)+O(R_{2}^{k-1}(n)\mathbbm{1}_{n\in 2\mathcal{P}^{2}})\right)

\displaystyle=2^{k}\sum_{n\leq x}R_{2}^{k}(n)+O\Bigl(\!\!\!\sum_{p\leq\sqrt{x}/2}R_{2}^{k-1}(2p^{2})\Bigr)=2^{k}\smash[b]{\sum_{n\leq x}}R_{2}^{k}(n)+O\Bigl(\frac{\sqrt{x}}{\log x}\Bigr).

(3.13)

The last equality is achieved as
R_{2}(2p^{2})\leq r_{0}(2p^{2})\leq 4
, so the sum in the error term is bounded by
4\#\{p\leq\frac{\sqrt{x}}{2}\}\ll\frac{\sqrt{x}}{\log x}
, using the Prime Number Theorem.

Lemma 3.4.3
.

\displaystyle P(x,k):=\smash[b]{\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k}{\ell}x(x-1)\dots(x-\ell+1)}=x^{k}
.

Proof.

We begin by stating an equivalent definition for
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k}{\ell}
. Note that
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k}{\ell}\cdot\ell!
is the number of ways to partition
k
distinct objects into
\ell
labelled sets, or equivalently, the number of surjections
\{1,\dots,k\}\to\{1,\dots,\ell\}
.

Suppose
x
is a given integer. Then
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k}{\ell}x(x-1)\dots(x-\ell+1)=\ell!\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k}{\ell}\binom{x}{\ell}
, which is the number of functions
\{1,\dots,k\}\to\{1,\dots,x\}
whose image contains exactly
\ell
elements. Hence
P(x,k)
is the total number of functions
\{1,\dots,k\}\to\{1,\dots,x\}
, as the image of such a function has size between
0
and
k
. We have
x
choices for each element of
\{1,\dots,k\}
, so
P(x,k)=x^{k}
for all integer
x
. By polynomial interpolation of degree
k
polynomials we in fact get equality for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
.
∎

By Lemma
3.4.3
, the main term of the right hand side of (
3.13
) can be rewritten as

\!\!\!\smash{\sum_{n\leq x}R_{2}^{k}(n)=\sum_{n\leq x}\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k}{\ell}R_{2}(n)(R_{2}(n)-1)\dots(R_{2}(n)-\ell+1)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k}{\ell}\mathcal{S}_{\ell}.}

(3.14)

(We may start the sum at
1
as
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k}{0}
, the number of ways to partition
k
objects into
0
nonempty subsets, is
0
.)

Thus, to find an estimate for the
k
-th moment of
r_{2}
, we seek bounds for the
S_{\ell}
.

Theorem 3.4.4
.

[
17
, Theorem 1.1]

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{2},\mathcal{S}_{3}

\displaystyle\smash[t]{\ll x\frac{(\log\log x)^{2}}{\log^{3}x},\ \mathcal{S}_{4}\ll x\frac{\log\log\log x}{\log x}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{k}

\displaystyle\ll_{k}\smash[b]{x(\log x)^{2^{k-1}-2k-1},k\geq 5.}

Lower bounds for the
S_{k}
would correspondingly give lower bounds for
k
-th moments of
r_{2}
. Sabuncu conjectures such bounds in his paper
[
17
]
. However, the results stated in Theorem
3.4.4
above do not rely on any conjecture, and lead to the following:

Corollary 3.4.5

(Higher moments of
r_{2}
)
.

\displaystyle\smash[t]{\sum_{n\leq x}r_{2}^{3}(n)}

\displaystyle=\smash[t]{\frac{4\pi x}{\log^{2}x}+O\Bigl(x\frac{(\log\log x)^{2}}{\log^{3}x}\Bigr)}

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq x}r_{2}^{4}(n)

\displaystyle\ll x(\log\log\log x)(\log x)^{-1}

\displaystyle\smash[b]{\sum_{n\leq x}r_{2}^{k}(n)}

\displaystyle\ll_{k}x(\log x)^{2^{k-1}-2k-1},k\geq 5.

Proof.

We state upper bounds for various
\mathcal{S}_{k}
in Theorem
3.4.4
. Note that for
k\geq 4
, the corresponding upper bound for
\mathcal{S}_{k}
is the main contribution in the upper bound obtained for
\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k}{\ell}\mathcal{S}_{\ell}
. Hence from (
3.14
) we immediately get the upper bounds for
\sum_{n\leq x}r_{2}^{k}(n),k\geq 4
.

For the third moment, we use (
3.13
) to get

\displaystyle\smash[t]{\sum_{n\leq x}r_{2}^{3}(n)}

\displaystyle=\smash[t]{2^{3}\sum_{n\leq x}R_{2}^{3}(n)+O\Bigl(\frac{\sqrt{x}}{\log x}\Bigr)=8\mathcal{S}_{1}+O\Bigl(x\frac{(\log\log x)^{2}}{\log^{3}x}\Bigr)+O\Bigl(\frac{\sqrt{x}}{\log x}\Bigr)}

\displaystyle=8\sum_{n\leq x}R_{2}(n)+O\Bigl(x\frac{(\log\log x)^{2}}{\log^{3}x}\Bigr)=4\sum_{n\leq x}r_{2}(n)+O\Bigl(x\frac{(\log\log x)^{2}}{\log^{3}x}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{4\pi x}{\log^{2}x}+O\Bigl(x\frac{(\log\log x)^{2}}{\log^{3}x}\Bigr).\qed

The proof of Theorem
3.4.4
relies on sieve methods, which we will learn about more in the next chapter. Indeed, the proof structure is similar to that of Theorem
6.3.1
, and Sabuncu remarks in
[
17
]
that one obtains analogously to his methods that
\sum_{n\leq x}r_{1}^{k}(n)\ll_{k}x(\log x)^{2^{k-1}-k-1}
for
k\geq 5.
We will see later that this is consistent with our results about moments of
r_{1}
.

We will now look at Sabuncu’s conjecture. We provide a definition from
[
17
]
:

Definition
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 2}
and

(u_{i},v_{i})\neq(u_{j},v_{j})

For a set
\{u_{1},v_{1},\dots,u_{k},v_{k}\}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
as above, define

. Then

by definition of the numbers
u_{i}
,

. Let
\mathcal{L}_{m}

\mathcal{L}_{m}=\{u_{i}x+v_{i}y,v_{i}x-u_{i}y\colon 1\leq i\leq k\}

Claim 3.4.6

(Conjecture,

]
)
.

Chapter 4
Sieves

In this chapter we will explore the basics of sieve theory and look at some particular examples of sieves that are used in analytic methods. Powerful results such as the fundamental lemma of sieve theory will be useful to us later on to provide upper bounds on certain sums when estimating asymptotics of representation numbers. For references, see
[
4
]
,
[
15
]
and
[
10
]
.

A general sieve problem typically requires the following ingredients:

•

A finite probability space
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
, whose size we can estimate.

•

A collection of primes,
\mathfrak{p}
, with respect to which we construct the sieve.

•

For each prime
p\in\mathfrak{p}
, a collection of residue classes
\mathfrak{r}(p)\subseteq\{0,1,\dots,p-1\}
modulo
p
.

•

A real number
z
.

A sieve problem usually deals with a multiset or sequence of natural numbers; our task is to sift out (remove) those that fall into one of our chosen residue classes modulo an element of
\mathfrak{p}_{\leq z}
, and estimate how many remain. Depending on the number of residue classes chosen modulo each prime
p\in\mathfrak{p}
, we may consider the sieve to be
small
(if
few
residue classes are chosen) or
large
(if
many
residue classes are chosen).

4.1
Definitions

We will need to introduce some additional definitions and notation to use in this chapter. Throughout the chapter, we will consider
\mathbb{P}
to be the uniform probability distribution – each element of
\mathcal{A}
has probability
\dfrac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|}
of occurring. The probability space will be discrete, i.e.
\mathcal{F}
is the power set of
\mathcal{A}
.

Definition
.

(Sieve notation)
For
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
a finite probability space,
\mathfrak{p}
a (possibly infinite) set of prime numbers and
z\in\mathbb{R}
:

•

We denote by
X
an estimate of
|\mathcal{A}|
, and let
R:=|\mathcal{A}|-X
(the remainder).

•

We define events
\mathcal{A}_{p},\ \forall p\in\mathfrak{p}
(often based on a congruence condition mod
p
).

•

For each squarefree
d\in\mathbb{N}
that is a product of primes in
\mathfrak{p}
, let
\mathcal{A}_{d}
be the event
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{d}=\bigcap_{p\mid d}\mathcal{A}_{p}
. In particular, we adopt the convention
\mathcal{A}_{1}=\mathcal{A}
.

•

\mathcal{P}(z)=\displaystyle\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq z\\
p\in\mathfrak{p}\end{subarray}}p
is the product of all primes in
\mathfrak{p}
that are at most
z
.

•

S(\mathcal{A},\mathfrak{p},z)=|\mathcal{A}|\cdot\mathbb{P}(\bigcap_{p\mid\mathcal{P}(z)}\overline{\mathcal{A}_{p}})=\#\{a\in\mathcal{A}:\forall p\mid\mathcal{P}(z),\ a\not\in\mathcal{A}_{p}\}
.

4.2
The small, or Selberg, sieve

In this section, we work with a special case of a multivariate polynomial small sieve; let
F\colon\mathbb{Z}^{k}\to\mathbb{Z}
,
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{k}
a finite subset, and define
\mathcal{A}_{p}=\{\textbf{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})\in\mathcal{A}:F(\textbf{a})\equiv 0\pmod{p}\}
. Indeed, setting
S=\{F(\textbf{a}):a\in\mathcal{A}\}
, our sieve problem is sift out all multiples of
p
for any
p\in\mathfrak{p}_{\leq z}
from
S
.

Recall the function
I(n)=\begin{cases}1,n=1\\
0,n>1\end{cases}
. Now, given
\textbf{a}\in\mathcal{A}
,
I(\gcd(F(\textbf{a}),\mathcal{P}(z)))
is an indicator function which determines whether
a
is counted towards the sum
S(\mathcal{A},\mathfrak{p},z)
. i.e.
\displaystyle S(\mathcal{A},\mathfrak{p},z)=\sum_{\textbf{a}\in\mathcal{A}}I(\gcd(F(\textbf{a}),\mathcal{P}(z)))
. Now, since
\mu*\mathbbm{1}=I
we have
\displaystyle S(\mathcal{A},\mathfrak{p},z)=\sum_{\textbf{a}\in\mathcal{A}}(\mu*\mathbbm{1})(\gcd(F(\textbf{a}),\mathcal{P}(z)))=\sum_{\textbf{a}\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{d\mid(F(\textbf{a}),\mathcal{P}(z))}\mu(d)
. We can reverse the order of summation to get

S(\mathcal{A},\mathfrak{p},z)=\sum_{d\mid\mathcal{P}(z)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\textbf{a}\in\mathcal{A}\\
d\mid F(\textbf{a})\end{subarray}}\mu(d)=\sum_{d\mid\mathcal{P}(z)}\mu(d)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\textbf{a}\in\mathcal{A}\\
d\mid F(\textbf{a})\end{subarray}}1=\sum_{d\mid\mathcal{P}(z)}\mu(d)|\mathcal{A}_{d}|.

(4.1)

Thus in order to estimate
S(\mathcal{A},\mathfrak{p},z)
, we estimate
|\mathcal{A}_{d}|
for squarefree
d\in\mathbb{N}
.

Definition
.

We define a multiplicative function
g
as follows:

•

\forall n>1:g(p^{n})=0
, for any prime
p
.

•

g(p)=0
for any prime
p\not\in\mathfrak{p}
.

•

For a prime
p\in\mathfrak{p}
,
0\leq g(p)<1
is chosen so that
g(p)X
is close to
|\mathcal{A}_{p}|
.

Due to the first condition,
g
is a function that is
0
outside of squarefrees, and by multiplicativity, for squarefree
d
we have
g(d)=\prod_{p\mid d}g(p)
.

We additionally define
R_{d}:=|\mathcal{A}_{d}|-g(d)X
; from the definition above we can see that
R_{p}
is small for primes
p\in\mathfrak{p}
. Now, we can rewrite (
4.1
) and obtain

\smash{S(\mathcal{A},\mathfrak{p},z)=X\sum_{d\mid\mathcal{P}(z)}\mu(d)g(d)+\sum_{d\mid\mathcal{P}(z)}\mu(d)R_{d}\leq X\sum_{d\mid\mathcal{P}(z)}\mu(d)g(d)+\sum_{d\mid\mathcal{P}(z)}|R_{d}|.}

(4.2)

Note that it is very difficult to control the error terms
R_{d}
, especially as we are summing for
d
up to
\mathcal{P}(z)
, which is large. One method to deal with this is to try and get rid of
R_{d}
for
d
past a certain point. We start with the following result.

Lemma 4.2.1
.

Let
\mu^{+}
be an arithmetic function so that for all
\textbf{a}\in\mathcal{A}
,

\sum_{d\mid\gcd(F(\textbf{a}),\mathcal{P}(z))}\mu^{+}(d)\geq\begin{cases}1,&\gcd(F(\textbf{a}),\mathcal{P}(z))=1\\
0,&\gcd(F(\textbf{a}),\mathcal{P}(z))>1\end{cases}.

Then
\displaystyle S(\mathcal{A},\mathfrak{p},z)\leq X\sum_{d\mid\mathcal{P}(z)}\mu^{+}(d)g(d)+\sum_{d\mid\mathcal{P}(z)}|\mu^{+}(d)R_{d}|
.

We now find a suitable function
\mu^{+}
. Let
\lambda_{d}\in\mathbb{R}
be an arbitrary sequence of real numbers, such that
\lambda_{1}=1
, and set
\displaystyle\mu^{+}(d)=\sum_{d=[d_{1},d_{2}]}\lambda_{d_{1}}\lambda_{d_{2}}
, where
[d_{1},d_{2}]
denotes the lowest common multiple of
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
. We check
\mu^{+}
satisfies the condition in Lemma
4.2.1
: If for some
\textbf{a}\in\mathcal{A}
we have
\gcd(F(\textbf{a}),\mathcal{P}(z))=1
, then
\sum_{d\mid\gcd(F(\textbf{a}),\mathcal{P}(z))}\mu^{+}(d)=\lambda_{1}^{2}=1\geq 1
, and if
\gcd(F(\textbf{a}),\mathcal{P}(z))>1
, then
\sum_{d\mid\gcd(F(\textbf{a}),\mathcal{P}(z))}\mu^{+}(d)=\Bigl(\sum_{d\mid\gcd(F(\textbf{a}),\mathcal{P}(z))}\lambda_{d}\Bigr)^{2}\geq 0
. Hence by Lemma
4.2.1
we get

S(\mathcal{A},\mathfrak{p},z)\leq X\sum_{d\mid\mathcal{P}(z)}\sum_{d=[d_{1},d_{2}]}\lambda_{d_{1}}\lambda_{d_{2}}g(d)+\sum_{d\mid\mathcal{P}(z)}\sum_{d=[d_{1},d_{2}]}|\lambda_{d_{1}}\lambda_{d_{2}}R_{d}|.

(4.3)

We can now restrict the maximum
d
for which
R_{d}
is considered by taking
\lambda_{d}=0
for
d\geq\xi
, some
\xi
; then
\mu^{+}(d)=0
for
d\geq\xi^{2}
, as
d,d^{\prime}<\xi\implies[d,d^{\prime}]\leq d_{1}d_{2}<\xi^{2}
so for
d\geq\xi^{2}
we have
\displaystyle\sum_{d=[d_{1},d_{2}]}\lambda_{d_{1}}\lambda_{d_{2}}=\sum_{d=[d_{1},d_{2}]}0=0
. To improve the bound on
S(\mathcal{A},\mathfrak{p},z)
as much as possible, we now optimise the values of
\lambda_{d}
for
d\leq\xi
.

Let
\displaystyle G:=\sum_{d\mid\mathcal{P}(z)}\sum_{d=[d_{1},d_{2}]}\lambda_{d_{1}}\lambda_{d_{2}}g(d)=\sum\!\!\!\!\sum_{[d_{1},d_{2}]\mid\mathcal{P}(z)}\mu^{+}([d_{1},d_{2}])g([d_{1},d_{2}])
.

The sieve problem now becomes an optimisation problem. We do not provide the reasoning for finding the optimal choice here, but it can be found in Heath-Brown’s lecture notes
[
10
]
, for example. Note that the convention followed in this text is to use a ‘weight’ function denoted
\omega
; our function
g(p)
corresponds to
\frac{\omega(p)}{p}
in
[
10
]
.

Definition
.

•

Let
h
be defined by
\frac{1}{h(n)}=\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(\frac{n}{d})g(d)^{-1}
. It is multiplicative as
\mu
,
g^{-1}
are, and
\frac{1}{h}
is a Dirichlet convolution. In particular,
\frac{1}{h(p)}=-1+\frac{1}{g(p)}=\frac{-g(p)+1}{g(p)}
, so
h(p)=\frac{g(p)}{1-g(p)}=g(p)(1-g(p))^{-1}
. Thus
h(d)=g(d)\prod_{p\mid d}(1-g(p))^{-1}
by multiplicativity of
h
and
g
.

•

G_{d}(\xi,z):=\sum_{dl\mid\mathcal{P}(z),l<\xi}h(l)
. We adopt the convention
G_{1}(\xi,z)=G(\xi,z)
, so

G(\xi,z):=\sum_{l\mid\mathcal{P}(z),l<\xi}h(l)
.

Heath-Brown shows in
[
10
]
that the bound is optimised when

\smash{\lambda_{d}=\mu(d)\prod_{p\mid d}(1-g(p))^{-1}\frac{G_{d}(\frac{\xi}{d},z)}{G(\xi,z)}.}

Substituting into (
4.3
) we get

\smash{\sum_{d\mid\mathcal{P}(z)}\sum_{d=[d_{1},d_{2}]}\lambda_{d_{1}}\lambda_{d_{2}}g(d)=\dfrac{1}{G(\xi,z)}.}

Hence we obtain

Lemma 4.2.2

(Fundamental lemma of sieve theory)
.

\smash{S(\mathcal{A},\mathfrak{p},z)\leq\frac{X}{G(\xi,z)}+\sum_{d\leq\xi^{2},d\mid\mathcal{P}(z)}3^{\omega(d)}|R_{d}|.}

Here, recall
\omega
is the function counting the number of distinct prime factors of
n
.

Halberstam and Richert showed in
[
8
, Lemma 5.4]
that
G(z,z)=\frac{1}{e^{\gamma\kappa}\Gamma(\kappa+1)}\prod_{p<z}(1-g(p))^{-1}(1+O(\tfrac{1}{\log z}))
. So setting
\xi=z
, we get

Theorem 4.2.3
.

\displaystyle S(\mathcal{A},\mathfrak{p},z)\leq e^{\gamma\kappa}\Gamma(\kappa+1)\prod_{p<z}(1-g(p))(1+O(\tfrac{1}{\log z}))+\sum_{d\leq z^{2},d\mid\mathcal{P}(z)}\!\!\!\!\!\!3^{\omega(d)}|R_{d}|.

Note that Lemma
4.2.2
and Lemma
4.2.3
are more general than the polynomial sieve case we discussed earlier; as long as a suitable function
g(d)
is defined in a sieving problem, we can use these results. In particular, in Chapter
7
we use a slightly modified polynomial sieve where the events
\mathcal{A}_{p}
are dependent on divisibility by
p^{2}
, not just
p
.

Chapter 5
Common divisors of sums of squares

In this chapter we will explore some modified representation numbers, as defined below; applying properties of these functions in combination with methods such as sieves will help us prove results about higher moments of
r_{1}
.

Definition
.

•

r_{0}^{*}(n):=\#\{a\geq 0,b>0:n=a^{2}+b^{2}\text{ and }\gcd(a,b)=1\}

•

r_{1}^{*}(n):=\#\{a\geq 0,p>0:n=a^{2}+p^{2}\text{ and }\gcd(a,p)=1,p\text{ prime}\}

•

r_{2}^{*}(n):=\#\{p,q>0:n=p^{2}+q^{2},\ p,q\text{ prime, }p\neq q\}

5.1
The function
r_{0}^{*}

Granville, Sabuncu and Sedunova’s paper
[
7
]
uses auxiliary results about
r_{0}^{*}
in their proof for an upper bound on expressions of form
\sum\binom{r_{1}(n)}{\ell}
. We provide full proofs of some of these results below, which were not given in the paper.

Lemma 5.1.1

(Properties of
r_{0}^{*}
)
.

r_{0}^{*}(2)=1
,
r_{0}^{*}(2^{k})=0
for
k\geq 2
,
r_{0}^{*}(p^{k})=1+(\frac{-1}{p})
for odd primes
p
, where
(\frac{\cdot}{p})
is the Legendre symbol modulo
p
.

Proof.

•

r_{0}^{*}(2)=1
as the only possible representation is
2=1^{2}+1^{2}
.

•

r_{0}^{*}(2^{k})=0
for
k\geq 2
as any multiple of
4
written as a sum of two squares must be written as a sum of two even squares, which are then not coprime.

•

If
p\equiv 3\pmod{4}
then only even powers of
p
have a representation as a sum of two squares, and in any such representation, both squares have to be multiples of
p
. Also,
-1
is not a square modulo
p
. Thus we indeed get
r_{0}^{*}(p^{k})=0=1-1=1+\bigl(\frac{-1}{p}\bigr)
.

•

If
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}
then, as shown in the proof of Lemma
3.1.1
, we have
r_{0}(p^{k})=k+1
. So in particular,
r_{0}(p)=2
. Hence
r_{0}^{*}(p)=2
, because any possible common factor of squares whose sum is
p
would be a factor of
p
, but
p<p^{2}
. Similarly, as
p^{2}=0+p^{2}
is the only representation of
p^{2}
where both squares are multiples of
p^{2}
(and the second one is positive), we get
r_{0}^{*}(p^{2})=3-1=2
. In general, for powers
k\geq 2
,
-1
is a square modulo
p
so we have

r_{0}^{*}(p^{k})=r_{0}(p^{k})-r_{0}(p^{k-2})=2=1+1=1+\bigl(\tfrac{-1}{p}\bigr).
∎

Lemma 5.1.2
.

r_{0}^{*}
is a multiplicative function.

Proof.

Firstly,
r_{0}
is multiplicative: Recall from equation (
3.1
) that
r_{0}(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}\chi(d)
. Since the non-principal Dirichlet character
\chi
in the proof of Lemma
3.1.1
is multiplicative, and so is the function
\mathbbold{1}
which takes the value
1
everywhere, we have that their convolution
r_{0}
is multiplicative too.

We start by checking that if
\gcd(a,b)=\gcd(c,d)=1
and
a^{2}+b^{2}=n_{1}
,
c^{2}+d^{2}=n_{2}
with
\gcd(n_{1},n_{2})=1
then
(a+bi)(c+di)
has coprime real and imaginary part.

We have that
\Re((a+bi)(c+di))=ac-bd
and
\Im((a+bi)(c+di))=ad+bc
. Suppose
p
is a common prime factor of
ac-bd
and
ad+bc
. Then in particular,
p
divides
c(ac-bd)=ac^{2}-bcd
and
d(ad+bc)=ad^{2}+bcd
so

p\mid a(c^{2}+d^{2})=an_{2}.

(5.1)

Similarly

p\mid abc-b^{2}d,p\mid a^{2}d+abc\implies p\mid d(a^{2}+b^{2})=dn_{1}.

(5.2)

Suppose
p\mid a
. Then, since
p\mid ac-bd
we get
p\mid bd
. But
a,b
are coprime, hence
p\mid d
. Since
c,d
are coprime,
p
does not divide
c
. But then
ad+bc
cannot be a multiple of
p
, which is a contradiction.

Similarly,
p
is not a factor of
d
. Hence (
5.1
)
\implies p\mid n_{2}
, and (
5.2
)
\implies p\mid n_{1}
. But
\gcd(n_{1},n_{2})=1
, so this is a contradiction.

Thus
ac-bd
and
ad+bc
have no common prime factors, so they are coprime.

We now conclude that
r_{0}^{*}
is multiplicative using the same ideas as in the proof of Lemma
3.1.1
:
r_{0}^{*}(n)
is the number of points
\alpha+\beta i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}[i]
on the circle of radius
\sqrt{n}
such that
\gcd(\alpha,\beta)=1
, and the way to count these points arises from considering the prime factorisation of
n
. All representations of
n
as a sum of two coprime squares arise from products of Gaussian prime factors of
n
, so using the above we get
r_{0}^{*}(n)=\prod_{i=1}^{k}r_{0}^{*}(p^{a_{i}})
where
n=\prod_{i=1}^{k}p^{a_{i}}
. Thus
r_{0}^{*}
is indeed multiplicative.
∎

Lemma
5.1.2
and Lemma
5.1.1
together determine the value of the function
r_{0}^{*}
at any integer. In particular, we see that
r_{0}^{*}(n)=0
if
4\mid n
or
p\mid n
for some
p\equiv 3\pmod{4}
; otherwise
r_{0}^{*}(n)=2^{j}
, where
j
is the number of distinct odd (congruent to
1
mod
4
) prime factors.

The following result is also from
[
7
]
.
I have provided a detailed proof below.

Lemma 5.1.3
.

Let
z=x^{1/L}
, and recall
P(n)
is the largest prime factor of
n
. Then for any integer
m\geq 1
,

\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
P(n)\leq z\text{ or }(P(n)^{2})\mid n\end{subarray}}}r_{0}^{*}(n)^{m}\ll_{m}\frac{x}{(\log x)^{1000}}.

Proof.

We split into two cases, according to the conditions on
P(n)
.

Case 1
: First, suppose
P(n)\leq z
.
Let
\sigma=1-\frac{\log(L\log L)}{\log z}
, where we recall
L=\log\log x
. Note that by definition of
z
the denominator is equal to
\frac{1}{L}\log x
. Since
L\log L\ll\log x
we have
\log(L\log L)\ll L
, so
L\log(L\log L)\ll L^{2}
. Hence
L\log(L\log L)<\frac{1}{2}\log x
for large enough
x
. Thus we can assume
\sigma\in(\frac{1}{2},1)
.

Since
n\leq x
, we can bound the LHS by another sum:

\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
P(n)\leq z\end{subarray}}}r_{0}^{*}(n)^{m}\leq\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
P(n)\leq z\end{subarray}}}r_{0}^{*}(n)^{m}\Bigl(\frac{x}{n}\Bigr)^{\sigma}\leq\sum_{P(n)\leq z}r_{0}^{*}(n)^{m}\Bigl(\frac{x}{n}\Bigr)^{\sigma}.

(5.3)

Recall that
r_{0}^{*}
is multiplicative. The
right hand side of (
5.3
) is the Dirichlet series
D(\sigma)=x^{\sigma}\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{r_{0}^{*}(n)^{m}\mathbbm{1}\{P(n)\leq z\}}{n^{\sigma}}
. Since all prime factors of
n
are bounded by
z
, we can write an Euler product

\smash{\sum_{P(n)\leq z}r_{0}^{*}(n)^{m}\Bigl(\frac{x}{n}\Bigr)^{\sigma}=x^{\sigma}\prod_{p\leq z}\left(1+\frac{r_{0}^{*}(p)^{m}}{p^{\sigma}}+\frac{r_{0}^{*}(p^{2})^{m}}{p^{2\sigma}}+\dots\right).}

We know from Lemma
5.1.1
that
r_{0}^{*}(p)\leq 2
for any prime
p
; using the Taylor expansion of the exponential function, the above is thus bounded by
x^{\sigma}\exp(\sum_{p\leq z}\frac{2^{m}}{p^{\sigma}})
.
We use Abel summation to obtain a bound for
x^{\sigma}\exp(\sum_{p\leq z}\frac{1}{p^{\sigma}})
; the original expression is
x^{\sigma}\exp(\sum_{p\leq z}\frac{1}{p^{\sigma}})^{O_{m}(1)}
.

Let
a_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
be
1
for prime
n
and
0
else, and define
f(x)=\frac{1}{x^{a}}
, for
a>0
. Then
f^{\prime}(x)=-ax^{-1-a}
. Thus

\smash{\sum_{p\leq u}\frac{1}{p^{a}}=\sum_{n\leq u}a_{n}f(n)=\pi(u)\cdot\frac{1}{u^{a}}+a\int_{1}^{u}\pi(t)t^{-1-a}\mathrm{d}t.}

(5.4)

Substitute
u=z
into the above. By the Prime Number Theorem,
\pi(z)=\frac{z}{\log z}+O\bigl(\frac{z}{(\log z)^{2}}\bigr)
, so
\pi(z)\cdot\frac{1}{z^{a}}=\frac{z^{1-a}}{\log z}+O\bigl(\frac{z^{1-a}}{(\log z)^{2}}\bigr)
. We can also apply this estimate to the integrand above to get

a\int_{1}^{z}\pi(t)t^{-1-a}\mathrm{d}t=a\int_{1}^{z}\frac{t^{-a}}{\log t}+O\Bigl(\frac{t^{-a}}{(\log t)^{2}}\Bigr)\mathrm{d}t=a\frac{z^{1-a}}{(1-a)\log z}+O\Bigl(\frac{z^{1-a}}{(\log z)^{2}}\Bigr).

Hence

\!\!\!\smash{\sum_{p\leq z}\frac{1}{p^{a}}=\frac{z^{1-a}}{\log z}+a\frac{z^{1-a}}{(1-a)\log z}+O\Bigl(\frac{z^{1-a}}{(\log z)^{2}}\Bigr)=\frac{z^{1-a}}{(1-a)\log z}+O\Bigl(\frac{z^{1-a}}{(\log z)^{2}}\Bigr).}

(5.5)

Substitute
\sigma
to get
\sum_{p\leq z}\frac{1}{p^{\sigma}}\ll\frac{z^{1-\sigma}}{(1-\sigma)\log z}
.
Now,
\sigma=1-\frac{\log(L\log L)}{\log z}
so
1-\sigma=\frac{\log(L\log L)}{\log z}
. Hence
\frac{z^{1-\sigma}}{(1-\sigma)\log z}=\frac{z^{1-\sigma}}{\log(L\log L)}
. But
z^{1-\sigma}=z^{\frac{\log(L\log L)}{\log z}}
and
z^{\frac{1}{\log z}}=e
so
z^{1-\sigma}=\exp(\log(L\log L))=L\log L
. Thus
\frac{z^{1-\sigma}}{(1-\sigma)\log z}=L\frac{\log L}{\log(L\log L)}\ll L
.

Finally, note that
x^{\sigma}=\dfrac{x}{x^{\frac{\log(L\log L)}{\log z}}}
and
\log z=\frac{1}{L}\log x
so
x^{\frac{1}{\log z}}=\exp(L)
. Hence

x^{\sigma}=\frac{x}{\exp(L\log(L\log L))}=\frac{x}{(L\log L)^{L}}.

We conclude:

\smash{x^{\sigma}\prod_{p\leq z}\left(1+\frac{r_{0}^{*}(p)^{m}}{p^{\sigma}}+\dots\right)\ll\frac{x}{(L\log L)^{L}}\exp\Bigl(\sum_{p\leq z}\frac{2^{m}}{p^{\sigma}}\Bigr)\ll\frac{x\exp(L)^{O_{m}(1)}}{(L\log L)^{L}}\ll_{m}\frac{x}{L^{L}}.}

This is indeed
O(x(\log x)^{-1000})
.

Case 2:

Now, suppose
P(n)>z
and
P(n)^{2}\mid n
. In particular,
P(n)\leq\sqrt{x}
. Considering possible values of
P(n)=:l
, we can write

\smash[t]{\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
P(n)>z,P(n)^{2}\mid n\end{subarray}}}r_{0}^{*}(n)^{m}=\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}l\leq\sqrt{x}\\
l>z\\
l\text{ prime}\end{subarray}}}\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
P(n)=l\\
l^{2}\mid n\end{subarray}}}r_{0}^{*}(n)^{m}=\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}l\leq\sqrt{x}\\
l>z\\
l\text{ prime}\end{subarray}}}\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}P(k)<l\\
l^{2}k\leq x\end{subarray}}}r_{0}^{*}(l^{2}k)^{m}.}

(5.6)

The last equality holds by definition of
P(n)
. Also,
l
is prime and
k
is a product of primes strictly smaller than
l
, so
(k,l)=1
. As
r_{0}^{*}
is multiplicative, (
5.6
) gives

\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
P(n)>z,P(n)^{2}\mid n\end{subarray}}}r_{0}^{*}(n)^{m}=\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}l\leq\sqrt{x}\\
l>z\\
l\text{ prime}\end{subarray}}}r_{0}^{*}(l^{2})^{m}\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}P(k)<l\\
k\leq x/l^{2}\end{subarray}}}r_{0}^{*}(k)^{m}\ll\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}z<l\leq\sqrt{x}\\
l\text{ prime}\end{subarray}}}\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}P(k)<l\\
k\leq x/l^{2}\end{subarray}}}r_{0}^{*}(k)^{m}\Bigl(\frac{x}{l^{2}k}\Bigr)^{\sigma_{l}}.

The last relation is because
r_{0}^{*}(l^{2})\leq 2
, so we can ignore the coefficients
r_{0}^{*}(l^{2})^{m}
as they are bounded by the constant
2^{m}
;
\sigma_{l}
are some positive numbers, and
n=l^{2}k\leq x
.

Similarly to case
1
, for any given
l
, the corresponding summand in the final expression above can be written as

\smash[t]{\Bigl(\frac{x}{l^{2}}\Bigr)^{\sigma_{l}}\prod_{p<l}\Bigl(1+\frac{r_{0}^{*}(p)^{m}}{p^{\sigma_{l}}}+\dots\Bigr)\leq\Bigl(\frac{x}{l^{2}}\Bigr)^{\sigma_{l}}\exp\Bigl(\sum_{p<l}\frac{2^{m}}{p^{\sigma_{l}}}\Bigr).}

We use (
5.5
), replacing
z
with
l
, to obtain
\displaystyle\sum_{p<l}\frac{2^{m}}{p^{\sigma_{l}}}\ll 2^{m}\dfrac{l^{1-\sigma_{l}}}{(1-\sigma_{l})\log l}\ll_{m}\dfrac{l^{1-\sigma_{l}}}{(1-\sigma_{l})\log l}
.

Let
\tau
be some positive constant, and set
\sigma_{l}=1-\frac{\log\tau}{\log l}
, so
1-\sigma_{l}=\frac{\log\tau}{\log l}
; thus we can rewrite the above as

\smash{\sum_{p<l}\frac{2^{m}}{p^{\sigma_{l}}}\ll_{m}\dfrac{l^{1-\sigma_{l}}}{\log\tau}=\dfrac{l^{\log\tau/\log l}}{\log\tau}=\dfrac{\tau}{\log\tau},}\text{ which is constant.}

So
\exp\Bigl(\sum_{p\leq l}\frac{2^{m}}{p^{\sigma_{l}}}\Bigr)\ll_{m}1
.

For the total sum we get

\displaystyle\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
P(n)>z,P(n)^{2}\mid n\end{subarray}}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!r_{0}^{*}(n)^{m}

\displaystyle\ll\smash[t]{\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}z<l\leq\sqrt{x}\\
l\text{ prime}\end{subarray}}}\Bigl(\frac{x}{l^{2}}\Bigr)^{\sigma_{l}}=\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}z<l\leq\sqrt{x}\\
l\text{ prime}\end{subarray}}}\frac{x}{l^{2}}\left(\frac{l^{2}}{x}\right)^{\frac{\log\tau}{\log l}}=\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}z<l\leq\sqrt{x}\\
l\text{ prime}\end{subarray}}}\frac{\tau^{2}}{l^{2}}\cdot x^{1-\frac{\log\tau}{\log l}}}

\displaystyle\ll\smash[b]{\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}z<l\leq\sqrt{x}\\
l\text{ prime}\end{subarray}}}\frac{x^{1-\frac{\log\tau}{\log l}}}{l^{2}}.}

Thus, to prove the stated bound we need
\displaystyle\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}z<l\leq\sqrt{x}\\
l\text{ prime}\end{subarray}}}\frac{x^{-\frac{\log\tau}{\log l}}}{l^{2}}\ll\frac{1}{(\log x)^{1000}}
. We again use the method of partial summation to do this: as previously, let
a_{l}\in\mathbb{C}
be
1
for prime
l
and
0
else.
We now set
f(l)=\dfrac{x^{1-\frac{\log\tau}{\log l}}}{l^{2}}
. Then, since
l\geq z=x^{1/L}\Rightarrow\frac{\log x}{(\log l)^{2}}=O(1)
, we have

\smash[t]{f^{\prime}(l)=\dfrac{x^{-\frac{\log\tau}{\log l}}}{l^{3}}\Bigl(-2+\frac{(\log\tau)(\log x)}{(\log l)^{2}}\Bigr)\ll\dfrac{x^{1-\frac{\log\tau}{\log l}}}{l^{3}}.}

Thus

\smash{\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}z<l\leq\sqrt{x}\\
l\text{ prime}\end{subarray}}}\frac{x^{-\frac{\log\tau}{\log l}}}{l^{2}}=\pi(\sqrt{x})\cdot\frac{x^{-\frac{\log\tau}{\log\sqrt{x}}}}{(\sqrt{x})^{2}}-\pi(z)\cdot\frac{x^{-\frac{\log\tau}{\log z}}}{z^{2}}-\int_{z}^{\sqrt{x}}\pi(t)f^{\prime}(t)dt.}

We calculate that

\displaystyle\int_{z}^{\sqrt{x}}\pi(t)f^{\prime}(t)dt

\displaystyle\ll\int_{z}^{\sqrt{x}}\pi(\sqrt{x})x^{-\frac{\log\tau}{\log t}}\cdot\frac{dt}{t^{3}}\ll\pi(\sqrt{x})x^{-\frac{\log\tau}{\log x}}\int_{z}^{\sqrt{x}}\frac{dt}{t^{3}}

\displaystyle\ll\frac{x^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\log\tau}{\log x}}}{\log x}\cdot\frac{1}{x}\ll\frac{1}{(\log x)^{1000}}.

Now, note that
\pi(\sqrt{x})\cdot\frac{x^{-\frac{\log\tau}{\log\sqrt{x}}}}{(\sqrt{x})^{2}}\ll\frac{\sqrt{x}}{\log x}\cdot x^{-1-\frac{2\log\tau}{\log x}}\ll\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}\log x}\ll\frac{1}{(\log x)^{1000}}
. Also,
\pi(z)\cdot\frac{x^{-\frac{\log\tau}{\log z}}}{z^{2}}\ll\frac{1}{z(\log z)^{2}}x^{-\frac{L\log\tau}{\log x}}\ll\frac{L^{2}}{x^{1/L}(\log x)^{2}}\ll\frac{1}{(\log x)^{1000}}
. So we are done.
∎

5.2
The function
r_{1}^{*}

We also briefly touch on
r_{1}^{*}
, providing another result from Granville, Sabuncu and Sedunova’s paper
[
7
]
that will be useful later on. The lemma below shows that the majority of solutions to
n=a^{2}+p^{2}
, where
p
is prime, satisfy
p\nmid a
. The proof follows the method in
[
7
]
.

Lemma 5.2.1
.

The number of pairs
(a,p)
such that
a^{2}+p^{2}\leq x
,
p\mid a
is

\smash{\sum_{n\leq x}(r_{1}(n)-r_{1}^{*}(n))=O(\sqrt{x}\log\log x).}

Proof.

Since
p\mid a
, we can write
a=pm
, for some integer
m
; moreover,
a^{2}+p^{2}=p^{2}(m^{2}+1)\leq x
so
m^{2}+1\leq\frac{x}{p^{2}}\Rightarrow m\leq\frac{\sqrt{x}}{p}
.
Also, for any possible value of
m
, the possible choices of
p
are restricted to primes up to
\sqrt{x}
as
p^{2}\leq\frac{x}{m^{2}+1}\leq x\Rightarrow p\leq\sqrt{x}
. Thus the total number of pairs
(a,p)
satisfying the conditions is
\displaystyle\sum_{p\leq\sqrt{x}}\#\{m:p^{2}(m^{2}+1)\leq x\}\leq\sum_{p\leq\sqrt{x}}\frac{\sqrt{x}}{p}=\sqrt{x}\sum_{p\leq\sqrt{x}}\frac{1}{p}
.

We use partial summation to estimate the sum, and apply (
5.4
) with
a=1
to get

\displaystyle\smash{\sum_{p\leq\sqrt{x}}\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle=\pi(\sqrt{x})\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}}+\smash[t]{\int_{1}^{\sqrt{x}}\pi(t)t^{-2}\mathrm{d}t=\frac{2}{\log x}+\int_{1}^{\sqrt{x}}\frac{1}{t\log t}\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=O(1)+\bigl[\log\log t\bigr]_{1}^{\sqrt{x}}=O(\log\log x).

Hence
\sum_{n\leq x}(r_{1}(n)-r_{1}^{*}(n))=\sqrt{x}\cdot O(\log\log x)=O(\sqrt{x}\log\log x)
, as required.
∎

6.1
Sums of reciprocals

In order to facilitate our proof of various results in Chapter
6
, we first provide the following well-known results, which will be used for bounding estimates in the proof of some subsequent Lemmata and Theorem
6.3.1
.

Lemma 6.1.1

(Mertens’ Second Theorem)
.

There exists
M\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\forall x\geq 2
:

\smash{\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{1}{p}=\log\log x+M+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{\log x}\Bigr).}

Lemma 6.1.2
.

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq x}\frac{1}{n}=\log x+\gamma+O(\tfrac{1}{x})
, where
\gamma
is the Euler-Mascheroni constant.

We will also require a theorem about sums of reciprocals of primes in arithmetic progressions, a stronger result than Lemma
6.1.1
, proved by Mertens in his paper “Ein Beitrag zur analytischen Zahlentheorie”. I provided a partial proof of the theorem in the special case that it is applied in, although the general case is true.

Theorem 6.1.3

(Mertens’ Theorem for Primes in Arithmetic Progressions)
.

Let
q\in\mathbb{N}
, and
a
an integer coprime to
q
.
There exists
M(q,a)\in\mathbb{R}
a constant dependent only on
q
and
a
such that
\forall x\geq 2
:

\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq x\\
p\equiv a\pmod{q}\end{subarray}}}\frac{1}{p}=\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\cdot\log\log x+M(q,a)+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{\log x}\Bigr).

Proof.

We only give a proof for
q=4
, although the general case is true.

Step 1:
We show that the series
\sum\frac{\chi(p)}{p}
converges, where
\chi
is the non-principal Dirichlet character modulo
4
– there is only one, with
\chi(2j)=0,\chi(4j\pm 1)=\pm 1
. Note that
\displaystyle L(1,\chi)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\chi(n)}{n}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{2j+1}=1-\frac{1}{3}+\frac{1}{5}-\frac{1}{7}+\dots
converges conditionally, by the alternating series convergence test. The sum is nonzero and positive, by considering pairs of consecutive nonzero terms.

We now consider the truncated sums of the series
\displaystyle L^{\prime}(1,\chi)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\chi(n)\log n}{n}=0-\frac{\log 3}{3}+\frac{\log 5}{5}-\frac{\log 7}{7}+\dots
, which is also conditionally convergent by the alternating series convergence test. Recall by Lemma
2.2.1
that
\log n=\sum_{k\mid n}\Lambda(k)
, so

\smash{\frac{\chi(n)\log n}{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k\mid n}\chi(n)\Lambda(k)=\sum_{k\mid n}\frac{\chi(n/k)\chi(k)\Lambda(k)}{n}.}

Here we used the total multiplicativity of
\chi
. Hence, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\chi(n)\log n}{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}k\mid n\\
km=n\end{subarray}}}\frac{\chi(m)\chi(k)\Lambda(k)}{n}=\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}k,m\\
km\leq N\end{subarray}}}\frac{\chi(m)\chi(k)\Lambda(k)}{km}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\leq N}\frac{\chi(k)\Lambda(k)}{k}\sum_{m\leq N/k}\frac{\chi(m)}{m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\leq N}\frac{\chi(k)\Lambda(k)}{k}\Bigl(L(1,\chi)+O\bigl(\tfrac{k}{N}\bigr)\Bigr).

(6.1)

The last equality comes from evaluating the tail of
L(1,\chi)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{\chi(n)}{n}
. Note that
\displaystyle\sum_{k\leq N}\frac{\chi(k)\Lambda(k)}{k}O\bigl(\tfrac{k}{N}\bigr)=O\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{N}\sum_{k\leq N}|\chi(k)\Lambda(k)|\Bigr)=O\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{N}\sum_{k\leq N}\Lambda(k)\Bigr)=O\bigl(\tfrac{\psi(N)}{N}\bigr)=O(1)
, by definition of the Chebyshev function
\psi
and its asymptotic behaviour,
\psi(x)\asymp x
(this is known as Chebyshev’s estimate). We let
C>0
be a constant so that
\displaystyle-C\leq\sum_{k\leq N}\frac{\chi(k)\Lambda(k)}{k}O\bigl(\tfrac{k}{N}\bigr)\leq C
for all
N\geq N_{0}
, some
N_{0}
. Substituting into (
6.1
) gives

\forall N\geq N_{0}\colon\smash[t]{\sum_{k\leq N}\frac{\chi(k)\Lambda(k)}{k}\cdot L(1,\chi)-C\leq\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\chi(n)\log n}{n}\leq\sum_{k\leq N}\frac{\chi(k)\Lambda(k)}{k}\cdot L(1,\chi)+C.}

Now,
L^{\prime}(1,\chi)
is a finite constant, so all the partial sums
\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\chi(n)\log n}{n}
are uniformly bounded. We thus get
\displaystyle\forall N\geq N_{1}\colon-C^{\prime}\leq\sum_{k\leq N}\frac{\chi(k)\Lambda(k)}{k}\cdot L(1,\chi)\leq C^{\prime}
, for
N_{1}
and
C^{\prime}
appropriately chosen integer and positive real constants.
L(1,\chi)\neq 0
is a constant so for
N\geq N_{1}
and
C^{\prime\prime}=\frac{C^{\prime}}{L(1,\chi)}
we have

\displaystyle-C^{\prime\prime}\leq\sum_{k\leq N}\frac{\chi(k)\Lambda(k)}{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p\leq N}\frac{\chi(p)\Lambda(p)}{p}+\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}q\leq N\\
q=p^{e},e\geq 2\end{subarray}}}\frac{\chi(q)\Lambda(q)}{q}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p\leq N}\frac{\chi(p)\log p}{p}+\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}p^{e}\leq N\\
e\geq 2\end{subarray}}}\frac{\chi(p^{e})\log p}{p^{e}}\leq C^{\prime\prime}.

Since
\displaystyle\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}p^{e}\leq N\\
e\geq 2\end{subarray}}}\left|\frac{\chi(p^{e})\log p}{p^{e}}\right|\leq\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}p^{e}\\
e\geq 2\end{subarray}}}\frac{\log p}{p^{e}}=\sum_{p\text{ prime}}\frac{\log p}{p(p-1)}:=D
, where
D
is a finite positive constant, we get from the above that
-D^{\prime}\leq\sum_{p\leq N}\frac{\chi(p)\log p}{p}\leq D^{\prime}
for all
N\geq N_{1}
, some finite
D^{\prime}>0
.
We now apply the Dirichlet convergence test: Let
a_{n}
be the sequence
\frac{1}{\log n}
of real numbers converging to
0
monotonically, and

b_{n}:=\begin{cases}\frac{\chi(p)\log p}{p},n=p\text{ is prime}\\
0\text{ else}\end{cases},

so that
|\sum_{n=1}^{N}b_{n}|\leq D^{\prime}
for all
N\geq N_{1}
. Then the series
\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 1}a_{n}b_{n}=\sum_{p\text{ prime}}\!\frac{\chi(p)}{p}
converges.

Step 2:
We now deduce the result. Since
\sum_{p\text{ prime}}\frac{\chi(p)}{p}
converges, in particular we have
\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{\chi(p)}{p}=O(1)
for all
x
. Thus

\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq x\\
p\equiv 1\!\!\!\pmod{4}\end{subarray}}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\frac{1}{p}-\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq x\\
p\equiv 3\!\!\!\pmod{4}\end{subarray}}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\frac{1}{p}=O(1)\implies\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq x\\
p\equiv 1\!\!\!\pmod{4}\end{subarray}}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\frac{1}{p}=\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq x\\
p\equiv 3\!\!\!\pmod{4}\end{subarray}}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\frac{1}{p}+O(1).

(6.2)

Mertens’ Second Theorem, Lemma
6.1.1
, gives
\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{1}{p}=\log\log x+M+O(\frac{1}{\log x})
. Substituting (
6.2
) into this, we get
\displaystyle\!\!\!\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq x\\
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}\end{subarray}}}\frac{1}{p}=\frac{1}{2}\log\log x+M/2+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{\log x}\Bigr)+O(1)
, and the same for primes that are
3
mod
4
. Let
M(q,a)=M/2+O(1)
to get the required result.
∎

Remark 6.1
.

A similar proof with extra steps works for other
q
. It is true in general that for non-principal Dirichlet characters
\chi
, the Dirichlet L-function
L(s,\chi):=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\chi(n)}{n^{s}}
and
L^{\prime}(s,\chi)=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}L(s,\chi)
converge for
\Re(s)>0
, in particular
s=1
, and
L(1,\chi)\neq 0
. We consider linear combinations of the non-principal Dirichlet characters to get the required result.

6.2
Integers with a fixed number of prime factors

We include this section here as it will be needed in the proof of Theorem
6.3.1
.

We would like to obtain an estimate on
\rho_{k}(x):=\#\{n\leq x\colon\omega^{*}(n)=k\}
. Here,
\omega^{*}(n)
is the number of odd prime factors of
n
. We provide some necessary notation below.

Notation
.

•

\mathcal{N}:=\{n\in\mathbb{N}\colon 4\nmid n,\text{ and }p\nmid n\text{ for all primes }p\equiv 3\pmod{4}\}
.

•

\mathcal{N}_{k}(x):=\{n\leq x\colon n\in\mathcal{N},\omega^{*}(n)=k\}

•

\rho_{k,\mathcal{N}}(x):=\#\{n\leq x\colon n\in\mathcal{N},\omega^{*}(n)=k\}

We first prove a preliminary result about bounding a sum which will be very useful to us in Lemma
6.2.2
.

Claim 6.2.1
.

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}q=p^{e},\text{ a prime power}\\
q\leq\sqrt{x}\\
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}\end{subarray}}}\frac{x}{q\log(x/q)}\leq\frac{x}{\log x}(\tfrac{1}{2}\log\log(x)+\gamma_{2})
, some constant
\gamma_{2}
.

Proof.

We prove that
\displaystyle\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}q=p^{e},\text{ a prime power}\\
q\leq\sqrt{x}\\
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}\end{subarray}}}\frac{\log x}{q\log(x/q)}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\log\log(x)+\gamma_{2}
. (Multiply by
\frac{x}{\log x}
to get the required bound for the claim.)

Note that the contribution of prime powers
q=p^{e}
where
e\geq 2
is small. Indeed,
\displaystyle\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\sum_{p<x}\frac{1}{p^{n}}=\sum_{p<x}\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{1}{p^{n}}=\sum_{p<x}\frac{1}{p^{2}}\frac{1}{1-\frac{1}{p}}=\sum_{p<x}\frac{1}{p(p-1)}<1
. Since
q\leq\sqrt{x}
, we have
\log x\leq 2\log(x/q)
, so

\smash[t]{\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}q=p^{e},\text{ a prime power}\\
q\leq\sqrt{x},\ e\geq 2\\
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}\end{subarray}}}\frac{\log x}{q\log(x/q)}\leq\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}q=p^{e},\text{ a prime power}\\
q\leq\sqrt{x}\\
e\geq 2\end{subarray}}}\frac{\log x}{q\log(x/q)}\leq 2\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}q=p^{e},\text{ a prime power}\\
q\leq\sqrt{x}\\
e\geq 2\end{subarray}}}\frac{1}{q}<2.}

Thus,

\smash[t]{\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}q=p^{e},\ q\leq\sqrt{x}\\
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}\end{subarray}}}\frac{\log x}{q\log(x/q)}\leq 2+\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}\\
p\leq\sqrt{x}\\
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}\end{subarray}}}\frac{\log x}{p\log(x/p)}.}

Now, for
p\leq x^{1/n}
we have
\log(x/p)\geq\log(x^{1-1/n})=\frac{n-1}{n}\log x
, so
\dfrac{\log x}{\log(x/p)}\leq\dfrac{n}{n-1}
. We thus obtain the bound

\displaystyle\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq x^{1/n}\\
x^{1/(n+1)}\leq p\\
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}\end{subarray}}}\!\!\!\!\frac{\log x}{p\log(x/p)}\leq\frac{n}{n-1}\!\!\!\!\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq x^{1/n}\\
x^{1/(n+1)}\leq p\\
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}\end{subarray}}}\!\!\!\!\frac{1}{p}

(6.3)

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{n-1}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\log\log(x^{\frac{1}{n}})-\log\log(x^{\frac{1}{n+1}})+O(\tfrac{1}{\log x})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{n-1}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(\log\bigl(\tfrac{1}{n}\log x\bigr)-\log\bigl(\tfrac{1}{n+1}\log x\bigr)+O(\tfrac{1}{\log x})\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{n-1}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\log\Bigl(\frac{\tfrac{1}{n}\log x}{\tfrac{1}{n+1}\log x}\Bigr)+O(\tfrac{1}{\log x})=\frac{n}{n-1}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\log\Bigl(\frac{n+1}{n}\Bigr)+O(\tfrac{1}{\log x}).

(6.4)

Here we used Theorem
6.1.3
to sum
\frac{1}{p}
for primes
p
congruent to
1
modulo
4
in the relevant interval. In this case we have
q=4
, so
\phi(q)=2
, and
a=1
.
Now

\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq\sqrt{x}\\
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}\end{subarray}}}\frac{\log x}{p\log(x/p)}=O(\tfrac{1}{\log x})+\sum_{n=2}^{\lfloor\log x\rfloor}\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}x^{1/(n+1)}<p\\
p\leq x^{1/n}\\
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}\end{subarray}}}\frac{\log x}{p\log(x/p)},

(6.5)

as we split the range between
e=x^{1/\log x}
and
\sqrt{x}=x^{1/2}
into intervals
[x^{1/(n+1)},x^{1/n}]
and
\frac{\log x}{2\log(x/2)}=O(\tfrac{1}{\log x})
. (2 is the only prime which falls outside of our intervals.)

We substitute (
6.4
) into (
6.5
) to get

\displaystyle\smash{\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq\sqrt{x}\\
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}\end{subarray}}}\frac{\log x}{p\log(x/p)}}

\displaystyle\leq O(\tfrac{1}{\log x})+\sum_{n=2}^{\lfloor\log x\rfloor}\frac{n}{2(n-1)}\log\Bigl(\frac{n+1}{n}\Bigr)+O(1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=2}^{\lfloor\log x\rfloor}\frac{n}{2(n-1)}\log\Bigl(1+\frac{1}{n}\Bigr)+O(1)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=2}^{\lfloor\log x\rfloor}\frac{n}{2(n-1)}\cdot\frac{1}{n}+O(1)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor\log x\rfloor-1}\frac{1}{j}+O(1)

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\log\log x+\tfrac{1}{2}\gamma+O(1).

In the last line we applied Lemma
6.1.2
. The
O(1)
term represents a constant, so
\displaystyle\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}q=p^{e},\text{ a prime power}\\
q\leq\sqrt{x},\ p\equiv 1\pmod{4}\end{subarray}}}\frac{\log x}{q\log(x/q)}\leq 2+\tfrac{1}{2}\log\log x+\tfrac{1}{2}\gamma+c
, for some constant
c
.
Setting
\gamma_{2}\geq 2+c+\frac{\gamma}{2}
gives
\displaystyle\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}q=p^{e},\text{ a prime power}\\
q\leq\sqrt{x},\ p\equiv 1\pmod{4}\end{subarray}}}\frac{x}{q\log(x/q)}\leq\frac{x}{\log x}(\tfrac{1}{2}\log\log(x)+\gamma_{2})
.
∎

Now we obtain an upper bound on the function
\rho_{k,\mathcal{N}}(x)
.

Lemma 6.2.2
.

\displaystyle\exists\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\colon\rho_{k,\mathcal{N}}(x)\leq\gamma_{1}\frac{x}{\log x}\frac{(\frac{1}{2}L+\gamma_{2})^{k-1}}{(k-1)!},\ \forall x\geq 1.

Proof.

(See
[
6
, §13.4]
)
We will prove the following statement by induction on
k
: “There exist
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}>0
such that for all
x\geq 1
and
k\in\mathbb{N}\colon\rho_{k,\mathcal{N}}(x)\leq\gamma_{1}\frac{x}{\log x}\frac{(\frac{1}{2}L+\gamma_{2})^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}
.”

Base case:

k=1
. Now,
\rho_{1,\mathcal{N}}(x)
is the number of prime powers that are at most
x
. Note that for a fixed integer
j
, the number of primes
p
such that
p^{j}\leq x
is
\pi(x^{1/j})
, where
\pi
is the prime counting function. Hence

\rho_{1,\mathcal{N}}(x)=\sum_{j\geq 1}\pi(x^{\frac{1}{j}})=\sum_{j\geq 1}^{\frac{\log x}{\log 2}}\pi(x^{\frac{1}{j}})\leq\sum_{j=1}^{\frac{\log x}{\log 2}}\frac{x^{\frac{1}{j}}}{\frac{1}{j}\log x}\leq\frac{x}{\log x}+\sum_{j=2}^{\frac{\log x}{\log 2}}\frac{x^{\frac{1}{2}}}{\frac{1}{2}\log x}.

The last inequality holds as
\pi
is an increasing function.
Now, the number of
2\leq j\leq\frac{\log x}{\log 2}
is bounded by
\log x
, so
\rho_{1,\mathcal{N}}(x)\leq\frac{x}{\log x}+\log x\cdot\frac{x^{\frac{1}{2}}}{\frac{1}{2}\log x}=\frac{x}{\log x}+2\sqrt{x}\leq 2\frac{x}{\log x}
, for
x>c
(where
c
is a constant independent of
x
). For
1\leq x\leq c
, the function
f(x)=1+2\frac{\sqrt{x}\log x}{x}
is continuous and hence bounded, so
\exists\gamma\colon\frac{x}{\log x}+2\sqrt{x}\leq\gamma\frac{x}{\log x}
, for all
x\geq 1
.

Thus the base case is proved; any
\gamma_{1}\geq\gamma
works, so some
\gamma_{1}
exists. We do not obtain any restriction on
\gamma_{2}
, so we pick a
\gamma_{2}
satisfying the conditions of Claim
6.2.1
.

Inductive step:
Suppose that the statement holds for
k-1
; we now prove for
k
. Note that
\rho_{k,\mathcal{N}}(x)
counts the number of integers
n
with prime factorisation
n=2^{e_{0}}p_{1}^{e_{1}}\dots p_{k}^{e_{k}}
, where
p_{i}
are primes that are
1\pmod{4}
, and
e_{0}\in\{0,1\}
by definition of
\mathcal{N}
. Without loss of generality, we may suppose that
p_{1}^{e_{1}}<\dots<p_{k}^{e_{k}}
. For each odd prime
p_{j}
, we can write
n=p_{j}^{e_{j}}m_{j}
, where
m_{j}\in\mathcal{N}
and
\omega^{*}(m_{j})=k-1
, and
p_{j}^{e_{j}}\mathrel{\|}n
(i.e.
p_{j}^{e_{j}}\mid n
and
p_{j}^{e_{j}+1}\nmid n
). Hence, given a prime power
p_{j}^{e_{j}}
,

\#\{n\leq x\colon n\in\mathcal{N}_{k}(x),p_{j}^{e_{j}}\mathrel{\|}n\}=\rho_{k-1,\mathcal{N}}\Bigl(\dfrac{x}{p_{j}^{e_{j}}}\Bigr).

(6.6)

For
j<k
, we further note that
p_{j}^{e_{j}}<p_{k}^{e_{k}}
and
p_{j}^{e_{j}}p_{k}^{e_{k}}\leq n
, so
(p_{j}^{e_{j}})^{2}<n\implies p_{j}^{e_{j}}<\sqrt{n}
. Since
n\leq x
, for all
j=1,\dots,k-1
we have
p_{j}^{e_{j}}\leq\sqrt{x}
. We will use this to get an upper bound for
\rho_{k,\mathcal{N}}(x)
using (
6.6
). Note that
(k-1)\rho_{k,\mathcal{N}}(x)
can be interpreted as multiple counting every
n\in\mathcal{N}_{k}(x)
from the point of view of each of its odd prime power factors except the largest one (corresponding to
p_{k}^{e_{k}}
above); each of those odd prime powers is at most
\sqrt{x}
, as shown. Thus

(k-1)\rho_{k,\mathcal{N}}(x)\leq\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}q=p^{e},\text{ a prime power}\\
q\leq\sqrt{x}\\
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}\end{subarray}}}\rho_{k-1,\mathcal{N}}\Bigl(\dfrac{x}{q}\Bigr).

We now apply the inductive hypothesis to each term in the summation to get

\displaystyle(k-1)\rho_{k,\mathcal{N}}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}q=p^{e},\text{ a prime power}\\
q\leq\sqrt{x}\\
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}\end{subarray}}}\gamma_{1}\frac{x/q}{\log(x/q)}\frac{(\frac{1}{2}\log\log(x/q)+\gamma_{2})^{k-2}}{(k-2)!}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}q=p^{e},\text{ a prime power}\\
q\leq\sqrt{x}\\
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}\end{subarray}}}\gamma_{1}\frac{x}{q\log(x/q)}\ \cdot\ \frac{(\frac{1}{2}\log\log(x)+\gamma_{2})^{k-2}}{(k-2)!}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma_{1}\frac{(\frac{1}{2}\log\log(x)+\gamma_{2})^{k-1}}{(k-2)!},\text{ by Claim~\ref{claim: bound for inductive step}}

\displaystyle\implies\rho_{k,\mathcal{N}}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{k-1}\cdot\gamma_{1}\frac{(\frac{1}{2}\log\log(x)+\gamma_{2})^{k-1}}{(k-2)!}=\gamma_{1}\frac{(\frac{1}{2}L+\gamma_{2})^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}.

This completes the inductive step, and hence we have finished the proof.
∎

6.3
Upper bounds on moments of

In this section we prove the following theorem:

Claim 6.3.2
.

\smash{\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
\omega^{*}(n)=k\end{subarray}}}\left(\binom{r_{1}(n)}{\ell}-\binom{r_{1}^{*}(n)}{\ell}\right)\ll_{\ell}x^{\frac{1}{2}+o(1)}.}

Proof.

\displaystyle\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
\omega^{*}(n)=k\end{subarray}}}\binom{r_{1}(n)}{\ell}-\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
\omega^{*}(n)=k\end{subarray}}}\binom{r_{1}^{*}(n)}{\ell}=\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\

\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
\omega^{*}(n)=k\end{subarray}}}\left(\binom{r_{1}(n)}{\ell}-\binom{r_{1}^{*}(n)}{\ell}\right)\ll_{\ell}\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
\omega^{*}(n)=k\end{subarray}}}(r_{1}(n)-r_{1}^{*}(n))r_{1}(n)^{\ell-1}.

We will bound

We apply Lemma

to bound the sum of

\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
\omega^{*}(n)=k\end{subarray}}}\left(\binom{r_{1}(n)}{\ell}-\binom{r_{1}^{*}(n)}{\ell}\right)\ll_{\ell}x^{\frac{1}{2}+o(1)}.\qed

Hence, to prove the theorem, we may now replace

in the statement, as we have shown the error accumulated through doing so is small. We next restrict the sum by introducing conditions on

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(x)\ll_{\ell}\frac{x}{m}\prod_{\ell+2<p\leq y}\left(1-\frac{\ell+1+(\frac{-1}{p})}{p}\right)\prod_{p\mid T}\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{p}\Bigr)^{O(\ell)}+E(x)

Definition

(The function

We define

Next we calculate the cardinality of

p\nmid a
(so
p\nmid b
). Note that if
p\mid s
, then
ar\equiv 0\pmod{p}\implies p\mid r
, as
p\nmid a
. But then
\gcd(r,s)>1
, which contradicts the definition of
I
. So we may assume
r,s
are invertible modulo
p
. Thus
ar+bs\equiv 0\pmod{p}\implies b\equiv-rs^{-1}a\pmod{p}
, so

\displaystyle p\mid a^{2}+b^{2}

\displaystyle\iff a^{2}\equiv-b^{2}\equiv-(rs^{-1})^{2}a^{2}\pmod{p}

\displaystyle\iff r^{2}(s^{-1})^{2}\equiv-1\pmod{p}\iff p\mid r^{2}+s^{2}=m\text{ (by definition of $I$).}

So given
p\mid\phi_{0}(a,b)
and
p\nmid a,b
, we have
p\mid a^{2}+b^{2}\iff p\mid m
. Hence, if
p\mid m
then
\bigcup_{\phi\in I}\{(a^{*},b^{*})\in(\mathbb{Z}_{p})^{2}:p\mid\phi(a^{*},b^{*})\}\subseteq\{(a^{*},b^{*})\in(\mathbb{Z}_{p})^{2}:p\mid(a^{*})^{2}+(b^{*})^{2}\}
, which is true whether
p\nmid a,b
or not. If
p\nmid m
, then the intersection of the events
\bigcup_{\phi\in I}\{(a^{*},b^{*})\in(\mathbb{Z}_{p})^{2}:p\mid\phi(a^{*},b^{*})\}
and
\{(a^{*},b^{*})\in(\mathbb{Z}_{p})^{2}:p\mid(a^{*})^{2}+(b^{*})^{2}\}
is
(0,0)
, and so the cardinality of their union is the sum of cardinalities minus 1.

Remark
.

These arguments break down if
p
is very small, in fact when
p\leq\ell
. We will deal with this case separately.

Combining the above arguments, we arrive at

Lemma

(Formula for
p^{2}g(p)
)
.

Let
p>\ell
be prime.

•

If
p\nmid T
(in particular
p\nmid m
) then
p^{2}g(p)=1+(p-1)\Bigl(\ell+1+\bigl(\frac{-1}{p}\bigr)\Bigr)
.

•

If
p\mid T
and
p\nmid m
then
p^{2}g(p)=1+(p-1)\Bigl(\ell_{p}+1+\bigl(\frac{-1}{p}\bigr)\Bigr)
, where
\ell_{p}
is the number of linear forms
\phi\in I
pairwise independent modulo
p
(
\ell_{p}\leq\ell
).

•

If
p\mid m
then
p^{2}g(p)=1+(p-1)\Bigl(1+\bigl(\frac{-1}{p}\bigr)\Bigr)
. Note that in this case,
\bigl(\frac{-1}{p}\bigr)=1
, as
m
is a sum of two coprime squares and has no prime factors congruent to
3
modulo
4
. Define
\ell_{p}:=0
in this case.

At this point we will take a slight detour. The next step after setting up the sieve problem is to apply a result to bound
\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{A},\mathfrak{p},z)
. We use Theorem
4.2.3
derived from the fundamental lemma of sieve theory given in Chapter
4
, which in particular bounds the total error term
E(x)
. In order to compute this estimate, we first bound the error terms
R_{d}
arising in the sieve problem, where
R_{d}=|\mathcal{A}_{d}|-g(d)X
. For example, for
d=1
, since
|\mathcal{A}|
is actually
\Bigl(\lfloor\sqrt{\frac{x}{m}}\rfloor\Bigr)^{2}
(
\mathcal{A}
is indexed by our choice of positive integers
a
and
b
each at most
\sqrt{\frac{x}{m}}
), we get
R_{1}=R=|\mathcal{A}|-X=\Bigl(\lfloor\sqrt{\frac{x}{m}}\rfloor\Bigr)^{2}-\frac{x}{m}=O(\sqrt{\frac{x}{m}})
.

To apply Theorem
4.2.3
, we estimate
\sum_{d\leq y^{2}}3^{\omega(d)}|R_{d}|
, where
\omega(d)
is the number of distinct prime factors of
d
.

Claim 6.3.6
.

\displaystyle\smash{\sum_{d\leq y^{2}}3^{\omega(d)}|R_{d}|=O\Bigl(\frac{x}{m}\frac{L^{O_{\ell}(1)}}{(\log x)^{\ell+1}}\Bigr)}=E(x)
.

Proof.

As noted in Remark
6.2
, we start by accounting for the error arising from
d\nmid\lfloor\sqrt{\frac{x}{m}}\rfloor
. Let
\displaystyle D:=d\left\lfloor\Bigl\lfloor\sqrt{\tfrac{x}{m}}\Bigr\rfloor/d\right\rfloor
be the largest integer multiple of
d
that is at most
\sqrt{\frac{x}{m}}
. If we considered the set
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}:=\bigl\{(a,b):1\leq a,b\leq D\bigr\}
instead of our original
\mathcal{A}
, we would get
|\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{d}|=g(d)D^{2}
, so we can rewrite
R_{d}=|\mathcal{A}_{d}|-|\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{d}|+g(d)D^{2}-g(d)X=|\mathcal{A}_{d}|-|\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{d}|+g(d)(D^{2}-X)
.

Indeed,
0\leq|\mathcal{A}_{d}|-|\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{d}|=|\mathcal{A}_{d}\setminus\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{d}|\leq|\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{A}^{\prime}|=X-D^{2}
, and
0\leq g(d)\leq 1
, so
R_{d}=\epsilon(X-D^{2})
for some
\epsilon\in[-1,1]
. Now,
0\leq\sqrt{\frac{x}{m}}-D\leq d
so
0\leq X-D^{2}\leq 2d\sqrt{\frac{x}{m}}
. Hence
|R_{d}|\leq 2d\sqrt{\frac{x}{m}}
.

Now we estimate
\sum_{d\leq y^{2}}3^{\omega(d)}|R_{d}|
. In fact,
\omega(d)\leq\log_{3}(d)+1
, as each prime factor of
d
apart from
2
is at least
3
. Using our estimate of
|R_{d}|
above gives a total error of
\sum_{d\leq y^{2}}3d\cdot 2d\sqrt{\frac{x}{m}}=6\sqrt{\frac{x}{m}}\sum_{d\leq y^{2}}d^{2}\ll\sqrt{\frac{x}{m}}(y^{2})^{3}=\sqrt{\frac{x}{m}}x^{6/10L}\ll\frac{x}{m}\frac{L^{O_{\ell}(1)}}{(\log x)^{\ell+1}}
. This holds as
L
is eventually large, and so
x^{6/10L}
is of smaller order than
\sqrt{x}\frac{L^{O_{\ell}(1)}}{(\log x)^{\ell+1}}
.
∎

We now continue the proof of Claim
6.3.5
.
Theorem
4.2.3
gives

\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}a^{2}+b^{2}\leq x/m\\
\gcd(a^{2}+b^{2},\prod_{q\leq y}q)=1\\
\prod_{\phi\in I}\gcd(\phi(a,b),\prod_{q\leq y}q)=1\end{subarray}}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!1\ \leq\ XG(y,y)+O\Bigl(\frac{x}{m}\frac{L^{O_{\ell}(1)}}{(\log x)^{\ell+1}}\Bigr),

where
G(z,z)=\frac{1}{e^{\gamma\kappa}\Gamma(\kappa+1)}\prod_{p<z}(1-g(p))^{-1}(1+O(\tfrac{1}{\log z}))
. Since
\kappa
is the sifting dimension and is at most
\ell+2
, we have
\frac{1}{e^{\gamma\kappa}\Gamma(\kappa+1)}=O_{\ell}(1)
. Now,
\displaystyle\prod_{p<y}(1-g(p))^{-1}=O_{\ell}(1)\!\!\!\!\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell+2<p<y\\
p\,\nmid\,T\end{subarray}}\left(1-\frac{1+(p-1)(\ell+1+(\frac{-1}{p}))}{p^{2}}\right)^{-1}\!\!\!\!\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell+2<p<y\\
p\,\mid\,T\end{subarray}}\left(1-\frac{1+(p-1)(\ell_{p}+1+(\frac{-1}{p}))}{p^{2}}\right)^{-1}
, where
\prod_{p\leq\ell+2}(1-g(p))^{-1}=O_{\ell}(1)
as well.

Note that
\forall p\mid T\colon\ell-\ell_{p}\ll\ell
. We have
\displaystyle 1-\frac{c}{p}\asymp\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)^{c}
for constants
c<p
, so
\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{1+(p-1)(\ell+1+(\frac{-1}{p}))}{p^{2}}\right)\left(1-\frac{1+(p-1)(\ell_{p}+1+(\frac{-1}{p}))}{p^{2}}\right)^{-1}\ll\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)^{O(\ell)}
.

Hence
\displaystyle\prod_{p<y}(1-g(p))^{-1}\ll_{\ell}\prod_{\ell+2<p<y}\left(1-\frac{1+(p-1)(\ell+1+(\frac{-1}{p}))}{p^{2}}\right)^{-1}\prod_{p\mid T}\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{p}\Bigr)^{O(\ell)}
.

Finally, we have
\Bigl(1-\frac{1+(p-1)(\ell+1+(\frac{-1}{p}))}{p^{2}}\Bigr)-\Bigl(1-\frac{\ell+1+(\frac{-1}{p})}{p}\Bigr)=\frac{\ell+(\frac{-1}{p})}{p^{2}}
; also,
\prod_{p}(1-\frac{1}{p^{s}})=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{1}{n^{s}}=O(1)
for all
s>1
so
\prod_{p}(1-\frac{\ell+(\frac{-1}{p})}{p^{2}})\ll O_{\ell}(1)\prod_{p\geq(\ell+2)^{2}}(1-\frac{1}{p^{3/2}})\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{1}{n^{3/2}}=O_{\ell}(1)\ll_{\ell}1
. We now use
1-\frac{c}{p^{2}}\asymp\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{2}}\right)^{c}
to finally get

\smash[b]{\prod_{p<y}(1-g(p))^{-1}\ll_{\ell}\prod_{\ell+2<p<y}\left(1-\frac{\ell+1+(\frac{-1}{p})}{p}\right)^{-1}\prod_{p\mid T}\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{p}\Bigr)^{O(\ell)}},

and substituting
X=\dfrac{x}{m}
gives the necessary bound on
\mathcal{C}(x)
.
∎
We next estimate
\displaystyle\frac{x}{m}\prod_{\ell+2<p\leq y}\left(1-\frac{\ell+1+(\frac{-1}{p})}{p}\right)\prod_{p\mid T}\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{p}\Bigr)^{O(\ell)}
; we wish to bound it asymptotically by
\dfrac{x}{m}\dfrac{L^{O_{\ell}(1)}}{(\log x)^{\ell+1}}
, so we bound the products over primes by
\dfrac{L^{O_{\ell}(1)}}{(\log x)^{\ell+1}}
.

Claim 6.3.7
.

\displaystyle\smash{\prod_{p\mid T}\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{p}\Bigr)^{O(\ell)}}\ll_{\ell}L^{O(1)}
.

Proof.

By Theorem
2.2.4
we have
\#\{p\mid T\}=\omega(T)\ll\frac{\log T}{\log\log T}
. Let
U:=\frac{\log T}{\log\log T}
. By the Taylor series for the
\exp
function,

\smash{\prod_{p\mid T}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)^{O(\ell)}\!\!\ll\prod_{p\leq U}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)^{O(\ell)}\!\!\ll\exp\Bigl(-\sum_{p\leq U}\frac{1}{p}\Bigr).}

Using Mertens’ Second Theorem to estimate
\sum_{p\leq U}\frac{1}{p}
we get

\displaystyle\prod_{p\mid T}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)^{O(\ell)}\!\!\ll\exp\left(-\log\log U-M+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{\log U}\Bigr)\right)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{1}{\log(\frac{\log T}{\log\log T})}\ll\frac{1}{\log\log T}.

So, to prove the necessary bound, we show that
T\ll\exp(\exp(L^{O(1)}))
, recalling that
\displaystyle T=m\prod_{i<j}(u_{i}v_{j}-v_{i}u_{j})(u_{i}u_{j}+v_{i}v_{j})
. For any given pair
(i,j)
, we have
\displaystyle u_{i}u_{j}+v_{i}v_{j}\leq\sqrt{(u_{i}^{2}+v_{i}^{2})(u_{j}^{2}+v_{j}^{2})}=\sqrt{m^{2}}=m
by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. Similarly,
u_{i}v_{j}-v_{i}u_{j}\leq u_{i}v_{j}+v_{i}u_{j}\leq\sqrt{(u_{i}^{2}+v_{i}^{2})(v_{j}^{2}+u_{j}^{2})}=m
. Since there are
\binom{r_{0}^{*}(m)}{2}
possible pairs
(i,j)
with
i<j
, we obtain
T\ll m^{\binom{r_{0}^{*}(m)}{2}+1}
.

Lemma
5.1.1
gives
r_{0}^{*}(2)=1
,
\forall j\geq 2\colon r_{0}^{*}(2^{j})=0
and
r_{0}^{*}(p^{j})=2
. With Lemma
5.1.2
, we get
r_{0}^{*}(m)=2^{\omega^{*}(m)}
, noting that
4\nmid m
since
m\in\mathcal{Q}
, as in the leftmost summation of (
6.7
). Additionally, in (
6.7
) we have
\omega^{*}(m)=k-1
, so
r_{0}^{*}(m)=2^{k-1}
.

By definition,
k\ll\log\log x
. So
r_{0}^{*}(m)\ll\log x
, and thus
T\ll m^{(\log x)^{2}}
. Hence we get
\log\log T\ll\log((\log x)^{2}\log m)=2L+\log\log m
. Moreover
m\leq x^{1-1/L}\leq x
, so
\log\log m\leq L
. Hence
\log\log T\ll 3L\ll L^{O(1)}
, as required.
∎

Next, we consider
\displaystyle\prod_{\ell+2<p\leq y}\Bigl(1-\tfrac{\ell+1+(\frac{-1}{p})}{p}\Bigr)
. Now,
(1-\frac{\ell+1+(\frac{-1}{p})}{p})\asymp(1-\frac{1}{p})^{\ell+1}\Bigl(1-\frac{(\frac{-1}{p})}{p}\Bigr)
, by considering the binomial expansion of
(1-\frac{1}{p})^{\ell+1}
.

Let
\chi_{4}
be the non-principal Dirichlet character modulo
4
. Then
\chi_{4}(p)=(\frac{-1}{p})
for
p>\ell+2
. We have
L(1,\chi_{4})<\infty
where
L(s,\chi)
is the Dirichlet
L
-function, as discussed in Theorem
6.1.3
; by considering the Euler product form of
L(1,\chi_{4})
we get
L(1,\chi_{4})\asymp\prod_{2<p\leq y}\left(1-\frac{(\frac{-1}{p})}{p}\right)
. Hence

\smash{\prod_{\ell+2<p\leq y}\left(1-\frac{\ell+1+(\frac{-1}{p})}{p}\right)\asymp\prod_{\ell+2<p\leq y}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)^{\ell+1}L(1,\chi_{4})^{-1}.}

Now,
\prod_{\ell+2<p\leq y}(1-\frac{1}{p})\asymp\prod_{p\leq y}(1-\frac{1}{p})
. Note that
\prod_{p\leq y}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\sum_{p\leq y}\frac{1}{p}\right)=\exp\left(-\log\log y-M+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{\log y}\Bigr)\right)\ll\frac{1}{\log y}
, by Mertens’ second theorem. Since
\log y=\log(x^{1/10L})=\frac{1}{10L}\log x
, we get

\displaystyle\prod_{\ell+2<p\leq y}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)^{\ell+1}L(1,\chi_{4})^{-1}\ll\frac{1}{(\log y)^{\ell+1}}=\frac{(10L)^{\ell+1}}{(\log x)^{\ell+1}}\asymp\frac{L^{O_{\ell}(1)}}{(\log x)^{\ell+1}}
.

This now gives us the required bound:

\prod_{\ell+2<p\leq y}\left(1-\frac{\ell+1+(\frac{-1}{p})}{p}\right)\prod_{p\mid R}\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{p}\Bigr)^{O(\ell)}\ll L^{O(1)}\cdot\frac{L^{O_{\ell}(1)}}{(\log x)^{\ell+1}}=\frac{L^{O_{\ell}(1)}}{(\log x)^{\ell+1}}.

(6.9)

We can now finish the proof of the main theorem of this section.

.

From (

\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\

6.4
The second moment of
r_{1}

Stephan Daniel’s paper
[
3
]
gives a good estimate for the second moment of
r_{1}
, showing the main term precisely and calculating an error term. He starts by proving the following Lemma:

Lemma 6.4.1
.

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq x}\binom{r_{1}(n)}{2}=\frac{9}{8}\frac{x}{\log x}+O\left(\frac{x(L(x))^{2}}{\log^{2}x}\right)
.

Since we know the asymptotics of the first moment, we can easily obtain an expression for the second moment of
r_{1}
by recalling the definition of binomial coefficients:

Lemma 6.4.2
.

\displaystyle\smash{\sum_{n\leq x}r_{1}(n)^{2}=\Bigl(\frac{\pi}{2}+\frac{9}{4}\Bigr)\frac{x}{\log x}+O\left(\frac{x(L(x))^{2}}{\log^{2}x}\right)}
.

Granville, Sabuncu and Sedunova obtain a weaker result in their paper
[
7
]
based on Theorem
6.3.1
and an additional calculation of lower bounds on moments of
r_{1}
, which we will not discuss in this dissertation.

Theorem 6.4.3

(Granville, Sabuncu, Sedunova, 2023)
.

For any integer
k\asymp L
,

\smash{\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
\omega(n)=k\end{subarray}}}\binom{r_{1}(n)}{2}\asymp\frac{xL^{O(1)}}{(\log x)^{3}}\frac{(2L)^{k}}{k!}.}

We give a brief explanation for why this result is consistent with Lemma
6.4.1
.

Lemma 6.4.4
.

\displaystyle\frac{xL^{O(1)}}{(\log x)^{\ell+1}}\frac{(2L)^{k}}{k!}
is maximised when
k
is approximately
2L
.

Proof.

Stirling’s approximation gives us that
k!\sim\sqrt{2\pi k}(\frac{k}{e})^{k}
. Thus, we seek to maximise
\dfrac{(2L)^{k}}{\sqrt{k}(\frac{k}{e})^{k}}=\dfrac{(2Le)^{k}}{k^{k+1/2}}\asymp(2Le)^{k}k^{-k-\frac{1}{2}}
.

Let
f(y)=(2Le)^{y}y^{-y-\frac{1}{2}}
. We calculate
f^{\prime}(y)
via the product rule. Note that
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}y}((2Le)^{y})=(2Le)^{y}(\log(2Le))=(2Le)^{y}(\log 2L+1)
, and
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}y}(y^{-y-\frac{1}{2}})=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}y}(e^{\log y(-y-\frac{1}{2})})=e^{\log y(-y-\frac{1}{2})}\bigl(-\log y-\frac{y+\frac{1}{2}}{y}\bigr)
. Hence

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} f^{\prime}(y)=f(y)\Bigl(\log 2L+1-\log y-1-\frac{1}{2y}\Bigr)=f(y)\Bigl(\log 2L-\log y-\frac{1}{2y}\Bigr).\end{aligned}

f(y)>0
for
y>0
and
\log 2L-\log y-\frac{1}{2y}
is a decreasing function on
y>0
so in this range,
f(y)
has a global maximum at its unique turning point satisfying the equation
\log y+\frac{1}{2y}=\log 2L
. The left hand side is approximately
\log y
, whence we get that
\displaystyle\frac{xL^{O(1)}}{(\log x)^{3}}\frac{(2L)^{k}}{k!}
is maximised for
k
close to
2L
.
∎

In Theorem
6.4.3
, substituting
k=2L
gives

\frac{xL^{O(1)}}{(\log x)^{3}}\frac{(2L)^{k}}{k!}\asymp\frac{xL^{O(1)}}{(\log x)^{3}}\frac{(2L)^{2L}}{\sqrt{4\pi L}(\frac{2L}{e})^{2L}}=\frac{xL^{O(1)}}{(\log x)^{3}}\frac{\exp(2L)}{\sqrt{4\pi L}}\asymp\frac{xL^{O(1)}}{(\log x)^{3}}\frac{(\log x)^{2}}{L^{O(1)}}=\frac{xL^{O(1)}}{\log x}.

Note that to obtain the second moment of
r_{1}
, we sum over all possible
k
, to count all possible
n\leq x
based on their number of prime factors. However, we expect the contribution of other
k
to be small compared to
k=2L
, hence the result is consistent with a main term of order
\frac{x}{\log x}
. Similarly, this is consistent with Theorem
3.4.7
.

Chapter 7
New problem: replace primes with sums of two squares

We have previously considered the representation numbers
r_{0}
and
r_{1}
. Both count the number of ways to write
n
as the sum of an integer square and the square of
something
. In the case of
r_{0}
, we allow the square of any (positive) integer, and in the case of
r_{1}
, only squares of primes are allowed. As one would expect, since
r_{1}
counts a more restricted set, the moments of
r_{1}
are smaller than those of
r_{0}
. In fact, the ratio of main terms in the first moments is
\frac{1}{\log x}
and the ratio of main terms in the second moments is
\frac{1}{\log^{2}x}
, up to a constant. One might guess this relationship from the Prime Number Theorem, which tells us that
\mathbb{P}(p\leq x\text{ is prime})=\frac{1}{\log x}
.

In this chapter we consider new representation numbers of the form

r_{S}(n):=\#\{(a,b)\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\times S:a^{2}+b^{2}=n\}.

We introduce the following notation, where
r_{S}^{*}
is a generalisation of
r_{0}^{*}
and
r_{1}^{*}
:

Notation
.

(
\mathcal{R}
,
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
,
r_{S}^{*}
)

•

\mathcal{R}:=\{k\in\mathbb{N}:r_{0}(k)\geq 1\}
is the set of numbers which can be written as a sum of two squares.

•

\mathcal{R}^{\prime}:=\{k\in\mathcal{R}:r_{0}^{*}(k)\geq 1\}
is the set of numbers which can be written as a sum of two coprime squares.

•

r_{S}^{*}(n):=\#\{(a,b)\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\times S:a^{2}+b^{2}=n,\ \gcd(a,b)=1\}
.

We can classify the elements of
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
entirely: in the prime factorisation of elements of
\mathcal{R}
, any prime that is
3
modulo
4
can only appear in an even power. Elements of
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
are not divisible by primes that are
3
modulo
4
, nor by
4
.

7.1
First moments of
r_{S}

We start by finding the first moments of
r_{\mathcal{R}}
and
r_{\mathcal{R}^{\prime}}
. Similar to our discussion at the start of the chapter, this will allow us to make a hypothesis about the behaviour of the second moments of these functions. The proof method is similar to that of Lemma
3.2.1
, but we use a slightly simpler approach which gives a weaker error term as our aim currently is to obtain the main term of these moments.

Lemma 7.1.1

(First moment of
r_{\mathcal{R}}
)
.

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq x}r_{\mathcal{R}}(n)=c\frac{x}{\sqrt{\log x}}\left(1+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{\log x}\Bigr)\right)
, where
c
is the constant
\ \displaystyle\frac{\pi}{4}\prod_{p\equiv 3\mod 4}\bigl(1-\tfrac{1}{p^{2}}\bigr)^{-\frac{1}{2}}
.

Proof.

We start by writing

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq x}r_{\mathcal{R}}(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\leq\sqrt{x}}\sum_{n\leq\sqrt{x-a^{2}}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{n\in\mathcal{R}\}}=\sum_{a\leq\sqrt{\frac{x}{2}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq\sqrt{x-a^{2}}\\
n\in\mathcal{R}\end{subarray}}1+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq\sqrt{\frac{x}{2}}\\
n\in\mathcal{R}\end{subarray}}\sum_{a\leq\sqrt{x-n^{2}}}1-\sum_{a\leq\sqrt{\frac{x}{2}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq\sqrt{\frac{x}{2}}\\
n\in\mathcal{R}\end{subarray}}1

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\leq\sqrt{\frac{x}{2}}}M_{0}(\sqrt{x-a^{2}})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq\sqrt{\frac{x}{2}}\\
n\in\mathcal{R}\end{subarray}}\lfloor\sqrt{x-n^{2}}\rfloor-\sqrt{x/2}\cdot M_{0}(\sqrt{x/2}),

(7.1)

where
M_{0}
is the zeroth moment of
r_{0}
. We recall Lemma
3.3.1
, which states that
M_{0}(x)=c^{\prime}\frac{x}{\sqrt{\log x}}\bigl(1+O((\log x)^{-1})\bigr)
, where
c^{\prime}:=\bigl(2\prod_{p\equiv 3\mod 4}(1-\tfrac{1}{p^{2}})\bigr)^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

We deal with the components in (
7.1
), proceeding as in Lemma
3.2.1
. For the first component, as
a\leq\sqrt{x/2}
we have

\log x\geq\log(x-a^{2})\geq\log(x/2)=\log x-\log 2
, so

\sqrt{\frac{2(x-a^{2})}{\log x}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{2(x-a^{2})}{\log(x-a^{2})}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{2(x-a^{2})}{\log x-\log 2}}.

Since

(\log x-\log 2)^{-1}=\log^{-1}(x)(1+O(\frac{1}{\log x}))
we get

M_{0}(\sqrt{x-a^{2}})=c^{\prime}\sqrt{\frac{2(x-a^{2})}{\log x}}\left(1+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{\log x}\Bigr)\right),

and thus
\sum_{a\leq\sqrt{\frac{x}{2}}}M_{0}(\sqrt{x-a^{2}})=c^{\prime}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\log x}}\sum_{a\leq\sqrt{\frac{x}{2}}}\sqrt{x-a^{2}}\left(1+O(\frac{1}{\log x})\right)
.

As in Lemma
3.2.1
we have
\sum_{a\leq\sqrt{x/2}}\sqrt{x-a^{2}}=\int_{0}^{\sqrt{x/2}}\sqrt{x-a^{2}}\mathrm{d}a+O(\sqrt{x})=\frac{\pi+2}{8}x\left(1+O(\frac{1}{\log x})\right)
, so

\sum_{a\leq\sqrt{\frac{x}{2}}}M_{0}(\sqrt{x-a^{2}})=c^{\prime}\frac{\sqrt{2}(\pi+2)}{8}\frac{x}{\sqrt{\log x}}\left(1+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{\log x}\Bigr)\right).

For the second component of (
7.1
), apply partial summation with
a_{n}=\mathbbm{1}_{\{n\in\mathcal{R}\}}
and
f(y)=\sqrt{x-y^{2}}
to get

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq\sqrt{\frac{x}{2}}\\
n\in\mathcal{R}\end{subarray}}(\sqrt{x-n^{2}}+O(1))=O(\sqrt{x})+M_{0}(\sqrt{x/2})\sqrt{x/2}+\smash[t]{\int_{1}^{\sqrt{x/2}}M_{0}(t)t(x-t^{2})^{-1/2}\mathrm{d}t}.

As previously the
M_{0}(\sqrt{x/2})\sqrt{x/2}
term is the third component of (
7.1
), and
O(\sqrt{x})
can be absorbed into the error term of the first component as
O(\sqrt{x})=O(\frac{x}{{\log x}^{3/2}})
. It remains to estimate the integral, noting that we can change the lower bound to
2
as
M_{0}(t)=0
for
t<2
. We substitute the main term of
M_{0}
into the integral.

We substitute
t=u\sqrt{x}
to get

\int_{2}^{\sqrt{x/2}}c^{\prime}\frac{t}{\sqrt{\log t}}\frac{t}{\sqrt{x-t^{2}}}\mathrm{d}t=c^{\prime}\sqrt{x}\int_{2/\sqrt{x}}^{\sqrt{1/2}}\frac{xu^{2}}{\sqrt{x}\sqrt{1-u^{2}}}\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\sqrt{\log(u\sqrt{x})}}.

Integration by parts, using the formula for
\int\frac{u^{2}}{\sqrt{1-u^{2}}}\mathrm{d}u
, gives

\displaystyle c^{\prime}\frac{x}{2}\left[\frac{\arcsin(u)-u\sqrt{1-u^{2}}}{\sqrt{\log(u\sqrt{x})}}\right]_{2/\sqrt{x}}^{\sqrt{1/2}}+c^{\prime}\frac{x}{4}\int_{2/\sqrt{x}}^{\sqrt{1/2}}\frac{(\arcsin(u)-u\sqrt{1-u^{2}})\mathrm{d}u}{u(\log(u\sqrt{x}))^{3/2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{c^{\prime}x(\frac{\pi}{4}-\frac{1}{2})}{2\sqrt{\log(\sqrt{\frac{x}{2}})}}+O(\sqrt{x})+\frac{c^{\prime}x}{4}\int_{2/\sqrt{x}}^{\sqrt{1/2}}\Bigl(\frac{\arcsin(u)}{u}-\sqrt{1-u^{2}}\Bigr)\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{(\log(u\sqrt{x}))^{3/2}}.

(7.2)

As previously, we split this last integral into ranges
[\frac{2}{\sqrt{x}},\frac{1}{\sqrt[4]{x}}]
and
[\frac{1}{\sqrt[4]{x}},\sqrt{1/2}]
. In the first component, we have
\frac{\arcsin(u)}{u}-\sqrt{1-u^{2}}=\frac{2}{3}u^{2}+O(u^{4})=O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}})
, and we bound
(\log(u\sqrt{x}))^{3/2}
from below by
(\log 2)^{3/2}
. In the second, we bound
\frac{\arcsin(u)}{u}-\sqrt{1-u^{2}}
by a constant, and bound
(\log(u\sqrt{x}))^{3/2}
from below by
(\log(\sqrt[4]{x}))^{3/2}
. Hence

\displaystyle\int_{2/\sqrt{x}}^{\sqrt{1/2}}\Bigl(\frac{\arcsin(u)}{u}-\sqrt{1-u^{2}}\Bigr)\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\log^{2}(u\sqrt{x})}

\displaystyle=O(x^{-1/4})+O((\log x)^{-3/2}).

(7.3)

Substituting (
7.3
) into (
7.2
) gives

\smash{\int_{2}^{\sqrt{x/2}}M_{0}(t)t(x-t^{2})^{-1/2}\mathrm{d}t=\frac{c^{\prime}x}{\sqrt{2\log x}}\Bigl(\frac{\pi}{4}-\frac{1}{2}\Bigr)+O\Bigl(\frac{x}{(\log x)^{3/2}}\Bigr).}

Substituting this into (
7.1
) gives

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq x}r_{\mathcal{R}}(n)

\displaystyle=c^{\prime}\frac{\pi+2}{4\sqrt{2}}\frac{x}{\sqrt{\log x}}\left(1+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{\log x}\Bigr)\right)+\frac{c^{\prime}x}{\sqrt{2\log x}}\cdot\frac{\pi-2}{4}+O\Bigl(\frac{x}{(\log x)^{3/2}}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=c^{\prime}\sqrt{2}\cdot\frac{\pi}{4}\frac{x}{\sqrt{\log x}}\left(1+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{\log x}\Bigr)\right).

(7.4)

By definition,
c=c^{\prime}\sqrt{2}\cdot\frac{\pi}{4}
, so we are done.
∎

The first moment of
r_{\mathcal{R}^{\prime}}
behaves very similarly:

Lemma 7.1.2

(First moment of
r_{\mathcal{R}^{\prime}}
)
.

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq x}r_{\mathcal{R}^{\prime}}(n)=c\frac{x}{\sqrt{\log x}}\left(1+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{\log x}\Bigr)\right)
, where

c=\frac{3\pi}{8}\sqrt{\prod_{p\equiv 3\pmod{4}}(1-p^{-2})}.

Proof.

The proof will be as above, except we must replace
M_{0}
with the zeroth moment of
r_{0}^{*}
, which we will denote
M_{0}^{*}
. We have

M_{0}^{*}(x)=\#\{n\leq x:n\in\mathcal{R}^{\prime}\}=\#\{n\leq x:4\nmid n,p\nmid n\text{ for all }p\equiv 3\pmod{4}\}.

Our task now is to evaluate this quantity.

We first calculate the number of
n\leq x
whose prime factorisation only contains primes that are
1
modulo
4
. This can be done through considering the Dirichlet series
F(s)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{a_{n}}{n^{s}}
with
a_{n}=\mathbbm{1}(p\mid n\Rightarrow p\equiv 1\pmod{4})
. Since this indicator function is multiplicative, we have an Euler product:
F(s)=\prod_{p\equiv 1\pmod{4}}(1-p^{-s})^{-1}
.

Recall the Riemann zeta function
\zeta(s)=\prod_{p}(1-p^{-s})^{-1}
and the Dirichlet L-function
L(s,\chi)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{\chi(n)}{n^{s}}
introduced in Theorem
6.1.3
, where
\chi
is the non-principal Dirichlet character modulo
4
. Since
\chi
is totally multiplicative we can write the Euler product
L(s,\chi)=\prod_{p}(1-\frac{\chi(p)}{p^{s}})^{-1}
. Thus

\smash{\frac{\zeta(s)L(s,\chi)}{F(s)^{2}}=(1-2^{-s})^{-1}\prod_{p\equiv 3\pmod{4}}((1-p^{-s})(1+p^{-s}))^{-1},}

(7.5)

by comparing the Euler products of the series.

The Riemann zeta function has a simple pole of residue
1
at
s=1
, and is meromorphic for
\Re(s)\geq 1
, with no other poles. Since
\chi
is a non-principal Dirichlet character, we have
L(s,\chi)
is holomorphic for
\Re(s)\geq 1
, and
L(1,\chi)\neq 0
. In fact,
L(1,\chi)=1-\frac{1}{3}+\frac{1}{5}-\dots=\frac{\pi}{4}
by using the arctan Taylor series. Thus
\zeta(s)L(s,\chi)
has a simple pole of residue
\frac{\pi}{4}
at
s=1
and is otherwise holomorphic on
\Re(s)\geq 1
. However, the right hand side in (
7.5
) is holomorphic for
\Re(s)>\frac{1}{2}
, as the Dirichlet series corresponding to
\prod_{p\equiv 3\pmod{4}}(1-p^{-2s})^{-1}
converges uniformly in this half-plane. Hence
F(s)^{2}
must have a simple pole at
s=1
as well, of residue
\frac{\pi}{4}(1-\frac{1}{2})^{-1}\prod_{p\equiv 3\pmod{4}}(1-p^{-2})=\frac{\pi}{2}\prod_{p\equiv 3\pmod{4}}(1-p^{-2})
.

We now apply a variant of the Wiener-Ikehara Theorem. The result used here was proved by Kato
[
12
, Theorem 3.1]
. We state it below:

Theorem 7.1.3

(Wiener, Ikehara, Delange, Kato)
.

Let
\{a_{n}\}
be a sequence of non-negative real numbers,
d\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and
m
a positive integer. Suppose that the Dirichlet series
L(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{n}}{n^{s}}
converges absolutely for
\Re(s)>d
. Also suppose that
L^{m}
has a meromorphic continuation to an open set containing the closed half-plane
\Re(s)\geq d
, holomorphic except for a pole of order
l
at
s=d
with
\lim_{s\to d}L(s)^{m}(s-d)^{l}=A^{m}
, where
A>0
. Then

\sum_{n\leq x}a_{n}\sim\frac{AX^{d}}{d\Gamma(\frac{l}{m})(\log x)^{1-l/m}}.

We substitute
L=F
,
m=2
,
d=1
,
l=1
,
A^{2}=\frac{\pi}{2}\prod_{p\equiv 3\pmod{4}}(1-p^{-2})
.
\Gamma
is the Gamma function as previously seen, and it is well-known that
\Gamma(\frac{1}{2})=\sqrt{\pi}
. Hence we get

f(x):=\sum_{n\leq x}a_{n}\sim\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\prod_{p\equiv 3\pmod{4}}(1-p^{-2})}\frac{x}{\sqrt{\log x}}.

Since
M_{0}^{*}(x)=f(x)+f(\frac{x}{2})
we obtain

M_{0}^{*}(x)\sim\frac{3}{2}\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\prod_{p\equiv 3\pmod{4}}(1-p^{-2})}\frac{x}{\sqrt{\log x}}.

Substituting this constant instead of the coefficient of
\frac{x}{\sqrt{\log x}}
in
M_{0}(x)
into the proof of
7.1.1
gives the required result.
∎

Remark
.

The proof method here is similar to Landau’s, but we use the more modern technique of the Wiener Ikehara theorem instead of Landau’s computations and integrals. Lemma
3.3.1
could also be proved in a similar way (without error term) if we were able to analyse the behaviour of the corresponding Dirichlet series.

.

\sum_{d\leq y^{2}}3^{\omega(d)}|R_{d}|\ll\frac{x}{m}\frac{L^{O_{\ell}(1)}}{(\log x)^{\ell+1}}
:

\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}a^{2}+b^{2}\leq x/m\\
\gcd(a^{2}+b^{2},\prod_{q\leq y}q)=1\\
\prod_{\phi\in I}\gcd(\phi(a,b),\prod_{q\leq y}q)=1\end{subarray}}}1\leq XG(y,y)+O\Bigl(\frac{x}{m}\frac{L^{O_{\ell}(1)}}{(\log x)^{\ell+1}}\Bigr).

We can use the same arguments as in Claim
6.3.5
, noting that

\prod_{p\equiv 3\pmod{4}}\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{s}}\right)\leq\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{1}{n^{s}}=O(1)

for
s>1
, and

\prod_{p\equiv 3\pmod{4}}\left(1-\frac{\ell+(\frac{-1}{p})}{p^{2}}\right)\ll\ O_{\ell}(1)\!\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\geq(\ell+2)^{2}\\
p\equiv 3\pmod{4}\end{subarray}}\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{p^{3/2}}\Bigr)\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{1}{n^{3/2}}=O_{\ell}(1)\ll_{\ell}1.

Hence we refine this to

\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}a^{2}+b^{2}\leq x/m\\
\gcd(a^{2}+b^{2},\prod_{q\in\mathfrak{p}_{y}}q)=1\\
\prod_{\phi\in I}\gcd(\phi(a,b),\prod_{q\in\mathfrak{p}_{y}}q)=1\end{subarray}}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!1\ll_{\ell}\frac{x}{m}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell+2<p\leq y\\
p\equiv 3\!\!\!\!\pmod{4}\end{subarray}}\left(1-\frac{\ell}{p}\right)\prod_{p\mid T}\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{p}\Bigr)^{O(\ell)}+O\Bigl(\frac{x}{m}\frac{L^{O_{\ell}(1)}}{(\log x)^{\ell+1}}\Bigr).\qed

Claim
6.3.7
already shows that
\displaystyle\prod_{p\mid T}\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{p}\Bigr)^{O(\ell)}\ll_{\ell}L^{O(1)}
, so we are left to bound

\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell+2<p\leq y\\
p\equiv 3\pmod{4}\end{subarray}}\left(1-\frac{\ell}{p}\right)\asymp\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell+2<p\leq y\\
p\equiv 3\pmod{4}\end{subarray}}(1-\frac{1}{p})^{\ell}\asymp\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq y\\
p\equiv 3\pmod{4}\end{subarray}}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right).

Where previously we used Mertens’ second theorem, now we use Mertens’ theorem for Primes in APs
6.1.3
, and we again only require the special case discussed previously of modulo
4
, which gives us that

\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq y\\
p\equiv 3\!\!\pmod{4}\end{subarray}}\!\!\!\!\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{p}\Bigr)\leq\exp\Bigl(-\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq y\\
p\equiv 3\!\!\pmod{4}\end{subarray}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\frac{1}{p}\Bigr)=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\log\log y-M+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{\log y}\Bigr)\right)\ll\frac{1}{\sqrt{\log y}}.

Since
\log y=\log(x^{1/10L})=\frac{1}{10L}\log x
, we get

\displaystyle\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell+2<p\leq y\\
p\equiv 3\pmod{4}\end{subarray}}\left(1-\frac{\ell}{p}\right)\ll\frac{1}{(\sqrt{\log y})^{\ell+1}}\asymp\frac{L^{O_{\ell}(1)}}{(\sqrt{\log x})^{\ell+1}}
.
This gives us

\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}a^{2}+b^{2}\leq x/m\\
\gcd(a^{2}+b^{2},\prod_{q\in\mathfrak{p}_{y}}q)=1\\
\prod_{\phi\in I}\gcd(\phi(a,b),\prod_{q\in\mathfrak{p}_{y}}q)=1\end{subarray}}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!1\ll_{\ell}\frac{x}{m}\frac{L^{O_{\ell}(1)}}{(\sqrt{\log x})^{\ell+1}}.

Proof of Theorem
7.2.1
.

The proof is finished in the same way as Theorem
6.3.1
. We have

\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
\omega^{*}(n)=k\\
P(n)>x^{1/L}\end{subarray}}}\binom{r_{\mathcal{R}^{\prime}}^{*}(n)}{\ell}\ll_{\ell}\frac{xL^{O_{\ell}(1)}}{(\sqrt{\log x})^{\ell+1}}\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq x^{1-1/L}\\
\omega^{*}(m)=k-1\\
m\in\mathcal{R}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}}\frac{1}{m}\cdot\#\{I\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{m}\colon|I|=\ell\},

where
\mathcal{L}_{m}
is the same set as before; hence we obtain the same result but with the
(\log x)^{\ell+1}
in the denominator replaced by
(\sqrt{\log x})^{\ell+1}
.
∎

Note that when considering the second moment (
\ell=2
), this is again maximised when
k
is approximately
2L
, in which case
\frac{xL^{O_{\ell}(1)}}{(\sqrt{\log x})^{\ell+1}}\frac{(2^{\ell-1}L)^{k}}{k!}
is approximately
x\sqrt{\log x}L^{O(1)}
.

7.3
Moments of
r_{\mathcal{R}}

As we have seen, many steps of the proof of Theorem
6.3.1
can be generalised to
r_{S}
, and the main change is the estimate we obtain on

\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}x^{1/L}<p\leq x/m\\
p>P(m)\text{ prime}\\
p\in\mathcal{R}^{\prime},\ p=a^{2}+b^{2},\ a<b\end{subarray}}}\!\!\!\!\!\mathbbm{1}(\forall\phi\in I:\phi(a,b)\in{S})

by sieve methods. In the cases
S=\mathbb{N}
and
S=\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
, we were able to use a regular Selberg sieve to sieve out all small primes in Theorem
6.3.1
, or only those congruent to
3
modulo
4
in Theorem
7.2.1
. However, in the case
S=\mathcal{R}
, we have to use a slightly modified Selberg sieve, which does not sieve based on residue classes modulo primes but rather modulo prime powers.

We start with the discrete uniform probability space
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
with
\mathcal{A}:=\bigl\{(a,b):1\leq a,b\leq\sqrt{\tfrac{x}{m}}\bigr\}
, of size approximately
X:=\frac{x}{m}
. We still wish our sieve problem to sift elements of
S=\{(a^{2}+b^{2})\prod_{\phi\in I}\phi(a,b):1\leq a,b\leq\sqrt{\tfrac{x}{m}}\}
, but the events
\mathcal{A}_{p}
are now
\emptyset
for
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}
, and for
p\equiv 3\pmod{4}
we have
\mathcal{A}_{p}=\{(a,b)\in\mathcal{A}:p^{e}\mathrel{\|}(a^{2}+b^{2})\prod_{\phi\in I}\phi(a,b),\text{ some odd }e\}
.

Remark
.

In fact, the events
\mathcal{A}_{p}
above do not represent all possible ways of failing the conditions of
a^{2}+b^{2}\in{\mathcal{R}},\forall\phi\in I:\phi(a,b)\in{\mathcal{R}}
. For example, given
p\equiv 3\pmod{4}
we could have
p
divides
\phi_{1}(a,b)
and
\phi_{2}(a,b)
to an odd power each, some
\phi_{1},\phi_{2}\in I
, but
p
divides
(a^{2}+b^{2})\prod_{\phi\in I}\phi(a,b)
to an even power. The sieve gives us an upper bound on our sum, which could possibly be further refined by considering these additional cases.

We will consider even smaller events
\mathcal{A}_{p}=\{p\mathrel{\|}(a^{2}+b^{2})\prod_{\phi\in I}\phi(a,b)\}
, which give an even cruder upper bound.
We want to find a formula for
g(p),\ p\equiv 3\pmod{4}
so that
|\mathcal{A}_{p}|-Xg(p)
is small, as previously. However, note that in the case
p\mid T
, there exist two linear forms in
I
that are linearly dependent modulo
p
, so
p\mid(a^{2}+b^{2})\prod_{\phi\in I}\phi(a,b)\iff p^{2}\mid(a^{2}+b^{2})\prod_{\phi\in I}\phi(a,b)
. Hence we set
g(p)=0
in this case.

If
p\nmid T
, we will count the number of residue classes
(a,b)\in\mathbb{Z}_{p^{2}}\times\mathbb{Z}_{p^{2}}
satisfying
p\mathrel{\|}(a^{2}+b^{2})\prod_{\phi\in I}\phi(a,b)
. Firstly, any pair
(pk_{1},pk_{2})
can be excluded as this implies
p^{2}\mid(a^{2}+b^{2})
. Note that all the linear forms are linearly independent modulo
p
for
p\nmid T
, as discussed in Claim
6.3.5
. So if
p\nmid a,p\nmid b
, then
p\mathrel{\|}(a^{2}+b^{2})\prod_{\phi\in I}\phi(a,b)\implies\exists\phi\in I:p\mathrel{\|}\phi(a,b)
.

Thus, we now calculate the cardinality of
\{(a^{*},b^{*})\in\mathbb{Z}_{p^{2}}\times\mathbb{Z}_{p^{2}}:p\mathrel{\|}\phi_{0}(a^{*},b^{*})\}
for a fixed
\phi_{0}=rx+sy\in I
. Suppose
s
is invertible modulo
p
– we can always assume one of
r
,
s
is not divisible by
p
as they are coprime. For every
a^{\prime}\pmod{p}
there is a unique
b^{\prime}\equiv-rs^{-1}a^{\prime}\pmod{p}
such that
p\mid\phi(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
. Hence, for each
a^{*}\pmod{p^{2}}
there are
p
possible
b^{*}
with
p\mid\phi(a^{*},b^{*})
, and out of those, a unique
b^{*}\equiv-rs^{-1}a^{*}\pmod{p^{2}}
such that
p^{2}\mid\phi(a^{*},b^{*})
. Hence
\#\{(a^{*},b^{*})\in\mathbb{Z}_{p^{2}}\times\mathbb{Z}_{p^{2}}:p\mathrel{\|}\phi_{0}(a^{*},b^{*})\}=p^{2}(p-1)
.

However, these
p^{2}(p-1)
residues include some of the form
(pk_{1},pk_{2})
. We calculate separately how many such residues have been included. Indeed,
p\mathrel{\|}\phi_{0}(pk_{1},pk_{2})\iff\phi_{0}(k_{1},k_{2})\not\equiv 0\pmod{p}
. We previously calculated
\#\{(k_{1},k_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}\times\mathbb{Z}_{p}:\phi_{0}(k_{1},k_{2})\equiv 0\pmod{p}\}=p
. Hence, for
p^{2}-p
residues
(a^{*},b^{*})\in\mathbb{Z}_{p^{2}}\times\mathbb{Z}_{p^{2}}
of the form
(pk_{1},pk_{2})
, we have
p\mathrel{\|}\phi_{0}(pk_{1},pk_{2})
. Thus
\#\{(a^{*},b^{*})\in\mathbb{Z}_{p^{2}}\times\mathbb{Z}_{p^{2}}\setminus p\mathbb{Z}_{p}\times p\mathbb{Z}_{p}:p\mathrel{\|}\phi_{0}(a^{*},b^{*})\}=p^{2}(p-1)-p(p-1)=p(p-1)^{2}
.

Lemma

(Formula for
g(p)
)
.

•

If
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}
or
p\mid T
then
g(p)=0
.

•

If
p\nmid T
then
p^{4}g(p)=\ell\cdot p(p-1)^{2}
.

We begin by estimating the error terms
R_{d}=|\mathcal{A}_{d}|-g(d)X
for squarefree products of primes that are
3
modulo
4
. We follow the same method as in Claim
6.3.6
, but the calculation is slightly different as this time we worked modulo
p^{2}
.

Indeed, we define
\displaystyle D:=d^{2}\left\lfloor\Bigl\lfloor\sqrt{\tfrac{x}{m}}\Bigr\rfloor/d^{2}\right\rfloor
to be the largest integer multiple of
d^{2}
that is at most
\sqrt{\frac{x}{m}}
, and
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}:=\Bigl\{(a,b):1\leq a,b\leq D\Bigr\}
satisfies
|\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{d}|=g(d)D^{2}
. Thus
R_{d}=|\mathcal{A}_{d}|-|\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{d}|+g(d)D^{2}-g(d)X=|\mathcal{A}_{d}|-|\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{d}|+g(d)(D^{2}-X)
.

As before,
R_{d}=\epsilon(X-D^{2})
for some
\epsilon\in[-1,1]
. Since
0\leq\sqrt{\frac{x}{m}}-D\leq d^{2}
we have
0\leq X-D^{2}\leq 2d^{2}\sqrt{\frac{x}{m}}
. Hence
|R_{d}|\leq 2d^{2}\sqrt{\frac{x}{m}}
.

We now estimate
\sum_{d\leq y^{2}}3^{\omega(d)}|R_{d}|
, where
\omega(d)\leq\log_{3}(d)+1
. Using our estimate of
|R_{d}|
above gives a total error of
\sum_{d\leq y^{2}}3d\cdot 2d^{2}\sqrt{\frac{x}{m}}=6\sqrt{\frac{x}{m}}\sum_{d\leq y^{2}}d^{3}\ll\sqrt{\frac{x}{m}}(y^{2})^{4}=\sqrt{\frac{x}{m}}x^{8/10L}\ll\frac{x}{m}\frac{L^{O_{\ell}(1)}}{(\log x)^{\ell+1}}
. This holds as
L
is eventually large, and so
x^{8/10L}
is of smaller order than
\sqrt{x}\frac{L^{O_{\ell}(1)}}{(\log x)^{\ell+1}}
. Hence we again get

\smash[t]{\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}a^{2}+b^{2}\leq x/m\\
\gcd(a^{2}+b^{2},\prod_{q\leq y}q)=1\\
\prod_{\phi\in I}\gcd(\phi(a,b),\prod_{q\leq y}q)=1\end{subarray}}}1\leq XG(y,y)+O\Bigl(\frac{x}{m}\frac{L^{O_{\ell}(1)}}{(\log x)^{\ell+1}}\Bigr)},

where
G(z,z)=O_{\ell}(1)\prod_{p<z}(1-g(p))^{-1}(1+O(\tfrac{1}{\log z}))
, and

\prod_{p<y}(1-g(p))^{-1}=O_{\ell}(1)\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell+2<p<y\\
p\equiv 3\!\!\!\!\pmod{4}\\
p\,\nmid\,T\end{subarray}}\left(1-\frac{\ell\cdot(p-1)^{2}}{p^{3}}\right)^{-1}.

Since
1-\dfrac{\ell\cdot(\frac{p-1}{p})^{2}}{p}\ll\Bigl(1-\dfrac{1}{p}\Bigr)^{O(\ell)}
, we can rewrite the RHS above as

O_{\ell}(1)\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell+2<p<y\\
p\equiv 3\!\!\!\!\pmod{4}\end{subarray}}\left(1-\frac{\ell\cdot(p-1)^{2}}{p^{3}}\right)^{-1}\prod_{p\mid T}\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{p}\Bigr)^{O(\ell)}.

We have from Claim
6.3.7
that
\displaystyle\prod_{p\mid T}\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{p}\Bigr)^{O(\ell)}\ll_{\ell}L^{O(1)}
, and it remains to bound
\displaystyle\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell+2<p<y\\
p\equiv 3\!\!\pmod{4}\end{subarray}}\left(1-\frac{\ell\cdot(p-1)^{2}}{p^{3}}\right)^{-1}
. Now,
\displaystyle\Bigl(1-\frac{\ell\cdot(p-1)^{2}}{p^{3}}\Bigr)-\Bigl(1-\frac{\ell}{p}\Bigr)=\ell\frac{2p-1}{p^{3}}
and
\displaystyle\prod_{p\equiv 3\pmod{4}}\left(1-\ell\frac{2p-1}{p^{3}}\right)\ll O_{\ell}(1)\prod_{p\geq(\ell+2)^{2}}\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{p^{3/2}}\Bigr)\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{1}{n^{3/2}}=O_{\ell}(1)\ll_{\ell}1
, so

\prod_{p<y}(1-g(p))^{-1}\ll_{\ell}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell+2<p<y\\
p\equiv 3\!\!\!\!\pmod{4}\end{subarray}}\left(1-\frac{\ell}{p}\right)^{-1}\prod_{p\mid T}\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{p}\Bigr)^{O(\ell)}.

We have now obtained the same bound as in Theorem
7.2.1
, hence we get

Theorem 7.3.1
.

For any fixed integer
\ell\geq 1
and integer
k\ll L
we have

\smash{\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
\omega^{*}(n)=k\end{subarray}}}\binom{r_{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(n)}{\ell}\ll_{\ell}\frac{xL^{O_{\ell}(1)}}{(\sqrt{\log x})^{\ell+1}}\frac{(2^{\ell-1}L)^{k}}{k!}.}

Although we used quite a crude sieve, the bound is of same order as for
r_{\mathcal{R}^{\prime}}^{*}
, which is smaller than
r_{\mathcal{R}}^{*}
everywhere. This suggests that no immediate improvement can be made on the result simply by defining the sieving criteria more carefully. In fact, this bound is possibly sharp in the case
\ell=2
.

Note that the theorems we have proved in this chapter are actually about moments of
r_{S}^{*}
for
S=\mathcal{R}
,
S=\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
, and not
r_{S}
itself. This is because the proof technique of Claim
6.3.2
, which relies on Lemma
5.2.1
, cannot be reproduced for the functions
r_{\mathcal{R}}
and
r_{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}.

Conclusion

In this thesis, we have seen results about the asymptotic behaviour of moments of representation numbers. We focused on
r_{1}(n)
, which counts the number of ways to represent
n
as the sum of a square and the square of a prime, and derived our own results about the behaviour of
r_{\mathcal{R}}(n)
, which counts the number of ways to represent
n
as the sum of a square and the square of a sum of two squares. In particular, the results about
r_{1}
proved by Granville, Sabuncu and Sedunova, which we investigated in detail and used as a foundation for other estimates, are consistent with Stephan Daniel’s theorem about second moments of
r_{1}
. This remarkable result has a very refined error bound, but it is difficult to generalise Daniel’s highly technical proof to higher moments. It would be interesting to develop a proof similar to Daniel’s about the second moment of
r_{\mathcal{R}}
and compare it to the results proved in this thesis to see whether the upper bounds obtained are reasonable.

Given more time, it may be possible to further refine some of the estimates in the results we showed, and continue to investigate moments of
r_{S}
for other interesting sets
S
. Granville, Sabuncu and Sedunova also worked on lower bounds, which I have not touched on in this paper. Although the lower bounds for moments of
r_{1}
are conjecturally of the same order of magnitude, the current lower bounds which can be proved without further assumptions are quite far from the upper bounds. Hopefully more progress will be made in the future to bring the two closer.
