CaMKIIβ insufficiency disrupts cortical networks, producing aberrant low‐gamma oscillations and seizure susceptibility
Authors: Hiroki Mutoh, Kazushi Aoto, Atsuo Fukuda, Hirotomo Saitsu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivePathogenic variants in the calcium/calmodulin‐dependent protein kinase II B gene (CAMK2B) have been associated with neurodevelopmental disorders, including epilepsy, yet the mechanisms underlying cortical dysfunction remain largely unclear. Building on our previous clinical report of a patient carrying the CaMKIIβ P213L variant and our prior characterization of the corresponding mouse models, we investigated how P213L‐associated CaMKIIβ insufficiency alters cortical network dynamics and susceptibility to pentylenetetrazol (PTZ)–induced seizures in vivo.MethodsWe performed electroencephalographic recordings for CaMKIIβ P213L knock‐in mice under baseline and pharmacological modulation. Susceptibility to seizure induction by the chemoconvulsant PTZ was assessed. Cortical CaMKIIβ expression and Thr287 phosphorylation levels were quantified and compared to those in CaMKIIβ knockout mice.ResultsHeterozygous and homozygous knock‐in mice exhibited aberrant low‐gamma (20–50 Hz) oscillations during resting state with behavioral immobility. These aberrant low‐gamma oscillations were sensitive to γ‐aminobutyric acid (GABA)‐ergic modulation: pentylenetetrazol (PTZ) induced a downward shift in the gamma‐band peak frequency, whereas isoflurane, diazepam, and valproic acid suppressed the aberrant low‐gamma oscillations. PTZ administration increased seizure severity in both heterozygous and homozygous knock‐in mice, but lethality occurred only in homozygous mice. We quantified cortical CaMKIIβ expression and Thr287 phosphorylation, both of which were reduced in knock‐in mice. Knockout mice recapitulated the aberrant low‐gamma oscillations and their pharmacological modulation observed in knock‐in mice, supporting the role of CaMKIIβ insufficiency in driving the phenotype.SignificanceThese findings suggest that CaMKIIβ insufficiency disrupts cortical excitatory–inhibitory balance, leading to the aberrant low‐gamma oscillations and increased seizure susceptibility. Our findings establish a mechanistic link between CaMKIIβ deficiency and epilepsy‐related phenotypes in neurodevelopmental disorders. The P213L variant represents a loss‐of‐function variant with reduced CaMKIIβ expression and phosphorylation, and provides a valuable model for investigating disease mechanisms and developing potential therapeutic strategies.Keywords: aberrant low‐gamma oscillations, CaMKIIβ, excitatory–inhibitory imbalance, haploinsufficiency, seizure susceptibility

INTRODUCTION

Calcium/calmodulin‐dependent protein kinase II (CaMKII) is a multifunctional serine/threonine kinase that translates calcium influx into diverse cellular responses such as synaptic plasticity and gene expression. 1 , 2 Among its four isoforms, CaMKIIα and CaMKIIβ are expressed predominantly in the brain 3 , 4 and are essential for learning, memory, cognitive functions, and neural circuit remodeling under physiological and pathological conditions. 5 , 6

Although early studies provided limited evidence linking variants in genes encoding CAMKICaMKII subunits to neurological disease, a rare CAMK2A variant was identified in a large‐scale schizophrenia sequencing study. 7 Definitive pathogenic variants in CAMK2A (encoding CaMKIIα) and CAMK2B (encoding CaMKIIβ) were first reported in patients with neurodevelopmental disorders, including intellectual disability and epilepsy. 8 , 9 Subsequent reports have expanded the catalog of variants and associated phenotypes. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 Collectively, these studies have established the clinical relevance of CAMK2A and CAMK2B variants in a range of neurodevelopmental disorders. However, how CaMKII dysfunction, particularly that of CaMKIIβ, perturbs neural circuit activity and contributes to epileptogenesis remains largely unclear.

Building on our previous finding that CaMKIIα and CaMKIIβ variants exhibit increased autophosphorylation in cell‐based assays, 9 we generated knock‐in (KI) mice harboring the CaMKIIβ P213L variant and knockout (KO) mice. 16 We demonstrated that homozygous CaMKIIβ P213L KI mice exhibited clear motor deficits and developmental delay, partially recapitulating the clinical features observed in the patient. Unexpectedly, this mutation resulted in a loss‐of‐function phenotype, as evidenced by reduced CaMKIIβ protein levels and decreased Thr287 autophosphorylation in the cerebellum, findings that contrasted with the enhanced phosphorylation observed in cell‐based assays. These results suggest that the mutant protein behaves differently depending on the biological context. 9 , 16

Recent studies have increasingly implicated CaMKIIβ in the regulation of neuronal excitability 17 and epileptogenesis. 18 CaMKIIβ plays both structural and regulatory roles in maintaining the excitatory–inhibitory (E/I) balance of cortical networks, by anchoring CaMKII holoenzymes to F‐actin within dendritic spines 19 and by influencing GABAergic interneuron activity. 20 Loss or dysfunction of CaMKII, including the β isoform, disrupts this delicate balance, leading to hyperexcitability and aberrant network synchronization. 17 Together, these findings indicate that CaMKIIβ insufficiency may lead to cortical network instability and abnormal oscillatory dynamics that underlie seizure susceptibility.

In this study, we investigated whether CaMKIIβ insufficiency caused by the P213L variant disrupts cortical network activity and increases seizure susceptibility in vivo.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Hamamatsu University School of Medicine (approval number: 25‐011), and conducted in accordance with the ethical guidelines of the Japan Neuroscience Society and the Ministry of Education, Culture, Sports, Science and Technology (MEXT), Japan.

Mice were housed under standard laboratory conditions (24°C, 12‐h light/dark cycle) with ad libitum access to food and water. Generation of CaMKIIβ P213L knock‐in (KI) and CaMKIIβ knockout (KO) mice was described previously. 16 Genotyping was conducted using high‐resolution melt analysis (Eco 48 real‐time PCR system, PCRmax) with the following primer set for both KI and KO mice: forward, 5'‐GCGCAAAGGGACACTGAGGACTGAC‐3′ and reverse, 5'‐ACTCACATCATACGCCCCAGCCTTGA‐3′.

Anesthesia protocols were selected according to the purpose of the procedure. For electrode implantation surgery for electroencephalogram (EEG) recordings, isoflurane anesthesia was induced at 3% and maintained at 1.5%–2%, delivered via a nose cone, as described previously. 21 For terminal tissue collection used in western blot analysis, mice were deeply anesthetized with an intraperitoneal injection of a three‐drug mixture of medetomidine (.3 mg/kg), midazolam (4.0 mg/kg), and butorphanol (5.0 mg/kg). 22

EEG electrode implantation, recording, and analysis

Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane, and a midline scalp incision was made to expose the skull. Small gold pin electrodes (1‐mm) were affixed to the skull surface using dental cement, with recording electrodes placed over the left and right sensorimotor cortices and a reference electrode positioned over the cerebellum. A stainless‐steel ground electrode was positioned over the interparietal bone, secured with dental cement, and connected to the fixation apparatus for head stabilization; no craniotomy or drilling was performed.

After surgery, mice were placed on a heating pad and monitored until fully awake (at least 1 h). During the postoperative period, animals were monitored for signs of pain or distress (e.g., reduced activity, abnormal behavior or posture, and wound abnormalities). Postoperative analgesics were not administered routinely because the primary outcome measures included cortical EEG and seizure susceptibility, which could be confounded by such pharmacological interventions. A rescue plan was in place: analgesia would have been provided as needed if signs consistent with pain or distress had been observed, and humane endpoints were applied to prevent undue suffering. No animals in this study required rescue analgesia or early euthanasia. Mice underwent a recovery period of at least 7 days, and EEG recordings were initiated only after confirming the absence of abnormal behavior or postoperative complications.

EEG recordings were performed in awake, head‐fixed mice to ensure recording stability during PTZ‐induced seizures and to reduce movement‐related artifacts. Each mouse generally underwent two EEG sessions: a brief habituation/connection session on the day prior to the main experiment and the main recording session on the experimental day. For compounds with longer‐lasting effects (e.g., diazepam or valproate), an additional follow‐up EEG session was performed in a subset of animals on the following day to confirm re‐emergence of aberrant low‐gamma oscillations after drug effects had resolved. During EEG recordings, mice were continuously video‐monitored to determine behavioral state (resting immobility vs active behavior).

EEG signals were amplified (×1000; bandpass filter, .1–500 Hz; HAS‐4 head amplifier system, Bio Research Center Co. Ltd.) and digitized at 4 kHz using a PowerLab 4/35 system (AD Instruments). EEG data were analyzed using LabChart 8 (AD Instruments) and OriginPro 2020b (LightStone). For spectral analysis, a .5–100 Hz bandpass filter was applied offline, and fast Fourier transform (FFT) was used to generate time–frequency spectrograms and power spectral density (PSD) plots.

For spectral quantification, artifact‐free EEG segments obtained during resting immobility were analyzed using FFT. Continuous segments (typically≥30 s) were selected to improve signal‐to‐noise ratio and to obtain stable estimates of peak frequency and peak power; this segment length was chosen for estimation reliability and was not used as a detection criterion. Spectra were used either in raw form or normalized to the total power, depending on the analysis. Peak frequency and peak power within the 20–50 Hz band were quantified from the PSD for group comparisons.

Low‐gamma activity was not detected as discrete oscillatory “events” using amplitude or duration thresholds. Instead, aberrant low‐gamma oscillations were identified as a sustained enhancement of spectral power within the 20–50 Hz range during resting immobility, evident in time–frequency spectrograms, and as a reproducible spectral peak in the PSD. Thus, no amplitude or duration criteria were applied to individual EEG waveforms for identifying gamma oscillations.

For state‐dependent analyses (Figure 2), resting immobility was additionally confirmed using electromyography (EMG) and a piezoelectric floor sensor. EEG segments were extracted from periods with no detectable EMG activity or movement, whereas active‐state segments were extracted from periods with clear EMG and movement signals.

The exact numbers used in each EEG analysis are summarized in Table S1.

Drug administration and PTZ‐induced seizure

To examine whether the abnormal low‐gamma oscillations reflect altered E/I balance, we selected pharmacological agents known to either reduce inhibitory tone (PTZ, a GABAA receptor antagonist) or enhance GABAergic inhibition (diazepam, a positive allosteric modulator of GABAA receptors; valproic acid, which enhances GABAergic function through multiple mechanisms; and low‐dose isoflurane, which potentiates GABAA receptor‐mediated inhibition). Isoflurane was additionally chosen because it enables rapid and reversible modulation with precise dose titration, allowing assessment of acute suppression and recovery of the oscillatory phenotype. Together, these agents allowed us to probe the sensitivity of the oscillatory phenotype to bidirectional pharmacological modulation of GABAergic function.

All pharmacological experiments were conducted under head‐fixed conditions to evaluate the effects of each drug on the aberrant low‐gamma oscillations observed in CaMKIIβ P213L KI and KO mice. Drug‐free baseline EEG was recorded for at least 30 min before administration to establish a physiological reference state. PTZ (35 or 70 mg/kg, i.p.) was used not only to induce seizures but also to assess how GABAA receptor blockade modulates aberrant low‐gamma oscillations. EEG spectral changes following PTZ administration were analyzed in parallel with behavioral seizure severity, which was scored using a modified Racine scale: Stage 2, short myoclonic jerk; Stage 3, continuous myoclonic jerks, axial rigidity; Stage 4, forelimb/hindlimb clonus; Stage 5, tonic–clonic seizures; and Stage 6, tonic convulsion, death. PTZ‐induced seizures were quantified behaviorally using synchronized video and a modified Racine scale under head‐fixed conditions. Seizure counts were defined as the number of discrete behavioral seizure events identified from synchronized video recordings (Racine stage ≥ 2). Frequent brief myoclonic jerks were counted individually, and continuous episodes could not always be grouped as a single seizure episode. Due to head‐fixed recording, immobility (Stage 1) was excluded from behavioral analysis. EEG was recorded simultaneously throughout PTZ administration to examine PTZ‐related modulation of aberrant low‐gamma activity.

Isoflurane, diazepam, and valproic acid were administered during EEG recordings without PTZ challenge to assess modulation of aberrant low‐gamma oscillations rather than anti‐seizure efficacy. To evaluate pharmacological modulation of aberrant low‐gamma oscillations, mice were treated with isoflurane (.5%, inhalation), diazepam (1 mg/kg, i.p.), or valproic acid (30 or 300 mg/kg, i.p.). EEG was recorded continuously throughout all pharmacological experiments. In all experiments, a baseline EEG recording of ≥30 min was obtained before drug administration. For isoflurane experiments, EEG was recorded during the 5‐min exposure and for ≥1 h after the end of exposure. For PTZ and diazepam experiments, EEG was recorded for ≥2 h after intraperitoneal injection. In the valproic acid experiment, EEG was recorded for 20 min after the first intraperitoneal injection (30 mg/kg), followed by a second injection (300 mg/kg), after which EEG recording continued for ≥1 h.

For PTZ‐induced seizure experiments in KI mice (Figure 3 and Figure S2), WT, Het KI, and Homo KI groups each included n = 5 animals. For pharmacological modulation experiments without PTZ challenge in KI mice (Figure 4 and Figure S4), WT, Het KI, and Homo KI groups each included n = 5 animals, except for the WT valproic acid group (n = 4). For KO validation experiments (Figure S5), WT, Het KO, and Homo KO groups each included n = 3 animals. The exact numbers used in each individual analysis are summarized in Table S1.

Western blotting

Western blotting was performed as described previously. 16 Cortical proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate‐polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE), transferred to Poly Vinyli dene Fluoride (PVDF) membranes, and probed with antibodies against CaMKIIβ (1:1000, GeneTex, #GTX133072), phospho‐CaMKII (Thr286/287) (1:2000, Cell Signaling Technology, #12716), CaMKIIα (1:1000, CST, #50049), and glyceraldehyde‐3‐phosphatedehydrogenase (GAPDH) (1:50 000, Proteintech, #60004‐1‐Ig). Signals were detected using horseradish peroxidase (HRP)–conjugated secondary antibodies and ECL substrate (Bio‐Rad), and band intensities were quantified using ImageJ2.

Western blot analyses of cortical lysates were performed using n = 5 animals per group in both the KI cohort (WT, Het KI, and Homo KI) and the KO cohort (WT, Het KO, and Homo KO).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using OriginPro 2020b (LightStone Corp., Japan). Data were compared using Student's t‐test, one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's post hoc test, or their non‐parametric equivalents (Mann–Whitney U test), as appropriate. Because no a priori sample size calculation or power analysis was performed, this study should be regarded as exploratory rather than confirmatory. Accordingly, p‐values are reported to aid interpretation of the observed group differences, but they should be interpreted cautiously and not as definitive confirmatory evidence. A p‐value <.05 was considered statistically significant. Data in graphs are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM).

Sample sizes were determined based on our previous experience with similar experimental paradigms and on prior studies in the field, including our previous reports. 16 , 21 The numbers of animals used for each genotype and for each analysis are summarized in Table S1.

RESULTS

CaMKIIβ P213L knock‐in mice exhibit aberrant low‐gamma oscillations

To determine whether the epileptiform activity observed in a patient with the CaMKIIβ P213L mutation 9 could be reproduced in a mouse model, we conducted EEG recordings in CaMKIIβ P213L heterozygous (Het) and homozygous (Homo) knock‐in (KI) mice. Compared to wild‐type (WT) controls, both Het and Homo KI mice exhibited a pronounced increase in aberrant low‐gamma oscillations (20–50 Hz) during wakeful resting states (Figure 1A–C). Gamma oscillations are classically defined within the 20–100 Hz frequency range. In this study, we focus on the 20–50 Hz band, referred to as low‐gamma oscillations, which were selectively augmented in KI mice.

Distinct aberrant gamma oscillation peaks were observed in Het KI (n = 16, 34.75 ± 1.76 Hz) and Homo KI (n = 9, 26.90 ± 1.48 Hz) mice, and were statistically compared using the Mann–Whitney U test (****p < .0001). Time‐frequency and PSD analyses further revealed that Het and Homo KI mice displayed distinct abnormal peaks within this low‐gamma range: Het KI mice showed a peak at 30–40 Hz, whereas Homo KI mice exhibited a lower‐frequency peak at 20–30 Hz (Figure 1D,E). This genotype‐dependent shift suggests that the severity of the CaMKIIβ P213L variant may influence the characteristics of the aberrant low‐gamma oscillations.

Furthermore, state‐dependent differences in EEG activity were identified. In Het KI mice, aberrant low‐gamma oscillations were observed predominantly during resting states, whereas spectral abnormalities were nearly absent during active states (Figure 2). Notably, Homo KI mice exhibited a bimodal distribution of aberrant low‐gamma oscillation frequencies, with peaks clustering around 26.90 ± 1.48 Hz and 23.50 ± .45 Hz (Figure S1).

These findings indicate that aberrant low‐gamma oscillations were observed persistently in CaMKIIβ P213L KI mice during the resting state, even in the absence of overt seizures or classical epileptiform discharges such as spike‐and‐wave complexes, suggesting altered cortical network synchronization and E/I balance.

Excitatory–inhibitory imbalance in CaMKIIβ P213L knock‐in mice

To further probe the mechanisms underlying the aberrant low‐gamma oscillations, we examined their sensitivity to agents that increase inhibitory tone (isoflurane, diazepam, and valproic acid) under normal EEG recording conditions (without PTZ challenge).

Following intraperitoneal administration of PTZ (35 mg/kg), Het and Homo KI mice exhibited a downward shift in the frequency of the aberrant gamma oscillation peaks, as seen in spectrograms (Figure 3A,B), and a corresponding leftward shift in PSD plots (Figure S2A,B), whereas WT mice remained unaffected. Behaviorally, this dose of PTZ significantly increased both seizure frequency and severity, as reflected by elevated Racine scores in KI mice relative to WT mice (Figure 3C).

At a higher PTZ dose (70 mg/kg), the aberrant low‐gamma oscillations in KI mice were further shifted to lower frequencies, accompanied by a marked exacerbation in seizure activity (Figure 3D–F, Figure S2C,D). Within the defined 30‐min post‐injection observation window, WT and Het KI mice were monitored for the full duration. In contrast, most Homo KI mice developed rapidly progressive tonic–clonic seizures, leading to premature termination of behavioral observation. Although the total number of seizures appeared lower in Homo KI mice due to early mortality, seizure frequency was numerically higher than in WT and Het KI mice. One Homo KI mouse survived the full 30‐min period and was analyzed over the entire observation window. Because early mortality in Homo KI mice resulted in systematically shortened observation periods, statistical comparisons at this dose were restricted to WT and Het KI mice, which were evaluated under equivalent 30‐min conditions. Homo KI data are presented separately to illustrate the severity of the phenotype. Strikingly, four of five Homo KI mice exhibited fatal tonic seizures within 10 min of administration, a phenomenon not observed in either WT or Het KI mice.

To further probe the pathophysiological basis of the aberrant low‐gamma oscillations, we assessed the effects of various agents that enhance inhibitory neurotransmission. Inhalation of 0.5% isoflurane (ISO), a condition under which animals retained voluntary motor responses (Figure S3) and responded to brief tactile stimuli, completely abolished the aberrant low‐gamma oscillations in KI mice, with activity resuming promptly upon cessation of ISO exposure (Figure 4A, Figure S4A,D). Intraperitoneal administration of diazepam (DZP, 1 mg/kg) and valproic acid (VPA, 30 or 300 mg/kg) similarly attenuated the aberrant low‐gamma oscillations, with VPA showing dose‐dependent suppression. These effects were sustained for several hours (Figure 4B,C; Figure S4B–D).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that CaMKIIβ P213L KI mice display increased susceptibility to PTZ‐induced seizures and exhibit aberrant low‐gamma oscillations sensitive to GABAergic modulation. These findings suggest that the aberrant low‐gamma oscillations in KI mice reflect an E/I imbalance within cortical circuits.

CaMKIIβ insufficiency causes aberrant cortical EEG activity

In our previous study, we reported a genotype‐dependent and graded reduction of CaMKIIα and CaMKIIβ expression and phosphorylation levels in the cerebellum of CaMKIIβ P213L KI mice. 16 Based on these findings, we next examined whether similar changes occur in the cortex. At the functional level, we next asked whether the aberrant low‐gamma oscillations observed in CaMKIIβ P213L KI mice are attributable to CaMKIIβ insufficiency itself. To address this, we examined EEG recordings from CaMKIIβ KO mice. As shown in Figure 6A–D, both Het and Homo KO mice displayed the aberrant low‐gamma oscillations characterized by a single, prominent peak within the early gamma frequency range (30–50 Hz) during resting states (Figure S5). Consistent with this, pharmacological modulation of the aberrant low‐gamma oscillations revealed a striking concordance between CaMKIIβ P213L KI and KO mice. Isoflurane suppressed the aberrant low‐gamma oscillations in both Het and Homo KO mice (Figure S5A,B), and PTZ administration in Het KO mice induced a shift of the aberrant low‐gamma oscillations toward lower frequencies (Figure S5C), similar to the shift observed in KI mice. These findings reinforce that the observed aberrant low‐gamma oscillations in KI mice are caused primarily by CaMKIIβ insufficiency, rather than by the P213L variant itself.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we provide in vivo evidence supporting an association between the CaMKIIβ P213L variant and cortical network dysfunction, highlighting its clinical relevance. Using a KI mouse model, we found that heterozygous KI mice, in which CaMKIIβ was reduced by ~25%, and showed aberrant low‐gamma oscillations and increased vulnerability to PTZ‐induced seizure induction, suggesting that CaMKIIβ insufficiency would underlie the patient's phenotype. These findings establish a novel role for CaMKIIβ in maintaining cortical oscillatory stability and offer a mechanistic connection between CAMK2B‐associated neurodevelopmental disorders and epileptic phenotypes. Previous studies have reported phenotypic abnormalities in CaMKIIβ KO and KI mice across various functional domains, including neuroplasticity, 19 , 23 motor function, 16 , 24 , 25 learning, 26 , 27 and sleep. 28 However, these abnormalities were observed exclusively in homozygous models. Of note, our study is the first to demonstrate that heterozygous CaMKIIβ P213L KI and KO mice exhibit distinct and reproducible phenotypic abnormalities. This finding indicates that even a partial reduction in CaMKIIβ levels is associated with altered cortical network activity and with persistent aberrant low‐gamma oscillations. These oscillations suggest changes in cortical network synchronization and E/I balance, underscoring CaMKIIβ insufficiency in CAMK2B‐related disorders, although their causal contribution to seizure generation remains to be determined.

Two previous studies recorded EEG in homozygous CaMKIIβ KO mice, 28 , 29 but neither reported the aberrant low‐gamma oscillations. This discrepancy may reflect methodological differences, including the timing of gene deletion, recording conditions, and EEG frequency ranges analyzed.

For instance, Kool et al. 29 used tamoxifen‐inducible, CaMKIIα/β double KO mice in which gene deletion occurred during adulthood. This temporal specificity suggests that CaMKIIβ is particularly critical during early brain development, when the establishment of E/I balance is highly dynamic. Consistent with this interpretation, embryonic knockdown of CaMKIIβ has been shown to disrupt cortical neuron migration, 30 underscoring its developmental importance. In addition, the simultaneous deletion of CaMKIIα in Kool et al. may have further altered neural circuit properties, potentially masking the aberrant low‐gamma oscillations attributable specifically to CaMKIIβ deficiency. Of note, Kool et al. also performed recordings under head‐fixed conditions in CaMKIIα/β double KO mice but did not detect any aberrant low‐gamma oscillations. This observation indicates that head fixation itself is unlikely to artificially generate or enhance the aberrant oscillations identified in our study. Instead, the absence of aberrant low‐gamma oscillations in Kool et al. is more plausibly explained by developmental timing and genetic context rather than by the recording conditions.

By contrast, Tone et al. 28 recorded EEG from CaMKIIβ KO mice under free‐moving conditions. The aberrant low‐gamma oscillations we observed in CaMKIIβ P213L KI mice appears to be brain‐state dependent and is most prominent during behavioral rest, when electromyographic (EMG) activity is minimal (Figure 2). Therefore, head fixation may provide a stable recording environment that facilitates the detection of subtle aberrant low‐gamma oscillations. Tone et al. focused on sleep research and thus limited their analysis to low‐frequency EEG activity. These differences in research focus and analytic range suggest that methodological factors must also be considered. Furthermore, their recordings used a narrower filter range (.5–60 Hz), whereas ours covered a wider range (.1–500 Hz). They also employed a lower sampling rate (100 or 128 Hz), compared to our higher rate (4 kHz). In the analysis step, a band‐pass filter was applied (1–50 Hz) that was narrower than in our approach (.5–100 Hz), which may have hindered the detection of aberrant low‐gamma oscillations in CaMKIIβ KO mice. Collectively, these methodological considerations offer a reasonable explanation for the discrepancy between our consistent detection of aberrant low‐gamma oscillations in CaMKIIβ KI and KO mice and their absence in previous studies.

As a potential molecular basis for the aberrant low‐gamma oscillations, we assessed regional expression and phosphorylation levels of CaMKII isoforms. Our analyses revealed that CaMKIIβ expression is reduced across all examined brain regions in KI mice, including the cortex (Figure 5B), hippocampus, and cerebellum. 16 In contrast, CaMKIIα levels were largely preserved in the cortex and hippocampus but were significantly diminished in the cerebellum. This vulnerability pattern appears to reflect the endogenous stoichiometry of CaMKII isoforms, with the cerebellum being β‐dominant and the forebrain α‐dominant. 31

Given that CaMKII holoenzymes assemble as CaMKII α/β heteromers, incorporation of mutant CaMKIIβ subunits may destabilize the entire complex. This effect appears most pronounced in CaMKIIβ‐dominant regions such as the cerebellum, potentially leading to co‐degradation of both CaMKII isoforms. 16 By contrast, in α‐dominant regions like the cortex and hippocampus, holoenzyme integrity seems to be preserved despite partial loss of CaMKIIβ, resulting in relatively stable CaMKIIα protein levels. Notably, however, phosphorylation of CaMKIIα at Thr286 was significantly reduced in the cortex of Homo KI mice, despite unaltered total CaMKIIα expression. This finding suggests that mutant CaMKIIβ subunits may interfere with kinase activation dynamics, possibly by impairing subunit interaction or autophosphorylation propagation within the complex. 32 , 33 Of interest, this phosphorylation deficit of CaMKIIα at Thr286 was absent in KO mice, despite the presence of similar aberrant low‐gamma oscillations in cortical EEG activity. This observation suggests that the reduction in CaMKIIα phosphorylation is not a causative factor in the observed network dysfunction. Likewise, changes in CaMKIIβ phosphorylation are unlikely to be causative. Similar aberrant low‐gamma oscillations were also observed in CaMKIIβ KO mice, which lack the protein entirely.

More fundamentally, both KI and KO mice share a more common molecular abnormality, reduction in CaMKIIβ expression, which likely impairs its actin‐binding ability and, consequently, disrupts the synaptic anchoring of the CaMKII holoenzyme complex. CaMKIIβ plays a crucial role in tethering the holoenzyme to F‐actin in dendritic spines. 33 , 34 , 35 Disruption of this synaptic localization is expected to impair localized CaMKII signaling, potentially leading to synaptic destabilization and an imbalance in E/I transmission. Notably, CaMKIIβ is expressed not only in excitatory neurons but also in inhibitory interneurons, such as (parvalbumin‐positive) PV+, (somatostatin‐positive) SST+, and (vasoactive intestinal peptide‐positive) VIP+ subtypes, where it plays essential roles despite being expressed at lower levels. 31 In this context, inhibitory interneurons, particularly PV+ cells, play a key role in regulating gamma oscillations. 36 , 37 Dysfunction of PV+ interneurons has also been reported to enhance gamma oscillations and increase hippocampal excitability, suggesting a link to seizure susceptibility. 20 Consistent with previous reports linking interneuron dysfunction to gamma dysregulation, our pharmacological findings further support a role for altered inhibitory control: the aberrant low‐gamma oscillations in CaMKIIβ P213L KI and KO mice are highly sensitive to GABA receptor modulation. The selection of pharmacological compounds was guided by the hypothesis that these oscillatory abnormalities arise from altered E/I balance. Thus, modulation of GABAergic tone was used to examine the mechanistic contribution of inhibitory dysfunction to the observed oscillatory phenotype rather than to evaluate anti‐seizure efficacy.

Collectively, these findings provide converging molecular, electrophysiological, and pharmacological evidence that CaMKIIβ insufficiency impairs neural circuit function, leading to the aberrant low‐gamma oscillations and increased susceptibility to PTZ‐induced seizures.

Because PTZ assays seizure susceptibility through GABAA receptor antagonism, the present findings should be interpreted as increased susceptibility specifically to PTZ‐induced seizures and may not necessarily generalize to seizures triggered by other mechanisms. Future studies employing complementary seizure‐induction paradigms, such as electrical stimulation/kindling or alternative chemoconvulsants, will be important to determine the generalizability of these results.

Although further studies are needed to elucidate the synaptic and circuit‐level mechanisms, these KI mice represent a promising platform for future mechanistic investigations and therapeutic development targeting CAMK2B‐related neurodevelopmental disorders.

An important limitation of this study is that no a priori sample size calculation or power analysis was performed. Therefore, the present work should be interpreted as exploratory rather than confirmatory. Although probability‐based statistics are reported to support interpretation of the observed group differences, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and require confirmation in future adequately powered studies.

CONCLUSION

Our study suggests that CaMKIIβ P213L variant impairs cortical E/I balance, resulting in the aberrant low‐gamma oscillations and increased seizure susceptibility. These pathological features are reproduced in CaMKIIβ knockout mice and are associated with similar pharmacological responses to GABAergic modulation, supporting a shared mechanism of CaMKIIβ insufficiency. Taken together, our findings establish a direct mechanistic link between CAMK2B variants and epilepsy‐related network dysfunction, and introduce a translationally valuable in vivo platform for dissecting disease mechanisms and exploring targeted therapies in CaMKIIβ‐associated neurodevelopmental disorders.
