A bone histological approach to distinguish periprosthetic joint infection from aseptic osteolysis in revision joint replacement
Authors: Dzenita Muratovic, Ryan D. Quarrington, Dongqing Yang, Masakazu Kogawa, Renjy Nelson, Boopalan Ramasamy, Lucian Bogdan Solomon, Gerald J. Atkins
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimsAccurate differentiation between periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) and aseptic osteolysis (AO) remains a major clinical challenge in revision joint arthroplasty. We examined bone biopsies for alterations in bone matrix integrity and the osteocyte lacunocanalicular network in order to differentiate between these pathologies.MethodsBone biopsies were taken from areas of osteolysis in patients who underwent revision arthroplasty for either PJI, diagnosed according to 2018 International Consensus Meeting criteria, or AO. Controls were obtained during primary arthroplasty. Biopsies were assessed histologically by Masson’s trichrome, silver and RGB trichrome staining, and for osteocyte lacunocanalicular characteristics. The diagnostic potential of histological measures was investigated using univariate area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) analysis.ResultsRGB trichrome staining was most effective for revealing both bone matrix and lacunocanalicular features. Osteocyte lacunar area and circularity were increased in PJI relative to both AO and Control bone. PJI bone exhibited reduced canalicular density compared to AO (p < 0.001), and shorter, narrower canaliculi than AO and Controls. Predictive univariate modelling revealed that all histological parameters measured except lacunar area were potentially diagnostic for PJI. Degraded bone matrix differentially predicted PJI (AUROC = 0.875, 88% sensitivity, 80% specificity) and AO (AUROC = 0.733, 63% sensitivity, 76% specificity), as did lacunar circularity (PJI: AUROC = 0.903, 75% sensitivity, 96% specificity; AO: AUROC = 0.797, 69% sensitivity, 84% specificity). Canalicular area fraction, length, and width all predicted PJI (AUROCs = 0.915, 0.982, 0.883), with canalicular length yielding 94% sensitivity and 100% specificity. Canalicular density differentially predicted PJI (AUROC = 0.770, 69% sensitivity, 80% specificity) and AO (AUROC = 0.757, 88% sensitivity, 60% specificity).ConclusionHistological assessment of bone matrix degradation and the osteocyte lacunocanalicular network reveals new potential diagnostic markers for PJI to support clinical decision-making and provides novel measures for distinguishing PJI from AO, which may be particularly useful in cases of low-grade and chronic infections. Based on these findings, larger confirmatory studies are warranted.Cite this article: Bone Joint Res 2026;15(8):973–986.

Introduction

Joint arthroplasty surgery, particularly for the hip and knee, is considered to be a highly successful treatment for end-stage osteoarthritis. However, major complications requiring revision surgery are a growing concern as the number of joint arthroplasties increases.1 Failure of joint arthroplasty is often associated with periprosthetic osteolysis, typically diagnosed using a range of imaging techniques.2 Critical to patient management is determining whether osteolysis resulted from periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) or was aseptic osteolysis (AO), a common feature of aseptic loosening (AsL). Distinguishing between these causes remains challenging. Up to 20-40% of PJIs are culture-negative,3,4 and some cases initially presumed to be aseptic are later confirmed to be infected.

PJI is diagnosed using a number of systems of criteria, including those published by the Musculoskeletal Infection Society (MSIS) in 2011,5 the updated MSIS criteria resulting from the 2018 International Consensus Meeting (ICM) findings,6 and the European Bone and Joint Infection Society (EBJIS) guidelines,7 among others. All of these definitions allow PJI to be diagnosed with the presence of single major criteria, culture of the same organism from at least two tissue samples, or the presence of a sinus tract communicating with the prosthesis. In the absence of major criteria, a suite of minor criteria, mostly related to blood or soft-tissue histological markers of inflammation, make up scoring algorithms of various degrees of likelihood of PJI. However, no single definition of PJI is universally accepted or used. Significant challenges remain in the diagnosis and management of PJI when major diagnosis criteria are absent. For example, in up to 20% to 40% of PJI cases, a pathogen cannot be identified using conventional culture.3,4 Furthermore, evidence suggests that at least some aseptic revisions may be undiagnosed, low-grade PJI.8 While identification of the causative organism in PJI was reported to be significantly improved using molecular approaches,9 failure to cure PJI remains very high at up to 46% at two years.10,11 These challenges underscore the urgent need for innovative approaches to enhance diagnosis and understanding of bone pathobiology.

While bone involvement, including osteomyelitis, is a key determinant of outcomes in PJI,12 hard bone histology is not routinely considered in current PJI diagnostic workflows. Within this framework, the term ‘bone biopsy’ is inconsistently applied and often refers to periprosthetic soft-tissue rather than bone.13 Either frozen or formalin-fixed sections of this generally soft tissue are commonly assessed for infiltrates of neutrophils, quantified as the number of polymorphonuclear cells (PMN) per high power field (HPF) using several scoring systems, where at least 5 PMN in five or more HPFs (> 5 PMN/5 HPF) is generally considered the best-performing histological criterion of infection,14,15 and indeed, the EBJIS recommendations assert that this single measure should also be considered definitive for infection.7 A recent meta-analysis from the 2025 ICM found that the specific measure of > 5 PMN/5 HPF performed well across reported studies, exhibiting 82.0% sensitivity and 94.7% sensitivity,15 while there is recognition that performance is better in early infections rather than chronic PJI.14

Bone histomorphology is governed by the activities of bone-resorbing osteoclasts, bone-forming osteoblasts, and the regulatory functions of osteocytes. Osteocyte perilacunocanalicular remodelling, otherwise known as osteocytic osteolysis, is a recognized means by which the osteocyte network regulates the mineral and organic phases of the surrounding bone matrix in both physiology and pathology.16,17 AO was found to involve osteolytic activities of osteocytes in response to wear debris from implants, in addition to the activities of myeloid lineage cells, primarily macrophages and osteoclasts.18-21 Recently, extensive bone matrix degradation surrounding osteocytes in bone biopsies taken from PJI patients has been reported,22,23 irrespective of the infective organism identified.22 The suitability of this observation for PJI diagnosis remains untested in larger, blinded studies. Furthermore, there is currently no hard bone tissue-based histological test available that can differentiate between PJI and AO-related failure of joint arthroplasties.8

In this study, we investigated potential histological differences between PJI and AO hard bone tissue, comparing them with each other and with control bone from patients undergoing a primary joint arthroplasty. A number of histological features were identified related to bone matrix integrity and also to the osteocyte lacunocanalicular network. We introduce a new diagnostic platform that combines an existing RGB trichrome stain with quantitative image analysis that may allow differential diagnosis of AO and PJI.

Methods

Participants and bone biopsies

This prospective study received institutional human research ethics approval (Approval No. 14466). All participants provided prior informed written consent. Intraoperative bone trephine and/or small osteotome biopsies, each generally measuring greater than 50 mm3, were collected. For all revision cases, periprosthetic bone samples were collected by harvesting bone pieces that would have been removed as part of the debridement or preparation of the acetabular, femoral or tibial bone to insert the new prostheses. As such, collecting the bone samples did not increase the risk of fracture. For the controls, the bone samples were collected either from parts of the bone removed for the routine preparation of the joint for implant insertion (e.g. femoral neck osteotomy for removal of the femoral head in total hip arthroplasty) or by using a trephine before drilling to insert an acetabular screw to augment the acetabular component. Biopsies were obtained from three distinct groups:PJI Group: Patients undergoing first- or single-stage revision total hip or knee arthroplasty for a confirmed PJI, as defined by the updated MSIS criteria from the 2018 ICM.6 All patients in this group had symptoms for more than four weeks prior to recruitment and thus were considered to be chronic cases.24AO Group: Patients undergoing revision hip or knee arthroplasty with radiological evidence of osteolysis, without clinical signs of infection and negative laboratory, microbiological, and histological findings. A diagnosis of aseptic failure was made only after thorough exclusion of PJI according to the updated MSIS criteria.6Control (CTRL) Group: Patients undergoing primary total hip or knee arthroplasty for end-stage osteoarthritis. These individuals had no signs of infection or prior joint arthroplasty and served as the reference for baseline bone morphology. From each patient, three to five bone samples were collected intraoperatively into separate tubes containing sterile saline and refrigerated at 4°C before processing. In total, 126 bone biopsies were obtained from the 41 participants. These included 31 biopsies from nine control patients, 50 from 16 AO cases, and 45 from 16 PJI cases.

Bone biopsy processing

Within 24 hours of collection, bone biopsies were fixed for 24 hours in 10% buffered formalin and demineralized in an ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA)-based decalcifying solution (Osteosoft, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) on a rocking platform at 4°C. Decalcification was monitored by X-ray imaging (Faxitron, Tucson, USA). Following decalcification, samples were processed for standard paraffin-embedding and sectioning.

Histological analysis

Sequential sections of each bone sample were stained using three different methods. Masson’s trichrome staining involved dewaxing, dehydration, and then sequentially staining with Mayer’s iron haematoxylin, 1% Biebrich scarlet-acid fuchsin, 2.5% phosphomolybdic-phosphotungstic acid solution, and 3% Aniline Blue, as previously described.22 The Ploton Silver Stain method to highlight the osteocyte lacunocanalicular network was used as reported.25 This involved dewaxing and rehydrating slides with graded ethanol and water. Slides were then incubated in a freshly prepared silver solution for 60 minutes in the dark to enhance tissue contrast. Following this, the stain was stabilized with 5% sodium thiosulphate, and the slides were dehydrated and mounted with coverslips for imaging. RGB trichrome stain involved dewaxing, dehydration, and then sequential staining with 1% Alcian Blue in 3% aqueous acetic acid solution (pH 2.5), 0.04% Fast Green, 0.1% Sirius Red in aqueous solution of Picric acid solution and differentiation in 1% acetic acid solution.26 All stained sections were imaged with a NanoZoomer scanner (Hamamatsu Photonics, Japan) and then images were analyzed using Fiji software (Open source, written in JAVA).

To assess bone matrix degradation, RGB trichrome images were processed using colour deconvolution to separate into red-green-blue channels, as previously described.22 Bone matrix degradation was quantified as the proportion of degraded collagen expressed as a percentage of total tissue area. Physiological osteoid appeared as a layer of newly formed non-mineralized bone along trabecular surfaces. Degraded or abnormal matrix was identified as irregular, diffuse regions distributed throughout the bone section, without the organized spatial pattern characteristic of osteoid formation.

For analysis of osteocyte lacunocanalicular morphology, image segmentation was performed using the Trainable Weka Segmentation plugin, which applies supervised machine learning to classify pixel features of objects.27 The classifier was trained to identify osteocyte lacunae (class 1) and canaliculi (class 2), generating separate binary masks for each compartment (Figure 1).

Workflow diagram for red-green-blue (RGB) trichrome-based histological image analysis of bone tissue. Images were acquired from stained sections, as described in the Methods, and pre-processed for brightness and contrast. Bone matrix degradation was assessed using colour deconvolution in Fiji software (Analysis 1). Osteocyte lacunocanalicular morphology was quantified using supervised segmentation via Trainable Weka Segmentation software (v. 3.3.4) in Fiji (Analysis 2), with class-specific masks generated for lacunae (yellow) and canaliculi (purple) for particle analysis.

Quantitative histomorphometry was subsequently performed on the segmented images. Osteocyte lacunae and canaliculi were analyzed separately using the ‘Particle Analysis’ tool, which enabled measurement of geometrical, morphological, and density-related parameters, including area, circularity, length, width, density, and areal fraction. A full list of extracted parameters, along with their acronyms, definitions, and units of measurement, is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS v. 28 (IBM, USA). Patient demographics were analyzed for differences in sex distribution using chi-squared tests for independence and in mean age using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Separate multivariate general linear regression models (GLMs) were developed to evaluate whether each bone matrix parameter differed by sample patient group (CTRL, AO, or PJI) or by patient demographics (sex and age). Each model was refined by iteratively removing non-significant demographic variables (sex and/or age) via a backwards stepwise approach (α = 0.05). Residual-versus-measured and residual-versus-predicted value scatterplots were generated for each refined GLM to confirm the assumptions of linearity and homoscedasticity. When patient group effects were significant, Bonferroni-adjusted post-hoc comparisons of estimated marginal means (EMMs) were conducted to identify specific differences between patient group pairs.

Univariate binary logistic regression models were then developed to evaluate the ability of each statistically significant parameter from the GLM analyses to distinguish between: 1) PJI and non-PJI (AO or CTRL) samples; and 2) AO and non-AO (PJI or CTRL) samples. As stated above, PJI diagnosis was based on the updated MSIS criteria from the 2018 ICM,6 while AO diagnosis was made following exclusion of these criteria. Separate models were constructed for each parameter, with PJI or AO diagnosis as the binary outcome variable. Model performance was assessed by calculating odds ratios (OR) with 95% CIs to estimate the strength of association between each parameter and the likelihood of PJI or AO. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses were conducted to evaluate the discriminatory performance of each parameter, with the area under the curve (AUROC) reported as a measure of classification accuracy; AUROC values were classified as follows: 0.5 = no discrimination (random chance); 0.2 to 0.3 or 0.7 to 0.8 = acceptable; 0.1 to 0.2 or 0.8 to 0.9 = excellent; and, < 0.1 or > 0.9 = outstanding discrimination.28 The respective optimal cut-off values for each measure were determined by Youden’s Index analysis from ROC curve coordinates.

Results

Participant demographics, microbiology, and samples

There were no significant differences in sex distribution between groups. Mean age between CTRL + AO and CTRL + PJI groups was not different, however the patients in the AO group were significantly older than the PJI group (p = 0.019, one-way ANOVA) (Table I).

A total of 11/16 of the PJI group were deemed ‘culture-positive’, growing identical organisms from at least two samples: 4/16 (25%) grew Staphylococcus aureus, 4/16 (25%) grew coagulase-negative Staphylococcus spp., 1/16 grew definitive vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus faecium; two patients grew definitive Gram-negative cultures, Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Morganella morganii (Table II). Just two of the culture-positive cohort (18%) also had a sinus tract communicating with their prosthesis. Of the remaining five patients, one had a sinus tract communicating with the prosthesis. The four patients presenting without a single major criterion all fulfilled the PJI definition by a combination of a single positive culture, a positive histological score of > 5 PMN/5 HPF from fixed or frozen biopsies, elevated serum CRP (Table II), ESR, white cell count (WCC), or intraoperative purulence.

RGB trichrome stain most effectively reveals the OLCN and bone matrix composition

Masson’s trichrome staining (Figure 2) revealed zones of intact and degraded bone matrix, the latter most notable in PJI bone samples, as reported previously.22 However, while osteocyte lacunae were also clearly delineated using this stain, canaliculi were difficult to discern. Ploton silver stain effectively highlighted the osteocyte lacunocanalicular network (OLCN), as expected.25 However, information regarding the composition of the bone matrix was indiscernible with this method. Compared to Masson’s trichrome and Ploton silver staining, RGB trichrome staining demonstrated superior colour resolution and structural contrast, clearly distinguishing between regions of mineralized and non-mineralized bone (osteoid), mature intact or degraded bone matrix, as well as sharply delineating osteocyte lacunae and canaliculi (Figure 2). These features enabled both qualitative assessment and subsequent quantitative morphometry of the OLCN. Importantly, RGB staining consistently highlighted these contrasts across multiple regions of interest.

Qualitative assessment of bone matrix and the OLCN between groups

Qualitatively, under RGB trichrome staining, CTRL samples displayed dense, organized bone matrix with osteocyte lacunae predominantly elliptical in shape, and fine, long canaliculi (Figure 3a). AO samples exhibited a distinctive ‘mottled’ appearance with scattered matrix degradation zones, remarkable lacunae, and seemingly enlarged and prominent canaliculi (Figure 3b). PJI samples demonstrated widespread bone matrix degradation, with prominent, circular osteocyte lacunae, accompanied by distinct collars of perilacunar non-mineralized matrix, and a markedly reduced canalicular network (Figure 3c). PJI cases that were negative for > 5 PMN/5 HPF by standard clinical histology showed a similar pattern of bone matrix degradation and lacunar morphology on RGB hard tissue histology (Figure 3d).

Quantitative measurement of bone matrix and OLCN features

Using image analysis software, we quantified bone matrix degradation and, using a trained image segmentation process (Figure 1 and Figure 4a), morphometric parameters of osteocyte lacunar and canalicular measures across all samples (summarized in Table III). Bone matrix degradation was increased in PJI bone relative to both controls and AO bone (Figure 4b). Osteocyte lacunar size was increased in both AO and PJI bone relative to controls, but was not different between the AO and PJI groups (Figure 4c). Osteocyte lacunar circularity was increased in PJI relative to both control and AO (Figure 4d). Canalicular features were also markedly altered in PJI. Canalicular density was significantly reduced in PJI compared to AO (mean difference (MD): – 4,070/mm², p = 0.002). Canalicular area fraction was reduced in both AO and PJI bone relative to controls but was further decreased in PJI samples (Figure 4f). PJI samples also exhibited shorter canaliculi with narrower widths relative to both AO and control (Figures 4g and 4h).

Predictive modelling for PJI and AO

The diagnostic potential of individual parameters was assessed using univariate binary logistic regression models and ROC analysis. Predictive modelling identified multiple histological parameters capable of distinguishing PJI from non-PJI samples. AUROC together with Youden’s Index analysis provided optimal cut-off values and the associated sensitivities and specificities for all measures, which we classify as indicative due to the relatively small sample size (Table IV). All significant variables except lacunar area demonstrated discriminatory ability for PJI. Bone matrix degradation, lacunar circularity, canalicular density, width, and length were identified as the strongest predictors of PJI, with canalicular length achieving AUROC = 0.982; this measure also achieved the strongest diagnostic performance metrics (Figure 5, Table IV). For comparative purposes, the ability of the > 5 PMN/5 HPF measure to predict PJI in the PJI cohort was also performed, using the binary readout of ‘yes’ or ‘no’ from clinical reports of soft-tissue sample histochemical stains (Table II); this yielded an AUROC of 0.833 (95% CI 0.677 to 0.989; Table IV).

In contrast to PJI, only three parameters – bone matrix degradation percentage, lacunar circularity and canalicular density – showed significant discriminatory power for distinguishing AO from non-AO samples (Figure 5, Table IV). Importantly, the B statistic for lacunar circularity and canalicular density (Table IV) was of opposite direction (positive or negative) for predicting either AO or PJI, indicating that these parameters differentially predict the two pathologies. Within this relatively small cohort, lacunar circularity (reduced relative to non-AO) displayed the strongest diagnostic performance metrics for diagnosing AO (Figure 5, Table IV). These features therefore provide a quantifiable histological signature of either infection- or aseptic-driven bone destruction.

Discussion

In this study, we identified distinct bone matrix and cellular morphological alterations differentially associated with PJI and AO, compared to each pathology and to control bone. PJI was characterized by significantly elevated bone matrix degradation, increased lacunar area fraction, increased lacunar circularity consistent with marked bone matrix degradation, and remodelling. Notably, prominent canalicular network disruption was evident in PJI, as reflected by significantly reduced canalicular density, area fraction, length, and width, and the strong (albeit indicative at this stage) diagnostic performance metrics of these measures. In contrast, AO bone demonstrated fewer features to distinguish them from the pool of PJI and control samples, however AO osteocyte lacunae were more elliptical and had a more prominent canalicular network, featuring as increased canalicular density.

The recognition of osteocytes as multifunctional, regulatory syncytia has emerged only in the last two decades,29,30 with understanding of the role of osteocytes in infection being even more recent.31-33 Our findings support published pathological models, in which PJI is characterized by disrupted bone microarchitecture through osteocyte-mediated collagen degradation,22 and AO driven by wear particle disease leading to osteocyte perilacunar remodelling or osteocytic osteolysis.19 Osteocyte activity in PJI leads to changes that contrast with the more stable, elliptical lacunae and intact canalicular network seen in AO, suggesting that osteocytes may respond differently to infectious versus aseptic stimuli. This is consistent with previous findings showing that osteocytes express matrix metalloproteinases and inflammatory cytokines in response to infection,32,33 while typically only a few inflammatory mediators are increased in response to wear particles.19,20

Biologically, the data suggest that during infection, the osteocyte degrades its immediate perilacunar matrix non-directionally into a more circular configuration and reduces the number of dendritic processes and associated canaliculi, while triggering extensive degradation of the interstitial bone matrix. Presumably, this results in an overall reduction of osteocyte interconnectivity, although it is likely that some intercellular communication is retained, either by direct contact of dendritic processes or by the secretion of soluble factors. However, in AO, the osteocyte enlarges its lacunae while preserving the natural elliptical shape, and increases the number of dendritic processes and associated canaliculi. These differences between PJI and AO bone may relate to the relative strength and duration of the inflammatory responses to either bacterial infection or wear particles, respectively.

Limitations of this study include that the findings are based on analysis of a relatively small patient cohort per group; the full clinical potential of this diagnostic approach will not be clear until a larger, blinded, and randomized clinical trial is conducted. The current small sample size allows only indicative diagnostic performance metrics for each measure to be derived, including sometimes broad 95% CIs. Despite a modest sample size, our results provide proof of principle that RGB trichrome-based histology offers clinically relevant insights into deep bone tissue alterations in revision arthroplasty. Future multicentre studies will be essential to validate this method and integrate it alongside existing diagnostic algorithms for the diagnosis of PJI. For example, frozen or fixed, mainly soft-tissue, sections are widely assessed for the presence of > 5 PMN/5 HPF, a measure that forms an important part of most PJI definitions. Here, despite this measure informing the inclusion of PJI samples in the current study, therefore imparting a strong selection bias, five cases negative for > 5 PMN/5 HPF displayed a similar PJI-like hard tissue morphology upon RGB staining to > 5 PMN/5 HPF-positive cases. Despite this, > 5 PMN/5 HPF displayed excellent predictive ability for PJI within the PJI cohort itself (AUROC = 0.882), suggesting that a combination of this and perhaps other existing criteria, together with hard tissue histology, might prove more powerful.

Furthermore, the changes identified in the PJI cohort may not be applicable to all pathogens. Our cohort consisted of PJI patients diagnosed with a range of major Gram-positive (S. aureus, coagulase-negative staphylococci, Enterococcus) and Gram-negative (P. aeruginosa and Morganella morganii) pathogens, however hundreds of causative bacterial species, as well as mycobacteria and fungi, have been reported. While the latter difficult-to-treat classes of pathogen remain untested, in a previous report of bone matrix destruction in PJI, a wider range of bacterial pathogens were implicated, including Cutibacterium acnes and Acinetobacter, Gemella, Bacillus, and Citrobacter, in addition to staphylococci.22 Interestingly, the evidence to date suggests that a similar pattern of either bone matrix degradation or other bone histological parameters exists in a (bacterial) pathogen-independent manner, with more virulent organisms such as S. aureus sometimes appearing to have a lesser effect than lower virulence pathogens (Figure 3). It is therefore possible that specific microstructural changes to the OLCN represents a new facet of the host bone immune response to infection, as well as to implant-derived wear particles in AO, an exciting possibility that requires further exploration. Indeed, the osteoblast lineage, including the osteocyte, is becoming increasingly recognized as immune-active, participating in both innate and adaptive immune responses in response to pathogen danger signals.33 It is also possible that direct bacterial invasion of the lacunocanalicular network contributes to some of the observed structural alterations, alongside host osteocyte-mediated responses. Other limitations of these findings are that only PJI patients with an active chronic infection were included; further studies will be needed to assess the utility of our approach for diagnosis of early or late acute infections, and as a test of cure to inform the timing of a second-stage revision procedure, for example.

Beyond its application in PJI, this histomorphometric approach holds strong potential for broader use in diagnosing other infectious or inflammatory bone conditions. For instance, osteomyelitis associated with diabetic foot infections presents similar diagnostic challenges, where distinguishing infected from non-infected bone remains a clinical dilemma. While here we used intraoperative bone biopsies, our readouts could not have been affected by contamination during sampling, which would allow analysis of bone biopsies taken percutaneously prior to surgery. Furthermore, our method requires only small bone biopsies, and is compatible with standard tissue processing workflows and routine histopathology platforms incorporating digital slide analysis. It can yield a result within 48 to 72 hours, which is less time than most culture-based diagnostics and, importantly, is microbiological culture- and contamination-independent. This method thus establishes a generalizable diagnostic platform for infection-driven bone pathology against aseptic causes of bone loss, offering a practical, rapid, and scalable tool that supports clinical decision-making and facilitates high-throughput research in bone infection, inflammation, and structural degradation.

In conclusion, histomorphometric analysis of bone samples focusing on the osteocyte lacunocanalicular network reveals distinctive features that prima facie differentiate PJI from aseptic osteolysis. The RGB trichrome staining method enhances the resolution of bone matrix and cellular morphology, allowing both qualitative assessment and precise quantification of both matrix degradation and alterations to the OLCN. This histological platform provides a promising additional tool for diagnostic assessment during revision arthroplasty. In particular, the novel measures identified may provide valuable additional criteria for diagnosing chronic PJI, to those already considered by the current definition systems.
