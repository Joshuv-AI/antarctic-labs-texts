Single-nucleus chromatin landscapes of cholestatic injury and repair in mice liver
Authors: Hong Wu, Yongqing Yang, Yan Wu, Jiangshan Xu, Pengcheng Guo, Shiping Liu, Yong Hou, Shijie Hao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLiver progenitor-like cells (LPLCs) are essential for liver regeneration during some injury process, yet their epigenetic characters remain poorly understood. Using single-nucleus assay for transposase-accessible chromatin sequencing (snATAC-seq), we profiled chromatin accessibility in a mouse model of 3,5-diethoxycarbonyl-1,4-dihydrocollidin (DDC) diet-induced cholestasis at six time points: homeostasis (D0), injury (D8, D17), and repair (R2, R7, R21). We analyzed 75,452 high-quality nuclei, identifying 15 liver cell types, including LPLCs. Among 221,845 accessible chromatin regions, 192,079 showed differential accessibility. LPLCs exhibited enriched binding motifs for SWI/SNF (SMARCC1) and AP-1 (FOS, JUND and JUNB) complexes, highlighting their roles in transcriptional regulation. This study provides a comprehensive chromatin accessibility landscape of liver injury and regeneration, suggesting SWI/SNF and AP-1 as potential therapeutic targets.

Background & Summary

The reprogramming of differentiated cells into progenitor-like cells is a critical injury-repair mechanism in mammalian epithelial tissues, including the liver1–6. Both major epithelial cell types—hepatocytes and cholangiocytes—have been reported to serve as potential cells of origin LPLCs under specific injury conditions, contributing to tissue regeneration. LPLCs are characterized by the co-expression of hepatocyte (Hnf4a) and progenitor (Sox9) marker genes and can differentiate into mature hepatocytes and cholangiocytes during injury while retaining self-renewal capabilities in vitro7,8.

The precise mechanisms by which epigenetic regulation governs cellular identity transitions, particularly in the context of hepatocyte reprogramming, remain incompletely characterized. To address this knowledge gap, we employed snATAC-seq to systematically map chromatin accessibility dynamics in our DDC-induced mouse model, which is well-established for driving the reprogramming of hepatocytes into LPLCs9.

We established a DDC-induced mouse model and analyzed six time points, spanning liver injury and regeneration. Our snATAC-seq dataset of 75,452 high-quality nuclei revealed 15 liver cell types, with LPLCs showing enriched binding motifs for SWI/SNF10 and AP-111 complexes, suggesting their potential role in hepatocyte reprogramming. By identifying these key regulators, this dataset provides a potential foundation for developing regenerative therapies.

Methods

All experimental procedures involving mice were conducted in compliance with the ethical standards and guidelines established by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees at the Guangzhou Institutes of Biomedicine and Health and the Shanghai Institute of Biochemistry and Cell Biology. The respective protocol approval numbers are IACUC2018038 and SIBCB-NAF-14-001-S327-010.

Mice

Male C57BL/6 J mice, aged between 8 and 12 weeks and weighing around 25-30 g, were sourced from Shanghai Jihui Laboratory Animal and Guangdong GemPharmatech. These mice were maintained in a controlled environment with regulated temperature and a 12-hour light/dark cycle within a specific-pathogen-free facility. For tissue collection, mice were anesthetized using isoflurane, followed by cervical dislocation at designated time intervals to obtain liver samples for snATAC-seq analysis. All methods involving animals were performed in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org).

Liver injury model

To establish a DDC-induced liver injury model, mice were provided with a standard chow diet supplemented with 0.1% DDC (Sigma) for 8–17 days (D8 and D17, n = 2 per time point), followed by a return to a normal diet to monitor liver recovery (R2, R7, and R21, n = 2 per time point) (Fig. 1A). Control snATAC-seq data were obtained from healthy adult male mouse livers (D0, n = 2), as previously described in our published study12.

Experiment design and snATAC-seq data analysis workflow of DDC injury mouse model. (A) This schematic illustrates the DDC-induced liver injury and repair phase, including homeostasis (D0), injury phase (D8 and D17), regeneration phase (R2 and R7) and recovery phase (R21). Liver samples from 6 time points (n = 2 mice per time point) were analyzed by snATAC-seq. (B) The analysis workflow of snATAC-seq dataset.

For tissue collection, DDC-treated mice were anesthetized in an induction chamber using 2% isoflurane (RWD, R510-22) and maintained under anesthesia on an operating pad via continuous isoflurane inhalation. The liver was promptly excised, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C. Euthanasia was performed by cervical dislocation following tissue collection.

This approach ensures consistent and ethical handling of animals while maintaining tissue integrity for downstream analyses.

Nuclei isolation from frozen tissues

The isolation of nuclei from cryopreserved mouse liver tissues was carried out under rigorously maintained ice-cold conditions, employing an optimized protocol adapted from established methodologies13. Briefly, liver tissues were finely minced and homogenized in a prechilled sucrose-based buffer (containing 500 mM sucrose, 20 mM Tris pH 8.0, 50 mM KCl, 10 mM MgCl₂, 0.1% NP-40, and 1 × protease inhibitor cocktail) using a Dounce homogenizer. To ensure efficient tissue disruption, sequential homogenization was performed with 25 strokes each using loose and tight pestles. The homogenate was then subjected to sequential filtration through 70-μm and 40-μm cell strainers to remove debris and large aggregates. The filtrate was centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 minutes to pellet the nuclei.

For further purification, the nuclei pellets were washed twice with a PBS-based buffer supplemented with 1% BSA and 0.2 U/μl RNasin, followed by centrifugation under the same conditions. The final nuclei suspension was prepared in PBS containing 0.04% BSA. Nuclei concentration and viability were assessed using DAPI staining, and the suspension was adjusted to a working concentration of 1,000 nuclei/μl for downstream applications.

snATAC library construction and sequencing

snATAC-seq libraries were constructed using the DNBelab C Series Single-Cell ATAC Library Prep Kit (MGI, 940-000793-00). The procedure was carried out in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines. Initially, isolated nuclei were treated with Tn5 transposase, which simultaneously fragmented accessible chromatin regions and incorporated sequencing adapters. The nuclei were then co-encapsulated with uniquely barcoded gel beads into oil-phase droplets using a microfluidic device. Within these droplets, the transposed DNA fragments were tagged with cell-specific barcodes and subjected to pre-amplification. Following droplet disruption, the barcoded DNA was recovered and further amplified via PCR to generate sequencing-ready libraries.

The concentration of the resulting libraries was quantified using a Qubit Fluorometer. A total of 34 libraries derived from multiple samples were pooled in equimolar ratios. The pooled library was then circularized to produce single-stranded DNA circles (ssDNA circles). DNA nanoballs (DNBs) were subsequently generated through rolling circle amplification and loaded onto patterned nanoarrays for sequencing14. The libraries were sequenced on the DNBSEQ-T series platform with paired-end reads.

snATAC-seq raw data processing

The snATAC-seq data were analyzed using a comprehensive pipeline (Fig. 1B), with detailed sequencing statistics summarized in Table 1. The initial step involved demultiplexing raw sequencing reads to separate insertions and barcodes. These reads were then subjected to quality filtering using PISA15 (version 1.1), applying a stringent minimum base quality score of 20 to ensure data reliability. The filtered reads were subsequently aligned to the mouse reference genome (mm10) using BWA16 (version 0.7.17-r1188), a widely recognized tool for sequence alignment.

To accurately identify individual cells, the aligned BAM files were processed with bap217 (version 0.6.2), which groups barcodes associated with the same cell. Quality control (QC) of the snATAC-seq

To ensure the reliability of the snATAC-seq dataset, standard quality control measures were implemented using the ArchR package (version 1.0.2)18. The initial filtering step involved the exclusion of low-quality cells based on two key metrics: the number of unique nuclear fragments (nFrags) and the transcription start site (TSS) enrichment score. Cells with fewer than 1,000 unique fragments or a TSS enrichment score below 4 were removed from further analysis.

To address the issue of potential doublets, the ‘addDoubletScores’ function was employed to calculate doublet scores for each cell. A conservative threshold (filterRatio = 4) was applied to identify and eliminate doublets, ensuring the integrity of single-cell resolution in the dataset. Following these filtering steps, only high-quality cells meeting the established criteria were retained for downstream analyses.

This quality control pipeline was designed to minimize technical artifacts and enhance the accuracy of chromatin accessibility profiling at the single-nucleus level. By maintaining high data quality, the workflow supports robust biological interpretations and reproducible results.

snATAC-seq gene activity score calculation

Gene activity scores for all genes across all snATAC-seq libraries were computed through ArchR. Briefly, the 32-gigabase-pair mouse genome was partitioned into 100-kb windows to assess accessibility in each cell. ArchR calculates a weighted average of peak accessibility, with weights assigned based on proximity to the TSS using distance decay. The gene score matrix was generated using the ‘addGeneScoreMatrix’ function.

Analysis of temporal accessibility dynamics

Marker genes were identified using the getMarkerFeatures function in ArchR with the GeneScoreMatrix, using time point as the grouping variable. Technical covariates including TSS enrichment score and log10-transformed nFrags (log10(nFrags)) were included in the model. Wilcoxon rank-sum test was employed with a significance threshold of false discovery rate (FDR) ≤ 0.1 and log2 fold change (log2FC) ≥ 0.5. Each time point, the top 250 most significant marker genes were selected for downstream analysis.

Gene ontology (GO) analysis

GO enrichment analysis was performed using the clusterProfiler package. Gene symbols were converted to ENTREZ IDs using the org.Mm.eg.db annotation package. GO biological process terms were analyzed with the ‘enrichGO’ function using a significance threshold of FDR ≤ 0.05. For visualization, enriched terms were first ranked by statistical significance. The top terms were then manually reviewed, and the five most representative terms biologically relevant to liver injury and repair were selected for each time point.

Dimension reduction, batch effect correction and clustering of snATAC cell dataset

The snATAC-seq dataset was processed through a multi-step computational pipeline to uncover chromatin accessibility patterns. Initially, dimensionality reduction was performed using latent semantic indexing (LSI) via the ‘addIterativeLSI’ function. This approach captures the intrinsic structure of chromatin accessibility data by identifying latent factors that explain the variance in the dataset.

To address batch effects between biological replicates, we applied Harmony integration (version 1.2.0)19 using the ‘addHarmony’ function of ArchR with default parameters. Harmony was applied to the LSI reduced dimensions (reducedDims = “IterativeLSI”) using “sample” (biological replicate) as the grouping variable (groupBy = “sample”). The integration used all available dimensions and applied correlation filtering (corCutOff = 0.75) to exclude dimensions strongly correlated with sequencing depth. All other parameters were used at their default settings as implemented in the ArchR package.

Following dimensionality reduction and batch correction, unsupervised clustering was conducted using the ‘addClusters’ function. This function employs the Seurat Leiden clustering algorithm to group cells based on their chromatin accessibility profiles, enabling the identification of distinct cell populations. To visualize the clustering results, a uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) embedding was generated from the reduced-dimensionality representation using the ‘addUMAP’ function. This two-dimensional visualization provides an intuitive representation of the dataset, facilitating the exploration of cell population heterogeneity.

Hepatocyte zonation score calculation and stratification

To infer the spatial zonation of hepatocytes, a zonation score was quantified for each cell based on snATAC-seq data and the chromatin accessibility of established central vein (CV) and portal vein (PV) marker genes. We used a set of 37 CV and 32 PV landmark genes previously identified by Wu et al.20, which were rigorously selected for their stable and specific expression across space and time in the DDC injury and regeneration model.

Module scores for the PV and CV gene sets were calculated using the addModuleScore function in ArchR with the following parameters: useMatrix = “GeneScoreMatrix”, nBin = 25, nBgd = 100. All other parameters were set to their default values. The final zonation score for each hepatocyte was defined as:

A higher positive zonation score indicates a portal vein (PV) identity, while a more negative score indicates a central vein (CV) identity.

To account for sample-specific technical variations and enable robust cross-sample comparisons, hepatocytes were stratified into discrete spatial layers within each sample based on their zonation scores. For each sample independently, cells were ranked by their zonation score and divided into nine layers (L1–L9) of equal cell number using the cut_number function from the ggplot2 R package.

Subsequently, these nine layers were aggregated into three broader zonation categories for downstream biological interpretation:

CV (Central Vein) zone: Layers 1–2 (lowest zonation scores)

MZ (Mid-Zone): Layers 3–6

PV (Portal Vein) zone: Layers 7–9 (highest zonation scores)

The resulting layer (L1–L9) and zonation (CV, MZ, PV) assignments were added to the cell metadata for all subsequent analyses.

Correlation analysis between nFrags and zonation score

To assess the potential influence of sequencing depth on zonation patterns, we correlated zonation scores with log10(nFrags). Spearman’s rank correlation was chosen due to the non-normal distribution of the data and was calculated using the cor.test function in R with default parameters. The correlation analysis included all hepatocytes from homeostasis and injured conditions. Given the large sample size and multiple testing, we applied Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction where appropriate.

Statistical analysis of cellular composition

The statistical significance of changes in cell type proportions between D17 and D0 was assessed using an exact binomial test. For each cell type, the observed cell count at D17 was tested against an expected count, which was calculated by applying the proportion observed at D0 to the total cell number at D17. P-values were corrected for multiple comparisons across all cell types using the Benjamini-Hochberg FDR procedure, with significance thresholds defined as *q < 0.05, **q < 0.01, ***q < 0.001, ns (not significant).

Peak calling of snATAC-seq

To identify cell type-specific chromatin accessibility regions, peak calling was conducted for each cell type using the MACS221 ‘callpeak’ function. This analysis was performed on Tn5-corrected insertion sites, which correspond to the endpoints of Tn5-corrected fragments. Pseudo-bulk replicate tracks were first generated for each cell type using the ‘addGroupCoverages’ function. Subsequently, a reproducible peak set was identified with the ‘addReproduciblePeakSet’ function, specifying the parameters: groupBy = “celltype”. Marker peaks for each cell type were then defined using the ‘getMarkerFeatures’ function, applying thresholds of FDR ≤ 0.1 and Log2FC ≥ 0.5.

Genomic annotation of temporally differential accessible peaks

Differentially accessible peaks across liver regeneration time points were identified using the ArchR ‘getMarkerFeatures’ function. Technical covariates, including TSS enrichment scores and log10(nFrags), were incorporated into the model. Statistical testing was performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, with peaks meeting a threshold of FDR ≤ 0.1 and Log2FC ≥ 0.5 considered differentially accessible. For each time point (D0, D8, D17, R2, R7 and R21), the top 1,000 most significant peaks were selected for downstream annotation.

Genomic annotation of these temporally differential peaks was performed using the ChIPseeker package with the ‘TxDb.Mmusculus.UCSC.mm10.knownGene’ package providing gene model definitions. Peaks were annotated to features including promoter regions (−3 kb to +3 kb from TSS), gene bodies, 5′ and 3′ UTRs, introns, and distal intergenic regions.

Motif enrichment analysis

Motif annotation was carried out using the ‘addMotifAnnotations’ function in ArchR, with default settings (motifSet = “cisbp”). Following this, motif enrichment analysis was performed on marker peaks using the ‘peakAnnoEnrichment’ function. The heatmap displays the top 10 cell type-specific enriched motifs for each cell type.

Footprinting analysis of transcription factors (TFs)

To investigate TFs binding activity, we first identified the genomic positions of cell type-specific motifs using the ‘getPositions’ function. Leveraging the precomputed group coverages, we then generated TF footprints for marker motifs using the ‘getFootprints’ function. These footprints were subsequently visualized with the ‘plotFootprints’ function, providing insights into the binding patterns of key transcription factors across different cell types.

Data Records

We present a comprehensive analysis of chromatin accessibility dynamics during liver injury and repair in a DDC-induced mouse model, offering novel insights into the epigenetic mechanisms driving LPLC formation and hepatocyte reprogramming. Our dataset encompasses chromatin accessibility profiles for 75,452 high-quality single cells, systematically collected across six distinct time points (D0, D8, D17, R2, R7 and R21), capturing the progression from liver homeostasis through injury to repair. The study includes two biological replicates for each time point, resulting in a total of 34 libraries. A detailed schematic of the experimental and computational workflow is illustrated in Fig. 1, while Table 1 provides a summary of key library quality metrics.

All raw sequencing data (FASTQ files) generated in this study for time points D8, D17, R2, R7 and R21 have been deposited in the Genome Sequence Archive (GSA)22 at the National Genomics Data Center23 under accession number CRA040821 (BioProject: PRJCA061263)24. The raw FASTQ files for the homeostatic (D0) time point are available from our previously published dataset12 in the China National GenBank Nucleotide Sequence Archive (CNSA) under accession number CNP000649125 and have also been re-deposited in GSA as part of this dataset (CRA040821) to comply with journal policy. Publicly available human single-nucleus RNA-seq (snRNA-seq) data for healthy controls and acute liver failure (ALF) patients were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) under accession number GSE22356126. Downstream analysis workflow scripts and associated sample metadata are available via Figshare27.

Technical Validation

Data quality control for snATAC-seq

To elucidate the epigenetic regulatory mechanisms of LPLCs in liver regeneration, we established a DDC-induced mouse model. Our experimental design encompassed six temporally distinct phases: baseline (D0), injury progression (D8 and D17), and recovery stages (R2, R7 and R21), as delineated in Fig. 1A (see Methods for comprehensive details). For each temporal checkpoint, we employed two genetically matched mice as biological replicates, with each biological replicate subsequently partitioned into 2-3 technical replicates to ensure experimental robustness. Each technical replicate was subjected to snATAC-seq library preparation, culminating in the generation of 34 distinct libraries across the experimental timeline. The cumulative sequencing output yielded 3,058,253,133 raw reads, of which 1,505,778,119 met stringent quality control metrics, as detailed in Table 1. The raw sequencing data in FASTQ format were subsequently subjected to a rigorous computational workflow, incorporating genomic alignment and advanced analytical procedures utilizing the ArchR platform, as schematically represented in Fig. 1B.

To ensure the integrity and reliability of our data, we implemented a rigorous quality assessment protocol, which included the computation of TSS enrichment scores, quantification of unique chromatin fragments, and enumeration of total fragments per individual cell. After applying standard quality control thresholds (TSS enrichment > 4, minimum fragment count > 1000, Fig. 1B, see Methods for detailed criteria), and a doublet filter ratio of 4, we successfully retained a dataset comprising 75,452 high-quality cells with robust chromatin accessibility profiles. The distribution of TSS enrichment scores and fragment counts (nFrags) across each biological replicate is visually represented (Fig. 2A). Within this curated dataset, the average fragment count per cell was 20,394, and the mean TSS enrichment score was 10.65 (Table 1, Fig. 2A, B). Additionally, the cell count distribution across biological replicates is depicted in a bar plot (Fig. 2C). Interestingly, we observed a systematic reduction in TSS enrichment scores during the injury and early regeneration phases (D8, D17, R2 and R7) compared to homeostasis (D0) (Fig. 2B). Crucially, this reduction was not accompanied by a parallel decrease in log10(nFrags) across libraries; some injury-phase libraries even exhibited higher fragment counts than D0 controls (Fig. 2A, B). This dissociation suggests that the observed shift in TSS enrichment reflects a specific biological alteration rather than a general deterioration in data quality.

Consistent with this interpretation, genomic annotation of differentially accessible peaks across these time points revealed a redistribution of chromatin accessibility. We found a significant increase in the proportion of peaks located in intronic and distal intergenic regions—genomic locations typically associated with enhancers—coupled with a corresponding decrease in promoter-associated peaks compared to D0 (Fig. 2D). This pattern indicates a substantial shift in the regulatory landscape during liver injury and regeneration, characterized by increased accessibility at putative enhancer regions relative to promoters.

Next, we assessed the correlation of all libraries at six time points based on the gene score matrix (Fig. 2E). Intriguingly, intra-group comparisons of biological replicates at identical time points consistently yielded elevated Pearson correlation coefficients, underscoring the reproducibility of our experimental approach. Temporally, the baseline (D0) and terminal recovery (R21) phases displayed the highest degree of concordance, presumably reflecting the re-establishment of hepatic homeostasis following the completion of the regenerative cascade. Similarly, the peak injury phase (D17) exhibited substantial correlation with the early repair stage (R2), indicative of shared epigenetic signatures during active tissue remodeling. Notably, while one D8 replicate (D8_1) clustered closely with the homeostatic states (D0/R21), the other (D8_2) aligned more closely with the peak injury phase (D17), suggesting that the D8 time point represents a transitional epigenetic state bridging homeostasis and maximal injury. This observation is consistent with the dynamic nature of the D8 phase in the injury-regeneration continuum. Furthermore, GO analysis was performed on genes associated with differential accessibility at each time point, which identified distinct and significantly enriched biological processes (Fig. 2F).

Cell type identification by chromatin accessibility

To process our snATAC-seq dataset, we employed the ArchR package. Before batch effect correction, initial clustering after iterative LSI dimensionality reduction revealed substantial batch effects, where cells grouped primarily by biological replicates rather than cell types (Fig. 3A,B). To address this, we applied Harmony integration with default parameters, using biological replicate as the grouping variable. This effectively removed replicate-specific technical variation while preserving biological signals, as evidenced by the appropriate mixing of replicates from the same time point in the integrated UMAP (Fig. 3C–E).

Projecting key quality metrics onto the UMAP revealed a pronounced gradient of log10(nFrags) specifically across the hepatocyte cluster under both homeostatic and injured conditions, which was absent in non-parenchymal cells (Fig. 3F,G). Given its cell-type specificity and consistency, we considered this gradient a notable technical feature of the hepatocytes. To ensure it did not confound biological interpretations as a technical artifact, we formally tested its influence on hepatocyte zonation.

We computed the zonation score based on chromatin accessibility of landmark genes20 (see Methods, Fig. 3H,I) and classified hepatocytes into pericentral hepatocytes (PC hep), mid-zone hepatocytes (MZ hep), and periportal hepatocytes (PP hep) (Fig. 3J). Critically, correlation analysis demonstrated only a very weak association between the zonation score and log10(nFrags) (Fig. 3K, Spearman’s ρ = −0.14, p < 2.2e-16). This confirms that the identification of biologically relevant zonation states is driven by genuine chromatin accessibility differences and is not substantially confounded by the observed log10(nFrags) gradient. This cell type-specific gradient may reflect inherent biological properties. Given that hepatocytes are known to exhibit polyploidy, the observed variation in log10(nFrags) could correlate with differences in nuclear DNA content. This observation merits further investigation but did not confound the zonation analysis presented here.

Based on the preliminary clustering outcomes and leveraging the expression profiles of well-established marker genes for various liver cell types derived from prior research, we manually annotated 15 major cell types (Fig. 4A). This annotation process was guided by the gene activity scores computed by ArchR, which infer transcriptional potential based on the accessibility of regulatory elements in the vicinity of gene loci. These cell types include mesothelial cell28 (Mes, Gpm6a+/Msln+/Pdpn+), cholangiocyte29 (Chol, Krt19+/Krt7+/Epcam+), hepatocyte (Hnf4a+/Mup20+/Apoc2+) and LPLC (Sox9+/Spp1+/Tnfrsf12a+), which represent a bipotent transitional state observed during injury. While lineage-tracing studies have established that LPLCs can originate from both hepatocytes and cholangiocytes, our study focuses on elucidating the chromatin regulatory mechanisms underlying the reprogramming of hepatocytes into LPLCs, a process evident in our data by their transcriptional and epigenetic continuity on the UMAP. We also annotated cell types including hepatic stellate cell30 (HSC, Lrat+/Colec11+/Hgf+), fibroblast (FB, Dpt+/Gpx3+/Acta2+), liver sinusoidal endothelial cell31 (LSEC, Dnase1l3+/Clec4g+/Pecam1+/Cdh5+), liver vein endothelial cell (LVEC, Vwf+/Adgrg6+), Kupffer cell32 (KC, Vsig4+/Clec4f+/Cd5l+), lipid-associated macrophage33 (LAM, Gpnmb+/Trem2+/Lgals3+), Monocyte (Ccr2+/Cx3cr1+), dendritic cell34 (DC, H2-Ab1+/H2-Eb1+/Flt3+), neutrophil (Cxcr2+/S100a8+/S100a9+), natural-killer/T cell (NK/T cell, Klrb1c+/Nkg7+/Cd3d+/Cd3e+), and B cell (Cd19+/Pax5+/Cd79b+) (Fig. 4B and Supplementary Fig. 2A). The predicted cell types were validated by the increased accessibility of the promoters of the respective marker genes (Fig. 4C).

Quantification of cell type proportions from homeostasis (D0) to peak injury (D17) revealed significant hepatocyte loss concurrent with expansion of non-parenchymal and immune cells (Fig. 4D). Specifically, LPLCs underwent significant expansion at D17 compared to D0 (exact binomial test, ***q < 0.001). Neutrophils and LAMs, both undetectable at homeostasis, were robustly recruited by D17 (vs. D0, exact binomial test, ***q < 0.001), highlighting their acute role in the injury response. Statistical comparisons for five time points (D8, D17, R2, R7 and R21) versus D0 are also provided in Supplementary Table.

Cell type-specific peak calling and motif enrichment

To characterize the regulatory landscape, we identified open chromatin regions from our snATAC-seq dataset across 15 cell types. Our analysis revealed a total of 221,845 accessible chromatin regions, of which 192,079 displayed significant cell type-specific accessibility patterns compared to all other cell types (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, FDR ≤ 0.1, |Log2FC| ≥ 0.5). Motif enrichment analysis of these specific regions pinpointed potential governing TFs (Fig. 5A). To obtain supportive evidence for the motif enrichment results, we performed transcription factor footprinting analysis. This analysis revealed patterns of chromatin accessibility at the identified motifs consistent with potential transcription factor occupancy in the corresponding cell types (Supplementary Fig. 2A).

The enrichment of HNF4G motifs in hepatocytes is intriguing given its reported role in cholangiocyte-to-hepatocyte trans-differentiation in other models35. In our DDC injury model, however, we observed a distinct pattern: although Hnf4g gene activity was highest in cholangiocytes, motif activity in this population remained low across all timepoints. Conversely, in LPLCs and hepatocytes, motif activity of HNF4G was consistently higher despite lower gene accessibility (Supplementary Fig. 2B, C). This dissociation suggests that the functional role of HNF4G may be context dependent. In the DDC-induced cholestatic injury model, its regulatory activity appears more strongly associated with the hepatocyte lineage and LPLC state rather than primarily driving a cholangiocyte-to-hepatocyte fate conversion.

Notably, LAMs, one of the most dynamically expanding immune populations, showed pronounced enrichment of motifs for SPI1 (PU.1), a transcription factor essential for macrophage differentiation36.

LPLCs were enriched for motifs of AP-1 complex and SWI/SNF complex core subunits, including SMARCC1. Accordingly, chromatin accessibility at Smarcc1, Arid1a, Dpf1 and Phf10 were higher in LPLCs than in hepatocytes (Fig. 5B,C), suggesting the potential presence of a specialized BAF complex variant in this progenitor-like population.

Analysis of published snRNA-seq dataset26,37 from acetaminophen-induced ALF (APAP-ALF) and non-A-E hepatitis ALF (NAE-ALF) patients — revealed significant up-regulation of most SWI/SNF core components (e.g., SMARCA4, SMARCC1 and ARID1A) in patient hepatocytes compared to healthy controls (Fig. 5D,E), indicating conserved activation in injury across species. We also observed nuanced, context-specific regulation of subunits like DPF3 (Fig. 5D).

Time-resolved analysis revealed that specific transcription factors displayed coordinated activity patterns within major lineages. This was evidenced by concurrent changes in their inferred gene activity and motif activity at six time points (Fig. 5F). In cholangiocytes, NF-κB family members (NFKB1 and REL) showed peak gene activity and motif activity at the R2 repair phase. In hepatocytes, PPARD, FOXA1 and ONECUT1 displayed high activity during homeostasis (D0) and late repair (R7/R21), with reduced activity at the peak injury (D17) and early regeneration (R2) phases. In LPLCs, AP-1 family components (JUNB and FOSL1) and NFE2 all reached maximum activity at the peak injury (D17).

In summary, our snATAC-seq dataset delineates the chromatin accessibility landscape of mouse liver injury and regeneration, captures dynamic cellular changes and reveals coordinated transcription factor activities and conserved chromatin remodeling events across species.
