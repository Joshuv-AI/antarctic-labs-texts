A randomized trial of continuous positive airway pressure for the asthma of obesity
Authors: Anne E. Dixon, Olivia J. Garrow, Brittany Duchene, Francesca Martin, David A. Kaminsky, Peter Callas, Gwen S. Skloot, Sonali S. Bose, Jason H. T. Bates
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPeople with obesity and asthma often experience poor asthma control and frequent exacerbations, and exhibit airway hyperresponsiveness (AHR) that may be mitigated by elevating lung volume. Our objective was to determine if nocturnal continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) mitigates AHR in people with obesity, both with and without asthma.MethodsWe performed a double-blinded randomized controlled trial at two centers. Participants with BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 with (n = 20) and without (n = 20) asthma were randomized to 7 nights of CPAP of 10 cmH2O or sham CPAP (4 cmH2O). Baseline airway physiology and response to methacholine were measured by oscillometry before and after CPAP intervention. Airway responsiveness was quantified by PC100AX - the concentration of methacholine producing a 100% increase in the area under the reactance curve, and also by the concentration response change in R5 and AX at the peak concentration of methacholine.ResultsThe change in resistance at 19 Hz improved numerically in controls assigned to CPAP 10 cmH2O versus sham (-0.29 ± 0.08 versus − 0.07 ± 0.31 H2O.s.L−1, p = 0.07). AHR to methacholine was reduced numerically in controls assigned to CPAP 10 cmH2O versus sham, with the mean PC100AX (SD) being 0.43 (0.64) versus − 0.87 (0.64) mg/ml, p = 0.17. There was a modest difference in the change in the R5 concentration response in controls with CPAP 10 versus sham (median (IQ range): -0.55 (-1.14, 0.05) versus 0.31 (-0.20, 0.51), p = 0.04). CPAP 10 cmH2O versus sham did not change lung function or airway responsiveness in participants with asthma, but there was a trend toward an ameliorating effect in individuals with obesity alone.ConclusionsAHR in obese asthma is not simply related to the mechanical effects of obesity causing a reduction in lung volume. Subclinical abnormalities in airway responsiveness in people with obesity but without asthma might improve with nocturnal CPAP.Trial registrationThis study was registered on clinicaltrials.gov (NCT02953431, last update June 21, 2024).

Take-home message

We found that airway responsiveness was not reduced by exposure to continuous positive airway pressure of 10 cmH2O for 7 nights in people with obesity and asthma, but there was a trend toward reduced responsiveness in people with obesity alone.

Introduction

People with obesity and asthma experience worse asthma control and increased exacerbations compared to lean people with asthma [1]. Unfortunately, standard controller therapy (inhaled corticosteroids, and combination inhaled corticosteroid/long-acting beta agonists) is less effective in people with obesity compared to lean people [2]. Developing treatments targeting this patient population is critical but is currently hampered by our poor understanding of the pathogenesis of asthma in obesity.

The fundamental physiological derangement in obesity would appear to be the depression of lung volume by the weight of excess adipose tissue in the chest wall and abdomen [3]. It is well-established that reductions in lung volume cause dramatic increases in airway responsiveness in the normal lung [4, 5], and one might reasonably expect this hallmark of asthma to be even more extreme in the presence of obesity. Obesity-induced reductions in functional residual capacity (FRC) are reported to be similar in people either with or without asthma however [6], so exactly what role depressed lung volume plays in the asthma of obesity remains unclear. Nevertheless, breathing chronically at volumes below normal FRC is unlikely to be beneficial for the lung since, during mechanical ventilation, it is a known risk factor for ventilator-induced lung injury [7, 8]. It has been suggested, for example, that spontaneous breathing below closing volume might be injurious in asthma and COPD [9].

The operating range of lung volume during breathing can be elevated with continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP). Animal studies suggest that CPAP reduces airway hyperresponsiveness (AHR) when administered chronically [10], and may even be effective for short (2-hour) durations [11], while seven nights of nocturnal CPAP reduced AHR in young adults with asthma [12]. The mechanisms responsible for these effects are not entirely clear but may involve resetting airway smooth muscle (ASM) length and functionality [10, 11, 13, 14]. Accordingly, the purpose of the present study was to determine the efficacy of CPAP to reduce airway responsiveness in people with obesity, both with and without asthma. We focus in this study on the late-onset non-allergic (LONA) form of obese asthma because it is specific to the obese state [15], suggesting that its pathophysiology may be particularly related to compression of the lung by mass loading.

Methods

We assessed the effects of 10 cmH20 CPAP versus sham CPAP, the latter set at 4 cmH20 as used in prior studies [16], on airway physiology and methacholine responsiveness in people with obesity both with and without asthma. We enrolled controls (people with obesity but without asthma) to distinguish physiological effects related to obesity alone from those related to the combination of obesity and asthma.

An adaptive 3 + 3 Phase 1/2 clinical trial study design [17] was used to determine the optimal duration and efficacy of CPAP. Phase 1 was performed at the University of Vermont (Burlington, Vermont) and phase 2 at the University of Vermont and the Icahn School of Medicine Mount Sinai (New York, New York). The study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards at both centers. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to conducting any study procedures. The study was registered on clinicaltrials.gov (NCT02953431).Phase I (Titration): We enrolled 3 participants, starting with 1 night of CPAP. If airway responsiveness improved in all 3 participants with only one night of CPAP, we would decrease the duration sequentially to 4, then 2 h, to determine the minimal duration for the phase 2 study. If airway responsiveness did not improve after one night, we would study 3 nights and then 7 nights (the maximum duration based on Busk et al. Participants

Inclusion criteria for people with obesity and asthma included BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2, age 18 to 65 years, ≤ 20-pack year smoking history, no smoking in the past 6 months, IgE < 100 IU/ml, hyperresponsiveness to methacholine (responded to a methacholine concentration of 16 mg/ml or less), and a physician diagnosis of asthma. Eligibility for controls was the same, except they had never been diagnosed with asthma and did not respond to a methacholine concentration of 16 mg/ml or less.

Exclusion criteria included history of allergic asthma (defined by elevated IgE and allergies worsening asthma), pregnancy or breast feeding, lung disease other than asthma, obstructive sleep apnea or a high likelihood of obstructive sleep apnea as assessed by the Berlin Sleep Questionnaire [18], asthma exacerbation in the prior 6 weeks, and FEV1 < 60% predicted or < 1.5 L. To ensure safety during spirometry testing, exclusion criteria also included stroke or myocardial infarction in the past 3 months, aortic aneurysm, renal failure, or eye surgery within the past month.

CPAP/Sham

Each participant was provided with a fitted Quattro FX full face mask (ResMed, San Deigo, CA) fitted by a trained research coordinator and assessed for leak. CPAP/SHAM was administered using the AirSense 10 CPAP device (ResMed). CPAP machines logged the time each participant was on CPAP during each of the 7 nights.

Methacholine testing

Visit 1: Participants underwent a baseline evaluation of lung function followed by a conventional methacholine challenge test. Methacholine was administered via nebulizer using the 2-minute tidal breathing method [19]. Participants inhaled increasing concentrations of methacholine. The concentrations for the asthma participants were 0.031, 0.062, 0.25, 1.0, 2.0, 4.0, 8.0 and 16.0 mg/ml, and for the control participants were 0.031, 0.25, 2.0, 4.0, 16.0, 32.0 and 64.0 mg/ml (to be sure that they were non-hyperresponsive). Challenges were terminated when FEV1 decreased by 20% or until the maximum concentration was reached, establishing the PC20 concentration of methacholine. Spirometry and lung volumes were performed according to ATS guidelines [19] using a body plethysmograph (at the University of Vermont site: MCG Diagnostics, St. Paul, MN; at the Mount Sinai, NY site: Sensormedics, Yorba Linda, CA) [20].Visit 2 and 3: Participants returned for methacholine testing with impedance response measured by oscillometry at visit 2 (before) and visit 3 (within 6 h after the period of nocturnal CPAP/sham). Oscillometry was performed in the seated position with nose plugs in place. Participants supported their cheeks with their hands. Deep breaths can have a potent mitigating effect on bronchoconstriction and thus can potentially mask the effects that obesity-related decrements in lung volume have on airway responsiveness [21–23]. Accordingly, oscillometry was used to measure airway responsiveness before and after the intervention because it avoids the deep breaths required for spirometry.

Oscillometry measurements

In Phase 1, impedance response was measured using a customized flexiVent as we have previously reported [6]; respiratory system impedance from 5 to 19 Hz was measured 4 times per minute while subjects inhaled the PC20 concentration of methacholine aerosol for 4.5 min, and measurements continued for 3.5 min after the methacholine (total 8 min) [6]. The single-compartment resistance-elastance-inertance model was fit to each impedance measurement to provide estimates of respiratory system resistance (R) and elastance (E) at each time point. Model fits corrupted by events such as coughing or swallowing, or of poor quality for other reasons, were discarded. For the remaining acceptable values of R and E, the differences between corresponding post-CPAP and pre-CPAP values were averaged for each subject. A positive mean value indicates that the parameter (either R or E) increased following CPAP, i.e., responsiveness increased.

In Phase 2, impedance was measured before, and 30, 90 and 180 s after increasing concentration of methacholine using a Tremoflo device (Thorasys, Montreal, Canada). The following parameters were extracted from each impedance measurement: resistance of the respiratory system at 5 Hz (R5) and 19 Hz (R19), reactance of the respiratory system at 5 Hz (X5), and the area under the reactance curve between 5 Hz and the resonant frequency (AX). Increasing concentration of methacholine were administered until the PC20 concentration from Visit 1 was achieved or there was a 50% increase in R5. The test was terminated following a 50% increase in R5 for safety reasons, as this was deemed a clinically significant increase in airway resistance in the absence of conventional vital capacity maneuvers.

Randomization

Randomization schema for phase 2 was generated by Excel and assigned through a sealed envelope system and implemented by an unblinded research coordinator not involved in study visits.

Statistical approach

The power analysis was originally based on the assumption that R5 would increase from 7.02 ± 1.80 cmH20.s.L−1 to 8.75 ± 2.20 cmH20.s.L−1 with CPAP 10 cmH2O and to 10.50 ± 2.20 cmH20.s.L−1 with sham (i.e., increase by 50% with sham and 25% with CPAP 10). These numbers assume a 25% difference in response represents a physiologically significant difference between groups and are based on data from our prior studies in obese asthma and controls [6, 15, 26]. Twenty patients (10 in each group) would then give us 90% power with two-sided α = 0.05 to see a difference between the two groups. Controls and those with asthma were to be analyzed separately.

We stopped the methacholine delivery for safety reasons when R5 increased by 50%, so some participants received different maximum concentrations of methacholine at Visits 2 and 3. We also recently found that the methacholine response in AX, rather than R5, is particularly pertinent to the asthma of obesity, and airway responses in obese controls [27]. Therefore, prior to data analysis, we replaced R5 with AX as our measure of methacholine response and updated the trial registry accordingly. We quantified airway responsiveness as the concentration of methacholine producing a 100% increase in AX, termed the PC100AX. Discussion

We found that, in the participants with asthma, AHR was not mitigated by 7 consecutive nights of CPAP 10 cmH2O versus sham (Table 5). In the control participants, however, treatment with CPAP 10 cmH2O showed some modest improvements (Table 5). This is not the outcome we had expected based on prior animal studies [10, 13], or indeed in humans. Seven nights of CPAP has been shown to be efficacious in asthma [12], and 12 weeks of sham versus 5 versus 10 cm H2O CPAP reduced AHR [29]. These prior human studies, however, involved younger participants (mean age 28 years compared to 45 years in the present study) [12], and excluded patients with significant obesity (BMI < 35 kg/m2) [29].

Our results thus suggest there is something about the combination of asthma and obesity that blocks the beneficial effects of chronic CPAP. One potential explanation for this is that asthma results in airway remodeling that stiffens the airway wall and so makes it less affected by changes in airway pressure, although we found little evidence of changes in airway wall mechanics in obese LONA asthma in a previous study [6]. Alternatively, the control participants had significantly lower FRCs than those with asthma, even though those with asthma were significantly heavier. Indeed, FRC in the participants with asthma trended greater than predicted (Table 2), yet they had much greater AX and more negative X5 compared to controls (Table 2). A likely explanation for this finding is that FRC determined by plethysmography measures the total volume of gas in the lungs, including any trapped gas, while AX and X5 are reflective only of the gas volume in communication with the airway opening. This implies that the participants with asthma experienced significant gas trapping, meaning that the volume of gas in communication with the airway opening was reduced compared to non-asthma (i.e., air trapping was greater), which is supported by the significant increases in RV/TLC % predicted. This conforms with our previous observations that while all individuals with obesity appear to suffer from dramatic lung derecruitment at low volumes [6, 30, 31], derecruitment manifests largely as atelectasis in those without asthma, but as closure of small airways in those with asthma [15, 32].

The physiologic significance of gas trapping in the participants with asthma is that it may have acted effectively like intrinsic positive end-expiratory pressure in the affected lung regions. These regions would have been maintained in a state of inflation regardless of whether or not CPAP was present, rendering CPAP essentially irrelevant. The effect of CPAP on the lung was not completely obliterated, however, because R19 exhibited a nearly significant decrease between Visits 2 and 3 (Table 4) indicative of a modest increase in central airway caliber [26]. In contrast, the control participants did not experience gas trapping and thus would have been able to passively exhale fully against the applied CPAP level. Nocturnal CPAP in the controls would thus have stretched the ASM in their small airways relative to sham, which could have reset ASM contractility to conform to the elevated lung volume at 10 cmH2O, thereby reducing airway responsiveness.

The above reasoning raises the question as to what caused peripheral airways to close and thus trap gas in the participants with obese asthma. Small airway closure typically results from the formation of liquid bridges that plug the lumen, which occurs when the fluid layer that lines the airways becomes thickened, predisposing it to bulge into the lumen such that opposing fluid surfaces meet to form a plug stabilized by surface tension [33]. Our prior studies have shown, using micro-CT imaging and computational modeling, that this is the mechanism at play in lean mouse models of allergic asthma [34, 35], and it is almost certainly a key physiologic event in acute lung injury [36, 37]. The AHR of obese late-onset non-allergic asthma may thus result from instabilities in the fluid layer that lines the small airways. Such instabilities can arise, for example, when surface tension increases due to impairments in surfactant function [37, 38], although whether this occurs in the asthma of obesity remains to be determined.

This study has a number of limitations. First, these were challenging experiments to perform, so we designed our study around what we calculated to be the minimum number of participants needed to obtain a significant effect. However, changing our primary outcome variable from R5 to AX may have reduced the statistical power of the study, although the methacholine concentration response analyses for both R5 and AX were completely negative for the participants with asthma, suggesting this was not a power issue (Table 5; Fig. 2). Recruitment challenges also meant that the study groups were not matched for sex and BMI, and there were some numerical differences between CPAP and sham subgroups. Although this would have been avoided with a cross-over study design, the participants could not have been blinded to differences in pressure or immune from the lingering effects of CPAP with such a design. In any case, each study group acts as its own control because we looked at the effects of either CPAP or sham on methacholine responsiveness within each group. A methodological limitation is that although the study was designed for 7 full nights of sleep with CPAP, the participant groups used CPAP for a mean of only between 3.42 and 4.65 h per night (Table 3). Nevertheless, there was no correlation between the mean number of nightly CPAP hours in individual patients and their respective changes in ΔPC100AX. Our conclusions are limited to the relative effects of 7 nights of CPAP of 10 versus 4 cmH2O. It is possible that higher levels of CPAP might have overcome the equalizing effects of small airway closure and thus proven efficacious in the participants with both obesity and asthma. Alternatively, mitigation of AHR may require significantly longer durations of 10 cmH2O CPAP treatment than the 7 nights we administered in the present study.

In conclusion, we found no effect on airway responsiveness of 7 nights of 10 cmH2O CPAP in individuals with obesity and asthma, but did find a modest ameliorating effect in individuals with obesity alone. This suggests that AHR in obese asthma is not simply related to reduced lung volumes in obesity but rather that gas trapping in obese asthma maintains the lung periphery in an inflated state regardless of whether CPAP is present. On the other hand, there are subclinical abnormalities of airway responsiveness in people with obesity [22] that might improve with nocturnal CPAP. We speculate this could improve nocturnal ventilation and thus add to the beneficial effects of CPAP in people with obesity treated for sleep apnea.
