Relativistic one-dimensional billiards
Alfonso Artigue

Abstract
. In this article we study the dynamics of one-dimensional relativistic billiards containing particles with positive and negative energy. We study configurations with two identical positive masses and symmetric positions with two massless particles between them of negative energy and symmetric positions. We show that such systems have finitely many collisions in any finite time interval. This is due to a phenomenon we call tachyonic collision , which occur at small scales and produce changes in the sign of the energy of individual particles. We also show that depending on the initial parameters the solutions can be bounded with certain periodicity or unbounded while obeying an inverse square law at large distances.

2.
Free particle

We start with one particle. Its position will be denoted as
x(t)\in\mathbb{R}
for all
t
in a real interval (mainly, it will be
t\in\mathbb{R}
).
As a free particle its velocity is a constant
v=\frac{dx}{dt}(t)
.
The particle has constant energy
E\in\mathbb{R}
,
E\neq 0
, and its momentum is defined as
P=Ev
.
The
squared mass
is
\mu=E^{2}-P^{2}
.

From these conditions it follows that for a massless particle,
\mu=0
, we have

E^{2}=P^{2}
and its velocity is
v=P/E=\pm 1
.
That is, we choose units so that the speed of light equals 1.
For
\mu<0
we have
|v|>1
and this particle is usually called a
tachyon
.
For
\mu>0
, if
m
is its mass then
\mu=m^{2}
.
For
\mu\geq 0
the particle is a
bradyon
.

Remark 1
.

With these definitions we avoid the square roots that appears in special relativity textbooks.
We can easily obtain
E^{2}=\mu/(1-v^{2})
, but to write the usual formula
E=m/\sqrt{1-v^{2}}
is a problem for us because, even assuming
1-v^{2}>0
, the signs of energy and mass are forced to coincide, as long as the chosen square root is the positive one.
But, as we will see in §
3.3
, we can have a particle changing the sign of its energy during a collision.

We derive a simple formula for the velocity that will be useful later.
Consider the quantities

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\sigma=E+P\\
\rho=E-P.\end{array}\right.

Remark 2
.

We see that
\sigma\rho=\mu
,
2E=\sigma+\mu/\sigma

and
2P=\sigma-\mu/\sigma
. Thus

(1)

v=\frac{P}{E}=\frac{\sigma-\mu/\sigma}{\sigma+\mu/\sigma}=\frac{\sigma^{2}-\mu}{\sigma^{2}+\mu}.

Remark 3

(Interpretation of negative energy)
.

It is important to remark that all we will do only depends on the squared masses and not on the mass.
However, we find useful to have a way of thinking about positive mass with negative energy.
Besides, this phenomenon is unavoidable in the dynamics we will study, as we will see in §
5
.
To choose a square root
m
of
\mu
we need extra structure: the particle will carry a watch.
The hand of the watch can spin clockwise or counterclockwise.
The spin velocity is defined as

\mathcal{S}=\frac{m}{E}

provided that
m
is any chosen square root of
\mu
,
m^{2}=\mu
.
Since
E^{2}=\mu/(1-v^{2})
we have
|E|\sqrt{1-v^{2}}=|m|
and
|\mathcal{S}|=\sqrt{1-v^{2}}
.
Also,
\sign(\mathcal{S})=\sign(m/E)
.
Therefore, for
m\neq 0
we have

E=\frac{m}{\mathcal{S}}=\sign(m/E)\frac{m}{\sqrt{1-v^{2}}}.

A particle whose watch spins counterclockwise (
\mathcal{S}<0
) is to be interpreted as traveling backward in time.
With full generality (any real value of
m
) we can write
m=\mathcal{S}E
, where vanishing mass is equivalent to a stopped watch.

3.
Definition of the dynamics

In this section we consider one-dimensional dynamics of
N\geq 3
particles with positions
x_{1}\leq x_{2}\leq\dots\leq x_{N}
.
The time evolution is defined by the rules:

(1)

between collisions the particles are free: have constant velocity
v_{i}=P_{i}/E_{i}
,

(2)

the collisions are elastic: the energy and momentum are preserved,

(3)

there are no multiple collisions at the same time and at the same point.

Remark 4
.

We have to rule out multiple simultaneous collisions at the same point because, in general, there is no satisfactory way to define the result. This is because we can approximate it by ordering the collisions in different ways and obtaining different results, which implies a discontinuity in the dynamics. However, this is a non-generic event.
We remark that more than one collision at the same time but at different places (a description of the events that depend on the reference frame) are allowed and will be used in §
4
.

3.1.
Collisions

The previous rules are sufficient to
resolve
the collisions, as we will see in Lemma
6
.
If we use primes to denote the final energy and momentum of the particles
i,j
after a collision, the conservation of energy and momentum gives us:

E_{i}+E_{j}=E^{\prime}_{i}+E^{\prime}_{j}
and
P_{i}+P_{j}=P^{\prime}_{i}+P^{\prime}_{j}
.
To solve these equations we follow
[
3
]
and
define

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\sigma_{i}=E_{i}+P_{i}\\
\rho_{i}=E_{i}-P_{i}\end{array}\right.

for each particle and the corresponding
primed
quantities after the collision.
Jointly with
E_{*}^{2}-P_{*}^{2}=\mu_{*}
(
*=i,j
) we have the system of equations:

(2)

\text{before collision}\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\sigma_{i}+\sigma_{j}=s\\
\rho_{i}+\rho_{j}=r\\
\sigma_{i}\rho_{i}=\mu_{i}\\
\sigma_{j}\rho_{j}=\mu_{j}\end{array}\right.\Rightarrow\text{after collision}\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\sigma^{\prime}_{i}+\sigma^{\prime}_{j}=s\\
\rho^{\prime}_{i}+\rho^{\prime}_{j}=r\\
\sigma^{\prime}_{i}\rho^{\prime}_{i}=\mu_{i}\\
\sigma^{\prime}_{j}\rho^{\prime}_{j}=\mu_{j}\end{array}\right.

where
s=\sigma_{i}+\sigma_{j}
and
r=\rho_{i}+\rho_{j}
are constants of the collision (energy and momentum conservation).

Remark 5

(Collision condition)
.

Given two particles
i,j
the condition for a collision is
v_{i}-v_{j}\neq 0
.
The fact that the collision occurs in the future or in the past depends on the positions, this condition just guarantees
a collision (in positive or negative time).
As the velocity of a particle
*
is

v_{*}=\frac{P_{*}}{E_{*}}=\frac{\sigma_{*}-\rho_{*}}{\sigma_{*}+\rho_{*}}

the collision condition is

(3)

\sigma_{i}\rho_{j}-\sigma_{j}\rho_{i}\neq 0\Leftrightarrow v_{i}-v_{j}\neq 0.

Lemma 6
.

If
sr\neq 0
then the system of equations (
2
) has just one other solution:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}_{i}=\rho_{i}\frac{s}{r}=\sigma_{i}-\frac{\sigma_{i}\rho_{j}-\sigma_{j}\rho_{i}}{r}\\
\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}_{j}=\rho_{j}\frac{s}{r}=\sigma_{j}+\frac{\sigma_{i}\rho_{j}-\sigma_{j}\rho_{i}}{r}\\
\end{array}\right.\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\rho^{\prime}_{i}=\sigma_{i}\frac{r}{s}=\rho_{i}+\frac{\sigma_{i}\rho_{j}-\sigma_{j}\rho_{i}}{s}\\
\displaystyle\rho^{\prime}_{j}=\sigma_{j}\frac{r}{s}=\rho_{j}-\frac{\sigma_{i}\rho_{j}-\sigma_{j}\rho_{i}}{s}\\
\end{array}\right.

which is different from the original if and only if
the collision condition (
3
) holds.

The proof of Lemma
6
is left to the reader.
Some remarks are in order.

Remark 7
.

The condition
sr=0
has probability zero, thus the dynamics is generically well defined.

Remark 8
.

From the definitions we have:

\begin{array}[]{rl}sr&=(\sigma_{i}+\sigma_{j})(\rho_{i}+\rho_{j})=(E_{i}+P_{i}+E_{j}+P_{j})(E_{i}-P_{i}+E_{j}-P_{j})\\
&=(\mathcal{E}+\mathcal{P})(\mathcal{E}-\mathcal{P})=\mathcal{E}^{2}-\mathcal{P}^{2},\\
\end{array}

where
\mathcal{E}=E_{i}+E_{j}
and
\mathcal{P}=P_{i}+P_{j}
.
The quantity
sr=\mathcal{E}^{2}-\mathcal{P}^{2}
represents
the rest mass of this system
.

Remark 9
.

Notice that
the collision of two particles with the same mass is
always solved (in a classical or a relativistic framework) by exchanging energy and momentum, or equivalently we can think that they just interact by exchanging their labels.

Remark 10
.

If we consider classical mechanics and solve a collision we find a denominator in the formula of the final velocity as
m_{i}+m_{j}
.
Therefore,
m
cannot collide with
-m
.
It is remarkable that
from the relativistic viewpoint we are considering, this collision is always allowed.
Indeed, by direct inspection of Equations (
2
) we see that
if
\mu_{i}=\mu_{j}
then

\sigma^{\prime}_{i}=\sigma_{j}
,

\sigma^{\prime}_{j}=\sigma_{i}
,

\rho^{\prime}_{i}=\rho_{j}
,

\rho^{\prime}_{j}=\rho_{i}

is the solution of the collision.
Considering the previous remark we can say that a particle of mass
m
does not interact with particles of mass
m
or
-m
.

3.2.
Tachyonic collisions

In the classical case the kinetic energy is
mv^{2}/2
and its sign only depends on the sign of the mass, thus the sign of the energy is preserved after collisions.
In the relativistic case the sign of the energy can change during a collision, see an example in §
3.3
.
A
tachyonic collision
is a collision with
sr=\mathcal{E}^{2}-\mathcal{P}^{2}<0
.

Proposition 11
.

If
\mu_{i}\geq 0
then the energy of the particle
i
changes its sign during a collision
if and only if it is a tachyonic collision.

Proof.

From definitions and Lemma
6
we have that

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\sigma_{i}\rho_{i}=\mu_{i},\\
2E_{i}=\sigma_{i}+\rho_{i},\\
2E^{\prime}_{i}=\sigma^{\prime}_{i}+\rho^{\prime}_{i}=\rho_{i}\frac{s}{r}+\sigma_{i}\frac{r}{s}=\frac{\rho_{i}s^{2}+\sigma_{i}r^{2}}{rs}.\\
\end{array}\right.

Since
\mu_{i}=\sigma_{i}\rho_{i}\geq 0
we have that
(\sigma_{i}+\rho_{i})(\rho_{i}s^{2}+\sigma_{i}r^{2})>0
.
Thus,
\sg(E_{i}E_{i}^{\prime})=\sg(rs)
.
∎

3.3.
Example of a zero-energy tachyonic collision

Let us consider a curious collision.
Suppose two particles, the particle 1 is at rest with mass
m=1
and the particle 2 is massless, has energy
E_{2}=-1
, velocity
-1
and momentum
P_{2}=1
.
In this way the system has vanishing energy
\mathcal{E}=0
and momentum
\mathcal{P}=1
.
In particular, as
\mathcal{E}^{2}-\mathcal{P}^{2}=-1<0
, their collision is tachyonic.
Lemma
6
gives us the energies and momenta of both particles after the collision:
E_{1}^{\prime}=-1
,
P_{1}^{\prime}=0
,
E_{2}^{\prime}=1
and
P_{2}^{\prime}=1
.
It is remarkable that particle 1 continues at rest after the collision.
Its energy turns negative since the collision is tachyonic.

4.
Mirror systems

In this section we extend the study of

[
1
]
. To simplify the analysis we consider a symmetric system.

4.1.
General properties of mirror systems

Suppose
N=4
particles under the following conditions:

x_{1}=-x_{4}
,
x_{2}=-x_{3}
,
\mu_{1}=\mu_{4}=\mu>0
.
The
middle particles
are massless:
\mu_{2},\mu_{3}=0
.
The collisions between the particles
1-2
are simultaneous with
3-4
.
Due to the symmetry the particles
2-3
have collisions only at
x=0

and there they exchange energy and momentum as explained in Remark
10
.

Suppose a collision
1-2
.
As particle 1 is on the left and particle 2 is massless we have

v_{2}=-1
.
In this case,
E_{2}=-P_{2}
before the collision.
Thus,
\sigma_{2}=0
and
\rho_{2}=2E_{2}
.
Next, particle 2 collides with particle 3 exchanging their momenta while both have the same energy, again, due to symmetry.
After this second collision we have
v_{2}^{\prime}=-1
,
E_{2}^{\prime}=-P_{2}^{\prime}
,
\sigma_{2}^{\prime}=0
and
\rho_{2}^{\prime}=2E_{2}^{\prime}
.

Applying Lemma
6
we obtain

(4)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}E^{\prime}_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{2}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}\rho_{2}\frac{s}{r}=E_{2}\frac{\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{2}}{\rho_{1}+\rho_{2}}=\frac{E_{2}\sigma_{1}}{2E_{2}+\mu/\sigma_{1}}\\
\sigma^{\prime}_{1}=\rho_{1}\frac{s}{r}=\frac{\mu}{\sigma_{1}}\frac{s}{r}=\frac{\mu}{2E_{2}+\mu/\sigma_{1}}\end{array}\right.

This reduces the dynamics to the study of the iterations of the two-dimensional map
(\sigma_{1},E_{2})\mapsto(\sigma_{1}^{\prime},E_{2}^{\prime})
, but it can be simplified even more.

4.1.1.
Reduction to a one-parameter system

As
\sigma_{1}=E_{1}+P_{1}
and
\rho_{1}=E_{1}-P_{1}
we have

\sigma_{1}+\rho_{1}=2E_{1}=2E-2E_{2}
, where
\mathcal{E}=E_{1}+E_{2}
(a constant due to symmetry).
Thus,
\sigma_{1}+\mu/\sigma_{1}=2\mathcal{E}-2E_{2}
,

(5)

2E_{2}+\mu/\sigma_{1}=2\mathcal{E}-\sigma_{1}\text{ and }\sigma^{\prime}_{1}=\frac{\mu}{2\mathcal{E}-\sigma_{1}}.

Thus, the dynamics is reduced to the one-dimensional map

(6)

f(\sigma)=\frac{\mu}{2\mathcal{E}-\sigma}

for any
\sigma\in\mathbb{R},\sigma\neq 2\mathcal{E}
.
Define

\Delta=\mathcal{E}^{2}-\mu.

As we will see, the sign of
\Delta
characterizes the dynamics.
For instance,
the fixed points of
f
are determined by the quadratic equation

\sigma^{2}-2\mathcal{E}\sigma+\mu=0
whose discriminant is
\Delta
.
Thus, in what follows
\Delta
will be called as
discriminant
of the system.

4.1.2.
A constant of motion

In what follows we derive a relation that will be useful in the next sections.
Consider
\tau_{n}
the time elapsed between the collisions of the particle 1 (with particle 2);
and
v_{1}^{n}
the velocity of particle 1 between such collision.
Let
x_{j}^{n}
denote the position of particle
j
when it has the
n
-th collision.
Let
E_{j}^{n},P_{j}^{n},v_{j}^{n}
be the energy, momentum and velocity of the particle
j
between its
n
and
n+1
collision, respectively.

Lemma 12
.

The number

{x_{1}^{n}E_{2}^{n}}/{\sigma_{1}^{n}}

is independent of

n\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

On the one hand, as
c=1
we have

\tau_{n}=-x_{1}^{n}-x_{1}^{n+1}
(the negative signs are due to the fact that the position

x_{1}^{n}
is negative).
On the other hand,
v_{1}^{n}=(x_{1}^{n+1}-x_{1}^{n})/\tau_{n}
.
Therefore,

v_{1}^{n}=\frac{x_{1}^{n+1}-x_{1}^{n}}{-x_{1}^{n}-x_{1}^{n+1}}\text{ and }\frac{x_{1}^{n+1}}{x_{1}^{n}}=\frac{1-v_{1}^{n}}{1+v_{1}^{n}}=\frac{\mu}{(\sigma_{1}^{n})^{2}}.

For the last equality we have applied Equation (
10
).
From
(
4
) and (
5
) we have

\frac{E_{2}^{n+1}}{E_{2}^{n}}=\frac{\sigma_{1}^{n}}{2E_{2}^{n}+\mu/\sigma_{1}^{n}}=\frac{\sigma_{1}^{n}}{2\mathcal{E}-\sigma_{1}^{n}}.

Then

\frac{x_{1}^{n+1}E_{2}^{n+1}}{x_{1}^{n}E_{2}^{n}}=\frac{\mu}{\sigma_{1}^{n}(2\mathcal{E}-\sigma_{1}^{n})}=\frac{\sigma_{1}^{n+1}}{\sigma_{1}^{n}}.\qed

4.2.
Non-negative discriminant

We will assume that
\Delta=\mathcal{E}^{2}-\mu\geq 0
.
Considering the map
f
from §
4.1.1

we need to know that the fixed points
\sigma_{*}
satisfies

(7)

\sigma_{*}^{2}-2\mathcal{E}\sigma_{*}+\mu=0.

If in addition
\mathcal{E}>0

the fixed points are labeled as:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\sigma_{at}=\mathcal{E}-\sqrt{\Delta}\\
\sigma_{re}=\mathcal{E}+\sqrt{\Delta}.\end{array}\right.

and the derivative at the fixed points is

\frac{df}{d\sigma}(\sigma_{at})=\frac{\mu}{(2\mathcal{E}-\sigma_{at})^{2}}=\frac{\mu}{2\mathcal{E}^{2}-\mu+2\mathcal{E}\sqrt{\Delta}}<1

Analogously,

\frac{df}{d\sigma}(\sigma_{re})=\frac{\mu}{2\mathcal{E}^{2}-\mu-2\mathcal{E}\sqrt{\Delta}}>1

thus,
\sigma_{at}
is an attractor and
\sigma_{re}
is a repeller.
If
\mathcal{E}<0
then we define (changing signs)

(8)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\sigma_{at}=\mathcal{E}+\sqrt{\Delta}\\
\sigma_{re}=\mathcal{E}-\sqrt{\Delta}.\end{array}\right.

and again
\sigma_{at}
is an attractor and
\sigma_{re}
is a repeller.
For
\Delta=0
there is just one fixed point
\sigma_{*}=\mathcal{E}
which is neither attractor nor repeller.
In what follows we
study the time limit of the systems we are considering.

Proposition 13
.

If
\Delta\geq 0
then
x_{1}^{n}\to-\infty
,
x_{4}^{n}\to+\infty
as
n\to\pm\infty
and the energy of particles 2 and 3 goes to zero as time goes to infinity.

Proof.

From our previous analysis we have that for any initial condition
\sigma_{1}\neq\sigma_{re}
we have
f^{n}(\sigma_{1})\to\sigma_{at}
as
n\to+\infty
.
Analogously, if
\sigma_{1}\neq\sigma_{at}
we have
f^{n}(\sigma_{1})\to\sigma_{re}
as
n\to-\infty
.
Also,

(9)

2E_{1}=\sigma_{1}+\rho_{1}=\sigma_{1}+\frac{\mu}{\sigma_{1}},

\mathcal{E}=E_{1}+E_{2}
and

2E_{2}=2\mathcal{E}-\sigma_{1}-\frac{\mu}{\sigma_{1}}.

Therefore, taking limit and applying
Equation (
7
)
we conclude

\lim_{n\to+\infty}2E_{2}^{n}=2\mathcal{E}-\sigma_{at}-\frac{\mu}{\sigma_{at}}=0.

Analogously, the energy of the second particle tends to zero as
n\to-\infty
.
The symmetry of the system allows us to obtain the same conclusions for the particle 3.

From Equation (
1
) we have

(10)

v_{1}=\frac{\sigma_{1}^{2}-\mu}{\sigma_{1}^{2}+\mu}.

Arguing as before we can take the limits

.
If
\Delta>0
and

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\lim_{n\to+\infty}v_{1}^{n}=\frac{\sigma_{at}^{2}-\mu}{\sigma_{at}^{2}+\mu}=\frac{\Delta-\mathcal{E}\sqrt{\Delta}}{\mathcal{E}^{2}-\mathcal{E}\sqrt{\Delta}}=-\frac{\sqrt{\Delta}}{\mathcal{E}}<0,\\

This implies that in the past the particle 1 has positive velocity, while in the future it is negative.
That is,
x_{1}^{n}\to-\infty
as
n\to\pm\infty
.
The result for

follows by symmetry.
The case
\Delta>0
and

If
\Delta=0
then the fixed point is

and

.
If
\mathcal{E}>0
then for

.
Thus,
\lim_{n\to+\infty}v_{1}^{n}=0^{-}
.
Analogously,
\lim_{n\to-\infty}v_{1}^{n}=0^{+}
.
This implies that the limit velocities have the same sign as for
\Delta>0
.
The case
\mathcal{E}<0

\Delta=0

∎

converges as

4.3.
Negative discriminant

In this section we will study the case
\Delta<0

for the mirror system of 4 particles as before.
The map
f
of Equation (
6
)

f(\sigma)=\frac{\mu}{2\mathcal{E}-\sigma}
has two fixed points
\sigma_{at}
and

\sigma_{re}
, which are complex since
\Delta<0
.
Let
\lambda=\frac{\sigma_{at}}{\sigma_{re}}
.
Since
|\lambda|=1
we can write
\lambda=e^{i\theta}
.

Let us remark some facts that will be used in the next proof.
First notice that
f
can be regarded as a Möbius transformation on the complex plane.
We will show that
f
is conjugate to the rotation
g(z)=\lambda z
as follows.
Let
h\colon\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
be defined as

h(\sigma)=\frac{-\sigma_{at}+\sigma}{\sigma_{re}-\sigma}=z\text{ with inverse }h^{-1}(z)=\frac{\sigma_{at}+z\sigma_{re}}{1+z}.

It satisfies:
h
transforms the real axis into the unit circle,
h(\sigma_{at})=0
,
h(\sigma_{re})=\infty
and
h(\mathcal{E})=1
.
To prove

g(z)=hfh^{-1}(z)
notice that

\sigma_{at}\sigma_{re}=\mu
and
\sigma_{at}+\sigma_{re}=2\mathcal{E}
. Then

\begin{array}[]{rl}h(f(\sigma))&=h\left(\frac{\mu}{2\mathcal{E}-\sigma}\right)=\frac{-\sigma_{at}+\frac{\mu}{2\mathcal{E}-\sigma}}{\sigma_{re}-\frac{\mu}{2\mathcal{E}-\sigma}}=\frac{\sigma_{at}}{\sigma_{re}}\left(\frac{-\sigma_{at}+\sigma}{\sigma_{re}-\sigma}\right)=\lambda h(\sigma).\end{array}

This shows that
f
is conjugate to a rotation if the discriminant is negative. However, this conjugacy is true for any sign of the discriminant.

Next we will show that for a dense set of the parameters
\mu
and
E
the solutions are periodic. In fact, we give a quite complete description of the solutions.

Theorem 17
.

For a mirror system of 4 particles, if
\Delta<0
then the solutions are bounded.
If in addition
\theta=\frac{a}{b}2\pi
, with
a,b
coprime positive integers,
then the solutions are periodic with period

T=\frac{2kb\mu}{\mu-\mathcal{E}^{2}}

where
k=\frac{x_{1}^{0}E_{2}^{0}}{\sigma_{1}^{0}}
.

Proof.

From Lemma
12
we known that

x_{1}^{n}=k\sigma_{1}^{n}/E_{2}^{n}
.
By Equation (
5
) we know that

2E_{2}^{n}=2\mathcal{E}-\sigma_{1}^{n}-\mu/\sigma_{1}^{n}
.
Thus

(12)

x_{1}^{n}=\frac{2k\sigma_{1}^{n}}{2\mathcal{E}-\sigma_{1}^{n}-\mu/\sigma_{1}^{n}}.

The solutions are bounded because
the map
L\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
given by

(13)

L(\sigma)=\frac{2k\sigma}{2\mathcal{E}-\sigma-\mu/\sigma}=\frac{2k\sigma^{2}}{2\mathcal{E}\sigma-\sigma^{2}-\mu}=\frac{-2k\sigma^{2}}{(\sigma-\mathcal{E})^{2}-\Delta}

is bounded since
\Delta<0
and it has finite limit as
\sigma\to\pm\infty
.

Now we use that
f
is conjugate to the rotation
g
of angle
\theta=\frac{a}{b}2\pi
in the circle
|z|=1
. Thus,

g^{b}(z_{0})=z_{0}
if
|z_{0}|=1
.
That is, the
discrete period
is
b
(the solution closes the cycle after
b
collisions). To calculate the time elapsed in this cycle we
consider the function
L
of Equation (
13
) given by

(14)

L(\sigma)=\frac{-2k\sigma^{2}}{(\sigma-\mathcal{E})^{2}-\Delta}.

As the speed of light is
c=1
we have that

-\frac{1}{2}T=\sum_{j=1}^{j=b}L(f^{j}(\sigma_{0}))

where
\sigma_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
is any initial condition for the iteration of
f
.
If we let
z_{0}=h(\sigma_{0})
and
z_{j}=g^{j}(z_{0})=\lambda^{j}z_{0}
then

f^{j}(\sigma_{0})=f^{j}(h^{-1}(z_{0}))=h^{-1}(g^{j}(z_{0}))=h^{-1}(z_{j})=\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{at}+z_{j}\sigma_{re}}{1+z_{j}}

and we can perform the following calculations:

L(f^{j}(\sigma_{0}))=\frac{-2k(\frac{\sigma_{at}+z_{j}\sigma_{re}}{1+z_{j}})^{2}}{(\frac{\sigma_{at}+z_{j}\sigma_{re}}{1+z_{j}}-\mathcal{E})^{2}-\Delta}

\begin{array}[]{rl}\frac{\sigma_{at}+z_{j}\sigma_{re}}{1+z_{j}}-\mathcal{E}&=\frac{(\sigma_{at}-\mathcal{E})+z_{j}(\sigma_{re}-\mathcal{E})}{1+z_{j}}=\frac{-\sqrt{\Delta}+z_{j}\sqrt{\Delta}}{1+z_{j}}\\
&=\frac{-1+z_{j}}{1+z_{j}}\sqrt{\Delta}\end{array}

to obtain

\begin{array}[]{rl}L(f^{j}(\sigma_{0}))&\displaystyle=\frac{-2k(\frac{\sigma_{at}+z_{j}\sigma_{re}}{1+z_{j}})^{2}}{(\frac{-1+z_{j}}{1+z_{j}}\sqrt{\Delta})^{2}-\Delta}=\displaystyle\frac{-2k(\frac{\sigma_{at}+z_{j}\sigma_{re}}{1+z_{j}})^{2}}{\frac{-\Delta}{(1+z_{j})^{2}}4z_{j}}\\

We have that

.
Therefore

-\frac{1}{2}T=\sum_{j=1}^{j=b}L(f^{j}(\sigma_{0}))=\sum_{j=1}^{j=b}\frac{-k(\sigma_{at}\sigma_{re})}{-\Delta}=b\frac{-k(\sigma_{at}\sigma_{re})}{-\Delta}

Since
\sigma_{at}\sigma_{re}=\mu
we conclude

as we wanted to prove.
∎

5.
Tachyonic collisions

In the next result we consider
full solutions
,
i.e.
defined for all time, positive and negative.

Theorem 18
.

For a mirror system of
N=4
particles there is a tachyonic collision if and only if

0<\sigma_{1}(\sigma_{1}-2\mathcal{E})
. Also:

•

if
\Delta<0
then every solution has infinitely many tachyonic collisions,

•

if
\Delta=0
then every solution has exactly two tachyonic collisions, which are consecutive,

•

if
\Delta>0
then a solution has tachyonic collisions if and only if
(\sigma_{1}^{0}-\mathcal{E})^{2}>\Delta
, and in this case the number of such collisions is two and they are consecutive,

where
\sigma_{1}^{0}
is the initial condition of the solution.

Proof.

By definition (recall §
5
)
a collision is tachyonic provided that
\mathcal{E}^{2}-\mathcal{P}^{2}<0
.
Since
\sigma_{1}=E_{1}+P_{1}
and
P_{2}=-E_{2}
, the tachyonic condition can be written as

\mathcal{E}^{2}<(P_{1}+P_{2})^{2}=(\sigma_{1}-\mathcal{E})^{2},

which is equivalent to

0<\sigma_{1}(\sigma_{1}-2\mathcal{E})
. This proves the first part.

Suppose that
\Delta<0
,
i.e.

\mathcal{E}^{2}<\mu
.
We derive the auxiliary inequality

(15)

\mu-2\sigma_{1}\mathcal{E}+\sigma_{1}^{2}>\mathcal{E}^{2}-2\sigma_{1}\mathcal{E}+\sigma_{1}^{2}=(\mathcal{E}-\sigma_{1})^{2}\geq 0.

This implies that
(f(\sigma_{1})-\sigma_{1})\sigma_{1}>0
because

(f(\sigma_{1})-\sigma_{1})\sigma_{1}=\left(\frac{\mu}{2\mathcal{E}-\sigma_{1}}-\sigma_{1}\right)\sigma_{1}=[\mu-\sigma_{1}(2\mathcal{E}-\sigma_{1})]\frac{\sigma_{1}}{2\mathcal{E}-\sigma_{1}}

Suppose that at
\sigma_{1}
there is not a tachyonic collision, which means

(16)

0\geq\sigma_{1}(\sigma_{1}-2\mathcal{E}).

This and Inequality (
15
) implies that

\sign[(f(\sigma_{1})-\sigma_{1})\sigma_{1}]=\sign[\mu-\sigma_{1}(2\mathcal{E}-\sigma_{1})]>0.

Now, if
\mathcal{E}>0
, from (
16
) we have
\sigma_{1}>0
and

f(\sigma_{1})>\sigma_{1}
.
If we iterate
f
to obtain a sequence
f^{j}(\sigma_{1})
,
j\geq 1
,
we see that it increases (as
\Delta<0
there is no fixed point)
until
f^{J}(\sigma_{1})>2\mathcal{E}
and at this moment there is a tachyonic collision.
1
1

1

It also holds that
f^{J+1}(\sigma_{1})<0
and there is one more tachyonic collision.

The case
\mathcal{E}<0
is analogous, considering that
\sigma_{1}<0
and the corresponding sequence is decreasing.

Suppose that
\Delta\geq 0
and assume that
(\sigma_{1}^{0}-\mathcal{E})^{2}>\Delta
(this is always the case if

\Delta=0
).
Since the interval between the fixed points is invariant by
f
we have that

(\sigma_{1}^{n}-\mathcal{E})^{2}>\Delta
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
,
i.e.
the iterates of
\sigma_{1}^{0}
by
f
are always outside that interval.
If
\mathcal{E}>0
and
\sigma_{1}^{0}
is at the right of the repeller and close to it, then the sequence of its iterates is increasing and for some
J>0
we have
f^{J}(\sigma_{1}^{0})>2\mathcal{E}
, which is the first tachyonic collision. The second one is next because if
x>2\mathcal{E}
then
f(x)<0
(
x=f^{J}(\sigma_{1}^{0}
).
The next iterates are positive (converging to the attractor) and there are no more tachyonic collisions.
∎

By Theorem
18
if
\Delta<0
then the tachyonic collisions are frequent

Thus, it seems interesting to give more details for the later case.

Remark 19
.

For a solution with tachyonic collisions we have that
\kappa>0
.
To prove it, we have to show that

From Equation (

If

Let

5.1.
An estimation for tachyonic collisions

The Newtonian potential energy is
U=-Gm_{1}m_{4}/R
, where

in units of time so that the speed of light equals 1,

R
is the distance between the particles 1 and 4,
i.e.

R=-2x_{1}^{n}
and
m_{1}m_{4}=\mu
.
That is

Considering Proposition
16
, in order to have a common limit value of the constant

we will assume that
\Delta=0
(
\mathcal{E}^{2}=\mu
).
From Lemma
12

If we identify the gravitational energy
U
and the energy of particles 2 and 3 we obtain

\frac{G\mu}{2x_{1}^{n}}=2\frac{k\mathcal{E}}{x_{1}^{n}}\Rightarrow k=\frac{G\mu}{4\mathcal{E}}=\frac{G\mathcal{E}}{4}.

Proposition
20
gives us that
|x_{1}^{n}|\leq 8|k|=2G|\mathcal{E}|

-th collision is tachyonic.
If
m

\mu

(because
\Delta=0
).

Consider that particles 1 and 4 are
heavy
, for instance they are neutrons,

.
This gives us a bound of

for the distance between the neutrons when they enter into the tachyonic collisions.
