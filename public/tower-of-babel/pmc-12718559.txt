Factors associated with the uptake of clinical breast examination for early detection of breast cancer: interaction analysis of the 2022 Tanzania Demographic and Health Survey and Malaria Indicator Survey
Authors: Tumaini Nyamhanga, Pankras Luoga, Heavenlight Paulo, Peter Wangwe, Jovinary Adam, Rosemary Eustace
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIn Tanzania, few studies have identified predictors of clinical breast examination (CBE). To the best of our knowledge, no published study has comprehensively identified factors associated with the uptake of CBE from a nationally representative sample. Therefore, this study fills this gap by conducting a secondary analysis of data from the Tanzania Demographic and Health Survey 2022 and the Malaria Indicator Survey. We answered this research question: What are the factors associated with the uptake of CBE for early detection of breast cancer among Tanzanian women aged 15–49 years?MethodsWe performed descriptive analysis by calculating weighted frequencies and percentages, which enabled us to determine the prevalence of CBE uptake across various characteristics of the study participants. We used the ‘svy’ command in Stata to assign sample weights and adjust for clustering effects and sample stratification. A multivariate binary logistic regression model was used to determine the statistical significance and strength of the influence of each independent variable on CBE uptake, after controlling for other explanatory variables.ResultsThe study has shown that women with low incomes, who are unemployed and those without health insurance have lower odds of undergoing CBE. The finding that there is an interaction between age and residence, as well as between age and education, in relation to the outcome variable (CBE), suggests that the relationship between age and CBE may be influenced differently based on where someone lives or their level of education. This could point to unique barriers or influences in different age groups, depending on their environment or educational background.ConclusionThe overall uptake of CBE among women aged 15–49 years in Tanzania is very low (5%). There is a need to urgently implement targeted breast health awareness education programmes at all health system levels.Keywords: education, Social Medicine, Sociodemographic Factors, Primary Prevention, Health Services Accessibility

Introduction

Breast cancer is a serious global health concern, with particularly high prevalence in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA).1 Despite the apparent lower disease frequency in SSA, breast cancer mortality rates are disproportionately higher than the rates in high-income countries.

Breast cancer in SSA shows a unique age-related pattern, with many cases occurring in women under 50, and peak incidence typically between ages 40 and 49.2 This is partly due to the region’s younger population and potentially earlier disease onset. However, women under 40 have the worst survival outcomes, with 5-year net survival as low as 36%, mainly because of late-stage diagnosis and limited access to timely treatment.3 Mortality increases with age, and older women also face high fatality rates due to late diagnosis and gaps in care.4 Likewise, in Tanzania, breast cancer constitutes 14.4% of all patients with newly diagnosed cancer, and most (80%) of these are diagnosed late with poor outcomes.5

Around the world, the key to improving breast cancer outcomes is early detection and treatment strategies. Nevertheless, in low- and middle-income nations (LMICs), such as those in SSA, survival rates remain low due to delayed presentation, which is a result of insufficient or a lack of early detection methods,26,8 including mammography.9,11

Therefore, early detection methods that are inexpensive, such as breast health awareness, breast self-examination (BSE) and clinical breast examination (CBE), are seen to be the first line of defence in enhancing outcomes and survival rates related to breast cancer.12,14 Although evaluation studies on the effectiveness of BSE and CBE in SSA are scarce, similar studies in India and Iran have found that BSE and CBE have a significant effect in detecting breast cancers at an early stage at the community level.15 16 Unfortunately, in Tanzania, breast health literacy—defined as knowledge of breast health and services for early detection of breast cancer—is still very low17 18 and rates of CBE are as low as 9% and 3% in the urban and rural settings, respectively.19 The determinants of CBE must be comprehensively explored to inform the development of strategies for increasing early detection and treatment of breast cancer in the country.

In Tanzania, few studies have identified predictors of CBE.17 20 However, these studies ultimately yielded surface-level correlations. To the best of our knowledge, no published study has progressed beyond surface-level correlations to provide an understanding of the nuanced and interdependent factors that influence CBE uptake. This study, therefore, fills this gap by conducting a secondary interaction analysis of data from the Tanzania Demographic and Health Survey and Malaria Indicator Survey 2022 (TDHS-MIS).19 21 This kind of analysis has enabled examining how different variables, such as demographics and socioeconomic factors, interact with each other to influence the decision to undergo a CBE.

Research question

What are the factors associated with the uptake of clinical breast examination for early detection of breast cancer among Tanzanian women aged 15–49 years?

Data and methods

Study design

This study analysed secondary data obtained from the TDHS-MIS 2022. The primary study employed a cross-sectional design.

Data

We used the most recent nationally representative 2022 TDHS-MIS data for this analysis. This survey was conducted from 24 February to 21 July 2022 by the Tanzania National Bureau of Statistics and the Office of the Chief Government Statistician in collaboration with the Ministry of Health, Tanzania Mainland and the Ministry of Health, Zanzibar.19 21 The 2022 TDHS-MIS employed a two-stage stratified cluster sampling design for households. In the first stage, a total of 629 clusters, or enumeration areas (EAs), were selected. Among them, 211 EAs were urban areas and 418 EAs were rural areas. The second stage involved selecting 26 households from each cluster, resulting in a total anticipated sample size of 16 354 households for the survey. A total of 15 699 eligible women aged 15–49 were selected, with 15 254 women completing the interview. A detailed description of the survey methods, data collection process and questionnaires is provided in the final report of the 2022 TDHS-MIS.19

Study variables

Outcome variable

The study’s outcome variable was the uptake of CBE among women aged 15–49 years. The respondents were asked: ‘Have you had a breast cancer clinical exam to detect breast cancer in the last 12 months?’ The response was binary with ‘Yes’ coded as ‘1’ denoting ‘have undergone CBE’ and ‘No’ coded as ‘0’denoting ‘have not undergone CBE’.

Independent variables

The explanatory variables were chosen based on their availability within the dataset and evidence from relevant literature on the topic.17 19 22 Thus, the variables included were age, educational achievement, wealth status, health insurance coverage, marital status, parity, currently pregnant, religion, frequency of watching television (TV), frequency of reading newspapers or magazines, frequency of listening to the radio, awareness of breast cancer, practiced BSE in the last 12 months, visited health facility in the last 12 months and distance to health facility and type of place of residence (see online supplemental figure 1). Statistical analyses

We used Stata V.18 for our data analysis. We performed descriptive analysis by calculating weighted frequencies and percentages, which enabled us to determine the prevalence of CBE uptake and describe the background characteristics of the study participants. We used the ‘svy’ command in Stata to assign sample weights and adjust for clustering effects and sample stratification. The bivariate analysis was conducted using the χ2 test of association to assess the relationships between CBE uptake and all independent variables. We checked for multicollinearity between the independent variables using the variance inflation factor, and no variable had a score of 5 or higher, indicating a lack of significant collinearity. The multivariate binary logistic regression model was used to determine the statistical significance and the strength of the influence of each independent variable (age, educational achievement, wealth status, health insurance coverage, marital status, parity, currently pregnant, religion, frequency of watching TV, frequency of reading newspapers or magazines, frequency of listening to the radio, awareness of breast cancer, practiced BSE in the last 12 months, visited health facility last 12 months and distance to health facility and type of place of residence) on the CBE uptake after controlling for other explanatory variables. Additionally, interaction analyses were conducted to assess how the effects of age interacted with residence type and educational attainment on CBE uptake. Separate logistic regression models were constructed for these interaction terms to explore their combined influence on CBE uptake. The Akaike information criterion (AIC) was used to compare different models, with the model having the lowest AIC being considered the best. The likelihood ratio test was used to assess the overall significance of the model. The subgroup analyses were conducted as a sensitivity analysis of interaction models. The significance level was set at 5%.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics of the study participants

The analysis for this study included a total sample of 15 254 women of reproductive age (15–49 years) in Tanzania. Most of the study participants were aged 15–29 years (64.3%). More than half of the study participants (54.7%) resided in rural areas. Most participants reported being employed (64.3%), and nearly half (48.1%) had their wealth categorised in the richest category. The majority had primary education (53.2%), and 16.1% reported having no formal education. The majority (60.7%) reported never having been married, while more than two-thirds (94.2%) were not covered by health insurance. Subgroup analysis

The subgroup analysis (online supplemental table 1) revealed interactions between age, residence and CBE uptake. Among younger women (aged 15–29 years), those residing in urban areas had 70% lower odds of undergoing CBE compared with their counterparts aged 30–49 years (aOR=0.30, 95% CI 0.22 to 0.42). Similarly, rural areas had 54% lower odds of undergoing CBE compared with older women aged 30–49 years (aOR=0.43, 95% CI 0.31 to 0.59).

Online supplemental table 2 further indicates that the effect of education on CBE uptake differs by age group. Among women aged 30–49 years, those with primary education or less had 36% lower odds of undergoing CBE compared with those with secondary education or higher (aOR=0.64, 95% CI 0.49 to 0.85).

Discussion

The goal of Tanzania’s national guidelines for early diagnosis of breast cancer and referral for treatment is to identify breast cancer as soon as possible and provide diagnosis and treatment in the earliest stages, when it is more effective, has fewer complications and is less costly. As such, the national guidelines recommend three methods for early diagnosis of breast cancer, namely: breast health awareness, CBE and diagnostic imaging ultrasound/mammography. According to the guidelines, CBE should be offered to: symptomatic women presenting with signs or symptoms; asymptomatic women aged 30+ years as a breast awareness education tool; women with a family history of breast cancer at a young age, such women may have CBE performed before 30 years of age.23 Indeed, Tanzania’s promotion of CBE is supported by the WHO, which recommends CBE as the most cost-effective screening modality for women in low-resource settings,24 as further corroborated by systematic reviews in SSA.25

Despite the existence of the above-cited national guidelines, calling for the implementation of low-cost and effective early detection strategies such as CBE,23 24 26 the overall uptake of CBE is as low as 5%. The findings mirror a recent study in Dar es Salaam, which reported CBE prevalence of 6.2%.27 These very low uptake rates of CBE are consistent with those of Kenya (5%), another SSA country.28 One possible explanation for these low rates is the lack of routine early detection methods in most LMICs. That is, CBE is occasionally done through donor-funded campaigns.18

Regarding factors associated with the uptake of CBE, the study has shown that poor women (Model 1: aOR=0.46, 95% CI 0.32 to 0.66; Model 2: aOR=0.45, 95% CI 0.32 to 0.65) and those who were not employed (Model 1: aOR=0.77, 95% CI 0.60 to 0.99; Model 2: aOR=0.79, 95% CI 0.61 to 1.01) had lower odds of undergoing CBE compared with women with rich wealth status. These findings are corroborated by another Demographic and Health Survey article for Tanzania, which reported that women who had limited ownership of resources and less decision-making power had lower odds of undergoing CBE.20 A possible explanation for the lower uptake of CBE among poor women is that most people are aware that there are no free healthcare services. Thus, even when women are encouraged to undergo CBE, they hesitate due to concerns about the cost implications.18 27 This assertion is corroborated by a similar finding from the current study, which shows that the uninsured women (Model 1: aOR=0.41, 95% CI 0.33 to 0.52; Model 2: aOR=0.42, 95% CI 0.33 to 0.53) had lower odds of undergoing CBE compared with those with health insurance. A lack of health insurance is known to affect access to healthcare services.29,32 In Tanzania, less than 20% of the population is covered by health insurance, with the National Health Insurance Fund being mandatory for all public sector employees and covering only 6.6% of Tanzania’s population.33 34 The Community Health Fund, a voluntary, community-based public social insurance fund designed for individuals with low incomes and those employed in the informal sector, covers only 7.3% of Tanzanians.33 35 Less than 1% of the population is covered by private health insurance schemes. Because fewer than 20% of the population has insurance, more than 80% of Tanzanians pay for medical services out of pocket. Consequently, low-income women are less likely to prioritise preventive practices such as CBE. Similar findings have been reported in Tanzania and elsewhere in SSA.23 36 37

Likewise, we found that women who had not visited healthcare facilities within the last 12 months (Model 1: aOR=0.73, 95% CI 0.57 to 0.92; Model 2: aOR=0.72, 95% CI 0.57 to 0.92) had lower odds of undergoing breast cancer screening (CBE) compared with those who had visited healthcare facilities. A possible explanation is that women who visit a health facility at least once a year are likely to be informed about breast cancer and the advantages of CBE through posters or leaflet displays. Similar findings have been reported by Ba et al.23

Furthermore, this study demonstrated that women who had no media exposure (not listening to the radio (Model 1: aOR=0.73, 95% CI 0.57 to 0.92; Model 2: aOR=0.72, 95% CI 0.57 to 0.92), and not watching TV (Model 1: aOR=0.80, 95% CI 0.61, 1.04; Model 2: aOR=0.80, 95% CI 0.61 to 1.05) had lower odds of undergoing CBE compared with those who reported having had media exposure. The radio and TV are essential sources of health education messages. However, gender norms and roles may limit some women’s access to listening to the radio or watching TV at times when health educational sessions are on air.31 38

In contrast, women who were using contraceptives had higher odds of undergoing CBE (Model 1: aOR=1.62, 95% CI 1.36 to 1.94; Model 2: aOR=1.61, 95% CI 1.34 to 1.93). This suggests that such women had access to healthcare delivery systems from which they could get multiple benefits, including contraceptives and CBE, as reported by a similar study in Ghana.39

Additionally, although bivariate analysis revealed a significant relationship between marital status and CBE uptake (p=0.0024), multivariate-adjusted estimates showed no significant association (Model 1: aOR=0.99, 95% CI 0.79 to 1.24; Model 2: aOR=0.90, 95% CI 0.78 to 1.21). This finding suggests marital status alone played a limited role, if any, in influencing the uptake of CBE. Indeed, a qualitative study18 that we conducted in the Songwe region in Tanzania revealed that limited partner support, attributable to men’s lack of knowledge about breast cancer, was one of the barriers to early detection of breast cancer.

Additionally, distance to the health facility was not significantly associated with the uptake of CBE, after adjusting for other confounding variables (Model 1: aOR=1.03, 95% CI 0.81 to 1.34; Model 2: aOR=1.04, 95% CI 0.81 to 1.34). This suggests that health sector developments in Tanzania have significantly reduced the distance to health facilities, making it a less compelling reason for not accessing facility-based care, such as CBE. Most of the women (10 861 (71.2%)) who participated in this study reported that distance to healthcare was not a problem.

Finally, the findings from the subgroup analysis, as presented in onlinesupplemental tables 1 2, reveal several essential trends in the likelihood of women undergoing CBE based on age, location and education level.

Age and location interaction

Younger women (aged 15–29 years) in urban areas have significantly lower odds (70% lower) of undergoing CBE compared with older women (aged 30–49 years). This suggests that age, combined with living in urban areas, plays a crucial role in determining whether women get access to or seek CBE. The aOR of 0.30 (with a 95% CI 0.22 to 0.42) strongly indicates that younger urban women are less likely to undergo this preventative health measure than older women in the same setting.

The same pattern is observed in rural areas, though to a lesser degree. Young women (aged 15–29 years) in rural areas are still 54% less likely to undergo CBE than older women in the same age group (aOR=0.43, 95% CI 0.31 to 0.59). The odds are relatively much lower in urban areas compared with rural areas. These findings suggest two issues. One, young women in urban and rural settings experience different levels of barriers, with those in urban settings being more disadvantaged. In both settings, younger women may not regularly interact with healthcare providers or may be less inclined to seek medical services unless they perceive a direct health concern. This is especially true for preventive measures, which may not feel as urgent as seeking care for an illness or injury. On the other hand, older women (30–49 years) might be more proactive about their health and screenings, especially as they approach the age when breast cancer risk increases. They might also be more likely to follow doctors’ recommendations for regular screening or might have prior experience with breast health issues, which makes them more aware of the need for CBE. Therefore, the finding suggests that targeted interventions to raise awareness about breast cancer risks and the importance of regular screenings among younger women are needed.

Age and educational attainment interaction

Among women aged 30–49 years, educational attainment has a significant impact on the likelihood of undergoing CBE. Women with lower educational attainment (primary education or below) have 36% lower odds of undergoing CBE compared with those with higher educational attainment (secondary education or above). The aOR of 0.64 (95% CI 0.49 to 0.85) indicates a clear association between higher education levels and greater participation in CBE. This finding suggests that education might influence both awareness of breast health and the ability to access medical services for screenings.

Strengths, limitations and implications of the study

The study draws its strengths from using the DHS’s large sample, which provides findings that are nationally representative and generalisable. However, the cross-sectional nature of the study limited the establishment of causal-effect relationships between the independent and dependent variables. Likewise, another limitation of our analysis is the inability to disentangle age effects from cohort effects in the observed interactions between CBE uptake and socio-demographic factors such as education and residence. While differences at younger ages may suggest that barriers diminish with age (an age effect), they could also reflect persistent disparities across birth cohorts (a cohort effect), which would be more concerning. Given the cross-sectional nature of the data, we are unable to determine which explanation predominates.

In addition, the study analysed secondary data and, therefore, could inherently incorporate the errors associated with the sampling design and other biases introduced during data collection. Nevertheless, the use of national survey data, along with the application of weighting, offers an advantage in eliminating biases typically associated with pooling observational data, sampling bias and measurement error. Overall, the multiple factors associated with low uptake of CBE revealed in this study suggest a need to design multilevel intervention studies that will contribute to closing the gap in the low uptake of CBE among rural women in Tanzania.18 40

Conclusion

This study has revealed a disappointingly low level of CBE uptake that partly explains why most women with breast cancer in Tanzania are diagnosed at a late stage. Specifically, the study has shown that women with low income, unemployed and without health insurance had lower odds of undergoing CBE. The finding that there is an interaction between age and residence, as well as between age and education, in relation to the outcome variable (CBE), suggests that the relationship between age and CBE may be influenced differently based on where someone lives or their level of education. This could indicate unique barriers or influences in different age groups, depending on their environment or educational background.

Therefore, to enhance early detection of breast cancer through CBE, it is essential to implement targeted short-term, medium-term and long-term measures. The short-term measures include the implementation of breast health awareness education programmes at all health system levels (Community, Dispensary services, Health Centre services, District Hospital services, Regional Hospital services, Zonal Referral Hospital services, National Level and Specialised Hospital services) as recommended by the national guidelines for early diagnosis of breast cancer and referral for treatment.20

Furthermore, medium-term and long-term measures include improving education by supporting most women to attain at least secondary education, as well as enhancing economic productivity to reduce poverty in rural settings.
