Occupational stress and its adverse health effects among metal mining workers: a cross-sectional study in China
Authors: Wenya Liu, Sijia Lvqiu, Duoduo Wang, Xinlv Dong, Yunfeng Nie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates the prevalence of occupational stress among metal mining workers in China and its association with depressive symptoms, insomnia, and work-related musculoskeletal disorders (WMSDs), while also exploring insomnia as a potential mediator. A cross-sectional survey of 1130 metal mining workers in China was conducted from June to November 2023. Occupational stress, depressive symptoms, insomnia, and WMSDs were measured using standardized instruments. Due to the high prevalence of the outcomes, Poisson regression models with robust variance were used to estimate adjusted prevalence ratios (RRs). Subgroup and mediation analyses were also performed. Restricted cubic splines were used to flexibly model the dose–response relationship between occupational stress scores and health outcomes. The prevalence of occupational stress was 28.8%. After adjustment, workers with occupational stress had a higher risk of depressive symptoms (RR = 3.24, 95% CI 2.42–4.34), insomnia (RR = 1.47, 95% CI 1.25–1.73), and WMSDs (RR = 1.19, 95% CI 1.10–1.28). Younger workers and those with lower incomes appeared more vulnerable. Insomnia was confirmed as a partial mediator in the relationship between occupational stress and both depressive symptoms and WMSDs. A clear dose–response relationship was observed; spline analysis further revealed a non-linear association for depressive symptoms, with the risk accelerating sharply at higher stress scores. Occupational stress was significantly associated with adverse mental and physical health outcomes among metal mining workers. Insomnia was identified as a potential mediating pathway, while the association with depressive symptoms was non-linear, characterized by a sharply higher risk at high stress scores. Introduction

With the rapid development of the global economy, labor-intensive industries and high-intensity work models have become increasingly prevalent. As occupational demands and workplace competition intensify, stress levels among workers continue to rise, contributing to a growing incidence of mental health issues1. Studies indicate that over 60% of professionals in the United States identify work-related stress as their primary source of pressure, with more than 40% experiencing chronic workplace stress and anxiety2. The Healthy China Program (2019–2030) recognizes occupational stress as a major workplace health challenge, highlighting the urgent need for effective interventions3.

Occupational stress, a key concept in occupational psychology, refers to the emotional, cognitive, physiological, and behavioral responses triggered by adverse work-related factors such as job demands, organizational structure, and work environment1. When workers struggle to meet job expectations, a mismatch arises between the individual, workplace, and organization, further exacerbating stress levels4,5. Early symptoms of occupational stress are primarily psychological, including anxiety, irritability, depression, and reduced job satisfaction6,7. Prolonged exposure to high-stress conditions can lead to severe physical health consequences, including coronary artery disease, hypertension, immune system dysfunction, and musculoskeletal disorders8–10. Additionally, occupational stress impairs cognitive function and emotional stability, increasing the risk of mental health disorders ranging from mild distress to severe psychiatric conditions11,12.

Beyond its impact on individual health, occupational stress imposes a significant economic burden13–15. The International Labour Organization (ILO) estimates that stress-related productivity losses and medical expenses result in approximately $300 billion in global economic losses annually16. In the United States alone, healthcare costs linked to stress-induced illnesses range from $500 billion to $1 trillion17, while in the United Kingdom, stress-related absenteeism accounts for millions of lost workdays each year. Given its widespread consequences, occupational stress is now recognized as a critical public health concern with far-reaching socioeconomic implications18,19.

While the link between occupational stress and adverse health outcomes is well-documented20–23, high-risk industries such as mining present a unique and amplified set of challenges that warrant specific investigation. Metal mining workers, in particular, are exposed to a distinct constellation of severe stressors that extend far beyond those found in typical industrial settings. These include the inherent physical dangers of underground work, the psychological strain of potential accidents, prolonged periods of isolation from family, and harsh environmental conditions such as poor ventilation, extreme temperatures, and excessive noise and vibration24. Furthermore, their psychological stress may be compounded by the demanding physical exertion and the disruptive nature of shift work, which is known to severely impact sleep patterns and circadian rhythms25. This unique combination of intense psychosocial, physical, and environmental demands suggests that the pathways linking stress to health outcomes in miners may be more pronounced or follow different patterns than in other populations.

Therefore, this study aims to not only confirm the general stress-health model within this high-risk group but also to explore specific pathways, such as the mediating role of insomnia, which may be particularly pronounced given the disruptive nature of shift work common in mining, .and to characterize the precise dose–response curve between stress and health risks using flexible regression models.

Materials and methods

Study population

This study utilized data from the 2023 Occupational Health Literacy Monitoring and Intervention Program for Key Populations in Hunan Province, China. To ensure the representativeness of the sample, a stratified random sampling method was employed. Based on the official classification standards for industrial enterprises, participating enterprises were stratified by size into three categories: large (≥ 999 workers), medium (300–999 workers), and small (fewer than 300 workers)26. Within each stratum, a fixed number of enterprises was randomly selected. Workers aged 16–60 with ≥ 6 months of employment were subsequently enrolled through simple random sampling. Exclusion criteria encompassed: (1) non-frontline personnel, defined as administrative, clerical, and managerial staff who do not engage in manual labor in extraction or processing areas; (2) pregnancy/lactation; (3) self-reported diagnosis of a severe chronic disease (e.g., cancer) that could independently affect the outcomes of interest; and (4) refusal to participate in the survey. A total of 1200 employees were surveyed to this cross-sectional study. Following the exclusion of 70 questionnaires with incomplete or inconsistent data, 1130 valid questionnaires were retained, achieving a 94.16% valid response rate (Supplemental Figure S1). The Ethics Committee of Hunan Prevention and Treatment Institute for Occupational Diseases, University of South China approved this study (No. 20230308001). Written informed consent explicitly documented voluntary participation, withdrawal rights without penalty, potential risks (including psychological distress from workplace identification), and data anonymization protocols, with full compliance to Declaration of Helsinki principles for observational research.

Basic information collection

Demographic characteristics, basic occupational features, occupational stress, and depressive symptoms were assessed using the Individual Questionnaire of the National Key Population Occupational Health Literacy Monitoring Survey (officially designated as Health Statistics Form 117 by the National Bureau of Statistics of China, published in 2022)27. For brevity, it will be henceforth referred to as "‌the Questionnaire" within this context. The demographic section covered gender, age, ethnicity, marital status, education level, and monthly income. Occupational characteristics included weekly working hours; night shifts; and shift patterns. The categorization of weekly working hours (≤ 40, 41–44, 45–48, 49–54, and ≥ 55) was designed based on established benchmarks and alignment with key thresholds identified in international research28,29. Participants completed the questionnaire via WeChat QR code scanning.

Occupational stress assessment

Occupational stress was assessed using the Core Occupational Stress Scale (COSS)30, a validated instrument with 17 items distributed across four dimensions: social support, organizational rewards, job demands, and autonomy. This scale has been widely applied in studies of occupational stress in Chinese populations, demonstrating its utility in assessing employee stress levels and yielding positive outcomes in relevant research31. A 5-point Likert scale (1 = “strongly disagree” to 5 = “strongly agree”) was employed. Consistent with the scale’s validation protocol, reverse scoring (6 minus raw score) was applied to the “social support” and “autonomy” dimensions. This procedure ensures that higher scores across all dimensions consistently indicate greater stress, with lower levels of support and autonomy contributing to higher overall stress scores. Stress levels were categorized: < 48 (no stress), 48- < 52 (mild), 52- < 56 (moderate), and ≥ 56 (severe). Mild to severe cases were combined as "presence of occupational stress." The total score was also used as a continuous variable in the dose–response spline analysis. The scale demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.761).

Depressive symptoms assessment

Depressive symptoms were evaluated using the 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9), which was originally validated by Kroenke et al. (2001)32. In the present study, the Chinese version of the PHQ-9 was utilized, which has been culturally adapted and validated by researchers in China33,34. Participants rated symptom frequency over the preceding two weeks on a 4-point scale (0 = Not at all to 3 = Nearly every day). A total score ≥ 10 indicated the presence of depressive symptoms, with the scale showing excellent reliability in this study (Cronbach’s α = 0.872).

Insomnia symptoms assessment

Insomnia symptoms were evaluated using a 3-item sleep module adapted from the standardized National Occupational Health Literacy Questionnaire (Health Statistics Form 117), which was derived from Nakata’s insomnia questionnaire35,36. Insomnia symptoms were identified through these three items in the Questionnaire: 1) Sleep latency > 30 min ("How long does it typically take you to fall asleep?"), 2) Persistent difficulty initiating sleep ("Do you experience difficulty falling asleep almost daily?"), 3) Early morning awakening ("Do you wake up too early almost daily?"). Meeting any criterion defined insomnia presence, supported by satisfactory scale reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.729). While this approach is broader than strict clinical criteria (e.g., DSM-5), it is designed for large-scale occupational health screening to identify workers at risk for sleep disturbances and was therefore deemed appropriate for the public health scope of this study.

Musculoskeletal disorders assessment

Work-related musculoskeletal disorders (WMSDs) were assessed using a validated questionnaire developed by Yang et al.37, which was based on the Nordic Musculoskeletal Questionnaire (NMQ)38 and the Dutch Musculoskeletal Questionnaire (DMQ)39. This questionnaire was adapted to suit the national context and research experience of Chinese scholars, making it suitable for evaluating WMSDs in the Chinese population. The questionnaire has demonstrated high structural validity, discriminant validity, and predictive validity, as supported by empirical evidence40. WMSDs were screened using a 9-body-region inventory (neck, shoulders, back, elbows, lower back, wrists, hips, knees, ankles/feet), excluding symptoms caused by non-work-related injuries. Participants reporting pain or discomfort in any region during the past year (excluding non-work-related injuries) were classified as WMSDs-positive. The instrument showed strong consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.852).

Quality control

Quality control measures were implemented throughout the study. The sampling protocol was developed and approved by an expert panel prior to data collection. A standardized implementation plan was established, defining roles for project leaders, coordinators, field investigators, quality control supervisors, and data managers, all of whom received unified training covering the study protocol, questionnaire administration, electronic survey completion guidelines, on-site investigation techniques, and quality assurance procedures. During field surveys, investigators provided real-time guidance to participants on electronic questionnaire completion and addressed technical issues, while quality control supervisors monitored compliance. Collected data underwent centralized management, where data managers conducted rigorous checks, including completeness verification and logical validation of responses.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.2). Socio-demographic and occupational characteristics were summarized with descriptive statistics (means ± SD, proportions, or frequencies). Continuous variables were assessed for normality (Shapiro–Wilk test) and homogeneity of variance (Levene’s test), with Student’s t-tests applied to normally distributed data. Categorical variables were analyzed via chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. Given that the prevalence of all three health outcomes exceeded 10%, standard logistic regression could yield odds ratios (ORs) that substantially overestimate the true relative risks. Therefore, Poisson regression models with robust variance estimation were employed to directly estimate risk ratios (RRs) and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between occupational stress and health outcomes. Both crude and adjusted models were fitted, controlling for age, ethnicity, education, monthly income, weekly working hours, night shifts, and shift work.

To characterize the continuous dose–response relationship, we used restricted cubic splines (RCS) with four knots placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of the stress score distribution. The RCS terms were incorporated into the multivariable Poisson models. We formally tested for potential non-linearity using a Wald test for the spline components. If the test for non-linearity was significant (P < 0.05), the RCS model was retained. Otherwise, the variable was treated as a linear term in the final model.

Subgroup analyses were performed to explore whether the association between occupational stress and health outcomes varied across strata of age, monthly income, weekly working hours, and night shifts. Our primary approach was a stratified analysis, where separate Poisson regression models were fitted within each subgroup stratum to estimate the stratum-specific RR. To formally test for effect modification across strata, we also constructed a separate unified regression model for each outcome that included a multiplicative interaction term (e.g., occupational stress × age group). The significance of this term was assessed using a likelihood ratio test (LRT) to generate the P for interaction.

A two-step analysis was used to examine the mediating role of insomnia. First, a logistic regression model assessed the association between occupational stress and insomnia (path a). Second, a Poisson regression model assessed the association between insomnia and the final health outcome, controlling for occupational stress (path b). Statistical significance was defined as two-tailed P < 0.05.

Results

Participant characteristics and prevalence of occupational stress

A total of 1130 participants were enrolled (82.3% male). The mean age was 45.63 ± 8.89 years, with the majority of workers in the 40– < 50 (37.6%) and 50– < 60 (37.3%) age groups. The sample was predominantly of Han ethnicity (91.9%). Educational attainment was mainly at the junior high school level (45.8%), and the most common monthly income category was 3,000–4,999 CNY (45.8%). A notable portion of the workforce engaged in long work hours (19.0% worked ≥ 55 h weekly), night shifts (50.9%), and rotational shifts (57.5%) (Table 1).

Association between occupational stress and health outcomes

Given the significant differences in age, ethnicity, education level, monthly income, weekly working hours, night shift, and shift work status among different occupational stress and health condition groups (P < 0.05), these variables were considered potential confounders. For insomnia symptoms, the adjusted RR was 1.47 (95% CI 1.25–1.73), and for WMSDs, it was 1.19 (95% CI 1.10–1.28) (Table 3).

Dose–response relationship

To investigate a potential dose–response relationship between the severity of occupational stress and health outcomes, we stratified occupational stress into four severity levels based on the established cut-off points of the COSS instrument (see Supplementary Table S2 for prevalence details). Compared to workers with no stress, the adjusted risk for depressive symptoms was progressively higher for mild (RR = 2.38) to moderate (RR = 4.22) and severe stress (RR = 6.40). Similar graded increases in risk were observed for insomnia symptoms and WMSDs (Table 4).

After adjustment, one-point increment in the occupational stress score was associated with a 4.2% higher risk of insomnia (RR = 1.04, 95% CI 1.03–1.06, P < 0.001) and a 1.9% higher risk of WMSDs (RR = 1.02, 95% CI 1.01–1.03, P < 0.001).

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses were conducted to explore the association between occupational stress and health outcomes across various subgroups (Fig. 2). For depressive symptoms (Fig. 2A) and WMSDs (Fig. 2C), the positive association with occupational stress was consistently observed across all subgroups, and formal tests for interaction found no significant effect modification (all P for interaction > 0.05). For insomnia symptoms (Fig. 2B), a significant interaction was detected with age (P for interaction = 0.047). The stratified RRs suggest a stronger association in younger age groups, particularly among workers aged 16 to < 30 years (RR = 2.10, 95% CI 1.20–3.68).

Mediation analysis

We conducted a two-step analysis to assess whether insomnia symptoms mediated the relationship between occupational stress and and two health outcomes: depressive symptoms and WMSDs. For depressive symptoms, the analysis showed that occupational stress was significantly associated with insomnia symptoms (path a: OR = 1.92, 95% CI 1.44–2.56, P < 0.001). Second, a Poisson regression model showed that insomnia symptoms were strongly and significantly associated with an increased risk of depressive symptoms, even after controlling for the direct effect of occupational stress (path b: RR = 3.42, 95% CI 2.49–4.71, P < 0.001). The direct effect of occupational stress on depressive symptoms also remained significant in the final model (RR = 2.65, 95% CI 1.97–3.56, P < 0.001).

Similarly, for WMSDs, the pathway from occupational stress to insomnia symptoms was identical and significant (path a: OR = 1.92, 95% CI 1.44–2.56, P < 0.001). The subsequent pathway from insomnia symptoms to WMSDs was also significant after controlling for occupational stress (path b: RR = 1.16, 95% CI 1.08–1.25, P < 0.001). The direct effect of occupational stress on WMSDs also remained significant in the final model (RR = 1.16, 95% CI 1.08–1.25, P < 0.001). The significant findings for both path a and path b for both outcomes, combined with persistent direct effects, provide strong evidence that insomnia acts as a partial mediator.

Discussion

In this large cross-sectional study of Chinese metal mining workers, occupational stress was strongly and significantly associated with a higher prevalence of screening positive for depressive symptoms, insomnia, and work-related musculoskeletal disorders (WMSDs). Our findings advance the understanding of these relationships in three key ways: first, by employing robust statistical methods to estimate risk ratios (RRs), which provide a more accurate measure of association than odds ratios in high-prevalence settings; second, by identifying insomnia as a significant partial mediator, elucidating a critical pathway through which stress impacts health; and third, by demonstrating a clear dose–response relationship whose form differs across health outcomes.

The prevalence of occupational stress (28.8%) in our sample is lower than that reported in some studies of Chinese miners (e.g., 42.65% among copper-nickel miners 41) but comparable to industrial populations (27.40%)42. However, such comparisons warrant caution due to potential methodological differences in assessment tools across studies. A more robust finding from our study is the clear dose–response relationship between occupational stress and all three health outcomes. Our analysis of occupational stress as a categorical variable first established a clear graded association, but a more nuanced understanding emerged from continuous analysis, which revealed that the nature of this relationship is heterogeneous.

The association with depressive symptoms was significantly non-linear, suggesting a potential threshold effect where risk accelerates dramatically at higher stress levels. This may reflect the collapse of coping mechanisms under severe stress43, or the triggering of more severe neurobiological changes, such as HPA axis dysregulation44. In contrast, the linear associations for insomnia and WMSDs suggest a more direct, cumulative physiological burden, a concept central to the theory of allostatic load45. This divergence implies that different psychobiological pathways may link occupational stress to somatic versus affective symptoms46. Our findings are consistent with the broader literature, as meta-analyses have confirmed that a greater psychosocial “dose” of stress is associated with a higher burden of musculoskeletal pain47, and systematic reviews support that cumulative stress is a critical determinant of sleep disturbance48.

A key contribution of this study is the elucidation of insomnia’s role as a potential partial mediator. The analysis provided strong evidence for a statistical pathway where occupational stress is associated with a higher likelihood of insomnia, which in turn is associated with a substantially higher risk of both depressive symptoms and WMSDs. This aligns with models where sleep disturbance is a core mechanism linking stressors to both psychological and somatic symptoms46,48,49. Research has demonstrated that chronic stress can activate the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis50,51, leading to physiological changes that impair sleep quality. Our findings extend this model to the mining population, suggesting that while stress has a strong direct impact on health, a portion of its detrimental effect is channeled through the disruption of sleep.

Our subgroup analyses suggest that the association between occupational stress and health may not be uniform across all workers. The relationship with insomnia appeared stronger among younger workers. This finding, while requiring cautious interpretation due to the smaller sample size in the youngest subgroup, is consistent with literature suggesting that younger workers may be more vulnerable due to factors such as career uncertainty and less developed coping strategies52. Similarly, the stronger association between stress and WMSDs among low-income workers supports the "socioeconomic status-health gradient" theory, which posits that financial strain and limited access to health resources can amplify the negative health consequences of stressy53,54.

Our findings offer several practical implications for occupational health policy in the high-risk mining sector. The pronounced non-linear relationship between occupational stress and depressive symptoms (Fig. 1) strongly suggests that a universal approach to stress management may be inefficient. Instead, health resources should be strategically allocated, prioritizing targeted screening to identify workers whose occupational stress scores are approaching the high-risk acceleration threshold. Furthermore, our subgroup analysis indicates that younger workers may represent a vulnerable population requiring enhanced surveillance. Crucially, the mediating role of insomnia reveals that merely addressing psychosocial stressors is insufficient. A comprehensive occupational health strategy must be multi-pronged, incorporating proactive measures such as optimizing shift planning to align with circadian principles and implementing evidence-based sleep interventions (e.g., cognitive-behavioral therapy for insomnia). By treating sleep health not as a secondary issue but as a core component of worker well-being, management and occupational health services within the mining industry can more effectively mitigate the cascading negative health consequences of occupational stress.

Despite its important findings, this study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes any conclusions about causality. Second, while we upgraded our analysis to use Poisson regression, the use of self-report questionnaires is subject to reporting bias. Third, the definition of insomnia was based on a screening tool designed for large-scale occupational health surveys rather than on strict clinical diagnostic criteria, such as the DSM-5. Consequently, the prevalence of insomnia reported in our study may be overestimated compared to that of clinically diagnosable insomnia disorders, although this approach is appropriate for identifying at-risk populations in a public health context. Fourth, a significant limitation is that this study did not account for specific physical and chemical occupational exposures prevalent in the mining industry, such as heavy metal dust, noise, and whole-body vibration. These factors are known to be independently associated with both musculoskeletal disorders and mental health outcomes and represent critical potential confounders. Future research should aim to integrate psychosocial and physical exposure assessments to disentangle these complex relationships. Lastly, as the sample was drawn from a single province, the generalizability of the findings may be limited.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study confirms a significant association between occupational stress and the prevalence of depressive symptoms, insomnia, and WMSDs among metal mining workers. By using robust statistical methods and exploring the specific shape of the dose–response relationships, we provide a more nuanced understanding of these risks. We identified insomnia as a partial, yet significant, mediator in these relationships. The findings underscore the need for targeted workplace interventions that not only address psychosocial stressors but also focus on improving sleep health as a potential pathway to mitigate the broader negative health impacts of occupational stress in this demanding and high-risk industry.
