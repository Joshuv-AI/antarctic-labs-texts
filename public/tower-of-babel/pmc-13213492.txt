Identification of Sn5 Active Site on SnO2(110) for CO2 Electroreduction via Constant-Potential Method and Microkinetic Modeling
Authors: Wenyu Pang, Hai-Yan Su, Jiarui Wang, Zhisheng Mao, Yu Shao, Xianfeng Hao, Yongchao Jia, Yuanhui Xu, Keju Sun, Faming Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Although tin oxide catalysts have been extensively used in electrocatalytic CO2 reduction (CO2ER) to HCOOH (including both HCOO– and HCOOH), the identification of the active site and the underlying reaction mechanism remain subjects of debate. Through constant-potential density functional theory (DFT) calculations and microkinetic simulations, we have pinpointed the Sn5 site on SnO2(110) surfaces as the active site, where CO2ER proceeds via a CO2_n (binding through O atoms)-mediated mechanism, noticeably distinct from the mechanisms identified via the constant-charge method. The significant stabilization of CO2_n species at the Sn5 site can be explained by the hard and soft acids and bases (HSAB) principle. Furthermore, the synergetic interaction between Sn5 and Sn4 sites offers an additional pathway for the formation of adsorbed hydrogen species at potentials more negative than −1.2 V, thereby enhancing the current density and Faradaic efficiency of HCOOH on SnO2(110). The simulated Faradaic efficiencies and current densities of products align well with experimental observations on tin oxide catalysts. This study highlights the essential role of the constant-potential method in elucidating the active site and CO2ER mechanism on SnO2(110), providing insights that can enrich the design principles for CO2ER and other technologically relevant reactions.

Introduction

Electrocatalytic CO2 reduction reaction (CO2ER) driven by renewable electricity offers a potential route to produce high-value-added chemicals and fuels, facilitating carbon-neutral energy cycles and mitigating the greenhouse effect. Among the possible CO2ER products, formic acid (HCOOH) stands out as a high-value liquid fuel with inherent advantages in energy density, storage, and broad industrial applicability, including pharmaceuticals, metallurgy, and hydrogen storage. Nevertheless, existing CO2-to-HCOOH electrocatalysts still fall short of meeting practical application standards. This is particularly evident in their ability to maintain a high Faradaic efficiency for HCOOH (including both formic acid and formate, hereafter referred to collectively) of over 70% at industrial-level current densities exceeding 200 mA cm–2. , Consequently, there is an urgent need to develop enhanced electrocatalysts for efficient CO2ER to HCOOH.

Tin oxide catalysts are typically used for CO2ER to HCOOH. However, the nature of the active sites and reaction mechanism on tin oxide catalysts remain under debate. , For instance, Qiao et al. have shown that the stabilization of Sn4+ through single-atom Cu doping is a prerequisite for efficient HCOOH production at high current densities. Liu et al. claim that the Sn2+ sites adjacent to oxygen vacancies on SnO2(110) contributes to the high HCOOH selectivity at low overpotentials. Moreover, Yao et al. have suggested that the synergistic role of mixed Sn4+/Sn2+ states and oxygen vacancies are responsible for CO2ER. These conflicting views hinder the rational design and optimization of catalysts, highlighting the need for fundamental studies to unbiasedly assess the intrinsic activity of different structural motifs/sites and their interactions. A key challenge in this endeavor lies in accurately unraveling the reaction mechanism and describing the associated kinetics.

In situ attenuated total reflection infrared (ATR–IR) have suggested that CO2 activation occurs via a one-electron transfer process, generating a *CO2 – species on the Sn-based catalysts. This species is subsequently protonated at the C end to yield *HCOO, which can undergo further electron transfer or proton-coupled electron transfer (PCET) to yield HCOOH. Additionally, ATR–IR studies have detected *COOH species, likely formed by protonation of *CO2 – at the oxygen atom. The *COOH species can either undergo hydrogen addition to produce HCOOH or C–O bond cleavage to yield CO.

However, many theoretical studies investigate the CO2ER mechanism by treating PCET as a concerted process using the constant-charge approach, thereby potentially overlooking one-electron transfer species such as CO2 –, or indirectly infer the electrode potential even when electron transfer (ET) and proton transfer (PT) steps are treated separately. , Recently, we used a constant-potential approach combined with microkinetic modeling to elucidate a bent CO2-mediated mechanism for CO2ER on the Sn(200) surface, which differs significantly from prior constant-charge studies. The simulated CO2ER/hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) polarization curves and Faradaic efficiency on Sn(200) align well with experimental observations. These results demonstrate that the constant-potential approach, when coupled with microkinetic modeling, can provide a robust framework for accurately determining CO2ER mechanisms and kinetics on tin oxide surfaces.

Using constant-potential density functional theory (DFT) calculations and microkinetic simulations, we studied the mechanism of CO2ER and HER on the rutile SnO2(110) surface in neutral media. It has been shown that the SnO2 catalysts can be reduced during CO2ER at high potentials. We thus remove the bridge oxygen atoms on SnO2(110), creating oxygen vacancies (O_v) and generating two distinct Sn sites: Sn5 (coordinated with five oxygen atoms) and Sn4 (coordinated with four oxygen atoms), as illustrated in Figure a,b. Bader charge analysis at U = −1.0 V reveals oxidation states of +4 for Sn5 (+3.04 |e–|) and +2 for Sn4 (+1.93 |e–|), with detailed data provided in Table S1 and Figure S1 of the Supporting Information. We identify the Sn5 site as the active site for CO2ER to HCOOH, where the reaction proceeds through a CO2_n-mediated mechanism. In this pathway, CO2 initially adsorbs onto the Sn5 site in a bidentate configuration, binding to surface Sn atoms through both oxygen atomsa state denoted as CO2_n. This is followed by a PCET step, which uses H+ or H2O as the proton source and directly generates the HCOO intermediate. Subsequently, the HCOO intermediate gains an additional electron and undergoes desorption to yield the final product, HCOO–. This mechanism is noticeably different from the CO2_v (binds through C and O atoms)-mediated mechanism observed at the Sn4 site and on the Sn(200) surface. The simulated polarization curves for CO2ER, and Faradaic efficiencies and current densities on SnO2(110) agree well with experimental data on tin oxide catalysts. This present work offers fundamental insights into CO2ER to HCOOH on tin oxide catalysts. The mechanistic understanding achieved can potentially be used to guide the rational design of enhanced electrocatalysts for CO2ER and other technologically important reactions.

Methods

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed using VASP with PBE. The interaction between valence electrons and ionic cores was described by the projector-augmented wave (PAW) method, and the Kohn–Sham valence states were expanded in a basis of plane wave with kinetic energy of 400 eV. The calculated bulk lattice constants (a = 0.479 nm, c = 0.322 nm) for rutile SnO2 agree well with the experimental values (a = 0.478 nm, c = 0.323 nm) , and previous theoretical studies.

It has been shown that SnO2 is thermodynamically prone to reduction to metallic Sn under strongly reducing potentials (below −1.2 V vs RHE) according to the Pourbaix diagram, which can significantly alter its catalytic behavior. Our calculated formation energy for bridging oxygen vacancies is 2.85 eV, significantly lower than that for in-plane oxygen vacancies (3.44 eV). This suggests that the bridging oxygen atoms are preferentially removed under moderate reducing potentials, leading to the formation of a partially reduced SnO2–x surface. This surface retains oxidized Sn character, which is considered crucial for high selectivity toward HCOOH/HCOO–. As the potential further decreases below −1.2 V, not only bridging oxygen but also the more stable in-plane oxygen can be extensively removed, resulting in the collapse of the SnO2 lattice and eventual reduction to metallic Sna state associated with lower formate selectivity. , Thus, the partially reduced SnO2–x surface represents an optimal active phase, situated between the “fully oxidized” and “fully reduced” states. Recent experimental efforts have focused on stabilizing SnO2 against excessive reduction through doping strategies. , In this work, we focus on the partially reduced SnO2–x surface featuring Sn5 and Sn4 sites (see Figure a), referred to as SnO2(110) unless stated otherwise.

The SnO2(110) surface was modeled with a three O–Sn–O trilayer slab with (2 × 1) periodicity. A vacuum region of 15 Å was used along the z-direction to avoid electrostatic interactions between repeated slabs. The two topmost layers and the adsorbates were fully relaxed, and the bottommost layer was fixed at the bulk positions. The Brillouin zone was sampled with a (6 × 6 × 1) Monkhorst-Pack grid. Transition states were located using the force-reversed method and validated by vibrational frequency analysis. The relaxations stopped when the residual forces on each atom were below 0.03 eV/Å. Activation free energies of the elementary reactions were calculated with respect to the most stable separate adsorption state of the species at the initial states.

The choice between explicit and implicit solvation models involves a trade-off between computational cost and descriptive accuracy. Explicit solvation provides detailed atomic-level descriptions, enabling simulation of water-induced activation and reconstruction of intermediates, as well as the capture of dynamic proton transfer equilibria between surface oxygen sites and interfacial water. However, the high computational cost limits accessible time and length scales and complicates alignment with constant-potential conditions. In contrast, implicit solvation models approximate the interface as a continuum, substantially reducing computational cost while still capturing key activity trends and adsorbate binding energies. This efficiency allows for systematic mechanistic screening across various conditions. In this work, we adopt VASPsol implicit solvation model to balance accuracy and computational feasibility. , The dielectric constant of 78.4 and Debye length of 3 Å were used to mimic aqueous conditions and ionic screening. van der Waals interactions were accounted for using the DFT-D3 method with BJ damping.

We employed a constant-potential DFT framework to model the electrochemical environment, following established approaches. , The total energy of the system is adjusted to account for the electrode potential by controlling the number of electrons, which effectively tunes the Fermi level of the catalyst surface to match the desired potential relative to the standard hydrogen electrode (SHE). This allows calculation of adsorbate energies under applied potentials.

The calculations include zero-point energy and entropic contributions (Tables S2 and S3), accounting for translational, rotational, and vibrational modes in gas-phase species, while only vibrational modes are considered for adsorbed species, in line with established practice. To address the known limitations of the PBE functional in describing carbon–oxygen double bonds, empirical energy corrections were applied to gaseous CO, CO2, and adsorbed OCO-containing species (Table S4), as reported previously. The chemical potentials of ions in solution (H+, OH–, HCOO–) were calculated based on standard thermodynamic relations and pH-dependent corrections. Neutral liquid-phase species such as H2O­(l) and HCOOH­(aq) were treated using the corresponding vapor-phase chemical potentials at equilibrium vapor pressure, as commonly adopted in electrochemical modeling.

Microkinetic modeling, based on the steady-state approximation, is conducted using the YSU_MKM program. This program has been applied to a wide range of reactions, including CO oxidation, HCOOH decomposition, and ammonia synthesis and decomposition. − Mass transport effects in the electrolyte were considered by modeling diffusion of solutes through a boundary layer, with convective transport assumed to dominate beyond this layer. More details of microkinetic modeling including the diffusion coefficients and boundary layer parameters can be found in the Supporting Information. Typical CO2ER conditions (298.15 K, 1 bar CO2 and pH = 7) are employed for microkinetic modeling unless stated otherwise.

Results and Discussion

CO2ER to HCOOH Mechanism

We investigated both the Langmuir–Hinshelwood (L–H) and Eley–Rideal (E–R) mechanisms for CO2ER to HCOOH, with H+ and H2O as possible hydrogen sources. In the E–R mechanism, gaseous CO2 can react directly with adsorbed H atoms, forming HCOO* or COOH* intermediates. In contrast, the L–H mechanism requires CO2 to adsorb on the catalyst surface prior to reaction.

As we will discuss in Section , CO2 exhibits three adsorption states on the SnO2(110) surface, and their stability is influenced by the applied potential. In addition to the physisorbed CO2 and chemisorbed CO2_v species, as previously observed on Sn(200), we identify a distinct CO2_n configuration on SnO2(110) (see Figure c). The CO2_v species binds to the surface via both C and O atoms, with one C–O axis lying parallel to the SnO2(110) surface and the other C–O bond oriented away from the surface. Geometrically, the C atom of CO2_v in contact with the surface is more likely to react with adsorbed H atoms, facilitating the formation of HCOO. In contrast, the O atom of CO2_v oriented away from the surface is more prone to undergoing proton-coupled electron transfer (PCET), leading to the formation of COOH. The CO2_n species, on the other hand, binds to the surface exclusively through O atoms, with the C atom positioned away from the SnO2(110) surface. This configuration readily undergoes PCET at the C atom, promoting the formation of HCOO. These findings highlight the critical role of adsorption structure and applied potential in determining the reaction pathway for CO2ER to HCOOH.

As shown in Figure b, OCO-containing species (CO2, CO2_v, CO2_n, HCOO, and COOH) occupy approximately two surface sites owing to their large molecular size, while adsorbed H atoms occupy only a single site. Note that the small size of H atoms enables them to occupy interstitial spaces between OCO-containing species. To accurately describe this phenomenon, we used a dual-site model in which half of the Sn4 ($) and Sn5 sites (*) are occupied by various adsorbates, while the remaining sites (% and &) are exclusively occupied by H atoms (Figure b). Adsorption of Species at Various Potentials on SnO2(110)

Using DFT calculations combined with a constant-potential approach, we systematically studied the adsorption behavior of various species involved in CO2ER on SnO2(110) at various potentials. Several high-symmetry sites were investigated, and only the results at the stable sites are summarized below. To compare the relative stabilities of the same species at different sites under a given potential, we performed a quadratic fit of the adsorption energies (E ads) as a function of the electrochemical potential (see Figure d–f). Three binding configurations of CO2 are observed at both Sn4 and Sn5 sites on SnO2(110), labeled as CO2, CO2_v, and CO2_n in Figure c and Table S5. We first focus our discussions on the Sn4 site (denoted as $). Figure d shows that linear CO2 ($CO2) exhibits minimal potential dependence, with E ads differing by no more than 0.13 eV across the entire applied potential range. In contrast, the adsorption of $CO2_v and $CO2_n is highly sensitive to applied potential (U). The E ads increases significantly (becomes less negative or more positive) with increasing applied potential. Below −1.5 V, *CO2_v is the most stable configuration, while $CO2_v becomes dominant at more cathodic potentials. $CO2_n is the least stable within the entire potential range.

Irrespective of applied potential, the E ads of linear CO2 (*CO2) at the Sn5 site is comparable to that at the Sn4 site, since it is physisored at both sites. However, *CO2_n binds more strongly to Sn5 site than Sn4 site at all potentials. For CO2_v, the preferred site is potential-dependent: Sn4 is more stable below −1.5 V, whereas Sn5 becomes favored at more cathodic potentials. Similar to the Sn4 site, *CO2 is the most stable at less negative potentials (<−1.4 V), while *CO2_v becomes dominant at more negative potentials at the Sn5 site. It is noteworthy that no stable CO2_v and CO2_n species were found using the constant-charge approach, highlighting the necessity of employing the constant-potential approach for an accurate description of the CO2ER mechanism.

Although nearly all adsorbed species (including HCOO, COOH, CO2_n, and CO2_v) acquire partial negative charge during the adsorption process, as demonstrated by Bader charge analysis (shown in Figure S1), they are treated as neutral adsorbates. This approach is based on both theoretical and practical considerations. From the perspective of strict DFT, the electrons on the surface adsorbates are part of the entire surface-adsorbate system and exist within the frontier molecular orbitals of the interfacial complex. Therefore, these adsorbates cannot be considered as free, independent anions. Additionally, treating all negatively charged adsorbates as neutral ensures the consistency of the overall methodology.

The E ads of *HCOO and *COOH gradually increase with increasing potential at the Sn5 site of the SnO2(110) surface (see Figure e). However, at the Sn4 site, the E ads of $HCOO and $COOH first increase and then slightly decrease with rising potential. Both species preferentially bind to the Sn5 site regardless of the applied potential. Furthermore, HCOO is more stable than COOH on both sites across the entire potential range, suggesting that HCOO formation is thermodynamically more favorable on SnO2(110). Since HCOO serves as an important intermediate in HCOOH production, its inherent stability provides a robust thermodynamic basis for the efficient synthesis of HCOOH. This thermodynamic preference for *HCOO over *COOH on SnO2(110) is consistent with recent DFT studies on Sn-based catalysts. As shown in Figure e, COOH binds to both Sn4 and Sn5 sites via its carbon atom, with the O–H bond oriented toward the surface. However, the adsorption configuration of HCOO varies depending on the binding site: it adopts a monodentate configuration on the Sn4 site, whereas a bidentate configuration is observed on the Sn5 site (as shown in Figure e). These findings are consistent with previous theoretical studies. ,

To understand the site preference of intermediates, we take CO2_n as an example to conduct detailed electronic structure analyses. The electrophilic Fukui function f +(r) in Figure S2 shows a pronounced distribution of f +(r) around the Sn5 site, in contrast to the significantly weaker signal near Sn4. This indicates that the Sn5 site possesses stronger electron-accepting capability than Sn4 site. Further, Figure S1 shows the Bader charges of the stoichiometric and reduced SnO2(110) surfaces, as well as those of adsorbed intermediates on the reduced SnO2(110) surface. At U = −1.0 V, when the CO2_n intermediate adsorbs at the Sn4 site, it gains a net 1.39 e–, while the Sn4 site loses 0.75 e–. In contrast, adsorption at the Sn5 site results in the CO2_n intermediate gaining 0.82 e–, while the Sn5 site itself gains 0.20 e– rather than losing chargeconsistent with work function analysis. The work function of the reduced SnO2(110) surface (3.17 eV) is significantly lower than that of the stoichiometric surface (5.92 eV). Upon CO2_n adsorption at the Sn4 site of the reduced SnO2(110) surface, the work function increases to 4.74 eV, whereas adsorption at the Sn5 site results in only a minor rise to 3.55 eV. This marked difference suggests that CO2_n acquires a greater negative charge at the Sn4 site than at Sn5. Similarly, the differential charge density maps (Figure S3) reveal considerable charge withdrawal from the Sn4 site upon CO2_n adsorption, indicative of electron donation to CO2_n. In contrast, at the Sn5 site, although charge accumulation around CO2_n (yellow region) is observed, pronounced charge accumulation appears along the Sn–O bonds, suggesting that the Sn5 site gains electrons during the adsorption process.

The analyses indicate that the Sn4 site tends to lose electrons, exhibiting softer Lewis acid character, whereas the Sn5 site gains electrons, displaying harder Lewis acid character. Guided by the hard and soft acids and bases (HSAB) principle, the electron-accepting nature of Sn5 classifies it as a harder Lewis acid, enabling stronger stabilization of the CO2_n intermediate, which acts as a hard base through bidentate O-coordination. In addition, the calculated chemical hardness (η) values confirm that HCOO, HCOO–, COOH, and COOH– are all hard bases (see Table S6). Consequently, their stronger interaction with the harder Sn5 acid site is energetically favored. The case of CO2 – further supports this interpretation, as it exhibits lower chemical hardness, especially in its CO2_v configuration where it interacts through the carbon atom, making it a softer base. This explains why CO2_v binds more strongly to the softer Sn4 site in some conditions.

In addition to these Sn sites, we identified a stable hydrogen adsorption state at the O site (see Figure f). Within the studied potential range, the E ads of H at this site falls between −0.51 and −0.05 eV, which is significantly stronger than that at the Sn sites, where the E ads of H is predominantly positive (Figure f). Moreover, on the O and Sn5 site, the hydrogen adsorption gradually weakens with increasing applied potential, in contrast to the adsorption trends observed at the Sn4 site, where the E ads initially increases and then decreases with increasing applied potential. The observed site-dependent potential response highlights a distinct electronic structure effect, which could be exploited for potential-tunable catalytic applications.

Elementary Reactions at Various Potentials on SnO2(110)

We located the transition states (TSs) of various elementary steps in CO2ER and HER on SnO2(110) at constant potentials. The TS structures of various elementary reactions are shown in Tables S7 and S8. The activation free energies (ΔG a) and reaction free energies (ΔG) of the elementary steps at various potentials are listed in Table S9. The free energy diagrams of CO2ER and HER exhibit similar characteristics when H+ or H2O is used as the hydrogen source. Reactions involving H2O are generally more endothermic by 0.83 eV (see Table S9). Thus, we will only summarize the elementary steps involving H+ in the following.

Figure a shows the free energy diagrams for two representative pathways leading to the formation of HCOO– on Sn4 site at U = −1.0 V. Here, HCOO– is designated as the final product rather than HCOOH because under the neutral conditions (pH = 7) considered in this study, formic acid (pK a ≈ 3.75) exists predominantly in its deprotonated form. The first pathway starts with the adsorption of CO2, leading to the formation of $CO2_n intermediate. This step is highly endothermic, with a reaction energy of 1.14 eV. Subsequently, the $CO2_n intermediate undergoes a spontaneous PCET step to yield $HCOO. The adsorbed $HCOO then combines with an electron to form HCOO–, with a negligible energy barrier. In the second pathway, CO2 first adsorbs at the Sn4 site, giving rise to the formation of $CO2_v. In comparison to $CO2_n, the greater stability of $CO2_v renders this step less endothermic, with a reaction energy of 0.31 eV. The $CO2_v binds to the surface through its C atom, and geometrically, it is more prone to react with adsorbed hydrogen ($H) atoms, resulting in the formation of $HCOO. The formation of $H through the Volmer process, that is, the recombination of a proton (H+) with electrons, has an energy barrier of 0.50 eV. Subsequently, $CO2_v combines with the adsorbed $H to produce $HCOO, with an energy barrier of 0.15 eV. It can be seen from Figure a that the second pathway is more favorable owing to the higher stability of $CO2_v compared to $CO2_n. In contrast, as depicted in Figure b, the first pathway is preferable at the Sn5 site. This is mainly attributed to two factors: (1) compared to the Sn4 site, CO2_n is greatly stabilized at the Sn5 site by 0.51 eV, thereby facilitating the first pathway; (2) the Volmer step at the Sn5 site has an energy barrier of 0.97 eV, which is 0.46 eV much higher than that at the Sn4 site, thus inhibiting the second pathway.

Free energy diagrams for the formation of HCOO–, CO, and H2 during electrochemical CO2 reduction reaction (CO2ER) and hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) at the (a) Sn4 and (b) Sn5 sites on SnO2(110) at an applied potential of U = −1.0 V.

The CO formation involves the adsorbed CO2_v* species on both Sn4 and Sn5 sites, which preferentially undergo a PCET process rather than direct addition of adsorbed H (see Table S9), resulting in the formation of COOH species ($COOH or *COOH). The $COOH (or *COOH) species either undergoes a second PCET process or decomposes directly, leading to the formation of CO. The former has a substantially lower activation free energy (ΔG a) than the latter at the Sn4 site, and thus is energetically more favorable. However, at the Sn5 site, the ΔG a of the former is slightly lower than the latter, and both pathways probably contribute to the formation of CO. Regardless of the sites, the PCET processes are quite facile, with activation barriers at most 0.24 eV.

The HER initiates with the Volmer process, yielding adsorbed H species ($H or *H) on both Sn4 and Sn5 sites, with activation barriers of 0.50 and 0.97 eV, respectively. Subsequently, the HER proceeds via the Tafel mechanism at the Sn4 site, where a second adsorbed H species (% H) is generated with an activation barrier of 0.50 eV, followed by recombination of the two H atoms into H2 with an activation barrier of 0.28 eV. Conversely, the Heyrovsky mechanism is more favorable at the Sn5 site, where the adsorbed *H species goes through a PCET process with an activation energy of only 0.07 eV.

Figure S4 presents the free energy diagrams for CO2ER to HCOO– and CO and HER on the Sn4 and Sn5 sites at various applied potentials. It is found that increasing the overpotential causes a reduction in the energies of all the adsorbed species and TSs irrespective of reaction pathways. This trend is in line with previous theoretical studies on Sn(200) and single-nickel atom catalysts. ,

It is worth noting that the HER and CO formation pathways in CO2ER involve multiple PCET steps, making their selectivity more sensitive to pH compared to the HCOOH pathway. PCET steps involving H+ as the proton donor generally exhibit lower energy barriers than those involving H2O. As the applied potential becomes more negative, the dynamic balance between H+ consumption and transport may be disrupted within a certain potential range, leading to a shift in the dominant proton source. This, in turn, can influence both product selectivity and reaction rates.

Microkinetic Modeling

Based on the DFT calculated energetics of various elementary reactions at constant potentials (Table S9), we conducted microkinetic modeling at 298.15 K, 1 bar CO2, and pH = 7 over a potential range of −0.4 to −1.6 V. The resulting reaction rates for Sn5, Sn4, and the Sn4/Sn5 dual-site system are summarized in Tables S10–S12, and the surface coverages and Stern layer concentrations of the main species during the electrochemical reactions are shown in Figures S5 and S6.

Figure a presents the current densities of HCOOH, CO, and H2 as a function of the electrochemical potential at the Sn5 site of SnO2(110). The current density for H2 shows a progressive increase with decreasing potentials. In contrast, the trends for HCOOH and CO are distinct: their current densities sharply rise when the potential varies from −0.4 to −1.2 V. At −1.2 V, the current densities for HCOOH and CO reach their maximum values. Subsequently, as the potential further decreases, the current densities for HCOOH and CO production decline slightly. The decrease in current densities of HCOOH is due to the accumulation of *COOH intermediates (see Figures S5a), which block the active surface sites and thus significantly reduce the availability of free sites for further reaction. Although *COOH is thermodynamically less stable than *HCOO at low potentials (Figures e and ), its steady-state coverage is significantly higher. This apparent paradox stems from kinetic competition: while the formation of *COOH is an electrochemical step that is enhanced at negative potentials, its primary consumption pathway (*COOH → CO­(g) + *OH) is a nonelectrochemical step, which is not promoted by increasing overpotential. As a result, at high overpotentials, the formation rate constant of *COOH far exceeds its consumption rate constant, leading to its accumulation on the surface. In contrast, the consumption of *HCOO is an electrochemical step that is greatly accelerated at high overpotentials, leading to its rapid removal once formed. In addition, the decrease in CO current density arises from the thermodynamic and kinetic suppression of the *COOH decomposition pathway, *COOH → CO­(g) + *OH. As the potential decreases from U = 0 to U = −2.0 V, the reaction free energy ΔG for this step increases from −0.50 eV to −0.28 eV. Kinetically, the energy barrier rises from 0.25 to 0.56 eV (Table S9). Across all potentials, the current densities follow the order: j HCOOH > j CO > j H2.

Similar to the Sn5 site, the Sn4 site exhibits a current density trend for CO that initially increases (U > −1.1 V) and then decreases (U < −1.1 V) as the applied potential decreases. However, in comparison to the Sn5 site, the current density of CO on the Sn4 site decreases more rapidly in the lower applied potential range. The current density trends for HCOOH and H2 on the Sn4 site differ from those observed on the Sn5 site: when the applied potential shifts from −0.4 V to −0.6 V, there is a significant increase in their current densities; as the potential continues to decrease, this increase becomes more gradual. Across the entire applied potential range, the current density of HCOOH on the Sn4 site remains higher than that of H2. For CO, its current density is lower than that of H2 at both lower (−1.6 to −1.3 V) and higher (−0.9 to −0.4 V) applied potentials, but it surpasses or matches that of H2 within the range of −0.9 V to −1.3 V. When the applied potential falls between −1.1 V and −1.0 V, the current density of CO is closest to that of HCOOH. In comparison to the Sn5 sites, both HCOOH and CO exhibit lower current densities on the Sn4 site throughout the entire applied potential range, while the current density of H2 is generally higher. These findings indicate that the Sn5 sites are more active for the formation of HCOOH than the Sn4 sites.

In the system where Sn4 and Sn5 sites coexist, the current density trends of the products are generally similar to those observed at the Sn5 site. However, in the applied potential range of −1.6 to −1.4 V, the current density of HCOOH shows a slight increase as the applied potential decreases. This behavior can be attributed to the synergistic interaction between the Sn4 and Sn5 sites, which facilitates the formation of % H species via the reaction *COOH + % → *CO2_v + % H at potentials more negative than −1.4 V. Specifically, compared to the energy barrier of 0.81 eV (at U = −1.5 V) for % H formation on Sn4, the % H formation via the *COOH + % → % H + *CO2_v reaction at both Sn4 and Sn5 site exhibits a 0.17 eV lower barrier, thereby enhancing both the current density and Faradaic efficiency of HCOO– on SnO2(110). In the dual-site system, the current density of the products consistently follows the order of j HCOOH > j CO > j H2, regardless of the applied potential. These findings suggest that the activity of the dual-site system is predominantly influenced by the Sn5 site. Additionally, a key difference between the dual-site system and the system containing only Sn5 is that the current density of H2 in the dual-site configuration is significantly higher than that in the Sn5-only system within the potential range of −1.6 to −0.5 V, primarily due to contributions from the Sn4 site.

The Faradaic efficiencies (FE) for products as a function of potential at various sites are shown in Figure d–f. As depicted in Figure d, HCOOH (including HCOO–) is the dominant product at potentials below −1.0 V, with an FE exceeding 88.5%. Small amounts of CO (≤11.5%) are also detected in this range. At higher potentials, HCOOH becomes the nearly exclusive product. On the Sn4 site, HCOOH is the sole product across most of the potential range, except between −1.2 V and −0.9 V, where CO is produced at levels of 0.1–3.7%. For the system with coexisting Sn4 and Sn5 sites, the trend in FE for the products generally similar to that of the Sn5 site, except at lower potentials (−1.6 to −1.4 V), where the FE of HCOOH slightly increases as the potential decreases, at the expense of CO. This increase can be attributed to the dual-site system exhibiting a higher HCOOH current density compared to the Sn5 site, primarily due to the significant contribution from the Sn4 site. These results indicate that Sn5 sites serve as the primary active centers in SnO2-based catalysts.

Due to the very low H2 Faradaic efficiency (FE) under the given reaction conditions, we magnified the H2 FE region at various sitesextracted directly from Figure d–fas shown in Figure S7. The H2 FE exhibits distinct trends across different sites. On Sn5 sites, H2 selectivity first decreases, then plateaus, and subsequently increases as the potential decreases; on Sn4 sites, it initially increases before decreasing; while on the Sn4/Sn5 coexisting surface, H2 selectivity rises monotonically. To rationalize these distinct selectivity trends, we compared the potential-dependent adsorption energies of H and CO2 on Sn4 and Sn5 sites (Figure d,f). On both sites, CO2 adsorption becomes increasingly favorable relative to H as the potential decreases. However, this thermodynamic trend alone cannot explain the nonmonotonic H2 selectivity observed in Figure S7, suggesting that kinetic competition between H* generation and consumption, as well as the specific active site structure, play critical roles. These observed H2 selectivity trends align with experimentally observed changes in H2 selectivity under different SnO2 pretreatment or electrochemical reconstruction conditions, , indicating that HER selectivity highly depends on both applied potential and active site structure.

As shown in Figure g, the simulated FE of HCOOH and CO for the Sn4/Sn5 dual-site at −1.2 V are 92% and 8%, respectively. These values align well with the corresponding experimental observations of 81–89% for HCOOH and 8–12% for CO on SnO2 catalysts. ,, Figure h presents the calculated Tafel slopes for HCOOH production on the Sn5 and Sn4 sites. For the Sn5 sites, the Tafel slope in the potential range of −0.4 to −0.8 V is approximately 61.3 mV/dec, which closely matches both the experimental values of 67–74 mV/dec , and the theoretical prediction of 59 mV/dec. , In the more negative potential range of −0.9 to −1.2 V, the Tafel slope increases to about 122.7 mV/dec. This value is consistent with some typical experimental values of 125–146 mV/dec , observed for highly active SnO2 catalysts and approaches the theoretical prediction of 120 mV/dec. The reliability of our microkinetic model is further corroborated by current density comparisons. At U = −1.2 V, the calculated current density for the Sn4/Sn5 coexisting system is 14.34 mA/cm2, which falls within the same order of magnitude as experimental values reported for pristine SnO2-based catalysts under similar conditions (e.g., ∼10.26 mA/cm2 and ∼8.2 mA/cm2). , Taken together, the agreement in both Faradaic efficiencies and current densities demonstrates that our model accurately captures not only the selectivity trends but also the kinetic behavior of SnO2 electrodes.

To investigate the effect of local pH on reaction selectivity, we took the reaction at the Sn4 site at U = −0.9 V as a representative case. By systematically reducing the OH– diffusion rate constant to 0.1, 0.01, 0.001, and 0.0001 times its original value, we simulated scenarios where OH– transport becomes increasingly limited, leading to elevated local pH in the Stern layer near the surface. As shown in Figure i, when the OH– diffusion coefficient decreases from 36.9 to 3.69 × 10–3 s–1 site–1, the OH– concentration in the Stern layer increases from 1.32 × 10–7 to 2.94 × 10–4 mol L–1, corresponding to a pH rise from 7.11 to 10.46. Concurrently, the H2-to-HCOOH production rate ratio decreases from 1.72 × 10–3 to 3.11 × 10–6, clearly demonstrating that elevated local pH effectively suppresses HER and enhances formate selectivity. This trend is consistent with previous experimental findings, confirming the crucial role of local pH in modulating product distribution during CO2ER.

The variation in Tafel slopes for HCOOH production on the Sn sites reveals a potential-dependent shift in the rate-determining step (RDS). In the moderate potential range (−0.4 to −0.8 V), the Tafel slope of approximately 61.3 mV/dec is consistent with a mechanism where the initial one-electron transfer to CO2 is fast and reversible, followed by a chemical RDS, such as the protonation of the CO2 •– intermediate. At more negative potentials (−0.9 to −1.2 V), the increase in the Tafel slope to around 122.7 mV/dec suggests a change in the RDS to an electron transfer process. This could be attributed to the first electron transfer to CO2 becoming rate-limiting under high overpotentials, possibly due to increased intermediate coverage or altered surface state of the active sites. This transition explains the high activity of Sn-based catalysts across a broad potential window and aligns with reported experimental observations for efficient SnO2 catalysts.

Figure a–c depict the ratio of rates for various HCOOH (including HCOO–) formation pathways and the overall HCOOH formation rate as a function of potential at the Sn4, Sn5, and combined Sn4/Sn5 sites on SnO2(110). As illustrated in Figure a, the $CO2_v-mediated pathway predominantly contributes to HCOOH formation at the Sn4 site across all potentials. Additionally, the $CO2_n-mediated pathway and the E–R pathway have minimal contributions within this potential range, accounting for at most 0.02% of the total. In contrast, at the Sn5 site, the *CO2_n-mediated pathway is solely responsible for HCOOH formation. As discussed in Section , this site-dependent mechanism arises primarily from the distinct stability of adsorbed CO2_v and CO2_n species at each site. Notably, adsorbed CO2_v demonstrates significantly greater stability at the Sn4 site compared to CO2_n, while the latter is remarkably stabilized at the Sn5 site. For the Sn4/Sn5 system, the preferred pathway is dependent on the potential: the *CO2_n-mediated route dominates at potentials above −1.4 V, whereas the $CO2_v-mediated pathway becomes more favorable at lower potentials. This behavior can be understood by noting that these two pathways exhibit their highest rates in different potential regimesspecifically, the *CO2_n-mediated route in higher potential ranges and the $CO2_v-mediated route in lower potential ranges (see Table S12).

Regardless of the potential, CO is exclusively formed through the PCET to the $COOH species at the Sn4 site due to its significantly lower ΔG a compared to the decomposition of $COOH (see Table S9 and Figure d). In contrast, the *COOH decomposition pathway predominates at the Sn5 site (see Figure e). This is attributed to the very low proton concentration, which ranges from 10–7 to 10–12, thereby suppressing the PCET to *COOH species. Since the *COOH decomposition step at the Sn5 site occurs more rapidly than the PCET to $COOH species at the Sn4 site across the studied potential range (see Table S12), the former pathway dominates the CO formation rate in the Sn4/Sn5 system.

Compared to the HCOOH and CO formation pathways, the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) pathway is more intricate. The efficiency of the HER pathway is significantly influenced by the potential at the Sn4 site. As illustrated in Figure g, the Tafel mechanism typically prevails at potentials exceeding −1.0 V, while the Heyrovsky mechanism becomes more favorable at lower potentials. According to Table S9, the activation free energy (ΔG a) for the Tafel step is notably lower than that for the Heyrovsky step, differing by 0.69–0.22 eV at potentials above −1.0 V, regardless of whether the conditions are acidic or alkaline. Additionally, the high coverage of adsorbed hydrogen further promotes the Tafel step. As the potential decreases, the ΔG a for the Heyrovsky step experiences a significant reduction from 1.38 eV (U = 0 V) to 0.58 eV (U = −2.0 V). This contrasts sharply with the Tafel step, where the overall change in ΔG a does not exceed 0.05 eV. Furthermore, the coverage of adsorbed hydrogen diminishes as the potential decreases (Figure S5c). Consequently, the Heyrovsky step is greatly enhanced at lower potentials, ultimately becoming the dominant pathway.

It is important to note that the proton (H+) serves as the primary hydrogen source in the Heyrovsky mechanism at lower overpotentials at the Sn4 site, while H2O becomes the dominant hydrogen source at higher overpotentials. This shift occurs because, under low overpotential conditions, protons near the surface are consumed at a slower rate than their influx from the bulk phase to the Stern layer, ensuring an adequate supply of protons as the hydrogen source. In contrast, at higher overpotentials, protons near the surface are consumed more rapidly than they can be replenished from the bulk phase, leading to an alkaline Stern layer and a transition in the hydrogen source to water.

Furthermore, we analyzed the dynamic balance between proton transport and consumption at different potentials. At the Sn4 and Sn4/Sn5 interfaces, when the applied potential is higher than −1.0 V, the H+ diffusion rate is relatively fast, maintaining the H+ concentration in the Stern layer close to that of the bulk solution (∼10–7, see Figures S5b and S6c). Under these conditions, H2 generation proceeds primarily via the Tafel mechanism involving surface H atom recombination (Figure g,i). In contrast, at potential below −1.0 V, the H+ consumption rate increases significantly, and diffusion begins to limit the overall reaction rate, resulting in a marked decrease in Stern layer H+ concentration (Figures S5b and S6c). This depletion of H+ drives the H2 generation mechanism to transition from the Tafel pathway to the Heyrovsky pathway, where H2O reacts with surface-adsorbed H (Figure g,i). A similar mechanistic transition was also observed at the Sn5 site. These results indicate that the dynamic balance between H+ consumption and transport can be disrupted within a specific potential range, leading to fundamental changes in the reaction mechanism.

At the Sn5 site, the Heyrovsky mechanism serves as the exclusive pathway within the relevant potential region. Similarly, H+ functions as the primary hydrogen source at lower overpotentials, while H2O becomes the main hydrogen source at higher overpotentials. In the Sn4/Sn5 system, the preferred reaction pathway corresponds to the Sn5 site at U = −0.4 V, whereas at lower potentials, it aligns with the Sn4 site. As previously discussed, this behavior is linked to the rates of hydrogen evolution pathways at specific applied potentials, with the reaction favoring the pathway that exhibits the higher rate. Notably, at higher applied potentials, the hydrogen evolution rate at the Sn5 site exceeds that at the Sn4 site; however, this trend reverses at lower applied potentials (refer to Table S12).

We also calculated the degree of selectivity control (SRC) for HCOOH as a function of applied potential. A positive SRC value indicates that reducing the ΔG a enhances selectivity toward HCOOH, while a negative SRC value suggests the opposite effect. As illustrated in Figure e, HCOOH is the exclusive product across the potential range studied at the Sn4 site, with the exception of the potential window −1.1 V < U < −1.0 V, where a small amount of CO (3.7%) is also produced. Figure b shows that the SRC values for both the acidic and alkaline Volmer steps, as well as for the $CO2_v + % H → HCOO + % step, are positive within the range of −1.1 V < U < −1.0 V. In contrast, the SRC values for CO formation and H+ diffusion are negative. This observation can be explained by the fact that a decrease in the ΔG a of the former steps favors the formation of HCOOH, whereas a decrease in ΔG a of the latter steps promotes CO formation.

As shown in Figure c, the step *CO2_n + H2O + e– → *HCOO + OH– exclusively controls the total reaction rate on the Sn5 site at potentials U > −0.6 V. As the potentials range from −0.6 V to −1.0 V, the DRC values for this step progressively decrease, while the DRC values for the *CO2 → *CO2_n step increase correspondingly. At U = −0.9 V and −1.0 V, both steps exhibit nearly equal DRC values, indicating that they equally influence the overall reaction rate. The potential-dependent shift in the RDS occurs because the *CO2_n + H2O + e– → *HCOO + OH– step involves electron transfer, which becomes increasingly favorable as the potential decreases. In contrast, the *CO2 → *CO2_n step shows little dependence on potential, resulting in its relative influence on the total reaction rate growing as the potential is lowered. At lower potentials, CO formation and the *CO2 → *CO2_v step also play significant roles in determining the total reaction rate. Both of these steps are associated with CO generation, as indicated in Figure d, where a small amount of CO is produced within this potential range.

We then examine the SRC for HCOOH as a function of applied potential at the Sn5 site. At potentials above −0.9 V, HCOOH is the sole product formed at this site. However, as the potential decreases, a minor amount of CO is generated (ranging from 1.1% to 11.5%). Within this potential range, the steps *CO2 → *CO2_n and *CO2_n + H2O + e– → *HCOO + OH– predominantly govern the formation of HCOOH, while the steps *CO2 → *CO2_v and *CO2_v + H2O + e– → *COOH + OH– significantly suppress HCOOH production. This behavior can be readily understood, as a reduction in the activation free energy of the former two reactions favors HCOOH generation, whereas a decrease in the activation free energy of the latter two reactions promotes CO formation.

For the Sn4/Sn5 site, the DRC trends are similar to those observed at the Sn5 site, except in the lower potential range (−1.4 V to −1.6 V), where the step *COOH + % → *CO2_v + H % has a significant impact on the total reaction rate, as illustrated in Figure e. This is primarily due to the fact that within this potential window, this step serves as a critical pathway for the generation of adsorbed H %, which subsequently reacts with $CO2_v to from $HCOO. The SRC for HCOOH at the dual-site is depicted in Figure f and shows similarities to that at the Sn5 site. Specifically, the reactions *CO2 → *CO2_n and *CO2_n + H2O + e– → *HCOO + OH– govern HCOOH formation, while the steps *CO2 → *CO2_v and *CO2_v + H2O + e– → *COOH + OH– inhibit HCOOH production at lower potentials (−1.0 V to −1.6 V). Additionally, the step *COOH + % → *CO2_v + H % and the step *COOH → CO­(g) + *OH, which are associated with HCOOH and CO formation, exert control and inhibition over HCOOH production, respectively.

Using constant-potential DFT calculations, we have identified a novel oxygen-bound *CO2_n intermediate at the Sn5 site of SnO2(110), which was not detected in constant-charge approaches. The significant stabilization of the *CO2_n species at this site, as explained by hard and soft acids and bases (HSAB) theory, contributes to enhanced activity and selectivity for HCOO– formation on SnO2(110). Furthermore, the synergistic interactions between the Sn5 and Sn4 sites create an additional pathway for generating adsorbed hydrogen species at potentials more negative than −1.2 V, thereby improving the current density and Faradaic efficiency for HCOOH. The simulated Faradaic efficiencies and current densities of the products align well with experimental data from tin oxide catalysts, reinforcing the validity of our theoretical framework. These insights provide a solid foundation for designing more efficient electrocatalysts for CO2 reduction and other technologically relevant reactions.

Conclusions

Constant-potential DFT calculations and microkinetic modeling are employed to unbiasedly assess the intrinsic activity of Sn5 and Sn4 sites on SnO2(110) and their synergetic interactions for CO2ER and HER. The Sn5 site exhibits superior activity for HCOOH formation compared with the Sn4 site across all potentials, indicating it as the active site on SnO2(110). The superior activity of the Sn5 site stems from a CO2_n-mediated mechanism, which is clearly distinct from the mechanisms revealed via the constant-charge method. The significant stabilization of CO2_n species at the Sn5 site can be rationalized by hard and soft acids and bases (HSAB) theory. The synergetic effect between Sn5 and Sn4 sites provides an additional pathway for the formation of adsorbed H species at potentials more negative than −1.2 V, thereby enhancing the current density and Faradaic efficiency of HCOO– on SnO2(110). The simulated Faradaic efficiencies and current densities of products align well with experimental data obtained from tin oxide catalysts. This study underscores the significance of the constant-potential method in elucidating the active site and CO2ER mechanism on SnO2(110), and these insights can enrich the design principles for CO2ER and other technologically relevant reactions.
