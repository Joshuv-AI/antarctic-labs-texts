KFASL: a variance-stable explainable AI framework for high-dimensional anomaly detection
Authors: Hassam Tahir, Mohammad Reza Jabbarpour, Bao Quoc Vo, Ryszard Kowalczyk, James Barr, Travis Bessell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) is increasingly required for anomaly detection in high-dimensional sensor systems operating in safety-critical and resource-constrained environments. While existing post-hoc explanation methods provide useful insights, they often suffer from high computational cost, unstable attributions, and limited applicability in unlabeled or unsupervised settings. This paper proposes KFASL, a variance-stable and computationally efficient XAI framework that approximates Shapley-based feature attributions using a variance-optimized weighting strategy. The framework integrates local and global explanations with causality-aware regularization to improve attribution stability and interpretability under limited labeling conditions. The proposed approach reduces the computational complexity of Shapley approximation from exponential to polynomial time, enabling scalable deployment for high-dimensional telemetry data. KFASL is evaluated using a combination of real and synthetic datasets, with spacecraft telemetry used as a representative safety-critical case study. Experimental results demonstrate improved attribution stability, explanation fidelity, and runtime efficiency compared to existing XAI techniques, including SHAP, Kernel SHAP, LIME, and Anchors. These results indicate that KFASL provides a general and practical solution for explainable anomaly detection in complex sensor-driven systems.

Introduction

The rise of autonomous systems across critical sectors such as aerospace, automotive, manufacturing, and healthcare has increased the demand for reliable, interpretable Artificial Intelligence (AI) solutions1. These sectors rely heavily on telemetry and sensor data to monitor and maintain system health in real-time, where early anomaly detection can prevent costly failures and ensure operational safety. However, modern AI models, while capable of accurate detection, often function as ”black boxes” and lack transparency, hindering trust and acceptance in engineering environments2, especially in space environments where post-launch physical access to spacecraft is extremely limited.

Beyond interpretability concerns, traditional AI-based anomaly detection pipelines also face three fundamental practical challenges: (i) high computational complexity associated with nonlinear high-dimensional feature interaction modelling, (ii) scarcity of labelled fault data due to the rarity of anomalous events in safety-critical systems, and (iii) instability of feature attribution mechanisms under correlated telemetry dynamics. These limitations restrict the operational deployment of purely accuracy-driven AI models in mission-critical diagnostic settings.

Anomaly detection and root cause analysis are essential in these domains for precise diagnostics and timely recovery. For instance, industrial machines, smart vehicles, and medical devices generate high-dimensional, temporally correlated data streams. Classical fault diagnosis methods, such as threshold-based monitoring or rule-based systems, struggle with the complexity, scale, and noise inherent in such telemetry. While machine learning (ML) approaches, particularly deep learning, have shown promise in capturing complex patterns, they are often opaque, which limits their use in high-stakes decision-making3.

Explainable AI (XAI) provides a principled pathway to address these challenges by enabling structured decomposition of model predictions into interpretable feature contributions. From a computational perspective, surrogate-based explanation mechanisms allow approximation of nonlinear model behaviour using locally tractable representations, thereby facilitating controllable fidelity–complexity trade-offs during inference. From a data-efficiency standpoint, attribution-driven reasoning enables anomaly localisation and diagnostic insight even under limited labelled data regimes, supporting semi-supervised and unsupervised fault monitoring strategies. Furthermore, variance-aware attribution modelling improves temporal consistency of explanations, mitigating instability issues that arise in conventional post-hoc explanation techniques when applied to correlated sensor streams.

Anomaly detection and root cause analysis are essential not only for human operators but also for enabling autonomous systems to make informed decisions, enact recovery strategies, and trigger real-time corrective actions4. However, traditional rule-based methods such as fault-tree analysis are not well suited for handling telemetry data’s inherent challenges, including noise, class imbalance, high dimensionality, and temporal dependencies. These limitations highlight the growing need for interpretable, robust solutions that align with the requirements of safety-critical systems5.

Explainable AI (XAI) aims to close this gap by making AI decisions interpretable and actionable. Popular XAI techniques such as SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanation (LIME), Anchors, and Integrated Gradients each offer distinct benefits: SHAP provides consistent global explanations, while LIME excels at generating local, instance-based insights. However, these methods also have notable limitations. SHAP and Kernel SHAP are computationally intensive; LIME and Anchors, though efficient, often provide limited generalization. Furthermore, these techniques primarily rely on correlational analysis, limiting their utility for actionable, causal understanding in fault-critical domains like spacecraft telemetry6–8.

To investigate and validate real-world challenges in anomaly detection and explainability, this paper adopts spacecraft telemetry systems as a representative use case. Spacecraft missions operate under some of the most demanding conditions, characterized by high-dimensional, sparse, and noisy telemetry data, all within highly resource-constrained environments. Faults in critical subsystems, such as the Attitude Determination and Control System (ADCS), can propagate rapidly, underscoring the importance of timely detection and explainable root-cause identification. It is worth noting that insights drawn from the space domain may be generalizable to other sensor-driven, safety-critical engineering domains. However, demonstrating such generalizability requires appropriate, domain-specific validation using each domain’s telemetry and operational data.

Literature review

Traditional AI models often lack interpretability, limiting their adoption in safety-critical engineering systems such as spacecraft operations9. XAI improves transparency and regulatory compliance10,11, yet computational overhead remains a major obstacle for real-time deployment in resource-constrained environments12–14. Deep learning models including CNNs, LSTMs, Transformers, and autoencoders perform well in spatial-temporal anomaly detection but operate as black boxes16–18. This limits root cause analysis and reduces operator trust in high-stakes missions. Techniques such as SHAP, LIME, and Layer-wise Relevance Propagation (LRP) have improved interpretability but are computationally intensive19,20. While interpretable GNNs and temporal-attention LSTMs offer enhanced performance, their complexity hinders real-time utility17,21.

Hybrid architectures such as CNN-LSTM, autoencoders with Mahalanobis distance, and GAN-KNN frameworks have shown promise in anomaly detection22–24, yet suffer from low interpretability and poor scalability in dynamic telemetry streams. Transfer learning and deep generative models address data scarcity25,26, but often require tuning and remain resource-heavy. Semi-supervised and federated learning strategies aim to reduce dependency on labeled data27,28, but trade off accuracy and explainability under constrained conditions.

Several XAI methods attempt to explain anomalies in post hoc fashion. SHAP and LIME are widely used13,29, but are often unsuitable for real-time feedback due to their overhead. Kernel SHAP provides more efficient approximations yet still struggles with high-dimensional inputs30. Although ensemble methods and GNN-based models offer improved accuracy31,32, their lack of transparent decision logic limits deployment in autonomous systems.

XAI integration into autonomous spacecraft systems is increasingly critical. Works such as33–35 emphasize the role of interpretability in operational diagnostics and trust. However, many XAI approaches remain tailored to supervised models and fail to generalize to unsupervised anomaly detection, which is essential in telemetry-driven environments20,36.

Recent surveys and comparative studies19,37 highlight the limitations of current XAI frameworks in terms of scalability, generalizability, and causal reasoning. To ensure trustworthy and autonomous decision-making in real-world applications–particularly in safety-critical domains like spacecraft operations–there is a growing need for XAI frameworks that satisfy three essential requirements: (1) compatibility with unsupervised learning, where labeled data is scarce or unavailable; (2) computational efficiency to enable deployment on resource-constrained onboard hardware; and (3) the ability to provide reliable, causally grounded attributions for effective root cause analysis. These requirements are especially critical in modern spacecraft, where telemetry data is high-dimensional, anomalies must be diagnosed in real time, and operational failures can lead to mission-critical consequences. Unlike existing methods that focus solely on correlation-based attributions or supervised scenarios, KFASL provides a unified approach to local and global explanation under unsupervised settings.

The KFASL algorithm begins by sampling feature subsets weighted by the VOWF [\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$W(S)$$\end{document}], which prioritizes intermediate-sized subsets to minimize variance and enhance computational efficiency. The approximate Shapley values \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{\phi }_i$$\end{document} are then computed by evaluating subset contributions \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$v(S)$$\end{document} across supervised, unsupervised, and causal models, ensuring that feature attributions remain robust across different modeling paradigms.

To further enhance local fidelity, a proximity weighting function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pi _x(z)$$\end{document} is introduced in the loss function, refining surrogate model training without distorting feature importance rankings. The surrogate model \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$g$$\end{document} is subsequently optimized to align with the computed Shapley values, maintaining consistency, interpretability, and causal coherence. This process enables efficient feature attribution, ensuring that insights derived from KFASL remain both computationally feasible and actionable for real-world decision-making. KFASL explains anomalies by distinguishing causal from correlational contributions. The causal regularization term in the KFASL objective incorporates structural relationships between attribution gradients through a causal adjacency matrix C. In simulator-based environments such as Basilisk, this graph is constructed directly from known spacecraft attitude determination and control system (ADCS) dynamics. Directed dependencies are defined according to physical propagation pathways linking actuator torque inputs, rigid-body rotational states, attitude error evolution, and sensor feedback signals. This physics-informed construction yields a structurally interpretable dependency graph consistent with the simulator’s nonlinear state-space equations and message-passing architecture, thereby providing a reliable causal prior for regularization.

In real telemetry settings, including NASA SMAP/MSL and ESA OPS-SAT datasets, explicit subsystem dependency models are not fully available. In these cases, the causal graph is estimated using time-series causal discovery applied to lag-augmented telemetry channels in order to capture delayed propagation of subsystem influence. Sparse structure-learning procedures combining conditional independence testing and score-based optimization under acyclicity constraints are employed to obtain a directed approximation of dominant inter-channel dependencies. The statistically inferred graph is subsequently refined using engineering-informed filtering to remove weak or non-interpretable edges inconsistent with known subsystem operational semantics, resulting in a hybrid statistical–physical causal representation.

To assess robustness to structural uncertainty, controlled perturbation experiments are conducted in which directed edges of the estimated graph are randomly added, removed, or reversed. Performance evaluation shows that KFASL exhibits gradual degradation in detection accuracy and attribution stability as graph corruption increases, indicating that the causal regularization term acts as a soft structural prior rather than a rigid constraint requiring exact causal knowledge. Even under substantial graph perturbations, the framework maintains improved explanation consistency and anomaly detection performance compared to non-causal variants. These observations confirm that the proposed method remains practically deployable in realistic spacecraft telemetry environments where causal topology can only be approximated.

Key anomaly indicators are identified via high reconstruction errors, while the causal constraint term \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda _3$$\end{document} isolates root causes, avoiding misleading correlations. Efficient sampling and causal reasoning make KFASL robust and computationally efficient in uncovering true root causes.

Variance in feature attribution methods, such as Shapley values and KFASL, affects the stability and reliability of explanations. A high variance indicates that feature importance values fluctuate significantly across different model runs, reducing confidence in the interpretations. Conversely, a lower variance ensures that feature importance values remain stable, enhancing interpretability. The variance of an estimated Shapley value \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{\phi }_i$$\end{document} for a feature i is defined as:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \text {Var}(\tilde{\phi }_i) = \mathbb {E}[(\tilde{\phi }_i - \mathbb {E}[\tilde{\phi }_i])^2] \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbb {E}[\tilde{\phi }_i]$$\end{document} represents the expected Shapley value, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{\phi }_i$$\end{document} is the estimated value from a sampled subset of data. A high variance in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{\phi }_i$$\end{document} implies that the importance of feature i is inconsistent across different samples, making the explanation less reliable.

If the variance is high, the importance assigned to a feature fluctuates across different runs, making it difficult to trust the explanation. For example, if in different runs, a feature’s importance values are 0.2, 0.6, and 0.3, the variance is high, leading to different rankings and inconsistent insights. Similarly, if two features contribute equally to the output but their variance levels differ significantly, then the one with higher variance may appear unstable, leading to misleading interpretations. If variance is minimized, the feature attributions remain consistent, leading to more robust and interpretable explanations. The confidence interval of the estimated Shapley value can be derived as:9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \tilde{\phi }_i \pm Z_{\alpha /2}\sqrt{\textrm{Var}(\tilde{\phi }_i)}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Z_{\alpha /2}$$\end{document} is the critical value for a given confidence level (e.g., 1.96 for 95% confidence), and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sqrt{\text {Var}(\tilde{\phi }_i)}$$\end{document} represents the standard deviation. If the variance is small, the confidence interval is narrow, meaning the explanation is more precise and stable. For instance, if a feature importance score is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0.5 \pm 0.2$$\end{document} with high variance versus \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0.5 \pm 0.05$$\end{document} with low variance, the latter provides a more precise interpretation, reducing ambiguity.

KFASL optimizes variance by prioritizing intermediate-sized subsets using the weighting function (refer to equation-2), which ensures that small and large subsets contribute less to the variance, leading to more stable Shapley estimates. Since variance is defined as:10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \text {Var}(\tilde{\phi }_i) = \sum W(S) (v(S \cup {i}) - v(S))^2 \end{aligned}$$\end{document}KFASL downweights high-variance subsets and emphasizes stable subsets, reducing overall variance. This ensures that explanations are more stable, as feature attributions remain consistent; more reliable, as decision-makers can trust feature importance values; and more actionable, as users can derive consistent insights from the model. KFASL achieves this by modifying the weighting function, ensuring that variance is minimized, thus improving interpretability and explanation fidelity.

Furthermore, The claim that Shapley value approximation complexity reduces from exponential \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(2^n)$$\end{document} to polynomial \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(n^3)$$\end{document} is derived from the computational steps in the proposed method. Traditional Shapley computations require evaluating all subsets of a feature set, leading to an intractable \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(2^n)$$\end{document} complexity. The proposed method mitigates this using a Monte Carlo approximation and an optimized subset selection strategy. Instead of evaluating all subsets, KFASL samples a polynomial number of relevant subsets based on the variance-optimized weighting function, significantly reducing computational overhead.

This reduction follows from three key computational steps. First, computing W(S) for each sampled subset requires evaluating binomial coefficients in O(1) . Second, computing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$v(S \cup {i}) - v(S)$$\end{document} for sampled subsets takes O(n) time. Finally, aggregating across all sampled subsets requires O(n) samples.

Thus, the total complexity is:11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} O(n) \times O(n) \times O(n) = O(n^3) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}which is significantly lower than the traditional \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(2^n)$$\end{document} complexity. For \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n = 15$$\end{document}, this translates into a reduction from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$2^{15} = 32,768$$\end{document} operations in the traditional approach to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$15^3 = 3,375$$\end{document} operations in KFASL, making it feasible for potential real-time onboard spacecraft anomaly detection. To summarize, KFASL excels in anomaly detection by isolating root causes with causal regularization and unsupervised adaptations. Moreover, Its focus on variance reduction and optimized computation ensures robust, effective interventions for high-stakes XAI applications.

To further substantiate the sampling-efficiency assumptions underlying the above computational complexity formulation, an empirical convergence evaluation of the variance-optimized Monte Carlo Shapley estimator is presented in the subsequent Results section. This analysis examines how attribution approximation error, estimator variance, and runtime behaviour evolve as the coalition sampling budget increases, thereby providing practical validation of the linear sampling regime assumed in the theoretical complexity discussion.

Results and discussion

The results are structured into four key sections. First, a comprehensive evaluation of major spacecraft telemetry datasets was conducted, leading to the selection of one for further experimentation. Second, a comprehensive comparison of current XAI techniques is conducted by evaluating LIME, SHAP, Kernel SHAP, and Anchors across multiple spacecraft telemetry datasets. These methods are analyzed in terms of their interpretability, computational efficiency, and ability to provide meaningful explanations for anomaly detection in high-dimensional telemetry data. Third, the proposed KFASL framework is introduced and assessed for its capability to enhance explanation fidelity while reducing computational overhead. KFASL integrates a Variance-Optimized Weighting Function (VOWF) to improve feature attribution stability and causal inference in resource-constrained environments. Finally, a comprehensive comparison of the proposed and current XAI algorithms is performed. KFASL is benchmarked against existing XAI techniques, with a primary focus on ADCS faults due to their critical role in spacecraft operations43. The evaluation includes comparisons against ground truth labels to assess accuracy, explanation consistency, and overall performance metrics.

Experimental setup

The experiments in this study utilized a mix of high-performance computing resources, including Swinburne IT and the Centre for Astrophysics & Supercomputing (CAS) OzSTAR supercomputer and cloud-based GPU instances. OzSTAR comprises Dell EMC PowerEdge R740 servers with dual Intel Xeon Gold 6140 18-core processors at 2.3 GHz and NVIDIA Tesla P100 GPUs, connected via Intel Omni-Path Architecture delivering 86.4 terabits per second of aggregate bandwidth with 0.9 microsecond latency. Additionally, cloud-based Tesla K80 GPUs, featuring 4,992 CUDA cores and 24 GB of GDDR5 memory, were employed for supplementary computations. This infrastructure enabled efficient processing of high-dimensional telemetry data, ensuring an effective evaluation of the KFASL framework for anomaly detection.

Dataset investigation

Detecting anomalies in spacecraft systems like the Attitude Determination and Control System (ADCS) is crucial for mission safety. ADCS relies on telemetry from reaction wheels and gyroscopes, making it a key focus for anomaly detection. Initial efforts targeted battery anomalies in LightSail 2 telemetry, using LSTM models to address data imbalance. This approach was later extended to ADCS, leveraging labeled NASA datasets (SMAP, MSL) and unlabeled LightSail 2 data, with artificial fault injection simulating ADCS failures.

Integrating NASA’s labeled anomalies with LightSail 2 data was challenging due to differences in mission architectures, limiting dataset generalizability. Synthetic datasets resolved these issues by enabling precise fault injection for ADCS components like unresponsive reaction wheels and gyroscopes, refining model accuracy without manual labeling.

NASA’s labeled datasets ensured precise fault detection, while LASP’s unlabeled data supported unsupervised learning. Synthetic datasets improved scalability by providing consistent fault simulations. Combining real and synthetic data established a robust foundation for ADCS anomaly detection and spacecraft health monitoring.

Empirical convergence behaviour of variance-optimized Monte Carlo attribution

To empirically validate the sampling efficiency assumptions underlying the proposed complexity formulation, a convergence study of the variance-optimized Monte Carlo Shapley estimator was conducted. Despite KFASL appearing computationally heavier than LIME and Anchors as shown in Figure 23, its efficiency remains justified due to multiple factors. Unlike Kernel SHAP, which randomly samples feature subsets, KFASL optimally selects subsets that minimize variance instability, ensuring a stable and interpretable feature importance ranking. Its polynomial complexity ensures that it remains scalable, making it an ideal alternative to SHAP for large-scale anomaly detection applications. The method’s ability to optimize variance without requiring exhaustive model queries differentiates it from other feature attribution methods, justifying its slightly higher computational cost relative to LIME and Anchors.

It is important to note that the notion of computational efficiency in the proposed KFASL framework is not defined solely in terms of raw execution time, but rather in relation to attribution stability, variance reduction, and structural faithfulness of the generated explanations. As observed in the runtime comparison, KFASL incurs higher computational cost than lightweight surrogate explanation methods such as LIME and Anchors. This additional cost arises from the incorporation of variance-optimized coalition weighting and causal regularization constraints, which increase per-sample computational overhead in order to achieve more stable and causally consistent attribution estimates in high-dimensional nonlinear telemetry environments.

In contrast, surrogate approaches such as LIME achieve lower runtime primarily due to simplified local approximation strategies that do not explicitly model combinatorial coalition interactions or subsystem dependency structures. While such approximations reduce execution latency, they may lead to increased sensitivity to sampling noise and reduced explanation robustness in safety-critical anomaly detection scenarios. Kernel SHAP similarly benefits from approximate sampling schemes but lacks the structural regularization mechanisms required to preserve attribution coherence under correlated sensor dynamics.

Importantly, empirical scaling analysis indicates that KFASL exhibits near-linear runtime growth within the practical linear sampling regime, and remains substantially more efficient than exact Shapley enumeration. Therefore, the computational efficiency claim in this work should be interpreted as efficiency relative to exhaustive combinatorial attribution methods and variance-unstable sampling strategies, rather than absolute runtime superiority over simplified surrogate explainers. From a system-level perspective, the modest runtime overhead represents a deliberate trade-off to ensure higher explanation fidelity, temporal stability, and causal interpretability in safety-critical spacecraft diagnostics.

KFASL achieves a middle ground between computational efficiency and explainability quality, outperforming SHAP in speed (as proved in theorem in Appendix as well) while offering significantly more robust and stable explanations compared to LIME and Anchors. By framing KFASL as a powerful hybrid method, it shifts the focus from pure speed to explanation quality.

It should be emphasized that the suitability of KFASL for resource-constrained or onboard diagnostic integration is argued in this work from an algorithmic and systems-level feasibility perspective rather than from direct deployment validation on flight-qualified hardware. The high-performance computing infrastructure used in the experiments was primarily required for offline model training, hyperparameter tuning, and large-scale attribution convergence studies, all of which represent a substantially more demanding computational regime than operational inference. In practical deployment, the anomaly detection backbone and surrogate approximation modules are pre-trained offline, and only inference-time attribution generation is executed onboard.

Under this operating scenario, the dominant online cost arises from repeated evaluation of marginal feature contributions under a bounded coalition sampling budget. Since the proposed variance-optimized sampling regime allows the number of sampled coalitions per feature to remain approximately linear in feature dimensionality while preserving stable attribution behaviour, the resulting inference complexity remains polynomial and latency-controllable. This is fundamentally different from exact Shapley evaluation, whose combinatorial growth makes onboard use impractical. In addition, the memory footprint of the online explanation module is determined primarily by the stored surrogate parameters and causal adjacency structure, both of which scale polynomially with telemetry dimension and do not require explicit coalition enumeration.

From a systems perspective, spacecraft health-monitoring architectures typically operate under supervisory diagnostic update cycles on the order of seconds to minutes, depending on subsystem criticality and telemetry acquisition frequency. The empirical convergence and runtime scaling results reported in this work indicate that KFASL can operate within bounded attribution budgets compatible with such diagnostic timescales. Therefore, although direct benchmarking on embedded or radiation-tolerant processors has not been performed in the present study, the combination of bounded sampling complexity, controllable fidelity–latency trade-offs, and polynomial memory scaling provides algorithmic evidence supporting the architectural feasibility of KFASL for near-real-time onboard diagnostic reasoning. Explicit embedded implementation and hardware-aware optimisation remain important directions for future work.

Limitations and potential risks

While KFASL demonstrates strong causal fidelity and computational efficiency in telemetry-driven experiments, several limitations remain. First, the current implementation assumes that causal graphs are either known a priori or can be reliably estimated from available data. In missions where telemetry channels are partially missing or anonymized, incorrect causal structures may lead to biased explanations. Second, the weighting strategy relies on a fixed sampling distribution (VOWF); in highly dynamic environments, adaptive re-weighting may be required to track time-varying dependencies. Third, the model’s variance optimization and kernel assumptions are validated primarily on medium-scale constellations; extreme feature dimensionality or edge computing constraints could still challenge real-time deployment. Finally, the framework inherits general XAI risks: explanations might be over-interpreted as causal proof, or users may rely excessively on automated attributions without cross-checking with physical diagnostics. Future work will address these issues through adaptive causal discovery, uncertainty quantification, and human-in-the-loop validation during on-orbit operations.

Conclusion

The proposed approach demonstrates improved root-cause analysis for anomaly detection by addressing key scalability and interpretability limitations observed in existing XAI methods. By integrating a variance-optimized Shapley approximation with causal regularization, the framework enables efficient and more stable fault attribution for high-dimensional telemetry data under the evaluated settings. The framework combines instance-level and global explanations through a Variance-Optimized Weighting Function (VOWF), which reduces attribution variance and improves Jaccard similarity and causal consistency across the studied scenarios. While anomalies involving very large feature subsets may be underrepresented, the proposed weighting strategy prioritizes compact, high-information subsets, mitigating this limitation in practical settings. This study also contributes toward explainability in unsupervised learning scenarios, an area that remains underexplored in current XAI research. Future research will focus on advancing adaptive subset weighting strategies, enhancing data-driven causal discovery mechanisms, and systematically evaluating the proposed framework in broader safety-critical monitoring contexts. In particular, while the present study is experimentally validated using spacecraft telemetry datasets, extending KFASL to other high-dimensional operational environments, including industrial condition monitoring, autonomous diagnostics, distributed sensor networks, and cyber-physical decision-support systems, represents an important and promising direction for future investigation.
