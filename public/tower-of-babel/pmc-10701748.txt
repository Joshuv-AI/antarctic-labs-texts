Segmental extraneural lipomatosis of the superficial peroneal nerve: illustrative case
Authors: Kitty Y Wu, Aditya Raghunathan, Robert J Spinner
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDAdipose lesions of nerve are rare tumors that can cause nerve symptoms from either intrinsic or extrinsic compression.OBSERVATIONSThe authors present a case of a patient with a 10-year history of progressive, persistent leg pain and dorsal foot paresthesias/dysesthesias. Imaging revealed several nondistinct nodules of indeterminate significance along the course of the superficial peroneal nerve (SPN). Surgery demonstrated six distinct extraneural lipomas studded on a 10-cm segment of the main SPN and one of its muscular branches in the midleg. The lesions were adherent to the SPN, without an easy dissection plane; therefore, a neurectomy was performed. Histology revealed the nerve was associated with multiple extraneural lipomas with focal evidence of prior trauma. At 4 months postoperatively, the patient’s pain had resolved completely, and she was able to resume normal physical activities.LESSONSThe current classification of adipose lesions of nerve includes intraneural and extraneural lipomas and lipomatosis of nerve (fibrolipomatous hamartoma). The unique features of the present case include the discrete and segmental nature of the extraneural lipomas adherent to the nerve. Its etiology is unknown, and the histology would be suggestive of either a traumatic or a degenerative process.

Adipose lesions of nerve usually occur within a spectrum, ranging from discrete lipomas (either intraneural or extraneural benign tumor) to lipomatosis of nerve (LN). These lesions, although all benign, have different clinical phenotypes and genotypes. An intraneural lipoma is typically a focal or more diffuse lipomatous mass, most commonly found within the median nerve in the carpal tunnel region.1 Patients present with symptoms of a mass and sensory disturbances associated with the affected nerve. Extraneural lipomas typically present with extrinsic compression of a nerve by an enlarging lipomatous mass. These can occur anywhere but are most often in anatomical areas known to predispose to compressive neuropathy, such as the carpal, radial, and tarsal tunnel.1 LN has diffuse fibrofatty involvement within the epineurium or infiltrated within fascicles.2 It is associated with nerve territory overgrowth in approximately 62% of cases and can result in nerve territory overgrowth affecting bone (macrodactyly, exostoses) or soft tissue (lipomas).3 Extreme cases of LN have been called “macrodystrophia lipomatosa.” LN has recently been established as part of the PIK3CA-related overgrowth spectrum, and this is in contrast to both intra- and extraneural lipomas, which do not have PIK3CA genetic mutations.4 Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is pathognomonic in these conditions. It can differentiate between intra- and extraneural tumors. In LN, there is fusiform nerve enlargement, a spaghetti-like appearance on coronal views, coaxial cables seen on axial views, and complete suppression of any hyperintense fat on both T1- and T2-weighted sequences with fat supression.5 We present a case of a segmental lesion with several distinct extraneural lipomas that were densely adherent to and surrounding the epineurium. This case expands on the current classifications of adipose lesions of nerve with its unique segmental nature and collection of discrete lesions with different morphologies.

Illustrative Case

History and Physical Examination

A 34-year-old woman presented with a 10-year history of right lateral lower leg and dorsal foot pain and paresthesias. There was a slow, insidious onset with worsening of her symptoms in the last several years, resulting in activity limitations. She more recently had noted dense numbness in the area of prior paresthesias. There was no history of any direct trauma or injury to her lower leg. She was otherwise healthy, with no personal or family history of schwannomatosis, neurofibromatosis, or other neurocutaneous syndrome or previous lipoma or lipomatosis. She denied any limb length discrepancy, hemihypertrophy, or associated overgrowth.

On physical examination, her body mass index (BMI) was 47.56 kg/m2. The point of maximal tenderness was over the anterolateral aspect of the distal fourth of the lower leg associated with the course of the superficial peroneal nerve (SPN). There was no palpable mass in this area. There was percussion tenderness localized in the middle to distal third of her leg, along the course of the SPN, radiating into the dorsal foot. She had full motor strength in ankle dorsiflexion, eversion, toe extension, ankle plantarflexion, inversion, and toe flexion. There was decreased sensation in the SPN-innervated territory but normal sensation in the dorsal first web space.

Electromyography was also normal with no evidence of any lower limb mononeuropathy or lumbosacral radiculopathy. Nerve conduction studies showed normal SPN sensory nerve action potentials, with normal amplitude and distal latency. Ultrasound showed multiple ill-defined, heterogeneous, eccentrically located nodules that were intimately associated with the SPN. Two larger, more distinct nodules measured up to 1 cm and were favored to be schwannomas. MRI with contrast showed similar findings with mild T2 hyperintensity and enlargement of the SPN (Fig. 1). This was most pronounced at the hiatus between the anterior and lateral compartments, where the nerve took an atypical archlike course. On imaging, the distal “lesion” was inseparable from the peroneus brevis muscle (Fig. 1B). The MRI findings were atypical in appearance for peripheral nerve sheath tumors and instead favored nerve entrapment or tethering of the SPN to the muscle fascia and/or peroneus brevis muscle. There was slight interval growth of the lesions compared with prior MRI from 6 years earlier. Given these findings, surgical intervention was discussed, including SPN neurolysis and fascial release if this was seen as a point of compression or neurectomy.

Coronal (A) and axial (B, C) T2-weighted MRI showing multiple discrete lesions (white arrows) adherent to the muscle fascia and peroneus brevis muscle.

Surgical Findings

A longitudinal incision over the anterolateral lower leg was used. The SPN was identified distally in the subcutaneous tissues about 6 cm above the lateral malleolus and then traced proximally, deep to the fascia. The nerve appeared attenuated and abnormal over a 10-cm segment. At a point 12 cm above the lateral malleolus, which coincided with the clinical location of maximal tenderness, there was an abnormal yellow fatty-appearing nodule intimately adhered to the SPN. There were two other fatty nodules more proximally located on the surface of the SPN as well as one within an SPN intramuscular branch to the peroneus brevis muscle (Fig. 2A). The two lipomatous nodules were studded on the SPN, and there was no easy plane of dissection between them and the SPN. One of these was circumferentially around the epineurium. Given the abnormal appearance of the nerve, the inseparability of the lipomatous nodules, and the patient’s preexisting dense numbness, a decision was made to perform a neurectomy of the SPN (Fig. 2B). The proximal stump of the SPN was then implanted as proximal as possible into the peroneus longus muscle to avoid painful neuroma.

The SPN appeared attenuated and abnormal over a 10-cm segment (A; white arrows), with the clinical point of maximal tenderness coinciding with the central lesion on the SPN. There was a separate nodule extending from an intramuscular branch within the peroneus brevis muscle (asterisks). The lipomatous nodules were densely adherent to the SPN with no easy plane of dissection; therefore, a neurectomy was performed, in addition to excision of the lesion within the peroneus brevis muscle (B).

Histopathologic Analysis

The resection revealed six discrete lipomatous lesions, ranging from 0.2 to 1.2 cm in greatest diameter, which were not readily separable from the SPN. Histological examination revealed peripheral nerve fascicles surrounded by lobules of mature adipose tissue, consistent with multiple lipomas, present within and outside the epineurium (Fig. 3). The largest lipoma also extended into the adjacent skeletal muscle. Focally, the peripheral nerve showed extensive fibrosis revealed by Masson’s trichrome staining and regions of axonal loss revealed by neurofilament staining (Fig. 4). Adjacent regions showed microfasciculations surrounded by fibrosis, composed of multiple small axonal clusters (“axonal sprouts”) highlighted by neurofilament, associated with individual Schwann cells revealed by S100 (Fig. 4). These findings are indicative of prior traumatic injury. The nerve demonstrated mild edema but no other abnormalities on6–8 routine histology (Fig. 3C).

Postoperative Course

At 4 months postoperatively, the patient had complete resolution of her pain. Her visual analog scale score decreased from 8 to 0. There were no new areas of numbness, paresthesias, or neuropathic pain. She was able to walk for 1 mile continuously, which represented a significant improvement compared with her preoperative activity level. Postoperative MRI of the lower extremities from the pelvis to the knees bilaterally did not reveal any evidence of adipose lesions of nerve or lipomas.

Discussion

Observations

We describe a novel case of multiple segmental epineural-based lipomas along a 10-cm segment in the middle to distal lower leg; five lesions were adherent to the parent SPN, and one arose from an intramuscular branch of the SPN to the peroneus brevis muscle. The lesions were localized just proximal to the site of usual SPN entrapment as it exits the fascia. There was no history of syndromic features, limb overgrowth, previous lipomas, or trauma to the area. The lesions showed an increased size on sequential imaging over several years. Histological examination, however, was suggestive of traumatic changes, which may reflect the consequences of relatively minor trauma affecting an already compromised nerve.

Lessons

Although this case would be classified as extraneural lipomatosis, several features are unique, including the presence of discrete lipomas in a segmental orientation. The multiple extrinsic extraneural lipomas were also adherent to and circumferentially involving the nerve. Rare cases of single extraneural lipomas with circumferential nerve involvement have been reported in the brachial plexus, sciatic nerve, dorsal cutaneous branch of the ulnar nerve, and SPN.6–8 Cases of intraneural lipomas of the SPN have also been reported (Ref 9). In our case, two lipomatous lesions demonstrated circumferential involvement (Fig. 3A and B); however, a separate nearby extraneural lesion was noncircumferential but also densely adherent to the nerve (Fig. 3C). One proposed theory is that circumferential lipomas first arise in the paraneurial space and later expand into the extraneural compartment.6,10 The various lipomatous lesions seen in our case may represent lesions at different stages of this process. The adherent noncircumferential extraneural lipoma may progress to encase the nerve circumferentially with more time. Although simple lipomas may be multiple and/or bilateral and are often associated with syndromic presentations or familial cases, they do not typically compress or adhere to nerves. The presentation of multiple discrete adherent lipomatous lesions, as seen in this case, is highly unusual.

The nature of lipomas and LN remains debated regarding whether these represent malformative or neoplastic lesions. The pathogenesis of the multiple lesions described in this case remains unknown. The microfasciculations seen on histology in our case were suggestive of a traumatic etiology; however, the patient denied any prior significant trauma to the region. Lipoma formation has been associated with macrotrauma,11,12 hypothesized to occur with cytokine and growth factor activation from degradation products of the post-traumatic hematoma that acts as a stimulus on precursor adipose cells.12,13 Lipomatous tissue rearrangement has also been reported with transfascial fat herniation secondary to deep blunt injury. Adipose tissue that prolapses out of its usual border can act as a trigger for lipoma formation.14 In our case, the formation of lipomatous lesions occurred near the site of typical entrapment where the SPN emerges from the fascia in the distal leg; therefore, trauma resulting in fat herniation through this space could have been an inciting factor. However, multiple discrete lesions arising from a traumatic event within a similar zone of injury would also be difficult to explain.

Another hypothesis would involve a degenerative process; however, this would be impossible to substantiate. Elevated BMI, as seen in spinal epidural lipomatosis (SEL), may be a possible risk factor in this patient. SEL represents the excessive deposition of adipose tissue within the spinal canal and is associated with obesity, Cushing syndrome, and exogenous steroid use.15,16 Obesity was found to be a risk factor in 52% of SEL cases in meta-analyses, and there was a positive correlation between the degree of obesity and the severity of SEL.17 This is also highly correlated with metabolic syndrome, where excessive visceral fat can then lead to ectopic fat deposition.18 The occurrence of multiple painful subcutaneous lipomas is a hallmark of adiposis dolorosa (Dercum’s disease), which is also associated with obesity; however, none of these entities have been reported with multiple intra- or extraneural lipomas or LN.
