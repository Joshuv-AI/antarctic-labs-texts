Placental insufficiency and markers of fetal growth are associated with longitudinal infant growth and body composition trajectories up to two years of age
Authors: Sanja Nel, Friedeburg Anna Maria Wenhold, Helen Mulol, Nonhlanhla Yende-Zuma, Ute Dagmar Feucht
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPlacental insufficiency affects fetal nutrition and growth. We describe two-year anthropometric and body composition trajectories in term-born South African infants with/without placental insufficiency, and early-life predictors thereof. Eighty-one infants were screened for placental insufficiency at 28-34 weeks’ gestation using Doppler-derived umbilical artery resistance index (UmA-RI). At eight postnatal time points, z-scores were calculated for weight-for-age (WAZ), length-for-age (LAZ), weight-for-length (WLZ), BMI-for-age (BMIZ), and mid-upper arm circumference-for-age (MUACZ). Fat-free mass (FFM) and fat mass (FM) were assessed using deuterium dilution, and z-scores calculated for FM-for-age (FMZ), FFM-for-age (FFMZ), FM index-for-age (FMIZ), and FFM index-for-age (FFMIZ). Latent class trajectory modelling identified characteristic growth trajectories, and multivariable logistic models investigated predictors of growth trajectories. Three trajectory classes were identified for WAZ, LAZ, WLZ, BMIZ, and FFMZ, and two for MUACZ, FMZ, FMIZ, and FFMIZ. In multivariable analysis, lower birth weight z-score (BWZ) was associated with lower trajectories for WAZ (OR:9.32(95%CI:2.32-37.50)), WLZ (OR:2.95(95%CI:1.33-6.55)), BMIZ (OR:3.70(95%CI:1.56-9.09)), FMZ (OR:2.78(95%CI:1.37-5.55)), and FFMZ (OR:2.63(95%CI:1.45-4.76)). Higher (more abnormal) UmA-RI was associated with lower trajectories for LAZ (OR:1.95(95%CI:1.15-3.30) and FFMZ (OR:1.78(95%CI:1.13-2.80)). Thus, while BWZ predicts WAZ, WLZ, BMIZ, FMZ and FFMZ trajectories, UmA-RI predicts LAZ and FFMZ. Introduction

Nutritional, health, and environmental experiences during the first thousand days of life – from conception to the second birthday – can have lifelong effects on health and development1. Fetal growth may predict postnatal growth and long-term health2, but monitoring fetal growth is challenging in resource-constrained environments with limited access to ultrasonography, and often relies on postnatal assessment of neonatal size and clinical features3. Small-for-gestational age (SGA, birth weight <10th percentile for gestational age) and asymmetric growth (birth head circumference (HC) percentile/z-score markedly exceeding that of birth weight) are among the commonly used anthropometric indicators of fetal growth restriction (FGR)3,4.

Placental insufficiency is the leading cause of FGR, since the placenta is the sole source of fetal nutrition2,5–7. Placental function can be assessed by Doppler ultrasonography of the umbilical artery, a technology that has recently become more accessible following the development of the low-cost, point-of-care Umbiflow™ Doppler device8. Unlike the pulsed-wave Doppler incorporated in conventional ultrasound devices, Umbiflow™ is affordable enough to be widely implemented at primary health care level in low-and-middle-income countries, with the potential to become part of the standard of care for pregnant women. Additionally, Umbiflow™ screening can be performed by nurses or clinical associates after relatively brief training, rather than relying on specialist obstetricians, further increasing its practicality as a screening tool for primary care8,9. The Doppler device measures systolic and diastolic blood flow velocity in the umbilical artery, from which various indices can be calculated, including the resistance index (RI; [peak systolic velocity – end diastolic velocity]/ peak systolic velocity)9,10. Umbiflow™ has been shown to have comparable accuracy to conventional pulsed-wave Doppler ultrasonography10, and can be reliably performed by trained nurses. In a healthy pregnancy, the umbilical artery RI (UmA-RI) decreases gradually throughout the third trimester, with an increased UmA-RI indicating impaired placental function11,12.

The prevalence of umbilical artery Doppler abnormalities appears to be high in low- and middle-income countries. South African studies using Umbiflow™ have found an unexpectedly high prevalence of abnormally elevated UmA-RI (11.7-13.0%) in otherwise healthy pregnant women, with absent end-diastolic flow (indicative of severe placental insufficiency) detected in 1.2-1.5%13,14. The Umbiflow International study found similarly high prevalences of placental insufficiency in Ghana (9.9%) and Rwanda (8.3%)8, while a recent systematic review suggests that these conditions are much less prevalent in high-income countries, where absent end-diastolic flow typically occurs in <0.3% of pregnancies15.

Pregnancies with elevated UmA-RI resulted in higher rates of low birth weight and SGA neonates14, and an overall decrease in birth weight-for-gestational age (GA) percentiles even at normal birth weights13, indicating some degree of FGR. Placenta-mediated nutrient restriction may also affect fetal fat deposition and muscle growth differently, resulting in altered neonatal body composition. In a South African cohort, infants with abnormal UmA-RI were found to have lower length-for-age z-scores (LAZ), higher percentage fat mass (%FM), and lower fat-free mass (FFM) at 6 weeks of age16. Repeated cross-sectional analyses at various time points in the first two years of life found that differences in weight-for-age z-score (WAZ) persisted, while differences in length and body composition parameters diminished over time17. However, such analyses do not account for the longitudinal nature of the data, or the fact that not every infant in the study attended every visit. Declining sample sizes at later ages further contribute to statistical volatility. Longitudinal modelling of growth trajectories aims to overcome some of these limitations.

Latent class trajectory modelling techniques, such as Latent Class Growth Analysis and Growth Mixture Modelling allow for the grouping of individual growth trajectories into a smaller number of representative trajectories18–21. The relationship of growth trajectories to other variables of interest can also be explored19,21. Published research has used latent class trajectory modelling to study linear growth and stunting22, relationships between linear and ponderal growth23, the determinants and outcomes associated with childhood body mass index (BMI) trajectories24, and fetal growth trajectories in relation to various outcomes25,26. No published research has investigated the postnatal effects of placental insufficiency on anthropometric or body composition growth trajectories.

This research aims to describe anthropometric and body composition trajectories during the first two years of life in a cohort of term-born South African infants with and without prenatal placental insufficiency, born to otherwise healthy pregnant women recruited at primary health care centers. Additionally, the characteristics of infants who follow different trajectories were investigated to identify whether early-life factors can predict anthropometric and body composition trajectories.

Methods

Study design and sampling

This study analysed data from the UmbiBaby Study, a longitudinal cohort study successive to the South African arm of the Umbiflow International study (described elsewhere8). The study was conducted in a low-income peri-urban area in Tshwane District (Gauteng Province, South Africa). We recruited pregnant women at primary health care facilities whose pregnancies were classified as low risk according to local antenatal care guidelines27. Women living with HIV were eligible since HIV infection is treated with antiretroviral therapy at primary health care level and they are not considered as high-risk in South Africa. Other pre-existing or pregnancy-related maternal medical conditions (such as diabetes mellitus and hypertensive disorders) were exclusion criteria. Likewise, mothers <18 years, with multiple pregnancies, and infants with severe chromosomal or structural abnormalities were ineligible. Only infants born at term were included in this analysis. The sample size was determined by the available funding and the willingness of participants to commit to the two-year follow-up period.

Data collection and preparation

Prenatal data (including UmA-RI) and birth information (date of birth, sex, GA, birth anthropometry) were obtained from the Umbiflow International Study records. Umbilical artery Doppler examination had been performed at 28-34 weeks’ gestation. The UmA-RI was classified by the Umbiflow software, using South African reference curves from Pattinson et al.28, based on data from a high-risk South African obstetric population. An UmA-RI >75th percentile was considered abnormal, as validation studies found that this predicted perinatal mortality in otherwise healthy pregnancies29. All women with an abnormal Umbiflow™ UmA-RI were referred for conventional ultrasound to confirm the findings; one false positive was detected and subsequently reclassified as having a normal Doppler. Additionally, UmA-RI z-scores (UmA-RIZ) were calculated using INTERGROWTH-21ST UmA Doppler reference values, analysed as a continuous variable30. For birth anthropometry, sex- and GA-specific z-scores and percentiles were calculated using the INTERGROWTH-21ST Newborn Size Standards31. Birth weight was classified as SGA (<10th percentile), appropriate-for-gestational age (AGA, ≥10th but ≤90th percentile) or large-for-gestational age (LGA, >90th percentile). The difference between birth HC z-score (BHCZ) and birth weight z-score (BWZ) was calculated, with a difference of >1 z-score considered indicating asymmetric fetal growth4.

Follow-up study visits took place at a dedicated research unit (Tshwane District, Gauteng Province) at 6, 10, and 14 weeks and 6, 9, 12, 18, and 24 months, alongside routine well-child services and immunizations. Age (in days) was calculated by subtraction of dates. Anthropometric measurements were taken by a trained research nurse. Infants were weighed naked using electronic infant weighing scales (Seca 354; Seca GmbH & Co. KG, Hamburg, Germany), to the nearest 0.01 kg. Recumbent infant length was measured using a rigid infantometer (Seca 416), to the nearest 0.1 cm. Head circumference was measured above the eyebrows and around the widest part of the occiput using a non-elastic measuring tape, to the nearest 0.1 cm. Mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) was measured midway between the acromion of the scapula and the olecranon of the ulna on the left arm, using a non-elastic measuring tape, to 0.1 cm. The WHO Growth Standards32,33 (WHO Anthro software v3.2.2 http://www.who.int/childgrowth/software/en/34) were used to calculate sex-specific z-scores for WAZ, length-for-age (LAZ), weight-for-length (WLZ), body mass index-for-age (BMIZ), HC-for-age (HCZ), and MUAC-for-age (MUACZ, from 3 months of age). Since all included infants were born at term, no GA correction was performed.

Infant body composition was assessed using deuterium dilution35. Infants received an oral dose of deuterium-labelled water (Sercon, 99.8%) (3g for infants <10kg, 6g for infants ≥10kg). Precise dose consumption was calculated by pre- and post-weighing syringes and tissues used to collect spills. Saliva samples (≥2mL) were collected before and 2.5 hours after dosing using a dental cotton swab. Pre- and post-dose saliva deuterium concentrations were measured using Fourier Transform Infrared Spectrophotometry (IR-Prestige-21 FTIR Spectrophotometer; Shimadzu, Japan), and deuterium enrichment was calculated. Deuterium enrichment was used to calculate total body water (TBW) and FFM, using age- and sex-specific hydration constants35,36. Fat mass (FM) was calculated by subtracting FFM from total body weight, and FM index (FMI = FM/[length in m]2) and FFM index (FFMI = FFM/[length in m]2) were calculated. Sex- and age-specific z-scores were calculated for FM, FFM, FMI, and FFMI using reference data from Wells et al37, the only available isotope dilution-based reference data for the age range of our sample. In line with international guidelines, instances where %FM <7.0% were excluded as biologically implausible38.

Data analysis

Analyses were performed using SAS v9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA, https://www.sas.com/en_us/home.html) and R v4.3.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria, https://www.r-project.org/), and hypotheses tested at a 5% level of significance. Latent Class Growth Analysis with fixed intercept and slope and Growth Mixture Modelling with random intercept and slope per class were used to identify 2-4 latent class trajectories19. Residual plots were used to assess adequate model fit. The framework described by Lennon et al18 was used to identify the best-fitting model with an adequate number of classes for each variable. Models were discarded if the Average of maximum Posterior Probabilities of Assignment (APPA) was <0.7, an Odds of Correct Classification (OCC) was <5.0, or if any one trajectory class included <10% of the total sample, whereafter, the lowest Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) indicated the best model fit. Each trajectory was labelled as “upper,” “middle” or “lower,” based on their position relative to other trajectories in the same model. The trajectory values as predicted by the models were plotted as z-score (y-axis) against infant age (x-axis). After matching participants to trajectory classes, the crude loess-smoothed z-scores of the actual data for each trajectory were plotted.

For each model, the infants displaying different growth trajectories were compared with regard to maternal and neonatal characteristics, placental function, and infant feeding practices using independent t-test or ANOVA (normally distributed data), Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney or Kruskal-Wallis test (non-normally distributed data), and Chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test (categorical data). In most cases, the best-fit models were obtained using Latent Class Growth Analysis with three classes (WAZ, LAZ, WLZ, BMIZ, HCZ, and FFMZ) or two classes (FMZ, FMIZ, and FFMIZ), with one index (MUACZ) resolving best with Growth Mixture Modelling (two classes) incorporating a random intercept and slope per class. Associations between maternal/ infant characteristics and trajectory membership were investigated using multivariable binary (MUACZ, FMZ, FMIZ, FFMIZ), multinomial (WAZ, WLZ, BMIZ), and ordinal (LAZ, HCZ, FFMZ) logistic regression models. The variables UmA-RI, BWZ and the difference between BHCZ and BWZ were included in the analyses as continuous variables to preserve statistical power. Since these variables are often dichotomized in clinical practice (normal vs. abnormal UmA-RI, SGA vs. not SGA, and asymmetric vs. symmetric intrauterine growth, as defined above), the analyses were repeated using these categorical variables to investigate the practical utility of these clinical categories in predicting growth trajectories. Variables where no significant difference was found between the different trajectory groups, and variables with high levels of missing data (e.g. some of the infant feeding variables), were not included in the multiple regression models.

Anthropometry

All three WAZ trajectories showed an initial increase followed by a gradual decline. Infants with different WAZ trajectories differed significantly with respect to UmA-RIZ and UmA-RI category (higher UmA-RIZ and more abnormal UmA-RI in the lower trajectory), birth GA (higher in the upper trajectory), BWZ and BHCZ (both increasing with upper trajectories) (Table 2). From multinomial logistic regression, using the middle trajectory class as reference, lower BWZ and GA increased the odds of belonging to the lower WAZ trajectory class and decreased the odds of belonging to the upper WAZ trajectory class. Higher maternal gravidity was also associated with the upper WAZ trajectory (Table 4).

The LAZ model described three near-parallel trajectories, with the actual data trajectories showing some non-linearity in the first 6-9 months. After one year of age, the upper trajectory declined gradually, while the middle and lower trajectories remained more constant. Infants in the lower trajectory class had significantly higher UmA-RIZ, larger proportion of abnormal UmA-RI, more maternal HIV, lower GA, lower BWZ and birth length z-score (BLZ), and higher rates of SGA (Table 2). In multivariable analysis from ordinal logistic regression, higher UmA-RIZ, and lower BLZ were associated with increased odds of being in the lower trajectory (Table 4).

The WLZ model described three trajectories that all declined over time, with the steepest initial decline in the lower trajectory. Significant differences were seen in BWZ (lowest in the lower trajectory, highest in the middle trajectory) (Table 2). In multivariable analysis (multinomial logistic regression) with the middle trajectory as the reference, lower BWZ was associated with increased odds of having lower and also upper WLZ trajectories (Table 4), with the former reaching statistical significance.

The BMIZ model described three trajectories that differed significantly with respect to GA at birth (lowest in the middle trajectory), BWZ and BHCZ (lowest in the lower trajectory), and birth weight class (highest proportion of SGA in the lower trajectory, but lowest proportion in the middle trajectory) (Table 2). Multivariable analysis using multinomial logistic regression found that, compared to the middle trajectory, a lower BWZ was associated with higher odds of having lower BMIZ trajectory, while a lower GA decreased the odds of belonging to the upper BMIZ trajectory class (Table 4).

The three HCZ trajectories increased only slightly over 2 years. Significant differences between the HCZ trajectory classes were seen for infant sex (greater proportion of males in the middle trajectory), BWZ and BHCZ (increasing with higher trajectories), and birth weight class (higher SGA rate in the lower trajectory) (Table 2). In multivariable analysis (ordinal logistic regression), lower BWZ was associated with increased odds of belonging to the lower HCZ trajectory, while a larger difference between BHCZ and BWZ, and greater maternal height both decreased the odds of belonging to the lower HCZ trajectory (Table 4).

The two MUACZ trajectories showed an initial increase followed by a decline back to 3-months values over time. Infants in the lower MUAC trajectory had significantly lower GA, BWZ, and BHCZ (Table 2). In multivariable binomial logistic regression analysis, lower BWZ was associated with reduced odds of having upper MUACZ trajectory (Table 4).

Body composition

The two FMZ trajectories converged by 2 years, following an initial increase and decrease. Infants in the lower FMZ trajectory had significantly lower GA, BWZ, and BHCZ, and higher rates of SGA and maternal HIV (Table 3). In multivariable binomial logistic regression analysis, maternal HIV and lower BWZ were associated with higher odds of having the lower FMZ trajectory (Table 5).

The FMIZ trajectories echoed the FMZ trajectories. Birth GA was significantly higher in the upper trajectory class (Table 3), but multivariable analysis using binomial logistic regression did not identify any statistically significant predictors.

The FFMZ model described three declining trajectories, losing approximately 1.2 FFMZ units by 24 months. This decline started earlier in the lower trajectory (~4 months) than in the middle trajectory (~10 months) or upper trajectory (~12 months). Infants in the lower trajectory had significantly higher UmA-RIZ and more abnormal UmA-RI, lower GA, lower BWZ, and a higher rate of SGA (Table 3). In multivariable analysis (ordinal logistic regression), higher UmA-RIZ and lower BWZ were associated increased odds of having the lower FFMZ trajectory (Table5).

The two FFMIZ trajectories similarly lost ~2 z-score units over the first two years. The upper FFMIZ trajectory had significantly higher BWZ, maternal gravidity, and maternal parity (Table 3), but multivariable binomial logistic regression analysis did not identify statistically significant predictors of FFMIZ trajectory classes (Table 5).

Predictive ability of continuous vs. categorical variables

The results of multivariable analyses using dichotomized clinical categories for BWZ (SGA vs. not SGA), UmA-RI (abnormal vs. normal), and BWZ-BHCZ (asymmetric vs. symmetric) are included in Supplementary Table 3. Dichotomization of continuous variables resulted in wider 95% confidence intervals, leading to a loss of efficiency in some cases. SGA was significantly associated with increased odds of being in lower trajectories for WAZ (OR 9.09 (1.75-50.0)), HCZ (OR 12.98 (3.81-44.21)), FMZ (OR 6.86 (1.77-26.69)), and FFMZ (OR 8.54 (2.37-30.78)); however, unlike BWZ, it was not significantly associated with trajectories of WLZ, BMIZ or MUACZ. Like UmA-RIZ, abnormal UmA-RI was associated with increased odds of being in lower trajectories for LAZ (OR 4.85 (1.39-16.95)) and FFMZ (OR 6.45 (2.05-20.24)). Asymmetry (i.e. a difference between the BHCZ and BWZ of >1) was not associated with any trajectory, while the continuous variable (difference between BHCZ and BWZ) was associated with the upper HCZ trajectory.

Discussion

This study shows anthropometric growth and body composition trajectories (patterns) within a sample of healthy, full-term infants over the first two years of life. Actual data trajectories for WAZ, LAZ, MUACZ, and HCZ showed little net change (<0.5 z-scores) over the course of two years, with a tendency to more rapid growth in the first 6-12 months, followed by slower growth thereafter. The FMZ and FMIZ trajectories converged over time. Trajectories for WLZ and BMIZ decreased slightly over two years, while FFMZ and FFMIZ decreased markedly. The period of the most rapid FFMZ and FFMIZ decline is echoed by declining MUACZ and WAZ trajectories in the second year of life. The small net two-year decreases in anthropometric trajectories do not reflect the magnitude of FFM depletion suggested by the FFMZ and FFMIZ trajectories. This supports previous research reporting that anthropometric indices such as BMI and WLZ inadequately reflect body composition in infancy and childhood39.

Among the potential indicators of FGR we investigated (summarized in Fig. 2), BWZ (and, in some cases, SGA and BLZ) and prenatal placental function (UmA-RI) predicted certain anthropometric and body composition trajectories, while asymmetry at birth was not a useful predictor. Overall, the study sample had a lower mean BWZ (-0.45 ± 1.02 vs. the expected 0.00 ± 1.00) and a higher rate of SGA (22.2% vs. 10% of the population that should mathematically fall below the 10th percentile cut-off) than would be expected in a healthy population. While many social, environmental, nutritional, and health-related factors contribute to birth size, the potential contribution of placental insufficiency should not be ignored13. The large mean difference between birth BHCZ and BWZ (0.98 ± 1.25), and high rate of asymmetry (>50%) in the study sample, alongside its inability to predict growth trajectories, suggest that this is not an appropriate indicator of FGR in the study population.

Summary of factors associated with anthropometric and body composition trajectories.

BWZ was strongly associated with weight-related anthropometric trajectories (WAZ, WLZ, BMIZ), MUACZ, FFMZ, and FMZ trajectories. Other research has reported birth weight indices to be associated with both FM and FFM40,41. FFMZ and LAZ trajectories were associated with UmA-RI in multivariate analyses. Numerous studies have reported associations between LAZ and FFM42,40,43,44, even into adulthood44,45. Our results suggest that impaired placental function may be a common factor predisposing infants to restricted length growth and FFM accretion.

Our study is not the first to suggest that intrauterine growth and nutrition affect growth and body composition in childhood and infancy. In low- and middle-income countries, especially, SGA or low birth weight often results in persistent growth deficits and high levels of stunting, wasting, and underweight46–49. Conflicting trends have been described for body composition: one systematic review concluded that FGR and SGA were both associated with lower infant FM and FFM50, while other research associated SGA with excess accumulation of FM, particularly intra-abdominal fat51. Part of the explanation for these differences may lie in the inability of the 10th percentile cut-off for SGA to distinguish true FGR from constitutional smallness. Doppler screening may be useful for distinguishing between these two groups, as demonstrated by a recent Austrian cohort study52: in preterm infants assessed at term-equivalent age, SGA infants with abnormal Doppler findings (i.e. true FGR) had significantly lower FFM and FFMZ than AGA infants, while SGA infants with normal Doppler findings (i.e. constitutionally small infants) did not differ significantly from AGA infants52. This indicates that placental insufficiency, rather than simply SGA, places infants at risk of FFM depletion – a finding supported by our results. On a physiological level, birth weight, placental function and the length of gestation (i.e. GA) are all interdependent. This raises the possibility of collinearity and possible confounding among these variables. Unfortunately, the small sample size precludes more sophisticated analyses of possible interactions between the different exposure variables; these questions should be explored in future research.

Postnatally, nutrition and growth in infancy also affect body composition outcomes. Rapid early weight gain, especially in SGA infants, is associated with higher FM53, whereas length growth is more strongly associated with FFM accretion44. Therefore, nutrition interventions and growth targets need to focus on linear growth and not simply on weight gain.

One of this study’s main strengths is the inclusion of much of the first thousand days of life. Multiple measurements over time allowed for longitudinal growth trajectory analysis, increasing the transferability of the results to individual level. Supporting optimal growth and body composition in children requires a life-course approach54,55, with the first thousand days being especially important56,57. This research adds to the evidence that early-life exposures have long-term effects, stressing the need for interventions targeting pre- and peri-conception maternal nutrition58, supporting fetal growth59, and optimizing infant feeding from birth through complementary feeding and beyond60.

It should be noted that the study sample had higher rate of SGA births (22.2%) than would be expected in a healthy population (~10%). This is not atypical for South Africa13,14,61 and many other low- and middle-income countries61, but it may limit the applicability of the findings to higher income settings where SGA birth rates are lower.

Our study is limited by the sample size, which likely introduced some statistical volatility, and the exclusion of preterm infants from the sample. Additionally, sample size limitations did not allow for statistical investigation of the potential mediatory role of birthweight in the pathway between placental insufficiency and infant growth/body composition. The use of routine birth anthropometry measurements presents a quality control challenge, especially for birth length measurement62. Nonetheless, the results suggest important avenues to be explored in larger studies. The associations between abnormal UmA-RI, LAZ, and FFM deserve further examination, particularly considering South Africa’s high prevalence of both stunting (~27% of children under five years63) and placental insufficiency (11-13% of healthy pregnancies13,14). Moreover, previous research has described associations between FFM and neurodevelopmental outcomes, particularly (but not exclusively) in preterm infants39. As early body composition often tracks into adulthood36,38, future research should investigate the possible effects of widespread FFM depletion on educational capacity and long-term earning potential, an association that has been well described in relation to poor linear growth64,65. Routine prenatal Doppler screening could help identify infants at risk of stunting. Finally, the long-term effects of placental insufficiency in preterm infants deserve further investigation, particularly since preterm birth in itself is a risk factor for poor growth outcomes. The emergence of GA as a significant predictor of outcomes even in term infants supports this.

Conclusion

Prenatal placental insufficiency, identified using UmA-RI Doppler screening, was associated with lower FFMZ and LAZ trajectories in term infants, while lower BWZ was associated with lower WAZ, WLZ, BMIZ, HCZ, MUACZ, FMZ, and FFMZ trajectories. Optimizing infant and child growth l requires attention to maternal nutrition and healthy pregnancies to optimize placental function and fetal growth.
