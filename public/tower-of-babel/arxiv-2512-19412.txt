Sculpting ultrafast mid-infrared light for solid-state high harmonic generation
Camilo Granados, Bálint Kiss, Eric Cormier, Bikash Kumar Das, Debobrata Rajak, Carmelo Rosales-Guzman, Rajaram Shrestha, Qiwen Zhan, Wenlong Gao

Abstract
The ability to sculpt light in space, time, and polarization has revolutionized studies of light–matter interaction and enabled breakthroughs in optical communication, imaging, and ultrafast science. Among the many degrees of freedom of light, orbital angular momentum (OAM) further expands these capabilities by unlocking new regimes of control in information encoding, particle manipulation, and symmetry-driven selection rules. However, exploiting OAM to drive nonlinear, non-perturbative effects in solids remains challenging, especially in the mid-infrared (MIR) spectral regime, a key region for accessing these effects in ambient air, where spatial light modulators do not operate. Here, we circumvent this limitation by generating femtosecond, few-cycle MIR Bessel-Gauss vortex (BGV) and Perfect optical vortices (POVs), using a robust, static spatial-shaping strategy. Using those beams to drive the high-harmonic generation (HHG) process in solids, we show that the resulting harmonic beams faithfully inherit the structural properties of the drivers: the constant-intensity ring of the POV is preserved across harmonic orders, while the harmonic BGVs retain their intrinsic TC-dependent profiles. Furthermore, by verifying the OAM up-scaling law, we confirm OAM conservation during HHG in solids. These results establish strong-field HHG in solids as a robust platform for synthesizing ultrafast structured harmonic light with controllable, high-value OAM.

The generation of MIR structured light based on static elements is then pivotal for advancing our knowledge on non-perturbative effects in matter. As such, the ultrashort structured light reported in this manuscript provides a versatile platform to investigate light-matter interactions in solid-state systems which intrinsically exhibit diverse symmetries, topologies, and spin–orbit couplings. This opens the door to address fundamental questions concerning OAM conservation in solid-state HHG. Additionally, it enables the development of a coherent, compact, and short-wavelength source of POV and BGV beams in ambient air, bypassing the large vacuum systems required in gaseous HHG experiments. Discussion

Our experimental results demonstrating the generation of ultrashort MIR structured light and its parametric conversion into the IR to visible spectral range, represents a major advance in our quest for controlling light’s degrees of freedom and advancing solid-state materials functionality. In IR to visible spectral range, creating fs BGV and POV beams would required specialized laser systems to generate fs Gaussian beams and spatial light modulators to imprint the TC. The latter are limited in efficiency, operation wavelength and bandwidth, all crucial for applications in nonlinear, non-perturbative physics in solids. We circumvents these limitations, offering a compact and flexible route to produce harmonic vortices with high topological charges (
l_{q}=35
for
q=7
) in the IR to visible spectral range. Furthermore, by driving the HHG process with fs MIR vortex beams it is possible to investigate non-perturbative effects in ambient air, highlighting its difference and advantage over the gas-phase studies which require large vacuum conditions.

Producing short wavelengths with large TC values via HHG is fundamental to further expand the study of the helical phase effects in different physical systems. For example, understanding the absorption of molecular switches and motors dissolved in liquids is pivotal to increase the efficiency of energy conversion. Vortex beams provide a potential route in this direction via the helical dichroism and OAM-dependent molecular transitions. Most importantly, studying those systems via transient absorption spectroscopy, for example, allows for understanding the environment’s effect on the molecular response to the helical light. In addition to this, the opportunity to generate and incorporate topological quantum light (quantum light carrying OAM) into ultrafast studies in solid systems could potentially open new opportunities to manipulate the harmonic spectrum basic characteristics.

Beyond demonstrating control over the spatial structure of harmonics, our results confirms the conservation of OAM in solid-state HHG for the POV and BGV beams. The harmonic vortices TC scales linearly with both the fundamental OAM and the harmonic order, closely following the up-scaling law previously observed in gases and also recently demonstrated in several solid-state materials
[
22
]
. This provides a robust experimental benchmark for theoretical models addressing microscopic and macroscopic electron dynamics in solids under helical strong-field excitation.

Generating harmonic vortices in semiconductor materials also expands the accessible spectral range of ultrashort structured light. Previously, harmonic vortex generation was largely restricted to the ultraviolet and extreme-ultraviolet in gases, where vacuum systems and low-efficiency XUV optics limit applications. Producing harmonic POV and BGV beams in the infrared-to-visible range opens opportunities for ultrafast spectroscopy. It also enables studies of the fundamental optics of ultrashort structured light: we demonstrate the conjugate nature of POV and BGV beams during propagation (see Supplementary Material), opening the door to investigate propagation of ultrashort structured light in different media and greatly expanding their applicability in both fundamental and technological contexts. Additionally, with the advent of more complex topological structures, such as optical skyrmions and Hopfions, solid-state HHG provides an ideal platform to explore these exotic light fields and to produced them in the ultrashort regime.

To summarize, we demonstrated the generation of fs MIR BGV and POV beams. Our results confirmed that, in the fs regime, POVs maintain a beam size independent of the TC, whereas, BGV beams exhibit a beam size dependence on TC values. Furthermore, these characteristics were shown to be preserved in the corresponding harmonic vortex beams, enabling the generation of POV and BGV beams in the shorter wavelength regimes with large TC values. Most importantly, we demonstrate here the possibility to study nonlinear effects in solid-state materials with the use of structure light. Our work establishes a foundation for investigating the high-harmonic generation process driven by POV and BGV beams and the generation of attosecond twisted pulses in solid-state systems.

Methods

Our experimental generation of harmonic POV beams relies on the optical Fourier transform. This relationship allows an arbitrary BGV beam to be mapped into a POV field, as defined by the general expression
[
30
]
:

E(r,\theta)=\frac{k}{2\pi if}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{2\pi}E(\rho,\phi)e^{-\frac{ik}{f}\rho rcos(\theta-\phi)}d\rho\rho d\phi.

(4)

here, the BGV field,
E(\rho,\phi)
, is given by Eq.
3
, with the helical phase accounting for the harmonic order,
\exp(iql\phi)
. Additionally,
f
is the focal length of the lens,
k=2\pi/\lambda
and
\lambda
the laser wavelength.
The solution of Eq.
4
, results in the POV field given in Eq.
1
, with the helical phase given by
\exp(iql\theta)
. This result demonstrates the conjugate relation between both beams and that the TC content of the POV beam can be extracted from the BGV beam, since the TC remains unchanged by the Fourier Transform.

Furthermore, the TC measurements of the fundamental and harmonic BGV beam are based on the principle of the
\pi/2
mode conversion. In this case, illuminating a CL with a BGV beam will produce tilted HG beams surrounded by multiple rings in the detection plane. Mathematically, this can be understood as follows: Under the small argument condition (
k_{r}r\ll
) the Bessel function of the first kind of order
\mathcal{L}
, describing the BGV beam, can be approximated as:

J_{\mathcal{L}}(k_{r}r)\approx\frac{(k_{r}r)^{\mathcal{L}}}{2^{\mathcal{L}}\mathcal{L}!},

(5)

here,
\mathcal{L}
represent the TC of the fundamental and harmonic field,
\mathcal{L}=l_{0}
for the fundamental field and
\mathcal{L}=ql_{0}
for the harmonic field. Using Eq.
5
we simplified Eq.
3
, which now takes the form:

E(r,\phi,z)\approx E_{0}\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{r^{2}}{w^{2}_{0}}\right)\frac{(k_{r}r)^{\mathcal{L}}}{2^{\mathcal{L}}\mathcal{L}!}\exp(i\mathcal{L}\theta)=A_{1}\exp\!\left(-\frac{(x^{2}+y^{2})}{w^{2}_{0}}\right)(x+iy)^{\mathcal{L}},

(6)

where
A_{1}
condenses all the constant values. Following the calculation presented in
[
29
]
, we derive the complex field amplitude of the beam at a distance
z
as:

E(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z)\propto H_{\mathcal{L}}(\alpha_{1}x^{\prime}+i\alpha_{2}y^{\prime}),

(7)

where
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
are constants related to the beam waist at a particular distance
z
. Equation
7
demonstrates that the number of intensity zeros in HG function, corresponding to the order of the HG function, is exactly equal to the TC carried by the transformed beam, in this case the BGV beam. We note that, to obtain the same HG pattern as shown in Fig.
