Incorporating External Controls for Estimating the Average Treatment Effect on the Treated with High-Dimensional Data: Retaining Double Robustness and Ensuring Double Safety
Chi-Shian Dai, Chao Ying, Yang Ning, Jiwei Zhao

Abstract
Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are widely regarded as the gold standard for causal inference in biomedical research. For instance, when estimating the average treatment effect on the treated (ATT), a doubly robust estimation procedure can be applied, requiring either the propensity score model or the control outcome model to be correctly specified. In this paper, we address scenarios where external control data, often with a much larger sample size, are available. Such data are typically easier to obtain from historical records or third-party sources. However, we find that incorporating external controls into the standard doubly robust estimator for ATT may paradoxically result in reduced efficiency compared to using the estimator without external controls. This counterintuitive outcome suggests that the naive incorporation of external controls could be detrimental to estimation efficiency. To resolve this issue, we propose a novel doubly robust estimator that guarantees higher efficiency than the standard approach without external controls, even under model misspecification. When all models are correctly specified, this estimator aligns with the standard doubly robust estimator that incorporates external controls and achieves semiparametric efficiency. The asymptotic theory developed in this work applies to high-dimensional confounder settings, which are increasingly common with the growing prevalence of electronic health record data. We demonstrate the effectiveness of our methodology through extensive simulation studies and a real-world data application.



Key Words:
External controls, average treatment effect on the treated, high-dimensional data, double robustness, double safety, efficient influence function.

1
Introduction

Understanding the treatment effect is a fundamental and widely studied question in scientific research, whether in randomized controlled trials (RCTs) or observational studies.
Central to this inquiry are causal estimands
(
Morgan & Winship 2014
;
Imbens & Rubin 2015
;
Hernan & Robins 2022
)
, such as the Average Treatment Effect (ATE) and the Average Treatment Effect on the Treated (ATT), which provide insights into the overall and subgroup-specific impacts of interventions, respectively.
ATE quantifies the average treatment effect across the entire study population, whereas ATT focuses on the average effect among those who actually received the treatment.
As argued by
Heckman & Smith 1995
and
Heckman & Vytlacil 2001
, ATT is often the implicit target in many economics and evaluation studies, making it more relevant and interpretable for policymakers.
For example, when evaluating a government-implemented smoking cessation campaign, researchers and policymakers are likely more interested in the intervention’s effect on those who actually participated (i.e., ATT) rather than on individuals who did not engage with the campaign
(
Wang et al. 2017
)
.
A similar focus arises in medical research, where the primary interest often lies in assessing the treatment effect on actual participants.
Consider, for instance, a study investigating the causality between antidepressant use during pregnancy and the risk of autism spectrum disorder in children
(
Boukhris et al. 2016
)
.
In such cases, the pertinent effect of interest is the impact of antidepressant use during pregnancy among women who have actually taken these medications. This subgroup likely includes individuals with symptoms of depression, anxiety, or other psychiatric disorders for which antidepressants are commonly prescribed.
The goal is to understand the average effect of administering antidepressants versus withholding them among women prescribed these medications, rather than the average effect across all women.

With advancements in modern technology and innovative data collection methods, researchers often have access to additional data sources, beyond the primary study which could be either a RCT or an observational study.
These external data, however, may not share the same structure as the primary study.
For instance, they might include only a control arm, as is often the case with data derived from historical records or third-party sources.
The integration of external control data raises intriguing statistical challenges regarding how to use these data safely and effectively.
In this paper, we adopt a potential outcomes framework within the primary study, focusing on estimating ATT in the presence of external controls, with potentially high-dimensional confounders.
We investigate when and how these external controls could improve ATT estimation, and propose methods for leveraging them effectively.

Our approach begins with the standard doubly robust estimator for ATT in the primary study, constructed using the efficient influence function.
This estimator is termed doubly robust because it requires only one of two models—either the propensity score model or the conditional mean model—to be correctly specified, though not necessarily both.
Building on this framework, a similar doubly robust estimator for ATT can be derived when incorporating external controls.
However, a key question arises: does the inclusion of external controls provide statistical benefits in terms of improved estimation efficiency?
For doubly robust estimators, this benefit is understood as follows: when only one of the two models (the propensity score model or the conditional mean model) is correctly specified, does the estimator that incorporates external controls remain more efficient than the one based solely on primary study data?
Existing research has explored the integration of external control data from both Bayesian
(
Yang et al. 2023
;
Ohigashi et al. 2024
)
and frequentist
(
Li et al. 2023
;
Gao et al. 2023
;
Liao et al. 2023
;
Schwartz et al. 2023
;
Valancius et al. 2024
;
Zhu et al. 2024
)
perspectives. However, ensuring the doubly safe use of external controls—guaranteeing efficiency gains while maintaining the doubly robust property—is a nontrivial challenge that has often been overlooked in the literature.

Another challenge addressed in this paper is the issue of high dimensionality, where the number of confounders can far exceed the sample size
n
of the primary study.
Specifically, modeling high-dimensional propensity score or conditional mean models poses significant difficulties, whether with or without external controls.
The problem of managing high-dimensionality under model misspecification has garnered considerable attention in recent literature, including works by
Farrell 2015
,
Belloni et al. 2018
,
Tan 2020a
, and
Ghosh & Tan 2022
.
In this paper, we extend these insights to the framework of ATT estimation with external controls.
We show that high-dimensional nuisance models can be effectively handled to facilitate the development of a doubly robust inference procedure.

In summary, our methodology begins by proposing a doubly robust estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{\rm eff}
based on the derived efficient influence function for estimating ATT in the presence of external controls.
This estimator is consistent when at least one of the nuisance working models is correctly specified and achieves the semiparametric efficiency bound when both models are correctly specified.
However,
\widehat{\theta}_{\rm eff}
may exhibit lower efficiency than the naive doubly robust estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{\rm nv}
which does not use external controls, in cases where only one of the nuisance working models is correctly specified.
This is problematic, as it implies that incorporating external controls could be detrimental to efficiency.
To address this issue, we propose an improved estimator,
\widehat{\theta}_{\widehat{a}}
, which guarantees an efficiency gain over the naive estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{\rm nv}
even when only one nuisance working model is correctly specified.
We refer to this new estimator as doubly safe.
Additionally, both of our proposed estimator,
\widehat{\theta}_{\rm eff}
and
\widehat{\theta}_{\widehat{a}}
, support a doubly robust inference procedure by effectively handling high-dimensional nuisance working models.

By doing so, our work makes two significant contributions.
Firstly, by appropriately addressing the high-dimensional nuisance working models and effectively applying the efficient influence function, our proposed estimators both
\widehat{\theta}_{\rm eff}
and
\widehat{\theta}_{\widehat{a}}
retain the double robustness property in both estimation and inference.
Secondly, the proposed estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{\widehat{a}}
enjoys the doubly safe property, in that, even when only one nuisance working model is correctly specified, either the propensity score model or the conditional mean model, it still guarantees an efficiency gain over the naive estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{\rm nv}
thus ensures the safe use of the external control data.

Preliminaries

2.1
Primary Study

In applications, the primary study is usually a randomized controlled trial (RCT), but without loss of generality we consider a more general set of assumptions (details below) in the standard potential outcome framework
(
Neyman 1923
;
Rubin 1974
)
, where
Y_{t}
is the outcome if the subject were assigned to treatment
T=t
,
t=0,1
.
Throughout the paper, we consider continuous outcomes, though our framework is able to be generalized to other outcome types.
We denote
{\bf X}
the
(d+1)
-dimensional covariate with
1
the first coordinate, and we also denote
n
the sample size in the primary study.
We adopt the following standard assumptions:

1

Consistency: The observed outcome
Y
is equal to the potential outcome
Y_{t}
when the treatment received is
t
,
t=0,1
. That is, the outcome we observe is
Y=Y_{1}T+Y_{0}(1-T)
.

2

Ignorability: The treatment assignment is conditionally independent of the potential outcomes given
{\bf X}
; i.e.,

\displaystyle T\perp\{Y_{1},Y_{0}\}\mid{\bf X}.

(1)

In particular for RCT, we have
T\perp\{Y_{1},Y_{0},{\bf X}\}
.

3

Positivity: For each level of the covariate
{\bf X}
, the probability of receiving either treatment or control is greater than zero.

2.3
Parameter of Interest

In this paper, our parameter of interest is the ATT in the primary study. That is,

\displaystyle\theta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E(Y_{1}-Y_{0}\mid T=1,R=1)

(3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E(Y_{1}-Y_{0}\mid T=1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\int\int yp({\bf x})f_{1}(y\mid{\bf x})g({\bf x})\differential y\differential{\bf x}-\int\int yp({\bf x})f_{0}(y\mid{\bf x})g({\bf x})\differential y\differential{\bf x}}{\int p({\bf x})g({\bf x})\differential{\bf x}}.

(4)

It is interesting to note that, from (
3
), the ATT in the primary study is actually the same as the ATT in the combined study, because we do not have
T=1
subjects in external controls.
Also, from (
4
), the only term that external controls might be beneficial for estimation is
f_{0}(y\mid{\bf x})
.
Indeed, the interesting phenomenon we figure out in this paper is, even though the combined study has a much larger sample size than the primary study for estimating
f_{0}(y\mid{\bf x})
, it does not automatically indicate a guaranteed statistical benefit.
It heavily depends on how the statistical procedure, including both estimation and inference, is devised, especially under high-dimensionality.

3
Methodology

3.1
Proposed Estimator with External Controls

Putting primary study data and external controls together, we consider the Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
of all one-dimensional zero-mean measurable functions of the data following the likelihood function proportional to

\displaystyle p({\bf x})^{rt}\{1-p({\bf x})\}^{r(1-t)}f_{1}(y\mid{\bf x})^{rt}f_{0}(y\mid{\bf x})^{1-rt}g({\bf x})^{r}h({\bf x})^{1-r},

with finite variance, equipped with the inner product
\langle h_{1},h_{2}\rangle=E\{h_{1}(\cdot)h_{2}(\cdot)\}
where
h_{1}(\cdot),h_{2}(\cdot)\in{\mathcal{H}}
.
Our first result below gives an orthogonal decomposition of the semiparametric tangent space
{\mathcal{T}}

(
Bickel et al. 1993
;
Tsiatis 2006
)
that is defined as the mean squared closure of the tangent spaces of parametric submodels spanned by the score vectors, and provides the efficient influence function for estimating
\theta
.

Proposition
1
.

The semiparametric tangent space
{\mathcal{T}}
can be decomposed as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{T}}=\Lambda_{p}\oplus\Lambda_{1}\oplus\Lambda_{0}\oplus\Lambda_{g}\oplus\Lambda_{h},

where

\displaystyle\Lambda_{p}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[r\ a({\bf x})\{t-p({\bf x})\}:\forall a({\bf x})\right],

\displaystyle\Lambda_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{rt\ s_{1}(y,{\bf x}):\int s_{1}(y,{\bf x})f_{1}(y\mid{\bf x})\differential y=0\right\},

\displaystyle\Lambda_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{(1-rt)\ s_{0}(y,{\bf x}):\int s_{0}(y,{\bf x})f_{0}(y\mid{\bf x})\differential y=0\right\},

\displaystyle\Lambda_{g}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[r\ u({\bf x}):\int u({\bf x})g({\bf x})\differential{\bf x}=E\{u({\bf X})\mid R=1\}=0\right],

\displaystyle\Lambda_{h}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[(1-r)\ v({\bf x}):\int v({\bf x})h({\bf x})\differential{\bf x}=E\{v({\bf X})\mid R=0\}=0\right],

are the tangent spaces with respect to
p({\bf x})
,
f_{1}(y\mid{\bf x})
,
f_{0}(y\mid{\bf x})
,
g({\bf x})
and
h({\bf x})
, respectively.
The notation
\oplus
represents the direct sum of two spaces that are orthogonal to each other.
Further, the efficient influence function for estimating
\theta
is
\phi_{\rm eff}(\mu^{*},\delta^{*})
, where

\displaystyle\phi_{\rm eff}(\mu,\delta)\equiv\frac{rt\{y-\mu({\bf x})-\theta^{*}\}}{\pi p}-\frac{(1-rt)\delta({\bf x})\{y-\mu({\bf x})\}}{\pi p\{1-\delta({\bf x})\}},

(5)

where
\mu^{*}({\bf x})
and
\delta^{*}({\bf x})
represent the true models of
E(Y_{0}\mid{\bf x})
and
\hbox{pr}(T=1\mid{\bf x})
,
\theta^{*}
represents the true value of the estimand of interest.

The proof of Proposition
1
can be found in Supplement
.
In general, one can construct the doubly robust and semiparametrically efficient estimator for
\theta
, according to the efficient influence function (EIF) in Proposition
1
.
To do so, one needs to build statistical models for
\mu^{*}({\bf x})
and
\delta^{*}({\bf x})
, respectively.
Under high dimensionality, bearing in mind the tradeoff between the model complexity and model interpretability, we fit working models
\mu_{\rm eff}({\bf x})\equiv{\bf x}^{\rm T}{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm eff}
and
\delta({\bf x})\equiv{\mbox{expit}}({\bf x}^{\rm T}{\bm{\gamma}})
.
Using the combined data from both primary study and external controls, we denote the estimate of
{\bm{\alpha}}
as
\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}
, its asymptotic limit as
\overline{{\bm{\alpha}}}
, and similarly,
\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}
and
\overline{{\bm{\gamma}}}
, respectively.
If
\overline{\mu}_{\rm eff}({\bf x})\equiv{\bf x}^{\rm T}\overline{{\bm{\alpha}}}
equals
\mu^{*}({\bf x})
, we call the working model
\mu_{\rm eff}({\bf x})={\bf x}^{\rm T}{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm eff}
correctly specified and otherwise misspecified.
Similarly, if
\overline{\delta}({\bf x})\equiv{\mbox{expit}}({\bf x}^{\rm T}\overline{{\bm{\gamma}}})
equals
\delta^{*}({\bf x})
, we call the working model
\delta({\bf x})={\mbox{expit}}({\bf x}^{\rm T}{\bm{\gamma}})
correctly specified and otherwise misspecified.
Thus, our proposed estimator is

\widehat{\theta}_{\rm eff}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm eff},\widehat{\bm{\gamma}})
, where

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{\rm eff}({\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm eff},{\bm{\gamma}})=\frac{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\{r_{i}t_{i}(y_{i}-{\bf x}_{i}^{\rm T}{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm eff})-(1-r_{i}t_{i})\exp(\x_i\trans\bg)(y_{i}-{\bf x}_{i}^{\rm T}{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm eff})\right\}}{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}r_{i}t_{i}}.

(6)

For convenience, we also denote

\displaystyle\phi_{\rm eff}({\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm eff},{\bm{\gamma}})\equiv\frac{rt(y-{\bf x}^{\rm T}{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm eff}-\theta^{*})}{\pi p}-\frac{(1-rt)\exp(\x\trans\bg)(y-{\bf x}^{\rm T}{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm eff})}{\pi p}.

(7)

By appropriately devising nuisance parameter estimates
\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm eff}
and
\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}
to be introduced in Section
3.4
, Proposition
2
in Section
4.1
rigorously shows the desired theoretical properties of the proposed estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{\rm eff}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm eff},\widehat{\bm{\gamma}})
; i.e., double robustness for estimation as well as double robustness for inference
(
Zhang et al. 2021
;
Athey et al. 2018
;
Sun & Tan 2022
;
Ning et al. 2020
;
Baybutt & Navjeevan 2023
;
Smucler et al. 2019
;
Wu et al. 2021
)
.
Roughly, we have, for estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{\rm eff}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm eff},\widehat{\bm{\gamma}})
,

DR1eff.

If
\overline{\mu}_{\rm eff}=\mu^{*}
but
\overline{\delta}\neq\delta^{*}
, it is consistent, and its variance is
E\{\phi^{2}_{\rm eff}(\mu^{*},\overline{\delta})\}/N
;

DR2eff.

If
\overline{\delta}=\delta^{*}
but
\overline{\mu}_{\rm eff}\neq\mu^{*}
, it is consistent, and its variance is
E\{\phi^{2}_{\rm eff}(\overline{\mu}_{\rm eff},\delta^{*})\}/N
;

DR3eff.

If both
\overline{\mu}_{\rm eff}=\mu^{*}
and
\overline{\delta}=\delta^{*}
, it is consistent and semiparametrically efficient, and its variance is
E\{\phi^{2}_{\rm eff}(\mu^{*},\delta^{*})/N\}
.

Remark
1
.

It warrants to emphasize that this type of result is not trivial.
In DR1eff and DR2eff when one of the two working models is misspecified, the variance of the estimator needs to take into account the uncertainty of the misspecified model
\overline{\delta}(\cdot)
or
\overline{\mu}_{\rm eff}(\cdot)
; however, the above result indicates that the corresponding variance still preserves the same format as the situation when both working models are correctly specified.

3.2
Naive Estimator without External Controls and the Comparison

In the absence of external controls, the naive estimator corresponds to the one that relies on the efficient influence function in the primary study.
This is a standard result in the literature.
Specifically, the EIF in (
5
) degenerates into
\phi_{\rm nv}(\mu^{*},p^{*})\equiv\frac{r}{\pi}\varphi_{\rm nv}(\mu^{*},p^{*})
, where

\displaystyle\varphi_{\rm nv}(\mu,p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{t\{y-\mu({\bf x})-\theta^{*}\}}{p}-\frac{(1-t)p({\bf x})\{y-\mu({\bf x})\}}{p\{1-p({\bf x})\}}.

Similar to Section
3.1
, we also fit working models
\mu_{\rm nv}({\bf x})\equiv{\bf x}^{\rm T}{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm nv}
,
p({\bf x})\equiv{\mbox{expit}}({\bf x}^{\rm T}{\bm{\beta}})
, and denote

\displaystyle\varphi_{\rm nv}({\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm nv},{\bm{\beta}})=\frac{t(y-{\bf x}^{\rm T}{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm nv}-\theta^{*})}{p}-\frac{(1-t)\exp(\x\trans\bb)(y-{\bf x}^{\rm T}{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm nv})}{p}.

(8)

Thus, the naive estimator is
\widehat{\theta}_{\rm nv}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm nv},\widehat{\bm{\beta}})
, where

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{\rm nv}({\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm nv},{\bm{\beta}})=\frac{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\{t_{i}(y_{i}-{\bf x}_{i}^{\rm T}{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm nv})-(1-t_{i})\exp(\x_i\trans\bb)(y_{i}-{\bf x}_{i}^{\rm T}{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm nv})\right\}}{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}}.

(9)

By appropriately devising nuisance parameter estimates
\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm nv}
and
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}
to be introduced in Section
3.4
, Proposition
in Supplement
rigorously shows the desired theoretical properties of the naive estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{\rm nv}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm nv},\widehat{\bm{\beta}})
; i.e., double robustness for estimation as well as double robustness for inference.
Roughly, we have, for estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{\rm nv}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm nv},\widehat{\bm{\beta}})
,

DR1nv.

If
\overline{\mu}_{\rm nv}=\mu^{*}
but
\overline{p}\neq p^{*}
, it is consistent, and its variance is
E\{\varphi^{2}_{\rm nv}(\mu^{*},\overline{p})\mid R=1\}/n
;

DR2nv.

If
\overline{p}=p^{*}
but
\overline{\mu}_{\rm nv}\neq\mu^{*}
, it is consistent, and its variance is
E\{\varphi^{2}_{\rm nv}(\overline{\mu}_{\rm nv},p^{*})\mid R=1\}/n
;

DR3nv.

If both
\overline{\mu}_{\rm nv}=\mu^{*}
and
\overline{p}=p^{*}
, it is consistent and semiparametrically efficient when only using primary study data, and its variance is
E\{\varphi^{2}_{\rm nv}(\mu^{*},p^{*})\mid R=1\}/n
.

Remark
2
.

As a special case, when the primary study is a RCT, there is no misspecification issue of the model
p({\bf x})
. Then the naive estimator becomes
\widehat{\theta}_{\rm nv}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm nv})
which is robust to the misspecification of the model
\mu_{\rm nv}({\bf x})
, where

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{\rm nv}({\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm nv})=\frac{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{t_{i}-p}{1-p}(y_{i}-{\bf x}_{i}^{\rm T}{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm nv})}{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}}.

Now we can evaluate the advantages of incorporating external control data by comparing the three properties of
\widehat{\theta}_{\rm eff}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm eff},\widehat{\bm{\gamma}})
with
\widehat{\theta}_{\rm nv}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm nv},\widehat{\bm{\beta}})
.
The comparison of DR3eff versus DR3nv is straightforward: when all of the four working models are correctly specified in that
\overline{\mu}_{\rm nv}=\overline{\mu}_{\rm eff}=\mu^{*}
,
\overline{p}=p^{*}
and
\overline{\delta}=\delta^{*}
, the proposed estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{\rm eff}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm eff},\widehat{\bm{\gamma}})
is certainly no less efficient than the naive estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{\rm nv}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm nv},\widehat{\bm{\beta}})
.
However, such a guarantee may not be ensured under propensity score misspecification (DR1eff versus DR1nv) or outcome model misspecification (DR2eff versus DR2nv).
Specifically, we have

(comp1)

DR1eff versus DR1nv: if
\overline{\mu}_{\rm nv}=\overline{\mu}_{\rm eff}=\mu^{*}
but at least one of the two,
\overline{p}\neq p^{*}
and
\overline{\delta}\neq\delta^{*}
, is noted, then
\widehat{\theta}_{\rm eff}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm eff},\widehat{\bm{\gamma}})
may be less efficient than
\widehat{\theta}_{\rm nv}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm nv},\widehat{\bm{\beta}})
; i.e., it is possible that
E\{\phi_{\rm eff}^{2}(\mu^{*},\overline{\delta})\}>\pi^{-1}E\{\varphi_{\rm nv}^{2}(\mu^{*},\overline{p})\mid R=1\}=E\{\phi_{\rm nv}^{2}(\mu^{*},\overline{p})\}
. Note that this could happen even when the primary study is a RCT.

(comp2)

DR2eff versus DR2nv: if
\overline{p}=p^{*}
and
\overline{\delta}=\delta^{*}
but at least one of the two,
\overline{\mu}_{\rm nv}\neq\mu^{*}
and
\overline{\mu}_{\rm eff}\neq\mu^{*}
, is noted, then, as long as
\overline{\mu}_{\rm nv}\neq\overline{\mu}_{\rm eff}
, the estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{\rm eff}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm eff},\widehat{\bm{\gamma}})
may be less efficient than
\widehat{\theta}_{\rm nv}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm nv},\widehat{\bm{\beta}})
; i.e., it is possible that
E\{\phi_{\rm eff}(\overline{\mu}_{\rm eff},\delta^{*})^{2}\}>\pi^{-1}E\{\varphi_{\rm nv}^{2}(\overline{\mu}_{\rm nv},p^{*})\mid R=1\}=E\{\phi_{\rm nv}^{2}(\overline{\mu}_{\rm nv},p^{*})\}
.
This could also happen when the primary study is a RCT.

Therefore, from either (comp1) or (comp2) above, directly employing the doubly robust estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{\rm eff}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm eff},\widehat{\bm{\gamma}})
in practical applications may amplify the asymptotic variance thus is
dangerous
.
In the following, we propose a novel estimator, that not only
retains the double

robustness property
in both estimation and inference as the estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{\rm eff}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm eff},\widehat{\bm{\gamma}})
, but also
ensures the double safety property
in that it is guaranteed to be no less efficient than the naive estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{\rm nv}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm nv},\widehat{\bm{\beta}})
in scenarios (comp1) and (comp2) when some working models are misspecified.
Certainly when all the working models are correctly specified, this newly proposed estimator coincides the previously proposed estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{\rm eff}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm eff},\widehat{\bm{\gamma}})
, and both of them are semiparametrically efficient.

3.3
Proposed Estimator that Ensures Double Safety

The proposed estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{\widehat{a}}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm nv},\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm eff},\widehat{\bm{\beta}},\widehat{\bm{\gamma}})
, shortly as
\widehat{\theta}_{\widehat{a}}
, satisfies the following three properties:

DSDR1.

If
\overline{\mu}_{\rm nv}=\overline{\mu}_{\rm eff}=\mu^{*}
but at least one of the two,
\overline{p}\neq p^{*}
and
\overline{\delta}\neq\delta^{*}
, is noted, the estimator is consistent (doubly robust estimation), more efficient than
\widehat{\theta}_{\rm nv}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm nv},\widehat{\bm{\beta}})
(doubly safe estimation), and can produce asymptotically valid interval estimates (doubly robust inference);

DSDR2.

If
\overline{p}=p^{*}
and
\overline{\delta}=\delta^{*}
, but at least one of the two,
\overline{\mu}_{\rm nv}\neq\mu^{*}
and
\overline{\mu}_{\rm eff}\neq\mu^{*}
, is noted, the estimator is consistent (doubly robust estimation), more efficient than
\widehat{\theta}_{\rm nv}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm nv},\widehat{\bm{\beta}})
(doubly safe estimation), and can produce asymptotically valid interval estimates (doubly robust inference);

DSDR3.

If
\overline{\mu}_{\rm nv}=\overline{\mu}_{\rm eff}=\mu^{*}
,
\overline{p}=p^{*}
and
\overline{\delta}=\delta^{*}
, the estimator is semiparametrically efficient, same as
\widehat{\theta}_{\rm eff}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm eff},\widehat{\bm{\gamma}})
.

The proposed estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{\widehat{a}}
is defined as

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{\widehat{a}}=\widehat{a}\ \widehat{\theta}_{\rm eff}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm eff},\widehat{\bm{\gamma}})+(1-\widehat{a})\widehat{\theta}_{\rm nv}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm nv},\widehat{\bm{\beta}}),

where

\displaystyle\widehat{a}=\frac{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\{\phi_{\rm nv}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm nv},\widehat{\bm{\beta}})-\phi_{\rm eff}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm eff},\widehat{\bm{\gamma}})\}\phi_{\rm nv}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm nv},\widehat{\bm{\beta}})}{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\{\phi_{\rm nv}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm nv},\widehat{\bm{\beta}})-\phi_{\rm eff}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm eff},\widehat{\bm{\gamma}})\}^{2}},

(10)

is an estimator of
a^{*}
, defined as

\displaystyle a^{*}=\argmin_{a}\hbox{Var}\{a\ \widehat{\theta}_{\rm eff}(\overline{{\bm{\alpha}}}_{\rm eff},\overline{{\bm{\gamma}}})+(1-a)\widehat{\theta}_{\rm nv}(\overline{{\bm{\alpha}}}_{\rm nv},\overline{{\bm{\beta}}})\}.

(11)

Theorem
2
in Section
4.2
rigorously shows these desired theoretical properties of the proposed estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{\widehat{a}}
.

3.4
Nuisance Parameter Estimation

In this section, we point out the basic ideas underlying the construction of the parameter estimators
\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm eff}
,
\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm nv}
,
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}
and
\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}
such that the estimators
\widehat{\theta}_{\rm eff}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm eff},\widehat{\bm{\gamma}})
and
\widehat{\theta}_{\rm nv}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm nv},\widehat{\bm{\beta}})
have the same large sample properties as
\widehat{\theta}_{\rm eff}(\overline{{\bm{\alpha}}}_{\rm eff},\overline{{\bm{\gamma}}})
and
\widehat{\theta}_{\rm nv}(\overline{{\bm{\alpha}}}_{\rm nv},\overline{{\bm{\beta}}})
, respectively, even with model misspecification.

Consider the Taylor expansion of
\widehat{\theta}_{\rm eff}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm eff},\widehat{\bm{\gamma}})
and
\widehat{\theta}_{\rm nv}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm nv},\widehat{\bm{\beta}})
:

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{\rm eff}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm eff},\widehat{\bm{\gamma}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{\rm eff}(\overline{{\bm{\alpha}}}_{\rm eff},\overline{{\bm{\gamma}}})+\Delta_{11}+\Delta_{12}+o_{p}(N^{-1/2}),

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{\rm nv}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm nv},\widehat{\bm{\beta}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{\rm nv}(\overline{{\bm{\alpha}}}_{\rm nv},\overline{{\bm{\beta}}})+\Delta_{21}+\Delta_{22}+o_{p}(N^{-1/2}),

with

\displaystyle\Delta_{11}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm eff}-\overline{{\bm{\alpha}}}_{\rm eff})^{\rm T}\times\frac{\partial}{\partial{\bm{\alpha}}}\widetilde{E}\{\phi_{\rm eff}({\bm{\alpha}},{\bm{\gamma}})\}\bigg|_{({\bm{\alpha}},{\bm{\gamma}})=(\overline{{\bm{\alpha}}}_{\rm eff},\overline{{\bm{\gamma}}})},

\displaystyle\Delta_{12}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}-\overline{{\bm{\gamma}}})^{\rm T}\times\frac{\partial}{\partial{\bm{\gamma}}}\widetilde{E}\{\phi_{\rm eff}({\bm{\alpha}},{\bm{\gamma}})\}\bigg|_{({\bm{\alpha}},{\bm{\gamma}})=(\overline{{\bm{\alpha}}}_{\rm eff},\overline{{\bm{\gamma}}})},

\displaystyle\Delta_{21}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm nv}-\overline{{\bm{\alpha}}}_{\rm nv})^{\rm T}\times\frac{\partial}{\partial{\bm{\alpha}}}\widetilde{E}\{\phi_{\rm nv}({\bm{\alpha}},{\bm{\beta}})\}\bigg|_{({\bm{\alpha}},{\bm{\gamma}})=(\overline{{\bm{\alpha}}}_{\rm nv},\overline{{\bm{\beta}}})},

\displaystyle\Delta_{22}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\widehat{\bm{\beta}}-\overline{{\bm{\beta}}})^{\rm T}\times\frac{\partial}{\partial{\bm{\beta}}}\widetilde{E}\{\phi_{\rm nv}({\bm{\alpha}},{\bm{\beta}})\}\bigg|_{({\bm{\alpha}},{\bm{\gamma}})=(\overline{{\bm{\alpha}}}_{\rm nv},\overline{{\bm{\beta}}})},

where the remainders are taken to be
o_{p}(N^{-1/2})
under mild regularity conditions.

In the presence of model misspecification, note that
\Delta_{11}+\Delta_{12}+\Delta_{21}+\Delta_{22}
is at least
O_{p}(N^{-1/2})
because the second terms of
\Delta_{11}
,
\Delta_{12}
,
\Delta_{21}
and
\Delta_{22}
are typically of the order
O_{p}(1)
, e.g., using the regularized maximized likelihood estimator, and
the first terms are at least
O_{p}(N^{-1/2})

Therefore, in order that the estimators
\widehat{\theta}_{\rm eff}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm eff},\widehat{\bm{\gamma}})
and
\widehat{\theta}_{\rm nv}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm nv},\widehat{\bm{\beta}})
possess desired theoretical properties,
we expect that all of the four terms
\Delta_{11}
,
\Delta_{12}
,
\Delta_{21}
and
\Delta_{22}
are of the order
o_{p}(N^{-1/2})
, which can be achieved if
the second terms in them are in the order of
o_{p}(1)
.
Thus, the population versions of the second terms in
\Delta_{11}
,
\Delta_{12}
,
\Delta_{21}
and
\Delta_{22}
are actually the estimating equations that the asymptotic limits
(\overline{{\bm{\alpha}}}_{\rm eff},\overline{{\bm{\gamma}}},\overline{{\bm{\alpha}}}_{\rm nv},\overline{{\bm{\beta}}})
are expected to satisfy:

\displaystyle E\{(1-RT)(Y-{\bf X}^{\rm T}\overline{{\bm{\alpha}}}_{\rm eff})\exp(\X\trans\overline\bg){\bf X}\}={\bf 0},

(12)

\displaystyle E\left[\{RT-(1-RT)\exp(\X\trans\overline\bg)\}{\bf X}\right]={\bf 0},

\displaystyle E\{R(1-T)(Y-{\bf X}^{\rm T}\overline{{\bm{\alpha}}}_{\rm nv})\exp(\X\trans\overline\bb){\bf X}\}={\bf 0},

\displaystyle E\left[\{RT-R(1-T)\exp(\X\trans\overline\bb)\}{\bf X}\right]={\bf 0}.

Theoretical Results for
\widehat{\theta}_{\rm eff}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm eff},\widehat{\bm{\gamma}})
and
\widehat{\theta}_{\rm nv}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm nv},\widehat{\bm{\beta}})

The estimator
\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}
is the minimizer of
\ell_{\lambda}({\bm{\gamma}})
in (
13

\lambda_{\bm{\gamma}}
in (

\lambda_{\bm{\gamma}}=A_{0}\lambda_{0}

\lambda_{{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm eff}}=A_{1}\lambda_{1}

For simplicity, we only present the most essential results for
\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}

\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm eff}

.
To proceed, we need the following assumption:

Assumption
1
.

We assume

(i)

\max_{j=1,\dots,d}|X_{j}|\leq C_{0}
, for a constant
C_{0}>0
.

(ii)

{\bf X}^{\rm T}\overline{{\bm{\gamma}}}\leq B_{0}
almost surely for
B_{0}\in{\cal R}
; that is,
\overline{\delta}({\bf X})={\mbox{expit}}({\bf X}^{\rm T}\overline{{\bm{\gamma}}})
is bounded from above by
(1+e^{-B_{0}})^{-1}.

(iii)

The compatibility condition (defined below) holds for
\Sigma_{\bm{\gamma}}
with subset
S_{{\bm{\gamma}}}=\{0\}\cup\{j:\gamma_{j}\neq 0,j=1,\dots,d\}
and constants
\nu_{0}
and
\xi_{0}>1
; it also holds for
\Sigma_{\bm{\gamma}}
with subset
S_{{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm eff}}=\{0\}\cup\{j:\alpha_{{\rm eff},j}\neq 0,j=1,\dots,d\}
and constants
\nu_{1}
and
\xi_{1}>1
, where
\Sigma_{\bm{\gamma}}=E\{\exp(\X\trans\overline\bg)(1-RT){\bf X}{\bf X}^{\rm T}\}
.

(iv)

Y-{\bf X}^{\rm T}\overline{{\bm{\alpha}}}_{\rm eff}
is uniformly sub-Gaussian given
{\bf X}
:
D_{0}^{2}E[\{\exp(Y-\X\trans\overline\ba\eff)^{2}/D_{0}^{2}\}-1|{\bf X}]\leq D_{1}^{2}.

(v)

|S_{\bm{\gamma}}|\lambda_{0}=o(1)
and
|S_{{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm eff}}|\lambda_{1}=o(1)
.

Assumption
1
(i)-(ii), commonly used in the literature
(
Tan 2020a
;
Tan 2020b
)
, is mainly used to bound the curvature of a symmetrized Bregman divergence associated with a non-quadratic loss function.

In Assumption
1
(iii), following the literature
(
Bühlmann & van de
Geer 2011
)
, the compatibility condition holds for the
(d+1)\times(d+1)
matrix
\Sigma
with the subset

\nu_{0}
and
\xi_{0}>1
,
if and only if

\nu^{2}_{0}\left(\sum_{j\in S}|b_{j}|\right)^{2}\leq|S|({\bf b}^{\rm T}\Sigma{\bf b})

for any vector

satisfying

, and its proof can be found in Section
.

Proposition
5
.

Under Assumptions
1
and
(presented in Supplement
), we have

\displaystyle|\widehat{\theta}_{\widehat{a}}-\overline{\theta}_{a^{*}}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O_{p}(1)|a^{*}|\pi^{-1}\left\{(|S_{{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm eff}}|+|S_{\bm{\gamma}}|)\lambda_{0}\lambda_{1}+|S_{\bm{\gamma}}|\lambda_{0}^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle+O_{p}(1)|1-a^{*}|\left\{(|S_{\alpha_{\rm nv}}|+|S_{\beta}|)\lambda_{01}\lambda_{11}+|S_{\beta}|\lambda_{01}^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle+\ O_{p}(1/\sqrt{N})(|S_{\gamma}|\lambda_{0}+|S_{{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm eff}}|\lambda_{1}+|S_{\beta}|\lambda_{01}+|S_{{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm nv}}|\lambda_{11}),

where
\overline{\theta}_{a^{*}}=a^{*}\widehat{\theta}_{\rm eff}(\overline{{\bm{\alpha}}}_{\rm eff},\overline{{\bm{\gamma}}})+(1-a^{*})\widehat{\theta}_{\rm nv}(\overline{{\bm{\alpha}}}_{\rm nv},\overline{{\bm{\beta}}})
.

The results in Proposition
5
also indicate, when
a^{*}=1
, the convergence rate of
|\widehat{\theta}_{\widehat{a}}-\overline{\theta}_{a^{*}}|
is
O_{p}\{\pi^{-1}(|S_{{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm eff}}|+|S_{\bm{\gamma}}|)\lambda_{0}\lambda_{1}\}+O_{p}(\pi^{-1}|S_{\bm{\gamma}}|\lambda_{0}^{2})
, echoes that of the estimator
|\widehat{\theta}_{\rm eff}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm eff},\widehat{\bm{\gamma}})-\widehat{\theta}_{\rm eff}(\overline{{\bm{\alpha}}}_{\rm eff},\overline{{\bm{\gamma}}})|
, while when
a^{*}=0
, the convergence rate of
|\widehat{\theta}_{\widehat{a}}-\overline{\theta}_{a^{*}}|
,

O_{p}\left\{(|S_{\alpha_{\rm nv}}|+|S_{\beta}|)\lambda_{01}\lambda_{11}+|S_{\beta}|\lambda_{01}^{2}\right\}
, reflects the convergence rate of
|\widehat{\theta}_{\rm nv}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm nv},\widehat{\bm{\beta}})-\widehat{\theta}_{\rm nv}(\overline{{\bm{\alpha}}}_{\rm nv},\overline{{\bm{\beta}}})|
.

Finally we present the asymptotic normality of the estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{\widehat{a}}
.

Theorem
2
.

Assume Assumptions
1
and
(presented in Supplement
) hold,
(|S_{{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm nv}}|+|S_{{\bm{\beta}}}|+|S_{{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm eff}}|+|S_{{\bm{\gamma}}}|)\log\ (d+1)/\sqrt{n}=o(1)
and either of the two working models is correctly specified. Then, we have

\displaystyle\sqrt{N}(\widehat{\theta}_{\widehat{a}}-\theta^{*})\xrightarrow{d}N(0,V_{a}),

which can also be written as

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\theta}_{\widehat{a}}-\theta^{*})\xrightarrow{d}N(0,\pi V_{a}),

where

\displaystyle V_{a}=E\{\phi_{\rm nv}^{2}(\overline{{\bm{\alpha}}}_{\rm nv},\overline{{\bm{\beta}}})\}-\frac{\left(E\left[\{\phi_{\rm nv}(\overline{{\bm{\alpha}}}_{\rm nv},\overline{{\bm{\beta}}})-\phi_{\rm eff}(\overline{{\bm{\alpha}}}_{\rm eff},\overline{{\bm{\gamma}}})\}\phi_{\rm eff}(\overline{{\bm{\alpha}}}_{\rm eff},\overline{{\bm{\gamma}}})\right]\right)^{2}}{E\left[\{\phi_{\rm nv}(\overline{{\bm{\alpha}}}_{\rm nv},\overline{{\bm{\beta}}})-\phi_{\rm eff}(\overline{{\bm{\alpha}}}_{\rm eff},\overline{{\bm{\gamma}}})\}^{2}\right]}.

Further, the
1-\alpha
confidence interval of
\theta^{*}
is

\displaystyle\left[\widehat{\theta}_{\widehat{a}}-z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\widehat{V}_{a}/N},\widehat{\theta}_{\widehat{a}}+z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\widehat{V}_{a}/N}\right],

where
z_{\alpha/2}
is the
1-\alpha/2
quantile of
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and

\displaystyle\widehat{V}_{a}=\widetilde{E}\{\phi_{\rm nv}^{2}(\widehat{{\bm{\alpha}}}_{\rm nv},\widehat{{\bm{\beta}}})\}-\frac{\left(\widetilde{E}\left[\{\phi_{\rm nv}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm nv},\widehat{\bm{\beta}})-\phi_{\rm eff}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm eff},\widehat{\bm{\gamma}})\}\phi_{\rm eff}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm eff},\widehat{\bm{\gamma}})\right]\right)^{2}}{\widetilde{E}\left[\{\phi_{\rm nv}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm nv},\widehat{\bm{\beta}})-\phi_{\rm eff}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm eff},\widehat{\bm{\gamma}})\}^{2}\right]}.

Theorem
2
indicates that the estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{\widehat{a}}
is no less efficient than the primary estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{\rm nv}
.
By rewriting

\displaystyle V_{a}=E\{\phi_{\rm eff}^{2}(\overline{{\bm{\alpha}}}_{\rm eff},\overline{{\bm{\gamma}}})\}-\frac{E^{2}\left[\{\phi_{\rm nv}(\overline{{\bm{\alpha}}}_{\rm nv},\overline{{\bm{\beta}}})-\phi_{\rm eff}(\overline{{\bm{\alpha}}}_{\rm eff},\overline{{\bm{\gamma}}})\}\phi_{\rm nv}(\overline{{\bm{\alpha}}}_{\rm eff},\overline{{\bm{\gamma}}})\right]}{E\left[\{\phi_{\rm nv}(\overline{{\bm{\alpha}}}_{\rm nv},\overline{{\bm{\beta}}})-\phi_{\rm eff}(\overline{{\bm{\alpha}}}_{\rm eff},\overline{{\bm{\gamma}}})\}^{2}\right]},

it is also no less efficient than the estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{\rm eff}
.
When both working models are correctly specified, we can obtain
a^{*}=1
, which means that the estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{\widehat{a}}=\widehat{\theta}_{\rm eff}
is also an efficient estimator and achieves the semiparametric efficiency bound.

Remark
6

(Discussion when
N\gg n
)
.

Sometimes, the external controls might have a much larger sample size than the primary study, we thus consider the
N\gg n
case.
Interestingly, the much larger external controls’ size does not help to reduce the convergence rate of the proposed estimators
\widehat{\theta}_{\rm eff}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm eff},\widehat{\bm{\gamma}})
and
\widehat{\theta}_{\widehat{a}}
; i.e., their convergence rates remain to be
n^{-1/2}
, as indicated in Theorem
1
and Theorem
2
.
For the estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{\rm eff}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm eff},\widehat{\bm{\gamma}})
, one can easily check that
\pi V_{1}=\pi E\{\phi^{2}_{\rm eff}(\overline{{\bm{\alpha}}}_{\rm eff},\overline{{\bm{\gamma}}})\}=O(1)
; thus, we write
\sqrt{n}\{\widehat{\theta}_{\rm eff}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{\rm eff},\widehat{\bm{\gamma}})-\theta^{*}\}\xrightarrow{d}N(0,\pi V_{1})
, as presented in Theorem
1
.
This also applies to the estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{\widehat{a}}
.

5
Simulations Studies

In this section, to comprehensively demonstrate the different performances of the three estimators
\widehat{\theta}_{\rm nv}
,
\widehat{\theta}_{\rm eff}
and
\widehat{\theta}_{\widehat{a}}
,
we consider the following two data generation models, the second of which considers three scenarios.

For
Model 1
, we first generate a
d
-dimensional multivariate normal random vector
{\bf U}\sim\mathcal{N}({\bf 0},\Sigma)
with
\Sigma_{jk}=2^{-|j-k|}
,
1\leq j,k\leq d
.
Then we set the covariate
{\bf X}=(1,X_{1},\dots,X_{d})^{\rm T}
to be
X_{j}=U_{j}~\mathrm{for}~1\leq j\leq d
and
{\bf X}^{\dagger}=(1,X^{\dagger}_{1},\dots,X^{\dagger}_{d})^{\rm T}

where
X^{\dagger}_{j}
is the normalized version of
U_{j}+\{(U_{j}+1)_{+}\}^{2}
with mean 0 and variance 1 for
j=1,\dots,4
, and
X^{\dagger}_{j}=U_{j}~\mathrm{for}~5\leq j\leq d
.
Then, we generate the binary indicator
R
by

\displaystyle\pi({\bf x})=\hbox{pr}(R=1\mid{\bf x})=\delta^{*}({\bf x})/p^{*}({\bf x}),

where
p^{*}({\bf x})={\mbox{expit}}(0.125x_{1})
and
\delta^{*}({\bf x})={\mbox{expit}}(-2+0.125x_{1})
.
Further, for the subjects with
R=1
, we generate the treatment variable
T
by

\displaystyle p({\bf x})=\hbox{pr}(T=1\mid{\bf x},R=1)=p^{*}({\bf x}).

Finally, for the potential outcomes
Y_{0}
and
Y_{1}
, we generate

Y_{0}=\mu^{*}_{0}({\bf X}^{\dagger})+\epsilon_{0}
and

Y_{1}=\epsilon_{1}
, where
\epsilon_{0}\sim N(0,1)
,
\epsilon_{1}\sim N(0,1/2)
, and
\mu^{*}_{0}({\bf x})=x_{1}+0.5x_{2}+0.25x_{3}+0.125x_{4}
.
Clearly,
Model 1
is designed for the situation that, both models
p({\bf x})
and
\delta({\bf x})
will be correctly specified, while the outcome mean model
\mu_{0}({\bf x})
will be misspecified.

For
Model 2
, we first generate a
d
-dimensional multivariate normal random vector
{\bf U}\sim\mathcal{N}({\bm{\mu}},\Sigma)
with
\Sigma_{jk}=2^{-|j-k|}
,
1\leq j,k\leq d
, and set the covariate
{\bf X}=(1,X_{1},\dots,X_{d})^{\rm T}
to be
X_{j}=U_{j}~\mathrm{for}~1\leq j\leq d
.
For subjects in the primary study with
R=1
, we set
{\bm{\mu}}={\bf 0}
, while for external controls with
R=0
, we set
\mu_{i}=-3
for
i=1\leq i\leq 4
and
\mu_{i}=0
for
i=5,\dots,d
.
Equivalently, this indicates that

\displaystyle\pi({\bf x})=\hbox{pr}(R=1\mid{\bf x})={\mbox{expit}}\{\log\pi-\log(1-\pi)-({\bf x}-{\bm{\mu}}_{1})^{\rm T}\Sigma^{-1}{\bm{\mu}}_{1}-0.5{\bm{\mu}}_{1}^{\rm T}\Sigma^{-1}{\bm{\mu}}_{1}\}.

where
{\bm{\mu}}_{1}=(-3,-3,-3,-3,0,\dots,0)^{\rm T}
.
Then for the subjects with
R=1
, we generate the treatment assignment as

\displaystyle p({\bf x})=\hbox{pr}(T=1\mid{\bf x},R=1)={\mbox{expit}}(-0.5+0.125x_{4}),

in
Case (i)
, and

\displaystyle p({\bf x})=\hbox{pr}(T=1\mid{\bf x},R=1)=0.7,

in both
Case (ii)
and
Case (iii)
.
Similar to Model 1, the potential outcomes are also generated by
Y_{0}=\mu^{*}_{0}({\bf X})+\epsilon_{0}
and

Y_{1}=\epsilon_{1}
, where
\epsilon_{0}\sim N(0,1)
,
\epsilon_{1}\sim N(0,1/2)
and
\mu_{0}^{*}(\cdot)
is the same as in Model 1.
In
Model 2
, the outcome mean model
\mu_{0}({\bf x})
will be correctly specified, and the model
p({\bf x})
will also be correctly specified, whereas the model
\delta({\bf x})=\pi({\bf x})p({\bf x})
will be misspecified in
Case (i)
but correctly specified in
Case (ii)
and
Case (iii)
.

In both Model 1 and Model 2, we consider a variety of combinations of
(N,d)
.
In Model 1, the data generating mechanism results in about 40% subjects from the primary study (the value
\pi
), and about 27% subjects in the primary study who receive the treatment (the value
p
).
In Model 2 Case (i) and Case (ii), the data generating mechanism results in about 40% subjects in the primary study who receive the treatment (the value
p
), and we fix the sample size of the external controls as
m=1,000
.
In Model 2 Case (iii), the data generating mechanism results in about 33% subjects from the primary study (the value
\pi
).

Based on 1,200 simulation replicates, we summarize the comparison results about the three estimators
\widehat{\theta}_{\rm nv}
,
\widehat{\theta}_{\rm eff}
, and
\widehat{\theta}_{\widehat{a}}
in Table

for

), in Table

\widehat{\theta}_{\rm nv}

\widehat{\theta}_{\rm eff}
or
\widehat{\theta}_{\widehat{a}}

\widehat{\theta}_{\rm nv}
.

.

d
but improves with the increasing sample size.

We are particularly interested in the efficiency comparisons among the three estimators.
In Tables
2
,
4
and
5
,
the ARE is always smaller than 1 indicating that both
\widehat{\theta}_{\rm eff}
and
\widehat{\theta}_{\widehat{a}}

\widehat{\theta}_{\rm nv}
.

\widehat{\theta}_{\widehat{a}}

\widehat{\theta}_{\rm eff}

\widehat{\theta}_{\widehat{a}}

\widehat{\theta}_{\rm eff}
.

(model
\delta({\bf x})

), we encounter a phenomenon that the ARE of the estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{\rm eff}
is greater than 1.
This means
\widehat{\theta}_{\rm eff}
is even less efficient than the estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{\rm nv}
—incorporating external controls might not obtain efficiency gain at all.
Luckily, the ARE of the proposed estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{\widehat{a}}
is strictly smaller than 1; it brings down the asymptotic variance and ensures the safe use of the external control data.

In summary,
\widehat{\theta}_{\rm eff}
could outperform
\widehat{\theta}_{\rm nv}
theoretically when all the nuisance models are correctly specified.
However, in real applications, estimation of the nuisance working models could be challenging, especially with high-dimensional covariates where correct model specification cannot be guaranteed.
In such cases, we recommend using
\widehat{\theta}_{\widehat{a}}
as it guarantees a safe and efficient estimate.

Discussion

This work is motivated by the growing interest in incorporating external control data, typically easier to obtain from historical records or third-party sources, into randomized controlled trials (RCTs).
Our initial analysis reveals that standard doubly robust estimation can paradoxically lead to reduced efficiency compared to estimators that do not use external controls.
This counterintuitive outcome suggests that the naive inclusion of external controls may, in fact, be detrimental to estimation efficiency.

To address this issue, we propose a novel doubly robust estimator that guarantees greater efficiency than the standard approach without external controls, even under model misspecification.
When all models are correctly specified, the proposed estimator coincides with the standard doubly robust estimator that incorporates external controls and achieves semiparametric efficiency.
The asymptotic theory developed here accommodates high-dimensional confounder settings, which are increasingly relevant given the growing prevalence of electronic health record data.

For clarity of presentation, we adopt linear regression as the working model for the outcome mean and logistic regression as the working model for the propensity score in high-dimensional settings.
This choice allows us to present the key ideas transparently.
Nonetheless, the proposed framework can be generalized to more complex working models.
The corresponding technical developments lie beyond the scope of this paper and merit further investigation in future research.
