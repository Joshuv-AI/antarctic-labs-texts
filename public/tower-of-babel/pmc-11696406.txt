Experimental and Comprehensive Study of a Full-Spectrum Solar Energy Efficient Cascade Utilization System Based on Spectral Splitting and Passive Cooling
Authors: Song Lv, Jingcai Deng, Juwen Ren, Mingming Zhang, Ying Guo, Mengqi Feng, Yangyang Wu, Bolong Zhang, Guoqing Shi, Yi Hu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSolar photovoltaic (PV) conversion has become a key area in today’s energy supply. However, incomplete utilization of the PV cell bandgap results in the conversion of photon energy outside the bandgap into waste heat, reducing the overall efficiency. Improving spectral utilization efficiency and mitigating the effects of PV waste heat are top priorities. In order to solve these problems, this study proposes a full-spectrum solar energy step utilization system that combines spectral splitting with passive radiative cooling. Spectral splitting is utilized to direct photon energy from inside and outside the bandgap to the PV cell and the thermoelectric generator, respectively, and passive radiative cooling is utilized to increase the thermoelectric temperature difference, broaden the spectral utilization range, and improve the energy utilization efficiency. The accuracy of the simulation model was verified through outdoor experiments, and the surface temperature of the PV modules was found to be reduced by 2.59 °C. With the help of the model, the study investigated the effect of environmental factors such as wind speed, humidity and ambient temperature on the performance of the system. This comprehensive analysis demonstrates the potential of combining radiative cooling with full-spectrum solar energy utilization for efficient and high-quality spectral utilization.

Introduction

With the escalation of energy crises and global climate change, the development of renewable energy has become a global consensus. Solar energy, as the predominant source of renewable energy on Earth, is receiving widespread attention and development due to its characteristics of being free, clean, green, and abundant in reserves.1,2 Photovoltaic (PV) cells directly convert solar energy into electricity through the PV effect, without any moving parts. This characteristic places them in a significant position within the renewable energy sector.3 However, traditional single-junction PV cells have a theoretical efficiency limit of approximately 33%, which is significantly lower than the Carnot limit (95%). This is primarily due to PV cells utilizing only a portion of the solar spectrum. A considerable amount of photon energy outside the bandgap is converted into waste heat, thereby reducing the overall performance of PV cells.4,5 In recent decades, there has been widespread attention on improving spectral utilization efficiency and reducing the impact of waste heat in PVs. Various designs and strategies have been proposed to address these challenges.

In the effort to enhance spectral utilization in PV cells, extensive research has focused on the synergistic coupling of solar energy based on the intrinsic properties of different devices. Stacking multiple PV cells with varying absorption bandgaps to construct multijunction solar cells has been deeply explored. This approach broadens the overall spectrum utilization of solar energy and enhances energy utilization efficiency. However, it still does not achieve the comprehensive utilization of full-spectrum solar energy.6−10 PV/T systems integrate solar collectors with PV cells to collect full-spectrum solar energy while providing both electricity and hot water. However, solar collectors require high temperatures to function effectively, whereas PV cells operate more efficiently at lower temperatures. Moreover, converting high-quality solar energy into low-grade thermal energy results in energy degradation and significant irreversible losses.11,12 Thermoelectric generators (TEG) directly convert thermal energy into electricity. Integrated with PV cells, they can lower the temperature of PV cells while simultaneously harnessing waste heat to produce clean electricity. This hybrid photovoltaic-thermoelectric (PV–TE) system converts full-spectrum solar energy into high-grade electrical energy.11,13−15 Khan et al.16 conducted experimental research on PV–TEG configurations. They observed that the temperature of the PV modules decreased from 55 to 52 °C. In comparison to standalone PV systems, the hybrid system’s output power increased from 8.78 to 10.84 W, marking a 19% improvement. However, PV cells and TEG exhibit opposite efficiency-temperature characteristics. Additionally, the system requires an additional cooling source to operate effectively. Therefore, the rational design of the coupling between PV cells and TEG, as well as the cooling structure, represents current research hotspots and challenges.

Existing cooling methods can be categorized into active cooling and passive cooling. Active cooling methods such as air cooling,17−19 water cooling,20,21 and nanofluid cooling22−25 are known for their high cooling power, effectively reducing the temperature of PV cells, and enhancing PV conversion efficiency. However, active cooling requires additional input power, and the limited improvement in PV efficiency does not necessarily offset the energy consumed. There is also controversy surrounding the overall energy gain.16,26 Although passive cooling methods such as heat pipes, heat sinks, and radiators do not consume additional energy, they are characterized by complex structures, large space requirements, and cooling temperatures limited by ambient conditions.1,27−31 Passive radiative cooling utilizes the atmospheric window (8–13 μm) to radiate heat to the cold outer space (∼3 K),1,32−34 achieving cooling below ambient temperature without consuming any energy.1 It is characterized by its simple structure, low cost, and finds wide applications in human thermal management, building cooling, and outdoor equipment cooling.1,35−40 Integrating radiative cooling with PV cells can passively lower the temperature of PV cells, thereby alleviating to some extent the decrease in electricity generation efficiency caused by PV cell heating.41−43 However, both radiative cooling (RC) and PV systems require sky-facing orientations to function effectively. Directly stacking RC and PV layers poses challenges in space utilization, and a significant amount of energy not beneficial to PV cells remains untapped, thereby hindering solar energy utilization efficiency. Therefore, addressing the spatial conflict between RC and PV and utilizing the cooling effect generated by RC to lower PV cell temperatures while enhancing spectral utilization of solar energy are current research focal points and challenges.

Based on the combined characteristics of RC, PV , and TEG, integrating RC with PV–TEG to absorb waste heat from PV cells for electricity generation offers unique advantages in enhancing solar energy conversion efficiency and overall energy yield. Guo et al.44 utilized spectral splitting theory to combine RC with PV–TE technology.45 Simulation results demonstrate that integrating RC with PV–TE systems in single-junction cell configurations effectively enhances solar PV conversion efficiency, approaching theoretical limits. Under 30 suns of illumination, the total efficiency can reach 39.4%. Lv et al.46 proposed a strategy to enhance sky radiative cooling using a solar photovoltaic-thermoelectric cooler. The results indicate that when the ratio of PV to radiative cooling area is 1, the maximum cooling energy gain achieved by the system within a day is 285.57 MJ/m2. Lv et al.47 developed a three-dimensional transient simulation model for PV–TE–RC systems, accurately predicting their operational limits. This model facilitates high-quality synergistic utilization of solar and spatial energy resources. The research on enhancing spectral utilization of PV cells through the integration of PV with radiative cooling and thermoelectric power generation has demonstrated the potential for efficiency improvement. However, the direct stacking approach faces challenges related to spatial utilization conflicts, hindering further enhancement of energy utilization efficiency. Moreover, studies on the integrated photovoltaic-radiative cooling-thermoelectric generation systems have largely remained at the simulation and modeling stages, lacking experimental validation of the practicality and performance of such hybrid power generation systems.

In this study, we propose an integrated full-spectrum solar energy cascade utilization system that combines spectral splitting with passive radiative cooling. This novel system utilizes spectral splitting technology to direct photon energy from both inside and outside the bandgap of PV cells to PV cells and TEG. Simultaneously, passive radiative cooling technology dissipates excess heat from the TEG to outer space, increasing the temperature difference across the TEG and expanding the solar spectrum utilization to the infrared band of the atmospheric window. This enables efficient and high-quality utilization of all segments of the solar spectrum. The internal energy transfer within the novel radiative cooling-thermoelectric-photovoltaic (RC–TE–PV) hybrid system is analyzed based on thermodynamic laws, and a theoretical mathematical model is developed. A three-dimensional model of the RC–TE–PV hybrid system is constructed using finite element method, establishing boundary conditions among system components and simulating complex internal conditions of the RC–TE–PV model. Combining thermal-electric and heat transfer modules in simulation software, a three-dimensional steady-state simulation model of the RC–TE–PV hybrid system is developed. Additionally, to evaluate the performance of the RC–TE–PV hybrid system, an outdoor experimental platform was constructed. A PV control group was established to conduct comparative experiments aimed at assessing the spectral splitting effect. This approach validated the accuracy of the simulation models. Using the validated simulation models, this study systematically analyzed the influence of environmental factors—including solar irradiance, wind speed, ambient temperature, and relative humidity—on the electrical and thermal performance of the system. By addressing a critical gap in experimental research on integrated systems that combine radiative cooling, PV, and thermoelectric power generation, this work provides valuable insights. Additionally, it offers a promising framework for advancing the efficient utilization of full-spectrum solar energy and radiative cooling in tandem. Furthermore, it presents a promising path for expanding the high-quality utilization of full-spectrum solar energy and radiative cooling in combination.

Methodology

System Description

Figure 1 shows the structure of the RC–TE–PV spectral splitting cascade utilization system. The device mainly consists of RC, TE, spectral separator, PV cell, and reflective mirror from top to bottom. The hot side of TE is in contact with the spectral separator through heat-conductive silica gel. The cold side of TE is in contact with RC through heat-conductive silica gel. The PV cell is placed on the upper side of the spectral separator. The specific performance is that the sunlight shines on the bottom reflector. The reflector reflects the sunlight beam to the bottom side of the spectral separator, and the PV side: the spectral separator blocks and absorbs heat from a part of the beam (the spectral band that cannot be used by the PV cell and will cause heating of the PV cell). The other part of the beam (the effective spectral band that allows the PV cell to output power) is directed to the PV cell surface through the spectral separator. The PV cell absorbs solar photons to output power externally. On the RC–TE side: the module uses the radiative cooling principle and the Seebeck effect to generate power output. The heat absorbed by the spectral separator is transferred to the bottom of the TE (TE hot side) by heat conduction. The top of the TE (TE cold side) is dissipated by RC. This increases the temperature difference between the hot and cold sides, thus increasing the power output of the TE.

Experimental Setup

To comprehensively evaluate the thermal and electrical performance of the RC–TE and PV components within the RC–TE–PV system, experiments were conducted using self-assembled experimental equipment and a constructed experimental platform.

Experimental Setup: The experiment was conducted on the rooftop of the student dormitory building at Yujiatou Campus, Wuhan University of Technology, Wuhan City, Hubei Province, China (longitude: 114.35, latitude: 30.60). The specific experimental equipment and experimental sites are shown in Figures 3 and 4. The experimental setup mainly consisted of the RC–TE–PV system, data acquisition unit, PV power meter, irradiance meter, reflectors, and a laptop computer. The thermal performance of PV and RC–TE components was monitored and recorded using thermocouple devices connected to the data acquisition unit. Additionally, the current, voltage, and power of the PV system were monitored externally via connections to the power meter, with the collected data subsequently transferred to the laptop computer for recording. Natural convection was facilitated by the experimental environment, and there were no specific isolation materials on the RC surface. Thermal conductive silicone gel was used for bonding between the spectrum splitter and the TE hot side, as well as between the RC and TE cold side. The experiment lasted for 15 min, primarily considering negligible changes in the angle of sunlight exposure within this time frame. Temperature and voltage data from the system components were collected by the data acquisition unit at a sampling frequency of 60 s.

Experimental Verification

In specific experiments, the influence of environmental factors is more complicated, and the environment set in COMSOL Multiphysics 6.0 cannot match the real environment well. Therefore, we did an error analysis on the two sets of data. As shown in Figures 5 and 6, the error analysis was plotted by calculating the mean, variance, and standard deviation of the data at the TE hot side temperature. The maximum value of the standard deviation was 7.66% and the minimum was 0.03%. The rationality of the simulation model was verified by experiments.

Effect of a Single Environmental Factor on the Performance of RC–TE–PV

Based on the analysis of the simulation models and experimental results mentioned above, various environmental parameters significantly influence the overall electrical efficiency and thermal performance of the RC–TE–PV system. Therefore, to investigate the impacts of different environmental factors (solar irradiance, wind speed, ambient temperature, relative humidity) on the system’s electrical efficiency and thermal performance, this paper utilizes validated simulation models to analyze the system’s sensitivity to environmental conditions.

Effect of Solar Irradiation on the Performance of RC–TE–PV

The input energy of the whole system comes from solar radiation. Solar radiation is the power source that enables PV and TE to generate electricity. With an ambient temperature of 298.15 K, the solar irradiance is calculated from 500 to 1500 W/m2 using a theoretical model. The results are shown in Figure 12. The PV conversion efficiency decreases from 17.17% to 13.95% and the TE conversion efficiency decreases from 4.28% to 1.28%. The cooling power of RC–TE decreases by 10.09 MJ/m2. As the irradiation increases, the overall electrical performance of RC–TE–PV decreases, and the RC radiative cooling power also decreases. TE conversion efficiency showed a significant decrease at solar irradiation of 500–600 W/m2. This can be explained by the fact that the electrical conversion efficiency of the TEG is not only related to the temperature difference between the hot and cold ends of the TE, but also to the average temperature of the TE. An increase in the TE temperature greater than the temperature difference between the hot and cold ends of the TE will lead to a decrease in the electrical conversion efficiency of the TEG.

Effect of Wind Speed on the Performance of RC–TE–PV

According to Figure 13 The PV power conversion efficiency increases almost linearly. The PV conversion efficiency increases by 5.84%. As the wind speed increases, the convective heat transfer between PV, TE, and the environment increases. The operating temperature of PV, RC–TE panels decrease, which promotes the PV conversion of RC–TE–PV system. But for TE the conversion efficiency is decreased. The radiative cooling performance increases with the increase in wind speed. The specific explanation for the generation of this phenomenon is as follows: on the one hand, the spectral separator and the top RC are exposed to the environment, and there is heat loss at both the hot and cold sides of the TE as the wind speed increases. On the other hand, the thermal conductivity of the spectral separator has certain limitations. Heat is gathered at the bottom of the spectral separator and dissipated through heat conduction and thermal convection. Therefore, in the experiment, we should consider the spectral separator with better thermal conductivity, and also increase the protection of the heat at the bottom spectral separator.

Effect of Relative Humidity on the Performance of RC–TE–PV

Relative humidity is also an environmental factor that must be considered. The fixed irradiance is 1000 W/m2 ambient temperature is 297.15 K. The variation of RC–TE–PV electrical efficiency versus cooling power is analyzed using a simulated system with humidity variation from 0.1 to 0.8. The results are shown in Figure 15. As the humidity increases, both RC–TE cooling power and TE electrical efficiency decrease. The TE efficiency decreases by 14.38% relative down and the RC–TE cooling power decreases by 90.00 MJ/m2. This phenomenon is explained by the fact that on the one hand, water vapor in the atmosphere emits long-wave radiation to the atmosphere, and precipitation affects the spectral transmittance of the atmosphere. The cooling capacity of RC decreases, and the temperature difference of TE decreases, which leads to a significant decrease in RC–TE cooling power. Therefore, the cooling effect and electrical performance of the RC–TE–PV system in dry climates are better than that in humid climates.

Conclusion

This study proposes an integrated full-spectrum solar energy cascade utilization system that combines spectral splitting with passive cooling. The system utilizes spectral splitting technology to effectively utilize various spectral bands of solar energy at high quality. Additionally, it introduces passive radiative cooling technology to extend the spectrum utilization range. Compared to active cooling methods, this approach reduces external energy input, thereby enhancing the overall feasibility and energy utilization efficiency of the system.
