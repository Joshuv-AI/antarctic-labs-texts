Classification of real nodal orders
Igor Burban, Yuriy Drozd

Abstract
. In this paper, we study properties of nodal orders defined over arbitrary base fields. In particular we give a classification of complete real nodal orders.



2010 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 16E60, 16G30, 14A22, 16K20

1.
Introduction

Nodal orders are non-commutative generalizations of the

\mathbbm{k}
-algebra
\mathbbm{k}\llbracket x,y\rrbracket/(xy)
, where
\mathbbm{k}
is a field. This class of algebras was introduced by the second-named author in
[
7
]
. Assume that
\mathbbm{k}
is algebraically closed. A characteristic property of nodal orders is that they are representation-tame, whereas all other orders are representation-wild
[
7
]
. In our joint work
[
3
]
it was proven that nodal orders are even derived representation-tame.

This class of orders appear in various representation theoretic setting. For example, they arise in the description of tame blocks of the categories of Harish-Chandra modules for real reductive groups
[
2
,
18
]
.
Of major interest is a global version of nodal orders given by (tame) non-commutative projective nodal curves; see for instance
[
4
]
.

Nodal orders over an algebraically closed field
\mathbbm{k}
were classified in

[
20
]
. Properties of nodal orders over arbitrary fields
\mathbbm{k}
were elaborated one step further in
[
9
,
5
]
.

This work provides a full classification of nodal orders over the field of real numbers
\mathbb{R}
. Note that there are three non-isomorphic commutative real nodal orders:
\mathbb{C}\llbracket x,y\rrbracket/(xy)
,
\mathbb{R}\llbracket x,y\rrbracket/(xy)
and
\mathbb{R}\llbracket x,y\rrbracket/(x^{2}+y^{2})
(whereas
\mathbb{C}\llbracket x,y\rrbracket/(xy)
is the only commutative nodal order over
\mathbb{C}
). One reason for a higher complexity of the combinatorics of real nodal orders is non-vanishing of the Brauer group
\mathsf{Br}\bigl(\mathbb{R}(\!(t)\!)\bigr)
, which leads to a broader variety of possible rational hulls compared to the case of an algebraically closed field. Another reason is that in comparison to the case of
\mathbb{C}
, we have more types of irreducible semi-simple nodal pairs over
\mathbb{R}
. Nonetheless, it turns out that real nodal orders are parametrized by appropriate discrete data, although the corresponding combinatorial pattern turns out to be much more complicated compared to the case of algebraically closed fields.

The plan of this paper is the following.

In Section
2
we recall the definition of nodal orders and illustrate it by numerous “typical” examples. The main result of this section is Theorem
2.12
describing nodal orders as appropriate “gluings” of their hereditary covers and semi-simple quotients.

In Section
3
we recall the classification of Harada
[
15
,
16
]
of hereditary orders over a complete discrete valuation ring. The main results are Theorem
3.7
and Corollary
3.8
describing the automorphism group of a complete hereditary order.

Complete real hereditary orders are classified in Section
4
. The main result here is a proof of the fact that there are precisely four non-isomorphic complete maximal scalar local real orders:
\mathbb{R}\llbracket t\rrbracket
,
\mathbb{C}\llbracket t\rrbracket
,
\mathbb{H}\llbracket t\rrbracket
and
\mathbb{C}\llbracket t\rrbracket^{\mathsf{tw}}
, where

(1)

\mathbb{C}\llbracket z\rrbracket^{\mathsf{tw}}=\Bigl\{\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}z^{k}\,\Big|\,\lambda_{k}\in\mathbb{C}\;\mbox{\rm for all}\;k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\Bigr\}\;\mbox{\rm and}\;z\lambda=\bar{\lambda}z\;\mbox{\rm for all}\;\lambda\in\mathbb{C}.

Finite-dimensional semi-simple nodal pairs over an arbitrary field
\mathbbm{k}
are studied in Section
5
. The corresponding classification is provided by Theorem
5.7
, which is of the key statements of this work.

Finally, real nodal orders are studied in Section
6
. The corresponding classification is given by Theorem
6.3
and Theorem
6.5
, which are the main results of this article.

2.
Basic features of nodal orders

Let
A
be a ring,
A^{\ast}
be its group of units and

(2)

J=\mathsf{rad}(A)=\left.\left\{a\in A\,\right|\,1+bac\in A^{\ast}\;\mbox{\rm for all}\;b,c\in A\right\},

be its Jacobson radical.
Recall that
A
is
semi-local
if
\bar{A}:=A/J
is artinian (note that
\bar{A}
is automatically semi-simple). If
\bar{A}
is simple then
A
is called
local
. Moreover,
A
is
scalar local
if
\bar{A}
is a skew field. If,
A/J
is a direct product
of skew fields, we call
A

basic
.
Also, if
M
is a left
A
-module, we put

\bar{M}:=M/JM
and denote by
\ell_{A}(M)
its length. For a pair of semi-local rings
A\subseteq H
we denote

\ell^{*}(A,H)=\sup\Bigl\{\ell_{A}(H\otimes_{A}U)\,\big|\,U\text{ is a simple left $A$--module}\Bigr\}.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
A\subseteq H
be a pair of semi-local rings.

(i)

This pair is called Backström if
\rad(A)=\rad(H)
.

(ii)

It is called
nodal
if it is Backström and
\ell^{*}(A,H)\leq 2
.

The following fact is obvious.

Proposition 2.2
.

If a pair
A\subseteq H
is nodal, so is the pair
\bar{A}\subseteq\bar{H}
. Conversely, if
A\subseteq H
is Backström and the pair
\bar{A}\subseteq\bar{H}
is nodal,
then the pair
A\subseteq H
is nodal too.

Let
R
be an excellent reduced equidimensional ring of Krull dimension one and

K:=\mathsf{Quot}(R)
be the corresponding total ring of fractions.

Definition 2.3
.

An
R
-algebra
A
is an
order
over
R
if the following conditions are satisfied:

(i)

A
is a finitely generated torsion free
R
-module.

(ii)

A_{K}:=K\otimes_{R}A
is a semi-simple
K
-algebra.

Note the following easy but useful fact; see for instance
[
4
, Lemma 2.8]
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
R
be as above,
R^{\prime}\subseteq R
be a finite ring extension and
A
be an
R
-algebra. Then
A
is an order over
R
if and only it is an order over
R^{\prime}
.
Moreover, if
K^{\prime}:=\mathsf{Quot}(R^{\prime})
then we have:
A_{K}\cong A_{K^{\prime}}
.

Definition 2.5
.

A ring
A
is an
order
if
its center
R=Z(A)
is a reduced excellent equidimensional ring of Krull dimension one, and
A
is an order over
R
. If

K:=\mathsf{Quot}(R)
then
Q(A):=K\otimes_{R}A
is called the
rational envelope
of
A
.

Definition 2.6
.

An order
A
is called
nodal
if its center is semi-local and there exists a
hereditary
overorder
H\supseteq A
such that
(A,H)
is a nodal pair.

Remark 2.7
.

It is clear that an order
A
is nodal if and only if its radical completion
\widehat{A}
is nodal. Moreover, given a nodal order
A
, a hereditary overorder
H
from Definition
2.6
is

uniquely determined
and admits the following intrinsic description:

(3)

H=\bigl\{a\in Q(A)\,\big|\,aJ\subseteq J\bigr\}\cong\End_{A}(J),

where
J
is viewed as a right
A
-module (this is essentially a consequence of
[
19
, Theorem 39.11 and Corollary 39.12]
). For a nodal order
A
, the order

H
will be called the
hereditary cover
of
A
. Hereditary orders form a special subclass of nodal orders.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
\mathbbm{k}
be a field. A
\mathbbm{k}
-order is a
\mathbbm{k}
-algebra
A
with a semi-local center
R
such that
R/\rad(R)
is finite-dimensional over
\mathbbm{k}
(note that
A
is semi-local, too). We say that such
A
is
complete
if
R
is a complete
\mathbbm{k}
-algebra. Two
\mathbbm{k}
-orders are called isomorphic if they are isomorphic as
\mathbbm{k}
-algebras.

Remark 2.9
.

Let
A
be a complete
\mathbbm{k}
-order.
Using Noether normalisation, we can view
A
as an order over the
\mathbbm{k}
-algebra of formal power series
\mathbbm{k}\llbracket t\rrbracket
(appropriately embedded into the center of
A
). From now on,
\mathbbm{k}
-orders are assumed to be complete.

Example 2.10
.

The following
\mathbbm{k}
-algebras are nodal orders.

(i)

Let
A=\mathbbm{k}\llbracket x,y\rrbracket/(xy)
.
Then in the above notation we have:
Q(A)\cong\mathbbm{k}(\!(x)\!)\times\mathbbm{k}(\!(y)\!)
and
H\cong\mathbbm{k}\llbracket x\rrbracket\times\mathbbm{k}\llbracket y\rrbracket
. The corresponding semi-simple nodal pair
(\bar{A},\bar{H}\bigr)
is given by the diagonal embedding
\mathbbm{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathsf{diag}}}{{\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow}}\mathbbm{k}\times\mathbbm{k}
.

(ii)

Let
R=\mathbbm{k}\llbracket t\rrbracket
,
I=(t)
and

M_{2}(R)\supset A=\left.\left\{\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}a_{11}&a_{12}\\
a_{21}&a_{22}\end{array}\right)\;\right|\;a_{11}(0)={a}_{22}(0)\;\mbox{\rm and}\;a_{12}(0)=0\right\}\cong\mathbbm{k}\langle\!\langle x,y\rangle\!\rangle/(x^{2},y^{2}).

Here,
\mathbbm{k}\langle\!\langle x,y\rangle\!\rangle
is the algebra of non-commutative formal power series in
x
and
y
and we identify
x
with

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&t\\
0&0\end{array}\right)
and
y
with
\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&0\\
1&0\end{array}\right)
.
Then we have:

Q(A)=M_{2}\bigl(\mathbbm{k}(\!(t)\!)\bigr)
and

H=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}R&I\\
R&R\end{array}\right)
. The induced nodal pair
(\bar{A},\bar{H}\bigr)
is given again by the diagonal embedding
\mathbbm{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathsf{diag}}}{{\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow}}\mathbbm{k}\times\mathbbm{k}
.

(iii)

Now, let
A=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}R&I\\
I&R\end{array}\right)
.
Then
Q(A)=M_{2}\bigl(\mathbbm{k}(\!(t)\!)\bigr)
and
H=M_{2}(R)
. This time, the semi-simple nodal pair
(\bar{A},\bar{H}\bigr)
is given by
by the canonical embedding
\mathbbm{k}\times\mathbbm{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathsf{can}}}{{\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow}}M_{2}(\mathbbm{k})
.

(iv)

Consider now the order
A=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}R&I&I\\
R&R&I\\
R&I&R\end{array}\right)
. Then
Q(A)=M_{3}\bigl(\mathbbm{k}(\!(t)\!)\bigr)
, whereas
H=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}R&I&I\\
R&R&R\\
R&R&R\end{array}\right)
. The induced nodal pair
(\bar{A},\bar{H}\bigr)
is given by the embedding

\mathbbm{k}\times\bigl(\mathbbm{k}\times\mathbbm{k}\bigr)\xrightarrow{\mathsf{id}\times\mathsf{can}}\mathbbm{k}\times M_{2}(\mathbbm{k}).

In this case,
A
is isomorphic to the arrow completion of the path algebra of the so-called Gelfand quiver

For
\mathbbm{k}=\mathbb{C}
, the category of finite-dimensional left
A
-modules is equivalent to the principal block of the category of Harish-Chandra modules for the Lie group

\mathsf{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
; see

\mathbbm{k}
is algebraically closed, nodal
\mathbbm{k}
-orders were classified in

.
The next example illustrates some phenomena arising in the classification of real nodal orders, which are absent in the complex case.

Example 2.11
.

Let
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}(\!(t)\!)
,

\mathbb{L}=\mathbb{C}(\!(t)\!)
,

\mathbb{O}=\mathbb{R}\llbracket t\rrbracket
and
\mathbbm{P}=\mathbb{C}\llbracket t\rrbracket
. The following
\mathbb{R}
-algebras are nodal orders.

(i)

Let
A=\mathbb{R}\llbracket x,y\rrbracket/(x^{2}+y^{2})
.
Then we have:
Q(A)\cong\mathbb{L}
and
H\cong\mathbbm{P}
. The embedding
A\lhook\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow H
is given by the assignment
x\mapstochar\rightarrow t,y\mapstochar\rightarrow it
. The corresponding semi-simple nodal pair
(\bar{A},\bar{H}\bigr)
is given by the embedding
\mathbb{R}\lhook\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
.

(ii)

Consider
H=\mathbb{C}\llbracket x\rrbracket^{\mathsf{tw}}\times\mathbb{C}\llbracket y\rrbracket\supset A=\left\{(f,g)\;\big|\;f(0)=g(0)\right\}
; see (
1
) for the definition of twisted power series. The corresponding semi-simple nodal pair
(\bar{A},\bar{H}\bigr)
is given by the diagonal embedding
\mathbb{C}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathsf{diag}}}{{\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow}}\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}
.

(iii)

Let

M_{2}(\mathbb{O})\supset A=\left.\left\{\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}a_{11}&a_{12}\\
a_{21}&a_{22}\end{array}\right)\;\right|\;a_{11}(0)=a_{22}(0)\;\mbox{\rm and}\;a_{12}(0)=-a_{21}(0)\right\}.

Then we have:
Q(A)=M_{2}(\mathbb{K})
and
H=M_{2}(\mathbb{O})
. The nodal pair
(\bar{A},\bar{H}\bigr)
is given by the embedding

\mathbb{C}\cong\left.\left\{\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}a&b\\
-b&a\end{array}\right)\right|a,b\in\mathbb{R}\right\}\subset M_{2}(\mathbb{R}).

(iv)

Consider

M_{2}(\mathbbm{P})\supset A=\left.\left\{\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}a_{11}&a_{12}\\
a_{21}&a_{22}\end{array}\right)\;\right|\;a_{11}(0)=\bar{a}_{22}(0)\;\mbox{\rm and}\;a_{12}(0)=0\right\}.

Then
Q(A)=M_{2}(\mathbb{L})
and
H=\left.\left\{\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}a_{11}&a_{12}\\
a_{21}&a_{22}\end{array}\right)\;\right|\;a_{12}(0)=0\right\}\subset M_{2}(\mathbbm{P})
. The nodal pair
(\bar{A},\bar{H}\bigr)
is given by the embedding

\mathbb{C}\xrightarrow{\mathsf{diag}^{\ast}}\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C},\;a\mapstochar\rightarrow(a,\bar{a}).
In this case we have:

A\cong\mathbb{R}\langle\!\langle x,y,i\rangle\!\rangle/(x^{2},y^{2},i^{2}+1,xi+ix,yi+iy).

(v)

In the same vein, let

M_{2}(\mathbbm{P})\supset A=\left.\left\{\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}a_{11}&a_{12}\\
a_{21}&a_{22}\end{array}\right)\;\right|\;a_{11}(0)=\bar{a}_{22}(0)\;\mbox{\rm and}\;a_{12}(0)=-\bar{a}_{21}(0)\right\}.

Then
Q(A)=M_{2}(\mathbb{L})
and
H=M_{2}(\mathbbm{P})
. The nodal pair
(\bar{A},\bar{H}\bigr)
is given by the embedding

\mathbb{H}\cong\left.\left\{\left(\begin{array}[]{rc}a&b\\
-\bar{b}&\bar{a}\end{array}\right)\right|a,b\in\mathbb{C}\right\}\subset M_{2}(\mathbb{C}),

where
\mathbb{H}
is the skew field of Hamilton quaternions.

Theorem 2.12
.

Let
\mathbbm{k}
be any field and
A
be a nodal
\mathbbm{k}
-order. Let
J
be the Jacobson radical of
A
,
H
be the hereditary cover of

3.
Generalities on hereditary orders and
their automorphisms

Let
R
be a complete discrete valuation ring and
K=Q(R)
be its quotient field. Next, let
F
be a finite-dimensional skew field over
K
and
O
be the maximal order in
F
. In fact,
O
is the integral closure of
R
in
F
(see
[
19
, Theorem 12.8]
), so
O
is actually unique. Moreover, if
L
is the center of
F
,
O
contains the integral closure of
R
in
L
.
Hence, we can suppose that
F
is central over
K
. Finally, let
I
be the Jacobson radical of
O
. We choose
w\in I

such that
I=wO=Ow
.

Theorem 3.1
.

For any
r\in\mathbb{N}
and

\vec{p}=\bigl(p_{1},\dots,p_{r}\bigr)\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
, consider the following order

(9)

H(O,\vec{p}):=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc|ccc|c|ccc}O&\dots&O&I&\dots&I&\dots&I&\dots&I\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots&&\vdots&&\vdots\\
O&\dots&O&I&\dots&I&\dots&I&\dots&I\\
\hline\cr O&\dots&O&O&\dots&O&\dots&I&\dots&I\\
\vdots&&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&&\vdots&&\vdots\\
O&\dots&O&O&\dots&O&\dots&I&\dots&I\\
\hline\cr\vdots&&\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
\hline\cr O&\dots&O&O&\dots&O&\dots&O&\dots&O\\
\vdots&&\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots&&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
O&\dots&O&O&\dots&O&\dots&O&\dots&O\\
\end{array}\right)\subset M_{p}(F)

over
R
,
where the size
of the
i
-th diagonal block is
(p_{i}\times p_{i})
for each
1\leq i\leq r
and
p:=|\vec{p}|=p_{1}+\dots+p_{r}
. Then the following statements are true.

(a)

The order
H(O,\vec{p})
is hereditary. Its center is
R
and its rational hull is
M_{p}(F)
.

(b)

Conversely, let
H
be a hereditary order over
R
in the simple
K
-algebra
M_{p}(F)
. Then there exist
r\in\mathbb{N}
and
\vec{p}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
such that
|\vec{p}|=p
and
H\cong H(O,\vec{p})
. More precisely, there exists
x\in\GL_{p}(F)
such that

H=\mathsf{Ad}_{x}\bigl(H(O,\vec{p})\bigr)=x\cdot H(O,\vec{p})\cdot x^{-1}
.

(c)

Two orders
H(O,\vec{p})
and
H(O,\vec{p}^{\prime})
are isomorphic if and only if
\vec{p}^{\prime}
can be transformed into
\vec{p}
by a cyclic permutation.

Proofs
of these results are due to Harada
[
15
,
16
]
; see also

[
19
, Theorem 39.14 and Corollary 39.24]
for a textbook treatment.

Remark 3.2
.

It is easy to see that the following properties of
H=H(O,\vec{p})
are true.

(a)

Let
x=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}x_{11}&\dots&x_{1r}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
x_{r1}&\dots&x_{rr}\end{array}\right)\in H
, where
x_{ij}
is a matrix of size
(p_{i}\times p_{j})
for any
1\leq i,j\leq r
. Then
x\in H^{\ast}
if and only if
x_{ii}\in\mathsf{GL}_{p_{i}}(O)
for all
1\leq i\leq r
. Similarly,
x\in\rad(H)
if and only if

x_{ii}\in M_{p_{i}}(I)
for all
1\leq i\leq r
.

(b)

Let
\bar{O}=O/I
be the residue skew field of
O
. Then we have:

\bar{H}=H/\rad(H)\cong M_{p_{1}}(\bar{O})\times\dots\times M_{p_{r}}(\bar{O}).

Moreover, the canonical map
H^{\ast}\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow\bar{H}^{\ast}
is surjective.

(c)

Let
y=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}y_{11}&\dots&y_{1r}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
y_{r1}&\dots&y_{rr}\end{array}\right)\in H
and
\bar{y}=\left(\bar{y}_{11},\dots,\bar{y}_{rr}\right)
be its image in
\bar{H}
. Then for any

we have:

(10)

\overline{\mathsf{Ad}_{x}(y)}=\bigr({\mathsf{Ad}_{\bar{x}_{11}}(\bar{y}_{11})},\dots,{\mathsf{Ad}_{\bar{x}_{rr}}(\bar{y}_{rr})}\bigr)

and any inner automorphism of
\bar{H}
is induced by an inner automorphism of
H
.

(d)

For any automorphism
\psi\in\Aut(O)
, let
\bar{\psi}\in\Aut(\bar{O})
be the induced automorphism. Since
\psi(I)=I
, we obtain (abusing the notation) induced automorphisms
\psi\in\Aut(H)
and
\bar{\psi}\in\Aut(\bar{H})
. Concretely, for any
x\in H
we have:

(11)

\overline{\psi(x)}=\bigl(\bar{\psi}(\bar{x}_{11}),\dots,\bar{\psi}(\bar{x}_{rr})\bigr).

(e)

Let
1\leq t\leq r
be minimal with the property that

\vec{p}=\bigl(p_{1},\dots,p_{t};p_{1},\dots,p_{t};\dots;p_{1},\dots,p_{t}\bigr).

For

we put

(12)

\varrho=\varrho_{\vec{p}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc}0&0&\dots&0&w\mathbbm{1}_{q}\\
\mathbbm{1}_{q}&0&\dots&0&0\\
0&\mathbbm{1}_{q}&\dots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\dots&\mathbbm{1}_{q}&0\end{array}\right)\in M_{p}(F),

where all blocks of
\varrho
are square matrices of size
q
. Note that

p=lq
and
r=lt
for some

.
Moreover,
\varrho\in H\setminus H^{\ast}
and
\mathsf{Ad}_{\varrho}(H)=H
. Since

, we conclude that

\mathsf{Ad}_{\varrho}^{l}(x)=wxw^{-1}
for any
x\in H
.

Let
\kappa\in\Aut(\bar{O})
be the automorphism induced by
\mathsf{Ad}_{w}\in\Aut(O)
.
Then the automorphism

\overline{\mathsf{Ad}}_{\varrho}\in\Aut(\bar{H})
induced by
\mathsf{Ad}_{\varrho}
is given by a twisted cyclic shift

(z_{1},\dots,z_{t},,\dots,z_{r-t+1},\dots,z_{r})\mapstochar\rightarrow\bigl(\kappa(z_{r-t+1}),\dots,\kappa(z_{r}),z_{1},\dots,z_{t},\dots\bigr).

\mathbb{C}(\!(z)\!)^{\mathsf{tw}}=\Bigl\{\sum\limits_{k=k_{0}}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}z^{k}\;\big|\,k_{0}\in\mathbb{Z},\lambda_{k}\in\mathbb{C}\;\mbox{\rm for all}\;k\in\mathbb{Z}\Bigr\},\;\mbox{\rm where}\;z\lambda=\bar{\lambda}z\;\mbox{\rm for all}\;\lambda\in\mathbb{C}.

Here, the isomorphism

\mathbb{C}(\!(z)\!)^{\mathsf{tw}}\cong\Sigma_{t}
identifies

with

.

For any

, let us now explicitly describe the corresponding maximal orders

(recall again that
O_{v}
is uniquely determined
[
19
, Theorem 12.8]
).
Let

be the Jacobson radical of
O_{v}
and
F_{v}=O_{v}/I_{v}
be the corresponding residue skew field.

Lemma 4.3
.

The following statements are true.

(a)

If
v=1
then
O_{1}=\mathbb{R}\llbracket t\rrbracket
and
F_{1}\cong\mathbb{R}
.

(b)

If
v=-1
then
O_{-1}=\mathbb{H}\llbracket t\rrbracket
and
F_{-1}\cong\mathbb{H}
.

(c)

If
v=\pm t
then
O_{\pm t}=\left\langle i,j\,\big|\,i^{2}=-1,j^{2}=\pm t,ij=-ji\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}\llbracket t\rrbracket}
and

F_{\pm t}\cong\mathbb{C}
.

Proof.

Note that for any
v\in\bigl\{1,-1,{t},-{t}\bigr\}
the algebra
O_{v}
defined in the statement of lemma is an order in the corresponding skew field
\Sigma_{v}
. Their maximality easily follows from the Auslander–Goldman criterion; see
[
19
, Theorem 18.4]
. For example, let
v=\pm t
. Then
I_{v}=O_{v}j=jO_{v}
is the Jacobson radical of
O_{v}
. It follows that
I_{v}
is projective as a left
O_{v}
-module. Moreover,
F_{v}=O_{v}/I_{v}\cong\mathbb{C}
is a field, what implies the statement.
∎

Remark 4.4
.

Note that from the point of view of classification of hereditary
\mathbb{R}
-orders, we may identify
O_{t}
and
O_{-t}
.
We have an isomorphism of
\mathbb{R}
-algebras
O_{\pm t}\cong\mathbb{C}\llbracket z\rrbracket^{\mathsf{tw}}
; see (
1
) for the definition
of
\mathbb{C}\llbracket z\rrbracket^{\mathsf{tw}}
.

Summary 4.5
.

We recapitulate some key facts about real hereditary orders.

(i)

By a classical Theorem of Frobenius,
\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbb{C}
and
\mathbb{H}
are the only finite-dimensional division algebras over
\mathbb{R}
.

(ii)

The only finite field extension of the field
\mathbb{R}(\!(t)\!)
up to
\mathbb{R}
-linear isomorphism are
\mathbb{R}(\!(w)\!)
or
\mathbb{C}(\!(w)\!)
; see Lemma
3.10
.

(iii)

It follows from the discussion made in this section that up to
\mathbb{R}
-linear isomorphisms, the only maximal scalar local real orders are
\mathbb{R}\llbracket t\rrbracket
,
\mathbb{C}\llbracket t\rrbracket
,
\mathbb{C}\llbracket t\rrbracket^{\mathsf{tw}}
and
\mathbb{H}\llbracket t\rrbracket
. Obviously, the residue skew field of
\mathbb{R}\llbracket t\rrbracket
is
\mathbb{R}
, for
\mathbb{C}\llbracket t\rrbracket
and
\mathbb{C}\llbracket t\rrbracket^{\mathsf{tw}}
it is
\mathbb{C}
and for
\mathbb{H}\llbracket t\rrbracket
it is
\mathbb{H}
.

(iv)

The orders
\mathbb{R}\llbracket t\rrbracket
and
\mathbb{C}\llbracket t\rrbracket
are commutative. The center of
\mathbb{C}\llbracket t\rrbracket^{\mathsf{tw}}
is
\mathbb{R}\llbracket t^{2}\rrbracket
, whereas the center of
\mathbb{H}\llbracket t\rrbracket
is
\mathbb{R}\llbracket t\rrbracket
. In all these four cases, the element
t
generates the Jacobson radical of the corresponding maximal order.

(v)

It is easy to see that the field automorphism of
\mathbb{C}
induced by

\mathbb{C}\llbracket t\rrbracket^{\mathsf{tw}}\xrightarrow{\mathsf{Ad}_{t}}\mathbb{C}\llbracket t\rrbracket^{\mathsf{tw}}
is given by the complex conjugation.

(vi)

An explicit classification of hereditary
\mathbb{R}
-orders is now provided by Theorem
3.1
and Theorem
3.11
of Harada. A description of the corresponding automorphism groups and their actions modulo the radical follows from Corollary
3.8
.

-algebras.

Theorem 6.5
.

The isomorphism classes of basic real nodal orders are parametrized by the equivalence types of extended tuples
\bigl(\Omega,\prec,\tau,\sim,\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\mathsf{wt}\bigr)
.

Proof.

Any real nodal order is isomorphic to
A\bigl(\Omega,\prec,\tau,\sim,\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\mathsf{wt}\bigr)
for an appropriate extended tuple. Moreover,

A\bigl(\Omega,\prec,\tau,\sim,\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\mathsf{wt}\bigr)\cong A\bigl(\Omega^{\prime},\prec^{\prime},\tau^{\prime},\sim^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime},\mathsf{wt}^{\prime}\bigr)

if and only if the corresponding extended tuples are equivalent. Proofs of these statements are completely analogous to the ones of Theorem
6.3
and are therefore left to an interested reader as an exercise.
