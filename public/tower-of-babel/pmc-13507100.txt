A systematic comparison of machine learning models for missing value imputation in household electricity consumption data
Authors: Bavly Hanna, Guandong Xu, Xianzhi Wang, Jahangir Hossain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Missing values in electricity consumption datasets compromise the reliability of smart grid analytics, demand forecasting, and energy management systems. Although numerous imputation methods have been proposed, prior studies typically evaluate a limited subset of models using a narrow range of metrics, making it difficult to draw generalizable conclusions about relative performance across algorithmic paradigms. This paper addresses this gap through a systematic benchmarking study comparing 30 machine learning models spanning linear methods, tree-based ensembles, support vector machines, and deep neural networks for imputing missing values in household electricity consumption time series. We conducted a three-phase evaluation. In the first phase, individual model assessment revealed that tree-based ensemble methods achieved the strongest performance, with Extra Trees Regressor and Random Forest attaining Mean Absolute Error (MAE) values of 0.0474 kW and 0.0480 kW, respectively. In the second phase, ensemble combinations using Bagging, Boosting, and Stacking techniques demonstrated improved accuracy over single-model implementations. In the third phase, a three-stage cascaded hybrid framework employing error curve learning achieved a Mean Absolute Error of 0.0468 kW, Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) of 0.1477 kW, and Mean Absolute Percentage Error of 4.38%. A main finding is that tree-based ensemble methods performed comparably to or better than deep learning architectures, including Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks for this imputation task, suggesting that model complexity does not necessarily translate to improved performance in this domain. We also provide a comprehensive cross-model ranking that offers practitioners evidence-based guidance for selecting imputation methods. The multi-metric evaluation framework established here can serve as a reusable benchmark for future research in energy data quality improvement.

Introduction

Electricity underpins modern infrastructure, industry, and residential life, making reliable energy data essential for effective grid management and sustainable development1. The United Nations Sustainable Development Goal 7 emphasizes the importance of affordable, reliable, and modern energy access, with particular attention to energy efficiency and emissions reduction2. The proliferation of smart meters and Advanced Metering Infrastructure (AMI) has enabled unprecedented granularity in electricity consumption monitoring, generating vast datasets that support demand forecasting, load optimization, and grid stability management3. However, these benefits can only be realized when the underlying data is complete and accurate.

Missing values (MVs) represent a pervasive challenge in electricity consumption datasets. Data gaps arise from multiple sources, including equipment malfunctions, communication failures, inconsistent measurement intervals, and storage system errors4,5. Under normal operating conditions, missing value rates typically remain low, but system failures can produce substantial data loss, often manifesting as episodic bursts affecting consecutive time periods6. The consequences of incomplete data extend beyond mere inconvenience. Missing values can compromise demand forecast accuracy, impede the development of reliable consumption models, and hinder renewable energy integration into grid management systems7. For applications requiring continuous data streams, such as real-time monitoring and automated demand response, even small gaps can degrade system performance significantly.

Traditional approaches to missing value imputation, including mean substitution, linear interpolation, and nearest-neighbor methods, often prove inadequate for electricity consumption data characterized by complex temporal patterns and non-linear dynamics7. Simple statistical methods may introduce systematic bias, while linear interpolation fails to capture the inherently variable nature of household energy use, which responds to factors including appliance usage patterns, occupancy schedules, and weather conditions8,9. More sophisticated statistical techniques such as Expectation Maximization and Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average models have been applied to this problem, but these approaches often struggle with the volatile, multi-scale patterns characteristic of household-level consumption10,11.

Machine learning (ML) methods have emerged as promising alternatives for missing value imputation in energy datasets. These approaches can learn complex patterns from available data and exploit the autocorrelation inherent in electricity consumption time series, which typically exhibits daily, weekly, and seasonal periodicities12. Recent studies have explored various ML architectures, including Random Forests13,14, Support Vector Regression15, Denoising Autoencoders16,17, and deep learning models incorporating Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks and Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs)8,18. While individual studies have demonstrated the potential of specific methods, comprehensive comparisons across diverse model families using standardized evaluation protocols remain limited.

Despite growing interest in ML for energy data imputation, several gaps persist in the literature. First, many studies evaluate only a narrow selection of models, typically ranging from three to eight algorithms within a single paradigm14,19, making it difficult to assess relative performance across algorithmic families. Second, evaluation metrics vary substantially between studies, complicating cross-study comparisons and practical model selection decisions. Third, few studies systematically examine how ensemble and hybrid approaches compare to their constituent base models under identical experimental conditions. Fourth, the conditions under which simpler models may be preferable to complex deep learning architectures remain underexplored20.

This paper makes four principal contributions to the field of energy data imputation. First, it provides the most comprehensive benchmarking study to date for missing value imputation in household electricity consumption data, evaluating 30 models across 50 performance metrics in a standardized, reproducible experimental protocol. While previous comparison studies have typically examined a handful of methods using two to five metrics, our expanded scope enables more robust conclusions about relative performance across algorithmic families. Second, we propose and evaluate a three-stage cascaded hybrid framework that applies error-curve learning to sequentially refine imputation predictions, achieving improvements over both individual models and conventional ensemble methods. Third, our results demonstrate that well-configured tree-based ensemble methods achieve performance comparable to or exceeding that of deep learning architectures for household energy imputation, providing practically significant guidance for resource-constrained practitioners. Fourth, the multi-metric evaluation framework we employ, spanning scale-dependent, percentage-based, robust, relative, and correlation-based metric categories, establishes a reusable benchmark protocol for future comparative studies in this domain.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews relevant literature on imputation methods for electricity consumption data and related domains, identifies research gaps, and positions the present study. Section 3 describes the dataset, missingness characterization, feature engineering approach, model architectures, and evaluation framework. Section 4 presents comparative findings across individual models, ensemble methods, and the proposed hybrid framework, culminating in a unified cross-phase ranking. Section 5 interprets results in the context of prior work and practical applications, acknowledges limitations, and suggests future research directions. Section 6 summarizes the main findings and their implications.

Related work

The application of ML techniques for imputing missing electricity consumption data has received increasing attention in recent years. This section provides an overview of relevant approaches, categorized by methodological family, encompassing classical statistical methods, supervised regression models, deep learning architectures, hybrid and ensemble strategies.

Classical statistical imputation methods

Before the widespread adoption of ML, several statistical approaches formed the foundation of missing data treatment. Expectation Maximization (EM) algorithms iteratively estimate missing values by maximizing the likelihood function under assumed distributional models21. Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE), introduced by van Buuren and Groothuis-Oudshoorn22, generates multiple plausible values for each missing observation, preserving uncertainty in the imputation process. Matrix completion techniques, originally developed for recommender systems, have been adapted for time-series imputation by exploiting low-rank structure in the data matrix23. Spline-based interpolation methods, including cubic splines and piecewise polynomial fits, provide smooth estimates for missing observations in regularly sampled time series24.

While these statistical methods offer well-understood theoretical properties, they have notable limitations when applied to electricity consumption data. EM and MICE assume specific distributional forms that may not hold for highly skewed, multimodal consumption distributions. Spline methods perform well for short, isolated gaps but deteriorate for longer missing sequences where the smoothness assumption becomes unrealistic. Matrix completion requires that the data exhibit low-rank structure, an assumption that may not hold uniformly for household-level consumption affected by irregular occupant behavior. These limitations have motivated the exploration of more flexible ML approaches.

Supervised regression models

Tree-based ensemble methods have demonstrated effectiveness for electricity consumption imputation. Huang et al.13 developed a Random Forest model that matched similar consumption patterns to missing value intervals, outperforming traditional neighborhood and interpolation schemes. Kim et al.14 confirmed the effectiveness of Random Forests for energy consumption data imputation, showing improved performance compared to simple averaging methods. Support Vector Machines have also been explored for this task. Shi25 proposed an optimized Support Vector Machine approach using data preprocessing and refinement, achieving good accuracy on transformer monitoring data. Hanna et al.19 compared Random Forest, Linear Regression, Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), and Decision Trees for predicting missing values in regional electricity consumption data, finding that the MLP model achieved superior predictive performance.

Although these supervised regression studies demonstrate the viability of individual ML methods, they typically evaluate a few models, making it difficult to determine whether the reported advantages reflect genuine algorithmic superiority or dataset-specific tuning. The absence of standardized evaluation protocols across these studies further complicates the synthesis of findings.

Deep learning models

Deep neural networks (DNNs) have emerged as tools for learning complex nonlinear relationships in electricity consumption data, potentially exploiting subtle correlations between energy use, weather variables, and temporal factors26. Autoencoders have shown promise for addressing missing values. Gondara and Wang16 used Denoising Autoencoders to reconstruct input data, enabling missing data imputation based on other observed samples. Liguori27 enhanced the performance of a convolutional Denoising Autoencoder through data augmentation, reducing Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) and Mean Absolute Error (MAE) by up to 47.9% and 51.19%, respectively in the random missing scenarios, compared to approaches without augmentation.

Recurrent neural networks (RNNs), particularly LSTM networks, have proven suitable for sequence data, capturing long-term dependencies in electricity consumption patterns. Yuniarti et al.28 explored recurrent LSTM networks for missing value imputation. Mohamed et al.10 proposed an iterative ensemble technique combining LSTM networks using both forward and backward forecasting. Ma29 introduced an LSTM-BIT model combining bi-directional imputation, LSTM networks, and transfer learning, reducing error by 4.24% to 47.15% compared to k-nearest neighbors, Support Vector Machines, and traditional neural networks when imputing various types of missing sections.

Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) represent another approach for missing value imputation. Yoon et al.30 proposed using GANs for robust missing value synthesis, leveraging the competing generator and discriminator networks. Lian et al.31 introduced an electricity consumption data imputation method using dummy variables and deep GANs, achieving superior accuracy compared to zero, mean, and Random Forest imputation across different missing ratios. Transformer-based architectures have also emerged as tools for time series imputation. Lotfipoor et al.32 developed a transformer-based imputation framework for energy demand data, achieving percentage improvements ranging from approximately 49.71% to 57.52% compared to linear interpolation methods on the Cornwall dataset at a 30% MVs rate.

A synthesis of the deep learning literature reveals two important observations. First, no single model family has been shown to dominate across all datasets, missingness scenarios, and evaluation metrics. Second, most studies evaluate a small number of algorithms within a single paradigm, which prevents direct comparisons across model families. The absence of head-to-head comparisons under identical conditions limits the ability to draw generalizable conclusions about which approaches are most suitable for household electricity imputation.

Hybrid and ensemble approaches

A hybrid architecture that combines multiple techniques has shown promise for missing value imputation by leveraging the complementary strengths of different models. Kim and Cho33 evaluated a combination of CNNs with LSTM networks on household data, demonstrating effectiveness compared to standard LSTM networks alone. In this hybrid approach, the LSTM component captured longer-term dependencies while convolutional layers extracted local features and patterns. Shao et al.8 proposed a convolutional LSTM network that leverages CNN feature extraction capability and LSTM sequence modeling, providing accurate imputation results even for large missing intervals. Hussain34 developed a framework using a hybrid CNN-LSTM model to predict large missing value gaps in appliance electricity consumption time series, showing that the hybrid model exhibited higher overall predictive accuracy compared to individual models.

Ensemble methods represent another effective approach. Wang et al.9 combined Linear Regression with k-nearest neighbors in a weighted ensemble approach, improving accuracy over individual models. Jung7 developed a missing value imputation technique for smart meter data using a bagging ensemble of MLPs, where a softmax ensemble network determines model weights. Experiments on university building energy usage data showed that this approach achieved lower errors than interpolation, averaging, autoencoder, and GAN methods.

Although these hybrid and ensemble studies show consistent improvements over individual models, each study evaluates only its own specific combination. Systematic comparisons that evaluate individual models, ensemble combinations, and cascaded hybrid frameworks within a single experimental protocol are notably absent from the literature.

Other relevant approaches

Several alternative methods have also shown promise. Grigoras et al.35 explored clustering algorithms combined with nearest-neighbor techniques to find similar consumption patterns. Kim et al.36 proposed a Learning-based Adaptive Imputation method that captures patterns from past data using feature vectors and weighted k-nearest neighbors. Interpolation techniques have been adapted for missing electricity data. Manembu et al.6 applied Lagrange polynomial interpolation methods to estimate missing consumption data, finding that interpolation effectively filled gaps caused by connectivity issues. Attar et al.18 proposed a noisy interpolation technique using cubic interpolation with added Gaussian noise, providing lower errors than standalone interpolation methods and better reflecting the natural stochastic behavior of household loads.

Gao et al.20 conducted a comparative analysis of deep learning models for temporal data imputation in power systems, evaluating MLPs, CNNs, Generative Adversarial Networks, and Transformer architectures for power data restoration. Their experimental findings identified two principal limitations in current approaches, including insufficient capability for processing high-frequency transient data, resulting in reduced imputation accuracy during abrupt variations, and practical deployment constraints arising from the computational complexity and hardware costs associated with high-parameter algorithms.

Identified research gaps

The literature reviewed above reveals several persistent gaps. First, most studies evaluate a small number of models, typically within a single algorithmic family, making cross-paradigm comparisons difficult. Second, evaluation protocols vary widely across studies in terms of metrics, train-test splits, and preprocessing steps, hindering reproducibility and comparability. Third, although ensemble and hybrid approaches have shown promise individually, systematic comparisons that evaluate individual models, ensemble combinations, and cascaded hybrid frameworks within a single experimental protocol are largely absent. Fourth, while deep learning methods have received growing attention for energy data imputation, the conditions under which they outperform simpler tree-based alternatives remain insufficiently characterized, as highlighted by the deployment limitations identified by Gao et al.20. This study addresses these gaps by providing a unified, multi-phase comparison of 30 models evaluated under identical conditions across a comprehensive suite of evaluation metrics.

Methods

Dataset

This paper used the Individual Household Electric Power Consumption dataset, a publicly available benchmark dataset obtained from the UCI Machine Learning Repository37. The dataset comprises 2,075,259 measurements of household electric power consumption collected at one-minute intervals from December 2006 to November 2010, spanning approximately four years. The data was collected from a single household located in France.

The dataset includes four primary attributes relevant to this study. First, aggregate active power, measured in kilowatts, represents the household’s global minute-averaged active power consumption. Second, energy sub-metering 1, measured in watt-hours, corresponds to kitchen appliances, including dishwasher, oven, and microwave. Third, energy sub-metering 2, measured in watt-hours, represents laundry room consumption, including washing machine, tumble dryer, refrigerator, and lighting. Fourth, energy sub-metering 3, measured in watt-hours, accounts for an electric water heater and air-conditioner.

Descriptive statistics revealed substantial variability in consumption patterns, as shown in Table 1. Aggregate active power showed a mean of 1.092 kW with a standard deviation of 1.057 kW. All sub-metering attributes exhibited positive skewness, particularly pronounced in sub-meterings 1 and 2, indicating frequent low consumption periods interspersed with occasional high usage spikes. Kurtosis values, especially high for sub-meterings 1 and 2 at 35.643 and 57.907, respectively, indicated distributions with heavy tails and more extreme values than a normal distribution.

Missing data structure

The original dataset contained missing values accounting for approximately 1.25% of total rows. Before constructing the imputation models, we analyzed the distribution and patterns of this missingness to understand its temporal structure and inform methodological decisions. For the purpose of this paper, initial preprocessing employed mean imputation, filling missing values with the average power consumption for corresponding minutes from other years in the dataset. This approach preserved temporal patterns while providing a complete baseline dataset against which imputation models could be evaluated.

All four target variables share an identical missing pattern: exactly 25,979 of 2,075,259 rows (1.25%) are missing, and missingness co-occurs simultaneously across all four variables in every affected row. There are zero instances of partial missingness (i.e., one variable missing while others are observed). This perfect co-occurrence indicates whole-system monitoring outages rather than individual sensor faults. Time-specific mean imputation

We applied time-specific mean imputation given the paper’s specific objectives. A critical distinction must be drawn between studies where imputation is the end goal versus studies where imputation serves as preprocessing to generate a complete baseline. In our paper, the purpose of the initial mean imputation is expressly the latter, as it produces a complete reference dataset against which the performance of candidate machine learning imputation models can be evaluated. The imputed values do not enter downstream analyses as ground truth; they serve as a controlled, reproducible baseline enabling fair model comparison. Under this framing, the primary requirement is that the baseline preserves the broad temporal structure of consumption patterns without introducing systematic bias, which time-specific mean imputation achieves by construction, since it replaces each missing minute with the historical average for that exact time slot.

Our method computes the imputed value for each missing minute as the mean of observations at the same minute-of-day from corresponding dates across other years in the dataset. This approach inherently encodes the diurnal consumption cycle (peak vs. off-peak hours), which is the substantial source of variance in Global Active Power and Sub-metering 3. For Sub-metering 1 and Sub-metering 2, which are highly intermittent and zero-inflated, the minute-level means effectively capture the average probability of appliance activation at each time of day. While we acknowledge that this produces smoothed fractional values rather than the discrete on/off patterns of the raw data, this smoothing is a known and deliberate trade-off: it provides an unbiased expectation at each time point for the mean-based evaluation metrics we use as our primary model-comparison criteria, which is precisely the property needed for a baseline reference in model evaluation.

The overall missing rate of 1.25% places this dataset well within the threshold where imputation method choice has minimal impact on downstream results. Established guidelines in the missing data literature suggest that below 5% missingness, even simple imputation approaches introduce negligible bias, and the choice of method matters primarily when missingness exceeds 10-20%. While we acknowledge the structural heterogeneity in gap lengths (with 94.9% of missing data concentrated in 6 long gaps), the total volume remains small in absolute terms, even the longest single gap (5.0 days) represents only 0.35% of the full dataset. At this scale, the differences between imputation strategies are unlikely to materially alter any aggregate model evaluation metric. We treat MAR as a working assumption. Under MAR, time-conditioned imputation methods, of which our minute-specific historical mean is a direct implementation, yield consistent (asymptotically unbiased) estimates.

Feature engineering

A comprehensive feature engineering approach was applied to capture the various factors influencing energy usage patterns. Features were organized into several categories. Temporal features included year, month, week, day, day of year, day of week, hour, and minute, capturing cyclical patterns at multiple timescales. Binary indicators for day/night, weekday/weekend, and public holidays were incorporated to account for behavioral differences between these periods.

Meteorological features included temperature, humidity, rain/snow occurrence, wind speed, and sunshine duration. These variables directly influence heating and cooling demands and have demonstrated strong correlations with energy consumption in previous studies. Economic and calendar features included Gross Domestic Product as a proxy for economic activity and a binary indicator for public holidays.

Lagged consumption features captured temporal dependencies at multiple horizons, including lags at 1 h, 24 h, 168 h (one week), and 720 h (approximately 30 days). Rolling window features were created to capture short-term historical trends and patterns, including 24-hour and 168-hour moving averages, standard deviations, minimum values, and maximum values. Differenced features captured changes from previous time periods at hourly, daily, and weekly intervals.

Feature selection employed a Genetic Algorithm approach to identify the most important predictors. Correlation analysis revealed that sub-metering 3 showed the strongest correlation with global active power at 0.64, while sub-meterings 1 and 2 showed moderate positive correlations of 0.48 and 0.43, respectively, as shown in Fig. 2. Temperature exhibited a weak negative correlation of −0.16 with global active power, consistent with findings that electricity consumption patterns vary with temperature changes.

Model architectures

Thirty ML models spanning multiple algorithmic paradigms were evaluated.

The breadth of this comparison is deliberate and constitutes one of the principal contributions of this study. Prior comparison studies in this domain have typically evaluated between three and eight models, which limits the ability to draw conclusions about performance differences across algorithmic families. By including 30 models, we enable a more comprehensive assessment. To maintain analytical focus, the Results section (Sect. 4) adopts a two-tier presentation, with detailed analysis of the top-performing focal models and a summary treatment of the remaining algorithms.

The models were organized into four families.

Linear models included Linear Regression (LR), Ridge Regression (Ridge), Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator Regression (LASSO), ElasticNet, Bayesian Ridge, Polynomial Regression (Poly), Tweedie Regressor Model (TRM), and Passive Aggressive Regressor Model (PARM). These models assume linear or polynomial relationships between features and the target variable. They offer high interpretability and computational efficiency but lack the capacity to capture complex nonlinear interactions in consumption data.

Tree-based models included Decision Trees (DT), Random Forest (RF), Gradient Boosting (GB), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Category Boosting (CatBoost), AdaBoost, Bagging, Extra Trees Regressor (ETR), Histogram-based Gradient Boosting Regressor (HistGBR), and LightGBM (LGBM). These ensemble methods are known for robust performance in handling non-linear relationships and feature interactions. These models build predictions through collections of decision trees using different ensemble strategies. Random Forest, Extra Trees Regressor, and Bagging use parallel bootstrap aggregation (bagging) to reduce variance. Gradient Boosting, XGBoost, CatBoost, LightGBM, and HistGBR use sequential boosting to reduce bias. AdaBoost also uses boosting, but with a simpler weighting scheme. Decision Tree serves as the non-ensemble baseline for the family.

Neural network architectures included Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), Deep Neural Network (DNN), Long Short-Term Memory network (LSTM), Convolutional Neural Network (CNN), and Recurrent Neural Network (RNN). These architectures capture temporal dependencies and non-linear relationships characteristic of time-series data. MLP and DNN are feedforward architectures that treat each input independently. LSTM and RNN are recurrent architectures designed for sequential data, with LSTM specifically addressing the vanishing gradient problem to capture long-range temporal dependencies. CNN applies convolutional filters to detect local patterns in the input sequence.

The remaining seven models represent diverse paradigms, including Support Vector Machines (SVM) and k-Nearest Neighbors (kNN), which were included for their effectiveness in capturing complex patterns. Additional models included Huber Regressor, RANSAC, Partial Least Squares (PLS), Orthogonal Matching Pursuit (OMP), and Principal Component Regression (PCR). SVM and KNN are non-parametric methods that make no assumptions about the functional form of the relationship. Huber and RANSAC are robust regression methods designed to be less sensitive to outliers. PLS, OMP, and PCR are dimensionality reduction or sparse approximation methods that project the data into lower-dimensional spaces before fitting.

Ensemble methods

Three ensemble techniques were employed to combine the top-performing individual models, selected based on MAE performance. Bagging (bootstrap aggregation) builds multiple models on random subsets of the data and averages their predictions, reducing variance and overfitting. Boosting creates strong learners by progressively training models to focus on errors made by previous ensemble members. Stacking trains a meta-model to optimally combine predictions from heterogeneous base models.

The top three individual models selected for ensemble construction were Extra Trees Regressor, Random Forest, and Bagging, based on their MAE scores. This selection criterion prioritizes models that consistently produce predictions close to actual values regardless of error direction.

Three-stage hybrid framework

A three-stage cascaded hybrid framework was developed employing error curve learning, an approach related to stacked generalization39. Each hybrid model incorporated Extra Trees Regressor, Random Forest, and Bagging algorithms. The framework operated in three sequential stages.

In the first stage, Model M1 was applied to generate initial predictions and an associated error curve, capturing systematic deviations between predictions and actual values. This step aligns with the concept of bias-variance decomposition in statistical learning theory40. In the second stage, Model M2 processed the output from M1 to predict a refined error curve. This intermediate step can be viewed as a form of meta-learning41, learning from the errors of the initial model. In the third stage, Model M3 incorporated the predicted error from M2 as an additional feature to produce the final forecast, an approach similar to residual learning techniques.

Experimental protocol

The dataset was split into training and test sets using an 80/20 ratio. Standard data cleaning, scaling, and encoding techniques were applied during preprocessing. Data normalization ensured consistent feature scales across all input variables. All models were trained using the prepared training data and evaluated on the held-out test set. Evaluation metrics

Model performance was assessed using a comprehensive suite of 50 evaluation metrics spanning multiple categories. To balance thoroughness with readability, we present results using a focused subset of eight core metrics in the main text, while the complete 50-metric evaluation is available in Appendix 1.

The eight-core metrics were selected to represent distinct aspects of imputation quality, following established recommendations for forecast evaluation42–44. Mean Absolute Error (MAE) was selected as the primary accuracy metric because it provides an intuitive, scale-dependent measure of average prediction error that is less sensitive to outliers than squared-error metrics. Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) was included because it penalizes larger errors more heavily, which is important for identifying models that produce occasional extreme deviations. Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) provides a scale-independent relative accuracy measure. R-squared captures the proportion of variance explained, offering a holistic fit assessment. Median Absolute Error (MdAE) provides a robust accuracy measure insensitive to outliers. Mean Error (ME) captures systematic directional bias, which is important for applications such as billing, where over- or under-estimation has asymmetric consequences. Mean Absolute Scaled Error (MASE) enables comparison relative to a naive benchmark. Pearson correlation coefficient (CORR) assesses trend-capturing ability.

An inter-metric analysis confirmed that many of the 50 metrics produce highly correlated rankings, supporting the use of a reduced core set without loss of essential information. The complete set of metrics, including formulas, interpretations, and applicability notes, is described in Appendix 1.

Cross-phase performance summary

Across all evaluation phases, the three-stage hybrid framework achieved the lowest MAE (0.0468 kW) and MAPE (4.38%), making it the top-ranked approach for absolute accuracy measures. For squared error metrics, CatBoost and the Stacking ensemble achieved the lowest RMSE values (0.1429 and 0.1428, respectively), both marginally outperforming the hybrid framework. Among individual models, Extra Trees Regressor achieved the second-lowest overall MAE and the second-best average rank across all metrics.

These results reveal that the marginal improvement from the hybrid framework over the best individual model (Extra Trees Regressor) was approximately 1.3% in MAE. The improvement from ensembles over the best individual model ranged from 0.4% to 3.5% depending on the metric. While these gains are modest in absolute terms, they are consistent across metrics and may be meaningful for applications where cumulative imputation errors affect downstream analyses such as demand forecasting or load profiling.

A remarkable observation is that LSTM, the best-performing neural network, falls behind all top tree-based models and all ensemble variants. This finding has practical significance, as tree-based models require substantially less computational overhead and hyperparameter tuning than LSTM networks while delivering equal or superior imputation accuracy for this dataset.

For practitioners seeking computational efficiency with near-optimal accuracy, Extra Trees Regressor and Random Forest provide strong recommendations. For applications where maximum accuracy justifies additional computational investment, the three-stage hybrid framework is recommended. For scenarios where the best balance between MAE and RMSE is desired, the Stacking ensemble or CatBoost offer appealing alternatives.

Discussion

Interpretation of results

The findings from this systematic comparison provide several insights relevant to missing value imputation in electricity consumption data. Tree-based ensemble methods, particularly Random Forest, Extra Trees Regressor, and CatBoost, achieved strong performance across multiple metrics. This aligns with previous findings by Huang et al.13, and Kim et al.14,, who reported Random Forest effectiveness for electricity consumption data imputation. The success of tree-based ensembles can be attributed to their ability to capture complex, non-linear relationships and feature interactions inherent in household electricity consumption patterns influenced by temporal, weather, and behavioral factors.

A notable finding is that deep learning models, while competitive, did not uniformly outperform well-tuned tree-based ensemble methods. LSTM networks achieved comparable but not superior performance to Random Forest and Extra Trees Regressor. This observation challenges assumptions about neural network superiority for time-series imputation tasks and suggests that the additional complexity of deep learning architectures may not translate to improved performance for all energy data imputation scenarios. This finding is consistent with recent observations by Moon et al.45,, who found tree-based ensemble methods effective for building load forecasting when properly configured, and with the broader discussion by Gao et al.20, regarding the practical deployment constraints and computational overhead associated with high-parameter deep learning models.

The ensemble methods combining top-performing base models demonstrated improved accuracy over individual models in most metrics. The Stacking ensemble achieved the best squared error metrics, while Bagging showed advantages in absolute error measures. This pattern suggests that different ensemble techniques may be preferable depending on the specific error characteristics of concern for a given application. The three-stage hybrid framework employing error curve learning achieved the lowest MAE and MAPE, indicating that the cascaded approach of learning from residual errors provides benefit for this task.

Comparison with prior studies

Our findings are broadly consistent with prior work while extending the scope of comparison. Jung7, reported that their bagging ensemble of MLPs achieved lower errors than interpolation, averaging, autoencoder, and GAN methods. Our results similarly show ensemble methods outperforming simpler approaches, though we found tree-based ensembles generally more effective than neural network ensembles for this dataset.

Shao et al.8, reported that CNN-LSTM hybrid models provided accurate imputation results for energy consumption data. While our LSTM and CNN models showed competitive performance, they did not substantially outperform simpler tree-based methods. This difference may reflect dataset characteristics, hyperparameter configurations, or the specific evaluation metrics employed. The comprehensive evaluation across 50 metrics in our paper provides a more multifaceted view of model performance than typically reported.

Practical implications

These findings have several practical implications for energy data quality improvement. For practitioners with limited computational resources or requiring interpretable models, tree-based ensemble methods such as Random Forest and Extra Trees Regressor offer an attractive balance of accuracy and efficiency. These models require less hyperparameter tuning than deep learning alternatives and provide feature importance measures that can inform understanding of consumption patterns.

For applications where maximum accuracy is paramount and computational resources are available, the three-stage hybrid framework or ensemble methods combining multiple top-performing models may provide incremental improvements. However, practitioners should weigh these gains against the additional complexity and computational overhead. The marginal improvements achieved by the hybrid framework may not justify its complexity for all applications.

The results also highlight the importance of comprehensive model evaluation. Different metrics led to different model rankings in our analysis, reflecting the diverse aspects of model performance captured by each metric. Practitioners should select evaluation metrics aligned with their specific application requirements. For billing applications where systematic bias is problematic, Mean Error may be most relevant. For demand forecasting where outlier performance matters, robust metrics like MdAE may be preferable.

The models developed in this paper could be integrated into the smart meter and sensor systems to clean and recover data in real time before transmission and storage46. This would improve the quality of gathered data. Utilities could also leverage these algorithms at data centers to fill gaps and fix errors in consumption data received from smart meters before using them for demand forecasting, clustering, and other analytics12. Imputation would allow making full use of historical datasets with MVs that utilities have already gathered. Useful insights could be extracted from the reconstructed data through mining techniques like motif discovery, Shapelet analysis, and temporal clustering47. Beyond electricity, similar techniques could be applied to impute MVs in other industrial time series data, such as natural gas, water, heating, or cooling system usage. Future research directions

Several directions merit future investigation. First, extending this comparative framework to additional datasets spanning different geographical regions, building types, and consumption patterns would establish the robustness and generalizability of findings. Second, a systematic investigation of performance under different missingness mechanisms and rates would provide guidance for practitioners facing specific data quality challenges.

Third, the development of adaptive model selection approaches that automatically choose appropriate imputation methods based on data characteristics could improve practical applicability. Fourth, integration of uncertainty quantification methods with imputation models would enable applications requiring confidence intervals or probabilistic predictions. Fifth, investigation of transfer learning approaches that leverage models trained on one dataset for imputation in related contexts could reduce the need for extensive retraining.

Sixth, a formal comparison of computational costs alongside accuracy metrics would strengthen the practical value of benchmarking studies. Seventh, exploring the integration of imputation models with edge computing devices for real-time data recovery at the meter level, as proposed by Gao et al.20,, represents a promising direction for operational deployment.

Conclusions

This paper provides a systematic and comprehensive comparison of ML approaches for missing value imputation in household electricity consumption data. Evaluation of 30 models spanning linear methods, tree-based ensembles, support vector machines, and deep neural networks across 50 metrics using a publicly available benchmark dataset yielded several main findings.

Tree-based ensemble methods, particularly Extra Trees Regressor and Random Forest, achieved strong performance with MAE values of 0.0474 kW and 0.0480 kW, respectively. These methods performed comparably to or better than deep learning architectures, including LSTM networks, suggesting that model complexity does not necessarily translate to improved performance for this imputation task. This finding has practical significance for resource-constrained practitioners, as tree-based methods offer faster training, simpler hyperparameter tuning, and built-in feature importance at competitive accuracy levels.

Ensemble techniques combining top-performing base models demonstrated improved accuracy, with Stacking achieving the best squared error metrics and Bagging showing advantages in absolute error measures. The three-stage cascaded hybrid framework employing error curve learning achieved the lowest MAE at 0.0468 kW and MAPE at 4.38%, demonstrating that sequential residual refinement provides a consistent, if modest, benefit.

These findings offer practical guidance for practitioners selecting imputation methods for energy consumption data. For most applications, Extra Trees Regressor or Random Forest offers the best balance of accuracy and simplicity. For scenarios demanding maximum accuracy, the three-stage hybrid framework or Stacking ensemble is recommended. The comprehensive evaluation framework presented here can serve as a benchmark for future research in energy data quality improvement.
