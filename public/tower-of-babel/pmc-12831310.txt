Engagement in advance care planning: effect of playing the card game Anticip’action in a community setting
Authors: Daniel Molnar, Virginie Metoukam Bauquis, Christine Clavien
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe card game Anticip’action was developed to foster Advance Care Planning (ACP) culture in medical and community settings. Available in a mobile (smartphone app) and paper formats, this conversation tool helps users to clarify and express their fears, hopes and life priorities, and to carry out actions in line with them.AimsThe goals of this study were to evaluate in a community setting, the effect of presenting Anticip’action on attitude towards ACP, self-perceived preparedness in case of a health crisis, and action readiness, as well as to collect users’ evaluation of the game, and their preference to use the game in mobile or paper formats.MethodsParticipants attended a 90-minute public event on the topic of advance directives, received information about ACP, filled questionnaire 1 (Q1), were introduced to the game, filled Q2, took the game home, and returned Q3 6–8 weeks later. Data collection included retention rates; pre-post responses on self-perceived preparedness in case of a health crisis, attitude towards ACP, and readiness to take related actions (analyzed using paired t-test on mean 1–5 Likert scores); proportions of anticipatory actions (planned or done) reported in Q3; average endorsement and perceived impact of the game (uMars, 1–5 Likert scores); proportions of reported intention and actual use of the game.ResultsOf the 124 attendees, 118 (mean age 75) completed Q1 & Q2, and 32 returned Q3. Mean baseline levels of attitude, self-perceived preparedness and readiness were uncommonly high (from 2.87 to 4.56). Discovering the game did not significantly impact these values. However, in Q3 (N = 32), 72% of respondents reported intending to take one or several specific anticipatory actions, and 22% had accomplished at least one since discovering the game. Anticip’action obtained high mean endorsement scores (Q2 = 3.67, Q3 = 3.9) and was evaluated as highly impactful for fostering anticipation (Q2 = 4.19, Q3 = 4.17). At home, participants generally preferred using the paper version.ConclusionsAnticip’action, especially in its paper format, is highly endorsed by a senior population with an existing interest in ACP and appears to effectively support the implementation of anticipatory actions. These findings need to be confirmed in larger and more diverse populations.RegistrationThe study was preregistered on the OSF platform (10.17605/OSF.IO/PN8HS) on 01.12.2023.

Introduction

Key priorities of medicine are to respect patients’ values and priorities, to cultivate the therapeutic relationship, and to provide goal-concordant care. The process of advance care planning (ACP) aims to achieve these goals [1], although its effectiveness remains a matter of debate [2–4]. ACP consists of identifying, in discussion with patients, what their priorities in life are, and what care they would (or would not) want to receive if they were no longer able to communicate their wishes. Despite its importance, ACP is often difficult to initiate and carry out, both within families and in the day-to-day reality of care. Many patients and health providers are reluctant to engage in conversations about end-of-life or serious health crises [5, 6]. In some cases, primary care providers may not see ACP as part of their role, leaving patients to confront these issues outside the medical sphere [7]. In general, well-conducted ACP is a multi-step procedure that can be difficult to carry out, notably due to organizational constraints within medical settings [8, 9].

Research suggests that gamification is a promising approach to address this sensitive topic and can help increasing engagement in ACP [10]. Serious discussion games, such as the Go Wish (a card game which focus on choosing one’s most important values and explaining why) or the Hello (which focuses on answering ACP questions and sharing one’s answers with others), have been developed for use in hospital and community settings [11–14], and studies report that these tools are well received by users and can encourage them to take meaningful steps towards ACP, such as discussing end-of-life issues with loved ones and health professionals, or writing advance directives [11, 15, 16]. However, few validated conversation games are available to the French-speaking community, except the Go Wish, a card game which is a valuable ACP tool when used with trained facilitators [12, 17].

In line with similar initiatives in other European countries [14, 18], to help initiate ACP conversations more flexibly and empower patients in their reflexive and decision-making process, an inter-professional research team at Geneva University and Geneva University hospitals (HUG) has created Anticip’action [19]. It is a card game inspired by the Go Wish, but with cards containing more explicit and guiding content, culturally adapted to the Swiss context, and with additional rules to prompt ACP decision-making without professional support. While playing, users of the game identify what matters most in their lives, express their values and priorities, and take concrete steps based on those reflections. Anticip’action is freely available in two formats: as a printed card deck [19], and as a digital version [20] within the Accordons-nous ACP mobile app which incorporates further information, discussion prompts, and an advance directive form [21, 22]. The rules of Anticip’action are described in Fig. 1. The game is designed for a wide range of settings. It can be used in clinical contexts with the support of health professionals who aim to define goals of care or limits to medical interventions corresponding to patients’ values. Since the backs of the cards contain useful supporting information and guiding action-motivation prompts, they can also be used independently at home, by patients or healthy individuals who wish to prepare for life adversity. The game covers topics related to healthcare and to further life-relevant matters.Fig. 1Description of the game Anticip’action In a complementary study conducted in a nephrology unit at the HUG, high acceptability and feasibility results were obtained with Anticip’action used in a 3-step structured ACP intervention involving nurses, physicians, patients and their caring relatives [23]. It remains to be seen whether the game can also be effectively used in a less structured community setting, without close supervision by healthcare professionals.

Description of the game Anticip’action

There is reason to believe that Anticip’action may motivate people to engage in anticipatory behaviours related to serious illness or end-of-life planning. Research shows that metacognitive experiences, such as becoming aware of the need to take action, can motivate behaviour change [24]. This resonates with the findings from a recent systematic review [25], which showed that increasing patient awareness about their health limitations and lifespan, the benefits of ACP, and the possibility to address it with concrete steps can significantly boost engagement in ACP. Anticip’action can foster this awareness because it contains cards covering topics such as “expressing my preferred place of care in case of illness”, “entrusting someone with the care of my pets”, “organizing my digital legacy”, or “reducing the burden on my loved ones”, “donating (or not) my organs”.

One might hypothesize that when individuals first encounter the game, they become aware of unaddressed areas in their life. This realization may initially decrease their belief of self-perceived preparedness, that is, their sense of being clear on their values, goals, and plans in case of a crisis. This awareness may then act as a powerful motivator, prompting users to take concrete anticipatory steps (e.g., write advance directives, express their values, organize practical matters). Over time, these actions are expected to restore or even enhance their self-perceived preparedness. Additionally, the game’s content may foster more positive attitudes toward ACP because the cards reveal a series of relevant ACP issues.

In this study, Anticip’action was introduced to a senior population in a community setting to examine its effect on participants’ attitude towards ACP, self-perceived preparedness, and readiness to anticipate a health crisis. The study also collected users’ evaluation of the game (endorsement, perceived impact) and observed their propensity to use the game in mobile or paper formats.

Methods

We employed a pre–post exposure study design involving the game’s target user population.

Recruitment

Advertisements for two public events on the topic of advance directives, held in December 2023, were distributed to members of a local senior club, shared through various online channels for public events, and published in one local newspaper. At the beginning of each event, attendees were informed about the opportunity to participate in the study by answering anonymous paper questionnaires. Declining to participate was not an obstacle for taking part in the event. No financial compensation was offered for participation.

Inclusion & exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: being an adult, attending the public event until its end, and freely completing the two questionnaires. During the analysis phase, participants were excluded if they did not meet minimum criteria for data completeness (i.e., if they answered less than 50% of the questionnaire items) and data coherence (i.e., if they provided multiple obviously incoherent responses).

Intervention

Participants attended a 90-minutes public event, received information about ACP, filled Questionnaire 1 (Q1), were introduced to the game, filled Q2, took the game home, and returned Q3 up to 8 weeks later, by post with the pre-stamped envelope (Fig. 2). The procedure was pilot tested with four participants to master the timing, material and technical aspects of the intervention.

Questions were aimed at evaluating the game on participants’ reported 1) Self-perceived preparedness in case of a health crisis (2-item mean score), 2) Attitude towards ACP (2-item mean score) and 3) Readiness to anticipate a health crisis (2-item mean score), as well as 4) participants’ perceived impact of the game (6-item uMARS, section f), and 5) endorsement (1 item uMARS, section e), and Use of the game” (intention to use the game and reported use of the game in mobile or paper formats). Items retrieved from the uMARS questionnaire [26] were translated into French following the good practices described in Wild et al. [27]. The 6 perceived impact items were selected because they reflect the Prochaska’s transtheoretical model of behaviour change [28] which is commonly used in ACP literature to evaluate participants’ change in ACP engagement [11].

In Q3, participants were asked to report whether they had planned or completed anticipatory actions since discovering the game: they could report actions in free text and classify each action as “planned” or “done”.

In order to improve and refine the game, qualitative comments on the clarity of the rules, on the content of the cards, and general comments on the game were collected in questionnaire 3 (Q3). These data will be analysed and reported in a separate publication.

Measures & statistical plan

Scores

All scores (Table 1) were developed as 1–5 Likert scales and calculated as average responses to their items. In case of a missing answer to one item, no score was calculated for that participant. Consistency of scores created for this study was evaluated by calculating the Cronbach’s alpha of participants’ responses to Q1&3. Results ranged between 0.85 and 0.87, indicating consistency without similarity.

Sample size and power calculation

Since attendance to the public events could only be roughly evaluated, it was not possible to predict the number of participants. Therefore, an indicative power analysis was preregistered. Based on ACP studies using similar endpoints, an effect size of 0.3 on a 1–5 scale can be interpreted as a meaningful result. A paired power t-test indicated that with a power set at 0.80 (SD = 1, p = 0.05), a sample of 90 participants is needed for pre-post or between groups comparisons.

Hypotheses

It was expected that presenting the game during the event would have a short-term negative impact on participants’ Self-perceived preparedness (because discovering the cards raises awareness of potential for improvement in anticipating a health crisis), which motivates to act and therefore increases long-term “Readiness” to discuss about values and write AD and correlatively increases long-term Self-perceived preparedness. Overall, it was expected that using the game would positively impact responses on Attitude and Readiness items and motivate participants to take anticipatory actions (reported in Q3). It was also expected that participants with a more positive Attitude towards ACP would score higher on Readiness and report higher Perceived impact, Endorsement and Intention to use the game.

Statistical analysis

Tests were performed with Jamovi [29] and scores were handled as numerical variables. Pre-post changes in Self-perceived preparedness and Readiness were analyzed with the paired t-test. The complementary Wilcoxon nonparametric test was conducted, and since coherent results were obtained, only t-test results are reported in the result section. Linear regression could not be used, as planned in the pre-registration, to check the effect of variations in Attitude on other factors (i.e., Readiness or Endorsement of the game) because the dataset did not fulfil the normality assumption (< 0.001 with Shapiro-Wilkinson test). Thus, considering the nonparametric nature of the data, simple Spearman’s correlations are reported in the result section.

Results

Data collected & retention rate

Of the 124 people who attended the two public events, 118 filled Q1 & Q2 (Fig. 3). By the latest two months after the introductory session, 32 participants returned Q3 that they had filled at home. Several participants did not answer all questions during the event and some pages were missing in one questionnaire returned by post, which explains the differences in the numbers reported below.

Two participants were excluded at data analysis stage: one because of a high rate of missing responses (exceeding the preset maximum of 50%) and another due to numerous incoherent answers, such as checking multiple options although the question called for a unique answer.

Self-perceived preparedness

Overall, baseline scores in Self-perceived preparedness were high, ranging between 3.51 and 3.7 on a 1–5 score (Table 3; Fig. 4). Contrary to expectations, participants’ Self-perceived preparedness was not reduced in the short term after having discovered the game and read the topics on the cards. To the contrary, a pre-post increase in Self-perceived preparedness could be observed between Q1 (before presenting the game) and Q2, at the end of the event after having tested the game (paired t-test: t(113) = 2.33, p = < 0.02, d = 0.22).

Readiness

Overall, baseline Readiness scores were high: 2.87 (on a 1–5 scale) in the entire cohort (N = 116) and 3.18 in the subgroup that answered all questionnaires (N = 31). A small pre-post increase in Readiness (+0.16 on 1–5 scale) could be observed between Q1 (before presenting the game) and Q3 (two months after the public event). It was not significant with this sample size (paired t-test: t [30] = 1.58, p =0.13, d = 0.28).

81% (26/32) of the Q1-Q3 subgroup (i.e. participants who returned Q3 after the event), which corresponds to 22% of the entire cohort (26/116), reported having done or planning to do an anticipatory action. In detail, 12 participants (i.e. 38% of the Q1-Q3 subgroup and 10% of the entire cohort) reported one or several actions done since they played with the game. Attitude

Overall, baseline scores in Attitude were high (between 4.56 and 4.5 on 1–5 scale) and no significant pre-post changes were observed. However, as expected a positive Attitude towards ACP in Q1 was correlated with higher Readiness in Q1 (Spearman, r(113) = 0.34, p = < 0.001), and with a more positive evaluation of the Perceived impact of the game (Spearman, r(105) = 0.45, p = < 0.001), a higher Endorsement of the game (Spearman, r(112) = 0.42, p = < 0.001), and a higher intention to use the game at home after the event (Spearman, r(110) = 0.37, p = < 0.001) in Q2.

Evaluation of the game

Overall, the game obtained high scores on the MARS-f subscale which measures participants’ Perceived impact of the game for anticipating difficult situations that may arise in the future (Table 3; Fig. 5).

Participants also strongly Endorsed the game by recommending it to other users (Table 3).

Discussion

This study highlights participants’ positive evaluation of Anticip’action, and their reported planned or accomplished anticipatory actions after playing the game. Endorsement scores were 3.67 on the day of the event and 3.9 in the final questionnaire returned 6–8 weeks later, meaning that participants would recommend the game to several or many people. Similarly, participants expressed a notably high opinion of the game’s potential impact in helping anticipating difficult situations: mean Perceived impact score was 4.19 on the day of the event and 4.17 6–8 weeks later, meaning that participants agreed or strongly agreed that Anticip’action contributed to raising awareness, increasing knowledge, fostering a positive attitude, motivating to seek further help and encouraging anticipatory behaviour.

This assessment is corroborated by the furnished list of actions, either planned or done (Table 5), that participants described in the final questionnaire. These actions included writing AD, discussing end-of-life issues with family or healthcare providers, planning funerals, or organizing financial matters. Specifically, 10% of the entire cohort (12/116) explicitly reported in the final questionnaire having completed one or more such actions since they played with the game, while an additional 12% (14/116) reported having planned specific actions. These numbers may actually underestimate the true results, as only 28% of the entire cohort responded to the final questionnaire. We do not have data regarding actions done or planned by the remaining 72% of the entire cohort. Their actions would add to the 22% explicitly reported. Overall, these findings suggest that Anticip’action empowers its users to engage in meaningful ACP related conversations and planning within their family environments, without the need for professional facilitation. The findings are consistent with those of other community-based tests of ACP games [10], some of which have reported even more impressive results [16].

However, these results should be interpreted in light of the fact that most participants had a pre-existing interest in ACP, as reflected in the unusually mean positive Attitude toward ACP (Q1 = 4.56). This is likely due to the study’s recruitment strategy: a public event advertised as a conference on the topic of advance directives, co-organized with a local senior club specialized in the organization of cultural events. This approach attracted a specific subgroup of the general population, predominantly educated beyond elementary school (89%) women (90%) with a mean age of 75. All these demographic factors are associated with increased ACP engagement, as documented in the literature [30–33].

Participants expressed uncommonly high mean baseline scores in positive Attitude (i.e. I think that ACP discussions and AD help receive better care), Self-perceived preparedness (i.e. I have clear values and am prepared in case of a health crisis) and Readiness (i.e. I have had ACP discussion and written AD). As a result, significant pre-post changes were difficult to detect. Mean scores in Attitude remained consistently high across the three questionnaires (Q1 = 4.56 ± 0.5; Q2 = 4.56 ± 0.5; Q3 = 4.5 ± 0.53), suggesting that the game’s role was more about prompting action than changing minds. As expected, Readiness score increased between inclusion and end of study, though not enough to reach statistical significance given the small sample size (Q1 = 2.87 ± 0.8, N = 115; Q3 = 3.34 ± 0.86., N = 31). Similarly, Self-perceived preparedness increased slightly between inclusion and end of study without reaching statistical significance (Q1 = 3.52 ± 0.8, N = 115; Q3 = 3.7 ± 0.68, N = 32).

To further explore the impact of Anticip’action in the general population, it would be interesting to test the effects of alternative implementation strategies that introduce the game to people who are unfamiliar with the topic of ACP or to people who are aware of advance directives but are not in the mood to prepare them. Different levels of knowledge about ACP or different levels of prior motivation to engage in ACP may change the effects of the game in ways that are difficult to predict.

One surprising statistically significant effect observed was a short-term increase in mean Self-perceived preparedness immediately after discovering the game during the public event (Q1 = 3.52 ± 0.8, Q2 = 3.63 ± 0.78, paired t-test: p = < 0.02, N = 115). This result contradicts the mechanistic hypothesis according to which the game would, in the short term, lower users’ perceived preparedness by revealing unaddressed issues, thereby triggering motivation to act. A possible explanation for this unexpected result may be that the game makes users cognitively aware of their vulnerability and mortality, prompting an anxiety response, which may be managed through denial and avoidance mechanisms, as reported in previous studies [34]. An alternative hypothesis could be that our participants, who were already positive toward ACP and had a strong sense of their own readiness and preparedness, felt affirmed by their experience at the live event. The event may have helped them confirm their feelings of clarity and preparedness. To disentangle these diverging explanations, it would be interesting to test the effect of the game on a group of people who are less positive about ACP.

In any case, further research is needed to explore the psychological mechanisms underlying the game’s motivating effect observed in this study. The most plausible hypothesis is that Anticip’action offers thought-provoking content and personally relevant prompts and suggestions that help users translate awareness into concrete actions.

During the public event, participants were encouraged to try the mobile version of Anticip’action and 70% did so. At home, where participants could choose feely between formats, reported use of the mobile version dropped to 44%, primarily due to perceptions that it was less convenient and too complex compared to the physical card deck. However, this finding must be considered in the context of the study sample in which 26% of participants reported not using smartphone apps at all.

Interestingly, because the mobile version of the game is nested in the broader Accordons-nous ACP app, participants who used the mobile version also engaged with the other features of the app, which includes informational content, suggested films and readings, conversation starters, contacts for obtaining professional support, and an AD form [21]. This reported usage pattern underscores the value of integrating various ACP tools with each other to maximize their complementary impact and promote a broader ACP culture.

Limitations

Participant selection strategy was based on the recruitment method: advertisements targeted to senior populations for attending a public event in French. However, no formal check for minimal hearing, cognition and French understanding was operated to further select participants.

One limitation of this study is the small sample size, particularly for long-term pre-post analyses. For instance, only large changes in Readiness score could have reached statistical significance with a dataset of 31. Moreover, it is reasonable to assume that participants who responded to the third questionnaire were predominantly those who enjoyed the game. Another limitation is the potential influence of the introductory speech delivered at the public event (which included an explanation of ACP and its importance) on participants’ responses to the questionnaires, notably the Attitude items. A further limitation, already noted above, is the limited external validity of the findings. The participant sample was demographically skewed: predominantly educated women of older age and already interested in the topic of advance directives. Results may not be generalizable to broader or more diverse population groups that were not represented in this study.

Conclusion

Anticip’action, particularly in its paper format, is highly endorsed by a senior population with an existing interest in ACP and appears to effectively support the implementation of anticipatory actions. It can thus be proposed as a valuable discussion tool to facilitate and sustain ACP processes. However, these findings need to be confirmed in larger and more diverse populations, including individuals who may not have a prior familiarity of interest in advance directives or related topics.
