Isolated axillary metastasis from early-stage dMMR endometrial carcinoma: a case report and mini-review
Authors: Yanying Yu, Xiaoyan Chang, Xiaorong Hou, Fuquan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction Axillary lymph node metastasis (ALNM) from endometrial carcinoma is extremely rare, particularly in tumors with early-stage, endometrioid histology. The clinical characteristics, underlying mechanisms, and optimal management strategies for this rare metastatic pattern remain insufficiently defined.Case presentation We report a case of a 63-year-old woman diagnosed with FIGO 2023 stage IIB, grade 2 endometrioid endometrial carcinoma exhibiting mismatch repair deficiency (dMMR) and substantial lymphovascular space invasion (LVSI). She underwent surgery followed by adjuvant pelvic radiotherapy and vaginal brachytherapy. Approximately one year later, the patient developed isolated right axillary lymphadenopathy. Histopathological and immunohistochemical analyses confirmed metastatic endometrioid adenocarcinoma of uterine origin. The patient was treated with tislelizumab and lenvatinib but experienced progressive disease, with enlarging axillary nodes and new chest wall involvement. Salvage radiotherapy with a simultaneous integrated boost was administered and achieved tumor regression. A focused review of previously reported cases of axillary lymph node metastasis from endometrial carcinoma was conducted to summarize clinicopathologic features and treatment approaches.Conclusion This case highlights an unusual metastatic pattern of endometrial cancer and underscores the need for individualized risk stratification, long-term surveillance, and timely integration of local therapy when systemic treatment proves insufficient.

Introduction

Endometrial carcinoma (EC) is the most common gynecologic malignancy in developed countries, with the majority of cases diagnosed at an early stage and exhibiting favorable prognosis [1, 2]. Most endometrioid-type tumors spread via direct extension or through regional pelvic and para-aortic lymphatic pathways, whereas distant metastases are relatively uncommon and typically involve the lungs, liver, or bone [3–7]. Extra-abdominal lymph node metastases, including axillary lymph node metastasis (ALNM), are exceedingly rare and have been predominantly reported in aggressive histologic subtypes such as serous carcinoma or carcinosarcoma [8, 9].

To date, isolated ALNM arising from early-stage, low-grade, dMMR endometrioid EC has not been documented in the literature. The mechanism of such metastasis is poorly understood, and optimal management strategies are undefined. Herein, we present a unique case of FIGO 2023 stage IIB, grade 2, dMMR endometrioid EC with substantial lymphovascular space invasion (LVSI) that developed an isolated right ALNM approximately one year after definitive surgery and adjuvant radiotherapy. In addition, we conducted a focused literature review to summarize the clinicopathologic and molecular features, risk factors, treatment strategies, and outcomes of previously reported cases, aiming to provide clinical insights into this rare recurrence pattern.

Case description

A 63-year-old postmenopausal woman presented with 6 days of vaginal bleeding in October 2023. She had a history of gravida 3, para 1, and had undergone natural menopause at age 55 without a history of menopausal hormone therapy. She had no significant past medical history, no relevant family history of malignancy, and no known hereditary cancer syndromes. Transvaginal ultrasound revealed a heterogeneous endometrial echo with a thickness of 0.6 cm. Hysteroscopy and endometrial curettage revealed a friable lesion on the anterior uterine wall measuring approximately 2 × 2 cm. Histology showed atypical glands favoring endometrioid adenocarcinoma, and immunohistochemistry (IHC) demonstrated ER and PR positivity with wild-type p53. CA125 was 18.6 U/mL (reference range < 35 U/mL). Pelvic MRI and whole-body 18 F-FDG PET/CT demonstrated an FDG-avid endometrial lesion (maximum SUV 27.3), without evidence of extrauterine extension or distant metastasis.

The patient underwent laparoscopic staging surgery in December 2023, including total hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, systematic pelvic lymphadenectomy and para-aortic lymph node dissection. Final pathology revealed a grade 2 endometrioid carcinoma with superficial myometrial invasion (< 50%) and extensive lymphovascular space invasion (LVSI). LVSI was classified as substantial based on diffuse, multifocal involvement of multiple lymphovascular spaces, rather than focal LVSI. The lower uterine segment, parametrium, adnexa, and all pelvic and para-aortic lymph nodes were uninvolved (0/32). IHC showed ER and PR positivity, Ki-67 index of 70%, patchy p16, and wild-type p53. Loss of MLH1 and PMS2 expression confirmed a mismatch repair–deficient (dMMR) phenotype. Paired germline(blood) and somatic(tumor tissue) next-generation sequencing was performed; no pathogenic Lynch syndrome–associated germline mutations or POLE exonuclease domain mutations were identified.

The diagnosis was FIGO 2009 stage IA endometrioid endometrial carcinoma with dMMR and substantial LVSI, corresponding to FIGO 2023 stage IIB under the updated staging system. According to the ESGO–ESTRO–ESP 2021 guidelines, the patient was classified as high-intermediate risk. In patients with stage I endometrial cancer who have undergone thorough surgical staging, adjuvant vaginal brachytherapy alone is often considered sufficient. However, given the presence of substantial LVSI, age over 60 years, and the patient’s strong preference for active adjuvant treatment, adjuvant pelvic external beam radiotherapy (45 Gy in 25 fractions) was administered to address potential pelvic nodal risk, followed by vaginal brachytherapy (10 Gy in 2 fractions) to enhance vaginal cuff control. She experienced only grade 1 diarrhea, and CA125 decreased to 12 U/mL. Imaging at six months showed no evidence of recurrence.

In September 2024, nearly one year after surgery, the patient reported right axillary pain, and a mass was later palpated. Initial antibiotic treatment was ineffective. Contrast-enhanced chest CT revealed a 2.4 × 2.0 cm right axillary lesion. CA125 remained within normal range at 12.5 U/mL. Biopsy of the axillary mass confirmed metastatic endometrioid carcinoma. Immunohistochemistry demonstrated tumor cell positivity for PAX8, ER, and PR, with negative staining for GATA3, TTF-1 and Napsin A, supporting a uterine origin and excluding breast of lung primary tumors. The Ki-67 proliferation index was high (approximately 90%) (Fig. 1). PET/CT showed a solitary hypermetabolic axillary lesion (SUVmax 19.9), with no other sites of metastasis. Serial PET/CT scans demonstrated progressive axillary disease and new hypermetabolic lymph nodes in the right chest wall (Fig. 2).

Combined immunotherapy with tislelizumab (Baize’an ®) and lenvatinib was initiated in December 2024 as an immunotherapy-based systemic approach, although lenvatinib was discontinued later due to intolerance. Despite therapy, the right axillary lesion gradually increased in size, and the patient developed worsening local pain. CA125 increased slightly to 20.2 U/mL. Subsequent PET/CT showed enlargement of axillary disease and emergence of new FDG-avid chest wall lymph nodes, indicating further disease progression (Fig. 2), which prompted consideration of definitive local treatment.

Salvage radiotherapy using intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT) was delivered to the right axilla and chest wall in July 2025. The clinical target volume (CTV) included the right supraclavicular and infraclavicular nodal drainage regions, the right axilla, and part of the right chest wall, while gross tumor volume (GTV) comprised metastatic lesions in the right axilla and chest wall; a 5-mm margin was added to the CTV to generate the planning target volume (PTV). A total dose of 50 Gy was prescribed to CTV, with a simultaneous integrated boost to 60 Gy to the GTV. Two additional cycles of tislelizumab were administered concurrently. One month after radiotherapy, follow-up ultrasound revealed a reduction in the size of the right axillary lymph nodes, indicating a partial response. Subsequently, the patient initiated systemic therapy with paclitaxel and carboplatin in combination with pembrolizumab. A schematic timeline of the patient’s clinical course is presented in Fig. 3. At the time of writing, approximately ten months have elapsed since the diagnosis of axillary metastasis, and the patient remains under active treatment. From the patient’s perspective, she expressed that although the treatment course has been physically and emotionally challenging, the multidisciplinary care and visible improvement on imaging have provided reassurance and strengthened her confidence in continuing therapy.

Discussion

This case highlights an unusual recurrence pattern of endometrial carcinoma, where a patient with early-stage, grade 2 endometrioid histology, dMMR status, and substantial LVSI developed isolated axillary lymph node metastasis approximately one year after surgery and adjuvant radiotherapy. Given the tumor’s molecular profile and limited disease burden, first-line treatment with immune checkpoint inhibitors was initiated, but disease progression prompted the need for salvage radiotherapy. The rarity of such a presentation raises important considerations regarding metastatic pathways, diagnostic work-up, and optimal management strategies for isolated, extra-abdominal nodal recurrence in endometrial cancer.

Lymphatic dissemination in endometrial carcinoma most frequently involves regional pelvic and para-aortic lymph nodes, with the incidence and pattern closely related to pathological risk groups [10]. In a multicenter analysis of 2322 surgically treated patients, Bendifallah et al. reported 5-year cumulative incidences of distant recurrence of 20.7% in the high-risk group, compared with only 5.6%, 3.5%, and 3.3% in the high-intermediate, intermediate, and low-risk groups, respectively [7]. In Aalders et al.’s classic series of 83 stage IV endometrioid EC cases, the lung was the most common extra-pelvic site (36%), while lymph node metastases outside the abdomen—inguinal, supraclavicular, and axillary—accounted for only 13%, with merely one patient demonstrating axillary node involvement amenable to radiotherapy [11]. In a recent large cohort study of stage I high-grade endometrial cancer, León-Castillo et al. demonstrated that, in this population, most recurrences were non-locoregional, predominantly presenting as abdominal or distant metastases rather than isolated vaginal or pelvic relapses, and that recurrence patterns varied substantially across molecular subgroups, with recurrences occurring more frequently in the NSMP and p53-abnormal subgroups [12].

By contrast, isolated axillary lymph node metastasis (ALNM) represents an exceptional recurrence pattern. To further contextualize our case, we reviewed eight published reports of ALNM from endometrial cancer (Table 1) [8, 13–15]. Among them, six patients developed ALNM as the first site of recurrence (4–29 months after diagnosis), and two had synchronous involvement at initial presentation. Most cases were associated with high-risk histologic subtypes, including serous carcinoma (n = 2), carcinosarcoma (n = 3), and dedifferentiated carcinoma (n = 1). These cases indicate that isolated lymph node recurrence, especially in non-regional sites like the axilla warrants careful clinical attention. In patients with a history of endometrial carcinoma, such findings should prompt consideration of metastatic recurrence. Careful histopathological evaluation is essential to distinguish metastatic EC from primary malignancies at the same site and avoid unnecessary breast-directed intervention [16]. In this case, immunohistochemistry demonstrated strong nuclear positivity for PAX8, which supported a Müllerian origin and confirmed the diagnosis of metastatic endometrial carcinoma.

Notably, previous studies have primarily focused on histologic subtype as a predictor of atypical metastasis. However, this case suggests that even intermediate-grade endometrioid carcinoma may develop unusual, non-locoregional recurrence when adverse pathological and molecular features coexist. In our patient, substantial lymphovascular space invasion (LVSI) represented the only conventional high-risk factor, while deficient mismatch repair (dMMR) status may have further contributed to tumor heterogeneity and dissemination potential. Substantial LVSI has been consistently associated with an increased risk of lymphatic dissemination and nodal involvement, and has been linked to a higher likelihood of non-locoregional recurrence rather than serving solely as a prognostic marker for survival outcomes [17–20]. At the same time, its independent impact on overall and recurrence-free survival in early-stage, node-negative endometrial cancer remains controversial, with recent data showing no significant survival difference after adequate surgical staging [21]. Taken together, these findings highlight a role for LVSI in recurrence patterns and routes of dissemination.

The mechanism underlying axillary lymph node spread in gynecologic malignancies remains poorly defined. Proposed pathways include transdiaphragmatic lymphatic dissemination via peritoneal surfaces and posterior drainage via the thoracic duct [14, 22]. However, in our case, the absence of peritoneal disease and supradiaphragmatic nodal involvement suggests a skipped or non-anatomical route of dissemination. Moreover, PET/CT revealed several new FDG-avid nodules along the right chest wall, without evidence of continuous nodal spread from the pelvis to the axilla. The location and distribution of these lesions raise the possibility of hematogenous dissemination to soft tissue, rather than lymphatic spread alone. A similar case of axillary metastasis from dedifferentiated endometrial carcinoma was reported by Morrison et al. [15], where widespread recurrence included vaginal, retroperitoneal, and axillary sites, and hematogenous spread was postulated as a plausible mechanism.

The therapeutic approach to isolated axillary metastasis from endometrial carcinoma is not well established due to its rarity. Among the eight patients summarized in Table 1, four patients received axillary lymphadenectomy, three underwent radiotherapy, and nearly all received systemic therapy, including chemotherapy or immune checkpoint inhibitors. Seven of eight patients were alive at follow-up, with survival reaching up to 134 months. In the series by Chen et al., two patients with isolated axillary metastasis were treated with chemotherapy in combination with axillary lymphadenectomy and radiotherapy, both achieving prolonged disease-free survival exceeding 10 years [14]. These outcomes suggest that, in selected patients, definitive local treatment may contribute to durable disease control [4, 23]. This aligns with the concept of oligometastatic disease, in which eradication of limited metastatic foci through surgery or high-precision radiotherapy can potentially prolong survival and delay systemic progression [24, 25]. Advances in radiotherapy techniques, such as intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) and simultaneous integrated boost (SIB), have further enabled the safe delivery of high- dose radiation to involved lymph nodes while minimizing toxicity to adjacent structures [26]. In our case, the disease progressed despite PD-1 inhibitor plus lenvatinib but was subsequently stabilized with high-dose radiotherapy. This underscores the potential role of early, definitive local treatment, either surgical or radiotherapeutic, in the management of isolated nodal recurrences, particularly when systemic disease burden is limited and systemic therapy alone may not be sufficient.

Conclusion

In summary, isolated ALNM from endometrial carcinoma is extremely rare but should not be overlooked, particularly in patients with adverse molecular or pathological features such as dMMR or substantial LVSI. Early recognition, accurate diagnosis, and timely integration of local treatment may improve disease control in selected cases. A multidisciplinary, individualized approach is essential for managing these atypical recurrences.
