The Lingering Phenomenon and Pattern Formation in a Nonlocal Population Model with Cognitive Map
Kyung-Han Choi, Thomas Hillen

Abstract
The rates at which individuals memorize and forget environmental information strongly influence their movement paths and long-term space use. To understand how these cognitive time scales shape population-level patterns, we propose and analyze a nonlocal population model with a cognitive map. The population density moves by a Fokker–Planck type diffusion driven by a cognitive map that stores a habitat quality information nonlocally. The map is updated through local presence with learning and forgetting rates, and we consider both truncated and normalized perception kernels. We first study the movement-only system without growth. We show that finite perceptual range generates spatial heterogeneity in the cognitive map even in nearly homogeneous habitats, and we prove a lingering phenomenon on unimodal landscapes: for the fixed high learning rate, the peak density near the best location is maximized at an intermediate forgetting rate. We then couple cognitive diffusion to logistic growth. We establish local well-posedness and persistence by proving instability of the extinction equilibrium and the existence of a positive steady state, with uniqueness under an explicit condition on the motility function. Numerical simulations show that lingering persists under logistic growth and reveal a trade-off between the lingering and total population size, since near the strongest-lingering regime the total mass can fall below the total resource, in contrast to classical random diffusive–logistic models.



Keywords:
Lingering phenomenon, Nonlocal perception,
Cognitive map, Fokker-Planck type diffusion, non-local PDEs

Introduction

The rates at which individuals memorize and forget new environmental information are closely linked to their spatial navigation and resulting patterns of space use. In neuroscience, studies have revealed a relationship between hippocampal neurogenesis and roaming patterns via
roaming entropy
, a measure of active territory coverage
[
10
,
16
]
. A recent study found that patients with Alzheimer’s disease show significantly different roaming entropy compared with healthy older adults
[
11
]
. In particular,
[
3
]
links spatial navigation and the formation of cognitive maps through roaming entropy, highlighting the importance of accounting for both individual memory variability and the structure of the environment.

Although neuroscientific insights into learning, forgetting, and roaming have rarely been extended to population-level ecology, recent mathematical models formalize spatial memory using cognitive map frameworks
[
24
,
25
,
26
,
27
]
and examine its consequences for home range formation and aggregation
[
20
,
28
,
31
]
. In addition,
[
30
]
outlines open modeling and analytical challenges for cognitive movement models. In many of these studies, cognitive movement is represented as diffusion and advection with a nonlocal advection term, where a nonlocal memory field biases movement. This nonlocality is thought to be biologically natural because organisms sense their surroundings beyond a single point through vision or smell, so models introduce various types of spatial kernels with a finite perceptual radius, such as the top-hat kernel and bump functions. As a result, much of the literature investigates how the kernel, especially its radius and shape, affects aggregation, home range size, and spatial patterning
[
9
,
14
,
20
,
23
]
. Those models have been studied under periodic boundary condition on the domain, which makes it difficult to fully capture how habitat boundaries influence the information animals perceive.

We hypothesize that memory-based navigation with nonlocal perception can generate heterogeneous patterns of space use and residence times even in nearly homogeneous landscapes. For example, even when food availability is uniform, limited visual range and habitat structure, such as obstacles or cover, create heterogeneity in the cognitive map, consistent with evidence that environmental geometry shapes cognitive maps
[
3
]
. On such a heterogeneous cognitive map, low-retention environmental cues are forgotten before they can guide movement, shifting use toward strongly remembered locations; therefore the rates of learning and forgetting are critical. These ideas lead to a central question about how learning and forgetting rates shape long-term population distributions in ecological systems. It is expected that insufficient memory yields diffusion-like wandering and poor patch use, whereas excessive memory yields maladaptive persistence to stale information. We also expect that there exists a moderate balance that maximizes residency near favorable regions. We refer to this effect as
lingering
.

1.1
The Mathematical Model

We formalize these ideas by coupling population density
u(x,t)
on a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
with a cognitive map
m(x,t)
that encodes spatial memory of habitat density
s(x)
. We work with a dimensionless formulation. The quantity
u(x,t)
denotes a scaled population density and
s(x)
represents the corresponding scaled habitat quality or carrying capacity, so that
(s(x)-u)u
has the form of a logistic growth term with spatially varying capacity. The nonlocal quantity
\bar{s}(x)
represents the perceived habitat quality obtained by spatially averaging
s
around
x
. Thus
s
,
\bar{s}
and
m
are all dimensionless and measured on the same scale. The movement and memory dynamics are

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}u=\Delta\big(\gamma(m)u\big)+(s(x)-u)\,u&&\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T],\\[4.0pt]
&\partial_{t}m=\big(\alpha\,\bar{s}(x)-m\big)\,u-\mu\,m&&\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T],\\
&\nabla\big(\gamma(m)u\big)\cdot\mathbf{n}=0&&\text{on }\partial\Omega\times(0,T],\\
&u(x,0)=u_{0}(x),\quad m(x,0)=m_{0}(x)&&\text{in }\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

(1)

where
\gamma(m)>0
renders memory-dependent mobility,
\alpha>0
represents the rate at which individuals memorize (or learning strength), and
\mu>0
is the memory decay rate, that is, how quickly they forget their memory.
The update term
(\alpha\bar{s}-m)u
implements “learn–where–you–are”: memory is reinforced (or down–weighted) proportionally to local presence
u
, depending on the perceived favorability
\alpha\bar{s}
. The memory equation for
m
provides a mechanistic way to construct a cognitive map. The similar dynamics for cognitive map is demonstrated in literature
[
20
,
30
]
. We define the nonlocal quantity

\bar{s}(x)=(K*s)(x)=\int_{B_{R}(x)}K(x,y)\,s(y)\chi_{\overline{\Omega}}(y)\,dy

(2)

with

K(x,y)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &J_{R}(x-y)\text{ or }\frac{J_{R}(x-y)}{Z_{R}(x)}&&\text{ if }x-y\in B_{R}(0),\\
&\qquad\qquad 0&&\text{ otherwise, }\end{aligned}\right.

(3)

where
Z_{R}(x)=\int_{B_{R}(x)}J_{R}(x-y)\chi_{\bar{\Omega}}(y)dy
and
J_{R}
encodes a perception kernel whose support is a ball
B_{R}(0)
centered at
0
with radius
R
.
\chi_{\bar{\Omega}}
is a characteristic function, that is, it is equal to 1 in
\overline{\Omega}
and otherwise it is 0, and we illustrate its use in Figure
2
. We will consider a normalized and a not-normalized version of the integral operator in (
3
).

The blue curve represents the boundary of the habitat
\Omega
occupied by the population. Dashed regions represent the nonlocal neighborhoods perceived under a finite perceptual radius at different locations.

We assume that
\gamma
is a positive, decreasing function of
m
. In other words, individuals move more slowly in regions where the memory variable
m
is high, tending to remain longer in locations they remember as favorable. Formally expanding the diffusion term generates a natural advection term induced by gradients of
m
, and since
\gamma^{\prime}<0
, this advection describes attraction toward regions of higher memory. The expansion of the diffusion term is

\Delta(\gamma(m)u)=\nabla\cdot\left(\gamma(m)\nabla u+\gamma^{\prime}(m)u\nabla m\right).

This type of diffusion operator,
\Delta(\gamma(m)u)
, is often referred to as a Fokker–Planck–type diffusion or a Chapman diffusion law and has been extensively used to model density-dependent dispersal on the heterogeneous media in biology and ecology, where the probability of leaving a location is determined by the local state (for example, the local density) rather than by its gradient
[
5
,
6
,
18
,
17
,
29
]
. In our setting, this motility depends on the memory field
m
rather than directly on the density
s
, but the underlying diffusion structure is of the same Fokker–Planck type.

It is noteworthy to mention that nonlocality enters only through the second equation in (
1
). Much of the existing literature introduces nonlocal space use via an advection term, which has typically restricted the analysis to periodic boundary conditions
[
20
,
28
,
31
]
. In contrast, our use of no-flux boundary conditions together with the domain-restricted kernel
J_{R}(x-y)\chi_{\bar{\Omega}}(y)
allows us to clarify how the geometric structure of a bounded domain shapes perceived habitat information. As illustrated in Figure
2
, the finite perceptual radius generates nonlocal neighborhoods near the boundary that differ from those in the interior, so the geometry of
\Omega
induces diverse types of spatial heterogeneity in the perceived environment.

On the other hand, we also consider the normalized kernel
K(x,y)=\frac{J_{R}(x-y)\chi_{\bar{\Omega}}(y)}{Z_{R}(x)}
to model situations in which individuals are only able to sense a limited amount of environmental cues. In this case, the normalization compensates for boundary effects, and the influence of the domain geometry on the perceived habitat information is suppressed.

2.1
Heterogeneity by nonlocal perception

This subsection demonstrates that non-normalized kernels introduce artificial heterogeneity near the boundary, even in a homogeneous environment, whereas normalized kernels eliminate this effect.
This is because the range in which the species perceives

Let us consider a homogeneous environment, that is,

Let

In this regard, the structure of the cognitive map becomes more important than the geometric shape of the environment itself. In the next subsection, our focus is therefore on lingering behaviour over a heterogeneous cognitive map rather than on boundary effects, and we accordingly adopt the normalized kernel for numerical simulations.

The lingering phenomenon on heterogeneous landscapes

Solving for
\frac{\partial K}{\partial\mu}
gives

\frac{\partial K}{\partial\mu}=\frac{\displaystyle\int_{-\ell}^{\ell}\frac{\frac{\partial F}{\partial\mu}}{\frac{\partial F}{\partial u}}\,dx}{\displaystyle\int_{-\ell}^{\ell}\frac{1}{\frac{\partial F}{\partial u}}\,dx}.

The mass constraint
M=\int_{-\ell}^{\ell}u_{\infty}dx
and (
9
) gives

u_{\infty}=\frac{K(\mu)}{\gamma(m_{\infty})}=\displaystyle\frac{M}{\gamma(m_{\infty})\int_{-\ell}^{\ell}\frac{1}{\gamma(m_{\infty})}dx}\to\frac{M}{\gamma(0)\frac{2\ell}{\gamma(0)}}=\frac{M}{2\ell}

uniformly in
x
.

We now claim that
\frac{\partial u_{\infty}}{\partial\mu}<0
for sufficiently large
\mu
. For large
\mu
,

\frac{\partial F}{\partial\mu}=-\alpha\bar{s}\gamma^{\prime}(m_{\infty})\frac{u_{\infty}^{2}}{(u_{\infty}+\mu)^{2}}=-\frac{\alpha\bar{s}(x)\gamma^{\prime}(0)M}{2\ell\mu^{2}}+O(\mu^{-2})

uniformly in
\overline{\Omega}
. Therefore, for large
\mu

\frac{\partial K}{\partial\mu}=\frac{\displaystyle\int_{-\ell}^{\ell}\frac{\frac{\partial F}{\partial\mu}}{\frac{\partial F}{\partial u}}dx}{\displaystyle\int_{-\ell}^{\ell}\frac{1}{\frac{\partial F}{\partial u}}dx}=\frac{\displaystyle\int_{-\ell}^{\ell}\frac{\frac{\partial F}{\partial\mu}}{\gamma(0)}dx}{\displaystyle\int_{-\ell}^{\ell}\frac{1}{\gamma(0)}dx}+O(\mu^{-1})=\overline{\frac{\partial F}{\partial\mu}}+O(\mu^{-1}),

where
\overline{\frac{\partial F}{\partial\mu}}=\frac{1}{2\ell}\int_{-\ell}^{\ell}\frac{\partial F}{\partial\mu}(x)dx
. Solve (
10
) for
\frac{\partial u_{\infty}}{\partial\mu}
, and we obtain

\frac{\partial u_{\infty}}{\partial\mu}=\frac{\frac{\partial K}{\partial\mu}-\frac{\partial F}{\partial\mu}(x)}{\frac{\partial F}{\partial u}}=\frac{\overline{\frac{\partial F}{\partial\mu}}-\frac{\partial F}{\partial\mu}(x)}{\gamma(0)}+O(\mu^{-2}).

Since
u_{\infty}
and
\bar{s}
has its maximum at
x=0
,
\overline{\frac{\partial F}{\partial\mu}}<\frac{\partial F}{\partial\mu}(x)
for large
\mu
, hence
\frac{\partial u_{\infty}}{\partial\mu}<0
for sufficiently large
\mu
.

Using
\frac{\partial F}{\partial u}>0
and
|\frac{\partial F}{\partial\mu}|\leq C/\mu^{2}
, we obtain

|\frac{\partial K}{\partial\mu}|\leq\frac{\displaystyle\int_{-\ell}^{\ell}\frac{|\frac{\partial F}{\partial\mu}|}{\frac{\partial F}{\partial u}}\,dx}{\displaystyle\int_{-\ell}^{\ell}\frac{1}{\frac{\partial F}{\partial u}}\,dx}\leq\frac{\displaystyle\frac{C}{\mu^{2}}\int_{-\ell}^{\ell}\frac{1}{\frac{\partial F}{\partial u}}\,dx}{\displaystyle\int_{-\ell}^{\ell}\frac{1}{\frac{\partial F}{\partial u}}\,dx}=\frac{C}{\mu^{2}}.

Finally, substituting it back into the expression for
\partial_{\mu}u
,

|\partial_{\mu}u|\leq\frac{|\frac{\partial K}{\partial\mu}|+|\frac{\partial F}{\partial\mu}(x,\mu)|}{\frac{\partial F}{\partial u}(x,\mu)}\leq\frac{1}{c_{0}}\left(\frac{C}{\mu^{2}}+\frac{C}{\mu^{2}}\right)=\frac{2C}{\mu^{2}},

for some constant
C>0
independent of
\mu
(for all
\mu\geq\mu_{0}
). This proves the claimed
O(\mu^{-2})
decay and uniform convergence

as
\mu\to\infty
.

(iv)
At
\mu=0
, we have

\frac{\partial F}{\partial u}=\gamma(\alpha\bar{s})>0,\text{ and }\frac{\partial F}{\partial\mu}=-\alpha\bar{s}\gamma^{\prime}(\alpha\bar{s})>0.

Therefore,
u
has its maximum at
x=0
by the relation
\gamma(\alpha\bar{s})u^{\prime}=-\alpha\gamma^{\prime}(\alpha\bar{s})\bar{s}^{\prime}
.
If we combine the constraint and (
10
), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\partial K}{\partial\mu}\Big|_{{\mu=0}}=\frac{\int_{-\ell}^{\ell}\frac{\partial F}{\partial\mu}\frac{\partial F}{\partial u}^{-1}dx}{\int_{-\ell}^{\ell}\frac{\partial F}{\partial u}^{-1}dx}=\frac{\int_{-\ell}^{\ell}-\alpha\bar{s}\gamma^{\prime}(\alpha\bar{s})\gamma(\alpha\bar{s})^{-1}dx}{\int_{-\ell}^{\ell}\gamma(\alpha\bar{s})^{-1}dx}.

Notice that
\frac{\partial K}{\partial\mu}
is a weighted average of
-\alpha\bar{s}\gamma^{\prime}(\alpha\bar{s})
over
(-\ell,\ell)
. We use (

\partial_{\mu}u\Big|_{\mu=0}=\frac{\frac{\partial K}{\partial\mu}\big|_{\mu=0}-(-\alpha\bar{s}\gamma^{\prime}(\alpha\bar{s}))}{\gamma(\alpha\bar{s})},

which implies that the sign of
\partial_{\mu}u\big|_{\mu=0}
is determined by the sign of
\frac{\partial K}{\partial\mu}\big|_{\mu=0}-(-\alpha\bar{s}\gamma^{\prime}(\alpha\bar{s}))
. Suppose that the map
z\mapsto-z\gamma^{\prime}(z)
is increasing on
(0,z_{0})
and decreasing on

for

. Since
\bar{s}(0)
is the maximum of
\bar{s}
, for
\alpha<\alpha_{1}

-\alpha\bar{s}(x)\gamma^{\prime}(\alpha\bar{s}(x))-(-\alpha\bar{s}(0)\gamma^{\prime}(\alpha\bar{s}(0))),

hence
\frac{\partial K}{\partial\mu}\big|_{\mu=0}-(-\alpha\bar{s}\gamma^{\prime}(\alpha\bar{s}))<0

at

.

∎

3
Population dynamics model

In the previous section, we studied lingering behaviour in a purely movement-based model without births and deaths. We now incorporate logistic population dynamics into the Fokker–Planck type diffusion model to examine how demographic growth interacts with memory-driven movement.

We consider the reaction–diffusion model:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &u_{t}=\Delta(\gamma(m)u)+f(s,u),&&\text{ in }\Omega\times(0,T),\\
&m_{t}=(\alpha\bar{s}-m)u-\mu m,\\
&\nabla(\gamma(m)u)\cdot\textbf{n}=0,&&\text{ on }\partial\Omega\times[0,T],\\
&u(x,0)=u_{0}(x)>0,m(x,0)=m_{0}(x)\geq 0,&&\text{ in }\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

(11)

where
s=s(x)
is a given food distribution. We also assume that
s(x)>0
for all
x\in\Omega
and
m_{0}(x)\leq\alpha\bar{s}(x)
. The kinetic term
f(s,u)
is of the form:

f(s,u)=g(s,u)u,

where
g(s,0)=s
and the map
u\mapsto g(s,u)
is decreasing, Lipchitz continuous in
u
, and

|g(s,u)|\leq C(1+|u|).

The function
g(s,u)
represents the per-capita growth rate in an environment with local resource level
s(x)
. The condition
g(s,0)=s
means that the initial growth rate is proportional to resource availability, while the monotonicity of the map
u\mapsto g(s,u)
encodes crowding effects, meaning that higher density reduces per-capita growth. Lipschitz continuity and the growth bound are technical assumptions for well-posedness.

The above model (
11
) has a few special cases that were studied before:

Example 1:

When
\gamma(z)=\gamma
for some positive constant
\gamma>0
and
g(s,u)=s-u
the system (
11
) reduces to a single reaction–diffusion equation

\left\{\begin{aligned} &u_{t}=\gamma\Delta u+u(s-u),&&\text{ in }\Omega\times(0,T),\\
&\nabla u\cdot\textbf{n}=0,&&\text{ on }\partial\Omega\times[0,T],\\
&u(x,0)=u_{0}(x)>0,&&\text{ in }\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

(12)

The equation (
12
) is a classical Fisher-KPP equation (or diffusive–logistic equation)
[
22
]
. It is well known that if
s(x)\geq 0
, then there exists a positive global equilibrium
u^{*}(x)
for a proper diffusion rate
\gamma

[
4
,
21
]
.

Example 2:

When
m=s(x)
and
g(s,u)=s-u
the system (
11
) reduces to a single reaction–diffusion equation

\left\{\begin{aligned} &u_{t}=\Delta(\gamma(s(x))u)+u(s(x)-u),&&\text{ in }\Omega\times(0,T),\\
&\nabla(\gamma(s)u)\cdot\textbf{n}=0,&&\text{ on }\partial\Omega\times[0,T],\\
&u(x,0)=u_{0}(x)>0,&&\text{ in }\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

(13)

In
[
29
]
the global dynamics of (
13
) and the total-mass property of its steady-state solution was studied. If we choose
\alpha=1
and
\mu=0
and assume that
\bar{s}=s
, then
m(x,t)\equiv s(x)
is a steady cognitive map and (
11
) coincides with (
13
). Biologically, this corresponds to individuals that use only local habitat information and do not forget.

3.1
Local existence

We first verify the well posedness of model (
11
) mathematically by proving the local existence of its unique solution.
To guarantee uniform ellipticity of the diffusion operator in the equation, we have the following hypothesis:

\text{The map }m\mapsto\gamma(m)>0\text{ is $C^{3}([0,\infty))$, decreasing,}

(14)

\displaystyle u_{0}\in C^{2+\theta}(\overline{\Omega}),\quad m_{0}\in C^{2+\theta}(\overline{\Omega})\text{ for some }\theta\in(0,1)\text{ and }m_{0}\leq\alpha\bar{s}.

(15)

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
0<\theta<1
. Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded domain with
C^{2+\theta}
boundary. Assume (
14
) and
s\in C^{2+\theta}(\overline{\Omega})
. Then there exists a unique nonnegative solution
(u,m)\in[C^{2+\theta,1+\theta/2}(\overline{\Omega}\times[0,T_{\max}))]^{2}
to (
11
), and for each
x\in\bar{\Omega}

0\leq m(x,t)\leq\alpha\bar{s}(x)\text{ for all }t\leq 0.

Additionally, the following holds: Either
T_{\max}=\infty
or
T_{\max}<\infty
, so that
\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\to\infty
as
t\to T_{\max}
.

Proof.

We use a fixed-point argument to show existence and uniqueness.
Let
X=C^{1+\theta,(1+\theta)/2}(\overline{\Omega}\times[0,T_{\max}])
, and choose small enough
T\in(0,1)
. We define a closed bounded convex subset

K_{T}=\{\tilde{u}\in X:\|\tilde{u}(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq M\text{ a.e. in }[0,T]\}

For a given

\tilde{u}\in K_{T}
we consider the auxiliary system

\left\{\begin{aligned} &u_{t}=\nabla\cdot(\gamma(m)\nabla u)+\nabla\cdot(\gamma^{\prime}(m)u\nabla m)+g(s,\tilde{u})u,&&\text{ in }\Omega\times(0,T)\\
&m_{t}=(\alpha\bar{s}-m)\tilde{u}-\mu m,\\
&\nabla(\gamma(m)u)\cdot\textbf{n}=0&&\text{ on }\partial\Omega\times[0,T],\end{aligned}\right.

(16)

Since
m
can be expressed in the explicit form in terms of
\tilde{u}
:

m(x,t)=e^{-(\int_{0}^{t}\tilde{u}dt+\mu t)}m_{0}(x)+\alpha\bar{s}(x)\int_{0}^{t}e^{-(\int_{\sigma}^{t}\tilde{u}dt+\mu(t-\sigma))}\tilde{u}(x,\sigma)d\sigma,

one can obtain the estimate for
m
:

\|m\|_{X}\leq C(\|\tilde{u}\|_{X}+\|m_{0}\|_{C^{1+\theta}(\overline{\Omega})}),

which results in
\nabla m\in L^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega}\times[0,T])
. By hypothesis (
14
),
\gamma^{\prime}(m)\nabla m\in L^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega}\times[0,T])
. Note that
g(x,\tilde{u})\leq C(1+M)
.

We claim that
m
is uniformly bounded. Indeed, if
m\geq\alpha\bar{s}
, then

m_{t}\leq-\mu m\implies m(x,t)\leq m(x,0)e^{-\mu t},

and we have
0\leq m(x,t)\leq\max\{m_{0}(x),\alpha\bar{s}(x)\}.
Since
\gamma
is decreasing, there exists a constant
\gamma_{0}>0
such that
\gamma(m)\geq\gamma_{0}
, which gives the uniform parabolicity to (
16
).
By Theorem V1.1 in
[
19
]
, there exists a unique solution
u
to (
16
) and

\|u\|_{C^{\theta_{1},\theta_{1}/2}(\overline{\Omega}\times[0,T])}\leq C

for some constant
C>0
. One can find

\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq\|u_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}+Ct^{\theta_{1}/2}

for all
t\in(0,T)
.
For sufficiently small
T
, we have
u(\cdot,t)\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. Due to the regularity of the initial condition and the parabolic regularity theory,
u\in X
, hence
u\in K_{T}
. We define a map
\Phi:K_{T}\to K_{T}
by
u=\Phi(\tilde{u})
.

Following
[
7
,
15
]
, the continuity and compactness of
\Phi
are obtained by analogous arguments.
By Schauder’s fixed point theorem we thus obtain a local solution on
[0,T]
. Nonnegativity is followed from the maximum principle. Since
M
depends only on the initial data, the construction can be iterated to extend the solution up to the maximal time
T_{\max}
. If
T_{\max}<\infty
,
\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\to\infty
as
t\to T_{\max}
. Otherwise, we can iterate the same process infinitely, so that
T_{\max}=\infty
.
Uniqueness follows from the standard subtraction method in
[
7
,
15
]
.

∎

3.2
Population persistence and steady-state solutions

In this subsection we show that the extinction equilibrium is unstable and that, under suitable conditions on
\gamma
, there exists a positive stable steady-state solution of (
11
). Biologically, this corresponds to persistence of the population.

We now specialize on the classical logistic growth
g(s,u)=s-u
and study the resulting steady-state structure. Our main questions are whether the population persists and whether the long-time spatial distribution is uniquely determined when individuals follow memory-based Fokker-Planck type diffusion.

Lemma 3.2

(Unstable extinction equilibrium)
.

Let
0<\theta<1
. Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded domain with
C^{2}
boundary. Assume (
14
),
s\in C^{2+\theta}(\overline{\Omega})
, and
s>0
in
\overline{\Omega}
. Suppose that
g(s,u)=s-u
. Then the extinction steady-state solution
(u,m)=(0,0)
of (
11
) is linearly unstable.

Proof.

Let
U=\gamma(m)u
. Then

U_{t}=\gamma(m)u_{t}+\gamma^{\prime}(m)um_{t}
, so

\left\{\begin{aligned} &U_{t}=\gamma(m)\Delta U+U\left(s(x)-\frac{U}{\gamma(m)}\right)+\gamma^{\prime}(m)\frac{U}{\gamma(m)}\left((\alpha\bar{s}(x)-m)\frac{U}{\gamma(m)}-\mu m\right),&&\text{ in }\Omega,\\
&m_{t}=(\alpha\bar{s}(x)-m)\frac{U}{\gamma(m)}-\mu m,\\
&\nabla U\cdot\textbf{n}=0,&&\text{ on }\partial\Omega\\
&U(x,0)=U_{0}(x),\,m(x,0)=m_{0}(x)&&\text{ in }\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

(17)

Linearizing the system around the extinction equilibrium
(0,0)
and dropping all quadratic terms gives

\partial_{t}\begin{pmatrix}U\\
m\end{pmatrix}=\mathcal{L}\begin{pmatrix}U\\
m\end{pmatrix}:=\begin{pmatrix}\gamma(0)\Delta+s(x)&0\\
\frac{\alpha\bar{s}(x)}{\gamma(0)}&-\mu\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}U\\
m\end{pmatrix}

(18)

Since
\mathcal{L}
is lower-triangular in block form, its spectrum is simply
\sigma(\gamma(0)\Delta+s(x))\cup\{-\mu\}
.
Since
s>0
, the principal eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
of
\gamma(0)\Delta+s(x)
under Neumann boundary condition is positive. Indeed, the Rayleigh quotient is

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}

\displaystyle=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\phi\in W^{1,2}(\Omega)\\
\phi\neq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{-\gamma(0)\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\phi|^{2}dx+\int_{\Omega}s(x)\phi^{2}dx}{\int_{\Omega}\phi^{2}dx}

(19)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\int_{\Omega}s(x)\cdot 1^{2}dx}{\int_{\Omega}1^{2}dx}=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}s(x)dx>0.

U=0
if and only if
u=0
by positivity of
\gamma(m)
due to boundedness of
m
. Therefore,
(u,m)=(0,0)
is linearly unstable.
∎

We use the Schaefer’s fixed point theorem to prove the existence of positive steady-state solutions of our model (
11
).

Theorem 3.3

(Schaefer’s Fixed Point Theorem
[
8
]
)
.

Let
X
be a Banach space,
K\subset X
be a nonempty closed convex set, and
T:K\rightarrow K
be continuous compact mapping. If a set

\{u\in K:u=\lambda Tu\text{ for some }0<\lambda\leq 1\}

is bounded, the map
T
has a fixed point in
K
.

For the uniqueness of the steady-state, we use the following lemma.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded domain with
C^{2}
boundary and let

f:\Omega\times(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
be a Carathéodory function, i.e.,

x\mapsto f(x,u)
is measurable for every
u>0
and
u\mapsto f(x,u)
is
continuous for a.e.
x\in\Omega
.
Assume that
f(\cdot,u(\cdot))\in L^{2}
and for a.e.
x\in\Omega
the map

u\mapsto\frac{f(x,u)}{u}\quad\text{is strictly decreasing on }(0,\infty).

Then the semilinear Neumann problem

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\Delta u+f(x,u)=0&&\text{in }\Omega,\\
&\nabla u\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0&&\text{on }\partial\Omega\end{aligned}\right.

(20)

admits at most one weak solution
u\in H^{1}(\Omega)
satisfying
u>0
a.e. in
\Omega
.
In particular, (
20
) has at most one positive classical solution

u\in C^{2}(\bar{\Omega})
.

Proof.

In
[
2
]
, they showed the uniqueness of the weak solution to the Dirichlet boundary problem

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\Delta u+f(x,u)=0&&\text{in }\Omega,\\
&u=0&&\text{on }\partial\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

Since the Dirichlet boundary condition was only used to cancel boundary terms in the
integration by parts in the paper, one can follow the similar argument to obtain the uniqueness of a positive weak solution to the Neumann boundary problem (
20
). Since any
C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
solution satisfies the weak
formulation, the uniqueness also holds in the class of positive classical
solutions.
∎

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
0<\theta<1
and
\mu>0
. Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded domain with
C^{2}
boundary. Assume that (
14
) holds and
s\in C^{2+\theta}(\overline{\Omega})
and
s>0
in
\overline{\Omega}
. Then there exists a positive steady-state solution
(u,m)
to (
11
) if

\sup_{z\in[0,M]}\left|\frac{\gamma^{\prime}(z)}{\gamma(z)}\right|<\frac{4}{M},

(21)

where
M=\alpha\|\bar{s}\|_{\infty}
. In addition, the solution exists uniquely if

\sup\limits_{[0,M]}\left|\frac{\gamma^{\prime}(z)}{\gamma(z)}\right|\leq\frac{\mu}{M\|s\|_{\infty}}

(22)

Proof.

The steady-state
(U,m)
of (
17
) satisfies
U_{t}=m_{t}=0
and

\left\{\begin{aligned} &0=\Delta U+\frac{U}{\gamma(m)}\left(s(x)-\frac{U}{\gamma(m)}\right),&&\text{ in }\Omega,\\
&0=(\alpha\bar{s}(x)-m)\frac{U}{\gamma(m)}-\mu m,&&\text{ in }\Omega\\
&\nabla U\cdot\textbf{n}=0,&&\text{ on }\partial\Omega\end{aligned}\right.

(23)

From the second equation, we have
m=\alpha\bar{s}\frac{U}{U+\mu\gamma(m)}
. Define a map
F
by

F(x,m,U)=m-\frac{\alpha\bar{s}U}{U+\mu\gamma(m)}.

Then

\displaystyle\partial_{m}F(x,m,U)=1+\alpha\bar{s}\frac{U\mu\gamma^{\prime}(m)}{(U+\mu\gamma(m))^{2}}\geq 1+\alpha\bar{s}\frac{\gamma^{\prime}(m)}{4\gamma(m)}>0,

where we have used
\frac{U}{(U+c)^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{4c}
for
c>0
and the last inequality comes from (
21
). By the implicit function theorem, we can write
m=m(U)
and get a semilinear equation for
U
:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &0=\Delta U+\frac{U}{\gamma(m(U))}\left(s(x)-\frac{U}{\gamma(m(U))}\right),&&\text{ in }\Omega,\\
&\nabla U\cdot\textbf{n}=0,&&\text{ on }\partial\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

Let
X=C^{0,\theta}(\overline{\Omega})
and let us define a closed convex subset
K\subset X
by

K=\{f\in X:f\geq 0\text{ in }\overline{\Omega}\}.

Choose
\tilde{U}\in K
.
Denote
\kappa(\tilde{U})=\gamma(m(\tilde{U}))
.
Consider the auxiliary equation for
U
:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &0=\Delta U+\frac{U}{\kappa(\tilde{U})}\left(s(x)-\frac{U}{\kappa(\tilde{U})}\right),&&\text{ in }\Omega,\\
&\nabla U\cdot\textbf{n}=0,&&\text{ on }\partial\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

(24)

The standard comparison method ensures the existence of the unique solution
U
to (
24
). Indeed, if we choose
0<\varepsilon<(\min_{\overline{\Omega}}s(x))\gamma(M)
, then
\underline{U}=\varepsilon
satisfies

\Delta\underline{U}+\frac{s}{\kappa(\tilde{U})}\underline{U}-\frac{1}{\kappa(\tilde{U})^{2}}\underline{U}^{2}=\frac{s}{\kappa(\tilde{U})}\varepsilon-\frac{1}{\kappa(\tilde{U})^{2}}\varepsilon^{2}=\frac{s\varepsilon}{\kappa(\tilde{U})}\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{s\kappa(\tilde{U})}\right)\geq 0,

hence
\underline{U}=\varepsilon
is a subsolution. Similarly, a constant
\overline{U}=\|s\|_{\infty}\gamma(0)
is a supersolution. By the standard iteration method, there exists a solution
U\in C^{2+\theta}(\overline{\Omega})
to (
24
), and the solution is uniformly bounded by
\|s\|_{\infty}\gamma(0)
. Positivity of
U
follows from the maximum principle and Hopf’s lemma. Uniqueness of the positive
U
is ensured due to the convexity of the logistic term in terms of
U
.
Define the mapping
T:K\to K
by

T(\tilde{U})=U.

(Compactness of
T
)
Suppose that
B\subset K
is bounded in
X
.
We claim that
T(B)
is precompact in
K
. Pick
\tilde{u}\in B
. Then

T(\tilde{U})=U\leq\overline{U}=\|s\|_{\infty}\gamma(0).

Choose
p>n
. The Neumann
W^{2,p}
estimate gives

\displaystyle\|U\|_{W^{2,p}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\|U\|_{L^{p}(\Omega))}+\left\|\frac{s}{\kappa(\tilde{U})}U-\frac{1}{\kappa(\tilde{U})^{2}}U^{2}\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C=C(\Omega,s,\gamma).

By using the Sobolev embedding, we have for
\delta=1-\frac{n}{p}\in(0,1)

\|U\|_{C^{1+\delta}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq C=C(\Omega,s,\gamma,p).

Therefore,
U
is also uniformly bounded in
C^{0,\theta}(\overline{\Omega})
.
The Neumann Schauder estimate yields

\displaystyle\|U\|_{C^{2+\theta}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq C\left(\|U\|_{C(\overline{\Omega}))}+\left\|\frac{s}{\kappa(\tilde{U})}U-\frac{1}{\kappa(\tilde{U})^{2}}U^{2}\right\|_{X}\right)

(25)

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}(1+[\tilde{U}]_{X}),

(26)

where we have used the
C^{1}
regularity of
\gamma
and the
C^{0,\alpha}
regularity of the map
m=m(\tilde{U})=\alpha\bar{s}\frac{\tilde{U}}{\tilde{U}+\mu}
. Since
\tilde{U}\in B
,
\|U\|_{C^{2+\theta}(\overline{\Omega})}
is uniformly bounded when
\tilde{U}\in B
. By the Sobolev embedding,
T(B)
is relatively compact in
K
.

(Continuity of
T
)
Suppose that
\tilde{U}_{n}\to\tilde{U}
in
K
as
n\to\infty
. Due to the regularity of
\kappa
, one can deduce that
\kappa(\tilde{U}_{n})\to\kappa(\tilde{U})
in
X
as
n\to\infty
. Note that
\tilde{U}_{n}
is bounded in
K
. The Neumann Schauder estimate (
25
) gives the uniform bound of
T(\tilde{U})
in
C^{2,\theta}(\overline{\Omega})
. Let
0<\beta<\theta
. Then by the Sobolev embedding
C^{2,\theta}(\overline{\Omega})\hookrightarrow C^{2,\beta}(\overline{\Omega})
, we can choose a convergent subsequence
\{v_{n_{k}}\}_{k}
of
v_{n}=T(\tilde{U}_{n})
, say
v_{n_{k}}\to\hat{U}
in
C^{2,\beta}(\overline{\Omega})
as
n\to\infty
. Then
\Delta\tilde{U}_{n_{k}}\to\Delta\hat{U}
in
C(\overline{\Omega})
, hence

\hat{U}
satisfies (
24
). On the other hand,
T(\tilde{U})
also solves (
24
). The uniqueness of its solution concludes that
T(\tilde{U})=\hat{U}=\lim_{n\to\infty}T(\tilde{U}_{n})
in
X
.

(Boundedness of
S
in
K
)
We define a subset

S\subset K
by

S=\{U\in K:U=\lambda T(U)\text{ for some }\lambda\in(0,1]\}.

We claim that
S
is bounded in
K
.
Since
U=\lambda T(U)
for
U\in S
for
\lambda\in(0,1]
, it suffices to prove that
V:=T(U)
is uniformly bounded in
K
.
We apply the comparison principle and the Neumann
W^{2,p}
estimate as we did above:

\displaystyle\|V\|_{W^{2,p}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\|U\|_{L^{p}(\Omega))}+\left\|\frac{s}{\kappa(U)}V-\frac{1}{\kappa(U)^{2}}V^{2}\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\right)

The comparison principle gives
V\leq\overline{U}=\|s\|_{\infty}\gamma(0)
, so
\frac{V}{\kappa(U)}\leq\frac{\overline{U}}{\gamma(M)}
. Therefore,
\|V\|_{W^{2,p}(\Omega)}
is uniformly bounded in
W^{2,p}(\Omega)
. By applying the Sobolev embedding theorem again, we obtain the boundedness of
V
in
K
.

We apply the Schaefer’s fixed point theorem
3.3
to obtain a fixed point
U\in K
such that
U=T(U)
. Denote
u=U/\gamma(m(U))
. Then
u
is positive and solves

\left\{\begin{aligned} &0=\Delta(\gamma(m)u)+u(s-u)&&\text{ in }\Omega\\
&0=(\alpha\bar{s}-m)u-\mu m&&\text{ in }\Omega\\
&\nabla(\gamma(m)u)\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0&&\text{ on }\partial\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

(Uniqueness)
Since (
23
) is of the form of the semilinear elliptic equation (
20
), it suffices to show for the uniqueness that the map

U\mapsto\frac{1}{\gamma(m(U))}\left(s-\frac{U}{\gamma(m(U))}\right)

is strictly decreasing. Note that
U=\gamma(m(u))u
and

\frac{\partial U}{\partial u}=\gamma(m(u))+\gamma^{\prime}(m(u))\frac{\alpha\bar{s}\mu u}{(u+\mu)^{2}}\geq\gamma(m(u))+\gamma^{\prime}(m(u))\frac{\alpha\bar{s}}{4}>0

due to the inequality
\frac{u\mu}{(u+\mu)^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{4}
and (
21
). Therefore, it suffice to show that the map

u\mapsto\frac{s-u}{\gamma(m(u))}

is strictly decreasing. The derivative of the map with respect to
u
is given by

\frac{1}{\gamma(m(u))^{2}}\left(-\gamma(m(u))-(s-u)\gamma^{\prime}(m(u))\frac{\alpha\bar{s}\mu}{(u+\mu)^{2}}\right).

Equation (
22
) yields

\displaystyle-\gamma(m)-\gamma^{\prime}(m)\frac{\alpha\bar{s}s\mu}{(u+\mu)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq-\gamma(m)+\left(\gamma(m)\frac{\mu}{M\|s\|_{\infty}}\right)\frac{\alpha\bar{s}s\mu}{(u+\mu)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\gamma(m)\left(-1+\frac{\alpha\bar{s}s\mu^{2}}{M\|s\|_{\infty}(u+\mu)^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle<0

which concludes the uniqueness of the positive solution to (
23
).

∎

Remark 3
.

The condition (
22
) on
\gamma
is a technical sufficient condition used to obtain uniqueness; the argument is not expected to be optimal, and a sharper proof might establish uniqueness of the steady state under weaker assumptions. Nevertheless, it is noteworthy that we can still guarantee a unique positive steady-state solution in the presence of the nonlinear diffusion term
\Delta(\gamma(m)u)
driven by the cognitive map. This suggests that the uniqueness result (
12
) in
[
4
]
could potentially be extended to the diffusive–logistic model with memory-based diffusion considered here.

3.3
Lingering phenomenon with population growth

We have analytically investigated the population persistence via existence theory and long-time behavior analysis. In this subsection, we present a partial analytical result of the lingering phenomenon for the model with logistic growth (
11
). Additionally, we observe the lingering through numerical simulation results of the population growth model (
11
).

Let
\Omega=(-\ell,\ell)
and
s
is even in
(-\ell,\ell)
and decreasing in
(0,\ell)
.
Let
Q
be a reflection operator centered at
x=0
. Note that
Qs=s
, so
\bar{Q}s=\bar{s}
. Then

\displaystyle Q\left[(\gamma(m)u)^{\prime\prime}+u(s-u)\right]=(\gamma(Qm)Qu)^{\prime\prime}+Qu(s-Qu),\text{ and }

\displaystyle Q[(\alpha\bar{s}-m)u-\mu m]=(\alpha\bar{s}-Qm)Qu-\mu Qm.

Therefore,
(u,m)
and
(Qu,Qm)
satisfy the steady-state problem

\left\{\begin{aligned} &0=(\gamma(\phi)v)^{\prime\prime}+v(s-v)\\
&\phi=\alpha\bar{s}\frac{v}{v+\mu}\\
&(\gamma(\phi)v)^{\prime}(\pm\ell)=0.\end{aligned}\right.

(27)

Due to the uniqueness of the steady-state solution, it follows that
u=Qu
and
m=Qm
, that is,
u
and
m
are symmetric at
x=0
.

For small
\alpha
, we will prove that the steady-state solution of (
27
) behaves similarly to the steady state in the constant-
\gamma
case (i.e. the steady-state solution of (
12
)). The following Lemma
3.6
provides a key monotonicity property of the steady-state solution to (
12
) (constant
\gamma
) near the boundary. A similar result was obtained by
[
12
]
under Dirichlet boundary conditions. In the Neumann case considered here, we use a different argument to establish the monotonicity.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
\gamma_{0},\ell>0
, and let
s\in C^{1}([-\ell,\ell])
be even and strictly decreasing on
(0,\ell)
.
Suppose that
v\in C^{2}([-\ell,\ell])
is the unique positive solution of

\gamma_{0}v^{\prime\prime}+v(s-v)=0\quad\text{in }(-\ell,\ell),\qquad v^{\prime}(-\ell)=v^{\prime}(\ell)=0.

(28)

Then
v
is even and nonincreasing on
[0,\ell]
. In particular,
v
attains its maximum at
x=0
.

Proof.

We first show that
v
is even. Define
\tilde{v}(x):=v(-x)
. Since
s
is even,
\tilde{v}
satisfies

\gamma_{0}\tilde{v}^{\prime\prime}+\tilde{v}(s-\tilde{v})=0\quad\text{in }(-\ell,\ell),\qquad\tilde{v}^{\prime}(-\ell)=\tilde{v}^{\prime}(\ell)=0,

and
\tilde{v}>0
on
[-\ell,\ell]
. By the uniqueness of the positive solution, we conclude that

v\equiv\tilde{v}
, hence
v
is even.

Next, we claim that there exists
x\in(0,\ell)
such that
v^{\prime}(x)\leq 0
.
Indeed, suppose by contradiction that
v^{\prime}(x)>0
for all
x\in(0,\ell)
, so that
v
is strictly increasing on
(0,\ell)
.
Since
v
is even, it follows that
v
attains its minimum at
x=0
, hence
v^{\prime}(0)=0
and
v^{\prime\prime}(0)\geq 0
.
Evaluating (
28
) at
x=0
gives

\gamma_{0}v^{\prime\prime}(0)=-v(0)\bigl(s(0)-v(0)\bigr)\geq 0,

and therefore
v(0)\geq s(0)
.
Similarly, since
v
attains its maximum at
x=\ell
and
v^{\prime}(\ell)=0
, we have
v^{\prime\prime}(\ell)\leq 0
, and thus

v(\ell)\leq s(\ell)
.
This is impossible because

v(\ell)\leq s(\ell)<s(0)\leq v(0),

while
v(0)
is the minimum of
v
.

Now set
w:=v^{\prime}
and define

E:=\{x\in(0,\ell):w(x)>0\}.

Then
E
is open by continuity of
w
. If
E
were nonempty, take any
x_{1}\in E
and let
(a,b)\subset(0,\ell)

be the connected component of
E
containing
x_{1}
. Then
w>0
on
(a,b)
and
w(a)=w(b)=0
.
Hence
w
attains its (positive) maximum at some
x_{*}\in(a,b)
, and thus

w^{\prime}(x_{*})=0,\qquad w^{\prime\prime}(x_{*})\leq 0.

Since
s\in C^{1}
and
v\in C^{2}
, we have
v^{\prime\prime}\in C^{1}
, and differentiating (
28
) yields

\gamma_{0}w^{\prime\prime}+(s-2v)w=-vs^{\prime}\qquad\text{in }(0,\ell).

(29)

Moreover,
v^{\prime\prime}(x_{*})=w^{\prime}(x_{*})=0
, so evaluating (
28
) at
x=x_{*}
gives

0=\gamma_{0}v^{\prime\prime}(x_{*})=-v(x_{*})\bigl(s(x_{*})-v(x_{*})\bigr),

and since
v(x_{*})>0
, we obtain
v(x_{*})=s(x_{*})
.

Substituting
v(x_{*})=s(x_{*})
into (
29
), we get

\gamma_{0}w^{\prime\prime}(x_{*})=s(x_{*})\bigl(w(x_{*})-s^{\prime}(x_{*})\bigr).

Because
w(x_{*})>0
and
s^{\prime}(x_{*})\leq 0
(as
s
is decreasing on
(0,\ell)
), the right-hand side is strictly positive.
Thus
w^{\prime\prime}(x_{*})>0
, contradicting
w^{\prime\prime}(x_{*})\leq 0
.
Therefore,
E
must be empty, and hence
v^{\prime}(x)=w(x)\leq 0
for all
x\in(0,\ell)
.

Since
v
is even and nonincreasing on
[0,\ell]
, it attains its maximum at
x=0
.
∎

We prove a no-lingering result for small
\alpha
.

Theorem 3.7
.

For
0<\theta<1
, let
\gamma\in C^{2+\theta}([0,\infty))
. Assume that
s\in C^{1+\theta}([-\ell,\ell])
is even and positive, decreasing on
(0,\ell)
. Let
\alpha,\mu\geq 0
. Let
u(x;\alpha)
be a solution of (
27
). If
s^{\prime\prime}(0)<0
, then there exists small
\alpha^{*}>0
such that
u(0;\alpha)<s(0)
for all
0<\alpha\leq\alpha^{*}
. In addition,

\int_{-\ell}^{\ell}u(x;\alpha)dx>\int_{-\ell}^{\ell}s(x)dx

for such
\alpha
.

Proof.

Suppose that
s^{\prime\prime}(0)<0
.
Let
\alpha=0
and denote the corresponding positive solution
u_{0}
, that is,

\left\{\begin{aligned} &0=\gamma(0)u_{0}^{\prime\prime}+u_{0}(s-u_{0})\\
&u_{0}^{\prime}(\pm\ell)=0.\end{aligned}\right.

(30)

Then
u_{0}
is even, positive, and decreasing in
(0,\ell)
by Lemma
3.6
. Since
u_{0}
has the maximum at
x=0
,

u_{0}(0)(s(0)-u_{0}(0))=-\gamma(0)u_{0}^{\prime\prime}(0)\geq 0\implies u_{0}(0)\leq s(0).

If
u_{0}(0)=s(0)
, then
u_{0}^{\prime\prime}(0)=0
.
Let
z=u_{0}-s
.
Then
z
satisfies

\gamma_{0}z^{\prime\prime}-u_{0}z=-\gamma_{0}s^{\prime\prime}\text{ and }z(0)=0,\,z^{\prime}(0)=0,

hence
z^{\prime\prime}(0)=-s^{\prime\prime}(0)>0
. We obtain that there exists a small
\delta>0
such that
z(x)>0
for all
x\in(0,\delta)
, hence
u_{0}(x)>s(x)
for all
x\in(0,\delta)
. From (
30
), for
x\in(0,\delta)
,

u_{0}^{\prime\prime}(x)=\frac{u_{0}(x)}{\gamma_{0}}(u_{0}(x)-s(x))>0.

However, since
u_{0}^{\prime}(0)=0
and
u_{0}
is decreasing, for
x\in(0,\delta)
,

u^{\prime\prime}(\xi)=\frac{u_{0}^{\prime}(x)-u_{0}^{\prime}(0)}{x-0}=\frac{u_{0}^{\prime}(x)}{x}\leq 0\quad\text{ for some }\xi\in(0,x).

This is a contradiction. Therefore,
u_{0}(0)<s(0)
.
The last conclusion for
\alpha=0
follows from
[
21
, Theorem 1.2]
.

Now it suffices to show that for sufficiently small
\alpha
,
u_{\alpha}\to u_{0}
in
C^{2+\theta}(\overline{\Omega})
as
\alpha\to 0
. For
\alpha>0
, define an operator

F(\alpha,v)=\begin{pmatrix}F_{1}(\alpha,v)\\
F_{2}(\alpha,v)\\
F_{3}(\alpha,v)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}(\gamma(\phi)v)^{\prime\prime}+v(s-v)\\
(\gamma(\phi)v)^{\prime}(-\ell)\\
(\gamma(\phi)v)^{\prime}(\ell)\end{pmatrix}

mapping
\mathbb{R}^{+}\cup\{0\}\times C^{2+\theta}([-\ell,\ell])\to C^{\theta}([-\ell,\ell])\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
, where
\phi=\alpha\bar{s}\frac{v}{v+\mu}
. Consider the Frechet derivative of
F
with respect to
v
at
(0,u_{0})

D_{v}F(0,u_{0})(v)=\begin{pmatrix}\gamma(0)v^{\prime\prime}+(s-2u_{0})v\\
\gamma(0)v^{\prime}(-\ell)\\
\gamma(0)v^{\prime}(\ell)\end{pmatrix}

Standard elliptic theory (
[
4
,
13
,
21
]
) implies that
D_{v}F(0,u_{0})
is a bounded Fredholm operator of index zero from
C^{2+\theta}([-\ell,\ell])\to C^{\theta}([-\ell,\ell])\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
. Moreover, any positive solution
u_{0}
of (
30
) is nondegenerate, that is,

L\psi:=\gamma(0)\psi^{\prime\prime}+(s-2u_{0})\psi=0,\quad\psi^{\prime}(\pm\ell)=0

admits only the trivial solution
\psi\equiv 0
(i.e.
0\notin\sigma(L)
). Thus,
D_{v}F(0,u_{0})
is an isomorphism. Therefore, by the inverse function theorem, there exists
\alpha^{*}>0
and a
C^{1}
branch
\alpha\mapsto u_{\alpha}
such that

F(\alpha,u_{\alpha})=0
for
0\leq\alpha<\alpha^{*}
and
u_{\alpha}\to u_{0}
in
C^{2+\theta}([-\ell,\ell])
as
\alpha\to 0

3.4
Memory regimes and population size trade-offs

When the random diffusive–logistic model (
12
) is considered, that is, when
\gamma(z)\equiv\gamma>0
for some positive constant
\gamma
, it is well known that if
s(x)
is non-constant, bounded, measurable, and nonnegative for all

satisfies

\int_{\Omega}u(x)\,dx>\int_{\Omega}s(x)\,dx

for all

When it comes to our cognitive movement model (

To illustrate these effects, we consider representative choices of

4
Discussion

In this work, we propose a population model with the Fokker–Planck type diffusion in which movement depends on a cognitive map
m(x,t)
constructed from nonlocal perception. We observed that nonlocal perception can cause spatial heterogeneity in cognitive maps and in the population density, particularly near the boundary of the domain. We identified a lingering phenomenon on heterogeneous cognitive maps, established the existence and stability of steady states for the logistic version of the model, and used simulations to show that lingering arises even in the presence of population growth.

Our results suggest that once the cognitive map is spatially non-uniform, high learning and moderate forgetting rates can create long-term concentration of individuals near the highest locations of the environment, even when the underlying resource landscape is nearly homogeneous. The cognitive diffusion
\Delta(\gamma(m)u)
with a cognitive map
m
effectively reduces movement in regions that are strongly represented in the cognitive map and thus generates lingering around high-valued memory peaks. When logistic growth is included, the learning rate
\alpha
and the forgetting rate
\mu
control a trade-off between strong lingering and total population size. For large
\alpha
, we found that an intermediate forgetting rate maximizes the peak density at the resource, whereas a larger
\mu
maximises the total mass. Very strong memory traps individuals in overcrowded patches, so the regime that maximizes lingering does not maximize population size. Hence the best strategy for population size is fast learning and reasonably fast forgetting, as to be able to adapt to a heterogeneous environment.

For constant motility
\gamma(z)\equiv\gamma
, our model reduces to the classical random diffusive–logistic equation, for which the total mass of the steady state always exceeds the total resource
[
21
]
. Our simulations show that this property can fail under cognitive diffusion: near the strongest-lingering regime, the total mass may drop below that of the landscape
s
. This reduction is also investigated in recent studies of resource-dependent dispersal
[
29
]
, where the fast decaying function
\gamma(z)
and local information can also lead to a reduction in total population size. In this sense, our work extends the theory of optimal population size from resource-dependent diffusion to memory-dependent diffusion.

This study has several mathematical limitations.
For the full model (
11
) we proved local well-posedness but we did not establish global-in-time existence of solutions.
The lack of diffusion in the
m
-equation and the strong coupling in the full system make a direct global existence theory difficult, and this remains an open problem.
In addition, our rigorous results on the lingering phenomenon concern the model without logistic growth and in the logistic case we rely on numerical simulations.
For the logistic model, we obtained partial results on lingering and total population size, mainly in the regime of small learning rates
\alpha
and a complete description for general
(\alpha,\mu)
is left for future work.

A natural question that arises from this work is an analysis of evolutionary benefits of learning and forgetting. We saw that fast learners and moderate forgetters do best in a heterogeneous environment. Hence next we can look at a competition model where species with different cognitive abilities compete for resources. We expect some interesting insights from such a study, and we leave it for future research.
