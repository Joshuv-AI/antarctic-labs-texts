Assessment of sunscreen knowledge, attitudes, and practices among nursing students in Kuwait
Authors: Seham Mustafa, Bashaer Baron
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Arabian Gulf countries, including Kuwait, have intense sunlight that poses substantial risks to skin health due to harmful ultraviolet (UV) radiation, leading to skin damage, premature aging, and increased cancer risks. This study assessed sunscreen knowledge, attitudes, and practices among nursing students, who are key agents in promoting health behaviors. Given that Kuwait has a single College of Nursing that attracts students from across the country, the sample represents the broader population.MATERIALS AND METHODS:A cross-sectional survey was conducted among 446 nursing students (50% male, 50% female), which is the quarter number of all the students from the College of Nursing in Kuwait between July and October 2024.RESULTS:Gender differences were notable. Females exhibited a superior understanding of sunscreen’s protective properties, including UVA/UVB coverage (75% vs 30% for males) and SPF effectiveness (72% vs 28% for males). Additionally, 41% of females used sunscreen year-round compared to 11% of males, who primarily used it during summer.CONCLUSION:Nursing students are pivotal in public health education but exhibit gaps in sunscreen knowledge and practices, with females demonstrating higher awareness than males. Gender-sensitive educational strategies in nursing curricula, alongside public health campaigns, should address misconceptions about sunscreen use and emphasize sun protection. Enhancing nursing students’ understanding of sunscreen use equips them to advocate for sun protection practices, potentially reducing skin cancer risk and improving public health outcomes locally and globally.

Introduction

In Arabian Gulf countries including Kuwait, the abundant sunshine and intense ultraviolet (UV) radiation pose significant challenges to skin health. With extended periods of intense sunlight throughout the year, the populace faces increased susceptibility to the adverse effects of UV radiation. This exposure has been linked to both immediate and long-term alterations in skin structure. Short-term effects include erythema (sunburns), followed by heightened melanin production and skin darkening (tanning).[12345] However, repeated exposure leads to irreversible loss of skin elasticity and elevates the risk of developing various skin cancers, including melanomas and nonmelanomas.[6] In 2020, an estimated 325,000 new melanoma cases and 57,000 deaths were reported worldwide.[7]

Scientific understanding identifies UV light as a primary contributor to skin damage. The solar spectrum includes UVB and UVA rays, capable of both penetrating Earth’s atmosphere and impacting the skin. UVB radiation primarily induces sunburns, triggering melanin production and epidermal thickening, resulting in tanning. Conversely, UVA rays penetrate deeper, causing long-term damage manifested as skin aging, including loss of elasticity and wrinkles.[8] The awareness of these risks has led to a growing emphasis on sun protection measures, particularly the use of sunscreens. Sunscreens act as protective barriers against UV rays, mitigating their harmful effects on the skin. Factors such as the Sun Protection Factor (SPF) and the distinction between physical and chemical sunscreens hold significance. SPF measures sunscreen’s ability to prevent erythema upon UV radiation exposure.[9] Physical sunscreens reflect both UVA and UVB light, using ingredients like titanium dioxide and zinc oxide, suitable for those allergic to chemical sunscreens. Chemical sunscreens absorb UV rays, using ingredients like avobenzone and benzophenone. Some chemical sunscreens offer broad-spectrum protection, including recent innovations like mexoryl.[10]

Studies have demonstrated the efficacy of sunscreens in reducing skin cancers, such as melanoma and squamous cell carcinoma.[111213] Regular and appropriate application of sunscreens is associated with decreased skin damage and reduced skin cancer risk,[14151617181920] underscoring their crucial role in safeguarding skin health in regions with high UV exposure like Kuwait.

Our research centers on examining sunscreen knowledge, attitudes, and practices among nursing students, acknowledging their pivotal role in disseminating knowledge to broader populations. This study aligns with ongoing initiatives advocating for sunscreen use as a vital element in protecting skin health against intense sunlight and UV radiation in Kuwait. The study aims to evaluate knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding sunscreen usage, estimate sun protection levels, and provides awareness of harmful solar radiation effects among nursing students. By comparing male and female students, this investigation aims to highlight the importance of their knowledge in promoting sun protection measures, improving sunscreen use practices.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

The research employs a cross-sectional design, employing a questionnaire administered online to male and female nursing students at The Public Authority for Applied Education and Training’s Nursing College during the past 4 months (July–October 2024).

Study participants and sampling

The participants were male and female nursing students aged 18 and above at Nursing College, Public Authority for Applied Education and Training. The sample size is 446 students, which is equal to quarter the number of students in Nursing College (1800 students). Participant selection is based on an equal number of male and female students. The sample size was calculated based on the Krejci and Morgan Sample Size Determination Table (http://bit.ly/2BpJvor).

Data collection tool and technique

The cross-sectional design employs a questionnaire administered online to the students. The questionnaire undergoes translation into Arabic. The instrument was validated in a pilot test with 32 randomly selected students, not included in the main study, assessing its effectiveness.

The questionnaire probes into participants’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices concerning sunscreens, encompassing demographic details, general information, sunscreen advantages and disadvantages, attitudes toward usage, frequency and reasons for use or nonuse, sun protection factor awareness, and factors influencing their choices.

Ethical consideration

The study ensures participants comprehend their objectives and methods, guaranteeing confidentiality through verbal consent before ward. Ethical approval is secured by both the Department of Biomedical Sciences and the College of Nursing Research Committees.

Statistical analysis method

Data undergo statistical analysis via SPSS software (version 28; SPSS, Inc, Chicago, IL, USA). Chi-square and t-tests, as appropriate, will evaluate significance among categorical variables. The study sets a significant level of P < 0.05 to determine statistical relevance.

Knowledge about sun exposure and sunscreen

Table 2 assesses participants’ knowledge regarding sun exposure and sunscreen use. Most were identified as having intermediate skin color (52% females, 63% males), with light skin second most common (21% females, 33% males) and dark skin least common (1% females, 4% males), with no significant gender differences. A large number (53%) correctly noted that sunscreens protect against UVA and UVB rays, with females significantly more aware than males (75% vs 30%, P < 0.001). Conversely, 16% of females and 50% of males incorrectly responded ‘No’ (P < 0.001), while 9% of females and 20% of males were unsure (P < 0.005), indicating a gender disparity in knowledge. When questioned about UVA’s ability to cause wrinkles, 64% of females correctly responded “Yes,” compared to 43% of males (P < 0.001). A substantial number remained unsure (32% females vs 47% males, P < 0.001). Similarly, 75% of females and 62% of males correctly identified that UVB can cause sunburn, though 21% of females and 31% of males were uncertain. Regarding the Sun Protection Factor (SPF), 72% of females and 28% of males correctly understood that SPF indicates the level of sun protection (P < 0.001), while a notable proportion remained unsure (25% females vs 69% males, P < 0.001). Additionally, only a small fraction correctly acknowledged that lip balm can protect against sun rays (6% females vs 6% males), with the majority either believing it ineffective or being unsure (P < 0.001).

Knowledge about sunscreen effects

Table 3 examines participants’ knowledge about sunscreen effects. Most understood sunscreen’s role in cancer prevention, with 67% of females and 53% of males affirming this, though some were unsure (16% females, 30% males, P < 0.001). In preventing wrinkles, 68% of females and 44% of males correctly identified sunscreen’s benefits, while others remained unsure (17% females, 45% males, P < 0.001). Regarding vitamin D production, 22% of both genders believed that sunscreens block its natural production, with almost the same uncertainty among females and males (42% vs 49%). Concerns about unwanted reactions were prevalent, with 69% of females and 48% of males acknowledging that sunscreens can cause irritation (P < 0.001). Similarly, 57% of females and 47% of males recognized that sunscreens can cause redness. Misconceptions persisted as 22% of females and 21% of males believed that sunscreens can cause swelling, and 65% of females versus 29% of males thought that sunscreens lead to an oily face (P < 0.001). Misconceptions about the combination of Vitamin A (Retinol) with sunscreens increasing sun sensitivity were prevalent, with 55% of females and 79% of males being unsure (P < 0.001). Additionally, 42% of females and 44% of males were uncertain about the environmental impact of sunscreens dissolving in swimming pools.

Attitudes toward sun protection

Table 4 details students’ attitudes toward sun protection. Most agreed that using sunscreen (55%), sunglasses (50%), and avoiding midday sun (50%) were effective protective measures. Females showed higher agreement rates for sunscreen use (75% vs 36%, P < 0.001). Preferences for wearing hats or veils (41% vs 20%, P < 0.001) and long-sleeved clothing (43% vs 16%, P < 0.001) were also more common among females. Umbrella use was the least popular option among both genders (15% vs 18%). When asked about personal experiences, 43% of both genders reported having suffered from sunburn previously, and similarly, 76% of females and 70% of males had experienced suntan. Regarding the perception of suntan enhancing attractiveness, 31% of females and 41% of males agreed, while a majority disagreed (69% females vs 59% males). Regarding clothing choices during summer, 9% of both genders always wore dark clothes, with higher instances of often and sometimes wearing dark clothes among females (42% and 46%) compared to males (36% and 42%). A substantial majority correctly identified that dark clothes can absorb UV light (85% females vs 87% males).

Practices of sunscreen usage

Table 5 presents the actual sunscreen usage practices among the participants. Out of 446 participants, 303 provided responses regarding their sunscreen practices. Among these, 202 were female and 101 male students (50% of females; P < 0.001) are using sunscreens. When applying sunscreen, 36% of all students applied it to all uncovered body parts, with females (30%) slightly less than males (49%) engaging in this comprehensive practice (P < 0.001). Sunscreen application patterns on the face and on face and hands were (32, 38 for female and 27, 24 for male). Reapplication during the day was reported by 29% of all students, with females (31%) slightly higher than males (27%). In terms of frequency, 54% of all students believed sunscreen should be reapplied every 4 hours, (52% for female and 58% for male), followed by 27% every 3 hours (30% for female and 22% for male) and 19% every 2 hours (18% for female and 20% for male). Regarding SPF levels, 54% of all students preferred SPF 30-50, with females (55%) and males (51%) showing similar preferences. Only 9% of females and 31% of males were using sunscreens with SPF less than 30, while 36% of females and 18% of males opted for SPF levels above 50, indicating a potential area for educational improvement. Satisfaction with current sunscreen usage was high, with 73% of all students satisfied, slightly lower among females (72%) compared to males (74%).

The timing of sunscreen application varied, with 31% using it all year round, 34% during the summer, and 35% only before sun exposure. There is a significant difference between genders. The application all year round was 41% for females and 11% for males, P < 0.001. During summer season, it was 22% for females and 58% for males, P < 0.001. Barriers to sunscreen use were predominantly lack of time (43%) and unpleasant feeling (39%), followed by perceiving no benefit (9%) and high cost (9%). Males reported higher barriers related to unpleasant feelings and lack of time (41% and 45%) compared to females (35% and 38%, respectively). 21% of females found it expensive compared to only 3% of males. Despite these barriers, a high percentage of students would recommend sunscreen to family and friends, with 82% of all students agreeing, slightly higher among females (90%) compared to males (74%).

Factors influencing the choice of sunscreen included recommendations from dermatologists (65% females vs 72% males), pharmacists (32% females vs 32% males), media (30% females vs 25% males), friends (27% females vs 27% males), and beauty specialists (17% females vs 16% males).

Discussion

This study assessed sunscreen knowledge, attitudes, and practices among nursing students, who are key agents in promoting health behaviors. Given that Kuwait has a single College of Nursing that attracts students from across the country, the sample represents the broader population plus they have medical knowledge. Only one study investigated sun exposure, photoprotection, and vitamin D status within the general population in Kuwait.[21] In our study, female nursing students exhibited significantly greater knowledge about sunscreen’s protective effects compared to their male counterparts. For instance, 75% of females were aware that sunscreen protects against both UVA and UVB rays, compared to only 30% of males. This disparity likely reflects greater exposure to health information, may be through beauty and skincare channels emphasizing sunscreen’s role in preventing skin aging and UV-induced damage. Conversely, males demonstrated lower awareness of the harmful effects of UVA and UVB rays, with only 43% recognizing UVA’s role in wrinkle formation and 62% understanding UVB’s contribution to sunburn, compared to 64% and 75% of females, respectively. These findings highlight the need for targeted educational initiatives to address gender-specific gaps in knowledge and promote sun protection practices among males.

Kuwait’s low skin cancer incidence in 14 cases accounting for 0.32% of total cancer diagnoses in 2022 is attributed to intermediate skin tones (~60% of the population) and cultural factors such as conservative dress.[22] Many Kuwaiti women and men cover their entire bodies, while the population as a whole engages more in indoor activities due to the hot desert climate. However, the effectiveness of traditional clothing in blocking UV radiation is not well established. While the risk of skin cancer may be lower, UV exposure still poses significant health risks, including suntan, sunburn, wrinkles, and other skin damage. Public health efforts should focus on raising awareness about these risks and promoting preventative measures, such as proper sunscreen use.

The results indicated that female students were knowledgeable about the harmful consequences of sun exposure, including wrinkle, sunburn, irritation, redness, and skin cancer. Their levels of awareness were higher than male. There was good knowledge of the risks of skin cancer, as reported by 60% of our participants. In the current study, we focused on female and male within a specific age group (i.e. university students) because university students are at an age when sun prevention behaviors have proven to prevent skin aging and cancer. In addition, most students were concerned about skin damage, especially those who were exposed to sunlight at peak hours of sunshine. Female students were more likely to use sunscreen regularly (40% vs 9% of males); sunburn rates were similar across genders (43%), underscoring the need for consistent application and reapplication of sunscreen.

The findings from studies of Western countries revealed a higher level of awareness among university students regarding the link between sun exposure and skin cancer. For instance, a study conducted in the United States by Spradlin et al.[23] assessed college students’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices related to the hazards of sun exposure (492 students). The study found that while most participants recognized that sun exposure increased the risk of skin cancer, only 29% were able to accurately identify behaviors that could mitigate this risk. However, a study by Saridi et al.[16] involving 387 university students in Greece reported that 91.7% of participants were aware of the dangers of sun exposure, and 98.7% understood that prolonged unprotected exposure significantly heightened these risks. The primary adverse effects of sun radiation noted by the students included skin cancer (97.7%), skin aging (72.9%), allergies (30.5%), and vision impairment such as blindness (12.9%). In the regional area, Arabian Gulf countries, studies in Saudi Arabia by Almuqati et al.[19] Almutlaq et al.[20] and by Hafez et al.[24] for Saudi nonmedical students found low despite having knowledge and awareness, while the rate of sunscreen use was average.

In our population study, there was a misconception of the relationship between sunscreen and vitamin D production levels, and only 22% of students agreed that sunscreen products are not harmful to the vitamin D levels in their body. Males were more likely to engage in risky behaviors, such as considering a suntan attractive (41% of males vs 31% of females). Promoting alternative beauty ideals and education on sun exposure risks could help shift these attitudes, particularly in males. Both genders reported similar sunburn rates (43%), despite females’ more frequent use of sunscreen and other protective measures, suggesting the need for consistent application and reapplication. According to experts, sunscreen products are considered the first line of defense against the harmful effects of radiation.[17] The use of sunscreen products requires greater attention. Even those who actively engage in sun protection methods fall prey to misconceptions regarding the correct usage of sunscreen products. For example, approximately 22% of students never apply sunscreen products, and only 9% male admit to regularly applying, in contrast with 40% of female, while reapplying sunscreens were 27% in male and 31% in female. There is minimal difference in sunscreen usage between Kuwaitis (56%) and non-Kuwaitis (54%). The reasons for not using sunscreen were largely similar across both groups, with the exception of cost. Expensive sunscreen was cited as a barrier by 7% of Kuwaitis compared to 12% of non-Kuwaitis. It is known that topical sunscreens contain molecules that absorb, reflect, or scatter UV rays. SPF, or Sun Protection Factor, evaluates the in vivo protection level against UVB rays responsible for sunburn.[2914] For example, SPF 30 means it theoretically takes 30 times longer to burn with sunscreen than without. Sunscreens reduce skin cancer, sunburn, and photoaging risks, with higher SPF values providing enhanced protection. The American Academy of Dermatology recommends broad-spectrum sunscreens with SPF ≥30. While higher SPF numbers offer greater protection, the difference between SPF 30, SPF 50, and SPF 100 is minimal; SPF 30 blocks about 97% of UVB rays, SPF 50 around 98%, and SPF 100 approximately 99%. The students have no scientific information about SPF since 84% of them are using SPF more than 50 for protection from radiation, which is more chemicals on their skin. Misconceptions regarding SPF values were widespread, possibly due to the fact that higher SPF provides substantially greater protection. Avoiding sun in the middle day was also 50%. The use of veil was mentioned by females and caps for males were 30%. Addressing these barriers through targeted health education programs is essential.

Gender differences in barriers to sunscreen use were evident. Males more often cited lack of time (45%) and perceived sunscreen as unnecessary (11%), while females reported cost as a barrier (21%). Despite these differences, both genders expressed similar satisfaction with their sunscreen products (73%), though reapplication practices were poor (29% overall). This is especially concerning in Kuwait’s hot climate, where sweat and water exposure can reduce sunscreen effectiveness. Educational campaigns should emphasize the importance of reapplication every 2 hours, particularly after swimming or excessive sweating.

Limitations and recommendations

This study was limited to nursing students. Additionally, the survey did not account for SPF-containing makeup, which many female participants may use. Future studies should involve a larger and more diverse sample, including medical students from various backgrounds, investigating the effects of different SPF formulations on skin protection among students. Using various types of sunscreens would provide valuable insights. Additionally, the long-term impact of sun exposure and protective measures among healthcare students should be assessed.

Conclusion

This study focused on nursing students who have a great role in educating the public. The findings highlight notable gaps and inconsistencies in sun harmful effects and sunscreen knowledge and practices among nursing students in Kuwait, with significant gender differences. Females showed higher awareness and more consistent use of sunscreen compared to males. This underscores the need for gender-sensitive educational strategies within nursing curricula. Public health campaigns by Ministries of Health and Education and Media should address misconceptions with regard to the correct use of sunscreen products such as SPF, promote proper sunscreen application and reapplication, and emphasize the importance of comprehensive sun protection measures. To foster healthy preventive habits within society, it is essential to introduce education about the effects of sun exposure and protective measures at the primary school level through school nurses. Schools provide an existing framework and curriculum that can easily incorporate such health education. School nurses can collaborate with the local community to implement a health-promotion approach in schools and utilize this approach to enhance personal, family, and school environments. These efforts are essential for reducing UV-related health risks and improving skin health outcomes in Kuwait.
