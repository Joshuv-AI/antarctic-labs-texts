Abstract
. Refining an earlier result due to Hahlomaa, we provide a new Carleson-type condition for k k -regular sets in the Heisenberg group ℍ n \mathbb{H}^{n} to have big pieces of Lipschitz images of subsets of ℝ k \mathbb{R}^{k} for 1 ≤ k ≤ n 1\leq k\leq n . Our approach passes via the corona decompositions by normed spaces, recently introduced by Bate, Hyde, and Schul. Along the way, we prove implications between several notions of quantitative rectifiability for low-dimensional sets in ℍ n \mathbb{H}^{n} .

A new sufficient condition for BPLI

We provide a new sufficient Carleson-type condition for a set in
\mathbb{H}^{n}
to have
big pieces of Lipschitz images (BPLI)
from
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, for
1\leq k\leq n

[
19

as a corollary. Along the way, we introduce

and relate them to other notions of corona decompositions in the literature
[

didonato
,

[

[

with a family of concrete examples, namely

-regular sets in the non-Euclidean spaces
(\mathbb{H}^{n},d)

1\leq k\leq n

f:A\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}\to(\mathbb{H}^{n},d)
is locally Lipschitz with respect to the Euclidean distance on
\mathbb{H}^{n}

horizontal
with respect to the sub-Riemannian structure on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
are Lipschitz in the Heisenberg metric

[

In this setting, we find new sufficient

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. If
E\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
is a
k
-regular set with the properties
E\in\mathrm{GLem}(\beta_{1,\pi,A(2n,k)},2)
and
E\in\mathrm{WGL}(\beta_{\infty,\mathcal{V}_{k}})
, then
E
has big pieces of Lipschitz images of subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
.

This also holds with “Lipschitz” replaced by “bi-Lipschitz”.
The restriction to
k\leq n
is natural since
(\mathbb{H}^{n},d)
is purely
k
-unrectifiable in Federer’s sense for
k>n

[
1
,
30

. In the range
k>n
,
uniform
k
-rectifiability should be built on intrinsic Lipschitz graphs
[
18
]
instead of metric Lipschitz images from
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, and this is a direction which we do not pursue here.

The concepts in Theorem
1.1
will be properly introduced in Definitions
3.1
,
3.2
,
3.9
,
3.15
, and
3.19

weak geometric lemma

\mathrm{WGL}(\beta_{\infty,\mathcal{V}_{k}})
is a way of quantifying that, for each precision level
\varepsilon>0

E
stays
\varepsilon

k
-planes in all balls centered on
E
, except perhaps for a family of balls satisfying a Carleson packing condition. It is known in Euclidean spaces that a weak geometric lemma alone does not even imply rectifiability
[
13
, §20]
since it does not sufficiently control how much the best-approximating planes at a point can spin around as one considers smaller and smaller scales. In the proof of Theorem
1.1
, the
geometric lemma

\mathrm{GLem}(\beta_{1,\pi,A(2n,k)},2)
allow us to control the amount of spinning for certain approximating horizontal
k
-planes. Remarkably, unlike the coefficients
\beta_{\infty,\mathcal{V}_{k}}
, the
\beta_{1,\pi,A(2n,k)}(x,r)
-numbers, for

is approximated by

\mathbb{R}^{2n}

.

A few words about the proofs

Our proof of Theorem
1.1
follows Hahlomaa’s proof of
Corollary
1.2
, which is in turn modeled on David and Semmes’ work in Euclidean spaces
[
13
, (C3)
\Rightarrow
(C4)
\Rightarrow
(C6)]
. We focus here on describing the main new elements in our approach.

Although not explicitly stated in this form, Hahlomaa deduces
the BPLI property for
k
-regular sets
E\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
from
\mathrm{GLem}(\beta_{1,\mathcal{V}_{k}},2)
by passing via an intermediate
corona decomposition
. Roughly speaking, this condition means that a system of dyadic cubes on
E
can be divided into a family of bad cubes, of which there are not too many, and the remaining good cubes can be further partitioned into a forest
\mathcal{F}
of – again not too many – trees, so that the set
E
has good approximation properties from the perspective of each tree
\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{F}
. We define
corona decompositions by horizontal planes (P-C)
(Definition
3.30
) and adapt Hahlomaa’s proof to verify, for
1\leq k\leq n
, that a
k
-regular set
E
in
\mathbb{H}^{n}
with
\mathrm{GLem}(\beta_{1,\pi,A(2n,k)},2)
and
\mathrm{WGL}(\beta_{\infty,\mathcal{V}_{k}})
satisfies (P-C) (Theorem
5.1
). This corona decomposition is formally stronger than the one implicitly contained in
[
19
]
,
and we verify the crucial approximation property in Lemma
6.6
.
The reason why our weaker assumption in terms of the
\beta_{1,\pi,A(2n,k)}
-numbers suffices for (P-C) becomes visible in Lemma
6.9
and how this lemma is used to control the trees in a certain subfamily of
\mathcal{F}
. These are trees for which a large proportion of minimal cubes has children whose best approximating horizontal planes are significantly rotated compared to best approximating plane of the top cube
Q(\mathcal{S})
. The amount of rotation is related to properties of the image of
E
under the projection
\pi:\mathbb{H}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
,
\pi(z,t)=z
. This observation allows us to control the rotation using
E\in\mathrm{GLem}(\beta_{1,\pi,A(2n,k)},2)
. On the other hand, this assumption (or the stronger condition
E\in\mathrm{GLem}(\beta_{1,\pi,\mathcal{V}_{k}},2)
)
alone
does not imply BPLI, as can be seen by considering a
1
-regular subset of the vertical
t
-axis, which is mapped to a single point under the projection
\pi
(Example
5.2
). For this reason we need
\mathrm{WGL}(\beta_{\infty,\mathcal{V}_{k}})
to establish that
E
is a priori sufficiently horizontal.

Having deduced from
E\in\mathrm{GLem}(\beta_{1,\pi,A(2n,k)},2)
and
E\in\mathrm{WGL}(\beta_{\infty,\mathcal{V}_{k}})
that
E
satisfies the corona decomposition (P-C), we could conclude Theorem
1.1
by following the approach in
[
19
]
. However, to highlight that this last step is independent of the specific ambient structure of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
(unlike the first part of the proof), we apply the result in
[
6
]
instead. More precisely, we observe in Lemma
3.34
that (P-C) implies a
corona decomposition by normed spaces
, as introduced in
[
6
]
, and Theorem
1.1
then follows from
[
6
, Theorem B]
.

Connections with other corona decompositions.

In Lemmas
3.32
–
3.33
we prove:

Theorem 1.6
.

A
k
-regular set
E\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
(
1\leq k\leq n
) satisfies (P-C) if and only if it admits a
corona decomposition by intrinsic Lipschitz graphs (ILG-C)
.

The (ILG-C) property is a variant of the corona decomposition studied by David and Semmes
[
13
]
, where from the perspective of each tree
\mathcal{S}
, the set
E
is well approximated by an
intrinsic Lipschitz graph
with small intrinsic Lipschitz constant (in the sense of Franchi, Serapioni, and Serra Cassano
[
39
]
). In contrast, (P-C), and the related corona decompositions by normed spaces, are defined via mapping properties rather than approximation by sets. Through the equivalence of (P-C) and (ILG-C), our approach to Theorem
1.1
via (P-C)
yields several corollaries of independent interest. These can be seen as counterparts for classical results in the Euclidean theory of uniform rectifiability
[
13
]
.

Corollary 1.7

(Corollary of Theorems
1.6
and
5.1
)
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. If
E\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
is a
k
-regular set such that
E\in\mathrm{GLem}(\beta_{1,\pi,A(2n,k)},2)
and
E\in\mathrm{WGL}(\beta_{\infty,\mathcal{V}_{k}})
, then
E
has a corona decomposition by intrinsic Lipschitz graphs.

Corollary 1.8

(Corollary of Theorem
1.6
and
[
6
]
)
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. If
E\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
is a
k
-regular set satisfying a corona decomposition by intrinsic Lipschitz graphs, then
E
has big pieces of Lipschitz images of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
.

The (ILG-C) property can be seen as a set-theoretic version of corona decompositions that were studied for intrinsic Lipschitz functions on
1
-dimensional horizontal subgroups in
\mathbb{H}^{n}

[
FO
,
didonato
]
.
In
[

, §3.1.1]
, the first author and Orponen proved for

that such intrinsic Lipschitz functions admit corona decompositions by intrinsic Lipschitz functions with small Lipschitz constant, and applied this result to prove that certain singular integral operators are

-bounded on regular curves in
\mathbb{H}^{1}
.
We hope that Corollary
1.7
could find applications for the study of
k
-dimensional singular integral operators in
\mathbb{H}^{n}
for all
1\leq k\leq n
, and more generally, that the present paper will serve as a step towards developing a theory of quantitative (or
uniform
) rectifiability for low-dimensional sets in Heisenberg groups. We believe that this is a natural setting to apply and advance the results in
[
6
]
, as there is already a rich theory of

,

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Notation

We write
a\lesssim b
to denote the existence of an absolute constant
C>0
with
a\leq Cb
. When writing
a\lesssim_{\lambda}b
, we allow
C
to depend on
\lambda
. We write
a\sim b
if
a\lesssim b
and
b\lesssim a
.

2.2.
Heisenberg groups

We recall the definition of the Heisenberg group, and introduce objects related to its horizontal structure relevant for the study of low-dimensional subsets.

Definition 2.1

(Heisenberg group)
.

The
n
-th Heisenberg group

\mathbb{H}^{n}
is defined as the set
\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}
equipped with the group product

(2.1)

(z,t)\cdot(z^{\prime},t^{\prime})=(z+z^{\prime},t+t^{\prime}+\omega(z,z^{\prime}))\quad\text{ for }z,z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n},t,t^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}.

where

(2.2)

\omega(z,z^{\prime}):=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(z_{i}z_{n+i}^{\prime}-z_{n+i}z_{i}^{\prime})\quad\text{ for }z,z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}.

We equip
\mathbb{H}^{n}
with left invariant vector fields

X_{i}=\partial_{z_{i}}-\tfrac{1}{2}z_{n+i}\partial_{t},\quad 1\leq i\leq n,\quad X_{i}=\partial_{z_{i}}+\tfrac{1}{2}z_{i-n}\partial_{t},\quad n+1\leq i\leq 2n,\quad T=\partial_{t}.

Here
\partial_{z_{i}}
and
\partial_{t}
denote the coordinate vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}
, which may be interpreted as operators on differentiable functions. If
[\cdot,\cdot]
denotes the Lie bracket of vector fields, then
[X_{i},X_{n+i}]=T
, while all the other commutation relations are trivial. Thus
\mathbb{H}^{n}
is a Carnot group of step
2
.

Definition 2.2
.

A vector in
\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}
is
horizontal
at
p\in\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}
if it is a linear combination of the vectors
X_{i}(p)
,
1\leq i\leq 2n
.
An absolutely continuous curve
\gamma
in the Heisenberg group is
horizontal
if, at almost every point
t
, the derivative
\gamma^{\prime}(t)
is horizontal at
\gamma(t)
.

For every absolutely continuous curve
\gamma_{I}:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
, one can find
\gamma_{2n+1}:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}
so that
\gamma:=(\gamma_{I},\gamma_{2n+1}):[a,b]\to\mathbb{H}^{n}
is horizontal. If
\gamma_{2n+1}(a)
is prescribed, the component
\gamma_{2n+1}

is uniquely determined by the horizontality condition, and
\gamma
is called a
horizontal lift
of
\gamma_{I}
.
Specifically, for
n=1
, if the curve
\gamma_{I}
is closed and
\gamma
is one of its horizontal lifts, then the total change in height
\gamma_{3}(b)-\gamma_{3}(a)
equals the signed area enclosed by
\gamma_{I}
; see for instance
[
8
]
.

Definition 2.3
.

A vector subspace
V\subset\mathbb{R}^{2n}
is said to be
isotropic
if
\omega(z,z^{\prime})=0
for all
z,z^{\prime}\in V
, where
\omega:\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}^{2n}\to\mathbb{R}
is the form defined in (
2.2
).

For
1\leq k\leq n
,
we denote by
\mathcal{V}_{k}^{0}
the family of
k
-dimensional
horizontal subgroups
of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
. Every
V\in\mathcal{V}_{k}^{0}
is of the form
V=V_{I}\times\{0\}
for a
k
-dimensional isotropic subspace
V_{I}
of
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
.

We call a subset
V
of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
an
(affine)
k
-dimensional horizontal plane
if it can be written as
x\cdot V_{0}
for some
x\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
and
V_{0}\in\mathcal{V}_{k}^{0}
. We denote by
\mathcal{V}_{k}
the collection of all affine horizontal
k
-dimensional planes of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
.

The family of arbitrary affine
k
-dimensional planes in
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
will be denoted by
A(2n,k)
.

Definition 2.4

(Projection onto horizontal subgroups)
.

Let
V=V_{I}\times\{0\}\in\mathcal{V}_{k}^{0}
be a
k
-dimensional horizontal subgroup of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
, where
V_{I}
is a
k
-dimensional isotropic subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
. The
horizontal projection
onto
V
is defined by

P_{V}:\mathbb{H}^{n}\to V,\quad P_{V}(z,t):=(\pi_{V_{I}}(z),0),

where
\pi_{V_{I}}:\mathbb{R}^{2n}\to V_{I}
stands for the standard Euclidean orthogonal projection onto
V_{I}
.

From the structure of the group law (
2.1
), it easily follows that horizontal projections are group homomorphisms. For a more thorough discussion of these mappings and their role in geometric measure theory on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
, we refer the reader to
[
32
,
4
]
.

Definition 2.5

(Projection onto affine horizontal planes)
.

Let
V=x\cdot V_{0}\in\mathcal{V}_{k}
be a
k
-dimensional horizontal plane with
x\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
and
V_{0}\in\mathcal{V}_{k}^{0}
. Then the
horizontal projection
onto
V
is defined by

P_{V}:\mathbb{H}^{n}\to V,\quad P_{V}(y):=x\cdot P_{V_{0}}(x^{-1}\cdot y).

The affine horizontal projection
P_{V}
is well-defined
since it does not depend on the specific choice of the point
x\in V
; see
[
19
, §2]
or
[
FV2
, §4.1.2]
for this and other properties of
P_{V}
.

It will be convenient to introduce also other kind of projections, which we may call
Euclidean coordinate projections
: if
p=(z,t)\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
, we define
\pi:\mathbb{H}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
and
\pi_{t}:\mathbb{H}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
as

\pi(p)=\pi(z,t):=z,\qquad\pi_{t}(p)=\pi_{t}(z,t):=t.

We will denote by
|\cdot|_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}
, or simply by
|\cdot|
, the Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. The Euclidean distance is
d_{\mathrm{Eucl}}(z,z^{\prime})=|z-z^{\prime}|
for every
z,z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
In the Heisenberg group
\mathbb{H}^{n}
, we let
d(x,y):=\|y^{-1}\cdot x\|
to be the
Korányi distance
induced by the homogeneous
Korányi norm

(2.3)

\|(z,t)\|:=\sqrt[4]{|z|_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}^{4}+16\,t^{2}}.

For any
V\in\mathcal{V}_{k}
, the horizontal projection
P_{V}
is 1-Lipschitz with respect to the Korányi
distance. In addition, the space
(V,d)
is isometric to
(\mathbb{R}^{k},|\cdot|_{\mathbb{R}^{k}})
. For
s\geq 0,
we will denote by
\mathcal{H}^{s}
the
s
-dimensional Hausdorff measure associated to the Korányi distance
d
, obtained via classical Carathéodory construction. Hausdorff measures with respect to
d_{\mathrm{Eucl}}
will be denoted
\mathcal{H}^{s}_{\mathrm{Eucl}}
.
The closed ball in
(\mathbb{H}^{n},d)
with center
x\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
and radius
r>0
is denoted by
B(x,r)
, while
B^{m}(z,r)
or
B_{\mathrm{Eucl}}(z,r)
denotes a Euclidean ball in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. The diameter of a set
A\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
with respect to the Korányi distance is denoted by
\mathrm{diam}(A)
.

Definition 2.6
.

A linear map
\varphi:\mathbb{H}^{n}\to\mathbb{H}^{n}
is called a
rotation
if for all
z,z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
,
t,t^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\pi_{t}(\varphi(z,t))

\displaystyle=t,

\displaystyle\omega\left(\pi(\varphi(z,t)),\pi(\varphi(z^{\prime},t^{\prime}))\right)

\displaystyle=\omega(z,z^{\prime}),

\displaystyle|\pi(\varphi(z,t))-\pi(\varphi(z^{\prime},t^{\prime}))|

\displaystyle=|z-z^{\prime}|.

Any rotation is a group homomorphism and an isometry with respect to
d
of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
. Moreover, for any
V,W\in\mathcal{V}_{k}
there exists a rotation
\varphi
and a point
p\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
such that
W=p\cdot\varphi(V)
. Rotations are exactly those maps which can be expressed in coordinates as
\varphi(z,t)=(Az,t)
with
A\in SU(n)
. For more details, see, e.g.,
[
4
, Section 2]

If
V\in\mathcal{V}_{k}^{0}
is a
k
-dimensional horizontal subgroup of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
, the
complementary orthogonal subgroup
of
V
inside
\mathbb{H}^{n}
is defined as the (Euclidean)
(2n+1-k)
-dimensional orthogonal complement of
V
inside
\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}
, which is a normal subgroup of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
. If
W
denotes the complementary orthogonal subgroup of
V
, then we define
P_{W}:\mathbb{H}^{n}\to W
as
P_{W}(y):=(P_{V}(y))^{-1}\cdot y
.
The next definition is due to Franchi, Serapioni, and Serra Cassano
[
18
,
39
]
.

Definition 2.7

(Intrinsic Lipschitz graph)
.

Let
V\in\mathcal{V}_{k}^{0}
and let
W
be the corresponding complementary orthogonal vertical subgroup. A map
\phi:V\to W
is called
intrinsic Lipschitz
if there exists
L>0
such that

(2.4)

\|P_{W}\left(\Phi(v^{\prime})^{-1}\Phi(v)\right)\|\leq L\,\|P_{V}\left(\Phi(v^{\prime})^{-1}\Phi(v)\right)\|\quad\text{ for every }v,v^{\prime}\in V,

where
\Phi:V\to\mathbb{H}^{n}
is the graph map associated to
\phi
, defined by
\Phi(v):=v\cdot\phi(v)
for all
v\in V
.
The
intrinsic Lipschitz constant
of
\phi
is defined as the best constant
L
such that (
2.4
) holds.

A set
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
is a
k
-dimensional intrinsic Lipschitz graph
, for
1\leq k\leq n
, if there exists
V\in\mathcal{V}_{k}^{0}
and
and an intrinsic Lipschitz function
\phi:V\to W
such that
\Gamma=\Phi(V)
.

If
V
is a horizontal subgroup with orthogonal vertical subgroup
W
, and
\phi:V\to W
is intrinsic Lipschitz as in Definition
2.7
, then the associated graph map
\Phi:V\to\mathbb{H}^{n}
is metrically Lipschitz since we have
for all
v,v^{\prime}\in V
that

\displaystyle d(\Phi(v),\Phi(v^{\prime}))

\displaystyle\leq\|P_{V}\left(\Phi(v^{\prime})^{-1}\cdot\Phi(v)\right)\|+\|P_{W}\left(\Phi(v^{\prime})^{-1}\cdot\Phi(v)\right)\|\ \leq(1+L)\|P_{V}\left(\Phi(v^{\prime})^{-1}\cdot\Phi(v)\right)\|

(2.5)

\displaystyle=(1+L)\|(v^{\prime})^{-1}\cdot v\|=(1+L)d(v,v^{\prime}).

We will also need precise quantitative information in the opposite direction: knowing that a graph map
\Phi(v)=v\cdot\phi(v)
is metrically Lipschitz with constant close to
1
, we can deduce that the function
\phi:V\to W
is intrinsic Lipschitz with small constant.

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
V
be a
k
-dimensional horizontal subgroup of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
, with complementary orthogonal subgroup
W
. Let
\Phi:V\to\mathbb{H}^{n}
denote the graph map associated to a function
\phi:V\to W
. Suppose that
\Phi
is
(1+\eta)
-Lipschitz with respect to the Korányi metric for some
0<\eta\leq 1
. Then
\phi
is intrinsic Lipschitz and its intrinsic Lipschitz constant is at most
6\sqrt[4]{\eta}
.

Proof.

Consider
v,v^{\prime}\in V
. Our assumption tells us that

(2.6)

\|\Phi(v^{\prime})^{-1}\cdot\Phi(v)\|\leq(1+\eta)\|(v^{\prime})^{-1}\cdot v\|=(1+\eta)|\pi(v)-\pi(v^{\prime})|_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}},

and our goal is to prove that

(2.7)

\|P_{W}(\Phi(v^{\prime})^{-1}\cdot\Phi(v))\|\leq 6\sqrt[4]{\eta}\,\|P_{V}(\Phi(v^{\prime})^{-1}\cdot\Phi(v))\|=6\sqrt[4]{\eta}\,|\pi(v)-\pi(v^{\prime})|_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}.

From (
2.6
), we deduce that

\begin{split}\sqrt{|\pi(v)-\pi(v^{\prime})|^{2}_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}+|\pi(\phi(v))-\pi(\phi(v^{\prime}))|^{2}_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}}&=|\pi(\Phi(v^{\prime})^{-1}\cdot\Phi(v))|_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\leq\|\Phi(v^{\prime})^{-1}\cdot\Phi(v)\|\\
&\leq(1+\eta)|\pi(v)-\pi(v^{\prime})|_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}},\end{split}

which implies, squaring both sides and collecting terms,

(2.8)

|\pi(\phi(v))-\pi(\phi(v^{\prime}))|_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\leq 2\sqrt{\eta}\,|\pi(v)-\pi(v^{\prime})|_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}.

Again, by (
2.6
), we get that

\sqrt[4]{|\pi(v)-\pi(v^{\prime})|_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}^{4}+16|\pi_{t}(\Phi(v^{\prime})^{-1}\cdot\Phi(v))|^{2}}\leq\|\Phi(v^{\prime})^{-1}\cdot\Phi(v)\|\leq(1+\eta)|\pi(v)-\pi(v^{\prime})|_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}.

Similarly as before, this implies that

(2.9)

|\pi_{t}(\Phi(v^{\prime})^{-1}\cdot\Phi(v))|\leq\sqrt{\eta}|\pi(v)-\pi(v^{\prime})|_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}^{2}.

We now write
V=V_{I}\times\{0\}
and consequently
W=V_{I}^{\perp}\times\mathbb{R}
, where
V_{I}
is an isotropic subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
. Using the definitions of
P_{V}
and
P_{W}
, we can explicitly write

\begin{split}P_{W}(z,t)=(z-\pi_{V_{I}}(z),t-\omega(\pi_{V_{1}}(z),z))&=(\pi_{V_{I}^{\perp}}(z),t-\omega(\pi_{V_{I}}(z),\pi_{V_{I}^{\perp}}(z)))\\
&=(\pi_{V_{I}^{\perp}}(z),t)\cdot(0,-\omega(\pi_{V_{I}}(z),\pi_{V_{I}^{\perp}}(z))),\end{split}

where
\omega:\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}^{2n}\to\mathbb{R}
is the form appearing in the group law (
2.1
). Using this expression for
P_{W}
, we get by the triangular inequality

\begin{split}\|P_{W}(\Phi(v^{\prime})^{-1}\cdot\Phi(v))\|\leq&\left\|\big(\pi(\phi(v))-\pi(\phi(v^{\prime})),\pi_{t}(\Phi(v^{\prime})^{-1}\cdot\Phi(v))\big)\right\|\,+\\
&+\left\|\big(0,-\omega(\pi(v)-\pi(v^{\prime}),\pi(\phi(v))-\pi(\phi(v^{\prime})))\big)\right\|.\end{split}

Now we estimate the two terms on the right-hand side.

\left\|\big(\pi(\phi(v))-\pi(\phi(v^{\prime})),\pi_{t}(\Phi(v^{\prime})^{-1}\cdot\Phi(v))\big)\right\|\leq|\pi(\phi(v))-\pi(\phi(v^{\prime}))|_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}+2\sqrt{|\pi_{t}(\Phi(v^{\prime})^{-1}\cdot\Phi(v))|}.

Using (
2.8
) and (
2.9
), we deduce that

\left\|\big(\pi(\phi(v))-\pi(\phi(v^{\prime})),\pi_{t}(\Phi(v^{\prime})^{-1}\cdot\Phi(v))\big)\right\|\leq 4\sqrt[4]{\eta}|\pi(v)-\pi(v^{\prime})|_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}.

Concerning the other term,

\begin{split}\left\|\big(0,-\omega(\pi(v)-\pi(v^{\prime}),\pi(\phi(v))-\pi(\phi(v^{\prime})))\big)\right\|&=2\sqrt{|\omega(\pi(v)-\pi(v^{\prime}),\pi(\phi(v))-\pi(\phi(v^{\prime})))|}\\
&\leq 2\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}|\pi(v)-\pi(v^{\prime})|_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}|\pi(\phi(v))-\pi(\phi(v^{\prime}))|_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}}\\
&\leq 2\sqrt[4]{\eta}|\pi(v)-\pi(v^{\prime})|_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}},\end{split}

where we used (
2.8
) and the simple inequality
|\omega(z,z^{\prime})|\leq\frac{1}{2}|z|_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}|z^{\prime}|_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}
for every
z,z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
. Combining the previous inequalities, we finally get (
2.7
).
∎

3.
Quantitative notions of rectifiability for low-dimensional sets in
\mathbb{H}^{n}

We discuss quantitative conditions that express in a multi-scale fashion how well a low-dimensional set
E\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
is covered or approximated by “nice” objects such as Lipschitz images, intrinsic Lipschitz graphs, or horizontal planes. This intuition is made precise with the notions of
big pieces
(Section
3.1
),
geometric lemmas
(Section
3.2
) and
corona decompositions
(Section
3.3
), originating from the work of David and Semmes in Euclidean spaces
[
13
,
14
]
. Here we present new and known incarnations of these classical notions.

3.1.
Big pieces of Lipschitz images

Definition 3.1

(
s
-regular sets)
.

Let
s>0
. A set
E\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
is said to be
AD-
s
-regular
(or simply
s
-
regular
) if it is closed and there exists a constant
C_{E}>1
such that

(3.1)

C_{E}^{-1}r^{s}\leq\mathcal{H}^{s}(E\,\cap\,B(x,r))\leq C_{E}r^{s}\quad\text{ for every }x\in E,\,0<r\leq{\rm diam}(E),\,r<\infty.

We call the
regularity constant
of
E
the best possible constant
C_{E}
satisfying (
3.1
).

Definition 3.2
.

Let
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. We say that a
k
-regular set
E\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
has
big pieces of Lipschitz images of subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
(BPLI) if there exist constants
c,L>0
such that for every
x\in E
,
0<r<{\rm diam}(E)
there exist
A\subset B^{k}(0,r)
and an
L
-Lipschitz function
f:A\to\mathbb{H}^{n}
with the property that

(3.2)

\mathcal{H}^{k}\left(E\,\cap B(x,r)\cap f(A)\right)\geq cr^{k}.

Slightly different definitions are used in the literature, but in our setting they turn out to be all equivalent.
First, by the Lipschitz extension result
[
41
]
, any Lipschitz function
f:A\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{H}^{n}
can be extended on the full
\mathbb{R}^{k}
with controlled Lipschitz constant. Notice that this is possible exactly when
k\leq n
(see
[
5
]
). Hence it is equivalent to consider functions defined on
B^{k}(0,r)
or on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, and replace in (
3.2
) the set
f(A)
by
f(B^{k}(0,r))
.
Remarkably, this is further equivalent to the condition where
f:A\subset B^{k}(0,r)\to\mathbb{H}^{n}
is required to be
bilipschitz
(by
[
38
, Corollary 1.9]
), as in
[
19
, Theorem 1.1]
. In particular, Theorem
1.1
and its corollaries in Section
1
all hold with the conclusion “big pieces of Lipschitz images” replaced by “big pieces of bi-Lipschitz images” in the sense of
[
19
, p.1]
; see also
[
6
, §1.2]
. We decided to focus on Definition
3.2
since in
[
6
]
, BPLI is taken as a definition of uniform rectifiability in metric spaces.

Because a Lipschitz function
f:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to(\mathbb{H}^{n},d)
is locally Euclidean Lipschitz, while the converse is not true, proving that a set
E\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
satisfies BPLI is not just a Euclidean result. Consequently, sufficient conditions for BPLI in
\mathbb{H}^{n}
, as introduced in the following, have to take into account the horizontal structure and cannot be mere copies of
the conditions in
[
13
]
.

3.2.
Geometric lemmas

For any
E\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
and
x\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
we denote by

d(x,E):=\inf\,\{d(x,y):y\in E\}

the distance between the point
x
and the set
E
.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
E\subset\mathbb{H}^{n},k\in\{1,\ldots,n\},p\geq 1,x\in\mathbb{H}^{n},r>0
. We define

\displaystyle\beta_{p,\mathcal{V}_{k}}(x,r)=\beta^{E}_{p,\mathcal{V}_{k}}(x,r)

\displaystyle:=\inf_{V\in\mathcal{V}_{k}}\left(r^{-k}\int_{E\cap B(x,r)}\left(\frac{d(y,V)}{r}\right)^{p}\,d\mathcal{H}^{k}(y)\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},\quad p<\infty

\displaystyle\beta_{\infty,\mathcal{V}_{k}}(x,r)=\beta^{E}_{\infty,\mathcal{V}_{k}}(x,r)

\displaystyle:=\inf_{V\in\mathcal{V}_{k}}\sup_{y\in E\cap B(x,r)}\frac{d(y,V)}{r}.

Definition 3.4
.

Let
E\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
be AD-
k
-regular,
1\leq p\leq\infty
,
1\leq q<+\infty
. We say that
E
satisfies the
q
-geometric lemma with respect to
\beta_{p,\mathcal{V}_{k}}
, and we write
E\in{\rm GLem}(\beta_{p,\mathcal{V}_{k}},q)
if there exists
C>0
such that for every
R>0
and every
x\in E
it holds

(3.3)

\int_{0}^{R}\int_{E\cap B(x,R)}\beta_{p,\mathcal{V}_{k}}(y,r)^{q}\,d\mathcal{H}^{k}(y)\frac{dr}{r}\leq CR^{k}.

Condition (
3.3
) is one way of expressing that the set
E
is well approximated by horizontal planes at most places and scales. This is equivalent to saying that
\beta_{p,\mathcal{V}_{k}}(y,r)^{q}d\mathcal{H}^{k}(y)\,\frac{dr}{r}
is a Carleson measure on
E\times\mathbb{R}^{+}
.
Some of the results we will use have been stated in terms of different versions of geometric lemmas, so we review the relevant definitions here. Instead of using the double integral in (
3.3
), geometric lemmas can be formulated with the help of

systems of dyadic cubes
.

3.2.1.
Carleson conditions for dyadic cubes

The following is a special case of a construction due to Christ
[
12
]
, with minor modifications that we will indicate below.

Definition and Theorem 3.5

(Dyadic cubes)
.

Assume that
E\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
is a
k
-regular set with regularity constant
C_{E}
.
Then there exist constants
\varrho\in(0,1)
and
D\in(1,\infty)
, depending only on
C_{E}
and
k
, and a collection
\mathcal{D}=\cup_{j\in\mathbb{J}}\mathcal{D}_{j}
where, for every
j\in\mathbb{J}
,
\mathcal{D}_{j}
is a family of pairwise disjoint Borel sets and

(1)

For each
j\in\mathbb{J}
,
E=\bigcup_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{j}}Q
.

(2)

If
Q_{1},Q_{2}\in\mathcal{D}
and
Q_{1}\cap Q_{2}\neq\emptyset
, then
Q_{1}\subseteq Q_{2}
or
Q_{2}\subset Q_{1}
.

(3)

If
j\in\mathbb{J}
and
Q\in\mathcal{D}_{j}
, then
\mathrm{diam}(Q)\leq D\varrho^{j}
.

(4)

For each
j\in\mathbb{J}
and
Q\in\mathcal{D}_{j}
, there exists
x_{Q}\in E
such that
B(x_{Q},D^{-1}\varrho^{j})\cap E\subset Q
.

(5)

\mathcal{H}^{k}(\{x\in Q:\,d(x,E\setminus Q)\leq\eta\varrho^{j}\})\leq D\eta^{\frac{1}{D}}\mathcal{H}^{k}(Q)
for all
j\in\mathbb{J}
,
Q\in\mathcal{D}_{j}
,
\eta>0
.

Here
\mathbb{J}=\mathbb{Z}
if
E
is unbounded, and
\mathbb{J}=\{j\in\mathbb{Z}:\,j\geq J_{0}\}
where
J_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}
is such that
\varrho^{J_{0}+1}\leq\mathrm{diam}(E)<\varrho^{J_{0}}
otherwise.

We also define

\mathcal{D}_{Q_{0}}:=\{Q\in\mathcal{D}:\,Q\subset Q_{0}\},\quad Q_{0}\in\mathcal{D},

and for a given constant
\lambda>1
, we set

\lambda Q:=\{x\in E:\,d(x,Q)\leq(\lambda-1)\mathrm{diam}(Q)\}.

Choosing
D
large enough, we may and will assume that

(3.4)

D^{-1}\varrho^{j}\leq\mathrm{diam}(Q)\leq D\varrho^{j}\quad\text{and}\quad D^{-1}\varrho^{jk}\leq\mathcal{H}^{k}(Q)\leq D\varrho^{jk},\quad Q\in\mathcal{D}_{j},\,j\in\mathbb{J},

cf.
[
19
, (14)]
or similar computations leading to
[
FV1
, (2.8)]
.
If
Q\in\mathcal{D}_{j}
for some
j\in\mathbb{J}
, we denote by
\mathcal{C}(Q)
the set of
children
of
Q
, namely
\mathcal{C}(Q):=\{R\in\mathcal{D}_{j+1}:R\subset Q\}
.
Also, for
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathcal{D}
, we let
\min(\mathcal{S})
to be the family of minimal (with respect to the inclusion) cubes in
\mathcal{S}
.

Remark 3.6
.

The cubes in Definition and Theorem
3.5
are essentially as in
[
19
, (8)–(14)]
with
\varrho=1/\alpha
and
\mathcal{D}_{j}
playing the role of cubes in generation “
-j
”.
The main difference is that we assume that the cubes of a fixed generation cover

entirely, not only up to a
\mathcal{H}^{k}
-null set. The existence of such cubes can be proven by modifying Christ’s original construction as explained in
[

, Section 4]
, see also
[

, Lemma 2.6.1]
. The resulting cubes are not necessarily open, but Borel sets, which is sufficient to run the argument in
[

]
.

Remark 3.7
.

Given dyadic cubes as in Definition and Theorem
3.5
for some
\varrho\in(0,1)
, one can always construct a new family that satisfies the same properties with “
\varrho
” replaced by “

” and some of the constants possibly depending on

. In particular, all

-regular sets in

admit dyadic systems with the properties stated in

.
We did not to specify the value of

in order to have a more flexible definition that allows us to apply results stated in the literature for various values of

.

Remark 3.8
.

The dyadic systems used in

We will compare geometric lemmas for different coefficient functions that we now introduce. We recall from Definition

Definition 3.9

(Coefficient functions)
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

,

, and

•

\displaystyle\beta_{p,\mathcal{V}_{k}}(\lambda Q)

\displaystyle:=\inf_{V\in\mathcal{V}_{k}}\left(\fint_{\lambda Q}\left(\frac{d(y,V)}{{\rm diam}(\lambda Q)}\right)^{p}\,d\mathcal{H}^{k}(y)\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},\quad p<\infty,

\displaystyle\beta_{\infty,\mathcal{V}_{k}}(\lambda Q)

\displaystyle:=\inf_{V\in\mathcal{V}_{k}}\,\sup_{y\in\lambda Q}\frac{d(y,V)}{{\rm diam}(\lambda Q)}

•

the

\beta

Remark 3.11

The horizontal projection

Lemma 3.12
.

For any
x,y,z\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
,
V\in\mathcal{V}_{k}
it holds

|\pi(x)-\pi(y)|=|\pi(z\cdot x)-\pi(z\cdot y)|,\quad d(P_{V}(x),P_{V}(y))=d(P_{z\cdot V}(z\cdot x),P_{z\cdot V}(z\cdot y)).

Moreover, if
z\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
and
E
is a
k
-regular set with a dyadic system
\mathcal{D}
, then
\{z\cdot Q\}
is a dyadic system for the
k
-regular set
z\cdot E
and, for all
p\in[1,\infty]
,
\lambda\geq 1,Q\in\mathcal{D}
, we have the following identities between the coefficient functions associated to
E
and
z\cdot E
, respectively:

\widehat{\beta}_{p,\mathcal{V}_{k}}(\lambda Q)=\widehat{\beta}_{p,\mathcal{V}_{k}}(\lambda(z\cdot Q)),\;\beta_{p,\pi,A(2n,k)}(\lambda Q)=\beta_{p,\pi,A(2n,k)}(\lambda(z\cdot Q)),\;\iota_{p,\mathcal{V}_{k}}(\lambda Q)=\iota_{p,\mathcal{V}_{k}}(\lambda(z\cdot Q)).

Proof.

The claim about the dyadic systems holds due to the left-invariance of
d
.
The property
|\pi(x)-\pi(y)|=|\pi(z\cdot x)-\pi(z\cdot y)|
follows immediately from the structure of the group law and the invariance under translations of the Euclidean distance.
Together with the left-invariance of
d
and the resulting left-invariance of
\mathcal{H}^{k}
, it implies the claimed left-invariance property of the
\widehat{\beta}
-numbers and of the
\beta_{\pi}
-numbers, observing also that
\mathcal{V}_{k}=\{z\cdot V:\,V\in\mathcal{V}_{k}\}
for all
z\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
, and
A(2n,k)=\{u+W:\,W\in A(2n,k)\}
for all
u\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
.

For the second property, recall from Definition
2.5
that if
V=q\cdot V_{0}
with
V_{0}
isotropic, then
P_{V}(x)=q\cdot P_{V_{0}}(q^{-1}\cdot x)
. This implies that
P_{z\cdot V}(z\cdot x)=z\,q\cdot P_{V_{0}}(q^{-1}z^{-1}z\,x)=z\cdot P_{V}(x).
In particular we get that

(3.5)

d(P_{V}(x),P_{V}(y))=d(z\cdot P_{V}(x),z\cdot P_{V}(y))=d(P_{z\cdot V}(z\cdot x),P_{z\cdot V}(z\cdot y)).

This identity, together with the left-invariance properties of
d
and
\mathcal{H}^{k}
, implies the claimed left-invariance property for the
\iota
-numbers.
∎

Lemma 3.14
.

Let

with

Definition 3.15

(Geometric lemma)
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, and let
h
be one of the coefficient functions in Definition

.
Given

, a
k
-regular set

\mathcal{D}
and

, we say that

q
-geometric lemma with respect to

such that

(3.6)

\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{Q_{0}}}h(\lambda Q)^{q}\,\mathcal{H}^{k}(Q)\leq C\mathcal{H}^{k}(Q_{0}),\quad Q_{0}\in\mathcal{D}.

A few remarks are in order:

Dyadic systems are not unique, but if

The result for arbitrary

Remark 3.17

For

Lemma 3.18
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
,
q\in[1,+\infty)
, and let
h
be one of the coefficient functions in Definition
3.9
.
Then a
k
-regular set
E\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
satisfies
E\in\mathrm{GLem}(h,q)
in the sense of Definition
3.15
if and only if there exists
C>0
such that for any
R>0
and
x\in E
it holds

(3.7)

\int_{0}^{R}\int_{E\cap B(x,R)}h(y,r)^{q}\,d\mathcal{H}^{k}(y)\,\frac{dr}{r}\leq CR^{k}.

Since the argument is standard, we only sketch the proof.

Proof.

Assume first that
E
satisfies (
3.7
). Let
\mathcal{D}
be a dyadic system on
E
,
\lambda>1
, write
\mu=\mathcal{H}^{k}_{\llcorner}E
, and fix
Q_{0}\in\mathcal{D}
. Let
j_{0}
be such that
Q_{0}\in\mathcal{D}_{j_{0}}
. The coefficient function
h
has the following crucial property: there exists a constant
M>1
, depending only on
C_{E}
,
k
, and
\lambda
, such that
for all
Q\in\mathcal{D}_{j}
,
Q\subset Q_{0}
, we have

(3.8)

h(\lambda Q)\leq Mh(y,Mr),\quad y\in B(x_{Q},\varrho^{j})\cap E,\,r\in[\varrho^{j},\varrho^{j-1}].

This, and the
k
-regularity of
E
, allows us to bound the sum over dyadic cubes as follows:

\displaystyle\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{Q_{0}}}h(\lambda Q)^{q}\mu(Q)

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{j\geq j_{0}}\int_{\varrho^{j}}^{\varrho^{j-1}}\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{j}\cap\mathcal{D}_{Q_{0}}}\int_{B(x_{Q},\varrho^{j})}h(y,Mr)^{q}\,d\mu(y)\,\frac{dr}{r}

(3.9)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\geq j_{0}}\int_{\varrho^{j}}^{\varrho^{j-1}}\int_{B(x_{Q_{0}},C_{1}\varrho^{j_{0}})}h(y,Mr)^{q}\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{j}\cap\mathcal{D}_{Q_{0}}}\chi_{B(x_{Q},\varrho^{j})}(y)\,d\mu(y)\,\frac{dr}{r},

where we have used in the last step that there exists a constant
C_{1}>1
, depending only on
C_{E}
and
k
, such that
B(x_{Q},\varrho^{j})\subset B(x_{Q_{0}},C_{1}\varrho^{j_{0}})
for all
Q\in\mathcal{D}_{j}\cap\mathcal{D}_{Q_{0}}
. By the properties of dyadic systems (Definition and Theorem
3.5
), we have
d(x_{Q},x_{Q^{\prime}})\geq D^{-1}\varrho^{j}
for
Q,Q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}_{j}
. The
k
-regularity of
E
and a standard volume counting argument then imply that
\mathrm{card}(Q\in\mathcal{D}_{j}\cap\mathcal{D}_{Q_{0}}:\,y\in B(x_{Q},\varrho^{j}))\lesssim 1
for all
y\in E
. This finally allows to bound (
3.9
) by

\int_{0}^{\varrho^{j_{0}-1}}\int_{B(x_{Q_{0}},C_{1}\varrho^{j_{0}})}h(y,Mr)^{q}\,d\mu(y)\,\frac{dr}{r},

from where
E\in\mathrm{GLem}(h,q)
follows (after a change of variables in
r
) since
Q_{0}
was chosen arbitrarily. Here, the constant in
\mathrm{GLem}(h,q)
depends on the constant
C
in (
3.7
), as well as possibly on
C_{E}
,
k
, and
\lambda
.

In the opposite direction, assume that
E\in\mathrm{GLem}(h,q)
in the sense of Definition
3.15
. Let
\mathcal{D}
be a dyadic system on
E
, pick
x\in E
and
R>0
. Let
j_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}
be such that
\varrho^{j_{0}}\leq R<\varrho^{j_{0}-1}
. We begin by writing

(3.10)

\int_{0}^{R}\int_{B(x,R)}h(y,r)^{q}\,d\mu(y)\,\frac{dr}{r}\sim\sum_{j\geq j_{0}}\int_{\varrho^{j}}^{\varrho^{j-1}}\int_{B(x,R)}h(y,\varrho^{j-1})^{q}\,d\mu(y)\,\frac{dr}{r},

where we used that
h(y,r)\lesssim h(y,\varrho^{j-1})
for
r\in[\varrho^{j},\varrho^{j-1}]
thanks to
k
-regularity of
E
. Moreover, there exists
Q_{0}\in\mathcal{D}
such that
B(x,R)\subset KQ_{0}
with
R\sim{\rm diam}(Q_{0})
and
K
depending only on
k
and
C_{E}
, see e.g.,
[
FV1
, (2.9)]
. We cover
KQ_{0}
with a number
m\lesssim 1
of cubes
Q_{0,1},\ldots,Q_{0,m}
from generation

as in
[
FV1
, (2.12)]
. An inequality similar to (
3.8
) with the roles of balls and cubes reversed, and the assumption

(with Remark
3.17
) finally allow us to bound the expression in (

) by
\mu(Q_{0,1})+\cdots+\mu(Q_{0,m})
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Definition 3.19

(Weak geometric lemma)
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
.

k
-regular set

and

such that

(3.11)

\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{Q_{0}},h(\lambda Q)>\varepsilon}\mathcal{H}^{k}(Q)\leq C(\varepsilon,\lambda)\mathcal{H}^{k}(Q_{0}),\quad Q_{0}\in\mathcal{D}.

Clearly, if

. By isometrically embedding

. Moreover, since the example in

is not Euclidean rectifiable in

fails to be Euclidean rectifiable and, in particular, cannot have BPLI in the sense of Definition

An example in

that satisfies

(since it satisfies a strong geometric lemma with exponent

for

Proposition 3.22
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Assume that
E\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
is a
k
-regular set and
\mathcal{D}
a dyadic system on
E
. Then we have, for
\lambda\geq 1
,

\widehat{\beta}_{1,\mathcal{V}_{k}}(\lambda Q)^{4}\lesssim_{\lambda}\iota_{1,\mathcal{V}_{k}}(\lambda Q),\quad Q\in\mathcal{D}.

In particular, if

E\in\mathrm{GLem}(\iota_{1,\mathcal{V}_{k}},1)
, then
E\in\mathrm{GLem}(\widehat{\beta}_{1,\mathcal{V}_{k}},4)
.

Proof.

Let
E
be
k
-regular with a dyadic system
\mathcal{D}
. For simplicity, we denote
\mu:=\mathcal{H}^{k}\llcorner_{E}
.
Fix
\varepsilon>0
. Then, by definition of
\iota_{1,\mathcal{V}_{k}}(\lambda Q)
, there exists
V\in\mathcal{V}_{k}
such that

\fint_{\lambda Q}\fint_{\lambda Q}\frac{\left|d(x,y)-d(P_{V}(x),P_{V}(y))\right|}{\mathrm{diam}(\lambda Q)}\,d\mu(x)\,d\mu(y)<\iota_{1,\mathcal{V}_{k}}(\lambda Q)+\varepsilon.

Therefore there exists at least a point
z_{0}\in\lambda Q
such that

(3.12)

\fint_{\lambda Q}\frac{\left|d(z_{0},y)-d(P_{V}(z_{0}),P_{V}(y))\right|}{\mathrm{diam}(\lambda Q)}\,d\mu(y)<\iota_{1,\mathcal{V}_{k}}(\lambda Q)+\varepsilon.

By Lemma
3.12
it suffices to prove
\widehat{\beta}_{1,\mathcal{V}_{k}}(\lambda[z_{0}^{-1}\cdot Q])^{4}\lesssim\iota_{1,\mathcal{V}_{k}}(\lambda[z_{0}^{-1}\cdot Q])
, so without loss of generality, we will assume in the following that
z_{0}=0
. Moreover,
by Lemma
3.14
, we can assume that
V=V_{I}\times\{0\}\in\mathcal{V}_{k}^{0}
.
In this case
P_{V}(y)=(\pi_{V_{I}}(\pi(y)),0)
. Then for every
y\in\lambda Q

\displaystyle|d(z_{0},y)-|\pi_{V_{I}}(\pi(z_{0}))-\pi_{V_{I}}(\pi(y))||

\displaystyle=\|y\|-|\pi_{V_{I}}(\pi(y))|

\displaystyle\geq|\pi(y)|-|\pi_{V_{I}}(\pi(y))|

\displaystyle=\frac{|\pi(y)|^{2}-|\pi_{V_{I}}(\pi(y))|^{2}}{|\pi(y)|+|\pi_{V_{I}}(\pi(y))|}=\frac{|\pi_{V_{I}^{\bot}}(\pi(y))|^{2}}{|\pi(y)|+|\pi_{V_{I}}(\pi(y))|}\geq\frac{|\pi_{V_{I}^{\bot}}(\pi(y))|^{2}}{2\,\mathrm{diam}(\lambda Q)}

(3.13)

\displaystyle=\frac{d_{\mathrm{Eucl}}(\pi(y),V_{I})^{2}}{2\,\mathrm{diam}(\lambda Q)}.

This estimate will be useful for one of the two summands appearing in the definition of
\widehat{\beta}_{1,\mathcal{V}_{k}}(\lambda Q)
. For the other summand, we use a similar estimate.

Denoting
a:=\|y\|
and
b:=|\pi_{V_{I}}(\pi(y))|
, we have

|d(z_{0},y)-|\pi_{V_{I}}(\pi(z_{0}))-\pi_{V_{I}}(\pi(y))||=a-b=\frac{a^{4}-b^{4}}{a^{3}+a^{2}b+ab^{2}+b^{3}}\gtrsim\frac{a^{4}-b^{4}}{\mathrm{diam}(\lambda Q)^{3}}.

Here

\displaystyle a^{4}-b^{4}

\displaystyle=|\pi(y)|^{4}+16\pi_{t}(y)^{2}-|\pi_{V_{I}}(\pi(y))|^{4}

\displaystyle=(|\pi_{V_{I}}(\pi(y))|^{2}+|\pi_{V_{I}^{\bot}}(\pi(y))|^{2})^{2}+16\pi_{t}(y)^{2}-|\pi_{V_{I}}(\pi(y))|^{4}

\displaystyle=|\pi_{{V_{I}}^{\bot}}(\pi(y))|^{4}+2|\pi_{V_{I}}(\pi(y))|^{2}|\pi_{{V_{I}}^{\bot}}(\pi(y))|^{2}+16\pi_{t}(y)^{2}

\displaystyle\gtrsim\|P_{V}(y)^{-1}\cdot y\|^{4}\geq d(y,V)^{4},

where we used the fact that

\displaystyle\|P_{V}(y)^{-1}\cdot y\|^{4}

\displaystyle=|\pi(P_{V}(y)^{-1}\cdot y)|^{4}+16(\pi_{t}(P_{V}(y)^{-1}\cdot y))^{2}

\displaystyle=|\pi_{{V_{I}}^{\bot}}(\pi(y))|^{4}+16(\pi_{t}(y)-\omega(\pi_{V_{I}}(\pi(y)),\pi(y)))^{2}

\displaystyle\leq|\pi_{{V_{I}}^{\bot}}(\pi(y))|^{4}+32\pi_{t}(y)^{2}+8|\pi_{V_{I}}(\pi(y))|^{2}|\pi_{V_{I}^{\bot}}(\pi(y))|^{2},

where
\omega
is the form appearing in the group law (
2.1
).
Thus

(3.14)

|d(z_{0},y)-|\pi_{V_{I}}(\pi(z_{0}))-\pi_{V_{I}}(\pi(z))||\gtrsim\frac{d(y,V)^{4}}{\mathrm{diam}(\lambda Q)^{3}}.

Finally combining (
3.13
) and (
3.14
), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d(z_{0},y)-|\pi_{V_{I}}(\pi(z_{0}))-\pi_{V_{I}}(\pi(y))|}{\mathrm{diam}(\lambda Q)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\frac{d(z_{0},y)-|\pi_{V_{I}}(\pi(z_{0}))-\pi_{V_{I}}(\pi(y))|}{\mathrm{diam}(\lambda Q)}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{d(z_{0},y)-|\pi_{V_{I}}(\pi(z_{0}))-\pi_{V_{I}}(\pi(y))|}{\mathrm{diam}(\lambda Q)}

\displaystyle\gtrsim\frac{d_{\mathrm{Eucl}}(\pi(y),V_{I})^{2}}{\mathrm{diam}(\lambda Q)^{2}}+\frac{d(y,V)^{4}}{\mathrm{diam}(\lambda Q)^{4}}.

Recalling the definition of
\widehat{\beta}
, and applying Jensen’s inequality with
\varphi(x)=x^{2}
and
\varphi(x)=x^{4}
, respectively, we deduce that

\displaystyle\widehat{\beta}_{1,\mathcal{V}_{k}}(\lambda Q)^{4}

\displaystyle\leq\fint_{\lambda Q}\frac{\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{Eucl}}(\pi(y),V_{I})^{2}}{\mathrm{diam}(\lambda Q)^{2}}+\frac{d(y,V)^{4}}{{\rm diam}(\lambda Q)^{4}}\,d\mu(y)

\displaystyle\lesssim\fint_{\lambda Q}\frac{|d(z_{0},y)-|\pi_{V_{I}}(\pi(z_{0}))-\pi_{V_{I}}(\pi(y))||}{{\rm diam}(\lambda Q)}d\mu(y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq_z_0}}}{{\leq}}\iota_{1,\mathcal{V}_{k}}(\lambda Q)+\varepsilon.

We get the conclusion by the arbitrariness of
\varepsilon
.
∎

Proposition 3.23
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Assume that
E\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
is a
k
-regular set and
\mathcal{D}
a dyadic system on

. Then we have, for

,

(3.15)

\beta_{1,\pi,\mathcal{V}_{k}}(\lambda Q)^{2}+\beta_{1,\mathcal{V}_{k}}(\lambda Q)^{4}\leq\widehat{\beta}_{1,\mathcal{V}_{k}}(\lambda Q)^{4},\quad Q\in\mathcal{D}.

Moreover, if

Proof.

We recall from Definition

which immediately implies (

). Clearly, this also shows that if

. This follows by the same argument as in Euclidean spaces, which works irrespective of the integrability exponents; see

[

Multiresolution families

Definition 3.25

(Dyadic nets and multiresolution families)
.

[
schul
,
Juillet
]

Given
E\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
compact, a

in

\Delta=(\Delta_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}
of subsets of

(i)

\Delta_{j}\subset\Delta_{j+1}

(ii)

If

x_{1}=x_{2}

(iii)

If

.

For fixed dyadic net
(\Delta_{j})_{j}
in
E
,
1\leq p\leq+\infty
and

, let also

(3.17)

B^{\Delta}_{p,d}(E):=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}2^{-j}\sum_{x\in\Delta_{j}}\beta^{2}_{p,\mathcal{V}_{d}}(x,A\,2^{-j}),

for some constant
A>1
.
The corresponding

is the collection

\mathcal{G}:=\{B(x,A\,2^{-j}):x\in\Delta_{j},\,j\in\mathbb{Z}\}.

In Section

, we will need the following connection (for
k=1
) between a geometric lemma and a multiresolution family as in Definition
3.25
.

Lemma 3.26
.

If
E\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
is bounded and
k
-regular with
E\in{\rm GLem}(\beta_{p,\mathcal{V}_{k}},2)
, then
B_{p,k}^{\Delta}(E)<\infty
for every dyadic net in
E
.

The proof is standard, so we only sketch the idea. Let
\mathcal{D}
be a system of dyadic cubes as in Definition
3.5
. Recalling Remark
3.7
, we may assume that the conditions hold with
\varrho=1/2
, so that a cube of generation
j
satisfies
\mathrm{diam}(Q)\sim 2^{-j}
.
The idea for Lemma

is to assign to each
B\in\mathcal{G}
(as in Definition
3.25
) a cube
Q_{B}\in\mathcal{D}
so that
B\cap E\subset KQ_{B}
for a constant independent of
B
, and then bound the
\beta
-number of
B
in terms of the corresponding number for
KQ_{B}
. The same

might arise as

for several

, but this multiplicity can be controlled using the

-regularity of

and property (ii) of the dyadic net

3.3.
Corona decompositions

In this section, we define different versions of corona decompositions for
k
-regular sets in
\mathbb{H}^{n}
. All of them are based on the notion of
coronization
, which is an adaptation of the original definition by David and Semmes
[
14
, (3.14)-(3.18)]
.

Definition 3.27

(Coronization)
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, and
E\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
be
k
-regular with a dyadic system
\mathcal{D}
. A
coronization of
E
with constant
C>0
is a decomposition
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{G}\dot{\cup}\mathcal{B}
of the dyadic cubes into a
good set

\mathcal{G}
and a
bad set

\mathcal{B}
with the following properties:

•

for every
R\in\mathcal{D}

\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{B},Q\subseteq R}\mathcal{H}^{k}(Q)\leq C\mathcal{H}^{k}(R);

•

the good set
\mathcal{G}
can be partitioned into a family
\mathcal{F}
of disjoint
trees

\mathcal{S}
(also called
stopping time regions
) such that:

–

each
\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{F}
is
coherent
: it has a (unique) maximal element, denoted by
Q(\mathcal{S})
, that contains all other elements of
\mathcal{S}
as subsets, has the property that if

Q\in\mathcal{S},Q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}
with
Q\subseteq Q^{\prime}\subseteq Q(\mathcal{S})
, then
Q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}
, and finally is such that if
Q\in\mathcal{S}
, then either all of the children of
Q
lie in
\mathcal{S}
or none of them do;

–

for every
R\in\mathcal{D}

(3.18)

\sum_{\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{F},Q(\mathcal{S})\subseteq R}\mathcal{H}^{k}(Q(\mathcal{S}))\leq C\mathcal{H}^{k}(R).

Coronizations are useful if the trees
\mathcal{S}
are such that
E
has good (approximation) properties at the scales and locations of each
Q\in\mathcal{S}
. In this case we will say that
E
admits a
corona decomposition
(with respective properties). As in the Euclidean case (
[
13
, p.20]
or
[
14
, p.58]
) the existence of such a decomposition should be independent of the specific choice of dyadic system in the definition, but we will not need this fact.

We introduce three definitions of corona decomposition for regular subsets
E\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
.
The first one, a corona decomposition by intrinsic Lipschitz graphs with small constants,
is inspired by the classical definition in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
of a corona decomposition
(see
[
14
, Definition 3.19]
or
[
13
]
). It is also related to a corona decomposition for intrinsic Lipschitz functions by intrinsic Lipschitz functions with small constants, which was established for
k=n=1
in
[
16
]
, motivated by an application to singular integral operators on regular curves. Moreover, it is an instance of the general corona decompositions studied in
[
7
]
. By
[
7
, Theorem 1.1]
, a set with (ILG-C) therefore has big pieces squared (in the sense of
[
7
, Definition 2.11]
) of intrinsic Lipschitz graphs.

Definition 3.28

(Corona decomposition by intrinsic Lipschitz graphs (ILG-C))
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, and
E\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
be
k
-regular. We say that
E
admits a
corona decomposition by intrinsic Lipschitz graphs
(ILG-C) if, for every
\eta>0
, there exists a constant
C=C(\eta)>0
such that
E
admits a coronization
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{G}\dot{\cup}\mathcal{B}
with constant
C

where, for each
\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{F}
, there exists a
k
-dimensional intrinsic Lipschitz graph
\Gamma=\Gamma_{\mathcal{S}}
with intrinsic Lipschitz constant bounded by
\eta
so that, for all
Q\in\mathcal{S}
, it holds that

(3.19)

\mbox{dist}(x,\Gamma)\leq\eta\,{\rm diam}(Q)\quad\text{if }x\in E,\,\mbox{dist}(x,Q)\leq{\rm diam}(Q).

Remark 3.29
.

We will need an improvement of (ILG-C), where we can replace “
d(x,Q)\leq\mathrm{diam}(Q)
” by “
d(x,Q)\leq N\mathrm{diam}(Q)
” in Definition
3.28
for some constant
N>1
, with the constant
C=C(\eta,N)
in the coronization allowed to depend also on
N
. This is a priori a stronger condition, but in fact the two versions are equivalent. In the Euclidean case, this is stated on
[
13
, p.20]
, see also

[
14
, Lemma 3.31]
, The proof does not use any features of Euclidean spaces and relies purely on axiomatic properties of dyadic families and coronizations, see the outline of the proof in
[
13
,
14
]
.

The next version of the corona decomposition is inspired by
[
19
, Lemma 4.3]
.
Here and in the following,
K_{0}
is a fixed constant, which will be chosen big enough depending on
k
and
C_{E}
, as in
[
19
, p.4]
. We also assume that at least
K_{0}\geq 2
.

Definition 3.30

(Corona decomposition by horizontal planes (P-C))
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, and
E\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
be
k
-regular.
We say that
E
admits a
corona decomposition by horizontal planes
(P-C) if, for every
\eta>0
, there exists a constant
C=C(\eta)>0
such that
E
admits a coronization
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{G}\dot{\cup}\mathcal{B}
with constant
C

where, for each
\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{F}
,
there exists an affine horizontal plane
V_{\mathcal{S}}
such that, whenever
x,y\in K_{0}Q(\mathcal{S})
are chosen in such a way that
d(x,y)>\eta\min\{h_{\mathcal{S}}(x),h_{\mathcal{S}}(y)\}
, then

(3.20)

d(x,y)\leq(1+2\eta)d(P_{V_{\mathcal{S}}}(x),P_{V_{\mathcal{S}}}(y)),

where

h_{\mathcal{S}}(x):=\inf\{d(x,Q)+{\rm diam}(Q):Q\in\mathcal{S}\}.

Hahlomaa worked with a condition analogous to (P-C), but with (
3.20
) only required to hold for
d(x,y)>D^{-2}\min\{h_{\mathcal{S}}(x),h_{\mathcal{S}}(y)\}
for a constant
D
depending on
k
and the Ahlfors regularity constant of
E
. However, an inspection of the proof in
[
19
]
reveals that in fact (P-C) can be obtained, even under our weaker Carleson-type assumptions on
E
, see the proof of Lemma
6.6
. The condition (P-C) can be compared more easily with other corona decompositions from the literature.

The third type of corona decomposition we consider is the one by normed spaces, introduced by Bate, Hyde, and Schul
[
6
, Definitions 6.4.1, 6.4.2]
in metric spaces:

Definition 3.31

(Corona decomposition by normed spaces (N-C))
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, and
E\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
be
k
-regular.
We say that
E\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
admits a
Corona decomposition by normed spaces
(N-C)
with constants
\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}\geq 1
if for every
\eta>0
there exists
C=C(\eta)>0
such that
E
admits a coronization
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{G}\dot{\cup}\mathcal{B}
with constant
C

where, for each
\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{F}
,
there exists a norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{S}}
on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, a point
y_{\mathcal{S}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
and a map
\varphi_{\mathcal{S}}:3B_{Q(\mathcal{S})}\to B_{\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{S}}}(y_{\mathcal{S}},3\mathrm{diam}(Q(\mathcal{S})))
such that if
Q\in\mathcal{S}
and
x,y\in 3B_{Q}
satisfy
d(x,y)\geq\eta\mathrm{diam}(Q)
, then

\frac{1}{\Lambda_{1}}d(x,y)\leq\|\varphi_{\mathcal{S}}(y)-\varphi_{\mathcal{S}}(x)\|_{\mathcal{S}}\leq\Lambda_{2}d(x,y),

where
B_{Q}
is defined as in
[
6
]
, while
B_{\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{S}}}
denotes a ball with respect to the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{S}}
.

In the subsequent Lemmas
3.32
–
3.34
, we discuss relations between the different corona decompositions stated in Definitions
3.28
(ILG-C),
3.30
(P-C), and
3.31
(N-C).

Lemma 3.32
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, and
E\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
be
k
-regular. If
E
satisfies (P-C), then also (ILG-C).

Proof.

Fix
\eta>0
. We will find
\vartheta=\vartheta(\eta)=\vartheta(\eta,K_{0},k,C_{E})>0
sufficiently small and apply (P-C) with parameter
\frac{\vartheta}{K_{0}}
.
Let
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{G}\dot{\cup}\mathcal{B}
be the coronization given by (P-C) applied with “
\eta
” replaced by “
\frac{\vartheta}{K_{0}}
”; this will also serve as a coronization for (ILG-C). We only need to verify the existence of intrinsic Lipschitz graphs as in (
3.19
).

Let
\mathcal{F}
be the forest of trees associated with the coronization, and fix
\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{F}
. For every
x,y\in K_{0}Q(\mathcal{S})
, with
x\neq y
, let us denote by
Q_{x,y}
a smallest – with respect to diameter – cube
in
\mathcal{S}

with
x,y\in K_{0}Q_{x,y}
. (While the cubes in
\mathcal{S}
of a fixed generation are disjoint, the
K_{0}
-enlarged cubes generally are not, so that the choice of
Q_{x,y}
need not be unique, but any admissible choice will do. Moreover, the assumption that
x,y\in K_{0}Q(\mathcal{S})
ensures that there exists at least one cube with the desired properties.)

We perform a construction inspired by
[
6
, Remark 6.4.3]
. Let
\mathcal{N}
be a
set in
K_{0}Q(\mathcal{S})
with the following property
(P)
:
if
x,y\in\mathcal{N}
are distinct, then
d(x,y)\geq\vartheta\,{\rm diam}(Q_{x,y})
, and
\mathcal{N}
is
maximal
in the sense that it is impossible to add an element to
\mathcal{N}
while preserving the separation condition.
By Zorn’s lemma, the existence of
\mathcal{N}
is guaranteed.

We claim that, choosing
\vartheta=\vartheta(\eta)
small enough, then for every
x\in K_{0}Q(\mathcal{S})
and any cube
Q\in\mathcal{S}
such that
x\in K_{0}Q
, there exists
x_{\mathcal{N}}\in\mathcal{N}
satisfying
d(x,x_{\mathcal{N}})\leq\eta\,{\rm diam}(Q)
. In fact,
let
x\in K_{0}Q
for some
Q\in\mathcal{S}
and assume by contradiction that

(3.21)

d(x,y)>\eta\,{\rm diam}(Q)\quad\text{for every }y\in\mathcal{N}.

In particular
x\not\in\mathcal{N}
. If we prove that
\mathcal{N}\cup\{x\}
still satisfies the separation property in
(P)
, we then conclude by maximality of
\mathcal{N}
. In other words, for fixed
y\in\mathcal{N}
we want to show that
d(x,y)\geq\vartheta\,{\rm diam}(Q_{x,y})
. Let
R\in\mathcal{S}
be the smallest ancestor of
Q
such that

(3.22)

{\rm diam}(R)\geq\frac{1}{K_{0}-1}d(x,y)+{\rm diam}(Q).

If such an ancestor does not exist in
\mathcal{S}
, then set
R=Q(\mathcal{S})
. In the first case, we have

d(y,R)\leq d(y,Q)\leq d(x,y)+d(x,Q)\leq d(x,y)+(K_{0}-1){\rm diam}(Q)\leq(K_{0}-1){\rm diam}(R).

This means that
y\in K_{0}R
. The same conclusion also holds in the case
R=Q(\mathcal{S})
, since clearly
y\in K_{0}Q(\mathcal{S})=K_{0}R
. Since also
x\in K_{0}R
, we get that
\mathrm{diam}(R)\geq\mathrm{diam}(Q_{x,y})
by the choice of
Q_{x,y}
.
Then we can estimate, by the minimality of
R
and properties of dyadic cubes (see (
3.4
) and Definition
3.5
), and our counter-assumption (
3.21
) that

\begin{split}D^{-2}\varrho\,{\rm diam}(R)\leq\tfrac{1}{K_{0}-1}d(x,y)+{\rm diam}(Q)\overset{\eqref{eq:CounterAss}}{\leq}\tfrac{1}{K_{0}-1}d(x,y)+\tfrac{1}{\eta}d(x,y)=\left(\tfrac{1}{K_{0}-1}+\tfrac{1}{\eta}\right)d(x,y).\end{split}

This holds also in the case in which we could not find the ancestor satisfying (
3.22
), since in that case

D^{-2}\varrho\,{\rm diam}(R)\leq{\rm diam}(R)\leq\frac{1}{K_{0}-1}d(x,y)+{\rm diam}(Q).

Hence we find that

d(x,y)\geq\frac{D^{-2}\varrho}{\frac{1}{K_{0}-1}+\frac{1}{\eta}}{\rm diam}(R)

and, choosing

(3.23)

\vartheta\leq\frac{D^{-2}\varrho}{\frac{1}{K_{0}-1}+\frac{1}{\eta}}\,(\lesssim_{k,C_{E}}\eta),

we finally get

d(x,y)\geq\vartheta\,{\rm diam}(R)\geq\vartheta\,{\rm diam}(Q_{x,y})

and the claim is proved.
Now, by construction of
\mathcal{N}
, for any
x,y\in\mathcal{N}
, we know

d(x,y)\geq\vartheta\,{\rm diam}(Q_{x,y})\geq\tfrac{\vartheta}{K_{0}}h_{\mathcal{S}}(x)\geq\tfrac{\vartheta}{K_{0}}\,\min\{h_{\mathcal{S}}(x),h_{\mathcal{S}}(y)\}.

The second inequality follows by the fact that
x\in K_{0}Q_{x,y}
, so that
h_{\mathcal{S}}(x)\leq d(x,Q_{x,y})+{\rm diam}(Q_{x,y})\leq(K_{0}-1){\rm diam}(Q_{x,y})+{\rm diam}(Q_{x,y})=K_{0}{\rm diam}(Q_{x,y}).

By (P-C), applied with
\frac{\vartheta}{K_{0}}
, we deduce that there exists an affine horizontal plane
V_{\mathcal{S}}
such that

(3.24)

d(x,y)\leq(1+2\tfrac{\vartheta}{K_{0}})d(P_{V_{\mathcal{S}}}(x),P_{V_{\mathcal{S}}}(y)),\quad x,y\in\mathcal{N}.

By Lemma
3.14
, we may without loss of generality assume that
V_{\mathcal{S}}
is a horizontal
subgroup
. Condition (
3.24
) implies that the
horizontal projection
{P_{V_{\mathcal{S}}}}|_{\mathcal{N}}
is injective. Thus there exists a well-defined map

\Phi_{\mathcal{S}}:=({P_{V_{\mathcal{S}}}}|_{\mathcal{N}})^{-1}:P_{V_{S}}(\mathcal{N})\to\mathcal{N}
that sends
P_{V_{\mathcal{S}}}(x)\mapsto x
, and
\mathcal{N}=\Phi_{\mathcal{S}}(P_{V_{\mathcal{S}}}(\mathcal{N}))
is an in intrinsic graph over
P_{V_{\mathcal{S}}}(\mathcal{N})\subset V_{\mathcal{S}}
. More precisely, by the injectivity of
{P_{V_{\mathcal{S}}}}|_{\mathcal{N}}
, we obtain that for every
v\in P_{V_{\mathcal{S}}}(\mathcal{N})
, there exists a unique
\varphi_{\mathcal{S}}(v)
in the complementary orthogonal subgroup
W
such that
v\cdot\varphi_{\mathcal{S}}(v)\in\mathcal{N}
. Then
\Phi_{\mathcal{S}}
is the graph map of
\varphi_{\mathcal{S}}
, and by (
3.24
), the latter
is intrinsic Lipschitz with intrinsic Lipschitz constant
\lesssim\sqrt[4]{\vartheta}
(see Proposition
2.8
). Thus, by (
3.23
), the intrinsic Lipschitz constant of
\varphi_{\mathcal{S}}
can be bounded by
\lesssim_{k,C_{E}}\sqrt[4]{\eta}
.
By
[
didonato
]
, we can extend
\varphi_{\mathcal{S}}
to the full space
V_{\mathcal{S}}
with a control on the Lipschitz constant. Let
\Gamma_{\mathcal{S}}
be the associated intrinsic Lipschitz graph.

Let now
Q\in\mathcal{S}
and
x\in E
with
d(x,Q)\leq{\rm diam}(Q)
. Then
x\in K_{0}Q
and, by the previous claim, there exists
x_{\mathcal{N}}\in\mathcal{N}
satisfying
d(x,y)\leq\eta\,{\rm diam}(Q)
. Therefore

(3.25)

d(x,\Gamma_{\mathcal{S}})\leq d(x,\mathcal{N})\leq d(x,x_{\mathcal{N}})\leq\eta\,{\rm diam}(Q).

This proves that the chosen coronization satisfies the condition required in (ILG-C). To be precise, the intrinsic graph we have constructed may not have intrinsic Lipschitz constant bounded by
\eta
; this is not a problem, since the constant is controlled by
\eta
, in the sense that it goes to
0
as
\eta\to 0
, so by choosing
\vartheta(\eta)
initially even smaller if needed, we can arrange that the intrinsic Lipschitz constant of
\Gamma_{\mathcal{S}}
is less than
\eta
while also (

) holds.
∎

Next, we consider the converse of the implication in Lemma

.

Lemma 3.33
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, and
E\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
be
k
-regular. If
E
satisfies (ILG-C), then also (P-C).

Proof.

Fix
\eta>0
. Our goal is to verify the conditions of (P-C) for this parameter. Find
0<\overline{\eta}\leq\eta
such that

(3.26)

\frac{1+\overline{\eta}^{2}}{1-6\,\overline{\eta}^{2}D^{2}\varrho^{-1}-6\overline{\eta}}\leq 1+2\eta.

Apply now (ILG-C) with parameter
\overline{\eta}^{2}
and find the corresponding coronization
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{G}\dot{\cup}\mathcal{B}
with forest
\mathcal{F}
and constant
C=C(\overline{\eta}^{2})=C(\eta,k,C_{E})
.
The only property that has to be verified is that for each
\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{F}
there exists an affine horizontal plane
V_{\mathcal{S}}
such that, whenever
x,y\in{K_{0}}Q(\mathcal{S})
are chosen in such a way that
d(x,y)>\eta\min\{h_{\mathcal{S}}(x),h_{\mathcal{S}}(y)\}
, then

(3.27)

d(x,y)\leq(1+2\eta)d(P_{V_{\mathcal{S}}}(x),P_{V_{\mathcal{S}}}(y)).

Fix
\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{F}
. We take as affine horizontal plane
V_{\mathcal{S}}
the domain of the Lipschitz graph
\Gamma_{\mathcal{S}}
which is associated to
\mathcal{S}
by the (ILG-C) property. Suppose now
x,y\in{K_{0}}Q(\mathcal{S})
with
d(x,y)>\eta\min\{h_{\mathcal{S}}(x),h_{\mathcal{S}}(y)\}
. Assume without loss of generality that
\min\{h_{\mathcal{S}}(x),h_{\mathcal{S}}(y)\}=h_{\mathcal{S}}(x)
. By definition of
h_{\mathcal{S}}
, there exists a cube
Q\in\mathcal{S}
such that
d(x,y)>\eta(\mathrm{diam}(Q)+d(x,Q))
. Consider now the minimal cube
R\supset Q
,
R\in\mathcal{S}
, such that
\mathrm{diam}({K_{0}}R)\geq d(x,y)
. Such a cube exists since
\mathrm{diam}({K_{0}}Q(\mathcal{S}))\geq d(x,y)
. Then there are two possibilities:

•

If there exists a child
\widehat{R}
of
R
which contains
Q
, then by minimality of
R
and by the properties of dyadic cubes,

\frac{\mathrm{diam}(R)}{D^{2}\varrho^{-1}}\leq\mathrm{diam}(\widehat{R}){\leq\mathrm{diam}(K_{0}\widehat{R})}<d(x,y),

Hence
\mathrm{diam}(R)\leq D^{2}\varrho^{-1}\,d(x,y)
;

•

If instead there exists no child of
R
containing

, this implies that
R=Q
. Therefore

\overline{\eta}\,\mathrm{diam}(R)\leq\eta\,\mathrm{diam}(R)=\eta\,\mathrm{diam}(Q)\leq\eta\,(\mathrm{diam}(Q)+d(x,Q))<d(x,y),

which gives
\mathrm{diam}(R)\leq\overline{\eta}^{-1}d(x,y)
.

In any case, we get
\mathrm{diam}(R)\leq(D^{2}\varrho^{-1}+\overline{\eta}^{-1})d(x,y)
.

Moreover
d(x,R)\leq d(x,Q)<\eta^{-1}d(x,y)\leq\eta^{-1}\mathrm{diam}({K_{0}}R){\leq 2\eta^{-1}K_{0}\mathrm{diam}(R)}
. Similarly, by the triangle inequality,
d(y,R)\leq d(x,y)+d(x,R)\leq(\eta^{-1}+1)\mathrm{diam}({K_{0}}R){\leq 2(\eta^{-1}+1)K_{0}\mathrm{diam}(R)}
. Therefore we can apply (ILG-C) (with the improvement from Remark
3.29

) and get

(3.28)

d(x,\Gamma_{\mathcal{S}})\leq\overline{\eta}^{2}\mathrm{diam}(R)\quad\text{and}\quad d(y,\Gamma_{\mathcal{S}})\leq\overline{\eta}^{2}\mathrm{diam}(R)

since
d(x,R)\leq N\mathrm{diam}(R)
and
d(y,R)\leq N\mathrm{diam}(R)
.

Let now
x_{\Gamma}
and
y_{\Gamma}
be two points on
\Gamma_{\mathcal{S}}
which realize

and

respectively. Since
\Gamma_{\mathcal{S}}
is an intrinsic Lipschitz graph with constant smaller than
\overline{\eta}^{2}
, we get by metric Lipschitz continuity of the graph map (
2.5
) that

(3.29)

d(x_{\Gamma},y_{\Gamma})\leq(1+\overline{\eta}^{2})d(P_{V_{\mathcal{S}}}(x_{\Gamma}),P_{V_{\mathcal{S}}}(y_{\Gamma})).

Therefore, if
\overline{\eta}
is small enough, (
3.28
),(
3.29
), and the
1
-Lipschitz property of

yield that

\begin{split}d(x,y)&\leq d(x,x_{\Gamma})+d(x_{\Gamma},y_{\Gamma})+d(y,y_{\Gamma}){\leq}(1+\overline{\eta}^{2})d(P_{V_{\mathcal{S}}}(x_{\Gamma}),P_{V_{\mathcal{S}}}(y_{\Gamma}))+2\overline{\eta}^{2}\mathrm{diam}(R)\\
&\leq(1+\overline{\eta}^{2})d(P_{V_{\mathcal{S}}}(x),P_{V_{\mathcal{S}}}(y))+(1+\overline{\eta}^{2})d(P_{V_{\mathcal{S}}}(x),P_{V_{\mathcal{S}}}(x_{\Gamma}))+(1+\overline{\eta}^{2})d(P_{V_{\mathcal{S}}}(y),P_{V_{\mathcal{S}}}(y_{\Gamma}))\\
&\quad+2\overline{\eta}^{2}\mathrm{diam}(R)\\
&\leq(1+\overline{\eta}^{2})d(P_{V_{\mathcal{S}}}(x),P_{V_{\mathcal{S}}}(y))+(1+\overline{\eta}^{2})d(x,x_{\Gamma})+(1+\overline{\eta}^{2})d(y,y_{\Gamma})+2\overline{\eta}^{2}\mathrm{diam}(R)\\
&\leq(1+\overline{\eta}^{2})d(P_{V_{\mathcal{S}}}(x),P_{V_{\mathcal{S}}}(y))+6\overline{\eta}^{2}\mathrm{diam}(R)\\
&\leq(1+\overline{\eta}^{2})d(P_{V_{\mathcal{S}}}(x),P_{V_{\mathcal{S}}}(y))+6\overline{\eta}^{2}(D^{2}\varrho^{-1}+\overline{\eta}^{-1})d(x,y).\end{split}

If

∎

If a set

4.
An elaboration on Juillet’s construction

The goal of this section is to prove Theorem
1.4
by using a construction due to Juillet.
In
[
Juillet
]
, the author constructed a Lipschitz curve
\omega:[0,1]\to\mathbb{H}^{1}
whose associated
\beta_{\infty,\mathcal{V}_{1}}
-numbers are not square-summable, providing in this way a counterexample to the classical traveling salesman problem (with exponent 2) in
\mathbb{H}^{1}
, which shows that the condition formulated in
[
17
]
for a compact set
E\subset\mathbb{H}^{1}
to be contained in a rectifiable curve is not necessary. The curve
\omega
is obtained as a horizontal lift of a Lipschitz curve
\omega^{\mathbb{C}}:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
, whose construction requires a uniform approximation by polygonal curves. Let
\Gamma
denote the support of the curve
\omega
. It was observed in
[
FV2
, Proposition 4.37]
that
\Gamma\not\in{\rm GLem}(\beta_{\infty,\mathcal{V}_{1}},2)
, but this does not a priori rule out the possibility that
\Gamma
could satisfy

{\rm GLem}(\beta_{p,\mathcal{V}_{1}},2)

with some exponent
p<\infty
. In order to show that the assumptions of our main result, Theorem
1.1
, are strictly weaker than the ones used by Hahlomaa in
[
19
]
, we aim to prove that
\Gamma
is a
1
-regular set in
(\mathbb{H}^{1},d)
(Theorem
4.2
) with
\Gamma\not\in{\rm GLem}(\beta_{1,\mathcal{V}_{1}},2)
(Theorem
4.4
), even if
\Gamma\in{\rm GLem}(\widehat{\beta}_{1,\mathcal{V}_{1}},4)
(Theorem
4.5
). The converse implication,

{\rm GLem}(\beta_{1,\mathcal{V}_{1}},2){\,\Rightarrow\rm GLem}(\widehat{\beta}_{1,\mathcal{V}_{1}},4)
,
always holds by Proposition
3.24
.

As in the rest of the paper, we work here with the Korányi distance
d
. In
[
Juillet
]
, the sub-Riemannian distance is used instead, but this change is insignificant due to the bi-Lipschitz equivalence of the two metrics.

We recall the following definition, which can be given in any metric space.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\gamma:[a,b]\to\mathbb{H}^{1}
be a (continuous) curve. If
d
denotes the Korányi distance, the
length
of
\gamma
in
(\mathbb{H}^{1},d)
is defined as

\ell_{d}(\gamma):=\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}d(\gamma(s_{i-1}),\gamma(s_{i})):a=s_{0}<s_{1}<\dots<s_{k}=b\right\}.

If
\gamma
is a horizontal curve, then its length
\ell_{d}(\gamma)
coincides with the
Euclidean
length of the projection
\pi\circ\gamma
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
(see
[
20
, Proposition 1.1]
for the analogous statement with the sub-Riemannian distance
d_{cc}
, and
[
25
, below Prop. 6.2]
,
[
MR4062792
, Lemma 5.1]
or
[
24
, Proposition 2.1]
for the inequality
d\leq d_{cc}
).

We now recall the main steps in the construction of the curve
\omega
with trace
\Gamma
in Theorem
1.4
, since we will use them in our argument (see
[
Juillet
]
for more details about the construction).
Let

(4.1)

\vartheta_{n}:=\frac{C_{0}}{n}

be a sequence of angles, where the constant
C_{0}
is chosen small enough (we will assume
C_{0}\leq 0.2
as in the original construction and point out all the instances where additional requirements for
C_{0}
appear). We start by defining a sequence
(\omega^{\mathbb{C}}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of planar curves in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. The curve
\omega_{0}^{\mathbb{C}}:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is simply the constant speed parameterization of the segment connecting the points
(-1,0)
and
(1,0)
. Each curve
\omega_{n}^{\mathbb{C}}:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a polygonal line formed by
4^{n}
segments of the same length
l_{n}
, parameterized with constant speed
L_{n}=4^{n}\cdot l_{n}
. Inductively, the curve
\omega_{n+1}^{\mathbb{C}}
is obtained from
\omega_{n}^{\mathbb{C}}
by replacing each segment line in
\omega_{n}^{\mathbb{C}}
by a polygonal curve (keeping the same starting and ending points) made of four new segments of length
l_{n+1}=\frac{l_{n}}{4\cos\vartheta_{n+1}}
, forming an angle
\vartheta_{n+1}
with the original one.

Figure 1.
The construction of the curve
\omega

Notice that, by construction,

(4.2)

2\cdot 4^{-n}\leq l_{n}\leq 2^{-n+1}\text{ for every }n\geq 0.

It is proved in
[
Juillet
, Proposition 3.1 and Lemma 3.2]
that the sequence of curves
(\omega_{n}^{\mathbb{C}})_{n}
uniformly converges to a Lipschitz curve
\omega^{\mathbb{C}}
of length
L\leq 2.4
, again parameterized with constant speed.
Actually, the proof of
[
Juillet
, Lemma 3.2]
shows that

(4.3)

L\leq 2\cdot\exp(0.08)\leq 2.2.

Moreover

(4.4)

L=\lim_{n\to\infty}4^{n}l_{n}=\sup_{n}\,4^{n}l_{n},

where
4^{n}l_{n}
represents the length of the curve
\omega_{n}^{\mathbb{C}}
.

Finally, let
\omega:[0,1]\to\mathbb{H}^{1}
be the horizontal lift of the curve
\omega^{\mathbb{C}}
starting from
(-1,0,0)
. We denote by
\omega_{n}
the horizontal lift of
\omega_{n}^{\mathbb{C}}
starting from the same point. Notice that the Heisenberg length of the curve
\omega
is still
L
, while the one of
\omega_{n}
is
4^{n}l_{n}
. By
[
Juillet
, Lemma 3.3]
, for every
n\leq m
, the curves
\omega_{n}
and
\omega_{m}
coincide at each parameter point of the form
\frac{\sigma}{4^{n}}
for
\sigma=0,1,\dots,4^{n}
: this is easy to see for the curves
\omega_{n}^{\mathbb{C}}
and
\omega_{m}^{\mathbb{C}}
, while it holds also for the lifted curves since the signed area spanned by
\omega_{n}^{\mathbb{C}}
and
\omega_{m}^{\mathbb{C}}
on an interval
[\frac{\sigma}{4^{n}},\frac{\sigma+1}{4^{n}}]
is the same; recall the comment below Definition
2.2
.
Moreover,
\omega_{n}\to\omega
uniformly on
[0,1]
. This is not explicitly stated in
[
Juillet
]
, but it follows by
the Arzelà–Ascoli theorem
from the uniform convergence
\omega_{n}^{\mathbb{C}}\to\omega^{\mathbb{C}}
, the horizontality of
\omega_{n}
and the equi-boundedness of the derivatives,
|\dot{\omega}_{n}^{\mathbb{C}}|\leq L_{n}\leq L
.
Denoting by
\Gamma
the support of
\omega
, our main goal is now to prove that
\Gamma\not\in{\rm GLem}(\beta_{1,\mathcal{V}_{1}},2)
, see Theorem
4.4
.

We first show that
\omega
is injective: the same argument is also used to prove 1-regularity of
\Gamma=\omega([0,1])
(with respect to the Korányi distance in
\mathbb{H}^{1}
):

Theorem 4.2
.

The curve
\omega^{\mathbb{C}}
(hence also
\omega
) is injective, and
\Gamma
is 1-regular in
(\mathbb{H}^{1},d)
.

Proof.

The proof of injectivity relies on the following claim:

Claim
. Let
p=\omega^{\mathbb{C}}(\sigma/4^{n})
for some
\sigma=0,1,\dots,4^{n}
. Then

(4.5)

B_{\rm Eucl}(p,0.6\,l_{n})\,\cap\,\omega^{\mathbb{C}}\left([0,1]\setminus\left[\frac{\sigma-1}{4^{n}},\frac{\sigma+1}{4^{n}}\right]\right)=\emptyset.

We now assume the claim and prove that
\omega^{\mathbb{C}}
is injective. Let
0\leq a<b\leq 1
and choose
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\sigma=0,1,\dots,4^{n}
such that

(4.6)

\left|a-\frac{\sigma}{4^{n}}\right|\leq\frac{1}{2\cdot 4^{n}}

and

(4.7)

\left|b-\frac{\sigma}{4^{n}}\right|>\frac{1}{4^{n}}.

This is always possible, since one can choose
n
large enough such that

|a-b|>\frac{3}{2\cdot 4^{n}}

and subsequently choose
\sigma=0,1,\dots,4^{n}
such that (
4.6
) holds. By the triangular inequality, (
4.7
) holds as well.
From (
4.6
) it follows that

(4.8)

\left|\omega^{\mathbb{C}}(a)-\omega^{\mathbb{C}}\left(\frac{\sigma}{4^{n}}\right)\right|\leq L\left|a-\frac{\sigma}{4^{n}}\right|\leq L\,\frac{1}{2\cdot 4^{n}}\overset{\eqref{eq:*}}{\leq}\frac{2.4}{2\cdot 4^{n}}=0.6\frac{2}{4^{n}}\overset{\eqref{eq:**}}{\leq}0.6\,l_{n}.

Hence
\omega^{\mathbb{C}}(a)\in B_{\rm Eucl}(p,0.6\,l_{n})
. On the other hand, (
4.7
) means that
b\in[0,1]\setminus[\frac{\sigma-1}{4^{n}},\frac{\sigma+1}{4^{n}}]
. Therefore, the claim implies that
\omega^{\mathbb{C}}(b)\not\in B_{\rm Eucl}(p,0.6\,l_{n})
and in particular we deduce
\omega^{\mathbb{C}}(a)\neq\omega^{\mathbb{C}}(b)
, proving injectivity of
\omega^{\mathbb{C}}
(and clearly of
\omega
).

Assuming the claim, we now show that
\omega([0,1])
is 1-regular. Fix now
x=(z,t)\in\omega([0,1])
and
r>0
. In order to prove upper 1-regularity, we can assume that
r<1/20
, since otherwise we use the simple estimate

\mathcal{H}^{1}\left(B(x,r)\,\cap\,\omega\left([0,1]\right)\right)\leq L\leq 20L\,r.

For
r<1/20
, choose
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
l_{n+1}/40\leq r<l_{n}/40
.
By
L
-Lipschitz continuity of
\omega^{\mathbb{C}}
, there exists
\sigma=0,1,\dots 4^{n}
such that
|z-p|\leq\frac{L}{2\cdot 4^{n}}
, where
p=\omega^{\mathbb{C}}(\sigma/4^{n})
. In particular,

|z-p|\leq\frac{L}{2\cdot 4^{n}}\overset{\eqref{eq:*}}{\leq}\frac{2.2}{2\cdot 4^{n}}=\frac{11}{20}\frac{2}{4^{n}}\overset{\eqref{eq:**}}{\leq}\frac{11}{20}l_{n}.

Hence, if
z^{\prime}\in B_{\rm Eucl}(z,r)
, then

|z^{\prime}-p|\leq|z^{\prime}-z|+|z-p|\leq r+\tfrac{11}{20}l_{n}\leq\left(\tfrac{1}{40}+\tfrac{11}{20}\right)l_{n}\leq 0.6\,l_{n}.

This proves that
B_{\rm Eucl}(z,r)\subseteq B_{\rm Eucl}(p,0.6\,l_{n})
. Moreover, (
4.5
) implies that

(4.9)

B_{\rm Eucl}(p,0.6\,l_{n})\,\cap\,\omega^{\mathbb{C}}\left([0,1]\right)\subseteq\omega^{\mathbb{C}}\left(\left[\frac{\sigma-1}{4^{n}},\frac{\sigma+1}{4^{n}}\right]\right).

Altogether, this shows that

\displaystyle\pi\left(B(x,r)\right)\cap\pi\left(\omega([0,1])\right)

\displaystyle=\pi\left(B(x,r)\right)\cap\omega^{\mathbb{C}}\left([0,1]\right)=B_{\mathrm{Eucl}}(z,r)\cap\omega^{\mathbb{C}}\left([0,1]\right)

\displaystyle{=}B_{\rm Eucl}(p,0.6\,l_{n})\cap\omega^{\mathbb{C}}\left([0,1]\right)\overset{\eqref{eq:BallCurveIncl}}{=}\omega^{\mathbb{C}}\left(\left[\frac{\sigma-1}{4^{n}},\frac{\sigma+1}{4^{n}}\right]\right).

Therefore,
\pi\left(B(x,r)\cap\omega([0,1])\right)\subset\omega^{\mathbb{C}}\left(\left[\frac{\sigma-1}{4^{n}},\frac{\sigma+1}{4^{n}}\right]\right)
. The injectivity of
\omega^{\mathbb{C}}
implies then that also

(4.10)

B(x,r)\cap\omega([0,1])\subset\omega\left(\left[\frac{\sigma-1}{4^{n}},\frac{\sigma+1}{4^{n}}\right]\right).

Moreover,
by the
L
-Lipschitz continuity of
\omega^{\mathbb{C}}
and the same estimates as in (
4.8
), we have

(4.11)

\mathcal{H}^{1}_{\mathrm{Eucl}}\left(\omega^{\mathbb{C}}\left(\left[\frac{\sigma-1}{4^{n}},\frac{\sigma+1}{4^{n}}\right]\right)\right)\leq L\,\frac{2}{4^{n}}\overset{\eqref{eq:**},\eqref{eq:*}}{\leq}2.4\,l_{n}.

The upper 1-regularity of
\omega([0,1])
then follows since, by (
4.10
) and the horizontality of the curve
\omega
,

\begin{split}\mathcal{H}^{1}(B(x,r)\cap\,\omega([0,1]))\leq\mathcal{H}^{1}\left(\omega\left(\left[\frac{\sigma-1}{4^{n}},\frac{\sigma+1}{4^{n}}\right]\right)\right)&=\mathcal{H}^{1}_{\mathrm{Eucl}}\left(\omega^{\mathbb{C}}\left(\left[\frac{\sigma-1}{4^{n}},\frac{\sigma+1}{4^{n}}\right]\right)\right)\\
&\overset{\eqref{1regularity}}{\leq}2.4\cdot 4\cdot 40\,r.\end{split}

The lower regularity of
\omega([0,1])
is immediate since it is a connected set.
∎

Proof of the Claim (
4.5
) .

We prove the claim by induction on
n
. Let us first show the conclusion for
n=1
. Since
\omega^{\mathbb{C}}
is
L
-Lipschitz, then
\omega^{\mathbb{C}}\left([0,1]\setminus\left[\frac{\sigma-1}{4^{n}},\frac{\sigma+1}{4^{n}}\right]\right)
lies in the
\frac{L}{16}
-neighbourhood of

E:=\left\{\omega^{\mathbb{C}}\left(\frac{\tau}{16}\right):\tau=0,\dots,4\sigma-4\,\text{ or }\tau=4\sigma+4,\dots,16\right\}.

Hence, if
q\in\omega^{\mathbb{C}}\left([0,1]\setminus\left[\frac{\sigma-1}{4^{n}},\frac{\sigma+1}{4^{n}}\right]\right)
, then there exists
\bar{q}\in E
such that
|q-\bar{q}|\leq\frac{L}{16}
. Then

(4.12)

\left|q-\omega^{\mathbb{C}}\left(\frac{\sigma}{4}\right)\right|\geq\left|\bar{q}-\omega^{\mathbb{C}}\left(\frac{\sigma}{4}\right)\right|-\left|q-\bar{q}\right|\geq l_{1}-\frac{L}{16}\geq l_{1}-\frac{2.4}{16}.

Here we exploit the fact that
\left|\bar{q}-\omega^{\mathbb{C}}\left(\frac{\sigma}{4}\right)\right|\geq l_{1}
: this follows if the constant
C_{0}
(and hence the angle
\vartheta_{1}
) in (

) is chosen small enough, so that if

are natural numbers, then

\displaystyle\left|\omega^{\mathbb{C}}\left(\frac{t_{1}}{16}\right)-\omega^{\mathbb{C}}\left(\frac{t_{2}}{16}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq(t_{2}-t_{1})\ell_{2}

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:theta_n}}{\leq}\left(1+t_{2}-t_{1}\right)\cdot\cos(\vartheta_{1}+\vartheta_{2})\ell_{2}

\displaystyle\leq(t_{3}-t_{1})\cdot\cos(\vartheta_{1}+\vartheta_{2})\ell_{2}\leq\left|\omega^{\mathbb{C}}\left(\frac{t_{1}}{16}\right)-\omega^{\mathbb{C}}\left(\frac{t_{3}}{16}\right)\right|.

The last inequality holds true since, if

and

are chosen small enough, the projection of

to the segment between

and
\omega^{\mathbb{C}}(1)

preserves the order in the following sense:
if

, then the first coordinates satisfy
(\omega_{2})_{1}^{\mathbb{C}}(\tau/16)<(\omega_{2})_{1}^{\mathbb{C}}(\tau^{\prime}/16)
, and moreover, the individual segments in

Since

, an analogous conclusion as in (

\left|\omega^{\mathbb{C}}\left(\frac{t_{1}}{4^{n}}\right)-\omega^{\mathbb{C}}\left(\frac{t_{2}}{4^{n}}\right)\right|\leq\left|\omega^{\mathbb{C}}\left(\frac{t_{1}}{4^{n}}\right)-\omega^{\mathbb{C}}\left(\frac{t_{3}}{4^{n}}\right)\right|
for every

and

for
\varrho=0,1,\dots,4^{n-2}-1
.
Moving back to (
4.12
), since by construction
l_{1}\geq\frac{1}{2}
, it follows
l_{1}-\frac{2.4}{16}\geq\,0.7l_{1}>0.6\,l_{1}
. We deduce that
q\not\in B_{\rm Eucl}(\omega^{\mathbb{C}}\left(\sigma/4\right),0.6\,l_{1})
, as desired.

We now deal with the inductive step. Let
p=\omega^{\mathbb{C}}(\sigma/4^{n})
for some
\sigma=0,1,\dots,4^{n}
. We distinguish two cases, according to the value of
\sigma
. If
\sigma=4\sigma_{0}
for some
\sigma_{0}=0,1,\dots,4^{n-1}
, we can write
p=\omega^{\mathbb{C}}(\sigma_{0}/4^{n-1})
. By induction hypothesis, since clearly
B_{\rm Eucl}(p,0.6\,l_{n})\subset B_{\rm Eucl}(p,0.6\,l_{n-1})
, we deduce that

B_{\rm Eucl}(p,0.6\,l_{n})\cap\omega^{\mathbb{C}}\left([0,1]\setminus\left[\frac{\sigma_{0}-1}{4^{n-1}},\frac{\sigma_{0}+1}{4^{n-1}}\right]\right)=\emptyset.

Notice that
\left[\frac{\sigma_{0}-1}{4^{n-1}},\frac{\sigma_{0}+1}{4^{n-1}}\right]=\left[\frac{\sigma-4}{4^{n}},\frac{\sigma+4}{4^{n}}\right]
: hence we only need to show that

\begin{cases}B_{\rm Eucl}(p,0.6\,l_{n})\cap\omega^{\mathbb{C}}\left(\left[\frac{\sigma-4}{4^{n}},\frac{\sigma-1}{4^{n}}\right]\right)=\emptyset\\
B_{\rm Eucl}(p,0.6\,l_{n})\cap\omega^{\mathbb{C}}\left(\left[\frac{\sigma+1}{4^{n}},\frac{\sigma+4}{4^{n}}\right]\right)=\emptyset.\end{cases}

These properties can be proved exactly with the same argument as in the
n=1
case, replacing
l_{1}
with
l_{n}
and 16 with
4^{n+1}
, using the relation
l_{n}\geq 2/4^{n}
from (
4.2
).

If instead
\sigma\in\{0,1,\ldots,4^{n}\}
in
p=\omega^{\mathbb{C}}(\sigma/4^{n})
is not an integer multiple of 4, choose
\sigma_{0}=0,1,\dots,4^{n-1}
such that
\frac{\sigma_{0}}{4^{n-1}}<\frac{\sigma}{4^{n}}<\frac{\sigma_{0}+1}{4^{n-1}}.

In particular
\sigma\in\{4\sigma_{0}+1,4\sigma_{0}+2,4\sigma_{0}+3\}
. Our goal is now to prove

(4.14)

B_{\rm Eucl}(p,0.6\,l_{n})\cap\omega^{\mathbb{C}}\left([0,1]\setminus\left[\frac{\sigma_{0}}{4^{n-1}},\frac{\sigma_{0}+1}{4^{n-1}}\right]\right)=\emptyset.

Once this is achieved, we reach the conclusion by arguing again as in the previous case. Let
p_{0}:=\omega^{\mathbb{C}}(\frac{\sigma_{0}}{{4^{n-1}}})
and
p_{1}:=\omega^{\mathbb{C}}(\frac{\sigma_{0}+1}{4^{n-1}})
. We first prove that

(4.15)

B_{\rm Eucl}(p,0.6\,l_{n})\subseteq B_{\rm Eucl}(p_{0},0.6\,l_{n-1})\cup B_{\rm Eucl}(p_{1},0.6\,l_{n-1}).

Assume for instance
\sigma=4\sigma_{0}+1
. Then, if
q\in B_{\rm Eucl}(p,0.6\,l_{n})
,

|q-p_{0}|\leq|q-p|+|p-p_{0}|\leq 0.6\,l_{n}+l_{n}=1.6\,\frac{l_{n-1}}{4\cos\vartheta_{n}}<0.6\,l_{n-1}.

If instead
\sigma=4\sigma_{0}+3
, then we argue in the same way considering
p_{1}
instead of
p_{0}
. Hence it remains to discuss the case
\sigma=4\sigma_{0}+2
. In this situation,
|p_{0}-p|=|p_{1}-p|=l_{n-1}/2
. Consider
q\in B_{\rm Eucl}(p,0.6\,l_{n})
and assume for instance that
|q-p_{0}|\leq|q-p_{1}|
(the other case is analogous). By Pythagorean theorem

|q-p_{0}|\leq\sqrt{(0.5\,l_{n-1})^{2}+(0.6\,l_{n})^{2}}=\left(0.25+\frac{0.36}{16\cos^{2}\vartheta_{n}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}l_{n-1}\leq 0.6\,l_{n-1},

proving (
4.15
). Let us now conclude by showing (
4.14
).

Let
q\in B_{\rm Eucl}(p,0.6\,l_{n})
. By (
4.15
),
q\in B_{\rm Eucl}(p_{i},0.6\,l_{n-1})
for some
i\in\{0,1\}
. Let us assume without loss of generality that
i=0
. Then, by inductive hypothesis,
q\not\in\omega^{\mathbb{C}}([0,1]\setminus\left[\frac{\sigma_{0}-1}{4^{n-1}},\frac{\sigma_{0}+1}{4^{n-1}}\right])
. In order to establish (
4.14
), we need then to prove that
q\not\in\omega^{\mathbb{C}}\left(\left[\frac{\sigma_{0}-1}{4^{n-1}},\frac{\sigma_{0}}{4^{n-1}}\right]\right)
.
This is achieved using a similar argument as before: since
\omega^{\mathbb{C}}
is
L
-Lipschitz,
\omega^{\mathbb{C}}\left(\left[\frac{\sigma_{0}-1}{4^{n-1}},\frac{\sigma_{0}}{4^{n-1}}\right]\right)
lies in the
\frac{L}{4^{n+1}}
-neighbourhood of

E_{0}:=\left\{\omega^{\mathbb{C}}\left(\frac{16\sigma_{0}-16}{4^{n+1}}\right),\omega^{\mathbb{C}}\left(\frac{16\sigma_{0}-15}{4^{n+1}}\right),\dots,\omega^{\mathbb{C}}\left(\frac{16\sigma_{0}}{4^{n+1}}\right)\right\}.

Assume by contradiction that
q=\omega^{\mathbb{C}}(s)
for some
s\in\left[\frac{\sigma_{0}-1}{4^{n-1}},\frac{\sigma_{0}}{4^{n-1}}\right]
. Then there exists
\bar{q}\in E_{0}
such that
|q-\bar{q}|\leq\frac{L}{4^{n+1}}
. Hence

(4.16)

|q-p|\geq|p-\bar{q}|-|q-\bar{q}|\geq l_{n}-\frac{L}{4^{n+1}}\geq l_{n}-\frac{2.4}{4^{n+1}}.

The fact that
|p-\bar{q}|\geq l_{n}
follows by the analogue observation after (
4.12
), if
C_{0}
is chosen small enough. However, while we previously had to consider only the points in one “package”, we now need the same conclusion for two consecutive “packages”. To this end,
we also use the fact that the angle between two consecutive segments of
\omega_{n-1}^{\mathbb{C}}
is always greater than
\pi-2\vartheta_{1}
, which can be made very close to
\pi
by choosing
C_{0}
small enough; recall (
4.1
).

Since
l_{n}\geq 2\cdot 4^{-n}
, it follows that
l_{n}-\frac{2.4}{4^{n+1}}\geq 0.7\,l_{n}>0.6\,l_{n}
, which implies by (
4.16
) that
q\not\in B_{\rm Eucl}(p,0.6\,l_{n})
, a contradiction.
∎

We next aim to show that
\Gamma=\omega([0,1])
is so badly approximable by horizontal lines that it does not satisfy
{\rm GLem}(\beta_{1,\mathcal{V}_{1}},2)
. To this end, we will use the following standard estimate for the distance between two points in
\mathbb{H}^{1}
in terms of the Euclidean distance between their projections and the area spanned by any horizontal curve connecting them.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\gamma:[a,b]\to\mathbb{H}^{1}
be a horizontal curve joining
p_{1}=\gamma(a)
and
p_{2}=\gamma(b)
. Then

(4.17)

d(p_{1},p_{2})\lesssim|\pi(p_{1})-\pi(p_{2})|+\sqrt{|A_{\gamma}|},

where
A_{\gamma}
denotes the algebraic area enclosed by the curve
\pi\circ\gamma
concatenated with the segment joining
\pi(p_{2})
and
\pi(p_{1})
.

Proof.

As both sides of the inequality (
4.17
) are invariant by left translations, we may assume without loss of generality that
p_{1}=\gamma(a)=0
. Since

d(\gamma(b),0)\lesssim|\pi(\gamma(b))|+\sqrt{|\gamma_{3}(b)|}

and, by horizontality of
\gamma=(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3})
, we have
\dot{\gamma}_{3}=\frac{1}{2}(\gamma_{1}\dot{\gamma}_{2}-\gamma_{2}\dot{\gamma}_{1})
almost everywhere, the claim follows, cf.
[
8
, (2.22)]
or
[
Juillet
, (1.1)]
.
∎

Theorem 4.4
.

\Gamma\not\in{\rm GLem}(\beta_{1,\mathcal{V}_{1}},2)
.

Proof.

We will show that

(4.18)

B_{1,1}^{\Delta}(\Gamma):=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}2^{-k}\sum_{x\in\Delta_{k}}\beta_{1,\mathcal{V}_{1}}(x,A\,2^{-k})^{2}=+\infty,

where
\Delta=(\Delta_{k})_{k}
is a dyadic net and
B_{1,1}^{\Delta}
is defined as in (
3.17
) with a constant

, cf.
[
Juillet
, (0.1)]
. Then the conclusion follows from Lemma
3.26
. Notice that the index
k
is chosen to uniformize the notation with the one used in
[
Juillet
]
and should not be confused with the dimension of
\Gamma
, which is 1.
In order to verify (
4.18
), we need suitable lower bounds for
\mathrm{card}(\Delta_{k})
and
\beta_{1,\mathcal{V}_{1}}(x,A\,2^{-k})
,
x\in\Delta_{k}
, respectively.

It is proved in
[
Juillet
, §4]
that
\mathrm{card}(\Delta_{k})\geq 2^{k}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Moreover, in
[
Juillet
, (4.1)]
, a lower bound for
\beta_{\infty,\mathcal{V}_{1}}(x,A\,2^{-k})
is derived. We need to verify an analogous bound for
\beta_{1,\mathcal{V}_{1}}(x,A\,2^{-k})
, which requires a finer analysis: we have to show that
\Gamma\cap B(x,A\,2^{-k})
stays at large distance from every horizontal line not only in a point, but in a large
\mathcal{H}^{1}
-measure set of points.

In
[
Juillet
, Section 4]
it is shown
that, taking
n=\lceil k/2\rceil
, for every
x\in\Delta_{k}
there exists
\sigma\in\{0,1,\dots,4^{n}-1\}
such that

(4.19)

\omega\left(\left[\frac{\sigma}{4^{n}},\frac{\sigma+1}{4^{n}}\right]\right)\subseteq B(x,A\,2^{-k}).

Nevertheless, it also holds that

(4.20)

\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Gamma\cap B(x,A\,2^{-k}))\lesssim\mathcal{H}^{1}\left(\omega\left(\left[\frac{\sigma}{4^{n}},\frac{\sigma+1}{4^{n}}\right]\right)\right).

Indeed, on the one hand,
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Gamma\cap B(x,A\,2^{-k}))\lesssim 2^{-k}
by 1-regularity of
\Gamma
established in Theorem
4.2
. On the other, since
\pi:(\mathbb{H}^{1},d)\to(\mathbb{R}^{2},d_{e})
,
\pi(x,y,t):=(x,y)
, is a 1-Lipschitz map, by construction of
\omega
we deduce

\begin{split}\mathcal{H}^{1}\left(\omega\left(\left[\frac{\sigma}{4^{n}},\frac{\sigma+1}{4^{n}}\right]\right)\right)&\geq\mathcal{H}^{1}_{\mathrm{Eucl}}\left(\pi\left(\omega\left(\left[\frac{\sigma}{4^{n}},\frac{\sigma+1}{4^{n}}\right]\right)\right)\right)\\
&=\mathcal{H}^{1}_{\mathrm{Eucl}}\left(\omega^{\mathbb{C}}\left(\left[\frac{\sigma}{4^{n}},\frac{\sigma+1}{4^{n}}\right]\right)\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{4^{n}}\mathcal{H}^{1}_{\mathrm{Eucl}}\left(\omega^{\mathbb{C}}\left([0,1]\right)\right)\sim 2^{-k}.\end{split}

Hence (
4.20
) follows. Recall the construction
of the curve described after (
4.1
).
Proceeding as in
[
Juillet
]
, we rescale
\omega\left(\left[\frac{\sigma}{4^{n}},\frac{\sigma+1}{4^{n}}\right]\right)
using the similitudes of
\mathbb{H}^{1}
(see
[
Juillet
, Section 1.3]
) and we obtain another horizontal curve
\Gamma_{n}
which passes through the subset
\Lambda_{\vartheta_{n+1}}
given by

(4.21)

\left\{(-1,0,0),\left(-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{\tan\vartheta_{n+1}}{2},\frac{\tan\vartheta_{n+1}}{4}\right),\left(0,0,\frac{\tan\vartheta_{n+1}}{4}\right),\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{\tan\vartheta_{n+1}}{2},\frac{\tan\vartheta_{n+1}}{4}\right),(1,0,0)\right\}

and coincides with the curve obtained as
\Gamma
, but using the sequence of angles
(\vartheta_{n+m})_{m\geq 1}
. The scaling factor between
\Gamma_{n}
and
\omega\left(\left[\frac{\sigma}{4^{n}},\frac{\sigma+1}{4^{n}}\right]\right)
is

. Let us denote

and let

D_{\vartheta}^{1}:=\inf_{\ell\in\mathcal{V}_{1}}\fint_{\Gamma_{n}}d(y,\ell)\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}(y).

Easy computations show that

\begin{split}\beta^{\,\Gamma}_{1,\mathcal{V}_{1}}(x,A\,2^{-k})&\sim\frac{1}{2^{-k}}\inf_{\ell\in\mathcal{V}_{1}}\fint_{\Gamma\cap B(x,A\,2^{-k})}d(y,\ell)\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}(y)\\

&=\frac{1}{2^{-k}}\frac{l_{n}}{2}D_{\vartheta}^{1}\gtrsim D_{\vartheta}^{1}.\end{split}

where we used the 1-regularity of
\Gamma
, (
4.19
) and (
4.20

, recall (
4.2

In order to conclude (
4.18

, which is the content of the following claim.

Claim 1:

D_{\vartheta}^{1}\gtrsim\sqrt{\vartheta}
.

Assuming the validity of Claim 1, we now conclude that

B_{1,1}^{\Delta}(\Gamma):=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}2^{-k}\sum_{x\in\Delta_{k}}\beta_{1,\mathcal{V}_{1}}(x,A\,2^{-k})^{2}\gtrsim\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}2^{-k}\,2^{k}\,(D_{\vartheta}^{1})^{2}\gtrsim\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\vartheta_{\lceil k/2\rceil+1}\overset{\eqref{eq:theta_n}}{\sim}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\tfrac{1}{\lceil k/2\rceil+1}=+\infty.

It remains to prove Claim 1, that can be read as follows: there exists a constant
\widetilde{K}>0
(not depending on
n
, and in particular not on
\vartheta=\vartheta_{n+1}
) such that, for all

\fint_{\Gamma_{n}}d(y,\ell)\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}(y)\geq\widetilde{K}\sqrt{\vartheta}.

By

such that, for any

and any

(for

.
Let
0<n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\ell\in\mathcal{V}_{1}
. We want to show that there exists a subset
N\subseteq\Gamma_{n}
satisfying
\mathcal{H}^{1}(N)\geq c\,\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Gamma_{n})
, for some constant

independent of

and

, such that

(4.22)

d(y,\ell)\geq\frac{K}{4}\sqrt{\vartheta}\quad\text{for every $y\in N$.}

If this is possible, then we reach the conclusion by simple estimates:

\begin{split}\fint_{\Gamma_{n}}d(y,\ell)\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}(y)&=\frac{1}{\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Gamma_{n})}\int_{\Gamma_{n}}d(y,\ell)\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}(y)\geq\frac{1}{\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Gamma_{n})}\int_{N}d(y,\ell)\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}(y)\\
&\geq\frac{c}{\mathcal{H}^{1}(N)}\int_{N}\frac{K}{4}\sqrt{\vartheta}\,\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}(y)=\frac{cK}{4}\sqrt{\vartheta}=:\widetilde{K}\sqrt{\vartheta}.\end{split}

Hence, it remains to construct the set

with the previous properties.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\Gamma_{n,1}
be the polygonal curve which connects the points in

. Notice that

is a horizontal curve. We show now that there exists a subset

and such that

(4.23)

d(y,\ell)\geq\frac{K}{2}\sqrt{\vartheta}\quad\text{for every }y\in M.

Then we will construct the corresponding set

on
\Gamma_{n}
.

KQ\subset V_{Q}(\varepsilon^{2}{\rm diam}(Q))
.
The assumption
E\in\mathrm{WGL}(\beta_{\infty,\mathcal{V}_{k}})
implies a Carleson-packing condition for the remaining cubes
\mathcal{D}\setminus\mathcal{G}_{1}
(in

[
19
, p.5]
this is deduced from the assumption
E\in\mathrm{GLem}(\beta_{1,\mathcal{V}_{k}},2)
).

Lemma 6.3
.

There exists a constant
C=C(\varepsilon,K)
such that

(6.2)

\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}\setminus\mathcal{G}_{1},\,Q\subset R}\mu(Q)\leq C\mu(R),\quad R\in\mathcal{D}.

If
a good cube
Q\in\mathcal{G}_{1}
is well approximated by
some
horizontal plane
V\in\mathcal{V}_{k}
,
then this plane
V
must make a small angle with
V_{Q}
, as made precise in the following lemma.

Lemma 6.4
.

If
Q\in\mathcal{G}_{1}
and
V\in\mathcal{V}_{k}
is such that
Q\subset V(2K\varepsilon^{2}{\rm diam}(Q))
then
\angle(V_{Q},V)\leq 1+\varepsilon
.

This is

[
19
, Lemma 3.2]
, which holds choosing
\varepsilon
small enough depending on
K
.
Bearing in mind Remark
6.1
about the definition of angles, this is similar in spirit to
[
13
, Lemma 5.13]
in the Euclidean setting, although weaker in terms of the exponents of
\varepsilon
. Lemma

will be applied in Section

for each tree

6.3.
Building the coronization

We now describe how to build the coronization
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{G}\dot{\cup}\mathcal{B}
. This is done exactly as in
[
19
, p.6–7]
taking the parameter
\delta=\eta
(see also
[
13
, Section 7]
for the original Euclidean construction). The reader can think that the good cubes
\mathcal{G}
are simply the family
\mathcal{G}_{1}
from Section
6.2
. This will be true for bounded
E
, and in the case of unbounded
E
one selects a family
\mathcal{G}\subset\mathcal{G}_{1}
to run a suitable localization argument similar to
[
13
, p.38]
. The family
\mathcal{G}
still satisfies (
6.2
) and is then divided into a forest
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G})
of disjoint trees
\mathcal{S}
, which is constructed in such a way that the following properties hold:

(1)

Each
\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{F}
has a maximal element (with respect to inclusion), which will be denoted by
Q(\mathcal{S})
;

(2)

If
Q\in\mathcal{S},R\in\mathcal{D}
are such that
Q\subset R\subset Q(\mathcal{S})
, then
R\in\mathcal{S}
;

(3)

For each
Q\in\mathcal{S}
,
\angle(V_{Q},V_{Q(\mathcal{S})})\leq 1+\eta
;

(4)

If
Q\in\mathcal{S},\,\mathcal{C}(Q)\subset\mathcal{G}
and
\angle(V_{R},V_{Q(\mathcal{S})})\leq 1+\eta
for all
R\in\mathcal{C}(Q)
, then
\mathcal{C}(Q)\subset\mathcal{S}
;

(5)

If
Q\in\mathcal{S}
, then
Q\in\min(\mathcal{S})
if and only if
\mathcal{C}(Q)\setminus\mathcal{G}\neq\emptyset
(“
Q
has at least one bad child”) or there is
R\in\mathcal{C}(Q)
such that
\angle(V_{R},V_{Q(\mathcal{S})})>1+\eta
(“
Q
has at least one child
R
with a large angle between
V_{R}
and
V_{Q(\mathcal{S})}
”).

The forest
\mathcal{F}
is further subdivided as
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}_{1}\cup\mathcal{F}_{2}\cup\mathcal{F}_{3}
, where the conditions for the definition of the
\mathcal{F}_{i}
’s are stated in terms of properties of the minimal (with respect to inclusion) cubes of the trees
\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{F}_{i}
.
In particular, for a given tree
\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{F}
we set

\begin{split}&m_{1}(\mathcal{S}):=\{Q\in\min(\mathcal{S}):\mathcal{C}(Q)\setminus\mathcal{G}\neq\emptyset\},\\
&m_{2}(\mathcal{S}):=\min(\mathcal{S})\setminus m_{1}(\mathcal{S}).\end{split}

Then we define the partition of the forest
\mathcal{F}
as

\begin{split}\mathcal{F}_{1}&:=\left\{\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{F}:\mu\left(\bigcup_{Q\in m_{1}(\mathcal{S})}Q\right)\geq\mu(Q(\mathcal{S}))/4\right\},\\
\mathcal{F}_{2}&:=\left\{\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{F}:\mu\left(Q(\mathcal{S})\setminus\bigcup_{Q\in\rm{min}(\mathcal{S})}Q\right)\geq\mu(Q(\mathcal{S}))/4\right\},\\
\mathcal{F}_{3}&:=\left\{\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{F}:\mu\left(\bigcup_{Q\in m_{2}(\mathcal{S})}Q\right)\geq\mu(Q(\mathcal{S}))/2\right\}.\end{split}

Although the definitions are different, this is similar in spirit to the definitions used in the corresponding Euclidean proof
[
13
, p.39]
. This is because, if
Q\in m_{2}(\mathcal{S})
, then there exists
R\in\mathcal{C}(Q)
with
\angle(V_{R},V_{Q(\mathcal{S})})>1+\eta
.
The maximal cubes
Q(\mathcal{S})
for
\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{F}_{1}\cup\mathcal{F}_{2}
are easily seen to satisfy a Carleson packing condition. This is the statement of
[
19
, Lemma 4.2]
, which has a purely axiomatic proof that applies verbatim in our situation,
see also
[
13
, p.39, Lemma 7.4]
. Precisely, the result reads as follows:

Lemma 6.5
.

There exists a constant
C=C(\varepsilon,K)
such that

\sum_{\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{F}_{1}\cup\mathcal{F}_{2},\,Q(\mathcal{S})\subset R}\mu(Q(\mathcal{S}))\leq C\mu(R),\quad R\in\mathcal{D}.

The crux of the matter is to prove the corresponding Carleson packing condition for the remaining trees
\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{F}_{3}
. It is in the proof of this statement, which corresponds to

[
19
, (19)]
, that our assumption “
E\in\mathrm{GLem}(\beta_{1,\pi,A(2n,k)},2)
” will finally become relevant.
This is the context of the Section
6.4
, which forms the core of the proof. More precisely, the main deviation of our proof from Hahlomaa’s will appear in Section
6.4.3
.

6.4.1.
Step 1: Projections are roughly bi-Lipschitz on top cubes

In this step, we verify property (
3.20
) in the definition of Corona decomposition by horizontal planes (P-C). If
\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{F}
is constructed as in Section
6.3
, recall that

(6.3)

h_{\mathcal{S}}(x):=\inf\{d(x,Q)+{\rm diam}(Q):Q\in\mathcal{S}\}.

The main core of this step is the following result (see
[
13
, Lemma 8.4]
for its Euclidean counterpart).

Lemma 6.6
.

Choosing
K
large enough (depending on
K_{0}
and on
\eta
) and choosing
\varepsilon
small enough (depending on
\eta
) one has the following property for the trees constructed in Section
6.3
.
If
\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{F}
and
x,y\in K_{0}Q(\mathcal{S})
with
d(x,y)>\eta\,\min\{h_{\mathcal{S}}(x),h_{\mathcal{S}}(y)\}
, then

d(x,y)\leq(1+2\eta)d(P_{V_{Q(\mathcal{S})}}(x),P_{V_{Q(\mathcal{S})}}(y)).

Proof.

We follow the proof given in
[
19
, Lemma 4.3]
with
\alpha=1/\varrho
, paying attention to our weaker assumption on
d(x,y)
. Let
\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{F}
, let
x,y\in K_{0}Q(\mathcal{S})
and assume for instance that
d(x,y)>\eta\,h_{\mathcal{S}}(x)
. Then there exists
Q\in\mathcal{S}
such that

d(x,y)>\eta(d(x,Q)+{\rm diam}(Q)).

Let
R\in\mathcal{S}
be the minimal cube such that
Q\subset R
and
{\rm diam}(K_{0}R)\geq d(x,y)
. Notice that such a cube exists, since by assumption
{\rm diam}(K_{0}Q(\mathcal{S}))\geq d(x,y)
. Then we have the following properties:

(6.4)

\displaystyle d(y,R)\leq d(x,y)+d(x,R)

\displaystyle\leq d(x,y)+d(x,Q)<d(x,y)+\eta^{-1}d(x,y);

(6.5)

\displaystyle{\rm diam}(R)

\displaystyle\leq(D^{2}\varrho^{-1}+\eta^{-1})d(x,y).

Property (
6.5
) can be deduced in the following way: if
Q=R
, then

{\rm diam}(R)={\rm diam}(Q)<\eta^{-1}d(x,y)<(D^{2}\varrho^{-1}+\eta^{-1})\,d(x,y).

If instead
R\neq Q
, then there exists
\widehat{R}
which is a child of
R
and contains
Q
. Then, by properties of dyadic cubes, see (
3.4
),
{\rm diam}(R)\leq D^{2}\varrho^{-1}\,{\rm diam}(\widehat{R})
. Therefore, using the minimality assumption on
R
, we get

{\rm diam}(R)\leq D^{2}\varrho^{-1}\,{\rm diam}(\widehat{R})\leq D^{2}\varrho^{-1}\,{\rm diam}(K_{0}\widehat{R})\leq D^{2}\varrho^{-1}\,d(x,y)\leq(D^{2}\varrho^{-1}+\eta^{-1})\,d(x,y).

By (
6.4
) and the fact that
{\rm diam}(K_{0}R)\leq 2K_{0}{\rm diam}(R)
, we get

\begin{split}d(y,R)&\leq(1+\eta^{-1})\,d(x,y)\leq(1+\eta^{-1}){\rm diam}(K_{0}R)\leq 2K_{0}(1+\eta^{-1}){\rm diam}(R),\\
d(x,R)&\leq d(x,Q)\leq\eta^{-1}d(x,y)\leq\dots\leq 2K_{0}(1+\eta^{-1}){\rm diam}(R).\end{split}

Hence, choosing
K
big enough depending only on
K_{0}
and
\eta
(in particular
K\geq 2K_{0}(1+\eta^{-1})+1)
, we get
x,y\in KR
. Recall that
R
is well approximated by the horizontal plane
V_{R}\in\mathcal{V}_{k}
, so that
x,y\in V_{R}(\varepsilon^{2}{\rm diam}(R))
. Let
z,w\in V_{R}
be the points realizing
d(x,z)=d(x,V_{R})
and
d(y,w)=d(y,V_{R})
and let us denote
P:=P_{V_{Q(\mathcal{S})}}
. From the previous observation we get
d(x,z)\leq\varepsilon^{2}{\rm diam}(R)
and
d(y,w)\leq\varepsilon^{2}{\rm diam}(R)
. Moreover, by property (3) in the construction of the forest
\mathcal{F}
, we can assume
d(z,w)\leq(1+\eta)\,d(P(z),P(w))
.
Combining the previous estimates, using the triangular inequality and the 1-Lipschitz continuity of
P
,

\begin{split}d(P(x),P(y))&\geq d(P(z),P(w))-d(P(x),P(z))-d(P(y),P(w))\\
&\geq d(P(z),P(w))-d(x,z)-d(y,w)\\
&\geq(1+\eta)^{-1}\,d(z,w)-2\varepsilon^{2}{\rm diam}(R)\\
&\geq(1+\eta)^{-1}(d(x,y)-2\varepsilon^{2}{\rm diam}(R))-2\varepsilon^{2}{\rm diam}(R)\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{2prop}}}{{\geq}}((1+\eta)^{-1}-4\varepsilon^{2}(D^{2}\varrho^{-1}+\eta^{-1}))d(x,y)\\
&\geq(1+2\eta)^{-1}d(x,y),\end{split}

where the last estimate holds if we choose
\varepsilon>0
small enough depending on
\eta
.
∎

Having proved Lemma
6.6
, the last step to get a corona decomposition by horizontal planes is a Carleson packing condition for the trees
\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{F}_{3}
. This is the content of the remaining steps.

6.4.2.
Step 2: Associating a Lipschitz function
g:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n-k}
to a tree
\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{F}

In this step, we follow
[
19
, Section 5]
: there is no modification in the argument, but we reproduce it here for the convenience of the reader and to fix the notation that will be used in the next steps. This construction relies on the original Euclidean arguments contained in
[
13
, Section 8]
. We emphasize the perhaps surprising fact that for the construction of the corona decomposition, it suffices to construct good Lipschitz functions
g:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n-k}
, rather than Lipschitz functions
g:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{H}^{n}
. This is the fundamental reason why in Theorem
5.1
we only need to assume a geometric lemma for the
projection

\beta
-numbers.

Fix
\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{F}
and assume, up to a translation and a rotation of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
(which are isometries), that
V_{Q(\mathcal{S})}=\{x\in\mathbb{H}^{n}:x_{i}=0\,\text{ for all }i>k\}
.
Here,
rotation
is defined as in Definition
2.6
.
Our goal is to construct a (Euclidean) Lipschitz function
g:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n-k}
, with Lipschitz constant controlled by
\sqrt{\eta}
, whose graph approximates
\pi(K_{0}Q(\mathcal{S}))
well at the scales associated to cubes
Q\in\mathcal{S}
; see Lemma
6.7
for the precise statement.

For this construction, we let
P:\mathbb{H}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
be the projection on the first
k
-coordinates, that is,
P(x):=(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
. Identifying
V_{Q(\mathcal{S})}\equiv\mathbb{R}^{k}
, we can regard
P
as the horizontal projection
P_{V_{Q(\mathcal{S})}}
. We also denote by
P^{\perp}:\mathbb{H}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n-k}
to be the orthogonal projection defined as
P^{\perp}(x):=(x_{k+1},\dots,x_{2n}).

We then define
H:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}
by setting

H(p):=\inf\{h_{\mathcal{S}}(x):x\in P^{-1}(\{p\})\},

where the map
h_{\mathcal{S}}
is defined in (
6.3
). It is easy to see that
H
can be equivalently computed by

(6.6)

H(p)=\inf\{d_{\mathrm{Eucl}}(p,P(Q))+{\rm diam}(Q):Q\in\mathcal{S}\}.

Hence it follows that
H
is a 1-Lipschitz map, with respect to the Euclidean distance on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
.
We let
Z:=\{x\in E:h_{\mathcal{S}}(x)=0\}
and it follows from the Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem that
p\in P(Z)
if and only if
H(p)=0
.

The idea is then to construct
g
by setting
g(P(z))=P^{\bot}(z)
for
z\in Z
(using that
P|_{Z}
is injective with Lipschitz inverse by Lemma
6.6
) and apply Whitney-type arguments to extend
g
to
\mathbb{R}^{k}
(or more precisely, to a ball
U_{0}\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}
).
To this end, one partitions
\mathbb{R}^{k}\setminus P(Z)
into a countable union of disjoint (up to edges) standard dyadic cubes
\{R_{i}:i\in\mathbb{N}\}
of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
such that the following Whitney-type condition holds

(6.7)

20\,{\rm diam}(R_{i})\leq H(p)\quad\text{for every }p\in R_{i}.

We also require each
R_{i}
to be maximal (with respect to inclusion) among all cubes satisfying (
6.7
).
Fix
x_{0}\in Q(\mathcal{S})
and set

(6.8)

U_{j}:=B^{k}\big(P(x_{0}),2^{-j}K_{0}{\rm diam}(Q(\mathcal{S}))\big),

where
B^{k}
denotes the Euclidean ball in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
.
We then associate a good cube
Q_{i}\in\mathcal{S}
to each dyadic cube
R_{i}
intersecting
U_{0}
. Precisely, for each
i\in I_{0}:=\{j\in\mathbb{N}:R_{j}\cap U_{0}\neq\emptyset\}
, there exists
Q_{i}\in\mathcal{S}
with diameter comparable to
R_{i}
, in the sense that

C^{-1}{\rm diam}(R_{i})\leq{\rm diam}(Q_{i})\leq C{\rm diam}(R_{i})

for a suitable constant
C
depending only on
K_{0}
. The choice of
Q_{i}
can be performed in the following way: by maximality of
R_{i}
, it follows that
H(p)\leq 42\,{\rm diam}(R_{i})
for every
p\in R_{i}
. Fix
p\in R_{i}\cap U_{0}
. By (
6.6
), there exists
Q\in\mathcal{S}
such that
d(p,P(Q))+{\rm diam}(Q)<90\,{\rm diam}(R_{i})
. If
d(p,P(Q))\leq K_{0}\,{\rm diam}(Q)
, then we set
Q_{i}=Q
. Otherwise, we replace
Q
with a suitable ancestor
Q_{i}
such that
K_{0}^{-1}d(p,P(Q_{i}))\leq{\rm diam}(Q_{i})\leq K_{0}\,d(p,P(Q_{i}))
. The existence of such an ancestor is guaranteed by the fact that
d(p,P(Q(\mathcal{S})))\leq K_{0}\,{\rm diam}(Q(\mathcal{S}))
, since
p\in U_{0}
.

We are now going to construct the desired map
g
. First, for each
i\in I_{0}
, let
A_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n-k}
be the Euclidean affine function whose graph is
\pi(V_{Q_{i}})
. We also pick
\widetilde{\phi}_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to[0,1]
to be a
C^{2}
cutoff function assuming value 1 on
2R_{i}
, value 0 on
\mathbb{R}^{k}\setminus 3R_{i}
and such that

|\partial_{j}\widetilde{\phi}_{i}|\leq C{\rm diam}(R_{i})^{-1},\quad|\partial_{j}\partial_{m}\widetilde{\phi}_{i}|\leq C{\rm diam}(R_{i})^{-2}\quad\text{ for all }j,m\in\{1,\dots,k\}.

These functions will be used for a Whitney-type construction on
U_{0}\setminus P(Z)
.
On the other hand, for each
p\in P(Z)
there exists a unique point
x(p)\in E
such that
P^{-1}(\{p\})\cap K_{0}Q(\mathcal{S})=\{x(p)\}
: recall that this
is an immediate consequence of Lemma
6.6
.
Hence we can finally define the function
g:U_{0}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n-k}
by setting

(6.9)

g(p):=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i\in I_{0}}\widetilde{\phi}_{i}(p)A_{i}(p)}{\sum_{i\in I_{0}}\widetilde{\phi}_{i}(p)}&\text{ if }p\in U_{0}\setminus P(Z)\\
P^{\perp}(x(p))&\text{ if }p\in P(Z)\end{cases}

The main features of the map
g
are summarized in the following Lemma, which mimics the corresponding statement
[
13
, Proposition 8.2]
in the construction of the classical corona decomposition.

Lemma 6.7
.

Choosing
\eta\leq 1
,
K
large enough depending on
K_{0}
and
\eta
, and choosing
\varepsilon
small enough depending on
\eta
and
K
, the following property holds: if
g:U_{0}\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n-k}
is the function constructed as above, then there is a positive constant
C
such that

(i)

g
is
C\sqrt{\eta}
-Lipschitz;

(ii)

|P^{\perp}(x)-g(P(x))|\leq C\sqrt{\varepsilon}h_{\mathcal{S}}(x)
for all
x\in K_{0}Q(\mathcal{S})
;

(iii)

|g(p)|\leq CK_{0}\sqrt{\eta}\,{\rm diam}(Q(\mathcal{S}))
for all
p\in U_{0}
.

Proof.

Properties (i)-(iii) are proven in
[
19
, Lemma 5.3, Lemma 5.6, and Lemma 5.9]
, using, in particular, the properties of the planes
V_{Q}
stated in Lemma
6.4
and Lemma
6.6
, as well as property (3) of the tree
\mathcal{S}
in Section
6.3
. The definition (
6.9
) of
g
with the (
2\sqrt{\eta}
-Lipschitz) affine functions
A_{i}
allows one to deduce the
C\sqrt{\eta}
-Lipschitz continuity of
g
, the approximation of
\pi(K_{0}Q(\mathcal{S}))
by the graph of
g
, and the estimate for
|g(p)|
from this geometric information.∎

Remark 6.8
.

\eta
and

in Lemma

look different from the corresponding ones in the Euclidean predecessor

6.4.3.
Step 3:
Pushing estimates on
\beta_{1,\pi}
from
E
down to the graph of
g

The proof in this section follows the outline in
[
19
, Section 6]
, which in turn is modeled after the Euclidean predecessor
[
13
, Section 13]
. However, there is a crucial difference in that the upper bound in Lemma
6.9
is expressed in terms of the
projection

\beta
-numbers, rather than the potentially larger “full”
\beta
-numbers.

As in
[
19
, Section 6]
, we use Lemma
6.7
(i),(iii) to extend the map
g
constructed in the previous step to a
C\sqrt{\eta}
-Lipschitz function defined on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
with support in
U_{-1}
, where the latter is defined as in (
6.8
) for
j=-1
. We then introduce new flatness coefficients associated to
g
: for
p\in\mathbb{R}^{k},\,t>0
, we set

\gamma(p,t):=t^{-k-1}\inf_{a}\int_{B^{k}(p,t)}|g(u)-a(u)|\,du,

where the infimum is taken over all affine functions
a:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n-k}
, and integration is with respect to the Euclidean
k
-dimensional Hausdorff measure on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
. By Lemma
6.7
(i), if
\eta
is chosen small enough, then

(6.10)

\gamma(p,t)\leq 2t^{-k-1}\inf_{M}\int_{B^{k}(p,t)}d_{\rm Eucl}((u,g(u)),M)\,du

being the infimum computed among all
k
-planes
M
in
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
.

The next lemma, which improves
[
19
, Lemma 6.1]
, shows that actually it is possible to bound the
\gamma
-numbers by means of the projection
\beta
-numbers associated to
E
.

Lemma 6.9
.

Let
T=K_{0}{\rm diam}(Q(\mathcal{S}))/2
. Let
\{U_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be as Section
6.4.2
, (
6.8
).
There exists a positive constant
C=C(K_{0})>0
such that

(6.11)

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{U_{1}}\gamma(p,t)^{2}\,dp\frac{dt}{t}\leq C\varepsilon\mu(Q(\mathcal{S}))+C\varepsilon^{-6k}\int_{K_{0}Q(\mathcal{S})}\int_{h_{\mathcal{S}}(x)/K_{0}}^{T}\beta_{1,\pi,A(2n,k)}(x,K_{0}t)^{2}\frac{dt}{t}d\mu(x).

Proof.

For any
p\in U_{1}
and
H(p)/60<t\leq T
, by (
6.6
) there exists a point
z_{p,t}\in Q(\mathcal{S})
such that
|p-P(z_{p,t})|\leq 60t
. For any
z\in B(z_{p,t},t)\cap E
and
\delta>0
, we find
W=W_{z,p,t,\delta}\in A(2n,k)
such that

(6.12)

\int_{B(z,K_{0}t)}d_{\rm Eucl}(\pi(x),W)\,d\mu(x)\leq(K_{0}t)^{k+1}[\beta_{1,\pi,A(2n,k)}(z,K_{0}t)+\delta],

by definition of
\beta_{1,\pi,A(2n,k)}
-numbers. Thanks to (
6.10
), we get that

(6.13)

\displaystyle\gamma(p,t)

\displaystyle\leq 2t^{-k-1}\int_{B^{k}(p,t)}d_{\rm Eucl}((u,g(u)),W)\,du.

The core of the proof is now to estimate the integral
with respect to
\mathcal{H}^{k}

on the Euclidean ball
B^{k}(p,t)
by the corresponding one with respect to
\mu=\mathcal{H}^{k}|_{E}
on
B(z,K_{0}t)
, in order to combine (
6.12
) and (
6.13
) and get a pointwise estimate for
\gamma(p,t)
in terms of
\beta_{1,\pi,A(2n,k)}(z,K_{0}t)
. If
u\in B^{k}(p,t)\cap P(Z)
, then there exists a (unique, by Lemma
6.6
)
x\in K_{0}Q(\mathcal{S})
such that
P(x)=u
. Since
x\in Z
, then
h(x)=0
and
\pi(x)=(u,g(u))\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
.
It then follows by Lemma
6.6
and the triangular inequality that
d(x,z)\leq K_{0}t
, provided that
K_{0}
is chosen big enough. By 1-Lipschitz continuity of
P
, we have
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{Eucl}}^{k}(\Omega)\leq P_{\sharp}\mu(\Omega)
for
\Omega\subset B^{k}(p,t)\cap P(Z)
, and combining all this with (
6.12
), we find

(6.14)

\displaystyle\int_{B^{k}(p,t)\cap P(Z)}d_{\rm Eucl}((u,g(u)),W)\,du

\displaystyle\leq\int_{B^{k}(p,t)\cap P(Z)}d_{\rm Eucl}((u,g(u)),W)\,dP_{\sharp}\mu(u)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{P(B(z,K_{0}t))}d_{\rm Eucl}((u,g(u)),W)\,dP_{\sharp}\mu(u)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{B(z,K_{0}t)}d_{\rm Eucl}(\pi(x),W)\,d\mu(x)

\displaystyle\leq(K_{0}t)^{k+1}[\beta_{1,\pi,A(2n,k)}(z,K_{0}t)+\delta].

On the other hand, we need to estimate
d_{\rm Eucl}((u,g(u)),W)
for
u\in B^{k}(p,t)\setminus P(Z)
. This corresponds to
[
19
, Lemma 6.3, inequality (34), Lemma 6.4 and Lemma 6.5]
: here we limit ourselves to collect the intermediate estimates together. Let
I(p,t):=\{i\in I_{0}:R_{i}\cap B^{k}(p,t)\neq\emptyset\}
and notice that
B^{k}(p,t)\setminus P(Z)=\bigcup_{i\in I(p,t)}(B^{k}(p,t)\cap R_{i})
, since
B^{k}(p,t)\subset U_{0}
. Combining this observation with the aforementioned estimates in
[
19
]
one gets

\begin{split}&\int_{B^{k}(p,t)\setminus P(Z)}d_{\rm Eucl}((u,g(u)),W)\,du\\
&\lesssim\sum_{i\in I(p,t)}\sqrt{\varepsilon}{\rm diam}(R_{i})^{k+1}+\sum_{i\in I(p,t)}\varepsilon{\rm diam}(R_{i})\mathcal{L}^{k}(R_{i})+\varepsilon^{-3k}\int_{B(z,K_{0}t)}d_{\rm Eucl}(\pi(x),W)\,d\mu.\end{split}

We can now use our refined estimate (
6.12
) to get

(6.15)

\int_{B^{k}(p,t)\setminus P(Z)}d_{\rm Eucl}((u,g(u)),W)\,du\lesssim\sum_{i\in I(p,t)}\sqrt{\varepsilon}{\rm diam}(R_{i})^{k+1}+\varepsilon^{-3k}\,t^{k+1}[\beta_{1,\pi,A(2n,k)}(z,K_{0}t)+\delta].

Combining (
6.13
), (
6.14
) and (
6.15
), using the arbitrariness of
\delta>0
, we conclude that

(6.16)

\gamma(p,t)\lesssim\varepsilon^{-3k}\beta_{1,\pi,A(2n,k)}(z,K_{0}t)+\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,t^{-k-1}\sum_{i\in I(p,t)}{\rm diam}(R_{i})^{k+1}.

The final part of the proof, which consists in deducing the integral estimate (
6.11
) from the pointwise inequality (
6.16
), can be verbatim carried out as in Hahlomaa’s paper, starting from equation
[
19
, (38)]
onward, simply replacing the horizontal
\beta
-numbers
\beta_{1,\mathcal{V}_{k}}
with the projection
\beta
-numbers
\beta_{1,\pi,A(2n,k)}
.
∎

6.4.4.
Step 4: Bounding
\mu(Q(\mathcal{S}))
for
\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{F}_{3}
from above in terms of the
\beta_{1,\pi,A(2n,k)}
-numbers

In order to prove a packing condition for trees in
\mathcal{F}_{3}
defined at the beginning of Section
6.3
, we estimate the measure of the corresponding top cubes in terms of the
\beta_{1,\pi,A(2n,k)}
-numbers. This will allow us in Step 5 to make use of our assumption
E\in\mathrm{GLem}(\beta_{1,\pi,A(2n,k)},2)
to obtain the conclusion.

Lemma 6.10
.

Let
\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{F}_{3}
. Then

(6.17)

\mu(Q(\mathcal{S}))<{\varepsilon}^{-6k-1}\int_{{K_{0}}Q(\mathcal{S})}\int_{h_{\mathcal{S}}(x)/{K_{0}}}^{{K_{0}}\mathrm{diam}(Q(\mathcal{S}))}\beta_{1,\pi,A(2n,k)}(x,{K_{0}}t)^{2}\frac{dt}{t}\,d\mu(x).

Proof.

With Lemma
6.9
in place, the proof (by contradiction) reduces to a statement about the
\gamma
-numbers associated to
g
. This corresponds to
[
19
, Section 7]
and
[
13
, Sections 11 and 14]
. In fact, let
\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{F}_{3}
and notice that we can assume
V_{Q(\mathcal{S})}=\{x\in\mathbb{H}^{n}:x_{i}=0\,\text{ for all }i>k\}
since all quantities in Lemma
6.10
are invariant under group translations and rotations, including
\beta_{1,\pi,A(2n,k)}
, cf. Lemmas
3.12
and
3.13
. By contradiction, if (
6.17
) does not holds, then we would get by Lemma
6.9

(6.18)

\int_{0}^{K_{0}{\rm diam}(Q(\mathcal{S}))/2}\int_{U_{1}}\gamma(p,t)^{2}\,dp\frac{dt}{t}\leq C(K_{0})\varepsilon\mu(Q(\mathcal{S})).

This is exactly
[
19
, equation (41)]
; thus the rest of the proof works verbatim the same as in Hahlomaa’s paper. Arguments in this vein have appeared for instance also in
[
26
, Section 5]
or
[
13
, Section 14]
. One uses (
6.18
) to estimate the oscillation of the function
g
in a way that is incompatible with the assumption
\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{F}_{3}
, recalling that
\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{F}_{3}
implies that a large (in terms of
\mu
-measure) part of
Q(\mathcal{S})
is covered by cubes
Q\in m_{2}(\mathcal{S})
, which have at least one child
R
with
\angle(V_{R},V_{Q(\mathcal{S})})>1+\eta
. Indeed, (
6.18
), together with an application of Calderón’s reproducing formula (see for instance
[
42
, Chapter 5]
) for the function
g
, yields

\mu\left(\bigcup_{Q\in m_{2}(\mathcal{S})}Q\right)\leq C(K_{0})\varepsilon^{1/3}\mu(Q(\mathcal{S})),

provided that the parameters
K_{0},\eta,\varepsilon,K
are chosen appropriately. This leads to a contradiction with the definition of
\mathcal{F}_{3}
, by selecting
\varepsilon
small enough depending on
K_{0}
.
∎

6.4.5.
Step 5: A Carleson packing condition for top cubes of trees
\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{F}_{3}

In this step we prove the final ingredient for the construction of the corona decomposition by horizontal planes. We explain how to derive the Carleson packing condition for the set
\mathcal{F}_{3}
, which allows us to deduce property (
3.18
) in the definition of coronization.
This can be seen as a variant of arguments in
[
13
, Section 12]
.
Here we make use of our new assumption
E\in\mathrm{GLem}(\beta_{1,\pi,A(2n,k)},2)
.

Lemma 6.11
.

There is a constant
C=C(\varepsilon,K_{0})>0
such that

\sum_{\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{F}_{3},Q(\mathcal{S})\subseteq R}\mu(Q(\mathcal{S}))\leq C\mu(R),\quad R\in\mathcal{D}.

Proof.

Proceeding exactly as in
[
19
, Lemma 8.1]
, the Ahlfors regularity of
E
gives a bound
N=N(K_{0})\in\mathbb{N}
such that, for every
x\in E
and any
t\in\mathbb{R}
, there are less than
N
trees
\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{F}
satisfying
x\in K_{0}Q(\mathcal{S})
and
h_{\mathcal{S}}(x)/K_{0}<t<K_{0}\,{\rm diam}(Q(\mathcal{S}))
. Combining Lemma
6.10
of the previous step with this observation, one estimates

\begin{split}\sum_{\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{F}_{3},Q(\mathcal{S})\subseteq R}\mu(Q(\mathcal{S}))&\leq\sum_{\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{F}_{3},Q(\mathcal{S})\subseteq R}{\varepsilon}^{-6k-1}\int_{{K_{0}}Q(\mathcal{S})}\int_{h_{\mathcal{S}}(x)/{K_{0}}}^{{K_{0}}\mathrm{diam}(Q(\mathcal{S}))}\beta_{1,\pi,A(2n,k)}(x,{K_{0}}t)^{2}\frac{dt}{t}\,d\mu(x)\\
&\leq N\varepsilon^{-6k-1}\int_{K_{0}R}\int_{0}^{K_{0}{\rm diam}(R)}\beta_{1,\pi,A(2n,k)}(x,{K_{0}}t)^{2}\frac{dt}{t}\,d\mu(x).\end{split}

By (
3.4
) and by our main assumption
E\in\mathrm{GLem}(\beta_{1,\pi,A(2n,k)},2)
, we get the desired final bound.
∎
