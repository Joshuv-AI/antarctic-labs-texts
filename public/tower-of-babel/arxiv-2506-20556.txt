On the Erdős-Ko-Rado problem of flags with type { 1 , n − 3 } \{1,n-3\} of finite sets
Philipp Heering

Abstract




Abstract

A flag of a finite set
S
is a set
f
of non-empty, proper subsets of
S
, such that
X\subseteq Y
or
Y\subseteq X
for all
X,Y\in f
. Two flags
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
of
S
are opposite if
X_{1}\cap X_{2}=\emptyset
, or
X_{1}\cup X_{2}=S
for all
X_{1}\in f_{1}
and
X_{2}\in f_{2}
. The set
\{|X|\mid X\in f\}
is the type of a flag
f
. A set of pairwise non-opposite flags is an Erdős-Ko-Rado set.
In 2022 Metsch posed the problem of determining the maximum size of all Erdős-Ko-Rado sets of flags of type
T
with
|T|=2
. We contribute towards this by determining the maximum size for flags of type
\{1,n-3\}
for finite sets with
n
elements.
Furthermore we answer an open questions of Metsch regarding a small case.

Keywords:
Erdős-Ko-Rado sets, Kneser graphs, independence number, shifting

Introduction

The Erdős-Ko-Rado (EKR) theorem
[
9
]
, published in 1961, is a fundamental result in extremal set theory. It states that for a finite set
S
of size
n
, the maximum intersecting family of
k
-subsets of
S
, where
2k\leq n
, has size
{n-1\choose k-1}
. Since then, numerous generalizations and extensions of the problem have been studied across countless combinatorial structures
[
11
,
15
,
23
]
. One natural way to generalize results on sets is to consider vector spaces over finite fields, where
k
-subsets become subspaces of dimension
k
. Indeed it was shown in
[
13
,
20
]
that sets behave like the limit case of the vector space analogue.

More recently, a lot of interest has developed around EKR theorems for flags of spherical Tits buildings
[
2
,
6
,
21
,
28
]

Here, the natural notion of non-intersecting is opposition. For spherical Tits buildings of type
A_{n}
the problem can be describe as follows.

Let
\mathbb{F}_{q}
be the finite field of order
q
and consider the vector space
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
. A
flag

f
of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
is a set of subspaces, such that
X\leq Y
, or
Y\leq X
for all
X,Y\in f
and the set
\{\dim(X)\mid X\in f\}
is called the
type
of
f
. Two subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
are
opposite
if they intersect trivially or if they span the entire vector space. Two flags
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
are called
opposite
if and only if all elements of
f_{1}
are opposite to all elements of
f_{2}
. By
\Gamma_{q}(n,T)
we denote the graph, whose vertices are the flags of type
T
of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, two vertices are adjacent if and only if they are opposite as flags. Maximal independent sets of

have been studied extensively, we give some references in Table

.
Let
S
be a finite set with
n
elements, w.l.o.g. we assume
S=[n]:=\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Two subsets
X
and
Y
of
[n]
are called
opposite
if and only if the union of
X
and
Y
is
[n]
, or if the meet of
X
and
Y
is empty. This means that they are opposite if and only if their intersection is as small as possible.
A
flag

f
of
[n]
is a set of subsets of
[n]
, such that
X\subseteq Y
, or
Y\subseteq X
for all
X,Y\in f
and the set
\{|X|\mid X\in f\}
is called the
type
of
f
.
Two flags
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
of
[n]
are called
opposite
if and only if all elements of
f_{1}
are opposite to all elements of
f_{2}
.
By
\Gamma(n,T)
we denote the graph, whose vertices are the flags of type

of

\Gamma(n,T)

In contrast to the
q
-analogue situation, little research has been conducted; in fact, as far as the author is aware,
[
25
]
is the only atricle in which independent sets of
\Gamma(n,T)
are studied.

In
[
25
]
, Metsch posed the problem of determining the independence number of
\Gamma(n,T)
for all cases with
|T|=2
.
If
T=\{a,b\}
with
a<b
, we denote
\Gamma(n,T)
as
\Gamma(n,a,b)

and the independence number as
\alpha(\Gamma(n,a,b))
.
The independence number of
\Gamma(n,a,b)
is known if
a+b=n
, or
a,b\leq n/2
, furthermore the graphs
\Gamma(n,a,b)
and
\Gamma(n,n-b,n-a)
are isomorphic
[
25
]
. Some of the remaining non-trivial cases are also settled.

Theorem 1.1
.

[
25
]

For
n<2b
and
a+3b\leq 2n
we have
\alpha(\Gamma(n,a,b))={n-1\choose b}{b\choose a}
.

This was shown using a variation of the cycle method. Furthermore, using induction on
n
, the following result was obtained.

Theorem 1.2
.

[
25
]

For
n\geq 5
, we have
\alpha(\Gamma(n,1,n-2))={n\choose 3}+2
.

We remark that the induction used in the proof of Theorem
1.2
also works in a more general setting, however the required basis is out of reach for computers.

We introduce two tools that have not been considered in the framework of flags of finite sets. In the context of extremal set theory one technique that is used in many proofs is ‘shifting’
[
10
,
12
]
. We generalize this technique to flags of sets.

In countless areas of combinatorics a notion of ‘weight’ is used at some point, sometimes explicitly
[
1
,
22
]

and sometimes implicitly
[
4
,
7
]
. In the study of the independence number for Kneser graphs on flags of spherical Tits buildings a lot of proofs use some notion of weight extensively
[
4
,
18
,
19
]
, we also apply this notion to our framework.

Next, we state our main result.

Theorem 1.3
.

For
n\geq 9
, we have
\alpha(\Gamma(n,1,n-3))={n\choose 4}+42
.

In Section
3
we give two examples of independent sets for
n\geq 9
that have size
{n\choose 4}+42
. The values for
n<9
are given in Section
4
.

Note that our proof involves an induction, where the cases
n=9,10
are dealt with by the computer. These computer results rely on a result of
[
27
]
on the automorphism group of
\Gamma(n,a,b)
.
Using this, Section
4
also provides an answers for a question of Metsch concerning small
n
.

The expressions for the independence number of
\Gamma(n,1,n-3)
and
\Gamma(n,1,n-2)
are quite similar in nature. Even though the value
42
is not without cultural significance, the reason for the appearance of this number is not immediately apparent from our proof. Based on some calculations and the work of
[
25
]
, we conjecture for general
s\geq 2
that
\Gamma(n,1,n-s)
has independence number
{n\choose s+1}+\frac{1}{s}{s^{2}\choose s+1}
if
n
is large enough compared to
s
.

2
Preliminaries

For the rest of the paper let
a
,
b
and
n
be natural numbers with
a+b<n
and
a<\frac{n}{2}<b
. We denote the vertices of
\Gamma(n,a,b)
as pairs
(A,B)
with
|A|=a
and
|B|=b
. By flags of
\Gamma(n,a,b)
, we also denote the vertices of
\Gamma(n,a,b)
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
(A_{1},B_{1})
and
(A_{2},B_{2})
be two vertices of
\Gamma(n,a,b)
.
Then
(A_{1},B_{1})
and
(A_{2},B_{2})
are adjacent and hence opposite as flags if and only if the following conditions hold:

•

B_{1}
and
B_{2}
are opposite, i.e.
B_{1}\cup B_{2}=[n]
.

•

A_{1}
and
B_{2}
are opposite, i.e.
A_{1}\cap B_{2}=\emptyset
.

•

A_{2}
and
B_{1}
are opposite, i.e.
A_{2}\cap B_{1}=\emptyset
.

Proof.

This follows immediately from the definition of opposite and the conditions
\frac{n}{2}<b
and
a+b<n
.
∎

We study independent sets of
\Gamma(n,a,b)
, i.e. sets of flags that are pairwise non-opposite. Two flags are non-opposite if they do not satisfy at least one condition stated in Lemma
2.1
.

Remark 2.2
.

Let
(A_{1},B_{1})
and
(A_{2},B_{2})
be non-opposite flags.
Then either
|B_{1}\cap B_{2}|>2b-n
, or
|A_{1}\cap B_{2}|>0
, or
|A_{2}\cap B_{1}|>0
. In any case the intersection is large enough.

Assume that
|B_{1}\cap B_{2}|>2b-n
. If
(A_{3},B_{3})
is another flag, such that
|B_{1}\cap B_{3}|\geq|B_{1}\cap B_{2}|
, then by transitivity
(A_{1},B_{1})
and
(A_{3},B_{3})
are also non-opposite. For the other cases analogue statements hold.

2.1
Weights

First, we assign a weight to the
b
-subsets of
[n]
.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be an independent set of
\Gamma(n,a,b)
. For a subset
B\subseteq[n]
, with
|B|=b
, the number of flags in
\mathcal{F}
that contain
B
is called the
\mathcal{F}
-weight
of
B
or, if there is no risk of confusion, simply
weight
of
B
.

Trivially we have that the weight of
B
is
\leq{b\choose a}
, since there are
{b\choose a}
flags in
\Gamma(n,a,b)
that contain
B
. When assuming that the independent set in question is maximal, the number of possible weights decreases. We remark here that we distinguish between maximal independent sets and maximum independent sets, where maximum means largest.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a maximal independent set of
\Gamma(n,a,b)
and let
B\subset[n]
with
|B|=b
. The weight of
B
is
{b\choose a}
if and only if, for every flag
(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})\in\mathcal{F}
, one of the following conditions holds.

•

B
and
A^{\prime}
are non-opposite, i.e.
A^{\prime}\cap B\neq\emptyset
.

•

B
and
B^{\prime}
are non-opposite, i.e.
B\cup B^{\prime}\neq[n]
.

Proof.

If all flags
(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
in
\mathcal{F}
satisfy one of the two conditions it is clear that all flags
(A,B)
with
A\subseteq B
and
|A|=a
are in
\mathcal{F}
.

Now let us assume that
B
has weight
{b\choose a}
. Let
(A,B)
be a vertex of
\Gamma(n,a,b)
and assume that there is a flag
(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
in
\mathcal{F}
, so that
A
and
B^{\prime}
are non-opposite, but
B
and
A^{\prime}
as well as
B
and
B^{\prime}
are opposite.
Let
A_{0}
be a subset of
B
with
|A_{0}|=a
, such that
A_{0}\cap B^{\prime}=\emptyset
. Such a set exists, since
a+b<n
and
B\cup B^{\prime}=[n]
. The flag
(A_{0},B)
cannot be in
M
, since it is opposite to
(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
, but this is a contradiction as
B
has weight
{b\choose a}
. This implies that no flag in
\mathcal{F}
has the properties of
(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
.
∎

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a maximal independent set of
\Gamma(n,a,b)
,
and
B\subset[n]
with
|B|=b
.
If the weight of
B
is less than
{b\choose a}
, it is at most
{b\choose a}-{n-b\choose a}
.

Proof.

Let
B
be a subset of weight
<{b\choose a}
. Then,
by Lemma
2.4
,
there is a flag
(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
in
\mathcal{F}
, with
B\cup B^{\prime}=[n]
and
A^{\prime}\cap B=\emptyset
.
As
B\cup B^{\prime}=[n]
, we have
|B^{\prime}\cap B|=2b-n
.
For any flag
(A,B)
that is in
\mathcal{F}
we have that
A
contains
at least one of the
2b-n
elements of
B^{\prime}\cap B
. There are
b-(2b-n)=n-b
elements in
B\setminus B^{\prime}
, so any flag
(A,B)
with
A\subseteq B\setminus B^{\prime}
is not in
\mathcal{F}
. There are
{n-b\choose a}
such flags and the statement follows.
∎

For the case
(a,b)=(1,n-3)
we thus have that an
(n-3)
-set has weight
n-3
or at most
n-6
.

We can also assign weights to the
a
-subsets of
[n]
.

Definition 2.6
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be an independent set of
\Gamma(n,a,b)
. For
A\subseteq[n]
with
|A|=a
, the number of flags in
\mathcal{F}
that contain
A
is called the
\mathcal{F}
-weight
of
A
or, if there is no risk of confusion, simply the
weight
of
A
.

We prove an analogue of the Lemmata
2.4
and
2.5
only in the special case
(a,b)=(1,n-3)
.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a maximal independent set of
\Gamma(n,1,n-3)
,
and
A\subseteq[n]
with
|A|=1
.
The subset
A
has weight
{n-1\choose 3}
if and only if we have
A\subseteq B^{\prime}
for all flags
(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
of
\mathcal{F}
.
If the weight of
A
is less than

{n-1\choose 3}
, it is at most
{n-1\choose 3}-{n-4\choose 2}
.

Proof.

Assume that there is a flag
(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
in
\mathcal{F}
with
A\cap B^{\prime}=\emptyset
. The number of flags
(A,\tilde{B})
that are opposite to
(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
can be counted as follows. There are
2
elements in
[n]
that are not in
A\cup B^{\prime}
. Therefore
\tilde{B}
has to contain these two elements, and also
A
. Hence we have to choose
(n-3)-3
elements. We can choose out of all the remaining
n-3
elements of
[n]
except the one in
A^{\prime}
. This yields that there are
{n-3-1\choose n-3-3}={n-4\choose 2}
flags that contain
A
and are opposite to
(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
.
∎

2.2
Shifting

The original Erdős-Ko-Rado Theorem was proven with an operation on set systems that is known as shifting.
The shifting-operator is defined as follows.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
1\leq i,j\leq n
and
A\subseteq[n]
. The
i,j
-shift
S_{i,j}(A)
of
A
is defined as follows.

\displaystyle(A\setminus\{i\})\cup j\qquad

\displaystyle\text{if}\ i\in A\ \text{and}\ j\notin A,

\displaystyle A\qquad

else.

With this we can define shifting for flags and sets of flags of
[n]
. We write the vertices of
\Gamma(n,T)
as tuples.

Definition 2.9
.

Let
1\leq i,j\leq n
and let
f=(F_{1},\ldots,F_{t})
be a vertex of
\Gamma(n,T)
for some
T\subseteq[n]
with
|T|=t
. We define the
i,j
-shift of
f
as follows.

S_{i,j}(f):=(S_{i,j}(F_{1}),\ldots,S_{i,j}(F_{t})).

If
\mathcal{F}
is a set of vertices of
\Gamma(n,T)
and
f\in\mathcal{F}
, we define

\displaystyle S_{i,j}(f)\qquad

\displaystyle\text{if}\ S_{i,j}(f)\notin\mathcal{F},

\displaystyle f\qquad

else.

and

S_{i,j}(\mathcal{F}):=\{S_{i,j}^{\mathcal{F}}(f)\mid f\in\mathcal{F}\}.

It is easy to observe that for
A\subseteq B\subseteq[n]
, we have
S_{i,j}(A)\subseteq S_{i,j}(B)\subseteq[n]
, so shifts of flags are flags.
Note that
S_{i,j}
is a cardinality-preserving map on the power set
of
[n]
. Therefore, the
i,j
-shift of any flag of type
T
is also a flag of type
T
.

Lemma 2.10
.

If
f
and
f^{\prime}
are flags of
\Gamma(n,T)
that are non-opposite, then
S_{i,j}(f)
and
S_{i,j}(f^{\prime})
are also non-opposite.

Proof.

If
f
and
f^{\prime}
are non-opposite, there are sets
A\in f
and
A^{\prime}\in f^{\prime}
such that
A
and
A^{\prime}
are non-opposite.
If
|A|+|A^{\prime}|<n
, we have
A\cap A^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
, and if
|A|+|A^{\prime}|\geq n
, we have
A\cup A^{\prime}\neq[n]
. In any case
|A\cap A^{\prime}|
is large enough. However
|A\cap A^{\prime}|\leq|S_{i,j}(A)\cap S_{i,j}(A^{\prime})|
. Therefore
f
and
f^{\prime}
are also non-opposite.
∎

Note that if
f
and
f^{\prime}
are flags of
[n]
with type
T
that are opposite, then
S_{i,j}(f)
and
S_{i,j}(f^{\prime})
are not necessarily opposite. Take for example
n=4
and
T=\{2\}
. Then the flags
(\{1,2\})
and
(\{3,4\})
are opposite, but
S_{3,2}((\{1,2\}))=(\{1,2\})
and
S_{3,2}((\{3,4\}))=(\{2,4\})
are not opposite.

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be an independent set of
\Gamma(n,T)
for some
T\subseteq[n]
. Then
S_{i,j}(\mathcal{F})
is also an independent set of
\Gamma(n,T)
with
|\mathcal{F}|=|S_{i,j}(\mathcal{F})|
.

Proof.

We use
S
to denote
S_{i,j}
.
First, let us assume that
S(\mathcal{F})
is not an independent set.
Then, we can find two flags
f
and
f^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{F}
with
S^{\mathcal{F}}(f)
and
S^{\mathcal{F}}(f^{\prime})
being opposite. Since
f
and
f^{\prime}
are non-opposite, Lemma
2.10
implies that
S(f)
and
S(f^{\prime})
are non-opposite, therefore we can assume w.l.o.g. that
S^{\mathcal{F}}(f)=f
, since
S(f)
is already in
\mathcal{F}
, and
S^{\mathcal{F}}(f^{\prime})=S(f^{\prime})\neq f^{\prime}
.

Let
A\in f
and
A^{\prime}\in f^{\prime}
be such that
A
and
A^{\prime}
are non-opposite. As, by assumption
S(f^{\prime})
and
f
are opposite, we get that
S(A^{\prime})
and
A
are opposite.
In view of Remark
2.2
this implies
|A\cap S(A^{\prime})|<|A\cap A^{\prime}|
.
It follows that
i\in A\cap A^{\prime}
, as well as
j\notin A\cup A^{\prime}
. Hence, we have
|A\cap S(A^{\prime})|=|S(A)\cap A^{\prime}|
which implies that
S(A)
and
A^{\prime}
are opposite.

Now, let
A\in f
and
A^{\prime}\in f^{\prime}
be such that
A
and
A^{\prime}
are opposite.
Assume that
S(A)
and
A^{\prime}
are non-opposite. This implies

|S(A)\cap A^{\prime}|>|A\cap A^{\prime}|
and therefore

i\in A
and
j\notin A
as well as
j\in A^{\prime}
and
i\notin A^{\prime}
.
Since
f^{\prime}\neq S(f^{\prime})
there is a subset
B^{\prime}
in
f^{\prime}
with
j\notin B^{\prime}
and
i\in B^{\prime}
.
This implies
A^{\prime}\not\subseteq B^{\prime}
and
B^{\prime}\not\subseteq A^{\prime}
, which is a contradiction.
Hence,
S(A)
and
A^{\prime}
are opposite.

We have shown for any
A\in f
and
A^{\prime}\in f^{\prime}
that
S(A)
and
A^{\prime}
are opposite. This implies that
S(f)
and
f^{\prime}
are opposite, which is a contradiction since both are in
\mathcal{F}
.

We have shown that
S(\mathcal{F})
is an independent set, now we show that
|\mathcal{F}|=|S(\mathcal{F})|
, by showing that
S^{\mathcal{F}}
is injective.
Let
f,f^{\prime}
be in
\mathcal{F}
.
If
S^{\mathcal{F}}(f)=f
and
S^{\mathcal{F}}(f^{\prime})=f^{\prime}
we have
S^{\mathcal{F}}(f)=S^{\mathcal{F}}(f^{\prime})
if and only if
f=f^{\prime}
.
If
S^{\mathcal{F}}(f)=S(f)\neq f
and
S^{\mathcal{F}}(f^{\prime})=f^{\prime}
we cannot have
S^{\mathcal{F}}(f)=S^{\mathcal{F}}(f^{\prime})
via the definition of
S^{\mathcal{F}}
.

Now, assume
S^{\mathcal{F}}(f)=S(f)\neq f
and
S^{\mathcal{F}}(f^{\prime})=S(f^{\prime})\neq f^{\prime}
with
S(f)=S(f^{\prime})
.
Let
f=(F_{1},\ldots,F_{t})
and
f^{\prime}=(F^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,F^{\prime}_{t})
.
First, consider the case
S(F_{k})=F_{k}
and
S(F^{\prime}_{k})=F^{\prime}_{k}
for some
1\leq k\leq t
. Then clearly
F_{k}=F^{\prime}_{k}
, as
S(F_{k})=S(F^{\prime}_{k})
.
Next, consider the case
S(F_{k})\neq F_{k}
and
S(F^{\prime}_{k})\neq F^{\prime}_{k}
for some
1\leq k\leq t
. This yields
i\in F_{k},F^{\prime}_{k}
and
j\notin F_{k},F^{\prime}_{k}
. As
S(F_{k})=S(F^{\prime}_{k})
, we have
F_{k}=F^{\prime}_{k}
.

Finally, consider the case
S(F_{k})=F_{k}
and
S(F^{\prime}_{k})\neq F^{\prime}_{k}
for some
1\leq k\leq t
. This yields
i\in F^{\prime}_{k}
and
j\notin F^{\prime}_{k}
. As
F_{k}=S(F_{k})=S(F^{\prime}_{k})
, we have

A shift

with

If

is a left-shifted independent set. We use this in Section

From the definition of shifting on independent sets we immediately get the following.

Lemma 2.12
.

y

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be an independent set of
\Gamma(n,1,n-3)
with

Proof.

We count the number of flags containing
A
that are opposite to
(A_{1},B_{1})
or
(A_{2},B_{2})
.
The number of flags containing
A
that are opposite to one of those is, according to Lemma
2.7
exactly
{n-4\choose 2}
and

.
Now, we count the number of flags containing
A
that are opposite to both
(A_{1},B_{1})
and
(A_{2},B_{2})
.

Such a flag
(A,B)
must satisfy

as well as

.
So

has to be a subset of
B
.
We have
|B_{1}|=|B_{2}|=n-3
and
|B_{1}\cup B_{2}|=n-2

Therefore,

elements freely out of the remaining

elements. This yields

flags and the statement follows. ∎

(b) which implies
|\mathcal{F}_{1}|\geq 2{n-3\choose 3}-{n-4\choose 3}
. Using (
1
), this yields

(n-2)(n-3)\geq|\mathcal{F}_{2}|\geq|\mathcal{F}_{1}|\geq 2{n-3\choose 3}-{n-4\choose 3}

and this is a contradiction for
n\geq 11
.
∎

Corollary 5.5
.

For
n\geq 11
, we have
\alpha(\Gamma(n,1,n-3))\leq{n\choose 4}+42
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a maximum left-shifted independent set of
\Gamma(n,1,n-3)
.
By Lemma
5.4
, the subset
\{1\}
has weight
{n-1\choose 3}
. Lemma
2.7
implies that every flag
(A,B)
in
\mathcal{F}
satisfies
1\in B
.
Consider the set of flags
(A,B)
in
\mathcal{F}
with
A\neq\{1\}
.
By considering
(A,B\setminus\{1\})
for any such flag, we obtain an independent set of
\Gamma(n-1,1,n-4)
. In particular we have

\alpha(\Gamma(n,1,n-3))\leq{n-1\choose 3}+\alpha(\Gamma(n-1,1,n-4)).

For
n=11
, we have
\alpha(\Gamma(n-1,1,n-4))\leq{n-1\choose 4}+42
by Section
4
, so for general
n
this is the induction hypothesis and we have

\displaystyle\alpha(\Gamma(n,1,n-3))\leq{n-1\choose 3}+{n-1\choose 4}+42={n\choose 4}+42

as claimed.
∎

Together with Remark
3.3
and the results of Section
4
, this proves Theorem
1.3
.
