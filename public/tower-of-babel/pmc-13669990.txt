Oxidative Stress, Post‐Translational Modifications, and Type 1 Diabetes Pathogenesis
Authors: Colleen J. Winstead, Miki Hodel, Hubert M. Tse
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTType 1 diabetes (T1D) is an organ‐specific autoimmune disease that destroys insulin‐producing beta‐cells. Insulin is a vital hormone for regulating glycemia that often undergoes post‐translational modifications during processing from pre‐proinsulin to biologically active insulin. While necessary for proper glucose homeostasis, insulin is also an autoantigen recognized by autoreactive T cells in T1D. Cellular stress pathways including endoplasmic reticulum stress and oxidative stress mediated by an increase in reactive oxygen species (ROS) synthesis and/or a concomitant decrease in antioxidant function can further drive post‐translational modifications of various autoantigens in T1D including insulin. Innate immune‐derived ROS generation is an essential frontline defense against microbial infections, but ROS can also function as signaling intermediates to properly stimulate T cell responses. Oxidative stress‐induced post‐translational modifications are thought to trigger autoreactive T cells in T1D by generating novel neoepitopes, eliciting epitope spreading, or enhancing autoantigen interactions with antigen‐presenting cells. With the current technology, detecting post‐translationally modified proteins is challenging. In this review, we will explore the role of ROS and other post‐translational modifications involved in T1D. We will also discuss islet transplantation strategies being used for patients with T1D including novel cell sources for beta‐cell replacement and management of oxidative stress after transplantation.

The Genetics of Type 1 Diabetes (T1D)

T1D is an autoimmune disease characterized by chronic hyperglycemia due to direct, immune‐mediated destruction of insulin‐producing pancreatic beta‐cells [1]. Half a century of research has highlighted genetic factors contributing to pathogenesis, including the critical role of inherited high‐risk HLA alleles and polymorphisms associated with minor disease susceptibility genes including insulin, CTLA4, and the protein tyrosine phosphatase PTPN22 to name a few [2]. Certain HLA Class II loci in the human genome confer either susceptibility to or protection from T1D. These genes are generally concentrated in HLA‐DQ and ‐DR loci. The HLA genetics of the disease is quite complex; however, in that varying degrees of linkage disequilibrium involving hundreds of proximal genes contribute to the impact of these loci and are the basis for screening of newborns for estimation of genetic risk [3]. Insulin, the primary beta‐cell hormone responsible for regulation of blood glucose, is a major autoantigen in T1D. CTLA4 and PTPN22 genes play major roles in controlling T cell‐driven autoimmunity. They are responsible for imparting immunoregulatory control of T cell responses and modulating downstream antigen‐specific T cell receptor activation. Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in these genes may result in dominant negative activity, disrupting control and maturation of autoreactive T cell responses [4].

To aid in the genetic and immunologic understanding of T1D, the non‐obese diabetic (NOD) mouse is a spontaneous autoimmune‐mediated model of T1D involving numerous immune cells culminating in the destruction of insulin‐producing beta‐cells. As a surrogate model for human disease, there are many similarities including genetic susceptibility genes, autoantigens recognized by T cells, autoantibodies for biomarker prediction, and dysregulated immune mechanisms [5]. While not a perfect model, the NOD mouse has enabled researchers to test future disease‐modifying therapies to delay disease progression including anti‐CD3 treatment, which has resulted in the FDA approval of Teplizumab [6, 7].

Oxidative Stress, Redox Imbalance, and ER Stress in Beta‐Cells

It has been hypothesized that indirect environmental stresses also contribute substantially to dysglycemia and disease pathogenesis, affecting factors intrinsic to the beta‐cells themselves [4]. The pancreas is very sensitive to oxidative stress and possesses lower antioxidative capacity for neutralization of reactive oxygen species (ROS) than most tissues (e.g., muscle) [8]. There are several reports demonstrating that beta‐cells express lower levels of antioxidant genes including superoxide dismutase, catalase, and glutathione peroxidase [9, 10, 11, 12]. The beta‐cell sustains an extremely high biosynthesis rate due to its role in rapidly synthesizing insulin to properly regulate blood glucose, making it very vulnerable to oxidative stress that disrupts mitochondrial homeostasis and critical crosstalk with the endoplasmic reticulum (ER). This disruption leads to impairment of dynamic glucose sensing, driving alternative gene splicing and failure to remove misfolded proteins from the ER [13]. If prolonged, this stress can lead to accumulation of proteins (e.g., pre‐proinsulin) in the cell and failure of cytoprotective mechanisms including antioxidants, autophagy to clear damaged organelles, and activation of pro‐apoptotic signaling pathways [14]. This redox imbalance with pancreatic beta‐cells unable to synthesize sufficient levels of antioxidants is further amplified in the context of an autoimmune response. Innate immune cells will rapidly synthesize ROS and in the presence of a susceptible beta‐cell, this increase in oxidative stress will contribute to beta‐cell dysfunction and decreased viability [15].

The Unfolded Protein Response (UPR) as Cytoprotection Against Oxidative Stress

Proteins generated in the cytosol are shuttled to the ER for proper folding and modification necessary for cell membrane expression or secretion. Under stressful conditions disrupting ER equilibrium, the UPR is one of the cytoprotective mechanisms activated in the beta‐cell to restore homeostasis. The UPR is comprised of three stress‐sensing ER transmembrane proteins and their downstream signaling activities resulting in increased protein folding capacity, a reduction in translation initiation and new peptide folding, and upregulation of transcription factors essential for expression of chaperones, oxidoreductases, and quality control proteins [16, 17].

Prolonged activity of the UPR indicating failure to restore homeostasis results in apoptosis of the cell. The three transmembrane signaling proteins, inositol requiring enzyme 1 (IRE1), double‐stranded RNA‐activated protein kinase (PKR)‐like ER kinase (PERK), and activating transcription factor 6 (ATF6) function in parallel to activate UPR target genes [18]. ATF6 senses accumulation of unfolded proteins in the ER and is transported to the Golgi where it undergoes fragmentation. The nucleus‐targeting fragment of ATF6 then facilitates activation of ER‐resident proteins involved in protein folding, such as chaperones of heat shock protein families HSP70 and HSP90, and protein disulfide isomerase. PERK is activated and oligomerizes upon sensing ER stress. This kinase phosphorylates itself and the ubiquitous translation initiation factor eIF2alpha, inhibiting translation of most mRNA and reducing accumulation of unfolded protein in the ER. Under conditions where it senses sustained high, prolonged ER stress that it cannot regulate, PERK will facilitate eIF2alpha dephosphorylation and trigger apoptosis. The third signaling protein, IRE1, is a bifunctional kinase/endoribonuclease activated by lateral oligomerization in the ER membrane. In its active form, IRE1 splices X‐box binding protein 1 (XBP1) mRNA resulting in an active form of this transcription factor that is essential for regulation of lipid biosynthesis and ER homeostasis. In 2013, the Hotamisligil Lab at The Broad Institute of Harvard and MIT identified defects in UPR mediators ATF6 and XBP1 in both mice and humans. In the same study, the authors demonstrate feasibility of targeting these defects with a chemical ER stress mitigator as a therapeutic intervention for T1D [19].

Common, Naturally Occurring Post‐Translational Modifications in Beta‐Cells from Patients With T1D

In addition to these metabolic defects, environmental stress induces release of calcium from the ER and activation of cytosolic enzymes (UPR) in the beta‐cell that induce specific post‐translational protein modifications (PTMs) [20]. PTMs refer to the breaking or generation of covalent bonds on protein backbones or amino acid side chains. They are not genetically encoded, allowing dynamic protein property alterations on a timescale supporting organismal complexity in both healthy and diseased states [21]. Homeostasis of these modifications is important for maintenance of human health. In healthy pancreatic beta‐cells, proinsulin is proteolytically cleaved into active insulin and C‐peptide—a normal and necessary function [22]. Failure in this particular PTM leads to aggregation of disulfide‐bonded proinsulin complexes and accumulation in patients with T1D. As mentioned above, many other PTMs are activated in beta‐cells in response to stress. Deamidation

Often spontaneous, this non‐enzymatic PTM removes an amide group from the side chains of asparagine or glutamine, resulting in altered protein structure and charge. This PTM is highly associated with protein aging, neurodegenerative diseases (e.g., Alzheimer's), and autoimmune diseases such as T1D. Most deamidations originate from glycosylated proteins that have undergone de‐glycosylation via the ER‐associated protein degradation, quality control pathway (ERAD) [24]. Deamidation of insulin‐derived epitopes may also facilitate increased HLA‐binding as predictive studies with HLA‐I binding peptides analyzed from the Immune Epitope Database demonstrated that deamidation (in addition to other PTMs discussed below including citrullination, oxidation, and chlorination) enhanced binding compared to control peptides [25]. The Roep Lab at the Leiden University Medical Center in the Netherlands published evidence in 2014 of proinsulin deamidation by tissue transglutaminase that increased binding affinity of epitopes (InsB30‐C13) to T1D‐associated HLA‐DQ8 [26].

Citrullination

Mediated by Peptidylarginine Deiminase (PAD) enzymes, citrullination is the enzymatic conversion of the amino acid arginine into the non‐standard amino acid citrulline. Beta‐cells synthesize intracellular calcium under stress, hyperactivating PAD2 and PAD4 which modify key islet autoantigens, such as glucokinase (GK), glutamic acid decarboxylase 65 (GAD65), and 78‐kDa glucose‐regulated protein (GRP78). Citrullinated glucokinase, for example, not only becomes an autoimmune target for T cells but also loses its ability to catalyze glucose phosphorylation, directly impairing glycolysis and insulin secretion [27].

Carbonylation

This PTM describes the introduction of a carbon monoxide molecule into an organic or inorganic substrate. The beta subunit of prolyl‐4‐hydroxylase (P4Hb), otherwise known as protein disulfide isomerase (PDI), is a molecular chaperone highly susceptible to carbonylation during early stages of T1D autoimmunity. When carbonylated, P4Hb loses its ability to fold proinsulin properly, leading to increased circulating levels of misfolded proinsulin and an abnormal proinsulin‐to‐insulin ratio and a decrease in glucose‐stimulated insulin release. Modified P4Hb proteins and accumulated misfolded proinsulin also act as neoantigens, triggering autoreactive T cell responses [28, 29, 30].

Glycation

While enzymatic glycosylation is a regulated process that aids in proper protein folding and function, glycation is a spontaneous, non‐enzymatic reaction where excess blood glucose binds to proteins, lipids, or nucleic acids. Higher intake of nitrates, nitrites, and N‐nitroso compounds from highly processed food with high amounts of refined carbohydrates and lower wholegrain content can trigger chronic hyperglycemia, which in turn accelerates glycation and produces irreversible sugar‐protein bonds known as advanced glycation end products (AGEs) [31]. These AGEs cross‐link with structural proteins (like collagen) and trigger cellular damage, contributing significantly to long‐term micro‐ and macrovascular disease and tissue complications of T1D, such as diabetic nephropathy, retinopathy, neuropathy, and cardiomyopathy [32]. AGEs have also been implicated in elevating inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) production, exacerbating developing T1D inflammation in pancreatic islets, resulting in reduced ATP synthesis and insulin secretion.

Oxidation/Disulfide Bonding

While balanced in a healthy state for stabilization of antioxidant defense, insulin protein folding in the ER, and apoptosis, the ratio of thiol groups (‐SH) to reversible disulfide bonds (‐S‐S‐) is altered in beta‐cells of children and adults with T1D in favor of covalent bonds between sulfur atoms and the formation of disulfides [33]. The high ratios of disulfide‐to‐native thiol in adult patients have been correlated with C‐reactive protein and chronic innate and adaptive inflammatory responses [34].

Sulfenylation

High ROS levels produced during early‐stage islet inflammation oxidize the thiol (‐SH) group of specific cysteine residues in proteins, forming sulfenic acid. Unlike protein carbonylation, which is typically permanent and destructive, sulfenylation acts as a reversible redox‐sensing switch. It regulates protein activity, cell signaling, and maintains balance in the cell as it responds to oxidative stress. In pancreatic beta‐cells, prolonged oxidative stress can lead to hyper‐sulfenylation or cause sulfenic acid to irreversibly oxidize into sulfinic or sulfonic acid. This damages cellular redox signaling pathways, causes protein misfolding, and impairs glucose‐stimulated insulin secretion, eventually inducing beta‐cell failure [35].

Phosphorylation

Protein phosphorylation is defined as the addition of a phosphate group by kinases to serine, threonine, and tyrosine, and is a vital, reversible regulatory mechanism for control of glucose sensing and insulin synthesis and secretory granule exocytosis. As mentioned above, SNPs in protein tyrosine phosphatase non‐receptor type 22 (PTPN22) are associated with the development of T1D. This gene encodes a tyrosine phosphatase: a negative regulator of T cell receptor signal transduction, itself regulated via phosphorylation. Studies suggest this mutated gene is redox‐regulated, in that mutant PTPN22 interaction with a component of the NADPH oxidase 2 (NOX2) complex, Ncf1, is essential for the induction of ROS in APCs responsible for driving T1D [36, 37].

O‐GlcNAcylation

A key PTM in the nucleus, cytosol, and mitochondria, O‐GlcNAcylation regulates beta‐cell function, insulin biosynthesis, and immune responses. It is a highly dynamic enzymatic process where O‐GlcNAc transferase (OGT) attaches a single sugar to serine/threonine residues of target proteins, and O‐GlcNAcase (OGA) removes it. O‐GlcNAcylation acts as a nutrient sensor, regulating key transcription factors like pancreatic/duodenal homeobox‐1 (PDX1) and NeuroD1, transcriptional regulator of the insulin gene. In T1D, fluctuating glucose levels and metabolic stress cause aberrant O‐GlcNAcylation, impairing glucose tolerance, decreasing sensitivity to insulin, and triggering beta‐cell apoptosis [38, 39].

Acetylation

By adding an acetyl group to lysine or any amino acid N‐terminal amino group, the action of acetyltransferases changes a protein's shape and charge, directly impacting chromatin structure, gene expression, and protein function. Maintaining a proper acetylation balance is critical for beta‐cell survival, as hyper‐acetylation can sometimes lead to impaired failure of glucose to regulate insulin secretion, increasing cellular stress. Histone deacetylases (HDACs) are enzymes that remove acetyl groups. Extrinsic to the beta‐cells, potentially pathogenic Th17 and regulatory T cells (Tregs) experience altered epigenetic regulation by acetyltransferases and HDACs at inflammatory cytokine and immune‐tolerance genetic loci, respectively, leading to unregulated autoimmune attack. In T1D research, chemical inhibitors that block HDACs are being studied for their potential to repress T cell inflammatory genes and allow pancreatic beta‐cell regeneration [40, 41].

Ubiquitination

Ubiquitination is the process of covalently attaching ubiquitin molecules to target proteins to dictate their cellular fate—recycling or degradation. Under normal conditions, ubiquitination marks misfolded or damaged proteins for destruction by the proteasome, which is vital for maintaining insulin gene transcription and secretion [42]. During diabetes‐induced oxidative stress, ubiquitin‐protein aggregates form in the cytoplasm that are cleared via autophagy, suggesting autophagy as another beta‐cell cytoprotective process in T1D [43]. In regard to inflammation‐induced autoreactive T cell activation, ubiquitination may alter proteolytic processing and presentation of modified protein epitopes on MHC Class I, potentiating autoimmune beta‐cell destruction.

Palmitoylation

Palmitoylation involves the reversible attachment of the fatty acid palmitate to specific cysteine residues in membrane‐associated proteins. This lipidation makes proteins highly hydrophobic, allowing them to bind tightly to cell membranes, associate with lipid rafts, and traffic properly [44]. S‐palmitoylation dynamically regulates pancreatic beta‐cell insulin synthesis, glucose‐stimulated insulin granule exocytosis, and immune responses, acting as a critical checkpoint in T1D disease progression. Dysregulation of palmitoylation, palmitoyltransferases, and acyl‐protein thioesterases can lead to protein misfolding, abnormal protein localization, and insulin hypersecretion [45, 46].

SUMOylation

SUMOylation is defined as the addition of an isopeptide bond consisting of the 97 amino acid Small Ubiquitin‐like Modifier (SUMO) peptides to target protein amine groups of lysine residues. Under diabetic stress this PTM is important for the activity of specific metabolic enzymes (such as GAPDH) and acts as a break on glucose‐stimulated insulin secretion [47, 48]. A specific SNP (M55V) in SUMO4 is strongly linked to T1D risk. This mutation impairs the normal ability of SUMO4 to modify IκBα, which in turn leads to hyperactive NF‐κB signaling and an up‐regulation of pro‐inflammatory cytokines that drive autoimmune beta‐cell destruction [49]. SUMOylation of Interferon Regulatory Factor 4 (IRF4) promotes its stability and drives protective, anti‐inflammatory macrophage polarization, and altered or diminished SUMOylation in these immune cells can disrupt energy homeostasis, exacerbate beta cell oxidative stress, and increase the risk of T1D [50].

Covalent Crosslinking Creating Fusion Peptides

Formed in the secretory granules of beta‐cells through peptide bond formation from two separate peptides not previously linked. Using the NOD mouse model, the Haskins Lab at the University of Colorado School of Medicine found hybrid insulin peptides (HIPs) that are fusions between fragments of proinsulin and peptide sequences from other beta‐cell granule proteins such as chromogranin A (ChgA) and islet amyloid polypeptide (IAPP). The authors validated these novel HIPs in vitro with both peripheral mouse CD4 T cells and human islet‐infiltrating T cells [51]. We refer the reader to a review on hybrid insulin peptides by Haskins et al. in this special Immunological Reviews edition on Post‐translational modifications of antigens in Autoimmunity [52].

The Contribution of Chemical Contaminants to Beta‐Cell Oxidative Stress

T1D can be triggered or exacerbated by exposure to environmental toxins [53]. For Type 1 diabetes, higher intake of nitrates, nitrites, and N‐nitroso compounds from food, as well as higher serum levels of polychlorinated biphenyls from exposure to legacy industrial or household electrical equipment and consumption of fish that have bioaccumulated these toxins in fat have been associated with increased risk. For example, the common household chemical and industrial solvent trichloroethylene has been found to induce protein oxidation leading to modifications, increased antigenicity, and disease‐inducing breaks in T cell immune tolerance [54].

The Impact of Viral Infection on T1D and Redox Signaling

Enteroviruses are among the most common human pathogens worldwide and are environmental triggers for T1D [55]. The ssRNA virus Coxsackievirus B (CVB) has been implicated in acceleration of disease onset on the basis of detection in pancreatic islets of T1D patients and temporal correlation of infections with autoantibody (aAb) seroconversion [56, 57, 58]. CVB targets immune receptors on the surface of cells (the Coxsackie and adenovirus receptor or ‘CAR’) and is detected once entry is achieved via Toll‐like receptor 3 (TLR3) and the innate viral sensor melanoma differentiation‐associated protein 5 (MDA5), which both initiate antiviral signaling including synthesis of Type 1 interferons (e.g., IFNα and IFNβ) [59, 60] (Figure 2A). CVB may establish long‐term noncytopathic infection, preceding the onset of insulitis, during which the virus co‐opts the host cell machinery for its own replication including PTM‐promoting enzymes. For example, CVB3 will disrupt host cell cycling via ubiquitin‐dependent proteolysis of cyclin D1 [61]. CVB3 will also facilitate phosphorylation of the Xbp1/Ire1 branch of the UPR, down‐modulating host cell ER stress sensing [62]. Viral persistence, characterized by low levels of viral replication is facilitated by at least three immunoregulatory pathways: (1) reduced number of pancreatic lymph node APCs and/or down‐regulation of MHC Class I, limiting presentation of viral antigens to CD8 T cells, (2) upregulation of programmed death ligand 1 (PD‐L1) on lymphoid cells, preventing expansion of diabetogenic, PD‐1‐expressing CD8 T cells, and (3) upregulation of MHC Class II, enhancing the number of regulatory CD4 T cells (Tregs) maintaining peripheral tolerance [25, 63, 64, 65]. During the acute phase of infection during which viruses are replicating in these cells, beta‐cell destruction leads to release of both viral and sequestered islet antigens [66] (Figure 2B). Some of the viral antigens display autoantigen epitope mimicry (GAD homology to P2C protein of CVB; tyrosine phosphatases IA‐2 and IAR molecular similarity to VP1 viral capsid protein), in that both T cell responses and autoantibodies specific to these autoantigens are enriched in the periphery of CVB‐infected T1D patients. Pro‐inflammatory macrophages respond to and phagocytose the infected and dying beta‐cells and travel to the pancreas‐draining lymph nodes. Some of these beta‐cell antigens (proinsulin and GAD65, for example) have undergone oxidative post‐translational modifications (oxPTM) in the islet allowing processed epitopes to bind to MHC with higher affinity, effectively creating secondary or ‘cryptic’ antigens through epitope spreading, allowing them to escape peripheral tolerance and activate dormant autoreactive T cells in the lymph nodes [25, 65, 67] (Figure 2C,D). This concept of epitope spreading and loss of T cell tolerance is discussed in more detail in the context of detection methods below.

ROS, such as superoxide, hydrogen peroxide, peroxynitrite, and hydroxyl radicals, are created by the reaction of oxygen free radicals leaked from the mitochondria during normal cellular respiration and with cytosolic enzymes. Under normal conditions, ROS can also act as a potent innate antimicrobial defense [68]. The NADPH oxidase (NOX) complex, expressed primarily by resident and infiltrating innate immune cells early in the disease process, is the predominant source of superoxide in the pancreas during T1D development [69, 70]. Chronic inflammation associated with the disease exacerbates the production of this and other cellular oxidants within the pancreatic islets, facilitating the secretion of inflammatory cytokines by macrophages and other professional antigen‐presenting cells (dendritic cells and B cells) both resident to and infiltrating the islets. We have shown that the antiviral response is also affected by the generation of NOX2‐derived superoxide. In parallel with activation of antigen processing and presentation machinery in the cell, phagocytosis of infected beta‐cells creates a cascade of superoxide and proinflammatory cytokines that potentiate autoreactive T cell responses and reinforce the pro‐inflammatory macrophage phenotype [71]. Infection of NOD.Ncf1 m1J mice lacking functional p47‐phox protein with the CVB3 serotype did not accelerate T1D compared to NOD mice, further suggesting that NOX2‐derived superoxide can influence antiviral signaling pathways including TLR3 and MDA5 [59, 72].

The influence of CVB infection on presentation of post‐translationally modified neoantigens in individuals predisposed to T1D, the functional phenotype of autoantigen‐specific T responses induced, and the impact on evolution of disease is an area of research that is currently lacking.

Detecting PTMs in T1D

The identification and study of PTMs, though incredibly important in cellular biology, remains technically very challenging. PTMs are dynamic in nature and cells follow functional cues to enzymatically apply, and sometimes remove, protein modifications. Proper analysis of PTMs often requires isolation and study of both modified and unmodified protein forms, and PTMs may be very heterogenous, multi‐site or site‐specific (e.g., phosphorylation) as well as quite infrequent, making robust analysis very difficult. In lieu of discussion on chemical methods for synthesis of PTMs and complementary isolation of substrate proteins, this review will briefly discuss traditional methods for detection of PTMs from tissues, focusing on those most relevant in T1D [73, 74].

As beta‐cells in the islets of Langerhans of pancreata (both mouse and human) are the primary targets for autoimmune destruction in T1D, ideal sample sources for isolation and analysis of PTMs are islets harvested from mice or cadaveric human donors. Though some inherent heterogeneity imparted by tissue context is lost in generating them, islet organoids and clonal beta‐cell lines may also serve as informative substrates for PTM analysis (Figure 3A1). The most widely accepted and specific methods for isolation of modified proteins involve the use of modification‐specific monoclonal antibodies (e.g., anti‐K‐epsilon‐GG for ubiquitinated proteins, anti‐AcK for acetylated proteins, and anti‐diglycine remnant for SUMOylated proteins) and immunoaffinity techniques. Antibodies do not exist for every modification, however, as many global protein modifications are transient or of low affinity/stoichiometry, necessitating more sophisticated chemical isolation methodology for their study [75]. Phosphorylation, the most frequent protein/peptide modification recognized in both healthy and diseased states, is one such example, often requiring enrichment from starting samples with metal oxide supports (TiO2) or immobilized metal ions in addition to anti‐phosphotyrosine immunoaffinity [76]. Glycopeptides are selectively captured via lectin affinity chromatography or liquid chromatography designed to isolate polar/hydrophilic substrates, and S‐nitrosylated proteins are captured using thiol‐ or biotin tag‐based affinity resins. The common technique for isolation of disulfide‐linked proteins from tissues is comparatively chemically simple, in that non‐reducing lysis and size‐exclusion chromatography will enrich these targets (Figure 3A2).

Sensitive analysis of samples enriched for protein modifications is most commonly done via high‐resolution liquid chromatography—tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS), wherein proteins are first physically separated in liquid, then ionized for fragmentation and counting for further in silico identification and mass quantification [77]. When paired with algorithmic prediction software designed to localize a particular PTM within a protein with high probability (machine learning), the effect a particular PTM has on that protein's function can be reliably predicted (Figure 3A3) [78]. Rough quantification of modified proteins can be performed via targeted detection and is largely dependent on starting sample type. Parallel Reaction Monitoring (PRM) is preferred for low through‐put, high resolution analysis of complex samples (e.g., pancreatic tissue) with unknown composition requiring a clearly delineated expression background. For high‐throughput clinical sampling of predefined precursor‐to‐fragment ion pairs from limited samples (e.g., chemically synthesized post‐translationally modified proteins), Multiple Reaction Monitoring (MRM) is recommended. Absolute quantification of modified proteins can be achieved with the use of synthetic heavy peptides as spiked tracers (AQUA) or amino acid stable isotope labeling (SILAC) to capture the spectra of specific proteins (e.g., GAD65) bearing specific PTMs (e.g., citrulline) [79] (Figure 3A4). Additional integration of bioinformatic analysis of proteomics data with data from ‘alternative’ omics platforms, such as traditional and spatial transcriptomics, metabolomics, and lipidomics, as well as use of machine learning and statistical modeling may enable diagnostic biomarker discovery and allow population‐wide predictive modeling of PTMs in T1D [80] (Figure 3A7).

Our understanding of T1D pathology and the role PTMs play in shaping that pathology requires a combined beta‐cell and T cell perspective [81]. Identification of the actual modified peptide presented by beta‐cells and other APCs to T cells provides a crucial half of this combination. Immunopeptidomics involves systematic study of the peptides bound to major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules on cell surfaces, providing real‐world evidence of protein processing and presentation beyond what may be predicted in silico [82]. Similar to the workflow for enrichment of modified proteins discussed above, immunopeptidomics requires initial antibody‐based isolation of peptide–MHC complexes from tissue/cell lysates by immunoaffinity purification, followed by peptide elution, and identification by LC–MS/MS (Figure 3A6). In vitro confirmation of T cell engagement and activation by neoantigens in the context of diabetes‐susceptible MHC alleles provides the other crucial half of the combined perspective, allowing investigation into the pathogenic phenotype of these autoreactive T cells [83].

Insulin PTMs and the Loss of T Cell Tolerance

PTM proteins, processed and presented as immunogenic, non‐native autoantigens on beta‐cells, are never presented to developing T cells in the thymus. As a result, T cells normally subject to and eliminated from the peripheral repertoire via central tolerance recognize these antigens in the periphery (Figure 3B). These ‘neoantigens’ are located in secretory granules in islets and also generated during antigen processing by professional antigen‐presenting cells (APCs) that present to autoreactive, diabetogenic T cells in pancreatic draining lymph nodes (PLNs) [84]. Hyperglycemia and elevated free fatty acid levels in the blood drive the production of ROS by both innate and adaptive immune cells in the T1D pancreas. The elevated glycolytic flux and increased calcium release from the ER inhibits properly folded proinsulin secretion in a nitric oxide‐dependent manner and induces the expression of chemokines and proinflammatory cytokines essential for autoreactive T cell maturation. Techniques for pairing of the immunopeptidome detection workflow with that for identification and quantification of modified peptides, as discussed above, have very recently been applied to identification of T1D autoantigens by two academic groups: the Stern Lab at the University of Massachusetts Chan Medical School and the Lichti and Wan Labs at the University of Washington in St. Louis. The Stern Lab identified 28 novel MHC Class I‐ and II‐binding autoantigens from in vitro‐stimulated cadaveric human islets and validated those native and post‐translationally modified epitopes—including the islet molecular chaperone protein Secretogranin V (SCG5) and the islet secretory granule proteins Chromogranin A (CHGA) and Chromogranin B (CHGB) – by demonstrating stimulation and expansion of functional (IFN‐γ‐secreting) T1D patient CD4 and CD8 T cells in vitro [85]. The Lichti and Wan Labs, using similar techniques and samples to the Stern Lab, identified a cysteine to serine thiol oxidation PTM of insulin (INS C19S) that occurs in T1D patients and animal models as a result of oxidative stress. The authors demonstrated that this neoantigen, when presented by high‐risk MHC Class II human and mouse alleles HLA‐DQ8 and I‐A g7 , respectively, on APCs in the pancreatic draining lymph nodes, activates and expands autoreactive and inflammatory CD4 T cells to drive disease progression [86].

While beta‐cell PTMs may contribute to autoreactive T cell activation and the loss of peripheral tolerance in T1D, they also have important implications for therapeutic strategies to restore beta‐cell mass and function. As discussed below, islet transplantation is a strategy providing experimental and clinical context in which one may investigate the effects of PTMs in beta‐cells on autoimmune re‐stimulation, graft survival, and disease progression.

Islet Transplant Strategies to Decrease PTMs in Beta‐Cells

Islet transplantation is an experimental approach to properly restore euglycemia in patients with T1D. In 1972, Dr. Paul Lacy established that islet transplants could cure diabetic mice, establishing the foundation for islet transplant biology [87]. Within a few years, the first human pancreatic islet transplants began. By 1980, Dr. Sutherland at the University of Minnesota had shown success with human islet transplant [88]. The Edmonton protocol, published in 2000, further revolutionized islet transplantation by turning islet transplantation into a clinical reality by establishing a protocol for multi‐donor islet transplantation and steroid‐free immunosuppression. Historically, islet transplantation has been limited by shortage of donor islets, toxicity of immunosuppressive drugs, and immune‐mediated graft rejection. Despite these challenges, the FDA granted approval to Donislecel (brand name is Lantidra), the first allograft islet transplant cell therapy, in 2023. Donislecel uses cadaver‐sourced islets for infusion into the recipient patient's portal vein [89]. The Donislecel clinical trial demonstrated 20/30 islet transplant recipients were insulin‐free 1 year after their last infusion [90].

While much effort has been made to improve the quality of human islets from donors, the supply of human donor islets remains a challenge. Xenografts and stem cell‐derived islets or beta‐cells are a potential source to fill the gap. Currently, there is interest in developing islet transplants from porcine sources. Pigs are considered ideal due to recent advances in genetic engineering and the close resemblance of pig and human insulin [91]. Genetically engineered pigs have been generated to lack the expression of galactose‐alpha‐1,3‐galactose (αGal), N‐glycolylneuraminic acid (Neu5Gc), and SDa blood group antigen (SDa) xenogenic carbohydrates to help prevent xenograft rejection. In addition to the development of hypoimmune pigs, other strategies to prevent islet xenograft rejection include the use of encapsulation. The first study of encapsulated porcine islet xenotransplantation is currently ongoing at the University of Illinois.

In addition to pig islets, the feasibility of stem cell‐derived islet transplants is currently in clinical trials. Early groundwork in pancreatic developmental biology established that mature beta‐cells can be differentiated with signaling molecules from pluripotent cells [92]. Today, the first stem cell‐derived T1D treatment, Zimislecel, is currently in Phase III trials [93]. Zimislecel, manufactured by Vertex Pharmaceuticals, is sourced from embryonic stem cells which are induced through several stages of development with the use of specialized media. Early Phase I and Phase II trials of Zimislecel demonstrated that 12/12 recipients were free of severe hypoglycemic events and 10/12 were free of exogenous insulin administration one year post‐transplantation [94]. Vertex is planning to submit Zimislecel for marketing applications to regulators in 2026. If approved, Zimislecel could be one of the first embryonic‐stem cell‐derived therapies approved by the FDA.

There are several striking benefits of using induced stem cells for transplantation. First, induced stem cells could potentially be directly taken from the recipient‐to‐be; and second, cellular modifications can increase graft viability or prevent immune rejection. The modifications made to induced stem cells for islet transplant are as creative as modern gene editing allows and have opened the door to engineered hypoimmune cells. Overexpressing PD‐L1 in induced stem cell‐derived islets has been shown to protect the grafts from autoimmune rejection in mice [95]. Depletion of HLA Class I and Class II and overexpression of CD47 in induced stem cells have been viable, functional, and curative of diabetes in non‐human primates [96]. The technology is advancing rapidly, and it is anticipated that induced stem cell islets will probably have double‐digit numbers of gene edits to ensure graft survival.

Encapsulation of Islets to Protect Against Oxidative Stress Following Transplantation

While islet transplantation has resulted in significant lifestyle improvements in patients with Type 1 diabetes that are unable to properly regulate blood glucose and euglycemia, there are still major hurdles that need to be met before this procedure can be applied. In addition to the lack of sufficient numbers of functional donor islets to be used for transplantation, strategies to delay islet allo‐ and autoimmune rejection are also needed. While the use of systemic immunosuppressants can delay islet graft rejection, these drugs can also negatively impact islet graft function following transplantation. Studies to increase our understanding of how the innate and adaptive immune system contributes to islet graft rejection are needed, as this knowledge may yield additional strategies to elicit immune suppression of transplanted islets.

Innate immune activation is one of the first steps involved in islet graft rejection including complement, neutrophil, and macrophage activation. Strategies to decrease proinflammatory innate immune responses involved in islet graft destruction have proven to be effective. The generation of oxidative stress and ROS synthesis has a two‐pronged effect on islet graft rejection. In addition to direct effects of ROS on mediating islet cell damage, loss of function, and viability [97, 98, 99, 100]; ROS can also function as key signaling molecules to stimulate redox‐sensitive signaling pathways necessary for immune cell activation [101]. The production of ROS is mediated within minutes following immune and non‐immune cell stimulation and occurs much earlier than early proinflammatory cytokine synthesis [102, 103, 104]. Many signaling pathways including NF‐kB, JAK/STAT, and T cell receptor signaling pathways have redox switches within proteins that consist of sulfur‐ or aromatic ring‐containing amino acids (cysteine, methionine, tryptophan, tyrosine, histidine) moieties that can be oxidized to affect protein function including phosphorylation, phosphatase, nuclear translocation, and DNA binding [105, 106, 107, 108]. These observations have provided the rationale for us to target oxidative stress and ROS synthesis as a potential novel strategy to inhibit immune‐mediated inflammatory responses in both Type 1 diabetes and islet transplantation rejection.

There are at least three signals required for efficient T cell activation including T cell receptor and major histocompatibility (MHC) interaction with a bound peptide (signal 1), co‐stimulatory molecule interactions (signal 2) with antigen‐presenting cells, and the synthesis of ROS and proinflammatory cytokines (signal 3). Our interest in targeting ROS synthesis and impacts on numerous signaling pathways, and subsequent proinflammatory cytokine generation, was shown to be necessary for efficient T cell maturation and secretion of effector cytokines, including IFN‐γ, IL‐2, and TNF [109]. By using mouse models that lack the ability to generate NADPH oxidase 2 (NOX2)‐derived superoxide, antigen‐specific T cells do not proliferate or produce significant levels of effector cytokines compared to T cells stimulated in the presence of superoxde [70]. The effect of inhibiting ROS as a third signal for efficient T cell activation can impact various facets of immune cell regulation, including second messengers for numerous signaling pathways and potentially, at the level of inducing post‐translational modifications of putative autoantigens (discussed above), and induction of bystander activation of autoreactive T cells. Oxidative post‐translational modifications can exacerbate autoreactive T cell responses by inducing epitope spreading and enhancing MHC/HLA binding to enhance TCR stimulation [25, 30, 86, 110].

Islet encapsulation is viewed as a potential method to protect transplanted islets from immune‐mediated rejection. Encapsulation can be performed with either macroencapsulation or microencapsulation strategies. Microencapsulation technology is favored due to a higher surface area to volume ratio and ease of nutrient transport. Some encapsulation materials are currently being developed to stimulate neovascularization around the islet graft and are embedded with pharmaceutical drugs for localized release [111, 112]. Coating material can also decrease instant blood‐mediated inflammatory reactions [113]. Alternatively, encapsulation materials can aid with reducing islet transplant oxidative stress [114].

In the Tse lab, we have created an encapsulation material that protects islets from oxidative stress and the damaging effects of ROS [115]. Using a layer‐by‐layer approach, we created a nanothin coating that combines tannic acid with poly(N‐vinylpyrrolidone) (PVPON). Tannic acid, naturally found in oak tree galls, is an antioxidant that easily creates thin films, nanoparticles, and hydrogels [116]. PVPON is a commonly used water‐soluble polymer [117]. We have found that the use of PVPON/TA does not affect islet function and is able to delay islet graft rejection. PVPON/TA has even been successful in decreasing graft rejection without the use of immunosuppressive drugs [114]. Furthermore, PVPON/TA has been shown to protect islets when transplanting donor porcine islets into murine recipients [118]. Given the unique chemical nature of PVPON, we developed encapsulation materials containing additional immune inhibitors including CTLA‐4‐Ig and in the near future, hope to also include PD‐L1 in our islet coatings to further delay islet graft destruction [119]. We have found that the addition of CTLA‐4‐Ig to the PVPON‐tannic acid encapsulation material increased the duration of graft survival without immunosuppression [119].

Some researchers are inventing devices to store and protect the islet from recipient immune response. Encillin's Encapsulated Cell Replacement Therapy (ENCRT) device is currently in Phase I clinical trials and is a form of microencapsulation that acts like a “tea bag around the islet”. Interim reporting has been positive in pointing out that ENCRT can sustain viable islets, promote vascularization around the device, and minimize fibrosis [120, 121].

Encapsulation of islets has been proposed to limit bystander activation. Encapsulation may block direct activation of immune cells. However, indirect activation is difficult to prevent with encapsulation [122]. Insulin, a 51 amino acid molecule, must be able to diffuse through encapsulation material for the islet transplant to properly function. Given that TCR epitopes are 8 to 25 amino acids in length, it is physically challenging to sequester antigens from the transplant within the encapsulation capsule. Additionally, the encapsulation barrier must allow for passage of oxygen, glucose, and other important nutrients. While this poses an engineering hurdle for islet transplants, certain organs such as the brain, eye, testes, and gravid uterus are naturally immune privileged. This is a subject area that has received little investigation and could perhaps benefit from more research, albeit with certain challenges.

Despite the challenges to create new islet transplant technology, we are currently living in exciting times for islet transplantation. The FDA has approved the first cadaver‐derived islet transplant. Stem cell‐derived islets are currently in Phase III trials. Encapsulation of islets holds vast potential to improve graft viability. Xenotransplantation technology is currently in clinical trials. With rapid advances in immunotherapies and biologic drugs, there are more immunosuppressants that are safer and more effective to use than ever before. It is safe to say we should be expecting breakthroughs to cure T1D in the near future.

Conclusions

In summary, the impact of oxidative stress and redox‐dependent inflammation on the emergence of post‐translationally modified autoantigens and exacerbation of autoimmunity is an important area of research investigation. Further increasing our understanding of how oxidative stress can influence autoimmune diabetes may increase our understanding of basic science investigation and pave the development of novel translational therapies for clinicians devoted to reducing the burden of disease on a growing population of patients with T1D. We are hopeful and heartened by the fact that strategies to detect, mitigate the impact of, and therapeutically target these protein modifications as a means to improve transplantation survival and therapeutic efficacy are steadily improving as technology evolves.
