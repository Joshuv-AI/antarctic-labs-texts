Providing diabetes prevention services and navigating care transitions: perspectives from primary care providers
Authors: Mary H. Smart, Yuka Asada, Deci Limpoco, Janet Y. Lin, Brian T. Layden, Yuval Eisenberg, A. Simon Pickard, Lisa K. Sharp, Alana Biggers, Angela Kong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis qualitative study examined how primary care providers (PCPs) prioritize addressing diabetes prevention with patients newly diagnosed with prediabetes and identified barriers and facilitators to providing diabetes prevention services (DPS). To examine transitions of care, we also evaluated how PCPs received and acted on screening information obtained outside of a primary care visit among their patients with prediabetes.MethodsWe interviewed PCPs practicing in Chicago using semi-structured interviews guided by the updated Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR 2.0) from March to May 2023. Framework analysis in combination with hybrid coding was used to analyze transcripts.ResultsNineteen PCPs were interviewed, and most (58%) prioritized talking to patients with prediabetes about diabetes prevention. Major themes identified included: (1) social determinants of health (SDOH) undermining diabetes prevention care, (2) perceptions of barriers and facilitators differing by provider background, and (3) delay of DPS due to prioritizing the patient’s other needs. Providers noted challenges in sharing information across health systems despite existing interoperability platforms.ConclusionsOur study observed challenges in providing DPS, particularly among PCPs serving patients impacted by SDOH. We also identified opportunities for improving transitions of care and engagement between patients with prediabetes and PCPs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12875-025-03040-4.

Background

There are over 130 million United States (U.S.) adults with type 2 diabetes and prediabetes[1]. Prediabetes, an intermediate high-risk condition of type 2 diabetes, is often asymptomatic and associated with poor health outcomes, impaired quality of life, and high economic burden[2, 3]. Early detection of prediabetes, followed by intense lifestyle changes and/or pharmacologic treatment, can delay or prevent type 2 diabetes and its associated complications[4].

The United States Preventive Service Task Force recognizes the importance of primary care providers (PCPs) in addressing diabetes prevention[5]. PCPs can facilitate early detection of prediabetes, provide lifestyle counseling, and refer patients to evidence-based diabetes prevention interventions[6]. However, gaps exist between guideline recommendations in providing these diabetes prevention services (DPS) and actual practice in primary care[7]. Challenges exist in providing and referring patients to DPS, which include PCPs’ lack of awareness, inadequate time, and perceived patient-related barriers such as economic constraints and other social determinants of health (SDOH)[8–11]. Provider-reported barriers that impact their patients’ participation in DPS include patients’ limited access to fresh or healthy foods, transportation issues, and lack of time[11, 12]. These challenges impede diabetes prevention efforts, particularly among patient populations disproportionately affected by diabetes[1, 13, 14] who may also be less likely to engage in preventive care that could delay diabetes[15–17]. These disparities in the continuum of prediabetes care contribute to diabetes-related health inequalities in the U.S[18, 19].

A novel emergency department(ED)-based diabetes screening program was implemented to expand diabetes screening opportunities to populations less likely to engage with primary care, which has been described elsewhere[20]. A goal of this ED-based screening program is to connect newly diagnosed patients with prediabetes during their ED visit to a PCP in routine care. Identifying patients with screen-detected prediabetes is only the first step in diabetes prevention, and effectively moving these patients from acute to primary care for diagnosis confirmation and disease management is needed[21, 22]. PCPs in the routine care setting are central to managing chronic health conditions, such as prediabetes[23]. The role of PCPs includes but is not limited to, receiving and acting on pertinent patient information and having an awareness of available evidence-based recommendations to offer[24]. However, currently how PCPs perceive these transitions of care (i.e., screening for prediabetes in ED to following up with primary care for diabetes prevention services) is unknown. Failure to understand PCPs’ perspectives, who are an important stakeholder in this care continuum, may result in poor follow-up of newly diagnosed patients with prediabetes, resulting in delays in care and worsening outcomes[25, 26]. Therefore, we conducted a multi-method study with PCPs to examine (a) current DPS provided by PCPs, (b) implementation barriers and facilitators in providing DPS, and (c) PCPs’ preferences for being informed of patients’ hemoglobin a1c (Hba1c) test result that was conducted in the ED.

Methods

Study design

In this multi-method study, we conducted semi-structured interviews with PCPs practicing in the Chicago metropolitan area from March to May 2023[27]. The interview guide was informed by the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR)[28]. The Institutional Review Board at the [identifying information removed] reviewed and approved the study (2021 − 1048). Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

Participants

A purposive sample of PCPs from [identifying information removed] and other health systems in the Chicago metropolitan area was identified through physician referrals and a [identifying information removed] internal medicine department staff meeting. Following the meeting, an email blast was sent to the entire internal medicine department, and interested PCPs contacted a research team member (MS) via email. Eligible providers were board-certified clinicians practicing in primary care (e.g., internal medicine, family medicine). Those scheduled for interviews received an email link to review an informed consent document; Zoom interviews were scheduled for approximately 30 min after informed consent was obtained electronically. Permission to audio-record (i.e. no video) the interview over Zoom was obtained prior to the start of the interview. Providers were also sent a brief demographic survey to complete prior to the interview.

Instrument development and data collection

The updated CFIR framework informed the development of the semi-structured interview and coding guides[28]. The CFIR framework provided a comprehensive overview of multilevel factors that may influence linkage to care and DPS delivery[29]. The interview guide (Appendix) was divided into two sections: (1) questions related to DPS provided by the PCP, and (2) questions related to the provider’s preferences in being alerted regarding patients’ elevated Hba1c result possibly indicative of prediabetes. The guide included both qualitative and quantitative questions. The initial interview guide was piloted with two internal medicine providers and revised for clarity and flow. All interviews were conducted via Zoom by a trained staff member (MS) studying pharmacy systems who was supervised by a PhD level qualitative research expert (YA). Responses to the interview questions, including supporting notes, were captured in REDCap. All interviews were audio recorded and professionally transcribed. We recruited PCPs until data saturation was achieved.

Data coding and analysis

We utilized framework analysis in combination with hybrid coding to analyze the data[30, 31]. An initial codebook was drafted based on the CFIR-informed interview guide. All transcripts were uploaded and analyzed using MAXQDA 2022. Three researchers (MS, AK, DL), under the oversight of the qualitative expert (YA), independently coded a set of transcripts. The coders met frequently to discuss coding discrepancies to ensure > 85% intercoder agreement[32]. At that time, the three coders independently coded the remaining transcripts. The analysis team applied summary grids and other MAXQDA visual tools to assist with theme generation. The analysis team also met to review and revise the language presentation of themes.

Results

Demographic characteristics

Table 1 describes the demographic and practice characteristics of the PCPs. Nineteen providers were interviewed; most were Doctors of Medicine (95%) and practicing in internal medicine (53%) with approximately 10 years of experience at their current place of employment. Respondents reported an average mean (SD) age of 44 (11) years, mostly female (68%), and self-identified as non-Hispanic white (58%), non-Hispanic Asian (37%), or Hispanic White (5%). There were 5 (26%) providers practicing at federally qualified health care (FQHC) facilities that were family medicine practice sites. All but one provider practiced at [identifying information removed]. There is a Diabetes Prevention Program (DPP) located at one of the [identifying information removed] FQHC clinics, which is offered at no cost or reduced cost to eligible patients.

Current DPS provided by PCPs

Most providers (58%) indicated they were very likely to prioritize intervening in a patient with prediabetes (Table 1). When asked, “What services are you (or your colleagues) currently offering to patients with prediabetes at your practice?”, providers most frequently mentioned: (1) counseling patients on lifestyle modifications (i.e., exercise, diet, weight loss); (2) routine health checkups including prediabetes-related follow-up care; (3) referrals to other services (i.e., nutrition, dietitian, exercise-related); and (4) prescription medications (i.e., Metformin, GLP-1). While the majority of providers indicated they were familiar with the DPP (58%) (Table 1), approximately only a third (n = 7) of the sample had ever made a referral to a DPP program despite there being a DPP located at [identifying information removed], which was where the majority of the PCPs practiced.

SDOH undermines diabetes prevention care for patients

In the CFIR framework, the outer setting refers to the community or system in which the providers’ practice exists[28], which includes local conditions like SDOH that impact the implementation and delivery of DPS. SDOH are the conditions in which people are born, grow, live, learn, work, and socialize that affect their health and quality of life[33].

When asked to describe the main challenges to providing DPS to their patients, most providers (79%) mentioned examples of SDOH hindering their patients from engaging, accessing, and utilizing DPS. Social determinants were often noted as the underlying and undermining factor of poor health and prediabetes.

Providers mentioned the challenges of recommending lifestyle modifications like diet and exercise to their patients, especially if patients have limited resources and live in neighborhoods where practicing such recommendations is difficult.

Another frequently described barrier is poor insurance coverage of certain DPS, including diet and nutrition consults, the DPP, and medications. Notably, providers mentioned a recent uptick in prescribing GLP-1 agonists but frequently encountered barriers in prescribing since many insurances are not willing to cover these prescriptions.

Other perceived barriers related to SDOHs included poor English literacy, transportation issues, lack of time, job insecurity, and mistrust of the medical community.

Differences in perceived relevant barriers and facilitators by providers’ practice background

Providers who practiced internal medicine more frequently mentioned patients’ individual motivations as an important factor in the success of DPS uptake by patients (or not). Additionally, internal medicine PCPs were more likely to describe deficits in their own knowledge of lifestyle counseling as a barrier to delivering DPS to their patients.

On the other hand, family medicine practitioners were more likely to mention food insecurity and lack of access to fresh produce as a barrier. Family medicine providers were also more likely to describe community partnerships to provide fresh produce and food as an important facilitator.

DPS is often deprioritized or delayed

Most providers (63%) mentioned not having enough time to provide DPS to their patients with prediabetes. Reasons included the provider prioritizing other more urgent health or non-health issues. Some providers, particularly those from FQHCs, described addressing food access and safe housing first because they are more pressing for the patient than a prediabetes diagnosis.

There are also health system-related factors that contributed to the delay of DPS delivery (Table 2). Providers indicated a lack of knowledge regarding what resources they could provide to patients (e.g., patient handouts) or services to refer patients to (e.g., nutrition consult or DPP). Providers also reported a lack of readily available resources. For example, some providers indicated that they were aware of the DPP affiliated with their practice site, but the short and sporadic enrollment periods and long commitments made it difficult to refer their patients to.

Other challenges described by providers include discrepancies between provider and patient health goals. For example, providers described how some patients liked their current body image and were unreceptive to weight loss recommendations.

Providers varied in their preferences for being notified regarding Hba1c results

Providers were given a scenario where a patient was admitted to the [identifying information removed] ED and had an elevated Hba1c result indicative of possibly undiagnosed prediabetes. The patient has a follow-up appointment scheduled with them. The provider was then asked the following: “Assuming that the patient’s health is stable with respect to their ED admission, how would you prefer to be informed of the Hba1c lab result? You can choose more than one.” Providers had three options: (1) being told directly by the patient, (2) seeing the Hba1c result in the electronic medical record (EMR), or (3) receiving communication from the ED staff. A follow-up probe asked for the reason behind their selection, with sample quotes provided in Table 4.

Most providers selected more than one option. Providers cited patient awareness and possible readiness to engage with the provider regarding the new prediabetes diagnosis as reasons for selecting direct patient notification. However, providers noted concerns about consistency and accuracy issues in relying on patients to convey important lab results.

Reasons for selecting the EMR included efficiency and preference for passive messaging, while reasons against it were being easy to miss the lab result. Providers who preferred the EMR noted reviewing labs from a recent ED visit is a common practice among PCPs.

Finally, reasons for selecting the option of receiving communication from ED staff included an affinity for direct provider-to-provider handoff. A provider described that they welcomed warm and personalized handoffs from providers within their own health system (Table 4). However, a con to direct communication from ED would be notification fatigue and more work for providers.

There are challenges with sharing information across health systems

Providers were asked whether their EMR system allowed for hospital interoperability. If yes, providers were then asked how they would prefer to be made aware of a patient’s Hba1c lab result indicative of prediabetes done at another health system’s ED that allows for interoperable exchange with their own EMR. All providers from [identifying information removed] (n = 18) were familiar with the health system’s EMR interoperability platform (i.e., Care Everywhere) that facilitates patient health information sharing with other health systems using Epic. Of those, 11 (61%) described challenges with using Care Everywhere, which included the following: (1) Disorganized summaries generated from outside institutions, (2) Incomplete patient information sharing, and (3) Poor patient information integration. As a result, providers mentioned difficulty in identifying specific information, such as Hba1c labs, which can be easily overlooked. Providers described that this could lead to a delay in providing patient care, especially if the patient is not already established.

Discussion

The goal of this study was to understand factors at the provider level that could impact connecting patients from the ED with screen-detected prediabetes to DPS within primary care in this metro-Chicago area. Our study highlighted multilevel barriers to facilitating DPS for patients with prediabetes by PCPs, including patient-related SDOH, providers’ lack of time, and the lack of readily available evidence-based resources within the health systems. To our knowledge, our work is the first to report on providers’ preferences for how to be informed regarding abnormal Hba1c results from the ED as a first step in facilitating transitions of care. While there was no clear consensus on a preferred method to being informed, providers did note challenges with sharing information across health systems.

The most commonly reported services offered by PCPs to patients with prediabetes are lifestyle counseling and prescription medications, which is consistent with existing literature[34–36]. A key facilitator identified by PCPs in providing DPS is having an in-house staff available to assist with lifestyle counseling (Table 2), though providers did not mention whether these individuals were certified diabetes educators[37]. Our providers reported challenges in providing DPS. Multilevel barriers reported were similar to those previously reported, which include patient-related barriers such as lack of insurance and SDOH; provider-related barriers such as lack of awareness of available DPS to refer patients to; and health system barriers such as lack of clinical-staff support[11, 36, 38]. The DPP is a DPS that is evidence-based[39, 40]; however, DPP referral rates among PCPs are generally low (0 to 4%)[41–43]. The PCPs in our study, particularly the providers with the FQHC, were more likely to refer patients to the DPP (37%) because they had a DPP program in their practice and were familiar with it. Nevertheless, there was a discrepancy between the PCPs’ awareness of DPP and program referral rates. Multiple literature-reported barriers to PCP’s referrals to the DPP include the provider’s lack of program knowledge[11, 44] and being unaware of a uniform referral process[45, 46]. Providers’ attitudes and perceptions about prediabetes have also been shown to impact their referral to DPP[47]. Providers have also reported perceived patient barriers related to SDOH, such as cost, reimbursement, time, and DPP location, that result in low PCP referral rates[7, 11, 45, 48]. Along with others, our results suggest a need for accessible, easily implementable, evidence-based diabetes prevention interventions that can overcome provider referral barriers.

A key theme we identified was differences in the commonly described barriers and facilitators to DPS between providers who practiced internal medicine and family medicine. These differences may stem from differing beliefs regarding the importance of external setting factors, such as SDOH, versus individual patient characteristics, like patient motivation[49]. It is unclear whether these differences are mainly driven by personal beliefs, medical training, or post-training experiences. Nevertheless, they highlight the need to educate PCPs about the far-reaching impacts of SDOH and the importance of addressing them beyond their medical practice[50]. The literature has shown that patients experiencing negative effects related to SDOH may struggle with individual-level interventions[51, 52]. Providers’ awareness of how SDOH impacts patients is an important step toward reducing diabetes-related health disparities[53, 54]. Since the goal of the ED-based screening program targets those less likely to be screened in primary care and who are also disproportionately burdened with the disease, PCP awareness of SDOH may help facilitate appropriate follow-up care among patients identified with prediabetes in the acute care setting[18, 55, 56].

An important aspect of connecting patients from the ED to the primary care setting is effectively communicating pertinent information (i.e., Hba1c results)[24]. Study providers indicated that there was a greater ease in sharing information with PCPs in the same health system as the ED, which was facilitated mainly by the system’s EMR. However, it is not uncommon for patients to use multiple health systems, particularly during a medical emergency[57]. Sharing patient health information across multiple health organizations enables providers to make relevant, up-to-date patient care decisions[58]. However, the study’s PCPs noted that the quality of information shared across health systems was poor even while using an interoperability platform. Consistent with the reported literature, most providers noted difficulties in interfacing with these platforms[59]. These issues influence the ease with which the [identifying information removed] ED-based diabetes screening program can facilitate linkage to primary care in other health systems and will also impact its future dissemination and implementation efforts.

There are several limitations to our study. First, the study was conducted using a purposive sample, and most PCPs were from a single large urban academic medical center. Additionally, although we aimed to recruit across all types of PCPs, most providers interviewed were MDs. This may affect the generalizability of our results to small or rural primary care sites and non-physician PCPs. Secondly, there may be self-selection bias, with providers who choose to participate may be keener toward providing prediabetes care to their patients. There may also be recall bias regarding provider practices and perceived barriers and facilitators reported. Additionally, our interview guide focused on Finally, our study focused on provider perspectives only and may not necessarily reflect those of the patients being served. Current research is underway to identify the views of ED patients identified with undiagnosed prediabetes in the ED on outpatient DPS.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that PCPs report prioritizing the management of prediabetes but note significant socio-environmental and health system barriers that hinder their efforts to provide DPS. Furthermore, improvements in health system infrastructure are needed to facilitate providers’ management of unestablished patients with prediabetes. These formative findings will serve to inform the future development of evidence-based interventions to prevent diabetes and strategies to facilitate the successful connections of patients with prediabetes to primary care.
