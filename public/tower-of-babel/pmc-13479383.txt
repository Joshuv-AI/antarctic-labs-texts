IMD-0354 optimization generates potent colistin adjuvants with in vivo activity and reduced eukaryotic toxicity
Authors: Luke J Cowart, Ansley M Nemeth, Leigh A Jania, Moira Overly, Courtney F Ellis, Beverly H Koller, Roberta J Melander, Yohei Doi, Robert K Ernst, Christian Melander
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe development of novel therapeutic approaches to combat infections that stem from extensively drug-resistant (XDR) gram-negative bacteria remains an area of significant unmet need. One such approach is the use of antibiotic adjuvants. Currently, colistin (polymyxin E) is used as the antibiotic of last resort for the treatment of XDR gram-negative bacterial infections. However, resistance to this antibiotic is on the rise. We previously reported that IMD-0354 (an IκB kinase-β inhibitor) and related salicylanilide adjuvants overcome colistin resistance in several gram-negative pathogens. However, this scaffold exhibits unfavorable eukaryotic toxicity, thought to arise from the salicyl moiety. Herein, we investigate the structure-activity relationship (SAR) of second-generation m-hydroxybenzanilide analogs of IMD-0354, to uncover compounds with reduced eukaryotic toxicity and IκB kinase-β inhibitory properties, while maintaining colistin adjuvant activity. We have identified new leads that lower the colistin minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) upwards of 2048-fold against highly colistin-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii and Klebsiella pneumoniae. In particular, NDM-622 exhibits reduced HepG2 toxicity compared to IMD-0354, with an IC50 of 125 ± 8.0 μM (59.4 ± 3.8 μg/mL) and a therapeutic index of ≥50. In a murine peritonitis model using a highly a colistin-resistant K. pneumoniae strain, NDM-622 and colistin together effect a decrease in colony forming units (CFUs) compared to treatment with colistin alone or vehicle controls. Preliminary mechanism-of-action (MoA) studies suggest that m-hydroxybenzanilides, including NDM-622, likely act via a mechanism distinct from IMD-0354.

Introduction

Infections caused by extensively drug-resistant (XDR) bacteria are an escalating health crisis, driven by the overuse and misuse of antibiotics, along with divestment in antibiotic development by pharmaceutical companies [1,2]. The ESKAPE pathogens, Enterococcus faecium, Staphylococcus aureus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Acinetobacter baumannii, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and the Enterobacter species, are known for high levels of antibiotic resistance, enabling them to “escape” effects of antibiotics [1]. Treatment options for the gram-negative ESKAPE pathogens are particularly limited, due to the gram-negative outer membrane (OM) restricting antibiotic access to cellular targets [3–5]. This has led to increased reliance on higher risk agents such as colistin (polymyxin E) [6]. Despite inherent nephro- and neurotoxicity, colistin remains a last-resort treatment for gram-negative XDR bacteria [7,8]. However, increasing incidence of colistin resistance is threatening the utility of this critical, last line antibiotic.

One approach to address the critical challenge of XDR bacterial infections and overcome colistin resistance is the use of small molecule antibiotic adjuvants [9]. Adjuvants typically lack standalone antibacterial activity, but augment effects of antibiotics by disrupting bacterial resistance mechanisms [9]. Previously, we reported that IMD-0354 (Fig. 1), originally developed as an inhibitor of IκB kinase-β (IKKβ), has potent colistin adjuvant activity [11,12]. IMD-0354 reduces the colistin minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) to or below the clinical breakpoint (2 μg/mL) at 5 μM (1.9 μg/mL) against highly colistin-resistant strains including, A. baumannii 4106 (AB4106) and K. pneumoniae B9 (KPB9), which exhibit baseline colistin MICs of 2048 μg/mL and 512 μg/mL, respectively [2,13]. A preliminary structure-activity relationship (SAR) study identified analogs of IMD-0354 with increased adjuvant activity and reduced standalone antibacterial activity [2]. Most analogs from the first generation retain the salicylanilide scaffold, and unfortunately, IMD-0354 and lead first generation analogs exhibit toxicity against HepG2 cells [14]. IMD-0354 has an IC50 of 22.1 ± 4.0 μM (8.5 ± 1.5 μg/mL) against HepG2 cells, whereas the first-generation lead compounds NDM-239 and NDM-240 (Fig. 1) have IC50s of 1.7 ± 0.2 μM (0.7 ± 0.1 μg/mL) and 1.6 ± 0.2 μM (0.5 ± 0.1 μg/mL), respectively.

Salicylanilides are proposed to act as proton shuttles that disrupt the cellular proton gradient, leading to cytotoxicity (Fig. 2) [15]. The pKa of the salicylic phenol group lies near physiological pH, with the protonated species stabilized by hydrogen bonding to the amide carbonyl, which facilitates membrane crossing [15]. Subsequent intracellular proton release will perturb the overall proton gradient. In contrast to salicylanilides, m-hydroxybenzanilides are not stabilized by intramolecular hydrogen bonding, and thus have a higher pKa, making them less effective proton shuttles [15,16]. We previously determined that the m-hydroxybenzanilide analog of IMD-0354, NDM-258 (Fig. 1) is a potent colistin adjuvant reducing the colistin MIC 512-fold and 1024-fold against AB4106 and KPB9, respectively. In contrast, the des-hydroxy analog NDM-257, is inactive [2,13]. Based on our hypothesis that colistin adjuvants lacking proton shuttling capability should exhibit reduced cytotoxicity, we performed analog synthesis on NDM-258 leading to the identification of analogs with enhanced colistin adjuvant activity and reduced HepG2 cytotoxicity.

Results and discussion

The benzanilides were formed via a one-step thionyl chloride-mediated condensation between a derivatized aniline and substituted m-hydroxybenzoic acid (Scheme 1). The bis-phenyl derivatives (NDM-765, NDM-766, NDM-767, NDM-768, NDM-769, NDM-770) were subsequently synthesized from the corresponding iodo-containing analogs (NDM-622 and NDM-618) via Suzuki coupling with aryl-boronic acid and Pd(dppf)Cl2 (Scheme 1) [17].

Initially, we explored the structural requirements of the aniline moiety of NDM-258, keeping the 3-chloro-5-hydroxybenzoic acid component unchanged while systematically modifying the substituents on the aniline ring (Fig. 3). All analogs were evaluated against colistin-resistant strains AB4106 and KPB9. In the absence of adjuvant, the MICs of colistin against AB4106 and KPB9 are 2048 μg/mL and 512 μg/mL respectively. For both species, the clinical breakpoint for colistin is 2 μg/mL [13]. At 10 μM (3.8 μg/mL), NDM-258 reduces the colistin MICs 2048-fold to 1 μg/mL and 0.25 μg/mL against AB4106 and KPB9, respectively (Table 1). To allow for direct comparison with NDM-258, all analogs were initially tested at 10 μM, and any compound with activity within a two-fold range of NDM-258 was considered equipotent (Table 1 and S1). All analogs exhibit standalone MICs of ≥200 μM against both strains, with the exception of NDM-735, which has an MIC of 100 μM (33.8 μg/mL) against AB4106, and NDM-728 and NDM-731, which have MICs of 100 μM (32.7 μg/mL and 33.7 μg/mL respectively) against KPB9 (Table S1).

Panel of colistin resistant isolates

We assessed the nine lead compounds and the parent, NDM-258, against an expanded panel of 28 additional colistin-resistant A. baumannii and K. pneumoniae strains, including isolates that harbor mcr-1 [18]. As previously stated, the clinical breakpoint for colistin is 2 μg/mL for both A. baumannii and K. pneumoniae [13]. All compounds were tested for their stand-alone toxicity and screened at 5 μM against each strain (Table S3–S6). All compounds have a standalone MIC of ≥100 μM, except for NDM-622, which has an MIC of 50 μM against AB1E6 and AB1F9 (Table S3 and S5). Data for the three most active compounds, NDM-619, NDM-620, and NDM-622 are summarized in Table 4. These analogs were chosen due to reductions in colistin MIC values that are equipotent to, or at least two-fold lower than those observed with NDM-258 across the panel. All three compounds lower the colistin MIC to the clinical breakpoint of 2 μg/mL or lower against each strain with minor exceptions. Against AB1A3 and AB3A4, NDM-619 reduces the colistin MIC to 32 μg/mL and 16 μg/mL, respectively; NDM-620 lowers the colistin MIC to 16 μg/mL in AB1A3, AB3A4, and KPA5; and NDM-622 decreases the colistin MIC to 32 μg/mL, 16 μg/mL, and 8 μg/mL in AB1A3, AB3A4, and KPA5, respectively.

Time-kill assay

Time-kill curves were constructed for compounds NDM-620 and NDM-622 in the presence and absence of colistin (Figs. 5 and 6 and S1, S2). Compounds selected are representative of the most active overall. Against AB4106, respective adjuvant/colistin combinations of 2.5 μM/2 μg/mL, 5 μM/4 μg/mL, and 10 μM/8 μg/mL were evaluated. Against KPB9, the following combinations were evaluated for NDM-620: 2 μM (0.8 μg/mL)/0.5 μg/mL, 4 μM (1.7 μg/mL)/1 μg/mL, and 8 μM (3.3 μg/mL)/2 μg/mL. For NDM-622, the combinations evaluated were 2 μM (1.0 μg/mL)/1 μg/mL, 4 μM (1.9 μg/mL)/2 μg/mL, and 8 μM (3.8 μg/mL)/4 μg/mL. Synergy was defined as ≥2-log10 decrease in colony forming units (CFUs) at each time point for the combination compared with the most active single agent [19]. Indifference was defined as a <2-log10 increase or decrease in CFUs at each time point for the combination relative to the most active drug alone [19]. Against both strains, the highest concentration (10 μM for AB4106 and 8 μM for KPB9), neither adjuvant exhibits stand-alone antibacterial activity (Figs. 5 and 6). Additionally, 64 μg/mL colistin has no effect on growth of either strain (Fig. S1 and S2).

In AB4106 (Fig. 5), the lowest adjuvant/colistin combinations tested (2.5 μM/2 μg/mL) for both adjuvants were considered indifferent relative to the control. At 5 μM/4 μg/mL, both compounds show significant reductions in CFUs; however, only the 2- and 4-h time points meet the criteria for synergy. At 10 μM/8 μg/mL, both NDM-620 and NDM-622 demonstrated synergistic activity at all time points assessed. In KPB9 (Fig. 6), all combinations produce significant reductions in CFUs and are considered synergistic at each time point, except for 2 μM (0.8 μg/mL)/0.5 μg/mL and 4 μM (1.7 μg/mL)/1 μg/mL adjuvant/colistin combinations of NDM-620, and 2 μM (1.0 μg/mL)/1 μg/mL and 4 μM (1.9 μg/mL)/2 μg/mL combinations of NDM-622 at 24 h. These findings indicate that the highest concentrations tested, 8 μM (1.7 μg/mL)/2 μg/mL for NDM-620 and 8 μM (3.3 μg/mL)/4 μg/mL for NDM-622, are synergistic at all time points and exhibit activity comparable to colistin alone at 512 μg/mL. Notably, at 24 h, the highest combinations resulted in lower overall CFU counts than colistin alone at 512 μg/mL.

The discrepancy in activity of NDM-620 and NDM-622 between MIC assays (Table 3) and the time-kill kinetics (Figs. 5 and 6), specifically at the lowest concentration required to potentiate colistin to the clinical breakpoint, is likely due to established differences in assay design and methodology. MIC determination is assessed 16 h using optical density at 600 nm (OD600), while time-kill assays are conducted over a 24-h period with intermittent sampling, vigorous mixing, and enumerations of CFU counts. These distinctions in experimental design and measurement techniques likely contribute to the variation in results.

Mechanism of action

Lipid A modification with phosphoethanolamine (pEtN) or aminoarabinose (Ara4N) has been established as a predominant mechanism of polymyxin resistance in many gram-negative bacteria. We explored whether NDM-618 and NDM-618, an active analogue from the first library, reverse or inhibit this modification by analyzing the lipid A portion of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) from KPB9 and lipooligosaccharide (LOS) from AB4106 grown in the absence or presence of 10 μM (3.8 and 4.8 μg/mL respectively) adjuvant for 8 h. Bacterial cells were isolated, lipid A was extracted and subsequently analyzed using Matrix-Assisted Laser Desorption/Ionization Time-of-Flight (MALDI-TOF) spectrometry. Analysis indicates no difference in the lipid A profile between treated and untreated samples (Fig. S4–S5). As the current MALDI-TOF method is not quantitative, one of two conclusions can be drawn [20]; first, the mechanism of action (MoA) is independent of lipid A modification, or second, achieving colistin susceptibility in colistin-resistant bacteria does not require full reversal of lipid A modifications, and slight reductions in lipid A modification underpinned by small molecule treatment, which would not be observable with the current MALDI-TOF protocol, are enough to reverse colistin resistance [20]. In contrast, previous studies of IMD-0354 reported reversal of pEtN and Ara4N modifications in AB4106 and KPB9 [2].

Polymyxin antibiotics are reported to induce cell death through the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [21,22], so we investigated whether lead compounds promote ROS production using 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescin diacetate (DCFH-DA), as previously described [23] (Fig. 7). In both AB4106 and KPB9, ROS levels were statistically indistinguishable from those in untreated cells following treatment with the active compounds (5 μM) alone or in combination with colistin (2 μg/mL) (Fig. 7). Overall, the results indicate that ROS generation likely does not contribute to the activity of the adjuvant–colistin combination.

It has been suggested that colistin may interact with outer membrane proteins (Omps), such as the extracellular domain of OmpW, which is involved in iron assimilation and is part of the iron regulon [24]. If compounds chelate iron and thereby reduce the availability of extracellular iron, the decreased competition for OmpW binding sites could enhance the interaction of colistin with OmpW and promote a stronger association of colistin with the OM [24]. To investigate whether such a mechanism might underlie the activity of the tested compounds, binding of NDM-258 and NDM-620 to iron was examined using isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC). No significant binding was observed between either compound and iron (II) sulfate heptahydrate or iron (III) chloride, suggesting that iron chelation is likely not the mechanism of action for these compounds (Fig. S6–S9).

Next, we investigated the effects of active compounds NDM-258, NDM-619, NDM-620, NDM-622, NDM-786, and inactive compound, NDM-721, upon membrane permeability utilizing the Baclight™ assay (Table 5). All compounds were tested at 5 μM in AB4106 and KPB9. In AB4106, no increase in membrane permeability is observed for any compound tested. In KPB9, inactive compound NDM-721 exhibits no effect on the membrane with an average increase in permeability of 0.47 ± 7.20%. Active compound NDM-768 exhibits the highest increase in membrane permeability at 20.98 ± 5.07%, while all other active compounds exhibit 8 – 14% increase in permeability (Table 5). These results indicate that the impact on the membrane is not the likely method by which these compounds reverse colistin resistance.

HepG2 toxicity

To evaluate if second-generation analogs display decreased eukaryotic toxicity, we assessed the in vitro cytotoxicity of lead compounds in HepG2 (human liver cancer) cells using a cell viability and proliferation XTT assay. This cell line is commonly used to screen for drug toxicity, as the liver is key in drug metabolism [27]. For each compound, the half-maximal inhibition activity (IC50) was determined (Table 8) and compared directly to the IC50 of IMD-0354. NDM-258, the direct analog of IMD-0354, exhibits significantly lower toxicity, with an IC50 of 135.2 ± 0.1 μM compared to 22.1 ± 4.0 μM (Table 8). Compound NDM-620, in which the chloro substituent of NDM-258 is replaced with a trifluoromethyl group, does exhibit higher HepG2 toxicity (IC50 = 88.3 ± 8.0 μM; 36.8 ± 3.3 μg/mL) than NDM-258; nonetheless, it remains ~3.5-fold less toxic than IMD-0354.

In adjuvant development, we define the therapeutic index (TI) as the ratio of the mammalian cell IC50 to the lowest adjuvant concentration that reduces the antibiotic MIC to breakpoint levels, with a TI of ≥50 desirable for further development [28]. TIs were calculated for all lead compounds based on their activity against AB4106 and KPB9 and compared to the parent compound IMD-0354, which has a TI range of 4.4–7.4 (Table 8). Relationship between IKKβ activity and adjuvant activity of second generation IMD-0354 derivatives

To further characterize the biochemical activity of the second-generation analogs, we assessed the inhibitory activity of NDM-258, NDM-620 and NDM-622 against IKKβ in vitro and compared this to IMD-0354, using a luciferase-based cellular reporter assay. As expected, IMD-0354 exhibits the strongest inhibitory activity against IKKβ compared to second-generation analogs, with an IC50 of 2.02 μM (Fig. 8A and B). NDM-258 showed weaker inhibition compared with IMD-0354, with an IC50 of 9.44 μM (Fig. S10 and 8B). This suggests that replacing the salicylamide group with m-hydroxybenzanilide reduces inhibitory activity. NDM-620 and NDM-622 exhibited approximately two-fold weaker inhibition than NDM-258, with similar IC50 values of 19.20 μM and 20.40 μM, respectively (Fig. 8). Additional compounds were evaluated in the same assay format and are summarized in Fig. 8B. NDM-258 and NDM-619 have IC50s of 9.44 μM and 9.07 μM, respectively, representing an overall smaller shift in IKKβ inhibitory activity than observed for NDM-620 and NDM-622. Based on relative inhibitory activity, compounds NDM-620 and NDM-622 were advanced to in vivo evaluation.

In vivo dose-response relationship of second-generation IMD-0354 derivatives and colistin in limiting K. pneumoniae growth

We evaluated whether the in vitro colistin adjuvant activities of NDM-620 or NDM-622 translated to altered colonization and growth of KPB9 in vivo. The dosing regimen consisted of 5 mg/kg colistin administered prior to the first dose of adjuvant, followed by two additional rounds of adjuvant dosing over the course of the experiment (Fig. 9C). Mice were dosed with the maximum solubility-permitted dose of NDM-622 (37.5 mg/kg) and NDM-622 (50 mg/kg). These compounds exhibit a solubility profile similar to IMD-0354, characterized by low aqueous solubility. This limited solubility may reduce the effective free concentration of the adjuvant in vivo. Similar to previous work, adjuvants are likely to cross the peritoneal–plasma barrier and be rapidly cleared from this compartment. This provided a platform for the split-dose regimen that was employed to prolong bacterial exposure to high concentrations of the adjuvant specifically within the peritoneal cavity [14]. Such a delivery strategy is well suited for early-stage studies of colistin–adjuvant interactions. Control mice received colistin or NDM-620 and were given vehicle in place of the adjuvant at all corresponding time points (Fig. 9 and S11). Mice used as a control group received colistin or NDM-620 but were treated with vehicle in place of adjuvant at all corresponding time points (Fig. 9 and S11).

Consistent with prior observations, KPB9 efficiently colonized the peritoneal cavity of colistin and vehicle-treated mice, with CFUs recovered from the peritoneal lavage fluid (PLF) increasing relative to the initial inoculum (Fig. 9B and C) [14]. We have previously shown little to no difference in KPB9 colonization in the peritoneum between untreated and colistin-treated mice [14]. Similarly, no difference in colonization between untreated mice and those treated with adjuvant NDM-620 (Fig. S11). Treatment with colistin in combination with either NDM-620 or NDM-622 resulted in a significant reduction in CFUs recovered from the peritoneal cavity compared to controls (Fig. 9B and C). These results demonstrate that both NDM-620 and NDM-622 function as effective in vivo colistin adjuvants in this peritonitis model.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we report a second-generation SAR study on IMD-0354 in which we investigate the potential of replacing the salicyl moiety with a m-hydroxybenzoyl moiety to reduce eukaryotic toxicity and retain colistin adjuvant activity. A number of structural trends governing colistin adjuvant activity for this scaffold mirror our previously reported SAR for IMD-0354, including tolerance for variation of the halogen substituent on the benzoic acid ring, interchangeability of the aniline ring trifluoromethyl groups with other halogens, and a general intolerance for electron-donating substituents. We have identified lead analogs with activity similar to parent compound NDM-258. Two of the leads (NDM-620, NDM-622) retain colistin adjuvant activity at 2.5 μM (0.8 and 1.2 μg/mL respectively). All lead compounds also exhibit reduced toxicity against HepG2 cells compared to IMD-0354, indicating that repositioning the hydroxyl group is a viable option to lower the eukaryotic toxicity of this scaffold. Both had roughly a five-fold decrease IKKβ activity compared to IMD-0354. In a murine peritonitis model using KPB9, co-administration of NDM-620 or NDM-622 with colistin resulted in a statistically significant reduction in CFUs compared with colistin alone. These second-generation analogs potentially act via a distinct MoA than the salicylanilides, as they do not observably modify the lipid A membrane under the same analysis conditions, do not increase ROS generation, nor increase membrane permeability. Ongoing work aims to identify the mechanism of action of this compound series, as well as to improve solubility and reduce IKKβ activity through further structural optimization.

General biological experimental

Bacterial strains, media, and antibiotics.

A. baumannii strains 4106, 4112, 4119, 3941, and 3942 were obtained from the Walter Reed Army Institute of Research (WRAIR). A. baumannii strain SM1536mcr−1 and K. pneumoniae strains B9, A5, C3, and F2210291mcr−1 were obtained from Professor Robert K Ernst at the University of Maryland, Baltimore. A. baumannii strains 1E4, 1H7, 1A7, 1A3, 1E6, 2A8, 3A4, 2B6, 1G2, and 1F9, K. pneumoniae strains F1, F3, F9, E6, E2, I6, I4, I2, and H2, and P. aeruginosa strains TRPA 161 and TRPA 162 were obtained from Professor Yohei Doi at University of Pittsburgh. Stocks of each bacterial strain were stored in 25% glycerol and maintained at −80 °C. Bacterial colonies were grown on Lennox LB [Fisher Scientific, BP1423–500] prior to use in biological assays. Strains harboring mcr-1 were plated on Lennox LB supplemented with 30 μg/mL gentamicin (Chem-Impex int’l, 00149) for plasmid retention. Cation-adjusted Mueller Hinton broth (CAMHB) was purchased from BD Diagnostics (212322–500) and used as the liquid medium for all assays unless otherwise stated. Colistin was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich [C4461–1G]. Unless otherwise stated, all assays were completed in biological duplicate at least two separate times. All compounds were dissolved in molecular biology grade DMSO (various commercial sources) as 100 mM stock solutions and stored at −20 °C until further use. Colistin was dissolved in sterile water.

Broth microdilution method for the determination of minimum inhibitory concentrations.

Guidelines of the Clinical Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) to determine the minimum inhibitory concentration of each compound or antibiotic, as previously described, were followed [13]. Bacterial strains were cultured for 4 to 6 h at 37 °C with shaking at 200 rpm in CAMHB and subcultured to approximately 5 × 105 CFU/mL. Bacterial strains harboring mcr-1 were cultured in CAMHB supplemented with 30 μg/mL gentamicin for plasmid retention. Aliquots (1 mL) were placed in culture tubes and dosed with antibiotic or compound from a stock solution, such that the concentration equaled the highest concentration tested. Samples were then aliquoted (200 μL) into the first row of a 96-well microtiter plate (Greiner Bio-One, 650161), with the remaining wells filled with 100 μL of initial bacterial subculture. Row one wells were mixed at a minimum of five times, and 100 μL was transferred to row two. Row two was then mixed at a minimum of five times, and 100 μL was transferred to row three. The serial dilution process was repeated until the second to last row had been mixed, leaving the final row as a blank of fresh CAMHB. Plates were then covered with GLAD Press n’ Seal and incubated under stationary conditions at 37 °C for 16–20 h. A BioTek Synergy HTX Multimode plate reader was used to determine the optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of cell growth in each well. The lowest dilution with <10% growth as compared to a positive control was recorded as the minimum inhibitory concentration.

Broth microdilution method for measurement of colistin potentiation.

Each treatment condition was serially diluted by mixing row one wells a minimum of five times and transferring 100 μL to the following row. Time–kill curves.

Bacterial strains were cultured overnight in CAMHB and subcultured to 1.04 × 106 CFU/mL for A. baumannii 4106 and 1.96 × 106 CFU/mL for K. pneumoniae B9 in fresh CAMHB. The subculture was then transferred to culture tubes in 3 mL aliquots, which were dosed with adjuvant, colistin, or adjuvant plus colistin. One aliquot was not dosed to serve as a control. All samples were then incubated without shaking at 37 °C. At 2, 4, 6, 8, and 24-h time points, 100 μL was taken from each sample and serially diluted 10-fold in fresh CAMHB up to seven times. From the diluted culture, 100 μL of diluted culture was plated on Lennox LB agar and incubated at 37 °C overnight. The total number of bacterial colonies on each plate was determined using a SphereFlash colony counter (NEUTEC Group Inc.). Curves were generated using GraphPad Prism.

Lipid A analysis.

Bacteria were initially cultured in 5 mL of CAMHB for 18 h at 37 °C and then subcultured to 1 × 106 CFU in 120 mL of fresh CAMHB. Subcultures were either left untreated or dosed with compound at the minimum effective concentration (10 μM) and incubated for 8 h. Experiments were performed in biological duplicates. Cells were pelleted by centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C, washed with 5 mL of endotoxin-free water, and pelleted again. Supernatants were discarded, and the resulting cell pellets were stored at −80 °C until shipment on dry ice.

Fast Lipid Analysis Technique (FLAT).

Lipid A structural analysis was performed, as previously described [29,30]. A single bacterial colony was scraped in technical duplicate directly onto a steel 96-well MALDI target plate (Pataigin, Baltimore, MD). FLAT extraction solution (1 μL; 0.2 M anhydrous citric acid, 0.1 M trisodium citrate dihydrate) was pipetted over bacterial spots. The FLAT target plate was incubated at 100 °C in a humidified heat block for 30 min. Bacterial spots were washed with Endotoxin-free water (Gibco, Grand Island NY, USA) and air-dried, followed by the addition of 1 μL of Norharmane matrix (10 mg/mL in 1:2 MeOH:CHCl3 – Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Mass spectra were collected in negative-ion mode using a Bruker MicroFlex® within the 1000–2400 m/z range. Agilent ESI Tune Mix was used to calibrate the Bruker MicroFlex®. MALDI-TOF MS data were processed and analyzed with flexAnalysis software (version 3.4).

Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) measurements.

ITC experiments were employed using a Malvern MicroCal PEAQ-ITC microcalorimeter (Malvern Instruments Ltd., UK) to determine binding and thermodynamic parameters, consisting of docking stoichiometry (N), docking affinity constant (K), and changes in entropy (ΔS) and enthalpy (ΔH). The small molecule and irons solution were prepared in HPLC-grade methanol. Then, loading of the small molecule (20 μM) and iron solution (300 μM) were done with the injection syringe and calorimeter cell, respectively. In the titration procedure, the overall number of drops for the syringe was 19, with the first drop being 0.4 μL and the following 18 drops being 2 μL each, and the titration was performed at 250 °C. The speed of stirring and spacing time were set as 750 rpm/min and 120 s, respectively. Besides, a blank experiment was applied via titrating iron solution into methanol to deduce the dilution heat of iron, and the data were fitted via the PEAQ-ITC analysis software (MicroCal, Malvern Instruments, Northampton, MA, USA) to determine K, ΔH, and ΔS.

ROS fluorescent dye assay for evaluating oxidative stress in bacterial cells.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) production was evaluated as previously described with minor changes [31]. To determine changes in general intracellular ROS upon treatment, 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescin diacetate fluorescent dye (DCFH-DA, Calbiochem) was used. Stocks of DCFH-DA (20 mM) prepared in DMSO were stored and protected from light at −80 °C. Bacterial strains were cultured and incubated overnight in 11 mL CAMHB at 37 °C with shaking at 200 rpm. Cultures were diluted 1:1 in fresh CAMHB for each condition tested. Subcultures with either left untreated or dosed with one of the following conditions: 100 μL 3% tert-butyl hydroperoxide (positive control), colistin, adjuvant, or a combination of colistin and adjuvant. Cultures were then incubated for 4 h at 37 °C with shaking at 240 rpm. After incubation, 2 mL of each culture was centrifuged for 6 min at 15,000 rcf and then subsequently washed with 1X PBS. Bacterial cell pellets were resuspended in 1 mL of 100 μM DCFH-DA (20 mM stock in DMSO diluted in 1X PBS) and incubated in the dark for 1.5 h at 37 °C with shaking at 240 rpm. After incubation, cells were pelleted for 6 min at 15,000 rcf, washed with 1X PBS, and finally resuspended in fresh CAMHB. Cells were then allowed to recover for 1 h at 37 °C. The cell suspension was aliquoted (100 μL) into a black-walled, clear-bottom 96-well plate. Fluorescence was measured on a BioTek HTX Synergy Multimode plate reader with excitation at 485/20 nm and emission at 528/20 nm. Separate treatments were measured at a minimum of five times in at least three separate biological samples. CAMHB without bacterial cells was measured for background fluorescence, and 100 μM DCFH-DA in 1X PBS was measured to track spontaneous fluorescence of the dye. Statistical analysis and graph generation were completed using GraphPad Prism version 11.0.0.

BacLight™ cell permeability assay.

The BacLight™ assay [Invitrogen] was used to determine membrane permeability as previously described [32]. A. baumannii 4106 or K. pneumoniae B9 were grown separately overnight in fresh CAMHB at 37 °C shaking at 200 rpm. The culture was diluted 1:40 in fresh CAMHB and incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 200 rpm for 4 h. Cultures were centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C followed by washing the cell pellet with 1 mL sterile water. Cell pellets were resuspended to 1/10 the original volume and diluted 1:20 in sterile water or water that contained tested adjuvants as desired concentrations. These suspensions were incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 200 rpm for 1 h. Samples were then centrifuged at 10, 000 rpm for 15 min, and cell pellets were washed with, and finally resuspended in sterile water. A mixture of 1:1 SYTO- and propidium iodide was added to each suspension to give a final concentration of 3 μg/mL for each treatment condition. The suspensions were aliquoted (100 μL) into each well of a black-walled, clear-bottom 96-well microtiter plate [Corning] and incubated in the dark at room temperature for 15 min. Green fluorescence (SYTO-9) was read at 530 nm and red fluorescence (propidium iodide) was read at 645 nm. Both dues have an excitation of 485 nm [BioTek Synergy HTX]. The ratio of green to red fluorescence was expressed as a percentage of the control to determine relative membrane permeability.

Mice were treated intraperitoneally with 150 mg/kg cyclophosphamide in PBS on days 4 and 1 days before inoculation with bacteria. Bacterial stocks were diluted 1:1000 in fresh CAMHB and incubated at 37 °C with shaking overnight, the day before inoculation. The next day, bacterial cultures were diluted 1:100 in fresh CAMHB and grown to an OD600 of 0.5 (3–4 h). Bacteria were pelleted by centrifugation and resuspended in 1x volumes of PBS. The peritoneal cavity of each animal was inoculated ~5 × 107 CFUs of bacteria per mouse following the temporal scheme associated with each figure. Delivery of colistin and adjuvant relative to infection of the mice varied, and the relative timing is shown for each study in the associated experimental timeline. In all experiments in which mice received colistin, the antibiotic was prepared in PBS and delivered i.p. at 5 mg/kg. Adjuvant was prepared at concentrations indicated in each figure in 5% DMSO/95% carboxymethylcellulose (0.5% in PBS) just prior to initiation of the experiment. Adjuvant and vehicle treatments relative to treatment infection and antibiotic treatment varied and are detailed in the experimental timelines.

Four hours post-bacterial infection, blood, peritoneal lavage fluid, and liver samples were harvested to quantify bacterial burden. Blood (0.2 mL) was obtained via cardiac puncture, diluted in 0.4 mL PBS containing 5 mM EDTA. The peritoneal cavity was infused with 3 mL PBS, followed by abdominal massage to facilitate the recovery of bacteria. The abdominal cavity was then opened, and all fluids were collected using a transfer pipette. The right lateral lobe of the liver was excised, homogenized in 1 mL PBS, and further diluted in PBS supplemented with 0.05% Triton X-100. These tissue and fluid samples were serially diluted and cultured on LB agar to enumerate CFUs.

Sample collection and bacterial quantification.

Four hours after infection, peritoneal lavage fluid was collected to assess bacterial burden. The peritoneal cavity was lavaged with 3 mL PBS, followed by gentle abdominal massage to facilitate bacterial recovery, after which the cavity was opened, and all fluid collected. All samples were serially diluted and plated on LB agar to enumerate colony-forming units (CFUs).

General methods for chemistry.

All reagents were purchased from commercially available sources and used without further purification. Reactions were monitored using 250 μm silica gel 60 F254 precoated thin layer chromatography (TLC) plates. Plates were visualized with 254 nm UV light. Flash chromatography was performed using 60 Å mesh standard-grade silica gel (Sorbtech). NMR solvents were obtained from commercially available sources and used without further purification. All 1H NMR spectra (400 MHz) and 13C NMR spectra (101, 126, and 201 MHz) were recorded at 25 °C on Bruker AVANCE III HD spectrometers. Chemical shifts (δ) are reported in parts per million (ppm) relative to the NMR solvent, and coupling constants (J) are given in Hertz (Hz). Abbreviations used include (singlet), d (doublet), dd (doublet of doublets), td (triplet of doublets), and m (multiplet). High-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) measurements were performed at the Mass Spectrometry and Proteomics Facility, University of Notre Dame. Infrared spectra were recorded on an FT/IR-4100 spectrophotometer (ν_max in cm−1), and UV–visible absorbance spectra were recorded on a Genesys 10 scanning UV/Vis spectrophotometer (λ_max in nm).

HPLC general procedure.

Samples were analyzed using reverse phase liquid chromatography coupled with low-resolution tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS). An Advion A-2030 Scientific Instruments LC system was used. Separation was achieved using a Phenomenex C18 reversed-phase column (5 μm, 150 × 4.6 mm). For analysis, 20 μL of a 1 mg/mL sample was injected. The method used a solvent flow of 1.000 mL/min. Initial gradient conditions (95% water:5% acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid) were held for 1 min. From 1 to 20 min, the mobile phase composition increased linearly to 5% water:95% acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid. From 20.1 to 25 min, the mobile phase composition was increased linearly to 1% water:99% acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid. At 25.1 to 30 min, the column was re-equilibrated with 95% water:5% acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid. HPLC data acquisition and analysis were performed using Advion LC Data Express software. All compounds showed >95% purity. Novel compound characterization data is provided in the SI.
