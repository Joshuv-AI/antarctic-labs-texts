Respectful community engagement in health research with diverse im/migrant communities
Authors: Stefanie Machado, Samira Karsiem, M Ruth Lavergne, Shira Goldenberg, Mei-ling Wiedmeyer
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
IntroductionGlobal migration and immigration are increasing, and migrants and immigrants (im/migrants) have specific health needs and healthcare experiences. Yet, im/migrant involvement in immigration and health research in Canada is inconsistent. Heretofore, involvement has primarily been in research planning, data collection and analysis, with little community involvement during knowledge exchange or through training and colearning opportunities. Community engagement has been especially uncommon in mixed-method and quantitative research in Canada.ObjectiveThis article describes lessons learnt from the Evaluating Inequities in Refugee & Immigrants’ Health Access (IRIS) project from 2018 to 2023, an ongoing mixed-method, community-based research project in British Columbia, Canada. Specifically, we share our core community engagement project structures, Commitments to Community and our Community Engagement Backbone, both collaboratively developed with im/migrant community memebers.ParticipantsPeople with varied experiences of im/migration and connections to multiple, specific im/migrant communities participate in the project as participants, community researchers, community advisory board members, faculty members and students. Core research activities are supported in English, Farsi, Spanish and Tigrinya. We engage community members throughout the research process, from identifying research topics to knowledge exchange.ConclusionWe found that these structures offer an accessible visual representation of the project’s commitments to community engagement, and the ways these commitments are demonstrated through values and action. Our training opportunities, colearning activities and knowledge exchange efforts also confirmed the accuracy of interpretation, prompted additional analysis to clarify or add depth to findings, and helped us identify additional research topics. We hope these learnings can be used to expand engagement with diverse im/migrant communities in health and immigration research.

Introduction

Global migration and immigration are increasing, and migrants and immigrants (im/migrants) have specific healthcare needs.1–3 We use the term im/migrants to include immigrants and migrants with permanent and precarious im/migration status, the latter of which includes people with temporary status or who are without immigration status.4 Today, international im/migrants make up over 272 million of the world’s population.5 Canada remains a top destination country for im/migrants globally,6 and British Columbia (BC) is a top destination province.7 8 The borders of BC were defined on the lands of over 200 Indigenous nations through resisted historical and ongoing colonial processes, including forced displacement and internal migration.9 10 The federal ministry, Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada, controls movement across international borders by issuing travel documents and screening potential permanent and temporary residents,10 11 and determines whether im/migrants are admitted as temporary or permanent residents. BC is most densely populated in the Lower Mainland (the area surrounding Vancouver), which is where this project took place. Federal immigration targets in Canada continue to increase; in fact, the government’s plans for the year 2024 include the country’s highest targets in im/migration history—an expected 430 000–450 000 permanent residents are to arrive.12 However, people will also arrive with other, more precarious im/migration statuses and live as temporary foreign workers, visitors, international students, and im/migrants without official status. These groups will also be without the healthcare, mobility and employment rights associated with permanent residency and citizenship.4 13 Accordingly, we must meaningfully understand the needs of im/migrant communities to appropriately address them.

Community-based research (CBR) offers approaches to more accurately understanding im/migrants’ needs. CBR is an approach to research that is participatory and equitably involves community members, organisations and researchers in all aspects of the research process, where partners contribute expertise, decision-making and take on various responsibilities.4 13 14 Grounded in teachings from community organising, social justice, Indigenous researchers and protocols, and feminist theory, CBR principles prioritise analyses built on respectful relationships, honest conversations and collaboration to support nuanced understandings of lived experiences.15 Community engagement can take place at various levels ranging from consultations (obtaining feedback on analysis and decisions) to collaboration (partnering on decision-making and generating recommendations) to empowerment (placing final decisions in the hands of community members).16 In previous research with im/migrants in Canada, CBR has particularly ensured that im/migrant identities, experiences and cultures ground and are reflected in research.17 However, there has been little use of CBR approaches in quantitative im/migrant health research or research that draws on administrative data in Canada, as well as CBR research in this area that simultaneously considers community engagement, researcher accountability, and the integration of findings. This is what we aimed to do in this project.

Practicing CBR in institutional settings has challenges, including the need for additional time and financial resources; perceptions of CBR as less academically rigorous; and barriers to participation for community members, including time and transportation.9 With a lack of time also comes challenges related to inadequate training and experience in CBR for both researchers and community members,18 as well as few opportunities to build relationships and trust—both of which are essential CBR principles.14 19 20 Research has also discussed differences in communication styles and language barriers as a key challenge in conducting CBR, given the importance of collaboration and partnership in this type of work,18 20 21 which is especially important within the context of im/migrant health research. Given these challenges, involvement of im/migrant communities in immigration and health research has been inconsistent in Canada. In our review of literature, we found that im/migrant involvement in Canadian research has primarily been in research planning and qualitative data collection,22 and sometimes qualitative data analysis.23 Im/migrant community members were infrequently involved during knowledge exchange, that is the exchange and sharing of ideas, evidence and experience, nor in training and colearning opportunities or engagement beyond the participant role.24

These conclusions drawn from Canadian literature align well with international findings about engaging with im/migrants in CBR. In their review of international CBR projects with refugees in North America, Australia and New Zealand, the Middle East, Africa and Europe, Filler et al25 found that while all 14 of the studies they reviewed involved participants in research planning, fewer studies engaged with participants in data collection (57%), knowledge translation (43%) and data analysis (29%). In a larger review of CBR projects with immigrants primarily in the USA, Vaughn et al17 assessed studies based on clarity of immigrant roles in research from level 1 (immigrant involvement unclear or not described) to level 4 (immigrant involvement clearly described). Of the 161 studies that were reviewed, only 52% were ranked as level 4. Level 4 studies also assessed im/migrant levels of engagement at each research stage. Engagement in data analysis and interpretation was much lower than in other stages of research.17 As well, only 43% of level 4 studies engaged immigrants in all research stages (study design, choosing data collection instruments, recruitment, data collection and data collection and interpretation). These reviews point to two central challenges in CBR with im/migrants: (1) a lack of full spectrum engagement with im/migrants in the research process, and (2) limited details about how im/migrants are engaged throughout the CBR process.

To our knowledge, few studies in Canada have engaged im/migrant communities in all research stages or in quantitative or mixed-method research; however, we identified two promising examples. In their project focused on identifying health promotion needs among South Asian women, Choudhry et al22 consulted with community members at the research planning stage who affirmed the research direction and relevance. Researchers worked with participants to assign research tasks, and held two knowledge exchange events to share findings with participants who were invited to reflect on their experiences working on the project, as well as present findings to key stakeholders. In another study focused on examining socio-cultural factors influencing tuberculosis prevention and treatment in im/migrant and Indigenous communities, Gibson et al23 hired community research associates (CAs) who led interviews and interpreted and analysed interview data. CAs were hired from the im/migrant and Indigenous communities that the research focused on. CAs received training and education on the research topic and interview strategies, and training materials were partially developed by a community advisory council and stakeholders.

Notably, there are also strong examples of CBR with im/migrants that live outside Canada. In research by Lightfoot et al,26 to identify the cultural health assets of Somali and Oromo communities in the Minneapolis and Saint Paul areas, community centre staff, community leaders, elders and healthcare providers collaborated across the study. Community members were also hired as community asset mappers (CAMs); CAMs were fluent in Somali or Oromo, plus English. With training, CAMs developed skills and knowledge on interviewing, research methods, research ethics and data analysis. CAMs were also involved in identifying key themes and coding. Finally, after analysis was complete, community member-led action teams were formed with an emphasis on using the cultural health assets learnt to promote health. As well, Revens et al27 focused on the supporting factors of resilience and mental health among Latinx immigrants from Mexico, Central and South America, and the Caribbean, leveraging an existing partnership between a local university and community centre. The community centre, which serves a predominately Latinx community, identified the research problem. Community advisory board members also had a significant role throughout the research process, receiving training to develop and accurately translate recruitment materials, collect and analyse data, and translate and exchange knowledge. Despite the strength of these studies, however, they mainly used qualitative approaches and engaged with one or two language groups at most. As such, there appears to be limited evidence and understanding of how to engage multiple, multilingual communities across the research process or through quantitative or mixed-method approaches.

In this article, we incorporated learnings from challenges and thoughtful examples of CBR with im/migrants in the Evaluating Inequities in Refugee & Immigrants’ Health Access (IRIS) project. Below, we describe a community engagement framework and tools developed within IRIS, which is a community-based, mixed-method study of im/migrants’ access to health services in BC, Canada. Our objective is to offer an accessible, visual representation of our commitments to community engagement, and the ways these commitments are demonstrated through values and action, so that other research teams can share our learning and expand CBR with different im/migrant communities.

The IRIS project

The IRIS project is hosted at the Centre for Gender & Sexual Health Equity in Vancouver, BC and is made up of a team of researchers, faculty members, students and community members, and health and im/migration partner organisations.28 The project aims to understand and generate recommendations for policies and programmes that improve health, well-being, and access to health services for im/migrants in BC. The quantitative arm analyses confidential, linked health and immigration system data to understand health services use. The qualitative arm involves initial and follow-up interviews with im/migrants and service providers to understand factors shaping health access. The IRIS project, including both arms, is guided by a community engagement framework that articulates activities and commitments at all research stages. IRIS holds ethical approval from the Simon Fraser University (SFU) and Providence Health Care/University of British Columbia harmonised ethics review boards.

IRIS began in 2018 with building relationships and consulting with im/migrants and health and social service providers about the health service access of im/migrants in Metro Vancouver. We did this by conducting consultations from September to November 2018 in English, Farsi, Spanish and Tigrinya, which were the preferred languages of the primary im/migrant communities served by community partner organisations. Community partners include settlement, health and advocacy organisations that primarily serve im/migrant communities and work to improve im/migrant health and well-being and access to health and social services, as well as advocate for im/migrant rights and justice. While some organisations are funded to serve im/migrants with permanent residency and fluency in English, others provide services to people with more precarious im/migration status, including work permit holders, international students, asylum seekers and people without official im/migration status, who speak a variety of languages. Building on learnings from community consultations and our review of literature, we worked with partners to identify and clarify the focus of IRIS: the healthcare access and experiences of im/migrants in BC. What came with the relationships we built was the addition of core team members with lived im/migration experience. By the end of 2018, the IRIS project was established and built on partnerships with local im/migrant-serving community organisations, and a research team with im/migration experience and expertise in qualitative, quantitative and health services research.

Community engagement within IRIS

‘Community’ within the IRIS project includes im/migrant research participants, team members, community advisory board members, community partners and the broader im/migrant community. Community advisory board members include individuals who wish to meet regularly and shape the direction of the IRIS project by sharing ideas, experiences and feedback on all stages of the research process. Interview participants include im/migrants who live in the Lower Mainland, BC, who have entered Canada through an international border, and who often have experience of precarious im/migration status. Im/migrant team members and community partners collaborate on the development of research materials, data collection, participatory analyses and privacy and confidentiality agreements; the cocreation of clear, accurate dissemination products; and knowledge exchange. IRIS uses a CBR design to attend to cultural humility, transparent and respectful partnerships, and a collaborative process that involves im/migrants leading this research.

IRIS has community relations at two levels with: (1) community partners and (2) community advisory boards and interview participants. These relationships were built over time and are strengthened through respectful communication and collaboration. Community partners include im/migrant-serving organisations that we regularly engage with; however, levels of engagement vary by organisation. For example, we prioritise engagement with organisations that offer services to people with precarious im/migration status. Community advisory boards function in four different languages (English, Farsi, Spanish, and Tigrinya) that support most languages spoken by participants. The communities we work with are also represented on the IRIS team—several advisory boards are facilitated by team members from the same cultural background or who speak a shared language, but who also recognise nuances within communities. This helps community members feel comfortable participating in activities and ensures that different perspectives are woven throughout the research.

Important to the IRIS project is honouring people’s lived experiences of im/migration, as well as compensating and supporting their time and expertise. Community advisory board members are paid for their time and any childminding or transportation necessary to support meeting attendance, offered active referrals to needed services, and provided a meal to enjoy when they attend a meeting. Where research with marginalised communities can sometimes be extractive and harmful, these activities demonstrate our respect for each participant’s safety and well-being.

Community engagement within IRIS includes moving beyond informing community members about the research to building a sense of collaboration and empowerment. This requires partnering with community members at each stage of research and inviting im/migrants to be decision-makers. Our Commitments to Community and the Community Engagement Backbone are tools that describe our commitments to community engagement in practice.

Our Commitments to Community

Our Commitments to Community (figure 1) is a visual that highlights who we work with, our values and our responsibilities to community members. Through an iterative process with team members and community advisories, we identified and defined three values that are core to IRIS: collaboration, respect and relationship building.

The Inequities in Refugee & Immigrants’ Health Access (IRIS) project’s commitments to community engagement.

Collaboration

Collaboration within IRIS means that we work to include a range of community perspectives at each stage of research. For example, at analysis and writing stages, we organise meetings to foster discussion around interpretation to ensure that a variety of perspectives are shared and incorporated into the research. We also acknowledge that power differences exist within the team and among community members and impact collaboration. These power differences may be based on factors like race and ethnicity, language preference, im/migration status, education and research experience, and payment from the research project to community members, as well as access to information and control of research funding in the case of principal investigators. We actively reflect on these power differences and learn from community members to try to mitigate harm and balance power. This includes ensuring that each research manuscript, for example, is not developed by the core research team in isolation, but collaboratively with community members with diversity in im/migration status, language preference and education and research experience, all of whom are paid for their time and expertise.

Respect

We deeply respect community members’ identities and lived experiences with im/migration and prioritise opportunities for community members to share their knowledge throughout the research process. Through research built on community engagement, we aim to provide community members with formalised research findings that reflect their experiences and inform community advocacy. For example, in developing research manuscripts and sharing findings with partner organisations, we work closely with community members to ensure that the interpretation of data and key messages of the research accurately reflect the lived experiences of im/migrant communities. Our goal is to ensure that the main research messages respectfully reflect im/migrant experiences and expertise.

Relationship building

We focus on building reciprocal relationships with community members and partners throughout the research process. While some community advisory board members and participants choose to use and develop skills they already have (eg, sharing findings through social media, creating posters, etc), we also offer training to support new skills. This includes formal training in qualitative data analysis, manual coding and research writing. Part of respect and relationship building is reciprocity, or giving back to people and communities we work with within and beyond the scope of IRIS. We realise this by offering people tangible support outside the project when we can, including immigration application support, recommendation letters for university applications, career references, or referrals to legal, health and social services.

We identified our responsibilities to community at the beginning of the IRIS project with the aim of addressing challenges to CBR identified in the existing literature and creating robust, respectful engagement with diverse im/migrant communities. Engaging in reflective conversations with participants and community members led to suggestions around our team’s responsibilities, ideas for providing support beyond the research, and the need to address power dynamics through care and capacity building. IRIS Community Engagement Backbone

The Community Engagement Backbone (figure 2) is collaboratively designed by community advisory board members and IRIS team members to visually represent the project’s commitment to community engagement at each stage of research. It also includes activities that community members may participate in and can be applied to individual analyses to maintain the integrity of engagement. The backbone is a living diagram that evolves alongside community and team needs.

Inequities in Refugee & Immigrants’ Health Access Community Engagement Backbone.

We primarily drew from Israel’s principles of CBR19 in codeveloping the IRIS Community Engagement Backbone. This means that we recognised the im/migrant community as a unity of identity; built on strengths within the community, including knowledge and experiences related to the research areas; facilitated collaboration and partnership throughout research stages; attended to power dynamics; fostered colearning and capacity building through research training and collaborative analyses; supported reciprocity in knowledge exchange; focused on issues that were relevant to community and that attended to multiple determinants of health; worked with community members and partners on knowledge exchange efforts; and supported community engagement through various years of relationship building.19 We drew on these principles while focusing on our core values of collaboration, respect and relationship building when developing the IRIS Community Engagement Backbone. This is evident through our efforts on capacity building through research, reciprocity in knowledge exchange, as well as priority of im/migrants’ lived experiences throughout the research process described below.

Community engagement is a point of integration for qualitative and quantitative methods at different stages of the research process.29 Regardless of whether a research product is in its initial stages, in-progress, or close to being finalised, community engagement is prioritised and adapted based on team capacity. Below we expand on the backbone with examples of how we commit to and work with community members at each stage of IRIS research.

We will listen and work with you to identify research areas that are important and relevant to your experiences

We host small group conversations in multiple languages, as well as large multilingual group discussions with community advisory board members and interested research participants to talk about potential research areas, brainstorm research questions and understand community perspectives on the relevance of the proposed topics. Decisions on the research topic, questions, and focus are shaped by these conversations.

Challenges and considerations

Like other CBR projects, community engagement within IRIS has encountered challenges, many of which have been discussed in previous research that attend to Israel14 and Wallerstein’s20 principles of CBR. Some key considerations that we attended to during the project include:

Funding: adequately resourcing the IRIS project required multiple grants, including pilot funds for initial community engagement (SFU internal award), ongoing direct support for community engagement (Vancouver Foundation), and traditional federal funding (Canadian Institute for Health Research). Respectful partnerships require time and resources for colearning, capacity building and relationship building. This consideration has been recognised in other CBR projects,14 18 21 30 and has sometimes been a challenge within IRIS.

Collaboration is a key facet of CBR, and strengthening relationships is important to foster this. As a lack of trust and respect has been identified as a challenge in CBR,14 21 we attempted to minimise power imbalances between institutions and community by offering tangible forms of support.

Counselling: as most team members have lived experience of im/migration, we meet monthly with a clinical counsellor who helps us navigate challenging feelings that arise during this work. These include triggering feelings or experiences of retraumatisation for im/migrant team members who are reading and analysing painful participant experiences that may be similar to their own. They also help us function in a culturally diverse team that exists within an immigration system that is often harmful for people with precarious im/migration status, where loss of status could bring loss of employment, income, health insurance and security. Counselling was not planned at study outset but incorporated in response to arising needs and challenges.18 31 This was especially important as immigration-related challenges emerged in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic, including border closures, prolonged family separation, immigration processing delays and isolation associated with public health measures that impacted both participants and team members.

Conclusion

Through our experience on the IRIS project, we have learnt deeply about community engagement and its feasibility and importance within im/migration and health research. We have learnt that it requires financial resources and time to support a strong research team with varied expertise and lived experiences, as well as clarity around community engagement activities. We have also learnt how to engage community members in mixed-method research, integrating both qualitative and quantitative study arms, which has not previously been documented in research to our knowledge. Finally, community engagement requires commitment and accountability from the research team.

Community engagement within IRIS is unique in that we engage community members throughout the research process, from research planning to dissemination, including as part of the research team. To our knowledge, IRIS is one of the only research projects in Canada that has conducted research with multiple im/migrant communities in this way; this is attributable to the establishment of community advisory board structures that facilitated confirmation of research directions, accuracy in research interpretation and participation in knowledge exchange across language groups. This deepened the rigour and quality of our research and required commitment to attending to the challenges and considerations outlined above. By sharing Our Commitments to Community, the Community Engagement Backbone, and examples of community engagement across the research process, we hope that researchers interested in doing similar work can build on our learnings and continue to expand research that engages im/migrant communities.
