A Reinforcement Learning Approach for RIS-aided Fair Communications
Alex Pierron, Michel Barbeau, Luca De Cicco, Jose Rubio-Hernan, Joaquin Garcia-Alfaro

Abstract
Reconfigurable Intelligent Surfaces (RISs) are composed of physical elements that can dynamically alter electromagnetic wave properties to enhance beamforming and leading to improvements in areas with low coverage properties. They have the potential to be combined with Reinforcement Learning (RL) techniques to achieve network performance and energy efficiency via optimization techniques. In addition to performance and energy improvements, it is also crucial to consider the concept of fair communications . RISs must ensure that User Equipment (UE) units receive their signals with adequate strength, without other UE being deprived of service due to insufficient power. In this paper, we address such a problem. We explore the fairness properties of previous work and propose a novel method that aims at obtaining an efficient and fair duplex RIS-RL system for multiple legitimate UE units. We report and discuss our experimental work and simulation results. We also release our code and datasets to foster further research in the topic.



I
Introduction

To ensure that future
Beyond-Fifth-Generation (B5G)
and 6G network systems are deployable and usable, it is essential to guarantee that they meet the necessary service and security criteria for optimal operation.
Reconfigurable Intelligent Surfaces (RISs)
, crucial and innovative elements of these futuristic networks, are subject to the same requirements due to their strategic role in the communication protocol.

RISs
offer significant potential for enhancing wireless communication in 5G and
B5G
network systems
[
1
]
.
RISs
are passive sub-wavelength elements that can dynamically alter electromagnetic wave properties to perform beamforming, with the goal of optimizing network performance
[
2
]
.

RISs
can also enhance network coverage by redirecting signals, mitigating blockages, and extending reach in urban environments, ensuring consistent connectivity
[
3
]
. They also contribute to energy efficiency by optimizing signal paths and reducing the need for high-power transmissions, supporting sustainable operations
[
4
]
. By redirecting the signal from the base antenna to end-users through the dynamic configuration of their sub-elements,
RIS
operate at the physical level. Through beamforming, they are responsible for ensuring that the physical signal correctly reaches the intended user, thereby directing the signal towards the right target.

The strategic positioning of
RIS
, combined with the necessity for rapid adaptability to support future scalability—such as accommodating numerous and mobile users, as well as those entering and exiting the system—poses significant challenges. Additionally, the limited computational resources of
RIS
, which can be considered embedded components, further complicate this scenario. To address these challenges, the implementation of
Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL)
is considered
[
5
]
. This approach is particularly suited for developing a controller capable of efficiently managing downlink and duplex communications for a large number of users.

Reinforcement learning
Reinforcement Learning (RL)

[
6
]
is a type of machine learning where an agent learns to make decisions by performing actions in an environment to achieve maximum cumulative reward. Unlike supervised learning, RL does not rely on labeled input/output pairs but instead learns from the consequences of its actions.
DRL

[
7
]
extends this concept by using deep neural networks to approximate the optimal action-value functions, enabling the handling of high-dimensional state spaces.

By leveraging
DRL
, the aim is to create a system that not only adapts dynamically to changing user demands but also optimizes resource allocation, ensuring robust and scalable communication solutions. Moreover, and beyond capacity and security concerns, it is also crucial to consider the concept of
fairness
, i.e., each user must receive their signal with adequate strength, without other users being deprived of service due to insufficient power. Several questions arise regarding the desired behavior and the methodology to achieve it.

In this paper, we address these challenges by proposing a novel framework that integrates
DRL
with fairness-aware mechanisms to enhance
RIS
-aided communication systems. We present experimental findings and simulation outcomes, and we make our code openly available to encourage further research in this area.
Based on the existing literature, our approach uses
DRL
algorithms such as
Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG)
and
Twin Delayed Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (TD3)
. This choice allows for a more effective comparison with the studies upon which our work is founded, enabling us to concentrate on the additional parameters that configure our decision-making agent. Thus, we refrain from utilizing state of the art algorithms such as
Soft-Actor Critic (SAC)

[
8
]
and
Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)

[
9
]
, to ensure a consistent progression between our work and the articles we are based on.
Our contribution includes a proposal of new reward functions to ensure equitable service distribution among users while maintaining high system performance and a discussion about the experimental results.

VI

This work serves as an introduction and is followed by the inclusion of eavesdroppers and potential attackers in the considered scenarios. By first obtaining an efficient and fair duplex

-

IV
Fairness Improvements

The objective is to develop a system that maximizes the legitimate data rate transmitted to
UE
, ensuring that each user receives a minimum usable data rate. As stated above we want to provide a fair service among all users despite the non-linearity of our optimization problem. Next, we summarize the reward functions of the two main approaches surveyed in Section
II
.

IV-A

Common Terms

We introduce the following terms that are used in all of the studied and proposed reward functions:

\displaystyle D_{i}=\log_{2}\left(1+\text{SINR}_{i}^{\text{D}}\right),

(1)

\displaystyle U_{i}=\log_{2}\left(1+\text{SINR}_{i}^{\text{U}}\right)

(2)

Equation (
1
) represents the downlink data rate received by the
i^{\text{th}}
user, and Equation (
2
) represents the uplink data rate emitted by the
i^{\text{th}}
user and received by the
BS
.
\text{SINR}_{i}^{\text{D}}
and
\text{SINR}_{i}^{\text{U}}
are the signal-to-interference-plus-noise ratios at downlink and uplink, respectively, for user
i
.

After introducing these important terms, we proceed by detailing the first reward function that we consider, which is directly adapted from Peng et al.
[
12
]
.

IV-B

Peng et al. Reward Function

The reward function presented in
[
12
]
forms the basis of our formulation, emphasizing pure capacity across all users. For each user
i
, the
Secrecy Rate (SR)

R_{i}
is defined as:

\displaystyle R_{i}

\displaystyle=\left[D_{i}+U_{i}\right]^{+},

(3)

where
[x]^{+}
corresponds to
\max(0,x)
. The
Sum Secrecy Rate (SSR)
, final reward, is given by:

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}R_{i}.

(4)

We use this reward as the baseline function in our study since it focuses solely on pure capacity without considering fairness. We further refer to this reward function as the
baseline
reward function for the curves of the experiments.

IV-C

Yang et al. Reward Function

The reward function presented in
[
13
]
takes into account the
Quality of Service (QoS)
requirements associated to the users. The reward proposed in
[
13
]
is only for the downlink scenario and with the presence of eavesdroppers. We therefore adapt it to keep the terms that are relevant for our duplex case without any eavesdroppers. We propose the following reward that we denote as
QoS
reward:

\displaystyle R_{QoS}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}R_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mu(p_{i}^{D}+p_{i}^{U})

(5)

with
\mu
a constant used to balance the combined performances and the individual constraints for each user.

p_{i}^{D}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }D_{i}<\epsilon_{i}^{D},\forall i\in[\![1,k]\!],\\
0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(6)

p_{i}^{U}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }U_{i}<\epsilon_{i}^{U},\forall i\in[\![1,k]\!],\\
0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(7)

where
\epsilon_{i}^{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
corresponds to the quality criteria for the downlink data rate associated to user
i
and
\epsilon_{i}^{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
is the quality criteria for the uplink data rate associated to user
i
. We further refer to this reward function by using the
QoS
acronym for the experiments curves.

By introducing the thresholds
\epsilon_{i}^{D}
and
\epsilon_{i}^{U}
, we can deal with a first notion of minimal coverage for each user. Nevertheless, this reward function may allow all users to reach a minimum service coverage if thresholds are reasonable, but once this condition is satisfied, the system is entirely free to maximize the data rate on a specific user to maximize easily the reward and thus leaving other users with minimum coverage, a situation that can have a high impact in case of real deployments.

We therefore propose a new reward system to improve the fairness among all others and still take into account the minimum service conditions.

IV-D

Extended Reward Function

We propose an extension to the reward function proposed in Peng
et al
.
[
12
]
by adding a
QoS
and a fairness incentived term. This reward is intended to reflect, for a designated user
i
, wether or not the system is currently ensuring the minimum capacity constraint and the resultant amount of data rate received.

For each user
i
we introduce the following reward
R^{i}_{\text{Q}}

\quad R_{\text{Q}}^{i}=\begin{cases}D_{i}+U_{i}&\text{if }D_{i}\geq\epsilon_{k}^{D}\text{~and}\ U_{i}\geq\epsilon_{i}^{U}\\
U_{i}&\text{if }D_{i}<\epsilon_{i}^{D}\text{~and}\ U_{i}\geq\epsilon_{i}^{U}\\
D_{i}&\text{if }D_{i}\geq\epsilon_{i}^{D}\text{~and}\ U_{i}<\epsilon_{i}^{U}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(8)

where
\epsilon_{i}^{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
\epsilon_{i}^{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
are the same thresholds for user
i
as defined in
IV-C
.

We first define a new reward denoted as
R_{\text{fairness}}

R_{\text{fairness}}=\frac{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}R_{i}\right)^{2}}{k\ \cdot\sum_{i=1}^{k}{R_{i}}^{2}}

(9)

which corresponds to the
Jain Fairness Index (JFI)

[
14
]
for the
SR

R_{i}
for all users
i
.

Notice that
R_{\text{fairness}}
takes into account the fairness regarding
SR
and
SSR
rewards because it is the effective data rate obtained in reality. It is supposed to help to better (and fairly) distribute the capacity of the system so one user is not obtaining an overly large proportion of the data rate, leading to deteriorated service for the other users.

Then, we extend the reward function to
R_{\text{fqos}}
, which feeds our
DRL
algorithm.
R_{\text{fqos}}
is expressed as follows

\displaystyle R_{\text{fqos}}=\left(1-\alpha\right)\sum_{i=1}^{k}R_{\text{Q}}^{i}+\alpha\cdot k\cdot\ R_{\text{fairness}}

(10)

where
\alpha\in[0,1]
is a hyperparameter that sets the tradeoff between pure
QoS
and pure fairness reward. We further refer to this reward function by using the
Fairness & Quality of Service (FQoS)
acronym.

We outline only the most critical parameters to ease comprehension and highlight significant changes between sequences. The learning rates are

\delta_{c}=1\cdot 10^{-3}

We utilize 15 parallel environments, each initialized with distinct internal seeds, to perform the roll-outs. To ensure reproducibility, all experiments share the same master random seed. For each experiment, we illustrate the progression of the average reward stored in the replay buffer during the training phase. As the replay buffer functions on a first-in, first-out basis, this average provides a dependable measure of the system’s learning advancement from a reward standpoint. The goal of the

V-D

Discussion

For an evaluation of the
RIS

Systems with lower performance achieve higher average fairness indices, between 0.67 and 0.65. However, this marginal increase in fairness does not compensate for the loss in of pure
SSR
in Figure
5
. To further understand the fairness extremes of the trained
RIS
-controllers, it is necessary to examine the
JFI

This analysis underscores the importance of embedding fairness signals in reward systems. Although the proposed

There is a clear need for extensive hyperparameter research with the existing reward functions, as well as a more comprehensive exploration of strategies to balance signal maximization, quality of service constraints, and fairness.
Since we only changed the reward functions, changing the composition of the state could also leverage additional performance and represent an other research direction.
This paves the way for enhanced

VI
Conclusion

We explored the concept of
fair communications
with respect to
DRL
-enabled
RIS
environments. We assumed that
RISs
must ensure that
UE
units receive their signals with adequate strength, without other
UE
being deprived of service due to insufficient power. We investigated such a problem and explored the fairness properties of previous work. We proposed a novel method that aims at obtaining an efficient and fair duplex
DRL
-
RIS
system, for multiple legitimate
UE
units. We reported experimental work and simulation results. We have also released our code and experiments to foster further research on this topic.
Future research directions may include an investigation into hyper-parameter optimization and reward engineering to achieve a better balance between pure capacity and perfect fairness, thereby attaining optimal performance. Subsequent studies could concentrate on the detailed analysis of more complex environments by incorporating user mobility patterns and expanding the scale of the system, specifically in terms of the number of users or
RIS
elements.
