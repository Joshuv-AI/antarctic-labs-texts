Sleep Apnoea Variability in Pacemaker Patients: A Women‐Predominant Phenotype
Authors: Philipp Spitaler, Valentin Bilgeri, Gudrun Feuchtner, Patrick Rockenschaub, Fabian Barbieri, Birkan Tugrul, Agne Adukauskaite, Markus Stühlinger, Bernhard Erich Pfeifer, Sabine Ludwig, Peter Willeit, Axel Bauer, Wolfgang Dichtl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTStudy ObjectivesSingle‐night polysomnography assumes stable sleep apnoea (SA) severity; night‐to‐night variability may identify clinically relevant phenotypes, particularly in women. Sex/gender‐specific longitudinal phenotypes were characterised using device‐detected respiratory disturbance index (RDI) and an instability metric independent of mean severity.MethodsPacemaker patients in the prospective ACaSA study with at least 180 valid nightly RDI measurements after a 30‐day post‐implant period were analysed. Severity was summarised by mean RDI. Variability was summarised by an Instability Index (residual from the log–log regression of RDI standard deviation on mean RDI). Phenotypes were defined by mean RDI (< 20 vs. ≥ 20 events/h) and instability (above vs. below median).ResultsAbout 37,260 nights from 207 patients (median age 76 years, IQR [69–81]) were analysed. Four phenotypes were identified: Mild‐Stable (n = 68), Mild‐Unstable (n = 67), Severe‐Stable (n = 35), and Severe‐Unstable (n = 37). Women comprised 37.7% of the total cohort and they were disproportionately overrepresented in the Mild‐Unstable versus in the Mild‐Stable phenotype (61.2% vs. 33.8%; p = 0.002). Among patients with mean RDI < 20 (n = 135), female sex/gender (adjusted OR 3.38, 95% CI 1.60–7.40; p = 0.002) and obesity (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2; adjusted OR 3.80, 95% CI 1.31–12.53; p = 0.019) were independent predictors of the Mild‐Unstable phenotype. Mild‐Unstable had more severe‐range nights (RDI ≥ 20/h) than Mild‐Stable (7.8% vs. 2.2%; p < 0.001). Severe‐Unstable recorded RDI < 20/h on 33.9% of nights versus 13.9% in Severe‐Stable (p < 0.001).ConclusionNight‐to‐night variability identifies longitudinal SA phenotypes not captured by mean RDI. A female‐ and obesity‐predominant Mild‐Unstable phenotype shows intermittent severe‐range nights despite low average burden, while instability in severe SA increases the likelihood of underestimating severity when classification is based on a single‐night test.

Introduction

Sleep apnoea (SA) is a highly prevalent sleep disorder associated with substantial cardiovascular, metabolic, and neurocognitive morbidity, including obesity, hypertension, diabetes and cognitive impairment [1, 2]. Despite its clinical importance, diagnosis and severity classification remain largely based on a single‐night polysomnographic assessment. This cross‐sectional approach implicitly assumes that apnoea severity is stable over time—an assumption that is increasingly questioned by evidence demonstrating marked night‐to‐night variability in apnoea–hypopnoea index (AHI) or respiratory disturbance index (RDI) measurements [3].

Physiological factors such as sleep architecture, body position, alcohol intake, and nasal obstruction can vary between nights and contribute to fluctuations in AHI/RDI [3, 4, 5, 6]. A growing body of literature demonstrates that such variability can lead to clinically meaningful misclassification when diagnosis is based on a single night, particularly in individuals with borderline or moderate disease severity [3, 7]. However, most prior studies rely on short‐term repeat polysomnography or limited home sleep testing and therefore do not capture long‐term disease dynamics under real‐world conditions.

Sex/gender differences further complicate the diagnostic landscape. Women with symptoms of sleep‐disordered breathing are less likely to be diagnosed and treated for sleep apnoea than men [8]. Rather than presenting with classic symptoms such as loud snoring and excessive daytime sleepiness, women more frequently report fatigue, insomnia, mood disturbance and morning headache [8, 9, 10]. At comparable AHI values, women often experience greater symptom burden and impaired quality of life [9, 10]. From a pathophysiological perspective, women more commonly exhibit REM‐predominant or positional sleep apnoea, phenotypes that may amplify night‐to‐night variability and reduce the reliability of single‐night severity assessment [4]. Despite these recognised differences, sex/gender‐specific longitudinal patterns of sleep apnoea severity and variability remain poorly characterised.

Capturing these temporal dynamics requires repeated measurements beyond a single‐night study. Recent advances in implantable cardiac devices permit continuous, long‐term monitoring of respiratory disturbance using thoracic impedance‐based algorithms. Although not a replacement for polysomnography, these systems enable observation of sleep apnoea behaviour across hundreds of nights under unconstrained, real‐world conditions. Device‐detected RDI has shown reasonable agreement with polysomnography for identifying severe sleep apnoea, as shown in the DREAM European study [11]. Such longitudinal data provide an opportunity to move beyond static severity classification toward phenotyping approaches that incorporate temporal dynamics.

In this study, long‐term device‐detected RDI data from patients enrolled in the prospective ACaSA study were used to develop a longitudinal phenotyping framework integrating both mean disease severity and night‐to‐night instability. It was hypothesised that (1) longitudinal analysis would identify clinically distinct sleep apnoea phenotypes not captured by average severity alone, and (2) instability would represent a defining feature of sleep apnoea expression in women, contributing to under‐recognition when conventional single‐night diagnostic approaches are applied.

Methods

Study Cohort

Patients enrolled in the prospective observational ACaSA study (NCT05127720) and implanted with a Microport BOREA DR or TEO DR pacemaker formed the analysis cohort. Participants were recruited at the Medical University Innsbruck after informed consent, and the protocol was approved by the local Ethics Committee (EK No.: 1322/2020). We included patients with at least 180 valid nightly RDI measurements after a 30‐day post‐implant stabilisation period. For each patient, the first 180 valid nights following this stabilisation period were included in the analysis (Figure 1).

Consort diagram.

Data Collection

Device‐detected sleep apnoea (DDSA) was defined by the device‐measured RDI. The respiratory disturbance index (RDI) was measured from midnight to 5 o'clock in the morning, using thoracic impedance monitoring the patient's respiratory effort. The sleep apnoea algorithm in Microport pacemakers counts an apnoea event in the absence of a significant respiratory cycle for > 10 s and a hypopnoea event upon a sustained (> 10 s) reduction of the respiratory amplitude by at least 50% compared to the mean minute ventilation of preceding validated respiratory cycles. The RDI correlates with the apnoea‐hypopnoea index (AHI) measured by polysomnography; an optimal cutoff value for detecting severe SA was found to be RDI > 20/h in the DREAM study [11]. Additionally, the measurements are labelled as invalid by the pacemaker software and not included in the analysis if there are more than 400 unstable or noisy signals per hour (due to movements while sleeping or active periods of the patients).

Because the device algorithm automatically invalidates and excludes nights with excessive signal noise (e.g., due to patient movement), the 180 valid nights were not strictly consecutive. In our cohort, the median chronological time required to accumulate 180 valid RDI measurements was 201 days (interquartile range: 189–224 days).

Baseline clinical data included demographics, comorbidities, medication use, laboratory parameters (including NT‐proBNP), echocardiographic measurements, and pulmonary function testing collected at study enrollment.

Demographic data, including sex/gender, were retrospectively extracted from routine administrative electronic health records collected at the time of study inclusion. Operationally, this variable was recorded strictly as a binary administrative category (female/male).

Longitudinal Feature Engineering

Two complementary dimensions of sleep apnoea behaviour were quantified over the 180‐night window:

Severity (Mean RDI): calculated as the mean of nightly RDI values for each patient.

Instability (Instability Index): night‐to‐night variability was initially quantified as the standard deviation (SD) of nightly RDI values. However, SD alone was not used as the final instability metric because patients with higher mean RDI inherently exhibit higher absolute variability (Spearman's ρ = 0.86, p < 0.001) even without disproportionate night‐to‐night instability. The coefficient of variation (SD/mean) was also not used because in our cohort the relationship between mean RDI and SD was nonlinear; dividing by the mean would therefore have disproportionately inflated variability estimates in patients with lower mean RDI values.

Because variability scaled nonlinearly with average severity, we modelled the relationship between SD and mean RDI using log–log regression to linearise the data and stabilise the variance. The appropriateness of this transformation was formally assessed: homoscedasticity was confirmed using the Breusch–Pagan test (p = 0.875), demonstrating that the transformation successfully decoupled variance from mean severity, while visual inspection of residual plots confirmed a linear overall fit (R 2 ≈0.80). The Instability Index was defined as the residual from this regression (Figure 2), representing whether a patient's variability was greater or lower than expected based on average severity. Phenotype Classification

Patients were classified into longitudinal sleep apnoea phenotypes using a two‐dimensional framework incorporating average disease severity and night‐to‐night instability, both derived from the 180‐night observation window. Severity was defined by mean nightly RDI and dichotomised at 20 events/h (mild: < 20; severe: ≥ 20), consistent with prior validation of device‐detected RDI for identifying severe sleep apnoea (9). Instability was defined by the Instability Index (residual from the cohort‐level log–log regression of SD on mean RDI) and dichotomised at the cohort median to yield balanced groups. Combining severity and instability created four phenotypes: Mild‐Stable, Mild‐Unstable, Severe‐Stable, and Severe‐Unstable.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics were compared using Fisher's exact test for categorical variables and Wilcoxon rank‐sum tests for continuous variables. Primary comparisons were performed within severity strata (Stable vs. Unstable within the Mild group; Stable vs. Unstable within the Severe group).

To evaluate whether the sex/gender‐association with instability persisted after accounting for potential confounding, we conducted a multivariable logistic regression restricted to patients with mild disease (Mean RDI < 20 events/h). The dependent variable was Unstable phenotype membership (Mild‐Unstable vs. Mild‐Stable). Covariates were selected a priori based on biological plausibility and clinical relevance: sex, age, obesity (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2), heart failure, arterial hypertension and diabetes. Results are reported as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI).

Sensitivity analyses repeated phenotyping across observation windows ranging from 90 to 365 nights to evaluate robustness. To evaluate potential confounding by atrial fibrillation on respiratory sensing, sensitivity analyses were repeated restricting the dataset to nights with 0% device‐detected AF burden and excluding patients with single chamber (VVI) devices lacking atrial sensing.

To validate the Instability Index, sensitivity analyses were performed treating instability as a continuous variable (per standard deviation increase) and categorising patients by quartiles rather than the median. The appropriateness of the log–log transformation was assessed using residual plots and the Breusch–Pagan test.

Analyses were performed using R version 4.5.1. A p < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Cohort Characteristics

Of 283 enrolled patients, a total of 207 patients met the inclusion criteria for the analysis (Figure 1). The median age was 76 years, and 37.7% were women. Across individuals, mean RDI and RDI SD showed a nonlinear relationship (Figure 2). Longitudinal Phenotypes Defined by Severity and Instability

Using the combined framework of Mean RDI and Instability Index, four longitudinal phenotypes were identified (Figure 3A): Mild‐Stable (n = 68), Mild‐Unstable (n = 67), Severe‐Stable (n = 35), and Severe‐Unstable (n = 37). Notably, patients with similar mean RDI values frequently exhibited distinct longitudinal patterns, indicating that average severity alone did not capture disease dynamics. These phenotype‐specific temporal patterns are illustrated in representative patient heatmaps (Figure 4), which highlight the stable low‐burden profile of Mild‐Stable, the intermittent ‘speckled’ pattern of Mild‐Unstable, the persistently high‐burden profile of Severe‐Stable, and the fluctuating high‐to‐mild pattern of Severe‐Unstable.

Sex/Gender‐Specific Distribution of Phenotypes

Women were significantly overrepresented in the Mild‐Unstable phenotype. They comprised 61.2% of Mild‐Unstable patients compared with 33.8% of Mild‐Stable patients (p = 0.002; Table 2, Figure 3B). In contrast, severe phenotypes were predominantly male, and sex/gender distribution did not differ significantly between Severe‐Stable and Severe‐Unstable groups (p = 0.768). Baseline medication use was comparable across these four distinct groups (Table S3). Sensitivity analyses demonstrated that the association between female sex and instability was consistent across observation windows exceeding 90 nights (Table S1).

Multivariable Predictors of Instability in Mild Disease

To assess whether the observed sex/gender‐associated instability could be explained by differences in age, adiposity, or comorbidities, we performed a multivariable logistic regression restricted to patients with mild mean severity (Mean RDI < 20 events/h; n = 135). In this expanded model, female sex/gender remained a strong independent predictor of being classified as Mild‐Unstable (adjusted OR 3.38, 95% CI 1.60–7.40; p = 0.002).

We also identified obesity (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2) as a second, independent driver of instability (adjusted OR 3.80, 95% CI 1.31–12.53; p = 0.019). Age retained a borderline association (OR 1.27 per 5 years; p = 0.049). Importantly, other cardiovascular comorbidities including heart failure, arterial hypertension and diabetes were not significantly associated with the unstable phenotype (Table 3). Conversely, in the severe disease cohort (n = 72), neither female sex (p = 0.701) nor obesity (p = 0.982) predicted instability.

Single‐Night Classification Vulnerability Driven by Instability

Instability was associated with marked vulnerability to single‐night severity misclassification (Table 4). In mild disease, Mild‐Unstable patients spent a significantly greater proportion of nights in the severe range (7.8% vs. 2.2% in Mild‐Stable; p < 0.001), indicating that a low mean RDI concealed frequent severe‐range excursions. In severe disease, instability was associated with frequent nights below the severe threshold: Severe‐Unstable patients recorded RDI < 20 events/h on 33.9% of nights versus 13.9% in Severe‐Stable patients (p < 0.001), indicating increased susceptibility to under‐classification when severity is inferred from a single‐night assessment.

Validation of the Instability Index

To ensure the findings were not artefacts of dichotomisation, the core relationships were re‐evaluated treating the Instability Index as a continuous variable (Z‐score). In a continuous multivariable linear regression model restricted to the mild cohort, female sex remained a robust, independent predictor of higher instability (β = 0.43 SD units, 95% CI [0.10–0.77]; p = 0.012), after adjusting for age, obesity, and above‐mentioned comorbidities. Obesity demonstrated a positive trend with continuous instability (β = 0.38 SD units; p = 0.089). Furthermore, the proportion of women showed a significant increasing trend across quartiles of instability (p‐trend = 0.008), rising from 32.4% in the lowest quartile to 54.5% and 69.7% in the upper quartiles.

The Instability Index also predicted night‐to‐night threshold crossings: in mild disease, every 1‐SD increase in instability was associated with 1.31‐fold higher odds of a night exceeding the severe threshold (OR 1.31, 95% CI 1.24–1.39; p < 0.001), independent of the mean RDI.

Sensitivity Analysis

To confirm that the observed instability was not driven by atrial fibrillation or artefacts, we performed a sensitivity analysis restricted to confirmed sinus rhythm (nights with 0% AF burden, excluding patients with single chamber [VVI] devices). The association between female sex and the Mild‐Unstable phenotype remained robust and statistically significant across all observation windows of 90 days or longer (Table S2).

Discussion

In this longitudinal analysis of device‐detected sleep apnoea, distinct phenotypes emerged when disease severity was characterised jointly by mean burden and instability. This framework identifies clinically important heterogeneity that is not apparent when sleep apnoea is summarised by a single‐night AHI/RDI or by average severity alone. The results extend prior observations of night‐to‐night variability by demonstrating that instability forms a reproducible dimension that stratifies patients into phenotypes with markedly different vulnerability to single‐night severity misclassification [3].

A central finding of this study is the identification of a distinct Mild‐Unstable phenotype, which multivariable analysis revealed is driven by two powerful, independent pathways: female sex/gender and obesity. Women were significantly overrepresented in this volatile phenotype, and female sex/gender remained a robust predictor (adjusted OR 3.38) even after adjusting for obesity and comorbidities. Simultaneously, obesity emerged as a distinct, independent driver of instability (adjusted OR 3.80). While obesity likely contributes to variability via anatomical mechanisms—such as positional dependence—the persistence of the female signal implies distinct non‐anatomical mechanisms. Recent data further suggest that women may be more susceptible to environmental influences on sleep‐disordered breathing, which could also contribute to the greater night‐to‐night variability observed in women [10]. Because our study lacks hormonal assays, sleep‐stage scoring, and body position data, the physiological drivers of this female‐predominant instability remain speculative. However, we hypothesise that in our predominantly elderly cohort, postmenopausal hormonal changes may play a role [11]. The withdrawal of progesterone (a respiratory stimulant) and altered sleep architecture (lowered arousal threshold) could theoretically generate a ‘physiological instability’. This hypothesised mechanism would result in an intermittent disease pattern consistent with known sex‐linked phenotypes, such as REM‐predominant or positional disease [4, 11], and mirrors the atypical symptom profiles reported by women that frequently contribute to clinical under‐recognition [8, 9, 11]. Further research incorporating full polysomnography is required to test these hypotheses.

The clinical relevance of instability is underscored by the magnitude of diagnostic volatility. Within mild disease, instability identified individuals who spent substantially more nights above the severe threshold despite low mean RDI. This ‘hidden severity’ provides a plausible mechanism by which a single‐night diagnostic assessment can underestimate true disease burden in intermittently severe patients, a limitation previously highlighted in studies of night‐to‐night variability [3]. In severe disease, the converse pattern was observed: Severe‐Unstable patients fell below the severe threshold on approximately one‐third of nights, indicating substantial vulnerability to under‐classification if severity were inferred from a single‐night assessment performed on a low‐burden night. Together, these findings indicate that night‐to‐night variability, as captured by the Instability Index, is not simply measurement noise but a clinically meaningful trait with direct implications for diagnostic reliability.

These results suggest that reliance on a single‐night assessment may be least reliable when sleep apnoea burden is intermittent. Multi‐night evaluation (repeat testing or multi‐night home sleep apnoea testing) may be most informative for symptomatic women with borderline/mild indices, suspected REM/positional disease, or discordant clinical suspicion and test results [3]. Incorporating instability metrics alongside mean severity could improve interpretation of sleep apnoea testing and reduce under‐recognition in phenotypes characterised by night‐to‐night fluctuation.

While our primary analysis utilised a median split for clarity, the relationship between instability and sex was robust to continuous modelling and quartile‐based sensitivity analyses. This suggests that the ‘unstable’ phenotype represents a biological continuum rather than a discrete category created by arbitrary cut‐points.

Several limitations should be considered. First, device‐derived RDI is a validated surrogate but has several technical limitations compared to polysomnography [12]. The thoracic impedance algorithm cannot reliably differentiate between obstructive and central sleep apnoea. Furthermore, because the device utilises a fixed time window (midnight to 05:00) rather than true sleep staging, periods of wakefulness occurring within this window may be misclassified as sleep, potentially underestimating the true RDI. While the algorithm demonstrates high sensitivity (89%) and specificity (85%) for severe disease, accuracy may be lower in patients with borderline or mild sleep apnoea. It also does not provide arousal scoring or desaturation metrics. Second, information on sleep apnoea treatment and nightly adherence (e.g., positive airway pressure) was not available and may influence night‐to‐night burden. Third, the cohort consisted entirely of elderly pacemaker recipients (median age 76 years) with high rates of cardiovascular comorbidity. This demographic differs substantially from the broader, generally younger sleep apnoea population evaluated in primary care or sleep clinics. Consequently, our conclusions regarding sex‐specific phenotypes and instability must be interpreted with caution, and extrapolation to younger or less comorbid populations requires confirmation in future studies. Fourth, residual confounding cannot be excluded, and future work integrating sleep‐stage information, body position, and concurrent polysomnography will be important to clarify physiological drivers of instability. The persistence of the sex–instability association in analyses restricted to sinus‐rhythm nights argues against atrial fibrillation–related sensing artefact as the primary explanation for the observed phenotype distribution. The sinus‐rhythm‐restricted analyses also reduce concern that irregular rhythm or mode‐specific sensing properties drive the observed mean–variance relationship. Together, these sensitivity analyses support that instability reflects a patient‐level feature rather than a device‐ or rhythm‐dependent artefact. Finally, regarding the operational definition of our demographic variables, sex/gender was acquired retrospectively as a binary administrative variable from electronic health records. Consequently, a strict retrospective differentiation between biological sex at birth and self‐identified gender cannot be determined. While we utilise the composite term ‘sex/gender’ to reflect the entanglement of biological and sociocultural dimensions driving sleep apnoea phenotypes [13], the inability to distinguish these factors in the raw operational data remains a limitation.

In summary, longitudinal profiling based on mean severity and instability identifies sleep apnoea phenotypes with strong sex/gender differences. A female and obesity‐predominant Mild‐Unstable phenotype is characterised by frequent severe‐night excursions despite low average burden, while instability in severe disease increases vulnerability to under‐classification when severity is inferred from single‐night severity assessment. These findings support consideration of longitudinal assessment strategies for sleep apnoea, particularly in patient groups where intermittency may undermine cross‐sectional diagnostic approaches.

Conclusion

Night‐to‐night variability in SA identifies a female‐ and obesity‐predominant Mild‐Unstable phenotype characterised by substantial hidden disease burden. Night‐to‐night variability in severe SA also poses a diagnostic challenge by increasing susceptibility to under‐classification. Recognising these specific volatility patterns could be critical for moving beyond single‐night diagnostics and ensuring equitable care for patients with intermittent, yet clinically significant, sleep apnoea.
