Geometric aspects of rank-3 vector bundles over surfaces and 2-plane distributions on 5-manifolds
Brandon P. Ashley, Michael T. Schultz

Abstract
. We study geometric aspects of horizontal 2-plane distributions on the 5-dimensional total space of a rank-3 vector bundle equipped with connection over a surface. We show that any surface in 3-dimensional projective space can be associated to such a geometric structure in 5-dimensions, and establish a dictionary between the projective differential geometry of the surface and the growth vector of the 2-plane distribution.

35A30, 53A20, 53B15

1.
Introduction

In differential geometry, the role of a connection on the tangent bundle of a manifold is of central importance to the intrinsic geometry of the space. Gauge theoretic considerations asks the question of how more general geometric structures may be associated to a given manifold. One interaction between these two areas can be seen in the induced extrinsic geometry obtained by immersing the manifold into an ambient space and restricting the ambient geometry onto the image.

The classical differential geometry of surfaces in
3
-dimensional euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
illustrates this interaction. Let
\Sigma
be a smooth surface and suppose that
\psi:\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
is an immersion. Then there is a natural orthogonal splitting of the rank-3 vector bundle
B=\psi^{*}T\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\Sigma
as
B=T\Sigma\oplus N_{\Sigma}
induced by the first fundamental form of
\Sigma
imparted by the immersion
\psi
. Here
N_{\Sigma}
is the normal bundle of
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
, which we identify with its image
\psi(\Sigma)
by working locally, and may regard
N_{\Sigma}\to\Sigma
and
B\to\Sigma
as being a trivial vector bundles. The fibres of
B
carry the obvious
\mathrm{GL}(3)
representation, and we may view
B
as being an associated bundle of a left principal
\mathrm{GL}(3)
-bundle over
\Sigma
.

The first fundamental form induces a canonical symmetric metric connection on
T\Sigma
, the Levi-Civita connection, and a the normal bundle
N_{\Sigma}
inherits a connection determined by the second fundamental form. These two connections fit together to form a
\mathrm{GL}(3)

\nabla

be local coordinates on
\Sigma

(1.1)

(x,y)\mapsto z(x,y)=\left(z_{1}(x,y),z_{2}(x,y),z_{3}(x,y)\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\,.

Then

(1.2)

\displaystyle z_{xx}=\Gamma^{1}_{11}z_{x}+\Gamma^{2}_{11}z_{y}+l\sigma\,,

\displaystyle z_{xy}=\Gamma^{1}_{12}z_{x}+\Gamma^{2}_{12}z_{y}+m\sigma\,

\displaystyle z_{yy}=\Gamma^{1}_{22}z_{x}+\Gamma^{2}_{22}z_{y}+n\sigma\,,

where
\Gamma^{i}_{jk}
are the Christoffel symbols defining the Levi-Civita connection, and
l,m,n
are the coefficients of the second fundamental form. Equation (
1.2

\mathfrak{gl}(3)

(1.3)

\omega=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&dx&dy\\
l\,dx+m\,dy&\Gamma^{1}_{11}\,dx+\Gamma^{1}_{12}\,dy&\Gamma^{2}_{11}\,dx+\Gamma^{2}_{12}\,dy\\

This connection naturally contains information on the geometry of

\displaystyle n_{x}-m_{y}=l\Gamma^{1}_{22}+m(\Gamma^{2}_{22}-\Gamma^{1}_{12})-n\Gamma^{2}_{12}

\displaystyle=d\sigma-pdx-qdy\,,

\displaystyle\omega_{x}

\displaystyle=dp-(l\sigma+p\Gamma^{1}_{11}+q\Gamma^{2}_{11})dx-(m\sigma+p\Gamma^{1}_{12}+q\Gamma^{2}_{12})dy\,,

In this article we initiate a study of relationships between two subjects shaped by the work of E. Cartan: on the one hand, his work
[
7
]
from 1920 on the differential geometry of surfaces in 3-dimensional projective space - in particular, those that admit projective deformations; on the other hand, his work
[
6
]
from 1910 on 2-plane distributions in 5-dimensions. To set the stage for our work, we utilize in §
2
the well known horizontal and vertical distributions on the total space of a vector bundle with connection over a manifold. In the case of a rank-3 vector bundle with connection over a surface, we obtain in Lemma
2.2
this horizontal distribution as a 2-plane distribution on the 5-dimensional total space of the bundle. Although elementary, these horizontal distributions appear to have not been widely studied in the relevant literature on 2-plane distributions in 5-dimensions. We prove an easy result (
2.3
) characterizing the growth vector of such distributions in terms of the curvature of the connection and its covariant derivatives that is useful in our work on projective surfaces.

The connection between these two areas comes through local study of an overdetermined rank-4 linear system of PDEs two variables, known to many of the old masters at the turn of the 20th century (Equations (
3.16
,
3.25

\Sigma\subset\mathbb{P}^{3}
, the differential invariants of
\Sigma
that are discussed in detail in §
3

\bar{\Delta}
on a 5-manifold that is the total space of a rank-3 vector bundle equipped with connection over
\Sigma
. The mechanism by which we obtain this geometric data from the rank-4 linear system is a non-traditional form of Cauchy reduction that we term
derived reduction
(Lemma
4.5
). This process may be of use in studying other linear systems of PDE, so we take care to develop this idea in §
4

\mathbb{P}^{3}
is classical, our insights here appear to be new, and are provided by analyzing the linear systems of PDE from the perspective of exterior differential systems. As an application, one of our main results is Theorem
5.4
, in which we establish a dictionary between the growth vector of the derived flag of the 2-plane distribution

and the projective differential invariants of
\Sigma
. The dictionary roughly resembles the rough classification of projective surfaces into quadrics, ruled surfaces, and general surfaces. One interpretation of these results can be seen as a consequence of the classical Ambrose-Singer theorem, applied to the connection. We demonstrate the utility of our perspectives by using this correspondence to produce an interesting family of projective surfaces.

Part of the novelty of the work in this article is that we study 2-plane distributions on 5-manifolds that are explicitly dependent on the
extrinsic
geometry of a surface, as opposed to much of the existing work in the literature, e.g., in the work referenced above on surfaces rolling without twisting or slipping. Moreover, in most work, it is required that the generic 2-plane distribution be bracket generating, and hence has growth vector
(2,3,5)
. Here, we demonstrate that the geometry of horizontal 2-plane distributions are connected to interesting and somewhat subtle geometry connected to projective surfaces even when they
fail
to be bracket generating.

2.
Rank-3 bundles over a surface

Let
\pi:B\to\Sigma
be a smooth rank-3 vector bundle over a surface
\Sigma
. Assume that
B
is an associated bundle of a smooth, left principal
\mathrm{G}
-bundle over
\Sigma
for some Lie group
\mathrm{G}
. Write
\mathfrak{g}
for the Lie algebra of
\mathrm{G}
. Assume further that
B
is endowed with a
\mathrm{G}
-connection
\nabla
. Working locally, let
\omega=(\omega^{\alpha}_{\beta})\in\mathrm{\underline{End}}(B)\otimes\Omega^{1}(\Sigma)
, where
\mathrm{End}(B)
has been equipped with the associated
\mathfrak{g}
-representation. Let
\mathbf{R}\in\mathrm{\underline{End}}(B)\otimes\Omega^{2}(\Sigma)
be the curvature tensor. We have then that
\mathbf{R}=d\omega-\omega\wedge\omega
, and that
g\in\mathrm{G}
acts on
\nabla
and
\mathbf{R}
by
\omega\mapsto\tilde{\omega}=g\omega g^{-1}+dg\cdot g^{-1}
, and
\mathbf{R}\mapsto\tilde{\mathbf{R}}=g\mathbf{R}g^{-1}
. Our calculations are purely local, so we may freely assume that
\Sigma
is connected and
B
is trivial. Write then that
\underline{B}=\mathrm{span}\{e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}\}
for global independent sections
e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}
of
B
.

In the context of this article, we are interested in how such extrinsic geometric data attached to
\Sigma
is encoded in the intrinsic geometry of the 5-dimensional total space
M^{5}=\mathrm{Tot}(B)
, which is endowed with the natural local coordinates
(x,y,z,p,q)
. This is most naturally encoded in the language of exterior differential systems, and their dual vector field systems (see for example
[
4
,
13
,
34
]
). We recall the following basic terminology.

A distribution
\Delta\subseteq TM
is a smooth sub-bundle of the tangent bundle of a manifold
M
. The dual object of
\Delta
is an exterior differential system (EDS)
\Omega=\mathrm{ann}(\Delta)\subseteq T^{*}M
consisting of the 1-forms that annihilate all the vector fields in
\Delta
, and naturally determines a differential ideal in the exterior algebra of
M
. The derived distribution of
\Delta
is by definition the distribution

\Delta^{\prime}=\Delta+[\,\Delta,\,\Delta\,]\,,

spanned by a local basis of vector fields for
\Delta
and their Lie brackets. A distribution is
integrable
if and only if
\Delta=\Delta^{\prime}
; this is the classical Frobenius theorem. Dualizing, one shows that

\Omega^{\prime}=\mathrm{ann}(\Delta^{\prime})=\{\,\theta\in\underline{T^{*}M}\,|\,d\theta\equiv 0\;\mathrm{mod}\,\Omega\,\}\,

forms the derived codistribution. By iteratively computing derived distributions, or their duals in the manner described above, one constructs the increasing filtration of distributions, called the derived flag of
\Delta
,

(2.1)

\Delta=\Delta^{(0)}\subseteq\Delta^{(1)}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq\Delta^{(\infty)}\subseteq TM,

where
\Delta^{(1)}=\Delta^{\prime}
,
\Delta^{(k+1)}=(\Delta^{(k)})^{\prime}
, and
\Delta^{(\infty)}=\Delta^{(\ell)}
where
\Delta^{(\ell)}=(\Delta^{(\ell)})^{\prime}
. The vector

(2.2)

\left(\mathrm{rank}\,\Delta,\,\mathrm{rank}\,\Delta^{\prime},\,\ldots,\,\mathrm{rank}\,\Delta^{(\infty)}\right)

is called the
growth vector
of
\Delta
, which is a diffeomorphism invariant of
\Delta\subseteq TM
. A distribution is said to be
\ell
-step bracket generating if
\Delta^{(\ell)}=TM
and
\Delta^{(\ell-1)}\neq TM
. Of course, there is a corresponding decreasing filtration of
T^{*}M
by iteratively derived codistributions, whose growth vector is the vector of coranks.

2.1.
Geometry of horizontal distributions

The discussion in §
1
clearly generalizes to the situation at hand, in that the pullback of the connection forms determine an EDS on
M^{5}
whose derived flag is determined by the curvature
\mathbf{R}
of
\nabla
and its covariant derivative
\nabla\mathbf{R}
. We state here the general story. In what follows, saying that “
B
is a rank-
k

\mathrm{G}
-bundle” means that
B
is a vector bundle of rank-
k
whose structure group is
\mathrm{G}\subseteq GL(k)
; hence the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}\subseteq\mathfrak{gl}(k)
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\pi:B\to\Sigma
be a trivial rank-
k

\mathrm{G}
-bundle over an
n
-manifold
\Sigma
, equipped with a compatible linear connection
\nabla
. Write
B=\mathrm{span}\{e_{\beta}\}
for global independent sections
e_{\beta}
,
\beta=1,\dots,k
. In local coordinates
(x^{1},\dots,x^{n})
on
\Sigma
, write the connection 1-forms
\omega=(\omega^{\alpha}_{\beta})\in\mathfrak{g}\otimes\Omega^{1}(\Sigma)
as
\omega^{\alpha}_{\beta}=\Gamma^{\alpha}_{i\beta}dx^{i}
. Let
M=\mathrm{Tot}(B)
be the
(n+k)
-dimensional total space of
B
, with local coordinates
(x^{i},e_{\beta})
. Then the EDS
\Omega:=\langle\pi^{*}\omega\rangle_{\mathrm{diff}}
generated by the pullback of the connection forms is a rank-
k
Pfaffian system generated by the 1-forms

(2.3)

\omega_{\beta}:=de_{\beta}-e_{\alpha}\Gamma^{\alpha}_{i\beta}dx^{i}\,,\quad\beta=1,\dots,k\,

which are semibasic with respect to
\pi
. Moreover,
\Omega=\mathrm{ann}(\Delta)
is the annihilator of an
n
-plane distribution
\Delta\subset TM
, naturally realized as the horizontal distribution

\nabla

(2.4)

X_{i}=\partial_{x^{i}}+\sum_{\beta=1}^{k}e_{\alpha}\Gamma^{\alpha}_{i\beta}\partial_{e_{\beta}}\,,\quad i=1,\dots,n\,.

The gauge group
\mathrm{G}
, which acts as usual via gauge transformation on the connection as
\omega\mapsto\tilde{\omega}=g\omega g^{-1}+dg\cdot g^{-1}
, acts from the left on
\Omega
by
g\cdot\omega_{\beta}=(g^{-1})^{*}\omega_{\beta}
.

Proof.

This is standard, and is essentially just a rephrasing in the language of exterior differential systems of what it means to pullback the tangent bundle
T\Sigma
to the total space
M=\mathrm{Tot}(B)
. The statement about the left
\mathrm{G}

\Omega
follows from the induced left, linear
\mathrm{G}
-action on

\mathrm{G}
-action on the horizontal distribution
\Delta
.
∎

Applying this well known situation to the case of a rank-3 vector bundle over a surface, we naturally obtain a horizontal 2-plane distribution on a 5-manifold.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\pi:B\to\Sigma
be a trivial rank-3
\mathrm{G}
-bundle over a surface
\Sigma
, equipped with a compatible linear connection
\nabla
. Then the horizontal distribution
\Delta=\pi^{*}T\Sigma\subset TM^{5}
is a 2-plane distribution on the 5-manifold
M^{5}=\mathrm{Tot}(B)
determined by the connection
\nabla
. For a generic connection
\nabla
, the derived flag of
\Delta
is two-step bracket generating
\Delta\subset\Delta^{\prime}\subset\Delta^{(2)}=TM^{5}
, with growth vector
(2,3,5)
. The derived distributions
\Delta^{\prime}\subset\Delta^{(2)}\subseteq V
are sub-bundles of the vertical bundle
V\subset TM^{5}
, consisting of vector fields tangent to the fibres of
\pi
, and are respectively determined by the curvature
\mathbf{R}
of
\nabla
and its covariant derivatives.

Proof.

This is the conditions of Proposition
2.1
with
n=2
and
k=3
. Indeed, write let
e=(e_{1},e_{2},e_{3})
be a global frame of
B
and local coordinates
(x^{1},x^{2})
on
\Sigma
. Then the horizontal distribution
\Delta=\mathrm{span}\,\{X_{1},X_{2}\}
is given by Equation (
2.4
). Write
\mathbf{R}=(R^{\alpha}_{\beta})\in\underline{End}(B)\otimes\Omega^{2}(\Sigma)
as the matrix of curvature 2-forms, and
R^{\alpha}_{\beta;k}=(\nabla_{\partial_{x^{k}}}R)^{\alpha}_{\beta}
for
k=1,2
.
The growth vector being
(2,3,5)
for a generic connection
\nabla
follows from Cartan
[
6
]
, which is true for a generic 2-plane distribution on any 5-manifold. Then write
\Delta^{\prime}=\mathrm{span}\{X_{1},X_{2},X_{3}\}
and
\Delta^{(2)}=\mathrm{span}\{X_{1},X_{2},X_{3},X_{4},X_{5}\}
with
X_{3}=[X_{1},X_{2}]
and
X_{k+3}=[X_{k},X_{3}]
for
k=1,2
. It is basic result in the theory of connections on a fibre bundle (e.g., expressing the curvature in terms of the Frölicher-Nijenhuis bracket) that the curvature be vertical valued; we have by direct computation that

(2.5)

X_{3}=\sum_{\beta=1}^{3}e_{\alpha}R^{\alpha}_{\beta}\partial_{e_{\beta}}\,.

Then the same is true of
\nabla\mathbf{R}
, and we have

(2.6)

X_{k+3}=\sum_{\beta=1}^{3}e_{\alpha}R^{\alpha}_{\beta;k}\partial_{e_{\beta}}\quad k=1,2\,.

∎

We have the following result on the growth vector of
\Delta
, obtained by imposing restrictions on the curvature tensor.

Theorem 2.3
.

Consider the horizontal 2-plane distribution
\Delta\subset TM^{5}
as in Lemma
2.2
. Then

(i)

The horizontal distribution
\Delta=\Delta^{\prime}
is integrable if and only if the curvature
\mathbf{R}=0
of
\nabla
vanishes.

(ii)

The growth vector of
\Delta
is
(2,3)
if and only if the curvature
\mathbf{R}\neq 0
and is covariantly constant,
\nabla\mathbf{R}=0
.

(iii)

The growth vector of
\Delta
is
(2,3,4)
if and only if
\nabla\mathbf{R}\neq 0
and there exists
X\in\underline{T\Sigma}
such that
\nabla_{X}\mathbf{R}=0
.

Proof.

The first two statements follow immediately from Lemma
2.2
. For the final statement, suppose that the growth vector is
(2,3,4)
. Then the vectors
X_{4},X_{5}\in\Delta^{(2)}
are linearly dependent. Since
\Delta^{(2)}\subset V
, they must be linearly dependent over
V
. But each vector has coefficients that are linear in the fibre variables from (
2.6
), so it follows that they are linearly dependent if and only if there is a linear relation over
C^{\infty}(\Sigma)
between
R^{\alpha}_{\beta;1}
and
R^{\alpha}_{\beta;2}
for local coordinates
(x^{1},x^{2})
on
\Sigma
. By the tensorality of linear connections in tangential directions to
\Sigma
, this is equivalent to the existence of
X\in\underline{T\Sigma}
such that
\nabla_{X}\mathbf{R}=0
.
∎

It follows then that
\Delta
admits an
2
-dimensional integral manifold
\iota:\Sigma\to M^{5}
on which
\iota^{*}(dx^{1}\wedge dx^{2})\neq 0
if and only if the curvature
\mathbf{R}=0
of
\nabla
vanishes; this is just another reinterpretation of what it means to embed a manifold into the total space of a vector bundle (without connection) as the zero section. There is then the question of how the gauge group interacts with the horizontal distribution. From Proposition
2.1
, there is a right
\mathrm{G}
-action on
\Delta
, and so it is natural to consider the distribution up to gauge equivalence. We have the following

Corollary 2.4
.

The growth vector of
\Delta
is gauge invariant.

Proof.

The conditions in Lemma
2.2
and Theorem
2.3
are all preserved under the action of the gauge group
\mathrm{G}
on the bundle
B
.
∎

While there is this natural gauge symmetry, it is not in general true that the infinitesimal action of the gauge group acts as an infinitesimal symmetry of the distribution, since

2.2.
Immersed surfaces in a 3-manifold

There is another direct analogue of the euclidean story in §
1
. Let
N
be a 3-manifold, equipped with a

-structure on the tangent bundle

In the remainder of this article, we examine an important case, that of the projective 3-space

3.
Projective surfaces & rank four linear systems of PDE in the plane

In this section we review relevant features of the local projective geometry of a surface
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{P}^{3}

1

[
38

37

[

[

\Sigma

\mathbb{P}^{3}

3.1.
Moving frame method for surfaces in
\mathbb{P}^{3}

A smooth surface
\Sigma

\Sigma

\Sigma

\Sigma

\psi(\Sigma)\subset\mathbb{P}^{3}

\psi

The given immersion

\Sigma

\Sigma

3.2.
Projective differential invariants of surfaces

We would like to know the differential invariants of an immersed surface
\psi(\Sigma)\equiv\Sigma\subset\mathbb{P}^{3}
that determine the surface up to a projective motion, independent of the choice of immersion
\psi:\Sigma\to\mathbb{P}^{3}

To begin, we first note the following. By construction, there is a canonical splitting of the vector bundle

3.3.
Rank-4 linear systems in the plane

Near

be local coordinates, and let

The key insight of Wilczynksi
[

[

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\frac{\partial^{2}z}{\partial x^{2}}=l\frac{\partial^{2}z}{\partial x\partial y}+\alpha\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}+b\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}+\mu z,\\
&\frac{\partial^{2}z}{\partial y^{2}}=m\frac{\partial^{2}z}{\partial x\partial y}+c\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}+\delta\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}+\nu z,\end{aligned}

to which the component functions

are linearly independent solutions of Equation (

[

In particular, the assumption that the image of

\nu\,dy&c\,dy&\delta\,dy&dx+m\,dy\\
&*&*&*\end{array}\right)\;.

Proposition 3.3

Let

A simple computation with the canonical system yields the claim, on the other hand, every such covariant transformation

We shall henceforth assume that

If

Lemma 3.6

A system of linear PDEs of the form (

The necessity is nothing but the integrability condition

3.4.
Projective geometry associated to the vanishing of differential invariants

It follows from Proposition
3.1
that generically, a projective surface
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{P}^{3}
is determined up to automorphism by the projective metric
\varphi
and cubic form
\Phi
. As a reminder, we say that such a surface is
very general
; in this case, we have

, and
b,c
do not depend on any auxiliary parameters. It is important to note that even in this case,

do
not
always determine the data

of both the surface
and

uniquely up to projective automorphism. Inherently, the canonical system (

) is sensitive to the choice of immersion; this data is reflected in

, particularly in the complex analytic category where it is common to immerse surfaces in projective space by Fuchsian systems of PDEs. In this case, fundamental solutions of the canonical system are multivalued, and it is of great importance to understand both the local and global aspects of the associated monodromy groups and their representation on fibres of the bundle
E
. See for example Sasaki & Yoshida
[
32
]
, Hara, Sasaki, & Yoshida
[
12
]
, Matsumoto, Sasaki & Yoshida
[
19
]
, Clingher, Doran, & Malmendier, Malmendier & the second author
[
17
]
, etc.

Even in the case that one is only concerned with the geometry of the surface
\Sigma
itself, there is still the chance that one may deform the surface
\Sigma
while keeping the projective metric
\varphi
and the cubic
\Phi
the same. Such surfaces are called
projectively applicable
, and were studied for example by Cartan
[
7
]
. This geometric behavior is unique in dimension two. We will give more detail in §

.

There is a very rough stratification of projective surfaces based on the relative vanishing of the invariants

, the proofs of which and references can be found in many places. See for example
[

]
and references therein.

3.4.1.
Quadric surfaces

Quadric surfaces in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
are characterized by the vanishing of the cubic
\Phi
. In fact, a similar statement holds for hypersurfaces in
\mathbb{P}^{n+1}
; see
[
30
, Theorem 2.3]
for a simple proof. It follows that quadric surfaces in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
are the
2
-dimensional projective analogue of conformally flat spaces in higher dimension
[
16
]
. Thus
b=c=0
, and so we also have that the projective metric
\varphi=0
vanishes.

The integrability conditions (
3.28
) under the quadric condition
b=c=0
simplify greatly to
\mu_{y}=\nu_{x}=0
; thus, the canonical system decouples into a system of second order ODEs:

(3.29)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}z}{\partial x^{2}}=\mu(x)z,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}z}{\partial y^{2}}=\nu(y)z.

Then the connection form (
3.26
) decomposes by inspection into the (outer) tensor product of the connection form of the respective ODEs. Over
\mathbb{C}
, when the ODEs are Fuchsian, an analogous statement holds for the associated monodromy representations, and the classical Gauss-Schwarz theory expresses
\mu,\nu
in terms of Schwarzian derivatives. Such decouplings play an important role in applications to subtle algebro-geometric aspects of lattice polarized K3 surfaces in the references above preceding this section.

By a suitable rescaling of
z
, we may assume
\mu=\nu=0
. This simplifies the equations dramatically,
z_{xx}=0=z_{yy}
, which is trivially solved. The independent solutions
z_{0}=xy,\,z_{1}=x,\,z_{2}=y,\,z_{3}=1
satisfy the quadratic equation
z_{0}z_{3}=z_{1}z_{2}
, which defines the (unique) quadric surface in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
up to projective automorphism. We again stress that such a rescaling is particularly violent at the level of the differential systems, since for example in the complex analytic category it discards
all
local and global data about the associated (Fuchsian) system. This discussion reflects the fact that quadric surfaces in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
are doubly ruled surfaces. More generally, we have the following.

3.4.2.
Ruled Surfaces

Recall that a projective surface
\Sigma
is

if it contains a 1-parameter family of projective lines

4

4

When we say that

is

we mean that it is singly ruled, ruling out the possibility that

be a quadric.
. Ruled surfaces play a very important role in the theory of surfaces in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
via constructions known as line congruences
[
30

. In practice, such a surface can be specified by a pair of curves

in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
with common local parameter

and line joining the respective points on each curve parameterized by

\psi:\Sigma\to\mathbb{P}^{3}
is given by

(3.30)

\psi(x,y)=s(x)+yt(x)\,\in\mathbb{P}^{3}\,.

It is straightforward to show that in this situation, the parameters

give asymptotic coordinates on
\Sigma

. Clearly, one could swap the roles of

and

in the argument above and get an analogous statement. Thus, a ruled surface

has vanishing projective metric
\varphi=0
and cubic form
\Phi\neq 0
. By Proposition

The integrability conditions (
3.28
) simplify in this case to
\mu_{yy}=0
,
b_{yy}+2\mu_{y}=0
. These equations can easily be integrated to find that the canonical system of a ruled surface is of the form

(3.31)

\frac{\partial^{2}z}{\partial x^{2}}=(\alpha(x)y^{2}+\beta(x)y+\gamma(x))\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}+(-\alpha(x)y+\delta(x))z\,,\hskip 8.53581pt\frac{\partial^{2}z}{\partial y^{2}}=0\,.

Evaluating (

(3.32)

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}s}{dx^{2}}=\delta(x)u(x)+\gamma(x)v(x)\,,

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}t}{dx^{2}}=-\alpha(x)u(x)+(\beta(x)+\delta(x))v(x)\,.

Ruled surfaces have their geometry determined by the curves
s(x),t(x)
, and the dependencies in the ODEs above. Writing
S(x)=(s(x),t(x))^{T}
and

3.4.3.
Projectively applicable surfaces

Examining the integrability conditions in Lemma
3.6
reveals that
b,c
do not determine
\mu,\nu
uniquely, and so there is a chance to deform
\Sigma
without changing
\varphi
and
\Phi
. Such surfaces are called projectively applicable
[
7
]
, and play an interesting role in the geometry of projective surfaces. In this section we follow the presentation of Sasaki
[
30
, §2.6]
.

Definition 3.7
.

A morphism
\mathscr{F}:\Sigma\to\Sigma^{\prime}
of projective surfaces is said to be a
projective deformation
of
\Sigma
into
\Sigma^{\prime}
if for all
p\in\Sigma
, there exists a projective transformation
g_{p}\in\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{P}^{3})\cong\mathrm{PSL}(4)
such that
g_{p}(p)=\mathscr{F}(p)
and that for any smooth curve
C\subset\Sigma
passing through
p
, the curve
g_{p}(C)
has second order contact at
\mathscr{F}(p)\in\Sigma^{\prime}
with the image curve
\mathscr{F}(C)\subset\Sigma^{\prime}
.

Definition 3.8
.

A projective surface
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{P}^{3}
is
projectively applicable
if it possesses a nontrivial projective deformation.

The significance of projective applicability is the following

Proposition 3.9
.

Suppose that
\mathscr{F}:\Sigma\to\Sigma^{\prime}
is a projective deformation, and suppose that
(x,y)
and
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
are asymptotic local coordinates on
\Sigma
and
\Sigma^{\prime}
, respectively. Then there is a canonical splitting
\mathscr{F}^{*}T\Sigma^{\prime}=\mathscr{F}^{*}(T_{x^{\prime}}\oplus T_{y^{\prime}})=T_{x}\oplus T_{y}
, i.e., a projective deformation carries asymptotic directions to asymptotic directions. Moreover, in the respective canonical systems (
3.25
) for
\Sigma,\Sigma^{\prime}
, we have
b=b^{\prime}
and
c=c^{\prime}
relative to the respective asymptotic coordinates. Consequently,
\mathscr{F}^{*}\varphi^{\prime}=\varphi
and
\mathscr{F}^{*}\Phi^{\prime}=\Phi
. The conditions above are also sufficient for the existence of a projective deformation.

Proof.

That
\mathscr{F}
is a projective deformation immediately implies the statement about preserving asymptotic directions by the necessary second order contactedness. Then locally we may regard
(x,y)=(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
, so that
\Sigma,\Sigma^{\prime}
share a common asymptotic parameterization. It follows that up to a projective transformation, we have
z^{\prime}=fz
for some nonvanishing
f
. Proposition
3.3
shows that such a covariant transformation acts isometrically on the constant tensor
h
in (
3.19
). Then an argument similar to that result shows that
b=b^{\prime}
and
c=c^{\prime}
. Hence
\mathscr{F}^{*}\varphi^{\prime}=\varphi
and
\mathscr{F}^{*}\Phi^{\prime}=\Phi
. The sufficiency follows by reversing the argument and noting that the derivatives of the scaling factor
f
are not constrained at the point of contact.
∎

Elementary examples of projectively applicable surfaces are quadrics and ruled surfaces, due to the arbitrary functions
\mu(x),\nu(y),\gamma(x)
that appear in the
z
-coefficients of (
3.29
) and (
3.31
) respectively. These surfaces have vanishing projective metric
\varphi=0
. There are many other examples of generically nonruled projectively applicable surfaces that are derived from multiparameter families of bivariate hypergeometric functions, such as Appell’s
F_{2}
and
F_{4}
functions. See for example
[
30
, Example 2.20]
and
[
29
]
.

In this generic case
bc\neq 0
, a straightforward argument shows that the coefficients
\mu,\nu
in the canonical system (
3.25
) may depend on at most three arbitrary constants
[
30
, Equation (2.37)]
. Such a situation is very special and reflects the projective geometry of
\Sigma
as follows.

Theorem 3.10

(Proposition 2.17,
[
30
]
)
.

Let
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{P}^{3}
be a generic surface equipped with asymptotic coordinates
(x,y)
and canonical system (
3.25
) with
bc\neq 0
, admitting three independent projective deformations, that is,
\mu,\nu
depend on three arbitrary parameters. Then the Gaussian curvature of the projective metric
\varphi
is constant.

Proof.

Let
K=K(\varphi)
be the Gaussian curvature of the projective metric
\varphi
of
\Sigma
. This is easily computable as

(3.33)

K=\frac{-1}{8bc}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x\partial y}\log(bc)\,.

In this case it follows by utilizing the integrability conditions in Lemma
3.6
that the three arbitrary parameters in
\mu,\nu
lead to the following quantities being identically zero:

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x\partial y}\log\left(\frac{b}{c}\right)=0\,,\quad\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\frac{1}{bc}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x\partial y}\log(c)\right)=0\,\quad\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(\frac{1}{bc}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x\partial y}\log(b)\right)=0\,.

These three equations together imply that
K\equiv\mathrm{const}
.
∎

4.
Derived reduction of the canonical system

Using the language of exterior differential systems (again see
[
4
]
and
[
13
]
, or
[
34
]
), we may locally encode every system of PDE as an exterior differential system (EDS), or dually as a vector field distribution, as in §

. In this section, we study the canonical system in Equation (
3.25
) from the EDS perspective. While this system is naturally associated to a flat connection on a rank-4 bundle
\pi:E\to\Sigma
as in §
3.3
, we show that there is a canonical way to reduce this data to the rank-3 sub-bundle
B:=E/L^{*}
, with an induced connection
\bar{\nabla}
that both completely determines and is completely determined by the projective geometry of
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{P}^{3}
.

4.1.
A rank-3 EDS determined by a projective surface

Consider the projective surface
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{P}^{3}
, equipped with asymptotic local coordinates
(x,y)
. Let
J^{2}=J^{2}(\Sigma,\mathbb{K})
denote the space of (local) 2-jets from
\Sigma
to
\mathbb{K}
. This is a geometric structure that encodes equivalence classes of local sections
z:\Sigma\to L
up to second order derivatives, from these considerations we assume
L\cong\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}
. The bundle
J^{2}
forms a smooth 8-dimensional manifold that fibres over
\Sigma
, equipped with local coordinates
(x,y,z,z_{x},z_{y},z_{xx},z_{xy},z_{yy})
and a canonical projection
\pi_{2}:J^{2}\to\Sigma
. There is an analogous 5-dimensional first order jet space
J^{1}\subset J^{2}
, equipped with a compatible projection
\pi_{1}:J^{1}\to\Sigma
.

On
J^{2}
, we may define the contact one-forms

(4.1)

\begin{cases}\theta_{z}=dz-z_{x}\,dx-z_{y}\,dy\,,\\
\theta_{x}=dz_{x}-\,z_{xx}\,dx-z_{xy}\,dy\,,\\
\theta_{y}=dz_{y}-\,z_{xy}\,dx-z_{yy}\,dy\,.\end{cases}

These one-forms generate a differential ideal,
\mathrm{Ct}=\langle\theta_{z},\,\theta_{x},\,\theta_{y}\rangle_{\mathrm{diff}}
, called the
canonical contact system
on
J^{2}
. Since the rank-4 linear system (
3.16
), and hence the canonical system (
3.25
), are of finite type (all third order derivatives of

can be expressed in terms of
J^{2}

and the ideal
\mathrm{Ct}
.
We thus obtain from the canonical system an embedded submanifold
M^{6}=\{(x,y,z,p,q,s)\}\subset J^{2}
from the locus of the linear equations

(4.2)

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle=bp+\mu z\,,

\displaystyle t

\displaystyle=cq+\nu z\,,

where we have set
p=z_{x}
,

,

,

, and

. From this perspective it is clear that

is the total space of the rank-4 vector bundle

in (

\iota:M^{6}\to J^{2}
be the associated inclusion map. Then the restriction

; explicitly,
\Omega=\langle\omega_{z},\,\omega_{x},\,\omega_{y}\rangle_{\mathrm{diff}}

\begin{cases}\omega_{z}=dz-pdx-qdy,\\
\omega_{x}=dp-(bq+\mu z)dx-sdy,\\
\omega_{y}=dq-sdx-(cp+\nu z)dy.\end{cases}

Moreover, the differential ideal
\Omega
is nothing but the ideal generated by the first three rows of the connection

pulled back to

under the bundle projection map

\omega_{s}=ds-D_{y}(r)dx-D_{x}(t)dy\,,

Proposition 4.1
.

The rank-4 Pfaffian system

Proof.

Thus we may regard Lemma

Proposition 4.3

Let

with associated rank-3 Pfaffian system

We will see the significance of the vector field

4.2.
Derived Cauchy reduction

A vector field
V\in\underline{\Delta}
is said to be a
Cauchy characteristic
if
[V,\Delta]\subseteq\Delta
, that is, the Lie derivative of any vector field in
\Delta
with respect to
V
lies in
\Delta
. The utility of Cauchy characteristics are as follows. A distribution admitting Cauchy characteristics can always be reduced to a lower rank distribution on a lower-dimensional space, the latter given by the level-sets of the associated invariant functions
[
34
, §3.4]
. A direct computation shows the following

Lemma 4.4
.

The 3-plane distribution
\Delta
admits no Cauchy characteristics. The vector field
Z
is the only Cauchy characteristic of the derived distribution
\Delta^{\prime}
. The same is true for all gauge equivalent 3-plane distributions under the
\mathrm{P}
-action on
\Delta
.

Although the distribution (
4.6
) admits no Cauchy characteristics, and therefore
\Delta
cannot be reduced in this way, we may still utilize the Cauchy characteristic of
\Delta^{\prime}
to reduce the 3-plane distribution
\Delta
as follows.

Let
M_{s_{0}}^{5}=\{(x,y,z,p,q,s=s_{0})\}\subset M^{6}
be the manifold defined by the level sets of the Cauchy characteristic vector field
Z=\partial_{s}
of the first derived
\Delta^{\prime}
, parameterized by constant local sections
s=s_{0}
of the (trivialized) normal bundle
\underline{L}^{*}\cong\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}
. For each such
s_{0}
, we may define the vector fields

(4.7)

\displaystyle X_{s_{0}}

\displaystyle=\partial_{x}+p\partial_{z}+(bq+\mu z)\partial_{p}+s_{0}\partial_{q},

\displaystyle Y_{s_{0}}

\displaystyle=\partial_{y}+q\partial_{z}+s_{0}\partial_{p}+(cp+\nu z)\partial_{q},

on
M^{6}
. The derived Cauchy characteristic
Z
is transverse to the submanifold
M^{5}_{s_{0}}
. These vector fields generate a one-parameter family of 2-plane distributions

for all

For all constant local sections

without imposing additional constraints on the geometry of

Proof.

From Lemma

(3,4,6)

5.
Horizontal 2-plane distributions from projective surfaces

Having reduced the problem studying the local geometry of a projective surface to a suitable
\mathrm{G}
-connection on a rank-3 bundle over the surface, we study here the confluence of results from §
2
and §
4
for projective surfaces
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{P}^{3}
, independent of the data of any immersion. We again emphasize that the integrability condition
\mathbf{R}=0
of the connection
\nabla
on the rank-4 bundle
E\to\Sigma
is satisfied for
any
surface in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
per Lemma
3.6

\bar{\nabla}
on the rank-3 bundle
B\to\Sigma
detects more refined aspects of the projective geometry of
\Sigma
.

An elementary computation reveals that the curvature tensor
\bar{\mathbf{R}}=d\bar{\omega}-\bar{\omega}\wedge\bar{\omega}
of
\bar{\nabla}
is given by

(5.1)

\bar{\mathbf{R}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&-(b\nu+\mu_{y})dx\wedge dy&(c\mu+\nu_{x})dx\wedge dy\\
0&-bc\,dx\wedge dy&(c_{x}+\nu)dx\wedge dy\\
0&-(b_{y}+\mu)dx\wedge dy&bc\,dx\wedge dy\\
\end{array}\right)\,.

Our first utility of the curvature
\bar{\mathbf{R}}
of
\bar{\nabla}
is the following observation. Upon inspection one sees that the last row of the connection form
\omega
for
\nabla
on the rank-4 bundle
E\to\Sigma
, encoded by the 1-form
\omega_{s}
in (
4.4
) and whose coefficients are expressed in terms of total derivatives, is simply obtained from suitable contractions of the components of
\bar{\mathbf{R}}
with the coordinate tangent vectors
\partial_{x},\partial_{y}
. In fact, we have

Theorem 5.1
.

The integrability conditions
\mathbf{R}=0
of the canonical system (
3.25
) are a consequence of the differential Bianchi identity
d_{\bar{\nabla}}\bar{\mathbf{R}}=0
.

Proof.

A direct computation of the necessary
d_{\bar{\nabla}}\bar{\mathbf{R}}=0
recovers the integrability equations in Lemma
3.6
.
∎

We now study the associated projective geometry of
\Sigma
according to the curvature
\bar{\mathbf{R}}
and covariant derivatives with respect to
\bar{\nabla}
, in the spirit of §
2.1
.

Lemma 5.2
.

The curvature tensor
\bar{\mathbf{R}}=0
if and only if the surface is a ruled surface whose ruling curve lies in the intersection of two linear complexes. In particular,
\bar{\mathbf{R}}=0
for a quadric surface.

Proof.

It is clear from (
5.1
) that if
\bar{\mathbf{R}}=0
, we must have
bc=0
; hence the projective metric
\varphi=0
vanishes and so
\Sigma
is ruled. For a quadric, we have
b=0=c
and
\mu=0=\nu
, so that
\bar{\mathbf{R}}=0
. Assume without loss of generality that
c=0
and
b\neq 0
. As in §
3.4.2
, we may further assume that
\nu=0
. Then the resulting equations from
\bar{\mathbf{R}}=0
are
\mu_{y}=0
and
b_{y}+\mu=0
, which are trivially solved as

(5.2)

b=\beta(x)y+\gamma(x)\,,\quad\mu=-\beta(x)\,.

Hence as in Equation (
3.31
), the canonical system of such a surface is given by

(5.3)

\frac{\partial^{2}z}{\partial x^{2}}=(\beta(x)y+\gamma(x))\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}-\beta(x)z\,,\hskip 8.53581pt\frac{\partial^{2}z}{\partial y^{2}}=0\,,

and the ruling curves
s(x),t(x)
from
\psi(x,y)=s(x)+yt(x)
satisfy (
3.32
), which here become

(5.4)

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}s}{dx^{2}}=-\beta(x)s(x)+\gamma(x)t(x)\,,

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}t}{dx^{2}}=0\,.

In fact, due to the relevant transformation properties of (
3.32
) (see Sasaki
[
30
, Theorem 1.8]
), we may consider
\beta=0
. Moreover, we have
\gamma=0
if and only if
\Sigma
is a quadric. Such doubly ruled surfaces lie in the intersection of two linear complexes. In case
\gamma\neq 0
, the curve
s(x)
can be shown to satisfy a
reducible
fourth order ODE with vanishing first Wilczynski invariant
[
30
, §1.1]
, and hence lying in the intersection of two linear complexes.
∎

Lemma 5.3
.

If
\Sigma
is a general ruled surface as in §
3.4.2
, then
\bar{\mathbf{R}}\neq 0
, and
\bar{\nabla}_{\partial_{y}}\bar{\mathbf{R}}=0
, where

Proof.

It follows from Equations (
3.31
) and (
5.1
) that for a general ruled surface,
\bar{\mathbf{R}}\neq 0
. Then a direct computation shows that
\bar{\nabla}_{\partial_{y}}\bar{\mathbf{R}}=0
.
∎

We may now characterize the horizontal 2-plane distribution

on

in terms of the projective geometry of

determined by the projective metric

and the cubic form

.

.

Let

be the horizontal 2-plane distribution on

of the connection

(

Each point follows from results in this section and §

.

(

)

This follows from Theorem

(

) and Lemma

.

(

)

A direct computation shows

(

)

(

(

)

This follows from Lemma

∎
