Between Dependence and Alienation: Understanding the Digital Minimalist Behavior of Chinese Millennials—An Analysis Based on SEM and fsQCA
Authors: Chao Zhang, Yinze Hao, Jingwen Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionFollowing rapid iterations in media technologies, Chinese millennials (born between 1980 and 1995) find themselves in a paradoxical state where digital saturation exists alongside fatigue, making them central practitioners of digital minimalist behavior.MethodsThis study, rooted in psychological reactance theory and the Technology Acceptance Model, employs a mixed‐method design that integrates SEM and fsQCA to examine the determinants of digital minimalist behavior among Chinese millennials. Using 560 valid responses gathered from June to August 2025, SEM analyzes the net effects, while fsQCA identifies the configurational pathways associated with high levels of behavior.ResultsThe SEM results indicate that psychological reactance‐related perceptions positively predict digital minimalist behavior, including inferred manipulative intent (β = 0.233, p < 0.001), perceived coercion (β = 0.182, p < 0.001), and perceived goal obstruction (β = 0.168, p < 0.01). Perceived Usefulness (β = −0.133, p < 0.01) and Perceived Ease of Use (β = −0.240, p < 0.001) negatively correlate with the behavior, while psychological reactance indirectly contributes to stronger digital minimalist behavior by weakening both perceptions. This indicates that when technology is perceived as intrusive, autonomy‐threatening, or disruptive to goals, Chinese millennials are more inclined to adopt selective, context‐specific minimalist adjustments, while functional dependence continues to constrain complete disconnection. The fsQCA further identifies six functionally equivalent configurational pathways, emphasizing the intricate nature of the causal conjunctions underlying this behavior.ConclusionThis study demonstrates that digital minimalist behavior among Chinese millennials is not a one‐way exit but an ongoing process of digital adjustment under media‐related pressures. It offers a context‐specific perspective for understanding their anxieties in mediated everyday life.

Introduction

In recent years, advancements in digital media technologies, including artificial intelligence (AI) and big data, have transformed media from mere information transmission into a basic infrastructure of social life (Sun 2020). However, the coupling of intelligent algorithms with capitalist logics has caused digital alienation, drawing people into mediated predicaments of information overload and social fatigue, while exacerbating cognitive load and psychological anxiety. Digital minimalist behavior has emerged as a countervailing media practice, with Chinese millennials—shaped by distinctive formative experiences—becoming a particular practitioner group. First, this cohort grew up during a media transition, directly witnessing the shift from traditional media, such as broadcasting, to digital media, such as AI (Li 2022). The discontinuous evolution of the media environment has resulted in a distinct cohort profile regarding technology acceptance. This group exhibits significant digital adaptability; however, early socialization into traditional media habits fosters a natural caution and psychological resistance to emerging digital technologies. Second, their formative years coincided with China's economic takeoff, social transformation, and rapid urbanization (Shen 2020). They capitalized on opportunities generated by rapid growth, but also faced intense competition, high living costs, workplace strain, and uncertainty amid social change. As a cohort shaped by the one‐child policy, they bear the dual family demands of intergenerational support and child‐rearing. Simultaneously, they face escalating competition and structural involutionary pressures in the workplace. In this context, the cohort seeks to alleviate distracted attention and psychological anxiety through minimalist practices; however, the practical demands of work coordination, social connection, and daily management continue to foster a habitual dependence on digital tools. This contradictory trend of minimizing intrusions while maintaining usage reflects a typical tension in their mediated existence, positioning them as a representative case for examining the complex mechanisms of digital minimalist behavior. This study examines the correlates of digital minimalist behavior among Chinese millennials and their configurational pathways, offering key insights into how larger populations adapt to digital anxiety and manage their media use. Building on psychological reactance theory and the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), this study integrates SEM and fsQCA to identify the causal relationships and configurational pathways underlying this behavior, thereby clarifying the mechanisms through which individuals actively reconstruct human‐technology relations in an era of digital saturation.

Literature Review

Research on Minimalism in Digital Media Behavior

Media technology evolution has reconfigured modes of information transmission and reshaped social connectivity, cognitive habits, and everyday life. Digital media, characterized by immediacy, interactivity, and immersion, has become integral to social structures and influences contemporary behavior (Sun 2020). Intelligent platforms reconfigure the logics of action and social interactions using algorithms and design (Li and Yao 2023). Social media can reshape group norms and collective emotions by influencing moral evaluations and boundaries of action in public discourse (Neumann and Rhodes 2024). However, their negative effects have become increasingly apparent. Excessive connectivity correlates with fragmented attention, emotional exhaustion, and relational alienation (Whelan et al. 2020). Among adolescents, social media use correlates with diminished sleep quality (Woods and Scott 2016) and increased anxiety and depression (Khan et al. 2024). Utilizing social media to address boredom or regulate emotions may foster problematic use and adverse psychological outcomes (Stockdale and Coyne 2020). Additionally, problematic smartphone usage correlates with decreased academic engagement and poorer sleep among adolescents (Li et al. 2023). When digital technology transitions from a mere tool to a disciplining mechanism, individuals develop a need for reflection and adjustment, which leads to “counter‐connection” minimalist practices. These include leaving social platforms, creating alternative environments, and adopting technological solutionism to enhance subjective agency (Hu 2023). “Digital strikers” also engage in disconnection practices, such as switching nodes, disciplining space, and shutting down interfaces (Li and Ji 2021). Consequently, digital minimalism has attracted increasing scholarly attention as a systematic set of ideas. Digital minimalism differs from digital detox, which is a temporary strategy for disconnecting or minimizing digital media use and emphasizes short‐term recovery (Syvertsen and Enli 2020). Moreover, it is different from digital disconnection, a more neutral umbrella term that emphasizes severing specific digital connections to optimize the digital environment (Chen and Yan 2025). The related concepts include psychological detachment and boundary control. Psychological detachment emphasizes disengagement from work during nonwork time and, in digital contexts, involves maintaining distance from devices to facilitate restorative experiences (Chen and Zhang 2021). Boundary control highlights the subjective control over boundary crossings among roles, including work and family (Boccoli et al. 2024). The goal is not necessarily to reduce usage; technology may occasionally facilitate cross‐domain resource shifting. In contrast, digital minimalism is a more explicitly normative philosophy of digital living and technology utilization, anchored in the principle of “less but better.”It promotes concentrating on a small set of genuinely significant, high‐value digital activities while deliberately excluding low‐value or distracting activities, thus limiting the encroachment of digital technology on offline life and optimizing the overall structure of digital engagement (Newport 2023). Influenced by digital minimalism, scholarship has increasingly developed discussions on digital minimalist behavior. Certain studies contend that digital minimalist behavior is not a mere technological rejection but rather an alternative life practice that integrates negative freedom with active construction (Wang 2024). Others, drawing on digital rights and normative rationale, consider it an essential means for individuals to achieve digital autonomy and resist technological alienation (Wang 2024). Interpretive frameworks in international research are frequently linked to individualist traditions and stress while maintaining autonomy, focus, and digital well‐being through diminished connectivity (Aylsworth and Castro 2021). However, these effects are not uniform or linear. In certain contexts, reduced usage co‐occurs with enhanced psychological states (Schraggeová and Bisaha 2025), whereas in others, it may be accompanied by cravings, withdrawal responses, or context‐dependent rebounds (Jakab 2025). Furthermore, digital minimalist behavior frequently manifests as ongoing cleaning and deletion, demonstrating both continuity and contextuality (Vitale et al. 2018).

Sustained research has transformed scholarly definitions of “digital minimalist behavior” from simple use reduction to a multidimensional, proactive regulation. Certain scholars have defined it as a practice that individuals develop under the influence of digital minimalism (Chen and Rong 2024). Others describe minimalist behavior as a collection of strategies, including utilizing cleaning apps and information feeds, limiting time and contexts (Jin and Yang 2025), and responding to platform rules while negotiating one's subjectivity (Chen and Chen 2025). Accordingly, based on prior studies and the aims of this study, digital minimalist behavior is defined as a self‐regulatory response to perceived digital overload and boundary encroachment that aims to restore autonomy over attention, time, and goal pursuit. Guided by digital minimalism, this entails reassessing one's relationship with digital technologies by selectively minimizing non‐essential attention‐draining usage while preserving essential instrumental use.

Research on Digital Minimalist Behaviors Among Young People

In a society saturated with digital technologies, the lived condition of youth as “digital natives” has become profoundly mediated (Feng 2025). Although smart media enables learning, work, and socialization, its dominant force is increasingly evident. Constant notifications, proliferating applications, and relentless entertainment content continue to attract young people's attention. Thus, digital minimalist behavior has emerged as a key topic in scholarship, representing a way in which youth resist digital alienation.

Current research on youth digital minimalist behavior centers largely on its manifestations and underlying motives. By synthesizing existing studies, these motives can be grouped into three levels. The first category comprises psychological reactance motives, which include resistance to the psychological burdens of a technological society (Wang 2024), anxiety about digital dependence, resistance to information overload, and critiques of social alienation (Wang 2024). The second category encompasses cognitive and affective motives, such as using digital minimalist behavior to mitigate digital identity crises, alleviate the fear of missing out and withdrawal reactions (Jin and Yang 2025), and reduce negative emotions while escaping algorithmic enclosure (Bi 2024). The third category entails value‐seeking motives, whereby youths seek to rebuild boundary order and value ethics (Wang 2024), pursue value and meaning in offline life (Jin and Yang 2025), and reconstruct their subjectivity (Chen and Rong 2024). In practice, these behaviors may manifest as technology abstinence, content forgetting, self‐initiated disconnection (Wang 2024), online silence, migration between social platforms (Zhu 2025), boundary negotiation, algorithmic calibration, and attention management (Chen and Rong 2024). However, although such practices can enhance young people's sense of control, they may also engender the burdens associated with minimalism, including higher decision costs and disruptions to social continuity (Zhu 2025), potentially resulting in individuals becoming ensnared in the paradox of technological fatigue (Bi 2024). Existing research on digital minimalist behavior predominantly emphasizes one or a few motivational dimensions and remains largely descriptive. Consequently, it struggles to capture the broader complex causal network and the underlying social issues, and it seldom provides a systematic analysis of the interplay among various factors. In addition, studies frequently regard youths as a single undifferentiated group, resulting in a deficiency of systematic, specialized studies on Chinese millennials.

Therefore, by employing a nuanced approach to examine the complex motives behind simplifying digital life during a critical life stage, this study focuses on digital minimalist behavior among Chinese millennials. It offers a more precise cohort‐based perspective for comprehending the dynamic relationship between individuals and technology in the digital era. Thus, it addresses the limited systematic attention given to groups experiencing media overuse in existing research. Moreover, it integrates SEM and fsQCA to examine the correlates and configurational effects of digital minimalist behavior, using both variable‐ and case‐oriented approaches. This supports a comprehensive, dynamic, and explanatory framework across macro‐ and micro‐levels and across individual and societal dimensions.

Theoretical Background and Hypotheses

Theoretical Foundation

Psychological Reactance Theory

Psychological reactance theory was initially articulated by American psychologist Jack Brehm in his 1966 book, A Theory of Psychological Reactance. When such freedoms are threatened, people are motivated to restore them, namely, psychological reactance (Rosenberg and Siegel 2025). The theory posits three key antecedents of reactance: perceived coercion, perceived goal obstruction, and inferred manipulative intent (Du and You 2019). Building on this theory, this study extends the attention from individual factors to media influences on user behavior and examines the relationship between these three factors and digital minimalist behavior among Chinese millennials.

TAM

The TAM, proposed by Fred D. Davis in 1989, explains users' acceptance and utilization of technology. It posits that intention to use is influenced by attitude, which, in turn, is collectively determined by Perceived Usefulness and Perceived Ease of Use (Davis et al. 1989). Later, Venkatesh and colleagues proposed the extended model UTAUT2, which encompasses seven independent variables and three control variables and accounts for 70% of the variance in technology use intention (Negm 2024). This study adopts the classic TAM rather than UTAUT2 for two main reasons. First, digital minimalist behavior focuses on reflecting on the value and costs of digital tools and managing their use in a restrained manner. The TAM's usefulness and ease of use framework captures the basic cognitive evaluations of technology(Davis et al. 1989). In contrast, UTAUT2 adds factors such as hedonic motivation and habit, which better explain continued use rather than minimalist, restraint‐based decisions (Tamilmani et al. 2021). Second, Chinese millennials frequently use digital technologies at work and in daily life, rendering digital tools indispensable for their daily operations. However, high‐frequency connections can cause burdens and reflections. The TAM's usefulness and ease of use can capture, at the cognitive level, the tension between essential usage requirements and the desire to minimize intrusions, rendering it appropriate for examining correlations with digital minimalist behavior. Accordingly, this study employs TAM's Perceived Usefulness and Perceived Ease of Use as internal perception factors for Chinese millennials and examines their influence on the formation of digital minimalist behavior.

This study integrates psychological reactance theory and TAM to develop a framework for analyzing Chinese millennials' complex media technology use in high‐connectivity contexts. Psychological reactance theory provides conceptual tools to comprehend resistant and avoidant responses when autonomy is threatened, goals are obstructed, or manipulation is perceived, as well as to explain motives for reducing technology use. The TAM, via evaluations of usefulness and ease of use, elucidates how efficiency and convenience promote continued use and why people retain the necessary instrumental use. Collectively, they enable analyses at both the net effect and configurational pathway levels of whether self‐management reflects the trade‐off between reducing information intrusions and maintaining key functions, as well as how psychological and technological conditions interact with stronger media management tendencies.

Research Hypotheses

Perceived Coercion

Perceived coercion denotes the psychological experience that emerges when one's active cognition is forcibly interrupted, and reflects the cognitive process of perceiving compelled behavior (Fransen et al. 2015). When individuals perceive threats to their freedom, they experience aversion and take countermeasures to restore their freedom (Guo 2024). In digital media use, this manifests as anxiety resulting from being compelled to frequently check notifications, forced information storage and forwarding, and difficulty disengaging from mobile applications. In this study's context, Chinese millennials may experience discomfort and reactance motives when they perceive their media use as influenced by coercive factors, such as algorithmic pushing rather than personal choice. They may perceive frequent interruptions as diminishing efficiency or regard evading coercive design as requiring extra effort, thereby becoming more inclined to embrace digital minimalist behavior to restore autonomy. Accordingly, the following hypotheses are proposed:Perceived coercion positively correlates with digital minimalist behavior among Chinese millennials. Inferred Manipulative Intent

Research on inferred manipulative intent stems from the Persuasion Knowledge Model. Consumers integrate their own views with expert opinions as well as the views of relatives and friends to build a dynamic persuasion knowledge system that they use to infer others' intentions to influence them (Van Reijmersdal et al. 2015). In this study's context, when Chinese millennials perceive that digital platforms are trying to manipulate their attitudes or behaviors, psychological reactance may be activated, and autonomy may be experienced as threatened. This may be associated with lower technology acceptance. On the one hand, users may perceive service value as compromised by manipulative intent and less capable of fulfilling genuine needs. On the other hand, negative experiences linked to reactance may increase perceived use costs, rendering the platform appear less user‐friendly. Ultimately, these perceptions may coincide with a stronger tendency toward digital minimalist behavior, as users endeavor to regain control over attention, time, and online experiences. Accordingly, the following hypotheses are proposed:Inferred manipulative intent positively correlates with digital minimalist behavior among Chinese millennials. Perceived Goal Obstruction

Perceived goal obstruction denotes a psychological reactance that occurs when an ongoing goal pursuit is interrupted; the perception of interference increases the likelihood of reactance (Du and You 2019). In digital contexts, Chinese millennials may perceive digital media as a threat to goal management when they feel that tools such as social media encroach on core goals, for example, by undermining deep work and study focus. This appraisal may coincide with instrumental frustration, wherein digital media transitions from a “helper” to a “disruptor,” and may even be accompanied by a perceived threat to autonomy. The following emotions of reactance may be linked to weakened technological perceptions. Regarding usefulness, users may perceive the medium as harmful, such as when entertainment pushes are regarded as hindrances to personal development. Regarding ease of use, even with smooth interaction, vigilance triggered by goal obstruction may erode this advantage because resisting interference needs additional self‐control. Ultimately, these experiences may co‐occur with an increased inclination toward digital minimalist behavior intended to limit media intrusions into one's goal system. Accordingly, the following hypotheses are proposed:Perceived goal obstruction positively correlates with digital minimalist behavior among Chinese millennials. Perceived goal obstruction negatively correlates with Perceived Usefulness. Perceived goal obstruction negatively correlates with Perceived Ease of Use.

Perceived Usefulness

Perceived Usefulness denotes the degree to which individuals believe technology can improve efficiency at work or in daily life (Desmaryani et al. 2024). As witnesses to China's Internet development, Chinese millennials rely heavily on digital technologies, the utility of which is embedded in their everyday lives and work (Li 2019). However, this structural reliance may function as an important countervailing force in minimalist practices. Despite the psychological strain of digital media, high usefulness, such as WeChat's role as an essential tool for professional communication and transactions, may compel individuals toward compromise or functional compliance, prioritizing rigid needs over psychological comfort in their trade‐offs. Accordingly, higher Perceived Usefulness may correlate with weaker minimalist intentions and lower enactment in this group's media use. Accordingly, we propose the following hypothesis:Perceived Usefulness negatively correlates with digital minimalist behavior among Chinese millennials.

Perceived Usefulness negatively correlates with digital minimalist behavior among Chinese millennials.

Perceived Ease of Use

Perceived Ease of Use denotes the degree of effort individuals assume is necessary to use a technology (Nusir et al. 2023). Chinese millennials, having been deeply entrenched in a highly optimized digital environment, have internalized smooth operation as a basic condition of digital living through designs such as one‐tap access and seamless switching (Ma 2023). Such high Perceived Ease of Use may not inherently support minimalist practice and may instead be associated with restrained simplification via multiple mechanisms. Low interaction barriers can routinize media use and weaken users' awareness of negative experiences and capacity for reflection. A one‐stop service and a highly polished design can create obstacles to switching and disengagement, necessitating that simplification involves adapting to fragmented services or compromising convenience. Superficial micro‐resistance strategies, such as disabling notifications, may become merely symbolic because they are easy to implement and reverse, thereby rendering deeper simplification difficult. Therefore, high Perceived Ease of Use, by reinforcing use inertia, increasing switching costs, and weakening follow‐through, may correlate with decreased willingness and ability to reduce digital media use. Accordingly, we propose the following hypothesis:Perceived Ease of Use negatively correlates with digital minimalist behavior among Chinese millennials.

Research Design

Questionnaire Design

The questionnaire comprised four sections: an introduction, basic demographics, daily time spent using digital media, and items measuring the study variables. The items were adapted from established scales and refined to ensure reliability and validity. Guided by psychological reactance theory and the TAM, the study variables encompassed perceived coercion, inferred manipulative intent, perceived goal obstruction, Perceived Usefulness, Perceived Ease of Use, and digital minimalist behavior. Each construct was measured using five items contextually tailored to the everyday mobile platform use of Chinese millennials. An initial item pool was developed based on prior research, and items were retained if they aligned with the theoretical framework and reflected platform‐based experiences within the Chinese context.

Perceived Coercion

Perceived coercion is a key antecedent of psychological reactance and is conceptually similar to the intrusive forced exposure to online pop‐up advertising. Discomfort and resistance are more likely to occur when unsolicited information is imposed on the user experience. This study operationalized perceived coercion and contextually adapted established scales to fit China's mobile platform experiences. First, it captured compelled contact with pushed information by drawing on advertising intrusiveness measures of forced exposure (Li et al. 2002). Second, it incorporated interruption and freedom threat formulations from forced exposure research to reinforce the construct's autonomy‐constraint element (Edwards et al. 2002). Third, it adapted items related to forced viewing and perceived interference from proactive recommendation settings to app‐push and algorithmic distribution contexts (Wang and Cheng 2013).

Inferred Manipulative Intent

This study operationalized inferred manipulative intent as a cognitive evaluative construct that reflects respondents' perceptions of the commercial motives underlying platform recommendations and their intent to influence behavior. This scale was contextually adapted from measures of inferred manipulative intent designed for proactive recommendation contexts (Wang and Cheng 2013). The original items emphasize attributes that recommend messages that aim to influence decisions, persuade for marketing purposes, and manipulate purchasing behavior. This study transferred these items to mobile Internet contexts involving algorithmic recommendations and interface‐based persuasive design, incorporating wording more congruent with contemporary platform mechanisms.

Perceived Goal Obstruction

Perceived goal obstruction is frequently regarded as an important trigger of psychological reactance. This study examines the extent to which Chinese millennials perceive technology as obstructing core goals, such as progress on intended tasks, in their daily digital usage. The measurement was operationalized as a reflective latent variable that captured the extent to which digital use hindered goal pursuit. Items were contextually rewritten from prior measures of perceived goal obstruction, drawing on the work of Chen et al. (2014), Fan et al. (2018), Du and You (2019), and Cho (2004), encompassing browsing, task interference, and blocked goal progress, with terminology standardized for digital media use contexts.

Perceived Usefulness

Perceived Usefulness denotes the extent to which respondents believe that smartphones and related apps enhance work and life efficiency as well as daily functional performance. Items were adapted from the classic TAM scales and related studies (Kim and Kankanhalli 2009; Davis 1989; Venkatesh and Davis 2000), and contextualized to everyday mobile use contexts.

Perceived Ease of Use

Perceived Ease of Use reflects respondents' subjective assessments that utilizing smartphones and related apps is effortless and easy to learn. Items were adapted from existing TAM scales (Kim and Kankanhalli 2009; Davis 1989; Venkatesh and Davis 2000) and contextually customized to Chinese millennials' high‐frequency mobile use.

Digital Minimalist Behavior

Digital minimalist behavior constitutes the focal dependent variable in this study. This is defined as an observable self‐regulatory behavioral practice. Because no extensively validated scale is available in Chinese or international scholarship, this study relied on prior work on digital disconnection, algorithm resistance, and intermittent social media use. The behavioral dimensions of these studies were integrated and adapted. First, measures from social media contexts, including information shielding, avoidance, and active control of platform pushes (Wang and Zhu 2020), were utilized to capture the active management aspect. Second, metrics for algorithm resistance and avoidance within short‐video and recommender algorithm contexts were incorporated to reflect regulation strategies oriented to algorithmic environments (Zhang et al. 2024; Le et al. 2025). Third, coping strategies derived from research on social network stress, such as interruption and reduced use, were employed to capture reduced participation intensity (Maier et al. 2015). The items covered typical simplification practices in digital life, including notification management, uninstalling rarely used apps, setting time limits for social media, scheduling phone‐free time, prioritizing utilitarian apps, and reducing entertainment use.

A questionnaire was administered to Chinese participants. For items adapted from English sources, a translation and back‐translation procedure was used by two independent bilingual researchers, with discrepancies addressed via discussion to ensure semantic equivalence and contextual fit. All items were measured on a five‐point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree). A pilot study was conducted on 100 Chinese millennials. Reliability analysis indicated Cronbach's α values above 0.80 for all constructs, and the overall scale reliability was 0.935, thus indicating high internal consistency. Data Collection

The data were collected between June and August 2025 using the Wenjuanxing platform. Snowball sampling was used, and the survey was distributed using mass messages and promoted on social platforms such as QQ, WeChat, and Xiaohongshu. The returned questionnaires were 647; after excluding invalid responses, 560 valid cases were retained, yielding an effective response rate of 86.6%.

Research Methods

This mixed‐methods design was employed to leverage complementary strengths. As a variable‐oriented approach (Woodside 2013), SEM assesses overall model fit based on psychological reactance theory and the TAM, while estimating average net associations and related pathways for digital minimalist behavior. However, human behavior, particularly in digital contexts, is frequently influenced by the simultaneous existence of various factors. As a case‐oriented set‐theoretic method (Woodside 2014), fsQCA facilitates the examination of how various configurations of antecedent conditions may correlate with the same outcome. By integrating the two approaches, this study not only evaluates which factors are significant on average but also demonstrates how specific combinations of conditions correlate with higher digital minimalist behavior among Chinese millennials, thereby capturing the phenomenon's complexity more comprehensively. Finally, triangulating and integrating the findings from the two sets of results clarifies key relationships and highlights context dependence and boundary conditions that linear models may not capture, offering a more comprehensive understanding of digital minimalist behavior within this group.

fsQCA Analysis

Relationships estimated using SEM are typically modeled as linear. However, digital minimalist behavior among Chinese millennials may exhibit nonlinear patterns influenced by various factors. Accordingly, fsQCA was used to examine the non‐linear configurational associations of perceived coercion, inferred manipulative intent, perceived goal obstruction, Perceived Usefulness, and Perceived Ease of Use with this behavior.

Calibration

fsQCA necessitates the conversion of raw data into fuzzy sets anchored at 0 (Afonso et al. 2018). Digital minimalist behavior among Chinese millennials was specified as the outcome, and five factors, including perceived coercion, were specified as conditions and calibrated. Using the five‐point Likert scale method, three anchors were established for calibration: full membership (5), crossover point (3), and full non‐membership (1), thereby converting the data to the [0, 1] interval (Pappas et al. 2019). Calibration was executed using the Calibrate function in the fsQCA. To prevent the exclusion of any cases from the analysis, membership scores of 0.50 were adjusted by adding 0.001 (Fiss 2011).

Configuration 1: Perceived Coercion With Lack of Perceived Ease of Use

In this configuration, perceived coercion is a core present condition, whereas Perceived Ease of Use is a core absent condition, reflecting a distinctive constraint faced by Chinese millennials during media transition. As a transitional cohort shifting from legacy to digital media, they are proficient with digital tools but tend to prefer linear, in‐depth information consumption. When encountering platform interfaces and feature arrangements oriented toward commercial conversion, marked by fragmentation, multitasking, and frequent updates, Chinese millennials are more inclined to report cognitive load and maladaptation, resulting in diminished evaluations of ease of use. Meanwhile, many are at a stage in which mortgages, childcare, eldercare, and workplace pressures converge, resulting in constrained time and cognitive resources. A combination of strong perceived coercion and negative experiences of operational complexity is more likely to co‐occur with psychological reactance. Even without explicitly attributing these experiences to platform manipulation, this dual discomfort arising from usability demands and life pressures may be associated with selective management practices, such as eliminating redundant apps and disabling notifications, to mitigate disruptions and restore attentional control.

Configuration 2: Inferred Manipulative Intent With Lack of Perceived Ease of Use

In this configuration, inferred manipulative intent is a core‐present condition, whereas Perceived Ease of Use represents a core‐absent condition. This pathway underscores Chinese millennials' rational critique and pragmatic orientation amid challenging socioeconomic conditions. Having grown up during China's marketization, this cohort remains highly vigilant toward the logics of commercial capital. When they perceive digital platforms as employing covert advertising and suggestive content to direct their attention and consumption toward profit, their critical awareness is activated. Simultaneously, in an involutionary labor market, they encounter intense competition and instability, rendering time and efficiency especially critical. If digital tools are perceived as cumbersome due to cluttered interfaces, redundant functions, and high learning costs, negative experiences may intensify. The sense of betrayal linked to manipulative intent can coincide with the inefficiency associated with low ease of use, reinforcing the perception that these tools are commercially manipulative and user‐unfriendly. Certain individuals may interpret the restrained digital minimalist management as a strategy that harmonizes efficiency with autonomy.

Configuration 3: Perceived Manipulative Intent With Lack of Perceived Usefulness

In this configuration, inferred manipulative intent is a core‐present condition, whereas Perceived Usefulness is a core‐absent condition. This pathway is most effectively understood through the lens of Chinese millennials' life course and value pursuits. Compared with Generation Z, their value formation has been influenced more by traditional elite culture and pragmatism, resulting in a heightened emphasis on the utilitarian value of digital technologies. However, under capital‐driven logics, platform design frequently diverges from utilitarian aims, prioritizing user retention and data monetization, which may diminish certain users' evaluations of the core utility. When Chinese millennials perceive this divergence in usefulness and attribute it to platform manipulation, it is often accompanied by stronger disappointment and greater psychological distance. This tendency may be particularly pronounced under routine pressures, as they evaluate the cost‐effectiveness of digital engagement more stringently. Digital services, which are perceived as useless and intentionally manipulative, are frequently the first to be reduced, reallocating resources for value creation in offline life.

Configuration 4: Dual Absence of Perceived Usefulness and Perceived Ease of Use

This configuration identifies Perceived Usefulness and Perceived Ease of Use as the core absent conditions, while perceived goal obstruction serves as a peripheral present condition that reflects a typical form of digital decluttering among Chinese millennials in highly uncertain contexts. Amid the shifting mobility of a liquid society, this cohort frequently encounters intense existential anxiety and starts to reassess how resources, including digital consumption, are allocated. When a digital product is deemed both useless and challenging to learn, its basic legitimacy becomes questionable. Even minor goal obstructions, such as occasional distractions, can become intolerable under a strict survival logic. This combination of low usefulness, low ease of use, and only slight goal obstruction is regarded as a net liability and contradicts the pursuit of certainty and practical effectiveness. In this context, digital minimalist behavior aligns with a rational, nearly instinctual approach to resource optimization, focused on eliminating redundancies in digital life and alleviating cognitive and psychological burdens. The configuration exhibits high consistency; however, its low unique coverage indicates that simultaneously rejecting both usefulness and ease of use is rare within the sample. This implies that, for numerous Chinese millennials, digital tools may continue to possess functional value or convenience, rendering a complete exit a non‐dominant scenario.

Configuration 5: Perceived Coercion and Perceived Goal Obstruction With Lack of Perceived Usefulness

In this configuration, perceived coercion and perceived goal obstruction are core present conditions; Perceived Usefulness is core absent; and Perceived Ease of Use is peripheral present. It captures a defining strain period for Chinese millennials. Many of them are at pivotal stages of career progression, family establishment, and elder care responsibilities. Fragmented time, depleted energy, and competing role expectations frequently define their day‐to‐day life. External, non‐self‐determined digital intrusions tend to encroach on already scarce resources and act as obstacles to core goals, thereby exacerbating goal obstruction. Amid significant life pressures, the instrumental demand for digital tools may increase. When a digital product cannot demonstrate tangible value, perceived coercion and goal obstruction are amplified, and its legitimacy is more readily questioned. In this context, digital minimalist behavior may be perceived by certain individuals as a practical way of managing stress.

Configuration 6: Multi‐Driver Path of Perceived Coercion, Inferred Manipulative Intent, and Perceived Goal Obstruction

In this configuration, perceived coercion, inferred manipulative intent, and perceived goal obstruction constitute the core present conditions, whereas Perceived Usefulness is the peripheral present condition. This pathway focuses on Chinese millennials' digital fatigue and real‐life anxiety, exhibiting the greatest coverage and representativeness. As a “fragmented generation” in the digital era, they have witnessed a shift from technological empowerment to technological burden, demonstrating heightened critical reflexivity and a clear awareness of coercive design, capital‐driven manipulation, and the ways in which digital use encroaches upon offline goals. Moreover, they are also entrenched in high‐pressure realities influenced by mortgages, education, healthcare, and workplace competition, where these negative perceptions starkly contrast with their desire for a stable, orderly, and autonomous life. Even if digital tools remain useful, they seem negligible under negative systemic appraisal. This sense of awakening and perceived real‐life pressure frequently co‐occur with reduced use and boundary rebuilding. This pattern can be understood both as an expression of individual psychological reactance and a broader practice orientation through which a generation, operating within specific socioeconomic structures, seeks to safeguard personal space and meaning in life. The peripheral presence of usefulness indicates that instrumental needs persist; however, under negative experiences and goal‐related pressure, utility is frequently subordinated.

Robustness Check

To assess robustness, the original consistency threshold was increased to 0.85, while maintaining the case frequency and PRI thresholds constant. The configuration solutions, overall coverage, and consistency remained unchanged, indicating the robustness of the results.

Discussion

Discussion of SEM Results

The SEM results supported all hypotheses, reinforcing the relevance of psychological reactance theory and the TAM in understanding digital minimalist behavior. As core antecedents, perceived coercion, inferred manipulative intent, and perceived goal obstruction correlated positively with digital minimalist behavior among Chinese millennials. Additionally, they correlated negatively with Perceived Usefulness and Perceived Ease of Use, establishing an indirect pathway from psychological reactance cues to reduced technology acceptance, subsequently leading to digital minimalist behavior. Specifically, when this cohort perceived coercive prompts from digital platforms, observed attention manipulation through inductive content, or found digital use to disrupt core goals such as deep work, their perceived autonomy in technology management weakened. This weakening was accompanied by an intensified inclination to loosen technological constraints, which correlated positively with practices such as uninstalling redundant applications and setting time limits.

Perceived Usefulness and Ease of Use correlated negatively with digital minimalist behavior, implying that digital minimalist behavior among Chinese millennials is frequently selective rather than complete. This cohort encounters practical constraints such as workplace competition, family responsibilities, and economic pressures. Digital tools are frequently regarded as key resources for addressing these challenges, aligning with functional dependence. The negative correlation with Perceived Usefulness indicates that digital technologies are intricately integrated in daily routines, consistent with the selective nature of minimalism. The negative relationship with Perceived Ease of Use indicates that seamless user experiences and entrenched habits make it more difficult to reduce use. Collectively, this dependence reflects a condition influenced by both structural pressures and individual adjustment, serving as a key factor in the selective nature of digital minimalist behavior within this group.

The SEM highlighted an inherent tension in digital minimalist behavior among Chinese millennials. This behavior is not solely based on psychological distancing, nor does it entirely dismiss technological dependence. Rather, it correlates with an ongoing tension between the two. Psychological reactance factors correlate with stronger minimalist intentions and weaker identification with technology. In contrast, TAM factors correlated with more stable functional reliance and stronger use inertia. Their co‐presence indicates that digital minimalist behavior reflects a dynamic, negotiated practice that oscillates between dependence and distancing.

Discussion of fsQCA Results

The fsQCA analysis enhanced the SEM by capturing a pattern of conjunctural causation that the SEM was unable to detect. The six configurations indicated that high digital minimalist behavior among Chinese millennials does not depend on a single necessary condition but correlates with multiple configurational combinations of psychological reactance and technology perception factors. Further, this result underscores the complexity of digital minimalist behavior. The pathways also exhibited distinct profiles of conditions associated with the outcome. Configuration 1 suggests that even without perceived manipulation or perceived goal obstruction, the combination of perceived coercion and a core absence of Perceived Ease of Use constitutes a sufficient configuration for high digital minimalist behavior. This explains why certain Chinese millennials uninstall apps even without an explicit goal. Configuration 3 highlights the link between recognizing the platform intent and evaluating the technological value. Chinese millennials, upon recognizing commercial manipulation and appraising the utilitarian value of digital technologies as minimal, tend to exhibit a heightened inclination toward minimalist practices. Configuration 5, exhibiting the highest coverage, indicated a significant co‐occurrence of various negative perceptions. Even when Perceived Usefulness was only peripherally present, the interplay of the three psychological reactance factors constituted a sufficient configuration associated with high digital minimalist behavior among Chinese millennials. This pattern is consistent with the coexistence of digital dependence and fatigue in this cohort. Greater digital immersion may be associated with stronger perceived negative effects, and accumulated negative experiences may coincide with a stronger tendency toward minimalist practices among some individuals.

Comparing SEM and fsQCA Results

A comparison of the SEM and fsQCA results revealed three key observations. First, the SEM indicated that the negative correlation between Perceived Ease of Use and digital minimalist behavior was the most significant. fsQCA, however, suggested that this relationship is context‐dependent. In Configurations 1 and 2, the core absence of Perceived Ease of Use was a core condition, whereas in Configurations 3 and 6, it was not. This pattern implies that the constraining influence of convenience‐based dependence is more salient when the psychological reactance conditions are relatively singular. When multiple reactance‐related conditions co‐occur, dependence‐related conditions may shift from core to non‐core, diminishing their correlation with the outcome, a nuance that SEM cannot capture.

Second, SEM captured linear net‐effect pathways among variables, whereas fsQCA highlighted asymmetry in configurational combinations. Configuration 4 exhibited exceedingly high consistency (0.961), indicating a strong correlation between low Perceived Usefulness, low Perceived Ease of Use, and high digital minimalist behavior. However, its unique coverage was only 0.004, indicating that scenarios in which both usefulness and ease of use are simultaneously low are rare among Chinese millennials. This rarity implies that functional reliance on digital technologies is prevalent within this group, and that recognizing technological value or integrating functions into daily routines is challenging to completely eradicate.

Third, SEM emphasized the relational pathways connecting psychological reactance factors to digital minimalist behavior, whereas fsQCA highlighted the supportive role of peripheral conditions. Given that the core combination was already aligned with the high outcome, the peripheral presence of Perceived Ease of Use in Configuration 5 did not inhibit high digital minimalist behavior. This finding indicates that individuals do not necessarily disengage entirely from digital technology. In instances of significant core distancing experiences, high levels of minimalism may still be attained by retaining essential usage while reducing non‐essential consumption. Accordingly, instead of relying on single variables in isolation, interventions should prioritize core conditions and context‐specific combinations.

The SEM and fsQCA results collectively indicate that digital minimalist behavior among Chinese millennials embodies a dynamic relationship between distancing and dependence. The SEM delineated systematic associations, whereas fsQCA illustrated more flexible combinations and ongoing negotiations among cases. This convergent evidence supports the primary assertion that digital minimalist behavior constitutes a contextual and strategic form of self‐regulation, enabling individuals to negotiate the dynamic tension between structural dependence and psychological distancing.

Contributions to Prior Research

The findings make two contributions to existing research on digital minimalist behavior. First, they provide empirical support for a broad correlation between psychological reactance mechanisms and digital minimalist behavior. This resonates with prior research emphasizing motives such as resistance and refusal (Chen and Chen 2025). Additionally, it complements the strategic resistance of young people to algorithmic governance (Jin and Yang 2025). In addition, it addresses a core paradox frequently noted and observed but rarely quantified in prior research, namely, why digital minimalist behavior is regularly incomplete (Xu and Zhu 2023). By incorporating TAM, the analysis quantified a key constraint. Beyond psychological reactance, higher Perceived Usefulness and Ease of Use are negatively associated with digital minimalist behavior, suggesting that, among some individuals, digital minimalism more often appears as strategic regulation rather than full disengagement.

Accordingly, digital minimalist behavior among Chinese millennials should not be simplified to mere technological rejection or a lifestyle choice. Rather, it can be perceived as a context‐sensitive, self‐regulatory response to digital overload and social pressures, whereby individuals strive to regain a sense of control and meaning. This study integrates SEM and fsQCA to demonstrate the link between this behavior and both reactance‐related perceptions of platform intrusion and continued reliance on the instrumental value and convenience of digital tools. Collectively, these patterns portray a persistent ambivalence wherein autonomy‐oriented impulses coexist with practical dependencies, emphasizing Chinese millennials' ongoing negotiations of agency and efficacy in daily digital life.

Conclusions

Theoretical Implications

This study enhances the explanatory application of psychological reactance theory in digital behavior research. It demonstrates positive correlations between perceived coercion, inferred manipulative intent, and perceived goal obstruction with digital minimalist behavior, alongside indirect links mediated by negative associations with Perceived Usefulness and Perceived Ease of Use. These results broaden the implications of psychological reactance from behavioral motivation to cognitive evaluation, enriching the theory's micro‐level explanatory utility in media research. This study additionally presents an innovative extension that effectively reverses the key implications of the TAM. Although the TAM is typically utilized to predict technology adoption, it was applied in this context to account for inhibitory technology behavior, revealing significant negative correlations between Perceived Usefulness, Perceived Ease of Use, and digital minimalist behavior. This indicates that, under conditions of technological overload, adoption‐related cognitions may, in certain circumstances, function as inertia that perpetuates dependence and limits behavioral change. The findings problematize a linear perspective of acceptance and use, underscoring the dialectical role of technology acceptance in ambivalent human‐technology relations. This study emphasizes a dual‐mechanism context for digital minimalist behavior among Chinese millennials. Psychological reactance and TAM‐based factors serve distinct but complementary functions. Psychological reactance, which indicates detachment, correlates positively with the behavior, whereas TAM factors, which represent dependence, exhibit a negative association. This divergent pattern indicates that the two frameworks represent opposing extremes of the behavioral continuum. Additionally, psychological reactance may be indirectly linked to behavior through its negative association with value perceptions as defined by TAM. This pattern implies a directional pathway between the two sets of constructs rather than a simple correlation or conceptual overlap. Collectively, they characterize a negotiated form of digital minimalist behavior influenced by the conflict between dependence and detachment. In addition, the focus on Chinese millennials addresses the limited subgroup differentiation in research on youth media behavior. This study offers a Sinicized interpretive template for theorizing digital minimalism by integrating technological and sociocultural perspectives.

This study broadens the cultural scope of research on digital minimalist behavior. In Western contexts, digital minimalism is frequently presented as a lifestyle decision rooted in individual agency, where reducing connections is anticipated to reinforce boundaries, improve focus, and enhance well‐being. The SEM results from a sample of Chinese millennials indicate that when platforms are perceived as intrusive, autonomy‐restricting, or goal‐disrupting, individuals are more inclined to decrease usage and regulate media engagement. Cognitive evaluations of the efficiency and convenience of digital tools correlated negatively with minimalist tendencies. The fsQCA results further indicate that various pathways to high levels of digital minimalist behavior do not rely on complete withdrawal. Rather, they reflect various combinations that jointly reduce intrusions while retaining key functions. This pattern aligns with the variations in digitalization trajectories and sociocultural contexts between Chinese and Western societies. In Western societies, technological change is generally more continuous and incremental, resulting in smoother shifts in media habits. In contrast, Chinese millennials encountered a rapid leap from traditional media to the mobile Internet and platform ecosystems in China. Initial media experiences and subsequently, high‐intensity platform life accumulate within the same cohort. This overlap is associated with a markedly fragmented media experience, and their digital minimalist behavior frequently appears internally ambivalent. At the cultural and social levels, Western individualism frequently perceives technology as a tool under personal control, rendering minimalism closer to rational self‐optimization. Conversely, Chinese society prioritizes collectivist culture, and Chinese millennials encounter workplace “involution” and intergenerational pressures. Because digital technologies are deeply embedded in everyday social functioning, their digital minimalist behavior is more complex, and motivation often stems from a desire for personal autonomy and space, whereas practice is constrained by relational costs and functional dependence. Accordingly, this study highlights an ongoing, negotiated tradeoff between connection fatigue and functional reliance rather than a one‐way reduction in utilization. This finding addresses the long‐standing scarcity of Chinese samples in international research on digital minimalism. Moreover, it offers a non‐Western pathway for comprehending how individuals in highly digitalized societies pursue agency within structurally coexisting relationships with technology.

This study innovatively integrates SEM and fsQCA to analyze the evolution of digital minimalist behavior through both net and configurational effects. SEM identifies key correlates and indirect linkage patterns, whereas fsQCA demonstrates how multiple pathways can be linked to the same outcome under dissimilar conditions. The results indicate that digital minimalist behavior cannot be accurately captured by a singular factor alone; rather, it aligns more closely with a context‐dependent combination of psychological reactance perceptions and technology evaluations. The variation among Chinese millennials is thus plausibly associated with the presence of equifinal pathways and the situational contingency of condition sets.

Practical Implications

In practice, these findings provide insights for optimizing digital minimalist behaviors among Chinese millennials. Digital service platforms should mitigate coercive pushes and manipulative content via algorithmic design, potentially diminishing perceived intrusiveness and distrust, thereby easing psychological reactance. Platforms may also incorporate user‐controlled content filtering and screen time settings to reinforce user agency. Governmental and societal actors must address the multiple pressures experienced by Chinese millennials as a core social force. Their digital minimalist behavior may serve as a strategy for managing stress and digital fatigue, while insufficient guidance may lead to mental health risks and broader social instability. Policy efforts can alleviate formalistic digital administrative burdens and expand public services that facilitate daily life and psychological well‐being. At the individual level, Chinese millennials should develop self‐regulation and avoid an all‐or‐nothing stance toward technology, relying neither on digital convenience nor rejecting its value as a whole. Media literacy initiatives may enhance context‐sensitive tool use and help establish rational rules, namely, using what is useful and simplifying what is not, thereby facilitating a sustainable balance between convenience and autonomy.

Limitations and Future Research

Although this study makes progress in theoretical and methodological integration, it still has several limitations. First, although a quantitative approach can examine the relationships among variables and configurational pathways, it restricts deeper inquiry into individuals' motives, emotions, and contextual practices. Future research should incorporate qualitative methods such as interviews and observations to triangulate the quantitative findings and more comprehensively capture the complexity of digital minimalist behavior. Second, this study focused on psychological and technology‐related perceptions and did not include macro‐level factors such as social norms, peer influence, or cultural values. Consequently, future research should integrate social cognitive theory or the Theory of Planned Behavior to develop a more comprehensive multilevel framework. Third, this study relies on self‐reported cross‐sectional data from a single survey wave. Despite the unequivocal theoretical reasoning, endogeneity and reverse causality cannot be fully ruled out. Unobserved common causes may also influence both psychological reactance perceptions and digital minimalist behavior, thereby increasing the risk of omitted variable bias. Future studies should employ longitudinal tracking, experimental designs, or objective behavioral logs to enhance the assessment of directionality and mitigate bias. Finally, the study examined only Chinese millennials and did not compare them with other cohorts, such as Generation Z. Subsequent research should conduct cross‐age mixed‐method studies to examine heterogeneity and developmental shifts in digital minimalist behavior, thereby enriching cohort mapping and theoretical frameworks in digital behavior research.
