Comparison of the effectiveness of two teaching methods of storytelling and interactive lecturing on long-term memory of Anesthesia Technology students
Authors: Elnaz Jalalkamali, Parisa Moradimajd, Shahnam Sedigh Maroufi, Jamileh Abolghasemi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Memorization involves storing and retrieving learned information, and effective teaching methods are crucial for this process. This study compared the effects of storytelling and interactive lecturing on the long-term memory of Anesthesia Technology students, highlighting the importance of memory in medical science education.MATERIALS AND METHODS:This quasi-experimental study used a pretest and two post-test designs. An immediate post-test followed weekly teaching sessions for 1 month to evaluate short-term retention, while a delayed post-test assessed long-term memory 3 weeks later. In 2023, a study with 52 undergraduate Anesthesia Technology students at Iran and Tehran University of Medical Sciences compared two teaching methods: storytelling (n = 31) and interactive lecturing (n = 21). Three tests were conducted: a preliminary test, an immediate post-test for short-term evaluation, and a delayed post-test for long-term evaluation 3 weeks later. This study utilized descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation, frequency) to summarize demographic data and applied inferential tests (paired t-tests, independent t-tests, repeated measures tests) to evaluate research hypotheses and relationships between variables.RESULTS:Statistical analysis revealed no significant demographic differences between groups. The findings indicated a significant difference in long-term memory retention, with the storytelling method leading to higher scores in delayed post-tests compared to the interactive lecturing approach (t = 3.388, P < 0.001). These results demonstrate that storytelling is more effective in enhancing long-term memory retention among students.CONCLUSION:The storytelling approach greatly improves long-term memory retention among Anesthesia Technology students in comparison to the interactive lecturing method. It is recommended to implement this approach to promote meaningful learning and improve patient management in both medical and paramedical disciplines.

Introduction

Medical education has special characteristics compared to other training fields due to the diverse learning needs of many human health and disease issues.[1] Acquiring knowledge and understanding concepts are essential to learning. The “memory process” refers to how we store and retrieve this knowledge.[2] Information in short-term memory is transferred to permanent memory if it is repeated, modified, and mixed with previous information.[3] Instead of the conventional approach, the focus has recently shifted to new techniques, such as active and student-centered learning.[4]

Since learning is an active process, interactive lecture techniques are thought to be the most effective teaching strategy.[5] During an interactive lecture, both the teacher and the students actively impart and receive knowledge.[6] Storytelling is also one of the simplest, most affordable, and most effective educational strategies.[7] A story is an integrated sequence of real and unreal events and experiences.[8] Storytelling visualizes and objectifies concepts and gives them mental meaning so that they are easily remembered.[9] The storyteller tells a story from the perspective of his or her experience and according to the predetermined goals and needs of the listener. Storytelling in medicine provides the context for patient-centered care.[10] Clinical errors can lead to complications such as death, permanent disability, or long-term hospital stays.[11] Recently, the integration of narrative medicine into medical education has demonstrated its effectiveness as a study conducted at a Dutch medical school involved all fourth-year students participating in a mandatory narrative medicine lesson.[12] The review of previous studies by the researcher resulted in contradictory results, and it appears that there may not be a study specifically comparing the effectiveness of two interactive lecturing and storytelling methods in Anesthesia Technology students. This study evaluates the effectiveness of storytelling versus interactive lecturing on long-term memory retention among sixth-semester Anesthesia Technology students at Iran University of Medical Sciences (IUMS) and Tehran University of Medical Sciences (TUMS). It addresses gaps in educational practices, emphasizing innovative teaching methods to enhance engagement and knowledge retention crucial for patient care.

Materials and Methods

Study design and settings

This study employed a quasi-experimental design and was conducted at IUMS and TUMS. The researchers used a pre-test–post-test control group design to compare the effectiveness of storytelling and interactive lecturing methods in teaching Anesthesia Technology students.

Study participants and sampling

This study was quasiexperimental and conducted at IUMS and TUMS. The study involved 52 Anesthesia Technology students, with 31 in the intervention group (IUMS) and 21 in the control group (TUMS), selected randomly by tables of random numbers. The sample size was determined based on a previous study,[3] and the inclusion criteria included enrolment in the unit for the first time, while the exclusion criteria included unwillingness, incomplete responses, and absenteeism.

Data collection tools and technique

The study used a two-part questionnaire to collect data. The first part included demographic variables (age, gender, interest in the field of study, and grade points average in the academic years spent), while the second part contained a researcher-made test to assess students’ knowledge at three critical points: preliminary test, immediate post-test, and delayed post-test. These tests measure students’ baseline knowledge before instruction, their learning gains immediately after instruction, and their ability to remember information in the long term (after 3 weeks), respectively. The test consisted of a total of 20 questions, which included both open-ended and close-ended questions, and each question was valued at one point. The content validity of the teaching materials and tests was established through expert review, with CVI and CVR values indicating high validity. The external reliability of the tests was measured through inter-rater agreement, with ICC values above 0.99, and the internal reliability was high, with Cronbach’s alpha above 0.79 for all three tests. The control group received interactive lectures with active student participation, while the intervention group used storytelling based on Mostafazadeh’s framework,[13] which is given in Diagram 1: Storytelling steps. Both groups covered the same content over four 2-hour weekly sessions taught by a single instructor to learn about anesthesia complication causes, diagnosis, and treatment. Ethical consideration

Ethics approval to conduct the study was received. IR.IUMS.REC.1402.971. Prior to the intervention, the researchers explained the study to the Anesthesia Technology students and obtained their informed consent. The individual data and scores remained secret. In the end, the storytelling method was taught to the control group.

In Diagram 2, it can be seen that the pretest results were alike in both the intervention and control groups. After teaching in both groups and administering immediate post-tests to the students, a noticeable boost in scores is evident. After 3 weeks, delayed post-tests were given to students in both groups to assess their long-term memory. Results showed that the intervention group had a smaller decrease in scores and higher scores in delayed post-tests compared to the control group. The research findings demonstrated that teaching through storytelling had a much greater effect on long-term memory compared to interactive lecturing.

In conclusion, the findings indicate that the storytelling method was superior to the interactive lecturing technique in improving Anesthesia Technology students’ long-term memory retention.

Discussion

The study compared interactive lecturing and storytelling for Anesthesia Technology students. Both methods showed short-term gains, but storytelling led to better long-term retention. This aligns with research on storytelling’s benefits in education. The results suggest incorporating storytelling techniques can optimize long-term knowledge retention for Anesthesia Technology students.

In contrast to this study, Zare et al.’s[9] study showed that there was no significant difference between lecture and storytelling groups regarding learning quality. However, this study was conducted in a different discipline (nursing).

In line with this study, a review article by Mostafazadeh found that storytelling, as an active educational method in clinical education, promotes deep learning, critical thinking, and clinical skills.[13] While this review provides supportive evidence, it did not quantify the magnitude of the effect.

Similarly, in the study by Kirilmazkaya et al., the findings indicated that incorporating storytelling into science teaching activities positively influences students’ academic performance and their attitudes toward science. However, this study was conducted with different target populations.[14]

In line with this study, Xue M et al. showed narrative medicine theory and narrative writing through online platforms can enhance the professionalism, empathy, and humanistic care skills of nursing undergraduates.[15]

In another study by Johnston et al. regarding simulated learning experiences, the results showed the positive effect of visual and auditory storytelling in undergraduate nursing students.[16] While this study used a different educational modality (simulation), it supports the general principle of using storytelling to enhance learning.

Finally, Daryazadeh et al.[10] recommended incorporating proper education and a narrative approach into the formal curriculum, considering students’ educational and cultural backgrounds while integrating medical evidence and facts.

In summary, while many studies support storytelling in health education, they often have limitations. This study shows that storytelling is more effective than interactive lecturing for long-term memory retention in Anesthesia Technology students. Further research is needed to confirm these results in larger, diverse groups and to explore how storytelling aids learning.

Limitations and recommendations

Challenges in implementing the storytelling approach included a time-consuming nature, ambiguity in evaluating goals, and potential student resistance. To overcome these constraints, the researchers implemented various measures. They provided clearer details on the content, highlighted key concepts in each storytelling session, and reiterated the significance of storytelling in promoting profound learning among students. The authors suggest conducting additional studies with bigger sample sizes and extended follow-up periods to expand on these findings and investigate the incorporation of story-related scenarios for anesthesia complications along with lectures into the Anesthesia Technology curriculum to improve the learning process and boost long-term memory retention.

Conclusion

The data analysis revealed a decrease in scores after 3 weeks in both groups, with a smaller decrease and higher scores in the intervention group for the delayed post-test. The research discovered that using storytelling had a much greater impact on improving the ability to remember things over a long period.
