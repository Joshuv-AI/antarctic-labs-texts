Abstract
. We introduce a new approach to the description of multi-sorted clones (sets of k k -tuples of operations of the same arity, closed under coordinatewise composition and containing all projection tuples) on a two-element domain. Leveraging the well-known Galois connection between operations and relations, we define a small class of canonical relations sufficient to describe all Boolean multi-sorted clones up to non-surjective operations. Furthermore, we introduce elementary operations on relations, which are less cumbersome than general formulas and have many useful properties. Using these tools, we provide a new and elementary proof of the famous Post’s lattice theorem. We also show that every multi-sorted clone of k k -tuples of operations decomposes into a surjective part described by canonical relations and 2 ​ k 2k clones of ( k − 1 ) (k-1) -tuples of operations. This structural understanding allows us to describe an embedding of the lattice of multi-sorted clones into a well-understood poset. In particular, we rederive – by a simpler method – a result of V. Taimanov originally from 1983, showing that every multi-sorted clone on a two-element domain is finitely generated. Finally, we also give a concise proof of the Galois connection between (surjective) multi-sorted clones and the corresponding closed sets of relations.

1.
Introduction and main results

1.1.
Introduction

Clones are sets of operations closed under composition and containing all projections; objects that naturally arise in most areas of mathematics. For instance, we know that composing monotone operations yields a monotone operation,
and composing linear operations yields a linear one, which gives rise to the notions of clones of monotone and linear operations, respectively.
Arguably the main result in clone theory is the description of all clones on a 2-element set obtained by E. Post in
[
24
,
25
]
. This result is not only elegant and important on its own – description of all clones is essentially the description of all algebraic structures – but has become a valuable tool in areas ranging from logic to complexity theory
[
12
]
.
In light of this result, a natural next step was to seek similar descriptions for larger domains. However, it seems unrealistic to describe all clones even on a three-element set as the lattice is uncountable
[
17
,
16
]
and very complicated
[
7
,
8
,
9
,
44
]
.

Probably the second most important result is a Galois connection between clones and relational clones
[
14
,
4
,
5
]
, where relational clones are sets of relations (or, equivalently, predicates, see Definition
2.3
) closed under primitive positive (pp for short) formulas (that is, formulas using only
\wedge
and
\exists
) and containing both empty and equality relations. Through this correspondence, every clone on a finite set can be described as the set of all operations preserving some (possibly infinite) set of relations. More importantly, the entire lattice of clones can be analyzed purely in terms of relations and pp formulas.

One key advantage of this perspective is that working with relations tends to be more tractable than working directly with operations, since pp definitions are often simpler to compute than compositions.
In fact, describing minimal clones (the atoms of the lattice of clones) requires dealing with operations and their composition, and to date, they have only been classified for three-element domain
[
13
]
, and even those were found by a computer. A classification for the four-element domain was announced in
[
31
]
, but to the best of our knowledge, it has not been fully confirmed.
In contrast, maximal clones (the coatoms of the lattice) on any finite set have been fully classified for over 50 years
[
30
]
, with
each maximal clone being defined by a concrete relation describing some nice property of operations such as being monotone or linear.

In this paper, we consider a generalization of clones known as multi-sorted clones,
where instead of operations, we work with
k
-tuples of operations of the same arity (referred to as
k
-operations in the paper).
Each operation in the tuple then corresponds to a specific sort,
and composition is defined coordinatewise (see the precise definition in Section
2
).

Various classification results are already known in the multi-sorted setting when the domain is fixed to
\{0,\,1\}
. It is known that there are only countably many
k
-sorted clones on a two-element domain and that each is finitely generated – a result first announced by V. Taimanov in 1983
[
32
]
and later established in detail in his 1984 PhD thesis
[
33
]
. More recently, Taimanov republished these results in a series of three shorter papers
[
34
,
35
,
36
]
. The works
[
32
,
33
]
also include a classification of the maximal multi-sorted clones on
\{0,\,1\}
, with alternative description given in
[
27
,
28
]
. A related result – the classification of maximal
k
-sorted clones where the first
k-1
sorts consist of operations on two-element set and the last on any arbitrary set – is presented in
[
29
]
, and the maximal multi-sorted clones with operations on either a two- or three-element set are described in
[
21
]
. Finally, a classification up to minion homomorphisms of multi-sorted clones on
\{0,\,1\}
determined by binary relations was recently given in
[
2
]
. The main aim of our paper is to continue this classification program by providing new tools and structural insights.

One reason the multi-sorted generalization is particularly natural is the Galois connection we can build between clones of
k
-operations and relational clones of
k
-sorted relations; that is, relations where each variable has assigned a sort from 1 to
k
and variables may only be identified if they share the same sort.
In fact, in both theory and applications, it is common for different coordinates of a relation to come from different domains, making multi-sorted relations natural objects in their own right.

Another important feature of working with relations is that we do not need all of them to define clones.
Knowing the Galois connection, we can argue that it suffices to consider relations that cannot be represented as conjunctions of relations of smaller arities – such relations are called
essential

[
39
,
40
]
.
Already, this simple observation led to a new proof of Post’s lattice theorem
[
39
]
and was the main idea behind the description of all clones below the maximal clone of self-dual operations on three-element domain
[
41
]
,
which, to this day, remains the only uncountable sublattice below a maximal clone that has been described.

A very natural next step is to consider only relations that
are
\cap
-irreducible in the corresponding relational clone, called
critical
in
[
20
]
and
maximal
in
[
41
]
.
However, since being critical heavily depends on the relational clone and is therefore hard to verify, it is more desirable to introduce a combinatorial property (similar to essentiality) that is almost as strong as criticality. Such a property, the notion of key relation, was introduced in
[
42
]
, and it is equivalent to being critical in the relational clone generated by the relation, assuming that all the coordinates are of different sorts.
Another motivation for studying key relations is
a nice description of all (multi-sorted) key relations on the two-element set:
they are exactly the relations that can be defined as disjunctions of linear equations
[
42
]
. Moreover, as explained in detail in Section
2
,
computing primitive positive formulas over such relations is very simple.
In the paper, we exploit these disjunctive relations to describe multi-sorted relational clones
on a two-element set.

Nevertheless, in order to characterize all multi-sorted clones on a two-element domain, we still need to consider all
combinations of such relations – and there are simply too many of them.
To overcome this, we take one more step: we strengthen our closure operator by allowing conjunctive formulas with both existential and universal quantifiers (called quantified primitive positive formulas, or qpp formulas for short; see Section
2
for the precise definition).
This closure operator also admits a Galois connection, but this time, we consider only surjective operations instead.
Fortunately, on a two-element domain, the only nonsurjective operations are constants, which can be easily added back to the surjective part afterward.
To make the consideration of all combinations feasible, we introduce
canonical relations
– a restricted class of relations satisfying the following three properties:
(1) any quantified relational clone can be defined by these relations,
(2) for any canonical relation
\rho
there are no weaker relations that altogether generate
\rho
,
(3) the arity of the relations cannot be reduced while preserving property (1).
To demonstrate the gap between arbitrary relations and canonical relations, notice that on a two-element set, there are
2^{2^{n}}\cdot k^{n}

n
-ary
k
-sorted relations,
about
(1-1/\sqrt{e})\cdot 2^{2^{n}}\cdot k^{n}

n
-ary
k
-sorted essential relations
[
38
]
,
about
2^{n^{2}/4}\cdot k^{n}

n
-ary
k
-sorted key relations,
and only about
2^{n}\cdot k^{n}

n
-ary
k
-sorted canonical relations. In fact, for
n>k
, the only
n
-ary
k
-sorted canonical relations are the relations of size
2^{n}-1
; see Definition
3.1
for details.

Let us note that a similar approach – restricting attention to carefully chosen relations – was already taken by B. Romov in
[
27
,
28
,
29
]
and by S. Marchenkov in
[
21
]
. Interestingly, some of the relations they considered are very similar to key and canonical relations, even though their work predates the theory introduced in
[
42
]
by more than 20 years. This highlights how natural this perspective is and further motivates the need for a unified framework.

1.2.
Main results

Since clones on a two-element set are fully characterized, while clones on larger domains are too complicated to describe,
it is natural to attempt a description of multi-sorted clones on a two-element domain.
In this paper, we develop a technique that makes this feasible.

The first main result of the paper is presented in Section
3
, where we define canonical relations and prove that they satisfy the properties (1)–(3) outlined above.
Further, in Section
4.1
, we introduce elementary operations and show how they can be used instead of qpp formulas for relations defined as disjunctions of linear equations.
These two ideas together are so powerful that deriving Post’s lattice from them becomes straightforward, as demonstrated in Section
4.2
.
Thus, a new short proof of Post’s lattice is another main result of the paper.

The third result states that any clone of
k
-operations can be decomposed into a surjective part definable by canonical relations, which therefore admits an embedding into a simple and well-understood poset,
and
2k
clones of
(k-1)
-operations.
This decomposition implies Taimanov’s result
[
32
,
33
,
34
,
35
,
36
]
– that there are only countably many
k
-sorted clones and that each of them is finitely generated.
Note that Taimanov worked mostly with operations, whereas in our setting operations are auxiliary objects that allow us to make decomposition – and theoretically, they could be avoided altogether.

The last contribution of this paper is a complete proof of a Galois connection
between clones of
k
-sorted surjective operations and quantified
k
-sorted relational clones.
Although our proof is essentially a generalization of the corresponding result from
[
11
]
to the multi-sorted setting, to our knowledge, it has never been properly written down.

1.3.
Applications

Even though we do not provide a complete description of multi-sorted clones on a two-element domain even for two sorts, it is plausible to derive such a description using the ideas developed in this paper.
Below, we give four other examples of how our results can be applied to concrete problems.

(1)

An important class of clones in universal algebra
consists of clones of idempotent operations, that is, operations satisfying
f(x,\dots,\,x)=x
.
For instance, the entire theory of Taylor varieties and Taylor identities was developed primarily for the idempotent reduct of algebras
[
37
,
22
]
.
Relational clones corresponding to idempotent clones are precisely those containing all constant relations. Since any qpp formula using universal quantifiers may be replaced by a pp formula using these constant relations, our technique provides a natural tool for characterizing idempotent clones.

(2)

One of the minimal clones on
\{0,\,1,\,2\}
is generated by
the following semiprojection
[
31
]
:

s_{5}(x,\,y,\,z)\coloneqq\begin{cases}x,&|\{x,\,y,\,z\}|<3,\\
y,&|\{x,\,y,\,z\}|=3.\\
\end{cases}

It was shown in
[
40
, Section 6]
that there are two types of
essential relations on
\{0,\,1,\,2\}

preserved by
s_{5}
:
graphs of permutations and
relations whose projection onto every coordinate is a two-element set.
The latter relations can be viewed as multi-sorted relations with variables of three sorts,
where sorts depend on the projections onto the corresponding coordinate, that is
\{0,\,1\}
,
\{1,\,2\}
, or
\{0,\,2\}
.
Therefore, clones on three elements containing this semiprojection can be embedded into the clones of 3-operations on a two-element domain
and it seems plausible that our technique could lead to a complete characterization of all such clones.

(3)

Primitive positive formulas play an important role in the study of the complexity of the
Constraint Satisfaction Problems
(CSP).
Let
\Gamma
be a set of multi-sorted relations on a two-element set, and let
\CSP(\Gamma)
denote a decision problem where the input
is a conjunction of relations from
\Gamma
, and the task is to decide whether it is satisfiable.
Since checking satisfiability is equivalent to evaluating a sentence after adding existential quantifiers,

\CSP(\Gamma_{1})
is LOGSPACE-reducible to
\CSP(\Gamma_{2})
whenever
\Gamma_{2}
pp-defines
\Gamma_{1}

[
18
,
19
]
.
Our results imply that instead of considering arbitrary
\Gamma
we may consider
\Gamma
consisting of disjunction of linear equations
and our technique can be further developed into an algorithm for CSP that eliminates existential quantifiers one by one using elementary operations. Furthermore, it is known that CSP over an arbitrary
\Gamma
is
equivalent to the CSP over its
core

[
18
]
,
and qpp formulas over a core can be replaced by equivalent pp formulas. Therefore,
for the purpose of studying complexity
of CSP for constraint languages consisting of
multi-sorted relations on a two-element set, it suffices to assume that
\Gamma
consists only of canonical relations. For more information about CSP and its connection with clones, see
[
3
]
.

(4)

Evaluating a quantified conjunctive sentence is another important problem in computer science, called
quantified
CSP
[
23
]
.
Similarly to the non-quantified case, the complexity depends only on the quantified relational clones generated by the constraint language
\Gamma

[
6
]
, and therefore we may assume that
all the relations in
\Gamma
are canonical. As canonical relations are very simple and there are only few of them, this substantially simplifies the task of characterizing the complexity for all sets of multi-sorted relations on a two-element domain.

1.4.
Structure of the paper

The paper is organized as follows.

In Section
2
, we fix notation and provide all necessary preliminary definitions. In particular, we formally introduce multi-sorted (operational) clones, relational clones, and quantified relational clones, and recall the standard notions of polymorphisms and invariant relations that will be essential throughout the text.

Section
3
is dedicated to canonical relations. We explicitly define canonical relations, state their properties, and outline the reasoning why each quantified relational clone on a two-element domain can be generated using only canonical relations.

In Section
4
, we introduce elementary operations on relations. These operations provide explicit and convenient closedness criteria for quantified relational clones. As an immediate consequence, we obtain a simplified proof of Post’s lattice theorem.

In Section
5
, we investigate the structure of the lattice of multi-sorted (quantified) relational clones. We establish embeddings of this lattice into simpler and better-understood structures, and use these embeddings to study some properties of multi-sorted clones. In particular, we revisit the aforementioned result concerning finite basis of multi-sorted clones, simplifying its original proof by V. Taimanov.

Section
6
contains a rigorous treatment of the Galois connection between clones of multi-sorted surjective operations and quantified relational clones, thus generalizing classical single-sorted results.

Finally, Section
7
provides detailed proofs of technical lemmata stated in previous sections.

Definition 2.3

(Relations, relational clones)
.

A

on a nonempty finite set

is called the

. When necessary, we denote the sorts of variables by superscript. By

By
\sigma_{\bot}

, which takes the value
\bot

\sigma_{=}^{i}(x,\,y)=\top\quad\iff\quad x=y\,,

where
x

i

Suppose

; then by

, we denote the set of all
k
-sorted predicates
\rho

Equivalently, we define a closure under

For simplicity, we usually omit brackets and write

We say that two predicates are

Remark 2.5

Theorem 2.8

Let

\displaystyle\subseteq\sPol\Inv\mathcal{F}\,,

\displaystyle=\sClo\mathcal{F}\,,

\displaystyle\Inv\sPol S

Next, we introduce a class of relations that will serve as a convenient tool for characterizing (quantified) relational clones.

Definition 2.9

(Linear equations)
.

An expression

a_{1}x_{1}+\dots+a_{n}x_{n}=b

where
a_{1},\dots,\,a_{n},\,b\in\{0,\,1\}
,
x_{1},\dots,\,x_{n}
are variables, and addition and multiplication are performed modulo
2
is called a
linear equation
. Fixing the order of variables, any linear equation may be viewed as a predicate that evaluates to
\top
if and only if the equation is satisfied. We say that a relation (/predicate)
\rho
may be represented as a
disjunction of linear equations
if and only if

\rho(x_{1},\dots,\,x_{n})=\left|\!\left|\begin{array}[]{c}\lambda_{1}(x_{1},\dots,\,x_{n})\\
\vdots\\
\lambda_{m}(x_{1},\dots,\,x_{n})\end{array}\right|\!\right|\coloneqq\lambda_{1}(x_{1},\dots,\,x_{n})\lor\dots\lor\lambda_{m}(x_{1},\dots,\,x_{n})

for some linear equations
\lambda_{1},\dots,\,\lambda_{m}
. The notation
\left|\!\left|-\right|\!\right|
will henceforth denote a relation formed as the disjunction of the listed relations, as shown above.

By
K\!R^{k}
, we denote the set of all relations from
R_{\{0,\,1\}}^{k}
that may be represented as disjunctions of linear equations.

The notation
K\!R^{k}
comes from the fact that these relations are precisely the
key relations
on
\{0,\,1\}
; see
[
42
]
for details. It was shown in
[
42
, Section 2.2]
that every relational clone is uniquely determined by the relations that may be represented as a disjunction of linear equations.

Theorem 2.10
.

[
42
, Section 2.2]

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
S\subseteq R_{\{0,\,1\}}^{k}
. Then

\left[S\right]_{\mathrm{pp}}\,=\left[\left[S\right]_{\mathrm{pp}}\,\cap K\!R^{k}\right]_{\mathrm{pp}}\,\,.

In Section
3
, we introduce an even smaller class of relations that retains this property in the context of quantified relational clones.

We conclude this section with the definition of dummy variables and a final convention to improve the overall clarity.

Definition 2.11
.

Let
A_{1},\dots,\,A_{n},\,B
be arbitrary sets, and let
f:A_{1}\times\dots\times A_{i}\times\dots\times A_{n}\to B
be some mapping. We say that the
i
-th variable of
f
is
dummy
if for all
(a_{1},\dots,\,a_{n})\in A_{1}\times\dots\times A_{n}
and for all
a_{i}^{\prime}\in A_{i}
, we have

f(a_{1},\dots,\,a_{i-1},\,a_{i},\,a_{i+1},\dots,\,a_{n})=f(a_{1},\dots,\,a_{i-1},\,a_{i}^{\prime},\,a_{i+1},\dots,\,a_{n})\,.

Notice that this definition also applies to relations, since we view them as predicates, that is, mappings to
\{\top,\,\bot\}
. Thus, we may speak of relations with or without dummy variables.

Convention 2.12
.

Since it is often sufficient to consider relations up to an order of variables and/or up to dummy variables, we sometimes simplify the notation by not distinguishing between relations and the formulas that define them. For example, instead of writing:

\rho(x,\,y,\,z)\coloneqq(x=0\lor y+z=1)\,,\quad S\coloneqq\left[\rho\right]_{\mathrm{qpp}}\,\,,

we use a more compact notation:

S\coloneqq\left[x=0\lor y+z=1\right]_{\mathrm{qpp}}\,\,.

3.
Canonical relations

We fix

in this section. We define the following class of relations.

Definition 3.1
.

A
k
-sorted relation without dummy variables is called

(c1)

for

(c2)

x^{i}=y^{i}\lor u^{j}=b
for

(c3)

x^{i}=y^{i}\lor u^{j}=v^{j}

for

,

(c4)

x^{i}+y^{i}=1
for

,

(c5)

x^{i}+y^{i}=u^{j}+v^{j}
for

(c6)

x^{s_{1}}+x^{s_{2}}+\dots+x^{s_{n}}=b

are pairwise distinct integers, and

(c7)

\left|\!\left|\begin{array}[]{r@{\mskip\medmuskip}c@{\mskip\medmuskip}l}x_{1}^{s_{1}}=b_{1}\lor x_{2}^{s_{1}}=b_{1}\,\lor\mskip 4.0mu plus 2.0mu minus 4.0mu&\cdots\hfil\mskip 4.0mu plus 2.0mu minus 4.0mu&\lor\,x_{m_{1}}^{s_{1}}=b_{1}\\

By

-sorted canonical relations.

Theorem
2.10
offers a convenient way to describe relational clones on a two-element domain. Analogously, the following theorems – arguably the main results of this paper – establish that canonical relations are sufficient to describe quantified relational clones and that they are, in some sense, the simplest relations with this property.

Let

\left[S\right]_{\mathrm{qpp}}\,=\left[\left[S\right]_{\mathrm{qpp}}\,\cap C\!R^{k}\right]_{\mathrm{qpp}}\,\,.

4.
Closedness criteria and Post’s lattice theorem

Although all quantified relational clones are uniquely described by the canonical relations they contain, two different sets of canonical relations do not necessarily generate different quantified relational clones. In this section, we introduce elementary operations, which are less cumbersome to work with than general qpp formulas, and we describe the properties that make them a valuable tool to decide whether two sets
S,\,S^{\prime}\subseteq C\!R^{k}
generate the same quantified relational clone or not. Using these tools, we subsequently describe all
1
-sorted quantified relational clones, which can be extended to a classification of relational clones, providing a concise proof of Post’s lattice theorem.

4.1.
Elementary operations

Definition 4.1

(Elementary operations)
.

Let

. We define the following

(eo1)

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

(1)

Assume a relation
\rho\in R_{\{0,\,1\}}^{k}
is defined by

\rho(x_{1},\dots,\,x_{n})=\exists y\,\rho_{1}(v_{1,1},\dots,\,v_{1,n_{1}})\land\dots\land\rho_{m}(v_{m,1},\dots,\,v_{m,n_{m}})

where
\rho_{1},\dots,\,\rho_{m}\in R_{\{0,\,1\}}^{k}
and

are symbols for variables. There are relations
\sigma_{1},\dots,\,\sigma_{l}\in\left[\rho_{1},\dots,\,\rho_{m}\right]_{(\mathrm{eo}1)-(\mathrm{eo}4)}\,
such that

\rho(x_{1},\dots,\,x_{n})=\sigma_{1}(x_{1},\dots,\,x_{n})\land\dots\land\sigma_{l}(x_{1},\dots,\,x_{n})\,.

(2)

Let

. For every
\rho\in\left[S\right]_{\mathrm{qpp}}\,
, there are relations
\sigma_{1},\dots,\,\sigma_{l}\in\left[S\right]_{(\mathrm{eo}1)-(\mathrm{eo}5)}\,
such that

\rho(x_{1},\dots,\,x_{n})=\sigma_{1}(x_{1},\dots,\,x_{n})\land\dots\land\sigma_{l}(x_{1},\dots,\,x_{n})\,.

Proof.

It is straightforward to see that there are relations
\rho_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\,\rho_{m}^{\prime}
defined from relations
\rho_{1},\dots,\,\rho_{m}
by rearranging, permuting variables, identifying variables, and appending/removing dummy variables, such that

\rho(x_{1},\dots,\,x_{n})=\exists y\,\rho_{1}^{\prime}(y,\,x_{1},\dots,\,x_{n})\land\dots\land\rho_{p}^{\prime}(y,\,x_{1},\dots,\,x_{n})\,\land\\
\land\rho_{p+1}^{\prime}(x_{1},\dots,\,x_{n})\land\dots\land\rho_{m}^{\prime}(x_{1},\dots,\,x_{n})\,,

where the first variable of relations
\rho_{1},\dots,\,\rho_{p}
is non-dummy. For the rest of the proof, we will write
\boldsymbol{x}
instead of

Noticing that the relations
\rho_{p+1}^{\prime},\dots,\,\rho_{m}^{\prime}
do not depend on the variable

\rho(\boldsymbol{x})=\rho_{p+1}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{x})\land\dots\land\rho_{m}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{x})\,\land\exists y\,\rho_{1}^{\prime}(y,\,\boldsymbol{x})\land\dots\land\rho_{p}^{\prime}(y,\,\boldsymbol{x})\,.

(4.1)

We now assume that

is trivial, and the case

can be interpreted as a composition of

with itself and subsequent identifications of variables. The following claim captures the core idea of the proof.

Claim 4.2.1
.

Let us denote

\displaystyle\tau(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\exists y\,\rho_{1}^{\prime}(y,\,\boldsymbol{x})\land\dots\land\rho_{p}^{\prime}(y,\,\boldsymbol{x})\,,

\displaystyle\tau^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\bigwedge_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,\,j\,\in\,E_{p}\\
i\,<\,j\end{subarray}}\exists z_{i,j}\,\rho_{i}^{\prime}(z_{i,j},\,\boldsymbol{x})\land\rho_{j}^{\prime}(z_{i,j},\,\boldsymbol{x})\,.

\tau(\boldsymbol{x})=\tau^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{x})

Proof of Claim
4.2.1
.

Fix
\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{a}\in\{0,\,1\}^{n}
. It is enough to prove that
\tau(\boldsymbol{a})=\top
if and only if
\tau^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{a})=\top
.

Assume
\tau(\boldsymbol{a})=\top

is then a witness for all existential quantifiers in
\tau^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{a})
, implying
\tau^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{a})=\top
.

Conversely, assume
\tau^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{a})=\top
and suppose

is not a witness for
y
in
\tau(\boldsymbol{a})
. This implies that
\rho_{q}^{\prime}(0,\,\boldsymbol{a})=\bot
for some

\tau^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{a})=\top

\exists z_{q,j}\,\rho_{q}^{\prime}(z_{q,j},\,\boldsymbol{a})\land\rho_{j}^{\prime}(z_{q,j},\,\boldsymbol{a})=\top

for every

∎

Using Claim

\rho(\boldsymbol{x})=\rho_{p+1}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{x})\land\dots\land\rho_{m}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{x})\,\land\bigwedge_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,\,j\,\in\,E_{p}\\
i\,<\,j\end{subarray}}\underbrace{\exists z_{i,j}\,\rho_{i}^{\prime}(z_{i,j},\,\boldsymbol{x})\land\rho_{j}^{\prime}(z_{i,j},\,\boldsymbol{x})}_{\text{composition of $\rho_{i}^{\prime}$ and $\rho_{j}^{\prime}$}}\,.

Finally, statement (2) follows from statement (1) and from the distributivity of universal quantifiers over conjunctions – specifically, by inductively eliminating all quantifiers from a general qpp formula.

Let

. Then
\left[S\right]_{(\mathrm{eo}1)-(\mathrm{eo}5)}\,\subseteq K\!R^{k}

As we have observed, working with each elementary operation, apart from (eo6), is straightforward and mechanical. While the computation of (eo6) is also mechanical in any given case, determining whether the resulting relation belongs to

Theorem 4.7
.

k\in\mathbb{N}

By Theorem

\rho(x_{1},\dots,\,x_{n})=\sigma_{1}(x_{1},\dots,\,x_{n})\land\dots\land\sigma_{m}(x_{1},\dots,\,x_{n})

Remark 4.8

(Checking for closedness)
.

Suppose that we need to decide if a set

•

Compute all joins in the lattice and check for closedness.

Each of these steps follows directly from the tools introduced in the previous section, mainly in Remark
4.5
and Remark

. For some details, we also point to the description of all quantified relational clones generated by a single canonical relation, as given in Table

and Theorem

∎

By the
\sPol
-
\Inv
Galois connection (Theorem

), quantified relational clones correspond to surjective clones. Thus, Theorem
4.9
also implicitly provides the classification of surjective clones.

Since the only non-surjective operations on
\{0,\,1\}
, up to arity, are the two constant operations, the classification of all clones – Post’s lattice theorem – follows as a direct corollary of Theorem

. We formalize this in the following remark.

Remark 4.10

(Post’s lattice theorem
[
24
,
25
]
)
.

Let
\mathfrak{0}
and
\mathfrak{1}
denote the unary constant operations returining
0
and
1
, respectively.

It follows directly from the definition that every clone is obtained by adding none, one, or both the constant operation to each surjective clone. That is, for each quantified relational clone
S
it is enough to consider the following (not necessarily distinct) clones

\Pol S\,,\quad\Clo(\Pol S\cup\{\mathfrak{0}\})\,,\quad\Clo(\Pol S\cup\{\mathfrak{1}\})\,,\quad\Clo(\Pol S\cup\{\mathfrak{0},\,\mathfrak{1}\})\,.

Moreover, this classification can be derived without explicit clone computations. By the well-known
\Pol
-
\Inv
Galois connection (see
[
14
,
4
,
5
]
or Corollary
6.9
), we obtain

\displaystyle\Inv\Pol S

\displaystyle=S\,,

\displaystyle\Inv\Clo(\Pol S\cup\{\mathfrak{0}\})

\displaystyle=S\cap\Inv\{\mathfrak{0}\}\,,

\displaystyle\Inv\Clo(\Pol S\cup\{\mathfrak{1}\})

\displaystyle=S\cap\Inv\{\mathfrak{1}\}\,,

\displaystyle\Inv\Clo(\Pol S\cup\{\mathfrak{0},\,\mathfrak{1}\})

\displaystyle=S\cap\Inv\{\mathfrak{0},\,\mathfrak{1}\}\,.

This follows from the fact that in the
\Pol
-
\Inv
connection, joins in the lattice of clones correspond to meets in the lattice of relational clones.

Thus, to classify all clones, it suffices to compute

S\,,\quad S\cap\Inv\{\mathfrak{0}\}\,,\quad S\cap\Inv\{\mathfrak{1}\}\,,\quad S\cap\Inv\{\mathfrak{0},\,\mathfrak{1}\}\,.

To simplify this even further, suppose
S=\left[T\right]_{\mathrm{qpp}}\,
for some
T\subseteq C\!R^{1}
. Then we claim:

Claim 4.10.1
.

\displaystyle S\cap\Inv\{\mathfrak{0}\}

\displaystyle=\left[\left[T\right]_{(\mathrm{eo}1)-(\mathrm{eo}5)}\,\cap\Inv\{\mathfrak{0}\}\right]_{(\mathrm{eo}6)}\,\,,

\displaystyle S\cap\Inv\{\mathfrak{1}\}

\displaystyle=\left[\left[T\right]_{(\mathrm{eo}1)-(\mathrm{eo}5)}\,\cap\Inv\{\mathfrak{1}\}\right]_{(\mathrm{eo}6)}\,\,,

\displaystyle S\cap\Inv\{\mathfrak{0},\,\mathfrak{1}\}

\displaystyle=\left[\left[T\right]_{(\mathrm{eo}1)-(\mathrm{eo}5)}\,\cap\Inv\{\mathfrak{0},\,\mathfrak{1}\}\right]_{(\mathrm{eo}6)}\,\,.

Proof.

We prove the first equality; the remaining cases are analogous. Theorem
4.3
gives the following equality:

S\cap\Inv\{\mathfrak{0}\}=\left[\left[T\right]_{(\mathrm{eo}1)-(\mathrm{eo}5)}\,\right]_{(\mathrm{eo}6)}\,\cap\Inv\{\mathfrak{0}\}\,.

Hence, it is enough to observe that

\left[\left[T\right]_{(\mathrm{eo}1)-(\mathrm{eo}5)}\,\right]_{(\mathrm{eo}6)}\,\cap\Inv\{\mathfrak{0}\}=\left[\left[T\right]_{(\mathrm{eo}1)-(\mathrm{eo}5)}\,\cap\Inv\{\mathfrak{0}\}\right]_{(\mathrm{eo}6)}\,\,.

This holds because a relation defined as a conjunction of other relations is preserved by
\mathfrak{0}
if and only if all the conjuncts are preserved by
\mathfrak{0}
.
∎

Thus, to obtain the full classification of
1
-sorted relational clones on
\{0,\,1\}
, i.e. the relational counterpart of the Post’s lattice, it suffices to:

•

Compute
\left[T\right]_{(\mathrm{eo}1)-(\mathrm{eo}5)}\,
for all sets
T\subseteq C\!R^{1}
described by Theorem

.

•

Compute the intersections of
\left[T\right]_{(\mathrm{eo}1)-(\mathrm{eo}5)}\,
with
\Inv\{\mathfrak{0}\}
,
\Inv\{\mathfrak{1}\}
, and
\Inv\{\mathfrak{0},\,\mathfrak{1}\}

This yields the classification of
1
-sorted relational clones, which correspond to

\Pol
-
\Inv
Galois connection, thereby providing a concise and straightforward proof of Post’s lattice theorem
[

,
25
]

5.1.
Embedding of the lattice

We assume the reader is familiar with the notion of partially ordered sets (shortly, posets) and begin by defining some perhaps less established concepts.

Definition 5.1

(Closure operator)
.

Let
A
be a nonempty set. An
algebraic closure operator
on
A
is a map
\cls:2^{A}\to 2^{A}
with the following properties

(cl1)

B\subseteq\cls B
for all
B\subseteq A
,

(cl2)

B\subseteq C\implies\cls B\subseteq\cls C
for all
B,\,C\subseteq A
,

(cl3)

\cls\cls B=\cls B
for all
B\subseteq A
,

(cl4)

\cls B=\bigcup_{C\,\subseteq\,B,\,|C|\,<\,\infty}\cls C
for all
B\subseteq A
.

A set
B\subseteq A
is called
closed
with respect to
\cls
if
B=\cls B
. We say a closed set
B
is
finitely generated
if there is a finite set
C\subseteq A
such that
B=\cls C
.

By
\mathfrak{L}_{\cls}(A)
we denote the set
\{B\subseteq A\mid B=\cls B\}
of all closed subsets of
A

Although, in general, closure operators are not required to satisfy the condition (cl4), we will only be concerned with algebraic closure operators. We may also notice that the set
\mathfrak{L}_{\cls}(A)
ordered by inclusion has a structure of a lattice, which, in particular, means that all the multi-sorted clones (or quantified relational clones, etc.) ordered by inclusion form a lattice; see Observation
5.3
. However, we do not assume nor require this property in any of the following claims.

Definition 5.2

(Order theory concepts I)

Let

For

\displaystyle\downset_{\leq}Q

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{p\in P\mid\exists\,q\in Q:p\leq q\}\,.\quad\text{(downset generated by $Q$)}

Let
(Q,\,\preceq)
be a poset. We say that

from

to

.

Let

,
(P_{2},\,\leq_{P_{2}})
be posets. We define the

on

by the following rule:

\left[(p_{1},\,p_{2})\preceq(p_{1}^{\prime},\,p_{2}^{\prime})\right]\iff\left[(p_{1}\leq_{P_{1}}p_{1}^{\prime})\text{ and }(p_{2}\leq_{P_{2}}p_{2}^{\prime})\right]\,.

To denote product posets, we will also use the notation

\prod_{i\,=\,1}^{n}(P_{i},\,\leq_{i})\coloneqq(P_{1},\,\leq_{1})\times\dots\times(P_{n},\,\leq_{n})\,.

5.2.
Inductive structure of the lattice

Let us recall that by the Galois connection (Theorem
2.8
), the posets

(\mathfrak{L}_{\left[-\right]^{k}_{\mathrm{qpp}}\,}(R_{\{0,\,1\}}^{k}),\,\subseteq)\quad\text{and}\quad(\mathfrak{L}_{\sClo^{k}}(\mathcal{O}_{\{0,\,1\}}^{k}),\,\subseteq)

are antiisomorphic, that is, there is a bijective, order-reversing mapping between them.

We will now demonstrate that the embedding described in Theorem
5.7
has implications not only for the lattice of quantified relational clones but also for the bigger lattice of (relational) clones. Moreover, as the following result suggests, this lattice has quite a simple inductive structure.

Theorem 5.8
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
k\geq 2
. The poset

(\mathfrak{L}_{\Clo^{k}}(\mathcal{O}_{\{0,\,1\}}^{k}),\,\subseteq)

embeds into the product poset

(\mathfrak{L}_{\sClo^{k}}(\mathcal{O}_{\{0,\,1\}}^{k}),\,\subseteq)\times\prod_{i\,=\,1}^{2k}\left((\mathfrak{L}_{\Clo^{k-1}}(\mathcal{O}_{\{0,\,1\}}^{k-1})\cup\{\emptyset\},\,\subseteq)\times(\{0,\,1\},\,\leq)\right)\,,

where
\leq
denotes the standard linear ordering on
\{0,\,1\}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a
k
-clone on
\{0,\,1\}
. For all
b\in\{0,\,1\}
and
i\in E_{k}
we denote

\mathcal{C}_{i,b}\coloneqq\{\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathcal{C}\mid\boldsymbol{f}^{(i)}\text{ is constant }b\}\,.

Notice that we may express
\mathcal{C}
as the following union of
2k+1
sets:

\mathcal{C}=\sClo^{k}\mathcal{C}\cup\bigcup_{i\,\in\,E_{k}}(\mathcal{C}_{i,0}\cup\mathcal{C}_{i,1})\,.

(
\ast
)

This decomposition highlights the main idea behind the embedding: since the
k
-operations in
\mathcal{C}_{i,b}
effectively reduce to
(k-1)
-operations, it is natural to expect the sets
\mathcal{C}_{i,b}
to correspond to
(k-1)
-clones. To formalize this intuition, we introduce the following notation and prove Claim
5.8.1
, which characterizes the structure of the sets
\mathcal{C}_{i,b}
.

Let
\mathcal{P}^{k-1}
be the
(k-1)
-clone generated by the empty set (i.e., the clone of
(k-1)
-projections), and let

\mathcal{C}_{i,b}^{\prime}\coloneqq\{(\boldsymbol{f}^{(1)},\dots,\,\boldsymbol{f}^{(i-1)},\,\boldsymbol{f}^{(i+1)},\dots,\,\boldsymbol{f}^{(k)})\mid\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathcal{C}_{i,b}\}\,.

Claim 5.8.1
.

For each
b\in\{0,\,1\}
and
i\in E_{k}
, the set
\mathcal{C}_{i,b}^{\prime}
is either empty, a
(k-1)
-clone, or a
(k-1)
-clone without all
(k-1)
-projections.

Proof of Claim
5.8.1
.

Assume that
\mathcal{C}_{i,b}^{\prime}
is nonempty. The following observations are sufficient to prove the claim:

(1)

The set
\mathcal{C}_{i,b}^{\prime}
is closed under compositions.

(2)

The set
\mathcal{C}_{i,b}^{\prime}\cup\mathcal{P}^{k-1}
is a clone.

(3)

If
\mathcal{C}_{i,b}^{\prime}
contains any
(k-1)
-projection, then it contains all
(k-1)
-projections.

As all the points are straightforward to verify, we provide just the following brief comments to clarify the key reasoning:

•

(1) follows from the fact that
\mathcal{C}_{i,b}
is itself closed under compositions.

•

(2) and (3) are direct consequences of (1) combined with the observation that the composition of
k
-projections with the
k
-operations in
\mathcal{C}_{i,b}
results in either a
k
-projection or a
k
-operation within
\mathcal{C}_{i,b}
.

Specifically, if
\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}_{i,b}^{\prime}
is a
(k-1)
-projection, then every
(k-1)
-projection can be obtained by composing the corresponding
k
-operation
\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathcal{C}_{i,b}
with suitable
k
-projections. ∎

Based on this claim, let us define the following mapping for each
b\in\{0,\,1\}
and
i\in E_{k}
:

\nu_{i,b}:(\mathfrak{L}_{\Clo^{k}}(\mathcal{O}_{\{0,\,1\}}^{k}),\,\subseteq)\to\left((\mathfrak{L}_{\Clo^{k-1}}(\mathcal{O}_{\{0,\,1\}}^{k-1})\cup\{\emptyset\},\,\subseteq)\times(\{0,\,1\},\,\leq)\right)

given by

\nu_{i,b}(\mathcal{C})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(\emptyset,0)\,,&\text{ if }\mathcal{C}_{i,b}^{\prime}\text{ is empty},\\
(\mathcal{C}_{i,b}^{\prime}\cup\mathcal{P}^{k-1},\,0)\,,&\text{ if }\mathcal{C}_{i,b}^{\prime}\text{ does not contain $(k-1)$-projections},\\
(\mathcal{C}_{i,b}^{\prime},\,1)\,,&\text{ if }\mathcal{C}_{i,b}^{\prime}\text{ contains $(k-1)$-projections}.\end{array}\right.

Notice that this mapping is well-defined, as the cases in the definition of
\nu_{i,b}
account for all possibilities described in Claim
5.8.1
. Furthermore, it is order-preserving because the definition of
\nu_{i,b}
ensures that
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{D}
implies
\mathcal{C}_{i,b}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{i,b}^{\prime}
, which in turn guarantees
\nu_{i,b}(\mathcal{C})\preceq\nu_{i,b}(\mathcal{D})
for all
k
-clones
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{D}
.

We now define the desired embedding:

\nu:\mathcal{C}\mapsto\left(\sClo^{k}\mathcal{C},\,\nu_{1,0}(\mathcal{C}),\,\nu_{1,1}(\mathcal{C}),\dots,\,\nu_{k,0}(\mathcal{C}),\,\nu_{k,1}(\mathcal{C})\right)\,.

It is clear that
\nu
is order-preserving. Finally, we observe that
\nu
is injective. Indeed, by the decomposition
(\ast)
and the definition of
\nu_{i,b}
, every
k
-clone
\mathcal{C}
is uniquely determined by its surjective part
\sClo^{k}\mathcal{C}
and the sets
\mathcal{C}_{i,b}
. Since
\nu_{i,b}
encodes the sets
\mathcal{C}_{i,b}
uniquely, the image
\nu(\mathcal{C})
completely determines
\mathcal{C}
, completing the proof.
∎

Corollary 5.9
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
k\geq 2
. The poset

(\mathfrak{L}_{\Clo^{k}}(\mathcal{O}_{\{0,\,1\}}^{k}),\,\subseteq)

embeds into the product poset

(2^{C\!R_{1-6}^{k}},\,\supseteq)\times(\mathfrak{L}_{\downset_{\preceq}}(\mathbb{N}_{0}^{2k}),\,\supseteq)\,\times\\
\times\prod_{i\,=\,1}^{2k}\left((\mathfrak{L}_{\Clo^{k-1}}(\mathcal{O}_{\{0,\,1\}}^{k-1})\cup\{\emptyset\},\,\subseteq)\times(\{0,\,1\},\,\leq)\right)\,,

\mathbb{N}_{0}^{2k}
, and

is the standard linear ordering on

We combine the results of Theorem
5.8
, Theorem
2.8
, and Theorem

.
∎

Although Theorem

and Corollary

Another key implication of this embedding is the fact that every
k
-clone is finitely generated. To observe this, we define a few more order theory concepts that allow us to relate a particular property of the lattice of clones to a structural property of the clones themselves.

Definition 5.10

(Order theory concepts II)
.

Let
(P,\,\leq)
be a poset. We say
P
satisfies the
ascending chain condition
(ACC) if for every ascending chain, that is, an infinite sequence
p_{1},\,p_{2},\,p_{3},\dots\in P
satisfying

p_{1}\leq p_{2}\leq p_{3}\leq\dots\,,

there is
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that
p_{m}=p_{n}
for all
n\geq m
.

Observation 5.11
.

Let
(Q,\,\preceq)
and
(P,\,\leq)
be posets.

(1)

If
(Q,\,\preceq)
embeds into
(P,\,\leq)
, and
(P,\,\leq)
satisfies ACC, then
(Q,\,\preceq)
satisfies ACC.

(2)

If
(Q,\,\preceq)
and
(P,\,\leq)
satisfy ACC, then
(Q,\,\preceq)\times(P,\,\leq)
satisfies ACC.

(3)

If the set
P
is finite, then
(P,\,\leq)
satisfies ACC.

The following lemma is a canonical result in algebra and a key component of the presented proof of Taimanov’s theorem. It establishes a connection between ACC – a structural property of the poset of closed sets – with the fact that every set is finitely generated. For further details, we refer the reader to
[
1
, Chapter 6]
,
[
15
]
, or any standard text on Noetherian modules, rings, or algebras.

While this result is often stated under the Axiom of Choice (or some of its weaker variants), the following formulation avoids the need for AC by assuming countability. If countability is not assumed, AC becomes necessary; see
[
15
]
.

Lemma 5.12
.

Let
A
be a nonempty countable set and let
\cls
be an algebraic closure operator on
A
. The poset
(\mathfrak{L}_{\cls}(A),\,\subseteq)
satisfies ACC if and only if every element of
\mathfrak{L}_{\cls}(A)
is finitely generated with respect to
\cls
.

Intending to use Observation
5.11
along with Theorem
5.7
, we present one final lemma before directly proceeding to prove Taimanov’s theorem.

Lemma 5.13
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\preceq
be the product ordering on
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{m}
. The poset
(\mathfrak{L}_{\downset_{\preceq}}(\mathbb{N}_{0}^{m}),\,\supseteq)
satisfies ACC.

Theorem 5.14

(Taimanov)
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Every
k
-clone on
\{0,\,1\}
is finitely generated with respect to
\Clo^{k}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
5.12
, it suffices to show that the poset
(\mathfrak{L}_{\Clo^{k}}(\mathcal{O}_{\{0,\,1\}}^{k}),\,\subseteq)
satisfies ACC. We prove this by induction on
k
.

For
k=1
, we have ACC by Post’s lattice theorem. Alternatively, this follows from the fact that
(\mathfrak{L}_{\Clo^{1}}(\mathcal{O}_{\{0,\,1\}}^{1}),\,\subseteq)
embeds into the poset

(\mathfrak{L}_{\sClo^{1}}(\mathcal{O}_{\{0,\,1\}}^{1}),\,\subseteq)\times(2^{\{\mathfrak{0},\,\mathfrak{1}\}},\,\subseteq)\,,

which, in turn, embeds into

(2^{C\!R_{1-6}^{1}},\,\supseteq)\times(\mathfrak{L}_{\downset_{\preceq}}(\mathbb{N}_{0}^{2}),\,\supseteq)\times(2^{\{\mathfrak{0},\,\mathfrak{1}\}},\,\subseteq)\,.

The first embedding is a consequence of the fact that there are only two non-surjective

-operations
\mathfrak{0}
and
\mathfrak{1}
(up to dummy variables), so any

-clone is determined by a surjective
1
-clone and a subset of

. The second embedding is then given by Theorem

2.8
.

By Observation
5.11
, the poset
(\mathfrak{L}_{\Clo^{1}}(\mathcal{O}_{\{0,\,1\}}^{1}),\,\subseteq)
satisfies ACC since it embeds into a product of two finite posets and a poset
(\mathfrak{L}_{\downset_{\preceq}}(\mathbb{N}_{0}^{2}),\,\supseteq)
, which satisfies ACC by Lemma
5.13
.

Now assume that

, and suppose that the poset
(\mathfrak{L}_{\Clo^{k-1}}(\mathcal{O}_{\{0,\,1\}}^{k-1}),\,\subseteq\nolinebreak)
satisfies ACC (induction hypothesis).

By Corollary

, the poset
(\mathfrak{L}_{\Clo^{k}}(\mathcal{O}_{\{0,\,1\}}^{k}),\,\subseteq)
embeds into

(2^{C\!R_{1-6}^{k}},\,\supseteq)\times(\mathfrak{L}_{\downset_{\preceq}}(\mathbb{N}_{0}^{2k}),\,\supseteq)\,\times\\
\times\prod_{i\,=\,1}^{2k}\left((\mathfrak{L}_{\Clo^{k-1}}(\mathcal{O}_{\{0,\,1\}}^{k-1})\cup\{\emptyset\},\,\subseteq)\times(\{0,\,1\},\,\leq)\right)\,.

To complete the proof, we note the following:

•

(2^{C\!R_{1-6}^{k}},\,\supseteq)
is finite,

•

(\mathfrak{L}_{\downset_{\preceq}}(\mathbb{N}_{0}^{2k}),\,\supseteq)

•

(\mathfrak{L}_{\Clo^{k-1}}(\mathcal{O}_{\{0,\,1\}}^{k-1})\cup\{\emptyset\},\,\subseteq)

•

(\{0,\,1\},\,\leq)

By Observation

∎

Convention 6.1

The first part of the theorem, that is, the fact that the mappings

\displaystyle S

Let

where arguments of

Given an

6.1.
Closed sets of operations

Theorem 6.5
.

Let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{A}^{k}
. Then

\displaystyle\Pol\Inv\mathcal{F}

\displaystyle=\Clo\mathcal{F}\,,

\displaystyle\sPol\Inv\mathcal{F}

\displaystyle=\sClo\mathcal{F}\,.

Proof.

We will only prove that
\Pol\Inv\mathcal{F}=\Clo\mathcal{F}
as
\sPol\Inv\mathcal{F}=\sClo\mathcal{F}
is an immediate corollary obtained by considering only the surjective
k
-operations.

First, we may notice that
\Clo\Pol S=\Pol S
for any set
S\subseteq R_{A}^{k}
. Indeed, all
k
-projections preserve all relations, and if some
k
-operations preserve a certain relation, their composition preserves such relation as well. Due to this, we have the inclusion

\Pol\Inv\mathcal{F}\supseteq\Clo\mathcal{F}

as
\Clo\mathcal{F}
is the smallest
k
-clone containing
\mathcal{F}
and
\Pol\Inv\mathcal{F}
is some
k
-clone containing
\mathcal{F}
.

For the converse inclusion, we consider any
\boldsymbol{f}\in\Pol\Inv\mathcal{F}
. Let
n
be the arity of
\boldsymbol{f}
. Let us prove the following claim.

Claim 6.5.1
.

\widetilde{\Clo\mathcal{F}}_{n}\in\Inv\mathcal{F}
.

Proof of Claim
6.5.1
.

Any
\boldsymbol{a}_{1},\dots,\,\boldsymbol{a}_{m}\in\widetilde{\Clo\mathcal{F}}_{n}
correspond to some
n
-ary
k
-operations
\boldsymbol{h}_{1},\dots,\,\boldsymbol{h}_{m}\in\Clo\mathcal{F}
, thus, for any
m
-ary
k
-operation
\boldsymbol{g}\in\mathcal{F}
, the tuple
\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{a}_{1},\dots,\,\boldsymbol{a}_{m})
corresponds to the composition of
\boldsymbol{g}
with
\boldsymbol{h}_{1},\dots,\,\boldsymbol{h}_{m}
, which is already contained in
\Clo\mathcal{F}
as it is a clone. Thus
\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{a}_{1},\dots,\,\boldsymbol{a}_{m})\in\widetilde{\Clo\mathcal{F}}_{n}
.
∎

Now, applying
\Pol
on both sides of
\widetilde{\Clo\mathcal{F}}_{n}\in\Inv\mathcal{F}
, we get

\boldsymbol{f}\in\Pol\Inv\mathcal{F}\subseteq\Pol\{\widetilde{\Clo\mathcal{F}}_{n}\}\,.

In particular, this means that
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{f}}\in\widetilde{\Clo\mathcal{F}}_{n}
as
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{f}}
is just the
k
-operation
\boldsymbol{f}
evaluated at the tuples in
\widetilde{\Clo\mathcal{F}}_{n}
corresponding to the projections. Therefore,
\boldsymbol{f}\in\Clo\mathcal{F}
by Observation
6.4
. This finishes the proof of the inclusion

\Pol\Inv\mathcal{F}\subseteq\Clo\mathcal{F}\,.

∎

6.2.
Closed sets of relations/predicates

Lemma 6.6
.

Let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{A}^{k}
. Then
\Inv{\mathcal{F}}
is a relational clone, that is

\left[\Inv\mathcal{F}\right]_{\mathrm{pp}}\,=\Inv\mathcal{F}\,.

Moreover, if
\mathcal{F}
contains only surjective operations, then
\Inv{\mathcal{F}}
is a quantified relational clone, that is

\left[\Inv\mathcal{F}\right]_{\mathrm{qpp}}\,=\Inv\mathcal{F}\,.

Proof.

Since empty relations and equalities are preserved by every surjective
k
-operation we only need to check that
\Inv\mathcal{F}
is closed under the elementary operations (Definition
4.1
and Lemma
4.2
). The arguments are mostly simple, we outline the basic ideas. Let
\rho,\,\rho^{\prime}\in\Inv\mathcal{F}
.

•

Closedness under (eo1) and (eo2) is clear.

•

If
\sigma
is defined from
\rho
by identification of variables (eo3), then
\sigma\in\Inv\mathcal{F}
since operations are applied coordinate-wise.

•

If
\sigma
is defined from
\rho
and
\rho^{\prime}
by conjunction (eo6), we have
\sigma=\rho\cap\rho^{\prime}
. Since both
\rho
and
\rho^{\prime}
are invariant under all
k
-operations in
\mathcal{F}
, applying any such
k
-operation to tuples from the intersection
\rho\cap\rho^{\prime}
will result in a tuple that also belongs to the intersection. Thus,
\sigma=\rho\cap\rho^{\prime}\in\Inv\mathcal{F}
.

•

If
\sigma
is defined from
\rho
by existential quantification of a variable, then
\sigma\in\Inv\mathcal{F}
since operations are applied coordinate-wise. It follows that
\Inv\mathcal{F}
is closed under composition (eo4).

This finishes the proof of
\left[\Inv\mathcal{F}\right]_{\mathrm{pp}}\,=\Inv\mathcal{F}
.

Now assume that all operations in
\mathcal{F}
are surjective. The main reason why
\Inv\mathcal{F}
is closed under universal quantification (eo5) is as follows:

•

Let
\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathcal{F}
be an
n
-ary operation,
\rho\in\Inv\mathcal{F}
, and

\sigma(x_{1},\dots,\,x_{m})=\forall y\,\rho(y,\,x_{1},\,x_{2},\dots,\,x_{m})\,.

If
\boldsymbol{a}_{1},\dots,\,\boldsymbol{a}_{m}\in\sigma
, then

\begin{pmatrix}b_{1}\\
\boldsymbol{a}_{1}^{(1)}\\
\boldsymbol{a}_{1}^{(2)}\\
\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{a}_{1}^{(m)}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}b_{2}\\
\boldsymbol{a}_{2}^{(1)}\\
\boldsymbol{a}_{2}^{(2)}\\
\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{a}_{2}^{(m)}\end{pmatrix},\dots,\,\begin{pmatrix}b_{n}\\
\boldsymbol{a}_{n}^{(1)}\\
\boldsymbol{a}_{n}^{(2)}\\
\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{a}_{n}^{(m)}\end{pmatrix}\in\rho

for all
b_{1},\,b_{2},\dots,\,b_{n}\in A
. Since
\boldsymbol{f}
is surjective operations which preserves
\rho
, this also means that

\begin{pmatrix}b\\
f(\boldsymbol{a}_{1}^{(1)},\,\boldsymbol{a}_{2}^{(1)},\dots,\,\boldsymbol{a}_{n}^{(1)})\\
f(\boldsymbol{a}_{1}^{(2)},\,\boldsymbol{a}_{2}^{(2)},\dots,\,\boldsymbol{a}_{n}^{(2)})\\
\vdots\\
f(\boldsymbol{a}_{1}^{(m)},\,\boldsymbol{a}_{2}^{(m)},\dots,\,\boldsymbol{a}_{n}^{(m)})\end{pmatrix}\in\rho

for all
b\in A
. Therefore,

\begin{pmatrix}f(\boldsymbol{a}_{1}^{(1)},\,\boldsymbol{a}_{2}^{(1)},\dots,\,\boldsymbol{a}_{n}^{(1)})\\
f(\boldsymbol{a}_{1}^{(2)},\,\boldsymbol{a}_{2}^{(2)},\dots,\,\boldsymbol{a}_{n}^{(2)})\\
\vdots\\
f(\boldsymbol{a}_{1}^{(m)},\,\boldsymbol{a}_{2}^{(m)},\dots,\,\boldsymbol{a}_{n}^{(m)})\end{pmatrix}\in\sigma\,.

Hence,
\Inv\mathcal{F}
is closed under universal quantification of a variable (eo5).∎

Lemma 6.7
.

Let
S\subseteq R_{A}^{k}
and let
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then
\widetilde{\Pol S}_{n}\in\left[S\right]_{\mathrm{pp}}\,
.

Proof.

We will verify that
\widetilde{\Pol S}_{n}=\sigma
, where

\sigma(x_{0,\dots,\,0}^{1},\dots,\,x_{l-1,\dots,\,l-1}^{1},\dots,\,x_{0,\dots,\,0}^{k},\dots,\,x_{l-1,\dots,\,l-1}^{k})=\\
=\bigwedge_{\begin{subarray}{c}\rho\,\in\,S\\
\boldsymbol{a}_{1},\dots,\,\boldsymbol{a}_{n}\,\in\,\rho\end{subarray}}\rho\left(x_{\boldsymbol{a}_{1}^{(1)},\,\boldsymbol{a}_{2}^{(1)},\dots,\,\boldsymbol{a}_{n}^{(1)}}^{i_{\rho,\,1}},\dots,\,x_{\boldsymbol{a}_{1}^{(m_{\rho})},\,\boldsymbol{a}_{2}^{(m_{\rho})},\dots,\,\boldsymbol{a}_{n}^{(m_{\rho})}}^{i_{\rho,\,m_{\rho}}}\right)\,.

Here, for
\rho\in S
, the arity of
\rho
is denoted by
m_{\rho}
, and the sort of its
j
-th variable by
i_{\rho,\,j}
. Let us remark that although this conjunction is infinite when
S
is infinite, the domain is finite, so we can restrict to a finite number of terms without changing result.

The reason behind the equality
\widetilde{\Pol S}_{n}=\sigma
is as follows. We may consider the tuples in
\sigma
to uniquely correspond to images of
n
-ary
k
-operations in some
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{A}^{k}
according to Remark
6.3
; that is,
\sigma=\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{n}
. Each term of the form

\rho\left(x_{\boldsymbol{a}_{1}^{(1)},\,\boldsymbol{a}_{2}^{(1)},\dots,\,\boldsymbol{a}_{n}^{(1)}}^{i_{\rho,\,1}},\dots,\,x_{\boldsymbol{a}_{1}^{(m_{\rho})},\,\boldsymbol{a}_{2}^{(m_{\rho})},\dots,\,\boldsymbol{a}_{n}^{(m_{\rho})}}^{i_{\rho,\,m_{\rho}}}\right)

then ensures that, for every
\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathcal{F}
, we have

\boldsymbol{f}\left(\boldsymbol{a}_{1},\dots,\,\boldsymbol{a}_{n}\right)\in\rho\,.

This means that
\mathcal{F}
contains exactly all the

, and thus

.
∎

Lemma 6.8
.

Let

and let

\rho\in\left[\widetilde{\Pol S}_{n}\right]_{\mathrm{pp}}\,

For the purpose of this proof, we assume that the tuples of every relation are ordered so that a

be the arity of
\rho

Using the notation from Definition

, we let
\boldsymbol{p}_{n,i}^{k}
denote the
n
-ary projection

i
-th coordinate. We now define the following
(k\cdot l^{n})
-ary relation of size
n
:

\theta=\begin{pmatrix}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{p}_{n,1}^{k}}&\widetilde{\boldsymbol{p}_{n,2}^{k}}&\cdots&\widetilde{\boldsymbol{p}_{n,n}^{k}}\end{pmatrix}\,.

Observe that the rows of

are structured as

•

-th row of
\rho

-th row of

•

the

Equivalently, this means that

\rho=\begin{pmatrix}\text{$r_{1}$-th row of $\theta$}\\
\text{$r_{2}$-th row of $\theta$}\\
\vdots\\
\text{$r_{m}$-th row of $\theta$}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(
\ast
)

which can also be formally expressed by the following pp formula:

\rho(x_{1},\dots,\,x_{m})=\\

\rho(x_{1},\dots,\,x_{m})=\\

Lemma 6.10
.

Let
S\subseteq R_{A}^{k}
and let
\rho\in R_{A}^{k}
. Then

\bigcap_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\,\in\,\left[S\right]_{\mathrm{pp}}\,\\
\rho\,\subseteq\,\sigma\end{subarray}}\sigma=\{\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{b}_{1},\dots,\,\boldsymbol{b}_{n})\mid\boldsymbol{b}_{1},\dots,\,\boldsymbol{b}_{n}\in\rho,\,\boldsymbol{f}\in\Pol S\}\,.

Proof.

By definition, the intersection on the left-hand side is the least relation in
\left[S\right]_{\mathrm{pp}}\,
that contains
\rho
. To show that the right-hand side also satisfies this property, we denote

\tau=\{\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{b}_{1},\dots,\,\boldsymbol{b}_{n})\mid\boldsymbol{b}_{1},\dots,\,\boldsymbol{b}_{n}\in\rho,\,\boldsymbol{f}\in\Pol S\}

and notice the following.

•

Since
\Pol S
contains projections, we immediately get that
\rho\subseteq\tau
.

•

\tau
is preserved by every operation in
\Pol S
. To see this, let
\boldsymbol{f}\in\Pol S
be an
r
-ary operation, and take
\boldsymbol{c}_{1},\dots,\,\boldsymbol{c}_{r}\in\tau
. We need to show that
\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{c}_{1},\dots,\,\boldsymbol{c}_{r})\in\tau
.

By the definition of
\tau
, for each
\boldsymbol{c}_{i}
, there exist some
q_{i}
-ary operation
\boldsymbol{g}_{i}\in\Pol S
and tuples
\boldsymbol{b}_{i,1},\dots,\,\boldsymbol{b}_{i,q_{i}}\in\rho
such that

\boldsymbol{c}_{i}=\boldsymbol{g}_{i}(\boldsymbol{b}_{i,1},\dots,\,\boldsymbol{b}_{i,q_{i}})\,.

Since
\rho
is finite, say
\rho=\{\boldsymbol{a}_{1},\dots,\,\boldsymbol{a}_{m}\}
, for each
\boldsymbol{g}_{i}
there exists an
m
-ary operation
\boldsymbol{g}_{i}^{\prime}\in\Pol S
such that

\boldsymbol{c}_{i}=\boldsymbol{g}_{i}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{a}_{1},\dots,\,\boldsymbol{a}_{m})\,.

This follows from the fact that
\Pol S
is closed under composition: the operation
\boldsymbol{g}_{i}^{\prime}
is simply the composition of
\boldsymbol{g}_{i}
with
m
-ary projections onto appropriate coordinates.

Thus, we have

\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{c}_{1},\dots,\,\boldsymbol{c}_{r})=\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{g}_{1}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{a}_{1},\dots,\,\boldsymbol{a}_{m}),\dots,\,\boldsymbol{g}_{r}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{a}_{1},\dots,\,\boldsymbol{a}_{m}))=\boldsymbol{h}(\boldsymbol{a}_{1},\dots,\,\boldsymbol{a}_{m})\,,

where
\boldsymbol{h}\coloneqq\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{g}_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\,\boldsymbol{g}_{r}^{\prime})\in\Pol S
by closedness under composition. Thus, by the definition of
\tau
, we have
\boldsymbol{h}(\boldsymbol{a}_{1},\dots,\,\boldsymbol{a}_{m})\in\tau
. By Corollary
6.9
, it follows that
\tau\in\Inv\Pol S=\left[S\right]_{\mathrm{pp}}\,
.

•

Assume there is
\tau^{\prime}\in\left[S\right]_{\mathrm{pp}}\,
such that
\rho\subseteq\tau^{\prime}
. By Corollary
6.9
, we have
\left[S\right]_{\mathrm{pp}}\,=\Inv\Pol S
, so applying any
\boldsymbol{f}\in\Pol S
to elements of
\tau^{\prime}
yields another element of
\tau^{\prime}
. In particular, for every
\boldsymbol{b}_{1},\dots,\,\boldsymbol{b}_{n}\in\rho\subseteq\tau^{\prime}
we have
\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{b}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{b}_{n})\in\tau^{\prime}
, so we conclude that
\tau\subseteq\tau^{\prime}
.

Therefore,
\tau
is the least relation in
\left[S\right]_{\mathrm{pp}}\,
containing
\rho
, as required.
∎

Theorem 6.11

Let

\Inv\sPol(S)=\left[S\right]_{\mathrm{qpp}}\,\,.

Proof.

The inclusion

\rho^{\prime}(x_{1}^{s_{1}},\dots,\,x_{n}^{s_{n}},\,y_{1}^{1},\dots,\,y_{l}^{1},\dots,\,y_{1}^{k},\dots,\,y_{l}^{k})\,.

\tau\subseteq\tau^{\prime}

\tau\in\left[S\right]_{\mathrm{qpp}}\,

By the definition of

Lemma
5.12
.

Let
A
be a nonempty countable set and let
\cls
be an algebraic closure operator on
A
. The poset
(\mathfrak{L}_{\cls}(A),\,\subseteq)
satisfies ACC if and only if every element of
\mathfrak{L}_{\cls}(A)
is finitely generated with respect to
\cls
.

Proof.

(
\implies
) Let us assume that
(\mathfrak{L}_{\cls}(A),\,\subseteq)
satisfies ACC. Let us pick
B\in\mathfrak{L}_{\cls}(A)
; we will show that
B
is finitely generated.

Since
A
is countable, we may put
B=\{a_{1},\,a_{2},\,a_{3},\dots\}
. We then assume the following infinite chain in
(\mathfrak{L}_{\cls}(A),\,\subseteq)
:

\cls\{a_{1}\}\subseteq\cls\{a_{1},\,a_{2}\}\subseteq\cls\{a_{1},\,a_{2},\,a_{3}\}\subseteq\dots\subseteq B\,.

Since
\mathfrak{L}_{\cls}(A)
satisfies ACC, there is
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\cls\{a_{1},\dots,\,a_{n}\}=\cls\{a_{1},\dots,\,a_{n},\,a_{n+1}\}=\cls\{a_{1},\dots,\,a_{n},\,a_{n+1},\,a_{n+2}\}=\dots

Thus

\bigcup_{l\,\in\,n}\cls\{a_{1},\dots,\,a_{l}\}=\cls\{a_{1},\dots,\,a_{n}\}\,,

and since

B=\bigcup_{l\,\in\,\mathbb{N}}\{a_{1},\dots,\,a_{l}\}\subseteq\bigcup_{l\,\in\,\mathbb{N}}\cls\{a_{1},\dots,\,a_{l}\}\subseteq\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}C\,\subseteq\,B\\
|C|\,\leq\,\infty\end{subarray}}\cls C=\cls B=B\,,

we have shown that
B
is finitely generated by the set
\{a_{1},\dots,\,a_{n}\}
.

(
\impliedby
) Let us assume that every element of
(\mathfrak{L}_{\cls}(A),\,\subseteq)
is finitely generated with respect to
\cls
. Let us consider an ascending chain

B_{1}\subseteq B_{2}\subseteq B_{3}\subseteq\dots\,,

where
B_{1},\,B_{2},\,B_{3},\dots\in\mathfrak{L}_{\cls}(A)
. In order to show that this chain terminates, we consider the set

B=\bigcup_{l\,\in\,\mathbb{N}}B_{l}\,.

We may notice that
B=\cls B
. Indeed, for any
a\in\cls B
, we have

a\in\cls B=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}C\,\subseteq\,B\\
|C|\,\leq\,\infty\end{subarray}}\cls C\,,

thus, there is a finite set
\{b_{1},\dots,\,b_{n}\}\subseteq B
such that
a\in\cls\{b_{1},\dots,\,b_{n}\}
. For each
i\in E_{n}
, the element
b_{i}
is contained within some
B_{q_{i}}
; thus, for
q=\max\{q_{1},\dots,\,q_{n}\}
, we have

a\in\cls\{b_{1},\dots,\,b_{n}\}\subseteq\cls B_{q}=B_{q}\subseteq B\,.

Hence,
B
is a closed set, which is finitely generated by the assumption. Let us put
B=\cls\{a_{1},\dots,\,a_{m}\}
for some

. Then, for each

, the element

B=\cls\{a_{1},\dots,\,a_{m}\}\subseteq\cls B_{p}=B_{p}\subseteq B_{p+1}\subseteq B_{p+2}\subseteq\dots\subseteq B\,.

Definition 7.2

(Order theory concepts III)
.

Let
(P,\,\leq)
be a poset. A set
O\subseteq P
such that
\forall\,p,\,q\in O:(p\nleq q)\land(q\nleq p)
is called
antichain
.

Let
Q\subseteq P
. An element
p\in Q
is called
minimal
in
Q
if there is no
q\in Q
such that
q\leq p
.

We say
P
is
well-founded
if every nonempty subset of
P
has a minimal element.

Lemma 7.3
.

Let
(P,\,\leq)
be a poset. If
P
is well-founded, then, for every upset
U\subseteq P
, there is an antichain
O\subseteq P
such that
U=\upset_{\leq}O
.

Proof.

Let
U
be an upset in
P
and let
O\subseteq U
be the set of minimal elements of
U
.
O
is nonempty since
P
is well-founded and
O
is an antichain since the comparability of any two distinct elements would contradict the minimality of one of them. We will prove
U=\upset_{\leq}O
.

Since
O\subseteq U
, we have
\upset_{\leq}O\subseteq\upset_{\leq}U=U
. Conversely, if
x\in U
, we consider the set
U_{x}=\{u\in U\mid u\leq x\}
. This set is nonempty (
x\in U_{x}
) and thus has a minimal element
m_{x}\in U_{x}
since
P
is well-founded. Moreover
m_{x}\in O
– if not, there is
m^{\prime}\in U\setminus U_{x}
such that
m^{\prime}\lneq m_{x}
, but then
m^{\prime}\lneq m_{x}\leq x
and thus, by definition,
m^{\prime}\in U_{x}
. This contradicts the minimality of
m_{x}
. Hence,
x\in\upset_{\leq}O
since
x\geq m_{x}\in O
, which concludes the proof of “
\supseteq
”.
∎

Lemma
5.13
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\preceq
be the product ordering on
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{m}
. The poset
(\mathfrak{L}_{\downset_{\preceq}}(\mathbb{N}_{0}^{m}),\,\supseteq)
satisfies ACC.

Proof.

The poset
(\mathfrak{L}_{\downset_{\preceq}}(\mathbb{N}_{0}^{m}),\,\supseteq)
of downsets with dual ordering satisfies ACC if and only if the poset
(\mathfrak{L}_{\upset_{\preceq}}(\mathbb{N}_{0}^{m}),\,\subseteq)
of upsets satisfies ACC.
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{m}
is clearly well-founded, thus, by Lemma
7.3
, every upset is generated by some antichain in
\mathbb{N}^{m}
with respect to
\upset_{\preceq}
. Moreover,
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{m}
is countable, hence, we only need to show that every antichain of
(\mathbb{N}_{0}^{m},\,\preceq)
is finite due to Lemma

.

We will finish the proof by induction on
m
. Every antichain in
\mathbb{N}_{0}
is finite since
\mathbb{N}_{0}
is linearly ordered.

Assume that every antichain in
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{m-1}
is finite (induction hypothesis). For the contradiction, let
O\subseteq\mathbb{N}_{0}^{m}
be an infinite antichain.

Let us pick arbitrary
(n_{1},\dots,\,n_{m})\in O
. Since
O
is an antichain, for every element
(n_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\,n_{m}^{\prime})\in O\setminus\{(n_{1},\dots,\,n_{m})\}
there is at least one

such that
n_{i}^{\prime}\lneq n_{i}
. This observation lets us decompose

as follows:

\displaystyle O

\displaystyle=\{(n_{1},\dots,\,n_{m})\}\cup\bigcup_{i\,\in\,E_{m}}\{(n_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\,n_{m}^{\prime})\in O\mid n_{i}^{\prime}\lneq n_{i}\}=

\displaystyle=\{(n_{1},\dots,\,n_{m})\}\cup\bigcup_{i\,\in\,E_{m}}\bigcup_{j\,\in\,E_{n_{i}-1}}\{(n_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\,n_{m}^{\prime})\in O\mid n_{i}^{\prime}=j\}\,.

This union is finite while
O
is infinite; thus, there are

and
j\in E_{n_{i}-1}
such that the set
\{(n_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\,n_{m}^{\prime})\in O\mid n_{i}^{\prime}=j\}
is infinite. Then, the set

\{(n_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\,n_{i-1}^{\prime},\,n_{i+1}^{\prime},\dots,\,n_{m}^{\prime})\mid(n_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\,n_{i-1}^{\prime},\,j,\,n_{i+1}^{\prime},\dots,\,n_{m}^{\prime})\in O\}

is an infinite antichain in
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{m-1}
which is the desired contradiction.
