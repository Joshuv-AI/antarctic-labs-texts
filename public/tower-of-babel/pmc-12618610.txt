Self-organizing group structure through rewiring for collective decision-making in evolving environments
Authors: Poong Oh, Andrew Schauf
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
DeGroot dynamics, a prototypical model of opinion formation under social influence, tends to yield biased consensus in centralized groups. However, such a consensus can be accurate if competent individuals occupy central positions and exert disproportionate influence. Focusing on groups with stable memberships that engage in iterative consensus formation across a sequence of issues, we propose social network rewiring as a self-organization mechanism that identifies competent members and positions them centrally, thereby empowering them to make decisions on behalf of the group. Numerical experiments show that the effects of rewiring on consensus accuracy and structural adaptability oppose each other depending on its search scope. Local rewiring—where agents search for new connections within their immediate neighborhoods—progressively concentrates influence among competent members and enhances consensus accuracy but reduces the group’s adaptability to evolving environments, modeled as temporal shifts in individual competence. In contrast, global rewiring—where agents may connect to any member of the group—preserves adaptability but significantly undermines accuracy. A unique contribution of this study lies in its novel approach to information integration for collective decision-making, shifting the focus from the intrinsic information individuals possess to information about agents’ competence, acquired from local interactions and communicated through rewiring.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23634-3.

Introduction

Classical models of collective decision-making suggest that groups can make more accurate decisions than individual members by aggregating individual opinions formed independently based on their private information1–3. Although individual opinions tend to contain non-trivial errors due to incomplete information they possess, these errors cancel each other out when opinions are aggregated using simple rules, such as the majority rule1 and mean aggregation3. Furthermore, the accuracy of collective decisions monotonically increases with group size, so sufficiently large groups almost always reach accurate decisions, achieving “collective intelligence”4–8. However, these models assume that individuals form their opinions independently and retain them over time, without influencing or being influenced by others, overlooking the social dynamics that shape real-world decision-making processes.

To incorporate peer influence among group members into collective decision-making processes, DeGroot9 proposed a model wherein individuals iteratively update their opinions by adopting the average opinion of their neighbors (i.e., naïve learning10–12). The model shows that a group eventually reaches a consensus as long as the social network of members remains connected. However, the consensus reached under these dynamics is generally not equal to a simple aggregation of initial opinions13. Instead, it is determined by a weighted mean of initial opinions, where the weights are proportional to the centralities of corresponding members in the group, representing their relative influence on the consensus. In a centralized group, where influence is concentrated among a few members, DeGroot dynamics therefore produces consensus driven by these central members, and errors in their opinions are not adequately corrected, biasing the resulting consensus toward their initial opinions rather than equally reflecting all opinions within the group14,15. The accuracy of this biased consensus, compared to that of a simple aggregation, depends on the competence of the central individuals who disproportionately shape the outcome.

Previous studies have examined the impact of central members on consensus accuracy. For example, Golub and Jackson10 and Acemoglu et al.16 argue that DeGroot dynamics can still yield sufficiently accurate collective decisions in the absence of “excessively” central agents (i.e., those modeled as hubs in star graphs), showing that the discrepancy between group consensus and a simple aggregation of individual opinions becomes negligible as the group size grows infinitely large. However, these studies (also Buechel et al.17 and Rauhut and Lorenz18) underestimate the impact of central members by overlooking the fact that the marginal gains in collective accuracy diminish as group size increases19. Therefore, DeGroot dynamics in centralized groups yield a more “biased” consensus than simple aggregation, even in the absence of excessively central agents20.

Although the term “bias” usually carries negative connotations21, a “biased” consensus that significantly deviates from the mean of individual opinions is not necessarily inaccurate. In fact, it can be maximally accurate if the most competent members occupy central positions, exerting greater influence while marginalizing the influence of less competent others22,23. While such a hierarchical structure—particularly its disproportionate distribution of influence among members—may appear to conflict with democratic and egalitarian ideals24–26, it aligns closely with Plato’s vision of an “epistemic autocracy” as depicted in The Republic27. In this idealized dictatorship, authority is concentrated among a select few—namely, the “philosopher kings”—who are deemed uniquely qualified to govern by virtue of their superior knowledge and judgment. However, a fundamental question arises regarding Plato’s ideal: How can a group correctly identify the most competent individuals and effectively position them in central roles when no central authority has been established to guide the process from the outset?

To address this question, we propose social network rewiring as a self-organization mechanism that effectively reallocates influence among members, potentially enhancing the accuracy of the group consensus reached through DeGroot dynamics. In our model, a group of agents—each with a distinct level of competence and connected to other agents who serve as its information sources—reaches consensus iteratively over a sequence of issues. Agents’ competence is not directly observable but can be inferred from the correctness of their initial opinions on a given issue, which is probabilistically determined by their competence and only revealed after the group reaches a consensus on the issue. Upon reaching a consensus on an issue, agents rewire their connections by replacing their existing sources that provided incorrect opinions on the issue with alternative ones, before proceeding to the next issue. New information sources are selected uniformly at random, either from the information sources of existing sources (i.e., local rewiring) or from the entire group (i.e., global rewiring).

Furthermore, we introduce environmental change as an external factor that causes the nature of issues to evolve over time, shaping which skills and knowledge are most relevant and should be prioritized for accurate decision-making. For example, during the COVID-19 pandemic, the focus of public health concerns shifted continually as the crisis unfolded28–30. Initial efforts prioritized containment and testing. As new variants emerged and vaccines became available, attention shifted toward vaccination strategies, healthcare capacity, and combating misinformation. Later, the focus expanded to include long-term societal challenges, such as economic recovery. At each stage, different expertise and knowledge became relevant for accurate decision-making. To capture this dynamic, our model operationalizes environmental change as shifts in individual competencies from one issue to the next, with the extent of change quantified by the correlation between individuals’ competencies across successive issues. In evolving environments, competence profiles evolve over time, forcing the adaptive replacement of previously competent agents with those whose competence better aligns with the new issue.

We expect that the effects of rewiring vary depending on the search scope within which individuals seek new information sources. Local rewiring—selecting new sources from immediate neighborhoods—is expected to effectively centralize group structure around competent members and enhance consensus accuracy in stable environments. This is because local rewiring enables agents to exploit competence-related information encoded in the group structure, particularly in the form of out-degree, which guides their decisions about whom to connect to. Agents with high out-degrees serve as information sources for many others and, therefore, are more frequently discovered as potential sources of information through local rewiring than those with low out-degrees31–33. Moreover, out-degree can serve as a proxy for competence, as incompetent members are unlikely to maintain high out-degrees due to their frequent incorrect opinions. Together, these mechanisms would facilitate the identification of competent members and their placement into central positions, thereby enhancing consensus accuracy.

However, local rewiring is expected to be less effective—and potentially detrimental—in changing environments, as the increasingly centralized group structure may progressively narrow the pool of new information sources available for rewiring, constraining the group’s capacity to adapt to evolving demands. Moreover, the reliability of out-degree as a signal of competence is likely to be undermined, since it reflects past competence that may no longer be relevant under current conditions. In contrast, global rewiring is expected to serve as a complementary mechanism, enabling agents to explore new information sources beyond their immediate neighborhoods. This broader search scope can preserve structural flexibility, promote diversity in sources of competence, and reduce the risk of premature centralization around previously competent agents. Nevertheless, global rewiring is also expected to have limitations. Because any member, regardless of their competence, can be selected as an information source, the group structure may remain decentralized over time, hindering the concentration of influence among genuinely competent members. Consequently, incompetent agents may continue to exert influence on consensus formation, undermining its accuracy.

Numerical experiments were conducted to examine the effects of rewiring on consensus accuracy and group structure under varying environmental conditions. The results confirm the contrasting effects of rewiring depending on its search scope. Furthermore, an intermediate search scope is identified as achieving an optimal balance between the search efficiency enhanced by local rewiring and the structural flexibility supported by global rewiring. This balance ensures consistently high consensus accuracy across varying environmental conditions. Notably, this optimal search scope leads to a hierarchical group structure with an approximate power-law degree distribution. In this structure, a few competent members occupy central positions but remain subject to replacement at any time. Meanwhile, the influence of most other agents is suppressed. However, these agents remain viable as alternative sources and can gain influence when their competence aligns with emerging needs.

An important contribution of this study is the extension of the DeGroot model along the temporal dimension. Previous models implicitly assume one-time consensus formation within temporary assemblies of strangers (e.g., jury panels), which disband immediately after a decision is made. While many of these models account for structural changes that can alter the course of opinion dynamics, they generally assume that such changes occur within a single round of consensus formation. In contrast, the current model assumes groups with fixed memberships and introduces an iterative process of consensus formation over a sequence of issues, each requiring different competencies for accurate decisions. Structural changes take place between issues through rewiring based on the inferred competence of agents based on their previous performance.

This perspective builds on and complements recent advances in the opinion dynamics and network dynamics literatures. Extensions of classical models, such as the Deffuant and Hegselmann–Krause frameworks, have incorporated features like circular opinions, repelling mechanisms, cooperation costs, and higher-order interactions, showing how network topology and strategic trade-offs can sustain disagreement or foster coevolution of consensus and cooperation34–36. More broadly, studies of temporal and coevolutionary networks have demonstrated that even short-lived connections can leave persistent effects on cooperation and that environmental variability can fundamentally shape the evolution of collective strategies37,38. Taken together, this body of work underlines two themes central to the present study: structural dynamics can have enduring effects beyond immediate interactions, and environmental conditions strongly influence how individuals adapt their strategies. These insights set the stage for our framework, which extends the DeGroot model temporally to capture path dependence in network evolution and to highlight competence-related information as a distinct driver of collective outcomes.

The implication of our framework is twofold. First, the substantial extension along the temporal dimension offers a foundation for investigating the path-dependent nature of collective dynamics, in which existing group structures both enable and constrain future structural changes39–42. Second, and more importantly, the framework offers a novel perspective on information integration within groups, focusing on a distinct type of information available to members. Previous models primarily focus on the information that individuals inherently possess about an issue, exploring how their opinions based on their private information are updated through social interactions and finally integrated to form collective decisions43. In contrast, the current model shifts the focus to the information that individuals acquire through interactions, specifically regarding who is competent on a given issue, and how this competence-related information is communicated to others. In our model, connections between agents function as signals created by individuals to encode information about their neighbors’ competence, which is then broadcast to others beyond the agents directly involved in the connections, guiding their decisions about whom to connect to. In this way, individuals—particularly those who are incompetent—can still contribute to collective decision-making, not by vocalizing their own opinions, but by endorsing more competent members and guiding others to do the same, effectively empowering those competent members to make decisions on their behalf.

The delayed revelation of opinion correctness ensures that competence inference is inherently retrospective, relying solely on observed outcomes from the previous round of consensus formation. After the correctness of initial opinions is revealed, agents update their connections based on the observed performance of their information sources through rewiring process. More specifically, each agent—if any of its information sources provided incorrect opinions—selects one of these sources uniformly at random and replaces it with a new source, preserving the total number of information sources \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k$$\end{document}. It is important to note that, although the removal of an existing source is uniformly random among those who offered incorrect opinions, agents with high out-degrees are more likely to lose connections when they provide incorrect opinions. This is because they serve as information sources for a larger number of agents and are therefore selected for removal more frequently. As a result, incompetent agents are unlikely to maintain high out-degrees for a long period of time due to their frequent incorrect opinions. This suggests that out-degrees can serve as a proxy for competence. This performance-driven rewiring process ensures that influence is redistributed in a decentralized and adaptive manner, embedding competence-related information in the evolving network structure and shaping consensus outcomes over time.

The impact of rewiring on the group structure is expected to vary depending on its search scope. In the case of global rewiring (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta =1$$\end{document}), any agent can be selected as a new information source with equal probability and thus gain influence over group consensus, regardless of their competence. Therefore, the group structure is expected to remain decentralized over time under a strong propensity for global rewiring. In contrast, with local rewiring (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta =0$$\end{document}), agents with higher out-degrees would be more likely to be chosen as new sources, thereby reinforcing their current centralities and influence. Similar to the process of selection for removal, agents with higher out-degrees, serving as information sources for many other agents, are more likely to be discovered and selected as potential sources of information31–33. This disproportionate selection through local rewiring is functionally akin to preferential attachment52 and the vertex copying process53, in the sense that it leads to the “rich get richer” phenomenon, where nodes with more connections are more likely to attract even more connections. The previous models eventually result in a power-law degree distribution, as they assume that networks grow over time in both the numbers of nodes and edges54. However, the current model keeps both the number of agents (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n$$\end{document}) and the total number of connections (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$nk$$\end{document}) fixed throughout the rewiring process. This constraint allows only the redistribution of connections rather than their addition or elimination. As such, local rewiring is expected to produce a maximally centralized network over time, forming a clique of a few agents to whom all others are connected, with no direct connections among the peripheral agents (i.e., a star-like graph55).

Environmental changes

We incorporate environmental changes as external factors that cause the nature of issues to evolve over time, thereby altering the relevance of individual competence for accurate decision-making on subsequent issues. In our model, these changes are operationalized as shifts in individual competence \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${p}_{i}$$\end{document}, with their magnitude quantified by the correlation coefficient between individual competencies on successive issues:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho =\text{corr}\left({\mathbf{p}}_{\tau }, {\mathbf{p}}_{\tau +1}\right)=\frac{{\mathbf{p}}_{\tau }\cdot {\mathbf{p}}_{\tau +1}}{\Vert {\mathbf{p}}_{\tau }\Vert \Vert {\mathbf{p}}_{\tau +1}\Vert }$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathbf{p}}_{\tau }$$\end{document} represents the competence vector on issue \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau$$\end{document}. The correlation coefficient \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho$$\end{document} captures the degree of environmental stability: higher values of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho$$\end{document} indicate greater stability in individual competence. To simulate environmental change, we generate a new competence vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathbf{p}}_{\tau +1}$$\end{document} as a function of the current competencies \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathbf{p}}_{\tau }$$\end{document} and the stability parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho$$\end{document}. The generative algorithm is detailed in the following subsection.

We considered two scenarios of environmental change: continual change, involving gradual shifts in individual competence in every round, and intermittent change, characterized by periodic but more intense disruptions that cause abrupt shifts in competence, while remaining stable between these disruptions (i.e., punctuated equilibria56–58). In both cases, the group must adaptively replace central members whose competencies have become less relevant with those better aligned with emerging issues to restore and maintain consensus accuracy.

Measures

To examine the impacts of rewiring with varying search scopes at different environmental conditions, we measure three aspects of the group after it has reached a consensus on each issue \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau$$\end{document}: (1) the accuracy of consensus that the group reaches on a given issue through DeGroot dynamics, (2) the extent to which individual agents’ influence is concentrated, and (3) the frequency with which agents’ influence is redistributed through rewiring.

Consensus accuracy (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${P}_{G}$$\end{document}) is measured by a weighted mean of individual competence:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${P}_{G}=\sum_{i}^{n}{v}_{i}{p}_{i}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${v}_{i}$$\end{document} represents agent i’s relative influence on group consensus, determined by its position in the group. This weight is measured by the agent’s normalized eigenvector centrality, ensuring that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sum }_{i}^{n}{v}_{i}=1$$\end{document}.

Influence concentration is measured using global reaching centrality (GRC) proposed by Mones et al.59 GRC quantifies how influence is distributed in a directed network by computing the average difference between each node’s reaching centrality and the maximum reaching centrality in the network:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$GRC=\frac{\sum_{i}^{n}\left({c}_{r}^{\text{max}}-{c}_{r}\left(i\right)\right)}{n-1}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${c}_{r}\left(i\right)$$\end{document} is the proportion of nodes reachable from node \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$i$$\end{document} (i.e., the reach centrality), \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${c}_{r}^{\text{max}}$$\end{document} is the maximum reach centrality in the network, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n$$\end{document} is the number of nodes. GRC ranges from 0 to 1, with a higher value indicating a more centralized structure, where influence is concentrated among a few nodes. A GRC value of 0 signifies a fully decentralized network in which all nodes have equal reach centrality, meaning that influence is evenly distributed and no single node holds a disproportionately high level of influence.

Influence reallocation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${R}_{f}$$\end{document}) is measured by tracking how frequently the relative influence \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${v}_{i}$$\end{document} shifts between successive issues \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau -1$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau$$\end{document}. Specifically, we define:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${R}_{f}=\frac{|R\left(\tau \right)|}{n}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R\left(\tau \right)=\left\{i|{v}_{i}\left(\tau -1\right)\ne {v}_{i}\left(\tau \right)\right\}$$\end{document} represents the set of agents whose influence has changed since the previous issue. A higher \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${R}_{f}$$\end{document} value reflects more frequent reallocations of agents’ influence through rewiring, resulting in rapid transformations in the group structure, whereas \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${R}_{f}=0$$\end{document} indicates the absence of structural changes between successive issues, leading to a stagnant state.

When only local rewiring is allowed (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta =0$$\end{document}, the top row), the group rapidly centralizes around competent members (i.e., green nodes), as agents with high out-degrees (illustrated here as large nodes) attract even more connections and become increasingly more influential, while incompetent agents (i.e., orange and red nodes) are progressively marginalized. By \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau =40$$\end{document}, a clique of five agents has formed, maximally concentrating influence among these clique members, while all remaining agents are connected to the clique members with no connections among themselves (i.e., a star-like graph). At this point, group consensus is dictated exclusively by the clique members, yielding a high accuracy (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${P}_{G}=0.758$$\end{document}). This outcome reflects the fact that the clique members’ competence has been effectively “validated” by their consistently high out-degrees over time, suggesting that out-degree can function as a reliable signal of competence.

However, the results indicate that the clique members are not necessarily the most competent individuals in the group. In the current experimental setting, even the most competent agents (e.g., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${p}_{100}=0.895$$\end{document}) may occasionally provide incorrect opinions during the early phase. These early errors hinder their ability to retain sufficient out-degrees, thereby reducing their chances of attracting new connections and accumulating influence in the later phase. This path-dependent process of influence accumulation may compromise the reliability of out-degree as a signal of competence, introducing the risk of overlooking genuinely competent agents through local rewiring. Therefore, the members in a clique formed through local rewiring may not always represent the most competent individuals in the group. This, in turn, suggests that consensus accuracy could be further improved by replacing the clique members with more competent agents. However, the results show that once a clique is established, no further changes in group structure or consensus accuracy occur. This is because all agents are connected to the clique members as their shared information sources, leaving no alternative sources available for replacement through local rewiring.

The expansion of search scope by allowing occasional global rewiring facilitates the reallocation of influence among agents. However, this enhanced flexibility does not necessarily lead to an improvement in consensus accuracy. When \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta =0.125$$\end{document} (the second row in Fig. 5), consensus accuracy increases until \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau =20$$\end{document}, but begins to decline thereafter. This suggests that replacement through global rewiring allows less competent (yellow nodes) or even incompetent (orange and red nodes) to be selected as new information sources. Furthermore, their centralities, as well as their influence, are likely reinforced through local rewiring, resulting in a decline in consensus accuracy during the later phase.

When only global rewiring is allowed (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta =1$$\end{document}, the bottom row in Fig. 5), the network structure remains highly decentralized throughout the dynamics (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$GRC\le 0.111$$\end{document}). During the early phase (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau \le 20$$\end{document}), the replacement of agents who provided incorrect opinions with randomly selected others leads to an improvement in group consensus. However, no significant improvement occurs thereafter, as randomly selected agents are not necessarily more competent than those they replace. Frequent random replacements allow incompetent agents to easily gain their connections and influence, which limits the influence of competent agents, hindering further improvement in consensus accuracy.

However, the resulting consensus accuracy remains considerably lower than the theoretical maximum (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${P}_{G}^{\text{max}}=0.848$$\end{document}), which could be achieved if influence were fully concentrated among the top five most competent members, whose competences are drawn from a Beta distribution with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha =\beta =3$$\end{document}. This implies that the most central members in the resulting group structure are not necessarily the most competent in the group, and that consensus accuracy could be further improved by identifying more competent members and replacing those at the center. Nevertheless, no reallocation of influence occurs after \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau =54$$\end{document} (Fig. 6c), implying that the group has lost structural flexibility and become trapped in a suboptimal state.

On the other hand, with exclusive global rewiring (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta =1$$\end{document}, indicated by the red lines in Fig. 6), consensus accuracy improves during the early phase but soon stabilizes at the lowest level \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${P}_{G}=0.582$$\end{document} (Fig. 6a). The group structure remains highly decentralized most of the time (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$GRC\le 0.079$$\end{document}, Fig. 6b), failing to effectively exclude the influence of incompetent agents. Additionally, influence reallocation occurs to nearly all agents in every round (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${R}_{f}\ge 0.969$$\end{document}, Fig. 6c), preventing stable leadership from emerging. By narrowing the search scope (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta =0.25$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta =0.5$$\end{document}, indicated by the yellow and orange lines, respectively, in Fig. 6), the group structure becomes more centralized, which leads to a substantial improvement in consensus accuracy. This reaffirms the effectiveness of local rewiring in improving consensus accuracy.

It is noteworthy that consensus accuracy exhibits a non-monotonic pattern within an intermediate range of search scopes, specifically \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0<\theta \le 0.125$$\end{document} (indicated by dark and light green lines in Fig. 6a). During the early phase (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau \le 20$$\end{document}, Fig. 7a–c), a consistent pattern emerges across all search scopes: changes in influence occur in direct proportion to competence, as indicated by ascending curves. More competent agents gain greater influence, while less competent agents experience correspondingly larger losses. This reallocation of influence toward competent agents leads to a substantial improvement in consensus accuracy, with the effect being most pronounced with exclusive local rewiring (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta =0$$\end{document}, indicated by the purple circles). Consensus accuracy eventually reaches a plateau, with only minor random fluctuations observed during the later phase (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau \ge 300$$\end{document}, Fig. 7l).

However, within the range of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0<\theta \le 0.125$$\end{document} (the dark and light green circles in Fig. 7), the direction of reallocation reverses around \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau =40$$\end{document}: competent agents begin to lose their influence, which is reallocated to incompetent agents, resulting in a subsequent decline in consensus accuracy. This reversal arises from the interplay between two mechanisms: (1) the strong centralizing force of local rewiring, and (2) the occasional but continual random replacement by global rewiring. When \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta$$\end{document} is small, local rewiring dominates and rapidly drives the network toward a highly centralized structure, concentrating influence among a few competent agents. However, because global rewiring is not entirely absent, competent central members are occasionally replaced by randomly selected agents of average competence (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\overline{p }=0.5$$\end{document}), who are unlikely to be more competent than those they replace. Each such random replacements is rare and has negligible immediate impacts, since competent agents seldom provide incorrect opinions and local rewiring remains the more frequent replacement mechanism within this range of search scopes. Nevertheless, random replacements continue to occur until less competent agents occupy central positions, at which point the average competence of these positions falls to the level expected under random selection. Therefore, the cumulative effect leads to a significant long-term decline in consensus accuracy, as shown in Fig. 6a. This reversed reallocation of influence and its negative effect on consensus accuracy become more pronounced as the search scope approaches \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta =0$$\end{document}. As \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta$$\end{document} nears zero, local rewiring produces a highly centralized group structure with fewer central positions, which are eventually filled by randomly selected agents through global rewiring. This implies that minimal but nonzero global rewiring significantly undermines the benefits of concentrated competence by local rewiring.

By contrast, when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta \ge 0.25$$\end{document}, the two mechanisms remain in play, but their balance differs. Although local rewiring still dominates, global rewiring occurs frequently enough that less competent or even incompetent members can enter and persist in central positions. This prevents the sharp initial surge in consensus accuracy observed at smaller \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta$$\end{document}. Moreover, when such less competent central members are replaced through global rewiring, the new sources are typically of average competence \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\overline{p }$$\end{document}, so random replacement does not necessarily reduce accuracy and may occasionally improve it. As a result, consensus accuracy stabilizes at an intermediate level rather than following a rise-and-fall trajectory.

The impacts of environmental changes

We consider two scenarios of environmental changes: continual changes, which cause gradual shifts in individual competence in every round, and intermittent changes, which occur periodically with greater intensity, leading to abrupt shifts in competence while remaining stable between these disruptions.

Continual changes

This advantage arises because a wider search scope allows individual agents to explore new information sources beyond their immediate neighborhoods, increasing the likelihood of identifying newly competent sources and expediting the redistribution of influence. Thus, global rewiring emerges as a critical mechanism for preserving adaptability and ensuring resilience under dynamic environmental conditions.

The optimal search scope

Thus far, the results indicate that the impacts of rewiring on group structure and consensus accuracy oppose each other depending on its search scope. With local rewiring, the group evolves into a highly centralized structure in which influence is concentrated among a few central members. While this structure effectively amplifies competence in stable environments, it struggles to adapt to change. The continued dominance of previously competent agents—whose influence persists even after their competence becomes irrelevant—ultimately leads to a decline in consensus accuracy. In contrast, global rewiring supports a more decentralized structure, ensuring greater adaptability by allowing for frequent turnover among influential agents. This enhances the group’s resilience to shifts in competence. However, the same decentralized nature makes it harder to regulate the influence of less competent agents, potentially leading to inefficiencies or a dilution of overall competence.

These contrasting effects suggest the existence of an optimal balance between local and global rewiring—one that enhances consensus accuracy while preserving adaptability in the face of environmental change. Discussion

Under DeGroot dynamics, a centralized group with a disproportionate distribution of influence tends to generate a biased consensus, deviating from an unweighted aggregation of individual opinions, as the influence of peripheral members is overshadowed by a few central individuals. However, unless the primary objective of collective decision-making is merely to identify the most representative opinion within the group, this bias can be beneficial. It can steer the group toward a more accurate collective decision, especially when the central positions are occupied by competent members, thereby marginalizing the influence of incompetent others. This underscores that the key determinant of consensus accuracy is not the extent of centralization per se, but rather the adequate alignment between individuals’ competence and their positions within the group22,23. The challenge, then, lies in how a group can accurately assess individuals’ competence and position them accordingly, especially in the absence of preexisting central authority to guide the process.

The current findings suggest that rewiring can function as a self-organization mechanism, effectively centralizing the group structure around competent individuals and producing a biased yet more accurate consensus. Numerical experiments show that the effects of rewiring vary significantly depending on its search scope, highlighting both its potential and limitations in optimizing group structure. Local rewiring, with a narrow search scope, effectively concentrates influence among competent members, thereby enhancing consensus accuracy in stable environments. However, this structure struggles to adapt to changing environmental conditions, causing a decline in consensus accuracy over time. In contrast, global rewiring sustains a more decentralized and adaptable structure by continually redistributing influence, allowing the group to adjust as the competence of individuals changes. Yet this adaptability comes at the cost of consensus accuracy, since the influence of incompetent members is not sufficiently restricted. An intermediate search scope achieves a balance between these opposing effects, fostering both adaptability and a more effective distribution of influence, resulting in a consensus accuracy that remains robust across various environmental conditions. Notably, rewiring with an optimal search scope stabilizes the group structure into a near power-law network, where a few competent individuals exert greater influence, while most others have little to no influence. However, this structure ensures adaptability, as peripheral members remain available for replacement if the competence of central members becomes irrelevant due to environmental changes.

A key contribution of this study is its emphasis on repeated and continual consensus formation processes within groups with stable memberships, marking a significant departure from previous models. Many previous models, particularly those based on the DeGroot framework, conceptualize consensus formation as isolated, one-off, event, disconnected from both prior and subsequent group decisions. That is, they implicitly assume that groups disband immediately upon reaching consensus. While this approach is useful for modeling decision-making in ad hoc groups, it overlooks the ongoing, path-dependent nature of decision-making in groups with fixed compositions, where existing influence structures constrain future adaptations and may lead to structural inertia39–42,60. In contrast, our model substantially extends the temporal dimension of the analysis, focusing on a sequence of consensus formations across multiple issues within groups that maintain fixed membership and enduring structures. Such groups are common in the real world, arguably more so than temporary assemblies of strangers. Examples include institutional and formal groups, such as legislative bodies deliberating on multiple bills throughout their terms61, journal editorial boards reviewing a continuous stream of submissions amid shifting academic priorities and evolving disciplinary trends62,63, corporate boards making strategic decisions in response to economic uncertainty64, and even informal groups like long-standing friend circles deciding on venues and activities for their gatherings, with preferences that may change as members grow older. By capturing these iterative and continual processes under the constraints of past decisions and established structures, our model provides a foundation for further investigation into the long-term dynamics of collective decision-making, offering insights into how group structures evolve over time in response to changing environments.

Most importantly, the current model introduces a novel approach to the integration of information for collective decision-making, highlighting the critical role of the information acquired and communicated throughout the process. Previous models largely focus on the information individuals inherently possess as the main “ingredient” for collective decisions, expressed through their opinions or judgments. While a simple aggregation of individual opinions preserves the total amount of information available within the group, peer influence often homogenizes opinions (e.g., reaching a consensus), inadvertently excluding the information embedded in marginalized opinions from the decision-making process. This reduction of available information can be further accelerated by structural changes based on various factors, including opinion similarity20,46,65,66, payoffs from connections67–69, trust levels70,71 or dispositional attributes like stubbornness16 and self-confidence17. Such information loss biases collective decisions, moving them away from the most representative opinion that encapsulates all available information. Such conclusions rest on an implicit assumption that each piece of information that individuals possess, though limited and incomplete, is equally contributive to collective decision-making.

However, our model departs from this assumption by allowing some individuals to have better access to more relevant information than others and, therefore, be more competent. Under this modified assumption, simply aggregating all the information with equal weight may obscure or dilute the unique contributions of those who are better informed, hindering further improvements of the accuracy of collective decisions. Instead, effective decision-making depends on the group’s ability to identify and elevate the input of its most competent members, ensuring that the most relevant insights exert a disproportionate—yet justified—influence on the collective outcome. What matters more, therefore, is not merely what each member knows about a given issue, but who is better informed and more capable of making accurate decisions. In our model, information about others’ competence is acquired by inferring it from their past performance and encoded by maintaining connections to them in subsequent rounds. Therefore, out-degrees—the accumulation of such connections—reflect individuals’ competence, which has been “cross-validated” over time by their neighbors. These out-degrees then function as social signals that guide others in deciding whom to connect to. As such decisions cascade through the network, competence-related information spreads more widely, increasing the visibility of competent individuals and reinforcing their centrality within the group. This self-reinforcing dynamic, facilitated by iterative rewiring over time, serves to centralize the group structure around its most competent members, ultimately enhancing the accuracy of collective decisions.

Our findings suggest that competence-related information should be recognized as a distinct dimension within broader theories of information aggregation. For example, classical models of information cascades11,72,73 demonstrate that when individuals face uncertainty, they often rely on the observed actions of others, sometimes producing herd behavior that overrides private information once a trend gains momentum. Our framework parallels this mechanism in that agents exploit structural cues—connections encoding others’ competence—to guide their decisions. Yet it departs from standard cascade models in an important way: while cascades rely on actions alone to transmit information, our model emphasizes how network rewiring actively shapes and redistributes influence, embedding competence-related information into the group’s decision process. In this sense, rewiring functions as a structural mechanism of cascade formation, where endorsements by less competent members amplify the visibility and impact of more competent ones. A similar distinction arises when comparing our approach to Bayesian learning models, which conceptualize individuals as rational learners updating probabilistic beliefs about the state of the world by integrating private signals with social observations10,74. In contrast, our model shifts the object of inference from issues to agents: rather than learning about an external state, individuals learn who is competent and communicate that information structurally through the network. This reframing highlights an alternative pathway for collective intelligence: groups can reach accurate decisions not only by aggregating distributed information about issues, but also by aggregating dispersed signals about people.

In our model, the effectiveness of rewiring in optimizing group structure for DeGroot dynamic depends on the reliability of out-degrees as signals of competence, which are, in turn, contingent upon several factors considered in our model: the accuracy of competence inference, the search scope of rewiring, and the variability of environmental conditions the group faces. First, decisions to remove existing connections are made based on the correctness of expressed opinions—which is probabilistically determined by competence—rather than direct observation of competence itself. Therefore, individuals may falsely infer competence from coincidentally correct opinions or, conversely, misjudge genuinely competent individuals as incompetent due to occasional errors. The rewiring process explored in this study can be seen as meritocratic in the sense that it concentrates influence among a few competent members, based on their successful track records of having their opinions proven “correct,” thereby empowering them to lead or influence collective decisions. However, a key difference lies in how this concentration of influence occurs. Whereas meritocratic systems typically rely on externally imposed hierarchical structures or institutional mechanisms to elevate individuals, our model is entirely self-organizing: influence emerges organically through decentralized, local interactions, without any preexisting authority or top-down power structure. This distinction highlights how leadership and authority can effectively arise without formal authority, instead driven by accumulated judgments and peer recognition—aligning, in part, with the democratic ideal of legitimacy grounded in collective endorsement. Still, the resulting concentration of influence may appear to conflict with democratic and egalitarian principles of collective decision-making, which emphasize equal participation and inclusive engagement from all members, typically achieved through unweighted aggregation of their voices.

However, this should not be interpreted to suggest that the rewiring process, or its real-world equivalent, always improves consensus accuracy or that it is inherently superior to alternative mechanisms of information integration. The current model does not fully account for several important factors that may affect the dynamics of consensus formation, and these factors should be explored further in future research.

One such factor is the potential for strategic or manipulative rewiring, in which a small but well-coordinated subgroup deliberately builds and maintains connections to one or more of its members with the intent to amplify their influence and visibility beyond what their actual competence would merit. This mirrors how minority groups can be misperceived as majority75, changing or even reversing prevailing social norms76,77 Such behavior can trigger a cascade effect15,78, whereby the targeted individuals gain disproportionate centralities in subsequent rounds without proper validation, ultimately enabling the subgroup to secure dominance in the decision-making process. By incorporating this form of strategic behavior into the model, future research can explore several critical questions: Under what conditions might even a small, strategically aligned subgroup act as “kingmakers,” tipping the balance of decision-making power in their favor? To what extent can the system detect and resist manipulative rewiring? And what forms of self-correcting mechanisms can effectively limit the influence of strategically positioned but unqualified members?

Another important extension of the current model could involve relaxing the assumption that agents sever connections to inaccurate information sources immediately after a single instance of error. In real-world decision-making contexts, individuals often exhibit a degree of tolerance or inertia79, opting to maintain existing connections unless presented with consistent evidence of incompetence over time. To capture this behavioral nuance, the model can be augmented with a memory-length parameter—defined as the threshold indicating the number of consecutive inaccuracies required before an agent initiates a rewiring action. Introducing this temporal dimension at the individual level would allow for a more refined analysis of the conditions under which agents reconstruct their informational neighborhoods. It would also enable investigations into how varying levels of epistemic patience, confirmation thresholds, or error accumulation influence the long-term effectiveness of rewiring in uncertain or rapidly changing environments. This modification would further facilitate the assessment of trade-offs between individual responsiveness and group-level adaptability.
