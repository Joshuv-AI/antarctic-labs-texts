Semantics of Division for Polynomial Solvers
Christopher W. Brown, United States Naval Academy

Abstract
How to handle division in systems that compute with logical formulas involving what would otherwise be polynomial constraints over the real numbers is a surprisingly difficult question. This paper argues that existing approaches from both the computer algebra and computational logic communities are unsatisfactory for systems that consider the satisfiability of formulas with quantifiers or that perform quantifier elimination. To address this, we propose the notion of the fair-satisfiability of a formula, use it to characterize formulas with divisions that are well-defined , meaning that they adequately guard divisions against division by zero, and provide a translation algorithm that converts a formula with divisions into a purely polynomial formula that is satisfiable if and only if the original formula is fair-satisfiable. This provides a semantics for division with some nice properties, which we describe and prove in the paper.



1
Introduction

The fundamental problem considered in this paper is how
occurrences of division in what would otherwise be first-order
formulas in elementary real algebra should be handled.
Specifically, we consider this question from the perspective of
tools from
symbolic computing and real algebraic geometry, which assume

polynomial
constraints — an assumption violated by divisions
with non-constant denominators.
The easiest approach, of course,
is to simply disallow division. However, that ship seems to have
sailed: not only do users ask to use division in input formulas, but
common tools like Maple and Mathematica allow division in their inputs
for polynomial solvers, and
the SMT-LIB allows division in its Non-linear Real Arithmetic (NRA) theory.
Unfortunately, what supporting
division entails, and even what division means, is not straightforward.
Moreover, solutions that make sense for one community may differ from
what is sensible for another community — a point which is taken up
more fully later in this section. This
paper examines the problem of supporting division from the perspective
of the computer algebra and computational algebraic geometry
communities, while contrasting that perspective with others, and
proposes a semantics of division that makes sense for
computer algebra and computational algebraic geometry solvers.

The remainder of this section looks at existing approaches to handing
division in solvers for real non-linear arithmetic, and finds each
approach deficient in various ways. Section
2

proposes the notion of a formula with divisions being “fair-SAT”,
which attempts to characterize when formulas are
satisfiable without divide-by-zero games that rely on a particular
definition of what division by zero means.
This leads to the notion of
a “well-defined” formula, which is one in which simply clearing
denominators leaves a polynomial formula whose satisfiability is
equivalent to the fair-SATness of the original formula.
Ultimately, we would like a procedure that translates formulas with
divisions to purely polynomial formulas in such a way that
fair-SATness is preserved. Section
3
present the
translation procedure, which gives us a practical way to translate
formulas with divisions into purely polynomial formulas that has nice
properties, some of which are explored in Section
4
.
Interestingly,
nullification
of polynomials, which is an
important issue in Cylindrical Algebraic Decomposition based
algorithms for computing with real polynomial constraints, plays an
important role in the translation algorithm.

The semantics of division — existing choices

The choice to assign division by zero the value zero has the advantage

On the other hand, it leaves users

The choice to keep

\text{div}(\cdot,0)

It is worth considering what standard mathematical practice would

equality/inequality can never be said to be satisfied at an assignment

x/x=1
is not considered to be satisfied at

0
. This is not an acceptable view for logicians, though, since it
leads to strange phenomena in logic. For example,

\exists x[1/x^{2}<0]
is clearly not satisfied, so we would expect its

\neg\exists x[1/x^{2}<0]

should be equivalent to
\forall x[1/x^{2}\geq 0]

The point here is that

\neg\exists x[1/x^{2}<0]\Leftrightarrow\forall x[1/x^{2}\geq 0]
is a general property of ordered rings,
not anything specific to the reals, and
\forall x[\text{div}(x,0)=\text{div}(x,0)]
is a
property of first-order logic with equality. So adopting the usual
mathematical practice that an (in)equality involving divisions is
never satisfied when one or more denominators vanish has the
consequence of invalidating logical theories that are more
fundamental than the real numbers — the theory of ordered rings,
for example, or even the theory of equality. This certainly makes it
clear why computational logic systems cannot adopt that practice!

1.2
Why supporting existing semantics from logic is undesireable

In this section we argue that it is undesireable to try to have
systems from computer algebra or computational algebraic geometry
that compute with real polynomial (in)equalities support division
in a way that mimics either the semantics of Isabelle/ACL2 or those of
SMT-LIB.
First of all, we have reservations about both choices. They do not, as
some of the above examples show, match common mathematical
practice. Of course, they cannot, because of the restrictions of the
domains these
systems work in. So people working in these areas made the compromises
that work best for them. But these are not necessarily compromises
that make sense for computer algebra or computational real algebraic geometry.

1.3
Problems with the
x/0=0
approach

The disadvantage of the divide-by-zero-gives-zero approach,
other than surprising the user with unexpected results as seen above,
is that the
translation process systematically introduces new polynomials that are
artifacts of the
x/0=0
choice, not of the problem itself, viewed from a natural
mathematical perspective. If an input formula contains a division
with denominator
D
in
atom
A\ \sigma\ 0
, then
A\ \sigma\ 0
must be rewritten as

D\neq 0\wedge A_{1}\ \sigma\ 0\vee D=0\wedge A_{2}\ \sigma\ 0
,
where
A_{1}
is the expanded form of
D^{2}\cdot A
and
A_{2}
is
A
with
any term
\text{div}(\cdot,D)
replaced with 0. Obviously there are some
situations in which this may be simplified, but in general this is what
will have to be done. The following example, while perhaps not the
simplest, shows how this process introduces new polynomials that would
not otherwise be part of the problem (meaning if standard mathematical
practice were followed). Consider the folowing formula (with one division).

F_{1}:=\exists x[z^{2}-3x+2y^{2}=1\wedge 8y+8y/x^{2}\leq 8-(z+y+1)^{2}

Translating this into an equivalent formula with only polynomial
constraints gives:

F^{\prime}_{1}:=\exists x[z^{2}-3x+2y^{2}=1\wedge[x\neq 0\wedge 8x^{2}y+8y\leq 8x^{2}-x^{2}(z+y+1)^{2}\vee x=0\wedge 8y\leq-(z+y+1)^{2}]]

Performing quantifier elimination on
F^{\prime}_{1}
we get the set depicted on
the left in Figure
1
. This figure illustrates some of the, perhaps,
surprising results of the
x/0=0
choice of semantics — results
which have negative impacts on computation too. First of all, a
lower-dimensional component to the solution is introduced, which is
not part of what standard mathematical practice would call the
solution. Second of all, the polynomial
8y-8+(z+y+1)^{2}
,
which is only connected to the problem because of the choice to define

x/0=0
,
is introduced as part of the solution. If this were part of a larger
problem, we would carry the computatoinal burden of these (from the standard
mathematical perspective) spurrious components and polynomials.
Thus, problems that are already computationally challenging will become
still harder, and I suspect that will often be unintentional on the
part of users.

1.4
Problems with leaving div
(\cdot,0)
uninterpreted

As noted above, the decision to leave
\text{div}(\cdot,0)

uninterpreted leads to some results that may be surprising to users.
However, adopting the SMT-LIB approach to division is actually much
more problematic for solvers from
computer algebra or computational real algebraic geometry. The file

Reals.smt2
, which
defines the theory of the reals in SMT-LIB, defines division “as a
total function that coincides with the real division function
for all inputs x and y where y is non-zero”, along with the
amplifying note:

Since in SMT-LIB logic all function symbols are interpreted as total
functions, terms of the form (/ t 0) *are* meaningful in every
instance of Reals. However, the declaration imposes no constraints
on their value. This means in particular that
- for every instance theory T and
- for every value v (as defined in the :values attribute) and
closed term t of sort Real,
there is a model of T that satisfies (= v (/ t 0)).

This means that an interpretation can have different results for

\text{div}(x,0)
for different values of
x
. In fact, it is
completely free so that
even statements like
y\cdot\text{div}(x,0)=\text{div}(x\cdot y,0)

need not hold. The problem is that this provides division with too
much expressivity for computer algebra and computational geometry
algorithms to support. Consider the following
examples:

•

Let
F(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})
be a quantifier-free formula without
division (i.e. purely polynomial).
F

is equi-satisfiable with the formula

F(\text{div}(1,0),\text{div}(2,0),\ldots,\text{div}(n,0))
.
Indeed these formulas are equivalent in the sense that satisfying
interpretations for the two are isomorphic under

x_{1}=\text{div}(1,0),x_{2}=\text{div}(2,0),\ldots,x_{n}=\text{div}(n,0)
.
For example
[x^{2}+y^{2}+3xy+(x-y)^{4}\leq-1/100]

is satisfiabile if and only if there is an interpretation for

\text{div}(\cdot,0)
that is a model for

[\text{div}(1,0)^{2}+\text{div}(2,0)^{2}+3\cdot\text{div}(1,0)\cdot\text{div}(2,0)+(\text{div}(1,0)-\text{div}(2,0))^{4}\leq-1/100].

So occurences of div can produce what are, from the perspective of
symbolic computing and real algebraic geometry, new variables, and of
course the number of variables is a critical complexity parameter for
algorithms in this area. In particular, in the above example
determining the satisfiability of a formula without variables
(SMT-LIB constants) requires determining the satisfiability of an

n
-variable Tarski formula.

It’s also worth noting that it isn’t necessarily easy to identify
divisions that act as variables in this way. For example, suppose a
formula has an (in)equality containing variables
x
and
y
and
terms
\text{div}(x,p(x,y))
,
\text{div}(y,p(x,y))
and

\text{div}(x+y,q(x,y))
, where
p
and
q
are polynomials.
In other words, assume that the (in)equality is of the form

A(x,y,\text{div}(x,p(x,y)),\text{div}(y,p(x,y)),\text{div}(x+y,q(x,y)))\ \sigma\ 0.

If
(x,y)=(\alpha,\beta)
is a point at which

p
and
q
have a common zero and at which
x
,
y
and

x+y
all have distinct values, deciding whether the constraint is
satisfied at
(\alpha,\beta)

requires considering the polynomial

A(\alpha,\beta,d_{1},d_{2},d_{3})
, where
d_{1},d_{2},d_{3}
are new
real-valued variables representing the interpretations
of
\text{div}(\alpha,0)
,
\text{div}(\beta,0)
and

\text{div}(\alpha+\beta,0)
. The point is, even recognizing that
there is a three-variable problem lurking in the original formula
means solving the systems
p(x,y)=q(x,y)=0
and checking for
solutions at which
x
,
y
and
x+y
are distinct. Moreover, the
three-variable problem we get will, in general, be over the
algebraic extension required to represent
\alpha
and
\beta
.

•

Many computer algebra systems and specialized programs from the
computer algebra community are able to do satisfiability checking
for formulas with quantifiers or even to do quantifier elimination.
Consider the problem of deciding the satisfiability of formulas
that include division following the SMT-LIB semantics
where quantification is allowed. Let
p
be an
n
-variable
polynomial with integer coefficients. Deciding the satisfiability
of

\forall z[(0<z<1\Rightarrow\text{div}(z,0)=0)\wedge\text{div}(z+1,0)=\text{div}(z,0)]\wedge\text{div}(x_{1},0)=1\wedge\cdots\wedge\text{div}(x_{n},0)=1\wedge p(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=0

is equivalent to determining whether
p
has a solution over the
integers, which is undecidable.

•

In some ways the biggest issue is that some problems that are
important in computer algebra and computational algebraic geometry
are not well-defined when we adopt the SMT-LIB semantics.
For
example, a common problem is to compute the dimension of a
semi-algebraic set given a defining formula. But consider the
set
S
defined by

S=\{(s,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}|1/t\leq s\leq 1\wedge t^{2}\leq 0\}
.
The dimension of
S

is 1, 0 or
-1
depending on the interpretation of

\text{div}(1,0)
. Obviously,
(s,t)
cannot be in
S
unless

t=0
. So
S
is really defined by
{\mathrm{div}}{\left(1,0\right)}\leq s\leq 1
.
Under an interpretation for which
{\mathrm{div}}{\left(1,0\right)}<1
,
S
is a
non-degenerate interval on the
t
-axis, and so has dimension one.
Under an interpretation for which
{\mathrm{div}}{\left(1,0\right)}=1
,
S
contains
only the single-point
(1,0)
, and so has dimension zero. Finally,
under any interpretation for which
{\mathrm{div}}{\left(1,0\right)}>1
,
S
is empty.

Another common problem with similar issues is the question of
whether the set of points satisfying a formula is connected. For
example, when there are two components connected by a single point,
the presence or absence of that point in the set defined by a
formula may depend on the interpretation of
{\mathrm{div}}{\left(\cdot,0\right)}
.

So, supporting the SMT-LIB semantics for division is not practical for
systems that are intended to compute with polynomials because it means
that the presence of division
can cause problems to jump in complexity, to switch from
decidable to undecidable, or to cease to be well defined. Even if we
consider the above examples to be “misuses” of
division, it is not at all clear (to me, at least) that one can detect
whether an input formula has divisions that cause these kind of
problems.

1.5
Problems with adding guards following existing computer algebra practice

The practice of adding guards as described for Mathematica’s

Resolve
and Maple’s
QuantifierElimination
commands is
pragmatic --- but it doesn’t provide a completely satisfactory
semantics. There are instances of formulas that are equivalent based
on fundamental laws like trichotemy
4
4

4

The Trichotemy law is an
axiom of ordered rings stating that any ring element is exactly one of
positive, negative or zero. As a consequence,

\neg[f<g]\Longleftrightarrow[f\geq g]
and the other similar
identities hold for any ordered ring, not just the reals.

or substitutivity of equality,
for which adding guards produces inequivalent formulas.

For example, if we assume the Trichotemy Law holds, the formula

F:=\exists x[1/x^{2}<0]
has the property that the guarded
versions of both
F
and
\neg F
are false.

\begin{array}[]{rl}\exists x[1/x^{2}<0]&\hskip 12.0pt\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{\tiny add guards}}}}{{\Longleftrightarrow}}\hskip 12.0pt\exists x[x\neq 0\wedge 1<0]\hskip 12.0pt\Longleftrightarrow\hskip 12.0pt\textsc{false}\\
\neg\exists x[1/x^{2}<0]&\hskip 12.0pt\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{\tiny trichotemy law}}}}{{\Longleftrightarrow}}\hskip 12.0pt\forall x[1/x^{2}\geq 0]\hskip 12.0pt\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{\tiny add guards}}}}{{\Longleftrightarrow}}\hskip 12.0pt\forall x[x\neq 0\wedge 1\geq 0]\hskip 12.0pt\Longleftrightarrow\hskip 12.0pt\textsc{false}\end{array}

A subtle effect of this kind of issue is that the result of the guarding process
depends on when it happens with respect to other, even very basic, rewriting steps.
For instance, in the second line above, had we done the
guarding prior to moving the negation inwards we would have gotten

\neg\exists x[1/x^{2}<0]\ \ \ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{\tiny add
guards}}}}{{\Longleftrightarrow}}\ \ \ \ \neg\exists x[x\neq 0\wedge 1<0]\ \ \ \Longleftrightarrow\ \ \ \forall x[x=0\vee 1\geq 0]\ \ \ \Longleftrightarrow\ \ \ \textsc{true}

… which is the opposite result.

In computer algebra solvers it is common to require prenex inputs, and
this issue of when the guarding happens becomes an issue when we
consider when the prenex conversion happens:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\forall y[\neg\forall x[1/x^{2}\geq 1/y]\vee y<0]&\ \ \ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{\tiny add
guards}}}}{{\Longleftrightarrow}}&\forall y[\neg\forall x[x\neq 0\wedge y\neq 0\wedge 1/x^{2}\geq 1/y]\vee y<0]\\
&&\forall y[\exists x[x=0\vee y\neq 0\vee 1/x^{2}<1/y]\vee y<0]\\
&&\forall y\exists x[x=0\vee y=0\vee y^{2}<x^{2}y\vee y<0]\\
&&\textsc{true}\\
\forall y[\neg\forall x[1/x^{2}\geq 1/y]\vee y<0]&\Longleftrightarrow&\forall y[\exists x[1/x^{2}<1/y]\vee y<0]\\
&\ \ \ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{\tiny add
guards}}}}{{\Longleftrightarrow}}&\forall y[\exists x[x\neq 0\wedge y\neq 0\wedge y^{2}<x^{2}y]\vee y<0]\\
&&\forall y\exists x[x\neq 0\wedge y\neq 0\wedge y^{2}<x^{2}y\vee y<0]\\
&&\textsc{false}\end{array}

So we get different results if the guarding is done before versus
after the conversion to prenex.

If we assume reflexivity and
substitutivity of equality, then

\forall x,y[x=y\Rightarrow 1/x=1/y]

is true, but

\forall x,y[x=y\Rightarrow 1/x=1/y]\ \ \ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{\tiny add
guards}}}}{{\Longleftrightarrow}}\ \ \ \forall x,y[x=y\Rightarrow x\neq 0\wedge y\neq 0\wedge y=x]

… is false, since the implication fails at
x=y=0
.

Finally consider the following example of two formulas that are
equivalent given only that “
\neq
” is the negation of “
=
”, and
yet give different results under the guarding regime:

\exists x[1/x=0]\ \ \ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{\tiny add
guards}}}}{{\Longleftrightarrow}}\ \ \ \exists x[x\neq 0\wedge 1=0]\Longleftrightarrow\textsc{false}

\exists x[\neg(1/x\neq 0)]\ \ \ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{\tiny add
guards}}}}{{\Longleftrightarrow}}\ \ \ \exists x[\neg(x\neq 0\wedge 1\neq 0)]\Longleftrightarrow\exists x[x=0\vee 1=0]\Longleftrightarrow\textsc{true}

In this example,
\exists x[1/x=0]
leads to
false
, which seems
clearly correct. One might be tempted to suggest that the guarding
works as long as any logical negations are pushed into the
inequalities before doing the guarding. But one can also construct
examples where this does not work. Consider:

\exists x\forall y[y^{2}>1\vee\neg((x/y)^{2}<4)]\ \ \ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{\tiny add
guards}}}}{{\Longleftrightarrow}}\ \ \ \exists x\forall y[y^{2}>1\vee\neg(y\neq 0\wedge x^{2}<4y^{2})]\Longleftrightarrow\textsc{true}\text{ (e.g. at $x=2$)}

\exists x\forall y[y^{2}>1\vee(x/y)^{2}\geq 4]\ \ \ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{\tiny add
guards}}}}{{\Longleftrightarrow}}\ \ \ \exists x\forall y[y^{2}>1\vee(y\neq 0\wedge x^{2}\geq 4y^{2})]\Longleftrightarrow\textsc{false}

I would argue that the former has a far better claim to being correct
than the latter, so pushing the logical negations into the
inequalities first did not work for this example, although it did for
the previous example.

The issues just described are serious. They show that this simple
guarding procedure conflicts with
the theory of ordered rings, and with the rules of first-order
logic with equality, and even with propositional logic plus the
definition of
\neq
as the negation of
=
.
In fact, I would argue that this is not really a well-defined
semantics — there is no clear connection between a formula with
divisions and the purely polynomial formula produced from it via the
guarding procedure other than the procedure itself.

2
What
can
be done about division?

The previous section argued that supporting division with the
semantics of “usual mathematical practice”, or the
x/0=0

semantics, or the unterpreted function semantics for

\text{div}(x,0)
,
or the usual computer algebra semantics
is not reasonable. The question then is what is reasonable? What
should we be looking for?

I am proposing that we should be looking for a procedure that
translates a formula with divisions into one or more formulas without
divisions. I propose treating division by zero as uninterpreted, but
I do not propose following SMT-LIB semantics (for the reasons
described above). Instead, I propose that for
well-defined

formulas (what that means will be explored later), we guarantee

equivalence
(once again, a precise definition of what that means comes
later) between the input formula and the result of the
translation process, while for formulas that are not well defined,
we do not provide this guarantee. However, for formulas that are not
well defined, we define the notion of
fair-SAT
, and we guarantee
that the original formula with divisions is fair-SAT if and only if
the translated formula is SAT. The idea behind fair-SAT is that a
formula is fair-SAT if and only if it is satisfiable without having
to rely on divide-by-zero shenanigans …
a more formal definition appears in Section
2.2
.

Finally, the mathematical practice of providing a “guard” against
division by zero, like the “
x\neq 0
” in

\exists x[x\neq 0\wedge x+1/x=0]
,
should produce formulas that meet the definition of

well-defined
, as we assume most people would expect.
Moreover, when an inequality is appropriately guarded,
clearing denominators should not change the meaning of the formula.

2.1
“Well-defined formula” … first attempt

We would like a good notion of formulas containing divisions being
“well-defined”. This is important because we might hope that for
well-defined formulas we can precisely describe what
we are trying to accomplish — for example we would like to say that
if
F
is a well-defined formula, then our translation to a formula
without divisions should be equivalent to
F
.
In first-order logic, all functions are total, so we cannot express
that division by zero is “undefined”. We can, however, express that
the satisfiability of a formula is not dependent on the choice of
what value to assign to the result of dividing by zero. That
motivates our first attempt at pinning down the concept of a
“well-defined” formula.

Definition 1
.

A formula
F
in the theory of real algebra with uninterpreted
function symbol
{\mathrm{div}}{\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)}
and additional axiom

\forall x,y[y\neq 0\Rightarrow{\mathrm{div}}{\left(x,y\right)}\cdot y=x]

is called
well-defined
if and only if
the true/false value of
F
is the same under
under any two interpretations that differ only in the interpretation
of
{\mathrm{div}}{\left(\cdot,0\right)}
.
Note: Other than
{\mathrm{div}}{\left(\cdot,0\right)}
, the only thing left to
interpret are the values of the free variables.

Under this definition, for example, the formula

\exists x,y[x-y\neq 0\wedge x+y=3x/(x-y)\wedge y+1=2x]

is well-defined (it is false).
On the other hand,
\exists x,y[x+y=3x/(x-y)\wedge y+1=2x]

is not well-defined according to this definition, since under the interpretation
in which
\text{div}(3,0)=2
, the formula is true (satisfied at

(x,y)=(1,1)
), but for all other interpretations it is false. This
definition of “well-defined” has some nice properties. For example,
it is immediate that under transformations from first-order logic and
ordered fields that produce equivalent formulas, well-definedness is
maintained. Moreover, it is clear that formulas written with
“guards”, in the usual sense, are well defined. For example:

\exists x,y[y\neq 0\wedge x^{2}/y^{2}+2x/y+1=0]
is
well-defined.

Unfortunately, this definition of well-defined causes some problems.
Specifically, we will look at two simple formulas — both closed with
only an existential quantifier block, and both well defined under
Defintition
1
— and show that whatever
combination of guarding and denominator-clearing approaches we take,
the resulting formula is not equivalent to the original under any
interpretation for
{\mathrm{div}}{\left(\cdot,0\right)}
.

Let
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
denote the following formulas:

F_{1}:=\exists x,y\left[y^{2}\left(1+x^{2}+\frac{1}{y^{2}}\right)\leq 0)\right],F_{2}:=\exists x\left[0\leq x\wedge\frac{1}{x}\leq\frac{1}{x}-1\right]

and note that

•

F_{1}
and
F_{2}
are well-defined by Definition
1
,
and

•

F_{1}
is true under any interpretation for

\text{div}(\cdot,0)
, and
F_{2}
is false under any interpretation
for
\text{div}(\cdot,0)
.

For fully existentially quantified conjunctions, there are two basic
approaches — clear denominators but add guards to guarantee that the
conjunction is false when a denominator is zero, or clear denominators
without adding guards. Additionally, sometimes denominators can be
cleared simply by “expanding”, whereas sometimes one has to multiply
both sides of an inequality by the square of the denominator. We
consider these possiblities below:

1.

Expanding to clear denominators, without adding “guards”:
for
F_{1}
this produces formula

\exists x,y\left[y^{2}+y^{2}x^{2}+1\leq 0\right]

which is false.

2.

Expanding to clear denominators, and adding “guards”:
for
F_{1}
this produces
formula

\exists x,y\left[y\neq 0\wedge y^{2}+y^{2}x^{2}+1\leq 0\right]

which is false.

3.

Multiplying both sides by the denominator squared and
expanding, without adding “guards”:
for
F_{2}
this produces

\exists x\left[0\leq x\wedge x\leq x-x^{2}\right]
, which is true.

4.

Multiplying both sides by the denominator squared and
expanding, and adding “guards”: for
F_{1}
this
produces formula

\exists x,y\left[y\neq 0\wedge y^{4}(y^{2}+y^{2}x^{2}+1)\leq 0\right]

which is false.

So the moral of the story, is that, under
Definition
1
, no matter which of the obvious rewriting strategies
you employ, there will be examples of well-defined formulas for which
the rewriting produces a formula that is not equivalent to the
original formula.

It is also interesting to note that recognizing that a formula is
well-defined according to this definition can be quite difficult.
In fact, through a variant of the second example from
Section
1.4
, it can shown to be undecidable.

2.2
“Well-defined formula” … second attempt

Our first attempt at a definition for “well-defined” was based on
the idea of a formula’s evaluation at an interpretation not depending
on what
real values
the interpretation assigns to

with
zero denominators.
In our second attempt at a servicable definition of a well-defined

on what
boolean values
are assigned to
atomic formulas

This will lead us to the definition of well-defined we will stick

2.2.1

2.2.2
Fair-SAT

In what follows, we will be starting with formulas in which the
variables are standardized apart and
\wedge/\vee/\neg
are the only
boolean operators and repeatedly peval-uating.
It is convenient to have a name for formulas of the form that arise
from this process.

Definition 6
.

We say closed formula
F
is
peval-safe
if the real variables
are standardized
apart, the only boolean operators are
\wedge
,
\vee
and
\neg
,
and for some non-negative integer constant
\#F
,
there are distinct integers
k_{1},\ldots,k_{\#F}

such that formula
F
has
exactly one occurrence of propositional variable
U_{k_{i}}
or
V_{k_{i}}
, but
not both, and these are the only propositional variables. Note that
divisions and true/false constants are allowed.

So, for example,

\exists x[U_{1}\wedge x^{2}<2]\vee\exists y[{\mathrm{div}}{\left(2,y,=\right)}0\wedge U_{2}]

is
peval-safe
, while the very similar formula

\exists x[U_{1}\wedge x^{2}<2]\vee\exists x[{\mathrm{div}}{\left(2,x,=\right)}0\wedge U_{2}]

is not, since the variables are not standardized apart. Also, the

\exists x[U_{1}\wedge x^{2}<2]\vee\exists y[{\mathrm{div}}{\left(2,y,=\right)}0\wedge V_{1}]

fails to be
peval-safe
because it has two variables with the
same subscript.

A common operation in what follows will be “flipping” the
U_{i}
s to

V_{i}
s, or combining that with logical negation, which we call
“flopping”.
This arises when we consider negations of
peval-safe
formulas. For
example, we consider the formula
F:=\forall x[x^{2}\geq 0\wedge U_{1}]

to be “false” (more precisely, it is not “fair-SAT”, a concept defined
shortly), because it is not true for both
values of
U_{1}
. However, the usual negation of
F
gives

\exists x[x^{2}<0\vee\neg U_{1}]
, which is also not satisfied
for both values of
U_{1}
.
So instead we both negate logically
and

U_{1}
to
V_{1}
— a combination we call “flop”. This
gives

\exists x[x^{2}<0\vee\neg V_{1}]
, which we do consider to be “true”
(more precisely, “fair-SAT”),
since the
V_{1}
is interpreted as existentially not universally quantified.
We next provide precise definitions of “flop” and “flip”, which
are then used in the definition of fair-SAT.

Note:
for the remainder of this paper, we will adopt the
convention that

\sigma

\overline{\sigma}
is

\sigma

2.2.3
A brief discussion of properties of fair-SAT

Hopefully the definition of fair-SAT seems defensible, at least given
the motivation of wanting the truth or falsity of formulas not to
depend on vanishing denominators. In this section we highlight some
attractive features of the definition of fair-SAT we have chosen.
Proofs of these properties, which are a bit involved, are presented
later. Specifically, fair-SATness is preserved by the kinds of basic
first-order logic rewritings that we saw in Section
1
can
cause problems for the usual computer algebra system practice of
guarding divisions with conditions on the non-vanishing of denominators.

1.

If
F
is fair-SAT, then
\neg F
is not fair-SAT.

2.

Fair-SATness is preserved when rewriting
inequality
f\ \sigma\ g
as
\neg[f\ \overline{\sigma}\ g]
, and vice
versa.

3.

Fair-SATness is preserved across applications of De Morgan’s laws.

4.

Fair-SATness is preserved when moving
negations through quantifiers. I.e.
\neg\exists x[F]
is fair-SAT
equivalent to
\forall x[\neg F]
.

Note that by putting these together we see that the usual process for
converting a formula to prenex form preserves fair-SATness.
So, for example, if we are given the formula

\forall x[\neg[0\leq 1/x+2\wedge 1/x-2\leq 0]\vee\exists y[y^{2}<5\wedge 3x^{2}-1/y+1<0]]

and we convert to prenex, simplifying away the negations as we go, we get

\forall x[\exists y[0>1/x+2\vee 1/x-2>0\vee y^{2}<5\wedge 3x^{2}-1/y+1<0]],

and we can be assured that the two formulas are fair-SAT
equivalent.

Of
course, there are some rewritings that do not preserve fair-SATness.
For example, the axioms of equality imply

\forall x[{\mathrm{div}}{\left(1,x\right)}={\mathrm{div}}{\left(1,x\right)}]
. Thus, we would expect

\exists x[x=0]
and
\exists x[x=0\wedge{\mathrm{div}}{\left(1,x\right)}={\mathrm{div}}{\left(1,x\right)}]
to be equivalent.
However, the former is fair-SAT, while the latter is not. In general,
rewritings that introduce new denominators or remove denominators have
the possibility to change the fair-SATness of a formula.
This appears to be unavoidable. None the less, the situation with
fair-SAT seems to be much better than for usual computer algebra
practice, since there is at least a clear
class of basic logical rewriting operators that preserve
fair-SATness.

3
Handling formulas that are not well defined

Ideally, users or programs that call polynomial constraint solvers
with input formulas that include divisions would only send solvers
well-defined formulas, since we could just clear denominators without
having to worry about whether they vanish or not.
However, experience shows that this is not
always the case. This motivates us to consider how best to handle
such formulas. Our goal is a translation procedure that maps formulas
with divisions into purely polynomial formulas such that:
1) a well-defined formula
F
is mapped to polynomial formula
that is equivalent to the result of simply clearing denominators from
F
,
and
2) non-well-defined formula
F
is mapped to a purely polynomial
formula that is SAT if and only if
F
is fair-SAT.

Where the usual guarding process fails

In Section

We noted that

4
Proving properties of fair-SAT-ness

The goal of this section is to show that for basic logical rewritings of a
formula that are not specific to real algebra and that
do not add or remove denominators, fair-SATness is preserved.
We show that moving quantifiers (in particular, for converting a
formula to prenex),
moving around logical negation operators according to the usual rules,
and absorbing logical negation into comparison operators all
preserve fair-SATness.

Note: for the rest of this section, we will always assume that we are
dealing with formulas in which
\wedge/\vee/\neg
are the only boolean
operators, and in which variables are standardized apart.

4.1
Properties of the
U_{i}
and
V_{i}
variables

Partial evaluation introduces
U_{i}
variables, and the “flop”
operation changes
U_{i}
variables to
V_{i}
variables (and back).
In this section we prove some basic results concerning these special
variables and their role in determining whether a formula is
fair-SAT. The first result, and its corollaries, concern formulas
containing only
U_{i}
and
V_{i}
variables, and boolean constants.
Such formulas are the base case in the recursive definition of
fair-SAT. These results show that, while the definition of fair-SAT
boils down in the base case to a quantified boolean formula, we
actually only need to consider one set of assignments to determine the
truth of that quantified boolean formula.

Theorem 2
.

Let
F
be a formula that is
peval-safe
, and which contains no real
variables.
If
U_{i}
appears in
F
then

\forall U_{i}[F]\Longleftrightarrow F|_{U_{i}=b_{i}}

where
b_{i}
is false if, in the positive form of
F
, the literal
containing
U_{i}
is positive, and true otherwise.
If
V_{i}
appears in
F
then

\exists V_{i}[F]\Longleftrightarrow F|_{V_{i}=c_{i}}

where
c_{i}
is true if, in the positive form of
F
, the literal
containing
V_{i}
is positive, and false otherwise.

Proof.

This is an obvious consequence of the fact that each
U/V
variable appears
exactly once.
∎

Corollary 1
.

If
F
satisfies the hypotheses of Theorem
2
,
then the truth of

\forall U_{i_{1}}\ldots U_{i_{a}}\exists V_{i_{1}}\ldots V_{i_{b}}[F]

is the same no matter how the quantifiers are reordered.

Corollary 2
.

If
F
satisfies the hypotheses of Theorem
2
,
then
F
is fair-SAT if and only if
{\mathrm{flop}}{\left(F\right)}
is not fair-SAT.

Proof.

We show that not
F
fair-SAT is equivalent to
{\mathrm{flop}}{\left(F\right)}
fair-SAT.

\begin{array}[]{rcll}\neg\forall U_{i_{1}}\ldots U_{i_{a}}\exists V_{i_{1}}\ldots V_{i_{b}}[F]&\Longleftrightarrow&\exists U_{i_{1}}\ldots U_{i_{a}}\forall V_{i_{1}}\ldots V_{i_{b}}[\neg F]&\\
&\Longleftrightarrow&\exists V_{i_{1}}\ldots V_{i_{a}}\forall U_{i_{1}}\ldots U_{i_{b}}[{\mathrm{flop}}{\left(F\right)}],&\text{renaming $V$s as $U$s and vice versa}\\
&\Longleftrightarrow&\forall U_{i_{1}}\ldots U_{i_{b}}\exists V_{i_{1}}\ldots V_{i_{a}}[{\mathrm{flop}}{\left(F\right)}],&\text{by Corollary~\ref{corollary:reorder}.}\\
\end{array}

∎

The following theorem builds on Theorem
2

by showing that, as long as we are dealing with positive, prenex
formulas, replacing the
U_{i}
and
V_{i}
variables with
appropriately chosen boolean constants produces a fair-SAT equivalent
formula. This theorem plays a crucial role in the proof-of-correctness
for the translate algorithm in Section
5
.

Theorem 3
.

Let
F
and
F^{\prime}
be prenex, positive and peval-safe formulas that
differ only in that either:
(a)
U_{i}
occurs in
F
but in
F^{\prime}
has been replaced with
boolean constant
b_{i}
, where
b_{i}
is false if the literal
containing
U_{i}
is positive, and true otherwise, or
(b)
V_{i}
occurs in
F
but in
F^{\prime}
has been replaced with
boolean constant
c_{i}
, where
c_{i}
is true if the literal
containing
V_{i}
is positive, and false otherwise.

F
and
F^{\prime}
are fair-SAT equivalent.

Proof.

We will assume that
F
(and thus
F^{\prime}
) have no free (unquantified)
real variables. If not, we note that for any assignment of values
to the free variables, the resulting partial evaluation of
F
and

F^{\prime}
will yield formulas satisfying the conditions of the theorem
with the same variable
U_{i}
(respectively
V_{i}
)
in the same literal in the “new”
F
, and with the same boolean
constant in the associated position in the “new”
F^{\prime}
.

We prove the theorem by induction on
k
, the number of quantifier
blocks in
F
. If
k=0
, the theorem is equivalent to
Theorem
2
. If
k>0
, we consider two cases:

Case 1: The outermost block consists
of existentially quantified variables
x_{j},\ldots,x_{j+d}
. By
definition
F
is fair-SAT if and only if there exist real constants

\alpha_{j},\ldots,\alpha_{j+d}
such that

{\mathrm{peval}}{\left(F_{qf},(x_{j},\ldots,x_{j+d}),(\alpha_{j},\ldots,\alpha_{j+d})\right)}

is fair-SAT, where
F=\exists x_{j},\ldots,x_{j+d}[F_{qf}]
.
Similarly, by
definition
F^{\prime}
is fair-SAT if and only if there exist real constants

\alpha_{j},\ldots,\alpha_{j+d}
such that

{\mathrm{peval}}{\left(F^{\prime}_{qf},(x_{j},\ldots,x_{j+d}),(\alpha_{j},\ldots,\alpha_{j+d})\right)}

is fair-SAT, where
F^{\prime}=\exists x_{j},\ldots,x_{j+d}[F^{\prime}_{qf}]
.
Since
{\mathrm{peval}}{\left(F_{qf},(x_{j},\ldots,x_{j+d}),(\alpha_{j},\ldots,\alpha_{j+d})\right)}

and

{\mathrm{peval}}{\left(F^{\prime}_{qf},(x_{j},\ldots,x_{j+d}),(\alpha_{j},\ldots,\alpha_{j+d})\right)}

satisfy the requirements of the theorem, but with fewer quantifier
blocks, we conclude by induction that they are fair-SAT equivalent.
Thus
F
and
F^{\prime}
are fair-SAT equivalent.

Case 2: The outermost block consists
of universally quantified variables
x_{j},\ldots,x_{j+d}
.
In this case,
F
is fair-SAT if and only if

\overline{F}:={\mathrm{flop}}{\left(F\right)}
is not fair-SAT.
Similarly,
F^{\prime}
is fair-SAT if and only if

\overline{F}^{\prime}:={\mathrm{flop}}{\left(F^{\prime}\right)}
is not fair-SAT.

Sub-case: Suppose
U_{i}
appears positively in
F
. Then
\neg V_{i}
appears
at the same location in
\overline{F}
.
In
F^{\prime}
, the constant

appears in place of

,
while in
\overline{F^{\prime}}
, that

is replaced with

\neg\textsc{true}
.
So,
\overline{F}
and
\overline{F^{\prime}}
are identical, other than

appearing in
\overline{F^{\prime}}
where
V_{i}
appears
in
\overline{F}
.
This means they satisfy the hypotheses of the theorem, though still
with

However, their outermost quantifier blocks consist of existentially
quantified variables

, so we can revert to Case

The other sub-cases:

U_{i}
appears negatively in
F
,

V_{i}
appears positively in
F
, and

V_{i}
appears negatively in
F
can be handled analogously.
∎

Finally, we show that whether a

variable appears positively or
negatively does not really matter in determining whether a formula
is fair-SAT.

Lemma 1
.

If closed formulas
F
and
F^{\prime}
are both

, with
\#F=\#F^{\prime}
, and
which differ only in that for exactly one

1\leq i\leq\#F
,

U_{k_{i}}
appears in one while
\neg U_{k_{i}}
appears in the other, or

V_{k_{i}}
appears in one while
\neg V_{k_{i}}
appears in the other, then

and
F^{\prime}
are fair-SAT equivalent.

Proof.

We prove this by induction on the size of the formula defined as
follows. Quantifier-free formulas have size zero. Otherwise the
size is
1+k_{Q}+k_{b}+2^{q}\cdot k_{n}
, where
q
is the number of
quantifiers in the formula and

•

k_{Q}
is the number defined
by viewing the quantifiers appearing in the formula from left to right
as a binary number, where
\forall=1
and
\exists=0
,

•

k_{b}
is the number of
\wedge/\vee
operators, and

•

k_{n}
is the sum over all
\neg
operators of the number of

\wedge/\vee/\exists/\forall
operators in the argument
to which the negation is applied.

We go through the cases from the definition of fair-SAT one by one.

Case 1,
F
and
F^{\prime}
contain no real variables. Note that this is the
base case.
If

U_{k_{i}}
appears in one and
\neg U_{k_{i}}
appears in the other, then

U_{k_{i}}
is universally quantified and appears only once. So if the
one formula holds for both values of
U_{k_{i}}
, the other holds for both
values of
\neg U_{k_{i}}
. If

V_{k_{i}}
appears in one and
\neg V_{k_{i}}
appears in the other, then

V_{k_{i}}
is existentially quantified and appears only once. So if
one formula holds for one value for
V_{k_{i}}
, the other formula holds
when
V_{k_{i}}
is assigned the opposite value.

Case 2,
F
is the negation of a smaller subformula, and we have
subcases we must consider. In each, the theorem clearly follows by
induction as long as we can show that the size of formulas is reduced
so induction does indeed apply.

•

F
is
\neg(A\wedge B)
, which is fair-SAT if and only if

\neg A\vee\neg B
is fair-SAT. Induction applies since

k_{n}
is reduced because the
\wedge
is no longer part of the
argument for any
\neg
operator.

•

F
is
\neg(A\vee B)
, same argument as above.

•

F
is
\neg\neg F^{\prime}
, which is fair-SAT if and only if
F^{\prime}
is
fair-SAT.
F^{\prime}
has at least one quantifier, since we would otherwise
be in Case 1, so
k_{n}
is reduced and induction applies.

•

F
is
\neg\exists x[F^{\prime}]
, which is fair-SAT if and only if

\forall x[\neg F^{\prime}]
is fair-SAT. In this situation,
k_{n}
is
reduced by one, which reduces the overall size by

, and
k_{b}
is
unchanged, but
k_{Q}
may increase.
However, for any formula

, so the overall size is
decreased and induction applies.

•

F
is
\neg\forall x[F^{\prime}]
, which is fair-SAT if and only if

\exists x[\neg F^{\prime}]
is fair-SAT. The argument for this is the same
as that for the preceding.

Case 3,
F=A\vee B
. Then
U_{k_{i}}
or
V_{k_{i}}
appears either in

or

, but not both. So

where

and

are
either identical, or satisfy the hypotheses of this lemma, and so are

induction (note that for both

and

,

is reduced,

k_{Q}
is not increased and
k_{n}
is unchanged).
The same argument applies to

and
B^{\prime}
. so
F
and
F^{\prime}
are
fair-SAT equivalent.

Case 4,
F=A\wedge B
. This follows the same argument as the
previous case.

Case 5,
F=\exists x[G]
. Then

, and for any

,
{\mathrm{peval}}{\left(G,x,\alpha\right)}
and
{\mathrm{peval}}{\left(G^{\prime},x,\alpha\right)}

satisfy the hypotheses of this lemma, and thus by induction (note that

is reduced, but

and

are unchanged) are
fair-SAT equivalent. This means

and
F^{\prime}
are fair-SAT equivalent.

Case 6,

.

is fair-SAT if and only if

\exists x[{\mathrm{flop}}{\left(G\right)}]
is fair-SAT.
F^{\prime}=\forall x[G^{\prime}]
is fair-SAT if and only if

\exists x[{\mathrm{flop}}{\left(G^{\prime}\right)}]
is fair-SAT. Note that
\exists x[{\mathrm{flop}}{\left(G\right)}]
and

\exists x[{\mathrm{flop}}{\left(G^{\prime}\right)}]
satisfy the requirements of this lemma, and are

and

,
since

is reduced,

is unchanged, and

is zero after

∎

4.2
Preserving fair-SATness across rewritings involving negation

A feature of ordered rings is that logical negation can be absorbed
into relational predicate expressions which, of course, we write using
the usual relational operators. For example, instead of
\neg[x>0]

we may write
[x\leq 0]
. When examining the usual practice of
computer algebra systems in handling division, we saw that such
rewritings could change the meaning of a formula. We first show that
this is not the case for fair-SATness. And then show that
fair-SATness is preserved, more generally, across the conversion of a
formula to its positive form.

Theorem 4
.

If
F
contains an occurrence of atom A :=
[f\ \sigma\ g]
, and
F^{\prime}

is the formula resulting from replacing the occurrence of
A
with

A^{\prime}:=\neg[f\ \overline{\sigma}\ g]
, then
F
and
F^{\prime}
are fair-SAT
equivalent.

Proof.

To prove this, we can apply an inductive argument that follows the
structure of the fair-SAT definition. Eventually,
A
will evaluate
to
true
or to
false
, in which case
A^{\prime}
will evaluate to

\neg\textsc{false}
or to
\neg\textsc{true}
, respectively, which is clearly equivalent.
This is straightforward except
that we may, in applying Point 5 of the definition of fair-SAT,
have atoms
A
and
A^{\prime}
get replaced with
U_{k_{i}}
and

\neg U_{k_{i}}
, respectively. However, if this
happens, Lemma
1
finishes off the proof.
∎

Theorem 5
.

Let
F
be a closed formula, possibly with
U_{i}
/
V_{i}
s.
Let
F^{\prime}={\mathrm{posform}}{\left(F\right)}
.
F
is fair-SAT if and only if
F^{\prime}
is
fair-SAT.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on the size of
F
, and consider the following
cases, which match the definition of fair-SAT:

1.

F
contains only propositional variables, true/false constants
and boolean operators.
In this case
F
and
F^{\prime}
are equivalent boolean formulas, so

\forall U_{i_{1}},\ldots,U_{i_{a}}\exists V_{j_{1}},\ldots,V_{j_{b}}[F]\Leftrightarrow\forall U_{i_{1}},\ldots,U_{i_{a}}\exists V_{j_{1}},\ldots,V_{j_{b}}[F^{\prime}],

which means
F
and
F^{\prime}
are fair-SAT equivalent.
Note: This is the base case!

2.

F
is
\neg G
, and we distinguish the following subcases:

(a)

F=\neg(A\vee B)
. In this case,
F
is fair-SAT if and
only if
\neg A\wedge\neg B
is fair-SAT. Note that

F^{\prime}={\mathrm{posform}}{\left(\neg A\right)}\wedge{\mathrm{posform}}{\left(\neg B\right)}
, which is

{\mathrm{posform}}{\left(\neg A\wedge\neg B\right)}
.
The fair-SAT equivalence of these two comes from case
4
.

(b)

F=\neg(A\wedge B)
. In this case,
F
is fair-SAT if and
only if
\neg A\vee\neg B
is fair-SAT. Note that

F^{\prime}={\mathrm{posform}}{\left(\neg A\right)}\vee{\mathrm{posform}}{\left(\neg B\right)}
, which is

{\mathrm{posform}}{\left(\neg A\vee\neg B\right)}
.
The fair-SAT equivalence of these two comes from
case
3
.

(c)

F=\neg\neg G
. In this case,
F
is fair-SAT if and
only if
G
is fair-SAT.
F^{\prime}={\mathrm{posform}}{\left(G\right)}
. By induction,
G

is fair-SAT equivalent to
{\mathrm{posform}}{\left(G\right)}
, so
F
and
F^{\prime}
are
fair-SAT equivalent.

(d)

F=\neg\exists x[G]
.
F
is fair-SAT if and only if

\forall x[\neg G]
is fair-SAT.

{\mathrm{posform}}{\left(F\right)}=\forall x[{\mathrm{posform}}{\left(\neg G\right)}]={\mathrm{posform}}{\left(\forall x[\neg G]\right)}
. By
case
6
,
\forall x[\neg G]
and

{\mathrm{posform}}{\left(\forall x[\neg G]\right)}
are fair-SAT equivalent.

(e)

F=\neg\forall x[G]
.
F
is fair-SAT if and only if

\exists x[\neg G]
is fair-SAT.

{\mathrm{posform}}{\left(F\right)}=\exists x[{\mathrm{posform}}{\left(\neg G\right)}]={\mathrm{posform}}{\left(\exists x[\neg G]\right)}
. By
case
5
,
\exists x[\neg G]
and

{\mathrm{posform}}{\left(\exists x[\neg G]\right)}
are fair-SAT equivalent.

3.

F=A\vee B
.
F
is fair-SAT if and only if
A
is fair-SAT or

B
is fair-SAT.
F^{\prime}={\mathrm{posform}}{\left(A\vee B\right)}={\mathrm{posform}}{\left(A\right)}\vee{\mathrm{posform}}{\left(B\right)}
.
By induction,
A
is fair-SAT equivalent to
{\mathrm{posform}}{\left(A\right)}
, and

B
is fair-SAT equivalent to
{\mathrm{posform}}{\left(B\right)}
. Therefore,
F
and

F^{\prime}
are fair-SAT equivalent.

4.

F=A\wedge B
.
F
is fair-SAT if and only if
A
is fair-SAT and

B
is fair-SAT.
F^{\prime}={\mathrm{posform}}{\left(A\wedge B\right)}={\mathrm{posform}}{\left(A\right)}\wedge{\mathrm{posform}}{\left(B\right)}
.
By induction,
A
is fair-SAT equivalent to
{\mathrm{posform}}{\left(A\right)}
, and

B
is fair-SAT equivalent to
{\mathrm{posform}}{\left(B\right)}
. Therefore,
F
and

F^{\prime}
are fair-SAT equivalent.

5.

F=\exists x[G]
.
F
is fair-SAT if and only if there exists a
real value
\alpha
for
which
{\mathrm{peval}}{\left(G,x,\alpha\right)}
is fair-SAT.

F^{\prime}={\mathrm{posform}}{\left(F\right)}=\exists x[{\mathrm{posform}}{\left(G\right)}]
, which is fair-SAT if
and only if there exists a real value
\alpha
for which

{\mathrm{peval}}{\left({\mathrm{posform}}{\left(G\right)},x,\alpha\right)}
is fair-SAT. Note that
G
and

{\mathrm{posform}}{\left(G\right)}
have the same atoms in their corresponding positions,
except that, in some cases,
G
may have
f\ \sigma\ g
at a position
at which
{\mathrm{posform}}{\left(G\right)}
has
f\ \overline{\sigma}\ g
. Let
G^{*}
be
the formula resulting from replacing each instance of such an
inequality in

G

with
\neg[f\ \overline{\sigma}\ g]
. Note that by the previous
theorem,
G^{*}
and
G
are fair-SAT equivalent. Note that

{\mathrm{posform}}{\left(G\right)}={\mathrm{posform}}{\left(G^{*}\right)}
. Since
G^{*}
and
{\mathrm{posform}}{\left(G^{*}\right)}
have
the same atoms, for any real value
\alpha
,

{\mathrm{posform}}{\left({\mathrm{peval}}{\left(G^{*},x,\alpha\right)}\right)}={\mathrm{peval}}{\left({\mathrm{posform}}{\left(G^{*}\right)},x,\alpha\right)}
and
so by induction
{\mathrm{peval}}{\left(G^{*},x,\alpha\right)}
is fair-SAT equivalent to

{\mathrm{peval}}{\left({\mathrm{posform}}{\left(G^{*}\right)},x,\alpha\right)}
. So
{\mathrm{peval}}{\left(G,x,\alpha\right)}
is fair-SAT equivalent to

{\mathrm{peval}}{\left({\mathrm{posform}}{\left(G\right)},x,\alpha\right)}
. Therefore,
F
and
F^{\prime}
are
fair-SAT equivalent.

6.

F=\forall x[G]
.
F
is fair-SAT if and only if
\exists x[{\mathrm{flop}}{\left(G\right)}]

is not fair-SAT.
F^{\prime}={\mathrm{posform}}{\left(F\right)}=\forall x[{\mathrm{posform}}{\left(G\right)}]

is fair-SAT if and only if
\exists x[{\mathrm{flop}}{\left({\mathrm{posform}}{\left(G\right)}\right)}]

is not fair-SAT.

Lemma 2
.

{\mathrm{flop}}{\left(G\right)}={\mathrm{flop}}{\left({\mathrm{posform}}{\left(G\right)}\right)}
, and

{\mathrm{flip}}{\left(G\right)}={\mathrm{flip}}{\left({\mathrm{posform}}{\left(G\right)}\right)}

(note: “
=
” here means syntactically identical).

This lemma is easily seen to be true, as flip and flop compute a
posform as they go.

Since
\exists x[{\mathrm{flop}}{\left(G\right)})]
and
\exists x[{\mathrm{flop}}{\left({\mathrm{posform}}{\left(G\right)}\right)}]
are
syntactically identical, they are fair-SAT equivalent.

∎

4.3
Fair-SATness and quantifiers

The last class of rewritings we prove preserve fair-SATness consists
of the usual rewritings involving quantifiers, e.g.
rewriting
A\wedge\exists x[B]
as
\exists x[A\wedge B]
(assuming

x
is not free in
A
). To help with these proofs, we introduce
“
{\mathrm{fse}_{\alpha}}{\left(F\right)}
”,
the “fair-SAT-evaluation of formula
F
at point
\alpha
”.

Definition 15
.

Let
F
be a formula that is
peval-safe
, with the further stipulation that
no negated subformula contains quantifiers. We will refer to the
variables appearing in
F
in left-to-right order as
x_{k_{1}},\ldots,x_{k_{n}}
.
Given

\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
the

fair-SAT evaluation of
F
at
\alpha
, denoted

{\mathrm{fse}_{\alpha}}{\left(F\right)}
(or
{\mathrm{fse}}{\left(F\right)}
if it is clear
what point
\alpha
we are evaluating at), is the
boolean formula defined by the following rules:
(observe that this definition directly follows the definition of fair-SAT)

1.

if
F
contains only boolean constants and propositional
variables,

{\mathrm{fse}}{\left(F\right)}=\forall U_{i_{1}},\ldots,U_{i_{a}}\exists V_{j_{1}},\ldots,V_{j_{b}}[F],

where

U_{i_{1}},\ldots,U_{i_{a}}
are the
U
-variables and

V_{j_{1}},\ldots,V_{j_{b}}
the
V
-variables in
F
.

{\mathrm{fse}}{\left(F\right)}=\neg{\mathrm{fse}}{\left(\exists x_{i}[{\mathrm{flop}}{\left(G\right)}]\right)}

Note: if this case applies it takes precedence over other cases.

2.

Note: if
F=\neg G
the requirements of
F
mean that we
are in case 1.

3.

if
F=G\vee H
, then

{\mathrm{fse}}{\left(F\right)}={\mathrm{fse}}{\left(G\right)}\vee{\mathrm{fse}}{\left(H\right)}

4.

if
F=G\wedge H
, then

{\mathrm{fse}}{\left(F\right)}={\mathrm{fse}}{\left(G\right)}\wedge{\mathrm{fse}}{\left(H\right)}

5.

if
F=\exists x_{k_{1}}[G]
then

{\mathrm{fse}_{(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})}}{\left(F\right)}={\mathrm{fse}_{(\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n})}}{\left({\mathrm{peval}}{\left(G,x_{k_{1}},\alpha_{1}\right)}\right)}

6.

if
F=\forall x_{k_{1}}[G]
then

{\mathrm{fse}}{\left(F\right)}=\neg{\mathrm{fse}}{\left(\exists x_{k_{1}}[{\mathrm{flop}}{\left(G\right)}]\right)}

The mechanics of this definition, though perhaps not its utility, may
be made clearer with a concrete example.
Let
F:=\forall x[\exists y[y-1/x^{2}>0\vee x/(y-1)\neq 0]]
.
We illustrate
{\mathrm{fse}}{\left(F\right)}
at four different points.

\begin{array}[]{ll}\begin{array}[]{l}{\mathrm{fse}_{(2,3)}}{\left(F\right)}\\
=\neg{\mathrm{fse}_{(2,3)}}{\left(\exists x[\forall y[y-1/x^{2}\leq 0\wedge x/(y-1)=0]]\right)}\\
=\neg{\mathrm{fse}_{(3)}}{\left(\forall y[y-1/4\leq 0\wedge 2/(y-1)=0]\right)}\\
={\mathrm{fse}_{(3)}}{\left(\exists y[y-1/4>0\vee 2/(y-1)\neq 0]\right)}\\
={\mathrm{fse}}{\left(\textsc{true}\vee\textsc{true}\right)}\\
=\textsc{true}\vee\textsc{true}\end{array}&\begin{array}[]{l}{\mathrm{fse}_{(0,3)}}{\left(F\right)}\\
=\neg{\mathrm{fse}_{(0,3)}}{\left(\exists x[\forall y[y-1/x^{2}\leq 0\wedge x/(y-1)=0]]\right)}\\
=\neg{\mathrm{fse}_{(3)}}{\left(\forall y[U_{1}\wedge 0/(y-1)=0]\right)}\\
={\mathrm{fse}_{(3)}}{\left(\exists y[\neg V_{1}\vee 0/(y-1)\neq 0]\right)}\\
={\mathrm{fse}}{\left(\neg V_{1}\vee\textsc{false}\right)}\\
=\exists V_{1}[\neg V_{1}\vee\textsc{false}]\end{array}\end{array}

\begin{array}[]{ll}\begin{array}[]{l}{\mathrm{fse}_{(2,1)}}{\left(F\right)}\\
=\neg{\mathrm{fse}_{(2,1)}}{\left(\exists x[\forall y[y-1/x^{2}\leq 0\wedge x/(y-1)=0]]\right)}\\
=\neg{\mathrm{fse}_{(1)}}{\left(\forall y[y-1/4\leq 0\wedge 2/(y-1)=0]\right)}\\
={\mathrm{fse}_{(1)}}{\left(\exists y[y-1/4>0\vee 2/(y-1)\neq 0]\right)}\\
={\mathrm{fse}}{\left(\textsc{true}\vee U_{1}\right)}\\
=\forall U_{1}[\textsc{true}\vee U_{1}]\end{array}&\begin{array}[]{l}{\mathrm{fse}_{(0,1)}}{\left(F\right)}\\
=\neg{\mathrm{fse}_{(0,1)}}{\left(\exists x[\forall y[y-1/x^{2}\leq 0\wedge x/(y-1)=0]]\right)}\\
=\neg{\mathrm{fse}_{(1)}}{\left(\forall y[U_{1}\wedge 0/(y-1)=0]\right)}\\
={\mathrm{fse}_{(1)}}{\left(\exists y[\neg V_{1}\vee 0/(y-1)\neq 0]\right)}\\
={\mathrm{fse}}{\left(\neg V_{1}\vee U_{2}\right)}\\
=\exists V_{1}\forall U_{2}[\neg V_{1}\vee U_{2}]\end{array}\end{array}

The purpose of defining
{\mathrm{fse}}{\left(F\right)}
is that it gives us a different way
to talk about formulas being fair-SAT, which is made clear by the
following theorem. Subsequently, we will leverage this new way to
characterize fair-SAT in order to prove that the usual rewritings
involving quantifiers preserve fair-SATness.

Theorem 6
.

Let formula
F
satisfy the
requirements of Definition
15
.
Let
x_{k_{1}},\ldots,x_{k_{n}}
be the variables in
F
in the
left-to-right order they are introduced, with respective
quantifiers
Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{n}
.

F
is fair-SAT if and only if

Q_{1}\alpha_{1}Q_{2}\alpha_{2}\cdots Q_{n}\alpha_{n}[{\mathrm{fse}_{(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})}}{\left(F\right)}]
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
k=\sum_{i}^{n}\left(\text{1 if $Q_{i}=\exists$ and 2 otherwise}\right)
.
When
k=0
we are in case 1 of the defintion of
{\mathrm{fse}}{\left(\right)}
, which is
the same as the definition of fair-SAT, so the theorem holds. Assume

k>0
.

If
F=A\vee B
, then
F
is fair-SAT if and only if
A
is fair-SAT
or
B
is fair-SAT.
By definition,
{\mathrm{fse}}{\left(F\right)}={\mathrm{fse}}{\left(A\right)}\vee{\mathrm{fse}}{\left(B\right)}
.
Note that for some
r
,
x_{k_{1}},\ldots,x_{k_{r}}
are the variables appearing
in
A
, and
x_{k_{r+1}},\ldots,x_{k_{n}}
are the variables appearing in
B
,
so

Q_{1}\alpha_{1}Q_{2}\alpha_{2}\cdots Q_{n}\alpha_{n}[{\mathrm{fse}_{(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})}}{\left(F\right)}]\Leftrightarrow Q_{1}\alpha_{1}\cdots Q_{r}\alpha_{r}[{\mathrm{fse}_{(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{r})}}{\left(A\right)}]\vee Q_{r+1}\alpha_{r+1}\cdots Q_{k}\alpha_{k}[{\mathrm{fse}_{(\alpha_{r+1},\ldots,\alpha_{k})}}{\left(B\right)}]
.
By induction,
A
is fair-SAT if and only if

Q_{1}\alpha_{1}\cdots Q_{r}\alpha_{r}[{\mathrm{fse}_{(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{r})}}{\left(A\right)}]

and
B
is fair-SAT if and only if

Q_{r+1}\alpha_{r+1}\cdots Q_{n}\alpha_{n}[{\mathrm{fse}_{(\alpha_{r+1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})}}{\left(B\right)}]
,
so the theorem holds in this case. The argument for the

F=A\wedge B
is basically the same.

If
F=\exists x_{k_{1}}[G]
, then
F
is fair-SAT if and only if there
exists an
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
such that
{\mathrm{peval}}{\left(G,x_{k_{1}},\beta\right)}

is fair-SAT. Note that
Q_{1}=\exists
. Assume
F
is fair-SAT with

\beta
being a satisfactory value for
x_{k_{1}}
,
then
{\mathrm{peval}}{\left(G,x_{k_{1}},\beta\right)}
is fair-SAT, and by induction
the formula
Q_{2}\alpha_{2}\cdots Q_{n}\alpha_{n}{\mathrm{fse}_{(\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n})}}{\left({\mathrm{peval}}{\left(G,x_{k_{1}},\beta\right)}\right)}

is true. Since

{\mathrm{fse}_{(\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n})}}{\left({\mathrm{peval}}{\left(G,x_{k_{1}},\beta\right)}\right)}={\mathrm{fse}_{(\beta,\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n})}}{\left(F\right)}
,
we get that

\exists\beta Q_{2}\alpha_{2}\cdots Q_{n}\alpha_{n}[{\mathrm{fse}_{(\beta,\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n})}}{\left(G\right)}]
is true,
which is what we had to prove.
Starting with
\exists\beta Q_{2}\alpha_{2}\cdots Q_{n}\alpha_{n}[{\mathrm{fse}_{(\beta,\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n})}}{\left(G\right)}]

being true, we can reverse this argument to show that
F
is fair-SAT.

If
F=\forall x_{k_{1}}[G]
, then
F
is fair-SAT if and only if

\exists x_{k_{1}}[{\mathrm{flop}}{\left(G\right)}]
is not fair-SAT, which is equivalent to
the assertion that for any
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
,
{\mathrm{peval}}{\left({\mathrm{flop}}{\left(G\right)},x_{k_{1}},\beta\right)}
is not
fair-SAT.
By induction,

{\mathrm{peval}}{\left({\mathrm{flop}}{\left(G\right)},x_{k_{1}},\beta\right)}
is not fair-SAT
if and only if
\neg[\overline{Q}_{2}\alpha_{2}\cdots\overline{Q}_{n}\alpha_{n}[{\mathrm{fse}_{(\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n})}}{\left({\mathrm{peval}}{\left({\mathrm{flop}}{\left(G\right)},x_{k_{1}},\beta\right)}\right)}]]
,
which we can rewrite as

Q_{2}\alpha_{2}\cdots Q_{n}\alpha_{n}[\neg{\mathrm{fse}_{(\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n})}}{\left({\mathrm{peval}}{\left({\mathrm{flop}}{\left(G\right)},x_{k_{1}},\beta\right)}\right)}]
,
which is equivalent to

Q_{2}\alpha_{2}\cdots Q_{n}\alpha_{n}[\neg{\mathrm{fse}_{(\beta,\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n})}}{\left(\exists x_{k_{1}}{\mathrm{flop}}{\left(G\right)}\right)}],

which is equivalent to

Q_{2}\alpha_{2}\cdots Q_{n}\alpha_{n}[{\mathrm{fse}_{(\beta,\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n})}}{\left(F\right)}]],

The assertion that this holds for any
\beta
is

\forall\beta Q_{2}\alpha_{2}\cdots Q_{n}\alpha_{n}[{\mathrm{fse}_{(\beta,\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n})}}{\left(F\right)}]],

which is what we had to prove.
∎

Corollary 3
.

Let
F
and
G
be two formulas satisfying the requirements of
Theorem
6
, with the same variables introduced in
the same order with the same quantifiers.
If for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

the quantified boolean formulas
{\mathrm{fse}_{\alpha}}{\left(F\right)}
and

{\mathrm{fse}_{\alpha}}{\left(G\right)}
are equivalent, then
F
and
G
are fair-SAT equivalent.

Now we start proving that the most fundamental rewritings we expect for
first-order logic preserve fair-SATness.

Theorem 7
.

If formula
\exists x_{1}[G]\wedge H
satisfies the requirements of
Theorem
6
, then

\exists x_{1}[G]\wedge H
and
\exists x_{1}[G\wedge H]
are fair-SAT
equivalent.

Proof.

Noting that
H
contains no occurrences of
x_{1}
:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\mathrm{fse}_{\alpha}}{\left(\exists x_{1}[G]\wedge H\right)}&=&{\mathrm{fse}_{(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{r})}}{\left(\exists x_{1}[G]\right)}\wedge{\mathrm{fse}_{(\alpha_{r+1},\ldots,x_{n})}}{\left(H\right)}\\
&=&{\mathrm{fse}_{(\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{r})}}{\left({\mathrm{peval}}{\left(G,x_{1},\alpha_{1}\right)}\right)}\wedge{\mathrm{fse}_{(\alpha_{r+1},\ldots,x_{n})}}{\left(H\right)}\\
\end{array}

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\mathrm{fse}_{\alpha}}{\left(\exists x_{1}[G\wedge H]\right)}&=&{\mathrm{fse}_{(\alpha_{2},\ldots,x_{n})}}{\left({\mathrm{peval}}{\left(G\wedge H,x_{1},\alpha_{1}\right)}\right)}\\
&=&{\mathrm{fse}_{(\alpha_{2},\ldots,x_{n})}}{\left({\mathrm{peval}}{\left(G,x_{1},\alpha_{1}\right)}\wedge H\right)}\\
&=&{\mathrm{fse}_{(\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{r})}}{\left({\mathrm{peval}}{\left(G,x_{1},\alpha_{1}\right)}\right)}\wedge{\mathrm{fse}_{(\alpha_{r+1},\ldots,x_{n})}}{\left(H\right)}\\
\end{array}

∎

Theorem 8
.

\forall x[G]\wedge H
and
\forall x[G\wedge H]
are fair-SAT
equivalent.

Proof.

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\mathrm{fse}_{\alpha}}{\left(\forall x_{1}[G]\wedge H\right)}&=&{\mathrm{fse}_{(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{r})}}{\left(\forall x_{1}[G]\right)}\wedge{\mathrm{fse}_{(\alpha_{r+1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})}}{\left(H\right)}\\
&=&\neg{\mathrm{fse}_{(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{r})}}{\left(\exists x_{1}[{\mathrm{flop}}{\left(G\right)}]\right)}\wedge{\mathrm{fse}_{(\alpha_{r+1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})}}{\left(H\right)}\\
\end{array}

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\mathrm{fse}_{\alpha}}{\left(\forall x_{1}[G\wedge H]\right)}&=&\neg{\mathrm{fse}_{(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})}}{\left(\exists x_{1}[{\mathrm{flop}}{\left(G\wedge H\right)}]\right)}\\
&=&\neg{\mathrm{fse}_{(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})}}{\left(\exists x_{1}[{\mathrm{flop}}{\left(G\right)}\vee{\mathrm{flop}}{\left(H\right)}]\right)}\\
&=&\neg\left[{\mathrm{fse}_{(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{r})}}{\left(\exists x_{1}[{\mathrm{flop}}{\left(G\right)}]\right)}\vee{\mathrm{fse}_{(\alpha_{r+1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})}}{\left({\mathrm{flop}}{\left(H\right)}\right)}\right]\\
&=&\neg{\mathrm{fse}_{(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{r})}}{\left(\exists x_{1}[{\mathrm{flop}}{\left(G\right)}]\right)}\wedge\neg{\mathrm{fse}_{(\alpha_{r+1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})}}{\left({\mathrm{flop}}{\left(H\right)}\right)}]\\
\end{array}

∎

Theorem 9
.

Assume
H
satisfies the conditions of Definition
15
.

{\mathrm{fse}_{\alpha}}{\left(H\right)}\Leftrightarrow\neg{\mathrm{fse}_{\alpha}}{\left({\mathrm{flop}}{\left(H\right)}\right)}

Proof.

Consider the case that
H
has no real variables. The theorem holds
in this case as a consequence of Corollary
2
.

Consider the case
H=A\ op\ B
, where
op
is
\wedge
or
\vee
.
Then
{\mathrm{fse}}{\left(H\right)}={\mathrm{fse}}{\left(A\right)}\ op\ {\mathrm{fse}}{\left(B\right)}
,

{\mathrm{flop}}{\left(H\right)}={\mathrm{flop}}{\left(A\right)}\ \overline{op}\ {\mathrm{flop}}{\left(B\right)}
, and

{\mathrm{fse}}{\left({\mathrm{flop}}{\left(H\right)}\right)}={\mathrm{fse}}{\left({\mathrm{flop}}{\left(A\right)}\right)}\ \overline{op}\ {\mathrm{fse}}{\left({\mathrm{flop}}{\left(B\right)}\right)}
.
By induction,
{\mathrm{fse}}{\left(A\right)}\Leftrightarrow\neg{\mathrm{fse}}{\left(flop{A}\right)}
and

{\mathrm{fse}}{\left(B\right)}\Leftrightarrow\neg{\mathrm{fse}}{\left(flop{B}\right)}
.
So

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\neg{\mathrm{fse}}{\left({\mathrm{flop}}{\left(H\right)}\right)}&=&\neg{\mathrm{fse}}{\left({\mathrm{flop}}{\left(A\right)}\right)}\ op\ \neg{\mathrm{fse}}{\left({\mathrm{flop}}{\left(B\right)}\right)}\\
&\Leftrightarrow&{\mathrm{fse}}{\left(A\right)}\ op\ {\mathrm{fse}}{\left(B\right)}.\end{array}

Consider the case that
H=\forall x_{k_{1}}[G]
.
By definition
{\mathrm{fse}}{\left(H\right)}=\neg{\mathrm{fse}}{\left(\exists x_{k_{1}}[{\mathrm{flop}}{\left(G\right)}]\right)}
, which is
exactly
\neg{\mathrm{fse}}{\left({\mathrm{flop}}{\left(G\right)}\right)}
.

Finally, consider the case
H=\exists x_{k_{1}}[G]
. By definition,

{\mathrm{fse}_{\alpha}}{\left(H\right)}={\mathrm{fse}}{\left({\mathrm{peval}}{\left(G,x_{k_{1}},\alpha_{1}\right)}\right)}{(\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n})}.

\neg{\mathrm{fse}_{\alpha}}{\left({\mathrm{flop}}{\left(H\right)}\right)}=\neg{\mathrm{fse}_{\alpha}}{\left(\forall x_{k_{1}}[{\mathrm{flop}}{\left(G\right)}]\right)}=\neg\neg{\mathrm{fse}_{\alpha}}{\left(\exists x_{k_{1}}[{\mathrm{flop}}{\left({\mathrm{flop}}{\left(G\right)}\right)}]\right)}={\mathrm{fse}}{\left({\mathrm{peval}}{\left({\mathrm{flop}}{\left({\mathrm{flop}}{\left(G\right)}\right)},x_{k_{1}},\alpha_{1}\right)}\right)}{(\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n})}.

∎

Corollary 4
.

If
\forall x[G]
and

are

, with

(i.e. with no

\forall x[G]
and

are fair-SAT equivalent,

and
is positive prenex form are fair-SAT equivalent, which is important,

5
Correctness of the translation procedure

Theorem 10
.

Algorithm translate(
F
,
A
) meets it specification.

Proof.

First note that in every
{\mathrm{div}}{\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)}
term in
A^{*}
we
maintain the property that the numerators do not contain

{\mathrm{div}}{\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)}
terms as we make the rewriting steps on lines 7
and 13.

Let
I=\{\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{n}|A^{*}\text{ illegal at }\gamma\}
.
and
N=\{\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{n}|\gamma\text{ satisfies
}\bigvee_{i=1}^{k+1}\bigvee_{G\in N_{i}}G\}
, and consider the
conditions:

1.

I\cup N=\{\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{n}|A\text{ illegal at }\gamma\}
.

2.

If
{\mathrm{div}}{\left(q^{\prime},p^{\prime}\right)}
is an occurrence of a div in
A^{*}
and

{\mathrm{div}}{\left(1,p\right)}
the associated occurrence of a div in
A
,
then for any
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

{\mathrm{peval}}{\left({\mathrm{div}}{\left(q^{\prime},p^{\prime}\right)},(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}),\gamma\right)}={\mathrm{peval}}{\left({\mathrm{div}}{\left(1,p\right)},(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}),\gamma\right)}
.

3.

If
A
is legal at
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
then

{\mathrm{peval}}{\left(A,(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}),\gamma\right)}={\mathrm{peval}}{\left(A^{*},(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}),\gamma\right)}
.

These three conditions are loop invariant for both
“while” loops of translate(
F
,
A
).
They hold initially, when
A=A^{*}
and taking them all
together, it’s clear that they are maintained at each step of both
loops. By the time we get to line 14,
A^{*}
has no
{\mathrm{div}}{\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)}

terms, which means
N
is exactly equal to the points at which

A
is illegal, and
A^{*}
is illegal nowhere.

If
A
is legal at some point
\gamma^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then since loop
invariant (1) tells us that, after the two while

7
Conclusion

This paper arose out of an attempt to produce a whitepaper to explain
why, for computer algebra solvers specializing in
quantified polynomial constraints, supporting division in a principled
way is impossible / impractical / undesirable — that all we could do is require our clients,
whether human users or software systems interacting via APIs, to
provide us with “well-defined” input formulas, allowing us to clear
denominators naively and continue from that point with polynomials.
However, even that turned out to be difficult: what
constitutes a “well-defined” formula?

The project then morphed into trying to produce a notion of
“well-defined formula” in the presence of divisions. What should
one look for in a definition of a well-defined formula with divisions?
At a minimum, one would hope that a formula with divisions could be
made to be “well defined” by some form of guarding, which is a common
mathematical practice, and be invariant under some (many?) kinds of
rewritings, like moving around quantifiers and negations.
Moreover, it would be nice to have the
question of whether a formula is well defined be decidable.
Ultimately, this paper does indeed provide such a definition: a
formula is well-defined if the Tarski formula resulting from naively
clearing denominators (i.e. without guarding) is equivalent to the
Tarski formula produced by the
translate
algorithm. The
translate algorithm uses guarding, and the resulting definition of
“well-defined formula” is invariant under many common rewritings.

However, what has resulted from this work is not just a definition of
“well-defined”. Instead of showing why it is impossible (or at
least unreasonable) for a quantified polynomial constraint solver to
handle division in a principled way, the
translate
algorithm

is
a principled way to handle division. It has a well-defined
semantics and is not unduly sensitive to innocuous differences of
input formula phrasings. Moreover, if a user does not like those
semantics, for example does not like that
\forall x[1/x^{2}\geq 0]

evaluates to
true
under them, they have clear rules for what is
required to produce “well-defined” input formulas, whose meaning is
unaffected by the
translate
algorithm.

It is important to also recognize the limitations to this approach of
handling division. It does not make sense for theorem provers or SMT
solvers or other systems that manipulate formulas in first-order logic
generally, and for which real polynomial constraints represents just
one of many theories to be handled. This is because with the fair-SAT
semantics,
{\mathrm{div}}{\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)}
is not really a function in the
sense of first-order logic. We see
this in the fact that

\exists x[x=0\wedge[1/x=0\vee\neg(1/x=0)]]

is not fair-SAT equivalent to

\exists x[x=0\wedge\textsc{true}]
.
That said, if an SMT solver wants to use a computer algebra solver
as a “theory solver”, this paper’s definition of well-defined at least
provides a basis for how that communication should be handled in the
presence of divisions.
