Progression and risk factors of hallux valgus angle in elite adolescent dancers: a cohort study
Authors: Zijian Liu, Shuo Chen, Takumi Okunuki, Hiroki Yabiku, Takuma Hoshiba, Toshihiro Maemichi, Yanshu Li, Hanye Zhao, Tsukasa Kumai
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
IntroductionTo the best of our knowledge, no previous studies have linked the progression of hallux valgus (HV) with plantar pressure distribution. Therefore, this study aimed to determine the progression of HV angle in elite adolescent dancers and evaluate the risk factors associated with foot plantar pressure during demi-pointe movement.MethodsFor this cohort study, 40 adolescent dancesport dancers (age: 14.5 ± 1.3 years; height: 168.3 ± 8.3 cm; weight: 52.4 ± 8.1 kg) were recruited from a dancesport specialty school. All participants reported no foot or lower limb injuries or symptoms in the past year and were not undergoing any rehabilitation or medication treatment. The HV angle was measured by photography (HVAp). The HVAp survey included baseline and follow-up assessments after 1 year. Foot plantar pressures (kPa) were obtained using the F-Scan measurement system (Tekscan, Tokyo, Japan), and the data were divided into two categories based on the foot location (type 1: hallux, toe, and metatarsophalangeal [MTP] joint area and type 2: medial, middle, and lateral areas). Data were analyzed using the Pearson’s chi-square test and multiple logistic regression.ResultsThe HVAp measurement demonstrated excellent reliability. A significant increase of 2.1° ± 3.5° in the HV angle of elite adolescent dancers was found at the 1-year follow-up assessment relative to that at baseline. There were significant correlations between the HVAp variation values and the hallux (r = − 0.480, P = 0.002), toe (r = − 0.313, P = 0.049), and MTP joint area (r = 0.446, P = 0.004) plantar pressure percentages in the type 1 category and medial area (r = − 0.331, P = 0.037) and middle area (r = 0.386, P = 0.014) pressure percentages in the type 2 category. Based on the multivariate logistic regression analysis, the HVAp variation values were associated with foot plantar pressure in the hallux (odds ratio [OR]: − 0.12, 95% confidence interval [CI]: − 0.191 to − 0.048, P = 0.002) and middle areas (OR: 0.09, 95% CI: 0.019 – 0.161, and P = 0.014) from the two categories, respectively.ConclusionThis study demonstrated significant progression of the HV angle in elite adolescent dancers over 1 year, which was significantly associated with increased foot plantar pressure in the middle area and decreased plantar pressure in the hallux area. These findings highlight the importance of monitoring and addressing foot plantar pressure in adolescent dancers.

Introduction

Hallux valgus (HV), introduced by Carl Heuterin in 1871, comprises a deformity of the first metatarsophalangeal (MTP) joint [1] that considerably affects patients’ quality of life [2]. In patients with this deformity, the affected first MTP joint plantar flexion range of motion is limited [3], and the risk of fall increases [4]. Patients’ sex (female) [5], and older age (> 65 years of age) [6] have been reported as risk factors for HV occurrence. Moreover, wearing high heels [7, 8], excessive weight bearing on the forefoot [9], and a lower transverse arch in the forefoot [10] are predisposing factors for HV.

Besides dancing, no other occupations have been associated with the HV occurrence [11]. One study reported that there was insufficient evidence to prove that HV is more common in dancers than in the general population [12], with subsequent studies supporting this view [13–15]. A survey of dancers with HV revealed that it affected their quality of life [16]. Another study of dancers suggested that a greater HV angle may decrease the plantar pressure in the hallux area during the demi-pointe movement [17]. Furthermore, a previous study on female ballet dancers reported that a greater HV angle during the plie technique resulted in greater retroversion of the pelvis [18]; therefore, HV occurrence in dancers should receive adequate attention. In studies involving the general population, HV occurrence typically results in decreased pressure in the hallux area, which may be related to functional impairment caused by metatarsal joint deformity [19]. Individuals with HV have also been observed to exhibit decreased pressure in the toe area (2–5 toes) and metatarsal heads (2–4 metatarsal heads) [20]. HV can affect an individual’s quality of life and alter plantar pressure distribution in dancers as well as in the general population, particularly in the hallux area. However, the relationship between plantar pressure distribution and HV progression has not been thoroughly discussed.

The demi-pointe, as the most representative movement in dance, is based on maximum ankle plantar flexion (90°–100°) and dorsiflexion (90°–100°) of the MTP joint [21]. Although it can provide a beautiful body posture, the movement requires that one’s entire body weight is placed on the forefeet during this movement, especially on the MTP head [22]. The transverse arch is formed by five MTP heads, with its apex located at the second MTP head [23]. The second and third MTP heads have been shown to exhibit significantly higher plantar pressure than the first MTP heads and fourth and fifth MTP heads during the mid-stance phase of the gait cycle [24]. One study reported that patients with moderate HV exhibited higher pressure in the forefoot central area than individuals without HV [25]; thus, the pressure in the forefoot central area is likely to be strongly associated with HV occurrence. Although, higher pressure on the transverse arch has been reported to lead to HV [9, 10], to the best of our knowledge, no previous studies have specifically linked HV progression with plantar pressure distribution.

Therefore, we speculated that HV occurrence and progression in elite adolescent dancers might be related to dance representative dance movements, such as the demi-pointe and could be influenced by the distribution of plantar pressure. We hypothesized that the HV angle increases following training and that a higher forefoot central area pressure on the MTP head during dance movements represents a risk factor for HV. However, relevant data and reports are lacking. Therefore, this study aimed to clarify the change in the HV angle in elite adolescent dancers and identify the associated risk factors for HV deformities.

Method

Participants

Dancers who had been training at the dancesport specialty school for at least 1 year were recruited for this cohort study. The training program at this school required all students to participate in 4 h of scheduled training classes and 2 h of individual practice from Monday to Friday, with an additional 2 h of individual practice each day on weekends. This system ensured that students engaged in at least 34 h of dance training per week. According to the school's policy, all students were required to participate in intra-school competitions each semester and were encouraged to actively participate in national or provincial competitions. Recruited adolescent participants were defined as those aged 10–19 years, as per the World Health Organization criteria (https://www.who.int/health-topics/adolescent-health#tab=tab_1).

Predictor correlations were used to calculate the total sample size a priori using G*Power 3.1.2 software (Franz Faul, University of Kiel, Kiel, Germany). The study parameters were set by referencing a similar previous study [26] and using four parameters (tail[s]: 2; effect size |ρ|: 0.5; α error prob [err prob]: 0.05; and power [1 – β err prob]: 0.8) to calculate the sample size. The results indicated that the study required at least 26 participants. All participants were recruited by their respective dancesport specialty school coaches, who provided and explained the details of the study to them. Initially, 128 elite adolescent dancesport dancers were recruited for this study. Dancers wtih foot or lower limb injuries or symptoms in the past year were excluded from the study, as were those who were suspended from school or had ceased training for ≧3 months in the past year or had undergone foot surgery and treatment. Additionally, during the one-year follow-up, some dancers reported injuries such as ankle sprains and withdrew from the study. Finally, 40 participants were included in the cohort study (elimination rates: 68.8%).

All participants were informed of the study purpose and the experimental procedure, and informed consent was obtained from all participants and their parents. This study was approved by the Ethical Review Committee on Research Involving Human Subjects at Waseda University (No.2019–343).

Protocol

Data regarding the dancers’age and training information (athletic career and weekly training time) were collected using a questionnaire. Participants’ physical information was based on the data collected by a school doctor. The study was conducted at the participants’ school to ensure that they would participate in the second measurement of the experiment. The HV angle and foot plantar measurement were measured at the first time point (first measurement), before conducting a second measurement for the same participant 1 year later. Both measurements were performed by a clinical physical therapist with over 5 years of experience in HV research.

Demographic data

Anthropometric measurements including height and weight were obtained for all participants. Height was measured using a Seca 213 portable stadiometer (Seca GmbH & Co. KG, Hamburg, Germany), with the participants positioned in a standard anatomical gaze-ahead posture, ensuring that the buttocks and the back of their heads were in contact with the machine. Weight was measured using a digital scale (OMRON, HN-289, Kyoto, Japan).

HV angle

The HV angle was measured using foot photography (HVAp) as previously reported [27]. The participants stood with their feet parallel and with equal weights on both feet. A camera(SX430is; Canon, Tokyo, Japan) was set up to simulate the X-rays and was set to photograph participants at a position of 15° oblique to the front of the second midfoot bone [27].

ImageJ software (1.53 k; National Institutes of Health Bethesda, MD, USA) was used to perform the HVAp measurements. A tangent line (AB) was drawn from the medial edge of the big toe (A) to the inner edge of the first metatarsal head (B). Point C was marked such that lines AB and BC were at the same distance from the contact point (B) along the medial side of the first metatarsal. The angle α between lines AB and BC was the metatarsal angle (Fig. 1) [27]. The HV angle for all participants was measured by a clinical physical therapist with > 6 years of experience in HV research. Each photograph was measured three times, and the intra-rater reliability was calculated based on the three sets of data obtained.Fig. 1Method for measuring the hallux valgus angle

The HVAp measurements were repeated to assess the intra-rater reliability (intraclass correlation [ICC] 1,1). The reliability thresholds for ICC values were defined as poor (< 0.50), moderate (0.50–0.75), good (0.75–0.90), and excellent (> 0.90) [28].

Foot plantar pressure measurements

A film-type pressure distribution measurement system (F-scan II; Tekscan, Japan) was used to measure the foot plantar pressure (kPa). This system has frequently been employed in previous studies to assess plantar pressure and weight distribution and is known for its high accuracy [19, 29]. Foot plantar pressure measurement was only collected in the first measurement. All participants stood on the pressure distribution measurement film sensor with their knees and hips in a neutral position, their backs straight, and their gaze directed forward. The system was calibrated for each patient using the patient’s weight before each testing session. We set the sensor to a 30° external rotation to the sagittal plane (60° in total) and instructed all participants to stand upright with their hands on their hips and to turn their lower limbs outward at 60°. Starting from a stable preparatory position, participants raised their heels to the demi-pointe position, as practiced in their regular training, before beginning the pressure distribution measurement. The data collection period was adjusted to 10 s, with a collection frequency of 50 frames/s.

The foot plantar pressure data were divided into three areas using two methods. Following the first method, the foot plantar pressure data were divided into the hallux, toe, and MTP joint areas (Fig. 2, type 1). Following the second method, the data were divided into the medial, middle, and lateral areas from the tibial side (Fig. 2, type 2). The plantar pressure percentage for each area (%pressure) was calculated as the ratio of the pressure applied to each area to the total pressure applied to the right foot.Fig. 2Division of the hallux, toe, and metatarsophalangeal (MTP) joint areas. The measurement error associated with repeated HVAp measurements was evaluated using the standard error measurement (SEM) and minimal detectable change (MDC). Data were analyzed were conducted using SPSS (version 29.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to verify the normality of the distribution of continuous variables. Paired-samples t-tests were used to analyze the change between the two measurements and calculate the variation values (variation value = second measurement – first measurement). The Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used to determine the correlation between variation values and foot plantar pressure. Significant or nearly significant findings in the univariate analysis were included in the multivariate logistic regression analysis. A posthoc power analysis was performed, and 95% CIs were calculated to estimate the effect size (d) and power (1–β err prob) using G*Power statistical software for t-tests. The effect size (d) categories were 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 for small, medium, and large effects, respectively [30]. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Discussion

This study revealed that an average annual increase of 2.1° in the HV angle and progression of HV in elite adolescent dancers could be related to higher foot plantar pressure in the middle area. This finding provides evidence for the high prevalence of HV among dancers.

Most previous studies have reported a several-fold higher incidence of HV in older adults than in other age groups [5, 6] However, this result does not imply the absence of HV in adolescents. Juvenile HV deformity is the varus deviation of the first metatarsal and valgus deviation of the first proximal phalanx in a skeletally immature child [31]. Although the overall prevalence of HV is highest in the older population (aged > 65 years), the deformity can frequently begin during adolescence or even in juveniles in many cases [32]. A previous study estimated that 40% − 50% of adult HV cases originate during childhood [33]. One study reported an HV prevalence of 7.8% in adolescents; however, follow-up was not performed [34]. In a longitudinal study of HV progression in 69 children (133 feet), the HV angle increased by 1.5° per year in patients aged < 10 years of age, whereas the HV angle progression in patients aged > 10 years of age was not statistically significant [35]. In a longitudinal study involving older individuals with HV, it was demonstrated that the length of the follow-up period may not be the cause of an increase in the HV angle [36]. In the present study, we included elite dancesport dancers and observed a significant increase in their HV angle during the follow-up period. A previous study demonstrated a correlation between dancesport training and the HV occurrence in adolescent dancesport athletes [37]. The participants in this study trained for at least 20 h per week during the 1-year follow-up period, which may have contributed to the difference in results compared to earlier studies. Based on our findings, elite dancesport dancers exhibited a significant increase in the HV angle after 1 year of pre-professional training. Therefore, regular follow-ups are crucial for elite dancers.

This study calculated the SEM and MDC to evaluate the variation value of HVAp. In previous studies, SEM and MDC have commonly been used as important indicators [38, 39], SEM reflects the random error introduced by the measurement tool or method [40], while MDC is used to identify the degree of change in the data. Changes greater than MDC are generally considered to be statistically significant [41], and the SEM and MDC can help determine whether the observed changes are due to true pathological or physiological change. In this study, the mean HVAp change between years (2.1º) was lower than both the standard error of measurement (SEM = 3.31º) and the minimal detectable change (MDC = 7.84º). Therefore, while we observed a statistically significant change in HVAp over the tracking period (p < 0.001), this result could potentially be attributed to measurement error. Additionally, the standard deviation (SD = 3.5º) was greater than the mean change, suggesting a substantial degree of individual variability. However, these interpretations are grounded in statistical analysis and may do not necessarily imply that our findings lack clinical significance [42]. Previous studies have reported correlations between hallux valgus and parameters such as foot plantar pressure [27] and pain scale [43]. Future research should integrate clinical observations to better assess and discuss the significance of smaller changes in the hallux valgus angle and their potential impact on these parameters.

HV has only been reported in dancers, with no known cases observed among individuals in other occupations [11, 37]. However, studies and reports on factors influencing the HV angle in dancers are currently lacking. In studies involving the general population, cross-sectional research comparing the physical characteristics of individuals with and without HV is more common [26, 29]. However, there is a lack of longitudinal studies investigating the causative factors of HV development. Herein, we examined the relationship between plantar pressure and increase in the HV angle. Our findings indicate that excessive loading in the midplantar position affects the HV angle among elite adolescent dancesport dancers.

The shape of the transverse arch is an important factor in HV occurrence [3]. Moreover, a previous anatomical study using cadavers reported that through the metatarsal head, the capsule below the metatarsal joint is thickened to form the “plantar plate” [44]. The plantar plate of the MTP joint and the deep transverse metatarsal ligaments (DTML) form a continuous band of strong ligamentous tissue across the forefoot [44]. When the transverse arch bears a high load, the DTML are stretched, causing the sesamoid bone to move externally [45]. A previous study reported that metatarsal rotation is systematically associated with a parallel displacement of the sesamoid band [46]. An ultrasound imaging study from the plantar direction reported that increased loading on the foot in healthy individuals may increase the sesamoid rotation angle, which is a common clinical indicator used to measure sesamoid rotation [23]. Although previous studies have not specifically reported on the state of forefoot loading, we hypothesized that excessive forefoot loading similar to that during demi-pointe movement could also cause sesamoid and first metatarsal rotation. Moreover, a previous study reported that metatarsal pronation was associated with HV [47], and a clinical study also reported that HV occurrence is often accompanied by forefoot transverse arch collapse; [48] the authors described this phenomenon as an adaptive change [48]. Therefore, owing to the unique soft tissue structure of the metatarsal heads, excessive loading may be related to HV occurrence.

Furthermore, the DTML form a strong ligamentous structure that prevents undue splaying of the forefoot [44]. In an anatomical study, after severing the DTML in three cadavers, the distal ends of the metatarsals were tractioned. Consequently, the ligaments of the first metatarsal moved more medially, and the fourth and fifth metatarsals moved more laterally away from the second and third metatarsals [44]. No studies have specifically investigated the characteristics of plantar pressure during dance movements in dancers. During the gait circle in the general healthy population, the highest pressures were found at the second and third metatarsal heads and the heel in the mid-stance phase [24]. Another previous study reported that the greater the stress on the second and third metatarsals, the higher the DTML stiffness required [49]. In a previous study, the anterior cruciate ligament was repetitively loaded four times with the participants’ body weight, and a disruption of collagen fibrils in the form of reduced topographical surface thickness was observed [50]. Repetitive loading may decrease ligament stiffness [51], thereby impairing its ability to maintain normal morphology. During demi-pointe movement, the entire body’s weight is concentrated on the forefoot (hallux, toes, and MTP areas), especially on the second and third metatarsal heads. Prolonged and intense training can decrease DTML stiffness, potentially leading to DTML failure and causing the first metatarsal bone to deviate medially. Medial deviation of the first metatarsal bone increases the intermetatarsal angle (IMA). In a longitudinal study, an increased IMA was identified as a risk factor for HV progression and an increase in the HV angle [36]. Therefore, during the demi-pointe movement, increased pressure in the middle area may cause the first metatarsal bone to deviate medially, leading to HV progression.

Additionally, in this study, we found a significant correlation between decreased toe pressure, particularly hallux pressure, and a greater chance of an increase in the HV angle. After the decline in toe pressure, there is an increase in metatarsal head pressure during the demi-pointe technique. Future research on the progression and prevention of HV in dancers should also consider improving toe strength, particularly that of the hallux.

This study represents a preliminary follow-up analysis of HV in dancers and identified that the change in the HV angle might be caused by an overload of middle area plantar pressure. A relationship between the prevalence and progression of injury and plantar pressure has also been found in different sports [52–54]. Based on these findings, preventive and therapeutic measures for HV should be implemented among elite adolescent dancers. Previous studies have indicated that surgical intervention should be avoided in this population because of its potential implications for the MTP joint range of motion in dancers and skeletal development in adolescents [11, 33]. Rehabilitation may be a suitable treatment option for elite adolescent dancesport dancers. Previous studies have reported the used of foot exercises or splints to manage and treat HV in the general adult population [55–57]; however, rehabilitation interventions for dancers have not yet been explored. Future studies should implement rehabilitation interventions that specifically target dancers and evaluate their efficacy.

Limitations

Although we described HV progression and the factors influencing its development in elite adolescent dancesport dancers, a recent study suggested that adolescents may be genetically predisposed to HV development [32]. Therefore, we also investigated the presence of HV in the parents of our participants during the study. However, > 80% of the respondents were unaware of HV, precluding a genetic analysis in our research.

Further, only dancesport dancers who performed in high heels were included in this study; other types of dancers, e.g., ballet dancers, were not included. As high heels are considered an important factor in HV development, their influence cannot be completely disregarded. Therefore, future studies should investigate other types of dancers.

This study focused on adolescent dancesport dancers. However, HV occurrence and causes may differ between adults and adolescents in the general population [32]. Future research should also explore whether the development of HV development in adult dancers is related to foot plantar pressure during the demi-pointe movement.

Finally, because of the training structure of pre-professional dancers, all athletes typically enroll in professional schools for pre-professional training after 12 years of age. Therefore, research participants aged < 10 years should be investigated and followed up in future studies.

In this study, the observed HVAp change was smaller than both the SEM and MDC, which suggests that this result could be attributed to the limited precision of the measurement methods used for hallux valgus assessment. Therefore, future studies should consider using more precise methods, such as X-ray imaging. In previous longitudinal studies on hallux valgus progression, an increase of 1.5º was observed over 1 year in adolescents [35], while an increase of 1ºwas seen in older adults during a median follow-up period of 49 months [36]. This suggests that a one-year observation period may not be sufficient to detect meaningful changes in hallux valgus progression. Future research should extend the observation period or focus on observing individual variability to better capture these changes.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated a significant progression of HV angle in elite adolescent dancesport dancers over 1 year. An increase in the HV angle was also observed. This progression was significantly associated with variations in foot plantar pressure on the hallux, toe, MTP joint, medial, and middle areas, especially in the hallux and middle areas. These findings highlight the importance of monitoring foot plantar pressure to mitigate the risk of HV progression in adolescent dancers. Future studies should investigate preventive measures and interventions aimed at reducing forefoot pressure during dance activities. Specifically, longitudinal studies with larger sample sizes are needed to better understand the long-term impact of dance training on foot health in adolescents. Additionally, exploring the effectiveness of specific foot exercises, footwear modifications, and training adjustments could provide valuable insights into preventing HV progression and improving overall foot health in young dancers.
