Abstract
. We prove flexibility of two families of affine varieties: the complement in ℙ n \mathbb{P}^{n} of a projective quadric of rank at least three and affine cones over a smooth complete intersection of two quadrics in ℙ n + 2 \mathbb{P}^{n+2} , n ≥ 3 n\geq 3 .

Primary 14J50, 14L30, 14M10; Secondary 14J45, 14R20

1.
Introduction

The geometry of automorphism groups has long offered a revealing lens through which to view the structure of algebraic varieties. In the affine setting, varieties that admit an abundance of unipotent transformations exhibit particularly rich behaviour. Among them, the
flexible varieties
form a distinguished class: they are those for which every tangent direction at a smooth point arises from an additive group action. Such varieties can be regarded as algebraically homogeneous in the strongest possible sense.

Throughout we work over an algebraically closed field
\mathbb{K}
of characteristic zero. A
variety
means an integral separated scheme of finite type over
\mathbb{K}
. We write
\mathbb{G}_{\mathrm{a}}=(\mathbb{K},+)
for the one-dimensional additive algebraic group. For a variety
X
, a point
x\in X
is said to be
flexible
if the tangent space
T_{x}X
is spanned by tangent vectors to orbits of effective
\mathbb{G}_{\mathrm{a}}
-actions on
X
; the variety
X
is
flexible
if every smooth point is flexible. Denote by
\operatorname{SAut}(X)\subseteq\operatorname{Aut}(X)
the subgroup generated by all one-parameter unipotent subgroups of automorphisms of
X
. A fundamental theorem of Arzhantsev, Flenner, Kaliman, Kutzschebauch, and Zaidenberg
[
1
]
shows that flexibility is equivalent to a strong homogeneity property of this group:

Theorem 1.1

(
[
1
, Theorem 0.1]
)
.

Let
X
be an affine variety. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

X
is flexible;

(2)

\SAut(X)
acts transitively on the smooth locus
X^{\mathrm{reg}}
;

(3)

if
\dim X\geq 2
,
\SAut(X)
acts infinitely transitively on
X^{\mathrm{reg}}
.

This theorem—widely regarded as a cornerstone in the development of the theory—places the notion of flexibility at the intersection of algebraic transformation groups and birational geometry. It identifies a distinguished class of affine varieties whose automorphism groups behave much like those of homogeneous spaces.

The idea of flexibility first appeared in the study of affine toric varieties, where the presence of additive group actions can be described combinatorially in terms of the defining fan
[
2
]
. Over time, the concept proved far more general and has become deeply intertwined with the geometry of
Fano varieties
and their
affine cones
. Given a projective variety
X
and a very ample divisor
H
on
X
, one can form the affine cone
\AffCone_{H}(X)
over the embedding
X\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{N}
defined by the complete linear system
|H|
. A natural question, raised a little over a decade ago, asks for which projective
rational
varieties
X
this affine cone is flexible. This problem has gradually shaped the modern theory of flexible varieties, linking unipotent group actions with the geometry of Fano varieties.

The first decisive progress was made by Perepechko
[
13
]
, who classified flexible affine cones over del Pezzo surfaces: they occur precisely for degrees
\geq 4
, while cones over surfaces of smaller degree fail to be flexible. This revealed, already in dimension two, a sharp boundary between flexible and rigid behaviour. In higher dimensions, Prokhorov and Zaidenberg
[
15
]
established flexibility for the affine cones over Fano–Mukai fourfolds of genus 10, and later Hang and the second author
[
6
]
extended the result to fourfolds of genus 7 (cf.
[
9
,
7
]
). The work of Cheltsov, Park, Prokhorov, and Zaidenberg
[
4
,
11
,
12
]
developed a geometric framework based on
cylinders
—open subsets isomorphic to products with an affine space—and showed that a transversal covering by such cylinders often guarantees flexibility. Together, these results connected flexibility with core geometric structures of Fano varieties, including families of lines, birational projections, and degenerations of hyperplane sections.

Within this evolution, a natural and historically significant next step concerns the class of
smooth complete intersections of two quadrics
. These varieties occupy a central place in algebraic geometry, appearing in the early classification of Fano manifolds and re-emerging in modern studies of birational rigidity, derived categories, and moduli (
[
16
,
8
,
5
]
). For
n\geq 3
, such a variety

X=Q_{1}\cap Q_{2}\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n+2}

is a Fano variety of Picard number 1 and index
n-1
; in the case
n=2
, one recovers the del Pezzo surface of degree 4. Despite their classical nature, the behaviour of their affine cones under unipotent automorphisms remained open. The present paper closes this gap by proving that the affine cones over all such varieties are flexible, thereby extending the known frontier of flexibility to one of the most fundamental families in Fano geometry.

Theorem 1.2

(Main Theorem)
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n+2}
be a smooth complete intersection of two quadrics with
n\geq 3
. Then the affine cone over
X
with respect to any very ample divisor is flexible.

The proof is geometric in spirit. We begin by establishing the flexibility of the complement in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
of a quadric of rank at least three, achieved by constructing an explicit transversal covering by principal cylinders. We then employ a birational model due to Kishimoto, Prokhorov, and Zaidenberg that relates open subsets of
X
to such complements, thereby transferring flexibility to the affine cone. In this way, flexibility of the affine cone over the intersection of two quadrics emerges as a direct consequence of the geometry of its ambient projective space.

The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the correspondence between
\mathbb{G}_{\mathrm{a}}
actions and cylindrical open subsets in affine varieties. Section 3 establishes a general criterion for flexibility in terms of transversal coverings by cylinders. Section 4 is devoted to proving the flexibility of affine cones over quadrics of rank at least three. Section 5 then shows that the complement of a quadric of rank
\geq 3
in projective space admits a transversal cylinder covering and is therefore flexible. Finally, Section 6 applies these results to smooth complete intersections of two quadrics, completing the proof of the main theorem.

2.
Cylinders and actions of the additive group

In this section we discuss cylinders in affine varieties and their connection with
\mathbb{G}_{\mathrm{a}}
-actions.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
X
be a variety and
k
be a positive integer. By a
k
-cylinder in
X
we mean an open subset
U
together with an isomorphism
U\cong\mathbb{A}^{k}\times Z
for some affine variety
Z
. By an
\mathbb{A}^{k}
-fiber
of a
k
-cylinder
U
we mean a fiber of the projection
U\to Z
to the second factor.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\alpha\colon\mathbb{G}_{\mathrm{a}}^{k}\times X\to X
be a
\mathbb{G}_{\mathrm{a}}^{k}
-action on a variety
X
. We say that
\alpha
is
cylindrical
if there is an isomorphism
X\cong\mathbb{A}^{k}\times Z
for some affine variety
Z
such that
\alpha
acts by translations along the first factor.

Note that if
\alpha
is a cylindrical
\mathbb{G}_{\mathrm{a}}^{k}
-action on a variety
X\cong\mathbb{A}^{k}\times Z
, then
X
is affine and every
\mathbb{A}^{k}
-fiber of
X
is an orbit of
\alpha
.

Let
X
be an affine variety. Recall the connection between principal
1
-cylinders in
X
and effective
\mathbb{G}_{\mathrm{a}}
-actions on
X
from
[
11
, Proposition 3.1.5]
. If
U\subseteq X
is a principal
1
-cylinder, then there exists an effective
\mathbb{G}_{\mathrm{a}}
-action
\alpha
on
X
such that every point in
X\setminus U
is fixed by
\alpha
and every
\mathbb{A}^{1}
-fiber of
U
is an orbit of
\alpha
. Conversely, if
\alpha
is an effective
\mathbb{G}_{\mathrm{a}}
-action on
X
, then there exists an
\alpha
-invariant
1
-cylinder
U\subseteq X
such that
\left.\kern-1.2pt{\alpha}\right|_{U}
is cylindrical.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
X
be an affine variety and
\alpha
be an effective
\mathbb{G}_{\mathrm{a}}
-action on
X
. Assume that there is an isomorphism
X\cong\mathbb{A}^{1}\times Z
such that every
\mathbb{A}^{1}
-fiber is an orbit of
\alpha
. Then
\alpha
is cylindrical.

Proof.

Let
\partial
be the locally nilpotent derivation on
\mathbb{K}[X]\cong\mathbb{K}[Z][t]
corresponding to
\alpha
. Then, since
\alpha
acts only along
\mathbb{A}^{1}
-fibers, we have
\mathbb{K}[Z]\subseteq\Ker\partial
. Since
\partial
is locally nilpotent, it is easy to see that
\partial(t)=h
for some
h\in\mathbb{K}[Z]
. Therefore,

\alpha\big(s,(t,z)\big)=(t+sh,z)\quad\text{for all}\quad(t,z)\in\mathbb{A}^{1}\times Z.

Since
\alpha
has no fixed points and every point of
\mathbb{V}_{X}(h)
is fixed by
\alpha
, we conclude that
h
is an invertible function on
X
. Consider an automorphism

\phi\colon\mathbb{A}^{1}\times Z\to\mathbb{A}^{1}\times Z,\quad\phi(t,z)=\left(\frac{t}{h},z\right).

Then the action
\beta
on
X
conjugated to
\alpha
by
\phi
is defined by

\beta\big(s,(t,z)\big)=\phi\Big(\alpha\big(s,(ht,z)\big)\Big)=\phi(ht+sh,z)=(t+s,z),

so that
\beta
acts by translations along the first factor.
∎

It follows that if
U\subseteq X
is a principal
1
-cylinder, then there exists an effective
\mathbb{G}_{\mathrm{a}}
-action
\alpha
on
X
such that every point in
X\setminus U
is fixed by
\alpha
and
\left.\kern-1.2pt{\alpha}\right|_{U}
is cylindrical.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
X
be a variety and
U\cong\mathbb{A}^{k}\times Z
be a
k
-cylinder in
X
. By a
coordinate decomposition
of
U
we mean a choice of coordinates
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}
in
\mathbb{A}^{k}
and a collection
U_{1},\dots,U_{k}
of
1
-cylinders in
X
such that for each
1\leq i\leq k
we have
U_{i}=U
and
U_{i}\cong\mathbb{A}^{1}\times Z_{i}
, where

\mathbb{A}^{1}=\Spec\mathbb{K}[x_{i}]\quad\text{and}\quad Z_{i}=\Spec\mathbb{K}[Z][x_{1},\dots,\hat{x}_{i},\dots,x_{k}].

Let
X
be an affine variety and
U\subseteq X
be a
k
-cylinder. Each
1
-cylinder in a coordinate decomposition of
U
defines a
\mathbb{G}_{\mathrm{a}}
-action on
X
and one can easily see that these
k
actions define an effective
\mathbb{G}_{\mathrm{a}}^{k}
-action
\alpha
on
X
such that every point in
X\setminus U
is fixed by
\alpha
and
\left.\kern-1.2pt{\alpha}\right|_{U}
is cylindrical.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
X
be an affine variety. Given a principal
k
-cylinder
U\subseteq X
, we say that an effective
\mathbb{G}_{\mathrm{a}}^{k}
-action
\alpha
on
X
is
induced
by
U
if every point in
X\setminus U
is fixed by
\alpha
and
\left.\kern-1.2pt{\alpha}\right|_{U}
is cylindrical.

Summing up, we see that if
U\subseteq X
is a principal
k
-cylinder in an affine variety
X
, then there exists a
\mathbb{G}_{\mathrm{a}}^{k}
-action on
X
induced by
U
.

Definition 2.6
.

Let
X
be a variety.

(1)

Let
U\cong\mathbb{A}^{k}\times Z
be a
k
-cylinder in
X
. Denote by
\pi\colon U\to Z
the natural projection. A subset
S\subseteq X
is called
invariant
with respect to
U
if

S\cap U=\pi^{-1}(\pi(S\cap U)).

(2)

Let
\cal{U}=\{U_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a collection of cylinders in
X
. A subset
S\subseteq X
is called
invariant
with respect to
\cal{U}
if
S
is invariant with respect to each
U_{i}
,
i\in I
.

In other words, a subset
S\subseteq X
is invariant with respect to a cylinder
U\subseteq X
, if whenever
S
contains a point
x\in U
, it also contains the whole fiber of
U
which contains
x
.

Let
X
be an affine variety and
\cal{U}=\{U_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a collection of cylinders in
X
. For each
i\in I
let
\alpha_{i}
be an action on
X
induced by
U_{i}
. One can easily see that a subset
S\subseteq X
is invariant with respect to
\cal{U}
if and only if
S
is
\alpha_{i}
-invariant for all
i\in I
.

3.
Cylinders and flexibility

In this section we establish a criterion of flexibility of an affine variety in terms of cylinders. We also recall a sufficient condition of flexibility of an affine cone over a projective variety in terms of cylinders.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
X
be a variety and
\cal{U}=\{U_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a collection of cylinders in
X
. The collection
\cal{U}
is called
transversal
if no proper subset of
\bigcup_{i\in I}U_{i}
is invariant with respect to
\cal{U}
.

The following lemma provides simple technical observations on transversal cylinder coverings.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
X
be a variety.

(1)

Let
\cal{V}=\{V_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a covering of
X
by some open subsets. Assume that for each
i\in I
there exists a transversal covering
\cal{U}_{i}=\{U_{i,j}\}_{j\in J_{i}}
of
V_{i}
by cylinders. Then

\cal{U}=\bigcup_{i\in I}\cal{U}_{i}

is a transversal covering of
X
.

(2)

Let
\cal{U}=\{U_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a covering of
X
by cylinders. For each
i\in I
let

\cal{V}_{i}=\{U_{i,1},\dots,U_{i,k_{i}}\}

be a coordinate decomposition of the cylinder
U_{i}
. Denote
\cal{V}=\bigcup_{i\in I}\cal{V}_{i}
. Then
\cal{U}
is transversal if and only if
\cal{V}
is transversal.

Proof.

(1)

Let
S\subseteq X
be a nonempty subset, which is invariant with respect to
\cal{U}
. Since
\cal{V}
is a covering of
X
, there exists
i\in I
such that the intersection
S\cap V_{i}
is nonempty. One can easily see that this intersection is invariant with respect to
\cal{U}_{i}
, hence
S\cap V_{i}=V_{i}\subseteq S
. Since
X
is irreducible, we conclude that
S
intersects
V_{i}
for all
i\in I
, so, by the same argument, we have
V_{i}\subseteq S
for all
i\in I
. Therefore,
S=X
.

(2)

The assertion follows from the fact that for each
i\in I
a subset
S\subseteq X
is invariant with respect to
U_{i}
if and only if
S
is invariant with respect to
\cal{V}_{i}
.

∎

The following two results relate flexibility of affine varieties to existence of a transversal covering by cylinders.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
X
be an affine variety. Assume that there exists a transversal covering
\cal{U}=\{U_{i}\}_{i\in I}
of
X
by principal cylinders. Then
X
is smooth and flexible.

Proof.

For each
i\in I
choose an action
\alpha_{i}
on
X
induced by
U_{i}
and denote by
H_{i}
the corresponding subgroup of
\Aut(X)
. Let
G
be the subgroup of
\Aut(X)
generated by all
H_{i}
. Then
G
acts on
X
transitively, hence
X
is smooth. Moreover,
G\subseteq\SAut(X)
, so
X
is flexible by Theorem
1.1
.
∎

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
X
be an affine variety of dimension at least one. Then
X
is flexible if and only if there exists a transversal covering of
X^{\mathrm{reg}}
by cylinders, which are principal in
X
.

Proof.

The case of
\dim X=1
is trivial since the only affine curve which admits an effective
\mathbb{G}_{\mathrm{a}}
-action is
\mathbb{A}^{1}
. Therefore, we may assume that
\dim X\geq 2
.

Assume that
X
is flexible. By Theorem
1.1
the group
\SAut(X)
acts transitively on
X^{\mathrm{reg}}
, hence there exists an effective
\mathbb{G}_{\mathrm{a}}
-action on
X
. It follows that there exists a principal
1
-cylinder
U=\mathbb{D}_{X}(h)
in
X
. Without loss of generality we may assume that
U\subseteq X^{\mathrm{reg}}
, see
[
12
, Section 1.1]
. Consider a collection

\cal{U}=\{gU\}_{g\in\SAut(X)}

of principal
1
-cylinders in
X
. Clearly,
\cal{U}
covers
X^{\mathrm{reg}}
. Consider a nonempty subset
S\subseteq X
which is invariant with respect to
\cal{U}
. There exists
U^{\prime}\in\cal{U}
such that
S\cap U^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
. It follows that there exists an
\mathbb{A}^{1}
-fiber
A
of
U
such that
A\subseteq S
. Consider two distinct points
p,x\in A
and let
y\in X^{\mathrm{reg}}
be an arbitrary point distinct from
p
. By Theorem
1.1
the group
\SAut(X)
acts infinitely transitively on
X^{\mathrm{reg}}
, hence there exists
g\in\SAut(X)
such that
g\cdot x=y
and
g\cdot p=p
. Then
p\in S\cap(gU^{\prime})
and the points
p,y
belong to the same
\mathbb{A}^{1}
-fiber of the cylinder
gU^{\prime}
. It follows that
y\in S
. Therefore,
S=X^{\mathrm{reg}}
and
\cal{U}
is transversal.

Conversely, assume that there is a transversal covering of
X^{\mathrm{reg}}
by cylinders, which are principal in
X
. By utilizing the same argument as in the proof of Lemma
3.3
, we see that
\SAut(X)
acts on
X^{\mathrm{reg}}
transitively, hence
X
is flexible by Theorem
1.1
.
∎

Now we move to flexibility of affine cones over projective varieties. Given a projective variety
X
and a very ample divisor
H
on
X
, denote by
\AffCone_{H}(X)
the affine cone over the embedding
X\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{m}
defined by
H
.

Definition 3.5
.

Let
X
be a projective variety,
H
be a very ample divisor on
X
, and
U\subseteq X
be an open subset. The subset
U
is called
H
-polar
if

X\setminus U=\Supp D

for some effective divisor
D
on
X
such that
D\sim dH
for some positive integer
d
.

Let
X
be a normal projective variety with
\Pic(X)\cong\mathbb{Z}
and
H
be a very ample divisor on
X
. Then every open subset
U\subseteq X
such that
X\setminus U
is of pure codimension one in
X
is
H
-polar. In particular, if
X
is smooth and
U
is affine, then
U
is
H
-polar.

The following generalization of
[
13
, Theorem 5]
about
1
-cylinders is obtained by analyzing the proof given in
[
13
]
, utilizing Lemma
3.2
.(2), and using the results of
[
11
, Theorem 3.1.9.(b)]
and
[
12
, Lemma 2.8]
. Recall that a projective variety
X
is called projectively normal with respect to a very ample divisor
H
on
X
if
\AffCone_{H}(X)
is normal.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
X
be a normal projective variety and
H
be a very ample divisor on
X
. Let
\{U_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a transversal covering of
X^{\mathrm{reg}}
by
H
-polar principal cylinders. Assume that either
X
is smooth and
\Pic(U_{i})=0
for all
i\in I
, or
X
is projectively normal with respect to
H
. Then
\AffCone_{H}(X)
is flexible.

4.
Affine cones over quadrics

The fact that affine cones over quadrics are flexible seems to be known. For the lack of a reference, we provide a proof for this fact in this section.

The following simple observation is a technical tool that will be used in the current and the next sections.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}
be a quadric of rank at least three and let
x\in X^{\mathrm{reg}}
. Then there exist coordinates
x_{0},\dots,x_{n}
in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
such that

x=(0:1:0:\ldots:0),\quad\mathbb{T}_{x}X=\mathbb{V}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}(x_{0}),

and
X
is given by a quadratic form

f=x_{0}x_{1}+g(x_{2},\dots,x_{n}).

Proof.

Choose coordinates
y_{0},\dots,y_{n}
in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
such that

x=(0:1:0:\ldots:0)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{T}_{x}X=\mathbb{V}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}(y_{0}).

Then a direct computation shows that
X
is given by a quadratic form

f=y_{0}h(y_{0},\dots,y_{n})+g(y_{2},\dots,y_{n})

such that
h(0,1,0,\ldots,0)=1
. By setting

x_{0}=y_{0},\quad x_{1}=h(y_{0},\dots,y_{n}),\quad\text{and}\quad x_{i}=y_{i}\quad\text{for}\quad i\geq 2

we obtain the required coordinates.
∎

The choice of coordinates as in Lemma
4.1
allows to find cylinders in a quadric.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}
be a quadric of rank at least three and let
x\in X^{\mathrm{reg}}
. Then the subset
X\setminus\mathbb{T}_{x}X
is an
(n-1)
-cylinder in
X
, that is,
X\setminus\mathbb{T}_{x}X\cong\mathbb{A}^{n-1}
.

Proof.

Let
x_{0},\dots,x_{n}
be coordinates in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
as in Lemma
4.1
. Then

X\setminus\mathbb{T}_{x}X=X\setminus\mathbb{V}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}(x_{0})\cong\mathbb{V}_{\mathbb{A}^{n}}\big(\left.\kern-1.2pt{f}\right|_{x_{0}=1}\big)\cong\mathbb{A}^{n-1},

since
\left.\kern-1.2pt{f}\right|_{x_{0}=1}=x_{1}+g(x_{2},\dots,x_{n})
.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}
be a quadric of rank at least three. Then the collection

\cal{U}=\{X\setminus\mathbb{T}_{x}X\}_{x\in X^{\mathrm{reg}}}

is a transversal covering of
X^{\mathrm{reg}}
by cylinders.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.1
each cylinder in
\cal{U}
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{A}^{n-1}
, so
\cal{U}
is clearly transversal. It remains to show that
\cal{U}
covers
X^{\mathrm{reg}}
, that is, for each
x\in X^{\mathrm{reg}}
there exists
y\in X^{\mathrm{reg}}
such that
x\not\in\mathbb{T}_{y}X
. Let
x_{0},\dots,x_{n}
be coordinates in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
as in Lemma
4.1
. Then for
y=(1:0:\ldots:0)
we have
\mathbb{T}_{y}X=\mathbb{V}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}(x_{1})
and

x=(0:1:0:\ldots:0)\not\in\mathbb{V}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}(x_{1})

as required.
∎

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}
be a quadric of rank at least three. Then every affine cone over
X
is flexible.

Proof.

Denote by
r
the rank of the quadric
X
. If
(n,r)\neq(3,4)
, then
\Pic(X)\cong\mathbb{Z}
so the assertion immediately follows from Lemma
4.3
and Theorem
3.6
. Assume that
(n,r)=(3,4)
, that is,
X
is a smooth quadric in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
. Then
X\cong\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
and there is a bidegree isomorphism

\Pic(\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1})\cong\mathbb{Z}^{2}.

The complement in
X
of cylinders constructed in Lemma
4.2
are supported at a divisor in the class
(1,1)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. Therefore, since the classes of ample divisors on
X
are of the form
(a,b)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
with
a,b>0
, the assertion again follows from Lemma
4.3
and Theorem
3.6
.
∎

Remark 4.5
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}
be a quadric of rank at least three. The fact that the affine cone over this standard embedding is flexible also follows from flexibility of suspensions, see
[
10
, Section 5]
and
[
2
, Theorem 3.2]
.

5.
Complements of quadrics in projective spaces

Recall that if
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}
is a hypersurface, then the complement
\mathbb{P}^{n}\setminus X
is an affine variety.

Let us present a construction of cylinders in the complement of a smooth quadric in a projective space. We utilize an argument, similar to the one from
[
3
, Lemma 5.21]
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}
,
n\geq 2
be a smooth quadric. Consider a point
x\in X
and denote
Y=\mathbb{P}^{n}\setminus X
. Then the subset
Y\setminus\mathbb{T}_{x}X
is a principal
(n-1)
-cylinder in
Y
.

Proof.

Let
x_{0},\dots,x_{n}
be coordinates in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
as in Lemma
4.1
. Then

Y\setminus\mathbb{T}_{x}X=\mathbb{D}_{Y}\left(\frac{x_{0}^{2}}{f}\right)=\mathbb{D}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}(x_{0}f)\cong\mathbb{D}_{\mathbb{A}^{n}}\big(\left.\kern-1.2pt{f}\right|_{x_{0}=1}\big)\cong\mathbb{A}^{1}_{*}\times\mathbb{A}^{n-1},

where the last isomorphism is given by the formulas

\begin{array}[]{rcl}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})&\mapsto&\big(f(1,x_{1},\dots,x_{n}),\ x_{2},\ \dots,\ x_{n}\big)\\
\big(t-g(x_{2},\dots,x_{n}),\ x_{2},\ \dots,\ x_{n}\big)&\mapsfrom&(t,x_{2},\dots,x_{n})\end{array}.

∎

Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}
,
n\geq 2
be a smooth quadric and denote
Y=\mathbb{P}^{n}\setminus X
. We want to show that cylinders constructed in Lemma
5.1
define a transversal covering of
Y
. We treat separately the two cases according to the parity of
n
.

First consider the case of odd
n=2m-1
and choose coordinates
x_{1},\dots,x_{m},y_{1},\dots,y_{m}
in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
such that
X
is given by a quadratic form

f=x_{1}y_{1}+\ldots+x_{m}y_{m}.

We will write coordinates of a point in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
in the following order:

(x_{1}:y_{1}:x_{2}:y_{2}:\ldots:x_{n}:y_{n}).

Fix an index
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
. Consider a point
p_{i}\in X
given by the equations

y_{i}=1,\quad x_{i}=0,\quad\text{and}\quad y_{j}=x_{j}=0\quad\text{for}\quad j\neq i.

Then
\mathbb{T}_{p_{i}}X=\mathbb{V}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}(x_{i})
. Denote
U_{i}=Y\setminus\mathbb{T}_{p_{i}}X
and

\sigma_{i}=x_{1}y_{1}+\ldots+\widehat{x_{i}y_{i}}+\ldots+x_{m}y_{m}=f-x_{i}y_{i}.

Then

U_{i}=\mathbb{D}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}(x_{i}f)\cong\mathbb{D}_{\mathbb{A}^{n}}(\left.\kern-1.2pt{f}\right|_{x_{i}=1})\cong\mathbb{A}^{1}_{*}\times\mathbb{A}^{n-1},

where the last isomorphism is given by the formulas

\begin{array}[]{rcl}(x_{1},y_{1},\dots,\hat{x}_{i},y_{i},\dots,x_{m},y_{m})&\mapsto&(\left.\kern-1.2pt{f}\right|_{x_{i}=1},x_{1},y_{1},\dots,\hat{x}_{i},\hat{y}_{i},\dots,x_{m},y_{m})\\
(x_{1},y_{1},\dots,\hat{x}_{i},t-\sigma_{i},\dots,x_{m},y_{m})&\mapsfrom&(t,x_{1},y_{1},\dots,\hat{x}_{i},\hat{y}_{i},\dots,x_{m},y_{m})\end{array}.

Therefore, for each
t\in\mathbb{A}^{1}_{*}
there is a fiber
A_{i}(t)
of the cylinder
U_{i}
consisting of points of the form

(a_{1}:b_{1}:\ldots:a_{i-1}:b_{i-1}:1:t-\sigma_{i}:a_{i+1}:b_{i+1}:\ldots:a_{m}:b_{m})

for
a_{j},b_{j}\in\mathbb{K}
.

Lemma 5.2
.

The collection
\cal{U}=\{U_{1},\dots,U_{m}\}
of cylinders in
Y
is a transversal covering.

Proof.

Clearly, for every point
p\in Y
at least one of the coordinates
x_{i}
of
p
is nonzero. Therefore,
\cal{U}
covers
Y
, so it remains to show that
\cal{U}
is transversal. Consider a nonempty subset
S\subseteq Y
which is invariant with respect to
\cal{U}
. Our goal is to show that
S=Y
. Take a point
p\in S
.

It is easy to see that there exists
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
such that both coordinates
x_{i}
and
y_{i}
of
p
are nonzero. By symmetry, it suffices to consider the case of
i=1
. We may assume that
x_{1}=1
and
p\in A_{1}(t)
for some
t\in\mathbb{A}^{1}_{*}
. By choosing another point in the fiber
A_{1}(t)
, we may assume that

p=(1:t:0:\ldots:0).

Consider a scalar
\lambda\in\mathbb{K}^{\times}
. By moving
p
along
A_{1}(t)
, we conclude that
S
also contains a point

(1:t:\lambda:0:\ldots:0)=\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}:\frac{t}{\lambda}:1:0:\ldots:0\right)\in A_{2}\left(\frac{t}{\lambda^{2}}\right).

Moving along
A_{2}(t\lambda^{-2})
, we obtain a point

\left(1:\frac{t}{\lambda}:1:\frac{t}{\lambda^{2}}-\frac{t}{\lambda}:0:\ldots:0\right)\in A_{1}\left(\frac{t}{\lambda^{2}}\right),

and moving along
A_{1}(t\lambda^{-2})
, we get a point

\left(1:\frac{t}{\lambda^{2}}:0:\ldots:0\right).

Therefore,
S
contains all points of the form
(1:t:0:\ldots:0)
, where
t\in\mathbb{K}^{\times}
. So
S
contains
U_{1}
. For each
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
we have
U_{1}\cap U_{i}\neq\emptyset
, hence by symmetry
S
contains
U_{i}
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
. We conclude that
S=Y
.
∎

Now consider the case of even
n=2m
and choose coordinates
x_{1},\dots,x_{m},y_{1},\dots,y_{m},z
in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
such that
X
is given by a quadratic form

f=x_{1}y_{1}+\ldots+x_{m}y_{m}+z^{2}.

We will write coordinates of a point in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
in the following order:

(x_{1}:y_{1}:x_{2}:y_{2}:\ldots:x_{n}:y_{n}:z).

Fix an index
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
. Similarly to the case of odd
n
, consider a point
p_{i}\in X
given by the equations

y_{i}=1,\quad x_{i}=0,\quad y_{j}=x_{j}=0\quad\text{for}\quad j\neq i,\quad\text{and}\quad z=0.

Then
\mathbb{T}_{p_{i}}X=\mathbb{V}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}(x_{i})
. Denote
U_{i}=Y\setminus\mathbb{T}_{p_{i}}X
and

\sigma_{i}=x_{1}y_{1}+\ldots+\widehat{x_{i}y_{i}}+\ldots+x_{m}y_{m}+z^{2}=f-x_{i}y_{i}.

Then for each
t\in\mathbb{A}^{1}_{*}
there is a fiber
A_{i}(t)
of the cylinder
U_{i}
consisting of points of the form

(a_{1}:b_{1}:\ldots:a_{i-1}:b_{i-1}:1:t-\sigma_{i}:a_{i+1}:b_{i+1}:\ldots:a_{m}:b_{m}:c)

for
a_{j},b_{j},c\in\mathbb{K}
.

We will also require another similar collection of cylinders. Namely, define points
q_{i}\in X
and cylinders
V_{i}
in
Y
by interchanging
x_{i}
with
y_{i}
in the definitions above. Then for each
t\in\mathbb{A}^{1}_{*}
there is a fiber
B_{i}(t)
of the cylinder
V_{i}
consisting of points of the form

(a_{1}:b_{1}:\ldots:a_{i-1}:b_{i-1}:t-\sigma_{i}:1:a_{i+1}:b_{i+1}:\ldots:a_{m}:b_{m}:c)

for
a_{j},b_{j},c\in\mathbb{K}
.

Consider a point
r=(0:\ldots:0:1)\in Y
. Note that
r\not\in U_{i}\cup V_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq m
, hence we will need at least one more cylinder. Let
q\in X
be a point such that
r\not\in\mathbb{T}_{q}X
: for example, one can take
q=(0:\ldots 0:-1:1:1)
. Denote
W=Y\setminus\mathbb{T}_{q}X
.

Lemma 5.3
.

The collection
\cal{U}=\{U_{1},V_{1},\dots,U_{m},V_{m},W\}
of cylinders in
Y
is a transversal covering.

Proof.

Denote
Y^{\prime}=Y\setminus\{r\}
and
\cal{U}^{\prime}=\cal{U}\setminus\{W\}
. Clearly, for every point
p\in Y^{\prime}
at least one of the coordinates
x_{i}
or
y_{i}
of
p
is nonzero. Therefore,
\cal{U}^{\prime}
covers
Y^{\prime}
. It follows that
\cal{U}
covers
Y
. We conclude that it remains to show that
\cal{U}^{\prime}
is a transversal covering of
Y^{\prime}
. Consider a nonempty subset
S\subseteq Y^{\prime}
which is invariant with respect to
\cal{U}^{\prime}
. Our goal is to show that
S=Y^{\prime}
. Take a point
p\in S
.

Consider
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
such that either coordinate
x_{i}
or
y_{i}
of
p
is nonzero. By symmetry, we can assume that
i=1
and
x_{1}=1
. Then
p\in A_{1}(t)
for some
t\in\mathbb{A}^{1}_{*}
, so after moving along the fiber
A_{1}(t)
we may assume that

p=(1:0:\ldots:0:\lambda),

where
\lambda^{2}=t
.

First, let us show that
S
contains a point
s_{\mu}=(1:0:\ldots:0:\mu)
for every
\mu\in\mathbb{K}^{\times}
. Move
p
along
A_{1}(\lambda^{2})
to get

(1:-3\lambda^{2}:0:\ldots:0:2\lambda)=\left(-\frac{1}{3\lambda^{2}}:1:0:\ldots:0:-\frac{2}{3\lambda}\right)\in B_{1}\left(\frac{1}{9\lambda^{2}}\right).

Consider a scalar
c\in\mathbb{K}
such that
c^{2}\neq\frac{1}{9\lambda^{2}}
. By moving
p
along
B_{1}\big((9\lambda^{2})^{-1}\big)
, we obtain a point

\left(\frac{1-9c^{2}\lambda^{2}}{9\lambda^{2}}:1:0:\ldots:0:c\right)=\left(1:\frac{9\lambda^{2}}{1-9c^{2}\lambda^{2}}:0:\ldots:0:\frac{9c\lambda^{2}}{1-9c^{2}\lambda^{2}}\right),

which can be moved by a fiber of
U_{1}
to a point
(1:0:\ldots:0:b)
, where

b^{2}=\frac{9\lambda^{2}}{1-9c^{2}\lambda^{2}}+\left(\frac{9c\lambda^{2}}{1-9c^{2}\lambda^{2}}\right)^{2}=\frac{9\lambda^{2}}{(1-9c^{2}\lambda^{2})^{2}}.

The right-hand side is a function of
c
with image equal to
\mathbb{K}^{\times}
, hence
b
can be an arbitrary nonzero scalar.

Since
s_{\mu}\in S
for all
\mu\in\mathbb{K}^{\times}
, we conclude that
U_{1}\subseteq S
. By symmetry we have
U_{i}\cup V_{i}\subseteq S
for all
1\leq i\leq m
, hence
S=Y^{\prime}
as required.
∎

Now we construct a transversal covering by cylinders of the complement of an arbitrary quadric of rank at least three. For this we present a bit more general argument.

We call two linear subspaces
K,L\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}
complementary if
K\cap L=\emptyset
and

n=\dim K+\dim L+1.

We say that coordinates
x_{0},\dots,x_{n}
in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
are compatible with
K
and
L
if
x_{0},\dots,x_{\dim K}
are coordinates in
K
and
x_{\dim K+1},\dots,x_{n}
are coordinates in
L
. In other words,

K=\mathbb{V}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}(x_{\dim K+1},\dots,x_{n})\quad\text{and}\quad L=\mathbb{V}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}(x_{0},\dots,x_{\dim K}).

Definition 5.4
.

Consider a projective space
\mathbb{P}^{n}
, complementary subspaces
K,L\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}
and a projective variety
X_{0}\subseteq K
. For each
x\in X_{0}
denote
L_{x}=\left\langle x,L\right\rangle_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}
. The projective variety
X=\bigcup_{x\in X_{0}}L_{x}
is called a
projective cone
with a
base

X_{0}
and a
vertex

L
.

Note that if
x_{0},\dots,x_{n}
are coordinates in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
compatible with
K
and
L
and
X_{0}\subseteq K
is given by equations
h_{1}=\ldots=h_{m}=0
for some homogeneous polynomials
h_{i}\in\mathbb{K}[x_{0},\dots,x_{\dim K}]
, then the projective cone
X
is given in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
by the same equations
h_{1}=\ldots=h_{m}=0
.

Let
K
and
L
be two complementary linear subspaces in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
. For a closed subset
W\subseteq K
denote by

\textstyle\hat{\mathchoice{\scalebox{.5}[1]{$\displaystyle W$}}{\scalebox{.5}[1]{$\textstyle W$}}{\scalebox{.5}[1]{$\scriptstyle W$}}{\scalebox{.5}[1]{$\scriptscriptstyle W$}}}

the projective cone with a base
W
and a vertex
L
. For an open subset
U\subseteq K
denote
\hat{U}=\mathbb{P}^{n}\setminus\mathchoice{\scalebox{2}[1]{$\displaystyle\hat{\mathchoice{\scalebox{.5}[1]{$\displaystyle W$}}{\scalebox{.5}[1]{$\textstyle W$}}{\scalebox{.5}[1]{$\scriptstyle W$}}{\scalebox{.5}[1]{$\scriptscriptstyle W$}}}$}}{\scalebox{2}[1]{$\textstyle\hat{\mathchoice{\scalebox{.5}[1]{$\displaystyle W$}}{\scalebox{.5}[1]{$\textstyle W$}}{\scalebox{.5}[1]{$\scriptstyle W$}}{\scalebox{.5}[1]{$\scriptscriptstyle W$}}}$}}{\scalebox{2}[1]{$\scriptstyle\hat{\mathchoice{\scalebox{.5}[1]{$\displaystyle W$}}{\scalebox{.5}[1]{$\textstyle W$}}{\scalebox{.5}[1]{$\scriptstyle W$}}{\scalebox{.5}[1]{$\scriptscriptstyle W$}}}$}}{\scalebox{2}[1]{$\scriptscriptstyle\hat{\mathchoice{\scalebox{.5}[1]{$\displaystyle W$}}{\scalebox{.5}[1]{$\textstyle W$}}{\scalebox{.5}[1]{$\scriptstyle W$}}{\scalebox{.5}[1]{$\scriptscriptstyle W$}}}$}}
, where
W=K\setminus U
.

Denote
\dim K=k
,
\dim L=l
and choose coordinates
x_{0},\dots,x_{k},y_{0},\dots,y_{l}
in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
compatible with
K
and
L
. Let
H\subseteq K
be a hyperplane given by a linear form
h\in\mathbb{K}[x_{0},\dots,x_{k}]
. Assume that
U\subseteq K
is an open subset such that
H\subseteq K\setminus U
. Then
\hat{U}
is an
(l+1)
-cylinder, namely

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\hat{U}&\cong&U\times\mathbb{A}^{l+1}\\
(x_{0}:\ldots:x_{k}:y_{0}:\ldots:y_{l})&\mapsto&\left((x_{0}:\ldots:x_{k}),\left(\frac{y_{0}}{h},\dots,\frac{y_{l}}{h}\right)\right)\\
(x_{0}:\ldots:x_{k}:ht_{0}:\ldots:ht_{l})&\mapsfrom&\big((x_{0}:\ldots:x_{k}),(t_{0}:\ldots:t_{l})\big)\end{array}.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
X_{0}\subseteq K
be a projective variety. Denote
Y_{0}=K\setminus X_{0}
and
X=\hat{X}_{0}
. Assume that there is a transversal covering
\cal{U}=\{U_{i}\}_{i\in I}
of
Y_{0}
by cylinders such that the complement
K\setminus U_{i}
contains a hyperplane in
K
for all
i\in I
. Then
\hat{\cal{U}}=\{\hat{U}_{i}\}_{i\in I}
is a transversal covering of
Y=\mathbb{P}^{n}\setminus X
.

Proof.

For
x\in K
denote
L^{\prime}_{x}=L_{x}\setminus L
, where
L_{x}=\left\langle x,L\right\rangle_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}
. For every point
p\in\mathbb{P}^{n}\setminus L
there exists a unique point
x\in K
such that
p\in L^{\prime}_{x}
, and, moreover, for distinct
x,y\in K
the subsets
L^{\prime}_{x}
and
L^{\prime}_{y}
of
\mathbb{P}^{n}
are disjoint. We can write

Y=\bigsqcup_{x\in Y_{0}}L^{\prime}_{x}\quad\text{and}\quad\hat{U}_{i}=\bigsqcup_{x\in U_{i}}L^{\prime}_{x}\quad\text{for all}\quad i\in I.

In particular, it follows that
\hat{\cal{U}}
is a covering of
Y
.

Consider a nonempty subset
S\subseteq Y
, which is invariant with respect to
\hat{\cal{U}}
. Take a point
p\in S
and let
x\in Y_{0}
be such that
p\in L^{\prime}_{x}
. By moving
p
along an
\mathbb{A}^{l+1}
-fiber of a cylinder in
\hat{\cal{U}}
, we see that
x\in L^{\prime}_{x}\subseteq S
. By moving
x
along a fiber of a cylinder in
\cal{U}
, we conclude that
Y_{0}\subseteq S
. It follows that
S=Y
.
∎

Now we are ready to state the main results of this section.

Proposition 5.6
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}
be a quadric of rank at least three. Then the complement
Y=\mathbb{P}^{n}\setminus X
admits a transversal covering by principal cylinders.

Proof.

If
X
is smooth, then the assertion was proved in Lemmas
5.2
and
5.3
. Assume that
X
is singular and denote by
r
the rank of
X
. Then
X
is a projective cone with a base
X_{0}
, where
X_{0}
is a smooth quadric in
\mathbb{P}^{r-1}
. One can easily see that the cylinders
\hat{U}_{i}
, constructed in Lemma
5.5
, are principal provided that the cylinders
U_{i}
are principal. Therefore, the assertion follows from Lemma
5.5
.
∎

Theorem 5.7
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}
be a quadric of rank at least three. Then the complement
Y=\mathbb{P}^{n}\setminus X
is flexible.

Proof.

Combine Propositions
3.4
and
5.6
.
∎

Remark 5.8
.

One can also deduce flexibility of the complement
Y=\mathbb{P}^{n}\setminus X
of a smooth quadric
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}
,
n\geq 2
from the following observation. A semisimple algebraic group
G=\SOop_{n+1}(\mathbb{K})
acts on
Y
transitively. By
[
14
, Lemma 1.1]
the group
G
is generated by its
\mathbb{G}_{\mathrm{a}}
-subgroups. Therefore,
Y
is flexible by Theorem
1.1
.

6.
Smooth complete intersections of two quadrics

In this section we prove flexibility of affine cones over a smooth complete intersection of two quadrics in
\mathbb{P}^{n+2}
,

.

and

in

.

\beta(w,w)=\gamma(w,w)=0

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
X
be a smooth complete intersection of two quadrics in
\mathbb{P}^{n+2}
,
n\geq 3
. Then every affine cone over
X
is flexible.

Proof.

Since
\Pic(X)\cong\mathbb{Z}
, by Theorem
3.6
it suffices to show that there exists a transversal covering of
X
by cylinders. By Lemma
6.2
, there exists a covering
\cal{U}=\{U_{i}\}_{i\in I}
of
X
by open subsets, where
U_{i}
is isomorphic to the complement of a quadric of rank at least three in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
for all
i\in I
. By Proposition
5.6
each
U_{i}
admits a transversal covering by cylinders. The assertion now follows from Lemma
3.2
.(1).
∎

Let
U\subseteq X
be a cylinder in the transversal covering of
X
, constructed in the proof of Theorem
6.3
. We see that the complement
X\setminus U
is the union of two prime divisors: a hyperplane section and a divisor of the form
D_{l}(X)
. Therefore, since
D_{l}(X)
is cut out by a quadric, we have
X\setminus U=\Supp D
, where
D\sim 3H
and
H
is a hyperplane section of
X
.

Corollary 6.4
.

Consider an affine space
\mathbb{A}^{n}
,
n\geq 6
with coordinates
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
. Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{A}^{n}
be an affine variety given by equations

x_{1}^{2}+\ldots+x_{n}^{2}=0\quad\text{and}\quad\lambda_{1}x_{1}^{2}+\ldots+\lambda_{n}x_{n}^{2}=0,

where
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}\in\mathbb{K}
are pairwise distinct. Then
X
is flexible.

Proof.

Use
[
16

and Theorem
6.3
.
∎

Note that the variety

is normal, since a smooth complete intersection of two quadrics is projectively normal.

It remains an interesting question whether flexibility holds for affine cones over complete intersections of three quadrics, or for mildly singular degenerations of the varieties considered above.
