Delayed medical care experiences among patients with head and neck cancers: an exploratory study using patient journey mapping
Authors: Yueyao Cao, Wenwen Cai, Lan Xiong, Qingcai Wu, Qiqing Tan, Yalan Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn China, significant delays in accessing medical care for patients with head and neck cancer (HNC) are a critical public health concern. Patients often experience a long delay from the onset of symptoms to receiving treatment, which affects prognosis. This study aimed to explore the entire experience of delayed medical treatment for HNC patients through patient journey maps, identify the key obstacles at each stage, and provide a basis for formulating targeted intervention measures.MethodsUsing a descriptive qualitative approach, this study systematically explored the lived experiences of 14 patients diagnosed with HNC through semi-structured in-depth interviews. Thematic content analysis was conducted to systematically analyze the data and construct a detailed patient journey map, revealing critical barriers to timely care and emotional challenges faced by this population.ResultsBased on the timeline of diagnosis and treatment, the findings were organized into four overarching themes: assessment delay period, diagnostic delay period, treatment delay period, and cross-cutting drivers across delay stages. A total of 19 sub-themes were identified, structured across five dimensions: behavior, thinking, emotion, pain points, and contact points. Specifically, five sub-themes were related to the assessment delay period, five to the diagnostic delay period, five to the treatment delay period, and four were cross-cutting drivers that influenced multiple stages.ConclusionsThe healthcare journey for patients with HNC is often complex, characterized by significant delays in seeking medical care. To address these challenges, we recommend three key strategies: promoting grassroots health education, developing digital warning tools, and establishing professional medical platforms. These efforts aim to improve the efficient use of medical resources, provide patients with treatment decision-making support, and deliver social support—ultimately better addressing their health needs.

Background

Head and neck cancer (HNC) is a prevalent malignant neoplasm worldwide. According to GLOBOCAN 2022 data, there were approximately 946,456 new cases and 482,001 deaths globally [1]. In China, 142,000 new cases were reported in 2020, with the incidence rate showing a consistent upward trend; these cases accounted for approximately 10% of all malignant tumors nationwide [2]. A meta-analysis reported a median time of 48 days from referral to treatment initiation for HNC [3]. Delayed medical attention can lead to adverse outcomes in patients with HNC, and delays exceeding four weeks can compromise treatment outcomes and increase mortality [4, 5]. Importantly, early detection enables cure in 60–65% of HNC patients through surgery and/or radiotherapy (RT) [6]. Furthermore, treatment delays in four HNC subtypes—oral, oropharyngeal, hypopharyngeal, and laryngeal cancers—are associated with reduced overall survival [7].

Diagnostic delay in head and neck cancer is not merely a clinical problem but a complex diagnostic challenge, particularly for rare or anatomically complex HNC subtypes. Recent comprehensive reviews highlight that even in specialized centers, the pathological diagnosis of rare head and neck cancers faces significant difficulties—histological complexity, the need for specialized molecular analysis, and the requirement for multidisciplinary expertise can all contribute to diagnostic delays [8]. China, as a country with a high incidence of head and neck cancer, faces both categories of challenges: on one hand, awareness and recognition of HNC vary considerably across primary care institutions; on the other hand, even in urban tertiary hospitals, the diagnosis of certain rare HNC subtypes requires multidisciplinary collaboration and specialized pathological techniques that may not be universally available [9]. A recent review of oral cancer delay in China further emphasizes that patient-related factors such as cancer awareness, health literacy, and socioeconomic status interact with healthcare system factors to influence timely diagnosis [10].

Identifying the drivers of treatment delays is fundamental to resolving delays in patient care. The patient journey can be defined as a visual, step-by-step diagram illustrating the interactions between patients and healthcare services across disease progression stages [11]. Journey maps are currently widely used in the field of chronic disease management. For instance, Dutch scholar Susanne applied them to the treatment process of Korsakoff syndrome, aiming to improve the patient’s path and reduce treatment delays [12], T. Justin Dhinsa conducted observational studies in the field of delayed identification of bone tumors in children [13], Stephen Gichuhi evaluated the referral pathways and treatment delays of patients with ocular surface squamous cell tumors in Kenya [14]. The patient journey offers unique, actionable insights from a distinct vantage point, enabling the design and implementation of tailored, journey-specific strategies to enhance patient healthcare experiences and outcomes [15, 16]. Yoori Koo conducted a comprehensive analysis of HNC treatment processes through the perspectives of nursing staff, medical professionals, and patients, improving patient experiences by tailoring interventions to evolving needs at each care stage [17]. These studies indicate that the patient journey map can be used to observe and optimize the medical treatment approach. This study adopts a descriptive qualitative approach to collect patient-related data and construct a journey map for delayed medical care among HNC patients. The goal is to identify pain points and unmet needs in the medical care-seeking process, thereby providing a foundation for developing multidimensional intervention tools to address delays.

Methods

Participants and data collection

Purposeful sampling was used to select patients who underwent treatment for HNC at a tertiary-level hospital in Guangzhou from January 2024 to March 2025 for interviews. Inclusion criteria: Pathologically confirmed diagnosis of malignant HNC, including nasopharyngeal carcinoma, oral cancer, thyroid cancer, and laryngeal cancer; Age ≥ 18 years; Able to communicate effectively in Mandarin or Cantonese; Patients must have received at least one form of cancer treatment (chemotherapy, surgery, or radiotherapy); Informed consent to participate in the study. Exclusion criteria: Patients with tumor recurrence; History of psychiatric illness or cognitive impairment affecting ability to participate in interviews; Difficulties in verbal or written communication; Presence of other primary malignancies; Severe comorbidities precluding study participation. This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Guangzhou Institute of Cancer Research, the Affiliated Cancer Hospital. Ethical approval number: KY-2025029-01.

Study design and setting

The research team comprised five members: one physician (male), one oncology nurse (Female), one outpatient nurse (Female), and two master’s students (male, Female) specializing in oncology. Yueyao Cao (CYY) was researching topics related to delayed medical treatment, Wenwen Cai (CWW) had extensive clinical and interview experience. All team members possessed extensive experience in qualitative research methods. The interviewers were graduate students with nursing backgrounds, and some interviewees had prior nurse-patient relationships with them. According to the steps for constructing a patient journey map [18, 19], the process included: (1) desktop research on clinical protocols to outline the journey framework; (2) participatory observation; (3) patient interviews to refine details; and (4) stakeholder validation. Ultimately, the map was evaluated and finalized in collaboration with stakeholders, including doctors, nurses, patients, and family members. The research team collected historical data on HNC patients (including current medical history, family history, and treatment history) from medical record systems over recent years through desk research. After conducting thematic discussions to synthesize findings, they developed the journey map timeline based on Andersen’s Generalized Total Delay Model [20]. Key nodes were marked to divide the timeline into three critical delay periods: (1) Assessment Delay Period: From initial symptom onset to first consultation/examination and triage; (2) Diagnostic Delay Period: Including follow-up consultations, further examinations, and definitive diagnosis; (3) Treatment Delay Period: Spanning treatment plan determination to treatment initiation. Two graduate students conducted interviews with four outpatients who had HNC and experienced delayed medical care, documenting their care journeys. Building on these interviews and relevant literature [21], the research team developed the task axis for the journey map, which included behavioral patterns, patient thoughts, emotional experiences, pain points, and key touch points. After stakeholder review and validation confirmed its accuracy, the task axis was finalized. A descriptive qualitative approach was employed, with semi-structured interviews conducted to explore patients’ decision-making needs. Developing the Interview Outline: A preliminary interview outline was drafted based on the research objectives. Pre-interviews with four HNC patients were then conducted using this outline; it should be noted that data from these pre-interviews were excluded from the final analysis. Drawing on insights from the pre-interviews, the research team revised and refined the outline to address issues specific to the assessment delay period, culminating in the final interview guide. Data analysis

Data collection continued until thematic saturation was achieved. Subsequent interviews did not generate new topics or insights related to the patient’s journey map. The interview content was finally confirmed by 14 participants, supplemented, and refined. The final results of the 14 participants (18 interviews) were considered sufficient to capture the range of experiences for different types of head and neck cancer and delayed periods. The framework of the refined thematic journey map was verified with stakeholders, demonstrating the sufficiency of the data [22]. Within 48 h after the interviews, two researchers collaboratively transcribed the text, read it independently, and refined the themes while ensuring that non-verbal expressions were not overlooked. All texts were coded using NVivo software and then analyzed and summarized thematically. Ultimately, they reviewed and refined the topics together, discussing any differences that arose or seeking assistance from a third researcher to aid in the analysis [23]. Results

According to the inclusion and exclusion criteria, a total of 28 patients were included in the hospital during the same period in this study. Among them, 2 patients refused to participate (1 reported fatigue caused by continuous treatment, and 1 indicated discomfort with the recording). We also sought four of them as subjects for pre-interviews. After we completed the interviews with fourteen subjects, the interview contents of the remaining eight subjects were encoded and organized, with no new content added. CYY conducted 12 interviews; CWW conducted 6. Eleven interviewees were interviewed once, while interviewees N8, N10, and N13 were each interviewed twice to ensure comprehensive data collection. All the interviews were conducted in separate rooms in the hospital. Each interview lasted 40–90 min. General demographic and clinical characteristics of the interviewees are presented in Table 3.

Through discussions with relevant personnel and stakeholders involved in the patient’s healthcare journey, the identified themes were synthesized and organized into four overarching themes with 19 sub-themes, structured across the dimensions of behavior, thinking, emotions, pain points, and touch points. In addition to stage-specific themes, we identified factors that consistently influenced delays across multiple stages. These were synthesized into a separate cross-cutting theme to capture persistent drivers throughout the patient journey. This process revealed the behaviors, thoughts, emotional fluctuations, and challenges encountered by patients at each stage of their cancer healthcare experience. By mapping out the storylines across different dimensions at each stage of the patient’s experience, we created a journey map for HNC patients, as shown in Fig. 1.

Delayed medical care journey map for patients with head and neck cancer

Theme 1 assessing delay period (symptom ambiguity and patient response)

Behavior: delayed help-seeking

Patients typically sought medical attention only when symptoms interfered with their quality of life. Early symptoms were generally mild and non-specific, such as a lump (N3; N10), white patches (N11), or nosebleeds (N4; N9).

Thinking: prioritizing convenience

Convenience was a primary consideration, with patients often prioritizing hospitals or clinics near their homes (N3; N5). When doubts persisted or symptoms worsened, patients tended to seek care at higher-level hospitals for reassessment and further examination—for example, persistent cough (N8), rhinitis (N1; N6), or oral ulcers (N11).

Emotions: from calm to mild anxiety

Many patients initially remained calm due to the mild nature of early symptoms and experienced optimism or denial—for instance, attributing symptoms to benign conditions like rhinitis (N4) or lymphadenitis (N9). Mild anxiety arose from ongoing uncertainty about persistent symptoms (N2; N3).

Pain points: misconceptions and misdiagnosis

Symptom distress remained unresolved, often due to misconceptions about the illness, resulting in delayed treatment. Some patients lacked awareness of the need for medical checkups and only sought care when pain became intolerable (N5; N10). Early symptoms of HNC were easily misdiagnosed due to their subtle and nonspecific nature, commonly mistaken for conditions like oral ulcers (N11) or otitis media (N6).

Contact: initial information sources

At this stage, patients first came into contact with relatives, friends, colleagues around them, and even some clinics and primary healthcare institutions, and searched for information online via their mobile phones. At this stage, the main people in contact were informal network members, including family, friends, colleagues, online communities, and traditional therapists.

Theme 2: diagnostic delay period (challenges in navigating the diagnostic pathway)

Behavior: seeking care again as symptoms worsen

As symptoms worsened, some patients were compelled to seek medical attention again due to the intolerable pain caused by their condition. Examples include a growing lump (N3; N8), hardening plaques affecting eating (N2; N11), and persistent nosebleeds (N4).

Thinking: shifting priorities to specialist care

With a clearer understanding of their illness, the priority of convenience diminished significantly. Patients became more inclined to follow the advice of local doctors or relatives and opt for hospitals renowned for advanced medical technology (N5; N10).

Emotions: anxiety, shock, and depression

Anxiety: Limited medical resources and prolonged wait times for test results contribute to significant anxiety during the waiting period (N1; N13). Confusion and Shock: Many patients believed cancer was a condition that would not affect them, having never heard of the disease before (N2; N10) or believing they were in good health (N5; N14).Depression: Upon receiving a definitive cancer diagnosis, patients often experienced a low mood (N6; N12).

Pain points: systemic and informational challenges

Accessing and Evaluating Medical Information: Despite having various information sources, such as family, social media, and the internet, patients found it challenging to effectively evaluate the complex information available. They remained unsure of which hospital to visit, often initially consulting traditional Chinese medicine practitioners (N6), and lacked reliable medical advice within their family (N11). Limited Medical Resources: Diagnosing HNC was particularly challenging. Many hospitals lack the necessary facilities, requiring specialized diagnosis and pathological biopsy for a confirmed diagnosis, leading to extended diagnostic timelines. Local hospitals were often unable to perform such tests (N9) and initially suspect other cancers (N4).

Contact: engaging with the healthcare system

At this stage, the patient began to come into contact with medical staff through local hospitals and examinations, including those around them who are engaged in medical treatment. However, their expertise was relatively weak, and they were unable to address the patient’s illness. At this stage, patients started to come into contact with formal healthcare Interactions, but for some, their main contacts are still through informal networks.

Theme 3: treatment delay period (treatment decision-making dilemmas)

Behavior: selecting treatment facilities

After deciding to undergo treatment, patients selected hospitals based on recommendations from local doctors, family members, or recovered patients, focusing on the reputation of the hospital or physician. After diagnosis, they considered referral to specialized facilities based on local medical conditions (N4). Local doctors often recommended treatment at specialized institutions (N8).

Thinking: prioritizing cure and expertise

Treatment Plan Selection: The potential for cure was a primary consideration for patients prior to treatment. The choice of treatment plan significantly impacted both effectiveness and prognosis. Patients frequently prioritized the reputation and capability of the hospital and department, as well as the physician’s prestige and clinical experience (N4; N8).

Pain points: economic, prognostic, and informational

Economic Conditions: Financial status is a critical factor influencing patients’ ability to complete their treatment regimen. While treatment costs for HNC are relatively lower compared to other cancers, some patients faced substantial economic pressure due to limited household income sources, making the burden heavier (N10). In some cases, without strong urging from family members, patients considered forgoing treatment (N11). Prognosis: Prognosis is particularly important for patients, especially for middle-aged individuals who wish to return to work after completing treatment. Surgery and chemotherapy/radiotherapy led to the loss of certain functions, making decision-making difficult (N5; N6). Difficulty in Treatment Plan Selection: The complexity of medical knowledge can be daunting; many patients find the information obscure and difficult to comprehend (N2; N4). Additionally, patients often reported insufficient time for communication with their doctors, though they understood the demands of the physicians’ workloads (N6).

Emotions: cautious optimism mixed with ongoing concerns

Compared to the time of diagnosis, patients often exhibited marked emotional improvement. For instance, nasopharyngeal carcinoma has a high five-year survival rate (N1; N3), and surgical techniques for thyroid cancer are well established, posing lower risks than surgeries for other cancers (N13; N14). Concerns: Patients began to worry about their ability to overcome cancer, the side effects associated with treatment, and the financial implications thereof. They may be uncertain about the timeline for completing treatment and whether they may face complications, such as blindness (N6). Concerns may also extend to the duration of subsequent immunotherapy and whether it will lead to a complete recovery from the disease (N8).

Thus, while patients generally showed emotional improvement compared to the diagnostic phase, their optimism remained guarded, intertwined with persistent anxieties about treatment outcomes, side effects, and financial burdens.

Contact: expanding support networks

At this stage, patients started to come into contact with different doctors and fellow patients who receive the same treatment methods. The connection between patients and their families became even closer. The patients mainly come into contact with formal healthcare Interactions, including primary care physicians, experts, nurses, and hospital systems.

Theme 4: cross-cutting drivers across delay stages

Information asymmetry and decision-making uncertainty

Across all three delay periods, patients consistently struggled with insufficient or incomprehensible information. During the assessment period, they lacked knowledge about symptom significance; during the diagnostic period, they struggled to evaluate conflicting advice; during the treatment period, they found medical information too complex to inform decisions. This persistent information asymmetry created decision paralysis at each critical juncture. “From the very beginning, I didn’t know this symptom was serious. Later, when I found out, I didn’t know whom to trust. In the end, I couldn’t figure out the treatment plan either. " (N6).

Economic vulnerability as a recurring barrier

Financial concerns emerged not as a single event but as a background condition influencing decisions at every stage. Economic constraints shaped choices about where to seek care (e.g., closer but potentially less specialized hospitals), whether to complete diagnostic tests, and ultimately which treatment pathway to pursue. “Every step has to be counted, the travel to a big hospital is expensive, the examination fee is expensive, and the treatment is more expensive, but it is not possible to go. " (N11).

Emotional trajectory: from denial to acceptance to new anxieties

Patients’ emotional experiences followed a discernible trajectory across stages, with each transition introducing new emotional challenges while resolving previous ones. Denial dominated the assessment period, shock and anxiety the diagnostic period, and cautious optimism mixed with treatment-related fears the treatment period.

Family as both facilitator and source of delay

Family involvement was not stage-specific but continuous, playing dual and sometimes contradictory roles. While family members often ultimately prompted care-seeking, their involvement could also introduce delays through disagreement, financial concerns, or patients’ desire to avoid burdening loved ones.

In order to better integrate some overlapping themes of each stage and highlight the cross-driving factors of delay, we identified Theme 4. This theme captures both internal and external factors that influenced the duration of each delay stage, and this theme complements the stage-specific themes by highlighting factors that operated across all three delay periods. Discussion

This study identified various challenges patients encountered during delays in medical treatment, with the results indicating that most of these challenges occurred in primary care settings. Early symptoms of HNC are often overlooked because they resemble those of other otolaryngology (ENT) conditions, and most patients exhibit weak health awareness, leading to delayed medical treatment. This phenomenon is particularly evident in rural areas, reflecting inadequate grassroots health education. Notably, patients tend to rely on the Internet for self-diagnosis; however, insufficient information screening ability can exacerbate anxiety and create a vicious cycle of “search-panic-avoidance.” The failure to detect early symptoms and patients’ weak health awareness are significant contributors to treatment delays. These findings align with previous literature on delayed tumor diagnosis and treatment in Africa, where patients consulting traditional therapists have a higher likelihood of delay between symptom onset and medical consultation [24]. Delays in Hungary’s HNC patients are particularly pronounced, especially for oropharyngeal and hypopharyngeal tumors, with studies indicating these cases are mostly locally advanced at diagnosis [25]. At a tertiary cancer center in Canada, significant delays were observed in clinical pathways, particularly from symptom onset to referral to the center [26]. In traditional Chinese culture, the family plays a significant role in caring for the elderly, with children typically assuming the primary responsibility. This family support system can influence the health and quality of life of older adults in various ways. Studies have found that the proportion of patients living with adult children who delay seeking medical treatment is significantly lower than that of patients without adult children living with them, indicating that the companionship and support of children may positively impact timely medical treatment [13]. Emergency department examinations or outpatient hospitalizations prior to diagnosis can enhance nursing coordination, accelerate time to treatment initiation, and more effectively reduce treatment delays [27]. Near-term, low-resource interventions could include community-based education, such as developing and disseminating dialect-specific educational materials about early head and neck cancer symptoms could address the knowledge gaps identified in our study. Feasibility considerations include partnering with existing community health worker networks and integrating messages into routine health education campaigns. However, our findings suggest that knowledge alone may be insufficient; messages must also address cultural norms around symptom endurance. Second, strengthening referral communication: Simple interventions such as standardized referral templates and clear instructions for patients about next steps could reduce fragmentation in the diagnostic pathway. Feasibility is high, as these require minimal resources and can be implemented at the institutional level.

Beyond health system barriers, our findings reveal several culturally specific factors that shaped patients’ delay experiences. Participants often viewed aging as a natural and inevitable process, which, combined with the cultural norm of patience, led to delayed medical treatment for minor ailments and a reduction in the use of preventive services [28]. Second, family dynamics played a dual role. In line with traditional Confucian values emphasizing filial piety, adult children were often deeply involved in their parents’ care decisions. While this involvement could facilitate timely care—consistent with findings that patients living with adult children had shorter delays [29]—it could also introduce delays when family members disagreed about treatment approaches or when patients avoided seeking care to avoid burdening their families financially (N11). The collectivist orientation of Chinese families means that treatment decisions are rarely individual; they are negotiated within the family unit, a process that can extend decision-making time. Third, cultural beliefs about cancer influenced both help-seeking and treatment choices. Some participants expressed fatalistic views, perceiving cancer as an incurable disease or a punishment, which contributed to initial avoidance of medical care. Others reported consulting traditional Chinese medicine practitioners before or alongside Western medical care (N6), reflecting the pluralistic healthcare-seeking behavior common in Chinese society [30]. While traditional medicine plays an important role in health maintenance, delays may occur when it is used as a substitute for, rather than a complement to, timely diagnostic evaluation.

This journey map indicates that limited medical resources and lengthy diagnosis cycles are significant challenges for patients during referrals. Patients face difficulties selecting specialists, cannot be timely referred from local hospitals to HNC specialists, and lack radiological or pathological investigations to definitively diagnose their disease and stage. The most common reason for delayed final diagnosis post-referral is the time to obtain pathological results after biopsy, which prolongs treatment delay in HNC patients [26]. Recent evidence highlights that even in specialized centers, the pathological diagnosis of rare head and neck cancers faces significant difficulties. A national survey and retrospective study by Filippini and colleagues [31] examined diagnostic challenges across multiple Italian centers, revealing that histological complexity, variability in immunohistochemical marker interpretation, and limited access to specialized pathology consultation all contribute to diagnostic delays in rare HNC subtypes. Although rare subtypes were not the focus of our study, these findings underscore the broader challenges in pathological diagnosis. Efficient use of medical resources includes constructing regional pathological diagnosis centers, establishing provincial-level HNC pathology diagnosis centers, and shortening report turnaround time through the “grassroots sampling-center testing” model. Management of HNC patients requires careful consideration of all patient- and non-patient-related factors that may affect care timeliness. Socioeconomic factors such as distance from hospitals and lack of social support negatively impact referral timeliness [32]. One study further suggests that financial constraints are the most common reason for delayed HNC referrals. Medicaid-enrolled patients have a lower incidence of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, and older patients with Medicaid exhibit a reduced risk of late diagnosis [33]. Interventions to reduce HNC diagnosis and treatment time vary, and evidence on their effectiveness remains limited. Future interventions should consider the complexity and dynamics of health systems and adhere to best-practice principles for early diagnosis research [34]. Medium-term, moderate-resource interventions may include decision support tools, such as developing patient-facing decision aids that present treatment options with plain language summaries, potential side effects, and cost information could address the decisional conflicts observed in our study. Feasibility considerations include the need for multidisciplinary input, regular updating as evidence evolves, and testing across literacy levels. Second, Primary care education: Targeted training for primary care physicians on early recognition of head and neck cancer symptoms could reduce misdiagnosis at first presentation. Feasibility depends on integration into existing continuing medical education programs and may require incentivization.

Our analysis indicates that for patients with a confirmed HNC, decision-making regarding treatment options is a primary concern and a major driver of delayed medical presentation. Due to concerns about treatment outcomes at medical institutions, patients tend to select hospitals and doctors with strong reputations, which may delay treatment until a plan is finalized. When discussing treatment plans with physicians, patients consider side effects, family financial status, treatment goals, and other factors; however, they often worry about treatment-related adverse reactions, prognostic function recovery, and become entangled in treatment choices—including difficulty understanding the plan, insufficient consultation time, and decision-making conflicts—all of which are key patient pain points. Most patients with head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) experience treatment initiation delays, particularly those with advanced-stage tumors or when radiotherapy is recommended [10]. Nearly half of HNC patients (48%) report clinically significant decisional conflicts, which can also impair quality of life [35]. Delayed surgery following neoadjuvant chemotherapy for HNC increases recurrence and mortality risks [36]. Greater community-level socioeconomic disadvantage has been independently associated with a higher likelihood of delayed postoperative radiotherapy (PORT) and prolonged treatment duration (TTP) in a dose-dependent manner [37], suggesting that economic and social environmental conditions also influence treatment delays.

Longer-term, higher-resource interventions could involve regional diagnostic networks, such as establishing centralized pathology services with rapid turnaround for suspected head and neck cancers could address the diagnostic delays identified in our study. However, this requires substantial investment, inter-institutional coordination, and quality assurance mechanisms. Pilot testing in high-burden regions may be a pragmatic first step. Second, Integrated care pathways: Developing formal, protocol-driven care pathways from primary care suspicion through specialist confirmation to treatment initiation could reduce fragmentation. Feasibility challenges include the need for stakeholder buy-in across multiple institutions, alignment with existing referral policies, and resources for coordination. Cross-Cutting Considerations. Several principles should guide any intervention development. First, interventions must acknowledge the persistent role of family in healthcare decisions and engage family members appropriately. Second, digital tools must be accessible to patients with limited literacy and technology access. Third, economic barriers require systemic solutions beyond individual-level interventions, such as expanded insurance coverage or financial navigation programs.

Limitations

This study has five limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the sample was drawn from a single tertiary hospital in Guangzhou, limiting geographical representativeness. Experiences of patients in other regions of China, particularly those with different healthcare access patterns, may differ. Second, the sample was predominantly composed of patients with nasopharyngeal carcinoma, reflecting the regional epidemiology of head and neck cancers in southern China. While this provided rich data on this specific subsite, it limits the transferability of findings to other head and neck cancer types, such as laryngeal, oropharyngeal, or salivary gland tumors. Third, recall bias is an inherent limitation of retrospective interview studies. Participants were asked to recount experiences that occurred months earlier, and their memories may have been influenced by subsequent events, emotional states, or discussions with family members. While we attempted to minimize this by triangulating across multiple participants and conducting follow-up interviews, some degree of recall inaccuracy is unavoidable. Fourth, the perspectives of family caregivers were not included. Given the central role of family in healthcare decision-making revealed in our findings, caregivers’ accounts might have provided additional insights into family dynamics, decision-making processes, and the nature of support provided. Future studies should intentionally recruit caregiver dyads. Fifth, we did not conduct quantitative analysis of delay consequences. The relationship between the delays described and clinical outcomes remains unexplored in this study.

Conclusion

Using patient journey mapping, this study systematically captured the full spectrum of delays experienced by head and neck cancer patients from symptom onset through treatment initiation. The findings demonstrate that delays are not a single event but a multistage phenomenon, with distinct barriers operating during the assessment, diagnostic, and treatment periods. Patient journey mapping proved valuable for visualizing these complex pathways, identifying cross-cutting drivers such as information asymmetry and economic vulnerability, and revealing the evolving roles of informal and formal support networks.
