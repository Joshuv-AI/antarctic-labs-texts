Sociodemographic profiles and maternal comorbidities associated with caesarean delivery: a comparison between nulliparous and multiparous mothers in Malaysia
Authors: S Maria Awaluddin, Norlaila Hamid, Wan Sarifah Ainin Wan Jusoh, Norliza Shamsuddin, Norhafizah Sahril, Yee Mang Chan, Noor Ani Ahmad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCaesarean section (CS) rates have been rising globally, with maternal comorbidities such as obesity, diabetes mellitus, and hypertensive disorders playing significant roles. Sociodemographic factors also critically influence delivery outcomes. However, limited data exist on the prevalence and determinants of CS in Malaysia. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of CS and its associated factors among nulliparous and multiparous mothers, focusing on sociodemographic profiles and maternal comorbidities.MethodologyA nationwide cross-sectional survey was conducted in Malaysia using multistage random sampling to select mothers who had delivered within the past two years. Data on delivery methods, sociodemographic factors, and maternal comorbidities were analyzed using descriptive statistics and multiple logistic regression in IBM SPSS version 25.0.ResultsAmong the 6,326 mothers surveyed, 29.1% (1,843) were nulliparous, and 70.9% were multiparous. The overall prevalence of CS was 26.2%, with nulliparous mothers having a significantly higher prevalence (31.1% vs. 24.4%, p < 0.001). Obesity (nulliparous: aOR: 2.51, 95% CI: 1.71, 3.69; multiparous: aOR: 2.00, 95% CI: 1.59, 2.53), diabetes mellitus (nulliparous: aOR: 1.74, 95% CI: 1.26, 2.40; multiparous: aOR: 1.37, 95% CI: 1.13, 1.67), overweight, and advanced maternal age were significant factors associated with CS in both groups. Hypertensive disorders were significant only among multiparous mothers who underwent CS (aOR: 1.78, 95% CI: 1.32, 2.40). Nulliparous mothers were additionally influenced by Indian ethnicity (aOR: 3.85, 95% CI: 1.28, 11.55), Chinese ethnicity (aOR: 3.07, 95% CI: 1.25, 7.55), tertiary education, and self-employment.ConclusionThis study found that obesity, overweight, diabetes mellitus, and advanced maternal age were associated with CS in both nulliparous and multiparous mothers, while hypertensive disorders were significant only among multiparous mothers. In addition, sociodemographic disparities were observed in both groups, highlighting the need for targeted interventions to reduce non-medically indicated CS in Malaysia.Keywords: Caesarean section, Maternal comorbidities, Household survey, Malaysia, Cross-sectional

Introduction

A medically indicated Caesarean section (CS) is an effective way to save mother and child after weighing the risks and benefits [1]. Similar to any surgical procedure, CS has short-term risks such as risks of anaesthesia complications, organ injuries, infections, and thromboembolic diseases while long-term complications may affect the subsequent pregnancy such as placenta praevia, placenta accreta, and intra-abdominal adhesions [2]. However, the prevalence of CS has been increasing globally, partly due to improvements in obstetric care, as well as non-clinical indications [3]. A study in Vietnam shows that the proportion of CS among mothers with non-previous uterine scars was 40.7% and noted some proportion of maternal request as one of the CS indications [4]. The current global estimate shows that the prevalence is at 21.1%, with a range of 5% in sub-Saharan Africa to 42.8% in Latin America and the Caribbean [5].

Factors contributing to CS trends are multifaceted, with maternal health conditions playing a significant role [6]. Obesity, for instance, has consistently been associated with a higher likelihood of CS, with studies indicating that obese women face a greater risk compared to those with normal body mass index (BMI) [7, 8]. This increased risk is often linked to complications such as labor dystocia, fetal macrosomia, and higher rates of gestational hypertension and diabetes [9]. Similarly, diabetes mellitus is a key predictor, as women with pre-existing or gestational diabetes are more likely to experience complications requiring surgical delivery, such as macrosomia and impaired fetal well-being [10]. Hypertensive disorders, including preeclampsia, further contribute to the rising rates of CS, with associated risks such as placental complications and restricted fetal growth often necessitating surgical intervention [11].

In addition to medical conditions, sociodemographic factors also influence CS rates. Mothers with higher income levels and those residing in urban areas or specific regions tend to have increased access to private healthcare facilities, where elective CS may be more common [6, 12, 13]. Geographic disparities in CS rates have also been observed, with some regions reporting significantly higher rates due to differences in healthcare practices, provider preferences, and maternal choices [4, 14]. These findings emphasize the interplay between maternal health conditions and sociodemographic factors in influencing delivery methods, highlighting the need for comprehensive strategies to address modifiable risks and reduce non-medically indicated CS rates.

In Malaysia, understanding the specific factors contributing to the rising rates of CS, particularly among mothers with comorbidities, is crucial as these factors are modifiable and preventable. The trend of CS in public hospitals in Malaysia was 28.4%, 28.9%, and 29.6% in 2018, 2019, and 2020, respectively [15], while the population-based survey covering public and private facilities in 2016 found a lower CS rate of 20.7% [16]. This study aims to address this gap by examining the prevalence and determinants of CS among Malaysian mothers who had a live birth in the last two years, with a particular focus on the sociodemographic characteristics and maternal comorbidities of both nulliparous and multiparous groups. By identifying the key factors associated with CS, this study provides evidence to inform targeted interventions aimed at reducing non-medically indicated CS. This is important to minimise unnecessary exposure of mothers to the short- and long-term risks associated with surgical delivery and to improve overall maternal health outcomes.

Methodology

Study design and study population

A nationwide survey focusing on maternal and child health has been conducted in Malaysia to supplement the Sustainable Development Goal and Universal Health Coverage indicators for women and children. This was a cross-sectional study among mothers (15–49 years) with the last child under the age of five using the household approach. However, this study targeted mothers whose last singleton pregnancy occurred within the past two years.

Sampling strategy and sample size

A two-stage stratified random sampling was conducted to select the Enumeration Blocks (EBs) as the primary sampling units and living quarters (LQs) within each selected EB as the secondary sampling units. A comprehensive listing was conducted at the LQ level to ensure only households with mothers and children under five years were included in the sampling frame before the random sampling was conducted. The survey used a single proportion for sample size calculation, focusing on the complete vaccination status across the 14 states. Sabah and the Federal Territory (FT) of Labuan were combined, as well as the FT of Kuala Lumpur and Putrajaya. Furthermore, Peninsular Malaysia was categorized into zones, north for Perlis, Kedah, Perak, and Pulau Pinang. East for Kelantan, Terengganu and Pahang. Central for FT Kuala Lumpur & Putrajaya, Selangor, and the south zone for Johor, Melaka and Negeri Sembilan. Sabah & FT Labuan and Sarawak were grouped into a single zone representing Malaysian Borneo.

This study utilized the two-proportion sample size calculation from the Epi Info StatCalc Sample Size and Power tool, considering the prevalence of CS among mothers with obesity (46.4%) and those with a normal BMI (39.4%) [8]. With a power of 80%, a two-sided confidence level of 95%, and a ratio of 1:1, the calculated minimum sample size was 785 per group (1,570 total). However, because the study was embedded within a nationwide maternal and child health survey using multistage stratified sampling across all states in Malaysia, the final dataset included a larger number of eligible respondents. Consequently, a total of 6,326 mothers with children aged less than two years were included in the analysis, which enhanced the statistical power and representativeness of the study findings.

Data collection

The national data collection team was formed with 55 teams for Peninsular Malaysia and 12 teams for Sabah & FT Labuan and Sarawak. Data collection was held for three months of August, September and October 2022. Each team consisted of trained data enumerators, registered nurses, and drivers and was led by a field supervisor who was the main research team member of this study. A set of structured, validated questionnaires integrated with a mobile device was used by the data collectors to conduct face-to-face interviews. The questionnaire adopted the Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey (MICS6) questionnaires, covering most of the mother and child health topics [17]. Additionally, registered nurses verified the information from the respondents with the antenatal book as well as the vaccination records to ensure a valid response.

Variables and measures

The sociodemographic data of the mothers included age, marital status, ethnicity, educational attainment, occupation, and household income. The mode of delivery was categorized into spontaneous vaginal delivery, breech delivery, vacuum delivery, forceps delivery, and CS. For regression analysis, the mode of delivery was recoded into a binary outcome variable (1 = CS, 0 = non-CS delivery). The non-CS category included all vaginal deliveries, namely spontaneous vaginal delivery, assisted vaginal delivery (vacuum or forceps), and breech vaginal delivery. Therefore, the analysis appropriately included the entire sample of mothers (N = 6,326) to compare those who underwent CS with those who did not. A nulliparous mother in this study was defined as one with no previous live births, while a multiparous mother was defined as one with more than one live birth. Maternal histories of diabetes and hypertension were recorded based on diagnoses by a doctor, as confirmed through antenatal care (ANC) records. The terms “diabetes in pregnancy” and “hypertension in pregnancy” were used without distinguishing between gestational diabetes mellitus and pre-existing diabetes, or between pregnancy-induced hypertension and chronic hypertension. Obesity was assessed using the BMI formula (weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters). Pre-pregnancy weight was derived from records in the ANC book documenting weight during the first three months of pregnancy. Other pregnancy-related variables, such as age at first pregnancy, total number of pregnancies, and total number of ANC visits during the last pregnancy, were also collected.

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The missing data for each variable were checked using a frequency table. A descriptive analysis was performed, followed by a bivariate analysis. A Rao-Scott p-value of less than 0.05 was taken as a significant association at the bivariable level. Complex sample logistic regression was conducted to determine associated factors initially using simple and finally using multivariable for each nulliparous and multiparous mothers’ group. The model was checked for multicollinearity and interaction terms. The final model results were reported as an adjusted odds ratio, 95% confidence intervals, and a p-value of less than 0.05.

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki to ensure adherence to research ethics and respondents’ anonymity. Ethical approval for the study was granted by the Medical Research and Ethics Committee of the Ministry of Health Malaysia, and the research was registered with the National Medical Research Registry (NMRR-20-959-53329). Written informed consent was secured from the respondents before data collection, ensuring adherence to ethical research practices. For respondents below the age of 18, parental or guardian consent was obtained in addition to the respondent’s assent to participate in the study.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of nulliparous and multiparous mothers

Among the 6,326 mothers surveyed, 29.1% (1,843) were nulliparous, and 70.9% were multiparous. Nulliparous mothers were significantly younger, with 90.4% aged ≤ 34 years compared to 64.7% of multiparous mothers (p < 0.001). Education levels also differed substantially, with a higher proportion of nulliparous mothers attaining tertiary education (47.6% vs. 41.4%, p < 0.001). Employment patterns varied, as multiparous mothers were more likely to be housewives (60.4% vs. 53.3%), while nulliparous mothers were predominantly employed in the private sector (27.2% vs. 15.1%, p < 0.001). Ethnic differences were also notable, with nulliparous mothers showing higher representation among Chinese (5.5% vs. 3.1%) and Indian (4.0% vs. 2.9%) ethnicities (p < 0.001) (Table 1).

Prevalence of maternal morbidities by parity

Significant differences in maternal morbidities were observed between nulliparous and multiparous mothers. Multiparous mothers exhibited a higher prevalence of hypertensive disorders (7.2% vs. 4.9%, p = 0.007), diabetes mellitus (28.3% vs. 24.4%, p = 0.008), and obesity (21.4% vs. 15.4%, p < 0.001). Overweight prevalence was also greater among multiparous mothers (28.6% vs. 21.2%, p < 0.001), indicating elevated health risks within this group (Table 2).

Prevalence of CS by parity

The overall prevalence of CS was 26.2%, with nulliparous mothers having a significantly higher prevalence (31.1% vs. 24.4%, p < 0.001). Among nulliparous mothers, the prevalence of CS was highest for those aged ≥ 35 years (46.5% vs. 29.4%, p < 0.001), similar to multiparous mothers but with a smaller gap (28.8% vs. 22.0%, p < 0.001). Nulliparous mothers with tertiary education had the highest CS prevalence (34.8%), whereas multiparous mothers showed no significant differences by education level.

Both groups exhibited higher CS prevalence in the presence of hypertensive disorders, diabetes mellitus, obesity, and overweight, with nulliparous mothers consistently having higher rates than multiparous mothers: hypertensive disorders (48.0% vs. 41.6%), diabetes mellitus (40.0% vs. 32.1%), obesity (48.0% vs. 35.8%), and overweight (35.9% vs. 24.8%). Other significant differences included zone, ethnicity, age at first pregnancy, and occupation, while household income, rural-urban strata, and place of delivery showed no significant differences (Table 3).

Factors associated with CS

Multivariable logistic regression analysis revealed distinct factors associated with CS in nulliparous and multiparous mothers. Among nulliparous mothers, significant factors included Indian ethnicity (aOR: 3.85, 95% CI: 1.28, 11.55), Chinese ethnicity (aOR: 3.07, 95% CI: 1.25, 7.55), maternal obesity (aOR: 2.51, 95% CI: 1.71, 3.69), self-employment (aOR: 2.41, 95% CI: 1.54, 3.79), tertiary education (aOR: 1.92, 95% CI: 1.04, 3.54), diabetes mellitus (aOR: 1.74, 95% CI: 1.26, 2.40), overweight (aOR: 1.51, 95% CI: 1.12, 2.03), and older age (aOR: 1.81, 95% CI: 1.18, 2.79). In multiparous mothers, key factors included maternal obesity (aOR: 2.00, 95% CI: 1.59, 2.53), hypertensive disorders (aOR: 1.78, 95% CI: 1.32, 2.40), residence in the North Zone (aOR: 1.65, 95% CI: 1.18, 2.29) or South Zone (aOR: 1.52, 95% CI: 1.06, 2.18), diabetes mellitus (aOR: 1.37, 95% CI: 1.13, 1.67), private sector employment (aOR: 1.34, 95% CI: 1.05, 1.72), overweight (aOR: 1.31, 95% CI: 1.05, 1.64), and older age (aOR: 1.26, 95% CI: 1.05, 1.52).

Across both groups, obesity, overweight, diabetes mellitus, and older age were strongly associated with CS, while hypertensive disorders were significant only in multiparous mothers. Nulliparous mothers, however, were uniquely associated with factors such as ethnicity (Indian and Chinese), tertiary education, and self-employment. These findings highlight the distinct sociodemographic and maternal morbidities patterns influencing CS decisions and underscore the need for tailored interventions to address these disparities (Table 4).

Discussion

This study reported an overall CS prevalence of 26.2% among Malaysian mothers who had a live birth in the last two years, reflecting a rising trend from the previous rate of 20.7% observed in the first survey and aligning with global increases in CS rates [16]. The World Health Organization estimated that nearly one-third of all births could be by CS by 2030, reflecting the global shift towards surgical deliveries [5]. Despite multiparous mothers having a higher prevalence of certain maternal comorbidities, nulliparous mothers exhibited a higher rate of CS in this study. This finding is consistent with previous studies indicating that first pregnancies are more likely to involve labour complications such as prolonged labour, labour dystocia, and increased clinical caution among healthcare providers, which may contribute to higher intervention rates [18, 19]. Additionally, nulliparous mothers are more likely to undergo primary CS delivery, which subsequently increases the likelihood of repeat Caesarean sections in future pregnancies.

Maternal comorbidities, particularly obesity and diabetes mellitus, were strongly associated with CS in both nulliparous and multiparous mothers. These findings are consistent with prior studies linking these conditions to increased risks of fetal macrosomia, labor dystocia, and obstetric complications necessitating surgical delivery [20–24]. These are modifiable factors that could be addressed through targeted public health interventions, such as promoting healthy weight management and effective glycaemic control during pre-pregnancy and antenatal periods, particularly in nulliparous mothers [25]. Hypertensive disorders, although significant only in multiparous mothers, may reflect a contribution from advanced maternal age or pre-existing essential hypertension, which often complicate later pregnancies [20].

The association of advanced maternal age with higher CS rates, particularly among nulliparous mothers, reflects sociodemographic shifts in modern society [26]. Despite only 10% of nulliparous mothers being classified as of advanced maternal age (≥ 35 years), nearly half of them required a CS. Delayed childbearing, driven by priorities of higher education and career advancement, has been linked to higher risks of CS [27]. This study also found that tertiary education was associated with higher odds of CS in nulliparous mothers (aOR: 1.92), which may reflect greater awareness or preferences for elective CS [28]. Ethnic variations further highlight disparities, with Chinese and Indian nulliparous mothers having significantly higher odds of CS. Socioeconomic factors, such as higher income levels and career priorities among Chinese women, likely influence these differences [26, 29]. However, different reasons for Indian ethnicity, as a previous study shows, may be due to an increased prevalence of obesity and gestational diabetes mellitus among Indian women [8, 30].

Regional and occupational factors further underscore disparities in CS rates. Mothers residing in the North and South zones of Peninsular Malaysia exhibited a higher likelihood of CS, suggesting potential differences in healthcare access, provider practices, or maternal choice [14]. Conversely, no association was found between CS and urban or rural residence, indicating that geographic disparities may be more complex than urban-rural dichotomies. However, previous studies have shown that rural residents have a lower risk of CS than urban residents [31, 32]. Self-employed mothers among nulliparous mothers were found to have a higher likelihood of CS, potentially due to occupational stress or irregular antenatal care, which warrants further investigation [33]. In contrast, the multiparous mothers who work in the private sector tend to have CS in this study, whether they have medical insurance or advancing age, which is in line with increasing risks of maternal morbidities as found in previous studies [13, 34, 35]. Notably, sociodemographic factors, such as education, ethnicity, and employment, played a more pronounced role among nulliparous mothers, highlighting the intersection between parity and social determinants of health.

Recommendations

Efforts to reduce CS rates should prioritize public health interventions targeting modifiable risk factors, including obesity and diabetes, through pre-pregnancy weight management programs and improved antenatal care services. Policies that promote equitable access to healthcare should address regional disparities and support timely antenatal care, particularly for self-employed and low-income mothers. Further research is needed to explore the cultural, social, and healthcare system factors associated with higher CS rates among specific ethnic groups and regions. Tailored interventions focusing on nulliparous mothers could help prevent non-medically indicated primary CS and improve long-term maternal and child health outcomes in Malaysia.

Strength and limitations

A major strength of this study is the use of a large, nationally representative sample, which enhances the generalizability of the findings to the Malaysian population. The study also examined both sociodemographic and maternal comorbidities associated with CS across parity groups, providing valuable insights for maternal health policy and clinical practice.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish causal relationships between the identified factors and CS delivery. Second, the dataset was derived from a household survey and did not include certain clinical variables required for Robson’s 10-group classification, such as fetal presentation, onset of labour, and gestational age [36]. As a result, classification of CS using Robson’s system was not possible. Additionally, the dataset did not distinguish between elective and emergency CS, which may provide further insights into clinical decision-making. Future studies incorporating detailed obstetric data are recommended to better understand the indications for CS delivery in Malaysia.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study identified distinct factors associated with CS among nulliparous and multiparous mothers in Malaysia, highlighting the influence of both maternal comorbidities and sociodemographic profiles. Obesity, overweight, diabetes mellitus, and advanced maternal age were consistently associated with increased likelihood of CS across both groups. However, hypertensive disorders were significantly associated with CS only among multiparous mothers, indicating a unique risk pattern in this group. Among nulliparous mothers, additional significant factors included Indian and Chinese ethnicity, tertiary education, and self-employment, suggesting that cultural and socioeconomic dynamics may influence delivery decisions in first-time mothers. These findings underscore the complex interplay of clinical and demographic factors in CS decisions and emphasize the need for targeted strategies to address modifiable risks, particularly obesity and diabetes, while considering the distinct profiles of nulliparous and multiparous mothers. Tailored interventions focusing on maternal health optimization and equitable healthcare access could help reduce non-medically indicated CS rates and improve maternal and child health outcomes.
