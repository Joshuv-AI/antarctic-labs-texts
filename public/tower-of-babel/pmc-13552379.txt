Clinical characteristics and outcomes of paediatric chronic non-bacterial osteomyelitis: a retrospective cohort study with focus on zoledronic acid
Authors: Yehonatan Herman, Jonathan D Akikusa, Julie Jones, Georgina M Tiller, Jane E Munro, Peter Simm, Margaret Zacharin, Neelika Liyanage, Nisha Varma, William D Renton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesChronic non-bacterial osteomyelitis is a rare autoinflammatory bone disease of childhood. Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs are often used first-line, while conventional and biologic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs and bisphosphonates are reserved for severe or refractory disease. Zoledronic acid (ZA) is used off-label, but robust data on its role remain limited. This study aimed to describe the clinical characteristics, management and outcomes of children with chronic non-bacterial osteomyelitis, with a primary focus on the effectiveness and tolerability of ZA.MethodA retrospective review was conducted of children diagnosed with chronic non-bacterial osteomyelitis between 2014 and 2024 at the Royal Children’s Hospital, Melbourne. Clinical and magnetic resonance imaging responses to ZA, as well as adverse effects, were evaluated.ResultsFifty-seven patients were included [mean age 10.3 years; 70% female; median follow-up 3.7 years (range 1.4–8.9)]. Multifocal disease was present in 86%, and 63% underwent biopsy. All received non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs initially. Twenty-two patients (37%) received zoledronic acid (mean 2.6 doses); 77% showed significant clinical improvement. Three later required tumour necrosis factor inhibitors, and two received tumour necrosis factor inhibitors without prior zoledronic acid. Among 10 patients with pre- and post-zoledronic acid whole-body magnetic resonance imaging, 80% demonstrated significant radiological improvement. Mild, transient flu-like symptoms were reported in 54% of patients treated with zoledronic acid; no serious adverse events occurred.ConclusionZoledronic acid was associated with high clinical and radiological response rates and was well tolerated. Its infrequent dosing schedule, typically one infusion every six months, offers a practical alternative to other bisphosphonates.

Introduction

Chronic non-bacterial osteomyelitis (CNO), also referred to as chronic recurrent multifocal osteomyelitis (CRMO), is a rare autoinflammatory bone disorder that typically presents in childhood or adolescence, with peak onset between 7 and 12 years of age. Reported sex ratios vary across cohorts, with most studies documenting a female predominance, although near-equal sex distribution has also been described [1–3]. CNO has been documented across all ethnic groups, without a clear geographical predilection [3].

The true incidence of CNO remains uncertain. A recent national survey from the United Kingdom estimated an incidence of 0.65 cases per 100 000 children and adolescents, although some series suggest higher rates [4, 5]. Pathogenesis is still poorly defined, but current evidence suggests dysregulation of the innate immune system, with an imbalance between pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines thought to be central [6, 7].

CNO is frequently associated with other autoinflammatory and immune-mediated conditions, such as inflammatory bowel disease and psoriasis [5, 8]. The clinical presentation is highly variable: while chronic bone pain with insidious onset is most common, patients may also have swelling, tenderness or overlying inflammatory skin changes. Disease may be unifocal or multifocal, symmetric or asymmetric, and either recurrent or persistent. Multifocal involvement is reported in up to 75–85% of cases. Systemic features such as fever occur less often, and arthritis has been reported in about 15–30% of cases [9]. Virtually any bone can be affected, although the metaphysis and epiphysis of long bones, the medial clavicle, pelvis and vertebral bodies are most frequently involved [2].

Diagnosis remains one of exclusion, drawing on history, examination, inflammatory markers, imaging, particularly whole-body MRI, and, when needed, histopathology. Classification criteria have been proposed to standardize case definition; the recently published EULAR/ACR criteria for paediatric CNO demonstrate good sensitivity and excellent specificity, and do not require bone biopsy [11]. Earlier criteria sets (Jansson, Bristol, Roderick) have also been described [9–11]. A major limitation in both research and clinical practice remains the absence of validated disease activity measures, which limits reliable assessment of treatment response and comparison across studies.

Management of CNO continues to be empirical, and treatment approaches vary between centres and individual clinicians. Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) are often used as first-line therapy and provide symptom relief for many patients. For those with persistent disease, spinal lesions or inadequate response to NSAIDs, second-line therapies such as corticosteroids, conventional disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drugs (cDMARDs), bisphosphonates and tumour necrosis factor inhibitors (TNFi) are used. The most recent consensus guidance from the Childhood Arthritis and Rheumatology Research Alliance (CARRA, 2018) reflects this strategy [12]. A similar approach has been adopted for adult CNO [13].

Among bisphosphonates, pamidronate has been the most widely studied in CNO [14–16]. Zoledronic acid (ZA), a potent third-generation intravenous bisphosphonate, is also commonly used in clinical practice [17]. Compared with pamidronate, ZA offers a more convenient dosing regimen, typically administered as a relatively short intravenous infusion every six months, whereas pamidronate requires slow infusions every three months, thereby reducing the treatment burden for children and families. Reports suggest that ZA may reduce pain and disease activity; however, data on its efficacy and safety in paediatric CNO remain limited. Most published experience comes from small case series or mixed cohorts, with few systematic or large-scale studies [17, 18]. This study describes a retrospective cohort of children with CNO managed at a tertiary paediatric centre, with a primary focus on outcomes in those treated with ZA.

Methods

We conducted a retrospective cohort study at the Royal Children’s Hospital (RCH), Melbourne, Australia, reviewing all children diagnosed with CNO between January 2014 and January 2024. Eligible patients were identified through the hospital’s electronic medical records. Inclusion criteria included a diagnosis of CNO made before the age of 18 years and management by the paediatric rheumatology service. Ethics approval was obtained from the Royal Children’s Hospital Human Research Ethics Committee (Department and Ethics Research Platform identifier 3824).

Diagnosis was made clinically by the treating paediatric rheumatologist, based on a combination of clinical history, physical examination, inflammatory markers and whole-body MRI findings, with bone biopsy performed where indicated to exclude infectious or malignant mimics. Following publication of the 2025 EULAR/ACR classification criteria for paediatric chronic non-bacterial osteomyelitis [19], these criteria were applied retrospectively to the cohort using available clinical, laboratory and imaging data. Where relevant, previously published classification frameworks (Jansson, Bristol and Roderick) informed diagnostic assessment. Patient treatment pathways were determined at the discretion of the treating rheumatologist. Patients were excluded if they had an alternative confirmed diagnosis or if clinical data were insufficient for review.

Data were collected from medical records using a standardized extraction tool. Information gathered included demographics, anthropometry, clinical features, laboratory results, MRI findings, treatment, disease course and bone density by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) assessment. Axial involvement was defined as the presence of lesions involving the spine, sacrum, pelvis, ribs or sternum. Spinal involvement was defined as lesions involving the cervical, thoracic or lumbar spine only, excluding sacral lesions and sacroiliac joint inflammation. Particular focus was placed on patients treated with intravenous ZA, with details recorded on treatment timing, number of infusions and concomitant therapies.

Clinical response to ZA was assessed independently by two paediatric rheumatologists using a structured, non-validated framework based on global physician assessment and patient-reported symptoms (Supplementary Table S1). Due to the lack of validated outcome tools in paediatric CNO, assessment was based on changes in reported pain, functional status and overall documented clinical impression, rather than formal quantitative disease activity measures or lesion counts. Inter-rater agreement was assessed using Cohen’s kappa coefficient.

Where available, whole-body MRI scans were independently reviewed by two paediatric radiologists with subspecialty expertise in musculoskeletal imaging, who were blinded to the clinical response assessments and treatments used. On pre-treatment MRI studies, all bone lesions were identified and counted. Post-treatment MRI images were then compared with baseline scans to evaluate each lesion for changes in size and signal intensity, as well as to identify any new lesions. Radiological response was based on predefined criteria (Supplementary Table S2). Cohen’s kappa coefficient was used to determine the initial score of inter-rater agreement.

Follow-up whole-body magnetic resonance imaging was not performed according to a standardized protocol; instead, timing was guided by clinical need and treating physician preference. Post-treatment scans were typically obtained 9–18 months after treatment initiation, although this varied between patients.

Results

During the study period, 66 children were diagnosed with CNO. Nine were excluded due to insufficient records, leaving 57 for analysis. The mean time from symptom onset to diagnosis was 13 months (range 1–38 months). The median age at diagnosis was 10.1 years (range 3.8–15.5), and there was a female predominance (70.1%; male-to-female ratio 1:2.3). Comorbidities were documented in 12.2% (7/57), most commonly psoriasis (n = 4) and inflammatory bowel disease (n = 3). Three patients fulfilled criteria for SAPHO syndrome, and 4% (4/57) had associated arthritis (Table 1). Most children (47/57, 86%) had multifocal disease, with 40% (23/57) demonstrating symmetric lesions. Eight patients (14%) presented with a single lesion, most often in the mandible (n = 3). Axial lesions were identified in 59% of patients (34/57), whereas spinal lesions were present in 16% (9/57). Patients with spinal involvement were more likely to require escalation to ZA treatment beyond NSAIDs, consistent with current CARRA recommendations [13].

Retrospective application of the 2025 EULAR/ACR classification criteria demonstrated that 55/57 (96.4%) patients fulfilled criteria for paediatric chronic non-bacterial osteomyelitis. The remaining 2/57 (3.5%) patients could not be definitively classified due to missing C-reactive protein (CRP) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) at diagnosis.

At presentation, inflammatory markers were frequently elevated. ESR was above 20 mm/h in 35 patients, with a median of 31 mm/h (IQR 14–51). CRP was above 5 mg/l in 23 patients, with a median of 5 mg/l (IQR 5–22). Fever was present in 3 of 57 (5.2%) at disease onset. Following treatment with NSAIDs or second-line agents, inflammatory markers improved in the majority of patients: median ESR decreased from 31 mm/h to 9 mm/h (IQR 3.8–15.5), and median CRP fell correspondingly, with only three patients demonstrating values above the reference range at follow-up.

Baseline serum vitamin D levels were available for 21 of the 22 ZA-treated patients, with a mean of 61 nmol/l (range 26–90 nmol/l). Four patients (19%) had suboptimal vitamin D status prior to treatment, two with deficiency (<30 nmol/l) and two with insufficiency (30–49 nmol/l). All four received a stoss dose of 150 000 IU vitamin D before their first infusion in accordance with local protocol. Pre-treatment DXA was available in 14 patients. Among the four patients with paired pre- and post-ZA DXA studies (median interval 1.3 years, range 1.1–6 years), the median change in height-adjusted lumbar spine BMD Z-score was +1.2 (range +0.4 to +1.7), and the height-adjusted total body BMD Z-score was +0.4 (range −0.5 to +1.8). No serious skeletal adverse events were observed during follow-up.

Bone biopsies were performed in 36 patients (63.1%). All patients with unifocal lesions underwent biopsy (n = 8), consistent with the need to exclude infection or malignancy in unifocal presentations. Among patients with multifocal disease, biopsy was performed in 57% (28/49), reflecting our centre’s practice during this period. The majority (32/36, 88%) showed non-specific chronic inflammation; normal bone was found in 3 (8.3%) and fibrosis without inflammatory infiltrate in 1 (2.7%).

All children received NSAIDs as initial therapy, and a substantial proportion (42%) required additional treatment. Corticosteroids were used in 28% (16/57), typically as short courses during flares or while awaiting the effect of longer-acting agents. Methotrexate was used in 15.7% (9/57) for persistent or relapsing disease, and ZA in 38% (22/57). Adalimumab was started in 12.2% (7/57), usually in those with more severe or refractory disease or in the presence of comorbid immune-mediated conditions. Pamidronate was not used at our centre during the study period, as ZA had become the preferred bisphosphonate. Notably, all patients who received methotrexate as second-line therapy subsequently transitioned to ZA (Fig. 1).

A subset of patients with inadequate response to NSAIDs were treated with intravenous ZA, most often for multifocal disease, spinal lesions or persistent pain despite other therapies. ZA dosing ranged from 0.025 mg/kg to 0.05 mg/kg, with a mean dose of 0.047 mg/kg. Across the cohort, treatment patterns varied: some patients received a single infusion with sustained benefit, others completed short courses of two to four infusions given approximately six months apart, and a smaller number received intermittent doses over several years. Clinical response was categorized using pre-defined criteria and showed that after ZA treatment, 77.2% (17/22) experienced significant clinical improvement, 18.1% (4/22) showed mild improvement and 4.5% (1/22) had no response (Table 2). Inter-rater agreement between the two paediatric rheumatologists was 0.69 using Cohen’s kappa coefficient, consistent with substantial agreement.

In the 10 patients with paired pre- and post-ZA WB-MRI, 80% showed marked radiological improvement, while 20% improved more modestly (Table 2). Inter-rater agreement for MRI response classification was moderate (Cohen’s κ = 0.40).

ZA was generally well tolerated. Mild acute phase reactions, most commonly fever and flu-like symptoms, occurred in 54% (12/22), consistent with the known adverse-effect profile of bisphosphonates [18]. No serious adverse events were identified. Asymptomatic transient hypocalcaemia was not systematically assessed, but no clinically significant hypocalcaemia was recorded, and no cases of anaphylaxis, osteonecrosis of the jaw or atypical fractures were observed. Growth velocity remained within the normal range before and after treatment, with a mean height-centile change of –0.6% (n = 19, range –12.49% to +9.26%) over a mean follow-up of 2.4 years.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this represents the largest retrospective cohort to date describing the use of ZA in children with CNO. We found that ZA was associated with marked clinical and radiological improvement in the majority of patients with refractory disease, while maintaining an acceptable safety profile.

Most children who received zoledronic acid in our cohort had persisting symptoms despite non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug use, which aligns with current treatment approaches that escalate to second-line therapies in persistent or severe disease. Following treatment, ∼80% of patients achieved meaningful clinical improvement. This response rate is consistent with previously published data. Bhat et al. reported comparable clinical and radiological improvement in children treated with pamidronate using whole-body magnetic resonance imaging as the primary outcome measure [14], while Hofmann et al. similarly demonstrated substantial improvement in most patients treated with pamidronate with standardized follow-up [16]. Miettunen et al. also described marked pain relief in children receiving pamidronate [15]. When compared with tumour necrosis factor inhibitor therapy, Schnabel et al. reported comparable outcomes between pamidronate and tumour necrosis factor inhibitors in a large international multicentre retrospective study, with no clear superiority of either approach [18]. Taken together, these findings suggest that multiple second-line therapies may offer similar effectiveness in chronic non-bacterial osteomyelitis. Importantly, in our cohort, radiological improvement on whole-body magnetic resonance imaging was observed in most patients with paired scans, supporting the clinical findings and reinforcing the potential therapeutic role of zoledronic acid.

Safety remains a key consideration when using bisphosphonates in paediatric practice. In this study, ZA was generally well tolerated. Mild acute-phase infusion reactions, most often fever and flu-like symptoms, were reported in just over half of treated patients, a rate consistent with the known side effect profile of bisphosphonates. Baseline vitamin D levels were available for most patients, with four identified as having suboptimal levels; all received replacement before infusion in accordance with local protocol. In keeping with larger paediatric and international bisphosphonate cohorts, no serious adverse events such as osteonecrosis of the jaw or pathological fractures were identified during follow-up. Growth velocity also remained stable, which is reassuring, although larger studies with longer follow-up are required to definitively establish the long-term skeletal safety of ZA. At our centre, dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry is routinely performed at baseline and during follow-up to monitor bone mass accrual and minimize the risk of overtreatment, recognizing the non-targeted effects of ZA on the skeleton; reassuringly, all scans remained within the expected range for age.

Inflammatory markers such as ESR and CRP were frequently elevated at diagnosis in our cohort, consistent with previous reports of their supportive but non-specific role in CNO [7, 11]. While they provide useful context at presentation, their variability and lack of correlation with disease activity limit their utility for ongoing monitoring, underscoring the need for validated biomarkers and disease activity measures in paediatric CNO.

CNO is frequently associated with other autoinflammatory and immune-mediated conditions, with previous studies reporting extraosseous manifestations in up to one quarter of patients, including psoriasis, inflammatory bowel disease and related disorders [5, 8]. In our cohort, comorbid immune-mediated conditions were documented in 12% of patients, most commonly psoriasis and inflammatory bowel disease, which is somewhat lower than previously reported. Where present, these comorbidities influenced treatment selection towards tumour necrosis factor inhibitor therapy (adalimumab), given the potential for concomitant benefit across conditions. This is consistent with practice patterns described in other cohorts and aligns with current expert recommendations for adult CNO [10]. Future studies should further explore the impact of comorbid disease on treatment decisions and clinical outcomes.

Although pamidronate has historically been more commonly reported in chronic non-bacterial osteomyelitis, zoledronic acid is increasingly preferred in paediatric practice due to its greater potency and more convenient dosing schedule. Pamidronate requires prolonged infusions every three months, whereas zoledronic acid is administered as a shorter infusion every six months, reducing treatment burden for patients and families.

Our findings demonstrate that zoledronic acid is an effective treatment option, achieving meaningful disease control with limited need for further escalation. While the evidence base remains largely retrospective, it is important to note that a similar level of evidence supports the use of pamidronate in this condition, supporting zoledronic acid as a pragmatic and effective alternative among second-line therapies. Notably, all patients treated with methotrexate as second-line therapy subsequently transitioned to zoledronic acid, suggesting a limited role for methotrexate in our cohort.

This study has several limitations. As a retrospective, single-centre analysis, the findings may not be generalizable to all populations. The sample size was modest, and clinical and radiological responses were assessed subjectively, reflecting the absence of validated paediatric disease activity tools, an important ongoing need in the field.

The limited cohort size also precluded meaningful statistical analysis of flare rates, time to flare, or duration and magnitude of response, and these observations should therefore be interpreted descriptively rather than statistically. Inter-rater agreement between the two reporting radiologists was only moderate, highlighting the inherent variability of qualitative MRI assessment in CNO. Even so, the use of simple, predefined response categories supported reasonable consistency between observers. Furthermore, although no serious adverse events were detected, the follow-up period may not have been long enough to capture very long-term complications.

The Chronic Nonbacterial Osteomyelitis MRI Scoring (CROMRIS) tool, first published in 2019 by Zhao et al. [19] was only preliminarily validated after completion of our study in August 2025 [20], and was therefore not applied to this cohort; its use in future studies should be considered to enable standardized and objective assessment of radiological outcomes.

The high rate of fulfilment of the 2025 EULAR/ACR classification criteria represents a strength of this study, supporting the relevance of validated classification frameworks, while the small number of non-classifiable cases reflects limitations inherent to retrospective data collection.

Overall, the high level of observed clinical and radiographic response, together with the low rate of escalation to alternative therapies over a relatively long follow-up period, suggests that ZA is an effective and well-tolerated treatment option for many children with CNO. Larger multicentre studies incorporating standardized outcome measures and longer follow-up are needed to better define its place among second-line therapies in paediatric CNO.
