Age at menarche and chronological age as explanatory factors of BMI: a cross-sectional study among Peruvian adolescents
Authors: Norma Del Carmen Gálvez-Díaz, David Javier-Aliaga, Christian Casas-Gálvez, Luz Antonia Barreto-Espinoza, Jacksaint Saintila, Yaquelin E. Calizaya-Milla
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAge at menarche has been associated with various indicators of adolescent health, including body mass index (BMI). However, its specific contribution to BMI across different geographical contexts remains largely unexplored.ObjectiveTo analyze whether chronological age and age at menarche act as explanatory factors of BMI in a sample of Peruvian adolescents.MethodsThis was an explanatory study. A non-probabilistic sample of 423 adolescents from Lambayeque, Peru, was included. A structured data collection sheet was used to assess age at menarche, as well as weight and height.ResultsThe multiple regression model identified age at menarche and chronological age as significant predictors of BMI. Model 2 demonstrated a better fit (BIC = 1658 vs. BIC = 1669 in Model 1) and explained 31% of the variability in BMI (adjusted R² = 0.310, F = 96.018, p < 0.001). A negative effect of age at menarche on BMI was observed (β = -0.161, p < 0.001), indicating that earlier menarche is associated with higher BMI. Similarly, chronological age had a significant positive effect (β = 0.516, p < 0.001) and was the variable with the greatest impact on BMI.ConclusionChronological age and age at menarche are important explanatory factors of BMI in adolescents. Earlier menarche is associated with higher BMI, underscoring the importance of considering pubertal development when evaluating nutritional status during this stage of life.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-025-04181-5.

Introduction

Overweight and obesity during adolescence have become public health issues of growing concern worldwide. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), in 2022, more than 340 million children and adolescents aged 5 to 19 were classified as overweight—a figure that has shown a steady increase since 1975 [1]. This trend is particularly alarming due to the short- and long-term consequences that excess weight can have on adolescents’ physical, emotional, and social health [2]. In Latin America, the prevalence of overweight and obesity among adolescents has increased significantly in recent decades, rising from 6.2% in 1990 to 7.5% in 2020 [3]. This phenomenon has been attributed to multiple factors, including rapid urbanization, increased consumption of ultra-processed foods, reduced physical activity, and shifts in lifestyle and eating patterns [2]. In the specific case of Peru, it is estimated that 62.7% of individuals aged 15 and older are overweight [4]. In this context, it is important to identify the biological and social factors that influence nutritional status during adolescence in order to design more targeted and culturally appropriate interventions.

Menarche, defined as the first menstrual period, is one of the most important milestones in female pubertal development [5]. This event marks the onset of reproductive capacity and reflects a complex interaction among genetic, hormonal, environmental, and nutritional factors that regulate growth and sexual maturation [6]. Menarche typically occurs between the ages of 10 and 16, but there is considerable individual and population-level variability influenced by socioeconomic conditions, nutritional status, physical activity, and levels of psychosocial stress [5, 6].

The age at which menarche occurs has important implications for the health of adolescent girls. Early menarche has been associated with alterations in body composition, greater accumulation of body fat, and an increased risk of developing overweight and obesity during both adolescence and adulthood [7]. This phenomenon may be explained by the hormonal changes that accompany early puberty, particularly the increase in estrogen levels, which promotes the deposition of adipose tissue, especially in the gluteal and abdominal regions [8]. Likewise, adolescent girls who experience early menarche tend to maintain a higher BMI over time and are at increased risk of developing metabolic and cardiovascular diseases in adulthood [9, 11]. This association is not only physiological but also behavioral, as adolescents who mature earlier may adopt less healthy lifestyles—such as reduced physical activity and poor eating habits—partly in response to the psychosocial changes they face as a result of developing earlier than their peers [12, 13].

On the other hand, during adolescence, chronological age is closely associated with a series of physiological changes that influence somatic growth, body composition, and metabolism [14]. This stage is characterized by an accelerated increase in height and weight, as well as a redistribution of body tissues, with an increase in fat mass in females due to sexual dimorphism—changes mediated by the activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis [15]. At the same time, energy metabolism undergoes several adaptations, such as a slight decrease in basal metabolic rate toward the end of adolescence, which, combined with reduced physical activity, can promote the accumulation of body fat if there is no adequate balance between energy intake and expenditure [16, 17]. In this sense, age may have a direct influence on BMI, as it reflects both the progression of biological development and the behavioral changes characteristic of this stage.

In fact, various studies have shown that BMI tends to increase with age during adolescence, although the pattern may vary depending on sex, sociocultural context, and lifestyle [18]. In Latin American populations, it has been observed that older adolescents are more likely to be overweight or obese than their younger counterparts. This may reflect not only the natural progression of physical development, but also greater exposure to risk factors such as a sedentary lifestyle, increased access to ultra-processed foods, and a low perceived risk regarding bodily health [19, 20]. Furthermore, one study suggests that BMI increases more rapidly during adolescence than in adulthood [21]. In contexts such as Peru, where poverty, rapid urbanization, and shifts in dietary patterns coexist [22], age may serve as a marker of the level of exposure to obesogenic environments [23]. For this reason, it is important to analyze its influence on BMI in combination with other biological factors, such as age at menarche, in order to gain a more comprehensive understanding of nutritional status during this critical stage of the life cycle.

Despite the evidence linking both chronological age and age at menarche to BMI in adolescents, few studies have examined the combined effect of these variables on nutritional status, particularly in Latin American contexts [7, 11, 15, 19]. Most research tends to address these factors in isolation, without considering the potential interaction between chronological and biological development as a dynamic process influencing bodily changes during adolescence [7, 11, 24]. This gap in the literature limits a comprehensive understanding of the biological determinants of overweight during this stage.

In the Peruvian context, empirical evidence on these issues remains limited, particularly among adolescent girls. Most existing studies have focused on traditional nutritional variables or on the social determinants of overweight [25, 26], overlooking aspects of pubertal development such as menarche. Furthermore, there are marked geographical and cultural differences within the country—between coastal, highland, and jungle regions—that may influence the age at menarche. This highlights the need for contextualized research that considers these particularities. Generating local evidence is essential for designing interventions and public policies that respond to the specific realities of the Peruvian adolescent population. Therefore, the objective of this study was to analyze whether chronological age and age at menarche act as explanatory factors of BMI in a sample of Peruvian adolescents.

Materials y methods

Design and participants

A cross-sectional and explanatory study was conducted between September and December 2023 at an educational institution located in the northern region of Lambayeque, Peru. The sample consisted of 423 adolescents aged between 14 and 17 years. Participants were selected using purposive non-probabilistic sampling during a health campaign at the participating school. Although the study design initially considered including younger adolescents, no participants younger than 14 years who had already experienced menarche were identified, and therefore the analytic sample was restricted to this age range. Data were collected using a structured registration form that included information on the age at menarche, sociodemographic characteristics, and anthropometric measurements such as weight and height. The independent variables were chronological age and age at menarche, while the dependent variable was BMI.

The required sample size for the multiple regression analysis was estimated using G*Power statistical software (version 3.1.9.7) [27]. Calculations indicated that a sample of 107 participants would be sufficient to detect effects with a significance level of α = 0.05, statistical power of 0.95, a moderate effect size (f² = 0.15), and two predictor variables. However, to improve the precision and robustness of the analyses, the sample size was increased to 423 participants.

Variables

Age at menarche

Participants were first asked whether they had already experienced their first menstrual period (menarche). Those who responded “yes” were then asked to report the age (in years) at which it occurred. Before responding, they were provided with a brief explanation of the term “menarche” to ensure understanding. The nurses responsible for data collection received comprehensive training to ensure clear and standardized explanations.

Age

Participants were asked to report their age at the time of the study. Chronological age was recorded in completed years and, in cases of uncertainty, verified using an identity document or school records. Additional sociodemographic data were also collected, including place of origin (coast, highlands, or jungle).

BMI

Weight and height were each measured twice using a previously calibrated SECA 700 mechanical column scale, with a maximum capacity of 220 kg and a measurement range of 60 to 200 cm (SECA®, Hamburg, Germany). Anthropometric assessments were conducted by two nurses during the early morning hours, when participants arrived at the educational institution for evaluation. Participants were asked to be barefoot and to wear light clothing during data collection. Subsequently, the BMI z-score was calculated and classified into the following categories: underweight (z-score < −1), normal weight (z-score between ≥ −1 and ≤ 1), and overweight (z-score > 1) [28]. All the sociodemographic and anthropometric data of the participants were collected using a structured registration form.

All sociodemographic and anthropometric data were collected using a structured registration form specifically developed for this study. An English version of this form has been uploaded as a supplementary file (see Supplementary File 1).

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS (version 29) and RStudio (version 2023.12.0–0). Descriptive statistics included measures of central tendency such as mean and standard deviation. For inferential analysis, Pearson’s correlation coefficient, one-way ANOVA, and multiple linear regression models were applied. In addition, eta squared (η²) was calculated to estimate the effect size and the magnitude of the differences observed. A significance level of 5% (p < 0.05) and a 95% confidence interval were used to assess the statistical significance of the correlations and the coefficients in the regression model.

Ethical aspects

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Universidad Señor de Sipán (Ref.: N°: 1084-CIEI), adhering to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants received detailed information about the study’s objectives, the voluntary nature of their participation, and their right to withdraw at any time without consequence. Confidentiality and anonymity were fully ensured throughout the process. As all participants were under 18 years of age, written informed consent was obtained from their parents or legal guardians prior to participation.

Table 3 presents the results of the multiple regression analysis for predicting BMI. Model 2 demonstrated a better fit compared to Model 1, as indicated by its lower BIC value (Model 2: BIC = 1658 vs. Model 1: BIC = 1669). This model was statistically significant overall (adjusted R² = 0.310, F = 96.018, p < 0.001), indicating that the predictor variables explained 31% of the variability in BMI. A significant negative effect of age at menarche on BMI was observed (β = − 0.161, 95% CI [–0.241, − 0.081], p < 0.001), suggesting that earlier menarche is associated with higher BMI. Likewise, a positive and highly significant effect of chronological age on BMI was observed (β = 0.516, 95% CI [0.436, 0.596], p < 0.001), suggesting that higher age is associated with higher BMI. Notably, among the two explanatory variables included in the model, chronological age had the strongest effect on BMI, as indicated by the highest standardized coefficient (β = 0.516), compared to age at menarche (β = − 0.161). Model 2 also demonstrated acceptable collinearity values, with Tolerance (TOL) > 0.98 and Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) ≈ 1.02, indicating no serious multicollinearity and supporting the stability of the model.

Discussion

BMI is a widely used indicator for assessing the nutritional status of adolescents and has been associated with biological, environmental, and sociocultural factors [29]. Among these factors, age at menarche and chronological age have been studied for their potential influence on growth, body composition, and the risk of overweight or obesity [9, 10, 24]. Menarche represents a key milestone in female pubertal development, and its age of onset can be associated with physiological changes that influence BMI. Understanding how these variables relate to one another in specific contexts, such as Peru, is essential for guiding timely interventions in adolescent health. The key findings of the multiple regression model identified both age at menarche and chronological age as significant predictors of BMI. Furthermore, Model 2 demonstrated a better fit and explained 31% of the variability in BMI.

One of the most relevant findings of the present study was the negative effect of age at menarche on BMI, indicating that adolescent girls who experienced earlier menarche tended to have a higher BMI. This result suggests an inverse relationship between age at menarche and nutritional status, aligning with previous literature that reports a consistent association between early menarche and increased metabolic and cardiovascular risk during adolescence and early adulthood [10, 11]. Furthermore, this finding underscores the importance of monitoring pubertal development in public health and school-based programs, as the early identification of early menarche may enable timely interventions aimed at preventing excess weight and its metabolic consequences later in life.

Studies conducted in different geographical contexts have supported this association. For example, a cross-sectional study of Italian adolescents found that those who experienced early menarche had higher rates of weight gain compared to their peers with later menarche [9]. Likewise, a systematic review and meta-analysis of 10 studies involving 13,338 girls and 12,796 boys concluded that precocious puberty is significantly associated with higher BMI in later stages of development [30]. In the Latin American and Caribbean context, research conducted in countries such as Chile, Cuba, and Mexico has also reported similar patterns, highlighting the influence of pubertal development on anthropometric indicators [31, 33].

The mechanisms underlying this relationship may be mediated by both biological and behavioral factors. From a physiological perspective, it has been suggested that earlier pubertal onset is associated with greater accumulation of adipose tissue, particularly in the abdominal region, due to hormonal changes related to increased estrogen levels [32]. Furthermore, early menarche may alter the programming of energy metabolism, promoting greater efficiency in energy storage and hindering the regulation of body weight over time [34]. In behavioral terms, adolescent girls who experience early menarche may be exposed to lifestyle changes such as reduced physical activity, higher caloric intake, and inappropriate eating behaviors, including body dissatisfaction and episodes of binge eating [35], all of which may contribute to an increase in BMI [36]. For example, a recent study found a significant association between early menarche and the onset of eating disorders [37]. Likewise, these adolescents may face greater social and psychological pressures related to body image and early maturation, which can affect their self-perception and promote the adoption of less healthy behaviors [38], potentially leading to increased body weight [39].

Another important finding was the significant positive effect of chronological age on BMI, making it the variable with the greatest impact in the predictive model. This result suggests that as age increases during adolescence, BMI also tends to increase. The relationship between age and BMI in adolescents has been widely documented in the literature. Several studies have reported that BMI tends to increase with age [40], especially among females, due to a combination of hormonal factors, changes in body composition, and behaviors related to diet and physical activity [41, 43]. For example, data from Peru have shown a sustained increase in both overweight and obesity among adolescents aged 10 to 19, in both girls and boys [25]. On the other hand, studies in Colombia have shown that obesity increases with age, beginning in childhood and continuing into adulthood, peaking around the ages of 40 to 50 [44].

From a physiological perspective, this increase in BMI with age can be attributed to the natural developmental processes of adolescence, which include an increase in fat mass in females as part of sexual dimorphism [14, 15]. Furthermore, factors such as a sedentary lifestyle, increased access to ultra-processed foods, and the lack of healthy habits tend to intensify with age in certain contexts, contributing to greater weight gain [19, 20]. Therefore, our findings reinforce the need to establish prevention strategies that take into account the chronological aspect of adolescent development, promoting healthy lifestyles from an early age.

Finally, Model 2, which included both age at menarche and chronological age, demonstrated a better statistical fit compared to Model 1 and explained 31% of the variability in BMI. This finding suggests that both biological and chronological factors jointly contribute to a better understanding of adolescents’ nutritional status, reinforcing the notion that BMI is the result of multiple influences related to pubertal development. An adjusted R² value of 31% indicates that approximately one-third of the variation in BMI can be explained by these two variables, which is considerable given the complexity of the phenomenon and the multitude of factors that influence body weight, such as socioeconomic environment, physical activity levels, dietary habits, and genetics [21, 26, 45]. Although a higher percentage of explained variance was not achieved, this result is consistent with a recent study conducted among Italian adolescents, which found that body mass index is inversely associated with age at menarche. Moreover, BMI measures at both the individual and class levels explained a substantial proportion of the regional variability in age at menarche, with values exceeding 100% in multilevel models [9]. These findings reinforce the notion that variables related to pubertal development and the immediate environment play a significant role in determining nutritional status during adolescence.

The improved performance of Model 2 is also reflected in its lower Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) value, suggesting greater parsimony and model efficiency by balancing goodness of fit with model complexity. This statistical improvement supports the simultaneous inclusion of both variables to better capture individual differences in growth and nutritional status during adolescence. Thus, the model not only provides empirical evidence for the analysis of BMI, but may also serve as a foundation for developing predictive tools or monitoring programs to assess nutritional status in school or community settings.

Finally, our results show that adolescents from the jungle region experienced a significantly earlier age at menarche compared with those from the coast and the highlands. This finding is noteworthy in the context of the Peruvian literature, which has consistently documented a delay in the age at menarche among populations residing in the highlands and at high altitudes. Gonzales et al. [46] reported that the median age at menarche was 13.08 years in Lima (150 m) and 14.33 years in Cerro de Pasco (4,340 m), regardless of socioeconomic status and ethnic background [1]. Similarly, another study by Gonzales and Ortiz [47] found that age at menarche increased with altitude, with mean ages of 13.1 years in Lima, 13.9 years in Cusco (3,400 m), and 14.6 years in Cerro de Pasco. In contrast to these findings, the earlier menarche observed among adolescents from the jungle region in our study may be associated with nutritional and environmental factors specific to this region, as well as with processes of urbanization and dietary transition that promote greater availability of energy-dense foods. This result underscores the importance of considering regional differences within the country and provides novel evidence on the diversity of factors influencing pubertal maturation in the Peruvian adolescent population.

Public health implications

The findings of this study have important public health implications, particularly for the design of preventive and intervention strategies targeting adolescents. The identification of early menarche as a factor associated with higher BMI highlights the need to establish systems for monitoring pubertal development in schools and communities. Such monitoring would allow for the early detection of adolescents at greater risk of becoming overweight or obese, thereby facilitating the timely implementation of interventions focused on promoting healthy lifestyles. Likewise, the significant association between chronological age and increased BMI reinforces the importance of tailoring nutrition education and physical activity strategies to the different stages of adolescent development. These interventions should take into account both the expected physiological changes and the risk behaviors that tend to intensify with age, such as sedentary lifestyles, increased consumption of ultra-processed foods, and low risk perception related to excess weight. Furthermore, the predictive model used in this study could serve as a valuable tool in schools or health centers to identify adolescents at risk of nutritional imbalances, allowing for better allocation of public health resources and efforts. In contexts such as Peru, where significant geographical and socioeconomic inequalities persist, such approaches may help reduce disparities in access to the prevention and treatment of overweight and obesity among adolescents.

Limitations and future research

This study has some limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, a cross-sectional design was used, which prevents the establishment of causal relationships between the variables analyzed. Although significant associations were identified, it cannot be concluded that age or age at menarche directly determine BMI. Second, the sample was non-probabilistic and limited to a specific region of Peru, which restricts the generalizability of the findings to other adolescent populations with different cultural, geographical, or socioeconomic contexts. Furthermore, other relevant variables that may influence BMI—such as physical activity, dietary habits, socioeconomic status, emotional state, and family history of metabolic diseases—were not included in this study. It is recommended that future research employ longitudinal designs to assess the evolution of BMI in relation to age at menarche over time, as well as studies with representative samples from different regions of the country. Likewise, the incorporation of psychosocial, behavioral, and environmental factors into future predictive models could enhance the accuracy of identifying at-risk adolescents and strengthen nutritional and public health intervention strategies. Finally, another limitation of this study is that it did not include any participants under the age of 14 who had already experienced menarche, which may limit comparisons with studies that apply the status quo method to broader age ranges.

Conclusions

The present study demonstrated that both chronological age and age at menarche are significant explanatory factors of BMI in Peruvian adolescents. Specifically, earlier menarche was associated with higher BMI, while increasing age also contributed significantly to BMI increase—chronological age being the variable with the greatest predictive weight. Furthermore, the regression model that included both variables explained a considerable proportion of the variability in BMI, highlighting the importance of simultaneously considering both chronological and biological aspects when evaluating nutritional status during this stage of development. These findings reinforce the need to integrate the monitoring of pubertal development into health prevention and promotion strategies targeting adolescents.
