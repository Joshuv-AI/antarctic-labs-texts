On the stability and conditioning of a fictitious domain formulation for fluid-structure interaction problems
Daniele Boffi, Fabio Credali, Lucia Gastaldi

Abstract
. We consider a distributed Lagrange multiplier formulation for fluid-structure interaction problems in the spirit of the fictitious domain approach. This is an unfitted method, which does not require the construction of meshes conforming to the interface. We focus on the stationary problem arising from the time discretization and we analyze the behavior of the condition number with respect to mesh refinement. At the numerical level, the computation of the term coupling the fluid and the solid mesh requires the knowledge of the intersection between fluid and mapped solid elements and it might happen that a portion of the intersected elements is very small. We show that our formulation is stable independently of such intersections and that the conditioning is not affected by the interface position. Keywords: Fluid-structure interactions, Fictitious domain, Stability, Condition number, Non-matching grids. Mathematics Subject Classification: 65N30, 65N12, 74F10

Introduction

The scientific community has a large interest in developing effective methods for solving partial differential equations modeling multi-physics and multi-bodies problems. Such equations are often defined on time dependent domains characterized by complex interfaces. In our research, we focus on fluid-structure interaction problems, which have several applications in biology, medicine, structural mechanics, and several other fields.

The discretization of these problems is challenging since it requires an accurate representation of the underlying geometry. To this aim, several methods have been developed during the last decades. They can be classified as
fitted
or
unfitted
depending on the fact that the computational mesh is aligned or not with the interface.

Among fitted approaches, the most popular is the Arbitrary Lagrangian–Eulerian (ALE)
[
33
,
34
,
24
]
. Fluid and solid domains are discretized by two meshes evolving around a shared interface. Despite the advantage of having the kinematic constraints imposed by construction, strong mesh distortions may appear.
Unfitted approaches may overcome
this issue
. In this case, fluid and solid are discretized independently, with meshes not fitting at the interface. Thus, the complexity of mesh generation is minimized.
In some cases,
this comes at the price of reducing the accuracy of the scheme unless special techniques are used.
Among unfitted approaches for FSI and multiphysics problems
, we mention, for instance, the level set method
[
38
,
19
]
, the immersed boundary method
[
36
,
37
]
, the fictitious domain approach
[
29
,
28
,
42
]
,
the immersogeometric method

[
35
]
, the shifted boundary method
[
21
]
, the fat boundary method
[
39
]
, and, finally, the Nitsche
method.
Within the family of Nitsche-type methods, we find schemes based on a fictitious domain approach as in
[
16
,
17
]
, the CutFEM
[
40
,
30
,
13
,
15
]
, the Finite Cell
Method
[
22
,
23
,
26
]
and the Nitsche–XFEM
[
1
,
14
]
.

Our approach falls within the class of unfitted methods for FSI. It originated from the finite element immersed boundary method
[
10
]
and further evolved into a fictitious domain method based on the use of a Lagrange multiplier
[
6
,
12
]
.

We consider the simulation of fluid-structure interaction problems which couple an incompressible Newtonian fluid with an incompressible visco-elastic solid body. This setting is motivated by the fact that many biological tissues satisfy this assumption, see e.g.
[
36
]
. Actually, our technique can be extended also to the case of compressible solids as in
[
11
]
. See also
[
31
]
, where the finite element immersed boundary method is generalized to an incompressible Newtonian fluid interacting with general hyper-elastic structures.

The main idea is to fictitiously extend the fluid domain to the region occupied by the immersed structure
so that we solve the Navier–Stokes equations using the Eulerian framework in a fixed domain.
For the solid deformation we use the standard Lagrangian framework and solve the governing elasticity equation on a reference domain mapped, at each time instant, into the
actual configuration.
A distributed Lagrange multiplier is introduced to deal with the kinematic constraint describing the interaction
between fluid and solid at variational level.

The fluid and the solid equations are then coupled by additional
terms depending on the Lagrange multiplier.
In particular, in the fluid equation, this term involves functions defined on the two domains,
hence we refer to it as
coupling term
.

At the computational level, the main advantage of our approach is that we can
use totally independent meshes which do not evolve in time. However, the
computation of the coupling term requires to integrate on the elements of the
reference mesh mapped on the background fluid mesh.
Such computation requires the knowledge of the intersections between the fluid mesh
and the mapped solid one. Hence, very small cells contained in the support of both fluid and mapped solid basis functions may originate. We refer to them as
small cut cells
. Then, the matrix associated with
the coupling term can be evaluated exactly by employing a
quadrature rule on each intersected element. An inexact procedure can
also be considered, using a single quadrature rule on each mapped element.
In this case, it might happen that no quadrature nodes fall inside a
small cut cell.
We estimated the error arising from the use of inexact quadrature
in our recent works
[
8
,
9
]
. In this paper, we discuss the effect of the presence of small cut cells on the stability and conditioning of the considered formulation. On the other hand, in the case of Nitsche-type methods,
the term
cut cell
refers to a mesh element which is cut by the
interface and it might happen that one portion of the cut cell is small. It is
known that this fact can produce instabilities in the solution, as well as ill-conditioning, unless stabilization/penalization terms, such as the ghost penalty technique
[
18
]
, are added to the variational formulation.

In this work, we recall the main features of our distributed Lagrange
multiplier formulation and we prove upper bounds for the condition number, depending only on the choice of the coupling term and not on the integration technique or
the interface position.
On the other hand, the inf-sup stability is guaranteed at the discrete
level without the need for any artificial penalization term dealing with
small cut cells.

Our theoretical results on the stability and conditioning are confirmed by
extensive numerical investigations.

A final numerical test involving a time dependent problem shows that the method is unconditionally stable in time, independently of the presence of small cut cells.

4

3
and
4
are essentially known. We summarized them here since we need them for our analysis of Section
5

Let

\|\cdot\|_{0,D}

2.
Problem setting

We focus on fluid-structure interaction problems consisting of an elastic body immersed in a Newtonian fluid. The configuration of the system is described by two regions
\Omega^{s}_{t}
and
\Omega^{f}_{t}
representing the position of solid and fluid at time
t
, respectively,
and sharing a common interface
\partial\Omega^{s}_{t}\cap\partial\Omega^{f}_{t}
. More precisely, both
\Omega^{s}_{t}
and
\Omega^{f}_{t}
can be either two- or three-dimensional domains. We point out that our approach can also deal with codimension one (see e.g.
[
6
,
4
]
) and codimension two solids (see e.g.
[
3
,
32
]
). We then introduce a third domain
\Omega=\Omega^{s}_{t}\cup\Omega^{f}_{t}
, acting as a container for the system and independent of time. We assume
that the solid domain
\Omega^{s}_{t}
is totally immersed in the fluid one, so that its closure does not touch the boundary of the container
\partial\Omega
. Therefore, the interface
\partial\Omega^{s}_{t}\cap\partial\Omega^{f}_{t}
coincides with
\partial\Omega^{s}_{t}
.

The fluid is described in the Eulerian framework, whereas the solid deformation is described in the Lagrangian setting. Hence, we introduce a solid reference domain
\mathcal{B}
; at each time instant,
\mathcal{B}
is mapped into the actual position of the body
\Omega^{s}_{t}
through the action of a map
\mathbf{X}:\mathcal{B}\rightarrow\Omega^{s}_{t}
. Thus,
\mathbf{X}
represents the position of the solid and each point
\mathbf{x}\in\Omega^{s}_{t}
can be expressed in terms of
\mathbf{s}
as
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{s},t)
.

We denote by
\mathbb{F}
the deformation gradient
\grads\mathbf{X}
, and
|\mathbb{F}|
its determinant. In case of incompressible materials,
|\mathbb{F}|
is constant during time. In particular,
|\mathbb{F}|=1
when the reference domain
\mathcal{B}
coincides with the initial configuration of the immersed body. In addition, the time derivative
\partial\mathbf{X}/\partial t
is equal to the material velocity
\mathbf{u}^{s}
of the solid, i.e.

(1)

\frac{\partial\mathbf{X}}{\partial t}(\mathbf{s},t)=\mathbf{u}^{s}(\mathbf{x},t)\qquad\text{for }\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{s},t).

.

Assuming that both fluid and solid materials are incompressible, the equations governing the evolution of our system are

(2)

\displaystyle\rho_{f}\bigg(\frac{\partial\mathbf{u}^{f}}{\partial t}+\mathbf{u}^{f}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}^{f}\bigg)=\div\bm{\sigma}^{f}

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega^{f}_{t}

\displaystyle\div\mathbf{u}^{f}=0

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega^{f}_{t}

\displaystyle\rho_{s}\frac{\partial^{2}\mathbf{X}}{\partial^{2}t}=\div_{\mathbf{s}}\mathbb{T}

\displaystyle\text{in }\mathcal{B}

\displaystyle|\mathbb{F}|=1

\displaystyle\text{in }\mathcal{B}.

Here,
\mathbf{u}^{f}
is the fluid velocity, while
\rho_{f},\,\rho_{s}
are fluid and solid densities. The symbols
\bm{\sigma}_{f}
and
\mathbb{T}
denote the Cauchy stress tensors for fluid and solid, respectively. More precisely,
\bm{\sigma}^{f}
is given by

\bm{\sigma}^{f}=-p^{f}\mathbb{I}+\nu_{f}\Grads(\mathbf{u}^{f}),

where
\nu_{f}>0
is the fluid viscosity,
p^{f}
the pressure, and
\Grads(\mathbf{u}^{f})=(\Grad\mathbf{u}^{f}+(\Grad\mathbf{u}^{f})^{\top})/2
is the symmetric gradient.

We consider a visco-elastic solid material; the associated Cauchy stress tensor is composed of two terms

(3)

\mathbb{T}=|\mathbb{F}|\bm{\sigma}_{v}^{s}\mathbb{F}^{-\top}+\mathbb{P}\qquad\text{with }\bm{\sigma}_{v}^{s}=-p^{s}\mathbb{I}+\nu_{s}\Grads(\mathbf{u}^{s}),

where
\bm{\sigma}_{v}^{s}
represents the viscous contribution, while
\mathbf{u}^{s}
,
p^{s}
are velocity and pressure of the solid, respectively, and
\nu_{s}>0
denotes the viscosity. The pressure
p^{s}
is the Lagrange multiplier associated to the incompressibility condition.
\mathbb{P}
is the first Piola–Kirchhoff stress tensor, which can be expressed in terms of a potential energy density
W(\mathbb{F},\mathbf{s},t)
as
\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{F},\mathbf{s},t)=\displaystyle\frac{\partial W}{\partial\mathbb{F}}(\mathbb{F},\mathbf{s},t)
.

The equations in (
2
) are completed by suitable interface, boundary, and initial conditions. Velocity and Cauchy stress are imposed to be continuous at the interface by the transmission conditions

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{f}=\mathbf{u}^{s}

\displaystyle\text{ on }\partial\Omega^{s}_{t}

\displaystyle\bm{\sigma}_{f}\mathbf{n}_{f}=-\big(|\mathbb{F}|^{-1}\mathbb{T}\mathbb{F}^{\top})\mathbf{n}_{s}

\displaystyle\text{ on }\partial\Omega^{s}_{t},

where
\mathbf{n}_{s}
and
\mathbf{n}_{f}
are the outer unit normal vectors to
\Omega^{s}_{t}
and
\Omega^{f}_{t}
, respectively. We finally add homogeneous boundary condition for the fluid velocity
\mathbf{u}^{f}=0
on
\partial\Omega
and the initial conditions

\mathbf{u}^{f}(0)=\mathbf{u}_{0}^{f}\ \text{ in }\Omega^{f}_{0},\qquad\mathbf{u}^{s}(0)=\mathbf{u}_{0}^{s}\ \text{ in }\Omega^{s}_{0},\qquad\mathbf{X}(0)=\mathbf{X}_{0}\ \text{ in }\mathcal{B}.

In order to present the fictitious domain formulation of the above equations, we extend the fluid variables to the entire
\Omega
incorporating the solid domain

(4)

\mathbf{u}=\begin{cases}\mathbf{u}^{f}\quad\text{ in }\Omega^{f}_{t}\\
\mathbf{u}^{s}\quad\text{ in }\Omega^{s}_{t},\end{cases}\qquad p=\begin{cases}p^{f}\quad\text{ in }\Omega^{f}_{t}\\
p^{s}\quad\text{ in }\Omega^{s}_{t}.\end{cases}

Since
\mathbf{u}
must coincide with
\mathbf{u}^{s}
in the solid region
\Omega^{s}_{t}
, we impose the following kinematic constraint in
\mathcal{B}
corresponding to (
1
)

(5)

\frac{\partial\mathbf{X}}{\partial t}(\mathbf{s},t)=\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{s},t),t)\quad\text{ for }\mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{B}.

We enforce (
5
) weakly by means of a distributed Lagrange multiplier. To this aim, we consider a suitable Hilbert space
{\bm{\Lambda}}
and we introduce a continuous bilinear form
\mathbf{c}:{\bm{\Lambda}}\times\mathbf{H}^{1}(\mathcal{B})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with the property

(6)

\mathbf{c}(\bm{\mu},\mathbf{Z})=0\quad\forall\bm{\mu}\in{\bm{\Lambda}}\quad\text{implies}\quad\mathbf{Z}=0.

Two possible choices of
{\bm{\Lambda}}
and
\mathbf{c}
are used in our model. We can set
{\bm{\Lambda}}_{0}=(\mathbf{H}^{1}(\mathcal{B}))^{\prime}
, i.e. the dual space of
\mathbf{H}^{1}(\mathcal{B})
, and define
\mathbf{c}_{0}
as the duality pairing

(7)

\mathbf{c}_{0}(\bm{\mu},\mathbf{Y})=\langle\bm{\mu},\mathbf{Y}\rangle\quad\forall\bm{\mu}\in{\bm{\Lambda}}_{0},\;\forall\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}(\mathcal{B}),

or we can choose
{\bm{\Lambda}}_{1}=\mathbf{H}^{1}(\mathcal{B})
and set
\mathbf{c}_{1}
as the inner product in
\mathbf{H}^{1}(\mathcal{B})

(8)

\mathbf{c}_{1}(\bm{\mu},\mathbf{Y})=(\bm{\mu},\mathbf{Y})_{\mathcal{B}}+(\grads\bm{\mu},\grads\mathbf{Y})_{\mathcal{B}}\quad\forall\bm{\mu}\in{\bm{\Lambda}}_{1},\,\forall\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}(\mathcal{B}).

From now on, we will use the generic notation
({\bm{\Lambda}},\mathbf{c})
and we specify

({\bm{\Lambda}}_{0},\mathbf{c}_{0})
or
({\bm{\Lambda}}_{1},\mathbf{c}_{1})
when necessary.
Finally, the weak formulation of our problem reads as follows (see
[
6
]
for its full derivation).

Problem 1
.

Given
\mathbf{u}_{0}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
and
\mathbf{X}_{0}:\mathcal{B}\longrightarrow\Omega
,
\forall t\in(0,T)
, find
(\mathbf{u}(t),p(t))\in\mathbf{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\times L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)
,
\mathbf{X}(t)\in\mathbf{W}^{1,\infty}(\mathcal{B})
and
\bm{\lambda}(t)\in{\bm{\Lambda}}
, such that for almost every
t\in(0,T)
it holds

\displaystyle\rho_{f}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\mathbf{u}(t),\mathbf{v})+b(\mathbf{u}(t),\mathbf{u}(t),\mathbf{v})+a(\mathbf{u}(t),\mathbf{v})

(9a)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad-(\div\mathbf{v},p(t))+\mathbf{c}(\bm{\lambda}(t),\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{X}(t)))=0

\displaystyle\forall\mathbf{v}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)

(9b)

\displaystyle(\div\mathbf{u}(t),q)=0

\displaystyle\forall q\in L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)

(9c)

\displaystyle\delta\rho\bigg(\frac{\partial^{2}\mathbf{X}}{\partial t^{2}},\mathbf{Y}\bigg)_{\mathcal{B}}+(\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{F}(\mathbf{s},t)),\grads\mathbf{Y})_{\mathcal{B}}-\mathbf{c}(\bm{\lambda}(t),\mathbf{Y})=0

\displaystyle\forall\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}(\mathcal{B})

(9d)

\displaystyle\mathbf{c}\bigg(\bm{\mu},\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{X}(\cdot,t),t)-\frac{\partial\mathbf{X}}{\partial t}(t)\bigg)=0

\displaystyle\forall\bm{\mu}\in{\bm{\Lambda}}

(9e)

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x},0)=\mathbf{u}_{0}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\forall\mathbf{x}\in\Omega

(9f)

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{s},0)=\mathbf{X}_{0}(\mathbf{s})

\displaystyle\forall\mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{B}.

Here, we adopted the following notation:
\delta\rho=\rho_{s}-\rho_{f}
and

a(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})=\int_{\Omega}\nu\Grads(\mathbf{u}):\Grads(\mathbf{v})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x},\qquad b(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w})=\frac{\rho_{f}}{2}((\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w})-(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{w},\mathbf{v})).

The extended viscosity
\nu
is equal to
\nu_{f}
in
\Omega^{f}_{t}
and
\nu_{s}
in
\Omega^{s}_{t}
. For simplicity, in the remainder of the paper, we neglect the non-linear convective term
b(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w})
.

Remark 1
.

By exploiting the incompressibility of both the fluid and solid materials, it is straightforward to impose the divergence free condition to the extended velocity. Notice that, in this case, the variable
p
represents the Lagrange multiplier associated to such constraint, as usual for the Navier–Stokes equations. This might be considered as a limitation of our method. However, it is possible to extend our fictitious domain approach to the case of compressible solids. This is done for instance in
[
11
]
, where a fictitious pressure field without any physical meaning is introduced in the solid domain, and it is weakly imposed to be zero.
Moreover, in our analysis we strongly rely on the assumption that the solid material has a viscous component. The method can be generalized to hyper-elastic structures as done in
[
31
]
, where the authors take due account of the additional viscous term that is fictitiously added to the extended fluid equation.

3.
Time discretization and saddle point problem

In this section, we introduce the time semi-discretization of Problem
1
. We consider a positive integer
N
and we subdivide the time domain
[0,T]
into
N
equal sub-intervals of size
\Delta t
. For
n=0,\dots,N
, we set
t_{n}=n\Delta t
. We consider a backward approximation of time derivatives, i.e. given the generic function
f

(10)

\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}(t_{n+1})\approx\frac{f^{n+1}-f^{n}}{\Delta t},\qquad\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial t^{2}}(t_{n+1})\approx\frac{f^{n+1}-2f^{n}+f^{n-1}}{\Delta t^{2}},

where
f^{n}=f(t_{n})
.

The time semi-discretization of Problem
1
is the following.

Problem 2
.

Given
\mathbf{u}_{0}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
and
\mathbf{X}_{0}\in\mathbf{W}^{1,\infty}(\mathcal{B})
, for
n=1,\dots,N
find
(\mathbf{u}^{n},p^{n})\in\mathbf{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\times L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)
,
\mathbf{X}^{n}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}(\mathcal{B})
, and
\bm{\lambda}^{n}\in{\bm{\Lambda}}
, such that

\displaystyle\rho_{f}\bigg(\frac{\mathbf{u}^{n+1}-\mathbf{u}^{n}}{\Delta t},\mathbf{v}\bigg)+a(\mathbf{u}^{n+1},\mathbf{v})

(11a)

\displaystyle\hskip 76.82234pt-(\div\mathbf{v},p^{n+1})+\mathbf{c}(\bm{\lambda}^{n+1},\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{X}^{n}))=0

\displaystyle\forall\mathbf{v}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)

(11b)

\displaystyle(\div\mathbf{u}^{n+1},q)=0

\displaystyle\forall q\in L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\delta\rho\bigg(\frac{\mathbf{X}^{n+1}-2\mathbf{X}^{n}+\mathbf{X}^{n-1}}{\Delta t^{2}},\mathbf{Y}\bigg)_{\mathcal{B}}+(\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{F}^{n+1}),\grads\mathbf{Y})_{\mathcal{B}}

(11c)

\displaystyle\hskip 165.02606pt-\mathbf{c}(\bm{\lambda}^{n+1},\mathbf{Y})=0

\displaystyle\forall\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}(\mathcal{B})

(11d)

\displaystyle\mathbf{c}\bigg(\bm{\mu},\mathbf{u}^{n+1}(\mathbf{X}^{n})-\frac{\mathbf{X}^{n+1}-\mathbf{X}^{n}}{\Delta t}\bigg)=0

\displaystyle\forall\bm{\mu}\in{\bm{\Lambda}}

The coupling terms are treated in a semi-implicit way by taking into account the position of the solid body at the previous time instant as in
\mathbf{c}(\bm{\lambda}^{n+1},\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{X}^{n}))
and
\mathbf{c}(\bm{\mu},\mathbf{u}^{n+1}(\mathbf{X}^{n}))
. Moreover, the term
\mathbf{X}^{-1}
can be computed by solving the following equation

\frac{\mathbf{X}^{0}-\mathbf{X}^{-1}}{\Delta t}=\mathbf{u}_{0}^{s}\quad\text{ in }\mathcal{B}.

We recall that the method is unconditionally stable, without any restriction on the choice of time step
\Delta t
, see
[
6
, Prop. 3]
.

The semi-discrete Problem
2
can be interpreted as a sequence of stationary saddle point problems, see
[
12
]
. Before presenting such formulation, we assume that the solid material is described by a linear constitutive law, hence we set
\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{F})=\kappa\mathbb{F}
. At a fixed time instant, unknowns and physical constants can be rearranged as

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{X}}=\mathbf{X}^{n},\quad\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{u}^{n+1},\quad p=p^{n+1},\quad\mathbf{X}=\frac{\mathbf{X}^{n+1}}{\Delta t},\quad\bm{\lambda}=\bm{\lambda}^{n+1},

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}=\frac{\rho_{f}}{\Delta t}\mathbf{u}^{n},\quad\mathbf{g}=\frac{\delta\rho}{\Delta t^{2}}(2\mathbf{X}^{n}-\mathbf{X}^{n-1}),\quad\mathbf{d}=\frac{1}{\Delta t}\mathbf{X}^{n},\quad\alpha=\frac{\rho_{f}}{\Delta t},\quad\beta=\frac{\delta\rho}{\Delta t},\quad\gamma=\kappa\Delta t,

so that we obtain the following saddle point problem.

Problem 3
.

Let
\overline{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathbf{W}^{1,\infty}(\mathcal{B})
be invertible with Lipschitz inverse. Given

\mathbf{f}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)
,
\mathbf{g}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(\mathcal{B})
, and
\mathbf{d}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}(\mathcal{B})
, find
(\mathbf{u},p)\in\mathbf{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\times L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)
,

\mathbf{X}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}(\mathcal{B})
, and
\bm{\lambda}\in{\bm{\Lambda}}
such that

(12a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}_{f}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})-(\div\mathbf{v},p)_{\Omega}+\mathbf{c}(\bm{\lambda},\mathbf{v}(\overline{\mathbf{X}}))=(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{v})_{\Omega}

\displaystyle\forall\mathbf{v}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)

(12b)

\displaystyle(\div\mathbf{u},q)_{\Omega}=0

\displaystyle\forall q\in L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)

(12c)

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}_{s}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})-\mathbf{c}(\bm{\lambda},\mathbf{Y})=(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{Y})_{\mathcal{B}}

\displaystyle\forall\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}(\mathcal{B})

(12d)

\displaystyle\mathbf{c}(\bm{\mu},\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{u}(\overline{\mathbf{X}}))=\mathbf{c}(\bm{\mu},\mathbf{d})

\displaystyle\forall\bm{\mu}\in{\bm{\Lambda}}

where

(13)

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}_{f}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})=\alpha(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})_{\Omega}+(\nu\Grads(\mathbf{u}),\Grads(\mathbf{v}))_{\Omega}

\displaystyle\forall\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}_{s}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})=\beta(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})_{\mathcal{B}}+\gamma(\grads\mathbf{X},\grads\mathbf{Y})_{\mathcal{B}}

\displaystyle\forall\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}(\mathcal{B}).

3.1.
Known theoretical results on continuous and discrete problems

In this section, we recall the theoretical results regarding Problem
3
and its finite element discretization. We also introduce the notation used in the rest of the paper. We first define the product space
\mathbb{V}=\mathbf{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\times\mathbf{H}^{1}(\mathcal{B})\times{\bm{\Lambda}}\times L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)
and the norm

(14)

\|\mathcal{V}\|_{\mathbb{V}}^{2}=\left\lVert\mathbf{v}\right\rVert^{2}_{1,\Omega}+\left\lVert\mathbf{Y}\right\rVert^{2}_{1,\mathcal{B}}+\left\lVert\bm{\mu}\right\rVert^{2}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}+\|q\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}.

The tuple
\mathcal{V}=(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{Y},\bm{\mu},q)
is a generic element of
\mathbb{V}
. For a fixed
\overline{\mathbf{X}}
, we introduce the bilinear form
\mathcal{L}:\mathbb{V}\times\mathbb{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined as

(15)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{V};\overline{\mathbf{X}})=

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}_{f}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})+\mathbf{a}_{s}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})

\displaystyle+\mathbf{c}(\bm{\mu},\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{u}(\overline{\mathbf{X}}))-\mathbf{c}(\bm{\lambda},\mathbf{Y}-\mathbf{v}(\overline{\mathbf{X}}))

\displaystyle-(\div\mathbf{v},p)_{\Omega}+(\div\mathbf{u},q)_{\Omega},

so that we can rewrite Problem

as follows.

Problem 4
.

Let
\overline{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathbf{W}^{1,\infty}(\mathcal{B})
be invertible with Lipschitz inverse, given
\mathbf{f}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)
,
\mathbf{g}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(\mathcal{B})
and
\mathbf{d}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}(\mathcal{B})
, find
\mathcal{U}\in\mathbb{V}
such that

(16)

\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{V};\overline{\mathbf{X}})=(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{v})_{\Omega}+(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{Y})_{\mathcal{B}}+\mathbf{c}(\bm{\mu},\mathbf{d})\qquad\forall\mathcal{V}\in\mathbb{V}.

Combining, in a classical way, the well-posedness theory presented in
[
12
]
with the results in
[
41
]
,
\mathcal{L}
satisfies the following inf-sup condition.

Proposition 1
.

There exists a positive constant
\eta
such that

\inf_{\mathcal{U}\in\mathbb{V}}\sup_{\mathcal{V}\in\mathbb{V}}\frac{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{V};\overline{\mathbf{X}})}{\|\mathcal{U}\|_{\mathbb{V}}\|\mathcal{V}\|_{\mathbb{V}}}\geq\eta.

In order to introduce the finite element discretization of
Problem
4
, we consider two meshes
\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\Omega}

and
\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\mathcal{B}}
decomposing
\Omega
and
\mathcal{B}
, respectively, into triangles.
We denote by
h_{\Omega}
and
h_{\mathcal{B}}

the mesh sizes of
\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\Omega}
and
\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\mathcal{B}}
, respectively.
For fluid velocity and pressure, we choose a pair of discrete spaces

(\mathbf{V}_{h},Q_{h})\subset\mathbf{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\times L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)
satisfying the
discrete inf-sup condition for the Stokes problem. For the solid unknowns,
we consider
\mathbf{S}_{h}\subset\mathbf{H}^{1}(\mathcal{B})
and
{\bm{\Lambda}}_{h}\subset{\bm{\Lambda}}
. For simplicity, we
assume that
\mathbf{S}_{h}={\bm{\Lambda}}_{h}
even if more general cases have been studied
(see
[
2
]
). From now on, we set

(17)

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{h}

\displaystyle=\{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega):\mathbf{v}_{\mid\mathrm{T}}\in[\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathrm{T})]^{2}\quad\forall\mathrm{T}\in\mathcal{T}_{h/2}^{\Omega}\}

\displaystyle Q_{h}

\displaystyle=\{q\in L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)\cap H^{1}(\Omega):q_{\mid\mathrm{T}}\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathrm{T})\quad\forall\mathrm{T}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\Omega}\}

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}_{h}

\displaystyle=\{\mathbf{w}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}(\mathcal{B}):\mathbf{w}_{\mid\mathrm{T}}\in[\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathrm{T})]^{2}\quad\forall\mathrm{T}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\mathcal{B}}\}

\displaystyle{\bm{\Lambda}}_{h}

\displaystyle=\{\bm{\mu}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}(\mathcal{B}):\bm{\mu}_{\mid\mathrm{T}}\in[\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathrm{T})]^{2}\quad\forall\mathrm{T}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\mathcal{B}}\}.

The pair of Stokes spaces
(\mathbf{V}_{h},Q_{h})
corresponds to the Bercovier–Pironneau element introduced in
[
5
]
, also known as
\mathcal{P}_{1}
-iso-
\mathcal{P}_{2}/\mathcal{P}_{1}
. The velocity mesh
\mathcal{T}_{h/2}^{\Omega}
is a refinement of
\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\Omega}
obtained by partitioning each
\mathrm{T}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\Omega}
into four sub triangles by connecting the three mid points of the edges of
\mathrm{T}
.

We make the following assumption on the considered meshes.

Assumption 1
.

We assume that
\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\Omega}
and
\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\mathcal{B}}
are quasi-uniform meshes with size
h_{\Omega},h_{\mathcal{B}}<1
.

Notice that, when
\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{c}_{0}
and if
\bm{\mu}\in\mathbf{L}^{1}_{loc}(\mathcal{B})
, the duality pairing in
\mathbf{H}^{1}(\mathcal{B})
can be identified with the scalar product in
\mathbf{L}^{2}(\mathcal{B})
, so that we set

(18)

\mathbf{c}_{0}(\bm{\mu}_{h},\mathbf{Y}_{h})=(\bm{\mu}_{h},\mathbf{Y}_{h})_{\mathcal{B}}\quad\forall\bm{\mu}_{h}\in{\bm{\Lambda}}_{h},\forall\mathbf{Y}_{h}\in\mathbf{S}_{h}.

Similarly to the continuous case, we now introduce the discrete product space
\mathbb{V}_{h}=\mathbf{V}_{h}\times\mathbf{S}_{h}\times{\bm{\Lambda}}_{h}\times Q_{h}
, so that the discrete counterpart of Problem
4
reads as follows.

Problem 5
.

Let
\overline{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathbf{W}^{1,\infty}(\mathcal{B})
be invertible with Lipschitz inverse, given
\mathbf{f}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)
,
\mathbf{g}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(\mathcal{B})
and
\mathbf{d}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}(\mathcal{B})
, find
\mathcal{U}_{h}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}
such that

(19)

\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{U}_{h},\mathcal{V}_{h};\overline{\mathbf{X}})=(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{v}_{h})_{\Omega}+(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{Y}_{h})_{\mathcal{B}}-\mathbf{c}(\bm{\mu}_{h},\mathbf{d})\qquad\forall\mathcal{V}_{h}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}.

This discrete problem is well-posed thanks to the theory discussed
in
[
12
]
combined with
[
41
]
. The following proposition
holds
true independently of how the fluid and the mapped solid meshes
intersect each other.

Proposition 2
.

There exists a positive constant
\tilde{\eta}
, independent of
h
, such that

\inf_{\mathcal{U}_{h}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}}\sup_{\mathcal{V}_{h}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}}\frac{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{U}_{h},\mathcal{V}_{h};\overline{\mathbf{X}})}{\|\mathcal{U}_{h}\|_{\mathbb{V}}\|\mathcal{V}_{h}\|_{\mathbb{V}}}\geq\tilde{\eta}.

Therefore, the convergence theorem directly follows.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\mathbf{V}_{h}
and
Q_{h}
satisfy the usual compatibility
conditions for the Stokes problem. If
\mathcal{U}=(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{X},\bm{\lambda},p)
and

\mathcal{U}_{h}=(\mathbf{u}_{h},\mathbf{X}_{h},\bm{\lambda}_{h},p_{h})
denote the solution for the
continuous and the discrete problem, respectively, then the following error estimate holds true

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}_{h}\right\rVert_{1,\Omega}

\displaystyle+\left\lVert p-p_{h}\right\rVert_{0,\Omega}+\left\lVert\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{X}_{h}\right\rVert_{1,\mathcal{B}}+\left\lVert\bm{\lambda}-\bm{\lambda}_{h}\right\rVert_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}

\displaystyle\leq C\big(\inf_{\mathbf{v}_{h}\in\mathbf{V}_{h}}\left\lVert\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}_{h}\right\rVert_{1,\Omega}+\inf_{q_{h}\in Q_{h}}\left\lVert p-q_{h}\right\rVert_{0,\Omega}+\inf_{\mathbf{Y}_{h}\in\mathbf{S}_{h}}\left\lVert\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{Y}_{h}\right\rVert_{1,\mathcal{B}}+\inf_{\bm{\mu}_{h}\in{\bm{\Lambda}}_{h}}\left\lVert\bm{\lambda}-\bm{\mu}_{h}\right\rVert_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}\big).

4.
Computational aspects regarding the coupling term

In the previous sections, we recalled the main results regarding stability and well-posedness of continuous and discrete problems. We now focus on the coupling term, which needs particular care during the assembly phase. To begin with, we rewrite Problem
5
in algebraic form as

(20)

\mathcal{A}\,\mathcal{U}_{h}=\mathcal{F}

where

(21)

\mathcal{A}=\left[\begin{array}[]{@{}cccc@{}}\mathsf{A}_{f}&0&\mathsf{C}_{f}^{\top}&-\mathsf{B}_{f}^{\top}\\
0&\mathsf{A}_{s}&-\mathsf{C}_{s}^{\top}&0\\
-\mathsf{C}_{f}&\mathsf{C}_{s}&0&0\\
\mathsf{B}_{f}&0&0&0\\
\end{array}\right],\qquad\mathcal{F}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{f}\\
\mathbf{g}\\
\mathbf{d}\\
\mathbf{0}\end{array}\right],

and by abuse of notation
\mathcal{U}_{h}
denotes the vector associated to the
unknowns.
Except for
\mathsf{C}_{f}
, each sub-matrix of
\mathcal{A}
can be assembled exactly provided that a sufficiently precise quadrature rule is employed. Indeed, while
\mathsf{A}_{f}
and
\mathsf{B}_{f}
are constructed on the fluid mesh
\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\Omega}
and
\mathsf{A}_{s}
,
\mathsf{C}_{s}
are defined on the solid mesh
\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\mathcal{B}}
, the interface matrix
\mathsf{C}_{f}
combines the behavior of fluid and solid in the fictitious region of the fluid domain.

\mathsf{C}_{f}
is the discrete counterpart of
\mathbf{c}(\bm{\mu}_{h},\mathbf{v}_{h}(\overline{\mathbf{X}}))
, which involves the computation of integrals on
\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\mathcal{B}}
of
\bm{\mu}_{h}\in{\bm{\Lambda}}_{h}
and
\mathbf{v}_{h}\in\mathbf{V}_{h}
, i.e. of functions defined on two independent meshes. More precisely,

(22a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{c}_{0}(\bm{\mu}_{h},\mathbf{v}_{h}(\overline{\mathbf{X}}))=\sum_{\mathrm{T}_{\mathrm{s}}\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{B}}_{h}}\int_{\mathrm{T}_{\mathrm{s}}}\bm{\mu}_{h}\cdot\mathbf{v}_{h}(\overline{\mathbf{X}})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{s},

(22b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{c}_{1}(\bm{\mu}_{h},\mathbf{v}_{h}(\overline{\mathbf{X}}))=\sum_{\mathrm{T}_{\mathrm{s}}\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{B}}_{h}}\int_{\mathrm{T}_{\mathrm{s}}}\left(\bm{\mu}_{h}\cdot\mathbf{v}_{h}(\overline{\mathbf{X}})+\grads\bm{\mu}_{h}:\grads\mathbf{v}_{h}(\overline{\mathbf{X}})\right)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{s}.

Exact integration
Inexact integration

Mapping of a solid element
\mathrm{T}_{s}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\mathcal{B}}
into the fluid mesh
T_{h/2}^{\Omega}
showing the support mismatch of fluid basis function (yellow) with the immersed solid element and quadrature nodes for inexact integration.

The composition of
\mathbf{v}_{h}
with the map
\overline{\mathbf{X}}
takes care of the

\mathcal{T}_{h/2}^{\Omega}

between the mapped solid element and the support of fluid basis functions.
An exhaustive discussion regarding the possible techniques for computing
the integrals in (

[
8

.
The exact computation of the integrals can be done with the help of a composite quadrature
rule defined on the intersection between the fluid and the mapped solid
mesh (see purple lines in Figure
2
)
which takes into consideration that
\mathbf{v}_{h}(\overline{\mathbf{X}})
is a piecewise
polynomial in each element
\mathrm{T}_{\mathrm{s}}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\mathcal{B}}
.

To this aim, we introduce a triangulation of the mapped elements by subdividing each intersection polygon into triangles, then we apply a suitable quadrature rule exact for the degree of the involved polynomials.
This “new mesh” is thus an implementation tool and it does not appear in the formulation of the problem. We emphasize that the well-posedness of Problem
5
is completely independent of
h
(see Proposition
2
). Therefore, the problem is naturally stable without the need of any artificial penalization term dealing with small cut cells.

An alternative procedure consists in approximating the above integrals
without taking into account that
\mathbf{v}_{h}(\overline{\mathbf{X}})
is piecewise polynomial in

\mathrm{T}_{s}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\mathcal{B}}
. In the rightmost picture of Figure
2
, we show quadrature nodes of an inexact quadrature rule of order two. Given a generic
triangle, we denote by
\{(\mathbf{p}_{k}^{0},\omega_{k}^{0})\}_{k=1}^{K_{0}}

and
\{(\mathbf{p}_{k}^{1},\omega_{k}^{1})\}_{k=1}^{K_{1}}
quadrature rules for
the
\mathbf{L}^{2}(\mathcal{B})
scalar product and the
\mathbf{L}^{2}(\mathcal{B})
scalar product of gradients,
respectively. In this case, the expressions in (

(23a)

\mathbf{c}_{0,h}(\bm{\mu}_{h},\mathbf{v}_{h}(\overline{\mathbf{X}}))=\sum_{\mathrm{T}_{\mathrm{s}}\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{B}}_{h}}\left\lvert\mathrm{T}_{\mathrm{s}}\right\rvert\sum_{k=1}^{K_{0}}\omega_{k}^{0}\,\bm{\mu}_{h}(\mathbf{p}_{k}^{0})\cdot\mathbf{v}_{h}(\overline{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{p}_{k}^{0})),

and

(23b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{c}_{1,h}(\bm{\mu}_{h},\mathbf{v}_{h}(\overline{\mathbf{X}}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathrm{T}_{\mathrm{s}}\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{B}}_{h}}\left\lvert\mathrm{T}_{\mathrm{s}}\right\rvert\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K_{0}}\omega_{k}^{0}\,\bm{\mu}_{h}(\mathbf{p}_{k}^{0})\cdot\mathbf{v}_{h}(\overline{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{p}_{k}^{0}))+\sum_{k=1}^{K_{1}}\omega_{k}^{1}\,\grads\bm{\mu}_{h}(\mathbf{p}_{k}^{1}):\grads\mathbf{v}_{h}(\overline{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{p}_{k}^{1}))\right).

It is clear that, if the coupling term is assembled with the formulas given
in (

been addressed in
[
9

. We report here the associated
quadrature error estimates.

Taking into account the finite element spaces we are using, we make the
following assumption.

Assumption 2
.

We assume that the quadrature rule

\{(\mathbf{p}_{k}^{0},\omega_{k}^{0})\}_{k=1}^{K_{0}}
is exact for quadratic
polynomials, whereas
\{(\mathbf{p}_{k}^{1},\omega_{k}^{1})\}_{k=1}^{K_{1}}
is exact
for constants.

The quadrature errors can be bounded in terms of the mesh sizes as
follows:

(24)

\displaystyle\left\lvert\mathbf{c}_{0}(\bm{\mu}_{h},\mathbf{v}_{h}(\overline{\mathbf{X}}))-\mathbf{c}_{0,h}(\bm{\mu}_{h},\mathbf{v}_{h}(\overline{\mathbf{X}}))\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq Ch_{\mathcal{B}}^{1/2}|\log h_{\mathcal{B}}|\left\lVert\bm{\mu}_{h}\right\rVert_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}\left\lVert\mathbf{v}_{h}\right\rVert_{1,\Omega}

(25)

\left\lvert\mathbf{c}_{1}(\bm{\mu}_{h},\mathbf{v}_{h}(\overline{\mathbf{X}}))-\mathbf{c}_{1,h}(\bm{\mu}_{h},\mathbf{v}_{h}(\overline{\mathbf{X}}))\right\rvert\leq C\bigg(h_{\mathcal{B}}^{1/2}|\log h_{\mathcal{B}}|+\frac{h_{\mathcal{B}}}{h_{\Omega}}\bigg)\left\lVert\bm{\mu}_{h}\right\rVert_{1,\mathcal{B}}\left\lVert\mathbf{v}_{h}\right\rVert_{1,\Omega}.

As we did in the continuous setting, we adopt the generic notation
\mathbf{c}_{h}
for
the inexact construction of both choices of coupling term.

5.
Estimate of the condition number

In this section, we study the condition number of the matrices
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{A}_{h}
associated with Problem
5
and Problem
6
, respectively.

A general framework for studying the condition number of linear systems arising from finite element approximations was presented by Ern and Guermond in
[
25
]
. The Euclidean condition number of our system behaves as described by the following theorem, where the notation
\mathcal{A}_{\square}
indicates both
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{A}_{h}
, as well as
\mathcal{L}_{\square}
stands for both
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{L}_{h}
.

Theorem 4
.

Let
C_{\star},\,C^{\star}
be two positive constants, independent of the mesh sizes, arising from the equivalence between the
L^{2}
norm of a finite element function and the Euclidean norm of the corresponding vector of degrees of freedom (see
[
25
, Sect. 2.3]
). Then, the following bounds hold

(30)

C_{\star}\frac{\omega}{\tau}\leq\mathsf{cond}(\mathcal{A}_{\square})\leq C^{\star}\frac{\omega}{\tau},

where

(31)

\omega=\sup_{\mathcal{U}_{h}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}}\sup_{\mathcal{V}_{h}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}}\frac{\mathcal{L}_{\square}(\mathcal{U}_{h},\mathcal{V}_{h};\overline{\mathbf{X}})}{\|\mathcal{U}_{h}\|_{0}\|\mathcal{V}_{h}\|_{0}},\qquad\tau=\inf_{\mathcal{U}_{h}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}}\sup_{\mathcal{V}_{h}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}}\frac{\mathcal{L}_{\square}(\mathcal{U}_{h},\mathcal{V}_{h};\overline{\mathbf{X}})}{\|\mathcal{U}_{h}\|_{0}\|\mathcal{V}_{h}\|_{0}}

and

(32)

\|\mathcal{V}_{h}\|_{0}^{2}=\|\mathbf{v}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+\|\mathbf{Y}_{h}\|_{0,\mathcal{B}}^{2}+\|\bm{\mu}_{h}\|_{0,\mathcal{B}}^{2}+\|q_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}.

In the remainder of this section, in order to bound
\mathsf{cond}(\mathcal{A}_{\square})
in terms of
h_{\Omega}
and
h_{\mathcal{B}}
, we study
\tau
and
\omega
.
The structure of the proof is the same for both the exact and inexact computation of the coupling term and for the two choices of
\mathbf{c}
, and can be organized into the following three steps:

i)

we prove an
h
-dependent norm equivalence between the
L^{2}
-norm defined in (
32
) and
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbb{V}}
,

ii)

we prove bounds for
\omega
and
\tau
by exploiting the well-posedness of the problem and continuity of the bilinear form
\mathcal{L}_{\square}
,

iii)

we apply Theorem
4
.

The first step, which does not depend on the assembly technique for the coupling term, is presented in the following lemma.

Lemma 1
.

Under Assumption
1
, there exist two positive constants
\underline{C},\,\overline{C}
such that for all
\mathcal{V}_{h}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}
we have

\underline{C}h_{\mathcal{B}}^{2-2\ell}\|\mathcal{V}_{h}\|_{0}^{2}\leq\|\mathcal{V}_{h}\|_{\mathbb{V}}^{2}\leq\overline{C}(h_{\Omega}^{-2}+h_{\mathcal{B}}^{-2})\|\mathcal{V}_{h}\|_{0}^{2}\qquad\text{for ${\bm{\Lambda}}={\bm{\Lambda}}_{\ell}$},

where
\ell=0,1
.

Proof.

We take first
{\bm{\Lambda}}={\bm{\Lambda}}_{0}
. We start proving the upper bound. By taking into account the following standard inverse inequalities (see
[
20
]
)

(33)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{v}_{h}\|_{1,\Omega}\leq Ch_{\Omega}^{-1}\|\mathbf{v}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}

\displaystyle\forall\mathbf{v}_{h}\in\mathbf{V}_{h},

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{Y}_{h}\|_{1,\mathcal{B}}\leq Ch_{\mathcal{B}}^{-1}\|\mathbf{Y}_{h}\|_{0,\mathcal{B}}

\displaystyle\forall\mathbf{Y}_{h}\in\mathbf{S}_{h},

\displaystyle\|\bm{\mu}_{h}\|_{0,\mathcal{B}}\leq Ch_{\mathcal{B}}^{-1}\|\bm{\mu}_{h}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}_{0}}

\displaystyle\forall\bm{\mu}_{h}\in{\bm{\Lambda}}_{h},

the inclusion
\mathbf{L}^{2}(\mathcal{B})\subset{\bm{\Lambda}}_{0}
, and the assumption on the mesh sizes, we can write

(34)

\|\mathcal{V}_{h}\|_{\mathbb{V}}^{2}\leq C\,(h_{\Omega}^{-2}\|\mathbf{v}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+h_{\mathcal{B}}^{-2}\|\mathbf{Y}_{h}\|_{0,\mathcal{B}}^{2}+\|\bm{\mu}_{h}\|_{0,\mathcal{B}}^{2}+\|q\|_{0,\Omega}^{2})\leq C\,(h_{\Omega}^{-2}+h_{\mathcal{B}}^{-2})\|\mathcal{V}_{h}\|_{0}^{2},

so that the bound is proved.
For the lower bound, we exploit an inverse inequality relating
{\bm{\Lambda}}_{0}
with
\mathbf{L}^{2}(\mathcal{B})
, so that we have

(35)

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{V}_{h}\|_{\mathbb{V}}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\|\mathbf{v}_{h}\|_{1,\Omega}^{2}+\|\mathbf{Y}_{h}\|_{1,\mathcal{B}}^{2}+h_{\mathcal{B}}^{2}\|\bm{\mu}_{h}\|_{0,\mathcal{B}}^{2}+\|q\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq C\,h_{\mathcal{B}}^{2}(\|\mathbf{v}_{h}\|_{1,\Omega}^{2}+\|\mathbf{Y}_{h}\|_{1,\mathcal{B}}^{2}+\|\bm{\mu}_{h}\|_{0,\mathcal{B}}^{2}+\|q\|_{0,\Omega}^{2})

\displaystyle\geq C\,h_{\mathcal{B}}^{2}\|\mathcal{V}_{h}\|_{0}^{2}.

Now, we consider
{\bm{\Lambda}}={\bm{\Lambda}}_{1}
. The proof of the lower bound is trivial and, for the upper bound, we exploit again the inverse inequalities in (
33
). By working as in (
34
), the proof is concluded.
∎

5.1.
Condition number in case of exact coupling

Let us consider the case of exact integration of the coupling term. In the following proposition we estimate
\omega
and
\tau
.

Proposition 4
.

Under Assumption
1
, there exist two positive constants
\underline{\gamma},\,\overline{\gamma}
such that the following inequalities hold true

\omega=\sup_{\mathcal{U}_{h}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}}\sup_{\mathcal{V}_{h}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}}\frac{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{U}_{h},\mathcal{V}_{h};\overline{\mathbf{X}})}{\|\mathcal{U}_{h}\|_{0}\|\mathcal{V}_{h}\|_{0}}\leq\overline{\gamma}\,(h_{\Omega}^{-2}+h_{\mathcal{B}}^{-2}),\qquad\quad\tau=\inf_{\mathcal{U}_{h}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}}\sup_{\mathcal{V}_{h}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}}\frac{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{U}_{h},\mathcal{V}_{h};\overline{\mathbf{X}})}{\|\mathcal{U}_{h}\|_{0}\|\mathcal{V}_{h}\|_{0}}\geq\underline{\gamma}\,h_{\mathcal{B}}^{2-2\ell},

where
\ell=0,1
is associated to the choice of
\mathbf{c}
.

Proof.

The bound of
\omega
is a consequence of the continuity of
\mathcal{L}
, which is easily obtained by combining the continuity of each term at the right hand side of (
15
) with the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality

(36)

\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{V};\overline{\mathbf{X}})\leq M\|\mathcal{U}\|_{\mathbb{V}}\|\mathcal{V}\|_{\mathbb{V}}\qquad\forall\mathcal{U},\mathcal{V}\in\mathbb{V}.

Using the upper bound in Lemma
1
, we find

\sup_{\mathcal{U}_{h}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}}\sup_{\mathcal{V}_{h}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}}\frac{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{U}_{h},\mathcal{V}_{h};\overline{\mathbf{X}})}{\|\mathcal{U}_{h}\|_{0}\|\mathcal{V}_{h}\|_{0}}=\sup_{\mathcal{U}_{h}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}}\sup_{\mathcal{V}_{h}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}}\frac{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{U}_{h},\mathcal{V}_{h};\overline{\mathbf{X}})}{\|\mathcal{U}_{h}\|_{\mathbb{V}}\|\mathcal{V}_{h}\|_{\mathbb{V}}}\cdot\frac{\|\mathcal{U}_{h}\|_{\mathbb{V}}}{\|\mathcal{U}_{h}\|_{0}}\cdot\frac{\|\mathcal{V}_{h}\|_{\mathbb{V}}}{\|\mathcal{V}_{h}\|_{0}}\leq C(h_{\Omega}^{-2}+h_{\mathcal{B}}^{-2}).

On the other hand, Proposition
2
and the lower bound in Lemma
1
give us

\inf_{\mathcal{U}_{h}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}}\sup_{\mathcal{V}_{h}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}}\frac{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{U}_{h},\mathcal{V}_{h};\overline{\mathbf{X}})}{\|\mathcal{U}_{h}\|_{0}\|\mathcal{V}_{h}\|_{0}}=\inf_{\mathcal{U}_{h}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}}\sup_{\mathcal{V}_{h}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}}\frac{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{U}_{h},\mathcal{V}_{h};\overline{\mathbf{X}})}{\|\mathcal{U}_{h}\|_{\mathbb{V}}\|\mathcal{V}_{h}\|_{\mathbb{V}}}\cdot\frac{\|\mathcal{U}_{h}\|_{\mathbb{V}}}{\|\mathcal{U}_{h}\|_{0}}\cdot\frac{\|\mathcal{V}_{h}\|_{\mathbb{V}}}{\|\mathcal{V}_{h}\|_{0}}\geq Ch_{\mathcal{B}}^{2-2\ell}.

∎

5.2.
Condition number in case of inexact coupling

We move to the case of inexact computation of the coupling term. We follow the same lines of the previous section. We stress the fact that one of the main arguments consists in the continuity of the bilinear form
\mathcal{L}_{h}
and, thus, of the bilinear form
\mathbf{c}_{h}
.

Proposition 5
.

Under Assumptions
1
and
2
and given
\overline{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathbf{W}^{1,\infty}(\mathcal{B})
, there exists a constant
C
, independent of
h
, such that the following inequality holds true

(37)

\mathbf{c}_{h}(\bm{\mu}_{h},\mathbf{v}_{h}(\overline{\mathbf{X}}))\leq C\|\bm{\mu}_{h}\|_{\bm{\Lambda}}\,\|\mathbf{v}_{h}\|_{1,\Omega}\qquad\forall\bm{\mu}_{h}\in{\bm{\Lambda}}_{h},\,\mathbf{v}_{h}\in\mathbf{V}_{h}.

Proof.

Case 1.
We set
\mathbf{c}_{h}=\mathbf{c}_{0,h}
. The continuity of

\mathbf{c}_{0,h}(\bm{\mu}_{h},\mathbf{v}_{h}(\overline{\mathbf{X}}))
is a direct consequence of the
continuity of
\mathbf{c}_{0}
combined with (
24
) and the
inclusion
\overline{\mathbf{X}}(\mathcal{B})\subset\Omega
; indeed it holds

\displaystyle\mathbf{c}_{0,h}(\bm{\mu}_{h},\mathbf{v}_{h}(\overline{\mathbf{X}}))

\displaystyle=\mathbf{c}_{0}(\bm{\mu}_{h},\mathbf{v}_{h}(\overline{\mathbf{X}}))+\big[\mathbf{c}_{0,h}(\bm{\mu}_{h},\mathbf{v}_{h}(\overline{\mathbf{X}}))-\mathbf{c}_{0}(\bm{\mu}_{h},\mathbf{v}_{h}(\overline{\mathbf{X}}))\big]

\displaystyle\leq C\big(\|\bm{\mu}_{h}\|_{\bm{\Lambda}}\,\|\mathbf{v}_{h}\|_{1,\Omega}+h_{\mathcal{B}}^{1/2}|\log h_{\mathcal{B}}|\left\lVert\bm{\mu}_{h}\right\rVert_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}\left\lVert\mathbf{v}_{h}\right\rVert_{1,\Omega}\big)

\displaystyle\leq C\big(1+h_{\mathcal{B}}^{1/2}|\log h_{\mathcal{B}}|\big)\left\lVert\bm{\mu}_{h}\right\rVert_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}\left\lVert\mathbf{v}_{h}\right\rVert_{1,\Omega}.

Case 2.
We consider
\mathbf{c}_{h}=\mathbf{c}_{1,h}
. Since now
{\bm{\Lambda}}=\mathbf{H}^{1}(\mathcal{B})
we
can bound directly the integral associated to the
\mathbf{L}^{2}(\mathcal{B})
scalar
product by applying the discrete Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and the
accuracy of the quadrature rule, hence

(38)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K_{0}}\omega_{k}^{0}\,\bm{\mu}_{h}(\mathbf{p}_{k}^{0})\cdot

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{h}(\overline{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{p}_{k}^{0}))\leq\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K_{0}}\omega_{k}^{0}\,\bm{\mu}_{h}^{2}(\mathbf{p}_{k}^{0})\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K_{0}}\omega_{k}^{0}\,\mathbf{v}_{h}^{2}(\overline{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{p}_{k}^{0}))\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\|\bm{\mu}_{h}\|_{0,\mathrm{T}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K_{0}}\omega_{k}^{0}\,\mathbf{v}_{h}^{2}(\overline{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{p}_{k}^{0}))\right)^{1/2}\leq C|\mathrm{T}|^{1/2}\|\bm{\mu}_{h}\|_{0,\mathrm{T}}\|\mathbf{v}_{h}(\overline{\mathbf{X}})\|_{\infty,\mathrm{T}}.

We combine (
38
) with the inverse inequality

\|\mathbf{v}_{h}(\overline{\mathbf{X}})\|_{\infty,\mathrm{T}}\leq C_{I}h_{\mathrm{T}}^{-1}\|\mathbf{v}_{h}(\overline{\mathbf{X}})\|_{0,\mathrm{T}}
, so
that we find

(39)

\sum_{k=1}^{K_{0}}\omega_{k}^{0}\,\bm{\mu}_{h}(\mathbf{p}_{k}^{0})\cdot\mathbf{v}_{h}(\overline{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{p}_{k}^{0}))\leq C|\mathrm{T}|^{1/2}h_{\mathrm{T}}^{-1}\|\bm{\mu}_{h}\|_{0,\mathrm{T}}\|\mathbf{v}_{h}(\overline{\mathbf{X}})\|_{0,\mathrm{T}}\leq C\|\bm{\mu}_{h}\|_{0,\mathrm{T}}\|\mathbf{v}_{h}(\overline{\mathbf{X}})\|_{0,\mathrm{T}}.

We now focus on the term involving gradients. By applying the same
procedure as in (
38
) and (
39
), we obtain

(40)

\sum_{k=1}^{K_{1}}\omega_{k}^{1}\,\grads\bm{\mu}_{h}(\mathbf{p}_{k}^{1}):\grads\mathbf{v}_{h}(\overline{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{p}_{k}^{1}))\leq C\,\|\grads\bm{\mu}_{h}\|_{0,\mathrm{T}}\|\grads\mathbf{v}_{h}(\overline{\mathbf{X}})\|_{0,\mathrm{T}}.

We sum up (
39
) and (
40
) over all
\mathrm{T}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\mathcal{B}}
. The inclusion
\overline{\mathbf{X}}(\mathcal{B})\subset\Omega
, together with the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, gives

\mathbf{c}_{1,h}(\bm{\mu}_{h},\mathbf{v}_{h}(\overline{\mathbf{X}}))\leq C\,\left(\|\bm{\mu}_{h}\|_{0,\mathcal{B}}\,\|\mathbf{v}_{h}(\overline{\mathbf{X}})\|_{0,\mathcal{B}}+\|\grads\bm{\mu}_{h}\|_{0,\mathcal{B}}\|\grads\mathbf{v}_{h}(\overline{\mathbf{X}})\|_{0,\mathcal{B}}\right)\leq C\,\|\bm{\mu}_{h}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}\,\|\mathbf{v}_{h}\|_{1,\Omega},

so that the result is proved.
∎

The continuity of
\mathcal{L}_{h}
is a direct consequence of the above proposition, and is stated as follows.

Proposition 6
.

Under Assumptions
1
and
2
, there exists a constant
M>0
, independent of

, such that

(41)

\mathcal{L}_{h}(\mathcal{U}_{h},\mathcal{V}_{h};\overline{\mathbf{X}})\leq M\|\mathcal{U}_{h}\|_{\mathbb{V}}\|\mathcal{V}_{h}\|_{\mathbb{V}}\qquad\forall\mathcal{U}_{h},\mathcal{V}_{h}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}.\\

Proof.

The result is obtained by combining Proposition
5
with the continuity of the other terms in definition (
26
).
∎

We now estimate
\omega
and
\tau
(see (

)) for
\mathcal{L}_{h}
.

Proposition 7
.

Under Assumptions
1
and
2
, there exist two positive constants, denoted again by
\underline{\gamma},\,\overline{\gamma}
, such that the following inequalities hold true

\omega=\sup_{\mathcal{U}_{h}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}}\sup_{\mathcal{V}_{h}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}}\frac{\mathcal{L}_{h}(\mathcal{U}_{h},\mathcal{V}_{h};\overline{\mathbf{X}})}{\|\mathcal{U}_{h}\|_{0}\|\mathcal{V}_{h}\|_{0}}\leq\overline{\gamma}\,(h_{\Omega}^{-2}+h_{\mathcal{B}}^{-2}),\qquad\quad\tau=\inf_{\mathcal{U}_{h}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}}\sup_{\mathcal{V}_{h}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}}\frac{\mathcal{L}_{h}(\mathcal{U}_{h},\mathcal{V}_{h};\overline{\mathbf{X}})}{\|\mathcal{U}_{h}\|_{0}\|\mathcal{V}_{h}\|_{0}}\geq\underline{\gamma}\,h_{\mathcal{B}}^{2-2\ell},

is associated to the choice of

.

5.3.
Main result

We conclude this section by presenting the estimate of the condition number in both cases of exact and inexact integration of the coupling term.

Theorem 5
.

Under Assumption
1
, for
\ell=0,1
depending on the definition of
\mathbf{c}
, we have

\mathsf{cond}(\mathcal{A})\leq Ch_{\mathcal{B}}^{-2+2\ell}(h_{\Omega}^{-2}+h_{\mathcal{B}}^{-2}).

Moreover, with the additional hypotheses stated in Assumption
2
, we have

\mathsf{cond}(\mathcal{A}_{h})\leq Ch_{\mathcal{B}}^{-2+2\ell}(h_{\Omega}^{-2}+h_{\mathcal{B}}^{-2}).

Proof.

The result is a direct consequence of Theorem
4
combined with Proposition
4
and Proposition
7
for the exact and inexact integration, respectively.
∎

Remark 2
.

We observe that, if
h=h_{\mathcal{B}}\approx h_{\Omega}
, then Theorem
5
gives the following bounds

(42)

\displaystyle\mathsf{cond}(\mathcal{A}_{\square})\leq Ch^{-4}

\displaystyle\text{if }\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{c}_{0}\text{ or }\mathbf{c}_{h}=\mathbf{c}_{0,h},

\displaystyle\mathsf{cond}(\mathcal{A}_{\square})\leq Ch^{-2}

\displaystyle\text{if }\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{c}_{1}\text{ or }\mathbf{c}_{h}=\mathbf{c}_{1,h}.

Remark 3
.

The condition number only depends on the mesh sizes
h_{\Omega}
and
h_{\mathcal{B}}
, while its growth rate depends on the choice of the coupling term, regardless of the implementation technique. We observed in Section
4
that the well-posedness of the discrete problem is independent of the size of the intersected cell. This fact is still true for the condition number, thus its behavior does not depend on the position of the interface as we show later in our numerical experiments.

Remark 4
.

We observe that the choice of coupling form
\mathbf{c}
and related assembly technique should be done with particular care. On the one hand, looking at Theorems
2
and
3
, we see that choosing
\mathbf{c}_{0}
instead of
\mathbf{c}_{1}
may be more convenient from the computational point of view: indeed,
\mathbf{c}_{0}
can be cheaply assembled using the inexact procedure, without negative effects on the accuracy. On the other hand, Theorem
5
says that the condition number increases faster in
h
when
\mathbf{c}_{0}
or
\mathbf{c}_{0,h}
are considered.

6.
Numerical tests

This section collects a wide range of numerical tests confirming our theoretical results for the condition number and the convergence of the method. We first describe the problem we are going to solve throughout the entire section.

Problem 7

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}_{f}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})-(\div\mathbf{v},p)_{\Omega}+\mathbf{c}(\bm{\lambda},\mathbf{v}(\overline{\mathbf{X}}))=(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{v})_{\Omega}

(43d)

\displaystyle\mathbf{c}(\bm{\mu},\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{u}(\overline{\mathbf{X}}))=\mathbf{c}(\bm{\mu},\mathbf{d})

where

In each test, we compute the right hand sides

6.3.
Time dependent problem: the stretched annulus

In this last subsection, we report some numerical results for the time dependent Problem
1
of a stretched annulus immersed in a square fluid domain. We focus on the effect of small cut cells on the stability in time.

Let
\Omega
be the unit square and
\mathcal{B}=\{\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\,0.125\leq|\mathbf{s}|\leq 0.25\}
the reference solid domain corresponding to the annulus at rest.
The physical parameters are densities
\rho_{f}=1.0
and
\rho_{s}=1.1
, equal fluid and solid viscosities
\nu=0.01
, and elasticity constant
\kappa=0.2
. We assume that at
t=0
the fluid is at rest, hence
\mathbf{u}_{0}=0
, while the annulus is stretched and its initial deformation is given by
\mathbf{X}_{0}=\left({s_{1}}/{1.4}+0.5,1.4\,s_{2}+0.5\right)
. The internal elastic forces drive the annulus back to its resting position, thus generating the motion of the system, which is analyzed until
T=4
.

The mesh for the pressure unknown,
\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\Omega}
, is obtained by dividing
\Omega
into
N^{2}
squares then split into two triangles, with
h_{\Omega}=1/N
. Consequently, the velocity unknown is approximated on
\mathcal{T}^{\Omega}_{h/2}
, see (
17
). The grid
\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\mathcal{B}}
is generated with
Gmsh

[
27
]
in such a way that
h_{\mathcal{B}}\approx(4/3)h_{\Omega}
.
We consider three configurations by setting
N=32\text{ (coarse)},\,64\text{ (medium)},\,128\text{ (fine)}
. For the time step, we choose
\Delta t=0.1,\,0.01
. For the coupling, we use
\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{c}_{0}
computed exactly.

In Figure
14
, some snapshots report the velocity contour lines, together with the position of the structure (top line) and the pressure profiles (bottom line). We plot the system configuration at time
t=0.5
,
t=1
and
t=4
: this choice is due to the fact that the evolution of the immersed structure is faster at the beginning, while after
t=2
, it almost reaches the resting position. Moreover, since the pressure is discontinuous along the interface and it is discretized by continuous piecewise linear elements, the Gibbs phenomenon appears. Notice that the value of
p_{h}
ranges in
(-0.05,0.05)
at
t=0.5
, then it decreases to values in
(0,0.01)
at
t=4
.

In order to assess the unconditional stability with respect to the time step, we compute the system energy

\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{u}_{h}^{n},\mathbf{X}_{h}^{n})=\frac{\rho_{f}}{2}\|\mathbf{u}_{h}^{n}\|^{2}_{0,\Omega}+\frac{\delta\rho}{2}\left\|\frac{\mathbf{X}_{h}^{n}-\mathbf{X}_{h}^{n-1}}{\Delta t}\right\|_{0,\mathcal{B}}^{2}+\frac{\kappa}{2}|\mathbf{X}_{h}^{n}|_{1,\mathcal{B}}^{2}.

Figure
15
displays the behavior of the energy ratio
\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{u}_{h}^{n},\mathbf{X}_{h}^{n})/\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{u}_{h}^{0},\mathbf{X}_{h}^{0})
, computed by setting
\mathbf{X}_{h}^{-1}=0
. We observe that the energy is decreasing in the interval of interest and again that the evolution is faster for
t\in(0,2)
. After that, the resting position is almost reached. Essentially, the energy behaves independently of the considered meshes and time steps. It seems that the possible presence of small cut cells does not have any effect on the energy. In order to check if the computation of the coupling term actually produces small cut cells, we follow the evolution of a randomly chosen solid element. At each time step, the picked element is subdivided into several (polygonal) cells taking into account the intersections with the underlying velocity mesh. We evaluate the area of these sub-cells and we mark it in the semi-log scatter plots in Figure
16
. We see that, for
\Delta t=0.1
, cells with area of the order
10^{-9}
–
10^{-10}
appear for all meshes, while for
\Delta t=0.01
, the smallest cells can have an area ranging from
10^{-12}
to
10^{-15}
, depending on the mesh refinement.

7.
Conclusion

We considered the fictitious domain formulation for fluid-structure interaction problems introduced in
[
6
]
. The approach uses a distributed Lagrange multiplier to enforce the kinematic condition and, consequently, to represent the interaction between fluid and solid. Thus, at discrete level, the so-called
coupling term
is defined through functions constructed over non-matching grids. As extensively described in
[
8
,
9
]
, the discrete coupling term can be constructed either exactly, i.e. by computing the intersection between the involved meshes, or in approximate way by accepting the presence of a quadrature error.

After recalling the existing theoretical results concerning the unconditional stability in time and the well-posedness of the mixed finite element discretization, we emphasized that such results are independent of the position of the interface, hence not affected by the presence of small cut cells while assembling the coupling matrix. Our formulation is naturally stable without resorting to any penalization term.

We then proved upper bounds for the condition number of the fluid-structure FEM system. To this aim, we made use of the theory provided by
[
25
]
. We observed that the condition number depends on the choice of coupling term, but does not deteriorate in the presence of small cut cells. Several numerical tests confirmed our theoretical findings.
