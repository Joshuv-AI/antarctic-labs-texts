Boron adsorption from aqueous solutions using chemically activated kaolin clay adsorbents kinetics isotherm and thermodynamic studies
Authors: Abdullah Y Al Haj Ahmed, Husam Al Najar, Nasser Abu Ghalwa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBoron contamination in water is a global concern due to its narrow threshold between beneficial and toxic levels for humans, crops, and ecosystems. Long-term exposure to elevated boron concentrations can cause serious health risks and reduce agricultural productivity, making its removal from water an urgent priority. Conventional treatment methods often fail to achieve efficient and sustainable boron removal, creating a need for low-cost and effective adsorbents. In this study, kaolin clay was chemically activated with hydrochloric acid (HCl) and phosphoric acid (H₃PO₄) to improve its adsorption capacity and surface reactivity. Structural and surface modifications were confirmed through FTIR, XRF, and BET analyses, which showed increased porosity, higher surface area, and the formation of functional groups favorable for boron binding. Batch adsorption experiments were performed to investigate the effect of pH, adsorbent dosage, initial boron concentration, contact time, and temperature. Raw kaolin exhibited negligible adsorption efficiency and was excluded from further tests. In contrast, activated kaolin achieved significantly higher capacities, with maximum adsorption of 2.39 mg/g for HCl-treated kaolin and 2.18 mg/g for H₃PO₄-treated kaolin at an initial concentration of 16.5 mg/L. Adsorption was most favorable at pH 4. Isotherm studies indicated that equilibrium data fitted well to Freundlich and Sips models, suggesting multilayer adsorption on heterogeneous surfaces. Kinetic analysis revealed excellent agreement with the pseudo-second-order model, while thermodynamic evaluation confirmed the exothermic and spontaneous nature of the process. Tests on groundwater samples from Gaza City showed performance consistent with laboratory results, confirming the reliability and scalability of the method. This study demonstrates that chemically activated kaolin is a promising, low-cost, and environmentally friendly adsorbent for boron removal, offering a practical solution for water treatment systems in regions facing boron contamination challenges.Keywords: Optimization, Activated kaolin, Adsorption efficiency, Efficient adsorbentsSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Materials science

Introduction

Water pollution remains one of the most pressing global challenges, significantly impacting ecosystems, agriculture, and human health. Industrialization, urbanization, and agricultural practices have contributed to the widespread contamination of aquatic environments with heavy metals, pesticides, metalloids, and other hazardous pollutants1. Among these contaminants, boron has attracted increasing attention due to its essential role at trace levels and its toxicity at elevated concentrations2. Boron contamination primarily originates from mining effluents, glass and ceramics production, detergents, and fertilizers, making it a serious environmental and agricultural concern3,4.

Although boron is required in trace amounts for plant growth and cell wall stability, excessive levels can impair plant development, cause necrosis in leaves, and ultimately reduce crop productivity5,6. Its narrow beneficial-to-toxic range in irrigation water makes monitoring boron concentrations critical. For instance, the European Union has set a maximum permissible limit of 1 mg/L for boron in irrigation water7. However, reports show that boron levels in several arid and semi-arid regions exceed these thresholds, posing risks to both agriculture and drinking water supplies8.

To mitigate boron contamination, various technologies have been investigated, including ion exchange, membrane processes, chemical precipitation, and advanced filtration techniques9–11. While these approaches are effective, they are often costly, energy-intensive, and generate secondary waste, limiting their large-scale application12. In comparison, adsorption techniques have emerged as a promising solution due to their low cost, ease of operation, and environmental compatibility13,14. Recent studies highlight the development of low-cost natural adsorbents and chemically modified materials that exhibit enhanced removal efficiency for boron15,16.

Kaolin clay, a naturally abundant aluminosilicate mineral, has been extensively studied as an adsorbent due to its favorable physicochemical properties, including high surface area, cation exchange capacity, and chemical stability17. The main advantages of kaolin are its wide availability, low cost, and non-toxic nature, making it an attractive material for large-scale water treatment18. However, raw kaolin often exhibits limited adsorption capacity for boron due to its relatively low porosity and surface activity19. Chemical activation (e.g., acid modification with HCl, H₂SO₄, or H₃PO₄) significantly enhances its adsorption properties by increasing surface area, generating active functional groups, and improving ion-exchange capacity2,20. On the other hand, disadvantages include possible leaching of acidic residues, regeneration challenges, and the need for optimization of treatment parameters to achieve competitive adsorption performance21,22.

Recent advancements in clay modification have further improved kaolin’s performance. For instance, sustainable hybrid materials combining kaolin with nano-bio adsorbents have been developed, achieving higher efficiency in boron removal23. Similarly, new studies emphasize the potential of chemically activated kaolin clay as a cost-effective and eco-friendly alternative for addressing boron contamination in water systems24.

Therefore, the present study focuses on investigating the adsorption of boron from aqueous solutions using chemically activated kaolin clay. Key operational parameters including pH, adsorbent dosage, boron concentration, contact time, and temperature were systematically optimized. The adsorption behavior was analyzed through isotherm, kinetic, and thermodynamic models to provide deeper insights into the adsorption mechanism and evaluate the feasibility of this adsorbent for real-world water treatment applications.

Materials and methods

Materials

The materials used in this study were selected to ensure the accuracy and reliability of the experiments conducted for the preparation of chemically activated kaolin clay adsorbent and the removal of boron from aqueous solutions. Natural raw kaolin clay, sourced from KaMin LLC, was used as the base material. Hydrochloric acid (0.5 M HCl) and phosphoric acid (0.5 M H₃PO₄) were obtained from HiMedia, while sodium hydroxide (0.3 M NaOH) from HiMedia was used for pH adjustments. Boric acid (H₃BO₃) was procured from HiMedia for preparing boron solutions. Potassium dichromate (K₂Cr₂O₇) supplied by Merck was utilized for standard solutions in analytical procedures. Distilled and deionized water was used throughout all experimental work to prevent interference from impurities and maintain result consistency. All chemicals were of analytical grade and used without further purification.

Preparation of boron solutions

Analytical-grade boric acid (H₃BO₃) and distilled water were used to prepare boron solutions with initial concentrations of 1.50, 4.50, 8.50, 12.50, and 16.50 mg/L. The solutions were freshly prepared prior to each adsorption experiment to ensure accuracy and consistency. After preparation, the solutions were stored in labeled, airtight containers to prevent contamination or alteration. The boron concentration in these solutions was measured using inductively coupled plasma–optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES) for precise quantification.

Preparation of adsorbent: chemically activated kaolin clay

The preparation of the chemically activated kaolin adsorbent was performed following a modified procedure based on previously reported methods25. Briefly, 10 g of raw kaolin clay was washed three times with distilled water at a mass-to-volume ratio of 1:4 to remove surface impurities. The washed kaolin was dried in an oven at 105 °C for 6.0 h and then ground into fine powder using a mortar and pestle.

The chemical activation was performed by mechanically stirring the kaolin sample in an acid solution (1 part acid:1 part water, v/v) for 4.0 h at room temperature (27 °C). The mixture was then kept overnight. The red-yellow supernatant was removed by decantation, and the treated kaolin was neutralized by 3.0 M NaOH under continuous stirring for 30 min. The neutralized clay was washed with distilled water until a neutral pH (≈ 7) was achieved, followed by oven-drying at 105 °C for 4.0 h. The dried product was ground again into fine powder and stored in airtight containers for further use. The same activation procedure was performed separately with both hydrochloric and phosphoric acid solutions.

Determination of point of zero charge (pHpzc)

The pHpzc of the activated kaolin was determined using the solid addition method26. A series of 50 mL NaCl solutions (0.1 M) with initial pH values adjusted between 2 and 12 were prepared using HCl or NaOH. A fixed mass of the adsorbent (0.1 g) was added to each solution and shaken for 48 h at room temperature. The final pH was recorded, and the pHpzc was determined as the point where the curve of (pH_final – pH_initial) versus pH_initial crosses zero.

Adsorbent regeneration procedure

The regeneration of the spent kaolin adsorbent was carried out to evaluate its reusability. After adsorption, the boron-loaded kaolin samples were separated by filtration and washed with distilled water. Desorption was performed by treating the spent adsorbent with 0.1 M HCl solution under stirring for 2 h at 25 °C27. The desorbed samples were then washed with deionized water until neutral pH, oven-dried at 105 °C for 4.0 h, and reused in subsequent adsorption cycles. The efficiency of regeneration was evaluated by comparing boron removal performance across five consecutive adsorption–desorption cycles.

Characterization of the adsorbent

The raw and chemically activated kaolin clays were characterized using Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) (PerkinElmer Frontier, 4000–400 cm⁻¹) to identify functional groups, Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) surface area and pore size distribution (Micromeritics ASAP 2020 Plus), and X-ray Fluorescence (XRF) (Thermo Fisher ARL 9900) to determine elemental composition and confirm activation effects.

Batch adsorption experiments

Batch adsorption studies were carried out to investigate the effects of pH (2–12), initial boron concentration (1.50–16.50) mg/L, adsorbent dosage (0.05–0.25) g, contact time (30–240) min, and temperature (25–45) °C on boron removal efficiency. In each experiment, a known mass of adsorbent was added to 50 mL of boron solution in 100 mL polyethylene bottles and shaken at 150 rpm. The pH was adjusted using 0.5 M HCl or 0.3 M NaOH. After equilibrium, solutions were filtered through 0.45 μm Whatman membrane filters, and residual boron concentrations were determined by ICP-OES. Kinetic and isotherm studies

Kinetic experiments were conducted by varying contact time (30–240) min and adsorbent dosage (0.1–0.25) g. The adsorption data were fitted to pseudo-first-order and pseudo-second-order models to identify the controlling mechanism. Isotherm experiments were conducted at different concentrations and fitted to Langmuir, Freundlich, and Sips models to describe adsorption equilibrium.

Design of experiments for boron removal from solutions using minitab 1818.0 program

To optimize boron removal using chemically activated kaolin, the statistical software Minitab 1818.0 was employed to design and optimize the input factors. The primary goal of the statistical analysis was to evaluate the percentage of boron removal, which served as the response variable. This percentage represents the efficiency of the adsorption process in removing boron from aqueous solutions. The study aimed to identify the key factors and their interactions that significantly influenced boron removal. To assess the reliability and significance of the statistical model, P-values were calculated for each factor and interaction. In statistical hypothesis testing, smaller P-values indicate stronger evidence against the null hypothesis, suggesting that a factor or interaction has a significant effect on boron removal. Conversely, larger P-values imply weaker evidence, indicating that the factor may not significantly influence the response. Additionally, the magnitude of the t-values associated with the model’s coefficient terms was considered. The t-value, representing the ratio of the estimated coefficient to its standard error, reflects the significance of each factor. Larger absolute t-values suggest that the coefficient is more significant, as the estimated value is larger relative to its variability. This approach allowed for a robust optimization of the boron removal process using hydrochloric and phosphoric acid-activated kaolin, ensuring the identification of the most influential factors for efficient adsorption.

Results and discussions

Performance characterization of adsorbents

FTIR analysis

Figure 1 presents the FTIR analysis of raw kaolin and chemically activated kaolin treated with HCl acid. The spectrum reveals key changes in the functional groups present in the kaolin before and after modification. The hydroxyl stretching vibration bands observed between 3612 and 3698 cm⁻¹ correspond to the inner surface –OH stretching vibration of kaolin, while the band at 3442 cm⁻¹ corresponds to the stretching vibration of the outer-surface hydroxyl groups, confirming the presence of kaolin in the raw clay material. After acid treatment, a significant disappearance of the bands at 3442 cm⁻¹ and 1775 cm⁻¹ indicates that the acid treatment effectively altered these functional groups. The band at 1775 cm⁻¹, typically attributed to the –OH bending vibration and C = O stretching vibration, also disappeared, suggesting that the treatment disrupted the bonding structure of the kaolin. Additionally, the bands at 915 and 1003 cm⁻¹ are associated with the Si–O–Si and Si–O–Al vibrations, indicating the presence of silicate structures within the kaolin.

FTIR spectra of (a) Raw kaolin and (b) Chemically activated kaolin by HCl.

These findings are consistent with previously reported FTIR spectra of acid-modified kaolin, where the disappearance of hydroxyl and carbonyl-related bands, along with the enhancement of Si–O vibrations, confirmed structural modifications due to chemical activation28. Such agreement further validates the successful alteration of the kaolin surface chemistry after HCl treatment in this study.

BET analysis

The surface area, pore volume, and pore size distribution of raw kaolin and chemically activated kaolin treated with phosphoric acid and hydrochloric acid were determined by BET analysis. The results (Table 1) revealed that the total surface area of raw kaolin decreased after chemical treatment, from 17.97 m²/g to 14.87 m²/g and 13.98 m²/g for phosphoric acid and hydrochloric acid treatments, respectively. This reduction in surface area can be attributed to the partial dissolution of kaolin during acid activation, which removes some of the mineral components and reduces the available surface for adsorption. However, despite the decrease in surface area, the pore volume increased from 0.041 cm³/g to 0.045 cm³/g and 0.049 cm³/g for the acid treatments, indicating that the acid treatment facilitated the formation of additional pores within the kaolin structure. Additionally, the pore diameter increased from 9.20 nm to 11.16 nm for phosphoric acid treatment and to 12.89 nm for hydrochloric acid treatment, suggesting significant changes in the structural properties of kaolin after activation.

Effect of pH value on boron adsorption process

Boron exists in aqueous solutions primarily as boric acid (B(OH)₃) or borate ions (B(OH)₄⁻), depending on the pH of the solution. At lower pH values, boron is predominantly present as boric acid (B(OH)₃), which is a neutral species. As the pH increases, boric acid undergoes hydrolysis, converting to the negatively charged borate ions (B(OH)₄⁻). This pH-dependent speciation is crucial in understanding how boron interacts with the surface of adsorbents like activated kaolin.

The point of zero charge (pHzpc) of the adsorbent plays a significant role in determining the surface charge of kaolin and the subsequent interaction with boron species. At the pHzpc of kaolin, the surface of the adsorbent is neutral, which influences the interaction between kaolin and boron ions. Below the pHzpc, the surface of kaolin is positively charged, while above this value, it becomes negatively charged. In this study, the pHzpc of activated kaolin treated with HCl was found to be 4.9, and for kaolin activated with phosphoric acid (H₃PO₄), it was slightly higher at 5.3. These values suggest that at pH 4, the surface of kaolin is still positively charged, which would favor the adsorption of negatively charged borate ions (B(OH)₄⁻).

The impact of pH on boron removal efficiency can be better understood by considering the interaction between the surface charge of kaolin and the speciation of boron ions. At pH 4, both boric acid and borate ions coexist in the solution. The positively charged kaolin surface at this pH attracts the negatively charged borate ions (B(OH)₄⁻), promoting adsorption. As shown in Fig. 3, the higher removal efficiency observed for HCl and H₃PO₄-activated kaolin at pH 4 (56.36% and 51.64%, respectively) suggests that the electrostatic interaction between the borate ions and the kaolin surface is a key factor in the adsorption process.

Effect of pH on the percent removal of boron by chemically activated kaolin via (a) HCl (b) (Experimental conditions: solution volume:50mL, contact time:3 h, temperature:25 °C, speed:150 rpm and adsorbent dosage:0.2 g).

Therefore, the adsorption of boron onto chemically activated kaolin can be attributed to electrostatic interactions between the negatively charged borate ions and the positively charged surface of the kaolin at pH 4. This mechanism is further supported by the significant increase in boron removal efficiency at this pH, highlighting the critical role of surface charge and ion speciation in the adsorption process.

Effect of adsorbent dosage on Boron adsorption process

The adsorbent dosage plays a crucial role in the adsorption process, as it directly affects the adsorbent’s capacity to remove boron from a given initial concentration in the solution. Figure 4 illustrates the impact of varying dosages of chemically activated kaolin on the adsorption capacity when using HCl and H₃PO₄ as activation agents. It was observed that the percentage of boron adsorption increases with the increase in adsorbent dosage, reaching its maximum at 0.2 g/50 mL. This result can be attributed to the fact that a higher adsorbent dosage provides a greater number of available sites for boron adsorption. As the dosage increases, the surface area and the number of active sites also increase, enhancing the removal efficiency of boron from the solution. However, beyond this optimal dosage, the removal efficiency may plateau or decrease due to saturation of adsorption sites.

Figure 5 shows the effect of initial boron concentration on the adsorption capacity of chemically activated kaolin using hydrochloric acid (HCl) and phosphoric acid (H₃PO₄). It is evident that the equilibrium adsorption capacity of boron increases with increasing initial boron concentration until it reaches a point where it becomes nearly constant. Effect of contact time on Boron adsorption process

During the experimental tests, a notable trend was observed regarding boron concentration and adsorption time. As the adsorption time increased, a rapid decrease in boron concentration was observed, with the most significant reduction occurring within the first 120 min. This sharp decline can be attributed to the combined effects of the decreasing boron concentration in the solution and the availability of active adsorption sites on the adsorbent.

Following this initial rapid adsorption phase, the decrease in boron concentration became more gradual, indicating that the chemically activated kaolin surface was approaching saturation. At this stage, most of the adsorption sites on the kaolin were occupied, resulting in a slower rate of boron adsorption using HCl- and H₃PO₄-activated kaolin. The experimental data revealed that after 180 min of contact time, changes in boron concentration were minimal, suggesting that the adsorption process had reached equilibrium. Effect of temperature on Boron adsorption process

The adsorption process is significantly influenced by temperature, which can result in either exothermic or endothermic behavior depending on the nature of the adsorbent–adsorbate interactions. In this study, the thermal behavior of boron adsorption onto chemically activated kaolin using HCl and H₃PO₄ was systematically investigated at 25 °C, 35 °C, and 45 °C. Experimental conditions were fixed at an initial boron concentration of 16.50 mg/L, adsorbent dosage of 0.2 g/50 mL, and pH 4. As depicted in Fig. 7, adsorption efficiency decreased with increasing temperature, indicating that the process is exothermic. Specifically, the maximum removal efficiency was observed at 25 °C, and efficiency gradually declined at higher temperatures, suggesting that physical adsorption predominates and that elevated temperatures may weaken the adsorptive forces between boron ions and the kaolin surface.

Effect of temperature on boron adsorption by chemically activated kaolin via (a) HCl (b) (Experimental conditions: solution volume:50 mL, pH value: 4, speed:150 rpm, dosage: 0.2 g and contact time: 3 h).

These findings are consistent with previous studies on similar adsorbents. For instance, Yavuz and Ozturk31 reported a comparable exothermic behavior in their investigation of boron removal using acid-activated red mud, where adsorption capacity also decreased at higher temperatures. They attributed this trend to the weakening of van der Waals forces and electrostatic interactions under elevated thermal conditions, which aligns with the behavior observed in this study. Similarly, Kaya and Onal33 observed that phosphoric acid-modified bentonite exhibited higher boron uptake at lower temperatures, further supporting the conclusion that temperature plays a critical role in controlling adsorption efficiency. These parallels reinforce the conclusion that boron adsorption using both HCl- and H₃PO₄-activated kaolin is thermodynamically exothermic and favored at lower temperatures, highlighting the importance of temperature optimization in practical applications.

Optimization of adsorption process: design of experiments using minitab program

The optimization methodology employed in this study involves a combination of experimental data and statistical modeling to fine-tune the conditions for optimal boron removal. Specifically, batch adsorption experiments were conducted under varying conditions of pH, initial boron concentration, and kaolin dosage to assess their impact on adsorption capacity.

Using chemically activated kaolin via HCl

The optimization methodology employed in this study combines experimental data and statistical modeling to fine-tune the conditions for effective boron removal using HCl-activated kaolin. The Langmuir isotherm, which assumes monolayer adsorption on a homogeneous surface with identical active sites, showed poor agreement with the experimental data. The R² values were 0.949 for HCl-activated kaolin and 0.952 for H₃PO₄-activated kaolin. More importantly, the calculated maximum adsorption capacities ​) were negative, indicating that the Langmuir model is not suitable for describing boron adsorption in this system. This is likely due to the heterogeneous nature of the kaolin surface and the multilayer adsorption mechanism involved.

In contrast, the Freundlich model, which describes adsorption on heterogeneous surfaces with non-uniform affinities, provided an excellent fit. The R² values reached 0.998 for HCl-activated kaolin and 0.993 for H₃PO₄-activated kaolin. The Freundlich constants supported this observation, with = 0.138 and n = 1.02 for HCl-activated kaolin, and = 0.115 and n = 1.05 for H₃PO₄-activated kaolin. These results confirm that adsorption follows a multilayer mechanism and that surface heterogeneity is a major controlling factor.

Furthermore, three-parameter models such as Redlich-Peterson and Sips were applied to provide a more accurate representation of the adsorption behavior. Error analysis (expressed as RMSE) indicated that the Sips model yielded the lowest errors, confirming the non-Langmuirian nature of the process and the predominance of multilayer adsorption on heterogeneous sites.

These findings are consistent with previous studies. For instance, Kaya and Onal43 reported that boron adsorption onto phosphoric acid-modified bentonite followed the Freundlich model rather than Langmuir, due to irregular surface morphology and variation in adsorption energy sites. Other studies have also highlighted that Freundlich-type behavior is common for chemically modified natural adsorbents, further confirming that surface heterogeneity is the key factor governing boron adsorption44,45.

Mechanism of boron adsorption on chemically activated kaolin

The adsorption mechanism of boron onto HCl- and H₃PO₄-activated kaolin can be explained through a combination of electrostatic interactions, ion exchange, and complexation with surface hydroxyl groups (–OH). At acidic pH, the surface of kaolin becomes positively charged, favoring electrostatic attraction with negatively charged borate species (B(OH)₄⁻). In addition, surface hydroxyl groups on activated kaolin provide active binding sites for boron through ligand exchange and hydrogen bonding, leading to stable surface complexes. The activation with HCl and H₃PO₄ increases surface area, enhances pore structure, and introduces additional acidic sites, which strengthen the adsorption affinity.

As illustrated in Fig. 13, the process involves three main steps: (i) diffusion of boron species from the bulk solution to the adsorbent surface, (ii) interaction and attachment of boron ions on active surface functional groups, and (iii) stabilization of boron by surface complexation and multilayer adsorption. These findings are consistent with previous studies, which reported that chemically modified clays and kaolin derivatives remove boron mainly via surface complexation and ion exchange rather than by simple physical adsorption46,47.

This mechanism also explains the excellent fit of the Freundlich and Sips isotherm models (Table 10; Fig. 12), highlighting the heterogeneous nature of the surface and the predominance of multilayer adsorption. Furthermore, the strong chemical interactions between boron and functional groups account for the relatively high adsorption capacity and stability, suggesting that regeneration can be achieved through pH adjustment or desorption using mild alkaline solutions.

In conclusion, the adsorption kinetics of boron onto chemically activated kaolin reveal that the process is governed primarily by chemisorption mechanisms. While the pseudo-first-order model showed limited agreement with the experimental data, the pseudo-second-order model provided an excellent fit, indicating that electron sharing or exchange plays a significant role in boron uptake. Additionally, the involvement of intraparticle diffusion, though evident, was not solely responsible for controlling the adsorption rate, as boundary layer effects and film diffusion also contributed. These insights underscore the complexity of the adsorption process and highlight the importance of surface properties and modification methods in enhancing adsorption performance.

Real world validation of activated Kaolin adsorbents for boron removal

To evaluate the practical applicability and efficiency of the developed adsorbents for boron removal, validation experiments were conducted using real water samples collected from a water well in Gaza City. These experiments aimed to assess the performance of the adsorbents under real-world conditions, contrasting controlled laboratory scenarios. The water samples were analyzed for ion concentrations using Inductively Coupled Plasma Optical Emission Spectroscopy (ICP-OES), revealing boron at 15.45 mg/l, Mg²⁺ at 50.10 ppm, Ca²⁺ at 98.62 ppm, Cl⁻ at 250 ppm, NO₃⁻ at 49.83 ppm, CO₃²⁻ at 247.90 ppm, and SO₄²⁻ at 220.20 ppm, providing a realistic baseline for testing. Both chemically activated kaolin adsorbents, treated with hydrochloric acid and phosphoric acid, were employed for boron removal under the optimal conditions used in earlier laboratory experiments, including a pH of 4 to ensure methodological consistency. The effect of competing ions on boron adsorption was thoroughly investigated, with hydrochloric acid-treated kaolin demonstrating higher efficiency. Mg²⁺ and Ca²⁺ significantly enhanced boron adsorption by forming surface complexes that increased adsorption sites or activated the adsorbent’s surface charge, while Cl⁻ and SO₄²⁻ negatively impacted adsorption by competing for active sites. NO₃⁻ had negligible effects, and CO₃²⁻ reduced boron adsorption due to competition for adsorption sites. Overall, the findings showed that Cl⁻, CO₃²⁻, and SO₄²⁻ slightly reduced boron removal efficiency. The performance in real-world samples closely matched the efficiency observed in laboratory conditions, highlighting the adsorbents’ potential as a novel and promising solution for boron removal in water treatment.

Regeneration cycles of adsorbents

The regeneration cycles of chemically activated kaolin, treated with hydrochloric acid (HCl) and phosphoric acid (H₃PO₄), were evaluated to assess its reusability for boron removal. The desorption procedure involved treating the spent adsorbent with a 0.1 M sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solution, which was selected for its ability to effectively desorb boron. The adsorbent was immersed in the NaOH solution for (2 h) at room temperature (25 °C) under continuous stirring at 150 rpm to ensure uniform desorption. After desorption, the adsorbent was thoroughly washed with deionized water to remove any residual NaOH and dried at 60 °C for (12 h) before being reused in subsequent cycles. Both adsorbents demonstrated significant retention of removal efficiency over multiple cycles, with only minor performance reductions. HCl-activated kaolin exhibited a decline from 56.36% in the first cycle to approximately 48% after five cycles, while H₃PO₄-activated kaolin decreased from 51.64% to around 44%. The slightly superior performance of HCl-activated kaolin is attributed to its higher initial adsorption capacity and stability under acidic conditions. These findings highlight the adsorbents’ potential for repeated use, making them cost-effective and sustainable for boron removal. Further optimization of the regeneration process, such as adjusting the desorption solution concentration or duration, could enhance their long-term usability in water treatment applications.

Conclusion

This study confirmed that chemically activated kaolin using HCl and H₃PO₄ is a promising, low-cost adsorbent for boron removal from water. Adsorption performance was governed by pH, initial concentration, dosage, and temperature, with maximum efficiency observed under acidic conditions (pH 4). HCl-activated kaolin exhibited slightly higher adsorption capacity than H₃PO₄-activated material. Equilibrium data fitted best to the Freundlich and Sips models, supporting multilayer adsorption on heterogeneous surfaces, while kinetics followed the pseudo-second-order model, indicating strong interactions between boron ions and surface sites. Thermodynamic analysis showed the process to be spontaneous and exothermic, and statistical optimization confirmed boron concentration and dosage as the most influential parameters. Tests on real water samples validated the laboratory findings, and compared with conventional adsorbents reported in literature, the modified kaolin materials demonstrated competitive or superior performance, underscoring their potential for practical water treatment applications.

Challenges and future perspectives

Despite the promising results, several challenges remain in optimizing and scaling the use of chemically activated kaolin for boron removal. One significant challenge is the potential interference of co-existing ions in real-world water samples, which may vary widely in concentration and composition. While this study addressed common ions such as Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺, Cl⁻, CO₃²⁻, and SO₄²⁻, further research is needed to explore the effects of more complex water chemistries, including organic matter and trace metals. Additionally, the exothermic and non-spontaneous nature of the adsorption process suggests limited efficiency at higher temperatures, which may restrict the adsorbent’s application in warmer climates or industrial effluents.

Future studies should focus on improving the thermal stability and adsorption performance of the material, possibly by integrating kaolin with other adsorbents or surface modifiers. Long-term assessments of the adsorbent’s regeneration capacity and cost-effectiveness at scale are also critical to ensuring its practical viability. Furthermore, the development of continuous-flow systems for industrial-scale applications could address challenges in batch processing and operational efficiency. Advancing the material’s performance while maintaining its low-cost and sustainable nature will be essential for maximizing its impact, particularly in water-scarce regions facing boron contamination challenges.
