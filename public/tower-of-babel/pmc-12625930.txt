Self-reported sexually transmitted infections among adults in South and Southeast Asian countries: a multilevel analysis of recent DHS data (2015–2023)
Authors: Kebede Gemeda Sabo, Kusse Urmale Mare, Begetayinoral Kussia Lahole, Beriso Furo Wengoro, Habtamu Solomon Demeke, Ausman Ahmed Mohamed, Mohammed Abdurahman Bilal, Abdulkerim Hassen Moloro
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveTo assess the pooled prevalence of self-reported sexually transmitted infections (STIs) and their associated factors among adults in South and Southeast Asia, using the Demographic and Health Survey data collected between 2015 and 2023.DesignA community-based cross-sectional study design was conducted using a multistage cluster sampling approach. Multilevel multivariable logistic regression analysis was employed to identify predictors of self-reported sexually transmitted infections (STIs). Model selection was guided by Akaike’s information criterion, and adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% CIs were estimated to determine statistically significant associations.SettingSouth and Southeast Asia.ParticipantsThis analysis included a weighted sample of 791 019 adults aged 15–49 who reported ever having had sexual intercourse. The majority of the participants were female (n=6 87 880; 87%), and most were from Southeast Asia (n=7 00 539; 89%).ResultsThe pooled prevalence of self-reported STIs among adults in South and Southeast Asia was 12.94% (95% CI 7.73% to 18.14%). At the individual level, higher odds of reporting STIs were associated with being female (AOR 1.84; 95% CI1.68 to 2.02), having middle (AOR 1.11; 95% CI 1.04 to 1.19) or high wealth status (AOR 1.15; 95% CI 1.07 to 1.24]), being employed (AOR 1.14; 95% CI 1.07 to 1.22), having multiple sexual partners (AOR 2.79; 95% CI 2.22 to 3.52) and having undergone HIV testing (AOR 1.10; 95% CI: 1.02 to 1.20). Conversely, lower odds of self-reported STIs were observed among individuals aged 35–39 years (AOR 0.78; 95% CI 0.66 to 0.92), 40–44 years (AOR 0.68; 95% CI 0.58 to 0.82) and 45–49 years (AOR 0.61; 95% CI 0.52 to 0.73); those who had ever been in a union (AOR 0.71; 95% CI 0.62 to 0.83); individuals with higher education (AOR 0.84; 95% CI 0.76 to 0.93); and those with comprehensive HIV knowledge (AOR 0.82; 95% CI 0.77 to 0.87). At the community level, high illiteracy rates (AOR 1.25; 95% CI 1.15 to 1.35) and high media non-exposure (AOR 1.11; 95% CI 1.02 to 1.20) were positively associated with STIs, while rural residence (AOR 0.81; 95% CI 0.74 to 0.89) and living in Southeast Asia (AOR: 0.47; 95% CI 0.42 to 0.53) were linked to lower odds of self-reported STIs.ConclusionsA substantial prevalence of self-reported STIs was observed among adults in South and Southeast Asia. Both individual- and community-level factors influence STI risk. The individual-level determinants include socio-demographic characteristics, sexual behaviours, HIV-related knowledge and testing history, while community-level factors reflect disparities in geographic location, educational attainment and media exposure.Keywords: Syphilis, Prevalence, Epidemiology, Public health, Cross-Sectional Studies

Introduction

Sexually transmitted infections (STIs), excluding HIV, are primarily acquired through sexual contact.1 2 They can also be transmitted from mother to child during pregnancy or childbirth, a mode known as vertical transmission.1 3 Common STIs include syphilis, bacterial vaginosis, hepatitis B, chlamydia, gonorrhoea and trichomoniasis.2 Moreover, research shows that having any of these common infections increases the risk of acquiring additional STIs, including HIV.3

Worldwide, untreated STIs remain a major public health concern, adversely affecting reproductive and sexual health.3 4 Beyond their immediate impact, STIs can lead to considerable long-term consequences. According to an analysis by the WHO and Sinka K, STIs increase the risk of contracting HIV, pelvic inflammatory disease, infertility, ectopic pregnancies and certain cancers.1 5 In addition, vertical transmission of STIs may result in stillbirth, prematurity, low birth weight, neonatal death, neonatal conjunctivitis, congenital anomalies and other related complications.5,7

The burden of STIs is particularly high in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), including regions such as South and Southeast Asia.6 8 Globally, about one million people contract curable STIs such as gonorrhoea, syphilis, trichomoniasis and chlamydia each day.1 7 9 In 2020, the estimated new incidence of treatable STI infections at the global level reached 374 million.10 Aside from curable STIs, other viral infections, such as genital herpes and hepatitis B infection, contribute significantly to morbidity and mortality. For instance, over 490 million individuals were living with genital herpes in 2016,11 and by 2022, more than 254 million people were infected with hepatitis B infection, resulting in nearly one million deaths due to related complications.1

Previous studies have documented the varying prevalence of sSTIs across different regions of the world. For example, scholarly papers carried out in Sub-Saharan African (SSA) countries have reported a wide range of STI prevalence among adults, from 3% to 25% in country-specific studies12,16 and from 3.8% to 6.9% in multi-country analyses.17 18 Likewise, the STI burden among adult populations in Asia remains substantial, with prevalence estimates ranging from 3.55% to 16%.710 19,21

A wide range of factors has been shown to increase the risk of acquiring STIs among adults. These include socio-demographic characteristics such as age,12,1517 18 21 place of residence,15 17 18 22 marital status,12 16 18 educational attainment,12 18 media exposure,13 18 22 employment status18 and household wealth.15 18 21 In addition, individual behaviours and health-related factors such as HIV testing status,14 15 comprehensive HIV knowledge,17 smoking,16 having multiple sexual partners,13,1518 22 condom use22 and age at first sexual intercourse13 14 18 are reported as having a significant association with STIs.

Despite substantial efforts by the WHO and other stakeholders to reduce the global impact of STIs,23,27 STIs remain a significant public health concern and are not declining at a sufficient rate.6 28 29 Moreover, although the Southeast Asia region has demonstrated some progress,30 overall efforts to control STIs have stagnated,31 and several countries in the region lack reliable data to monitor changes in the epidemiological landscape of STIs.32 Furthermore, recent evidence points to a global increase in the burden of STIs.29 33 These trends highlight the urgent need for strengthened epidemiological surveillance and improved data utilisation systems to effectively track regional progress towards achieving the global STI targets 2030.34

Moreover, previous studies in the region have primarily focused on specific countries or sub-populations, such as male or female adults, without adequately accounting for regional- and community-level factors alongside socio-demographic and behavioural factors.19,21 Additionally, prior research has highlighted that key subpopulations, including female sex workers and men who have sex with men (MSM),35 are disproportionately affected by STIs in the region. However, despite these insights, a comprehensive understanding of STI prevalence and its determinants among the general adult population remains limited. Therefore, examining self-reported STIs and their associated factors among adults in South and Southeast Asia is essential to inform policymakers about regional STI burden and to support the development of effective, context-specific public health interventions. In this study, we applied a multilevel mixed-effects analysis to Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) data collected between 2015 and 2023 across the included countries.

Methods

Study design

A community-based cross-sectional study design was conducted.

Source population and eligibility criteria

According to the DHS, the source population for this study comprised sexually active adults from the countries included in the analysis who were within the reproductive age range (15 years and older).

In addition, individuals who had ever engaged in sexual intercourse and responded to questions regarding STI symptoms or diagnosis within the 12 months preceding the survey were eligible for inclusion.

Sampling method

The DHS programme employs a stratified two-stage sampling design to recruit survey participants. In the first stage, the enumeration areas (EAs), also referred to as primary sampling units, are selected based on the most recent census data of each country. In the second stage, households within the selected EAs are randomly chosen. Then, eligible individuals from these selected households are invited to participate in the interview process.

Survey instruments

In the DHS programme, data on self-reported STIs are collected through individual questionnaires administered separately to females and males. The questionnaire includes the following three items: (1) have you had an STI in the past 12 months?, (2) have you experienced abnormal genital discharge in the past 12 months? and (3) have you had a genital sore or ulcer in the past 12 months? Each question offers three response options: ‘Yes’, ‘No’ and ‘Don’t know’.

Data source

The data used for this analysis were obtained from the official website of the DHS programme (http://dhsprogram.com).

Data extraction and management of missing observations

Before data extraction, we identified countries with DHS datasets from surveys conducted between 2015 and 2023, as our objective was to include only those with data collected during this period. Initially, 11 countries met this criterion. However, Bangladesh and the Philippines were excluded because their datasets contained information solely on ever-married women and lacked corresponding male data. Consequently, the final analysis included data from nine countries: Afghanistan, India, Indonesia, Cambodia, Myanmar, Maldives, Nepal, Pakistan and Timor-Leste.

To generate a single pooled dataset for this study, we initially extracted variables relevant to the analysis from both the Male Records and female Individual Records datasets of each country included. These datasets were then merged using key identifiers, specifically V001 and V002, with the female dataset serving as the base. After merging, we appended the datasets from the nine South and Southeast Asian countries selected according to their respective survey years.

Furthermore, missing responses for variables related to the outcome were handled in accordance with the Guide to DHS Statistics, version 8, as described on page 624.36 Specifically, observations with missing or ‘don’t know’ responses regarding signs or symptoms of STIs in the 12 months preceding the survey were treated as not having self-reported STIs. Furthermore, as a measure of robustness of our finding, we performed sensitivity analysis for the outcome variable by excluding uncertain responses (ie, missing and don’t know). This allowed us to examine how participants with uncertain responses influenced the magnitude of the outcome variable.

For independent variables, we applied complete case analysis, including only participants with non-missing values for all relevant variables, allowing missing values in variables used to construct the outcome variable. We also conducted a sensitivity analysis for covariates comparing the complete-case model, which includes all covariates, and a reduced model that contained only independent variables with minimal missingness (<2%). This enabled us to evaluate whether excluding participants with missing covariate data affected the significance and direction of association.

Moreover, to account for the complex survey design, non-response and to ensure comparability of the findings across countries, sample weighting was applied for our analysis. Following DHS programme recommendations, the sampling weight variable (ie, v005) was rescaled by dividing by 1 000 000. This yielded a weighted sample of 791 019 adults aged 15–49 years who had ever engaged in sexual intercourse, which served as the analytic sample. Additionally, the Svyset command was used to account for the complex survey design, incorporating the rescaled weight, primary sampling unit (v021) and sampling strata (v022). A detailed description of the DHS methodology, guidelines and procedures for handling missing data is available on the DHS website.37

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Study variables

Dependent variable

The dependent variable in this study was self-reported STIs among sexually active adults, measured as a dichotomous outcome (‘No’/‘Yes’). This variable was assessed based on participants’ self-reported experience of STI or STI-related symptoms in the 12 months preceding the survey. Accordingly, in the DHS, respondents were asked three questions regarding whether they had experienced any of the following in the past 12 months preceding the survey: (1) any STI, (2) genital discharge and (3) genital sore or ulcer. Each question offered three response options: ‘Yes’, ‘No’ or ‘Don’t know’. A positive response to any of these questions was coded as ‘Yes’ for self-reported STI, while a negative response, either ‘No’ or ‘Don’t know’, was coded as ‘No’ for self-reported STI in accordance with the DHS guideline.

Independent variables

In this study, independent variables were categorised into individual- and community-level factors. Individual-level factors included sex, age, marital status, educational attainment, wealth index, employment status and media exposure. The behavioural and health-related variables assessed in the study included contraceptive use, age at first sexual encounter, multiple sexual partners, history of HIV testing, health insurance coverage, awareness of HIV/AIDS, awareness of STIs and comprehensive knowledge of HIV. To address socio-economic disparities, the wealth index was recategorised into three groups: ‘poor’ (0) for households in the lowest two wealth quintiles, ‘middle’ (1) for those in the middle quintile and ‘rich’ (2) for households in the top two quintiles.

In this study, contraceptive use refers to the reported use of any current contraceptive method at the time of interview. Male and female respondents answered separate questionnaires regarding their own or their partner’s contraceptive use.

In the current study, multiple sexual partnership was defined as having two or more sexual partners within the 12 months preceding the survey, consistent with DHS guidelines. This measure was based on participants’ self-reported number of sexual partners, including their spouses.

Participants’ media exposure was assessed based on the frequency of engagement with newspapers, radio and television. Individuals who reported reading a newspaper, listening to the radio or watching television at least once per week were classified as having mass media exposure (coded as 1). Those who did not engage in any of these activities weekly were classified as having insufficient mass media exposure (coded as 0).38

Comprehensive knowledge of HIV was categorised using five survey questions focused on prevention methods and common misconceptions. Participants who answered all five questions correctly were classified as having comprehensive HIV knowledge (coded as ‘yes’), while those who did not meet this criterion were classified as having insufficient knowledge (coded as ‘no’).39

In this study, community-level variables included geographic region, place of residence, media non-exposure and illiteracy. Moreover, community-level illiteracy and media non-exposure were quantified by aggregating individual-level data at the cluster level and subsequently categorised as ‘low’ or ‘high’ based on median values. In the DHS, the term ‘clusters’ refers to the primary sampling units, often called EAs or communities, which represent the smallest geographic units used for sampling within each country.

Countries were grouped into two geographic regions: South Asia and Southeast Asia. The South Asia region comprised Afghanistan, India, the Maldives, Nepal and Pakistan, while Southeast Asia included Indonesia, Cambodia, Myanmar and Timor-Leste.

Data management and statistical analysis

This study used DHS data for all included countries, obtained with permission from the DHS Programme via its official website. Using STATA version 17, we processed and cleaned each country’s dataset, addressing inconsistencies and missing information. The cleaned datasets were then merged into a single pooled dataset. Variables were selected based on prior research, and the dataset was restricted to the target population, sexually active adults aged 15–49 years who had ever engaged in sexual intercourse. To preserve sample representativeness and ensure accurate estimates, sampling weights were applied. Descriptive statistics were summarised using frequencies and percentages.

Moreover, to identify the best-fitting fixed-effect model, we constructed four models. The first (null) model included only the outcome variable, self-reported STI, to estimate random variability in the intercept. The second and third models incorporated community- and individual-level variables, respectively. The fourth model (ie, full model) included both individual- and community-level variables. Model fit was assessed using the Akaike information criterion (AIC), and the fourth model with the lowest AIC value of 111 691.8 was selected as the best-fitting model.

In addition, binary logistic regression was performed, and variables with a p value less than 0.25 were included in the multivariable fixed-effects logistic regression model to examine associations between the outcome and independent variables. We also conducted multicollinearity diagnostics among predictor variables, using a cut-off value of 10. Accordingly, the generalised variance inflation factor for categorical variables with more than two levels and the variance inflation factor for binary variables ranged from 1.02 to 1.85, with a mean of 1.28, indicating that multicollinearity was not a concern.

Furthermore, random-effects analyses were conducted to assess variations in self-reported STIs among adults across Primary Sampling Units (PSUs), also referred to as EAs or communities, which represent the smallest geographic units used in DHS sampling. In addition, the ‘country’ was included as a random-effect variable in the multilevel mixed-effects logistic regression model to account for country-level variation in STI prevalence. This approach allowed us to estimate the pooled effects while adjusting for unobserved heterogeneities across countries.

To evaluate differences across PSUs within countries, we employed the intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC), median odds ratio (MOR) and proportion change in variance (PCV). The ICC quantified the proportion of total variation in self-reported STIs attributed to differences between clusters. The MOR assessed disparities in self-reported STI prevalence between high- and low-risk communities. Moreover, the PCV was used to measure the reduction in the variance of self-reported STIs after incorporating individual- or community-level variables into the model.

Explanatory variables with a p value less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant in the multivariable mixed-effects logistic regression analysis. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs), along with 95% CIs, were used to assess the strength and direction of association between the explanatory variables and outcome, while controlling for potential confounders. Besides, to minimise bias related to selection, measurement and interviewer effects, the DHS programme employs a stratified two-stage cluster sampling design, uses a standardised data collection instrument and provides comprehensive training for data collectors.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics

The majority of the study participants were female, totalling 687 880 individuals (87%). Most of the study participants, 776 072 (98%), had ever been in a union. Moreover, the largest portion of participants fell within the 25–39 age group, accounting for 426 870 individuals (54%). Regarding educational attainment, 345 145 participants (44%) had completed secondary education, while 96 963 (12%) had attended higher education. In this study, 321 511 adults (41%) belonged to the higher wealth quantile, and 454 875 (58%) were exposed to media. In addition, 526 513 participants (67%) resided in rural areas, and 700 539 (89%) were from the Southeast Asia region (see table 1).

Pooled prevalence of self-reported STIs

The pooled prevalence of self-reported STIs among adults was 12.94% (95% CI 7.73% to 18.14%). The highest prevalence was observed in Pakistan at 30.66% (95% CI 30.00% to 31.32%), while the lowest was recorded in India at 2.70% (95% CI 2.65% to 2.74%). However, a moderate level of statistical heterogeneity was detected (I²=43.7, p=0.03), indicating the presence of moderate variation across countries included in this study (see figure 1).

Model evaluation and results

The analysis revealed that self-reported STIs among adults in the study countries were significantly influenced by both individual- and community-level factors.

The null model indicated that 55.78% of the variation in self-reported STIs among adults in the region could be attributed to differences among clusters, underscoring the substantial role of community-level factors in shaping self-reported STIs.

In the full model, the PCV showed that the inclusion of both individual- and community-level covariates reduced the variance in self-reported STIs at the community level by 67%. In addition, the value of the median odds ratio (MOR) demonstrated that adults from clusters with the highest prevalence of self-reported STIs were 3.04 times more likely to report an STI compared with those from clusters with the lowest prevalence. This suggests that, even when individual characteristics are similar, the likelihood of reporting an STI is significantly higher for individuals residing in a high-prevalence community. The ICC for the full model was 29.4%, indicating that community-level factors contributed meaningfully to the observed variation in self-reported STIs across the region. Additionally, the final model (model IV), having the lowest AIC value of 111 691.8, was identified as the best-fitting model for this analysis. Determinants of self-reported STIs among adults in South and Southeast Asian countries

This study identified several factors associated with self-reported STIs among adults in the study population. At the individual level, variables such as sex, wealth status, employment status, having multiple sexual partners and HIV testing history were positively associated with self-reported STIs. In contrast, variables such as age, educational attainment, marital status and comprehensive HIV knowledge were negatively associated with STI reporting. At the community level, higher illiteracy rates and limited media exposure were positively associated with self-reported STIs, while rural residence and geographic region were negatively associated.

In this analysis, female adults were 1.84 times more likely to report STIs (AOR 1.84; 95% CI 1.68 to 2.02) compared with male participants. Compared with individuals classified as poor, those in the middle (AOR 1.11; 95% CI 1.04 to 1.19) and rich (AOR 1.15; 95% CI (1.07 to 1.24) were 1.11 and 1.15 times more likely to report STIs, respectively. Similarly, employed adults (AOR 1.14; 95% CI (1.07 to 1.22), those with multiple sexual partners (AOR 2.79; 95% CI (2.22 to 3.52) and those who had ever undergone HIV testing (AOR 1.10; 95% CI (1.02 to 1.20) had higher odds of reporting STIs than their respective counterparts.

Conversely, adults aged 35–39 (AOR 0.78; 95% CI (0.66 to 0.92), 40–44 (AOR 0.68; 95% CI (0.58 to 0.82) and 45–49 (AOR 0.61; 95% CI (0.52 to 0.73) had 22%, 32% and 39% less likely to report STIs, respectively. Likewise, adults who had ever been in a marital or cohabiting union (AOR 0.71; 95% CI (0.62 to 0.83), those with higher educational attainment (AOR 0.84; 95% CI (0.76 to 0.93) and those with comprehensive HIV knowledge (AOR 0.82; 95% CI (0.77 to 0.87) had 29%, 16% and 18% lower odds of reporting STIs, respectively, compared with their reference groups.

Regarding community-level factors, individuals from communities with high illiteracy rates (AOR 1.25; 95% CI (1.15 to 1.35) were 25% more likely to report STIs compared with those from communities with low illiteracy rates. Similarly, adults from communities with high levels of media non-exposure (AOR 1.11; 95% CI (1.02 to 1.20) were 11% more likely to report STIs compared with those from communities with greater media exposure.

In contrast, adults residing in rural areas (AOR: 0.81; 95% CI (0.74 to 0.89) and those living in Southeast Asia (AOR: 0.47; 95% CI (0.42 to 0.53) were 19% and 53% less likely to report STIs, respectively, compared with those living in urban areas and South Asia (see online supplemental table 4).

Moreover, to assess the robustness of our findings using alternative approaches to handling missing covariate data, we compared a complete case model (n=1 68 298) with a reduced model (n=2 95 957) that included only covariates with minimal missingness (<2%). Both analyses excluded the participants with uncertain responses (ie, missing or don’t know) for the outcome variable. Accordingly, the reduced model explained slightly more variation in the outcome (R2=0.019) than the complete case model (R2=0.017), but both models were consistent with respect to the direction and magnitude of association between covariates and self-reported STI. This finding shows that the observed associations are not driven solely by the exclusion of participants with missing data (see table 4).

Discussion

This study examined the pooled prevalence and determinants of self-reported STIs among adults in South and Southeast Asia. The overall pooled prevalence of self-reported STI was 12.94% (95% CI 7.73% to 18.14%). This estimate aligns with findings from Bangladesh reported by Hunda et al.21 However, it is higher than the prevalence reported in India,20 Senegal12 and the pooled estimate for SSA.17 On the contrary, the finding is lower than that observed in Sierra Leone,15 Morocco40 and Ghana.16 These discrepancies may be attributed to differences in sample sizes and the study populations. For instance, the studies carried out in India and Senegal focused exclusively on female participants, while the present study included both sexes.

This analysis also found that female participants were more likely to report STIs than male participants, consistent with findings from Southern India.41 This disparity may be explained by women’s greater awareness of STIs, which is often linked to higher utilisation of healthcare services, particularly those related to sexual and reproductive health.42 Moreover, biological susceptibility due to anatomical differences in the genitourinary tract may contribute to the increased risk among women. It is also important to note that the predominance of female participants in our sample may have influenced this result.

In line with studies conducted by Sales et al43 and Abbai et al,44 this study found that the likelihood of reporting an STI decreases with advancing age, underscoring the greater vulnerability of individuals in early adulthood. This might be attributed to the higher likelihood of engaging in risky sexual behaviours among young adults compared with their older counterparts.45 However, contrary to our findings, studies conducted in Sierra Leone15 and Senegal12 reported that the probability of self-reported STIs increases with age. This inconsistency may be explained by geographical differences in sexual behaviour and access to healthcare services. In Sierra Leone, for instance, the higher prevalence of self-reported STIs among older adults may be due to delayed healthcare-seeking behaviour and cumulative exposure over time. Conversely, in South and Southeast Asia, younger adults may report higher STI rates due to more frequent engagement in high-risk sexual activities than older individuals.46

Consistent with previous research by Huda et al21 and Hutton-Nyameaye et al,16 this study found that adults who had ever been in a marital union were less likely to report STIs compared with those who had never been married. A possible explanation is that individuals in marital unions may be more committed to maintaining monogamous relationships, thereby reducing their exposure to STI risk. This finding highlights the importance of targeting unmarried adults in public health initiatives for STI prevention and control.

Nevertheless, in contrast to our findings, studies done in Senegal12 and across 27 SSA countries18 reported a higher likelihood of self-reported STIs among adults who had never been in a union. This discrepancy may be due to geographical differences and variations in the demographic profiles of the study participants. Notably, the aforementioned studies were conducted in African settings, whereas the current study focused on adults from South and Southeast Asia.

This study found that adults with higher educational attainment were less likely to report STIs compared with those lacking formal education. This finding aligns with studies carried out by Winarto et al,47 Slurink et al,48 Dadzie et al18 and Senkoro et al49 in Indonesia, the Netherlands, SSA and Tanzania, respectively. A plausible explanation is that higher education exposes individuals to diverse sources of information on sexual health, enabling them to make more evidence-based decisions about their sexual well-being. However, inconsistent with our study findings, studies carried out in Senegal,12 Ethiopia50 and Malawi51 reported a higher likelihood of STIs among individuals with higher educational attainment.

Contrary to research from Bangladesh21 and India,52 but in line with a study from Africa,18 our study revealed that individuals with higher wealth status (ie, middle and rich) and those who were employed had higher odds of reporting STIs relative to adults with lower wealth status and those who were unemployed. One possible explanation is that adults with higher economic means may have increased access to several media and technological sources, which could expose them to explicit sexual material and promote exploratory behaviour.38 53 Additionally, financial advantage may boost their capability to attract partners and engage in risky sexual practices.54

This study also found that participants with a history of HIV testing had higher odds of reporting STIs compared with those who had never been tested. Similar findings were reported in studies from Papua New Guinea14 and Sierra Leone.15 This underlines the importance of integrating HIV and STI services within broader sexual and reproductive health frameworks, as recommended by the WHO.55

Although only 0.25% of adult participants reported having multiple sexual partners, consistent with previous studies,14 15 22 our findings showed that this group had significantly higher odds of reporting STI symptoms compared with those with fewer partners. This finding underscores the need for targeted public health interventions focused on this group with higher risk exposure to effectively address the burden and consequences of STIs.

Furthermore, in line with prior research,17 our study revealed that participants with comprehensive knowledge of HIV were less likely to report STI symptoms than those participants lacking such knowledge. This may be attributed to greater awareness of the risk associated with HIV and other STIs, which empowers individuals to adopt protective behaviour. These findings reinforce the importance of comprehensive sexual education in encouraging protective behaviours against STIs.56

In this study, individuals residing in rural areas and Southeast Asia reported more STI symptoms compared with those living in urban areas and in South Asia, respectively. These findings are consistent with prior research reports emphasising the influences of geographical and regional factors on health outcomes.15 17 18 This finding underlines the necessity to consider differences in STI predisposing factors, access to healthcare services and social interactions through different regions while implementing public health interventions and supports the findings of prior scholars.57 58

Finally, our study showed that adults from communities with high illiteracy rates and limited media exposure have a higher likelihood of reporting STI symptoms than those from communities with lower illiteracy and greater media access. This may be due to restricted access to health-related information in underserved communities, including STI awareness. These results highlight the need for targeted public health initiatives aimed at improving education and media access to enhance sexual and reproductive health awareness in underserved communities.59 60

Limitations

Despite the significant implications of this study, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, as participants were asked to self-report their STI status over the preceding 12 months, the data may be subject to recall and social desirability bias, potentially resulting in an underestimation of STI prevalence. Second, the cross-sectional design of the study limits the ability to infer causal relationships over time. Third, the lack of information on sexual orientation and gender of sexual partners in the DHS datasets limits the ability to assess STI risk among key populations, such as MSM. Fourth, although the pooled analysis provided a useful summary, it revealed moderate statistical heterogeneity and did not account for population diversity. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with caution. Future analysis incorporating population-size weighting may yield more accurate regional estimates. In addition, the prevalence estimates may be sensitive to assumptions about missing and do-not-know responses. While our initial analysis assumed uncertain cases (ie, missing and don’t know), our sensitivity analysis revealed a higher pooled prevalence when these cases were excluded (see figure 2).

Lastly, although ethnicity is potentially an important individual-level variable, it was excluded from the pooled analysis due to data limitations and inconsistent categorisation across countries. Data were available only for Afghanistan, India and Nepal, with substantial variation in definitions and reporting formats across these countries. Thus, to avoid bias from arbitrary grouping, ethnicity was not modelled. Future studies with harmonised ethnicity data across countries may help to clarify its role in STI risk. Online supplemental table 6 presents its distribution in the three countries with available data.

Conclusion

This study identified a substantial prevalence of self-reported STIs among adults in South and Southeast Asia. Both individual- and community-level factors were found to significantly influence STI prevalence. At the individual level, factors such as sex, age, marital status, educational attainment, wealth status, employment status, multiple sexual partnerships, HIV testing history and comprehensive HIV knowledge were associated with self-reported STIs. At the community level, variables such as place of residence, geographic region, illiteracy rates and limited media exposure were significantly associated with self-reported STIs. These findings underline the complex interplay of individual and contextual determinants of STI risk and the need for targeted context-specific public health interventions.
