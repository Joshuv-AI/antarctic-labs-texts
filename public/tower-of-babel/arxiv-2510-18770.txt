Long paths need not minimize H H -colorings among trees
David Galvin, Emily McMillon, JD Nir, Amanda Redlich

Abstract
Given a graph G G and a target graph H H , an H H -coloring of G G is an adjacency-preserving vertex map from G G to H H . By appropriate choice of H H , these colorings can express, for instance, the independent sets or proper vertex colorings of G G . Sidorenko proved that for any H H , the n n -vertex star admits at least as many H H -colorings as any other n n -vertex tree, but the minimization question remains open in general. For many graphs H H , path graphs are among the trees with the fewest H H -colorings, but work of Leontovich and subsequently Csikvári and Lin shows that there is a graph E 7 E_{7} on seven vertices and a target graph H H for which there are strictly fewer H H -colorings of E 7 E_{7} than of the path on seven vertices. We introduce a new strategy for enumerating homomorphisms from path-like trees to highly symmetric target graphs that allows us to make the previous observations completely explicit and extend them to infinitely many n n beyond n = 7 n=7 . In particular, we exhibit a target graph H H with the property that for each sufficiently large n n , there is a tree E n E_{n} on n n vertices that admits strictly fewer H H -colorings than the path on n n vertices.

2
Automorphic similarity and path-like families

In this section we present a strategy for efficiently calculating the number of
H
-colorings of certain families of graphs by using the concept of automorphic similarity, which we now define.

Let
H
be a target graph, possibly with loops. For vertices
u
and
v
in
H
, we say
u
and
v
are
automorphically similar
if there is an automorphism of
H
that sends
u
to
v
. Automorphic similarity defines an equivalence relation on
V(H)
, and we call the equivalence classes of that relation the
automorphic similarity classes of
H
. We denote these equivalence classes as
H^{1},\ldots,H^{k}
, where
k
is the number of automorphism orbits of
V(H)
. The partition
V(H)=\bigcup_{i}H^{i}
, sometimes referred to as the
orbit partition
, is an instance of an
equitable partition
of the graph
H
(see e.g.
[
11
, Section 9.3]
). This is a partition with the property that for any two classes
A,B
(not necessarily distinct) there is a constant
c(A,B)
with the property that every vertex in
A
has
c(A,B)
neighbors in
B
. For completeness, we present the next lemma, which verifies that
\bigcup_{i}H^{i}
is an equitable partition.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
H
be a graph, possibly with loops, and let
H^{1},\ldots,H^{k}
be the automorphic similarity classes of
H
. For any
1\leq i\leq k
, any
u,v\in H^{i}
, and any
1\leq j\leq k
,

|N(u)\cap H^{j}|=|N(v)\cap H^{j}|.

Proof.

Let
\varphi
be an automorphism of
V(H)
that sends
u
to
v
. Then
\varphi
maps each
w\in N(u)\cap H^{j}
to some
w^{\prime}
. As
w\sim u
, we see
w^{\prime}\sim v
, and as
w\in H^{j}
and
\varphi(w)=w^{\prime}
we see
w
and
w^{\prime}
are automorphically similar, so
w^{\prime}\in H^{j}
as well. We have shown
|N(u)\cap H^{j}|\leq|N(v)\cap H^{j}|
. Repeating the analysis with
\varphi^{-1}
, we see
|N(u)\cap H^{j}|\geq|N(v)\cap H^{j}|
and so equality holds.
∎

Lemma

2.1
permits us to define the numbers
m_{i,j}
to be the number of neighbors any
v\in H^{i}
has in
H^{j}
. The
automorphic similarity matrix
of
H
(relative to the particular order put on the automorphic similarity classes) is the
k\times k
matrix
M=M(H)
whose
(i,j)
-entry is
m_{i,j}
. This matrix is also referred to as the
quotient matrix
of the adjacency matrix of
H
relative to the equitable partition
\bigcup_{i}H^{i}
.

When
H
is highly symmetric, the automorphic similarity matrix of
H
is much smaller than the adjacency matrix and thus easier to analyze. When
T
is a tree, the automorphic similarity matrix, together with the sizes of the automorphic similarity classes, contains enough information to compute
\hom(T,H)
. This general fact is proved in
[
10
]
, but is not needed in this paper. Here we work only with families of path-like trees (to be defined shortly), and
Theorem

2.3
below gives us all that we need in terms of counting
H
-colorings of these trees.

For a graph
G
with designated vertex
v
, let
\Hom_{v\to i}(G,H)
denote the set of
H
-colorings of
G
in which
v
is mapped to some (arbitrary but specific) representative of
H^{i}
. We use
\hom_{v\to i}(G,H)
for the number of such
H
-colorings, and we define
\mathbf{h}(G,v)
to be the column vector whose
i^{\text{th}}
entry is
\hom_{v\to i}(G,H)
.

Although
\Hom_{v\to i}(G,H)
clearly depends on the choice of representative, we show in the following lemma that
\hom_{v\to i}(G,H)
does not. This allows us to extract crucial information from the automorphic similarity matrix.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
H
be a graph, possibly with loops, and let
G
be an arbitrary graph with distinguished vertex
v
. Suppose
w
and
w^{\prime}
are automorphically similar vertices in
H
. Let
{\mathcal{G}}
be the set of
H
-colorings of
G
in which
v
is sent to
w
, and let
{\mathcal{G}}^{\prime}
be the set of
H
-colorings of
G
in which
v
is sent to
w^{\prime}
. Then
|{\mathcal{G}}|=|{\mathcal{G}}^{\prime}|
.

Proof.

Let
\varphi
be an automorphism of
H
that sends
w
to
w^{\prime}
, which exists as
w
and
w^{\prime}
are automorphically similar. Then the function mapping
f
to
f\circ\varphi
is a bijection mapping
{\mathcal{G}}
to
{\mathcal{G}}^{\prime}
.
∎

We now define the families of graphs that will be our main concern, and then state and prove our main technical theorem. Let
G_{0}
be a graph (not necessarily a tree, although in each of our applications
G_{0}
will be a tree) and fix a vertex
v_{0}\in G_{0}
. For
n\geq 0
, define
G_{n}
to be the graph formed by appending a path with
n
new vertices to
v_{0}
. We refer to the family of graphs
\{G_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
as a
path-like family
of graphs, and we call the initial graph
G_{0}
the
seed graph
of the family. Note that by taking
G_{0}
to be a single vertex, we see the set of all paths form a path-like family.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
H
be a target graph, possibly with loops, and let
H^{1},\ldots,H^{k}
be the automorphic similarity classes of
H
, with
M
the associated automorphic similarity matrix. Denote by
\mathbf{a}(H)
the row vector whose
i^{\text{th}}
entry is
|H^{i}|
. Let
G_{0}
be a graph, fix a vertex
v_{0}\in G_{0}
, and let
\{G_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
be the path-like family with seed graph
G_{0}
and fixed vertex
v_{0}
.
Then

\hom(G_{n},H)=\mathbf{a}(H)M^{n}\mathbf{h}(G_{0},v_{0}).

Proof.

For
n\geq 1
, let
v_{n}
be the unique vertex in
V(G_{n})\setminus V(G_{n-1})
, the leaf at the end of the path extending from
v_{0}
. By
Lemma

2.2
,
\hom_{v_{n}\to i}(G_{n},H)
is independent of the choice of representative of
H^{i}
. By partitioning the
H
-colorings of
G_{n}
by the
H^{i}
to which we send
v_{n}
, we can write

\hom(G_{n},H)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}|H^{i}|\hom_{v_{n}\to i}(G_{n},H)=\mathbf{a}(H)\mathbf{h}(G_{n},v_{n}).

(3)

Note that equation (
3
) is valid for all
n\geq 0
, and at
n=0
it gives us our claimed expression for
\hom(G_{0},H)
.

For
n\geq 1
, we can further partition the
H
-colorings of
G_{n}
in which
v_{n}
is sent to
H^{i}
by the automorphic similarity class to which
v_{n-1}
is sent, where
v_{n-1}
is the unique neighbor of
v_{n}
in
G_{n}
. More specifically, we can map
v_{n-1}
to one of
m_{i,j}
vertices in
H^{j}
and then color
G_{n-1}
in
\hom_{v_{n-1}\to j}(G_{n-1},H)
ways. Summing over all choices of
j
, we see

\hom_{v_{n}\to i}(G_{n},H)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}m_{i,j}\hom_{v_{n-1}\to j}(G_{n-1},H)

or
\mathbf{h}(G_{n},v_{n})=M\mathbf{h}(G_{n-1},v_{n-1})

Applying this idea recursively gives
\mathbf{h}(G_{n},v_{n})=M^{n}\mathbf{h}(G_{0},v_{0})
. Plugging this equation into equation (
3
) gives

\hom(G_{n},H)=\mathbf{a}(H)M^{n}\mathbf{h}(G_{0},v_{0}),

(4)

as claimed.
∎

One consequence of
Theorem

2.3
is that if
G_{n}
is a member of a path-like family then the number of
H
-colorings of
G_{n}
can be written as a linear combination of
n^{\text{th}}
powers of the eigenvalues of
M
, the automorphic similarity matrix of
H
. The details are given in
Corollary

2.4
, which we now state and prove.

Corollary 2.4
.

Let
H
be a target graph, possibly with loops. Let
G_{0}
be a graph, fix a vertex
v_{0}\in G_{0}
, and let
\{G_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
be the path-like family with seed graph
G_{0}
and fixed vertex
v_{0}
. There are real numbers
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k}
dependent only on
H
, and real numbers
c_{1},\ldots,c_{k}
dependent on
G_{0}
,
v_{0}
, and
H
such that

\hom(G_{n},H)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}c_{i}\lambda_{i}^{n}.

Proof.

The automorphic similarity matrix
M
is not necessarily symmetric, but it is similar to a symmetric matrix. Indeed, let
B={\rm diag}(\sqrt{h_{1}},\sqrt{h_{2}},\ldots,\sqrt{h_{k}})
where
h_{i}=|H^{i}|
. The
(i,j)
-entry of
BMB^{-1}
is
\sqrt{h_{i}}\,m_{i,j}/\sqrt{h_{j}}
, so symmetry is implied by the equality

\frac{\sqrt{h_{i}}\,m_{i,j}}{\sqrt{h_{j}}}=\frac{\sqrt{h_{j}}\,m_{j,i}}{\sqrt{h_{i}}}

or equivalently
h_{i}m_{i,j}=h_{j}m_{j,i}
. This equality holds because both sides count the number of edges between
H^{i}
and
H^{j}
. Since
BMB^{-1}
is real and symmetric, it is diagonalizable, and so
M
is diagonalizable, say via
M=ADA^{-1}
with
D=\diag(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k})
, all
\lambda_{i}
real. Applying this identity to the count given in
Theorem

2.3
gives

\hom(G_{n},H)=\mathbf{a}(H)AD^{n}A^{-1}\mathbf{h}(G_{0},v_{0}).

The right-hand side can be written as a linear combination of the
\lambda_{i}^{n}
. The coefficient
c_{i}
of
\lambda_{i}^{n}
is determined by
\mathbf{a}(H)
and
A
, which depends on
H
, and by
\mathbf{h}(G_{0},v_{0})
, which depends on
G_{0}
,
v_{0}
, and
H
, so the result follows.
∎

By diagonalizing
M
, we have expressed the number of
H
-colorings of path-like families as a sum of a linear (in
k
) number of terms, each of which is exponential in
n
. When
n
is sufficiently large, that sum is dominated by the largest (in magnitude) eigenvalues whose coefficients are non-zero. This allows for straightforward comparisons of the asymptotic behavior of
H
-colorings of path-like different families.

.

4
Open problems

We conclude with a few questions that remain open and which we find of interest. Csikvári and Lin raise the following natural problem
[
6
, Problem 6.2]
:

Problem 4.1

(Csikvári, Lin)
.

Characterize those
H
for which
\hom(P_{n},H)\leq\hom(T_{n},H)
holds for all
n
and all
T_{n}\in{\mathcal{T}}_{n}
.

They also propose a weaker variant of
4.1
in which “all
n
” is replaced by “all sufficiently large
n
”. In the present paper we have shown that there are
H
’s which do not satisfy the condition imposed by the weaker variant of
4.1
. Settling the problem in general remains open.

It is possible to use
Theorem

2.3
to confirm that
E_{7}
is the tree on seven vertices which admits the fewest
T(18,3,32)
-colorings. We proved in
Theorem

1.4
that
E_{n}
admits fewer
\widehat{T}(400,3,800)
-colorings than
P_{n}
when
n
is sufficiently large, but it remains open which tree on
n
vertices admits the fewest
\widehat{T}(400,3,800)
-colorings.

More generally, we note that both
E_{n}
and
P_{n}
have large diameters. If
T_{n}
has diameter 2, then
T_{n}
must be a star graph and so
\hom(T_{n},H)\geq\hom(P_{n},H)
by
Theorem

1.1
. This leads to the following question:

Problem 4.2
.

For
H=\widehat{T}(400,3,800)
, or any other graph, what is the largest function
d_{H}(n)
for which all trees of diameter at most
d_{H}(n)
admit at least as many
H
-colorings as
P_{n}
?

In
Theorem

1.4
we exhibited a target graph
H
on
78
vertices that is Leontovich at some
n
(specifically
n=7
). By contrast, in
[
10
]
the authors prove that if
H
has fewer than four vertices, it is not Leontovich for any
n
. This suggests the question:

Problem 4.3
.

What is the smallest value of
m
such that there exists a target graph
H
on
m
vertices that is Leontovich at some
n
?

At present, we know
4\leq m\leq 78
, but narrowing this range is of interest. The range of possible values of the order of the smallest strongly Leontovich graph is even larger: combining
Theorem

1.4
with the results of
[
10
]
demonstrates it must be between
4
and
|\widehat{T}(400,3,800)|=961601
, though no attempt was made to minimize the order of the strongly Leontovich target graph in
Theorem

1.4
. We note, for example, that
\widehat{T}(313,3,646)
(which has 607847 vertices) numerically appears to be strongly Leontovich.

Finally, recall from the introduction that Csikvári and Lin
[
6
]
showed that for any connected
H
and any sequence
(T_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of trees
T_{n}
with
T_{n}\in{\mathcal{T}}_{n}
we have

\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(\frac{\hom(T_{n},H)}{\hom(P_{n},H)}\right)^{1/n}\geq 1.

This does not rule out the possibility that
\hom(T_{n},H)<\hom(P_{n},H)
infinitely often, and indeed in
Theorem

1.4
we find a connected target graph
H
and a sequence of trees
(T_{n})_{n\geq 4}
with
T_{n}\in{\mathcal{T}}(n)
with

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\hom(T_{n},H)}{\hom(P_{n},H)}=\frac{c^{\prime}_{1}}{c_{1}}<0.99994.

Problem 4.4
.

How small can
\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}(\hom(T_{n},H)/\hom(P_{n},H))
be made? Can it be made arbitrarily small?
