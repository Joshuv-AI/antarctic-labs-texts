“We feel like we don’t matter”: the work experience of caring for long-term care residents with suspected trauma-related responsive behaviors
Authors: Joel T Minion, Hadiya M Huijer, Tosin Y Akintunde, Kathy Hegadoren, Howard H Feldman, Ruth A Lanius, Sube Banerjee, Liane R Ginsburg, James L Silvius, Lorie A Little, Peter G Norton, Carole A Estabrooks
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThere is increasing awareness that many long-term care (LTC) residents may have experienced trauma or post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) in their lifetimes, conditions that can be comorbid with dementia and/or complex mental health conditions. In Canada, health care aides provide the vast majority of direct care to such residents. This qualitative study explored their experiences working with residents whose responsive behaviors may indicate past trauma or PTSD.MethodsIn Fall 2024, we conducted open-ended interviews with 24 care aides from 6 urban LTC homes in Alberta, Canada. Initial interview topics built on insights from 13 separate interviews conducted in 2023. Questions were iterated throughout data collection and the data analyzed using Braun and Clarke’s reflexive thematic analysis approach.ResultsOur participants broadly reflected the demographic make-up of Alberta’s care aide workforce: 91.7% female, 79.2% from racialized groups and 70.8% speaking English as an additional language. We identified 3 overarching themes in their experiences: (1) care aides regularly suspected some responsive behaviors to be trauma-related but seldom named these as such; (2) their strategies for managing these behaviors and providing personalized care involved adapting existing skills and innovating new ones; and (3) resident care connected to possible past trauma was complex and compounded current workplace challenges and struggles.ConclusionsTwo improvements could help care aides working with residents with suspected trauma backgrounds: greater understanding of trauma and knowledge of trauma terminology, and use of a trauma-informed care framework. Such changes would likely improve resident quality of life by helping care aides better differentiate responsive behaviors, deliver targeted and individualized care, and avoid resident retraumatization. Implementation of trauma-informed care, however, needs to build on existing care aide expertise to facilitate uptake and ensure sustainability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14785-8.Keywords: Trauma, PTSD, Long-term care, Trauma-informed care, Care aides, Dementia

Background

Psychological trauma can arise at any point in life following one or more experienced or witnessed events such as accidents, armed conflict, sexual violence or childhood abuse [1]. Trauma can manifest as physical, mental, behavioral and/or social symptoms, alter neurobiology, has been linked to accelerated changes in biomarkers of age, and can lead to mental health issues including depression, anxiety and addiction [2–4]. Over 70% of the older adult population is estimated to have experienced at least one traumatic event in their lifespan, with 20% to 40% developing post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) symptoms [4–6].

For residents with trauma histories in long-term care (LTC) homes, stimuli reminding them of past experiences can lead to emotional dysregulation, hypervigilance and marked cognitive, affective and behavioral disturbances, leaving them at higher risk of retraumatization [7, 8]. LTC settings themselves can also cause new trauma owing to residents’ reduced ability to control their environment (e.g., locked doors, lack of choice) and power imbalances between those receiving and delivering care [5, 6]. Day-to-day elements of LTC living (e.g., loud noises, other residents’ behaviors, infection-control lockdowns) risk retraumatizing residents through associated thoughts and emotions [9–11].

Individuals entering the LTC system are increasingly older and can have dramatically more complex health and social needs, including multifaceted mental health challenges and trauma [12]. Residents also increasingly include younger individuals with intractable mental health issues (e.g., psychiatric disorders), cognitive impairments or long-term effects of substance misuse [13]. Among residents with a diminished capacity to communicate (e.g., those living with advanced dementia), some reactive behaviors expressed or exacerbated during care delivery (hereafter termed responsive behaviors) may indicate psychological distress stemming from past trauma or PTSD [6, 7, 14].

Upwards of 90% of direct care in Canada is provided by a mostly unregulated1 workforce of health care aides (nursing assistants, personal support workers) with limited formal training [15]. They are predominantly women from immigrant and racialized communities working on low incomes [16]. Care aides may have opportunities for continuing education but frequently cite access barriers like a lack of time, excessive workload and limited management support [17]. Training may also be inadequately suited to their needs, focusing on practical skills rather than relational care, therein limiting the applicability of such opportunities [18, 19]. There is also growing awareness that care aides themselves may have experienced personal and workplace-related trauma [19].

Methods

This qualitative study examined the experiences of care aides in Alberta, Canada, working with LTC residents with dementia and/or complex mental health conditions whose responsive behaviors may suggest past psychological trauma or PTSD. The aim was to explore care aides’ perspectives on such work, including how they understand trauma and deliver care.

Study context and design

This study sits within a larger applied health services research program (Translating Research in Elder Care/TREC) ongoing since 2007. TREC seeks to evidence and develop interventions that can improve LTC work environments and enhance resident quality of life/care and staff quality of work life. The program uses an integrated knowledge translation approach and both quantitative and qualitative methods to conduct longitudinal and single study research.

The findings reported here are a constructivist inquiry grounded in the perspective that care aides provide resident care using knowledge and skills developed over time through direct work experience, including interactions with residents, their families and other staff. Our study used an interpretive, exploratory design and assumed a broadly ethnographic perspective, seeing care aides as an occupational group that could reasonably be expected to share many common understandings, beliefs and practices.

Setting and recruitment

Alberta, a province of 5 million, has 200 + LTC homes that provide 24/7 onsite nursing care to individuals with complex medical and/or functional needs who are unable to remain safely at home or live in settings with lower levels of support. Homes are operated by the public health service or contracted to for- and not-for-profit providers. We recruited six LTC homes across all three operating models in two large urban centers, whose administrative staff we knew from previous research were interested in trauma care. Assessment of trauma-informed care services was introduced to Alberta’s LTC home accreditation process shortly after our study was completed.

We asked local administrators to inform a cross section of their care aides (by sex, age, years in role, ethnicity and first language spoken) about our study. Individuals needed to be working with residents with dementia and/or complex mental health conditions, to have been in their current roles for at least six months, and to be comfortable being interviewed in English. Temporary workers, agency staff and trainees were excluded. Anyone interested was forwarded additional details by the study team before deciding whether to participate.

We interviewed 24 care aides. Our sample was not representative but rather reflected participants with direct experience undertaking the care work being studied. The final sample size was contingent on the quality of data collected, the study aims, and practical challenges recruiting staff in overstretched LTC homes.

Data collection

Our interview guide (see Additional File 1) was novel to these findings but informed by insights from 13 semi-structured interviews conducted initially in this study [20]. Those interviews indicated that an informal, less structured and more exploratory approach would yield wider ranging discussions of greater depth. Between August and December 2024, one author (TYA) conducted 24 open-ended interviews. He worked closely with a second author and more experienced qualitative researcher (JTM) to hone questions over time, identify new opportunities for exploration, and eliminate topics that had been considered comprehensively.

For scheduling reasons, participants from the same home were typically interviewed across a one-to-two day period. Discussions averaged 48 min and took place in-person in a private space in each home (one was conducted via Zoom). Participants gave prior informed consent and homes provided operational approval. Each care aide received a $20 gift card, while administrators provided release time during work hours. The interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed professionally, checked for accuracy and then anonymized.

Data analysis

We analyzed the interviews using Braun and Clarke’s reflexive thematic analysis (RTA) [21, 22]. Our approach was inductive and semantically-focused [22], with the entire text of each transcript considered the unit of analysis. Words, sentences and paragraphs were identified as meaning units. This process began concurrent with data collection. Initial codes were generated by TYA using open coding and NVivo 14 software. Regular meetings between TYA and JTM allowed them to consider interviews completed to date and refine their understanding accordingly. They also developed visual diagrams to interrogate code families and identify code relationships and interdependencies.

Following data collection (and due to study team changes), JTM and another author (HMH) reviewed and further collated extant codes, and then developed initial candidate themes. These captured patterns of meaning constructed by the researchers across participants’ narratives. The transcripts were re-coded to the resultant themes and associated codes in a recursive process, resulting in further refinement and ensuring coherence with the dataset. As expected in RTA, themes and an overarching narrative continued to be refined and detailed during the reporting phase [21]. Illustrative examples (data extracts) are included to support our analytic findings.

The analysis was grounded throughout in the data and not guided by a specific theoretical framework. The final themes were, however, reflected back to existing theories and interpretive frameworks to allow us to further synthesize, situate and explain our findings [23, 24].

Positionality and reflexivity

RTA is an active, generative process in which researcher subjectivity is integral to analysis [21, 25]. The three researchers most directly responsible were social scientists and had non-clinical understandings of trauma. They each sought to acknowledge their positionality relative to participants, particularly with respect to their respective experiences of aging, LTC and trauma. TYA (himself a racialized, recent immigrant to Canada) kept a reflexive journal throughout data collection, explored his observations at regular meetings with JTM, and volunteered in a LTC home not involved in this study. Members of the larger study team offered insights and critiques based on their extensive research, clinical and/or LTC sector expertise in geriatric, dementia and trauma care.

Trustworthiness

We looked to demonstrate trustworthiness in our findings by using the COREQ checklist [26] for qualitative, interview-based research. A few items (e.g., data saturation, inter-coder agreement) are not consistent with Braun and Clarke’s RTA approach and were adjusted accordingly to align with their current guidance. Transcript review and participant checking of findings were not possible given the workplace demands of participating care aides.

Results

Participants in our study broadly reflected the demographics of Alberta’s urban care aide workforce (Table 1): 91.7% were female, 79.2% were non-European and 70.8% spoke English as an additional language. Time worked as a care aide ranged from 3 to 49 years.

Characteristics of interview participants (n = 24)

Participants described regularly encountering responsive behaviors they suspected could indicate past trauma or PTSD (e.g., fear/paranoia, agitation, resistance to care). The care aides typically had no training in trauma-specific care or access to residents’ medical records. Their approach to responsive behaviors was informed and guided primarily by familiarity with each resident and their work experience as a care aide. None followed a trauma-specific framework or approach.

We identified three core themes running through our participants’ experiences: seeing but not naming; adapting and innovating; and surviving uncertainty and staying afloat.

Theme 1: Seeing but not naming

In their interviews, participants identified a wide array of resident responsive behaviors they suspected were linked to trauma (e.g., repeatedly yelling “fire”, resisting perineal care, phobia of mirrors). Their narratives indicated that trauma experiences varied widely by age of exposure and frequency (e.g., single event, repeated). The most cited or suspected causative events were sexual or physical abuse/assault and war/armed conflict, but also included personal loss (e.g., death of spouse) and accidents (e.g., a house fire).

The care aides reported little familiarity with trauma as a psychological condition or diagnosis, and said the topic was not commonly discussed in-depth among LTC staff.

I don’t know, I don’t really think about trauma when I think about my residents. I mean, I know everybody has a rough life a lot of the time coming in here because they grew up in a not so good era, but yeah, I don’t really think about it. (Participant 3)

For many, including more experienced care aides, trauma was either a new concept or one about which they were only becoming familiar.

Before you would not hear a lot about trauma. They [the residents] would do something to us, but we’re not clicking those two like it’s a trauma or stuff like that. You will get it, but I didn’t have the name. But once I start schooling…then the things that’s happening every day. (Participant 19)

Only a third of the care aides indicated an understanding of trauma’s long-lasting effects, such as how recall of a traumatic experience can remain even when much of an individual’s memory has been lost to dementia.

Trauma is something you cannot overcome or you cannot forget till you die. If something reminds you of that, if something reminds you of that trauma, you still live that moment what you went through that trauma. Yeah, that’s what I think. (Participant 10)

Most care aides described trauma more loosely as an “ongoing thing” (Participant 7) that would “be in [a resident’s] being until [they] don’t have any more feelings” (Participant 23).

Details of a resident’s known past trauma were seldom noted in care plans or shared with care aides. Family members often provided fragmentary information at best. When trying to make sense of responsive behaviors, participants relied primarily on context, knowledge of a particular resident and past work experience.

It depends. Sometimes we can differentiate [between trauma and dementia], sometimes…not, but it’s more likely knowing the person more. It gives you the ability to differentiate what is aggressive behavior, dementia behavior, or is related to the trauma behavior. It’s individuals. I can’t say something in general, but it’s most likely individual. We take it case by case. (Participant 13)

In a number of instances, participants said they drew inferences from a resident’s age, ethnicity or relationship history. Some described how novel responsive behaviors would pique their interest and prompt them to learn more (e.g., by asking other staff or searching online).

Theme 2: Adapting and innovating

Care aides found it challenging to identify behaviors as trauma-related and then adapt care accordingly. None reported much or any training in how to recognize or respond to behaviors associated with trauma, or could identify trauma-informed approaches implemented in their workplace.

I don’t know the symptoms of trauma. … Is it the screaming? Is that a symptom of trauma? … I honestly can’t tell you. I don’t know. (Participant 5)

By comparison, almost all were conversant with responsive behaviors associated with dementia, likely because most worked on units where many residents had a formal diagnosis of this condition and advanced disease. Dementia training was commonplace.

Care adapted to suspected trauma behaviors relied heavily on re-purposing dementia strategies and innovating based on past experience.

I’m learning every day with my experience. That’s how I am educating myself how to deal with the residents. (Participant 10)

Participants described turning to techniques like music or touch, redirecting conversations, avoiding topics or actions that triggered specific behaviors, and allowing time and space for situations to de-escalate. Some highlighted the importance of broader principles like compassion, patience, calmness and treating residents with respect and an open mind.

In my experience, you give them space. If they say no, you respect it, and then you just say, ‘I’ll come back again after five minutes when you’re ready.’ So I leave them and then just come back again. It’s reapproaching, reapproaching, reapproaching, reapproaching, not too often though, because they also get irritated that you just ask. (Participant 14)

Such approaches were guided not by formal understandings of trauma but by each care aides’ depth of knowledge of responsive behaviors and familiarity with a resident’s circumstances and known history.

When I found out that [resident] has a history of abuse, just every time I approach her, we approach her now like, ‘We’re here to help you. We’re not here to hurt you. We’re just trying to help you,’ anything like that. ‘You’re safe here. Nobody will hurt you here.’ And it helps. (Participant 23)

Theme 3: Surviving uncertainty and staying afloat

Resident responsive behaviors could be unpredictable and care aides often felt ill-equipped to respond effectively. The need to identify and make sense of behaviors as possibly trauma-related had been incorporated into their roles without specific training or recognition of expanded responsibilities. Such resident care received almost no dedicated workplace focus despite trauma-associated work being widely reported as compounding time pressures, increasing the emotional and physical toll on care aides, entrenching their low workplace status, and sometimes negatively affecting personal relationships at home.

We feel like we don’t matter. … we call ourselves the bottom of the shoes…nobody cares about us… When you’re HCA, nobody gives a shit. You are just low life workers so it can be very…sometimes you get emotional about it, you get so mad because we feel like we are doing most of the job. We are the first responder. We are there with the patient, we know them in and out, but they don’t consider us. (Participant 19)

A third of participants described incidents where their physical safety had been compromised by residents with suspected trauma behaviors (e.g., care aides being hit, choked or chased). Such encounters had resulted in significant injury, time off work and a lasting emotional toll.

For the first time I came here, after one and a half years off [following] my injury, that person who injured me, I’m scared. I don’t want to go to his room. I don’t even want to have face-to-face conversation to that person. (Participant 20)

Many care aides described negative effects on their mental and physical well-being (e.g., racing heartbeat, chills). Several felt under-supported following such occurrences, with a few saying they had requested a unit change to address issues of anxiety.

Where possible, care aides focused on what they could control when working with residents they suspected had experienced trauma. The importance of personal resilience was a common thread throughout the interviews. Several participants described reframing situations as growth opportunities to learn more about residents as well as themselves.

Working with them, it helped me a lot. Because there are things I do not know before mentally. For example, dementia, trauma…but now I know I can deal with them. (Participant 8)

A few participants described proactive steps they had taken to be more informed.

I mean, you have to have the knowledge, too, because without knowledge how can you help? Without reading, how can you help with person without knowing? You can say, “Oh, he’s traumatized”. So you just leave him there?? … First you have to understand why he’s like this. Why? What’s wrong with him? You have to learn his story…then you understand. You tend to have empathy. You tend to put yourself in their position and that’s where it kicks in, the care – the care kicks in your heart. Give the best, yeah. (Participant 1)

Almost all of the participants described feeling overwhelmed at times and struggling to stay afloat when tasked with caring for large numbers of residents during a shift and when the illness trajectory of residents was inevitably one of decline over time.

The most challenging to me is that when my resident is still cognitive and they’re going down. Physically and mentally…it hurts me. And one more thing if they change. Even though they are physically still alert and when they’re deteriorating mentally, because before you have a good communication with them. Suddenly they change mentally, that is very challenging. (Participant 6)

Discussion

This study explored the experiences of care aides working with LTC residents whose responsive behaviors indicated possible psychological trauma or PTSD comorbid with dementia or complex mental health conditions. Because trauma can arise from multiple causes and vary in expression, differentiating responsive behaviors and translating these into coherent, personalized care is complex work for LTC staff and an area of emergent research interest [4].

Two existing concepts helped us understand our findings: mindlines [27] and Figuring it Out in the Moment. [28] Our participants widely reported making sense of and responding to residents’ responsive behaviors through trial and error. This approach is characteristic of Gabbay and le May’s mindlines, the process of applying tacit knowledge gathered primarily from informal sources to inform care delivery [27, 29]. In the absence of operationalized trauma-specific approaches, our care aides strategized by using mindlines drawn from past experience, ongoing relationships with residents and adaptation of dementia-based techniques [6, 10]. Mindlines helped ensure essential care took place and the status quo was maintained as best as possible.

Gabbay and le May [29] have highlighted the importance of language to mindlines, arguing that such forms of knowledge require common terminology with precise meanings. Not only was a shared language of trauma missing from our participants’ experiences, they lacked the resources and training opportunities to develop it. This raised concerns among study team members as to whether mindlines established on inaccurate or incomplete knowledge about trauma risked unexpected or unwanted care outcomes (e.g., resident retraumatization).

Figuring it Out in the Moment [28] is a similar process by which care aides make care decisions and assess outcomes by combining observational information with prior knowledge. Participants in our study delivered care related to possible past trauma or PTSD based on context, critical thinking and differentiation between responsive behaviors, though without guidance similar to the dementia care framework studied by Janes et al. [28]. Our care aides demonstrated the need for individual adaptation and innovation in the absence of structured support and training. In both instances, however, care aides dealt with uncertainty and struggled to stay afloat in circumstances where they felt insufficiently supported.

Conclusions

The mindlines and Figuring it Out in the Moment frameworks indicate that our participants’ experiences, practices and strategies parallel those already identified elsewhere in LTC. Both concepts also point to possible interventions for improvement. Our findings highlight the need among care aides for evidence-based knowledge, terminology and frameworks for trauma care, even if residents’ histories are fragmentary or unknown. Such resources would better support care aides in responding to suspected responsive behaviors and to communicate about trauma and strategize care.

Trauma-informed care, characterized by the core principles of safety, trustworthiness, peer support, collaboration, empowerment and humility, has been shown to increase awareness of trauma and help prevent retraumatization [5, 30]. While it does not require a formal medical or psychological diagnosis nor does it aim to treat trauma, it does enable conditions of safety, security and autonomy and can help prevent retraumatization among both residents and care aides [14]. Trauma-informed care has been shown to facilitate patience, acceptance and inclusivity among care providers, offer choice (where appropriate) to residents, acknowledge power dynamics in settings like LTC homes, and reduce the risk of retraumatization [11]. Practical elements include ensuring workforce education on the signs of trauma, establishing welcoming physical environments and developing trauma services [5]. Trauma-informed care can benefit staff as well as organizations by reducing burnout, turnover and costs associated with care [4].

Introducing a trauma-informed framework to LTC is not without its challenges. The sector is characterized by acknowledged fragmentation, workforce hierarchies, chronic understaffing and increasing reliance on agency/casual staff [31–33]. Care aides face significant job stressors, including discrimination, regular loss (e.g., death of residents) and ongoing systemic devaluation [19]. There is also growing recognition of care aides’ own trauma histories [34]. Findings elsewhere indicate that LTC staff are vulnerable to personal trauma given the nature of their work, sociodemographic discrimination (e.g., race, sex) and a frequent lack of recognition [19, 34]. Staff who worked through the COVID-19 pandemic experienced varying levels of personal trauma [35].

LTC residents with psychological trauma or PTSD have unique care needs. Continuation of ad hoc approaches to trauma care is not likely to deliver improvement in resident quality of life or reduce retraumatization. Our study shows the current situation can also negatively affect care aides’ quality of work life. Implementation of a trauma-informed care framework or approach to care would equip care aides with essential and practical knowledge about how trauma is experienced and expressed across the lifespan as well as how trauma interacts with other conditions (e.g., dementia) as individuals age and move through care settings.

Limitations

We acknowledge limitations in our study. Our sample was drawn from urban homes we knew were already interested in resident trauma care, and our findings may not be transferable to care aides in rural settings or jurisdictions beyond Alberta or Canada. Our approach also did not seek to confirm care aides’ suspicions that responsive behaviors were indeed connected to trauma or investigate residents’ own trauma backgrounds.
