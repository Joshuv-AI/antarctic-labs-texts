Optimal accompanying diagnosis design through external validation of population pharmacokinetic models for moxifloxacin in tuberculosis patients
Authors: Hyunjung Lee, Woojin Jung, Min Jung Chang, Soyoung Lee, Jung-woo Chae, Radojka M Savic, Hwi-yeol Yun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWe aimed to design an optimal accompanying diagnosis, defined as a model-informed limited sampling strategy (LSS) that uses validated population pharmacokinetic (popPK) models to predict moxifloxacin exposures with minimal blood samples, facilitating therapeutic drug (TDM) while reducing patient burden, through external validation of published popPK models for moxifloxacin in patients with tuberculosis, for developing a limited sampling strategy to optimize drug monitoring.MethodsA systematic literature review identified seven popPK models for moxifloxacin. These models were externally validated using a dataset of 1,042 moxifloxacin concentrations from 113 multidrug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB) patients. The model performance was evaluated using various predictive performance metrics, including the median prediction error, mean absolute error, and root mean square error. A Bayesian forecasting analysis was conducted to determine the optimal sampling points for accompanying diagnosis.ResultsMost models showed acceptable predictive performance within ± 30% prediction error (F30). The model proposed by Pranger et al. demonstrated the best overall performance for external validation. Analysis of the accumulated data revealed that sampling at even-numbered weeks (2, 4, 6, 8, and 10) provided better predictive power than sampling at odd-numbered weeks. Data from the first week was insufficient for an accurate prediction. The model performance stabilized between two and eight weeks of accumulated data.ConclusionThis study suggests that popPK modeling with a limited sampling strategy can effectively predict moxifloxacin concentrations in patients with tuberculosis. Optimal accompanying diagnosis can be achieved with data collected within 1 month, preferably at even-numbered weeks, thereby reducing the burden of frequent blood sampling. These findings have implications for improving therapeutic drug monitoring in the treatment of multidrug-resistant tuberculosis, potentially improving patient compliance and treatment outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12403-2.Keywords: Optimal accompanying diagnostics, Population pharmacokinetics, External validation, MDR-TB, Moxifloxacin

Background

Tuberculosis (TB) remains one of the world’s deadliest infectious diseases, causing 1.6 million deaths in 2021, and the World Health Organization (WHO) has aimed to reduce tuberculosis-related deaths by 95% by 2035 [1, 2]. Treatment has been improving in recent decades, with approximately 85% of patients having been effectively treated using a 6-month drug regimen consisting of isoniazid, rifampin, ethambutol, and pyrazinamide [1, 3, 4]. However, TB remains one of the top 10 diseases with high mortality worldwide [2]. Drug-resistant TB has been one of the major factors contributing to this ongoing epidemic [5, 6]. The burden of this disease increased by 3% between 2020 and 2021, with 450,000 new cases of rifampicin-resistant TB reported in 2021. Russia and other countries in Eastern Europe and Central Asia reported the highest rates of multidrug-resistant (MDR) or rifampicin-resistant TB among previously treated people, with rates exceeding 50% [2]. Unlike non-MDR-TB treatment, MDR-TB has been treated with different combinations of drug therapy for longer periods [7–9].

Moxifloxacin has been considered a key drug in the long-term and short-term treatment of MDR-TB. In 2022, the WHO revised its guidelines to recommend its inclusion, along with rifapentine, in the first-line treatment of drug-susceptible tuberculosis [10]. WHO recommends a standard dose of 400 mg per day for patients without risk factors for toxicity, or a higher dose regimen of 400–800 mg per day, depending on the prescribed weight band [11]. While these dosing recommendations have provided a general guideline, it is important to understand the pharmacokinetic principles underlying moxifloxacin’s efficacy. The efficacy of moxifloxacin, in terms of bacterial kill, is related to the ratio of the area under the unbound concentration-time curve from 0 to 24 h to the MIC (fAUC0 − 24/MIC). Free or unbound fAUC0 − 24/MIC > 53 corresponds to a total (bound and unbound) AUC0 − 24/MIC > 106, assuming a constant protein binding of 50% [7, 11, 12]. However, calculating the AUC0 − 24 requires intense plasma concentration measurements widely distributed over the dosing interval. Intense plasma concentration measurements are necessary because pharmacokinetic (PK) variability due to individual differences in drug absorption and metabolic kinetics is clinically associated with poorer outcomes of tuberculosis treatment [13]. Additionally, long-term treatment is associated with poor adherence [1]. Several studies have demonstrated the impact of low drug concentrations on poor treatment outcomes [14, 15]. In light of this evidence, it has been assumed that therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) that targets appropriate dosing and exposure can improve treatment outcomes. However, the added value of TDM for MDR-TB treatment outcomes has not been directly studied [7]. In addition, TDM has not always been easily accessible in areas with a high TB burden for practical and financial reasons [16]. Given these challenges in implementing TDM, especially in high TB burden areas, alternative approaches have been explored to optimize drug dosing and monitoring. Adopting a limited sampling strategy (LSS) has reduced the burden of frequent sampling on both patients and staff, while providing reliable estimates using only a subset of blood samples [17]. The accuracy of popPK model performance is particularly critical for optimal dosing decisions, given the significant inter-individual variability in moxifloxacin pharmacokinetics. Different popPK models may yield varying predictions that directly impact clinical decisions. For instance, previous studies have shown that one-compartment models can underestimate moxifloxacin exposure by 5.1% to 10% when used alone or combined with rifampin [17, 18]. Even with Bayesian-based limited sampling strategies, drug exposure predictions can vary by -4.8% to -5.5%, depending on the model used [17, 18]. These variations become particularly concerning in the context of therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM), which is recommended to improve treatment response and reduce acquired drug resistance. The impact of model performance becomes even more crucial when combined with rifampin, where plasma concentrations of moxifloxacin can be reduced by up to 31% [17, 18]. Such model-dependent variations in exposure estimation could significantly affect therapeutic decisions, particularly when targeting the optimal unbound fAUC0 − 24/MIC > 53. While limited sampling strategies can reduce the burden of TDM by using a small number of appropriately timed samples, the reliability of these strategies heavily depends on the underlying popPK model’s accuracy. Despite the availability of various popPK models, their predictive performance in different populations and clinical scenarios has not been systematically evaluated, raising concerns about the reliability of model-based dosing recommendations.

In this study, we performed an external evaluation of the published population pharmacokinetics (popPK) model for MDR-TB to evaluate its predictive performance using Bayesian prediction methods. Optimal accompanying diagnosis, defined as a model-informed LSS that uses validated popPK models to predict drug exposures with minimal blood samples, facilitates TDM while reducing patient burden. This approach aims to optimize dosing decisions for moxifloxacin in multidrug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB) patients through robust model validation.

Methods

Literature research and model collection

A systematic literature search was conducted for publications on the population pharmacokinetic (pop PK) model of moxifloxacin published until September 27, 2024. The limited sampling strategy (LSS), which uses a reduced number of timed blood samples to estimate pharmacokinetic parameters, was employed to optimize therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM). Articles analyzing the PK parameters of adult TB using the popPK approach were included in this study. Additionally, articles were excluded if they were not original, if the model parameters were unavailable for external evaluation, or if they were duplicates.

Dataset for external evaluation

Clinical trial number: This study includes five clinical trials registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (Study 1 [B-130191-001], Study 2 [NCT0081646], Study 3 [NCT01498419], Study 4 [NCT02193776], and Study 5 [NCT02342886]). An external evaluation was performed using the dataset of Yun et al. [5] with the permission of all authors and all studies were approved by the relevant national control agency and ethics committee of each study site and were conducted in accordance with the principles of Good Clinical Practice Guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki. Patients with MDR-TB from five clinical trials were included for population study and data collection. The enrolled patients were diagnosed with MDR-TB resistant to isoniazid and rifampicin. During the clinical trial, moxifloxacin (400 mg) was administered once daily for at least two weeks. Patients also received at least two additional MDR-TB drugs. After moxifloxacin administration, blood samples were collected for analysis of plasma moxifloxacin concentration using dense sampling.

External evaluation with predictive performances

The popPK model for moxifloxacin identified in the literature was developed using Non-linear Mixed Effect modeling (NONMEM, version 7.5.1). The model parameters are described in this paper and are supported by NONMEM with PsN (version 5.2.6) for modeling the test dataset. R software (version 4.3.2) was used for the statistical analyses and graphics. PopPK parameters were fixed at the values reported in this study and predicted concentrations. To check whether the model was maintained by inserting external data, MAXEVAL = 0 (non-estimation) and 9999 (full dataset-estimation) for each model were also included and compared. If there was no significant difference between the two values, the model could be considered general-purpose and reflected, even if other data on moxifloxacin were imported. To evaluate the prediction performance of each model, we compared the goodness-of-fit graphs of the population-predicted and observed concentrations. The general delta objective function value (dOFV) represents the difference in the objective function value (OFV) between two nested models and approximately follows the chi-square (χ²) distribution. However, dOFV alone does not indicate clinical significance, and its interpretation should be complemented by predictive performance metrics such as median prediction error (MdPE). The degrees of freedom indicate the differences in the number of parameters between the two models. In this study, because it was used when different amounts of data were applied to the same model, dOFV represents the change in model fit due to the change in the amount of data and not the change in model performance. The non-estimated OFV minus the full dataset estimated OFV were calculated and compared. Using this method, the stability of the model can be evaluated by confirming the consistency of the model performance according to the change in the amount of data, and the minimum amount of data required can be estimated to analyze the sensitivity to sample size. Bias was assessed by calculating the following metrics prediction error (PE; in percent), median prediction error (MdPE; in percent), mean absolute prediction error (MAE) and mean absolute percentage Error (MAPE; in percent). The precision was evaluated by calculating mean squared prediction error (MSE) and the root mean square prediction error (RMSE) calculated using the following equation: [19–21].

The 95% Confidence intervals (CIs) for these metrics were estimated using a bootstrap method with 1,000 replicates and the percentile method to account for variability in the data. We also used a prediction error rate within ± 30% (F30) and a visual predictive check (VPC) to evaluate the model prediction performance.

Bayesian forecasting analysis for optimal sampling points of accompanying diagnosis

The data spanned a maximum of 1800 h. This time was divided into 7-day (weekly) intervals, each designated as a subset column with 24-hour increments. When sorted by week, columns containing weak information are grouped from 1 to 11, whereas columns without weak information are assigned to Group 12.

The predictive power of the model was tested by subdividing the observed dataset into odd and even weeks. This is estimated by gradually increasing the number of data points per state. Prediction bias, the difference between the predicted and observed values, was used to assess predictability.

All Bayesian predictions were performed using the NONMEM software. The external evaluation dataset included 1,042 moxifloxacin concentrations from 113 MDR-TB patients, collected through intense sampling (dense blood collection over the dosing interval) across multiple weeks, as described in Yun et al. [5]. The error values of the non-estimation and full-dataset estimations were compared to verify whether the model maintained its performance when incorporating external data. In the case of non-estimation, we predicted our data using the model parameters listed in the collected papers and did not optimize the model parameters. full dataset estimation was optimized by re-estimating the model parameters using the equation known in the paper and the external full dataset.

Results

Systematic review of published popPK models.

Among 147 articles, with the terms “Moxifloxacin” and “population modeling” appearing in their summaries, published between 1998 and 2024, several dealt with modeling or popPK analyses of TB. A detailed flowchart of the inclusion and exclusion criteria of papers is shown in Supporting Information Fig. 1. After selecting suitable articles, seven studies using non-linear mixed-effects models were included.

Overview of the strategy used in the literature search (n: number of articles returned by search)

Yun et al. [5] conducted the study in 2022, whereas other studies were reported between 2011 and 2019. Two studies were conducted in Korea [22, 23], two in Europe (the Netherlands [24], and Denmark [25]), one in the United States and Brazil [26], and for two studies [5, 27], the country of conduct could not be specified. All models were developed using adult data, and only two studies included > 100 patients [5, 27].

The demographic and pharmacokinetic characteristics of the published models are summarized in Table 1. Several studies [23–27] used a one-compartment model, one study used a two-compartment model [5], and another used both one- and two-compartment models [22]. Three models [23, 25, 27] used weight as a significant covariate, while other models [5, 24, 27] used HIV status, MTT assay, LBM (Lean Body Mass), Rifampin administration status, etc. None of the two models [22, 26] reported significant covariates.

External evaluation dataset

The external evaluation dataset comprised 1,042 moxifloxacin concentrations from 113 MDR-TB patients, including 73 men (64.4%) and 40 women (35.4%), with an average age of 34 years (range 18–89 years), average weight of 54 kg (range, 35–84 kg), racial composition of 68 Black (60.2%), 19 mixed ethnicity (16.8%), and 26 White and Asian (23.0%). Additionally, 69.9% (n = 79) had a negative HIV status, and 31.0% (n = 35) had a positive HIV status. Further details, including treatment duration and disease severity, are summarized in Supplementary Table S3, alongside covariates for each model.

External predictability evaluation

The objective function value (OFV) reflects the difference between the predicted and observed values that appear when fitting individual observed values. Therefore, as the number of observed values increases, OFV tends to increase. The delta objective function value (dOFV) represents the difference in the value based on the full dataset estimation. Accordingly, Fig. 2(A) compares the fit of various models to the same dataset using dOFV with the full data. As more samples accumulate, the dOFV becomes more stable, and the dOFV confirms that the model’s predictive power changes dramatically between two and eight weeks and maintains a constant value. In the box plots showing the prediction errors of population PK models of moxifloxacin, the red dotted line corresponds to the range of -30% to 30% (F30) difference from the mean error (Fig. 3). Most models fell within this range, except for Strydom et al. [22] (87.200% [73.200, 98.600]) and Chang et al. [23] (-1.460% [-3.990, 1.240]), which showed deviations. Florian et al. [27] (95.200% [79.700, 111.000]) and Obrink-Hansen et al. [25] (98.800% [82.000, 116.000]) exhibited significant over-prediction trends across other error metrics.

MAE represents the average absolute error, with lower values indicating better model performance. The Pranger et al. [24] model had the smallest MAE at 0.306 ug/mL [0.275, 0.343], suggesting superior accuracy. MAPE expresses MAE as a percentage, reflecting precision. Yun et al. [5] model showed a MAPE of 133.000% [110.000, 159.000], followed by Chang et al. [23] at 165.000% [134.000, 197.000] and Al-Shaer et al. [26] at 180.000% [155.000, 211.000]. Higher MAPE values, such as 662.000% [569.000, 758.000] for Florian et al. [27] and 775.000% [670.000, 896.000] for Obrink-Hansen et al. [25], indicate substantial prediction errors, likely influenced by outliers. MSE and RMSE are sensitive to outlier values due to their squared nature. The Pranger et al. [24] model demonstrated the best performance with the lowest MSE of 0.376 [ug/mL]2 [0.281, 0.503] and RMSE of 0.613 ug/mL [0.274, 0.339]. In contrast, Obrink-Hansen et al. [25] and Florian et al. [27] showed the highest MSE (16.700 [ug/mL]2 [13.300, 20.300] and 10.800 [ug/mL]2 [8.750, 13.00], respectively) and RMSE (4.090 ug/mL [1.860, 2.300] and 3.290 ug/mL [1.580, 1.910]), reflecting their sensitivity to extreme values. Full dataset estimation, involving up to 9,999 objective function evaluations to optimize population parameters, improved several models (Table 2). Notable improvements were observed in Florian et al. [27] (-9.940% [-13.500, -5.500]), Obrink-Hansen et al. [25] (-9.110% [-12.800, -3.470]), and Pranger et al. [24] (-1.434% [-3.390, 1.340]), with MdPE values closer to 0 compared to non-estimation results. MAE values remained similar to non-estimation for Yun et al. [5] (0.379 ug/mL [0.346, 0.418]), Al-Shaer et al. [26] (0.530 ug/mL [0.491, 0.574]), Pranger et al. [24] (0.311 ug/mL [0.283, 0.343]), and Chang et al. [23] (0.359 ug/mL [0.323, 0.398]). The best MAPE values were observed in Florian et al. [27] (126.000% [107.000, 146.000]) and Chang et al. [23] (156.000% [130.000, 184.000]), though still indicating significant errors. The Pranger et al. [24] model maintained the smallest MSE (0.331 [ug/mL]2 [0.250, 0.432]) and RMSE (0.575 ug/mL [0.283, 0.343]), confirming robust performance across datasets. Optimal accompanying diagnosis design prediction

As shown in the delta objective function value (dOFV) results, there was no significant difference from week 2 to week 8. To observe a clear difference, the data were classified and organized as shown in Supplementary Table (Table S2) to confirm the results and design an optimal accompanying diagnosis.

The top graph in Fig. 3(B) shows the results of combining the prediction error rates after estimating data for one week for each model and estimating data by accumulating the amount of data for one week. The full dataset estimation shows the best prediction error rate because it was estimated using the most data. Based on this, when checking one week at a time, it was confirmed that the data for the first and the 10th week were outside the F30. The MdPE closest to 0% showed a value most similar to 0 when only five weeks of data were used. (Supplementary Table 1 (Table S1) The second graph in Fig. 3(B) shows that when the data were accumulated by week, the accumulated data up to week 11, excluding the first week, were within the F30. Similar results were obtained for the MdPE values. From data size 10 (~ 10 weeks), MdPE (-5.262%) decreases noticeably.

Based on these initial results, an additional analysis of odd and even weeks was performed to determine the two-week differences. As a result of accumulating odd weeks, Fig. 3(B) confirms that the prediction error deviates from F30, even when data for up to seven weeks are accumulated. It showed the lowest MdPE value (-0.793%) when estimated with a data size of six (one, three, and five weeks). After accumulating the data for even-numbered weeks, no box exceeded F30. When checking data showing similar results to the full dataset estimation, the box sizes are similar, starting from data size 3 (2 weeks). Data size 10 (2, 4, 6, 8, 10 weeks), which has the most data, has the lowest MdPE of -3.711%, and data size 3 (2 weeks), which has the least data, does not have a large difference in MdPE compared to other data.

Discussion

TB is the most lethal infectious disease worldwide, and MDR-TB is a major problem. Moxifloxacin is a key drug used in the treatment of MDR-TB; however, TDM is important because of individual differences in drug responses. However, owing to the difficulties in implementing TDM, LSS and popPK modeling have been proposed as alternatives. This study aimed to design an optimal accompanying diagnostic tool for a group of patients with TB receiving moxifloxacin through external validation of a published popPK model. Additionally, evaluated and compared multiple published popPK models to identify the most robust for predicting moxifloxacin exposures in MDR-TB patients, rather than developing a single integrated model, ensuring applicability across diverse populations.

The results at non-estimation for the external validation of the model showed that, except for some models, the prediction performance of most popPK models fell within F30. (Fig. 3(A) Overall, the model from Pranger et al. [24]. performed the best in the prediction-based evaluation. The log-scale scatter plots in Supplementary Figure S1, showing observed versus predicted concentrations, reveal trends in overprediction and underprediction, with models like Pranger et al. demonstrating fewer deviations from the line of unity, suggesting improved reliability for therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM). This means that this model can predict moxifloxacin concentrations in external datasets more accurately and precisely than the other models. The median prediction error (MdPE) of models [27, 25], and [22] showed significant differences of approximately 93%. This indicates an overprediction of the moxifloxacin concentration. It is believed that the PE and MdPE of the model improved in the results of the full dataset estimation because of the influence of the data when constructing the existing model. When the difference between the non-estimation and full-dataset estimation is not large in the models, it is because the covariates reflect the model well.

In this study, data were divided and estimated to design an optimal accompanying diagnosis. The dataset with the shape most similar to the overall dataset predicts the best. As a result of predicting the model’s weekly data, two weeks of data within a month proved to be the best. Data from one week exceeded F30, which reduced the predictive power. These results were confirmed by accumulated data. When checking the graph that accumulates odd and even weeks, in the case of odd data that accumulates one week of data, most of the data have a wider distribution and can be said to be outside the F30. Conversely, in the case of even-numbered weeks, all the data fall within the F30 range. In addition, the results confirmed in this study were estimated from accumulated data divided into two weeks. The delta objective function value (dOFV) for accumulated data from odd weeks (1, 3, 5, 7, 11) and early even weeks (2, 4, 6) was higher than that of the full dataset estimation, indicating a less stable model fit. In contrast, accumulated data from even weeks 8 and 10 showed median prediction error (MdPE) values close to zero, suggesting higher predictive accuracy. Although these results are smaller than those of the full dataset, they show a better model performance because they use high-quality data from a specific period. It was confirmed that as more samples were accumulated, the OFV became more stable, was similar between 2 and 8 weeks, and improved with more sampling points. Certain models incorporated covariates, such as HIV status, weight, and rifampin co-administration, to account for inter-individual variability. However, the model was not robust and had poor generality; therefore, it was confirmed that the OFV deteriorated as sample points accumulated each week.

A limitation of this study is that the popPK models predict blood concentrations rather than direct therapeutic outcomes such as culture conversion, which may better reflect clinical efficacy. PopPK modeling can predict the drug responses of individual patients using data from various populations and integrates multiple variables (drug metabolism rate, administration route, and drug interactions). This allows for more precise drug control for various patient groups, flexibility in data use, unlike TDM, and can reduce the need for regular blood tests and drug concentration analysis.

In clinical practice, MDR-TB requires a longer treatment period, has higher costs, and a lower treatment success rate than susceptible tuberculosis. Consequently, it is difficult to ensure patient compliance with treatment. However, according to the results of this study, a good model can confirm sufficient results even with a small sample size. If sampling is performed for a certain period, the time and cost required for a patient’s blood tests can be significantly reduced.

Conclusions

Many failures have occurred in the treatment of MDR-TB, making the determination of appropriate treatment period is crucial. Through external validation of seven published popPK models, this study demonstrated that model-based predictions can show sufficient performance even with data collected within 1 month of the initial point. However, data from the first week did not reflect good diagnostic and prognostic results, and even-numbered weeks provided better predictive performance than odd-numbered weeks. This discrepancy may be attributed to the uneven distribution of sample collections across time points, as detailed in Supplementary Table S2. Specifically, even-numbered weeks (e.g., weeks 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10) had substantially higher sample counts (total n = 873, excluding week 0) compared to odd-numbered weeks (total n = 253 for weeks 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, and 11). For instance, week 2 had 300 samples, and week 8 had 114, while odd weeks like week 1 (77 samples), week 3 (63), and week 11 (2) had far fewer. This imbalance likely resulted in more robust accumulated data for even weeks, leading to lower median prediction errors (MdPE) and better model stabilization, as shown in Supplementary Table S1 and Fig. 3B. The first week’s data (week 1, n = 77) was particularly insufficient, possibly due to limited samples and potential early pharmacokinetic variability (e.g., from initial drug accumulation or patient adaptation), which may not capture steady-state conditions as effectively as later weeks. These findings underscore the importance of sample density in external validation and suggest prioritizing sampling during periods with higher data availability for optimal accompanying diagnostics.

Based on this study, future research should consider expanding the integrated model and accompanying diagnostic design to include the safety aspects of moxifloxacin, particularly by adding covariates that affect QTc interval prolongation, which were not reflected in this study.
