Bioactivity-guided evaluation of F7 fraction derived from Acalypha cuspidata on ovarian cancer models in vitro and in vivo
Authors: Herrera-Martínez Mayra, Hernández-Ramírez Verónica Ivonne, Hernández-Carlos Beatriz, Talamás-Rohana Patricia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Based on previous research, this study aimed to perform a bioactivity-guided evaluation of F7 fraction obtained from an ethyl acetate extract of Acalypha cuspidata (Jacq.) and to determine its anticancer activity on the SKOV-3 ovarian cancer cell line through in vitro and in vivo assays. Thus, ethyl acetate extract from A. cuspidata (9.8 g) was fractionated into 11 fractions (F1–F11) by open column chromatography, and chromatography-obtained fractions were evaluated using the WST-1 assay. Transmission electron microscopy, flow cytometry, Annexin V binding assay, and experimental model of the Nu / Nu mouse were carried out to analyze fraction 7 (F7) anti-tumor activity. The results showed that among the eleven fractions obtained, only F4 and F7 fractions showed lower cytotoxicity against SKOV-3 ovarian cancer cells and the best ability to selectively target cancer cells without affecting BEAS-2B non-cancer cell line. Toxicity data in primary culture of rat hepatocytes indicated that F7 fraction is less toxic than F4, hence, F7 was selected for further studies. F7 fraction (half maximal inhibitory concentration, IC50, 28 μg / mL)-treated SKOV-3 cells had morphological hallmarks of apoptosis, such as chromatin condensation, reduction in cellular size, phosphatidylserine externalization, and drastic damage to the mitochondria. In a xenograft mouse model of ovarian cancer, treatment with F7 fraction (40 mg/kg) was associated with a marked reduction in tumor growth compared with the vehicle-treated group, supporting the potential antitumor activity of this bioactive fraction.F7 fraction derived from A. cuspidata has cytotoxic and antitumor activity against ovarian cancer models. Studies are required to characterize the chemical composition of F7 fraction, elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms, and evaluate safety and pharmacological parameters.

Introduction

Worldwide, ovarian cancer was the third most lethal gynecological neoplasm, causing 206,956 deaths in 2022.1 In Mexico, ovarian cancer ranked third, producing 3360 deaths in 2022,1 and in many cases its diagnosis is made in advanced stages. Treatments for ovarian cancer include surgery followed by chemotherapy.2 In cancer chemotherapy, cytotoxic agents are used, which are not selective and affect both cancer cells and normal cells, causing severe adverse effects. Carboplatin is the drug of choice for ovarian cancer; however, ovarian cancer cells may be resistant to this drug and the survival rate for ovarian cancer is still not satisfactory.3 The resistance of cancer cells to current drugs and their toxicity make it necessary to search for new, more effective compounds with less cytotoxicity. Numerous methods have been employed to obtain compounds for drug discovery, including the long-standing practice of isolating them from medicinal plants.

In México, species of the genus Acalypha are used in several regions of the country against different types of cancer, such as cancer of the mouth, stomach, intestines, or skin; they are also used against skin infections or vaginal infections.4 Several compounds have been identified (167) from 19 species of this genus, and 16 of these have been reported as bioactive.4 Acalypha cuspidata (Jacq.) was studied extensively by Rosquete Porcar,5 who evaluated the cytostatic, spasmolytic, diuretic, and laxative activity of the essential oil of this species, and identified twenty-two compounds, eleven of them new. Its antimicrobial activity has also been observed in vitro against different strains of pathogenic bacteria.6 Lira-De León et al.7 observed that fractions of intermediate polarity (AaA7 and AaA8), derived from methanol extract of A. cuspidata had antitopoisomerase activity, an essential enzyme in DNA replication, and therefore in cell division,7, 8 suggesting A. cuspidata could be promising as a source of anticancer drugs. Although previous studies of A. cuspidata have explored methanolic extractions and its antimicrobial and antitopoisomerase activity, these investigations were often limited in scope, focusing on single bioactivities or lacking evaluations on cell lines. The present study bridges these gaps by using extraction with ethyl acetate, to allow enrichment of intermediate polarity compounds, which appear to be responsible for the antitopoisomerase activity and, through the use of primary cultures, cell lines and animal models. Given these previous findings, a bioactivity-guided fractionation approach was employed to identify fractions with selective anticancer activity and to evaluate their effects on ovarian cancer progression.

Thus, based on previous research, this study aimed to perform a bioactivity-guided evaluation of fractions obtained from an ethyl acetate extract of A. cuspidata and to determine the anticancer activity of the most active fraction on the SKOV-3 ovarian cancer cell line through in vitro and in vivo assays.

Material and methods

Plant extract

The plant A. cuspidata was collected from the Chepilme Botanical Garden, Pochutla, Oaxaca, México, where this plant is maintained and propagated for conservation, research, and educational purposes. A voucher specimen (25068) was deposited at the “Jorge Espinosa Salas” Herbarium of the Autonomous University of Chapingo, Texcoco, México. The aerial parts of the plant (flowers, leaves, and stem) were processed as previously described by Lira-De León et al.,7 consisting of cleaning, drying at 40 °C in a drying stove (BINDER, ED56), grinding, sieving (mesh No. 10), and macerated (200 g) with methanol (Me, 1000 mL) and left to stand for 2 weeks at room temperature (25 °C), in the absence of light. The extract was passed through filter paper (No. 1 Whatman), and the filtrates were concentrated to dryness under vacuum. The plant residue was further macerated with ethyl acetate (Ea, 1000 mL) for 2 weeks. The extract was passed through filter paper, and the filtrates were concentrated to dryness. Non-polar solvents (e.g. chloroform or n-hexane) were excluded according to the report of Lira-De León et al.7 who found activity only in Ea and Me. Each extract was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), and a concentrated stock was kept at 4 °C for further use.

Fractionation of the ethyl acetate extract

Ethyl acetate extract from A. cuspidata (9.8 g) was fractionated by open column chromatography, using a column (500 mm × 50 mm) packed with Silica gel 60 (230–400 mesh ASTM) and a combination of hexane: ethyl acetate with increasing solvent polarity. Volumes of 100 mL (Table S1) were collected, and the chromatographic profile was monitored using thin-layer chromatography (TLC) observed by visible light, short ultraviolet (UV) light (254 nm), long UV light (365 nm), and oxidation by resublimated iodine. Chromatographic fractions (F1-F11, Table S1) were obtained by pooling consecutive subfractions collected during column chromatography, which showed similar chromatographic behavior by TLC under the same solvent system. Although slight differences in band intensity were observed, the grouped subfractions shared comparable Rf values and overall profiles, which justified their combination. The fractions were protected from light and kept at −20 °C. For biological assays, fractions were dissolved in DMSO (100 mg / mL) and passed through a 0.22 μm filter (Pall Corporation, 4554).

Cell lines

Ovarian carcinoma cells (SKOV-3 ATCC 30–2007) were grown in McCoy's 5 A (Gibco, 16600–082), whereas the immortalized human bronchial epithelial cell line (BEAS-2B ATCC CRL-9609) was grown in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Gibco, 31600–083). Both culture media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (JRScientific Inc., 43640–500) and 1% streptomycin / penicillin (PAA, P11–002). Cultures were maintained at 37 °C in 5% CO2.

Cytotoxic assay

Cells (5 × 104 / 2 mL) were incubated in 24-well plates for 24 h. Then, they were treated with different concentrations of each extract (12.5–400 μg / mL) or fractions (5–120 μg / mL) or carboplatin (CB, 2.5–80 μg / mL) as a positive control during 72 h at 37 °C9. DMSO (0.1%, vehicle) was used as the negative control in all the assays. The cell viability was examined using the water-soluble tetrazolium salt (WST-1) assay according to the manufacturer's instructions (05015944001, Roche Diagnostics, Meylan, France). Absorbance measurements were performed in an Absorbance Microplate Reader (BioRad model 680) at 450 nm, and cell viability was obtained using Eq. 1:%Cell viability=Sample absorbanceUntreated cells absorbanceX100

The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) indicates the concentration required to inhibit cell viability by 50% and was calculated using GraphPad Prism. Likewise, the selectivity index (SI) shows the ability of the compound to selectively target cancer cells without affecting normal cells,10 and this parameter was obtained using Eq. 2:SI=IC50non cancerous cell lineIC50cancer cell line

Cytotoxicity of fractions on primary culture of rat hepatocytes

The effect of the fractions on the viability of primary cultured rat hepatocytes was evaluated according to the method described by Shen et al.11 For this, adult rats (200–300 g body weight) were housed in a controlled microenvironment at the Laboratory Animal Production and Experimentation Unit at Center for Research and Advanced Studies (UPEAL, CINVESTAV); the institution fulfills the technical specifications for production, care, maintenance, and use of laboratory animals, and it is certified by the Mexican regulations (NOM-062-ZOO-1999). The Institutional Ethics Committee approved all procedures (No. 0121–03). The animals were anesthetized with intraperitoneal Ketamine (87 mg / kg body weight) plus Xylazine (13 mg / Kg). The hepatic portal vein was exposed, and the liver was perfused with Perfusion Buffer I (HBSS, Invitrogen, 14174; 0.9 mM MgCl2; 0.5 mM EDTA; 25 mM HEPES). Subsequently, it was perfused for 5 min with 1% collagenase in perfusion buffer II (HBSS, Invitrogen, 14025; 25 mM HEPES). The liver was removed, and cuts were made to release the hepatocytes in DMEM with 10% fetal bovine serum, and allowed to settle for 20 min. The medium was discarded, and the pelleted hepatocytes were resuspended in fresh medium. The cells were then centrifuged with Percoll (two volumes of cell suspension mixed with 18 volumes of Percoll) at 1100 rpm for 5 min. The obtained pellet was resuspended in DMEM with 10% fetal bovine serum, and the hepatocytes were counted with trypan blue to verify that the viability was greater than 90%. Once the above was confirmed, 5 × 104 / 2 mL were seeded in 24-well plates and incubated for 24 h, after which time the medium was changed. The corresponding treatment was added: F4 and F7 fractions (12.5, 25, and 50 μg / mL), CB as positive control (12.5, 25, and 50 μg / mL), and DMSO (0.1%) as vehicle. After 72 h, micrographs were taken using a Nikon microscope, and cell viability was measured by WST-1.

Detection of apoptosis by flow cytometry

Cells (5 × 104 / 2 mL) were incubated for 24 h, then they were treated for 72 h with vehicle (0.1%), CB (0.25 μg / mL), and F7 fraction (28 μg / mL). After treatment, cells were detached with trypsin (Sigma-Aldrich, 59427C), washed twice with 1× binding buffer (500 μL), and incubated with Annexin V-Fluorescein (FITC, 5 μL) and propidium iodide (5 μL) at room temperature for 10 min in the dark,12 according to the manufacturer's instructions (Biovision, K101–100). Cells were read in the FACS-Calibur flow cytometer (Becton Dickinson). Data were analyzed using Flowing Software version 2.5.

Transmission electron microscopy

Cells (5 × 104 / 2 mL) were incubated for 24 h, then they were treated for 72 h with vehicle (0.1%), CB (0.25 μg / mL), and F7 fraction (28 μg / mL). The samples were fixed in 2.5% (v / v) glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer, pH 7.2, for 60 min 13. They were then post-fixed for 60 min with 1% (w / v) osmium tetroxide in the same buffer. Then, they were dehydrated in increasing ethanol and propylene oxide concentrations, and the samples were embedded in Polybed epoxy resins and polymerized at 60 °C for 24 h. Thin sections (60 nm) were contrasted with uranyl acetate and lead citrate before being examined in a Jeol JEM-011 electron microscope (Jeol Ltd., Tokyo, Japan).

Assessment of cell division by CFSE

Cell proliferation was tested using the fluorescent dye carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester (CFSE) as suggested by the manufacturer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, C34554). Briefly, cells (1 × 106 / mL) collected in logarithmic phase of growth, were incubated in CFSE (5 μM) for 20 min at 37 °C in the absence of light. Then, two washes with PBS were performed, and cells were resuspended in culture medium. Cells (5 × 104 cells / 2 mL) were incubated in a 24-well plate for 24 h in the presence of medium without serum to synchronize the cells. The medium was changed by adding serum and the vehicle (DMSO 0.1%), mitomycin (0.01 mg / mL), CB (50 μg / mL), or F7 fraction (28 μg / mL) to further incubate for 72 h 14. After the corresponding time, cells were detached with trypsin (Sigma-Aldrich, 59427C), washed, and resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) solution for later reading in the Fluorescence-Activated Cell Sorting (FACS)-Calibur flow cytometer (Becton Dickinson). Data were analyzed using Flowing Software version 2.5. The H-1 region represented the group of undivided cells, as it presented greater fluorescence and constituted most of the cells treated with mitomycin. The remaining region (H-2) was considered to represent dividing cells. Cell proliferation percentage was calculated using Eq. 3:%Cellproliferation=undivided cells of treatmentUndivided cells of vehicleX100

Scanning electron microscopy

Cells (5 × 104) were adhered and grown on coverslips (9 × 9 mm) for 24 h. Then, the cells were treated for 72 h with carboplatin or F7 fraction (28 μg / mL). Then, cells were fixed in 2.5% (v / v) glutaraldehyde and dehydrated in ethanol before critical point drying with CO2 in a Samdri-780 apparatus (Tousimis). Samples were gold-coated with an ion sputtering device (JEOL-JFC-1100) and examined in a Zeiss DSM-982 Gemini scanning electron microscope.15

In vivo model

Female athymic BALB / c nude (Nu / Nu) mice (18–22 g) were housed in a controlled and sterile microenvironment at the UPEAL-CINVESTAV; the Institutional Ethics Committee approved all procedures (No. 184–16). SKOV3 cells (5 × 106 cells in 0.1 mL of PBS) were injected subcutaneously at the right flank of female nude mice.9 When the tumor reached approximately 5 × 5 mm in size, between the fourth and fifth weeks, animals were divided randomly into four groups (n = 3 mice per group). The control group was treated only with vehicle. Intraperitoneal (i.p.) administration of F7 fraction (40 mg / kg) dissolved in PBS 16, was performed twice per week, for four weeks17, 18; tumor size was determined weekly for four weeks by measurement of the length (L) and width (W) of the tumor.9 At the end of treatments, mice were anesthetized with 0.1575 mg / 250 μL in PBS (v / v, i.p.) of sodium pentobarbital (PiSA®, Guadalajara, Jal., M.X.) for subsequent analyses. Tumor volumes were calculated as TV (mm3) = (L × W2) / 2, and weight was obtained with an electronic balance (CS200, Ohaus®, Parsippany, NJ, USA). Finally, we evaluated (i) body weight, (ii) liver weight and morphology, and (iii) tumoral lesions with scanning electron microscopy.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the GraphPad Prism software version 5.01 (GraphPad Software, Inc., California, USA). All results were expressed as means ± standard deviation (SD) of three independent experiments in triplicate. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or Student's t-test were performed, using the Bonferroni post-hoc test; p-values <0.05 were taken to indicate statistical significance.

F7 fraction showed no cytotoxicity on primary rat hepatocytes

Drugs are metabolized mainly in the liver, so the primary culture of hepatocytes is widely used to screen the metabolic toxicity of new drugs. Then, another approach to study further F4 and F7 fractions, was to measure the viability of primary culture hepatocytes treated with three concentrations (50, 25, and 12.5 μg / mL) of these two fractions and CB. Only F4 fraction was hepatotoxic at 50 μg / mL. F7 fraction did not induce loss of viability on the primary culture of rat hepatocytes (Fig. 1A); furthermore, morphology was similar to that observed in hepatocytes treated with vehicle (Fig. 1B). The F7 fraction concentration required to affect the viability of BEAS-2B and hepatocytes was greater than IC50 to SKOV-3 (28 μg / mL), demonstrating selective activity against SKOV-3 cells. To confirm this information, the ultrastructure of SKOV-3 cells treated with F7 fraction was analyzed (Fig. 3). The nuclei of the cells treated with carboplatin (Fig. 3B) and F7 fraction (Fig. 3C) were markedly condensed, a hallmark of the apoptotic process, unlike the cells treated with vehicle (Fig. 3A). Likewise, cells exposed to carboplatin (Fig. 3E) showed smaller mitochondria with slight changes in the morphology, where internal cristae were disrupted. The mitochondrial damage of cells treated with F7 fraction was more pronounced (Fig. 3F) than in carboplatin-treated cells, showing that the size decreased and internal cristae disappeared.

F7 fraction inhibits cell proliferation

One of the mechanisms of action of anti-cancer drugs is the inhibition of cell division. To evaluate this effect, proliferation was analyzed in F7 fraction-treated cells using CFSE staining. In vehicle-treated cells, 69% proliferated, while 27% retained the original dye, indicating a non-proliferative state (Fig. 4A). In contrast, higher proportions of non-proliferating cells were observed with mitomycin (86%), carboplatin (89%), and F7 fraction (73%) compared with vehicle. Consistently, F7 fraction (35%), carboplatin (22%), and mitomycin (28%) significantly reduced the percentage of proliferating cells relative to vehicle (Fig. 4B). F7 fraction affects the formation of tumor-like structures in vitro

Following the demonstration of F7 fraction effectiveness in different relevant ovarian cancer models, the effect of F7 fraction on SKOV-3 multilayers that formed tumor-like structures in vitro was explored. The samples were analyzed by scanning electron microscopy. Untreated SKOV-3 cells (Fig. 5A), further grown in the presence of the vehicle for 72 h, formed tumor-like structures (Fig. 5B). However, when the cells were treated with carboplatin for 72 h (Fig. 5C, D), the structures were affected, with a group of damaged cells showing large pores. Furthermore, F7 fraction induced cellular damage, the presence of pores, and a significant number of membrane prolongations (Fig. 5E, F). These tumor-like structures were counted in each coverslip: vehicle, 14; carboplatin, 0; and F7 fraction, 8 structures. Thus, it was observed that the number of tumor-like cell aggregates decreased in the presence of carboplatin and F7 fraction. F7 fraction decreases the size of SKOV-3-induced tumors in nu / nu mice

To determine the effect of F7 fraction on reducing SKOV-3 induced tumors in Nu / Nu mice, cells were inoculated subcutaneously to monitor tumor growth (day zero). When the tumors were 5 × 5 mm in size, CB (50 mg / kg) or F7 fraction (40 mg / kg) was administered intraperitoneally twice a week for four weeks (Fig. 6A). During that time, the tumor volume was also determined, and the size of the tumors was observed throughout the four weeks. The tumor volume increased over time. In animals treated with vehicle, the volume increased by approximately 700 mm3, whereas carboplatin and F7 fraction inhibited tumor growth at 200 and 100 mm3, respectively (Fig. 6B). Discussion

Partial progress has been made concerning new drugs to improve ovarian cancer patients' survival; therefore, the development of new drugs with higher effectiveness and fewer secondary effects is necessary. Natural products have been the source of new drugs for treatment of different diseases, specifically, in cancer.19 Notably, plants have played a significant role in producing effective anti-cancer agents; some plant derivatives include vincristine, vinblastine, irinotecan, topotecan, etoposide, podophyllotoxin, and paclitaxel. Species of the Acalypha genus used in traditional medicine have at least one type of biological activity listed below: antimicrobial, antidiabetic, antioxidant, antiinflammatory, larvicide, hepatoprotective, anti-cancer, leishmanicide, antihyperglycemic, anti-hypertensive, antipoison, analgesic, antiemetic, laxative, expectorant, diuretic, anti-fertility, and healing.4

In the present study, fractions (F4, F5, F6 and F7) derived from A. cuspidata showed lower IC50 (33.01, 23.87, 13.26 and 27.78 μg / mL, respectively) against SKOV-3 ovarian cancer cells than the extract from which they were derived (86.46 μg / mL). The increase in fractions bioactivity has been reported in other studies when the bioactive fraction is isolated.9, 20, 21 The ICLH fraction derived from Imperata cylindrica had an IC50 of 141 μg / mL against oral carcinoma cells SCC-9, and the AF02 fraction derived from Rhus trilobata was 38 μg/mL against ovarian cancer cells SKOV-3.9, 22 These four fractions derived from A. cuspidata were more effective than other bioactive fractions reported in previous studies.

The BEAS-2B bronchial epithelial cell line is widely used because it is an immortalized but non-tumorigenic human cell line established from normal bronchial epithelium. This cell line is commonly used for screening new bioactive compounds affecting carcinogenesis or cell proliferation.23 In the present study, BEAS-2B was used to obtain the SI values of all fractions. F4 and F7 fractions had the best SI (1.48 and 1.39, respectively), showing the ability of the fraction to selectively target cancer cells without affecting normal cells. The SI of both fractions (F4 and F7) was comparable to that of carboplatin (1.23), but IC50 values of F4 and F7 fractions (33.01 and 27.78 μg / mL, respectively) were lower than carboplatin (47.47 μg / mL), suggesting F4 and F7 fractions could represent promising fractions for further bioactivity-guided anticancer studies.

Although F4 and F7 fractions showed comparable or greater anticancer activity than carboplatin on SKOV-3, their safety profile remains to be fully established. Further pharmacokinetic, toxicological, and mechanistic studies are required before considering clinical applications. In that sense, the liver metabolizes a wide range of drugs, therefore, it is one of the main organs affected by the administration of drugs.24 Drugs, herbs, and supplements have been associated with hepatotoxicity, for that reason, evaluation of liver damage is important in drug discovery studies. Carboplatin (1.23) and F7 fraction (1.39) demonstrated a lower selective index toward BEAS-2B cells compared to F4 (1.48); however, toxicity data in primary hepatocytes indicate that carboplatin and F7 fraction are less toxic than F4. This could be explained because drug toxicity varies depending on its mechanism of action. Carboplatin has reduced hepatotoxicity25 and in this study it was observed that F7 fraction showed lower cytotoxicity in primary hepatocytes compared with F4.

Thus, F7 fraction was the most active (28 μg / mL) and selective of all fractions derived from ethyl acetate extract, hence, further characterization of F7 fraction may help determine its potential as a candidate for anticancer drug development continuing its study will allow us to move forward to know if F7 fraction could be an anti-cancer potential candidate.

Natural product-derived drugs have different mechanisms of action on cancer; some interfere with the initiation, development, and progression of cancer through the modulation of various mechanisms, including cell proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, angiogenesis, and metastasis.26 Data indicate that F7 fraction-treated cells showed morphological characteristics of cell death observed by flow cytometry and TEM, such as externalized phosphatidylserine, internalization of propidium iodide, condensation of the nucleus, and mitochondrial damage, suggesting that the cells were likely undergoing apoptosis could be dying mainly via apoptosis.27

The anti-tumor properties of carboplatin are attributed to its binding to DNA, causing interference with its replication, transcription, and other nuclear functions, and arresting cell proliferation and tumor growth.28 Carboplatin can bind to the mtDNA, leading to the formation of adducts and cross-links and, consequently, to its damage; this results in a change in the permeability of the mitochondrial membranes, which leads to apoptosis.29 These reports agree with our results, where carboplatin induced apoptosis and slightly damaged mitochondria. F7 fraction caused greater damage to the mitochondria than carboplatin, resulting in a smaller size and disappearance of the internal cristae. The observed changes in mitochondrial size and morphology of F7 fraction-treated cells could be related to energy deficits and greater mitochondrial dysfunction30 or suggest that F7 fraction compounds targeted mitochondrial molecules. Therefore, the mechanism by which F7 fraction induces apoptosis could be through mitochondrial damage; however, these data need to be studied more deeply.

Carboplatin disrupts normal DNA function in tumor cells through intrastrand and interstrand cross-linking.29 Our results showed that carboplatin induced electron-dense vacuoles inside the nucleus, which suggests DNA adduct formation. F7 fraction decreased SKOV-3 cell proliferation, like carboplatin. Fractions derived from A. cuspidata have shown inhibition against topoisomerase, an enzyme involved in DNA replication.7 This result could suggest that compounds present in F7 fraction target some molecules implicated in cell division, such as topoisomerases. Future studies on the bioactive compounds present in F7 fraction and their targets are necessary to elucidate the cellular mechanism of action.

The administration of F7 fraction in mice inoculated with SKOV-3 cells showed that tumor development was inhibited, similar to the cytostatic effect observed with carboplatin. F7 fraction (40 mg / Kg) reduced tumor size similar to carboplatin (50 mg / Kg) and other fractions reported in other studies.9, 22 Tumors induced by vehicle and F7 fraction were liquid, in contrast, those from carboplatin-treated animals were more solid. These observations are preliminary and may reflect differences in cellular density, necrosis, or extracellular matrix composition as has been reported in previous studies.9 The tumors were analyzed by transmission electron microscopy, revealing that those from animals treated with carboplatin and F7 fraction exhibited highly condensed nuclei and structures that could be autophagosomes (Fig. S2, white arrows). These results suggest that carboplatin and F7 fraction may induce apoptosis in tumors, consistent with observations made in vitro. It was also observed that the mitochondria of the F7 fraction-treated tumors were damaged, a finding consistent with previous in vitro observations, suggesting a similar in vivo mechanism of action.

A hepatotoxicity parameter that has been used is the ratio of liver weight to body weight.31 Therefore, the sacrificed mice's livers were dissected and weighed. No visible changes were observed in the appearance of the liver (Fig. S3A) or the ratio of liver weight to body weight (Fig. S3B), suggesting that F7 fraction is not hepatotoxic, as previously found in vitro. This correlation of toxicity results between in vitro and in vivo assays suggests that F7 fraction has a tumor-specific effect with far fewer effects on non-cancerous cells.

This study aimed, for the first time, to evaluate the in vitro and in vivo effect of F7 fraction (from A. cuspidata) on the SKOV-3 ovarian cancer cell line. F7 fraction exhibited selective activity against this cell line, inhibiting cell proliferation and inducing apoptosis; this activity was conserved when F7 fraction was administered to animals, resulting in reduced tumor size. SKOV-3 is an ovarian cancer model with epithelial-like morphology. There are reports where compounds are less effective against SKOV-3 than against other ovarian cancer cell lines, such as Ovcar-3 or A2780,9, 32, 33 demonstrating the resistance of SKOV-3 to cytotoxic drugs. The F7 fraction antitumoral effect observed on SKOV-3 allows us to propose an important next step: to examine F7 fraction on other cell lines to scale up and confirm the effectiveness of this fraction with different types of ovarian cancer.

Finally, in this study, TLC was performed to analyze the composition of F7 fraction, using Silica gel 60 as the stationary phase and hexane: ethyl acetate (6:4) as the mobile phase. Five bands were identified in the F7 fraction chromatographic profile (Fig. S4), where two bands, B1 and B3, probably correspond to chlorophylls. This result suggests that compounds with a retention factor close to chlorophylls could be responsible for the anti-cancer activity. F7 fraction was obtained using a 70:30 (n-hexane:EtOAc) solvent system, indicating a predominantly non-polar character. Although thin-layer chromatography suggests an enrichment of nonpolar constituents, such as fatty acids and chlorophyll-related compounds, the absence of advanced analytical techniques (e.g., LC-HRMS or NMR) precludes the identification of specific molecules responsible for the observed anticancer effects. We acknowledge that F7 fraction still contains multiple compounds. The presence of chlorophylls could be of paramount importance for the anti-tumor activity of F7 fraction, as has been previously reported.34, 35

Despite the relevant findings presented in this study, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, a major limitation of this study is the absence of advanced phytochemical characterization techniques such as LC-MS or NMR, which are necessary to identify the specific bioactive compounds responsible for the observed anticancer effects. Therefore, the findings of this study should be interpreted as preliminary and hypothesis-generating evidence derived from a bioactivity-guided exploratory approach. Future studies will focus on a detailed phytochemical characterization to establish structure–activity relationships.

Second, while the in vitro assays demonstrated selective cytotoxicity and apoptosis-related features in SKOV-3 cells, the molecular mechanisms underlying these effects at the signaling pathway level were not explored. The lack of analysis of specific apoptotic markers, such as caspase activation or Bcl-2 family proteins, limits the mechanistic interpretation of the observed cell death.

Third, although the in vivo xenograft model showed a significant reduction in tumor volume following F7 fraction treatment, histopathological evaluation of tumor tissues and the expression of pro-apoptotic or proliferation-related markers were not assessed. Therefore, the antitumor effects observed in vivo should be interpreted as preliminary evidence that warrants further validation through histological and molecular analyses.

Finally, this study was designed as a bioactivity-guided exploratory evaluation rather than a comprehensive pharmacological characterization. Consequently, pharmacokinetic parameters, toxicity profiles beyond tumor growth inhibition, and long-term safety assessments were not addressed and remain to be investigated.

Conclusions

In this study, we performed a bioactivity-guided evaluation of fractions obtained from an ethyl acetate extract of A. cuspidata and identified F7 fraction as the most active against SKOV-3 ovarian cancer cells. F7 fraction exhibited selective cytotoxicity in vitro on cancer cells, inducing morphological and biochemical features consistent with apoptosis, while exhibiting lower cytotoxicity toward non-cancerous cells.

The antitumor activity of F7 fraction was further supported by a reduction in tumor growth in a SKOV-3 xenograft model in Nu / Nu mice. However, these in vivo findings should be interpreted as preliminary, as histopathological and molecular analyses of tumor tissues were not performed. Likewise, the absence of a comprehensive chemical characterization of F7 fraction limits the identification of the specific compounds responsible for the observed biological effects.

Taken together, our results provide experimental evidence that A. cuspidata contains bioactive constituents with cytotoxic and antitumor activity against ovarian cancer models. Nevertheless, further studies are required to characterize the chemical composition of the active fraction, elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms, and evaluate safety and pharmacological parameters. These efforts will be essential to determine the translational relevance of A. cuspidata derived fractions as potential sources of anticancer agents.
