Tempeh, a fermented soybean, extends lifespan via MAPK pathway and improves healthy aging in Caenorhabditiselegans
Authors: Junhyo Cho, Chan-Hyo Cho, Yong Hoon Joo, Cheng-i Wei, Seong-Ho Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTempeh, a traditional fermented soybean product, is widely consumed for its nutritional and health-promoting properties, but its impact on aging is still unclear. In this study, we investigated the effects of tempeh on lifespan and healthspan using C. elegans as the study model. Soybean and tempeh extracts at concentrations between 50 and 200 μg/ml were tested. Both treatments significantly extended mean lifespan, with the greatest effect observed at 100 μg/ml. Tempeh required skn-1 and pmk-1 to extend the lifespan of C. elegans, suggesting PMK-1–SKN-1 pathway as the key signaling pathway, and independent to caloric restriction, insulin/insulin-like growth factor signaling, AMP-activated protein kinase, or sirtuin signaling pathways. Tempeh treatment at both 50 μg/ml and 100 μg/ml significantly improved healthspan during midlife, and reduced intracellular reactive oxygen species levels compared to the control group in C. elegans. These findings indicate that tempeh may promote healthy aging by enhancing oxidative stress resistance through MAPK signaling pathway. This study highlights the potential of tempeh as a functional food source for healthy aging improvement.

Introduction

Tempeh is a traditional fermented soybean product, commonly accomplished by using Rhizopus species. It is widely consumed in Southeast Asia and gaining global recognition for its health-promoting properties (Teoh et al., 2024). Tempeh is known to be rich in proteins, dietary fiber, and various bioactive nutrients, including isoflavones and antioxidants, which offers various health benefits such as anti-inflammation and antidiabetic effects (Teoh et al., 2024). While many studies have reported the potential health benefits of tempeh, its impact on aging remains unclear.

Aging is an inevitable biological process that gradually decreases the physiological function and increases the risk of chronic diseases including impared mobility and neurodegeneration (Cho et al., 2023). Thus, delaying the aging process can improve healthspan. However, aging studies face challenges and limitations, including identifying the working mechanisms, due to long lifespan of experimental models. To overcome these challenges and limitations, many researchers have used Caenorhabditis elegans for aging studies.

C. elegans is a transparent invertebrate eukaryotic animal model with a relatively short lifespan with over 65 % of its genes homologous to human disease-associated genes (Shen et al., 2018). C. elegans can be easily maintained under laboratory conditions with Escherichia coli OP50 as a standard food source (Cho et al., 2025). With a lifespan of 2–3 weeks, C. elegans offers advantages on aging studies (Shen et al., 2018). The current study aims to determine the effect of tempeh on healthy aging and the potential working mechanisms using C. elegans.

Material and methods

Materials

Soybean was purchased from local market (Queen Anne, MD, USA). Rhizopus microsporus var. oligosporus (CBS 388.34) was obtained from Raprima Ragi Tempe Inokulum produced by LIPI Bandung (Indonesia) (Hartanti et al., 2015). Ampicillin and carbenicillin were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). All other chemicals and materials were purchased from Fisher Scientific (Pittsburgh, PA, USA). All C. elegans strains and E. coli OP50 were purchased from the Caenorhabditis Genetics Center (University of Minnesota, Minneapolis, MN, USA).

Tempeh fermentation

Soybeans were washed and soaked in water for 2 h at room temperature. After they were boiled for 20 min and cooled in water overnight, they were dehulled and boiled again for 10 min. After the cooled soybeans were inoculated with Rhizopus and mixed evenly, they were placed in polypropylene bags with 2 mm holes in 1–2 cm intervals across their surface and incubated at 27 °C for 12 h. Bags were then flipped and incubated for an additional 24 h.

Soybean and tempeh extraction

Both soybean and tempeh were lyophilized for 24 h and then ground into powder. Soybean or tempeh powders were mixed in n-hexane (1:3 w/v) and extracted for 1 h at room temperature in shaking incubator at 300 rpm. Samples were defatted three times with 70 % ethanol for 10 min, sonicated, and stirred for 50 min. The supernatant of soybean and tempeh were filtered twice using filter paper (25 μm) and filter units (0.22 μm). The filtered supernatants were evaporated to remove ethanol and lyophilized to obtain extract powder.

C. elegans maintenance

C. elegans was maintained based on WormBook and previous publications with slight modifications (Cho et al., 2025; Stiernagle, 2006). C. elegans strains used for the study were: N2 Bristol (wild-type), daf-2(e1370)III, daf-16(mgDf50)I, eat-2(ad1116)II, sir-2.1(ok434)IV, aak-2(ok524)X, skn-1(mg570)IV, and pmk-1(km25)IV. Worms were maintained using nematode growth media (NGM). Eggs have been collected by removing the worms with sodium hydroxide/bleach solution and obtained synchronized worms. Synchronized L1 stage worms were grown in liquid nematode growth medium (S-complete) containing ampicillin (100 μg/ml) and carbenicillin (50 μg/ml). Live E. coli OP50 was fed as standard food source and incubated at 20 °C until worms were grown to L4 stage, except the daf-2 mutant at 15 °C.

Lifespan assay

L4 stage worms were transferred to Transwell®-96 well permeable plates (Corning Inc, NY, USA) and 2′-deoxy-5-fluorouridine (FUDR) at final concentration of 120 μM was added to S-complete to prevent eggs from hatching starting from L4 stage. Soybean and tempeh extract were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), and a final concentration range of 50–200 μM was used for treatment. All treatments, including control group, had a final concentration of 0.1 % DMSO. Media was exchanged with fresh media containing treatment and E. coli OP50 every 2 days using the Transwell®-96 well permeable plates with 3 μm membrane pore size (Cho et al., 2025). After 24 h of incubation, young adult stage worms were treated with samples and defined as day 0 of adult stage. Survival rate was recorded every 2 days until all worms died. Each treatment group has approximately 80–100 worms and all experiments were repeated 3 times.

Bending rate measurement

Bending rate has been previously reported to be able to represent various healthspan markers including pumping rate and speed (Cho et al., 2025). Synchronized L4 stage worms were seeded on a 6-well culture plate with S-complete. Worms were fed with E. coli OP50 and incubated at 20 °C for 24 h until first day of adult stage. S-complete medium was changed to fresh medium every 2 days and maintained total volume of 2 ml per well. Twelve random worms were selected to measure body bend under an optical microscope (Nikon SMZ745 Stereo Microscope, Nikon Instruments Inc., Melville, NY). Total number of body bends per 30 s were recorded every 2 days until all worms died.

Determination of healthy aging

Healthy aging was determined using ‘Dynamic-Scaled Value’ by Cho et al. (2025), which has been reported to determine health status by simultaneously considering lifespan and healthspan. Dynamic-scaled values using bending rate (DB(Sr)) from tempeh treatment was calculated using Equation (1). When the survival rate between control and treatment group was not precisely equal, linear interpolation was used to obtain the corresponding values. For control group data, the control values were used instead of treatment values. As a result, control values showed 1.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels

Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were measured using 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (H2DCF-DA) as previously described with modifications (Cho et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2023). Previously, 96 h was suggested as a timepoint when worms were significantly impacted by oxidative stress (Sun et al., 2024). Thus, tempeh was treated for 96 h and worms were washed thrice with M9 buffer to remove bacteria. Approximately 50 worms were transferred to 100 mL of M9 buffer containing 50 mM H2DCF-DA and incubated at 20 °C in dark for 2.5 h. Group with 50 mM H2DCF-DA and 0.1 % H2O2 was regarded as positive control. ROS levels were measured using SpectraMax i3 multimode microplate detection platform (Sunnyvale, CA) with emission at 535 nm, excitation at 485 nm, and temperature at 25 °C.

Statistical analysis

Lifespan data was analyzed with Log-rank (Mantel-Cox) tests. Average lifespan data, maximum lifespan, and intracellular ROS level measurement were analyzed with one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple comparison test. All data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism ver. 10.4.2 (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Groups with different letters are considered significantly different at P < 0.05.

DB(Sr) data were analyzed using polynomial heterogenous ANCOVA models, which is known to be the suitable method for analysis that incorporates the interactions between survival rate, treatment, time, and their interactions (Cho et al., 2025). To account for potential nonlinear effects across the full range of survival rates, both linear and quadratic polynomial functions were applied. The DP(Sr) data, which includes zero values observed in later life stages, was analyzed using a polynomial Tweedie ANCOVA model. The model incorporated first-, second-, and third-order polynomial terms to capture the curvature associated with survival rate. All ANCOVA analysis were followed by Tukey's multiple comparison test. Dynamic-scaled values were analyzed using the PROC MIXED, PROC GLIMMIX, and PROC GENMOD procedures in SAS software ver. 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Data were considered significant at P < 0.05.

Results

Soybean and tempeh extended lifespan of C. elegans

Previous studies have reported that soybean increased oxidative stress resistance at a high concentration up to 300 μg/ml in C. elegans and low concentration of 50 μg/ml in human Jurkat cells (J. Liu et al., 2025, Liu et al., 2025; Tanaka et al., 2014). Thus, a dose-response experiment using both soybean and tempeh extracts at concentrations between 50 and 200 μg/ml was selected to determine their lifespan extension effect in C. elegans based on previous publications. Both treatments significantly extended lifespan (Fig. 1). When worms were treated with soybean extract, the mean lifespan increased by approximately 30 %, 38 %, and 29 % at 50, 100, and 200 μg/ml, respectively. Tempeh increased the mean lifespan by 32 %, 45 %, and 39 % at 50, 100, and 200 μg/ml, respectively. Both soybean and tempeh showed the maximum lifespan extension at 100 μg/ml. Thus, tempeh at 50 and 100 μg/ml was selected as treatment concentrations for the following experiment.

Soybean and tempeh extracts extended the lifespan of wild-type C. elegans. The lifespan of C. elegans treated with soybean extract (A,B) and tempeh extract (C,D) were increased. Wild-type C. elegans were treated with samples at between 50 and 200 μg/ml (in 0.1 % DMSO) or 0.1 % DMSO as control group. Initial treatment was added from the young-adult stage (Day 0). Media exchange and survival rate of the worms were scored every 2 days until all worms were dead. Statistical analysis was performed using the log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test for survival curve and one-way ANOVA follwed by Tukey's multiple comparison for mean lifespan (n = 3 independent experiments; 85–111 worms/treatment in each experiment). Means with different letters are significantly different at P < 0.05.

Lifespan extension effect of tempeh is independent to food intake

Caloric restriction is well-known to extend the lifespan across species including C. elegans (Shen et al., 2018). Thus, we used eat-2 mutant, which carries a loss-of-function in the acetylcholine receptor gene, resulting in impaired pharyngeal pumping and reduced food intake, to determine whether the lifespan extension effect of tempeh is dependent on reduced food intake (Shen et al., 2018). Tempeh treatment at both 50 and 100 μg/ml significantly extended the lifespan of eat-2 mutants compared to the control (Fig. 2A and G). This indicates tempeh extends lifespan independent to food intake.

skn-1 is required for tempeh extract to increase the lifespan in C. elegans. Tempeh extract concentration at 50–100 μg/ml (in 0.1 % DMSO) or 0.1 % DMSO as control was treated to eat-2, daf-2, daf-16, aak-2, skn-1, and sir-2.1 null mutants (A–F). Tempeh extract treatment increased the lifespan and average lifespan of eat-2, daf-2, daf-16, aak-2, and sir-2.1 null mutants (A-D, F, G), except skn-1 null mutant (E, G). Media exchange and survival rate of the worms were scored every 2 days until all worms were dead. Statistical analysis was performed using the log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test for survival curve and one-way ANOVA follwed by Tukey's multiple comparison for mean lifespan (n = 3 independent experiments; 82–128 worms/treatment in each experiment). Means with different letters are significantly different at P < 0.05.

Tempeh does not target insulin/insulin-like growth factor-1 signaling pathway

To determine whether the lifespan extension effect of tempeh is dependent on insulin/insulin growth factor-1 signaling (IIS) pathway, we investigated its effect on daf-2 and daf-16 mutant strains. The IIS pathway is known to be one of the key regulators in aging, having DAF-2 as the homolog of insulin/insulin-like growth factor-1 receptor and DAF-16 encoding mammalian Forkhead box O transcription factor as its downstream target (Cho et al., 2025). Tempeh treatment at both 50 and 100 μg/ml extended the lifespan of daf-2 (Fig. 2B and G) and daf-16 (Fig. 2C and G) null mutants. These results suggest that daf-2 and daf-16 are genetically not required for tempeh's lifespan extension effect.

Lifespan extension effect of tempeh is independent to AMPK pathway

aak-2 encodes the catalytic α-subunit of AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) in C. elegans, which regulates cellular energy homeostasis that can modulate the lifespan in response to metabolic stress (Greer et al., 2007). Thus, we investigated the role of AMPK pathway in the lifespan extension effect of tempeh using aak-2 null mutant. Tempeh treatment extended the lifespan of aak-2 null mutants at both concentrations of 50 and 100 μg/ml (Fig. 2D and G), suggesting tempeh does not require aak-2 for its lifespan extension effect and independent to AMPK signaling pathway.

Tempeh extended the lifespan by targeting skn-1

SKN-1, an Nuclear respiratory factor (Nrf) family transcription factor, mediates oxidative stress response to regulate longevity (Blackwell et al., 2015). Our results showed that tempeh treatment failed to extend the lifespan of skn-1 null mutants (Fig. 2E and G), suggesting that skn-1 is genetically required for tempeh's lifespan extension effect.

sir-2.1 is not involved in the lifespan extension effect of tempeh

SIR-2.1 is the homolog of mammalian SIRT1, a member of sirtuin family of NAD + -dependent deacetylases, which is known to extend lifespan by modulating stress resistance (Mouchiroud et al., 2013). We investigated whether SIR-2.1 is required for its effect by using sir-2.1 null mutant. Tempeh treatment at both 50 and 100 μg/ml significantly extend the lifespan of sir-2.1 null mutants compared to the control group (Fig. 2F and G) indicating that sir-2.1 is not essential for the lifespan extension effect of tempeh and suggest that sirtuin pathway is not involved.

pmk-1 is involved in the lifespan extension effect of tempeh

PMK-1 encodes a mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) in C. elegans, which is the homolog to mammalian p38 MAPK (Yuan et al., 2023). The MAPK pathway is known to regulate oxidative stress response, innate immunity, and cellular homeostasis that can impact the lifespan (S. Liu et al., 2025). In addition, skn-1 is known to be a downstream target of MAPK pathway (Yuan et al., 2023). Thus, we investigated the role of MAPK pathway in the lifespan extension effect of tempeh using pmk-1 null mutant. Tempeh treatment failed to extend the lifespan of pmk-1 null mutants, which suggests that pmk-1 is genetically required for the lifespan extension effect of tempeh (Fig. 3). It was previously reported that SKN-1 is activated by PMK-1 via MAPK pathway to extend longevity by maintaining redox balance, which matches with our results that tempeh requires skn-1 and pmk-1 to increase lifespan (Yuan et al., 2023). Thus, the lifespan extension effect of tempeh may depend on MAPK pathway.

Tempeh extract had no effect on lifespan of pmk-1 null mutant. Tempeh extract concentration at between 50 and 100 μg/ml (in 0.1 % DMSO) or 0.1 % DMSO as control were treated to pmk-1 null mutant. Means with different letters are significantly different at P < 0.05.

Tempeh improves healthy aging in C. elegans

Next, we determined the impact of tempeh on healthy aging to understand its potential impact on health during aging. First, we measured bending rate of C. elegans as healthspan marker (Fig. S1). Then dynamic-scaled values were calculated to determine the effect of tempeh on healthy aging. During the mid-stage of aging, tempeh at both concentrations significantly improved health status of C. elegans (Fig. 4). In particular, tempeh at 100 μg/ml significantly improved healthy aging. These results suggest that tempeh treatment can improve the health status during aging process.

(1) and plotted over (A) survival rate and (B) corresponding time as days. The dashed line in (B) indicates the maximum lifespan of the control group. Values represent means ± S.D (n = 12). (C, D) DB(Sr) data of tempeh treated worms were analyzed using polynomial heterogenous ANCOVA models with survival rate, treatment, and their interactions.

Effect of tempeh on intracellular ROS levels

We further investigated the effect of tempeh on intracellular ROS levels in C. elegans. Accumulation of ROS is known to cause cellular damage and reduced ROS levels are known to be associated with increased stress resistance, which can lead to lifespan extension (Shields et al., 2021). Tempeh at both concentrations of 50 μg/ml and 100 μg/ml reduced the ROS levels in C. elegans by approximately 17 ± 1.5 % and 30 ± 2.3 % in a dose-response manner compared to control group (Fig. 5). This result suggests that reduced ROS levels may have contributed to lifespan extension effect of tempeh.

Tempeh extract reduced reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels of wild type C. elegans. Synchronized L1 stage worms were treated with tempeh extract for 96 h. Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) at 0.1 % was used as positive control. Values represent means ± S.E. (n = 3, 50 worms per sample). Means with different letters are significantly different at P < 0.05.

Discussion

Based on the health benefits of soy and fermented products, we investigated the potential impact of tempeh on healthy aging. Our results showed that tempeh significantly extended lifespan and improved physiological function of C. elegans during aging. In addition, we found that tempeh-mediated lifespan extension effect depends on pmk-1-skn-1 signaling of MAPK pathway while reducing ROS and improving healthspan.

The MAPK pathway is known to significantly affect longevity by regulating stress responses, including oxidative stress (Yuan et al., 2023). PMK-1 is the terminal kinase of MAPK signaling that regulates stress responses and activates SKN-1, a crucial transcription factor for ROS neutralization and redox homeostasis (Madrid et al., 2006; Yuan et al., 2023). Our findings show that tempeh failed to extend lifespan only in pmk-1 and skn-1 null mutants, which suggests that pmk-1 and skn-1 are key genes for tempeh's lifespan extension effect.

Oxidative stress from ROS accumulation is a major contributor to aging and reduced lifespan in C. elegans (Shen et al., 2018). Our results showed that tempeh treatment reduced intracellular ROS levels in C. elegans, suggesting that the lifespan extension effect of tempeh is regulated by MAPK pathway to reduce intracellular ROS accumulation during aging.

While tempeh's antioxidative effect appears responsible, its precise mechanism remains unclear. Tempeh contains various bioactive compounds of isoflavones, saponins, and phenolics, which can be found in its original source soybean (Chatterjee et al., 2018; Kim et al., 2021; Tan et al., 2024). These bioactive compounds include genistein, diazein, chlorogenic acid, ferulic acid, malonyldaidzein, and malonylgenistein, which have been suggested to show anti-oxidative effect (Król-Grzymała et al., 2020; Pratt and Birac, 2006). In addition to previous report that tempeh fermentation enhance digestibility of protein and carbohydrates, it is suggested that tempeh may still retain many bioactive compounds from the original source and show similar lifespan extension effect (Purwandari et al., 2024). Genistein is known to be one of the major soy isoflavones to reduce oxidative stress via IIS pathway, heat shock protein, MAPK, dietary restriction, and mitochondrial pathway in C. elegans (Zhang et al., 2023). However, only MAPK pathway was suggested as the working mechanism of tempeh in our study. This discrepancy may be due to a large gap between working concentration of genistein (54 μg/ml) and its content in tempeh extract (0.0155 μg/ml) (Haron et al., 2009). As tempeh contains many other bioactive compounds, the MAPK pathway could have been regulated by other bioactive compounds, such as ferulic acid regulating MAPK pathway in PC-12 rat pheochromocytoma cell and chlorogenic acid regulated MAPK pathway in mice model (Huang et al., 2023; Lin et al., 2015). Thus, this suggests the complexity of bioactivity in food matrix and the need of further understanding of bioactive compounds when present with other nutrients or compounds. As the precise components of tempeh remain unclear, additional studies to analyze these components are essential to understand the working mechanisms.

Although this study suggested that tempeh extract can extend the lifespan of C. elegans by regulating MAPK pathway, it is not without limitations. First, we suggested the bioactive compound in tempeh is conserved from its original source soybean to regulate MAPK pathway, but we have not explicitly determined the working mechanism of soybean extract. Also, the effect of Rhizopus has not been determined. Thus, additional studies on determining the effect of Rhizopus and identifying the working mechanism of soybean extract are needed to further understand the effect of tempeh extract on lifespan extension. Second, while our study used DMSO for control treatment, the study was completed without negative control to determine the potential effect of DMSO with tempeh, which suggests additional study on potential interaction or synergetic effect of DMSO and tempeh is needed. Third, our result suggested that tempeh extend lifespan by targeting skn-1 and pmk-1 in MAPK pathway is solely based on mutant phenotypes. To clarify these findings, additional studies on determination of skn-1 and pmk-1 mRNA expression change, and nuclear localization of SKN-1 using skn-1:GFP strain. Lastly, the chemical composition of tempeh and its original soybean source has not been explicitly investigated. Thus, further investigations on identifying the chemical components is essential to understand tempeh fermentation and underlying mechanisms for longevity. Also, our results are limited to C. elegans and challenges remain on dose translation and bioavailability, which suggest future studies needs to be done using higher organisms and potentially clinical trials to determine the regulation of MAPK pathway. These studies is expected to reveal the translational relevance between experiment models and human.

While soybeans are widely consumed for their nutritional value and health benefits, they are known to have potential toxicity, particularly at high concentrations or long term consumption (Messina, 1999). Thus, the decreasing trend of lifespan extension effect of soybean and tempeh extract at 200 μg/ml in C. elegans may have been a result of potential toxicity of soybeans, which reflects dose dependent toxicity of soy components that are present in both soybean and tempeh. Although, fermentation may reduce toxic components, but it could generate new antinutritional toxins. Thus, additional studies on profiling the composition of tempeh are essential to maximize the health benefits it can offer without toxicity when consumed.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study showed that tempeh significantly extends lifespan of C. elegans via PMK-1-SKN-1 signaling axis in MAPK pathway to enhance oxidative stress and reduce intracellular ROS levels in C. elegans. Therefore, the findings suggest tempeh as a plant-based protein source for anti-oxidation effect to promote healthy aging.
