Early ultrasound diagnosis and management of intramural pregnancy: a case series report utilizing the FIGO leiomyoma classification system and 3D sonographic mapping
Authors: Qinghong Zhao, Wenqiong Qin, Huijiao Pan, Wen Ding, Yandan Wang, Jia Huang, Qing Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIntramural pregnancy, a rare ectopic pregnancy with the gestational sac implanted within the myometrium, poses significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. Current diagnostic criteria lack anatomical precision, leading to delayed management and high morbidity. This study proposes a novel diagnostic framework integrating the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) leiomyoma classification system and advanced 3D transvaginal ultrasound, enabling anatomical risk stratification and individualized management.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed four intramural pregnancies, assessing clinical history, laboratory results, 2D/3D sonographic findings, treatment methods and outcomes. All cases underwent comprehensive evaluation using high-resolution transvaginal 2D/3D ultrasound (GE Voluson E8 or S8 systems with 5-6.5 MHz transvaginal probes). Lesions were systematically mapped according to the FIGO leiomyoma classification system, with particular attention to their spatial relationship with the uterine cavity and surrounding myometrium. Treatment strategies were tailored based on lesion characteristics, β-hCG levels, and fertility goals.ResultsTwo distinct sonographic patterns were identified: gestational sac-type (Cases 1–2) and heterogeneous mass-type (Cases 3–4). The FIGO classification system (Types 2–5) effectively predicted the depth of myometrial invasion and guided therapeutic decisions. Type 3 (Case 1): Medical management with methotrexate (MTX) achieved resolution. Type 2–5 (Case 2): Ultrasound-guided fetal reduction preserved intrauterine pregnancy to term. Type 4 and Type 5 (Cases 3–4): Laparoscopic excision prevented uterine rupture, with lesions demonstrating significant myometrial thinning or serosal protrusion. High risk factors and key sonographic features are summarized, and diagnostic criteria are proposed.ConclusionThe integration of FIGO-based anatomical staging with advanced 3D sonographic evaluation provides a robust framework for early diagnosis and risk-stratified management of intramural pregnancy. This innovative approach significantly enhances diagnostic accuracy, facilitates appropriate treatment selection, and optimizes fertility preservation outcomes.

Introduction

Ectopic pregnancy is defined as the abnormal implantation of the fertilized ovum outside the uterine cavity [1]. Intramural pregnancy, characterized by implantation of the gestational sac within the myometrium [2], accounts for less than 1% of all ectopic pregnancies, but carries high risks of uterine rupture and hemorrhage [3]. Recent epidemiological studies highlight an increased incidence of intramural pregnancy associated with assisted reproductive technologies (ART) and prior uterine surgery [4]. Diagnostic challenges arise from nonspecific symptoms and imaging overlap with interstitial or cornual pregnancies. While transvaginal ultrasound remains the cornerstone of diagnosis, existing criteria lack standardized anatomical descriptors, leading to inconsistent management. Despite advances in imaging, delayed diagnosis remains common, contributing to maternal morbidity [5]. Existing studies primarily focus on descriptive sonographic features, yet lack a standardized system for anatomical localization and risk-adapted treatment protocols. Notably, a recently proposed classification system further refines the characterization of subserosalintramural pregnancies [6], underscoring the ongoing efforts to improve anatomical precision in diagnosing this condition. This gap impedes clinical decision-making, particularly in differentiating intramural pregnancy from interstitial or cornual gestations.

This study analyzed the clinical data of four cases of intramural pregnancy to summarize its high-risk factors and provide standardized anatomical descriptors that could guide treatment decisions. This represents a critical gap in the management of this potentially life-threatening condition. The study introduced three significant advances in intramural pregnancy management: (1) Novel Anatomical Classification: First application of the FIGO leiomyoma classification system to map the lesions of intramural pregnancy, providing a standardized framework for lesion description and risk stratification; (2) Pathological-Sonographic Correlation: Comprehensive documentation of two distinct sonographic patterns (gestational sac vs. heterogeneous mass) with corresponding histopathological findings; (3) Evidence-Based Treatment Algorithm: Development of a management protocol integrating FIGO staging, quantitative β-hCG levels, and individualized fertility preservation goals.

Methods

A retrospective case series of four patients with histologically confirmed intramural pregnancy was conducted at our hospital. We analyzed four intramural pregnancies, assessing clinical history, laboratory results, Two-dimensional(2D) or three-dimensional (3D) sonographic findings, treatment methods and outcomes.

A transvaginal probe (GE Voluson E8 and S8 color Doppler ultrasound diagnostic instrument, with a frequency of 5.0- 6.5 MHz) was used to sequentially scan the cervix, uterine cavity, uterine horns, tubal interstitial areas, myometrium, bilateral ovaries, and adnexal regions. After identifying the lesion or gestational sac, the relationship between the lesion and the uterine cavity and myometrium was dynamically observed. Subsequently, three-dimensional ultrasound was performed to clarify the relationship between the lesion or gestational sac and the uterine cavity. Two-dimensional color Doppler imaging was used to demonstrate the perilesional vascularity.

Lesions were systematically classified according to the FIGO leiomyoma classification system as follows [7]: type 1:≥50% submucosal (<50% intramural); type 2:<50% submucosal (≥ 50% intramural component); type 3: Entirely intramural, contacting endometrium; type 4: Entirely intramural, no endometrial or subserosal contact; type 5: subserosal with ≥ 50% intramural component; type 6: subserosal, <50% intramural component; type 2–5: <50% submucosal component, and <50% subserosal component.

Treatment protocols were made by a multidisciplinary team based on the lesions of FIGO classification type, myometrial thickness at thinnest point, β-hCG levels, patient’s hemodynamic status and fertility preservation desires. All patients signed a written informed consent form before treatment. Institutional Review Board approval was not required for this case series report.

Case 1

Clinical presentation

A 34-year-old woman (G4P1A3) presented with 39 days of amenorrhea. Her serum β-human chorionic gonadotropin (β-hCG) level was 1981 IU/L, and she had no symptoms with a history of 3 artificial abortions.

Imaging findings

The gestational sac was located in the anterior wall, adjacent to the uterine cavity, resembling FIGO type 3 myoma. It was confined to the myometrium, myometrial thickness of 1.72 cm (Fig. 1a-c), the size of gestational sac was 1.5 cm*1.2 cm*1.0 cm (Table 2). A sinus tract between the uterine cavity and myometrium was observable on 2D and 3D sonography, which was highly likely to be the pathway through which the fertilized egg entered the myometrium from the uterine cavity (Fig. 1a-c).

Management and outcome

Systemic methotrexate administration was successful. This treatment was selected considering the patient’s fertility requirements, the size of the mass, her serum β-hCG level, and the absence of clinical symptoms.

Case 2

Clinical presentation

A 31-year-old woman (G1P0) with infertility who underwent assisted reproductive technology (ART) presented 30 days after embryo transfer. Her serum β-hCG level was 17,834 IU/L, and she had experienced abdominal distension for 5 days. She had a history of laparoscopic hysteromyomectomy.

Imaging findings

The patient was diagnosed with heterotopic pregnancy. The yolk sac, embryo, and fetal heartbeat were visible in both the intrauterine and extrauterine gestational sacs. The size of extrauterine gestational sac was 1.3 cm*1.1 cm*0.8 cm. The outer edge of the intramuscular pregnancy sac protrudes slightly from the serosa, and the medial edge compresses the interstitial part of the right fallopian tube and the right uterine horn, similar to FIGO type 2–5 myoma (Figure d-f, Table 2).

Management and outcome

Under abdominal ultrasound guidance, selective fetal reduction surgery was performed. Fluid was aspirated from the ectopic sac, and KCl was injected. The patient was then treated with progesterone. No postoperative symptoms of threatened abortion occurred, and the intrauterine pregnancy continued to term. An elective cesarean section was performed at 38 weeks.

Case 3

Clinical presentation

A 25-year-old woman (G1P0) with infertility who underwent ART presented 30 days after embryo transfer. Her serum β-hCG level was 9513 IU/L, and she had experienced abdominal distension for 2 days and a small amount of vaginal bleeding.

Imaging findings

A mixed heterogeneous mass was observed in the left sidewall of the uterus, completely within the myometrium and not connected to the uterine cavity, similar to FIGO type 4 myoma, and the size of the lesion was 2.9 cm*2.5 cm*1.8 cm (Figure g-i, Table 2).

Management and outcome

Due to the large mass size and elevated serum β-hCG levels, laparoscopic excision was carried out. Histopathological examination confirmed the presence of villous and trophoblast cells in the smooth muscle (Fig. 2).

Discussion

By adapting the FIGO leiomyoma classification to intramural pregnancy, we established a standardized system for lesion localization, which correlates with myometrial invasion depth and guides therapeutic decisions.

While previous studies have described intramural pregnancy ultrasound features, our work represents the first systematic application of a standardized anatomical classification system. To our knowledge, this is the first study to apply FIGO leiomyoma classification to describe the intramural pregnancy localization. We further propose (a) two distinct sonographic patterns linked to pathology, and (b) a treatment algorithm integrating FIGO staging, β-hCG levels, and fertility goals—addressing critical gaps in early diagnosis and management.

The pathogenesis of intramural pregnancy involves the disruption of the endometrial-myometrial junction. The key risk factors include assisted reproductive technology (ART) (odds ratio [OR] 3.1, 95% confidence interval [CI] 2.4–4.0), previous uterine surgery, and pelvic adhesions [8–11]. Prior myomectomy increases the risk of intramural pregnancy 4-fold, likely due to myometrial discontinuity [10].

All 4 patients in this study had high risk factors. In addition to the high-risk factors of ART, this study also found two high risk factors, sinus caused by artificial process between the uterine cavity and myometrium (case 1), and adhesion at the level of the fallopian tube and uterine serosa (case 4, Fig. 3d and e) may be risk factors for the occurrence of intramural pregnancy [11].

Diagnosis of intramural pregnancy mainly relies on ultrasound, while magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can be used as an adjunct in cases with ambiguous ultrasound findings [12, 13]. The presence of villous and trophoblast cells in smooth muscle on postoperative histopathologic examination is the gold standard for the diagnosis of intramural pregnancy [14].

There are two distinct sonographic patterns in intramural pregnancy: (1) the presence of a gestational sac and (2) the appearance of a heterogeneous mass. The first type is characterized by a ring-shaped hyperechoic gestational sac located within the myometrium, which may or may not contain a yolk sac, an embryo, or a fetal heartbeat. The second type mainly shows a mixed echo area within the myometrium, consisting of a peripheral hyperechoic area and an irregular echoless area without a yolk sac or embryo. During intramural pregnancy, the placental villi implant into the myometrium. As the pregnancy progresses, the extent of villous erosion of the myometrium increases, and the blood vessels in the myometrium dilate and become congested. Color Doppler imaging reveals abundant blood flow signals around the gestational sac or mass, which is a key factor contributing to the risk of uterine rupture. The discrimination between gestational sac and heterogeneous mass patterns carries substantial implications for clinical management. The organized sac configuration typically represents an earlier, potentially viable gestation that may be appropriate for medical therapy or minimally invasive approaches when detected in a timely manner. Conversely, the heterogeneous mass pattern generally reflects a more advanced or compromised gestational state marked by extensive trophoblastic invasion and tissue necrosis. This pathological profile is associated with significantly higher hemorrhage risk and typically necessitates definitive surgical intervention. Thus, this radiographic distinction provides crucial prognostic information that directly informs risk stratification and therapeutic decision-making.

High-resolution transvaginal and 3D ultrasound can precisely localize the gestational sac and define its spatial relationship with the uterine cavity and fallopian tubes. The gestational sac or mass is located in the myometrium, independent of the uterine cavity and not connected to the fallopian tube, which are specific ultrasound features of intramural pregnancy [15]. Therefore, accurately identifying the relationship between the gestational sac or mass and the uterine cavity and fallopian tube is crucial for the diagnosis of intramural pregnancy.

The ultrasonic imaging of interstitial tubal pregnancy is characterized by asymmetrical uterine enlargement. A uterine sac or heterogeneous mass can be detected in the lateral myometrium on one side of the uterine horn, which is not connected to the uterine cavity. An interstitial line between the pregnant sac edge and the endometrium can be visualized, and the muscular layer surrounding the gestational sac or heterogeneous mass is thin or discontinuous. The interstitial line sign, which refers to the linear echo extending from the edge of the gestational sac to the endometrium, is a specific manifestation of interstitial tubal pregnancy. Interstitial features have an 80% sensitivity and 98% specificity in the diagnosis of interstitial pregnancy [16, 17], and it is also the focus of differentiating interstitial pregnancy from intramural pregnancy. With the improvement of the resolution of ultrasound instruments and the progress of ultrasound technology, interstitial features can be well visualized in 2D and 3D ultrasound.

The ultrasound image of a pregnancy sac in the uterine horn is easily confused with that of a muscle-wall pregnancy, so differential diagnosis is necessary. Uterine horn pregnancy is an eccentric intrauterine pregnancy, not an ectopic pregnancy. The gestational sac is located at the horn of the uterus and communicates with the uterine cavity. As the pregnancy progresses, the lateral myometrium of the gestational sac may become thinner.

Pregnancy trophoblastic diseases invade the myometrium, causing vasodilation and congestion of the myometrium. Their sonographic images are easily confused with the mass of intramural pregnancy, so differential diagnosis is required. Serum β-hCG levels and the patient’s medical history play an important role in differentiating diagnosis of these two diseases [18]. In addition, patients with trophoblastic disease may also present with intramural infiltration and luteinized cysts of the ovary. Due to the erosion of uterine arcuate artery and uterine artery by trophoblast cells, the blood flow velocity of uterine artery in systole and diastole increased, and the resistance index of uterine artery decreases significantly. Therefore, in the early pregnancy of trophoblastic disease, low uterine artery resistance appears, while in the middle and late trimesters of normal pregnancy, the resistance of the uterine artery drops significantly. This is also an important differentiating point between intramural pregnancy and gestational trophoblastic disease.

Intramural pregnancy is a relatively rare condition. To date, only a limited number of cases have been reported, and there is currently no standardized treatment protocol [10]. Current treatment options for intramural pregnancy include conservative management and surgical intervention. Medical therapy (methotrexate ± uterine artery embolization) or surgical procedures (laparoscopic resection or hysterectomy) may be considered [19].

Treatment was individualized based on lesion size, β-hCG levels, and fertility goals. In case 1, as the patient’s mass was small, the remaining myometrium was thick, the serum β-hCG level was not high, and the patient had no clinical symptoms, medical management with MTX was made. Therefore, Medical management with MTX is first-line whenβ-hCG < 2,000 IU/L and myometrial thickness >5 mm [5]. In case 2, it was a special type of simultaneous intrauterine and extrauterine pregnancy. The patient had strong fertility requirements. Considering that laparoscopic lesion resection increased the risk of intrauterine pregnancy abortion, and surgical scars had the risk of causing uterine rupture during late pregnancy, according to the size of gestational sac and serum β-hCG level, selective fetal reduction was performed. Finally, the patient maintained intrauterine pregnancy to full term after surgery. Laparoscopic excision was performed in Cases 3 and 4 due to large lesion size and elevated β-hCG. In this study, 4 patients were diagnosed at the early stage, and the symptoms of rupture of uterus and massive hemorrhage were avoided, which provided an opportunity for clinical treatment. Our case-based management approach is consistent with the principles outlined in a recent comprehensive review of intramural pregnancy management [20], which synthesizes the current evidence and emerging trends.

The management of intramural pregnancies remains nuanced due to the lack of universally validated β-hCG cut-off values. Current evidence supports considering systemic methotrexate administration as primary intervention when β-hCG levels remain below 2,000–5,000 IU/L, particularly when accompanied by preserved myometrial thickness (>3–5 mm) and hemodynamic stability [5]. This therapeutic approach aligned with successful outcomes in our Case 1 (β-hCG 1,981 IU/L). In contrast, surgical intervention becomes imperative with markedly elevated β-hCG levels (exemplified by Case 3 at 9,513 IU/L and Case 4 at 65,765 IU/L), expansive lesion dimensions, or clinical evidence suggesting imminent rupture—features consistently observed in our more complex presentations.

Conclusion

This pilot study indicates that the FIGO classification combined with 3D ultrasound could offer a standardized anatomical framework for characterizing intramural ectopic pregnancies. Further validation through larger, multi-center studies is warranted to establish its prognostic value and potential integration into clinical guidelines. This investigation is subject to several important constraints. The principal limitation resides in the limited case series (n = 4), which restricts statistical power and generalizability of the proposed classification system. While our observations provide preliminary clinical insights and introduce a novel diagnostic framework, the evidence level remains appropriate for a descriptive observational study. Substantiation of the FIGO-based classification’s clinical applicability will require validation through larger-scale, multi-center prospective trials with standardized outcome measures.
