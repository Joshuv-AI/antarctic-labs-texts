A case of long-term follow-up of follicular development after laparoscopic retorsion for normal ovarian torsion

Background

Ovarian torsion is a gynecological emergency caused by rotation of the ovary on its vascular pedicle, resulting in impaired venous outflow, progressive congestion, reduced arterial perfusion, and potential infarction. It is most frequently associated with ovarian enlargement secondary to tumors or cysts [1], yet up to one-quarter of cases occur in morphologically normal ovaries [2]. This phenomenon is particularly frequent among pediatric and adolescent patients, with reports suggesting that 22–55% of torsion cases in those younger than 15 years involve normal ovaries [3, 4].

Diagnosis is often challenging due to the nonspecific nature of presenting symptoms, which typically include acute abdominal pain, nausea, and vomiting. Imaging modalities such as ultrasound, CT, and MRI are crucial in supporting diagnosis, with signs including a whirl sign and peripheral distribution of follicles [5].

Surgical intervention is the standard of care. Historically, adnexectomy was frequently performed when the ovary appeared ischemic. However, evidence increasingly supports conservative management, as recovery of ovarian function is possible even after prolonged ischemia. We present a rare case of normal ovarian torsion in an 11-year-old girl, followed for more than two years, in which follicular development reappeared after an initial period of apparent necrosis and atrophy.

Case presentation

An 11-year-old premenarchal girl with no significant past medical or surgical history presented with acute abdominal pain. The pain began suddenly on awakening the day before admission and persisted throughout the day. She initially consulted a local physician, who diagnosed constipation and prescribed laxatives. Despite this, her pain worsened and was accompanied by repeated vomiting. Later that night, she developed a low-grade fever of 37.8 °C. The following morning, she was evaluated at a pediatric clinic. At the clinic, contrast-enhanced CT of the abdomen revealed a substantial left lower quadrant mass. The absence of contrast uptake suggested compromised vascular supply, raising strong suspicion for ovarian torsion. She was urgently referred and admitted to our hospital. On admission, her vital signs were stable: height 142 cm, weight 29.2 kg, body mass index 14.5, heart rate 105 bpm, blood pressure 102/60 mmHg, respiratory rate 20/min, and SpO₂ 97%. The abdomen was flat and soft, with bilateral lower abdominal tenderness but no rebound pain or guarding. Laboratory evaluation revealed leukocytosis (WBC 12,590/µL), while inflammatory markers were unremarkable (CRP 0.01 mg/dL, PCT 0.025 ng/mL). Tumor markers were normal: CEA 1.7 ng/mL, CA19-9 31 U/mL, AFP 0.9 nM/mL, and CA125 9.5 U/mL. Pelvic MRI demonstrated a 70 × 47 mm lesion in the left ovary, with high signal on fat-suppressed T1-weighted imaging, low signal on T2-weighted imaging, and multiple peripheral follicles. The fallopian tube exhibited a whirl sign (Fig. 1), and ADC values were markedly reduced, suggesting hemorrhagic infarction. The right ovary was normal. Based on these findings, a diagnosis of torsion of a normal left ovary was made.

MRI T2-weighted image revealed the whirl sign of the fallopian tube, indicative of a twisted ovarian pedicle due to ovarian torsion

Emergency laparoscopy was performed under general anesthesia approximately 35 h after the onset of symptoms. Intraoperatively, the left ovary was found twisted 720° counterclockwise and displaced into the Douglas pouch. The ovary appeared dark purple, enlarged, and congested, with a small amount of bloody ascites noted (Fig. 2).

Intraoperative findings before detorsion revealed the left ovary exhibiting dark purple discoloration due to ovarian stem torsion

Following detorsion, the ovary was observed for 15 min. The fallopian tube demonstrated partial recovery with slight improvement in color. The ovary remained dusky and ischemic in appearance, raising concern for necrosis (Fig. 3).

Intraoperative findings 15 min after detorsion showed no improvement in the color tone and remained discolored in the ovary, in contrast to a slight improvement in the color tone of the fallopian tube

During the operation, the patient’s parents were carefully counseled. They were informed that ovarian function may recover even when the ovary initially appears nonviable, and that the probability of serious complications from preservation is exceedingly low. The possibility of reoperation due to bleeding, infection, or necrosis was explained. Because the adnexal tissue was fragile, fixation sutures could not be placed safely to prevent recurrence, and the parents were advised that recurrent torsion could occur. After thorough discussion, they consented to conservative management with detorsion alone, preserving the ovary. Postoperatively, the patient recovered uneventfully. She experienced neither fever nor abdominal pain, tolerated oral intake promptly, and was mobilized early. She was discharged on postoperative day 4.

At 1 month, pelvic MRI was performed to exclude tumorous lesions and evaluate the ovary. The left ovary remained enlarged but contained fewer visible follicles compared with the contralateral ovary, suggesting reduced reserve (Fig. 4).

MRI T2-weighted image obtained 1 month postoperatively revealed an enlargement of the left ovary, accompanied by a reduction in the number of follicles

At 6 months, the left ovary could not be visualized by transabdominal ultrasound. Transvaginal imaging was not feasible because of her premenarchal status, and she declined transrectal ultrasound. MRI revealed marked atrophy of the left ovary with complete absence of follicles, findings that strongly suggested necrosis (Fig. 5).

MRI T2-weighted image obtained 6 months postoperatively revealed atrophy of the left ovary, characterized by the absence of follicular structures, suggesting the potential occurrence of necrosis

At 1 year, as the ovary remained undetectable on ultrasound, MRI was again performed. Surprisingly, the study demonstrated reappearance of follicles in the left ovary, with recovery of ovarian volume to 4.6 cm³. Although this measurement was within the normal pubertal range (2–6 cm³), only 3 follicles were identified, compared with 17 follicles in the healthy contralateral ovary (Fig. 6).

MRI T2-weighted image obtained 1 year postoperatively revealed follicular development in the left ovary, with an increase in volume to 4.6 cm3, suggesting a return to its normal size. However, only 3 follicles were observed, which is below the average number of 12 follicles typically present at puberty

At 2 years, repeat MRI showed further improvement, with 8 follicles observed in the left ovary. Despite progressive follicular recovery, now 13 years old, remained premenarchal. No evidence of recurrence was detected during the two-year follow-up (Fig. 7).

MRI T2-weighted image obtained 2 years postoperatively revealed increased follicular development in the left ovary, with the number of follicles rising to 8

This case was remarkable for an ovary that initially appeared necrotic at surgery, showed atrophy and absent follicles at six months, yet demonstrated delayed follicular regeneration beginning one year postoperatively. Serial MRI not only documented changes in ovarian volume but also quantified follicle numbers, illustrating the gradual trajectory of recovery. These findings provided objective evidence that functional restoration of the ovary was possible even after a period of apparent ischemic damage.

Discussion

Ovarian torsion most commonly arises in the context of ovarian enlargement due to tumors or cysts, yet up to 25% of cases involve morphologically normal ovaries, particularly among pediatric patients. The nonspecific nature of symptoms—acute lower abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting—frequently leads to delayed diagnosis. In our patient, initial misdiagnosis as constipation prolonged the interval to surgery by 35 h, yet eventual recovery occurred, underscoring the resilience of ovarian tissue.

Ultrasound with Doppler flow is the gold standard in adults, but premenarchal children often cannot undergo transvaginal imaging. In such cases, CT and MRI are essential. The whirl sign of the fallopian tube and peripheral follicle distribution, combined with reduced ADC, strongly supported the diagnosis in our patient. Tumor markers and imaging showed no evidence of malignancy, justifying ovarian conservation.

At surgery, the ovary was grossly ischemic and showed no improvement after detorsion. Historically, such findings prompted adnexectomy. However, gross appearance is not a reliable predictor of viability: studies show that histological necrosis is present in only 20% of grossly blackened ovaries [6, 7]. Given the patient’s age and fertility considerations, detorsion without resection was chosen after parental counseling.

The most notable feature of this case was the delayed recovery. At 6 months, imaging suggested irreversible necrosis: the ovary was atrophic and devoid of follicles. Yet by 1 year, MRI revealed recovery of ovarian volume to 4.6 cm³, within the normal pubertal range (2–6 cm³) [8]. Although only 3 follicles were present compared with 17 in the contralateral ovary, this confirmed functional restoration. By 2 years, follicle number increased to 8, though still below the average of 12 typically seen during puberty [9]. These findings emphasize that absence of early follicular activity does not necessarily equate to loss of ovarian function. Recovery may occur beyond the conventional 3-to-6-month window, justifying extended follow-up.

Most prior studies report early recovery within 10 weeks. In a large series, 92% of ischemic-appearing ovaries demonstrated follicular development within three months [10]. However, these observations rarely extend beyond six months. Reports specifically describing long-term outcomes of atrophic ovaries are limited.

Gabriel et al. followed 37 women for an average of 4 years, documenting ovarian atrophy in only 1 case [11]. Geimanaite et al. reported a 2.6-year-old girl whose ovary became undetectable 1 month postoperatively [12]. Ahmet et al. described a 12-year-old patient with postoperative atrophy and no follicular recovery during 14 weeks of follow-up [13]. None of these cases reported delayed recovery beyond one year. Our case is therefore unique in demonstrating reappearance of follicles after initial atrophy, with quantifiable increases in ovarian volume and follicle number over 2 years.

Serial MRI allowed objective tracking of both ovarian volume and follicle count. At 1 year, ovarian volume had normalized to 4.6 cm³, despite a low follicle count of three. By two years, follicle number increased to eight. Although this remained below the contralateral ovary (17 follicles) and below the pubertal average (12 follicles), the trajectory suggested progressive recovery. This dual assessment provided clear evidence that conservative management was justified, highlighting the importance of quantitative imaging in evaluating ovarian function.

Three major clinical lessons emerge from this case: Ovarian conservation should be the first priority in pediatric torsion unless malignancy is suspected [14]. Follow-up should extend beyond 6 months, as late recovery may occur. Quantitative imaging using ovarian volume and follicle counts provides objective evidence of recovery, supporting patient counseling and clinical decision-making.

Our patient has not yet reached menarche, so endocrine and reproductive function remain unconfirmed. Further follow-up is essential. In addition, although no recurrence has been observed over two years, recurrence risk remains high in pediatric torsion, particularly in the absence of fixation [15]. Future studies should assess the role of prophylactic fixation and the correlation between imaging findings, hormonal markers, and fertility outcomes.

Conclusion

This case illustrates that even when intraoperative appearance and early imaging suggest necrosis, ovarian recovery is possible. At 1 year, ovarian volume normalized and follicular activity resumed; by 2 years, follicle count increased further. These findings underscore the importance of ovarian preservation, prolonged follow-up, and quantitative imaging in pediatric ovarian torsion. Conservative detorsion remains the optimal strategy to preserve reproductive potential.
