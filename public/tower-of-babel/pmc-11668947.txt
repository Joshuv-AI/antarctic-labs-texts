Pancreatic agenesis and altered m6A methylation in the pancreas of PDX1-mutant cynomolgus macaques
Authors: Wen-Hui Zhang, Jiong-Han Zhuang, Yun-Yi Guo, Xue-Ying Chen, Ya-Qing Li, Jie-Qiu Xu, An-Ran Zhang, Bao-Yi Chen, Wei Meng, Yan-Hua Zhu, Jun-Jiu Huang, Yong-Long Guo, Shi-Hua Yang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs an essential transcriptional activator, PDX1 plays a crucial role in pancreatic development and β-cell function. Mutations in the PDX1 gene may lead to type 4 maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY4) and neonatal diabetes mellitus. However, the precise mechanisms underlying MODY4 remain elusive due to the paucity of clinical samples and pronounced differences in pancreatic architecture and genomic composition between humans and existing animal models. In this study, three PDX1-mutant cynomolgus macaques were generated using CRISPR/Cas9 technology, all of which succumbed shortly postpartum, exhibiting pancreatic agenesis. Notably, one tri-allelic PDX1-mutant cynomolgus macaque (designated as M4) developed a pancreas, whereas the two mono-allelic PDX1-mutant cynomolgus macaques displayed no anatomical evidence of pancreatic formation. RNA sequencing of the M4 pancreas revealed substantial molecular changes in both endocrine and exocrine functions, indicating developmental delay and PDX1 haploinsufficiency. A marked change in m6A methylation was identified in the M4 pancreas, confirmed through cultured PDX1-mutant islet organoids. Notably, overexpression of the m6A modulator METTL3 restored function in heterozygous PDX1-mutant islet organoids. This study highlights a novel role of m6A methylation modification in the progression of MODY4 and provides valuable molecular insights for preclinical research. Keywords: PDX1, MODY4, Cynomolgus macaques, M6A methylation modification

INTRODUCTION

Monogenic diabetes, arising from mutations in a single gene, has garnered increasing scientific interest in recent years. Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) represents a comparatively rare form of diabetes, accounting for approximately 1% to 2% of global diabetes cases. MODY is caused by mutations in specific genes. Of note, pancreatic duodenal homeobox 1 ( PDX1) functions as a transcriptional activator for several pancreatic genes and plays a significant role in pancreatic development and β-cell function ( Jennings et al., 2020; Zhu et al., 2021). In humans, heterozygous mutations in PDX1 are frequently associated with type 4 MODY (MODY4) ( Abreu et al., 2021; Caetano et al., 2018; Deng et al., 2019; Gragnoli et al., 2005), while homozygous mutations in PDX1 often lead to neonatal diabetes mellitus (NDM) ( Kulkarni et al., 2017). Despite considerable advancements in diagnostic methodologies, many patients continue to be misdiagnosed with type 2 diabetes (T2D) due to the broad spectrum of disease phenotypes. MODY4 and NDM typically manifest as early as 2 years of age. In China, MODY4 accounts for 0.59% of early-onset type 2 diabetes (EOD) cases ( Lian et al., 2023). Consequently, there is a pressing need for a deeper understanding of the molecular changes involved in the pathogenesis of these conditions.

Animal models are critical for elucidating the pathological symptoms and underlying mechanisms of MODY4 and NDM. To date, PDX1-mutant models have been established in zebrafish, mice, sheep, and pigs ( Holland et al., 2006; Jensen et al., 2017; Potter et al., 2012; Tanihara et al., 2019, 2020; Vilarino et al., 2017, 2018). Although these animal models have successfully replicated pancreatic agenesis, they also exhibit significant limitations. Firstly, their pancreatic structure differs considerably from that of humans. Notably, disparities in endocrine cell composition and islet location between humans and animal models ( Steiner et al., 2010; Wieczorek et al., 1998) may result in different cellular communication within the pancreas. Research has shown that effective communication between endocrine and exocrine cells is essential for pancreatic growth, functionality, and disease pathogenesis ( Kahraman et al., 2022; Overton & Mastracci, 2022). Secondly, current animal models cannot fully elucidate the mechanisms of PDX1 haploinsufficiency associated with PDX1 heterozygous mutations. The genomic differences between these models and humans may obscure crucial molecular insights, hindering understanding of disease pathogenesis. The pronounced evolutionary variations in pancreatic structures and genomic composition emphasize the need for alternative model animals to study PDX1-mutant diabetes, such as the successful generation of type-1 diabetes mellitus (T1D) in non-human primates through streptozotocin (STZ) treatment ( Jin et al., 2010; Liu et al., 2015; Wu et al., 2009; Zhang et al., 2015). To date, however, no non-human primate model of MODY has been developed to mimic monogenic diabetes and facilitate the advancement of precision therapy for this condition.

Recent advancements in sequencing technologies and a deeper understanding of developmental biology have uncovered novel regulatory mechanisms, including m6A mRNA methylation ( Lin & Zheng, 2023; Ren et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2023). This epigenetic modulation has been implicated in a variety of biological processes in eukaryotes and is increasingly recognized for its role in diabetes mellitus onset and progression ( Benak et al., 2023; Cai et al., 2023; Ren et al., 2023) and pancreatic islet development ( Ma et al., 2022a). In T2D, m6A modulators such as METTL3 and METTL14 have been shown to affect β-cell differentiation by regulating PDX1 expression ( De Jesus et al., 2019). Similarly, in T1D, alterations in the expression of m6A modulators, including METTL3, IGF2BP2, and YTHDC1, suggest the involvement of m6A in the pathogenesis of the disease ( Wang et al., 2022). Given the importance of PDX1 in pancreatic islet development ( Shih et al., 2013; Zhu et al., 2016), it is reasonable to hypothesize that m6A mRNA methylation may contribute to the development of PDX1 mutation-induced diabetes.

In this study, we first generated PDX1-mutant cynomolgus macaques using CRISPR/Cas9 technology, which exhibited pathological features analogous to those observed in MODY4 patients. Through comprehensive molecular analyses, including RNA sequencing, we identified significant changes in m6A methylation within the pancreas of these macaques. We also established PDX1-mutant islet organoids, differentiated from human-induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs), to investigate the potential of m6A methylation in restoring β-cell function.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

All cynomolgus monkeys were provided and raised by Guangdong Blue Island Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (GBIB, China), an accredited member of the Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care. The study involved 22 healthy and sexually mature female monkeys (age 3–11 years, body weight 3.5–5 kg) and one male monkey with similar characteristics (age 7 years, body weight 9.5 kg). Among them, six female monkeys and one male monkey were selected as donors for oocytes and sperm collection. The remaining 15 female monkeys were selected as surrogates for embryo transfer. All animal procedures, including semen collection, ovarian stimulation, oocyte recovery, abdominal ultrasonography, embryo transfer, and cesarean section, were carried out in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of GBIB (No. LD2017-003).

Plasmid construction

For PDX1 deletion in hiPSCs, paired single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs) were designed using BENCHLING. PX459 (Addgene, #62988, USA) was digested by BbsbI. A pair of oligos (Supplementary Table S1) for PDX1 target sites were phosphorylated, annealed, and ligated to the linearized PX459. Both pB-CMV-METTL3 (human)-EF1α-GFP-Puro (P19465) and Super PiggyBac Transposase (P0179) were obtained from MiaoLingBio (China).

Production of Cas9 mRNA and sgRNA

The PX459 plasmids (Addgene, #48139, USA) were served as templates for polymerase chain reaction (PCR). The T7 promoter was added to the Cas9 and sgRNA template by PCR (Supplementary Table S2). The T7-Cas9 PCR product was gel-purified and used as the template for in vitro transcription using a mMESSAGE mMACHINE T7 ULTRA Kit (Life Technologies, Cat. No. AM1345, USA). The T7-sgRNA PCR product was gel-purified and used as the template for in vitro transcription using a MEGAshortscript T7 Transcription Kit (Life Technologies, Cat. No. AM1354, USA). The mRNA and sgRNAs were purified using a MEGAclear Kit (Life Technologies, Cat, No. AM1908, USA).

Microinjection

Microinjection was performed following previous protocols ( Ma et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2020). In brief, cynomolgus monkey zygotes were prepared by collecting semen from a male monkey by penile probe electro-ejaculation. Six female monkeys in menses were then intramuscularly administered 1.4 μg of recombinant human follitropin alfa (rhFSH; GONAL-F, Merck Serono, Germany) twice per day for eight days, followed by 80 μg of recombinant human chorionic gonadotropin alfa (rhCG; OVIDREL, Merck Serono, Germany) on day 9. Cumulus-oocyte complexes were collected by laparoscopic follicular aspiration 33–36 h after rhCG administration, and oocytes were stripped of cumulus cells by pipetting after brief exposure (<1 min) to hyaluronidase (0.5 mg/mL) at 37℃. Metaphase II (MII, first polar body present) oocytes were cultured in CMRL-1066 medium containing 0.1% Na-lactate and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; HyClone Laboratories, SH30088.02, USA) and fertilized by intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI). At 10–12 h after ICSI, a Cas9 mRNA (100 ng/μL) and sgRNA (50 ng/μL) mixture was injected into the cytoplasm of zygotes using the Eppendorf microinjection system and microinjector (FemtoJet 4i, Eppendorf, Germany). The zygotes were then cultured under mineral oil in hamster embryo culture medium-9 containing 10% FBS at 37℃ in 5% CO 2, 5% O 2, and 90% N 2. The zygotes were cultured for more than 3 days, then collected for genotyping or embryo transfer. For the embryo transfer process, 15 surrogate mothers were chosen based on specific criteria: they were required to have undergone two consecutive normal menstrual cycles (26–32 days), possess a freshly ovulated follicle, and demonstrate normal echogenicity in the uterine cavity immediately prior to transfer. Four to five embryos at the eight-cell to blastocyst stage were transferred into one oviduct of each recipient via laparoscopy with a fixed polyethylene catheter. Pregnancy in the surrogate mothers was confirmed by visualizing a conceptus with a detectable beating heart at 25 days post-transfer.

Cell culture and differentiation

The hiPSCs were seeded into 6-well tissue culture plates and cultured for 4 days with Essential 8 TM Base Medium (Gibco, USA) before differentiation. The medium was changed daily. The cells were then washed using Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS) (Life Technologies, USA), followed by single-cell dissociation using EDTA (Gibco, USA). The hiPSC spheroids were generated as described in previous research ( Guo et al., 2020). Briefly, rubber micro-molds were sterilized with absolute ethanol and allowed to dry in a sterile environment. A 2% agarose solution was prepared using Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) and heated in a microwave until the agarose dissolved completely. Subsequently, 150 µL of this solution was pipetted into each mold. After the agarose had set, the micro-molds were carefully flexed to extract the agarose dish, which was transferred into a 6-well tissue culture plate. Approximately 243 000 cells (3 000 cells per micro-well) were seeded into the agarose dish. The tissue culture plate was placed in an incubator, and the medium was exchanged as needed. The differentiation medium was prepared following previous study ( Ma et al., 2022b), with the following protocol: day 1: RPMI (Gibco), including 1:5 000 ITS-X (Gibco, USA), 0.2% FBS (Gibco, USA), activin A (100 ng/mL; PeproTech，USA), and 3 μmol/L CHIR99021 (TargetMol，USA); day 2: RPMI containing 0.2% FBS, 1:2 000 ITS-X, and activin A (100 ng/mL); day 3: RPMI containing 0.2% FBS, 1:1 000 ITS-X, KGF (25 ng/mL; PeproTech, USA), and 2.5 μmol/L A83-01 (TargetMol, USA); days 4 to 5: RPMI containing 0.4% FBS, 1:1 000 ITS-X, and KGF (25 ng/mL); days 6 to 7: DMEM (Gibco, USA) containing 1×B27 (Gibco, USA) and 3 nmol/L TTNPB (Sigma-Aldrich, USA); day 8: DMEM containing 1×B27, 3 nmol/L TTNPB, and epidermal growth factor (EGF; 50 ng/mL; PeproTech, USA); days 9 to 11: DMEM containing 1×B27, KGF (50 ng/mL), and EGF (50 ng/mL); days 12 to 16 (EFI6 medium): DMEM containing 1×B27, EGF (50 ng/mL), bFGF (10 ng/mL; PeproTech, USA), 10 μmol/L 616452 (TargetMol, USA), and 1 μmol/L I-BET151 (APExBIO, USA); days 17 to 23 (R6 medium): DMEM containing 1×B27, 0.05% bovine serum albumin (BSA; BioFroxx, USA), 10 μmol/L zinc sulfate (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), heparin (10 g/mL; Sigma-Aldrich, USA), 10 μmol/L 616452 (TargetMol, USA), 1 μmol/L T3 (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), 0.2 μmol/L LDN-193189 (Tocris, UK), 0.2 μmol/L compound E (MedChem Express, USA), 0.5 mmol/L vitamin C (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), and 10 μmol/L forskolin (TargetMol, USA); days 24 to 30 (R7 medium): DMEM containing 1×B27, 0.05% BSA, 10 μmol/L zinc sulfate, heparin (10 g/mL), 10 μmol/L 616452, 1 μmol/L T3, 1 mmol/L N-acetyl cysteine (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), 1 μmol/L Trolox (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), and 0.5 mmol/L vitamin C.

Cell transfection and screening

The hiPSCs were transfected following previously published methods ( Yamada et al., 2021), with some modifications. Typically, the cells regained optimal conditions after 2–3 passages post-thawing. The cells were dissociated into a single-cell suspension using ETDA. Cell count was determined using a cell counter (RWD, C100-SE) and 60 μL of cell-containing medium (1×10 6 cells) was used for transfection. A total of 10 μg of plasmid (5 μg for each plasmid) was prepared in 40 μL of Opti-MEM for each transfection. The cell and plasmid mixture were transfected using the NEPA21 system. The transfected cells were cultured for 24 h and screened using 1 μg/mL puromycin for 3 days. A single hiPSC clone was selected for genotyping and fluorescence detection.

Electron microscopy

Differentiated clusters were spun down and excess medium was removed, followed by the addition of ice-cold fixative (2.5% glutaraldehyde) and incubation on ice for 20 min. The clusters were further processed and analyzed using transmission electron microscopy (TECNAI G 20 TWIN, FEI, USA).

Glucose-stimulated insulin secretion (GSIS)

Before the glucose stimulation assay, the clusters were starved in 1 mL of KRBH buffer (128 mmol/L NaCl, 5 mmol/L KCl, 2.7 mmol/L CaCl 2, 1.2 mmol/L MgCl 2, 1 mmol/L sodium phosphate buffer, 1.2 mmol/L KH 2PO 4, 5 mmol/L NaHCO 3, 10 mmol/L HEPES, and 0.1% BSA) supplemented with 2 mmol/L glucose for 2 h in a 37℃, 5% CO 2 incubator. For glucose stimulation, the clusters were alternately stimulated by 300 μL of KRBH with low (2 mmol/L) or high (16.8 mmol/L) glucose. The resulting supernatants were collected after 1 h of each stimulation for insulin measurement using a Human Insulin Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) Kit.

Genotyping

Genomic DNA was amplified from embryos using a REPLI-g Single Cell Kit (QIAGEN, Cat. No. 150345, Germany) and PCR amplification was performed using PrimeSTAR GXL DNA Polymerase (Takara, Cat. No. R050A, Japan). The list of genotyping primers is shown in Supplementary Table S3. The PCR amplicons were gel purified and sequenced. Sanger sequencing results were analyzed by SnapGene (USA). To analyze mutation frequencies, the PCR products were subcloned using a Mighty TA-cloning Reagent Set for PrimeSTAR (Takara, Cat. No. 6019 SnapGene, USA) and transformed into E. coli. The plasmids were purified and analyzed by Sanger sequencing. For fetal genotyping, genomic DNA was extracted from mutant and wild-type (WT) monkey fetuses using a TIANamp Genomic DNA Kit (Tiangen, DP304, China). The PCR products were subjected to clone sequencing as above.

Whole-genome sequencing analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted from the pancreas of the M4 fetus and the blood of its father and mother using a TIANamp Genomic DNA Kit (Tiangen, DP304, China). The genomic DNA (200 ng) was subjected to whole-genome sequencing at a sequencing depth of 30–40 × using the Illumina HiSeq 2000 platform at the Novogene Bioinformatics Institute (China). Potential off-target sites for each sgRNA were predicted to analyze site-specific edits according to Cas-OFFinder (CRISPR RGEN Tools (rgenome.net)). Initially, all potential off-target sites in the mutant monkeys were compared with the reference genome. The filtered potential off-target sites were then compared to the parent genomes. After filtering, the final potential off-target sites were obtained and confirmed by Sanger sequencing. All primers are listed in Supplementary Table S3.

Reverse transcription quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis

Total RNA was isolated from the pancreas of the M5 and M4 monkeys using Trizol reagent (TaKaRa, Cat. NO. 9108, Japan) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The total RNA (500 ng) was utilized to synthesize cDNA using a PrimeScript TM RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (TaKaRa, Cat. No. RR047A, Japan). The transcript level of different genes was evaluated by RT-qPCR using the Applied Biosystems 7300 Plus Real-Time PCR System (USA) with Hieff-qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Yeasen, Cat. No. 11203ES03, China). The primers used for RT-qPCR are listed in Supplementary Table S4.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

The pancreatic tissues obtained from WT and M4 monkeys were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at 4°C for 2 days. The tissues were then dehydrated in increasing concentrations of ethanol (70% for 6 h, 80% for 1 h, 96% for 1 h, and 100% for 3 h), cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin for histological examination. Sections were cut (5 μm thick) for H&E staining and imaged using an inversion microscope.

Immunofluorescence staining

Immunofluorescence was performed on paraffin-embedded pancreatic sections. Pancreatic tissue was fixed overnight at 4℃ in 4% PFA (1:10 vol/vol tissue-to-PFA ratio), transferred to 70% ethanol for long-term storage, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned (5 μm thick). The sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated using a series of 5 min washes in xylene (×3) and graded alcohol solutions (100% ethanol×2, 95% ethanol×1, and 70% ethanol×1), followed by washing in PBS for 10 min on a shaker. Heat-induced epitope retrieval (HIER) was performed on slides for 10 min at 95°C in 10 mmol/L sodium citrate buffer (0.5% Tween 20, pH 6.0). Following HIER, the slides were placed under running tap water for 5 min, then washed for 5 min in de-ionized water on a shaker, washed using PBS, and blocked with blocking solution at 37°C for 1 h. The sections were then incubated at 4°C overnight with primary antibodies (PDX1: 1:200, R&D SYSTEMS, USA; C-peptide: 1:200, Abcam, UK; Insulin: 1:200 Abcam, UK; Glucagon: 1:50, Santa Cruz, USA; Somatostatin: 1:50, Santa Cruz, USA; METTL3: 1:100, Abcam, UK), then washed three times with PBS and incubated with secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor 488 donkey anti-mouse IgG (H+L) (1:1 000), Invitrogen, USA; Alexa Fluor 555 donkey anti-rabbit IgG (H+L) (1:1 000), Invitrogen, USA; Alexa Fluor 488 donkey anti-goat IgG (H+L) (1:1 000), Invitrogen, USA) for 1 h at room temperature. Finally, the sections were counterstained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) nuclear stain. Cell immunofluorescence staining was similar to tissue.

Western blotting

Pancreatic islet-like organoids were lysed with RIPA protein lysate containing 1% phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) for 30 min, with the lytic organoids then centrifuged at 4°C and 12 000 × g for 30 min. Protein concentration was determined using a BCA kit, and the concentrations were adjusted uniformly. The samples were then boiled for 10 min with 5×sodium dodecyl-sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) loading buffer, then transferred to a polyvinylidene ﬂuoride (PVDF) membrane. The membrane was then blocked with blocking solution at room temperature for 30 min, before being incubated overnight at 4°C with the primary antibody against METTL3 (1:1500, Abcam, UK), followed by incubation with the corresponding secondary antibody (horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-goat anti rabbit: 1:5 000, Abbkine, China) at room temperature for 30 min. Protein bands were then visualized using a protein imager (Tanon, China), and the grayscale value was analyzed using Image J.

RNA sequencing

Pancreatic samples were collected and used for RNA extraction (Trizol, Takara, Cat, NO.9108, Japan). RNA sequencing libraries were prepared by the Novogene Bioinformatics Institute (China) and sequenced using the Illumina Hiseq2500 platform (USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Different gene expression data are shown in Supplementary Table S5.

Statistical analysis

All data are expressed as mean±standard error of the mean (SEM), with at least three individual determinations for all experiments. The data were analyzed with t-tests using GraphPad Prism v.8.0. A probability of P<0.05 was considered statistically significant. ns: Not significant; *: P<0.05; **: P<0.01; ***: P<0.001.

RESULTS

PDX1-mutant cynomolgus macaques exhibited pancreatic agenesis

PDX1-mutant cynomolgus macaques were generated using the CRISPR/Cas9 system. In pancreatic β cells, the transcriptional activation domain (TAD) of PDX1 binds with the EIA-binding protein P300 (P300) and stimulates target gene expression (Supplementary Figure S1A) ( Stanojevic et al., 2004). Moreover, TAD mutation impairs β-cell development and function in humans ( Wang et al., 2019). The amino acid sequence of TAD is highly conserved between humans and cynomolgus monkeys (Supplementary Figure S1B). Therefore, we designed two sgRNAs to induce a large deletion in the TAD region of cynomolgus monkeys ( Figure 1A) and injected Cas9 or espCas9 mRNA and sgRNA mixture into the cytoplasm of cynomolgus monkey zygotes. The PDX1-mutant sequences from the edited embryos showed good editing efficiency (Supplementary Figure S2A). The transfer of 55 embryos into 15 surrogate mothers resulted in five pregnancies, yielding six fetuses (M1–M6), five of which died in early pregnancy or after cesarean delivery ( Figure 1B). Sanger sequencing was performed to identify the genotypes of the six fetuses. Results indicated that M1 and M2 were PDX1 mono-allelic mutants, M3 and M4 were PDX1 tri-allelic mutants, and M5 and M6 were WT monkeys (Supplementary Figure S2B). The body weights of M1, M2, and M4 were low compared to the WT monkeys (M5, dead after delivery on day 165, and M6, a newborn monkey) ( Figure 1B). Autopsies revealed that M1 and M2 contained no pancreas ( Figure 1C), while the pancreas in M3 was undetectable due to the retrieval of a non-intact body following a miscarriage. Morphological abnormalities in the pancreas, kidneys, and liver were observed in the M4 monkey ( Figure 1D). To detect pathological changes in the pancreas of the PDX1-mutant cynomolgus macaques, histopathological analysis using H&E staining was performed. Results revealed an absence of pancreatic islets in the M4 pancreas, as well as focal acinar cell degeneration, necrosis, and inflammatory cell infiltration ( Figure 1E). Consistently, based on immunofluorescence staining, pancreatic endocrine and exocrine marker gene expression levels were barely detected in M4, including PDX1, INSULIN, GLUCAGON ( Figure 1F), and CEL (Supplementary Figure S2C). To understand the varied phenotypes observed in M1, M2 (lacking a pancreas), and M4 (with a pancreas), analysis of the M4 genotype and protein sequences ( Figure 1G; Supplementary Figure S2D) was conducted. PDX1 was deleted in different tissues in the M4 monkey (Supplementary Figure S3A), while protein sequence analysis showed a 6-bp deletion in one of the mutant alleles that disrupted two amino acids in the pancreas ( Figure 1G), suggesting the potential presence of functional PDX1 in some pancreatic cells. The lack of a pancreas in M1 and M2 may be due to large fragment DNA deletion.

RNA sequencing revealed impaired pancreatic cell differentiation in M4 monkey

To investigate the molecular pathogenesis and identify potential therapeutic targets in PDX1-mutant diabetes mellitus, RNA sequencing was conducted to compare gene expression in the pancreas of M4 and M5 monkeys. Results showed that 2 263 genes were up-regulated and 5 593 genes were down-regulated in M4 relative to the WT monkey ( Figure 2A). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed differentially expressed gene (DEG) enrichment in cellular processes, such as intracellular organelle and cellular metabolism (Supplementary Figure S4A). Moreover, gap junctions in M4 appeared to be compromised, as evidenced by the relatively lower expression of extracellular matrix (ECM) genes compared to the WT monkey (Supplementary Figure S4B). Subsequent Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis indicated that the DEGs were most significantly enriched in pathways related to metabolic processes and pancreatic secretion ( Figure 2B). To clarify pancreatic development in M4, we analyzed endocrine- and exocrine-related genes. We analyzed four endocrine cell types, including Alpha, Beta, Delta, and pancreatic polypeptide (PP) cells in the pancreas. Results indicated that these cells were not well-differentiated ( Figure 2C). Focusing on β cells, essential for pancreatic islet function, we analyzed marker genes at various stages of β-cell differentiation, including definitive endoderm (DE), pancreatic progenitors (PP), endocrine progenitors (ED), and β cells. Results indicated early cessation of β-cell differentiation in the pancreatic development of M4 ( Figure 2D). We also analyzed two exocrine cell types, including ductal and acinar cells. Results showed that neither cell type developed well, as confirmed by marker gene expression ( Figure 2E, F). As important genes for β-cell differentiation, PDX1, MAFA, and NEUROG3 are specifically expressed in a dynamic temporal pattern during embryonic development in mice and humans ( Zhu et al., 2017). However, PDX1, MAFA, and NEUROG3 showed almost no expression in the pancreas of M4 ( Figure 2G). Thus, we hypothesize that the M4 pancreas was at an early stage of formation, such as pancreatic buds ( Figure 2H).

PDX1 haploinsufficiency in M4 pancreas and PDX1-mutant islet organoids

Pancreatic agenesis in M4 was marked by islet loss and exocrine dysfunction ( Figure 1E, F). Examination of endocrine-related genes in M4 revealed a near absence of their expression ( Figure 3A; Supplementary Figure S5A, B), similar to studies on hiPSC-induced differentiation of PDX1 heterozygous β cells with PDX1 haploinsufficiency ( Zhu et al., 2016). These findings suggest that PDX1 haploinsufficiency may also manifest in PDX1 heterozygous mutant cynomolgus monkeys. To further investigate PDX1 haploinsufficiency in M4, we constructed an islet-like organoid model using established methods ( Ma et al., 2022b) (Supplementary Figure S5C). Differentiation of pancreatic progenitor cells and β cells was confirmed by time-dependent expression levels of marker genes ( Figure 3B; Supplementary Figure S5D). Subsequently, PDX1-mutant heterozygous ( PDX1 +/- ) and homozygous-mutant ( PDX1 -/-) hiPSCs were constructed using CRISPR/Cas9 (Supplementary Figure S5E, F), resulting in the absence of PDX1 protein (Supplementary Figure S5G). Notably, both the PDX1 +/- and PDX1 -/- organoids demonstrated obvious morphological abnormalities ( Figure 3C), reduced expression of related marker genes ( Figure 3D), and reduced expression of ECM-related genes in β cells (Supplementary Figure S5H). Despite a significant reduction in PDX1 expression in both PDX1 +/- and PDX1 -/- mutants, NKX6.1 was stably expressed in both mutant organoids ( Figure 3D). The mRNA expression level of PDX1 in the PDX1 +/- group was less than 50% ( Figure 3D), as evidenced by immunofluorescence staining of C-peptide, insulin (INS), glucagon (GCG), and somatostain (SST) ( Figure 3E) and the quantification of insulin particles in the ultrastructure of the organoids ( Figure 3F). Moreover, a significant decrease in insulin secretion was detected in the PDX1-mutant organoids in response to glycemic stimulation in vitro ( Figure 3G).

Alterations in m6A methylation in the pancreas of PDX1-mutant cynomolgus monkeys and islet organoids

PDX1 haploinsufficiency has been described as a result of PDX1 regulatory cascade in pancreatic development ( Zhu et al., 2016). However, questions about the existence of other regulatory mechanisms remain unknown. Here, RNA sequencing analysis of pancreatic tissues revealed 7 700 DEGs between WT monkeys and M4, with a large number being down-regulated ( Figure 2A). Considering the significant role of m6A methylation modifications in regulating diabetic pathology and β-cell differentiation ( Li et al., 2023), it is crucial to explore their potential involvement in the pathogenesis of PDX1-mutant diabetes. Based on RNA sequencing, significant changes were detected in the expression of m6A modification-related genes ( Figure 4A), consistent with patterns observed in T2D patients (Supplementary Figure S6A) ( De Jesus et al., 2019). Concurrently, RT-qPCR analysis showed a marked decrease in IGF1R and MAFA, target genes of m6A RNA methylation modulators, in the pancreas of M4 ( Figure 4B). To validate these results, we detected METTL3 expression levels in both M4 and islet organoids. Results showed that METTL3 mRNA expression was decreased in M4 and PDX1-mutant β cells, as expected ( Figure 4C; Supplementary Figure S6B). Consistently, METTL3 protein levels were also significantly decreased in PDX1 +/+, PDX1 +/-, and PDX -/- ( Figure 4D, E).

METTL3 restored PDX1 haploinsufficiency in pancreatic islet organoids

To ascertain the function of METTL3 in PDX1 haploinsufficiency, we overexpressed METTL3 in PDX1-mutant hiPSCs, labeled as PDX1 +/-+ METTL3 or PDX1 -/-+ METTL3. Overexpressing METTL3 markedly improved the morphology of islet-like organoids during the progenitor cell stage, although this enhancement was not observed in the PDX1 -/- group at the pancreatic progenitor cell stage ( Figure 4F). Furthermore, the expression levels of ECM-related genes and target genes were significantly elevated (Supplementary Figure S6C, D). In vitro GSIS experiments further revealed that the PDX1 +/-+ METTL3 group exhibited an increased response to glycemic stimulation, unlike the PDX1 -/-+ METTL3 group ( Figure 4G, H). The m6A methylation sites of PDX1 were identified at the 3’UTR of PDX1 mRNA in both humans and cynomolgus monkeys (Supplementary Figure S6E). Consequently, PDX1 homozygous mutation could not be restored by METTL3 due to the absence of PDX1 mRNA. In summary, numerous genes were altered in PDX1 heterozygous mutant cynomolgus monkeys and pancreatic islet-like organs, among which the gene associated with m6A methylation modification, METTL3, was significantly decreased. As an important regulation for gene expression and transcription, m6A modification may modulate the development and function of the pancreas by controlling key genes for β-cell differentiation and survival, such as MAFA, PDX1, and IGF1R. Overexpressing METTL3 in PDX1 heterozygous mutant islet-like organs can relieve the functional insufficiency of the islet-like organs ( Figure 4I). Therefore, m6A modification is an important factor related to PDX1 haploinsufficiency in PDX1 heterozygous mutants.

Off-target analysis of PDX1-mutant cynomolgus macaques by whole-genome sequencing

In addition, whole-genome sequencing was performed to assess potential off-target sites. Results indicated obvious gene deletion at two target sites (Supplementary Figure S7A) and identified one off-target site in the intron of the Notch3 gene ( Figure 5A, B). To confirm the whole-genome sequencing results, Sanger sequencing was performed at the potential off-target site ( Figure 5C). Results demonstrated that the mRNA expression of Notch3 in different tissues of M4 was not altered compared to the WT monkeys ( Figure 5D). Thus, no apparent off-target effect on the M4 phenotype was observed.

DISCUSSION

The study of MODY4 has faced significant challenges due to the scarcity of clinical cases, which has hindered progress in our understanding of the disease. Existing animal models have failed to accurately replicate the clinical features observed in MODY4 patients, leaving the underlying molecular alterations and mechanisms largely unexplored. Generating effective disease models and clarifying key molecular changes are critical for advancing innovative diagnostic and therapeutic strategies for MODY4.

In this study, we generated PDX1-mutant cynomolgus macaques as a novel model to investigate the pathophysiology of MODY4 and to explore molecular changes associated with the disease. Pathological analysis and transcriptome sequencing revealed that PDX1 mutant monkeys exhibited pathological characteristics more closely resembling human MODY4 patients than previously available models. Notably, alterations in m6A methylation were also identified, highlighting a previously underappreciated regulatory mechanism. While current PDX1 mutant animal models predominantly exhibit perinatal death and pancreatic agenesis ( Holland et al., 2006; Tanihara et al., 2020; Vilarino et al., 2017), our PDX1-mutant cynomolgus macaques not only displayed these features but also developed symptoms such as exocrine insufficiency and pancreatitis ( Figure 1E), providing a more comprehensive representation of the clinical symptoms observed in human patients ( Aarthy et al., 2021; Abreu et al., 2021; Caetano et al., 2018; Deng et al., 2019). The discovery of m6A methylation changes in the pancreas of the M4 monkey is an important finding of this study. Similar epigenetic modifications have been identified in both T1D and T2D in humans ( Benak et al., 2023; Cai et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2022), suggesting that m6A modification may play a broader role in the regulation of diabetes pathogenesis. Most MODY4 patients carry a heterozygous mutation in the PDX1 gene, resulting in pancreatic islet dysfunction consistent with PDX1 haploinsufficiency ( Brissova et al., 2008; Sachdeva et al., 2007). Previous research has shown that PDX1 heterozygous mutations result in a significant reduction in PDX1 expression, often below 50% ( Zhu et al., 2016), which may be a contributing factor to PDX1 haploinsufficiency. Consistent with this, our results demonstrated that PDX1 expression in the M4 macaque and in the islet-like organoids was also significantly lower than 50% ( Figure 3D), further supporting this speculation. Given the crucial role of m6A modification in regulating gene expression and translation ( Li et al., 2024a, 2024b; Wang et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2023), and the fact that PDX1 is a known target for m6A modification ( De Jesus et al., 2019; Regué et al., 2021), it is possible that m6A dysregulation contributes to PDX1 haploinsufficiency. This speculation is further supported by our observation that overexpression of METTL3 in PDX1 +/- islet organoids restored functional insufficiency ( Figure 4F, G). Given the broad regulatory role of m6A in diabetes, targeting m6A modifications presents a promising avenue for future diagnostic and therapeutic developments in diabetes research.

All PDX1-mutant animal models have consistently exhibited perinatal lethality, as also observed in our PDX1-mutant cynomolgus macaques. However, unlike these animal models, MODY4 patients can survive despite the genetic mutation ( Aarthy et al., 2021; Deng et al., 2019; Mruthyunjaya et al., 2017; Yoshiji et al., 2022). The genetic basis of a disease strongly influences its phenotypic expression. MODY4 typically arises from a heterozygous point mutation in PDX1, whereas animal models are created by inducing large deletions in the PDX1 locus, likely contributing to the observed phenotypic differences between humans and these models. Moreover, significant lesions in the liver and kidneys were identified in the PDX1-mutant cynomolgus macaques ( Figure 1D), indicating that the complications associated with MODY4 should not be overlooked. Notably, complications such as diabetic nephropathy, hepatopathy, retinopathy, and neuropathy are well-documented in T1D and T2D ( Grigg et al., 2022; Llorián-Salvador et al., 2024; Sagheer et al., 2018; Shen et al., 2024; Tamai et al., 2021; Tang et al., 2024; Wu & Huang, 2024; Yang & Wu, 2024), but there is a paucity of reports on such complications in MODY4. Thus, the PDX1-mutant cynomolgus macaques present a valuable model for studying these potential complications in MODY4.

The findings of this study highlight the critical role and value of the macaque model in advancing our understanding of MODY4. However, only one PDX1 heterozygous mutant cynomolgus macaque with a pancreas was generated, which did not survive the perinatal period. This limitation introduces several shortcomings. Firstly, the detection of pathological changes, including body weight, blood glucose, blood biochemistry, and complications, could not be performed during MODY4 pathology, which is essential for diabetic study. Secondly, the limited number of PDX1 mutant monkeys precluded the possibility of obtaining statistically significant molecular data. Lastly, m6A levels in the M4 pancreas were not analyzed using MeRIP-seq, which could have provided deeper insights into the molecular mechanisms. To partially address these issues, we performed pancreatic islet-like organoid cultures, which corroborated the molecular findings observed in the M4 macaque. To overcome these limitations, it is important to construct PDX1-mutant monkey models that can survive beyond the perinatal stage. Advances in precise gene modification technologies offer promising avenues for generating patient-specific disease models efficiently and accurately ( Wang et al., 2020). While CRISPR/Cas9 can induce double-strand breaks (DSBs) repaired by non-homologous end joining (NHEJ), resulting in imprecise genetic modifications, newer gene editing technology, such as base and prime editing, can introduce precise mutations without DNA DSBs ( Brooks et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2021, 2024; Huang et al., 2021). These innovations hold the potential to generate viable PDX1-mutant cynomolgus macaques.

Our study successfully generated a PDX1-mutant cynomolgus macaque model, with pathological manifestations similar to those observed in human patients. Additionally, the model enabled the identification of the regulatory role of m6A modification in this disease. These results not only provide a valuable animal model for MODY4 research but also offer critical insights for the diagnosis and treatment of this condition.
