Highway Dimension: a Metric View


Abstract
Realistic metric spaces (such as road/transportation networks) tend to be much more algorithmically tractable than general metrics. In an attempt to formalize this intuition, Abraham et al. (SODA 2010, JACM 2016) introduced the notion of highway dimension. A weighted graph G G has highway dimension h h if for every ball B B of radius ≈ 4 ​ r \approx 4r there is a hitting set of size h h hitting all the shortest paths of length > r >r in B B . Unfortunately, this definition fails to incorporate some very natural metric spaces such as the grid graph, and the Euclidean plane. We relax the definition of highway dimension by demanding to hit only approximate shortest paths. In addition to generalizing the original definition, this new definition also incorporates all doubling spaces (in particular the grid graph and the Euclidean plane). We then construct a PTAS for TSP under this new definition (improving a QPTAS w.r.t. the original more restrictive definition of Feldmann et al. (SICOMP 2018)). Finally, we develop a basic metric toolkit for spaces with small highway dimension by constructing padded decompositions, sparse covers/partitions, and tree covers. An abundance of applications follow.

Introduction

General metric spaces can be very complicated. Indeed, consider an optimization benchmark problem such as the Travelling Salesperson problem (TSP). On general metric spaces, it is APX-hard.
On the other hand, on more
“realistic”
distance measures, such optimization problems tend to be more tractable. Indeed, in low dimensional Euclidean space, TSP admits a polynomial time approximation scheme (PTAS)
[
8
,
80
]
. More generally, metric spaces with constant doubling dimension

However,

The right way to define realistic distance measure turned out to be quite elusive. Bast

observed that road networks are highly structured. Specifically, when one travels long distances, one will necessarily travel through a small set of important traffic junctions (such as central airports and train stations). Bast

Abraham

In the journal version of

Later, Feldmann

For any fixed violation

Later, Feldmann and Saulpic
[

[

For further discussion and comparisons between the different previous definitions of highway dimension we refer to Blum

All these previous definitions have some major drawbacks: mainly they do not catch all the metric spaces which are intuitively
“realistic”
. One such example is the
\sqrt{n}\times\sqrt{n}
unweighted grid. This is a planar graph of constant doubling dimension which roughly resembles the road network of some modern cities (e.g. Manhattan), thus it clearly should be considered
“realistic”
. However, it has highway dimension
\Omega(\sqrt{n})
. Indeed, even if one slightly perturbs the weights so that every vertex pair will have a unique shortest path, still the columns constitute
\sqrt{n}
disjoint shortest paths, and thus any hitting set will have size
\Omega(\sqrt{n})
.
A second problem is that the definition of highway dimension is inherently restricted to graphs, and cannot be applied to continuous metric spaces such as the Euclidean plane (which in our view should be considered
“realistic”
as well).
We would like a definition which is more expressive: one that includes road/transportation networks and hub-and-spoke networks, but also the grid, the continuous Euclidean plane, and desirably every metric space with constant doubling dimension. At the same time, a good definition should also not be too expressive, so that it is still useful from an algorithmic viewpoint.

Our contribution (high level).

We propose a new definition for the highway dimension by relaxing the requirement for exact shortest paths in
subsection 1.1
. That is for every ball of radius roughly
4r
, there is a set of hubs
|H|
of size
|H|\leq h
, such that for every pair of points
u,z
at distance at least
r
in the ball, there is an approximate
u-z
shortest path going though

(see
1.1
).
This new definition has it all:
(1) It generalizes the
subsection 1.1

[
5
,
49
]
; indeed hitting exact shortest paths is only harder than hitting approximate ones.
(2) It generalizes the doubling dimension (see
subsection 1.1
); that is, given a metric with doubling dimension

-net (see
3.1
) in

is a set of
\varepsilon^{-O(d)}

-shortest path for every pair.
(3) It naturally applies to continuous spaces; that is, there is nothing inherently graphic about it.

First, we re-construct the toolkit of
[
49
]
developed for graphs of low highway dimension, and adapt it to the new definition (specifically

Next we construct a PTAS for TSP for metric spaces with small highway dimension (see

Finally, we continue our metric view and develop the basic metric toolkit for spaces with small highway dimension (see

In this paper we introduce a relaxed definition, where instead of hitting all the required shortest paths, it is enough to hit a single approximate shortest path.
As we will see, this change also makes the universal constant

Note that for the new definition the highway dimension is not a number but a function that depends on how well the hubs approximate the shortest path distance.
This is because choosing more accurate hubs will give better precision, and in an approximation scheme we will want to control the accuracy.

Definition 1.1

(Highway dimension)
.

A weighted graph

Computational aspects.

In general, given a center
v\in V

, and
\varepsilon\geq 0

satisfying the requirement in

Throughout this paper, when we say that a graph has highway dimension

of size

Note that any graph

Highway dimension of a metric space.

A major advantage of
1.1
is that is can be applied to metric spaces (even on infinite continuous spaces), and generalizes doubling dimension (see
subsection 1.1
).
Given a metric space
(X,d_{X})
, we can think of it as a complete graph
G=(X,E={\binom{X}{2}},w)
where the weight of an edge
xy
is equal to the metric distance between its endpoints, i.e.,
w(xy)=d_{X}(x,y)
. We will use
1.1
w.r.t. metric spaces. Note that here, the induced graph
G[B_{v}]
is simply the metric space
X
restricted to the points in the ball
B_{v}=B_{X}(v,(4+8\varepsilon)r)
. In particular, for every pair of points
u,z\in B_{v}
,
d_{G[B_{v}]}(u,z)=w(uz)=d_{X}(u,z)
. Hence, due to the triangle inequality, the property from
1.1
in metric spaces is equivalent to the following:
For every pair of points
u,z\in B_{v}
such that
d_{X}(u,z)>r
, there is a point
x\in H_{\varepsilon}
such that
d_{X}(u,x)+d_{X}(x,z)\leq(1+\varepsilon)d_{X}(u,z)
.
In this sense this definition naturally extends to metric spaces and can even be used on infinite metric spaces (such as the Euclidean space
(\mathbb{R}^{d},\|\cdot\|_{2})
).

Note that it is important that our
1.1
is w.r.t. graphs and not metric spaces in order to generalize
subsection 1.1
. This is as balls in graphs are not
“convex”
. That is, it is possible that
u,z\in B_{v}
, while their shortest path does not lie inside
B_{v}
. In particular, it is possible that a graph

will have small highway dimension, while the respective shortest path metric will not (see

).

Our Results

Our main theorem is a PTAS for TSP in metrics/graphs with bounded highway dimension.
In fact we prove our result for the Subset TSP problem, which is a generalization of TSP (see
5.1
).
We remark that, in contrast to doubling metrics, the highway dimension is not bounded when taking subgraphs, and thus solving Subset TSP is not trivial given an algorithm for TSP in our case.

Note that due to
subsection 1.1

et al.
[

and Banerjee

Furthermore, our result also improves and generalizes over the QPTAS by Feldmann
et al.
[

for TSP in graphs of low highway dimension according to

The

Doubling dimension.

A metric space

Definition 3.1
.

A

1.

A

where each
N_{i}
is a
2^{i}
-net of the metric can also be computed in this way, i.e., each
N_{i}
is a prefix of a Gonzales order of

.
The most crucial property of doubling metrics, that formalized as the packing property bellow, is the fact that they contain sparse nets.
The packing property can be proven by repeatedly applying the definition of doubling dimension
[
67
]
.

Basic Properties

In this section we adapt the toolkit for highway dimension created in previous works to our new definition.
As already shown by Abraham
et al.
[
5
]
, low highway dimension implies sparse shortest path covers, as defined next. Note that here we switch to (shortest-path) metrics instead of graphs (this is as the topology of the graph has no role here).

Definition 4.1

(shortest path cover)
.

For a metric
(X,d_{X})
,
r>0
, and
\varepsilon\geq 0
,
an
(r,\varepsilon)
-shortest path cover

\textsf{SPC}\subseteq X
is a set of
hubs

such that for any pair
u,z\in X
with
d_{X}(u,z)\in(r,(2+\varepsilon)r]
, there is a hub
x\in\textsf{SPC}

for which
d_{X}(u,x)+d_{X}(x,z)\leq(1+\varepsilon)d_{X}(u,z)
.
A shortest path cover
SPC
is called
locally
s
-sparse
for scale
r
,
if no ball of radius
(2+4\varepsilon)r
contains more than
s
vertices from
SPC
.

A proof by Abraham
et al.
[
5
]
generalized to
1.1
shows that any graph of highway dimension
h:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
has locally
h(\varepsilon)
-sparse
(r,\varepsilon)
-shortest path covers for any
r>0
and
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
.
Indeed, if we can compute hitting sets for approximate shortest paths in polynomial time, as discussed in
subsection 1.1
, then we may even compute shortest path covers with this sparseness guarantee in polynomial time.
This follows as the proof is constructive.

Lemma 4.2
.

Given a graph
G=(V,E)
, and parameters
r>0
,
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
, there is an
(r,\varepsilon)
-shortest path cover
SPC
, such that if
G
has highway dimension
h:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
, then
SPC
is locally
h(\varepsilon)
-sparse.

Proof 4.3
.

We present a local search procedure to compute the shortest path cover, which starts off with an arbitrary
(r,\varepsilon)
-shortest path cover
SPC
, e.g., the full vertex set
V
, and then repeatedly
finds the densest ball of radius
(2+4\varepsilon)r
and tries to sparsify it. The algorithm halts once the sparsification attempt fails.

In more detail, initially
\textsf{SPC}\leftarrow V
. In every iteration the algorithm finds the vertex
v\in V
maximizing
|B_{v}\cap\textsf{SPC}|
where
B_{v}=B_{G}(v,(2+4\varepsilon)r)
. The algorithm then constructs a set
H
such that
\textsf{SPC}^{\prime}=(\textsf{SPC}\setminus B_{v})\cup H
is still an
(r,\varepsilon)
-shortest path cover. If
|\textsf{SPC}^{\prime}|\geq|\textsf{SPC}|
, the algorithm halts and returns
SPC
. Otherwise, it continues for another iteration after setting
\textsf{SPC}\leftarrow\textsf{SPC}^{\prime}
.
Clearly, the returned set is an
(r,\varepsilon)
-shortest path cover. Furthermore, the algorithm halts after at most
n
iterations, since the size of the shortest path cover is decreased in each iteration.

The algorithm obtains the set
H
as a hitting set to a set system
\mathcal{H}\subseteq 2^{V}
over the vertex set
V
.
Specifically, a vertex pair
u,z\in V
for which (1)
d_{G}(u,z)\in(r,(2+\varepsilon)r]
, and (2) there exists a hub
x\in B_{v}\cap\textsf{SPC}
for which
d_{G}(u,x)+d_{G}(x,z)\leq(1+\varepsilon)d_{G}(u,z)
, is called a
B_{v}
-affected pair. For each
B_{v}
-affected pair
u,z
, the instance
\mathcal{H}
contains a set
S_{u,z}=\{y\in V\mid d_{G}(u,y)+d_{G}(y,z)\leq(1+\varepsilon)d_{G}(u,z)\}
, i.e., it consists of all the potential hubs for
u,z
. The set
H
is then obtained as a minimum sized hitting set to
\mathcal{H}
.
Clearly,
\textsf{SPC}^{\prime}=(\textsf{SPC}\setminus B_{v})\cup H
is still an
(r,\varepsilon)
-shortest path cover.

Suppose for the sake of contradiction that the returned set
SPC
is not locally
h(\varepsilon)
-sparse. That is, there is a vertex
v^{\prime}\in V
such that
|B_{v^{\prime}}\cap\textsf{SPC}|>h(\varepsilon)
.
In the last iteration of the algorithm, it chose a vertex
v\in V
that maximizes
|B_{v}\cap\textsf{SPC}|\geq|B_{v^{\prime}}\cap\textsf{SPC}|>h(\varepsilon)
.
Consider a
B_{v}
-affected pair
u,z
.
That is, there is a hub
x\in B_{v}\cap\textsf{SPC}
such that
d_{G}(u,x)+d_{G}(x,z)\leq(1+\varepsilon)d_{G}(u,z)
.
Let
Q_{u,z}
be any
u-z
path of length at most
(1+\varepsilon)d_{G}(u,z)
. Denote by
b
the vertex of
Q_{u,z}
that is farthest from the center
v
of the ball
B_{v}
. We can bound the distance between
b
and the hub
x\in B_{v}
as follows:

\displaystyle d_{G}(x,b)

\displaystyle\leq\min\{d_{G}(x,u)+d_{X}(u,b),\;d_{G}(x,z)+d_{G}(z,b)\}

\displaystyle\leq\textstyle\frac{1}{2}(d_{G}(u,x)+d_{G}(x,z)+d_{G}(u,b)+d_{G}(b,z))\quad\leq\quad(1+\varepsilon)d_{G}(u,z)~.

Hence the distance from
v
to
b
is
d_{G}(v,b)\leq d_{G}(v,x)+d_{G}(x,b)\leq(2+4\varepsilon)r+(1+\varepsilon)(2+\varepsilon)r\leq(4+8\varepsilon)r
, given that
x
lies in the ball of radius
(2+4\varepsilon)r
centered at
v
,
d_{G}(u,z)\leq(2+\varepsilon)r
, and
\varepsilon^{2}\leq\varepsilon
as
\varepsilon\leq 1
. As
b
is the vertex of
Q_{u,z}
farthest from
v
, the entire path
Q_{u,z}
lies within the ball
B^{\prime}_{v}=B_{G}(v,(4+8\varepsilon)r)
. In particular,
u,z\in B^{\prime}_{v}
,
d_{G}(u,z)>r
, and
d_{G[B^{\prime}_{v}]}(u,z)\leq(1+\varepsilon)d_{G}(u,z)
.
As we assumed that
G
has highway dimension
h:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
,

1.1
implies that there is a set
H_{\varepsilon}
of size at most
h(\varepsilon)
intersecting some
u-z
path
P_{u,z}
in
G[B^{\prime}_{v}]
of length at most
(1+\varepsilon)d_{G}(u,z)
for every
B_{v}
-affected pair
u,z
. Thus this set

is a hitting set for the instance

. Since our algorithm computed a solution

of minimum size, we have
|H|\leq|H_{\varepsilon}|\leq h(\varepsilon)
. But this implies that

|\textsf{SPC}^{\prime}|=\left|(\textsf{SPC}\setminus B_{v})\cup H\right|\leq|\textsf{SPC}|-|B_{v^{\prime}}\cap\textsf{SPC}|+|H|<|\textsf{SPC}|
, a contradiction to the fact that the algorithm halted.

Definition 4.4

(towns and sprawl)

Given an

-shortest path cover

\varepsilon\geq 0

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
T
be a town for an
(r,\varepsilon)
-shortest path cover

for some

and
\varepsilon\geq 0

The following properties hold:

1.

Small diameter: For every town

\mathrm{diam}(T)\leq r
.

2.

Separation: For every town

.

3.

Sprawl: For any node

Proof 4.6
.

The third property (sprawl) holds immediately from the definition of the towns.
Next we prove the first property regarding the bound on the diameter of a town. Consider a town
T=\{u\in X\mid d_{X}(u,v)\leq r\}
, and suppose for the sake of contradiction that there are vertices
u,z\in T
such that

.
By the triangle inequality,
d_{X}(u,z)\leq d_{X}(u,v)+d_{X}(v,z)\leq 2r
. Thus
d_{X}(u,z)\in(r,(2+\varepsilon)r]
, and hence there is a hub

that lies approximately between

Assume w.l.o.g. that

d_{X}(x,z)\leq\frac{1+\varepsilon}{2}\cdot d_{X}(u,z)\leq(1+\varepsilon)r

But then,

,
which contradicts
d_{X}(v,\textsf{SPC})>(2+\varepsilon)\cdot r

Finally, to prove the separation property, consider a town
T=\{u\in X\mid d_{X}(u,v)\leq r\}

u\in T
,
z\notin T
such that
d_{X}(u,z)\leq r

By the triangle inequality,
d_{X}(v,z)\leq d_{X}(v,u)+d_{X}(u,z)\leq 2r

From the other hand, as

,
d_{X}(v,z)>r

It follows that

where the inequality

Later we will want to cover the sprawl in a ball

5
PTAS for the Subset Traveling Salesman problem

High level overview.

Our basic approach is somewhat similar to the algorithm of
[
19
]
for doubling dimension (this idea was later generalized in
[
12
]
).
Roughly speaking,
[
19
]
look for a
“dense”
ball
B_{X}(v,r)
, meaning that the cost of the solution inside the ball is several orders of magnitude more expensive than the radius
r
. The weight of the MST in the ball is used as a rough estimate for the cost of the solution in the ball, and a ball is called dense if this estimate is very high.
If there is no such ball, then a dynamic program can find a near-optimal solution. Otherwise, let
B_{X}(v,r)
be a dense ball of minimum radius.
The idea in
[
19
]
is to then solve TSP on
X\setminus B_{X}(v,r)
recursively, and TSP on
B_{X}(v,r)
by exploiting the structure implied by the minimally of the ball. The crux is that stitching the two solutions together is at cost proportional to
r
(and the doubling dimension), while the cost of the solution in the ball
B_{X}(v,r)
is so high, that it can easily absorb the stitching cost.

Our algorithm for bounded highway dimension uses a similar approach, but the details differ significantly. We believe that this approach could also be used for designing approximation algorithms for other problems for metric spaces with low highway dimension. Very roughly speaking: we are looking for the ball of minimum radius with high doubling dimension. If there is no such ball, a solution for doubling metrics can be applied. Otherwise, we show that this ball intersects a huge number of towns. In each town we can compute a near-optimal solution (by minimality of the ball each town is doubling).
We compute the solution for the metric without the towns recursively, and then we stitch the two solutions together via the hubs of a shortest path cover. As the number of towns is very large, while the number of hubs is small, the solutions for the towns can easily absorb the cost of the stitching.
In order to compare the computed solution to the optimum, we show that we can
“massage”
the optimum solution so that all the outgoing edges from the towns will be into hubs from the shortest path cover.

We continue with a more detailed description.
For every
i\in\mathbb{N}
, fix
r_{i}=(1+\sigma)^{i}
for a carefully chosen value
\sigma>0
. For each level
i
we construct a corresponding shortest path cover
H_{i}
. We
“massage”
the covers so that they will be hierarchical
H_{0}\supseteq H_{1}\supseteq H_{2}\supseteq\dots
, while slightly losing in the sparsity (see Lemmas
5.3
,
5.5
, and
5.7
).
Let
i
be the minimum level such that there is a ball
B_{X}(v,(2+4\varepsilon)\cdot r_{i})
that intersect many
r_{i}
-level towns. Note that the town centers, and the hubs constitute a
(2+\varepsilon)\cdot r_{i}
-net due to
4.5
.
The covering radius
(2+\varepsilon)\cdot r_{i}
of the net is only slightly smaller than the ball radius of
(2+4\varepsilon)\cdot r_{i}
, but still enough to prove that if there is no ball intersecting many towns (for any
i
), then the entire space has small doubling dimension, and thus an approximation scheme for doubling dimension, as found in
[
12
]
, can be applied.
Otherwise, consider such a ball
B_{v}=B_{X}(v,(2+4\varepsilon)\cdot r_{i})
of minimal level
i
that intersects at least
q

r_{i}
-towns for some given threshold
q
. Note that each such town is contained in a smaller ball, and thus the induced metric on the town is doubling.

Next, we will consider a family of more structured solutions called
hub-net-respecting
(see
5.11
).
This is similar to net-respecting solutions as used in
[
19
]
(but also in other previous work on TSP and related problems, e.g.
[
85
]
).
In addition to the hierarchy of hubs
H_{0}\supseteq H_{1}\supseteq H_{2}\supseteq\dots
, we construct a hierarchy of nets
N_{0}\supseteq N_{1}\supseteq N_{2}\supseteq\dots
, where
N_{j}
is an
\varepsilon\cdot r_{j}
-net.
Roughly, a solution is called hub-net-respecting if every edge of weight about
r_{j}
is between a hub in
H_{j}
to a net point in
N_{j}
.
Using the properties of hubs, and the density of the nets, one can argue that there is a hub-net-respecting solution of weight at most
(1+O(\varepsilon))
times the optimum (see
5.12
).
Let
O^{HN}
be an optimal hub-net-respecting tour, and consider its behaviour around the
r_{i}
-towns intersecting the ball
B_{v}
.
Every edge of
O^{HN}
leaving a town must be of weight at least
r_{i}
(as each town is separated from the rest of the metric by
4.5
). However, the towns do not contain
r_{i}
-level hubs. By the structure of the hub-net-respecting tour
O^{HN}
every edge leaving the town is going to a hub, while the source inside the town is a net point.

By the definition of nets and the diameter of the ball
B_{v}
, there is at most one town (intersecting
B_{v}
) which contains a
\Theta(\frac{r_{i}}{\varepsilon})
-net point, i.e., of some level
j
where
\varepsilon r_{j}=\Theta(\frac{r_{i}}{\varepsilon})
. We will simply ignore this town (that is, it will be considered as part of the recursively solved part of the metric). As every edge in
O^{HN}
leaving a town is incident to a net point, it follows that no edges of weight above
\Theta(\frac{r_{i}}{\varepsilon})
leaves our towns.
Next, we construct an
interface

I
as follows: for every index
i\leq j\leq i+\Theta(\log_{1+\sigma}\frac{1}{\varepsilon})
, add all the
r_{j}
-level hubs around
B_{v}
to
I
. By the sparsity of our hierarchical hub sets (in particular
4.7
), the size of the interface is rather small.
Now, consider the solution restricted to these towns and their outgoing edges. All the outgoing edges are into the small interface set
I
, and each town
T
has small doubling dimension bounded by the threshold
q
.
We can thus compute a near-optimal solution to each of these towns and connect it to the interface.
Interestingly, we do not rely on dynamic programming in this step, in contrast to many algorithms of this type, e.g.,
[
12
,
19
,
85
]
.
Instead we treat the algorithm given by
[
12
]
as a black box to obtain a near-optimal solution for low doubling metrics.
Then we can stitch the tours of the different towns together by paying something proportional to the cost of the minimum spanning tree
\MST(I)
on the interface. In particular, as we computed a near-optimal tour for each town, the optimal hub-net-respecting tour will differ from our tour again by something proportional to
\MST(I)
. However,
|I|
is small, while the number of towns is much larger.
Thus by the separation property of the towns (

), the cost of
\MST(I)
can be absorbed by the cost of connecting the towns to the remaining metric many times over.
Finally, we recursively compute a solution for the rest of the metric, and stitch it together with our solution for the towns (again paying just order
\MST(I)
).

5.1
Hierarchical hubs and nets

In order to define towns and sprawl that give the structure of sub-instances we want to solve, we need the hub sets of shortest path covers.
However, we will need a hub set that forms a hierarchy, i.e., any hub on a level

Unfortunately, the shortest path covers

To obtain each

Each

for

iteratively: when we consider a hub

1.

Before finalizing the construction of the hub sets, let us prove the following simple fact.

Lemma 5.3

For every

Proof 5.4

Consider a pair

Lemma 5.5

By induction,

.

5.2
The divide step

Let
\mathcal{T}_{i}
be the set of towns for the shortest path cover

, as given by

Roughly speaking, the next lemma shows that the metric has bounded doubling dimension if the number of towns of every

intersecting each ball of level

is bounded.
However, we will focus our attention on the metric induced by the terminal set
K
lying in some given town
T\in\mathcal{T}_{i}
(recall that we are solving the subset TSP problem, see
5.1

The upper bound

is then on the number of towns for each level

, that contain a terminal and intersect a ball corresponding to level
j
inside

.
Here we let

be arbitrary, but we will choose a specific value for

as a function of the highway dimension and

for our purposes later on.

Lemma 5.14
.

Let
K
be a set of terminals in the metric

.
Assume that for every
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and

, the ball
B_{v}^{j}=B_{X}(v,(2+4\varepsilon)r_{j})
intersects at most
q
towns of
\mathcal{T}_{j}
containing a terminal, i.e.,

|\{T\in\mathcal{T}_{j}\mid(T\cap B_{v}^{j})\neq\emptyset\ \land\ (T\cap K)\neq\emptyset\}|\leq q.

Then the metric induced by
K
has doubling dimension
O\left(\log(q)+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\log(\frac{h(\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon})\right)
.

Proof 5.15
.

To bound the doubling dimension of
K
we need to show that for any
\ell>0
and
v\in K
, the ball
B_{2\ell}=B_{X}(v,2\ell)\cap K
of radius
2\ell
around

restricted to

can be covered by respectively bounded number of balls of radius
\ell
.
For this, let
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
be the smallest index for which

.
Note that this means that
2\ell>(2+4\varepsilon)r_{j-1}>2(1+\sigma)r_{j-1}=2r_{j}
as
\sigma=\frac{\varepsilon}{4+3\varepsilon}
, and so
\ell>r_{j}
.
By
4.5
, each town in
\mathcal{T}_{j}
has diameter at most

and can thus be covered by a ball of radius
\ell
.
To cover the terminals of

lying in towns of

\ell

We still need to cover any terminals in

that do not lie in a town of
\mathcal{T}_{j}

By

around hubs of

To cover these terminals in

The radius

of these balls is larger than

, but since

(2+\varepsilon)r_{j}=(2+\varepsilon)(1+\sigma)r_{j-1}<(2+4\varepsilon)r_{j-1}

For each of these balls we may therefore cover the terminals in the towns of

using at most

centered at terminals.
The terminals in the sprawl for

lying in one of these balls of radius

(2+4\varepsilon)r_{j-2}
.
We may repeat this until the balls are small enough, i.e., of radius at most
\ell
.
However, note that the balls covering the sprawl are not necessarily centered at terminals, but to bound the doubling dimension of

, ultimately we need the balls of half the radius covering

to have terminal centers.
We therefore repeat this process until we reach a level

for which
(2+4\varepsilon)r_{j^{\prime}}\leq\ell/2

At this point we may pick one terminal from each such ball and cover this ball by a ball of radius

Given that

is the largest index for which

Since on each level we cover the terminals in towns using

is at most

{\textstyle O((\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\log(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})\cdot h(\varepsilon))^{2\lceil\frac{10}{\varepsilon}\rceil})}+\sum_{k=0}^{\lceil\frac{10}{\varepsilon}\rceil-1}{\textstyle O((\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\log(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})\cdot h(\varepsilon))^{2k})\cdot q\ =\ (\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\log(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})\cdot h(\varepsilon))^{O(1/\varepsilon)}\cdot q\ .}

Thus the doubling dimension is
O\left(\log(q)+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\log(\frac{h(\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon})\right)
.

Since

.
The runtime will be as desired if we carefully choose

as a function of the highway dimension and

.

Otherwise, we identify a minimum index

of this level

That is, if

is the smallest index for which
|\mathcal{U}^{\prime}|>q

Furthermore, we will exclude a town that contains a certain net point of a higher level from this bound.
More concretely, let

Note that there is at most one such point since

Observe that the ball

contains

but also all towns intersecting the ball

, since the latter has radius

and each town has diameter at most

Now we let

be the set of all towns from

except the town containing

By minimality of

The idea is to solve each of the towns of

, and then stitch these solutions together with a recursively computed solution to the remaining instance.

at distance at most

\textstyle\mbox{\emph{interface}\qquad}I=\left\{x\in H_{j}\cap B_{X}\left(B_{v},(2+\varepsilon)\cdot r_{j}\right)\,\mid\,i\leq j\leq i+\log_{1+\sigma}(\frac{6}{\varepsilon}+8)\right\}

Lemma 5.16
.

Any hub-net-respecting walk is also interface-respecting.

Proof 5.17
.

To show that a hub-net-respecting walk

Lemma 5.18

If

First we will identify a vertex

5.3
The conquer step

In order to obtain a solution to the input instance, apart from the solutions to the towns of
\mathcal{U}
we also need a solution for the remaining instance.
In particular, the remaining instance is still given by the metric
X
, but we reduce the terminals to
K^{\prime}=\{t_{\mathcal{U}}\}\cup K\setminus(\bigcup_{T\in\mathcal{U}}T)
, where

is an arbitrary fixed terminal
among those in the towns of
\mathcal{U}
.
Note that the highway dimension of this instance remains the same since we did not change
X
, but now the ball
B_{v}
intersects at most one town of
\mathcal{T}_{i}
containing terminals, namely the one containing
t_{\mathcal{U}}
.
Thus this instance can be solved recursively.
Let
P^{HN}_{K^{\prime}}
denote the recursively computed hub-net-respecting
(1+O(\varepsilon))
-approximation for this instance.

We now show how to stitch these walks together in order to obtain a solution to the original instance. This lemma uses the well-known patching technique, which involves adding a minimum spanning tree
\textup{MST}(I)
on the interface

.

Lemma 5.20
.

Given a walk
W_{T}
for each
T\in\mathcal{U}
and a hub-net-respecting solution

for

, in polynomial time we can construct a hub-net-respecting Subset TSP solution

for

w(P^{HN})\leq w(P^{HN}_{K^{\prime}})+(1+77\varepsilon)(\sum_{T\in\mathcal{U}}w(W_{T})+2\cdot w(\textup{MST}(I)))

Proof 5.21
.

We claim that taking the union of the minimum spanning tree on the interface
I
and all walks
W_{T}
for towns
T\in\mathcal{U}
, contains a Subset TSP solution for the terminal set
\bigcup_{T\in\mathcal{U}}T\cap K
lying in these towns of
\mathcal{U}
, where each edge of
\textup{MST}(I)
is used at most twice.
We prove a slightly more general statement, which we will reuse later on.

Claim 3
.

Given a set of walks
W_{1},\ldots,W_{k}
with endpoints in
I
, there is a closed walk which traverses
W_{1},\ldots,W_{k}
and visits all of
I
, of total weight at most
\sum_{j=1}^{k}w(W_{j})+2\cdot w(\textup{MST}(I))
.

{subproof}

For each walk

be its endpoints lying in the interface. We construct an auxiliary multi-graph

over
I
as follows: (1) First for every walk

add an edge

between its endpoints to

to

be all the interface vertices that currently have odd degree (note that

has to be even), add a minimum weight matching

between the endpoints of

This finishes the construction of

is at most

. One can see this be recalling the proof of Christofides
[

w(M(A))\leq\frac{1}{2}\cdot w(\textup{TSP}(I))\leq w(\MST(I))
. That is, the weight of a perfect matching is at most half the weight of the optimal TSP tour over

, which by itself is at most twice the weight of
\MST(I)
. We conclude that the total weight of the black edges is

.
All the degrees in

are even, and hence there is an Eulerian tour of

By

, the union of

and the walks

for towns

of cost at most

.
Using

(1+77\varepsilon)(\sum_{T\in\mathcal{U}}w(W_{T})+2\cdot w(\textup{MST}(I)))
.
Note that

also visits the terminal

, which is contained in the set

, since

lies in some town of

.
Hence the union of

and the closed walk

contains a closed walk on all terminals in

Furthermore, the union of two hub-net-respecting solutions is again hub-net-respecting, since all connections fulfill the conditions of

It remains to show that the obtained solution is near-optimal and to bound the runtime.
To show the former, the main idea is to charge the extra cost incurred by the minimum spanning tree on the interface to the cost of connecting all towns of

Accordingly, we first bound the cost of a minimum spanning tree on the interface.

.

Metric Toolkit

In this paper we attempt to systematically study metric spaces with low highway dimension w.r.t. the new proposed

We will introduce several of the most important tools in the algorithm designer toolkit in this context. We will then mention a sample of applications achievable with this new toolkit. However, as the list of applications is very long, we will not attempt to mention all of them. We are sure that more applications will be found in future work.

Padded decompositions and sparse covers are very basic tools in algorithmic design for metric problems. The padding parameter of a metric space is a crucial parameter of the space.
Consider a
partition

\mathcal{P}
of

into disjoint clusters.
The weak diameter of a cluster

is
\max_{u,v\in C}d_{G}(u,v)
the maximum pairwise distance (w.r.t. the shortest path distance in the original graph).
The strong diameter of a cluster

is
\max_{u,v\in C}d_{G[C]}(u,v)
the maximum pairwise distance in the induced graph. Note that in metric spaces (a.k.a complete graphs with triangle inequality on the edge weights) the notions of strong and weak diameter are equivalent.
For a vertex
v\in V
, we denote by
P(v)
the cluster
P\in\mathcal{P}
that contains
v
.
A partition
\mathcal{P}
is strongly
\Delta
-
bounded
(resp. weakly
\Delta

P\in\mathcal{P}
is bounded by
\Delta
.
If the ball

of radius

around a vertex

is fully contained in

, we say that

is

by

. Otherwise, if

by the partition.

6.2
Padded Decomposition

Filtser
[
55
]
proved that if a graph has a
“sparse net”
, then it admits a strong padded decomposition.
Specifically, if there is a set
N\subseteq V
of center vertices such that every vertex
v\in V
has a center
x\in N
at distance at most
d_{G}(v,x)\leq\Delta
, and at most
|B_{G}(v,3\Delta)\cap N|\leq\tau
centers at distance
3\Delta
, then the graph admits a strong
(O(\log\tau),\frac{1}{16},4\Delta)
padded decomposition.
This meta theorem from sparse nets to padded decompositions almost fits our case of highway dimension graphs. Indeed, consider the set of hubs
SPC
, then every vertex has at most
2\cdot h(\varepsilon)^{2}
hubs at distance
(2.8+6\varepsilon)r
by
4.10
, and every vertex from the sprawl has a hub at distance at most
(2+\varepsilon)r
by
4.5
. There are two issues in applying the meta theorem:

1.

vertices in towns might not have a nearby hub, and

2.

the gap between the cover parameter
(2+\varepsilon)r
, to the radius of the sparse ball
\frac{2.8+6\varepsilon}{2+\varepsilon}
is less than
3
(unless we pick large
\varepsilon\geq 1
, but we usually think of
\varepsilon
as being rather small).

The second issue is of a technical nature, as the parameter
3
in
[
55
]
is somewhat arbitrary, and every constant gap will actually be sufficient.
A natural solution to the first issue is to
“enrich”
the set of hubs by adding a single representative point from every town. This will ensure that every point has a center at distance at most
(2+\varepsilon)r
, however, now there is no bound on the number of centers contained in a ball of radius
2.8r
(or even smaller).

Filtser’s
[
55
]
meta theorem is based on an interpretation of the exponential random shifts algorithm of Miller, Peng, and Xu
[
79
]
. This is an algorithm that given a set of centers
N
, sends each vertex to its closest center (that is Voronoi partition), but where the closest center is chosen w.r.t. a random shift sampled using an exponential distribution. Roughly speaking, the analysis shows that the resulting padding parameter is logarithmic in the number of nearby centers.
Our solution to the issue above is to sample the shifts for the town representatives using a different distribution from the one used for the centers in
SPC
. We will choose the distributions such that a vertex from the sprawl will never join a cluster centered in a town vertex. The analysis of the padding probability will now go through. We will prove the following:

6.2

We continue with background on the
[
79
]
clustering algorithm, and then with the algorithm we will be using, and finally with a formal analysis.

6.2.1
Clustering Algorithm Using Shifted Starting Times
[
79
]

Let
\Delta>0
be some parameter and let
N\subseteq V
be some set of centers such that for every

For each center
x\in N

to be chosen later.
The choice of
\{\delta_{x}\}_{x\in N}
differs among different implementations of the algorithm.
Each vertex

of the center

for which the value

x_{1},x_{2},\dots
, and among the centers

that maximize

\delta_{x_{i}}-d_{G}(x_{i},v)
,

An intuitive way to think about the clustering process is as follows: each center

wakes up at time
-\delta_{x}
and begins to

in a continuous manner. The spread of all centers is done in the same unit speed. A vertex

joins the cluster of the first center that reaches it.
The proof of the two following claim was known before (see e.g.
[
55
]
).

Claim 4

Truncated Exponential Distributions.

Similarly to previous works, we will use a truncated exponential distribution. That is, a exponential distribution conditioned on the event that the outcome lays in a certain interval.
The
[\theta_{1},\theta_{2}]
-truncated exponential distribution
with
parameter
\lambda
is denoted by
\mathsf{Texp}_{[\theta_{1},\theta_{2}]}(\lambda)
, and
the density function is:

f(y)=\frac{\lambda\,e^{-\lambda\cdot y}}{e^{-\lambda\cdot\theta_{1}}-e^{-\lambda\cdot\theta_{2}}}
, for
y\in[\theta_{1},\theta_{2}]
.

X
. Using the law of total probability, we can bound the probability that the cluster of
x
cuts
B_{\gamma}

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\mathcal{C}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{X}\Pr\left[\mathcal{C}\mid X\right]\cdot f(X)~dX

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\alpha\right)\cdot\int_{X}\left(\Pr\left[\mathcal{F}\mid X\right]+\frac{1}{e^{\lambda\cdot(b-a)}-1}\right)\cdot f(X)~dX

\displaystyle=\left(1-\alpha\right)\cdot\left(\Pr\left[\mathcal{F}\right]+\frac{1}{e^{\lambda\cdot(b-a)}-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\alpha\right)\cdot\left(\Pr\left[\mathcal{F}\right]+\frac{1}{e^{\frac{\lambda}{2}}-1}\right)~.

Next we consider the case where
x\notin\textsf{SPC}
. Here
u
belongs to a town
T
and
x=v_{T}
. The analysis follows the exact same lines with the only difference being that
\delta
is sampled from
[0,t]
instead of
[a,b]
. In particular, it holds that
\Pr\left[\mathcal{F}\mid X\right]=\Pr\left[\delta>\rho_{X}\right]=\frac{e^{-\lambda\cdot\tilde{\rho}_{X}}-e^{-\lambda\cdot t}}{1-e^{-\lambda\cdot t}}
, and

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\mathcal{C}\mid X\right]

\displaystyle\leq\Pr\left[\rho_{X}\leq\delta\leq\rho_{X}+2\gamma\right]\leq\frac{e^{-\lambda\cdot\tilde{\rho}_{X}}-e^{-\lambda\cdot(\tilde{\rho}_{X}+2\gamma)}}{1-e^{-\lambda\cdot t}}

\displaystyle=\left(1-e^{-2\gamma\cdot\lambda}\right)\cdot\frac{e^{-\lambda\cdot\tilde{\rho}_{X}}}{1-e^{-\lambda\cdot t}}=\left(1-\alpha\right)\cdot\left(\Pr\left[\mathcal{F}\mid X\right]+\frac{1}{e^{\lambda\cdot t}-1}\right)~.

Hence using the law of total probability it follows that

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\mathcal{C}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{X}\Pr\left[\mathcal{C}\mid X\right]\cdot f(X)~dX

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\alpha\right)\cdot\int_{X}\left(\Pr\left[\mathcal{F}\mid X\right]+\frac{1}{e^{\lambda\cdot t}-1}\right)\cdot f(X)~dX=\left(1-\alpha\right)\cdot\left(\Pr\left[\mathcal{F}\right]+\frac{1}{e^{\frac{\lambda}{2}}-1}\right)~.

To finalize the proof of
Theorem 6.2
, we bound the probability that the ball
B_{\gamma}
is cut.

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\cup_{i}\mathcal{C}_{i}\right]\leq\sum_{i=1}^{|N_{u}|}\Pr\left[\mathcal{C}_{i}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\alpha\right)\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{|N_{u}|}\left(\Pr\left[\mathcal{F}_{i}\right]+\frac{1}{e^{\frac{\lambda}{2}}-1}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-e^{-2\gamma\cdot\lambda}\right)\cdot\left(1+\frac{|N_{u}|}{e^{\frac{\lambda}{2}}-1}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-e^{-2\gamma\cdot\lambda}\right)\cdot\left(1+e^{-2\gamma\cdot\lambda}\right)=1-e^{-4\gamma\cdot\lambda}~,

where the last inequality holds as

e^{-2\gamma\lambda}=\frac{e^{-2\gamma\lambda}\left(e^{\frac{\lambda}{2}}-1\right)}{e^{\frac{\lambda}{2}}-1}\geq\frac{e^{-2\gamma\lambda}\cdot e^{\frac{\lambda}{2}-1}}{e^{\frac{\lambda}{2}}-1}\geq\frac{e^{\frac{\lambda}{4}-1}}{e^{\frac{\lambda}{2}}-1}\geq\frac{|N_{u}|}{e^{\frac{\lambda}{2}}-1}~.

Here the second inequality holds as
\gamma\leq\mu\leq\frac{1}{8}
, and the last inequality holds by the definition
of
\lambda=4\cdot(\ln\left(2\cdot h(\varepsilon)^{2}+1\right)+1)\geq 4\cdot(\ln\left(|N_{u}|\right)+1)
.
To conclude, we obtain a strongly
6\cdot(1+\varepsilon)
-bounded partition, such that for every
\gamma\leq\mu=\frac{1}{8}

and
v\in V
, the ball
B_{G}(v,\gamma)=B_{G}\left(v,\frac{\gamma}{6\cdot(1+\varepsilon)}\cdot 6\cdot(1+\varepsilon)\right)
is fully contained in a single cluster with probability at least

\Pr\left[B_{G}\left(v,\frac{\gamma}{6\cdot(1+\varepsilon)}\cdot 6\cdot(1+\varepsilon)\right)\subseteq P(v)\right]\geq e^{-4\cdot\gamma\cdot\lambda}=e^{-\frac{\gamma}{6\cdot(1+\varepsilon)}\cdot 24\cdot(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\lambda}~.

Thus we constructed a strong
\left(24\cdot(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\lambda,24\cdot(1+\varepsilon)\right)=\left(O(\log h(\varepsilon)),\Omega(1)\right)

padded decomposition scheme, as required.

For the sake of simplicity, we will assume that the minimum pairwise distance in

Fix

That is, for every pair

Next, we partition each set

, and

One can think about

as the gap between two consecutive levels in the same tree, while

is an initial shift.
In fact, in our construction,

will be a forest (that is, it might not be connected). But we can easily make it a tree by adding edges between the connected components (with large enough weights so that the distances will still be dominating).
As a result, we will obtain a collection of
h(\varepsilon)\cdot K=h(\varepsilon)\cdot O(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\cdot\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon})

The tree

will take care of all the hub sets

In more detail, consider
u,z\in V

On an intuitive level, the construction of the tree

Every vertex will be connected to its closest hub in
\textsf{SPC}_{q,j}

\textsf{SPC}_{q+k\cdot K,j}

\textsf{SPC}_{q+(k+1)\cdot K,j}

Construction of

.

Fix

. For every

, denote

.

For every

In general, after

Clearly,

{subproof}

7.1
Embedding into
\ell_{p}
spaces

A metric embedding is a map between two metric spaces that preserves all pairwise distances up to a small stretch. We say that embedding
f:X\rightarrow Y
between
(X,d_{X})
and
(Y,d_{Y})
has distortion
t
if for every
x,y\in X
it holds that
d_{X}(x,y)\leq d_{Y}(f(x),f(y))\leq t\cdot d_{X}(x,y)
. Krauthgamer, Lee, Mendel and Naor
[
75
]
(improving over Rao
[
83
]
) showed that every
n
-point metric space that admits a
(\beta,\frac{1}{\beta})
-padded decomposition scheme can be embedded into an
\ell_{p}
space with distortion
O(\beta^{1-\frac{1}{p}}\cdot(\log n)^{\frac{1}{p}})
.
By
Theorem 6.2
, we conclude:

Corollary 7.1
.

Let
G=(V,E,w)
be a weighted graph with highway dimension
h:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
, and fix
\varepsilon\in[0,\frac{1}{2}]
. Then there exists an embedding of
G
into
\ell_{p}
space (
1\leq p\leq\infty
) with distortion
O((\log h(\varepsilon))^{1-\frac{1}{p}}\cdot(\log n)^{\frac{1}{p}})
.

Since every finite subset of the Euclidean space
\ell_{2}
embeds isometrically into
\ell_{p}
(for
1\leq p\leq\infty
), it follows that for
1\leq p\leq\infty
a graph
G
with highway dimension
h:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
can be embedded into
\ell_{p}
space (in particular
\ell_{1}
) with distortion
O(\sqrt{\log h(\varepsilon)\cdot\log n})
.

A norm space of special interest is
\ell_{\infty}
, as every finite metric space embeds into
\ell_{\infty}
isometrically (i.e., with distortion
1
; this is the so-called Fréchet embedding). However the dimension of such an embedding is very large:
\Omega(n)
.
Recently Filtser
[
54
]
(generalizing and improving over
[
75
]
) showed that if a graph
G
admits a
(\beta,s)
-padded partition cover scheme, then
G
embeds into
\ell^{d}_{\infty}
with distortion

(1+\varepsilon)\cdot 2\beta
and dimension
d=O\left(\frac{\tau}{\varepsilon}\cdot\log\frac{\beta}{\varepsilon}\cdot\log(\frac{n\cdot\beta}{\varepsilon})\right)
. Using our
Theorem 6.5
, we conclude:

Corollary 7.2
.

Let
G=(V,E,w)
be a weighted graph with highway dimension
h:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
, and fix
\varepsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
. Then there exists an embedding of
G
into
\ell^{d}_{\infty}
space with distortion
O(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})
and dimension
d=O(h(\varepsilon)^{2}\cdot\log\frac{n}{\varepsilon}\cdot\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon})
.

7.2
Zero Extension

In the
0
-Extension
problem, the input is a set
X
, a terminal set
K\subseteq X
, a metric
d_{K}
over the terminals, and an arbitrary cost function
c:{\binom{X}{2}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
The goal is to find a
retraction

f:X\rightarrow K

that minimizes
\sum_{\{x,y\}\in{\binom{X}{2}}}c(x,y)\cdot d_{K}(f(x),f(y))
.
A retraction is a surjective function
f:X\rightarrow K
that satisfies
f(x)=x
for all
x\in K
. The
0
-Extension problem, first proposed by Karzanov
[
71
]
,
generalizes the
Multiway Cut
problem
[
44
]

by allowing
d_{K}
to be any discrete metric (instead of a uniform metric).

For the case where the metric
(K,d_{K})
on the terminals admits a
(\beta,\frac{1}{\beta})
-padded-decomposition scheme, Lee and Naor
[
77
]
(see also
[
7
,
35
]
) showed
an
O(\beta)
upper bound.
Given a
(\beta,\frac{1}{\beta},\Delta)
-padded decomposition for a metric space
(X,d_{X})
, note that the induced decomposition on every sub-metric
(K,d_{K})
, is also a
(\beta,\frac{1}{\beta},\Delta)
-padded decomposition.
By
Theorem 6.2
, we get:

Corollary 7.3
.

Consider an instance of the
0
-Extension problem
\left(K\subseteq X,d_{K},c:{\binom{X}{2}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}\right)
, where the metric
(K,d_{K})
is a sub-metric of a metric with highway dimension
h:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
, and fix
\varepsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
.
Then, one can efficiently find a solution with cost at most
O(\log h(\varepsilon))
times the cost of the optimal. In particular, there is an
O(\log h(\varepsilon))
-approximation algorithm for the
Multiway Cut
problem for graphs of highway dimension
h:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
.

For further reading on the

-Extension problem, see
[

,
46
,
7
,
77
,
45
,
58
]
.

7.3
Lipschitz Extension

For a function
f:X\rightarrow Y
between two metric spaces
(X,d_{X}),(Y,d_{Y})
, set
\|f\|_{{\rm Lip}}=\sup_{x,y\in X}\frac{d_{Y}(f(x),f(y))}{d_{X}(x,y)}
to be the Lipschitz parameter of the function. In the Lipschitz extension problem, we are given a map
f:Z\rightarrow Y
from a subset
Z
of
X
. The goal is to extend
f
to a function
\tilde{f}
over the entire space
X
, while minimizing
\|\tilde{f}\|_{{\rm Lip}}
as a function of
\|f\|_{{\rm Lip}}
.
Lee and Naor
[
77
]
, proved that if a space admits a
(\beta,\frac{1}{\beta})
-padded decomposition scheme, then given a function
f
from a subset
Z\subseteq X
into a closed convex set in some Banach space, then one can extend
f
into
\tilde{f}:X\rightarrow C

such that
\|\tilde{f}\|_{{\rm Lip}}\leq O(\beta)\cdot\|f\|_{{\rm Lip}}
.
By
Theorem 6.2
, we obtain:

Corollary 7.4

(Lipschitz Extension)
.

Consider a graph
G=(V,E,w)
with highway dimension
h:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
, and let
f:V^{\prime}\rightarrow C
be a map from a subset
V^{\prime}\subseteq V
into
C
, where
C
is a convex closed set of some Banach space.
Then for every
\varepsilon\in[0,\frac{1}{2}]
, there is an extension
\tilde{f}:V\rightarrow C
such that
\|\tilde{f}\|_{{\rm Lip}}\leq O(\log h(\varepsilon))\cdot\|f\|_{{\rm Lip}}
.

7.4
Distance Oracle

A distance oracle is a succinct data structure that efficiently answers distance queries (with some small multiplicative error).
It is known that for every integer
k\geq 1
, every
n
point metric space
(X,d_{X})
admits a distance oracle of size
O(n^{1+\frac{1}{k}})
that, given a query
u,v\in X
, in
O(1)
time returns an estimate
{\rm DO}(u,v)
such that
d_{X}(u,v)\leq{\rm DO}(u,v)\leq(2k-1)\cdot d_{X}(u,v)

[
40
]
(improving over
[
86
,
39
]
).

For the case of metric spaces with highway dimension
h:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
one can simply construct a distance oracle as follows: for every
i\in\mathbb{N}
, construct an
(\varepsilon,2^{i})
-shortest path cover
\textsf{SPC}_{i}
for scale
2^{i}
. For every point
v\in X
, store all the hubs
H_{v,i}
at distance at most
(2+\varepsilon)\cdot 2^{i}
from
v
.
Then given a query
u,v
, simply return
\min_{i}\min_{x\in H_{u,i}\cap H_{v,i}}\left(d_{X}(u,x)+d_{X}(x,v)\right)
.
From
4.1
, one can easily prove that the returned answer is indeed a
1+\varepsilon
estimate. The disadvantage of this approach is that the query time, as well as the size of the distance oracle, both depend on the aspect ratio
\Phi=\frac{\max_{u,v}d_{X}(u,v)}{\min_{u,v}d_{X}(u,v)}
of
X
, i.e., the ratio between maximum and minimum distances. This is an undesirable property, as the aspect ratio is a priori unbounded.

Our approach here is to construct a distance oracle using a tree cover (in similar fashion to previous work
[
78
,
2
,
59
,
52
,
36
]
).
Specifically, given a metric space with highway dimension
h:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
,
Theorem 6.7
gives us a
\left(1+\varepsilon,h(\frac{\varepsilon}{2})\cdot O(\varepsilon^{-1}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon})\right)
-tree cover.
Recall that in every tree
T
in the cover, the metric points
X
correspond to the leafs of
T
, where the ancestors of a point
u
are its representatives in different levels. In particular, for every pair
u,v
, there is a tree
T_{q,j}
in the cover where at some level
q^{\prime}
,
u
and
v
will have the same
q^{\prime}
-level representative
x
and it will hold that
d_{T_{q,j}}(u,v)\leq(1+\varepsilon)\cdot d_{X}(u,v)
. Furthermore, the distance from
u,v
to their lower level representatives are much smaller than
\frac{1}{2}\cdot d_{X}(u,v)
, and thus
x
is the LCA (least common ancestor) of
u,v
in
T_{q,j}
.
There is linear size data structure that given two leaves can find the LCA in constant time
[
69
,
24
]
(and the distance to the LCA).
To conclude, our distance oracle will simply store all the trees in the tree cover, and for each tree it will also store the LCA data structure. Given a query
u,v
, it will go over each tree in the cover
T
, find the LCA
x_{T}
and will return the minimum observed value
d_{T}(v,x_{T})+d_{T}(x_{T},u)
.

Corollary 7.5

(Distance Oracle)
.

Consider an
n
-point graph
G=(V,E,w)
with highway dimension
h:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
.
Then for every
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
, there is a
1+\varepsilon
-distance oracle of size
n\cdot h(\frac{\varepsilon}{2})\cdot O(\varepsilon^{-1}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon})
and
h(\frac{\varepsilon}{2})\cdot O(\varepsilon^{-1}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon})
query time.

7.5
Oblivious Buy-at-Bulk

In the
buy-at-bulk
problem we are given a weighted graph
G=(V,E,w)
, the goal is to satisfy a set of demands
A\subseteq{\binom{V}{2}}
by routing these demands over the graph while minimizing the cost.
In more detail,
\delta_{i}=(s_{i},t_{i})
is a unit of demand that induces an unsplittable unit of flow from source node
s_{i}\in V
to a destination
t_{i}\in V
. Given a set of demands
A=\{\delta_{1},\delta_{2},\dots,\delta_{k}\}
, a valid solution is a set of paths
\mathcal{P}=\{P_{1},\dots,P_{k}\}
, where
P_{i}
is a path from
s_{i}
to
t_{i}
. The paths can overlap. For an edge
e\in E
, denote by
\varphi_{e}
the number of paths in
\mathcal{P}
that use
e
.
A function
f:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is called a
canonical fusion function
if it is (1) concave, (2) non-decreasing, (3)
f(0)=0
, and (4) sub-additive (that is
\forall x,y\in\mathbb{N}
,
f(x+y)\leq f(x)+f(y)
).
In the buy-at-bulk
problem we are given a canonical fusion function
f
. The cost of a solution
\mathcal{P}
is
\cost(\mathcal{P})=\sum_{e\in E}f(\varphi_{e})\cdot w(e)
. The goal is to find a valid solution of minimum cost.
The canonical fusion function provides the following intuition: there is a discount as you use more and more of the same edge. So it is generally beneficial for the paths in
\mathcal{P}
to overlap.
Note that the buy-at-bulk problem is NP-hard, as the Steiner tree problem is a special case (where the canocical fusion function is
f(0)=0
and
f(i)=1
for
i\geq 1
).

In the
oblivious buy-at-bulk
problem we have to choose a collection of paths
\mathcal{P}
without knowing the demands. That is, for every possible demand
\delta_{i}\in{\binom{V}{2}}
, we have to add to
\mathcal{P}
a path
P_{i}
between its endpoints. Then given a set of demands
A\subseteq{\binom{V}{2}}
,

\cost(\mathcal{P},A)=\sum_{e\in E}f(\varphi_{e}(\mathcal{P},A))\cdot w(e)
, where
\varphi_{e}(\mathcal{P},A)=\left|\{P(\delta_{i})\mid e\in P(\delta_{i})~\land~\delta_{i}\in A\}\right|
is the number of paths associated with the demands in
A
that use
e
.
The approximation ratio of
\mathcal{P}
, is the ratio between the induced cost, to the optimal cost for the worst possible set of demands
A
:

{\rm Approximatio~ratio}(\mathcal{P})=\max_{A\subseteq{\binom{V}{2}}}\frac{\cost(\mathcal{P},A)}{\mathrm{opt}(A)}~.

Gupta
et al.

[
66
]
provided an algorithm achieving approximation ratio
O(\log^{2}n)
. Interestingly, their algorithm is oblivious not only to the demand pairs
A
, but also to the canonical fusion function
f
.

Srinivasagopalan
et al.

[
84
]
implicitly showed that if a graph
G
admits a strong
(\beta,s)
-sparse partition cover scheme, then one can efficiently compute a solution for the oblivious buy-at-bulk problem with approximation ratio
O(s^{2}\cdot\beta^{2}\cdot\log n)
.
9
9

9

In fact
[
84
]
required a
“colorable”
strong sparse cover scheme.
However, a sparse partition cover scheme is in particular a
“colorable”
strong sparse cover scheme. See also
[
54
]
.

Using our
Theorem 6.5
we conclude:

Corollary 7.6
.

Consider an
n
-point graph
G=(V,E,w)
with highway dimension
h:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
. Then for every
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
,
G
admits a solution to the oblivious buy-at-bulk problem with approximation ratio
O(\varepsilon^{-2}\cdot h(\varepsilon)^{4}\cdot\log n)
. Furthermore, the solution is also oblivious to the concave function
f
.

7.6
Flow Sparsifier

Given an edge-capacitated graph
G=(V,E,c)
and a set
K\subseteq V
of terminals, a
K
-flow is a flow where all the endpoints are terminals. A flow-sparsifier with quality
\rho\geq 1
is another capacitated graph
H=(K,E_{H},c_{H})
such that (a) any feasible
K
-flow in
G
can be feasibly routed in
H
, and (b) any feasible
K
-flow in
H
can be routed in
G
with congestion
\rho
(see
[
45
]
for formal definitions).

Englert
et al.

[
45
]
showed that given a graph
G
which admits a
(\beta,\frac{1}{\beta})
-padded decomposition scheme, for any subset
K
, one can efficiently compute a flow-sparsifier with
quality

O(\beta)
. Using their result, the following is a corollary of
Theorem 6.2
.

Corollary 7.7
.

Consider an
n
-point weighted graph
G=(V,E,w)
with highway dimension
h:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
. Then for every
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
,
G
admits a flow sparsifier with quality

O(\log h(\varepsilon))
.

7.7
Reliable Spanner

Given a metric space
(X,d_{X})
, a
t
-
spanner
is a graph
H
over
X
such that for every
x,y\in X
,
d_{X}(x,y)\leq d_{H}(x,y)\leq t\cdot d_{X}(x,y)
, where
d_{H}
is the shortest path metric in
H
.
A highly desirable property of a spanner is the ability to withstand massive node-failures.
To this end, Bose
et al.

[
30
]
introduced the notion of a
reliable spanner
.
A weighted graph
H
over point set
X
is a deterministic
\nu
-reliable
t
-spanner
of a metric space
(X,d_{X})
if
d_{H}
dominates

d_{X}
, and for every
set
B\subseteq X
of points, called an
attack set
, there is a set
B^{+}\supseteq B
, called a
faulty extension
of
B
, such that
(1)
|B^{+}|\leq(1+\nu)|B|
, and
(2) for every
x,y\notin B^{+}
,
d_{H[X\setminus B]}(x,y)\leq t\cdot d_{X}(x,y)
.
It is known that every
n
point metric space with doubling dimension
d
admits a
\nu
-reliable
(1+\varepsilon)
-spanner with
n\cdot\varepsilon^{-O(d)}\cdot\nu^{-6}\cdot\tilde{O}(\log n)

edges
[
33
,
60
]
.On the other hand, there is no sparse reliable spanner even for a simple metric like the star graph (which has highway dimension
1
). Indeed, for every
t<2
and
\nu
, every
\nu
-reliable
t
-spanner for the star graph must have
\Omega(n^{2})
edges
[
60
]
.

Nevertheless, it is possible to construct sparse oblivious reliable spanners
[
34
]
for the star graph, where the bound on the size of
B^{+}
is only in expectation. Formally, an oblivious
\nu
-reliable
t
-spanner is a distribution
\mathcal{D}
over dominating graphs
H
, such that for every attack set
B\subseteq X
and
H\in\mathrm{supp}(\mathcal{D})
, there exists a superset
B_{H}^{+}\supseteq B
such that, for every
x,y\notin B_{H}^{+}
,
d_{H[X\setminus B]}(x,y)\leq t\cdot d_{X}(x,y)
,
and
\mathbb{E}_{H\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[|B_{H}^{+}|\right]\leq(1+\nu)|B|
. We say that the oblivious spanner
\mathcal{D}
has
m
edges if every
H\in\mathrm{supp}(\mathcal{D})
has at most
m
edges.
In is known that general
n
point metric spaces admit
\nu
-reliable

O(k)
-spanners with
\tilde{O}(n^{1+\frac{1}{k}})\cdot\nu^{-1}
edges
[
68
,
60
]
, and fixed minor free graphs admit
\nu
-reliable
(2+\varepsilon)
-spanners with
n\cdot O(\nu^{-1}\cdot\varepsilon^{-2}\cdot\log^{5}n)
edges
[
60
]
. See
[
57
]
for results on light reliable spanners, and
[
53
]
for reliable spanners for high dimensional doubling metrics.

Consider an
n
-point metric space
(X,d_{X})
with highway dimension
h:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
.
From the fact that for every
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
,
X
admits a
(1+2\varepsilon,h(\varepsilon)\cdot O(\varepsilon^{-1}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}))
-tree cover (
Theorem 6.7
), it follows that
X
admits a
(h(\varepsilon)\cdot O(\varepsilon^{-1}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\cdot\log n),1+2\varepsilon)
-left sided LSO (we refer to
[
60
]
for definition and details). In
[
60
]
it was shown that if a metric admits a
(\tau,\rho)
-left sided LSO then for every
\nu\in(0,1)
it admits a
\nu
-reliable
2\rho
-spanner with
n\cdot O(\nu^{-1}\cdot\tau^{2}\cdot\log n)
edges. We conclude:

Corollary 7.8
.

Consider an
n
-point metric space
(X,d_{X})
with highway dimension
h:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
. Then for every
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
, and
\nu\in(0,1)
,
X
admits a
\nu
-reliable
(2+4\varepsilon)
-spanner with
n\cdot h(\varepsilon)^{2}\cdot O(\nu^{-1}\cdot\varepsilon^{-2}\cdot\log^{2}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\cdot\log^{3}n)
edges.
