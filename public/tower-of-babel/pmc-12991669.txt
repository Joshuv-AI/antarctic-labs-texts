Burden of Disability, Poverty, and Unmet Needs Among Persons Affected by Hansen’s Disease: Insights from Antulya Nagar, Pune- A Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Satyalakshmi Komarraju, D Sathyanath, Shrikanth Muralidharan, L. Nivethitha, Kalpana Devi, Naresh Kumar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Persons affected by leprosy (PAL) continue to face socio-economic deprivation and unmet health needs despite advances in treatment. Understanding their living conditions, health practices, knowledge, and felt needs is essential for planning effective interventions.Methods:A cross-sectional survey was conducted among 138 PAL residing in a resettlement colony. Data were collected on demographic characteristics, housing conditions, health practices, knowledge regarding leprosy, and perceived needs.Results:All participants belonged to the Below Poverty Line (BPL) category. Most resided in semi-pakka houses (77.5%), with 60.1% reporting unsafe construction and 46.4% inadequate living space. 73.2% reported no knowledge about the disease causation. The most common felt needs were free medical services (61.6%), housing support (19.6%), and livelihood opportunities (15.9%).Conclusion:PAL face multiple vulnerabilities arising from poverty, inadequate housing, risky health practices, and poor disease knowledge. Addressing these requires integrated strategies that combine medical care, health education, livelihood opportunities, and social security measures to improve quality of life and rehabilitation outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

The World Health Organization (WHO) recognizes Hansen’s disease, formerly known as leprosy, as one of the 20 neglected tropical diseases (NTDs), prompting the implementation of integrated, country-owned zero leprosy action plans in all endemic regions.[1] India accounts for around 58% of these cases, highlighting the disproportionate burden in the region.[2] Maharashtra recorded the highest incidence of cases at 17,014 in 2023.[34] Extended latency period, combined with the gradual onset of symptoms, significantly complicates early detection and contributes to transmission.[5] It carries a profound social burden characterized by stigma, discrimination, and social exclusion that not only complicate eradication efforts but also highlight broader challenges in securing adequate attention and resources for comprehensive control and elimination strategies.[6] The disease-related stigma may exacerbate pre-existing social inequalities.[7] Mahatma Gandhi played a pivotal role in modernizing medical approaches to Hansen’s disease, driven by compassion and empathy for affected individuals.[8] India currently maintains approximately 750 leprosy colonies that remain largely isolated from mainstream society due to persistent social stigma.[9] One such community is Antulya Nagar, a leprosy colony located in Yewalewadi, Pune, Maharashtra, India. Established in the early 1970s by survivors from the Dr. Bandorawalla Government Leprosy Hospital in Pune, the colony has become a permanent home for approximately 350 families. The community faces numerous challenges, including inadequate essential services, poor infrastructure, substandard living conditions, and family abandonment due to societal stigma.[10] This study aims to gather detailed information about family composition, socio-economic status, living conditions, health knowledge, and perceived needs among persons affected by Hansen’s disease.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The survey was carried out between October 2024 and November 2024. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of the National Institute of Naturopathy, Pune (IEC/NIN/202507/003). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

RESULTS

The majority were male (63.0%), and female participants accounted for 37.0%. Most respondents lived in nuclear families (93.5%), and the predominant religion was Hinduism (91.3%). In terms of caste/community categories, the largest proportion belonged to the open category (40.6%), followed by scheduled castes (22.5%) and other backward classes (15.9%). Occupationally, 34.1% were not in the labor force, while 29.7% worked as unskilled laborers. Regarding health status, 77.5% reported having a disability, while 42.8% reported diabetes and 11.6% hypertension as comorbidities. All participants (100%) belonged to the Below Poverty Line category, as evidenced by possession of white ration cards. Most (65.9%) lived in congested localities, and 77.5% resided in semi-pakka houses. Construction safety was absent in 60.1% of households. While 53.6% had an adequate number of living rooms, almost half (46.4%) lived in overcrowded settings. A separate kitchen was available in 80.4% of households, but kitchen ventilation was inadequate in 70.3%. Personal hygiene practices varied widely. While 81.9% washed hands before eating, 63.8% admitted to dipping fingers in drinking water, and 87.0% slept without mosquito nets. Open defecation was uncommon (8.0%), and 75.4% reported not sharing personal hygiene items. Among female participants, 36.2% reported using sanitary napkins. Knowledge about leprosy causation was generally poor. Only one participant (0.7%) correctly identified bacteria as the cause. Misconceptions such as attributing leprosy to viruses (5.8%), skin patches (8.7%), or general infection (1.4%) were prevalent. The most pressing felt need was access to free medical services (61.6%), including medicines, physiotherapy, dental and cataract services, and ambulance support. Housing or shelter support (19.6%) and employment opportunities (15.9%) were also key concerns. Other needs included government benefits (6.5%), nutrition (5.8%), education (3.6%), and infrastructure (3.6%).

DISCUSSION

All participants were found to be living below the Poverty Line which restricts access to quality housing, healthcare, sanitation, and other vital services, worsening the long-term effects of the disease and trapping those suffering from leprosy in a cycle of illness, poor living conditions, and poverty.[11] 65.9% of participants reported living in crowded areas, which can promote the transmission of infectious diseases due to inadequate ventilation, close interactions, and poor hygiene. 60.1% had structurally unsafe homes, indicating that many inhabit environments that pose physical hazards due to ongoing structural deterioration. These mirror those historically associated with leprosy-related stigma and exclusion, such as the notorious “leper colonies,” where saline environments, social isolation, and persistent stigma persisted despite cure availability.[12] Occupationally, a large proportion were either unemployed or engaged in unskilled labor, highlighting the economic marginalization that often accompanies chronic illnesses and disability. Socio-economic rehabilitation, including income-generating initiatives and vocational training, will encourage an attitudinal shift towards inclusivity for individuals impacted by leprosy, safeguarding their social value and enhancing their capacity. Notably, 77.5% of respondents reported Grade 2 disability, which is higher compared to other regions in India.[13] This delayed diagnosis and treatment perpetuates functional limitations and stigma. Comorbidities such as diabetes (42.8%) and hypertension (11.6%) were also frequent, suggesting a double disease burden that can worsen disability outcomes and limit rehabilitation opportunities. The examination of health practices reveals that there are considerable shortcomings in areas such as water safety, vector control, personal hygiene, and menstrual health, often influenced by poverty, insufficient resources, and limited access to health education rather than being purely individual choices. 73.2% of respondents indicated they were unaware of the causes of leprosy, signifying health communication initiatives have not been effective. While medical care is the immediate priority, long-term assistance must also encompass housing, livelihoods, social protection and education.

CONCLUSION

The study strongly indicates the need for multi-sectoral interventions focusing on healthcare access, education, housing, employment, and social security to improve the quality of life for persons affected by Hansen’s disease. Future initiatives should aim to provide holistic solutions that encompass medical, social, and economic dimensions to improve the overall well-being.
