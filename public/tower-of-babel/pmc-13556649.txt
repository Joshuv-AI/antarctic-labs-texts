871. The Neurobiology of Stuttering
Authors: S SheikhBahaei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStuttering is a common neurologic disorder with evidence implicating dysfunction in cortico-basal ganglia-thalamo-cortical networks. Although dopaminergic abnormalities have long been implicated in stuttering, emerging findings suggest that disrupted iron homeostasis within basal ganglia circuits may represent an additional and mechanistically informative pathway. Animal models provide an important platform for examining how these abnormalities may interact at circuit, cellular, and behavioral levels.Aims & ObjectivesTo examine molecular and dopaminergic dysregulation in animal models relevant to stuttering and to evaluate how these abnormalities may contribute to basal ganglia circuit dysfunction, altered vocal behavior, and mechanism-based therapeutic strategies.MethodAnimal models relevant to stuttering were studied using neuroanatomical, histologic, behavioral, and pharmacologic approaches. Basal ganglia molecular changes, dopaminergic pathway alterations, and vocal-motor phenotypes were evaluated within a translational framework linking preclinical mechanisms to potential therapeutic targets.ResultsThe findings support a model in which dopaminergic and related molecular abnormalities converge within basal ganglia circuits implicated in stuttering. These alterations are associated with changes in neural systems relevant to vocal-motor timing, behavioral output, and treatment responsiveness.Discussion & ConclusionsThese studies support the view that stuttering can be investigated as a biologically tractable disorder with identifiable molecular and circuit-level substrates. Animal models provide a valuable translational bridge between basal ganglia pathophysiology and the development of targeted therapies for stuttering disorders.
