Flecainide- and isoproterenol-induced alterations in heart rate variability and DFA-derived physiological complexity in anesthetized mice
Authors: Hasan Fehmi Ozel, Mustafa Ozbek, Hasan Kazdağlı, Şeyda Avcıl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHeart rate variability (HRV) provides insight into cardiac autonomic regulation, while nonlinear HRV indices may capture dynamic features of cardiac rhythm that are not fully reflected by conventional time- and frequency-domain parameters. Flecainide and isoproterenol influence cardiac rhythm through different mechanisms, sodium-channel blockade and β-adrenergic stimulation, respectively. This study investigated whether these pharmacological perturbations alter HRV and RR interval fractal organization in mice.MethodsTwenty-four adult male mice were assigned to control (CNT, n = 9), flecainide (FLE, n = 8), or isoproterenol (ISO, n = 7) groups. Electrocardiographic recordings were obtained under anesthesia before and after drug administration. HRV analysis was performed using RR interval series. Time-domain, frequency-domain, Poincaré plot, and detrended fluctuation analysis (DFA) parameters were calculated. Fractal complexity loss was assessed using DFA-derived area-based indices, including dS1, dS2, and total deviation score (TdS). Pre- and post-drug values were compared using a two-way mixed-design ANOVA (Group × Time) with Šídák-corrected post-hoc tests.ResultsA significant Group × Time interaction was observed for mean heart rate (p < 0.001) and mean RR interval (p = 0.013). Post hoc analyses showed that isoproterenol markedly increased heart rate from 391 to 548 BPM (p = 0.002), whereas flecainide produced no significant change in heart rate. DFAα1 also demonstrated a strong Group × Time interaction (p < 0.001), increasing after isoproterenol but decreasing after flecainide. All DFA-derived complexity-loss indices showed significant Group × Time interactions, including dS1 (p < 0.001), dS2 (p = 0.002), and TdS (p = 0.001). Flecainide increased fractal complexity loss, whereas isoproterenol reduced it; accordingly, post-drug complexity-loss values were higher in the flecainide group than in both the isoproterenol and control groups. In contrast, conventional HRV magnitude-based indices, including SDNN, spectral power measures, stress index, and DFAα2, did not show significant Group × Time interactions.ConclusionFlecainide and isoproterenol produced distinct alterations in cardiac rhythm dynamics in anesthetized mice. DFAα1 and DFA-derived area-based indices, particularly dS1 and TdS, provided complementary information about drug-induced changes in RR interval fractal organization. Introduction

Cardiac rhythm is not a simple metronomic process. Even under resting conditions, consecutive RR intervals fluctuate as a result of interactions among autonomic neural inputs, intrinsic cardiac electrophysiology, baroreflex mechanisms, respiration, and humoral influences [1, 2]. Heart rate variability (HRV) therefore provides a non-invasive tool for evaluating cardiac rhythm regulation, while nonlinear HRV approaches may provide additional information about irregularity, fractal organization, and adaptive complexity beyond conventional time- and frequency-domain indices [2, 3]. Clinical studies have also investigated HRV parameters in rhythm-related conditions, including patients with palpitations and ventricular repolarization abnormalities, as well as atrioventricular nodal reentrant tachycardia, supporting the broader relevance of HRV-based approaches in cardiac rhythm assessment [4, 5].

Beyond the magnitude of variability, the structure of variability itself may carry physiological meaning. Healthy biological systems typically exhibit complex and multi-scale fluctuations, whereas aging, disease, or excessive physiological stress may drive the system toward more regular, predictable, or constrained dynamics [1, 6]. Based on this framework, Özel and Kazdağlı proposed a DFA-derived area-based method to estimate fractal complexity loss in heart rate dynamics [7].

This perspective is particularly relevant in experimental models where cardiac rhythm is challenged pharmacologically [8]. Flecainide is a class IC antiarrhythmic agent that primarily blocks fast sodium channels, thereby slowing cardiac conduction and modifying myocardial excitability. Although flecainide has established antiarrhythmic indications, its electrophysiological effects can also create a narrow safety margin under certain conditions, especially when conduction reserve is reduced or proarrhythmic substrates are present [9]. Experimental and translational studies have therefore used flecainide as a tool to probe sodium-channel–dependent alterations in cardiac conduction and arrhythmogenic vulnerability [10].

Isoproterenol, in contrast, represents a different type of cardiac perturbation [11]. As a non-selective β-adrenergic agonist, it increases heart rate and myocardial contractility through β-adrenergic stimulation [11]. In rats, isoproterenol has been widely used to induce acute cardiac stress, arrhythmogenic susceptibility, myocardial injury, and autonomic imbalance [12]. Previous experimental work has shown that isoproterenol administration may alter heart rate, and HRV and provoke rhythm disturbances in telemetry-implanted rats, supporting its usefulness as a model of adrenergic cardiac stress [13].

Although flecainide and isoproterenol both influence cardiac rhythm, they do so through fundamentally different mechanisms. Flecainide mainly challenges the electrical conduction system through sodium-channel blockade, whereas isoproterenol imposes an adrenergic load through β-receptor stimulation [14]. Comparing these two interventions in the same experimental framework may therefore help determine whether HRV and DFA-derived complexity-loss indices show mechanism-specific response patterns under conduction-related versus adrenergic perturbation. This distinction is important because a reduction in HRV magnitude and a reduction in nonlinear complexity are not necessarily identical phenomena [7]. A rhythm may become less variable, less complex, or both; each pattern may have different physiological implications.

Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate the effects of two mechanistically distinct pharmacological perturbations, flecainide-induced sodium-channel blockade and isoproterenol-induced β-adrenergic stimulation, on commonly used conventional and nonlinear HRV indices in anesthetized mice. These drugs were not selected as clinically interchangeable interventions, but as experimental tools representing two different mechanisms of rhythm perturbation: conduction-related sodium-channel blockade and adrenergic chronotropic stimulation. In this exploratory framework, we examined whether these two perturbations were associated with distinct patterns of RR interval variability and DFA-derived fractal organization. We hypothesized that flecainide and isoproterenol would produce partially distinct HRV and DFA-derived response patterns reflecting their different electrophysiological and adrenergic mechanisms.

Methods

Animals and ethical approval

This experimental study was conducted using 24 adult male mice aged 4–6 months and weighing 25–30 g, obtained from the Experimental Animals Application and Research Center of Manisa Celal Bayar University. All experimental procedures were carried out in accordance with the approval of the Manisa Celal Bayar University Animal Experiments Local Ethics Committee (Approval No:77.637.435/326).

The animals were housed under standard laboratory conditions with a 12:12 h light/dark cycle, at an ambient temperature of 25 ± 2 °C. Standard pellet chow and tap water were provided ad libitum throughout the experimental period. All efforts were made to minimize animal discomfort and to reduce the number of animals used in the study.

Before electrocardiographic recordings and pharmacological interventions, all animals were anesthetized to prevent movement during ECG acquisition and to ensure adequate analgesia. Anesthesia was induced by intraperitoneal administration of a combination of sodium pentobarbital and fentanyl at doses of 75 µg/g and 0.04 µg/g body weight, respectively [15]. Adequate depth of anesthesia was confirmed before the initiation of ECG recording and drug administration.

Experimental design and groups

Twenty-four anesthetized mice were randomly divided into three experimental groups to evaluate the effects of pharmacological agents on cardiac rhythm dynamics and heart rate variability. The experimental groups were organized as follows:

Control group (CNT, n = 9): Mice in this group received 0.4 mL/kg saline i.p.Flecainide group (FLE, n = 8): Mice in this group received flecainide at a dose of 20 mg/kg i.p.Isoproterenol group (ISO, n = 7): Mice in this group received isoproterenol at a dose of 4 mg/kg i.p.

Flecainide group (FLE, n = 8): Mice in this group received flecainide at a dose of 20 mg/kg i.p.

Isoproterenol group (ISO, n = 7): Mice in this group received isoproterenol at a dose of 4 mg/kg i.p.

Isoproterenol group (ISO, n = 7): Mice in this group received isoproterenol at a dose of 4 mg/kg i.p.

The selected doses were intended to produce measurable pharmacological perturbations of cardiac rhythm while allowing stable ECG recording under anesthesia. Flecainide was used at 20 mg/kg i.p. as a sodium-channel blockade challenge expected to modify cardiac conduction and rhythm organization [16]. Isoproterenol was used at 4 mg/kg i.p. to produce systemic β-adrenergic stimulation and an acute chronotropic response during the ECG recording period [17]. These doses were selected based on previous experimental rodent studies using flecainide or isoproterenol to modulate cardiac electrophysiology and rhythm dynamics, and to generate detectable ECG/HRV changes within the recording period.

Electrocardiographic recording

Following anesthesia, ECG recordings were obtained from all animals under stable experimental conditions. Animals were maintained in a supine position throughout the recording period to minimize movement-related artifacts. Surface ECG electrodes were placed in a Lead II configuration, and ECG signals were acquired using a PowerLab data acquisition system with LabChart 7 software at a sampling frequency of 4 kHz.

For each animal, a 5-min baseline ECG segment was recorded immediately before intraperitoneal saline or drug administration and was defined as the PRE segment. After administration of saline, flecainide, or isoproterenol, ECG recording was continued, and the POST segment was defined as the first stable artifact-free 5-min ECG segment obtained between 5 and 10 min after injection. The same post-drug timing rule was applied to all groups.

R waves were detected using LabChart 7. The detected R peaks and the resulting RR interval series were visually inspected before HRV analysis. Missed or falsely detected R waves were corrected when possible, and segments containing excessive noise, unstable baseline, movement artifacts, ectopic beats, or unreliable RR interval series were excluded from the analysis. Only stable ECG segments with clearly identifiable R waves and acceptable RR interval series were included in the final HRV analysis.

Euthanasia/sacrifice method

At the end of the experimental protocol, animals were sacrificed by cervical dislocation under deep anesthesia. Anesthesia was induced by intraperitoneal administration of sodium pentobarbital and fentanyl at doses of 75 µg/g and 0.04 µg/g body weight, respectively, as used during ECG acquisition. Adequate depth of anesthesia was confirmed by the absence of withdrawal reflexes before the terminal procedure. Animals remained anesthetized and unconscious throughout the sacrifice procedure. Cervical dislocation was performed only after confirmation of deep anesthesia to ensure rapid death while minimizing pain and distress. This method was selected as a rapid and humane terminal procedure suitable for mice and was performed in accordance with the approval of the Manisa Celal Bayar University Animal Experiments Local Ethics Committee.

ECG rhythm and QRS duration analysis

Before HRV analysis, all accepted ECG segments were visually inspected for premature beats, ventricular arrhythmias, and overt conduction abnormalities. QRS duration was measured as a direct ECG-derived index of ventricular conduction. Measurements were performed from clearly identifiable ECG complexes within the accepted 5-min POST segments. QRS duration values were compared among the control, flecainide, and isoproterenol groups to determine whether the pharmacological interventions produced detectable conduction-related changes in addition to HRV alterations.

Heart rate variability analysis

RR interval series extracted from the ECG recordings were analyzed using Kubios HRV Scientific 4.2.0. After R-wave detection in LabChart 7, RR interval series were visually inspected and imported into Kubios HRV for HRV analysis. RR interval editing was performed by visual inspection of the ECG trace and tachogram. Minor isolated detection errors were corrected when possible, whereas segments with repeated detection errors, ectopic beats, excessive noise, unstable baseline, or movement artifacts were excluded. Time-domain, frequency-domain, Poincaré plot, and DFA-based parameters were calculated from the accepted 5-min PRE and POST RR interval series. DFA-derived dS1, dS2, and TdS values were subsequently calculated from DFAα1 and DFAα2 according to the area-based method described by Özel and Kazdağlı [7].

Time-domain analysis

Time-domain parameters were calculated to assess beat-to-beat variability during the analyzed ECG segments. Mean RR interval and mean heart rate were used to describe the general rhythm profile of each animal. The standard deviation of normal-to-normal intervals (SDNN) was calculated as an index of overall HRV, whereas the root mean square of successive RR interval differences (RMSSD) was used to reflect short-term beat-to-beat variability.

Frequency-domain analysis

Frequency-domain analysis was performed to examine the spectral components of HRV. Power spectral indices were calculated for the very low frequency (VLF), low frequency (LF), and high frequency (HF) bands, together with the low-frequency/high-frequency ratio (LF/HF). These parameters were used to evaluate alterations in autonomic oscillatory activity following flecainide and isoproterenol administration. Because the LF/HF ratio may not directly represent sympathovagal balance, particularly in experimental settings and under anesthesia, it was interpreted cautiously and together with the other HRV parameters.

Nonlinear HRV analysis

Nonlinear HRV analysis was performed to evaluate the temporal organization, irregularity, and fractal properties of RR interval dynamics. This approach was included because conventional HRV parameters mainly describe the amount of variability but may not fully capture the adaptive and complex structure of cardiac rhythm regulation.

Poincaré plot analysis was used to calculate the standard deviation perpendicular to the line of identity (SD1), the standard deviation along the line of identity (SD2), and the SD1/SD2 ratio. SD1 was interpreted as an index of short-term beat-to-beat variability, whereas SD2 reflected longer-term variability within the RR interval series. Fractal properties were evaluated using detrended fluctuation analysis (DFA), including the short-term scaling exponent (DFAα1) and long-term scaling exponent (DFAα2), which reflect short- and long-range correlation properties of heart rate dynamics, respectively.

Fractal complexity loss analysis

Fractal complexity loss was assessed using an area-based detrended fluctuation analysis approach, as previously described by Özel and Kazdağlı [7]. This method was used to quantify the deviation of heart rate dynamics from a theoretical 1/f-like fractal reference pattern. In brief, RR interval time series obtained from ECG recordings were subjected to detrended fluctuation analysis to calculate short-term and long-term scaling exponents, DFAα1 and DFAα2, respectively. DFAα1 was calculated over short-term window sizes, whereas DFAα2 was calculated over longer-term window sizes [7].

After obtaining DFAα1 and DFAα2, the DFA log-log curves were reconstructed in a standardized log-log plane. In this framework, the reference DFA line with a slope of 1 was used as a theoretical 1/f-like fractal reference pattern. The deviation of each animal’s DFA-derived line from this expected reference line was then quantified by calculating area differences within the standardized log-log plane.

The short-term area deviation was defined as dS1, representing the difference between the expected and observed standardized DFA area in the DFAα1 region. The long-term area deviation was defined as dS2, representing the corresponding difference in the DFAα2 region. The total fractal complexity loss was calculated as TdS, obtained by summing dS1 and dS2:

TdS=dS1+dS2Thus, dS1 reflected short-term fractal complexity loss, dS2 reflected long-term fractal complexity loss, and TdS represented the overall deviation from expected fractal complexity. Higher dS1, dS2, and TdS values were interpreted as greater deviation from the theoretical 1/f-like fractal reference pattern. In the present study, these parameters were therefore considered exploratory indices of altered RR interval fractal organization rather than direct markers of cardiac health status [7].

In the present study, dS1, dS2, and TdS were compared among the control, flecainide, and isoproterenol groups to determine whether pharmacological perturbation was associated with measurable alterations in RR interval fractal organization. This approach allowed the evaluation of drug-induced cardiac rhythm impairment not only through conventional HRV parameters but also through the magnitude of fractal disorganization in RR interval dynamics.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism 10. For each animal, parameters were calculated from a 5-min baseline ECG segment recorded immediately before injection (PRE) and from the first stable artifact-free 5-min post-drug segment recorded between 5 and 10 min after injection (POST). Data are presented as mean ± SD. Model residual normality and variance homogeneity were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Drug effects were tested using a two-way mixed-design ANOVA with Group (CNT, FLE, ISO) as the between-subjects factor and Time (PRE, POST) as the within-subjects factor. The Group × Time interaction was the primary effect of interest. Significant effects were followed by Šídák-corrected pairwise comparisons for within-group PRE–POST changes and between-group POST contrasts. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05; figure annotations denote *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001.

Results

A total of 24 mice were analyzed: control (CNT, n = 9), isoproterenol (ISO, n = 7) and flecainide (FLE, n = 8). For each animal, a baseline (PRE) and a post-drug (POST) 5-min ECG segment were extracted, and RR-interval series were used to compute time-domain, frequency-domain, Poincaré, and DFA-based indices, as well as the DFA-derived complexity-loss parameters dS1, dS2 and TdS. All parameters were analyzed with a two-way mixed-design ANOVA (between-subjects factor: Group; within-subjects factor: Time), and the Group × Time interaction was the primary term of interest.

Visual inspection of the accepted post-drug ECG segments did not reveal premature beats, ventricular arrhythmias, or overt conduction abnormalities in any group. QRS duration differed among the post-drug groups (Fig. 1). Flecainide showed significantly longer QRS duration than both the control and isoproterenol groups, whereas QRS duration did not differ between the control and isoproterenol groups. This finding is consistent with the expected conduction-slowing effect of flecainide and indicates that the isoproterenol response was not associated with QRS prolongation in the analyzed post-drug ECG segments.

At baseline (PRE), no parameter differed significantly among the three groups (all between-group PRE comparisons, p > 0.05), indicating comparable pre-treatment cardiac dynamics.

Based on the observed mean heart rates across groups and time points, each accepted 5-min ECG segment contained approximately 1900–2750 RR intervals.

Time-domain responses

Mean heart rate showed a significant Group × Time interaction (p < 0.001). Heart rate increased markedly from PRE to POST in the ISO group (391 ± 79 to 548 ± 120 BPM, p < 0.001) and slightly in CNT (441 ± 53 to 460 ± 48 BPM), whereas it was unchanged in FLE (450 ± 99 to 423 ± 48 BPM). Post-drug heart rate was higher in ISO than in FLE (p < 0.01). The reciprocal mean RR interval mirrored this pattern (p = 0.013), decreasing in ISO (158 ± 26 to 115 ± 30 ms) and differing between ISO and FLE post-drug. SDNN showed no significant interaction (p = 0.302). RMSSD showed a significant interaction (p = 0.027), with lower post-drug RMSSD in ISO (0.86 ± 0.56 to 0.41 ± 0.24) than in FLE (0.84 ± 0.31 to 1.09 ± 0.48; ISO vs. FLE post-drug, p < 0.05). The Baevsky stress index did not differ among groups (p = 0.137) (Fig. 2).

None of the spectral indices showed a significant Group × Time interaction (LF%: p = 0.464; HF%: p = 0.162; VLF%: p = 0.874). Within the CNT group, LF% decreased and HF% increased from PRE to POST, consistent with the saline time course, but no between-group difference reached significance (Fig. 3).

Nonlinear HRV responses

Among Poincaré indices, SD1 showed a significant interaction (p = 0.019). Post-drug SD1 was lower in ISO (0.60 ± 0.38 to 0.31 ± 0.17) than in FLE (0.59 ± 0.22 to 0.79 ± 0.31; ISO vs. FLE post-drug, p < 0.05), whereas SD2 showed no significant effect (p = 0.600). The short-term DFA scaling exponent DFAα1 showed the strongest nonlinear interaction (p < 0.001). DFAα1 increased from PRE to POST in ISO (0.57 ± 0.19 to 0.98 ± 0.32, p < 0.001) and decreased in FLE (0.77 ± 0.24 to 0.49 ± 0.16, p < 0.05). Post-drug DFAα1 separated ISO from FLE (p < 0.001) and ISO from CNT (p < 0.05). The long-term exponent DFAα2 showed no significant interaction (p = 0.912) (Fig. 4).

DFA-derived fractal complexity-loss responses

All three DFA-derived complexity-loss parameters showed significant Group × Time interactions: dS1 (p < 0.001), dS2 (p = 0.002) and the total deviation score TdS (p = 0.001). The direction was consistent across all three indices. Flecainide increased fractal complexity loss from PRE to POST (dS1, 0.044 ± 0.040 to 0.092 ± 0.030, p < 0.01; dS2, 0.144 ± 0.059 to 0.223 ± 0.076; TdS, 0.186 ± 0.099 to 0.316 ± 0.100, p < 0.05), isoproterenol decreased it (dS2, 0.195 ± 0.130 to 0.074 ± 0.061, p < 0.01; TdS, 0.274 ± 0.160 to 0.117 ± 0.088, p < 0.05), and the control group was unchanged. Post-drug complexity-loss values were higher in FLE than in ISO (dS1, p < 0.05; dS2, p < 0.01; TdS, p < 0.01) and, for dS2 and TdS, higher in FLE than in CNT (p < 0.05) (Fig. 5).

Discussion

The present study evaluated the effects of flecainide and isoproterenol on conventional HRV indices and DFA-derived fractal complexity loss parameters in mice. The main finding was that conventional HRV parameters showed relatively heterogeneous and mostly non-significant changes, whereas nonlinear and DFA-derived indices captured more distinct drug-related alterations. In particular, all three DFA-derived complexity-loss indices (dS1, dS2 and TdS) showed significant Group × Time interactions, with flecainide increasing these indices, isoproterenol decreasing them, and saline-treated animals remaining unchanged. This drug-specific dissociation suggests that DFA-derived area-based indices may capture different patterns of RR interval fractal organization induced by sodium-channel blockade and β-adrenergic stimulation. However, these parameters should be interpreted as exploratory measures of altered RR interval fractal organization, and their biological and electrophysiological relevance requires further validation before they can be considered markers of cardiac dysfunction or arrhythmia severity.

The comparison between flecainide and isoproterenol should be interpreted within an exploratory physiological framework rather than as a direct clinical comparison between two therapeutic agents. These drugs were selected because they represent two mechanistically different ways of perturbing cardiac rhythm regulation. Flecainide primarily modifies cardiac conduction through fast sodium-channel blockade, whereas isoproterenol increases β-adrenergic stimulation and imposes an acute chronotropic and adrenergic load. Therefore, the rationale for including these two agents was to determine whether HRV and DFA-derived fractal organization indices show different response patterns under conduction-related versus adrenergic perturbation. The present findings should not be interpreted as evidence that dS1, dS2, or TdS can diagnose specific arrhythmia types. Such diagnostic interpretation would require validation in established arrhythmia models and independent datasets. Rather, the current results suggest that these indices may provide complementary quantitative information about drug-induced changes in RR interval organization.

Physiological systems do not operate as simple linear or metronomic processes. Healthy heart rate dynamics are characterized by complex, scale-dependent fluctuations that arise from the interaction of autonomic, respiratory, hemodynamic, and intrinsic cardiac regulatory mechanisms [3, 18]. Goldberger and colleagues proposed that aging and disease are associated with a loss of physiological complexity, meaning that biological outputs may become more regular, more random, or less adaptable under impaired regulatory conditions [3]. In cardiac rhythm analysis, Peng et al. demonstrated that healthy heartbeat intervals exhibit long-range fractal correlations, whereas these correlations are altered or degraded in pathological conditions [18]. In the present study, this framework was extended to an acute pharmacological model by using both classical HRV metrics and area-based DFA-derived complexity loss parameters.

A consistent pattern was observed across the complexity-loss indices, which quantify deviation from the expected 1/f-like DFA organization. Flecainide increased dS1, dS2 and TdS from baseline, whereas isoproterenol decreased them and saline-treated animals were unchanged. This indicates that flecainide moved cardiac rhythm further away from the expected fractal organization at both short- and long-range scales, while β-adrenergic stimulation shifted it in the opposite direction. The area-based indices captured the deviation from the expected fractal pattern in addition to the scaling slope, thereby complementing DFAα1. The present findings are therefore in line with the approach proposed by Özel and Kazdağlı, in which dS1, dS2, and TdS were introduced to quantify fractal complexity loss from standardized DFA log-log plots [7] (Fig. 4).

Flecainide produced a significant increase in dS1, dS2 and TdS, and post-drug complexity-loss values were higher in the flecainide group than in both the isoproterenol and control groups. This result is biologically plausible considering the electrophysiological profile of flecainide. Flecainide is a class Ic antiarrhythmic agent that blocks fast sodium channels and slows cardiac conduction [14]. Although this mechanism may stabilize certain arrhythmias, sodium channel blockade can also alter impulse propagation and increase arrhythmogenic vulnerability under specific conditions [14]. The increase in dS1 observed in the flecainide group may therefore reflect a short-term disturbance in beat-to-beat regulatory organization caused by altered conduction dynamics. Of note, this effect was only partially reflected by conventional time-domain indices: SDNN showed no significant change, and although RMSSD differed between flecainide and isoproterenol after treatment, the magnitude indices did not capture the consistent short- and long-range fractal disorganization seen with the DFA-derived parameters. This distinction suggests that flecainide may alter the temporal structure of cardiac rhythm even when the overall magnitude of variability is not markedly changed.

The ECG-based QRS analysis further supports this interpretation. Flecainide significantly prolonged QRS duration compared with both the control and isoproterenol groups, consistent with its sodium-channel blocking and conduction-slowing effect. In contrast, isoproterenol did not prolong QRS duration, suggesting that its acute effect in the present recording window was primarily chronotropic rather than conduction-slowing. Importantly, no premature beats, ventricular arrhythmias, or overt conduction abnormalities were observed in the accepted ECG segments. Therefore, the observed HRV and DFA-derived changes should be interpreted as alterations in RR interval organization under pharmacological perturbation rather than as direct evidence of overt arrhythmic events.

Isoproterenol, in contrast, decreased the DFA-derived complexity-loss indices despite producing marked tachycardia and a clear increase in DFAα1. This finding may appear counterintuitive if dS1, dS2, and TdS are interpreted as direct markers of cardiac health status. However, these indices quantify deviation from a predefined 1/f-like DFA reference geometry and should not be interpreted as simple linear indicators of rhythm severity or physiological improvement [7, 18]. Therefore, the reduction in dS1, dS2, and TdS after isoproterenol does not imply that acute β-adrenergic stimulation produced a healthier cardiac state. Rather, the simultaneous increase in DFAα1 suggests that isoproterenol shifted the RR interval series toward more correlated, smoother, and more regular short-term dynamics within the analyzed early post-injection window, consistent with the interpretation of DFA as an index of fractal correlation properties in HRV [18, 19]. This pattern may reflect the effect of acute chronotropic acceleration caused by β-adrenergic stimulation, reduced beat-to-beat variability, anesthesia-related modulation of autonomic reflexes, or interaction between the drug effect and the selected recording window [13, 20]. Thus, the isoproterenol response should be interpreted as a distinct alteration in fractal organization, not as a reduction in cardiac stress or an improvement in physiological adaptability.

In the present dataset, most conventional HRV parameters showed less consistent Group × Time effects than the nonlinear and DFA-derived parameters. SDNN, spectral powers (LF%, HF%, VLF%), and the stress index did not show significant Group × Time interactions, although RMSSD differed between the flecainide and isoproterenol groups after treatment. These non-significant findings should not be interpreted as evidence that conventional HRV parameters are unaffected by flecainide or isoproterenol. Rather, given the small sample size and variability of HRV responses, the study may have been underpowered to detect more subtle changes in some conventional indices. Conventional HRV indices quantify the amount of variability within specific domains, but they do not fully describe the temporal organization, correlation structure, or complexity of RR interval dynamics [2]. In addition, LF/HF should not be interpreted as a direct measure of sympathovagal balance, particularly in anesthetized animal experiments and pharmacological intervention studies [21]. Billman (2013) emphasized that LF/HF does not accurately quantify cardiac sympathovagal balance, and this limitation is especially relevant in experimental settings where heart rate, respiration, anesthesia, and drug effects may interact [21]. For this reason, the present interpretation gives greater weight to the combined nonlinear and DFA-derived findings rather than to LF/HF alone.

Poincaré-plot analysis provided a further perspective on the short- versus long-term organization of the response. SD1 showed a significant Group × Time interaction (p = 0.019): Post-drug SD1 was reduced after isoproterenol but increased after flecainide, so that the two drugs diverged significantly at the post-drug time point (Fig. 3). Because SD1 is geometrically equivalent to RMSSD and reflects short-term, beat-to-beat variability [2], this pattern paralleled the RMSSD findings (higher in flecainide than isoproterenol after treatment) and reinforces the internal consistency of the short-term results. In contrast, SD2, which captures longer-term variability, showed no significant interaction (p = 0.600), indicating that the drug-related effects were concentrated in the short-term domain. Notably, SD1 and DFAα1 moved in opposite directions across the two drugs: isoproterenol decreased SD1 while increasing DFAα1, whereas flecainide increased SD1 while decreasing DFAα1. This inverse relationship is physiologically coherent, since a higher short-term scaling exponent corresponds to more correlated, smoother and therefore less variable beat-to-beat dynamics and is in line with previous reports that nonlinear indices provide complementary information beyond the magnitude of variability in animal models of altered cardiac control [19].

The use of anesthetized animals is an important methodological consideration. Anesthesia can substantially influence HRV by modifying autonomic tone, respiratory pattern, heart rate, cardiac rhythm, and baroreflex regulation. The anesthetic protocol used in the present study was selected based on our previous work comparing classical and nonlinear HRV parameters in mice under different anesthetic regimens, including sodium pentobarbital-based anesthesia [20]. In the present study, all animals were studied under the same anesthetic protocol, which reduced between-group variability related to anesthesia. However, the absolute HRV values and the direction of drug-induced changes should still be interpreted within the context of anesthetized experimental conditions. Therefore, the present findings should not be used to draw strong conclusions regarding sympathovagal balance or basal autonomic tone. This is particularly important for isoproterenol, since β-adrenergic responses may be modified by anesthetic depth, respiratory changes, and time-dependent physiological changes during the recording period.

The interpretation of the post-drug HRV profile should also consider the selected recording window. Flecainide and isoproterenol have different pharmacodynamic properties, and the POST segment represents the rhythm organization within the predefined early post-injection recording period rather than a full time-course analysis. Therefore, the present results should be interpreted as time-window-specific responses to sodium-channel blockade and β-adrenergic stimulation. Future studies using repeated post-drug ECG segments would be useful to characterize the temporal evolution of HRV and DFA-derived indices after each intervention.

The potential clinical implication of the present findings is that DFA-derived complexity-loss indices may provide complementary information about RR interval organization under different types of rhythm perturbation. This may be relevant because conventional HRV parameters and nonlinear HRV indices can capture different aspects of cardiac rhythm regulation. Clinical studies have also examined HRV parameters in rhythm-related conditions, including palpitations, ventricular repolarization abnormalities, and atrioventricular nodal reentrant tachycardia, supporting the broader relevance of HRV-based approaches in cardiac rhythm evaluation. However, the present study was conducted in anesthetized mice using an acute pharmacological model, and the findings should not be directly extrapolated to clinical diagnosis or risk stratification. Before dS1, dS2, or TdS can be considered clinically useful, they should be validated in established arrhythmia models, longer ECG recordings, independent datasets, and clinical populations with well-characterized rhythm disorders.

Conclusion

In conclusion, flecainide and isoproterenol produced distinct alterations in HRV and RR interval fractal organization in anesthetized mice. Flecainide increased DFA-derived area-based deviation indices, whereas isoproterenol decreased these indices despite producing marked tachycardia. Accordingly, reduced dS1, dS2, or TdS values under acute isoproterenol stimulation should not be interpreted as improved cardiac health, but as a time-window-specific change in RR interval fractal organization. The present results suggest the use of DFA-derived indices may provide complementary exploratory information in pharmacological studies of cardiac rhythm dynamics. Further studies using established arrhythmia models, larger sample sizes, direct ECG interval measurements with longer ECG recordings, and independent datasets are required to determine their broader physiological and translational relevance.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small, which may have limited statistical power, particularly for conventional HRV indices. Therefore, non-significant Group × Time interactions should not be interpreted as evidence of no effect.

Second, all ECG recordings were obtained under anesthesia. Although the same protocol was applied across groups and was selected based on our previous HRV work in anesthetized mice (Kazdağlı et al., [20]), anesthesia may influence autonomic regulation, respiration, heart rate, and HRV indices. Thus, absolute HRV values, frequency-domain indices, and LF/HF should be interpreted cautiously.

Third, the study did not use established arrhythmia models. Although direct ECG inspection and QRS analysis were added, the current data cannot determine whether dS1, dS2, or TdS can discriminate among arrhythmia types or serve as diagnostic markers.

Finally, the analysis was based on single 5-min PRE and POST ECG segments rather than longer recordings or a full post-injection time course. This limits interpretation of time-dependent responses and longer-range fractal indices such as DFAα2 and dS2. In addition, dS1, dS2, and TdS are relatively new DFA-derived complexity-loss parameters and require further validation in larger datasets, longer recordings, independent cohorts, and clinical or experimental rhythm-disorder models.
