YITP-25-167 Real eigenvalue/vector distributions of random real antisymmetric tensors
Nicolas Delporte, Giacomo La Scala, Naoki Sasakura, Reiko Toriumi

Abstract
Real eigenpairs of a real antisymmetric tensor of order p p and dimension N N can be defined as pairs of a real eigenvalue and p p orthonormal N N -dimensional real eigenvectors. We compute the signed and the genuine distributions of such eigenvalues of Gaussian random real antisymmetric tensors by using a quantum field theoretical method. An analytic expression for finite N N is obtained for the signed distribution and the analytic large- N N asymptotic forms for both. We compute the edge of the distribution for large- N N , one application of which is to give an upper bound (believed tight) of the injective norm of the random real antisymmetric tensor. We find a large- N N universality across various tensor eigenvalue distributions: the large- N N asymptotic forms of the distributions of the eigenvalues z z of the complex, complex symmetric, real symmetric, and real antisymmetric random tensors are all expressed by e N ​ B ​ h p ​ ( z c 2 / z 2 ) + o ⁡ ( N ) e^{N\,B\,h_{p}(z_{c}^{2}/z^{2})+o(N)} , where the function h p ​ ( ⋅ ) h_{p}(\cdot) depends only on the order p p , while B B and z c z_{c} differ for each case, N ​ B NB being the total dimension of the eigenvectors and z c z_{c} being determined by the phase transition point of the quantum field theory.

2
The Permuted Eigenvector Distribution

2.1
The Eigenvalue Problem

In order to introduce the problem of eigenvalues of antisymmetric tensors, let us temporarily restrict to an order 3 tensor. Let
T\in\otimes^{3}\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a real tensor, totally antisymmetric in the exchange of any two indices in the following sense:

T_{abc}=-T_{bac}=T_{bca}=-T_{cba}=T_{cab}=-T_{acb}\,.

(2.1)

The problem that we now consider is a generalisation of the eigenvalue problem, usually defined for matrices, inspired by the approach used in
[
13
]
. First, we introduce a family of distinct real vectors with two indices: a vector one in the
\mathbb{R}^{N}
space we are considering our tensor over, and the second indicating a space we will call “flavour”. These vectors take the form
w^{i}_{a}\in\mathbb{R}
, with
i\in\{1,2,3\}
being the flavour indices and
a\in\{1,\dots,N\}
the real space indices. We consider the following system of coupled equations, obtained by permuting the contractions of this tensor with each vector. Repeated indices are summed over, employing Einstein summation notation:

\begin{cases}T_{abc}w^{2}_{b}w^{3}_{c}=z^{1}w^{1}_{a}\\
T_{abc}w^{3}_{b}w^{1}_{c}=z^{2}w^{2}_{a}\\
T_{abc}w^{1}_{b}w^{2}_{c}=z^{3}w^{3}_{a}\,.\end{cases}

(2.2)

This system defines what we will call a “permuted eigenvector problem”, and it is solved by a tuple
(z^{1},z^{2},z^{3},w^{1},w^{2},w^{3})
, with
z^{1},z^{2},z^{3}\in\mathbb{R}
, where we take all three vectors to be normalised to
|w^{i}|=1

1
1

1

This can always be imposed by absorbing the norm of a non-normalised vector into redefinitions of
z^{1},z^{2},z^{3}
, so is without loss of generality.
. By contracting each vector of different flavour with itself and permuting tensor indices, we can show that all the
z^{i}
are equal to each other:

\displaystyle T_{abc}w^{1}_{a}w^{2}_{b}w^{3}_{c}=z^{1}w^{1}_{a}w^{1}_{a}=z^{2}w^{2}_{a}w^{2}_{a}=z^{3}w^{3}_{a}w^{3}_{a}

(2.3)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle z^{1}|w^{1}|^{2}=z^{2}|w^{2}|^{2}=z^{3}|w^{3}|^{2}

(2.4)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle z:=z^{1}=z^{2}=z^{3}\,.

(2.5)

Therefore, we call
z\in\mathbb{R}
one eigenvalue of the system, associated with the triple of vectors
(w^{1},w^{2},w^{3})
, which for convenience we will call an “eigentuple”. By tensor antisymmetry, the vectors in this tuple are all orthonormal:

\displaystyle zw^{1}_{a}w^{2}_{a}=T_{abc}w^{2}_{a}w^{2}_{b}w^{3}_{c}=-T_{bac}w^{2}_{b}w^{2}_{a}w^{3}_{c}=0,

\displaystyle zw^{2}_{a}w^{3}_{a}=\dots=0,\quad zw^{3}_{a}w^{1}_{a}=\dots=0

(2.6)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow w^{i}_{a}w^{j}_{a}=\delta^{ij}\,.

(2.7)

We therefore see that the solution to our permuted eigenvalue problem can be written as
(z,w^{1},w^{2},w^{3})
, with all vectors in the eigentuple being orthonormal to one another. In this paper, we will consider only the case where
z\neq 0
. In the homogenous case of
z=0
, the system has continuous degeneracies and the methods by which these eigentuples are studied cannot be applied. We also exclude trivial solutions where either
w^{i}=0
. Differently from the case of matrix eigenvalues, the different number of vectors contracted appearing on the two sides of the equation system (
2.2
) allows us to absorb the eigenvalue on the right into the norm of the vectors, by redefining them:

\displaystyle w^{i}_{a}=:zv^{i}_{a}

(2.8)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle|z|^{2}T_{abc}v^{2}_{b}v^{3}_{c}=|z|^{2}v^{1}_{a}\Rightarrow T_{abc}v^{2}_{b}v^{3}_{c}=v^{1}_{a}

(2.9)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle|z|^{2}=\frac{1}{|v^{i}|^{2}}\,.

(2.10)

When returning to the eigenvalue
z
, we can always take its positive value. Using this, we can rewrite the system of (
2.2
) in the following single expression:

\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{ijk}T_{abc}v^{j}_{b}v^{k}_{c}=v^{i}_{a},\quad\forall i\in\{1,2,3\},a\in\{1,\dots,N\}\,,

(2.11)

where
\epsilon^{ijk}
denotes the Levi-Civita symbol.

From this equation, we can generalise the permuted eigenvalue problem to any
p\geq 3
. Let
T\in\otimes^{p}\mathbb{R}^{N}
be an order
p
totally antisymmetric tensor, and take the unnormalised real vectors
v^{i}_{a}
to have flavours
i\in\{1,\dots,p\}
and
a\in\{1,\dots,N\}
, then we define the eigenvalue problem as the simultaneous solution of:

\frac{1}{(p-1)!}\epsilon^{ii_{2}\dots i_{p}}T_{aa_{2}\dots a_{p}}v^{i_{2}}_{a_{2}}\dots v^{i_{p}}_{a_{p}}=v^{i}_{a},\quad\forall i\in\{1,\dots,p\},a\in\{1,\dots,N\}\,.

(2.12)

In a similar fashion to equation (
2.10
), we then relate the unnormalised vectors to the eigenvalue of the normalised vectors by:

z=\frac{1}{|v^{i}|^{p-2}}\,.

(2.13)

Note that, for general
p
,
one can always take positive eigenvalues by flipping the overall sign of
w^{1}_{a}
.
These constitute the definitions for general
p
which we can now study in the context of a randomly distributed tensor
T
.

2.2
Randomising the Tensor and Obtaining the Density

The permuted eigenvalue problem (
2.12
) has been formulated for a fixed tensor
T\in\otimes^{p}\mathbb{R}^{N}
, which we now promote to being random. We take the tensor components to be i.i.d. centered Gaussian variables with variance
(2\alpha p!)^{-1}
and a constant
\alpha>0
. We define the measure:

\displaystyle\int\mathcal{D}T\,e^{-\alpha\sum_{a_{1}\dots a_{p}}T^{2}_{a_{1}\dots a_{p}}}\,\bullet:=\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{T}}\int\prod_{a_{1}<\dots<a_{p}}dT_{a_{1}\dots a_{p}}\,e^{-\alpha\sum_{a_{1}\dots a_{p}}T^{2}_{a_{1}\dots a_{p}}}\,\bullet\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{T}:=\int\prod_{a_{1}<\dots<a_{p}}dT_{a_{1}\dots a_{p}}\,e^{-\alpha\sum_{a_{1}\dots a_{p}}T^{2}_{a_{1}\dots a_{p}}}\,.

(2.14)

As a comparison to other matrix-valued probability distributions, this measure can be considered an adaptation of the Gaussian Orthogonal Ensemble (GOE) to tensors, with a further modification for the antisymmetry of the indices.

Given a fixed tensor, we define the following variables, which enforce that equation (
2.12
) is solved when they are set to zero:

f^{i}_{a}:=v^{i}_{a}-\frac{1}{(p-1)!}\epsilon^{ii_{2}\dots i_{p}}T_{aa_{2}\dots a_{p}}v^{i_{2}}_{a_{2}}\dots v^{i_{p}}_{a_{p}}\,.

(2.15)

Denote all eigentuples that solve (
2.12
), indexed by
I
, as
\{v\}_{I}(T)=(v^{1},\dots,v^{p})_{I}(T)
. Then, using equation (
2.15
) and a multi-index determinant, we have the following identity:

\prod_{i,a}\delta(f^{i}_{a})=\sum_{I}\Bigg|\det\left(\frac{\partial f^{i}_{a}}{\partial v^{j}_{b}}\big(\{v\}_{I}(T)\big)\right)\Bigg|^{-1}\delta(\{v\}-\{v\}_{I}(T))\,.

(2.16)

For a fixed tensor
T
, the eigenvector tuple density can be simply expressed in terms of
\delta
-functions at these
\{v\}_{I}(T)
and simplified with (
2.16
):

\rho(\{v\},T)=\sum_{I}\delta(\{v\}-\{v\}_{I}(T))=\Bigg|\det\left(\frac{\partial f^{i}_{a}}{\partial v^{j}_{b}}\right)\Bigg|\prod_{i,a}\delta(f^{i}_{a})\,.

(2.17)

In the above, the determinant is with respect to the multi-index defined by the pair
(i,a)
, and we have used the transformation identity for multi-dimensional delta functions to shift zeros from solution vectors to the
f^{i}_{a}
’s. Also, it is worth noting that this density is not normalised as a probability density but integrates to the number of eigentuples solving the problem. We can now obtain the tensor-averaged distribution of eigentuples by averaging over equation (
2.17
) with (
2.14
) as:

\rho(\{v\})``:="\Bigg\langle\Bigg|\det\left(\frac{\partial f^{i}_{a}}{\partial v^{j}_{b}}\right)\Bigg|\;\prod_{i,a}\delta(f^{i}_{a})\Bigg\rangle_{T}\,.

(2.18)

We call this expression the
genuine
distribution of eigentuples. We now introduce a modification which is easier for analysis using the partition function approach of Section
3
, by removing the absolute value from the determinant:

\widetilde{\rho}(\{v\})``:="\Bigg\langle\det\left(\frac{\partial f^{i}_{a}}{\partial v^{j}_{b}}\right)\prod_{i,a}\delta(f^{i}_{a})\Bigg\rangle_{T}\,.

(2.19)

We will call the latter the
signed
distribution of eigenvectors. While it is not the proper distribution we seek, we can still extract from it important quantities of the genuine case, as discussed in
[
46
]
, most importantly the location of its “edge” representing the largest eigenvalue. It also leads to exact computations at finite
N
and finite
p
. In Section
3.2
, we will see that in reality, not all
f^{i}_{a}
are truly independent and the above distributions are not well-defined (this is the reason for the double-quotes in our definitions). Both distributions will require a finite-
N
modification introducing a gauge-fixing, the subject of Section
3.3
.

3
The Signed Partition Function

3.1
Introducing the Fields

Having expressed the tensor-averaged genuine and signed distributions of eigenvectors
\rho(v)
and
\widetilde{\rho}(v)
in terms of determinants and delta-functions of the
f^{i}_{a}
, we can adopt the quantum-field-theoretic perspective
[
43
,
44
,
45
,
46
,
47
,
48
,
49
]
on the problem by rewriting the distributions in terms of integrals over bosonic and fermionic fields. Our goal here is to obtain a partition function with an associated action that we can manipulate. For the moment, we only consider the signed distribution
\widetilde{\rho}(\{v\})
of (
2.19
). To introduce the integrals, we rewrite the multi-index determinant in terms of Grassmann anticommuting fermionic variables
\bar{\psi}^{i}_{a},\psi^{i}_{a}
and we rewrite the delta functions in terms of bosonic real (Lagrange multiplier) variables
\lambda^{i}_{a}
(see (
A.1
) and (
A.3
) in Appendix
A
for the conventions used), where again we have
i\in\{1,\dots,p\},a\in\{1,\dots,N\}
. A final outer integral is given by the tensor averaging of (
2.14
). The expression that we obtain is:

\widetilde{\rho}(\{v\})=\int\mathcal{D}T\,\mathcal{D}\bar{\psi}\,\mathcal{D}\psi\,\mathcal{D}\lambda\,e^{\mathcal{S}}\,,

(3.1)

where we define and interpret the exponent as the following action:

\mathcal{S}:=-\alpha T^{2}+\bar{\psi}^{i}\cdot\frac{\partial f^{i}}{\partial v^{j}}\cdot\psi^{j}+\mathbf{i}\lambda^{i}\cdot f^{i}\,,

(3.2)

and
\mathbf{i}
denotes the imaginary unit.
In the above we have represented contractions in the
a_{1},\dots,a_{p}
index space as
\lambda^{i}\cdot f^{i}=\lambda^{i}_{a}f^{i}_{a}
or by squaring the tensor over all indices. Having an explicit expression for
f^{i}_{a}
in (
2.15
), we can compute the Jacobian derivative that appears in the above (
3.2
):

\frac{\partial f^{i}_{a}}{\partial v^{j}_{b}}=\delta^{ij}\delta_{ab}-\frac{1}{(p-2)!}\epsilon^{iji_{3}\dots i_{p}}T_{aba_{3}\dots a_{p}}v^{i_{3}}_{a_{3}}\dots v^{i_{p}}_{a_{p}}\,.

(3.3)

Thus, we can expand the partition function action for the signed distribution of eigentuples in the following explicit form:

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}=

\displaystyle-\alpha T_{a_{1}\dots a_{p}}T_{a_{1}\dots a_{p}}+\bar{\psi}^{i}_{a}\psi^{i}_{a}+\mathbf{i}\lambda^{i}_{a}v^{i}_{a}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{(p-2)!}\epsilon^{iji_{3}\dots i_{p}}T_{aba_{3}\dots a_{p}}\bar{\psi}^{i}_{a}\psi^{j}_{b}v^{i_{3}}_{a_{3}}\dots v^{i_{p}}_{a_{p}}-\frac{\mathbf{i}}{(p-1)!}\epsilon^{ii_{2}\dots i_{p}}T_{aa_{2}\dots a_{p}}\lambda^{i}_{a}v^{i_{2}}_{a_{2}}\dots v^{i_{p}}_{a_{p}}\,.

(3.4)

We will refer to separate parts of this action and the integrals over their components in the partition function as sectors.

3.2
Gauge Freedom

The formulation (
2.12
) of the eigenvalue equation has some gauge freedom, given by
\text{SO}(p)
transformations of the eigenvector tuple in the flavour index space. To see this, consider a transformation in the matrix
\text{GL}(p,\mathbb{R})
representation of the group, transforming the vectors as:

v^{i}_{a}\rightarrow M^{ii^{\prime}}v^{i^{\prime}}_{a},\quad M\in\text{SO}(p)\,.

(3.5)

Then, the left-hand side of equation (
2.12
) undergoes the transformation:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(p-1)!}\epsilon^{ii_{2}\dots i_{p}}

\displaystyle T_{aa_{2}\dots a_{p}}v^{i_{2}}_{a_{2}}\dots v^{i_{p}}_{a_{p}}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\frac{1}{(p-1)!}\epsilon^{ii_{2}\dots i_{p}}M^{i_{2}i^{\prime}_{2}}\dots M^{i_{p}i^{\prime}_{p}}T_{aa_{2}\dots a_{p}}v^{i^{\prime}_{2}}_{a_{2}}\dots v^{i^{\prime}_{p}}_{a_{p}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(p-1)!}\epsilon^{i^{\prime}i^{\prime}_{2}\dots i^{\prime}_{p}}(M^{-1})^{i^{\prime}i}\underbrace{\det(M)}_{=1}T_{aa_{2}\dots a_{p}}v^{i^{\prime}_{2}}_{a_{2}}\dots v^{i^{\prime}_{p}}_{a_{p}}

\displaystyle=M^{ii^{\prime}}\Big(\frac{1}{(p-1)!}\epsilon^{i^{\prime}i^{\prime}_{2}\dots i^{\prime}_{p}}T_{aa_{2}\dots a_{p}}v^{i^{\prime}_{2}}_{a_{2}}\dots v^{i^{\prime}_{p}}_{a_{p}}\Big)\,.

(3.6)

Therefore, the eigenvalue equation (
2.12
) is covariant with respect to the transformation
M
and the distributions of (
2.18
), (
2.19
) are in reality not well-defined. By considering the generators of the Lie algebra of
\text{SO}(p)
, that is
p\times p
antisymmetric matrices, and picking a basis for this space, we see that this gauge freedom is
\frac{1}{2}p(p-1)
-dimensional. Therefore, we can fix a gauge by specifying as many conditions on the vectors
v^{i}_{a}
, requiring that these can always be achieved by means of
\text{SO}(p)
transformations in the flavour index space. Hinting at having one gauge choice for each antisymmetric generator of the symmetry group, we define and write these general gauge-fixing variables as:

G^{i,i^{\prime}}\overset{!}{=}0,\quad i<i^{\prime}\,.

(3.7)

We also introduce the similar variables:

G^{ii^{\prime}}:=\begin{cases}G^{i,i^{\prime}},&i<i^{\prime}\\
0,&i\geq i^{\prime}\end{cases}\,,

(3.8)

such that we allow both indices
i,i^{\prime}
to run unrestricted between
1,\dots,p
for notational convenience.

Now, we wish to choose the
G^{i,i^{\prime}}
explicitly. A gauge choice that fixes the continuous
\text{SO}(p)
symmetry which we are always able to make (and will justify below) is the following:

v^{i_{1}}_{1}\overset{!}{=}0,\text{ for }i_{1}>1,\quad v^{i_{2}}_{2}\overset{!}{=}0,\text{ for }i_{2}>2,\quad\dots\quad v^{p}_{p-1}\overset{!}{=}0\,.

(3.9)

This indeed fixes
\frac{1}{2}p(p-1)
continuous parameters.
However, the gauge conditions (
2.12
) do not completely fix the SO(
p
) symmetry: one can also see that SO(
p
) transformation can flip
the signs of any of the
v^{i}_{i}\,(i=1,2,\cdots,p-1
).
Therefore, to remove this discrete gauge symmetry, we choose to focus on the sector of solutions such that:

v^{i}_{i}>0,\quad\text{for }i\in\{1,\dots,p-1\}\,.

(3.10)

The gauge conditions (
3.9
) and (
3.10
) above can be justified as follows. First, consider the flavour-space “vector”
v^{i}_{1}
, indexed by
i\in\{1,\dots,p\}
. Through the means of an
\text{SO}(p)
transformation as in (
3.5
), we can freely choose to align this vector to the first “axis” in its components. Specifically, we can ask that:

v^{2}_{1}\overset{!}{=}0\,\quad\dots\quad v^{p}_{1}\overset{!}{=}0\,.

(3.11)

Nevertheless,
v^{i}_{1}
can now either be aligned to the positive or negative first axis, so we additionally
apply an SO(
p
) transformation so that:

v^{1}_{1}>0\,.

(3.12)

With this alignment to be preserved, we now have a residual and available
\text{SO}(p-1)
transformation to fix. So, we consider the second vector
v^{i}_{2}
, in its residual
i\geq 2
components and ask it, in an exactly analogous way, to satisfy:

v^{3}_{2}\overset{!}{=}0\,,\quad\dots\quad v^{p}_{2}\overset{!}{=}0\,,\quad v^{2}_{2}>0\,.

(3.13)

This further pushes us down to an
\text{SO}(p-2)
symmetry. We can continue in this manner until we fix
the
\text{SO}(2)
symmetry.

Therefore, to conclude, the gauge-fixing variables that we choose to set to zero as in (
3.7
) are:

G^{i,i^{\prime}}:=\beta v^{i^{\prime}}_{i}\ (i<i^{\prime}),

(3.14)

where we have introduced an arbitrary fixed real parameter
\beta>0
in such a way that there is no effect on how the vector components are fixed (i.e.
G^{i,i^{\prime}}=0\iff v^{i^{\prime}}_{i}=0
). This parameter is convenient to probe what happens to certain sectors of the action as
\beta\to 0
, where we recover non-gauge-fixed results. Since the parameter is arbitrary, we expect it to drop out from all meaningful quantities of our problem. Furthermore, we will take condition (
3.10
) to implicitly restrict the sector of our distribution that we examine (see end of following Section
3.3
).

3.3
Gauge Fixing Procedure

The gauge-fixing procedure that we describe here is independent of the choice of gauge that we pick to study our problem. Therefore, we will explain it taking the
G^{i,i^{\prime}}
of (
3.7
) as general, without restricting to a specific gauge choice like (
3.14
). First, consider the following contraction:

\displaystyle f^{i}_{a}v^{i^{\prime}}_{a}

\displaystyle=\Big(v^{i}_{a}-\frac{1}{(p-1)!}\epsilon^{ii_{2}\dots i_{p}}T_{aa_{2}\dots a_{p}}v^{i_{2}}_{a_{2}}\dots v^{i_{p}}_{a_{p}}\Big)v^{i^{\prime}}_{a}

\displaystyle=v^{i}_{a}v^{i^{\prime}}_{a}-\frac{1}{(p-1)!}\epsilon^{ii_{2}\dots i_{p}}\epsilon^{i^{\prime}i_{2}\dots i_{p}}T_{aa_{2}\dots a_{p}}v^{1}_{a}v^{2}_{a_{p}}\dots v^{p}_{a_{p}}

\displaystyle=v^{i}_{a}v^{i^{\prime}}_{a}-\delta^{ii^{\prime}}T_{a_{1}\dots a_{p}}v^{1}_{a_{1}}\dots v^{p}_{a_{p}}\,.

(3.15)

In particular, for
i\neq i^{\prime}
, we have that:

f^{i}_{a}v^{i^{\prime}}_{a}=v^{i}_{a}v^{i^{\prime}}_{a}\,.

(3.16)

For any choice of
i
and
i^{\prime}
, we have the following result, that expresses the redundancy in the non-gauge fixed system:

f^{i}_{a}v^{i^{\prime}}_{a}-f^{i^{\prime}}_{a}v^{i}_{a}=0,\quad\forall i,i^{\prime}\in\{1,\dots,p\}\,.

(3.17)

We use the above result to motivate the following modification to the system we are solving for, in order to incorporate the fixing conditions. Let
G^{i,i^{\prime}}
be arbitrary but attainable conditions to be satisfied and define the following:

\displaystyle\widetilde{f}^{i}_{a}

\displaystyle:=f^{i}_{a}+\sum_{i^{\prime}>i}v^{i^{\prime}}_{a}G^{i,i^{\prime}}=f^{i}_{a}+v^{i^{\prime}}_{a}G^{ii^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=v^{i}_{a}-\frac{1}{(p-1)!}\epsilon^{ii_{2}\dots i_{p}}T_{aa_{2}\dots a_{p}}v^{i_{2}}_{a_{2}}\dots v^{i_{p}}_{a_{p}}+\sum_{i^{\prime}>i}v^{i^{\prime}}_{a}G^{i,i^{\prime}}\,.

(3.18)

In order to show that this modification of the
f^{i}_{a}
variables is a valid gauge-fixing procedure, we need to show both that
\tilde{f}^{i}_{a}=0
implies
G^{i,i^{\prime}}=0
and therefore that
\tilde{f}^{i}_{a}=0
also implies
f^{i}_{a}=0
. The way we do this is by first showing that the vectors
v^{i}_{a}
of different flavour that solve
\tilde{f}^{i}_{a}=0
, are all orthogonal. Then we show that this orthogonality also implies that all such vectors have the same norm. Finally, we show that this fixes all gauge-fixing conditions to be satisfied. We proceed.

To show orthogonality, we examine the contractions between the
\widetilde{f}
’s and the
v
’s. Assume
\widetilde{f}^{i}_{a}=0
and take
i<p
in the following:

\displaystyle 0=\widetilde{f}^{p}_{a}v^{i}_{a}=(f^{p}_{a}+\underbrace{\sum_{i^{\prime}>p}}_{=0}v^{i^{\prime}}_{a}G^{p,i^{\prime}})v^{i}_{a}=f^{p}_{a}v^{i}_{a}=v^{p}_{a}v^{i}_{a}

(3.19)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow v^{p}\cdot v^{i}=0\quad\forall i<p\,,

(3.20)

where we have used (
3.16
). Therefore, the vector
v^{p}
is shown to be orthogonal to all other vectors. Using this fact, we can show the same for
v^{p-1}
, now taking
i<{p-1}
:

\displaystyle 0=\widetilde{f}^{p-1}_{a}v^{i}_{a}=(f^{p-1}_{a}+\sum_{i^{\prime}>p-1}v^{i^{\prime}}_{a}G^{i,i^{\prime}})v^{i}_{a}=\underbrace{f^{p-1}_{a}v^{i}_{a}}_{v^{p-1}\cdot v^{i}}+\underbrace{v^{p}_{a}v^{i}_{a}}_{=0}G^{p-1,p}=v^{p-1}_{a}v^{i}_{a}

(3.21)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow v^{p-1}\cdot v^{i}=0\quad\forall i<p-1\,,

(3.22)

where we used (
3.20
). Therefore, we have
v^{p-1}\perp v^{i},i\neq p-1
. Repeating the procedure done on
v^{p}
and
v^{p-1}
iteratively for lower
i
and using the orthogonality derived from higher
i
in the gauge-fixing sum condition over
i^{\prime}>i
, we obtain that all vectors in the same eigentuple of the gauge-fixed system are orthogonal to each other:

v^{i}\cdot v^{j}=0,i\neq j\,.

(3.23)

Now, we perform the same kinds of contractions diagonally, for
i=i^{\prime}
, to show that, again, all norms are equal. In the following, the repetition of the
i
index is
not
contracted over. We assume that
\tilde{f}^{i}_{a}=0
is already fixed, then for arbitrary
i
:

\displaystyle 0=\underbrace{\widetilde{f}^{i}_{a}v^{i}_{a}}_{\text{fixed }i}

\displaystyle=f^{i}_{a}v^{i}_{a}+\sum_{i^{\prime}>i}v^{i^{\prime}}_{a}v^{i}_{a}G^{i,i^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=v^{i}_{a}v^{i}_{a}-\delta^{ii}T_{a_{1}\dots a_{p}}v^{1}_{a_{1}}\dots v^{p}_{a_{p}}+\sum_{i^{\prime}>i}\underbrace{v^{i^{\prime}}_{a}v^{i}_{a}}_{=0}G^{i,i^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=|v^{i}|^{2}-T_{a_{1}\dots a_{p}}v^{1}_{a_{1}}\dots v^{p}_{a_{p}}\,.

(3.24)

As this holds for any choice of
i
:

\displaystyle T_{a_{1}\dots a_{p}}v^{1}_{a_{1}}\dots v^{p}_{a_{p}}

\displaystyle=|v^{1}|^{2}=|v^{2}|^{2}=\dots=|v^{p}|^{2}

\displaystyle=:\nu^{2},\quad\nu>0\,.

(3.25)

Hence, by combining equations (
3.23
) and (
3.3
) we have shown that the eigenvector contractions in the
a_{1},\dots,a_{p}
indices can be expressed as:

v^{i}_{a}v^{j}_{a}=\nu^{2}\delta^{ij}\,.

(3.26)

Finally, we are in a position to use (
3.17
) and (
3.26
) to show that by imposing
\tilde{f}^{i}_{a}=0
, we obtain that necessarily
G^{i,i^{\prime}}=0
. We start by taking
i<i^{\prime}
without loss of generality, for which we have that
\tilde{f}^{i}_{a}=0
and
\tilde{f}^{i^{\prime}}_{a}=0
, then:

\displaystyle 0=\widetilde{f}^{i}_{a}v^{i^{\prime}}_{a}-\widetilde{f}^{i^{\prime}}_{a}v^{i}_{a}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{f^{i}_{a}v^{i^{\prime}}_{a}-f^{i^{\prime}}_{a}v^{i}_{a}}_{=0}+\sum_{i^{\prime\prime}>i}\underbrace{v^{i^{\prime\prime}}_{a}v^{i^{\prime}}_{a}}_{=\nu^{2}\delta^{i^{\prime\prime}i^{\prime}}}G^{i,i^{\prime\prime}}-\sum_{i^{\prime\prime}>i^{\prime}}\underbrace{v^{i^{\prime\prime}}_{a}v^{i}_{a}}_{=0\text{ as }i<i^{\prime}<i^{\prime\prime}}G^{i^{\prime},i^{\prime\prime}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i^{\prime\prime}>i}\nu^{2}\delta^{i^{\prime\prime}i^{\prime}}G^{i,i^{\prime\prime}}

\displaystyle=\nu^{2}G^{i,i^{\prime}}\Rightarrow G^{i,i^{\prime}}=0\,.

(3.27)

Trivially, inserting all
G^{i,i^{\prime}}=0
into equation (
3.3
) also shows that
f^{i}_{a}=0
. Therefore, we see that the gauge is fixed successfully.

With the gauge-fixed system of equations, we must modify the signed distribution (
2.19
) to contain the additional terms studied above. We perform the following replacements:

\displaystyle\prod_{i,a}\delta(f^{i}_{a})\quad

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\quad\prod_{i,a}\delta(\widetilde{f}^{i}_{a})

(3.28)

\displaystyle\Bigg|\det\left(\frac{\partial f^{i}_{a}}{\partial v^{j}_{b}}\right)\Bigg|\quad

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\quad\Bigg|\det\left(\frac{\partial\widetilde{f}^{i}_{a}}{\partial v^{j}_{b}}\right)\Bigg|\,.

(3.29)

For complete correctness, there should be a further modification imposing (
3.10
). This could be done by adding Heaviside functions to the distribution, but as the effect is just of reducing to one sector in its argument we will make this restriction choice implicit. Hence, the gauge-fixed signed density is reintroduced as a modification of (
2.19
) using the equations (
3.28
), (
3.29
) above:

\widetilde{\rho}(\{v\}):=\Bigg\langle\det\left(\frac{\partial\widetilde{f}^{i}_{a}}{\partial v^{j}_{b}}\right)\prod_{i,a}\delta(\widetilde{f}^{i}_{a})\Bigg\rangle_{T}\,.

(3.30)

A further observation to be made is that, given the
\delta
-function sector, we are able to use any result derived starting from the assumption
\widetilde{f}^{i}_{a}=0
inside the determinant sector. In particular, we can take
f^{i}_{a}=0
and
G^{i.i^{\prime}}=0
, from our reasoning above (the explicit justification in this case is covered in Appendix
B
regardless). With the modification to (
3.30
), our gauge-fixed signed distribution requires a recomputation of the derivatives that appear in the fermionic determinant term:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\widetilde{f}^{i}_{a}}{\partial v^{j}_{b}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial f^{i}_{a}}{\partial v^{j}_{b}}+\frac{\partial}{\partial v^{j}_{b}}\left(v^{i^{\prime}}_{a}G^{ii^{\prime}}\right)=\frac{\partial f^{i}_{a}}{\partial v^{j}_{b}}+\delta^{i^{\prime}j}\delta_{ab}\underbrace{G^{ii^{\prime}}}_{=0}+v^{i^{\prime}}_{a}\frac{\partial G^{ii^{\prime}}}{\partial v^{j}_{b}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial f^{i}_{a}}{\partial v^{j}_{b}}+v^{i^{\prime}}_{a}\frac{\partial G^{ii^{\prime}}}{\partial v^{j}_{b}}\,,

(3.31)

where we take
G^{ii^{\prime}}=0
from the
\delta
-function sector. Using this simplification, we obtain the expression for the gauge-fixed signed distribution expressed as a partition function from (
3.30
), in exactly the same way as for the ill-defined (
3.1
):

\displaystyle\widetilde{\rho}(\{v\})

\displaystyle=\int\mathcal{D}T\,\mathcal{D}\bar{\psi}\,\mathcal{D}\psi\,\mathcal{D}\lambda\,\exp\Big(-\alpha T^2 + \bar{\psi}^{i} \cdot\frac{\partial\widetilde{f}^{i}}{\partial v^{j}} \cdot\psi^{j} + \mathbf{i} \lambda^{i} \cdot\widetilde{f}^{i}\Big)

\displaystyle=:\int\mathcal{D}T\,\mathcal{D}\bar{\psi}\,\mathcal{D}\psi\,\mathcal{D}\lambda\,e^{\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}}\,,

(3.32)

where we can write the gauge-fixed action
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}
by defining the gauge-fixing contributions:

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{G,\psi}:=\bar{\psi}^{i}_{a}v^{i^{\prime}}_{a}\frac{\partial G^{ii^{\prime}}}{\partial v^{j}_{b}}\psi^{j}_{b},\quad\mathcal{S}_{G,\lambda}:=\mathbf{i}\lambda^{i}_{a}v^{i^{\prime}}_{a}G^{ii^{\prime}}

(3.33)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{G}:=\mathcal{S}_{G,\psi}+\mathcal{S}_{G,\lambda}\,,

(3.34)

and summing them to the original action
\mathcal{S}
of (
3.2
):

\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}:=\mathcal{S}+\mathcal{S}_{G}\,.

(3.35)

4

In multiple parts of the partition function action, we have direct index contractions between the variables of integration
\bar{\psi},\psi,\lambda
and the vectors in the eigentuple
v^{i}
. It turns out that important simplifications to the calculations can be made if variables are split between parts that live in the subspace spanned by these vectors and parts in its orthogonal complement. We start by the following simplifying definition of a metric/Gramian-like matrix
2
2

2

We aim to use the vectors in the eigentuple as a basis, therefore there is a correspondence between the metric on this space and the Gramian of the vectors.
in the eigenvector tuple subspace:

g^{ij}:=v^{i}\cdot v^{j}\,.

(4.1)

Then, we define the following parallel and transverse projectors:

\displaystyle I^{\parallel}_{ab}

\displaystyle=v^{i}_{a}(g^{-1})^{ij}v^{j}_{b}

\displaystyle I^{\perp}_{ab}

\displaystyle=\delta_{ab}-I^{\parallel}_{ab}\,.

(4.3)

By using the definition in (

(I^{\parallel})^{2}=I^{\parallel}
,
(I^{\perp})^{2}=I^{\perp}

I^{\parallel}I^{\perp}=0

\delta

as a consequence of gauge-fixing terms (in (

)), we do not rely on this diagonal form of

(5.42)

So, we can perform the following manipulations:

\displaystyle\bar{\psi}^{ij}\widetilde{G}^{ij\ i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}\psi^{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\bar{\psi}^{(ij)}\widetilde{G}^{(ij)\ i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}\psi^{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}+\bar{\psi}^{[ij]}\widetilde{G}^{[ij]\ i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}\psi^{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\bar{\psi}^{(ii)}\widetilde{G}^{(ii)\ i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}\psi^{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}+\sum_{i\neq j}\bar{\psi}^{(ij)}\widetilde{G}^{(ij)\ i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}\psi^{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}+\sum_{i\neq j}\bar{\psi}^{[ij]}\widetilde{G}^{[ij]\ i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}\psi^{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{i<j}\bar{\psi}^{(ij)}\widetilde{G}^{(ij)\ i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}\psi^{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}+2\sum_{i<j}\bar{\psi}^{[ij]}\widetilde{G}^{[ij]\ i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}\psi^{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\rightarrow 2\sum_{i<j}\bar{\psi}^{[ij]}\widetilde{G}^{[ij]i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}\psi^{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\bar{\psi}^{[ij]}\widetilde{G}^{[ij](i^{\prime}j^{\prime})}\psi^{(i^{\prime}j^{\prime})}+\bar{\psi}^{[ij]}\widetilde{G}^{[ij][i^{\prime}j^{\prime}]}\psi^{[i^{\prime}j^{\prime}]}\,,

(5.43)

where (in the second-to-last step) we justify absorbing the symmetric
\bar{\psi}^{(ij)}
terms by an appropriate linear shift of the integration measure on the antisymmetric
\bar{\psi}^{[ij]}
components, which are independent of the
\psi^{[ij]}
, as well as (
5.42
). Now, with these manipulations, we see that:

\displaystyle\bar{\psi}^{ij}\widetilde{M}^{ij\ i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}\psi^{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\nu^{2}(2\bar{\psi}^{(ij)}\psi^{(ij)}-\bar{\psi}^{(ii)}\psi^{(jj)})

\displaystyle\quad+\nu^{2}\bar{\psi}^{[ij]}\widetilde{G}^{[ij](i^{\prime}j^{\prime})}\psi^{(i^{\prime}j^{\prime})}+\nu^{2}\bar{\psi}^{[ij]}\widetilde{G}^{[ij][i^{\prime}j^{\prime}]}\psi^{[i^{\prime}j^{\prime}]}

\displaystyle=\nu^{2}(2\bar{\psi}^{(ij)}\psi^{(ij)}-\bar{\psi}^{(ii)}\psi^{(jj)})+\nu^{2}\bar{\psi}^{[ij]}\widetilde{G}^{[ij][i^{\prime}j^{\prime}]}\psi^{[i^{\prime}j^{\prime}]}+\underbrace{\dots}_{\text{irrelevant}}\,,

(5.44)

where we have separated out the symmetric-antisymmetric term that is irrelevant in the computation of the determinant of
\widetilde{M}
. To perform the integration, we transform the fermion measures to integrate over these symmetric-antisymmetric components:

\mathcal{D}[\bar{\psi}^{\parallel}]\,\mathcal{D}[\psi^{\parallel}]=2^{-p(p-1)}\mathcal{D}[\bar{\psi}^{(\parallel)}]\,\mathcal{D}[\bar{\psi}^{[\parallel]}]\,\mathcal{D}[\psi^{(\parallel)}]\,\mathcal{D}[\psi^{[\parallel]}]\,.

(5.45)

With this, we can rewrite (
5.44
) as:

\displaystyle\bar{\psi}^{ij}\widetilde{M}^{ij\ i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}\psi^{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=4\nu^{2}\sum_{i<j}\bar{\psi}^{(ij)}\psi^{(ij)}+\nu^{2}\left(2\sum_{i}\bar{\psi}^{(ii)}\psi^{(ii)}-\sum_{i,j}\bar{\psi}^{(ii)}\psi^{(jj)}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+4\nu^{2}\sum_{i<j}\sum_{i^{\prime}<j^{\prime}}\bar{\psi}^{[ij]}\tilde{G}^{[ij][i^{\prime}j^{\prime}]}\psi^{[i^{\prime}j^{\prime}]}+\dots

\displaystyle=:\sum_{i<j}\sum_{i^{\prime}<j^{\prime}}\bar{\psi}^{(ij)}\widetilde{M}^{ij\ i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}_{S}\psi^{(i^{\prime}j^{\prime})}+\sum_{i}\sum_{j}\bar{\psi}^{(ii)}\widetilde{M}^{ij}_{D}\psi^{(jj)}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i<j}\sum_{i^{\prime}<j^{\prime}}\bar{\psi}^{[ij]}\widetilde{M}^{ij\ i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}_{A}\psi^{[i^{\prime}j^{\prime}]}+\dots\,.

(5.46)

Therefore, in order to compute equation (
5.37
) using (
5.45
) and (
5.46
), we have that:

\det\widetilde{M}=2^{-p(p-1)}\det\widetilde{M}_{S}\,\det\widetilde{M}_{D}\,\det\widetilde{M}_{A}\,,

(5.47)

where all multi-index determinants are computed along
i<j
,
i^{\prime}<j^{\prime}
. The first determinant is simply diagonal:

\det\widetilde{M}_{S}=(4\nu^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}p(p-1)}\,.

(5.48)

The second determinant can be computed, for example by computing eigenvalues and respective multiplicities, to be:

\det\widetilde{M}_{D}=(\nu^{2})^{p}\big(2-p\big)2^{p-1}\,.

(5.49)

For the third determinant, we have:

\displaystyle\det\widetilde{M}_{A}

\displaystyle=(\nu^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}p(p-1)}\det_{i<j,i^{\prime}<j^{\prime}}(4\widetilde{G}^{[ij][i^{\prime}j^{\prime}]})

\displaystyle=(\nu^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}p(p-1)}\det_{i<j,i^{\prime}<j^{\prime}}\Big(\widetilde{G}^{ij\ i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}-\widetilde{G}^{ij\ j^{\prime}i^{\prime}}\Big)\,.

(5.50)

Therefore, using the results in (
5.47
), (
5.49
) and (
5.50
), we have that:

\displaystyle\det\widetilde{M}

\displaystyle=2^{-p(p-1)}(4\nu^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}p(p-1)}(\nu^{2})^{p}(2-p)2^{p-1}(\nu^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}p(p-1)}\Delta

\displaystyle=-(p-2)2^{p-1}(\nu^{2})^{p^{2}}\Delta\,,

(5.51)

where we define:

\Delta:=\det_{i<j,i^{\prime}<j^{\prime}}\Big(\widetilde{G}^{ij\ i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}-\widetilde{G}^{ij\ j^{\prime}i^{\prime}}\Big)\,.

(5.52)

Therefore:

\Rightarrow\int\mathcal{D}\bar{\psi}^{\parallel}\,\mathcal{D}\psi^{\parallel}\,e^{\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}^{\;\setminus\{T,\lambda\}}_{\psi^{\parallel}}}=-(p-2)2^{p-1}\Delta\,.

(5.53)

If we use the specific gauge-fixing condition of (
3.14
), we see that
\Delta\propto\beta^{\frac{1}{2}p(p-1)}
. Therefore, we briefly comment that without gauge-fixing (i.e.
\beta=0
) our determinant integral would be equal to zero. Now, we define
\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}^{\;\setminus\{T,\lambda,\psi^{\parallel}\}}
to incorporate parallel sector integrations for the
\bar{\psi},\psi
-fields such that:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\rho}(\{v\})

\displaystyle=\int\mathcal{D}\bar{\psi}^{\perp}\,\mathcal{D}\psi^{\perp}\,e^{\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}^{\;\setminus\{T,\lambda\}}-\tilde{\mathcal{S}}^{\setminus\{T,\lambda\}}_{\psi^{\parallel}}}\int\mathcal{D}\bar{\psi}^{\parallel}\,\mathcal{D}\psi^{\parallel}\,e^{\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}^{\;\setminus\{T,\lambda\}}_{\psi^{\parallel}}}

\displaystyle=:\int\mathcal{D}\bar{\psi}^{\perp}\,\mathcal{D}\psi^{\perp}\,e^{\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}^{\;\setminus\{T,\lambda,\psi^{\parallel}\}}}\,.

(5.54)

Therefore, the last remaining integration to compute to obtain the full partition function is the integral:

\widetilde{\rho}(\{v\})=\underbrace{e^{\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}^{\;\setminus\{T,\lambda,\psi^{\parallel}\}}-\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}^{\;\setminus\{T,\lambda,\psi^{\parallel}\}}_{\psi^{\perp}}}}_{\text{all const. w.r.t. }T,\lambda,\psi}\int\mathcal{D}\bar{\psi}^{\perp}\,\mathcal{D}\psi^{\perp}\,e^{\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}^{\;\setminus\{T,\lambda,\psi^{\parallel}\}}_{\psi^{\perp}}}\,.

(5.55)

For
\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}^{\;\setminus\{T,\lambda,\psi^{\parallel}\}}_{\psi^{\perp}}
, we combine the remainder of the
\psi
-dependent part of (
5.1
) after parallel-sector integration with equation (
5.8
), that gives the quartic terms after
T
-integration, and use (
5.23
) to contract
\epsilon
-symbols to obtain:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}^{\;\setminus\{T,\lambda,\psi^{\parallel}\}}_{\psi^{\perp}}=\bar{\psi}^{\perp i}\cdot\psi^{\perp i}+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\alpha p!(p-2)!}\epsilon^{i_{1}i_{2}i_{3}\dots i_{p}}\epsilon^{i^{\prime}_{1}i^{\prime}_{2}i^{\prime}_{3}\dots i^{\prime}_{p}}\big(-(\bar{\psi}^{\perp i_{1}}\cdot\bar{\psi}^{\perp i^{\prime}_{1}})(\psi^{\perp i_{2}}\cdot\psi^{\perp i^{\prime}_{2}})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad+(\bar{\psi}^{\perp i_{1}}\cdot\psi^{\perp i^{\prime}_{2}})(\bar{\psi}^{\perp i^{\prime}_{1}}\cdot\psi^{\perp i_{2}})\big)g^{i_{3}i^{\prime}_{3}}\dots g^{i_{p}i^{\prime}_{p}}

\displaystyle=\bar{\psi}^{\perp i}\cdot\psi^{\perp i}+

\displaystyle\frac{(\nu^{2})^{p-2}}{4\alpha p!}\delta^{i_{1}i_{2}}_{i^{\prime}_{1}i^{\prime}_{2}}\big(-(\bar{\psi}^{\perp i_{1}}\cdot\bar{\psi}^{\perp i^{\prime}_{1}})(\psi^{\perp i_{2}}\cdot\psi^{\perp i^{\prime}_{2}})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad+(\bar{\psi}^{\perp i_{1}}\cdot\psi^{\perp i^{\prime}_{2}})(\bar{\psi}^{\perp i^{\prime}_{1}}\cdot\psi^{\perp i_{2}})\big)

\displaystyle=\bar{\psi}^{\perp i}\cdot\psi^{\perp i}-

\displaystyle\frac{(\nu^{2})^{p-2}}{4\alpha p!}\big((\bar{\psi}^{\perp i}\cdot\bar{\psi}^{\perp j})(\psi^{\perp i}\cdot\psi^{\perp j})+(\bar{\psi}^{\perp i}\cdot\psi^{\perp i})(\bar{\psi}^{\perp j}\cdot\psi^{\perp j})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad-(\bar{\psi}^{\perp i}\cdot\psi^{\perp j})(\bar{\psi}^{\perp i}\cdot\psi^{\perp j})\big)\,.

(5.56)

In the above, we have used anticommutativity of the Grassmann variables to eliminate the term including
\bar{\psi}^{\perp i}\cdot\bar{\psi}^{\perp i}=\bar{\psi}^{\perp i}_{a}\bar{\psi}^{\perp i}_{a}=-\bar{\psi}^{\perp i}_{a}\bar{\psi}^{\perp i}_{a}=0
and similarly
\psi^{\perp i}\cdot\psi^{\perp i}=0
. The computation of the final partition function integral over this action
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}^{\setminus\{T,\lambda,\psi^{\parallel}\}}_{\psi^{\perp}}
requires using a different specific method, so is covered in isolation in the following Section
5.4
.

5.4
Four-Fermi Theory Evaluation

Equation (
5.56
) shows that we obtain a four-fermion theory in the transverse sector of the
\psi
fields after integrating out the tensor. This means that the final remaining integral of the partition function that we have to compute is:

\mathcal{Z}_{\perp}:=\int\mathcal{D}\bar{\psi}^{\perp}\,\mathcal{D}\psi^{\perp}\,e^{\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}^{\;\setminus\{T,\lambda,\psi^{\parallel}\}}_{\psi^{\perp}}}\,.

(5.57)

Once this integral is computed, we will have the final form of the signed distribution given schematically by the fully integrated action
\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}^{\setminus\{T,\lambda,\psi\}}
:

\widetilde{\rho}(\{v\})=e^{\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}^{\;\setminus\{T,\lambda,\psi^{\parallel}\}}-\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}^{\;\setminus\{T,\lambda,\psi^{\parallel}\}}_{\psi^{\perp}}}\mathcal{Z}_{\perp}=:e^{\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}^{\;\setminus\{T,\lambda,\psi\}}}\,.

(5.58)

In this section, we discuss how the integration can be evaluated, in principle in fully closed form when
N
is finite. Define the following quadratic fermion action and associated partition function:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}_{\perp,2}:=\bar{k}_{ij}(\bar{\psi}^{\perp i}\cdot\bar{\psi}^{\perp j})+k_{ij}(\psi^{\perp i}\cdot\psi^{\perp j})+\widetilde{k}_{ij}(\bar{\psi}^{\perp i}\cdot\psi^{\perp j})

(5.59)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{\perp,2}:=\int\mathcal{D}\bar{\psi}^{\perp}\,\mathcal{D}\psi^{\perp}\,e^{\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}_{\perp,2}}\,.

(5.60)

Here,
\bar{k},k,\tilde{k}
are taken to be arbitrary-valued variables. Define the following differential operator:

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial k^{2}}:=\sum_{ij}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\bar{k}_{ij}\partial k_{ij}}+\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\widetilde{k}_{ii}\partial\widetilde{k}_{jj}}-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\widetilde{k}_{ij}\partial\widetilde{k}_{ij}}\right)\,.

(5.61)

Now, using equation (
C
) in the appendix, we recognise that the four-fermion action can be written by exponentiating the differential operator of (
5.61
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{\perp}

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{(\nu^{2})^{p-2}}{4\alpha p!}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial k^{2}}}\mathcal{Z}_{\perp,2}\big|_{\bar{k}=k=0,\tilde{k}=\mathds{1}}

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{(\nu^{2})^{p-2}}{4\alpha p!}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial k^{2}}}\int\mathcal{D}\bar{\psi}^{\perp}\,\mathcal{D}\psi^{\perp}\,e^{\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{\perp,2}}\big|_{\bar{k}=k=0,\tilde{k}=\mathds{1}}\,.

(5.62)

In this form, the quadratic partition function can be fully evaluated. While
\bar{k},k
are not restricted, we notice that their contractions with Grassmann anticommuting in variables in (
5.59
) only keep their antisymmetric components. Hence, we can manipulate the integral to yield a Pfaffian of a block matrix we call
K
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{\perp,2}

\displaystyle=\int\mathcal{D}\bar{\psi}^{\perp}\,\mathcal{D}\psi^{\perp}\,\exp\Big(
\begin{pmatrix}
\bar{\psi}^{\perp}_{a} & \psi^{\perp}_{a}
\end{pmatrix}
\underbrace{
\begin{pmatrix}
\frac{1}{2}(\bar{k} - \bar{k}^{T}) & \frac{1}{2} \tilde{k} \\
-\frac{1}{2} \tilde{k}^{T} & \frac{1}{2}(k - k^{T})
\end{pmatrix}
}_{=: -K/2}
\begin{pmatrix}
\bar{\psi}^{\perp}_{a} \\
\psi^{\perp}_{a}
\end{pmatrix}
\Big)

\displaystyle=(\text{pf}\,K)^{N-p}\,.

(5.63)

We notice that the Pfaffian is a finite polynomial, therefore the application of the exponential differential operator to any of its finite powers terminates even prior to setting
\bar{k}=k=0,\tilde{k}=\mathds{1}
. This follows from the fact that both the quadratic action and the original four-fermi action are Grassmann integrals, and are therefore terminating series. We can express (
5.62
) through Wick contractions, for which we define the following block matrix
A
, schematically acting on multi-indexed vectors of the form
\partial/\partial k=(\partial/\partial\bar{k_{ij}},\partial/\partial k_{ij},\partial/\partial\tilde{k}_{ij})
:

e^{-\frac{(\nu^{2})^{p-2}}{4\alpha p!}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial k^{2}}}=:e^{\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial k}\cdot A\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial k}}\,.

(5.64)

Then, the transverse fermion action can be computed as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{\perp}

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{(\nu^{2})^{p-2}}{4\alpha p!}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial k^{2}}}(\text{pf}\,K[\bar{k},k,\tilde{k}])^{N-p}\Big|_{\bar{k}=k=0,\tilde{k}=\mathds{1}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\hbox to6.3pt{\vbox to6.14pt{\pgfpicture\makeatletter\hbox{\hskip 3.14888pt\lower-3.07222pt\hbox to0.0pt{\lxSVG@begingroup@{_scopebegin} \lxSVG@begingroup@{stroke} \lxSVG@begingroup@{fill} \lxSVG@setlinewidth{\the\pgflinewidth}\lxSVG@begingroup@{stroke-width} \lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\nullfont\hbox to0.0pt{\lxSVG@begingroup@{_scopebegin}

6
The Signed Distribution

6.1
Collecting the Partition Function

Finally, we are able to collect all contributions to the partition function represented schematically in equation (
5.58
) and present them as the one single expression for our signed eigenvector distribution
\widetilde{\rho}
. First, we combine equations (
5.26
), (
5.27
), (
5.53
) and (
5.57
) and we make the dependencies of
\Delta
and
\mathcal{Z}_{\perp}
explicit:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\rho}(\{v\})

\displaystyle=-\frac{(p-2)2^{p-1}\Delta(\{v\})}{(2\pi)^{p(N-p)}}\left(\frac{(4\pi\alpha p!)^{p}}{(\nu^{2})^{p(p-1)}}\right)^{\frac{N-p}{2}}\sqrt{\frac{\alpha p!}{\pi}}e^{-\frac{\alpha p!}{(\nu^{2})^{p-2}}}\ \mathcal{Z}_{\perp}(\nu^{2},N)

\displaystyle\quad\times\prod_{i<j}\delta(g^{ij})\prod_{i=2}^{p}\delta(g^{ii}-g^{i-1\,i-1})\prod_{i<j}\delta(G^{i,j})\,,

(6.1)

which, simplifying and rescaling
\delta
-functions, becomes:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\rho}(\{v\})

\displaystyle=-(p-2)\Delta(\{v\})\left(\frac{\alpha p!}{\pi}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}(p(N-p)+1)}\nu^{-p(p-1)(N-p)-(p+1)(p-1)}e^{-\frac{\alpha p!}{(\nu^{2})^{p-2}}}

\displaystyle\quad\times\mathcal{Z}_{\perp}(\nu^{2},N)\prod_{i<j}\delta(\hat{v}^{i}\cdot\hat{v}^{j})\prod_{i=2}^{p}\delta(|v^{i}|-|v^{i-1}|)\prod_{i<j}\delta(G^{i,j})\,,

(6.2)

where by setting
v^{i}=|v^{i}|\hat{v}^{i}
, we separate vector magnitude and direction. Above, in (
6.2
), we keep using
\nu=|v^{1}|=|v^{2}|=|v^{3}|
to highlight the parts of the action that only depend on the vector norm. Also, it is worth to point out that the
\beta
parameter dependence in
\tilde{\rho}
can be cancelled. In fact, the distribution should not depend on arbitrary gauge-fixing parameters and we expect this to happen.

6.2
Gauge-Invariant Expression and Norm Distribution

Now, we can use (
6.2
) to extract the density for the eigenvector norm, corresponding to the signed distribution of inverse eigenvalues of the random tensors. The strategy is to first obtain a gauge invariant expression for (
6.2
) using a DeWitt-Faddeev-Popov method (see e.g.
[
54
]
), then integrate the
pN
-dimensional density of the vector components to the
1
-dimensional density of their norm. Firstly, we define the gauge-invariant density by smearing the signed density over gauge orbits of
\text{SO}(p)
, integrating with respect to a Haar measure on the group:

\tilde{\rho}_{\text{inv}}(\{v\})=\frac{1}{|\text{SO}(p)|}\int_{\text{SO}(p)}d\Omega\,\tilde{\rho}(\Omega\{v\})\,,

(6.3)

where we are using the shorthand notation for the
\text{SO}(p)
transformation:

\Omega\{v\}:=\Omega(v^{1},\dots,v^{p}):=(\Omega^{1i}v^{i},\dots,\Omega^{pk}v^{k})\,.

(6.4)

The translation invariance of the Haar measure then explicitly gives us that:

\tilde{\rho}_{\text{inv}}(\Omega\{v\})=\tilde{\rho}_{\text{inv}}(\{v\}),\quad\forall\Omega\in\text{SO}(p)\,.

(6.5)

Let us make the dependence of the gauge-fixing variables
G^{i,i^{\prime}}
on the components of the vectors
v^{i}
explicit, as well as collecting the multi-index
I=(i,i^{\prime})
,
i<i^{\prime}
. Then, we can split the density of (
6.2
) and define
\hat{\rho}
by:

\widetilde{\rho}(\{v\})=:\hat{\rho}(\{v\})\prod_{I}\delta(G^{I}(\{v\}))\,.

(6.6)

We begin with the following manipulation using properties of the Haar integral.
Through the smearing in (
6.3
), we assign a non-zero density to all tuples
\{v\}
that can be reached from a gauge-fixed solution to the permuted eigenvalue problem
\{v_{0}\}
by an
\text{SO}(p)
transformation, which we know will all be solutions of (
2.12
). On the other hand, if
\{v\}
solves (
2.12
) then, given that the gauge-fixing correctly removes continuous symmetries and we restrict to one of the sectors equivalent under the residual discrete symmetry, such a
\{v_{0}\}
satisfying the gauge-fixing conditions and representing the gauge orbit is determined uniquely. Let
\Omega_{0}\in\text{SO}(p)
be the (
\{v\}
-dependent) transformation relating the two. Then, on the support of
\tilde{\rho}_{\text{inv}}
:

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}_{\text{inv}}(\{v\})=\frac{1}{|\text{SO}(p)|}

\displaystyle\int d\Omega\,\tilde{\rho}(\Omega\{v\})

\displaystyle\overset{\Omega\to\Omega\Omega_{0}}{=}\frac{1}{|\text{SO}(p)|}

\displaystyle\int d\Omega\,\hat{\rho}(\Omega\{v\})\prod_{I}\delta(G^{I}(\Omega\{v\}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\text{SO}(p)|}

8.2
).
To compute
\Psi^{\alpha}J\Psi^{\beta}
, let us first decompose

\Psi
into its parallel and transverse parts against
v
as was previously performed in Section
4
:

\Psi^{\alpha}=I^{\parallel}\Psi^{\alpha}+I^{\perp}\Psi^{\alpha}=:\Psi^{\parallel\alpha}+\Psi^{\perp\alpha}\,.

(8.12)

With the flavour and vector indices being explicit, the transverse part satisfies

\Psi_{a}^{\perp\alpha i}v^{j}_{a}=0\ \forall i,j
, and the parallel part can be
expanded in terms of
v^{i}_{a}
, namely,
\Psi^{\parallel\alpha i}_{a}=\Psi^{\alpha ii^{\prime}}v^{i^{\prime}}_{a}
,
by introducing
\Psi^{\alpha ii^{\prime}}
. Then, as shown in Appendix
D
,
we find:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Psi^{\alpha}J\Psi^{\beta}=&\nu^{2}\Psi^{\parallel\alpha ii^{\prime}}\left(\delta^{ij}\delta^{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}-\delta^{ii^{\prime}}\delta^{jj^{\prime}}+\delta^{ij^{\prime}}\delta^{i^{\prime}j}\right)\Psi^{\parallel\beta jj^{\prime}}\\
&+\Psi_{a}^{\perp\alpha i}\Psi_{a}^{\perp\beta i}-\frac{1}{(p-2)!}\epsilon^{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{p}}T_{a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{p}}\Psi_{a_{1}}^{\perp\alpha i_{1}}\Psi_{a_{2}}^{\perp\beta i_{2}}v_{a_{3}}^{i_{3}}v_{a_{4}}^{i_{4}}\cdots v_{a_{p}}^{i_{p}}.\end{split}

(8.13)

There are two important properties: the tensor
T
couples only to the transverse part, and
the parallel and the transverse parts decouple from each other.
Then from (
8.9
) and (
8.13
), all the terms containing
T
can be collected as:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{T}

\displaystyle:=-\alpha T^{2}

\displaystyle\ -T_{a_{1}\cdots a_{p}}\left\llbracket\frac{\mathbf{i}}{(p-1)!}\epsilon^{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{p}}\lambda_{a_{1}}^{i_{1}}v_{a_{2}}^{i_{2}}\cdots v_{a_{p}}^{i_{p}}+\frac{1}{(p-2)!}\epsilon^{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{p}}\Gamma_{\alpha\beta}\Psi^{\perp\alpha i_{1}}_{a_{1}}\Psi_{a_{2}}^{\perp\beta i_{2}}v^{i_{3}}_{a_{3}}\cdots v_{a_{p}}^{i_{p}}\right\rrbracket\,,

(8.14)

which is a generalisation of (
5.3
).
Here:

{\rho}(\{v\})=\int\mathcal{D}\lambda\,\mathcal{D}\Psi\,\underbrace{e^{{{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm tot}}-{\mathcal{S}}_{T}}}_{T\text{-indep.}}\int\mathcal{D}T\,e^{{\mathcal{S}}_{T}}\,.

(8.15)

Then the Gaussian integral over
T

adds the square of the term

\llbracket\cdots\rrbracket
of (
8.14
) to the action.
One important fact is that, in the square, the cross terms vanish because
of
\Psi_{a}^{\perp\alpha i}v_{a}^{j}=0
, in exactly the same fashion as in (
5.7
). After a short computation, we obtain the result of
the integration over
T
as:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}^{\backslash\{T\}}

\displaystyle={{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm tot}}-{\mathcal{S}}_{T}-\frac{1}{4\alpha\left((p-1)!\right)^{2}}\left\llbracket\epsilon^{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{p}}\lambda_{a_{1}}^{i_{1}}v_{a_{2}}^{i_{2}}\cdots v_{a_{p}}^{i_{p}}\right\rrbracket^{2}

\displaystyle\ \ +\frac{\nu^{2(p-2)}}{2\alpha p!}{\Gamma^{\rm sym}}_{\alpha\beta}{\Gamma^{\rm sym}}_{\alpha^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}}(-1)^{[\alpha^{\prime}][\beta]}\delta_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}^{ij}(\Psi^{\perp\alpha i}\cdot\Psi^{\perp\alpha^{\prime}i^{\prime}})(\Psi^{\perp\beta j}\cdot\Psi^{\perp\beta^{\prime}j^{\prime}})\,,

(8.16)

where
[\alpha]
denotes the grade of the field
\Psi^{\alpha}
, namely,
[\alpha]=0
for

\alpha=1,2
and
[\alpha]=1
for
3\leq\alpha\leq 6
. We defined:

\displaystyle{\Gamma^{\rm sym}}_{\alpha\beta}:=\frac{1}{2}\left(\Gamma_{\alpha\beta}+(-1)^{[\alpha][\beta]}\Gamma_{\beta\alpha}\right)\,,

(8.17)

and we recall the notations
\Psi^{\perp\alpha i}\cdot\Psi^{\perp\beta j}=\Psi_{a}^{\perp\alpha i}\Psi_{a}^{\perp\beta j}
,
\delta_{jj^{\prime}}^{ii^{\prime}}=\delta^{ij}\delta^{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}-\delta^{ij^{\prime}}\delta^{i^{\prime}j}
.
The genuine density writes now as follows:

{\rho}(\{v\})=\int\mathcal{D}\lambda\,\mathcal{D}\Psi\,e^{{\mathcal{S}}^{\backslash\{T\}}}\,.

(8.18)

8.3
Integration Over
\lambda

From (
8.9
) and (
8.16
) the terms containing
\lambda
are collected as:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{\lambda}=-\frac{1}{4\alpha\left((p-1)!\right)^{2}}\left\llbracket\epsilon^{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{p}}\lambda_{a_{1}}^{i_{1}}v_{a_{2}}^{i_{2}}\cdots v_{a_{p}}^{i_{p}}\right\rrbracket^{2}+\mathbf{i}\lambda^{i}_{a}\left(f_{a}^{i}+v^{i^{\prime}}_{a}G^{ii^{\prime}}\right)\,,

(8.19)

with:

{\rho}(\{v\})=\int\,\mathcal{D}\Psi\,\underbrace{e^{{\mathcal{S}}^{\backslash\{T\}}-{\mathcal{S}}_{\lambda}}}_{T,\,\lambda\text{-indep.}}\int\mathcal{D}\lambda\,e^{{\mathcal{S}}_{\lambda}}\,.

Since
{\mathcal{S}}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int\mathcal{D}\lambda\,e^{{\mathcal{S}}_{\lambda}}=&\left(\frac{\alpha\,p!}{\pi}\right)^{\frac{p(N-p)+1}{2}}\nu^{-p(p-1)(N-p)}e^{-\frac{\alpha p!}{\nu^{2(p-2)}}}\\

(8.21)

8.4
Computing the Quantum Field Theory

8.4.1
Computation of the Parallel Part

The integration over
\Psi
proceeds by first splitting the parallel and transverse components, as was performed in the signed case
in Section
4
. The parallel components couple at most quadratically, and the computation proceeds in parallel with that
of the signed case, including the decoupling of the transverse part in the gauge-fixing sector, as was performed in Section
5.3
.
Therefore the computation of the parallel part reduces to the computation of some determinants from both the fermions and the bosons.
The simplicity is that the vanishing limit of the regularisation parameter,
\epsilon,\tilde{\epsilon}\rightarrow+0
, is regular
in the computation, and the final result is the same as (
5.53
) except for the overall positivity, namely,

\displaystyle\int\mathcal{D}\Psi^{\parallel}\,e^{{\mathcal{S}}^{\backslash\{T,\lambda\}}_{\Psi^{\parallel}}}=2^{p-1}(p-2)\Delta,

(8.22)

where:

\displaystyle\Delta:=\det_{i<j,i^{\prime}<j^{\prime}}\left(\widetilde{G}^{ij\,i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}-\widetilde{G}^{ij\,j^{\prime}i^{\prime}}\right)\,,

(8.23)

and:

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}^{ij\,i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}=\frac{\partial G^{ij}}{\partial v^{i^{\prime}}_{a}}v^{j^{\prime}}_{a}\,.

(8.24)

This identical result with the signed case (except for the overall sign) can be attributed to the fact that the extra contributions coming
from the extra fermions and bosons,
compared with the signed case, indeed cancel with each other because of the presence of the supersymmetry (
8.7
)
in the case that the limit
\epsilon,\tilde{\epsilon}\rightarrow+0
is smooth.

From (
8.20
), (
8.21
) and (
8.22
), we obtain in the same style as (
6.2
):

\displaystyle\begin{split}\rho(\{v\})=&(p-2)\Delta\left(\frac{\alpha p!}{\pi}\right)^{\frac{p(N-p)+1}{2}}\nu^{-p(p-1)(N-p)-p^{2}+1}e^{-\frac{\alpha p!}{\nu^{2(p-2)}}}\\
&\ \ \times Z_{\perp}\prod_{i<j}\delta\left(\hat{v}^{i}\cdot\hat{v}^{j}\right)\prod_{i=1}^{p-1}\delta\left(|v^{i}|-|v^{i+1}|\right)\prod_{i<j}\delta\left(G^{i,j}\right)\,,\end{split}

(8.25)

where
Z_{\perp}
is similar to (
5.57
) and is defined by:

\displaystyle Z_{\perp}=\int\mathcal{D}\Psi_{\perp}e^{{\mathcal{S}}_{\perp}}\,,

(8.26)

with:

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\mathcal{S}}_{\perp}&=-(\phi^{\perp})^{2}+\Gamma_{\alpha\beta}\Psi^{\perp\alpha i}\cdot\Psi^{\perp\beta i}-\epsilon(\sigma^{\perp})^{2}+\mathbf{i}\sqrt{\tilde{\epsilon}}\,{\bar{\psi}}^{\perp 1}\psi^{\perp 1}-\mathbf{i}\sqrt{\tilde{\epsilon}}\,{\bar{\psi}}^{\perp 2}\psi^{\perp 2}\\
&\ \ \ \ \ \ +\frac{\nu^{2(p-2)}}{2\alpha p!}{\Gamma^{\rm sym}}_{\alpha\beta}{\Gamma^{\rm sym}}_{\alpha^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}}(-1)^{[\alpha^{\prime}][\beta]}\delta_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}^{ij}(\Psi^{\perp\alpha i}\cdot\Psi^{\perp\alpha^{\prime}i^{\prime}})(\Psi^{\perp\beta j}\cdot\Psi^{\perp\beta^{\prime}j^{\prime}}).\end{split}

(8.27)

For later convenience,
Z_{\perp}
in (
8.26
) has been normalised for the free field theory:

\displaystyle Z_{\perp}=1\hbox{ for }\nu=0.

(8.28)

The smearing in the gauge direction and the integration over
v
can be performed in the same
manner as in the signed case described in Section
6.2
. We obtain:

\displaystyle\rho(\nu)=\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{p}S_{N-i}}{|{\rm SO}(p)|_{\theta}}(p-2)\left(\frac{\alpha p!}{\pi}\right)^{\frac{p(N-p)+1}{2}}\nu^{-p(p-1)(N-p)-p^{2}+1+p(N-1)}e^{-\frac{\alpha p!}{\nu^{2(p-2)}}}Z_{\perp}\,,

(8.29)

which is identical with (
6.2
) except for the missing sign from equation (
8.22
) and the quartic theory in both fermions and bosons.

8.4.2
Computation of the Transverse Part in the Large
N
Limit

So far, the computation of the genuine distribution is essentially identical with that of the signed case. This can be attributed to the fact that
the
\epsilon,\tilde{\epsilon}\rightarrow+0
limit is smooth for the parallel part, and the supersymmetry (
8.7
) holds in this limit.
In other words, one can compute the parallel part by setting
\epsilon,\tilde{\epsilon}
to zero from the beginning, in which the supersymmetry (
8.7
) exactly holds and the contributions of the additional bosons and fermions cancel with each other.

However, the computation of the transverse partition function is more difficult and subtle. In the signed case, the quantum field theory describes fermions coupled quartic couplings, and one can simply expand the fermion four-couplings in series. In the genuine case, the integration over
the bosons with quartic couplings is difficult to compute. It is not generally possible to obtain the integration in a closed form. In addition,
computing
Z_{\perp}
in perturbations of the four couplings would lead to a wrong result. This can
been seen in the explicit computation performed by a similar field theoretical method for the eigenvalue/vector distribution of
the real symmetric random tensor (order-three) in
[
45
]
. In that computation, the regularisation parameter was essentially important to avoid the divergence of the integrations until the final result. Moreover, the final result in
[
45
]
shows that the partition function contains not only
the polynomials of
\nu^{2(p-2)}
, but also terms with the non-perturbative factors
e^{-{\rm const.}/\nu^{2(p-2)}}
, that cannot be obtained by a series expansion of (
8.27
).

Because of the above technical difficulties, we will not try to exactly compute
Z_{\perp}
, but will
rather determine its leading asymptotic form for large
N
.
We will apply the method first used in
[
44
]
,
which was based on the Schwinger-Dyson equation.
To avoid unnecessary complications of notations/computations, the difference between
N-p
and
N

will be ignored for large
N
. In particular,
the dimension will be pretended to be
N
and the subscript
\perp
of the fields will be neglected
in this section.
We start with some assumptions about the
two-field expectation values,
\langle{\cal O}\rangle:=Z_{\perp}^{-1}\int\mathcal{D}\Psi{\cal O}e^{{\mathcal{S}}_{\perp}}
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\langle\phi^{i_{1}}_{a_{1}}\phi^{i_{2}}_{a_{2}}\rangle=Q^{B}_{1}\delta^{i_{1}i_{2}}\delta_{a_{1}a_{2}},\\
&\langle\phi^{i_{1}}_{a_{1}}\sigma_{a_{2}}^{i_{2}}\rangle=\langle\sigma^{i_{1}}_{a_{1}}\phi_{a_{2}}^{i_{2}}\rangle=Q^{B}_{2}\delta^{i_{1}i_{2}}\delta_{a_{1}a_{2}},\\
&\langle\sigma^{i_{1}}_{a_{1}}\sigma_{a_{2}}^{i_{2}}\rangle=Q^{B}_{3}\delta^{i_{1}i_{2}}\delta_{a_{1}a_{2}},\end{split}

(8.30)

for bosons, and:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\langle{\bar{\psi}}^{\kappa_{1}i_{1}}_{a_{1}}\psi_{a_{2}}^{\kappa_{2}i_{2}}\rangle=Q^{F}_{\kappa_{1}}\delta^{\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}}\delta^{i_{1}i_{2}}\delta_{a_{1}a_{2}},\\
&\hbox{Others}=0,\end{split}

(8.31)

for fermions, respectively, where the superscript
\kappa_{i}=1,2
for the fermions serves to label
the two pairs of fermions introduced in (
8.5
), and
Q^{B}_{i},Q^{F}_{i}
are functions of
\nu

to be determined below.

These forms have been uniquely fixed by requiring some symmetries on these expectation values.
Since the SO
(N)
and SO
(p)

symmetries, in the vector and flavour index spaces respectively,
are the intrinsic symmetries of the eigenvector problem,
it will be reasonable to require them.
The starting action
{{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm BF}}
in (
8.5
)
has the symmetry (
8.6
), which contains non-compact directions.
The breakdown of the symmetry would lead to a non-compact continuous degeneracy of the solutions,
that would make later analysis involved.
We assume this does not happen: the symmetry (
8.6
) holds in (
8.31
).

In the Schwinger-Dyson method, the values of
Q
are determined by the stationary points of an effective action which is
the sum of the expectation value of
{\mathcal{S}}_{\perp}
and the logarithmic terms which originate from
the transition of the variables from bosons and fermions to the expectation values (for instance, see Appendix A of
[
44
]

for the derivation):

\displaystyle{{\mathcal{S}}^{\rm eff}}

\displaystyle=\langle{\mathcal{S}}_{\perp}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\log\det M^{B}-\log\det M^{F},

(8.32)

where
M^{B}
and
M^{F}
are the matrices with components:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&M^{B}_{\kappa ia\ \kappa^{\prime}i^{\prime}a^{\prime}}=\langle X^{\kappa i}_{a}X^{\kappa^{\prime}i^{\prime}}_{a^{\prime}}\rangle\ \ (X^{1i}_{a}\equiv\phi^{i}_{a},\ X^{2i}_{a}\equiv\sigma^{i}_{a}),\\
&M^{F}_{\kappa ia\ \kappa^{\prime}i^{\prime}a^{\prime}}=\langle{\bar{\psi}}^{\kappa i}_{a}\psi^{\kappa^{\prime}i^{\prime}}_{a^{\prime}}\rangle.\end{split}

(8.33)

Using the explicit forms of
{\mathcal{S}}_{\perp}
given in Appendix
E
and the assumptions (
8.30
)
and (
8.31
), we obtain an effective action in terms of
Q
as:

\displaystyle\begin{split}{{\mathcal{S}}^{\rm eff}(Q)}&=Np\Bigg[\sum_{\kappa=1}^{2}\left(-\frac{\xi}{2}(Q^{F}_{\kappa})^{2}+Q^{F}_{\kappa}-\log Q^{F}_{\kappa}+\mathbf{i}s_{\kappa}\sqrt{\tilde{\epsilon}}\,Q_{\kappa}^{F}\right)\\
&\ \ \ \ -2\xi\left(Q^{B}_{1}Q^{B}_{3}+(Q^{B}_{2})^{2}\right)-Q^{B}_{1}+2\mathbf{i}Q^{B}_{2}-\epsilon Q^{B}_{3}+\frac{1}{2}\log\left(Q^{B}_{1}Q^{B}_{3}-(Q^{B}_{2})^{2}\right)\Bigg]\end{split}

(8.34)

with
s_{1}=1,s_{2}=-1
, and:

\displaystyle\xi=\frac{N\nu^{2(p-2)}}{2\alpha p(p-2)!},

(8.35)

which is identical to the definition in (
7.13
).
Some details of the derivation of (
8.34
) are given in
Appendix
E
.
We assume that the large-
N
scaling is taken so that
\xi
is kept finite, namely,

\nu^{2(p-2)}
scales like
1/N
.
One reason to take this scaling is that the interesting structure of the distribution, namely its edge, exists in this order.

An important property of (
8.34
) is that the bosonic and fermionic parts do not interact with each other.
Therefore we can solve the stationary point equations by considering them independently. We will find multiple
solutions for these equations,
and the proper one can be selected by their values at
\xi=0
or equivalently
\nu=0
.
Since the case with
\xi=0
is a free field theory (see (
8.27
)), the proper solution should satisfy:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&Q^{B}_{1}=0,\ Q^{B}_{2}=\frac{\mathbf{i}}{2},\ Q^{B}_{3}=\frac{1}{2},\\
&Q^{F}_{\kappa}=1,\end{split}

(8.36)

for
\xi,\epsilon,\tilde{\epsilon}\rightarrow+0
.

Once we have obtained the solution, the partition function in the leading order of
N
is determined to be:

\displaystyle Z_{\perp}=e^{{{\mathcal{S}}^{\rm eff}}(Q)-{\mathcal{S}}^{\rm eff}_{0}+o(N)},

(8.37)

where
{\mathcal{S}}^{\rm eff}_{0}
is the value of
{{\mathcal{S}}^{\rm eff}}(Q)
at
\xi=0
with (
8.36
), and the subtraction is chosen to be consistent with the normalisation
condition (
8.28
).

8.4.3
Solving for
Q^{F}
(Fermionic Sector)

The saddle point equation for
Q^{F}
is given by:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{{\mathcal{S}}^{\rm eff}(Q)}}{\partial Q^{F}_{\kappa}}=Np\left(-\xi Q^{F}_{\kappa}+1-\frac{1}{Q^{F}_{\kappa}}+\mathbf{i}s_{\kappa}\sqrt{\tilde{\epsilon}}\right)=0.

(8.38)

Between the two solutions
\Big(1+\mathbf{i}s_{\kappa}\sqrt{\tilde{\epsilon}}\pm\sqrt{(1+\mathbf{i}s_{\kappa}\sqrt{\tilde{\epsilon}})^{2}-4\xi}\Big)/(2\xi)
,
we pick the one with the
-
sign that satisfies the condition (
8.36
)
8
8

8

We take the standard branch convention for the square root, i.e. the cut runs
along the negative real axis.
. Taking
\tilde{\epsilon}\rightarrow+0
, we obtain:

\displaystyle Q^{F}_{\kappa}=\frac{1-\sqrt{1-4\xi}}{2\xi}\hbox{ for }\xi\leq\frac{1}{4},

(8.39)

and:

\displaystyle Q^{F}_{1}=\frac{1+\mathbf{i}\sqrt{4\xi-1}}{2\xi},\ Q^{F}_{2}=\frac{1-\mathbf{i}\sqrt{4\xi-1}}{2\xi}\hbox{ for }\xi\geq 1/4.

(8.40)

An important matter here is that, for
\xi\geq 1/4
,
the regularization parameter
\tilde{\epsilon}
has uniquely required
Q^{F}_{\kappa}

to take the solutions with the opposite signs
of the square root to each other.
If there were no
\tilde{\epsilon}
,
the choice would become ambiguous, namely, they could take the solutions with the same sign.
This uniqueness leads to that of the value of
the saddle point action.

8.4.4
Solving for
Q^{B}
(Bosonic Sector)

The saddle point equations are given by:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\frac{\partial{{\mathcal{S}}^{\rm eff}(Q)}}{\partial Q^{B}_{1}}=-2\xi Q^{B}_{3}-1+\frac{1}{2}\frac{Q^{B}_{3}}{Q^{B}_{1}Q^{B}_{3}-(Q^{B}_{2})^{2}}=0,\\
&\frac{\partial{{\mathcal{S}}^{\rm eff}(Q)}}{\partial Q^{B}_{2}}=-4\xi Q^{B}_{2}+2i-\frac{Q^{B}_{2}}{Q^{B}_{1}Q^{B}_{3}-(Q^{B}_{2})^{2}}=0,\\
&\frac{\partial{{\mathcal{S}}^{\rm eff}(Q)}}{\partial Q^{B}_{3}}=-2\xi Q^{B}_{1}-\epsilon+\frac{1}{2}\frac{Q^{B}_{1}}{Q^{B}_{1}Q^{B}_{3}-(Q^{B}_{2})^{2}}=0.\end{split}

(8.41)

The last equation suggests
Q^{B}_{1}=0
in the limit
\epsilon\rightarrow+0
. Assuming this, one can consistently obtain
a solution:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&Q^{B}_{1}=0,\\
&Q^{B}_{2}=\frac{\mathbf{i}(1-\sqrt{1-4\xi})}{4\xi},\\
&Q^{B}_{3}=\frac{1}{1-4\xi+\sqrt{1-4\xi}},\end{split}

(8.42)

which satisfies (
8.36
), for
\xi\leq 1/4
. The solution cannot be extended to
\xi>1/4
, because then the action becomes
complex, that contradicts the positivity of the denominator of (
8.3
).

The correct solution can be obtained by carefully taking the
\epsilon\rightarrow+0
limit of the
solution which satisfies (
8.36
). For
\xi>1/4
we obtain:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&Q^{B}_{1}=\frac{{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\sqrt{4\xi-1}}{4\xi},\\
&Q^{B}_{2}=\frac{\mathbf{i}}{4\xi},\\
&Q^{B}_{3}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\frac{\sqrt{4\xi-1}}{4\xi},\end{split}

(8.43)

in the leading order of
\epsilon
.
Here note that it is necessary to keep the
\sqrt{\epsilon}
order of
Q^{B}_{1}
, because
(
8.34
) contains
Q^{B}_{1}Q^{B}_{3}
, and
Q^{B}_{3}
diverges
like
1/\sqrt{\epsilon}
.
Though the values of
Q^{B}
have no well-defined limits as
\epsilon\rightarrow+0
, the action (
8.34
)
does, which is our only concern.

8.4.5
The Result

By explicitly putting the above solutions
(
8.39
), (
8.40
), (
8.42
), and (
8.43
)
to (
8.34
) and using (
8.37
),
we find a rather compact expression valid for both ranges of
\xi
as:

\displaystyle Z_{\perp}=e^{Np\,{\rm Re}[s^{\rm eff}]+o(N)},

(8.44)

where
\rm Re
denotes the real part, and:

\displaystyle s^{\rm eff}=-\frac{\xi}{2}(Q^{F})^{2}+Q^{F}-\log Q^{F}-1

(8.45)

with:

\displaystyle Q^{F}=\frac{1-\sqrt{1-4\xi}}{2\xi}.

(8.46)

Two comments are in order about this final result. One is that the expression (
8.45
) looks like half
of the fermionic part of (
8.34
). This observation is correct: the bosonic part exactly cancels half of the fermionic part.
This cancellation could be attributed to the (half) supersymmetry (
8.7
), but the
things seem more subtle. The subtlety comes from the fact that we cannot set
\epsilon,\tilde{\epsilon}
to zero from the beginning,
as the above computations show, and therefore the computations in the middle stage are violating the supersymmetry.
We would need a more detailed analysis to understand this cancellation.
The other comment is that, though the solutions look different between the two ranges of
\xi
, the final
form has the compact expression above for both ranges by taking the real part for
\xi>1/4
. This would imply that
the partition function has a well-behaved analyticity.

8.5
Large-
N
Asymptotic Form of
\rho
and Location of Edge

It is now straightforward to compute the large-
N
asymptotic form of
\rho(\nu)
.
By assuming the same scaling
\nu^{2(p-2)}
with
1/N
as in Section
7
, and
applying the Stirling’s approximation to the sphere volume
S_{N-1}=2\pi^{N/2}/\Gamma(N/2)
,
we obtain from (
8.29
):

\displaystyle\rho(\nu)=\left(\frac{2e\alpha p!}{\nu^{2(p-2)}N}\right)^{\frac{Np}{2}}\exp(-\frac{\alpha p! }{\nu^{2 (p-2)}}+o(N))\,Z_{\perp}

(8.47)

in the leading order of
N
.
Putting (
8.44
) into this expression, we finally obtain:

\displaystyle\rho(\nu)=e^{Np\,{\rm Re}[{h_{p}}(x)]+o(N)},

(8.48)

where
{h_{p}}(x)
is
defined in (
7.19
).
Here
x=\xi/\xi_{c}
with
\xi_{c}=1/4
, or equivalently:

\displaystyle x=\frac{\nu^{2(p-2)}}{\nu_{c}^{2(p-2)}}=\frac{z_{c}^{2}}{z^{2}}

(8.49)

with:

\displaystyle\nu_{c}^{2(p-2)}=\frac{\alpha p(p-2)!}{2N},\ \ z_{c}=\sqrt{\frac{2N}{\alpha p(p-2)!}},

(8.50)

where we have also expressed the distribution in terms of the eigenvalues
z
by using the relation (
2.13
)
9
9

9

More rigorously, the distribution of the eigenvalues
z
must be given by
\rho(\nu)|d\nu/dz|
because of the change of
the measure between
z
and
\nu
. However, the factor is irrelevant in the large-
N

asymptotic form.
.

The location of the edge of the distribution is determined by the solution to
{\rm Re}[{h_{p}}(x^{\rm edge}_{p})]=0
.

{h_{p}}(x)
is a real monotonically increasing function with negative and positive regions in
x<1
,
while
{\rm Re}[{h_{p}}(x)]>0
for
x\geq 1
. Therefore the solution uniquely exists in the region
x<1
,
and actually satisfies
{h_{p}}(x^{\rm edge}_{p})=0
. This implies that the signed and the genuine distributions share
the same edge location.
For instance, for
p=3
we numerically obtain
x^{\rm edge}_{p=3}\approx 0.971236
, or:

\displaystyle\nu_{{\rm edge}\,p=3}^{2}\approx 0.971236\cdot\frac{3\alpha}{2N}

(8.51)

from (
8.49
) and (
8.50
).

The location of the edge of the distribution gives a bound on the injective norm of the random tensor for large-

The tensor eigenvalue/vector problems have various versions.

9.2

The eigenvalue/vector problem of this case has some similarities with the current case.
It is described by a system of eigenvector equations:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&v^{(1)}_{a_{1}}=T_{a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{p}}(v^{(2)}_{a_{2}})^{*}\cdots(v^{(p)}_{a_{p}})^{*},\\

The signed distribution has been computed in

\displaystyle Q=p\cdot\frac{\sqrt{1+\frac{4g}{p}y^{2}}-1}{2gy},

In this work, we defined real eigenpairs of a real antisymmetric tensor of
order
p

p

the previous studies
[

,
44
,
45
,
46
,

,

,

The asymptotic distributions for large

We found a large-

]

[

]

]

We conjecture that the same universality holds for all cases in which

As in the previous cases

We obtained an analytic expression of the signed distribution for finite

As shown in (

The quantum field theory of the signed distribution is purely fermionic, and therefore

In other words, it is intrinsically

In fact, the previous exact computation for another case

Appendix A
Conventions

A.1
Fields and Integrals

The
\lambda
and
\bar{\psi},\psi
fields of the partition function initially appear from expressing delta functions and determinants within the field theoretic framework. We pick the following convention for
n
-dimensional delta function integrals, obtained in the context of Fourier transforms that avoid having a rescaling factor in the exponential:

\delta(x)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n}}\int\prod_{i}d\mu_{i}\,e^{\mathbf{i}\mu\cdot x}\,.

(A.1)

With this convention, we define the bosonic integration measure
\mathcal{D}\lambda
:

\mathcal{D}\lambda\,:=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n}}\prod_{i,a}d\lambda^{i}_{a}\,.

(A.2)

We take the fermionic integration measure
\mathcal{D}\bar{\psi}\mathcal{D}\psi
to have the following definition:

\mathcal{D}\bar{\psi}\,\mathcal{D}\psi\,:=\prod_{i,a}(d\psi^{i}_{a}\,d\bar{\psi}^{i}_{a})\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\int\mathcal{D}\bar{\psi}\,\mathcal{D}\psi\,e^{\bar{\psi}M\psi}=\det M\,,

(A.3)

which is automatically normalised when
M=\mathds{1}
and does not pick up a sign in parallel-transverse transformation of the measure. Similarly, the joint bosonic field integration measure of Section
8
is defined by:

\mathcal{D}\phi\mathcal{D}\sigma:=\frac{1}{\pi^{n}}d\phi\,d\sigma=\frac{1}{\pi^{n}}\prod_{i,a}d\phi^{i}_{a}\,d\sigma^{i}_{a}\,,

(A.4)

so that
\int\mathcal{D}\phi\mathcal{D}\sigma e^{-\phi^{2}-\sigma^{2}}=1
.

Finally, we have the result for the surface integral of an
n
-dimensional hypersphere of unit radius:

S_{n-1}=\frac{2\pi^{n/2}}{\Gamma\big(\frac{n}{2}\big)}

(A.5)

A.2
Matrices and Contractions

Many matrix-to-
\epsilon
contractions appear in our calculations, so we collect the following simple manipulations involving a general matrix
M
and its determinant:

\displaystyle\epsilon^{i_{1}\dots i_{p}}\det M

\displaystyle=\epsilon^{i^{\prime}_{1}\dots i^{\prime}_{p}}M^{i_{1}i^{\prime}_{1}}\dots M^{i_{p}i^{\prime}_{p}}

(A.6)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow(M^{-1})^{i^{\prime\prime}_{1}i_{1}}\epsilon^{i_{1}i_{2}\dots i_{p}}\det M

\displaystyle=\epsilon^{i^{\prime\prime}_{1}i^{\prime}_{2}\dots i^{\prime}_{p}}M^{i_{2}i^{\prime}_{2}}\dots M^{i_{p}i^{\prime}_{p}}

(A.7)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow(M^{-1})^{i^{\prime\prime}_{1}i_{1}}(M^{-1})^{i^{\prime\prime}_{2}i_{2}}\epsilon^{i_{1}i_{2}i_{3}\dots i_{p}}\det M

\displaystyle=\epsilon^{i^{\prime\prime}_{1}i^{\prime\prime}_{2}i^{\prime}_{3}\dots i^{\prime}_{p}}M^{i_{3}i^{\prime}_{3}}\dots M^{i_{p}i^{\prime}_{p}}

(A.8)

Recall that in the particular case where
M\in\text{O}(p)
, this simplifies further using
M^{-1}=M^{T}
and if
M\in\text{SO}(p)
then also
\det M=1
.

Manipulation of matrices and indexed objects sometimes includes symmetrisations and antisymmetrisations. Our conventions
are taken to be:

\displaystyle M^{(i_{1}\dots i_{p})}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{p!}\sum_{\sigma}M^{\sigma(i_{1})\dots\sigma(i_{p})}

(A.9)

\displaystyle M^{[i_{1}\dots i_{p}]}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{p!}\sum_{\sigma}\text{Sign}(\sigma)M^{\sigma(i_{1})\dots\sigma(i_{p})}\,.

(A.10)

For objects with lower indices taking values on
\{1,\dots,N\}
we also use the double bracket notation, e.g.:

\llbracket P_{a_{1}\dots a_{p}}\rrbracket:=P_{[a_{1}\dots a_{p}]}

(A.11)

We also take the following convention for the generalised Kronecker delta:

\delta^{i_{1}\dots i_{p}}_{i^{\prime}_{1}\dots i^{\prime}_{p}}=\sum_{\sigma}{\rm Sign}(\sigma)\,\delta^{i_{1}}_{\sigma(i^{\prime}_{1})}\delta^{i_{2}}_{\sigma(i^{\prime}_{2})}\cdots\delta^{i_{p}}_{\sigma(i^{\prime}_{p})}\,,

(A.12)

with
\sigma(i^{\prime}_{1}),\sigma(i^{\prime}_{2}),\cdots,\sigma(i^{\prime}_{p})
being permutations of
i^{\prime}_{1},i^{\prime}_{2},\cdots,i^{\prime}_{p}
and
{\rm Sign}(\sigma)=\pm 1
being the signature of
\sigma
.

A.3
Parallel and Transverse Subspace Measures

Finally, we introduce notation for integration measures on parallel and transverse subspaces with respect to the eigenvector tuple. We consider an integral over a
\lambda
-field that splits in the following way as an example:

\lambda^{i}_{a}=\lambda^{\parallel ij}v^{j}_{a}+\lambda^{\perp ij}\bar{v}^{j}_{a}

(A.13)

Where the
v^{i}
span the parallel subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
\bar{v}^{i}
span the transverse subspace
\mathbb{R}^{N-p}
. It follows that integration over the
p\times N
variables
\lambda^{i}_{a}
precisely corresponds to integrating over the
p\times p
coefficients
\lambda^{\parallel ij}
and the
p\times(N-p)
coefficients
\lambda^{\perp ij}
. Let
\mathcal{D}[\lambda^{\parallel,\perp}]
represent the “matrix-like” integration over the parallel or alternatively the transverse subspace coefficients. Then we can derive the change of measure through the following:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\lambda

\displaystyle=\Big|\det\left(\frac{\partial\lambda^{i}_{a}}{\partial\{\lambda^{\parallel jk},\lambda^{\perp jk}\}}\right)\Big|\,\mathcal{D}[\lambda^{\parallel}]\,\mathcal{D}[\lambda^{\perp}]

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\Big|\det\left(\frac{\partial\lambda}{\partial\{\lambda^{\parallel},\lambda^{\perp}\}}\right)^{T}\left(\frac{\partial\lambda}{\partial\{\lambda^{\parallel},\lambda^{\perp}\}}\right)\Big|}\,\mathcal{D}[\lambda^{\parallel}]\,\mathcal{D}[\lambda^{\perp}]

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\Big|\det\left(\frac{\partial\lambda}{\partial\lambda^{\parallel}}\right)^{T}\left(\frac{\partial\lambda}{\partial\lambda^{\parallel}}\right)\Big|}\,\mathcal{D}[\lambda^{\parallel}]\times\sqrt{\Big|\det\left(\frac{\partial\lambda}{\partial\lambda^{\perp}}\right)^{T}\left(\frac{\partial\lambda}{\partial\lambda^{\perp}}\right)\Big|}\,\mathcal{D}[\lambda^{\perp}]

\displaystyle=:\Big|\det\Big(\frac{\partial\lambda}{\partial\lambda^{\parallel}}\Big)\Big|\,\mathcal{D}[\lambda^{\parallel}]\times\Big|\det\Big(\frac{\partial\lambda}{\partial\lambda^{\perp}}\Big)\Big|\,\mathcal{D}[\lambda^{\perp}]

(A.14)

In the first two lines, we use
\partial/\partial\{\lambda^{\parallel jk},\lambda^{\perp jk}\}
to schematically represent taking the Jacobian of the transformation of
\lambda
with respect to the new separate variables (or, more precisely, coefficients)
\lambda^{\parallel}
and
\lambda^{\perp}
. In the third line, squaring the Jacobian matrix separates the contributions of the
\partial/\partial\lambda^{\parallel}
and
\partial/\partial\lambda^{\perp}
derivatives within the Jacobian’s block form, allowing us to split the two determinants. In this way, we interpret the determinant notation of the last line in (
A.14
) in the Gramian sense. We take the following to be equivalent notations for the measures integrating an action:

\displaystyle\int\mathcal{D}\lambda^{\parallel}\,\mathcal{D}\lambda^{\perp}e^{f(\lambda^{\parallel},\,\lambda^{\perp})}

\displaystyle=\Big|\det\Big(\frac{\partial\lambda}{\partial\lambda^{\parallel}}\Big)\Big|\int\mathcal{D}[\lambda^{\parallel}]\,\mathcal{D}\lambda^{\perp}\,e^{f(\lambda^{\parallel ij},\,\lambda^{\perp})}

\displaystyle=\Big|\det\Big(\frac{\partial\lambda}{\partial\lambda^{\parallel}}\Big)\Big|\Big|\det\Big(\frac{\partial\lambda}{\partial\lambda^{\perp}}\Big)\Big|\int\mathcal{D}[\lambda^{\parallel}]\,\mathcal{D}[\lambda^{\perp}]\,e^{f(\lambda^{\parallel ij},\,\lambda^{\perp ij})}

(A.15)

Where, again, the two determinants of rectangular matrices should be interpreted in the Gramian sense of (
A.14
). It is worth noting that in the parallel transverse split of (
A.13
), the parallel determinant just yields the metric of (
4.1
):

\displaystyle\Big|\det\Big(\frac{\partial\lambda}{\partial\lambda^{\parallel}}\Big)\Big|=\sqrt{\Big|\det\left(\frac{\partial\lambda}{\partial\lambda^{\parallel}}\right)^{T}\left(\frac{\partial\lambda}{\partial\lambda^{\parallel}}\right)\Big|}=\sqrt{|\det(v\cdot v)|^{p}}=|\det g|^{\frac{p}{2}}

(A.16)

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\mathcal{D}\lambda^{\parallel}=|\det g|^{\frac{p}{2}}\mathcal{D}[\lambda^{\parallel}]

(A.17)

The analogous result holds for the metric defined on the transverse subspace basis vectors (although it is never required in calculations):

\displaystyle\bar{g}^{ij}=\bar{v}^{i}\cdot\bar{v}^{j}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\Big|\det\Big(\frac{\partial\lambda}{\partial\lambda^{\parallel}}\Big)\Big|=|\det\bar{g}|^{\frac{p}{2}}

(A.18)

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\mathcal{D}\lambda^{\perp}=|\det\bar{g}|^{\frac{p}{2}}\mathcal{D}[\lambda^{\perp}]

(A.19)

Also, repeating the calculation with Grassmann variables
\psi
and
\bar{\psi}
yields the same Jacobian factors but with inverse power:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\psi^{\parallel}=|\det g|^{-\frac{p}{2}}\mathcal{D}[\psi^{\parallel}]

(A.20)

Appendix F
Numerical Simulations

We have gathered our numerical computations in a
Mathematica notebook at the link:
https://github.com/dlprtn/AntisymmetricTensorEigenvalues
.

Firstly, it contains the numerical evaluation of the transverse action by acting with the exponential of the quadratic derivative operator on the pfaffian of the quadratic transverse action (
5.65
).

Secondly, it details how to obtain the generalised eigenvector equations for the totally antisymmetric Gaussian tensors. We relied on the Mathematica numerical solver to exhaustively search for all solutions to the eigenvector equations. Equation (
6.2
) presented the signed distribution for a fixed
p
and fixed finite
N
. By choosing each, we compared the distribution with numerical simulations and verified that theoretical and empirical results match. The chosen parameters for numerical comparison are
p=3
,
p=4
for
N=4,5,6,7
. This means that we considered the eigenvalues of order-three and order-four tensors. The averaging was done by sampling over
10000
tensors, each having totally-antisymmetric entries that are randomly generated according to the Gaussian distribution in (
2.14
). As explained in Sec.
3.2
, while the distribution is independent of gauge, we chose to fix the conditions (
3.14
), as well as the discrete symmetry by
v_{i}^{i}>0
,
1\leq i\leq p-1
, in the numerical solver of the eigenvalue equations to get unique solutions among gauge-equivalent solutions.

In the simulation of the signed distribution,
each solution must be weighted by the sign of the Jacobian
\det\frac{\partial\tilde{f}}{\partial v}
. The sign actually depends on
the gauge conditions (including the discrete gauge choice), as is explicit in (
5.50
). In the numerical simulation, however, we rather computed
the sign by taking the product of the non-zero eigenvalues of
\frac{\partial f}{\partial v}
, ignoring the zero eigenvalues.
This procedure is independent of the gauge choice,
and should be equivalent to removing the global sign factor which is explained below (
6.11
).
