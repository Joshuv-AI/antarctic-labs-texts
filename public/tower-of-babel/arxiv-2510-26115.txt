Quenched coalescent for diploid population models with selfing and overlapping generations
Louis Wai-Tong Fan, Maximillian Newman, John Wakeley

Abstract
We introduce a general diploid population model with self-fertilization and possible overlapping generations, and study the genealogy of a sample of n n genes as the population size N N tends to infinity. Unlike traditional approach in coalescent theory which considers the unconditional (annealed) law of the gene genealogies averaged over the population pedigree, here we study the conditional (quenched) law of gene genealogies given the pedigree. We focus on the case of high selfing probability and obtain that this conditional law converges to a random probability measure, given by the random law of a system of coalescing random walks on an exchangeable fragmentation-coalescence process of [ 5 ] . This system contains the system of coalescing random walks on the ancestral recombination graph as a special case, and it sheds new light on the site-frequency spectrum (SFS) of genetic data by specifying how SFS depends on the pedigree. The convergence result is proved by means of a general characterization of weak convergence for random measures on the Skorokhod space with paths taking values in a locally compact Polish space.

Introduction

Coalescent processes have been widely used as models of gene genealogies that describe the ancestral structure of a sample of
n
genes, when the total population size
N

The Kingman coalescent

Other models such as the coalescent with asynchronous multiple mergers

;

The simultaneous multiple-mergers coalescent, also called the

Traditionally, coalescent models are obtained by taking average over the population pedigree, the graph that represents the total history of reproductive relationships in the population. Namely, implicit in the approach of papers in classical coalescent theory

;
Sag 99
;
Pit 99
;
Sch 00
;

this tradition of averaging over the pedigree is questionable because there is only one population pedigree, and all genetic information across loci is passed through this same pedigree.
For example, in order for unlinked loci to have independent genealogies (as they must by definition), under this averaging they would also need to have independent pedigrees. This is problematic because even unlinked loci are subject to the same pedigree.

This conceptual flaw was not unrecognized

;
WKLR 12
;
WKW 16
;
WBLW 17
;

We believe that, at least at the conceptual level,

This has been the basis of the mathematically rigorous works

;

;

;

While the Cannings model in

The recent work

From the application standpoint,
the main motivation of coalescent theory (and our quenched-coalescent theory) is to describe the patterns of genetic variation expected under various biological scenarios to provide frameworks for statistical inference about past events and processes affecting populations.
In a given population, gene genealogies are tree structures which emerge from tracing the ancestral lines of these sample backwards in time until the most recent common ancestor (MRCA). Mutations occurred in the past, along the ancestral lines, result in genetic diversity among the sample.
For example, the site-frequency spectrum (SFS) is a commonly used measure of genetic variation upon which statistical inferences are based

;

;

;

;

Our main result sheds new light on the SFS of genetic data, by specifying how SFS depends on the pedigree. More precisely, under the conditional coalescent, the SFS should be viewed as a conditional SFS given the single pedigree as a latent variable. In Figure
5

2
An exchangeable diploid model with selfing and overlapping generation

2.1
The model

In this paper, we consider a diploid, monoecious, panmictic (well-mixed and randomly mating) population of constant size
N
, evolving in discrete time-steps with overlapping generations. This model generalizes the diploid Cannings model in
ABFW 25
;
BLS 18
to allow overlapping generations and selfing, and extends the haploid model in
SW 08
to diploid populations while supporting general offspring distributions.

Precisely,
the population model is specified by
a deterministic number
\alpha_{N}\in[0,1]
and
the joint distribution of a random variable
K_{N}
and a random symmetric matrix
\textbf{V}=(V_{i,j})_{1\leq i,j\leq N}
.
The number
\alpha_{N}
represents the
selfing probability
for each offspring, and
K_{N}
and
V
represent, at an arbitrary timestep, the
total offspring number
and the
pairwise offspring numbers
respectively.
We assume the following:

•

K_{N}
and all entries of
V
take values in
\{0,1,2,\ldots,N\}
.

•

The total number of offspring satisfies
\sum_{i\leq j}^{N}V_{i,j}=K_{N}
.

•

The full matrix
V
is exchangeable, i.e.,

\left(V_{i,j}\right)_{1\leq i,j\leq N}\overset{d}{=}\left(V_{\sigma(i),\sigma(j)}\right)_{1\leq i,j\leq N}

for any permutation
\sigma
of
[N]=\{1,2,\ldots,N\}
.

We consider discrete time-steps indexed by
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
, where
k=0
is the present,
k=1
the previous time-step, and so on
backward into the past
. Let
\big\{(K^{(k)}_{N},\textbf{V}^{(k)})\big\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
be a sequence of i.i.d. random variables that have the same distribution as
(K_{N},\textbf{V})
. Reproduction events in different time-steps are taken to be independent, and are described as follows:

For each
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,
K^{(k)}_{N}
individuals are chosen uniformly without replacement, among the
N
individuals from the
k
-th time-step, to be children.
To describe parentage, we suppose that the
individuals at each time-step are labeled by
[N]
, and we let
V^{(k)}_{i,j}
be the number of offspring produced by the pair of individuals
(i,j)
in the
(k+1)
-th time-step in the past.
The
K_{N}^{(k)}
individuals defined by the entries of
\textbf{V}^{(k)}
constitute the new individuals in time-step
k
. Each individual has two sets of chromosomes, as it is a diploid population. Genetic lineages at an autosomal locus are transmitted according to Mendel’s law of random segregation, which means each gene copy in the offspring chooses independently from the two gene copies in the parent from which the gene copy is inherited.

The remaining
N-K_{N}^{(k)}
individuals are carried over unchanged (without random segregation) from time-step
k+1
, which can induce
overlapping generations
.

We further assume that
each of the
K^{(k)}_{N}
children independently chooses to have either a single parent (selfing) or two distinct parents (outcrossing), with probabilities
\alpha_{N}
and
1-\alpha_{N}
respectively. Therefore, conditional on
K_{N}^{(k)}
, the number of selfed offspring
S_{N}^{(k)}:=\sum_{i=1}^{N}V_{i,i}^{(k)}
is an
independent binomial random variable:
\mathrm{Bin}(K_{N}^{(k)},\alpha_{N})
. In other words, we assume that
(K_{N},\textbf{V})
satisfies

\sum_{i=1}^{N}V_{i,i}\sim\mathrm{Bin}(K_{N},\alpha_{N})\qquad\text{given }K_{N}.

2.2
The pedigree as important latent variable of the population

The population dynamics described in Section
2.1
give rise to a random directed graph
\mathcal{G}_{N}

that encodes the population pedigree, i.e. the reproductive relationships among all individuals. We give a formal definition below and offer the left panel of Figure
2
as an illustration.

Each individual is diploid and carries two copies of each autosomal locus. Genetic lineages are transmitted through the population pedigree via
Mendelian segregation
: each gene copy in the offspring randomly inherits one allele from the corresponding gene copies in the designated parent (or one of the two gene copies in the case of selfing). The pedigree is described explicitly in Definition
2.3
.

Definition 2.3

(Pedigree)
.

The population pedigree, or simply the pedigree, refers to an undirected multi-graph

\mathcal{G}_{N}
with vertex set
[N]\times\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, where a vertex
(\ell,k)
represents the individual with index
\ell
at the
k
-th time-step. The edges of
\mathcal{G}_{N}
are between vertices in consecutive time-steps.
For each
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, there are two edges connecting each of the
K^{(k)}_{N}
children with its parent(s). If the child has two distinct parent (outcrossing), then there is one edge to each parent; if the child has only one parent (selfing), then there are two edges connecting the child to its parent (hence
\mathcal{G}_{N}
is a multigraph).
For each of the remaining
N-K^{(k)}_{N}
individuals, there is a single edge connecting to itself in the consecutive time-steps.

The importance of the pedigree, as described in greater detail in the section “Previous Work on Pedigrees” of
DFBW 24
, is that it encapsulates population dynamics that are common to every single locus on the genome. Even loci extremely far apart are coupled by its dynamics. In particular, gene genealogies far apart on the genome are described by conditionally independent realizations of the ancestral process with respect to the pedigree. Gene genealogies, even conditional on the pedigree, are stochastic due to Mendelian randomness. This is demonstrated in Figure
3
. Classical coalescent theory implicitly averages over realizations of the pedigree to determine the
average
gene genealogy. However, this annealing forces distinct loci to be independent, which is a priori unjustifiable due to the stochasticity of the gene genealogies conditional on the pedigree. Indeed, early biological works of
BNA 90
;
WKLR 12
demonstrate how the pedigree can affect the structure of gene genealogies, and the mathematically rigorous works of
Tyu 15
;
DFBW 24
;
NWF 25
;
ABFW 25
have begun to show new scaling limits, and the limitations of the annealing of classical coalescent theory.

Definition 3.1

(Ancestral line)
.

For each
1\leq i\leq n
, the process
X_{i}^{N}=(X_{i}^{N}(k))_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}

Each process

.
See Figure
3

If we condition on the pedigree, then these random walks are conditionally independent, and the transition probabilities conditional on the pedigree is given by the Mendelian randomness.

The size of the state space of the family of coalescing random walks, namely
(\{0,1\}\times[N])^{n}

which is huge when

is large. It is customary to reduce the state space
by ignoring the indices of the ancestral individuals and only keep track of the coalescence of sample genealogies as we go backward in time. This is done via partitions

For

i\sim_{k}j\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad X_{i}(k)=X_{j}(k).

This way, one obtains a stochastic process (called a coalescent process) with state space

The initial state of this coalescent process is the partition into singletons

For our diploid population, we need to account for which ancestral individuals contain two ancestral gene copies.

to

Hence, each element
\xi\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
is of the form

\{(C_{1},C_{2}),(C_{3},C_{4}),\ldots,(C_{2x-1},C_{2x}),C_{2x+1},\ldots,C_{b}\},

(3.3)

where
\{C_{1},C_{2},\ldots,C_{b}\}\in\mathcal{E}_{n}
. We let
x:=\left|\left|\xi\right|\right|
be the number of individuals in a population that contain two sample lineages, and
b=|\xi|
be the number of lineages (or blocks) remaining in a sample. We can and will view
\mathcal{E}_{n}
as a subset of
\mathcal{S}_{n}
. Clearly,
\xi\in\mathcal{E}_{n}
if and only if
\left|\left|\xi\right|\right|=0
.

Definition 3.2
.

We define an
\mathcal{S}_{n}
-valued stochastic process

\chi^{N,n}=(\chi^{N,n}(k))_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
as follows:
for
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,

•

i
and
j
are in the sample block in
\chi^{N,n}(k)
if and only if
X_{i}(k)=X_{j}(k)
, i.e. the ancestral lines of the
i
-th and the
j
-th samples coalesced
k
time-steps in the past, and

•

two blocks are in a set together in
\chi^{N,n}(k)
if and only if
\hat{X}_{i}(k)=\hat{X}_{j}(k)
, i.e. the two extant lineages corresponding to these blocks are in the same individual in the population
k
time-steps in the past.

We call this process
\chi^{N,n}
the
ancestral process
of the sample.

We will investigate the convergence of a time-rescaling of
\chi^{N,n}
with a random time change
S
defined by

S(k):=\sup\left\{l\in\{0,1,2,\ldots,k\}:\,\chi^{N,n}(l)\in\mathcal{E}_{n}\right\},

(3.4)

the last time-step (up to
k
) at which the ancestral process
\chi^{N,n}
is totally dispersed (i.e. lies in
\mathcal{E}_{n}
). Note that
S(k)
is well-defined since by our assumption
\chi^{N,n}(0)\in\mathcal{E}_{n}
.
We define

\widetilde{\chi}^{N,n}
to be the random time-change of
\chi^{N,n}
as follows:

\widetilde{\chi}^{N,n}(k):=\chi^{N,n}(S(k)).

The
\mathcal{E}_{n}
-valued process
\widetilde{\chi}^{N,n}
is non-Markovian. However, the random time-change (
3.4
) not only keeps the process to live in the state space
\mathcal{E}_{n}
for which our weak convergence method works, but also prevents accumulation of jumps of the process in the limit as the population size
N
goes to infinity.

3.2
An annealed scaling limit

To connect to classical results in coalescent theory, including
Kin 82
;
Möh 98
;
Pit 99
;
Sag 99
;
Sch 00
;
BLS 18
, we shall consider the unconditional (a.k.a. annealed) law, one that averages over the random pedigree, of the ancestral process.

Denote by

\mathfrak{p}_{\xi\eta}^{N,n}:=\mathbb{P}\left(\chi^{N,n}(1)=\eta\mid\chi^{N,n}(0)=\xi\right),

(3.5)

the one-step transition probabilities for the ancestral process
\chi^{N,n}
.
Let
c_{N}=\mathfrak{p}_{\xi_{0}^{2}\mathbf{1}_{2}}^{N,n}
be the probability that two randomly chosen genes
from two distinct
individuals
coalesce in one time-step in the past. Then

\displaystyle c_{N}=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{(N-K)K}{N^{2}\,(N-1)}\right]\;+\;\frac{1}{\binom{N}{2}}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N}\!\left(\frac{1}{2}\binom{V_{i,i}}{2}+\frac{1}{4}\,V_{i,i}\!\sum_{j\neq i}V_{i,j}+\frac{1}{8}\binom{\sum_{j\neq i}V_{i,j}}{2}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{(N-K)K}{N^{2}\,(N-1)}\right]\;+\;\frac{1}{\binom{N}{2}}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{16}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigl(\tilde{V}_{i}^{\,2}-\tilde{V}_{i}\bigr)-\frac{S}{8}\right],

(3.6)

where the first term comes from one newborn and one carryover, and the second term comes from two newborns. This formula generalizes that in

(
BLS 18
, Equation 1.4)
.

Convergence will be established for the time-rescaled ancestral process
\left(\bar{\chi}^{N,n}(t)\right)_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}
defined by

\bar{\chi}^{N,n}(t)=\widetilde{\chi}^{N,n}\left(\lfloor tc_{N}^{-1}\rfloor\right)=\chi^{N,n}\left(S\lfloor tc_{N}^{-1}\rfloor\right).

(3.7)

We can and will consider this process
\bar{\chi}^{N,n}=\left(\bar{\chi}^{N,n}(t)\right)_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}
as a random variable taking value in the Skorokhod space
\mathcal{D}\left(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathcal{E}_{n}\right)
equipped with the
J_{1}
topology (see
EK 09
).

We introduce three assumptions on the asymptotic behaviors of our model as
N\to\infty
below. The first assumption ensures a continuous-time limiting model.

Assumption 3.3
.

Suppose
\lim_{N\to\infty}c_{N}=0
, or equivalently,
\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}[V_{1}^{2}]}{N}=0
where
V_{i}:=\sum_{j=1}^{N}V_{i,j}
is the total number of offspring for individual
i
.

Asymptotically, one unit of (continuous) time corresponds to
\lfloor c_{N}^{-1}\rfloor
time-steps.
To quantify the time-scale on which distinct sample lineages in the same individual disperse, we let
d_{N}
denote the probability that, in a single time-step in the past, two sample lineages in the same individual would disperse into two different individuals. Precisely,

d_{N}:=\mathbb{P}\left(\chi^{N,2}(1)=\Big\{\{1\},\{2\}\Big\}\mid\chi^{N,2}(0)=\Big\{(\{1\},\{2\})\Big\}\right)=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{K_{N}-S_{N}}{N}\right]=\frac{1-\alpha_{N}}{N}\mathbb{E}\left[K_{N}\right].

(3.8)

We refer to
d_{N}^{-1}
as the dispersal or outcrossing timescale.

The present work focuses on the asymptotic regime where the outcrossing timescale is comparable to, or slower than, the coalescence timescale. We refer this as the

limited outcrossing regime
, which generalizes the setting of the Moran model studied in
NWF 25
. This regime is formalized in Assumption
3.4
below.

Assumption 3.4

(Limited outcrossing)
.

We assume that
\alpha_{N}\to 1
and
d_{N}c_{N}^{-1}\to\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
as
N\to\infty
.

Assumption
3.4
together with
(
3.8
) implies that

1-\alpha_{N}\;=\;\frac{N\,c_{N}}{\mathbb{E}[K_{N}]}\,\lambda\,(1+o(1))\to 0

(3.9)

as
N\to\infty
. Hence,
\alpha_{N}\to 1
at a rate proportional to
\frac{N\,c_{N}}{\mathbb{E}[K_{N}]}
when
\lambda>0
,

Remark 3.5
.

It is notable that the assumption
d_{N}c_{N}^{-1}\to\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
together with Assumption
3.3
implies that the selfing probability
\alpha_{N}
tends to 1 in many cases including the case when

(e.g. Wright-Fisher model) and

(e.g. Moran model). Indeed,

3.8

imply the equality in (

).
Assumption
3.3
then implies that

if
\mathbb{E}[K]
is of order
N
. On other hand, note that (
3.6

independent of

. Hence

if
\frac{\mathbb{E}[K^{2}]}{N\mathbb{E}[K]}\to 0
.

Our third and last assumption is on the generator of the ancestral process. Let
F_{\rm hap}:\mathcal{S}_{n}\to\mathcal{E}_{n}
denote the

defined by

\{(C_{1},C_{2}),\ldots,(C_{2x-1},C_{2x}),C_{2x+1},\ldots,C_{b}\}\mapsto\{C_{1}\cup C_{2},\ldots,C_{2x-1}\cup C_{2x},C_{2x+1},\ldots,C_{b}\}.

(3.10)

This map announces that lineages in the same individuals have coalesced, essentially reducing a diploid model to a haploid model. This map is different from the “complete dispersal” map

;
ABFW 25
.

Define the matrix
\mathit{P}_{N,n}=\left(\mathit{p}_{\xi\eta}^{N}\right)_{\xi,\eta\in\mathcal{E}_{n}}
by

\mathit{p}_{\xi\eta}^{N}:=\mathbb{P}\left(F_{\rm triv}\left(\chi^{N,n}(1)\right)=\eta\,\Big|\,\chi^{N,n}(0)=\xi\right)=\sum_{\zeta\in\mathcal{S}_{n}:\,F_{\rm triv}(\zeta)=\eta}\mathfrak{p}_{\xi\zeta}^{N}.

Assumption 3.6
.

For any integer
n\geq 2
, the limit

Q_{n}:=\lim_{N\to\infty}c_{N}^{-1}\left(\mathit{P}_{N,n}-I\right)

exists as a real-valued matrix
Q_{n}=(q^{n}(\xi,\eta))_{\xi,\eta\in\mathcal{E}_{n}}
. Furthermore,
\left(Q_{n}\right)_{n\geq 2}
is a consistent family, which means that for any

under
\varpi_{n,m}
is
Q_{m}
(i.e.

for all
\xi,\eta\in\mathcal{E}_{n}
), where
\varpi_{n,m}:\mathcal{E}_{n}\to\mathcal{E}_{m}
is the projection obtained by restricting the partition to

.

To describe the annealed limit, we also describe an
n
-
\Xi
-coalescent following
(

, P.4)

Definition 3.7
.

Let
\Xi
be a finite measure on the infinite ordered simplex

\Delta:=\{x=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots)\in[0,1]^{\infty}:x_{1}\geq x_{2}\geq\ldots,\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}x_{i}\leq 1\}.

An
n
-
\Xi
-coalescent is a continuous-time Markov process
\chi^{n}=\left(\chi^{n}(t)\right)_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}
taking values in
\mathcal{E}_{n}

\xi
in
\mathcal{E}_{n}
of consisting of
b
blocks, suppose that
\eta
is an element of
\mathcal{E}_{n}
obtained from
\xi
by keeping
s
of the blocks of
\xi

b-s

r

The transition rate from
\xi
to
\eta

\lambda_{b;k_{1},\ldots,k_{r};s}:=\mathbbm{1}_{(r,s)=(1,b-2)}\Xi(\mathbf{0})+\int_{\Delta\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}}\sum_{l=0}^{s}\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{r+l}\text{ distinct}}^{\infty}\binom{s}{l}\prod_{m=1}^{r}x_{i_{m}}^{k_{m}}\prod_{m=r+1}^{r+l}x_{i_{m}}(1-\left|x\right|)^{s-l}\frac{\Xi(dx)}{\left\langle x,x\right\rangle},

where
\left\langle x,x\right\rangle=\sum_{i}x_{i}^{2}
and
\left|x\right|=\sum_{i}x_{i}
.

Informally, an
n
-
\Xi
-coalescent can be described in terms of interval partitions. Take

at rate
\frac{1}{\left\langle x,x\right\rangle}\Xi

\Xi(\mathbf{0})
.

.

We shall show in Lemma

Recall the pedigree

Briefly, let
\lambda
and

be given by Assumption

and Remark

\mathcal{L}^{N,n}
converges in distribution to a

described as follows:
Consider a random graph

, to be called a

-

n

4.1
Random walks on exchangeable fragmentation-coalescence processes

The (random)
Q
-
\lambda
graph
G_{Q,\lambda}^{n}
mentioned above is a subgraph of
an exchangeable fragmentation-coalescence process (EFC), where the EFC was introduced in

Ber 04
. Here we give a concise description of EFCs.

Definition 4.1

(
Ber 04
)
.

An exchangeable fragmentation-coalescence (EFC) process
\Pi
is a
\mathcal{E}_{\infty}
-valued exchangeable Markov process so that the restriction to
\mathcal{E}_{n}
, denoted by
\Pi^{n}
, is a cádlág finite state Markov chain which can only evolve by fragmentation of one block or by coalescence.

By
Ber 04
[p. 781]
\Pi
is fully characterized by
\Pi(0)
, finite measures
\nu_{Disl}
and
\nu_{Coag}
on
\Delta
, and non-negative constants
c_{k},c_{e}
. In this paper we will always consider the case
\nu_{Disl}=0
.
c_{k}
and
\nu_{Coag}
form the coalescing part of the EFC. Specifically, for any finite subset
l
of particles in the EFC, they coalesce as if they were an
l
-
\Xi
-coalescent where
\Xi=c_{k}\delta_{\mathbf{0}}+\nu_{Coag}
. That is, the non-Kingman coalescences are governed by
\nu_{Coag}
and
c_{k}
is the rate at which any pair of particles coalesce.
c_{e}
is simply the rate at which each particle in the EFC fragments into two particles.

In this section, we discuss some important applications of our main result that motivate this study.

In Section

(

5.2
Convergence of tree statistics

The notion of convergence in what follows is weak convergence in distribution of random measures, which we make precise in Section
6
. The standard reference is
(
Kal 17
, Chapter 4)
. As described briefly in Remark
4.8
, the form of weak convergence established in Theorem
4.7
implies that, for any compactly supported function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathcal{E}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, the associated integral functional
\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi(s,\cdot(s))ds
converges in distribution, i.e.

\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi\left(s,\bar{\chi}^{N,n}(s)\right)ds\in\cdot\,\mid\,\mathcal{A}_{N}\right]\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{{d}}}}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi\left(s,\chi^{n}(s)\right)ds\in\cdot\,\mid\,\Pi\right].

An important class of these integral functionals, which in fact motivated our choice of the form of convergence of the random measures
\mathcal{L}^{N,n}
, are the internal and external branch lengths of the coalescent associated with the SFS. Formally, the
r
th total branch length
\tau^{n,r}
of a coalescent
\chi^{n}\in\mathcal{D}\left(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathcal{E}_{n}\right)
of a sample of size
n
is defined by

\tau^{n,r}(\chi^{n})=\int_{0}^{\infty}\#\{C\in\chi^{n}(s):|C|=r\}ds.

That is,
\tau^{n,r}
is the sum of the lengths of the edges in the coalescent ancestral to
r
samples. Note that
\tau^{n,r}
is not of the form of
\varphi
described above, so it requires an additional argument to show that these functionals do indeed converge. This is done via a truncation argument.

Proposition 5.4
.

Suppose that, as
N\to\infty
, that Assumptions
3.3
,
3.4
, and
3.6
hold. Then, for any finite
s\in\mathbb{N}
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\tau^{n,r}(\bar{\chi}^{N,n})\right)_{1\leq r\leq s}\,\mid\,\mathcal{A}_{N}\right]\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{{d}}}}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\tau^{n,r}(\bar{\chi}^{N,n})_{1\leq r\leq s}\,\mid\,\Pi\right],

where
\Pi
is as described in Theorem
4.7
.

Proof.

For each
r\geq 1
and
\xi\in\mathcal{E}_{n}
, set
g_{r}(\chi^{n})=\#\{C\in\chi:|C|=r\}
, which is bounded above by
\lfloor n/r\rfloor
.
For
T>0
, define the truncated branch length

\tau^{n,r,T}(\chi)=\int_{0}^{T}g_{r}(\chi(t))\,dt.

By Remark
4.8
, the occupation measures

\mu_{\bar{\chi}^{N,n}}
converge vaguely to
\mu_{\chi^{n}}
, so for any bounded continuous cutoff

\psi_{T,\varepsilon}\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
with
\int_{0}^{\infty}|\psi_{T,\varepsilon}(s)-\mathbf{1}_{[0,T]}(s)|ds\leq s
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\psi_{T,\varepsilon}(t)g_{r}(\bar{\chi}^{N,n}(t))\,dt\,\mid\,\mathcal{A}_{N}\right]\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{{d}}}}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\psi_{T,\varepsilon}(t)g_{r}(\chi^{n}(t))\,dt\,\mid\,Pi\right].

By letting
\varepsilon\to 0
we obtain

\tau^{n,r,T}(\bar{\chi}^{N,n})\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{{d}}}}\,\tau^{n,r,T}(\chi^{n}).

Finally, monotone convergence as
T\to\infty
yields joint convergence of
(\tau^{n,r})_{1\leq r\leq s}
under the quenched laws.
∎

5.2.1
The site-frequency spectrum under nearly complete selfing

In light of the importance of the SFS and other measures of genetic variation for inferring past events and processes affecting populations, it would be of some interest to go beyond the convergence result in Proposition
5.4
and to describe expected patterns of variation for a range of specific diploid population models conditional on the pedigree. We leave this to future research, as it is outside the scope of the present work, but we note that a key question is the extent to which the SFS and other measures of genetic variation depend on the pedigree.

Here we illustrate this using simulations of the (robust) conditional limit described in Theorem
5.2
, namely, the coalescing random walk on the ancestral recombination graph (ARG).
In this limit, the ARG representing all the information of the pedigree relevant to the sample is generated by an EFC in which every distinct pair of ancestral lineages coalesce with rate
2
and every single lineage split at rate
\lambda
. After an exponentially distributed waiting time with rate parameter
n(\lambda+n-1)
, one of these
\binom{n}{2}+n
events is chosen in proportion to its rate. If two lineages coalesce, the number of ancestral lineages increases by one. If a lineage splits, the number of ancestral lineages increases by one. All events and their times are recorded, and the process stops the first time there is just a single ancestral lineage.

Note that the marginal coalescent process for the sample is a Kingman coalescent process, which does not depend on
\lambda
. However, the time it takes to reach a single lineage in the EFC process when
\lambda
is large may far exceed any reasonable time for any coalescing random walk on the resulting ancestral graph to reach its most recent common ancestor. Thus, there should be little error in stopping the EFC process before it reaches a single lineage. The way time is measured here, there would be only a
e^{-20}\approx 2\times 10^{-9}
chance of a pairwise coalescence time greater than
10
in the marginal process. In our simulations, we set the splitting rate to zero at
10
units of time, so the remainder of the graph is a single Kingman coalescent tree, which we note preserves the marginal distribution of the process.

The gene genealogy at a locus is generated by tracing ancestral lineages backward in time through the graph, as in the presentation of Figure
4
. When a lineage encounters a split, it follows one or the other ancestral line with equal probability,
1/2
. When two lineages meet at a coalescent event in the graph, they necessarily coalesce because
\alpha_{N}\to 1
under limited outcrossing. Unlinked loci have independent gene genealogies, conditional on the graph. Figure
5
displays simulation results for the SFS for six different values of
\lambda
ranging from
1000
to
0
. Each panel shows the SFS for five independently generated ancestral graphs assuming a sample of size
n=20
. Given each graph, gene genealogies of
10^{5}
unlinked loci were simulated and their values of
\tau^{n,r}
were recorded. Figure
5
plots the averages
\overline{\tau^{n,r}}
of these branch lengths over the
10^{5}
gene genealogies, normalized to sum to one, that is divided by
\sum_{r}\overline{\tau^{n,r}}
.

In Figure
5
(a), which has
\lambda=1000
, the SFS for every graph is not noticeably different than under the Kingman coalescent (not shown). We note that this is also the expectation for a fixed
\alpha_{N}=\alpha

ND 97
;
NWF 25
. A small effect of the graph (or pedigree) can be seen even when
\lambda=100

5.4

Many models of interest involve mixed demographic behavior, such as Kingman-like behavior with rare extreme events, as in

;

. Suppose that we have two different parent-child distributions
\pi_{N}^{(1)}
and
\pi_{N}^{(2)}
on
[N]\times[N]
for the Sargasyan-Wakeley model of Example
2.2
. Let
c_{N}^{(1)}
and
c_{N}^{(2)}
denote the one-step transition probabilities for two sample lineages in distinct individuals coalescing in a single time-step. Similarly, we let
d_{N}^{(1)}
and
d_{N}^{(2)}
denote the outcrossing probabilities for a single time-step for each of the two demographies. For simplicity, we assume that the selfing probabilities
\alpha_{N}^{(1)}
and
\alpha_{N}^{(2)}
for the two demographic histories are identical, i.e.
\alpha_{N}^{(1)}=\alpha_{N}^{(2)}=\alpha_{N}
. For any fixed
\rho>0

by

\pi_{N}=(1-\rho c_{N}^{(1)})\pi_{N}^{(1)}+\rho c_{N}^{(1)}\pi_{N}^{(2)}.

Finally, let
\mathcal{A}_{N}^{\rm mixed}
denote the random pedigree governed by the mixed demography
\pi_{N}
and
\bar{\chi}^{N,n}
denote the coalescent with time rescaled by
c_{N}^{(1)}
, as in Equation (
3.7
). The quenched limit of gene genealogies for the mixed demography is characterized by the following corollary.

Corollary 5.6
.

Suppose that, as
N\to\infty
,
c_{N}^{(1)}\to 0
,
\rho c_{N}^{(2)}\to\rho^{\prime}<\infty
, that
\pi_{N}^{(i)}
satisfies Assumption
3.6
and the measure on
\Delta
governing the coalescences of the demography of

is
\Xi^{(i)}
for each

\left(c_{N}^{(1)}\right)^{-1}d_{N}^{(1)}\to\lambda<\infty
, and that

. Then the random measure

\mathbb{P}\left(\bar{\chi}^{N,n}\in\cdot\,\mid\,\mathcal{A}_{N}^{\rm mixed}\right)

converges weakly in distribution to
\mathcal{L}_{\Pi}^{n}
, where
\Pi
is an EFC with characteristics
c_{k}=\Xi^{(1)}(\mathbf{0})+\rho^{\prime}\Xi^{(2)}(\mathbf{0})
,
\nu_{Coag}=\Xi^{(1)}+\rho^{\prime}\Xi^{(2)}-c_{k}\delta_{\mathbf{0}}
,

, and
\nu_{Disl}=0
.

Proof.

As the two demographies satisfy Assumption

so to does
\pi_{N}
. If

then we have that the generator of the mixed demography

Observe that consistency of generators is closed under linearity, and so

the relationship connecting infinitesimal generators on

associated to the family

\pi_{N}^{\rm BBE}=\left(1-\frac{\rho}{N(N-1)}\right)\delta_{1}\otimes\delta_{N}+\frac{\rho}{N(N-1)}\delta_{\lfloor\psi N\rfloor}\otimes\delta_{2},

(5.3)

where
\psi
is a fixed number in

. That is, with probability
1-\frac{\rho}{N^{2}}
a single time-step consists of a single reproductive event, a single child born to any one of the
N
potential parents with probability
\alpha_{N}
or to any of the

pairs of parents with probability

; that is, it behaves as a Moran model as in

. With probability
\frac{\rho}{N^{2}}
, two parents reproduce to form
\lfloor\psi N\rfloor
of the individuals in the nest time-step (going forward in time.) Let
\mathcal{A}_{N}^{\rm BBE}
denote the pedigree associated to
\pi_{N}^{\rm BBE}
.

Corollary 5.7
.

Suppose that, as
N\to\infty
, we have that
(1-\alpha_{N})N^{-1}\to\lambda<\infty
. Then the quenched law of the time-rescaled coalescent associated to
\pi_{N}^{\rm BBE}

\mathbb{P}\left(\tilde{\chi}^{N,n}\left(\lfloor t\left(c_{N}^{(1)}\right)^{-1}\rfloor\right)\in\cdot\,\mid\,\mathcal{A}_{N}^{\rm BBE}\right)

converges weakly in distribution as a random measure to
\mathcal{L}_{\Pi}^{n}
, where
\Pi
is the EFC of Example
4.3
.

Proof.

We follow the notation introduced for Corollary
5.6
, where
\pi_{N}^{(1)}=\delta_{1}\otimes\delta_{2}
and
\pi_{N}^{(2)}=\delta_{\lfloor\psi N\rfloor}\otimes\delta_{2}
. With this notation we have that

c_{N}^{(1)}=\frac{1}{N(N-1)}\quad\text{ and }c_{N}^{(2)}=\frac{\psi^{2}}{2}+o(1).

Therefore we may write Equation (
5.3
) as

\pi_{N}^{\rm BBE}=(1-\rho c_{N}^{(1)})\pi_{N}^{(1)}+\rho c_{N}^{(1)}\pi_{N}^{(2)},

and we see that
\rho c_{N}^{(2)}\to\rho\frac{\psi^{2}}{2}

Observe now that the probability of three sample lineages in three distinct individuals coalescing in a single time-step for
\pi_{N}^{(1)}
is zero. One readily checks that the assumptions of Theorem
5.2
are satisfied and hence
\Xi^{(1)}=2\delta_{\mathbf{0}}
.

We now calculate the transition-rate matrix
Q_{l}^{(2)}=\left(q^{l,2}_{\xi\eta}\right)_{\xi,\eta\in\mathcal{E}_{l}}
for
\pi_{N}^{(2)}
. It suffices to do so for
q_{\xi_{0}^{l}\eta}^{l}
for a fixed
l\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
by exchangeability. Observe firstly that if
\eta
consists of at least three non-singleton blocks, then we immediately have that
q_{\xi_{0}^{l}\eta}^{l}=0
. This is simply because their are precisely two parents under
\pi_{N}^{(2)}
.

Suppose now that
\eta
consists of
b
blocks
C_{,}C_{2},...,C_{b}
of sizes
k_{1},k_{2},\ldots,k_{b}
, at most two of which are non-singletons. Where
s
is the number of singletons, we write that
\eta
is of type
b;k_{1},\ldots,k_{r};s
, as in
Sch 00
. We only need to consider the cases now where
s\geq b-2
. Observe that the probability that any of the
l
individuals containing a sample lineage is one of the two parents is
o(1)
, which will be negligible for
\pi_{N}^{(2)}
. We proceed now assuming that
k_{1}\geq 2
and that
k_{1}\geq k_{2}\geq\ldots\geq k_{b}
without loss of generality.

For any
j
of the
l
sample lineages, the probability that they are the child of one of the two parents is
\psi^{j}(1-\psi)^{l-j}+o(1)
. If
k_{2}\geq 2
, then there is only one way for
\eta
to appear, by
k_{1}
sample lineages falling into one parent,
k_{2}
in the other, and the remaining
l-k_{1}-k_{2}
remaining sample lineages not being children. Therefore

q^{l,2}_{\xi_{0}^{l}\eta}=2^{1-k_{1}-k_{2}}\psi^{k_{1}+k_{2}}(1-\psi)^{l-k_{1}-k_{2}}+o(1).

(5.4)

The factor of
2
comes from the
k_{1}+k_{2}
sample lineages sorting themselves into the right parents, where the order of the parents does not matter.

If
k_{2}=1
, then
k_{1}
sample lineages fall into one of the two parents. It is possible that exactly one or 0 of the remaining
l-k_{1}
sample lineages fall into the other parent. Therefore

q^{l,2}_{\xi_{0}^{l}\eta}=2\left(\frac{\psi}{2}\right)^{k_{1}}\left(\frac{\psi}{2}(1-\psi)^{l-k_{1}-1}+(1-\psi)^{l-k_{1}}\right)+o(1)=2^{1-k_{1}}(1-\psi)^{l-k_{1}}\frac{2-\psi}{2-2\psi}+o(1).

(5.5)

Note that the first term in the summand comes from one of the
l-k_{1}
remaining sample lineages falling into one of the two parents, while the second term comes from calculating that none of the remaining sample lineages do.

Finally, we calculate
q^{l,2}_{\xi_{0}^{l}\xi_{0}^{l}}
. There can be zero, one, or two of the
l
sample lineages that enter the two parents. Zero lineages entering the two parents occurs with probability
(1-\psi)^{l}+o(1)
. There are
\binom{l}{1}
ways for
1
sample lineage to be in one of the two parents, and so this occurs with probability
\binom{l}{1}\psi(1-\psi)^{l-1}+o(1)
. There are
\binom{l}{2}
ways for
2
sample lineages to be in the two parents, and in this case they do not coalesce together with probability
\frac{1}{2}
. Therefore

q^{l,2}_{\xi_{0}^{l}\xi_{0}^{l}}=(1-\psi)^{l}+l(1-\psi)^{l-1}\psi+\frac{l(l-1)}{4}(1-\psi)^{l-2}\psi^{2}+o(1).

(5.6)

Combining Equations (
5.4
), (
5.5
), and (
5.6
) shows that
Q_{n}^{(2)}
corresponds exactly to the measure
\Xi^{(2)}=\delta_{\left(\frac{\psi}{2},\frac{\psi}{2},0,\ldots\right)}
. The result then follows from Corollary
5.6
.

Characterization of weak convergence in distribution of random measures on
\mathcal{D}\left(\mathbb{R}_{+},E\right)

Possible references for what follows are

;

;

.

To prove Theorem

we require a characterization of weak convergence in distribution of random measures. Following
(
Kal 17
, Theorem 4.19, pp. 111, 126)
, we provide the following definition of weak convergence in distribution for random probability measures.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
\mu
and
(\mu_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
denote random probability measures on a Polish space
S
. Then
\mu_{N}
converges weakly in distribution to
\mu
if for any continuous
f
of bounded support that

\mu_{N}(f)\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{{d}}}}\,\mu(f).

When this convergence holds we write
\mu_{N}\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{{wd}}}}\,\mu
.

We provide in this section sufficient criteria to characterize weak convergence in distribution when the Polish space
S
in question is precisely
\mathcal{D}\left(\mathbb{R}_{+},E\right)
with the
J_{1}
topology, where
E
is a locally compact Polish space. The basic strategy is to construct a suitable dense family in
C_{b}\left(\mathcal{D}\left(\mathbb{R}_{+},E\right)\right)
and then to devise criteria by which evaluations our random measures against this suitable class of test functions converge.

6.1
A dense family in
C_{b}\left(\mathcal{D}\left(\mathbb{R}_{+},E\right)\right)

Suppose that

is a locally compact Polish space. Then
\mathcal{D}\left(\mathbb{R}_{+},E\right)
is a Polish space under the
J_{1}
topology
(
EK 09
, Theorem 5.6, p.121)
. We shall describe a dense subset of
C_{b}\left(\mathcal{D}\left(\mathbb{R}_{+},E\right)\right)
in Lemma
6.4
below when
C_{b}\left(\mathcal{D}\left(\mathbb{R}_{+},E\right)\right)
is given the topology of uniform convergence on compacta. To this end, we first describe an explicit continuous function on
\mathcal{D}\left(\mathbb{R}_{+},E\right)
.

Fix
\varphi\in C_{c}\left(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times E\right)
and
a\in\mathbb{R}
, and define the function
I(\varphi,a):\,\mathcal{D}\left(\mathbb{R}_{+},E\right)\to\mathbb{R}
by

I(\varphi,a)(x):=a+\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi(s,x(s))ds.

Here, for any topological space
X
we take
C_{c}(X)
to denote the continuous functions from
X
into
\mathbb{R}
with compact support.

Lemma 6.2
.

For any
\varphi\in C_{c}\left(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times E\right)
and
a\in\mathbb{R}
,
I(\varphi,a)
is a bounded and continuous function on
\mathcal{D}\left(\mathbb{R}_{+},E\right)
.

Proof.

We need to show that for any sequence
x_{N}
in
\mathcal{D}\left(\mathbb{R}_{+},E\right)
converging to a path
x
therein that
I(\varphi,a)(x_{N})
converges to
I(\varphi,a)(x)
.

As
\varphi
has compact support, there exists a finite
T
in
\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that

. Therefore

I(\varphi,a)(x_{N})=a+\int_{0}^{T}\varphi(s,x_{N}(s))ds.

As
x
has at most countably many points of discontinuity
(
EK 09
, Lemma 5.1)

\lim_{N\to\infty}x_{N}(s)=x(s)
for all continuity points of

converges to

almost surely on

. Therefore, by continuity

converges to

|\varphi(s,x_{N}(s))|\leq\left|\left|\varphi\right|\right|_{\infty}\mathbbm{1}_{[0,T]}(s)
, the sequence of functions

is dominated by an integrable function. The dominated convergence theorem therefore gives the convergence.
∎

We now define the collection
\mathcal{I}:=\{I(\varphi,a):\,\varphi\in C_{c}\left(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times E\right),\,a\in\mathbb{R}\}

Lemma 6.3
.

For any locally compact Polish space

,

separates points in
\mathcal{D}\left(\mathbb{R}_{+},E\right)
.

Proof.

If

, then there is a continuity point

of both

and

such that either

. Because

is locally compact and Hausdorff, there exist disjoint open neighborhoods with compact closure

and

of

and

, which exists by Urysohn’s lemma.

As

is a continuity point, there is a

and

satisfy
\psi_{|[t_{0}-\frac{1}{2}\delta,t_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\delta]}\equiv 1

Define
\varphi
by

\varphi(s,e):=\psi(s)\theta(e).

\varphi
clearly belongs to

. Further

\displaystyle I(\varphi,0)(x)>0=I(\varphi,0)(y),

so

separates

and

separates points of
\mathcal{D}\left(\mathbb{R}_{+},E\right)

∎

We now define

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\prod_{j=1}^{k_{i}}I_{ij},

where each

and

. An extension of this class of continuous, bounded integral functionals will, by an application of Stone-Weierstrass

[Chapter 7, p. 11], be dense in

Lemma 6.4
.

Suppose that

is locally compact and Polish. Then

is dense in

in the topology of uniform convergence on compacta.

Proof.

To show that
\bar{\mathcal{I}}
is dense in

in the topology of uniform convergence on compacta, we need to show that for any function

in

, any
\varepsilon>0
, and any compact set

in
\bar{\mathcal{I}}
such that

\sup_{e\in K}|f(e)-I(e)|<\varepsilon.

We begin by observing that
\bar{\mathcal{I}}
contains constants; take
\varphi\equiv 0
and

to be free.
\bar{\mathcal{I}}
also is closed under addition, multiplication, and scalar multiplication, which is immediate. By Lemma

\mathcal{I}

Fix any compact set

and any

. The restriction of elements of

to

of continuous functions from

that contains the constants and separates points. By the Stone-Weierstrass theorem for compact Hausdorff spaces

6.2
A characterization of weak convergence in distribution

We show by means of a density argument that it suffices to check, for weak convergence in distribution of random measures, joint convergence in distribution when testing against integral functionals, as described by the following Theorem.

Theorem 6.5
.

Let
\mu_{N}
denote a sequence of random measures in
\mathcal{M}_{1}\left(\mathcal{D}\left(\mathbb{R}_{+},E\right)\right)
for a locally compact, Polish space
E
. Then
\mu_{N}
converges weakly in distribution to
\mu
if and only if for any finite collection
\{\varphi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
of elements in
C_{c}\left(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times E\right)
that

\left(\mu_{N}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\varphi_{i}(s,x(s))ds\right)\right)_{1\leq i\leq k}\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{{d}}}}\,\left(\mu\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\varphi_{i}(s,x(s))ds\right)\right)_{1\leq i\leq k}.

(6.1)

Proof.

From Definition
6.1
it suffices to show that for any
f
in
C_{b}\left(\mathcal{D}\left(\mathbb{R}_{+},E\right)\right)
that

\mu_{N}(f)\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{{d}}}}\,\mu(f).

(6.2)

By Lemma
6.4
it suffices, without loss of generality, to demonstrate (
6.2
) for
f
in
\bar{\mathcal{I}}
. For any element
f
in
\bar{\mathcal{I}}
there is an array of elements
\left(I_{ij}\right)_{i\in[n],j\in[k_{i}]}
of
\mathcal{I}
such that

f=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\prod_{j=1}^{k_{i}}I_{ij}.

Linearity of expectation yields

\mu_{N}(f)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{N}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{k_{i}}I_{ij}\right).

(6.3)

By assumption,
\mu_{N}(\left(I_{ij}\right)_{i\in[n],j\in[k_{i}]})
converges in distribution to
\mu(\left(I_{ij}\right)_{i\in[n],j\in[k_{i}]})
. As polynomials are continuous and continuity preserves convergence in distribution, (
6.3
) converges in distribution to

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu\left(\prod_{j=1}^{k_{i}}I_{ij}\right)=\mu(f),

which gives the claim.

We now prove the necessity of Equation (
6.1
). By Lemma
6.2
the integral functionals are all continuous. The continuous mapping theorem then gives the claim.

∎

The characterization of weak convergence in distributions of random measures on
\mathcal{D}\left(\mathbb{R}_{+},E\right)
for locally compact and Polish
E
given by Theorem

allows one to give classical-like characterizations of this form of weak convergence, as typified by the following following.

Definition 6.6

(Weak convergence in finite-dimensional distribution)
.

Let
\mu
,
(\mu_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
denote random probability measures over the space
\mathcal{D}\left(\mathbb{R}_{+},E\right)

\displaystyle\pi_{\vec{t}}:\mathcal{D}\left(\mathbb{R}_{+},E\right)

\displaystyle\to E^{k}

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\mapsto\left(x(t_{i})\right)_{1\leq i\leq k}

\left(\pi_{\vec{t}}\right)_{*}\mu_{N}\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{{wd}}}}\,\left(\pi_{\vec{t}}\right)_{*}\mu.

We know define an analogue of the compact containment condition for random measures on Skorokhod space
(

Definition 6.7

Let
\mu_{N}
denote a sequence of random measures on

\mathcal{D}\left(\mathbb{R}_{+},E\right)
.

Theorem 6.8
.

Suppose
\mu
is a random measure on
\mathcal{D}\left(\mathbb{R}_{+},E\right)
, where
E
is a locally compact Polish space. Suppose now that
\mu_{N}
is a sequence of random measures on
\mathcal{D}\left(\mathbb{R}_{+},E\right)
such that

•

for any finite collection of times
\left(t_{i}\right)_{1\leq i\leq k}
we have that
\left(\pi_{\vec{t}}\right)_{*}\mu_{N}\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{{wd}}}}\,\left(\pi_{\vec{t}}\right)_{t}\mu
,

•

and
\mu_{N}
satisfies the compact containment condition for random measures from Definition
6.7
,

Then
\mu_{N}\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{{wd}}}}\,\mu
.

Proof.

By Theorem

it suffices to show that, for any finite collection
\left(\varphi_{i}\right)_{1\leq i\leq k}
of elements of

that

\left(\mu_{N}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi_{i}(s,x(s))ds\right)\right)_{1\leq i\leq k}\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{{d}}}}\,\left(\mu\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi_{i}(s,x(s))ds\right)\right)_{1\leq i\leq k}.

As each
\varphi_{i}
is compactly supported, there exists

such that

a compact set in

\mathbb{P}\left(\mu_{N}\left(x([0,T])\subset K(\eta,T)\right)\geq 1-\eta\right)\geq 1-\rho

(6.4)

for all sufficiently large

.

Since

is continuous on

For all other

(6.6)

Combining (

) yields

\left|\int_{0}^{T}\varphi_{i}\left(s,x(s)\right)-\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\left(t_{j+1}-t_{j}\right)\varphi_{i}(t_{j},x(t_{j}))\right|\leq T\varepsilon+2T\left|\left|\varphi_{i}\right|\right|_{\infty}\mathbbm{1}_{\{x([0,T])\not\subset K(\eta,T)\}}

\mu_{N}\left(\left(\left|\int_{0}^{T}\varphi_{i}\left(s,x(s)\right)-\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\left(t_{j+1}-t_{j}\right)\varphi_{i}(t_{j},x(t_{j}))\right|\right)_{1\leq i\leq k}\in\cdot\right)\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{{wd}}}}\,\delta_{0}

As
\left(\pi_{\vec{t}}\right)_{*}\mu_{N}\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{{wd}}}}\,\left(\pi_{\vec{t}}\right)_{*}\mu
, the vector of Riemann sums satisfies

\left(\pi_{\vec{t}}\right)_{*}\mu_{N}\left(\left(\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\left(t_{j+1}-t_{j}\right)\varphi_{i}(t_{j},x(t_{j}))\right)_{1\leq i\leq k}\in\cdot\right)\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{{wd}}}}\,\left(\pi_{\vec{t}}\right)_{*}\mu\left(\left(\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\left(t_{j+1}-t_{j}\right)\varphi_{i}(t_{j},x(t_{j}))\right)_{1\leq i\leq k}\in\cdot\right).

This gives the claim.

Remark 7.7
.

Note that a sequence

belongs to
O(b_{N})
if there exists a constant

for all

\{a_{N}\}
belongs to

if
\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{a_{N}}{b_{N}}=0
. Note that

and

consist of collections of real-valued sequences and so set-valued inclusions between

if

.

Proof.

Observe that the probability of either an outcrossing or a coalescence of the
l
nodes is bounded above by
2c_{N}\binom{l}{2}+ld_{N}
. By Assumption
3.4
, the probability of neither a coalescence nor an outcrossing is
1-l^{2}O(c_{N})
. This gives the first line.

Now we show that the probability that two outcrossings occur in the same time-step occurs with negligible probability, implying line 3. Indeed, the probability that two outcrossings occur is bounded by the probability that any of the
\binom{l}{2}
possible pairs of particles are both children and both are the result of outcrossing. The probability that any such pair consists of two children is
\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{K_{N}(K_{N}-1)}{N(N-1)}\right]
. The probability that they both are the result of outcrossing is
(1-\alpha_{N})^{2}
. Therefore the probability of at least two outcrossings is at most

\binom{l}{2}(1-\alpha_{N})^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{K_{N}(K_{N}-1)}{N(N-1)}\right]\leq\binom{l}{2}d_{N}(1-\alpha_{N}).

This, together with the fact that
\alpha_{N}\to 1
(by Assumption
3.4
), implies that the probability of at least two outcrossings is bounded by
\binom{l}{2}o(d_{N})
. This gives the third line.

For any
\eta\in\mathcal{E}_{n}
not equal to
\xi_{0}^{l}
, the probability in one time-step that there is any coalescence of the
l
nodes in the graph with structure
\eta
and without outcrossing is

which gives line
5
.

We now show that an outcrossing occurs during a coalescence with negligible probability, implying the fourth line.
As
\xi_{0}^{l}
consists of particles in
l

\eta
and an outcrossing is therefore
O((1-\alpha_{N})q^{N,l}_{\xi\eta})\subset o(c_{N})
. This gives the fourth line.

Appendix Appendix A
Convergence Criteria for the diploid model with selfing and overlapping generations

To provide convergence criteria for our diploid exchangeable model with selfing and overlapping generations, we proceed along the lines of
[
37
]
and
[
10
,
1
]
. We define the offspring count
V_{i}
for the
i
th individual to count the number of genetic descendents among the
2N
genes in the next time-step that can trace their lineage back to the
i
th individual. Formally, we have

V_{i}=\sum_{j}V_{i,j}+2V_{i,i}.

Selfing counts twice as each individual contains two sample lineages. Denote by

V_{(1)}\geq V_{(2)}\geq\ldots\geq V_{(N)}

the ranked version of the total offspring numbers
\left(V_{i}\right)_{1\leq i\leq N}
and by

\Phi_{N}:=\mathscr{L}\left(\frac{V_{(1)}}{2N},\frac{V_{(2)}}{2N},\ldots,\frac{V_{(N)}}{2N},0,0,\ldots\right)

the law of the ranked offspring frequencies, viewed as a probability measure on the infinite ordered simplex
\Delta
.

Lemma Appendix A.1
.

Suppose, as
N\to\infty
, that Assumption
3.3
holds and that

\frac{1}{2c_{N}}\Phi_{N}(dx)\to\frac{1}{\left\langle x,x\right\rangle}\Xi^{\prime}(dx)

(Appendix A.1)

vaguely on
\Delta\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
, where
\Xi^{\prime}
is a probability measure on
\Delta
. Then Assumption
3.6
holds where, for each
n\geq 2
,
Q_{n}
is the generator of an
n
-
\Xi
-coalescent with
\Xi=2\Xi^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathit{P}_{N,n}=\left(\mathit{p}_{\xi\eta}^{N,n}\right)_{\xi,\eta\in\mathcal{E}_{n}}
denote the one-step transition matrix of the ancestral process after applying the haploid map
F_{\rm hap}
of Equation (
3.10
) so that

\mathit{p}_{\xi\eta}^{N,n}=\mathbb{P}\left(F_{\rm hap}(\chi^{N,n}(1))=\eta\,\mid\,\chi^{N,n}(0)=\xi\right).

To prove the claim we need to show that
c_{N}^{-1}\left(\mathit{P}_{N,n}-I\right)
converges to a matrix
Q_{n}
that is the generator of an
n
-
\Xi
-coalescent with
\Xi=2\Xi^{\prime}
.

Without loss of generality, consider
\xi=\xi_{0}^{b}
for some
1\leq b\leq n
, and let
\eta
be of
(k_{1},\ldots,k_{r};s)
-type. That is,
\eta
is obtained from
\xi_{0}^{b}
by keeping
s
of the blocks as singletons and coalescing the remaining
b-s
blocks into
r
blocks of sizes
k_{1},\ldots,k_{r}
. Fix
\varepsilon>0
and define
A_{\varepsilon}:=\{x\in\Delta:\langle x,x\rangle>\varepsilon\}
, with complement
A_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{c}}:=\Delta\setminus A_{\varepsilon}
. We decompose

Q_{N,n}=c_{N}^{-1}\left(\mathit{P}_{N,n}-I\right)=\Phi_{N}(A_{\varepsilon})\,Q_{N,n}^{\varepsilon}+\Phi_{N}(A_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{c}})\,Q_{N,n}^{\varepsilon,\mathrm{c}},

(Appendix A.2)

where
Q_{N,n}^{\varepsilon}
and
Q_{N,n}^{\varepsilon,\mathrm{c}}
are the conditional transition matrices given
x\in A_{\varepsilon}
and
x\in A_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{c}}
, respectively. By Assumption (
Appendix A.1
),

\frac{\Phi_{N}(A_{\varepsilon})}{2c_{N}}\to\int_{A_{\varepsilon}}\frac{1}{\langle x,x\rangle}\,\Xi^{\prime}(dx),

and hence
\Phi_{N}(A_{\varepsilon})\to 0
and
\Phi_{N}(A_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{c}})\to 1
as
N\to\infty
by Assumption
3.3
.

For
x\in A_{\varepsilon}
, denote by

\Phi_{b;(k_{1},\ldots,k_{r};s)}(x):=\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}\ \mathrm{distinct}}\Big(\prod_{j=1}^{r}x_{i_{j}}^{k_{j}}\Big)\,T^{(r)}_{r,s}(x_{i_{1}},\ldots,x_{i_{r}}),

the probability that
b
blocks thrown uniformly on
[0,1]
with a paintbox
x
form a partition of type
k_{1},\ldots,k_{r};s
. Here
T^{(r)}_{r,s}
is the inclusion–exclusion term enforcing that the
s
singletons fall into distinct intervals from
[
10
, p. 41, Appendix A]
. Then, conditional on
x
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(F_{\rm hap}(\chi^{N,n}(1))=\eta\mid x,\chi^{N,n}(0)=\xi_{0}^{b}\right)=\Phi_{b;(k_{1},\ldots,k_{r};s)}(x)+\Delta_{N}(x),\qquad\text{with }|\Delta_{N}(x)|\leq\frac{K_{b}}{N}.

That
|\Delta_{N}(x)|
is
O(\frac{1}{N})
follows simply from the observation that the probability of the
n\ll N\sum_{i}x_{i}
sample lineages falling into each of the intervals of
x
are asymptotically independent, with corrections at worst of order
\frac{1}{N}
, which can be uniformly controlled by compactness of
\Delta
.
Therefore,

\frac{1}{c_{N}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{A_{\varepsilon}}\,\mathbb{P}\left(F_{\rm hap}(\chi^{N,n}(1))=\eta\mid x,\chi^{N,n}(0)=\xi_{0}^{b}\right)\right]=\frac{1}{c_{N}}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{A_{\varepsilon}}\,\Phi_{b;(k_{1},\ldots,k_{r};s)}(x)\right]+o(1),

since the
\frac{1}{N}
coupling error is multiplied by
\Phi_{N}(A_{\varepsilon})/c_{N}=O_{\varepsilon}(1)
. Here
O_{\varepsilon}(1)
simply means that, the term is
O(1)
for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
. By Assumption (
Appendix A.1
), vague convergence on
\Delta\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
yields

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{c_{N}}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{A_{\varepsilon}}\,\Phi_{b;(k_{1},\ldots,k_{r};s)}(x)\right]=2\int_{A_{\varepsilon}}\frac{\Phi_{b;(k_{1},\ldots,k_{r};s)}(x)}{\langle x,x\rangle}\,\Xi^{\prime}(dx),

and letting
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
by monotone convergence gives

2\int_{\Delta\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}}\frac{\Phi_{b;(k_{1},\ldots,k_{r};s)}(x)}{\langle x,x\rangle}\,\Xi^{\prime}(dx),

which coincides with the transition for
\Xi\!\restriction_{\Delta\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}}=2\Xi^{\prime}
.

For
x\in A_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{c}}
we have
\langle x,x\rangle\leq\varepsilon
, hence
\max_{i}x_{i}\leq\|x\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon^{1/2}
. Then

\sum_{i}x_{i}^{3}\;\leq\;(\max_{i}x_{i})\sum_{i}x_{i}^{2}\;\leq\;\varepsilon^{1/2}\sum_{i}x_{i}^{2},\qquad\Big(\sum_{i}x_{i}^{2}\Big)^{2}\;\leq\;\varepsilon\,\sum_{i}x_{i}^{2}.

Consequently,

\mathbb{P}(\text{non-binary}\mid x)\;\leq\;C_{b}\!\left(\sum_{i}x_{i}^{3}+\Big(\sum_{i}x_{i}^{2}\Big)^{2}\right)\;\leq\;C_{b}\big(\varepsilon^{1/2}+\varepsilon\big)\sum_{i}x_{i}^{2}\;\leq\;2C_{b}\,\varepsilon^{1/2}\sum_{i}x_{i}^{2}

for some constant
C_{b}
depending only on
b
. Therefore, using
\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{c_{N}}\mathbb{E}\!\big[\sum_{i}x_{i}^{2}\big]=2\Xi^{\prime}(\Delta\setminus{\{\mathbf{0}}\})
,

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{c_{N}}\,\mathbb{E}\!\big[\mathbf{1}_{A_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{c}}}\mathbb{P}(\text{non-binary}\mid x)\big]\;\leq\;2C_{b}\,\varepsilon^{1/2}\,\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{c_{N}}\mathbb{E}\!\Big[\sum_{i}x_{i}^{2}\Big]\;=\;4C_{b}\Xi^{\prime}(\Delta\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\})\,\varepsilon^{1/2}.

In particular,

\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\;\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{c_{N}}\,\mathbb{E}\!\big[\mathbf{1}_{A_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{c}}}\mathbb{P}(\text{non-binary}\mid x)\big]=0.

For any unordered pair
\{a,b\}
,

\mathbb{P}(\{a,b\}\text{ coalesce}\mid x)=\sum_{i}x_{i}^{2}+O\!\Big(\sum_{i}x_{i}^{3}\Big),

and on
A_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{c}}
the error satisfies

\frac{1}{c_{N}}\,\mathbb{E}\!\big[\mathbf{1}_{A_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{c}}}\sum_{i}x_{i}^{3}\big]\;\leq\;\varepsilon^{1/2}\,\frac{1}{c_{N}}\,\mathbb{E}\!\Big[\sum_{i}x_{i}^{2}\Big]\;\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{}\;2\Xi^{\prime}(\Delta\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\})\,\varepsilon^{1/2}.

Thus

\frac{1}{c_{N}}\,\mathbb{E}\!\big[\mathbf{1}_{A_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{c}}}\mathbb{P}(\{a,b\}\text{ coalesce}\mid x)\big]=\frac{1}{c_{N}}\,\mathbb{E}\!\big[\mathbf{1}_{A_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{c}}}\sum_{i}x_{i}^{2}\big]+o_{\varepsilon}(1)

with
o_{\varepsilon}(1)\to 0
as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
uniformly in
N
. Decomposing,

\frac{1}{c_{N}}\,\mathbb{E}\!\big[\mathbf{1}_{A_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{c}}}\sum_{i}x_{i}^{2}\big]=\frac{1}{c_{N}}\,\mathbb{E}\!\Big[\sum_{i}x_{i}^{2}\Big]-\frac{1}{c_{N}}\,\mathbb{E}\!\big[\mathbf{1}_{A_{\varepsilon}}\sum_{i}x_{i}^{2}\big].

By the normalization and the vague convergence assumption (
Appendix A.1
),

\frac{1}{c_{N}}\,\mathbb{E}\!\Big[\sum_{i}x_{i}^{2}\Big]\;\to\;2\,\Xi^{\prime}(\Delta\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}),\qquad\frac{1}{c_{N}}\,\mathbb{E}\!\big[\mathbf{1}_{A_{\varepsilon}}\sum_{i}x_{i}^{2}\big]\;\to\;2\,\Xi^{\prime}(A_{\varepsilon}),

hence

\frac{1}{c_{N}}\,\mathbb{E}\!\big[\mathbf{1}_{A_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{c}}}\sum_{i}x_{i}^{2}\big]\;\to\;2\,\Xi^{\prime}(\Delta\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\})-2\,\Xi^{\prime}(A_{\varepsilon})\xrightarrow[\varepsilon\downarrow 0]{}\;0.

By the normalization so that all binary mergers occur at rate
2c_{N}
, the remaining
2-2\Xi^{\prime}(\Delta\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\})
mass is concentrated on binary coalescences, which gives the Kingman part of the
\Xi
-coalescent.

Combining the contributions from
A_{\varepsilon}
and
A_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{c}}
and setting
q_{n}(\xi,\xi)=-\sum_{\eta\neq\xi}q_{n}(\xi,\eta)
, we conclude that

c_{N}^{-1}\left(\mathit{P}_{N,n}-I\right)\ \to\ Q_{n},

where
Q_{n}
is the generator of an
n
-
\Xi
-coalescent with
\Xi=2\Xi^{\prime}
. This completes the proof.
∎

Remark Appendix A.2
.

[
10
, Theorem 1.1]
establishes that,
if Assumption
3.3
and
(
Appendix A.1
) hold, then the rescaled ancestral process converges to the
\Xi
-coalescent
with
\Xi=2\Xi^{\prime}\circ\varphi^{-1}
, where
\varphi
denotes the halving map
\varphi:\Delta\to\Delta
defined by

\left(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots\right)\mapsto\left(\frac{1}{2}x_{1},\frac{1}{2}x_{1},\frac{1}{2}x_{2},\frac{1}{2}x_{2},\ldots\right).

Note that this
\Xi
(which is under no selfing) is different from the
\Xi=2\Xi^{\prime}
in the annealed convergence in Theorem
3.9
. This difference is expected because when
\alpha_{N}\not\to 1
, the annealed limit of the rescaled ancestral process will not have
Q_{n}
as its generator. The haploid map
F_{\rm hap}
in (
3.10
), which appears in the definition of
Q_{n}
, makes
Q_{n}
the generator of the limiting process where two blocks that enter the same individual always coalesce instantaneously in the limit. When
\alpha_{N}\not\to 1
, blocks of the ancestral process that enter the same individual may disperse before coalescing, and this is captured in the true limiting generator.

We refer the interested reader to
[
10
, Appendix A]
for several equivalent formulations to Equation (
Appendix A.1
).

Appendix Appendix B
Proof of the Theorem
3.9

We produce a small lemma here to show that, for our notion of weak convergence in distribution that the intensity measure map sending a random measure
\mu
to its intensity measure
\mathbb{E}\left[\mu\right]
is continuous.

Lemma Appendix B.1
.

Suppose that
\mu_{N}
is a sequence of random measures on
\mathcal{D}\left(\mathbb{R}_{+},E\right)
that converge weakly in distribution, in the sense of Definition
6.1
, and that
E
is a locally compact Polish space. Then the sequence of intensity measures
\mathbb{E}\left[\mu_{N}\right]
converge weakly in distribution to
\mathbb{E}\left[\mu\right]
when
C_{b}\left(\mathcal{D}\left(\mathbb{R}_{+},E\right)\right)
is given the topology of uniform convergence on compacta.

Proof.

Recall that for a random measure
\mu
that the intensity measure
\mathbb{E}\left[\mu\right]
is defined as the unique measure for which

\mathbb{E}\left[\mu(f)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mu\right](f)

for any
f
in
C_{b}(\mathcal{D}\left(\mathbb{R}_{+},E\right))
.

The topology on
\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathcal{D}\left(\mathbb{R}_{+},E\right))
is such that the evaluation maps
T_{f}
sending
\mu
to
\mu(f)
is continuous and bounded. By the continuous mapping theorem therefore
T_{f}(\mu_{N})=\mu_{N}(f)
converges in distribution to
T_{f}(\mu)=\mu(f)
. Because
T_{f}
is continuous and bounded, convergence in distribution implies convergence in expectation. This yields the claim.

∎

With Theorem
4.7
in hand, we are ready to prove Theorem
3.9
.

Proof.

Note that the assumptions of Theorem
3.9
are the same as those of Theorem
4.7
. Consequently, by Theorem
4.7
we have that the sequence of random measures
\mathcal{L}^{N,n}
converges weakly in distribution to
\mathcal{L}_{\Pi}^{n}
where
\Pi
is an EFC with coalescence measure
\Xi
on
\Delta
, no non-binary fragmentation, and binary fragmentation with rate
\lambda
. By Lemma
Appendix B.1
, the intensity measure mapping is continuous, and so
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{L}^{N,n}\right]=\mathbb{P}_{\xi_{0}^{n}}\left(\bar{\chi}^{N,n}\in\cdot\right)
converges weakly to
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\Pi}^{n}\right]
. It suffices, therefore, to show that
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\Pi}^{n}\right]
is the law of an
n
-
\Xi
-coalescent.

Consider a fixed sample
m\leq n
of particles located in the EFC at time
t
. Then, regardless of the position of the
m
particles, when we anneal over the EFC the times at which these
m
particles coalesce is governed by
Q_{m}
, the projection of the generator
Q
on
\mathcal{E}_{\infty}
associated to
\Xi
by Remark
3.8
. But this is precisely the definition of the generator of an
m
-
\Xi
-coalescent in its initial state. This gives the claim.

Appendix Appendix C
Combinatorics for the general model

Let
N
be fixed and consider the general diploid overlapping-generations model described at the beginning of Section
2
. At time step
0
, let
K:=K_{N}^{(0)}
,
S:=S_{N}^{(0)}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}V^{(0)}_{i,i}
,

v_{i}:=V^{(0)}_{i,i}
and
u_{i}:=\sum_{j\neq i}V^{(0)}_{i,j}
for
i\in[N]
.

Lemma Appendix C.1

(One-step triple coalescence)
.

The probability that three distinct sampled lineages from three distinct individuals at time
0

coalesce into a single ancestor in one step (to time
1
in the past) is

\displaystyle\mathfrak{c}_{3}:=\mathbb{P}_{\xi_{0}^{3}}\left(\chi^{N,n}(1)=\mathbf{1}_{3}\right)=\frac{1}{\binom{N}{3}}\,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\bigg[\frac{N-K}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\!\left(\frac{1}{4}\binom{v_{i}}{2}+\frac{1}{8}\,v_{i}u_{i}+\frac{1}{16}\binom{u_{i}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\;+\;\sum_{i=1}^{N}\!\left(\frac{1}{8}\binom{v_{i}}{3}+\frac{1}{16}\binom{v_{i}}{2}\,u_{i}+\frac{1}{32}\,v_{i}\binom{u_{i}}{2}+\frac{1}{64}\binom{u_{i}}{3}\right)\bigg].

(Appendix C.1)

Here the outer expectation is over the joint law of
(K_{N}^{(0)},V^{(0)})
;

\alpha_{N}
influences
\mathfrak{c}_{3}
only through this law and does not appear explicitly in
(
Appendix C.1
).

Proof.

Sample uniformly without replacement three distinct individuals at time
0
; there are
\binom{N}{3}

unordered choices. Condition first on the realized newborn set
B
(of size
K
), the carried-over set

~C
(of size
N-K
), and the parentage matrix
V^{(0)}
.

There are exactly two ways to obtain a one-step triple merger:

(A) Two newborns share a parent
i
and the third sampled individual is that parent
i
carried over.

Fix
i\in C
. Among the
n_{i}:=v_{i}+u_{i}
newborns that have
i
as a parent, there are

\binom{v_{i}}{2}
pairs where both are selfed by
i
,
v_{i}u_{i}
mixed pairs, and
\binom{u_{i}}{2}
pairs
with both outcrossing via
i
. Tracing one step back, to coalesce with the gene copy traced from the
carried-over
i
, each newborn must choose the same copy in
i
:
probabilities
1/2
(selfed) and
1/4
(outcrossed), independently of the carried-over copy choice.
Hence the Mendelian factors are
1/4
,
1/8
, and
1/16
, respectively. Summing over
i\in C
gives the
conditional contribution

\frac{1}{\binom{N}{3}}\sum_{i\in C}\!\left(\frac{1}{4}\binom{v_{i}}{2}+\frac{1}{8}\,v_{i}u_{i}+\frac{1}{16}\binom{u_{i}}{2}\right).

(B) Three newborns share a common parent
i
(not necessarily carried over).

For a fixed
i\in[N]
, the three newborns can be all selfed (
\binom{v_{i}}{3}
triples),
two selfed and one outcrossed (
\binom{v_{i}}{2}u_{i}
triples), one selfed and two outcrossed
(
v_{i}\binom{u_{i}}{2}
triples), or all outcrossed (
\binom{u_{i}}{3}
triples). To coalesce one step back,
all three lineages must choose the same copy in
i
, yielding Mendelian factors
1/8
,
1/16
,
1/32
,
and
1/64
, respectively. Summing over
i
gives the conditional contribution

\frac{1}{\binom{N}{3}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\!\left(\frac{1}{8}\binom{v_{i}}{3}+\frac{1}{16}\binom{v_{i}}{2}\,u_{i}+\frac{1}{32}\,v_{i}\binom{u_{i}}{2}+\frac{1}{64}\binom{u_{i}}{3}\right).

Adding (A) and (B) yields the conditional probability of triple coalescent given
V^{(0)},B,C
as

\frac{1}{\binom{N}{3}}\left[\sum_{i\in C}\!\left(\frac{1}{4}\binom{v_{i}}{2}+\frac{1}{8}\,v_{i}u_{i}+\frac{1}{16}\binom{u_{i}}{2}\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\!\left(\frac{1}{8}\binom{v_{i}}{3}+\frac{1}{16}\binom{v_{i}}{2}\,u_{i}+\frac{1}{32}\,v_{i}\binom{u_{i}}{2}+\frac{1}{64}\binom{u_{i}}{3}\right)\right].

Now average over the uniformly random carried-over set
C
of size
N-K
, and
finally, take expectation with respect to
(K_{N}^{(0)},V^{(0)})
to obtain (
Appendix C.1
).
∎

By
\xi\prec\eta
we denote that
\eta
is obtainable from
\xi
via a single binary merger of blocks of
\xi
. In the following lemma we calculate the one-step transition probabilities for non-binary mergers, and the one-step exit probability for a state
\xi
.

Lemma Appendix C.2
.

Suppose that
\mathfrak{c}_{3}\in o(c_{N})
as
N\to\infty
. Then the one-step transition probability
\mathit{p}_{\xi\eta}^{N,n}

for any
\eta
that cannot be obtained from
\xi
via a single binary merger, i.e. where
\xi\not\prec\eta
, satisfies
\mathit{p}_{\xi\eta}^{N,n}c_{N}^{-1}\to 0
. Further,
\mathit{p}_{\xi\xi}^{N,n}=1-2\binom{|\xi|}{2}c_{N}+o(c_{N})
.

Proof.

Note that there are
\binom{|\xi|}{2}
possibly binary mergers for any given
\xi
, and each is equally likely by exchangeability. Therefore, it suffices to show that the exit probability after factoring through the haploid map
F_{\rm hap}
for any
\xi
is
2c_{N}\binom{|\xi|}{2}+o(c_{N})
. This will follow by finiteness of the state space if for any
\eta
not obtainable by a single binary merger from
\xi
that the one-step transition probability from
\xi
to
\eta
is
o(c_{N})
, which we show below. We proceed by a monotonicity argument.

Let
r
denote the largest number of blocks of
\xi
that are coalesced together into a single block of
\eta
. Suppose to start that
r\geq 3
after factoring through the haploid map. For any subset of size
3
of these
r
blocks, of which there are
\binom{r}{3}
, the probability of that given subset coalescing in a single time-step is
\mathfrak{c}_{3}
. Therefore the one-step transition probability
\mathit{p}_{\xi\eta}^{N,n}
that
\eta
is obtained from
\xi
is at most
\binom{r}{3}\mathfrak{c}_{3}
, which is
o(c_{N})
by assumption. This shows that any
\eta
obtained by coalescing
r\geq 3
blocks of
\xi
into a single block happens with
o(c_{N})
probability.

Suppose then that
r=2
and that
\xi\not\prec\eta
. Then there are at least four blocks
C_{1},C_{2},C_{3},C_{4}
of
\xi
coalesced as pairs
C_{1}\cup C_{2},C_{3}\cup C_{4}
in
\eta
. We denote this transition by
\mathbb{P}\left(2,2\to 1,1\right)
. Note that any pairwise coalescence event, conditional on
V
, is bounded above by

\frac{1}{4N^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(V_{i})_{2}.

(Appendix C.2)

Therefore, where we condition on there being no triple merger, we have that

\mathbb{P}\left(2,2\to 1,1\,\mid\,V,\text{ no triple}\right)\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{1}{4N^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(V_{i})_{2}\right)^{2}\right].

By averaging over
V
we then have

\mathbb{P}\left(2,2\to 1,1\right)\leq\binom{4}{3}\mathfrak{c}_{3}+\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{16N^{4}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}(V_{i})_{2}\right)^{2}\right].

The second summand is
O(c_{N}^{2})\subset o(c_{N})
by a direct comparison, and by assumption
\mathfrak{c}_{3}
is
o(c_{N})
. This shows that when
r=2
with
\xi\not\prec\eta
that
\mathit{p}_{\xi\eta}^{N,n}\in o(c_{N})
.

Summing over the single-pair coalescence probabilities among the
\binom{|\xi|}{2}
unordered pairs yields the exit rate
2\binom{|\xi|}{2}c_{N}+o(c_{N})
, which completes the proof.

∎
