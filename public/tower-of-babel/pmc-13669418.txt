Mapping Projectome Heterogeneity of Subiculum Neuron Cell Types
Authors: Ann W. Saustad, Michael S. Bienkowski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe subiculum (SUB) is the main output structure of the hippocampus, influencing diverse behaviors through its widespread cortical and subcortical connections. Our previous work creating the mouse Hippocampus Gene Expression Atlas (HGEA) identified four genetically distinct cellular layers across five columnar domains in the SUB, with gene expression boundaries corresponding to distinct connectivity patterns and brain‐wide networks involved in spatial navigation, social behavior, and neuroendocrine regulation. Using the Digital Brain Mouse Projectome Atlas (MPA) tool, we conducted ‘virtual tract‐tracing’ to assess whether connectivity patterns of single‐neuron 3D reconstructions aligned with HGEA‐defined SUB cell types. We classified 689 SUB projection neurons into 12 HGEA cell‐type groups based on their laminar and columnar distributions, whose spatial organization recapitulated HGEA‐defined 3D boundaries. Using this population sample, we performed a SUB cell‐type census, characterized neuronal heterogeneity and projection prevalence, identified common and rare connectivity motifs and axonal collateralization patterns, and defined distinct projection themes for each SUB cell type. Together, this analysis integrates single‐neuron and population‐level data to advance understanding of SUB cell type organization and its contributions to brain‐wide networks regulating diverse behaviors.

Introduction

The subiculum (SUB) is an allocortical brain structure anatomically positioned as a major gateway for hippocampal output, influencing a diverse array of animal behaviors, from spatial navigation to goal‐directed action selection (Lee and Lee 2026). In rodents, the SUB is classically divided into dorsal and ventral subregions (SUBd and SUBv, respectively) based on its bisection by the caudal CA1 in coronal sections and lesions of these regions produce functional deficits in different behaviors (Fanselow and Dong 2010; Moser and Moser 1998). However, gene expression and anatomical tracing studies spanning the last several decades have revealed that the anatomical organization of the SUB is more complex, with both columnar and laminar organization of its neuronal cell types (Bienkowski et al. 2018; Boccara et al. 2015; Strange et al. 2014; Kishi et al. 2000).

In our previous study creating the Hippocampus Gene Expression Atlas (HGEA), we integrated gene expression and connectivity data to identify four genetically‐distinct layers of cell types distributed across five columnar regions constituting the entire mouse SUB (Bienkowski et al. 2018). Subsequent gene expression analysis in humans suggest that this columnar and laminar organization is conserved across mammalian species, albeit with divergent combinatorial gene expression patterns (Bienkowski et al. 2021). Recently, high‐throughput single neuron projectome studies, such as CEBSIT(ION) Digital Brain's Mouse Projectome Atlas (MPA), and the Janelia MouseLight Project, have provided unprecedented detail of single‐cell connectivity across the whole mouse brain (Qiu et al. 2024; Winnubst et al. 2019). These datasets offer a new opportunity to evaluate how SUB cell type connectivity at the single‐cell level maps onto the HGEA columnar and laminar framework, and to analyze connectivity‐based cell type heterogeneity and axon collateralization patterns that are critical for understanding systems‐level information processing. Using the Mouse Projectome Atlas Digital Brain (www.digital‐brain.cn), we performed virtual tract tracing experiments on 689 3D‐reconstructed MPA SUB neurons and classified each neuron into an HGEA‐defined cell type based on its soma location and projection pattern. Here, we present the results of this classification, characterize the connectivity motifs and collateralization patterns that define each SUB cell type, and discuss their implications for brain‐wide networks regulating navigation, memory, and affect.

Subiculum

Neuroanatomical Organization of the Subiculum

Early anterograde and retrograde tracing studies provided initial evidence for both a columnar and laminar organization of SUB pyramidal cell types. Anterograde injections along the longitudinal axis of the SUB revealed a topographic organization of the SUB axonal projections to the septum and hypothalamus (Swanson and Cowan 1975). Rather than forming a continuous gradient as seen in Figure 1A, injections targeting specific domains within the SUB labeled distinct but partially overlapping topographic subregions in these target areas. In rats, retrograde tracer injections into brain areas innervated by the SUB revealed laminar distributions of labeled neurons within the SUB pyramidal layer (Ishizuka 2001). Although a sublayer organization was clearly present within the SUB pyramidal layer, retrograde labeling only partially captured the full laminar organization, as tracer injection sites cannot typically encompass an entire target region while maintaining anatomical specificity. Each injection therefore reveals only those neurons projecting to the sampled target, making it difficult to determine the total number of layers and their full distribution across the SUB axis.

Complementing the anatomical retrograde labeling patterns, laminar gene expression patterns within the SUB were later identified using in situ hybridization data from the Allen Brain Atlas database (Lein et al. 2007). Genes associated with inhibitory neurons are expressed broadly and sparsely across both the SUB pyramidal and molecular layers. In contrast, genes associated with excitatory neurons and other neurochemical systems are expressed primarily within the pyramidal layer, with combinatorial laminar patterns extending across the entire SUB axis. By analyzing these combinatorial expression patterns in creating the HGEA, we identified four relatively discrete neuronal sublayers within the SUB pyramidal layer that are differentially distributed along the longitudinal axis, each delineated by a representative marker gene: Nts (SUB_1), Dlk1 (SUB_2), Teddm3 (SUB_3), and Tle4 (SUB_4) (Bienkowski et al. 2018; Figure 1B). Briefly, SUB_1 occupies a superficial position within the pyramidal layer of the distal dorsal SUB and continues to the caudal end of the SUB, where it curves ventrally and slightly back rostrally, forming a C‐shaped trajectory. SUB_2 contains the most superficial neurons of the ventral SUB, with SUB_3 occupying the layer immediately deep to SUB_2. At the caudal levels where CA1 ends, both layers extend dorsally to become adjacent to SUB_1 in the distal dorsal SUB. Here, SUB_3 but not SUB_2 continues rostrally adjacent to SUB_1, forming the superficial layer of the ProSUB. SUB_4 is the deepest layer across all SUB regions, positioned closest to the alveus white matter, and likely corresponds to the polymorphic cell layer described by Cajal, which contains pyramidal, stellate, triangular, and fusiform neurons (DeFelipe and Fariñas 1992; Ramón y Cajal 1909). SUB_4 is present across all parts of the SUB and is generally thin (1–2 cells wide) but thickens at both the dorsal and ventral tips, forming characteristic bulb‐shaped endings. In three‐dimensions, these cell type layers resemble sheets or tectonic plates that have shifted atop or beneath each other across the hippocampal axis. How these layers are represented in specific parts of the SUB is an important distinction for how regional areas of the SUB form structural/functional columns for processing hippocampal inputs and distributing encoded neural signals to downstream brain networks.

Integrating Gene Expression and Connectivity to Understand Laminar and Columnar Organization

To validate that gene expression boundaries correspond to differences in anatomical connectivity, we systematically compared HGEA gene expression patterns with retrograde tracing data from the Mouse Connectome Project (www.mouseconnectome.org). This integration revealed that each of the four SUB pyramidal layers exhibits relatively distinct projection patterns, confirming that combinatorial gene expression delineates anatomically and functionally distinct cell populations (Bienkowski et al. 2018).

Just as variation in laminar composition defines distinct cortical areas with specialized connectivity (e.g., Brodmann areas; García‐Cabezas et al. 2019), the differential distribution of SUB layers along the dorsoventral axis creates five columnar domains (Figure 1C). The distal SUB is bilaminar, containing SUB_1 and SUB_4, and extends along a curved trajectory from its rostral‐dorsal position (SUBdd), corresponding to the classical dorsal subiculum, to a caudal‐ventral extension (SUBdv), where SUB_4 forms the characteristic bulb‐shaped enlargements at the dorsal and ventral tips. The prosubiculum (ProSUB), located adjacent to CA1, is also bilaminar but composed of SUB_3 and SUB_4, consistent with the position and cellular composition of Lorente de Nó's original description (Lorente De Nó 1934; Rosenblum et al. 2024). The ventral subiculum (SUBv) has a trilaminar organization with layers SUB_2, SUB_3, and SUB_4, while its most ventral portion (SUBvv) exhibits altered layer proportions and connectivity patterns more closely resembling ProSUB than SUBv proper.

This laminar‐columnar organization reveals that the classical division of the SUB into dorsal and ventral regions, based on their separation by CA1 at rostral levels, does not accurately reflect the underlying cellular architecture (compare Figure 1A with Figure 1B,C). At caudal levels where CA1 ends, these classically defined regions become continuous, with layers SUB_2 and SUB_3 extending dorsally to merge with SUB_1 territories. The SUB is therefore better understood as two major divisions with fundamentally different laminar architectures: SUBdd/SUBdv (layers SUB_1 and SUB_4) versus ProSUB/SUBv/SUBvv (SUB_2, SUB_3, and SUB_4; layer SUB_2 absent in ProSUB). These divisions exhibit distinct brain‐wide connectivity patterns, suggesting they serve fundamentally different functional roles in transforming hippocampal output.

While the HGEA is based on both genetic and connectomic data, subsequent single‐cell RNA sequencing studies have validated and extended these gene expression patterns. Cembrowski, Phillips, et al. (2018) and Cembrowski, Wang, et al. (2018) identified structural and functional differences between proximal and distal dorsal SUB, corresponding to ProSUB and SUBdd, respectively, and delineated 8 pyramidal cell subclasses with discrete laminar and columnar spatial domains (Cembrowski, Phillips, et al. 2018; Cembrowski, Wang, et al. 2018). Alternative nomenclatures have since been proposed (Ding et al. 2020), though the precise correspondence between these frameworks and the HGEA organization remains to be fully resolved. More recently, the Allen Brain Cell (ABC) Atlas integrated single‐cell and spatial transcriptomics across the entire mouse brain, providing high‐resolution molecular definitions of SUB cell types organized into hierarchical levels: class, subclass, supertype, and cluster (Yao et al. 2023). The HGEA laminar organization aligns with intermediate levels of this hierarchy, with SUB_2 corresponding to the CA1‐ProS subclass, SUB_1 and SUB_3 to SUB‐ProS glutamatergic supertypes, and SUB_4 encompassing a combination of deep‐layer subclasses including corticothalamic and intratelencephalic glutamatergic neurons (Figure 2).

Single‐Cell Projectomes Enable Analysis of Heterogenous Projection Neuron Collateralization

Classical anatomical studies using retrograde and anterograde tracing suggested that individual SUB neurons exhibit relatively limited axonal collateralization, with most neurons projecting to a single target region (Naber and Witter 1998; O'Mara et al. 2001; Witter 2006). Using double‐retrograde labeling, Naber and Witter (1998) reported that only 0%–12% of SUB neurons were double‐labeled across all four classically defined subicular subregions, with no systematic difference between them, and concluded that subicular outputs are organized predominantly as parallel, non‐collateralized projections. This estimate stood in marked contrast to the substantially higher collateralization they observed in hippocampal CA1 (17%–39% double‐labeled) and reinforced the view that SUB projections are organized in a largely columnar and laminar fashion, with distinct neuronal populations innervating different downstream targets (Ishizuka 2001; Swanson 1981; Witter 2006). More recent double‐labeling studies have demonstrated that SUB collateralization is more prevalent than previously appreciated, with approximately half of dorsal SUB neurons projecting to the retrosplenial cortex also innervating the mammillary body (MBO), with additional populations targeting both the MBO and entorhinal cortex (ENT) (Aggleton and Christiansen 2015; Kinnavane et al. 2018). A likely source of this discrepancy is methodological. These experiments need to hit the same topographically corresponding subregion within each target to produce overlapping retrograde labeling fields within the same SUB area. As perfect overlap is technically improbable, this leads to some single‐labeled cells being mis‐identified as non‐collateralizing. Therefore, multiple retrograde labeling approaches likely undersample collateralization. Recent advances in sparse viral labeling combined with whole‐brain imaging have enabled comprehensive reconstruction of complete single‐neuron morphologies, resolving the full set of targets innervated by individual cells rather than the pairwise subsets comparisons produced by bulk retrograde double‐labeling.

Sparse viral labeling combined with whole‐brain imaging has enabled reconstruction of complete single‐neuron morphologies since the early 2010s, beginning with micro‐optical sectioning tomography (MOST) of Golgi‐stained tissue (Li et al. 2010) and its fluorescence‐based extension, fMOST (Gong et al. 2013), followed by viral‐labeling approaches such as that of Economo et al. (2016). The Janelia MouseLight project substantially scaled this approach, using serial two‐photon tomography to reconstruct over 1000 complete projection neurons across the mouse brain (Winnubst et al. 2019). Notably, their dorsal subiculum reconstructions revealed neurons projecting to multiple targets in diverse combinations that earlier pairwise retrograde studies had failed to resolve (Winnubst et al. 2019; Naber and Witter 1998), directly demonstrating the undersampling issue described above. However, because complete‐arbor reconstruction remains labor‐intensive even with high‐throughput imaging, MouseLight characterized only a modest number of subicular neurons (n = 73). Characterizing connectivity‐based heterogeneity and rare collateralization motifs at the population level instead requires reconstruction at far greater scale, from a single, uniformly registered pipeline. The CEBSIT(ION) Digital Brain MPA achieved this throughput using fluorescence micro‐optical sectioning tomography (fMOST), reconstructing over 10,000 individual neurons in the hippocampus and subicular complex (Qiu et al. 2024). This openly accessible dataset (https://mouse.digital‐brain.cn/hipp) provides complete 3D axonal morphologies at single‐cell resolution, enabling systematic re‐evaluation of SUB collateralization patterns and projection diversity across the entire HGEA laminar‐columnar framework.

Single‐cell projectome studies in the neocortex have demonstrated that projection neurons can be classified into distinct collateralization types based on their axonal targets: intratelencephalic (IT) neurons projecting bilaterally to cortex and striatum, extratelencephalic (ET) neurons with ipsilateral descending subcortical projections, and corticothalamic (CT) neurons targeting the thalamus (Mao and Staiger 2024; Harris et al. 2019; Winnubst et al. 2019). Population‐level tracing data from the HGEA suggested that the gene‐defined SUB layers exhibit projection patterns analogous to these cortical types, with the Nts‐ and Teddm3‐defined SUB_1 and SUB_3 resembling layer 5 ET neurons, the Dlk1‐defined SUB_2 exhibiting layer 2/3‐like IT connectivity, and the Tle4‐defined SUB_4 showing mixed CT/IT patterns characteristic of layer 6 (Bienkowski et al. 2018). Whether individual SUB neurons conform to these broad classes, and whether their collateralization patterns are organized systematically across HGEA layers, has not been fully resolved at the single‐cell level.

Methodology and Results

Virtual Tract Tracing to Identify and Classify HGEA SUB Cell Types at the Single‐Cell Level

To systematically characterize SUB projection patterns at single‐cell resolution and test whether individual neurons conform to HGEA layer‐specific connectivity profiles, we performed virtual tract tracing on neurons from the MPA. The MPA Digital Brain (https://mouse.digital‐brain.cn/projectome) currently contains over 45,000 publicly available single‐neuron reconstructions registered to the Allen CCFv3 reference atlas. Since the MPA dataset uses CCFv3 anatomical definitions, HGEA SUB cell types were expected to distribute across CCFv3 subiculum (SUB), prosubiculum (ProS), and potentially the hippocampo‐amygdalar transition area (HATA) subregions. According to Ding et al. (2020), the CCFv3 SUB region, whose nomenclature was established by Wang et al. (2020), should correspond primarily to HGEA SUBdd/SUBdv (containing SUB_1 and SUB_4), while CCFv3 ProS should align with HGEA ProSUB (containing SUB_3 and SUB_4).

Using retrograde labeling patterns that were found to relatively specifically label the SUB cell type layers in the HGEA (Bienkowski et al. 2018), we queried the MPA database using soma location (subiculum) and projection target filters characteristic of each layer: SUB_1 neurons projecting to ventral retrosplenial cortex (RSPv), SUB_2 neurons projecting to taenia tecta (TT), SUB_3 neurons projecting to nucleus accumbens (ACB), and SUB_4 neurons projecting to nucleus reuniens (RE). We initially restricted our search to neurons with somata in the CCFv3 SUB region, expecting to identify primarily SUB_1 and SUB_4 cell types consistent with the proposed correspondence to HGEA SUBdd/SUBdv. Instead, we identified neurons with projection patterns characteristic of all four HGEA layers within the CCFv3 SUB boundaries (Figure 2A), indicating that the CCFv3 anatomical delineations do not precisely align with the HGEA gene expression‐based framework. This discrepancy between anatomical atlases has important implications for interpreting connectivity data across studies using different coordinate systems (see Section 4). It is important to note that 946 neurons are queried when inputting subiculum‐specific somata. However, this led to the retrieval of interneurons or neurons that were not correctly registered to the subiculum region of interest, further supporting the use of subiculum‐specific projection target filters that led to a total of 670 neurons.

To comprehensively characterize SUB projection diversity, we expanded our analysis to include the CCFv3 HATA region, giving us a total of 708 neurons. Given that MPA reconstructions are registered from multiple individual brains to a 10 μm isotropic resolution atlas, some spatial imprecision is expected. We manually verified soma locations and axonal trajectories for the 708 initial neurons and discarded 19 of those neurons due to misregistration of soma or soma connection to its axon, resulting in a final total of 689 neurons that were then classified into HGEA cell types based on combined soma position and projection pattern criteria. The number of neurons and neuronal reconstruction IDs in each cell type group are noted in Table 1 and Supplementary Table S1, respectively. To resolve which targets co‐occur, we calculated the prevalence of each projection‐target combination within every HGEA cell‐type group, reporting the proportion of neurons with single‐, double‐, triple‐, and multiple‐target axons (Table 2). When visualized in 3D space, the color‐coded MPA reconstructions recapitulated the laminar and columnar organization defined by the HGEA (Figure 3A,B, Supplementary Video S1), with clear topographic axonal connectivity patterns evident for both laminar (Figure 3C) and columnar groupings (Figure 3D). For each HGEA region (Table 3) and layer (Table 4), we then quantified the percentage of neurons innervating 28 major brain structures, calculated as the number of neurons in a group that project to a target region divided by the total number of neurons in that group, multiplied by 100. A target region was counted if a neuron's axon arborized within that target's region of interest. Neurons passing through the region were not included. These percentages are visualized as radar plots for each SUB region (Figure 4) and cell‐type layer (Figure 5). To resolve which targets co‐occur within individual neurons, we additionally calculated the prevalence of each observed projection‐target combination within every HGEA cell‐type group, reporting the most frequent combinations alongside the single‐ and multiple‐target distribution (Tables 5, 6, 7). Together, these analyses enabled us to evaluate individual neuronal conformity to layer‐specific patterns and to characterize the extent of axonal collateralization across functionally distinct brain networks.

These data bridge single‐cell heterogeneity with population‐level organizational patterns, revealing both common SUB connectivity profiles and rare cell‐type specific variations within each HGEA group. Notably, these data also highlight SUB projections extending beyond classical hypothalamic targets to include substantial innervation of midbrain periaqueductal gray (PAG) and superior colliculus (SC) regions. While these descending pathways were first described by Kohler (1990), they have received limited attention in subsequent connectivity studies, and their relationship to HGEA laminar organization has not been previously characterized. In the following sections, we describe the connectivity features of each HGEA region and cell‐type layer in detail.

Dorsal Subiculum (SUBdd/dv)

Gene expression and connectivity analyses from the HGEA revealed two major cell‐type layers within both SUBdd (n = 167) and SUBdv (n = 83): SUB_1, comprising primarily IT and ET neurons, and SUB_4, comprising primarily IT and CT neurons (Figure 6A). Together, SUBdd and SUBdv constitute the bilaminar distal SUB, which extends along a curved dorsoventral trajectory from its rostral‐dorsal position, corresponding to the classical dorsal SUB as seen in Figure 1A (SUBdd) to a caudal‐ventral extension (SUBdv) where SUB_4 forms characteristic bulb‐shaped enlargements (Figure 6B). In the Allen CCFv3, this region corresponds broadly to the CCFv3 SUB subregion, though as discussed above, CCFv3 SUB boundaries do not appear to accurately align to the SUBdd/SUBdv boundaries as expected. Despite their shared bilaminar architecture, SUBdd and SUBdv exhibit complementary topographic projection patterns to shared target regions and are distinguished by their position along the hippocampal axis and differential inputs from dorsal vs. ventral CA1 regions.

Across all SUBdd and SUBdv neurons, the most common projections are to the entorhinal cortex (ENTm‐l; SUBdd = 83%, and SUBdv = 93%) and mammillary body (MBO). Specifically, 81% of SUBdd and 90% of SUBdv neurons project to the medial ENT (ENTm), whereas 25% and 34% of SUBdd and SUBdv neurons project to the lateral ENT (ENTl), respectively (Figure 6C,D, Supplementary Video S2). Furthermore, 77% of SUBdd and 80% of SUBdv neurons project to the MBO, with SUBdd neurons projecting more anterior compared to SUBdv MBO‐projecting neurons (Figure 6E). Notably, the most defining output signatures of the SUBdd and SUBdv are projections to the ventral retrosplenial cortex (RSPv), the subicular complex, including the postsubiculum (POST), presubiculum (PRE), and parasubiculum (PARA), and anterior thalamic nuclei. These projections are not only highly represented but also distributed differently across layers rather than uniformly shared, as detailed in subsections below. Notably, septo‐hypothalamic projections, which comparatively dominate the output profiles of ProSUB, SUBv, and SUBvv, are sparse in the SUBdd/dv, indicating a key connectivity distinction between the two major columnar architectures.

SUBdd/SUBdv SUB_1 Cell Types

SUB_1 neurons are predominantly ET‐class neurons, with collateralized output dominated by the MBO (SUBdd_1 = 91%, SUBdv_1 = 92%; Figure 6E) and ipsilateral RSPv (94% SUBdd_1, 89% SUBdv_1; Figure 6F). Individual SUB_1 neurons send multiple separate axon collaterals to innervate various combinations of RSPv layers 2/3, 5, and/or 6 (layer 2/3 being the primary target), with some neurons potentially sending two different collaterals to different portions of the same layer. For axon collaterals with multiple branches within a region, the bifurcation point of these projections was highly variable: some axons traveled as a single fiber before splitting within RSPv, while others bifurcated near the soma and followed separate pathways to reach the same target. In total, SUBdd_1 (n = 137) had 88% of its neurons co‐collateralize to RSPv and MBO, whereas SUBdv_1 (n = 64) had 83%, substantially exceeding the approximately half implied by dual‐retrograde tracing, consistent with that method's expected tendency to undercount collateralization (table 2; fig. 6G‐H, 7A, Kinnavane et al. 2018). An in‐depth analysis of SUBdd_1 and SUBdv_1 axon collateralization to RSPv, MBO, and ENTm‐l and to the subicular complex and area prostriata (APR) can be seen in Figure 7.

SUB_1 neurons also send collaterals to the POST (SUBdd_1 = 46%, SUBdv_1 = 28%; Figure 8A, Supplementary Video S3), PRE (SUBdd_1 = 45%, SUBdv_1 = 56%; Figure 8B), PAR (SUBdd_1 = 46%, SUBdv_1 = 55%; Figure 8C), and ENTm‐l (SUBdd_1 = 85%, SUBdv_1 = 92%). Within the ENT, SUB_1 projections were strongly biased toward the medial subdivision, with 85% of SUBdd_1 and 92% of SUBdv_1 neurons projecting to ENTm versus only 23% and 28%, respectively, to ENTl (Figure 6C,D). Nearly all ENTl‐projecting neurons also targeted ENTm (SUBdd_1, 31/32; SUBdv_1, 18/18), indicating that lateral ENT input arises predominantly as a co‐collateral of the medial projection rather than as a separate population. ENT axon collaterals were relatively short, innervating layers 5 and 6 of the caudal dorsomedial ENT (Van Groen 2001), a region containing grid cells with the highest spatial grid scale (Brun et al. 2008). Deep‐layer ENT neurons have in turn been shown to innervate superficial ENT layers (Simonsen et al. 2022), forming a feedback loop that may influence ENT‐hippocampal input connectivity. Across the subicular complex, 76% of SUBdd_1 and 80% of SUBdv_1 neurons projected to at least one of POST, PRE, or PAR, while 15% and 11%, respectively, co‐projected to all three (regardless of APR status; summed across Figure 7B). In SUBdd_1, these collateral combinations were roughly evenly distributed across categories (8%–15% each; Figure 7), whereas in SUBdv_1 projections were biased toward PRE (56%) and PAR (55%) over POST (28%; Table 3).

The area prostriata (APR), a region implicated in processing fast motion in the peripheral visual field (Mikellidou et al. 2017), was recently identified in mice and shown to receive inputs from primary visual cortex and dorsal, but not ventral, SUB (Hu et al. 2020; Lu et al. 2020). We found that 23% of SUBdd_1 and 27% of SUBdv_1 neurons projected to APR (Figure 8D), each with a clearly branched axon collateral. Because the APR in turn projects back to POST and PRE (Chen et al. 2021), these SUB_1 neurons appear to participate in a reciprocal visuospatial circuit linking the subicular complex and APR (Figure 8E). A small proportion of SUB_1 neurons also sent back projections to other hippocampal structures, most commonly CA1 (Figure 8F), with one neuron targeting the dentate gyrus (DG), typically as a short branch off the axon traveling through the alveus. Six percent of SUBdd_1 neurons projected to the CA1dc, whereas SUBdv_1 had no projections to CA1dc. In contrast, 0% of SUBdd_1 and 11% of SUBdv_1 neurons projected to CA1i/v, consistent with these subicular regions' topographical preference for input connectivity with the dorsal and ventral CA1 subregions (Bienkowski et al. 2018).

SUBdd/SUBdv SUB_4 Cell Types

SUB_4 neurons (SUBdd_4 n = 30, SUBdv_4 n = 19) are primarily classified as CT‐type neurons, with 90% of SUBdd_4 and 95% of SUBdv_4 neurons projecting to at least one anterior or midline thalamic nucleus, including the reuniens (RE), anteroventral (AV), anterodorsal (AD), anteromedial (AM), and laterodorsal (LD) nuclei. However, many of these neurons also have collateral projections to a variety of extra‐telencephalic brain regions. Retrograde tracer injections into these thalamic regions revealed that the SUB_4 of the SUBdd and SUBdv contains a specialized sublayer primarily containing the AV‐, AD‐, and LD‐projecting CT subpopulations where SUB_4 becomes thicker at its distal ends (Bienkowski et al. 2018). In contrast, the RE‐projecting subpopulations are broadly located across the deeper parts of SUB_4. To simplify the naming of these sublayers, we will refer to the deeper RE‐projecting sublayer as SUB_4.2 and the superficial sublayer containing other cell types as SUB_4.1. At the most caudal level of the SUB (HGEA 96) where only SUB_4 exists, the SUBdd and SUBdv SUB_4.1 sublayers become continuous along the border with the presubiculum (PRE).

Within the MPA dataset, RE‐projecting neurons (SUB_4.2) comprised 47% of SUBdd_4 and 26% of SUBdv_4 neurons (Figure 8G). Consistent with Bienkowski et al. (2018), the majority of RE‐projecting SUBdd_4 neurons (64%, 9/14) had no collateral projection to AV, AD, or LD, supporting RE‐projecting neurons as a largely distinct subpopulation from the superficial AV‐, AD‐, and LD‐projecting layer. A similar majority is also found in SUBdv_4 (60%, 3/5), though this rests on a small sample (n = 5 RE‐projecting neurons). Among RE‐projecting neurons, 64% of SUBdd_4 (9/14) targeted RE alone, while the remaining 36% (5/14) also had collateral projections to septo‐hypothalamic targets (LS, HY). In SUBdv_4, this pattern was reversed as every RE‐projecting neuron had a septo‐hypothalamic collateral, with none targeting RE alone (Figure 8H). In SUBdd_4, AV‐projecting neurons were the most prevalent thalamic subtype (50%; Figure 8I), and AD projections appeared almost exclusively in this column (SUBdd_4 = 20%, SUBdv_4 = 0%; Figure 8J). SUBdv_4 instead showed a broader thalamic profile, with substantial and comparable proportions of AV‐projecting (58%; Figure 8I) and AM‐projecting (58%; Figure 8K) neurons alongside the RE‐projecting population. SUB_4 neurons also projected heavily to the entorhinal cortex (ENTm‐l; SUBdd_4 = 70%, SUBdv_4 = 95%; Figure 6C,D). Beyond these targets, SUBdv_4 neurons were more extensively collateralized overall than SUBdd_4 (Table 2), including more frequent projections to the hypothalamus (42% vs. 13%; Figure 8L) and mammillary body (37% vs. 13%; Figure 6E). Both columns projected to RSPv at comparable rates (SUBdd_4 = 26.7%, SUBdv_4 = 26.3%; Figure 6F); however, SUBdv_4 projections to RSPv innervated deep cortical layers, in contrast to SUBdd_1 and SUBdv_1 neurons, which target RSPv layer 2.

Prosubiculum (ProSUB)

The ProSUB (n = 111) is a dorsal region that contains two cell‐type layers, SUB_3 (n = 59), comprising primarily ET neurons, and SUB_4 (n = 52), comprising primarily IT and CT neurons, distinguishing it from the SUBv/SUBvv by its lack of superficial SUB_2 (Figure 9A).

In the HGEA, the ProSUB first appears rostrally as the CA1/SUB border shifts lateral to the perpendicular of the dentate gyrus blade tip, positioning it proximal to SUBdd between CA1 and the distal SUB (Figure 9B). At its caudal end, the ProSUB terminates at HGEA level 91, where SUB_2 rises dorsally to meet the SUBdd border, and the region transitions into SUBv. In the Allen CCFv3, the prosubiculum is defined more broadly, encompassing nearly the entire ventral SUB area that the HGEA defines as SUBv and parts of the SUBvv, with no separate ventral area. While the ProSUB and SUBv share some similar output targets and their SUB_3 and SUB_4 cell‐type groups are anatomically continuous, we distinguish them as separate structures based on their fundamentally different laminar architectures, bilaminar (SUB_3 and SUB_4) versus trilaminar (SUB_2, SUB_3, and SUB_4), and the distinct connectivity profiles described below.

Across all ProSUB layers, the most prevalent shared projections are to the lateral septum (LS; 96%), hypothalamus (90%), ACB (81%), and MBO (79%), with substantial midbrain projections, which include the PAG and SC (43%) (Figure 9C–G, Supplementary Video S4). In striking contrast to SUBdd/dv, RSPv connectivity is nearly absent (2%), representing a fundamental shift in the cortical output profile. ENTm‐l projections are well‐represented overall (53%) but are unevenly distributed across layers (ProSUB_3 = 42%, ProSUB_4 = 65%; Figure 9H). Notably, ProSUB targets deep layers of the lateral ENT (ProSUB = 46%), which is topographically distinct from the caudal dorsomedial ENT region (ProSUB = 36%) favored by SUBdd/dv. Additional projections include the prefrontal cortex (IL/PL/TT/ORB; 27%; Figure 9I) and bed nucleus of the stria terminalis (BST; 20%; Figure 9J). Importantly, non‐canonical back projections to the hippocampus are far more prevalent in ProSUB than in SUBdd/dv: 60% of ProSUB neurons projected to dorsal CA1 and 20% to the more distant CA1i/CA1v, though these groups overlap substantially (91% of CA1i/v‐projecting neurons also project to CA1dc), such that 62% of ProSUB neurons project to at least one of these targets (Figure 9K).

ProSUB SUB_3 Cell Types

ProSUB_3 neurons are primarily ET‐class neurons and are most clearly distinguished from SUB_4 by more prevalent projections to the prefrontal cortex (36% vs. 17%; Figure 9I), amygdala (CEA/MEA = 25% vs. 2%; BLA/BMA = 14% vs. 0%; Figure 9L,M), and the perirhinal and ectorhinal cortex (PERI/ECT = 14% vs. 4%; Figure 9N). Although ACB and MBO projections are common across both layers, they are approximately 30% more prevalent in SUB_3, consistent with the ET‐class designation (ACB = 98%, MBO = 92%; Figure 9E,F).

ProSUB SUB_4 Cell Types

ProSUB_4 neurons are primarily CT‐class neurons and target the reuniens nucleus (RE) at one of the highest rates of any SUB_4 population (75%; Figure 9O), with many of these neurons also sending collateral projections to a variety of telencephalic and extra‐telencephalic regions. Unlike SUBdd/dv_4, ProSUB_4 shows a marked shift away from SUBdd/dv_4‐like anterior thalamic targeting, with very low rates of AV‐ (4%), AD‐ (0%), and LD‐projecting (0%) neurons. Instead, interanteromedial (IAM; 12%) and anteromedial (AM; 29%) thalamic projections are observed (Figure 9P). Projections to midline thalamic structures like paraventricular thalamic nucleus (PVT; 23%) and paratenial nucleus (PT; 10%; Figure 9Q) projections are also substantially higher than in SUBdd_4 (PVT = 0%, PT = 0%) or SUBdv_4 (PVT = 0%, PT = 5%). This thalamic targeting profile positions ProSUB_4 as an intermediate CT population, sharing the RE‐dominant motif common to all SUB_4 subtypes while transitioning toward the midline and paraventricular thalamic targets that characterize SUBv/SUBvv_4.

Ventral Subiculum (SUBv/SUBvv)

The SUBv (n = 273) and SUBvv (n = 55) are both trilaminar structures containing SUB_2, SUB_3, and SUB_4, distinguishing them from the bilaminar ProSUB and SUBdd/dv (Figure 10A). SUBv occupies the intermediate ventral SUB, while SUBvv represents its most ventral extent, where the relative size and shape of the layers shift as they approach the ventral tip (Figure 10B). In the Allen CCFv3, part of this ventral SUB area has been re‐designated as the hippocampal amygdala transition area (HATA). However, our recent work demonstrates that this area reflects the anatomical transition of separate CA1 and SUB laminar cell types rather than a discrete structural region (Pachicano et al. 2025). Because MPA neurons registered to the CCFv3 showed profiles consistent with SUBv/SUBvv, we assigned HATA neurons to their corresponding SUB layers and treated them as part of the SUBv/SUBvv population throughout this analysis.

Across both SUBv and SUBvv, the most prevalent shared projections are to the lateral septum (LS; SUBv = 95%, SUBvv = 89%), hypothalamus (SUBv = 84%, SUBvv = 86%), ACB (SUBv = 82%, SUBvv = 51%), and MBO (SUBv = 71%, SUBvv = 66%; Figure 10C–F, Supplementary Video S5). ENTm‐l projections are also well‐represented across both regions (SUBv = 71%, SUBvv = 73%). However, they exhibit a laminar bias as ACB‐projecting neurons are more prevalent in superficial layers (SUB_2 and SUB_3) while ENT‐projecting neurons predominate deeper layers (SUB_3 and SUB_4). Nonetheless, 46% of SUBv neurons target ENTm and 64% target ENTl, whereas 58% of SUBvv neurons target ENTm and 44% target ENTl, reversing the lateral bias seen in SUBv and ProSUB. In contrast to SUBdd/dv, RSPv projections are nearly absent across both regions, while the high prevalence of septo‐hypothalamic and limbic outputs marks SUBv/SUBvv as the primary SUB conduit to motivational and neuroendocrine networks.

SUBv/SUBvv SUB_2 Cell Types

SUBv/SUBvv_2 neurons are primarily IT‐class neurons and are uniquely distinguished from other SUB layers by two defining output features identified in the HGEA: strong projections to the anterior olfactory nucleus (AON) and local collateralization along the CA1‐SUB hippocampal axis. In the HGEA, SUB_2 neurons were characterized as a specialized population transferring information along the transverse axis from SUBvv toward SUBv, complementary to the reverse directionality seen in SUB_4 (SUBv → SUBvv). In addition, both SUB_2 and SUB_4 send strong back projections to CA1, with SUB_2 having projections to CA1i/v and SUB_4 having projections to CA1vv. Within SUB_2 specifically, back projections to CA1 were prevalent in both SUBv_2 (CA1dc = 40%, CA1i/v = 25%) and SUBvv_2 (CA1i/v = 76%), confirming non‐canonical hippocampal interconnectivity that is well‐represented by this layer (Gao et al. 2026; Xu et al. 2016, Figure 11A, Supplementary Video S6).

AON projections were strongly SUB_2‐enriched, with 68% of SUBv_2 and 24% of SUBvv_2 neurons projecting to AON (Figure 11B), compared to substantially lower rates in SUB_3 (SUBv = 25%, SUBvv = 8%) and SUB_4 (SUBv = 7%, SUBvv = 0%). The lower AON projection rate in SUBvv_2 may partly reflect sparse sampling, as AON‐projecting neurons appear clustered at the ventral tip of SUB_2. Notably, 53% of SUBv_2 neurons had collaterals that extended dorsally from AON into the prefrontal cortex (infralimbic (IL), prelimbic (PL) taenia tecta (TT), and orbitofrontal (ORB) cortex), establishing a direct SUB‐to‐prefrontal pathway. While PFC projections occur in SUB_3 and SUB_4 as well (SUBv_3 = 33%, SUBv_4 = 24%), these largely arise independently of AON (only 31% and 17% of their PFC‐projecting neurons also project to AON, versus 81% in SUBv_2), indicating this AON‐linked route is enriched in, though not exclusive to, SUB_2 (Figure 11C).

SUBv/SUBvv SUB_3 Cell Types

SUBv/SUBvv_3 neurons are primarily ET‐class neurons and are distinguished from other SUB layers by more prominent projections to amygdalar nuclei and midline thalamic nuclei. Within the amygdala, 35% and 41% of SUBv_3 neurons project to the BLA/BMA and CEA/MEA respectively (Figure 11D,E), while SUBvv_3 has substantially fewer amygdala projections (8% to both BLA/BMA and CEA/MEA). Many of these amygdala‐projecting axons can take one or both of two routes: a direct anterior projection route through the ventral SUB, or as a longer collateral branching from the fornix, traveling laterally through the hypothalamus.

Beyond the amygdala, SUBv_3 neurons also strongly target midline thalamic nuclei, which is atypical of SUB_3 cell types elsewhere in the SUB. While 41% of SUBv_3 neurons project to the RE, this decreases substantially to 17% in SUBvv_3 (Figure 11F). In contrast, both SUBv_3 (26%) and SUBvv_3 (50%) show strong projections to the PVT, indicating a pronounced bias toward SUBvv_3 for this pathway (Figure 11G). Although these thalamic projections in SUB_3 are notable, they remain less prevalent than the thalamic projections of SUBv/SUBvv SUB_4.

SUBv/SUBvv SUB_4 Cell Types

SUBv/SUBvv_4 neurons are primarily CT‐class neurons and also show the highest degree of collateralized output to the ENT of any SUB layer (Figure 10G,H). Like other SUB_4 SUB neurons, the RE is the most prevalent thalamic target, innervated by 71% of SUBv_4 and 100% of SUBvv_4 neurons (Figure 11F). SUBv/SUBvv_4 neurons also strongly collateralize to the PVT (SUBv_4 = 30%; SUBvv_4 = 20%) and PT (SUBv_4 = 33%; SUBvv_4 = 30%), with axons typically entering the PVT at its rostral end and continuing caudally at varying lengths (Figure 11G). Notably, a subset of neurons sends an axon into the PVT from the caudal end as a collateral of the ascending branch targeting the midbrain PAG/SC. Via these routes, 46% of SUBv_4 and 10% of SUBvv_4 neurons project to the midbrain (Figure 11H), a finding discussed in the midbrain section below.

Across all five HGEA‐defined SUB regions, the MPA single‐neuron reconstructions reveal both the organizing principles that unify each cell‐type group and the heterogeneity that distinguishes individual neurons within them. In the following sections, we discuss how these findings confirm, extend, and in some cases substantially revise our understanding of SUB cell type connectivity and its contributions to brain‐wide networks.

Discussion

Laminar and Columnar Organization of the SUB at Single‐Cell Resolution

Through virtual tract tracing of 689 MPA single‐neuron reconstructions, this study provides a comprehensive single‐cell validation of the laminar‐columnar framework of the SUB established by the HGEA. Individual SUB neurons exhibit projection patterns that closely match population‐level predictions based on retrograde tracing and gene expression studies (Bienkowski et al. 2018, 2021). When visualized in 3D space, connectivity‐based classification alone recapitulates the spatial organization of HGEA defined layers and columns, demonstrating that the gene expression boundaries correspond to functionally distinct organizational units at the single‐cell level. While the large majority of neurons conform to layer‐specific projection motifs, single‐cell data also reveal variation within each cell‐type group that extends beyond what population‐level tracing could resolve, providing new insights into the heterogeneity and collateralization principles that distribute SUB output throughout the brain.

Underlying this classification are potential reconstruction errors, registration issues that arise from registering projectome data from multiple brains into the 3D CCFv3 atlas space, and the imperfect alignment between HGEA‐defined cell‐type boundaries (Figure 2B) and CCFv3 anatomical delineations (Figure 2A). While CCFv3 SUB corresponds primarily to HGEA SUBdd/SUBdv and CCFv3 ProS to HGEA ProSUB, we identified neurons with projection patterns characteristic of all four HGEA layers within CCFv3 SUB boundaries. This likely reflects both genuine differences in how the two frameworks define regional boundaries and spatial imprecision inherent in registering individual MPA brains to the CCFv3 reference atlas. Notably, MPA neurons registered as CCFv3 ProS neurons likely also include all 4 layers and would include more neurons from the HGEA SUBvv that are undersampled here. Irrespective of the atlas boundaries and simply focusing on the 3D space, the classification of SUB MPA neurons as laminar cell types shows remarkable similarity to the 3D HGEA model (compare Figure 2A vs. Figure 1B). However, the CCFv3 HATA region presents an additional challenge: while Ding et al. (2020) characterized HATA as a discrete structural region with unique transcriptomic identity, our recent work suggests it instead represents a transitional zone where CA1v/vv and SUBv/vv laminar cell types converge (Pachicano et al. 2025), and MPA neurons registered to HATA showed connectivity profiles consistent with SUBv/SUBvv, supporting their inclusion in that population here. Thus, misalignment between frameworks has practical implications for cross‐study comparisons where data registered to CCFv3 anatomical boundaries may inadvertently sample from different combinations of HGEA layers. A related methodological consideration is the reliability of manual axon tracing itself: because MPA reconstructions rely on sparsely labeled neurons, closely spaced somata can produce overlapping axon collaterals that are difficult to assign to a single neuron with certainty, a limitation also noted by Qiu et al. (2024) that could in principle inflate apparent projection targets. We mitigated this by requiring that a target be counted only where local axonal branching was confirmed during manual curation (Section 3.1) and by manually verifying soma position and soma–axon connectivity for all 708 candidate neurons prior to classification, discarding 19 that failed this check.

CT/IT/ET Framework Applied to SUB Cell Types

Previous studies established a tripartite classification of cortical projection neurons, including intratelencephalic (IT) neurons projecting to cortex and striatum, extratelencephalic (ET) neurons to subcortical regions, and corticothalamic (CT) neurons to the thalamus. Despite a great diversity of cortical neuron cell types, these general classifications have been proven to be applicable across neocortical areas (Shepherd 2013; Harris et al. 2019; Winnubst et al. 2019; Yao et al. 2023). Although the SUB is regarded as an allocortical structure, SUB pyramidal cell type layers exhibit analogous projection classes and later transcriptomic studies substantiated this view (Yao et al. 2023). Our observations of the MPA single‐neuron projectome data further validate this perspective but also demonstrate that SUB neurons don't follow the rules as strictly as neocortical neurons (fig. 12, left; Bienkowski et al. 2018; Gao et al. 2026). For example, the high percentage of SUB neurons with subcortical projections to hypothalamus or MBO would, by strict definition, classify nearly all as ET neurons. Indeed, thalamic‐projecting CT neurons and cortical‐projecting IT neurons without subcortical collaterals exist but are more rare when compared to the whole population. Still, given a more relaxed projection‐based definition and relationships to neocortical transcriptomic profiles, Layer 6‐like SUB_4 neurons align to the CT‐class across all five SUB regions, consistently targeting RE and other thalamic nuclei whereas SUB_1, SUB_2, and SUB_3 neurons closely resemble mixed IT or ET classes. SUB_2 neurons show the closest conformation to the IT‐class with projections to cortical structures and striatal‐like LS and ACB. Finally, SUB_3 neurons conform to the ET‐class as they have increased subcortical outputs to the amygdala and midbrain. Evolutionary studies of the brain suggest that the hippocampus and SUB were some of the earliest cortical areas to evolve in the brain (Woych et al. 2022), so their strong association with hypothalamus and other subcortical structures should be expected as there were few other cortical areas to connect with in the early vertebrate brain. This suggests that the subcortical connectivity was originally inherent to early cortical cell types and that ET and IT neurons as segregated classes likely diverged later in evolution as cortical regions expanded. Thus, the SUB may be an important site for understanding cortical brain architecture where the IT/ET/CT cell type framework may have first emerged and where laminar and columnar organizational principles converged to form the template for canonical cortical microcircuits (although further study of synaptic connections between SUB cell types is needed) (Bastos et al. 2012). Overall, a conserved organizational principle of cortical output may extend beyond the neocortex to allocortical structures, even as the specific downstream circuits diverge considerably.

Cell‐Type Heterogeneity and Connectivity‐Based Combinatorics

Beyond these atlas‐level organizational principles, single‐cell data reveals structured variation within each HGEA group that reflects genuine cell‐type heterogeneity rather than noise. Unlike transcriptomic studies where heterogeneity has been characterized primarily through gene expression, our analysis quantifies it through connectivity, which is an approach that directly reflects functional output diversity. Consistent with this, Campbell et al. (2026) recently demonstrated that SUB projection neurons exhibit variable dendritic and axonal complexity, such that morphological heterogeneity within the SUB is itself an organizational feature characteristic of projection‐specific input–output relationships. Rather than representing random complexity, we believe this heterogeneity is a key organizational feature of brain circuits, enabling flexible and context‐dependent network engagement rather than the rigid, homotypic activation that would result from populations of identically wired neurons.

Single cell transcriptomics has led to new insights into the heterogeneity of individual neurons in the brain but also raises important questions about the concept of a ‘cell type’ and how we define them (Zeng 2022; Fleck et al. 2023). Notably, these projectome data reveal how cellular heterogeneity can be viewed in two ways based on inclusive or exclusive combinatorics. Inclusive combinatorics determines how many neurons contain at least this combination of projections whereas exclusive combinatorics examines the number of neurons with exactly the same pattern. When viewed with inclusivity, many neurons share similar collateral patterns, particularly at low combinatorial factors. In contrast, when viewed with exclusivity, individual SUB neurons appear highly heterogenous with most individual neurons having unique connectivity profiles. Together, we believe these two combinatoric approaches reveal how the same neurons can seem individually distinct at the single cell level, while simultaneously organized as cell types at the tissue level (Figure 12, middle‐right). Ultimately, both perspectives are valid and axon collateralization appears to be a key organizing principle across the whole brain.

The SUBdd_1 population illustrates this principle clearly. Approximately 90% of SUBdd_1 neurons have an axon collateral that targets the subicular complex (POST, PRE, or PAR), APR, or ENT, which are brain regions strongly implicated in visuospatial navigation (Boccara et al. 2010; Hafting et al. 2005; Peyrache et al. 2017; S.‐Y. Zhang et al. 2022). However, the specific combination of targets innervated by any individual neuron is highly variable: among the 137 SUBdd_1 neurons, we identified 88 distinct target combinations, and just 5.8% of neurons shared the single most common combination (PRE + ENTm + RSPv + MBO) (Table 8). Despite this variability, SUBdd_1 neurons provide roughly equivalent total input to POST, PRE, and PAR, and APR projections consistently appear as a collateral of subicular complex projections rather than as a sole target. This suggests that while individual neurons vary in precise combinations of regions they target, the population as a whole maintains structured and balanced output to this visuospatial circuit. Understanding these combinatorial principles for axon collateralization, or the rules that constrain which combinations of targets co‐occur and which do not, is essential for linking single‐cell connectivity to population‐level dynamics.

Dorsal Subiculum (SUBdd/dv)

Prior population tracing and gene expression studies established that SUBdd and SUBdv_1 neurons project to the subicular complex (POST, PRE, and PAR), RSPv, ENT, and MBO, while SUB_4 neurons predominantly target the anterior thalamic nuclei, LD, and RE (Bienkowski et al. 2018). Classical tracing demonstrated that these projections are topographically organized rather than uniform, with SUBdd and SUBdv showing complementary biases across shared targets (Swanson and Cowan 1975), and dual retrograde tracing further suggested that approximately half of dorsal SUB neurons collateralize to both RSPv and MBO, implicating a shared circuit for spatial memory consolidation (Kinnavane et al. 2018; Umaba et al. 2021). The MPA single‐neuron reconstructions confirm and substantially extend these findings. RSPv/MBO dual collateralization in SUB_1 neurons exceeds prior retrograde tracing estimates, with more than 80% of both SUBdd_1 and SUBdv_1 neurons projecting to both targets. This is consistent with recent fMOST‐based single‐neuron projectome data in the dorsal SUB (J. Zhang et al. 2025). Furthermore, SUBdd_1 was biased toward POST and caudal PAR, SUBdv_1 toward PRE and PAR, SUBdd_1 toward medial RSPv, and SUBdv_1 toward lateral RSPv, which substantially revises the earlier view of O'Mara et al. (2001) that PRE and PAR receive only weak SUB input. ENT projections show complementary banding with SUBdd_1 targeting lateral ENT and SUBdv_1 targeting intermediate ENT, while SUB_4 ENT projections diverge substantially between regions, with SUBdv_4 (95%) exceeding SUBdd_4 (70%). SUB_4 neurons confirm their CT‐class identity through preferentially targeting anterior thalamic nuclei, LD, and RE, which are nuclei strongly implicated in head direction coding, spatial memory, and retrosplenial circuit coordination (Dillingham and Vann 2019), with SUBdd_4 biased toward more caudal portions of these nuclei and SUBdv_4 more rostrally.

The most notable finding from the SUBdd/dv dataset is the identification of area prostriata (APR) as a collateral target of SUB_1 neurons. APR is a multimodal limbic structure that receives projections from visual and auditory cortices, subiculum, presubiculum, and anterior thalamic nuclei, and plays a critical role in fast processing of peripheral visual motion (Hu et al. 2020; Mikellidou et al. 2017). APR projections were found in 23% of SUBdd_1 and 27% of SUBdv_1 neurons, and importantly, APR projections exclusively co‐occurred with subicular complex collaterals. Specifically, over 50% of neurons projecting to POST, PRE, and PAR also had an APR collateral in both SUBdd_1 and SUBdv_1. Because APR in turn projects back to POST and PRE (Chen et al. 2021), SUB_1 neurons appear to participate in a reciprocal visuospatial circuit linking the subicular complex and APR that has not been previously characterized at single‐cell resolution. Together, these data support the long‐standing view of the distal SUB as a structured interface between hippocampus and dispersed visuospatial navigation networks (Lever 2026; Newman et al. 2026; Place et al. 2026; Sun et al. 2024; Muessig et al. 2024; Lever et al. 2009), while revealing a higher degree of single‐cell collateralization and topographic specificity than previously appreciated by Naber and Witter (1998).

Prosubiculum (ProSUB)

Population‐level tracing and gene expression analysis from the HGEA established that the ProSUB is primarily dominated by septo‐hypothalamic outputs, including LS, hypothalamus, ACB, and MBO, and is distinguished from SUBdd/dv by near‐absent RSPv connectivity, indicating a fundamental shift in cortical output profile (Bienkowski et al. 2018). SUB_3 neurons were classified as ET‐class and SUB_4 as CT‐class with RE as the dominant thalamic target, and earlier tracing studies suggested proximal SUB projections to prefrontal cortex, though primarily attributed to ventral rather than dorsal SUB regions (Jay and Witter 1991; Finch 1993; O'Mara et al. 2001). The MPA single‐neuron data confirm these findings at single‐cell resolution, where the LS (96%), hypothalamus (90%), ACB (81%), and MBO (79%) dominance is seen across both layers with near absent RSPv connectivity (2%), validating the cortical output shift as a genuine cell‐type organizational feature rather than a sampling artifact. At the laminar level, ET‐class SUB_3 shows greater prevalence of PFC (36% vs. 17%), amygdala, and PERI/ECT projections relative to CT‐class SUB_4, consistent with an ET‐class output profile targeting both cortical and subcortical limbic structures. ProSUB Layer 4 targets RE at the highest rate of any SUB_4 population (75%), with a thalamic profile that shifts away from the anterior visuospatial thalamic nuclei that dominates SUBdd/dv_4 toward midline and intralaminar nuclei. This intermediate thalamic profile, connecting between the dominant anterior thalamic‐projecting pattern of SUBdd/dv_4 and the midline thalamic‐projecting pattern of SUBv/SUBvv_4, is consistent with ProSUB's structural and positional transition between these two major subdivisions.

Beyond supporting established connectivity patterns, the MPA single‐cell data reveals several previously uncharacterized features of ProSUB output. Most notable is the high rate of non‐canonical back projections to CA1 (Sun et al. 2019), as 60% of ProSUB neurons projected to dorsal CA1 and 20% to CA1i/v, with substantial overlap between these groups (91% of CA1i/v‐projecting neurons also project to CA1dc) such that 62% of ProSUB neurons projected to at least one of these targets. This rate far exceeds that observed in SUBdd/dv, suggesting that the ProSUB plays a specialized role in hippocampal circuit regulation. SUB projections to midbrain structures were also identified, a finding that is discussed in detail in the midbrain section below. Finally, while ENT projections are well represented in the ProSUB, the topographic organization of these projections differs from the SUBdd/dv, with ProSUB axons targeting deeper layers of the lateral ENT and distal SUB axons targeting the caudal dorsomedial ENT. This distinction is functionally significant, as the caudal dorsomedial ENT contains the highest density of large‐scale grid cells while the lateral ENT is more closely associated with olfactory and object‐related information processing, suggesting that ProSUB and SUBdd/dv make fundamentally different contributions to entorhinal‐hippocampal circuit function despite both projecting to ENT (Brun et al. 2008).

Ventral Subiculum (SUBv/SUBvv)

Classical anatomical tracing studies established the output profile of the ventral SUB as a primary hippocampal output to septo‐hypothalamic, limbic, and striatal circuits, with dense projections to the LS, hypothalamus, ACB, and MBO via the medial corticohypothalamic tract (Canteras and Swanson 1992; Witter and Groenewegen 1990). ACB and amygdala also arise preferentially from the ventral SUB (Aylward and Totterdell 1993; Canteras and Swanson 1992), and the HGEA later identified a laminar organization underlying these outputs with SUB_2, SUB_3, and SUB_4 neurons showing distinct projection profiles that place SUBv/vv within a brain‐wide network that mediates social behavior and neuroendocrine regulation (Bienkowski et al. 2018). The MPA single‐neuron reconstructions confirm these findings at single‐cell resolution: septo‐hypothalamic dominance, IT‐class SUB_2 with AON projections, ET‐class SUB_3 amygdala projections, and CT‐class SUB_4 RE dominance with PVT and PT as additional strong targets are all validated across SUBv and SUBvv.

Several features of SUBv/SUBvv connectivity emerge for the first time from the MPA single‐cell data. Most notably, 53% of SUBv_2 IT‐class neurons extended AON collaterals into the prefrontal cortex, establishing a direct SUB‐to‐prefrontal pathway that has not been previously described at single‐cell resolution. While PFC projections from the ventral SUB have been attributed to ventral SUB populations, the single‐cell data indicate that this pathway is specifically enriched in SUB_2 neurons. Entorhinal targeting also reverses direction between SUBv and SUBvv, as SUBv is ENTl‐biased (46% ENTm vs. 64% ENTl) while SUBvv reverses toward ENTm (58% vs. 44%), a pattern consistent at every individual layer within each region (SUBv_2/3/4 all lateral‐biased; SUBvv_2/3 medial‐biased, SUBvv_4 tied). Rather than a simple proximal‐to‐distal gradient, this suggests SUBvv's entorhinal output re‐engages with the spatially tuned dorsomedial ENT circuit (Brun et al. 2008) despite its more ventral position. Within the thalamus, SUBvv_3 ET‐class neurons show a pronounced PVT bias (50%) compared to SUBv_3 (26%), suggesting specialized midline thalamic routing in the most ventral tip of the SUB that may reflect SUBvv's differential connectivity with endocrine‐regulating hypothalamic pathways. Consistent with this, SUBvv shows stronger connectivity with sexually dimorphic brain areas and medial hypothalamic regions compared to SUBv, which more closely resembles ProSUB in its output topography (Bienkowski et al. 2018; Miller and Aoto 2026). Finally, projections to PAG and SC were identified in 46% of SUBv_4 and 10% of SUBvv_4 CT‐class neurons via ascending axonal branches that collateralize through the PVT, a finding discussed in detail in the midbrain section below.

SUB Projections to Midbrain

Among the most notable cross‐regional findings emerging from the ProSUB and SUBv/SUBvv analyses is a set of descending midbrain projections, specifically the PAG, precommissural nucleus (PRC), and SC, a pathway that has received little attention since first described by Kohler (1990). Re‐examination of Mouse Connectome Project PHA‐L anterograde tracing confirms this projection, although AAV viral tracing with less than 3–4 weeks may not sufficiently label this collateral that is roughly 20 mm in axonal path length from the soma (Bienkowski et al. 2018; http://www.mouseconnectome.org). The Allen Mouse Brain Connectivity data using AAV tracing with 3–4 week survival time similarly does not appear to label this pathway. The MPA data, which uses AAV injections in mice with longer survival times, reveals that these projections arise mostly from ProSUB (ProSUB_3 = 46%, ProSUB_4 = 40%) and SUBv (SUBv_3 = 33%, SUBv_4 = 46%), with additional contribution from only 10% of SUBvv_4 neurons. Notably, these axons reach their midbrain targets via two routes: (1) via an ascending branch that first collateralizes near the MBO, (2) caudal extension of the axon that runs entirely through the PVT. Simultaneously engaging midline thalamic, hypothalamic, and midbrain circuits, the functional significance of this pathway is underappreciated given that PAG is a central hub for defensive behavior, stress responses, and fear processing circuits that overlap with the limbic outputs of ProSUB and SUBv (Bandler and Shipley 1994; Benarroch 2012). Meanwhile, the SC integrates multisensory information and generates orienting and defensive responses to salient stimuli (Evans et al. 2018; Wei et al. 2015), and its receipt of subicular input suggests a role for the ProSUB and SUBv in directing behavioral responses toward salient environmental stimuli with either positive or negative valence. Together, these projections position the ProSUB and SUBv as regions essential for not only limbic and emotional regulation but also for descending control of allostatic behaviors and warrant further investigation.

Conclusion

From Single Cells to Brain‐Wide Networks

This study provides a comprehensive, single‐cell validation of the HGEA's laminar and columnar organization of the SUB. Neurons classified into 12 HGEA cell‐type groups relatively reproduced the expected 3D spatial boundaries, confirming that the gene expression‐based divisions correspond to functionally distinct projection populations. Key findings include: more than 80% of dorsal SUB_1 neurons co‐project to both RSPv and MBO, substantially exceeding prior dual‐retrograde estimates; ProSUB and SUBv send previously underappreciated descending projections to the PAG and SC via routes through the PVT and MBO; and IT‐class SUBv_2 neurons establish a direct pathway to the prefrontal cortex that has not been described at single‐cell resolution. Together, these data reveal that axon collateralization in the SUB is not random but instead a functional feature, with individual neurons conforming to layer‐specific motifs while contributing structural heterogeneity across the population. This dataset provides the cell‐type‐specific wiring detail needed for the next generation of multiscale computational models to accurately simulate how hippocampal output coordinates activity across the brain's spatial, limbic, and neuroendocrine networks (F. Wang and Bicanski 2026). Incorporating realistic wiring diversity, like that across HGEA‐defined cell types, rather than relying on homogenous neuron populations, will be essential for understanding how distributed brain networks give rise to complex behavior.
