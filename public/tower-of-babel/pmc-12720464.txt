The impact of cognitive emotion regulation and social alienation chain intermediate role in breast cancer patients experiencing postoperative recurrence of dread for post-traumatic stress disorder
Authors: Yanan Zhou, Yani Zhou, Hui Tan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis study aims to quantify the extent to which cognitive emotional regulation and social alienation independently and interactively predict the severity of post-traumatic stress disorder symptoms in patients after radical mastectomy for breast cancer. To test whether cognitive emotional regulation strategies mediate the relationship between fear of cancer recurrence and post-traumatic stress disorder, and to determine whether social alienation mediates the impact of fear of cancer recurrence on post-traumatic stress disorder. It is hypothesized that higher maladaptive cognitive emotional regulation and higher social alienation will respectively explain the unique variations in the severity of post-traumatic stress disorder, rather than merely sociodemographic and medical covariates; The higher the level of cognitive emotional regulation, the more obvious the connection between fear of cancer recurrence and post-traumatic stress disorder. Social distancing can partially mediate the pathway of fear of cancer recurrence - post-traumatic stress disorder. Clarifying these modifiable psychosocial pathways will provide a theoretical basis for alleviating post-traumatic stress disorder in postoperative breast cancer survivors.MethodsWe recruited 256 eligible patients who had undergone breast cancer surgery to participate in the study using a cross-sectional design. The study discussed the interplay among fear of cancer recurrence, social alienation, cognitive emotion regulation, and post-traumatic stress disorder and aimed to develop effective stress intervention strategies for clinical researchers.Resultspost-traumatic stress disorder was positively connected with anxiety about cancer recurrence, maladaptive cognitive emotion regulation, and social alienation in patients after breast cancer surgery. Furthermore, the connection between fear of cancer recurrence and post-traumatic stress disorder was influenced by individual maladaptive cognitive emotion regulation and social alienation.ConclusionThe fear of cancer recurrence can lead to an increase in post-traumatic stress disorder among breast cancer patients after surgery. This increase is influenced by the adaptability of cognitive emotion regulation and social alienation. Hence, Efforts should be made to prevent negative cognitive emotion regulation. Additionally, patients should be encouraged and supported to enhance their confidence in social interactions and reduce social alienation. These measures aim to alleviate the symptoms of post-traumatic stress disorder.Clinical trial numberNot applicable

Introduction

Breast cancer is a major global public health problem, and its disease burden and current status of diagnosis and treatment constitute the research background. In 2020, the number of new breast cancer cases in the world reached 2.26 million, accounting for 11.7% of all new cancers, surpassing lung cancer to become the malignant tumor with the highest incidence [1]. According to the 2008 report of the World Health Organization, female breast cancer has become the leading cause of death from malignant tumors in 104 countries. Under the bio-psycho-social medical model, surgery is the preferred treatment for breast cancer, while health-related quality of life and patient satisfaction have become the core concerns of policy making and clinical research [2]. With the advancement of medical technology, the average survival rate of patients has been significantly improved, forming a large and special postoperative survival group. However, due to the characteristics of cancer, the risk of recurrence or metastasis is still high, and comprehensive methods such as chemotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted therapy and endocrine therapy are needed to control disease progression after surgery [3]. While prolonging life, these treatments also lead to different degrees of negative changes in physical appearance, adverse drug reactions and sexual function [4].

In this context, postoperative psychological stress disorder has become the core problem affecting rehabilitation. Studies have shown that postoperative pain has a significant impact on the psychological state of patients, and preoperative anxiety and depression may exacerbate postoperative pain, forming a vicious circle, and then damaging the overall quality of life [5–7]. Emotional changes caused by long-term psychological stress are particularly prominent in breast cancer patients, and can last for a long time after surgery [8]. Among them, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and fear of cancer recurrence (FCR) were two key psychological variables. PTSD is a serious mental illness triggered by traumatic experiences, which not only affects mental health, but also may increase the risk of suicide [9].

Due to the threat to their life and health, breast cancer patients often experience extreme psychological stress after surgery, which leads to persistent negative emotions and PTSD symptoms, and then affects the quality of life and disease prognosis [10]. Although the symptoms of some patients can be relieved over time, the treatment cycle of breast cancer up to 5–10 years makes the prevention of PTSD after surgery particularly important [11]. At the same time, FCR refers to the patient’s concern about cancer recurrence or progression, which is common in patients with temporary remission and advanced stage, and is closely related to psychological variables such as anxiety and depression, further aggravating the psychological burden [12, 13]. The incidence of FCR in postoperative breast cancer patients is as high as 55% [14]. This fear is not limited to the diagnosis and treatment, but runs through the whole process of the disease and exists for a long time [15]. High levels of FCR can increase the risk of recurrence, worsen the prognosis, increase the sense of burden, trigger physiological symptoms, and even directly lead to PTSD [16–18].

Cognitive emotion regulation strategies and social alienation, as two important mediating variables, play a key role in the relationship between stressors and psychological outcomes. Cognitive emotion regulation strategy refers to the way that individuals use cognitive ways to manage negative life events, which can be divided into adaptive strategy and non-adaptive strategy. Adaptive strategy can promote mental health, interpersonal relationship and post-traumatic growth, while non-adaptive strategy can easily lead to depression, anxiety and other mental disorders [19]. Individuals have significant differences in cognitive emotion regulation, and such differences affect emotion management strategies and are used for health outcomes under stress [20].

Emotion processing is a complex and dynamic process, which goes beyond a single cognitive regulation strategy and includes the detection, expression, and suppression of emotions, as well as the intensity and complexity of the experience. In breast cancer patients, these emotional processing dimensions are interrelated and significantly affect the mental health of patients.Firstly, at the level of emotional perception and recognition, breast cancer patients show a high sensitivity to threat-related cues, which may be derived from their traumatic experiences, leading to emotional numbness or dissociation. Studies have shown that patients with post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) often show significant difficulties in the regulation of negative emotions, increasing their avoidance responses to trauma-related cues [21]. Insufficient awareness of emotions will weaken their cognitive reappraisal ability, and then affect the effective management of emotions. Secondly, in the dimension of emotional expression and suppression, breast cancer patients often adopt the strategy of emotional suppression due to stigma and body image disorder, and actively avoid talking about the emotional experience brought by the disease. Studies have found that long-term emotional suppression not only aggravates physical arousal and psychological distress, but also may form a vicious cycle between inhibition and rebound, thus further worsening PTSD symptoms [22, 23]. This phenomenon suggests that expression of emotion and inhibition are not isolated, but interact in pathology. Moreover, the emotional experience of cancer patients is more complex and intense, especially the association of fear of recurrence, which often includes anticipatory grief for multiple losses such as death, disability, and family burden. The emotional granularity associated with PTSD is not only more refined, but also appears to be more persistent over time [24]. The depth and complexity of such emotions make it more difficult for patients to deal with their emotions, especially when they are faced with physical pain and intertwined with emotional states, forming a closed-loop feedback mechanism between pain and negative emotions [25]. In addition, the lack of social support further aggravates the difficulty of emotional processing, and the lack of a safe environment for emotional expression makes patients more isolated when facing emotional difficulties, thus enhancing the tendency of emotional inhibition. This sense of social isolation interacts with emotional inhibition and may constitute the basis of emotional susceptibility to the development of PTSD [26].

Social alienation is characterized by an individual’s active avoidance of social interaction with family members and others, including loneliness, helplessness, and social avoidance behavior, which is both emotional experience and behavioral change. The incidence of social alienation in breast cancer patients ranges from 36.0% to 78.1% [27], and its causes mainly include three aspects: First, as a symbol of female form, sexual characteristics and temperament, breast resection results in body asymmetry, deformity and unconcealed scars, which not only cause physical pain, but also continue disease stigma, social inferiority and psychological problems [28, 29]. Secondly, adverse reactions such as alopecia caused by chemotherapy drugs significantly affect body image disorders and quality of life [30]. Third, the decrease of doctor-patient interaction after treatment leads to the decrease of support, and family support, as the core of social support, has an important impact on patients’ emotion and subjective well-being [31]. In addition, patients are placed in a disadvantaged social position due to health threats, and limited adaptability further exacerbates social isolation [32].

Based on self-regulation theory, individuals adapt to internal and external stimuli by perceiving and responding to cognitive or emotional pain, and these adaptation processes can affect physical and mental responses, disease process and treatment outcome [33]. Therefore, a four-variable integrated model was constructed: breast cancer and its related pain management, as the primary stressor throughout the whole process of the disease, stimulated the fear of cancer recurrence in patients, and then triggered physical, physical and psychological symptoms (including PTSD) [34]. In this process, social alienation and dysfunctional cognitive emotion regulation strategies serve as mediating factors to intensify the occurrence and development of PTSD [35]. This model reveals the causal chain from disease stress to psychological outcome, and elucidates the regulatory mechanism at the cognitive and social levels, which provides a theoretical basis for targeted psychological intervention [36].

Methods

Participants

Our research has been approved by the Research Ethics Committee (No0.27,2024). From January 2023 to May 2024, we used a simple sampling method to select 256 patients who had undergone breast cancer surgery from the oncology outpatient department and the thyroid and breast surgery outpatient department of the Affiliated Hospital of Qingdao Binhai University as the research subjects. The inclusion criteria for participants were: (1) being female and aged between 18 and 80 years old; (2) being within 1 year post-surgery or post-chemotherapy; (3) having no cognitive or communication impairments; and (4) having awareness of their own condition and expressing willingness to participate in this study. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) the presence of comorbid mental disorders; (2) significant impairment in the functioning of vital organs such as the heart, brain, or kidneys; and (3) the presence of primary sleep problems.

Sample size

We have a rough estimate method to calculate sample size; we have used all the survey scales with a total of 30 variables; we selected seven times the number of variables, because this method is derived from Kendall’s empirical interval of 5 to 10 times, and 7 times happens to be in the middle value, it can ensure the stability of the regression coefficient. but given that the questionnaire is lost or invalid, this study was conducted on the basis of the original sample size and increased by 20%, ultimately determining the sample size of 256 cases.

Questionnaire

Questionnaire for gathering social demographic information

We are responsible for creating and constructing survey data as well as demographic information such as age, nationality, marital status, place of residence, employment status, educational level, healthcare preferences, family income, number of children, personality traits, family support, and satisfaction with 11 specific aspects. These aspects include cancer staging, disease-related information, history of surgery, chemotherapy, and treatment course. Example item:“I am satisfied with the support my family members have given me.”

Fear of Progression Questionnaire-Short Form (FoP-Q-SF)

The questionnaire is a measurement tool used to assess an individual’s fear or anxiety related to the process of personal growth and advancement. The Mehnert scale was devised, with 12 items and 2 dimensions: physical health and social/family. It was employed to evaluate the apprehension of disease advancement in patients with breast cancer. The overall score varied between 12 and 60 points, with a higher score indicating a greater severity of patients’ dread. The Cronbach alpha coefficient of the scale in this study was 0.838 [37]. Example item:“Thinking that the disease might progress, I became anxious.”

Cognitive Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (CERQ)

The questionnaire is a tool used to measure cognitive strategies individuals use to regulate their emotions, and was devised by Garnefski. We employed the maladaptive regulation component of the Maladaptive Cognitive Emotion Regulation Scale, which comprised 16 inquiries and 4 dimensions, namely catastrophizing, rumination, self-blame, and blaming others. The ratings for each dimension varied between 4 and 20. The Conbrach’s alpha coefficient in this study is 0.769 [30]. Example item:“When I experience something bad, I think it’s my fault.”

General Alienation Scale (GAS)

The questionnaire is a measurement tool used to assess general feelings of alienation. The scale was established by Jessor and comprised of 15 items and 4 dimensions, including alienation of others, helplessness, self-alienation, and meaninglessness. The overall score spans from 15 to 60, with higher scores denoting increased estrangement. The scale used in this investigation has a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.8 [38]. Example item:“When I encounter problems or need others’ help, it’s hard for me to count on them.”

Stanford Acute Stress Response Questionnaire (SASRQ)

The questionnaire is a measurement tool used to assess acute stress responses，which was assembled by Cardena and consists of a comprehensive set of 30 questions. These questions cover many aspects, such as dissociation symptoms, avoidance symptoms, re-history symptoms, hypervigilance symptoms, and social function impairment. The overall score ranged from 0 to 150 points, with a score above 40 indicating the presence of PTSD and a score equal to or greater than 57 indicating severe PTSD. As the score increases, the severity of PTSD also increases. The Conbrach’s alpha coefficient of this scale in the study was 0.964 [39]. Example item:“I tried to avoid talking about the fact that I had a mastectomy.”

Research methodology

Prior to the survey administration, the research team, comprising master’s students and hospital nurses, provided participants with a comprehensive overview of the study’s objectives and ensured them that all responses would be strictly confidential. The data collection was conducted through face-to-face interviews carried out by the research team members. Upon completion of each survey, the questionnaires were collected immediately. Any questionnaire found to be incomplete for any reason was excluded from the final analysis. A total of 260 questionnaires were distributed, all of which were returned; of these, 256 were determined to be valid, yielding an effective response rate of 98.5%.

Analysis

Following the collection of all the data, statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 26.0 and AMOS 26.0 software. (1) The scores for fear of cancer recurrence, maladaptive cognitive emotion regulation, social alienation, and PTSD in patients who underwent breast cancer surgery were described using the mean (M) and standard deviation (SD). (2) Pearson correlation analysis was employed to examine the relationship between fear of cancer recurrence, maladaptive cognitive emotion regulation, and PTSD in postoperative breast cancer patients. (3) The structural equation model was constructed using AMOS 26.0 software to investigate the interaction path between fear of cancer recurrence, maladaptive cognitive emotion regulation, and PTSD.

Discussion

The objective of this study was to investigate the preventative impacts of cancer recurrence anxiety, maladaptive cognitive emotion regulation, and social isolation on PTSD in Chinese patients following breast cancer surgery. Based on the premise, we noted varying degrees of PTSD in patients following breast cancer surgery, which aligns with the findings of Wu X ‘s study [40]. Additionally, we noticed that women diagnosed with breast cancer exhibited a higher tendency to employ maladaptive cognitive emotion regulation strategies compared to the control group of women. Previous research has established that individuals diagnosed with breast cancer tend to adopt negative coping mechanisms such as avoidance, assigning blame, and resigning themselves to their circumstances [41]. This suggests that, when confronted with emotional distress resulting from the challenges of the illness, including diagnosis and treatment, breast cancer patients are particularly susceptible to its impact. A greater number of individuals employed emotion-focused negative coping techniques, while a smaller proportion utilized problem-focused coping strategies.

We have also verified the notion that increased levels of anxiety about cancer recurrence are linked to elevated levels of PTSD. Ultimately, this link is somewhat influenced by non-adaptive cognitive-emotional control and the extent to which patients distance themselves socially following breast cancer surgery.

The current state of PTSD in individuals who have undergone surgery for breast cancer

During the initial phases of a patient’s cancer diagnosis, caregivers encounter a greater number of symptoms related to hypervigilance and avoidance. Patients had the most severe dissociation symptoms, followed by hypervigilance symptoms and the least severe recurrence symptoms [42]. The analysis can be attributed to the fact that patients experience discomfort when exposed to factors that remind them of the disease and its associated risks [43]. As a result, they actively avoid any stimuli that remind them of the disease in order to divert their attention. This leads to a decrease in patients’ adherence to the treatment plan. In light of this, it is imperative for academic research teams and psychiatric specialists to devise appropriate preventive strategies aimed at mitigating cognitive and emotional avoidance among individuals in this demographic, with the goal of effectively managing post-traumatic stress disorder within the context of oncology. This study revealed a high prevalence of PTSD among patients following breast cancer surgery. Consequently, it is recommended that clinical medical personnel enhance the regular assessment of PTSD in these patients. This will enable the early identification of warning signs of PTSD and facilitate timely and effective intervention. Furthermore, patients diagnosed with PTSD should receive personalized guidance and treatment. For instance, the combination of pharmacotherapy and cognitive behavioral intervention treatment can effectively alleviate psychological distress in those with a high susceptibility to PTSD [44].

Correlation between fear of cancer recurrence, non-adaptive cognitive emotion regulation, social distancing and PTSD

The findings of this study suggest that there is a notable and positive association between the fear of cancer recurrence, maladaptive cognitive-emotional regulation, social distancing, and PTSD in patients who have undergone breast cancer surgery. These findings indicate that intense anxiety about cancer returning, ineffective management of cognitive and emotional responses, and significant levels of social isolation are all associated with increased PTSD in patients following breast cancer surgery [45].

The study findings indicate a substantial positive correlation between the fear of cancer recurrence and PTSD. Furthermore, the fear of cancer recurrence can serve as a positive predictor of PTSD. When individuals confront the apprehension of disease advancement following a distressing incident, like a car crash or natural calamity, this simultaneous tension can intensify their psychological stress reaction and heighten the likelihood of acquiring PTSD [11]. The apprehension of illness advancement might hinder patients’ ability to manage the psychological distress caused by a traumatic incident, leading to more intense or challenging alleviation of symptoms associated with PTSD. We discovered a strong correlation between the fear of cancer recurrence and symptoms such as avoidant cognition, re-experience, avoidance, and hypervigilance. However, in clinical treatment, medical technicians tend to focus more on patients’ physical health and common side effects while neglecting the psychological well-being of patients after breast cancer surgery, leading to the potential oversight of psychological symptoms [46, 47]. Patients who experience cancer recurrence fears and clinically significant symptoms of PTSD following breast cancer surgery should receive regular monitoring and prompt psychological care upon diagnosis. This will offer specific help to alleviate their emotional distress.

Cognitive emotion regulation plays a significant role in the genesis and rehabilitation of post-traumatic stress disorder. After experiencing a traumatic experience, an individual’s emotional response may be influenced by their cognitive judgment. Individuals who have a tendency to perceive and assess traumatic experiences in a negative manner may be more prone to experiencing intensified symptoms of PTSD due to their involvement in negative emotions [48]. On the other hand, favorable assessments of cognitive abilities might assist in diminishing negative feelings and facilitating the process of recuperation. Furthermore, cognitive emotion control procedures can be employed as a therapeutic approach for post-traumatic stress disorder. Patients can be assisted in improving their ability to cope with and regulate the emotional symptoms related to PTSD through the instruction of cognitive restructuring techniques, relaxation training, and emotional regulation tactics [49]. These tactics are formulated to assist patients in reevaluating traumatic situations, reducing their emotional repercussions, and strengthening their psychological resilience. Nevertheless, due to the unique conditions and requirements of each patient, it is imperative to create a customized therapy strategy based on the individual’s specific situation in regards to cognitive emotion regulation.

When individuals suffer a traumatic event, social separation may increase the symptoms of PTSD. Insufficient social support and fragile interpersonal ties can intensify individuals’ psychological distress by making them feel more alienated and powerless in the face of traumatic experiences [50]. Furthermore, the manner in which individuals mentally handle traumatic experiences can also impact their adherence to social distancing measures, diminishing their inclination to seek assistance and support and intensifying symptoms of PTSD. On the other hand, people with PTSD may also have a greater inclination towards social separation. Post-traumatic events in breast cancer patients can lead to social alienation as they may develop dread, anxiety, or avoidance emotions, causing them to avoid interacting with others [51]. This detrimental cycle can exacerbate the challenges faced by patients in their recovery from trauma and heighten their susceptibility to the emergence of further psychiatric issues. Thus, social separation and PTSD have a reciprocal effect on each other. In order to mitigate these diseases, breast cancer patients should proactively seek social support and cultivate good connections, while also receiving competent psychotherapy to effectively manage the psychological repercussions of the traumatic experience [52, 53]. Society should prioritize providing attention and assistance to post-breast cancer patients who have had trauma and exhibit a proclivity for social isolation, aiding them in reintegrating into society and achieving a level of well-being [54].

The mediating role of non-adaptive cognitive emotion regulation and social distancing between fear of cancer recurrence and PTSD

The study confirmed the whole-chain mediation model of the impact of fear of cancer recurrence on PTSD by incorporating non-adaptive cognitive emotion regulation and social distancing [55]. The findings indicate that the apprehension of cancer recurrence can impact PTSD by means of the intermediary influence of maladaptive cognitive emotion control and social distancing. The mediating impact can be observed through three distinct pathways: the first channel involves non-adaptive cognitive emotion regulation as an independent mediator; the second pathway involves social alienation as an independent mediator; and the third pathway involves social distancing as a chain mediator through non-adaptive cognitive emotion regulation. There is an anticipation assumption regarding this outcome.

This study offers novel insights into the mechanism by which the fear of cancer recurrence impacts PTSD. As the level of anxiety about cancer recurrence increases, breast cancer patients are more likely to employ negative emotional regulation strategies, such as anticipating the worst outcome, magnifying the severity of the situation, self-blaming, or blaming others[56]. Patients have heightened negative emotional states, leading to their reluctance to engage with the external environment and articulate their internal ideas. This, in turn, manifests as a tendency to conceal their true selves, a deterioration in their social skills, and a separation from social interactions [57]. Consequently, the degree of social alienation is influenced, and when negative emotions collect, the ability to adjust to the illness, life, family, and society diminishes, thereby exacerbating the symptoms of PTSD. Family support, being a crucial element of social support, significantly impacts an individual’s PTSD. Research has demonstrated that strong spiritual communication and effective communication within families are crucial for developing individuals’ ability to regulate positive cognitive emotions [58]. Following breast cancer surgery, women tend to become more emotionally sensitive and face concerns about changes in appearance, fear of the cancer returning, uncertainty about the future, and worries about their quality of life. These factors can lead to increased psychological pressure, inner conflicts, and contradictions [59]. During this period, patients require additional care and support from their families and partners. Thus, it is advisable for families to support patients in articulating their inner ideas and help them communicate their emotions in a rational manner. Simultaneously, it is crucial for families and medical personnel to promptly recognize patients’ incorrect cognitive emotions and develop appropriate psychological intervention measures to guide them in employing effective emotion regulation strategies. This will aid in fostering positive attributes such as self-assurance and optimism in patients following breast cancer surgery, while also promoting social engagement and diminishing social isolation. In order to effectively manage stress and alleviate symptoms of PTSD.

Prior research has indicated that individuals with strong psychological adaptation skills can mitigate the negative impacts caused by external stress and stimulation [60]. This researcher posits that the cognitive emotion regulation strategies acquired by individuals while adapting to the social environment will be tailored to specific forms when utilized to address practical issues. For instance, if individuals are unable to effectively manage stressful situations by employing negative cognitive regulation strategies, they will experience significant distress, and social separation becomes crucial in this context. Having a strong sense of self-assurance, a positive outlook, and effective interpersonal skills can partially reduce the distress caused by failure [61]. Additionally, a person’s level of social disconnection can mitigate or counterbalance some of the negative consequences resulting from the fear of relapse and ineffective cognitive emotion regulation strategies [62]. Postoperative breast cancer patients who have persistently high levels of concern over cancer recurrence may find it challenging to quickly alter their maladaptive cognitive thinking. In addition, certain individuals have introverted and sensitive tendencies, often concealing their own emotions and thoughts. Individuals are susceptible to pessimistic thinking patterns and maladaptive emotional regulation strategies following traumatic events; once these cognitive biases become established, they prove difficult to modify. However, targeted interventions aimed at enhancing social self-efficacy and reducing social isolation may effectively mitigate the subsequent severity of post-traumatic stress disorder [63].

The study explores the chain mediation impact of cognitive emotion regulation and social alienation, building upon and expanding previous studies on fear of cancer recurrence, cognitive emotion regulation, social alienation, and PTSD. By integrating these four variables, we can delve further into the internal workings of the fear of cancer recurrence in patients following breast cancer surgery, which holds practical importance for the management of PTSD. Clinical and related researchers should focus on correcting patients’ erroneous cognitive thinking and developing their ability to make accurate cognitive adjustments in future therapeutic efforts. Additionally, it is important for both family members and psychotherapists to promote patients’ social engagement and excitement, foster positive psychological traits such as self-assurance, hope, optimism, and resilience, and prevent social isolation. Simultaneously, harness the collective influence of medical, familial, and societal factors on patients while also acknowledging the influence of society and family on patients while prioritizing disease treatment.

Conclusion

Our cross-sectional study confirms that postoperative breast cancer patients are vulnerable to post-traumatic stress disorder, with elevated fear of cancer recurrence (FCR) being positively associated with PTSD severity. Importantly, maladaptive cognitive emotion regulation and social isolation act as sequential mediators in the FCR-PTSD relationship: fear of cancer recurrence increases reliance on maladaptive cognitive emotion regulation, which in turn enhances social isolation, ultimately exacerbating post-traumatic stress disorder symptoms. These findings highlight the need for a holistic approach to postoperative care that addresses both physical and psychological health. Clinicians should prioritize routine screening for fear of cancer recurrence, maladaptive cognitive emotion regulation, social isolation, and post-traumatic stress disorder, and implement targeted interventions to reduce maladaptive cognitive patterns, enhance social engagement, and mitigate fear of cancer recurrence. Future longitudinal and interventional studies are needed to validate the causal mechanisms identified and develop effective strategies for improving mental health outcomes in this population.

Implications for clinical practice

To mitigate post-traumatic stress disorder in postoperative breast cancer patients, target the chain mediating pathway: fear of cancer recurrence → maladaptive cognitive emotion regulation → social alienation. Conduct routine early postoperative screening of these factors for timely high-risk identification; use cognitive-behavioral therapy to modify maladaptive cognitive emotion regulation and disrupt the fear of cancer recurrence-maladaptive cognitive emotion regulation link. Strengthen social support to alleviate social alienation, and promote multidisciplinary collaboration for coordinated care. For Chinese patients, integrate culturally tailored strategies to enhance acceptability. Collectively, proactive screening, pathway-targeted interventions, and culturally responsive care optimize postoperative mental health and reduce post-traumatic stress disorder risk.

Limitation

First, cross-sectional design prevents definitive causal inference; reverse causality and unadjusted confounders (socioeconomic status, treatment intensity, pre-existing mental conditions) cannot be ruled out, biasing associations.

Second, sampling limits generalizability (rural patients, advanced disease), and self-reports may introduce response bias (social desirability, recall bias).

Third, model comprehensiveness is constrained: maladaptive cognitive emotion regulation-social isolation mechanism was understudied, and other mediators (social support quality, resilience) were excluded. Future studies could address these via longitudinal designs, representative sampling, objective assessments, and more mediators to refine PTSD understanding in postoperative breast cancer patients.
