Global, regional, and national age-gender-specific trends, decomposition analysis, and Bayesian age-period-cohort modelling for projections of the burden of leishmaniasis: A population-based ecological study
Authors: Yongbin Wang, Renfang Zhang, Zhenzhou Liu, Chunjie Xu, Chenguang Zhang, Fei Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLeishmaniasis, a neglected tropical disease transmitted by sandflies, continues to pose a significant public health challenge, especially in tropical and subtropical regions. This study aims to deliver a comprehensive analysis of the global burden of leishmaniasis, exploring trends, disparities, and future projections to inform and enhance public health strategies. Using the data from the Global Burden of Disease database, we analysed leishmaniasis prevalence, incidence, mortality, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) across 204 countries and territories from 1990 to 2021. We calculated age-standardised rates (ASRs) and estimated annual percentage changes (EAPCs) to assess trends. The Kitagawa decomposition method was employed to quantify contributions of population ageing, population growth, and epidemiological changes. A Bayesian age-period-cohort (BAPC) model was used to project disease burden through 2036. Globally, the age-standardised prevalence rate (ASPR) showed a slight increase from 1990 to 2021 (EAPC = 0.71%, 95% CI: 0.66–0.76%), while the age-standardised incidence rate (ASIR), mortality rate (ASMR), and DALY rate (ASDR) declined (ASIR EAPC = −0.51%, 95% CI: −1.09–0.05%; ASMR EAPC = −6.84%, 95% CI: −7.74 to −5.93%; ASDR EAPC = −5.17%, 95% CI: −5.99 to −4.33%). In 2021, there were 6.21 × 106 (95% UI: 5.83 × 105–6.69 × 106) prevalent cases and 5.48 × 103 (95% UI: 1.61 × 103–1.78 × 104) deaths globally. Marked disparities were observed across Socio-demographic Index (SDI) regions, with low SDI regions bearing the highest burden (ASPR = 139.66 per 100,000). The Syrian Arab Republic had the highest ASPR (4.73 × 103 per 100,000), while Romania and Serbia had the lowest (0.01 per 100,000). Decomposition analysis revealed that population growth was the primary driver of increased prevalence burden globally (78.5%), while epidemiological changes contributed to reduced incidence (−288%) and mortality (−118.66%) burdens. Projections indicate that by 2036, male ASPR will rise to 119.31 (95% UI: 92.32–146.30) per 100,000, while ASIR and ASMR will remain stable. Despite declining incidence and mortality, leishmaniasis prevalence is projected to increase, with persistent significant disparities across regions, ages, and genders. Targeted interventions addressing population growth, epidemiological factors, and socio-economic determinants are crucial for reducing the future burden of leishmaniasis, particularly in low SDI regions.Keywords: Leishmaniasis, Global burden of disease, Age-period-cohort analysis, Disease projections, Socio-economic disparities, Decomposition analysis, Epidemiological trends

Introduction

Leishmaniasis is a parasitic neglected tropical disease (NTD) caused by Leishmania spp. parasites and transmitted by phlebotomine sand flies, with endemic distribution across global tropical and subtropical regions (Depaquit et al., 2019). Its clinical manifestations vary significantly based on parasite species and host immune responses, primarily manifesting as visceral leishmaniasis (VL), cutaneous leishmaniasis (CL), mucocutaneous leishmaniasis (MCL), and post-kala-azar dermal leishmaniasis (PKDL). Among these forms, untreated VL poses a particularly dire prognosis, with mortality rates soaring up to 95% (Belo et al., 2014).

The four clinical subtypes of leishmaniasis exhibit distinct disease severity and health burdens (Zhang et al., 2025; Carrer et al., 2026). VL is the leading cause of leishmaniasis-related mortality and years of life lost (YLL). By comparison, CL and MCL are rarely lethal but account for most disease cases and the majority of years lived with disability (YLD), driven by chronic skin lesions, permanent disfigurement, and associated social stigma. As a long-term complication of treated VL, PKDL also causes sustained disease morbidity. These subtype-specific differences are critical for accurate interpretation of leishmaniasis epidemiological profiles and burden assessments.

As a poverty-related NTD, leishmaniasis is closely associated with socio-economic deprivation, malnutrition, human population migration, and ecological degradation (WHO, 1971). In many endemic areas, inadequate access to healthcare, poor housing conditions, and limited vector control measures exacerbate the disease’s impact on vulnerable communities. Moreover, climate change-driven expansions of sand fly habitats are extending the disease’s transmission range poleward, intensifying public health challenges (Fischer et al., 2011). The encroachment of transmission zones into previously unaffected areas not only exposes new populations to infection but also strains existing healthcare systems ill-equipped to handle emerging cases.

Despite its substantial global health and economic tolls, leishmaniasis has historically received limited international attention and funding, predominantly affecting marginalised and resource-constrained populations. Recent decades have witnessed transformative shifts in its epidemiology, fueled by factors including increased human mobility, environmental alterations, and emerging drug resistance (Nightingale et al., 2024). For instance, the rise of urbanisation in endemic regions has altered the interaction patterns between humans and vectors, while the emergence of resistance to traditional antileishmanial drugs like sodium stibogluconate threatens to undermine decades of treatment progress (García-Hernández et al., 2015; Talbi et al., 2020).

Most prior Global Burden of Disease (GBD) studies on leishmaniasis merely conducted holistic epidemiological analyses of neglected tropical diseases, lacking refined stratified exploration (Zhu et al., 2024; Lin et al., 2022). Differing from previous research, this study further identified distinct temporal, regional, age- and gender-specific epidemiological characteristics of leishmaniasis. We adopted decomposition analysis to quantify driving factors underlying disease burden variations and applied the Bayesian Age-Period-Cohort (BAPC) model to predict future epidemiological trends. Additionally, this study clarified the correlations between socio-economic determinants, public health policies and leishmaniasis-related disease burden. Cross-regional comparison covering areas at diverse socio-economic development levels enabled accurate identification of high-burden endemic zones. These findings offer targeted references for formulating prevention strategies and advancing leishmaniasis control worldwide.

Materials and methods

Data source

This study utilises data from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021, a comprehensive and standardised database managed by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME) at the University of Washington. The GBD 2021 database provides estimates of disease burden (prevalence, incidence, mortality, DALYs), population metrics, and risk factor attributions for 204 countries and territories from 1990 to 2021. Data are sourced from vital registration systems, censuses, and published studies using an integrated approach to ensure robustness. Detailed information on data collection and estimation methods is available in the GBD Study 2021 documentation (Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation, 2024), and the complete datasets are publicly accessible via the Global Health Data Exchange (GHDx) website (http://ghdx.healthdata.org/) (GBD 2021 Diseases and Injuries Collaborators, 2024).

Data collection

Leishmaniasis data (all clinical forms of VL, CL, MCL, and PKDL), including the number of cases, prevalence, incidence, mortality rate, age-specific data, and age-standardised rates, were extracted from the GBD 2021 dataset for the global level, Socio-demographic Index (SDI) regions, GBD regions, and all age groups from 1990 to 2021. While the GBD study provides an estimation framework for 204 countries and territories, this analysis focuses on the 83 countries for which leishmaniasis-specific data were available. Countries without data were excluded from the comparison as they were either non-endemic or had zero reported cases. Stratified dimensions included global level, SDI regions, GBD geographical regions, and all standard GBD age groups. Uniform 5-year interval age stratification was adopted in this study: < 5, 5–9, 10–14, 15–19, 20–24, 25–29, 30–34, 35–39, 40–44, 45–49, 50–54, 55–59, 60–64, 65–69, 70–74, 75–79, 80–84, 85–89, 90–94, and ≥ 95 years. The GBD 2021 database utilises the SDI, a composite indicator reflecting social and economic development and its influence on health. SDI values in GBD 2021 range from 0.00 to 1.00, categorising countries into five development levels: low (< 0.46), low-middle (0.46–0.60), middle (0.61–0.69), high-middle (0.70–0.81), and high (> 0.81) (Zeng et al., 2021).

Statistical analysis

To quantify the long-term burden of leishmaniasis, we analysed four core outcomes: prevalence, incidence, mortality, and DALYs. Corresponding age-standardised rates (ASRs) were calculated to eliminate confounding effects of heterogeneous population age structures and enable cross-population comparison. Estimated annual percentage changes (EAPCs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were computed to quantify temporal trends of ASRs from 1990 to 2021. where ASRt denotes the ASR in year t, α represents the intercept, β is the time slope coefficient, and ε is the random error term. The final EAPC (%) was defined as 100 × (eβ − 1), and 95% CIs of EAPC were obtained from the 95% CI bounds of the regression coefficient β.

Statistically significant increasing/decreasing trends were identified when the 95% CI of EAPC did not overlap zero. Smoothing spline regression models were constructed to evaluate nonlinear associations between continuous SDI values and leishmaniasis ASRs (GBD 2016 Neurology Collaborators, 2019).

To quantify the factors contributing to long-term changes in the disease burden of leishmaniasis, we employed the Kitagawa decomposition method, using 1990 as the baseline (Jdanov et al., 2017). This analysis assessed how population ageing, population growth, and epidemiological changes, reflected in shifts in age-specific rates and changes in population age structure, contributed to observed trends in leishmaniasis incidence, mortality, prevalence, and DALYs from 1990 to 2021, highlighting the relative impact of each factor. The detailed methodology is provided in Supplementary Text S1.

We constructed a Bayesian age-period-cohort (BAPC) model to generate gender-stratified projections of leishmaniasis prevalence, incidence, mortality and DALYs burden (Havulinna, 2014). To resolve the statistical identifiability issue of classical APC frameworks and accommodate sparse national count data with overdispersion, all primary models adopted Integrated Nested Laplace Approximation (INLA) for Bayesian posterior inference (Yang et al., 2016). where g() represents the logarithmic link function, μa,p,c denotes expected leishmaniasis burden for age group a, calendar period p, and birth cohort c, γ is the global intercept, αa, πp, and χc are age, period, and cohort random effects assigned second-order intrinsic random walk (RW2) smoothing priors, and Xδ stands for fixed covariate terms adjusting for gender (Knoll et al., 2020). Regarding prior specification, weakly informative Gaussian hyperpriors (mean = 0, precision = 0.001) were assigned to the log-precision parameters of RW2 random walk effects for age, period and cohort; a non-informative normal prior N (0, 1000) was set for the global intercept γ; a gamma prior (shape = 1, rate = 0.005) was applied to the overdispersion parameter of negative binomial likelihood; and flat normal priors with large variance were configured for all fixed regression coefficients. For convergence assessment of INLA-based BAPC models, we set 10,000 internal optimisation iterations. Model convergence was confirmed by inspection of marginal posterior density and trace plots (Supplementary Fig. S1 and S2), alongside effective sample size (ESS) > 1000 for all estimated hyperparameters. The 95% uncertainty intervals for all projected rates were derived via log-scale back-transformation: exp [ln(μ)±1.96×(σ/μ)], where μ is the point estimate, and σ is the standard deviation (Bernard, 2000).

To further validate the robustness of our projections, we additionally used autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) models, a widely used time-series approach for mortality and morbidity forecasting, as an alternative method (Wu et al., 2023). ARIMA models were fit to aggregated annual data using automatic order selection, and forecasts were generated for the same time horizon as the BAPC model. The results of this comparison are presented in Supplementary Fig. S3. Univariate ARIMA time-series models were simply fitted as a descriptive reference for overall temporal trend comparison, yet ARIMA could not decompose age, period and cohort heterogeneity or produce age- and gender-stratified projections as achieved by the BAPC-INLA framework. All analyses were run in R v4.5.0 with three specified packages: INLA (v.24.06.18) as the core Bayesian computing engine, bapc (v.0.3.2) with the core function bapcbapc_inla() for APC modelling, and forecast (v.8.21) to build univariate ARIMA benchmarks through automatic lag selection via auto.arima().

Results

Global level

Globally, leishmaniasis remained a severe public health threat in 2021. The prevalent cases reached 6.21 × 106 (95% UI: 5.83 × 106–6.69 × 106), a 95% increase versus 1990. The ASPR rose slightly from 62.94 (95% UI: 52.92–76.63) per 100,000 in 1990 to 76.96 (95% UI: 71.89–82.99) per 100,000 in 2021, with EAPC of 0.71% (95% CI: 0.66–0.76%), notably lower than that of Southeast Asia (EAPC = 2.35%, 95% CI: 1.75–2.95%). The 2021 incident cases were 1.10 × 106 (95% CI: 9.70 × 105–1.24 × 106), up 8.39% since 1990, whereas ASIR declined from 17.82 (95% UI: 15.14–21.05) to 14.34 (95% UI: 12.66–16.19) per 100,000, with an EAPC of −0.51% (95% CI: −1.09–0.05%); by contrast, Southeast Asia presented a sharp ASIR rise (EAPC = 7.48%, 95% CI: 5.81–9.19%). In 2021, global leishmaniasis death cases were 5.48 × 103 (95% UI: 1.61 × 103–1.78 × 104), with ASMR of 0.07 per 100,000 (95% UI: 0.02–0.24, EAPC = −6.84%, 95% CI: −7.74 to −5.93%). Global DALYs were 7.81 × 105 (95% UI: 4.68 × 105–1.64 × 106), with ASDR of 10.27 per 100,000 (95% UI: 5.99–21.96, EAPC = −5.17%, 95% CI: −5.99 to −4.33%). Overall, ASPR increased globally from 1990 to 2021, while ASIR, ASMR and ASDR decreased (Table 1, Table 2, Table 3, Table 4 and Fig. 1).

Regional level

Leishmaniasis burden showed obvious socio-economic disparities correlated with the SDI. In 2021, low SDI regions had the highest ASPR (139.66 per 100,000; 95% UI: 125.52–156.55) and the fastest ASPR growth (EAPC = 1.74%, 95% CI: 1.46–2.03%), while high middle SDI regions had the lowest ASPR (11.91 per 100,000; 95% UI: 10.00–14.92). Low-middle SDI regions yielded the largest ASPR reduction (EAPC = −1.18%, 95% CI: −1.26 to −1.10%). For ASIR, low SDI regions had the highest rate (24.83 per 100,000), middle SDI regions showed the steepest ASIR increase (EAPC = 2.23%), and high SDI regions had the lowest ASIR (0.58 per 100,000) and the sharpest ASIR decline (EAPC = −4.72%). All SDI strata exhibited downward ASMR and ASDR trends, with low SDI regions obtaining the most prominent reductions (ASMR EAPC = −8.76%; ASDR EAPC = −7.53%), indicating effective local control programmes (Table 1, Table 2, Table 3, Table 4 and Fig. 1).

At the subregional level, North Africa and the Middle East had the highest 2021 ASPR (642.90 per 100,000, 95% UI: 592.06–712.93), followed by Andean Latin America (548.72 per 100,000, 95% UI: 487.32–619.84) and Tropical Latin America (297.45 per 100,000, 95% UI: 269.82–330.72), whereas high-income North America had the lowest ASPR (0.10 per 100,000, 95% UI: 0.06–0.16). East Asia achieved the greatest ASPR reduction (EAPC = −2.86%, 95% CI: −2.94 to −2.79%). ASIR distribution was consistent with ASPR patterns; Eastern sub-Saharan Africa had the largest ASIR drop (EAPC = −6.41%, 95% CI: −7.86 to −4.94%). Tropical Latin America, Central and Eastern sub-Saharan Africa had elevated ASMR (>0.30 per 100,000). Except for Western sub-Saharan Africa and Central Asia, all subregions had declining ASMR, with Central Europe showing the maximum decrease (EAPC = −10.93%, 95% CI: −11.81 to −10.04%). Tropical Latin America, North Africa and the Middle East bore the highest ASDR. ASDR decreased across most subregions except Western sub-Saharan Africa and high-income North America, with Central Europe leading the reduction (EAPC = −10.59%, 95% CI: −11.43 to −9.73%). ASMR and ASDR were negatively correlated with SDI (Table 1, Table 2, Table 3, Table 4, Fig. 2, Fig. 3).

National level

Between 1990 and 2021, there were significant disparities in the relative changes in leishmaniasis cases across 204 countries and regions worldwide (the GBD database only provides leishmaniasis-related data for 83 countries). In terms of ASPR, the Syrian Arab Republic ranked first in 2021 with 4734.44 cases per 100,000 population (95% UI: 4143.02–5554.19). Twenty-four countries showed an increasing trend, among which the Kingdom of Morocco had the largest increase (EAPC = 5.37%, 95% CI: 4.83–5.90%). In stark contrast, the Republic of Serbia (0.01 cases per 100,000 population; 95% UI: 0–0.04) and Romania (0.01 cases per 100,000 population; 95% UI: 0–0.01) had the lowest ASPR. Additionally, 59 countries, including Romania (EAPC = −18.12%, 95% CI: −19.49 to −16.72%) and the Republic of Serbia (EAPC = −14.53%, 95% CI: −15.91 to −13.14%), exhibited a significant downward trend in leishmaniasis prevalence. In terms of ASIR, the Syrian Arab Republic reported the highest leishmaniasis ASIR in 2021 at 1.97 × 103 cases per 100,000 population (95% UI: 1.48 × 103–2.53 × 103), far exceeding most countries (e.g. Romania with 0.02 cases per 100,000 population, 95% UI: 0–0.06). Among the 23 countries showing an upward trend, the Principality of Monaco demonstrated the most significant growth (EAPC = 10.06%, 95% CI: 7.83–12.35%). Conversely, Romania led the 60 countries with declining trends, registering an EAPC of −18.12% (95% CI: −19.49 to −16.72%). An analysis of 2021 leishmaniasis ASMR revealed that the Republic of South Sudan had the highest ASMR, at 6.13 cases per 100,000 population (95% UI: 3.18–9.92). Nineteen countries showed an upward trend, with the Principality of Monaco once again recording the most significant increase (EAPC = 10.24%, 95% CI: 7.44–13.11%). The remaining 64 countries exhibited a downward trend, with the Islamic Republic of Pakistan showing a striking decrease (EAPC = −24.71%, 95% CI: −26.95 to −22.41%). For ASDR, the Republic of South Sudan topped the list in 2021 at 443.04 cases per 100,000 population (95% UI: 230.74–725.39). Seventeen countries showed ASDR increases, with the Principality of Monaco again leading (EAPC = 10.03%, 95% CI: 7.27–12.86%). The remaining 66 countries, including Romania (EAPC = −18.08%, 95% CI: −19.56 to −16.57%), displayed decreasing trends (Fig. 2, Supplementary Table S1). This overlapping pattern highlights that these nations face substantial leishmaniasis burdens, impacting not only mortality rates but also overall population health and quality of life.

Age and gender patterns

Data analysing the global burden of leishmaniasis from 1990 to 2021 reveals striking gender and age disparities in its distribution. Regarding age-related patterns, ASPR exhibits a “cumulative rise” with advancing age: indicators for the under-5 age group remain notably low, climbing gradually from ages 15–19 to 45–49, accelerating after 45, and ultimately peaking in the elderly (over 75 years). Across all age groups, male prevalence consistently exceeds that of females. The age trend for ASIR aligns broadly with ASPR but with a key difference: it peaks first in the under-15 children’s group, then declines gradually before accelerating again in middle age (45–59 years) and reaching its highest point in the over-75 group. Within age subgroups, females under 25 show higher ASIR than males, while males dominate in all older age brackets. ASMR and ASDR mirror the trend observed in ASIR, with dual peaks in the under-15 children’s group and the over-75 elderly group. In every age category, male rates remain consistently higher than those in females.

In terms of leishmaniasis burden differences across SDI strata, there is a negative correlation between disease burden and SDI. High SDI regions exhibit low ASPR, ASIR, ASMR, and ASDR across all age groups, with gently rising trends over time and small gender gaps; males are only 1.2–1.5 times more affected than females. By contrast, low SDI regions show uniformly high values for all four metrics across ages, with sharp increases from middle to older adulthood. The very elderly (95+ years) in these regions have markedly higher peak values than elsewhere, and gender disparities are stark, with males experiencing 2–3 times the burden of females. Middle SDI regions fall midway in disease burden, with metric values and gender gaps between those of high and low SDI areas. From 1990 to 2021, gender differences here narrowed slightly but insignificantly. This consistent pattern holds for ASPR, ASIR, ASMR, and ASDR alike (Fig. 4, Fig. 5, Fig. 6, Fig. 7, Fig. 8).

Decomposition analysis

This study further decomposed leishmaniasis-related metrics to quantify the effects of population ageing, population growth and epidemiological changes on disease epidemiology during 1990–2021. For ASPR, leishmaniasis prevalence burden increased globally and across all SDI strata, with the maximum increment (1.40 × 106) in middle SDI regions. Globally, ageing, population growth and epidemiological changes contributed 1.54%, 78.50% and 19.95% to rising prevalence burden, respectively, and these three factors exerted the strongest effects in low-middle SDI regions (47.74%, 159.30%, and −107.04%, respectively). Globally, all three factors aggravated prevalence burden. Ageing reduced prevalence burden in high and low SDI regions, while population growth and epidemiological changes elevated the burden; middle SDI regions followed the global trend. By comparison, ageing and population growth increased prevalence burden in high-middle and low-middle SDI regions, while epidemiological changes offset such burden.

For ASIR, incidence burden rose globally and in middle SDI regions (2.20 × 105) but declined in other SDI regions. Global attribution values for burden reduction were −57.47% for ageing, 445.47% for population growth, and −288% for epidemiological changes, indicating population growth increased incidence burden, whereas ageing and epidemiological changes decreased it. Low SDI regions showed the most prominent factor impacts (296.01%, 8018.58%, −8414.58%), where ageing and population growth raised incidence burden, and epidemiological changes mitigated it.

ASMR presented consistent declining trends globally and across all SDI regions, with the largest reduction (−4.15 × 104) in low SDI regions. Global contributions to mortality burden reduction were −8.42% (ageing), 27.08% (population growth) and −118.66% (epidemiological changes). Ageing elevated mortality burden only in high SDI regions (9.43%), while population growth and epidemiological changes dominated burden changes in low SDI regions (66.91% and −162.36%). Overall, ageing and epidemiological changes reduced leishmaniasis mortality burden worldwide, and population growth increased it, especially in low SDI regions.

DALYs declined globally and in most regions, except high and middle SDI regions. Globally, ageing, population growth and epidemiological changes contributed −11.57%, 30.65%, and −119.08% to ASDR burden variation, respectively. Ageing and epidemiological changes lowered DALY burden, while population growth increased it, and this pattern was most obvious in low-middle SDI regions (Fig. 9, Supplementary Tables S2–S5).

Future forecasts of global burden of leishmaniasis

BAPC-based projections from 2022 to 2036 reveal heterogeneous trends in global leishmaniasis burden, which should be interpreted cautiously. As a historical-trend extrapolation model, BAPC predictions can be biased by climate change, population migration, armed conflicts, emerging drug resistance and updated surveillance systems. Globally, ASPR is predicted to increase, reaching 119.31 (95% UI: 86.54–164.48) per 100,000 in males and 84.87 (95% UI: 62.65–114.96) per 100,000 in females by 2036. By contrast, ASIR and ASMR will remain stable, with slight declines in male and female ASIR estimated at 15.41 (95% UI: 2.62–90.83) and 10.13 (95% UI: 1.57–65.56) per 100,000, and corresponding ASMR of 0.06 (95% UI: 0.00–1.21) and 0.03 (95% UI: 0.00–0.28) per 100,000. A mild decline is projected for ASDR, at 10.06 (95% UI: 1.73–58.39) per 100,000 in males and 6.03 (95% UI: 1.27–28.64) per 100,000 in females. Across the projected period, males exhibit higher ASPR, ASIR, ASMR and ASDR than females (Fig. 10, Supplementary Table S6).

Discussion

A comprehensive analysis of the global leishmaniasis burden from 1990 to 2021 revealed complex disparities and evolving trends across regions and SDI strata. Globally, ASPR of leishmaniasis showed a mild upward trend over the study period, whereas ASIR, ASMR and ASDR declined noticeably. These trends align with findings from prior GBD studies on leishmaniasis epidemiology (Zhang et al., 2025). The falling ASMR and ASDR are likely associated with improved VL management, including expanded early diagnosis and effective treatment, which reduced VL-related YLL and YLD (Zhang et al., 2025). In contrast, the stable or rising ASPR is largely linked to the chronic, non-lethal nature of CL and MCL, whose cases continue to accumulate in endemic populations (Carrer et al., 2026).

Marked regional disparities were observed across all four core indicators. Regions with low SDI presented markedly higher ASPR, ASIR, ASMR and ASDR, while high SDI regions maintained consistently low levels of these metrics. Based on existing literature and epidemiological reasoning, this gap is plausibly driven by combined socio-economic, environmental and healthcare-related factors. Low SDI settings typically feature poor sanitation, insufficient public health investment and inadequate vector management, which create favorable conditions for sand fly breeding and increase human exposure to vectors (Salah et al., 2020). Populations in these areas also engage in more outdoor labor with limited personal protection, and frequent cohabitation with animals facilitates parasite transmission (Abdullahi et al., 2024). In addition, under-resourced healthcare systems in low SDI regions lead to frequent missed or delayed diagnoses and limited access to medications (Sikorska et al., 2022). Concurrent issues including malnutrition, HIV co-infection and population displacement further exacerbate disease burden (Daoudi et al., 2019; Nweze et al., 2020). By comparison, high SDI regions implement effective sand fly control, standardise healthcare services, and maintain better living and working conditions, all of which lower transmission and adverse health outcomes (Maia-Elkhoury et al., 2021; Luo et al., 2022). These disparities reflect inequities in public health development and resource distribution across regions.

Regional epidemiological profiles also varied by dominant disease subtype. In North Africa and the Middle East, CL predominates, driving elevated ASPR and ASIR due to its chronic course and disability burden (Zhang et al., 2025). Arid and semi-arid landscapes in this region provide suitable habitats for sand flies, and long-term social unrest may disrupt routine vector control and healthcare services, potentially contributing to sustained transmission and accumulation of chronic cases (Al-Koleeby et al., 2022). Tropical Latin America recorded high ASMR and ASDR, primarily attributable to VL (Daraban Bocaneti et al., 2022). In many local areas, limited medical resources and uneven healthcare distribution delay VL treatment and cause deaths (Daraban Bocaneti et al., 2022). Local tropical climate, deforestation, agricultural expansion and loose management of reservoir hosts may further facilitate parasite transmission (Reguera et al., 2016; Santos et al., 2021). Malnutrition and HIV co-infection in vulnerable populations also worsen disease severity and disability (Nweze et al., 2020).

Several regions achieved notable improvements in leishmaniasis metrics. East Asia experienced the largest reduction in ASPR, which is plausibly linked to economic growth, strengthened public health systems, improved access to diagnosis and treatment, and enhanced environmental governance and vector control (Luo and Wang, 2022). Eastern sub-Saharan Africa showed the most substantial drop in ASIR, likely resulting from targeted vector control, improved sanitation, health education and reservoir host management that interrupted transmission chains (Dahl et al., 2021). Central Europe reported prominent declines in ASMR and ASDR, supported by well-established healthcare systems enabling rapid VL diagnosis and reliable drug supply, alongside robust disease surveillance to prevent large-scale outbreaks (Vandeputte et al., 2020).

Country-level trends further illustrate heterogeneous epidemiological patterns. For example, Morocco showed increases in all four age-standardised indicators (Daoudi et al., 2022). Hypothetically, compromised vector control, insufficient surveillance and diagnostic capacity, and suboptimal living conditions may collectively drive rising transmission, unrecognised cases and higher mortality and disability (Driemeier et al., 2015). Conversely, Romania achieved declines across all metrics, which may be attributed to standardised diagnosis and treatment networks, effective environmental and vector management, comprehensive surveillance and improved socioeconomic conditions (Daraban Bocaneti et al., 2022). It should be emphasised that the above interpretations of regional and country-level drivers are plausible hypotheses derived from ecological associations and published evidence.

Notably, despite falling age-standardised rates globally, the absolute number of prevalent and new leishmaniasis cases increased from 1990 to 2021, especially in low- and middle-income countries. Decomposition analysis confirmed that demographic factors, including population growth and population ageing served as major drivers of rising absolute disease burden, particularly in low- and low-middle SDI regions. By contrast, epidemiological interventions such as vector control, early diagnosis and treatment acted as protective factors and offset part of the upward pressure brought by demographic changes (Vandeputte et al., 2020; Carrer et al., 2026). These findings yield targeted public health implications: low SDI regions with the heaviest burden need intensified intervention alongside population-related planning; high SDI regions should prioritise management of chronic cases and age-related disease risks; middle and low-middle SDI regions require integrated strategies to reduce both mortality and prevalence. Overall, global leishmaniasis control needs differentiated, region-specific interventions and sustained investment in control systems.

Epidemiological disparities also existed across age and gender groups, shaped by immune status, behavioral patterns and exposure risks. In terms of age distribution, ASPR rose progressively with age and peaked among the elderly, which may be explained by age-related immune decline, underlying comorbidities and lifelong cumulative exposure to transmission environments; this pattern is most evident for chronic CL (Carvalho et al., 2015). ASIR, ASMR and ASDR peaked in populations aged under 15 years, as children and adolescents have immature immune function, higher outdoor vector exposure and greater risk of developing severe disease after infection, with VL being the main cause of mortality and severe disability (Andreottola et al., 2025). For gender differences, males generally carried a higher disease burden across most age groups in low SDI regions, largely due to higher occupational outdoor exposure to sand flies (Wenning et al., 2022). Biological factors such as androgens may also influence disease progression, though behavioral exposure is considered the primary driver (Sellau et al., 2020). Females aged under 25 presented relatively higher ASIR, possibly due to household outdoor activities and insufficient protective measures. Corresponding prevention strategies should adopt age- and gender-targeted protection and intervention measures. For high-risk children under 15, schools deliver health education, distribute child-safe repellents and special insecticidal nets, and organise regular screening for early leishmaniasis diagnosis. As males face higher outdoor sand fly exposure from farming and grazing, treated protective clothes and repellents are provided for field male workers. For females staying mostly indoors, guidance on household vector control and free bednet maintenance are offered to cut indoor biting risks. Customised health promotions for different age and gender groups help achieve precise leishmaniasis prevention.

Based on historical epidemiological extrapolation, the BAPC model preliminarily projected that global ASPR may rise from 2022 to 2036, accompanied by stable ASIR and ASMR, as well as a slight decline in ASDR. Notably, these modelled trends require cautious interpretation. This tentative projection may imply that new VL infections and mortality could be relatively controlled, whereas the cumulative burden of chronic CL cases is likely to increase (Talbi et al., 2020; Nightingale et al., 2024). Several contextual factors may contribute to such modelled trends: persistent transmission risks driven by poorly managed sand fly habitats in low-income endemic areas; restrained growth of new cases benefited from sustained vector control interventions; stabilised mortality facilitated by improved accessibility to standard anti-leishmanial therapies (Cota et al., 2013); and decreased disease-related disability attributable to updated diagnostic protocols and standardized clinical management, which may further lower global ASDR marginally. Future regional leishmaniasis trends could be modulated by population change, urbanisation, population ageing, climate change and inequitable healthcare allocation, potentially exacerbating inter-regional health disparities to an uncertain extent. Of note, steady incidence and mortality rates might potentially conceal the growing burden of chronic cases, which constitutes an underestimated public health challenge. Considering the inherent uncertainty of long-term model prediction, targeted strategies including stratified case management, rational healthcare resource distribution, as well as gender-specific and region-tailored prevention programmes are recommended to mitigate future disease burden.

The year 2021 fell during the peak of the COVID-19 pandemic, which exerted short-term impacts on leishmaniasis epidemiology. Redistribution of public health resources delayed leishmaniasis diagnosis, treatment and routine vector control, which may have contributed to the slight global ASPR increase in 2021 (Joshi et al., 2023). Meanwhile, movement restrictions temporarily reduced human exposure to sand flies and helped stabilise ASIR (Shams et al., 2023). Nevertheless, the pandemic did not alter the long-term epidemiological trends of leishmaniasis, given that the fundamental drivers of disease transmission remained unchanged, and access to life-saving treatments was maintained in high-burden areas.

This study, based on GBD datasets, has several limitations. First, severe underreporting, weak surveillance and inconsistent diagnostics in low-SDI endemic regions bring uncertainties to GBD estimates. Secondly, as all leishmaniasis subtypes are combined in the dataset, we cannot analyse VL, CL, MCL and PKDL separately, which hinders accurate attribution of mortality and disability burden. Thirdly, we only detected ecological associations between disease trends and relevant factors without quantitative causal verification, so related interpretations are merely reasonable hypotheses. Fourthly, the 2022–2036 projections fail to consider unforeseen events, including climate change, drug resistance and socio-political instability. Finally, this study did not conduct health inequality analysis to assess burden disparities by region and socio-economic status. Future work could integrate socio-economic and healthcare access indicators for equity-oriented exploration.

Conclusions

We systematically analysed the global leishmaniasis burden from 1990 to 2021 and projected trends for the next 15 years. While declining ASMR and ASDR reflect progress against leishmaniasis, the persistent and projected rise in ASPR highlights the ongoing challenge of leishmaniasis and its associated disability. Though ASRs decline, the burden grows, with notable disparities across SDI regions, ages and genders. Healthcare resources for low-SDI areas, males, children and the elderly should be prioritised. Future research should explore effective interventions, especially in high-burden regions, and focus on interactions between social behaviors, environment and demographics as key to the disease’s prevalence. Disentangling the epidemiology of leishmaniasis is essential for developing form-specific interventions.
