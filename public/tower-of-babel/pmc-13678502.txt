Green synthesis of copper oxide using Litchi chinensis leaf extract and nanocomposite formation with MXene for antimicrobial and antinematicidal activity
Authors: Rida Zameer, Wajid Rehman, Liaqat Rasheed, Thoraya A. Farghaly, Muhammad Shakeel, Muhammed Sajid, Abdullah Y. A. Alzahrani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to develop an eco-friendly CuO-loaded Ti3C2Tx MXene nanocomposite using Litchi chinensis leaf extract and evaluate its potential for antimicrobial and antinematicidal applications. The objective was to employ a green synthesis strategy in which the leaf extract served as a natural reducing, stabilizing, and capping agent for CuO nanoparticle formation, while Ti3C2Tx MXene was prepared from Ti3AlC2 through chemical etching. The CuO/MXene nanocomposite was fabricated by dispersing CuO nanoparticles with MXene to obtain a hybrid material with improved physicochemical and biological performance. FTIR analysis confirmed the presence of plant-derived functional groups and Cu–O bonding, indicating the successful formation and surface stabilization of CuO nanoparticles. XRD confirmed the crystalline nature of CuO; SEM showed CuO nanoparticles ranging from 82.82–147.53 nm, MXene sheets around 696.04–740.39 nm, and CuO/MXene particles between 118.03 and 134.37 nm. TGA showed high thermal stability with residues of 88.7% for CuO, 93.5% for MXene, and 85.22% for CuO/MXene. Antibacterial results showed inhibition zones of 11 mm for CuO against S. aureus and 8 mm against E. coli, while CuO/MXene showed 8 mm and 7 mm, respectively. Antinematicidal activity against C. elegans reached 45.67% for CuO, 67.67% for MXene, and 63% for CuO/MXene at 100 µg µl−1. Overall, the study revealed that the synthesized nanocomposites may serve as promising future bioagents.

Introduction

Nanotechnology has become an important field in modern materials science because nanomaterials show unique physical, chemical, and biological properties compared with their bulk forms.1 Copper oxide nanoparticles have gained strong research interest due to their low cost, good stability, high surface reactivity, and promising antimicrobial potential.2,3 CuO nanoparticles can interact with microbial cells, damage cell membranes, generate oxidative stress, and disturb normal cellular functions. Because of these properties, CuO-based materials are being explored for environmental, biomedical, and agricultural applications.4 In recent years, green synthesis has been preferred over conventional chemical methods because it is safer, cheaper, and more environmentally friendly,5–8 while nanoparticle synthesis via a chemical route can be toxic.9–12 Plant-mediated synthesis uses natural bioactive compounds such as flavonoids, phenolics, alkaloids, proteins, and organic acids as reducing and stabilizing agents.13 These compounds help convert metal salts into metal/metal oxide nanoparticles with controlled size, shape, and surface functionality.14Litchi chinensis leaf extract is a useful biological source for green synthesis because it contains several phytochemicals that can assist in nanoparticle formation and stabilization.15,16 Therefore, using Litchi chinensis leaves offers a sustainable route for preparing CuO nanoparticles.17–20

MXenes are another important class of two-dimensional materials that have attracted attention due to their layered structure, large surface area, surface functional groups, and good chemical reactivity.21 Tiwari et al. reported that Ti3C2Tx, MXene, commonly prepared from the Ti3AlC2 MAX phase through chemical etching, has shown strong potential in catalysis, sensors, energy storage, adsorption, and antimicrobial applications.22,23 The surface of MXene usually contains functional groups such as –O, –OH, and –F, which improve its interaction with other nanoparticles and biological systems.21,24–26 However, MXene alone may show limited biological activity in some cases, so combining it with metal oxide nanoparticles can improve its performance.27

Bacterial infections and nematode infestations are serious problems in medical, environmental, and agricultural fields.28 Pathogenic bacteria such as Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli can cause infections and contamination, while nematodes can affect plant health and reduce crop productivity.29 Therefore, there is a need to develop effective and eco-friendly materials that can control both microbial growth and nematode survival.29 The formation of CuO-loaded MXene nanocomposites can provide a useful strategy to enhance antimicrobial and antinematicidal activity. In such composites, CuO nanoparticles offer biological toxicity against microorganisms, while MXene provides a layered support with high surface area for better dispersion and interaction.5,30 This combination may reduce nanoparticle aggregation and improve contact with bacterial cells or nematodes.31 CuO/MXene nanocomposites may show improved activity compared with individual CuO or MXene materials.

In this study, CuO nanoparticles were synthesized using Litchi chinensis leaf extract, MXene was prepared from Ti3AlC2, and CuO/MXene nanocomposites were developed by combining both materials. The synthesized samples were characterized using FTIR, XRD, SEM, and TGA to confirm their structure, morphology, functional groups, and thermal stability. Their antibacterial activity was tested against S. aureus and E. coli, while antinematicidal activity was evaluated against Caenorhabditis elegans. This work aims to explore CuO/MXene nanocomposites as promising green materials for antimicrobial and agricultural applications.

Experimental

Material

Materials, which were integrated in this study were chosen due to their cuteness and applicability in the synthesis and characterization of the CuO/MXene nanocomposites. Copper(ii) chloride dihydrate (CuCl2·2H2O) was bought from Sigma-Aldrich, and it was used as the starting material in the production of CuO nanoparticles. Litchi chinensis leaf extract was obtained in Haripur, Pakistan and based on the green synthesis of CuO nanosystems, Litchi chinensis leaf extract was utilized. MXene was fabricated based on Ti3AlC2 (MAX phase) and the chemical etching process that was employed to fabricate the MXenes involved the purchase and utilisation of hydrofluoric acid (HF) and hydrochloric acid (HCl), both of which are manufactured by Merck. In the production of the CuO/MXene nanocomposites, deionized (DI) water was utilized in the whole process, and this guaranteed good dispersions of the nanoparticles. Intercalation and dispersion solvents MXene intercalation and dispersion solvents were dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), which was also acquired at Sigma-Aldrich.

Synthesis of Ti3C3Tx MXene

The synthesis of Ti3C2Tx MXene follows a well-established chemical etching method using a mixture of hydrofluoric acid (HF) and hydrochloric acid (HCl). Initially, Ti3AlC2 (MAX phase) is treated with a solution containing 10% HF (by weight) and 1 M HCl, typically in a 1 : 1 ratio, for 24–48 hours at room temperature to selectively etch away the aluminum layer. After etching, the resulting Ti3C2Tx MXene is thoroughly washed with deionized (DI) water to remove residual acids and impurities. The solution is then subjected to centrifugation to separate the MXene layers, which are then intercalated using dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to facilitate the delamination of MXene sheets into individual layers. Following this, the MXene is washed again with DI water and ethanol to remove any residual DMSO and other solvents, after which another round of centrifugation is performed. Finally, the MXene sheets are subjected to ultra-sonication to further assist in delamination, resulting in single- or few-layered Ti3C2 MXene. The product is then dried in a vacuum oven at 60 °C to obtain the final material (Fig. 1).

Collection and preparation of Litchi chinensis leaf extract

Fresh Litchi chinensis leaves were collected from Haripur, Pakistan. The plant was identified and authenticated by a taxonomist (Dr Alia Gul) at the Department of Botany, Hazara University, Mansehra. The plant is deposited in the Digital Herbarium, Department of Botany, Hazara University, Mansehra (Voucher specimens: 30/18084 dated 04-05-2026). The leaves were selected for their freshness and absence of visible damage. The collected leaves were dried under shade to avoid degradation of sensitive compounds due to direct sunlight. The drying process was repeated until the leaves got fully dried. The leaves were then dried and then crushed with the help of a pestle and mortar so as to get fine powder. This is necessary in order to enhance the surface area of the leaves and thus make the extraction process easier. 2 Grams of the powdered Litchi chinensis leaves were dissolved in 100 mL of distilled water and stirred. The mixture was left to steep 2–3 hours without stirring, at times, then stirred at 300 rpm for 6 h, so that bioactive compounds were effectively extracted. Following extraction, the mixture was filtered to remove any solid particles, leaving behind the liquid extract for further use in synthesizing nanoparticles (Fig. 2).

Preparation of Litchi chinensis leaf extract.

Phytochemical analysis of Litchi chinensis leaf extract

Phytochemical screening of aqueous Litchi chinensis leaf extract was performed through qualitative chemical tests for the detection of different secondary metabolites and other phytoconstituents. For all the analyses, aqueous leaf extract was freshly prepared. Molisch's, Benedict's and Fehling's tests were used to study the carbohydrates. Mayer's reagent, Wagner's reagent and Dragendorff's reagent were used in the screening of alkaloids. The foam test was used for the evaluation of saponins and modified Borntrager's test for the detection of glycosides. The presence of phytosterols and terpenoids was determined by Salkowski's test. A standard flavonoid screening test was done for flavonoids. The proteins and amino acids were determined by the xanthoproteic, the ninhydrin and the Millon's tests and the presence of tannins was determined by the ferric chloride test. The proper sample volume of the aqueous extract was reacted with the particular reagent and was monitored for a characteristic color change, precipitate formation or foaming. The reactions observed were graded on a semi-quantitative scale. The presence (+) or absence (−) of the respective phytochemicals as well as the moderate (++) or comparatively abundant (+++) response are indicated. All screening tests were conducted in the same laboratory with freshly prepared reagents.

Synthesis of copper oxide (CuO) nanoparticles

To begin with, 9 g of copper(ii) chloride dihydrate (CuCl2·2H2O) is added to 100 mL of distilled water. Magnetic stirrer is used to stir the solution until copper salt had fully dissolved. 10 mL Of Litchi chinensis leaves (Section 2.2) was transferred to the copper chloride solution.15,32 This extract is the reducing agent in the synthesis. The pH of the solution was maintained at 9 using the 1 M NaOH. On addition of NaOH, the solution color will change and change to a brownish color which signifies the initial stages of copper oxide nanoparticles formation. The subsequent change in color of the solution to dark greenish-black is the indication that copper oxide (CuO) nanoparticles are formed. This transformation is also accompanied by the emergence of precipitates in the solution. The mixture is dried in an oven afterwards. The dry process just aids in eliminating any surplus water and organic content and, thus, the solid copper oxide nanoparticles get formed. The centrifugation (30 minutes) of the dried sample is used to remove the nanoparticles to the reaction mixture. This process is used in the separation of the CuO nanoparticles with the liquid. The obtained precipitates are then sonicated over a period of 30 minutes to dissolve any agglomerates and make the CuO nanoparticles uniformly dispersed (Fig. 3), the percentage yield of CuO nanoparticles obtained was 26.1%.

Synthesis of copper oxide (CuO) nanoparticles.

Synthesis of CuO/MXene nanocomposite

In the process of making the CuO/MXene nanocomposite, copper oxide nanoparticles (CuO NPs) (0.25 g) were first dispersed in 5 mL of distilled water. Sonication of the mixture took a period of 30 minutes to make sure that the nanoparticles were dispersed evenly. In the meantime, 0.1 g of MXene is mixed in 10 mL of 10% acetone solution. To promote the dissolution and calibration of the layers of MXene, the solution of the material is stirred at 300 rpm after 30 minutes. After the preparation of the two solutions, the CuO NPs dispersion is introduced to the MXene solution. The two-component mixture is then left to mix for 30 minutes in order to achieve the proper mixing of the two elements. After this, sonication is done on the whole mixture to have a uniform dispersion of the CuO/MXene nanocomposites. A sonication process is followed after which the mixture is allowed to dry overnight in an appropriate environment. After drying, CuO/MXene nanocomposite is crushed, and it is used again, the obtained yield of the nanocomposites was 40.8%. The Schematic diagram is shown in Fig. 4.

Characterization techniques

The FTIR spectroscopy is used to determine the functional groups in the CuO/MXene nanocomposites. FTIR analysis was done in the following way: A portion of dried CuO/MXene nanocomposite was blended with a little potassium bromide (KBr), to create a pellet. The FTIR spectra were taken between the period of 4000 and 400 cm−1 in PerkinElmer FTIR Spectrometer in Central Research Laboratory, Hazara University Mansehra. XRD analysis was carried out using a Bruker D8 Advance X-ray diffractometer with Cu-Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) at the Institute of Space Technology, Islamabad. The diffraction patterns were recorded from 10° to 80° (2θ) at a scan rate of 0.02° per second. The XRD peaks were matched with standard reference data from the JCPDS database to identify the phases present in the CuO/MXene nanocomposites. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was employed to investigate the elemental composition and surface chemical states of MXene and CuO/MXene. Survey spectra were recorded, followed by high-resolution scans of C 1s, Cu 2p, and O 1s regions. Peak deconvolution was performed after background correction to assign individual chemical environments. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) were used to examine the morphology, CuO distribution, lattice structure, and interfacial features of the CuO–Ti3C2Tx MXene nanocomposite. Selected-area electron diffraction (SAED) was further employed to evaluate the crystalline nature of the synthesized composite. SEM is utilized to observe the morphology and surface structure of the CuO/MXene nanocomposites. The following protocol was used: a small amount of the CuO/MXene nanocomposite was mounted on an aluminum stub using carbon tape. The sample was then sputter-coated with a thin layer of gold to prevent charging under the electron beam. SEM analysis was performed using a Hitachi SU-70 SEM at an accelerating voltage of 5 kV at the Institute of Space Technology, Islamabad. Nitrogen adsorption–desorption measurements were performed to evaluate the textural properties of pristine MXene and CuO/MXene. The samples were degassed before analysis and examined using a surface-area and porosity analyzer with N2 as the adsorbate at 77 K. Adsorption–desorption isotherms were recorded over the relative-pressure range P/P0 = 0–1.1, while pore-size distributions were derived from the desorption branch using a standard BJH-type approach. Raman spectroscopy was used to investigate structural and vibrational changes in MXene, CuO, and CuO/MXene using a Raman spectrometer equipped with a visible-laser excitation source during spectral acquisition. TGA is employed to assess the thermal stability and decomposition behavior of the CuO/MXene nanocomposites. The following procedure was followed: Approximately 10 mg of the CuO/MXene nanocomposite was placed in a platinum crucible. The TGA was conducted using a Mettler Toledo TGA/DSC1 thermogravimetric analyzer, Central Research Laboratory, Hazara University Mansehra. The sample was heated from room temperature to 800 °C at a constant heating rate of 10 °C per minute in a nitrogen atmosphere to avoid oxidation.

Applications

Antibacterial activity

The antibacterial activity of CuO nanoparticles, MXene, and CuO/MXene nanocomposites was evaluated using the agar well diffusion method against two bacterial strains: Staphylococcus aureus (Gram-positive) and Escherichia coli (Gram-negative). The bacterial strains were cultured in nutrient agar plates. Wells were created in the agar using a sterile borer, and different concentrations of CuO nanoparticles, MXene, and CuO/MXene nanocomposites were introduced into the wells. The plates were then incubated at 37 °C for 24 hours. The areas of inhibition around the individual wells were then measured after incubation to determine the levels of antibacterial actions of the materials. The outcomes were compared with a control group (e.g., standard antibiotic or solvent) to find the relative performance of the nanocomposites in the fight against bacterial strains.

Antinematicidal activity

Caenorhabditis elegans (C. elegans) was used as a model organism in measurement of the antinematicidal of the CuO nanoparticles, MXene, and CuO/MXene nanocomposites. The nanomaterials of different concentrations were made and applied to synchronized groups of C. elegans. A duration of 48 hours was chosen after which the nanomaterials were exposed to the nematodes and survival rate, movement and morphology of the worms were observed under a microscope. The antinematicidal activity was determined as mortality and morphological change of the nematodes. The outcomes were contrasted with control group that comprised of untreated C. elegans or the one treatment with a commercial nematicides, in order to evaluate the efficacy of the CuO and MXene nanocomposites and the CuO/MXene nanocomposites in the control of nematodes. The cytotoxicity was also determined and any major decrease on the survival of nematodes was indicative of efficacy of the nanocomposites.

Results and discussion

The results and discussion of the synthesized CuO nanoparticles, MXene and CuO/MXene nanocomposites are introduced. These were tested in terms of the antibacterial and antinematicidal properties, with the greatest antibacterial performance of CuO and the increased cytotoxicity of CuO/MXene on C. elegans. The outcome of characterization proves the effective synthesis and characteristics of the nanocomposites.

Phytochemical analysis of Litchi chinensis leaf extract

Qualitative phytochemical screening showed that the water extract of Litchi chinensis contained multiple classes of biologically active compounds. The presence of soluble carbohydrate components in the extract was confirmed by the positive results of the three tests. The three tests, Molisch's test, Benedict's test and Fehling's test, were used to confirm the presence of soluble carbohydrate components in the extract. The alkaloid test results were as follows: Mayer's test was negative, Dragendorff's test was negative, and Wagner's test was strongly positive (+++), indicating the presence of Wagner-reactive alkaloidal constituents. The varying sensitivities and selectivities of the qualitative alkaloid tests must be taken into account and should only be considered as tentative evidence of the presence of different alkaloids. Foam test did not give any positive reaction in any sample, while modified Bornträger's test gave positive in all the samples tested for glycosides. No phytosterols and terpenoids were found under the Salkowski test conditions. The aqueous extract was positive for flavonoids, suggesting the presence of the flavonoid type constituents in the extract. Proteins and amino acids were also identified; moderate responses (++) were obtained in the xanthoproteic and ninhydrin test, and a positive (+) response was obtained in Millon's test. Ferric chloride test was also used for the detection of tannins. In conclusion, the results indicate that aqueous extract of L. chinensis contains carbohydrates, flavonoids, glycosides, tannin, amino acids, proteins and alkaloid-reactive constituents. Flavonoids, tannins, and other oxygen or nitrogen containing biomolecules are found in the extract, suggesting a possible chemical mechanism for their interaction with CuO precursor species and CuO surface during the preparation of CuO nanoparticles. These are qualitative tests, however, and do not identify which individual phytochemicals are responsible specifically for reduction, nucleation, or stabilization. Hence these constituents cannot be regarded as confirmed reducing or capping agents, but as potential agents for green synthesis (Table 1).

Fig. 5(b) shows the FTIR spectrum of MXene will provide important data related to the functional groups present in it whose presence determines its composition and structure. It was observed that there were three major peaks at certain wavenumbers: this peak is the Ti–C bond stretching, as it is one of the primary characteristics of MXene materials.38 This peak existence proves that there is an interaction between titanium (Ti) and the carbon (C) atoms in the MXene structure, meaning that titanium atoms are bonded to carbon atoms which is a characteristic feature of the MXene family of materials.39 The highest value of 640 cm−1 is related to Ti–O bending vibration. This implies that there is oxygen atom coordination to the titanium atoms in MXene, and this is in line with the oxygen-contamination terminations to the surfaces of MXenes namely the Ti–O bonds. The interactions that the material has with other species, e.g. in catalysis or adsorption applications are also essential to this functional group. The highest peak of 510 cm−1 is tied to Ti–O, vibration.40 This is yet another confirmation that the MXene structure contains titanium–oxygen bonds. Ti–O stretching vibration is linked to the connection between titanium and oxygen atoms and this is part of the structural integrity and functionality of MXene materials. On the whole, these FTIR peaks prove the formation and integrity of MXene, in general, and the presence of titanium–carbon and titanium–oxygen bonds. It is also possible to consider the identified functional groups and assume that MXene can be widely used in different applications in energy storage, sensors, or catalysis because of the nature of surface chemistry and reactivity that are peculiar to MXene.41

Fig. 5(c) shows the FTIR spectrum of the CuO/MXene nanocomposites samples presents a number of observable functional groups that are expected characteristics of both materials used as the components of the nanocomposites, CuO and MXene. A wide peak with 3450 cm−1 is a sign of the OH stretching vibration which is demonstrative of the presence of hydroxyl groups and it may be connected to water molecules or hydroxyl groups that are on the MXene or CuO nanoparticles.42 The highest peak of the spectrum at 2990 cm−1 is related to C–H stretching that reveals the existence of aliphatic hydrocarbons, which could be attributed to organic functional groups located on the surface of the nanocomposite. Its peak at 1680 cm−1 may be attributed to CO stretching implying the presence of carbonyl group either as a result of the organic molecules employed in the synthesis process or a remnant of the compounds in the nanocomposite.43 Also, the maximum at 1450 cm−1 is associated with C–H bending, and the maximum at 1120 cm−1 is corresponded with C–O stretching as a further confirmation of organic compounds on the surface of the nanocomposites. The structure with the peaked characteristic at 840 cm−1 and 570 cm−1 is suggestive of the Cu–O stretch, which justifies the presence of copper oxide in the nanocomposites. These findings indicate the effective synthesis of the CuO/MXene nanocomposites, whose functional groups are diverse and that are likely to add value to their superior functionality including the antimicrobial and antinematicidal effects. The FTIR results affirm addition of both CuO and MXene and several organic functional groups, which render the nanocomposites to a variety of applications.44

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis

Fig. 6(a) shows the XRD image of the CuO nanoparticles depicts the emergence of very sharp peaks which are attributed to a few crystallographic planes which are a confirmation of the highly crystalline structure. The peaks observed are coded based on the Miller indices, and the large ones with the values of 2θ values of 30°, 35°, and 38° are typical of the CuO phase. These peaks will be (021), (110) and (002) respectively. Other peaks of 45°, 53°, 58°, 62°, and 72° are ascribed to the planes (111), (022), (202) and (113) (022), (220), and (222). The planes are in line with the crystal structure that is monoclinic crystal structure of CuO verified with availability of above-mentioned diffraction peaks. Lattice parameters of the XRD data are a = 0.4685 nm, b = 0.3425 nm, c = 0.5130 nm, and they match the overall values of CuO using JCPDS card number 45–0937.45 The well-sharpened peaks and the high intensity of the strongest diffraction signals prove that the crystallinity of the CuO nanoparticles is prominent. These enhancements prove the effective preparation of the CuO nanoparticles in monoclinic crystal structure that would be applied in various ways like antimicrobial use. The peaks in the diffraction graph are also indicative that the CuO nanoparticles were crystallized uniformly, which may be of importance in other applications, such as catalysis, energy storage, and remediation of the environment.44

XRD of (a) CuO NPs, (b) MXene, and (c) CuO/MXene nanocomposites.

Fig. 6(b) shows the X-ray diffraction pattern of MXene shows a clear peak at the 2θ = 10–25° and which reflects a highly crystallized structure. The high crystallinity of MXenes is indicated by this peak shape whereby MXenes are highly crystallized materials. This sharpness of the peak also proves that the amorphous phases of the MXene are not very strong, but it carries a well-organized crystalline structure. Conversely, this is also evidenced by the relatively low intensity at the higher angles meaning that the MXene crystallized into a highly exfoliated, layered form with minimal interlayer stacking. This is expected in MXenes because they are also famous to exfoliate into thin, single layers and thus the broadening of the peaks at higher 2θ values and reduction in intensity dominance above the range of 2θ = 20°. The XRD analysis indicates that MXene was successfully synthesized and obtained at the desired structural properties, which means that it can be utilized in different fields, including energy storage, catalysis, and sensors, because it has a large surface area and defines crystalline properties. This crystalline property gives the material stability and application in a wide range of functional applications.46

Fig. 6(c) shows the XRD pattern of CuO/MXene nanocomposite gives distinct peaks at 2θ values of 30°, 35°, and 38° corresponding to the crystallographic planes of 111, 110 and 022, respectively. Both these peaks indicate the presence of a well-defined crystalline structure of nanoparticles of CuO. The sharp peak at 30° (111) suggests that there is high level of crystallinity at the CuO phase. These peaks confirm that the synthesis of the CuO in the nanocomposite was successful.45 Besides, other peaks of higher 2θ values including 62° (220) and 70° (222) are present. These peaks additionally indicate that the CuO phase is crystalline and show that the incorporation of the CuO nanoparticles in to the MXene structure is successful. The increased upper angle peaks also indicate that the CuO nanoparticles are well dispersed through the MXene crystal structure, and this also adds to the success of the nanocomposite. The general XRD profile of the CuO/MXene nanocomposite demonstrates good evidence in the crystalline nature of the CuO phase in the MXene. This confirms that the synthesis technique was effective in allowing the form of a well-ordered material that would be applicable in many things, including antimicrobial, and antinematicidal. The distinct and undulating peaks, and the lack of broad peaks that signify the amorphous material only serve to proof the good quality and structural integrity of the CuO/MXene nanocomposite, and this is a good indication that the material can be used in the various functional applications in material science and nanotechnology.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS)

The XPS spectra provide evidence for the elemental composition and surface chemical states of the synthesized MXene and CuO/MXene nanocomposite. As shown in Fig. 7(a), the survey spectrum of pristine MXene contains characteristic signals corresponding to C 1s, Ti 2p, O 1s, and F 1s. After CuO incorporation, an additional Cu 2p signal appears in the CuO/MXene spectrum, confirming the presence of copper-containing species on the MXene-based material. The high-resolution C 1s spectrum, Fig. 7(b), was deconvoluted into four components located at approximately 282.2, 284.8, 286.4, and 288.7 eV. These peaks are assigned to C–Ti, C–C, C–O, and CO chemical environments, respectively. The C–Ti contribution represents the carbide framework characteristic of Ti3C2Tx MXene, whereas the oxygen-containing carbon components are associated with surface functional groups. Fig. 7(c) shows the Cu 2p spectrum, with major peaks at 932.7 and 952.6 eV corresponding to Cu 2p3/2 and Cu 2p1/2, respectively.46 The spin–orbit separation is approximately 19.9 eV. Additional satellite features observed near 944.2 and 962.6 eV support the presence of Cu(ii) species, consistent with CuO formation. The O 1s spectrum of MXene, Fig. 7(d), contains components at approximately 529.9, 531.4, and 532.0 eV, attributed to Ti–O, C–Ti–Ox, and hydroxyl-containing surface terminations, respectively. After CuO loading, Fig. 7(e) shows O 1s components near 530.0, 530.4, and 531.9 eV, assigned to Ti–O, Cu–O, and C–Ti–(OH)x environments. The emergence of the Cu–O contribution together with changes in the O 1s region supports successful incorporation of CuO into the MXene composite and indicates modification of the surface chemical environment.47

TEM/HRTEM analysis

The TEM images provide direct evidence of the morphology and nanoscale association of CuO with Ti3C2Tx MXene. Fig. 8(a) shows the characteristic stacked, layered morphology of MXene, with CuO nanoparticles distributed mainly along the outer surface and edges of the sheets. The higher-magnification image in Fig. 8(b) further reveals nanosized CuO domains associated with the MXene substrate, supporting successful formation of the CuO/MXene nanocomposite. The HRTEM image in Fig. 8(c) displays well-resolved lattice fringes from both components. An interplanar spacing of approximately 0.251 nm is assigned to the Ti3C2Tx MXene (006) plane, whereas a spacing of about 0.232 nm corresponds to the CuO (111) plane. The simultaneous observation of these lattice fringes within the same region provides stronger evidence of intimate nanoscale contact between CuO and MXene than SEM alone. Fig. 8(d) presents the selected-area electron diffraction pattern, showing distinct diffraction spots and ring-like features. These features indicate the presence of crystalline domains within the composite and are consistent with the coexistence of crystalline CuO and MXene-related structures. BET analysis, pore-size distribution analysis

The nitrogen adsorption–desorption results demonstrate a clear improvement in the textural properties of MXene after CuO incorporation. In Fig. 10(a), CuO/MXene shows a consistently higher N2 adsorption volume than pristine MXene throughout the investigated relative-pressure range. Both samples exhibit noticeable hysteresis and a pronounced uptake at high relative pressure, indicating the presence of mesoporous/interparticle void structures. The larger adsorption capacity of CuO/MXene suggests an increased accessible surface area and pore volume, which can provide more exposed active sites for surface-dependent processes (Fig. 10).

(a) N2 sorption, (b) pore-size distribution, and (c) Raman spectra of MXene and CuO/MXene.

The pore-size distribution in Fig. 10(b) further supports this interpretation. CuO/MXene displays a broader and more intense pore distribution than MXene, with substantial contributions from small pores and additional features in the mesoporous range, particularly near approximately 20 nm and 40–50 nm. In contrast, pristine MXene shows a much lower pore-volume contribution over most of the measured diameter range. This difference indicates that CuO decoration modifies the stacking or aggregation behavior of MXene sheets and generates additional accessible voids between particles and layers.

Raman spectroscopic analysis

The Raman spectra in Fig. 10(c) provide complementary evidence of structural modification. Pristine MXene exhibits characteristic low-wavenumber vibrations together with broader bands at higher Raman shifts. CuO shows its own low-frequency vibrational features, whereas the CuO/MXene composite exhibits enhanced and broadened Raman bands, including the marked Ti–C-related region and strong features in the approximately 1200–1650 cm−1 range. The changes in intensity, band shape, and position relative to the individual components indicate interaction between CuO and the MXene framework. Overall, the combined adsorption, pore-distribution, and Raman results confirm successful structural integration of CuO with MXene and show that CuO incorporation improves porosity while modifying the local bonding environment. These combined changes are advantageous for applications requiring interfacial reactions because a porous network can facilitate reactant diffusion, improve electrolyte or gas accessibility, and increase utilization of active sites.

The high percentage of residue also suggests the purity and integrity of the CuO phase, there is no severe decomposition and loss of material in the heating process. Applications

Antibacterial activity

This is demonstrated by the outcomes of the antibacterial testing, in which the CuO nanoparticles have the highest antibacterial efficacy against Staphylococcus aureus (Gram-positive), with a zone of inhibition standing at 11 mm (Fig. 12). This hints at the high efficacy of CuO nanoparticles toward Gram-positive bacteria, which is probably explained by the already developed process of interaction of CuO nanoparticles with cell membranes of bacteria, causing their structural damage and subsequent death.42 The ability of CuO to inhibit S. aureus is better than that of other materials, and this fact supports the idea of using the substance as an antibacterial agent. MXene, in its turn, showed comparably weak antibacterial capability, and its zone of inhibition was 6 mm was observed against S. aureus, and 5 mm was observed against Gram-negative Escherichia coli.50 These findings indicate that MXene is weak as a single agent with respect to antibacterial activity. The MXene compound is characterized with the highly conductive, layered structure which may contribute to its interaction with bacterial cells, but its own antibacterial potential is not as high as the one of the CuO nanoparticles. The relatively weak areas of inhibiting the two types of bacteria suggest that MXene can be improved or functionalized to create more potent antibacterial activity.44

The CuO/MXene nanocomposites showed a significant improvement in antibacterial activity compared to MXene alone, with zones of inhibition of 8 mm for S. aureus and 7 mm for E. coli.51 The combination of CuO and MXene, where the CuO nanoparticles provide strong antibacterial action, while the MXene layers assist in increasing the surface area for interaction with bacterial cells. The CuO/MXene nanocomposites, therefore, appear to be more effective than MXene alone, making them a promising candidate for antimicrobial applications (Table 2). ROS generation, membrane damage, or oxidative stress on bacteria and nematodes

The antibacterial and nematocidal activities of CuO/MXene may be attributed to multiple interacting mechanisms (Fig. 15). The CuO nanoparticles decorated on MXene sheets can promote the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), including hydroxyl radicals (˙OH), superoxide radicals (O2˙−), and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). Excessive ROS production may induce oxidative stress by damaging essential biomolecules such as lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids. In bacterial cells, ROS-mediated oxidative damage together with direct contact between MXene nanosheets and the microbial surface can disrupt the cell wall and plasma membrane, resulting in leakage of intracellular components, metabolic imbalance, and reduced cell viability. For nematodes, CuO/MXene exposure may affect the external cuticle structure and increase surface permeability, facilitating interaction between nanoparticles and biological tissues. The generated ROS may disturb cellular components and mitochondrial function, leading to impaired energy metabolism, oxidative stress, reduced motility, and physiological dysfunction. Additionally, Cu2+ ions released from CuO nanoparticles may contribute to toxicity through redox reactions and interactions with cellular molecules. However, ROS generation, membrane damage, and oxidative stress pathways were not directly measured in the present study; therefore, these mechanisms are proposed based on reported CuO- and MXene-based nanomaterials and require further experimental validation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, CuO nanoparticles, MXene, and CuO/MXene nanocomposites were prepared and their antibacterial and antinematodal properties were comparatively evaluated. The Litchi chinensis leaf extract green synthesis method was found to be a good method of producing CuO nanoparticles whereas MXene was prepared by the chemical etching method which proved to produce high quality exfoliated sheets of MXene. The successful preparation of the materials was ensured by the characterization of the materials by FTIR, XRD, SEM and TGA, which indicated that CuO, MXene and CuO/MXene nanocomposites had different structural and thermal characteristics successfully. FTIR analysis showed that both CuO and MXene have characteristic functional groups and XRD patterns indicated that both CuO and MXene are crystalline and the MXene is layered. SEM images revealed that the distribution of CuO nanoparticles in MXene surface was uniform, TGA revealed that all materials were highly thermally stable. The maximum inhibition zone on Staphylococcus aureus was found with the antibacterial activity of the CuO nanoparticles, then came CuO/MXene nanocomposites which had a significant inhibition on both S. aureus and Escherichia coli. The CuO/MXene nanocomposites were found to have a greater antibacterial effect which showed that MXene synergized with CuO. As far as antinematicidal activity is concerned, CuO nanoparticles were found to be moderately cytotoxic to C. elegans, although MXene was less active. CuO/MXene nanocomposites were found to exhibit high cytotoxicity and the best level was detected at 100 µgM µl−1 proved that the nanocomposites could be used in antinematicidal applications. These results point to the possible positive uses of CuO/MXene nanocomposites in antimicrobial and agricultural practice, which could provide an environmentally friendly alternative to control the bacterial infections and nematode infestations. To validate the surface chemistry, chemical states, phase composition, and detailed nanoscale morphology of the synthesized nano-material Raman, XPS, and TEM analyses are recommended for future work.
