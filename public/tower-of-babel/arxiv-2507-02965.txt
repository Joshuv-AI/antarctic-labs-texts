Concept-based Adversarial Attack: a Probabilistic Perspective
Andi Zhang, Xuan Ding, Steven McDonagh, Samuel Kaski

Abstract
We propose a concept-based adversarial attack framework that extends beyond single-image perturbations by adopting a probabilistic perspective. Rather than modifying a single image, our method operates on an entire concept — represented by a probabilistic generative model or a set of images — to generate diverse adversarial examples. Preserving the concept is essential, as it ensures that the resulting adversarial images remain identifiable as instances of the original underlying category or identity. By sampling from this concept-based adversarial distribution, we generate images that maintain the original concept but vary in pose, viewpoint, or background, thereby misleading the classifier. Mathematically, this framework remains consistent with traditional adversarial attacks in a principled manner. Our theoretical and empirical results demonstrate that concept-based adversarial attacks yield more diverse adversarial examples and effectively preserve the underlying concept, while achieving higher attack efficiency.

Introduction

Adversarial attacks aim to deceive a classifier while preserving the original meaning of the input object
[
3
,
9
,
24
,
25
]
. We refer to the manipulated instance as an adversarial example. Early work by
Szegedy et al. 2014
introduced adversarial attacks against deep learning models for images.

In the image-based adversarial setting, it is widely accepted that controlling the geometric distance between the adversarial example and its original image is crucial for maintaining the original image’s meaning. Consequently, many adversarial attack algorithms constrain perturbations using norms such as
L_{1}
,
L_{2}
, or
L_{\infty}
. Moreover, given the rapid progress in machine learning research, fair comparisons across different adversarial methods have become essential. Numerous benchmarks and competitions
[
8
,
26
]
therefore focus on attack success rates under the constraint that geometric distance does not exceed a threshold
\delta
.

However, as adversarial defense techniques improve, small geometric perturbations alone increasingly fail to generate adversarial examples that reliably fool classifiers — particularly when strong transferability is required
[
2
,
38
,
45
]
. This shortcoming has led researchers to explore unrestricted adversarial attacks, which involve larger geometric perturbations. Although “unrestricted” implies that adversarial examples need not be bounded by strict geometric distance, these examples must still remain faithful to the semantics of the original image; otherwise, the core goal of preserving the input’s meaning is lost.

Zhang et al. 2024b
introduced a probabilistic perspective on adversarial attacks, showing that traditional geometric constraints can be interpreted as specific “distance” distributions
p_{\text{dis}}
. Generating adversarial examples then amounts to sampling from the product of
p_{\text{dis}}
and the “victim” distribution
p_{\text{vic}}
(representing the target classifier under attack). This approach, depicted on the left side of Figure
1
, indicates that the distance distribution
p_{\text{dis}}
need not stem solely from geometric constraints; instead, one can fit a probabilistic generative model (PGM) around the original image so that the PGM’s semantic understanding implicitly defines a semantics-based distance.

Building on this probabilistic perspective, we expand the distance distribution from operating on a single image to operating on an entire concept representing an object. As shown on the right side of Figure
1
, this generalization introduces a new class of adversarial attacks. Rather than perturbing a single image, we generate a fresh image that captures the same underlying concept yet deceives the classifier. We refer to this approach as a concept-based adversarial attack. Mathematically, it remains consistent with traditional adversarial attacks when viewed under the probabilistic framework. As we demonstrate, broadening the distance distribution to concept-level information reduces its gap from the victim distribution
p_{\text{vic}}
, resulting in substantially higher attack success rates.

Our main contributions are as follows:

•

Concept-based adversarial attack
: We introduce a new type of adversarial attack that moves beyond single-image perturbations to a concept described by a set or a model. Under a probabilistic perspective, this new approach aligns with traditional adversarial attacks.

•

Theoretical and empirical validation
: We provide both theoretical proof and experimental evidence showing that expanding the attack from a single image to an entire concept reduces the distance between
p_{\text{vic}}
and
p_{\text{dis}}
.

•

Concept augmentation
: We propose a practical concept augmentation strategy using modern generative models, enhancing the diversity of the distance distribution.

•

Higher success rates
: Our experiments confirm that concept-based adversarial attacks achieve higher targeted attack success rates while preserving the original concept.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Probabilistic Generative Models (PGMs) and Their Likelihoods

The goal of probabilistic generative models is to build a parameterized model
p_{\theta}
that captures the true distribution
p
. However, in practice, we only have access to a finite dataset
\mathcal{D}=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
, where
n
is the size of the dataset. A common approach is to maximize the likelihood of this dataset.

For image modeling, popular models such as Variational Autoencoders (VAEs)
[
20
]
and diffusion models
[
14
,
37
]
typically do not maximize the likelihood directly. Instead, they maximize a lower bound on the log-likelihood (often referred to as the evidence lower bound, or ELBO). Given an image
x
, the corresponding likelihood
p_{\theta}(x)
can thus be estimated via this lower bound. In practice, the process of likelihood evaluation involves
calculating this ELBO to estimate the likelihood
[
4
,
27
]
.

2.2
Adversarial Attack

Given a classifier
C:[0,1]^{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
, where
n
is the input dimension and
\mathcal{Y}
is the label space, consider an original image
x_{\text{ori}}\in[0,1]^{n}
and a target label
y_{\text{tar}}\in\mathcal{Y}
. The goal of a targeted adversarial attack is to construct an adversarial example
x_{\text{adv}}
such that
C(x_{\text{adv}})=y_{\text{tar}}
while keeping
x_{\text{adv}}
close to
x_{\text{ori}}
. The corresponding optimization problem is

\min\mathcal{D}(x_{\text{ori}},x_{\text{adv}})\quad\text{subject to}\quad C(x_{\text{adv}})=y_{\text{tar}}\quad\text{and}\quad x_{\text{adv}}\in[0,1]^{n},

where
\mathcal{D}
measures the distance (similarity) between
x_{\text{ori}}
and
x_{\text{adv}}
, typically via an
\mathcal{L}_{1}
,
\mathcal{L}_{2}
, or
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
norm. Directly solving this constrained optimization can be challenging. To address this,
Szegedy et al. 2014
propose a relaxation:

\min\;\mathcal{D}(x_{\text{ori}},x_{\text{adv}})\;+\;c\,f(x_{\text{adv}},y_{\text{tar}})\quad\text{subject to}\quad x_{\text{adv}}\in[0,1]^{n},

(1)

where
c
is a constant, and
f
is an objective function that guides the classifier’s predictions toward the target label. In
[
40
]
,
f
is taken to be the cross-entropy loss;
Carlini and Wagner 2017
present additional choices for
f
.

2.3
Probabilistic Adversarial Attack

By employing Langevin Dynamics as an optimizer for (
1
),
Zhang et al. 2024b
derive a probabilistic perspective on adversarial attacks. They introduce the adversarial distribution:

p_{\text{adv}}(x_{\text{adv}}\mid x_{\text{ori}},y_{\text{tar}})\propto p_{\text{vic}}(x_{\text{adv}}\mid y_{\text{tar}})\,p_{\text{dis}}(x_{\text{adv}}\mid x_{\text{ori}}),

(2)

where
p_{\text{vic}}(x_{\text{adv}}\mid y_{\text{tar}})\propto\exp\bigl(-c\,f(x_{\text{adv}},y_{\text{tar}})\bigr)
is the “victim” distribution emphasizing misclassification toward
y_{\text{tar}}
, and
p_{\text{dis}}(x_{\text{adv}}\mid x_{\text{ori}})\propto\exp\bigl(-\mathcal{D}(x_{\text{ori}},x_{\text{adv}})\bigr)
is the “distance” distribution around
x_{\text{ori}}
. This formulation leverages the fact that Langevin Dynamics converges to the corresponding Gibbs distribution
[
23
]
, thereby providing a probabilistic interpretation of adversarial attack generation.

This probabilistic perspective aligns with traditional geometry-based adversarial attacks. For example, if
\mathcal{D}
is the
\mathcal{L}_{1}
norm, then
p_{\text{dis}}(x_{\text{adv}}\mid x_{\text{ori}})\propto\exp(-\lVert x_{\text{adv}}-x_{\text{ori}}\rVert_{1})
takes the form of a Laplace distribution. Similarly, if
\mathcal{D}
is the squared
\mathcal{L}_{2}
norm, then
p_{\text{dis}}(x_{\text{adv}}\mid x_{\text{ori}})\propto\exp(-\lVert x_{\text{adv}}-x_{\text{ori}}\rVert_{2}^{2})
is a Gaussian distribution.

Zhang et al. 2024b
indicate that the distance distribution
p_{\text{dis}}
can be any distribution centered around
x_{\text{ori}}
, meaning the choice of
p_{\text{dis}}
implicitly defines the distance
\mathcal{D}
. Consequently, using a PGM centered on
x_{\text{ori}}
as
p_{\text{dis}}
yields a semantic-aware notion of distance. By then sampling from the corresponding adversarial distribution
p_{\text{adv}}
, one can generate semantic-aware adversarial examples.

3
Concept-based Adversarial Attack

Building on the probabilistic perspective of adversarial attacks, we can generalize the notion of an original image
x_{\text{ori}}
to an original concept
\mathcal{C}_{\text{ori}}
. For example,
\mathcal{C}_{\text{ori}}
could represent a specific object or identity, such as the small Corgi (dog) with slightly elongated ears in Figure
1
. Rather than applying imperceptible perturbations to
x_{\text{ori}}
, concept-based adversarial attacks aim to generate an adversarial example
x_{\text{adv}}
that preserves the conceptual identity of
\mathcal{C}_{\text{ori}}
(i.e., the same Corgi) while allowing variation in pose, background, and viewpoint.

Technically, defining an explicit distance
\mathcal{D}(x_{\text{adv}},\mathcal{C}_{\text{ori}})
is challenging. However, the probabilistic perspective offers a natural alternative: we define a distance distribution
p_{\text{dis}}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{C}_{\text{ori}})
that implicitly imposes a distance measure. Formally,

p_{\text{adv}}(x_{\text{adv}}\mid\mathcal{C}_{\text{ori}},y_{\text{tar}})\propto p_{\text{vic}}(x_{\text{adv}}\mid y_{\text{tar}})\,p_{\text{dis}}(x_{\text{adv}}\mid\mathcal{C}_{\text{ori}})

(3)

where
p_{\text{adv}}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{C}_{\text{ori}},y_{\text{tar}})
is the adversarial distribution relative to the concept
\mathcal{C}_{\text{ori}}
and the target label
y_{\text{tar}}
. The distance distribution
p_{\text{dis}}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{C}_{\text{ori}})
is a distribution centered around the concept
\mathcal{C}_{\text{ori}}
. From a practical standpoint, one feasible approach is to construct a PGM for
\mathcal{C}_{\text{ori}}
. In practice, this is often done by fine-tuning an existing PGM on a set of images that represent the concept. Consequently, throughout the remainder of this work, we will represent the concept
\mathcal{C}_{\text{ori}}
using a set of images
\mathcal{C}_{\text{ori}}=\{x_{\text{ori}}^{(1)},\dots,x_{\text{ori}}^{(K)}\}
, where
K
is the number of images depicting
\mathcal{C}_{\text{ori}}
. The probabilistic adversarial attack, that manipulates only one image, can then be expressed as a special case where
\mathcal{C}_{\text{ori}}=\{x_{\text{ori}}\}
or
|\mathcal{C}_{\text{ori}}|=1
.

3.1
Concept-based Adversarial Attacks Generate Higher Quality Adversarial Examples

From the probabilistic perspective, generating adversarial examples amounts to sampling from the overlap between
p_{\text{vic}}
and
p_{\text{dis}}
, since
p_{\text{adv}}
is proportional to their product
[
13
]
. For the common case of attacking a single original image
x_{\text{ori}}
, this procedure is illustrated on the left side of Figure
1
. Empirical research has shown that modern robust classifiers can produce high-quality images of the target classes
[
33
,
49
,
52
]
, causing
p_{\text{vic}}
to concentrate on the semantics of those classes. Consequently, as
x_{\text{ori}}
does not depict the target class, the intersection between
p_{\text{vic}}
and
p_{\text{dis}}
is small. Since high-quality images rarely appear in low-density regions of the distribution, the resulting adversarial examples drawn from this limited intersection tend to be of lower quality.

We claim that our concept-based adversarial attacks reduce the distance between the
p_{\text{vic}}
and the
p_{\text{dis}}
, thereby increasing their overlap. This broader overlap yields higher-quality adversarial examples and improves targeted attack success rates, as illustrated on the right side of Figure
1
.

To justify this claim, we must address two key questions:

•

Do concept-based adversarial attacks indeed decrease the distance between
p_{\text{vic}}
and
p_{\text{dis}}
?

•

Do they genuinely produce better adversarial examples?

The remainder of this paper focuses on answering these questions.

3.2
The Distance Between Distributions: A Theoretical Study

In the whitebox adversarial attack scenario, both the victim classifier and target label are provided, which means
p_{\text{vic}}
remains fixed. We assume that the distance distributions
p_{\text{dis}}(\cdot\mid x_{\text{ori}})
and
p_{\text{dis}}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{C}_{\text{ori}})
follow Gaussian distributions with identical means
\mu
, but with
p_{\text{dis}}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{C}_{\text{ori}})
exhibiting greater variance than
p_{\text{dis}}(\cdot\mid x_{\text{ori}})
. This assumption is reasonable since
x_{\text{ori}}
represents an instance of concept
\mathcal{C}_{\text{ori}}
, placing their means in proximity to each other. Additionally, the distance distribution surrounding the concept
\mathcal{C}_{\text{ori}}
should naturally demonstrate higher variance compared to that of the specific instance
x_{\text{ori}}
. The following theorem enables us to demonstrate that concept-based adversarial attacks decrease the KL divergence between
p_{\text{vic}}
and
p_{\text{dis}}
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
p
be a probability distribution and
q
be a Gaussian distribution with mean
\mu
and variance
\sigma^{2}
. Then
KL(p||q)
is a decreasing function of
\sigma^{2}
when
\sigma^{2}<\mathbb{E}_{X\sim p}[(X-\mu)^{2}]
.

The proof for Theorem
1
is provided in Appendix
A
. By Theorem
1
, it follows that
KL(p_{\text{vic}}\,\|\,p_{\text{dis}})
decreases when
\sigma^{2}<\mathbb{E}_{X\sim p_{\text{vic}}}[(X-\mu)^{2}]
, where
\mu
and
\sigma^{2}
are the mean and variance of
p_{\text{dis}}
. In the probabilistic adversarial attack framework, this condition is always satisfied because it is equivalent to

\mathbb{E}_{X\sim p_{\text{dis}}}\bigl[(X-\mu)^{2}\bigr]<\mathbb{E}_{X\sim p_{\text{vic}}}\bigl[(X-\mu)^{2}\bigr].

Intuitively, samples drawn from
p_{\text{vic}}
lie farther from the mean of
p_{\text{dis}}
than samples from
p_{\text{dis}}
itself. If that were not the case, our core assumption that
p_{\text{dis}}
represents a distance distribution capturing points close to
x_{\text{ori}}
or
\mathcal{C}_{\text{ori}}
would be violated.

3.3
The Distance Between Distributions: An Empirical Study

The following theorem provides a tractable expression for the difference in KL divergence between a fixed victim distribution and two different distance distributions.

Theorem 2
.

Let
p_{\text{dis}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}=p_{\text{dis}}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{C}_{\text{ori}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)})
and
p_{\text{dis}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}=p_{\text{dis}}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{C}_{\text{ori}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)})
be two distance distributions, and let
p_{\text{vic}}(\cdot\mid y_{\text{tar}})
be the victim distribution corresponding to a victim classifier
p(y_{\text{tar}}\mid x)
. Then, the difference

\Delta:=KL\bigl(p_{\text{dis}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}\,\|\,p_{\text{vic}}\bigr)\;-\;KL\bigl(p_{\text{dis}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\,\|\,p_{\text{vic}}\bigr)

is given by

\Delta=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim p_{\text{dis}}^{(1)}}\bigl[\log p_{\text{dis}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}(X)-c\,\log p(y_{\text{tar}}\mid X)\bigr]-\mathbb{E}_{X\sim p_{\text{dis}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}\bigl[\log p_{\text{dis}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}(X)-c\,\log p(y_{\text{tar}}\mid X)\bigr].

The proof is provided in Appendix
A
. We estimate
\Delta
via Monte Carlo integration and further reduce variance by using common random numbers in the sampling process; details of such practical techniques are introduced in Appendix
B
. In section
5.3
, we empirically show that, concept-based adversarial attacks reduce the distance between distributions
p_{\text{vic}}
and
p_{\text{dis}}
by showing
\Delta<0
when
p_{\text{dis}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}
is a distance distribution around only one image and
p_{\text{dis}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}
is a distance distribution around a concept.

4.1
Augment Concept Datasets by Modern Generative Models

[

4.2
Sample Selection

A key advantage of probabilistic adversarial attacks is that we can draw multiple samples from
p_{\text{adv}}
and select the best ones. In a white-box scenario, we can simply discard samples that fail to deceive the classifier (rejection sampling). However, if
p_{\text{dis}}
and
p_{\text{vic}}
overlap only slightly, this may lead to high rejection rates (especially under a top-1 success criterion).

As a workaround, we first sample
M
adversarial examples from
p_{\text{adv}}
and select the best among them. For small batches, it is feasible to manually choose which examples preserve the original concept. However, because we need a large number of adversarial examples, we use an automated approach: we sort the samples by how highly they rank the target class and, in the event of a tie, we employ one of two strategies — referred to here as the “conservative strategy” (CONS) and the “aggressive strategy” (AGGR). Under the conservative strategy, we pick the example with the lowest softmax probability, thereby filtering out samples that deviate significantly from the original concept. Under the aggressive strategy, we pick the example with the highest softmax probability, helping us select samples with the greatest adversarial potential.

5
Experiments

5.1
Data Preparation

We choose the DreamBooth
[
32
]
dataset, which contains 30 different objects to represent the concepts. Each object is initially described by 5 to 6 images; these objects may be animals, dolls, or everyday items such as vases or candles. Because each object has distinctive individual characteristics, the DreamBooth dataset is well-suited for representing concepts. We then apply the augmentation method described in Section
4.1
to generate additional images. For each concept, we produce 30 augmented images, excluding the 5–6 original images to ensure more diverse scenes. We refer to this new, fully augmented dataset as DreamBoothPlus. Among the 30 objects of DreamBooth, we only augment 26, excluding four that pose challenges for text generation or require different fine-tuning parameters for cartoon-style content.

5.2
Fitting the Distance Distributions

We use the DreamBoothPlus dataset to finetune the diffusion model
[
10
,
28

to fit the distance distribution
p_{\text{dis}}
(Details in Appendix

[

is the image and
y

5.3
Calculating the Difference between KL Divergences

We empirically estimate the difference between two KL divergences,

\Delta:=KL(p_{\text{dis}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(1)}}\,\|\,p_{\text{vic}})\;-\;KL(p_{\text{dis}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(2)}}\,\|\,p_{\text{vic}}),

by using the Monte-Carlo method and the practical techniques introduced in Section
3.3
and Appendix
B

\tilde{\Delta}
. Concretely, for each concept in DreamBoothPlus, we fine-tune a diffusion model on the entire concept to obtain
p_{\text{dis}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(1)}}

p_{\text{dis}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(2)}}
. Next, we calculate the empirical difference
\tilde{\Delta}
and find that
\tilde{\Delta}<0
for every concept. This strongly suggests that
\Delta<0
, confirming our hypothesis from Section
3.3
. The table in Appendix
B

6
Related Works

To the best of our knowledge, no prior adversarial attack research has defined a distance between an adversarial example and a concept described by a set of images. The most closely related work is
[
38
]
. However,
Song et al. 2018
treats a “class” (e.g., cat, dog, or truck) as the concept, which cannot precisely capture something like an individual identity. In our approach, we represent a concept using a set of images or a probabilistic model, allowing it to be either an identity or a class. By contrast, other unrestricted adversarial attack methods focus solely on generating adversarial examples from a single image. Even advanced methods such as ACA
[
7
]
and DiffAttack
[
6
]
simply apply the attack gradient to the latent space induced by the DDIM
[
36
]
from a single image. Color-based transformations have proven effective in preserving semantic content for untargeted attacks
[
2
,
15
,
34
,
47
,
51
]
, yet they perform poorly in targeted scenarios
[
6
]
, a result confirmed by our experiments.

Our approach is directly inherited from
[
49
]
. The probabilistic perspective of adversarial attacks proposed by
[
49
]
serves as a bridge between our concept-based adversarial attack and traditional adversarial attacks. Conceptually, however, our main difference from Zhang et al. is that they aim to leverage prior knowledge from PGM to make semantically meaningful modifications to individual images — an approach essentially similar to ACA and DiffAttack — whereas our work highlights the importance of constructing a concept-based distribution. From this probabilistic viewpoint, we emphasize that adversarial examples arise at the intersection of
p_{\text{dis}}
and
p_{\text{vic}}
, and we strive to expand this intersection at the semantic level.

7
Limitations

Our approach is slower than similar methods because it requires time-consuming fine-tuning on a concept dataset
\mathcal{C}_{\text{ori}}
, which may also need to be built or expanded if unavailable. However, in adversarial attack scenarios, even one successful example can cause severe damage, highlighting the practical importance of our method.

8
Practical Usage Guidelines

Although the term “concept” is difficult to define precisely, our method provides a clear definition: a concept can be specified by either a set
\mathcal{C}_{\text{ori}}
or a probabilistic model. Users can therefore construct
\mathcal{C}_{\text{ori}}
to include whatever concept variations they desire. In our experiments, we demonstrate a broad range of concept variations — background, pose, and viewpoint — leading to a highly diverse
\mathcal{C}_{\text{ori}}
and scenarios like the one shown in Figure
1
(right).

In practice, constraints may prevent such extensive concept variations. For example, one might need to fix the background and viewpoint, leaving only the object’s pose or special variations (e.g., dressing a dog in different outfits). However, if the concept variations are too limited, the intersection between
p_{\text{dis}}
and
p_{\text{vic}}
may be insufficient, making it harder to generate adversarial samples. Practitioners should be mindful of this trade-off.

9
Conclusions

The essence of adversarial attacks is to create examples that are imperceptible to humans yet harmful to computational systems. Our work demonstrates that in an era of powerful generative models, creating an adversarial example from scratch — one that humans perceive as conceptually correct — can be more flexible, more realistic, and ultimately more potent than simply perturbing a single image. Leveraging modern generative models, adversarial noise can be concealed in subtle changes to viewpoint, pose, or background, making it exceedingly difficult to detect. We believe that our concept-based adversarial attack heralds the future of adversarial attacks, posing new challenges to the field of AI security. Defending against such threats will be crucial for advancing AI security research.

Appendix A
Proof of the Theorems

Theorem 1
.

Let
p
be a probability distribution and
q
be a Gaussian distribution with mean
\mu
and variance
\sigma^{2}
. Then
KL(p||q)
is a decreasing function of
\sigma^{2}
when
\sigma^{2}<\mathbb{E}_{X\sim p}[(X-\mu)^{2}]
.

Proof.

According to the definition of KL divergence, we have

KL(p||q)=\int p(x)\log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}dx=\int p(x)\log p(x)dx-\int p(x)\log q(x)dx

Since
\int p(x)\log p(x)dx
is independent of
\sigma^{2}
, we can treat it as a constant. Let us denote the
\sigma^{2}
-dependent component as
f(\sigma^{2})
, which gives us

\displaystyle f(\sigma^{2})

\displaystyle=-\int p(x)\log q(x)dx

\displaystyle=-\int p(x)\left[-\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^{2})-\frac{(x-\mu)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right]dx

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim p}\left[\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^{2})+\frac{(X-\mu)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^{2})+\frac{\mathbb{E}_{X\sim p}\left[(X-\mu)^{2}\right]}{2\sigma^{2}}

Taking the derivative with respect to
\sigma^{2}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d(\sigma^{2})}f(\sigma^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}-\frac{\mathbb{E}_{X\sim p}\left[(X-\mu)^{2}\right]}{2(\sigma^{2})^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2(\sigma^{2})^{2}}\left(\sigma^{2}-\mathbb{E}_{X\sim p}\left[(X-\mu)^{2}\right]\right)

Therefore, when
\sigma^{2}<\mathbb{E}_{X\sim p}\left[(X-\mu)^{2}\right]
, the derivative becomes negative. This implies that both
f(\sigma^{2})
and consequently
KL(p||q)
decrease as
\sigma^{2}
increases.
∎

Theorem 2
.

Let
p_{\text{dis}}^{(1)}=p_{\text{dis}}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{C}_{\text{ori}}^{(1)})
and
p_{\text{dis}}^{(2)}=p_{\text{dis}}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{C}_{\text{ori}}^{(2)})
be two distance distributions, and let
p_{\text{vic}}(\cdot\mid y_{\text{tar}})
be the victim distribution corresponding to a victim classifier
p(y_{\text{tar}}\mid x)
. Then, the difference

\Delta=KL\bigl(p_{\text{dis}}^{(1)}\,\|\,p_{\text{vic}}\bigr)\;-\;KL\bigl(p_{\text{dis}}^{(2)}\,\|\,p_{\text{vic}}\bigr)

is given by

\Delta=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim p_{\text{dis}}^{(1)}}\bigl[\log p_{\text{dis}}^{(1)}(X)-c\,\log p(y_{\text{tar}}\mid X)\bigr]-\mathbb{E}_{X\sim p_{\text{dis}}^{(2)}}\bigl[\log p_{\text{dis}}^{(2)}(X)-c\,\log p(y_{\text{tar}}\mid X)\bigr].

Proof.

Recall that for distributions
p
and
q
on the same space, the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence is

KL(p\,\|\,q)\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{X\sim p}\bigl[\log p(X)\;-\;\log q(X)\bigr].

Hence, for
p_{\text{dis}}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{C}_{\text{ori}})
and
p_{\text{vic}}(\cdot\mid y_{\text{tar}})
,

KL\bigl(p_{\text{dis}}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{C}_{\text{ori}})\,\|\,p_{\text{vic}}(\cdot\mid y_{\text{tar}})\bigr)\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{X\sim p_{\text{dis}}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{C}_{\text{ori}})}\Bigl[\log p_{\text{dis}}(X\mid\mathcal{C}_{\text{ori}})\;-\;\log p_{\text{vic}}(X\mid y_{\text{tar}})\Bigr].

Given that the victim distribution is proportional to

p_{\text{vic}}(x\mid y_{\text{tar}})\;\propto\;\exp\!\bigl(-c\,f(x,y_{\text{tar}})\bigr),

where
f(x,y_{\text{tar}})
is the cross-entropy loss, i.e.
f(x,y_{\text{tar}})=-\log p(y_{\text{tar}}\mid x)
. Thus we can write

p_{\text{vic}}(x\mid y_{\text{tar}})\;=\;\frac{\exp(-c\,f(x,y_{\text{tar}}))}{\int\exp(-c\,f(x,y_{\text{tar}}))\,dx}.

Let
Z:=\int\exp\!\bigl(-c\,f(x,y_{\text{tar}})\bigr)\,dx
. Then

\log p_{\text{vic}}(X\mid y_{\text{tar}})\;=\;\log\exp\bigl(-c\,f(X,y_{\text{tar}})\bigr)\;-\;\log Z\;=\;-c\,f(X,y_{\text{tar}})\;-\;\log Z.

Therefore,

KL\bigl(p_{\text{dis}}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{C}_{\text{ori}})\,\|\,p_{\text{vic}}(\cdot\mid y_{\text{tar}})\bigr)\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{X\sim p_{\text{dis}}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{C}_{\text{ori}})}\bigl[\log p_{\text{dis}}(X\mid\mathcal{C}_{\text{ori}})\;+\;c\,f(X,y_{\text{tar}})\bigr]\;+\;\log Z.

Since
f(x,y_{\text{tar}})=-\log p(y_{\text{tar}}\mid x)
, we get

c\,\mathbb{E}_{X\sim p_{\text{dis}}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{C}_{\text{ori}})}[f(X,y_{\text{tar}})]\;=\;-c\,\mathbb{E}_{X\sim p_{\text{dis}}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{C}_{\text{ori}})}[\log p(y_{\text{tar}}\mid X)].

Hence

KL\bigl(p_{\text{dis}}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{C}_{\text{ori}})\,\|\,p_{\text{vic}}(\cdot\mid y_{\text{tar}})\bigr)\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{X\sim p_{\text{dis}}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{C}_{\text{ori}})}\bigl[\log p_{\text{dis}}(X\mid\mathcal{C}_{\text{ori}})\;-\;c\,\log p(y_{\text{tar}}\mid X)\bigr]\;+\;\log Z.

Now take two such distributions,
p_{\text{dis}}^{(1)}
and
p_{\text{dis}}^{(2)}
. Because
\log Z
does not depend on which
p_{\text{dis}}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{C}_{\text{ori}})
we use, it cancels when we form the difference:

\displaystyle\Delta=

\displaystyle KL\bigl(p_{\text{dis}}^{(1)}\,\|\,p_{\text{vic}}\bigr)\;-\;KL\bigl(p_{\text{dis}}^{(2)}\,\|\,p_{\text{vic}}\bigr)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Bigl(\mathbb{E}_{X\sim p_{\text{dis}}^{(1)}}\bigl[\log p_{\text{dis}}^{(1)}(X)-c\log p(y_{\text{tar}}\mid X)\bigr]+\log Z\Bigr)

\displaystyle-\Bigl(\mathbb{E}_{X\sim p_{\text{dis}}^{(2)}}\bigl[\log p_{\text{dis}}^{(2)}(X)-c\log p(y_{\text{tar}}\mid X)\bigr]+\log Z\Bigr)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X\sim p_{\text{dis}}^{(1)}}\bigl[\log p_{\text{dis}}^{(1)}(X)\;-\;c\,\log p(y_{\text{tar}}\mid X)\bigr]\;-\;\mathbb{E}_{X\sim p_{\text{dis}}^{(2)}}\bigl[\log p_{\text{dis}}^{(2)}(X)\;-\;c\,\log p(y_{\text{tar}}\mid X)\bigr],

which is precisely the claimed result.
∎

B.1
Common Random Numbers

In practice, our distance distributions
p_{\text{dis}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}
and
p_{\text{dis}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}
are instantiated by diffusion models: each image
X
is generated from noise
\epsilon
via a generator
\mathcal{G}
. We write
X=\mathcal{G}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}(\epsilon)
to indicate that
X
is sampled from
p_{\text{dis}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}
, and
X=\mathcal{G}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}(\epsilon)
to indicate that
X
is sampled from
p_{\text{dis}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}
. In this common-noise setup, the difference in KL divergences becomes

\Delta=\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon}\Bigl[\log p_{\text{dis}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}(\mathcal{G}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}(\epsilon))-c\log p(y_{\text{tar}}\mid\mathcal{G}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}(\epsilon))-\log p_{\text{dis}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}(\mathcal{G}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}(\epsilon))+c\log p(y_{\text{tar}}\mid\mathcal{G}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}(\epsilon))\Bigr].

B.2
Likelihood Correction

We posit a probabilistic model
p_{\text{share}}
that captures the non-semantic, shared features of the images. Specifically, we assume that for samples
X
drawn from
p_{\text{dis}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(1)}}
and
p_{\text{dis}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(2)}}
, the expected values
\mathbb{E}_{X\sim p_{\text{dis}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(1)}}}[\log p_{\text{share}}(X)]
and
\mathbb{E}_{X\sim p_{\text{dis}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(2)}}}[\log p_{\text{share}}(X)]
are equal. This assumption is reasonable because the two distributions, in principle, can generate images sharing the same non-semantic details, differing only in their semantic content.

Then, under our diffusion-model instantiation, we have

\mathbb{E}_{X\sim p_{\text{dis}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(1)}}}[\log p_{\text{share}}(X)]=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim p_{\text{dis}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(2)}}}[\log p_{\text{share}}(X)]\Rightarrow\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon}\left[\log p_{\text{share}}(\mathcal{G}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}(\epsilon))-\log p_{\text{share}}(\mathcal{G}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}(\epsilon))\right]=0

for
\Delta
, following (
4
), we have the equations:

\displaystyle\Delta=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon}\Bigl[\log p_{\text{dis}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}(\mathcal{G}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}(\epsilon))-c\log p(y_{\text{tar}}\mid\mathcal{G}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}(\epsilon))-\log p_{\text{dis}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}(\mathcal{G}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}(\epsilon))+c\log p(y_{\text{tar}}\mid\mathcal{G}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}(\epsilon))\Bigr]

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon}\left[\log p_{\text{share}}(\mathcal{G}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}(\epsilon))-\log p_{\text{share}}(\mathcal{G}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}(\epsilon))\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon}\Bigl[\log p_{\text{dis}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}(\mathcal{G}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}(\epsilon))-\log p_{\text{share}}(\mathcal{G}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}(\epsilon))-c\log p(y_{\text{tar}}\mid\mathcal{G}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}(\epsilon))

\displaystyle-\log p_{\text{dis}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}(\mathcal{G}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}(\epsilon))+\log p_{\text{share}}(\mathcal{G}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}(\epsilon))+c\log p(y_{\text{tar}}\mid\mathcal{G}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}(\epsilon))\Bigr]

In practice, however, the assumption
\mathbb{E}_{X\sim p_{\text{dis}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(1)}}}[p_{\text{share}}(X)]=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim p_{\text{dis}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(2)}}}[p_{\text{share}}(X)]
may not hold perfectly, due to differences in model capacity, finetuning steps, and other factors. For example, a model
p_{\text{dis}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(1)}}
finetuned on more images might actually lose some non-semantic details compared to
p_{\text{dis}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(2)}}
, which is finetuned on a single image. The extra
p_{\text{share}}
-based terms can thus be viewed as a correction that accounts for these mismatches in non-semantic content.

Following
[
48
]
, a good practical choice for
p_{\text{share}}
is often the pretrained model, because it has been trained on a large, diverse dataset and therefore captures broad, shared non-semantic features of images.

Appendix G
Additional Qualitative Analysis

Due to space constraints, the qualitative comparison figures in the main text are relatively small. Therefore, in this section, we present enlarged qualitative comparisons between DiffAttack and our approach. We focus on comparing DiffAttack in particular because, although it achieves a high target attack success rate, it produces lower-quality images, as shown in our user study and image quality tests. Here, we examine specific adversarial examples generated by DiffAttack to illustrate why its image quality is inferior.

•

Border Collie (first column of Figure
5
).
DiffAttack removes all the dog’s fur details and replaces its eyes with those of another canine, while the nose and tongue become stylized with a graffiti-like look, losing realistic details. In contrast, although our concept-based adversarial example changes the dog’s pose, it preserves the animal’s fur and facial details.

•

Shiny Sneakers (second column).
DiffAttack turns the sneakers into a shoe design with sharp edges, losing the smooth curves of the original model.

•

Chow Chow (third column).
DiffAttack alters all of the fur details and erases the dog’s forelegs, resulting in a shape that resembles a drumstick rather than a chow chow.

•

Colorful Sneakers (fourth column).
While DiffAttack retains the purple front section and the blue rear section, it removes the yellow line in the middle and the adjacent cyan trim. Without these details, the sneaker’s appearance changes to a different style altogether.

•

Cat (fifth column).
DiffAttack modifies the cat’s fur patterns and gives it a distorted facial expression.

These observations further show that DiffAttack’s adversarial examples degrade crucial details, resulting in a significant drop in image quality and diminished fidelity to the original concept.
To emphasize that this qualitative study is not cherry-picking, we provide the complete set of adversarial examples — both from other methods and ours — in the code repository.

Appendix J
Broader Impacts of this work

This study introduces concept-based adversarial attacks, providing valuable insights into the vulnerabilities of sophisticated classifiers.

On the positive side, our work exposes critical weaknesses in systems previously considered robust, highlighting the need for enhanced security measures in classifier design. By identifying these vulnerabilities, we contribute to the development of more resilient artificial intelligence systems.

However, we acknowledge potential negative implications. The concept-based attack methods described could be misappropriated by malicious actors, for example, for identification purposes. We emphasize the importance of developing countermeasures against such exploitation and encourage the research community to consider ethical implications when building upon this work.
