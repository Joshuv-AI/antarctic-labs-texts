Biomechanical consequences of minor medial joint line elevation after medial unicompartmental knee arthroplasty
Authors: Yao Xu, Zuling Yi, Yingkai Zhang, Naicheng Diao, Heyong Yin, Yike Dai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMedial joint line elevation (JLE) commonly occurs during unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (UKA), but its biomechanical effects remain unexplored. This study addresses this gap by evaluating the biomechanical consequences of global JLE across different range of flexion angles.MethodsA three-dimensional finite element model of the knee was constructed from CT and MRI data and validated against published experimental data. Medial fixed-bearing UKA was simulated with three joint line conditions (native, + 1 mm, and + 2 mm global elevation) at four flexion angles (0°, 30°, 60°, 90°), resulting in 12 models. All models were subjected to physiological axial loads simulating daily activities for comparative biomechanical analysis.ResultsThe biomechanical impact of JLE was profoundly dependent on flexion angle and was mediated by induced coronal plane alignment changes. At full extension (0°), JLE triggered a compensatory valgus shift, increasing lateral compartment stress. During mid‑flexion (30° and 60°), a varus alignment trend led to a paradoxical “biphasic effect”-lateral cartilage stress decreased, while medial polyethylene insert stress increased, peaking with a 12.4% rise at 60° under 2 mm elevation. A critical finding was a sharp “stress concentration peak” in the tibial cancellous bone beneath the prosthesis at 60° flexion with 2 mm JLE, where stress surged by 55.5%. At 90° flexion, the pattern shifted again, with JLE re‑inducing a valgus trend and elevating lateral stress toward physiological levels.ConclusionThis study reveals a novel, angle-dependent biomechanical cascade triggered by minor JLE, driven by dynamic alignment changes. Precise joint line restoration and avoidance of repetitive 60° flexion weight-bearing are suggested as potential considerations for surgical practice and postoperative rehabilitation, although this remains a hypothesis that requires clinical validation.

Introduction

Knee osteoarthritis (OA) is the most common degenerative joint disease, severely affecting the quality of life of the elderly population and has become one of the fastest-growing causes of disability worldwide[1, 2]. In cases of knee OA confined to a single compartment, involvement of the medial compartment is most prevalent. UKA has emerged as an ideal surgical option among joint surgeons in recent years, owing to its advantages of minimal invasiveness, superior postoperative function, and faster recovery times[3]. An analysis of a large cohort of knee OA patients based on articular cartilage wear and anterior cruciate ligament status revealed that approximately 42% of patients are suitable candidates for UKA surgery[4]. UKA effectively restores knee kinematics[5, 6], numerous studies also indicate that patients achieve satisfactory functional outcomes and perceptions following UKA[7–9]. However, with the increasing popularity of UKA among surgeons in recent years, a greater number of complications and failure cases have been reported[10–13]. The primary causes of failure include the progression of osteoarthritis in the unresurfaced compartments, polyethylene insert wear, and periprosthetic fractures[14–16]. Accuracy of implant positioning and restoring the proper limb alignment are required for the long-term survival of the implants[17, 18]. Recent biomechanical studies have further demonstrated that femoral component positioning[19] and lower limb alignment[20] significantly influence joint loading and stress distribution. A finite element study by Ma et al. reported that coronal tilt of the femoral component exceeding 6° significantly increased polyethylene liner stress [28]. Similarly, Wang et al. found that varus or valgus alignment of the femoral prosthesis affects lateral compartment loading[20]. Clinically, a study by Vossen et al. demonstrated that preservation of the preoperative coronal plane alignment of the knee phenotype yielded significantly better functional outcomes[21]. These findings underscore the importance of precise surgical technique, yet the biomechanical effects of joint line elevation remain incompletely understood.

Typically, the minimum thickness of the tibial component (including the metal tray and polyethylene insert) in traditional fixed-bearing UKA is 10 mm. If a 10-mm tibial resection is performed, excessive bone removal may lead to increased stress concentration and elevate the risk of postoperative periprosthetic fracture[22], and potentially damage the deep insertion of the medial collateral ligament. Therefore, to balance bone preservation and the integrity of the deep medial collateral ligament, surgeons often intentionally reduce the tibial resection depth and compensate by increasing the femoral resection, a maneuver that frequently results in a postoperative elevation of the medial joint line by 1–2 mm. Although a limited number of studies have preliminarily explored the impact of joint line position on contact pressure in UKA[23, 24], their methodologies present notable limitations. Critically, these studies simulated only the knee in full extension, overlooking the fact that daily activities—such as walking, sitting, and stair climbing—involve a dynamic range of flexion angles. Joint loading varies significantly with flexion angle, and the biomechanical consequences of joint line elevation may therefore be angle-dependent. Additionally, these studies modeled joint line alteration solely in the coronal plane at the distal femur, whereas actual surgery involves a composite change encompassing both the distal femur and the posterior femoral condyles. This complex, multi-planar biomechanical scenario is not adequately reflected by existing models. Evaluating multiple flexion angles is therefore essential to capture the full biomechanical impact of joint line elevation and to provide clinically relevant insights across the range of activities patients perform postoperatively.

To address the aforementioned issues, this study aims to achieve the following objectives: 1. To develop and validate a finite element model capable of simulating the dynamic biomechanics of the knee before and after medial fixed-bearing UKA across different flexion angles; 2. To systematically investigate the biomechanical impact of a global medial joint line elevation (involving both the distal femur and posterior condyles) following fixed-bearing medial UKA on the lateral compartment and the implant-bone composite. We hypothesize that global medial joint line elevation after medial fixed-bearing UKA induces flexion-angle-dependent biomechanical alterations in both the medial and lateral compartments.

Material and methods

Healthy knee model

Based on computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans from a 35-year-old healthy male volunteer, a three-dimensional finite element (FE) knee model was established. The subject had no history of musculoskeletal disorders, lower limb malalignment, or prior surgery. CT scanning was utilized for bone identification, while MRI was employed to delineate cartilage, menisci, and soft tissues. The CT and MR images were imported into image processing software (Mimics 13.1, Materialise, Leuven, Belgium) for geometric segmentation and generation of 3D models for all structures. The resulting triangulated surface data were exported in stereolithography (STL) format for post-processing using Geomagic Studio (version 12.0.0, Geomagic, North Carolina, USA). A numerical model of the native knee was subsequently developed, encompassing the bony structures, articular cartilage, menisci, as well as the patella and the major ligamentous complexes (collateral and cruciate ligaments). Articular cartilage thickness was derived from MRI segmentation, with the cartilage layer modeled as a uniform shell of approximately 1.5–2.5 mm on the femoral condyles and tibial plateau, consistent with anatomical measurements from the volunteer’s imaging data. The major ligamentous structures, including the anterior cruciate ligament (ACL), posterior cruciate ligament (PCL), medial collateral ligament (MCL), and lateral collateral ligament (LCL), were modeled as nonlinear spring elements with material properties derived from published experimental data[25, 26]. The insertion points of the ligaments were defined based on anatomical landmarks identified from the MRI data, and the ligaments were considered to be in a pre-tensioned state at full extension, with initial strains based on published values[27].

Based on established literature[25, 28], the material properties for the native knee model were assigned as follows: cortical bone was modeled as transversely isotropic, while cancellous bone, articular cartilage, and the menisci were modeled as linearly isotropic. A friction coefficient of 0.01 was applied to the interactions between the femoral cartilage and the patellar cartilage, menisci, and tibial cartilage.

UKA knee model

Following the development of the native knee model, a fixed-bearing, metal-backed UKA prosthesis was virtually implanted into the medial compartment. A size 4 component was selected for both the femur and tibia based on anatomical dimensions, paired with an 8 mm polyethylene insert.

Three distinct positions for UKA prosthesis placement were designed and investigated at four different flexion angles, resulting in a total of 12 models. All placements were subsequently validated by two experienced joint surgeons:

1. Native joint line: The femoral component was positioned by referencing the planes of the native distal femur and posterior condylar cartilage surfaces. This ensured that the postoperative joint line was consistent with the native joint line. Following surgical guides, a tibial component with a thickness of 10 mm was used to achieve a balanced configuration, representing a well-balanced knee. This thickness included the polyethylene insert (8 mm) and the tibial tray (2 mm). 3. Medial joint line elevated by 2 mm: The medial joint line of the model was global elevated by 2 mm. This involved moving the distal portion of the femoral component proximally by 2 mm and simultaneously translating the posterior condyle of the femoral component anteriorly by 2 mm. The model was then set to 0°, 30°, 60°, and 90° of flexion following prosthesis displacement (Fig. 1).

The traditional fixed-bearing UKA prosthesis has a minimum total thickness of 10 mm. To preserve bone stock and protect the deep medial collateral ligament, the prosthesis design allows for reduced tibial resection compensated by increased femoral resection, which frequently results in 1–2 mm of joint line elevation. Moreover, joint line elevation exceeding 2 mm has been associated with decreased prosthesis survival and worse functional outcomes[29]. Therefore, the selected elevation values of 1 mm and 2 mm are clinically relevant and represent the typical magnitude encountered in routine surgical practice.

The femoral component, tibial tray, and tibial insert were fabricated from cobalt-chromium alloy (CoCr), and ultra-high-molecular-weight-polyethylene (UHMWPE), respectively. Consistent with the native knee model, all implant materials were modeled as homogeneous, isotropic, and linear elastic[30, 31]. The material properties, in terms of Young’s modulus and Poisson’s ratio, were as follows: CoCr: E = 220,000 MPa, ν = 0.3[30]; UHMWPE: E = 685 MPa, ν = 0.4[32]. A coefficient of friction of 0.2 was considered for the interaction between the femoral component and the tibial insert[33].

Measurement of local coronal alignment angle

To quantify the alignment changes induced by joint line elevation, the local coronal alignment angle was measured at each flexion angle for all joint line elevation conditions. This angle was defined as the angle between the mechanical axis of the femoral component (perpendicular to its distal resection plane) and the tibial plateau (perpendicular to the tibial resection plane) in the coronal plane.

Sensitivity analysis

First, to assess the impact of material property simplifications, a sensitivity analysis was performed by varying the elastic modulus of cancellous bone (± 20%) and articular cartilage (± 15%) within reported physiological ranges[25, 28]. For each variation, peak stresses on the medial polyethylene insert and lateral tibial cartilage were recalculated under 2 mm joint line elevation at 0° and 60° flexion. The analysis confirmed that while absolute stress values varied, the relative differences between joint line elevation groups remained consistent (variations < 5%), supporting the robustness of the observed biomechanical trends.

Second, a load sensitivity analysis was performed to evaluate whether variations in loading magnitude affect the observed biomechanical trends. The applied axial loads were varied by ± 10% (from 225 to 275% BW) relative to the baseline loads, reflecting the reported in vivo variation in knee joint forces during daily activities[34]. For each load level, peak stresses on the medial polyethylene insert and lateral tibial cartilage were recalculated under 2 mm joint line elevation at 60° flexion. The results demonstrated that while absolute stress values varied proportionally with load magnitude (by approximately ± 10%), the relative differences between joint line elevation groups remained highly consistent. The percentage increase in medial polyethylene insert stress at 60° flexion under 2 mm elevation compared to the restored group was 12.4% at baseline load, 12.1% at − 10% load, and 12.7% at + 10% load (variations < 5%). Similarly, the paradoxical unloading effect in the lateral compartment at 60° flexion (18.2% decrease in lateral cartilage stress under 2 mm elevation) remained consistent across load levels (17.9% to 18.5% decrease).

Third, a friction coefficient sensitivity analysis was performed by varying the coefficient of friction from 0.1 to 0.3, representing the range reported for CoCr-UHMWPE articulation[33, 35]. For each value, peak stresses on the medial polyethylene insert were recalculated under 2 mm joint line elevation at 0° and 60° flexion. The results showed that while absolute stress values varied by up to 12%, the relative differences between joint line elevation groups remained consistent, with the percentage increase at 60° flexion ranging from 11.8% to 12.9% compared to the baseline of 12.4%. All observed biomechanical trends were preserved across the friction coefficient range.

These findings confirm that the observed biomechanical trends are robust to variations in material properties, loading magnitude, and friction coefficient.

Boundary and loading conditions

Based on the in vivo biomechanical data reported by Kutzner et al.[34], physiologically relevant axial loads were applied to simulate weight-bearing conditions. The distal ends of the tibia and fibula were fully constrained in all degrees of freedom. A concentrated force was applied vertically downward to the proximal end of the femur. To more accurately reflect the variation in joint loading across different flexion angles, the magnitude of this force was differentiated. For the lower flexion angles of 0° and 30°, a load corresponding to 260% of body weight (BW) was applied, simulating the load during activities like level walking. For the higher flexion angles of 60° and 90°, a load of 250% BW, representative of high-flexion activities such as knee bending, was used. Given a reference body weight of 70 kg, the calculated forces were 1780 N (for 0° and 30°) and 1715 N (for 60° and 90°). This loading protocol was consistently applied to all four models (Intact, UKA-JL0, UKA-JL + 1, UKA-JL + 2) at their respective flexion angles to ensure a comparative analysis of the biomechanical effects induced by joint line elevation.

Knee joint model validation

Validation was performed under two distinct quasi-static loading scenarios to quantitatively assess the accuracy of the finite element model.

First, anterior and posterior tibial translations were evaluated by applying 150 N anterior and posterior loads to the tibia at 30° of knee flexion, simulating clinical drawer tests[27, 36]. Under anterior loading, the model predicted an anterior tibial translation of 4.8 mm, which is within the range of 4.5–5.2 mm reported in experimental studies[36]. Under posterior loading, the model predicted a posterior tibial translation of 3.9 mm, consistent with the 3.5–4.2 mm range reported in the literature[36]. These values demonstrate good agreement with published experimental data.

Second, to validate the contact mechanics of the joint, a 1150 N axial load was applied to the intact model at full extension. The predicted peak contact pressure on the medial tibial cartilage was 5.21 MPa, compared to 5.35 MPa reported in the validated finite element study by Peña et al.[37], representing a difference of 2.6%. The predicted lateral tibial cartilage contact pressure was 4.58 MPa, compared to 4.70 MPa in the reference study[37], a difference of 2.5%. The overall contact pressure distribution patterns were also qualitatively consistent with the reference model.

These quantitative comparisons demonstrate that the model accurately reproduces both kinematic and contact mechanical behavior, confirming its reliability for subsequent comparative analyses.

Finite element analysis

All models were discretized using four-node tetrahedral elements. Tetrahedral elements with an approximate element size of 2 mm were used for the mesh of all the developed models. The final models consisted of approximately 450,000–520,000 elements and 85,000–95,000 nodes, depending on the specific configuration. Load distribution on the femoral and tibial cartilage, subchondral bone, menisci, and polyethylene insert of the prosthesis in both medial and lateral compartments was extracted for all established models (4 intact knee models and 12 post-arthroplasty models) (Fig. 1).

A mesh convergence study was performed to ensure the numerical accuracy and stability of the finite element predictions. The model was discretized using tetrahedral elements with progressively refined global element sizes of 3.0 mm, 2.5 mm, 2.0 mm, 1.5 mm, and 1.2 mm. For each mesh density, the peak von Mises stress on the medial polyethylene insert and the lateral tibial cartilage was recorded under 2 mm joint line elevation at both 0° and 60° flexion. The convergence criterion was defined as a relative change in peak stress of less than 5% between successive mesh refinements. Based on this analysis, a global element size of 2.0 mm was selected for all subsequent simulations, as it provided convergence at both flexion angles with a balance between computational efficiency and solution accuracy.

In this finite element study, all simulations were performed under identical boundary conditions with only the joint line height as the variable. Therefore, the observed differences in stress distribution are directly attributable to the changes in joint line elevation rather than random variability. Consequently, biomechanical outcomes are reported as percentage changes and comparative trends between groups, which is the standard approach in deterministic finite element analysis. Statistical testing (e.g., ANOVA) is not applicable in this context because the model does not incorporate random error or biological variability. The robustness of the observed trends was further confirmed through sensitivity analyses (material properties, loading magnitude, friction coefficient, and mesh convergence), which demonstrated that the relative differences between joint line elevation groups remained consistent across parameter variations.

Results

Model validation and baseline biomechanical characteristics

Before conducting the analysis on joint line height intervention, the established finite element knee model was validated. Under simulated axial compression and anterior drawer loads, the joint displacements and stress distribution patterns calculated by the model were consistent with previously reported in vitro experimental and simulation results in the literature[37]. This indicates that the model possesses good biomechanical credibility and is suitable for subsequent comparative analysis.

Baseline analysis of the intact knee revealed its physiological load pattern. As the flexion angle increased from 0° to 90°, the mechanical environment of the knee became more stressed. The stress increase in the lateral compartment was particularly significant: the peak lateral meniscus stress rose from 6.64 MPa at 0° to 15.66 MPa at 90°, while the lateral tibial cartilage stress increased from 4.62 MPa to 5.42 MPa. The medial compartment showed a similar rising trend, with both medial meniscus and cartilage stresses peaking at 90° (15.70 MPa and 7.55 MPa, respectively), both initial and final data were showed in Table 2. This aligns with the classic biomechanical principle of reduced contact area and increased soft-tissue tension during deep knee flexion [34, 38].

Coupled phenomenon: joint line elevation and induced coronal plane malalignment

During the modeling of medial joint line elevation, a key surgical nuance was observed: To achieve a stable, impingement-free articulation—simulating the surgeon’s intraoperative effort to balance flexion–extension gaps—the elevated joint line models required compensatory adjustments in coronal plane alignment. This necessity arose from geometric interference between the femoral component and the tibial insert, the nature of which was flexion-angle-specific.

At full extension (0°) and deep flexion (90°), joint line elevation caused impingement at the distal femur and posterior condyle, respectively. To resolve this, the models adopted a compensatory valgus alignment (Fig. 2A, D). Quantification of local coronal alignment

The local coronal alignment of the tibiofemoral joint (angle between the femoral component axis and the tibial plateau) was quantified under each condition. At 0° flexion, joint line elevation of 1 mm and 2 mm induced a valgus shift of approximately 1.0° and 2.0°, respectively. At 30° and 60° flexion, varus shifts of approximately 0.8°–1.0° (1 mm) and 1.5°–2.0° (2 mm) were observed. At 90° flexion, valgus shifts of approximately 1.2° (1 mm) and 2.5° (2 mm) were noted. It should be noted that the reported alignment angles represent local tibiofemoral joint angle changes rather than full-limb HKA angles. However, these local changes directly reflect the geometric coupling between joint line elevation and coronal plane alignment induced by the implant design. Therefore, they are sufficient to support the proposed mechanism.

2. Mid-flexion (30°-60°): paradoxical unloading: The trend reversed during mid-flexion. Joint line elevation (especially 2 mm) reduced stress in the lateral compartment. At 30°, a 2-mm elevation decreased lateral meniscus stress by 4.2% (from 6.45 MPa to 6.18 MPa) and decreased lateral cartilage stress by 8.1% compared to the initial position group (from 2.61 MPa to 2.40 MPa). This unloading effect peaked at 60°: a 2-mm elevation substantially reduced lateral meniscus stress by 29.6% (from 10.05 MPa to 7.07 MPa) and lateral cartilage stress by 18.2% (from 3.44 MPa to 2.81 MPa) (Fig. 3 and 4).

3. Deep flexion (90°): load return and potential overload risk: At 90°, the unloading effect disappeared. The stress in the lateral compartment of the 2-mm elevation group not only exceeded that of the initial position group but also approached or even surpassed the stress levels of the intact knee at the same angle (lateral meniscus: 16.12 MPa vs. Intact 15.66 MPa) (Fig. 3 and 4). This suggests that during high-flexion activities such as deep squatting, joint line elevation may subject the preserved lateral compartment to physiological or even supraphysiological loads once again.

The influence of joint line elevation on the medial (replaced) compartment was equally complex, showing a clear dichotomy between different flexion angles, revealing distinct risk scenarios for implant wear and joint stability. 1. Polyethylene insert stress: risk concentrated in mid-flexion: The peak contact stress on the polyethylene insert exhibited a flexion-angle-dependent pattern. At 0° and 90°, insert stress decreased with increasing joint line elevation, whereas at 30° and 60°, it increased. The most critical finding was at 60° flexion, where 2 mm elevation resulted in a 12.4% increase in peak stress compared to the restored joint line group (detailed values in Figs. 3 and 4, and Table 2). It should be noted that finite element outputs are sensitive to modeling assumptions; therefore, these values should be interpreted as comparative trends rather than absolute predictors of clinical wear.

2. Tibial trabecular bone stress beneath the prosthesis: a specific "stress peak" at 60° flexion: The stress response in the tibial cancellous bone followed a similar pattern, with a particularly prominent finding at 60° flexion. Under 2 mm elevation, peak stress increased by 55.5% relative to the restored group (detailed values in Figs. 3 and 4, and Table 2), forming a distinct "stress peak." Consistent with the inherent limitations of finite element modeling, these findings should be interpreted cautiously as indicators of relative risk rather than precise clinical thresholds.

Discussion

This study utilized three-dimensional finite element analysis to evaluate the biomechanical effects of overall joint line elevation following medial fixed-bearing UKA. Two key findings emerged: first, the lateral compartment exhibited a flexion-angle-dependent "biphasic effect"; second, the medial compartment showed a critical "stress peak" at 60° flexion. These findings are discussed below in the context of previous studies and their potential clinical implications. The mechanism underlying all biomechanical changes observed in this study stems from the surgically induced "medial joint line elevation" and the subsequent "dynamic alignment coupling effect." This primarily arises from inherent constraints in implant design: the distal femoral resection plane and the posterior condylar resection plane for a fixed-bearing UKA femoral component are not perpendicular in the sagittal plane. Consequently, when adjusting the component position during surgery to achieve overall joint line elevation, a simple vertical translation is not performed; rather, it triggers a coupled displacement—proximal translation of the distal femur is often accompanied by relative sinking of its posterior condylar resection plane, and vice versa. This geometric constraint is precisely the core reason for the subsequently observed, highly flexion-angle-dependent mechanical responses. From a surgical technique perspective, joint line elevation commonly occurs in two situations: Firstly, a design-based compensation: The total thickness of the tibial component (metal tray and polyethylene insert) in a traditional fixed-bearing UKA is 10 mm. To avoid damaging the deep fibers of the medial collateral ligament and bone preservation, the amount of tibial resection often needs to be reduced, which then requires compensatory increased resection of the femur. Secondly, a technical error, when tibial resection is insufficient, to achieve balanced flexion–extension gaps, the resection of the femoral posterior condyle is often increased, both leading to actual joint line elevation. Based on this geometric and surgical foundation, this study used a finite element model to reveal the biomechanical effects of this composite displacement through alignment coupling at different flexion angles, assuming that the ligaments are well balanced: 1. At full extension (0°), when simulating proximal translation of the distal femur and posterior condyle, the actual displacement of the distal femur was insufficient due to their non-perpendicular relationship, leading to geometric interference between the femoral component and the tibial insert in full extension. To eliminate this interference and simulate the intraoperative process of obtaining balanced gaps, the model spontaneously induced a compensatory overall lower limb alignment valgus shift. This mechanism directly caused relative narrowing of the lateral compartment space and an increase in contact stress. This aligns with clinical intraoperative observations: when the medial compartment is overly filled, the knee assumes a valgus alignment, and the load on the lateral compartment increases accordingly (Fig. 2). 2. At flexion (30° and 60°), the composite proximal translation of the femoral posterior condyle and distal portion generates an upward resultant force at the tibiofemoral articular contact surface. The effect of this resultant force causes excessive proximal translation of the femoral component relative to the tibial plateau in flexion, thereby inducing a trend toward overall lower limb alignment varus. Simultaneously, the lateral compartment exhibited a significant reduction in stress (Fig. 2). 3. At 90° flexion, again constrained by the geometric relationship between the distal femur and posterior condyle, the femoral posterior condyle fails to achieve sufficient proximal translation at 90° flexion, causing interference between the femoral posterior condyle and the posterior edge of the tibial insert. This situation triggers a compensatory mechanism similar to that in full extension—a trend toward lower limb alignment valgus shift reappears. As a result, the stress level in the lateral compartment not only exceeded that of the ideal prosthesis position group but even surpassed the physiological load of the healthy knee at the same flexion angle (Fig. 2). This suggests that for patients with joint line elevation, high-flexion activities such as deep squatting or kneeling may subject the preserved lateral compartment to supraphysiological loads, potentially accelerating its degenerative process.

The "biphasic effect" in the lateral compartment carries clear clinical implications. Our findings diverge from the finite element study by Kwon et al., who suggested that significant biomechanical effects occur only when joint line changes exceed ± 6 mm[23]. This discrepancy likely stems from methodological differences: Kwon et al. modeled only vertical displacement of the distal femoral joint line, whereas our study incorporates the more surgically realistic composite displacement of both the distal femur and posterior condyle. By simulating this global elevation, we demonstrate that minor changes of 1–2 mm can trigger significant, angle-dependent chain reactions. Our findings are consistent with the retrospective study by Kuwashima et al., who reported that joint line elevation correlates with valgus alignment [39]. However, their assessment was based solely on full-extension X-rays, whereas our study extends this by revealing how the mechanical consequences vary across the full range of flexion activities. First, at full extension and 90° flexion, the lateral compartment indeed faces a higher load risk. It reinforces the importance of precise alignment. Numerous registry systems and long-term follow-up studies indicate that the primary cause of revision after UKA is the progression of arthritis in the contralateral compartment [16, 35, 40]. Our findings may provide a mechanical explanation that could serve as a reference for understanding this clinical observation. Therefore, to prevent the progression of OA in the lateral compartment, ensuring proper postoperative knee alignment is extremely important. Several studies have reported the influence of postoperative knee alignment on the long-term prognosis of UKA[41–43]. Therefore, reconstructing the physiological joint line and alignment through precise osteotomy and soft tissue balance intraoperatively is the cornerstone for protecting the lateral compartment and ensuring the long-term survival of the prosthesis. Secondly, during mid-flexion activities such as mild squatting, the lateral compartment may instead be in a relatively "unloaded" state. This implies that assessing the "compression" of the lateral compartment solely based on static postoperative full-extension X-rays may not fully predict its long-term degeneration risk. The patient’s specific activity patterns will become a key influencing factor.

Another clinically relevant finding is the "stress peak" in the medial compartment at 60° flexion, an angle corresponding to rising from a seated position. The stress concentration in the polyethylene bearing and underlying cancellous bone at this angle suggests a potential mechanism that may contribute to early polyethylene wear and aseptic loosening—two common failure modes in fixed-bearing UKA[15, 44]. The peak stress observed in the polyethylene insert at 60° flexion (41.70 MPa under 2 mm elevation) exceeds the reported yield strength of UHMWPE (approximately 22.3 MPa) and approaches its ultimate tensile stress (approximately 40.0 MPa)[45, 46]. While these values suggest a potential risk of accelerated wear, direct correlation with clinical wear rates requires consideration of additional factors such as cross-shear, loading frequency, and lubricant conditions[47, 48]. For cancellous bone, the stress values observed in this study (4.37 MPa under 2 mm elevation) are below the reported compressive yield strength of proximal tibial cancellous bone (approximately 5.8 ± 3.4 MPa)[49], but approach the upper range of this value under the 2 mm elevation condition. Repetitive cyclic loading at this level may still contribute to bone remodeling and potential loosening over time [50]. However, it is important to note that finite element simulations provide biomechanical insights into relative trends rather than absolute predictions of material failure. Quantifying the exact fatigue life of the polyethylene insert or the precise risk of bone resorption would require additional experimental testing and material-specific fatigue models, which are beyond the scope of this study.

Based on the above findings, we propose the following actionable clinical recommendations: First, precise restoration of joint line height should be emphasized by surgeons. It is recommended to use prostheses designed to preserve the joint line height, actively utilize reliable bony landmarks such as the adductor tubercle intraoperatively, or employ navigation/robotic technology to strive to control the error in joint line height within a strict range of ± 1 mm. Secondly, surgeons need to fully recognize during preoperative planning and intraoperative procedures that any adjustment to the femoral posterior condylar resection made to balance flexion–extension gaps is a key step in initiating the adverse coupling loop of "joint line elevation—alignment change", which may trigger unforeseen long-term biomechanical consequences. For cases with existing joint line elevation, individualized postoperative rehabilitation may be particularly important. Based on our biomechanical findings, it may be prudent to consider avoiding repetitive weight-bearing activities at approximately 60° flexion during the early rehabilitation phase, as this angle exhibited the highest stress concentrations in the prosthesis and underlying bone. However, this suggestion remains speculative and should be interpreted with caution, as it is derived from finite element simulations rather than clinical outcome data. Future clinical studies are needed to validate whether such activity modification translates into improved implant survival or functional outcomes.

The present study was constructed based on imaging data from a healthy middle-aged volunteer, which differs from the typical UKA patient population—elderly individuals with longstanding medial compartment osteoarthritis. Regarding bone quality, osteoporosis is prevalent in the elderly UKA population. Recent finite element study has demonstrated that osteoporosis significantly affects stress distribution following UKA. Liu et al. reported that under identical implant positioning, osteoporotic bone exhibited higher peak stresses on the polyethylene liner and cortical bone compared to normal bone [51]. This finding suggests that while the relative biomechanical trends observed in our study are likely preserved in osteoporotic patients, the absolute stress magnitudes may be higher. Regarding pre-existing varus deformity, clinical study has shown that patients with preoperative tibial varus often retain a mild residual varus alignment postoperatively, yet this does not compromise functional outcomes[52]. Importantly, the core mechanism identified in this study stems from the geometric constraints of the implant design rather than preoperative alignment, therefore, regardless of baseline alignment, the relative trends induced by joint line elevation remain consistent. In recent years, implant design modifications have been introduced to reduce tibial bone resection, such as all-polyethylene (AP) components and vitamin E-infused highly cross-linked polyethylene (VE-HXLPE) inserts. All-polyethylene (AP) tibial components eliminate the metal tray, allowing for less bone resection than metal-backed (MB) designs; however, systematic review reported that MB implants had significantly lower revision rates (OR, 0.49; p = 0.003) [53]. More recently, VE-HXLPE has emerged as a bearing material that enhances wear resistance and oxidative stability, enabling the use of thinner polyethylene inserts[54]. These design strategies offer potential approaches to mitigate joint line elevation.

This study also has several limitations. First, model validation was performed solely through quantitative comparisons with previously published finite element results, without direct experimental or cadaveric validation. While this approach is commonly accepted in comparative finite element studies, the lack of experimental validation means that the absolute numerical values may not fully replicate in vivo conditions. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted primarily as comparative biomechanical trends rather than absolute predictions. Second, the present study did not quantitatively analyze ligament strain or tension changes under different joint line elevation conditions. Joint line elevation can substantially alter ligament tension, which may affect knee stability and long-term clinical outcomes. However, the primary focus of this study was on stress distribution in the articular cartilage, polyethylene insert, and implant-bone interface. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that the absence of ligament strain analysis limits the comprehensive evaluation of joint line elevation effects on knee stability. Future studies should incorporate detailed ligament strain analysis to further elucidate the biomechanical consequences of joint line elevation. Third, the inherent simplifying assumptions of the finite element method differ from the nonlinear, anisotropic properties of biological tissues. Fourth, the finite element model was constructed based on anatomical data from a single healthy male volunteer. While this approach is standard in computational biomechanics for mechanistic hypothesis testing, it does not account for the anatomical variability, age-related bone density changes, or pre-existing degenerative changes present in the typical UKA patient population. Therefore, the absolute stress values reported here should be interpreted with caution, and future studies using patient-specific models (e.g., elderly patients with osteoarthritis) are warranted to validate and extend our findings. Fifth, the current simulated loading conditions focus on axial compression and do not include the multi-direction complex loads present during daily activities, such as rotational moments and shear forces that occur during gait and pivoting movements. Additionally, muscle forces (quadriceps and hamstrings) were not included in the model, as our focus was on comparative trends under axial loading. This simplification is common in comparative finite element studies and, according to prior work [55], does not alter the relative differences between simulated conditions. Consequently, the absolute numerical values obtained in this study may not fully reflect the complex loading environment of the knee, and the findings should be interpreted primarily as comparative trends under predominantly axial loading conditions. However, as UKA is a resurfacing procedure, the postoperative kinematics remain largely driven by the native knee’s movement patterns. Therefore, despite the inherent simplifications of finite element modeling, the observed biomechanical trends across different flexion angles—particularly the angle-dependent relationship between joint line elevation and mechanical response—retain biological plausibility and offer meaningful mechanistic insights for understanding clinical phenomena.

Conclusion

This study confirms that medial joint line elevation following fixed-bearing medial UKA triggers complex, angle-dependent biomechanical alterations: it increases load on the lateral compartment at full extension; induces paradoxical unloading during mid-flexion (30°-60°), with a specific stress concentration in the medial compartment at 60° flexion; and causes the lateral load to rise again to near physiological limits at 90° flexion. This phenomenon offers a potential mechanistic insight that may help explain early lateral compartment arthritis progression, polyethylene wear, and implant loosening. The results support the hypothesis that minor joint line elevation influences knee biomechanics in an angle-dependent manner. However, caution should be exercised when translating these computational findings into clinical recommendations, and future clinical studies are needed to validate their relevance.
