Gender, STEM career interest, and computational thinking: a social cognitive career theory perspective from Turkish adolescents
Authors: Mehmet Bars
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Motivational factors are widely recognized as central to students’ engagement in cognitively demanding learning; however, the role of STEM career interest in the development of computational thinking during adolescence remains insufficiently understood. It is also unclear whether this association differs by gender. Grounded in Social Cognitive Career Theory, this study examined the association between STEM career interest and computational thinking among high school students and tested the moderating role of gender. Data were collected from 467 students (Mage = 16.05, SD = 1.20; 57.2% female) enrolled in public science high schools in Diyarbakır, Türkiye, using a descriptive correlational design. Participants completed the STEM Career Interest Scale and the Computational Thinking Skills Scale. Moderation analysis was conducted using PROCESS (Model 1) with 5,000 bootstrap resamples. STEM career interest was positively associated with computational thinking. Gender showed no significant main effect, and the interaction between STEM career interest and gender was not significant, indicating that the strength of this association was similar for female and male students. These findings suggest that, within academically selective STEM-focused environments, motivational orientations toward STEM are linked to computational thinking in comparable ways across genders. The results highlight the importance of supporting students’ motivational engagement, alongside instructional practices, in fostering computational thinking during secondary education.

Introduction

In today’s world, the influence of science, technology, engineering, and mathematics (STEM) on social, economic, and individual life continues to expand. The acceleration of digitalization, the widespread use of data-driven decision-making, and the integration of complex technological systems into everyday life require individuals not only to acquire knowledge but also to apply it effectively. In this context, one of the central aims of contemporary education is to develop individuals who can adapt to changing conditions, analyze multidimensional problems, and generate viable solutions1.

Problems encountered in STEM fields often do not have a single correct answer, require the simultaneous consideration of multiple variables, and place substantial demands on abstract thinking. Addressing such problems therefore requires more than subject-matter knowledge; it involves systematic thinking, the ability to structure solution pathways, and the capacity to make sense of underlying processes. For this reason, cognitive processes such as problem solving and algorithmic thinking occupy a central place in contemporary STEM education2. Within learning environments characterized by increasing interaction with complex systems, it becomes especially important for students to structure the problems they encounter and to interpret their solution processes meaningfully. These abilities are viewed not only as supports for academic achievement but also as core competencies that foster active engagement in learning and contribute to cognitive flexibility3.

Within this broader context, educational research has increasingly focused on how students respond to the cognitive demands of STEM learning and which thinking skills they draw upon in doing so. The ways of thinking students employ during problem-solving processes, in particular, shape their learning experiences and prepare them for future academic and professional challenges. Accordingly, understanding students’ capacity to engage with complex problems is not only a pedagogical concern but also a matter of long-term individual and societal development4.

STEM career interest

Students’ perceptions of cognitive demands in STEM fields are closely linked to their career-related orientations toward these domains5. Particularly during high school, career interests are not merely abstract, future-oriented preferences but function as an important motivational resource that shapes students’ engagement with learning and their approaches to cognitively demanding tasks6.

Social Cognitive Career Theory (SCCT) provides a useful framework for understanding how these orientations are formed and how they translate into learning-related behaviors. Developed by Kızılay et al. 7, SCCT proposes that engagement with academic and career-relevant activities is shaped by three interrelated mechanisms: interest, self-efficacy, and outcome expectations. Interest reflects the extent to which a domain is experienced as personally meaningful; self-efficacy refers to individuals’ beliefs about their capability to perform domain-relevant tasks; and outcome expectations concern the anticipated consequences of such engagement. Within this framework, self-efficacy and outcome expectations are understood to support and sustain the development of interest over time7.

Accordingly, STEM career interest can be conceptualized as a multidimensional motivational orientation that reflects students’ perceived competence, confidence in their abilities, and expectations regarding the outcomes of their efforts7,8. From this perspective, it provides a useful lens for understanding how students allocate cognitive effort, persist in the face of difficulty, and engage with problem-solving processes9. Students with higher levels of STEM career interest are more likely to approach learning tasks as meaningful and worthwhile, to sustain effort when faced with complex problems, and to employ structured and strategic approaches during problem solving10–12.

Within SCCT, these processes collectively support engagement in learning, sustained cognitive effort, and persistence in complex tasks, thereby creating conditions that facilitate the use of higher-order cognitive strategies. The present study adopts a theoretically bounded approach to SCCT by focusing specifically on STEM career interest as a composite motivational construct associated with computational thinking. Rather than modeling the full SCCT framework, this study isolates a single theoretically specified pathway in order to examine its relationship with a higher-order cognitive outcome. This approach is consistent with prior work suggesting that examining individual pathways within SCCT can provide meaningful contributions to theory development7. Moreover, STEM career interest is operationalized as a composite construct to reflect the integrated nature of adolescents’ motivational orientations, which are often not yet fully differentiated across specific STEM domains. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted within the scope of this focused, pathway-based design rather than as a comprehensive test of the full SCCT model.

Within this framework, STEM career interest represents not only a future-oriented inclination but also an indicator of students’ ongoing motivational investment in learning. As this investment increases, students are more likely to approach problem-solving processes systematically, organize solution steps more effectively, and engage more actively with higher-order thinking strategies13. Computational thinking

In the present study, computational thinking is conceptualized as a higher-order cognitive competence that is analytically distinct from STEM career interest. Whereas STEM career interest reflects students’ motivational orientations toward a domain, computational thinking captures how students engage with problem situations, organize solution processes, and regulate their cognitive efforts during task performance. Accordingly, it is treated as an independent construct that reflects observable patterns of thinking within learning contexts rather than a motivational disposition14.

Within educational settings, computational thinking refers to the ability to interpret complex problems, decompose them into manageable components, and structure solution pathways in a systematic and logical manner. This form of thinking is inherently multidimensional, encompassing interrelated processes such as problem solving, algorithmic reasoning, critical thinking, creativity, and collaboration. Together, these processes enable students to approach problem situations in a deliberate, coherent, and flexible way, extending beyond a narrow focus on arriving at correct answers15.

The development of computational thinking, however, is not uniform across learners and does not occur independently of context. Rather, it is shaped by students’ learning experiences and by the extent to which they actively engage with cognitively demanding tasks over time16. This becomes particularly salient in STEM learning environments, where tasks often involve uncertainty, require the integration of multiple variables, and do not yield a single solution pathway. Such conditions call for students to move beyond the passive application of knowledge and instead reorganize, adapt, and apply what they know in new and evolving contexts2. From this perspective, computational thinking can be understood as a dynamic competence that develops through ongoing engagement with complex learning processes. Students who sustain cognitive effort during problem-solving are more likely to employ these processes effectively16,17. In this sense, computational thinking reflects not only what students know, but how they use and extend that knowledge in response to challenging tasks.

STEM career interest and computational thinking

Examining the relationship between STEM career interest and computational thinking represents a meaningful line of inquiry within the existing literature. Previous studies indicate that students who demonstrate stronger interest in STEM fields and higher levels of perceived competence tend to engage more actively in problem-solving processes and sustain their involvement in cognitively demanding tasks over time21. These patterns suggest that students’ motivational orientations toward STEM are closely associated with how they engage with complex cognitive tasks.

This relationship can be understood in terms of sustained sustained engagement during learning. Students who perceive themselves as capable in STEM domains are more likely to interpret challenging tasks as manageable, which encourages deeper engagement and sustained investment in solution processes18. Similarly, positive outcome expectations support continued engagement by linking learning activities to longer-term goals rather than short-term task completion9. In addition, students with stronger interest in STEM are more likely to approach learning tasks voluntarily and to engage more intensively with different stages of problem solving. This sustained engagement supports the development and use of structured strategies, including algorithmic reasoning and flexible thinking processes10,11.

Within this framework, computational thinking can be understood as a cognitive outcome associated with students’ ongoing engagement in STEM-related learning. As motivational resources strengthen, students are more likely to allocate sustained cognitive effort, which in turn supports the effective use of higher-order thinking processes7,9. Accordingly, the association between STEM career interest and computational thinking can be described as one that is directly observable, while also reflecting underlying processes related to engagement and cognitive effort. At the same time, it is important to clarify how this relationship is operationalized in the present study. Although SCCT conceptualizes self-efficacy and outcome expectations as distinct constructs, the current study employs a composite measure of STEM career interest that integrates these components. This approach is consistent with the original development and validation of the STEM Career Interest Survey, which was designed to capture students’ overall motivational orientation toward STEM careers19.

In addition, this operationalization is aligned with the developmental characteristics of the study sample. During adolescence, STEM-related interests tend to remain relatively broad, with clearer differentiation across specific domains emerging at later stages6,20. For students in grades 9–12, who are still forming general orientations toward STEM fields, a composite measure provides a theoretically meaningful representation of their motivational profile. This reflects a deliberate methodological choice aligned with the study’s focus on examining the association between motivational orientation and computational thinking, rather than modeling the full complexity of SCCT. Within this conceptual scope, the role of this relationship in the present study is to provide a theoretical basis for examining whether and how it may vary across contexts, particularly with respect to gender21.

Moderation role of gender

In research on learning processes and cognitive outcomes in STEM fields, gender is often treated as a complex and multidimensional construct rather than a simple basis for comparison. Inconsistent findings regarding differences between female and male students suggest that treating gender as a single, determinative factor offers limited explanatory value21,22. Accordingly, it is more informative to consider gender in terms of the conditions and mechanisms through which it may influence learning processes.

From this perspective, it cannot be assumed that the association between STEM career interest and computational thinking operates uniformly across all students. Instead, this relationship may vary depending on contextual factors that shape students’ engagement with learning. Differences in how students interpret academic demands, invest cognitive effort, and respond to learning environments may lead to variation in how motivational orientations are translated into cognitive outcomes21.

Within the SCCT framework, gender is conceptualized not as a direct determinant of learning, but as a contextual factor that interacts with self-efficacy beliefs, outcome expectations, and the structure of available opportunities9. Students’ perceptions of their competence, the expectations they encounter, and the supports or constraints present in their environments may differ in ways that are shaped by gender-related social experiences7,9. These influences are therefore expected to operate indirectly, through process-based pathways, rather than as direct effects on cognitive outcomes21. This perspective is particularly relevant during adolescence, a developmental period in which social expectations and opportunity structures play an important role in shaping academic orientations. Students’ engagement with STEM is influenced not only by individual characteristics but also by the broader educational contexts in which they are situated6. Within such contexts, gender can be understood as one element within a broader set of social–cognitive processes that shape learning experiences9. Accordingly, in the present study, gender is conceptualized as a moderating factor that may influence how the association between STEM career interest and computational thinking is expressed under different conditions. Rather than assuming the presence of gender differences, the analysis examines whether this relationship varies across female and male students, thereby providing a context-sensitive test of the proposed theoretical framework.

Present study

Existing research suggests that career interest in STEM fields is associated with students’ learning processes and cognitive outcomes. Similarly, a growing body of work has examined how computational thinking develops within STEM learning environments and which individual characteristics are linked to this development17,23. However, studies that directly examine these two constructs together remain limited, and the conditions under which their relationship may vary have not been sufficiently explored.

In particular, whether the association between STEM career interest and computational thinking operates similarly for female and male students has largely been addressed only indirectly. Although gender has often been included as a descriptive or control variable, its potential role in shaping the strength or direction of this relationship has not been examined systematically21. This gap is especially relevant in the context of adolescence, where motivational orientations, cognitive processes, and contextual influences interact in complex ways6,21. Taken together, these considerations point to the need for a more focused examination of how gender may function within this relationship.

Accordingly, the primary aim of the present study is to examine the association between high school students’ STEM career interest and their computational thinking skills, and to test whether this relationship varies as a function of gender. In doing so, the study focuses on how students’ motivational orientations toward STEM are reflected in their computational thinking, while considering gender as a contextual moderator. Based on this framework, the following hypotheses were tested:

H1. STEM career interest is positively associated with computational thinking.

H2. Gender is associated with differences in computational thinking.

H3. Gender moderates the association between STEM career interest and computational thinking.

Method

Design

This study was designed to examine the relationship between high school students’ career interest in STEM fields and their computational thinking skills, as well as the moderating role of gender in this relationship. The research employed a descriptive and correlational design aimed at identifying associations among variables. The theoretical framework was informed by SCCT and is grounded in the assumption that STEM career interest is associated with cognitive outcomes through motivational and cognitive orientations9. Accordingly, the research model included the association between STEM career interest and computational thinking, as well as the moderating role of gender in this relationship. Participants and procedure

This study was conducted with high school students enrolled in public science high schools located in Diyarbakır, Türkiye. Data were collected between December 2025 and January 2026, during the first semester of the 2025–2026 academic year. Science high schools were selected because they provide an instructional environment in which STEM-related learning activities are emphasized and students are frequently exposed to cognitively demanding problem-solving tasks. This context is particularly relevant for examining the relationship between STEM career interest and computational thinking during adolescence.

A total of 467 students participated in the study. Of these, 200 were male (42.8%) and 267 were female (57.2%). Participants were enrolled in grades 9 through 12, with age ranging from 13 to 18 years (M = 16.05, SD = 1.19). An a priori power analysis conducted using GPower (v.3.1) indicated that a minimum sample size of 353 participants was required to detect small-to-medium effects (f2 = 0.03) with α = 0.05 and statistical power (1 − β) =.9024. The final sample of 467 therefore provided sufficient statistical power for the analyses. Participants were recruited using a purposeful convenience sampling strategy. Access to schools was determined by administrative permissions and logistical feasibility, while the selection of science high schools reflected the theoretical focus of the study on STEM-oriented learning environments. This approach allowed the study to balance practical constraints with conceptual relevance.

Ethical approval was obtained from the Dicle University Social and Human Sciences Ethics Committee (Decision No. 550, dated 15/12/2025). Students were informed about the purpose of the study, the voluntary nature of participation, and the confidentiality of their responses. Written informed consent was obtained from parents or legal guardians, and assent was obtained from all students. Data were collected using paper-and-pencil questionnaires administered during class time. Completion required approximately 20–25 min, and all responses were recorded anonymously.

Measures

STEM career ınterest

STEM career interest was assessed using the STEM Career Interest Scale19, adapted into Turkish by Kızılay et al.7. The scale measures students’ motivational orientations toward STEM careers within the SCCT framework. An example item is: “I can imagine myself working in a career related to science/mathematics/engineering/technology.” The scale consists of 20 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree) and comprises three dimensions: interest, self-efficacy, and outcome expectations. Higher scores indicate stronger interest, higher perceived self-efficacy, and more positive outcome expectations. Internal consistency in the present study was high (α = 0.83–0.93).

A confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) supported the three-factor structure (χ2/df = 4.76, CFI = 0.89, TLI = 0.86, RMSEA = 0.09). Although the CFI and TLI values were slightly below the conventional 0.90 threshold and the RMSEA marginally exceeded 0.08, these indices were interpreted in light of the cross-cultural adaptation of the scale and the characteristics of the sample. Previous research has noted that commonly used fit index cutoffs should not be treated as absolute criteria but rather evaluated in relation to model complexity and context25–27. Modification indices were inspected following the initial model estimation. No theoretically justifiable correlated residuals were identified, and the factor structure was therefore retained as originally specified. Together with the high internal consistency coefficients, these results were considered to provide adequate support for the construct validity of the scale for the purposes of the present study.

Computational thinking

Computational thinking was assessed using the Computational Thinking Skills Scale, originally developed by Korkmaz et al.28 to evaluate individuals’ computational thinking competencies. An example item is: “I am good at preparing regular plans regarding the solution of the complex problems.” The scale consists of 29 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree) and comprises five dimensions: creativity, algorithmic thinking, collaboration, critical thinking, and problem solving. Higher scores indicate stronger computational thinking skills. In the present study, the scale demonstrated good internal consistency (α = 0.84).

CFA was conducted to examine the factor structure of the scale. The results supported the five-factor model (χ2/df = 2.73, CFI = 0.84, TLI = 0.83, RMSEA = 0.06). Although the CFI and TLI values were below the conventional 0.90 threshold, the RMSEA indicated acceptable model fit. Fit indices were interpreted in light of the sample characteristics and prior literature suggesting that cutoff values should be considered as guidelines rather than absolute criteria25–27. Modification indices were also examined, and no theoretically justifiable correlated residuals were identified. Accordingly, the original factor structure was retained. Overall, the results were considered to provide adequate support for the construct validity of the scale for the purposes of the present study.

Common method bias and multicollinearity

Because all variables were measured using self-report instruments, the potential for common method bias (CMB) was examined using Harman’s single-factor test. An exploratory factor analysis (EFA) including all measurement items indicated that multiple factors with eigenvalues greater than 1 emerged. The first unrotated factor accounted for 21.6% of the total variance, which is below the commonly suggested threshold of 40%, suggesting that common method bias is unlikely to pose a serious threat to the validity of the findings29. It should be noted, however, that Harman’s single-factor test is widely regarded as a preliminary diagnostic rather than a definitive assessment of CMB, as it may lack sensitivity under certain conditions29. More advanced approaches, such as the inclusion of a latent method factor or the use of marker variables within a CFA framework, have been recommended to provide a more rigorous assessment of method effects. To further reduce the risk of method bias, several procedural safeguards were implemented during data collection. Participants were informed that their responses were anonymous and that participation was entirely voluntary. They were also instructed to respond honestly and independently, and were assured that their responses would not affect their academic standing. These procedures are expected to reduce the likelihood of systematic response biases, such as social desirability or evaluation apprehension29.

Multicollinearity among predictors was also assessed prior to hypothesis testing. Collinearity diagnostics indicated that variance inflation factor (VIF) values were 1.00 and tolerance values were 1.00, suggesting no multicollinearity concerns. The highest condition index observed in the model was 2.68, well below the critical threshold of 30. Together, these results indicate that multicollinearity did not affect the regression estimates30.

Data analysis

Data analyses were conducted using SPSS (v.23) and AMOS (v.23). Descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations, and Pearson correlation coefficients, were computed using SPSS. The construct validity of the measurement instruments was examined through CFA conducted in AMOS. Maximum likelihood estimation was employed, and model fit was evaluated using multiple goodness-of-fit indices. Prior to the main analyses, the assumptions of parametric testing were assessed. The normality of the data was examined using skewness and kurtosis values, which were within acceptable ranges. Multicollinearity among the study variables was evaluated using VIF and tolerance values, and the results indicated no evidence of multicollinearity. Autocorrelation was also assessed and did not present a concern for the analyses. The internal consistency of the measurement instruments was evaluated using Cronbach’s α coefficients.

Bivariate relationships among the study variables were examined using Pearson correlation analysis. To examine the moderating role of gender in the relationship between STEM career interest and computational thinking, the PROCESS macro (v.4.3) developed by31 was employed. Specifically, Model 1 was used to estimate the moderation model. The analysis was conducted using a bootstrap procedure with 5,000 resamples to obtain bias-corrected confidence intervals (CIs) for the estimated effects. Statistical significance was evaluated at the 0.05 level.

Results

Preliminary analysis

Preliminary analyses were conducted to examine the descriptive characteristics, distributional properties, reliability estimates, and bivariate associations among the study variables. Descriptive statistics indicated that the mean levels of STEM career interest and computational thinking were moderate to high. Skewness and kurtosis values fell within acceptable ranges, suggesting no substantial deviations from normality. Internal consistency was evaluated using McDonald’s omega (ω) and Guttman’s lambda 6 (λ6), both of which provide robust estimates for latent constructs. The results indicated satisfactory to high reliability for both STEM career interest and computational thinking. Zero-order Pearson correlation analysis revealed a positive and statistically significant association between STEM career interest and computational thinking, indicating that higher levels of STEM career interest were associated with higher levels of computational thinking. Collectively, these findings support the suitability of the data for subsequent moderation analyses. Moderation analysis

A moderation analysis was conducted using Model 1 to examine whether gender moderated the association between STEM career interest and computational thinking. The overall model was statistically significant, F(3, 463) = 22.80, p <.001, explaining approximately 12.9% of the variance in computational thinking (R2 = 0.13), which represents a modest but meaningful proportion in the context of educational research. STEM career interest was positively associated with computational thinking (β = 0.34, p <.05), supporting H1. However, gender did not have a significant main effect on computational thinking (β = −0.02, p >.05), providing no support for H2. In addition, the interaction between STEM career interest and gender was not statistically significant (β = 0.01, p >.05), and the change in explained variance attributable to the interaction was negligible (ΔR2 < 0.001). These results indicate that the association between STEM career interest and computational thinking does not vary as a function of gender. Accordingly, H3 was not supported (cf. Fig. 4).

Discussion

This study examined the relationship between high school students’ career interest in STEM fields and their computational thinking skills, as well as the potential moderating role of gender in this relationship. The findings indicate a significant and positive association between STEM career interest and computational thinking, while showing that gender does not have a direct effect on computational thinking and does not moderate this relationship. Rather than pointing to the absence of gender-related differences, these results suggest that the influence of STEM career interest on cognitive outcomes operates primarily through students’ sustained engagement and effort in learning. In this sense, the findings contribute to a more nuanced understanding of STEM learning processes by highlighting the role of motivational mechanisms rather than treating gender as a determining factor.

The first key finding shows that students’ career interest in STEM fields is positively associated with their computational thinking skills. This suggests that STEM career interest is not only a future-oriented preference but also reflects the extent to which students invest effort in cognitively demanding tasks. Students with higher levels of interest are more likely to engage deeply with problem-solving processes, sustain effort over time, and apply structured strategies when facing complex tasks17,23. This pattern aligns with the motivation–behavior linkage emphasized in SCCT, where interest functions as a resource that supports engagement and persistence9–11. Given that computational thinking involves planning, iterative problem solving, and cognitive flexibility15,17, it is reasonable to interpret this relationship as reflecting the role of sustained active engagement in supporting these processes. Accordingly, the present findings suggest that computational thinking is shaped not only by instructional factors but also by students’ orientations toward learning.

At the same time, the use of a composite measure of STEM career interest should be considered when interpreting this relationship. During adolescence, STEM-related interests are often broadly defined, with clearer differentiation across subdomains emerging at later stages6,20. In this context, treating STEM career interest as a general motivational orientation is appropriate for capturing students’ overall engagement with STEM. However, STEM fields are not homogeneous, and the motivational processes underlying interest may vary across domains such as mathematics, engineering, or technology6,20. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted as reflecting a general orientation toward STEM rather than domain-specific patterns, and they may obscure potential differences across subdomains.

A second finding is that gender does not have a significant direct effect on computational thinking. This suggests that, within the context of the present sample, computational thinking may be shaped more by shared academic experiences than by demographic characteristics. Students in science high schools are selected through similar academic criteria and are exposed to comparable curricular structures and expectations32,33. As a result, they develop within a relatively uniform learning environment that emphasizes problem solving, analytical reasoning, and sustained cognitive engagement. Under such conditions, differences in computational thinking are less likely to be associated with gender and more likely to reflect common educational experiences16,17,34. This interpretation is consistent with prior research indicating that gender differences in cognitive outcomes tend to diminish in academically selective and cognitively demanding settings28,34.

Moreover, the absence of a direct gender effect should not be interpreted as evidence that gender is irrelevant. Rather, it suggests that gender may operate through indirect pathways, such as self-efficacy, outcome expectations, or access to learning opportunities, as proposed in SCCT9. From this perspective, the findings point to the importance of considering how contextual conditions shape the expression of individual differences. In educational settings where students encounter similar expectations and opportunities, gender-related differences in cognitive outcomes may become less pronounced. Consistent with this interpretation, the results also indicate that gender does not moderate the relationship between STEM career interest and computational thinking33,34 In other words, the positive association between interest and computational thinking appears to follow a similar pattern for both female and male students. This finding suggests that, under certain conditions, motivational processes may operate in comparable ways across genders35, 36. One explanation relates to the characteristics of the study context. Because the sample consists of students in academically selective science high schools, participants are likely to share similar levels of academic preparation, learning opportunities, and support structures. Such contextual homogeneity may reduce variability in both sustained engagement and performance in learning, thereby attenuating potential gender-based differences and limiting the likelihood of detecting moderation effects. Within this relatively uniform environment, the translation of STEM career interest into cognitive engagement may occur through similar pathways for all students. Notably, this characteristic of the sample suggests that the present findings should be interpreted as context-dependent rather than universally generalizable. The relatively homogeneous and high-achieving nature of science high school students may have shaped both the level and variability of the observed variables, thereby contributing to the absence of gender differences in this study.

Taken as a whole, these findings highlight the importance of interpreting gender not as an isolated determinant but as a context-dependent factor whose influence may vary across educational settings. Rather than asking whether gender differences exist in general, the results point to the need to examine the conditions under which such differences emerge or diminish. In this sense, the absence of a moderating effect should be understood as context-specific rather than universal. When all findings are considered together, they suggest that the relationship between STEM career interest and computational thinking is shaped by students’ engagement with learning tasks and the conditions in which this engagement occurs. This relationship should not be interpreted as a comprehensive explanation of computational thinking, but rather as evidence for a specific motivational pathway within a broader set of influencing factors. The results therefore support the view that cognitive outcomes are linked to students’ motivational orientations while also being shaped by contextual conditions. In this respect, the study contributes to a more integrated understanding of how motivational and contextual factors interact in the development of cognitive competencies.

Finally, the proportion of explained variance observed in this study (R2 = 0.13) indicates that computational thinking is influenced by a wider range of factors beyond STEM career interest alone14,16,17. This is consistent with the focused scope of the present model, which was designed to examine a specific theoretically derived relationship rather than to provide a comprehensive account of all determinants9. Future research could build on this finding by incorporating additional predictors, such as academic achievement, learning engagement, and contextual or school-level variables, in order to develop more comprehensive models of computational thinking.

Novelty, limitations and future directions

This study contributes to the literature by examining the relationship between STEM career interest and computational thinking within the framework of SCCT, while treating gender as a moderating factor rather than a descriptive variable. By focusing on a specific motivational pathway linking STEM career interest to a higher-order cognitive outcome, the study adopts a theoretically bounded approach that complements broader structural models7. In doing so, it highlights the role of motivational orientations in shaping students’ cognitive engagement during adolescence and extends existing research by integrating cognitive and career-related perspectives.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the sample consisted of students enrolled in science high schools in Diyarbakır, which represent academically selective and STEM-focused environments. As a result, the findings may not generalize to students in other educational contexts. Importantly, this characteristic of the sample should be understood not only as a limitation but also as a contextual boundary condition of the findings. The relatively homogeneous and high-achieving nature of science high school students may have influenced both the level and variability of the observed variables, potentially contributing to the absence of gender differences and shaping the strength of the reported relationships. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted within the context of academically selective educational settings. Second, the use of self-report instruments may introduce biases related to social desirability and response tendencies, despite the strong psychometric properties of the measures. Third, the cross-sectional design limits conclusions about the directionality of the relationships among variables. In addition, the modest proportion of explained variance indicates that computational thinking is likely influenced by a broader set of factors beyond STEM career interest alone.

Future research can build on these findings in several ways. Replication across different school types, socioeconomic contexts, and cultural settings would strengthen the generalizability of the results, while longitudinal designs could help clarify how STEM career interest and computational thinking develop over time. Incorporating additional theoretically grounded predictors, such as academic achievement, learning engagement, and contextual or school-level variables, may provide a more comprehensive understanding of computational thinking. Further work may also examine the underlying mechanisms proposed by SCCT in greater detail. Modeling self-efficacy beliefs and outcome expectations as separate latent variables within a structural equation modeling framework would allow for a more detailed examination of how motivational orientations translate into cognitive outcomes. In addition, conditional process models could be used to explore whether these mechanisms operate differently across genders or educational contexts37. Finally, although STEM career interest was treated as a composite construct in line with the instrument’s validated scoring procedure19, the STEM Career Interest Survey also provides separate scores for science, technology, engineering, and mathematics. Future research could examine these domain-specific interests separately to determine whether their relationships with computational thinking differ in strength or direction. Such analyses may reveal patterns that are not observable when STEM is treated as a single construct and would allow for a more fine-grained understanding of motivational processes across STEM fields. This distinction is particularly important for practice, as domain-level findings could better inform career counseling by enabling guidance strategies that align with students’ emerging, field-specific interests.

Theoretical and practical implications

The findings of this study offer a basis for reinterpreting the relationship between STEM career interest and computational thinking from a social–cognitive perspective. In particular, examining gender as a moderating context highlights the need to move beyond explanations of cognitive outcomes that rely solely on individual characteristics. Instead, the results point to the importance of the dynamic interplay among self-efficacy beliefs, outcome expectations, and contextual conditions emphasized by SCCT7,9. Within this framework, computational thinking can be understood not as a fixed cognitive capacity but as a process shaped by students’ motivational orientations. STEM career interest, therefore, reflects more than an occupational preference; it also signals the extent to which students invest cognitive effort in learning tasks. The absence of a moderating effect of gender further reinforces the view that contextual conditions may shape how motivational processes translate into cognitive outcomes. Taken together, these findings contribute to a more integrated perspective on cognitive development in STEM by linking motivational and contextual processes, particularly during adolescence.

The findings also carry important implications for educational practice. First, efforts to develop computational thinking should not be limited to technical or purely cognitive instruction. Interventions that do not consider students’ interest in STEM, their self-efficacy beliefs, and their future-oriented expectations may have limited impact. Instead, STEM-based instructional programs should be designed to support students’ motivational engagement. For example, learning activities that foster a sense of competence, provide meaningful feedback, and make future applications visible may indirectly strengthen computational thinking. In addition, the findings related to gender suggest that educational practices should remain flexible and responsive to contextual differences rather than relying on uniform approaches. From a policy and school-level perspective, supporting students’ STEM career interest from early stages appears to be closely linked to the development of cognitive skills20,21, particularly in career counseling contexts where guidance strategies need to align with students’ emerging, domain-specific interests. More broadly, these results suggest that STEM education should move beyond a narrow focus on academic performance and instead consider students’ patterns of engagement and cognitive investment in learning processes. Such an approach may support the development of more inclusive and sustainable educational practices by recognizing the role of students’ motivational orientations in shaping cognitive outcomes.

Conclusion

This study examined the relationship between high school students’ STEM career interest and their computational thinking skills. The findings indicate a significant and positive association between these variables, suggesting that STEM career interest is meaningfully related to students’ engagement with cognitively demanding tasks. At the same time, the results show that this relationship does not differ significantly by gender, pointing to a similar pattern across female and male students. Taken together, these findings highlight the role of motivational orientations in the development of computational thinking and suggest that, within academically selective contexts, shared learning experiences may play a more prominent role than demographic differences. By positioning STEM career interest as a relevant motivational factor associated with computational thinking, the study contributes to a more context-sensitive understanding of cognitive development during adolescence within a social–cognitive framework.
