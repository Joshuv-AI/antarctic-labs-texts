Abstract
. We compute the Cox ring of an embedded variety X ⊆ Z X\subseteq Z within a Mori dream space, under the assumption that the pullback map induces an isomorphism at the level of divisor class groups. We show that the Cox ring of X X is the intersection of finitely many localizations of a quotient image of the Cox ring of Z Z . As a consequence, we provide an algorithm that terminates if and only if the Cox ring of X X is finitely generated, thereby generalizing previous works on the subject [ 2 , 9 , 12 , 1 ] . We apply these results to compute the Cox ring of hypersurfaces in smooth projective toric varieties.

D(m_{i})\subseteq\tilde{X}_{1}
is normal, and
normalization commutes with localization, so

(R^{\nu})_{m_{i}}=R_{m_{i}}
. Thus this preliminary step does not change the
intersection to be computed.

For any intermediate ring
A
such that

R\subseteq A\subseteq R_{m_{1}}\cap\cdots\cap R_{m_{s}}
, and for every
integer

, we set

I_{k}(A):=\bigcap_{i=2}^{s}\langle m_{1}^{k}\rangle_{A}:\langle m_{i}\rangle_{A}^{\infty}.

Thus

consists of those elements

such that

belongs to

for every

.

Algorithm 1

Computing the intersection of localizations

Input :
A finitely generated normal domain

over a field and non-zero elements

Output :

Remark 2.1
.

When
R
is a graded ring, as in the case of Cox rings, and the elements

m_{1},\dots,m_{s}
are homogeneous, the ideals
\langle m_{1}^{k}\rangle_{R}
and

\langle m_{i}\rangle_{R}
are homogeneous. Since colon ideals and saturations of
homogeneous ideals are homogeneous, each ideal
I_{k}(R)
is homogeneous.

Therefore one may choose a homogeneous generating set for
I_{k}(R)
. With this
choice, the elements of
B_{n}
are homogeneous. If one gives
S_{f}
the degree

\deg(f)-n\deg(m_{1})
, then the relation
S_{f}m_{1}^{n}-f
is homogeneous.
Thus the quotient appearing in Algorithm
1
is graded. Moreover, the
normalization of a finitely generated graded domain inside its homogeneous
fraction field is again graded. Hence the algorithm is compatible with the Cox
ring grading.

Before proving the algorithm, we recall two elementary lemmas.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
A
be a noetherian normal domain, and let
m_{1},\dots,m_{s}\in A
be
non-zero elements. Then

A=\bigcap_{i=1}^{s}A_{m_{i}}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\operatorname{codim}_{\operatorname{Spec}(A)}V(m_{1},\dots,m_{s})\geq 2.

Equivalently, if
R\subseteq A\subseteq R_{m_{1}}\cap\cdots\cap R_{m_{s}}
and

A
is a noetherian normal domain, then
A=R_{m_{1}}\cap\cdots\cap R_{m_{s}}

if and only if
V(m_{1},\dots,m_{s})
has codimension at least
2
in

{\rm Spec}(A)
.

Proof.

Let
K=\operatorname{Frac}(A)
. Since
A
is a noetherian normal domain, it
is a Krull domain. Hence
A
is the intersection, inside
K
, of the
local rings
A_{\mathfrak{p}}
, where
\mathfrak{p}
runs over the
height-one primes of
A
. Similarly,
A_{m}
is obtained by intersecting only
those
A_{\mathfrak{p}}
such that
m\notin\mathfrak{p}
.

It follows that
\bigcap_{i}A_{m_{i}}
is the intersection of the height-one
localizations
A_{\mathfrak{p}}
such that
\mathfrak{p}
does not contain
all the elements
m_{1},\dots,m_{s}
. If
V(m_{1},\dots,m_{s})
has codimension at
least
2
, no height-one prime contains all the
m_{i}
. Therefore the above
intersection is the intersection over all height-one primes, hence it is
A
.

Conversely, suppose that
V(m_{1},\dots,m_{s})
has codimension
1
. Then there
is a height-one prime
\mathfrak{p}
containing
(m_{1},\dots,m_{s})
. Since

A
is a Krull domain, the approximation theorem gives an element

x\in K
with negative valuation along
\mathfrak{p}
and non-negative
valuation along every other height-one prime. Then
x\notin A
. However,
after localizing at any
m_{i}
, the prime
\mathfrak{p}
disappears, because

m_{i}\in\mathfrak{p}
. Hence
x\in A_{m_{i}}
for every
i
. Therefore

A\subsetneq\bigcap_{i}A_{m_{i}}
.

The final statement follows because, if

R\subseteq A\subseteq R_{m_{1}}\cap\cdots\cap R_{m_{s}}
, then localizing at

m_{i}
gives
A_{m_{i}}=R_{m_{i}}
for every
i
.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
A
be an integral domain, let
m\in A
be non-zero, and let

f_{1},\dots,f_{t}\in A
,
r_{1},\dots,r_{t}>0
. Let

A^{\prime}=A[f_{1}/m^{r_{1}},\dots,f_{t}/m^{r_{t}}]\subseteq A_{m}
. Then

A^{\prime}\simeq A[S_{1},\dots,S_{t}]\Big/\left(\langle S_{1}m^{r_{1}}-f_{1},\dots,S_{t}m^{r_{t}}-f_{t}\rangle:\langle m\rangle^{\infty}\right).

Proof.

Consider the natural surjective homomorphism

\varphi:A[S_{1},\dots,S_{t}]\to A^{\prime}
sending
S_{j}
to
f_{j}/m^{r_{j}}
.
After localizing at
m
, its kernel is generated by

S_{j}-f_{j}/m^{r_{j}}
, or equivalently by
S_{j}m^{r_{j}}-f_{j}
, for

j=1,\dots,t
. The kernel of
\varphi
is the contraction of this localized
kernel to
A[S_{1},\dots,S_{t}]
. This contraction is precisely the saturation
with respect to
\langle m\rangle
. This proves the claim.
∎

Proof of Algorithm
1
.

Let
R_{0}
denote the initial ring, and set

T:=(R_{0})_{m_{1}}\cap\cdots\cap(R_{0})_{m_{s}}\subseteq K
.
We prove by induction that the current ring
A
is a finitely generated
normal domain contained in
T
, and that

A_{m_{i}}=(R_{0})_{m_{i}}
for every
i
. This is clear at the beginning,
where
A=R_{0}
.

Assume that these properties hold for the current ring
A
. If

V(m_{1},\dots,m_{s})
has codimension at least
2
in
{\rm Spec}(A)
, then
Lemma
2.2
gives
A=T
, and the algorithm returns the desired
intersection.

Suppose instead that
V(m_{1},\dots,m_{s})
has codimension
1
in

{\rm Spec}(A)
. Again by Lemma
2.2
, we have
A\neq T
.
Choose
x\in T\setminus A
. Since
T\subseteq A_{m_{1}}
, we may write

x=f/m_{1}^{n}
, with
f\in A
and
n>0
. Since
x\notin A
, one has

f\notin\langle m_{1}^{n}\rangle_{A}
. Moreover, the condition

x\in A_{m_{i}}
, for
i\geq 2
, is equivalent to saying that some power of

m_{i}
sends
f
into
\langle m_{1}^{n}\rangle_{A}
. In other words,

f\in I_{n}(A)
. Thus
I_{n}(A)\not\subseteq\langle m_{1}^{n}\rangle_{A}
, so the
integer searched for by the algorithm exists.

Let
B_{n}
be obtained from a finite generating set of
I_{n}(A)
by keeping
only those generators which do not belong to
\langle m_{1}^{n}\rangle_{A}
.
Adjoining the fractions
f/m_{1}^{n}
, with
f\in B_{n}
, is enough to adjoin

h/m_{1}^{n}
for every
h\in I_{n}(A)
, since the discarded generators give
fractions already contained in
A
.

By Lemma
2.3
, the saturated quotient appearing in the algorithm is
the subring of
A_{m_{1}}
generated by
A
and the fractions

f/m_{1}^{n}
, with
f\in B_{n}
. All these fractions belong to
T
, so this
new ring is contained in
T
. Since
T
is an intersection of normal
domains inside
K
, it is normal. Therefore the normalization of the new
ring in
K
is still contained in
T
.

Finally, localizing the new ring at any
m_{i}
gives the same localization as
before: the new ring contains
A
and is contained in
A_{m_{i}}
. Thus
after localization at
m_{i}
we get

A_{m_{i}}\subseteq A^{\prime}_{m_{i}}\subseteq A_{m_{i}},

where
A^{\prime}
denotes the normalized new ring. Hence

A^{\prime}_{m_{i}}=A_{m_{i}}=(R_{0})_{m_{i}}
for every
i
.
The inductive properties are preserved, and this proves the correctness of
the algorithm.
∎

Proposition 2.4
.

Algorithm
1
terminates if and only if

R_{m_{1}}\cap\cdots\cap R_{m_{s}}
is a finitely generated
R
-algebra.

Proof.

Let
T:=R_{m_{1}}\cap\cdots\cap R_{m_{s}}
.

If the algorithm terminates, then by the proof of correctness the final ring is

T
. At each step one adjoins finitely many fractions and then takes the
normalization of a finitely generated domain over a field. Since normalization
is finite in this setting, the final ring is a finitely generated

R
-algebra. Thus
T
is finitely generated over
R
.

Conversely, assume that
T
is finitely generated over
R
. Choose
generators
\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{t}
such that
T=R[\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{t}]
.
Let
A
be an intermediate ring appearing during the algorithm. Since

R\subseteq A\subseteq T
, the same elements generate
T
as an

A
-algebra.
For each
j
, write
\theta_{j}=a_{j}/m_{1}^{r_{j}}
with
a_{j}\in A
, choosing

r_{j}
minimal. Let
\rho(A)
be the maximum of these exponents. If

\rho(A)=0
, then all
\theta_{j}
belong to
A
, hence
A=T
, and the algorithm
stops by Lemma
2.2
.

Assume that
\rho(A)>0
, and let
n
be the integer chosen by the algorithm.
If
r_{j}>0
, then
\theta_{j}\notin A
, so
a_{j}\notin\langle m_{1}^{r_{j}}\rangle_{A}
.
Since
\theta_{j}\in T
, we have
a_{j}\in I_{r_{j}}(A)
; hence, by the minimality
of
n
, one has
n\leq r_{j}
.

Moreover
a_{j}/m_{1}^{n}
belongs to
T
, and
a_{j}\in I_{n}(A)
. The new ring
contains all fractions
h/m_{1}^{n}
with
h\in I_{n}(A)
, because these are
generated by the fractions associated with a set of generators of
I_{n}(A)
,
up to elements already in
A
. Therefore, in the next intermediate ring,

\theta_{j}
can be written with denominator exponent at most
r_{j}-n
.
Thus every positive denominator exponent strictly decreases, while
zero exponents remain zero. Hence
\rho(A)
decreases by at least one at
each nonterminal step, proving termination.
∎

At each step of the algorithm, one has to compute rings obtained by adjoining
fractions to
R
, typically by means of saturation and normalization. The next
criterion provides a way to certify finite generation of the intersection of
localizations without performing all the intermediate saturation and
normalization computations. Namely, once a finite set of candidate generators
has been found, it suffices to verify a codimension condition and normality.

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
R
be a normal finitely generated
\mathbb{K}
-domain and let

m_{1},\dots,m_{k}\in R
be non-zero elements. Assume that

q_{j}:=f_{j}/m_{1}^{r_{j}}
belongs to
R_{m_{1}}\cap\cdots\cap R_{m_{k}}
for

j=1,\dots,t
. Let
P:=R[s_{1},\dots,s_{t}]
and let

I:=\langle s_{1}m_{1}^{r_{1}}-f_{1},\dots,s_{t}m_{1}^{r_{t}}-f_{t}\rangle.

Assume that there exists an ideal
J\subseteq P
such that

I\subseteq J\subseteq I:\langle m_{1}\rangle^{\infty}

and, setting
A:=P/J
, assume that
A
is equidimensional and that

\operatorname{codim}_{\operatorname{Spec}(A)}V(m_{1},\dots,m_{k})\geq 2.

Let
\overline{A}
be the image of
A
in
R_{m_{1}}
under the homomorphism

s_{j}\mapsto q_{j}
. Equivalently,
\overline{A}\simeq P/(I:\langle m_{1}\rangle^{\infty})
.
Let
\overline{A}^{\nu}
be the normalization of
\overline{A}
in

\operatorname{Frac}(R)
. Then

\overline{A}^{\nu}=R_{m_{1}}\cap\cdots\cap R_{m_{k}}.

In particular, if
\overline{A}
is normal, it is already the desired
intersection. Alternatively, equidimensionality of
A
may be omitted
if the codimension hypothesis is imposed directly in

\operatorname{Spec}(\overline{A})
.

Proof.

Consider the homomorphism
\varphi:P\to R_{m_{1}}
defined by

s_{j}\mapsto q_{j}
. By Lemma
2.3
, its kernel is

\ker(\varphi)=I:\langle m_{1}\rangle^{\infty}
. Hence the image of
A=P/J

under
\varphi
is
\overline{A}\simeq P/(I:\langle m_{1}\rangle^{\infty})
and,
under this identification,
\overline{A}=R[q_{1},\dots,q_{t}]\subseteq R_{m_{1}}
.
Since each
q_{j}
belongs to
R_{m_{1}}\cap\cdots\cap R_{m_{k}}
, we have

\overline{A}\subseteq R_{m_{i}}
for every
i=1,\dots,k
.
Since
A_{m_{1}}=R_{m_{1}}
is a domain, the kernel of

A\to\overline{A}
is the unique minimal prime of
A
not containing

m_{1}
. Thus
\operatorname{Spec}(\overline{A})
is an irreducible
component of
\operatorname{Spec}(A)
. By equidimensionality it has the
same dimension as
A
. The assumed codimension bound on
A
therefore
implies the same bound on
\operatorname{Spec}(\overline{A})
.
If this latter bound is assumed directly, no equidimensionality
assumption is needed.
For every
i
, localizing the inclusions

R\subseteq\overline{A}\subseteq R_{m_{i}}
at
m_{i}
gives

\overline{A}_{m_{i}}=R_{m_{i}}
. Since normalization commutes with localization
and
R_{m_{i}}
is normal, we get
(\overline{A}^{\nu})_{m_{i}}=R_{m_{i}}
for all

i=1,\dots,k
. Moreover,
\overline{A}^{\nu}
is finite over
\overline{A}
,
because
\overline{A}
is a finitely generated
\mathbb{K}
-algebra. Hence the
inverse image of
V(m_{1},\dots,m_{k})
in
\operatorname{Spec}(\overline{A}^{\nu})

still has codimension at least
2
.
Now
\overline{A}^{\nu}
is normal. Applying Lemma
2.2
to

\overline{A}^{\nu}
, we obtain

\overline{A}^{\nu}=(\overline{A}^{\nu})_{m_{1}}\cap\cdots\cap(\overline{A}^{\nu})_{m_{k}}=R_{m_{1}}\cap\cdots\cap R_{m_{k}}.

If
\overline{A}
is normal, its normalization can be omitted.
∎

Proof of Theorem

.

We first establish the geometric properties needed for
Theorem
1
. The restriction map

\operatorname{Cl}(\mathbb{P})\xrightarrow{\sim}\operatorname{Cl}(X)

is an isomorphism by
[

]
*Thm. 1 when
n\geq 4
.
When

, the Cartier assumption on

implies that

\mathbb{P}
is Gorenstein. By

*Prop. 1, ampleness of

imply that

is very ample.
Since toric varieties have rational singularities, the
Grauert–Riemenschneider canonical sheaf equals
\omega_{\mathbb{P}}
.
Thus
[
14
]
*Thm. 1 applies and gives the same isomorphism for
very general

.

By Bertini,

is normal and

is a nonempty big open subset of
X
.
The characteristic space
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}
is a smooth open subset
of
\mathbb{A}^{r}
, and the Cox-coordinate sections of

have no common
zero on it. Bertini therefore shows that

V(f)\cap\widehat{\mathbb{P}}
is smooth for general
f
.
Moreover,
p_{\mathbb{P}}^{-1}(\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{Sing}})
has
codimension at least two in
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}
: points with at most
one zero Cox coordinate map to the torus or to a codimension-one torus

\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{reg}}
.
A general section contains none of the components of this inverse

still has codimension at least

.
Consequently

\widehat{X}=V(f)\cap\widehat{\mathbb{P}},\qquad p_{\mathbb{P}}^{-1}(X_{0})\text{ is big in }\widehat{X}.

The space
p_{\mathbb{P}}^{-1}(X_{0})
is a characteristic space for
X_{0}

by
[
3
]
*Cor. 4.1.1.2, hence is irreducible.
It follows that
\widehat{X}
is irreducible. Since the irrelevant locus
has codimension at least two in
\mathbb{A}^{r}
, every irreducible
component of
V(f)
meets
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}
. Smoothness there also
excludes repeated factors. Thus

is irreducible,

\tilde{X}=V(f)
, and

Every ample homogeneous section vanishes on the irrelevant locus.
Its unique codimension-two component

is therefore a

\tilde{X}
. All other irrelevant components have ambient

10
]
*Theorem 1
and Theorem 2 using the language
of fans. Here we provide
a shorter argument via the Cox ring
description.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\mathbb{P}
be a smooth projective
toric variety of Picard rank two.
Then the grading matrix of the Cox ring

{\mathbb{K}}[T_{1},\dots,T_{n+2}]
of
\mathbb{P}

and the irrelevant ideal are

\begin{bmatrix}1&\cdots&1&0&-a_{1}&\cdots&-a_{k}\\
\makebox[0.0pt][l]{$\smash{\underbrace{\phantom{\begin{matrix}0&\cdots&0\end{matrix}}}_{\text{$n-k+1$}}}$}0&\cdots&0&1&1&\cdots&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad\langle T_{1},\dots,T_{n-k+1}\rangle\cap\langle T_{n-k+2},\dots,T_{n+2}\rangle\\

where
1\leq k\leq n-1
,
and
0\leq a_{1}\leq\dots\leq a_{k}
.
Moreover
\mathbb{P}
is Fano if and only if

\sum_{i}a_{i}<n-k+1
.

Proof.

Let
w_{i}=\deg(T_{i})
for any
i

and let
v_{i}

be the corresponding primitive
generator of the one dimensional
cone of the fan
\Sigma=\Sigma(\mathbb{P})
.
Let
p_{\sigma}\in\mathbb{P}

be the invariant point defined by
a maximal cone
\sigma\in\Sigma
.
Since
p_{\sigma}
is a smooth point of

\mathbb{P}
it is a factorial point, that is the
local class group
{\rm Cl}(\mathbb{P},p_{\sigma})
is
trivial (in a toric variety being smooth
and being factorial is the same).
By
[
3
]
*Proposition 3.3.1.5
we have

{\rm Cl}(\mathbb{P},p_{\sigma})\simeq{\rm Cl}(\mathbb{P})/\langle w_{i}\,:\,v_{i}\notin\sigma(1)\rangle.

The free abelian group in the above denominator
is generated by the two classes
w_{i},w_{j}

such that
{\mathbb{Q}}_{\geq 0}\cdot v_{i}
and

{\mathbb{Q}}_{\geq 0}\cdot v_{j}
are not one
dimensional rays of
\sigma
.
Thus the factoriality of
\mathbb{P}
at
p_{\sigma}

is equivalent to ask for the matrix
(w_{i},w_{j})

to have determinant
\pm 1
.

Since
\mathbb{P}
is smooth projective
its nef cone is full dimensional.
Let
w
be an ample class. Then
by
[
3
]
*Corollary 1.6.3.6
the irrelevant ideal is generated by
the monomials
T_{i}T_{j}
such that

w\in{\rm cone}(w_{i},w_{j})
. Equivalently
the complement
\{1,\dots,n+2\}\setminus\{i,j\}

of any such pair of indices
i,j
defines
a maximal cone of
\Sigma
. Thus
by the previous argument we have
that each such matrix
(w_{i},w_{j})

has determinant
\pm 1
.
In particular we can assume without loss of
generality that the nef cone of
\mathbb{P}

is the first quadrant.
If there are other rays different from
(1,0)
and

(0,1)
, by smoothness hypothesis they must
be of the form
(-a,1)
or
(1,-b)
, for
a,b>0
.
Since the variety is complete the effective cone
is pointed so that, if there exists at least
one ray
(-a,1)
, there can be no rays of the form

(1,-b)
. So up to exchanging the axes
we can assume that there are no
rays of the form
(1,-b)
and in particular
the ray
(1,0)
is an extremal ray of the
effective, moving and nef cone at the
same time. The situation is summarized
in the following picture.
w_{n-k+2}
w_{1},\dots,w_{n-k+1}
w_{n+2}

Since the
(1,0)
is an extremal ray of the
moving cone, by
[
3
]
*Proposition 3.3.2.3
we deduce that
n-k+1\geq 2
. For the same reason
we have
k\geq 1
.
Finally
\mathbb{P}
is Fano if and only if
the sum
\sum_{i}w_{i}
lies in the
interior of the nef cone, equivalently
if
\sum_{i}a_{i}<n-k+1
.
∎

Observe that a smooth projective toric variety

\mathbb{P}
has a component of the irrelevant
locus of codimension
2
when either
k=n-1
or

k=1
, with the same notation of
Proposition
4.1
.
The grading matrices for these two cases are
those which appear in the statement of
Corollary
3
.

Proof of Corollary
3
.

Let
f
be a defining polynomial for
X
.

We prove case
(1)
.
Since the degree of
f
is
[a,b]
, in Cox coordinates
we can write

f=\sum_{i=0}^{a}(-1)^{i}f_{i}T_{1}^{a-i}T_{2}^{i},

where
f_{0},\dots,f_{a}
are homogeneous of degree

[0,b]
. If we set
m:=\#\{i\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}:a_{i}=0\}
,
when we evaluate
f_{0},\dots,f_{a}
in
T_{1}=T_{2}=0
,
we get
a+1
general homogeneous polynomials
of degree
b
in the
m+1
variables

T_{3},\dots,T_{m+3}
. Therefore the
condition
a\leq m

guarantees that the codimension of
V(T_{1},T_{2},f_{0},\dots,f_{a})
is at least
a+3
, so that
f

satisfies the hypotheses of
Theorem
2
.

We prove
(2)
.
In this case we can write

f=\sum_{i=0}^{b}(-1)^{i}f_{i}T_{n+1}^{b-i}T_{n+2}^{i},

where
f_{i}
is homogeneous of degree

a+ia_{1}
in the variables
T_{1},\dots,T_{n}
, for
any
0\leq i\leq b
. Under the hypothesis

, these general
b+1

impose independent conditions, so that
the codimension of
V(T_{n+1},T_{n+2},f_{0},\dots,f_{b})

is
b+3
. Therefore also in this case
f

satisfies the hypotheses of
Theorem
2
.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

When all the twists in Corollary
3
vanish, the ambient
variety is
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
, after exchanging the
factors when necessary. Thus the Cox ring of a general hypersurface
of bidegree
[a,b]
, with
1\leq a\leq n-1
and

, has the

.
This agrees with
[
12
]
*Thm. 1.1.
For a very general hypersurface with

and

, the
effective cone is not closed and the Cox ring is not finitely

[

]
*Thm. 5.6.
The case

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
X=V(f)\subseteq{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{n-1}
, with
n\geq 4
, where
f
is a general
homogeneous polynomial of degree
[d,1]
, with
d\geq 0
. Let

a\in\{0,\dots,n-2\}
be the residue of
d
modulo
n-1
.
Then
X
is the scroll

S(\makebox[0.0pt][l]{$\smash{\underbrace{\phantom{\begin{matrix}0,\dots,0\end{matrix}}}_{\text{$n-a-1$}}}$}0,\dots,0,\makebox[0.0pt][l]{$\smash{\underbrace{\phantom{\begin{matrix}1,\dots,1\end{matrix}}}_{\text{$a$}}}$}1,\dots,1).\

In particular, if
(n-1)\mid d
then
X\simeq{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{n-2}
.

Proof.

Put
r=n-1
and write

f=\sum_{i=0}^{d}(-1)^{i}f_{i}s^{d-i}t^{i},

where the
f_{i}
are general linear forms in
y_{0},\dots,y_{r}
.
Set
A=\mathbb{K}[s,t,y_{0},\dots,y_{r}]/(f)
.
By Theorem
1
, we compute

\mathcal{R}(X)=A_{s}\cap A_{t}
using the fraction adjunctions
of Algorithm
1
. Normalization, whenever required,
does not change these localizations.

The successive fractions

z_{1}=-\frac{f_{0}}{t},\qquad z_{i+1}=-\frac{f_{i}+sz_{i}}{t}\quad(1\leq i<d)

belong to
A_{s}\cap A_{t}
: the equation
f=0
also expresses
them with powers of
s
as denominators. Thus they are
admissible new generators in the algorithm, all of degree

(-1,1)
.

If
1\leq d\leq r
, adjoining
z_{1},\dots,z_{d}
gives

f_{0}+tz_{1}=0,\qquad f_{i}+sz_{i}+tz_{i+1}=0\quad(1\leq i<d),\qquad f_{d}+sz_{d}=0.

Since
f_{0},\dots,f_{d}
are linearly independent, these
relations eliminate
d+1
of the
y
-variables.
The resulting ring is polynomial, with
r-d
generators
of degree
(0,1)
and
d
generators of degree
(-1,1)
,
besides
s,t
. Its localizations at
s
and
t
agree
with those of
A
, and
V(s,t)
has codimension two,
so the algorithm terminates with this ring.
For
d=0
, simply eliminate
f_{0}
.

Suppose now that
d>r
and put
m=d-r
.
After a linear change of the
y
-variables, assume

f_{i}=y_{i}
for
0\leq i\leq r
.
Adjoin
z_{1},\dots,z_{n}
and use

y_{0}=-tz_{1},\qquad y_{i}=-sz_{i}-tz_{i+1}\quad(1\leq i\leq r)

to eliminate all the
y
-variables.
Substitution in
f
gives
f=(-1)^{n}t^{n}g
, where

g=s^{m}z_{n}+\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}(-1)^{j}f_{n+j}(-tz_{1},-sz_{1}-tz_{2},\dots,-sz_{r}-tz_{n})s^{m-1-j}t^{j}.

Hence
B:=\mathbb{K}[s,t,z_{1},\dots,z_{n}]/(g)
satisfies

B_{s}=A_{s}
and
B_{t}=A_{t}
.
Indeed, the substitutions have inverses on both charts:
on
D(t)
use the recursive fractions; on
D(s)
,
the relation

s^{m}z_{n}=-\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}(-1)^{j}f_{n+j}(y)s^{m-1-j}t^{j}

determines
z_{n}
, and the preceding relations then
determine
z_{r},\dots,z_{1}
backwards.
They also identify the corresponding projective
hypersurfaces over
\mathbb{P}^{1}
.
In the basis where
\deg z_{i}=(0,1)
, the new equation
has bidegree
(m,1)
.

We verify that
g
is general up to a linear change
of its
z
-variables, so that this reduction can be iterated.
Let
N
be the
n\times n
matrix with ones immediately
below the diagonal and zeros elsewhere, and let

e_{n}^{\mathsf{T}}=(0,\dots,0,1)
.
The row of coefficients of
g
has the form

s^{m}e_{n}^{\mathsf{T}}-h(s,t)(tI+sN),

where
h
is an arbitrary row of
n
binary forms
of degree
m-1
.
Every row
G
of binary forms of degree
m
has a unique
division

G(s,t)=H(s,t)(tI+sN)+s^{m}R,

with
R
a constant row.
For general
G
, the last entry of
R
is nonzero.
There is then an invertible matrix
C
, polynomial
in
N
, such that
RC=e_{n}^{\mathsf{T}}
.
Since
C
commutes with
N
,

GC=s^{m}e_{n}^{\mathsf{T}}+(HC)(tI+sN).

Thus every general coefficient row is linearly equivalent
to one in the displayed family.
In particular, this family meets every nonempty open
condition invariant under linear changes of the fiber
coordinates. This proves the generality needed for induction.

Each reduction lowers
d
by
r
and replaces the fiber
generators by generators whose first degree is one less.
Writing
d=qr+a
, with
0\leq a<r
, iteration and the
base case therefore give

\mathcal{R}(X)=\mathbb{K}[s,t,u_{1},\dots,u_{r}],\qquad\deg u_{i}=\begin{cases}(-q,1),&1\leq i\leq r-a,\\
(-q-1,1),&r-a<i\leq r.\end{cases}

After the change of basis

(\alpha,\beta)\mapsto(\alpha+q\beta,\beta)
,
these degrees are
(0,1)
and
(-1,1)
, respectively.
Together with the two base charts above, this is
the Cox presentation of

S(\makebox[0.0pt][l]{$\smash{\underbrace{\phantom{\begin{matrix}0,\dots,0\end{matrix}}}_{\text{$r-a$}}}$}0,\dots,0,\makebox[0.0pt][l]{$\smash{\underbrace{\phantom{\begin{matrix}1,\dots,1\end{matrix}}}_{\text{$a$}}}$}1,\dots,1).

If
a=0
, it is
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{n-2}
.
∎
