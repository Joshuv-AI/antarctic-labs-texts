(Real)linear preservers of multiples of unitaries and matrix pairs with some extremal norm properties
Bojan Kuzma, Chi-Kwong Li, Edward Poon

Abstract
We determine the structure of linear maps on complex (real) square matrices sending unitary (orthogonal) matrices to multiples of unitary (orthogonal) matrices. The result is used to determine the linear preservers of matrix pairs satisfying the extremal norm properties ‖ A ​ B ‖ = ‖ A ‖ ​ ‖ B ‖ \|AB\|=\|A\|\|B\| , ‖ A ∗ ​ B ‖ = ‖ A ‖ ​ ‖ B ‖ \|A^{*}B\|=\|A\|\|B\| , or ‖ A ​ B ∗ ‖ = ‖ A ‖ ​ ‖ B ‖ \|AB^{*}\|=\|A\|\|B\| , for the spectral norm ∥ ⋅ ∥ \|\cdot\| .



AMS Classification. 15A60; 15A18; 15A86; 15A04.

Keywords. Linear preserver, unitary matrix, orthogonal matrix, norm multiplicative pairs.

Introduction

Let
M_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
be the set of
n\times n
matrices over
{\mathbb{F}}
, where
{\mathbb{F}}
is the complex field
{\mathbb{C}}
or the real field
{\mathbb{R}}
.
Denote by
{\mathcal{U}}_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
the set of
A\in M_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
such that
A^{*}A=I_{n}
. Here

A^{*}
is the conjugate transpose of
A
if
{\mathbb{F}}={\mathbb{C}}
and
A^{*}
is the transpose
A^{T}
of
A
if
{\mathbb{F}}={\mathbb{R}}
.
We will show that, when
n\geq 3
, a bijective linear map sending matrices in
{\mathcal{U}}_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
to multiples of matrices in

{\mathcal{U}}_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
has the form

A\mapsto rUAV\quad\hbox{ or }\quad A\mapsto rUA^{T}V

for some nonzero
r\in{\mathbb{F}}
and
U,V\in{\mathcal{U}}_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
.

Denote by
\|\cdot\|
the spectral norm on
M_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
defined by
\|A\|=\max\{\|Ax\|:x\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n},\|x\|=1\}
(recall that
\|A\|
is the maximal singular value of
A
).
Using the above result, we determine the structure of
bijective
linear maps
T\colon M_{n}({\mathbb{F}})\rightarrow M_{n}({\mathbb{F}})

preserving matrix pairs
satisfying some extremal norm properties. For example, when
n\geq 3

and
T\colon M_{n}({\mathbb{F}})\rightarrow M_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
is a bijective linear map, we show that

(a)

\|T(A)T(B)\|=\|A\|\|B\|
whenever
A,B\in M_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
satisfy

\|AB\|=\|A\|\|B\|
if and only if
T
has the form

A\mapsto rUAU^{*}

for some nonzero
r\in{\mathbb{F}}
and
U\in{\mathcal{U}}_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
;

(b)

T
has the form

A\mapsto rUAV

for some nonzero
r\in{\mathbb{F}}
and
U,V\in{\mathcal{U}}_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
if and only if any of the following holds.

(b.1)
\|T(A)^{*}T(B)\|=\|T(A)\|\|T(B)\|
whenever
\|A^{*}B\|=\|A\|\|B\|
,

(b.2)
\|T(A)T(B)^{*}\|=\|T(A)\|\|T(B)\|
whenever
\|AB^{*}\|=\|A\|\|B\|
.

The case when
n=2
is also treated in a separate section since it requires different arguments and the structure of the maps is more complicated.

People often consider real-linear maps on finite-dimensional complex normed spaces
because distance preserving maps

f
, i.e., maps satisfying
\|x-y\|=\|f(x)-f(y)\|
are always
invertible real-linear up to a translation (see, e.g.,
[
5
, p. 500]
and
[
11
]
for a very short proof).
In this paper, we will also determine real-linear maps on complex matrices sending multiples of unitary matrices to
multiples of unitary matrices, or preserving extremal norm properties mentioned above. As we shall see
in Section 3, some proofs can be simplified
if we impose the complex linearity assumption.

Note that related studies on maps that leave the group
{\mathcal{U}}_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
invariant (rather than mapping it into

{\mathbb{F}}\,{\mathcal{U}}_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
) were carried out in

[
10
,
13
]
, and a unified approach was given in
[
4
]
.
In particular,
[
10
,
13
]
did not assume bijectivity. In our study,
the bijectivity is imposed to rule out degenerate maps. For example,
for any linear functional
f
on
M_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
and any
Z\in{\mathcal{U}}_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
,
the singular map
T
defined by
T(A)=f(A)Z
will send any matrix
A\in{\mathcal{U}}_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
to a multiple of

Z\in{\mathcal{U}}_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
,
and satisfy

\|T(A)T(B)\|=\|T(A)\|\|T(B)||
,
\|T(A)^{*}T(B)\|=\|T(A)^{*}\|\|T(B)\|
, and
\|T(A)T(B)^{*}\|=\|T(A)\|\|T(B)\|

for all
A,B\in M_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
. It would be nice to give complete descriptions of the preservers in our study without the
bijective assumption.

In our discussion, we shall write
e_{j}
for the column vector (i.e., an
n
–by–
1
matrix)
whose only nonzero entry is a one in the
j
th entry, and set
E_{ij}=e_{i}e_{j}^{*}
.

2
Real-linear preservers on matrices of size at least 3

2.1
Real-linear preservers of multiples of unitary/orthogonal matrices

The main theorem of this section is the following.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
n\geq 3
and let
T\colon M_{n}({\mathbb{F}})\to M_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
be a real-linear map.
The following conditions are equivalent.

(i)

T
is bijective and maps matrices in
{\mathcal{U}}_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
to multiples of
matrices in
{\mathcal{U}}_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
.

(ii)

There are
U,V\in{\mathcal{U}}_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
and
r>0
such that
T
takes one of the following four forms

X\mapsto rUXV\quad\hbox{ or }\quad X\mapsto rUX^{T}V\quad\hbox{ or }\quad X\mapsto rU\overline{X}V\quad\hbox{ or }\quad X\mapsto rU\overline{X}^{T}V.

Moreover, if
T\colon M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})\to M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
is complex-linear then the last two forms do not exist.

The bulk of the proof is a reduction to rank-one preservers. We do it in a separate lemma. By
{\mathbb{H}}_{n}
we denote the real-linear space of hermitian matrices.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
n\geq 3
. A bijective real-linear map
T\colon M_{n}({\mathbb{F}})\to M_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
which maps
{\mathcal{U}}_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
to
{\mathbb{F}}\,{\mathcal{U}}_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
preserves the set of rank one matrices.

Proof.

Suppose
A
is rank one with norm one such that
T(A)=B
has rank
k
. We may replace

T
by
V_{1}T(U_{1}XU_{2})V_{2}
for suitable
U_{1},U_{2},V_{1},V_{2}\in{\mathcal{U}}_{n}({\mathbb{F}})

and assume that
T(E_{11})=D=D_{1}\oplus 0_{n-k}
, where

D_{1}={\rm diag}(d_{1},\dots,d_{k})

with
d_{1}\geq\dots\geq d_{k}>0
. Let
X=[0]\oplus X_{1}
with
X_{1}\in U_{n-1}({\mathbb{C}})
, and
Y=T(X)=\begin{pmatrix}Y_{11}&Y_{12}\cr Y_{21}&Y_{22}\cr\end{pmatrix}
with
Y_{11}\in M_{k}({\mathbb{C}})
.
Then
T(E_{11})+\mu T(X)=D+\mu Y
are multiples of unitary (orthogonal if
{\mathbb{F}}={\mathbb{R}}
) matrices for every
\mu\in\{-1,1\}
.
In particular,
(D+Y^{\ast})(D+Y)=\alpha_{1}I

and
(D-Y^{\ast})(D-Y)=\alpha_{2}I
. Notice that
(D\pm Y^{\ast})(D\pm Y)
are positive semidefinite, so
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\geq 0
.
By subtracting the two equations we get
DY+Y^{\ast}D=\beta I
where
\beta=(\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2})/2I\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Due to
D=D_{1}\oplus 0_{n-k}
we easily compute

DY+Y^{\ast}D=\begin{pmatrix}D_{1}Y_{11}+Y_{11}^{*}D_{1}&D_{1}Y_{12}\cr Y_{12}^{*}D_{1}&0_{n-k}\cr\end{pmatrix}=\beta I.

(2.1)

Note that we also have

(D+Y)(D+Y^{*})=\alpha_{1}I
and
(D-Y)(D-Y^{*})=\alpha_{2}I
, which gives

DY^{*}+YD=\begin{pmatrix}D_{1}Y_{11}^{*}+Y_{11}D_{1}&D_{1}Y_{21}^{*}\cr Y_{21}D_{1}&0_{n-k}\cr\end{pmatrix}=\beta I.

(2.2)

If
k=n
, then, by (
2.1
),
Y_{11}=Y
satisfies
DY+Y^{\ast}D=\beta I
, or equivalently,
(DY-\beta/2I)+(DY-\beta/2I)^{\ast}=0
. Since, in this case,
D
is invertible, we see that the set of all such
Y
equals
D^{-1}\bigl({\mathbb{A}}_{n}({\mathbb{F}})+{\mathbb{R}}I\bigr)
, where
{\mathbb{A}}_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
is the space of skew-hermitian (skew-symmetric if
{\mathbb{F}}={\mathbb{R}}
) matrices.
If
{\mathbb{F}}={\mathbb{C}}
we can find
2(n-1)^{2}
(respectively,
(n-1)^{2}
if
{\mathbb{F}}={\mathbb{R}}
) real-linearly independent matrices
X_{1}\in U_{n-1}({\mathbb{F}})

–so that
T([0]\oplus X_{1})=Y

satisfies
DY+Y^{*}D\in{\mathbb{R}}I
.
So,
T
maps the real-linear space

{\mathbf{V}}={\mathop{\mathrm{Span}}\nolimits}_{\mathbb{R}}\{[0]\oplus X:X\in U_{n-1}({\mathbb{F}})\}=0\oplus M_{n-1}({\mathbb{F}})

into a space of dimension bounded by
n^{2}+1
,
i.e.,
2(n-1)^{2}\leq n^{2}+1
(respectively, into a space of dimension bounded by
n(n-1)/2+1
,
i.e.,
(n-1)^{2}\leq n(n-1)/2+1
, if
{\mathbb{F}}={\mathbb{R}}
), which is impossible unless
n\leq 3
.

If
k<n
, then
\beta=0
, and (
2.1
)–(
2.2
) imply
D_{1}Y_{11}+Y_{11}^{*}D_{1}=D_{1}Y_{11}^{*}+Y_{11}D_{1}=0_{k}

and
Y_{12}=Y_{21}^{*}=0_{k,n-k}
.
Then
T
will map
{\mathbf{V}}
into a real-linear subspace
\tilde{\mathbf{V}}
consisting of matrices of the form

Y_{11}\oplus Y_{22}
with
D_{1}Y_{11}+Y_{11}^{*}D_{1}=0_{k}
.
Now,
\dim_{{\mathbb{R}}}\tilde{\mathbf{V}}=k^{2}+2(n-k)^{2}
(respectively,
\dim_{{\mathbb{R}}}\tilde{\mathbf{V}}=k(k-1)/2+(n-k)^{2}
if
{\mathbb{F}}={\mathbb{R}}
).
So, if
{\mathbb{F}}={\mathbb{C}}
we have
2(n-1)^{2}\leq k^{2}+2(n-k)^{2}

(respectively, if
{\mathbb{F}}={\mathbb{R}}
we have
(n-1)^{2}\leq k(k-1)/2+(n-k)^{2}
), which is impossible unless
k=1
.

As a result, except when
n=3
and
T(E_{11})=D
is invertible,
we see that
T
sends rank one matrices to rank one matrices.
If
n=3
and
D
is invertible and
{\mathbb{F}}={\mathbb{C}}
, we must add one more equation to reach a contradiction:

(YD-\beta/2I)+(YD-\beta/2I)^{\ast}=0\hbox{ or equivalently }Y\in\bigl({\mathbb{A}}_{3}({\mathbb{F}})+{\mathbb{R}}I\bigr)D^{-1},

(2.3)

which is obtained from (
2.2
). Following the above arguments we see that
T
maps a space
0\oplus M_{2}({\mathbb{C}})
whose real dimension is
8
into a space

D^{-1}\bigl({\mathbb{A}}_{3}({\mathbb{F}})+{\mathbb{R}}I\bigr)\cap\bigl({\mathbb{A}}_{3}({\mathbb{F}})+{\mathbb{R}}I\bigr)D^{-1}.

(2.4)

This intersection consists of matrices
X
with
X=D^{-1}(iH+\lambda I)=(iK+\mu I)D^{-1}
, or equivalently,

iK+\mu I=D^{-1}(iH+\lambda I)D

for some skew-hermitian
iH,iK
and real
\lambda,\mu
. Since skew-hermitian matrices have purely imaginary eigenvalues, and the spectrum does not change under conjugation by
D
, we see that
\lambda=\mu
, and hence
K=D^{-1}HD
. Since
K,H,D
are all hermitian, this forces
D^{-1}HD=(D^{-1}HD)^{\ast}=DHD^{-1}
, or equivalently,
H
commutes with
D^{2}
. Recall that
D
is diagonal and positive-definite with monotonically decreasing positive eigenvalues. If it is not a scalar matrix, then
D^{2}
has at least two distinct eigenvalues, in which case the real-linear subspace of such skew-hermitian
iH
is contained in
i{\mathbb{H}}_{2}\oplus i{\mathbb{R}}
(or in
i{\mathbb{R}}\oplus i{\mathbb{H}}_{2}
) with dimension
5
. In this case, the dimension of (
2.4
) is bounded above by
5+1=6
which contradicts the fact that it contains the image of
0\oplus M_{2}({\mathbb{C}})
under a real-linear bijection
T
.

The only possibility left to consider is that
D=\mu_{0}I\in{\mathbb{R}}I
. Recall that
D
was obtained by first modifying
T
into
X\mapsto V_{1}T(U_{1}XU_{2})V_{2}
and then evaluating at
X=E_{11}=U_{1}^{\ast}AU_{2}^{\ast}
. Hence, the arguments so far show that
T
maps rank-one either into rank-one or into a scalar multiple of some unitary matrix. Let
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
be a standard basis of
{\mathbb{F}}^{n}
.
Now, the first column
{\mathbb{C}}^{3}e_{1}^{\ast}
is a real-linear subspace of dimension six which, besides
0
, consists only of rank-one matrices. A real-linear bijection
T
maps it into a real-linear subspace of
M_{3}({\mathbb{C}})\subseteq M_{6}({\mathbb{R}})
. By Adam’s fundamental result on dimensions of spaces of invertible matrices (
[
1
]
,
[
2
]
), in
M_{6}({\mathbb{R}})
(hence also in
M_{3}({\mathbb{C}})
), the maximal possible dimension of a real-linear space which, besides
0
, consists of invertible matrices only, is
\rho(6)=2
, where
\rho
denotes the Radon-Hurwitz function (c.f. also
[
2
]
for a more general result valid for any size). Thus, there exists a nonzero vector
x\in{\mathbb{C}}^{3}
such that
T(xe_{1}^{\ast})=uv^{\ast}
is of rank-one. But then rank-one
E_{11}+\lambda xe_{1}^{\ast}=(e_{1}+\lambda x)e_{1}^{\ast}
(
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
) is mapped by (modified)
T
into

\mu_{0}I+\lambda uv^{\ast},

which is never of rank-one and which also fails to be a scalar multiple of a unitary for at least one real
\lambda
, yielding the desired contradiction. Indeed, if
{\mathbb{F}}={\mathbb{C}}
, then
T
leaves the set of rank-one matrices invariant.

If
n=3
and
D
is invertible and
{\mathbb{F}}={\mathbb{R}}

we again add (
2.3
).
Following the above arguments we see that
T
maps a
4
-dimensional space
0\oplus M_{2}({\mathbb{R}})
into a space

D^{-1}\bigl({\mathbb{A}}_{3}({\mathbb{R}})+{\mathbb{R}}I\bigr)\cap\bigl({\mathbb{A}}_{3}({\mathbb{R}})+{\mathbb{R}}I\bigr)D^{-1}
. Since

\dim D^{-1}\bigl({\mathbb{A}}_{3}({\mathbb{R}})+{\mathbb{R}}I\bigr)=4
and
T
is bijective we conclude that

D^{-1}\bigl({\mathbb{A}}_{3}({\mathbb{R}})+{\mathbb{R}}I\bigr)=\bigl({\mathbb{A}}_{3}({\mathbb{R}})+{\mathbb{R}}I\bigr)D^{-1}.

It follows that
D
must be a scalar matrix. To see this, note that for every

A\in{\mathbb{A}}_{3}({\mathbb{R}})
there is
B\in{\mathbb{A}}_{3}({\mathbb{R}})
and a scalar
\delta
such that

D^{-1}A=(B+\delta I)D^{-1}
. Multiplying by
D
and
taking the trace gives
\delta=0
, and hence
B=D^{-1}AD\in{\mathbb{A}}_{3}({\mathbb{R}})
. By arbitrariness of
A\in{\mathbb{A}}_{3}({\mathbb{R}})
and as
D
is positive definite, it must indeed be a scalar matrix.

In this case,
D=\gamma I
and

T(0\oplus X)=Y\in D^{-1}\bigl({\mathbb{A}}_{3}({\mathbb{R}})+{\mathbb{R}}I\bigr)={\mathbb{A}}_{3}({\mathbb{R}})+{\mathbb{R}}I
for every
orthogonal
X\in U_{2}({\mathbb{R}})
and, moreover,
(D+Y)(D+Y^{T})=\alpha_{1}I
reduces to
YY^{T}\in{\mathbb{R}}I
. Decompose

Y=\beta I+A
for some skew-symmetric
A
to see
YY^{T}=\beta^{2}I-A^{2}=\beta^{2}I+AA^{T}\in{\mathbb{R}}I
, and since

3
-by-
3
skew-symmetric matrices are never invertible we see that
A=0
. This implies the bijection
T
maps a
basis of
0\oplus M_{2}({\mathbb{R}})
composed of matrices
0\oplus X
with
X

orthogonal to
Y=T(0\oplus X)\in{\mathbb{R}}I
, a
contradiction.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.1
.

The sufficiency is clear. To prove the necessity, Lemma
2.2
implies that
T
sends rank one matrices to rank one matrices.
The bijective preservers of rank-one operators are known even if they are merely additive; on rank-one matrices they take one of the two forms
(a)
xy^{\ast}\mapsto(M_{\sigma}x)(N_{\sigma}y^{\ast})
or (b)
xy^{\ast}\mapsto(M_{\sigma}y)(N_{\sigma}x^{\ast})
for some
\sigma
-linear bijections
M_{\sigma}\colon M_{n\times 1}({\mathbb{C}})={\mathbb{C}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
and
N_{\sigma}:M_{1\times n}({\mathbb{C}})\to M_{1\times n}({\mathbb{C}})
, where
\sigma\colon{\mathbb{C}}\to{\mathbb{C}}
is a field isomorphism; see e.g., Theorem 2.1 of
[
8
]
. In our case,
T
being real-linear implies
\sigma
must fix every real number, and since it is a homomorphism of
{\mathbb{C}}
, it is either the identity or complex conjugation. Define a linear bijection
M\colon{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
on a standard basis by
Me_{i}:=M_{\sigma}e_{i}
and likewise for
N
; then the forms (a)–(b) for
T
read

(a)\ A\mapsto MA^{\sigma}N^{\ast}\quad\hbox{or}\quad(b)\ A\mapsto M(A^{\sigma})^{T}N^{\ast}

where
A^{\sigma}:=\bigl(\sigma(a_{ij})\bigr)_{ij}
is obtained from
A
by applying
\sigma
entrywise.

If
\sigma
is complex conjugation we replace
T
by the map
X\mapsto T(\overline{X})
; the new
T
will be linear, bijective, and will still leave the set
{\mathbb{C}}\,{\mathcal{U}}_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
invariant.

It remains to show
M,N
are multiples of unitaries. We may write

M=X\,{\rm diag}(r_{1},\dots,r_{n})Y^{*}
and
N=R\,{\rm diag}(s_{1},\dots,s_{n})S^{*}
for some

X,Y,R,S\in{\mathcal{U}}_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
,
r_{1}\geq\cdots\geq r_{n}>0
and

s_{1}\geq\cdots\geq s_{n}>0
. Let
A=YS^{*}
. Then
T(A)
or
T(A^{T})
are multiples of unitaries, from which we deduce that
r_{1}s_{1}=r_{n}s_{n}
. Thus
r_{1}=r_{n}
and
s_{1}=s_{n}
, so
M/r_{1},N/s_{1}\in{\mathcal{U}}_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
as desired.
∎

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
n\geq 3
. A real-linear bijection
T\colon M_{n}({\mathbb{F}})\to M_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
leaves the set
{\mathbb{F}}\,{\mathcal{U}}_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
invariant if and only if there exists
r>0
and a unitary
U
such that
X\mapsto rUT(X)
is an isomorphism or an anti-isomorphism of
{\mathcal{U}}_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
.

2.2
Matrix pairs with product attaining maximum norm value

For notational simplicity, we shall occasionally use

{\mathcal{U}}_{n}
to represent
{\mathcal{U}}_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
.
It is known that the spectral norm
\|\cdot\|
is submultiplicative, i.e.,

\|AB\|\leq\|A\|\|B\|
for any
A,B\in M_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
.
We have the following result characterizing linear maps preserving matrix pairs attaining the equality.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
n\geq 3
.
A bijective real-linear map
T\colon M_{n}({\mathbb{F}})\to M_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
satisfies

\|T(A)T(B)\|=\|T(A)\|\,\|T(B)\|\quad\text{whenever}\quad\|AB\|=\|A\|\,\|B\|

if and only if
T
has the form
A\mapsto\gamma UAU^{*}
or the form
A\mapsto\gamma U\overline{A}U^{*}
for some nonzero
\gamma\in{\mathbb{F}}
and
U\in{\mathcal{U}}_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
.

The sufficiency of the result is clear. We will prove the converse after the next two lemmas; the first one will be used also in the concluding subsections.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
A,B\in M_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
be nonzero.

(i)

\|AB\|=\|A\|\|B\|
if and only if there exists a unit vector
x
satisfying
B^{*}Bx=\|B\|^{2}x
and
A
attains its norm at
Bx
.

(ii)

\|AB^{*}\|=\|A\|\|B^{*}\|
if and only if there exists a unit vector
y
that is norm-attaining for both
A
and
B
.

Proof.

For the first assertion,
\|AB\|=\|A\|\|B\|
if and only if there exists a unit vector
x
such that
B
attains its norm at
x
and
A
attains its norm at
Bx
. But
B
attains its norm at
x
if and only if
B^{*}Bx=\|B\|^{2}x
.

For the second assertion,
\|AB^{*}\|=\|A\|\|B^{*}\|
if and only if there exists a unit vector
x
such that
A
attains its norm at
B^{*}x
and
B^{*}
attains its norm at
x
. But, as follows from its SVD,
B
attains its norm at
B^{*}x
.

Conversely, if
y
is norm-attaining for both
A
and
B
, then
B^{*}By=\|B\|^{2}y
. Thus
By
is norm-attaining for
B^{*}
, and its image under
B^{*}
is norm-attaining for
A
.
∎

Lemma 2.6
.

The following is equivalent for a matrix
A\in M_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
.

(i)

\|AB\|=\|A\|\cdot\|B\|
for every
B\in M_{n}({\mathbb{F}})

(ii)

\|AB^{\ast}\|=\|A\|\cdot\|B^{\ast}\|
for every
B\in M_{n}({\mathbb{F}})

(iii)

\gamma A\in{\mathcal{U}}_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
for some
\gamma\in{\mathbb{F}}
.

Proof.

Claims (i) and (ii) are clearly equivalent.

Suppose (iii) holds with
A=\mu U
,
U\in{\mathcal{U}}_{n}
and
\mu\in{\mathbb{F}}
. Then
\|AB\|=\|\mu UB\|=|\mu|\|UB\|=|\mu|\|B\|=\|A\|\|B\|

for any
B\in M_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
, as claimed by (i).

Conversely, suppose (i) holds but with a matrix
A
which is not a multiple of a matrix in
{\mathcal{U}}_{n}
.
By the singular value decomposition, we see that
A=P(\sum_{j=1}^{n}s_{j}E_{jj})Q
with
P,Q\in{\mathcal{U}}_{n}

and
s_{1}\geq\cdots\geq s_{n}\geq 0
such that
s_{1}>s_{n}
. Let

B=Q^{*}E_{nn}
. Then
\|AB\|=s_{n}<s_{1}=\|A\|\cdot\|B\|
, a contradiction.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.4
.

Suppose that
T
is bijective real-linear such that

\|T(A)T(B)\|=\|T(A)\|\|T(B)\|\quad\hbox{ whenever }\quad\|AB\|=\|A\|\|B\|.

If
A\in{\mathcal{U}}_{n}
, then
\|AB\|=\|A\|\|B\|
for all
B\in M_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
. It follows that

\|T(A)T(B)\|=\|T(A)\|\|T(B)\|
. So,
T(A)
is a multiple of a matrix in
{\mathcal{U}}_{n}
. By
Theorem
2.1
,

has the form

A\mapsto\gamma UAV\quad\hbox{ or }\quad A\mapsto\gamma UA^{T}V\quad\hbox{ or }\quad A\mapsto\gamma U\bar{A}V\quad\hbox{ or }\quad A\mapsto\gamma U\bar{A}^{T}V

for some
\gamma\neq 0
and
U,V\in{\mathcal{U}}_{n}
. We replace
T
by the map

X\mapsto VT(X)V^{*}/\gamma
(or, in the last two forms, by the map
X\mapsto VT(\bar{X})V^{*}/\gamma
; notice that
X\mapsto\bar{X}
preserves norm-multiplicative pairs) and assume that
T
has the form (a)
X\mapsto WX
, or (b)
X\mapsto WX^{T}
with
W=VU
.
Suppose
W
is not a scalar matrix. If (a) holds, then there is a unit vector

such that
|x^{\ast}Wx|<1

so that we have
\|AB\|=\|A\|\|B\|

for

, but

\|T(A)T(B)\|=\|Wxx^{*}Wxx^{*}\|=|x^{*}Wx|\|Wxx^{*}\|<\|Wxx^{*}\|\|Wxx^{*}\|.

Similarly, we can show that
W
is a scalar matrix if (b) holds. Thus,
T
has the form
X\mapsto\mu UAU^{*}

or
X\mapsto\mu UA^{T}U^{*}
. Again, we may replace
T
by
X\mapsto U^{*}T(X)U/\mu
and assume that

T(X)=X
for all
X
, or
T(X)=X^{T}
for all
X
. Note that the latter case
cannot hold because we can choose

and
B=E_{12}
so that
\|AB\|=\|A\|\|B\|

but
\|A^{T}B^{T}\|=0<\|A^{T}\|\|B^{T}\|
.
∎

Related results

Clearly

\|AB^{*}\|\leq\|A\|\|B^{*}\|=\|A\|\|B\|
.
Using the same ideas to prove Theorem

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
\|\cdot\|
be the spectral norm on

, and let

\|T(A)^{*}T(B)\|=\|T(A)\|\|T(B)\|
whenever

(ii)

\|T(A)T(B)^{*}\|=\|T(A)\|\|T(B)\|
whenever
\|AB^{*}\|=\|A\|\|B\|
.

(iii)

There are
r>0
, and

such that

or

Let us firstly directly show that (i) and (ii) are equivalent.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let

be a real-linear surjective map. Let
\Phi(X)=X^{*}
and let

. Then

\|A^{*}B\|=\|A^{*}\|\|B\|\implies\|S(A)^{*}S(B)\|=\|S(A)^{*}\|\|S(B)\|\quad\forall A,B\in M_{n}({\mathbb{F}})

Proof.

Notice that
\Phi
is conjugate-linear, so
T
is
real-linear. Now,
the first statement is equivalent to

\|A^{*}(B^{*})^{*}\|=\|A^{*}\|\|(B^{*})^{*}\|\implies\|T(A^{*})T(B^{*})^{*}\|=\|T(A^{*})\|\|T(B^{*})^{*}\|.

Apply it with
C=A^{*}
and

to get the second statement.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.7
.

By Lemma
2.8
it suffices to prove that (ii) implies (iii). By Lemma
2.6
such
T
will leave the set
{\mathbb{F}}\,{\mathcal{U}}_{n}
invariant. Hence,
T(I)\in{\mathbb{F}}\,{\mathcal{U}}_{n}
and by replacing
T
with

we achieve that it is unital. The rest of the arguments follow the proof of Theorem

2
-by-
2

In many preserver problems on matrices or operators,
the case of
2
-by-
2
matrices is special because there are many special subgroups
in the semigroup of linear maps on 2-by-2 matrices. This also happens to our problems.
For one, the real-linear bijective preservers of
{\mathbb{F}}\,{\mathcal{U}}_{2}
have a richer structure than with
{\mathbb{F}}\,{\mathcal{U}}_{n}
,
n\geq 3
, so the proofs will have to use different arguments. Besides, as we show in Theorem
3.4
below, the preservers of norm multiplicative pairs on real
2
-by-
2

n\geq 3
.

3.1
Preservers of multiples of unitary/orthogonal matrices

When
n=2
, we note that the eight matrices

I,\quad\Sigma_{1}=\left[\begin{matrix}0&i\\
i&0\end{matrix}\right],\quad\Sigma_{2}=\left[\begin{matrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{matrix}\right],\quad\Sigma_{3}=\left[\begin{matrix}i&0\\
0&-i\end{matrix}\right],\quad iI,\quad i\Sigma_{1},\quad i\Sigma_{2},\quad i\Sigma_{3}

(3.1)

are all unitary and form a real-linear basis for
M_{2}({\mathbb{C}})
. Also,
\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2},\Sigma_{3}
obey the same multiplication rules as the standard quaternionic units and thus

{\mathbf{V}}_{3}:={\mathop{\mathrm{Span}}\nolimits}_{{\mathbb{R}}}\{I,\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2},\Sigma_{3}\}

is (isometrically isomorphic to) a quaternion division algebra.
We have the following.

Theorem 3.1
.

A bijective real-linear map
T\colon M_{2}({\mathbb{F}})\rightarrow M_{2}({\mathbb{F}})

maps matrices in
{\mathcal{U}}_{2}({\mathbb{F}})
to multiples of matrices in
{\mathcal{U}}_{2}({\mathbb{F}})
if and only if
T(I)
is a nonzero multiple of a matrix in
{\mathcal{U}}_{2}({\mathbb{F}})
and the map
L
defined by
L(X)=T(I)^{-1}T(X)
satisfies one of the following.

(i)

{\mathbb{F}}={\mathbb{R}}
and
L({\mathbf{V}}_{j})={\mathbf{V}}_{j}
for
j=1,2
, where

{\mathbf{V}}_{1}={\mathop{\mathrm{Span}}\nolimits}\{I_{2},E_{12}-E_{21}\}
and
{\mathbf{V}}_{2}={\mathop{\mathrm{Span}}\nolimits}\{E_{11}-E_{22},E_{12}+E_{21}\}
.

(ii)

{\mathbb{F}}={\mathbb{C}}
and there are
U,V\in{\mathcal{U}}_{2}({\mathbb{C}})
and
\mu\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\mathbb{R}}
such that the unital map
L^{\prime}\colon X\mapsto VL(UXU^{\ast})V^{\ast}
satisfies
L^{\prime}(iI)=\mu I
and

L^{\prime}(\Sigma_{j})=a_{j}\Sigma_{j}+b_{j}I_{2}\quad\hbox{and}\quad L^{\prime}(i\Sigma_{j})=\mu L^{\prime}(\Sigma_{j})

for some
real
a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},b_{1},b_{2},b_{3}
with
a_{1}\geq a_{2}\geq|a_{3}|>0
. If
T
is complex-linear, then
\mu=i
.

Remark 3.2
.

When
n\geq 3
, a linear bijection sending
{\mathcal{U}}_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
to

{\mathbb{F}}{\mathcal{U}}_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
will map rank-1 matrices to rank-1 matricees. This is not the case when
n=2
.
The map
L
in Theorem
3.1

could send rank-1 matrices to rank-2 matrices. For example, consider a linear map
L
which fixes
I
and
E_{12}-E_{21}
, and swaps
E_{11}-E_{22}
with
2(E_{12}+E_{21})
. Then
L
leaves the sets
{\mathbf{V}}_{1},{\mathbf{V}}_{2}
, and
{\mathbf{V}}_{3}={\mathop{\mathrm{Span}}\nolimits}_{{\mathbb{R}}}\{I,\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2},\Sigma_{3}\}
invariant but maps
E_{11}
to the rank-2 matrix
\frac{1}{2}I+E_{12}+E_{21}
.

The proof will rely on the following result.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\Sigma_{j}
be as in (
3.1
). Suppose a real-linear and unital
L\colon M_{2}({\mathbb{C}})\to M_{2}({\mathbb{C}})
leaves
{\mathbf{V}}_{3}={\mathop{\mathrm{Span}}\nolimits}_{{\mathbb{R}}}\{I,\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2},\Sigma_{3}\}
invariant. Then there are
unitary
U,V\in M_{2}({\mathbb{C}})
such that
L^{\prime}\colon M_{2}({\mathbb{C}})\to M_{2}({\mathbb{C}})
, defined by

L^{\prime}(X)=VL(UXU^{*})\,V^{*}

satisfies

L^{\prime}(I)=I,\quad L^{\prime}(\Sigma_{1})=a_{1}\Sigma_{1}+b_{1}I,\quad L^{\prime}(\Sigma_{2})=a_{2}\Sigma_{2}+b_{2}I,\quad L^{\prime}(\Sigma_{3})=a_{3}\Sigma_{3}+b_{3}I,

where
a_{k},b_{k}
are real with
a_{1}\geq a_{2}\geq|a_{3}|
.

Proof.

Since
L(I)=I
and
L({\mathbf{V}}_{3})\subseteq{\mathbf{V}}_{3}
, if we use the orthonormal
basis of
{\mathbf{V}}_{3}
(with respect to standard inner product
\langle A,B\rangle:=\mathop{\rm tr}B^{\ast}A
)

{\bf B}=\left\{\frac{I}{\sqrt{2}},\ \frac{\Sigma_{1}}{\sqrt{2}},\ \frac{\Sigma_{2}}{\sqrt{2}},\ \frac{\Sigma_{3}}{\sqrt{2}}\right\},

then the matrix of transformation
L
with respect to the basis
\bf B
has the form

R=\begin{bmatrix}1&w\\
0&S\end{bmatrix}

where
w=(w_{1},w_{2},w_{3})
is a real vector and

is a real matrix.
Let

be such that

PSQ=\operatorname{diag}(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}),

where

a_{1}\geq a_{2}\geq|a_{3}|
are the singular values of
S
.
Let
\hat{P}=[1]\oplus P
and
\hat{Q}=[1]\oplus Q
. Then

\hat{P}\,R\,\hat{Q}=\begin{bmatrix}1&b\\
0&D\end{bmatrix}

with
b=wQ=(b_{1},b_{2},b_{3})
and

.
Here,
a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}=\det(S)
.

By the classical correspondence of Lie groups,
\mathrm{U}(2)/\mathrm{U}(1)\cong\mathrm{SO}(3)
, where
\mathrm{U}(1)
is considered as a normal subgroup of
\mathrm{U}(2)
consisting of all unimodular multiples of
I
. In fact, this correspondence takes
U\in\mathrm{U}(2)
into (an orthogonal) matrix representing the transformation
X\mapsto UXU^{\ast}
, restricted to an invariant subspace
{\mathop{\mathrm{Span}}\nolimits}_{{\mathbb{R}}}\{\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2},\Sigma_{3}\}\subseteq{\mathbf{V}}_{3}
and relative to basis
{\bf B}\setminus\{I/\sqrt{2}\}
; see, e.g.,
[
3
, Exercise B.4]
or
[
6
, p. 54]
;
elementary arguments were given in
[
9

(with correction
P_{W_{1}}=[1]\oplus\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\cos\varphi_{1}&-\sin\varphi_{1}\\
\sin\varphi_{1}&\cos\varphi_{1}\end{smallmatrix}\right]
).
Hence, we see that the
linear maps
L_{1}
and
L_{2}

with
\hat{Q}
and
\hat{P}
as the operator matrices with respect to
\bf B

correspond to the linear maps

L_{1}(A)=UAU^{*}\quad\text{and}\quad L_{2}(A)=VAV^{*}

for some
U,V\in\mathrm{U}(2)

∎

.

3.2
Matrix pairs with product attaining maximum norm value

The main result here is the following.

Theorem 3.4
.

A bijective real-linear map
T\colon M_{2}({\mathbb{F}})\rightarrow M_{2}({\mathbb{F}})
satisfies

\|T(A)T(B)\|=\|T(A)\|\|T(B)\|\qquad\hbox{ whenever }\qquad\|AB\|=\|A\|\|B\|

if and only if one of the following holds.

(a)

{\mathbb{F}}={\mathbb{R}}
,
there exist a nonzero

,

, and

for all

.
Here
\Phi_{c}\colon M_{2}({\mathbb{R}})\to M_{2}({\mathbb{R}})
is a linear bijection defined by

\Phi_{c}\left(\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
-b&a\end{bmatrix}\right)=\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
-b&a\end{bmatrix},\quad\Phi_{c}\left(\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
b&-a\end{bmatrix}\right)=c\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\

(b)

{\mathbb{F}}={\mathbb{C}}

,
\gamma>0

and
\mu\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\mathbb{R}}

such that the real-linear map

has the form

(X+iY)\mapsto X+\mu Y
for

. If

is complex linear, then

.

It is interesting to note that the linear preservers of norm multiplicative pairs on complex

We need some technical lemmas to prove this result.
We first show that preservers of norm-multiplicative pairs will send
{\mathbb{F}}I

.

Lemma 3.5
.

Suppose a bijective real-linear map
T\colon M_{2}({\mathbb{F}})\rightarrow M_{2}({\mathbb{F}})
satisfies

\|T(A)T(B)\|=\|T(A)\|\|T(B)\|\qquad\hbox{ whenever }\qquad\|AB\|=\|A\|\|B\|.

Proof.

Note that

T
preserves

which, by
[

and
[

is the set of normal matrices.
For

, let

We claim that
\mathop{\mathrm{Span}}\nolimits_{{\mathbb{R}}}P(A)=M_{2}({\mathbb{F}})
if and only if
A\in{\mathbb{F}}I
. In fact, if
A\in{\mathbb{F}}I
, then
P(A)
is the set of all normal matrices and its span is
M_{2}({\mathbb{F}})
(namely, hermitian and skew-hermitian matrices are normal). Conversely, if
A
is not a scalar matrix, then
N\in P(A)
if and only if
N\in{\mathcal{N}}

Now if, for some
