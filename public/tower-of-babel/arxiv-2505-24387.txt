Angela Pistoia, Giuseppe Mario Rago, Giusi Vaira

Abstract
. The paper addresses the existence of multi-bubble solutions for the well-known Brezis–Nirenberg problem. Although there is extensive literature on the subject, the existence of solutions that blow up at multiple points in a 4D bounded domain remains an open problem. The goal of the present paper is to resolve this longstanding issue. In particular, we exhibit examples of domains where a large number of multi-bubble solutions exist. Our result can also be seen as the counterpart of the asymptotic analysis carried out by König and Laurin in Ann. Inst. H. Poincaré C Anal. Non Linéaire, 2024 .

1.
Introduction and main results

Let us consider the well-known
Brezis-Nirenberg problem, i.e.

(1.1)

\begin{cases}-\Delta u=|u|^{2^{*}-2}u+\varepsilon u\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ &\mbox{in}\ \ \Omega,\\
u=0\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ &\mbox{on}\ \ \partial\Omega\\
\end{cases}

where
\Omega
is a bounded regular domain in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
with
N\geq 3
,
\varepsilon
is a positive parameter and
2^{*}:=\frac{2N}{N-2}
is the critical exponent for the Sobolev embedding.

Problem (
1.1
) has been introduced by Brezis and Nirenberg in their celebrated paper
[
8
]
.

A particular feature of (
1.1
), due to the critical behaviour of the nonlinearity, is the possible existence of solutions which blow-up at one or more points in the domain as the parameter
\varepsilon
approaches
0
in dimension
N\geq 4
or the parameter
\varepsilon
approaches a positive number
\lambda_{*}
in dimension
N=3
. The description
of the profile of the positive blowing-up solutions has been the subject of a wide
literature. Starting from the pioneering paper by Brezis and Peletier
[
9
]
where
the authors consider the radial case, a lot of results have been obtained concerning the asymptotic profiles of solutions to (
1.1
). Firstly, Han
[
15
]
and Rey
[
30
]
studied the profile of one-peak solutions in a
general domain. Most recently, a fine multi-bubbles analysis has been object of two papers by König and Laurin. Indeed, in
[
16
]
the authors give an accurate description of the
blow-up profile of a solution with multiple blow-up points when
N\geq 4
while in
[
17
]
they consider the case
N=3
. The 3-dimensional case was firstly faced by Druet
[
12
]
.

In particular, the asymptotic analysis of blowing-up solutions ensures that the
blow-up points are nothing but the critical points of a suitable function which
involves the Green’s function of
-\Delta
in
\Omega
with Dirichlet boundary
condition and the Robin’s function.
While the behavior of blowing-up positive solutions is by now clear, the profile
of the sign-changing solutions is far being completely understood. As far as we
know the complete scenario of sign-changing solutions has been obtained only in
the radial case by Esposito, Ghoussoub, Pistoia and Vaira in
[
13
]
in dimensions
N\geq 7

and by Amadori, Gladiali, Grossi, Pistoia and Vaira
[
1
]
in lower dimensions.

Another interesting field of research is to find a solution to (
1.1
) which blows up at one or more points whose profile is the one predicted by the asymptotic analysis.

The first result is due to Rey that in
[
30
]
builds a solution that blows-up at one non-degenerate critical point of the Robin function when
N\geq 5
. In
[
23
]
, Musso and Pistoia construct positive solutions with many blow-up points when
N\geq 5
while Musso and Salazar
[
24
]
consider the case of multiple blow-up points in dimension
N=3
. Sign-changing solutions have been built by Iacopetti and Vaira
[
18
,
19
]
, Liu, Vaira, Wei and Wu
[
21
]
, Morabito, Pistoia and Vaira in
[
22
]
, Pistoia and Vaira in
[
27
,
28
]
and Premoselli in
[
29
]
.

Although there is a huge literature on (
1.1
), many problems are still open.
Among others, one is concerning the existence of positive blowing-up solutions in dimension
N=4
.
Remarkably, differently from solutions with only one blow-up point (see for example Pistoia and Rocci
[
26
]
) the case with several blow-up points has not been studied
in the literature, yet. The goal of the present paper is to close
this gap.

Let us introduce the objects necessary to state our result.
Let
G(x,y)
be the Green function of the Laplace operator with Dirichlet boundary conditions and
H
be its regular part, i.e.

G(x,y):=\frac{1}{2\omega}\frac{1}{|x-y|^{2}}-H(x,y)

where
\omega:=2\pi^{2}
denotes the surface area of the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
and

H(x,y)
,
x,y\in\Omega
, satisfies

-\Delta H(x,y)=0\ \hbox{in}\ \Omega,\ H(x,y)=\frac{1}{|x-y|^{2}}\ \hbox{on}\ \partial\Omega.

For every
x\in\Omega
the leading term of the regular part of the Green’s function

\tau_{\Omega}(x)=H(x,x)

is called the
Robin function
of
\Omega
at the point
x
.

Next, given an integer
k\geq 1
we define

\Omega^{*}_{k}:=\left\{\boldsymbol{\xi}:=(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{k})\in\Omega^{k}\,\,:\,\,\xi_{i}\neq\xi_{j}\,\,\mbox{for}\,\,i\neq j\right\}.

and for any
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\Omega^{*}_{k}
we define the symmetric matrix
M(\boldsymbol{\xi})=\left(m_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\right)_{1\leq i,j\leq k}
whose components are given by

(1.2)

m_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\xi}):=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\tau_{\Omega}(\xi_{i})\quad\ \ \ \ &\mbox{if}\,\,i=j\\
&-G(\xi_{i},\xi_{j})\quad&\mbox{if}\,\,i\neq j.\\
\end{aligned}\right.

We denote by
\Lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{\xi})
the smallest eigenvalue of
M(\boldsymbol{\xi})
.
Using the Perron-Frobenius theorem it is easy to show (see
[
7
]
) that the eigenvalue
\Lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{\xi})
is simple and the corresponding eigenvector can be chosen to
have strictly positive components. We denote by
\boldsymbol{{\mathtt{e}}}(\boldsymbol{\xi}):=({\mathtt{e}}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\xi}),\ldots,{\mathtt{e}}_{4}(\boldsymbol{\xi}))^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
the unique vector such that

(1.3)

M(\boldsymbol{\xi})\boldsymbol{{\mathtt{e}}}(\boldsymbol{\xi})=\Lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\boldsymbol{{\mathtt{e}}}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\ \hbox{and}\ \boldsymbol{{\mathtt{e}}}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\xi})=1.

Finally, we remind the definition of
stable critical set
introduced by Y.Y. Li in
[
20
]
.

Definition 1.1
.

Given a smooth function
f:D\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R},
a set
\mathscr{K}
of critical point of
f
is stable if there exists a neighbourhood
\Theta
of
\mathscr{K}
such that the Brouwer degree
\mathtt{deg}(\nabla f,\Theta,0)\not=0,
where

\mathtt{deg}
denotes the Brouwer degree.

Examples of stable critical sets are listed below.

•

\mathscr{K}
is a strict local minimum (or maximum) set of
f,
i.e.
f(x)=f(y)
for any
x,y\in\mathscr{K}
and

f(x)<f(y)
(or
f(x)>f(y)
) for any
x\in\mathscr{K}
and
y\in\Theta\setminus\mathscr{K}

•

\mathscr{K}=\{x_{0}\}
is an isolated critical point of
f
with
\mathtt{deg}(\nabla f,B(x_{0},\rho),0)\not=0
for some small
\rho>0
(e.g.
x_{0}
is a non-degenerate critical point of
f
).

Our result is the following.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\mathscr{K}
be a stable critical set of
\Lambda_{1}
with
\Lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{\xi})>0
for any
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathscr{K}.
Then, there exists a family of solutions of

(1.4)

-\Delta u=u^{3}+\varepsilon u\ \hbox{in}\ \Omega,\ u>0\ \hbox{in}\ \Omega,\ u=0\ \hbox{on}\ \partial\Omega

which blows-up at the points
\xi_{1_{0}},\ldots,\xi_{k_{0}}
with rates of concentration
\delta_{1_{\varepsilon}},\ldots,\delta_{k_{\varepsilon}}
such that
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}:=(\xi_{1_{0}},\ldots,\xi_{k_{0}})\in\mathscr{K}
and

(1.5)

\varepsilon\log\delta_{i,\varepsilon}^{-1}\to\Lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}),\quad\mbox{as}\,\,\ \varepsilon\to 0.

The result is new and it can be seen as the counterpart of the asymptotical analysis performed by König and Laurin’s result in
[
16
]
. Indeed in
[
16
, Theorem 1.1]
the authors proved that if
u_{\varepsilon}
is a solution to (
1.4
) which blows-up at
k
points
\xi_{1_{0}},\ldots,\xi_{k_{0}}
, then
the rates of concentration
\delta_{1_{\varepsilon}},\ldots,\delta_{k_{\varepsilon}}
must satisfy (
1.5
) and
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}:=\left(\xi_{1_{0}},\ldots,\xi_{k_{0}}\right)\in\Omega_{k}^{*}

and
\boldsymbol{d}:=\left(d_{1},\dots,d_{k}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}
, with
d_{i}:=\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0}{\delta_{i_{\varepsilon}}\over\delta_{1_{\varepsilon}}}
, must be a critical point of the function

(1.6)

(\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{\xi})\longrightarrow\boldsymbol{d}M(\boldsymbol{\xi})\boldsymbol{d}^{T}-\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\Lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0})\boldsymbol{d}\boldsymbol{d}^{T}.

The proof of our result relies on a classical Ljapunov-Schmidt procedure, which is described in Section
2
. The exponential decay of the concentration rates (see (
1.5
))
makes it harder to derive the so-called reduced problem (
2.24
), which at a first glance does not have a variational structure.
In fact, the major difference with the higher dimensional case (see
[
23
]
) and the main novelty of this paper lie in the study of the reduced problem, which is carried out in Section
3
. In Section
4
we show a couple of examples for which multipeaks solutions do exist. The first example is the dumbbell-shaped domain constructed by joining
k
disjoint subdomains via thin necks. The second example is a thin annulus for which we prove the existence of solutions concentrating at two and four points. The restriction on the number of blowing-up points is merely due to technical reasons, indeed we strongly believe that the number of peaks grows as the thickness of the annulus decreases. (see (
4.41
)).
All the technical estimates needed in the reduction procedure are contained in Appendix
A
.

2.
The reduction procedure

2.1.
Preliminaries

Let
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
be the Hilbert space equipped with the usual inner product and the usual norm

\langle u,v\rangle:=\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v,\quad\|u\|:=\left(\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

For
r\in[1,+\infty)
the space
L^{r}(\Omega)
is equipped with the standard norm

\|u\|_{r}=\left(\int_{\Omega}|u|^{r}\right)^{\frac{1}{r}}.

As usual, let
i^{*}_{\Omega}:L^{\frac{4}{3}}(\Omega)\to H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
be the adjoint operator of the embedding
i_{\Omega}:H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\to L^{4}(\Omega)
, i.e.

u=i^{*}_{\Omega}(f)\ \hbox{if and only if}\ \langle u,f\rangle=\int_{\Omega}f(x)\varphi(x)\ dx\ \hbox{
for all}\ \varphi\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega).

The operator
i^{*}_{\Omega}:L^{\frac{4}{3}}(\Omega)\to H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
is continuous as

\|i^{*}_{\Omega}(f)\|_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}\leq S^{-1}\|f\|_{\frac{4}{3}}

where
S
is the best constant for Sobolev embedding.

Therefore, the problem (
1.4
) can be rewritten as

(2.7)

u=i^{*}_{\Omega}(f(u)+\varepsilon u),\ u\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega),

where
f(u)=(u^{+})^{3}.

Next, we introduce the well-known Aubin-Talenti bubbles

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{\delta,\xi}(x)=\alpha_{4}\left(\frac{\delta}{\delta^{2}+|x-\xi|^{2}}\right)=\delta^{-1}\mathcal{U}_{1,0}\left(\frac{x-\xi}{\delta}\right),\ \delta>0,\ x,\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{4},\

with
\alpha_{4}:=2\sqrt{2}
. We denote by
{\mathcal{U}}:=\mathcal{U}_{1,0}
.
They are all the positive solutions of (see
[
2
]
,
[
10
]
,
[
32
]
) the critical problem

(2.8)

-\Delta\,\mathcal{U}_{\delta,\xi}=\mathcal{U}_{\delta,\xi}^{3}\ \hbox{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{4}.

Given a point
\xi\in\Omega,
we denote by
P\mathcal{U}_{\delta,\xi}
the projection of
\mathcal{U}_{\delta,\xi}
into
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, i.e. the unique solution of

(2.9)

\displaystyle-\Delta\,P\mathcal{U}_{\delta,\xi}=\mathcal{U}_{\mu,\xi}^{3}\ \hbox{in}\ \Omega,\ P\mathcal{U}_{\delta,\xi}=0\ \hbox{on}\ \partial\Omega.

It is well known that the following expansion holds (see
[
30
]
)

(2.10)

P{\mathcal{U}_{\delta,\xi}}:={\mathcal{U}_{\delta,\xi}}-\mathfrak{C}\delta H(x,\xi)+\mathcal{O}\left(\delta^{3}\right)\quad\mbox{as}\,\delta\to 0

uniformly with respect to
x\in\Omega
and
\xi
in compact sets of
\Omega
and

(2.11)

P{\mathcal{U}_{\delta,\xi}}:=\mathfrak{C}\delta G(x,\xi)+\mathcal{O}\left(\delta^{3}\right)\quad\mbox{as}\,\delta\to 0

uniformly with respect to
x
in compact sets of
\Omega\setminus\{\xi\}
and
\xi
in compact sets of
\Omega
where
\mathfrak{C}:=2\alpha_{4}\omega=8\sqrt{2}\pi^{2}
.

2.2.
The ansatz

Let
k\geq 1
be a fixed integer. We look for a solution to (
2.7
) of the form

(2.12)

u_{\varepsilon}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}P{\mathcal{U}_{\delta_{i},\xi_{i}}}+\phi_{\varepsilon},

where the blow-up rates are
\delta_{i}=\delta_{i}(\varepsilon)
are choosen as

(2.13)

\delta_{1}:=e^{-\frac{8\pi^{2}\lambda}{\varepsilon}},\ \delta_{i}:=e^{-\frac{8\pi^{2}\lambda}{\varepsilon}}d_{i}\ \hbox{with}\ \lambda>0\ \hbox{and}\ d_{i}>0\ \hbox{for}\ i=2,\ldots,k

and the blow-up points are
\xi_{i}
belong to the set

D_{\rho}=\{\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\Omega^{*}_{k}\,\,:\,\,\mbox{dist}(\xi_{i},\partial\Omega)\geq 2\rho,|\xi_{i}-\xi_{j}|\geq 2\rho,\,\,\forall i,j=1,\cdots,k,i\neq j\}.

for some
\rho>0
small. In the following, we agree that
\boldsymbol{\delta}:=(\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{k})
and
\boldsymbol{\xi}:=(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{k}).

The higher order term
\phi_{\varepsilon}
belongs to the space

\mathcal{K}^{\perp}_{\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\xi}}=\left\{\phi\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\,\,:\,\,\ \langle\phi,\psi\rangle=0\ \forall\ \psi\in\mathcal{K}_{\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\xi}}\right\},

where

\mathcal{K}_{\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\xi}}=\mbox{span}\{P\psi_{\delta_{i},\xi_{i}}^{j}\,\,:\,\,i=1,\cdots,k,j=0,\cdots,4\}.

Here
P\psi_{\delta_{i},\xi_{i}}^{j}
are the orthogonal projections onto
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
of the functions

\psi_{\delta_{i},\xi_{i}}^{j}(x)=\frac{1}{\delta_{i}}\psi^{j}\left(\frac{x-\xi_{i}}{\delta_{i}}\right),\ j=0,\ldots,4,\ i=1,\ldots,k,

where

\psi^{0}(x):={\mathcal{U}}(x)+\nabla{\mathcal{U}}(x)\cdot x=\alpha_{4}\frac{|x|^{2}-1}{(1+|x|^{2})^{2}}

and

\psi^{j}(x):=\frac{\partial{\mathcal{U}}}{\partial x_{j}}(x)=-2\alpha_{4}\frac{x_{j}}{(1+|x|^{2})^{2}},\ j=1,\cdots,4.

generate the space of solutions of the linear equation

-\Delta\psi=3{\mathcal{U}}^{2}\psi\ \hbox{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{4}.

It is useful to recall the well-known expansions

(2.14)

P\psi^{0}_{\delta_{i},\xi_{i}}=\psi^{0}_{\delta_{i},\xi_{i}}-\mathfrak{C}\delta_{i}H(x,\xi_{i})+\mathcal{O}(\delta_{i}^{2})

and for all
j=1,\ldots,4

(2.15)

P\psi^{j}_{\delta_{i},\xi_{i}}=\psi^{j}_{\delta_{i},\xi_{i}}-\mathfrak{C}^{2}\delta_{i}^{2}\partial_{\xi_{j}}H(x,\xi_{i})+\mathcal{O}(\delta_{i}^{3}).

2.3.
An equivalent system

Let us introduce the linear projection
\Pi_{\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\xi}}:\mathcal{K}_{\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\xi}}\to\mathcal{K}_{\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\xi}}
and
\Pi^{\perp}_{\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\xi}}:\mathcal{K}^{\perp}_{\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\xi}}\to\mathcal{K}^{\perp}_{\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\xi}}
which are defined by

\Pi_{\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\xi}}(\phi)=\sum_{i=1,\cdots,k\atop j=0,\cdots,4}\langle\phi,P\psi_{i}^{j}\rangle P\psi_{i}^{j}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\Pi^{\perp}_{\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\xi}}(\phi)=\phi-\Pi_{\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\xi}}(\phi).

In oder to simplify the notations we let also
W_{\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\xi}}:=\sum_{i=1}^{k}P{\mathcal{U}_{\delta_{i},\xi_{i}}}
.

Since we look for a solution of the form
W_{\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\xi}}+\phi_{\varepsilon}
then
equation (
2.7
) can be rewritten as the following system of two equations

(2.16)

\Pi^{\perp}_{\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\xi}}[\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\xi}}(\phi_{\varepsilon})-\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\xi}}-\mathcal{N}_{\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\xi}}(\phi_{\varepsilon})]=0

and

(2.17)

\Pi_{\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\xi}}[\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\xi}}(\phi_{\varepsilon})-\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\xi}}-\mathcal{N}_{\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\xi}}(\phi_{\varepsilon})]=0,

where the linear operator
\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\xi}}
is

(2.18)

\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\xi}}(\phi_{\varepsilon})=\phi_{\varepsilon}-i^{*}_{\Omega}(3\phi_{\varepsilon}W_{\delta,\xi}^{2}+\varepsilon\phi_{\varepsilon}),

the error term
\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\xi}}
is

(2.19)

\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\xi}}=i^{*}_{\Omega}(W_{\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{3}+\varepsilon W_{\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\xi}})-W_{\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\xi}}

and the nonlinear term
\mathcal{N}_{\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\xi}}
is

(2.20)

\mathcal{N}_{\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\xi}}(\phi_{\varepsilon})=i^{*}_{\Omega}\left(f(W_{\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\xi}}+\phi)-f(W_{\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\xi}})-f^{\prime}(W_{\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\xi}})\phi\right).

2.4.
Solving equation (
2.16
)

The solvability of (
2.16
) in terms of
(\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\delta})
is the first step in the Ljapunov-Schmidt procedure and follows by the proposition below.

Proposition 2.1
.

For any
\rho>0
there exist
C>0
and
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that for any
\varepsilon,\delta_{i}\in(0,\varepsilon_{0})
and any
\xi\in D_{\rho}
there exists a unique
\phi=\phi_{\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in\mathcal{K}^{\perp}_{\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\xi}}
solving (
2.16
) and satisfying

(2.21)

\|\phi\|\leq C\left(|\boldsymbol{\delta}|^{2}+\varepsilon|\boldsymbol{\delta}|\right).

Proof.

The proof is standard and we refer to
[
23
,
26
]
for the details. In particular, estimate (
2.21
) follows by the estimate of the error term
\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\xi}}
whose proof is postponed in the Appendix.
∎

2.5.
Solving equation (
2.17
)

Let
u_{\varepsilon}=W_{\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\xi}}+\phi
where
\phi\in\mathcal{K}^{\bot}_{\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\xi}}
is the function founded in
Proposition
2.1
. Equation (
2.17
) rewrites as

(2.22)

-\Delta u_{\varepsilon}-f(u_{\varepsilon})-\varepsilon u_{\varepsilon}=\sum_{j=0,\ldots,4\atop h=1,\ldots,k}\mathfrak{c}^{i}_{h}f^{\prime}({\mathcal{U}_{\delta_{i},\xi_{i}}})\psi^{j}_{\delta_{h},\xi_{h}}

for some real numbers
\mathfrak{c}^{i}_{h}
that depend on
\boldsymbol{\delta}
and
\boldsymbol{\xi}
.
The second step in the Ljapunov-Schmidt procedure
consists in finding the rate parameters
\boldsymbol{\delta}
and the points
\boldsymbol{\xi}
so that all the
\mathfrak{c}^{j}_{h}
’s in (
2.22
) are zero and this is done by solving a reduced problem as stated in the Proposition below, whose proof is postponed in the Appendix.

Proposition 2.2
.

It holds true that (up to some constants)

\mathfrak{c}^{0}_{h}=\delta_{h}^{2}\tau_{\Omega}(\xi_{h})-\sum_{i\neq h}\delta_{i}\delta_{h}G(\xi_{i},\xi_{h})+\frac{1}{4\omega}\varepsilon\delta_{h}^{2}\ln\delta_{h}+o(|\boldsymbol{\delta}|^{2}),\ h=1,\dots,k

and

\mathfrak{c}^{i}_{h}=\frac{\partial}{\partial(\xi_{h})_{l}}\left[\delta_{h}^{2}\tau_{\Omega}(\xi_{h})-2\sum_{h\neq i}\delta_{i}\delta_{h}G(\xi_{i},\xi_{h})\right]+o(|\boldsymbol{\delta}|^{2}),\ i=1,\dots,4,\ h=1,\dots,k

as
\varepsilon\to 0
, uniformly with respect to
(\boldsymbol{\xi},\mathbf{d},\lambda)
(see (
2.13
)) in compact sets of
D_{\rho}\times(0,+\infty)^{k-1}\times(0,+\infty).

By Proposition
2.1
and Proposition
2.2
, it follows that the problem reduces to find
\delta_{i}>0
and
\xi_{i}\in\Omega
for
i=1,\ldots,k
such that

(2.23)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\left(\delta_{i}\tau_{\Omega}(\xi_{i})-\sum_{j\neq i}\delta_{j}G(\xi_{i},\xi_{j})+\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\varepsilon\delta_{i}\ln\delta_{i}\right)\left(1+o(1)\right)=0\,\quad&i=1,\ldots,k\\
\\
&\left(\delta_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial(\xi_{i})_{\ell}}\tau_{\Omega}(\xi_{i})-2\sum_{j\neq i}\delta_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial(\xi_{i})_{\ell}}G(\xi_{i},\xi_{j})\right)\left(1+o(1)\right)=0\quad&i=1,\ldots,k.\end{aligned}\right.

More precisely, taking into account the choice of the
\delta_{i}
’s in (
2.13
), we have to find
\lambda>0,

d_{2},\dots,d_{k}\in(0,+\infty)

and
(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{k})\in D_{\rho}
solution of the system

(2.24)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\tau_{\Omega}(\xi_{1})-\sum_{j=2}^{k}d_{j}G(\xi_{1},\xi_{j})-\lambda+o(1)=0\,\\
&d_{i}\tau_{\Omega}(\xi_{i})-G(\xi_{1},\xi_{i})-\sum_{j=2\atop j\neq i}^{k}d_{j}G(\xi_{i},\xi_{j})-\lambda d_{i}+o(1)=0,\ i=2,\ldots,k\\
&\frac{\partial}{\partial(\xi_{1})_{\ell}}\tau_{\Omega}(\xi_{1})-2\sum_{j=2}^{k}d_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial(\xi_{1})_{\ell}}G(\xi_{1},\xi_{j})+o(1)=0,\ \ell=1,\ldots,4\\
&d_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial(\xi_{i})_{\ell}}\tau_{\Omega}(\xi_{i})-2\frac{\partial}{\partial(\xi_{i})_{\ell}}G(\xi_{1},\xi_{i})-2\sum_{j=2\atop j\neq i}d_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial(\xi_{i})_{\ell}}G(\xi_{i},\xi_{j})+o(1)=0,\\
&\hskip 256.0748pti=2,\ldots,k,\ \ell=1,\ldots,4.\end{aligned}\right.

3.
The reduced problem

3.1.
Proof of Theorem
1.2
: completed

We aim to solve system (
2.24
). We set

\mathbf{d}:=(d_{2},\ldots\ldots,d_{k})
and we introduce three different functions.
The first one
F_{1}:D_{\rho}\times(0,+\infty)^{k-1}\times(0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}

is related to the first equation in (
2.24
):

F_{1}(\boldsymbol{\xi},\mathbf{d},\lambda)=\tau_{\Omega}(\xi_{1})-\sum_{j=2}^{k}d_{j}G(\xi_{1},\xi_{j})-\lambda.

The second one
F_{2}:D_{\rho}\times(0,+\infty)^{k-1}\times(0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^{k-1}

is related to the second
k-1
equations in (
2.24
):

F_{2}(\boldsymbol{\xi},\mathbf{d},\lambda)=\left(\overline{M}(\boldsymbol{\xi})-\lambda\mathtt{Id}\right)\mathbf{d}^{T}-\overline{G}(\boldsymbol{\xi})

where

\overline{M}(\boldsymbol{\xi}):=\left(\begin{matrix}\tau_{\Omega}(\xi_{2})&-G(\xi_{2},\xi_{3})&\ldots&-G(\xi_{2},\xi_{k})\\
-G(\xi_{2},\xi_{3})&\tau_{\Omega}(\xi_{3})&\ldots&-G(\xi_{3},\xi_{k})\\
\ldots&\ldots&\ldots&\ldots\\
\ldots&\ldots&\ldots&\ldots\\
G(\xi_{2},\xi_{k})&-G(\xi_{3},\xi_{k})&\ldots&\tau_{\Omega}(\xi_{k})\end{matrix}\right)\ \hbox{and}\ \overline{G}(\boldsymbol{\xi}):=\left(\begin{matrix}G(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\\
G(\xi_{1},\xi_{3})\\
\ldots\\
\ldots\\
G(\xi_{1},\xi_{k})\end{matrix}\right)

The third one
F_{3}:D_{\rho}\times(0,+\infty)^{k-1}\times(0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^{4k}

is related to the last
4k
equations in (
2.24
):

\displaystyle F_{3}(\boldsymbol{\xi},\mathbf{d},\lambda)=\left(\widetilde{M}^{1}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\mathbf{d}^{T},\widetilde{M}^{2}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\mathbf{d}^{T},\widetilde{M}^{3}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\mathbf{d}^{T},\widetilde{M}^{4}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\mathbf{d}^{T}\right)

where

(3.25)

\widetilde{M}^{\ell}(\boldsymbol{\xi})=\left(\widetilde{m}_{ij}^{\ell}(\xi)\right)_{1\leq i,j\leq k}\ \hbox{and}\ \widetilde{m}_{ij}^{\ell}(\xi):=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{\partial}{\partial(\xi_{i})_{\ell}}\tau_{\Omega}(\xi_{i})\quad\ \ \ &\mbox{if}\,\,i=j\\
&-2\frac{\partial}{\partial(\xi_{i})_{\ell}}G(\xi_{i},\xi_{j})\quad&\mbox{if}\,\,i\neq j.\\
\end{aligned}\right.

Finally we also define

F(\boldsymbol{\xi},\mathbf{d},\lambda):=(F_{1}(\boldsymbol{\xi},\mathbf{d},\lambda),F_{2}(\boldsymbol{\xi},\mathbf{d},\lambda),F_{3}(\boldsymbol{\xi},\mathbf{d},\lambda)).

It is clear that solving (
2.24
) is equivalent to finding
\boldsymbol{\xi}=\boldsymbol{\xi}(\varepsilon)\in D_{\rho}
,
\lambda=\lambda(\varepsilon)\in(0,+\infty)
and
\mathbf{d}=\mathbf{d}(\varepsilon)=(d_{2},\ldots,d_{k})\in(0,+\infty)^{k-1}
so that

(3.26)

F(\boldsymbol{\xi},\mathbf{d},\lambda)+o(1)=0,

where
o(1)
is a continuous function in
(\boldsymbol{\xi},\mathbf{d},\lambda)
which converges toward zero as
\varepsilon\to 0

uniformly in compact sets of
D_{\rho}\times(0,+\infty)^{k-1}\times(0,+\infty).

We shall use Lemma
3.1
to prove that if
\mathscr{K}
is a stable critical set of
\Lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{\xi})
in the sense of Definition (
1.1
) then there exists a set
\mathscr{Z}
such that

F(\boldsymbol{\xi},\mathbf{d},\lambda)=0
for any
(\boldsymbol{\xi},\mathbf{d},\lambda)\in\mathscr{Z}
and

\mathtt{deg}\left(F,\Xi,0\right)\not=0
for an open neighbourhood of
\mathscr{Z}
. By the properties of Brouwer degree, we immediately deduce the existence as
\varepsilon
is small enough of
(\boldsymbol{\xi}(\varepsilon),\mathbf{d}(\varepsilon),\lambda(\varepsilon))
close to
\mathscr{Z}

solution of (
3.26
).

First of all, we observe that for any
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in D_{\rho}
there exists a unique
\mathbf{d}=\mathbf{d}(\xi)\in(0,+\infty)^{k-1}
and

\lambda=\lambda(\xi)\in(0,+\infty)
such that

F_{1}(\boldsymbol{\xi},\mathbf{d}(\boldsymbol{\xi}),\lambda(\boldsymbol{\xi}))=0\ \hbox{and}\ F_{2}(\boldsymbol{\xi},\mathbf{d}(\boldsymbol{\xi}),\lambda(\boldsymbol{\xi}))=0.

Indeed, those equations are equivalent to the system

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\tau_{\Omega}(\xi_{1})-\sum_{j=2}^{k}d_{j}G(\xi_{1},\xi_{j})-\lambda=0\\
&\left(\overline{M}(\boldsymbol{\xi})-\lambda\mathtt{Id}\right)\mathbf{d}^{T}-\overline{G}(\boldsymbol{\xi})=0.\end{aligned}\right.

It is immediate to check that it has the solution

(3.27)

\lambda(\xi):=\Lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\quad\hbox{and}\quad\mathbf{d}(\xi)^{T}:=\left(\overline{M}(\boldsymbol{\xi})-\Lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\mathtt{Id}\right)^{-1}\overline{G}(\boldsymbol{\xi}).

Indeed by the first equation we deduce

\lambda=\tau_{\Omega}(\xi_{1})-\sum_{j=2}^{k}d_{j}G(\xi_{1},\xi_{j})

and combining the two equations we get

(3.28)

M(\boldsymbol{\xi})(1,\mathbf{d})^{T}=\lambda(1,\mathbf{d})^{T}.

Now if we multiply (
3.28
) by
{\mathtt{e}}(\boldsymbol{\xi})
given in (
1.3
) we get

\Lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\langle{\mathtt{e}}(\boldsymbol{\xi})^{T},(1,\mathbf{d})^{T}\rangle=\langle{\mathtt{e}}^{T}(\boldsymbol{\xi}),M(\boldsymbol{\xi})(1,\mathbf{d})^{T}\rangle=\lambda\langle{\mathtt{e}}(\boldsymbol{\xi})^{T},(1,\mathbf{d})^{T}\rangle,

which implies that

\lambda=\Lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{\xi}).
Moreover, since
\Lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{\xi})
is simple, it is not difficult to check that the matrix
\overline{M}(\boldsymbol{\xi})-\Lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\mathtt{Id}
is invertible, i.e.

(3.29)

{\rm det}\left(\overline{M}(\boldsymbol{\xi})-\Lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\mathtt{Id}\right)\neq 0.

Then it follows the existence of
\mathbf{d}
as in (
3.27
).

Let us prove (
3.29
).
Assume by contradiction that there exists
\bar{\mathtt{e}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k-1}
such that

\left(\overline{M}(\boldsymbol{\xi})-\Lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\mathtt{Id}\right)\bar{\mathtt{e}}=0.

We claim that
(0,\bar{\mathtt{e}}^{T})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
is an eigenvector of
M(\boldsymbol{\xi})
associated with the eigenvalue
\Lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{\xi})
which is not a multiple of
{\mathtt{e}}(\xi)
(see (
1.3
)) and a contradiction arises.
Indeed,

\left(M(\boldsymbol{\xi})-\Lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\mathtt{Id}\right)(0,\bar{\mathtt{e}})=(-\sum_{i=2}^{k}G(\xi_{1},\xi_{i})\bar{e}_{i-1},0),

the eigenvector
{\mathtt{e}}(\xi)=(1,\tilde{\mathtt{e}})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{k-1}
satisfies

\left(\overline{M}(\boldsymbol{\xi})-\Lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\mathtt{Id}\right)\tilde{\mathtt{e}}=-\left(G(\xi_{1},\xi_{2}),\dots,G(\xi_{1},\xi_{k})\right)^{T}

and so

-\sum_{i=2}^{k}G(\xi_{1},\xi_{i})\bar{e}_{i-1}=\bar{\mathtt{e}}^{T}\left(\overline{M}(\boldsymbol{\xi})-\Lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\mathtt{Id}\right)\tilde{\mathtt{e}}=\tilde{\mathtt{e}}^{T}\left(\overline{M}(\boldsymbol{\xi})-\Lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\mathtt{Id}\right)\bar{\mathtt{e}}=0.

Next we observe that

(3.30)

D_{\mathbf{d},\lambda}\left(F_{1}(\boldsymbol{\xi},\mathbf{d},\lambda),F_{2}(\boldsymbol{\xi},\mathbf{d},\lambda)\right)_{\mathbf{d}=\mathbf{d}(\xi),\lambda=\lambda(\xi)}\quad\hbox{is invertible.}

Indeed

D_{\mathbf{d},\lambda}\left(F_{1}(\boldsymbol{\xi},\mathbf{d},\lambda),F_{2}(\boldsymbol{\xi},\mathbf{d},\lambda)\right)_{\mathbf{d}=\mathbf{d}(\xi),\lambda=\lambda(\xi)}=\left(\begin{matrix}&-\overline{G}(\boldsymbol{\xi})^{T}&-1\\
&\overline{M}(\boldsymbol{\xi})-\Lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\mathtt{Id}&-\mathbf{d}(\boldsymbol{\xi})^{T}\\
\end{matrix}\right)

and by Schur complement formula

\displaystyle\mathtt{det}\ \left(\begin{matrix}&-\overline{G}(\boldsymbol{\xi})^{T}&-1\\
&\overline{M}(\boldsymbol{\xi})-\Lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\mathtt{Id}&-\mathbf{d}(\boldsymbol{\xi})^{T}\\
\end{matrix}\right)

\displaystyle=-\mathtt{det}\left(\overline{M}(\boldsymbol{\xi})-\Lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\mathtt{Id}\right)\left(1+\overline{G}(\boldsymbol{\xi})^{T}\left(\overline{M}(\boldsymbol{\xi})-\Lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\mathtt{Id}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{d}(\boldsymbol{\xi})^{T}\right)

\displaystyle=-\mathtt{det}\left(\overline{M}(\boldsymbol{\xi})-\Lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\mathtt{Id}\right)\left(1+\mathbf{d}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\mathbf{d}(\boldsymbol{\xi})^{T}\right)\not=0,

because (
3.29
) holds true and

\left(\overline{M}(\boldsymbol{\xi})-\Lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\mathtt{Id}\right)\mathbf{d}(\boldsymbol{\xi})^{T}=\overline{G}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\ \Rightarrow\ \overline{G}(\boldsymbol{\xi})^{T}=\mathbf{d}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\left(\overline{M}(\boldsymbol{\xi})-\Lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\mathtt{Id}\right)^{T}=\mathbf{d}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\left(\overline{M}(\boldsymbol{\xi})-\Lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\mathtt{Id}\right).

Finally, we claim that

(3.31)

\varphi(\boldsymbol{\xi}):=F_{3}(\boldsymbol{\xi},\mathbf{d}(\boldsymbol{\xi}),\Lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{\xi}))=\nabla\Lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{\xi}).

From (
3.28
) it follows that

\displaystyle\Lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{\xi})

\displaystyle:=\frac{(1,\mathbf{d})M(\boldsymbol{\xi})(1,\mathbf{d})^{T}}{\left(1+\sum_{j=2}^{k}d_{j}^{2}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tau_{\Omega}(\xi_{1})+\sum_{j=2}^{k}d_{j}^{2}\tau_{\Omega}(\xi_{j})-2\sum_{j=2}^{k}d_{j}G(\xi_{1},\xi_{j})-\sum_{j=2}^{k}\sum_{\ell\neq 1\atop\ell\neq j}d_{j}d_{\ell}G(\xi_{j},\xi_{\ell})}{\left(1+\sum_{j=2}^{k}d_{j}^{2}\right)}.

Now we compute
\nabla\Lambda_{1}
.

\displaystyle\left(1+\sum_{j=2}^{k}d_{j}^{2}\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial(\xi_{1})_{1}}\Lambda_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial(\xi_{1})_{1}}\tau_{\Omega}(\xi_{1})-2\sum_{j=2}^{k}d_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial(\xi_{1})_{1}}G(\xi_{1},\xi_{j})

\displaystyle+\left[\sum_{j=2}^{k}\left(2d_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial(\xi_{1})_{1}}d_{j}\tau_{\Omega}(\xi_{j})-2\frac{\partial}{\partial(\xi_{1})_{1}}d_{j}G(\xi_{1},\xi_{j})\right.\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left.\qquad-\sum_{\ell\neq 1\atop\ell\neq j}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial(\xi_{1})_{1}}d_{j}d_{\ell}G(\xi_{\ell},\xi_{j})+d_{j,}\frac{\partial}{\partial(\xi_{1})_{1}}d_{\ell}G(\xi_{\ell},\xi_{j})\right)\right)\right]

\displaystyle-\Lambda_{1}\sum_{j=2}^{k}2d_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial(\xi_{1})_{1}}d_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial(\xi_{1})_{1}}\tau_{\Omega}(\xi_{1})-2\sum_{j=2}^{k}d_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial(\xi_{1})_{1}}G(\xi_{1},\xi_{j})

\displaystyle+\left[\sum_{j=2}^{k}2\frac{\partial}{\partial(\xi_{1})_{1}}d_{j}\left(d_{j}\tau_{\Omega}(\xi_{j})-G(\xi_{1},\xi_{j})-\sum_{\ell\neq 1\atop\ell\neq j}d_{\ell}G(\xi_{\ell},\xi_{j})\right)\right]-\Lambda_{1}\sum_{j=2}^{k}2d_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial(\xi_{1})_{1}}d_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial(\xi_{1})_{1}}\tau_{\Omega}(\xi_{1})-2\sum_{j=2}^{k}d_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial(\xi_{1})_{1}}G(\xi_{1},\xi_{j})+\left[\sum_{j=2}^{k}2\frac{\partial}{\partial(\xi_{1})_{1}}d_{j}d_{j}\Lambda_{1}\right]-\Lambda_{1}\sum_{j=2}^{k}2d_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial(\xi_{1})_{1}}d_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial(\xi_{1})_{1}}\tau_{\Omega}(\xi_{1})-2\sum_{j=2}^{k}d_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial(\xi_{1})_{1}}G(\xi_{1},\xi_{j})=\left(\widetilde{M}^{1}(\xi)\mathbf{d}^{T}\right)_{1}

since for any
i

d_{i}\tau_{\Omega}(\xi_{i})-G(\xi_{1},\xi_{i})-\sum_{j\neq i\atop j\neq 1}d_{j}G(\xi_{i},\xi_{j})=\Lambda_{1}d_{i}

Similarly, a direct computations shows that

\left(1+\sum_{j=2}^{k}d_{j}^{2}\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial(\xi_{j})_{\ell}}\Lambda_{1}(M(\xi))=d_{j}\left(\widetilde{M}^{\ell}(\xi)\mathbf{d}^{T}\right)_{j}.

Hence (
3.31
) follows.

Now, if

\mathscr{K}
is a stable critical set of
\Lambda_{1},
then by Definition
1.1
and (
3.31
)
\mathtt{deg}(\varphi,\Theta,0)\not=0
for some neighbourhood
\Theta
of
\mathscr{K}.
Therefore, by
Lemma
3.1
the set
\mathscr{Z}:=\left\{(\boldsymbol{\xi},\mathbf{d}(\xi),\Lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{\xi}))\ :\ \boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathscr{K}\right\}
is such that

\mathtt{deg}(F,\Xi,0)\not=0,
for some neighbourhood
\Xi
of
\mathscr{Z}.
This completes the proof.

3.2.
A key lemma

Let
\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{B}
an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{h}
and define the the
C^{1}-
maps

\Phi_{1}:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{B}\to\mathbb{R}^{h},\quad\quad\Phi_{2}:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{B}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}.

Let, also, for every
(x,y)\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{B}

(3.32)

\Phi(x,y)=(\Phi_{1}(x,y),\Phi_{2}(x,y)).

The following result holds.

Lemma 3.1
.

Assume for any
x\in\mathcal{A}
there exists a unique
y:=y(x)\in\mathcal{B}
such that
\Phi_{1}(x,y(x))=0
and
D_{y}\Phi_{1}(x,y(x))
is invertible. Let
\varphi(x):=\Phi_{2}(x,y(x)).

If
\mathscr{K}\subset\mathcal{A}
is such that

\varphi(x_{0})=0\ \hbox{for any}\ x_{0}\in\mathscr{K}\ \hbox{and}\ \mathtt{deg}\left(\varphi,\Theta,0\right)\neq 0

for some open neighbourhood
\Theta
of
\mathscr{K}
, then the
set

\mathscr{Z}:=\{(x_{0},y_{0})\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{B}\ :\ x_{0}\in\mathscr{K},\ y_{0}=y(x_{0})\}

is such that

\Phi(x_{0},y_{0})=0\ \hbox{for any}\ (x_{0},y_{0})\in\mathscr{Z}\ \hbox{and}\ \mathtt{deg}\left(\Phi,\Xi,0\right)\neq 0,

for some open neighbourhood
\Xi
of
\mathscr{Z}.

Proof.

First of all, we observe that
\Phi(x_{0},y_{0})=0
for any
(x_{0},y_{0})\in\mathscr{Z}
, since
y_{0}=y(x_{0})
and

\Phi_{1}(x_{0},y_{0})=0\ \hbox{and}\ \Phi_{2}(x_{0},y_{0})=\varphi(x_{0})=0.

Next, we prove that
there exists
\rho_{0}>0
such that if
\rho\in(0,\rho_{0})
,
x\in\Theta
and
|y-y(x)|=\rho
then
\Phi_{1}(x,y)\not=0.

Assume by contradiction there exist sequences
\rho_{n}\to 0,

x_{n}\to x_{0}
and
y_{n}\in\mathcal{B}
such that

|y_{n}-\tilde{y}_{n}|=\rho_{n},
with
\tilde{y}_{n}=y(x_{n})\to\tilde{y}_{0}=y(x_{0}),
and
\Phi_{1}(x_{n},y_{n})=0.
Then by Taylor’s formula

\Phi_{1}(x_{n},y_{n})=\Phi_{1}(x_{n},\tilde{y}_{n})+D_{y}\Phi_{1}(x_{n},\tilde{y}_{n})(y_{n}-\tilde{y}_{n})+\mathcal{O}\left(|y_{n}-\tilde{y}_{n})|^{2}\right),

we deduce

D_{y}\Phi_{1}(x_{n},\tilde{y}_{n}){y_{n}-\tilde{y}_{n}\over\rho_{n}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\rho_{n}\right),

so passing to the limit, taking into account that (up to a subsequence)
{y_{n}-\tilde{y}_{n}\over\rho_{n}}\to z
with
|z|=1
, we get

D_{y}\Phi_{1}(x_{0},\tilde{y}_{0})z=0
and a contradiction arises since
D_{y}\Phi_{1}(x_{0},\tilde{y}_{0})
is invertible.
Now, we can define the admissible neighbourhood of
\mathscr{Z}
as

\Xi:=\{(x,y)\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{B}\ :\ x\in\Theta,\ |y-y(x)|<\rho\}

and it is immediate to check that

\Phi(x,y)\not=0
for any
(x,y)\in\partial\Xi.
Indeed if
|y-y(x)|=\rho
then
\Phi_{1}(x,y)\not=0
by the above remark. On the other hand if
x\in\partial\Theta
and
\Phi_{1}(x,y)=0
then
y=y(x)
and
\Phi_{2}(x,y)=\varphi(x)\not=0.

Finally, to derive
\mathtt{deg}\left(\Phi,\Xi,0\right)
we need to compute
D\Phi
. First, we point out

(3.33)

\displaystyle D_{x}\varphi

\displaystyle=D_{x}\Phi_{2}(x,y(x))+D_{y}\Phi_{2}(x,y(x))D_{x}y

\displaystyle=D_{x}\Phi_{2}(x,y(x))-D_{y}\Phi_{2}(x,y(x))\circ\left(D_{y}\Phi_{1}(x,y(x))\right)^{-1}\circ D_{x}\Phi_{1}(x,y(x)),

because by
\Phi_{1}(x,y(x))=0
and the invertibility
D_{y}\Phi_{1}(x,y(x))

D_{x}\Phi_{1}(x,y(x))+D_{y}\Phi_{1}(x,y(x))D_{x}y=0\ \Rightarrow\ D_{x}y=-\left(D_{y}\Phi_{1}(x,y(x))\right)^{-1}\circ D_{x}\Phi_{1}(x,y(x)).

Next, we have

D\Phi=\left(\begin{matrix}D_{x}\Phi_{1}&D_{y}\Phi_{1}\\
D_{x}\Phi_{2}&D_{y}\Phi_{2}\end{matrix}\right)

and by (
3.33
) and Schur complement formula

\displaystyle\mathtt{det}(D\Phi)

\displaystyle=\mathtt{det}(D_{y}\Phi_{1})\mathtt{det}\left(-D_{x}\Phi_{2}+D_{y}\Phi_{2}\circ\left(D_{y}\Phi_{1}\right)^{-1}\circ D_{x}\Phi_{1}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathtt{det}(D_{y}\Phi_{1})\mathtt{det}(D_{x}\varphi).

The claim follows by the definition of the Brouwer degree.
∎

4.
Examples

4.1.
The dumb-bell

Let us consider
k
smooth bounded domains
\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{k}
with
\overline{\omega}_{i}\cap\overline{\omega}_{j}=\emptyset
if
i\not=j.

Given
\rho>0
small enough we consider the domain
\Omega_{\rho}
obtained joining the
k
domains with thin channels of size
\rho.
Set
\Omega_{0}:=\omega_{1}\cup\dots\cup\omega_{k}.

Given a point
\boldsymbol{\xi}=(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{k})\in\Omega_{\rho}^{k}
we define the matrix

M_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\xi})=\left(\begin{matrix}&\tau_{\rho}(\xi_{1})&-G_{\rho}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})&\dots&-G_{\rho}(\xi_{1},\xi_{k})\\
&-G_{\rho}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})&\tau_{\rho}(\xi_{2})&\dots&-G_{\rho}(\xi_{2},\xi_{k})\\
&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
&-G_{\rho}(\xi_{1},\xi_{k})&-G_{\rho}(\xi_{2},\xi_{k})&\dots&\tau_{\rho}(\xi_{k})\\
\end{matrix}\right)

where
G_{\rho}
is the Green’s function and
\tau_{\rho}
is the Robin’s function associated with the domain
\Omega_{\rho}
. We agree that

G_{0}
is the Green’s function and
\tau_{0}
is the Robin’s function associated with the domain
\Omega_{0}
. In particular, we observe that

G_{0}(\xi_{i},\xi_{j})=0\ \hbox{if}\ \xi_{i}\in\omega_{i},\ \xi_{j}\in\omega_{j},\ i\not=j\ \hbox{and}\ \tau_{0}(\xi_{i})=\tau_{\omega_{i}}(\xi_{i})\ \hbox{if}\ \xi_{i}\in\omega_{i}.

We introduce the matrix

M_{0}(\boldsymbol{\xi}):=\left(\begin{matrix}&\tau_{\omega_{1}}(\xi_{1})&0&\dots&0\\
&0&\tau_{\omega_{2}}(\xi_{2})&\dots&0\\
&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
&0&0&\dots&\tau_{\omega_{k}}(\xi_{k})\\
\end{matrix}\right),\ \xi_{1}\in\omega_{1},\dots,\xi_{k}\in\omega_{k}.

Proposition 4.1
.

Assume that

(4.34)

\min\limits_{\omega_{1}}\tau_{\omega_{1}}<\min\left\{\min\limits_{\omega_{2}}\tau_{\omega_{2}},\dots,\min\limits_{\omega_{k}}\tau_{\omega_{k}}\right\}.

Then if
\rho
is small enough the first eigenvalue of the matrix
M_{\rho}
has a stable minimum set.

Proof.

First of all, we remind that (see
[
23
, Lemma 3.2]
)

\lim_{\rho\to 0}\tau_{\rho}(\xi)=\tau_{0}(\xi)\quad\hbox{$C^{1}$-uniformly on compact sets of $\Omega_{0}$}

and

\lim_{\rho\to 0}G_{\rho}(\xi,\eta)=G_{\omega_{i}}(\xi,\eta)\quad\hbox{$C^{1}-$ uniformly on compact sets of $\Omega_{0}\times\Omega_{0}\setminus\{\xi=\eta\},$}

which imply

(4.35)

\lim\limits_{\rho\to 0}M_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\xi})=M_{0}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\ \hbox{
$C^{1}-$ uniformly on compact sets of $\omega_{1}\times\omega_{k}$.}

We will prove that the first eigenvalue
\Lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{\xi})
of the matrix
M_{0}(\boldsymbol{\xi})
has a strict local minimum set in
\omega_{1}\times\dots\times\omega_{k}
. The claim will follow immediately by (
4.35
) and Definition
.

Now, we observe that by (
4.34
) the minimum value of
\Lambda_{1}
is

\min\limits_{\omega_{1}\times\dots\times\omega_{k}}\Lambda_{1}=\min\limits_{\omega_{1}}\tau_{\omega_{1}}

and
is achieved on the set

\mathscr{K}:=\left\{(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\dots,\xi_{k})\in\omega_{1}\times\dots\times\omega_{k}\ :\ \min\limits_{\omega_{1}}\tau_{\omega_{1}}=\tau_{\omega_{1}}(\xi_{1}),\ \xi_{i}\in\omega_{i},\ i=2,\dots,k\right\}.

Next,
we consider the open set

\Theta=\left\{\boldsymbol{\xi}=(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{k})\in\omega_{1}\times\dots\times\omega_{k}\ :\ \tau_{\omega_{1}}(\xi_{1})<\min\left\{\tau_{\omega_{2}}(\xi_{2}),\dots,\tau_{\omega_{k}}(\xi_{k})\right\}\right\}.

Firstly, we point out that
\Theta
is a neighbourhood of
\mathscr{K},
i.e.
\mathscr{K}\subset\Theta
. Indeed by

\min\limits_{\omega_{i}}\tau_{\omega_{i}}\leq\tau_{\omega_{i}}(\xi_{i})\ \hbox{for any}\ \xi_{i}\in\omega_{i}

we deduce

\min\left\{\min\limits_{\omega_{2}}\tau_{\omega_{2}},\dots,\min\limits_{\omega_{k}}\tau_{\omega_{k}}\right\}\leq\min\left\{\tau_{\omega_{2}}(\xi_{2}),\dots,\tau_{\omega_{k}}(\xi_{k})\right\}\ \hbox{for any}\ \xi_{i}\in\omega_{i}.

Therefore if
(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\dots,\xi_{k})\in\mathscr{K},
since
\tau_{\omega_{1}}(\xi_{1})=\min\limits_{\omega_{1}}\tau_{\omega_{1}}
, by (
4.34
)

\tau_{\omega_{1}}(\xi_{1})=\min\limits_{\omega_{1}}\tau_{\omega_{1}}<\min\left\{\min\limits_{\omega_{2}}\tau_{\omega_{2}},\dots,\min\limits_{\omega_{k}}\tau_{\omega_{k}}\right\}\leq\min\left\{\tau_{\omega_{2}}(\xi_{2}),\dots,\tau_{\omega_{k}}(\xi_{k})\right\},

that is
(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\dots,\xi_{k})\in D.

Next, we observe that if
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\partial\Theta
by (
4.34
)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{\xi})

\displaystyle=\min\limits_{i=1,\dots,k}\tau_{\omega_{i}}(\xi_{i})=\tau_{\omega_{1}}(\xi_{1})=\min\left\{\tau_{\omega_{2}}(\xi_{2}),\dots,\tau_{\omega_{k}}(\xi_{k})\right\}

\displaystyle\geq\min\left\{\min\limits_{\omega_{2}}\tau_{\omega_{2}},\dots,\min\limits_{\omega_{k}}\tau_{\omega_{k}}\right\}>\min\limits_{\omega_{1}}\tau_{\omega_{1}}.

On the other hand, it is clear that

\min\limits_{\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\Theta}\Lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{\xi})=\min\limits_{\omega_{1}}\tau_{\omega_{1}}=\min\limits_{\omega_{1}\times\dots\times\omega_{k}}\Lambda_{1}.

Therefore,
\mathscr{K}
is a strict local minimum set of
\Lambda_{1}
. That concludes the proof.
∎

4.2.
The thin annulus

Let us consider the annulus

\Omega_{\rho}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{4}:\rho<|x|<1\},\ 0<\rho<1.

Given an integer
k
, taking into account the symmetries of the annulus,
we can look for solutions which are even with respect to
x_{3}
and
x_{4}
and invariant with respect to a rotation of an angle
\frac{2\pi}{k}

in the
(x_{1},x_{2})-
plane (see
[
11
,
25
]
) and concentrate at points

\xi_{j}(r)=(re^{2\pi\mathtt{i}\frac{j-1}{k}},0)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\ \ \ \ j=0,\cdots k-1.

Define the matrix

\mathtt{M}(r)=M(\xi(r))

where
\xi(r)=(\xi_{1}(r),\cdots,\xi_{k}(r))
and denote by
\Lambda_{j}(r)
the eigenvalues of
\mathtt{M}(r)
being
\Lambda_{1}(r)
the smallest one. It is clear that we are lead to find stable critical points of the one-variable function
r\to\Lambda_{1}(r),

r\in(\rho,1).

We note that the matrix
\mathtt{M}(r)
is a circulant symmetric matrix (see
[
24
]
), i.e. each column is obtained from the previous one by a rotation in the components:

A=\left(\begin{matrix}a_{0}&a_{k-1}&a_{k-2}&\cdots&a_{2}&a_{1}\\
