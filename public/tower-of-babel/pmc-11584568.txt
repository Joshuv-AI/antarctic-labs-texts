Catalyzing progress: Unveiling the impact of metallic improved cooking stoves on energy security and sustainable development
Authors: Amir Budhathoki, Chomsri Choochuay, Shital Bhandary
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe global energy crisis, characterized by shortages of fossil fuels, increasing energy needs, and climate change, requires sustainable energy solutions. Renewable energy technologies provide important options to reduce GHG emission and improve energy security. In developing nations, the use of traditional biomass-fueled cooking stoves contributes to environmental damage, deforestation, and health problems. This research evaluates the impact of MICS on Energy Security and Sustainable Development in Bajura District, Nepal, where firewood is the main source of cooking and heating fuel. Employing an Explanatory Sequential Research Design, the study integrated quantitative survey followed by qualitative approach. A household survey was carried out using KoBo Toolbox among 507 households, followed by focus group discussion and in-depth interviews among 63 people. Survey data was analyzed in SPSS to see the impacts of MICS usage. Findings demonstrated significant reduction of firewood by 693.78 tons (33.85 %) and CO2 emissions by 1247.4 tons (60.93 %) per annum improving energy security, and positive socio-economic and environmental impacts. Research emphasizes the significance of MICS on Energy Security and SD, offering valuable insights for decision-makers. Despite the benefits of MICS, only 44 % of households had adopted them due to high costs, lack of awareness, and maintenance challenges as barriers, advocating for policies to increase adoption through subsidies, awareness campaign, and maintenance assistance as 94.5 % of the surveyed population still use firewood for cooking and heating.Keywords: Renewable energy technology, MICS, Energy security, Sustainable development, CO2 emission, Nepal

Introduction

The world is currently going through an unparalleled energy crisis, which is characterized by a shortage of fossil fuels, an increase in energy demand, and a pressing need to address climate change. With implications for markets, politics, and economies around the world, Europe has been most heavily affected. The current crisis has compelled a reconsideration of energy security regulations and a transition towards sustainable energy sources [1]. In order to achieve energy security, reduce greenhouse gas emissions, and promote sustainability, sustainable energy solutions are essential. These alternatives include energy-diverse and less reliant on fossil fuels renewable energy technologies including solar, wind, hydro, and geothermal [2]. Energy crisis has bolstered the emergence of adopting renewable energy technologies that are energy efficient (see Fig. 1)

Conceptual framework of the study.

Particularly in the underdeveloped nations, cooking stoves have major impact on the environment and energy consumption. Conventional stoves powered by biomass, which are frequently found in rural regions, cause deforestation, indoor air pollution, and negative health effects [3] Conventional cooking techniques that rely on solid biomass fuels like firewood have a major negative impact such as health problems, deforestation, indoor air pollution, and women and children in particular [4,5] Research conducted in areas such as Benus State, Nigeria, and India emphasized the widespread use of fuelwood for cooking, contributing to environmental deterioration. The reasons for this include; the scarcity of less expensive substitutes and the size of families. With 26 % of the population depending on it for cooking, firewood is a common source of greenhouse gas emissions and health hazards, especially women who use three stone fires [6] It is crucial to switch to cleaner cooking technologies in order to lessen the detrimental effects on human health and the environment. Improved Cookstoves have shown promise in lowering firewood usage, pollutants, and health risks [5]. To solve these problems, Improved Cook Stoves have been introduced, however many regions have not adopted them yet. Empirical studies demonstrate that MICS can enhance household well-being, minimize fuelwood, and yield better environmental results [3].

Metallic Improved Cooking Stoves (MICS), also known as Improved Cooking Stoves, are essential in addressing issues related to traditional cooking practices [7]. Studies have shown that MICS can significantly decrease the use of fuelwood, reduce emissions of pollutants such as CO2 and Particulate Matter (PM2.5) [8] and enhance health outcome by decreasing air pollution [6]. These stoves not only help lessen the strain on forests by lowering the need for fuelwood but also promote sustainable development through increased thermal efficiency and decreased pollutant emissions [9]. Embracing MICS enables communities to strike a balance between environmental preservation, better health, and sustainable resource management, thus playing a crucial role in tacking the obstacles associated with traditional cooking methods.

In Nepal, despite the coordinated efforts of government and non-governmental organizations to encourage the use of MICS, a considerable number of individuals still heavily depend on traditional cooking methods [10]. Study in western Uganda has given insights on eco-friendly sustainable cooking methods further highlighting its significance [11]. The Nepalese study stresses the significance of identifying the obstacles to accepting and maintaining the usage of health-enhancing technologies such as MICS similar to Ghana, Fiji, and Nigeria [12]. Moreover, the investigation showcases the effectiveness of utilizing change strategies based on the Integrated Behavioral Model for Water, Sanitation and Hygiene (IBM-WASH) in driving successful interventions that promote the adoption and continuous utilization of clean cooking solutions across various communities [13]. It is imperative to conduct more in-depth analysis and implement targeted policies to tackle the barriers impeding the widespread application of clean cooking practices in Nepal and comparable regions worldwide. Therefore, primary objective of this research is to assess the impact of MICS on energy security and sustainable development with specific goals; a. documenting the positive impacts of MICS on fuelwood consumption and it's socio-economic and environmental sustainability and b. Identifying barriers to the adoption of MICS and propose strategies to overcome those barriers.

Research conducted on the efficacy of MICS in a variety of geographical areas such as Sub-Sharan Africa, Peru, and India has indicated their efficacy in diminishing fuelwood consumption and reducing indoor air pollution [6,7,[14], [15], [16]]. Nevertheless, there are existing gaps in the comprehension of the specific obstacles hindering the adoption of MICS and their enduring effects on communities, emphasizing the necessity for further examination. Various studies have indicated the effectiveness of MICS in diminishing emissions, especially carbonaceous aerosols, and enhancing the air quality within households that utilize solid fuels for heating and cooking purposes. Nonetheless, there is a deficiency in comprehensive analysis regarding the barriers to adoption in high-altitude regions like Bajura, Nepal, where distinctive environmental and socio-economic elements might impact the acceptance and continuous utilization of MICS. This triggers the requirement for additional research to address these knowledge lacunae and guarantee the successful implementation of MICS in demanding setting.

In Southeastern Ethiopia, the propensity of households towards the Tikikil stove was shaped by various socio-economic factors and stove attributes such as cost, material, cooking time, and smoke emission reduction. Challenges like limited wood access, high expenses, fuel compatibility issues, and maintenance requirements have hindered the adoption of MICS. The success of the MICS is moderately satisfactory, pointing towards areas that can be enhanced. Socio-economic aspects including education levels, household size, and level of awareness influence the adoption of the Stoves [17]. While involving users and regional artisans in bottom-up approach has been a crucial factor in self-sustained use of MICS in Sub-Saharan regions [7].

The theoretical and conceptual framework begins with a global perspective, which involves comparing energy transitions and sustainable practices in Russia and Africa to reinforce the universal challenges and advantages of embracing energy-efficient technologies, such as MICS [18,19]. The independent variables are defined as energy security, based on the Theory of Energy Security [20], and sustainable development, grounded in the theory of Sustainable Development [21]. The framework suggests that the adoption of MICS will reduce reliance on traditional biomass fuels, thereby enhancing energy security and generating positive outcomes on socio-economic (such as cost reduction and time saving), environmental benefits (such as reduced greenhouse gas emissions, improved health outcomes, and forest conservation), ultimately contributing to overall environmental sustainability.

Additionally, the framework incorporates the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) to explain MICS adoption behavior, the Energy Security Theory to emphasize reliable sustainable energy sources, and Sustainable Development to harmonize social, economic, and environmental aspects [22,23]. This methodological approach underpinned by theoretical and conceptual framework will guide to systematically examine the influence of MICS on energy security and sustainable development, presenting insights into managing associated challenges and opportunities.

There are three types of ICS to replace traditional ones that rely on firewood for cooking and heating in Nepal. Since 2009, the Alternative Energy Promotion Center (APEC) has been implementing MICS within the framework of the Biomass Energy Support Program. This initiative aims to cater to the dual needs of cooking and space heating in the remote hills of Nepal. The target, demographic for MICS includes extremely isolated areas situated above 1500 m from the sea level [24].

The findings have practical implications for policymakers, practitioners and stakeholders working to promote clean energy and sustainable development. Likewise, academicians will be benefitted from the actual data on the impacts and adoption hurdles in high altitude regions, the study will add to the body of knowledge on MICS, sustainable development, and energy security.

Study area

The study area is the Bajara District, situated in the far western province of Nepal, encompassing an area of 2188 km2 (See Fig. 3). The district's administrative center is Martadi, and it comprises four Municipalities and five Rural Municipalities. Bajura is geographically located between 81o 10′20″ to 81o 48′ 27″ East and 29o 16′ 21″ to 29o 56′ 56″ North. The topography of Bajura is diverse, including the Himalayas, high and mid hills, with temperature ranging from 0o C to 40o C.

Location of Budhinanda (study area) Municipality.

The specific study was conducted in Budhinanda municipality, located in the southeast part of the district, at an altitude above 1500 m from the sea level. This area was selected due to the government's priority of distributing MICS with subsidy policy, the prevailing climate crisis [25], and remote location which is representative of similar context in the region and worldwide as a rural setting.

Budhinanda municipality comprises 3453 households with a total population of 20,745 where 80 % of the population rely on agriculture for their livelihood [26] This municipality exemplifies the broader context of rural Nepal, where development is hindered by difficult terrain and where promotion of sustainable technologies such as MICS is crucial for improving living standards and environmental conservation.

Methodology

This study employed Explanatory Sequential Research Design [27]. Within this methodological framework, an initial quantitative survey was conducted based on the research objectives that included data collection, compilation, and analysis. Subsequently, findings were subjected to triangulation process, incorporating insights from comprehensive literature review followed by qualitative process which included focused group discussion with MICS users and in-depth interviews with government stakeholders of Budhinanda Municipality.

Quantitative/household survey

The Quantitative/Household Survey was conducted among 507 households that included both MICS users and non-users. Quantitative data was collected through KoBo Tool box, a digital platform of data collection. KoBo Tool box provided a strong, efficient, and environmentally friendly alternative to traditional paper-based data collection methods, making it an ideal tool for initiatives aimed at lowering carbon emissions and increasing operational efficiency [29,30]. Questionnaires for quantitative data collection were set based on the set research objectives and technically checked by an expert. After the finalization questionnaires were set in KoBo (both in Nepali and English), training was provided to 5 enumerators from the local community to collect data on KoBo Toolbox. The questionnaires were piloted and further improvised as per the feedback from enumerators, then finally enumerators were deployed in the field.

Qualitative method

Focus Group Discussions and Key Informant Interviews were carried out further validating results from the quantitative survey and also exploring their observation and experiences as part of qualitative data. Six focus group discussions were conducted in various locations to gain in-depth understanding of issues related to MICS in relation with energy security and sustainable development, especially around socio-economic and environmental impacts they have observed or experienced. The groups were purposefully selected those who were in community-based forest user's group consisting of MICS users and traditional stoves users, so that both MICS users and non-users share their perception. Similarly, key informant's interviews were undertaken with Mayor and Chief Executive Officer of Budhinanda Municipality. These interviews provided valuable insights into the municipality's priorities, plans, past experience of MICS and other cooking stoves. This information was crucial in accessing how the municipality's initiatives align with the Biomass Energy Strategy, National Renewable Energy Framework, Renewable Energy Subsidy policy along with its practical gaps and challenges realized over the period in the context of remote location.

Data interpretation/analysis

Data collected through quantitative survey using KoBo tool underwent real-time monitoring and validation, ensuring the prompt correction and omissions. Subsequently, data with incomplete and inconsistent details were meticulously cleaned to enhance the data quality. The cleaned dataset was then exported to Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS).

To investigate variation in firewood usage before and after the MICS installation in the households, the Wilcoxon Signed Rank Test, non-parametric statistical test, was employed. This analysis aimed to identify statistically significant differences in firewood consumption pattern in the context of study area. It also included descriptive and scale test for demographic and perception-based responses respectively.

Moreover, these differences in firewood usage comprehensively explored in conjunction with energy security and the sustainable development pillars, encompassing social, economic and environmental dimensions. This exploration was facilitated through focus group discussion with participants and government stakeholders. Additionally, insights gleaned from a comprehensive literature review complemented the findings, enriching the contextual understanding of the relationship between firewood consumption, MICS adoption and the broader objectives of sustainable development.

The combined quantitative and qualitative approach, coupled with statistical analysis and contextual exploration, allowed for a holistic assessment of the impact of MICS on firewood use and its implications for energy security [31] and sustainable development [32] across multiple dimensions. Energy source for light

One of the essential energy requisites pertains to lighting. As depicted in Fig. 5, preeminent energy source of illumination is electricity. Among the cohort of 507 respondents, a notable majority, precisely 402 (79.3 %), have incorporated electricity as their primary means of illumination within their households. In stark contrast, a mere two respondents (0.4 %), have indicated reliance on kerosene for this purpose. In addition, an alternative energy source, namely solar power, has been adopted for lighting purposes by 20.3 % of the surveyed population. Remarkably, nearly the entirety of population, with the exception of a mere 0.4 %, now enjoys access to the clean energy option, namely electricity or solar power, to fulfill their lighting needs.

Respondents with main sources of lighting.

Despite the presence of electricity supply infrastructures, micro hydro and solar PV systems within the community, it is noteworthy that irregular power supply and low voltage levels have restricted its utility primarily to lighting applications. Discussion in a group revealed that a limited number of households have managed to install small-scale solar power units to cater emergency lighting requirements. However, it is pertinent to acknowledge that electrical equipment with high voltage demands remain incompatible with the prevailing electrical infrastructures.

Types of cooking fuel

Various cooking fuels are employed in rural areas based on economic factors and their availability, shedding light on the country's energy security dynamics. To investigate this aspect, a survey was conducted among respondents to ascertain the types of cooking fuels utilized in their households. The findings indicate that a substantial majority, specifically 479 respondents (94.5 %), rely on firewood for cooking purposes (Fig. 6). The ubiquity of firewood usage can be attributed to its indispensability, particularly for cooking animal food, which lacks viable alternatives. However, it is imperative to recognize that the utilization community forest resources for firewood, coupled with inefficient exploitation of biomass fuels, poses to a potential threat to sustainable energy management as emphasized during focus group discussion in Budhinanda Municipality by a female, 34 years, FGD participants.

Types of cooking fuel used by respondents during the survey.

In contrast, the adoption of Liquefied Petroleum Gas (LPG) and biogas as cooking fuels remains quite limited, as evidenced by the data presented in Fig. 6. Only 10 respondents opted for LPG, while 18 households utilize biogas for cooking purposes. The utilization of cleaner energy sources for household cooking is notably low. This discrepancy can be attributed, in part, to the challenging logistical aspects associated with LPG, including transportation costs, particularly in areas lacking accessible road infrastructure. The reliance on porters for LPG transport escalates expenses, contributing to the community's hesitance to prioritize this energy source.

In summary, the prevalent use of firewood for cooking, driven by its essential role in animal fodder preparation, highlights the significance of this traditional fuel source. However, the associated challenges and potential sustainability issues necessitate further exploration of cleaner energy alternatives, particularly in regions where transportation and logistics present substantial hurdles as highlighted by the mayor and executive officer of Budhinanda Municipality.

Sources of firewood

The responses concerning the sources of firewood underscore a pressing concern regarding the sustainability of the community forest, which represents the sole forest resources available to the community (Fig. 8). In the context of Nepal, as of 2020, there were a total of 22,266 community forest user groups [33], with 25 of them operating within the study area. The data on firewood sources alludes to the potential exploitation of the community forest, which has been conserved over an extended period. Alarmingly, nearly half of the respondents continue to employ open and traditional cooking stoves for their culinary needs, as illustrated in Fig. 7.

Sources of Firewood consumption of the households.

Prior research findings have demonstrated the efficacy of MICS in curbing firewood consumption. For instance, it has been established that MICS can reduce firewood consumption by as much as 58 % compared to traditional and open stove types [34] and by up to 45 % in Nepalese context [35]. Therefore, the overarching objective of this research is to scrutinize whether the community forest, despite the availability of alternative firewood-saving options, is being preserved or if its resources are being overexploited, thereby jeopardizing the community's energy security.

Fig. 8 provides a stark depiction of the community's heavy reliance on the community forest as the primary source of firewood, with a staggering 500 out of 507 respondents (virtually all households) attesting to this dependence. A minuscule fraction, approximately 1.4 % of respondents, derive their firewood from alternative sources such as agricultural land and residential areas.

This overwhelming reliance on the community forest for firewood presents an unequivocal threat to the forest's conservation. As population growth and household numbers continue to rise, perpetuating the use of open and traditional stoves for cooking, concerns about the sustainability and preservation of the forest's resources become increasingly salient. Insights gathered during FGD, 46 Male (CFUG member) posed the urgency of addressing this issue to ensure the forest's long-term conservation and the community's energy security.

Energy security

The MICS implemented in the study area have demonstrated a noteworthy reduction in firewood consumption. While it is generally claimed that M/ICS can reduce firewood consumption by 30–40 percent, this study's empirical finding illustrates a tangible reduction from 19.85 to 13.13 bhari (cart) per month. This calculation is based on the measurement provided by the community people, generally one cart (locally termed as ‘bhari’) of firewood is equivalent to 35 kg. This reduction signifies a substantial improvement in the efficient use of fuel resources. In comparison, different studies have shown varying results. For instance, study in rural Ethiopia reported that Mirt Injera, Improved Stove saved approximately 634 kg of firewood [36]. Similarly, study in Tigrey region of Ethiopia, reported that 35 % of firewood reduction from the use of ICS [37]. In contrast, research in India reported a decrease by 25 %, per capita, of firewood consumption [38]. Additionally, findings are associated with family size in the decreasing the consumption of firewood [39].

The concept of energy security, as outlined in the National Energy Security Strategy, 2018, revolves around the assurance of access to reliable and sustainable energy sources [40]. Similarly, the concept of energy security evolves with the changing circumstances, advancement of technologies, and growing awareness of long-term vision [41]. In this context, the study findings take on added significance. By significantly diminishing the consumption of firewood through the adoption of MICS, this study contributes to the enhancement of energy security within the community.

Specifically, the deployment of MICS has resulted in the preservation of 80.64 carts (2822.4 kg) of firewood per household per annum. MICS installation, in the study area, has reduced 693.78 tons of firewood annually. This conservation of firewood not only aligns with the principles of sustainable energy management but also bolsters the community's energy security by reducing its dependence on potentially dwindling and unsustainable fuel sources.

Perception of MICS impact on firewood consumption

The assessment of the deployed concept's effectiveness is contingent upon capturing the perspectives of the users on the ground. The success of MICS deployment hinges on the perceptions of the end users. Positive perceptions regarding the impact of MICS adoption in reducing wood consumption pave the way for sustainable development. This particular facet of the survey was conducted exclusively with MICS users.

Fig. 10, as depicted in the findings, portray a compelling picture. An overwhelming majority of respondents, precisely 96.4 %, strongly agreed with the statement that ‘MICS reduce firewood consumption’. Additionally, seven respondents expressed agreement with this assertion, while no respondents registered disagreement.

Agreement on whether MICS reduce firewood consumption.

The tangible impact of MICS adoption is further highlighted by a tangible shift in firewood consumption patterns. Before embracing MICS, each household, on average, consumed 35 kg of firewood in just two days. However, following the adoption of MICS, this same amount of firewood is now consumed over a span of seven days. This remarkable transformation underscores the role of MICS as a catalyst for enhanced energy security and their efficacy in curbing the exploitation of the community forest. As articulated during the FGD involving a female participant from a CFUG, the MICS has proven to be a valuable asset in conserving energy resources and contributing to the sustainable management of the community's ecosystem.

Sustainable development

This study has illuminated the multifaceted contribution of MICS to each of the sustainable development pillars. Firstly, the adoption of MICS has brought about a significant reduction in indoor air pollution, thereby creating a smoke-free environment within households. This transformation has had a profound impact, particularly on children who can now engage in reading and writing in an atmosphere that is free from smoke. A parallel study conducted in other districts of Nepal corroborates these findings, revealing that MICS reduced PM2.5 concentration in Indoor Air Pollution (IAP) by an impressive 63.2 % when compared to traditional cook stoves. Over the course of one year, the PM2.5 concentration decreased from 2.5mg/m3 to 0.76 mg/m3. Similarly, the concentration of CO2 was reduced to 5.62 ppm from 21.5 ppm within a year, representing an average reduction of 60 % [42]. On the other hand, WHO is setting criteria to aware people on the concentrations of PM2.5 and its fatal impact on health, therefore people become aware to use technologies that reduces indoor air pollution [43].

The benefits extended beyond improved indoor air quality. As a result of these positive changes, there has been an increase in school attendance among children, as they can now complete their tasks without fear in a healthier environment. Additionally, elderly individuals have reported a decrease in the incidence of airborne diseases such as coughing and asthma-related problems, as illustrated in Fig. 11.

Furthermore, the adoption of MICS has facilitated increased community engagement, with MICS users actively participating in group discussions during various meetings of forest user's group, mother's group, farmer's group, and saving groups. These interactions reflect the broader community based social capital and benefits stemming from MICS adoption, contributing to the social pillar of sustainable development [44].

Fig. 11 shows a survey response from a total of 220 respondents on the given statement, ‘Use of MICS has helped me to control eye-related diseases’, in scale from 1 to 5 (Strongly Disagree to Strongly Agree). While combining the “Agree” and “Strongly Agree” responses, 99 % of the respondents found MICS to be effective in controlling eye-related diseases while there were no responses on Strongly Disagree and Disagree from the MICS users. This indicates a strong consensus among the participants regarding the positive impacts of MICS on eye health. Similarly, there is 100 % score while combining “Agree” and “Strongly Agree” on the statement, ‘Use of MICS has helped me to manage time to participate in group discussion’. This reflects unanimous agreement on the time saving benefit of MICS. On the statement, ‘School going kids are happy to read and write in the absence of smoke inside’, have a combined score of 78 %. This depicts that the absence of smoke inside the house due to MICS usage positively impacts school going children's ability to study and engage in academic activities.

The implementation of MICS has brought about notable reduction in cooking time, as indicated by a survey of MICS users. A substantial 83.3 % of respondents reported that they are now able to prepare their morning meals in just 1 h, a significant improvement compared to the previous 2 h it used to take with traditional cooking methods. Time efficacy is one of the major benefits of MICS. The ability to cook meals more quickly allows individuals, particularly women, to utilize their time more efficiently. This time-saving aspect of MICS adoption can be redirected towards income generating activities or other productive endeavors as cited in the focus group discussion with MICS users. Saving in firewood consumption is another benefit of MICS. The study references a broader research effort conducted in India, Tanzania, Ethiopia, and Kenya, which found that MICS led to a substantial reduction in firewood consumption ranging from 18% to 56|% when compared to traditional three stone stoves [34]. This reduction in fuel consumption translates directly into time savings, as less time is required for collecting firewood and preparing food. Other benefit of MICS usage is income generating opportunities. The time saved due to reduced cooking time and firewood collection can be effectively utilized for income generation such as livestock rearing and agriculture related activities as articulated in the discussion and as shown in Fig. 12. This indicates an opportunity for households to engage in additional work or business, potentially increasing their overall income [45,46].

In summary, the adoption of MICS not only significantly reduces cooking time but also results in time savings related to firewood collection. These time efficiencies have the potential to enhance livelihoods by allowing for increased engagement in income generating activities.

The study's findings are remarkable, highlighting the substantial impact of MICS on firewood consumption and, by extension, carbon dioxide (CO2) emission reduction. Firstly, firewood saving is evidently captured in the study. Our study, survey, showed that each household is able to save 235.3 kg of firewood per month, 2822 kg per annum. Therefore, with this calculation, 223 households in the study area are saving 693.78 tons of firewood. This reduction in firewood consumption represents a significant resource conservation. Secondly, CO2 emission reduction is also explicitly evident through this study. It is estimated that 1 kg of firewood typically produces 1.8 kg, in average, of CO2 when burned in the context of Nepal depending on the types of firewood, moisture contents and how efficiently it burns [45]. Using this measure of 1.8 kg of CO2 per kilogram of firewood, the adoption of MICS has resulted reduction of 423.36 kg of CO2 emission per household per month and 5080.32 kg of CO2 per household per annum. In total, in study area, 223 households have contributed in reducing 1247.4 tons of CO2 emission in the environment through the use of MICS. Our study estimated that per capita CO2 emission decreased by 1.18 tons per year while the per capita use of firewood consumption is reduced by 0.64 tons annually. Whereas the study in India has indicated reduced per capita consumption of firewood by 0.61 kg/day conserving 1171 tons of firewood consumption annually, thereby decreasing 1953 tons of CO2 emission per annum (Kulkarni et al., 2022). Thirdly, environmental impact is contributed by reduction in CO2 emission. By reducing CO2 emissions, the adoption of MICS helps mitigate climate change and contributes to a healthier environment for all. Lastly, forest conservation is preeminently observed due to lower firewood consumption. Preserving forest is vital for biodiversity, ecosystem services, and maintaining a healthy environment. MICS adoption has supported forest conservation by reducing the demands for firewood and contributing to combat global environmental challenges by reducing the CO2 emissions.

This contribution not only supports local forest conservation but also aligns with global efforts to address climate and environmental sustainability.

Barriers to the adoption of metallic improved cooking stoves

The findings of this study have unveiled a critical insight: over half the respondents, representing more than 50 % of the surveyed population, have not adopted MICS in the study area. In response to this noteworthy observation, the feasibility variables have been thoroughly examined to investigate the root causes underlying the non-utilization of MICS within the community.

Table 2 provides insights into the barriers to the adoption of MICS among the study participants. Majority of respondents accounting 181 out of 256 (70.7 %) respondents who do not adopt MICS perceived that the cost of MICS as prohibitively high, which serves as a significant barrier to adoption. In comparison, study conducted in Sub-Saharan Africa, have reported similar findings where cost is the primary obstacle. However, Kenya in the Sub-Saharan Africa indicated cultural factor as one of the barriers in adopting improved cookstove [7]. 23 in 256 (9 %) respondents cite lack of information or awareness as a factor that hinders their adoption of MICS which suggests that communities lack to the access of the information as evidently survey depicts that almost 1/3rd of the survey participants are illiterate, meaning they can't read and write which restricts them from reliable information about the MICS. This corroborates to the similar study findings in Ethiopia, lack of information was one of the obstacles in Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) in adopting of improved cooking stoves [17]. In contrast, 16 out of 256 (6.9 %) indicated a lack of interest in adopting MICS as a reason for their non-installation which reflects a disinterest in transitioning to this cooking technology. Similar to reluctancy and socio-cultural barriers as reported in the Sub-Saharan Africa. Another 23 out of 256 (9 %) respondents expressed concern about the difficulty of maintaining MICS which they perceive as a barrier. During our discussion, it was revealed that users have to travel long way spending significant time and amount to find parts such as the stove's chimney and repairing parts when broken as its not available in the nearby market in the study area. Repair and Maintenance Service Centers are not yet established in the nearer location that could potentially contribute to accept the wider use of the technology. Lastly, 13 in 256 (5.07 %) respondents pointed to the infrequency or absence of subsidies as a factor influencing their decision not to adopt MICS.

Conclusion and policy recommendation

The research emphasizes the critical importance of shifting towards sustainable energy options, especially in elevated area such as the study area. Here, conventional biomass-fueled stoves play a major role in causing deforestation, indoor air pollution, and negative health outcomes. The results indicate that despite the significant benefits of MICS in reducing firewood usage, thereby improving energy security and contributing to sustainable development, there exist notable obstacles hindering their widespread acceptance of the technology.

The research uncovers various significant findings. In terms of demographics, the majority of participants fall within the 20–60 age bracket, with a higher proportion of females, many of whom are involved in agricultural activities. Educational attainment levels are generally low, with a considerable segment of the populace either lacking literacy skills or possessing basic reading and writing abilities only. Concerning energy consumption, most households depend on electricity for lighting, but frequently encounter challenges related to power supply. Firewood remains the primary source of cooking fuel, leading to a heavy reliance on community forests. Despite the acknowledged advantages of MICS, only 44 % of households have adopted them, mainly due to costliness, lack of awareness, and maintenance issues. Nonetheless, households utilizing MICS witness significant reduction in firewood usage and indoor air pollution, which in turn leads to better health outcomes and increased school attendance among youngsters, cost and time saving incurred in the firewood collection, increased community capitals. The MICS adoption has also contributed, significantly, decreasing CO2 emissions and forest preservation in alignment with global sustainable development objectives.

To overcome the obstacles seen in this study and encourage the utilization of MICS and similar clean technologies, the subsequent policy suggestions are put forward: Enhance financial assistance for MICS to encompass a larger portion of the overall expenses, specifically targeting the additional costs faced in remote such as Bajura, and implement financing programs to enhance affordability for low-income households. Execute focused awareness to educate communities on the advantage of MICS and the significance of sustainable energy practices, as well provide instruction on the operation, upkeep, and repair MICS to ensure their durability and continued utilization in the spirit of Technology Acceptance Model. Establish local supply chains and maintenance services for MICS to diminish the travel and financial burden on users, and invest in infrastructures enhancements to enhance the dependability and voltage of electricity supply, facilitating its usage beyond just lighting. Involve local governing bodies and community organizations based on the regional contexts, as it has unique cultures, in the promotion and disseminations of MICS to bolster community acceptance and involvement, and create incentive schemes for communities that achieve substantial reduction in firewood consumption and enhancements in energy security and contributing in the dimensions of sustainable development. Establish an effective monitoring mechanism from the local governing bodies along with policy facilitation in the communities.
