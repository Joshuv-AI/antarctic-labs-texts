Building a bridge over troubled waters during salvage esophageal reconstruction
Authors: Ian G. Christie, Evan T. Alicuben, Ryan M. Levy
Published: 2026

Salvage esophageal reconstructions represent the some of the most challenging and daunting operations encountered by thoracic surgeons. Creating these complex bridges requires navigation of turbulent waters of perioperative morbidity and mortality. Surgeons must construct a durable conduit that restores a functional alimentary tract while sparing the patient undue morbidity, often via alternative anatomic routes. Barron and colleagues report an impressive 25-year experience with salvage esophageal reconstruction using colonic interposition, in which microvascular augmentation was associated with a marked reduction in anastomotic leak (1). By enhancing perfusion to the distal portion of the conduit, the authors show dramatic reductions in anastomotic and ischemia-related complications.

Over a 25-year period, 99 patients underwent esophageal reconstruction with an isoperistaltic segment of transverse colon. Among the 83 patients who underwent no microvascular supercharge, 36 (43%) experienced esophagocolic anastomotic leak and 10 patients (12%) experienced conduit necrosis, requiring takedown and resection of the colonic conduit in four patients. Thirty-nine of these patients (47%) required reoperation, and median length of stay was 19 days. In contrast, among the 16 patients who underwent microvascular supercharge, there was only one esophagocolic leak (6%), and no cases of conduit necrosis. Reoperation was required in four patients (25%) and the median length of stay was 13 days.

The authors highlight technical pearls providing a compass to guide surgeons through the complex navigation of substernal reconstruction. These include resection of the left sternoclavicular joint to avoid obstruction at the thoracic inlet, and preservation of the pleura so as to prevent pleural soilage in cases of leak. They recommend precise positioning of the colonic conduit to avoid mesenteric twisting, and careful alignment of the middle colic pedicle with the internal mammary vessels to facilitate vascular anastomosis. Resection of the right colon is suggested to allow for an ileocolic anastomosis, avoiding the colo-colonic anastomosis, which may be more prone to leak, although these potential technical advantages should be balanced against the possible impact of reduced functional colonic length on long-term bowel function and quality of life.

Although the authors focus on substernal reconstruction, the optimal route should be individualized according to prior operative history, mediastinal contamination, radiation exposure, conduit selection, recipient vessel availability, and institutional expertise (2). While the posterior mediastinal route provides the most direct physiologic reconstruction and may offer favorable functional outcomes, it may be less suitable in the setting of prior mediastinal or pleural contamination, where reconstruction outside the contaminated field may be preferred. Conversely, although presternal reconstruction is generally less cosmetically desirable, it facilitates postoperative assessment of conduit perfusion and early recognition of conduit compromise, including through the use of an exteriorized sentinel monitor (“canary”) segment. Retrosternal reconstruction provides a protected extra-anatomic route that avoids a hostile posterior mediastinum while maintaining acceptable conduit length, although meticulous attention to thoracic inlet dimensions and conduit perfusion remains essential. Ultimately, route selection should be tailored to the patient’s anatomy, prior interventions, reconstructive goals, and surgeon experience.

Over a 25-year period, refinement of operative technique, advances in perioperative care, accumulated institutional experience, and patient selection likely contributed to the observed reduction in morbidity. Nevertheless, the marked reduction in anastomotic leak and conduit necrosis suggests an important role for microvascular augmentation, although the relative contribution of these factors cannot be fully distinguished in this retrospective experience.

DeMeester set a standard for results after colon interposition, reporting 82% long-term satisfaction but significant perioperative mortality of 9% (3). A large heterogeneous series published by Suzuki et al. reviewed outcomes of 170 patients managed with esophageal exclusion at a high volume esophageal center (4). Among the 97 patients who underwent salvage reconstruction, leak was observed in 44% of patients who underwent gastric reconstruction and 75% of patients who underwent alternate conduit with jejunum or colon. Barron’s study demonstrates that, with a refined technique and the addition of microvascular supercharge, rates of major complications, particularly anastomotic leak, may be reduced to levels comparable with those seen after index esophagectomy. Although minimizing perioperative complications is paramount, the ultimate success of salvage esophageal reconstruction is also determined by long-term functional outcomes, including swallowing, nutritional status, conduit durability, and quality of life, underscoring the need for continued longitudinal evaluation of these complex patients.

When there is a need to re-route esophageal conduits via a substernal path, gastric tube reconstruction remains the preferred conduit when feasible, allowing restoration of alimentary continuity with a single conduit and anastomosis. When the stomach is unavailable, both colonic and jejunal interposition remain as valuable reconstructive alternatives. Microvascular augmentation of pedicled colonic or jejunal conduits, including arterial supercharge and venous superdrainage, has emerged as an important adjunct in complex salvage reconstruction (5). Although these techniques add technical complexity, they can be performed safely in experienced centers with established multidisciplinary collaboration and microsurgical expertise.

The findings of Barron and colleagues support strong consideration of microvascular augmentation in appropriately selected patients at experienced multidisciplinary centers, where optimization of conduit perfusion may reduce the risk of ischemic and anastomotic complications. By enhancing conduit perfusion, vascular augmentation likely protects the most vulnerable portion of the reconstruction—the esophagocolonic anastomosis and distal conduit. Further study in larger contemporary cohorts will be important to better define its role in complex salvage reconstruction.
