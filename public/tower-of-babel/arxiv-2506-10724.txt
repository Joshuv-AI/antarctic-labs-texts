Detecting transitions from steady states to chaos with gamma distribution
Haiyan Wang

Abstract




Abstract

In this paper, we introduce a novel method to identify transitions from steady states to chaos in stochastic models, specifically focusing on the logistic and Ricker equations by leveraging the gamma distribution to describe the underlying population. We begin by showing that when the variance is sufficiently small, the stochastic equations converge to their deterministic counterparts. Our analysis reveals that the stochastic equations exhibit two distinct branches of the intrinsic growth rate, corresponding to alternative stable states characterized by higher and lower growth rates.

Notably, while the logistic model does not show a transition from a steady state to chaos, the Ricker model undergoes such a transition when the shape parameter of the gamma distribution is small. These findings not only enhance our understanding of the dynamic behavior in biological populations but also provide a robust framework for detecting chaos in complex systems.

keywords:
Chaos, steady state, logistic difference equation, Ricker difference equation, gamma distribution

Mathematical relation between deterministic and stochastic equations

The following relation between stochastic and deterministic equations indicates that the stochastic equations converge to their corresponding deterministic equations when the variance of

Theorem 1

Let

be a random variable representing the population at time

y_{t+1}=ry_{t}(1-y_{t})-r\text{Var}(X_{t})

And stochastic Ricker equation (

y_{t+1}=y_{t}\,\exp\!\Bigl(r(1-y_{t})\Bigr)+O\Big(\operatorname{Var}(X_{t})\Bigr).

(11)

Proof of Theorem
11
Let’s start with stochastic equation (
4

X_{t}
satisfies (
4

\displaystyle y_{t+1}

\displaystyle=E[rX_{t}(1-X_{t})\epsilon_{t}]

\displaystyle=rE[X_{t}]-rE[X_{t}^{2}]

\displaystyle=ry_{t}-r(y_{t}^{2}+\text{Var}(X_{t}))

\displaystyle=ry_{t}-ry_{t}^{2}-r\text{Var}(X_{t})

\displaystyle=ry_{t}(1-y_{t})-r\text{Var}(X_{t})

(12)

which is
10
. Apparently, the gamma distribution assumption is not used in the proof for
10
. Now if
X_{t}
satisfies stochastic Ricker equation (
4
), then

X_{t+1}=X_{t}\exp\!\Bigl(r(1-X_{t})\Bigr)\epsilon_{t},

(13)

and that
X_{t}
is a Gamma-distributed random variable with parameters

and
\theta
, so that

E[X_{t}]=y_{t}=k\,\theta\quad\text{and}\quad\operatorname{Var}(X_{t})=k\,\theta^{2}.

We write

X_{t}=y_{t}+\beta_{t},\quad\text{with }E[\beta_{t}]=0.

(14)

Then,

X_{t+1}=X_{t}\exp\!\Bigl(r(1-X_{t})\Bigr)\epsilon_{t}=(y_{t}+\beta_{t})\exp\!\Bigl(r\Bigl(1-(y_{t}+\beta_{t})\Bigr)\Bigr)\epsilon_{t}.

(15)

Rewrite the exponential as

\exp\!\Bigl(r\Bigl(1-(y_{t}+\beta_{t})\Bigr)\Bigr)=\exp\!\Bigl(r(1-y_{t})\Bigr)\exp\!\Bigl(-r\beta_{t}\Bigr).

(16)

Expand
\exp(-r\beta_{t})
about
\beta_{t}=0
up to third order:

\exp(-r\beta_{t})=1-r\beta_{t}+\frac{1}{2}(r\beta_{t})^{2}-\frac{1}{6}(r\beta_{t})^{3}+R_{3}(\beta_{t}),

where the remainder

is of order

Thus, we have

\displaystyle X_{t+1}

\displaystyle=(y_{t}+\beta_{t})\,\exp\!\Bigl(r(1-y_{t})\Bigr)\Bigl[1-r\beta_{t}+\frac{1}{2}(r\beta_{t})^{2}-\frac{1}{6}(r\beta_{t})^{3}+R_{3}(\beta_{t})\Bigr]\epsilon_{t}.

(18)

Taking the expectation of both sides and using the linearity of expectation, we obtain

\displaystyle E[X_{t+1}]

\displaystyle=\exp\!\Bigl(r(1-y_{t})\Bigr)\Biggl\{y_{t}

\displaystyle\quad+\Bigl[-y_{t}\,E[\beta_{t}]+E[\beta_{t}]\Bigr]

\displaystyle\quad+\Bigl[\frac{1}{2}y_{t}\,E[(r\beta_{t})^{2}]-E[(r\beta_{t})^{2}]\Bigr]

\displaystyle\quad+\Bigl[-\frac{1}{6}y_{t}\,E[(r\beta_{t})^{3}]+\frac{1}{2}E[(r\beta_{t})^{3}]\Bigr]

\displaystyle\quad+E\Bigl[(y_{t}+\beta_{t})R_{3}(\beta_{t})\Bigr]\Biggr\}.

(19)

Since
E[\beta_{t}]=0
, the first-order term cancels. Moreover, noting that,

E[(r\beta_{t})^{2}]=r^{2}E[\beta_{t}^{2}]=r^{2}\,\operatorname{Var}(X_{t}),

and that the third-order term is
O((\operatorname{Var}(X_{t}))^{3/2})
from (
62
), and from (
63
),

E\Bigl[(y_{t}+\beta_{t})R_{3}(\beta_{t})\Bigr]=O\Bigl((\operatorname{Var}(X_{t}))^{2}\Bigr),

we obtain the final approximate expression:

\displaystyle y_{t+1}

\displaystyle=E[X_{t+1}]=y_{t}\exp\!\Bigl(r(1-y_{t})\Bigr)

(20)

\displaystyle\quad+\exp\!\Bigl(r(1-y_{t})\Bigr)\left(\frac{r^{2}y_{t}}{2}-r\right)\operatorname{Var}(X_{t})

\displaystyle\quad+O\Bigl((\operatorname{Var}(X_{t}))^{3/2}\Bigr)+O\Bigl((\operatorname{Var}(X_{t}))^{2}\Bigr).

which is (
11

4
Discussion

Chaos represents a fundamental change in system dynamics, often involving complex processes that are both theoretically and practically fascinating. The transition between a steady state and chaos is particularly interesting because it reveals the complex, nonlinear nature of many systems. It highlights how small changes can lead to unpredictable, large-scale dynamics. Understanding this transition is crucial for managing ecosystems, predicting climate patterns, and controlling chaotic behaviors in technology and biology
[
23
]
.

In this paper, we first demonstrate that the interplay between deterministic and stochastic equations, and then present a novel approach for identifying such a transition from a steady state to chaos based on the classical chaos results and the gamma distribution for the underlying population. Our findings using the gamma distribution in this paper reveals that, both (
4
) and (
5
) have two branches of the intrinsic growth rate,
r_{+}
and
r_{-}
, representing alternative stable states corresponding to higher and lower growth rates. However, there is a striking difference in between the logistic model and Ricker model: there is no transition between a steady state and a chaotic state for the logistic model, but there is a transition from a steady state and a chaotic state when the shape parameter
k
of the gamma distribution is small.

The striking difference for the transition from steady states to chaos between of the stochastic logistic and Ricker model even exhibits in deterministic equations. In fact, the Ricker model more readily exhibits chaotic behavior compared to the logistic model, primarily due to its nonlinear density dependence and exponential growth suppression. The logistic model, given by
x_{n+1}=rx_{n}(1-x_{n})
, assumes linear density dependence, where population growth slows as the population approaches the carrying capacity
[
28
]
. In this model, chaos emerges at higher growth rates (
r>3.57
) following a period-doubling route to chaos
[
2
]
. In contrast, the Ricker model, defined as
x_{n+1}=x_{n}e^{r(1-x_{n})}
, incorporates exponential density dependence, leading to sharper suppression of population growth at higher densities
[
29
]
. This sharper feedback allows the Ricker model to exhibit chaotic dynamics at much lower growth rates (
r\sim 2.5
) compared to the logistic model. The exponential term in the Ricker model results in faster and more intense oscillations, making chaotic behavior more prevalent in systems such as fish populations or insect dynamics
[
30
]
. Consequently, while the logistic model provides a simpler framework for understanding chaos, the Ricker model is often better suited for studying real-world populations where overshooting and collapses are common.

Automated methods for chaos detection in noisy empirical data have also been developed, addressing the limitations of classical approaches that are sensitive to noise
[
24
]
. Furthermore, combining deterministic and stochastic dynamics provides a framework for understanding extreme events in chaotic systems, such as those observed in climate variability and ecological dynamics
[
25
]
. Advanced methods, such as deep learning, are now being employed to differentiate chaotic and stochastic behavior in biological systems, including lineage trees, offering robust tools for chaos detection in noisy and high-dimensional data
[
26
]
.

The method with the gamma distribution in this paper provide complementary approaches to detecting chaos in biological systems. Based on the theoretical relationships between stochastic models and statistical distributions, future research could leverage laboratory and real-world ecological population data to analyze parameter-dependent transitions to chaos and differentiate chaos from noise or periodicity. This process would involve fitting the stochastic models to empirical data to estimate their parameters, as well as deriving the corresponding distribution parameters. By applying this methods along with other relevant chaos detection methods, researchers can uncover deterministic chaos, informing fields such as population ecology, neuroscience, and cardiology. Future research should also focus on improving these techniques’ robustness to noise and their applicability to high-dimensional systems.

It is easy to compute that

\displaystyle E[X_{t}]

\displaystyle=\mu=k\theta,

(28)

\displaystyle E[X_{t}^{2}]

\displaystyle=k(k+1)\theta^{2}.

(29)

\displaystyle E[X_{t}^{n}]

(30)

Since

E[X_{t+1}]=E\left[rX_{t}\left(1-X_{t}\right)\epsilon_{t}\right]=E\left[rX_{t}\left(1-X_{t}\right)\right]E[\epsilon_{t}].

Since

, and at equilibrium, (

\mu=r\left(\mu-E[X_{t}^{2}]\right).

(33)

Subtract

\mu(1-r)=-rE[X_{t}^{2}].

(34)

Substituting
\mu=k\theta
and
E[X_{t}^{2}]=\sigma^{2}+\mu^{2}
into the equilibrium equation (

):

k\theta(1-r)=-r\left(k\theta^{2}+k^{2}\theta^{2}\right).

(35)

Divide both sides by

(since

(1-r)=-r\theta(1+k).

(36)

Solve for

\theta=\frac{r-1}{r(1+k)}.

(37)

Therefore, this suggests that for

, this equilibrium may exist under the gamma distribution assumption.

In addition to the mean, we can compare the variance condition at equilibrium

\text{Var}[X_{t+1}]=\text{Var}[X_{t}].

This provides additional conditions that can help determine the gamma distribution parameters. To compute
\text{Var}[X_{t+1}]
, we need
E[X_{t+1}]
and
E[X_{t+1}^{2}]
:

\text{Var}[X_{t+1}]=E[X_{t+1}^{2}]-\left(E[X_{t+1}]\right)^{2}.

We already have
E[X_{t+1}]=\mu=k\theta
. To compute
E[X_{t+1}^{2}]
, we expand
X_{t+1}^{2}
:

\displaystyle X_{t+1}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(rX_{t}-rX_{t}^{2}\right)^{2}\epsilon_{t}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(r^{2}X_{t}^{2}-2r^{2}X_{t}^{3}+r^{2}X_{t}^{4}\right)\epsilon_{t}^{2}

We take expectations:

E[X_{t+1}^{2}]=\left(r^{2}E[X_{t}^{2}]-2r^{2}E[X_{t}^{3}]+r^{2}E[X_{t}^{4}]\right)E[\epsilon_{t}^{2}].

(38)

At equilibrium,
\text{Var}[X_{t+1}]=\text{Var}[X_{t}]=k\theta^{2}
. Therefore,

E[X_{t+1}^{2}]-(k\theta)^{2}=k\theta^{2}

(39)

and

E[X_{t+1}^{2}]=k\theta^{2}(1+k).

(40)

We can cancel
\theta^{2}
from both sides as
\theta>0
:

\frac{E[X_{t+1}^{2}]}{\theta^{2}}=k(1+k).

(41)

Compute the required terms:

\displaystyle E[X_{t}^{2}]

\displaystyle=\theta^{2}k(k+1),

\displaystyle E[X_{t}^{3}]

\displaystyle=\theta^{3}\frac{\Gamma(k+3)}{\Gamma(k)}=\theta^{3}(k+2)(k+1)k,

\displaystyle E[X_{t}^{4}]

\displaystyle=\theta^{4}\frac{\Gamma(k+4)}{\Gamma(k)}=\theta^{4}(k+3)(k+2)(k+1)k.

With cancellation of
\theta^{2}
, (
41
) and (
38
) give

\displaystyle\Big(r^{2}k(k+1)-2r^{2}\theta(k+2)(k+1)k+r^{2}\theta^{2}(k+3)(k+2)(k+1)k\Big)E[\epsilon_{t}^{2}]=k(1+k).

Dividing by
E[\epsilon_{t}^{2}]
and subtracting
k(k+1)
at both sides give

\displaystyle(r^{2}-1)k(k+1)-2r^{2}\theta(k+2)(k+1)k+r^{2}\theta^{2}(k+3)(k+2)(k+1)k=\frac{k(1+k)}{E[\epsilon_{t}^{2}]}-k(k+1).

Multiplying by
-1
at both sides produces

\displaystyle(1-r^{2})k(k+1)+2r^{2}\theta(k+2)(k+1)k-r^{2}\theta^{2}(k+3)(k+2)(k+1)k=\frac{E[\epsilon_{t}^{2}]-1}{E[\epsilon_{t}^{2}]}k(k+1).

Since
Var[\epsilon_{t}]=E[\epsilon_{t}^{2}]-E[\epsilon_{t}]^{2}=E[\epsilon_{t}^{2}]-1
,

\displaystyle(1-r^{2})k(k+1)+2r^{2}\theta(k+2)(k+1)k-r^{2}\theta^{2}(k+3)(k+2)(k+1)k=\frac{Var[\epsilon_{t}]}{Var[\epsilon_{t}]+1}k(k+1).

Note that
\theta=\frac{(r-1)}{r(1+k)}
, therefore,

(1-r^{2})k(k+1)+2r(r-1)k(k+2)-(r-1)^{2}k\cdot\dfrac{(k+2)(k+3)}{k+1}=\frac{Var[\epsilon_{t}]}{Var[\epsilon_{t}]+1}k(k+1)

(42)

Dividing both sides by
k(r-1)
, we have

\dfrac{(1-r^{2})k(k+1)}{k(r-1)}+\dfrac{2r(r-1)k(k+2)}{k(r-1)}-\dfrac{(r-1)^{2}k\cdot\dfrac{(k+2)(k+3)}{k+1}}{k(r-1)}=\frac{Var[\epsilon_{t}]}{Var[\epsilon_{t}]+1}\frac{k+1}{r-1}

(43)

After simplification, equation (
43
) becomes:

-(r+1)(k+1)+2r(k+2)-\dfrac{(r-1)(k+2)(k+3)}{k+1}=\frac{Var[\epsilon_{t}]}{Var[\epsilon_{t}]+1}\frac{k+1}{r-1}

(44)

Multiply equation (
44
) by
k+1
:

-(r+1)(k+1)^{2}+2r(k+2)(k+1)-(r-1)(k+2)(k+3)=\frac{Var[\epsilon_{t}]}{Var[\epsilon_{t}]+1}\frac{(k+1)^{2}}{r-1}

(45)

The left side of (
45
) is

-rk^{2}-2rk-r-k^{2}-2k-1+2rk^{2}+6rk+4r-rk^{2}-5rk-6r+k^{2}+5k+6

Thus, with simplification of the left side of (
45
), we arrive at

-r(k+3)+3k+5=\frac{Var[\epsilon_{t}]}{Var[\epsilon_{t}]+1}\frac{(k+1)^{2}}{r-1}

(46)

Since
r>1
, it follows that

-r(k+3)+3k+5\geq 0

(47)

and

1<r\leq\frac{3k+5}{k+3}<3

(48)

Multiply both sides of (
46
) by
(r-1)
:

(r-1)\Big(-r(k+3)+3k+5\Big)=\frac{Var[\epsilon_{t}]}{Var[\epsilon_{t}]+1}(k+1)^{2}

(49)

Simplification of the left side of (
49
) leads to:

-r^{2}(k+3)+4kr+8r-3k-5=\frac{Var[\epsilon_{t}]}{Var[\epsilon_{t}]+1}(k+1)^{2}

(50)

Now express (
50
) as a quadratic equation in terms of
r
:

(k+3)r^{2}-\left(4k+8\right)r+\left(3k+5+\dfrac{Var[\epsilon_{t}]}{Var[\epsilon_{t}]+1}(k+1)^{2}\right)=0.

(51)

Let
A
,
B
, and
C
in (
51
) for computing its discriminant as:

\displaystyle A=k+3,B=-\left(4k+8\right)=-4k-8,C=3k+5+\dfrac{Var[\epsilon_{t}]}{Var[\epsilon_{t}]+1}(k+1)^{2}

and

\displaystyle B^{2}

\displaystyle=16k^{2}+64k+64,

\displaystyle 4AC

\displaystyle=4(k+3)\left(3k+5+\dfrac{Var[\epsilon_{t}]}{Var[\epsilon_{t}]+1}(k+1)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=4(k+3)(3k+5)+\dfrac{4Var[\epsilon_{t}](k+3)(k+1)^{2}}{Var[\epsilon_{t}]+1}

\displaystyle=12k^{2}+56k+60+\dfrac{4Var[\epsilon_{t}](k+3)(k+1)^{2}}{Var[\epsilon_{t}]+1}.

Thus the discriminant
D=B^{2}-4AC
of the quadratic equation with respect to
r
is given by:

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle=\left(16k^{2}+64k+64\right)-\left(12k^{2}+56k+60+\dfrac{4Var[\epsilon_{t}](k+3)(k+1)^{2}}{Var[\epsilon_{t}]+1}\right)

\displaystyle=4k^{2}+8k+4-\dfrac{4Var[\epsilon_{t}](k+3)(k+1)^{2}}{Var[\epsilon_{t}]+1}

\displaystyle=4(k+1)^{2}\left(\dfrac{1-Var[\epsilon_{t}](k+2)}{Var[\epsilon_{t}]+1}\right).

For real solutions, the discriminant
D
must be non-negative. Therefore, the condition for real solutions is:

0<k\leq\dfrac{1}{Var[\epsilon_{t}]}-2.

(52)

We now have the maximum of the feasible variance of
\epsilon_{t}

0\leq Var[\epsilon_{t}]\leq 0.5.

(53)

Using the quadratic formula, we have

\displaystyle r_{\pm}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{4k+8\pm 2(k+1)\sqrt{\dfrac{1-Var[\epsilon_{t}](k+2)}{Var[\epsilon_{t}]+1}}}{2(k+3)}

(54)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{2k+4\pm(k+1)\sqrt{\dfrac{1-Var[\epsilon_{t}](k+2)}{Var[\epsilon_{t}]+1}}}{k+3}.

(55)

It is easy to see that both
r_{\pm}\geq 1
. Since
r_{+}>r_{-}
and
\dfrac{1-Var[\epsilon_{t}](k+2)}{Var[\epsilon_{t}]+1}\leq 1
and therefore

2k+4-(k+1)\sqrt{\dfrac{1-Var[\epsilon_{t}](k+2)}{Var[\epsilon_{t}]+1}}\geq k+3

We summarize the above result as Theorem
2
.

5.2
Properties of Gamma Distribution

5.2.1
Identity of Gamma Distribution

In this section, our goal is to prove the following identity for
s\geq 0

E\left[X_{t}^{n}e^{-sX_{t}}\right]=\frac{\Gamma(k+n)}{\Gamma(k)}\frac{\theta^{n}}{(1+s\theta)^{k+n}}

(56)

where
X_{t}\sim\text{Gamma}(k,\theta)

f_{X_{t}}(x)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(k)\theta^{k}}x^{k-1}e^{-x/\theta},\quad x>0.

(57)

We aim to compute the expected value:

\displaystyle E\left[X_{t}^{n}e^{-sX_{t}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{n}e^{-sx}f_{X_{t}}(x)\,dx

(58)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{n}e^{-sx}\left(\frac{1}{\Gamma(k)\theta^{k}}x^{k-1}e^{-x/\theta}\right)dx

(59)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Gamma(k)\theta^{k}}\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{n+k-1}e^{-x(s+1/\theta)}dx

(60)

Recall the standard gamma integral

\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{\alpha-1}e^{-\lambda x}dx=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha)}{\lambda^{\alpha}},\quad\text{for }\lambda>0,\ \alpha>0

(61)

Applying (
61
) to (
60
) with
\alpha=n+k,\,\lambda=s+1/\theta
, we get (
56
)

\displaystyle E\left[X_{t}^{n}e^{-sX_{t}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Gamma(k)\theta^{k}}\cdot\frac{\Gamma(n+k)}{\left(s+\dfrac{1}{\theta}\right)^{n+k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma(k+n)}{\Gamma(k)}\frac{\theta^{n}}{(1+s\theta)^{k+n}}

5.2.2
Third Central Moment for Gamma Distribution

Assume that

X_{n}\sim\operatorname{Gamma}(k,\theta),

so that

E[X_{n}]=k\theta\quad\text{and}\quad\operatorname{Var}(X_{n})=k\theta^{2}.

Define the fluctuation

\beta_{n}=X_{n}-E[X_{n}]=X_{n}-k\theta.

The third central moment is given by

E[\beta_{n}^{3}]=E\Bigl[(X_{n}-k\theta)^{3}\Bigr].

We will derive the following estimation.

E[\beta_{n}^{3}]=O\Bigl((\operatorname{Var}(X_{n}))^{3/2}\Bigr).

(62)

Express this in terms of the raw moments:

E[\beta_{n}^{3}]=E[X_{n}^{3}]-3E[X_{n}]\,E[X_{n}^{2}]+2E[X_{n}]^{3}.

Using the identity

E[X_{n}^{n}]=\frac{\Gamma(k+n)}{\Gamma(k)}\,\theta^{n},

we obtain:

E[X_{n}]=k\theta,\quad E[X_{n}^{2}]=(k+1)k\,\theta^{2},\quad E[X_{n}^{3}]=(k+2)(k+1)k\,\theta^{3}.

Substitute these into the expression for
E[\beta_{n}^{3}]
:

\displaystyle E[\beta_{n}^{3}]

\displaystyle=(k+2)(k+1)k\,\theta^{3}-3\,(k\theta)\,((k+1)k\,\theta^{2})+2\,(k\theta)^{3}

\displaystyle=k\,\theta^{3}\Bigl[(k+2)(k+1)-3k(k+1)+2k^{2}\Bigr].

Notice that

(k+2)(k+1)=k^{2}+3k+2.

Then,

\displaystyle(k+2)(k+1)-3k(k+1)+2k^{2}

\displaystyle=(k^{2}+3k+2)-3k^{2}-3k+2k^{2}

\displaystyle=k^{2}+3k+2-3k^{2}-3k+2k^{2}

\displaystyle=2.

Thus,

E[\beta_{n}^{3}]=k\,\theta^{3}\cdot 2=2k\,\theta^{3}.

Since

\operatorname{Var}(X_{n})=k\,\theta^{2},

we have

(\operatorname{Var}(X_{n}))^{3/2}=(k\,\theta^{2})^{3/2}=k^{3/2}\,\theta^{3}.

or equivalently,

E[\beta_{n}^{3}]=O\Bigl((\operatorname{Var}(X_{n}))^{3/2}\Bigr).

5.2.3
Fourth Central Moment for Gamma Distribution

Assume that

X_{t}\sim\operatorname{Gamma}(k,\theta),

so that the mean and variance are

E[X_{t}]=k\theta,\quad\operatorname{Var}(X_{t})=k\theta^{2}.

We define the fluctuation

\beta_{t}=X_{t}-E[X_{t}].

Our goal is to show that

E[\beta_{t}^{4}]=O\Bigl((\operatorname{Var}(X_{t}))^{2}\Bigr).

(63)

The identity (
56
)
implies that by setting
s=0
we obtain the raw moments:

E[X_{t}^{n}]=\frac{\Gamma(k+n)}{\Gamma(k)}\,\theta^{n}.

In particular, we have:

\displaystyle E[X_{t}]

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(k+1)}{\Gamma(k)}\,\theta=k\,\theta,

\displaystyle E[X_{t}^{2}]

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(k+2)}{\Gamma(k)}\,\theta^{2}=(k+1)k\,\theta^{2},

\displaystyle E[X_{t}^{3}]

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(k+3)}{\Gamma(k)}\,\theta^{3}=(k+2)(k+1)k\,\theta^{3},

\displaystyle E[X_{t}^{4}]

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(k+4)}{\Gamma(k)}\,\theta^{4}=(k+3)(k+2)(k+1)k\,\theta^{4}.

The fourth central moment (which is
E[\beta_{t}^{4}]
) is given by:

E[(X_{t}-E[X_{t}])^{4}]=E[X_{t}^{4}]-4E[X_{t}]\,E[X_{t}^{3}]+6\bigl(E[X_{t}]\bigr)^{2}E[X_{t}^{2}]-3\bigl(E[X_{t}]\bigr)^{4}.

Substitute the moments computed above:

\displaystyle E[\beta_{t}^{4}]

\displaystyle=(k+3)(k+2)(k+1)k\,\theta^{4}

\displaystyle\quad-4\,(k\,\theta)\,(k+2)(k+1)k\,\theta^{3}

\displaystyle\quad+6\,(k\,\theta)^{2}\,(k+1)k\,\theta^{2}

\displaystyle\quad-3\,(k\,\theta)^{4}.

Factor out the common factor
k^{2}\theta^{4}
and simplify them:

E[\beta_{t}^{4}]=\left(3+\frac{6}{k}\right)k^{2}\theta^{4},

Since

\operatorname{Var}(X_{t})=k\theta^{2},

we have

\operatorname{Var}(X_{t})^{2}=k^{2}\theta^{4}.

Therefore, we conclude the verification of (
63
).
