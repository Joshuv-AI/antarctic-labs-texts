Analysis of inverse problem for pseudo-hyperbolic equation under periodic boundary condition
Authors: İrem Bağlan, Akbala Yernazar, Erman Aslan, Hüseyin Budak, Miguel Vivas-Cortez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis research paper investigates an inverse problem involving time-dependent unknown coefficients in a one-dimensional nonlinear pseudo-hyperbolic equation with nonlocal boundary conditions. The Fourier method is employed, and the convergence, uniqueness, and stability of the solution are demonstrated. Additionally, the Finite Difference Method (FDM) is applied to address the inverse problem numerically. A numerical example is provided to demonstrate the performance of the method. In the Finite Difference Method, two finite difference schemes with different levels of accuracy are used and compared with each other. Furthermore, the cases of ε = 0 (hyperbolic) and ε ≠ 0 (pseudo-hyperbolic) are also compared.Keywords: Inverse problem, Nonlinear pseudo-hyperbolic equation, Periodic boundary condition, Fourier method, Finite difference methodSubject terms: Applied mathematics, Computational science

Introduction

Hyperbolic partial differential equations (PDEs) naturally arise in various physical and engineering contexts, particularly in problems involving wave propagation and dynamic systems. These equations describe fundamental processes such as sound waves, electromagnetic waves, and fluid flows, and play a crucial in fields including acoustics, seismology, electromagnetism, and medical imaging. Notable examples of hyperbolic equations include the wave equation, the Klein–Gordon equation, and equations governing the propagation of seismic or electromagnetic waves.

When applied in these contexts, the direct problem of hyperbolic equations typically involves determining the solution given specific initial and boundary conditions. However, in contrast, inverse problems seek to recover unknown parameters or conditions based on observed data. These inverse problems are central to numerous practical applications, including the identification of the source term from wave measurements, identifying material properties from wave propagation data, and reconstructing the initial state of a system from its final observations. The inherent complexity of inverse problems-many of which are ill-posed-presents significant mathematical and computational challenges.

To address these challenges, inverse problems for hyperbolic equations can be categorized into several types, each focusing on different aspects of the equation’s unknowns. For instance, the Inverse Source Problem involves identifying an unknown source term given the solution at later times. This problem commonly arises in seismology, where determining the location and strength of a seismic source from recorded seismic waves is a central task. In addition, the Inverse Coefficient Problem aims to recover unknown coefficients from observed data, a critical task in fields like medical imaging and geophysics. In these applications, the primary objective is to infer the internal structure of a medium from external wave propagation measurements.

Many of these inverse problems are ill-posed, meaning that they may not have a unique solution, and small errors in the data can lead to significant inaccuracies. Recent studies1, have contributed to addressing some of these challenges by demonstrating the existence and uniqueness of solutions to inverse boundary value problems for hyperbolic equations. These works address both time-dependent and constant coefficients under specified boundary conditions2–4. Furthermore, stability analyses, such as those conducted by5,6, have investigated the reconstruction of time-dependent coefficients and examined their implications for the reability of the solutions.

In addition to these advancements, the incorporation of physical models opens new avenues for addressing inverse problems. For instance,7 presents an inverse coefficient problem related to the Euler–Bernoulli beam theory, which parallels investigations of hyperbolic equations. By employing generalized Fourier methods, this approach establishes conditions under which existence, uniqueness, and continuous dependence on data are guaranteed. This conceptual framework exhibits strong similarities with that of hyperbolic equations, suggesting that methodologies developed in one field may offer valuable insights into the other.

To further address the complexities of inverse hyperbolic problems, the integration of multiple methodologies, such as Fourier analysis, has proven effective, particularly in the presence of periodic boundary conditions6,8,9.

For nonlinear hyperbolic equations, Carleman estimates have become a widely used analytical tool10–14. These estimates yield fundamental stability and uniqueness results, helping to overcome the challenges of ill-posedness and ensuring that such problems are well-posed and solvable.

Given the challenges associated with the direct application of classical numerical methods, inverse hyperbolic problems often require specialized techniques. Among the most widely employed approaches are finite difference15,16, finite element17–19, and finite volume methods20–23, while spectral and wavelet methods are known to offer higher accuracy. The choice of method is typically influenced by the specific problem type, boundary conditions, and the quality of the data available. As a result, much of the research in this field has focused on developing regularization techniques that stabilize inverse problems, thereby rendering them solvable in practice. Tikhonov regularization, introduced in the early 1960s, plays a fundamental role in this context. Various optimization algorithms also contribute to stabilizing the solutions. Numerous studies emphasize the importance of Tikhonov regularization as a key technique for addressing ill-posed inverse problems in hyperbolic equations14,24–28.

This study aims to solve inverse hyperbolic and pseudo-hyperbolic equations with periodic boundary conditions using both analytical and numerical approaches. The novelty of this work lies in the fact that the considered inverse coefficient problem has not previously been solved analytically under periodic boundary conditions; to date, it has been addressed only through numerical methods. In this study, the problem is investigated analytically and numerically, with the results systematically compared. For the analytical solution, the well-established Fourier method is employed, while a finite difference method is subsequently applied for numerical analysis.

This method is also computationally efficient, particularly for problems defined on structured grids, and generally requires fewer computational resources than more complex approaches like the Finite Element or Spectral Methods.

Grid Independence study and time step size determination

Seven different grid densities are used to determine the grid independence number for the numerical solutions. The grid numbers used are 20, 40, 80, 160, 320, 640, and 1280. Additionally, six different time steps are tested: 0.01 s, 0.005 s, 0.0025 s, 0.00125 s, 0.000625 s, 0.0003125 s.

Both the grid independence study and the time step determination analysis are carried out at ε = 0 and ε = 3 separately for Eqs. (24) and (25). As a first step, for the case ε = 0, the grid independence study for Eq. (24) is conducted across all time steps, as illustrated in Fig. 1. In this analysis, both the solution of the wave equation, v(z,t), and the inverse coefficient a(t) are evaluated simultaneously. To facilitate a clearer comparison, all graphs are plotted on the same scale, with the exact solutions represented by dashed lines. It is observed that, at all time steps, the inverse coefficient exhibits lower values for coarse grids, but increases with mesh refinement and approaches the exact value. Conversely, the v exhibits higher magnitudes for coarse grids and decreases as the mesh number increases, similarly converging to the exact solution. For both quantities, a mesh size of 640 can be considered sufficient to achieve grid independence, as further refinement does not alter the numerical results.

Conclusion

An analytical and numerical investigation of the one-dimensional nonlinear hyperbolic (ε=0) and pseudo-hyperbolic (ε≠0) equations under periodic boundary conditions has been carried out. This study involves an inverse problem with time-dependent unknown coefficients.

For the analytical solution, the generalized Fourier method was used to compute the Fourier coefficients. Additionally, an iterative approach was employed to ensure convergence while evaluating the uniqueness and stability of the solution for the nonlinear problem.

For the numerical solution, two implicit finite difference equations with different levels of accuracy are applied. In the first equation (Eq. 24), a first-order accurate time discretization and a second-order accurate spatial discretization scheme are used. In the second equation (Eq. 25), a second-order accurate time discretization and a fourth-order accurate spatial discretization scheme were applied.
