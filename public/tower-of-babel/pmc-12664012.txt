Creatine kinase imaging (CKI) for in vivo whole-brain mapping of creatine kinase reaction kinetics
Authors: Mark Widmaier, Antonia Kaiser, Pontus Pandurevic, Song-I Lim, André Döring, Zhiwei Huang, Daniel Wenz, Ying Xiao, Yun Jiang, Yan Lin, Lijing Xin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The creatine kinase (CK) is a key enzyme involved in brain bioenergetics, playing an important role in brain function and the pathogenesis of neurological and psychiatric diseases and cancers. However, imaging its activity noninvasively in the human brain remains a significant challenge. This study aims to advance 31P magnetic resonance fingerprinting for Creatine Kinase Imaging (CKI). The method was implemented and validated on a clinical 7 Tesla MRI scanner. CKI enables whole-brain mapping of CK forward rate constant, revealing regional differences. Furthermore, a functional CKI (fCKI) study demonstrated a CK activation map in response to visual stimulation, revealing an increase in CK forward rate constant in the visual cortex. The novel imaging modalities, CKI and fCKI, have the potential to offer insights into brain bioenergetics both at rest and during activity, in both healthy and pathological conditions.

The brain’s energy requirements are substantial, accounting for roughly 20% of the body’s total energy usage. This highlights the role of metabolic processes in maintaining neural function. Understanding the dynamics of brain energetics is vital for the advancement of diagnostic and therapeutic strategies for various health conditions. One of the key enzymes in cellular energy metabolism is Creatine Kinase (CK), which catalyzes the reversible reaction to transfer high-energy phosphate between phosphocreatine (PCr) and triphosphate (ATP): PCr+ADP+H+⇌kATP+ creatine. It plays a key role in cellular energy buffering, regeneration, and transport, and has emerged as a promising biomarker with diverse clinical implications.

Dynamic responses of CK have been shown during physical training (1–3) and brain activation (4–6). Alterations in CK turnover are found in aging (7) and neurodegenerative diseases (8–10). Recent findings suggest a link between mitochondrial dysfunction and psychiatric disorders, as evidenced by reduced CK turnover rates in schizophrenia (22%) (11), first episode bipolar disorder (14%) (12), and first-episode psychosis (12%) (13). Importantly, CK/creatine energetics has recently been shown to be an essential metabolic vulnerability in liver (14), breast (15) and brain (16) cancers, as well as in myeloid leukemia (17). Potential therapeutic approaches targeting CK energetics could be an effective strategy for selectively eliminating cancer cells (18). To assess the potential effectiveness of novel CK-targeted therapeutic strategies, noninvasively, we require imaging tools capable of mapping CK activity within living organisms.

31P-MRS with magnetization transfer including saturation transfer (ST) or inversion transfer (IT) is a noninvasive tool (19) to measure the CK reaction kinetic parameters, i.e., pseudo-first-order reaction rate constants. It suffers from low sensitivity compared to 1H MRI due to low metabolite concentrations (e.g., PCr 0.004 M vs. water 43 M in the human brain) and a gyromagnetic ratio approximately 2.5 times lower than that of 1H. Signal averaging is commonly used to enhance sensitivity, but it also increases acquisition time.

Furthermore, the long T1 relaxation times of the 31P metabolites further prolong the acquisition and hinder efficient spatial sampling. Accelerated saturation transfer methods under partially relaxed conditions have been proposed (20–23) to shorten scan times, but they require either additional measurements of T1 or assumptions about T1 values. To date, only one study by Bottomley et al. (24) in 1992 demonstrated 2D forward rate constant (kCK) maps with a spatial resolution of 20×20×50 mm3 using ST and Cartesian spatial encoding at 4 T, requiring a scan time of 34 min. Efficient 3D mapping of the CK reaction kinetics within a clinically acceptable time remains a challenge until now.

Although ST methods have been the traditional choice because of the strong MT effect, the use of saturation pulses results in excessive RF energy deposition, reducing the measurement efficiency and limiting their application in the human brain at ultrahigh fields (UHF) due to specific absorption rate (SAR) limits. To benefit from the increased signal sensitivity at UHF, IT methods have recently gained attention for their lower SAR burden (3, 25). However, IT requires multiple measurements, involving varying inversion times, to determine CK rate constant, levels of PCr and ATP, as well as T1 relaxation times.

Recently, Wang et al. have undertaken a novel approach to CK kinetic measurements in rodent muscle (26) (CK-MRF) and Widmaier et al. in the human brain (27) (MT-31 P-MRF), adapting the magnetic resonance fingerprinting (MRF) framework (28) to phosphorus MT measurements. The MRF framework has proven to be time-efficient for multiparametric estimations. Similarly to the initial application for proton MRI, a balanced steady-state free precession (bSSFP-)type of acquisition scheme was chosen in phosphorus MRF applications because of its highest signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) efficiency among existing MR sequences. The MT approach, however, differs between the two studies. Wang et al. used an ST scheme, as rodent applications are not limited by SAR. Widmaier et al. employed an IT approach for the implementation at UHF with a human brain application. CK-MRF and MT-31P-MRF both demonstrated the potential of MRF to estimate CK rate constant, showing improved precision in shorter acquisition times compared to reference methods (25), with up to a fourfold scan time reduction in human brain applications. However, MT-31P-MRF has, so far, been restricted to a small region of interest (ROI) using 1D localization.

In this work, the MT-31P-MRF framework has been advanced to perform 3D Creatine Kinase Imaging (CKI), delivering full-brain CK forward rate constant (kCK) maps. This was achieved by an SNR-efficient sequence design, with an ultrashort acquisition delay time (TE) of 0.5 ms and a bSSFP-type sequence using a spiral k-space sampling. CKI leverages a target-oriented imaging approach by selectively sampling either PCr or γ-ATP maps. In addition to CK rate constant, the CKI acquisition scheme enables simultaneous mapping of the concentration ratio between ATP and PCr, the longitudinal relaxation time of PCr, and phosphorus-based B0 maps. Notably, the CKI application offers ease of use with a simple push-button acquisition and reconstruction process. The technique was first demonstrated with a 49:30-min acquisition time on a clinically approved MRI scanner. Within-session reproducibility further suggests that a feasible application could be achieved in just 25 min. In a proof-of-concept application, CKI was applied dynamically during visual stimulation, providing the first-ever 3D CK activation clusters in the visual cortex. This CKI application introduces a novel functional imaging modality, functional Creatine Kinase Imaging (fCKI), which provides insights into the bioenergetic mechanisms underlying brain function.

Results

CKI Sequence.

Similar to our previous 1D MT-31P-MRF approach (27), a bSSFP-type sequence (Fig. 1A) is used to achieve high SNR efficiency for CKI. The gradient moment is set to be net-zero for each TR (Fig. 1B), and the flip angle (FA) input pattern (Fig. 1A) is applied with alternating phases of 0° and 180°. In this approach, 3D 31P images of PCr and γATP are acquired as inputs for a two-pool chemical exchange model to estimate the creatine kinase forward rate constant kCK, the concentration ratio γATP are acquired as inputs for a two-pool chemical exchange model to estimate the creatine kinase forward rate constant kCK, the concentration ratio Cr=MPCr0/MATP0, the longitudinal relaxation time of PCr T1PCr, and the off-resonance ΔB0. Frequency selectivity on the metabolites of interest (PCr or γATP) was achieved by a 10 ms Gaussian pulse with a bandwidth of 170 Hz, while the frequency difference between PCr or γATP is ~302 Hz. Furthermore, the signal obtained in a bSSFP-type of sequence is off-resonance dependent and exhibits a periodic pattern with alternating passbands and stopbands. The widths of these bands depend on the TR and FA. Therefore, TR and FA in the bSSFP was also optimized to minimize signal contamination to the metabolites of interest from the nontargeted metabolites. ATRof 19.82 ms was chosen, with <1% contamination in the expected FA range given by the input pattern (SI Appendix, Supporting Text 1 and Fig. S1).

In Vivo Demonstration.

Examples of in vivo signal evolutions and their matched dictionary entries for different ROIs of participant 6 are shown in Fig. 2A. The visible alterations in the PCr signal result from the TE variations shown in Fig. 1B. The transversal maps of kCK, Cr, ΔB0, and T1PCr with an actual voxel volume of 14 ml (nominal 1ml) obtained in a 49:30-min acquisition, are shown in Fig. 2B. For anatomical reference, 1H GRE images and the 31P-bSSFP-type images of PCr and ATP are shown above. Overall, the mean values of kCK (Table 1) are in the range of reported values (Table 2). No significant difference in kCK between gray and white matter tissues was found. However, regional differences in kCK were observed (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 3, Table 1, and SI Appendix, Table S2). The parietal lobe showed the highest kCK value, with significant differences compared to the caudate, cerebellum, and putamen. In contrast, the putamen exhibited the lowest kCK value, significantly lower than those in the other four ROIs (insula, frontal, occipital, and parietal lobes; Fig. 3 and SI Appendix, Table S2). A significantly lower (P < 0.0001) mean Cr value in white matter than gray matter was detected (Fig. 3 and SI Appendix, Table S3). With a mean Cr of 1.01, 0.97, 1.10, the thalamus, caudate, and putamen showed significantly lower concentration ratio compared to other ROIs. On the other hand, the cerebellum and temporal lobe showed significantly higher concentration ratios, with a Cr of 1.39 and 1.42, respectively, compared to other ROIs. T1 maps and values show significantly lower (P = 0.012) values in the white matter than gray matter. Additionally, significant differences between several ROIs are found (SI Appendix, Table S2). The ΔB0 maps show that most areas are within a ±10 Hz range (Fig. 2B). Toward the frontal lobe, the values of ΔB0 deviated, as expected, due to locally high susceptibility effects. Moreover, the regional mean SNR of the MRF signal (Fig. 2A) ranged from 7.6 dB in the thalamus to 17.6 dB in the parietal lobe, indicating good overall image quality across brain regions. When averaged across tissue types, the mean SNR was 19.8 dB in WM and 20.0 dB in GM. Noise robustness was evaluated through Monte Carlo simulations, demonstrating that SDs of matching errors of kCK, Cr, and T1PCr remained below 14%, 5%, 16.5%, respectively, when SNR > 8 dB, highlighting the reliability of the method (SI Appendix, Supporting Text 3 and Fig. S3).

Intrasession Reproducibility.

To assess measurement reproducibility and the potential for reducing scan time by using fewer averages, the full dataset was split into two subsamples of 16 averages each to calculate the intrasession coefficient of variation (CV). Table 1 shows the CV values for kCK, Cr, and T1PCr. The mean CV of ROIs did not exceed 15.6%, 2.3%, and 9.0% for kCK, Cr, and T1PCr, respectively. Note that the caudate, thalamus, putamen, and insula have relatively small volumes and therefore exhibit higher CVs. In contrast, for larger regions such as the frontal, occipital, parietal, and temporal lobes, as well as the cerebellum, the mean CVs were within 6.1% for kCK. Excellent intrasession reproducibility was achieved with half of the measurement time with mean CVs below 3.9%, 1.1%, and 3.1% for kCK, Cr, and T1PCr in white matter and 3.9%, 1.7%, and 6.3% in gray matter, respectively. Furthermore, the voxel-wise CVs and SNRs for each participant were presented as histogram plots in SI Appendix, Fig. S4 along with example maps (SI Appendix, Fig. S5) for the participant shown in Fig. 2. Median CVs of 16 to 19% were observed for kCK across all participants.

Functional Creatine Kinase Imaging.

A functional study using dynamic CKI is demonstrated, as an exemplary application of CKI. Fig. 4A shows the visual stimulation paradigm applied in fCKI. An increase in kCK is visually notable in the visual cortex of the brain between REST and STIM (Fig. 4B). ROI analyses revealed for each participant a mean absolute kCK increase of 0.04 s−1 in the occipital lobe, with a mean relative increase of 13.7%. A mean relative decrease of 3.7% (absolute: −0.05 [-]) in PCr/ATP was observed in the occipital lobe. This finding is in line with the percentage difference map (DIFF) in Fig. 4B. Activation clusters (size ≥20) with a percentage difference in kCK between STIM and REST blocks (DIFF) ≥10% are primarily located in the posterior part of the brain, predominantly within the visual cortex and were reproducible across all participants. The positive kCK responses within posterior brain clusters showed changes of 27.2%, 25.0%, and 35.0% across the three participants. Positive response clusters were also observed in the two superior slices, likely corresponding to the motor cortex. Negative response clusters were explored with kCK percentage changes ≤ −10% (Fig. 4B). Negative responses were observed lateral to the positive response clusters in participant 3, but were not detected in participant 2. Additional clusters appeared in the frontal and superior regions of the brain for participant 1. Overall, the negative responses were not reproducible. PCr/ATP response clusters with a percentage difference ≥5% and ≤ −5% were also shown in Fig. 4B. Most PCr/ATP response clusters were negative and colocalized with positive kCK response clusters in the posterior brain regions, showing changes of −9.2% and −9.1% in participants 1 and 3, while no response clusters were detected in participant 2 (≤ −5%).

Discussion

The CKI sequence achieved excellent performance in vivo, allowing the measurement of whole-brain maps of kCK, Cr, ΔB0, and T1PCr. The obtained values in different ROIs are reported and consistent with previously reported values (Table 2). Regional differences in kCK are revealed. The reproducibility of CKI was evaluated through within-session variability analyses. Half of the scanning data (16 averages, 25 min), shows mean CVs below 10% for all parameters and ROIs (except for the caudate, putamen, and thalamus, which have small volumes), indicating consistent performance.

Heterogeneity in PCr/ATP ratios was observed across brain ROIs as well as between gray matter and white matter. PCr/ATP values were significantly higher in GM (1.25 ± 0.07) compared to WM (1.14 ± 0.07), consistent with most prior 31P MRSI studies investigating tissue differences. For example, a recent 9.4T study reported a mean PCr/ATP value of 1.18 in GM and 1.01 in WM (37). Other studies have similarly found higher values in GM than WM, including 1.64 and 0.98 (38), 1.19 and 0.84 (39), 2.08 and 1.09 (40), 0.99 and 0.81 (20), as well as in a recent high-resolution MRSI study (41). In contrast, one earlier report found no tissue difference, with values of 1.00 and 1.03 for GM and WM (42). Our ROI analysis also revealed regional variation, with the highest PCr/ATP values observed in the cerebellum and temporal lobe. Regional investigations remain relatively sparse in the literature, though higher cerebellar values compared with the cerebrum have been reported previously (38, 40, 43). As expected, subcortical ROIs such as the caudate, thalamus, and putamen, which are surrounded by WM, showed the lowest PCr/ATP values. The PCr concentration in muscle is approximately 6 to 8 times higher than in the brain, and ATP levels are also elevated, resulting in a PCr/ATP ratio about 2 to 3 times greater than that observed in brain tissue (38). This difference may partly affect the measured values due to the point spread function in the spatial encoding, particularly for the lateral surface of the temporal lobe, and could contribute to the elevated ratios observed in this study. To minimize signal contamination from muscle, we applied a mask excluding voxels with PCr/ATP ≥ 2. Future improvements in spatial encoding will help reduce the influence of muscle tissue.

Regional differences in kCK and T1PCr were investigated across 9 ROIs in the brain. Although no significant differences in kCK were observed between GM and WM, regional variation among the 9 ROIs revealed the highest kCK values in the occipital and parietal lobes, which are GM-enriched regions. ATP production via oxidative phosphorylation occurs in mitochondria, while ATP-consuming processes primarily take place in the cytosol. The PCr/CK system acts as a high-speed energy shuttle, transporting energy to utilization sites. The CK reaction and ATPase are tightly coupled to maintain ATP homeostasis in the brain (44). Previous PET and 13C MRS studies measuring CMRO2, CMRglc, or VTCA reported higher values in the occipital-parietal lobes compared to global brain averages [as summarized by Zhu et al. (20)]. To sustain ATP homeostasis, ATPase and CK activities are highly regulated; therefore, it is not surprising that the occipital-parietal lobes show the highest kCK values. In addition, a prior 31P MRSI study using saturation transfer experiments also revealed elevated kCK and kATPase in ROIs located in the occipital-parietal lobes compared to WM-dominated regions (20). The putamen, by contrast, showed the lowest kCK levels. In general, the human striatum, including the caudate and putamen, exhibits lower values, likely due to its enrichment with GABAergic neurons, which are less energy-demanding than glutamatergic neurons (45, 46). A previous 1D saturation transfer study reported higher kCK values in GM (0.30 s−1) compared to WM (0.16 s−1) (31), a difference that we did not observe in the present study. It is worth noting, however, that the target region in that work was the temporo-parietal cortex, thereby reflecting more localized GM-WM differences, whereas in our study kCK values were measured from global GM and WM. The paired t test also revealed a significant difference (P = 0.011) in kCK between the parietal lobe and WM in our study. Consistent with our results, previous 2D kCK mapping also revealed higher values in the temporo-parietal area (0.44 ± 0.03) compared to the frontal region (0.37 ± 0.07) (24). Given the regional heterogeneity demonstrated in our ROI analysis, such generalized tissue differences may not be representative for the entire brain. Instead, our data suggest that kCK may depend on energy demand of cell types. The significantly higher kCK in the parietal-occipital lobes compared with the putamen likely reflects differences in cell-type composition, with the putamen being enriched with inhibitory neurons. Two creatine kinase isozymes are present in the brain, i.e. cytosolic brain-type CK (BCK) and ubiquitous mitochondrial CK (uMtCK), which are not colocalized and are differentially expressed depending on the cell type (47). Immunohistochemical studies have shown that uMtCK is expressed exclusively in neurons, whereas BCK is expressed at lower levels in excitatory neurons but more prominently in astrocytes and GABAergic neurons (47). Although MR methods cannot distinguish between these two isozymes, their differential expression across cell types may allow us to probe their kinetics using ROI analyses in regions that predominantly express either uMtCK or BCK.

In this proof-of-concept study, the method was embedded in a functional task, demonstrating fCKI as a potential functional imaging modality that offers a means to inspect brain activity from a bioenergetic perspective. In a visual stimulation task, activation clusters were detected predominantly in the visual cortex. Single-subject spatial information on CK functional activation is provided. The detected 13.7% mean increase of kCK in the occipital lobe is in line with a previous single voxel study which reported 17.5% increase during visual stimulation of similar length (average changes over both blocks) (5). However, this increase is lower than the first report (34%) from Chen et al. (4) using a LED goggle flashing at 8 Hz, and higher than a recent report (6) (5%) using a 31P-MT CSI sequence. With voxel-wise analysis, a mean 29% increase in kCK was observed in the clusters located in the posterior part of the brain, which was higher than that from ROI analysis. This discrepancy is likely due to partial volume effects, as negative kCK responses were observed lateral to the positive clusters, partly in the visual cortex, which may cancel out changes in the ROI analysis. These findings suggest that fCKI can capture spatially resolved kCK responses and help avoid potential partial volume effects inherent in single-voxel fMRS studies. Reports of PCr/ATP changes in the literature have been inconsistent, with most studies not detecting significant alterations (4–6, 48, 49). In this study, we observed a −3.7% change in PCr/ATP within the occipital lobe using ROI analysis, whereas voxel-wise analysis revealed a −9.1% change in PCr/ATP within clusters showing increased kCK responses for two participants. Our results suggest that PCr/ATP changes are less pronounced than those of kCK, and that previous inconsistencies in PCr/ATP findings may reflect heterogeneity of responses within the visual cortex, highlighting the advantage of studying spatially resolved responses by fCKI. Note that all previous results were based on the group analysis. In a future study, more participants are needed to evaluate the potential of fCKI comprehensively.

The FA pattern of simple sinusoids in combination with magnetization preparation pulses has proven to be effective for the sensitivity of parameter estimation in prior MRF frameworks (27, 50). Incorporating IR and MT pulses increased the sensitivity to T1 and kCK, similar to our previous work (27). IR for T1 sensitivity was applied only to PCr due to its higher SNR relative to ATP, and the ATP acquisition pattern is repeated twice to boost SNR. For the same reason, Te was only varied in the PCr acquisition, to provide additional sensitivity to ΔB0. Although the feasibility of this pattern is demonstrated, it should not be considered optimal. Future work could increase parameter sensitivity, matching robustness, and SNR by optimizing the design of the acquisition scheme (51–53).

At ultrahigh fields, B0 inhomogeneities are a common obstacle for MRI. As bSSFP-type acquisitions are especially sensitive to changes in B0, incorporating ΔB0 in the estimation is necessary. This approach accounts for pattern changes caused by the shift in the bSSFP frequency response profile and frequency-selective pulse profile. However, inhomogeneities due to susceptibility effects in the frontal area remain challenging, as in this region signals may fall into stop bands of the bSSFP frequency response profile, leading to significant signal loss. This issue can be addressed by local shimming on the affected area, trading off B0 homogeneities in other regions, or by using additional local shim coils to mitigate the effect.

B1 inhomogeneity may present as a challenge in MRF (28, 54). Considering the similar Larmor frequency between 1H at 3 T and 31P at 7 T, and the use of a birdcage volume coil for transmission, a homogeneous transmit field is assumed in this proof of concept study, similar to the approach in the initial demonstration in 1H MRF by Ma et al. (28). However, incorporating B1 maps could significantly improve the robustness of parameter estimation (SI Appendix, Supporting Text 2 and Fig. S2). Although the acquisition of experimentally measured B1 maps could be time-consuming, our recent work indicates that 31P whole-brain B1 maps are feasible in less than 15 min, making it a practical option (55). An alternative solution could be found by optimizing the FA pattern to be less sensitive to B1 or include B1 as a parameter to be estimated (54). Incorporating B1 in the fitting is expanding the computational complexity, as the dictionary size grows exponentially with the number of parameters to fit.

For the same reason in the current approach, T2 of PCr and ATP, and T1ATP are fixed to literature values. Therefore, the matching time and computational load in dictionary generation is reduced, similar to prior work (26, 27). Simulations showed that the kCK estimation is robust to a bias between the assumption and ground truth values. The matching error stays below 13% for ±25% alteration of the fixed assumption for each of the three parameters (SI Appendix, Supporting Text 2 and Fig. S2). As mentioned in earlier publications (26, 27), no studies have investigated changes in T2 of PCr and ATP in the brain upon pathological conditions. However, there are reports of alterations in T2 of 1H metabolites in ischemic tissues and during brain development (56, 57), which leaves the debate open if phosphorus T2 values might also facing alterations in different health and pathological conditions. If the computational challenges can be addressed in the future, incorporating T2 into the matching process is expected to enhance the accuracy of parameter estimations.

The use of a two-pool (PCr-ATP) chemical exchange model simplifies the problem further. As shown in previous 31P-MRF studies (26, 27), the interaction with the inorganic phosphate pool over the ATP synthesis was mitigated. For CKI an overestimation of 6% can be expected within the in vivo range of the ATP synthase exchange rate (SI Appendix, Supporting Text 4 and Fig. S6). Including the ATPase exchange rate in future analyses could refine the model further. To do so, the input signals might need to be extended (e.g. Pi maps), potentially lengthening the acquisition time. Incorporating all parameters mentioned above will increase the complexity of the model and more sophisticated matching approaches are needed to avoid a computational overload. This could potentially be addressed by using the nested iteration interpolation method (NIIM) (27) or AI-based approaches.

The CKI is a user-friendly method with a push-button acquisition. The reconstruction and matching procedures are fully automated. The excellent robustness of the method, coupled with its ease of application, underscores the potential for its use in clinical studies. Moreover, the method is not restricted to the brain; with adjustments to the readout trajectory and pattern to accommodate different tissue properties, it can be applied to other organs.

In conclusion, CKI provides an imaging method for whole-brain kCK mapping. Offering high SNR efficiency and easy applicability with push-button acquisition and reconstruction, CKI can be used for future clinical and research applications. Applied along with a functional task, fCKI provides a new functional imaging modality. The kCK activation mapping allows access to information on brain bioenergetics underlying brain function. CKI and fCKI have the potential to enhance our understanding of brain function and neurological dysfunctions, including neurodegenerative and psychiatric disorders.

Materials and Methods

31P-MRF Sequence Design.

The FA pattern shown in Fig. 1A comprises 800 radio frequency (RF) pulses, organized into 4 sinusoids of 200 FAs each (A sin⁡(π/200⋅x)0.6). Sinusoids 1 and 3 have a maximal FA of A = 35°, while sinusoids 2 and 4 have a maximal FA of A = 25°. The maximum amplitude of the sinusoids was chosen to maximize the SNR (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B). Preceding each sinusoid, a 40 ms asymmetric inversion pulse (58) prepares the longitudinal magnetization, followed by a 2 ms delay and a 8 ms crusher gradient to spoil the transverse magnetization (Fig. 1B). The carrier frequency (at the center of the transition band) is set at +1.25 ppm for the first and −1.25 ppm for the second to the fourth preparation pulses (Fig. 1C). Inversion profiles for the first and third preparation pulses are opposite to those of the second and fourth. After the last FA of a sinusoid, a400 ms pause is added to allow partial relaxation of the longitudinal magnetization. The RF carrier frequency of excitation pulses in the first and third sinusoids is set at 0 ppm for PCr, while in the second and fourth sinusoids, it is set at −2.52 ppm for γ-ATP. Frequency selective excitation is achieved with a 10 ms Gaussian pulse (FWHM = 170 Hz). The TR alternates for PCr acquisition between TR,1 = 19.82 ms and TR,2 = TR,1+Te+ = 23.82 ms, while for ATP acquisition, it remains fixed at TR,1. The delay time between the end of the RF pulse and the start of the acquisition is Te = 410 μs, including the spatial encoding gradient in kz. Every second PCr acquisition includes an additional delay of Te+ = 4 ms before Te. The readout trajectory in kxy is a 8.61 ms (861 points) nonuniform spiral with 33% kxy coverage (Fig. 1D). This readout is followed by a 920 μs delay, including refocusing in kxy and kz. Five repetitions of the whole FA pattern are needed to measure the 5 central kz planes from the overall 11 kz planes. The outer 3 kz planes on each side are zero-filled. In total, 1:33 min is needed to acquire one 3D volume.

Dictionary Creation and Parameter Matching.

A dictionary was created using the Bloch-McConnell equations (27). A single isochromat per voxel and RF excitation with the nominal FA is assumed. 4 parameters were estimated through MR fingerprinting signal matching: the CK forward rate constant kCK, the concentration ratio Cr=MPCr0MATP0, the longitudinal relaxation time of PCr T1PCr, and the off-resonance ΔB0 for both PCr and ATP. Parameter ranges are listed in SI Appendix, Table S1. To simplify the matching problem, another 3 parameters were fixed to literature values (27, 35): T1ATP=1s,T2PCr=135ms, and T2ATP = 25 ms. Measured MRF signal evolutions and dictionary entries were processed equally before matching. Individual PCr signal evolutions were phased according to the mean phase of all odd FAs (the transient with no Te+). Individual ATP signal evolutions were phased according to the mean phase of all ATP signal evolutions. The real (400 points) and complex (400 points) parts of the PCr signal evolution and the real part of the ATP signal evolution (400 points) were concatenated, resulting in a signal evolution with 1,200 points in total. Matching was performed on the L2-norm of signals by finding the maximum inner product of the dictionary entries and measured MRF signal evolutions. Reconstruction and matching were completed in approximately 1 h on a dedicated server (Intel Xeon Silver 4216).

In Vivo Validation (CKI).

Six healthy participants (3 females; 3 males; aged 26.5 ± 3.9 y old), who provided written informed consent, were included in the study. The experiments were performed with the approval of the local ethics committee (Commission cantonale d’éthique de la recherche sur l’être humain [CER-VD]; ID: 2020-02527). MR experiments were conducted on a Siemens Terra X 7 T/80 cm MR scanner (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). Participants were scanned using a double-tuned (Tx/Rx) 31P/1H birdcage coil along with a 32 channel-Rx phased array 31P coil (RAPID Biomedical, Rimpar, Germany). Following a localizer image, a shim volume covering the whole brain was placed, and the 3D-volume shim (Siemens: GRE brain method) with intermittent frequency adjustment was applied. The mean and SD of the 1H linewidths in the shim area provided by Siemens frequency adjustment was 30.1 ± 2.3 Hz. Finally, the 31P main frequency was adjusted in the same shim volume. The MRF sequence was acquired with 32 averages, leading to a total scan time of 49:30 min including one dummy cycle. The field-of-view (FOV) was set to 230 × 230 × 220 mm3 centered at the isocenter. The matrix size of the reconstructed images was 32 × 32 × 11. Reference 1H GRE images were measured before and after the MRF protocol in the same FOV (matrix size = 128 × 128 × 11, TE = 3.37 ms, TR = 15 ms, total acquisition time of 26 s). After the 31P protocol, the coil was changed to a 1H 32 channel Nova coil (Nova Medical, Inc., MA, USA) to acquire an anatomical MP2RAGE (59) image (4:30 min, 1 mm3 isotropic, FOV 230 × 230 × 224 mm1, TI1/TI2 = 870/2,700 ms TE/TR = 2.88/6,000 ms, FA1/FA2 = 4°/5°) for tissue and ROI segmentation.

fCKI.

Three healthy participants (3 females; 30 ± 2 y old), who provided written informed consent, participated in a functional study. The experiments were performed with the approval of the local ethics committee (Commission cantonale d’éthique de la recherche sur l’étre humain [CER-VD]; ID: 2023-00370). The scan protocol of the CKI was adapted to 30 averages (46:30 min), to match the total duration of the paradigm. The paradigm is depicted in Fig. 4A and consists of alternating blocks of REST (4.65 min, 3 averages) and STIM with flashing checkerboard stimuli (60) (4.65 min, 3 averages). The participants viewed the stimuli through a mirror on a backprojection screen. Visual stimuli were full-field checkerboards, contrast reversing at 8 Hz (100% contrast). In the rest condition, a black screen with a colored fixation dot was used. In both conditions (REST and STIM), the fixation dot changed color for each average (total of 30). The participant was asked to respond to the color change with a button press (summing up all button press events = response count). The acquisition, the protocol, and the setup remained as described above (In Vivo Validation).

Reconstruction.

Reconstruction and matching were performed in MATLAB (Mathworks, Natick, Massachusetts, USA) (72). The MRF data acquired from the scanner had a tensor size of 861 × 5 × 800 × 32 × 32, where the dimensions correspond to the spiral readout (first), the kz index (second), the FA index (third), the coil index (fourth), and averages (fifth). Reconstruction of the images consisted of three steps prior to matching:

Transformation into image domain: First, a 1D Fourier transformation was applied along the slice dimension. The density compensation function (DCF) was calculated based on the Voronoi diagram (61–63). Before regridding, the DCF was multiplied by a half-periodic Hanning filter to reduce the influence of high-frequency components on the image. Regridding was performed using a Kaiser-Bessel kernel (64, 65) to transform the data into Cartesian k-space. Last, a 2D-FFT transformed the k-space data into the image domain. Images were reconstructed separately for each coil, FA index, and the kz index.

In Vivo Data Analyses.

The 31P anatomical images shown in this work from PCr and ATP are bSSFP-type magnitude images extracted from the MRF acquisition. Therefore, the signal intensity is averaged over all FA indices with the RF carrier frequency at PCr for PCr images and at ATP for ATP images. The color bars of kCK, ΔB0, and T1PCr are set according to the minimum and maximum of the dictionary boundaries. For Cr, the upper limit of the color bar was set to 2 instead of the dictionary upper bound of 6, to increase the visibility of contrast between gray and white matter.

The SNR is evaluated using the snr function of MATLAB. This function estimates the SNR in dB as the ratio between the signal power and noise power. The matched dictionary entry and the residual of the matched and the measured signal, used as noise approximation, are inputs for the function.

To investigate the changes in kCK during visual stimulation (fCKI), the percentage difference between the kCK maps of rest and stimulation was calculated. kCK maps were estimated by fixing T1 of PCr to 4.5 s (closest dictionary value to the mean T1 value of gray and white matter, see Table 1) and setting B0 to the estimate of the combined dataset of rest and stimulation during matching. Clusters (size ≥20) of an absolute percentage change ≥10% were considered for kCK, and clusters (size ≥20) of an absolute percentage change ≥5% were considered for Cr. To calculate the mean percentage difference over clusters in the posterior brain, clusters that overlap with the Occipital lobe ROI were considered. The mean value was then calculated over all voxels in those clusters. Reconstructed voxel-wise signal evolutions and maps are available in a repository (73).

Anatomical Image Segmentation.

All 1H MR image processing and analysis were performed using FSL version 6.0.7.8 (70). Brain extraction was performed on the T1-weighted images using the Brain Extraction Tool (BET) in FSL and manually on the reference 1H-GRE images using an in-house MATLAB script. BET was applied with a fractional intensity threshold of 0.5 to ensure optimal removal of nonbrain tissue.

Tissue segmentation of the brain-extracted T1-weighted images into gray and white matter was conducted using FMRIB’s Automated Segmentation Tool (FAST) in FSL. This process involved specifying the input image type as T1-weighted, requesting three tissue-type segmentation, and applying bias field correction.

To coregister the T1-weighted image and its segmented images to the reference 1H-GRE image, FMRIB’s Linear Image Registration Tool (71) (FLIRT) was employed. The T1-weighted image was registered to the 1H-GRE image using six degrees of freedom (DOF) rigid body transformation. The resulting transformation matrix was then applied to the gray matter, white matter, and CSF segmented images to achieve alignment with the 1H-GRE.

Additionally, ROI segmentation (ATLAS 152MNI) was performed. The 152MNI ATLAS T1-weighted image was coregistered to the reference 1H-GRE image. Therefore, the 152MNI ATLAS T1-weighted image was first coregistered to the participant-specific T1-weighted image with an affine registration using 12 DOFs. The participant-specific T1-weighted image was coregistered to the reference 1H-GRE image as described above. Nine secondary image transformations gave the region-wise segmentation (taken from the 152MNI atlas) of Caudate, Cerebellum, Frontal Lobe, Insula, Occipital Lobe, Parietal Lobe, Putamen, Temporal Lobe, and Thalamus. The alignment between the registered and segmented images, and the quantitative maps from CKI were assessed with visual inspection.

Statistical Analyses.

Regional differences were investigated with a one-way ANOVA test, followed by a Tukey’s post hoc test to compare all pairs of VOIs. Tissue type (gray vs. white matter) differences were tested by the paired t test. These tests were performed in the GraphPad Prism 5.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA). The intrasession reproducibility was assessed by splitting the full dataset (32 averages) of each participant into two subsamples of 16 averages.
