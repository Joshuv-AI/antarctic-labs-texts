Effective skin cancer classification by modified and optimized inception-ResNet-V2 model
Authors: K. Umapathi, R. Vanithamani, L. Priya, K. Harini, Mohsin Dadi, Saurav Dixit, Choon Kit Chan, Satish Kumar, Md Irfanul Haque Siddiqui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Artificial Intelligence tools are flourishing in biomedical diagnosis, particularly in oncology. The prediction of skin cancer from dermoscopic images using deep learning neural networks has gained importance in recent years because of their inherent early non-invasive diagnostic capabilities. This study presents the results of the classification of benign nevus and malignant melanoma lesions using a deep learning model. The model incorporates an efficient pre-processing stage powered by median filtering and a class-balancing stage powered by the Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) to improve classification results. First, the classification efficiency of four pre-trained models, namely, ResNet50, EfficientNet B0, Inception-V3, and Inception-ResNet-V2, were studied, and the results revealed that they achieved accuracies of 93.90%, 94.37%, 94.87%, and 95.77%, respectively. Second, the effect of optimization and hyperparameter tuning on the Inception-ResNet-V2 model is studied considering Adam, Nadam, and AdaMax optimizers, with fivefold cross validation. The experimental results revealed that the AdaMax optimizer with validation achieved an overall consistent performance with accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity of 97.65%, 96.67%, and 98.92%, respectively. The results support the efficacy of the model in predicting skin cancer malignancy; thus, its integration into clinical practice could benefit healthcare services.

Introduction

Skin cancer is characterized by the abnormal growth of skin cells. According to reports by the World Health Organization, the primary cause of skin cancer is exposure to ultraviolet radiation. Surveys depict that skin cancer is the 23th leading cause of death worldwide with highest mortality incidences registered in Europe1. It is estimated that, 112,690 new skin cancer cases with 14,110 deaths in the US in the year 20252. These statistics reveal the impact skin cancer has and its geographical variations across the globe. Skin cancer is broadly classified into three types depending on the type of skin cells: melanoma (MEL), basal cell carcinoma (BCC), and squamous cell carcinoma (SCC). More than 100 subcategories of skin lesions have been identified to date. Depending on the severity of the disease, skin lesions are classified as benign or malignant. As with any type of cancer, skin cancer can be a life-threatening condition when it enters amalignant state. Therefore, early diagnosis of skin cancer can save lives.

The conventional diagnosis of skin cancer starts with visual inspection of the lesion by a physician, followed by biopsy if required. Biopsied tumours carry a wound capable of pro-metastatic progression which causes mortality if surgery is delayed3. Computer-aided diagnosis of skin cancer using dermoscopic imaging is a painless alternative. Therefore, it could be used as an early diagnostic tool. The conventional diagnosis of skin lesions based on the asymmetry, border, color, diameter, and evolution (ABCDE) rule for staging skin cancer has been adapted by many computer-aided design (CAD) tools for the effective classification of cancer stages4,5. However, with the advent of artificial intelligence (AI) techniques, machines can learn the image features themselves, making diagnosis better. AI-powered automatic diagnosis of skin cancer could facilitate expert level diagnosis feasible in a non-invasive manner which is highly beneficial in clinical decision making6. The two main categories of AI algorithms are machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL). ML frameworks demand a feature extractor block after preprocessing7. While a DL method can extract features efficiently when trained with sufficient data and thus are adequately investigated for implementing dermoscopic image based skin cancer diagnosis8. In specific, pre-trained models and ensemble learning based models could achieve the desired accuracy even with minimal data9,10.

Significant challenges associated with the use of AI models for medical diagnosis relies in the insufficient, imbalanced training data, which often hurdles model performance as well as generalisation ability11. Most existing methods concentrate on using augmentation to balance the dataset and leave diversification of dataset unattended. This limits model predictions when images acquired under practical setups—which may include artifacts and are often distorted. Such a limitation hurdles practical integration of model with smart device for enabling remote diagnostic facilities. The proposed method employs artifact removal and data augmentation that addresses class imbalance and ensures robustness under practical image acquisition. Thus, enables the model to generalise well with images acquired under practical conditions (when new images are distorted due to lighting or environmental conditions, acquired in different geometries). A robust modified Inception-ResNet-V2 model is chosen for classification which ensures robustness of predictions to unseen data. Further, model generalisation and robustness to unseen data are validated by fivefold cross-validation and hyperparameter tuning. Such a model generalisation and convergence become important when the proposed method is integrated with smart device based diagnosis systems. Related work

The performance of an AI tool directly depends on the quality and quantity of the input data used for training. Thus, various pre-processing techniques adapted to improve dermoscopic image quality and their impact on segmentation are studied in literatures12. Further, segmentation of skin lesions for improving prediction efficacy are also investigated. For instance, Res-UNet model were employed for skin lesion segmentation and demonstrated to achieve a Jaccard index of 77.2% while segmenting skin lesions13. In another experiment, grab-cut segmentation, Gray-level Co-Occurrence Matrix (GLCM)-based feature extraction and ML based classification was studied. And the experimental results demonstrated the efficacy of Support Vector Machine (SVM) predictions14. Further, a recent researches work employed Adaptive snake and region growing method for lesion segmentation and SVM for classification. Such a method was demonstrated to achieve improved accuracy of predictions15. SVM classifiers demonstrate improved model performance compared to other ML models16. Though machine learning methods could achieve the desired performance in classifying skin lesions, DL methods excel in making predictions with real-world data and providing reliable results17–20. Specifically, convolutional neural network (CNN) models were demonstrated to achieve the desired prediction performance in case of image based diagnosis21.

Specifically, transfer learning techniques have emerged as a boon in the classification of skin lesions. For instance, DL models developed by adapting transfer learning from MobileNet-V2 combined with an LSTM network could classify the HAM-10000 dataset into three classes with an accuracy of 88%22. Numerous models have been developed by adapting transfer learning techniques, and have been found to be effective in classifying skin lesions23. For example, the EfficientNet architecture showed an AUC of 0.968 in identifying melanoma and non-melanoma from the ISIC-2019 and ISIC-2020 datasets24. While, AlexNet achieved classification accuracies of 96%, 97%, and 95% with the MED-NOD, Derm, and ISIC datasets, respectively25. In addition, ensemble of pre-trained networks was found to improve classification efficiency. For example, the ensemble average of forecasts made by ResNet-152, Se-ResNeXt-101, NASNet, and DenseNet-161 was demonstrated to achieve, 80% accuracy and 98% AUC in classifying the DermNet dataset and 93% accuracy and 98% AUC in classifying the ISIC dataset26. While, ensemble of ResNet-50 models achieved a top-1 accuracy of 93% in classifying the HAM-10000 dataset27. Experimental results show that modifying and tuning of pre-trained models can achieved excellent classification performance in comparison with readily using pre-trained models28.

Despite the massive use of transfer learning techniques for skin cancer diagnosis tasks, custom deep convolutional neural network (DCNN) models have been developed and demonstrated to classify benign and malignant melanomas from ISIC datasets29. For instance, a DCNN with dynamic kernels was shown to efficiently classify the HAM-10000 dataset into seven classes30. A hybrid DCNN model with three unique tailored feature extractor modules was developed and demonstrated to classify three variants of the ISIC dataset,namely, ISIC 2016, 2017, and 2018, with a good AUC31. Potential directions of research pertaining to skin cancer diagnosis is primarily motivated towards development of combining patient meta-data with screening images in order to advance AI tools, from diagnosis to prognosis32. For instance, a Convolutional Neural Network—Artificial Neural Network (CNN-ANN) model that can identify malignant and benign melanoma from the shortlisted ISIC 2019 dataset and the corresponding patient metadata was developed33. In addition, predictions considering multiple criteria were demonstrated to achieve excellent classification accuracies34. Similarly, introducing Explainable AI (XAI) techniques with model are vastly investigated to enable AI acceptance in medical applications35. For instance, Machine learning models add XAI in the form of permutation importance plots, partial dependence plots (PDP), and SHapley Additive exPlanation (SHAP) plots for skin cancer classification36. In summary, although each AI technique has unique advantages in classifying skin cancer images, pretrained models and ensemble learning techniques have shown promising results. Additionally, future AI models for medical diagnosis will be capable of providing prognostic solutions based on patient metadata and justifying their decisions with the power of XAI.

Materials and methods

Dataset

The ISIC 2019 dataset is used in this study. The native ISIC dataset was downloaded from “https://challenge.isic-archive.com/data/#2019”. This dataset has eight different classes of skin lesions. From this dataset a subset of 1065 images were obtained with 600 images pertaining to malignant melanoma class and 465 images pertaining to benign nevi class. Along with these images a binary coded ground truth. “csv” file is used for training the model. This dataset is split in 80:20 ratio for training and testing the model. Stratified sampling is adapted for ensuring equal test and train split. Further, the training data is augmented for diversification of the input data that represent images obtained under various environmental conditions. The testing data is not augmented in order mimic real practical images.

Noise removal

Dermoscopic images are often corrupted by hairlines, air bubbles, ink markings, and black frames, which hinder effective image analysis. Thus, artifact removal techniques such as edge detection, inpainting, region expansion, and intensity thresholding are often used to improve image quality and acquire clearer images that better suit precise skin lesion investigation and identification.

Data augmentation

Medical image datasets are often imbalanced because of the rarity of the disease and availability of variants. Imbalanced datasets, when used directly for training AI models, result in biased classification towards the majority class, and thus seek attention. Class imbalances are addressed by different methods, such as (i) weighted class learning, where the loss function is weighted to give importance to the minority class, (ii) oversampling the minority classes, and (iii) under sampling or removing samples from the majority classes. However, removing the samples may lead to underfitting; thus, oversampling is typically adopted. Simple data duplication may result in overfitting; thus, data-interpolation-based synthetic data generation was adapted for oversampling minority classes.

Residual networks

As the neural networks deepened, the network’s learning is affected by the vanishing gradient (VG) problem. Repeated multiplication of derivatives of activation function results in exponentially small gradients at the end of forward pass. This extremely smaller gradient when multiplied with the calculated loss results in a very smaller value which when backpropagated makes only minimal changes (than required) to model weights that directly slowed down or stopped learning process. However, skipping intermediate layers using identity blocks enables the network to learn the residual function instead of the direct relationship between the input and output. This arrangement helps overcome the vanishing gradient problem with deeper networks and aids in faster model convergence. Let the input be ‘x’ and the corresponding output distribution be h(x). Inception networks

Inception networks, in simpler terms, can be defined as network of networks. An inception network is built using multiple inception modules. Each inception module learns the input features by applying multiple convolutional filters in parallel and concatenating their output. An important building block in the inception module is the 1 × 1 convolution block, also known as the projection block, which performs channel-wise convolution, and thus, reduces the depth of the feature map. In addition, the module includes a max-pooling layer that learns the maximum value from a set of nonoverlapping regions of the input. Inception modules and, thus, inception networks simultaneously improve feature learning and model convergence, and avoid overfitting. The sample inception module is illustrated in Fig. 3.Fig. 3Basic inception module.

Projection block: The input and output dimensions of the projection block differ. It has a convolution layer with a (1 × 1) kernel and stride (2 × 2) to change the input depth and match it with the output depth. This reduced the number of feature maps.

Pre-processing & Data augmentation

One of the major sources of noise in dermoscopic images is hair lines and clinical markings, which hinder the efficiency of classification. These noises often appear as thin lines in dermoscopic images.

Artifact removal: Median filtering is employed for removing artifacts from the acquired images. Median filters, which are efficient in preserving edges while removing noise, can easily differentiate these dark line artifacts from the lesion boundaries. The median filter replaces each pixel with the median of that pixel and its neighbouring pixels. Thus, when a median filter encounters pixels belonging to the hairline or clinical marking (which are very thin), it replaces them with the median of their neighbourhood, resulting in the removal of dark lines by skin color. This improves image quality and facilitates efficient feature extraction, optimized model performance. Modified inception-ResNet-V2 architecture

Inception-ResNet-V2 is a pretrained CNN model developed using inception blocks and residual connections. Residual blocks enable training deep neural networks efficiently by overcoming the vanishing gradients. Specifically, skip connections enable learning the residue that is needed to produce the required output. Further, these networks facilitate faster model convergence along with the desired accuracy. In addition, Inception blocks with parallel layers enable faster feature extraction by processing multi-scale features simultaneously. This facilitates extraction of both global and local features with reduced computational complexity. Residual connections ensure faster model convergence and avoid the vanishing gradient problem, whereas inception blocks avoid model ensure a balance between feature depth and efficiency by imparting parallel learning layers. Both, residual networks and Inception networks aim to achieve deeper learning—for accurate feature extraction, without overfitting, even with minimal dataset. Thus, the hybrid Inception-ResNet-V2, provide a powerful learning network, which is robust and generalises well to unseen real world data. The architecture of Inception-ResNet-V2 is shown in Fig. 5.Fig. 5Architecture of inception-ResNet-V2.

Pre-trained models are an efficient choice for classifying imbalanced medical data, as these models have optimized model parameters previously trained on large image datasets such as ImageNet. Further, re-training the model for the custom dataset optimizes their parameters. The modifications carried out to the base model are represented by colourless blocks in the Inception-ResNet-V2 block (left-end). The Modified Inception-ResNet-V2 is defined by replacing the topmost layer with an average pooling layer, followed by a flatten layer, dense layer with rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation, dropout layer, and output dense layer with SoftMax activation. Introduction of pooling layer reduces the size of the feature map, which is significant for capturing the most important features readily. Specifically, average pooling works by dividing its input feature map into (N) number of non-overlapping blocks. The pixel values pertaining to each block is calculated. These average values together form the new feature map. Thus, the feature map that results from average pooling will have only ‘N’ pixels in it. Diagrammatic representation of working of average pooling layer with a numerical example of average pooling function is given in Fig. 6a. There are two parameters that determine the size and value of the resulting feature map. One is the kernel size which depicts the number of pixels to be averaged and the stride which depicts the number of pixels to skip after each average calculation.Fig. 6Schematic representation layers in Modified Inception-ResNet-V2 model, (a) averaging pooling layer functions, (b) drop-out layer functions.

The pooling layer is followed by a flatten layer to convert the feature map to a single vector and then is fed to the dense layer. This dense layer and the output dense layer are connected through a drop-out layer in order to force a robust learning. The dropout layer randomly disables some of its neurons in order to force the network to learn robustly. The dropout layer randomly deactivates a fraction of its neurons in each iteration and the modifies network structure dynamically during each iteration in order to ensure a robust learning and model generalisation. Model optimisation & Hyperparameter tuning

The proposed modified Inception-ResNet-V2 model is optimized using three optimizers, namely Adam, Nadam, and AdaMax, along with fivefold cross validation, to study the impact of optimization and tuning on the model performance. Validation carried out at the end of eachtraining epoch to ensures model generalisation to unseen data. Training phase involves, training models of different hyperparameters (here learning rate) to obtain multiple models each with their own weights and biases. Validation involves holding out a portion of training dataset and testing each of this model on the validation data. In this process, validation accuracy is compared to tune the model parameter (weight & biases) of each of the model (with their own specific hyperparameter) and the best performing model, which holds the most optimal model weights and hyperparameters is saved for evaluation phase. In simple programming terms this resembles a two level loop function where the hyperparameters are control variables in outer loop and model parameter namely weights and biases are control variables of inner loop. This validation phase introduces unseen data to the model, and checks model performance to unseen data. This process is repeated until there is no improvement in validation accuracy or till the maximum number of epochs, whichever is earlier. This study uses fivefold cross validation technique along with random search based hyperparameter tuning in order to obtain the best performing model. Further, as the input dataset is imbalanced, a stratified sampling is employed while splitting data. In the present study, the batch size and epochs are fixed and learning rate alone is tuned for efficacy. The model performance improvement with optimization and tuning, and the results in comparison with other pre-trained models and existing DL models, are discussed in the next section. Comparison of area under the receiver operating characteristics curve

The AUC-ROC is a measure of how well a model can discriminate between classes. An ROC curve was obtained by plotting the TP rate against the FP rate for the different decision thresholds. The ROC plot has a 45O-reference line which indicates the model’s differentiating power against random chance. The curve can be interpreted by studying the AUC range, the upper left corner of the curve, its curvature, and its closeness to the 45O reference line. An ROC curve with an AUC close to 0.5, will result in an ROC curve close to the reference line, indicating the model’s inability to discriminate samples. An AUC in the range of 0.7–0.9 will appear well above the reference line and indicates that the model could discriminate between positive and negative samples well. Similarly, the upper left corner is close to 0.8, 0.9, and indicates good model performance. This curve creates a right-angle at the left corner. Conclusion

This study showed that the Inception-ResNet-V2 architecture combined with optimization methods, such as Adam, Nadam, and AdaMax, can effectively classify skin cancer stages. By utilizing the characteristics of deep learning and adjusting model parameters, it is possible to precisely differentiate between malignant and benign skin lesions. The models Inception-ResNet-V2 with Adam, Nadam, and AdaMax showed accuracies of 95.77%, 96.24%, and 97.65%, respectively. These results highlight the potential of the proposed model as a useful tool in dermatological applications. However, model robustness can be further improved by training and assessing the model with more diverse datasets. In addition, explainable AI can be introduced into the model using a suitable method. These requirements will be addressed in future studies.
