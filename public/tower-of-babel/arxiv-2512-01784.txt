On the origin and properties of dipolar recoil force and torque
Sebastian Golat, Nathaniel Levy, Francisco J. Rodríguez-Fortuño

Abstract
The recoil optical force and torque acting on an electromagnetic dipole are typically derived by computing the imbalance in radiated linear and angular electromagnetic momentum coming from the source, using Maxwell stress tensor integration. This quantifies the recoil’s outcome without revealing its physical origin or the underlying forces that produce it. The recoil force and torque exist even in the absence of external illumination, such that an isolated dipole emitter can experience them. In contrast to the other terms, the recoil terms are odd under time reversal. To clarify their nature and properties, we re-derive the recoil force from first principles using the total Lorentz force on a system of charges that form a simultaneous electric and magnetic dipole. The results agree with the standard momentum-based derivation and reveal their ultimate origin from retardation effects—arising from the finite speed of light—in the mutual interactions between charges. This result provides fundamental insight into the recoil mechanism, offering a clearer conceptual foundation for future theoretical and experimental studies of light-induced forces.

III
Derivation of recoil terms from the Lorentz force

Our aim is to compute the dipolar recoil force from first principles, using the Lorentz force between charges, in order to clearly understand its origin and its time-odd behaviour. This requires using a model of charges and/or currents that implements an electric and magnetic dipole. Doing it in general would be a very cumbersome analytical procedure. Instead, we choose a specific scenario (circularly polarised orthogonal but coplanar electric and magnetic dipoles, realised via co-rotating electric and magnetic point charges) that will greatly simplify the analytical calculation of the Lorentz force, while still allowing us to discern the origin of the recoil force and its time-symmetry. The correctness and validity of the recoil force is not in question, as it has been derived in the past from fundamental momentum conservation arguments, so we do not need to test it generally. Instead we wish to understand its microscopic origin, so considering a specific example is enough for this purpose.

IV
Conclusions

In this work, we have rederived the recoil optical force acting on an electromagnetic dipole directly from the Lorentz force between the constituent moving charges, rather than relying on the Maxwell stress tensor. This first-principles approach reproduces the established recoil terms while clarifying their physical origin and symmetry properties under time reversal.

We demonstrated that the recoil force naturally arises from the retarded interactions between the electric and magnetic charges forming the dipoles, with the asymmetry between leading and trailing charges being essential to generate a net non-zero Lorentz force. This retardation breaks time-reversal symmetry, making the recoil term odd under time reversal, in contrast with the interaction terms, which are even. As a result, the recoil force behaves analogously to dissipative forces, reflecting the irreversible conversion of mechanical energy into radiated electromagnetic momentum.

Furthermore, the comparison of time-reversal symmetries across power, force, and torque reveals a consistent physical picture. While the force and power contain both even and odd terms under time reversal, the torque’s interaction and recoil components are both even. In the physical sense, this corresponds to the recoil torque opposing the system’s angular momentum in the forward-time process, while in the time-reversed picture the torque acts in the same direction as the rotation, causing it to accelerate. This symmetry relationship is consistent with intuition about how the system’s rotational and translational motions evolve under time reversal.

Altogether, our derivation connects the microscopic Lorentz-force picture with the macroscopic recoil phenomena, providing clear physical insight into how an isolated Huygens dipole exchanges momentum and angular momentum with its own radiation. These results consolidate the understanding of recoil forces as self-induced yet momentum-conserving effects, relevant to self-propelling and radiation-driven dipolar systems.

{\bf\it\mathcal{r}}_{ij}(t)={\bf\it r}_{j}(t)-{\bf\it r}_{i}(t-\Delta{t}_{ij})=2R\sin(\frac{\upDelta\alpha_{ij}-\omega\upDelta{t}_{ij}}{2})\left[\sin(\omega{t}+\frac{\upDelta\alpha_{ij}-\omega\upDelta{t}_{ij}}{2})\hat{{\bf\it x}}-\cos(\omega{t}+\frac{\upDelta\alpha_{ij}-\omega\upDelta{t}_{ij}}{2})\hat{{\bf\it y}}\right],

this means that
eq.

19
can be rewritten for phase retardation as:

\omega\Delta{t}_{ij}=2\beta\absolutevalue{\sin(\frac{\upDelta\alpha_{ij}-\omega\upDelta{t}_{ij}}{2})}\,,

(20)

where we used the fact that the orbital velocity
v=\beta c=\omega R
.
We will want to take the dipole limit (where the size of the system is much smaller than the wavelength); this is essentially the limit when the orbital speed is non-relativistic:

kR=\frac{2\pi R}{\lambda}=\frac{\omega R}{c}\ll 1\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\beta\ll 1\,.

(21)

Since the retarded phase vanishes when
\beta=0
we can expect it to have the following Taylor expansion:

\omega\Delta{t}_{ij}=a_{1}\beta+a_{2}\beta^{2}+a_{3}\beta^{3}+a_{4}\beta^{4}+\order{\beta^5}\,,

(22)

where we expand to the fourth order since
eq.

52
is of order
k^{4}=(\beta/R)^{4}
.
We can plug this expansion into
eq.

20
, expand the sine function, and then solve order by order for coefficients
a_{n}
:

\displaystyle a_{1}

\displaystyle=2\absolutevalue{\sin(\tfrac{1}{2}{\upDelta\alpha_{ij}})}\,,

\displaystyle a_{2}

\displaystyle=-\sin(\upDelta\alpha_{ij})\,,

\displaystyle a_{3}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\quantity[1+3\cos({\upDelta\alpha_{ij}})]\absolutevalue{\sin(\tfrac{1}{2}{\upDelta\alpha_{ij}})}\,,

\displaystyle a_{4}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{3}\quantity[\sin(\upDelta\alpha_{ij})-2\sin(2\upDelta\alpha_{ij})]\,.

The retarded phase for the opposite charge
\Delta\alpha_{ij}=\pi
is:

\omega\Delta{t}_{ij}(\pi)=2\beta-\beta^{3}+\order{\beta^5}\,.

(23)

This reproduces the expected result that, at the lowest order, we have precisely the distance between the charges
c\Delta{t}_{ij}(\pi)\approx 2R
.
For neighbouring charges separated by
\Delta\alpha_{ij}=\pm\pi/2
, we have:

\omega\Delta{t}_{ij}\quantity(\pm\frac{\pi}{2})=\sqrt{2}\beta\mp\beta^{2}+\frac{\beta^{3}}{2\sqrt{2}}\pm\frac{\beta^{4}}{3}+\order{\beta^5}\,,

(24)

which is again at the lowest order
c\Delta{t}_{ij}(\pm{\pi}/{2})\approx R\sqrt{2}
matching the expected result. A crucial point is that the even orders have opposite signs for the leading and trailing charges, therefore the two will have different retarded times
\Delta{t}_{ij}\quantity({\pi}/{2})\neq\Delta{t}_{ij}\quantity(-{\pi}/{2})
, which means that the force on the leading and trailing neighbouring charges will be different. This difference is ultimately responsible for a net recoil force in the system, as we will show in the next section.

Appendix B
Lorentz force of a Huygens dipole from first principles

This supplementary will derive the Lorentz force of a circularly-polarised Huygens dipole from first principles using the retarded fields
eq.

11
. The fields produced by an electric charge
q_{i}
and experienced by a charge
q_{j}
are as follows:

{\bf\it\mathcal{E}}_{\text{e},i}({\bf\it r}_{j},t)=\frac{q_{i}}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}}\frac{|{\bf\it\mathcal{r}}_{ij}|}{({{\bf\it\mathcal{r}}_{ij}\dotproduct{\bf\it u}_{ij}})^{3}}[\underbrace{(c^{2}-v^{2}){\bf\it u}_{ij}}_{\text{near field}}+\underbrace{{{\bf\it\mathcal{r}}}_{ij}\times({\bf\it u}_{ij}\times{\bf\it a}_{i})}_{\text{far field}}]\Big|_{\mathrm{ret}}\,,\quad{\bf\it\mathcal{B}}_{\text{e},i}({\bf\it r}_{j},t)=\frac{1}{c}[\hat{{\bf\it\mathcal{r}}}_{ij}\crossproduct{\bf\it\mathcal{E}}_{\text{e},i}({\bf\it r}_{j},t)]\Big|_{\mathrm{ret}}\,,

(25)

and similarly, a magnetic charge
q_{i}
will produce the fields:

{\bf\it\mathcal{B}}_{\text{m},i}({\bf\it r}_{j},t)=\frac{\mu_{0}q_{i}}{4\pi}\frac{|{\bf\it\mathcal{r}}_{ij}|}{({{\bf\it\mathcal{r}}_{ij}\dotproduct{\bf\it u}_{ij}})^{3}}[\underbrace{(c^{2}-v^{2}){\bf\it u}_{ij}}_{\text{near field}}+\underbrace{{{\bf\it\mathcal{r}}}_{ij}\times({\bf\it u}_{ij}\times{\bf\it a}_{i})}_{\text{far field}}]\Big|_{\mathrm{ret}}\,,\quad{\bf\it\mathcal{E}}_{\text{m},i}({\bf\it r},t)=-c[\hat{{\bf\it\mathcal{r}}}_{ij}\crossproduct{\bf\it\mathcal{B}}_{\text{m},i}({\bf\it r}_{j},t)]\Big|_{\mathrm{ret}}\,,

(26)

where the retarded separation vector
{\bf\it\mathcal{r}}_{ij}
is given by

{\bf\it\mathcal{r}}_{ij}(t)={\bf\it r}_{j}(t)-{\bf\it r}_{i}(t-\Delta{t}_{ij}),

(27)

We recall
{\bf\it r}_{j}(t)
is the position vector of the test charge at the observation point, and
{\bf\it r}_{i}(t-\Delta{t}_{ij})
is the position of the retarded source charge that produces a field at point
{\bf\it r}_{j}(t)
. We define the vector
{\bf\it u}_{ij}
as

{\bf\it u}_{ij}(t)=c{\hat{{\bf\it\mathcal{r}}}}_{ij}(t)-{\bf\it v}_{i}(t-\Delta{t}_{ij}),

(28)

where the retarded separation unit vector is
\hat{{\bf\it\mathcal{r}}}_{ij}={\bf\it\mathcal{r}}_{ij}/|{\bf\it\mathcal{r}}_{ij}|
, the retarded source velocity is
{\bf\it v}_{i}={\derivative{t}}{\bf\it r}_{i}
, and the retarded acceleration is
{\bf\it a}_{i}={\derivative{t}}{\bf\it v}_{i}
. We will also use the generalised Lorentz force equation to determine the force experienced by each charge

{\bf\it F}_{ij}({\bf\it r}_{j},t)=q_{\text{e},j}[{\bf\it\mathcal{E}}_{i}({\bf\it r}_{j},t)+{\bf\it v}_{j}\times{\bf\it\mathcal{B}}_{i}({\bf\it r}_{j},t)]+q_{\text{m},j}[{\bf\it\mathcal{B}}_{i}({\bf\it r}_{j},t)-\varepsilon_{0}\mu_{0}{\bf\it v}_{j}\times{\bf\it\mathcal{E}}_{i}({\bf\it r}_{j},t)],

(29)

where
{\bf\it v}_{j}
in this case is the velocity of a charge at the observation point
{\bf\it r}_{j}(t)
, and we must consider the total electric field
{\bf\it\mathcal{E}}_{i}={\bf\it\mathcal{E}}_{\text{e},i}+{\bf\it\mathcal{E}}_{\text{m},i}
and total magnetic field
{\bf\it\mathcal{B}}_{i}={\bf\it\mathcal{B}}_{\text{e},i}+{\bf\it\mathcal{B}}_{\text{m},i}
. However, since our system has no particles with both electric and magnetic charge, both
{\bf\it\mathcal{E}}_{i}
and
{\bf\it\mathcal{B}}_{i}
will only have one of the two (e/m) contributions.

B.1
Determining the General Force Expression

Since each of the four charges in the Huygens dipole exert forces on each of the other charges and themselves, the total force of the Huygens dipole will consist of a sum of sixteen terms denoted by the following:

{\bf\it F}=\sum_{i}\sum_{j}{\bf\it F}_{ij}\quad\text{where}\quad i,j\in\{+,-,\textsc{n},\textsc{s}\}\,.

(30)

Note that by symmetry, it can be determined that the electric-electric and magnetic-magnetic forces will be equal and opposite and hence sum to zero:

{\bf\it F}_{+-}+{\bf\it F}_{-+}={\bf\it F}_{\textsc{ns}}+{\bf\it F}_{\textsc{sn}}=0.

(31)

Likewise, we can demonstrate that the sum of the four Abraham-Lorentz self-forces of each of the charges of the Huygens dipole in
fig.

1
will equal to zero. First, note that the Abraham-Lorentz formula reads:

{\bf\it F}^{\text{e}}_{\text{self}}=\frac{q_{\text{e}}^{2}}{6\pi\varepsilon_{0}c^{3}}\dot{{\bf\it a}},\quad\mbox{or}\quad{\bf\it F}^{\text{m}}_{\text{self}}=\frac{\mu_{0}q_{\text{m}}^{2}}{6\pi c^{3}}\dot{{\bf\it a}}

(32)

where
\dot{{\bf\it a}}
is the first time derivative of the acceleration of the charge in question. At any instant, it can be shown that the
\dot{{\bf\it a}}
vectors for the electric charges (
\dot{{\bf\it a}}_{++}=-\dot{{\bf\it a}}_{--}
) and the
\dot{{\bf\it a}}
vectors of the magnetic charges (
\dot{{\bf\it a}}_{\textsc{nn}}=-\dot{{\bf\it a}}_{\textsc{ss}}
) are equal and opposite, therefore, the four Abraham-Lorentz self-forces of the Huygens dipole will sum to zero:

{\bf\it F}_{++}+{\bf\it F}_{--}={\bf\it F}_{\textsc{nn}}+{\bf\it F}_{\textsc{ss}}=0.

(33)

Thus, we are left with only the four electric-magnetic and the four magnetic-electric force contributions with a non-zero sum.

This would not be the case if the effects of retardation were not accounted for since the distance between the leading and trailing charges to a reference charge would be equal (see
fig.

3
). Due to this, and the fact that leading and trailing charges have opposite signs, the force on the reference charge due to the leading charge would be equal and opposite to that of the trailing charge and hence the total sum of the forces of the rotating Huygens dipole would be zero. If retardation is taken into account however, then the retarded distance between the leading and trailing charges to the reference charge is no longer equal (see
figs.

2
and
3
), hence, the forces due to them experienced by the reference charge are no longer equal and opposite, and therefore the sum of these forces is non-zero. Following the method laid out above, and using Wolfram Mathematica to handle the algebraic steps in
sections

B.2
and
B.3
, we find the final force expression to be:

{\bf\it F}=-\frac{q_{e}q_{m}}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}c}\frac{8\beta^{4}}{3R^{2}}\hat{{\bf\it z}}.

(34)

This net force originates from and acts on the system itself, thus violating the law of conservation of mechanical momentum and therefore Newton’s third law. Total momentum is conserved however as this arrangement of charges radiates away electromagnetic momentum that exactly accounts for the loss of mechanical momentum of the system.

B.2
Electric-to-magnetic and Magnetic-to-electric contributions

The force contributions that do not cancel are the following:

{\bf\it F}=\underbrace{\overbrace{{\bf\it F}_{+\textsc{n}}+{\bf\it F}_{-\textsc{s}}}^{\text{trailing}}+\overbrace{{\bf\it F}_{+\textsc{s}}+{\bf\it F}_{-\textsc{n}}}^{\text{leading}}}_{\text{Electric-to-magnetic}}+\underbrace{\overbrace{{\bf\it F}_{\textsc{n}+}+{\bf\it F}_{\textsc{s}-}}^{\text{leading}}+\overbrace{{\bf\it F}_{\textsc{s}+}+{\bf\it F}_{\textsc{n}-}}^{\text{trailing}}}_{\text{Magnetic-to-electric}}

(35)

and they can be separated into groups depending on which type of charge (electric or magnetic) is the source and depending on whether the souce charge is leading or trailing. One can use the second half of
eq.

25
and
eq.

29
to show that the electric-to-magnetic contributions (electric source charge
q_{i}
and magnetic target charge
q_{j}
) will be of the form:

{\bf\it F}{\kern 0.50003pt}^{\text{e}}_{ij}=\frac{q_{j}}{c^{2}}(c^{2}{\bf\it\mathcal{B}}_{ij}-{\bf\it v}_{j}\times{\bf\it\mathcal{E}}_{ij})=\frac{q_{j}}{c^{2}}(c\hat{{\bf\it\mathcal{r}}}_{ij}-{\bf\it v}_{j})\times{\bf\it\mathcal{E}}_{ij}\,,

(36)

where
{\bf\it\mathcal{E}}_{ij}={\bf\it\mathcal{E}}_{i}({\bf\it r}_{j})
and
{\bf\it\mathcal{B}}_{ij}={\bf\it\mathcal{B}}_{i}({\bf\it r}_{j})
. Similarly,
eq.

26
and
eq.

29
lead to magnetic-to-electric components (magnetic source
q_{i}
and electric target
q_{j}
) having a very similar expression:

{\bf\it F}{\kern 0.50003pt}^{\text{m}}_{ij}={q_{j}}({\bf\it\mathcal{E}}_{ij}+{\bf\it v}_{j}\times{\bf\it\mathcal{B}}_{ij})=-{q_{j}}(c\hat{{\bf\it\mathcal{r}}}_{ij}-{\bf\it v}_{j})\times{\bf\it\mathcal{B}}_{ij}\,.

(37)

Notice that we have added a superscript to the force to indicate whether it is electric-to-magnetic (
{\bf\it F}{\kern 0.50003pt}^{\text{e}}_{ij}
) or magnetic-to-electric component (
{\bf\it F}{\kern 0.50003pt}^{\text{m}}_{ij}
).
The reason we have done that is that if we directly substitute for
{\bf\it\mathcal{E}}_{ij}
in
eq.

36
from
eq.

25
and likewise
{\bf\it\mathcal{B}}_{ij}
in
eq.

37
from
eq.

26
we obtain a general expression:

{\bf\it F}{\kern 0.50003pt}^{\text{e,m}}_{ij}=\sigma_{\text{e,m}}\frac{\mu_{0}{q}_{i}{q}_{j}}{4\pi}\frac{(c\hat{{\bf\it\mathcal{r}}}_{ij}-{\bf\it v}_{j})\times[{(c^{2}-v^{2}){\bf\it u}_{ij}}+{{{\bf\it\mathcal{r}}}_{ij}\times({\bf\it u}_{ij}\times{\bf\it a}_{i})}]}{|{\bf\it\mathcal{r}}_{ij}|^{2}({\hat{{\bf\it\mathcal{r}}}_{ij}\dotproduct{\bf\it u}_{ij}})^{3}}\,,

(38)

where
\sigma_{\text{e}}=1
and
\sigma_{\text{m}}=-1
is a sign that depends on whether the source
q_{i}
is an electric or magnetic charge, respectively. At this stage, the best we can do is explicitly evaluate
eq.

38
for a pair of charges. As a reminder from
appendix

A
, we can describe the position of any test charge as:

{\bf\it r}_{j}(t)=R[\cos(\omega t)\hat{{\bf\it x}}+\sin(\omega t)\hat{{\bf\it y}}]\,,

and the retarded position of source charge as

{\bf\it r}_{i}(t-\Delta{t}_{ij})=R[\cos(\omega{t}-\omega\upDelta{t}_{ij}+\upDelta\alpha_{ij})\hat{{\bf\it x}}+\sin(\omega{t}-\omega\upDelta{t}_{ij}+\upDelta\alpha_{ij})\hat{{\bf\it y}}]\,,

their separation will be

{\bf\it\mathcal{r}}_{ij}(t)={\bf\it r}_{j}(t)-{\bf\it r}_{i}(t-\Delta{t}_{ij})=2R\sin(\frac{\upDelta\alpha_{ij}-\omega\upDelta{t}_{ij}}{2})\left[\sin(\omega{t}+\frac{\upDelta\alpha_{ij}-\omega\upDelta{t}_{ij}}{2})\hat{{\bf\it x}}-\cos(\omega{t}+\frac{\upDelta\alpha_{ij}-\omega\upDelta{t}_{ij}}{2})\hat{{\bf\it y}}\right],

and the remaining vectors are defined in terms of these:

{\bf\it v}_{i}=\derivative{\vc{r}_i}{t},\quad{\bf\it v}_{j}={\derivative{\vc{r}_j}{t}},\quad{\bf\it a}_{i}={\derivative{\vc{v}_i}{t}},\quad{\bf\it u}_{ij}=c{\hat{{\bf\it\mathcal{r}}}}_{ij}-{\bf\it v}_{i},\quad\hat{{\bf\it\mathcal{r}}}_{ij}=\frac{{\bf\it\mathcal{r}}_{ij}}{|{\bf\it\mathcal{r}}_{ij}|}\,.

which can all be substituted into
eq.

38
. After lengthy algebra handled by Wolfram Mathematica, we arrive at the expression:

{\bf\it F}{\kern 0.50003pt}^{\text{e,m}}_{ij}=\sigma_{\text{e,m}}\frac{{q}_{i}{q}_{j}}{4\pi c\varepsilon_{0}R^{2}}\hat{{\bf\it z}}\underbrace{\quantity[{\sin(\upDelta\alpha_{ij}-\omega\upDelta{t}_{ij})+\frac{2}{\beta}\abs{\sin(\frac{\upDelta\alpha_{ij}-\omega\upDelta{t}_{ij}}{2})}}]^{-1}}_{\displaystyle f_{ij}(\Delta\alpha_{ij})}.

(39)

Equation

39
together with the
\beta
expansion for
\Delta{t}_{ij}(\Delta\alpha_{ij})
in
eq.

22
can now be used to obtain every non-zero term of the force. At this point, we can appreciate the fact that each of these terms is independent of
t
and depends only on
\Delta\alpha_{ij}-\omega\Delta{t}_{ij}
, so we can equate the instantaneous force on the system
{\bf\it F}
with its time average
\langle{\bf\it F}\rangle
. In all of these terms the charges are separated by
\Delta\alpha_{ij}=\pm\pi/2
leading to the expansion:

f_{ij}\quantity(\pm\frac{\pi}{2})=\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{2}}-\frac{\beta^{3}}{4\sqrt{2}}\pm\frac{\beta^{4}}{3}+\order{\beta^5}\,,

(40)

Finally, we can obtain the force due to trailing charges, which are all equal to:

{\bf\it F}_{+\textsc{n}}={\bf\it F}_{-\textsc{s}}={\bf\it F}_{\textsc{s}+}={\bf\it F}_{\textsc{n}-}=\frac{{q}_{\text{e}}{q}_{\text{m}}}{4\pi\varepsilon_{0}cR^{2}}f_{ij}\quantity(-\frac{\pi}{2})\hat{{\bf\it z}}\,,

(41)

and similarly leading charges, which are equal:

{\bf\it F}_{\textsc{n}+}={\bf\it F}_{\textsc{s}-}={\bf\it F}_{+\textsc{s}}={\bf\it F}_{-\textsc{n}}=-\frac{{q}_{\text{e}}{q}_{\text{m}}}{4\pi\varepsilon_{0}cR^{2}}f_{ij}\quantity(\frac{\pi}{2})\hat{{\bf\it z}}\,,

(42)

and indeed they are not equal, nor opposite as
f_{ij}\quantity(-\frac{\pi}{2})\neq\pm f_{ij}\quantity(\frac{\pi}{2})
. Adding all of these terms together leads to the final expression for the total force:

{\bf\it F}=-\frac{{q}_{\text{e}}{q}_{\text{m}}}{\pi\varepsilon_{0}cR^{2}}\quantity[f_{ij}\qty(\frac{\pi}{2})-f_{ij}\qty(-\frac{\pi}{2})]\hat{{\bf\it z}}=-\frac{{q}_{\text{e}}{q}_{\text{m}}}{\pi\varepsilon_{0}cR^{2}}\quantity[\frac{2\beta^4}{3}+\order{\beta^5}]\hat{{\bf\it z}}\,,

(43)

which in the dipolar limit (
R\ll\lambda
, hence
\beta=v/c=2\pi R/\lambda\ll 1
) agrees with the well-known
eq.

52
obtained for this setup from the usual expression derived from Maxwell’s stress tensor.

B.3
Determining the Near and Far Field Contributions

We can repeat the calculation for the near and far field components of
eqs.

25
and
26
separately:

{\bf\it F}=\frac{{q}_{\text{e}}{q}_{\text{m}}}{\pi\varepsilon_{0}cR^{2}}\Big[\underbrace{\quantity(\beta^2-\frac{19\beta^4}{6})}_{\text{near field}}\underbrace{\mathop{-}\quantity(\beta^2-\frac{5\beta^4}{2})}_{\text{far field}}+\order{\beta^5}\Big]\hat{{\bf\it z}}\,,

(44)

doing so we can see that while they partly cancel out, they don’t cancel out completely, and both contribute to the total force.

Appendix C
Dipole phasors

C.1
Force and torque

The relationship between a time harmonic physical quantity
{\bf\it\mathcal{A}}(t)
and its phasor representation
{\bf\it A}
is

{\bf\it\mathcal{A}}(t)=\Re({\bf\it A}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega t})\,.

One of the many advantages of this representation is that it allows finding time-averaged quantities (quadratic in fields) such as power, force, torque, etc. The time average is performed over one period
T=2\pi/\omega
, for example if
{\bf\it F}(t)
is an instantaneous force, then
\expectationvalue{\vc{F}}=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}{\bf\it F}(t)\differential{t}
. One can show that if you have two time-harmonic fields
{\bf\it\mathcal{A}}
and
{\bf\it\mathcal{B}}
with phasor representations
{\bf\it A}
and
{\bf\it B}
we have:

\expectationvalue{\vc{\mathcal{A}}\cp\vc{\mathcal{B}}}=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}{\bf\it\mathcal{A}}(t)\crossproduct{\bf\it\mathcal{B}}(t)\differential{t}=\frac{1}{2}\Re({\bf\it A}^{*}\crossproduct{\bf\it B})\,.

(45)

Using the phasor representation of dipoles, the recoil force, torque, and scattered power are

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\vc{F}_\text{rec}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{k^{4}}{12\pi\varepsilon_{0}c}\Re({\bf\it p}^{*}\crossproduct{\bf\it m})\,,

(46)

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\vc{\Gamma}_\text{rec}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{k^{3}}{12\pi}\Im\quantity(\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}\vc{p}^*\!\cp\vc{p}+\mu_0\vc{m}^*\!\cp\vc{m}),

(47)

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{P_\text{sca}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega k^{3}}{12\pi}\quantity(\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}\abs{\vc{p}}^2\!+\!\mu_0\abs{\vc{m}}^2).

(48)

C.2
Spinning Huygens dipole

The electric and magnetic dipoles placed at right angle with each other, spinning in the anticlockwise sense, can be represented by the following phasors:

{\bf\it p}=q_{\text{e}}(2R)({\hat{{\bf\it x}}+\mathrm{i}\hat{{\bf\it y}}})=q_{\text{e}}{\bf\it d}\,,\quad{\bf\it m}=q_{\text{m}}(2R)({\hat{{\bf\it y}}-\mathrm{i}\hat{{\bf\it x}}})=-\mathrm{i}q_{\text{m}}{\bf\it d}\,,

(49)

where
d=2R
is the separation of the two opposite charges and
q_{\text{e}}
,
q_{\text{m}}
are respectively the electric and magnetic charges. It is worth noticing that the phasor
(\hat{{\bf\it x}}+\mathrm{i}\hat{{\bf\it y}})
represents a vector field that is spinning anticlockwise:

\Re[(\hat{{\bf\it x}}+\mathrm{i}\hat{{\bf\it y}})\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega t}]=(\hat{{\bf\it x}}\cos{\omega t}+\hat{{\bf\it y}}\sin{\omega t})\,.

(50)

The cross product of
{\bf\it d}=2R({\hat{{\bf\it x}}+\mathrm{i}\hat{{\bf\it y}}})
and its conjugate is:

{{\bf\it d}^{*}\crossproduct{\bf\it d}}=4R^{2}(\hat{{\bf\it x}}-\mathrm{i}\hat{{\bf\it y}})\crossproduct(\hat{{\bf\it x}}+\mathrm{i}\hat{{\bf\it y}})=4R^{2}[{\mathrm{i}\hat{{\bf\it x}}\crossproduct\hat{{\bf\it y}}-\mathrm{i}\hat{{\bf\it y}}\crossproduct\hat{{\bf\it x}}}]=8\mathrm{i}R^{2}\hat{{\bf\it z}}.

(51)

Now all that is left to do is plug these dipoles into
eqs.

46
and
47
. Doing this for the recoil force we find it indeed matches
eq.

43
:

\begin{split}\expectationvalue{\vc{F}_\text{rec}}&=-\frac{k^{4}}{12\pi\varepsilon_{0}c}\Re[q_{\text{e}}(-\mathrm{i}q_{\text{m}}){\bf\it d}^{*}\crossproduct{\bf\it d}]=-\frac{2}{3\pi\varepsilon_{0}c}q_{\text{e}}q_{\text{m}}(k^{4}R^{2})\hat{{\bf\it z}}=-\frac{2}{3\pi\varepsilon_{0}c}q_{\text{e}}q_{\text{m}}\quantity(\frac{\beta^4}{R^2})\hat{{\bf\it z}}\,.\end{split}

(52)

where in the last line we used the definition of
\beta=v/c=\omega R/c=kR
. The same can be done for the torque:

\begin{split}\expectationvalue{\vc{\Gamma}_\text{rec}}=-\frac{2}{3\pi}\quantity(\frac{q_\text{e}^2}{\varepsilon_0}+{\mu_0\,q_\text{m}^2})(k^{3}R^{2})\hat{{\bf\it z}}=-\frac{2}{{3\pi}}\quantity(\frac{q_\text{e}^2}{\varepsilon_0}+{\mu_0\, q_\text{m}^2} )\quantity(\frac{\beta^3}{R})\hat{{\bf\it z}}\,.\end{split}

(53)

The calculation of dipolar torque using Lorentz forces, for an isolated electric and magnetic dipole, is a textbook problem that agrees with the result above (Ref.
Griffiths 2012
). To generalise this to a combination of electric and magnetic dipole, we must simply add the torque contributions
{\bf\it\varGamma}_{ij}^{\text{e,m}}={\bf\it r}_{j}\times{\bf\it F}{\kern 0.50003pt}^{\text{e,m}}_{ij}
caused by the electric-to-magnetic and magnetic-to-electric forces
{\bf\it F}{\kern 0.50003pt}^{\text{e,m}}_{ij}
that were computed exactly above, and can be shown to cancel out pairwise, hence not contributing to the net torque. Finally, the power scattered by the spinning Huygens dipole:

\begin{split}\expectationvalue{P_\text{sca}}=\frac{2c}{3\pi}\quantity(\frac{q_\text{e}^2}{\varepsilon_0}+\mu_0q_\text{m}^2)k^{4}=\frac{2c}{3\pi}\quantity(\frac{q_\text{e}^2}{\varepsilon_0}+\mu_0q_\text{m}^2)\quantity(\frac{\beta}{R})^{4}.\end{split}

(54)
