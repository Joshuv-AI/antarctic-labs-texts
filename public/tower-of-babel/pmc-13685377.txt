Umbilical cord ulceration complicating fetal upper gastrointestinal obstruction: a case report
Authors: Yuanyuan Zhu, Tangyi Geng, Miao Qian, Yun Gu, Li Zhang, Rui Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Umbilical cord ulceration (UCU) is an uncommon but devastating cause of acute fetal hemorrhage, often associated with fetal upper gastrointestinal obstruction. We report a woman aged 30–35 years (gravida 4, para 1) referred at 23 weeks of gestation for suspected fetal gastrointestinal abnormality. Prenatal ultrasonography showed an enlarged fetal stomach with a double-bubble sign, and subsequent examinations demonstrated progressive polyhydramnios with increasing bowel dilatation. At 32 weeks and 4 days of gestation, after previously reassuring fetal assessment, the fetal heart rate suddenly fell and remained at 90–97 beats per minute, prompting emergency caesarean delivery. A large volume of blood stained amniotic fluid was noted and a longitudinal vessel rupture at the fetal end of the umbilical cord was identified with active bleeding. The neonate had profound anemia and died after withdrawal of care. Histopathology confirmed umbilical cord ulceration with vessel rupture and erythrocyte extravasation. This case highlights the abrupt onset of UCU and supports heightened surveillance when fetal upper gastrointestinal obstruction is suspected.

Background

Umbilical cord ulceration (UCU) refers to the thinning of Wharton’s jelly in the umbilical cord and the exposure of umbilical blood vessels, which may cause rupture and bleeding of these vessels at the same site, leading to acute fetal blood loss. UCU is an underreported but significant cause of intrauterine fetal death or severe neonatal asphyxia. It’s a fatal complication associated with cases of fetal upper gastrointestinal obstruction, wherein its incidence has been reported as 6.5% to 13.6%, with substantial perinatal mortality [1, 2]. Although first reported in 1991, the relationship between UCU and fetal congenital intestinal atresia remained exceptionally rare, with only sporadic case reports appearing in literatures over the subsequent years [3–5]. Routine prenatal screening methods have limitations in diagnosing UCU, and clinical diagnosis still depends on observations such as the presence of blood-stained amniotic fluid, exposed umbilical vessels, and neonatal anemia at birth [2]. Moreover, due to its rarity, sudden onset, and high mortality rate, clinicians should be fully aware of the potential risk of UCU in cases of fetal upper gastrointestinal obstruction. Herein, we report a case of UCU secondary to fetal upper gastrointestinal obstruction and discuss its clinical implications for antenatal management, including considerations for risk-based early intervention.

Case report

A woman aged 30–35 years with no significant medical history (gravida 4, para 1, including one previous term vaginal delivery, one pregnancy terminated for fetal anomaly, and one miscarriage) was referred for prenatal evaluation because of suspected fetal gastrointestinal abnormalities. At 23 weeks of gestation, routine obstetric ultrasonography revealed an enlarged fetal stomach measuring 3.98 × 1.78 cm and dilatation of a lower abdominal intestinal loop up to 1.40 cm. A characteristic double-bubble sign was detected as shown in Fig. 1, raising the suspicion of a congenital gastrointestinal obstruction. Amniocentesis was performed, and chromosomal microarray analysis revealed no clinically significant abnormalities. Further investigations, including whole-exome sequencing of amniotic fluid and fetal abdominal MRI, were recommended but declined by the patient.

Follow-up ultrasonography at 29 + 5 weeks of gestation demonstrated progressive polyhydramnios and worsening intestinal dilatation, suggesting fetal upper gastrointestinal obstruction, with possible pyloric involvement. At 31 + 6 weeks of gestation, the patient was admitted because of mild vaginal bleeding accompanied by lower abdominal discomfort. Fetal heart rate monitoring on admission was reactive. Ultrasonography revealed marked polyhydramnios, with a maximum vertical pocket of 12.1 cm. The fetal stomach measured 5.79 × 2.52 cm, and a markedly dilated intestinal segment beneath the stomach measured approximately 10.1 × 2.20 cm, containing multiple punctate echogenic foci within an anechoic lumen. A possible communication between the stomach and dilated intestinal segment was observed, as depicted in Fig. 2.

After excluding maternal infection, tocolytic therapy was initiated on the day of admission. On hospital day 1 (32 + 0 weeks of gestation), preterm premature rupture of membranes occurred. We administered antenatal corticosteroids for fetal lung maturation and magnesium sulfate for fetal neuroprotection, together with intravenous ampicillin (2 g every 6 h) and erythromycin (250 mg every 6 h) for infection prophylaxis. Tests for serial complete blood counts and C-reactive protein levels revealed no evidence of infection. After 3 days of intravenous antibiotics, the prophylaxis was switched to oral roxithromycin (333 mg every 8 h) combined with amoxicillin (250 mg every 8 h) at 32 + 3 weeks of gestation. Daily fetal heart rate monitoring remained reactive (122–156 beats per minute). At 32 + 4 weeks of gestation, fetal heart monitoring in the morning was reactive, and the patient reported normal fetal movements. In the early afternoon, the fetal heart rate was 147 beats per minute. However, about 2 h later, auscultation revealed persistent fetal bradycardia (90–97 beats per minute). Intrauterine resuscitative measures, including maternal oxygen administration and positional changes, failed to restore normal fetal heart rate. Vaginal examination revealed cephalic presentation, a Bishop score of 5, no palpable umbilical cord, and no visible amniotic fluid. We suspected acute fetal distress and immediately performed an emergency caesarean section.

Approximately 8 min after the decision for surgery, a live male infant was delivered. The Apgar scores were 0 at 1 min, 1 at 5 min, 4 at 10 min, and 7 at 15 min. Birthweight was 1490 g. Intraoperatively, approximately 2000 mL of blood-stained amniotic fluid was detected. The placenta was delivered intact, with no evidence of retroplacental clots, excluding placental abruption. Neonatal examination showed a thin umbilical cord, approximately 0.8 cm in diameter and 70 cm in length. A longitudinal rupture approximately 1 cm in length was identified at the fetal end of the umbilical cord, with active bleeding as depicted in Fig. 3.

Because of severe asphyxia and hemorrhagic shock, the neonate was admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit. Initial laboratory examination revealed a hemoglobin concentration of 65 g/L. Abdominal ultrasonography showed dense gas echoes in the upper abdomen, poor gastric filling, mild dilatation of the horizontal portion of the duodenum with visible intraluminal contents, poorly visualized distal bowel loops with collapsed appearance, and intraperitoneal fluid measuring up to 1.53 cm with flocculent internal echoes. These findings were suggestive of suspected duodenal obstruction with associated ascites. Despite intensive supportive treatment, the parents elected to withdraw care after 2 days in the NICU, and the neonate died on the day after discharge. Pathological examination of the ruptured fetal end of the umbilical cord was not feasible. Histopathological examination of the remaining umbilical cord revealed focal thinning and loss of Wharton’s jelly with displacement and exposure of umbilical vessels toward the cord margin, as shown in Fig. 4. A focal rupture of the umbilical vessel wall with extensive extravasation of erythrocytes was identified, in the absence of significant inflammatory infiltration or vascular reactive changes. These observations were consistent with UCU complicated by acute hemorrhage.

Discussion

Fetal gastrointestinal obstruction accounts for approximately 10% of all congenital fetal anomalies, With upper gastrointestinal obstruction primarily including esophageal atresia and duodenal stenosis or atresia [6]. UCU represents one of the most severe complications of fetal upper gastrointestinal obstruction, with reported rates of intrauterine fetal death, neonatal death, and overall survival are 32%, 32%, and 37%, respectively [5]. The incidence of duodenal obstruction is estimated at 1–10 per 100,000 live births. Prenatal ultrasonography typically demonstrates the characteristic “double-bubble” sign. In the present case, both level - III prenatal ultrasonography and postnatal abdominal ultrasonography suggested duodenal obstruction. Impaired intestinal recanalization and extrinsic compression are the principal mechanisms underlying duodenal obstruction, and chromosomal abnormalities including trisomy 21, deletions at 13q, 10q26, 4q24, and 2p24have been implicated as potential genetic contributors [7]. However, previous studies indicate that chromosomal abnormalities exert no significant impact on overall prognosis because their prevalence does not differ significantly between liveborn and intrauterine fetal death cases [8–10].

Despite the reported 6.5%–13.6% incidence of UCU among cases of fetal upper gastrointestinal obstruction, its pathogenesis and pathophysiology remain incompletely understood. Traditionally, proposed mechanisms include abnormal vascular reactivity, primary epithelial abnormalities, and the reflux of gastric or duodenal digestive fluids [1, 3]. In recent years, reflux of digestive fluids driven by proximal intestinal obstruction has emerged as a major focus, with consistent reports of elevated concentrations of bile acids in the amniotic fluid of affected fetuses [11]. For instance, Ohyama et al. described a neonatal death associated with fetal upper gastrointestinal obstruction and UCU, in which the bile acid concentration in amniotic fluid was markedly higher than that in controls (24.7 vs. 2.1 µmol/L) [1]. Furthermore, Nakamura et al. found that all of the 46 cases in their study complicated by UCU had amniotic fluid bile acid concentrations of ≥ 10 µmol/L, significantly higher than those without ulceration [10]. Fujimori et al. further reported a case of hemorrhagic UCU with elevated levels of phospholipase A2, trypsin, lipase, and bile acids in the amniotic fluid, with no vascular reactivity or epithelial abnormalities detected pathologically, supporting reflux of digestive fluids as a primary mechanism [12]. In addition, more than half of fetuses with UCU have been reported to develop preterm premature rupture of membranes and preterm birth, consistent with the present case [5]. Besides digesting the umbilical cord, high concentrations of digestive enzymes in the amniotic fluid may degrade the fetal membranes, contributing to membrane rupture. These findings suggest that digestive enzyme levels in the amniotic fluid represent a potential biomarker for monitoring and predicting UCU, and preterm premature rupture of membranes may serve as a clinically observable indicator of disease progression.

Although no gold standard exists for the early diagnosis of UCU, ultrasonography is not technically challenging for detecting active bleeding once ulceration occurs. In fact, Katsura et al. reported that ultrasonography may be more informative than fetal heart rate monitoring during episodes of UCU [13]. As bleeding most commonly occurs near the fetal end of the umbilical cord, careful evaluation of this region is recommended in high-risk pregnancies. In this regard, Huang et al. summarized the prenatal ultrasonographic features of UCU as patchy or linear echogenic structures surrounding the cord and reported favorable neonatal after emergency cesarean delivery on the basis of these findings. Nonetheless, they also observed that UCU often presents abruptly and progresses rapidly and therefore may not always be detected in real time by prenatal ultrasonography. Immediate delivery in the presence of fetal heart rate abnormalities remains a crucial approach for preventing intrauterine fetal death and improving neonatal outcomes [4].

A review by Nakamura et al. indicated that UCU in fetuses with upper gastrointestinal obstruction generally occurs after 30 weeks of gestation, with a mean gestational age of 33.3 ± 2.7 weeks [10]. In the present case, ulceration occurred at 32 + 4 weeks of gestation, consistent with previous reports. Currently, evidence-based guidelines for the antenatal management of fetal upper gastrointestinal obstruction remain lacking, despite the existence of empirical clinical strategies [13]. Therefore, clinicians should remain vigilant regarding the risk of UCU and intrauterine fetal death in such cases. Some authors advocate close surveillance from 30 weeks of gestation onward and individualized determination of the optimal timing of delivery [12]. Preterm delivery may represent a strategy to prevent sudden unexplained intrauterine fetal death in fetuses with upper gastrointestinal obstruction, although this must be carefully balanced against the risks associated with prematurity. Measurement of digestive enzyme levels in the amniotic fluid, specific prenatal ultrasonographic features, and the occurrence of preterm premature rupture of membranes may provide useful clues for timely identification and intervention. Nevertheless, because UCU is rare and has primarily been described in isolated case reports, further accumulation of clinical data is required to establish evidence-based strategies for prenatal monitoring and management aimed at reducing perinatal mortality.

Currently, UCU is clinically diagnosed, based on findings at birth such as blood-stained amniotic fluid, exposed umbilical vessels, and neonatal anemia. This diagnostic approach reflects the inherent difficulty of prenatal identification. Routine ultrasonography has limited sensitivity for early detection, because ulceration generally occurs at the fetal insertion site of the umbilical cord, a concealed location where subtle changes, such as thinning of Wharton’s jelly and exposure of umbilical vessels, are easily overlooked unless overt hemorrhage occurs. In the present case, umbilical cord rupture and hemorrhagic amniotic fluid were identified only during the emergency cesarean section prompted by sudden fetal bradycardia, and the diagnosis was confirmed based on severe neonatal anemia (hemoglobin level 65 g/L) highlighting the ongoing challenge of prenatal prediction and diagnosis.

Considering the high lethality of UCU, optimizing pregnancy management in women carrying fetuses with confirmed upper gastrointestinal obstruction remains a major clinical challenge. As there is no effective intrauterine treatment for UCU, management depends on the early identification of high-risk features, close fetal surveillance, and timely delivery. Pregnant women should be adequately counselled regarding the potential risks, particularly when marked intestinal dilatation and polyhydramnios are detected on ultrasonography, and multidisciplinary collaboration involving obstetricians, ultrasonographers, pediatric surgeons, and neonatologists is essential. More intensive antenatal monitoring may be warranted. In addition to routine fetal heart rate monitoring, serial ultrasonographic assessment of intestinal dilatation, amniotic fluid volume, and umbilical cord blood flow may help detect evolving abnormalities, although no definitive warning indicators have been established. In the present case, daily fetal heart rate monitoring remained reactive until rapid deterioration occurred, with only a 2-hour interval between normal findings and acute fetal distress. This observation suggests that conventional fetal heart rate monitoring is insufficient to detect acute hemorrhagic events caused by UCU. Moreover, it is necessary to further investigate whether higher-frequency monitoring or the use of remote fetal monitoring technologies could improve outcomes in such high-risk pregnancies.

Conclusion

UCU is a rare but fatal complication of fetal upper gastrointestinal obstruction and is characterized by sudden onset and rapid clinical deterioration. This case demonstrates that acute fetal compromise may occur despite previously reassuring fetal surveillance, underscoring the limitations of conventional monitoring. In pregnancies complicated by fetal upper gastrointestinal obstruction, heightened clinical vigilance, close multidisciplinary surveillance, and timely delivery are crucial for improving perinatal outcomes. Further studies are required to identify reliable prenatal predictors and optimize management strategies for this life-threatening condition.
