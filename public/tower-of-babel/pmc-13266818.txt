Diagnostic value of lesion-to-spinal cord ratio and apparent diffusion coefficient in differentiating benign and malignant pulmonary nodules and masses
Authors: Heng Li, Wei Wei, Chanjuan Yu, Na Zhao, Man Xu, Xumiao Li, Xiao Yang, Xiuzheng Yue, Zhiwei Shen, Yuedong Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPulmonary nodules and masses (PNMs) are closely associated with lung cancer, for which early and accurate diagnosis is essential. However, current diagnostic methods have inherent limitations and may produce false-positive or false-negative results, thereby complicating clinical decision-making. Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) has emerged as a promising tool for distinguishing benign lesions from malignant lesions, although the combined diagnostic value of lesion-to-spinal cord ratio (LSR) and apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) remains underexplored. This study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic value of LSR and ADC in distinguishing benign PNMs from malignant PNMs.MethodsMulti–b-value DWI, commonly used for intravoxel incoherent motion (IVIM) acquisition, was retrospectively performed in patients with PNMs. Lesions were classified based on histopathological findings or clinical follow-up. ADC values were calculated from multi–b-value DWI data using a monoexponential fit, and LSR values were measured from DWI images at selected b-values. Independent-samples t-test was employed to assess statistical differences between the benign and malignant groups. Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis was conducted to determine the optimal cutoff values using the maximal Youden index and to evaluate the diagnostic performance of each parameter.ResultsAmong 56 lesions, 34 were benign and 22 were malignant. Malignant lesions exhibited significantly lower ADC values and higher LSR values compared with benign lesions (P<0.05). The area under the curve (AUC) for ADC was generally higher than that for LSR (P<0.05). Among ADC parameters, the maximum ADC value had the highest AUC [0.759, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.631–0.887], with a sensitivity of 0.636 (95% CI: 0.407–0.828), a specificity of 0.824 (95% CI: 0.655–0.932), and a diagnostic threshold of 1.265×10−3 mm2/s. Among the LSR parameters, LSR at b=500 s/mm2 had the highest AUC (0.682, 95% CI: 0.530–0.834), with a sensitivity of 0.591 and a specificity of 0.794. The combination of ADC and LSR outperformed either parameter alone, achieving an AUC of 0.787 (95% CI: 0.658–0.885), a sensitivity of 0.909 (95% CI: 0.708–0.989), and a specificity of 0.559 (95% CI: 0.379–0.728).ConclusionsBoth ADC and LSR provide valuable diagnostic information for distinguishing benign PNMs from malignant PNMs. Integrating ADC and LSR further enhances diagnostic accuracy, supporting their combined application as a non-invasive, complementary imaging biomarker in clinical evaluation of PNMs.Keywords: Pulmonary nodule mass (PNM), diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), multi–b-value DWI, lesion-to-spinal cord ratio (LSR), apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC)

Highlight box

Key findings

• Malignant pulmonary nodules and masses exhibited significantly lower apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) values and higher lesion-to-spinal cord ratio (LSR) values compared with benign lesions. Both biomarkers demonstrated diagnostic utility, and their combination yielded the highest diagnostic accuracy (area under the curve =0.787, sensitivity =0.909, specificity =0.559), outperforming either parameter alone.

What is known and what is new?

• Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) provides quantitative indices such as ADC and LSR, which are widely used to differentiate benign from malignant tumors across various organs. However, evidence regarding pulmonary nodules and masses remains limited and inconsistent.

• This study adds new insights by integrating ADC values derived from multi–b-value DWI using a monoexponential fit with LSR measurements across multiple b-values. Notably, LSR at b=500 s/mm2 (LSR500) achieved the highest diagnostic performance among LSR indices, and the combined use of ADC and LSR substantially improved the discrimination between benign and malignant pulmonary lesions.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Multi–b-value DWI provides non-invasive, radiation-free, and contrast-free parameters for lung lesion characterization. The combined assessment of ADC and LSR should be considered in clinical practice to enhance diagnostic confidence, minimize the need for invasive procedures, and guide timely management. Among the evaluated parameters, ADCmax demonstrated the best diagnostic performance as a single parameter, while the combined model further enhanced diagnostic efficacy.

Introduction

Pulmonary nodules and masses (PNMs) represent a frequent medical concern, and their management is complex. Among PNMs, lung cancer is the most serious type, representing one of the most malignant and life-threatening tumors. It has high incidence and mortality rates, mainly accompanied by an insidious onset. Regrettably, the majority of patients are diagnosed at advanced stages, leading to a poor prognosis (1-3). Therefore, accurate and early diagnosis is crucial to improving patient outcomes. Studies have demonstrated that early diagnosis of patients with lung cancer is the key to prolonging their survival (4,5).

Conventional diagnostic tools, including fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography (FDG-PET/CT), are limited. While FDG-PET/CT provides a high sensitivity of approximately 80–96%, its specificity is variable (38–87%) and may be reduced in regions with a high prevalence of inflammatory diseases (6-9). Moreover, FDG-PET/CT can yield false-negative results in low-metabolism tumors, such as pulmonary adenocarcinomas or small lesions, and false-positive results in inflammatory nodules (10-12). Computed tomography (CT) remains the primary modality for initial detection, whereas FDG-PET/CT is commonly employed for further characterization and risk stratification. These limitations highlight the need for complementary imaging approaches that exploit distinct biological principles.

In recent years, diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) has remarkably gained clinicians’ attention in the clinical studies on PNMs. The apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) is a commonly used quantitative index, while the lesion-to-spinal cord ratio (LSR) is also an important parameter to differentiate benign lesions from malignant lesions (13). Several studies have demonstrated that the ADC and LSR values from DWI are valuable in distinguishing benign tumors from malignant tumors across various organs (14,15). ADC values are correlated with key histopathological features, including tumor cellularity, Ki-67 proliferation index, and microvessel density, reflecting underlying tumor biology (14-17). Furthermore, meta-analyses across multiple tumor types support ADC as a non-invasive biomarker for tissue characterization and risk stratification (18-22). In parallel, multi-b-value DWI (commonly used for intravoxel incoherent motion imaging) extends conventional diffusion analysis by separating diffusion and perfusion components, providing surrogate markers of microvascular perfusion and tissue heterogeneity (23,24). However, reliable IVIM parameter estimation requires stable biexponential fitting and sufficient image quality, which may be challenging in small pulmonary lesions. Collectively, these findings highlight the potential of DWI-based metrics not only for lesion discrimination, but also for linking imaging phenotypes with histopathological processes.

However, few studies have investigated the combined use of ADC and LSR for diagnosing PNMs, and the results have been inconsistent (13-15,18,25,26). Additionally, the combined diagnostic value of ADC derived from multi–b-value DWI and LSR measured across multiple b-values has not yet been fully established. Therefore, this study aimed to explore the diagnostic value of ADC derived from multi–b-value DWI using a monoexponential fit, combined with the LSR, in distinguishing benign PNMs from malignant PNMs. We present this article in accordance with the STARD reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2025-1870/rc).

Methods

Study cohort

This retrospective study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments, and it was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xi’an Gaoxin Hospital (No. GXYYEC-KTSB-2020-03-01). All participants provided written informed consent.

Between July 2021 and September 2022, adult patients with PNMs who were admitted to our hospital were retrospectively enrolled. The etiology of all cases was confirmed through surgery, biopsy, or clinical follow-up. The inclusion criteria were summarized as follows: (I) a single or multiple solid nodules with an average diameter >5 mm; (II) availability of high-resolution magnetic resonance (MR) images; (III) an interval of ≤3 days between CT and MR imaging (MRI) examinations. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (I) age under 18 years, pregnancy, or breastfeeding; and (II) contraindications to MRI, such as cardiac pacemakers or ferromagnetic implants.

Lesion size was defined as the mean of the longest and shortest axes measured on CT images. A minimum size threshold of 5 mm was applied to ensure reliable lesion visualization and quantitative measurement on DWI, given the spatial resolution limitations and potential partial volume effects associated with smaller nodules.

ADC and LSR measurements

Equipment

The MRI was performed using a Philips Ingenia CX 3.0T MRI scanner (Philips, Amsterdam, the Netherlands) with a 16-channel abdominal phased array coil. The images were post-processed using IntelliSpace Portal V9 software (Philips). ADC values were calculated using the workstation post-processing interface for monoexponential ADC analysis; no perfusion-related IVIM parameters were extracted.

Scanning

Patients were positioned supine with feet first, and surface coils were used to enhance image quality, and regular breathing training was conducted before scanning. PNMs identified on CT were scanned within the appropriate range. A multi–b-value DWI sequence, commonly used for IVIM acquisition, was performed using single-shot echo-planar imaging (EPI) with breath-triggered acquisition. Thirteen b-values were employed: 0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 50, 80, 150, 300, 500, 800, and 1,000 s/mm2. ADC maps were generated from the multi–b-value DWI data using a monoexponential model. The spectral presaturation with inversion recovery (SPIR) technique was applied for fat suppression. Acquisition parameters included repetition time of 1,111 ms, echo time of 67 ms, slice thickness/spacing of 3.0/0.3 mm, acquisition matrix of 124×103, field of view of 375×315 mm2, and isotropic voxel size of 3.0×3.0×3.0 mm3. Total scan duration was 8 min.

To supplement DWI, ultrashort echo time (UTE) imaging was performed using a three-dimensional multi-echo sequence (3D_UTE_MUTIECHO) with the following parameters: repetition time (TR) =17 ms; echo time (TE) =0.26, 3, 5, 8, 10, 12 ms; flip angle =3.3°; slice thickness =3 mm; field of view =400×400 mm2; matrix =268×268; voxel size =1.49×1.49×3.0 mm3; and acquisition time =8 min 42 s. Although the multi-b-value acquisition could theoretically support IVIM modelling, IVIM biexponential fitting was not performed in the present study because of the limited robustness of biexponential fitting in small pulmonary lesions and the acquisition design of this retrospective study. Therefore, IVIM-derived parameters, including D, D*, and f, were not generated or analyzed. Lesions smaller than 5 mm were excluded to reduce partial volume effects and ensure quantitative accuracy.

Imaging analysis

All MR images were independently reviewed by two experienced physicians, each with over 10 years of expertise in imaging examination of PNMs. The measurement protocol was established prior to the analysis. When selecting each region of interest (ROI), areas with calcification or liquefactive necrosis were excluded. Measurements were taken three times for each ROI, and the average value was used for statistical analysis.

For ADC measurement, the ROI was selected along the edge of the lesion at its largest cross-section on the multi–b-value ADC map, ensuring that the lesion was fully included as far as possible. ADC values were measured at three different locations in the lesion, and the following were recorded: mean ADC value (ADCmean), minimum ADC value (ADCmin), and maximum ADC value (ADCmax).

For LSR measurement, the ROI was first manually defined on the ADC map and then automatically propagated to all corresponding DWI images (b=300, 500, 800, and 1,000 s/mm2) using the image registration function of the workstation to ensure spatial consistency. These b-values were selected to provide progressively increasing diffusion weighting while maintaining an acceptable signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), allowing for characterization of signal decay across different diffusion sensitivities. The mean signal intensity (SI) of the pulmonary nodules was recorded, followed by the SI of the spinal cord at the same level. The LSR was calculated using the following formula: LSR = SIpulmonary nodules/SIspinal cord. LSR values were recorded for each b-value as LSR at b=300 s/mm2 (LSR300), LSR500, LSR800, and LSR1000.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 22.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and MedCalc 23.4.9 (MedCalc Software Ltd., Ostend, Belgium) software. A two-sided P<0.05 was considered statistically significant. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using independent-samples t-test, while non-normally distributed variables were reported as median [interquartile range (IQR)] and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated for individual parameters (ADC and LSR), and optimal cutoffs were determined using the maximal Youden index. Differences among LSR values at different b-values were evaluated using the Friedman test. Collinearity was assessed using Spearman correlation, variance inflation factor (VIF), and tolerance, with |r| >0.8, VIF >10, or tolerance <0.1 as indicative of high collinearity.

A multivariable logistic regression model including ADCmax and LSR500 was established to evaluate combined diagnostic performance: logit(P) = β0 + β1 × ADCmax + β2 × LSR500, where P represents the predicted probability of malignancy. Model calibration was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test. ROC curves were compared using the DeLong test. Internal validation was performed via bootstrap resampling (1,000 iterations) to assess model stability and overfitting. Generalized estimating equations (GEE) were applied to account for within-patient clustering, using patient ID as the clustering variable, an exchangeable correlation structure, and robust standard errors.

Results

Patients’ and lesions’ characteristics

A total of 40 patients with PNMs were included, comprising 21 men and 19 women, with a mean age of 57.33±11.26 (range, 19–76) years. In total, 56 lesions were analyzed, including 34 benign (60.7%) and 22 malignant lesions (39.3%). Because some patients presented with multiple lesions, additional analyses were performed to account for within-patient clustering.

All malignant lesions were confirmed histopathologically and consisted predominantly of primary lung tumors (19/22, 86.4%), including adenocarcinoma (14/22, 63.6%), squamous cell carcinoma (3/22, 13.6%), and small cell lung cancer (2/22, 9.1%), with the remaining cases representing other malignant subtypes, such as metastatic lesions (3/22, 13.6%). Benign lesions were confirmed either by histopathology or by imaging follow-up of at least 24 months demonstrating stability or regression. Histologically, benign lesions included inflammatory nodules (6/34, 17.6%), tuberculomas (11/34, 32.4%), hamartomas (3/34, 8.8%), and other benign entities (14/34, 41.2%).

All lesions met the predefined eligibility criterion of a diameter greater than 5 mm. A substantial proportion of lesions were small nodules: 14 of 56 (25.0%) measured >5–≤10 mm, comprising 13 benign lesions and 1 malignant lesion. Lesions measuring >10–≤30 mm comprised 21/34 (61.8%) benign lesions and 17/22 (77.3%) malignant lesions, whereas lesions >30 mm were found exclusively in the malignant group (4/22, 18.2%). Malignant lesions were significantly larger than benign lesions, with median diameters of 1.95 cm (IQR, 1.40–2.70 cm) and 1.15 cm (IQR, 0.78–1.35 cm), respectively (P<0.001). Sub-centimeter lesions (≤1 cm) were predominantly benign (38.2% vs. 4.5%). Detailed clinical and pathological characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

Specifically, ADCmean, ADCmin, and ADCmax were significantly reduced in malignant lesions compared with those in benign lesions (0.88±0.39 vs. 1.26±0.46, P=0.002; 0.61±0.32 vs. 1.20±0.42, P=0.02; 1.17±0.49 vs. 1.63±0.52, P=0.001, respectively). Collinearity analysis demonstrated strong correlations among variables in both ADC and LSR parameter groups. ADC parameters showed high pairwise correlations (r=0.851–0.930, all P<0.001), with VIFs ranging from 4.68 to 19.29. Similarly, LSR parameters were highly correlated (r=0.834–0.918, all P<0.001), with VIFs ranging from 18.82 to 36.49. These findings indicate a considerable multicollinearity in parameter groups.

Based on these results, and to minimize redundancy and reduce the risk of model instability, ADCmax and LSR500, which demonstrated the highest diagnostic performance within their respective parameter groups, were selected for subsequent multivariable modeling.

Diagnostic performance of individual parameters and the combined model

The ROC curve analysis demonstrated that ADC parameters generally outperformed LSR parameters (Table 3, Figure 3). Among all individual parameters, ADCmax exhibited the highest diagnostic performance [area under the curve (AUC) =0.759, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.631–0.887], with a sensitivity of 0.636 (95% CI: 0.407–0.828) and a specificity of 0.824 (95% CI: 0.655–0.932) at an optimal cutoff value of 1.265×10−3 mm2/s.

Among LSR parameters, LSR500 achieved the highest AUC (0.682, 95% CI: 0.530–0.834), with a sensitivity of 0.591 (95% CI: 0.364–0.793) and a specificity of 0.794 (95% CI: 0.621–0.913) at a cutoff value of 0.495. Other parameters demonstrated lower or comparable performance (Table 3).

Multivariate logistic regression analysis identified both ADCmax and LSR500 as independent predictors of malignancy [ADCmax: odds ratio (OR) =65.27, 95% CI: 2.76–1,542.49, P=0.01; LSR500: OR =7.14, 95% CI: 1.22–41.76, P=0.03]. The regression model was formulated as follows: logit(P) = −4.687 + 4.178 × ADCmax + 1.966 × LSR500, accompanied by a satisfactory calibration (Hosmer-Lemeshow test: χ2=7.85, P=0.35).

The combined model demonstrated improved diagnostic performance compared with individual parameters, with an AUC of 0.787 (95% CI: 0.658–0.885). At the optimal probability threshold of 0.269, the model achieved a sensitivity of 0.909 (95% CI: 0.708–0.989) and a specificity of 0.559 (95% CI: 0.379–0.728).

Model stability and clustering-adjusted analysis

Internal validation using bootstrap resampling (1,000 iterations) demonstrated stable model performance, with a mean AUC of 0.782 and minimal bias (−0.005) compared with the original AUC (0.787, 95% CI: 0.658–0.885). The 95% CI for the optimal cutoff probability ranged from 0.156 to 0.291, indicating a low risk of overfitting.

To account for within-patient clustering, GEE analysis was performed. After adjustment, neither ADCmax nor LSR500 remained as statistically significant predictors (ADCmax: B =0.347, 95% Wald CI: −0.076 to 0.770, P=0.11; LSR500: B =0.298, 95% Wald CI: −0.092 to 0.687, P=0.13).

These findings indicated that the lesion-level associations identified in the primary analysis were attenuated after accounting for intra-patient correlation, indicating that clustering may influence statistical significance. However, the overall diagnostic performance of the combined model remained stable in internal validation, supporting its potential utility as an exploratory diagnostic approach.

Discussion

Previous studies have evaluated single- and multi-b-value DWI models for isolated pulmonary lesions; however, optimal b-values and ADC cutoff thresholds remain inconsistent (27-29). Lung ADC measurement faces technical challenges: (I) abundant air-tissue interfaces induce magnetic susceptibility effects, causing local signal loss and geometric distortion on echo-planar DWI sequences; (II) necrotic or cystic regions within lesions may bias measurements; and (III) partial volumetric effects are prominent in lesions <2 cm, reducing reproducibility (30). As a result, other semi-quantitative and quantitative DWI methods warrant further exploration. Previous research suggested that LSR evaluation was more practical and reliable than ADC, diffusion coefficient (D), and perfusion fraction (f), as LSR is less affected by b-values (31). Although ADC demonstrated superior diagnostic efficacy for primary lung cancer compared with LSR, the difference was not statistically significant (P>0.05) (32). In the present study, it was found that malignant PNMs had lower ADC values and higher LSR values than benign PNMs. The combined ADCmax + LSR500 model achieved the highest diagnostic performance (AUC: 0.787, 95% CI: 0.658–0.885; sensitivity: 0.909, 95% CI: 0.708–0.989; specificity: 0.559, 95% CI: 0.379–0.728), indicating complementary value of microstructural diffusion and relative SI metrics. After adjustment for intra-patient clustering using GEE, associations were attenuated and no longer statistically significant, indicating that lesion-level analyses may be influenced by non-independence of observations and limited sample size.

The single-parameter findings of the present study align with those reported previously. ADC-based metrics for pulmonary lesions reported AUCs of 0.71–0.89, sensitivities of 63–76%, and specificities of 82–96% (33,34). LSR measurements demonstrated AUCs of 0.68–0.79, sensitivities of 59–68%, and specificities of 73–82% (33,34). In this cohort, ADCmax achieved an AUC of 0.759 (95% CI: 0.631–0.887), a sensitivity of 0.636 (95% CI: 0.407–0.828), and a specificity of 0.824 (95% CI: 0.655–0.932); LSR500 achieved an AUC of 0.682 (95% CI: 0.530–0.834), a sensitivity of 0.591, and a specificity of 0.794, being consistent with external reports. The combined model outperformed individual parameters, emphasizing the complementary nature of ADC and LSR. Compared with FDG-PET/CT, which has been reported to yield pooled sensitivities of 82–97% and specificities of 67–87% (34), the combined model exhibited comparable diagnostic potential while avoiding radiation exposure and reducing cost. These findings reinforce the potential clinical utility of multi-parameter DWI, particularly in settings where PET is not readily available, and highlight the rationale for integrating ADC and LSR to improve non-invasive characterization of pulmonary lesions (34).

The results of the present study indicated that the ADC values for differentiating benign PNMs from malignant PNMs were as follows: ADCmean was 1.26×10⁻3 mm2/s for benign PNMs and 0.88×10⁻3 mm2/s for malignant PNMs, ADCmin was 1.20×10⁻3 mm2/s for benign PNMs and 0.61×10⁻3 mm2/s for malignant PNMs, and ADCmax was 1.63×10⁻3 mm2/s for benign PNMs and 1.17×10⁻3 mm2/s for malignant PNMs. The ADC thresholds were 1.175×10⁻3, 0.84×10⁻3, and 1.265×10⁻3 mm2/s, respectively. Factors influencing ADC values include tumor cell density, nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio, tumor stroma, and macromolecular content in cytoplasm. Histogram-based analyses further demonstrated correlations between diffusion metrics and histopathological features, including cellularity, Ki-67 proliferation index, and microvessel density. In previous IVIM studies, the perfusion fraction has been reported to correlate with microvessel density, whereas ADC- and D-related parameters have been associate with proliferative activity (35). Tumors with densely packed cells, a high nucleus-to-cytoplasm ratio, and minimal supporting tissue restrict the movement of water molecules, resulting in higher DWI signals and lower ADC values.

Although modelling can provide additional parameters, including D, D*, and f, reflecting tissue diffusion, perfusion, and microvascular architecture, these parameters were not analyzed in the present study because IVIM biexponential fitting was not performed. Reliable IVIM estimation requires high SNR, stable biexponential fitting, and sufficiently large lesions (36-38). In this cohort, several lesions were small (≥5 mm) and approached the spatial resolution of DWI (≈3 mm isotropic voxel), where partial volume effects, reduced SNR, and amplified fitting noise compromise IVIM parameter stability. Perfusion-related metrics (D*, f) are particularly affected and show poor reproducibility in small pulmonary lesions (39). Evidence from high-resolution imaging further indicates that while f may correlate with vascular density, D* exhibits a limited biological correlation and a remarkable estimation error (40). Restricting analysis to larger lesions (>1.5 cm) improves stability, while it reduces sample size and introduces selection bias (41). Moreover, inconsistencies between IVIM perfusion metrics and established imaging or physiological references have been reported (e.g., paradoxically lower f in hepatocellular carcinoma compared with normal liver tissue) (42). Given these limitations, this study concentrated on ADC and LSR, which are robustly measurable across a wide range of lesion sizes and readily applicable in clinical practice. Future investigations incorporating larger lesions, optimized acquisition protocols, and standardized b-value distributions are required to evaluate the incremental diagnostic value of IVIM-derived parameters.

The ADC thresholds reported in this study differ from those in previous studies, which could be attributed to variations in selected b-values, hardware and software configurations (e.g., field strength and pulse sequences), respiratory trigger patterns, and the histological types of lesions. Notably, this study ranks the diagnostic efficacy of ADC indices as ADCmax > ADCmean > ADCmin, contrasting with prior findings (43). While most studies utilize ADCmean as a key biomarker for distinguishing between benign and malignant pulmonary nodules, it was evidenced that ADCmin may be superior because it reduces the impact of necrotic foci, inflating ADCmean values (43-45). Necrotic foci are more frequently found in malignant lesions, and ADCmin provides more accurate quantification by minimizing their influence.

The inclusion of ADCmax in this study adds a more comprehensive perspective, and the results indicate that ADCmax outperformed ADCmean and ADCmin in diagnostic efficacy. This may be related to variations in cell types and nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratios. Consistently, ADCmean, ADCmin, and ADCmax values were significantly lower in malignant PNMs than those in benign ones. However, discrepancies in diagnostic thresholds across studies persist. For instance, Çakmak et al. achieved ADCmin values of 1.45×10⁻3 and 2.40×10⁻3 mm2/s for malignant and benign lesions, respectively (46), while Çakir et al. found ADCmean values of 1.195×10⁻3 and 2.02×10⁻3 mm2/s. These inconsistencies likely stem from differences in pathological lesion types, study methodologies, and technical factors (27).

Several studies have demonstrated that LSR can effectively differentiate between benign and malignant lesions, and malignant lesions typically exhibit higher LSR values than benign ones (27,31,44). The present study provided a more thorough evaluation of LSR compared with previous research. Multiple b-values (300, 500, 800, and 1,000 s/mm2) from multi–b-value DWI were analyzed, and the corresponding LSR values (LSR300, LSR500, LSR800, and LSR1000) were calculated. Consistently, the LSR (LSR300, LSR500, LSR800, and LSR1000) values in the malignant group were significantly higher than those in the benign group, with optimal diagnostic thresholds of 0.495, 0.495, 0.485, and 0.73, respectively. The thresholds for LSR500 and LSR1000 align with findings from earlier research (32). Among the evaluated indices, LSR500 demonstrated the highest diagnostic efficacy, with an AUC of 0.682 (95% CI: 0.530–0.834), supporting the recommendation to use DWI with a b-value of 500 s/mm2 for LSR measurement to assess benign and malignant PNMs. This may be attributed to higher b-values, reducing microcirculatory perfusion effects and enhancing diffusion effects, thereby improving diagnostic performance despite a reduced SNR. When using a threshold of 0.495, LSR500 achieved high sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy. Henz Concatto et al. similarly reported LSR values of 1.24 for malignant lesions and 0.55 for benign lesions, with a threshold of 0.86 yielding a sensitivity of 69%, a specificity of 85%, and an accuracy of 74% (47). Discrepancies between our findings and previous studies may result from variations in the b-values used (27,44,47).

It is important to note that granulomas and areas of active inflammation can exhibit high SI on DWI, along with elevated LSR values and reduced ADC values, which may overlap with the diagnostic characteristics of malignant lung lesions. This overlap can lead to diagnostic challenges and potential false positives. For instance, in the present study, a case of a fungal granuloma nodule demonstrated these confounding features. The nodule exhibited very low ADC and very high LSR values, leading to an initial false-positive diagnosis of malignancy. FDG-PET/CT would likewise be expected to demonstrate elevated SUVmax in such lesions, indicating that both diffusion- and metabolism-based imaging modalities may be confounded by highly cellular inflammatory nodules. Comprehensive clinical correlation, histopathological confirmation, and/or follow-up imaging remain essential to minimize misdiagnosis and ensure accurate patient management.

This study exhibited several notable advantages. By integrating both ADC and LSR measurements, it provided a comprehensive framework for differentiating benign lesions from malignant pulmonary lesions. These parameters showed promise as quantitative imaging biomarkers; however, their reproducibility across different b-values, scanners, and field strengths requires further validation. Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. Firstly, this was a single-center study with a relatively small sample size, which might limit generalizability and increase the risk of overfitting, despite internal validation indicating stable diagnostic performance. Secondly, analyses were performed at the lesion level; although GEE were applied to account for intra-patient clustering, adjusted results were attenuated and the associations were no longer statistically significant, indicating potential residual clustering effects. Thirdly, the study did not examine differences in ADC and LSR values across specific malignant histological subtypes, which might provide additional insights into their utility for precision diagnostics. Fourthly, although a multi–b-value DWI sequence was acquired, IVIM-derived parameters were not analyzed because IVIM biexponential fitting was not performed; this limited our ability to assess perfusion-related metrics. Inter-reader agreement was not formally evaluated, which might affect measurement reliability. Although LSR values across different b-values exhibited similar overall diagnostic performance, the optimal threshold for LSR1000 (0.73) was notably higher than that of other b-values (~0.49). This discrepancy indicates that threshold determination is sensitive to the underlying signal distribution and may not be directly comparable across b-values. Optimal b-value selection has not been standardized, as low b-values could be influenced by microvascular perfusion and high b-values could reduce SNR. Thresholds derived from this single-center cohort may not be generalizable to other populations or acquisition protocols. Despite these limitations, the study provided proof-of-concept evidence, supporting the combined use of ADCmax and LSR500 as complementary, non-invasive imaging biomarkers. All evaluated indices (ADCmean, ADCmin, ADCmax, LSR300, LSR500, LSR800, and LSR1000) demonstrated high diagnostic efficacy, with ADCmax showing the second highest combined performance. LSR measurements were robust and easily obtained, highlighting their potential applicability in routine clinical practice. Future multicenter studies with larger, stratified cohorts, standardized acquisition protocols, and external validation are warranted to confirm these findings, refine b-value selection, and optimize clinical implementation.

As a non-invasive, contrast-free, and radiation-free imaging biomarkers, ADCmean, ADCmax, ADCmin, LSR300, LSR500, LSR800, and LSR1000 derived from multi–b-value DWI demonstrated diagnostic utility, with ADCmax showing the best performance among individual parameters. LSR measurements were easy to obtain and may be suitable for clinical application.

Conclusions

In conclusion, malignant PNMs exhibited significantly lower ADC values and higher LSR values compared with benign lesions, rendering these metrics as valuable imaging biomarkers for differentiation. The combination of ADCmax and LSR500 provided superior diagnostic performance compared with either parameter alone, supporting their complementary utility. However, after adjustment for intra-patient clustering using GEE, these associations were attenuated and the associations were no longer statistically significant, indicating that the findings should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Future studies with larger, stratified, and multicenter cohorts are warranted to confirm these results, evaluate performance across diverse pathological subtypes, and refine the clinical application of multi–b-value DWI-derived ADC and LSR in precision diagnostics.
