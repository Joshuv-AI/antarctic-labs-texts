Emerging trends and hotspots in lung-protective ventilation from 2006 to 2025: a bibliometric analysis
Authors: Shenqi Wang, Chen Xie, Xinchen Tao, Tao Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPostoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs) significantly contribute to perioperative mortality and morbidity worldwide. Lung-protective ventilation minimizes ventilator-induced lung injury (VILI) and improves outcomes, but implementation varies widely across individuals and practices. Enhancing patient outcomes can be achieved by comprehending the research trends and current hot topics in lung-protective ventilation. This bibliometric analysis evaluates publication trends and research focus in the field of lung-protective ventilation, aiming to highlight potential future research trajectories.MethodsThe current publication trends and hotspots in lung-protective ventilation research from 2006 to 2025 were analyzed using the CiteSpace software, R package bibliometrix, Online Analysis Platform of Literature Metrology, and VOSviewer. Science Citation Index-Expanded (SCI-E) within the Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC) was used to retrieve English articles.ResultsA total of 818 articles focusing on lung-protective ventilation research from the past two decades were collected. The leading countries in this domain were the USA and China. The University of Toronto and Paolo Pelosi stood out as the primary institute and scholar, respectively. The top 10 primary clusters identified in the co-occurrence cluster analysis were coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), PPCs, extracorporeal CO2 removal, respiratory distress syndrome, one lung ventilation, titration of positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP), acute respiratory failure, oxygenation, work of breathing, and intensive care unit (ICU). The references burst reveal an evolutionary advancement from ventilation techniques to supplementary treatments to current precision medicine approaches. Keyword burst detection emphasized PPCs, mechanical power, driving pressure, and respiratory mechanics.ConclusionsGlobal publications on lung-protective ventilation research have experienced significant growth over the past 20 years, reflecting its clinical importance in critical care. This trend emphasizes the need for continued research into innovative strategies to optimize ventilation practices and improve patient outcomes while minimizing mechanical ventilation complications. Future research should prioritize patient-centered outcomes to translate scientific advances into practical improvements for respiratory disorders.

Highlight box

Key findings

• This is the first bibliometric analysis specifically focusing on lung‑protective ventilation research from 2006 to 2025. Research hotspots have shifted from low tidal volume and positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) to driving pressure, mechanical power, and respiratory mechanics. Postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs), mechanical power, driving pressure, and respiratory mechanics are the current research frontiers.

What is known and what is new?

• Lung‑protective ventilation reduces ventilator‑induced lung injury and improves outcomes in acute respiratory distress syndrome and surgical patients.

• This study provides the first objective, data‑driven roadmap of lung‑protective ventilation research over the past two decades. It quantifies publication trends, identifies leading contributors, and maps the paradigm shift from fixed protective strategies to individualized, physiology‑guided ventilation (driving pressure, mechanical power, respiratory mechanics).

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The persistent focus on PPCs, driving pressure, and mechanical power indicates that the field has moved beyond simply “using low tidal volume” toward precision medicine in mechanical ventilation.

• Future research should prioritize driving pressure‑guided ventilation protocols in clinical trials, validation of mechanical power as a practical bedside target, and implementation science to translate individualized PEEP and driving pressure into routine perioperative practice.

Introduction

Postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs) are recognized as a major factor in mortality and morbidity during the perioperative period, impacting both non-cardiothoracic surgical populations and cardiothoracic surgery (1,2). PPCs can involve a range of pathologies, including worsening pulmonary embolism, pneumothorax, pneumonia, atelectasis, acute lung injury (ALI), pulmonary edema, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), and respiratory failure. Weiser et al.’s study gathered surgical data from 56 out of 192 World Health Organization (WHO) member states, estimating that 234.2 million major surgical procedures are performed each year globally, which contributes to the burden of PPCs (2). Milder events, such as atelectasis (55.8%) (1), are common, while severe complications like acute ARDS (<2%) (3) and respiratory failure (3.4%) (4) are less frequent but entail serious consequences (5). For this reason, PPCs are strongly linked to higher patient mortality rates during across short- and long-term follow-ups, prolonged hospital stays, and substantial healthcare costs (6,7). Factors contributing to an elevated risk of PPC can be categorized as patient-related (e.g., advanced age, poor health, substance use, and comorbidities) and procedure-related, such as duration, type of surgery, and anesthetic method. These factors may outweigh patient-related factors in risk assessment (8). Addressing PPCs is crucial for enhancing surgical outcomes and alleviating healthcare burdens.

PPCs are, at least partially, influenced by the manner in which intraoperative ventilation is administered. Therefore, PPCs could be considered a form of ventilator-induced lung injury (VILI) that may be mitigated through the implementation of lung-protective ventilation strategies (9). Unlike the concept of “lung protection”, which involves various perioperative strategies like smoking cessation, pharmacological interventions, ventilatory techniques, physiotherapy, and fluid management, “lung-protective ventilation strategies” focus on minimizing VILI, optimizing oxygenation, and utilizing specific physiologic parameters. These strategies include applying low tidal volumes (Vts), moderate levels of positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP), and limiting plateau pressures to reduce barotrauma and volutrauma, ultimately leading to better outcomes in patients (8). Over the last 20 years, the concept of lung-protective ventilation strategies has become a fundamental principle in the care of individuals experiencing respiratory failure (9). Despite the adoption of lung-protective ventilation, there is still uncertainty surrounding the advantages of using higher levels of PEEP for postoperative ventilation in specific patients, highlighting the need for additional research. Exploring the optimal balance of PEEP levels tailored to individual patient needs could enhance postoperative recovery and reduce complications. Lung-protective ventilation has evolved beyond low Vt strategies in ARDS patients to encompass driving pressure limitation, mechanical power optimization, individualized PEEP, and respiratory mechanics monitoring. Concurrently, research in this domain exhibits a distinct interdisciplinary character, encompassing critical care medicine, anesthesiology, respiratory physiology, perioperative medicine, and translational mechanism studies. By integrating knowledge from various fields, providing comprehensive information on lung-protective ventilation strategies and research hotspots for these departments will enhance clinical practice, ensuring patients receive optimal respiratory support during and after surgery.Bibliometric analysis employs mathematical and statistical methods to provide a comprehensive overview of a particular research field (10,11). Through objective mapping of citation networks, co-citation clusters, and keyword bursts, it is possible to systematically display the knowledge structure, collaboration networks, research topic shifts, keyword emergences, and potential research gaps in the field. Given the rapid evolution of lung-protective ventilation, transitioning from a uniform approach to a personalized, multidisciplinary focus underscores the urgent requirement for a comprehensive and impartial overview. By analyzing publications from 2006 to 2025, the main aim of this study is to identify the key knowledge base of lung-protective ventilation research, describe the evidence of research themes transitioning from low Vt and PEEP to driving pressure, mechanical power, and individualized ventilation, and highlight future directions for enhancement, providing guiding information for the implementation and advancement of lung-protective ventilation strategies across multiple disciplines. We present this article in accordance with the BIBLIO reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-0738/rc).

Methods

Data sources and search strategies

The extraction and downloads of the data were conducted on April 28, 2026, to prevent any bias from database updates, ensuring that all procedures were finalized on the same day. The relevant publications were retrieved in Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC) by using the following search strategy: topic search (TS) = ((“lung protection” OR “pulmonary protection” OR “protect lung” OR “protect pulmonary” OR “lung protective” OR “pulmonary protective” OR “lung-protective” OR “pulmonary-protective”) AND (“ventilation” OR “ventilatory” OR “ventilation strategy” OR “ventilatory strategy”)); time period =2006–2025; language = English; and document type = original article. The exclusion criteria comprised: (I) studies that discuss “lung protection” in a general manner without addressing specific ventilation or VILI frameworks; (II) studies on pharmacological lung protection or mechanisms unrelated to ventilation strategies; and (III) document types excluding articles (e.g., book chapters, reviews, conference summaries, meta-analyses, editorial material, letters, comments, etc.). Data comprising complete records were obtained from the Science Citation Index-Expanded (SCI-E) within WoSCC, in the format of text files. After conducting the primary data search, two researchers (S.W. and T.L.) independently assessed all manuscripts to ensure their relevance to the study’s focus. In instances of disagreement, the final determination was made by a third researcher, C.X., who possesses nearly a decade of experience in lung research. Bibliometric platform analysis

All data, along with the referenced sources and records that fulfilled the criteria of the WoSCC database (http://wcs.webofknowledge.com), were downloaded and saved as .txt files. The next step involved importing them into CiteSpace V6.2.R7 (Drexel University, Philadelphia, PA, USA), Online Analysis Platform of Literature Metrology (https://bibliometric.com/app), VOS viewer 1.6.19 (Leiden University, Leiden, the Netherlands), and R package bibliometrix for further analysis to outline all literature characteristics concerning lung-protective ventilation.

The widely recognized bibliometric visualization tool, CiteSpace, was used to visualize the lung-protective ventilation research landscape through co-citation analysis, author collaboration patterns, citation bursts, and keyword trends. Knowledge maps visualize entities (authors, references) as nodes and relationships (collaboration, co-citation) as links, varying in size.

The Online Analysis Platform of Literature Metrology generated a histogram illustrating annual publication trends by country and pinpointed the 10 most highly cited journals in the field of lung-protective ventilation. VOSviewer software visualized country/institutional partnerships, with node size indicating publication volume and line width representing cooperation strength. The R package bibliometrix generated a keyword cloud of the top 100 high-frequency keywords.

Results

Quantity and trends of published articles

Out of 2,143 articles published in the SCI-E between 2006 and 2025, 1,325 were excluded based on our criteria. Consequently, our analysis centered on 818 articles concerning lung-protective ventilation during the specified period (Figure 1). The annual publication count is depicted in Figure 2A. From 2006 to 2020, publications rose steadily with minor fluctuations. In contrast, the period spanning 2021 to 2025 saw a notable increase in publications, almost three times the previous average. Furthermore, a growth trend model was created using Microsoft Excel 2021 with the equation f(x) = 0.1274x2 + 0.5975x + 16.349 (R2=0.8396). This model forecasted around 111 articles to be published by 2030. Among 818 articles, 723 focused on clinical and 95 on basic research, with clinical articles outnumbering basic articles annually. Figure 2B depicts the number of publications from the top 10 countries over the last 20 years. The USA led in lung-protective ventilation research with 245 publications, followed closely by China and Germany with 183 and 164 publications, respectively. Until 2020, the USA held the top spot in annual publications, while China experienced a notable surge and surpassed the USA in the past 5 years.

Highly cited articles analysis

The quantity of citations is a critical determinant of an article’s influence in a particular research domain. Once the citations for 818 articles have been tallied and ranked, the top 10 are showcased in Table 3. The article with the highest number of citations was published in 2013 by Futier et al. of Harvard University in the N Engl J Med (12), and it has been cited 1,039 times. Examining 400 adults at intermediate to high risk of pulmonary complications undergoing major abdominal surgery, this multicenter, double-blind, randomized controlled trial investigated the impact of intraoperative lung-protective ventilation vs. nonprotective ventilation. The study results demonstrated that intraoperative lung-protective ventilation enhances clinical outcomes in intermediate-to-high-risk patients undergoing major abdominal surgery, leading to a decrease in postoperative major pulmonary and extrapulmonary complications within the first 7 days (10.5% vs. 27.5%) and a lower need for noninvasive ventilation or intubation (5% vs. 17%).

References reveal document connections through co-citation analysis, which identifies references cited together across multiple publications. Figure 5A visualizes a network with 869 nodes and 3,522 links. Figure 5B displays the top 25 burst strength references out of 818 focusing on lung-protective ventilation from 2006 to 2025. The green line spans 2006–2025, while the red line shows a sudden citation increase for a specific reference. Among the 25 references, mechanical ventilation and lung protection emerge as the central theme. Several highly impactful studies focus on “lung-protective ventilation strategies”, aiming to investigate how adjusting respiratory parameters (such as low Vt, high frequency, and pressure-controlled ventilation) can mitigate lung injury in patients. Over time, the early period [2006–2012] was dedicated to establishing and validating core protective ventilation strategies like low Vt and recruitment maneuvers. In the mid-term [2013–2020], the bibliometric patterns were consistent with a transition towards driving pressure, adjunctive therapies [prone positioning and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO)], and expanding the application of protective ventilation during surgery. In the recent period [2021–2025], the co-citation structure suggested the most intense literature outbreaks centered on mechanical power and respiratory mechanics, and updates on the latest ARDS guidelines, indicating a trend towards more precise, personalized, and advanced life support measures in research. The latest reference burst was in 2023, focusing on the European Society of Intensive Care Medicine (ESICM) guidelines regarding the definition, phenotyping, and respiratory support strategies for ARDS (13). The guidelines recommend utilizing low Vt ventilation combined with PEEP titration, prone positioning, and avoiding routine prolonged high-pressure lung recruitment in patients with severe ARDS [partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2)/fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) <150 mmHg]. Additionally, high-flow oxygen therapy can reduce the risk of intubation in non-mechanically ventilated patients with acute hypoxemic respiratory failure. The strongest reference burst (19.31) originates from a retrospective secondary analysis study that appeared in N Engl J Med in 2015 (14). Researchers conducted an examination of individual data from 3,562 ARDS patients participating in nine previously documented randomized trials. Through the application of multilevel mediation analysis, they determined that driving pressure exhibited the strongest correlation with survival among ventilation parameters. Notably, decreases in driving pressure resulting from modifications in ventilator settings were significantly linked to enhanced survival rates.

Discussion

Our study conducts an initial bibliometric and visual analysis of lung-protective ventilation research spanning from 2006 to 2025. By identifying research trends and hotspots in this area, our analysis offers valuable insights for researchers and can serve as a guide for future studies.

Overall trends in the field of lung-protective ventilation research

The number of lung-protective ventilation studies showed a slight increase in the first 15 years, but there was a sudden surge after 2021, with four times more publications compared to the average number before. This rapid growth indicates a heightened awareness and urgency surrounding lung-protective ventilation, likely influenced by recent global health challenges. Since 2020, the global pandemic of COVID-19, an infectious respiratory tract disease caused by the newly emergent coronavirus severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), underscores the urgent need for the medical scientific community to explore new therapies (15,16). Additionally, the emergence of new technologies and methodologies has facilitated more comprehensive investigations into respiratory mechanisms. In the past decade, lung ultrasound has gained increasing recognition and popularity in the detection of pulmonary complications in anesthesiology and ICU patients due to its non-invasive, sensitive, and portable advantages, with a plethora of related articles emerging rapidly (1,17). Recently, electrical impedance tomography (EIT) has become a non-invasive method for lung imaging, offering immediate insights into regional tidal ventilation and lung mechanics. This technology shows potential in assisting with PEEP selection by reducing lung collapse and overdistension, enabling real-time monitoring of lung function and ventilation distribution in patients (18,19). Besides, basic study findings have also highlighted the importance of understanding the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying lung injury, paving the way for innovative therapeutic strategies. Research has increasingly focused on the role of inflammatory pathways, surfactant dynamics, nervonic acid, and the impact of mechanical ventilation settings on lung health (20,21). These advancements enable researchers to assess the effectiveness of various protective strategies more accurately. Furthermore, collaborations between multidisciplinary teams have fostered the development of novel approaches, integrating insights from molecular biology, engineering, and clinical practice (22,23). As a result, the landscape of lung-protective ventilation research is evolving, with an emphasis on personalized medicine and tailored interventions that consider individual patient characteristics and responses. This shift not only enhances the efficacy of treatments but also aims to minimize potential adverse effects associated with traditional lung-protective ventilation strategies.

The USA has always been the primary country involved in lung-protective ventilation research for the past two decades, leading in the total number of publications. Furthermore, in numerous other research fields, the USA consistently ranks first in terms of publication output (11,24,25). Several contextual factors, such as the country’s strong healthcare infrastructure, substantial investment in biomedical research, close cooperation with other countries, and a culture that prioritizes innovation in academic and clinical environments, may be relevant. Since 2021, China has significantly increased its publication output, surpassing that of the USA. Descriptively, this period coincides with increased national funding for biomedical research and a rapidly aging demographic profile (26,27). However, a causal link between these demographic or policy factors and publication output cannot be directly established from our bibliometric data. Therefore, while the temporal correlation is noteworthy, any interpretation of the drivers behind this increase remains speculative and requires further qualitative or policy-level investigation. Canada, especially Toronto, has made significant contributions to the field of lung-protective ventilation based on factors such as the number of citations, institutional collaborations, and high productive authors. The main reasons for this are Toronto’s pioneering history and world-class research platform in lung transplantation. The world’s first successful lung transplant was performed at Toronto General Hospital in 1983 (28). Renowned experts in the field of global lung transplantation, including Dr. Shaf Keshavjee and Dr. Mingyao Liu from the University of Toronto, have pioneered groundbreaking techniques such as ex vivo lung perfusion (EVLP) (29,30). This innovative approach is widely recognized as a significant advancement and is highly valued in the field of lung transplantation. Furthermore, the partnership among prestigious universities, research institutions, and healthcare organizations has enabled groundbreaking studies that have influenced current practices. Emerging nations are also making strides, providing valuable insights and diverse perspectives that enhance the global conversation on lung-protective ventilation. With ongoing advancements in the field, incorporating artificial intelligence and machine learning into pulmonary research shows potential for improving diagnostic precision and personalized treatment, potentially transforming patient care in respiratory medicine.

Prior research and emerging trends focus on lung-protective ventilation research

From 2006 to 2012, establishing lung-protective ventilation strategies was a key focus of research at that time. The core of this phase was to validate and promote the application of low Vt ventilation and high PEEP in ARDS patients. In the study by Terragni et al. (31), it was found that limiting Vt to 6 mL/kg predicted body weight and plateau pressure to 30 cmH2O may not be sufficient in patients characterized by a larger nonaerated compartment. However, the meta-analysis by Serpa Neto et al. (32) demonstrates that protective ventilation with lower Vt was associated with better clinical outcomes. Mechanical ventilation with conventional Vt (10 mL/kg) contributes to the development of lung injury in patients without ALI at the onset of mechanical ventilation (33). Gattinoni’s research introduced the concept of the percentage of potentially recruitable lung and confirmed that this percentage varies significantly and is closely linked to the response to PEEP in ARDS patients (34). All these findings have played a pivotal role in shaping evidence-based strategies for low Vt in lung-protective ventilation. This indicates that in the early stages of ARDS research, the primary focus of the academic community was to validate and promote the fundamental concept of “lung-protective ventilation” with the aim of establishing clinical gold standards.

In 2013, Futier et al.’s study (12) (highest cited) revealed that employing a lung-protective ventilation approach during major abdominal surgeries for intermediate and high-risk patients led to enhanced clinical results and decreased healthcare expenses. This milestone clinical research publication represents the successful expansion of lung protection principles from the ICU to the operating room, greatly broadening the application scope of lung-protective ventilation strategies. Besides, Slutsky’s review (35) emphasized the concept of “biotrauma”—mechanical ventilation not only causes ventilator-associated lung injury but also affects systemic organs by releasing inflammatory mediators, driving the expansion of lung-protective strategies to a systemic level. In addition to low Vt or PEEP, highly cited literature also reflects the expansion of lung-protective ventilation research towards driving pressure. Neto’s study (36) suggests that driving pressure was the only significant mediator of the effects of protective ventilation on development of PPCs. Amato et al. (14) reanalyzed randomized controlled trial (RCT) data and found driving pressure more prognostically valuable than Vt or PEEP, prompting a shift from volume-control to stress/strain-guided ventilation strategies that prioritize driving pressure assessment. Our burst analysis shows that studies related to driving pressure remained highly active well into the early 2020s, underscoring that limiting cyclic lung stress (rather than simply lowering Vt or PEEP) has become a central therapeutic target.

In recent years, the focus of scholarly literature has further deepened and refined. International expert panel-based consensus recommendations for lung-protective ventilation suggest that individualized mechanical ventilation may improve the mechanics of breathing and respiratory function, and prevent PPCs (37). The escalation in COVID-19-related research underscores the rapid shift in research priorities during a global health crisis. Beyond the immediate effects of the pandemic, COVID-19 has highlighted enduring lessons that continue to influence the field, such as the importance of respiratory mechanics (specifically low compliance and high drive), the efficacy of prone positioning for non-intubated patients, and the need for individualized PEEP titration (13,38). The concurrent rise in the utilization of “respiratory mechanics” as a keyword indicates that the pandemic has reaffirmed the pivotal role of bedside physiological monitoring, encompassing parameters like driving pressure, elastance, and transpulmonary pressure, in guiding timely ventilator adjustments.

Hotspots in lung-protective ventilation research

The keyword burst reflected increased trends in lung-protective ventilation over the last two decades in different time frames. Initially, the emphasis was on developing mechanical ventilation techniques like lung recruitment, permissive hypercapnia and pressure support ventilation. The focus shifted towards extending lung-protective ventilation practices from the ICU to the operating room, as detailed in the reference bust.

The continuous significance of PPCs between 2024 and 2025 highlights the rise of perioperative lung-protective ventilation as an independent and essential research domain, generating extensive clinical interest and potential applications. This underscores the requirement for heightened attention in the future towards a comprehensive management approach incorporating preoperative risk assessment, intraoperative protective strategies, early postoperative interventions, and personalized protection protocols tailored to diverse surgical procedures and patient populations.

Mechanical power refers to the integrates multiple ventilator variables (Vt, driving pressure, PEEP, and respiratory rate) into a unified measure of energy transfer to the respiratory system (39). This approach aims to refine ventilation strategies by focusing on the overall energy delivered to the lungs, potentially offering a more comprehensive framework for understanding and mitigating VILI (40). As research progresses, the integration of mechanical power into clinical practice may enhance patient outcomes by allowing clinicians to tailor interventions based on a more nuanced understanding of lung mechanics (41). By prioritizing these advanced metrics, the medical community hopes to establish clearer guidelines that reflect the complexities of individual patient responses, ultimately leading to more effective and personalized ventilation strategies.

Since 2023, driving pressure has emerged as a key term in the field. The traditional ventilation strategy based on “6–8 mL/kg of ideal body weight” is a population-average empirical formula, inherently overlooking individual differences. Driving pressure, calculated as plateau pressure minus PEEP for passive patients, directly reflects the pressure exerted per unit lung volume. Numerous studies confirm driving pressure as the ventilation variable most closely linked to survival rate changes, with patients having driving pressure greater than 14 cmH2O facing increased mortality risk (42,43). The concept of driving pressure highlights the core objective of lung-protective ventilation -minimizing the cyclic stress on lung parenchyma, marking a milestone in the history of lung-protective ventilation research. The emergence of driving pressure paves the way for a resurgence in respiratory mechanics.

Respiratory mechanics refer to the expression of lung function through measures of pressure and flow (44). From these, a variety of derived indices can be determined, such as flow, pressure, volume, compliance, resistance, and work of breathing. Lung volumes are directly influenced by these factors, consequently affecting functional residual capacity (FRC) and gas exchange. By conducting real-time assessments of respiratory mechanics, clinicians can address stress and strain, prevent alveolar overdistension and collapse, and optimize oxygenation and ventilation, thereby enhancing patient outcomes (45). The ongoing exploration of respiratory mechanics and their implications for patient outcomes signifies a pivotal transition in critical care, fostering a more nuanced approach to managing respiratory failure and optimizing ventilatory support.

Limitations and future directions

Our analysis has several limitations. Initially, it was confined to articles within the WoSCC database, potentially limiting the representation of lung-protective ventilation research publications. However, the WoSCC data provided essential bibliometric information, such as title, author, institute, and references. Secondly, despite a recent increase in publications, the overall volume remains relatively low, potentially introducing bias due to limited literature. To enhance future studies, researchers could broaden the scope by incorporating additional databases and languages for a more comprehensive evaluation of lung-protective ventilation research. Lastly, our study identifies and visualizes the evolution of research themes, representing a well-established historical narrative in ARDS management. However, it does not discover new scientific milestones through the CiteSpace software. Therefore, our analysis does not claim causal novelty; rather, it provides a quantitative, data-driven confirmation of this known trajectory. The value of our study lies in objectively mapping the intensity, timing, and interconnectedness of these research themes as reflected in citation patterns. Consequently, our findings should be interpreted as a descriptive and confirmatory complement to existing knowledge, not as a source of new mechanistic or clinical discovery.

The recent surge in research attention towards PPCs, mechanical power, driving pressure, and respiratory mechanics has provided clear guidance for researchers in the field of lung-protective ventilation. Emerging research directions identified by our analysis include: (I) the validation of mechanical power as a clinically actionable target for minimizing VILI; (II) the integration of bedside respiratory mechanics monitoring such driving pressure into routine perioperative care; (III) the development of personalized PEEP titration protocols based on individual lung mechanics rather than fixed PEEP/FiO2 tables. Clinicians and researchers can derive two actionable insights from this analysis. Firstly, prioritize driving pressure-guided ventilation protocols over fixed Vt strategies in future clinical trials, as evidence indicates that reducing driving pressure is more strongly linked to survival than simply lowering Vt. Secondly, urgent implementation science is required to translate established evidence on individualized PEEP and driving pressure into routine perioperative practice, especially in non-academic and resource-limited settings.

Conclusions

Using a bibliometric tool, we summarized and assessed publications in the field of lung-protective ventilation between 2006 and 2025. The USA, China, and University of Toronto emerged as the major contributors to this area. The research in this field showcases an evolutionary progression: from the establishment of essential ventilation strategies offering survival benefits, to the exploration of diverse additional therapies, and further advancement towards the current emphasis on individualized precision treatments, with recent keyword surges highlighting ongoing efforts on integrated ventilation strategies to address PPCs. In the evolving field of lung-protective ventilation research, it is crucial to prioritize patient-centered outcomes to effectively translate scientific progress into tangible improvements for individuals with respiratory disorders.
