Abstract




Abstract

We report X-ray Thomson scattering measurements of shock-compressed aluminium at densities between 3.75 and 4.5 g/cm
3
and a temperature of approximately 0.6 eV, performed at the HED-HiBEF instrument of the European XFEL using the DiPOLE-100X laser drive. By probing plasmon dispersion across momentum transfers
k=0.99
– 2.57
{\mathrm{\text{Å}}}^{-1}
with high statistical fidelity, we directly test competing theoretical descriptions of electron dynamics under extreme conditions. Time-dependent density functional theory reproduces both the observed plasmon energies and spectral shapes across the full
k
-range, whereas standard mean-field and static local field correction models systematically overestimate the plasmon frequency, even for aluminium – a canonical uniform electron gas metal. By considering the localisation of electrons around ions and the loss of crystalline symmetry due to disorder in liquid aluminium, our results provide direct experimental evidence for the failure of simple uniform electron gas models in warm dense matter and establish time-dependent density functional theory as a reliable approach for describing electronic correlations under these conditions.

Introduction

Warm dense matter (WDM) – an extreme state of matter between traditional condensed matter and plasma conditions – has emerged as a highly active subject of research spanning plasma physics, material science, and quantum chemistry
[
1
,
2
]
. Unlike cold solids or strongly coupled plasmas, WDM is defined by moderate temperatures, densities, and pressures, sitting between the well-understood limits of solid-state physics and classical plasma theory. These conditions abound in a variety of astrophysical objects
[
3
]
, such as giant planet interiors
[
2
]
, brown dwarfs
[
4
]
and white dwarf atmospheres
[
5
,
6
]
.
In addition, WDM is relevant for material science, with the recent experimental demonstrations of nanodiamond formation
[
7
,
8
,
9
]
and liquid carbon
[
10
]
at high pressures being pertinent cases.
A particularly important technological application of WDM is given by inertial confinement fusion
[
11
,
12
,
13
]
, where both the fusion fuel and the ablator material have to traverse the WDM regime in a controlled way to reach ignition
[
14
,
15
]
.

Theoretical descriptions of WDM are complicated by the interplay of Coulomb coupling, thermal excitations, quantum degeneracy, delocalisation, and partial ionisation
[
1
,
16
,
17
]
. Therefore, WDM theory often relies on a number of model assumptions, such as the widely used Chihara decomposition into effectively bound and free electrons within the chemical picture
[
18
,
19
]
. This separation, though widely used, lacks a clear theoretical justification in the warm dense regime and serves mainly as a practical modelling convenience. State-of-the-art
ab initio
density functional theory (DFT) simulations
[
2
]
are not based on this assumption, but their accuracy is determined by the choice of the electronic exchange – correlation (XC) functional, and, in the case of time-dependent DFT (TDDFT)
[
20
]
, the dynamic XC-kernel or dynamic XC-potential; these cannot be obtained within DFT itself and have to be approximated
[
21
]
. While quantum Monte Carlo (QMC) simulations, are, in principle, capable of providing exact results without the need for any empirical input
[
22
,
23
]
, they are currently restricted to light elements at relatively high temperatures.
This bottleneck is further exacerbated by the near complete absence of reliable experimental measurements that are constrained enough to decide between different theoretical models over a significant part of the relevant WDM parameter space.

In this work, we remedy this situation by presenting angular-resolved X-ray Thomson scattering (XRTS) and X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements
[
24
]
of plasmon excitations in shock-compressed warm dense aluminium performed at the HED-HiBEF instrument of the European X-ray Free Electron Laser (XFEL)
[
25
]
. Our approach combines self-seeded
[
26
]
XFEL pulses
[
27
]
with the stable and high repetition-rate optical DiPOLE (Diode Pumped Optical Laser for Experiments) laser
[
28
,
29
,
30
]
to obtain inelastic scattering spectra across multiple scattering angles with high statistics. This approach enables us to probe electronic correlations through measurements of the dynamic structure factor (DSF)
S(k,\omega)
across a broad range of momentum transfers,
k=0.99
to
2.57

{\mathrm{\text{Å}}}^{-1}
. At low
k
, the inelastic scattering spectrum is dominated by sharp collective modes, plasmons – that reflect the coordinated motion of many electrons. As
k
increases, we observe a smooth transition towards a regime where the response is shaped by the behaviour of individual electrons and subtle interactions among them (exchange and correlation effects). This crossover from collective to single-particle excitations allows us to test theoretical models under conditions where the underlying electron dynamics are particularly sensitive to how these many-body effects are treated
[
31
,
32
]
.

Systematic studies of inelastic X-ray scattering (IXS) in simple metals have played a central role in testing electronic structure theories across a wide range of momenta. Among these, the pioneering work of Schülke
et al.

[
33
]
on aluminium mapped out both plasmon resonances and particle-hole excitations, providing dynamic structure factors that serve as critical benchmarks for theoretical approaches using synchrotron radiation. Their experimental dynamic structure factors provided crucial data for validating theoretical models, highlighting clear deviations from simple jellium calculations based on the random phase approximation (RPA). These discrepancies underscored the necessity of including local-field effects and momentum-dependent quasiparticle lifetimes for accurately capturing electron dynamics, even at ambient conditions
[
34
,
35
]
. Indeed, subsequent studies combining advanced
ab initio
calculations with IXS data demonstrated that static local field corrections alone are insufficient, and time-dependent density functional theory – particularly with explicit treatment of dynamic electron correlations and lifetime effects – is essential to achieve quantitative agreement with measured spectra at large momentum transfers
[
35
]
. Nonetheless, subtle residual differences at higher energy losses and large
k
remain, often attributed to incomplete descriptions of electron lifetime and exchange-correlation effects. Further work was undertaken in the past using electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS) under ambient conditions
[
36
,
37
]
and more recently XRTS measurements at the European XFEL were successfully used to benchmark theoretical predictions
[
38
,
39
]
.

However, XRTS measurements of plasmon-type features in the warm dense matter regime have faced significant experimental limitations due to the low brightness and large bandwidth of the X-ray scattering source, drive laser and spectrometer used. Moreover, EELS is not practically realisable in this regime due to the thin sample thicknesses required, which does not allow shock-compression nor a large enough scattering volume for the electrons. Earlier work at the Linac Coherent Light Source (LCLS) observed pronounced collisional damping in aluminium plasmas at solid density heated with the X-rays, but with conclusions that depended sensitively on model assumptions and suffered from limited angular coverage
[
40
]
. Later work at LCLS by Preston
et al.

[
41
]
measured XRTS in shock-compressed aluminium but was limited by large experimental uncertainties, which meant that even fundamentally different theoretical models could not be reliably distinguished within the reported error bars. In parallel, Witte
et al.

[
42
]
reported the first demonstration of excellent agreement between DFT-MD simulations and XRTS measurements of aluminium at the LCLS, without recourse to fitting the dynamic structure factor. Their analysis highlighted the extraction of nonlinear Landau damping and clear deviations from Drude-like behaviour in a nominally simple metal, a finding later extended in follow-up work
[
43
]
.
As a result, ongoing debate has persisted regarding the interpretation of plasmon features in the warm dense regime
[
44
]
.

A central advance of this work lies in the ability to rapidly and reproducibly change the experimental configuration, enabling systematic exploration of different scattering vectors with precision diagnostics. By coupling the high-stability DiPOLE laser to the self-seeded XFEL beamline at the European XFEL, we achieve consistent drive conditions, enabling systematic exploration of warm dense aluminium across multiple scattering geometries. The reproducibility of the shock – verified through hydrodynamics simulations, velocity interferometry (VISAR
[
45
]
), and X-ray diffraction – ensures a plasma state with a well-characterised density profile suitable for stringent model comparison. The X-ray source at the European XFEL is bright and has a narrow bandwidth when operated in self-seeded mode (see Methods). This, in conjuction with the spectroscopy platform at the HED-HiBEF instrument, is capable of resolving the structure of the XRTS in aluminium over multiple momentum transfers with high fidelity.

The quality of the data allows it to be compared with various theoretical models and for state-of-the-art TDDFT simulations
[
46
]
to be conclusively validated. It is found that more simple approaches, based on the uniform electron gas (UEG) model
[
47
,
31
]
such as the bare random phase approximation (RPA) or its combination with a QMC based static local field correction (LFC)
[
48
,
49
]
, break down even for aluminium, the prototypical uniform electron gas metal. Instead TDDFT is required. This advantage arises because TDDFT inherently accounts for the localisation of electrons and the loss of long-range crystalline order – i.e., the emergence of disorder in the liquid phase – which uniform electron gas models simply cannot capture.
Our results thus have important implications for a plethora of practical applications such as the modelling of astrophysical objects, the interpretation of experiments with WDM
[
1
]
and integrated multi-scale simulations of inertial fusion energy set-ups.
Moreover, our reference data may guide the development of improved future models and further highlights the importance of high-precision experiments for our understanding of extreme states of matter.

Results

In our experiment, ambient and shock-compressed aluminium samples were probed by a self-seeded XFEL pulse with a duration of 25 fs, photon energy of 8307 eV, and a spectral width of approximately 1 eV for the main seeded spike. The effective total experimental resolution, including the broader SASE pedestal and the spectrometer response, amounts to 3.5 eV (FWHM) as determined from fits to the elastic line (see Methods). X-ray diffraction and X-ray Thomson scattering spectra were collected simultaneously using Jungfrau detectors and a von Hámos HAPG spectrometer of resolution
\sim 2
eV
[
50
]
, respectively. The spectrometer angle was varied between
2\theta=13.5^{\circ}
and
35.5^{\circ}
with respect to the incident X-ray axis, corresponding to a momentum transfer range of
k=0.99
to
2.57

{\mathrm{\text{Å}}}^{-1}
, using a motor with an encoder. This arrangement enabled precise control over the probed
k
-vector, while our use of independent diagnostics meant we could directly measure and verify the plasma conditions, including the density gradient, for all scattering measurements. For further details on the experimental configuration see Methods below, as well as the Supplementary Information.

In Figure
1
a we present representative inelastic XRTS spectra for ambient (blue) and shock-compressed (red) aluminium, each measured at distinct
k
-vectors. Each spectrum is the average of around 40 individual laser shots at fixed geometry, with more shots accumulated at higher
k
to account for the reduced scattering cross-section and increased spectral width. This high shot count at each setting is a key advantage of the present experiment, made possible by the repetition-rate and stability of the DiPOLE-XFEL platform. The data are normalised to the elastic peak, and the plasmon feature appears as a signal on the positive energy-loss side of each spectrum.
All spectra clearly exhibit distinct inelastic scattering features, while the sharp rise in intensity below 10 eV originates from strong quasi-elastic scattering by both the ablator and the aluminium sample itself. As the momentum transfer increases in the driven spectra, the plasmon peak systematically shifts to higher energy loss, accompanied by pronounced broadening and growing asymmetry. At the highest
k
, this feature becomes notably broad and weak – a behaviour not attributable to instrumental effects, but rather a natural consequence of enhanced damping as the collective mode fades into the particle-hole continuum. We note that the apparent increase in intensity on the energy gain side is a feature of the source spectrum: when operated in self-seeded mode there is a
\sim 20
eV broad SASE pedestal underneath the self-seeded spike centred at a higher photon energy.

These broadening effects are attributed to increased density and growing Landau damping. For reference, the measured cold plasmon dispersion, along with ambient reference data from previous work
[
38
]
is provided in the Supplementary Information. The measured plasmon peak positions agree with established results at low
k
, validating the calibration of our spectrometer and scattering angles. At higher
k
, we observe a systematic softening of the plasmon as it enters the pair continuum, consistent with enhanced damping and increased coupling to single-particle excitations in the shock-compressed case.

To test various DFT simulation models, we compare their results to each measured momentum transfer. In order to compare experiment and theory on equal footing, we must handle the broadening of the spectra by the combined source-and-instrument function (SIF) of the setup. We treat the SIF as the sum of two Voigt profiles: this accounts for the self-seeded probe (source) consisting of a narrow seeded spike atop a broader SASE pedestal, and the IF being approximately Voigt-like
[
50
]
(For details and the averaged SIF profile used in all fits, see Supplementary Section I and Fig.
12
.). This SIF shape was fitted to the quasi-elastic scattering feature. Since direct deconvolution of the experimental data is unstable, we instead convolved all theoretical spectra (TDDFT, RPA, LFC) with the SIF to model the inelastic spectrum, and then added this with the SIF (i.e. the elastic scattering) to construct theoretical spectra, with the relative inelastic and elastic contributions chosen to best match the full spectrum at each
k
.
To estimate the uncertainty in the experimental response function, we performed double-Voigt fits to the quasi-elastic peaks at all four measured
k
-values, obtaining four independent sets of SIF parameters. We then used the four independently fitted SIF profiles, each reflecting the uncertainties from the double-Voigt approximations at the respective
k
-values, and convolved each theoretical spectrum with this set of profiles. This approach propagates the fitting uncertainties of the source-and-instrument function – obtained separately at each
k
– through to the final convolved theoretical spectra essentially capturing the uncertainty in the experimental response itself.

This closes the gap left by earlier work, firmly validating TDDFT in the regime probed and demonstrating that the previously reported model ambiguity was primarily a consequence of limited experimental resolution.

Discussion

We have carried out new high-precision XRTS measurements of shock-compressed aluminium by exploiting the unique combination of the high repetition-rate optical DiPOLE 100-X laser with the seeded XFEL configuration and efficient X-ray spectrometers at the European XFEL. This set-up allows for exceptionally controlled and reproducible shock conditions, making it an ideal benchmark for the testing of different theoretical models and ab-initio simulations. To this end, we have performed measurements over a broad range of scattering angles, thereby probing electronic correlations on different length scales from the collective, long-wavelength plasmon regime to the pair continuum where electronic XC effects are important.

In contrast to earlier studies
[
40
,
41
]
, our new results, for the first time, enable a clear and direct discrimination between theoretical models of increasing sophistication. Approximations grounded in the uniform electron gas (UEG) – notably the standard random phase approximation (RPA)
[
31
]
and its extensions with QMC-based local field corrections (LFC)
[
48
,
49
]
– are shown to break down even for aluminium, long considered the prototypical UEG-like metal.

While the shock-compressed region exhibits a density variation of approximately 17%, this non-uniformity is well characterised via simultaneous XRTS and XRD measurements. The temperature, on the other hand, remains essentially uniform throughout most of the target, except for a narrow region from the blow-off plasma of the ablator; this feature is spatially very limited and, given our diagnostic sensitivity and geometry, does not influence the main results. The stability of both the DiPOLE drive and target conditions ensures that shot-to-shot variation remains minimal, so accumulating multiple spectra does not increase this density range. This reproducibility, combined with the sensitivity of our diagnostics, allows us to directly resolve and benchmark theoretical models against well-defined experimental profiles, rather than relying on idealised assumptions of uniformity. We find that state-of-the-art TDDFT calculations
[
46
]
with an adiabatic XC-kernel work very well over the entire considered range of momentum transfers, whereas RPA and LFC overestimate the plasmon shift. TDDFT treats the density response with an explicit exchange–correlation (XC) kernel, so the electron–hole interaction and short-range correlation screening that soften the collective mode are built in. In contrast, RPA ignores the XC kernel and LFC schemes only approximate it, so they miss part of this electron–hole coupling and predict a plasmon that is too stiff, leading to an over-estimated blue-shift. By incorporating the full linear-response XC effects – even in a simple adiabatic form – TDDFT effectively captures these correlation contributions and yields the correct, lower plasmon energy. Beyond this, TDDFT also inherently reflects the loss of crystalline symmetry and increased electron localisation – hallmarks of disorder in the liquid state – that simple UEG-based models neglect.

We note that parallel advances have recently been made in modelling collision-induced broadening and damping of collective excitations using quantum average-atom (AA) models
[
56
]
. By incorporating dynamic, frequency-dependent electron-ion collision frequencies – computed self-consistently from T-matrix and Kubo-Greenwood approaches – within the Mermin dielectric formalism, these models can reproduce much of the plasmon red-shift and broadening observed in experiment and TDDFT for simple metals such as aluminium. However, while this brings AA-Mermin calculations closer to parameter-free predictions, some degree of model dependence and approximation remains, particularly outside the nearly-free-electron limit. The predictive power of TDDFT, with explicit exchange–correlation kernels and no adjustable parameters, remains essential for benchmarking theory and experiment in more strongly correlated and complex WDM regimes.

Additionally, the high quality of our scattering spectra means they are even sensitive to density inhomogeneities within the sample. Consistent estimations of the density distribution come from both XRD and inelastic X-ray scattering. This further highlights the strength of this setup’s use of both XRTS and XRD to cross-validate density conditions in the sample.

These conclusions are of direct relevance for the theoretical modelling of extreme states of matter in a variety of contexts.
In particular, we have experimentally verified the limitations of the UEG model, which is at the heart of many practical WDM related applications.
We note that the present study is focused on the low-temperature end of the WDM regime, which is most relevant to material science applications and planetary science.
These conditions currently remain inaccessible to recent model-free XRTS methodologies
[
57
]
, making the availability of accurate simulation tools particularly important.
At the same time, a similar breakdown of the UEG assumption has been theoretically predicted for hydrogen at temperatures of several electronvolts based on exact QMC calculations
[
23
]
, which will also be of direct relevance for inertial fusion energy applications
[
1
]
.

In addition to these novel theoretical insights, the continuing developments at the European XFEL position it as a state-of-the-art facility, capable of making remarkable high-repetition rate measurements in shock-compressed matter. We expect future improvements in target delivery through the implementation of tape-delivery systems, that can then fully exploit the ultimate repetition rate of the DiPOLE-100X laser. In particular, our set-up will serve as the basis for future investigations of a plethora of other materials over a broad range of conditions, which will open up new avenues for high-precision measurements of extreme states of matter.

Methods

This experiment was performed within a larger community campaign (Proposal 6656) at the High Energy Density (HED) instrument of the European XFEL
[
25
]
, combining the DiPOLE 100-X optical drive laser with X-ray diagnostics optimized for approximately 3.5 eV spectral resolution. The DiPOLE system provided nanosecond-duration, temporally flat-top pulses with exceptional stability (shot-to-shot variations below 0.75%) and operated at 1 Hz repetition rate, enabling laser stability for consistent accumulation over hundreds of laser-driven shots. Laser-driven shots were taking in a pulse-on-demand mode with the laser and X-rays co-timed. At the same time, newly-commissioned HAPG crystals with a large radius of curvature of 120 mm operated in the (004) reflection enabled high-resolution scattering measurements to be collected.

Samples.

The targets consisted of 50 µm-thick polycrystalline aluminium foils (99.999% purity). A 25 µm-thick black Kapton layer served as the laser ablator, uniformly adhered to the aluminium using vacuum-assisted epoxy gluing. This method resulted in a consistent adhesive layer thickness of 10
\pm
1 µm, verified across 160 prepared targets. Additional thin foils (2 µm Fe, 25 µm Kapton, and 50 µm PMMA) were used for spectrometer calibration.

Optical drive laser.

The DiPOLE 100-X laser

29

This pulse duration was optimised to match the shock breakout timing, ensuring continuous compression throughout laser irradiation. The laser intensity profile was flat-top, with short ramps (0.2 ns each) at the beginning and end of the pulse. The beam was focused onto the target at an angle of 22.5

XFEL delivery.

The European XFEL delivered self-seeded X-ray pulses at a photon energy of 8307 eV. The main seeded spike had a shot-averaged intrinsic bandwidth of approximately 1 eV (FWHM), superimposed on a broader SASE pedestal. For spectral analysis, the total source-and-instrument function (SIF) was modelled as the sum of two Voigt profiles: a narrow component with FWHM of 3.3 eV, and a broader pedestal with FWHM of 22.7 eV and a relative peak height of 0.083. The final effective experimental resolution, obtained by direct fits to the elastic line, was 3.5 eV (FWHM). This value was used in all convolutions of theoretical spectra throughout the analysis.

The average pulse energy (
600\,\mu
J) was measured by an X-ray gas monitor (XGM) in the tunnel. X-rays were focused onto the target to form a spot of about 35 µm diameter. The XFEL probe timing was fixed at 6.3 ns, precisely set to diagnose plasma conditions just before the shock breakout as determined by VISAR, and remained constant for all measurements. The timing jitter between the 50 fs short X-ray pulse and the DiPOLE 100-X laser pulses was monitored to be within 100 ps.

Using velocity interferometer system for any reflector (VISAR

Hydrodynamic simulations.

Target conditions under laser compression were modelled using the Lagrangian one-dimensional (1D) radiation-hydrodynamics code HELIOS-CR
[
51
]
, employing the PROPACEOS equation of state for all material layers. The simulation includes the full target structure (Kapton ablator, epoxy glue, aluminium sample) with measured thicknesses, and experimental laser parameters.

The incident laser pulse was represented as a flat-top temporal profile of 8 ns duration at 515 nm wavelength, focused to a supergaussian spot of 250 µm diameter. To scale the 1D simulation to experiment, the input laser energy was calibrated by matching the breakout time predicted by HELIOS-CR to the observed optical breakout in the VISAR data. This yielded an effective absorbed energy of 19 J, corresponding to roughly 75% of the nominal pulse energy, after accounting for optical losses and reflection at the ablator surface.

Output profiles for the density, temperature, and pressure as functions of target depth were extracted at the XFEL probing time of 6.3 ns. The simulations predict a stable compressed region of aluminium with density spanning 3.75–4.5 g/cm
3
and temperature close to 0.6 eV, in good agreement with conditions inferred from XRD (see Figure
2
b). Over the central portion of the target (5–28 µm), the density exhibits a gentle gradient of about 15%, while the temperature is essentially flat to within 2%. Importantly, this density gradient is explicitly accounted for in our analysis: experimental XRD structure factors are compared with TDDFT predictions at several densities within the simulated range, rather than assuming a uniform state. Furthermore, density averaging is used to compare the theoretical XRTS spectra to experiment.

We note that HELIOS-CR simulations are inherently one-dimensional and do not capture possible lateral inhomogeneities or edge effects near the focal spot. However, given the 250 µm focal diameter and 35 µm XFEL probe size, together with the 50 µm sample thickness, such effects are expected to be negligible in the central region examined here.

Independent validation of the simulated compression conditions comes from XRD data. Individual shots were summed and normalised, yielding an averaged experimental ionic structure factor of compressed liquid Al. This was found to closely match TDDFT-based predictions for densities between 4.0 and 4.5 g/cm
3
, supporting the density and temperature range determined from hydrodynamic modelling.

H: Origin of Energy-Gain-Side Signal in XRTS Spectra

As noted in the Results, a small but visible intensity appears on the energy-gain (negative energy loss) side of the XRTS spectra in both ambient and shock-compressed aluminium. Here, we provide the physical and technical background underlying this effect and clarify its implications for data analysis.

For a system in thermal equilibrium, the dynamic structure factor is strictly governed by the detailed balance relation
[
70
,
71
,
72
]
:

S(k,-\omega)=e^{-\hbar\omega/k_{\mathrm{B}}T}S(k,\omega)\,.

(1)

Given plasmon energies
\hbar\omega\simeq 15
–
35
eV and experimental temperatures from
T_{\text{cold}}=0.026
eV to
T_{\text{hot}}\simeq 0.6
eV, the suppression factor becomes

e^{-\hbar\omega/k_{\mathrm{B}}T}\sim\begin{cases}10^{-25}\text{–}10^{-15}&(\text{cold})\\
10^{-11}\text{–}10^{-7}&(\text{hot})\end{cases}

(2)

making any genuine anti-Stokes (energy-gain) process undetectable.

Instead, the observed intensity on the negative energy-loss side is fully accounted for by the convolution of two well-understood, temperature-independent effects: (i) the inherently asymmetric source-and-instrument function (SIF) of the HAPG von Hamos spectrometer, and (ii) the spectral structure of the seeded XFEL pulse. In seeded operation, the XFEL spectrum always features a narrow (
\sim
3.3 eV) “seeded spike” on the low-photon-energy side of a broader SASE pedestal
[
26
,
27
,
50
,
25
]
. This is a deliberate result of monochromator tuning, which ensures spike purity but necessarily creates a low-energy shoulder. When scattered elastically, the resulting line inherits this double structure, and the asymmetric HAPG response (from mosaicity and depth penetration, “Johann error”) further stretches the signal towards negative energy loss
[
73
]
. Ray-tracing and analytic studies confirm that the amplitude of this tail scales with the total elastic and quasi-elastic intensity.

This left-hand (negative energy) wing becomes more pronounced in shock-compressed (hot) spectra – not due to real anti-Stokes processes, but because thermal diffuse scattering and quasi-elastic broadening increase with temperature, as
I_{\mathrm{TDS}}\propto T

[
72
,
74
]
and
I_{\mathrm{qe}}\propto\sqrt{T}

[
74
]
. Sufficient statistics reveal the same effect even in cold spectra. Our analysis routine models the elastic peak as the sum of two Voigt profiles (seed spike plus pedestal) and convolves all theoretical spectra with the measured SIF
[
26
,
50
]
. Minor flat backgrounds from Compton scattering in the Kapton ablator or plasma bremsstrahlung
[
75

are broadband and negligible for the plasmon fits.
