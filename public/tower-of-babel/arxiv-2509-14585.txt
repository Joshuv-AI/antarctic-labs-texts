Online Reinforcement Learning via Sparse Gaussian Mixture Model Q-Functions


Abstract
This paper introduces a structured and interpretable online policy-iteration framework for reinforcement learning (RL), built around the novel class of sparse Gaussian mixture model Q-functions (S-GMM-QFs). Extending earlier work that trained GMM-QFs offline, the proposed framework develops an online scheme that leverages streaming data to encourage exploration. Model complexity is regulated through sparsification by Hadamard overparametrization, which mitigates overfitting while preserving expressiveness. The parameter space of S-GMM-QFs is naturally endowed with a Riemannian manifold structure, allowing for principled parameter updates via online gradient descent on a smooth objective. Numerical tests show that S-GMM-QFs match the performance of dense deep RL (DeepRL) methods on standard benchmarks while using significantly fewer parameters, and maintain strong performance even in low-parameter-count regimes where sparsified DeepRL methods fail to generalize.



1
Introduction

Reinforcement learning (RL) is a machine-learning framework in which an agent controls a system through interactions
with its environment, with the objective of learning an optimal policy that maximizes the expected cumulative reward
resulting from its actions
[
1
,
2
]
. RL provides a principled framework for
addressing challenging sequential decision-making problems across diverse domains, including data mining, and the
training of large language models.

A key concept in RL is the
Q-function,
which estimates the expected cumulative reward after the agent takes an
action in a given state under a specific policy. Classical approaches like Q-learning
[
3
]
and
SARSA
[
4
]
use tabular representations of Q-functions, computing values for all possible state-action
pairs. While effective for discrete-space problems, these methods become impractical for large or continuous
state-action spaces. To address this issue, RL algorithms based on functional approximation of Q-functions—called

approximate
RL—have gained significant research interest
[
1
]
.

In approximate RL, classical kernel-based methods
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
model
Q-functions in Banach spaces of bounded functions, whereas temporal difference (TD)
[
8
]
, least-squares
(LSTD)
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
, Bellman residual (BR)
[
12
]
, and more
recent nonparametric approaches
[
13
,
14
,
15
]
represent them in reproducing
kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHSs)
[
16
,
17
]
. Notwithstanding, nonparametric (kernel)
models typically expand with the amount of data, creating scalability challenges in online settings. Dimensionality
reduction through sparsification
[
9
,
13
]
can mitigate this issue, but often at the cost of degraded
Q-function approximation. Gaussian mixture models (GMMs) have also been used in
distributional
RL, though
their typical role as probability density estimators—either for the joint
p(Q,\mathbf{s},a)

[
18
,
19
,
20
,
21
]
or for the conditional
p(Q{\mathbin{}\mid\mathbin{}}\mathbf{s},a)

[
22
]

distribution—where the Q-function
Q
, state
\mathbf{s}
, and action
a
are treated as random variables, typically
under strong statistical assumptions such as joint Gaussianity of the sampled data
[
21
]
.

A major advance in approximate RL is using deep neural networks as functional Q-function approximators, exemplified by
deep Q-networks (DQNs)
[
23
,
24
]
. While DQNs require many learnable parameters, their parametric
nature provides strong representation power and avoid the model-growth issues of nonparametric methods. Typically, DQNs
learn from past experience
[
25
]
, enabling exploration beyond the current policy. However, these
usually large “black-box” networks are vulnerable to sudden changes in dynamic environments and provide little insight
into the features influencing agent decisions. To reduce model size, sparsification techniques are used: pruning
gradually removes trivial connections from a dense network
[
26
,
27
]
, while sparse training fixes a
sparsity pattern from the start and dynamically adjusts connections
[
28
,
29
]
. These approaches
maintain performance with smaller models, but interpretability remains limited, challenging real-world deployment.

Searching for greater expressive power in approximate RL,
[
30
]
introduced Gaussian mixture model
Q-functions (GMM-QFs) to represent Q-functions as weighted sums of multivariate Gaussian components. In this
formulation, not only the mixture weights but also the mean vectors and covariance matrices of the Gaussian components
are treated as learnable parameters. The number of mixture components is user-specified, allowing control over model
complexity and mitigating the curse of dimensionality often encountered in nonparametric approaches.

In
[
30
]
, GMM-QFs were used to develop an on-policy, offline policy-iteration (PI) framework that does
not exploit experience data, which can bias agents toward suboptimal policies. In contrast, the present work proposes a
PI framework that learns from online streaming data, simultaneously constructing an experience buffer that the agent can
leverage to enhance exploration and policy evaluation. To reduce the number of learnable parameters, the novel class of
sparse GMM-QFs (S-GMM-QFs) is introduced via Hadamard overparametrization, enabling a transparent and interpretable
sparsification of model parameters—unlike in DeepRL, where sparsification typically targets network structures and
often lacks interpretability. Learning with S-GMM-QFs is performed through online gradient descent on a suitably defined
smooth objective over the Riemannian manifold of model parameters. Numerical tests on benchmark datasets show that
S-GMM-QFs maintain strong performance while substantially reducing model complexity. They achieve results comparable
to—and in some cases superior to—DeepRL models with significantly more parameters, and retain their effectiveness
even in low-parameter-count regimes where sparse DeepRL methods fail to generalize. Owing to space limitations, detailed
derivations, convergence analyses, and additional numerical tests will be reported in a separate publication.

2.1
RL notation and basics on Bellman mappings

Let

stands for the long-term cumulative reward achievable if the
agent selects action

in state

. Following
[
1
]

maps a state to an action, as in
\mu(\cdot)\colon\mathfrak{S}\to\mathfrak{A}\colon\mathbf{s}\mapsto\mu(\mathbf{s})
. Denote also the set of all mappings from
\mathfrak{S}
to action
\mathfrak{A}
by
\mathscr{M}
. Let
also
\overline{1,N}\coloneqq\{1,\ldots,N\}
.

The Bellman mapping is a fundamental concept in RL, quantifying the expected cumulative reward an agent can obtain by
taking action
a
in state
\mathbf{s}
. More precisely, when Q-functions are drawn from the functional space

\mathscr{B}
—typically the Banach space of essentially bounded functions
[
1
]
—the (classical)

\mathbfcal{T}^{\diamond}_{\mu}\colon\mathscr{B}\to\mathscr{B}\colon Q\mapsto\mathbfcal{T}^{\diamond}_{\mu}Q
for

is defined as:
\forall(\mathbf{s},a)
,

\displaystyle(\mathbfcal{T}_{\mu}^{\diamond}Q)(\mathbf{s},a)

\displaystyle\coloneqq r(\mathbf{s},a)+\alpha\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{s}^{\prime}{\mathbin{}\mid\mathbin{}}(\mathbf{s},a)}\bigl[Q(\mathbf{s}^{\prime},\mu(\mathbf{s}^{\prime}))\bigr]\,,

(1)

where
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{s}^{\prime}{\mathbin{}\mid\mathbin{}}(\mathbf{s},a)}[\cdot]
is the conditional expectation operator with respect to the
next state
\mathbf{s}^{\prime}
conditioned on
(\mathbf{s},a)
, and

being the discount factor. A greedy
version of (
1
) is the Bellman mapping
\mathbfcal{T}^{\diamond}\colon\mathscr{B}\to\mathscr{B}\colon Q\mapsto(\mathbfcal{T}^{\diamond}Q)(\mathbf{s},a)\coloneqq r(\mathbf{s},a)+\alpha\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{s}^{\prime}{\mathbin{}\mid\mathbin{}}(\mathbf{s},a)}[\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{A}}Q(\mathbf{s}^{\prime},a^{\prime})]
.

The fixed-point set of
\mathbfcal{T}^{\diamond}_{\mu}
is defined as
\Fix\mathbfcal{T}^{\diamond}_{\mu}\coloneqq\{Q\in\mathscr{B}\nonscript\,|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}Q=\mathbfcal{T}^{\diamond}_{\mu}Q\}
. It is well-known that identifying a fixed point
Q^{\diamond}_{\mu}\in\Fix\mathbfcal{T}^{\diamond}_{\mu}
plays a central role in computing optimal policies that maximize cumulative
rewards. Usually,
\alpha\in[0,1)
to assure that (
1
) becomes a strict contraction, hence

\Fix\mathbfcal{T}^{\diamond}_{\mu}
is a singleton
[
1
,
31
]
. It follows from
(
1
) that identifying
Q^{\diamond}_{\mu}
requires access to
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{s}^{\prime}{\mathbin{}\mid\mathbin{}}(\mathbf{s},a)}[\cdot]
, which, however, is typically unavailable to RL agents in practice.

2.2
Sparse GMM-QFs

Motivated by
[
30
]
, the proposed functional class of S-GMM-QFs is defined as follows: for user-defined
K,J\in\mathbb{N}_{*}
,

\displaystyle\mathscr{Q}_{K}\coloneqq\Bigl\{Q

\displaystyle\colon\mathfrak{S}\times\mathfrak{A}\to\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\colon(\mathbf{s},a)\mapsto Q(\mathbf{s},a)\coloneqq\sum_{k=1}^{K}\prod_{j=1}^{J}\upsilon_{k,j}(a){G}(\mathbf{s}{\mathbin{}\mid\mathbin{}}\mathbf{m}_{k},\mathbf{C}_{k})

\displaystyle\Big|\,\upsilon_{k,j}(a)\in\mathbb{R},\mathbf{m}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{D_{s}},\mathbf{C}_{k}\in\mathbb{S}_{++}^{D_{s}}\Bigl\}\,,

(2)

denotes the set of all
D_{s}\times D_{s}
positive definite matrices, while
\intercal
stands for vector/matrix transposition.

To justify the design, the functional class (
2
) enjoys the following “universal-approximation” property.

Proposition 1
.

The union
\cup_{K=1}^{\infty}\mathscr{Q}_{K}
is dense in the space of all square-(Lebesgue)-integrable functions on

\mathfrak{S}\times\mathfrak{A}
.

The proposed S-GMM-QFs extend the model of
[
30
]
, where a single scalar weight
\xi_{k}(a)
was used in place
of
\prod_{j=1}^{J}\upsilon_{k,j}(a)
. Define the
K\times N_{a}
matrix

(recall
N_{a}=\lvert\mathfrak{A}\rvert

, where

denotes the Hadamard product. This overparametrization of

promotes dimensionality reduction, thereby enhancing efficiency and robustness

32

33

34

35
]
. More specifically, it has been shown that such overparametrization, combined with
the smooth regularizer
\sum_{j=1}^{J}\lVert\bm{\Upsilon}_{j}\rVert_{\text{F}}^{2}
(with
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\text{F}}
being
the Frobenius norm), induces the nonconvex quasi-norm
\lVert\bm{\Xi}\rVert_{2/J}^{2/J}
for

, which yields sparser
solutions for
\bm{\Xi}
than classical
\ell_{1}
-norm regularization
[
35
]
. This Hadamard
overparametrization offers a transparent and interpretable mechanism for sparsifying the Gaussian mixture in
(
2
), as it enables users to readily identify the Gaussian components that contribute most to the Q-function
estimate. Finally, overparametrization, through increasing the number of degrees of freedom, may further improve
approximation quality
[
32
,
33
,
34
,
35
]
.

Let now the
D_{s}\times K
matrix
\mathbf{M}\coloneqq[\mathbf{m}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{m}_{K}]
. Then, the learnable parameters
can be collected as
\bm{\Omega}\coloneqq(\bm{\Upsilon}_{1},\dots,\bm{\Upsilon}_{J},\mathbf{M},\mathbf{C}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{C}_{K})
, so that each
\bm{\Omega}
specifies a single S-GMM-QF in (
2
), denoted hereafter

. Altogether, these learnable parameters define the parameter space
\mathfrak{M}_{K}\coloneqq(\mathbb{R}^{K\times N_{a}})^{J}\times\mathbb{R}^{D_{s}\times K}\times(\mathbb{S}_{++}^{D_{s}})^{K}
, which becomes a Riemannian manifold
because it is the Cartesian product of Riemannian manifolds
[
36
,
37
]
. Consequently, the tangent space to
\mathfrak{M}_{K}
at
\bm{\Omega}
becomes

T_{\bm{\Omega}}\mathfrak{M}_{K}=(\mathbb{R}^{K\times N_{a}})^{J}\times\mathbb{R}^{D_{s}\times K}\times T_{\mathbf{C}_{1}}\mathbb{S}_{++}^{D_{s}}\times\ldots\times T_{\mathbf{C}_{K}}\mathbb{S}_{++}^{D_{s}}
, where
T_{\mathbf{C}_{k}}\mathbb{S}_{++}^{D_{s}}
stands for the tangent space
to
\mathbb{S}_{++}^{D_{s}}
at
\mathbf{C}_{k}
, known to be the set of all
D_{s}\times D_{s}
symmetric matrices
[
36
,
37
]
.

Motivated by the significance of the fixed point
Q^{\diamond}_{\mu}\in\Fix\mathbfcal{T}^{\diamond}_{\mu}
, the widely
used Bellman-residual (BR) approach
[
12
,
11
]
estimates

by any
minimizer in
\arg\min_{\overline{Q}}(1/T)\sum_{t=1}^{T}[\overline{Q}(\mathbf{s}_{t},a_{t})-r_{t}-\alpha\overline{Q}(\mathbf{s}^{\prime}_{t},\mu(\mathbf{s}^{\prime}_{t}))]^{2}

and a user-defined Q-function

\forall\bm{\Omega}\in\mathfrak{M}_{K}

\displaystyle L_{\mu}(\bm{\Omega};\overline{Q},\mathscr{D})\coloneqq\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bigg[

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\prod_{j=1}^{J}\upsilon_{k,j}(a_{t}){G}_{k}(\mathbf{s}_{t})

\displaystyle-r_{t}-\alpha\overline{Q}(\mathbf{s}^{\prime}_{t},\mu(\mathbf{s}^{\prime}_{t}))\bigg]^{2}\,.

(3a)

Note that only the column vectors of
\{\bm{\Upsilon}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{J}
corresponding to

. The remaining columns, which do not appear in

\displaystyle{L}_{\mu}(\bm{\Omega};\overline{Q},\mathscr{D})\coloneqq L_{\mu}(\bm{\Omega};\overline{Q},\mathscr{D})+\underbrace{\rho\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{J}\lVert\bm{\Upsilon}_{j}\rVert_{\text{F}}^{2}}_{{R}(\bm{\Omega})}\,,

sparsity-inducing

Online Approximate PI via S-GMM-QFs

More precisely, at each time instance

4
Numerical tests

Two classical benchmark RL tasks with finite action spaces—the acrobot
[
41
]
and the
cartpole
[
42
]
—are selected to validate the proposed framework against the popular online-learning DeepRL
methods of DQN
[
23
]
and PPO
[
43
]
, as well as against their sparsified variants:

(i)

pruned (dense-to-sparse)
[
27
]
,

(ii)

static sparse
[
26
]
, which initializes and trains a fixed sparse network, and

(iii)

SET
[
28
]
, which updates the sparse network according to a cosine decay criterion
[
44
]
.

PPO
[
43
]
employs two neural networks—one for the Q-function (critic) and another for the policy
(actor)—whereas DQN, similarly to
algorithm

1
, models only the Q-function. The performance metric (vertical axis
in subsequent figures) represents the cumulative reward obtained by the agent until the task is terminated under the
learned policy
\mu_{n}
, with
n
denoting the index of the incoming data as well as the index of operation for

algorithm

1
(horizontal axis). To demonstrate the effect of sparsification, performance is also plotted vs. the
number of model’s learnable parameters. Algorithms are evaluated every
1\text{\times}{10}^{4}
incoming data,
with
results averaged over
20
independent episodes using a separate testing emulator for each environment. Numerical
comparisons of the proposed framework’s predecessor
[
30
]
with state-of-the-art kernel-based and distributional
RL methods are reported in
[
30
]
.

The “acrobot”
[
41
]
is an underactuated two-link robot. The goal is to swing the free end from the
downward to the upright position in as few steps as possible. The state includes joint angles and angular velocities of
both links, and the action space has
3
options: accelerate left, right, or do nothing. The agent receives a reward
of
0
only upon reaching the upright configuration and
-1
otherwise. Episodes start from the downward position and
finish upon reaching the goal or after
500
steps. The “cartpole”
[
42
]
task involves balancing a pole
on a cart by applying left or right forces. The state consists of cart position, velocity, pole angle, and angular
velocity. Episodes start from the upright position and terminate when position or angle limits are exceeded or after

500
steps. A reward of
1
is given at every step,
encouraging longer balance and avoiding losing all rewards
by termination.

Hyperparameters for
algorithm

1
are chosen as follows: number of Hadamard weight components
J=3
, and maximum
capacity
B
of
\mathscr{B}_{n}
is
1\text{\times}{10}^{4}
, with the size of
\mathscr{D}_{n}
set to
T=64
.
To retain importance of updated information in objective function when sampling from experience data, the latest
incoming data tuple is always included in
\mathscr{D}_{n}
. The learning rate of R-Adam
[
38
]
is set to

3\text{\times}{10}^{-4}
.
Following the suggestion of
[
26
]
, DQNs and critic of PPO have a dense network with 2 hidden
layers of size
512
to represent Q-functions, whereas in PPO the policy function (actor) is modeled separately
using a dense network of 2
64
-neuron hidden layers. Adam
[
45
]
is used to train the neural networks, with
the learning rate
1\text{\times}{10}^{-3}
for DQNs and
3\text{\times}{10}^{-4}
for PPOs, as well as for all of their sparse training variants.
Note that, contrary to DeepRL methods whose sparsification
is user-defined, sparsification of S-GMM-QFs is
controlled by the regularizers
\rho
, and only values of

\rho
with
significant difference in model size are chosen to show
in
figs.

1
and
2
.

The performance of the compared methods on the acrobot task is shown in
fig.

1
. As seen
in
fig.

1a
,
algorithm

1
(
K=500
) converges quickly and shows stable performance. PPO
initially improves rapidly but settles on suboptimal policies even with large amounts of data, whereas DQN eventually
reaches the desired performance but converges more slowly. S-GMM-QFs use far fewer parameters to learn than DeepRL
models (about
2.7\text{\times}{10}^{5}
for a 2-layer
512
-neuron DQN), yet, as shown in
fig.

1b
,

algorithm

1
maintains its performance even under high sparsification, while DQN and PPO degrade significantly as
parameters are pruned.

Similar trends are observed in
fig.

2
. In
fig.

2a
, PPO performs best, followed by

algorithm

1
with
K=50
and
K=500
. Notice also that
algorithm

with
K=50
is more stable with less
variance than PPO and the larger scale S-GMM-QFs of

after achieving the optimal performance. DQN’s rewards

to the acrobot case. This behavior persists in
fig.

their dense counterparts. Pruned PPO
[
