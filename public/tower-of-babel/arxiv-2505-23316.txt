Proximalized Preference Optimization for Diverse Feedback Types: A Decomposed Perspective on DPO
Kaiyang Guo, Yinchuan Li, Zhitang Chen

Abstract
Direct alignment methods typically optimize large language models (LLMs) by contrasting the likelihoods of preferred versus dispreferred responses. While effective in steering LLMs to match relative preference, these methods are frequently noted for decreasing the absolute likelihoods of example responses. As a result, aligned models tend to generate outputs that deviate from the expected patterns, exhibiting reward-hacking effect even without a reward model. This undesired consequence exposes a fundamental limitation in contrastive alignment, which we characterize as likelihood underdetermination. In this work, we revisit direct preference optimization (DPO)—the seminal direct alignment method—and demonstrate that its loss theoretically admits a decomposed reformulation. The reformulated loss not only broadens applicability to a wider range of feedback types, but also provides novel insights into the underlying cause of likelihood underdetermination. Specifically, the standard DPO implementation implicitly oversimplifies a regularizer in the reformulated loss, and reinstating its complete version effectively resolves the underdetermination issue. Leveraging these findings, we introduce PRoximalized PReference Optimization (PRO), a unified method to align with diverse feeback types, eliminating likelihood underdetermination through an efficient approximation of the complete regularizer. Comprehensive experiments show the superiority of PRO over existing methods in scenarios involving pairwise, binary and scalar feedback.

Introduction

Learning from feedback provides a powerful paradigm to align large language models (LLMs) with nuanced human preferences.
Instead of relying on meticulously crafted gold standards for supervision, it leverages preference feedback to promote desired behaviors and inhibit undesired ones. This shift in learning signal facilitates scalable data generation, yet simultaneously introduces the need for more advanced alignment techniques.

Initially, reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) transforms pairwise preference feedback into continuous rewards, which are then used to train LLM via reinforcement learning

1
,
2
]
.
Although RLHF has shown empirical effectiveness, it presents significant computational challenges, including the need for training an additional reward model and generating on-policy responses with LLM. Moreover, as an imperfect proxy, the reward model may produce unreliable evaluations for responses that fall outside training distribution, making RLHF susceptible to reward hacking
[
3
,

,

,

,

]
.

Direct preference optimization (DPO) circumvents the need for an explicit reward model and learns directly from offline preference data
[
8
]
. Its core methodology involves constructing a contrastive loss, that maximizes the likelihood differences between preferred and dispreferred responses. Owing to the simplicity, DPO has inspired the development of numerous contrastive approaches
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
.

However, an unanticipated phenomenon has been frequently reported in DPO: the likelihoods of both preferred and dispreferred responses decrease after alignment
[
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
]
. This reduction inadvertently encourages the generation of out-of-distribution responses, suggesting persistent reward hacking even without an explicit reward model
[
16
]
.
Recent research on this phenomenon predominantly focuses on the training dynamics of DPO and its general variants. The proposed explanations include embedding similarity between paired responses
[
15
]
, asymmetric update ratios for the probabilities of paired responses
[
17
]
, and the “squeezing effect” of softmax when applying gradient ascent to dispreferred responses
[
18
]
.
While these studies offer valuable insights, they largely overlook the intrinsic limitation of the loss function itself.
In particular, when attention is limited to relative likelihood differences, the contrastive loss becomes insensitive to simultaneous decreases or increases in absolute likelihoods. We characterize this issue as likelihood underdetermination.
Several works also noticed and attempted to remedy it, but they incorporate additional supervised or regression signals into DPO
[
12
,
14
]
, which may unintentionally compromise the original intent of alignment.

The aforementioned line of research aligns LLMs using pairwise feedback. In addition, parallel studies have explored alternative feedback types for alignment.
Considering that response-pair annotation demands more effort than single-response evaluation, Kahneman-Tversky optimization (KTO) aligns with binary feedback by constructing separate utility functions for desired and undesired responses
[
19
]
. Conversely, in scenarios where finer-grained scalar feedback is available, noise contrastive alignment (NCA) formulates a classification task to capture the varying degree of desirability for each labeled response
[
13
]
.
Both KTO and NCA depart from the contrastive framework.
Although they can either directly or under certain assumption be adapted to pairwise feedback, KTO does not support scalar feedback, and NCA is incompatible with binary feedback. Currently, there is still no unified approach that seamlessly accommodates all these feedback types. For a more detailed discussion of related works, see Appendix
A
.

In this study, we revisit DPO, the seminal method in field of direct alignment. Through a theoretical reformulation of its loss, we highlight DPO’s inherent capacity to accommodate diverse feedback, and reveal new insights into the underlying cause of likelihood underdetermination. Building on these findings, we propose a practical approach that not only addresses the underdetermination issue, but also unifies alignment across various feedback types, thereby achieving the best of both worlds pursued in previous works. Specifically, our contributions are as follows:

•

DPO Reformulation

We show that the DPO loss admits a decomposed reformulation, which consists of separate optimizer and regularizer terms. The optimizer reorganizes pairwise feedback into a pointwise signal, naturally extending applicability to a wider range of feedback types. The regularizer is independent of the preference label, and thus allows for a more flexible development of sample-based variant.

•

Unveiling Likelihood Underdetermination

We find that the standard DPO implementation implicitly oversimplifies the regularizer introduced in the reformulation. Importantly, once the full regularizer is reinstated, any optimal solution to the reformulated loss—if it exists—no longer suffers from likelihood underdetermination.

•

Practical Approach

However, computing the regularizer in full is intractable, and the optimal solution may not always exist. To overcome these challenges, we propose PRoximalized PReference Optimization (PRO), which employs a
hyper response
mechanism for efficiently approximating the regularizer, and guarantees the existence of an optimal solution whenever its hyperparameter is properly chosen.

•

Empirical Evaluation

The experiments show that PRO mitigates likelihood underdetermination, performs effectively across diverse feedback types, and achieves performance comparable to or better than DPO and several other methods designed for specific feedback types. Remarkably, even in the challenging scenario with extremely imbalanced binary feedback (desired:undesired = 1:100), PRO demonstrates comparable performance to that obtained with fully balanced feedback.

Preliminaries

In LLM alignment, preference feedback data is commonly collected through two steps: prompting a baseline model with inputs
x
to generate response pair
y_{1},y_{2}\sim\mu(y|x)
; human annotators labeling the generated responses to indicate which is preferred. Let
y_{w}\succ y_{l}|x
denote the preference feedback, where
y_{w}
and
y_{l}
are preferred and dispreferred responses amongst
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
.

RLHF leverages reward modeling to transform the pairwise feedback data
\mathcal{D}=\big\{x^{(i)},y_{w}^{(i)},y_{l}^{(i)}\big\}_{i=1}^{I}
into quantifiable rewards, and subsequently uses these signals to optimize LLM via reinforcement learning.
Recently, DPO
[
8
]
circumvents the need for explicit reward modeling, by recognizing that the optimal solution to the RL problem possesses a closed-form relationship with the reward function. In consequence, the reward function can be expressed using the LLM to be optimized:

\displaystyle r_{\theta}(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)},

where
\pi_{\text{ref}}
is a reference model that the LLM is regularized towards during training.
Alignment is then performed by fitting the LLM-induced reward model to preference data via the following loss:

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta};\pi_{\text{ref}})=-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{w},y_{l})\sim\mathcal{D}}\Big[\log\sigma\big(r_{\theta}(x,y_{w})-r_{\theta}(x,y_{l})\big)\Big].

We use the hatted notation
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{DPO}}
to emphasize that the loss is computed based on preference samples.

DPO has been widely adopted in LLM alignment
[
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
]
, yet an overlooked issue is likelihood underdetermination of its loss function. Specifically, if
\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}|x)
and
\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}|x)
are shifted by a same constant, the loss value remains unaffected. Furthermore, as indicated by its gradient:

\displaystyle\hskip-5.69054pt\nabla_{\hskip-1.42262pt\theta}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta};\pi_{\text{ref}})\hskip-1.42262pt=\hskip-1.42262pt-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{w},y_{l})\sim\mathcal{D}}\!\Big[\hskip-0.42677pt\underbrace{\sigma\hskip-0.42677pt\big(r_{\theta}(x,y_{l})\!-\!r_{\theta}(x,y_{w})\hskip-0.42677pt\big)\hskip-1.13809pt}_{\text{importance weight}}\cdot\beta\big(\hskip-0.42677pt\nabla_{\hskip-1.42262pt\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{w})\!-\!\nabla_{\hskip-1.42262pt\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{l})\hskip-0.42677pt\big)\hskip-0.42677pt\Big],\hskip-3.1298pt

(1)

the importance weight for model update approaches zero whenever the relative difference
\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}|x)-\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}|x)
is sufficiently large, regardless of the absolute probability values. Such characteristics can substantially hinder effective alignment: After pretraining or supervised finetuning, the values of
\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}|x)
and
\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}|x)
are markedly higher than those assigned to meaningless sentences. As the DPO loss focuses solely on the relative difference between
\log p(y_{w}|x)
and
\log p(y_{l}|x)
, their absolute values are prone to decrease due to catastrophic forgetting, and there is no incentive to increase them again once the relative difference has been sufficiently enlarged.

3
Theoretical Re-Examination of DPO

Prior studies on DPO and its variants have primarily focused on sample-based losses. In this work, to seek a more in-depth understanding of DPO, we revisit its loss in a population-based formulation:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta};\pi_{\text{ref}})=-\mathbb{E}_{y_{1},y_{2}\sim\mu}\Big[p(y_{1}\succ y_{2})\cdot\log\sigma\big(r_{\theta}(y_{1})-r_{\theta}(y_{2})\big)\Big].

While the population-based loss is impractical, Section
3.1
shows that it can be reformulated into an appealing form, which naturally accommodates diverse feedback. Moreover, as elaborated in Section
3.2
, it yields new insights into the root cause of likelihood underdetermination in sample-based DPO, and motivates the development of a novel sample-based variant that addresses this issue.

3.1
Reformulation of Population-Based DPO

DPO originally centers on a non-linear mapping of the relative likelihood gap between preferred and dispreferred responses.
Interestingly, the following theorem shows that its loss can be reformulated to explicitly account for the absolute likelihoods of labeled responses.

Theorem 3.1
.

The population-based DPO loss is equivalent to the following one, in that they share same gradient:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{eDPO}}(\pi_{\theta};\hskip-1.42262pt\pi_{\text{ref}})\!=\!\underbrace{-\beta\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\mu}\big[s(y)\hskip-1.13809pt\cdot\hskip-1.13809pt\log\pi_{\theta}(y)\big]}_{\text{optimizer}}+\!\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{y_{1},y_{2}\sim\mu}\Bigg[\!D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\!\mathcal{B}\hskip-1.42262pt\left(\hskip-0.85358pt\frac{1}{2}\hskip-0.85358pt\right)\hskip-3.41432pt\;\middle|\middle|\;\mathclap{\phantom{\!\mathcal{B}\Big(\hskip-1.42262pt\sigma\big(r_{\theta}(y_{1})\!-\!r_{\theta}(y_{2})\big)\Big)\!}}\allowbreak\!\mathcal{B}\Big(\hskip-1.42262pt\sigma\big(r_{\theta}(y_{1})\!-\!r_{\theta}(y_{2})\big)\Big)\!\right)\!\Bigg]}_{\text{regularizer}}\!,

where
\mathcal{B}
denotes Bernoulli distribution,

\displaystyle s(y)=\mathbb{E}_{y^{\prime}\sim\mu}\big[p(y\succ y^{\prime})\big]-\frac{1}{2}

is a score function indicating the extent to which
y
is favored across other responses and satisfies
\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\mu}[s(y)]=0
.

The reformulation decomposes the loss into an optimizer and a regularizer. Upon examination of these components, we identify two attractive properties:

•

The optimizer reorganizes pairwise feedback into a pointwise signal
s(y)
, with which
\log\pi_{\theta}(y)
is independently optimized for each response. This property naturally extends the applicability of
\mathcal{L}_{\text{eDPO}}
. For instance, given
-1/2\leq s(y)\leq 1/2
, we can interpret
s(y)
as the expected value of a Bernoulli distribution, with binary feedback in
\{-1/2,+1/2\}
serving as its empirical sample for loss evaluation.
Alternatively,
s(y)
can be viewed as the expectation of a continuous reward distribution, where scalar feedback represents realized reward samples during training.

•

The optimizer relies on preference feedback, whereas the regularizer operates independently of such information. Given the limited availability of preference feedback in practice, the optimizer must be estimated from a finite dataset. In contrast, the regularizer can be applied to an expanded set of responses, irrespective of preference labels. This decomposition therefore provides greater flexibility in developing sample-based loss.

These two properties play pivotal roles in this work. The first one enables devising a unified alignment loss for diverse feedback types. The second property offers an elegant way to understand and resolve likelihood underdetermination, as detailed in the following section.

3.2
The Form of Regularizer Matters in Sample-Based Loss

For practical use, it is essential to develop a sample-based loss whose computation only requires the limited feedback data. The most straightforward strategy is to estimate both the optimizer and regularizer in eDPO using the labeled responses.
1
1

1

That means replacing
\mu
and
p
by their empirical counterparts estimated from the dataset.
As can be verified by applying Theorem
3.1
in reverse, this substitution recovers the sample-based DPO loss.
However, there appears to be a contradiction regarding the existence of likelihood underdetermination. On one hand, the gradient of sample-based DPO in (
1
) includes an importance weight, causing it to vanish whenever the likelihood difference between response pair is sufficiently large—regardless of their absolute values. On the other hand, the optimizer in eDPO directly evaluates the absolute log-probabilities of labeled responses; even estimated with limited samples, its gradient remains free of any importance weighting. Considering that eDPO incorporates an additional regularizer, it is plausible that this term is responsible for the likelihood underdetermination presented in sample-based DPO. We investigate its effect below.

A key observation of the regularizer is that, when
\mu(y)>0
for all
y
, the regularizer effectively constrains
\pi_{\theta}
around
\pi_{\text{ref}}
and its value becomes zero only if
\pi_{\theta}=\pi_{\text{ref}}
. In other words, the regularizer is well-defined albeit in a contrastive form analogous to DPO.
However, it is easy to verify that, when estimated with partial response pairs, the regularizer revives the issue of likelihood underdetermination.
2
2

2

In other words, the value of regularizer only depends on the relative likelihood gap between paired response.
Moreover, as indicated by the recovery to sample-based DPO, this underdetermination dominates the optimizer’s effect on absolute likelihoods, rendering the overall loss function underdetermined.

However, the regularizer itself is independent of preference labels, thus need not be restricted to labeled responses. This motivates us to study whether the underdetermination can be addressed by retaining the full regularizer in sample-based loss. Formally, define the sample-based eDPO loss as:

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{eDPO}}(\pi_{\theta};\hskip-1.42262pt\pi_{\text{ref}})\!=\!-\beta\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\hat{\mu}}\big[\hat{s}(y)\hskip-1.13809pt\cdot\hskip-1.13809pt\log\pi_{\theta}(y)\big]\!+\!\frac{\alpha}{2}\mathbb{E}_{y_{1},y_{2}\sim\mu}\hskip-1.42262pt\Bigg[\!D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\!\mathcal{B}\hskip-1.42262pt\left(\hskip-0.85358pt\frac{1}{2}\hskip-0.85358pt\right)\hskip-3.41432pt\;\middle|\middle|\;\mathclap{\phantom{\!\mathcal{B}\Big(\hskip-1.42262pt\sigma\big(r_{\theta}(y_{1})\!-\!r_{\theta}(y_{2})\big)\Big)\!}}\allowbreak\!\mathcal{B}\Big(\hskip-1.42262pt\sigma\big(r_{\theta}(y_{1})\!-\!r_{\theta}(y_{2})\big)\Big)\!\right)\!\Bigg]\!,

where
\alpha>0
is a newly introduced coefficient for general tradeoff between preference optimization and regularization,

\hat{s}(y)\!=\!\begin{cases}\mathbb{E}_{y^{\prime}\sim\hat{\mu}}\Big[\hat{p}(y\succ y^{\prime})\Big]-\frac{1}{2}~&\text{for pairwise feedback}\\[8.5359pt]
\hat{b}(y)-\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\hat{\mu}}\Big[\hat{b}(y)\Big]&\text{for pointwise feedback}\end{cases}\!,

is the empirical score,
\hat{\mu}
and
\hat{p}
are respectively the empirical response distribution and pairwise preference,
\hat{b}(y)
is the sample average of the pointwise feedback. The structure of
\hat{s}
for pointwise feedback is designed so that
\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\hat{\mu}}[\hat{s}(y)]=0
, mirroring the property of
s
established in Theorem
3.1
.

To gain theoretical insight into
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{eDPO}}
, we consider
\pi_{\theta}
as any flexible distribution within
\Delta=\big\{\pi\mid\pi(y)>0,\forall y\in\mathcal{Y}\text{ and }\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\pi(y)=1\big\}
. This allows us to derive the necessary condition for its optimal solution as follows.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\mu:=\mu(y)>0
for all
y\in\mathcal{Y}
. If the optimal solution
\pi^{*}
to
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{eDPO}}
exists, it satisfies the following condition for any
y\in\mathcal{Y}

\displaystyle\alpha\mathbb{E}_{y^{\prime}\sim\mu}\!\Bigg[\sigma\!\left(\!\beta\log\!\frac{\pi^{*}(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)}\!-\!\beta\log\!\frac{\pi^{*}(y^{\prime})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^{\prime})}\!\right)\!-\!\frac{1}{2}\Bigg]\mkern-7.0mu=\mkern-3.0mu\frac{\hat{\mu}(y)}{\mu(y)}\hat{s}(y).

(2)

Condition (
2
) can be interpreted as a weighted score matching: The expectation term on the left-hand side acts as a modeled score, analogous to the empirical score
\hat{s}
for pairwise feedback except that it is derived from the LLM;
The weight on the right-hand side contains
\mu(y)
and
\hat{\mu}(y)
, which respectively signify the strengths of the regularizer and the observed evidence.
When
\hat{\mu}(y)>\mu(y)
, response
y
receives more preference labels than its prior expectation, leading to a more confident estimation of
\hat{s}(y)
. This increased confidence, in turn, amplifies the influence of the empirical score in (
2
), adaptively encouraging a more aggressive model update. The weight
\alpha
on the left similarly modulates the effect of empirical score, but operates as a global hyperparameter affecting all responses uniformly.

Recall that
\hat{s}(y)
indicates whether
y
is preferred over other responses. When condition (
2
) holds, the modeled score should reflect the preference accordingly. In particular, the sign of
\hat{s}(y)
should determine how
\pi^{*}(y)
deviates from
\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)
. This relationship is formally confirmed in Corollary

.

Corollary 3.3
.

Under the preconditions of Theorem
3.2
, the following results hold for a constant
C
:

\displaystyle\frac{\pi^{*}(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)}=C,\quad

\displaystyle\forall y:\hat{\mu}(y)=0\text{ or }\hat{s}(y)=0,

(3)

\displaystyle\frac{\pi^{*}(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)}>C,\quad

\displaystyle\forall y:\hat{\mu}(y)>0\text{ and }\hat{s}(y)>0,

(4)

\displaystyle\frac{\pi^{*}(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)}<C,\quad

\displaystyle\forall y:\hat{\mu}(y)>0\text{ and }\hat{s}(y)<0.

(5)

Equation (
3
) sets the probability ratios for both unobserved and tied responses as fixed constant, establishing a baseline. Inequalities (
4
) and (
5
) describe how the ratios for labeled responses vary relative to this baseline. Thus, the absolute likelihoods of both labeled and unobserved responses are not arbitrarily adjustable, resolving the underdetermination issue.
Overall, these analyses conclude:

Likelihood underdetermination stems from oversimplifying the regularizer in eDPO,

and can be resolved by restoring the regularizer to its complete form.

4
Proximalized Preference Optimization

The results in previous section rely on the preconditions that
\mu
assigns non-zero probability to all responses and the optimal solution
\pi^{*}
exists. While the first condition can be satisfied by presetting
\mu
appropriately, computing the regularizer in
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{eDPO}}
requires traversing all responses with non-zero probability under
\mu
.
Given the enormous cardinality of
\mathcal{Y}
, the regularizer rapidly becomes computationally intractable.
We address this challenge by developing a carefully crafted approximation in Section
4.1
. To meet the second condition, Section
4.2
establishes a sufficient condition guaranteeing the existence of
\pi^{*}
. A concrete instance satisfying this condition for pairwise feedback is further constructed and shown to have a more direct relation to sample-based DPO.

4.1
Introducing Hyper Response for Tractable Loss Approximation

To circumvent the enumeration of all possible responses, one may simply incorporate additional samples from
\mu
, alongside the labeled responses, in the regularizer computation. Although this yields a more exact approximation than sample-based DPO, auto-regressive sampling by LLM is computationally expensive, typically 10-100x slower than training. Moreover, this approximation again reduces
\mu
to an empirical distribution with limited supports, risking a recurrence of underdetermination.

To avoid these problems, we propose a novel approximation mechanism that computes the regularizer over a compact yet complete response space. The key idea is to introduce a
hyper response

\mathcal{H}\subset\mathcal{Y}
, which aggregates multiple individual responses in
\mathcal{Y}
without distinction. This aggregation leads to the following modified response space:

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{\mathcal{H}}=\{\mathcal{H}\}\cup\{y\mid y\notin\mathcal{H}\}.

Then, the regularizer computation involves accessing the probability values
\mu(y)
,
\pi_{\theta}(y)
and
\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)
over the response space
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathcal{H}}
. Specifically, for the case where
y=\mathcal{H}
, we define
p(\mathcal{H})
for any probability distribution
p
over
\mathcal{Y}
as:

\displaystyle p(\mathcal{H})=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{H}}p(y)=1-\sum_{y\notin\mathcal{H}}p(y).

(6)

Crucially,
p(\mathcal{H})
can be derived from the probabilities of its complement. For example, when
\mathcal{H}
encompasses all unobserved responses, calculating
p(\mathcal{H})
only requires the probabilities of labeled responses, without need for additional samples. Besides, such a construction of
\mathcal{H}
also produces the most compact
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathcal{H}}
, as it augments the labeled responses with a single hyper response. Considering the two benefits, we treat this construction as default.

Building on the above foundations, we now introduce the refined loss:

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{PRO}}(\pi_{\theta};\hskip-1.42262pt\pi_{\text{ref}})\!=\!-\beta\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\hat{\mu}}\big[\hat{s}(y)\hskip-1.13809pt\cdot\hskip-1.13809pt\log\pi_{\theta}(y)\big]\!+\!\frac{\alpha}{2}\mathbb{E}_{y_{1},y_{2}\mathrel{\dot{\sim}}\mu}\hskip-1.42262pt\Bigg[\!D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\!\mathcal{B}\hskip-1.42262pt\left(\hskip-0.85358pt\frac{1}{2}\hskip-0.85358pt\right)\hskip-3.41432pt\;\middle|\middle|\;\mathclap{\phantom{\!\mathcal{B}\Big(\hskip-1.42262pt\sigma\big(r_{\theta}(y_{1})\!-\!r_{\theta}(y_{2})\big)\Big)\!}}\allowbreak\!\mathcal{B}\Big(\hskip-1.42262pt\sigma\big(r_{\theta}(y_{1})\!-\!r_{\theta}(y_{2})\big)\Big)\!\right)\!\Bigg]\!.

Its distinction from
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{eDPO}}
is the use of
y_{1},y_{2}\mathrel{\dot{\sim}}\mu
in the regularizer. Here,
y\mathrel{\dot{\sim}}\mu
specifies that
y
is drawn according to
\mu
over the response space
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathcal{H}}
. Accordingly, for any function
f
, the expectation with respect to
y\hskip-1.42262pt\mathrel{\dot{\sim}}\hskip-1.42262pt\mu
simplifies to
\mathbb{E}_{y\mathrel{\dot{\sim}}\mu}[f(y)]\hskip-2.27621pt=\!\mu(\mathcal{H})\hskip-0.14226ptf(\mathcal{H})\hskip-0.28453pt+\hskip-0.36989pt\sum_{y\notin\mathcal{H}}\mu(y)\hskip-0.14226ptf(y)
, substantially reducing the number of terms compared to the full expectation over
\mathcal{Y}
. When substituting this expectation into our regularizer, the values of
\mu(\mathcal{H}),\pi_{\theta}(\mathcal{H})
and
\pi_{\text{ref}}(\mathcal{H})
can be readily computed via (
6
). Notably, under the aforementioned construction of
\mathcal{H}
,
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{PRO}}
introduces negligible additional computational overhead compared to
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{DPO}}
, as it involves only simple operations on the response probabilities already used in DPO.

Three natural questions arise regarding
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{PRO}}
: (a) how its optimal solution relates to that of
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{eDPO}}
, (b) whether it preserves protection against underdetermination, and (c) what the cost of such an approximation is. The following theorem and subsequent discussions address these questions.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}\setminus\operatorname{supp}(\hat{\mu})
and
\mu:=\mu(y)>0
for all
y\in\mathcal{Y}
. If the optimal solutions
\pi^{*}
and
\pi_{\mathcal{H}}^{*}
to
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{eDPO}}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{PRO}}
exist, they satisfy:

\displaystyle\pi_{\mathcal{H}}^{*}(y)=\pi^{*}(y),\quad\forall y\in\mathcal{Y}\setminus\mathcal{H},

(7)

\displaystyle\sum_{y\in\mathcal{H}}\pi_{\mathcal{H}}^{*}(y)=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{H}}\pi^{*}(y)=C\cdot\sum_{y\in\mathcal{H}}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y),

(8)

where
C
is the constant as defined in Corollary
3.3
.

The precondition
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}\setminus\operatorname{supp}(\hat{\mu})
implies that the hyper response does not include any labeled response. Then, the set
\mathcal{Y}\setminus\mathcal{H}
contains all labeled responses.
According to (
7
), these responses retain the properties outlined in Corollary
3.3
—their absolute likelihoods, not just relative gaps, are well determined after optimization. Therefore, this approximation preserves protection against underdetermination.

Adopting the hyper response mechanism entails losing the ability to differentiate the probabilities of individual responses within
\mathcal{H}
. Nevertheless, we contend that it is considerably less problematic than the underdetermination encountered in sample-based DPO. As shown in (
8
), the total probability mass assigned to the elements in
\mathcal{H}
is fixed.
This constraint prevents any individual response from continuously increasing its probability during training.
Besides,
\mathcal{H}
comprises only unobserved responses, the vast majority of which begin with extremely low probabilities. Even with moderate probability increases during training, their overall likelihood of being generated remains limited.

In summary, the hyper response enables an efficient and effective approximation to
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{eDPO}}
. With the guarantee of mitigating likelihood underdetermination, the LLM is successfully proximalized around reference model. We thus refer to the approach as PRoximalized PReference Optimization (PRO).

4.2
Guaranteeing the Existence of Optimal Solution for PRO

Lastly, we derive a sufficient condition for the existence of optimal solutions to the previously considered losses.
Since
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{eDPO}}
can be restored from
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{PRO}}
by letting
\mathcal{H}
consist of only a single individual response, we focus on
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{PRO}}
as the general problem.

Recall that the feasible region for
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{PRO}}
, namely
\Delta=\big\{\pi\mid\pi(y)>0\ \forall y\in\mathcal{Y}_{\mathcal{H}},\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}_{\mathcal{H}}}\pi(y)=1\big\}
, is an open set. The absence of optimal solution would imply the existence of a sequence approaching the boundary of
\Delta
, along which the loss function strictly decreases (see Lemma
3.1
in Appendix
B.4
). However, it can be verified that as one approaches the boundary, i.e,
\pi(y)\rightarrow 0
for some
y
, the regularizer in PRO increases to positive infinity. Thus, the loss improvement must come from the optimizer’s faster decrease to negative infinity.

The above observation motivates us to ask: can we adjust the value of
\alpha
such that the regularizer term dominates the loss function at the boundary? A positive answer would prevent the endless improvement of the loss value, thereby ensuring the existence of an optimal solution. The following theorem confirms this conjecture.

Theorem 4.2
.

Given any
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}\setminus\operatorname{supp}(\hat{\mu})
and
\mu:=\mu(y)>0,\forall y\in\mathcal{Y}_{\mathcal{H}}
, there is a threshold
\alpha_{0}
such that whenever
\alpha>\alpha_{0}
the optimal solution
\pi_{\mathcal{H}}^{*}
to
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{PRO}}
exists.

To illustrate, we present a concrete instance in the context of pairwise feedback. The instance is constructed by choosing
\mu=\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{\mu}
, defined by

\displaystyle\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{\mu}(y)=\begin{cases}\eta\cdot\hat{\mu}(y)~&\text{if }y\in\operatorname{supp}(\hat{\mu})\\
(1-\eta)\cdot\rho(y)&\text{otherwise}\end{cases},

(9)

where
\rho:=\rho(y)>0
can be any probability distribution over
y\in\mathcal{Y}_{\mathcal{H}}\setminus\operatorname{supp}(\hat{\mu})
, and
0<\eta<1
is a preset hyperparameter.
We consider it as a universal and practical choice because, in real-world settings, labeled responses yield
\hat{\mu}
, while the true underlying distribution
\mu
from which these responses are sampled may remain unknown.

Under this choice of
\mu
, the following theorem characterizes an admissible range of
\alpha
, and also establishes a direct connection between the resulting loss and sample-based DPO.

Theorem 4.3
.

Consider the pairwise feedback setting, where
\mu=\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{\mu}
and
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}\setminus\operatorname{supp}(\hat{\mu})
.
The optimal solution
\pi_{\mathcal{H}}^{*}
to
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{PRO}}
exists for any
\alpha\geq 1/\eta^{2}
. Moreover, when
\alpha=1/\eta^{2}
, the PRO loss is equivalent to the following one in that they share same gradient:

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{PRO-P}}(\pi_{\theta};\pi_{\text{ref}})=-\frac{1}{\eta^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{y_{1},y_{2}\mathrel{\dot{\sim}}\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{\mu}}\Big[\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{p}(y_{1}\succ y_{2})\cdot\log\sigma\big(r_{\theta}(y_{1})-r_{\theta}(y_{2})\big)\Big],

where

\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{p}(y_{1}\succ y_{2})=\begin{cases}\hat{p}(y_{1}\succ y_{2})~&\text{if }y_{1},y_{2}\in\operatorname{supp}(\hat{\mu})\\
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

is an augmented empirical preference.

The PRO-P loss can be viewed as an enhanced variant of sample-based DPO that integrates pseudo preference labels and employs the hyper-response approximation. Nevertheless, it should be noted that PRO-P is merely a special case resulting from particular choices of
\mu
and
\alpha
. More broadly, PRO offers a general approach that accommodates diverse feedback types and permits versatile control over both the strength and distribution of regularization.

5
Experiments

Our experiments investigate the following questions: (a) To what extent PRO alleviates likelihood underdetermination in practice? (b) How does PRO compare with alternative alignment methods under pairwise/binary feedback? (c) Given that KTO is applicable to imbalanced binary feedback, does PRO provide additional advantage when the feedback is extremely imbalanced? (d) While NCA is tailored for scalar feedback, does PRO yield comparable or better results in such cases?

We use two datasets to construct the three types of feedback for different experimental settings. The Anthropic-HH dataset originally contains 170k pairwise feedback examples
[
25
]
. Following
[
19

[
26

[
24

We apply PRO to each type of feedback: PRO-P refers to employing
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{PRO-P}}

\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{PRO}}

\alpha

6
Discussion

While DPO has become a predominant approach for aligning LLMs, it remains limited to pairwise feedback. Meanwhile, the DPO loss is affected by likelihood underdetermination, which inadvertently encourages reward hacking.
This study presented a decomposed perspective on DPO, which not only reveals its potential to accommodate broader feedback types but also pinpoints the root cause of likelihood underdetermination. Building on these insights, we proposed PRO, a practical method unifying alignment across diverse feedback and free from the underdetermination issue. Empirical results demonstrate its superiority over several existing methods. The reformulation of DPO also hints at an intriguing connection to RLHF, which raises research problems discussed in Appendix
C
.

Appendix A
Related Work

Reward Hacking in RLHF

RLHF employs a learned reward model to align LLM. While the reward model faithfully ranks responses within the training distribution
[
25
,
37
,
38
,
39
]
, it often fails to generalize beyond. Consequently, LLM can exploit this weakness to achieve high rewards without genuinely matching human intent. This effect, known as reward hacking, poses a significant challenge in RLHF
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
.

Recent studies have explored various mitigation strategies, covering the improvements in reward modeling, policy optimization and data augmentation. For instance, the research in
[
40
]
introduces an information bottleneck framework to filter out irrelevant noise that may introduce spurious features in reward modeling. Recognizing the limitations of a single reward model, works in
[
41
,
42
]
propose the use of reward ensembles, which aggregate outputs from multiple models to produce more robust reward estimates; these models can be further combined through weight averaging
[
43
]
to improve efficiency. Additionally, authors in
[
44
]
advocate a conservative approach by optimizing LLMs against the minimum reward predicted from a plausible set of reward models. In terms of RL algorithms, several studies
[
45
,
46
]
argue that the widely used proximal policy optimization
[
2
]
is insufficient to prevent reward hacking, and suggest incorporating explicit constraints to enforce more cautious use of reward model. Regarding training data, demonstrations are utilized to guide LLM towards generating responses with calibrated rewards
[
47
]
; and augmentation tools
[
48
]
are applied to diversify the dataset in hopes of improving model generalization. Despite these advances, reward hacking remains a challenging and unresolved issue in RLHF.

Direct Alignment with Pairwise Preference

Direct alignment methods
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
bypass the need for explicit reward models and instead optimize LLMs directly using the preference data.
Removing reward model not only lowers computational cost, but also restricts loss evaluation to the offline data. Since no on-policy samples are involved, these methods were initially considered immune to reward hacking.
However, recent studies
[
29
,
30
,
11
]
have shown that length exploitation—a familiar form of reward hacking observed in RLHF
[
27
,
28
]
—persists in methods like DPO. To address, several methods have been proposed: R-DPO
[
29
]
incorporates response length as a penalty into the DPO loss; SamPO
[
30
]
proposes down-sampling tokens of both preferred and dispreferred responses to equal lengths when computing the implicit reward in DPO; and SimPO
[
11
]
introduces length-normalized rewards to define a novel alignment loss. Although these methods effectively reduce length exploitation, they rely on explicit manipulation or regularization of response length, which unlikely addresses general reward hacking issues.
As reported in RLHF literature
[
49
]
, reward hacking can also manifest as lazy generation
[
6
]
, degraded downstream task performance
[
45
]
, and hedging or self-doubt
[
50
]
. These observations underscore the necessity for a more comprehensive understanding of reward hacking in direct alignment methods.

An important clue arises from the widely reported phenomenon where the likelihoods of both preferred and dispreferred responses decrease after alignment
[
12
,
13
,
14
]
. In consequence, the generation probabilities for unobserved responses are unintentionally elevated, echoing the reward-hacking effect in RLHF. Several research efforts examine this likelihood decline, attributing it to factors such as embedding similarity between paired responses
[
15
]
, asymmetric update ratios for the probabilities of paired responses
[
17
]
, and the “squeezing effect” of softmax when applying gradient ascent to dispreferred responses
[
18
]
. Notably, these conclusions are chiefly drawn from the analyses of training dynamics in DPO and its variants. In contrast, the presented work focuses on the loss function itself, providing new insights into the underlying cause of this phenomenon and proposing a natural resolution. Additionally, our study also differs from previous attempts to remedy the likelihood decrease, which commonly incorporate additional supervised or regression signals into DPO
[
12
,
14
]
and may inadvertently compromise the original intent of alignment.

Direct Alignment with Pointwise Feedback

Parallel studies have explored alignment methods that utilize feedback beyond the pairwise format. In
[
51
]
, an upper bound is derived for DPO to accommodate binary feedback, and it is shown that refining this bound can enhance alignment performance. Departing from the DPO framework, KTO
[
19
]
utilizes prospect theory
[
52
]
to create a utility function tailored for binary feedback. More generally, there is an extensive body of literature concerning binary feedback that focuses on model unlearning
[
53
,
54
,
55
]
, where negative responses are used to eliminate unwanted behaviors from LLMs. Among them, gradient-ascent methods
[
56
,
57
]
are the most straightforward but often lead to catastrophic collapse. Interestingly, alignment-inspired methods exponentially slow down this collapse
[
53
]
. Apart from binary feedback, NCA
[
13
]
considers scalar feedback, and formulates a classification task to capture the varying degree of desirability for each labeled response. While these existing methods tackle the different types of feedback, a unified approach capable of handling pairwise, binary and scalar feedback is still lacking.

\displaystyle r(y^{0})<r_{\infty}(y^{+})-\epsilon,\quad\forall y^{0}\in\mathcal{Y}^{0},\forall y^{+}\in\mathcal{Y}^{+},

Appendix C
Comparison of PRO Loss and RLHF Objective

Both the PRO loss and the RLHF objective incorporate an optimizer and a regularizer, yet they differ in the specific implementations. This section discusses these differences in detail and identifies several research problems that warrant further investigation.

The objective function (to be minimized) in RLHF is

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta}}\big[r_{\phi}(y)\big]+\beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\pi_{\theta}\;\middle|\middle|\;\mathclap{\phantom{\pi_{\text{ref}}}}\allowbreak\pi_{\text{ref}}\right),

which shares the same gradient with

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\text{sg}(\pi_{\theta})}\big[r_{\phi}(y)\cdot\log\pi_{\theta}(y)\big]+\beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\pi_{\theta}\;\middle|\middle|\;\mathclap{\phantom{\pi_{\text{ref}}}}\allowbreak\pi_{\text{ref}}\right),

(27)

where
\text{sg}(\cdot)
denotes the operation of stop gradient.
For convenience of comparison, we rewrite
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{PRO}}
here:
5
5

5

\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{eDPO}}
can be viewed as an instantiation of
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{PRO}}
by letting
\mathcal{H}
consist of only a single individual response.

\displaystyle-\beta\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\hat{\mu}}\big[\hat{s}(y)\cdot\log\pi_{\theta}(y)\big]+\frac{\alpha}{2}\mathbb{E}_{y_{1},y_{2}\mathrel{\dot{\sim}}\mu}\Bigg[D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\mathcal{B}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\;\middle|\middle|\;\mathclap{\phantom{\mathcal{B}\Big(\sigma\big(r_{\theta}(y_{1})-r_{\theta}(y_{2})\big)\Big)}}\allowbreak\mathcal{B}\Big(\sigma\big(r_{\theta}(y_{1})-r_{\theta}(y_{2})\big)\Big)\right)\Bigg].

(28)

The key distinctions between (
27
) and (
28
) are highlighted as below:

Hypothesis Reliance

RLHF optimizes LLM through the guidance provided by a learned reward model.
One crucial yet often overlooked element in this framework is selecting an appropriate hypothesis for the reward model. The Bradley-Terry model, appreciated for its simplicity and intuitive nature, is commonly used by default.
However, when actual user preferences deviate from its underlying assumptions (e.g., user preferences may be non-transitive, which the Bradley-Terry model cannot accommodate
[
58
,
59
]
), the reward model can produce inexact evaluations that mislead LLM optimization. As comparison, the PRO loss directly leverages the preference signal without relying on the rewards derived from another model. This direct approach hopefully improves robustness against discrepancies between the model hypothesis and true nature of user preferences.

Weighting Factor of
\mathbf{\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y)}
in Loss Gradient

Equation (
1
) illustrates that examining the loss gradient, particularly the weighting factor associated with
\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y)
, provides valuable insights into the alignment process. We now inspect this factor in the contexts of RLHF and PRO, revealing another noteworthy distinction.
First, when computing the optimizer gradients, RLHF weights
\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y)
with the unbounded reward value
r_{\phi}
, whereas PRO uses the bounded preference score
\hat{s}
.
Second, by simplifying the regularizer gradients as

\displaystyle\beta\nabla_{\theta}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\pi_{\theta}\;\middle|\middle|\;\mathclap{\phantom{\pi_{\text{ref}}}}\allowbreak\pi_{\text{ref}}\right)

\displaystyle=\int\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)}\cdot\nabla_{\theta}\pi_{\theta}(y)dy+\int\pi_{\theta}(y)\cdot\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y)dy

\displaystyle=\int\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)}\cdot\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y)\cdot\pi_{\theta}(y)dy+\nabla_{\theta}\int\pi_{\theta}(y)dy

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta}}\Big[\underbrace{r_{\theta}(y)}_{\text{Unbounded}}\cdot\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y)\Big],

(29)

and

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{2}\nabla_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{y_{1},y_{2}\mathrel{\dot{\sim}}\mu}\Bigg[D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\mathcal{B}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\;\middle|\middle|\;\mathclap{\phantom{\mathcal{B}\Big(\sigma\big(r_{\theta}(y_{1})-r_{\theta}(y_{2})\big)\Big)}}\allowbreak\mathcal{B}\Big(\sigma\big(r_{\theta}(y_{1})-r_{\theta}(y_{2})\big)\Big)\right)\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{2}\mathbb{E}_{y_{1},y_{2}\mathrel{\dot{\sim}}\mu}\Bigg[-\frac{1}{2}\Big(\nabla_{\theta}\log\sigma\big(r_{\theta}(y_{1})-r_{\theta}(y_{2})\big)+\nabla_{\theta}\log\sigma\big(r_{\theta}(y_{2})-r_{\theta}(y_{1})\big)\Big)\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{2}\mathbb{E}_{y_{1},y_{2}\mathrel{\dot{\sim}}\mu}\Bigg[\!-\!\frac{1}{2}\Big(\sigma\big(r_{\theta}(y_{2})\!-\!r_{\theta}(y_{1})\big)\!-\!\sigma\big(r_{\theta}(y_{1})\!-\!r_{\theta}(y_{2})\big)\Big)\cdot\big(\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{1})\!-\!\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{2})\big)\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{2}\mathbb{E}_{y_{1},y_{2}\mathrel{\dot{\sim}}\mu}\Bigg[\bigg(\sigma\big(r_{\theta}(y_{1})-r_{\theta}(y_{2})\big)-\frac{1}{2}\bigg)\cdot\big(\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{1})-\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{2})\big)\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{2}\mathbb{E}_{y_{1},y_{2}\mathrel{\dot{\sim}}\mu}\Bigg[\bigg(\sigma\big(r_{\theta}(y_{1})-r_{\theta}(y_{2})\big)-\frac{1}{2}\bigg)\cdot\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{1})\Bigg]

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{\alpha}{2}\mathbb{E}_{y_{1},y_{2}\mathrel{\dot{\sim}}\mu}\Bigg[\bigg(\sigma\big(r_{\theta}(y_{2})-r_{\theta}(y_{1})\big)-\frac{1}{2}\bigg)\cdot\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{1})\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{y_{1},y_{2}\mathrel{\dot{\sim}}\mu}\Bigg[\underbrace{\alpha\bigg(\sigma\big(r_{\theta}(y_{1})-r_{\theta}(y_{2})\big)-\frac{1}{2}\bigg)}_{\text{Bounded}}\cdot\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{1})\Bigg].

we find that the weight boundedness here is consistent to that in the optimizers. Overall, the integrated weight of
\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y)
for PRO is bounded, in contrast to the unbounded one in RLHF. Given the stochastic nature of loss gradient and the tendency of LLM to forget, the bounded weight would result in a more cautious model update, which potentially improves training stability and model performance when utilizing PRO.

Mode Seeking v.s. Mass Covering

The RLHF objective includes reverse KL divergence as a regularizer, which is characterized by mode-seeking behavior. This means, minimizing the reverse KL tends to concentrate probability mass around the modes of the target distribution, while relatively neglecting low-probability regions. Formally, when
\pi_{\theta}(y)\rightarrow 0
for certain
y
, the regularizer diminishes even if
\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)
is non-zero:

\displaystyle\lim_{\pi_{\theta}(y)\rightarrow 0}\pi_{\theta}(y)\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)}=\lim_{\pi_{\theta}(y)\rightarrow 0}\pi_{\theta}(y)\log\pi_{\theta}(y)=\lim_{\pi_{\theta}(y)\rightarrow 0}\frac{\log\pi_{\theta}(y)}{\frac{1}{\pi_{\theta}(y)}}=\lim_{\pi_{\theta}(y)\rightarrow 0}\frac{\frac{1}{\pi_{\theta}(y)}}{-\frac{1}{\pi_{\theta}(y)^{2}}}\!=\!0,

where the penultimate equation follows from L’Hôpital’s rule.
This behavior has been reported to reduce diversity in LLM generation
[
60
,
61
]
. By contrast, PRO employs forward KL divergence as the regularizer, which diverges to infinity as
\pi_{\theta}(y)\rightarrow 0
:

\displaystyle\lim_{\pi_{\theta}(y)\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\log\frac{\frac{1}{2}}{\sigma\big(r_{\theta}(y)-r_{\theta}(y^{\prime})\big)}+\log\frac{\frac{1}{2}}{\sigma\big(r_{\theta}(y^{\prime})-r_{\theta}(y)\big)}\bigg)

\displaystyle=-\log(2)-\lim_{\pi_{\theta}(y)\rightarrow 0}\frac{\log\sigma\big(r_{\theta}(y)-r_{\theta}(y^{\prime})\big)+\log\sigma\big(r_{\theta}(y^{\prime})-r_{\theta}(y)\big)}{2}=+\infty,~~~\forall y^{\prime}:\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime})>0.

This regularizer prevents response probability from collapsing to zero, exhibiting mass-covering behavior.
Besides, it is computed over every response pair constructed from
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathcal{H}}
. By adjusting the composition of
\mathcal{H}
, we can flexibly modulate the extent of probability mass coverage over the original response space
\mathcal{Y}
.

The presented work focuses exclusively on direct alignment, specifically optimizing LLMs using offline data, without the inclusion of on-policy or online samples. However, we want to emphasize that the proposed PRO method can be directly adopted in on-policy or online scenarios. Considering the above distinctions between PRO and RLHF, several followup research problems arise:

•

Despite the risk of unreliable evaluations, the reward model in RLHF provides additional alignment signals for unlabeled responses, which may further improve performance if applied appropriately. This motivates extending PRO to incorporate on-policy samples within such a paradigm, particularly when computational resources are sufficient. A key difference in implementation is that we require a score model to directly predict
\hat{s}(y)
, rather than the reward model. Since the score is no longer a latent variable inferred from user preferences, it does not rely on any model hypothesis. Moreover, by its definition and the property established in Theorem
3.1
, the score is bounded in
[-1/2,1/2]
and has an expectation of zero. These information can be leveraged to calibrate the score model or determine when it is reliable. Considering these potential benefits, it is worthwhile to investigate how PRO performs compared to RLHF when applied in the on-policy setting.

•

Recent progress has shown that online reinforcement learning can substantially enhance the reasoning capabilities for LLMs
[
62
]
. Popular approaches, such as PPO
[
2
]
and GRPO
[
63
]
, incorporate
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\pi_{\theta}\;\middle|\middle|\;\mathclap{\phantom{\pi_{\text{ref}}}}\allowbreak\pi_{\text{ref}}\right)
as a regularizer. However, it is reported
[
64
]
that the regularizer is excessively strong, resulting in overly constrained optimization of the LLM. Although fully removing the regularizer mitigates this issue, it may lead to performance degeneration on other unconsidered tasks during post training. This highlights the need for alternative methods to address over-regularization. As noted, the regularizer in PRO is milder since its gradient involves a bounded weighting factor on
\mathbf{\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y)}
.
Given the rule-based rewards are also bounded, PRO presents a promising objective function for such settings and warrants further empirical investigation.

•

During the reinforcement learning stage, LLMs explore response space by leveraging the prior knowledge acquired from pretraining or supervised finetuning. Maintaining response diversity is critical for enabling a broad spectrum of meaningful exploration, which in turn fosters the development of more advanced reasoning abilities
[
65
]
. Since the regularizer in PRO exhibits a mass-covering behavior, it is likely more effective at preserving diversity during post-training. We consider examining its practical effects as another future research direction.

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{y\in\{y_{w},y_{l}\}}\bigg(\frac{1}{2}\log\sigma\big(r_{\theta}(x,y)-r_{\theta}(x,\mathcal{H})\big)+\frac{1}{2}\log\sigma\big(r_{\theta}(x,\mathcal{H})-r_{\theta}(x,y)\big)\bigg)\bigg],

where

\displaystyle r_{\theta}(x,\mathcal{H})=\beta\log\frac{1-\sum_{y\in\{y_{w},y_{l}\}}\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{1-\sum_{y\in\{y_{w},y_{l}\}}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}.

The value of
\eta
is chosen so that all response pairs from
\{y_{w},y_{l},\mathcal{H}\}
contribute equally to the overall loss.

Binary Feedback

Following the KTO implementation, the binary-feedback data is structured as
\mathcal{D}=\big\{(x^{(i)},y^{(i)},s^{(i)})\big\}_{i=1}^{I}
. That is, even though multiple responses may exist for a prompt, they are treated separately. Consequently, the empirical distribution reduces to
\hat{\mu}(y)=1
. By setting
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{Y}\setminus\{y\}
and
\mu=\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{\mu}
as in (
9
), we can rewrite
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{PRO}}
as

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{PRO-B}}(\pi_{\theta};\pi_{\text{ref}})

\displaystyle\!=\!-\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x,y,s)\sim\mathcal{D}}\Bigg[s\log\pi_{\theta}(y)+\alpha\bigg(\frac{1}{2}\log\sigma\big(r_{\theta}(x,y)\!-\!r_{\theta}(x,\mathcal{H})\big)\!+\!\frac{1}{2}\log\sigma\big(r_{\theta}(x,\mathcal{H})\!-\!r_{\theta}(x,y)\big)\bigg)\Bigg]\!,

where

\displaystyle r_{\theta}(x,\mathcal{H})=\beta\log\frac{1-\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{1-\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}.

The regularizer coefficient is in fact
\alpha\eta(1-\eta)
. Since there is no need to have two parameters to determine the regularization strength, we re-denote the coefficient by
\alpha
for notational simplicity. In the experiments, we tune
\alpha
so that the performance of PRO-B on Anthropic-HH test dataset matches that of PRO-P under
\beta=0.003
. The resulting value is
\alpha=2.5
, which we use throughout unless otherwise noted.

Scalar Feedback

Following the NCA implementation, the scalar-feedback data is structured as
\mathcal{D}=\big\{(x^{(i)},y_{1:N}^{(i)},s_{1:N}^{(i)})\big\}_{i=1}^{I}
, where
N
is the number of labeled responses per prompt. Then, the empirical distribution over responses for a prompt
x
is
\hat{\mu}(y_{n})=\frac{1}{N}
for all
n\in\{1,\cdots,N\}
. By setting
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{Y}\setminus\{y_{1:N}\}
,
\mu=\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{\mu}
and
\eta=\frac{N}{N+1}
, we can rewrite
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{PRO}}
as

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{PRO-S}}(\pi_{\theta};\pi_{\text{ref}})

\displaystyle=-\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{1:N},s_{1:N})\sim\mathcal{D}}\Bigg[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}s_{n}\cdot\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{n}|x)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{2\alpha}{N(N+1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n,n^{\prime}\in\{1,\cdots,N\}\\
n<n^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\bigg(\frac{1}{2}\log\sigma\big(r_{\theta}(x,y_{n})-r_{\theta}(x,y_{n^{\prime}})\big)+\frac{1}{2}\log\sigma\big(r_{\theta}(x,y_{n^{\prime}})-r_{\theta}(x,y)\big)\bigg)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{2\alpha}{N(N+1)}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\bigg(\frac{1}{2}\log\sigma\big(r_{\theta}(x,y_{n})-r_{\theta}(x,\mathcal{H})\big)+\frac{1}{2}\log\sigma\big(r_{\theta}(x,\mathcal{H})-r_{\theta}(x,y_{n})\big)\bigg)\Bigg],

where

\displaystyle r_{\theta}(x,\mathcal{H})=\beta\log\frac{1-\sum_{n=1}^{N}\pi_{\theta}(y_{n}|x)}{1-\sum_{n=1}^{N}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{n}|x)}.

The value of
\eta
is chosen so that all response pairs from
\{y_{1:N},\mathcal{H}\}
contribute equally to the overall loss. The regularizer strength is in fact given by
\frac{\alpha}{(N+1)^{2}}
. We rescale it to
\frac{2\alpha}{N(N+1)}
to ensure that the optimizer-regularizer weight ratio remains consistent with
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{PRO-B}}
for any
\alpha
. In accordance with the binary-feedback case, we set
\alpha=2.5
by default.

A common characteristic of the three cases above is that
\mathcal{H}
contains a countless number of responses, making
\pi_{\theta}(\mathcal{H}|x)
and
\pi_{\text{ref}}(\mathcal{H}|x)
extremely close to one. Moreover, a small step of parameter update along any direction does not significantly alter the closeness of
\pi_{\theta}(\mathcal{H}|x)
to one. Consequently, both the value and the gradient of
r_{\theta}(x,\mathcal{H})
are approximately zero, allowing us to safely omit this term from the loss function. This behavior was verified in our preliminary experiments, and in all the reported experiments we set
r_{\theta}(x,\mathcal{H})=0
for simplicity.

Appendix E
Performance Degeneration of KTO

We hypothesize that KTO’s performance degradation in Section
5.1
is due to the direct use of sigmoid function in its loss formulation:

\displaystyle\widehat{L}_{\text{KTO}}(\pi_{\theta};\pi_{\text{ref}})=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{w},y_{l})\sim\mathcal{D}}\big[\lambda_{D}\sigma\big(\beta(r_{\theta}(x,y_{w})-z_{0}\big)+\lambda_{U}\sigma\big(\beta(z_{0}-r_{\theta}(x,y_{l})\big)\big],

where
\lambda_{D},\lambda_{U}
are hyperparameters and
z_{0}
is a non-negative reference value.
The sigmoid function exhibits a saturation effect if its input deviates significantly from zero. During training, particularly in later stages of an epoch, the term
r_{\theta}(x,y_{w})-z_{0}
may become highly negative. This happens because previous model updates based on other data can lead to forgetting the likelihood of responses that appear in later phases. As a result, when optimizing for these responses, the decreased value of
r_{\theta}(x,y_{w})
can diminish the gradient of the sigmoid function and hinder effective learning. In contrast, NCA and the PRO series incorporate the log-sigmoid function in their loss functions, which only saturates on one side, thus avoiding this issue.

Appendix F
The Role of
\alpha
and
\beta
in PRO Regularizer

The regularizer in PRO minimizes the following KL divergence, for any given
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
:

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{2}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\mathcal{B}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\;\middle|\middle|\;\mathclap{\phantom{\mathcal{B}\bigg(\sigma\bigg(\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{1})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{1})}-\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{2})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{2})}\bigg)\bigg)}}\allowbreak\mathcal{B}\bigg(\sigma\bigg(\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{1})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{1})}-\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{2})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{2})}\bigg)\bigg)\right),

To explore how the hyperparameters
\alpha
and
\beta
influence the regularizer, we denote

\displaystyle\delta=\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{1})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{1})}-\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{2})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{2})},

and plot the value and the gradient of

\displaystyle f(\delta)=\frac{\alpha}{2\beta}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\mathcal{B}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\;\middle|\middle|\;\mathclap{\phantom{\mathcal{B}\Big(\sigma(\beta\delta)\Big)}}\allowbreak\mathcal{B}\Big(\sigma(\beta\delta)\Big)\right)

in Figure
3
. We incorporate
\beta
into the denominator, because it acts as a weighting factor in the optimizer. Normalizing by it emphasizes the variation in relative strength between the regularizer and the optimizer as
\alpha
and
\beta
change.

Figure 3:
\alpha
determines the maximum norm of the regularizer gradient, while
\beta
controls the rate at which the gradient norm increases from zero to its maximum value.

As shown in Figure
3
, increasing
\beta
accelerates the growth of the regularizer for medium magnitudes of
\delta
. However, for very large or very small
\delta
, the growth stabilizes and becomes independent of
\beta
. This can result in the regularizer being too weak to effectively constrain the model, especially when the optimizer has a large gradient norm. The introduction of
\alpha
increases flexibility in this regard; by varying its value, the maximum gradient norm of the regularizer can be adjusted as needed. This explains why increasing
\beta
fails to mitigate performance deterioration but increasing
\alpha
helps in Section
5.3
.

Appendix G
Additional Experimental Setup

For the Pythia-6.9B model, we first utilize the preferred responses from Anthropic-HH dataset for supervised finetuning prior to alignment, following the procedures in
[
8
]
. Since the Mistral-7B-sft model is already finetuned, we apply it directly for alignment.

Hyperparameters

We adopt a uniform set of hyperparameters across all models, methods and datasets, for both supervised finetuning and alignment. Specifically, the maximum prompt length is 1024, and the maximum sequence length is 2048. Models are trained for 1 epoch with RMSprop as the optimizer. The learning rate linearly increases from 0 to 5e-7 over the first 150 training steps and remains constant thereafter. The batch size is 64 for KTO and PRO-B, and 32 for the other methods. This adjustment is due to each binary sample containing a single response, which is half that of pairwise samples. To ensure a consistent number of gradient steps when comparing methods using pairwise and binary feedback, we double the batch size for binary feedback.

When evaluating KTO with imbalanced binary feedback, we implement a reweighting strategy in the loss function to balance the effective proportions of desired and undesired samples at a 1:1 ratio. For instance, if the original ratio of desired to undesired samples is 100:1, we multiply the loss for undesired samples by a factor of 100. This reweighting technique was initially introduced in the KTO paper. We also apply it to PRO.
