The Auranofin–Colistin Combination Efficiency Against the Gram-Negative Strains: A Review
Authors: Mihaela Ileana Ionescu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMultidrug-resistant bacteria that possess resistance genes against last-resort antibiotics, such as colistin, are notably prevalent in hospital environments. Therapeutic options for addressing these infections are limited, prompting researchers to explore alternative strategies. One promising approach is drug repurposing, which involves testing combinations of antibiotics with other medications to discover potential synergistic effects against multidrug-resistant (MDR) strains. Auranofin, an antirheumatic drug known for its anti-inflammatory and antineoplastic properties, has recently been investigated in combination with colistin as a potential therapeutic strategy for treating infections caused by multidrug-resistant bacteria. A review of 10 articles retrieved from five public scientific databases indicates that all authors reported synergistic activity of the auranofin–colistin combination against multidrug-resistant (MDR) species, with effectiveness demonstrated in both in vitro and in vivo experiments. However, the minimal inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of this combination vary, and the precise mechanism of action remains unclear. Recent studies examining the crystal structure of metallo-beta-lactamase type 2 (NDM-1) and a probable phosphatidylethanolamine transferase, MCR-1 (which confers mobilized colistin resistance), suggest that activation with glutathione or N-acetylcysteine is necessary for efficacy. Additionally, there is strong evidence indicating that auranofin acts as a dual inhibitor of both MCR and metallo-beta-lactamase (MBL) by displacing the Zn(II) cofactor.

Introduction

The management of opportunistic infections caused by Gram-negative bacilli in immunocompromised patients is frequently complicated by the emergence of multidrug-resistant (MDR), extensively drug-resistant (XDR), and pan-drug-resistant (PDR) strains. Multiple definitions exist for these resistance classifications.1 Healthcare professionals must stay informed about international guidelines regarding antimicrobial susceptibility. According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), the definition of the MDR phenotype for Gram-negative non-fermenting bacilli is as follows: Pseudomonas aeruginosa is considered multidrug-resistant if it tests either intermediate (I) or resistant (R) to at least one drug in at least three of the following five categories: extended-spectrum cephalosporins, fluoroquinolones, aminoglycosides, carbapenems, and piperacillin/tazobactam. According to European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST), the term “Intermediate” (I) should be interpreted as “Susceptible, increased exposure, when there is a high likelihood of therapeutic success because exposure to the agent is increased by adjusting the dosing regimen or by its concentration at the site of infection” (EUCAST v 16.0, page 3) (v_16.0_Breakpoint_Tables.pdf). (accessed on 31th January 2026). Additionally, according to CDC, any Acinetobacter species isolate is classified as multidrug-resistant if it tests intermediate or resistant to at least one drug in at least three of the following six categories: extended-spectrum cephalosporins, fluoroquinolones, aminoglycosides, carbapenems, piperacillin/tazobactam, and ampicillin/sulbactam. (https://www.cdc.gov/nhsn/pdfs/ps-analysis-resources/phenotype_definitions.pdf) (accessed on 31th January 2026).

Infections caused by MDR Gram-negative bacteria are particularly challenging to treat because these bacterial strains are resistant to nearly all antibiotics.2 These infections predominantly occur in critically ill patients within hospital settings.3 Various strategies are being explored to improve treatment outcomes for MDR infections. One such approach is the discovery and optimization of new antibiotic classes, such as tethered macrocyclic peptides, which have shown promise against carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii.4 Additionally, designing new compounds that inhibit peptidoglycan synthesis is a viable option, as peptidoglycan is a unique structure found in prokaryotic cell walls but not in eukaryotic cells.5 Currently, combining beta-lactams with beta-lactamase inhibitors provides significant advantages.6–9 Many studies are focusing on identifying natural compounds with antibiotic properties.10–12 Furthermore, there is a growing interest in reversing antibiotic resistance or repurposing existing drugs for antimicrobial use.13–15

Polymyxins, specifically polymyxin B and colistin, are considered last-resort antibiotics that target cellular membranes.16 Gram-negative bacteria have an outer membrane made up of lipopolysaccharides as part of their cell wall structure. This characteristic limits the effectiveness of polymyxins only against Gram-negative species. In contrast, Gram-positive bacteria lack an outer membrane and possess a much thicker layer of peptidoglycan.17 Furthermore, the similarities between the cellular membranes of prokaryotes and those of eukaryotes explain the restrictions on the use of polymyxins in treating human infections.

Recently, metal-based compounds were tested for their ability to reduce the minimal inhibitory concentration (MIC) of various antibiotics.18 Cobalt coordination (CoIII) complexes were found to reduce the MIC of the antibiotic nalidixic acid by synergistically targeting DNA.19 Auranofin (AUR), a gold-based antirheumatic agent, is known to suppress inflammation and stimulate cell-mediated immunity. Its antibacterial activity has been primarily demonstrated against Gram-positive bacterial species, including Mycobacterium tuberculosis.20–23 Auranofin also inhibits biofilm formation in Bacteroides fragilis, a Gram-negative anaerobic bacillus.24 However, the exact mechanism of action of AUR is not yet fully understood. Its anti-inflammatory effects are attributed to a reduced capacity of immune sera to participate in antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC), as well as a decrease in complement-dependent responses.25 Research by Sonzogni-Desautels et al indicates that auranofin influences both upstream and downstream processes of NF-κB-dependent IL-6 expression, leading to a decrease in IL-6 levels.26–28

In this review, we focus on the effectiveness of combining polymyxins (either polymyxin B or colistin) with auranofin for treating multidrug-resistant (MDR) Gram-negative infections. The paper is structured into three sections. First, we provide a literature review and detail the selection criteria for the articles included in our analysis. Next, we discuss the potential mechanisms behind the auranofin–colistin combination in combating infections caused by MDR Gram-negative species. Finally, we present a summary of the drug properties and the chemical analyses conducted in this study.

Search Strategy

We searched five public databases: PubMed, Web of Science (WOS), Clarivate Analytics, Cochrane Library, Google Scholar, and the RCSB (PDB) Protein Databank. Whenever possible, we utilized the advanced search tools available in each database. This advanced searching in PubMed, Clarivate Analytics, Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar yielded 597 references. Resistance Studies

Minimal inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimal bactericidal concentration (MBC) assays are primary methods for assessing the inhibitory effects of individual drugs and drug combinations. Colistin antimicrobial susceptibility testing presents a significant challenge for clinical laboratories because, at present, only the broth microdilution method (BMD) (a serial two-fold serial dilution) is recognized as the reference method by both EUCAST and CLSI.41–43 Antimicrobial susceptibility testing of colistin, auranofin, and their combination was performed using BMD, a method that allows to measure the MIC.29,31–33,38 The measurement of MBC was made indirectly by counting of bacterial colonies on the Luria-Bertani agar inoculated with serial dilution of inhibitors, including colistin, auranofin, and other compounds tested.31 Table 4 presents MIC data for auranofin alone and in combination with colistin. Both drug-resistant and drug-sensitive Gram-negative strains were tested, revealing substantial variability in auranofin MIC values across species, independent of colistin sensitivity.

Ding X. et al (2020) evaluated the efficacy of the polymer pEt-20 in reversing the antibiotic resistance phenotype of a multidrug-resistant (MDR) Acinetobacter baumannii strain. The study also investigated the effects of combining auranofin and colistin. The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of the auranofin and colistin combination against MDR A. baumannii (BAA-1789) was determined using the BMD. Additionally, the minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) of auranofin alone (125 μg/mL) was compared to that of the auranofin–colistin combination (3.91 μg/mL), demonstrating the enhanced efficacy of the combination against MDR A. baumannii BAA-1789. The lack of additional studies utilizing similar methodologies limits further comparative analysis.31 Some authors provide succinct details about susceptibility testing. Peryclitt et al (2022) mentioned that antibiotic susceptibility was made by “the standard disk diffusion method and breakpoint assessment were performed according to the European Committee of Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) recommendations. When breakpoints were not available in EUCAST, we used recommendations from the French Society of Microbiology. (CA-SFM)”.34 Limitations of in vitro colistin susceptibility testing restrict the available methods for further evaluation of colistin synergy with other antibiotics or non-antibiotic agents. The following assays assess the synergy between colistin and auranofin: checkerboard assays, high‑throughput screening assays, time-kill assays, cellular morphology, antibiofilm activity, mutation rates, biochemical analysis, equilibrium dialysis and thermal stability, crystallographic studies, and in vivo models. High Throughput Screening Assays

A high-throughput screening assay is a rapid way to select suitable drugs from a large panel of compounds already approved for human treatment. Sun W. et al (2016) selected 25 compounds from 5170 drugs using a quantitatively screening method - an automated ultra-high‑throughput bacterial assay (HIGA). These 25 compounds were further evaluated for their efficacy against MDR clinical isolates and for testing synergic combination by test drug combination (TDC) assay and standard microdilution broth assay (BMD). They confirmed that 1 μg/mL gentamicin reduced the MIC of polymyxin from 8 μg/mL to 2 μg/mL for an MDR K.pneumoniae strain. Also, 5 μM auranofin re-sensitize MDR K.pneumoniae strain to antibiotics, but this auranofin concentration is above the auranofin peak human plasma concentration (1 μM). The authors further tested different three – drug combinations and demonstrated that two combinations suppressed >80% growth of 10 MDR Gram-negative bacterial strains – K. pneumoniae, A. baumannii, P. aeruginosa, C. freundii, E. cloacae, and E.coli. The first combination is colistin 1.7 μM, auranofin 1 μM, and ceftazidime 15 μM and the second combination is colistin 1.9 μM, auranofin 1 μM, and rifabutin 0.2 μM. The authors tested MIC and demonstrate synergistic effect of auranofin, colistin and rifabutin combination against an MDR E.coli strain (FICi = 0.44).29

Peyclit L. et al (2022) in an in vitro screening study of 1280 drugs against the MDR isolate from patients with cystic fibrosis. The authors suggest that, in clinical practice, the term “difficult-to-treat” resistant bacteria is most appropriate, as there are strategies that could overcome the infections caused by MDR strains. For example, in vitro assay shows that extended-drug resistant XDR P. aeruginosa could be inhibited by three alternative strategies – increasing dose of drug (colistin), drug repurposing, or potential combination therapy. Among drug repurposing, the combination auranofin (+colistin ± ceftazidime) confirmed the results published in the Sun W. et al (2016) paper.34 However, these strategies should consider monitoring the dosage of the drugs and correlate with the peak plasma concentration, renal and hepatic functions, and other clinical recommendations.29,34

Time-Kill Assays

This method confirms the synergistic effect, and Feng X. et al (2021) demonstrate that the colistin–auranofin combination effectively eliminates the colistin-resistant strains harboring different mcr genes – mcr-8 positive K. pneumoniae, mcr-1 positive K.pneumoniae, or mcr-1 positive E. coli.33,46 Sun H. et al (2020) report that 6 μg/mL auranofin combined with 2 μg/mL colistin kills E. coli carrying MCR-1-J53.32

Cellular Morphology

Cellular morphology is an important indicator of inhibitory effect of different drug combinations.

Feng X. et al (2021) investigated the effects of the colistin–auranofin combination on cellular morphology of colistin-resistant K. pneumoniae and A. baumannii (Table 3) using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Auranofin monotherapy does not induce changes in cellular morphology, while colistin monotherapy results only in cellular membrane blebbing. In contrast, the combination of auranofin and colistin leads to extensive cell-surface disruptions and cellular lysis in colistin-resistant K. pneumoniae. Furthermore, auranofin–colistin treatment of colistin-resistant A. baumannii results in significant cell length reduction and increased cell surface irregularity compared with the control group.33

More recently, Lu J. et al (2025) reported that the colistin–auranofin combination induces multiple forms of cellular damage in Edwardsiella piscicida, a Gram-negative fish pathogen that poses challenges to the aquaculture industry, by targeting the cell membrane, lipopolysaccharide, and flagella. The study examined three E. piscicida strains isolated from diseased fish (Table 3). The Live/Dead bacterial cell viability assay indicated that auranofin or colistin monotherapy resulted in a dead/live ratio comparable to the control group. In contrast, the combination of auranofin and colistin increased the dead/live ratio to 7.8%, likely due to enhanced cell membrane permeability and subsequent bacterial cell death from cytoplasmic leakage. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed that changes in cellular morphology were indicative of extensive membrane damage. Furthermore, cultivation of E. piscicida with gradually increasing concentrations of auranofin and 16 μg/mL colistin resulted in increased outer membrane permeability, suggesting that auranofin augments the membrane damage induced by colistin. Among other cellular components of E. piscicida impaired by auranofin–colistin combination are bacterial mobility, bacterial two-component system, lipopolysaccharide, and secretion and virulence system, mainly those genes involved in detection of environmental signals. Notably, the expression levels of flagellar-related genes of filA, filC, flhC, and flhD were reduced by 67.65, 67.7, 17.67, and 49.57, respectively. The mobility inhibition further impairs biofilm formation.38

Antibiofilm Activity

Torres N.S. et al (2018) show that certain drug combinations are effective against preformed biofilms on glass disk of two P. aeruginosa strains - P. aeruginosa PA01 and a clinical strain (Table 3). Biofilm formation was verified using confocal laser scanning microscopy and the Filmtracer Live/Dead Biofilm Viability Kit. Subsequently, the efficacy of drug combinations was assessed against biofilms on glass disks. Following incubation, five 10-fold serial dilutions from each biofilm sample were plated on blood agar, and the resulting colonies were automatically counted. In particular, the most active combinations with antibiofilm potential are colistin-clomiphene citrate and colistin–auranofin. Notably, both combinations determine a 2-log reduction in cell survival compared to individual drugs and control biofilm.30

Lu J. et al (2025) evaluated the antibiofilm efficacy of auranofin in combination with colistin against three E. piscicida strains (Table 3) using the crystal violet staining method. Neither colistin nor auranofin alone produced significant inhibition of Edwardsiella piscicida biofilm formation. In contrast, the combined treatment reduced E. piscicida biofilm formation by approximately 24.29%.38

Mutation Rates

The resistance development is a crucial issue of the emerging new strains that hamper the infectious diseases. Yu H. et al (2022) determined the MIC and MBC of the individual drug and drug combinations. Their study made a comparison of two drug combinations – auranofin–colistin and auranofin-pentamidine against clinical isolates of MDR mcr gene-positive Gram-negative strains. They conclude that auranofin-pentamidine slowed down the resistance to auranofin, compared with the auranofin–colistin.35 Sun H. et al (2020) propose that auranofin lowered the colistin resistance development in Enterobacterales.32

Enzyme Kinetics/Biochemical Analysis

Sun H. et al (2020) advance the hypothesis that auranofin is a dual inhibitor of mobilized colistin resistant (MCR) and metallo-β-lactamase (MBL) enzymes via displacement of Zn (II) cofactor. Because the auranofin has to be metabolized to the active compound [Au[Pet3)]+, the kinetic studies were made using the auranofin analogs. The [Au[Pet3)]+ inhibits the NMD-1 with IC50 of 437.9 ± 29.1 nM. Kinetic experiments show that a noncompetitive inhibition – Vmax decreases from 19.41 μM/min to 3.35 41 μM/min with the Km ~ 67.3 μM.32 In other recent study, Zung Q. et al (2023) demonstrate that the gold-based drugs are pro-drugs that require pre-activation with glutathione or N-acetyl cysteine.37

Equilibrium Dialysis and Thermal Stability

Sun H. et al (2020) investigate the inhibitory effect of auranofin against MCR-1 and Au(I) ion bind Zn-MCR-1-S with the binding affinity Kd of 3.36 ± 0.60 μM – for [Au[Pet3)]+; Zn(II) continuously decreases. The thermal stability experiments further confirm the auranofin binding to MCR-1.32

Polymyxins

The colistins A (polymyxin E1) and B (polymyxin E2) are naturally compounds produced by Bacillus colistinus. The colistins are peptide antibiotics that disrupt the osmotic integrity of the cell membrane.53 Related compounds are designed for oral or intravenous administration – colistin sulphate and colistin metasulphate, respectively. The colistin sulfate salt (Coly-Mycin S, Colimycin sulfate or Bleomycin) (CID 73090) has the molecular weight 1267.5 g/mol and molecular formula C53H102N16O17S. Coly-Mycin S. was submitted for approval in 05/17/1962 - New Drug Application (NDA): 050356 according to FDA U.S. Food and Drug Administration (accessed on 31 January, 2026). Colistin metasulphate is an inactive pro-drug that is converted in vivo into the active form, colistin. Colistin serves as a last-resort antibiotic for treating infections caused by multidrug-resistant strains. Due to its suitability for nebulized administration, colistin is useful for respiratory infections caused by Pseudomonas aeruginosa in patients with cystic fibrosis or bronchiectasis.54

Mechanism of Resistance to Colistin

Antibiotic resistance continues to evolve. According to the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) (https://www.eucast.org/) and the Clinical & Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) Guidelines (https://clsi.org), determining susceptibility or resistance to colistin remains challenging. Recommendations are frequently updated, necessitating continuous review of the latest scientific data.55 Detection of acquired resistance to polymyxin B or colistin is hindered by the absence of interpretive guidelines for commonly used clinical laboratory methods, such as diffusion methods, automated systems like Vitek, or E-test. Current clinical guidelines from EUCAST and CLSI (M100, Performance Standards for Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing, 35th Edition) recommend the broth microdilution (BMD) method.56,57

There are two main types of antibiotic resistance at polymyxin B/colistin: intrinsic (inherent) resistance and acquired resistance.

The intrinsic (inherent) resistance at polymyxin B / colistin is a characteristic of many Gram-negative species. According to the EUCAST expert rules, the following Enterobacterales exhibit intrinsic resistance at polymyxin B / colistin: Morganella morgani, Proteus mirabilis, Proteus vulgaris, Proteus penneri, Providencia rettgeri, Providencia stuartii, Serratia marcerscens, Yersinia pseudotuberculosis. The intrinsic resistance at polymyxin B/colistin in non-fermentative Gram-negative bacteria is reported at: Burkholderia cepacia complex and Elizabethkingia meningoseptica.58 Also, Neisseria spp. are highly intrinsic resistant at cationic antimicrobial peptides, including polymyxins.59 There are notable differences in the structure of the lipopolysaccharide (LPS) of the species that show intrinsic resistance at polymyxin B / colistin. Because polymyxins not only disrupt cell membrane but bind the lipid A part of the LPS, modifications in the lipid A of some bacterial species explain the intrinsic resistance at polymyxins.

The acquired resistance to polymyxin B / colistin is due to the following molecular mechanisms: chromosomal mutation or plasmid-mediated transferable mechanism. The alterations in the outer membrane of Gram-negative species by mutations in genes involved in lipid A synthesis and by overexpression of chromosomal two-component system genes – PmrAB and PhoPQ.60 In November 2015 was reported the first plasmid-mediated polymyxin resistance that harbored the mcr-1 gene at an E. coli strain SHP45 isolated from a pig. An efflux pump/potassium system (RosA and RosB) responsible for resistance to cationic antimicrobial peptides, including polymyxins, was reported in Yesinia spp.61 There are hypotheses that plasmid-born mcr genes have evolved from environmental bacterial species with innate resistance to polymyxins.60

Discussions

The global emergence of the acquired colistin-resistance phenotype is frequently linked to resistance to other antibiotics, thereby complicating treatment. Due to limited therapeutic options, there is considerable interest in developing strategies to both overcome colistin-resistance mechanisms and enhance colistin activity.62 Colistin, regarded as a last-resort antibiotic, is used to treat infections caused by multidrug-resistant strains, which often affect patients with underlying conditions, particularly those in intensive care units. Prolonged antibiotic therapy can result in the emergence of colistin-resistant strains and co-infections with bacterial species that possess intrinsic resistance to colistin, including Proteus spp., Providencia spp., Morganella spp., Serratia spp., and S. paucimobilis. This review evaluates published data on the efficacy of the auranofin–colistin combination. The combination of an antibiotic with an anti-inflammatory agent is a logical strategy, given the complexity of sepsis. Significant organ damage frequently results from the proliferation of microorganisms.63

Auranofin has been combined with various antibiotics in an effort to reverse antibiotic resistance phenotypes. The auranofin-aztreonam-avibactam combination demonstrates greater efficacy against MBL-producing Enterobacterales than against KPC-producing strains.64 There is significant interest in evaluating the synergistic effects of the auranofin–colistin combination for the treatment of multidrug-resistant (MDR) infections. The checkerboard assay is the most commonly used method to calculate the fractional inhibitory concentration index (FICi). Additionally, high-throughput screening enables the evaluation of a large panel of drugs already approved for human use. Results from in vitro experiments should be validated through subsequent in vivo studies. Determining the crystal structures of resistance-associated enzymes co-crystallized with relevant drugs significantly enhances the understanding of enzyme kinetics and the mechanisms underlying antibiotic resistance.

The recent studies analyzed in this review permit advances in some further perspectives of non-antibiotic combinations against bacterial infections. There are large panels of non-antibiotic combinations that show efficiency in the inhibition of Gram-negative and Gram-positive species. Moreover, there are some combinations that reverse some antibiotic resistance. Although the mechanisms are not fully understood, the in vitro and in vivo studies show promising results. Herein, we only show some of the results in comparison with the auranofin–colistin combination. Although we are focused on studying the synergistic drug combination against MDR bacterial species, we should also be aware of the antagonistic effects of some combinations that include antibiotics and other drugs.

Although there are many details that are not fully understood, the results recently published provide useful insight into the mechanisms of action of the gold drugs and colistin against MDR species. Also, the number of articles retrieved hampers a thorough statistical analysis. In spite of these limitations, we consider that a deep analysis of the results is useful for the identification of future studies.

Conclusions

Drug repurposing is a valuable therapeutic strategy when infections with MDR strains are difficult to treat. Many recent studies demonstrate that the combinations of non-antibiotic drugs with certain antibiotics exhibit synergistic activity against MDR strains and could even reverse some antibiotic-resistant phenotypes. Numerous recent studies provide strong evidence that auranofin in combination with antibiotics could be a therapeutic strategy against infections with bacterial species harboring MBLs and MCRs. However, we should be aware that drug combinations could have antagonistic effects; therefore, it is crucial to advance the understanding of the complex mechanisms beyond the activity of the drug combination against bacterial species infection to avoid the collateral damages.
