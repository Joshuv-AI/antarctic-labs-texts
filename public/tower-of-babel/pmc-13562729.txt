A gapless 100 m resolution daily mean land surface temperature dataset over the Tibetan Plateau in 2019
Authors: Xiaoxiao Kong, Yingbao Yang, Penghua Hu, Xin Pan, Zhenwei Wang, Xinyi Lu, Yang Dai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Tibetan Plateau (TP), known as the “Third Pole”, is highly sensitive to climate change. Fine-scale daily mean land surface temperature (DMLST) data are needed to study regional climate processes, permafrost dynamics, glacier melt, and land surface energy balance. However, existing DMLST products over the TP generally have spatial resolutions of 1 km to 0.05°, which are too coarse to resolve local processes in glacier forefields, permafrost slopes, and other heterogeneous landscapes. Here, we developed a partition-based spatiotemporal fusion framework that integrates ERA5-Land reanalysis data with MODIS and Landsat LST products to generate a gapless 100 m-resolution DMLST dataset over the TP for 2019. Validation against ground-based observations shows that the dataset achieves a root mean square error (RMSE) of 3.13 K, a mean absolute error (MAE) of 2.42 K, a mean bias error (BIAS) of −0.81 K, and a coefficient of determination (R²) of 0.93 at the daily scale. Compared with an existing 1 km product, the proposed dataset improves error metrics by approximately 0.50–0.60 K. The 100 m resolution improves the representation of slope aspect, microtopography, land-cover contrasts, local temperature gradients, and transition zones in glacierized and other typical regions. This dataset provides a high-resolution and gapless DMLST resource for quantitative studies of land surface energy balance, permafrost degradation, glacier melt, and related environmental processes over the TP.

Background & Summary

The Tibetan Plateau (TP) is one of the world’s most climate-sensitive high-mountain regions, characterized by its rugged topography, extensive cryosphere, and its critical role in the Asian water cycle1–3. These unique features make land surface temperature (LST) an essential variable for understanding key processes such as glacier melt, permafrost stability, runoff generation, and ecosystem responses4,5. Many of these processes operate at fine spatial scales, ranging from tens to hundreds of meters. For example, the local solar heating is strongly influenced by slope aspect, while transitions like permafrost thaw slumps and glacier forefields lead to sharp thermal gradients6–8. However, such fine-scale spatial variability cannot be effectively captured by LST products with resolutions ranging from 1 km to 0.05°, highlighting the need for higher-resolution LST data.

Available LST data each have important limitations for TP scale fine resolution applications9. Ground stations provide accurate point measurements but are sparse. ERA5-Land reanalysis dataset offers continuous hourly coverage but at 0.1° resolution. Moderate resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) satellite provides daily 1 km observations, but cloud contamination creates large gaps, and Landsat provides 100 m thermal information but with an 8 to 16 day repeat interval10,11. These spatial, temporal and cloud-related limitations motivate a gapless daily product at 100 m resolution12. Meanwhile, daily mean land surface temperature (DMLST) is more useful than single overpass temperatures for energy balance, permafrost and climate applications because it represents the mean thermal state over a full day13–15. It can also be aggregated directly to monthly and annual means, facilitating long-term climate change monitoring and analysis.

Current approaches for estimating DMLST from satellite data can be divided into two categories. The first derives DMLST directly from instantaneous measurements. For example, Williamson et al.16 calculated DMLST as the mean of daily maximum LST from MODIS and nighttime LST from Aqua, showing strong correlation with daily air temperature. Chen et al.17 estimated monthly mean LST from daytime and nighttime MODIS products and validated against ground observations and ERA-Interim, finding that averaging four daily observations provides a reliable approximation with RMSE around 2.65 K. However, these methods are sensitive to observational gaps, limiting their accuracy. The second category uses diurnal temperature cycle (DTC) models to estimate DMLST indirectly by modeling LST evolution18. Ouyang et al.19 applied Sin-Cos and Sin-Linear DTC models over the TP, showing that the Sin-Linear model minimizes errors (R² > 0.9). However, Robust DTC fitting typically requires at least four valid day-night observations with a complete clear-sky sequence, which is challenging over the TP due to frequent clouds and complex terrain20.

In recent years, several LST datasets have been successively produced. For instance, Zhan et al.21 combined annual and diurnal temperature cycles to produce a global 1-km DMLST dataset (2003–2019) with site-based RMSE < 3 K. Liu et al.22 and Li et al.23 developed machine learning-based global products, achieving RMSEs of 1.6–2.2 K and maintaining spatiotemporal continuity even under cloudy conditions. Although numerous studies have attempted to estimate DMLST using various methods, most of them have focused on global scales, and their applicability to regions like the TP, with complex topography and unique climatic conditions, remains to be fully assessed. At the same time, existing LST products typically have spatial resolutions ranging from 1 km to 0.05°, which limits current DMLST products for high-resolution land surface process studies over the TP, particularly for accurately characterizing key areas such as glacier forefields and permafrost slopes. Therefore, developing a high-resolution DMLST dataset tailored to the TP is of great importance for advancing our understanding of the regional surface energy balance, climate change trends, and eco-environmental responses. To investigate DMLST at fine spatial scales, Hu et al.24 proposed the Two-stage Hierarchical Spatiotemporal Fusion Network (THSTNet) based on the Swin Transformer, and successfully applied it to estimate fine-scale 100 m DMLST over the Zhangye area. The TP is characterized by pronounced topographic relief and small-scale surface heterogeneity, such as permafrost slopes and thaw slumps, which challenge conventional LST estimation methods. THSTNet is particularly suitable for this environment because its hierarchical architecture and shifted-window self-attention enable the model to capture both local microtopography variations and broader spatial patterns, thereby providing accurate daily mean LST estimates across complex terrain25–27. By effectively modeling these fine-scale processes, the approach addresses the unique challenges of the plateau rather than using the method solely for its general performance.

In this study, we developed a gapless DMLST dataset over the TP at 100 m spatial resolution for 2019, using a multi-source data fusion approach. ERA5-Land reanalysis data, MODIS, and Landsat8/9 LST products were integrated through THSTNet to produce a high-resolution DMLST product that captures fine-scale surface temperature variations over complex terrains such as glacier forefields and permafrost slopes. The accuracy and reliability of the dataset were evaluated against in situ observations from 17 meteorological stations distributed across the plateau, and the results demonstrate improved performance relative to existing coarser-resolution products. This 100 m-resolution DMLST dataset is designed to support reproducible studies of fine-scale surface thermal patterns and TP processes that are not well captured by existing 1 km to 0.05° products.

Methods

Data

Reanalysis data

ERA5-Land provides hourly skin temperature at 0.1° × 0.1° resolution globally28. For this study, we extracted all 2019 records (24 hourly values per day, 00:00–23:00 UTC) and averaged them to obtain DMLST at 10 km resolution, forming the baseline for subsequent downscaling. ERA5-Land was chosen for its continuous temporal coverage and extensive spatial completeness, which ensure reproducible baseline daily temperature fields across the TP. The dataset is freely available from the Copernicus Climate Change Service (C3S) Climate Data Store (CDS) platform (https://cds.climate.copernicus.eu/).

Remote sensing data

MODIS Terra LST (MOD11A1, 1 km) and Landsat 8/9 LST (100 m) were used as remote sensing inputs29–31. We used only MODIS Terra daytime LST because Landsat 8/9 provides only daytime observations, and the overpass time of Terra daytime LST closely matches Landsat, which facilitates accurate spatiotemporal fusion. MODIS data were quality-controlled to retain only “good quality” pixels; after cloud and shadow removal, each subregion retained 30–50 days of images, with higher seasonal availability in winter than summer. Approximately 20% of pixels were masked or filled. Landsat 8/9 LST data were similarly filtered, yielding about 30 high-resolution training samples per subregion. These data were used in the two-stage downscaling process (10 km to 1 km and to 100 m) to train the THSTNet network. Each stage was divided into blocks of 64 × 64 (1 km) or 256 × 256 (100 m) pixels, with valid-pixel thresholds of 80% and 70%, respectively. Within each block, 80% of the samples were randomly assigned to the training set and 20% to the testing set to ensure independent evaluation. The MODIS and Landsat LST products are freely available from NASA’s Earth Observing System Data and Information System (https://ladsweb.modaps.eosdis.nasa.gov/search/) and the USGS Landsat 8/9 Level-2 archive (https://earthexplorer.usgs.gov/).

For cross-validation, we used Zhan’s 1 km DMLST product over the TP in 2019 as the benchmark. This product was generated from MODIS Terra/Aqua LST observations using annual and diurnal temperature cycle models, and was downloaded from the National Ecosystem Science Data Center website (http://www.nesdc.org.cn/sdo/detail?id=60f4e35e7e28173cf0c8a771).

Ground observation

To quantitatively assess the quality of the DMLST dataset developed for the TP, we used ground-based meteorological station observations for validation. The station data were obtained from the “China Surface Climate Data Daily Dataset (V3.0)” released by the National Meteorological Information Center of China, which provides daily records of surface pressure, air temperature, evaporation, relative humidity, wind direction and wind speed, sunshine duration and 0 cm LST elements from 699 reference and basic meteorological stations across China since January 1951; the 0 cm LST elements include daily maximum, minimum and mean LST. In this study, we extracted DMLST from stations within the Tibetan Plateau, removed missing or invalid records based on quality-control flags, and excluded stations with poor observation quality or substantial missing records to ensure reliable validation. The remaining 17 evenly distributed stations provide a representative coverage of the plateau in terms of elevation, land cover types, and climatic conditions, allowing a robust assessment of our dataset. Detailed information and the spatial distribution of validation sites are shown in Table 1 and Fig. 1. The land cover type data is sourced from the Annual China Land Cover Dataset (CLCD), which provides annual 30 m resolution land cover information for China from 1985 to 2024, including nine land cover types: cropland, forest, shrubland, grassland, water bodies, ice and snow, bare land, impervious surface, and wetlands.

Technical workflow.

Generation of 1 km resolution DMLST

In this step, ERA5-Land LST and MODIS LST are used to train the THSTNet spatiotemporal fusion network, which then downscales the 10 km DMLST from the previous step to a 1 km resolution over the TP. THSTNet is a two-stage network designed for fine-scale fusion tasks: the first stage captures global structures, while the second reconstructs fine-scale details. The upsampling module uses cross-stage feature fusion, combining global information from the first stage with detailed features from the second, thereby enhancing the quality of the fine-resolution predictions24.

Spatiotemporal fusion uses a pair of fine- and coarse-resolution images at a reference time and a coarse-resolution image at the target time to predict the fine-resolution image at the target time. This method requires high spatial consistency and completeness between the reference images, which can be affected when the input data is incomplete. However, due to the limitations of optical remote sensing, which cannot penetrate clouds and precipitation, the MODIS images used in this study suffer from large data gaps, a problem exacerbated when the study area expands, making it challenging to ensure data completeness.

To address this limitation, we designed a partition-based search framework that divides the study area into smaller experimental units. Specifically, the TP is divided into a 3 × 10 grid of subregions, within which MODIS imagery is independently screened and processed to identify complete pairs of reference images for local fusion. The locally fused results are then mosaicked to create a seamless product for the entire TP, as illustrated in Fig. 3. First, we applied MODIS quality-control files to filter the imagery, retaining only high-quality pixels with an RMSE < 1 K. Recognizing that clouds and fog often affect large, spatially contiguous areas of remote sensing images, we further subdivided each subregion into a 9 × 6 grid of 64 × 64 pixel blocks (at 1 km resolution) and required that at least 80% of the pixels in each block be valid. If all blocks within a subregion satisfied this threshold for a given acquisition, the image for that date was considered free from cloud contamination and retained for constructing training samples. This approach effectively minimizes the impact of clouds and outliers on sample quality, with any small residual gaps in the retained images attributed to sensor imaging issues or terrain occlusion, rather than cloud contamination.

A regionalized spatiotemporal fusion framework for DMLST over the TP.

For the screened MODIS LST data, small gaps still remain. To address this, we applied a self-supervised masking strategy to reconstruct these gaps32. First, we randomly masked 30% of the valid pixels in each cropped MODIS LST image to simulate realistic data loss patterns. These masked images were then used as input, with the original unmasked images serving as target outputs, to train a context autoencoder (CAE). By learning to reconstruct a large set of image samples, the CAE captures the spatial structure and contextual dependencies of LST fields, effectively restoring the missing values. The training loss is defined as the RMSE, guiding parameter optimization. Finally, the trained model is used to predict and reconstruct the missing pixels in the screened images. This approach enables the model to infer and fill gaps based on the spatial patterns of neighboring pixels, resulting in a smooth and spatially continuous LST field.

During the spatiotemporal fusion, the coarser ERA5-Land LST is first resampled to 1 km to match the MODIS LST grid, and the processed MODIS and ERA5-Land LST fields are then jointly used to downscale the 10 km DMLST to 1 km, yielding a 1 km DMLST dataset over the TP.

Generation of 100 m resolution DMLST

To further enhance spatial resolution, we use Landsat LST to downscale the 1 km DMLST. Unlike MODIS, the thermal infrared sensor onboard Landsat offers higher spatial resolution but has a 16-day repeat cycle, making continuous coverage in both space and time challenging. Previous studies have shown that two adjacent scenes along the same orbit differ in overpass time by about 24 seconds, with the minimum interval between adjacent orbits being about 7 days. For two adjacent orbits covering the same row, the within-day overpass times differ by approximately 6 minutes. Based on this, reducing the size of the subregions helps mitigate LST discrepancies caused by overpass time mismatches. Balancing sample size and computational efficiency, we ultimately partitioned the TP into a 6 × 15 grid, resulting in 90 subregions.

During preprocessing, we first remove cloud from Landsat 8/9 Level-2 Collection 2 band 10 images by discarding pixels flagged as cloud or cloud shadow (values 3 and 4 in the QA_PIXEL band) and mosaicking all relevant paths within the study area. For overlapping areas, valid pixels are selected according to a row-column priority rule to avoid inconsistencies in the mosaicked product. We then apply the same partition-based screening framework as used for MODIS (Fig. 3) to quality-control the Landsat imagery. Each subregion is further divided into 11 × 10 blocks of 256 × 256 pixels (at 100 m resolution), and, given the relatively sparse Landsat coverage, the threshold for the proportion of valid pixels within each block are relaxed to 70% to balance data quality and sample size. For the screened images, any remaining local gaps are filled using the same self-supervised masking strategy to produce high-resolution scenes as complete as possible. MODIS LST is resampled to 100 m, and the corresponding images are used as training samples.

In the fusion stage, the processed MODIS LST and Landsat LST are used to construct training samples for joint learning of spatial detail patterns, and the locally fused results are then aggregated to produce a complete output for the entire region, thereby downscaling the DMLST over the TP from 1 km to 100 m. This approach fully exploits the temporal continuity of the 1 km DMLST and the high spatial resolution of Landsat data, yielding a 100 m DMLST dataset covering the TP and providing a robust data basis for subsequent investigations of spatiotemporal LST variability and associated land-surface processes at the regional scale.

Data Records

The dataset33 described in this paper can be accessed on the Qinghai-Tibetan Plateau Data Center’s data sharing platform at https://doi.org/10.11888/Terre.tpdc.303145. The dataset includes TP’s DMLST in 2019. The data are stored in GeoTIFF format with a spatial resolution of 100 m and units in Kelvin.

Naming convention for data files: The specific naming convention for data files is “100 m_result_chunkX.tif”, where < X > represents the block index of the TP. Each file contains 365 bands, corresponding to daily images from Day 1 to Day 365 of 2019. The dataset is stored in TIF format, projected using WGS84, with a spatial resolution of 100m and a file size of approximately 9.8GB per file.

It can be seen from Fig. 6 that after downscaling from 10 km to 100 m, the accuracy at all stations improved substantially. Across all sites, RMSE decreased from 5.02 K to 3.13 K, MAE from 3.85 K to 2.42 K, and R² increased from 0.80 to 0.93, indicating that the TPDM dataset achieved significant enhancements in daily-scale accuracy while markedly improving spatial resolution, effectively capturing regional-scale heterogeneity. Some stations showed particularly pronounced improvements, such as Nuomuhong, where the annual RMSE of the original 0.1° DMLST was 5.31 K and decreased to 2.47 K after downscaling to 100 m, with BIAS reduced from 1.46 K to −0.03 K. In contrast, other stations, such as Lazi, exhibited more modest gains, with RMSE decreasing from 4.71 K to 4.25 K and MAE from 3.78 K to 3.55 K. Overall, the 100 m downscaled estimates fit the station observations more closely, reflecting not only improvements in the overall trend but also an enhanced ability to capture short-term fluctuations in land surface temperature.

Validation results of 10 km and 100 m TPDM products by all sites at a daily scale (a) 10 km; (b) 100 m).

Our study found that the 1 km intermediate product and the final 100 m product exhibit highly consistent scatterplots and time series at the stations. Therefore, the validation results for the 1 km intermediate product were not shown in Figs. 4 and 5. By comparing scatterplots and DOY time series before and after downscaling, it is evident that the original ERA5-Land substantially underestimated LST during spring and winter at certain stations, such as Lazi. After the two-stage resolution enhancement, the TPDM dataset partially mitigated this underestimation, but slight biases remain, reflecting the inherent limitations of the downscaling method in correcting systematic errors.

Figure 7 shows the spatial distribution of RMSE for the stations, with bubble size representing the magnitude of RMSE and color indicating R² values (blue for lower correlation, red for higher correlation). It can be seen that stations in southeastern Tibet, such as Yanyuan and Xianggelila, and stations in northeastern Tibet, including Gonghe, Guinan, and Nuomuhong, exhibit relatively low errors, with RMSE values below 3 K and high correlation with in situ observations (R² up to 0.97). In contrast, stations in southwestern Tibet, such as Lazi, and in northern Tibet, including Wudaoliang and Lenghu, show relatively higher errors, with RMSE exceeding 3.60 K.

Bubble chart of TPDM errors at validation sites.

Analysis of station characteristics shows that low-error stations are generally located in lower-elevation areas, with land cover dominated by cropland or alpine grassland, whereas high-error stations are mostly situated at higher elevations, with land cover consisting of alpine desert or alpine meadow. For example, Yanyuan station, at 2532 m, is located on flat cropland, and Nuomuhong station, at 2798 m, is also on cropland with low local spatial heterogeneity, resulting in substantial accuracy improvements after downscaling. In contrast, Lazi station, at 4418 m, is situated in a high-elevation, complex terrain region, and Lenghu station lies in the alpine desert of the Qaidam Basin, both exhibiting higher local spatial heterogeneity and relatively larger errors. These representative stations indicate that the accuracy of the downscaled results is partly related to land cover and elevation, while overall trends and regional-scale accuracy are markedly improved.

Overall, the TPDM dataset significantly enhances spatial resolution through the two-stage spatiotemporal fusion while improving daily-scale LST accuracy, providing reliable data support for studies over the TP. Quantitative validation across stations indicates that the dataset performs well in capturing regional-scale spatial heterogeneity and short-term temperature fluctuations.

Validation by all sites at a monthly scale

By aggregating daily results to the monthly scale, we obtained monthly mean LST fields. Figure 8 presents the validation results before and after downscaling, which are quantitatively evaluated using RMSE, MAE, and BIAS. The figure shows that both the original and downscaled products exhibit the smallest errors and biases during June-August, i.e., the warm summer period. In contrast, errors and biases are relatively larger in winter, especially from November to the following February. Specifically, during June-August, the mean RMSE of TPDM is 2.54 K, substantially lower than the 3.94 K obtained from the original 10 km DMLST, while the MAE decreases from 3.04 K to 1.99 K and the BIAS from 1.12 K to 0.71 K, indicating particularly pronounced accuracy gains in the high-temperature season. This improvement is mainly attributable to the relatively low cloud cover, higher-quality remote sensing data, and weaker spatial heterogeneity of LST in summer, all of which favor accurate predictions by the spatiotemporal fusion model. In contrast, during December-February, frequent snowfall, low temperatures, and complex surface conditions over the plateau, such as snow cover and active permafrost, yield more complex spatial patterns of LST and limit the accuracy improvements achievable through downscaling. For example, at some stations, the wintertime RMSE of TPDM remains around 4.30 K, with an MAE of about 3.50 K and a BIAS that can fluctuate in sign under the influence of local microclimate conditions. Nevertheless, compared with the original 10 km DMLST, the winter accuracy still improves noticeably, with RMSE and MAE reduced by about 1.20 K and 0.80 K, respectively, and the absolute value of BIAS also decreases.

Validation results of the 10 km and 100 m TPDM products at a monthly scale ((a) 10 km; (b) 100 m).

Further analysis indicates that the limited accuracy improvement in winter is likely due to several factors: first, remote sensing data in winter are strongly affected by cloud cover and snow, making it more difficult to obtain valid observations; second, surface temperatures on the plateau are low during winter, so even minor temperature fluctuations can lead to relatively large errors; third, local microclimatic processes, such as terrain shading and temperature inversion layers, have a more pronounced impact on LST in winter, yet these processes are complicated for models to capture at fine scales accurately.

Validation by land cover types at a daily scale

LST is closely linked to land-cover type, and the scatterplots in Fig. 9, summarizes the 2019 validation results for the three DMLST products (ERA5-Land, Zhan’s product, and TPDM) across different land-cover classes within the TP. The results indicate that TPDM achieves high accuracy across all major land-cover types, suggesting that the product developed in this study provides more accurate and reliable predictions for the dominant surfaces over the TP.

Validation of three DMLST products (a) ERA5-Land; (b) Zhan’s product; (c) TPDM) by land cover types (Grassland: 1; Cropland: 2; Barren: 3) in 2019 at a daily scale.

Among the land-cover classes, the highest accuracy is obtained over cropland (RMSE = 2.21 K), followed by grassland (RMSE = 3.10 K), whereas accuracy is relatively lower over bare land (RMSE = 3.48 K). As shown in Fig. 9, for grassland, cropland, and bare land areas on the TP, the remote-sensing-based DMLST products consistently outperform DMLST derived directly from ERA5-Land, mainly because satellite observations capture finer-scale surface temperature features. In contrast, the coarser reanalysis fields are unable to resolve temperature contrasts among different land-cover types fully. By combining the complementary strengths of ERA5, MODIS, and Landsat, TPDM delivers higher accuracy than single-source reanalysis-only or remote-sensing-only DMLST products under all land-cover conditions. For cropland in particular, TPDM is markedly more accurate than the remote-sensing-only DMLST product because the multi-source fusion leverages the high spatial resolution of Landsat LST in cropland areas together with the atmospheric information embedded in the reanalysis fields, thereby mitigating retrieval errors associated with vegetation cover, irrigation, and other agricultural practices when using a single satellite dataset. In cropland regions, the RMSE of TPDM is about 1.18 K lower than that of the remote-sensing DMLST product, and its BIAS is closer to zero, indicating more accurate LST retrievals for these areas. Over grassland, TPDM likewise performs very well, with its accuracy gains primarily attributable to the improved representation of LST heterogeneity afforded by the multi-source fusion. For bare land, TPDM is slightly more accurate than the remote-sensing product, with an RMSE that is about 0.36 K lower. However, both TPDM and the reanalysis- and remote-sensing-based DMLST products show their lowest accuracy for this land-cover type. This reduced accuracy over bare land is likely related to its low heat capacity and weak thermal inertia, which cause LST to respond strongly to radiative forcing and make it difficult for models and retrieval algorithms to capture its rapid temperature variations. Yet, TPDM still exhibits comparatively better performance through multi-source data fusion and thus provides a more realistic representation of LST conditions over the TP.

Validation by elevation ranges at a daily scale

In addition, Fig. 10 summarize the validation results for several DMLST products across the TP at different elevations, showing that elevation systematically influences LST retrieval accuracy. We grouped the stations into three elevation classes (2000–3000 m, 3000–4000 m, and > 4000 m), and the validation indicates that, except for a slight improvement of the reanalysis product in the 3000–4000 m band relative to 2000–3000 m, all products exhibit decreasing accuracy with increasing elevation. That is, precision is comparatively poorer in high-altitude regions, as exemplified by the TPDM RMSE increasing from 2.86 K to 3.27 K. This pattern is likely related to the thin atmosphere, low water vapour content, harsh natural environment, and complex terrain in high-elevation areas, which jointly increase the uncertainty of LST retrievals.

Validation of three DMLST products (a) ERA5-Land; (b) Zhan’s product; (c) TPDM) by different elevation ranges (2000–3000 m: 1; 3000–4000 m: 2; > 4000 m: 3) in 2019 at a daily scale.

Nevertheless, TPDM consistently outperforms the other products across all elevation bands: in the 2000–3000 m range its RMSE is 2.86 K, clearly lower than 4.86 K for the reanalysis product and 3.61 K for the remote-sensing product; in the 3000–4000 m range its RMSE is 3.12 K, again superior to the other two; and even above 4000 m TPDM maintains a distinct advantage with an RMSE of 3.27 K. Analysis of the BIAS further shows that TPDM exhibits a bias closer to zero, indicating better agreement with in situ observations, higher reliability and a stronger capability to represent the spatial distribution of LST over the TP.

Cross validation

TPDM provides 100 m resolution DMLST data for the year 2019. Most existing products offer limited temporal coverage for 2019 and generally have coarser spatial resolutions, making direct comparisons less consistent or informative. Therefore, Zhan’s 1 km product was selected as the reference for comparison, as it provides the most appropriate combination of temporal alignment and spatial resolution for a robust and meaningful evaluation. To quantitatively compare the accuracy of the DMLST product developed in this study with existing products, we validated the intermediate 1 km TPDM (derived during the construction of the 100 m TPDM) and Zhan’s 1 km product against site observations. We evaluated their station-scale performance using RMSE, MAE, BIAS, and R2.

As shown in Table 3, The validation results show that, at most stations, the 1 km TPDM exhibits lower RMSE and MAE than the existing 1 km product, indicating closer agreement with observations and higher accuracy, particularly pronounced at several sites in the southeastern TP. For example, at Yanyuan station the RMSE and MAE of the 1 km TPDM are 1.86 K and 1.45 K, respectively, compared with 3.82 K and 3.12 K for Zhan’s product, while at Daocheng station the RMSE and R2 of 2.63 K and 0.95 also markedly outperform the 5.99 K and 0.80 of Zhan’s product, demonstrating a clear accuracy advantage of the product developed here. However, at a few stations strongly affected by local microclimate and exhibiting substantial systematic cold biases, such as Lazi and Guide, the 1 km TPDM is slightly less accurate than the existing product, further highlighting the challenges posed by complex local climatic conditions in synthesizing ERA5-Land skin temperature fields. As shown in Fig. 11, when aggregating the validation results across all stations, the 1 km TPDM achieves an RMSE of 3.08 K, an MAE of 2.35 K, a BIAS of −0.77 K, and an R2 of 0.93, outperforming Zhan’s 1 km product on all metrics. Overall, the station-based comparison with Zhan’s 1 km product confirms that the intermediate 1 km TPDM has superior accuracy relative to the existing 1 km DMLST product, thereby laying a solid foundation for generating the 100 m DMLST dataset over the TP.

Spatial detail of the product

Figure 12 shows the spatial distribution of LST over the Qarhan Salt Lake region after TPDM is downscaled from 1 km to 100 m. The 100 m product provides substantially greater spatial detail than the 1 km field. As seen in Fig. 12b, at 1 km resolution, the LST pattern over Qarhan Salt Lake appears as a coarse, blurry block structure, with overly smoothed local thermal contrasts and insufficiently resolved temperature gradients, making it challenging to capture minor temperature differences within the area. When the resolution is increased to 100 m, as shown in Fig. 12d, the spatial detail of the LST field is immensely enriched. The evaporative ponds of the salt works clearly appear as alternating strip-like and patchy features, with temperature contrasts between pond walls and interiors and along the transition zones between the lake surface and adjacent bare land being finely resolved. The spatial pattern of LST is consistent with the actual surface layout shown in Fig. 12c. Small-scale temperature differences among shallow-water zones, salt-pan boundaries, and nearby bare surfaces, which are completely obscured in the 1 km product, are explicitly captured, and the LST gradients become smoother and more physically realistic. The 100 m product thus resolves pronounced surface temperature gradients and rich texture patterns, allowing the surface thermal field to exhibit a more realistic degree of spatial heterogeneity. This marked improvement in spatial detail enables the 100 m TPDM dataset to more faithfully represent the temperature distribution under the TP’s complex surface conditions, effectively reducing mixed-pixel effects and providing richer and more accurate LST information for related studies.

Spatial distribution derived from downscaling 1 km to 100 m at Qarhan Salt Lake area on June 15, 2019 (b: 1 km; d: 100 m).

Figure 13 shows the January-December evolution of LST over the western Nyainqêntanglha glacierized region. The region exhibits a pronounced seasonal cycle in LST, with the lowest values in winter, when the glacier tongue behaves as a persistent cold island, with local minima approaching 235 K. In contrast, in June-August (summer) temperatures increase sharply. LST over bare ground and talus slopes can reach about 295 K. This large seasonal amplitude reflects the strong thermal response of glacier forefields on the TP under a plateau continental climate. The 100 m spatial resolution allows microscale thermal contrasts under complex local topography to be revealed: in summer, sun-facing slopes receive more solar radiation and ridge tops and exposed bedrock surfaces show marked heat accumulation, while valleys and glacier lee slopes act as persistent cold sources, so that illuminated slopes are substantially warmer than shaded ones. In the transition zones between the glacier front and terminal moraines, LST exhibits sharp jumps that reflect substantial differences in energy absorption and transfer between ice surfaces and adjacent bare ground. At the same time, subtle temperature gradients are finely resolved; as illustrated in panel a of Fig. 13, the high-resolution TPDM data effectively capture thermal heterogeneity over glacier surfaces caused by topographic undulations, spatially variable snow depth, and other factors. In contrast, such fine-scale features are often smoothed or blurred in 1 km or coarser products. The 100 m TPDM thus provides the level of detail needed to investigate the energy balance and coupled hydrothermal processes of glacierized regions on the TP.

Monthly-Scale Variation of DMLST in the glacierized areas of the western Nyainqêntanglha mountains, where panels (a) to (l) correspond to January through December.

Taken together, the station-based validation, cross-validation, and accuracy assessments across different land-cover types and elevation ranges show that TPDM maintains a stable, relatively high level of accuracy across a wide range of surface conditions. This indicates that the dataset effectively integrates the strengths of multiple data sources and can more accurately characterize the complex LST patterns over the TP, thereby providing essential support for studies of spatiotemporal LST variability and its climatic responses in the plateau environment.
