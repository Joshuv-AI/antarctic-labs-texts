Identification of the optimal manipulation medium temperature for in vitro handling of oocytes and embryos during in vitro maturation, parthenogenesis, and in vitro culture in pigs
Authors: So Yeon NAM, Su Bin JUNG, Ha Rin NAMKUNG, Ji won HAN, Jeong Min LEE, Young Jae LEE, Jung Im YUN, Eun Song LEE, Jeong Mook LIM, Seung Tae LEE
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Exposure of cumulus–oocyte complexes (COCs), mature oocytes, and preimplantation embryos to subphysiological temperatures during in vitro maturation (IVM), parthenogenesis (PG), and in vitro culture (IVC) has been shown to impair oocyte maturation, embryogenesis, and developmental competence. However, most previous studies have focused primarily on defining optimal embryo culture temperatures. Therefore, the present study aimed to establish optimal temperature conditions for in vitro manipulation, to minimize impairment of oocyte maturation, embryogenesis, and preimplantation development. All in vitro manipulation steps during the IVM, PG, and IVC processes were conducted using manipulation media maintained at defined temperatures (39°C, 38°C, 37°C, 35°C, 34°C, and 33°C) through the combined use of microscope warming plates and infrared irradiation. Oocyte maturation and the preimplantation developmental competence of embryos following parthenogenetic activation were subsequently evaluated. The use of infrared irradiation to control manipulation medium temperature precisely did not adversely affect oocyte maturation or embryonic developmental competence. Among the subphysiological temperature conditions examined (33°C–38°C), maintaining the in vitro manipulation media at 35°C throughout the IVM, PG, and IVC processes resulted in significantly reduced perivitelline space size, increased intra-ooplasmic glutathione levels, improved mitochondrial distribution normality, and enhanced preimplantation development compared with manipulation at 39°C, the physiological body temperature of pigs. Based on these results, we suggest that in vitro manipulation of immature COCs, oocytes, and embryos in pigs should be performed using manipulation media maintained at 35°C.

Assisted reproductive technologies (ART) have become essential tools in reproductive biology and animal biotechnology, with wide-ranging applications in both research and practice. Among these, core technologies such as in vitro maturation (IVM) of oocytes, in vitro fertilization (IVF), and in vitro culture (IVC) are critical for producing high-quality embryos. During these procedures, oocytes and embryos are manipulated outside the body and are highly sensitive to environmental conditions. Among these factors, temperature is a particularly important determinant influencing cell viability and developmental competence [1, 2].

In vivo, folliculogenesis and embryonic development occur optimally at a species-specific physiological temperature, for example, approximately 39°C in pigs. Moreover, the temperature within the ovarian cortex influences not only nuclear [1,2,3,4] and cytoplasmic maturation of oocytes but also cumulus cell expansion [4,5,6], mitochondrial function [5], redox balance [7] and, ultimately, preimplantation embryo development [2]. However, during in vitro manipulation procedures such as micromanipulation, medium exchange, embryo separation, and embryo monitoring, oocytes and embryos are inevitably exposed to transient reductions in temperature below physiological levels. These subphysiological exposures can disrupt intracellular metabolism [8] and structural integrity [8,9,10], leading to reduced developmental competence [1, 9].

To date, most studies have focused on determining the optimal culture temperature for embryos derived from specific species [2]. However, despite the critical importance of optimizing the temperature of in vitro manipulation media used during oocyte and embryo handling in IVM, IVF, parthenogenesis (PG), and IVC to improve the efficiency and quality of in vitro embryo production systems, systematic research on appropriate temperature conditions during these manipulation procedures remains limited.

In this study, pigs, which have significant relevance in biotechnology, medicine, and economics, were used as a model to evaluate systematically the effects of different in vitro manipulation medium temperatures during the manipulation stages (IVM, PG, and IVC) on oocyte maturation and embryonic developmental competence. This study aimed to define optimal medium temperature conditions for the in vitro manipulation of oocytes and embryos in pigs, thereby contributing to improvements in the efficiency and stability of porcine in vitro embryo production systems.

Materials and Methods

Collection of immature cumulus–oocyte complexes (COCs) from medium antral follicles (MAFs)

All animal experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Kangwon National University (approval no. KW-240326-4). Ovaries were obtained from prepubertal domestic pigs slaughtered at the Gangwon Livestock Processing Center (Wonju, Korea). Immediately after collection, ovaries were placed in a thermally insulated container containing sterile saline solution (Dai Han Pharm, Seoul, Korea) maintained at 37°C–38°C and transported to the laboratory within 2 h. Immature COCs were aspirated from MAFs (3–8 mm in diameter) using an 18-gauge needle attached to a disposable syringe (Korea Vaccine, Seoul, Korea). COCs exhibiting homogeneous cytoplasm and surrounded by at least three uniform layers of unexpanded cumulus cells were selected and subsequently washed several times in 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid (HEPES)-buffered Tyrode’s lactate medium (TLH) supplemented with 0.05% (w/v) polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) (TLH-PVA).

IVM

The basic IVM medium was prepared by supplementing Medium 199 (M199; Welgene, Daegu, Korea) with 10% (v/v) porcine follicular fluid, 0.83 mM L-cysteine (Sigma-Aldrich), 0.4 mM sodium pyruvate (Sigma-Aldrich), 75 µg/ml kanamycin (Sigma-Aldrich), 15 ng/ml mouse epidermal growth factor (Sigma-Aldrich), and 1 µg/ml insulin (Sigma-Aldrich). Immature COCs were cultured in four-well culture plates (Nunc, Roskilde, Denmark) containing 500 µl of gonadotropin-supplemented IVM medium, which consisted of the basic IVM medium supplemented with 5 IU/ml human chorionic gonadotropin (Chorulon; Merck, Kenilworth, NJ, USA) and 10 IU/ml pregnant mare serum gonadotropin (Daesung Microbiological Labs, Uiwang, Korea). The COCs were incubated at 39°C under a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2 for 22 h. Subsequently, the cultured COCs were washed three times in gonadotropin-free IVM medium and cultured for an additional 22 h at 39°C under a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2. After completion of maturation, cumulus cells surrounding the in vitro-matured COCs were removed by gentle pipetting in gonadotropin-free IVM medium supplemented with 0.06% (w/v) hyaluronidase (Sigma-Aldrich). Oocytes with a first polar body (MII-stage oocytes) were collected in TLH-PVA or TLH supplemented with 0.57 mM L-cysteine, 7.00 mM taurine (Sigma-Aldrich), 5.00 mM hypotaurine (Sigma-Aldrich), 0.80 mM CaCl2·2H2O (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Osaka, Japan), and 4.0 mg/ml bovine serum albumin (BSA; Sigma-Aldrich) (TLH-Ca2+) and subsequently allocated to the following experiments.

Analysis of cumulus cell expansion and perivitelline space (PVS) size

After IVM, cumulus cell expansion was scored as follows: 0, no response; 1, minimal observable response characterized by rounding and glistening of the outermost cumulus cell layer; 2, expansion of the outer cumulus cell layer; 3, expansion of all cumulus cell layers except the corona radiata; and 4, expansion of all cumulus cell layers, including the corona radiata. In addition, photomicrographs of cumulus cell–free MII-stage oocytes were captured using a digital camera (DS-Ri1; Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) mounted on an inverted microscope (TE300; Nikon). Image analysis was performed using ImageJ software, version 1.52 (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). The diameters of the oocytes and the inner boundary of the zona pellucida were determined by averaging the maximum vertical and horizontal measurements. The size of the PVS was calculated by subtracting the oocyte diameter from the inner diameter of the zona pellucida.

Measurement of glutathione (GSH) and reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels

Intracellular GSH and ROS levels in cumulus cell–free MII-stage oocytes were measured according to previously described protocols [11, 12]. For GSH measurement, oocytes were incubated with 10 µM CellTracker™ Blue CMF2HC (4-chloromethyl-6,8-difluoro-7-hydroxycoumarin; Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) diluted in TLH-PVA medium for 30 min on a warming plate (MATS-USP; Tokai Hit, Shizuoka, Japan) maintained at 39°C. The oocytes were then transferred to PZM-3 medium and incubated for an additional 30 min at 39°C under a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2. For ROS measurement, oocytes were stained with 10 µM H2DCFDA (2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate; Invitrogen), diluted in TLH-PVA medium, for 30 min on a warming plate maintained at 39°C. After staining, the oocytes were washed in Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS; Welgene) supplemented with 0.1% (w/v) PVA. Fluorescence signals were visualized using an epifluorescence microscope (TE300; Nikon), and fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software, version 1.52.

Measurement of mitochondrial activity and distribution

To assess intra-ooplasmic mitochondrial activity, cumulus-free MII-stage oocytes were incubated for 30 min at 39°C in gonadotropin-free IVM medium containing MitoTracker™ Red CMXRos (Thermo Fisher) diluted 1:50, allowing staining of both active and inactive mitochondria. The oocytes were then fixed in 4% (v/v) paraformaldehyde (Biosesang, Gyeonggi, Korea) at room temperature (RT) for 30 min, permeabilized with 0.5% (v/v) Triton X-100 (Biopure, Burlington, Canada) at RT for 30 min, and blocked with 3% (w/v) bovine serum albumin (Sigma-Aldrich) in DPBS containing 0.1% (w/v) PVA at RT for 1 h. The oocytes were then incubated overnight at 4°C with a mouse IgG anti-TOM20 primary antibody (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA) diluted 1:100 to detect active mitochondria. After three washes with DPBS containing 0.1% (w/v) PVA, the oocytes were incubated with an Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA) (1:100) at 39°C for 1 h. To examine mitochondrial distribution, cumulus-free MII-stage oocytes were incubated with MitoTracker™ Red CMXRos (1:50) in IVM medium at 39°C for 30 min, fixed in 1% (v/v) paraformaldehyde at 4°C overnight, mounted on glass slides, and observed using an epifluorescence microscope. Fluorescence intensity was analyzed using ImageJ software (version 1.52).

Parthenogenetic activation (PA) and preimplantation development

MII-stage oocytes were placed in a fusion chamber overlaid with 1 ml of activation medium consisting of 280 mM D-mannitol (Sigma-Aldrich) supplemented with 0.05 mM magnesium chloride hexahydrate and 0.1 mM calcium chloride dihydrate. Electrical activation was induced by applying two direct-current pulses of 120 V/mm for 60 µsec using a cell fusion generator (LF101; Nepa Gene, Chiba, Japan). For chemical activation, electrically activated oocytes were cultured for 4 h in four-well culture dishes containing 500 µl PZM-3 medium supplemented with 5 µg/ml cytochalasin B (Sigma-Aldrich) at 39°C under a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2. Parthenogenetic embryos were then cultured for 7 days in four-well culture dishes containing 500 µl PZM-3 medium at 39°C under a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2, 5% O2, and 90% N2. Preimplantation development to the two-cell or blastocyst stage was monitored at 48 and 168 h after completion of PA using a stereomicroscope (SZX16; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). Blastocysts were then stained with 5 µg/ml bisbenzimide H33342 (Sigma-Aldrich) for 15 min, and the total cell number per blastocyst was determined by counting stained nuclei under an epifluorescence microscope (Eclipse TE300; Nikon).

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total mRNA was isolated from blastocysts using the Dynabeads™ mRNA DIRECT™ Purification Kit (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Complementary DNA was synthesized using the ReverTra Ace™ qPCR RT Master Mix with gDNA Remover Kit (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using Prime Q-Mastermix (GeNet Bio, Daejeon, Korea) on a qTOWER3 real-time PCR thermal cycler (Analytik Jena, Jena, Germany). PCR amplification consisted of an initial denaturation at 94°C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation (94°C, 30 sec), annealing (60°C, 45 sec), and extension (72°C, 30 sec). Amplification specificity was confirmed by melting curve analysis. Relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method, where Ct represents the threshold cycle, ΔCt = Ct (target gene) − Ct (internal reference; β-actin), and ΔΔCt = ΔCt (treatment group) − ΔCt (control group). Primer sequences were designed using Primer3Plus software based on porcine complementary DNA sequences obtained from GenBank. Primer information is provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Experimental design

As shown in Supplementary Fig. 1, all in vitro procedures including maturation of immature COCs derived from MAFs, parthenogenetic embryo production, and culture of parthenogenetic embryos were conducted using stage-specific in vitro manipulation media maintained at defined temperatures.

In Experiment 1, a protocol was established to maintain stage-specific in vitro manipulation media at specific temperatures outside the incubator. In vitro manipulation media (TLH-PVA, M199, TLH-Ca2+, activation medium, and PZM-3) were prewarmed to 39°C under a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 and placed on a microscope warming plate set to 39°C, 37°C, or 35°C, either with or without infrared (IR) irradiation. IR irradiation was applied using an infrared lamp (Cat. No. BR125 Red 1CT/10; Philips, Amsterdam, Netherlands) with an emission wavelength range of 700–3000 nm, positioned approximately 30 cm above the microscope stage. After 25 min, media temperatures were measured using a noncontact IR thermometer (HS-33CT; Hansung, Gyeonggi, Korea).

In Experiment 2, to exclude potential effects of IR irradiation on oocytes and embryos, its influence during IVM, PG, and IVC was evaluated. For eliminate temperature-related influences, all invitro manipulation media (TLH-PVA, M199, TLH-Ca2+, activation medium, and PZM-3) were prewarmed for 25 min to achieve a final temperature of 37°C under two distinct settings: (i) 39°C microscope warming plates without IR irradiation (herein as referred to as ‘Without IR irradiation’) and (ii) 36°C microscope warming plates with IR irradiation (herein as referred to as ‘With IR irradiation’). Subsequently, all invitro manipulation procedures were performed under strictly controlled and equivalent thermal conditions. During IVM, selection of immature COCs and transfer into gonadotropin-free IVM medium were conducted outside the incubator for 25 min and 3 min, respectively, under either Without IR irradiation or With IR irradiation conditions. Nuclear maturation was assessed by evaluating extrusion of the first polar body into the PVS. During PG, selection of MII-stage oocytes and electrical PA were conducted outside the incubator for 17 min and 8 min, respectively, under the same conditions. Cleavage was assessed 48 h after PG. During IVC, embryo washing and monitoring of two-cell embryos were conducted outside the incubator for 3 min each, under the same conditions, and blastocyst formation was evaluated after 120 h. Finally, all steps performed outside the incubator during IVM, PG, and IVC were conducted under either Without IR irradiation or With IR irradiation conditions, followed by blastocyst evaluation and total cell number analysis.

In Experiment 3, the effects of different in vitro manipulation medium temperatures during IVM on oocyte maturation were examined. Selection of immature COCs, transfer into gonadotropin-free IVM medium, and collection of MII-stage oocytes were performed outside the incubator using TLH-PVA (25 min), M199 (3 min), or TLH-Ca2+ medium (25 min). Media were maintained at 39°C, 38°C, 37°C, 35°C, 34°C, or 33°C. Oocyte maturation was evaluated based on cumulus cell expansion, PVS size, first polar body extrusion, intra-ooplasmic GSH and ROS levels, and mitochondrial activity and distribution. All analyses were conducted using oocytes derived from three independent biological replicates, and the number of oocytes analyzed is specified in the corresponding figures.

In Experiment 4, the effects of temperature differences in in vitro manipulation media during IVM, PG, and IVC on preimplantation development were investigated. All procedures performed outside the incubator including COC selection, transfer, MII oocyte collection, PA, embryo washing, and cleavage monitoring were conducted using defined media and durations. Preimplantation development and HSP70 transcript levels in blastocysts were analyzed and compared among groups.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SAS software version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). Treatment effects were evaluated using a general linear model (PROC GLM) within the SAS program. After analysis of variance, least squares means or Duncan’s multiple range test were used to determine significant differences among groups. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Results

Experiment 1: Establishment of a protocol to maintain stage-specific in vitro manipulation media at defined temperatures during in vitro procedures

To maintain in vitro manipulation media at specific target temperatures, media (TLH-PVA, M199, TLH-Ca2+, activation medium, and PZM-3) pre-equilibrated in a 39°C incubator were placed on microscope warming plates set at 39°C, 37°C, or 35°C for 25 min, either with or without IR irradiation. As shown in Supplementary Table 1, all media placed on warming plates set at 39°C were maintained within a temperature range of 37.2°C–37.9°C without IR irradiation and 39.2°C–39.9°C with IR irradiation. For this study, temperatures within these ranges were designated as 37°C and 39°C, respectively. Media placed on warming plates set at 37°C were maintained between 35.0°C and 35.8°C without IR irradiation and between 38.0°C and 38.7°C with IR irradiation, which were designated as 35°C and 38°C, respectively. Finally, media placed on warming plates set at 35°C were maintained between 33.1°C and 33.8°C without IR irradiation and between 34.0°C and 34.8°C with IR irradiation, designated as 33°C and 34°C, respectively. Based on these results, a protocol was established to maintain stage-specific in vitro manipulation media at defined temperatures during experimental procedures.

Discussion

In this study, we identified an optimal temperature for in vitro manipulation media used in porcine ART, with the aim of minimizing thermal shock–induced damage to oocytes and embryos. Based on the facts that temperatures within reproductive tissues (e.g., ovarian stroma, follicular fluid, and oviduct) are slightly lower than core body temperature [14,15,16,17,18], the temperatures of the in vitro manipulation media were set at 39°C, 38°C, 37°C, 35°C, 34°C, and 33°C using microscope warming plates and/or IR irradiation, the latter of which was validated to exert no physiological effects on oocytes and embryos. Although the internal body temperature of the female reproductive organs in pigs is approximately 39°C, in vitro manipulation media maintained at 39°C resulted in the most severe thermal shock-induced damage to oocytes and embryos during ART procedures. By contrast, in vitro manipulation media maintained at 35°C were associated with the least thermal shock–related damage. Based on these results, we identified 35°C as the optimal temperature for the in vitro manipulation of porcine oocytes and embryos during ART. These findings highlight the importance of optimizing species-specific in vitro manipulation temperatures independently of internal body temperature, to improve the efficiency of producing developmentally competent oocytes and embryos through ART procedures.

As shown in Fig. 1, IR irradiation for up to 25 min had no detectable effects on oocyte maturation, embryogenesis, or preimplantation development. However, previous studies have demonstrated that prolonged or high-intensity IR exposure can exert both beneficial and detrimental effects on cellular physiology. Reported beneficial effects include increased adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production via stimulation of mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase [19], reduction of oxidative stress through induction of antioxidant responses [20], enhanced cell viability [21, 22], and decreased cellular damage [23, 24]. By contrast, adverse effects such as thermal damage [25, 26], photoaging [27], and DNA damage induced by elevated intracellular ROS [28] have also been reported. The absence of observable effects in the present study may be attributed to the IR irradiation being insufficient in duration or intensity to induce physiological alterations at the embryonic level. These findings suggest that if IR irradiation is applied during ART, exposure durations of up to 25 min may be considered safe and feasible.

Numerous studies have demonstrated that abnormal enlargement of the PVS is associated with reduced oocyte quality, resulting from cytoskeletal abnormalities [29, 30], meiotic defects [30, 31], disruption of intracellular homeostasis [30], and an imbalance between cytoplasmic and nuclear maturation [32, 33]. As shown in Fig. 2B, manipulation of COCs during IVM in media maintained at 39°C the physiological body temperature of pigs led to significantly greater PVS expansion in matured oocytes compared with manipulation in media maintained at lower temperatures. A similar phenomenon has been reported in cattle [25]. Although the physiological body temperature in cattle ranges from 38.0°C to 39.3°C, oocytes cultured under sub-39°C conditions exhibited reduced PVS size. These observations may be attributed to heat stress–induced cytoskeletal abnormalities, consistent with previous findings showing that oocytes exposed to heat stress display disrupted cytoskeletal organization [5]. Therefore, manipulating oocytes at slightly lower-than-physiological temperatures during the IVM process may help minimize cytoskeletal abnormalities and prevent abnormal PVS expansion. Collectively, these results suggest that in vitro manipulation temperatures below 39°C provide a more favorable environment for maintaining intra-ooplasmic cytoskeletal integrity in pigs.

As shown in Fig. 3B, intra-ooplasmic ROS levels were not affected by changes in in vitro manipulation temperature. By contrast, a significant increase in GSH levels was observed only when manipulation was performed at 35°C (Fig. 3A). These findings indicate that cytoplasmic GSH synthesis in oocytes is modulated by extracellular temperature. This temperature dependency likely reflects the thermo sensitivity of key enzymes involved in GSH biosynthesis, including γ-glutamylcysteine ligase and glutathione synthetase [34, 35], whose enzymatic activity and structural stability vary in response to temperature changes [36]. In addition, modest temperature reduction may suppress excessive ROS generation [37], thereby reducing GSH consumption and stabilizing redox homeostasis [38]. Furthermore, because cellular metabolic activity and intercellular transport processes are inherently temperature-sensitive, temperature modulation may indirectly influence cumulus–oocyte metabolic cooperation [39, 40], which plays a critical role in regulating cysteine availability for GSH synthesis [39, 41]. Together, these mechanisms provide a coherent explanation for the selective increase in GSH observed at 35°C and suggest that this temperature creates a more favorable biochemical environment for oocyte quality and developmental competence.

Minor temperature fluctuations were observed during invitro manipulation; however, these variations fall within the physiological range of temperature differences reported in vivo [42,43,44]. In mammals, body temperature is not maintained at a fixed value but fluctuates within a narrow physiological range depending on physiological conditions. Previous studies have also demonstrated that temperatures within reproductive tissues vary within a defined range [45], indicating that the reproductive microenvironment is not strictly constant but exhibits limited variability. Therefore, the temperature fluctuations observed in this study are unlikely to have significantly affected the experimental outcomes.

In this study, target temperatures during in vitro manipulation were maintained either using a microscope warming plate alone or in combination with IR irradiation. The 37°C, 35°C, and 33°C conditions were achieved using only the warming plate, whereas the 39°C, 38°C, and 34°C conditions required concurrent IR irradiation. As shown in Fig. 3C, manipulation temperature did not significantly affect mitochondrial activity. However, Fig. 3D demonstrates that oocytes manipulated at 37°C, 35°C, and 33°C using the warming plate alone exhibited a significantly higher proportion of MII-stage oocytes with normal mitochondrial distribution than did those manipulated at 39°C, 38°C, and 34°C under IR irradiation. These results suggest that IR irradiation affected intracellular spatial organization rather than mitochondrial function. IR exposure during manipulation may induce localized and transient micro-thermal changes within the oocyte, which could influence the stability or dynamics of cytoskeletal structures involved in mitochondrial positioning. This is supported by previous reports indicating that mitochondrial positioning is sensitive to temperature fluctuations [5, 46] and that mitochondrial transport and anchoring are regulated by cytoskeletal components such as microtubules and actin filaments [47,48,49]. Accordingly, IR irradiation appears to have selectively impaired the maintenance of normal mitochondrial spatial distribution in the oocyte cytoplasm without markedly altering overall mitochondrial physiological function.

Although the physiological body temperature of approximately 39°C in pigs has generally been assumed to be optimal for in vitro manipulation, the present study demonstrates that lower temperature conditions are associated with improved embryo development. These findings indicate that temperature acts as a critical regulatory factor influencing the structural stability and functional integrity of intracellular components. Previous studies have reported that mammalian preovulatory follicles maintain temperatures up to 2.5°C lower than surrounding ovarian tissues and core body temperature [14,15,16], and such localized cooling has been proposed as an essential physiological requirement for proper follicular maturation and ovulation [17, 18]. Therefore, simply replicating physiological body temperature in vitro does not necessarily provide optimal culture conditions. Instead, precise control of absolute temperature levels and minimization of subtle thermal fluctuations throughout the culture process may be more critical for preserving oocyte quality and enhancing developmental competence.

In the in vivo environment, oocytes and embryos are tightly regulated not only by temperature but also by multiple protective mechanisms, including dynamic buffering systems, molecular chaperones, antioxidant defenses, and continuous fluid exchange [50,51,52]. In contrast, these regulatory components are largely absent under in vitro conditions, resulting in fundamental differences even when nominal temperatures are equivalent. Moreover, during microscopic manipulation, transient and localized temperature fluctuations may occur due to exposure outside the incubator, increasing the susceptibility of oocytes to thermal stress even within the physiological range. Under these conditions, exposure to 39°C during in vitro manipulation may represent a relatively elevated temperature compared to the follicular microenvironment, potentially inducing stress responses such as widening of PVS. This finding should not be interpreted as evidence of intrinsic toxicity at 39°C, but rather as a limitation of current in vitro systems in fully recapitulating the stability and protective capacity of the in vivo environment. Accordingly, a moderately reduced temperature, such as 35°C, may mitigate cumulative thermal stress during repeated manipulation and contribute to preserving oocyte structural integrity.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that maintaining in vitro manipulation temperatures below the porcine physiological body temperature of 39°C, particularly at 35°C, throughout invitro oocyte maturation and parthenogenetic embryo culture contributes to improved maintenance of oocyte quality and enhanced embryonic developmental competence in parthenogenesis. These findings underscore the necessity of precise temperature control throughout all stages of in vitro manipulation, including IVM, PG, and IVC, rather than merely replicating physiological temperature conditions. Overall, this temperature-optimization strategy has the potential to improve the efficacy of porcine parthenogenesis and enhance the reproducibility and reliability of in vitro embryology research focus on parthenogenetic systems. Furthermore, additional studies under IVF conditions are required to validate the broader applicability of these findings.
