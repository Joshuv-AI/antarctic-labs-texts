Solving Inverse Problems via Diffusion-Based Priors: An Approximation-Free Ensemble Sampling Approach
Haoxuan Chen, Yinuo Ren, Martin Renqiang Min, Lexing Ying, Zachary Izzo

Abstract
Diffusion models (DMs) have proven to be effective in modeling high-dimensional distributions, leading to their widespread adoption for representing complex priors in Bayesian inverse problems (BIPs). However, current DM-based posterior sampling methods proposed for solving common BIPs rely on heuristic approximations to the generative process. To exploit the generative capability of DMs and avoid the usage of such approximations, we propose an ensemble-based algorithm that performs posterior sampling without the use of heuristic approximations. Our algorithm is motivated by existing works that combine DM-based methods with the sequential Monte Carlo (SMC) method. By examining how the prior evolves through the diffusion process encoded by the pre-trained score function, we derive a modified partial differential equation (PDE) governing the evolution of the corresponding posterior distribution. This PDE includes a modified diffusion term and a reweighting term, which can be simulated via stochastic weighted particle methods. Theoretically, we prove that the error between the true posterior distribution can be bounded in terms of the training error of the pre-trained score function and the number of particles in the ensemble. Empirically, we validate our algorithm on several inverse problems in imaging to show that our method gives more accurate reconstructions compared to existing DM-based methods.

Preliminaries

In this section, we provide a quick overview of problem setup, basic concepts, and existing work related to solving Bayesian inverse problems (BIPs) with diffusion models.

2.1
Basics of Inverse Problems

In BIPs, we aim to recover a ground truth parameter
{\bm{x}}
from measurements
{\bm{y}}
. The relationship between
{\bm{x}}
and
{\bm{y}}
is described by:

{\bm{y}}={\mathcal{A}}({\bm{x}})+{\bm{n}},

(2.1)

where
{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
{\bm{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
{\mathcal{A}}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a differentiable forward operator (linear or nonlinear), and
{\bm{n}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
represents measurement noise. Under the Bayesian framework, the posterior distribution we seek to sample from is:

p({\bm{x}}|{\bm{y}})\propto p_{0}({\bm{x}})p({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})=p_{0}({\bm{x}})\exp(-\mu_{{\bm{y}}}({\bm{x}})),

(2.2)

where
p_{0}({\bm{x}})
denotes the prior distribution and
\mu_{{\bm{y}}}({\bm{x}})=-\log p({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})
is the negative log-likelihood function for a fixed observation
{\bm{y}}
.

Many practical inverse problems are ill-posed due to measurement noise and non-injective forward models, making unique solutions impossible to obtain. Traditional optimization-based methods often fail to capture the complex solution landscape, motivating the use of Bayesian formulations where posterior sampling methods can systematically account for uncertainty and explore multiple plausible solutions. For a comprehensive treatment of BIPs, we refer readers to
[
62
]
.

Deep generative models have emerged as powerful prior distributions that can capture complex solution spaces while remaining computationally tractable. Unlike traditional priors that rely on structural assumptions, these models effectively represent high-dimensional and multi-modal distributions given sufficient training data. In this work, we focus on diffusion models (DMs), which represent the current state-of-the-art in generative modeling with successful applications across physics

Theoretical Analysis

In this section, we present our theoretical results of the ensemble-based posterior samplers introduced in Section
3
. Our analysis is conducted in continuous time, based on the weighted particle dynamics (

) and (

). The impact of numerical discretization, as implemented in Algorithm

and
\sigma(t)=t

Assumption 4.1

(Regularity of the log-likelihood)
.

The log-likelihood function
\mu_{{\bm{y}}}
is twice differentiable and lower bounded by some constant
C^{(1)}_{{\bm{y}}}
depending only on the observation
{\bm{y}}

Assumption 4.2

(Bounded second moment)
.

The prior distribution
p_{0}
satisfies a second-moment bound:
\mathbb{E}{p_{0}}\left[\|{\bm{x}}\|_{2}^{2}\right]\leq m_{2}^{2}
.

Assumption 4.3

(Score matching error)
.

The neural network estimator
{\bm{\phi}}_{\theta}({\bm{x}},t)
approximates the score function
\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\log{\mathchoice{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\reflectbox{$\displaystyle\vec{}\mkern 4.0mu$}\cr\kern-4.30554pt\cr$\displaystyle p$\cr}}}{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\reflectbox{$\textstyle\vec{}\mkern 4.0mu$}\cr\kern-4.30554pt\cr$\textstyle p$\cr}}}{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\reflectbox{$\scriptstyle\vec{}\mkern 4.0mu$}\cr\kern-3.01389pt\cr$\scriptstyle p$\cr}}}{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\reflectbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\vec{}\mkern 4.0mu$}\cr\kern-2.15277pt\cr$\scriptscriptstyle p$\cr}}}}_{t}({\bm{x}})
with uniformly bounded error across
t\in[0,T]
:

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\|{\bm{\phi}}_{\theta}({\bm{x}},t)-\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\log{\mathchoice{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\reflectbox{$\displaystyle\vec{}\mkern 4.0mu$}\cr\kern-4.30554pt\cr$\displaystyle p$\cr}}}{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\reflectbox{$\textstyle\vec{}\mkern 4.0mu$}\cr\kern-4.30554pt\cr$\textstyle p$\cr}}}{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\reflectbox{$\scriptstyle\vec{}\mkern 4.0mu$}\cr\kern-3.01389pt\cr$\scriptstyle p$\cr}}}{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\reflectbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\vec{}\mkern 4.0mu$}\cr\kern-2.15277pt\cr$\scriptscriptstyle p$\cr}}}}_{t}({\bm{x}})\right\|_{2}^{2}{\mathchoice{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\reflectbox{$\displaystyle\vec{}\mkern 4.0mu$}\cr\kern-4.30554pt\cr$\displaystyle p$\cr}}}{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\reflectbox{$\textstyle\vec{}\mkern 4.0mu$}\cr\kern-4.30554pt\cr$\textstyle p$\cr}}}{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\reflectbox{$\scriptstyle\vec{}\mkern 4.0mu$}\cr\kern-3.01389pt\cr$\scriptstyle p$\cr}}}{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\reflectbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\vec{}\mkern 4.0mu$}\cr\kern-2.15277pt\cr$\scriptscriptstyle p$\cr}}}}_{t}({\bm{x}}){\mathrm{d}}{\bm{x}}\leq\epsilon_{{\bm{s}}}^{2}.

Assumption
4.1
ensures the absence of singularities in the log-likelihood
\mu_{{\bm{y}}}
, which is a condition adopted in existing work on BIPs

and satisfied by common noise models such as Gaussian and Poisson (with

and

are aligned with recent theoretical frameworks for diffusion models

quantifies the approximation error due to neural network training and reflects the quality of the pre-trained score function.

We now present our first main result, which quantifies the discrepancy between the true posterior
q_{{\bm{y}},0}
and the distribution
\widehat{q}_{{\bm{y}},T}
obtained by evolving our derived PDE dynamics (

Theorem 4.1

(Error Bound for Posterior Estimation)
.

Under Assumptions
4.1
,
4.2
, and
4.3
, the total variation (TV) distance between the approximate and true posterior satisfies:

\mathrm{TV}(\widehat{q}_{{\bm{y}},T},q_{{\bm{y}},0})\leq C^{(2)}_{{\bm{y}}}\sqrt{\frac{m_{2}^{2}}{T^{2}}+T^{2}\epsilon_{{\bm{s}}}^{2}}

where
q_{{\bm{y}},0}({\bm{x}})\propto p_{0}({\bm{x}})\exp(-\mu_{{\bm{y}}}({\bm{x}}))
is the exact posterior, and
\widehat{q}_{{\bm{y}},t}
is the solution to the posterior evolution (
3.4
). The constant
C^{(2)}_{{\bm{y}}}
depends only on the observation
{\bm{y}}
. Optimizing the right-hand side yields the asymptotic bound
\mathrm{TV}(\widehat{q}_{{\bm{y}},T},q_{{\bm{y}},0})\lesssim\sqrt{\epsilon_{\bm{s}}}
when
T\asymp\sqrt{\epsilon_{\bm{s}}^{-1}}
.

A detailed proof of Theorem
4.1
can be found in Section
C.1
. It essentially combines techniques from the theory of diffusion models
[
115
,
78
]
and the well-posedness theory of Bayesian inverse problems, which is closely related to
[

. The result of Theorem
4.1
reveals a trade-off controlled by the time horizon
T
between the prior mismatch and score approximation error.

Next, we study the particle approximation to the PDE solution (
3.4

3.6

Assumption 4.4

(Boundedness and Lipschitz continuity of
I
)
.

Define the function

I({\bm{x}},t):=\|\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\mu_{{\bm{y}}}({\bm{x}})\|_{2}^{2}-\Delta_{{\bm{x}}}\mu_{{\bm{y}}}({\bm{x}})-2{\bm{\phi}}_{\theta}({\bm{x}},t)^{\intercal}\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\mu_{{\bm{y}}}({\bm{x}}).

is uniformly bounded and Lipschitz continuous over

\max\{\|I\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,T])},\mathrm{Lip}(I)\}\leq B_{{\bm{y}}}
,
for some constant
B_{{\bm{y}}}
depending only on
{\bm{y}}
.

Assumption
4.4
ensures stability of the evolving particle weights, preventing degeneracy or explosion over time. In practice, this condition is enforced via the resampling step in Algorithm

[

119

120

Theorem 4.2

(Convergence in the Many-Particle Limit)
.

Under Assumptions
4.1
–
4.4

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{W}}^{2}_{2}(\gamma^{N}_{t},\gamma_{t})]=0,

where
\gamma_{t}

governed by (

6
Discussion and Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced a new method for solving Bayesian inverse problems using diffusion models as the prior. Our method derives a novel PDE that exactly characterizes the exact posterior dynamics under an evolving diffusion prior, avoiding the heuristic approximations employed by previous methods and leading to better SMC-type algorithms in practice.
Theoretically, we provide the error bounds of the posterior sampling algorithm in terms of the score function error, and justify the convergence of the ensemble method in the many-particle limit. Empirically, our method outperforms state-of-the-art diffusion-based solvers across a range of computational imaging tasks.

This work opens several promising directions for future research.
Our method applies to other inverse problems arising in various fields with twice-differentiable log-likelihoods, including optics, medical imaging, video analytics, geoscience, astronomy, fluid dynamics, chemistry and biology

Lemma B.1
.

The PDE dynamics governing the evolution of the unnormalized posterior distribution
\widehat{Q}_{{\bm{y}}}({\bm{x}},t):\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
is given by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\widehat{Q}_{{\bm{y}}}=

\displaystyle-\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\cdot\left(\left(\widehat{{\bm{H}}}({\bm{x}},t)-V(t)^{2}\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\mu_{{\bm{y}}}\right)\widehat{Q}_{{\bm{y}}}\right)+\frac{1}{2}V(t)^{2}\Delta_{x}\widehat{Q}_{{\bm{y}}}

(B.1)

\displaystyle+\left(\frac{1}{2}V(t)^{2}\left(\|\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\mu_{{\bm{y}}}\|_{2}^{2}-\Delta_{{\bm{x}}}\mu_{{\bm{y}}}\right)-\widehat{{\bm{H}}}({\bm{x}},t)^{\intercal}\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\mu_{{\bm{y}}}\right)\widehat{Q}_{{\bm{y}}},

where

\widehat{{\bm{H}}}({\bm{x}},t):=-F(t){\bm{x}}+\frac{G(t)^{2}+V(t)^{2}}{2}{\bm{\phi}}_{\theta}({\bm{x}},t)

(B.2)

denotes the original drift in the prior diffusion.

Proof.

We begin by rewriting the PDE dynamics that need simplification:

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\widehat{Q}_{{\bm{y}}}=-\frac{1}{f_{{\bm{y}}}}\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\cdot\left(\widehat{{\bm{H}}}({\bm{x}},t)\widehat{Q}_{{\bm{y}}}f_{{\bm{y}}}\right)+\frac{1}{2f_{{\bm{y}}}}V(t)^{2}\Delta_{x}(\widehat{Q}_{{\bm{y}}}f_{{\bm{y}}}).

(B.3)

Let
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
denote the two terms on the right-hand side:

I_{1}:=-\frac{1}{f_{{\bm{y}}}}\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\cdot\left(\widehat{{\bm{H}}}({\bm{x}},t)\widehat{Q}_{{\bm{y}}}f_{{\bm{y}}}\right),\ I_{2}:=\frac{1}{2f_{{\bm{y}}}}V(t)^{2}\Delta_{x}(\widehat{Q}_{{\bm{y}}}f_{{\bm{y}}}).

(B.4)

Note that
\widehat{{\bm{H}}}({\bm{x}},t):\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
is vector-valued, while both
\widehat{Q}_{{\bm{y}}}:\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
f_{{\bm{y}}}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are scalar-valued. A direct computation shows that the first term
I_{1}
simplifies to:

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{f_{{\bm{y}}}}\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\cdot\left(\widehat{{\bm{H}}}({\bm{x}},t)\widehat{Q}_{{\bm{y}}}f_{{\bm{y}}}\right)

(B.5)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{f_{{\bm{y}}}}\left(\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\cdot\left(\widehat{{\bm{H}}}({\bm{x}},t)\right)\widehat{Q}_{{\bm{y}}}f_{{\bm{y}}}+\widehat{{\bm{H}}}({\bm{x}},t)^{\intercal}\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\left(\widehat{Q}_{{\bm{y}}}f_{{\bm{y}}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\cdot\left(\widehat{{\bm{H}}}({\bm{x}},t)\right)\widehat{Q}_{{\bm{y}}}-\frac{1}{f_{{\bm{y}}}}\widehat{{\bm{H}}}({\bm{x}},t)^{\intercal}\left(\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\widehat{Q}_{{\bm{y}}}f_{{\bm{y}}}+\widehat{Q}_{{\bm{y}}}\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}f_{{\bm{y}}}\right)

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\cdot\left(\widehat{{\bm{H}}}({\bm{x}},t)\right)\widehat{Q}_{{\bm{y}}}-\widehat{{\bm{H}}}({\bm{x}},t)^{\intercal}\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\widehat{Q}_{{\bm{y}}}-\left(\widehat{{\bm{H}}}({\bm{x}},t)^{\intercal}\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\mu_{{\bm{y}}}\right)\widehat{Q}_{{\bm{y}}},

where the last equality above follows from the fact that
\frac{1}{f_{{\bm{y}}}}\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}f_{{\bm{y}}}=\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\mu_{{\bm{y}}}
for
f_{{\bm{y}}}=\exp(\mu_{{\bm{y}}})
.

Similarly, expanding the Laplacian term
\Delta_{{\bm{x}}}(\widehat{Q}_{{\bm{y}}}f_{{\bm{y}}})
allows us to simplify the second term
I_{2}

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2f_{{\bm{y}}}}V(t)^{2}\left(\left(\Delta_{{\bm{x}}}\widehat{Q}_{{\bm{y}}}\right)f_{{\bm{y}}}+2\left(\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\widehat{Q}_{{\bm{y}}}\right)^{\intercal}\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}f_{{\bm{y}}}+\widehat{Q}_{{\bm{y}}}\left(\Delta_{{\bm{x}}}f_{{\bm{y}}}\right)\right)

(B.6)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}V(t)^{2}\Delta_{{\bm{x}}}\widehat{Q}_{{\bm{y}}}+V(t)^{2}\left(\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\widehat{Q}_{{\bm{y}}}\right)^{\intercal}\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\mu_{{\bm{y}}}+\frac{1}{2}V(t)^{2}\left(\Delta_{{\bm{x}}}\mu_{{\bm{y}}}+\|\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\mu_{{\bm{y}}}\|_{2}^{2}\right)\widehat{Q}_{{\bm{y}}},

where the last equality above follows from the fact that
\frac{1}{f_{{\bm{y}}}}\Delta_{{\bm{x}}}f_{{\bm{y}}}=\Delta_{{\bm{x}}}\mu_{{\bm{y}}}+\|\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\mu_{{\bm{y}}}\|_{2}^{2}
for
f_{{\bm{y}}}=\exp(\mu_{{\bm{y}}})
.

Summing the two expressions in (
B.5
) and (
B.6
) then yields

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\widehat{Q}_{{\bm{y}}}

\displaystyle=I_{1}+I_{2}=-\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\cdot\left(\widehat{{\bm{H}}}({\bm{x}},t)\right)\widehat{Q}_{{\bm{y}}}+\left(V(t)^{2}\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\mu_{{\bm{y}}}-\widehat{{\bm{H}}}({\bm{x}},t)\right)^{\intercal}\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\widehat{Q}_{{\bm{y}}}

(B.7)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}V(t)^{2}\Delta_{{\bm{x}}}\widehat{Q}_{{\bm{y}}}+\left(\frac{1}{2}V(t)^{2}\left(\Delta_{{\bm{x}}}\mu_{{\bm{y}}}+\|\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\mu_{{\bm{y}}}\|_{2}^{2}\right)-\widehat{{\bm{H}}}({\bm{x}},t)^{\intercal}\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\mu_{{\bm{y}}}\right)\widehat{Q}_{{\bm{y}}}

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\cdot\left(\left(\widehat{{\bm{H}}}({\bm{x}},t)-V(t)^{2}\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\mu_{{\bm{y}}}\right)\widehat{Q}_{{\bm{y}}}\right)-V(t)^{2}\Delta_{{\bm{x}}}\mu_{{\bm{y}}}\widehat{Q}_{{\bm{y}}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}V(t)^{2}\Delta_{{\bm{x}}}\widehat{Q}_{{\bm{y}}}+\left(\frac{1}{2}V(t)^{2}\left(\|\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\mu_{{\bm{y}}}\|_{2}^{2}+\Delta_{{\bm{x}}}\mu_{{\bm{y}}}\right)-\widehat{{\bm{H}}}({\bm{x}},t)^{\intercal}\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\mu_{{\bm{y}}}\right)\widehat{Q}_{{\bm{y}}}

which is exactly the dynamics given in (
B.1
), as desired.
∎

Lemma B.2
.

Consider the following PDE dynamics governing the evolution of some unnormalized density
\widehat{Q}({\bm{x}},t):\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\widehat{Q}({\bm{x}},t)=-\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\cdot\left(K({\bm{x}},t)\widehat{Q}({\bm{x}},t)\right)+\zeta(t)\Delta_{{\bm{x}}}\widehat{Q}({\bm{x}},t)+J({\bm{x}},t)\widehat{Q}({\bm{x}},t),

(B.8)

where
\zeta:[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
K,J:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Then we consider the normalized density
\widehat{q}({\bm{x}},t):\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,T]\rightarrow[0,1]
defined as below

\widehat{q}({\bm{x}},t):=\frac{\widehat{Q}({\bm{x}},t)}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\widehat{Q}({\bm{x}},t){\mathrm{d}}{\bm{x}}},\ t\in[0,T].

(B.9)

The PDE dynamics governing the evolution of the normalized density
\widehat{q}({\bm{x}},t)
is then given by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\widehat{q}({\bm{x}},t)

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\cdot\left(K({\bm{x}},t)\widehat{q}({\bm{x}},t)\right)+\zeta(t)\Delta_{{\bm{x}}}\widehat{q}({\bm{x}},t)

(B.10)

\displaystyle+\left(J({\bm{x}},t)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}J({\bm{x}},t)\widehat{q}({\bm{x}},t){\mathrm{d}}{\bm{x}}\right)\widehat{q}({\bm{x}},t).

Proof.

By using
Z(t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\widehat{Q}({\bm{x}},t){\mathrm{d}}{\bm{x}}
to denote the normalizing constant for any
t\in[0,T]
, we can then compute the time derivative of
Z(t)
by plugging in (
B.8
) as follows

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}Z(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\widehat{Q}({\bm{x}},t){\mathrm{d}}{\bm{x}}\right)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\widehat{Q}({\bm{x}},t)\right){\mathrm{d}}{\bm{x}}

(B.11)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(-\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\cdot\left(K({\bm{x}},t)\widehat{Q}({\bm{x}},t)\right)+\zeta(t)\Delta_{{\bm{x}}}\widehat{Q}({\bm{x}},t)+J({\bm{x}},t)\widehat{Q}({\bm{x}},t)\right){\mathrm{d}}{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}J({\bm{x}},t)\widehat{Q}({\bm{x}},t){\mathrm{d}}{\bm{x}}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\cdot\left(\zeta(t)\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\widehat{Q}({\bm{x}},t)-K({\bm{x}},t)\widehat{Q}({\bm{x}},t)\right){\mathrm{d}}{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}J({\bm{x}},t)\widehat{Q}({\bm{x}},t){\mathrm{d}}{\bm{x}}.

Furthermore, we may rewrite the normalized density as
\widehat{q}({\bm{x}},t)=\frac{1}{Z(t)}\widehat{Q}({\bm{x}},t)
and differentiate the expression with respect to
t
, which yields

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\widehat{q}({\bm{x}},t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z(t)^{2}}\left(\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\widehat{Q}({\bm{x}},t)\right)Z(t)-\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}Z(t)\right)\widehat{Q}({\bm{x}},t)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z(t)}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\widehat{Q}({\bm{x}},t)\right)-\frac{1}{Z(t)}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}Z(t)\right)\left(\frac{1}{Z(t)}\widehat{Q}({\bm{x}},t)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z(t)}\left(-\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\cdot\left(K({\bm{x}},t)\widehat{Q}({\bm{x}},t)\right)+\zeta(t)\Delta_{{\bm{x}}}\widehat{Q}({\bm{x}},t)+J({\bm{x}},t)\widehat{Q}({\bm{x}},t)\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{Z(t)}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}J({\bm{x}},t)\widehat{Q}({\bm{x}},t){\mathrm{d}}{\bm{x}}\right)\widehat{q}({\bm{x}},t)

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\cdot\left(K({\bm{x}},t)\widehat{q}({\bm{x}},t)\right)+\zeta(t)\Delta_{{\bm{x}}}\widehat{q}({\bm{x}},t)+\left(J({\bm{x}},t)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}J({\bm{x}},t)\widehat{q}({\bm{x}},t){\mathrm{d}}{\bm{x}}\right)\widehat{q}({\bm{x}},t).

where the second last equality above follows from (
B.8
) and (
B.11
) the last equality is deduced from the definition of the normalized density
\widehat{q}({\bm{x}},t)
. This concludes our proof.
∎

Lemma B.4

Consider a single particle

Definition B.5

(Semigroup Operator)
.

For a single particle
({\bm{x}}_{t},\beta_{t})
with initial condition
({\bm{x}}^{\ast},\beta^{\ast})
, and any suitable test function
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, the semigroup operator
{\mathcal{T}}^{({\bm{x}},\beta)}_{t}
is defined as:

{\mathcal{T}}^{({\bm{x}},\beta)}_{t}\phi({\bm{x}}^{\ast},\beta^{\ast}):=\mathbb{E}\left[\phi({\bm{x}}_{t},\beta_{t})\ |\ ({\bm{x}}_{0},\beta_{0})=({\bm{x}}^{\ast},\beta^{\ast})\right].

(B.14)

Definition B.6

(Infinitesimal Generator)
.

Let
{\mathbb{I}}
be the identity operator. The infinitesimal generator
\mathcal{L}^{({\bm{x}},\beta)}
associated with the semigroup
{\mathcal{T}}^{({\bm{x}},\beta)}_{t}
is defined for any suitable test function
\phi
as:

\mathcal{L}^{({\bm{x}},\beta)}\phi\left({\bm{x}}^{\ast},\beta^{\ast}\right):=\lim_{\Delta t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{1}{\Delta t}\left({\mathcal{T}}^{({\bm{x}},\beta)}_{\Delta t}\phi({\bm{x}}^{\ast},\beta^{\ast})-\phi({\bm{x}}^{\ast},\beta^{\ast})\right).

(B.15)

For any test function
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and input
({\bm{x}}^{\ast},\beta)
, the following communtativity holds:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{T}}^{({\bm{x}},\beta)}_{t_{2}}\circ{\mathcal{T}}^{({\bm{x}},\beta)}_{t_{1}}\phi({\bm{x}}^{\ast},\beta^{\ast})

\displaystyle={\mathcal{T}}^{({\bm{x}},\beta)}_{t_{1}}\circ{\mathcal{T}}^{({\bm{x}},\beta)}_{t_{2}}\phi({\bm{x}}^{\ast},\beta^{\ast})

(B.16)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\phi({\bm{x}}_{t_{1}+t_{2}},\beta_{t_{1}+t_{2}})\ |\ ({\bm{x}}_{0},\beta_{0})=({\bm{x}}^{\ast},\beta^{\ast})\right],

demonstrating that
{\mathcal{T}}^{({\bm{x}},\beta)}_{t_{1}}\circ{\mathcal{T}}^{({\bm{x}},\beta)}_{t_{2}}={\mathcal{T}}^{({\bm{x}},\beta)}_{t_{2}}\circ{\mathcal{T}}^{({\bm{x}},\beta)}_{t_{1}}
for any times
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
.

Combining (
B.16
) with the definition of the infinitesimal generator in (
B.15
), we can show that for any input
({\bm{x}}^{\ast},\beta)
and test function
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\mathcal{T}}^{({\bm{x}},\beta)}_{t}\circ\mathcal{L}^{({\bm{x}},\beta)}\phi\left({\bm{x}}^{\ast},\beta^{\ast}\right)={\mathcal{T}}^{({\bm{x}},\beta)}_{t}\lim_{\Delta t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{1}{\Delta t}\left({\mathcal{T}}^{({\bm{x}},\beta)}_{\Delta t}-{\mathbb{I}}\right)\phi({\bm{x}}^{\ast},\beta^{\ast})

(B.17)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{\Delta t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{1}{\Delta t}{\mathcal{T}}^{({\bm{x}},\beta)}_{t}\left({\mathcal{T}}^{({\bm{x}},\beta)}_{\Delta t}-{\mathbb{I}}\right)\phi({\bm{x}}^{\ast},\beta^{\ast})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{\Delta t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{1}{\Delta t}\left({\mathcal{T}}^{({\bm{x}},\beta)}_{\Delta t}-{\mathbb{I}}\right){\mathcal{T}}^{({\bm{x}},\beta)}_{t}\phi({\bm{x}}^{\ast},\beta^{\ast})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{({\bm{x}},\beta)}\circ{\mathcal{T}}^{({\bm{x}},\beta)}_{t}\phi\left({\bm{x}}^{\ast},\beta^{\ast}\right),

i.e.
, the semigroup
{\mathcal{T}}^{({\bm{x}},\beta)}_{t}
also commutes with the infinitesimal generator

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x_{i}},{\bm{K}}_{t,i}\gamma_{t}\right\rangle+\left\langle\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial\beta},b_{t}\gamma_{t}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}V(t)^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\frac{\partial^{2}\varphi}{\partial x_{i}^{2}},\gamma_{t}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\left\langle\varphi,\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\left({\bm{K}}_{t,i}\gamma_{t}\right)+\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\left(b_{t}\gamma_{t}\right)+\frac{1}{2}V(t)^{2}\Delta_{{\bm{x}}}\gamma_{t}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle\varphi,-\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\cdot\left({\bm{K}}_{t}\gamma_{t}\right)-\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\left(b_{t}\gamma_{t}\right)+\frac{1}{2}V(t)^{2}\Delta_{{\bm{x}}}\gamma_{t}\right\rangle,

where the second last equality above follows from integration by parts.

Substituting the last expression in (
B.24
) above into (
B.20
) then gives us the weak form of the PDE associated with the joint distribution
\gamma_{t}
in (
B.18
).

Proof of Lemma
B.4
.

By defining

to be the weighted projection of
\gamma_{t}
, we then have that

.
Below we proceed to derive the PDE govering the evolution of
\gamma^{{\bm{P}}}_{t}
based on (
B.18
).

For any test function

, taking

in the weak form derived in (

) and (

) yields

\displaystyle\left\langle\psi,\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\gamma^{{\bm{P}}}_{t}\right\rangle=\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\left\langle\psi,\gamma^{{\bm{P}}}_{t}\right\rangle=\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\psi({\bm{x}})\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\beta\gamma_{t}({\bm{x}},\beta){\mathrm{d}}\beta\right){\mathrm{d}}{\bm{x}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\left\langle\varphi,\gamma_{t}\right\rangle=\left\langle\varphi,\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\gamma_{t}\right\rangle=\left\langle\varphi,-\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\cdot\left({\bm{K}}_{t}\gamma_{t}\right)-\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\left(b_{t}\gamma_{t}\right)+\frac{1}{2}V(t)^{2}\Delta_{{\bm{x}}}\gamma_{t}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\left\langle\varphi,\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\cdot\left({\bm{K}}_{t}\gamma_{t}\right)\right\rangle-\left\langle\varphi,\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\left(b_{t}\gamma_{t}\right)\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}V(t)^{2}\left\langle\varphi,\Delta_{{\bm{x}}}\gamma_{t}\right\rangle.

), we can further simplify them as follows

\displaystyle\left\langle\varphi,\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\cdot\left({\bm{K}}_{t}\gamma_{t}\right)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\beta\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\psi({\bm{x}})\left(\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\cdot\left({\bm{K}}_{t}({\bm{x}})\gamma_{t}({\bm{x}},\beta)\right)\right){\mathrm{d}}{\bm{x}}\right){\mathrm{d}}\beta

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\psi({\bm{x}})\left(\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\cdot\left({\bm{K}}_{t}({\bm{x}})\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\beta\gamma_{t}({\bm{x}},\beta){\mathrm{d}}\beta\right)\right)\right){\mathrm{d}}{\bm{x}}

and

\displaystyle\left\langle\varphi,\Delta_{{\bm{x}}}\gamma_{t}\right\rangle

(B.28)

to denote the integrand in the definition of

as follows

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\psi({\bm{x}})\gamma_{t}({\bm{x}},\beta)\left({\bm{J}}_{t}({\bm{x}})-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\bm{J}}_{t}({\bm{x}}^{\ast})\gamma^{{\bm{P}}}_{t}({\bm{x}}^{\ast}){\mathrm{d}}{\bm{x}}^{\ast}\right)\beta{\mathrm{d}}{\bm{x}}{\mathrm{d}}\beta

t\in[0,T]

D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho_{T}\|\widetilde{\rho}_{T})\leq D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho_{0}\|\widetilde{\rho}_{0})+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{1}{2c(t)^{2}}\left\|{\bm{b}}({\bm{x}},t)-\widetilde{{\bm{b}}}({\bm{x}},t)\right\|_{2}^{2}\rho_{t}({\bm{x}}){\mathrm{d}}{\bm{x}}{\mathrm{d}}t.

(C.2)

Proof.

We remark that the proof of this lemma is essentially the same as the derivations in many previous works on the theoretical analysis of DMs and variants. Examples include, but are not limited to,
[
115
, Lemma C.1]
,
[
18
, Lemma 2.22]
, and
[
36
, Lemma A.4]
. For the sake of completeness, we include a detailed derivation here.

The main idea is to use the Fokker-Planck equations associated with the diffusion processes in (
C.1
) and differentiate the KL divergence between the two evolving densities with respect to time. Specifically, we have that
\rho_{t}
and
\widetilde{\rho}_{t}
satisfy the following Fokker-Planck equations:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\rho_{t}

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\cdot\left({\bm{b}}({\bm{x}},t)\rho_{t}\right)+\frac{1}{2}c(t)^{2}\Delta_{{\bm{x}}}\rho_{t},

(C.3)

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\widetilde{\rho}_{t}

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\cdot\left(\widetilde{{\bm{b}}}({\bm{x}},t)\widetilde{\rho}_{t}\right)+\frac{1}{2}c(t)^{2}\Delta_{{\bm{x}}}\widetilde{\rho}_{t}.

From the definition of the KL divergence

D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho_{t}\|\widetilde{\rho}_{t})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\log\frac{\rho_{t}({\bm{x}})}{\widetilde{\rho}_{t}({\bm{x}})}\rho_{t}({\bm{x}}){\mathrm{d}}{\bm{x}},

we can differentiate it with respect to the time variable
t
, which yields

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho_{t}\|\widetilde{\rho}_{t})=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\log\frac{\rho_{t}}{\widetilde{\rho}_{t}}\frac{\partial\rho_{t}}{\partial t}{\mathrm{d}}{\bm{x}}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\log\rho_{t}-\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\log\widetilde{\rho}_{t}\right)\rho_{t}{\mathrm{d}}{\bm{x}}

(C.4)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\log\frac{\rho_{t}}{\widetilde{\rho}_{t}}\frac{\partial\rho_{t}}{\partial t}{\mathrm{d}}{\bm{x}}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\frac{1}{\rho_{t}}\frac{\partial\rho_{t}}{\partial t}-\frac{1}{\widetilde{\rho}_{t}}\frac{\partial\widetilde{\rho}_{t}}{\partial t}\right)\rho_{t}{\mathrm{d}}{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\log\frac{\rho_{t}}{\widetilde{\rho}_{t}}\frac{\partial\rho_{t}}{\partial t}{\mathrm{d}}{\bm{x}}-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{\rho_{t}}{\widetilde{\rho}_{t}}\frac{\partial\widetilde{\rho}_{t}}{\partial t}{\mathrm{d}}{\bm{x}}

For the first term in (
C.4
) above, we plug in (
C.3
) and use integration by parts, which yields

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\log\frac{\rho_{t}}{\widetilde{\rho}_{t}}\frac{\partial\rho_{t}}{\partial t}{\mathrm{d}}{\bm{x}}

(C.5)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\log\rho_{t}-\log\widetilde{\rho}_{t}\right)\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\cdot\left(\left({\bm{b}}-\frac{c(t)^{2}}{2}\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\log\rho_{t}\right)\rho_{t}\right){\mathrm{d}}{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\log\rho_{t}-\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\log\widetilde{\rho}_{t}\right)^{\intercal}\left({\bm{b}}-\frac{c(t)^{2}}{2}\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\log\rho_{t}\right)\rho_{t}{\mathrm{d}}{\bm{x}}.

To simplify the second term in (
C.4
), we plug in (
C.3
) apply integration by parts again to obtain that

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{\rho_{t}}{\widetilde{\rho}_{t}}\frac{\partial\widetilde{\rho}_{t}}{\partial t}{\mathrm{d}}{\bm{x}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{\rho_{t}}{\widetilde{\rho}_{t}}\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\cdot\left(\left(\widetilde{{\bm{b}}}-\frac{c(t)^{2}}{2}\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\log\widetilde{\rho}_{t}\right)\widetilde{\rho}_{t}\right){\mathrm{d}}{\bm{x}}

(C.6)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left[\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\cdot\left(\widetilde{{\bm{b}}}-\frac{c(t)^{2}}{2}\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\log\widetilde{\rho}_{t}\right)\rho_{t}+\left(\widetilde{{\bm{b}}}-\frac{c(t)^{2}}{2}\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\log\widetilde{\rho}_{t}\right)^{\intercal}\frac{\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\widetilde{\rho}_{t}}{\widetilde{\rho}_{t}}\rho_{t}\right]{\mathrm{d}}{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\widetilde{{\bm{b}}}-\frac{c(t)^{2}}{2}\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\log\widetilde{\rho}_{t}\right)^{\intercal}\left(\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\log\widetilde{\rho}_{t}-\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\log\rho_{t}\right)\rho_{t}{\mathrm{d}}{\bm{x}}

Furthermore, substituting and into then yields

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho_{t}\|\widetilde{\rho}_{t})=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\log\rho_{t}-\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\log\widetilde{\rho}_{t}\right)^{\intercal}\left({\bm{b}}-\frac{c(t)^{2}}{2}\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\log\rho_{t}\right)\rho_{t}{\mathrm{d}}{\bm{x}}

(C.7)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\log\widetilde{\rho}_{t}-\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\log\rho_{t}\right)^{\intercal}\left(\widetilde{{\bm{b}}}-\frac{c(t)^{2}}{2}\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\log\widetilde{\rho}_{t}\right)\rho_{t}{\mathrm{d}}{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{c(t)^{2}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\|\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\log\widetilde{\rho}_{t}-\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\log\rho_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}\rho_{t}{\mathrm{d}}{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left({\bm{b}}-\widetilde{{\bm{b}}}\right)^{\intercal}\left(\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\log\rho_{t}-\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\log\widetilde{\rho}_{t}\right)\rho_{t}{\mathrm{d}}{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2c(t)^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\|{\bm{b}}-\widetilde{{\bm{b}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\rho_{t}{\mathrm{d}}{\bm{x}}=\frac{1}{2c(t)^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\|{\bm{b}}({\bm{x}},t)-\widetilde{{\bm{b}}}({\bm{x}},t)\right\|_{2}^{2}\rho_{t}({\bm{x}}){\mathrm{d}}{\bm{x}}

where the last inequality follows from the AM-GM inequality,
i.e.

{\bm{x}}^{\intercal}{\bm{y}}\leq\frac{1}{2c(t)^{2}}\|{\bm{x}}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{c(t)^{2}}{2}\|{\bm{y}}\|_{2}^{2}
for any vectors
{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
t\in[0,T]
.

Integrating (
C.7
) from
t=0
to
t=T
then yields (
C.2
), which concludes our proof.
∎

Inverse problem setups

Below we provide a discussion on the mathematical formulations of the four inverse problems we tested on here.

Super-resolution

The forward model in (
2.1
) associated with the super-resolution problem we test on here can be written as

{\bm{y}}=P_{f}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{n}}

where
P_{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{\frac{n}{f}\times n}
implements a block averaging filter that downscales each image by a factor of
f
and
{\bm{n}}\sim{\mathcal{N}}({\bm{0}},0.2{\bm{I}}_{\frac{n}{f}})
. Using similar setups as many previous work
[
29
,
43
,
36
]
on solving inverse problems via diffusion models, here we pick
f=4
.

Gaussian and motion deblurring

The forward model associated with any deblurring problem can be summarized as

{\bm{y}}=B_{k}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{n}}

where
{\bm{n}}\sim{\mathcal{N}}({\bm{0}},0.2{\bm{I}}_{n})
and
B_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is a circulant matrix that realizes a convolution with the kernel
k
under circular boundary condition. Again, we adopt the same settings used in most previous work
[
29
,
43
,
36
]
.

Specifically, for the Gaussian deblurring problem, the convolutional kernel
k
is fixed to be a Gaussian kernel of standard deviation
3.0
and size
61\times 61
. For the motion deblurring problem, the kernel
k
is randomly generated via code used in previous work
[
43
,
36
]
, where the size is chosen to be
61\times 61
and the intensity is set to be
0.5
. In order to ensure a fair comparison, we use the same motion kernel
k
for each image across different methods.

Box inpainting

The forward model for the box inpainting problem is given by

{\bm{y}}=D{\bm{x}}+{\bm{n}}

where
{\bm{n}}\sim{\mathcal{N}}({\bm{0}},0.2{\bm{I}}_{n})
and
D
is a diagonal matrix with either
0
or
1
on its diagonal. In particular, here we choose

such that a centered square patch of size

(i.e., the side length is a quarter of the original image’s side length) is masked out.

Appendix E
Additional Experimental Results and Discussions

In this section, we provide additional experimental results and detailed qualitative comparisons between our proposed methods and existing baselines.

Summary.

Across the diverse inverse problems evaluated on FFHQ-256 and ImageNet-256 (detailed in Table
1
and Table
2
), the AFDPS framework consistently delivers strong results. The AFDPS-SDE variant, in particular, frequently distinguishes itself by producing visually compelling outcomes, excelling in the generation of sharp details and fine textures that contribute to high perceptual quality. This is evident in Figures
3
-
6
, where AFDPS-SDE’s reconstructions often appear more intricate and realistic. The AFDPS-ODE variant also provides coherent results, which are typically characterized by a notable smoothness. For tasks where capturing the utmost detail and textural accuracy is paramount, AFDPS-SDE often provides a particularly effective solution, frequently leading in or strongly competing for the best perceptual metrics (LPIPS).

Gaussian Deblurring.

In Gaussian deblurring, AFDPS-SDE showcases its ability to produce perceptually rich outputs, achieving the best LPIPS on ImageNet-256 (0.3925) and a competitive LPIPS on FFHQ-256 (0.2580). Figure
3
highlights SDE’s strength in rendering sharp, defined textures like the dog’s fur (ImageNet, row 2). Concurrently, AFDPS-ODE achieves high PSNR on both datasets and the best LPIPS on FFHQ-256 (24.98 PSNR, 0.2560 LPIPS), delivering notably clean and smooth outputs, for example, on the baby’s facial skin (FFHQ, row 2).

Motion Deblurring.

For motion deblurring, AFDPS-SDE demonstrates strong perceptual quality, securing the best LPIPS score (0.2869) on FFHQ-256, while PF-SMC-DM leads in PSNR. Figure
4
emphasizes SDE’s proficiency in transforming blurred images into sharp, detailed reconstructions, meticulously recovering fine details like individual hair strands in FFHQ portraits (e.g., row 5). AFDPS-ODE also effectively removes blur, yielding coherent results, typically with a characteristically smoother finish.

Super-Resolution.

AFDPS-SDE stands out in super-resolution, achieving the best PSNR and LPIPS scores on both FFHQ-256 (22.96 PSNR, 0.3063 LPIPS) and ImageNet-256 (20.97 PSNR, 0.4643 LPIPS). Figure
5
compellingly shows SDE generating sharp, highly detailed images from severely degraded inputs, adeptly reconstructing fine facial features (FFHQ, row 2 and 5) and intricate object textures like butterfly patterns (ImageNet, row 2). AFDPS-ODE also provides coherent upscaled outputs, especially for FFHQ dataset, reaffirming the metrics in the tables.

Box Inpainting.

In box inpainting combined with denoising, AFDPS-SDE shows robust performance, securing the highest PSNR on ImageNet-256 (23.15). Figure
6
highlights SDE’s ability to generate detailed and realistically textured inpainted regions, such as the intricate dog fur (ImageNet, row 1) or sharp keyboard key structures (ImageNet, row 4). AFDPS-ODE also performs strongly, achieving best LPIPS on both datasets (FFHQ: 0.1969, ImageNet: 0.2716) and best PSNR on FFHQ (25.73), producing notably smooth and coherent fills, like seamless facial features (FFHQ, row 1).
