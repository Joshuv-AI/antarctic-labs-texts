A narrative review of behavioral tests across mammalian models in neuroscience
Authors: Huiling Huang, Hongping Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMammalian models are widely employed in the research of human diseases. Behavioral tests in animals is a critical evaluative approach in scientific research, offering insights into complex disease pathophysiology and facilitating the assessment of novel therapeutic interventions. Currently, behavioral testing paradigms are extensively applied in mammalian research, particularly in rodent models. Compared to rodents, the brains of large mammals exhibit closer anatomical and biochemical homology to the human brain, thereby endowing them with significant value in neuroscience. Numerous neurological disorders have been successfully used large mammals as models, with the widespread application of behavioral testing methods for these species. This review summarizes established behavioral testing methodologies developed for both small and large mammalian species, and discusses their applications, efficacy, and limitations.Graphical Abstract Keywords: Behavioral phenotyping, Translational neuroscience, Mammal model

Introduction

Mammalian models have been extensively utilized in biomedical research owing to their physiological and structural homology with humans. In particular, primates—with their extended lifespans and complex behavioral repertoires—constitute an ideal model system for studying human diseases [1]. The advancement of biomedical science, coupled with the growing application of mammalian models in medical research, has propelled significant technological progress in animal behavioral experimentation. Through observational and experimental techniques, researchers collect, data analysis, which is then extrapolated to humans to elucidate underlying physiological and pathological mechanisms. For instance, behavioral assays can detect alterations in behavior under various pathological states, thereby offering valuable insights for early disease diagnosis and therapeutic development. These approaches not only advance our understanding of disease pathophysiology but also generate critical data to inform the development of personalized treatment strategies. This is especially critical in the field of neuroscience [2].

Initially, animal behavioral experimental methods were primarily applied to the study of learning and memory, and have since expanded to encompass other domains including emotion, motor function, social behavior, nociception, and addiction. Learning is a neurobiological process through which the nervous system acquires and encodes new information or behaviors in response to environmental changes. Memory involves the storage and subsequent retrieval of information or behavioral patterns acquired via learning, typically comprising three phases: encoding (acquisition), consolidation, and retrieval. Learning and memory are therefore intrinsically linked neurobiological functions [3]. Currently, major behavioral paradigms primarily assess learning and memory, depression-like and anxiety-like behaviors, fear conditioning, spontaneous locomotor activity, circadian rhythms, aggression, defensive and reproductive behaviors, as well as social behaviors. Within this spectrum, social behavior paradigms specifically encompass communicative, prosocial, and dominance hierarchy behaviors. Among these, learning and memory, affective behaviors, and locomotor activity represent the most extensively researched domains [4].

However, how to select a appropriate mammalian model and corresponding behavioral assays is a significant challenge. Researchers need to take three critical considerations into account: conclude translational relevance (phylogenetic proximity and spontaneous vs. induced pathologies), practical feasibility (costs, husbandry, and species-specific toolkit availability), and ethical responsibility [5, 6]. Different animal species occupy distinct niches along this translational spectrum, offering complementary strengths and inherent limitations. This review adopts a comparative perspective, evaluating these trade-offs across model systems, from high-throughput rodent screens to cognitively complex primate studies, to provide a strategic guide for researchers aiming to align their behavioral phenotyping approaches with specific, translationally meaningful neuropsychiatric and neurodegenerative research questions.

By encompassing disease modeling and current technological advances, this narrative review aims to serve as a valuable reference for studies integrating behavioral experiments with translational biomedical research. Specifically, this review sets out to: (1) outline and compare the established behavioral testing methodologies developed for major mammalian orders, from Rodentia to Primates. (2) critically evaluate the applications and inherent limitations of these paradigms across different species in modeling human neurological and psychiatric disorders. (3) discuss current research directions and technological prospects for enhancing the translational value of behavioral phenotyping. To achieve these aims, the article is organized as follows: following this introduction, we sequentially review the behavioral assays, applications, and limitations in rodents, tree shrews (Scandentia), artiodactyls (pigs), carnivores (dogs), and non-human primates. We conclude by synthesizing the prospects and challenges of these methods in the context of personalized medicine (Fig. 1).

Application and limitations of rodent behavioral assays in medical research

While over 99% of mouse genes have identifiable human homologs, this high genetic homology does not equate to functional equivalence in terms of neural circuitry, development, or behavior. Collectively, their compact size, ease of handling, and high experimental throughput establish rodents as a premier model system [7]. Consequently, the repertoire of behavioral testing techniques developed for rodents is both more mature and more diverse than that available for other mammalian species. This repertoire encompasses widely used paradigms such as affective behavioral tests, various maze-based assays for memories and motor behavioral assays [4].

Affective behaviors in animals are primarily categorized into three domains: depression-like, anxiety-like, and fear-related phenotypes. Given the inability of animals to verbally report internal states, behavioral assays represent the principal methodology for evaluating affective states. Depression-like behavior is characterized by a constellation of behavioral phenotypes—including anhedonia, behavioral despair, and learned helplessness—which typically emerge following prolonged exposure to uncontrollable or chronic stressors. Common behavioral assays used to quantify depression-like phenotypes include the learned helplessness paradigm, forced swim test (FST), tail suspension test (TST), sucrose preference test (SPT). The learned helplessness paradigm involves exposing subjects to a series of inescapable and unpredictable aversive stimuli, most commonly foot shocks. Subsequently, when placed in a context where escape is possible, subjects frequently fail to initiate escape responses. This passive coping strategy, termed behavioral despair, is interpreted as an analog of depressive-like states [8]. In the Tail Suspension Test (TST), the subject is suspended by its tail, with the nose positioned approximately 20–25 cm above the base of the apparatus. Behavioral responses are recorded using a high-resolution video camera, typically over a standardized 6-min trial [9]. In the Forced Swim Test (FST), subjects are placed in a confined water-filled chamber from which escape is not possible, thereby inducing a swimming response. Owing to their innate aversion to water, rodents initially display vigorous, escape-directed behaviors. Over time, subjects typically transition to a state of behavioral despair, characterized by passive floating or prolonged immobility. The TST and FST are based on a common principle: both paradigms place the subject in an inescapable and aversive situation, ultimately eliciting a state of passive immobility that serves as a model for depressive-like states [9]. The Sucrose Preference Test (SPT) capitalizes on rodents’ innate preference for sweet-tasting solutions. Following a period of fasting or chronic stress, rodents typically display a diminished preference for sucrose solutions. This reduction models anhedonia—a core symptom of depression characterized by the loss of pleasure or interest. Anxiety-like behavior in rodents is conceptualized as arising from a conflict between the innate drive to explore novel environments and the concurrent fear response elicited by such novelty. Behavioral assays for assessing anxiety-like states are broadly classified into two categories: unconditioned (innate) and conditioned (learned) paradigms. Representative unconditioned paradigms include the elevated plus maze (EPM), light-dark box (LDB) test, and novelty-suppressed feeding (NSF) test. Conditioned paradigms primarily involve conflict-based procedures, such as the Vogel conflict test and the Geller-Seifter conflict test [10]. Compared to non-stressed controls, animals subjected to chronic stress exhibit reduced time spent in and fewer entries into the open arms of the maze, reflecting an anxiety-like state. Similar behavioral outcomes, indicative of increased anxiety, have been reported using the open field and light-dark box tests, as these paradigms operate on analogous ethological principles [11].

It is crucial to acknowledge that the interpretation of “anxiety-like behavior” in these paradigms is not always straightforward and is subject to significant caveats regarding construct validity. Construct validity refers to the extent to which a test measures the theoretical construct it claims to measure—in this case, anxiety. A primary criticism is that these tests often conflate general states of fear, neophobia, risk assessment, locomotor activity, and even exploratory drive. For instance, reduced open-arm exploration in the EPM or center avoidance in the OFT [12], while commonly interpreted as heightened anxiety, could alternatively reflect altered exploration, novelty-seeking, or baseline activity levels unrelated to an emotional state of anxiety. This ambiguity is compounded by the fact that different anxiety tests (e.g., EPM vs. NSF) may not correlate well with each other, suggesting they tap into overlapping but distinct neurobiological and behavioral dimensions [13, 14].

Furthermore, the term “anxiety-like behavior” is frequently over-interpreted as a direct analog to human anxiety disorders. While these tests are invaluable for screening anxiolytic drug efficacy or probing neural circuits involved in threat response, they model specific behavioral endpoints rather than the complex, subjective experience of clinical anxiety. Therefore, researchers must exercise caution in extrapolating findings from a single behavioral paradigm to a broad construct of “anxiety.” A more rigorous approach involves using a battery of complementary tests and emphasizing that these paradigms measure behavioral indices with ethological relevance to anxiety rather than anxiety perse [15]. This critical perspective is essential for accurate data interpretation and for guiding the development of more refined, translationally relevant models.

Moreover, the marble-burying test has gained prominence as a paradigm for assessing anxiety- and compulsion-like behaviors, though its interpretation requires careful consideration. The test capitalizes on rodents’ natural digging and burying instincts when presented with novel, aversive objects (marbles). In a typical protocol, mice are exposed to marbles in a novel environment, and the extent of burying is quantified. Stress-exposed mice that exhibit anxiety-like phenotypes not only bury more marbles but also display increased digging duration and reduced feeding frequency, particularly in the peripheral zones of the cage [16]. The marble-burying test has also been adapted to model obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD)-like behaviors, where repetitive burying is viewed as a compulsive act [11, 16]. The primary challenge with this paradigm is its ambiguous construct validity. Increased marble burying is frequently interpreted as an index of heightened anxiety or neophobia. However, the same behavior can be driven by increased digging motivation, repetitive/compulsive tendencies, or heightened general activity, complicating direct attribution to a specific emotional state. The test’s validity can be compromised if not properly controlled. Factors such as baseline digging propensity (strain differences), marble size/texture, bedding depth, and prior habituation can significantly influence results, leading to false positives or negatives [17, 18]. It is strongly recommended that marble burying not be employed as a standalone behavioral measure. Instead, it could be better incorporated into a comprehensive behavioral test battery [19, 20]. For instance, marble burying test can be combined with other anxiety-specific assays such as the elevated plus maze (EPM), light–dark box (LDB), and open field test (OFT) to dissociate general anxiety from compulsive digging [21, 22]. Meanwhile, incorporating the assessments of other compulsive-like behaviors, including grooming analysis, schedule-induced polydipsia, nestlet shredding tests can further strengthen the identification of compulsive-like phenotypes [18, 23]. Advanced analyses, such as quantifying behavioral microstructure and visualizing spatial activity patterns via trajectory heatmaps, provide deeper insights into behavioral organization that cannot be captured by simple marble-burying counts alone. As depicted in Fig. 2, these heatmaps reveal distinct genotypic differences in the spatial distribution of burying events, demonstrating that quantifying such complex behavioral parameters enables the identification of unique features underlying marble-burying behavior [16]. However, these sophisticated measures still require the complementary contextual framework provided by additional behavioral assays to achieve meaningful interpretation.

Maze-based behavioral paradigms evaluate an animal’s capacity to orient and navigate in a novel environment using either allocentric cues or egocentric strategies, and to remember previously visited locations across short temporal intervals, reflecting working memory, or longer intervals, reflecting reference memory, which is primarily employed in models of neurodegenerative diseases, such as Alzheimer’s disease [24]. The Y-maze test is used to measure the short-term spatial memory of animals as a type of recognition, which uses a Y-shaped chamber [25, 26]. This test is based on the rodents’ intrinsic curiosity to explore new environments while there are no positive or negative stimuli in the maze [27, 28]. And other tests include the Morris water maze (MWM) and The Radial Arm Water Maze (RAWM). The MWM task requires animals to find a hidden platform in a large, circular pool of water that is colored opaque with powdered non-fat milk (or) non-toxic tempera paint where they must swim to the hidden platform. Because they are in the opaque water, the animals cannot see the platform and cannot rely on scent to find the escape route. Instead, they must rely on extra-maze cues. Rodent behavior can be evaluated by analyzing the different parameters such as escape latency, swim speed, and path length, and probe trail [29]. RAWM is a hybrid task combining the spatial navigation component of the Morris water maze with the choice structure of the radial arm maze. Animals must locate a hidden platform in a circular pool with multiple radiating arms, using distal visual cues. It evaluates working memory through repeated trials within a single session, measuring the ability to remember the platform location over short intervals. And assessed reference memory across days, reflecting long-term retention of the platform position [30]. To investigate the role of astrocytic autophagy in cognitive function, Kim et al. utilized the Novel Object Recognition (NOR) and Novel Object Place Recognition (NOPR) tests, paradigms originally established and validated by Ennaceur and Delacour [32] (for NOR) and Dix and Aggleton [31] (for NOPR), respectively [31–33]. The NOR test protocol comprises three phases conducted sequentially: habituation, training, and testing. During the habituation phase, the mouse explores the empty arena for a few minutes. In the training sessions, the mouse rats are exposed to one or to two identical objects (samples). After an intertrial interval, one familiar object is replaced with a novel object for testing. The NOPR test was extended to look at other types of recognition memory, memory for place (recognition that an object is in a location where previously there had been no object), memory for object in place (recognition that a specific object has changed position with another object) and memory for context (recognition that a familiar object is in a context different to that in which it was previously encountered). Testing sessions are video-recorded, and parameters such as exploration time and discrimination index are subsequently analyzed. These recognition measures are influenced by the interval between time spent with novel object and time spent with sample object as well as the time allowed for rodents to explore the sample in a first trial. So the paradigms are used to assess short-term or long-term memory, depending on the interval between the training and testing phases [34]. Specifically, short retention intervals (e.g., minutes to a few hours) are used to evaluate short-term memory, whereas longer intervals (typically 24 h) are employed to assess long-term memory [32, 34, 35]. For example, Moore et al. conducted a short (2 min) and long (24 h) delay period to clarify the influence of light on short-term and long-term memory respectively, assuming all other conditions remain the same [36].

In rodent models, treadmill running paradigms are primarily employed to investigate endocrine, metabolic, and related disorders, including diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, and musculoskeletal conditions [37, 38]. The rotarod test is a widely used paradigm for assessing motor coordination and balance in rodents. The apparatus consists of a rotating rod that accelerates at predetermined intervals. The animal is placed on the rod at a height sufficient to deter jumping, with soft padding positioned beneath to cushion any falls. Animals with motor deficits typically fall from the rod at lower rotational speeds and after shorter latencies [39]. Behavioral assays for assessing motor function encompass several distinct categories: evaluation of general locomotor activity (e.g., open field test), assessment of coordinated motor skills (e.g., rotarod and balance beam tests), measurement of muscle strength (e.g., grip strength test), and evaluation of endurance (e.g., weighted swim test and treadmill running) [40]. The rotarod test is widely used to evaluate the motor coordination of rodents, and is especially sensitive in detecting cerebellar dysfunction. This method is widely used for assessing motor function in neurodegenerative disease models, such as Parkinson’s disease and Huntington’s disease, testing drug efficacy, and analyzing phenotypes in genetically modified animals. By recording the latency of animals falling from an accelerating rotating rod and analyzing their movement trajectories, the test objectively reflects their neuromuscular coordination.

However, rodent models possess inherent limitations. These limitations stem from factors including species divergence, shorter lifespans, and key neuroanatomical differences—notably the absence of a gyrencephalic cortex and the attenuated age-related neural changes compared to humans and other large mammals. Consequently, rodent models may not fully recapitulate the complexity of human disease pathology, which can impede a comprehensive understanding of underlying mechanisms [41–43]. Thus, despite ongoing refinements in rodent behavioral methodologies, their applicability is ultimately constrained by the inherent limitations of the model system.

Application and limitations of behavioral assays in tree shrews (Scandentia)

The tree shrew has gained attention as a promising non-traditional model organism in biomedical research. Its strength lies in a compelling phylogenetic rationale: bearing closer physiological, anatomical, and genetic homology to primates than rodents do. This evolutionary proximity is reflected in relevant phenotypes for translational neuroscience. For instance, aged tree shrews spontaneously develop key features of Alzheimer’s disease pathology, including elevated amyloid-beta and phosphorylated tau, alongside cognitive decline. Furthermore, chronic social stress in subordinate males induces sustained physiological alterations (e.g., elevated cortisol) and behavioral changes analogous to human depression. These intrinsic, spontaneous disease-relevant phenotypes constitute a primary translational advantage over rodent models, which often require extensive genetic or invasive surgical manipulation to elicit comparable pathology [44–46].

In behavioral testing, tree shrews’ strong exploratory drive has allowed researchers to adapt many cognitive and anxiety-related paradigms from rodent research, such as various maze tasks and the modified hole-board test. For instance, in the modified hole-board test, the apparatus comprises a black PVC board (105 × 297 mm) featuring 10 white holes (15 mm in diameter) arranged in three staggered rows. Each hole is covered by a removable white lid fabricated from the same material. The subject must lift a lid to access a food reward concealed beneath. Prior to testing, animals undergo a 2-week acclimatization period upon arrival at the facility. On day 1, subjects are trained to lift lids to retrieve food rewards from randomly selected holes. Over the subsequent three training days, they learn to associate distinct visual cues on the lids with the presence of a food reward. On day 5, subjects first perform a 10-min trial to locate baited holes. This is followed by two memory retention trials, conducted at 1-h and 24-h intervals, respectively, using the same baited holes as during training (Fig. 3). Video recordings were analyzed to quantify total exploration time, the number of exploration attempts, and error choices (defined as opening unbaited holes). Between-group differences were assessed using Student’s t-tests. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), with statistical significance set at P < 0.05 [47]. Owing to shared behavioral traits—particularly exploratory drive—many behavioral testing methods for tree shrews have been adapted from established rodent paradigms, including the radial arm maze, cheeseboard maze, and water maze [48].

Modified hole-board apparatus [47]

However, the practical application of tree shrew models involves significant trade-offs. The field faces notable challenges, including higher individual variability (potentially compromising reproducibility), limited global breeding and research infrastructure, and a stark paucity of species-specific molecular tools (e.g., antibodies, detection kits) compared to the highly developed toolkit for mice. These limitations increase research costs and constrain deep mechanistic investigations at the molecular level [48]. The tree shrew offers a unique “middle ground” for behavioral neuroscience—providing a phylogenetically closer model to primates with spontaneous disease phenotypes that are highly relevant to human neuropsychiatric and neurodegenerative disorders. However, its current utility is balanced by practical constraints related to model availability, reproducibility, and molecular toolkit development, factors that researchers must carefully consider when choosing this model system.

Application and limitations of behavioral assays in Artiodactyla

The pig has emerged as a critical large animal model for translational biomedical research, primarily due to its striking anatomical and physiological homology with humans in two key systems: the brain (including cortical gyrification) and the cardiometabolic system. Pigs exhibit close anatomical parallels with humans in the digestive tract and cardiovascular system. Furthermore, key physiological processes, including cardiac energy metabolism and the digestion and absorption of macronutrients, closely parallel those in humans [49, 50]. This dual relevance positions the pig uniquely for studying neurological disorders and cardiovascular/metabolic diseases [43]. Importantly, pigs generally raise fewer ethical concerns than non-human primates, facilitating their broader use [43]. However, their distinct sensory profile—including dichromatic vision, acute olfaction, and less pronounced novelty-driven exploration compared to rodents—must be carefully considered in behavioral assay design to avoid confounding [51]. As dichromats, pigs possess only two cone photopigment types, with peak spectral sensitivities near 439 nm and 556 nm. This renders them sensitive to blue and green wavelengths but limits their ability to discriminate colors in the red spectrum [51, 52].

Owing to their gyrencephalic brain and complex cognition, pigs are particularly suited for assessing higher-order functions relevant to neurodegenerative and neuropsychiatric conditions. Behavioral paradigms are often adapted from rodents but must account for porcine sensory and motivational characteristics [51]. Tasks like the Morris water maze and spatial hole-board are used to model hippocampal-dependent memory deficits analogous to those in Alzheimer’s disease. Adaptations include larger pools and arenas, and protocols that prevent development of fixed response patterns to ensure true memory assessment [53]. The Morris water maze (MWM), originally developed for rodents, has been adapted for use in piglets. The porcine adaptation consists of an inflatable pool (diameter 3.6 m) filled with 50 cm of opaque water maintained at a thermoneutral temperature. Similar to the rodent paradigm, an escape platform is submerged just below the water surface, allowing piglets to swim freely until they locate and mount the platform [51]. Siegford et al. employed this task to assess stress responses associated with early weaning adaptation and to evaluate training interventions designed to enhance cognitive outcomes [54]. In the spatial hole-board task, food rewards are concealed in 4 of 16 equally spaced holes within an open arena. Quantifying visits to baited versus non-baited holes provides measures of working memory and reference memory performance [51, 55]. During training, performance on both working memory and reference memory measures improved. However, because pigs were consistently released from the same start point, they likely developed a fixed visitation pattern, which substantially reduced the working memory demand of the task. Consequently, subsequent testing methodologies have been refined. In refined protocols, pigs are trained to locate rewards in containers where baited positions are randomized across trials, enabling a more accurate assessment of cognitive abilities [51]. Netzley et al. have developed a spatial baited ball pit task to assess executive function, processing speed and spatial learning and memory, which was inspired by the cognitive hole-board test. In this test, the pig is required to locate and retrieve six apple slices hidden at fixed positions within a ball pit. The time taken to find all six slices is recorded. They hypothesize that this paradigm can serve as a measure of situational memory and contextual conditioning (Fig. 4) [56, 57].

There are a few studies employing the Open Field Test and Novel Object Recognition Test in piglet studies as standardized measures to quantitatively assess spontaneous locomotor activity and exploratory behavior, respectively. It was realized by analyzing the total distance moved, velocity, mobile time of any body part, and moving time of the body center point in the OFT, and the time spending on familiar and novel objects in NORT [58, 59]. But some specialists believe the standard rodent Open Field Test has limited utility due to pigs’ low exploratory drive and stress from social isolation. Therefore, it is often modified (e.g., group testing, daytime sessions) or combined with species-appropriate assays like the Human Approach Test to better quantify anxiety-like states relevant to psychiatric research [60, 61]. In the Human Approach Test (HAT), pigs can freely interact with a stationary human observer within the arena. Key behavioral metrics include the latency to approach the observer and the duration of contact [61, 62]. Advanced paradigms such as operant conditioning, set-shifting, and reversal learning tasks tap into prefrontal cortex function. Pigs typically outperform rodents on these, providing a more nuanced model for assessing executive function impairments [51]. Furthermore, the use of pigs in experimental research remains subject to ethical considerations and regulatory oversight [63].

For translational research on heart failure (including Heart Failure with preserved Ejection Fraction, HFpEF), metabolic syndrome, and stroke recovery, pigs are invaluable because their cardiovascular system, exercise physiology, and body size closely mirror humans. Here, behavioral assays focus on quantifying functional capacity and therapeutic efficacy [64]. Treadmill protocols are the gold standard for objectively measuring functional limitation, a core clinical endpoint. Submaximal tests (e.g., sustained brisk walking) reliably elevate heart rate to human-relevant levels and can quantify impairment through metrics like exercise duration and heart rate recovery—directly analogous to clinical stress tests [65]. In addition, pig models of stroke are well-established. Naturalistic behaviors such as gait analysis, skilled limb use, and foraging tasks could be developed to assess sensorimotor recovery, leveraging the pig’s size and manipulability [66]. The main limitations of swine to model human stroke are more likely to be the brain structure differences, including the rete mirabile, the significant contribution of both posterior and anterior cerebral arteries to the circulation through the circle of Willis, and the plasminogen has resistance to tissue plasminogen activator (tPA)-mediated activation. These restrictions compromise the translatability of experimental findings to real-world human performance. Besides, pigs take longer than rodents to reach sexual maturity, along with a great cost [67].

Application and limitations of behavioral assays in Carnivora

Compared to pigs, the domestic dog occupies a unique niche in translational research due to its coevolution with humans, which has endowed it with complex social cognition, shared environments, and a striking propensity for spontaneous, age-related diseases that closely mirror human conditions. This positions the dog not merely as an experimental subject, but as a co-patient model for specific research areas [68–70]. However, its use necessitates a clear ethical and scientific calculus, distinguishing areas of unparalleled value from those where its application is less justifiable.

The strongest translational argument for canine models lies in the study of naturally occurring pathologies, which avoid the artificiality of induced models and encompass complex genetic and environmental interactions. Aged dogs spontaneously develop cognitive decline, β-amyloid pathology, and cerebral atrophy, closely modeling human Alzheimer’s disease progression. Behavioral assays are central here, with tasks like the Size Discrimination/Reversal (SD, SDR) and radial arm maze specifically designed to track age-dependent deficits in learning, memory, and cognitive flexibility longitudinally. These tests provide direct, clinically relevant readouts of cognitive function [70–72].

Canine behavioral testing leverages their unique attributes. Beyond discrimination tasks, paradigms like the Y-maze with social/food choice probe the neural basis of social motivation [73]. For functional assessment, adapted treadmill protocols (using motivational cues appropriate to welfare standards) and open field tests with physiological monitoring (cortisol, heart rate) are used to quantify anxiety and exercise capacity [74–77]. For example, Gruen et al. used the OFT to evaluate sound-induced behaviors in Labrador Retrievers. Quantitative assessment primarily relied on analyzing video recordings from daily 9-min test sessions. Additional evaluation metrics included measurements of distance traveled (locomotor activity), heart rate, body temperature, and salivary cortisol concentration [77].

In addition, canine spontaneous osteosarcoma is genetically, biologically, and clinically analogous to the pediatric human disease. Behavioral and functional assessments are critical clinical endpoints in this context. Force plate gait analysis (Fig. 5) provides an objective, quantifiable measure of pain and limb function (via metrics like Peak Vertical Force and Symmetry Index), directly informing therapeutic efficacy in a way that rodent models cannot [68, 78]. While often a clinical population, pet dogs with spontaneous spinal injuries are studied to understand natural recovery processes [79]. Treadmill-based locomotor analysis (e.g., measuring step coordination) in this setting generates ecologically valid functional recovery data relevant to human rehabilitation [75, 76]. In the field of locomotor ethology, Olby et al. published a study in 2014 describing a method to generate continuous functional data on hindlimb recovery in spinal cord-injured dogs using a treadmill. Scoring was based on two metrics: (1) Step Score (hindlimb steps/forelimb steps × 100), (2) Coordinated Step Score (coordinated hindlimb steps/total hindlimb steps × 100).The sole equipment requirements were a treadmill and strategically placed cameras, enabling this system to be applied in multicenter clinical trials for generating continuous data on neurological recovery in dogs [79].

The decision to use dogs must be guided by the principle of necessity. Their use is most justified—and publicly acceptable—when studying spontaneous conditions where they serve as a parallel patient population. Conversely, creating severe, non-naturalistic diseases (e.g., inducing heart failure, traumatic brain injury, or cancer) in healthy dogs is increasingly difficult to justify ethically and scientifically [80]. For many such questions, equally informative but less sentient models (e.g., pigs for cardiovascular size, rodents for initial mechanistic screening) are available and should be prioritized. Public sensitivity toward dogs further mandates that any research use must demonstrate an irreplaceable contribution that cannot be obtained from other models.

The dog is a powerful translational model specifically and primarily for the study of its own spontaneous, complex diseases—notably neurocognitive aging and certain cancers. In these areas, behavioral and functional assays provide direct, clinically analogous endpoints. A critical design principle is to utilize positive reinforcement and minimize damage, aligning with their status as a favored species. Its use should be rigorously confined to these areas of unique, naturalistic relevance, avoiding induced disease paradigms for which alternative models exist. The ethical cost of using this favored species must be offset by exceptional, disease-naturalistic scientific value.

Application and limitations of behavioral assays in primate

Non-human primates (NHPs) represent the gold standard for modeling complex human cognitive functions and neuropsychiatric disorders due to their unparalleled phylogenetic proximity, shared brain architecture (e.g., prefrontal cortex elaboration), and spontaneous development of age-related pathologies like β-amyloid accumulation [41, 81]. However, their use imposes profound ethical, practical, and financial burdens, demanding a rigorous justification that balances scientific necessity against welfare costs.

NHP cognitive testing focuses on advanced functions with direct human parallels, primarily utilizing automated touchscreen systems or the Wisconsin General Test Apparatus (WGTA). Key paradigms include:

Spatial and Visual Working Memory: Tasks like the Delayed Response (DR) and Spatial Memory Array tasks assess the ability to hold and manipulate spatial information over short delays, a function critical to executive control. Non-human primates are capable of tracking multiple spatial locations. In an early study, Tinklepaugh demonstrated that chimpanzees could remember which of two cups placed in different locations contained food, even when the cups were positioned across 16 distinct rooms and tested after a 24-h memory retention interval [82]. Menzel reported that chimpanzees memorized the locations of 18 distinct food items scattered across an open field. They accurately retrieved all items and achieved this by navigating the shortest possible path between food locations [83]. In a task analogous to a radial arm maze paradigm, MacDonald and her colleagues required animals to locate food from eight distinct containers positioned at varied locations within an enclosure. The study demonstrated that Old World monkeys (yellow-nosed monkeys), New World monkeys (marmosets), and two species of great apes—gorillas and orangutans—were able to visit all food sites with minimal revisitation rates [84–86]. In a follow-up experiment, the primates were allowed to observe the containers and found that only four of them (randomly selected) contained food. And in the subsequent memory test, they returned to the enclosure and could access any container, but only the four previously baited containers were refilled with food (win-stay task), or only the four previously empty containers were refilled with food (win-shift task) [70]. The Delayed Response (DR) task, typically administered using the Wisconsin General Test Apparatus, assesses spatial working memory. In this paradigm, the subject must recall the location of a briefly presented reward after a variable delay period. Performance is quantified by systematically increasing the delay until a predetermined failure criterion is met, with the maximum successfully retained delay serving as the key outcome measure [87].

Performance is quantified by systematically increasing the delay until a predetermined failure criterion is met, with the maximum successfully retained delay serving as the key outcome measure [87].

Recognition and Associative Memory: The Delayed Non-Matching-to-Sample (DNMS) and Delayed Matching-to-Sample (DMTS) tasks probe medial temporal lobe-dependent recognition memory and rule learning, central to Alzheimer’s disease research. DNMS task assesses recognition memory by requiring subjects to select a novel object over a previously shown sample after a variable delay. Memory load is systematically tested by progressively increasing the delay interval between the sample presentation and the choice test. Overall performance is typically summarized as a composite score, representing the average accuracy across a range of delay durations (Fig. 6) [88]. The Delayed Match-to-Sample (DMTS) task was similar to the DNMS task. In this task, the subject is required to remember a visual sample stimulus and, after a variable delay period, correctly identify it from a set that includes a novel distractor. Mastery of this rule and the subject’s learning speed serve as key metrics for assessing visual recognition memory and associative learning [87]. In addition to cognitive testing via tasks like DMTS, behavioral phenotyping of depressive-like states is conducted in NHPs through ethogram-based video analysis. Key scored behaviors include hunched posture, stereotypies, reduced locomotion/exploration, and altered self-care activities (e.g., excessive grooming). This multimodal approach—linking quantifiable affective behaviors with cognitive metrics—aims to model the comorbid nature of depression and uncover its underlying pathophysiology [89].

Complex Problem-Solving and Social Cognition: Beyond basic memory tasks, advanced paradigms such as object discrimination (OD), reversal learning, and attentional set-shifting are employed to probe complex cognitive functions like rule acquisition, cognitive flexibility, and inhibitory control—processes largely dependent on prefrontal cortical circuits implicated in psychiatric disorders. In a typical OD task, for instance, subjects learn through repeated trials to associate one of two distinct objects with a reward, a process that evaluates their ability to form and retain stimulus-reward associations [90]. A critical advantage of NHP models is the ability to correlate precise behavioral metrics from these tasks with invasive neural recordings (e.g., electrophysiology, optogenetics) and neuroimaging (fMRI). This multi-modal approach enables causal mechanistic insights that are not feasible in human subjects. For instance, studies combining perceptual decision-making tasks with optogenetic inhibition in NHPs have definitively established the causal role of specific frontal cortical subregions in choice behavior, a level of proof that cannot be attained in human research [91].

However, the application of these paradigms is not trivial and involves significant trade-offs. The extensive training burden poses a significant challenge. Tasks like DNMS require months to years of training to establish stable baseline performance, especially for long-delay intervals. This lengthy timeline directly impacts animal lifetime use and study cost. We need to consider welfare-driven design imperative: The stress of isolation, repetitive testing, and fluid/food control can confound results and raise ethical concerns. Modern best practices mandate the use of positive reinforcement training, environmental enrichment, and social housing to ensure psychological well-being and data validity. Furthermore, while NHPs provide the closest cognitive analog to humans, their behavioral complexity also introduces variability. Disentangling motivational states, individual personality differences, and social dynamics requires careful experimental design and nuanced interpretation [92].

The high costs of NHP research dictate a strict “necessity principle” for their use, including essential applications and avoidable applications: NHPs are indispensable for: (1) Final-stage preclinical testing of biologics, devices, or complex interventions (e.g., deep brain stimulation) where neuroanatomical specificity and systemic physiology are critical; (2) Causal investigation of neural circuits underlying higher-order cognition and social behavior that are not conserved in lower species; (3) Study of spontaneous, aged-related neurodegenerative processes that uniquely mirror human pathology. More importantly, we propose NHPs not be used for: (1) Early-stage screening of compounds or initial proof-of-concept studies, which can be conducted in rodents; (2) Modeling simple behaviors or endpoints (e.g., basic motor function, anxiety in open fields) adequately addressed by other models; (3) Any study where the scientific question does not explicitly require the unique neurobiological or cognitive homology that NHPs provide.

The NHP model is a powerful but finite resource. Its deployment must be reserved for questions that sit at the apex of translational complexity, where the fidelity of cognitive, neuroanatomical, and physiological homology is non-negotiable. Researchers must justify its use by demonstrating that the scientific objective cannot be achieved with a less sentient or more tractable model, while simultaneously committing to the highest standards of ethical welfare and experimental design to ensure both scientific rigor and moral responsibility.

Summary of behavioral tests across mammalian models in neuroscience

Animal models are crucial for studying complex human diseases, understanding biological functions, and assessing the safety and efficacy of novel therapeutic approaches. Behavioral tests serve as a vital bridge in this process. In behavioral testing of mammalian models, commonly employed methods can be categorized into three major types: learning and memory, emotional behaviors, and motor functions, aimed at evaluating anxiety, learning capacity, social behavior, and more in animals. Learning and memory assessments primarily involve various maze tests. Motor function evaluation is mainly conducted through treadmill experiments. Methods related to emotional behaviors are designed to simulate human emotional states such as anxiety and depression, tailored to specific research objectives. These approaches can be applied individually or in combination for comprehensive assessment, with researchers selecting and implementing them based on a holistic consideration of their study goals (Table 1).

Current technology of behavioral tests in biomedical research

Current technological advancements have facilitated the increasingly widespread application of emerging methodologies in assessing behaviors in animals. Fiber photometry, a calcium imaging technique, detects changes in intracellular calcium concentration as a proxy for neuronal activity. The primary strength of fiber photometry is its ability to provide stable, long-term, and behaviorally-relevant recordings of defined neural population activity in freely behaving animals. It balances high temporal resolution, experimental flexibility, and technical accessibility, making it a cornerstone tool for modern systems neuroscience to decode brain function in health and disease. The GCaMP calcium indicator is delivered via viral vectors to achieve neuron-specific expression within targeted brain regions. Subsequently, an optical fiber is surgically implanted at the target site. The implanted fiber, affixed to the animal’s head, is connected to a recording system that emits excitation light and concurrently collects the resulting fluorescence signals. Neuronal activity dynamics are then derived through computational processing and analysis of the recorded fluorescence signals. A key advantage of this technique is its capacity for real-time recording of neural activity in freely behaving animals. When integrated with behavioral assays, this approach yields data that more accurately reflects neuronal activity under ethologically relevant, naturalistic conditions [93]. Optogenetics is a biotechnology that combines genetic engineering and optics to enable the expression of light-sensitive proteins in specific cell populations, thereby allowing precise, rapid, and reversible control of their activity with light. The paramount advantage of optogenetics is the powerful fusion of cell-type specificity with millisecond temporal precision. It acts as a “light-guided scalpel” for the brain, allowing scientists to causally dissect and manipulate the logic of complex biological systems with a degree of control that was previously unimaginable. It is now a cornerstone methodology in neuroscience and a catalyst for the development of next-generation, circuit-based therapies. Initially, a recombinant viral vector is employed to deliver transgenes encoding light-sensitive opsins (e.g., channelrhodopsin-2) into targeted neuronal populations. Subsequently, an optical fiber is surgically implanted to permit precise light delivery to the target region. The subsequent delivery of light at a specific wavelength through the implanted fiber activates the expressed opsins. This activation induces the influx or efflux of ions across the neuronal membrane, thereby modulating the cells’ membrane potential and spiking activity [94]. The advent of optogenetics provides researchers with the capability to precisely manipulate the activity of defined neuronal populations, thereby significantly advancing mechanistic investigations into various psychiatric disorders. For instance, optogenetic inhibition of dopaminergic neurons in healthy mice has been demonstrated to significantly increase immobility time in the Forced Swim Test (FST) and markedly reduce sucrose preference in the Sucrose Preference Test (SPT), thereby inducing depression-like behavioral phenotypes. Collectively, these findings establish a causal link between dopaminergic signaling and depression-like behaviors, thereby contributing to our understanding of the neural mechanisms underlying depression [95]. The advent of these novel technologies has markedly advanced methodological development in animal behavioral research.

The ultimate goal of translational neuroscience extends beyond modeling average disease phenotypes; it aims to capture the heterogeneity inherent in human neuropsychiatric disorders to inform personalized medicine. This requires shifting from coarse behavioral readouts to high-dimensional, quantitative phenotyping that can identify individual-specific behavioral signatures (“digital biomarkers”) and link them to underlying neural circuitry. Modern computational tools are pivotal in this shift. DeepLabCut exemplifies this progress. Beyond its dramatic efficiency gains over manual scoring (reducing weeks of labor to minutes), its core value lies in enabling unbiased, granular quantification of naturalistic behavior across species—from rodent social interactions to primate gestures. This allows researchers to move beyond predefined, experimenter-chosen categories (e.g., “immobility”) to discover subtle, clinically relevant behavioral patterns that may differentiate disease subtypes or predict treatment response [96]. DeepLabCut is an open-source software package developed by Mathis et al. This toolbox utilizes the DeeperCut feature detector. It first extracts different frames from videos for labeling, then generates training data based on these labels, trains the network to identify the desired feature set, and extracts the locations of these features from unlabeled data. A typical use case involves the experimenter extracting distinct frames from videos and labeling body parts of interest to create a customized part detector. The developers have demonstrated its applicability in species such as mice and fruit flies [97]. Some researchers have further validated the practical utility of DeepLabCut by comparing the ability of humans and machines to track rat activity. For the same workload of 7014 frames, a trained human would require 11 days, 17 h, and 25 min of continuous work to complete the labeling, whereas DeepLabCut accomplishes this in just 10.5 min. Moreover, the 3D point error of the trained dataset is only 1.9 ± 0.1 mm [98]. Moreover, DeepLabCut can also individually track animals such as crickets, water fleas, and zebrafish [99–101]. It can even track the movements of multiple different species simultaneously using multiple training sets [102].

Concretely, these tools advance personalized medicine through several pathways. Firstly, By analyzing high-dimensional behavioral data from animal models (e.g., stress susceptibility in mice or cognitive trajectories in NHPs), machine learning can cluster subjects into distinct subgroups that may mirror clinical heterogeneity, paving the way for tailored interventions. Secondly, The precise kinematic data generated (e.g., gait dynamics, fine motor control, or social approach vectors) can be correlated with simultaneous neural activity recordings (fMRI, electrophysiology). This creates causal maps linking dysfunctional circuits to specific, quantifiable behavioral deficits, rather than broad syndrome labels. Most importantly, the behaviors quantified in animals (e.g., movement smoothness, exploratory entropy, vocalization patterns) can inform the search for analogous, objectively measurable digital biomarkers in human patients via wearable sensors or video analysis, bridging preclinical and clinical assessment.

Challenges of behavioral experimental methods in personalized medicine

Of course, current methods in mammalian behavioral experiments still face certain challenges. For instance, differences in anatomical markers across species, along with the broad heterogeneity in experimental subjects, disease processes, and measurement indicators, may all lead to significant inconsistencies between results from human clinical trials and animal studies [103]. For example, a systematic review indicated that corticosteroids could reduce adverse outcomes in animal models of traumatic brain injury, yet no such benefits were observed in clinical trials. Similarly, tirilazad was shown to reduce infarct size and improve neurobehavioral scores in animal models, but the results from clinical trials were contradictory [104]. This suggests that beyond genetic differences between species, variations in environmental conditions may also exist. The outcomes of behavioral tests (such as the open field test and water maze for mice) are influenced by multiple factors, including experimental equipment, materials, procedures, environmental conditions, and data collection and analysis.

To bridge this translational gap and mitigate the “reproducibility crisis” in behavioral neuroscience, the field must adopt a more rigorous, transparent, and standardized framework. We propose the following actionable recommendations:

Comprehensive Standardization & Protocol Sharing: Detailed, publicly accessible Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs) should be established for all aspects of animal husbandry, behavioral apparatus, test protocols, and environmental conditions (e.g., light/dark cycle, noise). As demonstrated by studies on mouse strains like C57BL/6J, consistent personnel and settings are crucial for reproducible results [105]. Variability can be further reduced by using automated, home-cage monitoring systems where possible.

By mandating standardization, transparency, and robust design (blinding, randomization, appropriate statistics), researchers can significantly enhance the reproducibility, reliability, and ultimately, the predictive value of animal behavioral research for human health.
