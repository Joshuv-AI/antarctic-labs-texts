Abstract
In this work we uncover an intimate relation between Lévy processes and data sketches for generalized moment estimation and weighted sampling. Let 𝐱 ∈ ( ℝ d ) n \mathbf{x}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n} be a vector subject to element updates and f : ℝ d → ℝ + f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{+} . By definition, the f f -moment of 𝐱 \mathbf{x} is f ⁡ ( 𝐱 ) = ∑ v ∈ [ n ] f ⁡ ( 𝐱 ⁡ ( v ) ) . f(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{v\in[n]}f(\mathbf{x}(v)). The f f -moment estimation problem is to ( 1 ± ϵ ) (1\pm\epsilon) -approximate f ⁡ ( 𝐱 ) f(\mathbf{x}) whereas the f f -sampling problem is to select an index v ∗ ∈ [ n ] v_{*}\in[n] with probability f ⁡ ( 𝐱 ⁡ ( v ∗ ) ) / f ⁡ ( 𝐱 ) f(\mathbf{x}(v_{*}))/f(\mathbf{x}) . Our primary conceptual contributions are I. to draw a close connection between f f -moment estimation sketches of 𝐱 ∈ ( ℝ d ) n \mathbf{x}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n} in the turnstile model and generic Lévy processes over ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} , and II. to draw a close connection between f f -samplers of 𝐱 ∈ ℝ n \mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n} in the incremental model (positive updates only) and one-dimensional, non-negative Lévy processes, aka subordinators . Through these connections, we can apply the powerful Lévy-Khintchine representation theorem from the theory of Lévy processes to design new sketches for f f -moment estimation and sampling. 1.1
A New Perspective

The main take-away message of the present work is that

f
-moment estimation in the
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-turnstile model and
G
-moment estimation and
G
-sampling in the
\mathbb{R}_{+}
-turnstile model can all be done in a uniform manner by simulating Lévy processes.

Lévy processes
have been studied since the early 20th century,
and have been used to model phenomena in various fields, e.g.,
physics (how does a gas particle move?) and finance (how does the stock price change?).
We will show that all Lévy processes have algorithmic interpretations
in the context of streaming sketches, and
the fundamental
Lévy-Khintchine representation theorem
leads to a unified view of sketching for moment estimation
and sampling. To our knowledge, this
is the first application of the Lévy-Khintchine theorem
in theoretical computer science.

We give a detailed technical synopsis of Lévy processes in
Section

2
.
For the time being, a Lévy process
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, where
X_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
is defined by having independent, stationary increments. I.e., for any
t_{1},t_{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,

(X_{t_{1}+t_{2}}-X_{t_{1}})\sim X_{t_{2}}
,
and for any
t_{1}<t_{2}<\cdots<t_{k}
,
the increments

X_{t_{1}},X_{t_{2}}-X_{t_{1}},\ldots,X_{t_{k}}-X_{t_{k-1}}

are mutually independent.
Some common one-dimensional Lévy processes are

Linear drift.

X_{t}=\gamma t
with drift rate
\gamma
.

Wiener process/Brownian motion.

X_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,t\sigma^{2})
with variance
\sigma^{2}
.

Poisson process.

X_{t}\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda t)
with rate
\lambda
.

Compound Poisson process.

X_{t}=\sum_{k=1}^{Z}J_{k}
, where the number of
jumps

Z\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda t)
follows a Poisson process, while each jump
J_{k}\sim\mathcal{J}
is sampled independently from a common
jump distribution
.

\alpha
-stable process.

Defined by
X_{t}/t^{1/\alpha}\sim X_{1}\sim\text{$\alpha$-stable}
. (For
\alpha\in(0,2]
, the
\alpha
-stable random variable
X_{1}
on
\mathbb{R}
is defined by
\mathbb{E}e^{izX_{1}}=e^{-|z|^{\alpha}}
, for any
z\in\mathbb{R}
.)

The Lévy processes defined above can all be generalized to higher dimensions.
They may also be combined, as Lévy processes are closed under addition.

The characteristic function
\varphi_{X}(z)=\mathbb{E}e^{izX}

of a random variable
X\in\mathbb{R}

is equivalent to the Fourier transform of its pdf,
when its pdf exists.
It uniquely determines the distribution of
X
.
For example, whenever it exists,
the
k
th moment of
X
can be recovered
from the
k
th derivative of
\varphi_{X}
evaluated at zero.
When
X\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\varphi_{X}(z)=\mathbb{E}e^{i\langle X,z\rangle}
,
where
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\langle X,z\rangle
is the inner product.

The Lévy-Khintchine representation theorem identifies every Lévy
process
X
with its
characteristic exponent

f_{X}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
,
where for any
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\mathbb{E}\exp(i\langle X_{t},z\rangle)=\exp(-tf_{X}(z)).

When
X
is a non-negative Lévy process in one dimension
(also known as a
subordinator
), it is characterized by its

Laplace exponent

G_{X}\colon\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
where for any
z\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,

\mathbb{E}\exp(-zX_{t})=\exp(-tG_{X}(z)).

Note that the
stationary increments
property
implies that a non-negative Lévy process has only non-negative increments.

We briefly explain why Lévy processes

In other words, we should have

and also for the limiting case as

In other words,

. After normalizing the time scale,

4

1.3
f
-Moment Estimation and Lévy Processes

Given any Lévy process
X=(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, by
the Lévy-Khintchine representation theorem (
Theorem

6
in
Section

2
), there exists a function
f=f_{X}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
such that for any
t\geq 0
and
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\mathbb{E}e^{i\langle z,X_{t}\rangle}=e^{-tf(z)}
. Suppose now we
project the input vector to a single cell
C_{t}
by sampling the Lévy processes at time
t
, as follows.

\displaystyle C_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in[n]}\left\langle X_{t}^{(v)},\mathbf{x}(v)\right\rangle.

Here the
X_{t}^{(v)}
are i.i.d. copies of the Lévy process
X_{t}
.
Clearly
C_{t}
is a linear sketch that can be maintained over a distributed stream. We thus have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}e^{iC_{t}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}e^{i\sum_{v\in[n]}\left\langle X_{t}^{(v)},\mathbf{x}(v)\right\rangle}

(definition of
C_{t}
)

\displaystyle=\prod_{v\in[n]}\mathbb{E}e^{i\left\langle X_{t}^{(v)},\mathbf{x}(v)\right\rangle}

(by independence)

\displaystyle=\prod_{v\in[n]}e^{-tf(\mathbf{x}(v))}

(by Lévy-Khintchine)

\displaystyle=e^{-tf(\mathbf{x})},

(definition of
f(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{v\in[n]}f(\mathbf{x}(v))
)

from which the
f
-moment
f(\mathbf{x})

can be recovered by choosing a suitable time

t\approx\Theta(1/|f(\mathbf{x})|)
.
Of course, we do not know
f(\mathbf{x})
in advance
and therefore do not know the optimum
t
in advance, but we achieve reasonable coverage by
maintaining
(C_{t})

for many
t
, evenly
spaced on a logarithmic scale.
This is the key observation that lets us estimate
any
f
-moment, so long as

f
is the characteristic
exponent of some Lévy process.

Definition 2

A

(v_{*},h_{*})\in([n]\cup\{\perp\})\times(\mathbb{R}_{+}\cup\{\infty\})

(\perp,\infty)

It is associated with a certain hash function

H:[n]\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{Z}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}

A vector update

\mathsf{Update}(v,\Delta)
(issued at time
k

If
H(v,\Delta,k)<h_{*}
, then set
(v_{*},h_{*})\leftarrow(v,H(v,\Delta,k))
.

Assume for simplicity that
\Delta=1
in all updates.
Suppose
\mathbf{x}(v)=w
,
being incremented at

k_{1},\ldots,k_{w}
.
The minimum hash value produced
at index

Z_{v}=\min(H(v,1,k_{1}),\ldots,H(v,1,k_{w})).

The observation is that, no matter what the weight function

is, the probability that

(k_{1},\ldots,k_{w})
.
Thus, we want the random sequence

\displaystyle H(v,1,1),H(v,1,2),H(v,1,3),\ldots

(1)

5
5

5

A finite random vector

remains unchanged after reordering the coordinates. An infinite
random sequence is exchangeable if any finite prefix is
exchangeable. See
[

De Finetti’s theorem

6

then implies that
there is a non-negative random process

such
that

(H(v,1,k))_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}=(\{\inf\{t\geq 0:X_{t}>E_{k}\}\})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}},

where the

are i.i.d. standard exponential
random variables. In addition, in order to sample with the correct

Note that

Now suppose

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\min(H(v,1,k_{1}),\ldots,H(v,1,k_{w}))>z)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(\min(H(v,1,1),\ldots,H(v,1,w))>z)

((

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(\min(\inf\{t\geq 0:X_{t}>E_{1}\},\ldots,\inf\{t\geq 0:X_{t}>E_{w}\})>z)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(\inf\{t\geq 0:X_{t}>\mathrm{Exp}(w)\}>z)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(X_{z}\leq\mathrm{Exp}(w))

(non-negativity)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(\mathbb{P}(X_{z}\leq\mathrm{Exp}(w))\mid X_{z})

(law of total probability)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}e^{-wX_{z}}=e^{-zG_{X}(w)}.

Note that
\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Exp}(\lambda)>z)=e^{-z\lambda}

Therefore we know that

\displaystyle\min(H(v,1,k_{1}),\ldots,H(v,1,k_{w}))\sim\mathrm{Exp}(G_{X}(w))=\mathrm{Exp}(G_{X}(\mathbf{x}(v))).

I.e., the minimum hash value produced by an element

replaced by the

This is the underlying intuition for the

2.1

Definition 3

(Lévy processes

A random process

(B) Independent Increments.

for
0\leq t_{1}<t_{2}\ldots<t_{k}

X_{t_{1}},X_{t_{2}}-X_{t_{1}},\ldots,X_{t_{k}}-X_{t_{k-1}}

(C) Stochastic Continuity.

X_{0}=0
almost surely and
\lim_{t\searrow 0}\mathbb{P}(|X_{t}|>\epsilon)=0
for any
\epsilon>0
.

A process that satisfies (A) and (B)

,
i.e., conditioned on
X_{t^{*}}

(X_{t})_{t>t^{*}}
is independent of
(X_{t})_{t<t^{*}}
.

One-dimensional, non-negative Lévy processes occupy a special place in the theory of Lévy processes

Theorem 7

(6)

Conversely, given any

,
and measure

G_{X}(z)=-\log\mathbb{E}e^{-zX_{1}}

Subordinators are necessarily non-decreasing. The parameter

Sato’s text

However, we do want to consider killed processes (those with

There is a standard way to kill a process.

Lemma 2

We are given a subordinator

\displaystyle X^{\prime}_{t}=\begin{cases}X_{t},&t<Y,\\

Then for

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}e^{-zX^{\prime}_{t}}=e^{-t(G(z)+c\mathbbm{1}\!\left\{{z>0}\right\})},

where

For example, if

Lemma 3

Recall from

Lemma 4

For any

Proof.

By Chebyshev’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}e^{iC_{t}^{(j)}}-e^{-tf(\mathbf{x})}\right|>\eta\right)\leq\frac{\mathbb{V}e^{iC_{t}}}{m\eta^{2}}.

Furthermore, since

, and

we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}e^{iC_{t}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left|e^{iC_{t}}\right|^{2}-\left|\mathbb{E}e^{iC_{t}}\right|^{2}=1-e^{-2t\cdot\mathfrak{Re}f(\mathbf{x})}\leq 2t\cdot\mathfrak{Re}f(\mathbf{x})\leq 2t|f(\mathbf{x})|.

Lemma 5

With probability at least

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=\log_{2}|f(\mathbf{x})|}^{\infty}\frac{2\cdot 2^{-k}|f(\mathbf{x})|}{m\eta^{2}}

Thus, it suffices to choose

3.3
Lévy-Stable
Sketches

Recall that a
d
-dimensional Lévy process
X
is
\alpha
-stable
if for any

,
X_{t}\sim t^{1/\alpha}X_{1}
. We call the characteristic exponents of stable processes
stable moments
.
Stable moments are of special interest in the context of streaming sketches since there is no need to store the whole tower; only
m

i.i.d. samples suffice to return an estimate with relative error
O(1/\sqrt{m})
.
9
9

9

The
Lévy-Tower

self-similar
: all registers in the tower are identically distributed with a proper normalization.

The only symmetric one-dimensional stable processes are
\alpha
-stable random processes, which correspond to Indyk’s sketches
[
29
]
. On the other hand, there is a rich class of higher dimensional stable processes, the one implicit in Ganguly et al.
[
28
]
for estimating

F_{p,q}
hybrid moments being just a single special case.

Theorem 10

(Lévy-Khintchine for symmetric stable processes
[
50
, page 86]
)
.

Let
X
be a Lévy process on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

X
is
2
-stable if and only if
\mathbb{E}e^{i\langle x,X_{t}\rangle}=e^{-\frac{1}{2}t\langle x,Ax\rangle}
for some covariance matrix
A
. If
X
is symmetric, then it is
\alpha
-stable for
\alpha\in(0,2)
if and only if there is a finite, positive, symmetric measure
\mu
on the sphere
\mathbb{S}_{d-1}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:|x|=1\}
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}e^{i\langle x,X_{t}\rangle}=\exp\left(-t\int_{\mathbb{S}_{d-1}}|\langle x,\xi\rangle|^{\alpha}\,\mu(d\xi)\right).

Remark 3
.

For simplicity we only consider symmetric processes here when
\alpha\in(0,2)
so that the characteristic exponent
f_{X}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})=f_{X}(|x_{1}|,\ldots,|x_{d}|)
for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. See
[
50
]
for the full characterization of (not necessarily symmetric) stable processes over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

It turns out that
any
multidimensional
\alpha
-stable moment is as simple to estimate as the one-dimensional
F_{\alpha}
-moment, since it is possible to maintain a random variable distributed as
f(\mathbf{x})^{1/\alpha}Y_{\alpha}
where
Y_{\alpha}
is a unit
\alpha
-stable random variable. We now give the formal definition of the
Lévy-Stable
sketch together with the proof of the emulation theorem.

Definition 5

(
(f,m)
-
Lévy-Stable
)
.

Let
f
be the characteristic exponent of an
\alpha
-stable Lévy process
X
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. An

(f,m)
-
Lévy-Stable

is a vector of
m

, initialized as all zero. For any element
v\in[n]
and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the vector update
\mathbf{x}(v)\leftarrow\mathbf{x}(v)+y
is effected by:

\mathsf{Update}(v,y):

For each

,

, where
X^{(v)}=(X^{(v)}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is an independent copy of the Lévy process

with characteristic exponent

Proof of
Theorem

3
.

Since
X
is Lévy,
X_{1},X_{2}-X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}-X_{m-1}
are i.i.d. with the same distribution as
X_{1}
,
so it suffices to look at the first register
T_{1}=\sum_{v\in[n]}\left\langle\mathbf{x}(v),X^{(v)}_{1}\right\rangle
. Recall that for a stream with frequency vector
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
, Indyk
[
29
]
stores
I_{1}=\sum_{v\in[n]}\mathbf{x}^{\prime}(v)Y^{(v)}_{\alpha}
where
Y_{\alpha}
is a unit
\alpha
-stable random variable. By the properties of stable random variables, we have

\displaystyle I_{1}=\sum_{v\in[n]}\mathbf{x}^{\prime}(v)Y^{(v)}_{\alpha}\sim\left(\sum_{v\in[n]}|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}(v)|^{\alpha}\right)^{1/\alpha}Y_{\alpha}

To show the simulation theorem, we need to prove

\displaystyle T_{1}=\sum_{v\in[n]}\left\langle\mathbf{x}(v),X^{(v)}_{1}\right\rangle\sim\left(\sum_{v\in[n]}f(\mathbf{x}(v))\right)^{1/\alpha}Y_{\alpha},

so that
T_{1}\sim I_{1}
if the
f
-moment of
\mathbf{x}
is equal to the
F_{\alpha}
-moment
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
. We compute the characteristic function

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}e^{izT_{1}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}e^{iz\sum_{v\in[n]}\left\langle\mathbf{x}(v),X^{(v)}_{1}\right\rangle}=\prod_{v\in[n]}\mathbb{E}e^{i\left\langle\mathbf{x}(v),zX^{(v)}_{1}\right\rangle}

(by independence)

Since
X^{(v)}
is
\alpha
-stable, we have
zX_{1}^{(v)}\sim X_{z^{\alpha}}^{(v)}

\displaystyle=\prod_{v\in[n]}\mathbb{E}e^{i\left\langle\mathbf{x}(v),X^{(v)}_{z^{\alpha}}\right\rangle}

\displaystyle=\prod_{v\in[n]}e^{-z^{\alpha}f(\mathbf{x}(v))}

(by Lévy-Khintchine)

\displaystyle=e^{-z^{\alpha}f(\mathbf{x})}=e^{-(zf(\mathbf{x})^{1/\alpha})^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}e^{izf(\mathbf{x})^{1/\alpha}Y_{\alpha}}.

(by Lévy-Khintchine)

Thus
T_{1}
has the same characteristic function with

4.1
The Raw

Recall that a generic min sketch (
Definition

H:[n]\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{Z}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
. The sketch stores a pair
(v_{*},h_{*})

(issued at time

, then set
(v_{*},h_{*})\leftarrow(v,H(v,\Delta,k))
.

Based on the discussion in
Section

1.4
, we now consider hash functions induced by subordinators.

Theorem 11

(Raw
Lévy-Min-Sampler
)
.

For
u\in[n],\Delta\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, and
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, define

\displaystyle H(u,\Delta,k)=\inf\left\{t\geq 0:X^{(u)}_{t}>Z_{k}/\Delta\right\},

where the
X^{(u)}=(X^{(u)}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
are i.i.d. subordinators with Laplace exponent
G:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
and the
Z_{k}\sim\mathrm{Exp}(1)

are i.i.d. standard exponential random variables. Then the min sketch induced by
H
is a
G
-sampler. I.e., the resulting state
(v_{*},h_{*})
satisfies the following.

•

For any
u\in[n]
,
\mathbb{P}(v_{*}=u)=G(\mathbf{x}(u))/G(\mathbf{x})
.

•

h_{*}\sim\mathrm{Exp}(G(\mathbf{x}))
.

The proof of
Theorem

11

makes use of the following standard properties of
exponential random variables.

Lemma 8

(Properties of exponential random variables)
.

Let
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{k}
be independent random variables where
Y_{j}\sim\mathrm{Exp}(\lambda_{j})
for
j\in[k]
. Then

1.

\min\{Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{k}\}\sim\mathrm{Exp}(\lambda_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{k})
.

2.

\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{u}<\min_{j\neq u}Y_{j}\right)=\frac{\lambda_{u}}{\sum_{j\in[k]}\lambda_{j}}
.

Proof of
Theorem

11
.

Suppose element
u
is updated at times
(k_{j})
with increments
(\Delta_{j})
, where
\sum_{j}\Delta_{j}=\mathbf{x}(u)
.
Observe that for any
z\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\min_{j}\{H(u,\Delta_{j},k_{j})\}\geq z\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\inf\left\{t\geq 0:X^{(u)}_{t}>\min_{j}\{Z_{k_{j}}/\Delta_{j}\}\right\}\geq z\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(X^{(u)}_{z}\leq\min_{j}\{Z_{k_{j}}/\Delta_{j}\}\right).

Since the
Z_{k_{j}}
are i.i.d.
\mathrm{Exp}(1)
random variables,
\min_{j}\left\{Z_{k_{j}}/\Delta\right\}\sim\mathrm{Exp}\left(\sum_{j}\Delta_{j}\right)=\mathrm{Exp}(\mathbf{x}(u))
. Continuing,

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(X^{(u)}_{z}\leq\mathrm{Exp}(\mathbf{x}(u)))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathbb{P}\left({X^{(u)}_{z}\leq\mathrm{Exp}(\mathbf{x}(u))}\;\middle|\;X^{(u)}_{z}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}e^{-\mathbf{x}(u)X^{(u)}_{z}},

and since
X^{(u)}
has Laplace exponent
G
, this is equal to

\displaystyle=e^{-zG(\mathbf{x}(u))}.

By the CDF of the exponential distribution,

\displaystyle\min_{j}\{H(u,\Delta_{j},k_{j})\}

\displaystyle\sim\mathrm{Exp}(G(\mathbf{x}(u))).

∎

Proof.

Note that

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\mathrm{Exp}(\mathbf{x}(u))\geq z^{1/\alpha}W^{(u)}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Exp}(\mathbf{x}(u))\geq z^{1/\alpha}W^{(u)})\;\middle|\;W^{(u)}\right)

5.1

Definition 7

Let

be the size of cell

\Delta>0

as

Definition 8

Let

be the corresponding Laplace exponent. Upon an update with

\mathsf{Update}(v,\Delta)

Lemma 9
.

Given any input vector

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(c=1)

\displaystyle=1-e^{-s(c)G(\mathbf{x})}.

Proof.

The independence is given by construction.

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(c=0)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\forall v\in[n].\;X_{s(c)}^{(v,c)}\leq\mathrm{Exp}(\mathbf{x}(v))\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{v\in[n]}\mathbb{P}\left(X_{s(c)}^{(v,c)}\leq\mathrm{Exp}(\mathbf{x}(v))\right)

(by independence)

\displaystyle=\prod_{v\in[n]}\mathbb{E}e^{-X_{s(c)}^{(v,c)}\mathbf{x}(v)}

\displaystyle=\prod_{v\in[n]}e^{-s(c)G(\mathbf{x}(v))}=e^{-s(c)G(\mathbf{x})}.

(by Lévy-Khintchine)

Hence

6
Previous Sketches and Lévy Processes

In this section we will discuss in greater detail
how previous sketches can be viewed
as being constructed from
Lévy processes.
The guiding question is: what will happen if the input vector
is replicated a large number of
times on disjoint supports?

6.1
Single-Level Aggregation and the Central Limit Theorem

We start from the
AMS
sketch by Alon, Matias, and Szegedy
[
1
]
, where a cell
Q
stores

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in[n]}\mathbf{x}(v)\xi_{v}.

Here
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

is updated in an
\mathbb{R}
-turnstile and the

\xi_{v}\in\{-1,1\}
are i.i.d. Rademacher random variables.
12
12

12

Again, in Alon, Matias, and Szegedy’s analysis
[
1
]
, 4-wise independent hashing suffices to guarantee a good enough estimate but we need to assume they are i.i.d. here to talk about the exact distribution of the final state.
Now suppose the input vector is repeated
w
times on disjoint supports, i.e.,
\mathbf{x}_{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{nw}
with

\mathbf{x}_{w}(v)=\mathbf{x}(v\bmod n)
.
Then the final state of the sketch for
\mathbf{x}_{w}
would be

\displaystyle M_{w}=Q_{1}+Q_{2}+\cdots+Q_{w},

where the
(Q_{j})
are i.i.d. copies of
Q
. Note that
\mathbb{E}Q=0
and

\mathbb{V}Q=|\mathbf{x}|^{2}=\sum_{v\in[n]}\mathbf{x}(v)^{2}
. Thus as
w\to\infty
the normalized final state
M_{w}/\sqrt{w}
converges to a centered Gaussian random variable with variance
\sum_{v\in[n]}\mathbf{x}(v)^{2}
in distribution, by the central limit theorem. Thus, in the regime as
w\to\infty
, the
AMS
sketch eventually becomes
the same as a cell in the
Lévy-Tower

\sum_{v\in[n]}\left\langle X^{(v)}_{1},\mathbf{x}(v)\right\rangle
, where the
(X^{(v)})
are i.i.d. one-dimensional Wiener processes/Brownian motion and
X_{1}^{(v)}
is the value of the process at time
t=1
.
Of course, since a Wiener process/Brownian motion is self-similar, one does not need to sample the Lévy processes at exponentially spaced intervals; samples from
m
independent processes at time
t=1
suffice to approximate
\sum_{v\in[n]}\mathbf{x}(v)^{2}
with
O(1/m)
relative variance.
The characteristic exponent of the Wiener process/Brownian motion is the target function for the
F_{2}
-moment:
f(x)=|x|^{2}
.

Another illuminating example is Ganguly’s
[
25
]

F_{k}
-moment estimator for
k\geq 3
.
Ganguly randomly projects the elements and stores

S=\sum_{v\in[n]}\mathbf{x}(v)Z^{(v)}
where the
Z^{(v)}
are i.i.d. uniformly random roots of

x^{k}=1
on the complex unit circle.
The statistic
S^{k}

seems
to be a good estimator for the
F_{k}
-moment since
\mathbb{E}S^{k}=\sum_{v\in[n]}\mathbf{x}(v)^{k}
. However, since this random projection has finite variance, the normalized sum
S/\sqrt{\sum_{v\in[n]}|\mathbf{x}(v)|^{2}}

will converge to a complex Gaussian
as the input vector is replicated on disjoint supports.
In the limit, the only information remaining in the sketch pertains to the
F_{2}
-moment.
Indeed, one in fact needs the number of i.i.d. registers to
grow
polynomially
in the support-size for it to be able to estimate
F_{k}

[
25
]
.

By the generalized central limit theorem, if for some sequences
(a_{w})_{w\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(b_{w})_{w\in\mathbb{N}}
,
(M_{w}-b_{w})/a_{w}
converges to some non-degenerate random variable
Y
as
w\to\infty
, then
Y
has to be
\alpha
-stable for
\alpha\in(0,2]
. If in addition,
\mathbb{V}Q<\infty
, then
Y
is Gaussian.
Non-Gaussian stable distributions
will be discussed shortly,
in
Section

6.3
.

6.2
Multi-Level Subsampling and the Poisson Limit Theorem

Another important sketching technique is
subsampling
. The idea is to devise a sketch that works for
\Theta(m)
elements and then solve the generic case by subsampling the stream
at rates
2^{-k}
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
, one of which reduces it
down to
\Theta(m)
elements. Without loss of generality, suppose now we have
m
non-zero elements with distinct values
\mathbf{x}(1),\ldots,\mathbf{x}(m)
. Once again,
let
\mathbf{x}_{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{nw}

be
\mathbf{x}
repeated
w
times on disjoint supports.
To obtain a
\Theta(m)
-size set of subsamples, one needs to subsample
\mathbf{x}_{w}
with rate
1/w
. Let
Y_{w,j}
be the indicator that the
j
th copy of
\mathbf{x}(1)
is sampled where
\mathbb{E}Y_{w,j}=1/w
. The number of elements with value
\mathbf{x}(1)
in the subsampled set is

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{w}Y_{w,j}\to\mathrm{Poisson}(1)\text{ in distribution as $w\to\infty$}.

Here we have invoked the Poisson limit theorem (see
[
21
, Theorem 3.6.1]
)
which can be applied since

\mathbb{E}\sum_{j=1}^{w}Y_{w,j}=1
and
\max_{j=1}^{w}\mathbb{P}(Y_{w,j}\neq 0)=\frac{1}{w}\to 0
as
w\to\infty
. Similarly, the number of elements with value
\mathbf{x}(j)
is also
\mathrm{Poisson}(1)
and the occurrences of different values are independent. It is well known that such a
limiting distribution can be simulated algorithmically by duplicating
every element
a
\mathrm{Poisson}(1)
number of times (see
[
24
,
23
,
47
,
55
]
).
On the other hand, such limit distributions can be equivalently simulated by hashing each element to a Poisson process and then sampling at different times. Thus, all sketches based on subsampling can be simulated by the
Lévy-Tower

with the corresponding (compound) Poisson processes.

6.3
Stable Random Variables and Stable Processes

Whenever
Q
has a finite variance,
(Q_{1}+Q_{2}+\cdots+Q_{w})/\sqrt{w}
goes to Gaussian as
w\to\infty
. Nevertheless, with infinite variance,
Q
can lie in the domain of attraction of an
\alpha
-stable distribution for any
\alpha\in(0,2)
. Indeed, the use of stable random variables is another sketching technique that directly corresponds to Lévy processes, namely
\alpha
-stable processes. Similar to the Gaussian case (
Section

6.1
),
\alpha
-stable processes are self-similar so there is no need to store the whole tower with exponentially spaced sample times
2^{-k}
. Rather, it suffices to sample
m
independent processes at time
t=1
.

Indyk
[
29
]
uses one-dimensional
\alpha
-stable random variables for
\alpha\in(0,2]
to estimate the
F_{\alpha}
-moment,
and indeed, the characteristic exponent of the
\alpha
-stable process is
f(x)=|x|^{\alpha}
.
Ganguly, Bansal, and Dube
[
28
]
consider the higher dimensional case, where each element has
d
attributes. We now show how to reconstruct
[
28
]
’s sketch from the perspective of Lévy processes. The target function is

\displaystyle f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R},\quad f(x)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}|x_{j}|^{p}\right)^{q},

where
p\in(0,2],q\in(0,1]
.
Such
f
-moments are called
F_{p,q}
hybrid moments in
[
28
]
.
By the subordination theorem (
Theorem

8
; see
[
50
, page 197]
),
this function is the characteristic exponent of a vector of
d
independent
p
-stable processes that are subordinated by a common
q
-stable subordinator.
That is, the Lévy process is precisely
Y_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\displaystyle Y_{t}=(X_{Z_{t}}^{[1]},\ldots,X_{Z_{t}}^{[d]}),

where
Z
is a
q
-stable subordinator and the

are i.i.d.

-stable processes. Note that since

is
p

.
Thus, sampling
Y
at time 1 yields

\displaystyle Y_{1}\sim Z_{1}^{1/p}\left(X_{1}^{[1]},\ldots,X_{1}^{[d]}\right).

Take the inner product and we have, for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\left\langle x,Y_{1}\right\rangle=Z_{1}^{1/p}\sum_{j=1}^{d}x_{j}X_{1}^{[j]},

which is exactly the random projection defined by the update algorithm of Ganguly, Bansal, and Dube
[
28
]
.

7
Tractability and Lévy Processes

The most basic complexity-theoretic question in any model of computation is to separate the
tractable
from the
intractable
, e.g.,
to characterize the class

\mathbf{P}
in Turing-machine complexity.
Braverman and Ostrovsky
[
9
]
defined an analogue of
\mathbf{P}
for
f
-moment estimation.

Definition 10

(Tractability)
.

A function
f:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
is
tractable

if it is possible to
(1\pm\epsilon)
-approximate
the
f
-moment of a vector

\mathbf{x}\in\{-n,\ldots,0,\ldots,n\}^{n}

updated in a turnstile stream, with a
\operatorname{poly}(\epsilon^{-1},\log n)
-bit sketch.

Braverman, Chestnut, Woodruff, and Yang
[
8
]
relaxed this definition to include
n^{o(1)}\operatorname{poly}(\epsilon^{-1})
-bit sketches, which slightly broadens the class of tractable functions, but does not seem to affect its character very much.
Braverman and Ostrovsky
[
9
]

characterized the set of tractable
f
that are monotonically non-decreasing on
[0,\infty)
,
and Braverman, Chestnut, Woodruff, and Yang
[
8
]

gave a nearly complete characterization of all
tractable functions—monotone or not—excluding what they termed “nearly periodic functions.”
As our results apply to
f
-moments where
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
and
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
, we expand the definition of tractability to explicitly include the domain.

Definition 11

(
M
-Tractability)
.

Let
M
be a commutative monoid.
A function
f:M\to\mathbb{C}
is
tractable

if for any parameter
\epsilon>0
, it is possible to
(1\pm\epsilon)
-approximate
the
f
-moment of a vector
\mathbf{x}\in M^{n}

updated in an
M
-turnstile stream (
Definition

1
), with a
\operatorname{poly}(\epsilon^{-1},\log n)
-bit sketch.
(When
M
is discrete, say
\mathbb{Z}
, we must assume some bound
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{\infty}<n
.
When
M
is continuous let us additionally suppose that there are
\operatorname{poly}(n)
updates, and each update is presented to
O(\log n)
bits of precision.)
Define
\mathcal{T}[M]
to be the set of all
M
-tractable functions.

The present work gives us a completely new way to approach the tractability question.
We have shown that the
Lévy-Tower
estimates
every function
f
that is the characteristic exponent of a Lévy process, and such exponents are characterized by the Lévy-Khintchine representation theorem.
In this section we investigate the class of
tractable functions captured by the Braverman et al.
[
8
]

sketch and the Lévy-Khintchine-representable functions captured by the
Lévy-Tower
sketch.
The Lévy-Khintchine-class includes many multidimensional functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
outside the scope of Braverman et al.
[
8
]
,
so we shall mainly investigate each classes’s coverage of
\mathcal{T}[\mathbb{Z}]
and
implications for
\mathcal{T}[\mathbb{R}]
.
In the remainder of this section we present the following observations and results.

I.

We show that some easy tests for

7.1
I: Intractability and Non-Lévy-Khintchine-representability

We give a comparison between one-dimensional real characteristic exponents and existing sketching lower bounds based on
communication complexity lower bounds.
Such exponents can be written as
f(x)=Ax^{2}+\int_{0}^{\infty}(1-\cos(xs))\,\nu(ds)
, since
f
being real implies that
\nu
is symmetric; see
Remark

1
.

Lemma 12
.

Let
f(x)=Ax^{2}+\int_{0}^{\infty}(1-\cos(xs))\,\nu(ds)
. The following statements are true.

•

f(x)\geq 0
for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
. [Commentary: functions with both positive and negative values require
\Omega(\operatorname{poly}(n))
-size sketches for constant factor approximations
[
12
]
.]

•

For any
z\in\mathbb{Z}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
f(zx)\leq z^{2}f(x)
. [Commentary: functions increasing faster than quadratic require
\Omega(\operatorname{poly}(n))
-size sketches for constant factor approxiamtions
[
1
,
4
]
.]

•

For any
z\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
f(z)=0
if and only if
f(x+z)=f(x),\forall x\in\mathbb{R}
, i.e.,
f
is periodic with period
z
. [Commentary: functions that have zeros other than the origin require
\Omega(\operatorname{poly}(n))
-size sketches, unless the function
is periodic with period
\min\{z>0:f(z)=0\}

[
12
]
.]

Proof.

The first statement is obvious. For the second statement, it suffices to prove that
with
y=xs
,

(1-\cos(zy))\leq z^{2}(1-\cos(y)),

for any
y\in\mathbb{R},z\in\mathbb{Z}
.
When
z=0
, the statement trivially holds. Without loss of generality, assume
z\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and let
g(y)=z^{2}(1-\cos(y))-(1-\cos(zy))
. Check that
g^{\prime}(y)=z^{2}\sin(y)-z\sin(zy)
and
g^{\prime\prime}(y)=z^{2}\cos(y)-z^{2}\cos(zy)
. Clearly for
y\in[0,\pi/z]
, we have
\cos(zy)\leq\cos(y)
and thus
g^{\prime\prime}(y)\geq 0
. Note
g^{\prime}(0)=0
and therefore
g(y)\geq 0
for
y\in[0,\pi/z]
. Now consider
y\in[\pi/z,\pi]
,
z^{2}(1-\cos(y))\geq z^{2}(1-\cos(\pi/z))\geq 2\geq 1-\cos(zy)
, which implies
g(y)\geq 0
on
[0,\pi]
. Thus we have
g(y)\geq 0
for
[\pi,2\pi]
too by symmetry. The statement is thus proved since
g(y)
is periodic in
2\pi
.

For the third statement,

f(z)=0
necessarily implies that
\sigma=0
and the measure
\nu
concentrates on
2\pi j/z
for
j\in\mathbb{Z}
. Thus
f(x)=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}(1-\cos(2\pi jx/z))\nu(\{2\pi j/z\})
is periodic with period
z
.
∎

7.2
II: Tractability over Integers vs. Reals

It is known that
F_{p}
is intractable for
p>2

[
11
,
26
,
41
,
56
]
.
Nonetheless, there are tractable
f
that grow slightly
super-quadratically, such as
f(x)=x^{2}\log^{c}(1+|x|)
, for
c=O(1)
; see
[
9
,
8
]
.
To be more specific, such
f
are tractable over the integers,

but not the reals
, as we now prove.

Theorem 17
.

\mathcal{T}[\mathbb{Z}]\neq\mathcal{T}[\mathbb{R}]
. Specifically,
f(x)=x^{2}\log(1+|x|)\in\mathcal{T}[\mathbb{Z}]-\mathcal{T}[\mathbb{R}]
.

Proof.

When
\mathbf{x}\in\{-n,\ldots,n\}^{n}

it is known that

(1\pm\epsilon)
-approximating
F_{3}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j}|\mathbf{x}(j)|^{3}

requires
\tilde{\Omega}_{\epsilon}(n^{1/3})

space
[
11
,
26
,
41
,
56
]
.
Map
\mathbf{x}
to
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{x}/n^{2}
.
Then

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{y})=\sum_{j}f(\mathbf{y}(j))=\sum_{j}\mathbf{y}(j)^{2}\log(1+|\mathbf{y}(j)|)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\frac{\mathbf{x}(j)^{2}}{n^{4}}\log\left(1+\frac{|\mathbf{x}(j)|}{n^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\left(\frac{|\mathbf{x}(j)|^{3}}{n^{6}}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{|\mathbf{x}(j)|^{4}}{n^{8}}+O\left(\frac{|\mathbf{x}(j)|^{5}}{n^{10}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\in[(1-1/n)n^{-6}F_{3}(\mathbf{x}),n^{-6}F_{3}(\mathbf{x})].

Thus any
(1\pm\epsilon)
-approximation of
f(\mathbf{y})
yields a
(1\pm(\epsilon+1/n))
approximation of
F_{3}(\mathbf{x})
.
∎

7.3
III: Nearly Periodic Functions

Braverman, Chestnut, Woodruff, and Yang
[
8
, §5]

gave the following example of a
nearly periodic function that cannot be
solved in their
F_{2}
-heavy hitter-based framework, but can be estimated using
ad hoc
algorithmic tricks,
in
O(\epsilon^{-8}\log^{15}n)
space.

Definition 12

(
g_{np}
, a nearly periodic function
[
8
, §5]
)
.

For
x\in\mathbb{N}
,
g_{np}(x)=2^{-\tau(x)}
, where
\tau(x)=\max\{j\in\mathbb{N}:2^{j}|x\}
, i.e., the position of the first “1” in the binary representation of
x
.

The reason that
g_{np}
cannot be tracked by finding
F_{2}
-heavy hitters is that
g_{np}(x)
can occasionally become polynomially small in
x
. In particular, for
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
g_{np}(2^{k})=2^{-k}
.
We now demonstrate how the function
g_{np}
can be estimated
directly
using the
Lévy-Tower
.

To prove that
g_{np}\in\mathcal{T}[\mathbb{Z}]

we may assume that
\mathbf{x}\in\{-n,\ldots,0,\ldots,n\}^{n}
,
so it suffices to consider a
2^{w}
-periodic version of
g_{np}
with
w>\log n
.

Definition 13

(
2^{w}
-periodic
g_{np}
)
.

For
x\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle g_{np,w}(x)

\displaystyle=g_{np}(x\operatorname{mod}2^{w})

We show that, indeed,
g_{np}
corresponds to a Lévy process,
specifically a compound Poisson process.

Figure 5
:
Left:
g_{np,5}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{31}\frac{2^{2\tau(j)+1}+1}{1536}(1-\cos(2\pi jx/32)).
The black dots mark the values at integer
x
s. Right: The jump distribution of the compound Poisson process
X
with characteristic exponent
g_{np,5}
. Such nearly periodic functions do not fit in the
F_{2}
-heavy-hitter based framework in
[
8
]
. Nevertheless, one may compute the corresponding Lévy process and apply the
Lévy-Tower
.

Lemma 13
.

Fix
w\in\mathbb{N}
. For any
x\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle g_{np,w}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{2^{w}-1}\frac{2^{2\tau(j)+1}+1}{3\cdot 2^{2w-1}}(1-\cos(2\pi jx/2^{w})).

Proof.

This expression was derived by taking the Fourier transform of
g_{np,w}

over
\mathbb{Z}/2^{w}\mathbb{Z}
but we shall prove the lemma without going through Fourier transforms.
When
x=0
,
g_{np,w}(x)=0
as all the cosines are 1.
When
x\in(0,2^{w})
write it as
x=y2^{r}
where
y
is odd,
so our goal is to show that

g_{np,w}(x)=2^{-r}
.

\displaystyle g_{np,w}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{2^{w}-1}\frac{2^{2\tau(j)+1}+1}{3\cdot 2^{2w-1}}(1-\cos(2\pi jx/2^{w}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3\cdot 2^{2w-1}}\sum_{s=0}^{w-1}(2^{2s+1}+1)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=z2^{s}\\
z\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}(1-\cos(2\pi yz/2^{w-r-s}))

Now when
s\geq w-r
the second sum vanishes as all cosines are 1. Moreover,
z\in\{1,3,\ldots,2^{w-s}-1\}
takes on
2^{w-s-1}
values,
and since
y
is coprime to every power of 2, the second sum runs through
the
2^{w-r-s-1}
odd residues of
2^{w-r-s}
,
2^{r}
times each. Continuing, we have

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3\cdot 2^{2w-1}}\sum_{s=0}^{w-r-1}(2^{2s+1}+1)\cdot 2^{r}\sum_{z\in\{1,3,\ldots,2^{w-r-s}-1\}}(1-\cos(2\pi z/2^{w-r-s}))

When
w-r-s>1
the cosines of the odd residues sum to 0, whereas when
w-r-s=1
there is only one odd residue and
1-\cos(2\pi/2)=2
.

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3\cdot 2^{2w-1}}\sum_{s=0}^{w-r-1}(2^{2s+1}+1)\cdot 2^{r}\cdot\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}2^{w-r-s-1}&\text{ when $w-r-s>1$,}\\
2&\text{ when $w-r-s=1$.}\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3\cdot 2^{2w-1}}\left(\sum_{s=0}^{w-r-2}(2^{2s+1}+1)2^{w-s-1}+(2^{2(w-r-1)+1}+1)2^{r+1}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3\cdot 2^{2w-1}}\left(2^{w}\sum_{s=0}^{w-r-2}(2^{s}+2^{-s-1})+(2^{2w-r}+2^{r+1})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3\cdot 2^{2w-1}}\left(2^{w}(2^{w-r-1}-2^{-(w-r-1)})+(2^{2w-r}+2^{r+1})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{3\cdot 2^{2w-r-1}}{3\cdot 2^{2w-1}}=2^{-r}.

∎

By Lévy-Khintchine,
g_{np,w}
is the characteristic function of a compound Poisson process

(X_{t})
with jump rate

\sum_{j=1}^{2^{w}-1}\frac{2^{2\tau(j)+1}+1}{3\cdot 2^{2w-1}}=\frac{2^{2w}-1}{3\cdot 2^{2w-1}},

where the jump distribution is symmetric, with jump
J
distributed as

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(J=2\pi j/2^{w})=\mathbb{P}(J=-2\pi j/2^{w})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\frac{2^{2\tau(j)+1}+1}{2^{2w}-1},\quad\text{for $j=1,\ldots 2^{w}-1.$}

Moreover, the parameter
w
for the random jump induced by
g_{np,w}
has a neat algorithmic interpretation as the max recursion depth if one implements each random jump by a recursive algorithm
14
14

14

(Halt with prob. 1/2; go left and repeat with prob. 1/4; and go right and repeat with prob. 1/4; see
Fig.

5
).
.
The case where
w\to\infty
corresponds to the algorithm without depth limit.
Let
\mathbb{B}\subset[0,1)
be the set of all real fractions with finite binary representations. Note that the jump distribution converges pointwisely to

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(J=2\pi x)=\mathbb{P}(J=-2\pi x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}2^{1-2\tau_{*}(x)}=2^{-\tau_{*}(x)},

for any
x\in\mathbb{B}
, where
\tau_{*}(x)

is the length of the representation of
x
, e.g.,

\tau_{*}(3/8)=\tau_{*}((0.011)_{2})=3
.
This jump distribution can be simulated in unbounded input streams as follows.
Let
A=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}A[j]2^{-j}

be uniform in
[0,1]
,

T\sim\operatorname{Geometric}(1/2)
,
and
\xi\in\{-1,1\}
be Rademacher.
Then the jump
J
is distributed as:

\displaystyle J\sim 2\pi\,\xi\left(\sum_{j=1}^{T-1}A[j]2^{-j}+2^{-T}\right).

7.4
IV: The Fourier-Hahn-Lévy Method

The example of
g_{np}
shows that it is possible to
systematically
sketch nearly periodic functions by sketching periodic functions and letting the period go to infinity. (See Wang
[
54
]
for other examples.)
Given the success of the
Lévy-Tower
in estimating both
“standard”
f
-moments and exotic ones like the
g_{np}
-moment, it is natural to conjecture that the Lévy-Khintchine representation theorem actually defines
the set of tractable functions
f
.
Unfortunately, reality is not quite this clean, and indeed, this simplistic conjecture is
absolutely false
.
We begin by giving a simple function
f
that is obviously tractable, and obviously not Lévy-Khintchine-representable.
Nonetheless, the conjecture can be salvaged by encoding
f
as the difference between
two Lévy-Khintchine-representable functions,
via what we call the

Fourier-Hahn-Lévy
method.

Example 5

(The 0-1-5 Problem)
.

Consider the function
f:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
, where

f(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\text{ if $x=0$,}\\
1&\text{ if $|x|=1$,}\\
5&\text{ if $|x|>1$.}\end{array}\right.

The
f
-moment is easy to estimate to within a
1\pm\epsilon
factor in
O(\epsilon^{-2}\operatorname{polylog}(n))
space,
using
F_{0}
-sampling
[
19
]
.
However, no Lévy process has its characteristic function equal to
f
, for otherwise we would have
f(2\cdot 1)\leq 2^{2}f(1)
by
Lemma

12
,
which implies
5\leq 4
, a contradiction.

7.4.1
The Periodic Fourier-Hahn-Lévy Method

Let us now extend our method to handle
the
0-1-5 problem
and others.
Suppose that
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{\infty}<n
.
It suffices to consider a periodic version

f_{p}
of
f
with period
p\geq 2n

that agrees with
f
on the range
\{-n,\ldots,n\}
. For example,
in the case of the 0-1-5 problem we would
define
f_{p}
as

f_{p}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\text{ if $|x|_{p}=0$,}\\
1&\text{ if $|x|_{p}=1$,}\\
5&\text{ if $|x|_{p}>1$,}\end{array}\right.

where
|x|_{p}=\min\{x\operatorname{mod}p,p-x\operatorname{mod}p\}
.
The three-step
Fourier-Hahn-Lévy method

is executed as follows.

Fourier Transform.

Decompose
f(x)-f(0)=\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}(1-\cos(2\pi jx/p))\hat{f}(j)
where the coefficient
\hat{f}(j)
can be computed by taking the Fourier transform of
f
.
15
15

15

We have
f(x)-f(0)=\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}\tilde{f}(j)(e^{2\pi ijx/p}-1)
.
Since
f
is real and symmetric
\tilde{f}
is real and
\tilde{f}(j)=\tilde{f}(p-j)
,
so we can pair up the summands
j
and
p-j
as follows:

\tilde{f}(j)(e^{2\pi ijx/p}-1)+\tilde{f}(p-j)(e^{-2\pi ijx/p}-1)=-2\tilde{f}(j)(1-\cos(2\pi jx/p))=-2\tilde{f}(p-j)(1-\cos(2\pi jx/p))
.
Setting
\hat{f}(j)=-\tilde{f}(j)
we have
f(x)-f(0)=\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}(1-\cos(2\pi jx/p))\hat{f}(j)
.

Note that
\hat{f}
is real since we have assumed
f
to be real and symmetric.

Hahn Decomposition.

Decompose
\hat{f}=\hat{f}_{+}-\hat{f}_{-}
where
\hat{f}_{+}
and
\hat{f}_{-}
are non-negative real functions.

Lévy Process Simulation.

Define functions
f_{+},f_{-}
as the inverse Fourier transform of
\hat{f}_{+},\hat{f}_{-}
respectively.
Since the Fourier transform is linear, we have
f=f_{+}-f_{-}
. By construction,
f_{+},f_{-}
are each characteristic exponents of some Lévy process. Estimate those function moments separately with
Lévy-Tower
sketches parameterized by
m
,
and take their difference to yield an estimate of the
f
-moment.

Lemma 14
.

Given any symmetric function

f:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
with
f(0)=0
and any stream

\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
,
let
\widehat{f(\mathbf{x})}
be the estimator derived from

Then
\widehat{f(\mathbf{x})}
has error

\displaystyle\left|\widehat{f(\mathbf{x})}-f(\mathbf{x})\right|=O\left(\frac{|f_{+}(\mathbf{x})|+|f_{-}(\mathbf{x})|}{\sqrt{m}}\right),

with probability at least 98/100.

7.4.2
The Approximate Fourier-Hahn-Lévy Method

At this point we realize that it is counterproductive to consider only
exact

decompositions of
f=f_{+}-f_{-}
. In order to
(1\pm\epsilon)
-approximate
the
f
-moment

f(\mathbf{x})

with a
\operatorname{poly}(\epsilon^{-1},\log n)
-bit sketch,
it suffices to find a function

g=g_{+}-g_{-}

such that
g_{+},g_{-}
are
Lévy-Khintchine-representable
and for all relevant
|x|<n
,

g(x)\in[(1-\epsilon)f(x),(1+\epsilon)f(x)]

is a good approximation of
f

and

g_{+}(x)+g_{-}(x)\leq\operatorname{poly}(\epsilon^{-1},\log n)f(x)

is not
too
much larger than
f
.
(It is not necessary that
g
be periodic, nor that it have any prescribed behavior outside of
|x|<n
.)
Rather than do this analytically, we can generate
g,g_{+},g_{-}
by solving
a linear program encoding the relevant constraints.
Let
(\omega_{k})
be a sufficiently dense set
of frequencies.
For approximating
f
,
\omega_{k}=(1+\epsilon)^{-k}
for
k\in\{-\Omega(\epsilon^{-1}),\ldots,0,\ldots,\log_{1+\epsilon}n+O(1)\}

suffices.
Let
u_{k},v_{k}
be the non-negative coefficients of
g_{+},g_{-}
corresponding to
\omega_{k}
, and
u_{*},v_{*}
be the coefficients of
x^{2}
. In other words,
g_{+},g_{-}
are

g_{+}(x)=u_{*}x^{2}+\sum_{k}u_{k}(1-\cos(\omega_{k}x))\qquad\text{and}\qquad g_{-}(x)=v_{*}x^{2}+\sum_{k}v_{k}(1-\cos(\omega_{k}x)).

The linear program is then:

Minimize
\rho
subject to:

\displaystyle\forall x\in\{1,\ldots,n\}.\quad(u_{*}-v_{*})x^{2}+\sum_{k}(u_{k}-v_{k})(1-\cos(\omega_{k}x))
