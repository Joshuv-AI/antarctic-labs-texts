Targeting mesothelin in cancer
Authors: Cem Elbi
Published: 2023

Main text

Mesothelin is a tumor-differentiation antigen discovered more than 30 years ago, although the development of novel and effective therapies targeting mesothelin still draws considerable interest in the oncology drug discovery and development field.1,2,3 In the December 19th issue of Molecular Therapy Oncolytics, a new study by Sun and colleagues reports the isolation and characterization of a novel human antibody heavy-chain variable (VH) domain named 3C9, targeting mesothelin as an antibody-drug conjugate (ADC).4 To extend the half-life and assess the tissue distribution kinetics and anti-tumor activity of the VH domain, the authors systematically first fused it with human immunoglobulin G1 (IgG1) Fc protein and then conjugated it with a toxic payload to generate VH-Fc 3C9-MMAE ADC. Furthermore, the authors determined the crystal structure of full-length mesothelin and its complex with VH 3C9 to gain insights into the binding sites and their interaction with the novel antibody domain.4 The structure of mesothelin was similar to the previously reported study.5

Mesothelin is normally expressed on the surface of mesothelial cells in the pleura, pericardium, and peritoneum. However, it is overexpressed in a variety of cancers including malignant mesothelioma, ovarian cancer, pancreatic cancer, breast cancer (particularly the triple-negative subtype), lung adenocarcinoma, biliary cancer, and gastric cancer.2,3,6 The limited expression in normal tissues and overexpression in tumor tissues potentially resulting in reduced nonspecific toxicity makes the mesothelin an attractive target for oncology drug discovery and development. Mesothelin-targeting therapies in development include monoclonal antibodies, immunotoxins, ADCs, vaccines, and chimeric antigens receptor-T (CAR-T) cells. To date, clinical trials on anti-mesothelin therapies show good or manageable safety profiles but limited clinical activity.2,3,6,7 Here, authors conjugated the novel anti-mesothelin human VH-Fc 3C9 to the anti-mitotic agent named monomethyl auristatin E (MMAE) to generate VH-Fc 3C9-MMAE ADC.4 MMAE has been utilized as toxic payload by other targeted ADCs including the approved ADCs by the US Food and Drug Administration such as enfortumab vedotin and tisotumab vedotin.

Mesothelin is a 41-kD glycophosphatidylinositol (GPI)-linked cell-surface glycoprotein. The mesothelin gene encodes a 71-kD precursor protein. It is cleaved by the furin endoprotease in the trans-Golgi apparatus to release soluble megakaryocyte potentiating factor (MPF) from the N-terminal region and the cell-membrane-bound mature mesothelin.2,3,6 Mature mesothelin can also be released as soluble mesothelin-related peptide (SMRP) by the action of proteases, such as tumor necrosis factor-α-converting enzyme (TACE/ADAM17). SMRP can be detected and quantified in serum and peritoneal and pleural fluid.2,3,6 Although SMRP might serve as a potential noninvasive biomarker, it does not seem to have the sensitivity or specificity to predict outcome or monitor tumor response in mesothelioma and other cancers.

Crystal structure of full-length mesothelin shows a compact, right-handed solenoid with twenty-four short helices and connecting loops.5 It contains seven ARM/HEAT repeats, with each repeat comprising two or three helices. The extracellular domain of mesothelin contains N-terminal region I and region II and C-terminal region III. Region I is the membrane-distal region, which can also interact with other proteins, and this may interfere or compete with the binding and function of the region I anti-mesothelin antibodies.5 Mucin, MUC16 (CA125), also binds to this region and is expressed in malignant mesothelioma and ovarian cancer. Both mesothelin and MUC16 often express simultaneously in cancer cells, inducing cell-to-cell adhesion.2,3 Any anti-mesothelin therapy targeting the N-terminal region will potentially compete with MUC16 as well. Interestingly, the novel human antibody domain VH 3C9 reported here by Sun and colleagues also targets the N-terminal region I of mesothelin.4 The authors did not show any competitive binding between MUC16 and VH 3C9; however, the competition for mesothelin binding was not observed between VH 3C9 and MORAb-009.4 MORAb-009 (amatuximab) is a chimeric high-affinity monoclonal IgG1/k antibody and binds to the same N-terminal region I. Amatuximab plays role in the inhibition of cell adhesion and antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity in vitro. In vivo, amatuximab shows anti-tumor activity in tumor xenografts with mesothelin expression. However, in phase 1 and phase 2 studies, amatuximab showed a limited clinical activity.2,6

Tomar and colleagues designed a YP218 monoclonal antibody targeting a membrane-proximal region III of mesothelin since the anti-mesothelin antibodies targeting the membrane-distal N-terminal region I can compete with other cellular factors.8 Using anti-mesothelin hYP218 in the development of CAR-T cells, they showed anti-tumor activity both in vitro and in vivo with increased persistence and tumor infiltration.

Recent studies also revealed the importance of the modulated and differential shedding of mesothelin in tumor cells by families of proteases such as ADAM, MMPs (matrix metalloproteases), and BACE (β-secretase).9 Shed mesothelin present in tumors and body fluids (blood, ascites, and pleural fluid) could function as a trap or release the antibody-based therapeutic agents leading to reduced anti-tumor activity. Monoclonal antibodies targeting the C-terminal region III of mesothelin can block this shedding and show anti-tumor activity in mice when used in the development of CAR-T cells.9 Future clinical trials will potentially assess whether these key features will translate into an increased clinical activity for new anti-mesothelin therapeutics.

The functional role of mesothelin is not well established. It is nonessential in normal tissues since the mesothelin knockout mice do not show any particular phenotype or abnormalities in development, reproduction, and platelet cells in the blood.10 Mesothelin does not have an intracellular binding domain or a kinase domain. Accordingly, it does not function as a typical oncogene. However, mesothelin activates downstream oncogenic pathways for cell proliferation, cell survival, and invasion.2,3,6 The mechanisms of activation of some signaling pathways involve FOXO3 activation and DKK1 inhibition leading to the increase in MMP for cell migration and upregulation of ERK1/2 and AKT resulting in the inhibition of anti-apoptotic pathways through Bad, Bax, and Bim. Here, Sun and colleagues showed that anti-mesothelin VH-Fc 3C9 MMAE induces G2/M-phase cell-cycle arrest and apoptosis in tumor cells with mesothelin expression.4 The authors also demonstrated the anti-tumor activity of the novel ADC in a mouse xenograft model with mesothelin expression. The treatment of mice with a 10 mg/kg dose resulting in more tumor growth inhibition and body weight lost compared to a 2 mg/kg dose suggests a narrow window of anti-tumor activity versus toxicity. The authors proposed the optimization of conjugation process to enhance the activity and reduce the toxicity of the 3C9 antibody domain as one of the future strategies. Sun and colleagues postulate that the novel anti-mesothelin VH-Fc 3C9 MMAE ADC with high affinity, specificity, and anti-tumor activity along with the full-length structure of the mesothelin-3C9 complex show a potential for further development. It will be interesting to see how these key features will translate into an improved clinical development profile in future studies.
