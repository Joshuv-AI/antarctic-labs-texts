Calcific tendinitis with intraosseous migration mimicking malignancy
Authors: Kameel Khabaz, Cyrus Safinia, Rashed Alananzeh, Varand Ghazikhanian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCalcific tendinitis with intraosseous migration represents a diagnostic pitfall that frequently simulates aggressive bone neoplasms on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), potentially triggering unnecessary oncologic workup. The authors report the case of a 26-year-old woman on immunosuppressive therapy presenting with acute, debilitating left shoulder pain. Initial MRI demonstrated a permeative-appearing process of the proximal humeral metaphysis with extensive bone marrow edema and cortical erosion, prompting a referral to orthopedic oncology. Subsequent multimodality correlation and computed tomography (CT)-guided fine-needle aspiration confirmed focal hydroxyapatite deposition without evidence of malignancy or infection. This case highlights the "edema-dominant" presentation of the resorptive phase and underscores the necessity of radiographic or CT correlation to identify MRI-occult mineralization. Recognizing enthesis-centered distribution and smooth cortical remodeling allows for confident conservative management of this self-limiting condition.Keywords: Calcific tendinitis, Diagnostic errors, Magnetic resonance imaging, Bone tumors

Introduction

Calcific tendinitis is a common musculoskeletal disorder characterized by the deposition of calcium hydroxyapatite crystals within tendons affecting up to 40% of symptomatic shoulders [1]. While its pathogenesis remains debated, the condition follows a predictable evolution through precalcific, calcific (formative and resting), and resorptive phases [1]. The resorptive phase represents the clinical ``danger zone,'' characterized by excruciating pain and limited range of motion due to increased intratendinous pressure, neovascularization, and a robust inflammatory response [2]. During this stage, liquefied calcium crystals can migrate into adjacent bursa, muscle, or, more deceptively, into the subcortical bone [1].

Intraosseous migration often creates a ``pseudo-aggressive'' appearance on radiographs and MRI that mimics primary bone sarcomas, lymphoma, or osteomyelitis [1,3,4]. This diagnostic challenge is amplified when a profound ``edema-dominant'' pattern on MRI eclipses a small or fragmented calcific nidus [6]. Extensive intramedullary tracking of calcium can further confound the differential [5]. These alarming imaging features frequently trigger oncologic referrals and biopsy to exclude malignancy [7].

Recognizing this “great mimicker” is critical to avoid invasive management for a condition that typically responds to nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) or image-guided lavage [8]. We describe a case of intraosseous migration in an immunosuppressed patient, illustrating the imaging features that distinguish this benign entity from malignancy.

Case presentation

A 26-year-old woman with a history of uveitis managed with adalimumab and methotrexate presented with acute, severe left shoulder pain and restricted range of motion. There was no history of trauma. Physical examination revealed exquisite tenderness over the greater tuberosity. Laboratory studies showed an elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (96 mm/hr, reference ≤ 25 mm/hr) and C-reactive protein (2.6 mg/dL, reference < 0.8 mg/dL), raising concern for an inflammatory or infectious process given her immunosuppressed state.

Radiographs and CT of the left shoulder were obtained concurrently with the MRI at initial presentation. MRI demonstrated a permeative-appearing signal abnormality within the proximal humeral metaphysis and epiphysis. Coronal T1-weighted sequences showed ill-defined marrow replacement, while T2-weighted sequences, including steady-state free precession (SSFP) and true fast imaging with steady-state precession (TRUFI), demonstrated extensive, high-signal marrow edema with associated cortical indistinctness (Figs. 1A–B). A focal signal void was noted at the infraspinatus insertion on the posterior greater tuberosity, associated with perifascial edema (Figs. 1C–D). In the context of the aggressive MRI appearance, radiographs and CT were interpreted as showing an infiltrative lesion with focal cortical destruction. Based predominantly on the aggressive MRI findings and the patient's medication profile, orthopedic oncology was consulted to rule out malignancy or atypical osteomyelitis.

To reach a definitive diagnosis, CT-guided fine-needle aspiration was performed (Fig. 2). Cytology revealed mixed inflammatory cells and focal calcifications; core biopsy showed bony remodeling and dystrophic calcification without evidence of malignancy or fungal/bacterial organisms. Retrospective review of radiographs obtained at initial presentation identified a faint, cloud-like opacity near the greater tuberosity (Figs. 3A–B). Retrospective review of axial CT also obtained at initial presentation confirmed the diagnosis, showing focal cortical erosion with reactive sclerosis and high-attenuation calcific deposits tracking into the marrow (Fig. 3C).

Internal (A) and external (B) rotation radiographs obtained at initial presentation demonstrate a faint, cloud-like calcific density (arrow) adjacent to the greater tuberosity, characteristic of the resorptive phase of calcific tendinitis. (C) Axial CT confirms the diagnosis, showing reactive sclerosis and high-attenuation calcific deposits tracking into the subcortical marrow.

The patient was managed conservatively with activity modification and analgesics. A two-month follow-up MRI showed persistent marrow edema and progressive cortical scalloping, despite clinical improvement (Fig. 4). By eight months, repeat MRI demonstrated marked improvement of the marrow edema and stabilization of the cortical changes (Fig. 5), correlating with the patient’s full clinical recovery.

Two-month follow-up MRI imaging. (A) Axial and (B) sagittal T2-weighted fat-saturated images show persistent, though decreased marrow edema and a residual signal void within a subcortical cyst at the greater tuberosity. (C) Sagittal T1-weighted image demonstrates an interval increase in the extent of smooth cortical scalloping/erosion despite the patient's symptomatic improvement, representing a paradoxical radiologic-clinical dissociation.

Eight-month follow-up MRI imaging. (A) Axial and (B) sagittal PD fat-saturated and (C) coronal T1-weighted images demonstrate near-complete resolution of the previously seen bone marrow edema and stabilization of the cortical remodeling, as well as resolved perifascial and peritendinous edema, confirming the self-limiting nature of the inflammatory process.

Discussion

Calcific tendinitis with intraosseous migration represents a diagnostic pitfall that frequently simulates aggressive bone neoplasms on MRI, potentially triggering unnecessary biopsies and oncologic referrals [6,7,10]. While calcific tendinitis is a common musculoskeletal disorder found in up to 40% of symptomatic shoulders, intraosseous migration during the resorptive phase creates an alarming "pseudo-aggressive" imaging features [5,6]. This occurs when increased intratendinous pressure forces liquefied calcium hydroxyapatite through the cortical shell and into the subcortical marrow, initiating a robust inflammatory response.

The initial MRI in our patient demonstrated an "edema-dominant" pattern, where the volume of reactive marrow signal and perilesional soft tissue edema was disproportionately large compared to the small calcific nidus. In a 26-year-old patient, the combination of marrow signal abnormality, surrounding soft tissue edema, and destructive cortical changes is a classic "red herring" for malignancy. Specifically, the imaging features raised significant suspicion for sarcoma or lymphoma, necessitating an urgent orthopedic oncology consultation. The patient’s immunosuppressive therapy also placed atypical osteomyelitis on the differential.

Standard MRI carries a reported sensitivity of only 59% for detecting these focal calcifications. The signal void of hydroxyapatite is frequently indistinguishable from tissue interface artifacts or volume averaging with low-signal fibrocartilage [10]. To avoid misdiagnosis, several key MRI features help distinguish calcific tendinitis from malignancy:

•Anatomic centering: The pathology is centered at the enthesis (eg, the rotator cuff footprint) [5,6]. A process centered at the greater tuberosity in an acutely symptomatic patient should always prompt a search for a tendon-driven etiology.

Given MRI’s limitations, radiographic or CT correlation is mandatory. Radiography can reveal amorphous, cloud-like densities that are nearly invisible on MRI, while CT identifies subtle mineralization, cortical involvement, and compensatory bony sclerosis [11]. In this patient, close retrospective review of axial CT was critical in narrowing the differential, as it demonstrated sclerosis and hyperdense foci representing calcific deposits rather than the solid soft-tissue component expected in a sarcoma.

The natural history of calcific tendinitis is inherently dynamic, and clinical context often serves as a vital diagnostic tie-breaker. While primary bone tumors typically present with insidious, progressive pain, calcific tendinitis frequently affects the 30-50 age demographic with an acute, debilitating onset of symptoms [10]. During the resorptive phase, serial MRI often demonstrates a rapid resolution of marrow edema within weeks to months. However, as illustrated in this case, follow-up imaging may reveal a radiologic-clinical dissociation, where cortical erosions appear to progress despite significant symptomatic improvement. This paradoxical worsening is a recognized feature of the resorptive phase and should not prompt concern for malignancy if the clinical trajectory is favorable [5]. Per American College of Radiology (ACR) Bone-RADS guidelines, recognizing these hallmarks allows for a “Low Risk” (Bone-RADS 3) designation. In such cases, short-term surveillance (6-8 weeks) is often more appropriate than immediate biopsy, as calcific tendinitis will typically show a dramatic reduction in edema over time, whereas a malignancy would remain stable or progress [9].

The co-occurrence of aggressive-appearing MRI findings, elevated inflammatory markers, and immunosuppressive therapy in a young patient represents a challenging diagnostic scenario raising concern for malignancy or atypical infection. Ultimately, this case illustrates that MRI findings should not reflexively confirm a neoplastic diagnosis when the clinical context and multimodality imaging are discordant. Careful correlation with radiography or CT, combined with an understanding of the "pseudo-aggressive" nature of the resorptive phase, can establish the diagnosis of calcific tendinitis and prevent unnecessary, high-stakes invasive procedures.

Conclusion

Intraosseous migration of calcific tendinitis is a formidable diagnostic pitfall. Radiologists must maintain a high index of suspicion when encountering an "edema-dominant" marrow pattern centered at a tendon footprint. The cornerstone of diagnosis remains the identification of the calcific nidus via multimodality correlation with radiographs or CT. Recognizing these "pseudo-aggressive" hallmarks is essential to prevent unnecessary oncologic intervention and guide the patient toward conservative management.
