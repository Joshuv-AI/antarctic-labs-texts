Longitudinal variability of blood eosinophils impact lung function decline in the general population
Authors: Charmaine J.M. Lim, Oliver Helk, Robab Breyer-Kohansal, Marie-Kathrin Breyer, Emiel F.M. Wouters, Sylvia Hartl
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBlood eosinophil counts (BECs) show moderate fluctuations over time but their long-term variability and clinical relevance in the general population remain unclear. We hypothesise a temporal variation of BECs over two visits (V1 and V2; 4.3±0.6 years) in the population-based Austrian LEAD study.MethodsAdults (n=6932) were categorised by their BEC levels: consistently high (BECHIGH, ≥210 cells·µL−1 at V1 and V2), consistently low–normal (BECLOW/NORMAL, <210 cells·µL−1 at V1 and V2), variably increasing (BECINCREASE, <210 cells·µL−1 at V1 and ≥210 cells·µL−1 at V2) and variably decreasing (BECDECREASE, ≥210 cells·µL−1 at V1 and <210 cells·µL−1 at V2). Associations with lung function and respiratory conditions were analysed.ResultsMost individuals remain within their baseline BEC threshold (83.7% in BECLOW/NORMAL and 67.3% in BECHIGH). BEC variability exceeded the normal range (−30 to 60 cells·µL−1) BECINCREASE (+150 cells·µL−1) and BECDECREASE (−144 cells·µL−1) groups. BEC variability significantly affected lung function (ΔFEV1−BECDECREASE, 26.14 (95.75–46.52) and BECINCREASE, −21.58 (−38.41– −4.75); and ΔFVC−BECDECREASE, 28.74 (8.14–49.34) and BECINCREASE, −20.78 (−37.73– −3.83)). BECHIGH individuals were most associated with respiratory symptoms.ConclusionsBEC is not a static biomarker; longitudinal variability predicts changes in lung function. Current guidelines rely on single time-point BEC measurements, but our findings suggest that repeated assessments and the use of thresholds to determine normal BEC variability over time may better guide treatment decisions in high-risk patients.

Introduction

Peripheral blood eosinophil counts (BECs) have emerged as a promising surrogate biomarker of eosinophilic airway inflammation [1, 2]. Granule proteins and chemical mediators produced by eosinophils may contribute to tissue damage, repair, remodelling and disease persistence [3]. Interleukin (IL)-5, IL-4 and IL-13, T-helper 2 (Th2)-derived cytokines, are involved in proliferation, maturation, activation and recruitment of eosinophils to target eosinophilic inflammation [4]. In respiratory diseases, BEC is a known theragnostic marker to target eosinophils directly or indirectly through biological therapies inhibiting IL-5, IL-4 and IL-13 signalling or by inhaled corticosteroid (ICS) treatments [5, 6].

The application of BECs as a biomarker in clinical practice is, however, still accompanied by several challenges [7]. A variety of intrinsic and extrinsic factors may modify absolute BEC counts in the general population and in airways disease with little knowledge about the long-term stability of BECs in respiratory and nonrespiratory conditions. Oshagbemi and colleagues [8] reported that the proportion of stable BECs progressively decreases with duration of follow-up and was lower in participants with COPD. In eosinophilic asthma, a comprehensive analysis reported that over a 1-year follow-up period only 44–66% of subjects remained in the same BEC group. Since a low BEC was reported to require repeated measurements for the diagnosis of severe eosinophilic asthma [9], it can be presumed that a single measurement of eosinophils is not sufficient to confirm clinical characteristics.

Here we address the variability of BEC over time in a general population and the influence of intrinsic and extrinsic factors associated with BEC variability over time. Changes in lung function and pulmonary conditions were additionally analysed with relevance to BEC variability.

Methods

Study design and study population

This study was conducted in the LEAD study (ClinicalTrials.gov; NCT017257518), a longitudinal, observational, population-based Austrian cohort study comprising 11 426 participants aged 6–82 years recruited between 2011 and 2016 from Vienna and lower Austria with a follow-up every 4 years. The study (protocol number EK-11-117-0711) was approved by the local ethics committee of Vienna and all methodologies were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study design and methodological details of home interview and clinical examinations at LEAD have been published elsewhere [10].

Briefly, whole-blood samples were collected from fasted participants for complete blood cells counts and differential counts analysis in a certified hospital laboratory. Lung function in the present study comprised of post-bronchodilator spirometry (BT-MasterScope Body 0.478, Jaeger, Germany; SentrySuite software v.3.20.1) and predicted lung function parameters were obtained from the Global Lung Initiative (GLI) reference equations [11]. Definitions of airflow limitation (forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1)/forced vital capacity (FVC) ratio < lower limit of normal (LLN; z-scores, 1.645)) and positive reversibility (post-bronchodilator FEV1 ≥200 mL or pre-bronchodilator FEV1 >12%) [12] were further included. Changes in lung function measurements were determined by subtracting measurements at V1 from measurements at V2 and further divided with follow-up time in years to obtain the rate of change in lung function for sensitivity analyses. Height and weight were measured with a stadiometer and high precision scale for calculations of body mass index (BMI; calculated as weight/height2).

Extrinsic parameters such as sociodemographic characteristics (residence type and proximity to the main road); exposure to dust, second-hand smoking and cumulative tobacco smoking (pack-years); smoking status (never, former and current); and socioeconomic states (SES) were obtained by questionnaires [13]. Intrinsic factors such as age, sexand family history of smoking, allergy, COPD and asthma were obtained by questionnaires. Information on known allergies, the use of inhaled corticosteroids (ICS), respiratory symptoms and chronic obstructive diseases were based on medical anamnesis and self-reporting. Participants were assessed for atopic status using standardised skin-prick test (where at least one reaction (≥3 mm) was considered positive [14]; ALK- Abelló® test kit) with selected allergens. All antihistamines or glucocorticoids were withdrawn for at least 48 h prior to testing. Only participants ≥18 years of age were included in this analysis and had interpretable and valid BECs and spirometric measurements at both V1 and V2.

Statistical analyses

Blood eosinophils thresholds (defined as >75th percentile) best fitted to the Austrian general population was previously published elsewhere [15]. A BEC of <210 cells·µL−1 was considered to be low–normal BEC levels and ≥210 cells·µL−1 as high BEC levels. Eligible individuals were assigned into four independent groups according to their BEC patterns over time with 210 cells·µL−1 as the upper threshold: consistently high (BECHIGH, ≥210 cells·µL−1 at V1 and V2), consistently low–normal (BECLOW/NORMAL, <210 cells·µL−1 at V1 and V2), variably increasing (BECINCREASE, <210 cells·µL−1 at V1 and ≥210 cells·µL−1 at V2) and variably decreasing (BECDECREASE, ≥210 cells·µL−1 at V1 and <210 cells·µL−1 at V2).

All statistical analyses were performed in R (v.4.3.0; R foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Comparisons between subjects were studied using ANOVA or Welch's t-test for ordinal variables and chi-squared tests were used for binary variables. Repeatability was assessed by Bland–Altman analysis and Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) analysis based on an absolute agreement, two-way mixed effects model. For ICC analysis, BECs were normalised with a logarithmic (BEC+0.1) transformation in consideration of zero values and approximate normal distributions [16]. Multivariable logistic regression analysis to confirm the associations between BEC variability groups and risk factors at baseline were unadjusted and p-values were obtained by Wald's test. Respiratory and extrapulmonary conditions were used as independent categorical factors to determine the associations with BEC variability groups in multivariable logistic regressions adjusted for potential covariates. Linear regression models for the associations of lung function changes and BEC variability groups were presented with unadjusted and adjusted models. Adjusted models accounted for age, sex, height, weight, smoking status and pack-years. All models were fitted as cross-sectional (V1) instead of longitudinal as observation time was simultaneous for both exposure of BEC changes and outcome.

Separate sensitivity analyses were performed with the standard upper BEC threshold of 300 cells·µL−1 which is the consensus threshold provided by the Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA) guidelines [14] and a cohort with individuals diagnosed with COPD and/or asthma. Ordinal logistic regression analyses for prespecified parameters were conducted against the main analysis cohort using the BEC variability groups as a categorical outcome. Statistical significance was set at a 95% confidence level.

Results

Prevalence and characteristics

6923 participants had valid and interpretable data on spirometry and BEC measurements in both V1 and V1 and were therefore eligible for inclusion in the study (supplementary figure S1). Baseline demographics and clinical characteristics at V1 and V2 (n=6392) are presented in table 1. While pack-years, BMI, neutrophilic and leukocyte counts did not differ between visits, there were fewer current smokers in V2. The proportion of participants with different thresholds of eosinophil count was constant between visits. Lung function and burden of respiratory symptoms and asthma decreased while the proportion of COPD-diagnosed individuals increased (4.6% in V1 and 5.5% in V2) with similar findings in spirometry-defined obstructive lung diseases. Comparisons of baseline characteristics between retained and nonparticipants were observed in supplementary table S1. Overall, nonparticipants were younger, had fewer smoking exposures, higher inflammatory markers, lower FEV1 and FVC but higher FEV1/FVC, and higher ICS use.

Longitudinal variability of BECs

The variability of BECs over time is visualised in an alluvial plot (figure 1). Of the participants with a normal BEC at V1, 4398 (83.7%) remained stable (BECLOW/NORMAL) and of the participants with a high BEC at V1, 1130 (67.3%) remained stable (BECHIGH). 20.3% of total participants showed variable BECs over their predefined thresholds between V1 and V2: BEC decreased in 7.9% (BECDECREASE) and increased in 12.3% (BECINCREASE) of participants over time. A low–normal BEC at V1 was less variable than a high BEC at V2 and participants with a low–normal BEC at V1 were less likely to transit to a high BEC at V2.

Stability of blood eosinophil counts over time. Participants were categorised into consistently high (≥210 cells·µL−1 at visits 1 and 2), consistently low–normal (<210 cells·µL−1 at visits 1 and 2), variably increasing (<210 cells·µL−1 at visit 1 and ≥210 cells·µL−1 at visit 2) or variably decreasing (≥210 cells·µL−1 at visit 1 and <210 cells·µL−1 at visit 2). Text on alluvium represents n (%) of participants who transitioned from low or high blood eosinophil counts at visit 1. Text on the left side of the plot represents individuals (% total cases in visit 1) and on the right side of the plot, individuals (% total cases in visit 2).

BEC at baseline and follow-up were moderately correlated (ρ=0.66, p<0.001; ICC 0.614, 95% CI 0.597–0.630; figure 2a). Visual inspection of the Bland–Altman analysis showed a widening trend with greater differences between measurements observed at higher mean BEC (mean difference of −152.9 cells·µL−1 with 95% limits of agreements between 160.6 and −466.5 cells·µL−1; figure 2b). The change in BEC between V1 and V2 ranged from −30 cells·µL−1 and +60 cells·µL−1 (25th and 75th percentiles; median, 10 cells·µL−1) in the general population and was robust to sensitivity analyses; supplementary figure S2). While changes in BEC for BECLOW/NORMAL and BECHIGH (cells·µL−1: 7.5±47.0 and 21.3±178.9 respectively) were relatively small and remained within the normal range of change in the total population, changes in BEC in the variable BEC groups were substantially different (cells·µL−1: BECDECREASE, −144.2±120.2 and BECINCREASE, +150±126.7; table 2).

Characteristics of participants by variability patterns

Table 2 presents baseline characteristics and lung function affected by BEC variation patterns over time. Among all BEC groups, there were significant differences in smoking exposure, anthropometry, white blood cell counts, lung function and burdens of respiratory conditions, with the highest symptom load and lowest FEV1/FVC generally found in those with BECHIGH. The use of ICS was generally low and differed among all three groups (BECHIGH, 5.9%, ∼4% in variable BEC individuals and 1.7% in BECLOW/NORMAL individuals). Unadjusted multivariable logistic regression models (figure 3) referenced to BECLOW/NORMAL individuals showed that ICS use was most significantly associated with BECHIGH individuals (OR 3.58, 95% CI 2.56–5.01), followed by bronchodilator reversibility, FEV1/FVC<LLN, current smoking and FEV1<LLN (OR 2.51, 95% CI 1.79–3.51; 2.05, 1.79–3.51; 2.04, 1.73–2.39; and 1.88, 1.38–2.54, respectively; p<0.001). A similar trend was observed for those with BECINCREASE with the corresponding OR 2.64, 95% CI 1.76–3.90 in ICS use, 1.90, 1.25–2.82 in bronchodilator reversibility, 1.55, 1.07–2.20 in FEV1<LLN and 1.35, 1.11–1.64 in current smoking (p<0.05). In BECDECREASE individuals, however, bronchodilator reversibility was the most significant independent risk factor (OR 2.45, 95% CI 1.55–3.75), followed by ICS use, current smoking, familial predisposition to asthma and BMI (OR 2.38, 95% CI 1.43–3.79; 1.8, 1.46–2.27; 1.58, 1.15–2.13; and 1.52, 1.27–1.81, respectively; p<0.010). Collectively, male sex, former smoking and higher values in BMI, dust exposure and neutrophil counts had significant and positive associations with all BEC variability patterns. These findings were robust to sensitivity analyses, with very few covariates changing the direction of ORs or statistical significance, and none with both a direction and significance change (supplementary tables S2–S4).

Lung function changes in BEC variability patterns

Table 3 shows the change in lung function and is supplemented with linear regression analyses. The largest decline in lung function was observed BECINCREASE individuals (ΔFEV1, −104.4±248.7 mL and ΔFVC, −25.4±329.9 mL) and was preceded by a loss in lung function individuals with BECHIGH (ΔFEV1, −90.9±253.3 mL and ΔFVC, −1.1±334.0 mL). The smallest decline in FEV1, but not FVC, was observed in BECDECREASE individuals (ΔFEV1, −48.9±216.2 mL and ΔFVC, 45.1±316.9 mL) compared with those with BECLOW/NORMAL (ΔFEV1, −76.2±226.7 mL). In linear regression analysis adjusted for age and sex, these associations were significant in individuals with BECDECREASE (ΔFEV1, β=26.14, 95% CI 95.75–46.52 and ΔFVC, β=43.76, 16.72–70.79) and BECINCREASE (ΔFEV1, β=−21.58, 95% CI −38.41– −4.75 and ΔFVC, β=−14.55, −36.87–7.7), but not BECHIGH. Although similar findings were observed in the model further adjusted for other covariates, the association between BECINCREASE and lung function changes in FEV1 and FVC was lost. All outcome findings were robust to sensitivity analyses (supplementary tables S5 and S6).

Association of BEC variability patterns to respiratory conditions

Further analysis with multivariate logistic regressions using burdens of symptoms and obstructive lung diseases as independent categorical predictors of BEC variability patterns were performed (figure 4). In age- and sex-adjusted models, variability in BEC over time (BECINCREASE and BECDECREASE) were associated with respiratory conditions. While chronic cough associated more with BECINCREASE individuals (OR 1.16, 95% CI 0.88–1.51 versus 1.03, 0.72–1.44 in BECDECREASE individuals), sputum production, wheezing, dyspnoea, asthma and COPD were associated more with BECDECREASE individuals. The greatest effect is presented in individuals with BECHIGH, who were most significantly associated with all assessed respiratory conditions. However, statistical significances were lost for chronic cough, dyspnoea and COPD, but not asthma, wheezing and sputum production, in models adjusted for baseline age, sex, BMI, smoking status, pack-years and FEV1. Generally, respiratory symptoms without a respiratory diagnosis had no significant association with high BECs, demonstrating that high or variable BEC patterns are specific to underlying airway inflammatory diseases, also shown in the sensitivity analyses (supplementary figures S3 and S4).

Discussion

The present study provides the following key findings: 1) the normal range of BEC change over 4 years for the Austrian population majority was −30 to +60 cells·µL−1; 2) individuals in predefined threshold [7, 12] groups generally do not transit across the threshold over time, with changes in BECs falling within the normal range of BEC change in both groups; 3) a small proportion of the population presents variable BEC changes beyond the normal range of change, with mean changes of +150.1±126.7 and −144.2±120.2 cells·µL−1, and transits between predefined thresholds; and 4) individuals with variable BECs, similar to individuals with consistently high BECs, were significantly associated with lung function impairment and respiratory conditions.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to establish a normal range of change in BEC levels in the general population. 83.7% with low–normal BEC levels (averaging at 100.1±46.5 cells·µL−1) remained stable with a low variation of BEC (7.5±46.5 cells·µL−1) and seem to represent a healthier subpopulation with a significantly higher proportion of never-smokers, and fewer cases of chronic airway disease and allergy. Individuals with high baseline BECs had lower consistency in BEC levels after 4 years, yet 67.3% remained stable. A large proportion of these individuals had high allergic sensitisation in the skin-prick test and self-reported allergy. These individuals were also more likely to report wheezing and chronic obstructive disease. High BEC levels were previously reported to have associations with positive reversibility tests in people with asthma and COPD populations [17, 18]. The present study confirms that those with consistently high BECs had the strongest risk association with airflow reversibility, FEV1<LLN, FEV1/FVC<LLN and clinical diagnoses of asthma and COPD. Those with high BEC variability showed similar risk associations as those with consistently high BEC.

Importantly, the variability of BEC predicts changes in lung function. Individuals with increasing BEC had the highest loss of FEV1 and FVC, exceeding those with stable high BEC, while those with decreasing BEC showed improved FVC but not FEV1. Previously, Barnes et al. [19] have reported that higher BEC was associated with the smaller rate of decline in FEV1 in patients with moderate and severe COPD, with relevance to the susceptibility for ICS treatment. Likewise, Hancox and colleagues [20] found that adults, with high baseline BEC (≥400 cells·µL−1) also present an increased rate of FEV1 decline irrespective of asthma. Tan and colleagues [21] also found that individuals with high BECs had an accelerated decline in FEV1 compared with those with low BECs, irrespective of COPD. Newby and colleagues [22] identified that the degree of change and the variability in BEC levels across thresholds in the sputum of people with severe asthma were the highest risk factors for lung function impairment. Our study with the general population, including people with asthma, revealed similar results. It can thus be affirmed that BEC variability serves as a predictor of impaired lung function over time, irrespective of the underlying clinical diagnoses. Our results indicate that BEC variability beyond −30 to +60 cells·µL−1 predicts lung function changes, emphasising the need for repeated assessments rather than relying on single time-point measurements. Nonetheless, it is important to recognise that BEC is a continuous variable, and minor changes may underlie substantial changes in lung function, thereby warranting more comprehensive studies with medication.

Current GINA and GOLD guidelines recommend BECs for guiding corticosteroid therapy but rely on single time-point measurements. As BEC variability may be reflective of increased variability in eosinophilic airway inflammation, repeated measurements are needed in clinical practice. Additionally, our study suggests that BEC fluctuations over time may have clinical implications that are currently under-recognised in treatment algorithms. Newby and colleagues [22] listed susceptibility to environmental exposures, variations in key effector cytokines and chemokines resulting in cyclical eosinopoiesis or tissue recruitment, or varying susceptibility of airway structural cells to corticosteroid use as possible reasons for oscillations in eosinophilic airway inflammation. In line with this, BEC variability groups in our study have also shown differences in associations of risk factors for Th2 inflammation.

The present study has several strengths and limitations. We are able to validate previous findings from specific cohorts in an unselected general population. We show that changes in lung function in accordance with BEC variability also occur in subjects with normal to mildly impaired lung function. Although sputum eosinophils would be more ideal for detection of eosinophilic airway inflammation, BECs were shown to be an excellent surrogate biomarker [23, 24]. Crucially, they offer a distinct advantage in guiding therapy for severe asthma as their assessment is much less invasive than bronchoalveolar lavage. Additionally, a high standardisation and quality of testing was ensured by the fact that all study-related measurements were taken in a single centre under the same protocols between visits. As our study is also population-based and includes participants recruited from both urban and rural regions, external validity is increased. Moreover, the findings align with previous studies on BEC variability, suggesting that the observed trends are not site-specific but instead reflect general eosinophil behaviour in a stable population.

Our study is limited by lack of reliable information on short-term variations in BEC within individuals, particularly where allergy-related conditions are concerned. The burden of exposure for allergens could also be influenced by climate change [25–27], even if testing was conducted in the same season for all individuals. However, Spector & Tan [28] observed that BEC variability cannot not be attributed to fluctuations in seasons and weather patterns and thus, seasonality and diurnal variation are unlikely to account for eosinophilic variation. Given that the study spans multiple years, seasonal fluctuations in BECs would likely be randomly distributed across participants and are unlikely to introduce systematic bias. Our study is a population-based cohort where most participants are stable and do not experience frequent exacerbation, thereby limiting our study where exacerbation-driven BEC fluctuations, which are relevant in clinical cohorts of asthma or COPD patients. However, this study examines long-term eosinophil variability, which is unlikely to be driven by transient acute events. Additionally, variation of BECs could be related to adherence behaviour regarding corticosteroid use. However, only a small minority of subjects with high BEC variability was prescribed corticosteroid treatment and it is, therefore, unlikely that varying adherence to treatment would have significantly affected the results of the whole group. Irrespective of treatment effects, the negative associations between increasing BEC and lung function calls for a more nuanced medical follow-up in patients with fluctuating BEC levels.

In conclusion, we established thresholds of blood eosinophil variability that are strongly associated with pulmonary function decline, offering new perspectives on the role of inflammatory markers in respiratory health. Our findings also suggest that relying on a single BEC measurement may not fully capture a patient's inflammatory phenotype. Given the observed variability, repeated BEC assessments are necessary to improve the accuracy of treatment decisions and to ensure a more nuanced medical follow-up in patients with fluctuating BEC levels. Future guidelines should consider incorporating BEC trends rather than single values when guiding therapy.
