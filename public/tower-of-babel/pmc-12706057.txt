Dynamic kernel generation through hybrid involution and convolution neural networks for leukemia and white blood cell classification
Authors: Osama M. Alshehri, Ahmad Shaf, Unza Shakeel, Tariq Ali, Muhammad Irfan, Mohammed M. Jalal, Malik A. Altayar, Mohammed H. Abu-Alghayth, Humood Al Shmrany
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Blood cancer diagnosis through microscopic image analysis is challenging due to subtle morphological differences between cell stages and subtypes. This study aims to develop a Hybrid Involutional-Convolutional Neural Network (HICNN) for automated leukemia detection and white blood cell morphology analysis, enabling precise classification of leukemia stages and leukocyte subtypes to improve diagnostic accuracy. The HICNN integrates involution layers, which dynamically generate spatially adaptive kernels for context-specific feature extraction, with convolutional layers that capture translation-invariant hierarchical patterns. This hybrid architecture leverages parallel processing within hybrid blocks to enhance discrimination of subtle cellular variations. The HICNN achieved exceptional performance with 99.5% accuracy on a leukemia staging dataset and 98.00% accuracy on a leukocyte subtyping dataset, surpassing state-of-the-art models. Model reliability was demonstrated through Brier scores of 0.0019 and 0.0069, with inter-class misclassifications below 2% and minimal overlap between class confidence scores. Training performance showed stable convergence within 50 epochs, with validation accuracy exceeding 99% by epoch 30. The HICNN advances automated hematological diagnostics by successfully addressing feature discrimination and model calibration challenges. The framework demonstrates robust performance in both cancer staging and cell subtype classification tasks, showing promise for reducing diagnostic ambiguity and improving early detection in leukemia and related hematological disorders.

Introduction

Leukemia is a highly aggressive malignancy found in the hematopoietic system and originates from the bone marrow. This condition has a role in disrupting the normal production and function of white blood cells (WBCs)1,2. This form of cancer is classified into two major classes; acute leukemia and chronic leukemia having distinct clinical attributes and trajectories3–6. The acute form of leukemia advances and progresses rapidly, showing more pronounced clinical symptoms. However, the chronic form of leukemia progresses slowly with initially the WBCs resembling the normal cells. This form of leukemia is severe as it becomes more challenging to detect and classify the malignancy7. The major classes of leukemia are further distinguished into sub-classes including lymphoid and myeloid which in result give four primary sub-classes; ALL (the acute lymphoblastic leukemia), AML (the acute myeloid leukemia), CLL (chronic lymphocytic leukemia), and lastly CML (chronic myeloid leukemia)3,8,9.

The ALL class has predominant effect on the children as it causes proliferation of the immature lymphocytes10. This proliferation compromises the normal immune function and it can metastasize or spread to the other parts of the body such as lymph nodes, brain, and liver if left untreated. On the other hand, the AML class is more common in adults and involves the rapid expansion of malignant myeloid cells11,12. CLL and CML, the chronic leukemias typically progress very slowly and as initially the WBCs resemble the normal cells it is very difficult to detect as well. Chronic leukemias if left untreated can cause long-term significant and fatal health risks13.

Therefore, precise diagnosis strategies and system along with the targeted treatment methods are highly important to distinguish between these leukemia sub-classes. Advanced systems such as the FAB (the French-American-British) classification system further subdivides the ALL leukemia class based on the chromatin structure, the cellular morphology, and the cytoplasmic features into L1, L2, and L3 sub-types14–16. For effective leukemia management, it is highly critical to timely and accurately diagnose the disease with its sub-classes. Manual differentiation is helpful in the Leukemia diagnosis but the diversity of WBC characteristics complicates the process. The area of research on Leukemia focus on the fundamental and translational studies. The fundamental or basic research studies work on investigating the genetic mutations, cellular pathophysiology, and the proliferation attributes and patterns leading to leukemia. On the other hand, the translational research studies focus on the clinical aspects such as drug testing, detection tools, and advancement in the imaging techniques.

Both of the fundamental and translational approaches can help refine the early leukemia detection process as well as enhance the therapeutic interventions17,18. The traditional diagnostic methods and frameworks used for the identification of leukemia include blood smear microscopy and bone marrow biopsies. The blood smear microscopy works by identifying abnormal WBCs with the help of stained blood samples examined direct under the microscope. While, the bone marrow biopsy detects leukemia by extracting a bone marrow tissue and analyzing it completely to identify any abnormal indicative marker or cell proliferation. These methods were informative yet labor-intensive, costly, and require experts in the field19,20.

To overcome these challenges, some advanced alternatives such as percutaneous aspiration and molecular cytogenetic techniques were introduced. The aCHG (Array Comparative Genomic Hybridization) was utilized as an advanced diagnostic option for leukemia detection and classification as it provided a detailed and expanded analysis21,22. However, this approach has several constraints including resolution, sensitivity, and cost.

In recent years, machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) have transformed leukemia diagnostics, introducing automated blood smear analysis for more accurate and rapid leukemic cell classification. In a study23, the authors published a review on the detection of ALL class of leukemia. This research mainly focused on detecting ALL by utilizing several image processing and traditional ML based methods. This study did not cover challenging issues of ML approaches, analyze the DL techniques, transfer learning, and also future scopes.

In another study24, the authors generated an advanced automated system for detection of acute leukemia. The system worked by taking the microscopic digital images and then applying the noise and blurring preprocessing techniques. Various factors like texture, geometry, color, and statistical characteristic were analyzed and retrieved. According to these factors, the leukemia was classified as benign or malignant. This automated system utilized the power of K-NN (the K-nearest neighbor) method and naïve bayes method for experimental study25. 60 blood samples were taken as dataset and results depicted that the K-NN classifier is far more superior in leukemia detection based on the high classification accuracy score.

In another research study26, authors presented a well-done survey on ALL detection using various segmentation techniques. In this survey, the focus discussion was based on segmentation task utilizing the traditional image processing and ML techniques and not the classification task. A classification technique was created for AML classification. This approach utilizing SVM classifier improved the image contrast and also worked well in identifying five features27.

The experimental study was set up on a dataset containing 50 blood sample microscopic images. Results of this study showed that the model performed well in detecting and classifying AML. In another study28, a famous classifier RF (random forest) was utilized for the detection and classification WBCs and its subtypes related cancer. This method performed well in accurately diagnosing, detecting, and identifying the cancer. A classification-based technique was proposed for AML cells and its subtypes including M4, M5, and M729. This method worked by first segmenting the cells using a color k-means approach. A multi-class SVM classifier was then used for training the 6 statistical features retrieved from the segmentation. This model outperformed the results of traditional methods by achieving higher accuracy score in both segmentation and classification task.

In another study30, an ABC-BPNN based novel PCA (the principle component analysis) technique was designed and utilized for leukemia classification. This model worked well by reducing the computation time and achieving high accuracy score. In another study31, the BSA-based clustering method was used for segmentation of leukemia images. This approach was used to identify ALL class of leukemia by applying Jaya framework. This framework is integrated with multiple popular ML based classifiers including SVM, DT (decision tree), Naïve Bayes, LDA (Linear Discriminant Analysis), ensembled-random under-sampling boost, and K-NN32. The results depicted that Jaya with SVM and DT achieve high performance. A PCA framework integrated with LDA model was utilized for diagnosing and classifying leukemia. In this method, DOST technique (Discrete Orthogonal Stockwell Transform) was utilized for feature extraction of the microscopic data images.

These ML based methods performed well in leukemia classification but face challenges related to computation power, speed, and efficiency when working with larger datasets. DL approaches, particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs), efficiently process large image datasets, identifying complex cellular patterns in blood smears with higher accuracy and speed than traditional methods. These advanced techniques automate the extraction of features including geometric, chromatic, and textural attributes minimizing human error and streamlining the diagnostic workflow. Three pre-trained CNN models were combined and implemented as an architecture for feature extraction was used in a study. The classification task was completed on hybrid database utilizing SVM classifier without implementing segmentation. Another study33, presented a CNN architecture for binary classification of ALL class leukemia and its subtypes consisting of 5 convolutional layers. The model worked well and classified the ALL and its types accurately. In another research study34, the CNN framework based on Tversky loss function was modeled to classify the acute leukemia images. This model included 4 dense layers, 6 convolutional layers, and a SoftMax function for activation. However, this type of architecture performs well on larger datasets which can be time consuming and labor intensive.

Moreover, for the efficient classification of leukemia, CNNs often struggle with capturing subtle, localized variations in blood cell images due to their fixed-filter approach, leading to limited representational flexibility. CNNs are powerful for most standard image tasks but have limitations in flexibility and efficiency when dealing with complex, context-specific patterns. In this paper, we focus on implementing Involutional Neural Networks (INNs) over CNNs for leukemia detection and classification due to INNs’ ability to capture spatially adaptive, detailed patterns crucial for distinguishing between subtle cellular variations in blood samples. An INN works by applying involution operations, which adapt filters based on each pixel’s spatial context, making it more flexible and parameter-efficient. Unlike CNNs, INNs reduce redundant computations by dynamically adjusting filters for each region, leading to a more efficient and accurate classification process. This adaptability makes INNs particularly well-suited for handling the complexity and variability present in leukemia images. Both datasets underwent standardized preprocessing pipelines to ensure consistency in input quality and resolution.

Proposed invo-convo neural network

The Hybrid Involution-Convolution Neural Network (HICNN) is a novel architecture designed to combine the benefits of both involutional and convolutional layers for effective blood cancer classification. Traditional convolutional layers are widely used for feature extraction due to their ability to capture spatial hierarchies. However, convolutional operations apply the same transformation across all spatial locations, which may limit their ability to model long-range dependencies effectively. To overcome this limitation, we incorporate involution layers, which allow the model to adapt its receptive field dynamically based on spatial locations. Involutional neural networks: learning spatially-adaptive kernels

Traditional convolutional layers, while effective in capturing spatial hierarchies, employ fixed kernels across all spatial locations. This inherent limitation hinders their adaptability to the diverse and complex patterns often encountered in medical images. Involutional Neural Networks (INNs) offer a powerful alternative by learning a distinct kernel for each spatial location, enabling dynamic and context-aware feature extraction. This spatial adaptivity is particularly crucial in medical image analysis, where subtle variations in tissue or cell morphology can be indicative of disease. INNs empower the network to focus on the most salient features at each location, leading to potentially more accurate diagnoses.

Our implementation of involution leverages the following key components:

Adaptive Kernel Generation: Instead of relying on pre-defined filters, our network learns spatially-varying kernels through a two-stage process using 1x1 convolutional layers. This dynamic kernel generation allows the network to automatically discover the most relevant filters for each specific region of the input feature map.

Convolutional neural networks: capturing spatially-invariant features

While involutional neural networks excel at capturing spatially-adaptive features, traditional CNNs remain a crucial component for learning spatially-invariant representations. These representations capture global patterns and provide a foundational understanding of the input data, complementing the localized focus of involution. CNNs leverage the power of convolutional filters to learn hierarchical features, progressively extracting more complex patterns from the input image. In our architecture, we incorporate a standard convolutional layer and a max pooling operation to establish this foundational feature extraction.

Initial Convolutional Layer: The first convolutional layer plays a vital role in capturing low-level features from the input image. This layer employs a set of learnable filters, each of which is convolved with the input to produce a feature map. Let X represent the input image (or the output of a previous layer) and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$W_c$$\end{document} represent the convolutional kernel. The convolution operation can be expressed as:5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} F_c = \sigma (W_c * X + b_c) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_c$$\end{document} is the resulting feature map, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma$$\end{document} is a non-linear activation function, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$b_c$$\end{document} is the bias term, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$*$$\end{document} denotes the convolution operation. The kernel \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$W_c$$\end{document} is typically small and slides across the input image, allowing the network to detect local patterns such as edges, corners, and textures. Our initial convolutional layer uses a kernel size of 7, a stride of 2, and appropriate padding. The larger kernel size allows for a wider receptive field, capturing slightly more global context even at this early stage. The stride of 2 downsamples the feature map, reducing the computational load and increasing the network’s robustness to minor spatial variations. Padding is used to control the size of the output feature map and prevent information loss at the boundaries of the image.

Max Pooling: Following the initial convolution, we employ a max pooling operation. Max pooling is a downsampling technique that selects the maximum value within a local region of the feature map. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_c$$\end{document} be the input feature map to the max pooling layer. The max pooling operation can be defined as:6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} F_p(i, j) = \max _{k \in R_{ij}} F_c(k) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_p$$\end{document} is the output feature map after max pooling, i and j are the indices of the output feature map, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_{ij}$$\end{document} represents the local region centered at location (i, j) in the input feature map. Max pooling serves several important purposes:

Dimensionality Reduction: It reduces the spatial dimensions of the feature maps, decreasing the number of parameters and computations in subsequent layers. Robustness to Spatial Variations: By selecting the maximum value, max pooling makes the network more robust to small shifts or distortions in the input image. Increased Receptive Field: Max pooling effectively increases the receptive field of subsequent convolutional layers, allowing them to capture more global context.

Our architecture uses a max pooling operation with a kernel size of 3 and a stride of 2. This downsamples the feature maps by a factor of 2 in both height and width.

Conclusion

This study presents a Hybrid Involutional-Convolutional Neural Network (HICNN) for precise and reliable classification of blood cancer stages and leukocyte subtypes. By synergistically integrating involution layers for spatial-aware feature extraction and convolutional operations for translation-invariant hierarchical learning, the HICNN addresses critical challenges in hematological image analysis, including subtle morphological variations and class imbalance. Evaluated on two datasets-Hema-DA (cancer staging) and Hema-DB (leukocyte subtyping)-the model achieves state-of-the-art performance, with 99.5% accuracy on Hema-DA and 98.00% accuracy on Hema-DB, outperforming existing methods such as EfficientNet-B3, Hybrid PSO-PCA, and Advanced CNN.

The HICNN’s robustness is further validated through rigorous calibration analysis, yielding near-perfect Brier scores (0.0019 for Hema-DA and 0.0069 for Hema-DB), alongside confusion matrices demonstrating <2% misclassification rates. Probability density distributions confirm the model’s high confidence in predictions, with minimal overlap between class scores. Training dynamics reveal rapid convergence (<50 epochs) and stable generalization, even on imbalanced data.

Clinically, the model’s exceptional performance in early-stage detection (99.85% recall for Hema-DA’s “Early” class) and leukocyte subtyping (99.65% accuracy for Hema-DB’s “Neutrophils”) underscores its potential to reduce diagnostic delays and improve patient outcomes. The hybrid architecture’s adaptability to diverse datasets highlights its versatility for broader applications in medical imaging.

Despite its strengths, the HICNN has limitations. The Hema-DA and Hema-DB datasets may not fully capture real-world clinical variability. Validation on multi-institutional datasets is needed. The hybrid architecture’s computational complexity may hinder deployment in resource-constrained settings. Finally, while preprocessing addresses class imbalance, performance on rare leukemia subtypes requires further study. Future work will validate the model on diverse datasets, optimize efficiency, and enhance interpretability.
