Effect of jacket configuration and anchorage in hybrid strain hardening cementitious composites with steel mesh strengthening of reinforced concrete beams
Authors: Abdulaziz Alaskar, Arsalan Mahmoodzadeh, Galal Elsamak, Yahia Iskander, Ramy I. Shahin, Mohamed Ghalla
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Reinforced concrete (RC) structures often need shear retrofit due to factors like design deficiencies, aging, or increased loads. This study explores an innovative hybrid retrofit system for RC beams with shear defects, using an external jacket composed of strain-hardening cementitious composites (SHCC) and welded steel mesh (WSM). An experimental program including nine RC beams, involving control, defected, and strengthened specimens, was prepared to assess the effectiveness of many jacket arrangements (one-sided, two-sided, U-wrapped, and fully wrapped) and the influence of mechanical anchorage. In addition, the experimental work was simulated using Abaqus program to replicate the beams’ behavior. Experimental findings showed that the shear defect significantly reduced load capacity, stiffness, and energy absorption. All strengthening configurations improved performance, with effectiveness generally increasing from one-sided to full wrapping. Anchorage bolts enhanced load capacity and energy absorption, particularly for partial jackets and U-wraps. Particularly, the fully wrapped configuration restored the beam’s ultimate load capacity nearly to the level of the control beam, even without anchorage, and significantly enlarged absorbed energy. The FE model accurately simulated the experimental failure modes, crack patterns, and load-displacement responses, validating its reliability. Full jacketing with anchorage improved the ultimate load capacity by up to 75% compared to unstrengthened beams. Crack propagation was considerably delayed, and average crack widths were reduced by 30–35%. Moreover, the retrofitted beams exhibited a remarkable increase in energy absorption capacity, achieving up to 3.8 times the energy dissipation of the control specimens. These findings clearly demonstrate the necessity and effectiveness of the proposed retrofit system for extending the service life and resilience of RC beams.

Introduction

Existing RC structures may experience inadequate shear capacity due to design deficiencies, aging, increased service loads, poor maintenance, or material degradation1. This often requires strengthening interventions to restore or enhance their structural performance2,3. Among the failure modes, shear failure is mainly brittle and sudden, thus requiring urgent care when retrofitting existing structures. Numerous experimental and analytical searches have been presented to explore the efficiency of various strengthening strategies on the shear behavior of RC beams. These methods can be categorized into externally bonded fiber-reinforced polymers (FRPs)4, near-surface mounted (NSM) systems, use of steel-based solutions, application of advanced cementitious materials such as strain-hardening cementitious composites (SHCC), and hybrid strengthening techniques. The next part aims to review findings from recent research on the improvement of shear strength in RC beams.

Literature review

Externally bonded FRP systems have become popular due to their high strength-to-weight ratio, practicality, and resistance against corrosion. Researchers have explored the use of carbon (CFRP), glass (GFRP), and basalt (BFRP) fiber composites in several arrangements such as U-wraps, side wraps, and full wraps. Several researches approve that the orientation, number of layers, and anchorage of FRP sheets notably affect the enhancement in capacity of RC beams under shear1,5–12. The combination of horizontal and vertical FRP strips has shown enhanced performance over conventional unidirectional configurations5,6. The use of prestressed FRP was also studied and reported to improve efficiency13,14.

NSM techniques, wherein FRP rods or strips are embedded into grooves cut into the concrete surface and bonded using epoxy, have demonstrated greater bond behavior and enhanced shear strengthening compared to externally bonded systems. Studies have proved the effectiveness of NSM carbon rods in enhancing shear capacity while reducing debonding failures15–19. The NSM method, especially when combined with effective anchorage and epoxy bonding, was shown to outperform traditional externally bonded methods11,16,17,20.

Steel plates and strips have also been used to increase shear resistance. Configurations include side bonding of steel strips or plates using epoxy or mechanical anchorage. Results show that such interventions can improve stiffness and load capacity, especially when combined with other materials like FRP21–24. U-shaped and L-shaped steel anchors have been effective in reducing debonding and increasing load resistance. Despite their effectiveness, concerns related to corrosion and additional dead load persist25.

The strain-hardening cementitious composite (SHCC) used in this study is a PVA fiber-reinforced mortar designed to develop multiple fine cracks and high tensile ductility. Unlike ultra-high-performance fiber-reinforced concrete (UHPFRC), which emphasizes compressive strength through dense microstructure and steel fibers, SHCC prioritizes tensile performance, ductility, and crack control. While engineered cementitious composites (ECC) share the strain-hardening principle, the SHCC employed here was formulated using locally available materials and optimized PVA fiber content to achieve similar ductile behavior at lower cost and with easier practical applicability. This makes SHCC particularly suitable for retrofit systems where ductility and energy absorption are essential.

The SHCC as a retrofit overlay or infill has also gained attention. These materials show multiple cracking behavior and high ductility, which contribute to better shear performance and energy dissipation. Studies reported that RC beams strengthened with SHCC showed significant improvements in shear capacity and cracking behavior26–29. SHCC retrofitting also contributed to the confinement of existing concrete and improved ductility under cyclic loading28. Such type of high performance concrete was studied by many researchers30,31.

Combining different materials and methods has led to hybrid strengthening tactics that take advantage of each component. Examples include combining FRP wraps with steel plates or applying SHCC in conjunction with FRP sheets. These hybrid systems often result in enhanced performance due to collaborative effects22,23. Innovative methods such as using high-performance fiber-reinforced cementitious composites (HPFRCC) and textile-reinforced mortar (TRM) have also been used in hybrid forms32.

Effective anchorage systems are important for maximizing the contribution of the strengthening material. Several researches highlighted the importance of mechanical anchorage and proper detailing to avoid early debonding and to ensure effective force transfer between the FRP and the concrete substrate6,33–35. Techniques such as mechanical end anchorage and fiber wrapping of critical sections improved overall system efficiency.

Recent studies have contributed to advancing retrofit strategies for reinforced concrete members36–38. For example, Zou et al.39 investigated the dynamic response of strengthened members under seismic-type loading and highlighted the potential of hybrid retrofits in improving structural resilience. Similarly, Zouet al.40 proposed innovative jacketing systems for beams, demonstrating improved shear resistance but without detailed analysis of the stress transfer mechanism or numerical–experimental validation. In another contribution, Jiang et al.41 explored advanced fiber-based strengthening materials, focusing mainly on flexural enhancement rather than comprehensive shear behavior. In contrast, the present study distinguishes itself by systematically combining SHCC and welded steel mesh in a hybrid jacketing retrofit, experimentally validating nine beam specimens with different configurations and anchorage conditions, and further clarifying the shear stress transfer mechanism through finite element modeling. This integrated approach provides broader applicability and greater scientific depth compared with existing literature.

Research significance and scope

This study aims to investigate a novel hybrid strengthening technique designed to improve the shear ductility of RC beams. The proposed system integrates welded steel mesh (WSM) and (SHCC) to form an external strengthening jacket. This innovative hybrid approach is expected to get the mechanical benefits of both components: the confinement and ductility of SHCC, and the anchorage and crack-bridging properties of the welded steel mesh. The main contribution of this study is the proposal and validation of a novel hybrid strengthening system combining SHCC and welded steel mesh, applied in different wrapping and anchorage configurations to deficient RC beams. Unlike previous works that investigated SHCC or steel reinforcement separately, this research systematically compares nine retrofit cases to quantify the effect of jacket geometry and anchorage on shear resistance, ductility, and energy absorption. In addition, finite element simulations were used not only for validation but also to clarify the retrofit mechanism by examining shear stress redistribution across the retrofitted section. These combined contributions provide both scientific insights and practical guidance for developing efficient and sustainable strengthening solutions.

Experimental program

A comprehensive experimental program was established to examine the impacts of different retrofit arrangements on the shear performance of RC beams.

Specimens’ details and test program

The test matrix contains nine RC beams divided into four groups. All beams have typical length of 1600 mm, 120 mm in width, and 240 mm in depth. Longitudinal reinforcement contains two top bars 12 mm diameter (2D12) and two bottom bars 16 mm diameter bars (2D16). The stirrup configuration varies depending on the beam type.

Group G1, as illustrated in Fig. 1; Table 1, includes two beams: the control beam (B1) and a defected beam (B2). Beam B1 features uniformly spaced stirrups with 8 mm diameter (D8) spaced 150 mm along the entire span. Beam B2 is a purposely weakened beam with the same stirrup spacing on one half of the span, while the other half (defected zone) contains only a single stirrup located at the center.

Group G2 explores the effect of one-sided external jackets on shear strengthening on defected zone. This group comprises beam B2 and two beams strengthened on one side using SHCC and WSM jackets: B2-1, which is non-anchored, and B2-2-A, which is anchored. Both strengthened beams include two layers of welded steel mesh in the jacket, as shown in Table 1; Fig. 2(a). The main focus in this group is to compare the efficiency of anchorage on the behavior of the one-sided strengthening configuration.

Group G3 focuses on the behavior of two-sided jacketed beams on defected zone. It includes beam B2, a non-anchored two-sided jacketed beam (B2-2), and an anchored two-sided jacketed beam (B2-2-A), both incorporating two welded steel meshes, as shown in Table 1; Fig. 2(b). This group is intended to assess the improvement in shear capacity due to the application of the hybrid jacket and the significance of mechanical anchorage.

Group G4 aims to explore the effect of U-wrapping and full wrapping configurations on defected zone. It includes the reference beam B2, a non-anchored U-wrapped beam (B2-U), an anchored U-wrapped beam (B2-U-A), and a fully wrapped beam without anchorage (B2-F), as shown in Table 1; Fig. 2(c). Group G4 was designed as a U-shaped jacket, where anchorage could be applied only at the top region due to geometric constraints. This explains the difference in anchorage details compared with the fully or side-wrapped groups. All jackets in this group also contain two layers of welded steel mesh. The aim of this group is to explore the impact of wrapping continuity and anchorage in achieving higher ductility and enhanced shear resistance. Although the fully wrapped configuration is rarely feasible in real construction practice, it was included in the test program as a reference benchmark to define the maximum possible strengthening effect of the hybrid SHCC–WWM system. The performance of more practical configurations (partial wrapping and anchored jackets) can thus be meaningfully compared with this upper-bound case, enabling a clearer evaluation of efficiency and applicability.

Investigated parameters

The current study explores several key parameters that affect the shear performance of strengthened RC beams. These parameters contain: (1) Jacket Configuration, where four external strengthening layouts are examined—one-sided, two-sided, U-shaped, and fully wrapped jackets. These configurations are designed to assess the influence of the extent and symmetry of confinement on shear enhancement and failure modes. In the U-shaped retrofitting configuration, the welded steel mesh (WSM) was arranged continuously along the sides and bottom of the beam to maintain integrity of the jacket. This continuous placement enhanced shear transfer across the jacket and prevented weak points that could arise from disconnected mesh segments; (2) Anchorage Condition, where each jacket configuration is evaluated in both anchored and non-anchored forms to govern the role of mechanical anchorage in improving the bond behavior, force transfer, and overall effectiveness of the hybrid retrofit system; (3) Hybrid Strengthening System, where the proposed system integrates strain-hardening cementitious composites (SHCC) and welded steel mesh. The SHCC provides enhanced ductility and crack distribution control, while the steel mesh serves as a mechanical reinforcement element that facilitates stress redistribution. The number of steel mesh layers and their positioning are kept constant across all specimens to isolate the effects of jacket layout and anchorage. The SHCC thickness was limited to 5 mm to ensure practical application, minimize self-weight increase, and highlight the interaction with the welded steel mesh. A thicker SHCC layer might obscure the relative contribution of the mesh and increase construction challenges, while the selected thickness ensured crack control and bond transfer without excessive material use.

Material properties and mix proportion

Two kinds of concrete materials were utilized in the experimental program: conventional normal concrete (NC), and (SHCC) for the external strengthening jackets. The mix proportions and properties of both materials are detailed in Table 2. The normal concrete used in beam casting consisted of 350 kg/m³ of cement, 700 kg/m³ of fine aggregate, and 1150 kg/m³ of coarse aggregate, with a water-to-binder ratio of 0.42. No admixtures or supplementary cementitious materials were included in this mix. The NC achieved an ultimate compression stress (fc`) of 30 MPa and a tensile strength (Ft) of 2.6 MPa, as documented in Fig. 3.

The SHCC material used for the strengthening jackets featured a larger cement content of 1355 kg/m³ and fine aggregate of 150 kg/m³. Coarse aggregate was omitted, and 240 kg/m³ of silica fume was incorporated to improve the matrix density and bond characteristics. The water-to-binder ratio was reduced to 0.26 to improve mechanical performance. Polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) fibers were added at a volume fraction of 2.0% to provide strain-hardening behavior and distributed micro-cracking, characteristic of SHCC. The PVA fibres incorporated in the SHCC were monofilament polyvinyl alcohol type with the following properties: nominal length 12 mm, nominal diameter ~ 40 μm, tensile strength ≈ 1500 MPa, and Young’s modulus ≈ 40 GPa (manufacturer’s typical values). Fibres were used at a volume fraction of 2.0%, which provided sufficient bridging capacity to activate multiple-cracking and strain-hardening in the SHCC matrix for the present mix design. Additionally, 32.0 kg/m³ of high-range water reducer (HRWR) was utilized to maintain workability. The SHCC exhibited superior mechanical performance, achieving an ultimate compression stress (fc`​) of 68 MPa and a tensile strength (Ft) of 6.35 Mpa.

The properties and stress-strain relationship of the reinforcement parts was estimated using dog-bone-shaped test samples exposed to uniaxial tension tests, as shown in Fig. 4; Table 3.

The welded steel mesh used in this study consisted of cold-drawn steel wires with a nominal diameter of 1 mm, yield strength of approximately 400 MPa, and ultimate tensile strength of 550 MPa. The mesh was manufactured in square grids of 10 × 10 mm spacing, providing adequate confinement and bonding interface with the SHCC overlay. Anchorage was achieved using mechanically fastened steel bolts with a diameter of 10 mm, anchored to the concrete substrate at an effective embedment depth of 50 mm. The bolts were tightened with nuts against the mesh surface to ensure firm mechanical interlock and stress transfer. This configuration enabled efficient load sharing between the SHCC layer, the steel mesh, and the original RC beam, thereby improving the global load-carrying capacity and energy absorption.

Preparation and casting

The arrangement of the strengthened reinforced concrete beam specimens followed a carefully structured process to ensure the efficiency of the hybrid strengthening system. Initially, the defected zones of the beams were treated by surface roughening, as shown in Fig. 5(a). This process aimed to enhance the mechanical bond between the existing concrete substrate and the overlaying retrofit layers. The roughened surface provided the necessary texture for improved adhesion.

Following surface treatment, a liquid epoxy bonding agent was applied uniformly over the roughened area, as shown in Fig. 5(b). This epoxy layer served to promote strong adhesion between the original concrete and the subsequent strengthening materials. Once the epoxy was applied, the first layer of welded steel mesh (WSM) was placed onto the coated surface, as shown in Fig. 5(c). A patch of freshly prepared strain-hardening cementitious composite (SHCC), as shown in Fig. 5(e), was then applied, embedding the mesh and forming the initial part of the strengthening jacket.

After the initial SHCC layer had begun to set, a second layer of WSM was positioned on top, and another SHCC layer was applied to fully encapsulate the mesh and level the jacket surface, as shown in Fig. 5(d). The thickness and coverage of the SHCC were controlled to ensure uniformity across all strengthened specimens.

For beams requiring mechanical anchorage, additional steps were incorporated. Anchor positions were carefully marked based on the strengthening layout, and holes were drilled using a rotary drilling machine, as shown in Fig. 5(f). Anchor units were then inserted into the drilled holes and secured in place, as shown in Fig. 5(g) and (h).

Loading arrangement, test setup and instrumentation

The testing of the RC beam samples was executed at the Structural Engineering Laboratory of Kafr Elsheikh University. The test setup was planned to facilitate a three-point loading arrangement to evaluate the shear behavior of the beams under controlled conditions. The layout of the loading system and instrumentation is illustrated in Fig. 6.

Each beam sample, with an overall span of 1600 mm and a clear span of 1400 mm between loading points, was simply supported on two steel supports. A hinged support was located at one end, while a roller support was used at the opposite end, allowing for rotation and longitudinal movement. The supports were mounted on movable steel frames, ensuring proper alignment and load transfer.

The loading system comprised a hydraulic jack positioned vertically above the beam mid-span. Load was applied through a calibrated load cell located between the jack and the beam to accurately record the applied force. A steel load transfer column was employed to load transmission from the hydraulic jack to the beam.

To record the vertical deflection at mid-span, a Linear Variable Differential Transformer (LVDT) was mounted directly beneath the center of each beam. The load and displacement are collected using data logger system.

Test outcomes and discussions

This chapter offerings an in-depth discussion of the test results obtained from the specimens. The primary focus is on assessing the structural behavior of the control, defected, and strengthened specimens. Key performance indicators are inspected in detail, including the ultimate load capacity, elastic stiffness index, and absorbed energy estimated from the load-displacement curves. Also, the observed failure modes and crack patterns for each beam are described and compared. The load-displacement performance throughout the testing process is analyzed to understand the effect of shear deficiency and subsequent strengthening. A central purpose of this discussion is to assess the impact and effectiveness of the different strengthening techniques employed. The reference and shear-deficient beams were considered in line with the approach adopted by El-Samak et al.42in their study.

Failure modes

Control Beam B1, as shown in Fig. 7(a), exhibits a typical shear failure pattern for a reinforced concrete beam with adequate shear reinforcement. Multiple diagonal cracks initiate near the supports and propagate towards the central loading point, distributed along the beam span. Beam B2 (Defected) as illustrated in Fig. 7(b), shows a noticeable brittle shear failure concentrated in the defected zone (right side) where stirrups were minimal. A single, large diagonal shear crack dominates this zone, indicating failure occurred owing to the lack of shear reinforcement in that specific area. The non-defected side shows minor cracking. For group G2, according to Fig. 8, failure for beam B2-1 occurs via a major diagonal shear crack in the strengthened (defected) zone. The crack pattern suggests that while the jacket provided some resistance, the lack of anchorage might have led to premature debonding or ineffective load transfer, resulting in a failure similar to B2 but potentially at a higher load. For beam B2-1-A, failure is still dominated by shear cracking in the strengthened defected zone. However, the cracks appear in the left side as well, suggesting the anchorage improved the bond and interaction between the jacket and the concrete beam, leading to better utilization of the strengthening material before failure. The failure patterns for group G3, as shown in Fig. 9, shows that diagonal shear cracks form within the jacketed defected zone. The two-sided jacket appears to control cracking better than the one-sided non-anchored jacket (B2-1). Failure remains shear-dominated in the strengthened region. Failure patterns for beam B2-2-A, illustrated in Fig. 9(b), shows well-distributed, finer diagonal shear cracks in the strengthened defected zone. The combination of two-sided jacketing and anchorage significantly improves shear performance and crack control, indicating strong composite action. The failure mode is still shear within the jacketed zone but likely occurs at a substantially larger load compared to B2 and the non-anchored versions.

Ultimate load and load-displacement relationship

The test outcomes, summarized in Fig. 11; Table 4, demonstrate that the introduction of a shear defect impacted the beam’s load-bearing capacity, as evidenced by comparing the control beam (B1) and the defected beam (B2) in Group G1. Beam B1, with adequate stirrups throughout, reached an ultimate load (Pu) of 75.26 kN. In contrast, beam B2, lacking sufficient stirrups in one half, failed at a much lower load of 42.96 kN. This noticeable reduction highlights the important effect of shear reinforcement, as the defect caused the beam’s ultimate capacity to drop by approximately 43%, making the control beam 75% stronger than its defected counterpart.

Exploring strengthening techniques in Group G2 revealed the effects of one-sided jacketing on the defected beam. Applying a non-anchored, one-sided jacket (B2-1) yielded a modest 15% increase in ultimate load to 49.61 kN compared to the unstrengthened defected beam (B2). However, incorporating anchorage (B2-1-A) significantly improved performance, raising the ultimate load to 58.85 kN, a 37% increase over B2 and provide more ductile failure, as illustrated in Fig. 11(b). This comparison highlights the influence of mechanical anchorage for one-sided strengthening systems to ensure effective load transfer and maximize the capacity gain.

Group G3 proved the enhanced effectiveness of two-sided jacketing. The non-anchored two-sided jacket (B2-2) provided a substantial capacity increase, reaching 63.22 kN (a 47% improvement over B2), significantly outperforming B2-1 by 22%. Adding anchorage (B2-2-A) further improved the ultimate load to 66.63 kN, achieving a 55% increase compared to B2 and outperforming B2-1-A by 13% with similar post peak behavior, as shown in Fig. 11(c). So, it can be concluded that while anchorage provided an additional benefit for the two-sided configuration, the results suggest that the primary advantage over Group G2 stems from applying the jacket to both sides of the beam.

It is acknowledged that only one beam was tested in each group, which limits statistical elimination of uncertainty in shear resistance. This may explain why the difference in ultimate capacity between anchored and unanchored specimens was small. However, anchorage was observed to play an important role in the shear transfer mechanism. Anchored jackets showed reduced mesh slippage, improved crack distribution, and greater ductility, confirming that the anchor improved the retrofit performance even if the peak load increase was modest. The main benefit of anchorage thus lies in modifying the failure mode and enhancing energy absorption rather than producing a large increase in ultimate capacity under a single test.

The investigation into wrapping configurations in Group G4 showed further improvements in ultimate load capacity. A non-anchored U-wrap (B2-U) raised the capacity to 68.08 kN (58% increase over B2), surpassing even the anchored two-sided jacket B2-2-A. Anchoring the U-wrap (B2-U-A) yielded an even higher load of 72.33 kN (68% increase over B2). Most notably, the fully wrapped beam (B2-F), despite lacking anchorage, accomplished the greatest ultimate load among all strengthened specimens at 75.15 kN. This represents a 75% increase over the defected beam and almost completely restored the capacity and better post peak behavior to that of the original control beam (B1), signifying the superior confinement and effectiveness of full wrapping for shear strengthening, as illustrated in Fig. 11(d) and Table 4. To further interpret the retrofit mechanism, a comparative numerical assessment was carried out to approximate the separate contributions of SHCC and welded steel mesh. The analysis confirmed that while the mesh largely governs the shear capacity improvement, the SHCC layer significantly enhances crack distribution, interface bonding, and ductility, underlining the necessity of combining both materials in the proposed hybrid system.

In summary, all tested strengthening methods successfully increased the ultimate load capacity of the defected beam. The outcomes suggest a clear order in effectiveness, generally progressing from one-sided jacketing, to two-sided jacketing, to U-wrapping, and ending with full wrapping. Mechanical anchorage consistently provided additional capacity gains for jacketed and U-wrapped beams. However, the full wrapping technique proved exceptionally effective in terms of capacity and post peak behavior, nearly recovering the original beam’s strength even without anchorage, highlighting the significant benefit of complete section confinement.

Absorbed energy and elastic stiffness

The presence of the shear defect profoundly affected the beam’s mechanical properties beyond just its ultimate strength, as explained in Table 5. Comparing the control beam (B1) with the defected beam (B2) in Group G1 reveals a sharp decline in both elastic stiffness (K) and absorbed energy (E). The elastic stiffness index dropped from 57.37 for B1 to 18.96 for B2, meaning the defect reduced stiffness by about 67%, as shown in Table 5; Fig. 12. The absorbed energy of each specimen was calculated as the total area under the load–displacement curve up to ultimate failure, thereby incorporating both elastic and plastic contributions43–45. This unified approach was applied across all specimens, regardless of whether the failure mode was brittle or ductile. In the same way, the absorbed energy dropped from 705.41 to 193.41 (EB/EB2 = 3.65 for B1), indicating a reduction of roughly 73%, as shown in Table 5; Fig. 13. This proves that the defect not only weakened the beam but also made it significantly less stiff and severely reduced its ability to absorb energy before failure, implying a loss of toughness.

Analyzing the effects of one-sided strengthening in Group G2 shows moderate improvements. The non-anchored jacket (B2-1) increased stiffness slightly to 21.23 (KB/KB2 = 1.12) and absorbed energy to 319.61 (EB/EB2 = 1.65). Adding anchorage (B2-1-A) provided a marginal further increase in stiffness to 22.31 (KB/KB2 = 1.18) but resulted in a much more substantial boost in absorbed energy to 494.81 (EB/EB2 = 2.56), as shown in Table 5; Fig. 13. This suggests that while one-sided strengthening offers some benefit, anchorage is particularly effective in enhancing the energy absorption capacity (toughness) for this configuration, more so than its effect on initial stiffness.

Two-sided jacketing in Group G3 produced more significant improvements. The non-anchored beam (B2-2) showed improved stiffness at 24.59 (KB/KB2 = 1.30) and considerably higher absorbed energy at 512.01 (EB/EB2 = 2.65), as shown in Table 5; Figs. 12 and 13. Anchoring this jacket (B2-2-A) further increased stiffness to 26.18 (KB/KB2 = 1.38) and energy absorption to 651.41 (EB/EB2 = 3.37). Both stiffness and absorbed energy values for the two-sided jackets were clearly larger to their one-sided counterparts. Again, anchorage contributed positively to both metrics, but its impact was particularly pronounced on the absorbed energy, indicating improved ductility and toughness.

The wrapping configurations in Group G4 confirmed the most substantial improvements. The non-anchored U-wrap (B2-U) elevated stiffness to 28.88 (KB/KB2 = 1.52) and absorbed energy to 654.24 (EB/KB2 = 3.38), as shown in Table 5; Figs. 12 and 13. Anchoring the U-wrap (B2-U-A) provided only a slight additional stiffness increase to 29.46 (KB/KB2 = 1.55) but significantly boosted absorbed energy to 866.01 (EB/KB2 = 4.48). The fully wrapped beam (B2-F) achieved the highest stiffness among all strengthened beams at 34.20 (KB/KB2 = 1.80) and the highest absorbed energy at 945.51 (EB/KB2 = 4.89), as shown in Table 5; Figs. 12 and 13. This indicates that full wrapping was extremely effective in restoring both stiffness and, especially, energy absorption capacity, surpassing even the original control beam’s absorbed energy value.

In conclusion, while the shear defect severely decreased both elastic stiffness and energy absorption, all retrofit methods provided improvements. Stiffness gains followed the general trend of one-sided, two-sided, U-wrap, and full wrap, with anchorage providing relatively minor additional stiffness benefits. However, absorbed energy, a measure of toughness, was significantly improved by strengthening, particularly with anchorage. Anchorage played a vital role in boosting energy absorption for jacketed and U-wrapped beams. Full wrapping provided the most substantial recovery in both stiffness and absorbed energy, signifying its superior effectiveness in enhancing the overall behavior and ductility of the defected beam.

Numerical modelling

This chapter discuss the development of a three-dimensional (3D) nonlinear finite element (FE) model created using Abaqus program. The model aims to replicate the structural response recorded in the experiments. The following sections explain the model’s geometry, material properties, element selection, component interactions, applied boundary conditions, meshing strategy, and validation of the model. The finite element analysis was employed not simply to replicate the experimental tests using ABAQUS, but to enhance understanding of the retrofit mechanism. While the global response was validated against experiments, the simulations also provided insight into shear stress transfer paths, the role of interface bonding, and the influence of anchorage. In addition, parametric variations were investigated numerically to explore scenarios beyond the scope of the laboratory program. This ensured that the modelling contributed scientific value beyond reproduction of the test results.

Modeling of materials

To represent the behavior of concrete within the model, the Concrete Damaged Plasticity (CDP) framework was utilized, which selected for its capability to effectively represent the intricate nonlinear response characteristics exhibited by concrete under different loading conditions20,21. The specific parameters defining the CDP model were established based on findings from published literature. A dilation angle (ψ) of 28° was adopted, as suggested by46. The flow potential eccentricity (e), which influences the shape of the yield surface, was set to 0.147. Following recommendations in48, a viscosity parameter (µ) of 0.00 was implemented to regulate the viscoplastic regularization of the concrete constitutive equations. The ratio of the second stress invariant on the tensile meridian to that on the compressive meridian (Kc) was defined as 0.6618,49–51. The ratio of biaxial to uniaxial compressive strength (fbo/fco) was considered within the range of 1.10 to 1.1652–55. After performing a comparative analysis, a value of 1.16 for this ratio demonstrated the closest agreement with experimental observations and was consequently used in the simulations. The fundamental stress-strain relationship governing the concrete’s constitutive behavior was formulated following the methodology developed by Carreira and Chu56.

For the steel reinforcement components, the material properties were characterized using data obtained from experimental testing. Stress-strain curves were generated for different reinforcement types, specifically 10 mm diameter steel bars, 8 mm diameter steel bars, and steel wire, as depicted graphically in Fig. 3(b). To facilitate computational analysis, these experimentally derived stress-strain relationships were idealized into a tri-linear material model. This idealized model captured the key phases of steel reinforcement behavior: an initial linear elastic phase extending up to the yield point, followed by a strain hardening phase exhibiting reduced stiffness beyond yielding, and culminating in a softening phase that represented the material’s behavior up to its ultimate strength and subsequent failure.

Interactions, boundary conditions, and element types

The numerical model utilizes several element types to represent the different structural components. The main concrete beam, the surrounding SHCC jacket, the supporting plates at both ends, and the loading plate are all represented by C3D8R elements, which are 8-node linear bricks with reduced integration suitable for solid bodies. Reinforcement, including the top and bottom longitudinal bars as well as the stirrups, is modeled using T3D2 elements, 2-node linear 3D truss elements designed for axial loads. A steel mesh component is modeled using S4R elements, which are 4-node shell elements with reduced integration appropriate for thin surfaces carrying membrane and bending loads, as presented in Fig. 14. The anchorage system, both the steel strip and the steel anchor are modeled with C3D4 elements, 4-node linear tetrahedral elements often used for meshing complex solid geometries.

Boundary conditions represent real support conditions and the loading scenario of the experiments. At the left end, the supporting plate is assigned roller properties, typically constraining vertical and out-of-plane translation while permitting horizontal movement and rotation. On the other hand, the right supporting plate is assigned hinge properties, which constrains translation in all directions but allows rotation. Loading is presented via a displacement control method, where a prescribed vertical displacement (indicated as U2) is applied downwards onto the top surface of the loading plate to simulate the actual test setup.

Several interaction types were employed in the numerical model, as explained in Fig. 15. The interaction between the supporting plates, the loading plate, and the beam was represented using a surface-to-surface contact definition. This involved specifying “Hard contact” for the normal behavior, allowing separation between the surfaces if tensile forces develop, and using the penalty method for tangential behavior to simulate friction. Based on recommendations from literature35,46, a friction coefficient of 0.3 was applied.

For components embedded within others, the embedded region constraint was utilized. Specifically, the internal reinforcement (top bars, bottom bars, and stirrups) was defined as an embedded region within the concrete host region. Similarly, the steel mesh was treated as an embedded region within the SHCC jacket host region, simulating a full bond between these elements.

In the finite element simulations, the endpoint of the load–deflection curve was defined by the loss of convergence resulting from severe material nonlinearity, characterized by crushing of concrete in compression and extensive cracking in tension near the shear-critical regions. This criterion was taken as the numerical ultimate state and was consistent with the experimentally observed failure of the beams. This proves the model’s capability to simulate the developing stiffness of the beams accurately. The FEM results further illustrate the underlying mechanism of retrofit effectiveness. In the shear-deficient beam, stress contours indicated a highly localized diagonal shear transfer path, which triggered early diagonal cracking and brittle failure. After the application of the SHCC–steel jacket, the stress distribution became more dispersed, with the welded steel mesh and surrounding SHCC layer forming an alternative shear transfer network. This redistribution reduced peak shear stresses in the original beam, delayed crack localization, and contributed to the enhanced ductility and load capacity observed experimentally.

The low standard deviation and coefficient of variation for these ratios across all specimens further demonstrate the model’s reliability for both load capacity and deformation characteristics up to the peak point.

Parametric study

Effect of SHCC thickness

The thickness of the SHCC overlay plays a crucial role in governing the efficiency of the hybrid strengthening system. By increasing the thickness, a larger zone becomes available to redistribute shear stresses and delay the development of major cracks. A thicker layer enhances the confinement of the original concrete core and allows the welded wire mesh to be better embedded within the matrix, thereby promoting stronger composite action. This additional confinement helps to transform the cracking pattern from few dominant cracks into multiple micro-cracks, which not only delays brittle shear failure but also improves ductility and post-cracking performance. Nevertheless, excessive thickness can lead to practical challenges such as higher self-weight, more complex construction, and increased material consumption, making it necessary to strike a balance between mechanical benefits and constructability.

The experimental results confirmed that increasing the SHCC thickness from 5 mm to 25 mm improved the ultimate load capacity from 69.41 kN to 80.01 kN as shown in Fig. 19a. The improvement trend, however, was not linear. Significant gains were observed when increasing thickness from 5 mm to 15 mm, while the improvement became less pronounced beyond 20 mm. This diminishing return indicates that while thickness is an effective parameter for enhancing capacity, its effect plateaus at higher values. Hence, an intermediate thickness in the range of 10–20 mm appears to provide the most efficient balance between strength enhancement and construction feasibility.

Effect of fiber content in SHCC

The fiber content in SHCC is a key parameter that activates the strain-hardening behavior and controls the tensile and shear response of the composite. Polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) fibers, when distributed at sufficient volume fraction, ensure that cracks remain fine and widely distributed rather than localizing into a single major crack. This crack-bridging mechanism enhances ductility, increases energy absorption, and provides a more gradual transition from elastic to plastic behavior. Moreover, the interaction of fibers with the cementitious matrix facilitates sustained stress transfer even after initial cracking, ensuring that the retrofitted layer continues to contribute effectively under increasing load. As such, the fiber content is vital not only for tensile strength but also for crack control and overall toughness of the strengthened beams.

When analyzing the effect of fiber volume fraction, the results demonstrated a steady increase in ultimate load capacity from 69.41 kN at 2% fiber content to 75.68 kN at 3% as shown in Fig. 19b. Although the magnitude of improvement was smaller compared with changes in mesh reinforcement ratio, the effect of fibers was particularly evident in enhancing ductility and post-peak energy absorption. The contribution of fibers is therefore less about raising the peak load dramatically and more about controlling damage evolution and ensuring a ductile mode of failure. This makes fiber dosage a critical design parameter in retrofitting strategies where durability and service performance are prioritized alongside strength.

Effect of WWM reinforcement ratio

The welded wire mesh reinforcement ratio was found to be the most influential parameter in the hybrid retrofitting system. The mesh acts as a direct load-carrying element, creating alternative shear transfer paths that compensate for the absence or weakness of internal stirrups. By increasing the wire diameter and reducing the spacing, the reinforcement ratio rises, resulting in stronger mechanical interlock and improved confinement of the SHCC jacket. This not only increases the shear capacity but also prevents premature debonding or sliding of the retrofit layer. In essence, while SHCC provides the ductile matrix, the welded wire mesh delivers the structural skeleton that carries most of the shear force, making the synergy between both materials critical to the retrofit mechanism.

The influence of mesh reinforcement ratio on ultimate load was evident in the numerical results. When the wire diameter increased from 1 mm to 3 mm, the ultimate load rose from 69.41 kN to 79.39 kN as shown in Fig. 19c. This improvement was greater than that observed from variations in either SHCC thickness or fiber content, confirming that the mesh is the dominant factor governing the peak shear resistance. Anchored and continuous mesh further amplified this effect by ensuring efficient force transfer into the retrofitted jacket. Therefore, it can be concluded that optimizing the reinforcement ratio of the welded wire mesh provides the most significant improvement in ultimate shear capacity, while the SHCC overlay ensures ductility and crack control, together forming a complementary and effective strengthening system.
