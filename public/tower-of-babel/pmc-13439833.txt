Proxy reports on the EQ-5D-5L in residential aged care facilities: a validation study
Authors: Tiet-Hanh Dao-Tran, Syed Afroz Keramat, Christopher Etherton-Beer, Amy Page, Vasi Naganathan, Dee Mangin, Kathleen Potter, Sarah Hilmer, Tracy Comans
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe EuroQol 5-Dimension 5-Level questionnaire (EQ-5D-5L) is a common scale to measure health-related quality of life in residential aged care facilities. Many residents are unable to answer the scale independently; therefore, a proxy usually responds on the residents’ behalf. The quality of data reported by proxy is not well understood. This study examines the psychometric properties, including convergent validity, known-group validity, interrater reliability, and responsiveness of proxy reports on the EQ-5D-5L.MethodsThe study used data from 281 residents participating in a 12-month deprescribing randomised control trial in Australia. Interview-administered questionnaires were used for data collection. Convergent validity, known-group validity, and responsiveness were evaluated using a hypothesis-testing approach. Interrater reliability was assessed by calculating the intra-class correlation coefficient, weighted Cohen’s kappa coefficient, and generating Bland-Altman plots and bar charts.ResultsProxy (staff) reports on EQ-5D-5L were significantly associated with residents’ cognitive functioning, physical functioning, neuropsychiatric behaviours and frailty. Proxy (staff) reports on EQ-5D-5L were significantly different across groups of patients with varying levels of cognitive and physical functioning, frailty and neuropsychiatric behaviours. Changes over 12 months in residents’ cognitive and physical functioning, frailty and neuropsychiatric behaviours were associated with changes in proxy reports on EQ-5D-5L. Interrater correlation at the total score measured by the intra-class correlation coefficient was 0.744 at baseline and 0.664 at 12 months (moderate). Interrater correlation at the domain level, measured by weighted Cohen’s kappa, ranged from 0.206 to 0.432 (minimal to weak).ConclusionProxy reports on the EQ-5D-5L demonstrated satisfactory convergent validity, known-group validity, and responsiveness. Interrater reliability was moderate at the total utility score but poor at the domain level. Proxy reports on the EQ-5D-5L can serve as an alternative method to measure residents’ HRQOL. However, data from proxy reports at the domain level is not recommended for future research and practice evaluation.Clinical trial registrationThis study is part of a large deprescribing trial known as ‘Opti-Med’, which has been registered with the Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry (Registry number: ACTRN12613001204730, dated 4 November 2013) and the World Health Organisation Clinical Trials Registry Platform (the universal trial number: U1111-1148-6094).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12955-026-02553-5.Keywords: Validity, Reliability, EQ-5D-5L, Proxy, Quality of life, Nursing home

Background

Health-related quality of life (HRQOL) is a common outcome measured in residential aged care facilities (RACF) [1]. The EuroQol 5-Dimension questionnaire (EQ-5D) has often been used to measure the HRQOL because this scale is short and easy to administer, enables comparability of outcomes across different health conditions, and allows estimation of health utilities for economic evaluations [2]. Of the three versions of EQ-5D, the EuroQol 5-Dimension 5-Level questionnaire (5Q-5D-5L) shows good measurement properties across various adult populations, conditions, and settings [3], with improvements in ceiling effects that can capture health issues better than the EuroQol 5-Dimension 3-Level questionnaire (EQ-5D-3 L) [4, 5].

In RACFs, because many residents experience cognitive impairment and cannot answer EQ-5D-5L on their own, staff usually complete the EQ-5D-5L on residents’ behalf [6, 7]. Literature suggested that there were discrepancies between self-and proxy reports of the quality of life on EQ-5D-5L [8, 9], and evidence of the responsiveness of the EQ-5D-5L was still limited [10]. The measurement properties of the proxy reports of residents’ HRQOL on the EQ-5D-5L are less well-researched than the self-report [11]. A recent systematic review of proxy reports of residents’ HRQOL on EQ-5D reported inconsistent findings on construct validity, poor to moderate interrater reliability and limited evidence on responsiveness [1]. This systematic review recommended conducting more validation studies of proxy reports of residents’ HRQOL using the EQ-5D [1].

Australia has 185,000 people aged 65 and over living in RACFs [12]. The Royal Commission recommended using quality indicators for service evaluation and continuous improvement [13], HRQOL has usually been measured. Despite the recent introduction of the Quality of Life – Aged Care Consumers (QOL-ACC) [13] for use in Australian aged care, the EQ-5D-5L remains valuable. Yet, to date, only one study has validated the measurement properties of EQ-5D-5L in Australian RACFs [14]. This study reported that EQ-5D-5L had satisfactory convergent and known group validity for different clinically recognised cognitive and physical functioning thresholds [14]. However, the study analysed EQ-5D-5L data from pooled data from residents and their proxies, which limited the evaluation of the measurement properties of the proxy reports of residents’ HRQOL using EQ-5D-5L. This study employed a cross-sectional design, which limited its examination of the responsiveness of the EQ-5D-5L. The understanding of the resident-proxy agreement (inter-rater reliability) regarding the EQ-5D-5L for residents in RACFs in Australia needs to be clarified. For these reasons, further evaluation of the measurement properties of the proxy reports of residents’ HRQOL using EQ-5D-5L among Australian RACF residents is necessary.

This study assesses the convergent validity, known-group validity, interrater reliability, and responsiveness of proxy reports of residents’ HRQOL using the EQ-5D-5L. Findings from this study can inform the consideration of using proxy reports of residents’ HRQOL on the EQ-5D-5L in future research and practice evaluations in RACF settings.

Methods

Setting and design

This study is part of a larger deprescribing randomised, double-blinded, placebo-controlled trial known as the Opti-Med study, conducted in 17 Australian RACFs across Sydney (New South Wales) and Perth (Western Australia) between 2014 and 2018 [15–17]. The Opti-Med study aimed to investigate the effects of deprescribing on older people living in RACF. Details of the recruitment process were published elsewhere [15–17].

Participants

For validation purposes, the current study utilised data from 281 residents at baseline and 12 months. Eligible participants were English-speaking residents aged 65 years or older who took at least one regular medication. Consent was obtained before data collection. Details of the consent process were published elsewhere [15–17].

Measures

Proxies (staff) were asked to rate the participant’s quality of life on the EQ-5D-5L. The EQ-5D-5L covers five domains: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression [18, 19]. The scale has a 5-point grading response (1 (no problems), 2 (“slightly” for anxiety/depression and “slight” for the remaining four domains), 3 (some problems), 4 (“very” for anxiety/depression and “a lot” for the other four domains) and 5 (extreme problems). Responses were converted into a preference-based single index value (utility) using the Australian value set [20]. The index value indicates a unique health state with 0 (representing death) and 1 (representing full health) [20]. The proxy (staff) was there as a safety net if residents couldn’t (cognitive impairment) at baseline or follow-up. Even where residents could cognitively consent, we had a proxy (staff) just in case they declined. Staff indicated how they believe residents would rate their HRQOL if they could complete the EQ-5D-5L themselves as their proxies at baseline and 12 months. Residents with sufficient cognitive capacity to answer EQ-5D-5L (MMSE ≥ 24) [21] were also asked to rate their HRQOL on EQ-5D-5L at baseline and 12 months. Staff used validated scales in interview-administered paper-based questionnaires to collect data on residents’ cognitive (Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE)) [22], physical functioning (Modified Barthel Index (MBI) [23], neuropsychiatric behaviours (Neuropsychiatric Inventory (NPI-10) scale [24]) and frailty (40-item Rockwood Frailty Index (RFI) [25]) at baseline and 12 months. Gender (male/ female) data were also collected. Detailed descriptions of the MMSE, MBI, NPI and RFI scales, including the measured construct, scoring, and interpretation of the direction, were published elsewhere [15–17].

Data management and analysis

The research team entered the collected data into REDCap for data management [26]. Stata SE 18.0 was used to perform data analysis [27]. For the study, missing data (See Supplementary Table 1) were not imputed. Frequency and percentage were used to describe participants’ gender. The distributions of continuous variables were assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test (See Supplementary Table 2). Where the continuous variables were not normally distributed, the median and interquartile range (IQR) were used, along with non-parametric tests.

Data from proxy reports of the residents’ HRQOL on EQ-5D-5L were used throughout these tests. Convergent validity refers to the associations between the measured outcome and theoretically related concepts [28]. We examined the associations between residents’ proxy reports of HRQOL on the EQ-5D-5L (utility) and their cognitive status (MMSE), physical functioning (MBI), neuropsychiatric behaviours (NPI), and frailty (RFI) using Spearman’s Rho (ꝭ), with data collected at baseline and 12 months to assess convergent validity. We hypothesised that residents with higher levels of cognitive functioning (MMSE) and physical functioning (MBI) would also have better levels of HRQOL (proxy, utility) [29, 30], and those with more neuropsychiatric behaviours and frailty would also have lower levels of HRQOL (proxy, utility) [31, 32]. The magnitude of the association coefficients below 0.3 was considered weak, between 0.3 and < 0.5 was considered moderate, between 0.5 and < 0.6 was considered strong, and 0.6 or greater was considered very strong [33].

Known-group validity tests examine the differences in measured outcomes across subgroups [28]. We discussed the difference in proxy reports of residents’ HRQOL on EQ-5D-5L (utility) across clinical severity thresholds of cognitive function (impairment defined as MMSE < 24 and sufficiently defined as MMSE ≥ 24) [21], physical functioning (defined independence as MBI = 100, slight dependence as MBI = 91–99, moderate dependence as MBI = 61–90, severe dependence as MBI = 21–60, and total dependence as MBI = 0–20) [25], neuropsychiatric behaviours (NPI < 11 and NPI ≥ 11) [34] and frailty (non-frail as RFI ≤ 0.08, pre-frail as 0.08 < RFI < 0.25, frail as RFI ≥ 0.25)) [35] using Man-Whitney and Kruskal-Wallis tests as appropriate for data collected at baseline and 12 months for known group validity. We hypothesised that proxy reports of residents’ HRQOL (utility) significantly differed across clinical severity thresholds of cognitive and physical functioning, neuropsychiatric behaviours, and frailty.

Interrater reliability reflects agreement among people using the scale to measure the construct on the same individuals [28]. Data from proxy reports and self-reports of residents’ HRQOL on the EQ-5D-5L, collected at baseline and 12 months, were used to examine inter-rater reliability. Two different approaches were applied to test interrater reliability. Two two-way intra-class correlation coefficients (ICC) based on mixed effect and absolute agreement were calculated for inter-rater reliability at total utility scores [36]. The ICCs of 0.50, 0.75, 0.90, and above indicate moderate, good, and excellent interrater reliability, respectively [36]. The Bland–Atman plots were also used to visualise the relative differences between the residents’ reports by comparing the averages of the residents’ reports and proxy reports for resident-proxy agreement [37]. It is expected that 95% of the ratings should fall within the limit of agreement, calculated as the mean difference between the two raters (proxy and resident) ± 1.96 times the standard deviation of the difference. Five per cent of the ratings fall outside the limit of agreement, suggesting excellent agreement. Otherwise, a percentage greater than 5% but less than 10% suggests moderate disagreement, and a percentage greater than 10% suggests poor agreement. Weighted Cohen’s kappa and p values were calculated for inter-rater reliability at the domain level. P values smaller than 0.05 indicate that the agreement differs significantly from random agreement. Weighted Cohen’s kappa of and greater than 0.01, 0.21, 0.40, 0.60, 0.80, and 0.91 indicates slight, minimal, weak, moderate, strong, and almost perfect agreement [38]. Bar charts visualised the frequency and percentage of proxies who agreed and rated their residents higher or lower than their own self-ratings.

We applied a construct approach, including hypothesis testing, to assess the responsiveness of the proxy report of the resident’s HRQOL using the EQ-5D-5L [39]. We examined the association between the proxy report of residents’ HRQOL at EQ-5D-5L (utility) change over 12 months and the residents’ changes in cognitive functioning (MMSE), physical functioning (MBI), neuropsychiatric behaviours (NPI) and frailty (RFI) over 12 months using Spearman rho tests. We hypothesised that proxy reports of residents’ HRQOL at EQ-5D-5L (utility) change over 12 months were significantly associated with changes in residents’ cognitive and physical functioning, neuropsychiatric behaviours, and frailty over the same period [29–32]. All statistical tests were 2-sided; statistical significance was p < 0.05.

Results

Participants’ characteristics

At baseline, 281 residents participated. At 12 months, 213 residents remained because 68 had died (24.2%) (20 deaths in the blinded intervention group, 28 deaths in the open intervention group, and 20 deaths in the control group). Three-quarters of our group were female, with an average age of 85. Participants at 12 months had lower cognitive, physical functioning, and HRQOL (utility) in both proxy reports and self-reports, but similar neuropsychiatric behaviours and frailty levels compared to those who participated at baseline. A small number of residents had MMSE ≥ 24 and could self-report their HRQOL on EQ-5D-5L (n = 76 out of 281 residents (27.0% at baseline and 42 out of 213 residents (19.7% at 12 months) (see Table 1).

Validity and reliability testing results

Spearman rho tests found that increased residents’ HRQOL (proxy report, utility) at baseline was significantly associated with better residents’ cognitive functioning (MMSE) (ꝭ=0.482, p < 0.01) and physical functioning (MBI) (ꝭ=0.854, p < 0.01); less neuropsychiatric behaviours (NPI-10) (ꝭ=-0.456, p<0.001) and lower level of frailty (RFI) (ꝭ=-0.278, p < 0.01) at baseline. Increased residents’ HRQOL (proxy report, utility) at 12 months was also significantly associated with better residents’ cognitive functioning (MMSE) (ꝭ=0.434, p < 0.01) and physical functioning (MBI) (ꝭ=0.866, p < 0.01); less neuropsychiatric behaviours (NPI-10) (ꝭ=-0.413, p<0.001) and lower level of frailty (RFI) (ꝭ=-0.366, p < 0.01) at 12 months (see Supplementary Table 3).

Mann-Whitney tests found significant differences in residents’ HRQOL (proxy report, utility) between residents with cognitive functioning (MMSE < 24) and residents with sufficient cognitive functioning (MMSE ≥ 24) at baseline (z=-4.588, p < 0.001) and at 12 months (z=-3.088, p < 0.005); between residents with NPI < 11 and residents with NPI ≥ 11 at baseline (z = 6.487, p < 0.001) and at 12 months (z = 4.521, p < 0.001). Kruskal-Wallis tests found significant differences in residents’ HRQOL (proxy report, utility) across different physical functioning levels at baseline (ꭓ2(3) = 183.814, p < 0.001), and at 12 months (ꭓ2(3) = 146.481, p < 0.001); and across frailty levels at baseline (ꭓ2(2) = 21.216, p < 0.001), and at 12 months (ꭓ2(1) = 21.994, p < 0.001) (see Supplementary Table 3).

At baseline, 76 resident-proxy dyads had utility data. At 12 months, 42 pairs of residents and proxies had utility data. Bland–Altman plots further indicated that at baseline, only 7.9% of residents did not have expected agreement in utility scores between proxy and self-report; this number increased to 9.5% at 12 months, suggesting moderate disagreement (see Fig. 1).

The ICC for utility score was 0.744 (95% CI, 0.596–0.838) at baseline and 0.664 (95% CI, 0.379–0.819) at 12 months. The proxy reports’ utility median score was lower than the resident report’s utility median score (0.59 [0.28-0.89] vs. 0.87 [0.48–0.93] at baseline and 0.56 [0.23-0.90] vs. 0.85 [0.55–0.90] at 12 months). Weighted Cohen’s kappa tests were significant on mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/ discomfort and anxiety/depression domain at baseline and on self-care, usual activities, usual activities, and pain domain at 12 months, indicating an agreement significantly different from the random agreement in these domains, with Cohen’s kappa ranging from 0.206 to 0.432, indicating minimal to weak inter-rater reliability at domain level (see Table 2).

Discussion

Evidence of the measurement properties of the proxy reports on the HRQOL of aged care residents using the EQ-5D-5L is limited [3]. This study assessed the convergent validity, known-group validity, inter-rater reliability, and responsiveness of proxy reports on residents’ HRQOL using the EQ-5D-5L. A few residents in RACF could self-report their HRQOL using the EQ-5D-5L, reinforcing the importance of examining the measurement properties of the proxy reports of residents’ HRQOL on the EQ-5D-5L. Our study found that proxy reports of residents’ HRQOL on the EQ-5D-5L had satisfactory convergent validity, known-group validity, responsiveness, and moderate interrater reliability (resident-proxy agreement) at the utility score level. Interrater reliability was poor at the domain level.

In line with the literature [29–32], the study found significant associations between residents’ cognitive (moderate), physical functioning (very strong), neuropsychiatric behaviours (moderate), and frailty (weak to moderate) and proxy reports of residents’ HRQOL on EQ-5D-5L and significant difference in proxy reports of residents’ HRQOL on EQ-5D-5L across clinical severity thresholds of cognitive and physical functioning, neuropsychiatric behaviours and frailty. This finding supports satisfactory convergent and known group validity of the proxy reports on EQ-5D-5L.

Over 12 months, there were 68 resident deaths, causing a significant attrition rate (24%) in RACFs (n = 68, 24% in a year). A high attrition rate causes difficulty obtaining consecutive measurements, which is a challenge in measuring the success of interventions/ programs at RACFs.

Consistent with previous studies, we found the proxy report on EQ-5D-5L could reflect the residents’ self-reports on EQ-5D-5L to a moderate extent [10, 40, 41]. This study examined the proxy rating from the resident’s perspective, and the agreement reported was higher than that between the proxy rating from the proxy’s perspective and the resident’s rating [40]. Also, in this study, proxies were staff, not informal caregivers. Literature reported that the proxy-resident agreement was lower when proxies were staff and higher when proxies included informal carers [1]. Despite the moderate agreement, in line with existing evidence, the current study found that the overall total utility score was higher in self-reports and lower in proxy reports [10, 40, 41], suggesting that proxies often underreport the residents’ HRQOL.

The current study also found poor inter-rater reliability (resident-proxy agreement) at domain levels, consistent with previous studies [40, 42, 43]. However, the agreement in the mobility, pain, and anxiety/depression domains fluctuated between baseline and 12-month data, being neither significant at baseline nor at 12-month data, suggesting that the agreements could be at random. Even though proxy reports of residents’ HRQOL on EQ-5D-5L showed significant agreement with residents’ self-reports in the self-care and usual activities domains at baseline and 12-month data, the agreement was slight to minimal.

Consistent with previous studies, the current study found significant agreement on more observable domains (self-care and usual activities) [40, 42, 44–46] at both baseline and 12-month data. While the previous studies suggested more agreement in the mobility domain [40, 42, 44–46], our study found that agreement in mobility domains was either random (12 months) or slight (baseline). Staff often rated residents’ mobility lower than the residents themselves rated it; more residents were underrated in the mobility domain at 12 months (45% vs. 40%). A potential reason is that aged care residents may have incorporated mobility aids when rating their mobility, as they may not have felt limited by the use of walking aids [47, 48]. In addition, while previous studies suggested less agreement in the depression domains [40, 42, 44–46], we also found that the agreement was highest for anxiety/ depression at baseline (61%) and reduced at 12 months (43%). A potential explanation is that staff in RACFs may have provided direct care to residents for a long time, allowing them to develop a holistic approach; this may have led to a good understanding of the residents’ emotions. However, the residents’ conditions may have deteriorated over time, which could challenge their understanding at 12 months.

Using data from an RCT, our study provided additional evidence of the responsiveness of the proxy report of residents’ HRQOL on the EQ-5D-5L, aligning with the current literature. Our findings indicated that proxy reports of residents’ HRQOL on the EQ-5D-5L were able to reflect changes in cognitive and physical functioning, neuropsychiatric behaviours, and frailty over 12 months in residents.

Strengths and limitations

The study performed multiple tests for convergent and known-group validity of the proxy reports of residents’ HRQOL on EQ-5D-5L. The study examined resident-proxy agreements on EQ-5D-5L in terms of both utility scores and individual domains to assess inter-rater reliability. This study utilised longitudinal data from a randomised controlled trial, which enabled researchers to examine the responsiveness of the proxy reports on the EQ-5D-5L. Findings from this study provided comprehensive evidence of the validity and reliability of proxy reports of residents’ HRQOL on EQ-5D-5L in the Australian RACF context. In addition, the study employed valid scales to measure the study variables, clearly stated the hypotheses of the associations among variables in the research and described the measurements before conducting the validity tests. This robust research methodological approach helps readers to justify the quality of the study findings.

Although efforts were taken to improve the quality of the study, the current study also has some limitations. First, only residents with an MMSE score of 24 and above rated themselves on the ED-5D-5L, as this is the minimum level of cognitive functioning required to be considered sufficient for answering the EQ-5D-5L [21]. Therefore, a small number of residents in the RACF were able to self-report their HRQOL using the EQ-5D-5L. To be more inclusive, older people with lower cognitive functioning should have had the opportunity to receive additional support to participate in research activities if they wished to do so. This inclusion would also provide researchers with a chance to examine whether the level of discrepancies differs significantly across different groups of residents with varying levels of cognitive functioning. Second, while it is recommended to perform test-retest reliability of the measure in the validation process, this was not done in this study due to considerations regarding time (two weeks for another data collection using the same questionnaire) and the residents’ and proxies’ willingness to spend time completing the questionnaire. Third, as the study utilised data initially collected for the Opti-Med study, the associations could only be tested on the variables included in the Opti-Med study. Finally, we did not collect data on proxy characteristics; therefore, it was not possible to examine the associations between proxy characteristics and the agreement between residents and proxies.

Conclusion

Proxy reports on the EQ-5D-5L demonstrated satisfactory convergent and known-group validity for different clinical severity thresholds of cognitive and physical functioning, neuropsychiatric behaviours, and frailty, as well as responsiveness, suggesting a potential alternative way to measure residents’ HRQOL. Interrater validity was moderate at utility scores, even though proxies often rate residents’ HRQOL worse than residents’ self-rates. Interrater validity was poor in individual domains, suggesting that using data at the domain level is not recommended. The evidence generated from this study should inform the consideration of using proxy reports on the EQ-5D-5L in this setting for future research and practice.
