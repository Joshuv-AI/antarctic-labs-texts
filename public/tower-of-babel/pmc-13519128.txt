Delmarva legacy phosphorus perspective
Authors: Nicole M. Fiorellino, Amy L. Shober
Published: 2026

The Delmarva Peninsula, encompassing 14 counties across Delaware, Maryland, and Virginia, faces significant phosphorus (P) imbalances stemming from concentrated grain and broiler chicken production. The region's inability to produce sufficient feed for its roughly 600 million annual broilers necessitates importing grain (Delmarva Chicken Association, 2025). A portion of the nutrients from these imported feeds are excreted in manure that is subsequently used as fertilizer for regional crops. Historical nitrogen (N)‐based manure applications, coupled with the use of inorganic P fertilizers, has led to elevated soil test P levels, increasing the potential for water quality degradation in the adjacent Chesapeake Bay (Qin & Shober, 2018; A. Sharpley et al., 2013; Sims et al., 2000). Current state and Federal regulations restrict P applications to P‐enriched soils (Mehlich‐3 soil test P > 150 mg kg−1; Shober, 2025), limiting the land base for manure application despite ongoing manure generation. These regulations also reduce the other soil health benefits of manure application, like building organic matter and utilizing other nutrients for crop production. Consequently, modern manure management is constrained by persistent water quality issues resulting from the regional nutrient imbalance and past practices.

Regional scientists often define “legacy P” as anthropogenic P from fertilizers and manure applied in excess of crop needs, particularly in soils with “excessive” soil test P (Qin & Shober, 2018; A. N. Sharpley et al., 2020), aligning with definitions from other regions (Osterholz et al., 2020; Withers et al., 2019). This definition emphasizes the regional concern for P‐enriched soils and their elevated risk of P transfer to surface waters. While “legacy P” is often associated with “excessive” soil test P on the Delmarva Peninsula, it encompasses P distributed across various soil pools with the recognition that labile P pools pose the greatest water quality risk and we acknowledge that management strategies must address multiple P forms. Furthermore, collaboration within the SERA‐17 workgroup has broadened the definition of “legacy P” to include all anthropogenic P remaining after crop removal and potentially geogenic P redistributed by human activities (Shober et al., 2024).

Legacy P research on the Delmarva Peninsula spans more than three decades, focusing on understanding and mitigating the environmental impact of accumulated soil P. Early research emphasized environmental soil testing (Sims, 1993; Sims et al., 2000, 2002) and the development of P risk assessment tools (Coale et al., 2002). Ongoing efforts to refine P risk assessment tools (Fiorellino, McGrath, et al., 2017; Shober et al., 2017) are informed by studies on P dynamics in manured (Lucas et al., 2022; Mosesso et al., 2024; Vadas & Sims, 1998) and biosolids‐amended soils (Shober & Sims, 2009), as well as P solubility and speciation in organic amendments (Shober et al., 2006; Toor et al., 2005). Understanding P losses through runoff (McGrath et al., 2010; Vadas, Kleinman, et al., 2004; Vadas, Meisinger, et al., 2004; White et al., 2010), leachate (Kleinman et al., 2015; Toor & Sims, 2015, 2016), and subsurface flow in ditch‐drained systems (Mosesso et al., 2021; Thompson et al., 2025) is critical for understanding and predicting P transfers to aquatic systems. Recent research explores P tradeoffs with implementation of conservation (Kleinman et al., 2022) and soil health practices (Mosesso & Shober, 2023) as well as management strategies to minimize P transfer while maintaining or enhancing crop production, including optimizing manure use for other nutrients, soil test P drawdown by phytoremediation (Fiorellino, Kratochvil, et al., 2017; Gatiboni et al., 2025), and engineered P removal structures (Penn et al., 2014; Qin et al., 2018; Stoner et al., 2012). Research aimed at monitoring landscape changes (e.g., land use and sea level rise) that could affect P dynamics is also underway.

Addressing the “wicked problem” of legacy P on the Delmarva Peninsula requires regional acknowledgement of the problem, along with innovation and economic investment. Researchers must refine the definition and measurement of legacy P to facilitate continued investigation of P dynamics and transfer risks to further guide management strategies for manure and P‐enriched soils (Qin & Shober, 2018). Improving risk assessment tools and models for subsurface P transfer is crucial. Developing practical management options to enhance crop uptake of legacy P, potentially through novel crops or soil amendments, necessitates understanding human behavior dynamics and availability of economic incentives. Ultimately, resolving the regional P imbalance requires major action and behavior changes, such as fostering circularity in the poultry‐grain system, rescaling the poultry industry, implementing policy changes, or making significant economic investments to reduce manure application on P‐enriched soils.
