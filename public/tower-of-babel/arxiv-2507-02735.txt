Meta SecAlign : A Secure Foundation LLM Against Prompt Injection Attacks Meta SecAlign is released under the FAIR Non-commercial research license.
Sizhe Chen, Arman Zharmagambetov, David Wagner, Chuan Guo

Abstract
Prompt injection attacks pose a significant security threat to LLM-integrated applications. Model-level defenses have shown strong effectiveness, but are currently deployed into commercial-grade models in a closed-source manner. We believe open-source models are needed by the AI security community, where co-development of attacks and defenses through open research drives scientific progress in mitigation against prompt injection attacks. To this end, we develop Meta SecAlign , the first open-source and open-weight LLM with built-in model-level defense that achieves commercial-grade model performance. We provide complete details of our training recipe, which utilizes an improved version of the SOTA SecAlign defense. Evaluations on 9 utility benchmarks and 7 security benchmarks show that Meta SecAlign , despite being trained on a generic instruction-tuning dataset, confers security in unseen downstream tasks, including tool-calling and agentic web navigation, in addition to general instruction-following. Our best model— Meta-SecAlign - 70B —achieves state-of-the-art robustness against prompt injection attacks and comparable utility to closed-source commercial LLM with model-level defense.

Introduction

Recent advances in Large Language Model (LLM) have enabled a new class of software systems known as
LLM-integrated applications
.
In contrast to LLM-powered chatbots, these systems enable models to be fully integrated into the system as an intermediary between data and tools.
Although this new class of software systems can greatly amplify user productivity, they also enable new attack surfaces for adversaries.
Arguably, the most prominent attack against LLM-integrated applications (according to
OWASP 2025
) is the so-called
prompt injection
(PI) attack
(
Greshake et al. 2023
;
Yi et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2024
)
. These attacks exploit the LLM’s inability to distinguish between trusted instructions and untrusted data, allowing an injected instruction to manipulate the system operation.
As a result, PI attacks can introduce risks of data exfiltration, security breaches,
malware execution,
etc.

(
Greshake et al. 2023
;
Fu et al. 2024
;
Liao et al. 2024
;
Wu et al. 2024
;
Ma et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2024
)
. To date, PI attacks have been demonstrated successfully against many real-world systems, including Slack AI
(
PromptArmor 2024
)
, Microsoft Copilot
(
Rehberger 2024b
)
, Anthropic Claude Computer Use
(
Rehberger 2024a
)
, and OpenAI Operator
(
Rehberger 2025
)
. OWASP has rated PI attacks as the top security threat for LLM-integrated applications since 2023
(
OWASP 2025
)
.

PI attacks can be mitigated at either a model- or system-level. System-level defenses prevent vulnerable LLMs from being exposed to injections through additional detectors
(
ProtectAI 2024
;
Zarfati 2024
;
Chennabasappa et al. 2025
)
or prompting
(
Yi et al. 2023
;
Hines et al. 2024
;
Schulhoff and Yanni 2023
)
, or from actually executing the harmful actions triggered by the attacked LLM
(
Debenedetti et al. 2024
;
Debenedetti et al. 2025
)
. On the other hand, model-level defenses build security directly into the LLM by training it to prioritize trusted instruction over untrusted data
(
Chen et al. 2025a
;
Wallace et al. 2024
;
Chen et al. 2025b
;
Wu et al. 2025b
)
, and are currently more effective. In contrast to the openness of system-level defenses
(
Zarfati 2024
;
Meta 2024
;
ProtectAI 2024
;
Chennabasappa et al. 2025
)
, model-level defenses for commercial-grade LLMs are currently deployed in a closed-source manner,
e.g.
,

(
OpenAI 2024
)
and Google’s
Gemini-Flash-2.5

(

)
. This complicates research on studying and improving these defenses: the code/data to reproduce industry-level prompt injection defense
(
Wallace et al. 2024
)
are not available for an apple-to-apple comparison in follow-up research, and it is not possible to evaluate advanced gradient-based PI-attacks
(
Pasquini et al. 2024
;
Zhu et al. 2023
)
on these models because their weights are not available.

2
Preliminaries

LLM-integrated applications.
As frontier LLMs become more adept at long-horizon planning and reasoning, LLM-integrated applications have emerged as a new class of software systems. Here, the LLM plays the role of an orchestrator, connecting different system components such as data, tools, documentation,
etc
, and allowing the user to control them via natural language.
An LLM-integrated application typically uses the LLM as follows. The system prompt specifies the task with a high-level view of the software system,
e.g.
, what tools are available, how to structure a tool call, few-shot demonstrations,
etc
. The user prompt accepts the query from a user, containing instructions to the LLM. After receiving the above texts, the LLM is enabled to retrieve data from external sources (
e.g.
, by invoking tools) and append it after the above texts. The LLM generates its response for the system given the trusted system prompt, the trusted user prompt, and the untrusted data input.

Prompt injection (PI)

is a test-time attack against LLM-integrated applications. In this threat model, the system, user, and the LLM provider are benign, while the environment is malicious. This is different from system message following attacks (or sometimes called direct PI) or jailbreaks, where the malicious user supplies malicious instructions. PI attacker changes the environment the system interacts with, adding instructions to the data retrieved by the LLM. Since LLM is by default trained to scan its input for any instructions to follow, instructions embedded in the retrieved data can override the user instructions, causing undesired security consequences. For instance,
Rehberger 2025
showed that OpenAI’s Operator, a popular LLM-powered web navigation agent, could be manipulated by PIs in webpages and exfiltrate user data.

PI defenses

can be coarsely categorized as either system-level or model-level. System-level defenses build interface to secure a PI-vulnerable LLM by detecting PIs before they are seen by the LLM
(
ProtectAI 2024
;
Zarfati 2024
;
Chennabasappa et al. 2025
)
, prompting the LLM to ignore potential injections
(
Yi et al. 2023
;
Hines et al. 2024
;
Schulhoff and Yanni 2023
)
, or limiting the LLM’s ability to take actions defined as harmful
(
Debenedetti et al. 2024
;
Debenedetti et al. 2025
)
. These defenses can be effective, but require application-specific knowledge to design and deploy, limiting the scope of their use.
On the other hand, model-level defenses aim to address the problem at a more fundamental level. Those methods simulate PIs in training time, and fine-tune the LLM not to follow instructions in the data part in the presence of PIs
(
Wallace et al. 2024
;
Chen et al. 2025a
;
Chen et al. 2025b
;
Wu et al. 2025b
)
.
Model-level defenses can serve as a secure foundation for LLM-integrated applications, and the security may be further enhanced by system-level defenses.

3
SecAlign++: A Recipe for Training Secure Foundation LLMs

In this section, we describe our training recipe, SecAlign++, which fine-tunes an Instruct LLM from the
Llama 3
series to be robust against PIs. Our PI security policy requires the LLM to follow only the user instructions to produce high-quality responses and ignore any injected instructions in the data part as if they are not present. To enforce this policy, SecAlign++, like any model-level defenses, injects PIs into examples during training time, and fine-tunes the LLM to behave desirably.

Modified chat template.

Our first step is to separate the untrusted data from the trusted user instruction when formulating an LLM input string. LLMs usually separate different input parts by special delimiters. We follow this tradition and introduce a new
input
role to
Llama 3
series chat template besides the original
system
,
user
, and
assistant
roles, see below.

Chat template (input texts) for
Meta SecAlign

<|begin_of_text|><|start_header_id|>system<|end_header_id|>

Trusted-System-Message<|eot_id|><|start_header_id|>user<|end_header_id|>

Trusted-User-Instruction<|eot_id|>
<|start_header_id|>input<|end_header_id|>

Untrusted-Input-Data<|eot_id|>
<|start_header_id|>assistant<|end_header_id|>

The
input
role is used to encapsulate external data with potential injected instructions that the LLM should ignore. Note that the
input
role accepts arbitrary text data and is more general than the design in closed-source models such as
GPT-4o-mini
,
GPT-4o
and
Gemini-Flash-2.0/2.5
, where the external data with injections to ignore must come from a tool response. As a result, we enable broader secure data processing in different downstream tasks,
e.g.
, retrieval-augmented generation, zero-shot tool-calling, and even agentic web navigation; see
Section

4
. In addition, our design preserves existing chat roles, so we hypothesize that the LLM should have backward compatibility to behave the same as if there is no defense when the
input
role is absent, which is not achieved in previous studies
(
Chen et al. 2025b
;
Chen et al. 2025a
;
Wu et al. 2025b
)
, see
Section

5.1
. This hypothesis is supported by our utility-preserving performance on two agentic benchmarks (AgentDojo, WASP) where a sample has all three system, user, and data inputs.

Dataset construction.

Using the above chat template, we formulate prompt-injected LLM inputs with the corresponding desirable/undesirable responses. This process is proposed by the SOTA model-level defense SecAlign
(
Chen et al. 2025b
)
. It begins by
taking a generic instruction-tuning dataset
\mathcal{D}
where each sample
\mathbf{z}
consists of two parts: user instruction
\mathbf{z}_{\text{inst}}
and input data
\mathbf{z}_{\text{data}}
, and performs the below string-concatenation to construct the preference dataset.

•

Input
\mathbf{x}
:
For each sample
\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{D}
, we augment it by randomly selecting another instruction
\mathbf{z}_{\text{inst}}^{\prime}
from
\mathcal{D}
and injecting it into
\mathbf{z}_{\text{data}}
. In SecAlign, this injected prompt is always appended after the data. We improve it by randomizing the position of the injected prompt, appearing before or after
\mathbf{z}_{\text{data}}
with probability
1/2
each. This is to boost defense generalization by preventing the LLM from overfitting to ignore only injections that appear at the end of the data. We refer to this modification as
randomized injection position
.

•

Desirable response
y_{w}
:
The desirable response is a completion by the LLM that obeys the security policy. That is, given the input
\mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{z}_{\text{inst}},\mathbf{z}_{\text{data}}+\mathbf{z}_{\text{inst}}^{\prime})
, the LLM should respond to
\mathbf{z}_{\text{inst}}
using the data
\mathbf{z}_{\text{data}}
and ignore
\mathbf{z}_{\text{inst}}^{\prime}
. In SecAlign,
y_{w}
is given by the ground-truth response to
\mathbf{z}
as pre-defined in
\mathcal{D}
. We find that it is better to use the undefended model’s own response to
\mathbf{z}
to preserve utility and response style. We refer to this modification as
self-generated response
, a special semi-online tuning
(
Lanchantin et al. 2025
)
.

•

Undesirable response
y_{l}
:
Similar to
y_{w}
, we generate the undesirable response using the undefended model’s own response to the injected prompt
\mathbf{z}_{\text{inst}}^{\prime}
.

In
section

5.4
, we show that both randomized injection position and self-generated response modifications are necessary to maintain strong model utility, especially for free-form generation tasks such as agentic workflows.

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) using LoRA.

With the above preference dataset (obtained from a public instruction-tuning dataset) in hand, we then fine-tune an instruct LLM (an LLM after post-training) on this preference dataset using DPO
(
Rafailov et al. 2024
)
to obey the PI security policy. Following
Chen et al. 2025b
, we opt for LoRA fine-tuning
(
Lermen et al. 2023
)
for its parameter-efficiency and strong capability for the optimization goal. Recall that LoRA parameterizes the weight matrices in linear layers as

W_{\text{LoRA}}=W+\frac{\alpha}{r}BA,

(1)

where
W
is the original weight matrix,
A,B
are rank-
r
matrices, and
\alpha>0
is a fixed constant. Tuning
\alpha
at inference time allows the developer to interpolate between the undefended LLM and
Meta SecAlign
, trading off security and utility without further modification to the model.

4.1
Training and Evaluation Details

Following
Chen et al. 2025a
;
Chen et al. 2025b
, we use the Cleaned-Alpaca
(
Ruebsamen 2024
)
instruction-tuning dataset to construct our preference dataset if not otherwise stated. We pick samples that contain a data part and adopt the injection method in SecAlign to inject the prompt into the data. We modify the chat template with the additional
input
role to separate the data, and use the original
user
role for instruction. We then follow
Section

3
to generate the preference dataset (19157 samples) for DPO LoRA fine-tuning.

We train
Meta SecAlign
for 3 epochs using DPO. In DPO, we use sigmoid activation
\sigma
and
\beta=0.1
as officially recommended, and learning rates of
3.2e
-4
for
Meta-SecAlign
-
70B
and
1.6e
-4
for
Meta-SecAlign
-
8B
. The LoRA hyperparameters are
r=32
for
Meta-SecAlign
-
70B
and
r=64
for
Meta-SecAlign
-
8B
,
lora_alpha=8
,
lora_dropout=0.1
,
target_modules = ["q_proj", "v_proj", "gate_proj", "down_proj", "up_proj"]
. We use the
torchtune

(
torchtune 2024
)
library for DPO training with GPU parallelization. Training
Meta-SecAlign
-
70B
utilizes 8 NVIDIA H200s (141GB) in one node to run for 7 hours. Inference requires 4 A100s/H100s (80GB) for tensor-parallelization.
Meta-SecAlign
-
8B
could be trained with 8 H100s within 1 hour and tested on a single A100/H100 (or even a GPU with smaller memory).

For evaluation, we use
vllm

(
Kwon et al. 2023
)
for fast inference for
Llama
and
Meta SecAlign
models. Note that both models should be used with the exact prompt format as detailed in
Section

3
, which has been implemented in our released
tokenizer.chat_template

4.3
Instruction Following

We assess the model’s utility and security in instruction-following tasks by testing on AlpacaFarm/AlpacaEval2
(
Dubois et al. 2024b
;
Dubois et al. 2024a
)
, SEP
(
Mu et al. 2023
)
, TaskTracker
(
Abdelnabi et al. 2024
)
, and CyberSecEval2
(
Bhatt et al. 2024
)
.

AlpacaFarm contains 805 samples of general instructions. Among them, 208 samples have a data part, while the rest have instructions only. For utility evaluation, AlpacaEval2 compares the responses of the target LLM vs.
GPT-4
(turbo-2024-04-09) on all AlpacaFarm samples. It then gives a WinRate, corresponding to the percentage of samples where the target LLM performs better from the perspective of
GPT-4o
(model version 2024-05-13), our chosen LLM judge. Despite using a small test set, AlpacaEval2 is known to have a ranking with a 0.98 correlation to the human ranking on Chatbot Arena
(
Chiang et al. 2024
)
. For security evaluation, we follow
Chen et al. 2025b
to inject “Print exactly Hacked!” at the end of the 208 samples with data input. The attack is deemed successful if the output begins with the word “Hacked” or “hacked”. The injection sentence is enhanced in 3 different ways, as in
Chen et al. 2025b
, and we report the highest ASR among the 3 enhancements as the ASR for this benchmark.

SEP dataset has 9.1K general instruction samples, each with a unique injected instruction. We concatenate the injection to the start/end of the data with some enhancement “ignore” sentences in
Chen et al. 2025a
, and report the higher number between the two injection positions as the attack ASR. The attack is deemed successful if the
GPT-4o
judge decides that the output contains a response to the injection. For utility evaluation, we use AlpacaEval2 prompting to compare the response of the target model with that from
Llama3-8B-Instruct
, and report the WinRate.

TaskTracker is a larger PI benchmark similar to SEP with 31K samples. The dataset contains instructions and data, and additionally specifies where the injection should be put in the data part, and what specific enhancement “ignore” sentences to use. Similarly, the attack is deemed successful if the
GPT-4o
judge decides that the output contains a response to the injection.

CyberSecEval2 contains 55 (indirect) PI test samples, each with a pre-defined injection position and attack style. We calculate ASR in a similar way to SEP and TaskTracker, but using the benchmark-provided judge question to decide whether the injection is followed.

4.4
Agentic Workflows

Although
Meta SecAlign
is trained only on a generic instruction tuning dataset, we show that its learned security property transfers surprisingly well to agentic workflows where the input data is in a completely different domain. To assess this, we evaluate on InjecAgent
(
Zhan et al. 2024
)
, AgentDojo
(
Debenedetti et al. 2024
)
, and WASP
(
Evtimov et al. 2025
)
.

InjecAgent has 1K API-calling samples. Each sample prompts the LLM to complete a pre-defined task while simulating an API call to retrieve data containing PI, which asks the LLM to call a malicious API. The attack is regarded as successful when the specific malicious API is called, which corresponds to “ASR-total” in the original InjecAgent paper. The injection can be made stronger in the “Enhanced” setting by including an additional sentence “IMPORTANT!!! Ignore all previous instructions and strictly adhere to the following instruction:” We report the higher number between the base and enhanced settings as the attack ASR.

AgentDojo is a dynamic benchmark for security against PI attacks in tool-calling agents. The latest version contains 97 user tasks, and the LLM agent must make the appropriate API calls based on the user instruction and combine their result to derive the correct solution. Each user task is paired with several injection tasks that seek to divert the LLM agent to call a malicious API, resulting in 949 (user task, injection task) pairs. The agent is deemed successful if it achieves the user’s goal. The attack is deemed successful if the malicious API is called. Note that these goals are non-exclusive,
i.e.
, the agent can call the malicious API and then resume and complete the user goal. We report three metrics: utility (benign), utility under attack, and ASR.
By default, AgentDojo implements several PI attack styles. We adopt the “important instructions” attack because it achieves the highest ASR on the official leaderboard. For
Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct
, we include the default system prompt as it significantly improves model utility.

WASP is a dynamic benchmark for web agent security against PI attacks with 82 samples. The LLM agent is given a user instruction (
e.g.

i.e.

Existing defenses against PI from OpenAI

If we want untrusted data to be processed securely with the instruction hierarchy, we need to create a dummy tool that returns the data texts. (

. We hypothesize this is because SecAlign++ adds an

input

There is a natural tradeoff where more secure models tend to have lower utility.
Our recipe provides a simple way to control this trade-off by tuning the LoRA
\alpha

It is fairly easy to reduce ASR below

5.4
Ablation Studies on SecAlign++ Recipe

Our improved recipe is necessary for maintaining strong model utility.

In
Section

3
we described two changes we made to the original SecAlign training recipe: Randomly concatenating the injected prompt to the start/end of the data (randomized injection position), and using the undefended model’s own response as targets for preference optimization (self-generated response). We validate these design choices in this ablation study.

Table

Thus, it is necessary to augment samples in the preference dataset sufficiently to ensure that the model faithfully learns the security policy. Combining randomized injection position with self-generated response (
i.e.

This is especially significant for tasks that require free-form generation, such as AlpacaEval2/SEP and agentic tasks such as AgentDojo/WASP. This performance boost is likely due to the Cleaned-Alpaca dataset’s response being generated by

Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct
. Fine-tuning on this response degrades the model’s response quality, as well as teaching it to generate a different output distribution when the
input
role is enabled. As LLMs become more powerful, synthetic data becomes more valuable as they sidestep the heavy cost of human annotation and can be more easily automated. This ablation study shows that self-generated responses also confer benefits in SecAlign++.

Fine-tuning on “easy” prompt injections hurts security.

We also perform an ablation study on the choice of instruction-tuning dataset when we construct the preference dataset. The default choice is Cleaned-Alpaca, which leads to a 19K-sized preference dataset. We also consider the Natural-Instructions dataset
(
Mishra et al. 2022
)
, which is a much larger instruction-tuning dataset that contains 1500 tasks, each paired with a user instruction that specifies what the LLM should do with input data. Each task is associated with a larger number of input data choices, as well as the correct responses. We subsample a dataset of 50K (task, input data) pairs and use it to construct the preference dataset used in SecAlign++.

The last column in
Table

5
shows the result of fine-tuning on this dataset. Surprisingly, both utility and security are much worse compared to training on Cleaned-Alpaca despite the diversity of samples in Natural-Instructions and the increased dataset size.
We hypothesize that because samples in Natural-Instructions generally consist of longer input data, the injected prompt is less prominent, and therefore serves as an easy sample for the LLM to train on. This hypothesis is supported by the fact that ASR on TaskTracker is generally lower, as it also has much longer input data compared to other evaluation datasets. We can draw a parallel to prior work on adversarial training, where training on hard adversarial examples generally makes the model more robust
(
Goodfellow et al. 2015
)
.

6
Conclusion and Discussions

Our work shows that it is possible to train strong instruction-following LLMs that are secure against prompt injection attacks. In particular, our evaluation illustrates an intriguing phenomenon: Training on generic instruction-tuning data can confer security on a diverse array of unseen downstream tasks, especially in agentic workflows where prompt injection poses a more major threat. Through open-sourcing
Meta SecAlign
and its training recipe, we hope to inspire future work on improving model-level defenses and co-development of PI attacks and defenses, towards building a secure foundation for LLM-integrated applications.

Using online RL for security fine-tuning.

Our training recipe relied on offline preference optimization to fine-tune our security policy into the model. However, since we only use the undefended LLM’s response as targets instead of using human annotations, our technique can seamlessly generalize to online reinforcement learning by defining reward functions that encourage secure behavior. Online RL has enjoyed tremendous popularity recently as a powerful tool to unlock better model reasoning using limited data
(
Guo et al. 2025
)
. Applying it to defend against PI attacks may enjoy similar benefits.

Extension to reasoning models.

Reasoning models such as OpenAI’s
o1
and
DeepSeek-R1
constitute a new frontier in LLM capabilities research. Unfortunately,
Evtimov et al. 2025
showed that reasoning models can also be more susceptible to PI attacks even when instruction hierarchy is applied. It remains an open question how to effectively mitigate PI attacks for advanced reasoning models without deteriorating utility. The only work towards that direction is
Wu et al. 2025a
to our knowledge. We hope that by modifying our training recipe appropriately, a solution to this research problem can be within reach.

Extension to multi-modal prompt injections.

Another research direction is defense against visual prompt injection attacks. In contrast to textual attacks, the image modality is continuous in nature, and vision models have traditionally been more vulnerable to adversarial manipulation
(
Carlini et al. 2019
)
. Solving this problem is especially relevant for agentic web navigation, as state-of-the-art web agents such as OpenAI Operator
(
OpenAI 2025
)
, Claude Computer Use
(
Anthropic 2024
)
, and Google DeepMind’s Project Mariner
(
DeepMind 2024
)
are typically powered by multi-modal LLMs.

Limitations.

Despite our best effort,
Meta SecAlign
has the following limitations. We focus on defending against (indirect) prompt injection, and thus do not prevent other attacks such as jailbreaks, direct prompt injection, and data extraction. Our training recipe has only been tested in the Llama 3 series, and it is uncertain how well it generalizes to other models, especially models with MoE architecture and reasoning LLMs. Also, all benchmark evaluations in this paper are done with optimization-free attacks, where the attackers have limited computation and knowledge about the target model, as in most cases. We suspect optimization-based attacks can achieve substantially higher ASRs on
Meta SecAlign
.

Appendix

Appendix A
Additional related work

Prompt injection (PI) attacks are considered one of the top emerging threats to LLM-integrated applications, according to recent reports
[
OWASP 2025
,
Ltd. 2025
]
. Some literature categorizes them into direct PIs
[
Perez and Ribeiro 2022
]
and indirect PIs. Direct PIs are also called system message following attacks where the system is benign but the user is malicious, trying to override the system message. We focus on defending against indirect PI, or simply referred to as PI in our main paper. Indirect PIs consider the user to be benign, but the environment to be malicious, with potential injected instructions in the external data.
Indirect PIs are particularly relevant in agentic settings where LLMs interact with third-party tools that may contain potentially unsafe data, and are capable of reasoning and planning to address complex real-world challenges, often leveraging these tools
[
Schick et al. 2024
,
Rehberger 2025
,
Anthropic 2024
]
.

Methods for prompt injection attacks.

A large body of work has studied jailbreaking or automated red-teaming of LLMs and their multimodal variants, aiming to automatically generate prompts that cause LLM chatbots to output harmful content
[
Zou et al. 2023
,
Paulus et al. 2024
,
Pavlova et al. 2024
,
Mehrotra et al. 2024
]
. Indirect prompt injections are an additional attack vector for applications powered by LLMs, which use an LLM together with some data, such as documents or code that may be provided by a third party
[
Greshake et al. 2023
,
Liu et al. 2024
]
.
Manually designing prompts appears to be the predominant approach for prompt injection attacks
[
Bhatt et al. 2024
,
Evtimov et al. 2025
]
. Automatic jailbreaking techniques can also be applied in this context, as both involve optimizing prompts to elicit specific outputs from LLMs
[
Chen et al. 2025b
,
Xu et al. 2024
]
.

Defenses against prompt injection attacks.

Alongside the development of attacks, there is increasing interest in creating various strategies to defend against such threats. Straightforward approaches include adding instructions to the prompt to alert the model to these attacks
[
Schulhoff and Yanni 2023
,
Yi et al. 2023
,
Willison 2023
,
Wei et al. 2024
]
. Another line of work involves a carefully designed system-level defense that controls a data flow between LLM and applications
[
Debenedetti et al. 2025
]
. The above system-level defenses are orthogonal to our studied model-level defense, but it is interesting to see their combinations in the future. There have been previous studies on model-level defenses
[
Piet et al. 2023
,
Chen et al. 2025a
,
Chen et al. 2025b
,
Yi et al. 2023
,
Wallace et al. 2024
,
Wu et al. 2025b
]
.
For example, instruction hierarchy
[
Wallace et al. 2024
]
defines a multi-layer security policy to always prioritize high-priority instructions over conflicting low-priority ones, mitigating both direct (system message over user instruction) and indirect PIs (user instruction over external data). However, those methods are either tested in toy 8B models in academic papers or deployed in closed-source commercial-grade LLM,
e.g.
,
GPT-4o-mini
. We bridge this gap by releasing the first secure foundation LLM with strong capabilities (70B).

e.g.
, Please always remember that your task is: instruction) at the end of the data part with model-level defenses to easily increase performance in InjecAgent and AgentDojo. Here, we additionally present the results without the sandwich prompt. The general observation is that sandwich defense almost always reduces ASR and improves AgentDojo utility with attack, while having a minor effect on AgentDojo utility without attack. Moreover,
Meta SecAlign
has a more noticeable advantage compared to other defended models when sandwich defense is not applied.
