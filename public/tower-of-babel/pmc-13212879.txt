A new diagnostic kit using real-time polymerase chain reaction for the detection of Mycobacterium tuberculosis
Authors: Reyhaneh-Sadat Mousavi-Kohnesari, Mahsa Kavousi, Abbas Doosti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe prevalence of infections by nontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM), which present symptoms similar to tuberculosis (TB), is rising worldwide, highlighting the critical need for precise differential diagnosis. To minimize false-positive TB diagnoses, this study focused on a novel target unique to Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB). A real-time PCR assay was developed using specific primers and probes targeting the IS6110 region. Specific primers and probes were designed using Primer3plus and evaluated in silico using the NCBI database. An internal control was incorporated by synthesizing and cloning a fragment of the 16 S rRNA gene into the pcDNA3.1(+) vector, which was then transformed into Escherichia coli Top10F. Standards were prepared to determine the limit of detection (LOD) and assay efficiency. The kit’s accuracy and reproducibility were evaluated using real-time PCR on 15 MTB-positive DNA samples. The designed primers and probes specifically amplified a 365 bp fragment of the IS6110 region. The extracted plasmid concentration was 76.6 ng/µl. The assay utilized probes labeled with HEX for the IS6110 gene and FAM for the internal control. The standard curve demonstrated a linear dynamic range with a correlation coefficient (R²) of > 0.98 and an efficiency of 100%. The LOD was determined to be 10 copies/µL. Evaluation of accuracy and repeatability indicated high consistency, with minimal variation in Ct values across repetitions. Furthermore, the kit demonstrated stability across different manufacturing LOTs. In conclusion, this study demonstrates that the developed quantitative Real-Time PCR assay, utilizing IS6110-HEX and 16 S rRNA-FAM probes, effectively identifies M. tuberculosis. Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB), a contagious respiratory disease transmitted primarily via airborne particles, remains a major global public health concern. It is the second leading cause of death from infectious diseases worldwide, following HIV (Wei et al. 2020; Bosworth et al. 2022). The disease is mainly caused by members of the Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTC), with M. tuberculosis recognized as the principal pathogen in human TB cases (Bosworth et al. 2022). Other MTC species such as M. canettii and M. africanum are also implicated in human infections, particularly in specific African regions (Esteban & Muñoz-Egea, 2017).

While M. bovis BCG remains the only attenuated strain used for vaccination, several other MTC members—including M. bovis, M. caprae, M. microti, and M. pinnipedii—are predominantly associated with animal tuberculosis, though all possess zoonotic potential (El-Sayed et al. 2016; Pérez de Val et al. 2022). Among these, M. bovis and M. caprae are phylogenetically close and represent the primary agents of zoonotic TB (Teppawar et al., 2018; Reddington et al. 2011).

The global burden of zoonotic TB is likely underestimated, especially in low-resource regions where diagnostic capacity is limited and extrapulmonary manifestations are common. Estimates indicate that in high-income nations, zoonotic TB accounts for 0.5–7.2% of cases, while in low-income settings the proportion may reach 15% (De la Rua-Domenech 2006; Djelouadji et al. 2008). The absence of simple, rapid, and affordable diagnostic tools in endemic regions continues to hinder disease control (Schemes 2019). According to the World Health Organization, more than 10 million new TB cases occur annually, with over 95% reported in low- and middle-income countries, leading to approximately 1.8 million deaths each year (Sotgiu et al., 2017).

Advancements in TB diagnostics include a variety of molecular and culture-based approaches (Lessem 2016; Hernández-Neuta et al. 2019). However, conventional methods such as smear microscopy and culture have notable limitations. Smear microscopy lacks sensitivity—particularly in HIV-coinfected individuals—while culture methods are labor-intensive and may require up to 10 weeks to yield results (Reid and Shah 2009; Souii et al. 2016).

Nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs), particularly real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR), offer rapid and highly sensitive detection of TB (Mohammed et al. 2015). RT-PCR monitors DNA amplification in real-time using fluorescent probes, generating a cycle threshold (Ct) value that reflects initial target DNA levels (Mazzeo et al. 2024; Bapat et al. 2017; van den Brom et al. 2020; Jagielski et al. 2016; Piri-Gharaghie et al. 2022, 2023; Balcells et al. 2011; Guescini et al. 2013). RT-PCR combines sensitivity, specificity, and automation, reducing operator time and contamination risk (Babafemi et al. 2017). It can detect as few as 6 bacterial DNA copies/mL, compared with the 5000–10,000 bacilli/mL required for smear microscopy (Zheng et al. 2021; Steingart et al. 2006). Although culture remains the gold standard, it requires at least 100 viable bacilli, making it unsuitable for rapid diagnosis in clinical or field settings (Tissari et al. 2010; Ray et al., 2013).

Given these limitations, there is an urgent need for accurate, rapid, and field-applicable diagnostic platforms capable of detecting TB and differentiating MTC members from non-tuberculous mycobacteria (NTM). In this study, we developed a multiplex real-time PCR assay designed to simultaneously detect M. tuberculosis and distinguish it from NTM species—addressing a critical gap relevant to both human and veterinary medicine.

Materials and methods

Conventional and real-time PCR primers and probe design

Oligonucleotide primers and probes were designed by aligning genetic diagnostic target genes identified in this investigation, complying with general recommendations and criteria [30]. Metabion Company (Metabion, Germany) synthesized the primers and probes (Table 1). Primers were engineered to have a melting temperature (Tm) of 57 °C, whereas probes targeting IS6110 were developed to have a Tm approximately 3 °C higher. The probe intended for RNase-P detection exhibited a Tm of 66.7 °C, attributable to a target region with a high G/C content (60.2%). Probes were meticulously crafted to ensure specificity for each target. Oligonucleotide assays were developed with similar thermodynamic characteristics to facilitate the straightforward multiplexing of monoplex assays following the completion of specificity and sensitivity evaluations. The sensitivity and specificity of all real-time PCR assays were first assessed in a monoplex format utilizing probes labeled with HEX and Black Hole Quencher 1 (BHQ1). Following optimization, each probe was labeled with distinct fluorescent dyes for integration into a multiplex real-time PCR format. The IS6110-specific probe was labeled with HEX (5’-HEX, 3’-BHQ1). The 16 S rRNA-specific probe was labeled with FAM (5’-FAM, 3’-BHQ1). The RNase-P specific probe was labeled with CY5 (5’-CY5, 3’-BHQ2).

Conventional PCR

Conventional PCR was conducted using the primers specified in Table 1 on a thermal cycler (AusDiagnostics, Australia). The total volume for all reactions was 25 µL, which included 12.5 µL of Master mix buffer, 1 µL each of forward and reverse primers (0.2 µM final concentration), 2.5 µL of template cDNA, and 9 µL of nuclease-free water. The cycling parameters consisted of initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 32 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C (1 min), annealing at 64 °C (1 min), and elongation at 72 °C (1 min), with a final elongation step at 72 °C for 10 mins.

Preparation of standard plasmid

This study utilized recombinant vectors as positive controls. To obtain recombinant plasmids pcDNA3.1(+)/MTB IS6110-16 S rRNA, specific target sections were first amplified using the primers listed in Table 1 and then inserted into the pcDNA3.1(+) plasmid (Generay, China). The IS6110 and 16 S rRNA genes were incorporated into the plasmids to create pcDNA3.1(+)/MTB IS6110-16 S rRNA (pDNA-IS6110-16 S rRNA). The cloning procedure was confirmed by polymerase chain reaction (PCR), restriction enzyme digestion, and sequencing. The IS6110 and 16 S rRNA genes were amplified using PCR with the designated primers (Table 1), and the resulting product was extracted from a 1% agarose gel. The restriction enzymes EcoRI and XhoI (GENEray) were employed to cleave the recombinant vector. GENEray performed nucleotide sequencing of the recombinant plasmid.

Transformation of pDNA-IS6110-16SrRNA recombinant vector in E. coli Top10F with calcium chloride method

The pDNA-IS6110-16 S rRNA recombinant vector was transformed into E. coli TOP10F′ competent cells using the calcium chloride (CaCl2) method. Recombinant plasmids were screened by selecting transformants on LB-ampicillin agar. The presence of the IS6110-16 S rRNA gene insertion was confirmed by colony PCR analysis. Competent cells were inoculated onto Luria–Bertani (LB) agar supplemented with ampicillin and incubated at 30 °C overnight. The recombinant plasmid was isolated using a Plasmid Mini Extraction Kit and examined with specific primers.

Determining the concentration of extracted plasmids and preparing kit standards

The concentration of extracted recombinant plasmids was measured using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. To assess purity, absorbance was measured at 260 nm and 280 nm wavelengths. The quantity of the target plasmid was calculated using the following equation: Copy number (copies/µL) = (DNA amount (g)) / [DNA length (bp) 660 (g/mol/bp)]. These synthetic IS6110-16 S rRNA gene sequences, subcloned into the vector, serve as the standards for this kit (TBS1–TBS5). The vector materials were diluted with DEPC water and aliquoted into control microtubes upon receipt and quality inspection. Standard curves were generated using a tenfold serial dilution of the recombinant plasmid harboring the M. tuberculosis gene segment, with concentrations ranging from to copies per mL. Ct values for each dilution were assessed in triplicate, and a standard curve was constructed for each run using linear regression analysis.

Production of a mastermix for M. tuberculosis kit

A commercial master mix (Amplicon, England) was used as the base for constructing the tuberculosis kit. This master mix contains deoxynucleotide triphosphates (dNTPs), magnesium ions, DNA polymerase, and necessary buffers. The final master mix (TB Mix) was prepared by adding primers and probes (Table 1) to the base master mix to a final concentration of 100 pmol/µL. The master mix was supplemented with the HEX-labeled probe for the IS6110 gene (yellow fluorescence), the FAM-labeled probe specific for the 16 S rRNA gene segment of Mycobacterium spp. (green fluorescence), and the CY5-labeled probe specific for the RNase-P gene (far-red fluorescence) as an internal control.

Internal control of M. tuberculosis kit

The Master Mix Kit includes an integrated control designed to identify reaction inhibition. The internal control consists of a primer set and a CY5-labeled probe. Similar to the positive control, this sequence was synthesized via gene synthesis (Generay, China). The plasmid was amplified by transformation using the calcium chloride technique, followed by plasmid extraction. The components prepared for this kit are listed in Table 2.

Real time PCR reaction

All tubes were initially positioned on ice to ensure thorough thawing of contents. Components were mixed gently, followed by brief vortexing. Seven PCR microtubes were placed in a cold block: one for the tested sample, five for standards, and one for the negative control. 15 µL of TB Mix was immediately added to each tube. Subsequently, 1 µL of DNA, standard, or water was introduced into each tube. In each assay, one tube served as the No Template Control (NTC), containing water instead of DNA. Instructions were provided for Real-Time PCR instruments capable of detecting red, green, and yellow channels (Ausdiagnostic, Australia). The device was programmed according to the timetable in Table 3, and the reaction was initiated. Fluorescence measurement was conducted at 60 °C for CY5, FAM, and HEX dyes. An increase in yellow fluorescence was associated with the M. tuberculosis complex, whereas a rise in green fluorescence originated from Non-tuberculous Mycobacteria. A sample was deemed positive only if it exhibited a sigmoid curve and a logarithmic phase; under these conditions, the Ct value was considered valid. In the absence of a sigmoid curve, the sample was deemed negative, and any Ct value present was considered insignificant.

Analytical performance evaluation of M. tuberculosis diagnostic kit

Assessment of analytical specificity

Specificity tests were conducted in two phases, in silico and in vitro, to assess the sensitivity and accuracy of the reaction and eliminate false positives. The primer and probe sequences utilized by this kit were first subjected to a BLAST search in the NCBI database to confirm 100% specificity for M. tuberculosis.

Determining the limit of detection (LOD) of the kit

The limit of detection (LOD) was established using plasmid standards. A tenfold serial dilution series of plasmid standards for M. tuberculosis was assessed. A standard stock containing copies per microliter was diluted by a factor of 10 to reach a concentration of copies/µL. Six samples were generated with precise dilutions, and real-time PCR reactions were conducted for each sample in triplicate.

Evaluation of accuracy and repeatability of the kit

The accuracy and reproducibility of the kit were assessed using two methods. First, repeatability was examined across various LOTs of the kit (three distinct LOTs). Second, intra-assay repeatability was conducted by two distinct users using the same LOT. To assess repeatability, 15 DNA samples associated with the M. tuberculosis complex were individually examined over three consecutive days with three iterations per run, in accordance with the CLSI EP05 standard.

Assessment of kit stability

An in-use stability test was conducted via multiple freeze-thaw cycles to assess the stability of the developed kit. This approach used positive DNA samples with concentrations of 1000, 100, and 10 copies/µL. Three kits from three distinct batches were analyzed under five different time conditions to assess their stability in reactions with the three diluted positive samples.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 5.0 software was employed to analyze the acquired findings. The one-way ANOVA approach was utilized to compare means. PASW software was utilized to ascertain the detection limit of the kit (95%). Significant differences were defined as p < 0.05.

Determination of the limit of detection (LOD)

To establish the kit’s limit of detection (LOD), a recombinant plasmid containing the IS6110-16SrRNA fragment was initially quantified and subjected to serial 10-fold dilutions. The analytical sensitivity was evaluated using these dilutions. The LOD was defined as the lowest concentration at which 95% of replicates were positive.

As shown in Table 4, the mean Ct values increased as the DNA concentration decreased, indicating an inverse relationship between template quantity and Ct. The lowest concentration consistently detected was 10 copies/µL, with a mean Ct of 35.1. At concentrations below this threshold (1 copy/µL), detection was inconsistent (mean Ct: 38.5), as evidenced by the higher standard deviation and failure to detect the target in all replicates. These results demonstrate the kit’s capacity to recognize target DNA even at low concentrations.

Discussion

M. tuberculosis is a pathogenic species belonging to the genus Mycobacterium and is the primary etiological agent of TB (Gagneux 2018). Tuberculosis is a complex and significant public health challenge in both developed and developing regions (Chakaya et al. 2021). According to the WHO, approximately 8.8 million active TB cases are diagnosed annually, resulting in nearly 2 million deaths (Glaziou et al. 2018). Although once considered manageable, TB incidence is rising due to the emergence of aggressive and drug-resistant strains. While most cases occur in low-income nations—where 22 countries account for 80% of the global burden—global mobility and efficient transmission mechanisms increase the risk of spread worldwide (Liebenberg et al. 2022).

Current TB control strategies face significant limitations, with less than 25% of cases detected globally (Chiang et al. 2013). The absence of rapid and accurate diagnostic tools remains one of the major barriers to effective TB control, as timely diagnosis is critical for interrupting transmission (Kant et al., 2024).

A wide range of diagnostic approaches—microscopy, culture, histopathology, tuberculin skin testing (TST), serology, interferon-gamma release assays (IGRAs), and nucleic acid amplification (NAA) tests—are used to detect TB (Arzi 2018; Natarajan et al. 2020). Smear microscopy, though widely used, has variable and often poor sensitivity (0–40%) and cannot differentiate MTC from NTM species (den Hertog et al. 2010). Culture methods show higher sensitivity but require long incubation periods (2–10 weeks) and expert personnel (Parsons et al. 2011). Histopathological examination can support diagnosis by identifying granulomatous inflammation but cannot reliably distinguish extrapulmonary TB from other granulomatous diseases, including NTM infections, sarcoidosis, or leprosy (Hunter 2011; Brito et al. 2022).

RT-PCR provides rapid, sensitive, and specific detection of TB DNA and is increasingly used for clinical diagnosis (Soni et al. 2022). Its major advantages include short turnaround time, quantification of bacterial load, and reduced contamination risk due to automation (Sefers and Schmitz 2018). In this study, we evaluated an RT-PCR assay targeting the IS6110 gene, a well-established and highly specific marker for the M. tuberculosis complex. Our results indicate that the IS6110-RT-PCR assay demonstrates high specificity and diagnostic performance, consistent with prior findings (Reddington et al. 2011).

We designed specific primers and probes targeting the IS6110 region and cloned this fragment into the pcDNA3.1(+) vector to generate stable kit standards. This allowed efficient production of positive controls while reducing overall kit manufacturing costs. The assay demonstrated a linear detection range of 10 copies/µL, supporting its potential utility for the quantification of MTBC in clinical specimens.

Our findings align with the two-step real-time PCR developed by Park et al. (2023), which successfully differentiated M. tuberculosis from six dominant NTM species. While our current study relied on in silico analysis for specificity, our assay offers a simpler and more cost-effective workflow. Similarly, our results are consistent with Lorente-Leal et al. (2019), who validated a real-time PCR assay targeting the mpb70 gene for MTBC detection in animal tissues. Additionally, the IS4 primer/probe set developed by Wang et al. (2019) emphasizes the value of IS6110 as a high-sensitivity target across diverse strains.

A key feature of our assay is the incorporation of a synthetic internal control, enabling the detection of false negatives. The optimized single-temperature amplification protocol (60 °C) allows for potential multiplexing with additional targets in the future. Considering the persistence of TB as a global health challenge, accurate and accessible diagnostics remain essential. The simplicity, adaptability, and strong performance of our assay in this initial evaluation suggest it holds promise as a cost-effective diagnostic tool. However, further validation with larger clinical cohorts and experimental testing against Non-Tuberculous Mycobacteria (NTM) is required before it can be implemented for routine diagnostic use.

A limitation of this study is the specificity validation regarding Non-Tuberculous Mycobacteria (NTM). While in silico analysis strongly suggests that the primers and probe are specific to M. tuberculosis and do not cross-react with NTM, the current study did not include experimental wet-lab validation using NTM isolates. Therefore, further studies with a larger sample size and the inclusion of clinical NTM strains are recommended to fully confirm the analytical specificity and rule out potential cross-reactivity in a laboratory setting. The selection of both the IS6110 and 16 S rRNA genes serves distinct and complementary purposes in this assay. The IS6110 element is highly specific to the Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTBC) and is present in multiple copies within the genome, which enhances the sensitivity of the assay for TB detection. In contrast, the 16 S rRNA gene is a conserved region found across all Mycobacterium species. By including the 16 S rRNA target, the assay can detect the broader presence of mycobacterial DNA. In a multiplex format, this combination allows for the differentiation of MTBC (positive for IS6110) from Non-Tuberculous Mycobacteria (NTM) (positive for 16 S rRNA but negative for IS6110), thereby increasing the diagnostic specificity and reducing false negatives.

Conclusion

Real-time PCR has recently emerged as a promising tool for diagnosing Mycobacterium infections. This approach facilitates the rapid identification of M. tuberculosis in clinical samples and offers potential for detecting treatment resistance using specialized probes. In this study, we designed specific primers and probes targeting the IS6110 region to identify members of the M. tuberculosis complex.

The assessment of accuracy and repeatability within a single LOT, between two users, and across different LOTs demonstrated that the kit exhibits high consistency. The Ct values obtained from tests and repetitions showed minimal variation, indicating good reproducibility. In conclusion, our findings suggest that this novel real-time PCR-based assay is effective for detecting pathogenic M. tuberculosis complex species in sputum samples and culture isolates. While these initial results are promising, further extensive validation with larger clinical cohorts is necessary to establish the assay as a routine diagnostic procedure in clinical practice.
