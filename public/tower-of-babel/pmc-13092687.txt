Comment on “Prevalence and risk factors of paediatric seizures in COVID-19 infection: A study from Thailand”
Authors: Vidya Singaravelu, Amit Singh Pawaiya, Priyanka Aher, Hariharan Srinivasan
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with interest the article titled, “Prevalence and risk factors of pediatric seizures in COVID-19 infection: A study from Thailand,” reporting seizure prevalence and outcomes among 963 paediatric patients with SARS-CoV-2 infection admitted to a single centre, of whom 40 (4.2%) presented with clinical seizures and were followed for up to two years [1]. The authors conclude that seizures were predominantly febrile during the Omicron period, that younger age and severe COVID-19 are risk factors, and that neurodevelopmental outcomes were generally favourable. We commend the authors for addressing an important question in paediatric neuroinfectious disease.

A first concern is that the description of the study population and the reported prevalence of seizures appears internally and externally inconsistent. In Results, 40 of 963 admissions had seizures, which corresponds to 4.2%, yet the abstract and discussion quote a seizure prevalence of “0.76” or “0.76%,” which is mathematically incompatible with 40/963 and may reflect misplacement of a decimal point. This figure is substantially lower than the 13.4% seizure prevalence among paediatric SARS-CoV-2 admissions in a population-based cohort from Sweden [2] and the Omicron-era series from China, where prolonged seizures, status epilepticus and early recurrence were not uncommon among febrile children with SARS-CoV-2 infection [3]. Clarifying whether 0.76 represents a proportion per 1000 admissions, a typographical error, or a differently defined denominator (e.g. seizures per all paediatric admissions rather than COVID-19 admissions) would greatly aid interpretation.

A further issue is that the target population and risk-factor analysis would benefit from clearer specification. The Methods describe inclusion of paediatric patients “who had clinical seizures as a primary symptom alongside COVID-19 infection,” whereas the Results section refers to 963 paediatric COVID-19 admissions, of whom 40 had seizures. This ambiguity makes it difficult to understand whether the denominator comprised all SARS-CoV-2 admissions or only those presenting with seizures. Large multicentre cohorts and registry studies have shown that neurologic complications, especially seizures, are neither rare nor uniformly benign in children hospitalised with COVID-19, and are associated with higher intensive care use, longer length of stay, and greater risk of new neurocognitive or functional morbidity [2], [4], [5]. Greater detail on how non-seizure COVID-19 admissions were identified, and whether multivariable modelling was performed to adjust for chronic neurologic comorbidity and MIS-C, would allow more robust comparison with these datasets.

The conclusion that two-year neurodevelopmental outcomes were favourable warrants cautious framing. The follow-up appears to have been based on chart review rather than systematic neurocognitive or functional assessment. In contrast, prospective international cohorts and long-term electronic health record analyses suggest that children with SARS-CoV-2–related neurologic manifestations, including seizures, are at increased risk of new neurocognitive or functional morbidity at discharge [4] and of epilepsy or seizures over a two-year horizon compared with other respiratory infections [6]. Without standardised outcome measures, it is difficult to exclude subtle cognitive, behavioural or educational impacts, particularly in younger children. Even a brief description of follow-up methods (e.g. structured clinic assessment versus telephone report) and attrition would strengthen the authors' reassuring message and help clinicians counsel families.

We believe that clarifying the seizure denominator and prevalence, more precisely defining the study cohort, and qualifying the interpretation of long-term outcomes in the context of emerging international evidence would enhance the contribution of this valuable study. Such refinements would improve comparability across cohorts and help clinicians and families better understand the true burden and prognosis of COVID-19–associated seizures in children.
