Abstract
. In this work we establish several monotonicity and decomposition results in the framework of random regular graphs. Among other results, we show that, for a wide range of parameters d 1 ≤ d 2 d_{1}\leq d_{2} , there exists a coupling of G ⁡ ( n , d 1 ) G(n,d_{1}) and G ⁡ ( n , d 2 ) G(n,d_{2}) satisfying that G ⁡ ( n , d 1 ) ⊆ G ⁡ ( n , d 2 ) G(n,d_{1})\subseteq G(n,d_{2}) with high probability, confirming a conjecture of Gao, Isaev and McKay in a new regime. Our contributions include new tools for analysing contiguity and total variation distance between random regular graph models, a novel procedure for generating unions of random edge-disjoint perfect matchings, and refined estimates of Gao’s bounds on the number of perfect matchings in random regular graphs. In addition, we make progress towards another conjecture of Isaev, McKay, Southwell and Zhukovskii.



1.
Introduction

The theory of random graphs has seen a number of fascinating advancements over the last few decades.
Part of the effort has been focused on understanding the relations between different models or instances of the same model.
The simplest example of such a relation is illustrated by the widely used technique of
sprinkling
in Erdős-Rényi graphs or, more generally, in percolation on graphs and lattices.
The technique exploits the simple observation that, given a graph
G
, the
p
-percolated graph
G_{p}
can be written as a union of independent random subgraphs of
G
percolated with probabilities
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
for
p,p_{1},p_{2}\in[0,1]
satisfying
1-p=(1-p_{1})(1-p_{2})
.
While sprinkling has immensely advanced the field of percolation, its applicability is limited to models where edges appear independently of each other.

The efficiency of powerful percolation techniques has motivated the need for establishing connections with other random graph models where methods like sprinkling are not directly available.
A cornerstone work in this direction is due to Kim and Vu
[
17
]
who showed that the Erdős-Rényi random graph
G\sim G(n,p)
and the random regular graph
H\sim G(n,d)
with
\omega(\log n)=np\approx d\leq n^{1/3-o(1)}
can be coupled so that, roughly speaking,

•

if
np
is slightly smaller than
d
, then
G\subseteq H
, and

•

if
np
is slightly larger than
d
, then
H\setminus G
contains very few edges.

Moreover, Kim and Vu famously conjectured that the range of values where the above relations hold can be extended to
np\approx d\leq(1-\varepsilon)n
,
and the second statement can be improved to an exact inclusion of
H
in
G
.
The significance of this conjecture in the framework of random regular graphs is indisputable: indeed, while switching techniques for computing probabilities in dense random regular graphs and, more generally, random graphs with fixed degrees, exist
[
10
]
(see also
[
5
,
15
,
16
,
21
,
22
,
23
,
25
,
28
]
for related applications of switchings), the technical proofs of many related results often lack the elegance of their Erdős-Rényi counterparts.
The conjecture has seen significant developments in the last twenty years
[
3
,
9
,
19
]
and is currently confirmed for the range
np\approx d\geq(\log n)^{4}
due to Gao, Isaev, and McKay
[
8
]
.

While the Kim–Vu conjecture serves as a bridge between Erdős-Rényi graphs and random regular graphs, comparing random regular graphs of different degree has also attracted attention
[
12
,
14
,
26
,
28
]
.
Note that monotonicity for random regular graphs with different degrees (with respect to inclusion) is related to the Kim–Vu conjecture, yet it does not follow from it in any straightforward way.
At the same time, establishing monotone couplings remains highly desirable.
Such monotonicity for random regular graphs was conjectured by Gao, Isaev and McKay
[
9
]
.

Conjecture 1.1

(Conjecture 1.2 in
[
9
]
)
.

Consider integers
d_{1}\leq d_{2}
with
d_{1},d_{2}\in[1,n-1]
, such that
(d_{1},d_{2})\neq(1,2)
,
(d_{1},d_{2})\neq(n-3,n-2)
and each of
d_{1}n
and
d_{2}n
is even. Then, there exists a coupling of
G_{1}\sim G(n,d_{1})
and
G_{2}\sim G(n,d_{2})
such that
\mathbb{P}(G_{1}\subseteq G_{2})=1-o(1)
.

Conjecture

1.1
is known to hold in a few cases
[
6
,
8
,
12
]
:

•

d_{1}=1
and
d_{2}\in[3,n-1]
,

•

\omega((\log n)^{7})=d_{1}\leq n^{1/7}/\log n
and
d_{2}\in[d_{1},n-1]
,

•

\omega(1)=d_{1}\leq n^{1/3}
and
d_{2}=d_{1}+1
,

•

(\log n)^{4}\leq cd_{1}\leq d_{2}\leq n-(\log n)^{4}
for some constant
c>1
,

•

\min\{d_{1},d_{2}-d_{1}\}=\omega(n/\log n)
.

A closer look at the listed cases reveals that, while the monotonicity properties of random regular graphs of large polylogarithmic degrees are relatively well understood, graphs with slowly growing degrees remain elusive.
Our first result bridges this gap and confirms
Conjecture

1.1
in the case when
n
is even and
d_{1}
is not too large.

Theorem 1.2
.

Consider
n
even,
d_{1}\in[3,n^{1/7}/\log n]
and
d_{2}\in[d_{1},n-1]
with
d_{2}=\omega(1)
.
Then there exists a coupling of
G_{1}\sim G(n,d_{1})
and
G_{2}\sim G(n,d_{2})
such that
\mathbb{P}(G_{1}\subseteq G_{2})=1-o(1)
.

Theorem

1.2
will be derived by combining the second point in the above list with several technical results guaranteeing that, in a certain sense,
G(n,d_{2})
can be decomposed into edge-disjoint
G(n,d_{1})
and
d_{2}-d_{1}
perfect matchings for
3\leq d_{1}\leq d_{2}\leq n^{1/10}
.

While
Conjecture

1.1
requires that the graphs
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are chosen uniformly at random, no restriction is imposed on the graph
G_{2}\setminus E(G_{1})
.
With an eye towards recovering the properties of the sprinkling technique in the framework of random regular graphs, requiring that the complement of
G_{1}
behaves as a random
(d_{2}-d_{1})
-regular graph is a natural additional restriction.
More precisely, for
d_{1},d_{2}\in[1,n-1]
with
d_{1}\leq d_{2}
, we denote by
G(n,d_{1})\oplus G(n,d_{2}-d_{1})
the random
d_{2}
-regular graph obtained by sampling independently
G_{1}\sim G(n,d_{1})
and
G_{2}^{\prime}\sim G(n,d_{2}-d_{1})
conditionally on
G_{1},G_{2}^{\prime}
being edge-disjoint, and taking their union.
Isaev, McKay, Southwell and Zhukovskii
[
12
]
conjectured that the distribution of
G(n,d_{1})\oplus G(n,d_{2}-d_{1})
is essentially the one of
G(n,d_{2})
as long as
d_{2}
tends to infinity.

Conjecture 1.3

(Conjecture 1.2 in
[
12
]
)
.

For
d_{1},d_{2}\in[1,n-1]
with
d_{1}\leq d_{2}=\omega(1)
, there exists a coupling of
G_{\oplus}\sim G(n,d_{1})\oplus G(n,d_{2}-d_{1})
and
G\sim G(n,d_{2})
such that
\mathbb{P}(G_{\oplus}=G)=1-o(1)
.

The authors of
[
12
]
verified
Conjecture

1.3
in several cases, namely:

•

d_{1}=1
,
d_{2}=\omega(1)
and
d_{2}=o(n^{1/3})
,

•

\min\{d_{1},d_{2}-d_{1},n-d_{2}\}=\omega(n/\log n)
,

•

d_{1}^{2}(n/\min\{d_{2}-d_{1},n-d_{2}+d_{1}\})^{2d_{1}}\leq(\log n)/3
.

They also observed that the assumption
d_{2}=\omega(1)
is actually necessary. We contribute to the understanding of
Conjecture

1.3
by verifying that, for large
d_{2}\leq n^{1/10}
, the distributions of
G(n,d_{1})\oplus G(n,d_{2}-d_{1})
, with
d_{1}
far from
1
and
d_{2}
, are asymptotically the same.

Theorem 1.4
.

Consider
n
even,
d_{2}=\omega(1)
and
d_{2}=O(n^{1/10})
.
Fix
d_{1},d_{1}^{\prime}
with

\min\{d_{1},d_{1}^{\prime},d_{2}-d_{1},d_{2}-d_{1}^{\prime}\}=\omega(1).

Then, there exists a coupling of
G_{\oplus}\sim G(n,d_{1})\oplus G(n,d_{2}-d_{1})
and
G_{\oplus}^{\prime}\sim G(n,d^{\prime}_{1})\oplus G(n,d_{2}-d_{1}^{\prime})
such that
\mathbb{P}(G_{\oplus}=G_{\oplus}^{\prime})=1-o(1)
.

Simply put, while
Theorem

1.4
does not claim that
G(n,d_{2})
can be decomposed as a disjoint union of
G(n,d_{1})
and
G(n,d_{2}-d_{1})
, it confirms that this disjoint union has asymptotically the same distribution as long as each of
d_{1}
and
d_{2}-d_{1}
is large.

A classic result due to Molloy, Robalewska, Robinson and Wormald
[
26
]
ensures that, when
d=O(1)
and
d_{1}+\dotsb+d_{k}=d
with
(d,d_{1},d_{2})\neq(2,1,1)
, the properties holding with high probability in the model
G(n,d_{1})\oplus\dotsb\oplus G(n,d_{k})
also hold with high probability in the model
G(n,d)
and vice versa.
Using this result to decompose a random
d
-regular graph on an even number of vertices into a disjoint union of perfect matchings and Hamilton cycles has sometimes been instrumental for the analysis of random regular graphs, see
[
1
,
18
,
28
]
.
In a more structural setting, Ferber and Jain
[
4
]
showed that pseudorandom
d
-regular graphs on an even number of vertices can be decomposed into
d
edge-disjoint perfect matchings, that is, such graphs have a 1-factorisation.
While the latter result holds in vast generality and, in particular, implies that
G(n,d)
has a 1-factorisation with high probability, the probabilistic properties of the exhibited 1-factorisation remain unclear.
In this work, at the price of restricting the range of values for
d
, we show that the model
G(n,1)\oplus\dotsb\oplus G(n,1)
, with
G(n,1)
repeated
d
times, serves as a good approximation of the model of random
d
-regular graphs in the following sense.

Theorem 1.5
.

Consider
n
even and
d\in[3,n^{1/10}]
.
Fix a sequence
(A_{n})_{n\in 2\mathbb{N}}
where
A_{n}
is a set of
d
-regular graphs on
n
vertices.
If
G(n,1)\oplus\dotsb\oplus G(n,1)\in A_{n}
holds with high probability, then
G(n,d)\in A_{n}
holds with high probability as well.

We remark that, with some more effort, the range of values for
d
in
Theorems

1.4
and
1.5
may be slightly improved but the current estimates on the variance of the number of perfect matchings in random regular graphs (see
[
6
, Theorem 10]
needed for our
Proposition

3.8
) do not allow us to go beyond
d\leq n^{1/7-\varepsilon}
.
We restrict our analysis to the range
d\in[3,n^{1/10}]
for the sake of simplicity.
In general, we believe that
Theorem

1.5
as well as its reciprocal implication should hold for
d\in[3,n-3]
.

Conjecture 1.6
.

Consider
n
even and
d\in[3,n-3]
.
Fix a sequence
(A_{n})_{n\in 2\mathbb{N}}
where
A_{n}
is a set of
d
-regular graphs on
n
vertices.
Then,
G(n,1)\oplus\dotsb\oplus G(n,1)\in A_{n}
holds with high probability if and only if
G(n,d)\in A_{n}
holds with high probability.

Outline of the main ideas.

The proofs of
Theorems

1.4
and
1.5
are based on two results:
Corollary

2.9
and
Corollary

3.2
.

Corollary

2.9
specialises a more general technical result,
Proposition

2.8
, which establishes that, for measures
\eta_{n}
,
\tilde{\eta}_{n}
and
\tau_{n}
on the space of
n
-vertex graphs,
if the measures
\eta_{n},\tilde{\eta}_{n}
are close (in total variation distance, or contiguous) and
\tau_{n}
satisfies a weak technical assumption, then the measures
\eta_{n}\oplus\tau_{n},\tilde{\eta}_{n}\oplus\tau_{n}
are also close in the corresponding sense.

Corollary

2.9
then specialises this observation to the setting where
\tau_{n}
has distribution
G(n,d_{1})\oplus G(n,d_{2})\oplus\dotsb\oplus G(n,d_{k})
for a wide range of parameters
k
and
d_{1},\ldots,d_{k}
.

Corollary

3.2
bounds the total variation distance between
G(n,d)\oplus G(n,1)
and
G(n,d+1)
whenever
d\leq n^{1/10}
.
We observe that a weaker version of
Corollary

3.2
can be derived directly from a work of Gao
[
6
]
.
However, the explicit error bounds one can extract from Gao’s proof are inconveniently large, and do not allow us to iterate the result to conclude that
G(n,d_{1})\oplus G(n,1)\oplus\dotsb\oplus G(n,1)
and
G(n,d+1)
are close in total variation distance for
d_{1}=o(d)
.
To overcome this inconvenience, we build on Gao’s approach, which itself relies on the orthogonal decomposition and projection method introduced by Janson
[
13
]
.
An application of this technique allows us to approximate well the number of perfect matchings via a linear regression involving the number of triangles, which is significantly easier to analyse due to the locality of the triangle count.
Computing the third and the fourth moments of the number of triangles in the random
d
-regular graph (see
Lemma

3.5
) allows us to sharpen concentration estimates coming from the second moment method.
In turn, this enables us to refine Gao’s bounds through the orthogonal decomposition and projection method.
Equipped with our knowledge about perfect matchings in random regular graphs, we analyse the total variation distance between
G(n,d)\oplus G(n,1)
and
G(n,d+1)
by using a result of McKay
[
24
]
(see
Theorem

2.1
) about counting sparse graphs with given degree sequence in dense host graphs, and a convenient consequence of Strassen’s theorem observed by Isaev, McKay, Southwell and Zhukovskii
[
12
]
(see
Theorem

2.3
).

Finally, while
Theorem

1.2
can be derived from
Corollary

2.9
and
Corollary

3.2
when
d_{1}=\omega(1)
, the two results are insufficient when
d_{1}=O(1)
.
Indeed, when
d_{1}=O(1)
, the bound on the total variation distance between
G(n,d)\oplus G(n,1)
and
G(n,d+1)
given by
Corollary

3.2
is a positive constant and, therefore, does not provide an inclusion-wise monotone coupling with high probability.
In this case, for all
k\geq 1
, our approach relies on developing an alternative sampling procedure (see
Definition

5.1
) to approximately generate
G(n,1)\oplus\ldots\oplus G(n,1)
, with
G(n,1)
repeated
kd_{1}
times, in blocks of
d_{1}
perfect matchings.
To do so, we rely on a random overlay idea:
we show that, for
m=O(1)
unlabelled regular graphs with degrees of order
O(1)
, by attributing the same vertex labels to them uniformly at random and independently, the resulting labelled graphs are edge-disjoint with probability bounded away from
0
and depending only on
m
and the degree of each graph (see
Lemma

2.10
).
Then, we show that, for every
\varepsilon>0
, one can choose a large constant
k=k(\varepsilon)\geq 1
and couple
G(n,d_{1})
and the graph generated by the alternative procedure so that
G(n,d_{1})
coincides with a graph corresponding to one of the
k
blocks with probability at least
1-\varepsilon
(see
Lemmas

5.3
and
5.4
).
We note that, with very minor modifications,
Lemma

5.3
provides a general coupling tool of measures
\mu
and
\nu\times\ldots\times\nu
where
\mu,\nu
are absolutely continuous measures on the same space, and may be of independent interest.

Notation and preliminaries

We first recall (mostly classic) notation and terminology used in the sequel.

2.1.
Notation and terminology

General notation.

For a positive integer
n
, we denote
[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}
. For positive integers
m\leq n
, we also write
\binom{[n]}{m}
for all subsets of
[n]
of size
m
.
For real numbers
a,b,c
with
c>0
, we write
a=b\pm c
to mean that
a\in[b-c,b+c]
.
Floor and ceiling notation is omitted when rounding is insignificant for the argument.

Given a graph
G
, we write
V(G)
for its vertex set and
E(G)
for its edge set.

For integers
n\geq 1
and
d\in[1,n-1]
, we denote by
\mathcal{G}_{d}=\mathcal{G}_{d}(n)
the family of
d
-regular graphs on
n
vertices.
We denote by
\mu_{d,n}
the uniform probability distribution on the family
\mathcal{G}_{d}(n)
, but often omit the index
n
and write
\mu_{d}
for better readability.
For integers
k\geq 1
and
d_{1},\ldots,d_{k}\geq 1
with
d_{1}+\dotsb+d_{k}\leq n-1
, we denote by
G(n,d_{1})\oplus\dotsb\oplus G(n,d_{k})
the union of independent copies of
G(n,d_{1}),\ldots,G(n,d_{k})
on the same vertex set conditionally on being edge-disjoint.
We denote by
\nu_{d,n}
the probability distribution on
\mathcal{G}_{d}(n)
such that
\nu_{d,n}(G)
is proportional to the number of 1-factorisations of
G
.
Again, we often write
\nu_{d}
for better readability.
Given two measures
\mu
and
\nu
on the same family of graphs
\mathcal{G}
,
\mu\oplus\nu
is a measure on
\mathcal{G}
such that, for every
G\in\mathcal{G}
,

(\mu\oplus\nu)(G)
is proportional to

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}G_{1},G_{2}\in\mathcal{G}\\
G_{1}\cup G_{2}=G\\
G_{1}\cap G_{2}=\emptyset\end{subarray}}\mu(G_{1})\nu(G_{2})\,,

where
G_{1}\cap G_{2}=\emptyset
means that the edge sets of
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are disjoint.

We work with a measurable space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
where
\Omega
is always a finite or countable set. By default, we assume
\mathcal{F}
to be the family of all subsets of
\Omega
and omit it from the notation.
A sequence of events
(E_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is said to hold
with high probability
or
whp
if
\mathbb{P}(E_{n})\to 1
as
n\to\infty
.

We use standard asymptotic notations.
For functions
f=f(n)
and
g=g(n)
(with
g
non-negative), we write
f=O(g)
to say that there is a constant
C
such that
|f(n)|\leq Cg(n)
, and we write
f=\Omega(g)
to say that there is a constant
c>0
such that
f(n)\geq cg(n)
for sufficiently large
n
.
We write
f=\Theta(g)
to say that
f=O(g)
and
f=\Omega(g)
, and we also write
f=o(g)
or
g=\omega(f)
to say that
f(n)/g(n)\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

Coupling.

For two (probability) distributions
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
on the measurable spaces
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
, respectively, a
coupling
of
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
is a (probability) distribution on the space
\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2},
with first marginal
\mu_{1}
and second marginal
\mu_{2}
.

Total variation distance.

For two probability distributions
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
defined on a common measurable space
\Omega
, the
total variation distance
between
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
is defined as

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})

\displaystyle=\sup_{A}|\mu_{1}(A)-\mu_{2}(A)|,

where the supremum is taken over all subsets of
\Omega
.
Moreover, since we assume the sample space
\Omega
to be finite or countable, the above definition is equivalent to

(1)

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in\Omega}|\mu_{1}(x)-\mu_{2}(x)|.

Contiguity.

Fix a sequence of measurable spaces
(\Omega_{n})_{n\geq 1}
. For every
n\geq 1
, consider two probability measures
\mu_{n}
and
\nu_{n}
on the space
\Omega_{n}
.
We say that
(\mu_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and
(\nu_{n})_{n\geq 1}
are
contiguous
if, for every sequence of events
(A_{n})_{n\geq 1}
,
we have that

\mu_{n}(A_{n})\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}1\iff\nu_{n}(A_{n})\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}1\,.

For convenience, we will often abuse terminology and say that
\mu_{n}
and
\nu_{n}
are contiguous.
It is easy to see that
\mu_{n}
and
\nu_{n}
are contiguous if
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})=o(1)
.
However, the converse does not hold in general, as contiguity only concerns sequences of events which hold whp.
For instance, by setting
\Omega_{n}=\{0,1\}
,
\mu_{n}=\mathrm{Ber}(1/3)
and
\nu_{n}=\mathrm{Ber}(2/3)
for all
n\geq 1
, the measures
\mu_{n}
and
\nu_{n}
are contiguous but
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})=1/3
for all
n\geq 1
.

Preliminary results

2.2.1.
Helpful results from the literature

We begin this section with a theorem of McKay
[
24
]
determining the number of graphs with a given (sparse) degree sequence
g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}
in the complement of a (sparse) graph
X
.

Theorem 2.1

(Theorem 4.6 in
[
24
]
)
.

Fix
\varepsilon\in(0,2/3)
, a graph
X
with vertex set
[n]
and degree sequence
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
, and integers
g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}\in[0,n-1]
with even sum. Define

e(G)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i},\quad\lambda=\frac{1}{4e(G)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}(g_{i}-1)\quad\text{and}\quad\mu=\frac{1}{2e(G)}\sum_{ij\in E(X)}g_{i}g_{j}.

Suppose that

\hat{\Delta}:=2+\max_{i\in[n]}g_{i}\bigg(\frac{3}{2}\max_{i\in[n]}g_{i}+\max_{i\in[n]}x_{i}+1\bigg)\leq\varepsilon\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}.

Then, the number of graphs with degree sequence
g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}
on the vertex set
[n]
which are edge-disjoint from
X
is equal to

\frac{(2e(G))!}{e(G)!\,2^{e(G)}\prod_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}!}\exp\left(-\lambda-\lambda^{2}-\mu-O\bigg(\frac{\hat{\Delta}^{2}}{e(G)}\bigg)\right),

where the constant in the
O(\cdot)
term is uniform over the choice of all parameters.

The following preliminary result concerns contiguity between models of random regular graphs of bounded degree.
It is a particular case of Corollary 4.17 in
[
28
]
; see also
[
14
,
26
]
.

We start with a simple observation used several times throughout the proof, often without explicit mention.

Lemma 2.5

(
\oplus
is associative for measures)
.

Fix three probability distributions
\mu,\nu,\eta
on the same family of graphs
\mathcal{G}
. Then,
(\mu\oplus\nu)\oplus\eta=\mu\oplus\nu\oplus\eta

Proof.

For any
G\in\mathcal{G}

((\mu\oplus\nu)\oplus\eta)(G)
is proportional to

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}G_{1},G_{3}\in\mathcal{G}\\
G_{1}\cup G_{3}=G\\
G_{1}\cap G_{3}=\emptyset\end{subarray}}(\mu\oplus\nu)(G_{1})\cdot\eta(G_{3})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}G_{1},G_{2},G_{3}\in\mathcal{G}\\
G_{1}\cup G_{2}\cup G_{3}=G\\
G_{1},G_{2},G_{3}\text{ pairwise edge-disjoint}\end{subarray}}\mu(G_{1})\nu(G_{2})\eta(G_{3})\,.

Moreover,
(\mu\oplus\nu\oplus\eta)(G)
is also proportional to the same quantity. Since each of
(\mu\oplus\nu)\oplus\eta
and
\mu\oplus\nu\oplus\eta
is a probability distribution, they must coincide.

The next proposition quantifies the contiguity relation between two measures and shows that, if two sequences are contiguous, then their total variation distance is bounded away from
1
.

Proposition 2.6
.

Fix a sequence of finite or countable measurable spaces
(\Omega_{n})_{n\geq 1}

and contiguous probability distributions
\eta_{n}
and
\tilde{\eta}_{n}
on
\Omega_{n}
.
Denote
\eta=(\eta_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and
\tilde{\eta}=(\tilde{\eta}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
.
Then, each of the following holds.

(i)

For every
\delta>0
, there exist
\gamma=\gamma(\delta,\eta,\tilde{\eta})\in(0,\delta)
and
n_{0}=n_{0}(\delta,\eta,\tilde{\eta})\geq 1
such that, for every
n\geq n_{0}
and every event
A_{n}\subseteq\Omega_{n}
,
\eta_{n}(A_{n})\leq\gamma
implies
\tilde{\eta}_{n}(A_{n})\leq\delta
and
\tilde{\eta}_{n}(A_{n})\leq\gamma
implies
\eta_{n}(A_{n})\leq\delta
.

(ii)

There exist an absolute constant
\varepsilon>0
and
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\eta_{n},\tilde{\eta}_{n})\leq 1-\varepsilon
for all
n\geq n_{0}
.

Proof.

For (i), we show the first implication; the second one is derived similarly.
Suppose for contradiction that there is
\delta>0
and a sequence
1\leq n_{1}<n_{2}<\dotsb
such that, for every
i\geq 1
, there is an event
E_{n_{i}}
such that
\eta_{n_{i}}(E_{n_{i}})\leq 1/i
but
\tilde{\eta}_{n_{i}}(E_{n_{i}})>\delta
.

Define events
(A_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
where
A_{n}=\Omega_{n}
for
n\notin\{n_{1},n_{2},\dots\}
and, for every
i\geq 1
,
A_{n_{i}}=E_{n_{i}}^{c}
. Then, by construction, we have that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\eta_{n}(A_{n})=1
, which by contiguity implies
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\tilde{\eta}_{n}(A_{n})=1
. However, this contradicts the fact that
\limsup_{i\rightarrow\infty}\tilde{\eta}_{n_{i}}(A_{n_{i}})\leq 1-\delta
.
Thus, there exists a constant
\gamma_{0}>0
satisfying (i), and choosing
\gamma=\min(\delta,\gamma_{0})
finishes the proof of (i).

Now, we deduce (ii) from (i). Fix any
\delta\in(0,1)
and
\gamma,n_{0}
as in (i).
Fix any sequence of events
(A_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and
n\geq n_{0}
.
We show that
|\eta_{n}(A_{n})-\tilde{\eta}_{n}(A_{n})|
is bounded away from
1
.
If
\eta_{n}(A_{n})\leq\gamma
, then
\tilde{\eta}_{n}(A_{n})\leq\delta
and
|\eta_{n}(A_{n})-\tilde{\eta}_{n}(A_{n})|\leq\delta
, which is bounded away from 1.
Note that a similar inequality holds when
\tilde{\eta}_{n}(A_{n})\leq\gamma
.
Finally, if
\min(\eta_{n}(A_{n}),\tilde{\eta}_{n}(A_{n}))\geq\gamma
, then
|\eta_{n}(A_{n})-\tilde{\eta}_{n}(A_{n})|\leq 1-\gamma
, which is again bounded away from 1, completing the proof.
∎

The following lemma shows that contiguity has a convenient fractional reformulation.

Lemma 2.7
.

Fix a sequence of finite or countable measurable spaces
(\Omega_{n})_{n\geq 1}

and contiguous probability measures
\eta_{n}
and
\tilde{\eta}_{n}
on
(\Omega_{n})_{n\geq 1}
.
Fix a family
\{a_{x,n}:n\geq 1,x\in\Omega_{n}\}
of uniformly bounded non-negative real numbers.
Then,

\sum_{x\in\Omega_{n}}a_{x,n}\,\eta_{n}(x)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0\iff\sum_{x\in\Omega_{n}}a_{x,n}\,\tilde{\eta}_{n}(x)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0\,.

Note that one retrieves the classic definition of contiguity when
a_{x,n}\in\{0,1\}
for every
n\geq 1
and
x\in\Omega_{n}
.

Proof.

We show that convergence on the left-hand side implies convergence on the right-hand side; the inverse implication follows by symmetry.
Suppose that
\sum_{x\in\Omega_{n}}a_{x,n}\eta_{n}(x)\to 0
.
Fix
\varepsilon>0
and, for every
n\geq 1
, define the sets
S_{n}=\{x\in\Omega_{n}:a_{x,n}\leq\varepsilon\}
and
L_{n}=\{x\in\Omega_{n}:a_{x,n}>\varepsilon\}
.
Then,

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\Omega_{n}}a_{x,n}\,\eta_{n}(x)\geq\sum_{x\in L_{n}}a_{x,n}\,\eta_{n}(x)\geq\varepsilon\,\eta_{n}(L_{n}).

Hence,
\eta_{n}(L_{n})\to 0
and, by contiguity of the measures
\eta_{n}
and
\tilde{\eta}_{n}
, we have
\tilde{\eta}_{n}(L_{n})\to 0
.
As a result, for every
\varepsilon>0
, there is
n_{0}\geq 1
such that, for every
n\geq n_{0}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\Omega_{n}}a_{x,n}\,\tilde{\eta}_{n}(x)=\sum_{x\in L_{n}}a_{x,n}\,\tilde{\eta}_{n}(x)+\sum_{x\in S_{n}}a_{x,n}\,\tilde{\eta}_{n}(x)\leq\max\{a_{x,n}:n\geq 1,x\in\Omega_{n}\}\,\tilde{\eta}_{n}(L_{n})+\varepsilon\tilde{\eta}_{n}(S_{n})\leq 2\varepsilon.

As the latter holds for every
\varepsilon>0
, this completes the proof.
∎

The next proposition relates the total variation distance and contiguity of measures with the operation
\oplus
.

Proposition 2.8
.

For every integer
n\geq 1
, fix probability distributions
\eta_{n}
,
\tilde{\eta}_{n}
and
\tau_{n}

on the family
\mathcal{F}_{n}
of labelled
n
-vertex graphs.
For every graph
G\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
, define

(2)

\beta_{n}(G)=\sum_{H\in\mathcal{F}_{n}:\,G\cap H=\emptyset}\tau_{n}(H).

Suppose that, for some constant
\gamma>0
and every
G,G^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
, we have
\beta_{n}(G)\leq\gamma\beta_{n}(G^{\prime})
. Then, each of the following holds.

(a)

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\eta_{n}\oplus\tau_{n},\tilde{\eta}_{n}\oplus\tau_{n})\leq 2\gamma d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\eta_{n},\tilde{\eta}_{n})
.

(b)

Suppose that the sequences of measures
(\eta_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and
(\tilde{\eta}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
are contiguous. Then, the sequences
(\eta_{n}\oplus\tau_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and
(\tilde{\eta}_{n}\oplus\tau_{n})_{n\geq 1}
are contiguous as well.

Note that, in
Proposition

2.8
,
\mathcal{F}_{n}
is not necessarily the support of
\eta_{n}
,
\tilde{\eta}_{n}
and
\tau_{n}
, meaning that some graphs
G\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
can be attributed weight 0 by any of these measures.

Proof of
Proposition

2.8
.

To begin with, we focus on the proof of part (a). Denote for brevity
\lambda_{n}=\eta_{n}\oplus\tau_{n}
and
\tilde{\lambda}_{n}=\tilde{\eta}_{n}\oplus\tau_{n}
.
Moreover, for every
S\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
, define

\displaystyle\psi_{n}(S)\coloneqq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}G,H\in\mathcal{F}_{n}\\
G\cup H=S\\
G\cap H=\emptyset\end{subarray}}\eta_{n}(G)\tau_{n}(H)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Psi_{n}\coloneqq\sum_{S\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\psi_{n}(S),

and denote by
\tilde{\psi}_{n}(S)
and
\tilde{\Psi}_{n}
the analogous quantities obtained by replacing
\eta_{n}
with
\tilde{\eta}_{n}
.
Then, we have

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\lambda_{n},\tilde{\lambda}_{n})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{S\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}|\lambda_{n}(S)-\tilde{\lambda}_{n}(S)|=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{S\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\left|\frac{\psi_{n}(S)}{\Psi_{n}}-\frac{\tilde{\psi}_{n}(S)}{\tilde{\Psi}_{n}}\right|.

By summing over
G
first, note that
\Psi_{n}=\sum_{G\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\eta_{n}(G)\beta_{n}(G)
, and similar equality holds for

\tilde{\Psi}_{n}
and
\tilde{\eta}_{n}
.
In particular, by the triangle inequality,

(3)

|\Psi_{n}-\tilde{\Psi}_{n}|\leq\sum_{G\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}|\eta_{n}(G)-\tilde{\eta}_{n}(G)|\beta_{n}(G)\leq 2d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\eta_{n},\tilde{\eta}_{n})\max_{G\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\beta_{n}(G).

By using (
3
) and the triangle inequality again, we obtain that

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\lambda_{n},\tilde{\lambda}_{n})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{S\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\left|\frac{\psi_{n}(S)}{\Psi_{n}}-\frac{\tilde{\psi}_{n}(S)}{\Psi_{n}}\right|+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{S\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\left|\frac{\tilde{\psi}_{n}(S)}{\Psi_{n}}-\frac{\tilde{\psi}_{n}(S)}{\tilde{\Psi}_{n}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sum_{G,H\in\mathcal{F}_{n}:\,G\cap H=\emptyset}|\eta_{n}(G)-\tilde{\eta}_{n}(G)|\tau_{n}(H)}{2\Psi_{n}}+\sum_{S\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\frac{\tilde{\psi}_{n}(S)}{2}\frac{|\Psi_{n}-\tilde{\Psi}_{n}|}{\Psi_{n}\tilde{\Psi}_{n}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{G\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}|\eta_{n}(G)-\tilde{\eta}_{n}(G)|\beta_{n}(G)}{2\Psi_{n}}+\frac{|\Psi_{n}-\tilde{\Psi}_{n}|}{2\Psi_{n}}\leq\frac{2d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\eta_{n},\tilde{\eta}_{n})\max_{G\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\beta_{n}(G)}{\Psi_{n}}.

Finally, by using that
\Psi_{n}=\sum_{G\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\eta_{n}(G)\beta_{n}(G)\geq\min_{G\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\beta_{n}(G)
and the assumption on
\beta_{n}
in the statement, we obtain that

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\lambda_{n},\tilde{\lambda}_{n})\leq\frac{2d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\eta_{n},\tilde{\eta}_{n})\max_{G\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\beta_{n}(G)}{\min_{G\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\beta_{n}(G)}\leq 2\gamma d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\eta_{n},\tilde{\eta}_{n}),

which finishes the proof of part (a).

We turn to the proof of part (b). For every
n\geq 1
, fix
A_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{n}
.
Then,

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}(A_{n})=\frac{\sum_{S\in A_{n}}\psi_{n}(S)}{\Psi_{n}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\tilde{\lambda}_{n}(A_{n})=\frac{\sum_{S\in A_{n}}\tilde{\psi}_{n}(S)}{\tilde{\Psi}_{n}}.

Suppose that
\lambda_{n}(A_{n})\to 0
.
For every
n\geq 1
and
G\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
, denote

a_{G,n}\coloneqq\frac{\sum_{H\in\mathcal{F}_{n}:\,G\cup H\in A_{n},\,G\cap H=\emptyset}\tau_{n}(H)}{\max_{G\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\beta_{n}(G)}\in[0,1].

Using that
\Psi_{n}\leq\max_{G\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\beta_{n}(G)
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}(A_{n})=\frac{\sum_{S\in A_{n}}\psi_{n}(S)}{\Psi_{n}}\geq\sum_{G\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\frac{\sum_{H\in\mathcal{F}_{n}:\,G\cup H\in A_{n},\,G\cap H=\emptyset}\tau_{n}(H)}{\max_{G\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\beta_{n}(G)}\eta_{n}(G)=\sum_{G\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}a_{G,n}\eta_{n}(G).

Since
\lambda_{n}(A_{n})\to 0
, the right-hand side of the above inequality does as well.
Thus, by
Lemma

2.7
and the contiguity of
\eta_{n}
and
\tilde{\eta}_{n}
, it follows that
\sum_{G\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}a_{G,n}\tilde{\eta}_{n}(G)\to 0
.
However, by assumption on
\beta_{n}
and the inequality
\min_{G\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\beta_{n}(G)\leq\tilde{\Psi}_{n}
,

\displaystyle\tilde{\lambda}_{n}(A_{n})=\frac{\sum_{S\in A_{n}}\tilde{\psi}_{n}(S)}{\tilde{\Psi}_{n}}\leq\sum_{G\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\frac{\sum_{H\in\mathcal{F}_{n}:\,G\cup H\in A_{n},\,G\cap H=\emptyset}\tau_{n}(H)}{\min_{G\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\beta(G)}\tilde{\eta}_{n}(G)\leq\gamma\sum_{G\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}a_{G,n}\tilde{\eta}_{n}(G),

implying that
\tilde{\lambda}_{n}(A_{n})\to 0
.
Since the implication
\tilde{\lambda}_{n}(A_{n})\to 0\Rightarrow\lambda_{n}(A_{n})\to 0
is derived similarly, the proof of part (b) follows.
∎

The last lemma in this section shows that, for
m\geq 2
unlabelled regular graphs on
n
vertices and with uniformly bounded degrees, by attributing labels in
[n]
independently to each of them (thus realising them on the same vertex set), the probability that these graphs remain are pairwise edge-disjoint only depends on
m
and the degrees of the given graphs.
This is shown via a moment computation indicating convergence of the number of repeated edges to a Poisson random variable.
A similar argument is presented in a slightly different context in Section 2.3 in
[
28
]
; a proof is provided for completeness nonetheless.
Given a labelled graph
G
, the
skeleton
of
G
is the unlabelled graph obtained from
G
by erasing the labels of its vertices.

Lemma 2.10
.

Fix
m\geq 2
,
d_{1},\ldots,d_{m}\geq 1
and
m
unlabelled regular graphs on
n
vertices with degrees equal to
d_{1},\ldots,d_{m}
, respectively.
For every
i\in[m]
, denote by
H_{i}
the (random) graph obtained from the
i
-th unlabelled graph by attributing the labels in
[n]
bijectively, uniformly at random and independently for different

.
Then, the probability that

are pairwise edge-disjoint is
\exp(-D/2)+o(1)
where
D=\sum_{1\leq k<\ell\leq m}d_{k}d_{\ell}
and the
o(1)
is uniform over all choices of
H_{1},\ldots,H_{m}
.

Proof.

For convenience, we identify each graph with its edge set.
Denote by
X
the number of repeated edges, that is, edges appearing in at least two of the graphs
H_{1},\ldots,H_{m}
.
To establish the lemma, it suffices to show that the factorial moments of

converge to the factorial moments of a Poisson random variable with parameter
D/2
(see, for instance, the case

of Lemma 2.8 in
[
28
]
).

For every
r\geq 1
, denote by
\Lambda_{r}
the set of (ordered)
r
-tuples of distinct edges in

, and denote by
\Lambda_{r}^{\prime}\subseteq\Lambda_{r}
the subset of
r
-tuples of edges which do not induce a matching.
Observe that the
r
-th factorial moment of

is given by the sum
\sum_{(e_{1},\ldots,e_{r})\in\Lambda_{r}}\mathbb{P}(e_{1},\ldots,e_{r}\text{ are repeated edges})
.
For distinct edges
e_{1},\dots,e_{r}
, we define the graphs
E_{i}=H_{i}\cap\{e_{1},\dots,e_{r}\}
for all
i\in[m]
.
We first show that the contribution of the
r
-tuples in
\Lambda_{r}^{\prime}
to the
r
-th factorial moment is of order
o(1)
.

Claim 2.11
.

For every
r\geq 1
,
\sum_{(e_{1},\ldots,e_{r})\in\Lambda_{r}^{\prime}}\mathbb{P}(e_{1},\ldots,e_{r}\text{ are repeated edges})=o(1)
.

Proof.

Note that

(5)

\sum_{(e_{1},\ldots,e_{r})\in\Lambda_{r}^{\prime}}\mathbb{P}(e_{1},\ldots,e_{r}\text{ are repeated edges})=\sum_{(e_{1},\ldots,e_{r})\in\Lambda_{r}^{\prime}}\mathbb{P}(\forall i\in[r],\exists\text{ distinct }j,\ell\in[m],e_{i}\in H_{j}\cap H_{\ell}).

Fix
(e_{1},\ldots,e_{r})\in\Lambda_{r}^{\prime}
and suppose that
e_{1},\ldots,e_{r}
all are repeated edges, and that
e_{1}\cup\cdots\cup e_{r}
induces a graph
E
on
t\in[2r-1]
vertices.
Denote by
\Psi_{m}
the family of
m
-tuples of sets
E_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,E_{m}^{\prime}\subseteq\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{r}\}
where every edge among

is contained in at least two sets.
In particular, the event that
e_{1},\ldots,e_{r}
are repeated edges is the same as the event that
E_{1},\dots,E_{m}
is an
m
-tuple in
\Psi_{m}
.

Now, fix an
m
-tuple
(E_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,E_{m}^{\prime})
in
\Psi_{m}
. For every
i\in[m]
, we assume that
E_{i}^{\prime}
has
s(i)\geq 1
connected components containing at least one edge, and consider a forest
F_{i}
consisting of disjoint trees
T_{1}^{i},\ldots,T_{s(i)}^{i}
spanning these components.
Observe that, since
(e_{1},\ldots,e_{r})\in\Lambda_{r}^{\prime}
, we may (and do) select the said forests so that there is a vertex
v\in V(E)
incident to at least three edges in
\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}F_{i}
, counting with multiplicity.

Given
e_{1},\ldots,e_{r}
and
F_{1},\ldots,F_{m}
, we show that

(6)

\mathbb{P}(\forall i\in[m],F_{i}\subseteq H_{i})\leq\prod_{i=1}^{m}\bigg(\frac{d_{i}}{n-2r}\bigg)^{e(F_{i})}.

To this end, we attribute labels to the skeletons of the graphs
H_{1},\ldots,H_{m}
in this order.
For every
i\in[m]
, root each of the trees
T_{1}^{i},\ldots,T_{s(i)}^{i}
in arbitrary vertices and reveal the labels of the roots in the skeleton of
H_{i}
.
Starting from the roots, we order the remaining vertices in the forest
F_{i}
so that every vertex
v
is preceded by its parent
p(v)
.
Then, for every new vertex
v
, the probability that
v
is adjacent to
p(v)
in
H_{i}
given the labels of all preceding vertices is at most
d_{i}/(n-2r)
since
H_{i}
is a
d_{i}
-regular graph which has received at most
t\leq 2r-1
of its vertex labels.
Since the forest
F_{i}
contains
e(F_{i})
edges, (
6
) follows.

We show that

\sum_{i=1}^{m}e(F_{i})\geq t+1
.
On the one hand, to see that the latter sum is bounded from below by
t
, note that every vertex incident to
e_{1}\cup\dotsb\cup e_{r}
belongs to at least two different forests among
F_{1},\ldots,F_{m}
and, therefore, is incident to at least two edges in
\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}F_{i}
, counting with multiplicity.
Moreover, to have
\sum_{i=1}^{m}e(F_{i})=t
, every vertex in
E
must be incident to
exactly
two edges in
\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}F_{i}
, counting with multiplicity.
However, this assumption does not hold for the vertex
v
, showing that
\sum_{i=1}^{m}e(F_{i})\geq t+1
.

Thus, for every
r
-tuple of edges in
\Lambda_{r}^{\prime}
and every choice of forests
F_{1},\ldots,F_{m}
, (
6
) is of order
O(n^{-t-1})
.
Since there are
O(n^{t}\cdot t^{2r})
choices for the edges
e_{1},\ldots,e_{r}
and
O(2^{rm})
choices for the forests
F_{1},\ldots,F_{m}
, we conclude (
5
) is
o(1)
, as desired.
∎

By
Claim

2.11
, it suffices to estimate

(7)

\Sigma\coloneqq\sum_{(e_{1},\dots,e_{r})\in\Lambda_{r}\setminus\Lambda_{r}^{\prime}}\mathbb{P}(e_{1},\ldots,e_{r}\text{ are repeated edges})\,.

To begin with, fix
(e_{1},\dots,e_{r})\in\Lambda_{r}\setminus\Lambda_{r}^{\prime}
and observe that

(8)

|\Lambda_{r}\setminus\Lambda_{r}^{\prime}|=\binom{n}{2r}\frac{(2r)!}{2^{r}}=(1+o(1))\frac{n^{2r}}{2^{r}}.

By utilising the idea of label attribution from the proof of
Claim

2.11
again, we conclude that the contribution coming from realisations of
E_{1},\ldots,E_{m}
where some of
e_{1},\ldots,e_{r}
is repeated at least three times is
o(1)
.

We end the proof by analysing the following procedure. We process the edges
e_{1},\ldots,e_{r}
consecutively.
For every
i\in[r]
, we expose the labels of the endpoints of the edge
e_{i}=u_{i}v_{i}
in each of the (unlabelled) graphs
H_{1},\ldots,H_{m}
and check if these graphs contain the edge
e_{i}
or not.

Note that the events
e_{i}\in H_{\ell}
and
e_{i}\in H_{k}
are independent for distinct
\ell,k\in[m]
.
Next, we analyse the probability that
e_{i}
is contained in
H_{j}
for some fixed
j\in[m]
conditionally on the vertex labels
u_{1},\dots,u_{i-1},v_{1},\dots v_{i-1}
being exposed in
H_{j}
.
Note that
H_{j}\setminus\{u_{1},v_{1},\ldots,u_{i-1},v_{i-1}\}
contains at least
nd_{j}/2-2(i-1)d_{j}=(1+o(1))nd_{j}/2
edges, and the probability that
e_{i}
is one of them is

(1+o(1))\frac{nd_{j}}{2}\bigg/\binom{n-2(i-1)}{2}=(1+o(1))\frac{d_{j}}{n}\,.

Hence, for every
i\in[r]
, conditionally on the past steps, the edge
e_{i}
belongs to precisely two graphs among
H_{1},\ldots,H_{m}
with probability

(9)

(1+o(1))\sum_{1\leq k<\ell\leq m}\frac{d_{k}d_{\ell}}{n^{2}}=(1+o(1))\frac{D}{n^{2}}.

By multiplying the latter probabilities for all
i\in[r]
and using (
8
), it follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{(e_{1},\dots,e_{r})\in\Lambda_{r}\setminus\Lambda_{r}^{\prime}}\mathbb{P}(e_{1},\ldots,e_{r}\text{ are repeated edges})=|\Lambda_{r}\setminus\Lambda_{r}^{\prime}|\left((1+o(1))\frac{D}{n^{2}}\right)^{r}=(1+o(1))\left(\frac{D}{2}\right)^{r}.

Combining this with
Claim

2.11
, we deduce that the
r
-th factorial moment of
X
is equal to
(1+o(1))(D/2)^{r}
, as desired.
∎

3.
Main technical result: decomposing one matching at a time

This section is dedicated to the proof of the following technical proposition.
For the sake of coherence, we state the following results with the same restriction of
d\leq n^{1/10}
. The proof works for a slightly larger range of
d
, but clearly cannot go pass
n^{1/7}
. We made no attempt at determining the best possible range.

Proposition 3.1
.

There exists a constant
C_{0}>0
such that, for all even
n
and
d=d(n)\in[3,n^{1/10}]
, the number of perfect matchings
Y
in
G(n,d)
satisfies

\Prob(|Y-\mathbb{E}[Y]|\geq d^{-1.1}\mathbb{E}[Y])\leq C_{0}d^{-1.1}.

The following consequence of
Theorem

2.3
and
Proposition

3.1
will be used in the proofs of each of our main results.

Corollary 3.2
.

There exists a constant
C_{1}>0
such that, for all even
n
and
d=d(n)\in[3,n^{1/10}]
,

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mu_{d}\oplus\mu_{1},\mu_{d+1})\leq C_{1}d^{-1.1}.

Proof of
Corollary

3.2
assuming
Proposition

3.1
.

Consider an auxiliary bipartite graph
H
with parts

S=\{(G_{d},G_{d+1})\in\mathcal{G}_{d}(n)\times\mathcal{G}_{d+1}(n):G_{d}\subseteq G_{d+1}\}

and
T=\mathcal{G}_{d+1}(n)
where
(G_{d},G_{d+1})\in S
is adjacent to
G_{d+1}^{\prime}\in T
if
G_{d+1}=G_{d+1}^{\prime}
.
Note that every vertex in
S
has exactly one neighbour in
T
and thus
|E(H)|=|S|
.
Moreover, the degree of a vertex
G
in
T
is equal to the number of perfect matchings in
G
, meaning that
|E(H)|=|T|\cdot\mathbb{E}[Y]
, where
Y
is the number of perfect matchings in
G(n,d+1)
.
By
Proposition

3.1
, at least
(1-C_{0}d^{-1.1})|T|
of the vertices in
T
have at least
(1-d^{-1.1})\mathbb{E}[Y]=(1-d^{-1.1})|E(H)|/|T|
neighbours in
S
.
By applying
Theorem

2.3
to the graph
H
with
\varepsilon=d^{-1.1}
and
\delta=C_{0}d^{-1.1}
, we find a coupling
(X,Z)
with
X
uniformly distributed on
S
and
Z
uniformly distributed on
T
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(XZ\notin E(H))=O(d^{-1.1}).

However, by definition, the second marginal
X^{\prime}
of
X
is distributed according to
\mu_{d}\oplus\mu_{1}
, and thus

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mu_{d}\oplus\mu_{1},\mu_{d+1})\leq\mathbb{P}(X^{\prime}\neq Z)=\mathbb{P}(XZ\notin E(H))=O(d^{-1.1}),

which concludes the proof.
∎

In the remainder of this section, we focus on the proof of
Proposition

3.1
.
Recall that
Y
stands for the number of perfect matchings in the random
d
-regular graph
G(n,d)
.
To show
Proposition

3.1
, one may be tempted to compute the first two moments of
Y
and apply Chebyshev’s inequality.
While such an estimate exists in the literature
[
6
]
, it only shows that
\mathbb{P}(|Y-\mathbb{E}[Y]|\geq d^{-1}\mathbb{E}[Y])=O(d^{-1})
, which is insufficient for our purposes due to the divergence of the harmonic sum.
We extend this approach by analysing higher moments.

Computing higher moments of
Y
directly becomes a complicated task.
One elegant approach for studying the number of perfect matchings relies on a method known as orthogonal decomposition and projection introduced by Janson
[
13
]
and first applied in the setting of random regular graphs by Gao
[
6
]
.
Here, we follow Gao’s presentation in
[
6
]
.
We define
X
to be the number of triangles in
G(n,d)
and
Y^{*}=aX+b
with
a=\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)/\mathrm{Var}[X]
and
b=\mathbb{E}[Y]-a\mathbb{E}[X]
, where we recall that
\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)=\mathbb{E}[XY]-\mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y]
.
Then, by choice of
a
and
b
, it is easy to verify that
\mathbb{E}[Y^{*}]=\mathbb{E}[Y]
but it also holds that
Y^{*}
minimises
\mathbb{E}[(Y-Y^{*})^{2}]
.
We will control the moments of
Y-Y^{*}
and
Y^{*}
separately.

First, we collect several relevant estimates due to Gao
[
6
,
7
]
.
We recall that asymptotic notation is used with respect to
n\to\infty
and hidden constants are universal.

Claim 3.3

(
[
6
,
7
]
)
.

Consider
d=d(n)\in[3,n^{1/10}]
. Each of the following holds.

1.

\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)=O(\mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y]/d^{3})
,

2.

\mathbb{E}[X]=(1+O(n^{-1}))(d-1)^{3}/6
.

3.

\mathrm{Var}(X)=(1+O(n^{-1}))(d-1)^{3}/6
.

4.

\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]=(1+d^{-3}/6+O(d^{-4}+d^{-3}/n+\sqrt{d/n}(\log n)^{3}))\mathbb{E}[Y]^{2}
.

The first and fourth points are from
[
6
, Theorem 10]
, the second point is from
[
6
, Theorem 9]
, and the third point follows from
[
7
, Section 4.2]
.
Another tool used in our subsequent analysis is the following theorem from
[
7
]
estimating the probability that an edge belongs to a random regular graph conditionally on some part of it being already exposed.

Theorem 3.4

(Theorem 2 in
[
7
]
)
.

Fix
d=o(n)
, a random
d
-regular graph
G\sim G(n,d)
and a graph
H
on the same vertex set and with maximum degree at most
d
.
Suppose that
dn-|E(H)|=\Omega(dn)
and that
uv\notin E(H)
for some vertices
u,v
.
Then,

\Prob(uv\in G\mid H\subseteq G)=\frac{(d-\deg_{H}(u))(d-\deg_{H}(v))}{dn}\left(1-\frac{\phi_{H}(uv)}{dn}\right)\left(1+O\left(\frac{|E(H)|}{n^{2}}+\frac{|E(H)|^{2}}{d^{2}n^{2}}+\frac{d^{2}}{n^{2}}\right)\right)

where

\phi_{H}(uv)=\deg_{H}(u)\deg_{H}(v)+\!\!\sum_{x\in N_{H}(u)}\!\!\deg_{H}(x)+\!\!\sum_{y\in N_{H}(v)}\!\!\deg_{H}(y)-d-2|E(H)|-(d-1)(\deg_{H}(u)+\deg_{H}(v))

with
N_{H}(u)
and
N_{H}(v)
denoting the neighbourhoods of
u
and
v
in
H
, respectively.
Thus, if we have
|E(H)|=O(1)
and
d^{2}\leq n
, then

\Prob(uv\in G\mid H\subseteq G)=\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right)\frac{(d-\deg_{H}(u))(d-\deg_{H}(v))}{dn}\,.

In
[
7
, Section 5.2]
, it is shown that
\mathbb{E}[X^{k}]=(1+o(1))\mathbb{E}[X]^{k}
for all fixed
k\geq 1
when
d=\omega(1)
and
d=o(n^{1/2})
.
However, this estimate is not sufficiently precise for our purposes when
k\in\{2,3,4\}
.
The goal of the next lemma is to go further in the understanding of the error term when
d\leq n^{1/10}
.
We remark that, while the same arguments could be extended to larger values of
k
at the cost of an additional technical effort (and smaller values for
d
), we focus on the cases
k\in\{2,3,4\}
relevant to our proof.

Lemma 3.5
.

Consider
d=d(n)\in[3,n^{1/10}]
.
Then, for each
k\in\{2,3,4\}
,

\mathbb{E}[X^{k}]=\mathbb{E}[X]^{k}+\binom{k}{2}\mathbb{E}[X]^{k-1}+O(\mathbb{E}[X]^{k-2})\,.

Proof.

For
k=2
, the proof follows directly from
Claim

3.3
.
Thus, we are left to handle the cases
k=3
and
k=4
.
Set
N=\binom{n}{3}
and define
H_{1},\dots,H_{N}
to be an arbitrary ordering of all triangles in
K_{n}
.
Also, define
X_{i}
to be the indicator random variable of the event
H_{i}\subseteq G(n,d)
, so we have
X=\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}
.

The case
k=3
.

We will in fact show that
\mathbb{E}[X_{(3)}]=\mathbb{E}[X]^{3}+O(\mathbb{E}[X])
, where
X_{(3)}=X(X-1)(X-2)
is the third factorial moment of
X
. As
\mathbb{E}[X_{(3)}]=\mathbb{E}[X^{3}]-3\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]+2\mathbb{E}[X]
, the result will follow by applying the lemma for
k=2
.
We now compute
\mathbb{E}[X_{(3)}]=\sum_{(i,j,k)\in\binom{[N]}{3}}\mathbb{E}[X_{i}X_{j}X_{k}]
by distinguishing cases.
Note that, by a simple application of the second part of
Theorem

3.4
,
for all distinct
i,j,k\in[n]
,
R=H_{i}\cup H_{j}\cup H_{k}
, any subgraph
S
of
R
and for every edge
uv\in E(R)\setminus E(S)
,

(10)

\displaystyle\Prob(uv\in G(n,d)\mid S\subseteq G(n,d))=\frac{(d-\deg_{S}(u))(d-\deg_{S}(v))}{dn}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right).

We write
\mathbb{E}[X_{(3)}]=\Sigma_{1}+\Sigma_{2}
where
\Sigma_{1}
is the contribution to
\mathbb{E}[X_{(3)}]
by the triplets
(H_{i},H_{j},H_{k})
whose union contains more edges than vertices, and
\Sigma_{2}
is the contribution to
\mathbb{E}[X_{(3)}]
by the triplets
(H_{i},H_{j},H_{k})
whose union contains at most as many edges as vertices.
We start with the following claim.

Claim 3.6
.

\Sigma_{1}=O(\mathbb{E}[X])
.

Proof.

Fix a triplet
(H_{i},H_{j},H_{k})
whose union
R=H_{i}\cup H_{j}\cup H_{k}
contains
h=|E(R)|\in[5,9]
edges and at most
h-1
vertices.
Consider an arbitrary ordering
e_{1},\dots,e_{h}
of the edges of
R
, and denote by
R_{i}
the graph induced by
e_{1},\dots,e_{i}
.
By (
10
), for every
i\in[h]
,

(11)

\displaystyle\Prob(e_{i}\subseteq G(n,d)\mid R_{i-1}\subseteq G(n,d))=\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right)\frac{(d-\deg_{R_{i-1}}(u))(d-\deg_{R_{i-1}}(v))}{dn}=O\left(\frac{d}{n}\right).

Therefore, by multiplying the probabilities in (
11
) for every
i\in[h]
, we obtain that

(12)

\displaystyle\Prob(R\subseteq G(n,d))=O\bigg(\bigg(\frac{d}{n}\bigg)^{h}\bigg).

By combining (
12
), a simple union bound over the values
h\in[5,9]
, the inequality
d^{10}\leq n
and the second point of
Claim

3.3
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\Sigma_{1}=\sum_{h=5}^{9}O\bigg(n^{h-1}\bigg(\frac{d}{n}\bigg)^{h}\bigg)=O\bigg(\frac{d^{9}}{n}\bigg)=O(d^{3})=O(\mathbb{E}[X]),

as desired.
∎

We now estimate
\Sigma_{2}
, that is, the contribution of the triplets
(H_{i},H_{j},H_{k})
such that their union
R
satisfies
|E(R)|\leq|V(R)|
.
In this case, observe that actually
|E(R)|=|V(R)|
since every connected component of
R
contains a cycle, and therefore has at least as many edges as vertices.
Moreover, every connected component of
R
contains a single cycle, implying that different triangles in the triplet
(H_{i},H_{j},H_{k})
must be (vertex-)disjoint.

Consider an arbitrary ordering
e_{1},\dots,e_{9}
of the edges of
R
, and denote by
R_{i}
the graph induced by
e_{1},\dots,e_{i}
for all
i\in[9]
. By (
10
), we have

\displaystyle\Prob(e_{i}\subseteq G(n,d)\mid R_{i-1}\subseteq G(n,d))=\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right)\frac{(d-\deg_{R_{i-1}}(u))(d-\deg_{R_{i-1}}(v))}{dn}.

Without loss of generality, assume each of
\{e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}\}
,
\{e_{4},e_{5},e_{6}\}
and
\{e_{7},e_{8},e_{9}\}
form a triangle.
By multiplying the said probabilities, we obtain

\displaystyle\Prob(R\subseteq G(n,d))=\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right)^{9}\bigg(\frac{d^{2}}{dn}\cdot\frac{d(d-1)}{dn}\cdot\frac{(d-1)^{2}}{dn}\bigg)^{3}=\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right)\frac{(d-1)^{9}}{n^{9}}.

By summing over all triplets
(H_{i},H_{j},H_{k})
giving rise to
R
as described above and using that
d^{10}\leq n
, it follows that

(13)

\displaystyle\hskip-6.99997pt\Sigma_{2}=\binom{n}{3}\binom{n-3}{3}\binom{n-6}{3}\cdot\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right)\frac{(d-1)^{9}}{n^{9}}=\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right)\frac{(d-1)^{9}}{216}=\mathbb{E}[X]^{3}+O(\mathbb{E}[X]).

Thus, by combining
Claim

3.6
and (
13
), we obtain that
\mathbb{E}[X_{(3)}]=\mathbb{E}[X]^{3}+O(\mathbb{E}[X])
, as desired.

The case
k=4
.

Analogously to the previous case, we show that
\mathbb{E}[X_{(4)}]=\mathbb{E}[X]^{4}+O(\mathbb{E}[X]^{2})
, where
X_{(4)}=X(X-1)(X-2)(X-3)
. As
\mathbb{E}[X_{(4)}]=\mathbb{E}[X^{4}]-6\mathbb{E}[X^{3}]+11\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]-6\mathbb{E}[X]
, the result will follow from the cases
k=2
and
k=3
of the current lemma.
Having
\mathbb{E}[X_{(4)}]=\sum_{(i,j,k,\ell)\in\binom{[N]}{4}}\mathbb{E}[X_{i}X_{j}X_{k}X_{\ell}]
, we again write
\mathbb{E}[X_{(4)}]=\Sigma_{1}+\Sigma_{2}
, where
\Sigma_{1}
is the contribution to
\mathbb{E}[X_{(4)}]
of the tuples
(H_{i},H_{j},H_{k},H_{\ell})
whose union contains more edges than vertices, and
\Sigma_{2}
is the contribution to
\mathbb{E}[X_{(4)}]
of the tuples
(H_{i},H_{j},H_{k},H_{\ell})
whose union contains at most as many edges as vertices.
We start with a claim analogous to
Claim

3.6
for the case
k=4
.

Claim 3.7
.

\Sigma_{1}=O(\mathbb{E}[X]^{2})
.

Proof.

Similarly to the proof of
Claim

3.6
, fix a tuple
(H_{i},H_{j},H_{k},H_{\ell})
whose union
R=H_{i}\cup H_{j}\cup H_{k}\cup H_{\ell}
contains
h=|E(R)|\in[5,12]
edges and at most
h-1
vertices.
Similarly to (
12
), we obtain

(14)

\displaystyle\Prob(R\subseteq G(n,d))=O\bigg(\bigg(\frac{d}{n}\bigg)^{h}\bigg).

By combining (
14
), a simple union bound over the values
h\in[5,12]
, the inequality
d^{10}\leq n
and the second point of
Claim

3.3
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\Sigma_{1}=\sum_{h=5}^{12}O\bigg(n^{h-1}\bigg(\frac{d}{n}\bigg)^{h}\bigg)=O\left(\frac{d^{12}}{n}\right)=O(d^{6})=O(\mathbb{E}[X]^{2}),

as desired.
∎

We now estimate
\Sigma_{2}
.
As in the case
k=3
, all tuples
(H_{i},H_{j},H_{k},H_{\ell})
with union

contributing to
\Sigma_{2}
satisfy that every connected component of

contains a single cycle and, therefore, any two triangles must be (vertex-)disjoint.
Similarly to (
13
), we obtain

(15)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{2}

\displaystyle=\binom{n}{3}\binom{n-3}{3}\binom{n-6}{3}\binom{n-9}{3}\cdot\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right)\frac{(d-1)^{12}}{n^{12}}=\mathbb{E}[X]^{4}+O(\mathbb{E}[X]^{2}).

Thus by
Claim

3.7
and (
15
) we obtain that
\mathbb{E}[X_{(4)}]=\mathbb{E}[X]^{4}+O(\mathbb{E}[X]^{2})
, completing the proof of
Lemma

3.5
.
∎

Finally, we estimate
\mathbb{E}[(Y-Y^{*})^{2}]
.
Our proof closely follows the proof of Claim 12 in Gao’s work
[
6
]
, but we use
Lemma

3.5
to obtain a more precise estimate.

Proposition 3.8
.

Consider
d=d(n)\in[3,n^{1/10}]
. Then,
\mathrm{Var}[Y-Y^{*}]=O(\mathbb{E}[Y]^{2}/d^{4})
.

Proof.

By definition of
Y^{*}=aX+b
,

\mathrm{Cov}(Y^{*},Y^{*}-Y)=\mathrm{Var}[Y^{*}]-\mathrm{Cov}(Y^{*},Y)=a^{2}\mathrm{Var}[X]-a\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)=0.

Moreover, by the above equality and the fact that
\mathbb{E}[Y]=\mathbb{E}[Y^{*}]
, we obtain that

(16)

\begin{split}\mathrm{Var}[Y-Y^{*}]=\mathrm{Cov}(Y,Y-Y^{*})&=(\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]-\mathbb{E}[(Y^{*})^{2}])+(\mathbb{E}[(Y^{*})^{2}]-\mathbb{E}[YY^{*}])\\
&=(\mathrm{Var}[Y]-\mathrm{Var}[Y^{*}])+\mathrm{Cov}(Y^{*},Y^{*}-Y)\\
&=\mathrm{Var}[Y]-\mathrm{Var}[Y^{*}].\end{split}

On the one hand, by the fourth point in
Claim

3.3

and the fact that
d^{10}\leq n
, we deduce that

(17)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}[Y]=\left(\frac{1}{6d^{3}}+O\left(\frac{1}{d^{4}}+\frac{d^{3}}{n}+\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}(\log n)^{3}\right)\right)\mathbb{E}[Y]^{2}=\frac{\mathbb{E}[Y]^{2}}{6d^{3}}+O\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}[Y]^{2}}{d^{4}}\right).

On the other hand, by the first point in
Claim

3.3

and
Lemma

3.5
for
k=2
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}[Y^{*}]=\frac{\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)^{2}}{\mathrm{Var}[X]}

\displaystyle=\frac{(d^{-3}+O(d^{-4}+d/n))^{2}(\mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y])^{2}}{\mathbb{E}[X]+O(1)}.

Lastly, using the second point in
Claim

3.3
and the fact that
d^{10}\leq n
, we obtain that

(18)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}[Y^{*}]=\frac{\mathbb{E}[Y]^{2}}{6d^{3}}+O\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}[Y]^{2}}{d^{4}}\right).

Therefore, by combining (
16
), (
17
) and (
18
), it follows that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}[Y-Y^{*}]=\mathrm{Var}[Y]-\mathrm{Var}[Y^{*}]=O(\mathbb{E}[Y]^{2}/d^{4}),

as desired.
∎

We are ready to finish the proof of
Proposition

3.1
.

Proof of
Proposition

3.1
.

First, note that

(19)

\mathbb{P}(|Y-\mathbb{E}[Y]|\geq d^{-1.1}\mathbb{E}[Y])\leq\mathbb{P}(|Y-Y^{*}|\geq d^{-1.1}\mathbb{E}[Y]/2)+\mathbb{P}(|Y^{*}-\mathbb{E}[Y^{*}]|\geq d^{-1.1}\mathbb{E}[Y^{*}]/2).

For the first term, Chebyshev’s inequality and
Proposition

3.8
implies that

(20)

\mathbb{P}(|Y-Y^{*}|\geq d^{-1.1}\mathbb{E}[Y]/2)\leq\frac{\mathrm{Var}[Y-Y^{*}]}{d^{-2.2}\mathbb{E}[Y]^{2}/4}=O(d^{-1.8}).

To bound the second term, we express
\mathbb{E}[(Y^{*}-\mathbb{E}[Y^{*}])^{4}]
as a function of the moments of
X
. We have that

\displaystyle a^{-4}\mathbb{E}[(Y^{*}-\mathbb{E}[Y^{*}])^{4}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[(X-\mathbb{E}[X])^{4}]=\mathbb{E}[X^{4}]-4\mathbb{E}[X^{3}]\mathbb{E}[X]+6\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]\mathbb{E}[X]^{2}-3\mathbb{E}[X]^{4}.

By applying
Lemma

3.5
for each
k\in\{2,3,4\}
, we obtain that

\displaystyle a^{-4}\mathbb{E}[(Y^{*}-\mathbb{E}[Y^{*}])^{4}]=\binom{4}{2}\mathbb{E}[X]^{3}-4\binom{3}{2}\mathbb{E}[X]^{3}+6\binom{2}{2}\mathbb{E}[X]^{3}+O(\mathbb{E}[X]^{2})=O(\mathbb{E}[X]^{2}).

Combining this with the definition of
a
,
Claim

3.3
, the fact that
\mathbb{E}[X]=\Theta(\mathrm{Var}[X])=\Theta(d^{3})
from the
k=2
case of
Lemma

3.5
, and Markov’s inequality yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|Y^{*}-\mathbb{E}[Y^{*}]|\geq d^{-1.1}\mathbb{E}[Y]/2)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(|Y^{*}-\mathbb{E}[Y^{*}]|^{4}\geq d^{-4.4}\mathbb{E}[Y]^{4}/16)\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}[(Y^{*}-\mathbb{E}[Y^{*}])^{4}]}{d^{-4.4}\mathbb{E}[Y]^{4}/16}

\displaystyle=O\left(\frac{a^{4}\mathbb{E}[X]^{2}}{d^{-4.4}\mathbb{E}[Y]^{4}}\right)=O\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}[X]^{2}}{d^{12-4.4}}\right)=O(d^{-1.6}).

Together with (
19
) and (
20
), this finishes the proof.
∎

5.
Coupling lemmas: prelude to the proof of
Theorem

1.2

In this section, we present several technical lemmas in preparation for the proof of
Theorem

1.2
.

To begin with, we define the
isomorphism class
of a graph
G
to be the set of graphs
H
for which
V(G)=V(H)
for which there exists a bijective map
\sigma:V(G)\to V(H)
such that
\sigma(G)=H
, that is, for every pair of distinct vertices
u,v\in V(G)
,
uv\in E(G)
if and only if
\sigma(u)\sigma(v)\in E(H)
.
For fixed
n\geq 1
and
d\in[n-1]
, we denote by
\mathcal{C}_{d}=\mathcal{C}_{d}(n)
the family of isomorphism classes in
\mathcal{G}_{d}(n)
; in particular,
\mathcal{C}_{d}
is a partition of the set of
d
-regular graphs on
n
vertices.
We define the probability distributions
\bar{\mu}_{d}=\bar{\mu}_{d,n}
and
\bar{\nu}_{d}=\bar{\nu}_{d,n}
on the space
\mathcal{C}_{d}
by setting,
for each
A\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{d}
,

\bar{\mu}_{d,n}(A)=\sum_{S\in A}\sum_{G\in S}\mu_{d,n}(G)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\bar{\nu}_{d,n}(A)=\sum_{S\in A}\sum_{G\in S}\nu_{d,n}(G).

Note that for every class
S\in\mathcal{C}_{d}
and for every graph
G\in S
, we have
\mu_{d}(G)|S|=\bar{\mu}_{d}(S)
since
\mu_{d}(G)=1/|\mathcal{G}_{d}(n)|
for all
G\in\mathcal{G}_{d}(n)
. Moreover, the same holds for
\nu_{d}
, that is,
\nu_{d}(G)|S|=\bar{\nu}_{d}(S)
: indeed, every two graphs in the same isomorphism class have the same number of 1-factorisations.

Next, we fix an integer
d\geq 1
, define a procedure for sampling a union of
kd
disjoint perfect matchings on
n
vertices based on the isomorphism class structure of
\mathcal{G}_{d}(n)
, and show that it recovers the distribution
\nu_{kd,n}
.

Definition 5.1

(Alternative Sampling Procedure (ASP))
.

Fix a sequence of independent and identically distributed random variables
(Y_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
on the space
\mathcal{C}_{d}(n)
with distribution
\bar{\nu}_{d,n}
.
For every
k\geq 1
, conditionally on a realisation of
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{k}
, we define the random graph
H_{1}\oplus\dotsb\oplus H_{k}
to be a random element of the set
Y_{1}\oplus\dotsb\oplus Y_{k}
where, for every
i\in[k]
,
H_{i}
is sampled according to the uniform distribution on the isomorphism class
Y_{i}
conditionally on
H_{1},\ldots,H_{k}
being edge-disjoint.
We denote the distribution of
H_{1}\oplus\dotsb\oplus H_{k}
by
\eta_{k,d,n}
or simply
\eta_{k,d}
.

Lemma 5.2
.

For every
k,d\geq 1
and every graph
G\in\mathcal{G}_{kd}
,
\eta_{k,d}(G)=(1+o(1))\nu_{kd}(G)
where the
o(1)
is uniform over all choices of
G\in\mathcal{G}_{kd}
.

Proof.

Fix
k,d\geq 1
and recall that, by
Lemma

2.10
, for any choice of
k
(not necessarily distinct) classes
A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}\in\mathcal{C}_{d}
, the proportion of the
k
-tuples of graphs in
A_{1}\times A_{2}\times\ldots\times A_{k}
such that every two graphs are edge-disjoint is
\mathrm{e}^{-D/2}+o(1)
where
D=\tbinom{k}{2}d^{2}
and the
o(1)
is uniform over the choice of the classes.
For any graph
G\in\mathcal{G}_{kd}
, by the previous observation and the definition of the ASP,
\eta_{k,d}(G)
is equal to

\displaystyle\sum_{A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}\in\mathcal{C}_{d}}\,\prod_{i=1}^{k}\bar{\nu}_{d}(A_{i})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}G_{1}\in A_{1},\ldots,G_{k}\in A_{k}\\
\text{edge-disjoint and}\\
G_{1}\cup\dotsb\cup G_{k}=G\end{subarray}}\,\frac{1}{(\mathrm{e}^{-D/2}+o(1))\prod_{i=1}^{k}|A_{i}|}

\displaystyle=\sum_{A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}\in\mathcal{C}_{d}}\,\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}G_{1}\in A_{1},\ldots,G_{k}\in A_{k}\\
\text{edge-disjoint and}\\
G_{1}\cup\dotsb\cup G_{k}=G\end{subarray}}\,\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{k}\nu_{d}(G_{i})}{{\mathrm{e}^{-D/2}+o(1)}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}G_{1},\ldots,G_{k}\in\mathcal{G}_{d},\\
\text{edge-disjoint and}\\
G_{1}\cup\dotsb\cup G_{k}=G\end{subarray}}\,\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{k}\nu_{d}(G_{i})}{{\mathrm{e}^{-D/2}+o(1)}},

where the first equality used that
\nu_{d}(G_{i})|A_{i}|=\bar{\nu}_{d}(A_{i})
for every
i\in[k]
and the
o(1)
is uniform over the choice of
G
.
However, the last term is also proportional to
(\nu_{d}\oplus\dotsb\oplus\nu_{d})(G)=\nu_{kd}(G)
with
\nu_{d}
repeated
k
times,
where the equality follows from the associativity of the
\oplus
operation (
Lemma

2.5
).
Since each of
\eta_{k,d}
and
\nu_{kd}
is a probability distribution, these two distributions must coincide up to the multiplicative
(1+o(1))
-factor, as desired.
∎

The next lemma couples the distribution of the variables
(Y_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
from the definition of the ASP with the distribution
\bar{\mu}_{d}
so that, for every suitably large
k
and a random variable
X
with distribution
\bar{\mu}_{d}
, we have
X\in\{Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{k}\}
with probability close to 1.

Lemma 5.3
.

For every
\varepsilon>0
and
d\geq 1
, there are
k=k(\varepsilon,d)\geq 1
and
n_{0}=n_{0}(\varepsilon,d,k)\geq 1
with the following property: for every
n\geq n_{0}
, there is a coupling of the random variable
X
with distribution
\bar{\mu}_{d}
and the random variables
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{k}
from
Definition

5.1
such that
X\in\{Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{k}\}
with probability at least
1-\varepsilon
.

Proof.

Fix
k\geq 1
to be chosen suitably large in the sequel.
We inductively couple
X
and the sequences
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{i}
for every
i\geq 1
by using a sequence of Bernoulli random variables
I_{0}=0,I_{1},I_{2},\ldots
.
For every
i\geq 1
, the event
I_{i}=1
will indicate that
X\in\{Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{i}\}
: in particular, showing that
I_{k}=1
holds for some
k\geq 1
with probability at least
1-\varepsilon
is enough to conclude.

We iteratively construct a sequence of measures
\zeta_{i}=\zeta_{i,k,d,n}
on
\mathcal{C}_{d}
and partition functions

Z_{i}=Z_{i,k,d,n}=\sum_{A\in\mathcal{C}_{d}}\zeta_{i}(A)

for all
i\geq 0
, starting with
\zeta_{0}=\bar{\mu}_{d}
and
Z_{0}=1
.
Fix
i\geq 1
and suppose that
X
and the sequence
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{i-1}
have been coupled, the case
i=1
being trivial.
If
I_{i-1}=1
, we set
I_{i}=1
,
\zeta_{i}=\zeta_{i-1}
and proceed to the next step.
If
I_{i-1}=0
, suppose that the distribution of
X
conditionally on
I_{i-1}=0
is equal to
\zeta_{i-1}
; note that this assumption holds when
i=1
.
Then, by
Lemma

2.4
, we fix a coupling of
X
conditionally on
I_{i-1}
and
Y_{i}
so that
X=Y_{i}
with probability
1-d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\zeta_{i-1}/Z_{i-1},\bar{\nu}_{d})
.
As mentioned earlier, we let
I_{i}
denote the indicator random variable of the event
X=Y_{i}

and, for every
S\in\mathcal{C}_{d}
, we set

(24)

\zeta_{i}(S)=\frac{\zeta_{i-1}(S)}{Z_{i-1}}-\min\bigg(\frac{\zeta_{i-1}(S)}{Z_{i-1}},\bar{\nu}_{d}(S)\bigg)\qquad\text{and}\qquad Z_{i}=\sum_{A\in\mathcal{C}_{d}}\zeta_{i}(A).

By
Lemma

2.4
(iii) applied with
\mu=\zeta_{i-1}/Z_{i-1}
and
\nu=\bar{\nu}_{d}
, it follows that
\zeta_{i}/Z_{i}
is the distribution of
X
conditionally on
I_{i}=0
and, moreover, conditionally on
I_{i-1}=0
, the event
I_{i}=0
is independent of the random variables
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{i-1}
.
Furthermore, note that
Z_{i}=\mathbb{P}(I_{i}=0\mid I_{i-1}=0)=d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\zeta_{i-1}/Z_{i-1},\bar{\nu}_{d})
and, as a consequence, for every
k\geq 1
, the probability that
I_{k}=0
is equal to the product
Z_{1}\cdots Z_{k}
by the chain rule. The remainder of the proof is dedicated to showing that this product tends to 0 as
k\to\infty
.

We argue by contradiction. Suppose that
I_{i}=0
and the (decreasing) sequence
(Z_{1}\dotsb Z_{i})_{i\geq 1}
converges to a strictly positive limit
\alpha>0
. Fix
k\geq 1
and, for every
i\geq 0
, denote by
B_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{d}
the subset of classes
S
where
\zeta_{i}(S)>0
and note that the sequence of sets
(B_{i})_{i\geq 0}
is decreasing with respect to inclusion.
We show by induction that, for each class
S\in B_{k}
and for every
i\in\{k-1,k-2,\ldots,0\}
,

\zeta_{i}(S)\geq\bar{\nu}_{d}(S)\sum_{j=i}^{k-1}\prod_{\ell=i}^{j}Z_{\ell}.

The case
i=k-1
requires that
\zeta_{k-1}(S)\geq\bar{\nu}_{d}(S)Z_{k-1}
, which follows from the assumption
S\in B_{k}
and the definition of
\zeta_{k}
in (
24
).
Suppose that the statement holds for
i+1\in[k-1]
.
Since
S\in B_{k}\subseteq B_{i+1}
, we know that
\zeta_{i+1}(S)>0
and, therefore,
\min(\zeta_{i}(S)/Z_{i},\bar{\nu}_{d}(S))=\bar{\nu}_{d}(S)
.
Then, by using (
24
) again, we deduce that

\zeta_{i+1}(S)=\frac{\zeta_{i}(S)}{Z_{i}}-\bar{\nu}_{d}(S)\geq\bar{\nu}_{d}(S)\sum_{j=i+1}^{k-1}\prod_{\ell=i+1}^{j}Z_{\ell}\quad\text{and therefore, }\quad\frac{\zeta_{i}(S)}{Z_{i}}\geq\bar{\nu}_{d}(S)+\bar{\nu}_{d}(S)\sum_{j=i+1}^{k-1}\prod_{\ell=i+1}^{j}Z_{\ell},

implying the induction hypothesis for
i
.

By the induction hypothesis for
i=0
, for every
S\in B_{k}
, we have that

\bar{\mu}_{d}(S)=\zeta_{0}(S)\geq\bar{\nu}_{d}(S)\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\prod_{\ell=0}^{j}Z_{\ell}\geq\bar{\nu}_{d}(S)\cdot\alpha k.

In particular,
\bar{\mu}_{d}(B_{k})\geq\bar{\nu}_{d}(B_{k})\cdot\alpha k
and, as a consequence,

1\geq\mu_{d}(\{G:\exists S\in B_{k},G\in S\})\geq\nu_{d}(\{G:\exists S\in B_{k},G\in S\})\cdot\alpha k.

In particular, by
Proposition

2.6
(i) and the contiguity of
\mu_{d}
and
\nu_{d}
justified by
Theorem

2.2
,
there exists a large enough
k
so that

\nu_{d}(\{G:\exists S\in B_{k},G\in S\})\leq(\alpha k)^{-1}\qquad\text{implies that}\qquad\mu_{d}(\{G:\exists S\in B_{k},G\in S\})<\alpha.

Furthermore, note that, under the event
I_{k}=0
,
X
must belong to a class in
B_{k}
with probability 1. Thus,

\mu_{d}(\{G:\exists S\in B_{k},G\in S\})\geq\mathbb{P}(I_{k}=0)=Z_{1}\dotsb Z_{k}\geq\alpha,

leading to a contradiction.
∎

Finally, we show that
Lemma

5.3
implies that
G(n,d)
and
G(n,1)\oplus\dotsb\oplus G(n,1)
repeated
kd
times can be coupled so that the former random graph is included in the latter with probability close to 1.

Lemma 5.4
.

For every
\varepsilon>0
and
d\geq 1
, there exists
k=k(\varepsilon,d)\geq 1
with the following property: there is a coupling of the random graphs
G
with distribution
\mu_{d}
and

G_{\oplus}
with distribution
\nu_{kd}
so that
G\subseteq G_{\oplus}
with probability at least
1-\varepsilon
.

Proof.

First of all, fix
k=k(\varepsilon/2,d)
as given in
Lemma

5.3
and sample the corresponding (coupled) random variables
X,Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{k}
.
Next, we sample (according to a coupling to be explained shortly) the random graph
G
uniformly at random in the set
X
, and the random graph
G_{\oplus}^{\prime}=H_{1}\oplus\dotsb\oplus H_{k}
according to the ASP.
By definition of
X
, the graph
G
is sampled uniformly at random in
\mathcal{G}_{d}
while the graph
G_{\oplus}^{\prime}
has distribution
\eta_{k,d}
.

By
Lemma

5.3
, with probability at least
1-\varepsilon/2
, there exists
i\in[k]
such that
X=Y_{i}
.
Moreover, observe that, conditionally on the sets
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{k}
, for every
i\in[k]
, the graph
H_{i}
is distributed uniformly on the set
Y_{i}
: indeed, by symmetry, every graph in
Y_{i}
participates in the same number of
k
-tuples of edge-disjoint graphs in
Y_{1}\times\dotsb\times Y_{k}
.
As a result, conditionally on the sets
X,Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{k}
and the event
X=Y_{i}
for some

, the graphs
G
and
H_{i}
can be coupled so that
G=H_{i}
with probability 1. Since the probability measures
\nu_{kd}
and
\eta_{k,d}
coincide up to a
(1+o(1))
-factor by
Lemma

5.2
, one can couple the graphs
G_{\oplus}
and
G_{\oplus}^{\prime}
so that
G_{\oplus}=G_{\oplus}^{\prime}
with probability
1-o(1)
, which is enough to conclude.
∎

6.
Proof of
Theorem

1.2

We are ready to prove
Theorem

1.2
.

Proof of
Theorem

1.2
.

Recall that, by
[
6
, Theorem 6]
,
Theorem

1.2
holds when
\omega((\log n)^{7})=d_{1}\leq d_{2}\leq n-1
.
Therefore, it is enough to show the statement when
d_{1}\leq d_{2}\leq z(n):=(\log n)^{8}
: indeed, for every
d_{1}\leq z(n)
and
d_{2}\in[z(n),n-1]
, a coupling satisfying whp
G(n,d_{1})\subseteq G(n,d_{2})
can be constructed via two couplings where whp
G(n,d_{1})\subseteq G(n,z(n))
and
G(n,z(n))\subseteq G(n,d_{2})
, respectively.
We thus focus on the regime
d_{2}\leq z(n)
.
We consider the cases
d_{1}=O(1)
and
d_{1}=\omega(1)
separately.

Case 1:
d_{1}=O(1)
.

First, we set
d^{\prime}=d_{2}/2
and show that
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mu_{d^{\prime}}\oplus\nu_{d_{2}-d^{\prime}},\mu_{d_{2}})=o(1)
. By using the triangle inequality and
Corollary

2.9
(a), we get

(25)

\begin{split}d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mu_{d^{\prime}}\oplus\nu_{d_{2}-d^{\prime}},\mu_{d_{2}})&\leq\sum_{i=0}^{d_{2}-d^{\prime}-1}d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mu_{d^{\prime}+i}\oplus\nu_{d_{2}-d^{\prime}-i},\mu_{d^{\prime}+i+1}\oplus\nu_{d_{2}-d^{\prime}-i-1})\\
&=\sum_{i=0}^{d_{2}-d^{\prime}-1}d_{\mathrm{TV}}((\mu_{d^{\prime}+i}\oplus\mu_{1})\oplus\nu_{d_{2}-d^{\prime}-i-1},\mu_{d^{\prime}+i+1}\oplus\nu_{d_{2}-d^{\prime}-i-1})\\
&\leq C\cdot\sum_{i=0}^{d_{2}-d^{\prime}-1}d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mu_{d^{\prime}+i}\oplus\mu_{1},\mu_{d^{\prime}+i+1})\,,\end{split}

where

is the absolute constant in

Then, by

On the one hand, we show that, for any choice of

7.
Concluding remarks

In this paper, we exhibited several results related to the monotonicity and the nesting properties of random regular graphs, making progress towards
Conjecture

1.1
by Gao, Isaev and McKay
[
9
]
, and
Conjecture

1.3
by Isaev, McKay, Southwell and Zhukovskii
[
12
]
.
While partial progress has been obtained on each of them, completing the general picture remains an important open problem.
One particular point of interest is the converse implication of
Theorem

1.5
for
d\leq n^{1/10}
, or more generally for
d\in[n-1]
.
While our proof fails to establish contiguity between
G(n,d)
and
G(n,1)\oplus\dotsb\oplus G(n,1)
repeated
d
times, this would follow directly by combining our approach and a version of
Theorem

2.2
for
k=d
and
d_{1}=\dotsb=d_{k}=1
with arbitrarily slowly growing
d=\omega(1)
.

It would also be interesting to extend
Theorems

1.2
and
1.4
for odd
n
(as long as the random regular graphs with those parameters exist).
Again, our approach could be instrumental in deriving a similar bound to
Corollary

3.2
for
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mu_{d}\oplus\mu_{2},\mu_{d+2})
but important estimates for the count of 2-factors in random regular graphs are unavailable.
A key missing ingredient is exhibiting good concentration for the number of these
2
-factors: to the best of our knowledge, no such result currently exists in the literature for regular graphs with growing degree (see
[
27
]
for results concerning random regular graphs of bounded degree).

We hope that the results in this work could serve as a valuable tool in the study of random regular graphs. In a forthcoming work
[
11
]
, the authors employ
Theorem

1.5
to establish an approximate version of the Itai-Zehavi conjecture for random regular graphs.
