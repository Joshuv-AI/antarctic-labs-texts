Flocking in weakly nonreciprocal mixtures
Charlotte Myin, Benoît Mahault

Abstract
We show that weakly nonreciprocal alignment leads to large-scale structure formation in flocking mixtures. By combining numerical simulations of a binary Vicsek model and the analysis of coarse-grained continuum equations, we demonstrate that nonreciprocity destabilizes the ordered phase formed by mutually aligning or anti-aligning species in a large part of the phase diagram. For aligning populations, this instability results in one species condensing in a single band that travels within a homogeneous liquid of the other species. When interactions are anti-aligning, both species self-assemble into polar clusters with large-scale chaotic dynamics. In both cases, the emergence of structures is accompanied by the demixing of the two species, despite the absence of repulsive interactions. Our theoretical analysis allows us to elucidate the origin of the instability, and show that it is generic to nonreciprocal flocks.

V_{\textsl{{b}}\textsl{{a}}}q_{\|}&V_{\textsl{{b}}\textsl{{b}}}q_{\|}&V_{\textsl{{b}}\theta}q_{\perp}\\
V_{\theta\textsl{{a}}}q_{\perp}&V_{\theta\textsl{{b}}}q_{\perp}&V_{\theta\theta}q_{\|}\end{pmatrix}+\mathcal{O}(|\bm{q}|^{2}).

(4)

q_{\|}
and
q_{\perp}
in Eq. (
4
) denote the components of
\bm{q}
along and transverse to order, respectively,
while the
V
terms depend on the coefficients of Eq. (
2
) in a complex way
sup
.
To further simplify the analysis, we note from Fig.
3
(a) that upon decreasing
\eta
the homogeneous flocking solution is firstly unstable to longitudinal perturbations, such that we set
q_{\perp}=0
.
The matrix
\mathbb{M}_{\rm h}
then has a purely imaginary eigenvalue
\Lambda_{\theta}=iV_{\theta\theta}q_{\|}
,
while the remaining two are given by

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\pm}=\frac{iq_{\|}}{2}\left(V_{\textsl{{a}}\textsl{{a}}}+V_{\textsl{{b}}\textsl{{b}}}\pm\sqrt{\cal R}\right),

(5)

where
\mathcal{R}\equiv 4V_{\textsl{{a}}\textsl{{b}}}V_{\textsl{{b}}\textsl{{a}}}+(V_{\textsl{{a}}\textsl{{a}}}-V_{\textsl{{b}}\textsl{{b}}})^{2}
.
When
\mathcal{R}>0
, the eigenvalues in Eq. (
5
) are purely imaginary, and the stability of the solution to long-wavelength perturbations is determined at
{\cal O}(|\bm{q}|^{2})
.
On the other hand, for
\mathcal{R}<0
—i.e., when
V_{\textsl{{a}}\textsl{{b}}}V_{\textsl{{b}}\textsl{{a}}}<-\tfrac{1}{4}(V_{\textsl{{a}}\textsl{{a}}}-V_{\textsl{{b}}\textsl{{b}}})^{2}
—
the eigenvalue
\Lambda_{-}
acquires a positive real part,
indicating the emergence of an instability.
In particular, for reciprocally aligning populations
V_{\textsl{{a}}\textsl{{b}}}
and
V_{\textsl{{b}}\textsl{{a}}}
share the same sign and
{\cal R}>0
,
while the condition
V_{\textsl{{a}}\textsl{{b}}}V_{\textsl{{b}}\textsl{{a}}}<0
requires a sufficient amount of nonreciprocity.
Anti-flocking mixtures are more difficult to analyze, as in this case the instability is not purely longitudinal or transverse [Fig.
3
(b)].
Nevertheless, the solid pink lines in Figs.
3
enclosing regions for which
\mathbb{M}_{\rm h}
has an eigenvalue with positive real part show excellent agreement with the direct numerical estimate based on the six-dimensional system.
In particular, for
\bar{\chi}>0
the pink line corresponds to
{\cal R}=0
when the instability is longitudinal.

It is clear from Eqs. (
4
,
5
) that the new instability relies on the coupling between the two density fields.
Therefore, it cannot arise in the single-species Vicsek model, or when nonreciprocally aligning spins are fixed on a lattice
Guislain and Bertin 2024
;
Avni et al. 2025
;
Blom et al. 2025
.
This also explains why it was missed in Ref.
Fruchart et al. 2021
, where fluctuations of the densities are discarded in the analysis of the continuum model.
Although
\cal R
cannot be compactly expressed in terms of the microscopic model parameters,
the density couplings in Eq. (
4
) can be written as

V_{\textsl{{s}}\textsl{{u}}}=v_{0}^{\textsl{{s}}}\partial_{\rho_{\textsl{{u}}}}{\cal L}^{\textsl{{s}}}
, where
{\cal L}^{\textsl{{s}}}
is a linear combination of the
\alpha^{\textsl{{u}}}_{\textsl{{v}}}(\rho^{\textsl{{a}}},\rho^{\textsl{{b}}})
coefficients in Eq. (
2b
) (details in Appendix C).
This formulation notably highlights that the instability is driven by a combination of two ingredients: the particles’ self-propulsion encoded by
v_{0}^{\textsl{{s}}}
, and the local (anti)-alignment of velocities,
which is reflected in the density dependencies of the
\alpha^{\textsl{{u}}}_{\textsl{{v}}}
coefficients.

We have shown that even weakly nonreciprocal interactions significantly impact the collective dynamics of flocking active matter.
Combining agent-based simulations and the analysis of a coarse-grained theory, our results demonstrate that nonreciprocal alignment alone constitutes a generic route to demixing, without resorting to other mechanisms such as population heterogeneity
Maity and Morin 2023
or pairwise repulsion
Kreienkamp and Klapp 2024a
;
Kreienkamp and Klapp 2024b
.
Our theoretical study further emphasises that this phenomenology is induced by the coupling between density and orientational order, which shapes most of the characteristics of flocks.
Our findings echo recent works highlighting the fragility of flocks
Benvegnen et al. 2023
and their sensitivity to the presence of spatial anisotropy
Solon et al. 2022
, inclusions and boundaries
Codina et al. 2022
;
Fava et al. 2024
, and quench disorder
Morin et al. 2017
;
Chardac et al. 2021
;
Duan et al. 2021
.
Since they rely solely on weakly nonreciprocal alignment, we believe that our results will have implications for the modeling of a variety of systems,
including mixtures of Quincke rollers
Maity and Morin 2023
or Janus colloids
Tucci et al. 2024
, and migrating heterogeneous cellular tissues
Scita et al. 2025
.

Appendix B: Derivation of the continuum model.—

We coarse-grained a multi-population VM analogous to that defined in Eqs. (
1
) via the Boltzmann-Ginzburg-Landau (BGL) approach
Bertin et al. 2006
;
Bertin et al. 2009
;
Peshkov et al. 2014
.
To keep the derivation general, we consider here
n_{\textsl{{s}}}
populations.
Particles of species
s
self-propel at constant speed
v_{0}^{\textsl{{s}}}
while we model the influence of angular noise via stochastic tumbling events happening at a frequency
\lambda^{\textsl{{s}}}
and with a distribution
P_{\eta^{\textsl{{s}}}}(\theta)
with zero mean and standard deviation
\eta^{\textsl{{s}}}
.

As indicated in the main text, the BGL method consists in first writing a Boltzmann equation governing the evolution of the single-body distribution
f^{\textsl{{s}}}(\bm{r},\theta,t)
associated with species
s
.
Here, we have

\partial_{t}f^{\textsl{{s}}}+v_{0}^{\textsl{{s}}}\hat{\bm{u}}(\theta)\cdot\nabla f^{\textsl{{s}}}=I_{\rm sd}^{\textsl{{s}}}[f^{\textsl{{s}}}]+I_{\rm al}^{\textsl{{s}}}[\{f^{\textsl{{u}}}\}_{\textsl{{u}}=1\ldots n_{\textsl{{s}}}}],

(B1)

where the
I_{\rm sd}^{\textsl{{s}}}
and
I_{\rm coll}^{\textsl{{s}}}
account for rotational self-diffusion and (anti-)alignment, respectively. These integrals read

\displaystyle I_{\rm sd}^{\textsl{{s}}}[f^{\textsl{{s}}}]=\lambda^{\textsl{{s}}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}{\rm d}\phi\,f^{\textsl{{s}}}(\bm{r},\phi,t)P_{\eta^{\textsl{{s}}}}(\theta-\phi)-\lambda^{\textsl{{s}}}f^{\textsl{{s}}}(\bm{r},\theta,t),

(B2)

\displaystyle I_{\rm al}^{\textsl{{s}}}[\{f^{\textsl{{u}}}\}]=-\int_{0}^{2\pi}{\rm d}\phi\,\sum_{\textsl{{u}}}f^{\textsl{{s}}}(\bm{r},\theta,t)f^{\textsl{{u}}}(\bm{r},\phi,t)K^{\textsl{{s}}\textsl{{u}}}(\theta-\phi)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{2\pi}{\rm d}\phi_{1}\int_{0}^{2\pi}{\rm d}\phi_{2}\,\sum_{\textsl{{u}}}f^{\textsl{{s}}}(\bm{r},\phi_{1},t)f^{\textsl{{u}}}(\bm{r},\phi_{2},t)

\displaystyle\times K^{\textsl{{s}}\textsl{{u}}}(\phi_{2}-\phi_{1})P_{\eta^{\textsl{{s}}}}[\theta-\phi_{1}-H^{\textsl{{s}}\textsl{{u}}}(\phi_{2}-\phi_{1})].

(B3)

Equation (
B3
) is written under the standard assumptions behind the formulation of the Boltzmann equation.
Namely, we assume that collisions involving more than two particles can be neglected, while they are sufficiently local such that all distributions in (
B3
) depend on
\bm{r}
only.
In addition, we used the molecular chaos hypothesis to factorize the two-body particle distribution.

The kernel
K^{\textsl{{s}}\textsl{{u}}}(\phi_{2}-\phi_{1})
is proportional to the rate of collisions between particles from species
s
and
u
with pre-collisional orientations
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{2}
, respectively.
Following a similar approach as for single-species flocks
Chaté and Mahault 2022
yields

\displaystyle K^{\textsl{{s}}\textsl{{u}}}(\phi_{2}-\phi_{1})=2r^{\textsl{{s}}}_{0}\left|v_{0}^{\textsl{{u}}}\hat{\bm{u}}(\phi_{2})-v_{0}^{\textsl{{s}}}\hat{\bm{u}}(\phi_{1})\right|

\displaystyle=2r^{\textsl{{s}}}_{0}v_{0}^{\textsl{{s}}}\left[1+\left(\frac{v_{0}^{\textsl{{u}}}}{v_{0}^{\textsl{{s}}}}\right)^{2}-2\frac{v_{0}^{\textsl{{u}}}}{v_{0}^{\textsl{{s}}}}\cos(\phi_{2}-\phi_{1})\right]^{\tfrac{1}{2}},

where
r_{0}^{\textsl{{s}}}
is the radius of interaction associated with species
s
.
Furthermore,
\phi_{1}+H^{\textsl{{s}}\textsl{{u}}}(\phi_{2}-\phi_{1})
corresponds to the post-collisional angle taken by both particles, such that we have

H^{\textsl{{s}}\textsl{{u}}}(\phi_{2}-\phi_{1})=\,{\rm arg}[1+\chi^{\textsl{{s}}\textsl{{u}}}e^{i(\phi_{2}-\phi_{1})}]
.

Expanding the distribution
f^{\textsl{{s}}}
into angular Fourier modes as
f^{\textsl{{s}}}(\bm{r},\theta,t)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{k}f_{k}^{\textsl{{s}}}(\bm{r},t)e^{-ik\theta}
, the latter obey [from Eq. (
B1
)]

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f^{\textsl{{s}}}_{k}+\frac{v_{0}^{\textsl{{s}}}}{2}\left[\triangledown^{*}f_{k+1}^{\textsl{{s}}}+\triangledown f_{k-1}^{\textsl{{s}}}\right]=\tilde{\lambda}^{\textsl{{s}}}_{k}f_{k}^{\textsl{{s}}}+\sum_{\textsl{{u}},q}J_{kq}^{\textsl{{s}}\textsl{{u}}}f_{q}^{\textsl{{u}}}f_{k-q}^{\textsl{{s}}},

(B4)

where the complex gradient is defined as
\triangledown\equiv\partial_{x}+i\partial_{y}
,
while
\tilde{\lambda}^{\textsl{{s}}}_{k}\equiv\lambda^{\textsl{{s}}}(P_{k}^{\textsl{{s}}}-1)

with
P_{k}^{\textsl{{s}}}
the
k^{\rm th}
Fourier mode of the distribution
P_{\eta^{\textsl{{s}}}}
.
The mode coupling coefficients are given by
J_{kq}^{\textsl{{s}}\textsl{{u}}}=P_{k}^{\textsl{{s}}}I_{kq}^{\textsl{{s}}\textsl{{u}}}-I_{0q}^{\textsl{{s}}\textsl{{u}}}
with

I_{kq}^{\textsl{{s}}\textsl{{u}}}\equiv\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}{\rm d}\phi\,K^{\textsl{{s}}\textsl{{u}}}(\phi)e^{-iq\phi+ikH^{\textsl{{s}}\textsl{{u}}}(\phi)}.

(B5)

The zeroth and first modes
f_{0}^{\textsl{{s}}}
and
f_{1}^{\textsl{{s}}}
are of particular interest, as they correspond to the complex representations of the local particle densities
\rho^{\textsl{{s}}}
and polarities
\bm{p}^{\textsl{{s}}}
, respectively.
Equation (
B4
) defines an infinite hierarchy, which we close by assuming a standard scaling ansatz
Bertin et al. 2006
;
Peshkov et al. 2014
describing the relative amplitudes of the Fourier modes and their space and time derivatives at the onset of global order.
Namely,

|f_{k}^{\textsl{{s}}}|\simeq\varepsilon^{k}\;(k>0),\qquad|f_{0}^{\textsl{{s}}}-\rho_{0}^{\textsl{{s}}}|\simeq\varepsilon,\qquad\partial_{t}\simeq\triangledown\simeq\varepsilon,

where
\varepsilon
is a small parameter.
Formally, this scaling ansatz supposes that all species exhibit comparable global polarities. This should remain true in the presence of weak population heterogeneity, which is the regime of interest here.

We truncate the hierarchy at the first nontrivial order
\varepsilon^{3}
.
At this order, the equations for the particle densities
f_{0}^{\textsl{{s}}}=\rho^{\textsl{{s}}}
are exact,
while
f_{1}^{\textsl{{s}}}
obeys

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f_{1}^{\textsl{{s}}}=

\displaystyle-\frac{v_{0}^{\textsl{{s}}}}{2}\triangledown\rho^{\textsl{{s}}}-\frac{v_{0}^{\textsl{{s}}}}{2}\triangledown^{*}f_{2}^{\textsl{{s}}}+\sum_{\textsl{{u}}}M_{1}^{\textsl{{s}}\textsl{{u}}}(\{\rho^{\textsl{{v}}}\})f_{1}^{\textsl{{u}}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{\textsl{{u}}}\left[J_{12}^{\textsl{{s}}\textsl{{u}}}f_{2}^{\textsl{{u}}}f_{1}^{\textsl{{s}}*}+J_{1-1}^{\textsl{{s}}\textsl{{u}}}f_{2}^{\textsl{{s}}}f_{1}^{\textsl{{u}}*}\right]+O(\varepsilon^{5}),

(B6)

where

M_{k}^{\textsl{{s}}\textsl{{u}}}(\{\rho^{\textsl{{v}}}\})\equiv[\tilde{\lambda}^{\textsl{{s}}}_{k}+(\sum_{\textsl{{v}}}J_{k0}^{\textsl{{s}}\textsl{{v}}}\rho^{\textsl{{v}}})]\delta^{\textsl{{s}}\textsl{{u}}}+J_{kk}^{\textsl{{s}}\textsl{{u}}}\rho^{\textsl{{s}}}
.
The modes
f_{2}^{\textsl{{s}}}
appear in Eq. (
B6
) only in
O(\varepsilon^{3})
terms.
Hence, we write the equation for
f_{2}^{\textsl{{s}}}
at
O(\varepsilon^{2})
:

\sum_{\textsl{{u}}}M_{2}^{\textsl{{s}}\textsl{{u}}}(\{\rho_{0}^{\textsl{{v}}}\})f_{2}^{\textsl{{u}}}-\frac{v_{0}^{\textsl{{s}}}}{2}\triangledown f_{1}^{\textsl{{s}}}+\sum_{\textsl{{u}}}J_{21}^{\textsl{{s}}\textsl{{u}}}f_{1}^{\textsl{{s}}}f_{1}^{\textsl{{u}}}+O(\varepsilon^{3})=0.

(B7)

We close the hierarchy by solving Eq. (
B7
) for
f_{2}^{\textsl{{s}}}
and inserting the solution into Eq. (
B6
).
Considering two species with identical speed
v_{0}
, interaction radius
r_{0}
and noise strength
\eta
,
we then recover Eqs. (
2
) of the main text after adopting the vector representation
f_{1}^{\textsl{{s}}}\to\bm{p}^{\textsl{{s}}}
.
In all numerical calculations, we used a Gaussian noise distribution such that
P_{k}^{\textsl{{s}}}=\exp[-k^{2}\eta^{2}/2]
.

For arbitrary alignment couplings
\chi
, the integrals (
B5
) do not admit simple analytical expressions, such that the coefficients in Eqs. (
2
) cannot be simply expressed in terms of the parameters of the microscopic model.
For convenience, we thus provide in
sup
a Mathematica notebook which can be used to evaluate numerically the coefficients of the continuum model.

Appendix C: Enslaving of the non-hydrodynamic modes.—
Here, we detail the enslaving procedure leading to Eq. (
4
).
We consider a
m
-dimensional linear system of the form
\partial_{t}\bm{X}=\mathbb{M}(\bm{q})\cdot\bm{X}
, where
\mathbb{M}(\bm{q})
is a wave-vector dependent
m\times m
matrix.
As we are interested in the
q=|\bm{q}|\to 0
limit, we expand
\mathbb{M}

as

\mathbb{M}(\bm{q})=\mathbb{M}_{0}+iq\mathbb{M}_{1}-q^{2}\mathbb{M}_{2}+\mathcal{O}(q^{3}),

(C1)

where the
\mathbb{M}_{i}
matrices are independent of
q
but may depend on the direction of
\bm{q}
.

We denote
\{\bm{Y}_{j}\}
as the left eigenbasis of
\mathbb{M}_{0}
, such that for all
j=1,\ldots,m
,
\bm{Y}_{j}^{T}\cdot\mathbb{M}_{0}=\Lambda_{j}\bm{Y}_{j}^{T}
,
where
\{\Lambda_{j}\}
are the corresponding eigenvalues.
Assuming that the initial linear system describes fluctuations around a solution which is stable under
q=0
perturbations, all
\Lambda_{j}\leq 0
.
We now suppose that the problem has
n_{\rm h}
hydrodynamic modes, and we reorder the eigenvalues such that

\Lambda_{1},\ldots,\Lambda_{n_{\rm h}}=0
and
\Lambda_{n_{\rm h}+1},\ldots,\Lambda_{m}<0
.
Defining

\tilde{\bm{X}}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{Y}_{1}&\cdots&\bm{Y}_{m}\end{pmatrix}^{T}\cdot\bm{X}\equiv\mathbb{P}\cdot\bm{X},

(C2)

the first
n_{\rm h}
elements of
\tilde{\bm{X}}
are the hydrodynamic modes, while the other
m-n_{\rm h}
are fast.
This can be straightforwardly shown from

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\tilde{\bm{X}}

\displaystyle\underset{q=0}{=}\mathbb{P}\cdot\partial_{t}\bm{X}=\mathbb{P}\cdot\mathbb{M}_{0}\cdot\bm{X}={\rm diag}(\lambda_{1}\dots\lambda_{m})\cdot\mathbb{P}\cdot\bm{X}

\displaystyle={\rm diag}(\lambda_{1}\dots\lambda_{m})\cdot\tilde{\bm{X}},

where we have used that
\mathbb{P}\cdot\mathbb{M}_{0}\cdot\mathbb{P}^{-1}={\rm diag}(\lambda_{1}\dots\lambda_{m})
.
While the slow modes are uniquely defined, the fast modes may be expressed differently so long as they are associated with a nonzero damping rate. The formulation (
C2
) nevertheless provides a compact change of basis formula between the slow and fast modes and the original variables.
Going back to
q>0
, the dynamics of
\tilde{\bm{X}}
then follows

\partial_{t}\tilde{\bm{X}}=\tilde{\mathbb{M}}(\bm{q})\cdot\tilde{\bm{X}}
, where
\tilde{\mathbb{M}}\equiv\mathbb{P}\cdot\mathbb{M}\cdot\mathbb{P}^{-1}
.
In the regime of interest,
\mathbb{P}
is always invertible.
For convenience, we write separately the dynamics of the hydrodynamic (
\bm{X}_{\rm h}
) and fast (
\bm{X}_{\rm f}
) modes (dropping the tildes to lighten notations):

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\bm{X}_{\rm h}-\mathbb{M}^{\rm h}_{\rm h}\cdot\bm{X}_{\rm h}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{M}^{\rm h}_{\rm f}\cdot\bm{X}_{\rm f},

(C3)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\bm{X}_{\rm f}-\mathbb{M}^{\rm f}_{\rm f}\cdot\bm{X}_{\rm f}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{M}^{\rm f}_{\rm h}\cdot\bm{X}_{\rm h},

(C4)

in terms of the block matrices
\mathbb{M}^{x}_{y}
.
By construction, all elements of
\mathbb{M}^{\rm h}_{\rm h}
and
\mathbb{M}^{\rm h}_{\rm f}
are zero for
q=0
, while
\mathbb{M}^{\rm f}_{\rm f}(q=0)
is diagonal with negative entries representing the finite damping rates of the fast modes.
Expanding all block matrices similarly to (
C1
), we then enslave the fast modes by solving Eq. (
C4
) in the limit
q\to 0
.
Here, we focus on the leading order solution, while a derivation including
{\cal O}(q^{2})
corrections is presented in
sup
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\bm{X}_{\rm f}=-\left({\mathbb{M}}^{\rm f}_{{\rm f},0}\right)^{-1}\cdot{\mathbb{M}}^{\rm f}_{{\rm h},0}\cdot\bm{X}_{\rm h}+\mathcal{O}(q).

Replacing this solution in Eq. (
C3
),
we then find that the hydrodynamic modes obey
\partial_{t}\bm{X}_{\rm h}=\mathbb{M}_{\rm h}(\bm{q})\cdot\bm{X}_{\rm h}
with

\mathbb{M}_{\rm h}(\bm{q})=iq\left(\mathbb{M}^{\rm h}_{{\rm h},1}-\mathbb{M}^{\rm h}_{{\rm f},1}\cdot\left({\mathbb{M}}^{\rm f}_{{\rm f},0}\right)^{-1}\cdot{\mathbb{M}}^{\rm f}_{{\rm h},0}\right)+{\cal O}(q^{2}).

(C5)

Performing this analysis for the linear stability described in the main text, we find three slow modes:
\delta\hat{\rho}^{\textsl{{a}},\textsl{{b}}}
and
\bar{\theta}=s^{\textsl{{a}}}_{0}F^{\textsl{{b}}}_{\textsl{{a}}}\delta\hat{\theta}^{\textsl{{a}}}+{s^{\textsl{{b}}}_{0}}{F^{\textsl{{a}}}_{\textsl{{b}}}}\delta\hat{\theta}^{\textsl{{b}}}
.
The expressions of the various matrices involved in the derivation are quite lengthy and not very informative.
We can nevertheless express the coefficients that couple the density fluctuations in Eq. (
4
) as

\displaystyle V_{\textsl{{a}}\textsl{{a}}}

\displaystyle=v_{0}^{\textsl{{a}}}\left[C_{\textsl{{a}}\textsl{{a}}}E^{\textsl{{a}}}_{\textsl{{a}}}+C_{\textsl{{a}}\textsl{{b}}}E^{\textsl{{b}}}_{\textsl{{a}}}\right],

\displaystyle V_{\textsl{{a}}\textsl{{b}}}

\displaystyle=v_{0}^{\textsl{{a}}}\left[C_{\textsl{{a}}\textsl{{a}}}E^{\textsl{{a}}}_{\textsl{{b}}}+C_{\textsl{{a}}\textsl{{b}}}E^{\textsl{{b}}}_{\textsl{{b}}}\right],

\displaystyle V_{\textsl{{b}}\textsl{{a}}}

\displaystyle=v_{0}^{\textsl{{b}}}\left[C_{\textsl{{b}}\textsl{{a}}}E^{\textsl{{a}}}_{\textsl{{a}}}+C_{\textsl{{b}}\textsl{{b}}}E^{\textsl{{b}}}_{\textsl{{a}}}\right],

\displaystyle V_{\textsl{{b}}\textsl{{b}}}

\displaystyle=v_{0}^{\textsl{{b}}}\left[C_{\textsl{{b}}\textsl{{a}}}E^{\textsl{{a}}}_{\textsl{{b}}}+C_{\textsl{{b}}\textsl{{b}}}E^{\textsl{{b}}}_{\textsl{{b}}}\right],

where the
C_{\textsl{{s}}\textsl{{u}}}
coefficients are constant and depend on the model parameters, while
E^{\textsl{{s}}}_{\textsl{{u}}}=\left(s_{0}^{\textsl{{a}}}\partial_{\rho^{\textsl{{u}}}}\alpha^{\textsl{{s}}}_{\textsl{{a}}}+\epsilon s_{0}^{\textsl{{b}}}\partial_{\rho^{\textsl{{u}}}}\alpha^{\textsl{{s}}}_{\textsl{{b}}}\right)_{\rho^{\textsl{{s}}}=\rho_{0}}
with
\epsilon={\rm sign}(\bar{\chi})

sup
.
