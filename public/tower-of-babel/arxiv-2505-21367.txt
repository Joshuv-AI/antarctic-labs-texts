Abstract
There are many intriguing questions in extremal graph theory that are well-understood in the undirected setting and yet remain elusive for digraphs. A natural instance of such a problem was recently studied by Hons, Klimošová, Kucheriya, Mikšaník, Tkadlec and Tyomkyn: What are the digraphs that have to appear as a subgraph in all digraphs of sufficiently large minimum out-degree? Hons et al. showed that all such digraphs must be oriented forests with a specific structure, and conjectured that vice-versa all oriented forests with this specific structure appear in any digraph of sufficiently large minimum out-degree. In this paper, we confirm their conjecture.



1
Introduction

There is a large body of open problems in extremal combinatorics concerning minimum degree conditions on a graph or a digraph which enforce the existence of a specific substructure, such as a certain subgraph, a minor or a subdivision. An intriguing phenomenon which occurs for many if not most of these problems is that there seems to be a stark difference between the difficulty of undirected and directed instances, with the latter often remaining wide open or even being false. In the following, let us only discuss three selected examples which illustrate this point:

•

The famous and still widely open
Caccetta-Häggkvist-conjecture

[
7
]
from 1978 posits that the minimum out-degree threshold enforcing the existence of a directed cycle of length at most
g
in an oriented graph with
n
vertices equals
\left\lceil\frac{n}{g}\right\rceil
. The most popular special case of this problem that still remains open is when
g=3
, which corresponds to the minimum out-degree threshold forcing a directed triangle in oriented graphs. The best known upper bound is of the form
(1+\varepsilon)\frac{n}{3}
for some
\varepsilon>0

[
10
]
, see also
[
18
]
for a survey. In contrast, the minimum degree threshold enforcing a triangle in
n
-vertex undirected graphs is well-known to be precisely
\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor+1
by Mantel’s theorem.

•

A classic result in extremal graph theory, due to Bollobás-Thomason
[
5
]
and independently Komlós-Szemerédi
[
12
,
13
]
states that the minimum degree threshold enforcing a
subdivision
of a clique on
t
vertices is of order
\Theta(t^{2})
. In contrast, the corresponding result turns out to be wrong for digraphs, even qualitatively. Namely, it is known that there exist digraphs of arbitrarily large minimum out-degree which do not contain an even directed cycle
[
21
]
, and it is easy to observe that such digraphs cannot contain a subdivision of a complete digraph of order
t
for any
t\geq 3
. Motivated by this discrepancy, Mader
[
14
]
conjectured in 1985 that for every
t\in\mathbb{N}
there exists a constant
d(t)\in\mathbb{N}
such that every digraph of minimum out-degree at least
d(t)
contains a subdivision of the
transitively oriented
tournament of order
t
. Mader’s conjecture remains widely open and has only been proved for
t\leq 4

[
15
]
. Note that Mader’s conjecture can be restated as saying that for every acyclic digraph
F
there exists a constant
d(F)\in\mathbb{N}
such that all digraphs of minimum out-degree at least
d(F)
contain a subdivision of
F
. This statement has so far only been verified for precious few classes of digraphs
F
, such as oriented cycles
[
9
]
and special types of oriented trees
[
1
]
. It remains open whether it holds for all oriented trees.

•

A fundamental question raised by Stiebitz
[
16
]
in 1995 and independently by Alon
[
2
,
3
]
in 1996 and again in a survey article in 2006 asks whether for every pair
s,t
of positive integers there exists some
d(s,t)\in\mathbb{N}
such that every digraph
D
with minimum out-degree
d(s,t)
allows for a partition
A\cup B
of its vertex-set such that
\delta^{+}(D[A])\geq s
and
\delta^{+}(D[B])\geq t
. This question remains widely open for all pairs
(s,t)
except
(s,t)=(1,1)
. For the latter case an affirmative answer follows from the fact that digraphs of sufficiently large minimum out-degree contain many disjoint directed cycles
[
19
,
2
,
6
]
, which is related to another famous conjecture in extremal digraph theory, namely the so-called
Bermond-Thomassen conjecture

[
4
]
. Petrova and the authors
[
8
]
recently proved that the question of Stiebitz and Alon can be reduced to the case
(s,t)=(2,2)
in a strong sense.

Also here, the analogous question for undirected graphs turns out to be much easier and was settled affirmatively by Thomassen
[
20
]
back in 1983. The optimal bound on the necessary minimum degree in this case was later determined to be
s+t+1
by Stiebitz
[
17
]
.

In this paper, we shall be concerned with another highly natural problem of this type which was recently introduced and studied by Hons, Klimošová, Kucheriya, Mikšaník, Tkadlec and Tyomkyn
[
11
]
. Following their terminlogy, let us say that a (di)graph
F
is
\delta
-enforcible
/
\delta^{+}
-enforcible
if there exists a constant
d(F)\in\mathbb{N}
(depending only on
F
) such that every (di)graph with minimum (out-)degree at least
d(F)
contains a sub(di)graph isomorphic to
F
. It is easy to observe that an undirected graph
F
is
\delta
-enforcible if and only if it is a forest. To see this, note that a greedy embedding strategy proves that every graph with minimum degree at least
k-1
contains every forest on at most
k
vertices as a subgraph. In the other direction, note that the existence of graphs with arbitrarily large minimum degree and girth implies that no graph containing a cycle is
\delta
-enforcible.

One may naively expect that the situation for digraphs is analogous and that the
\delta^{+}
-enforcible digraphs simply consist of all oriented forests. However, as proved by Hons et al.
[
11
]
this is not the case: All
\delta^{+}
-enforcible digraphs are oriented forests with a restricted structure, namely, all their vertices of in-degree at least two must be of the same “height”. To state their result more precisely, for a digraph
D
, let us define a
height function
of
D
as any mapping
h:V(D)\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
such that
h(v)=h(u)+1
for every arc
(u,v)\in A(D)
. Note that every oriented forest admits a height function and that the latter is unique up to uniform shifts within connected components. Let us say that an oriented forest/tree is a
grounded forest/tree
if it admits a height function that is constant on the set of vertices of in-degree at least
2
. See Figure
1
for an example of such a tree.

Figure 1:
A grounded tree. The height function is reflected by the heights of vertices in the picture (with height increasing from top to bottom) and the vertices of in-degree at least two are circled.

We are now ready to state the aforementioned result of Hons et al.

Theorem 1.1

(cf.
[
11
]
, Theorem 1.3)
.

Every
\delta^{+}
-enforcible digraph is a grounded forest.

One of the main open problems posed by Hons et al.
[
11
]
is the conjecture that the necessary condition above for a digraph to be
\delta^{+}
-enforcible is also sufficient.

Conjecture 1.2

(KAMAK tree conjecture, cf. Conjecture 1.4 in
[
11
]
)
.

Every grounded forest is
\delta^{+}
-enforcible.

As the main result of this paper, we prove this conjecture. It is worth noting that Conjecture
1.2
reduces to the case when
F
is connected (i.e. an oriented tree). Indeed, suppose an oriented forest
F
has connected components
F_{1},\ldots,F_{k}
and
d_{1},\ldots,d_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
are integers such that every digraph of minimum out-degree at least
d_{i}
contains a subdigraph isomorphic to
F_{i}
. Then, given any digraph
D
with minimum out-degree at least, say,
\max\{d_{1},\ldots,d_{k}\}+|V(F_{1})|+\cdots+|V(F_{k})|
, one can embed disjoint copies of
F_{1},\ldots,F_{k}
into
D
one by one. Hence, the following result proves Conjecture
1.2
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Every grounded tree is
\delta^{+}
-enforcible.

As explained in the previous discussion, this implies a characterization of
\delta^{+}
-enforcible digraphs.

Corollary 1.4
.

A digraph
F
is
\delta^{+}
-enforcible if and only if it is a grounded forest.

Organization.

In the remainder of the paper, we work towards proving Theorem
1.3
. We start in Section
2
by introducing a key structure in our proof, called
(k,d)
-broom digraphs
, and by proving several auxiliary statements that show how one can find highly structured “sub-broom digraphs” in a given broom digraph. Finally, in Section
3
we use these results to embed any given grounded tree (after some slight cleaning) into any given
(k,d)
-broom digraph for sufficiently large
d
, and thus, into any digraph of sufficiently high minimum out-degree, proving Theorem
1.3
.

Notation and Terminology.

For an integer
k\geq 1
we use
[k]
as a shorthand for the first
k
integers. We denote by
V(D)
and
A(D)\subseteq\{(u,v)\in V(D)^{2}|u\neq v\}
, respectively, the vertex and arc set of a digraph
D
. For a vertex
v\in V(D)
, we denote by
N_{D}^{+}(v),N_{D}^{-}(v)
the out- and in-neighborhood of
v
, respectively, and by
d_{D}^{+}(v),d_{D}^{-}(v)
its out- and in-degree in
D
. We denote by
\delta^{+}(D),\delta^{-}(D)
the minimum out- and in-degree of
D
. Given a subset
X
of
V(D)
or
A(D)
, we denote by
D-X
the digraph obtained from
D
by deleting all elements in
X
from
D
. Similarly, for
X\subseteq V(D)
we denote by
D[X]
the induced subdigraph of
D
with vertex set
X
.

2
Auxiliary lemmas

In this section, we prepare the proof of Theorem
1.3
by proving some important auxiliary results. These results are concerned with so-called
(k,d)
-broom digraphs
, which are certain structured digraphs that will turn out to be useful for embedding grounded trees. Before being able to define a
(k,d)
-broom digraph (Definition
2.2
) we first need to define a special type of out-arborescences that we call
(k,d)
-brooms
. Recall that an
out-arborescence
is an oriented tree which has a designated root vertex such that all arcs of the tree are oriented away from the root. In the following we say that an out-arborescence is
balanced
if every root-to-leaf path has the same length. The
height
of an out-arborescence is the maximum length of a root-to-leaf path.

Definition 2.1

(Broom)
.

Let
k,d\in\mathbb{N}
. A
(k,d)
-broom
T
rooted at
r
is defined to be an out-arborescence with root
r
such that there exists an integer
1\leq\ell\leq k+1
for which one of the following holds.

•

\ell\leq k
:
T
is a balanced out-arborescence of height
\ell
where all the vertices besides the leaves have out-degree
d
;

•

\ell=k+1
:
T
is obtained from a balanced out-arborescence of height
k+1
where all vertices except the leaves have out-degree
d
by subdividing each out-arc of
r
an arbitrary number of times.

The
internal vertices
of a
(k,d)
-broom are the vertices which are neither the root nor a leaf.

Definition 2.2

(Broom digraph)
.

Let
D
be a digraph and let
R\subseteq V(D)
be non-empty. We say that
D
is a
(k,d)
-broom digraph
D
with root set
R
if there are subdigraphs
(T_{r})_{r\in R}
of
D
such that:

•

D=\bigcup_{r\in R}{T_{r}}
.

•

For each
r\in R
, we have that
T_{r}
is a
(k,d)
-broom with root
r
such that all leaves are in
R
and all internal vertices are in
V(D)\setminus R
.

•

For any distinct
r_{1},r_{2}\in R
, we have
V(T_{r_{1}})\cap V(T_{r_{2}})\subseteq R
(that is, distinct broom digraphs are internally disjoint).

Note that by this definition, every digraph
D
such that
d_{D}^{+}(v)=d
for every
v\in V(D)
is a
(k,d)
-broom digraph for any
k\geq 1
. This is because we can simply put
R:=V(D)
and define, for each
r\in R
, the tree
T_{r}
as the out-arborescence of height
1
consisting of the root
r
and all
d
out-arcs of
r
in
D
.

After introducing these central definitions, we proceed with several auxiliary results for the proof of Theorem
1.3
. We start with a simple observation that will be needed repeatedly later.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
D
be a
(k,d)
-broom digraph with root set
R
. Let
v\in V(D)
and suppose that there is a directed path of length at most
k
from
v
to
R
. Then
d_{D}^{+}(v)=d
.

Note that we may assume that
v\in V(D)\setminus R
as every vertex in
R
has out-degree
d
. Let
P
be a shortest directed path from
v
to
R
. The internal vertices of
P
are in
V(D)\setminus R
as otherwise it is not the shortest directed path from
v
to
R
. Let
r\in R
be the root of the unique
(k,d)
-broom
T_{r}
containing
v
. Then, there exists a path
P^{\prime}
in
T_{r}
from
r
to
v
. Note that
P
is in
T_{r}
as well, since it does not have any internal vertices in
R
. Then,
P^{\prime}\cup P
is a directed path in
T_{r}
from
r
through
v
to a leaf of
T_{r}
.
As
D
is a
(k,d)
-broom digraph,
T_{r}
is a
(k,d)
-broom. Therefore, any vertex on
P^{\prime}\cup P
with distance at most
k
from the endpoint in
P
has out-degree
d
in
T_{r}
.
In particular, since
P
has length at most
k
and
v\in V(P)
, this implies that
d_{D}^{+}(v)=d
, as desired.
∎Note that in order to later embed grounded trees into a given
(k,d)
-broom digraph, it will be necessary to find vertices of high in-degree (since those may exist in the grounded tree). Motivated by this, our next goal will be to establish an auxiliary statement (namely Lemma
2.6
below) which shows that from any
(k,d)
-broom digraph
D
with root set
R
we can move to a subdigraph which is a
(k,d^{\prime})
-broom digraph for a somewhat smaller but still large value of
d^{\prime}
, such that all vertices in its new root set
R^{\prime}\subseteq R
have high in-degree in
D
. As one ingredient in the proof of this important lemma, we use the classic Lovász local lemma in the following form.

Lemma 2.4

(Lovász Local Lemma)
.

Let
A_{1},\ldots,A_{m}
be a sequence of events such that each event occurs with probability at most
p
and is independent of all the other events except at most
d
of them. If
epd\leq 1
then the event
\bigcap_{i=1}^{m}\overline{A_{i}}
occurs with positive probability.

As further preparation before the proof of Lemma
2.6
, we need the following statement about pruning an out-arborescence to obtain a broom.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
k,d\in\mathbb{N}
and let
T
be an out-arborescence with root
r
such that
d_{T}^{+}(r)\geq d
. Suppose that on every directed path in
T
with vertex sequence
r=v_{1},\ldots,v_{h}
from
r
to a leaf
v_{h}
, it holds that
d_{T}^{+}(v_{i})\geq d
, for every index
i
satisfying
\max\{1,h-k+1\}\leq i\leq h-1
. Then,
T
contains a
(k-1,\lceil d/k\rceil)
-broom rooted at
r
whose leaves are leaves of
T
.

Consider a function
\phi:V(T)\to\{0,\ldots,k-1\}
defined as follows. Let
(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}=r)
be a linear ordering of
V(T)
such that
u_{i}
is a leaf of in-degree
1
in
T[u_{i},\ldots,u_{n}]
for every
i\in[n-1]
. We now iteratively define
\phi(u_{i})
for
i=1,\ldots,n
as follows: If
u_{i}
is a leaf of
T
then we define
\phi(u_{i}):=0
. Otherwise, we define
\phi(u_{i}):=\min\{\varphi(u_{i})+1,k-1\}
where
\varphi(u_{i})\in\{0,\ldots,k-1\}
is defined as the smallest index such that there exist at least
\lceil d_{T}^{+}(u_{i})/k\rceil
out-neighbors
x
of
u_{i}
with
\phi(x)=\varphi(u_{i})
. This is well-defined, since by choice of the ordering
(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})
,
\phi
has already been defined on
N_{T}^{+}(u_{i})
when processing
u_{i}
. Let
H\subseteq T
be the subdigraph defined by keeping only the arcs of the form
(u,v)\in V(T)
such that
\phi(v)=\varphi(u)
. Note that by definition of
\phi
, we then have
d_{H}^{+}(u)\geq\lceil d_{T}^{+}(u)/k\rceil
for every
u\in V(H)
.

Let
T^{\prime}\subseteq T
be the out-arborescence rooted at
r
that forms the unique connected component of
H
containing
r
. We then have
d_{T^{\prime}}^{+}(u)=d_{H}^{+}(u)\geq d_{T}^{+}(u)/k
for every
u\in V(T^{\prime})
. In particular,
u
is a leaf of
T^{\prime}
only if it was also a leaf of
T
. Therefore, a directed path
v_{1},\ldots,v_{h}
on
T^{\prime}
from
r=v_{1}
to a leaf
v_{h}
is also a root-to-leaf path in
T
and hence it still holds that
d_{T}^{+}(v_{i})\geq d
and thus,
d_{T^{\prime}}^{+}(v_{i})\geq\lceil d/k\rceil
, for all indices
i
such that
\max\{1,h-k+1\}\leq i\leq h-1
.

For each
u\in V(T^{\prime})
, denote by
T_{u}\subseteq T^{\prime}
the subtree of
T^{\prime}
rooted at
u
(which also forms an out-arborescence). Suppose for a moment that
\varphi(u)\leq k-2
. Then,
\phi(u)=\min\{\varphi(u)+1,k-1\}=\varphi(u)+1
. By the definition of
\phi
and the choice of
T^{\prime}
, we get that every path from
u
to a leaf in
T_{u}
has length
\phi(u)
(since the value of
\phi
decreases by one at each step along the path). Next, let us consider an arbitrary vertex
w\in V(T_{u})
which is not a leaf. Then, there exists a directed path
P
in
T^{\prime}
from
r
through
w
to a leaf. Note that
P
contains
u
and, thus, also a sub-path from
u
through
w
to a leaf. Therefore,
w
is among the last
\phi(u)+1\leq k
vertices on
P
. By our observation above, this implies that
d_{T^{\prime}}^{+}(w)\geq\lceil d/k\rceil
. Summarizing, we have shown that every non-leaf
w\in V(T_{u})
has out-degree at least
\lceil d/k\rceil
and that
T_{u}
is balanced with height
\phi(u)
. By pruning this tree, it follows that for every
u\in V(T^{\prime})
with
\varphi(u)\leq k-2
there exists
T_{u}^{\prime}\subseteq T_{u}
which is a balanced out-arborescence of height
\phi(u)
where all vertices which are not leaves have out-degree exactly
\lceil d/k\rceil
, and such that all leaves of
T_{u}^{\prime}
are also leaves of
T
.

Proceeding with the main argument, let us first consider the case that
\varphi(r)\leq k-2
. Then,
T_{r}^{\prime}
is a
(k-1,\lceil d/k\rceil)
-broom of height
\phi(r)
and we are done. Next, consider the remaining case that
\varphi(r)=k-1
. Note that
d_{T^{\prime}}^{+}(r)\geq d_{T}^{+}(r)/k\geq d/k
by our assumption on
r
in the lemma. Let
P_{1},\ldots,P_{\lceil d/k\rceil}
be a collection of directed paths in
T^{\prime}
from
r
to a leaf which are vertex-disjoint besides
r
. For
1\leq i\leq\lceil d/k\rceil
, let
u_{i}
be the last vertex on
P_{i}
with
\phi(u_{i})=k-1
. Note that this is well-defined, since
\phi
changes by at most
1
when moving along any arc of
T^{\prime}
, and since
\phi(r)=k-1
and
\phi(x)=0
for every leaf
x
of
T^{\prime}
. Let
P_{i}^{\prime}\subseteq P_{i}
denote the sub-path from
r
to
u_{i}
. Note that the choice of
u_{i}
implies that
\varphi(u_{i})=k-2
, and hence
u_{i}\neq r
for every
i
.
Finally, consider the out-arborescence
T^{\prime\prime}
obtained as the union of
P_{i}^{\prime}
as well as
T_{u_{i}}^{\prime}
for
1\leq i\leq\lceil d/k\rceil
. One easily checks that this is now a
(k-1,\lceil d/k\rceil)
-broom, and all its leaves are also leaves of
T^{\prime}
. This concludes the proof.
∎With Lemma
2.4
and Lemma
2.5
at hand, we are now ready to formulate and prove the aforementioned result about finding a sub-broom digraph with root-set of high in-degree.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
d,k\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
d\geq\max\{(4k)^{6},5\cdot 10^{12}\}
and let
D
be a
(k,d)
-broom digraph with root set
R
. Then, there exists a
(k,\lfloor d^{1/(7k)}\rfloor)
-broom digraph
D^{\prime}\subseteq D
with root set
R^{\prime}\subseteq R
such that
d^{-}_{D}(r)\geq d^{1/10}
for all
r\in R^{\prime}
.

Let
U:=\{u\in V(D)|d_{D}^{+}(u)=d\}
and let
W:=\{w\in R|d_{D}^{-}(w)<d^{1/10}\}
. Note that by definition of a
(k,d)
-broom, we have
R\subseteq U
. For each arc
(u,v)\in A(D)
with
u\in U
, let
X_{(u,v)}
be a Bernoulli random variable that takes value
1
with probability
d^{-2/3}
. For arcs
(u,v)
with
u\notin U
, we set
X_{(u,v)}=1
. Note that by definition of a
(k,d)
-broom digraph, this does not include any arc ending in a vertex in
R
. In particular, for every arc
e
ending in
W\subseteq R
we have that
X_{e}=1
with probability
d^{-2/3}
. Let
H\subseteq D
denote the random subdigraph obtained by keeping only the arcs
e
for which
X_{e}=1
.

For each
u\in U
, let
A_{u}
be the random event that
d^{+}_{H}(u)\leq d^{1/3}/2
. A simple application of Chernoff’s inequality shows that
\mathbb{P}[A_{u}]\leq\exp(-d^{1/3}/8)
. For each
w\in W
, let
B_{w}
be the random event that
d^{-}_{H}(w)\geq 2
. By a union bound over all potential choices of two incoming edges, we obtain
\mathbb{P}[B_{w}]\leq\binom{d^{1/10}}{2}d^{-4/3}\leq d^{-17/15}
. Furthermore, the events
A_{u}
depend on at most
d
events of the form
B_{w}
and are independent of all other events. The events
B_{w}
depend on at most
d^{1/10}\leq d
events of the form
A_{u}
and are independent of all other events. Using our assumption on
d
, one now easily checks that
epd\leq 1
for
p:=\max\{\exp(-d^{1/3}/8),d^{-17/15}\}
and hence we can apply Lemma
2.4
, which implies that with positive probability none of the events
A_{u},u\in U
or
B_{w},w\in W
occur.

In the following, with a slight abuse of notation, we use
H
to denote an
instance
of the random subdigraph
H
for which none of the mentioned events occur, that is,
d_{H}^{+}(u)>d^{1/3}/2
for all
u\in U
and
d_{H}^{-}(w)\leq 1
for all
w\in W
. Also note, that by definition of
H
, we have
d_{H}^{+}(x)=d_{D}^{+}(x)\geq 1
for every
x\notin U
, and hence
\delta^{+}(H)\geq 1
.

Let
R^{\prime}
denote the set of vertices with in-degree at least
2
in
H
and note that
R^{\prime}

For each

be the digraph obtained from

is an out-arborescence with root

We claim that each leaf

must have (in

is a leaf.

Next, consider any vertex

. Then, we either have

and satisfies

|N_{H}^{+}(u)\cap(R^{\prime}\setminus\{r\})|=d_{H}^{+}(u)-d_{H-(R^{\prime}\setminus\{r\})}^{+}(u)=d_{H}^{+}(u)-d_{H[U_{r}]}^{+}(u)\geq d_{H}^{+}(u)-d_{D_{r}}^{+}(u)-1\geq\frac{d_{H}^{+}(u)}{2}-1,

or we have

, in which case, by definition of

and

d_{S_{r}}^{+}(u)=d_{D_{r}}^{+}(u)>\frac{d_{H}^{+}(u)}{2}
.

Recall that
D
is a
(k,d)
-broom digraph and
S_{r}\subseteq D
. Let

be the vertex-sequence of any directed path

from the root

to a leaf

in

has an out-neighbor in

, it follows that, for

,

) of length at most

, it follows that

. Furthermore, since

.

Observe that by the freedom of choice of

.

Let

.
As we have shown that every vertex in

in

. One can easily check that our assumption on

.

Note that it follows, by definition of a

, we may extend

Note that the root and leaves of

-broom digraph with root set

∎

After having proven the first key auxiliary statement, let us now turn to the second one. The second main idea of the proof of Theorem
1.3
will be to “clean” a given
(k,d)
-broom digraph in such a way that we can predict the positions of intersection points with the root set for any directed path up to a certain length, just based on knowing its starting point. This will later enable us to control path lengths when attempting to embed a grounded tree into a
(k,d)
-broom digraph. We start by formally fixing the properties we need in the next definition.

Definition 2.7

(Typed)
.

Let
D
be a
(k,d)
-broom digraph with root set
R
. Let further
\mathbf{a}=(\mathbf{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{t})\in\{0,1\}^{t}
be a binary word. We say that a vertex
v\in V(D)

has
t
-type
\mathbf{a}
if for every
i=1,\ldots,t
the following holds: For every vertex
u
in
D
that can be reached from
v
via a directed walk in
D
of length exactly
i
, we have that
u\in R
if and only if
\mathbf{a}_{i}=1
. We further say that
D
is
t
-typed
if for every
v\in V(D)
there exists some
\mathbf{a}\in\{0,1\}^{t}
such that
v
has
t
-type
\mathbf{a}
.
Note that, by this definition, every
(k,d)
-broom digraph is (vacuously)

-typed.

The next statement is a simple preparatory Ramsey-type result that allows us to pass from any given out-arborescence with a coloring of its leaves to a sub-arborescence whose leaf-set is monochromatic and which preserves a good fraction of the out-degrees.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
T
be an out-arborescence with root
r
equipped with a coloring of its leaves with up to
C
colors. Then, there exists an out-arborescence
T^{\prime}\subseteq T
rooted at
r
such that the leaves of
T^{\prime}
are a monochromatic subset of the leaves of
T
and for all
u\in V(T^{\prime})
, it holds that
d_{T^{\prime}}^{+}(u)\geq d_{T}^{+}(u)/C
.

We perform induction on the height
\ell\geq 0
of
T
. The statement is trivially true for
\ell=0
. Suppose then that
\ell\geq 1
and we have already shown the statement for out-arborescences of height up to
\ell-1
.

Let
L\subseteq V(T)
denote the set of vertices at distance
\ell-1
from the root
r
which are not leaves of
T
. Note that
L\neq\emptyset
, for otherwise
T
would have height less than
\ell
. By the pigeon-hole principle, each vertex
u\in L
has at least
d^{+}_{T}(u)/C
children in some color
c
. For every
u\in L
, let us pick any such color
c
and let us color
u
with
c
.

Let
F\subseteq T
be the out-arborescence of height
\ell-1
obtained by removing all leaves of
T
at height
\ell
. Note that the leaf-set of
F
is given by the union of
L
as well as the set of leaves of
T
at height at most
\ell-1
. Note that then, by our above color-assignment to the vertices in
L
, each of the leaves of
F
is colored with one of
C
colors. Therefore, we can apply induction to
F
equipped with this coloring, obtaining a nonempty out-arborescence
F^{\prime}\subseteq F
with root
r
, such that the leaves of
F^{\prime}
form a monochromatic subset of the leaves of
F
, say, they all have color
c
, and such that
d^{+}_{F^{\prime}}(u)\geq d^{+}_{F}(u)/C
for every
u\in V(F^{\prime})
. Finally, for each
u\in L\cap V(F^{\prime})
, by the above definition of the colors assigned to
L
, we have that there exist at least
d^{+}_{T}(u)/C
children of
u
in
T
which were assigned color
c
by the original coloring of the leaves of
T
. By adding the edges from
u
to these children of color
c
back to
F^{\prime}
, for every

, we now obtain an out-arborescence
T^{\prime}\subseteq T
of height
\ell
such that all its leaves are

-colored leaves of
T
. Furthermore, we have
d_{T^{\prime}}^{+}(u)\geq d_{T}^{+}(u)/C
for every

by definition of

, showing that

satisfies the induction hypothesis. This concludes the proof.
∎Lemma
2.8
directly implies the following.

Corollary 2.9
.

Let

be a
(k,d)
-broom rooted at

-broom

are a monochromatic subset of the leaves of

Using Corollary

-broom digraph to another broom subdigraph of slightly lower degree, but which is typed.

Let

We prove the statement by induction on

3
Embedding the tree

In this section, we use our preparations from the previous section to finally prove Theorem
1.3
. We start by setting up more notation and terminology. In the following, given any grounded tree
T
, we will use the notation
G(T):=\{x\in V(T)|d_{T}^{-}(x)\geq 2\}
for the set of vertices of in-degree at least two and
Z(T):=\{x\in V(T)|d_{T}^{-}(x)=0\}
for the set of sources. A useful notion we will consider in the following is that of
max-grounded trees
. We say that a grounded tree
T
is
max-grounded
if for some (or, equivalently, every) height function
h:V(T)\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
for
T
we have that
h(x)\geq h(y)
for every
x\in G(T)
and
y\in V(T)
, in other words, if the vertices in
G(T)
are of maximum
h
-value. The reason for considering max-grounded trees is twofold: First, as we shall see next (Observation
3.1
below), to prove Theorem
1.3
it is sufficient to prove it for all max-grounded trees. Second, knowing that the tree at consideration is max-grounded comes with some convenient properties which will enable us to embed it into any
(k,d)
-broom digraph for sufficiently large
d
and hence into all digraphs of sufficiently large minimum out-degree (Lemma
3.2
below). The combination of these two insights will then prove Theorem
1.3
.

Observation 3.1
.

If every max-grounded tree is
\delta^{+}
-enforcible, then so is every grounded tree, i.e., Theorem
1.3
reduces to proving it for all max-grounded trees.

Suppose towards a contradiction that every max-grounded tree is
\delta^{+}
-enforcible, and yet there exists some grounded tree which is not
\delta^{+}
-enforcible. Let
T
be a grounded tree of minimum order which is not
\delta^{+}
-enforcible. Let
h
be a height function for
T
. Let
v
be a vertex in
T
maximizing
h(v)
. Since
T
is not max-grounded, we must have
h(v)>h(x)
for some (and hence, every)
x\in G(T)
. Note that, by definition of height functions and by choice of
v
, we must have
d_{T}^{+}(v)=0
. Since also
v\notin G(T)
, we must have
d_{T}^{-}(v)=1
, that is,
v
is a leaf of
T
of indegree
1
. By minimality of the order of
T
, we know that the grounded tree
T-v
must be
\delta^{+}
-enforcible. Hence, there exists some constant
d(T-v)\in\mathbb{N}
such that all digraphs of minimum out-degree at least
d(T-v)
contain a copy of
T-v
as a subdigraph. But then it is easy to see that every digraph of minimum out-degree at least
d(T):=\max\{d(T-v),|V(T)|\}
contains a copy of
T
: We take a copy of
T-v
contained in the digraph and augment it with an additional out-edge from the vertex representing the unique neighbor of
v
in
T
to a copy of
T
. Hence,
T
is
\delta^{+}
-enforcible, a contradiction to our initial assumption on
T
. This concludes the proof.
∎To state the next lemma, which is the final step of the proof of Theorem
1.3
, we show that all max-grounded trees can be embedded into
(k,d)
-brooms in a particular way. To make this precise, we use the following definition: Let
D
be a
(k,d)
-broom digraph with root set
R
, and let
T
be a max-grounded tree with height function
h:V(T)\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
such that
\max_{x\in V(T)}{h(x)}=0
. For each vertex
u\in V(D)
, let us denote by
P_{D}(u)
the unique shortest path in
D
from
R
to
u
(in other words, this is the root-to-leaf path of the unique
(k,d)
-broom in
D
containing
u
as a root or internal vertex). With this setup, we may finally define a
proper copy of
T
in
D
as any copy
F\subseteq D
of
T
such that:

•

every vertex
x\in V(T)
with
h(x)=0
corresponds to a vertex of
F
that is contained in the root set
R
of
D
, and

•

The paths
(P_{D}(x))_{x\in Z(F)}
are pairwise vertex-disjoint and disjoint from
V(F)\setminus Z(F)
.

Having put this important notion in place, we can now state the lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
T
be a max-grounded tree, and let
k\geq|V(T)|
and
d\geq 10^{13k^{3}\cdot(8k)^{|V(T)|}}
be integers. Then every
(k,d)
-broom digraph contains a proper copy of
T
.

Towards a contradiction suppose the statement of the lemma is false, and let
T
be a max-grounded tree of minimum order for which it fails. It follows that there exist integers
k\geq|V(T)|
and
d\geq 10^{13k^{3}\cdot(8k)^{|V(T)|}}
as well as a
(k,d)
-broom digraph
D
such that
D
contains no proper copy of
T
. Then, since every
(k,d)
-broom digraph trivially contains a proper copy of the single-vertex tree, we must have
|V(T)|\geq 2
. Let

be the unique height function of

such that
\max_{x\in V(T)}{h(x)}=0
. Then, by definition of a max-grounded tree, we have

for every

. Pick a leaf

of

minimizing

. The choice of

is non-empty. Let

from

to a vertex in

such that the two segments

between

and

and

between

and

of

(note that one of these two paths could be of length

). Observe that

is at most as long as

. In the following let us denote

is a max-grounded tree smaller than

Let

as well as

.

Since

and

with parameters

meet the conditions of the lemma, and since the lemma holds for

, we now find that there exists a proper copy

of

in

. Let

onto

. Note that by definition of a proper copy, and since

, we have

for every

with

and the paths
(P_{D^{\prime}}(x))_{x\in Z(F^{\prime})}

.

We divide the remainder of the proof into three cases based on the way that

. In each case, we show that

. Let

is the unique in-neighbor of
u
in
T
. Hence,
u\in Z(T^{\prime})
and
Z(T)=(Z(T^{\prime})\setminus\{u\})\cup\{l\}
. As
\iota(u)\notin R^{\prime}
, we get that
P_{D^{\prime}}(\iota(u))
is of length at least
1
. Let
z
be the unique in-neighbor of
\iota(u)
on
P_{D^{\prime}}(\iota(u))
. Since
F^{\prime}
is a proper copy, we have
z\notin V(F^{\prime})
. Let
F\supseteq F^{\prime}
be the copy of
T
in
D
obtained by embedding
l\in V(T)
on
z\in V(D^{\prime})\subseteq V(D)
. We claim that
F
is a proper copy of
T
in
D
. The first condition of a proper embedding is satisfied since
h(l)\leq h(u)-1\leq-1
and since
F^{\prime}
was assumed to be a proper copy of
T^{\prime}
. For the second condition, it remains to verify that the paths
(P_{D}(x))_{x\in Z(F)}
are pairwise vertex-disjoint and disjoint from
V(F)\setminus Z(F)
. However, this easily follows since
Z(F)=(Z(F^{\prime})\setminus\{\iota(u)\})\cup\{z\}
,
P_{D^{\prime}}(z)\subseteq P_{D^{\prime}}(\iota(u))
by definition and
P_{D}(x)\subseteq P_{D^{\prime}}(x)
for every
x\in V(D)
because
R^{\prime}\subseteq R
.

Case 2.

l
is an in-neighbor of
u
and
\iota(u)\in R^{\prime}
.

It follows that
d^{-}_{D}(\iota(u))\geq d^{1/10}
. Note that by definition of a broom digraph, we have that
\iota(u)
has at most one in-neighbor in each of the
(k,d)
-brooms whose union forms
D
. As
d^{1/10}\geq k>|V(F)|-1
by our assumptions on
k
and
d
, this implies that we can pick an in-neighbor
z\in N^{-}_{D}(\iota(u))
such that
P_{D}(z)
is disjoint from
P_{D}(x)
, for all
x\in V(F^{\prime})
. We may now extend
F^{\prime}
to a copy
F
of
T
in
D
by embedding
l
on
z
. Again, it is not hard to see that this is a proper embedding: As above we have
h(l)\leq h(u)-1\leq-1
, so that the first condition of properness is satisfied since
F^{\prime}
is a proper copy of
T^{\prime}
in
D^{\prime}
. The second condition follows since
Z(F)=(Z(F^{\prime})\setminus\{\iota(u)\})\cup\{z\}
, the paths
P_{D}(x)\subseteq P_{D^{\prime}}(x)
for
x\in Z(F^{\prime})\setminus\{\iota(u)\}
are pairwise disjoint from each other and from
(V(F^{\prime})\setminus Z(F^{\prime}))\cup\{\iota(u)\}\supseteq V(F)\setminus Z(F)
and since by our choice of
z
we have that
P_{D}(z)
is disjoint from all the above paths as well as from
V(F^{\prime})=V(F)\setminus\{z\}
.

Case 3.

l
is an out-neighbor of
u
.

Let
\ell_{1},\ell_{2}
denote the lengths of the paths
P_{1},P_{2}
, respectively, and note/recall that
1\leq\ell_{2}\leq\ell_{1}<|V(T)|\leq k
. Let
Q_{1}:=\iota(P_{1})
be the image of
P_{1}
in the copy
F^{\prime}
of
T
. Since
F^{\prime}
is a proper copy of
T^{\prime}
in
D^{\prime}
, the endpoint of
Q_{1}
, namely
\iota(v)
, must be contained in
R^{\prime}
(this is because
v\in B
and hence
h(v)=0
by our choice of
v
). Hence,
Q_{1}
is a directed path of length
\ell_{1}\in[k]
from
\iota(s)
to a vertex in
R^{\prime}
. Since
D^{\prime}
is
k
-typed, it follows that
every
directed walk in
D^{\prime}
of length
\ell_{1}
starting in
\iota(s)
must end in
R^{\prime}
. Let
Q_{2}:=\iota(P_{2}-l)
denote the image of
P_{2}-l
in
F^{\prime}
with endpoints
\iota(s)
and
\iota(u)
. We next claim that there exists a directed path of length at most
k
in
D^{\prime}
from
\iota(u)
to
R^{\prime}
. To see this, let
Q
be a shortest directed path in
D
from
\iota(u)
to
R^{\prime}
(note that such a path exists by definition of a broom digraph). Towards a contradiction, suppose that
|A(Q)|>k
and consider the directed walk
W
in
D^{\prime}
starting in
\iota(s)
which is obtained by concatenating
Q_{2}
and
Q
. By assumption, the length of
W
is at least
|A(Q)|>k
. Then by the above we must have that the
(\ell_{1}+1)
-st vertex we meet when traversing
W
starting from
\iota(s)
must be contained in
R^{\prime}
. Since further the initial segment
Q_{2}
of
W
has length
|A(Q_{2})|=\ell_{2}-1<\ell_{1}
, it follows that
x
must be a vertex of
Q
distinct from its endpoint. This, however, is a contradiction, since
x\in R^{\prime}
and
Q
was a shortest directed path from
\iota(u)
to
R^{\prime}
. This shows that our above assumption was wrong, there indeed exists a directed path in
D^{\prime}
of length at most
k
from
\iota(u)
to
R^{\prime}
.

Hence, we may apply Lemma
2.3
to the vertex
\iota(u)
in
D^{\prime}
, which yields that
d_{D^{\prime}}^{+}(\iota(u))=d^{\prime}\gg k
. Observe that by definition of a
(k,d)
-broom digraph, for each
x\in V(F^{\prime})
, at most one out-neighbor of
\iota(u)
in
D^{\prime}
is contained in
P_{D^{\prime}}(x)
. Thus, we may choose a vertex
z\in N^{+}_{D^{\prime}}(\iota(u))
which does not appear on any of the paths
P_{D^{\prime}}(x)
,
x\in V(F^{\prime})
. Now let
F
be the copy of
T
in
D^{\prime}\subseteq D
obtained by embedding
l
at
z
. We claim that this is a proper copy of
F
in
D
: Indeed, we have
Z(F)=Z(F^{\prime})
and hence the paths
P_{D}(x)\subseteq P_{D^{\prime}}(x)
for
x\in Z(F)
are pairwise disjoint and disjoint from
V(F)\setminus Z(F)
, since
F^{\prime}
is a proper copy of
T^{\prime}
in
D^{\prime}
, and by our choice of
z
. It thus remains to verify that every vertex
x\in V(T)
with
h(x)=0
is embedded into
R
. Since
F^{\prime}
is a proper copy of
T^{\prime}
in
D^{\prime}
and since
R^{\prime}\subseteq R
, this is indeed the case for every
x\in V(T^{\prime})=V(T)\setminus\{l\}
. It then remains to verify that
h(l)=0
implies that
z\in R^{\prime}
. Note that by definition of a height function and since
P_{1},P_{2}
are directed paths starting in
s
in
T
, we have that
h(l)=h(v)-\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}=\ell_{2}-\ell_{1}
. Hence,
h(l)=0
implies that
\ell_{1}=\ell_{2}
. Recall that we established above that every directed path in
D^{\prime}
starting in
\iota(s)
of length
\ell_{1}
ends in
R^{\prime}\subseteq R
. In particular, this is the case for the path
Q_{2}\cup\{(\iota(u),z)\}
in
D^{\prime}
, which is of length
|A(Q_{2})|+1=(\ell_{2}-1)+1=\ell_{2}=\ell_{1}
. Hence, we indeed have
z\in R
if
h(l)=0
, as desired. This shows that
F
is indeed a proper copy of
T
in
D
, as desired.

As we found the desired contradiction in each of the three cases, this means that our initial assumption on the existence of
T
was correct. This shows that the statement of the lemma holds, concluding the proof.
∎

Finally, we can easily complete the proof of our main result, Theorem
1.3
by combining Observation
3.1
and Lemma
3.2
.

By Observation
3.1
, it suffices to show that every max-grounded tree is
\delta^{+}
-enforcible. So let
T
be any given max-grounded tree, let
k:=|V(T)|
and define
d=d(T):=10^{13k^{3}(8k)^{k}}
. Let
D
be any digraph satisfying
\delta^{+}(D)\geq d
. By keeping exactly
d
out-arcs at each vertex, we may w.l.o.g. assume
d_{D}^{+}(v)=d
for each
v\in V(D)
. By the comment after Definition
2.2
we have that
D
is a
(k,d)
-broom digraph. Additionally, one can observe that
D
meets the conditions of Lemma
3.2
. Hence, by the lemma there exists a (proper) copy of
T
in
D
. Since
D
was chosen arbitrarily, this by definition proves that
T
is
\delta^{+}
-enforcible, as desired. This concludes the proof of the theorem.
∎

4
Concluding remarks

Let
d_{k}
denote the minimum
d
such that every digraph with minimum out-degree at least
d
contains every grounded tree on
k
vertices. Our result shows that
d_{k}
exists and
d_{k}\leq 2^{(Ck)^{k}}
for some large enough constant
C
. It would be interesting to understand the dependence of
d_{k}
on
k
better. For example, is it polynomial, exponential or double exponential?
