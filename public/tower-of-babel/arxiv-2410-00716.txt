.
By the binomial theorem, we have

\left(\frac{k-1}{k}+O\left(\frac{1}{k^{2}}\right)\right)^{r}=\left(\frac{k-1}{k}\right)^{r}+O\left(\frac{1}{k^{2}}\right).

Another application of the binomial theorem gives

\displaystyle\left(\frac{k-1}{k}\right)^{r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{r}\binom{r}{m}(-1)^{m}k^{-m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\frac{r}{k}+\frac{\binom{r}{2}}{k^{2}}-\cdots+\frac{(-1)^{r}}{k^{r}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{k-r}{k}+O\left(\frac{1}{k^{2}}\right).

Thus
{\mathcal{P}}(\Gamma^{\prime})=\frac{k-r}{k}+O(\frac{1}{k^{2}})
as
k\to\infty
, and the result follows.

\blacksquare

Suppose
\Phi_{k}(x)
is separable with
r
irreducible factors. Then Gal
(\Phi_{k}(x)/\mathbb{Q})

is a subgroup of
\mathbb{Z}/k\mathbb{Z}\text{ wr }S_{n}
with
r
orbits, so it satisfies (
7
).
Then by the Chebotarev density theorem, the proportion of primes
p
such
that
f(x)\pmod{p}
has a
k
-cycle is bounded above by
r/k
. This proves
Theorem
1.2
.

4
Polynomials with Irreducible
\Phi_{k}
for
Infinitely Many
k

In the previous section we proved that whenever
\Phi_{k}(x)
is irreducible,
there is a bound on the density of primes
p
such that
f(x)\pmod{p}

has a
k
-cycle. Namely, we showed that

\mathcal{Q}(\Gamma)\leq\frac{1}{k},

where
\Gamma=\text{Gal}(\Phi_{k}(x)/\mathbb{Q})

and
\mathcal{Q}(\Gamma)
denotes the proportion of non-derangements in
\Gamma
.

In this section we prove Theorem
1.1
. To do so, we begin with a lemma from

[
9
]
, whose proof will be useful for our proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Lemma 4.1
.

If
q=p^{s}
for some prime
p
and
f(x)=x^{q}+x
, then
\Phi_{k}(x)
is
Eisenstein over
\mathbb{Q}
whenever
k
is a power of
p
.

Proof

Fix the prime
p
in
f(x)=x^{q}+x
; we show that
\Phi_{k}(x)
is
Eisenstein with respect to
p
. The operators
\pi(x):=x^{q}
and

1(x):=x
are commutative modulo
p
, so by the binomial
theorem

\displaystyle f^{n}(x)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle(\pi+1)^{n}(x)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{n}\binom{n}{m}\pi^{m}(x)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{n}\binom{n}{m}x^{q^{m}}\pmod{p}

Thus if
n=p^{s}
, we have

f^{n}(x)\equiv x^{q^{p(s)}}+x\pmod{p}

(8)

where
p(s):=p^{s}
, so

\Phi_{n}(x)=\frac{f^{p^{s}}(x)-x}{f^{p^{s-1}}(x)-x}\equiv x^{q^{p(s)}-q^{p(s-1)}}\pmod{p}.

(9)

It remains to show that
p
exactly divides
\Phi_{n}(0)
. To this end, we prove the congruence

f^{n}(x)\equiv nx^{q}+x\pmod{x^{q+1}}.

This is true for
n=1
, so assume it holds for
n=k
. Write the congruence as

f^{k}(x)=kx^{q}+x+x^{q+1}g(x)

for some
g(x)
in
\mathbb{Z}[x]
. Then
f^{k+1}(x)=k(x^{q}+x)^{q}+x^{q}+x+f(x)^{q+1}g(f(x))\equiv(k+1)x^{q}+x\pmod{x^{q+1}}
, and the result follows by induction. We now have

\Phi_{p^{s}}(x)=\frac{p^{s}x^{q}+x^{q+1}g_{1}(x)}{p^{s-1}x^{q}+x^{q+1}g_{2}(x)}=\frac{p^{s}+xg_{1}(x)}{p^{s-1}+xg_{2}(x)}.

Hence
\Phi_{p^{s}}(0)=p
, and the theorem is proved.

\blacksquare

We now extend this result to an infinite family of polynomials, which contains

f(x)=x^{q}+x
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Fix a prime
p
and let
q
be a power of
p
.
For every polynomial in the infinite family
{\mathcal{F}}:=\{f(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]:f(x)\pmod{p}=x^{q}+x\}

the corresponding polynomial
\Phi_{k}(x)
is Eisenstein over
\mathbb{Q}
whenever
k=p^{s}
,

s\geq 2
.

Proof

By Lemma
4.1
,
\Phi_{p^{s}}(x)\equiv x^{q^{p(s)}-q^{p(s-1)}}\pmod{p}
, so we need
only show that
p
exactly divides
\Phi_{p^{s}}(x)
for every
s\geq 2
. We show that if
p^{r}

exactly divides
f^{p^{s}}(0)
, then
p^{r+1}
exactly divides
f^{p^{s+1}}(0)
, which suffices
since
\Phi_{p^{s}}(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
,

\Phi_{p^{s}}(0)=\frac{f^{p^{s}}(0)}{f^{p^{s-1}}(0)},

and
p\mid f^{p}(0)
.

Let
p^{r}
be the
largest power of
p
that divides
f^{p^{s-1}}(0)
, and write
f^{p^{s-1}}(0)=p^{r}C_{1}

for some integer
C_{1}
such that
p\nmid C_{1}
.
In the Taylor expansion

f^{(p-1)\cdot p^{s-1}}(x)=\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\frac{(f^{(p-1)\cdot p^{s-1}})^{(\ell)}(0)}{\ell!}x^{\ell},

we set
x=f^{p^{s-1}}(0)
, giving

\displaystyle f^{p^{s}}(0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\frac{(f^{(p-1)p^{s-1}})^{(\ell)}(0)}{\ell!}(f^{p^{s-1}}(0))^{\ell}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f^{(p-1)p^{s-1}}(0)+(f^{(p-1)p^{s-1}})^{\prime}(0)f^{p^{s-1}}(0)+\frac{1}{2}(f^{(p-1)p^{s-1}})^{\prime\prime}(0)(f^{p^{s-1}}(0))^{2}+\cdots.

Since
f
is a polynomial, one sees that
\ell!\mid(f^{n})^{(\ell)}(0)
. By hypothesis,
the polynomial
f(x)
takes the form

f(x)=x^{q}+a_{q-1}x^{q-1}+\cdots+a_{1}x+a_{0}

where
a_{i}\equiv 0\pmod{p}
for every
i\neq 1
, while

a_{1}\equiv 1\pmod{p}
. Let
a_{1}=1+pa
and
a_{0}=pb
.
By induction on
i
, one easily obtains the congruence

f^{i}(0)\equiv pb(pa+i)\pmod{p^{2}}
. Thus with
i=p
we see that

f^{p}(0)
is at least divisible by
p^{2}
. It follows that
all terms following the first two summands are divisible by at least
p^{2r}\geq p^{r+2}
.

We now analyze the first two summands in the Taylor expansion, starting with the
second. Our first claim is
that the derivative
(f^{jp^{s-1}})^{\prime}(0)\equiv 1\pmod{p^{2}}
for every
1\leq j\leq p-1
.
We have

\displaystyle(f^{jp^{s-1}})^{\prime}(0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{i=0}^{jp^{s-1}-1}f^{\prime}(f^{i}(0))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{i=0}^{jp^{s-1}-1}(q(f^{i}(0))^{q-1}+\cdots+2a_{2}f^{i}(0)+a_{1})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle(a_{1})^{jp^{s-1}}\pmod{p^{2}},

where the last equivalence follows from the fact that
p\mid f^{i}(0)
for every
i\geq 0

and
p\mid a_{i}
for every
i\neq 1
.
But now note that
(a_{1})^{jp^{s-1}}=(a_{1}^{p^{s-1}})^{j}\equiv 1\pmod{p^{2}}
,
since if
x\equiv 1\pmod{p}
then
x^{p}\equiv 1\pmod{p^{2}}
. This fact can be seen by
writing
x=1+pn
and expanding
(1+pn)^{p}
, noting that
p\mid\binom{p}{s}
for

2\leq s\leq p-1
.

As for the first summand in the Taylor expansion, we prove that

f^{jp^{s-1}}(0)\equiv jp^{r}C_{1}\pmod{p^{r+2}}

for every
1\leq j\leq p-1
by induction on
j
. The result certainly holds
for
j=1
, therefore assume it holds for
j-1\leq p-1
. We have

\displaystyle f^{jp^{s-1}}(0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f^{(j-1)p^{s-1}}(f^{p^{s-1}}(0))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f^{(j-1)p^{s-1}}(0)+(f^{(j-1)p^{s-1}})^{\prime}(0)f^{p^{s-1}}(0)+\frac{1}{2}(f^{(j-1)p^{s-1}})^{\prime\prime}(0)(f^{p^{s-1}}(0))^{2}+\cdots

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(j-1)p^{r}C_{1}+p^{r+2}C_{2}+(p^{2}n+1)p^{r}C_{1}+p^{2r}C_{3}\qquad(C_{2},C_{3}\text{ nonnegative integers})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle jp^{r}C_{1}+p^{r+2}C_{2}+p^{r+2}nC_{1}+p^{2r}C_{3}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle jp^{r}C_{1}\pmod{p^{r+2}},

as was to be shown. Now put
j=p
in
the congruence above:
f^{p^{s}}(0)=p^{r+1}C_{1}+p^{r+2}C_{2}+p^{r+2}nC_{1}+p^{2r}C_{3}=p^{r+1}(C_{1}+pC_{2}+pnC_{1}+p^{r-1}C_{3})
, which is exactly divisible by
p^{r+1}

since
p\nmid C_{1}
and
r\geq 2
.

\blacksquare

It is interesting to note that this theorem fails for
s=1
. Indeed, take

f(x)=x^{2}+3x+2
with
p=3
. Then
\Phi_{3}(x)=x^{6}+10x^{5}+46x^{4}+122x^{3}+199x^{2}+192x+91
is actually reducible.
