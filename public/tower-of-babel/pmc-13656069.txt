Comparative Diagnostic Performance of Three Commercial Real‐Time PCR Assays for the Detection of Mycobacterium tuberculosis Complex in Bronchial Washing Specimens: A Large Retrospective Study
Authors: Jimyung Kim, Yumi Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundMolecular assays are essential for the rapid detection of Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTBC). However, head‐to‐head comparisons of widely used commercial assays remain limited. This study compared the diagnostic performance of three real‐time PCR assays—AdvanSure TB/NTM, Cobas MTB, and Xpert MTB/RIF—using bronchial washing specimens.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed molecular assay results from 3348 bronchial washing specimens obtained between January 2021 and May 2024. Among them, 740 specimens were included in a head‐to‐head comparison of the three assays. Diagnostic performance was evaluated using culture‐confirmed MTBC as the reference standard.ResultsIn the head‐to‐head comparison (n = 740), the overall concordance rate was 93.6%. Using culture‐confirmed MTBC as the reference standard, the sensitivities of the AdvanSure, Cobas, and Xpert assays were 49.1%, 96.2%, and 90.6%, respectively. In smear‐negative cases (n = 713), AdvanSure showed markedly lower sensitivity (30.8%) than the Cobas (94.9%) and Xpert (87.2%). AdvanSure showed the highest overall specificity (98.7%), followed by Cobas (96.9%) and Xpert (96.4%). The Xpert MTB/RIF assay demonstrated complete concordance with drug susceptibility testing for detecting rifampicin resistance, with a sensitivity of 100% (95% CI, 59.0%–100.0%) and a specificity of 100% (95% CI, 97.3%–100.0%).ConclusionIn this real‐world cohort with a high proportion of smear‐negative cases, the Cobas and Xpert assays exhibited significantly higher sensitivity for MTBC detection than the AdvanSure assay. These findings highlight the importance of highly sensitive molecular assays for the early diagnosis of pulmonary tuberculosis, particularly in patients with low bacillary burden.Keywords: AdvanSure TB/NTM, bronchial washing, Cobas MTB, Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex, Xpert MTB/RIF

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) is a major global public health concern. According to the World Health Organization's Global Tuberculosis Report 2025, 10.7 million individuals developed TB, and approximately 1.23 million died from the disease worldwide in 2024 [1]. TB remains the leading cause of death from a single infectious agent worldwide [1]. Therefore, early and accurate diagnosis is essential to reduce TB transmission and mortality.

Although acid‐fast bacilli (AFB) culture is regarded as the gold standard for TB diagnosis because of its high sensitivity [2], confirmation requires 6–8 weeks due to the slow growth of Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB), a member of the Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTBC) [3].

In addition, a recent nationwide study in South Korea reported that up to 40% of patients with pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB) were culture‐negative, underscoring the substantial diagnostic challenges encountered in routine clinical practice [4, 5].

Molecular diagnostic methods based on nucleic acid amplification tests, particularly real‐time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assays, have reduced diagnostic turnaround times while maintaining high sensitivity and specificity compared with conventional methods [6]. Although sputum is the most commonly used specimen for PTB diagnosis, its collection carries a risk of contamination and may yield suboptimal sensitivity, particularly in patients with a low bacillary burden [6, 7]. In contrast, high‐quality respiratory specimens obtained through bronchoscopy, such as bronchoalveolar lavage fluid or bronchial washing specimens, can improve diagnostic accuracy in patients with minimal disease or early‐stage PTB and facilitate a more rapid diagnosis [8, 9, 10]. Numerous studies have demonstrated the diagnostic value of bronchoscopic specimens, particularly in AFB smear‐negative patients with PTB [7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13].

Among the commercial real‐time PCR assays currently used in clinical microbiology laboratories, the AdvanSure TB/NTM real‐time PCR, Cobas MTB, and Xpert MTB/RIF assays are widely implemented. However, most previous studies have focused on pairwise comparisons, limiting the ability to directly compare their relative diagnostic performance. To date, few studies have conducted head‐to‐head comparisons of all three assays within the same clinical cohort under standardized conditions. A direct comparison of these assays under identical clinical and laboratory conditions is important to determine their relative diagnostic performance and potential clinical utility.

In this retrospective study, we compared the diagnostic performance of the AdvanSure, Cobas, and Xpert assays using a large cohort of bronchial washing specimens. By using a uniform specimen type and standardized bronchoscopic and laboratory procedures, specimen‐related heterogeneity was reduced, facilitating direct comparison of the diagnostic performance of the three assays. In this study, we aimed to evaluate and compare the clinical performance of these three assays with AFB smear microscopy, mycobacterial culture, and drug susceptibility testing (DST).

Materials and Methods

Subjects

We retrospectively analyzed the results of molecular assays performed on bronchial washing specimens obtained from patients who underwent bronchoscopy for suspected PTB at Chungnam National University Hospital between January 2021 and May 2024. A total of 3359 testing episodes were identified, of which 11 were excluded because of incomplete AFB smear or culture results. The remaining 3348 specimens from 2981 unique patients were included in the final analysis (Figure 1). For the head‐to‐head comparison, only cases in which all three molecular assays (AdvanSure TB/NTM, Cobas MTB, and Xpert MTB/RIF) were concurrently requested and performed on the same bronchial washing specimen obtained during a single bronchoscopy session as part of routine clinical practice were included in the analysis. The head‐to‐head comparison cohort comprised 740 specimens obtained from 696 unique patients. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Chungnam National University Hospital (IRB No. CNUH 2024–07‐044), and requirement for informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study.

Bronchoscopy Procedures

Bronchoscopy was performed by board‐certified pulmonologists at our institution. Bronchial washing was conducted in target bronchial segments showing abnormal lesions suggestive of PTB or other pulmonary diseases based on chest computed tomography findings. Sterile normal saline was instilled in 10 mL aliquots, and bronchial washing fluid was aspirated until a total volume of 20–30 mL was obtained and collected in a sterile trap bottle. The collected specimens were subsequently transported to the clinical microbiology laboratory for microbiological analysis.

AFB Smear and Culture

All bronchial washing specimens were liquefied and decontaminated with 4% sodium hydroxide. Following centrifugation at 3000 × g for 20 min, the sediment was used for AFB staining. AFB smears were initially examined using auramine–rhodamine fluorescent staining and subsequently confirmed via Ziehl–Neelsen staining. Smear results were graded as negative, 1+, 2+, 3+, or 4+ according to the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention guidelines. For mycobacterial culture, the sediment was inoculated onto 3% Ogawa solid medium (Asan Pharmaceutical Co., Seoul, Republic of Korea) and incubated for up to 8 weeks at 5%–10% CO2. Simultaneously, liquid culture was performed using the BacT/Alert 3D system (bioMérieux, Durham, NC, USA) and incubated for up to 6 weeks according to the manufacturer's instructions. Positive culture isolates were identified using the AdvanSure TB/NTM real‐time PCR assay. DST was performed on culture‐positive isolates.

Real‐Time PCR Assays

AdvanSure TB/NTM real‐time PCR (Invitros, Seoul, Republic of Korea), Cobas MTB assay (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland), and Xpert MTB/RIF assay (Cepheid, Sunnyvale, CA, USA) were performed according to the manufacturers' instructions using the SLAN real‐time PCR detection system (Invitros), automated Cobas 6800 system (Roche Diagnostics), and GeneXpert instrument (Cepheid), respectively [14, 15]. The AdvanSure TB/NTM real‐time PCR assay enables the simultaneous detection of MTBC and nontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) [16]. The Xpert MTB/RIF assay was designed to simultaneously detect MTBC and rifampicin resistance [17]. At our institution, the Xpert MTB/RIF assay was introduced in July 2017, AdvanSure TB/NTM real‐time PCR assay in November 2017, and Cobas MTB assay in August 2021. Because the Cobas MTB assay was introduced last, all head‐to‐head comparisons were performed using specimens collected after the implementation of all three assays. According to routine laboratory procedures, bronchial washing specimens were divided into separate aliquots for mycobacterial culture and molecular testing in the bronchoscopy unit before being transported to the clinical microbiology laboratory. For cases included in the head‐to‐head comparison, separate aliquots from the same bronchial washing specimen were used for the AdvanSure TB/NTM, Cobas MTB, and Xpert MTB/RIF assays. When molecular assays were not performed on the day of specimen collection, specimens were routinely stored at 2°C–8°C until testing. No additional preservation solution was used, and freeze–thaw cycles were not routinely performed. During the study period, the Xpert MTB/RIF assay was performed daily on weekdays, whereas the AdvanSure TB/NTM and Cobas MTB assays were performed in batch mode two to three times per week according to the routine laboratory workflow. All molecular assays were completed within 1 week of specimen collection.

For the AdvanSure TB/NTM assay, a cycle threshold (Ct) value < 35 was considered positive according to the manufacturer's instructions [14]. Results were automatically interpreted and reported as TB complex positive, NTM positive, TB complex and NTM positive, or negative based on the manufacturer's algorithm. For the Cobas MTB assay, results were automatically interpreted according to the manufacturer's proprietary analysis algorithm and reported as MTB positive or MTB negative. For the Xpert MTB/RIF assay, results were automatically interpreted according to the manufacturer's proprietary analysis algorithm and reported as MTB detected or MTB not detected. For MTB‐positive specimens, the assay additionally provided semiquantitative categories (very low, low, medium, or high) [14, 18]. Rifampicin resistance results were reported as detected, not detected, or indeterminate [18]. An indeterminate rifampicin resistance result indicates that the MTB target was detected, but the assay could not accurately determine the presence of mutations in the rpoB gene because of insufficient signal detection [19].

Mycobacterial Drug Susceptibility Testing

DST for rifampicin was performed at the Korean Institute of Tuberculosis using the absolute concentration method. Rifampicin susceptibility results obtained by DST were compared with rifampicin resistance results obtained using the Xpert MTB/RIF assay. Only culture‐positive isolates with available rifampicin DST results were included in the rifampicin resistance analysis.

Analysis of Discrepant Results

For secondary analysis, discrepant results identified during the head‐to‐head comparison were further evaluated through a retrospective review of medical records, including demographic, clinical, and laboratory data. Clinically defined true‐positive cases were defined as those meeting at least one of the following criteria: (a) positive culture results on either liquid or solid media; or (b) negative culture with a positive PCR result accompanied by supporting clinical evidence, including (i) concordant culture‐positive results from other specimens obtained from the same patient; (ii) ongoing anti‐TB treatment at the time of specimen collection; or (iii) clinical features consistent with TB, such as compatible symptoms, radiographic findings, positive interferon‐gamma release assay results, histopathological evidence, and documented clinical improvement following anti‐TB therapy.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), accuracy, and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using mycobacterial culture as the primary reference standard. Additional analyses were performed using clinically defined true‐positive cases as secondary reference standards. For the overall head‐to‐head cohort (n = 740), pairwise comparisons of the sensitivity and specificity of the three assays were performed using McNemar's test based on paired results obtained from the same specimens. A two‐sided p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Diagnostic Performance of the AdvanSure, Cobas, and Xpert Assays Using Mycobacterial Culture as the Reference Standard

In the overall assay‐specific cohorts, the proportions of MTBC culture‐positive cases were 5.6% (153/2726) for AdvanSure, 6.9% (54/787) for Cobas MTB, and 6.6% (172/2600) for Xpert MTB/RIF. The corresponding AFB smear‐positive rates ranged from 3.9% to 4.9%. Using culture‐confirmed MTBC as the reference standard, the overall sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV of the AdvanSure assay were 61.4%, 99.0%, 79.0%, and 97.7%, respectively (Table 1). For the Cobas assay, the overall sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV were 96.3%, 97.1%, 71.2%, and 99.7%, respectively. For the Xpert assay, the overall sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV were 92.4%, 96.8%, 67.1%, and 99.4%, respectively.

Head‐To‐Head Comparison of the AdvanSure, Cobas, and Xpert Assays for MTBC Detection

In the head‐to‐head comparison subset, the proportion of culture‐positive MTBC cases was 7.2% (53/740), while AFB smear‐positive cases accounted for 3.6% (27/740). In this comparison, the overall concordance rate among the three real‐time PCR assays was 93.6% (693/740). Among the 47 discordant specimens, the most common discordant pattern was concurrent positivity by Cobas and Xpert with a negative AdvanSure result (28/47, 59.6%), followed by Xpert‐positive/Cobas‐negative/AdvanSure‐negative results (10/47, 21.3%) and Cobas‐positive/Xpert‐negative/AdvanSure‐negative results (9/47, 19.1%) (Table S1). Using culture‐confirmed MTBC as the reference standard, the overall sensitivities of the AdvanSure, Cobas, and Xpert assays were 49.1%, 96.2%, and 90.6%, respectively (Table 2). All three assays demonstrated 100.0% sensitivity for smear‐positive cases. In smear‐negative cases, the sensitivities of the Cobas and Xpert assays were 94.9% and 87.2%, respectively, whereas that of the AdvanSure assay was markedly lower at 30.8%. The overall specificities of the AdvanSure, Cobas, and Xpert assays were 98.7%, 96.9%, and 96.4%, respectively. For smear‐positive cases, the AdvanSure assay showed 100.0% specificity, whereas both Cobas and Xpert assays demonstrated a specificity of 92.3% and a PPV of 93.3%. In smear‐negative cases, the specificities were 98.7%, 97.0%, and 96.4% for AdvanSure, Cobas, and Xpert, respectively. The overall PPVs were 74.3%, 70.8%, and 65.8% for AdvanSure, Cobas, and Xpert, respectively. For smear‐negative cases, the PPVs of the AdvanSure, Cobas, and Xpert assays were 57.1%, 64.9%, and 58.6%, respectively.

In the overall head‐to‐head cohort, pairwise comparisons using McNemar's test demonstrated that both Cobas and Xpert had significantly higher sensitivity than AdvanSure (both p < 0.001). No significant difference in sensitivity was observed between the Cobas and Xpert assays (p = 0.450). Regarding specificity, AdvanSure demonstrated significantly higher specificity than Cobas (p = 0.0015) and Xpert (p < 0.001), whereas the difference between Cobas and Xpert was not statistically significant (p = 0.386).

When clinically defined true‐positive cases were used as a secondary reference standard, similar trends in assay performance were observed, with the Cobas and Xpert assays demonstrating higher sensitivities than the AdvanSure assay (Table S2).

Culture‐Based Comparative Assessment of AdvanSure TB/NTM Assay for the Detection of NTM

Using culture‐confirmed NTM as the reference standard, the sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV of the AdvanSure TB/NTM assay for NTM detection were 30.5%, 99.7%, 91.8%, and 92.3%, respectively (Table 3). When TB and NTM detection results were combined, the assay showed a sensitivity and specificity of 41.1% and 98.6%, respectively.

Evaluation of the Xpert MTB/RIF Assay for Detection of Rifampicin Resistance Using DST as the Reference Standard

Among 237 Xpert MTB/RIF‐positive specimens, 159 had culture‐confirmed MTBC, and rifampicin drug susceptibility testing (DST) results were available for 144 culture‐positive isolates, which were included in the analysis. Using phenotypic DST as the reference standard, the Xpert MTB/RIF assay correctly identified all seven rifampicin‐resistant and 137 rifampicin‐susceptible isolates, resulting in a sensitivity of 100.0% (95% CI, 59.0%–100.0%), a specificity of 100.0% (95% CI, 97.3%–100.0%), a positive predictive value of 100.0% (95% CI, 59.0%–100.0%), and a negative predictive value of 100.0% (95% CI, 97.3%–100.0%) (Table 4). No indeterminate results for rifampicin resistance were observed in the present study.

Discussion

In this study, we performed a direct comparison of three commercially available real‐time PCR assays for the detection of MTBC using bronchial washing specimens and demonstrated significant differences in diagnostic performance, particularly in smear‐negative cases. The use of bronchial washing as a uniform specimen type reduced specimen heterogeneity, enabling a reliable head‐to‐head comparison among the three assays. In addition, we evaluated the utility of the AdvanSure assay for NTM detection and assessed the performance of the Xpert assay for rifampicin resistance.

Among the three assays, the Cobas and Xpert assays demonstrated similarly high sensitivity and NPV. These findings support the reliability of both assays for ruling out TB, particularly in smear‐negative cases. Previous studies have reported the high diagnostic performance of the Cobas MTB assay for the detection of MTBC in respiratory specimens, consistent with our findings [18, 20, 21].

In contrast, the AdvanSure assay exhibited substantially lower sensitivity, particularly in smear‐negative cases, resulting in a higher proportion of false‐negative results and limiting its clinical utility in detecting paucibacillary disease. Consistent with our findings, several studies have reported a higher sensitivity of the Xpert assay than that of the AdvanSure assay for detecting smear‐negative cases [22, 23]. For example, a previous study using bronchial washing specimens reported that the sensitivity of the Xpert assay for smear‐negative cases was 70.6%, which was significantly higher than that of the AdvanSure assay (52.9%, p = 0.018) [22]. Similarly, a previous study using bronchoscopic specimens demonstrated the superior performance of the Xpert assay compared with the AdvanSure assay in detecting smear‐negative cases [23].

The inferior sensitivity of the AdvanSure assay in smear‐negative cases may be explained by several factors. According to the manufacturer's criteria, a Ct value of < 35 is required to define a positive result, which may restrict detection in specimens with a low bacillary burden [14]. In addition, the requirement for decontamination and concentration before amplification may contribute to the loss of mycobacterial DNA during sample preparation, thereby reducing analytical sensitivity [22]. In contrast, the Xpert assay uses a fully automated, cartridge‐based nucleic acid amplification system that minimizes sample manipulation and preserves mycobacterial DNA throughout testing [24, 25]. This streamlined workflow likely contributes to its superior performance in specimens with low bacillary burden and supports its utility in clinical settings where rapid and sensitive detection is required [18]. All assays showed high overall specificity, with AdvanSure demonstrating the highest. Despite high specificities across all assays, PPVs were comparatively lower, particularly for smear‐negative cases. This may partly reflect PCR‐positive but culture‐negative results observed in patients receiving anti‐TB treatment, in whom residual DNA from nonviable MTBC organisms can be detected despite negative culture results [14]. When clinically defined true‐positive cases were incorporated as a secondary reference standard, several discrepant cases were reclassified as clinically true‐positive cases, resulting in a marked increase in PPV. Accordingly, positive molecular results should be interpreted in conjunction with clinical, radiological, and microbiological findings, particularly in smear‐negative patients and in settings with a low prevalence of culture‐confirmed MTBC.

In the direct head‐to‐head comparison of the three assays, the concordance rate was high, indicating substantial overall agreement. Nevertheless, discrepancies were predominantly observed in smear‐negative cases, highlighting the importance of assay selection in this challenging diagnostic context. These findings suggest that assay selection may have a meaningful impact on diagnostic sensitivity in smear‐negative cases, a clinical setting in which microbiological confirmation is often challenging in routine clinical practice.

With respect to NTM detection, the AdvanSure assay exhibited limited sensitivity but maintained high specificity and NPV. Similar to the findings observed for MTBC detection, the relatively low sensitivity may partly reflect the assay's detection characteristics in specimens with a low organism burden. These findings suggest that although a positive result may be helpful in detecting NTM, the limited sensitivity of the assay may restrict its utility as a primary detection tool, particularly in specimens with a low organism burden. Consequently, the possibility of missed NTM detection cannot be completely excluded in PCR‐negative specimens.

Rifampicin resistance is widely regarded as a surrogate marker for multidrug‐resistant tuberculosis (MDR‐TB), as monoresistance to rifampicin is uncommon and is frequently accompanied by isoniazid resistance [26, 27]. According to the national surveillance data from 2022, the prevalence of multidrug‐ or rifampicin‐resistant tuberculosis in South Korea was 3.0% among newly diagnosed patients and 8.4% among previously treated patients [28].

In the present study, the Xpert assay demonstrated excellent diagnostic performance for detecting rifampicin resistance, showing 100% sensitivity (95% CI, 59.0%–100.0%) and 100% specificity (95% CI, 97.3%–100.0%). However, only seven rifampicin‐resistant isolates were included in the analysis, resulting in a relatively wide confidence interval for sensitivity. Therefore, the sensitivity estimate should be interpreted with caution. These findings are consistent with previous reports showing a sensitivity and specificity of 100% and 99.1%, respectively, for rifampicin resistance detection using the Xpert assay [22]. The ability of the Xpert system to simultaneously detect MTBC and rifampicin resistance provides a substantial advantage for rapid clinical decision‐making in patients with suspected MDR‐TB [5].

This study has several limitations. First, the three PCR assays were not always performed on the same day. However, all tests were completed within 1 week under standardized laboratory conditions. Specimens were refrigerated during the interim period, and no freeze–thaw cycles were routinely performed. Therefore, although minor pre‐analytical variability cannot be completely excluded, its potential impact on assay performance is expected to be limited.

Second, this was a retrospective single‐center study, and the head‐to‐head comparison was restricted to the 740 specimens in which all three commercial PCR assays were ordered as part of routine clinical practice. This approach reflects real‐world diagnostic workflows in which multiple assays are requested for cases with clinical suspicion of tuberculosis or when confirmatory testing is required. Although this nonrandom selection may introduce selection bias and limit generalizability, the study cohort still represents one of the largest datasets available for a direct comparison of these three widely used assays. In addition, the analysis was performed at the specimen level rather than at the patient level. The overall cohort consisted of 3348 specimens obtained from 2981 unique patients, whereas the head‐to‐head comparison cohort consisted of 740 specimens obtained from 696 unique patients. Although a small proportion of patients contributed more than one specimen, the majority contributed only a single specimen (89.6% and 94.3% in the overall and head‐to‐head cohorts, respectively). Therefore, the potential influence of within‐patient correlation on the overall findings is likely to be limited.

Third, MTBC and NTM species identification of culture‐positive isolates was performed using the AdvanSure TB/NTM real‐time PCR assay as part of the routine laboratory workflow. Accordingly, the MTBC/NTM classification used in this study was not fully independent of one of the index assays, and potential classification bias cannot be excluded.

Fourth, the secondary analysis based on clinically defined true‐positive cases may have been affected by incorporation bias because PCR positivity was included as a component of the clinical reference standard. Although discrepant cases were adjudicated through retrospective review of clinical, radiological, microbiological, and treatment‐related information, the inclusion of PCR results in the adjudication criteria may have led to an overestimation of assay specificity. Therefore, the results derived from the clinically defined reference standard should be interpreted with caution and regarded as supportive rather than definitive evidence of diagnostic performance.

Finally, as a single‐center study conducted in South Korea, an intermediate TB‐burden country with low HIV prevalence, the findings may not be directly generalizable to regions with different tuberculosis epidemiology, HIV prevalence, and bacillary burden, particularly those in high‐burden, high‐HIV settings [18, 29, 30]. The diagnostic performance of molecular TB assays may vary according to patient populations, specimen types, smear status, and disease characteristics. Therefore, our results should be interpreted within the local clinical and epidemiological context.

Despite these limitations, the present study provides valuable real‐world evidence regarding the comparative performance of three widely used commercial PCR assays in bronchial washing specimens. Because all three assays were evaluated using the same bronchial washing specimen obtained during a single bronchoscopy session, the observed differences are more likely to reflect differences between the diagnostic platforms under routine clinical conditions. Considering the logistical and financial constraints associated with performing multiple molecular assays simultaneously in routine clinical practice, the present findings provide clinically meaningful evidence to inform assay selection for the rapid and accurate diagnosis of PTB.

In conclusion, although the AdvanSure assay offers the advantage of simultaneous detection of TB and NTM, its sensitivity for TB detection was lower than that of the Cobas and Xpert assays. In contrast, the Cobas and Xpert assays demonstrated high diagnostic performance in both smear‐positive and smear‐negative cases, supporting their use for routine molecular diagnosis of PTB. These findings may help inform the selection of molecular diagnostic platforms for the rapid and accurate diagnosis of PTB in routine clinical practice.
