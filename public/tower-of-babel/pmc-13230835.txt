Evaluation of season and sex specific differential gonadal transcriptomics in Himalayan snow trout Schizothorax plagiostomus, Heckel, 1838
Authors: Indrashis Bhattacharya, Harish Kothandaraman, Sachin Kothiyal, Partigya Sharma, Shriya Purohit, Arumugam Anitha, N K Agarwal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite its wide presence in cold-water bodies of central Asian mountains, Schizothorax plagiostomus is highly neglected Himalayan ichthyofauna. Here we have performed de novo RNA-sequencing in adult S. plagiostomus gonads captured from natural habitat of Garhwal Himalaya [during three distinct maturational stages (pre-spawning, spawning and post-spawning) for both sexes] and compared the season and sex specific differential transcriptomic profiles. Functional analyses of KEGG pathway and GO enrichment demonstrated augmentation in cellular signaling for multiple growth factors, chemokines, cytokines, exosomes and vascular patterning in pre-spawning gonads, rise in t-RNA biosynthesis, chaperone cofactor dependent protein refolding, steroidogenic and calcium signaling network at spawning time, whereas elevated proteolysis, autophagy and apoptotic pathways were prevalent during regressing stages. Among differentially expressed genes (DEGs) distinct sexual dimorphic pattern of male specific Dmrt1, Sox18, Amh, Wt1, GDNF receptor α and female specific Foxl2, Gdf9, Zar1,Cyp19a1a, Wntless were observed.Collectively, this is the first comprehensive gonadal atlas of season dependent developmental transcriptome with respective gametogenic output in any Schizothoracine species. This data will potentially serve as a vital resource for future programs in conservation management and artificial breeding of schizothoracine fishes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47745-7.Keywords: Gametogenesis, Transcriptomics, Gonads, Testis, Ovary, SchizothoraxSubject terms: Developmental biology, Genetics, Molecular biology, Zoology

Introduction

Fishes are commercially valuable animal taxa contributing around 20% of overall animal protein consumption per person for more than 3 billion people globally1. In 2022, out of the total global aquaculture production (130.9 million tons, valued USD 312.8 billion), farmed and captured fisheries contribute about 51% and 49%, respectively2.The small-scale fisheries constitute approximately 40% of the global catch supporting around 500 million people (45% women) worldwide2. In Indo-Himalayan region, small-scale based cold-water fishery harbors 258 species under 21 families and 76 genera viz., schizothoracines (Schizothorax and Schizothoraichthys) and cyprinids (Tor, Labeo and Crossocheilus) and contribute around 3.32% to the total fish germplasm resource of India3–6.

Schizothoracines or Himalayan snow trout/minnows (order cypriniformes, family cyprinidae, sub-family schizothoracinae with 12–15 genera and more than 70 species) are important edible and/or game fish found in the tributaries, streams, rivers and lakes of the Hindukush- Karakoram-Himalayan foothills/mountains of central Asia (ranging around 2400 km from eastern-Iran, Kazakhstan, Tajikistan, Uzbekistan, Afghanistan, Pakistan, India, Qinghai-Tibetan plateau, Nepal, Bhutan, China to Myanmar) at the elevation of 1180–3000 m from the sea level7–12. However, despite having such a wide distribution pattern and significant economic value schizothoracine fishes are highly neglected ichthyofauna with only 331 hits in pubmed upto March 2026.

Schizothorax (S.) plagiostomus, Heckel, 1838 is one of the most dominant hill stream teleost species of the Indo-China region having immense commercial value with adequate taxonomical data11,13–17. Sexually mature adult S. plagiostomus may weigh up to 2–2.5 kg and attain 50–60 cm length18,19. The fish become sexually mature by 2–2.5 years of age with 18–24 cm length. Females usually spawn in transparent water at pH 7.5 and gravelly pebbled bed that is about 10–30 cm deep with gravel size 50–60 mm having suitable water current between 2.8–4.8 ms−1 and 10-15mgl−1 dissolved oxygen18,19.

During the past two decades, the rapid and uncontrolled anthropogenic activities in Garhwal Himalaya, Uttarakhand, India (e.g.- water pollution, urbanization, construction of roads/hydroelectric projects, over-exploitation and introduction of exotic species etc.) has resulted a massive decline in the indigenous Schizothorax population due to asynchronization and impairment of gonadal maturation4,20. Particularly, mature males often show early gonadal recrudescence, leading to preterm spawning compared to that found in mature females4. Therefore, during the breeding season, males become spent, whereas the females are still ripe/gravid and keep producing eggs. Therefore, deploying artificial breeding strategies become critical for restoration of these species to maintain the ecological balance intact, satisfying the growing demand of food security.

The robust reproductive competence of teleost fishes is attributed by remarkable developmental plasticity of the gonads with unique germline resilience during seasonal and/or climatic changes21–25. Previous studies investigating the breeding biology of S. plagiostomus were limited to only annual gonadal architecture and function26–32.

However, despite such preliminary information available on gonadal structure/function, molecular data on schizothoracine gonadal maturation and output remain insufficient. Furthermore due to inadequate knowledge of essential captive breeding parameters (viz., appropriate age of sexual maturity, optimal requirement of sex ratio, brood-stock density, quality of food and nutritional demand, critical temperature and photoperiod cues, mating behaviour, spawning frequency, ovarian fecundity rates and sperm viability etc.) multiple attempts of establishing an successful induced breeding programme have been failed for Schizothorax species so far.

This study was specifically aimed to generate the necessary transcriptional circuit for understanding the mechanist network underlying the gametogenic drive from the seasonal initiation, progression (or maintenance) to regression in wild adult S. plagiostomus gonads by RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq). Results

DNA barcoding for species identity

Collected fish samples showed uniform nucleotide composition for mitochondrial CO-I and Cyt-b genes with respect to the authentic reference sequence of S. plagiostomus available in NCBI Genbank (Accession number: NC023531) thus validating species accuracy (data not shown). Supplemental Fig. S1 shows the representative images of adult male and female pre-spawning (A) spawning (B) and post-spawning (C) In S. plagiostomus, notable sexual dimorphism was visible only during spawning time where ripe males developed maxillary tubercles and gravid females showed typical swollen belly.

Gonadal histology confirming the maturational status

Gonadal tissues from three distinct maturational stages viz., pre-spawning (June-July) spawning (September–October) and post-spawning stage (November–December) exhibit typical features in morphology, GSI values and histological architecture.

Males

Pre-spawning testes were pinkish-red, with visible lobular structures (Supplemental Fig. S2A) having mean GSI value 0.523 ± 0.30 with predominance of undifferentiated spermatogonia (Sg1), primary spermatocytes (Sc1) (Supplemental Fig. S2B). Spawning testes were creamy-white with firm elongated lobules capable of oozing milt on mild pressure (Supplemental Fig. S2C), having mean GSI value 7.104 ± 1.62 where round spermatids (Rspt) and spermatozoa (Spz) were discernible (Supplemental Fig. S2D). Post-spawning, testes were whitish-grey where lobules became slender and flaccid (Supplemental Fig. S2E), having mean GSI value 3.231 ± 1.863 with few residual/leftover spermatozoa (Spz) (Supplemental Fig. S2F).

Females

Pre-spawning ovaries (mean GSI value 5.02 ± 1.65) were white in color with distinct blood vessels (Supplemental Fig. S3A), having cortical alveoli (CA), primary vitellogenic oocyte 1(Vtg1) and secondary vitellogenic oocytes 2 (Vtg2) mean egg diameter around 1.13 ± 0.09 to 1.2 ± 0.16 mm (Supplemental Fig. S3B). Spawning ovaries (mean GSI value 12.24 ± 0.29) reddish-yellow in color consisted by mature yolky ova (mean diameter around 1.7 ± 0.12 to 2.003 ± 0.012 mm) with massive vasculature (Supplemental Fig. S3C), having mature vitellogenic oocytes 2 and 3 (Vtg2, Vtg3) (Supplemental Fig. S3D). Post-spawning ovaries (mean GSI value 3.58 ± 1.20) blackish-yellow in color, shrunken, flaccid filled with un-ovulated egg (mean diameter around 1.1 ± 0.14 to 1.18 ± 0.20 mm) (Supplemental Fig. S3E), having vitellogenic atretic follicles (VAF) and atretic oocytes (AO) (Supplemental Fig. S3F).

Differential gene expression

PCA analyses—The relative transcriptional complexity (either uniqueness and/or uniformity) of three different maturational stages was examined in both testes (Fig. 1A) and ovaries (Fig. 1B). The pre-spawning gonads were found to be transcriptionally heterogeneous as compared to that of the spawning and post-spawning stages in both the sexes.

Principal component analysis of testes (A) and ovaries (B), indicating transcriptomic homogeneity within spawning and post-spawning stages for both sexes; whereas the pre-spawning gonads of each set of both the sexes were found to have heterogeneous gene expression patterns.

Differential analyses

The seasonal transcriptional variations for testicular gene expression have been represented in the venn diagrams (Fig. 2A) and volcano plots (Fig. 2B). Total numbers of differential transcript-clusters were 5462 (pre-spawning vs spawning), 1139 (post-spawning vs spawning) and 6239 (post-spawning vs pre-spawning testes. Heat maps

DEG profiles of non-redundant top 20 genes were displayed for male (Fig. 5A) and female (Fig. 5B) gonads. This data suggested that despite having distinct stage dependent robust transcriptional differences, individual transcripts do substantially vary within a particular stage, indicating sign of asynchronous state of seasonal maturation. Such stage dependent differential expression profile of twelve critical spermatogenic genes [e.g.- Voltage dependent L type calcium channel subunit α (LTCCα) and β (CaVβ), Voltage dependent T type Ca+2 channel subunit α (Cacna1h) Doublesex and mab related transcription factor (Dmrt1), Fibroblast growth factor(s) (Fgf), Calcium transporting ATPase, Mullerian inhibiting factor (Amh), Wilms tumor protein homolog (Wt1), GDNF family receptor α (Gdnf-rα) and Transcription factor Sox (Sox18)] were found to be highly dynamic in adult testes (Fig. 5C). The mean expression [in terms of variance stabilizing transformation DEseq2 (VST) values] of Fgf, Gdnf-rα, Amh were found to be elevated (Amh with highest expression level maximal) in pre-spawning testes, whereas expression of Dmrt1, Wt1, Sox18 and Ca+2 transporting ATPase remain unaltered throughout the three maturational stages (Supplemental Fig. S5A). On the other hand, unlike testes, stage dependent expressions of ten critical oogenic genes [Forkhead box protein L2 (Foxl2), ovarian aromatase (Cyp19a1a), Cathepsin L1(Ctsl1), Wntless (Gpr177), Catenin β1 (Ctnnb1), Growth differentiation factor 9 (Gdf9), Zygote arrest protein 1(Zar1), Zona pellucida sperm binding protein 3 and 4 (Zp3 & Zp4) and Runt related transcription factor (Runx1)] showed minor variations (Fig. 5D). The mean expression (in terms of VST values) of Zar1, Wntless, ZP3, ZP4, Cyp19a1a and Gdf9 were found to be higher as compare to Foxl2, Ctnnb1 and Ctsl1 with minimal level of Runx1 throughout the three maturational stages (Supplemental Fig. S5B).

Discussion

The current study was aimed to determine the season and sex specific differential transcriptomic landscape of adult S. plagiostomus gonads. Schizothoracine fishes are bottom dwellers found in cold-water bodies/hill-streams of Hindukush-Karakoram-Himalayan mountains covering around 2400 km of central Asia3,7,8,12. Out of 3 sympatric species under genus Schizothorax, (S. richardsonii, Gray,1832 S. plagiostomus, Heckel,1838 and S. progastus,McClelland,1839) found in Garhwal-Himalaya, Uttarakhand, India. S. plagiostomus,Heckel,1838 was selected for this study due to its wide range of presence [parts of eastern Iran, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, Uzbekistan, Afghanistan, Pakistan, India, Nepal, Bhutan, Qinghai-Tibetian plateau, China (river Yangtze) upto Myanmar (river Mekong)]9–11, high preference as food (due to its moderate size, tender flesh with delicious taste and high nutritional value) with critical role in sustaining the local rural economy33.

Sample authentication

Morphologically, S. plagiostomus has a particular jaw structure due to the presence of thick smooth sheath on the margin of the lower jaw forming a suctorial mouth and upon extending the anal fin touches the caudal fin9,34,35. To confirm the uniformity of the species identity examined in this study, we have meticulously screened all the freshly captured fishes (each sex for three maturational stages) by classical morphometric parameters9,34,35 and advance DNA barcoding approach using mt markers (Cytb and Co-I)17. Like most of the freshwater gonochoristic teleosts24, Schizothorax is also reported to exhibit robust seasonal variations during annual reproductive cycle with 5 district maturational stages resting/dormant (April–May), regenerating/preparatory (June-July), developing/pre-spawning (July-Aug), spawning (Sept-Oct), regressing/post-spawning (Nov-Dec)31,32.

Since the sexual identity remains indistinguishable during resting and preparatory stage (April-June), three major maturational stages (viz., pre-spawning, spawning, post-spawning) showing significant changes in respective GSI values, were selected in order to investigate the regulatory genetic networks critical for initiating and maintaining the gametogenic output in this species. To confirm the maturational status of each stage/fish, respective gonadal histology was examined by TEM imaging. Furthermore, 3–5 individual fishes were pooled for a particular stage only (considering one biological set) having comparable/similar range of GSI values ensuring all stages might represent the comparable status of sexual maturation.

Differential transcriptomics

Obtaining differential transcriptomic profile by bulk RNA-seq approach is being widely used for non-model organisms particularly in fishes36 due to efficient acquiring of high-throughput data. Notably, recently developed single cell-RNA-seq is not popularized yet, thereby restricted to model organisms like zebrafish, Danio rerio37.Notably, substantial studies have deployed de novo assembly and reported season and/or sex specific differential transcriptomics in multiple teleost gonads like spotted snakehead Channa punctatus38 common carp, Cyprinus carpio39, D. rerio40, Pacific bluefin tuna, Thunnus orientalis41, Nile tilapia, Oreochromis niloticus42, pompano, Trachinotus ovatus43, Asian arowana, Scleropages formosus44, red-tail catfish, Hemibagrus wyckioides45, Chinese yellow catfish, Pelteobagrus fulvidraco46 etc.

Despite collecting pre-spawning gonads having closest/comparable ranges of GSI values (mean GSI for males 0.523 ± 0.3 and females 5.02 ± 1.6), the maturational status of these gonads in terms of gametogenic development remained unsynchronized. This might have been reflected in the PCA data where transcriptional heterogeneity was observed among the biological triplicates. However, for both the sexes, minimal transcriptional variance(s) were found with the peak of the gametogenic drive during the spawning phase or thereafter with synchronized regression of post- spawning gonads.

Although a distinguishable transcriptional profile was observed at a particular gonadal maturational stage in both the sexes, such differences were more profound and robust in testes as compared to that of the ovaries. Such distinct variations in testicular transcriptional dynamics has been reported in cypriniformes {C. carpio39,47 rohu, Labeo rohita48}, anabantiformes {C. punctatus38}, cichliformes [O. niloticus42}, siluriformes {African sharp-tooth catfish, Clarias gariepinus49 H. wyckioides45}and others50. On the other hand, such stage dependent distinct transcriptional differences were also observed in ovarian gene expression profile for S. plagiostomus which is consistent with previous findings from diverse teleost orders viz., cypriniformes C. carpio51, siluriformes, channel catfish, Ictalurus punctatus52, salmoniformes, {coho salmon, Oncorhynchus kisutch53, rainbow trout, Oncorhynchus mykiss54}, perciformes, red-spotted grouper, Epinephelus akaara55, gadiformes, Atlantic cod, Gadus morhua56, centrarchiformes, largemouth bass, Micropterus salmoides57 etc.

A distinct sexually dimorphic gene expression pattern was observed in all three maturational stages. Despite having a unique transcriptional signature found in each maturation stage per sex (gonads), the transcript levels (degree of gene expression in terms of VST values) found to be drastically fluctuating within that stage of gonadal development. This is reflected by the variable transcript levels of a particular gene among three biological sets within a particular maturational stage. Such developmental difference in gene expression data might have resulted from relative differential status of maturation of the gonadal cells during the annual reproductive cycle24. Furthermore, schizothoracine fishes exhibit natural tetra/hexaploidy (4N/6N) which also might have contributed to such transcriptional discrepancies within a particular maturational stage58,59.

Natural tetraploidy (4N) is common in teleosts [like cyprinids (e.g., common carp Cyprinus carpio or goldfish Carassius auratus) and salmonids (Atlantic salmon Salmo salar)] and it have been evolved due to an ancestral whole-genome duplication [either by self-doubling (autopolyploidy) or via hybridization between different species (allopolyploidy)]60.Recent data suggest that an auto-polyploidization (4N or 6 N) in schizothoracine fishes has been found to be occurred with global cooling down event during the transitions from Eocene to Oligocene (33.9 Mya) and Pliocene to Pleistocene (2.58 Mya)61. Furthermore, re-diploidization of the polyploid Schizothorax genomes might have occurred with high altitude adaptation during the upliftment of the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau and therefore played a critical role in schizothoracine phylogeny and speciation59.

Notably, polyploidy contributes significant genomic complexities and therefore identifying orthologous and paralogous genes across the teleosts remains challenging60. Particularly, the presence of two similar sub-genomes (duplicated gene pairs originated by self-doubling or hybridization termed as homeologs), potentially results a significant expressional discrepancies, due to rapid transcriptional shock, fallacy in expression(s) of the transcript(s), biased (either dominance or silence) expressions of homeologs etc60..

The intra and inter stage variations were distinctly discernible for both spermatogenic (e.g.- α & β subunits of voltage dependent L and T type Ca+2 channel, Dmrt1, Fgf, Ca+2 transporting ATPase, Amh, Wt1, Gdnf-rα and Sox18) and oogenic (e.g.- Foxl2, Cyp19a1a, Cathepsin L1, Wntless, Ctnnb1, Gdf9, Zar1, Zp3 & Zp4 and Runx1) genes studied here.

Fishes follow annual reproductive cycles thereby exhibiting seasonal variations for gonadal maturation24. Teleost testes show a unique cystic type of spermatogenic progression where the core functional unit of the spermatogenic epithelium appears like a cyst. This is formed by a dynamic group of somatic stem derived Sertoli cell populations efficiently nursing the surrounded synchronously developing germ cell clone25,62. In cypriniformes species (including Schizothorax) the spermatogonial populations are unrestrictedly distributed within the germinal compartment whereas the cyst remains fixed at the same position throughout the development62. In contrast, the ovarian maturation cycle found to be either synchronous (oocytes develop and ovulate together), or group-synchronous (two populations of oocytes present throughout the reproductive season) or totally asynchronous (all stages present without any dominant population63. S. plagiostomus inhabiting at Garhwal Himalaya, found to be an asynchronous ovulator since the eggs get matured and subsequently ovulate in multiple batches within an extended spawning time (September–October followed by February–March) depending on rain and water temperature32.

Hill-stream Schizothoracine fishes exhibits remarkable high-altitude cold-water (4–8 °C) adaptation like metabolic shifts (elevated levels of serum cholesterol, triglycerides and glucose), energy homeostasis [increase in biosynthesis of amino acids, rise in omega-3 fatty acids (eicosapentaenoic acid and docosahexaenoic acid), decrease of long chain monounsaturated fatty acid (nervonic acid)], inhibition of TCA cycle (by reduction in L-malic acid and cis-aconitic acid production), accelerating purine (uridine and cytidine) transport, efficient hypoxic tolerance, restricting swimming speed and critical regulation of temperature-sensitive cellular pathways as shown in S. grahami, Regan, 190464 and in S. prenanti, Tchang, 193065.

Notably, our gonadal transcriptomic data obtained during different seasonal periods reflecting different developmental stages were found to be well corroborated with multiple cellular events like pre-meiotic divisions, meiotic progression and post-meiotic maturation for each sex. Advanced KEGG and GO analyses revealed distinct molecular pathways being differentially enriched during the annual gonadal cycle coinciding with season dependent gametogenic onset and maintenance. Such critical pathways includes homologous recombination, exonuclease activity, DNA repair (non-homologous end joining, mismatch and nucleotide–excision repair), synaptonemal complex, kinetochore assembly etc. indicating meiotic division; vesicular transport, t-RNA biosynthesis, proteasome, chaperone cofactor dependent protein refolding peptidase, nc-RNA-processing etc. suggest regulation of gene expression and biosynthesis of novel proteins; oxidative phosphorylation, ATPase activity, peroxidase activity, glutathione transferase activity for differential redox potential; cAMP-dependent protein kinase complex, phosphodiesterase, tyrosine/serine/threonine phosphatase activity for vibrant kinome status of the gonadal tissues; shelterin complex autophagosome, lysosome p53 signaling etc. suggesting apoptosis; carboxypeptidase, endopeptidase activity, PPAR signaling, biosynthesis of N-glycan, fatty acid, sphingolipids, steroids etc. for regulating metabolism; growth factor, chemokine, cytokine, exosome signalings for cellular differentiation and growth; collagen fibril organization, ECM-receptor interaction, VEGF activity, integrin binding, anchoring junctions etc. indicating tissue remodeling and vasculature patterning; axonemal dynein complex, egg-coat etc. indicating typical structural support for gamete formation.

Apart from reporting the annual transcriptional changes in the both the gonads, we here also have highlighted the sex specific differences in gonadal gene expression. Our KEGG analyses suggested ovary specific (in all three maturational stages) enrichment of pathways associated with N- Glycan biosynthesis, mannose type O glycan biosynthesis, glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI) anchor biosynthesis, homologous recombination, protein export, non-homologous end joining, SNARE interactions in vesicular transport, sulfur relay system, fatty acid biosynthesis and nitrogen metabolism etc. However, GO-BP analyses showed ovary specific (in all three maturational stages) upregulation of pathways like mitochondrial respiratory chain assembly, dolichol linked oligosaccharide biosynthetic process, tRNA wobble uridine modification, AMP salvage, kinetochore assembly, mitotic G2 DNA damage checkpoint signaling, mitochondrial mRNA stability, spindle assembly, egg coat formation, transcription by RNA polymerase I, II & III, endosomal transport, mitotic cell cycle phase transition, Mg+2 homeostasis etc. However, genes associated with pathways like sphingosine I phosphate receptor signalling, reciprocal meiotic recombination, cytoplasmic translational elongation, antigen processing and presentation of peptide antigen via MHC class I were found to upregulated in all three maturational stages of testes. Furthermore, GO-MF analyses indicated ovary specific augmentation in the activities of ribonuclease P, RNA methyltransferase, cycline dependent protein serine/threonine kinase, ATPase activator whereas testis specific enrichments were found in activities of phospholipase inhibitors, ceramidase, proton channels, NAD(P)H oxydase, sphingosine 1 phosphate receptors and LIM domain binding etc. Finally, GO-CC based data showed ovary specific upregulation of endoribonuclease complex and mitochondrial inner membrane components whereas proteasome activator complex, dynein axonemal particle and respiratory chain complex IV were found to be elevated in testes. Collectively, these data indicated a distinct sexually dimorphic transcriptional dynamics in Schizothorax gonads across the three maturational stages significantly involving pathways/events like mRNA stability, tRNA modifications, meiotic chromosomal assembly, vesicular transport, immune response, mitochondrial biogenesis with energy homeostasis and sugar metabolism etc.

Differential gene expression

Schizothorax gonads showed remarkable sexual dimorphism in terms of key gametogenic genes having significant contribution towards seasonal reproductive development and output. Male biased expressions were found to be prominent for Sox18, Dmrt1, Wt1, Fgf, Amh and Gdnfr-α, where Sox18, Dmrt1 and Wt1 expressions remain consistent from pre-spawning stage to post-spawning stage. Notably, Fgf, Amh and Gdnfr-α mRNAs were elevated in pre-spawning testes.

Sox-18 [Sry-related high mobility group (HMG) box (Sox) gene- family] codes for multiple transcription factors in teleosts having diverse functions in cellular differentiation and morphogenesis, including sex determination, gonadal development66. Sox18 has been found in the brain and gonads of O. niloticus67 and olive flounder, Paralichthys olivaceus68. Consistent with our findings Sox18 has been reported to be elevated in Chinese sturgeon, Acipenser sinensis testis69. Taken together, in pre-spawning Schizothorax testes, Sox-18 probably supports pre-meiotic spermatogonial differentiation. Our data suggests Dmrt1 as a potential male specific gene expressed in all stages of annual testicular development. Dmrt1 acts as a critical spermatogenic regulator in multiple teleost species (O. niloticus, Japanese rice-fish medaka Oryzias latipes, O. mykiss and Yangtze catfish Silurus meridonalis)70,71. Dmrt1bY/Dmy (the mammalian Dmrt1- orthologous gene in the Y chromosome) found in O. latipes directs sex determination and testicular development72,73. Dmrt1 expression gets upregulated during larval to juvenile transition in Tibetan/Chinese snow-trout S. kozlavi, Nikolskii, 190374. Testicular stage dependent expressions of Dmrt gene(s) -family observed in different gonochoristic annual breeders C. gariepinus75, D. rerio and G. morhu, O. mykiss71.

Wt1 belongs to the zinc-finger family of transcription factors (having two splice-variants Wt1a and Wt1b in teleosts) shown to be critical for the development of the urogenital system76. In O. latipes, Wt1a expresses gonadal ridge prior to (expressions of Dmy and Dmrt1) sex determination77 and supports the survival of primordial germ cells78. We found Wt1 expression was elevated in Schizothorax testes in all three stages indicating a critical role in spermatogenic development and progression. Such male biased expression is consistent with previous observation in O. niloticus79 and O. latipes78, Indian catfish, Clarias batrachus, where Wt1a and Wt1b are localized to somatic and male germ cells, respectively80. In adult testes of C. batrachus, Wt1 coordinates with Ad4bp/sf-1 and Gata4 to augment the expression of steroidogenic genes coding for androgen biosynthesis pathway80. On the other hand, Wt1 also found to be expressed in Schizothorax ovaries which were further supported by observations made in the Asian seabass, Lates calcarifer81, D. rerio82.

We found high levels of Fgf mRNAs in pre-spawning testes, which might have an essential role in cellular proliferation and differentiation as reported in C. carpio83 and Black rockfish, Sebastes schlegeli84. In O. latipes testes, Fgf(s) predominantly express in Sertoli cells85. Fgf24 has been shown to regulate early gonadal morphogenesis in D. rerio, where Fgf24 mutants develop as sterile adults86.

GDNFr-α has been reported as markers for undifferentiated spermatogonial stem/progenitor cells. In O. mysis, GDNFr-α is expressed in A spermatogonia, with dramatic decline in B spermatogonia and fully undetectable in spermatids87. Elevated expression of GDNFr-α detected in our pre-spawning testes of S. plagiostomus was thereby justified.

The expression (highest among all transcripts studied) of Amh was found to be maximal during pre-spawning testes and remain elevated as compared to ovaries in subsequent stages suggesting its critical role in governing the male sex in schizothoracine species. This observation is consistent with other species like Patagonian pejerrey Odontesthes hatcheri88, O. niloticus89, northern pike Esox lucius90. Spermatogonial differentiation in fishes is mostly induced by 11-keto-testoststone (11-KT) and Amh expression is reported to get suppressed by 11-KT91–93, therefore enrichment of Amh transcripts in pre-spawning testes also being supported sensibly.

Voltage-dependent Ca+2 channels are two types, L (high voltage-activated) or T (low voltage-activated) and are reported to be expressed in mammalian spermatozoa promoting sperm motility94. No such augmentation of these transcripts (L -type α and β) was found by us during spawning testes whereas α-submit of T type Ca+2 channel was found to be elevated in ovaries and maximally expressed in post-spawning stage as reported in murine ovaries95.

In contrast, identified prominent female genes like Wntless, Ctsl1, Foxl2, Gdf9, Cyp19a1a, Zar1, Runx1, ZP3 & Zp4 etc. where only Cyp19a1a and Zar1 mRNAs were elevated in pre-spawning ovaries. Wntless is coded by Gpr177or Evi and an essential regulator of WNT protein secretion96. Components of WNT signaling found to be critical for mammalian ovarian development viz., Wnt4/β-Catenin97, Wnt2a,2b/Frizzled-498 etc. However, Wnt(s) signaling play essential role in spermatogenic development in rodents99. In accordance with this, no sexual biasness was found for β-catenin expression in Schizothorax gonads. Cathepsin L is a cysteine lysosomal protease, regulates vitellogenesis in D. rerio100 and degeneration of the postovulatory follicle in O. latipes101. Foxl2 has been critical for ovarian differentiation and estrogen (E2) production in multiple piscine species102. Foxl2 functions as a transcriptional inducer of Cyp19a1a gene (coding for ovarian aromatase) promoter for E2 biosynthesis103,104. High levels of Foxl2 expression remained uniform in all three stages of Schizothorax ovaries. Notably, stage dependent changes were observed in Foxl2 transcription during annual ovarian cycles of anchovy, Coilia nasus104, Celebes medaka, O. celebensis105 and C. gariepinus106. However, like S. plagiostomus, gonadal Foxl2 expression is found to be stable (independent of different temperature fluctuations) in Tibetan/Chinese snow-trout S. kozlovi107. Despite no apparent changes in Foxl2 expression, ovarian aromatase Cyp19a1a was found to be maximal in pre-spawning ovaries of S. plagiostomus. Zar1 is oocyte specific maternally inherited RNA binding protein that regulates murine oocyte-embryo transition107,108. Zar1 expression is predominantly expressed in D. rerio110, O. mykiss111, and Japanese eel, Anguilla japonica112, O. niloticus113 etc. and essential for oogenesis. Our data indicated that Zar1 expression was most prevalent during the pre-spawning stage.

The expression of Gdf9 was found to be stable in Schizothorax ovaries throughout all the three maturational stages. Gdf9 plays a pivotal role in the oocyte development/maturation along with BMP1563. Studies in multiple fish species {e.g.- D. rerio114, Japanese flounder, Paralichthys olivaceus115, European seabass Dicentrarchus labrax,116,117, O. mykiss118 and short-finned eel Anguilla australis119,120 unambiguously demonstrate high expressions of Gdf9 and Bmp15 during the primary and early secondary stages of oocyte development with a subsequent decline during oocyte maturation. In Chinese Ya-fish, Schizothorax prenanti, Tchang, 1930, Gdf9 expression is predominantly found in the cortical alveoli (CA) stage121. Finally, our data showed relatively low (higher than that found in testes) but uniform levels of Runx1, Zp3 and Zp4 mRNAs during the three different stages of ovarian maturation in S. plagiostomus. Runx1 has been found to be critical for specification and differentiation of ovarian granulosa cells in O. mykiss122, whereas ZP genes code for different glycoproteins constituting the fish egg envelope i.e. chorion that supports the crucial cellular events of oogenic development, egg deposition, fertilization, and early-embryogenesis in D. rerio and O. latipes123–126.

Concluding remarks

Collectively, this study provides a comprehensive summary of sex specific gonadal transcriptomic profile observed during the three distinct maturational stages (within the annual reproductive cycles) for a schizothoracine species. Pre-spawning gonads were transcriptionally heterogeneous, however, a synchronized gene expression pattern was observed during spawning and post-spawning stages. Our data further compared the season dependent unique gene expression profiles of gonads in both the sexes. Transcriptional dynamics was found to be robust in testes as compared to ovaries, with hallmark expressions of key spermatogenic (Sox18, Amh and Dmrt1) and oogenic (Wntless, Foxl2, Cyp19a1a, Gdf9) genes. The transcripts identified for S. plagiostomus gonads showed significant phylogenetic resemblance with other freshwater teleost (or even mammalian) species, indicating a novel functional evolutionary convergence throughout vertebrate lineage as argued earlier127–129. To the best of our knowledge and belief, this is the first comprehensive report comparing the sex and season specific differential transcriptional atlas for any schizothoracine gonads. This study will serve as a potential data resource for investigating the regulatory genetic networks underlying the seasonal and sex specific gonadal maturation with gametogenic output of this ichthyofauna. However, further subsequent research is critically essential to reveal the mating-behaviour regulating the final gonadal maturation and spawning frequencies along with the environmental impact in this species at captivity, towards establishing a sustainable breeding program and/or conservation strategy.

Material and methods

Sample collection

Collection site & time

Adult male and female S. plagiostomus were obtained from wild/natural habitat of Garhwal Himalaya, Uttarakhand, India [Alaknanda river at Srinagar (30º13′28.83’'N and 78º47′55.03’'E) Nandakini river at Nandaprayag (30.3320ºN, 79.3205ºE)] and its vicinity during June-July (pre-spawning stage), September–October (spawning stage) and November–December (post-spawning stage) by the help of local fishermen. However, no captured fish was deployed for any live experimentation at laboratory condition. Live fishes were anesthetized by 2-phenoxyethanol (Ethylene glycol monophenyl ether, SRL-India with dose of 500 µl/L water) prior to dissecting the gonads.All fish handling and dissection were performed following the ethical approval under the guidelines provided by the Committee for the Purpose of Control and Supervision of the Experiments on Animals (CPCSEA), Govt. of India.

Species identification

Total 50–60 freshly captured Schizothorax fishes were obtained (for each month) and initially were screened for species identity (separating S. plagiostomus, Heckel,1838 from two sympatric sibling species S. richardsonii, Gray, 1832 and S. progastus, McClelland,1839) based on available morphometric features34,130,131 followed by DNA-barcoding using popular mitochondrial (mt) markers cytochrome C oxidase subunit I (CO-I) and cytochrome b (Cytb) as previously reported11.

Identification of maturational status

Identification of the maturational stage/status was performed as described previously for testis31 and ovary32 using Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) carried out with Morgagni 268D Transmission Electron Microscope (FeiCo, The Netherlands) at AIIMS, New Delhi, India.

Storage for RNA-seq

Gonadal tissues (from atleast three individual fishes (sharing closed/comparable GSI values for each maturational stage per sex) were pooled to make one biological set of samples and were saved in RNA later (Qiagen, 10 mg tissue/100 µl). The samples from pre-spawning testes (Y1 IA, Y2 IA, Y6 IA) and ovaries (X1 1 A, X2 1 A, X4 IA), spawning testes (Y1IIA, Y3IIA, Y4IIA) and ovaries (X1 2 A, X2 2 A and X3 IIA) and post-spawning testes (Y1IIIB, Y1IIIC, Y1IIIB_2) and ovaries (X1 3 A, X4 IIIA, X4 IIIA2) were stored at −20ºC (short term) −80ºC (long term) prior to transcriptome sequencing performed at Redcliffe life sciences, Delhi, India.

RNA extraction, cDNA library preparation and sequencing

Three biological sets (pooled samples) from each stage (pre-spawning, spawning and post-spawning) were processed for total RNA extraction with Trizol reagent (Sigma). RNA concentration and integrity were evaluated with Qubit 4.0 Fluorimeter and Agilent TapeStation 4150, respectively. Samples having A260/A280 absorption ratios within range of 1.9–2.1 and RNA integrity number (RIN) 6.5 or above were selected for cDNA library preparation. The prepared library quality was assessed on an Agilent Bioanalyzer DNA Chip. High quality RNA-Seq libraries were subsequently sequenced (2 × 150 bp) at Nova-Seq 6000 V I 6.5 using Illumina software followed by generation of FASTQ files.

Data filtering and curating thede novoassembly

Raw data obtained from each sample was subjected to quality check using Fastp and then were trimmed and adaptor sequences were removed. Thereafter, the reads were selected for de novo assembly by using Trinity (v2.8.5)132 by pooling the datasets. We screened the transcriptome assembly using Kaiju for contaminants using the nr + euk set from the web-server and prepared a repertoire of protein sequences from common carp, goldfish, rohu, gold-line barbel, silver crucian carp, chilin fish, tiger barb and cavefish from sequences downloaded from NCBI. Priority weights were assigned to fishes to resolve ambiguous flagging of transcripts in case of a match to both the fish proteome and nr + euk. Contaminant free transcripts were then selected for further analyses. Unclassified sequences (i.e. sequences with no hits to fishes or nr + euk database), were then subjected to continuous and discontinuous megablast with ncRNA sequences from the above listed fish species using the R package metablastr reporting top 100 hits and filtered for a bitscore of 50. The contaminant free and potential ncRNA transcripts were passed through the evidential-gene pipeline (trc2aacds4) to remove redundant transcripts with a heterozygosity factor 5 as well as trncrna pipeline and obtain the total transcriptome of S. plagiostomus gonads by combining the “okay” and “okalt” sets of transcripts. We assigned transcripts to gene clusters with grouper using discontinuous megablast with goldfish CDS as a labelling information and salmon quantifications. Transcripts were annotated using the goldfish proteome from uniport using a blastx analyses with a threshold expectation value of 0.01 to identify GO and KEGG annotation.

Differential expression and pathway analyses

Transcript-clusters with less than 10 counts across the samples to yield a total of 81,028 transcript-clusters as an input for Differential Expression testing. The differential expression analyses was performed using DESeq2 version 1.38.0 in R 4.2.2. Genes with an absolute logFC > 1 and Benjamini–Hochberg corrected p-values < 0.05 were termed as differentially expressed133–138. Pathway analyses were performed in cluster Profiler’s Universal Enrichment paradigm136–138. Heatmaps and boxplots for visualizing contrasting expression patterns were performed in the R packages complex Heatmap and ggpubr packages. Each transcript level expression shown in heatmaps, boxplots were based on the variance stabilizing transformation DEseq2 (VST) values138.

Data representation and statistical analyses

The TEM images shown in this study was a representation of at least three independent samples (for all stages and sex) performed on different calendar dates and each image was captured from 10 random fields per sample. For RNA-seq, one biological set of data for each maturational stage per sex has been generated from atleast six pooled gonads from three-individual fishes having closest/comparable gonadal somatic index (GSI) values. Three of such biological sets were prepared on different calendar dates and were deployed to interpret the data. Statistical analyses of data were performed on the non-parametric Student’s t-test for comparison between two groups or one-way ANOVA analysis of variance followed by Dunnett’s stest for comparison between multiple groups. Statistical tests were carried out by using GraphPas Prism 5.01 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, Calif., USA). P-values ˂0.05 were considered as statistically significant. For Fig. 8, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test or the Mann–Whitney U test, with the significance level/p-value ˂0.05 have been performed.

Limitations of the study

There has been a scarcity in regular availability/supply of live fish capturing. Therefore despite sincere attempts the authors failed to perform the revalidation of the selected enriched genes by RT-qPCR analyses. However, few genes have been revalidated by semi-quantitative RT-PCR and already published, link- 10.18311/jer/2024/35089.
