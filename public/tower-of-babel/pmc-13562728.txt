Biochar as an integrated management tool against Phytophthora cinnamomi, a key driver of Quercus suber decline
Authors: Manuel Trindade, Giovanni Del Frari, Henrique Ribeiro, Ricardo Ferreira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPortugal has over one-third of the Quercus suber L. world area, a natural treasure threatened by a progressive decline, which has been witnessed during the last decades by increasing tree mortality, where Phytophthora cinnamomi Rands is considered the main cause. Biochar has proven to be a product of great interest for application in agriculture due to its benefits in terms of soil chemical and physical properties. There is also evidence of phytosanitary status improvement, including P. cinnamomi, namely inducing resistance in plants. Three biochars of different origins were tested under lab and greenhouse conditions: one prepared from Acacia spp., another from a mixture of woody plant species, and a third from macroalgae Oedogonium intermedium Wittr. The aim was to evaluate the biochars effect on root rot disease caused by P. cinnamomi infection testing their ability to inhibit P. cinnamomi mycelium and reproductive structures and on young Q. suber plants. Generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs) identified dose-dependent biochar responses, with Acacia at 1% and wood_mix at 5% (w/w) emerging as optimal concentrations. These treatments markedly suppressed P. cinnamomi reproductive structures (p < 0.001), promoted cork oak plant growth (root recovery 79–80% above pathogen-challenged controls), and maintained favorable soil chemical parameters, representing a compelling integration of pathogen suppression with plant health benefit. The possibility that biochar may act as a duplibiotic in relation to soil microbiota is discussed. Our observations allow us to conclude that biochar could be an option to include in an integrated strategy plan to control the root rot disease in cork oak caused by P. cinnamomi.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-52847-3.Keywords: Cork oak, Acacia, Oedogonium, Duplibiotic, Hormesis, Soil amendmentSubject terms: Ecology, Microbiology, Plant sciences, Ecology

Introduction

Phytophthora cinnamomi Rands is among the most destructive soil-borne pathogens affecting Mediterranean forest ecosystems, with Quercus suber L. (cork oak) being particularly susceptible. Cork oak woodlands are both ecologically and economically indispensable, supporting exceptional Mediterranean biodiversity while sustaining activities such as cork production, livestock grazing, and a wide range of ecosystem services1. Consequently, they hold substantial environmental and economic significance across the Mediterranean basin, especially in Portugal, which contains more than one-third of the global cork oak area (736,000 ha) and stands as the world’s leading producer and exporter (APCOR,2.

Increasing Q. suber mortality has been reported in Portugal over the past decades, with affected trees exhibiting a progressive decline. Numerous authors have linked cork oak decline to P. cinnamomi, widely regarded as the primary driver of this decline syndrome in southern Europe, particularly across the Iberian Peninsula3,4 and representing a particularly urgent challenge.

The complex life cycle of this pathogen, together with the limited number of effective agrochemicals currently authorized for the control of Phytophthora spp., with phosphonates remaining the most prominent5, highlights the urgent need for research aimed at identifying chemical compounds and/or biological agents with robust antagonistic activity against oomycetes in general and P. cinnamomi in particular. This effort must be guided by a strong ecological imperative, especially considering the human activities intrinsically associated with these forest ecosystems, and should prioritize the development of ecologically compatible disease suppression strategies.

Biochar has emerged as a promising tool capable of simultaneously advancing disease suppression and soil restoration. It is a carbon-rich, highly porous material produced through the pyrolysis of organic feedstocks at 200–900 °C under limited oxygen conditions6. Its physicochemical properties, including its high specific surface area, collectively confer a range of environmental benefits, particularly in agricultural systems and in the broader context of climate change mitigation7.

Biochar amendments influence soil properties particularly relevant to oomycete pathogen dynamics.

From a physical standpoint, biochar increases soil porosity8, providing microsites for disease-suppressive microorganisms while enhancing water retention9 and sorption behaviour10. These properties directly influence zoospore motility and chemotaxis, key processes underpinning P. cinnamomi root infection11,12. Moreover, reductions in bulk density8,13 and improvements in soil structure and aggregation14–17 reshape water distribution within the soil matrix, thereby affecting zoospore dispersal and survival. Chemically, biochar modifies cation dynamics and enhances cation exchange capacity8,18. It also influences redox properties19,20, which can affect pathogen metabolism, and buffers soil pH toward neutrality, often exerting liming effects8,21,22. This is particularly relevant given the pH-dependent virulence of P. cinnamomi, which grows optimally at pH 5.0–6.5 and shows markedly reduced pathogenicity under alkaline conditions. Higher soil electrical conductivity changes the ionic balance of the rhizosphere, potentially influencing the behaviour of oomycete zoospores23.

These combined modifications of the soil matrix indirectly shape microbial traits including fitness and reproductive capacity24–26 and ultimately modulate soil microbial community structure27 toward disease-suppressive consortia. Biochar stimulates soil microbial activity, particularly of bacteria and fungi, even over short timescales28,29, enhancing beneficial microbial groups30,31 that compete with or antagonize pathogens, with notable effects on crucial rhizomicrobial communities32,33 that mediate plant-pathogen interactions at infection sites. Jin and colleagues34 demonstrated that these impacts operate through three synergistic mechanisms: providing microbial sanctuary within biochar’s porous matrix, supplying carbon and nitrogen to beneficial organisms, and modifying soil conditions (pH, aggregation, enzyme activity) that favor disease-suppressive microbial communities over pathogen proliferation.

Conversely, disease suppression by biochar appears to arise from a network of interconnected ecological processes rather than from any single mechanistic pathway. Consistent with this view, Bonanomi and colleagues 35 identified five key mechanisms: (i) induction of systemic resistance in host plants (ii) increased abundance of beneficial microorganisms, particularly mycorrhizal fungi that compete with pathogens for root colonization; (iii) improvements in soil quality that modulate nutrient-mediated plant defense; (iv) the release of fungitoxic compounds from the biochar matrix and (v) the sorption of phytotoxic allelopathic substances that would otherwise weaken host resistance. None of these explain on its own the reason why biochar inhibits growth of some fungi/oomycetes but promotes the growth of others. As biochar exhibits dual microbiome-modulating effects by both enhancing beneficial microbial activity and suppressing at least some pathogenic or competitive microorganisms we consider biochar consistent with the functional definition of a duplibiotic in relation to soil microbiota36.

Critically, these mechanisms interact, for instance, pH modification simultaneously affects pathogen growth directly while altering microbial community composition toward disease-suppressive consortia. This multifactorial action explains both biochar’s efficacy across diverse pathosystems and the high variability observed in disease suppression outcomes, as effectiveness depends on matching biochar properties to specific pathogen-host-soil contexts.

Evidence for biochar-mediated disease suppression spans diverse pathosystems but reveals critical gaps. Bonanomi and colleagues35 documented suppression of fungal genera (Fusarium, Rhizoctonia, Botrytis), bacteria (Ralstonia solanacearum), and oomycetes including Pythium aphanidermatum. However, their review identified only two woody species-oomycete systems: Phytophthora cactorum in Acer rubrum and P. cinnamomi in Quercus rubra. Iacomino and colleagues37 comprehensive analysis of 117 case studies revealed that merely 6% investigated oomycetes despite their ecological importance, with application rates (1–3%) showing unexplained variability in suppression efficacy. Importantly, both reviews emphasized that biochar properties, particularly feedstock type and pyrolysis temperature (optimal: 350–600 °C for woody feedstocks), are primary determinants of pathogen suppression. Yet virtually no studies systematically compare multiple biochar types within a single pathogen-host system, leaving fundamental questions unanswered: Do different feedstocks suppress the same pathogen through common or distinct mechanisms? Can biochar selection be optimized for specific forest pathosystems?

These knowledge gaps are particularly critical for Mediterranean forest conservation. Although P. cinnamomi poses a major threat to cork oak ecosystems, no comparative biochar studies have yet targeted this pathosystem. The ecological and economic stakes, including biodiversity conservation, soil protection, and the sustainability of cork production, demand evidence-based management tools that can be deployed at landscape scales.

Moreover, the high variability in biochar efficacy reported across studies suggests that successful implementation requires understanding which biochar properties drive suppression in specific ecological contexts.

Given these gaps, we evaluated whether different biochar feedstocks differentially suppress P. cinnamomi in the cork oak system. We compared three biochar types (Acacia, wood_mix and Oedogonium) at multiple concentrations, hypothesizing that woody-derived biochars would provide different pathogen suppression compared to algal biochar due to differences in structural properties and effects on disease-suppressive microbial communities. Our approach integrated in vitro assays quantifying biochar impacts on mycelial growth and reproductive structures (chlamydospores, sporangia and zoospores) with greenhouse experiments assessing disease development in infected young Q. suber trees. This comparative framework provides essential data for understanding feedstock-specific mechanisms of disease suppression and contributes evidence-based strategies for sustainable Mediterranean forest management.

Phytophthora cinnamomi isolate

In all in vitro and greenhouse trials included in this study the isolate PH1247 of P. cinnamomi, collected in 2012 from a cork oak stand in Montemor-o-Novo, Portugal, was used, as it demonstrated greater aggressiveness when compared to those isolates tested in a previous study38. In that study, isolates were obtained from symptomatic host tissues, their identity confirmed, and their pathogenic aggressiveness comparatively assessed under controlled conditions; PH1247 consistently showed the highest aggressiveness. The isolate is maintained in culture following standard collection procedures and routinely subcultured on potato dextrose agar (PDA) prior to use.

In vitro experiments

Laboratory experiments were conducted to evaluate the direct effect of biochar on both mycelial growth and the reproductive structures of P. cinnamomi. These in vitro assays aimed to assess pathogen responses under controlled conditions, as detailed in the following subsections.

A 5 mm P. cinnamomi-inoculated plug was placed into each Petri dish (50 mm diameter), and 10 mL of the corresponding SS was added for each treatment. The same procedure was carried out using a mixture of SS with clarified V8 broth (1:1). After 12 days of incubation at 24 °C ± 2 °C, the mycelial mass was dried in a drying oven for 48 h at 60 °C, and the respective dry weights were determined.

Effect of biochar on P. cinnamomi reproductive structures – chlamydospores and sporangia

The assay followed a completely randomized design including 15 treatments (three biochar types x five doses: 1, 2, 5, 10 and 20% w/w) plus one control (soil suspension without biochar), with five replicates per treatment (n = 80 dishes). Soil suspensions (SS) were non-autoclaved in this assay.

To determine the effect of biochar on the production of reproductive structures of P. cinnamomi, mycelial growth was first induced in liquid culture for 7 days at 24 °C ± 2 °C by placing a 5 mm diameter P. cinnamomi-inoculated plug in each 50 mm Petri dish containing 10 mL of clarified V8 broth. After 7 days, the mycelium was washed with sterile distilled water and then transferred to a new Petri dish containing 10 mL of non-autoclaved SS corresponding to each treatment (as described in Sect. 2.3.1).

Plates were randomized in the incubator (24 °C ± 2 °C, dark conditions) and incubated for 5 days, after which chlamydospores and sporangia were counted within 30 mm² of mycelial area by selecting thirty non-overlapping fields per dish using stratified random sampling to ensure balanced coverage of both central and peripheral mycelial regions. Counting was performed blind to treatment.

Effect of biochar on P. cinnamomi reproductive structures – zoospores

This assay constituted a separate in vitro experiment focused on zoospore production. Following the same protocol described in Sect. 2.3.2 for obtaining P. cinnamomi cultures in liquid medium, zoospore concentration was quantified by performing ten Neubauer chamber counts per Petri dish (BRAND®, Germany) after inducing zoospore release by thermal shock. The same biochar treatment structure described above was applied, consisting of 3 biochar types x 5 concentrations + control with five Petri dishes per treatment as replicate.

Effect of biochar on P. cinnamomi reproductive structures – zoospores growing inhibition

The influence of different SS_biochar treatments on zoospore germination was also evaluated. First, a procedure similar to the one described in 2.3.2. was followed to obtain P. cinnamomi mycelial mass in a Petri dish. After this period, the culture medium was replaced with an SS, prepared as described in Sect. 2.3.1., following the washing of the mycelium with sterile distilled water.

After five days, zoospore production was induced by thermal shock, resulting in a zoospore suspension adjusted to a concentration of 105 mL− 1. The microplate assay was conducted in two versions. In the first version, 160 µL of each SS_Biochart_[Biochart]j (t= Acacia, wood_mix, Oedogonium; j = 1%, 2%, 5%, 10%, 20% w/w) and 80 µL of the zoospore solution were pipetted into each well, with 16 replicates per treatment; for the control, 160 µL of each SS and 80 µL of the zoospore solution were used. In the second version, 80 µL of PDB (potato dextrose broth) (Difco Laboratories, Detroit, MI, USA), 80 µL of each SS_Biochart_[Biochart]j (t= Acacia, wood_mix, Oedogonium; j = 1%, 2%, 5%, 10%, 20% w/w), and 80 µL of the zoospore solution were pipetted into each well, again with 16 replicates per treatment; for the control, 80 µL of PDB, 80 µL of each SS, and 80 µL of the zoospore solution were used.

The microplates were incubated at 24 °C ± 2 °C in the dark, and evaluations were performed 24 h later by measuring absorbance at 600 nm using a microplate reader (Biotek Synergy HT Multi-Mode Microplate Reader, Winooski, Vermont, USA).

Greenhouse experiment

To assess the putative role that biochar may play in the Phytophthora - Quercus suber pathosystem we set up a greenhouse experiment, using Q. suber plants provided by Mata Nacional de Valverde, ICNF nursery (Instituto da Conservação da Natureza e das Florestas). Sixteen-month-old plants were transplanted into pots (11 cm top edge, 8 cm base edge, 27 cm height; ca. 2.5 L) containing peat-based substrate amended with biochar at 1%, 5%, or 10% (w/w), using three biochar types, according to the following experimental design:

Biochar treatments

2 biochar types (Acacia, wood mix) x 3 concentrations (1%, 5%, 10% w/w).

1 biochar type (Oedogonium) x 2 concentrations (1%, 5% w/w).

For each biochar x concentration combination: 12 inoculated plants + 12 non-inoculated plants.

Control: Substrate without biochar (SS_control) – 12 inoculated plants + 12 non-inoculated plants.

Overall structure: 8 biochar treatments + 1 control; two inoculation levels: inoculated versus non-inoculated; 12 plants per inoculation level in each treatment/control.

Potted plants were grown in peat substrate amended with biochar for 15 months, allowing interaction between plant roots (and associated microbiome) and the amended substrate. Following this, infection with Phytophthora was carried out using a mixture of vermiculite and V8 broth. To prepare the inoculum, 12-day-old cultures of the P. cinnamomi isolate PH1247 grown on potato dextrose agar (PDA) (Difco Laboratories, Detroit, MI, USA) were cut into plugs and mixed with the vermiculite/V8 broth, using one Petri dish per liter of mixture. The final mixture was incubated at 24 °C ± 2 °C for a three-week period, after which P. cinnamomi mycelium developed throughout the vermiculite substrate base, forming a uniform mass of vermiculite and mycelium. To the substrate of each potted plant, a volume of this inoculum mass corresponding to approximately 10% of the pot volume was added, marking the inoculation point from which the infection process would proceed naturally.

The greenhouse was equipped with an automated environmental control system comprising ventilation (two fans), air conditioning, and evaporative cooling. Temperature was maintained between 18 and 24 °C under natural daylight for the duration of the experiment. Irrigation was monitored and adjusted according to seasonal conditions to ensure adequate substrate moisture. Plants received controlled-release granular NPK fertilizer on three occasions: at transplanting, seven months later, and two weeks prior to pathogen inoculation.

After 10 months of plant–pathogen interaction, both inoculated and non-inoculated plants were harvested, and the presence of P. cinnamomi in root and stem tissues was assessed by reisolation using PARPHN selective medium41. Root and stem portions were collected from all greenhouse plants using a consistent sampling protocol: approximately 3 cm of terminal fine roots, 3 cm of secondary roots adjacent to the main root, and 1 cm from the collar region.

Following the completion of the 25-month greenhouse trial (15 months of plant-biochar interaction prior to inoculation, followed by 10 months of simultaneous plant-biochar-pathogen interaction) we proceeded with three analyses: measurement of stem height and diameter and measurement of roots dry weight after 5 days in a chamber at 60 °C.

The greenhouse trial tested three concentrations per biochar type (1%, 5% and 10% w/w for Acacia and wood_mix), representing low, intermediate and high application rates. Testing the full in vitro concentration range (1–20% w/w) was not feasible given the substantially greater quantities of biochar and greenhouse space required per treatment. Oedogonium was tested at two concentrations only (1% and 5% w/w), as the dry biomass supplied by collaborating researchers was insufficient to prepare the substrate volume required for the 10% (w/w) treatment.

In the greenhouse experiment, biochar concentrations refer to weight/weight ratios within the growing substrate: Acacia at 2% (w/w) indicates that 2% of the total substrate weight consists of Acacia biochar, with the remaining 98% being the base growing medium.

Chemical analyses carried out in parallel

To better understand the effect of the levels of various elements on the biochars, the following chemical analyses were also performed:

For elements Na, K, Ca, Mg, P, S, Fe, Cu, Zn, Mn, B, Cr, Ni, Cd and Pb determination, 0.20 g of each material sample (biochars, subtrate, sandy soil) was digested in a mix of concentrated HCl (12 mL) and HNO3 (4 mL) at 105 °C in a block digestion system (Digipress MS, SCP Science, Baie-d’Urfé, QC, Canada) following an adapted version of the CSN EN 13,650. After digestion, the elements were quantified by ICP-OES (Inductively Coupled Plasma – Optical Emission Spectroscopy) (iCAP 7000 Series ICP Spectrometer, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and their concentration were expressed as mg kg− 1.

In each of the SSs, controls, and SSs with the various concentrations of the three biochars, pH and electrical conductivity were measured at room temperature (22 °C) in a Thermo Scientific Orion™ 3 Star™ Benchtop pH Meter and in a Thermo Scientific Orion™ Star™ 212 Conductivity Benchtop Meter, respectively.

Statistical analysis

The following response variables were subjected to statistical analysis: mycelial dry weight, chlamydospore counts, sporangia counts, zoospore production, zoospore density (absorbance 600 nm), stem diameter, stem height, and root dry weight. Complementary non-parametric and parametric frameworks were employed: initial Kruskal-Wallis tests with Dunn´s post-hoc comparisons provided distribution-free exploratory analysis (results in Supplementary Figs. 1–6). To enhance statistical rigor, we additionally implemented Generalized Linear Mixed Models (GLMMs) with distributions appropriate to each response variable (negative binomial for count data, Gaussian for continuous measurements), accommodating experimental design structure and providing precise effect size estimates through Dunnett contrasts against control. Both analytical approaches yielded highly concordant results (Supplementary Table: STable_18; Spearman ρ = 0.971, p < 0.001), validating conclusions as robust across methodological frameworks. GLMM results are presented as primary analysis due to enhanced precision, with Kruskal-Wallis visualizations retained in Supplementary Information. Treatment effects are displayed through dose-response curves (biological patterns) and forest plots (statistical inference with 95% CI). For log-transformed parameters, effect sizes represent multiplicative changes. Significance: * p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01, *** p < 0.001. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software42 and and R Statistical Software (v R 4.3.0; R Core Team,43. Generalized Linear Mixed Models were fitted using lme444, with post-hoc pairwise comparisons performed using emmeans45.

Results

In vitro trials

Effects of biochar on mycelial dry weight: concentration-dependent suppression

Biochar amendments significantly affected P. cinnamomi mycelial growth, with effects varying by biochar origin, concentration, and medium nutritional status (Figs. 2 and 3; SFigure 1). ANOVA revealed highly significant main effects of biochar type (SS: F3, 83 = 8.234, p < 0.001; SS + V8: F3,83 = 7.453, p < 0.001), concentration (SS: F1,83 = 169.876, p < 0.001; SS + V8: F1,83 = 89.341, p < 0.001), and biochar x concentration interactions (SS: F3,83 = 2.989, p = 0.035; SS + V8: F3,83 = 4.119, p = 0.009; Stable 1), with models explaining 73% and 64% of variance respectively. The triple interaction (biochar x concentration x medium: F3,166 = 6.080, p < 0.001) confirmed fundamental modulation by nutritional conditions.

Acacia biochar demonstrated strongest suppression across media. In SS medium (control: 2.58 ± 0.33 mg), GLMM Dunnett contrasts revealed significant reductions at all concentrations (1–20% w/w, all p ≤ 0.002; STable 2), with maximum suppression at 2% (-1.483 ± 0.188 mg, 57% inhibition, p < 0.001; Fig. 3b). Dose-response analysis showed peak efficacy at 2–5% followed by gradual attenuation (Fig. 3a; SFigure 1a–b). Kruskal-Wallis analyses corroborated these findings, identifying Acacia_2% and Acacia_5% as significantly different from control and Oedogonium biochar treatments (p < 0.05; SFigure 1a–b). In SS + V8 (control: 30.82 ± 4.12 mg), suppression remained significant at 1–10% concentrations (-3.180 to -2.420 mg, p ≤ 0.012) but declined at 20% (p = 0.344; Fig. 3c–d; SFigure 1c–d; STable 2). Relative growth rate calculations confirmed Acacia biochar as delivering the highest inhibition rates (> 50%) in SS medium (SFigure 1b, d).

Wood_mix biochar suppressed mycelial growth consistently across concentrations in SS (-0.683 to -1.003 mg, 26–39% inhibition, all p ≤ 0.007; Fig. 3a–b; STable 2), exhibiting a flat dose-response pattern indicating concentration-insensitive efficacy (Fig. 3a; SFigure 1a). In SS + V8, significant reductions occurred at 2%, 10%, and 20% (-2.510 to -2.230 mg, p ≤ 0.029; Fig. 3c–d; SFigure 1c–d; STable 2). Kruskal-Wallis analysis validated wood_mix biochar´s reliable suppression distinguishable from Oedogonium stimulatory effects (p < 0.05; SFigure 1a, c).

Oedogonium biochar exhibited classic hormetic behavior in SS: significant stimulation at 1–2% (w/w) (+ 0.918 mg, + 36%, p < 0.001; Fig. 3a–b; SFigure 1a–b; STable 2) followed by neutralization at higher concentrations (+ 0.078 to + 0.358 mg, all p > 0.05). Both Kruskal-Wallis and GLMM consistently identified this biphasic pattern (p < 0.05 for comparisons with Acacia/wood_mix biochars; Fig. 3; SFigure 1a; STable 2). In SS + V8, hormesis was dramatically attenuated, with only modest non-significant effects at 1–5% and marginal suppression at 10–20% (-2.070 mg, p = 0.055; Fig. 3c–d; SFigure 1c–d; STable 2). Relative growth calculations showed 36% stimulation in SS versus only 3.3% in SS + V8 (SFigure 1b, d).

Effects of biochar on reproductive structures: chlamydospore and sporangia production

Biochar amendments profoundly influenced reproductive structure formation with distinct patterns for chlamydospores versus sporangia (Fig. 4; SFigure 2; STables 3–6).

For chlamydospore production (control: 182.2 ± 76.1 per 30 mm²), ANOVA revealed highly significant biochar type effects (χ23 = 45.041, p < 0.001) and biochar × concentration interaction (χ22 = 7.964, p = 0.019; STable 3), confirming biochar-specific dose-response patterns. Acacia biochar demonstrated the strongest suppression with significant reductions at all concentrations (1–20% w/w, all p ≤ 0.035; Fig. 4a–b; STable 4), achieving maximum 88% reduction at 20% (-2.132 ± 0.279, p < 0.001), in precise agreement with Kruskal-Wallis calculations (88.1% inhibition; SFigure 2b).

Wood_mix biochar showed significant suppression at 1–10% (68–83% reduction, p < 0.001) but loss of efficacy at 20% (p = 0.137), a unique U-shaped pattern validated by both methods (Fig. 4a–b; SFigure 2a–b; STable 4).

Oedogonium biochar exhibited consistent suppression across all concentrations (56–76% reduction, all p ≤ 0.024), with relatively flat dose-response indicating concentration-independent efficacy (Fig. 4a–b; SFigure 2a-b; STable 4).

For sporangia production (control: 1329.4 ± 454.5 per mm2), concentration emerged as the dominant factor (χ21 = 9.992, p = 0.002; STable 5), rather than biochar type (χ23 = 5.087, p = 0.166; STable 5), with dramatic threshold effects at 20% concentration. All three biochars showed lower effects at low-intermediate concentrations but highly significant suppression exclusively at 20%: Acacia biochar 97% reduction (-3.395 ± 0.854, p < 0.001), wood_mix biochar 98% (-3.812 ± 0.856, p < 0.001) and Oedogonium biochar 99% (-4.359 ± 0.858, p < 0.001; Fig. 4c–d; SFigure 2c–d; STable 6). Both Kruskal-Wallis (p = 0.011 at 20% for all biochars; SFigure 2c) and GLMM analyses consistently validated this all-or-nothing threshold response.

High concordance between analytical frameworks (STables 3–6; SFigs. 2 and 4) validated structure-specific sensitivities: chlamydospores responding to biochar-specific properties with varying dose-responses, sporangia requiring critical threshold concentrations (20%) regardless of biochar type. Comprehensive analyses including negative binomial overdispersion parameters (theta = 3.93 for chlamydospores, 0.49 for sporangia) are detailed in STables 3 and 5.

Biochar amendments dramatically suppressed zoospore production with exceptional consistency across all treatments (Fig. 5; SFigure 3). The large sample size (n = 30 per treatment, 480 total observations) provided extraordinary statistical power. ANOVA revealed highly significant main effects of biochar type (χ23 = 213.024, p < 2 × 10− 16) and concentration (χ21 = 147.934, p < 2 × 10− 16; STable 7), with non-significant interaction (χ22 = 3.646, p = 0.162), indicating that their effects were statistically independent under our experimental conditions.

Greenhouse experiment

Successful re-isolation of P. cinnamomi from inoculated plant tissues validated effective pathogen inoculation and tissue infection at the end of the experiment.

Stem diameter: insensitive to biochar-mediated protection

Stem diameter measurements revealed minimal biochar effects and negligible disease impacts, contrasting sharply with pronounced protection observed in height and root biomass (Fig. 7a-b, e; SFigure 5a-b). ANOVA demonstrated predominantly non-significant treatment effects with poor model fit (R2 = 0.104 non-inoculated, 0.085 inoculated; STables 11–12), indicating diameter as an insensitive parameter for evaluating biochar efficacy in root pathogen systems. Both Kruskal-Wallis and linear models yielded concordant outcomes, consistently identifying diameter as poorly responsive to biochar amendments and disease pressure.

In non-inoculated plants (control: 6.75 ± 0.36 mm), biochar produced minimal effects with no significant biochar type effect (F2,135 = 2.087, p = 0.128) and only marginal concentration significance (F2,135 = 3.339, p = 0.038; STable 11) and no biochar x concentration interaction (F3,135 = 0.851, p = 0.469; STable 11). P. cinnamomi inoculation produced minimal detectable impact on stem diameter: inoculated control plants (6.62 ± 0.31 mm) showed only 2% reduction versus non-inoculated controls (6.75 mm), a non-significant difference (Mann-Whitney p = 0.736). ANOVA on inoculated plants revealed no significant biochar effects (F2,135 = 0.126, p = 0.881), with the model explaining only 8.5% of the variance (STable 11). The Kruskal-Wallis test identified only marginal within-biochar differences (Acacia_1% > Acacia_10%, p = 0.014; SFigure 5b), with dose-response patterns remaining flat (Fig. 7b).

The stark contrast with height responsiveness, diameter R2 = 0.085 and p = 0.881 versus height R2 = 0.361 and p < 0.001 in inoculated plants (STable 13), demonstrates diameter´s fundamental unsuitability for evaluating biochar disease protection. Diameter is determined by early-stage secondary growth established before substantial disease impact, whereas height reflects ongoing resource-dependent apical activity. Relying solely on diameter would lead to erroneous conclusion that no biochar protection exists, thereby missing dramatic effects evident in height (e.g., Acacia_1%: 67.3 cm vs. diseased control 32.8 cm, a 105% increase). Concordance between Kruskal-Wallis and ANOVA consistently validated diameter´s insensitivity as a genuine biological phenomenon rather than an analytical artifact.

Stem height: substantial biochar-mediated disease protection

Stem height revealed profound biochar-mediated protection against P. cinnamomi, contrasting starkly with diameter insensitivity (Fig. 7c-d, f; SFigure 5c-d). ANOVA demonstrated highly significant biochar effects under disease pressure, explaining 36.1% of variance in inoculated plants (R2 = 0.361), 4-fold higher than diameter (R2 = 0.085), establishing height as far more sensitive indicator of disease impact and protection (STables 13–14). Both Kruskal-Wallis and linear models yielded highly concordant results, validating height as the optimal aboveground parameter.

In non-inoculated plants (control: 49.1 ± 3.85 cm), biochar produced variable effects with non-significant biochar type effects significant (F2,135 = 1.103, p = 0.335; STable 13) but significant concentration effects (F2,135 = 5.119, p = 0.007**) and biochar x concentration interaction (F3,135 = 2.961, p = 0.035*; STable 13), explaining 14.5% of variance. Wood_mix_1% achieved 60.6 cm (23% above control), significantly taller than Acacia_10% (p < 0.05; SFigure 5c), though no treatments differed significantly from control. Dose-response patterns diverged: wood_mix exhibited U-shaped response (60.6 cm at 1%, declining to 36.7 cm at 5%, recovering to 43.2 cm at 10%), Acacia biochar showed declining trend (44.9 → 34.7 cm) and Oedogonium biochar remained stable (46–49 cm; Fig. 7c).

Disease pressure transformed biochar effects dramatically. ANOVA revealed highly significant biochar type (F2,135 = 10.997, p < 0.001***), concentration (F2,135 = 10.284, p < 0.001***), and interaction effects (F3,135 = 8.450, p < 0.001***; STable 13), explaining 36.1% of variance, the highest among aboveground parameters. P. cinnamomi produced severe height suppression in controls (32.8 ± 2.33 cm inoculated vs. 49.1 cm non-inoculated, 33% reduction).

Acacia_1% achieved spectacular protection: 67.3 ± 3.28 cm, 105% taller than diseased controls (32.8 cm) and remarkably 50% taller than Acacia_1% non-inoculated plants (44.9 cm). Kruskal-Wallis confirmed Acacia_1% significantly exceeded diseased controls and all other treatments except highest performers (p < 0.05; SFigure 5d), validating this as the most effective treatment. This remarkable pattern, where diseased+biochar plants exceed healthy+biochar plants, suggests not merely protection but synergistic growth stimulation under pathogen stress. Dose-response showed striking peak at 1% (67.3 cm) followed by dramatic decline at higher concentrations (41.6 cm at 5%, 43.7 cm at 10%), representing a 38% reduction from 1% optimum, evident in both ANOVA interaction (p < 0.001) and Kruskal-Wallis trends (Fig. 7d; SFigure 5d).

Wood_mix biochar provided moderate protection across 1–10% (39.1–44.1 cm, 19–34% above diseased controls), optimal at 5% (44.1 cm), with relatively flat dose-response (Fig. 7d). While significantly better than diseased controls, Wood_mix remained inferior to Acacia_1% (p < 0.05; SFigure 5d). Oedogonium biochar demonstrated concentration-dependent efficacy: Oedogonium_1% achieved 45.2 cm (38% above diseased controls), but Oedogonium_5% collapsed to 33.2 cm, essentially equivalent to diseased controls (32.8 cm), indicating a complete loss of protective capacity and potential phytotoxicity (SFigure 5d; STable 13).

Concordance between Kruskal-Wallis and ANOVA validated: (i) dramatic disease impact in controls (-33%), (ii) Acacia_1% providing spectacular protection (67.3 cm, + 105% vs. diseased controls, + 50% vs. healthy Acacia_1%), (iii) biochar-specific dose-responses with Acacia biochar peaking at 1% and declining thereafter, (iv) Oedogonium_5% providing no protection and (v) height being far superior to diameter for detecting protection (R2 0.36 vs. 0.9, biochar p < 0.001 vs. p = 0.881). Height’s superiority reflects continuous resource-dependent apical growth integrating disease impact cumulatively over 15 months, whereas diameter reflects early-determined structural allocation.

It should be noted that initial plant height was not recorded at transplanting; however, all plants were sourced from the same nursery batch and assigned to treatments following a completely randomized design, minimising the potential confounding effect of initial size variability.

Roots dry weight: definitive evidence of biochar protection

Root biomass provided the most definitive evidence of biochar protection, revealing effects that aboveground parameters substantially underestimated (Fig. 8a, b,c; SFigure 6). As the direct target organ of this root pathogen, root biomass captured disease impact and protection with exceptional clarity, yielding the highest model fit among all parameters (R2 = 0.710 for inoculated and 0.678 for non-inoculated; STables 15–17). ANOVA demonstrated highly significant biochar and concentration effects under disease pressure with critical biochar x concentration interaction revealing biochar-specific optimal rates. Both Kruskal-Wallis and linear models yielded highly concordant results, validating root biomass as optimal parameter and establishing clear recommendations: Acacia biochar optimal at 1%, wood_mix biochar at 5%, Oedogonium biochar unsuitable due to severe phytotoxicity.

Wood_mix biochar demonstrated the most robust protection, maintaining efficacy across 1–5%. Wood_mix_5% achieved 146.62 ± 8.45 g, the highest among inoculated treatments, 12% above diseased controls (p = 0.588), representing 80% recovery to non-inoculated controls, the best recovery percentage across all treatments. Wood_mix_1% performed nearly identically (144.59 g, + 11%), indicating stable protective plateau. Wood_mix_10% declined to 120.52 g (-8%), suggesting that excessive concentrations reduce efficacy. Oedogonium biochar exhibited catastrophic concentration-dependent phytotoxicity. Oedogonium_1% inoculated achieved 135.00 g (+ 3.4% vs. diseased control, p = 0.991), providing minimal protection. At 5%, Oedogonium biochar produced catastrophic collapse: inoculated plants reached 72.83 ± 2.81 g, 44% below diseased controls (p < 0.001), representing only 40% of non-inoculated control biomass, the single worst outcome across the entire experimental matrix. Both ANOVA and Kruskal-Wallis identified Oedogonium_5% as catastrophically inferior (p < 0.001 vs. control, p < 0.05 vs. all wood_mix concentrations; STable 17; SFigure 6b). This contrasts starkly with its in vitro pathogen suppression (90–99% sporangia, 44–94% zoospores), illustrating that in vitro suppression is necessary but insufficient; biochar must be safe for plants, requiring greenhouse validation.

Root biomass superiority over stem parameters provides critical methodological insights (STables 15–17). In inoculated plants, roots explained 71.0% of variance (biochar p < 0.001, interaction p = 0.004), height explained 36.1% (biochar p < 0.001, interaction p < 0.001) and diameter only 8.5% (biochar p = 0.881, interaction p = 0.122). Disease impact manifested dramatically in roots (control: -28.7%, p < 0.001) and height (-33% in some treatments) but minimally in diameter (control: -2%, p > 0.73), confirming roots as the primary disease target and the most informative response variable.

Concordance between Kruskal-Wallis and ANOVA validated: (i) dramatic disease impact (-28.7% root loss, p < 0.001), (ii) Acacia_1% and wood_mix_5% as best protective treatments (79–80% healthy biomass maintained), (iii) biochar-specific dose-responses (Acacia optimal at 1%, wood_mix optimal at 5%), (iv) Oedogonium_5% catastrophically toxic (72.83 g, p < 0.001) and (v) root biomass being far superior to stem parameters (R2 0.71 vs. 0.36 vs. 0.09). Root biomass superiority lies in its direct reflection of the disease site. P. cinnamomi colonizes roots causing necrosis and biomass loss directly quantified by root dry weight. Height integrates disease impact over time with lower sensitivity (R2 0.36). Diameter, determined before substantial disease progression, exhibits minimal response (R2 0.09) and fails as a protection endpoint.

The substantial protection observed for Acacia_1% and wood_mix_5% (79–80% healthy biomass maintained) validates in vitro findings and demonstrates successful laboratory-to-greenhouse translation. The significant interaction (p = 0.004; STable 15) confirms biochar-specific optimal concentrations: Acacia biochar peaking at 1% and declining at higher rates, wood_mix biochar maintains protection across 1–5% peaking at 5%, and Oedogonium biochar exhibits severe toxicity at all concentrations. Field recommendations emerge clearly: (i) Acacia at 1% for P. cinnamomi management providing 79% recovery, (ii) wood_mix at 5% providing 80% recovery with broader concentration tolerance, (iii) explicit avoidance of Oedogonium due to severe phytotoxicity (-47% healthy biomass, -44% diseased biomass at 5%), (iv) mandatory greenhouse screening for both pathogen suppression and plant safety.

Discussion

Biochar amendments reveal dose-dependent mechanisms in Phytophthora root rot mitigation

Our results demonstrate that biochar amendments can significantly enhance cork oak resilience to P. cinnamomi, with efficacy critically dependent on feedstock type, application rate, and their interaction with the plant. The most striking finding, 105% height increase and 79% root recovery with Acacia biochar at 1% (w/w), positions this treatment as the most promising candidate for cork oak protection. Wood_mix biochar exhibited robust performance at 5% (w/w), delivering 80% root recovery, a notably different optimal concentration that reflects intrinsic feedstock properties rather than a universal dose-response pattern. Optimal performance emerges from a narrow concentration window where soil physicochemical improvements coincide with direct pathogen antagonism, while excessive dosages trigger phytotoxicity that overwhelms any protective benefits.

These findings are broadly consistent with the application range of 1–3% (w/w) identified by Bonanomi and colleagues35 for oomycete control, and with previous work demonstrating biochar’s capacity to alleviate Phytophthora-induced stress in woody hosts: Q. robur inoculated with P. cinnamomi and Acer rubrum inoculated with P. cactorum both showed reduced disease progression following biochar amendment at 5% (v/v)46. In tobacco systems, biochar combined with Bacillus subtilis effectively controlled P. nicotianae through pathogen inhibition, soil nutrient improvement and rhizomicrobial community shifts33,47, highlighting that biochar interactions with other biological elements may be key to maximizing field efficacy. Conversely, biochar did not suppress Phytophthora root rot in highbush blueberry32, and in potato late blight caused by P. infestans, a single biochar application failed to significantly reduce disease incidence, instead enriching the rhizosphere with beneficial Rhizobium48. These contrasting outcomes reinforce that neither optimal concentration nor the mechanism of biochar influence on the pathogen can be generalized across host-pathogen systems.

Mechanistic interpretation: dose-response patterns and hormetic response in mycelium growth

In vitro tests clearly demonstrated that biochar acts as a disruptive element in P. cinnamomi mycelial development, with non-linear dose-response relationships observed across all biochar types underscoring a fundamental principle: more is not always better.

Classical hormesis, characterized by biphasic dose-response with low-dose stimulation and high-dose neutralization or inhibition (Agathokleous et al., 2019), was exclusively observed in mycelial dry weight measurements for Oedogonium biochar in SS medium. At a concentration of 1%, Oedogonium biochar stimulated P. cinnamomi mycelium growth above control levels (≈ 3.5 mg vs. 2.58 mg), representing genuine low-dose enhancement. However, at concentrations of 5–20%, mycelium weight returned to baseline control levels (≈ 2.6–2.9 mg), demonstrating high-dose neutralization rather than suppression. This biphasic pattern constitutes true hormesis and was not observed with Acacia or wood_mix biochars, both of which exhibited consistent mycelial suppression across all concentrations.

Notably, this hormetic response was medium-dependent, appearing only in the nutrient-limited SS medium and not in the enriched SS + V8 medium, where Oedogonium showed monotonic decline. The mechanistic basis for this hormesis likely involves sublethal stress responses: at low Oedogonium concentrations, mild alkalinization (pH ≈ 8) and modest elemental enrichment may trigger compensatory fungal growth responses, while higher concentrations impose sufficient physiological stress (pH > 9, EC > 0.5 mS/cm, excessive Na/K/Mg) to neutralize growth advantages. This hormetic pattern in mycelial growth did not extend to reproductive structures (chlamydospores, sporangia, zoospores), which showed distinct dose-response patterns dominated by threshold behaviors rather than hormesis, indicating that different P. cinnamomi life stages respond differently to biochar-mediated stress.

In vitro validation: medium-dependency and soil solution dynamics

The in vitro trials employed soil solutions (SS) as a reasonable approximation of the complex soil water environment in which biochar dissolution occurs over time. As biochar weathers and fractures through edaphoclimatic processes, water-mediated dissolution increases dissolved organic carbon, cations, anions, electrical conductivity and pH throughout the soil profile, extending biochar’s physicochemical influence beyond the point of initial application.

While the SS system more closely mimics natural soil solution conditions, the SS + V8 variant was intentionally designed as a nutrient-enriched environment to explore biochar-pathogen interactions under enhanced growth conditions for P. cinnamomi.

The artificially nutrient-enriched SS + V8 medium produced approximately tenfold higher average mycelial weight compared to SS alone, yet the overall inhibition trend was maintained for Acacia and wood_mix. This attenuated inhibition indicates that a nutrient-rich environment partially offsets biochar’s suppressive effects on P. cinnamomi, demonstrating that biochar efficacy is conditioned by the nutritional status of the growth medium. Oedogonium showed contrasting behaviour between media: promoting mycelial growth at all concentrations in SS, yet exhibiting inhibition at the two highest concentrations in SS + V8, consistent with the hypothesis that nutrient buffering modifies the biochar-pathogen interaction. A closely parallel trend was observed in the zoospore inhibition assay, where SS + V8 absorbance values were approximately tenfold higher, yielding lower inhibition rates that mirror the mycelial growth findings.

Physicochemical mediators: pH, electrical conductivity, and elemental composition

The physicochemical characteristics of soil solutions underlie many of the biological effects observed. P. cinnamomi prefers slightly acidic to neutral conditions (pH 5.0-6.5)49, with disturbances in zoosporogenesis reported above pH 750. This aligns with the observed zoospore inhibition, particularly at higher biochar concentrations where pH reached ≈ 8–9. Elevated EC additionally impairs zoospore motility and encystment through osmotic stress51, with values exceeding 0.5 mS/cm, as observed with Oedogonium at 10–20% (w/w), likely sufficient to trigger physiological disturbances. These physicochemical conditions may simultaneously create unfavourable conditions for plant roots and beneficial microorganisms, partially explaining the poor plant performance observed with Oedogonium in the greenhouse trial.

The elemental composition of the three biochars (Table 3) further contextualises these effects. Oedogonium biochar contains Na concentrations approximately ten times higher than the substrate and 300 times greater than typical sandy soils, raising concerns about phytotoxicity analogous to that reported for P. parasitica root rot under salinity stress in citrus52 and tomato53. Regarding phosphorus, a constituent of phosphonates, Wilkinson and colleagues54 observed that phosphite reduces viable zoospore production in infected Banksia grandis and Eucalyptus marginata, while Serrano and colleagues5 demonstrated that phosphonate efficacy against P. cinnamomi in Q. suber is significantly enhanced when combined with zinc and calcium. Although the specific chemical forms of the analysed elements remain unidentified, the exceptional concentrations of Na, Mg, K and P in Oedogonium biochar relative to the substrate and soil (Table 3) likely contribute substantially to the phytotoxic and anti-pathogenic effects observed at higher application rates.

Does biochar (sensu lato) act as a duplibiotic?

Our results are consistent with the conclusions of several other authors, as referenced in Sect. 1. (Introduction) and Sect. 4. (Discussion), where the detrimental effects of biochar on undesirable microorganisms, namely plant pathogens such as P. cinnamomi, are clarified. Section 1. also highlights, based on the review by Bonanomi and colleagues35, one of the proposed mechanisms underlying disease suppression: the enhancement of the abundance and activity of beneficial microbes, including mycorrhizal fungi, as supported by various authors (e.g., Lehmann30, Rillig16, Sales32, Warnock31.

Furthermore, the work by Rodríguez-Daza and colleagues36 demonstrates the capacity of (poly)phenols to promote beneficial gut bacteria through both their direct and synergistic utilization by microbes, as well as their inhibitory action against potential pathogenic species. These authors also propose the term duplibiotic to describe an unabsorbed substrate capable of modulating the gut microbiota through both antimicrobial and prebiotic modes of action.

In this context and considering that BLAD, a polypeptide with antifungal activity55, has recently been shown (in data currently under publication) to stimulate beneficial plant-associated microorganisms, this concept of duplibiotic may be suitably extended to BLAD in relation to the plant microbiota. Based on the arguments presented above, we also propose the potential application of the term duplibiotic to biochar sensu lato, with respect to its effects on soil microbiota.

Translating findings into integrated disease management strategies

The main conclusions are synthesized in Table 6, which summarizes the chemical properties and biological effects of Acacia, wood_mix, and Oedogonium biochars, highlighting their impact on pathogen suppression, plant growth, and soil chemistry (pH and EC). This study demonstrates that biochar type and application rate are critical determinants of outcomes: all three biochars suppress P. cinnamomi reproductive structures and zoospore viability in a concentration-dependent manner, and this laboratory evidence aligns with greenhouse observations, reinforcing that suppression of pathogen reproductive structures directly influences protective effects on plant performance under inoculation stress. Acacia_1% and wood_mix_5% (w/w) consistently emerge as the best-performing treatments, effectively suppressing P. cinnamomi, supporting plant growth, and maintaining reasonable soil chemistry. In contrast, Oedogonium biochar presents a “double-edged sword”: effective at direct pathogen inhibition through chemical effects, but potentially harmful to plant growth; its use should be avoided or restricted to concentrations below 2% (w/w) until further studies clarify safe application thresholds.

The inclusion of Acacia-derived biochar reflects a dual ecological purpose. With more than ten invasive Acacia species present in Portugal, utilizing this biomass as a raw material for a product recognized for its ecological benefits represents a valuable and sustainable strategy. Beyond improving plant nutrition and soil water-holding capacity, a critical factor in the montado ecosystem given the sandy soils characteristic of a large proportion of the montado area, this study explores biochar’s additional potential as a tool in disease suppression.

This study does not advocate the exclusive application of biochar as a P. cinnamomi control measure; the parameters evaluated fall short of full plant protection product efficacy. Nevertheless, given the severity of cork oak root rot caused by P. cinnamomi, arguably the most important component of montado decline, and the near-impossibility of eradication due to the pathogen´s long-term soil survival, resistant structures and water dispersal, all tools contributing to disease suppression are worthwhile when integrated strategically. Only through management strategies that integrate all available prevention and control measures, both established and emerging, can progress be made in combating P. cinnamomi in the cork oak ecosystem.

Translating greenhouse findings into field management requires careful consideration of practical, economic, and ecological factors. Target zones should prioritize areas most conducive to pathogen establishment: proximity to water courses, low-lying areas with poor drainage, shaded humid zones, and sites prone to waterlogging. A significant practical constraint is application methodology: mechanical incorporation of biochar into established montado stands risks root damage, making biochar amendment most pragmatic during reforestation of affected areas or as a preventive measure in new plantings.

Critical knowledge gaps remain regarding long-term effects under natural field conditions. While our 25-month greenhouse trial demonstrates biochar’s capacity to suppress the pathogen and support plant growth, interactions between biochar, native soil microbiomes, and P. cinnamomi populations over multi-year timeframes in heterogeneous field soils remain unexplored. Future research must address: (i) biochar persistence and sustained efficacy in montado soils, (ii) impacts on beneficial mycorrhizal associations and soil microbial communities, (iii) integration with other management practices such as resistant rootstocks and drainage improvement, and (iv) cost-benefit analyses under operational conditions. Only through such interdisciplinary field-scale studies can biochar´s role in integrated cork oak disease management be fully validated and operationalized.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that biochar amendment can significantly enhance cork oak resilience to Phytophthora cinnamomi, but efficacy is critically dependent on feedstock type and application rate. Among the materials tested, Acacia biochar at 1% (w/w) emerged as the most effective treatment, achieving 105% height increase and 79% root recovery relative to inoculated controls, while wood_mix biochar at 5% (w/w) provided robust and consistent protection across multiple parameters. These concentrations represent optimal windows where direct pathogen suppression, soil physicochemical improvement, and plant performance converge.

In vitro assays confirmed that all three biochars disrupt the P. cinnamomi life cycle in a concentration-dependent manner, suppressing mycelial growth, reproductive structures, and zoospore viability. The medium-dependent nature of these effects underscores that biochar efficacy is conditioned by the nutritional and physicochemical environment in which it operates. Oedogonium biochar, despite showing strong direct anti-pathogenic activity, demonstrated severe phytotoxicity at 5% (w/w), attributable to its marine-origin elemental composition; its application should be restricted to concentrations below 2% (w/w) until further safety thresholds are established.

Based on these findings and drawing on the concept proposed by Rodríguez-Daza and colleagues36, we propose that biochar sensu lato may function as a duplibiotic in the soil environment, simultaneously suppressing plant pathogens and promoting beneficial microbial communities. This dual mode of action positions biochar, particularly Acacia-derived biochar from invasive species, as a promising, ecologically sustainable component within integrated disease management strategies for cork oak ecosystems threatened by P. cinnamomi decline.
