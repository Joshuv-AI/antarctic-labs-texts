The complexity of being monitorable
Riccardo Camerlo, Francesco Dagnino

Abstract
. We study monitorable sets from a topological standpoint. In particular, we use descriptive set theory to describe the complexity of the family of monitorable sets in a countable space X X . When X X is second countable, we observe that the family of monitorable sets is 𝚷 3 0 \boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{3} and determine the exact complexities it can have. In contrast, we show that if X X is not second countable then the family of monitorable sets can be much more complex, giving an example where it is 𝚷 1 1 \boldsymbol{\Pi}^{1}_{1} -complete.

1.
Introduction

Runtime Verification (RV)
[
9
,
3
]

is a formal verification technique that, differently from others such as Model Checking,
focuses on the analysis of actual system executions, rather than on a model (that is, an abstraction) of it.
More precisely,
given a formal specification or property of the expected system behaviours,
one synthesises a
monitor
, that is, a piece of software that observes system executions and tries to establish whether they satisfy or violate the specification.
This makes RV a lightweight and scalable approach to formal verification at the price of losing exhaustiveness:
the verdict a monitor can reach is valid only for the executions it has observed, not for all possible ones.

Since in many cases monitors have to run together with the system they observe,
one of the key challenges in RV is to ensure that the monitor runs only if it has the possibility of eventually concluding a verdict, thus avoiding a waste of resources.
Unfortunately, one soon realises that there are properties whose associated monitor could end up in situations where, no matter what it observes, it would never be able to determine the satisfaction or the violation of the property.
This has led to the notion of
monitorability

[
10
]
.
Roughly, a property is said to be
monitorable

if we can synthesise a monitor which has always the possibility of eventually verifying the satisfaction or the violation of the property, that is,
the above situation cannot happen. (Actually, there various flavours of monitorability, depending on the operational guarantees one wants to obtain on monitors; we refer to
[
1
]
for a discussion of the whole spectrum.)

Monitorability is typically studied at the syntactic level separately for each specification language, see
e.g.
,
[
4
,
7
,
2
]
.
This has led to a scattered family of results with several concrete applications that share many similarities but lack a unifying picture.
In order to achieve a general and syntax independent treatment of monitorability,
Diekert and Leucker
[
6
]
propose to take a more abstract semantic perspective, studying monitorability with topological tools.
The key insight is that possible system behaviours can be viewed as points in a topological space where open subsets abstractly model the
observations
a monitor can perform.
Within this framework, a property is simply a subset of the space—
i.e.
, a set of valid behaviours.
Therefore, given a topological space
X
,
monitorable properties determine a subset
\mathcal{M}(X)\subseteq\mathcal{P}(X)
, where
\mathcal{P}(X)
denotes the powerset of
X
,
i.e.
, the collection of all properties on
X
.

The purpose of this paper is the study of the complexity of
\mathcal{M}(X)
as a subset of
\mathcal{P}(X)
when
X
is a countable space.
Indeed, when
X
is countable, its powerset
\mathcal{P}(X)
can be endowed with a topology homeomorphic to the Cantor space, so
one can use the tools and techniques of descriptive set theory
[
8
]
to determine the topological complexity of
\mathcal{M}(X)
.
We do this in the same vein as
[
11
]
, where Todorčević and Uzcátegui studied the complexity of topologies as subsets of
\mathcal{P}(X)
.

3.
Monitorable sets in countable spaces

The purpose of this section is to discuss the descriptive set theoretic complexity of
\mathcal{M}(X)
as a subset of
\mathcal{P}(X)
when
X
is a countable space.
We first recall basic notions in descriptive set theory (Section
3.1
).
Then we describe the possible complexities of the family of monitorable sets in second countable spaces (Section
3.2
), and apply these results to topologies induced by transition relations (Section
3.3
).
Finally, we show by an example that for non-second countable spaces the situation is far more complicated (Section
3.4
).

3.1.
Preliminaries on descriptive set theory

We recall basic notions in descriptive set theory that we will use in the rest of this section, referring to
[
8
]
for a discussion in full detail.

Given a topological space
Z
, denote
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{0}_{1}(Z)
the open family of
Z
, and
\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{1}(Z)
the closed family of
Z
.
By recursion, for
2\leqslant\alpha<\omega_{1}
let
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{0}_{\alpha}(Z)
be the family of at most countable unions of members of
\bigcup_{\beta<\alpha}\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{\beta}(Z)
, and set
\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{\alpha}(Z)=\{Z\setminus A\mid A\in\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{0}_{\alpha}(Z)\}
.
For
1\leqslant\alpha<\omega_{1}
let also
\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{0}_{\alpha}(Z)=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{0}_{\alpha}(Z)\cap\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{\alpha}(Z)
.
The
\sigma
-algebra
B(Z)=\bigcup_{1\leqslant\alpha<\omega_{1}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{0}_{\alpha}(Z)=\bigcup_{1\leq\alpha<\omega_{1}}\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{\alpha}(Z)
is the Borel family of
Z
.
Note that the classes
\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{0}_{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{0}_{\alpha}
are
boldface pointclasses
, that is, they are closed under continuous preimages.

A
Polish
space is a separable, completely metrisable topological space.
If
Z
is Polish, then
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{0}_{\alpha}(Z)\cup\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{\alpha}(Z)\subseteq\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{0}_{\beta}(Z)
whenever
\alpha<\beta
.
A subset
A
of
Z
is
analytic
, or
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1}_{1}
, if there exist a Polish space
Y
and a continuous function
f:Y\to Z
whose range is
A
; it is
coanalytic
, or
\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{1}_{1}
, if its complement is analytic.

We will consider Polish spaces of the form
\mathcal{P}(X)
where
X
is a countable topological space: indeed, identifying
\mathcal{P}(X)
with
2^{X}
(via the identification of every subset with its characteristic function) and endowing the latter with the product topology,
2
being given the discrete topology,
\mathcal{P}(X)
is homeomorphic to the Cantor space.

If
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
is a boldface pointclass and
Z
a Polish space, a set

is
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
-
hard
if for every zero-dimensional Polish space

and every
B\in\boldsymbol{\Gamma}(Y)
, set

is
Wadge reducible
to set
A
, that is, there exists a continuous function
f:Y\to Z
such that
B=f^{-1}(A)
.
Set

is
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
-complete if
A\in\boldsymbol{\Gamma}(Z)
and
A
is
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
-hard.
A way to prove that a set is
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
-hard is to show that some
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
-hard set Wadge reduces to it.

If
Z
is a Polish space, then every set in
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{0}_{\alpha}(Z)\setminus\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{\alpha}(Z)
is
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{0}_{\alpha}
-complete, and every set in
\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{\alpha}(Z)\setminus\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{0}_{\alpha}(Z)
is
\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{\alpha}
-complete.
Every set in
\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{0}_{1}(Z)\setminus\{\emptyset,Z\}
is
\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{0}_{1}
-complete.
On the other hand, if

then no set in
\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{0}_{\alpha}(Z)
is
\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{0}_{\alpha}
-complete; we say that

is
\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{0}_{\alpha}

if

.

3.2.
The second countable case

In this section we determine the sharp complexities of the sets
\mathcal{M}(X)
, for
X
ranging over countable, second countable topological spaces.

Proposition 7
.

Let
X
be a countable, second countable topological space.
Then
\mathcal{M}(X)
is a
\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{3}
subset of
\mathcal{P}(X)
.

Proof 3.1
.

Fix an at most countable basis
\mathcal{B}
for
X
.
The subformula
U\subseteq\mathsf{Cl}(A)
in (
1
) means
\forall V\in\mathcal{B}\ (V\subseteq U\Rightarrow V\cap A\neq\emptyset)
, that is,
\forall V\in\mathcal{B}\ (V\subseteq U\Rightarrow\exists x\in X\ (x\in V\land x\in A))
; similarly, the subformula
U\subseteq\mathsf{Cl}(X\setminus A)
means
\forall V\in\mathcal{B}\ (V\subseteq U\Rightarrow\exists x\in X\ (x\in V\land x\notin A))

Both subformulas define a
\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{2}

The following characterises the second countable topologies, no matter the cardinality, in which all sets are monitorable.

Proposition 8
.

Let

be a second countable space.
Then
\mathcal{M}(X)=\mathcal{P}(X)
if and only if the set of isolated points is dense in
X
.

Proof 3.2
.

Assume first that the set of isolated points is dense and let

Since

Conversely, assume that there exists a non-empty, open subset

If

Otherwise every non-empty, open set included in

Let

Define by recursion distinct elements

3.3.
An application: transition relations

The results of section

show that for a second countable topology on a countable set

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{0}_{2}
subset of
\mathcal{P}(X)
) or complicated (namely, a
\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{3}
-complete subset of
\mathcal{P}(X)
).
Moreover, Remark

yields a procedure to distinguish the two situations.
In this section, we apply this procedure to a class of topologies induced by transition relations on a countable set,
proving that most of them give rise to a simple family of monitorable subsets
(Theorem

).

Let
X
be a countable set, and let
E
be a non-empty, at most countable set.
Recall that a transition relation on
X
is a subset
R\subseteq X\times E\times X
.
Then, for every
R\subseteq X\times E\times X
, we have a topology
\mathcal{T}_{R}
on

, generated by the family
\mathcal{B}_{R}=\{U_{F}^{R}\mid F\text{ a finite subset of }E^{<\omega}\}
where

\begin{split}U_{F}^{R}=\{q_{0}\in X\mid&\forall s\in F\ \exists q_{1},\ldots,q_{{\rm{length}}(s)}\in X\\
&\forall h\in\{0,\ldots,{\rm{length}}(s)-1\}\ (q_{h},s(h),q_{h+1})\in R\}\end{split}

The family
\mathcal{B}_{R}
is obviously at most countable and indeed a basis, as
U_{F}^{R}\cap U_{G}^{R}=U_{F\cup G}^{R}
.
Therefore
\mathcal{T}_{R}
is second countable.

Remark 21
.

This topology has been considered in
[
5
]
(even if the authors do not use explicitly a topological language)
as a semantic framework for runtime verification of branching-time properties.
Intuitively, the set
X
is the state space of a system under analysis and the elements of
E
are the possible observable events.
Then, the topology
\mathcal{T}_{R}
abstractly models monitors that can observe finitely many finite sequences of events generated by system executions.
It is worth noticing that, as discussed in
[
5
]
, this setting is quite different from others used in the literature
[
7
]
, which however use also a different notion of monitorability.

Let us endow the set
\mathcal{P}(X\times E\times X)
with the product topology, after identifying each transition relation with its characteristic function.
We consider the following two subsets of
\mathcal{P}(X\times E\times X)
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{0}=

\displaystyle\{R\in\mathcal{P}(X\times E\times X)\mid\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{T}_{R})\in\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{0}_{2}(\mathcal{P}(X))\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{1}=

\displaystyle\{R\in\mathcal{P}(X\times E\times X)\mid\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{T}_{R})\text{ is }\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{3}\text{-complete}\}=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(X\times E\times X)\setminus\mathcal{R}_{0}

Therefore,
\mathcal{R}_{0}
collects those transition relations inducing a simple family of monitorable subsets, while
\mathcal{R}_{1}
those whose family of monitorable subsets is complex.
Recall that a subset

of a topological space is
meagre
if it is an at most countable union of nowhere dense sets, while it is

if its complement is meagre.

(2)
