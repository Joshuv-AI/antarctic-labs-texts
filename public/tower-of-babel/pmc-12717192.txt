Image Dataset of Microfauna in Activated Sludge
Authors: Zheng Pang, Aijie Wang, Ying Chen, Tiefu Xu, Zhiming Shao, Chang Ge, Chuan He, Yu Tao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicrofauna play a crucial role in the ecological functioning and monitoring of activated sludge systems. However, existing studies often rely on manual observation and lack standardized image datasets for developing automated recognition models. Here, we present a Microfauna Dataset in Activated Sludge (MD-AS-2025), a publicly available image dataset designed to support object detection, classification, and related computer vision tasks. The dataset comprises two components: an annotated dataset (Dataset A) containing 4,000 full-frame microscopic images labeled across five morphologically and ecologically distinct microfaunal groups, and a cropped dataset (Dataset B), consisting of 14,257 single-object images generated from model-assisted detection and manual verification. All images were acquired under realistic activated sludge conditions using a high-throughput imaging system. The dataset reflects practical functional groupings commonly used in activated sludge analysis. MD-AS-2025 offers a reusable and extensible resource for developing intelligent microfauna analysis tools and provides a scalable framework for dataset construction in aquatic environments.Subject terms: Water microbiology, Scientific data, Environmental impact

Background & Summary

Microfauna, primarily comprising heterotrophic protozoa (unicellular) and metazoans (multicellular), including nematodes, rotifers, and tardigrades1, are ubiquitous in urban wastewater systems2,3 and natural aquatic ecosystems4. As integral constituents of microbial food webs, microfauna interact with bacteria and algae, and drive the cycling of carbon, nitrogen5 and phosphorus6. Furthermore, they contribute to plant disease resistance7 and their predatory activities facilitate nitrate release for plant uptake8. Crucially, microfauna serve as sensitive bioindicators. Protozoan functional traits exhibit notable spatial variability under differing environmental conditions9. Moreover, acute toxicity assays using rotifers10 have established their role as model organisms in ecotoxicology11, widely utilized in assessing water quality12 and aquatic toxicity13.

Microfauna are indispensable bioindicators in activated sludge, wherein fluctuations in their community structure reflect wastewater treatment efficiency14. Early studies in the 1980s initially demonstrated correlations between microfaunal population and activated sludge health15. Subsequently, in the 1990s, quantitative frameworks such as the Sludge Biotic Index (SBI) based on microfaunal communities16 and multiple regression models predicting effluent quality17 were developed. Since the early 21st century, research has increasingly focused on the relationships between specific microfauna and wastewater treatment processes. For example, bodonids and small amoebae are closely linked with effective nitrification, whereas ciliates correlate strongly with chemical oxygen demand (COD)18. Additionally, microfauna exhibit higher sensitivity to toxicants than traditional water-quality indicators. For instance, while high-quality effluent can be maintained despite hexavalent chromium exposure, noticeable shifts in dominant protozoan communities occur with increasing chromium concentrations19. Moreover, microfaunal predation significantly contributes to the reduction of excess sludge accumulation20.

However, current methodologies for microfaunal observation still depend on conventional manual microscopic examination. Such morphology-based methodologies are labor-intensive and susceptible to considerable subjective variability21, underscoring an urgent need for automated, intelligent analytical tools. Real-time Online Microscopic Data Analysis System (ROMIDAS) has been successfully applied for continuous microscopic image acquisition in complex activated sludge environments22,23. To further reduce human-induced errors, artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted object detection methodologies capable of precise and efficient recognition in complex activated sludge matrices are becoming increasingly essential. Nonetheless, the successful deployment of AI-driven detection models requires extensive, well-annotated image datasets for model training and validation24.

Currently, suitable image datasets tailored specifically for activated sludge environments are notably lacking. Existing datasets, such as EMDS-7 and plankton datasets, have limitations when applied to complex activated sludge environments. For example, the EMDS-7 dataset typically has simplified backgrounds25, potentially limiting the robustness of models trained on it. This inherent limitation renders most existing microfaunal image datasets derived from natural water bodies inappropriate for activated sludge applications. Additionally, plankton datasets based on holographic microscopy provide images with relatively limited color and texture detail26, which could impact accurate microfauna identification. Therefore, specialized microfauna image datasets tailored explicitly for activated sludge environments are needed to bridge this gap.

To meet the pressing need for high-quality microfauna datasets in activated sludge environments, we constructed the Microfauna Dataset in Activated Sludge (MD-AS-2025), a publicly available image dataset designed to support object detection, image classification, and related tasks. The dataset comprises two components: an Annotated Dataset (Dataset A), consisting of 4,000 annotated microscopic images with bounding boxes across five taxonomic groups, suitable for training and evaluating object detection models; and a Cropped Dataset (Dataset B), containing 14,257 single-object images that were extracted from the original frames using the YOLOv5 object detection model and subsequently categorized by taxon. These cropped images, each containing a single microfaunal instance, can be directly applied to classification tasks or integrated into object detection workflows. It is undeniable that our dataset includes a small number of instances exhibiting boundary truncation, limited resolution, and missing protozoan fine details. However, these “hard” instances may serve as natural occlusion augmentation, thereby enhancing model robustness in real-world applications (SI 1.1). All data were acquired under realistic operating conditions using ROMIDAS. MD-AS-2025 offers a versatile and task-oriented dataset to advance intelligent monitoring in wastewater treatment and support research in environmental microbiology and ecological assessment.

Methods

This study presents a high-throughput workflow for constructing microfauna image datasets from activated sludge samples (Fig. 1). Activated sludge samples were collected from laboratory-scale sequencing batch reactors (SBRs) and 12 wastewater treatment plants across six cities in China (Tianjin, Xingtai, Shenzhen, Beijing, Hegang and Wuchang, SI 1.2, Table S1). From the videos captured using ROMIDAS, keyframes were extracted and quality-filtered for manual annotation and model development. YOLOv5 models were then trained and applied to detect additional instances across the original frames. Detected objects were cropped and manually confirmed to produce a cropped dataset of single-object images. This pipeline enables efficient generation of large-scale, well-structured microfauna image datasets suitable for downstream analysis.

Workflow for constructing MD-AS-2025, including an annotated dataset and a cropped dataset. From 6.27 TB of activated sludge videos, frames were extracted and filtered. A total of 4,000 images were manually labeled across five microfaunal groups to create the annotated dataset. A YOLOv5 model trained on the annotated dataset was used to detect objects in original frames. The resulting cropped instances were manually verified and categorized, producing 14,257 single-object cropped images.

WWTPs and bioreactors

Wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs) are employed globally and host diverse microbial communities27. Sequencing batch reactors (SBRs) have demonstrated effective treatment of domestic wastewater28 and are considered straightforward to implement. The cylindrical poly (methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) SBRs featured an internal diameter of 200 mm, a height of 400 mm, and an effective volume of 8.8 L. Peristaltic pumps (YZ1515X, Lange Constant Flow Pump Co., Ltd., Baoding, China) regulated the upper influent and middle effluent, maintaining a volume exchange ratio of approximately 0.5. This setup ensured precise control over the wastewater treatment process. Cantilever electric mixers (LC-ES-120, Shanghai Lichen Instrument Technology Co., Ltd., China) were used to achieve effective sludge mixing during the influent, anoxic, and aerobic phases. Additionally, a microporous aeration disc was installed at the bottom of the SBR to supply oxygen to the activated sludge. A rotameter was used to regulate the aeration rate. All processes were automated via a time relay, minimizing the need for human intervention and enabling precise regulation of the full SBR operational cycle (SI 1.3).

To maintain an optimal sludge retention time (SRT) of approximately 20 days and a mixed liquor suspended solids (MLSS) concentration of 3000–4000 mg/L, a fixed amount of activated sludge was manually discharged from the SBR each night. During operation, a multi-parameter analyzer (DZS-706-B, INESA Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) was employed for the online detection and recording of temperature, pH, and dissolved oxygen.

To consistently generate a continuous supply of microfauna images from activated sludge under varying operational conditions, influent parameters were regularly modified.

ROMIDAS

ROMIDAS is a highly modular hardware system designed for the online and real-time acquisition of microscopic images (Fig. 2). The system comprises a sample droplet and circulation system, a compression assembly, a microscopic image acquisition and analysis system, a transmission system, and a membrane storage unit. Activated sludge samples were delivered to ROMIDAS using a peristaltic pump. Controlled by a time relay (YYC-2S, YUEYU, Dongguan, China), the activated sludge samples were added dropwise within ROMIDAS onto the linear low-density polyethylene (LLDPE) film (roll length of 150 m, width of 100 mm, thickness of 2.2 µm, Shanghai Lede Packaging Materials Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Two LLDPE films and two pressure rods compressed the activated sludge samples into a thin film suitable for microscopic observation. All images were collected through a BLD-200FB inverted biological microscope (Cossim, Beijing, China) with a 10 × objective lens and an E3ISPM series C-mount USB 3.0 CMOS image sensor (Cossim) with ImageView software for video capture. Illumination was provided by a Philips low-voltage halogen lamp (6 V, 20 W).

Microfauna image acquisition from activated sludge using ROMIDAS. Samples are introduced into a circulation system and dispensed onto a polyethylene (PE) film sheet at timed intervals. The upper and lower film sheets are then pressed together to compress the samples into a thin layer. The conveyor belt then transports the film containing the samples under a microscope equipped with a CMOS image sensor for imaging.

Image collection

In this study, we employed ROMIDAS to compress activated sludge samples into thin films and transfer them to a microscope for real-time imaging. During the SBR stirring phase, we adjusted the sample injection and reflux system to transport activated sludge samples to the compression assembly within 25 s at a flow rate of 55 mL/min using a peristaltic pump. The excess activated sludge was directed to a reflux tank and subsequently returned to the SBR via a reflux pipe at the same flow rate using the identical peristaltic pump. After compaction, the thin films containing the activated sludge samples were conveyed forward by transmission rollers and conveyor belts at an approximate speed of 165 µm/s for imaging.

The compacted activated sludge samples were imaged using a 10 × objective under halogen illumination. Real-time microscopic videos of the activated sludge were captured at resolutions of 1824 × 1216 pixels using a CMOS sensor. The videos were then synchronized to a network-attached storage (NAS) device from the CMOS sensor and the control terminal. While imaging, we aligned and secured the LLDPE film with samples to the microscope’s focal plane, monitored focus drift during runs and corrected it (SI 1.4). After image acquisition, the membranes containing the samples were stored for retrospective sampling and observation of key regions in the images through the membrane storage system. The SBR and ROMIDAS operated continuously, enabling real-time, online high-throughput imaging. The entire process, including sample injection and imaging, was completed within 5 min, ensuring rapid data acquisition. Raw images collected contain occasional artifacts (black lines, focus and lighting variability) and they do not obscure targets or materially affect dataset usability or model performance (SI 1.5).

Choice of image format

We selected JPG as the format of the images in our dataset (SI 1.6). JPG is widely used in benchmark datasets (such as COCO29, ImageNet30) and industrial workflows. In the controlled experiments, we found that JPG achieves large storage savings, mean JPG/PNG ≈ 7.6, while producing only a negligible change in object detection model performance, mean mAP@0.5 ≈ 0.0039 (Table S2).

Image preprocessing

All experiments were conducted on a custom-built desktop computer equipped with an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU, an AMD EPYC™ 9654 CPU, 128 GB of RAM, and 3 TB storage capacity. We utilized Python to preprocess the acquired microscopic videos, which consisted of tens of millions of frames. Frames were extracted from the videos at fixed intervals to reduce computational load and minimize redundant sampling of microfauna. Images were exported at a resolution of 1824 × 1216 pixels using OpenCV cv2. without overriding the default encoder settings (OpenCV cv2. __version__ = 4.x, default JPG quality = 95). To eliminate blurred images (SI 1.7) that hinder accurate identification of microfauna, we applied a Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) method as described by Adrian Rosebrock31. Additionally, we filtered out images with predominantly blank or dark areas by setting a threshold on the gray-level standard deviation (SI 1.8). During the initial stages, hardware parameters such as frame rate, lighting conditions, and transmission speed varied among the video sets. To reduce the computational load for subsequent model training, we selected suitable parameters for each video based on its unique characteristics. The selected parameters were then used to extract key frames.

Image quality control

To ensure broad applicability, especially for industrial and field use, we did not rigidly constrain white balance or exposure time, but rather implemented a set of standardized, objective image quality metrics. The following five widely used, non-biased metrics were used for initial quality screening (SI 1.9), each with an industry-accepted (and slightly relaxed) threshold to facilitate large-scale acquisition under real-world conditions:

Resolution: 1824 × 1216 pixels.

RMS contrast32: >15 (99.99% met).

Entropy33 (information content): >5 (99.995% of images met this threshold).

SNR34 (Signal-to-Noise Ratio): >10 (100% met).

STD35 (Noise standard deviation): <5 (100% met).

Mean brightness36: between 50 and 200 (92.77% met, the main exceptions were due to light-blocking activated sludge flocs).

More than 90% of all images satisfied each criterion, in line with accepted statistical norms for high-throughput quality control.

Annotated dataset (Dataset A)

Due to the lack of publicly available datasets with detailed annotations of microfauna in complex environments such as activated sludge, we utilized LabelImg37, a well-used graphical image annotation tool for object detection, to create our own annotated dataset. Given the complexity of activated sludge images and the sporadic occurrence of many taxa, from the initial 81,751 microscopic key frames, we prioritized five common and readily identifiable groups at the order level, and assigned them abbreviation symbols: Arcellinida (Ar), Digononta (Do), Monogononta (Mo), Nematoda (Ne), and Hypotrichida (Eu) (SI 1.10). Since functional roles are frequently shared across closely related species and trait-based metrics better predict process performance than taxonomy-based indices38,39, annotation at the order level is an appropriate and ecologically informative choice (SI 1.11). Each identifiable individual was labeled within a rectangular bounding box that captured the full body contour, with care taken to minimize overlap between adjacent instances whenever possible. Multiple microfaunal instances can appear in a single image, and all visible instances were labeled. In total, 4,000 images were manually labeled, totaling 5,821 labeled instances, comprising 2,808 Arcellinida, 1,352 Digononta, 669 Monogononta, 560 Nematoda, and 432 Hypotrichida (SI 2.1, Figure S1). The annotation process was guided by morphological references from a standard atlas of microfauna in activated sludge. To ensure consistency across the dataset, annotations were refined over multiple iterations. And in cases where individuals appeared partially occluded or near the image borders, the corresponding video footage was reviewed to confirm accurate labeling.

Cropped dataset (Dataset B)

Following the construction of Dataset A, we trained a YOLOv5 object detection model using manually annotated images to detect microfauna in additional unlabeled frames. The trained model was then applied to the 81,751 extracted images to locate individual microfauna. Detected regions were cropped based on the predicted bounding boxes and exported as separate single-object images. Each cropped image was individually reviewed to ensure that the object was properly captured and clearly visible. Images with poor resolution, blur, or incomplete contours were manually excluded. The remaining valid images were then categorized into the same five taxonomic groups based on the classification scheme established in the manually annotated dataset (Dataset A). This process resulted in a secondary dataset, referred to as Dataset B, containing 14,257 images, each representing a single microfaunal instance. All cropped images were saved in JPG format, organized by group label into separate folders. Dataset B can be used for image classification, dataset augmentation, and lightweight object detection tasks.

Data Records

MD-AS-2025 is publicly accessible at Zenodo40–45. The annotated dataset contains 4,000 microscopic images stored in the train_val_test/images/ folder in JPG format, with corresponding bounding box annotations stored in the train_val_test/labels/ folder in PASCAL-VOC (XML) format. Each annotation file shares the same base filename as its associated image. Cropped dataset contains 14,257 JPG images, each representing a single microfaunal instance extracted from the original frames using YOLOv5. These images are organized into five folders by category: Arcellinida/, Digononta/, Monogononta/, Nematoda/, and Hypotrichida/. The directory structure is shown in Fig. 3. An overview of image types across the annotated, detected, and cropped datasets, as well as the object count distribution per image, is provided in Fig. 4. The image size ranges of the five taxonomic groups in the cropped dataset are predominantly: Arcellinida 20–250 µm, Digononta 40–420 µm, Monogononta 40–210 µm, Nematoda 40–660 µm, Hypotrichida 40–190 µm.

Technical Validation

Object detection model evaluation (for Dataset A and B)

Model selection and comparison

YOLOv5 is a widely adopted object detection model, known for its balance of accuracy, speed, and deployment simplicity. As a single-stage, lightweight model, YOLOv5 achieves real-time inference while maintaining strong detection performance46. These advantages make it particularly suitable for use in our study.

Moreover, YOLOv5 has demonstrated robust performance in the field of activated sludge analysis. Previous studies have applied it successfully to microfauna detection tasks within wastewater microscopy datasets, showing better accuracy and speed trade-offs than other object detection models such as Faster R-CNN47,48.

Since the release of YOLOv5, Ultralytics, the main developers of the YOLO series, have introduced several newer versions, including YOLOv8 (2023) and YOLO11 (2024), each offering architectural updates and performance optimizations. However, as demonstrated by recent comparative studies, newer models do not always outperform earlier versions, especially when applied to domain-specific datasets49–51.

In our study, on the same dataset, after hyperparameter fine-tuning, the optimized YOLOv5 model performed better than the YOLO11 model (SI 2.2). Given YOLOv5’s smaller model footprint and faster inference, we therefore adopted YOLOv5 as the baseline model.

Data splitting

We used a dataset comprising 4,000 images, from which a fixed subset of 400 images (10%) was randomly selected as an independent test set. This ensured a consistent evaluation benchmark across all experiments. The remaining 3,600 images were allocated for model training and validation. For experiments involving the complete dataset, we applied an 8:1 ratio to partition the training and validation subsets. To ensure robustness and mitigate variability caused by data sampling, we generated five independent training-validation splits using distinct random seeds (101–105). Models were trained separately on each split, and their performances were evaluated consistently using the designated test set. The final reported results represent the average performance across these five runs. To explore the relationship between training set size and model performance, additional experiments were conducted with progressively varied training set sizes. Specifically, subsets containing 1/5, 2/5, 3/5, and 4/5 of the original 3,600 training images were randomly sampled using five random seeds (43–47), resulting in 20 distinct sub-datasets (4 subset sizes × 5 seeds). Given the reduced dataset sizes, we adopted a train-validation split ratio of 4:1 to retain adequate training data.

Evaluation metrics

The trained models’ performances were quantitatively assessed using three standard metrics: AP50, mAP@0.5, and F1 score. Specifically, AP50 measures the average precision at an Intersection over Union (IoU) threshold of 0.5, while mAP@0.5 denotes the mean average precision at IoU = 0.5 across all classes. The F1 score represents the harmonic mean of precision and recall.

Hyperparameter optimization and model comparison

To benchmark object detection performance, we systematically compared three configurations: YOLOv5 (default hyperparameters), YOLOv5_a (tuned hyperparameters, Table S3), the latest YOLO version, YOLO11 (default hyperparameters).

We found that YOLO11 slightly outperformed YOLOv5 on mAP@0.5 (+0.01) but showed a small drop in F1 score (−0.006). Notably, the tuned YOLOv5_a model surpassed YOLO11, reaching an mAP@0.5 = 0.895, exceeding that of YOLO11 by 0.007 (Table 1). This suggests that with appropriate tuning, YOLOv5 can match or even outperform YOLO11 on this dataset.

Analysis of the training process

To further analyze the training results, we analyzed the model behavior and failure modes of the best-performing model, YOLOv5_a, to better understand both its strengths and remaining limitations. We found that class-wise performance is heterogeneous (Fig. 5a): taxa with distinctive morphology and numerous labels (such as Arcellinida and Digononta) attain high detection recall (0.94 and 0.87, respectively), whereas Nematoda achieves substantially lower accuracy (0.67). Per class F1 curves peak at intermediate confidence values, approximately 0.2 to 0.6 (Fig. 5b). Consequently, calibrating class specific confidence thresholds, for example by selecting the threshold that maximizes per class F1 or by choosing thresholds that meet specified precision or recall targets, can substantially improve the tradeoff between precision and recall for different application objectives. Training loss decreases rapidly in early epochs while validation loss follows at a slightly higher level, producing a transient generalization gap. Later in training both losses level off and the gap narrows. Precision, recall and mAP increase early and then stabilize, with validation traces closely tracking training thereafter. The small and decreasing generalization gap indicates acceptable learning dynamics for the chosen model and dataset (Fig. 5c).

Training-data size analysis

A comparative evaluation of model performance on the test set, based on training sets of different sizes (Figure S2, Table S4), reveals that our model attains a notably high performance level even with a dataset containing only 1,440 images (mAP@0.5 = 0.808). Increasing the dataset size from 2,880 to 3,600 images results in minimal improvements in specific categories, while the overall performance remains stable (mAP@0.5 from 0.858 to 0.878).

As the training set size increases from 720 to 3,600 images, the model’s detection performance for Hypotrichida (mAP@0.5 range: 0.937–0.986) and Arcellinida (mAP@0.5 range: 0.932–0.960) categories remains relatively stable. This suggests that for microfauna with distinct features and simple structures, a dataset comprising only a few hundred images is sufficient for the model to extract the crucial identifying features. Conversely, the detection performance for the Nematoda category exhibits more significant improvement as the dataset size increases, with mAP@0.5 rising from 0.545 (720 images) to 0.710 (3,600 images). Similarly, the model’s performance in detecting the Monogononta category significantly improves with larger training sets (mAP@0.5 increases from 0.626 to 0.839). These outcomes can be attributed to the inherent characteristics of microfauna. Monogononta and Nematoda display pronounced body shape variability during movement, including rapid bending, stretching and tumbling. Consequently, their outline patterns change markedly from frame to frame, increasing appearance diversity across categories. Accurate detection of these categories therefore requires a larger and more diverse training set than is typically needed for morphologically stable categories such as Arcellinida. By contrast, Hypotrichida generally maintains relatively consistent body outlines while moving, which reduces pose variability. Additionally, expanding the dataset introduces new scenarios and motion postures, which can enhance the model’s robustness by exposing it to a broader range of variations, thereby improving its generalization capabilities.

In summary, the detection performance of our model improves as the number of images in the training dataset increases. Microfauna with more complex image features in microscopic images require larger datasets or more annotated bounding boxes for effective training. Generally, for a specific microfauna category, a dataset comprising approximately 1000–1500 images or annotated boxes is sufficient to develop a well-performing detection model suitable for practical use. This finding highlights the adaptability and robustness of small-scale datasets across various environments. Furthermore, a limited number of annotated images may be adequate for new scenes, significantly reducing the time required for subsequent deployment and utilization.

Cropped dataset review (for Dataset B)

To validate the reliability and representativeness of the cropped dataset (Dataset B), we performed a series of visual and morphological analyses based on the bounding boxes generated by the trained object detection model. All cropped images were manually reviewed to ensure clarity, completeness, and correct categorization. Images with motion blur, partial occlusion, or low resolution were excluded. Each accepted image was then categorized into one of the five taxonomic groups, following the same classification framework established in Dataset A.

To assess morphological consistency across microfaunal categories, we analyzed the pixel dimensions (length and width) of the cropped regions (Fig. 6). The scatterplots revealed distinct distribution patterns among the five categories, reflecting their biological characteristics.

Pixel dimension distributions (length and width) of five activated sludge microfauna derived from cropped images. (a) Arcellinida. (b) Digononta. (c) Monogononta. (d) Hypotrichida. (e) Nematoda.

The pixel distribution of length and width for Arcellinida images was depicted, highlighting that these images predominantly aligned along the y = x line of the scatterplot (Fig. 6a). Arcellinida, mainly represented by the genera Arcella and Centropyxis in the activated sludge system, typically exhibited regular circular shapes in microscopic images, which was reflected in the relatively uniform pixel values for image dimensions. A few outliers were evident, likely due to the limitations in the microscope’s field of view, which captured only parts of the shells, affecting the length-to-width ratios. Despite some minimal potential noise errors, the scatterplot also indicated that the sizes of the Arcellinida images were primarily concentrated around three ranges: 100 × 100 pixels, 150 × 150 pixels, and 200 × 200 pixels. These size categories may have represented smaller genera like Arcella with dimensions of 100 × 100 pixels, and larger genera such as Centropyxis for the 150–200 × 150–200 pixels. This distribution not only supported the accuracy of our model’s detection results but also underscored the utility of using pixel values of length and width as a direct method for identifying Arcellinida and potentially distinguishing between genera such as Arcella and Centropyxis (Fig. 6a).

The pixel distribution of length and width for Digononta images was depicted (Fig. 6b). Digononta, primarily represented by genera such as Rotaria and Philodina in the activated sludge system, often exhibited telescopic, sheath-like movements, resulting in elongated body shapes. Consequently, Digononta images appeared as elongated rectangles, leading to imbalanced length-to-width ratios. Additionally, when Digononta were oriented diagonally within the field of view, they also exhibited similar length and width values, which contributed to the skewed distribution observed (Fig. 6b). As most Digononta are larger than Arcellinida, they predominantly clustered within the region, ranging from 200 to 300 pixels. Occasionally, larger Digononta appeared around 400–500 pixels in the outer region. This pixel pattern represented a crucial pixel feature for larger microfauna belonging to Digononta in activated sludge microscopic images.

The pixel distribution of length and width for Monogononta images was depicted (Fig. 6c). Monogononta, primarily represented by genera such as Lecane in the activated sludge system, often exhibited non-telescopic, non-sheath-like movements, and their body morphology tended to remain relatively stable without significant elongation or contraction. As a result, a concentrated cluster within the smaller range of 100–150 pixels was revealed, displaying a symmetric relationship along the y = x axis (Fig. 6c).

The pixel distribution of length and width for Hypotrichida images was depicted (Fig. 6d). Hypotrichida, predominantly represented by Euplotes in the activated sludge system, typically exhibited swimming-type movements. The pixel distribution shape of Hypotrichida, like Monogononta, was symmetrically distributed along the y = x line. However, Hypotrichida generally exhibited larger pixel dimensions compared to Monogononta, falling within the 150–200 pixels range, which allowed for effective differentiation between these two categories based on pixel size.

The pixel distribution of length and width for Nematoda images was depicted (Fig. 6e). Nematoda, commonly used as an indicator of sludge accumulation in the activated sludge system, typically appeared elongated when stretched, resulting in pixel values exhibiting length-width imbalances within the range of 600–800 pixels. Additionally, Nematoda exhibited curvature at times, leading to a concentration of pixel values with relatively minor differences in length-to-width ratios within the range of 200–400 pixels. This distribution feature served as an essential pixel feature for Nematoda and similar elongated microfauna within activated sludge microscopic images.

In summary, our cropped dataset contained cropped images of microfauna from the Arcellinida, Digononta, Monogononta, Hypotrichida, and Nematoda categories. The pixel information in these images provided detailed microscopic details about the microfauna. The observed differences in pixel-level dimensions among taxa further support the morphological consistency and labeling accuracy of the dataset. Moreover, based on pixel features, these data helped researchers quickly grasp information about dominant populations within microscopic images of typical and common microfauna in activated sludge systems. These characteristics collectively validate the reliability of Dataset B for downstream applications such as classification and detection model development.

Usage Notes

MD-AS-2025 is publicly accessible at our project website (https://microfauna-romidas.mystrikingly.com), where both subsets described in this paper are introduced and linked under the MD-AS-2025 section. To support effective use of the MD-AS-2025 dataset, the following notes are provided:

Taxonomic resolution

Due to the complexity of activated sludge environments, reliable identification at the genus or species level typically requires molecular or staining methods. Our dataset adopts a practical taxonomic grouping based on morphological traits and ecological function, which can support preliminary research and routine monitoring tasks in activated sludge systems.

Class distribution

Category imbalance reflects the natural dominance patterns of microfauna groups in activated sludge, further influenced by the exclusion of low-quality or ambiguous images. Users may apply balancing strategies when training models.

Application potential

The dataset and its rapid construction workflow using ROMIDAS can be adapted to other aquatic systems, supporting broader applications in environmental monitoring, ecological assessment, and intelligent sensing.
