Problem-solving capabilities in healthcare education: a multigroup analysis of psychological factors in medical and dental students
Authors: Gökçe İşcan, Osman Gurdal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProblem-solving is essential for healthcare professionals; nevertheless, the psychological mechanisms influencing it in educational settings remain insufficiently examined. Guided by an integrated framework combining Cognitive Load Theory, Self-Determination Theory, and Professional Identity Formation, this study examined how self-esteem and anxiety interact to influence problem-solving, anticipating systematic variations across programs and gender.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 513 participants (397 medical and 116 dental students) who completed validated instruments: the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale, State-Trait Anxiety Inventory, and Problem-Solving Inventory. Structural equation modeling assessed direct and indirect relationships among the constructs, and multigroup comparisons, mediation and moderation analyses tested differences between medical and dental students as well as by gender.ResultsSelf-esteem significantly predicted problem-solving ability (β = 0.36, p < 0.001). Multigroup analyses revealed significant differences between medical and dental students, with dental students showing stronger direct self-esteem effects (β = 0.41 vs. β = 0.34, p < 0.05). Trait anxiety partially mediated the self-esteem–problem-solving relationship (β = 0.16, p < 0.001), whereas state anxiety did not demonstrate significant mediation (β = 0.08, p = 0.062). A serial mediation pathway was identified wherein self-esteem influenced problem-solving through sequential effects on state anxiety and trait anxiety (β = 0.07, p < 0.001), with total indirect effects accounting for 38.9% of the self-esteem effect on problem-solving. Gender comparisons revealed no statistically significant differences in structural paths (all p > 0.216), though males showed numerically larger direct self-esteem effects (β = 0.41 vs. β = 0.33), while females exhibited significant state anxiety effects not observed in males.ConclusionsPsychological mechanisms involving self-esteem and anxiety, both directly and through mediation pathways, shape the problem-solving abilities of healthcare students. These relationships differ between medical and dental students, underscoring the need for program-specific educational strategies. Theory-informed interventions that integrate self-esteem enhancement with anxiety management, grounded in cognitive load and motivational principles, may strengthen professional problem-solving among future healthcare practitioners.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-025-08397-9.Keywords: Problem-solving abilities, Self-esteem, Anxiety, Structural equation modeling, Medical education

Introduction

Problem-solving in healthcare professions is a complex cognitive-behavioral process essential for clinical practice [1, 2]. In medical and dental education, critical thinking represents the self-evaluated ability to identify issues, formulate solutions, and implement effective resolutions in curriculum contexts [3]. This multifaceted concept, which includes problem-solving confidence, approach-avoidance strategies, and personal control [4], is eventually linked to patient care results [5].

Self-esteem, characterized as an individual’s subjective assessment of their worth, is significantly associated with cognitive performance and academic success [6]. Baumeister et al. [7] assert that self-esteem comprises perceptions regarding one’s capabilities and future prospects, affecting individuals’ approaches to problems and their resilience in the face of adversity. Maloney et al. [8] highlighted that anxiety substantially corresponds with problem-solving via two separate modalities. State anxiety encompasses transient, context-dependent reactions, whereas trait anxiety denotes enduring dispositional characteristics, as elucidated by Eysenck et al. [9]. Excessive anxiety shows negative relationships with working memory, attention, and cognitive flexibility, which are critical elements of efficient problem-solving [10].

Previous studies have examined these associations separately within educational environments [11]. Elevated self-esteem is linked to superior performance on intricate tasks [7], whereas both types of worry may impair cognitive performance, as evidenced by Owens et al. [12]. Colbert-Getz et al. [13] revealed that medical students’ self-efficacy relates to clinical reasoning capability, whereas Stormon et al. [14] indicated that dental students exhibiting elevated anxiety manifest inferior clinical decision-making.

Nonetheless, the concurrent, complex relationships among self-esteem, anxiety, and problem-solving capabilities are still not fully elucidated [15]. Prior studies have predominantly been correlational, limiting their ability to explain the complex interactions among self-esteem, anxiety, and problem-solving capabilities. Additionally, high levels of anxiety are common among university students, as documented by Ozen et al. [16]. Moreover, the disparities between medical and dental students have not been comprehensively examined, notwithstanding their unique educational requirements [17].

Medical and dental programs offer fundamentally distinct environments that systematically correspond with psychological dynamics. Medical education emphasizes comprehensive diagnostic reasoning and gradual increases in responsibility, whereas dental education prioritizes procedural competencies with prompt feedback and earlier clinical independence, as described by Allen and Finnegan [18]. We hypothesized that educational differences would influence how psychological traits contribute to problem-solving development, with dental students showing stronger direct effects of self-esteem on problem-solving due to earlier procedural demands, and medical students showing stronger anxiety-mediated pathways due to prolonged diagnostic uncertainty. These predictions align with distinct cognitive load patterns and processes of professional identity formation across the two disciplines.

At the current study institution in Turkey, these educational differences are exemplified in the curriculum structures. The medical program follows a six-year curriculum with clinical exposure beginning in the third year and intensifying in the fourth year, emphasizing case-based learning and diagnostic reasoning. In contrast, the dental curriculum spans five years with hands-on clinical practice starting in the second year, focusing on procedural skills development with immediate technical feedback. These distinct educational pathways provide an ideal context for examining how self-esteem and anxiety are differentially related to problem-solving capabilities in these healthcare disciplines.

Methods

Study design and participants

A cross-sectional survey was carried out during 2021-2022 at a respected university’s Schools of Medicine and Dentistry in Turkey. All registered students, around 2,200, were invited through institutional email and academic social media platforms. The invitation outlined the study’s objective, guaranteed confidentiality, and provided a link to the online survey platform. The final study included 513 students with complete data, out of the 544 preliminary responses (24.7%). The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Suleyman Demirel University Medical School’s Ethics Committee (Approval No. 82, dated 12 March 2020). All participants provided informed consent electronically prior to completing the survey, and the study adhered to the ethical standards outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Measures

This investigation utilized three previously validated psychological questionnaires administered online: the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale [20], the Problem-Solving Inventory [21], and the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory [22]. All instruments were administered in their entirety, with specific items chosen for SEM analysis based on confirmatory factor analysis loadings.

Rosenberg self-esteem scale (RSES)

The 10-item RSES evaluates overall self-worth utilizing a 4-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 4 = strongly agree), with elevated scores signifying enhanced self-esteem. The scale has shown remarkable psychometric development, with internal consistency coefficients ranging from α = 0.84 to 0.95 across populations [23, 24]. Gray-Little et al. [23] also reported robust construct validity, evidenced by associations with self-concept and psychological well-being across multiple studies. These established psychometric properties justified the inclusion of the RSES in the current study. Four items with the highest factor loadings were chosen for the final measurement model and yielded strong internal consistency in our sample, α = 0.86.

Problem-solving inventory (PSI)

Heppner and Petersen’s 32-item Problem-Solving Inventory assesses self-perceived problem-solving behaviors and attitudes across three dimensions: problem-solving confidence, approach-avoidance style, and personal control. Items employ a 6-point Likert scale (1 = strongly agree to 6 = strongly disagree), with elevated scores signifying superior problem-solving capabilities. Since its debut, the PSI has demonstrated solid psychometric properties, with internal consistency coefficients ranging from α = 0.66 to 0.85 for its subscales [4, 21]. The instrument has shown strong construct validity through correlations with measures of psychological adjustment [21]. Four elements exhibiting the highest factor loadings were chosen for the structural equation model and demonstrated strong internal consistency in our sample, α = 0.86.

State-trait anxiety inventory (STAI)

Spielberger’s STAI uses distinct 20-item subscales assessed on 4-point Likert scales (1 = not at all to 4 = very much so) to measure both transient, situation-specific anxiety (state anxiety) and persistent, dispositional anxiety (trait anxiety). Higher scores indicate higher anxiety levels. The STAI is among the most widely used instruments for anxiety assessment, with internal consistency coefficients ranging from α = 0.65 to 0.96 for the state anxiety subscale and α = 0.16 to 0.76 for trait anxiety in a meta-analysis of reliability coefficients [25]. Additionally, Kabacoff et al. [26] reported that extensive investigation across multiple studies has established the STAI’s construct validity and sensitivity to stress, supporting its selection for this study. Both state anxiety (4 items) and trait anxiety (5 items) were chosen using factor analysis. For the state anxiety subscale, the selected items concluded excellent internal consistency, α = 0.91. For the trait anxiety, the designated items also showed strong reliability in our study, α = 0.81.

All validated instruments used in this investigation, the RSES, the PSI, and the STAI scales, are freely available for research purposes and do not require special permission from copyright holders. The instruments were freely obtained from publicly accessible academic resources.

Item selection and psychometric validation

All participants completed full instrument versions (RSES, PSI, SAI, TAI). We implemented systematic item selection using confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), following established SEM practices [27, 28]. After reverse-coding and screening for missingness, models were estimated via Maximum Likelihood (ML), and results were validated using Generalized Least Square (GLS) and Asymptotically Distribution-Free (ADF) methods.

For each construct, single-factor CFAs were fitted to full item sets. Items were retained based on: (1) standardized factor loadings ≥ 0.60 (0.50 acceptable for theoretically essential indicators), (2) absence of substantial cross-loadings or local dependence (modification indices, residual correlations), and (3) balanced construct representation. Minimum three indicators per latent variable were ensured while avoiding redundancy.

Internal consistency was determined using Cronbach’s alpha and McDonald’s omega. Convergent validity, composite reliability, and discriminant validity were evaluated in accordance with established protocols. The invariance of measurement (configural, metric, scalar) was tested prior to group comparisons.

Statistical analysis

The data were processed using IBM SPSS version 29, AMOS version 24, and validated with R (Lavaan package) and Python (Semopy library). Prior to the main analysis, data were processed for normality, linearity, homoscedasticity, and multicollinearity. Missing data (< 2%) were handled with maximum likelihood estimation.

Measurement model

Confirmatory factor analysis was utilized to validate the measurement model. Convergent validity was assessed through Average Variance Extracted (AVE > 0.50) and Composite Reliability (CR > 0.70), while discriminant validity was evaluated using the Fornell-Larcker criterion [29], wherein the square root of AVE for each construct should exceed its correlations with all other constructs.

Structural equation modeling

We examined a theoretically driven series of nested models, including (1) direct effects, (2) simple mediation, (3) multiple mediation, (4) serial mediation, (5) moderation, and (6) moderated mediation models. Model fit was evaluated using conventional criteria: χ²/df < 3, CFI > 0.95, TLI > 0.95, and RMSEA < 0.05 [27].

Mediation, moderation, and moderated mediation analysis

Mediation was assessed for the indirect effect of self-esteem on problem solving via state and trait anxiety using bias-corrected bootstrap confidence intervals based on 5,000 resamples, while direct effects were estimated concurrently. Moderation by educational program and gender was evaluated with multigroup SEM using sequential measurement/structural invariance testing and χ²-difference comparisons of constrained versus unconstrained path models. Moderated mediation was examined by comparing the bootstrapped indirect effects across groups (bias-corrected 95% CIs for between-group contrasts). Multigroup SEM was used to compare medical vs. dental and male vs. female groups, with measurement invariance established prior to interpreting differences in structural paths.

Results

The final sample consisted of 513 people, including 397 medical students and 116 dental students. Medical students exhibited a marginally greater mean age of 22.9 years compared to 22.1 years for dental students (p = 0.002), although dental students had a higher percentage of females at 75.9% against 65.2% for medical students (p = 0.043). The distributions of study year and living situation did not exhibit significant differences across the groups (Table 1).

Measurement model validation

CFA validated the measurement structure, and all items loaded significantly (p < 0.001), with standardized loadings ranging from 0.67 to 0.93. Implementing correlation analysis of selected items, we found consistently positive relationships within constructs. Item pairings demonstrated moderate to strong correlations (r = 0.51–0.72), indicating internal consistency. In our results, there were no correlations that exceeded the multicollinearity threshold (r > 0.90). However, weak correlations (r < 0.30) between PSI and TAI items were retained due to their theoretical importance and appropriate factor loadings.

In our calculation, all the constructs demonstrated strong convergent validity (AVE > 0.50) and composite reliability (CR > 0.70): RSES (AVE = 0.62, CR = 0.87), PSI (AVE = 0.68, CR = 0.89), State Anxiety (AVE = 0.72, CR = 0.91), and Trait Anxiety (AVE = 0.58, CR = 0.87). We confirmed discriminant validity by finding that the square root of AVE for each construct exceeded its correlations with all other constructs. Internal consistency was strong across constructs for Cronbach’s α and McDonald’s ω. Convergent validity was supported (all AVE > 0.50), and discriminant validity met the Fornell–Larcker criterion. Structural equation modeling

The combined measurement and structural model showed an excellent fit with χ²/df = 1.971, GFI = 0.952, NFI = 0.964, TLI = 0.972, CFI = 0.978, RMSEA = 0.044, and PCLOSE = 0.890. In addition, self-esteem had a notable direct impact on problem-solving ability (β = 0.36, p < 0.001) and significantly affected both state anxiety (β = 0.60, p < 0.001) and trait anxiety (β = 0.40, p < 0.001). Trait anxiety was a significant predictor of problem-solving ability (β = 0.28, p < 0.05); nevertheless, state anxiety did not achieve statistical significance (β = 0.10, p = 0.070). A notable serial mediation pathway was identified from self-esteem through state and trait anxiety to problem-solving (β = 0.074, p < 0.001), with the total indirect effect representing 38.9% of the total relationship. The model accounted for approximately 41% of the variance in problem-solving, 36% in state anxiety, and 41% in trait anxiety. The patterns were comparable across subgroups, with slight variations between medical and dental students (Fig. 1b).

Mediation, moderation, and moderated mediation analysis

The direct effect of self-esteem on problem-solving remained significant (β = 0.499, 95% CI [0.327–0.661], p < 0.001), demonstrating partial mediation with the bootstrapping processes of 5000 samples.

There were no significant moderating effects of gender or program type throughout the studied routes (p-values ranged from 0.216 to 0.951). However, moderated mediation analyses revealed that medical and dental students had distinct explained variances for trait anxiety (medical: 37% vs. dental: 44%) and problem-solving (medical: 43% vs. dental: 49%), indicating that model performance varies across settings.

Consistent with H2, trait anxiety substantially mediated the self-esteem–problem-solving link (β = 0.159, 95% CI [0.088–0.256], p < 0.001). State anxiety alone did not significantly mediate this relationship (β = 0.084, 95% CI [−0.005-0.189], p = 0.062).

Supporting H3, a strong serial mediation pathway emerged where self-esteem influenced state anxiety, which impacted trait anxiety, thereby ultimately affecting problem-solving (β = 0.074, 95% CI [0.039–0.129], p < 0.001). The total indirect effect was significant (β = 0.317, 95% CI [0.212–0.464], p < 0.001) and accounted for 38.9% of the overall effect (Table 3).

Multigroup analysis: educational context and gender differences

Multigroup analysis indicated substantial differences between medical and dental students, supporting theoretical assumptions. Self-esteem had a higher direct effect on problem-solving for dental students (β = 0.41) than medical students (β = 0.34), validating Cognitive Load Theory expectations (Fig. 2).

In accordance with Self-Determination Theory, the relation between self-esteem and trait anxiety differed considerably between groups (Δχ² = 4.18, p < 0.05), with dental students having a higher influence (β = 0.51) than medical students (β = 0.36). Medical students showed a greater state-trait anxiety association (β = 0.34) compared to dental students (β = 0.26), indicating distinct anxiety development patterns across contexts.

The model fit remained excellent for both groups (χ²/df = 1.701, GFI = 0.972, CFI = 0.968, RMSEA = 0.037), confirming an adequate model structure across educational curricula despite pathway differences. These findings offer empirical credence to our integrated theoretical approach.

We also conducted multigroup analysis to examine gender differences in structural relationships. Male students showed a non-significant connection between state anxiety and problem-solving (p = 0.827), whereas female students demonstrated significant relationships across all pathways (Supplementary Fig. 1). The direct effect of self-esteem on problem-solving was stronger in male students (β = 0.41) compared to female students (β = 0.33). Further analyses revealed that the composite scale models corroborated the previous findings, indicating more pronounced direct impacts of self-esteem on problem-solving in male students (β = 0.90) than in female students (β = 0.48) (Supplementary Fig. 2). Composite models also confirmed stronger direct effects of self-esteem on problem-solving in dental students (β = 0.82) compared to medical students (β = 0.55) (Supplementary Fig. 3). The convergence of results between latent variable and composite scale approaches strengthens confidence in the observed group differences. Formal moderation tests indicated that these group differences in individual paths did not constitute statistically significant moderation effects (all p > 0.05).

Discussion

This study employed structural equation modeling to investigate how self-esteem and anxiety relate to problem-solving abilities among medical and dental students, guided by an integrated theoretical framework. Program-specific and gender findings showed that psychological constructs vary in influence across educational contexts. Baumeister et al. [7] demonstrated how self-esteem relates to performance differently across contexts, consistent with stronger self-esteem effects in dental students (β = 0.41) versus medical students (β = 0.34) [15]. These patterns accord with contextual learning perspectives [18, 30], which emphasize that cognition and behavior develop within socially and culturally situated environments. Anxiety management is especially relevant for medical students who frequently encounter abstract diagnostic reasoning tasks. Young et al. [31] reported systematic variation in cognitive load across healthcare education contexts, a pattern consistent with program-specific mediation differences. Consistent with elevated anxiety among medical students, high distress was reported; accordingly, anxiety-mediated pathways were more pronounced in medical education [32–34]. Taken together, these results provide empirical support while delineating context-specific differences. Our outcomes present strong empirical validation for predicted theoretical relationships while revealing important context-specific differences in healthcare education.

Theoretical validation and novel contributions

This investigation supports an integrated framework in which Cognitive Load Theory (CLT), Self-Determination Theory (SDT), and Professional Identity Formation operate as interrelated mechanisms. Self-esteem functions as a multifaceted psychological resource that buffers working memory against anxiety-related intrusions (CLT), maintains competence-need satisfaction (SDT), and facilitates PIF. However, these mechanisms vary between curricula.

Dental students showed significantly stronger direct effects of self-esteem on problem-solving (β = 0.41 vs. β = 0.34), indicating that when extraneous cognitive load is elevated by procedural, multi-step tasks, psychological resources become proportionally more consequential for performance. This distinction between load types is critical: extraneous load stems from instructional design and environmental demands, while intrinsic load arises from content complexity. The documented instructional patterns show that dental education involves earlier procedural training requiring simultaneous management of technical execution and environmental demands, increasing extraneous load, whereas medical education’s extended pre-clinical phase emphasizes conceptual learning with high intrinsic load from complex, interconnected knowledge domains [31]. Al-Saud et al. [35] demonstrated that complex dental procedural tasks impose substantial working memory demands, and psychological factors become more critical in high-load learning environments [36]. Self-esteem reduces anxiety-related intrusive thoughts that burden working memory, as Moran’s [37] meta-analysis revealed [28, 38]. SDT clarifies why anxiety mediates differently across contexts. The stronger relationship between self-esteem and trait anxiety in dental students (β = 0.51 vs. β = 0.36) reflects how immediate, frequent competence feedback in procedural training creates tighter coupling between self-evaluation and performance anxiety [39], with this coupling stronger in dental than medical students. Ryan and Deci [40] established that competence constitutes a fundamental psychological need, and feedback timing significantly influences motivation and emotional reactions. Divaris et al. [41] documented how dental students receiving immediate procedural feedback experience conditions where threats to perceived competence more rapidly translate into persistent anxiety.

Conversely, medical students’ delayed diagnostic feedback allows more temporal separation between self-evaluation and anxiety development, as ten Cate et al. [34] linked to differential motivational patterns. PIF provides the developmental context binding these mechanisms over time. Early clinical exposure in dentistry enhances identity-related appraisal, making competence signals and cognitive load jointly identity-salient. Monrouxe and Rees [42] observed this developmental challenge begins earlier for dental students, interacting with cognitive load management and competence satisfaction [43]. The serial mediation pathway (self-esteem → state anxiety → trait anxiety → problem-solving; β = 0.074, p < 0.001) represents a significant contribution, indicating psychological factors interact dynamically [44]. Eysenck and Derakshan [38] explained how momentary affective interference crystallizes into trait-like patterns influencing cognitive efficiency and professional role internalization [43, 44]. CLT explains the proximal cognitive channel, SDT explains how learning environments tune this channel via competence needs, and PIF explains how repeated appraisal cycles crystallize into identity-level dispositions.

Although formal interaction effects were not detected, explanatory power varied systematically between programs, which is consistent with educational structure influencing which mechanism becomes most salient [34, 40]. The substantial total indirect effect (38.9% of total effects) suggests that addressing anxiety pathways may be as important as building self-esteem for enhancing problem-solving capabilities [45]. A sizeable direct path from self-esteem to problem-solving remained (β = 0.36, p < 0.001), supporting interventions that target both self-esteem and anxiety pathways. To our knowledge, this is the first study to test, via SEM, the RSES → SAI → TAI → PSI pathway in a combined medical–dental student cohort with multigroup comparisons by program and gender, revealing a novel serial mediation mechanism.

Consistent with these theoretical frameworks, our predictions regarding intrinsic and extraneous load differences are grounded in documented curricular patterns: dental education typically involves earlier procedural training requiring simultaneous management of technical execution and environmental demands, increasing extraneous load, whereas medical education’s extended pre-clinical phase emphasizes conceptual learning with high intrinsic load from complex, interconnected knowledge domains [29, 30]. At our institution, these patterns are reflected in curricular structures where medical students face delayed clinical exposure emphasizing diagnostic reasoning, while dental students engage in earlier hands-on practice with immediate procedural demands. Institutions vary in timing and intensity of clinical exposure, and our results suggest that the strength of these psychological pathways may vary systematically with curricular structure, making multi-institutional validation an important direction for future research to establish the boundary conditions and generalizability of these mechanisms across diverse educational contexts.

Comparison with previous research

The present study’s findings align with and extend previous research on psychological factors affecting healthcare students. Stormon et al. [14] reported that 44% of dental students experienced moderate or higher anxiety and 24% experienced depression, comparable to psychological distress in our sample. A systematic review by Rotenstein et al. [32] found that 27.2% of medical students screened positive for depression across 195 studies (n = 129,123). However, these studies documented prevalence rates using correlational approaches rather than testing mechanisms linking self-esteem, anxiety, and problem-solving. Relatedly, personality profiles have been linked to coping patterns among dental students, highlighting mechanism-oriented perspectives, such as coping styles and regulation strategies, that move beyond prevalence estimates and motivate mediation-focused models [46]. Although research has established that self-esteem predicts academic achievement [7, 8, 22] and that anxiety can impair working memory [15, 32, 37], the specific pathways remained unclear.

Our study addresses this gap by employing structural equation modeling to reveal specific pathways through which self-esteem influences problem-solving abilities. We identified that trait anxiety significantly mediates this relationship (β = 0.159, p < 0.001), while state anxiety alone does not (β = 0.084, p = 0.062). Furthermore, we uncovered a novel serial mediation pathway wherein self-esteem influences state anxiety, which affects trait anxiety, which in turn impacts problem-solving abilities (β = 0.074, p < 0.001). In addition, our multigroup analysis revealed differences between medical and dental students, with dental students showing stronger direct self-esteem effects (β = 0.41 vs. β = 0.34), indicating that program-specific intervention strategies are warranted.

Educational implications and evidence-based interventions

Our integrated framework and findings indicate that interventions should be tailored to the distinctive demands of medical and dental curricula while addressing shared psychological risk factors.

In dental education, where self-esteem significantly influences problem-solving (β = 0.41), instructional design needs to encourage procedural self-efficacy by competency-based advancements. It was observed that high-fidelity simulation with clear mastery criteria, systematic checklists, and immediate specific feedback normalizes error management and reinforces technique [35]. Near-peer mentoring, pairing senior and junior students, provides technical guidance and professional identity support [14, 47]. Brief confidence-building interventions, goal setting, self-appraisal aligned with rubrics, and graduated complexity progressions strengthen perceived competence and transfer to clinical problem-solving [18, 46].

In medical education, where anxiety-related pathways are more prominent, curricula should target sustained stress regulation and tolerance of diagnostic uncertainty. Artino et al. and De Vibe et al. [48, 49] demonstrated that mindfulness-based programs, which consist of 6–8-week courses with weekly practice and daily exercises, significantly reduce student stress. Embedding these longitudinally alongside reflective seminars on diagnostic ambiguity, scaffolded uncertainty debriefs, and cognitive-behavioral skills support clinical reasoning under pressure [34, 50]. Ryan and Deci [40] emphasized that competence-supportive feedback remains essential even when assessment and performance are temporally separated. Cognitive load management using structured reasoning frameworks and case-based progressions can protect working memory while building knowledge structures [31, 36]. Universal principles emerge from the serial mediation pathway (β = 0.074, p < 0.001): early state anxiety management prevents trait-anxiety consolidation. Both programs should implement time-point screening at stress peaks (clinical transitions, major examinations, licensing assessments) with rapid referral to counseling, skills workshops, and resilience sessions. Proactive self-esteem enhancement before major challenges creates psychological resilience, buffering anxiety development [7, 51]. Jarvis-Selinger et al. [30] demonstrated that explicit identity formation support through structured mentorship and normalization of struggle develops growth-oriented professional self-concepts. Dyrbye et al. [47] provided systematic evidence supporting such infrastructures.

Program leaders should operationalize these priorities by (i) mapping interventions to curricular milestones (e.g., simulation blocks in dentistry; case-reasoning conferences in medicine), (ii) specifying delivery personnel (faculty coaches, near-peers, counseling services), and (iii) monitoring impact with aligned indicators each semester (PSI, SAI, TAI; Objective Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE)/skills performance; remediation rates; well-being metrics). Because a substantial portion of the total effect is indirect (38.9%), effective programs must pair self-esteem enhancement with anxiety reduction, addressing cognitive load (CLT), motivational support (SDT), and identity formation (PIF) in concert, linking psychological mechanisms to concrete practice without diluting disciplinary aims.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant note. First, this cross-sectional design limits causal inference; longitudinal repeated-measures and cross-lagged models, plus experimental or quasi-experimental interventions, are needed to test mechanisms and temporal precedence [48]. Second, results stem from a single institution; because clinical timing and intensity vary across programs, multi-institutional replication across cultural contexts and institutional settings is required. Third, reliance on self-report may bias estimates and underrepresent performance under cognitive load; future studies should pair surveys with objective assessments (OSCE analytics, error rates, rubric-based procedural scores) and real-time load proxies (secondary-task performance, pupillometry). Our model explained 41% of variance in problem solving, indicating room for additional determinants. Candidate moderators include prior achievement, support systems, perfectionism, and program culture [52]. Expanded models should also evaluate measurement invariance across gender and program and consider multilevel designs linking student factors to instructor practices and curricular structures. Future studies should test these mechanisms longitudinally across institutions using objective performance metrics.

Conclusions

This investigation demonstrates that problem-solving capabilities in healthcare students are influenced by self-esteem through both direct pathways and anxiety-mediated mechanisms. Significant differences were observed between medical and dental students, with dental students showing stronger direct self-esteem effects on problem-solving (β = 0.41 vs. 0.34, p < 0.05). The serial mediation (self-esteem → state anxiety → trait anxiety → problem-solving), accounting for 38.9% of the total effect, illustrates how momentary affective states consolidate into trait-like patterns that influence professional problem-solving.

An integrated framework combining Cognitive Load Theory, Self-Determination Theory, and Professional Identity Formation provides a coherent lens for interpretation. These program-specific differences underscore the need for differentiated interventions: dental programs may emphasize confidence-building through competency-based progressions and timely feedback, while medical programs may prioritize sustained stress management and tolerance for diagnostic uncertainty. Across programs, early identification and targeted support for state anxiety can help prevent its progression into trait anxiety.

Overall, these results support program-specific, context-sensitive educational strategies to strengthen problem-solving development in future healthcare professionals. Multi-institutional longitudinal research is warranted to establish causal mechanisms, assess generalizability, and clarify the temporal ordering among self-esteem, anxiety, and problem-solving.
