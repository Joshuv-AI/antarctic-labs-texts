To the Editor:

It is recommended that all interstitial lung disease (ILD) patients should be considered for a risk assessment prior to air travel due to the risk of hypobaric hypoxia [1]. The hypoxic challenge test (HCT) is a clinical tool used to assess whether in-flight oxygen supplementation is required for a patient. A decrease in arterial oxygen tension (PaO2) to <6.6 kPa or oxygen saturation to <85% during the test indicates the need to recommend supplemental in-flight oxygen, signifying a “failed HCT” [2]. An algorithm developed by Barratt et al. [3] in 2018 used resting capillary oxygen tension ≤9.42 kPa and transfer factor of the lung for carbon monoxide (TLCO) <50% predicted to support decision making on whether 1) supplemental in-flight oxygen was recommended, 2) the patient could fly without oxygen or 3) advice was given for further pre-flight assessment with HCT. The recent British Thoracic Society (BTS) clinical statement on air travel suggests consideration of HCT in patients demonstrating exertional desaturation to <95%, and/or reduced gas transfer (TLCO <50% predicted) or resting PaO2 ≤9.42 kPa [1]. We read with interest the research letter by Robertson et al. [4] in 2025 who have proposed an alternative threshold of resting PaO2 >9.30 kPa as sufficient to advise travel without supplemental oxygen. ILD patients with PaO2 ≤9.30 kPa require a HCT to determine whether in-flight oxygen is required.

Our primary objective was to determine the sensitivity and specificity of this newly proposed algorithm in predicting outcomes of HCT in an independent cohort of ILD patients. We used a cohort of patients with ILD that had been assembled and previously published as part of a separate study validating the Barratt et al. [3] and BTS algorithms [1, 5].

Data were compiled retrospectively from 172 consecutive ILD patients at North Bristol National Health Service Trust attending for HCT between March 2017 and April 2023. The HCT was carried out using the venturi mask method [6]. Capillary blood gases were used to measure PaO2 via the ear lobe. Pulmonary function tests and 6-min walk tests (6MWTs) were performed in accordance with BTS guidelines with parameters collected within ±6 months of HCT performance [7]. The 6MWT was performed in accordance with American Thoracic Society guidelines [7]. Statistical analyses were performed using Statistical Product and Services Solution (IBM, version 23).

Of 172 patients identified, 126 had complete datasets for subsequent analysis (failed HCT, n=18; passed HCT, n=108). Patients were excluded from the final dataset (n=46) due to missing PaO2 values in the medical record (n=35) or because physiology tests were performed >6 months from the HCT (TLCO, n=8; 6MWT, n=3).

Baseline demographic data have been reported previously [5]. In summary, approximately half of the cohort were male (n=66, 52.3%), with a median age of 72 years (interquartile range (IQR) 12.8 years). Lung function showed a median forced vital capacity of 75.0% predicted (IQR 24.8% predicted) and a median TLCO of 45.8% predicted (IQR 18.6% predicted), indicating at least moderate ILD severity. Around half of the patients had a multidisciplinary team diagnosis of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (n=64, 50.8%).

Validating the threshold of PaO2 >9.30 kPa, the algorithm had a sensitivity of 82.4% (89 out of 108 “passed HCT” cases) and a specificity of 61.1% (11 out of 18 “failed HCT” cases). 30 patients (out of 126, 23.8%) would have been advised to undertake HCT.

Our recent validation of the modified BTS algorithm using this same cohort, achieved a sensitivity of 70.3% (45 out of 64 “passed” cases) and a specificity of 83.3% (10 out of 12 “failed” cases), although 50 patients (39.7%) would have been referred for HCT using this model [5]. It was acknowledged that there were a substantial number of false positives, whereby patients would be unnecessarily advised to arrange in-flight oxygen, involving extra planning, documentation and potential costs for the patient that might discourage them from flying. Incorporating the Robertson et al. [4] proposed threshold of PaO2 ≤9.30 kPa into the BTS algorithm [1] improves its sensitivity to 75.9% (identifying most passed cases correctly, 44 out of 58) but reduces the specificity to 71.4% (five out of seven failed cases correctly identified) and with additional referrals for HCT (n=61, 48.4%) compared to the PaO2 ≤9.42 kPa threshold.

The newly proposed threshold by Robertson et al. [4] presents advantages over existing algorithms by potentially reducing the number of HCTs needed, lowering associated costs, decreasing patient burden in terms of time and blood sampling, and minimising false positives that would otherwise result in unnecessary recommendations for supplemental oxygen. However, a significant proportion of patients might be advised that they can fly without supplemental oxygen, despite being at risk of developing significant hypoxia, raising important safety concerns. Incorporating this new PaO2 threshold into the existing BTS algorithm, whilst also retaining TLCO, improves specificity with similar sensitivity, but importantly, substantially increases the number of HCT required.

Several potential limitations of this study should be acknowledged. Firstly, the retrospective design relied on data extracted from medical records, introducing potential bias related to missing information and the exclusion of patients with incomplete datasets (27%). Secondly, the cohort was drawn from a single specialist ILD centre, which may limit the generalisability of the findings to the wider ILD population. The study population was also subject to selection bias by indication; nevertheless, we believe the cohort is broadly representative of patients with ILD undergoing pre-flight assessment. Finally, HCT is not the gold standard for assessing in-flight oxygen requirements when compared with hypobaric chamber testing, which is not widely available.

In conclusion, the proposed algorithm by Robertson et al. [4] offers advantages over existing algorithms, including greater simplicity, fewer required HCTs and reduced false positives. However, a significant proportion of patients could still be advised that they can fly without supplemental oxygen despite being at risk of developing significant hypoxia, highlighting important safety concerns. Importantly, these algorithms do not negate the need for thorough assessment of patient clinical status and relevant comorbidities before air travel. A prospective, multicentre study is needed to further refine practical guidance for patients with ILD, adopting a flight outcome-based approach that carefully balances potential risks and benefits.
