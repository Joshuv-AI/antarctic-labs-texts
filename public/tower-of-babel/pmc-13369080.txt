Comprehensive bioinformatics analysis targeting sphingosine-related genes in head and neck cancer
Authors: Masakazu Hamada, Yoshiaki Yura, Narikazu Uzawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A comprehensive bioinformatics analysis of the expression of sphingosine-related genes and prognosis of patients with head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) was performed using TCGA data and TIMER. A keyword search using sphingosine identified 29 genes, 10 of which were associated with patient survival. The expression of CERS2, CERS5, PLPP3, SGPP1, and S1PR4 significantly differed between tumors and normal tissue. A protein–protein interaction analysis revealed a dense network of these genes. Survival rates decreased in patients with the high expression of CERS2, CERS5, and SGPP1, and increased in those with the high expression of PLPP3 and S1PR4. An examination of the relationship between the expression of these five genes and classical clinical risk factors revealed that the high expression of CESR5 negatively correlated with survival for all classical clinical risk factors, while that of S1PR4 positively correlated with survival. The TIMER analysis showed that CERS5 and S1PS4 expression on tumor-infiltrating immune cells from HNSCC patients was associated with survival. Collectively, these results indicate the potential of CERS5 as a marker of a poor prognosis, and the contribution of S1PR4 to a favorable prognosis through its expression on immune cells. Therefore, the expression of sphingosine-related genes on tumor cells and infiltrating immune cells is important for the prognosis of HNSCC patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-05216-5.

Introduction

Sphingolipids are a class of lipids that primarily localize to cell membranes and regulate the cell signaling pathways involved in cell physiology and biological outcomes, including cellular senescence, differentiation, apoptosis, cell–cell arrest, migration, proliferation, angiogenesis, and inflammation [1, 2]. The main bioactive lipids produced by the sphingolipid synthesis pathway are ceramide, sphingosine, and sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) [3, 4]. Ceramides are well-known sphingolipids in cancer because of their association with several growth inhibitory stimuli and inflammatory signaling effects [5–7]. Previous studies reported the production of ceramides from complex sphingolipids or their synthesis by de novo or salvage pathways [2, 8, 9]. In the de novo biosynthesis of ceramide in the endoplasmic reticulum, the ceramide synthase (CERS)-dependent acylation of dihydrosphingosine produces dihydroceramide, which is then converted by dihydroceramide desaturase 1 (DES1) to ceramide. Ceramide, in turn, is hydrolyzed by ceramidase to generate sphingosine [2]. Sphingosine kinases (SPHKs) phosphorylate sphingosine to produce S1P, which activates a number of intracellular pathways by binding to one of the five G protein-coupled receptors (S1PR) [10, 11].

Head and neck cancer (HNC) is the sixth most common type of cancer globally, with 58,450 new cases of cancer of the oral cavity and pharynx being reported annually in the U.S. [12]. Squamous cell carcinomas (SCC) histologically arising from the mucosa of the oral cavity, pharynx, or larynx account for approximately 95% of HNC cases. Although locally advanced head and neck SCC (HNSCC) is treated using multimodality therapy combining surgery and postoperative adjuvant chemoradiotherapy or platinum-based concurrent chemoradiotherapy, less than 50% of patients respond to this treatment [13, 14]. Ceramide is mainly produced in cancer cells treated with radiotherapy or chemotherapy through the hydrolysis of sphingomyelin by sphingomyelinase (SMase) or its biosynthesis via CERS [15, 16]. The targeting of ceramide-metabolizing enzymes, including DES1, acid ceramidase (ACDase), and SPHK1, and specific chain lengths of ceramide may sensitize resistant cells to various therapies and enhance HNSCC cell killing [17–20]. Previous studies reported that elevated SPHK1 levels were associated with a poor prognosis [21, 22], and, thus, the therapeutic effects of SPHK inhibitors, such as safingol, PF543, and FTY720, against HNSCC have been investigated [23–27].

High-throughput sequencing technology has become an effective tool for the generation of large biological datasets and the identification of promising cancer biomarkers [28, 29]. The use of databases, including The Cancer Gene Atlas (TCGA) and Gene Omnibus (GEO), resulted in the identification of genes that correlated with the progression and prognosis of HNSCC, including fibronectin 1 (FH1), drug adsorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME), transmembrane emp24 structural domain (TMED), IL-17A, myosin VA (MYO5A), collagen triple helix repeat containing 1 (CTHRC1), and plasminogen activator, urokinase (PLAU) [30–36]. This information may also be applied to the establishment of robust models for the diagnosis and treatment of HNSCC. The profiles of immune cell infiltration of the tumor microenvironment (TME) have also been examined using databases [37, 38]. However, the relationship between sphingosine-related genes and the prognosis of patients with HNSCC currently remains unclear. Therefore, we herein conducted a bioinformatics analysis to identify novel prognostic factors related to sphingosine in HNSCC patients using the TCGA, OncoDB, and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) databases. Tumor-infiltrating immune cells in the TME were also investigated using the web platform Tumor Immune Estimation Resource (TIMER) [39, 40].

Survival analysis of 29 sphingosine-related genes in TCGA-HNSCC patients

To clarify the relationship between the expression of the selected genes and patient survival, patients were classified into high and low expression groups, with the former showing higher values than the median. When survival probability (overall survival) was assessed using the Kaplan–Meier method, 10 of the 29 genes were associated with survival: ACER3, neutral ceramidase B (ASAH2B), CERS2, CERS5, ezrin (EZR), phospholipid phosphatase 3 (PLPP3), S1P phosphatase 1 (SGPP1), SPHK2, serine palmitoyltransferase long-chain base subunit 1 (SPTLC1), and S1P receptor 4 (S1PR4), (Table 1, Fig. 3). Survival rates were lower in patients with the high expression of ACER3, ASAH2B, CERS2, CERS5, EZR, SGPP1 and SPTLC1 (Fig. 3A–E, G, I), and were higher in those with the high expression of PLPP3, SPHK2, and S1PR4 (Fig. 3F, H, J). Expression levels of genes differentially expressed in tumor and normal tissues and a protein–protein interaction (PPI) analysis of gene products

The expression levels of the 10 genes associated with survival were measured in tumor and normal tissues, and the results obtained showed that the expression of CERS2, CERS5, SGPP1, and S1PR4 was higher in tumors than in normal tissues, while PLPP3 expression was lower (Fig. 4A). The expression of ACER3, ASAH2B, EZR, SPHK2, and SPTLC1 did not significantly differ between tumors and normal tissues. A PPI analysis of the 29 genes revealed the formation of a dense network, which included 5 genes that were characteristically expressed in tumors (Fig. 4B). Extraction of differentially expressed genes with CERS5 and S1PR4 and a KEGG pathway analysis

To elucidate the mechanisms by which CERS5 and S1PR4 affect patient survival, a two-sample comparative analysis was employed to extract genes whose expression differed between the high and low expression groups. Fold change of ≥ 2.0 or ≤ 0.5 for CERS5 and ≥ 3.0 or ≤ 0.33 for S1PR4 were applied, respectively, which determined approximately 100 up- and down- regulated genes to facilitate KEGG pathway analysis. Ninety-two and 110 up- and down-regulated genes were extracted for CERS5 and S1PR4, respectively (Fig. 7A, B) and were used in the KEGG pathway analysis. CERS5 and S1PR4 expression levels in tumor-infiltrating immune cells and levels of infiltrating immune cells

The heterogeneity of gene expression profiles using microarrays and RNA sequencing is attributed to the intermingling of a number of components, such as tumor cells, stromal cells, and infiltrating immune cells in the TME. TIMER provides a web platform to interactively examine the relationships between immune infiltration and a wide range of factors, such as gene expression, clinical outcomes, somatic mutations, and somatic copy number alterations. TIMER was used in the present study to examine the relationships between CERS5 and S1PR4 expression in bulk tumors and its correction with inferred immune cell infiltration and cumulative survival (overall survival). Survival rates were lower in patients with high CERS5 expression than in those with low CERS5 expression; the P-value was 0.01 by the Log-rank test (Fig. 8A). The Gene module of TIMER revealed the expression of CERS5 in infiltrating immune cells, including B cells, CD8+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, macrophages, neutrophils, and dendritic cells, and, thus, we investigated the relationships between the expression of CERS5 and immune cell infiltration levels. Partial correlation values for B cells, CD8+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, macrophages, neutrophils, and dendritic cells were 0.086, 0.074, 0.203, 0.241, 0.135, and 0.242, respectively (Fig. 8C). The expression of S1PR4 was also detected in these immune cells, with partial correlation values for B cells, CD8+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, macrophages, neutrophils, and dendritic cells of 0.428, 0.581, 0.472, 0.491, 0.479, and 0.531, respectively (Fig. 8D). These results indicate that S1PR4 expression levels correlated with the levels of all types of infiltrating immune cells, with CD8+ T cells and dendritic cells showing stronger correlations.

Discussion

Few studies have investigated the relationships between sphingosine-related gene expression and the prognosis of HNSCC patients. Silva et al. [41] examined sphingolipid-related gene expression profiles in oral cancer and demonstrated that the expression of SET nuclear oncogene (SET), ACER2, SPHK1, and S1PR5 was predominantly up-regulated, while that of ATP-binding cassette subfamily G member 2 (ABCG2), S1PR1, ATP-binding cassette subfamily C member 1 (ABCB1), and sphingolipid transporter 2 (SPNS2) was down-regulated.

Since sphingosine may be exchanged for apoptosis-promoting ceramide and survival-promoting S1P, it plays a major role in sphingolipid-related gene pathways. We herein used sphingosine as a keyword in a bioinformatics analysis of TCGA-HNSCC and normal tissue samples. We initially identified 29 genes associated with sphingosine, 10 of which were found to be associated with the prognosis of HNSCC patients in a Kaplan–Meier analysis: ACER3, ASAH2B, CERS2, CERS5, EZR, PLPP3, SGPP1, SPHK2, and S1PR4. The expression of CERS2, CERS5, PLPP3, SGPP1, and S1PR4 significantly differed between tumor and normal tissues. Univariate and multivariate analyses of 5-year all-cause mortality using these five genes and classical risk factors, namely, smoking, alcohol consumption, age, and the TNM stage, as variables revealed the potential of CERS5 and S1PR4 as biological prognostic markers in HNSCC. High CERS5 expression was associated with a poor prognosis and high S1PR4 expression with a good prognosis. This result was confirmed using the OncoDB database. Silva et al. did not identify CERS5 or S1PR4 as a prognostic marker in oral cancer, which is not consistent with the present results; however, SHPK2 was associated with a favorable outcome in HNSCC patients in both studies.

We then focused on CERS5 and S1PR4 to identify differentially expressed genes and examined biological signaling pathways using KEGG. A pathway analysis showed that CERS5 was enriched in the pathways of Staphylococcus aureus infection, IL-17 signaling, estrogen signaling, and amebiasis, suggesting its association with inflammatory signals and immune responses. A number of pathways were identified for S1PR4, including primary immunodeficiency, Th17 cell differentiation, Th1 and Th2 cell differentiation, cell adhesion, hematopoietic cell lineage, and T-cell receptor signaling pathways. Therefore, the primary function of S1PR4 may be as an immune cell modulator. An analysis of the relationship between infiltrating immune cells and overall survival for CERS5 and S1PR4 using TIMER revealed a poor prognosis for CERS5 and a favorable outcome for S1PR4, which is consistent with studies in the TCGA database containing the gene expression profiles of all cells in tumors. The TIMER Gene module provides correlations between bulk tumor gene expression and estimated immune cell abundance, and does not directly measure expression in individual cell populations. Based on these results, CERS5 and S1PR4 expression in immune cells and tumor cells may both contribute to the prognosis of patients. However, the role of CERS5 on immune cells in patient survival may not be of significance because its P-value in immune cells (0.01) was lower than that in all cells in the tumor (< 0.001). A TIMER analysis also revealed that gene expression levels correlated the levels of infiltrating immune cells (B cells, CD8+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, macrophages, neutrophils, and DCs). Partial correlation values for CERS5 were low, ranging between 0.074 and 0.241. Therefore, CERS5 expression on immune cells, particularly B cells and CD8+ T cells, may not markedly affect the prognosis of patients. On the other hand, the expression level of S1PR4 strongly correlated with the level of infiltration, with partial correlation values ranging between 0.428 and 0.581; CD8+ T cells and dendritic cells showed strong correlations. This result indicates a regulatory role for S1PR4 in the function of infiltrating immune cells.

Six CERS have been identified in mammals [42, 43]. CERS5 plays a role in controlling the development and onset of a number of human cancers. Its expression was identified as an independent risk factor for a poor prognosis in gastric cancer [44]. Furthermore, strong membranous CERS5 staining was detected in 56% of patients with colorectal cancers, and a multivariate Cox regression analysis revealed a decrease in the 5-year overall survival rate. Weak membranous CERS5 staining was associated with apoptosis, and strong staining with autophagy-related proteins, indicating a shift in the CRES5 signaling pathway from apoptosis to autophagy depending on CERS5 expression levels in tumors [45]. Using a multiple omics integration analysis, Wang et al. [46] recently identified CERS5 as a molecular marker in pan-cancer. An immunofluorescence analysis also showed that the CSER5 protein was abundant in the nucleoplasmic reticulum and nuclear membrane of tumor cells. In in vitro studies, the ability of gastric cancer cells to proliferate, migrate, and invade was significantly suppressed by the knockdown of CERS5, while their rate of apoptosis increased. In tumors, S1P exerts its effects by binding to intracellular targets or S1PR1-5 and induces carcinogenesis by promoting tumor growth, angiogenesis, and metastasis [10, 11, 47]. S1PR4, which is highly expressed in lymphocytes, has been associated with the regulation of myeloid cell activation, but not lymphocyte trafficking, which may affect lymphocyte responses [48, 49]. However, Xiong et al. [50] demonstrated the necessity of S1PR1 and S1PR4 on T cells for migration across lymphatic endothelial cells and into lymphatic vessels. A bioinformatics analysis of oral SCC predicted that S1PR4 is a good prognostic marker [51]. Huang et al. [52] classified HNSCC patients according to G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR)-based types and identified S1PR4 as an important regulator that positively correlated with CD8+ T-cell proliferation and cytotoxicity in HNSCC. The up-regulated expression of S1PR4 promoted T-cell function during CAR-T cell therapy. Furthermore, the activation of S1PR4 has been proposed to enhance the toll-like receptor response of resident macrophages, thereby increasing the production of C–C motif chemokine 2 (CCL2), which attracts more macrophages [53]. The present results are consistent with these findings and indicate the potential of S1PR4 expression as a prognostically favorable marker for HNSCC patients, possibly through its effects on the migration and proliferation of anti-tumor immune cells. On the other hand, Olesch et al. [54] found that the ablation of S1PR4 in mouse models of breast cancer and colitis-associated colorectal cancer delayed tumor development and improved treatment success, with an increase in the abundance of CD8+ T cells. The high expression of S1PR1 in esophageal SCC has been identified as a biomarker for a poor prognosis. The silencing of its expression inhibited the proliferation of esophageal SCC cells and promoted their apoptosis [55]. However, it currently remains unclear whether the ablation of S1PR4 on tumor cells has a negative impact on the growth of HNSCC. S1PR4 gene expression on immune cells, but not tumor cells, appears to be crucial for a favorable prognosis in patients with HNSCC (Fig. 9).

Similar to ionizing radiation with X-rays, photodynamic therapy (PDT) induces endoplasmic reticulum stress and ceramide accumulation in mitochondria involves the de novo synthesis pathway and CERS, leading to cell death by apoptosis [56]. This finding suggests an important role for CERS in the efficiency of RT and PDT against HNSCC. Machine learning to assess drug sensitivity showed that sensitivity to doxorubicin and vincristine, but not to cisplatin or carboplatin, was higher in patients overexpressing CERS5 [52]. The S1PR functional antagonist FTY720, which binds to S1PR1, is currently used as an immunosuppressive agent to treat multiple sclerosis [57]. FTY720 also has potential as an antitumor agent because it binds to the C-terminal region of SET, thereby preventing SET from interacting with protein phosphatase 2 (PP2A) and reactivating PP2A, a tumor suppressor gene [58, 59]. FTY720 has also been shown to increase the efficacy of paclitaxel for cisplatin-resistant oral SCC [27]. Further searches for sphingosine-related genes will provide important insights into the mechanisms underlying resistance to radio- and chemotherapies in HNSCC and contribute to the development of novel therapies that promote the migration of cytotoxic T cells via S1PR while still exerting anti-proliferative effects on cancer cells. It is important to acknowledge the limitations of this study, particularly the use of only the TCGA-HNSCC and OncoDB cohorts. This study was limited to single-gene analyses, and the effects of co-expression of CERS5 and S1PR4 on prognosis, as well as the underlying molecular mechanisms, remain unexplored. These points should be addressed in future study. Furthermore, the present study is entirely based on bioinformatic analyses, thus the proposed mechanistic roles of CERS5 and S1PR4 in promoting tumorigenesis and modulating immune cell function remain speculative. Future research, including in vitro and in vivo and prospective studies, is needed to address the limitations. Nevertheless, it should be emphasized that the results of this study demonstrate an association between the expression of CERS5 and S1PR4 and the prognosis of patients with HNSCC. To our knowledge, this is the first report of such findings and provides a basis for further experiments to elucidate the role of these identified genes.

In conclusion, survival rates were decreased in HNSCC patients with high CERS5 expression in tumors, and increased in those with high S1PR4 expression. Furthermore, CERS5 and S1PS4 expression on tumor-infiltrating immune cells was associated with the survival of HNSCC patients. Collectively, the present results indicate that sphingosine-related genes in tumor cells and infiltrating immune cells have an important impact on the prognosis of HNSCC patients.

Materials and methods

Data collection from databases

Using Subio Platform [60] software ver 1.24.5859 (Subio Inc. Aichi, Japan), we obtained RNA-Seq count data (HTSeq version) on TCGA-HNSC (499 primary tumor samples and 45 solid normal tissue samples) from the GDC Data Portal [61] (accessed on 20 March 2019.). The same workflow was used for TCGA RNA-Seq and our RNA-seq data, apart from the thresholds employed. Genes with read counts below 50 across all samples were excluded. We transformed the counts to log2 ratios relative to the average of solid normal tissue samples and removed genes with log2 ratios between -1 and 1 in 500 of 545 samples from further analysis. Gene annotation information displayed in the ‘‘Annotations’’ tab of the Subio platform was searched using the keyword ‘‘Sphingosine’’. Genes containing the term ‘‘Sphingosine’’ in their annotation fields were extracted, resulting in 29 candidate genes for subsequent analyses. The expression analysis was cross-validated using the TCGA-based platforms, OncoDB (https://oncodb.org/) [62] and TIMER2.0 (https://cistrome.shinyapps.io/timer/) [40].

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis

Survival analysis was performed as in our previous studies [36, 63]. To compare survival times using the Kaplan–Meier method, TCGA-HNSC primary tumor samples were divided into the following groups for each of the 29 selected genes: those with counts higher or lower than the median. Survival differences were assessed using both the Log-rank test and the Wilcoxon test to account for overall and early survival differences.

Extraction of genes differentially expressed with CERS5 and S1PR4

Regarding CERS5, 92 up- and down-regulated genes were extracted between the high and low expression gene groups using the criteria of a fold change ≥ 2.0 or ≤ 0.5 and p < 0.05 in the Mann–Whitney U test. Concerning S1PR4, 110 up- and down-regulated genes were extracted between the high and low expression groups using the criteria of a fold change of ≥ 3.0 or ≤ 0.33 and p < 0.05 in the Mann–Whitney U test.

Functional pathways and PPI analyses

The molecular pathways of the selected genes for KEGG pathways were investigated using the Database for Annotation, Visualization, and Integrated Discovery server, with a focus on identifying significantly enriched KEGG pathways to provide insights into signaling pathways. These genes were then used to establish a PPI network according the STRING online database (https://string-db.org/ accessed on 29 February 2024). The most significant modules in PPI networks were then visualized.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using the Student’s t-test with Microsoft Excel (Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA). Results are expressed as means ± SD. P-values < 0.05 indicated a significant difference. In the survival analysis, the hazard ratio (HR) relative to the indicated reference (ref) value, its 95% confidence interval (CI), and P-value (values < 0.05 are indicated in bold) for the Cox hazard model are shown. Following a proper assessment of the assumptions of Cox regression models using the survival package, HR and its 95% CI were calculated by a Cox regression analysis.
