Freedom of choice supports social complexity in chimpanzees
Authors: Andrew Halloran, Jake A Funkhouser, Sarah Poirier, Darby Proctor, Caroline Griffis, Skye Conners, Ashley Cooley, David Morgan, Crickette Sanz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryFission-fusion sociality offers almost infinite options for individuals to make self-determined choices about how to range across space, spend time in different activities, and associate with conspecifics. Yet, how such freedoms contribute to the construction of functional social groups has received little attention. In this investigation, we found that sanctuary-living chimpanzees residing within spacious outdoor habitats with choice among a variety of social partners formed integrated communities that showed emergent properties of the fission-fusion social systems. Social network topology varied across communities, indicating species-typical intraspecific variation in social complexity. This variation was positively associated with indicators of behavioral diversity and wellbeing. Our results offer newfound understanding of the factors that facilitate fission-fusion societies, which, in turn, foster agency, social belonging, and culture that enable apes to thrive.Subject areas: Wildlife behavior, Zoology, Evolutionary biology

Introduction

Individual agency can be operationalized as the capacity to make self-determined acts in the face of multiple options to produce effects that ultimately shape one’s own experiences and surroundings.1,2,3 Related to agency, “freedom of choice” has been invoked across many disciplines and applied settings to describe one’s ability to express individual agency to make choices based on their own preferences. In addition to humans, chimpanzees have been cited as incorporating freedom of choice in their evaluation of social actions.4,5,6 Compared with species who live in cohesive groups, species with fission-fusion communities have greater freedom to choose how they construct subunits (i.e., subgroups or parties) of a flexible composition that vary non-randomly in membership across time and space.7,8,9 These choices produce measurable differences in the strength and type of social relationships that can be compared across communities to better understand the variation in fission-fusion dynamics.10 The benefits of such flexibility may include the identification and exploitation of key resources, reduction of predation pressure, and even an enhanced ability to behave flexibly across a diversity of contexts to successfully navigate structures of social power, culture, and wellbeing (Figure 1). Greater freedom of choice also allows individuals to leverage social relationships for present or future behavioral activities and exchanges.11 In this study, we assessed the social structure of ten chimpanzee communities residing on island habitats to explore how the freedom of choice may promote variation in the expression of fission-fusion sociality and associated outcomes, such as behavioral diversity and cultural behaviors.

Freedom of choice in fission-fusion societies

It is commonly accepted that chimpanzees (as well as other primates and social mammals) are capable of making active social choices, capabilities enhanced by theory of mind and the ability to perceive, recognize, manipulate, and predict the behavior of conspecifics.12 There is further evidence that chimpanzees exercise the cognition necessary to make choices in recruiting and coordinating joint actions in cooperative tasks,5,13,14,15 collaborations,16,17,18 and joint commitments toward common goals.19,20,21,22,23,24,25 The role of individual agency likely has considerable influence on the interplay between all aspects of social interactions including patterns of social relationships, emergent social network properties, and species-typical social organization.26,27 However, there is a need for further research on how the freedom of choice of individual chimpanzees contributes to the construction of their complex fission-fusion societies.

“Fission-fusion” sociality was initially used to describe seemingly endogamous hunter-gatherer groups splitting into two or more factions due to social tension.28,29,30 The term was later extended to describe the flexible nature of some non-human primate group sizes31,32 and then subsequently applied to describe the amount of spatiotemporal cohesion and compositional variation across subunits within communities.7 Importantly, this social structure allows individuals to make active social choices and flexibly respond via diverse behavioral repertoires to the local social and ecological environment.33 Such social flexibility and behavioral diversity produce varying levels of fission-fusion complexity as indicated by individuals choosing to associate with a diverse array and number of available conspecifics over time.34,35 While variable socioecological pressures have been shown to influence fission-fusion dynamics, variation in subgrouping patterns across communities in similar environments remains poorly understood.9

Fission-fusion social dynamics have been reported across a wide range of taxa, including early and modern human populations.36 This is a key aspect of the social structure observed in free-ranging chimpanzees,37 bonobos,38 spider monkeys,39 muriquis,40 elephants,41 dolphins,42 and hyenas.43 To effectively study the variation in subgrouping patterns in any of these fission-fusion societies, it would be ideal to have the ability to observe all or most individuals across multiple social groups living in comparable environments. However, this is nearly always impossible in the wild due to the spatiotemporal variation inherent in this social structure. Therefore, sanctuary settings provide opportunities to address the scarcity of research on subgrouping variation in fission-fusion dynamics.

As a complement to studies in natural habitats, sanctuaries can provide the opportunity to identify and assess aspects of the environment that may influence this type of social structure. However, human-managed fission-fusion groups—where care staff actively attempt to reproduce fission-fusion social dynamics by dividing groups across multiple, separated spaces—reduce the individual agency of the animals in those groups by pre-determining the spatial and temporal timeline of party compositions. Alternatively, providing opportunities for individuals in captive settings to express freedom of choice and agency in their social interactions within spacious habitats with minimal human influence may elicit emergent fission-fusion sociality and behavioral diversity analogous to that of their counterparts in the wild.44 Such opportunities should include access to a variety of social partners, places to socialize, sizes and diversities of spatial landscapes, and the timescale where social interactions of various types can occur. Ideally, this type of setting would simulate the array of complex social decisions that their wild counterparts encounter daily, eliciting fission-fusion social systems with similar social properties. Similarly, these types of fission-fusion social systems should stimulate the type of behavioral diversity and inter-community cultural variation that is characteristic of wild chimpanzee populations. The chimpanzees at Save the Chimps sanctuary—the site of our current investigation—are managed with a philosophy of care that is centered on providing these types of freedoms to choose.

Chimpanzees are of particular interest both due to their close evolutionary relationship to humans as well as their complex social cognition and the need to provide adequate opportunities for them to use those abilities to promote psychological wellbeing in captivity. Wild chimpanzee communities range in size from 15 to more than 200 individuals.25,45,46,47,48,49,50,51,52,53 Chimpanzee communities routinely divide into parties or subgroups consisting of 2–25 individuals.39,54,55,56,57,58 The size and composition of the party may change several times within a day, with the Taï population spending 14 to >126 min together in a party.25 The flexible size and spatiotemporal distribution of these subunits may enable chimpanzees to cope with predation pressure,59 increase male access to sexually receptive females,57,60,61 avoid or reduce competition for resources,39,56,62,63 engage in and transmit tool-assisted foraging skills,64,65 mitigate travel distances,66 and respond to changes in group demographics.67 Wild chimpanzees also exhibit cultural variation in a diverse array of behavioral domains, including social and tool-use behaviors.68,69 The species-typical cultural variation of chimpanzees is supported, in part, by their considerable behavioral diversity,70 from which community-specific, socially learned customs can take root.71 Additionally, chimpanzees living in sanctuary environments have been described to exhibit community-specific patterns of behavioral diversity and cultural variation,72,73 where both have been described as indicators of positive wellbeing.74,75,76,77 While the aforementioned social and ecological factors that shape the fission-fusion dynamics of wild chimpanzees have been described, the relationship between the importance of having freedom of choice in making social decisions, the expression of fission-fusion dynamics, behavioral diversity, and cultural variation remains largely unexamined.

In the current investigation, we examined how the freedom of choice (i.e., available space and individualized agency in making social choices with whom to associate) facilitates variation in fission-fusion dynamics of chimpanzees. We evaluated differences in the subgrouping patterns and social network properties of ten communities of chimpanzees (n = 185 individuals) at Save the Chimps sanctuary. Each group comprised 14–23 adult chimpanzees living in large, outdoor, island habitats (1.5–2.2 ha), which provide the opportunity to express freedom of choice in where and with whom they spend their time in ways that are likely similar to their wild counterparts. We quantified social choices through a combination of behavioral and geospatial indicators that were validated as species typical and appropriate for the island habitats of these chimpanzees. We then used spatial proximity and party co-attendance as an indicator of social choice in this setting and applied standard metrics of social complexity to determine if the fission-fusion dynamics of these communities were similar to those reported from wild chimpanzee populations and other species with similar social systems. Our party co-attendance association dataset is ideal for this investigation as this is the only type of data that can yield standardized and systematic comparisons of social complexity (entropy,35 association diversity,78 and coefficient of variation79). Further, we examined the effects of observed variation in social complexity on community-specific patterns of cultural behaviors72,80 (grooming,72,73,80,81,82,83,84,85,86 play,87,88,89,90 and tool use91,92) and interindividual variation in behavioral diversity (one’s variance in engagement of cultural behaviors, an indicator of positive wellbeing76,77) as potential benefits of freedom of choice and the expression of fission-fusion sociality.

Results

Across 10 chimpanzee communities composed of an average of 19 individuals (SD: 3; range: 14–22 chimpanzees per group; and a total of 185 individuals), we recorded 2,829 video group scans (n = 282.8 ± 61.5 per group).

Our results indicate that chimpanzees at Save the Chimps live in integrated communities that are composed of individuals who regularly associate with one another in accordance with the fluid nature of the fission-fusion social structure. These results are based on measures of party co-attendance within 50 m that are both comparable to wild chimpanzee parties as well as specifically validated in this study as representative of these sanctuary chimpanzees living in spacious island habitats. Simple ratio association indexes, which indicate the strength of each dyad’s relationship, ranged from 0 to 0.73 (0.13 ± 0.07). The density of relationships ranged from 0.08 to 0.18 (mean = 0.13, SD = 0.02), which reflects the average strength of dyadic associations across the network, while accounting for both the strength and variation in social ties. Both measures fit well within the documented range of wild chimpanzee social networks derived both from direct observation and remote monitoring methods: 0.02–0.38.93 Further, dyadic association patterns across all communities were distributed non-randomly (CV = 0.48 ± 0.08), and community modularity scores did not meet the standard threshold, which would indicate hierarchical community clusters of a fixed composition (leading eigenvector modularity: 0.04 ± 0.01; threshold: 0.30).94 In other words, there is no evidence of fixed, hierarchically structured patterns of subgrouping among individuals across these ten chimpanzee communities.49,93,95 Table 1 details the group demography and resulting social complexity scores for each chimpanzee group in this investigation. We observed levels of social complexity in these chimpanzee communities that are indicative of fission-fusion social structures. Observed group-level social complexity scores (a metric of the overall degree of variation and diversity in party compositions within communities) ranged from 6.98 to 8.11 (mean = 7.57, SD = 0.36). The social complexity that individuals experience (a metric of the variation and diversity of each individual’s associations) ranged from 3.32 to 7.52 (mean = 6.00, SD = 0.78). The social complexity observed herein fits well within the distribution of scores that have been observed in other fission-fusion social taxa (chimpanzee group: H = ∼8.5 to 10.0, individual H range = ∼1.0–10.0; and spider monkey group: H = ∼8.0, individual H range = ∼4.0–9.5) and was much greater than that observed in multilevel (non-fission-fusion) gelada societies (group H = ∼5.0, individual range H = ∼2.0–6.0).34,35 Additionally, the social complexity scores (H) that we observed are much closer to the maximum expected entropy for groups and datasets of this size (current study: group JS distance mean = 0.29, SD = 0.10, individual JS distance range = 0.66–0.99; compared to wild chimpanzees35: group JS distance mean = 0.73, SD = 0.03, individual JS distance range = 0.86–0.99), thereby indicating that the social complexity we observed is greater than what has been documented in other fission-fusion social systems. This trend was similar for our observed association diversity scores, which indicate the number of possible, unique classes of different types of relationships (S = average 0.80, SD = 0.26, and range = 0.12–1.17 across 3 ± 1 classes of relationships) as it is within the range of fission-fusion bottlenose whale societies (S = 0.69)78 and outside of the range of values typical of multilevel social structures that are characterized by more distinct classes of relationships, for example, sperm whales (S = 0.91)78 and mountain gorillas (S = ∼0.90–1.60).101

We observed considerable variation in community-specific patterns of behavioral diversity in grooming, tool-use, and play behaviors across communities. We observed a total of 6,108 parties or subunits (610.8 ± 111.1 parties per group) of 3.1 ± 2.3 chimpanzees per party (range: 1–17 individuals). During each group scan, we observed 2.3 ± 1.0 parties per group (range: 1–7 parties) across a variety of behavioral contexts (Figure 3). During focal follows, we observed 537 allo-grooming parties (53.7 ± 24.6 parties per group, 2.8 ± 0.2 chimpanzees per party, range: 2–10 chimpanzees per party). Across group scans and focal follows, we observed 46 bouts of social play (5.8 ± 6.7 bouts per group, 2 ± 0.1 chimpanzees per bout, range: 2–4 chimpanzees per bout) and 38 tool-using parties at several simulated termite mounds (4.8 ± 3.2 tool-using parties per group, 3.0 ± 1.1 chimpanzees per party, range: 1–7 chimpanzees per party). Notably, these results support the idea that higher levels of social complexity are likely to support cultural variation in chimpanzee societies (Figure 4).

The results of our GLMM analyses indicated that individual-level social complexity (entropy, Hi) increases with behavioral diversity (an indicator of positive wellbeing) and age (collective significance: χ2 = 17.85, df = 3, p < 0.01; Figure 5). Behavioral diversity had a significant positive effect on individual entropy (estimate = 17.10, SE = 4.70, Z = 3.64, p < 0.001), indicating that greater behavioral diversity and wellbeing are associated with higher social complexity. Year of birth also showed a significant negative effect (estimate = −0.11, SE = 4.70, Z = −2.03, p = 0.042), suggesting that younger chimpanzees tend to have lower individual levels of social complexity. In contrast, sex did not have a significant effect on entropy (estimate = 1.59, SE = 0.99, Z = 1.60, p = 0.110). Discussion

By providing sanctuary-living chimpanzees with opportunities to express freedom of choice and agency, a fission-fusion social system has emerged that mirrors the social interactions, behavioral diversity, and cultural variation across communities observed among their wild counterparts. We first used multiple geospatial analyses to validate the use of 50-m search areas to define subunit co-attendance as an indicator of social choice for these sanctuary chimpanzees and potential fission-fusion social dynamics. Using social network analysis, we then quantified the levels of social complexity at Save the Chimps and found them to be within the range of wild populations and other species with similar social systems. We also detected evidence of variation in subgrouping dynamics, social network topology, and cultural variation in grooming, play, and tool use across groups. Furthermore, at the individual level, our results indicated a positive relationship between the ability to express freedom of choice, social complexity, culture, behavioral diversity, and wellbeing. The theoretical implications of these findings are relevant to understanding social integration and culture, while also providing practical applications for improving approaches to captive management that promote opportunities for all individuals to thrive.

Unlike cohesive social groups where most individuals are within proximity of all group mates, fission-fusion systems involve an inherent spatiotemporal scale that makes it rare for most individuals of the same community to be at the same place at the same time. Additionally, likely because fission-fusion parties vary in composition across behavioral contexts, individuals must track their knowledge and the knowledge states of others by remembering what they saw, encoding who else was present when the event occurred, and inferring the lack of knowledge by those who were not present.102,103 Equipped with this social information and more, individuals can enact the freedom to choose whom to associate with based on the information they may need to acquire or relationships they need to maintain to optimize the accuracy of their predicted outcomes of social interactions.21 For example, individuals in cohesive groups (e.g., geladas, mountain gorillas, and sperm whales) can use selective attention cues to sort social information from conspecifics who are likely always present and, therefore, typically share the same opportunities to learn from and about others.104 In contrast, individuals in fission-fusion social systems (e.g., humans, chimpanzees, spider monkeys, elephants, and dolphins) must actively seek specific information about the knowledge and relationships of others as uncertainty would arise from social shifts that occurred in their absence.19,105 As fission-fusion party compositions vary across space and time, intraindividual differences arise in social experiences; thus, individuals respond differently to the same social options, and enhanced cognitive mechanisms control how individually determined strategies are enacted. Indeed, we would promote future research to comprehensively examine how and why chimpanzees choose to enact their individual behavioral strategies, both in party co-attendance across contexts34 as well as in other type of social interactions.87,106

Maintaining a level of social complexity indicative of fission-fusion sociality requires numerous social and cognitive competencies. For chimpanzees and other fission-fusion taxa, social complexity is likely a product of learned mechanisms to express social agency. The older adult chimpanzees (31.2 ± 9 years) studied in this investigation have experienced a wide variety of developmental contexts, often including some degree of atypical social opportunities early in life (e.g., some caught in the wild as infants, others born and raised in laboratory nurseries with humans or peers, a few raised with their mothers, and others as pets within human homes or in the entertainment industry). Despite this, our results indicated that social complexity increases with age in the chimpanzees at Save the Chimps. Given that a majority of ape social skills are learned,107 these chimpanzees learned their social skills throughout their adult lifetimes during experiences with unrelated peers.108 Given experimental evidence that chimpanzees consider the freedom of choice of others, it seems likely that they make adaptive social choices to fulfill their present or future needs.4,5,6 Tracking variation in chimpanzee decision-making strategies and the functional outcomes of these strategies would be an exciting avenue for further investigation.

The cognitive mechanisms and social competencies implicated in constructing and navigating social bonds within these chimpanzee communities are likely to confer both functional and psychological benefits.109 While human psychology has come to recognize the emergent and reinforcing system that produces positive affect from the development of social competencies, relationships, integration, and belonging,110 the interplay of these factors in producing positive social wellbeing in non-humans has yet to be examined. Emerging from one’s patterns of direct relationships is their indirect embeddedness within extended networks of social support, with each type of social integration benefiting highly connected individuals.111 Individuals also generate perceptions and characterizations about their relationships with others, for example, peoples’ sense of belonging has also been tied to many positive indicators of wellbeing.112 Furthermore, the ability to engage in community-specific behavioral diversity, social customs, and cultural norms—when balanced with cognitive load and potential stressors—produces positive affect as these are additional ways that someone can signal their belonging to a group.110,113 The social complexity, behavioral diversity, and cultural variation that we observed mirror the fission-fusion social systems of wild chimpanzees and thus likely produce species-typical levels of integration and belonging.

Humans flexibly engage in social interactions within close friend groups, households, families, neighborhoods, and groups across further distances and wider periods than other species. The cooperation, prosocial activities, exchange of knowledge, and cumulative culture of hunter gatherers are enhanced by living in societies where fluid fission-fusion dynamics are expressed across a multitude of nested structures that contain many individuals.114,115,116,117,118,119,120,121 It has even been suggested that the increased encephalization over the course of hominin evolution may have been influenced by the greater cognitive demands associated with fission-fusion sociality and associated social complexity, such as remembering, constructing, and maintaining social relationships over large distances and long durations.122,123 Prior to this investigation, the benefits of social variability conferred by fission-fusion sociality had only rarely been studied outside of human societies. Our results indicate that behavioral diversity, culture, and wellbeing are associated with social complexity in the fission-fusion societies of chimpanzees and help to broaden our understanding of the influence of fission-fusion across hominin evolutionary history.

Social complexity increases the variation and accumulation of cultural traits, as evidenced in laboratory observations, computer simulations, ethnographic studies of hunter-gatherer societies, and our investigation of chimpanzee societies.116,124,125,126,127,128,129 What remains elusive is whether social complexity is a cultural trait. In its most general form, evidence of community-specific cultural traits can be identified as those that either arise through direct observational learning107 or can be inferred as socially learned through the exclusion of genetic and ecological explanations.69 At its core, culture requires social learning and the diffusion of cultural information across community members. Because social learning is dictated by the timing and spacing of repeated associations with a diverse or fixed set of social partners, variation in social complexity has been tied to the variation in social learning and, therefore, a bedrock feature of cultural variation. We found evidence suggesting that variation in social complexity is associated with variation in other cultural contexts and wellbeing across neighboring communities of chimpanzees that are composed of almost all unrelated adult individuals who live in the same ecological environment. Others have argued that this variation alone indicates community-specific levels of social dynamics that can be tied to tolerant, cultural, and even prosocial roots.72,80,130,131 Given that our methods for calculating social complexity are robust for comparisons across groups,132 the evidence we present is a compelling account of group-specific social complexity representing a cultural trait. Further, we assert that social complexity is a core universal aspect of chimpanzee cultures that can function to improve collective senses of social belonging and integration in social groups, both in captive and wild settings.107,133

We have shown that chimpanzees at Save the Chimps express freedom of choice in making decisions about where, with whom, and how to spend their time. The level of social complexity we observed in this population is striking, particularly when considering individual histories of these chimpanzees and given the myriad challenges involved in providing physical and social environments that may promote fission-fusion sociality. Innovative management practices that provide maximum individual determination and social agency, like the large open-air outdoor island habitats at Save the Chimps, could be applied to all social mammals as they represent a set of considerations that can be tailored to any taxa or social system. Our findings are also applicable to other managed contexts, as generating a better understanding about the emergence of social complexity, behavioral diversity, and culture aids in expanding the list of species-specific traits that are crucial for population maintenance, wellbeing, conservation, and survival.134,135,136

Limitations of the study

We would encourage future consideration of how and why chimpanzees with freedom of choice enact specific behavioral strategies in a variety of social contexts (e.g., competing for and maintaining social status, building relationships with potential mates, and considering indirect relationships with conspecifics who are not preferred partners). Study groups that encompass a greater variety of community compositions (e.g., number of immatures and presence of kin) and including data on intrinsic interindividual differences (e.g., precise accounts of previous life experiences137 and personality138) would also advance our understanding of why chimpanzees make the social choices that they do.

Experimental model and study participant details

Save the Chimps Inc. is located in Fort Pierce, Florida and provides lifelong care for chimpanzees whose previous life experiences extend across research laboratories, the pet trade, and the entertainment industry. At the time of data collection, the 10 communities included in this investigation ranged in size from 14 to 22 (19 ± 3) individuals and encompassed 185 individuals (n = 105 females, 80 males) that ranged in estimated age from 10 to 57 (31.2 ± 9) years. On average, communities were comprised of 10.4 females (SD = 2.8, min = 4, max = 14) and 8.0 males (SD = 2.3, min = 4, max = 11). None of the communities at Save the Chimps contain immature chimpanzees. Male chimpanzees at the sanctuary have been vasectomized and so there was no need for the female chimpanzees to be contracepted. Air Force group was an exception, in that all of the females were contracepted for a short period to determine if that might reduce social tensions.

Each group of chimpanzees has access to a large outdoor island habitat (1.5–2.2 ha, with multiple climbing structures and natural features) and one indoor building of multiple rooms. The islands are separated by waterways that are greater than approximately 30m. The chimpanzee have free access to the outdoor habitat and indoor rooms, except during cleaning or scheduled mealtimes. All island habitats were constructed with comparable vegetation and environmental features.

The chimpanzees are served multiple meals of fresh fruits and vegetables each day within their indoor building, which is not likely to influence the social groupings that we observed in the outdoor habitat. Food is sometimes scattered in the outdoor habitat to stimulate natural foraging behavior, but the chimpanzees were not fed at specific locations on their outdoor islands in a way that would systematically bias our results. The chimpanzees have ad libitum access to water and are provided multiple daily opportunities for enrichment. Some enrichment activities may include food items, but these are usually very small portions that last only a short period of time.

Method details

Video data collection

From February 2020-2022 we recorded 2,829 video group scans (n = 282.8 ± 61.5 per group) and 3,349 video focal follows (n = 289.4 ± 59.0 per group) using remotely operated, high-definition, high-resolution, pan-tilt-zoom (20-40x zoom) video cameras positioned on the external mid-point of each group’s building oriented toward their outdoor island habitat. The camera’s view was manipulated from the furthest leftmost visible point of the island in complete clockwise 360-degree views. As chimpanzees were encountered, observers increased the magnification to record each individual’s identifying features (e.g., face, body, anogenital region) and behavior for no more than 10-s each (Figure 3). Video group scans were no longer than 10-min in total duration and circled the island a minimum of two times. Only the first observation of every individual is included in this dataset. During the collection of video focal follows, observers manipulated the camera’s field of view to first record each individual’s identifying features and then followed the action of the focal for 10-min while keeping at least 3-meters of visible space around the focal and all proximate conspecifics in view. We also studied patterns of grooming, play, and tool use by compiling all occurrences of social play and co-attendance in tool-using parties at faux termite mounds across all group scan and video focal follow files. All occurrences of grooming parties were exclusively compiled from video focal follow files. All video files were recorded as color.MP4 files, stored on external hard drives, converted to a standard 1290 x 1080 aspect at 30 frames per second in.AVI format, then linked and scored in INTERACT version 18.1.139

Subunit co-attendance search area criteria

Decisions about including individuals in the same fission-fusion subunit would ideally represent the choices of individuals to spend time with some conspecifics but not others.79,140 Deriving appropriate, meaningfully relevant criteria for inclusion within subunits is critical as incorrectly defined search areas will yield social networks that misrepresent real network properties.141 Subunit co-attendance search distances that are too conservative (i.e., small) will likely under-record preferred social relationships whereas distances that are too generous (i.e., large) will overestimate dyadic relationships that are not preferred or even avoidant.79 The application of GPS tags to defining biologically relevant spatial and temporal association criteria has been effective in common waxbills,142 sociable weavers,143 sheep,144 horses,145 and olive baboons.146 However, none of these methods have been extended to non-invasive observational methods nor to fission-fusion societies.

Therefore, we used multiple geospatial analysis tools to define meaningful and biologically relevant search criteria to assign individual chimpanzees to subunits with conspecifics. Video group scans with two or more visible chimpanzees were randomly selected from across the study period for geospatial analysis (N = 750 video group scans, 75 scans per island, no more than one scan per day). The location of each visible chimpanzee was plotted as an event point in ArcGIS Pro 2.9 (N = 6295 points, 7 ± 3.5 individuals per group scan).

First, we analyzed the two-dimensional spatial distribution of chimpanzees across their outdoor island spaces using re-iterative average nearest neighbor analyses for each group scan. Average nearest neighbor analyses calculate each point’s distance to their nearest neighbor and if this distribution is less than the average for an expected null distribution of randomly distributed points, the observed distribution is considered to be clustered147 (Table S1). The distribution of chimpanzees was found to be significantly clustered in all group scans (average distance to nearest neighbor, mean = 10.29m, SD = 2.84m [CI: 9.65, 10.94], expected average distance to nearest neighbor under the null hypothesis of randomly dispersed points, mean = 34.27, SD = 2.61 [CI: 33.68, 34.86], Z mean = −11.44, SD = 1.49 [CI: −11.75, −11.10], p < 0.001).

Next, we investigated the spatial scale of chimpanzee point clustering across their outdoor island spaces using unweighted multi-distance spatial clustering analyses via re-iterative Ripley’s K-function algorithm for each group scan. This same algorithm has been used to define wild chimpanzee core areas and neighborhoods of overlap in Gombe, Tanzania.148,149,150 Ripley’s K-function tests for significant clustering or dispersion of event points at incremental distance radii by computing the average number of neighboring points at distances closer than the defined search radius. If the average number of neighboring points at each search radius increment is higher than the average concentration of points throughout the study area, the distribution is clustered.151,152 In our analyses of each group scan, the search for nearest neighbors was contained with island boundaries and started at 2-meters increasing incrementally by 2-meters thereafter (Table S2). The distribution of chimpanzees was found to be significantly clustered across all spatial scales (2–120 m). However, the observed clustering coefficient (K-value) dropped below the expected distribution at 72-meters, indicating this is the maximum meaningful search criteria (Figures S2 and S3, and Table S4).

Finally, we validated the results of average nearest neighbor and multi-point spatial clustering analyses by performing independent t-tests on the average distance between geospatial points within and outside defined clusters.153 By doing so, we tested the assumption that clusters are aggregations of chimpanzees where the distance between any two within-cluster individuals is less than their distance to any individual not in the cluster.154,155 To do this, we re-iteratively searched each group scan and assigned each chimpanzee within a given radius to the same cluster. We then calculated the near point distances for all points within group scans and conducted a t test to examine differences in these distance distributions for points within and outside of clusters (Figure S1 and Table S4).

We calculated validation tests for distance intervals between the calculated average distance to nearest neighbor (10 m, a search distance that would produce conservative cluster assignments) and the distance band typically used in field observations of wild chimpanzee party associations (50 m). All distanced intervals used in validation tests were found to produce significant clustering of points (all intervals below 72 m, above), thereby validating the multi-distance clustering results (Table S5).

At the 50-meter distance, the average distance between points within clusters best matched results from average nearest neighbor analyses (approx., 10 m; Table S6). Additionally, the effect of the t test statistic for the 50-meter distance band was the greatest and was accompanied by the narrowest 95% confidence interval (unchanged from 40-meters). The 50-meter distance band also represented an optimal trade-off of inclusive but conservative cluster assignments and few lone individuals outside of any cluster. Given that (1) these results validate significant chimpanzee clustering within 50-meteres, (2) are supported by descriptive statistics that match the idea that clusters are units of chimpanzees where the distance between any two within-cluster individuals is significantly less than their distance to any individual not in the cluster, and (3) that this distance band is a commonly used metric for defining associations of wild chimpanzees25,34,35,156,157,158,159,160,161,162 we elected to use a 50-meter association criteria to define subunit attendance in our analyses of social complexity at Save the Chimps.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Social complexity analyses

Subunits were defined as all individuals that were within 50 m. Every visible chimpanzee was scored as belonging to a unique subunit based on the location where they were first observed during the group scan. Chimpanzees often gathered in distinct clusters in which individuals were in close spatial proximity but distant from other subunits, similar to their behavior and the analysis of their fission-fusion dynamics in the wild.25,34,35,156,157,158,159,160,161,162 We did not apply adjacency-based criteria or chaining rules. Subunit co-attendance simple ratio association matrices were constructed using SOCPROG 2.9163 and graphed in iGraph 1.3.099: x/(x+Ya+Yb), where x is the number of group scans where individuals a and b were observed in the same party, and y is the number of group scans where a and b were observed elsewhere. Individuals were excluded prior to analysis if they had not yet been introduced to the entire group and their tenure in the group during data collection was < 6-month. Subunits were excluded during analysis if more than one individual was unidentifiable in units of two or more (n excluded = 826, 82.6 ± 40.4 subunits per group).

Social complexity was calculated using the Shannon’s entropy (H) algorithm provided by Ramos-Fernandez et al.35 and has been employed in other analyses of chimpanzee sociality34: -∑p[a]log2p[a], where p[a] = a/A (a is the number of observed parties and A is the total number of parties for the entire community). Shannon’s entropy has been validated as a useful general metric of overall degree of social variation and complexity in the associations of any individual, thus it is a good metric for this dimension of spatiotemporal cohesion and association variability in fission-fusion dynamics. Shannon’s entropy can only be calculated with association data. Further, in validation tests the algorithm was found to be resistant to influence from differences in sample size and group size, making it useful in the social complexity of different groups. This algorithm outputs both observed (H) and bootstrapped (Hb) values of group and individual-level entropy (Hi, Hbi), where H represents a comparative measure that ranges from 0 to 1, with lower values indicating higher probabilities of uncertainty that one would have about the composition of a party chosen at random. Similarly for individual-level entropy values, Hi is calculated the same way as H but only with parties within which the selected individual (i) was observed and by considering unique compositions of parties in terms of the remaining n-1 individuals. The difference between observed and bootstrapped entropy values, JS distance, helps to infer if the observed entropy is significantly larger or smaller than the bootstrapped maximum entropy that would be expected in a dataset of the same size and subunit size distribution if all compositions were equally likely. When this algorithm was validated with two separate years of subunit co-attendance data from fission-fusion social systems, the social complexity of wild chimpanzees of the Sonso community (Budongo Forest, Uganda; N = 30 adults, 2 years of interval sampling during 15-min focal follows) was calculated as H = ∼10 with a −0.725 JS distance, and the social complexity of spider monkeys of the Otoch Ma’ax yetel Kooh protected area (Yucatan, Mexico; N = 25 adults, 4 years of 20-min group scans) was calculated as H = 8.0 to 8.5 with a −0.75 JS distance. In another application of this algorithm, the social complexity of wild chimpanzees in the Moto community (Goualougo Triangle, Republic of Congo; N = 45 adults and 22 immatures, 5 years of 20-min group scans) was calculated as H = 8.65–9.29.34 In contrast to the high degree of complexity from chimpanzees and spider monkeys, lower entropy values (∼4.0–5.0) with greater JS distances (−4.0 to −1.0) were reported for geladas (Simien Mountains National Park, Ethiopia; N = 126 adults, 2 years of 30-min group scans), a species whose social system is typically characterized as hierarchically structured or multilevel35,164 and are therefore less complex than fission-fusion social structures. We report observed entropy scores (H) and JS distances as these are likely the best available metrics for understanding variation in social complexity within fission-fusion social systems.

Historically, it has been difficult to quantify and compare the complexity of social structures. In wide comparative investigations, group size or the number of relationships between dyads have been used as general proxies of complexityS1. However, these broad measures likely underestimate complexity at intraspecific scales. Methods have also been proposed to measure the number of differentiated social relationships that individuals regularly navigate in cohesive groups (i.e., network diversityS2), either via the number of different types of behavioral contexts within which individuals interactS3 or the number of estimated types of relationships present based on distributions of association indexesS4-S6. Measuring social complexity in fission-fusion systems is uniquely challenging given the need for analyses to account for the heterogeneous distribution of opportunities for individuals to spend time together and interact. Shannon’s entropyS7 is a promising candidate algorithm for this task as it can handle data from party or subgroup co-attendance observations rather than accounts of dyads spending time within proximity of one another. With data on parties or subgroups, Shannon’s entropy calculates the overall degree of variation in subunit composition across samples. Entropy scores therefore likely reflect the amount of social variation and relationship certainty that individuals experience as a result of making social choicesS8-S11. There are two predominant algorithms for calculating Shannon’s entropy, one that calculates the amount of variation in number and strength of association scores across an estimated number of relationships typesS6 and another that calculates the extent that subunit composition is predictably sampled from a finite set of possible combinationsS11, S12. Both algorithms are advantageous in allowing for comparisons across groups while controlling for group and sample size.

Association diversity (S) was calculated using the protocol provided by Weiss et al. in SOCPROG 2.978,163: -∑q[k]·ln(q[k]), q[k]=(μk·αk)/(∑μk·αk), αk=μk−1/∑μk−1, where relationships of class k are detected by the mixture model with a frequency of at αkand an association rate of μk. This algorithm searches the distribution of observed association indices using mixture models to find the most appropriate number of relationship classes as is consistent with the idea of social complexity as defined by other authors.101,165,166 Then, the observed diversity (i.e., complexity) of associations is extracted from the frequency of associations within binned classes of relationships, with each class resembling an inferred different type or strength of relationship. This algorithm is also not influenced by differences in group size but can be skewed downward in poorly sampled networks and skewed upward in subunit co-attendance assignments during group scans. Additionally, this algorithm was designed exclusively for analyses of association data and therefore accounts for many observations within the same sampling period and can be used generally in the study of this type of social system. Therefore, it is not tailored specifically to the party or subgroup composition observations typical of fission-fusion social systems. When the algorithm was validated with 15-year of association data (observations of individuals within 10-min scans) from a fission-fusion social system, the association diversity of bottlenose whales (off Nova Scotia, Canada) was calculated as S = 0.69 with two classes of relationships. Higher levels of association diversity and more classes of relationships have been documented in multi-level and cohesive social systems; for example, female sperm whales,78,167,168 multimale-multifemale and one-male mountain gorilla groups.101,169 This trend suggests this algorithm is better matched with proximity data from systems where hierarchically clustered types of relationships contain a wide range of relationship strengths. In other words, the mixture model approach to measuring social complexity as the number of classes of different types or strengths of relationships is likely better suited to measuring features of cohesive and multilevel societies. We report association diversity scores (S) and the number of relationship classes to better understand the gradient of social complexity within the chimpanzee communities at Save the Chimps.

Coefficient of variation (CV) analyses were calculated in SOCPROG 2.979,163: cvo/sij, cvo=sd(xij)/x¯(xij), sij = σ(Xij)/μ(Xij), where Xij is the estimated association index of all dyads (i,j) and xij is the observed association index. These analyses compare the observed network against the null hypothesis that individuals are equally likely to associate (i.e., randomly) with all other individuals given their availability.170 Coefficient of variation analyses can only be conducted with association data. We permuted the datasets 1000 times to examine if the distribution of observed simple ratio association indices were significantly different from the distribution of random null association indices generated from the permuted datasets.

Generalized linear mixed model

We used a linear mixed model (LMM) with a Gaussian error distribution to examine the effects of behavioral diversity, age, and sex on individual social complexity scores (entropy Hi), applying a square transformation to the entropy scores. The model was run in R (version 4.3.3) using glmmTMB.171 As the response variable, individual entropy scores (mean = 6.01, SD = 0.76) were examined with our fixed effects predictors: behavioral diversity (mean = 0.14, SD = 0.10), year of birth (median = 1990, SD = 9 years), and sex (females = 105, males = 80). For these analyses, behavioral diversity was measured by calculating the variance of each individual’s eigenvector centrality scores in their community’s grooming, play, and tool use networks. Networks were derived from grooming and play interactions, as well as co-attendance in tool using parties. Eigenvector centrality for each chimpanzee within their community’s cultural networks was calculated using the following equation: λx,λxi=∑j=1naijxij,i=1,…,n, where x centrality of individual i is proportional to the sum of the centralities of all the relationships that are connected to it, a is the association matrix, λ is the largest eigenvalue of a, and n is the number of other j nodes.172,173 This calculation was performed separately for grooming, play, and tool use networks. Eigenvector centrality scores are an indicator of an individual’s overall influence and relative contribution to the structure of a network96,172,173,174 and is independent of entropy calculations.35 The variance of these eigenvector centrality scores serves as a proxy for the diversity within each individual’s behavioral repertoire,76 while taking into account observed community-specific patterns of cultural behaviors. The variance of each chimpanzee’s eigenvector centrality scores was calculated using the following equation: (1/(k−1))∑d(xid−x¯i)2, where xᵢd is the eigenvector centrality of individual i in domain d, x¯ᵢ is the mean eigenvector centrality of that individual across the k domains (here, k = 3). Our measure of behavioral diversity reflects how much an individual’s relative importance varies across their community’s networks of cultural behaviors: grooming, play and tool use. In interpreting this measure, low values indicate low diversity (i.e., high consistency) in an individual’s importance across their community’s networks of cultural behaviors, while high values indicate greater behavioral diversification in the individual’s relative importance across these three cultural networks. In our GLMM, we chose to use year of birth instead of age in years to account for the two-year data collection period for this investigation. We did not include previous life experience in our GLMM analyses because the majority of chimpanzees included in this study arrived to sanctuary from research laboratories; therefore this variable would not contain enough variability to be informative. We also included community identity as a random effect (Table 1) in this model. To establish the significance of the test predictors collectively, we compared our constructed model to a null model that included only random effects using a likelihood ratio test.
