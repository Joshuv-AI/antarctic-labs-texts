Oscillometry changes reflect clinically meaningful improvement after bronchial thermoplasty for uncontrolled asthma: A case report
Authors: Niranjan Setty, Alice Crawford, Brooke Kyle, Dhaval Thakkar, Rajesh Thomas, John Blakey
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAsthma is a highly prevalent but heterogenous condition. Uncontrolled disease is relatively common and may be due to ongoing inflammation and/or persisting bronchial hyper‐reactivity. Bronchial thermoplasty has been in use for many years but optimal case selection and post‐procedure assessment remain uncertain. We present a case of a gentleman in his 50s with lifelong asthma who experienced a persistent loss of control following influenza A in 2017. Despite multidisciplinary assessment and guideline‐based escalation of therapy, he had persisting symptoms. In the context of his atopy, he was trialled unsuccessfully on dupilumab. Bronchial thermoplasty (BT) was undertaken with good clinical benefit. Although his spirometry was little changed by BT, there was improvement in forced oscillometry technique (FOT) measurements. FOT may therefore be a useful addition to the assessment and monitoring of individuals considered for, or undergoing, BT.Keywords: asthma, bronchial thermoplasty, FOT, severe asthma, spirometry

INTRODUCTION

Asthma is highly prevalent worldwide, and a common cause of physical and psychological morbidity. For those with treatment resistant airway hyper‐reactivity or ongoing inflammation bronchial thermoplasty (BT) is an option. BT reduces smooth muscle mass by applying thermal energy to airways, offering modest improvement in quality of life, and reducing exacerbations in clinical trials. Despite no dramatic change in spirometry, previous case series suggest improvements in small airways measures like residual volume, and gas trapping on imaging. Therefore, there is potential to use non‐invasive, point‐of‐care forced oscillometry technique (FOT) which can better assess and monitor the effect of BT on small airways.

CASE REPORT

A 52‐year‐old man, treated for asthma since childhood, was referred to our severe asthma clinic after an exacerbation in 2017 due to Influenza A. He required hospitalization with supplemental oxygen and a short course of oral corticosteroids (Prednisolone 40 mg once daily for 5 days). His symptoms were well‐managed since childhood with inhaled corticosteroid and long‐acting beta‐agonist (ICS + LABA). He had good health otherwise, no comorbidities, never smoked, active (cycling over 100 km per week), and worked in construction. Over the subsequent 6 months he avoided hospitalization, however his symptoms remained uncontrolled. Salbutamol was required for daily activities and exertion. Asthma Control Questionnaire (ACQ 5) score was 2.4 and two courses of oral steroids were needed to manage his symptoms. His investigations are detailed in Table 1.

The patient's persistent issues prompted re‐discussion at the Asthma Multidisciplinary Team meeting, and it was decided to start on Dupilumab 400 mg subcutaneous loading dose then 200 mg every 2 weeks. After 6 months, no improvement was observed; his ACQ scores ranged from 2.4 to 3, and there was no substantial change to his lung function. Upon re‐evaluation in the MDT, a consensus was reached to explore BT and discontinue biologic treatment. At this stage a cycle ergometer cardio‐pulmonary exercise test revealed no additional diagnosis.

The patient underwent three BT sessions under general anaesthesia, spaced 1 month apart, with a total of 231 activations altogether in the right lower lobe, left lower lobe and in the bilateral upper lobes. No complications were noted. Four months later, the patient's condition improved significantly, with restored exercise capacity and reduced reliance on salbutamol (Table 2).

DISCUSSION

Bronchial thermoplasty (BT) is a potential therapeutic approach in severe asthma management, with potential benefits in reducing exacerbation frequency and improving quality of life. 3 The mechanism of BT is not well understood but it appears to be mediated through a reduction in airway smooth muscle, leading to improved ventilation homogeneity and reduced gas trapping. The FOT report from this case would be consistent with this mechanism as the improvement in reactance and frequency dependency of resistance indicate improved ventilation homogeneity. 4 , 5

However, the uncertainty around optimal patient selection presents a challenge in determining suitable candidates for BT. For example, trials have enrolled individuals with FEV1 >50% of predicted values and seen no change in spirometry, but case series show successful BT in patients with lower FEV1 levels. 6 , 7

The use of more detailed and sensitive measures may improve case identification and monitoring. FOT appears a good candidate for this: a previous case series has highlighted baseline resistance and reactance FOT measures as significantly correlated with asthma related quality of life and BT response. 7 A previous case report has described a similar improvement in oscillometry after BT 8 that predated spirometric changes, but a case series found no change in oscillometry results (albeit not reporting R5‐R20 assessed by FOT). 9 A further case series has described no change in small airways measures by oscillometry, but a difference in reactance. 10 The variation in findings may represent heterogeneity in patients, their asthma, and prior/concurrent medication.

This case highlights a key aspect of the widespread issue of translating promising treatments and clinical trial efficacy into real world effectiveness. Measurements such as spirometry that are embedded in clinical practice and enshrined in guidelines often give an incomplete description of an individual's disease. It is therefore difficult to select cases for targeted therapies and to adequately assess a patient's response. Future large‐scale research into the use of oscillometry and other complementary investigations would contribute to refining treatment protocols and establishing more robust guidelines for patient selection, ultimately improving the clinical applicability and success of add‐on therapies in asthma management.
