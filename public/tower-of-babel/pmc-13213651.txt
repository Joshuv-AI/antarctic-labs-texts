Time-sequentially released bilayer sandwich cerium oxide nanoparticles on femtosecond laser-treated zirconia implant surface for enhanced antibacterial and osseointegration capacity
Authors: Jianye Song, Weiwei Guo, Xu He, Wenhao Yang, Zuge Yang, Shiwu Dong, Yuncan Ma, Kun Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Zirconia implants offer excellent aesthetics and biocompatibility but are limited by intrinsic biocatalytic inertness, which impairs osseointegration and antimicrobial activity. Unlike existing zirconia surface modifications (e.g., passive micro/nano structuring or single-layer coatings) that lack time-controlled, multi-stage functionality. Here, we developed a bilayer “sandwich” structure (Trbs-CeO2) of cerium dioxide nanoparticles (CeO2 NPs) on the surface of zirconia by combining high-repetition-rate femtosecond laser physical modification with chemical modification using (CeO2 NPs). The outer layer functions as a rapid-release layer, delivering high CeO2 concentrations within hours to days post-implantation to emphasize anti-inflammatory and antibacterial effects; the inner layer is a slow-release layer characterized by a femtosecond laser-engineered micro-nano surface infused with CeO2, which continues to release low CeO2 concentrations for several weeks after the outer layer is exhausted, thereby synergizing with the micro-nano structure of the zirconia substrate to enhance long-term osteogenesis. The anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, and osteogenic differentiation effects of Trbs-CeO2 were evaluated in vitro, whereas its osteointegration capacity was evaluated in animal models. Compared with the control group, the Trbs-CeO2 group exhibited significantly improved surface roughness, wettability, and biocompatibility of the zirconia surface. Trbs-CeO2 exerts potent antibacterial activity by compromising bacterial cell membranes, induces M2 macrophage polarization, exhibits anti-inflammatory properties by inhibiting the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling pathway in RAW264.7 cells, and facilitates osteogenic MC3T3-E1 cell differentiation. Trbs-CeO2 significantly enhances in vivo osseointegration efficiency. Trbs-CeO2 provides a distinct, time-programmed bilayer strategy that integrates antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and osteogenesis-promoting functions, offering valuable insights for advanced zirconia-based implants.

Introduction

Titanium implants have consistently been regarded as the “gold standard” in dental and orthopedic restoration. As patients have grown more apprehensive regarding long-term functionality, aesthetics, and systemic health implications, the inherent limitations of titanium have gradually emerged. These include chronic inflammatory responses induced by the release of metal ions, bone resorption caused by stress shielding, and radiographic artifacts and aesthetic defects such as gray lines [1,2]. These challenges have prompted researchers to explore alternative materials with superior aesthetic properties and biocompatibility. Zirconia has attracted considerable attention owing to its white appearance, low tendency for plaque adhesion, and potential immunocompatibility benefits [3,4]. However, the biologically inert surface of zirconia, its limited osteointegration capacity, and the lack of an active antimicrobial mechanism hinder the attainment of rapid and stable osseointegration in complex clinical situations such as immediate implant placement [5,6].

During the early stages of implant placement, the implant encounters the interaction of three pathological pressures—an influx of inflammatory factors, bacterial colonization, and osteogenesis inhibition—which considerably impede the osseointegration process [7,8]. During the acute phase, high concentrations of pro-inflammatory factors, including tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α and interleukin (IL)-1β, can directly inhibit osteogenic signaling pathways, whereas lipopolysaccharides (LPS) from Gram-negative bacteria further exacerbate the inflammatory response by activating the TLR4/NF-κB axis, establishing a positive feedback loop between infection and inflammation [9]. Excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) in the wound site can impair proteins and nucleic acids, trigger inflammation, and suppress immunity, thus hindering repair [[10], [11], [12]]. Concurrently, local hypoxia and compromised osteoblast progenitor cell migration collectively hinder the onset of mineralization, rendering conventional monofunctional surface strategies inadequate in addressing this complex pathological network [13].

Multiple surface modifications of dental implants can dynamically and synergistically enhance their anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, and osteogenic characteristics. Cerium oxide nanoparticles (CeO2 NPs) exhibit various enzymatic activities and can catalyze intracellular redox reactions through the reversible valence transition between Ce3+ and Ce4+, effectively scavenging ROS in inflammatory conditions. They exhibit remarkable potential in facilitating osteoblast differentiation, inhibiting bacterial proliferation, and regulating inflammation [14,15]. However, single-layer coating strategies generally utilize a constant release profile, which fails to meet the complex and dynamically changing biological demands of procedures, including immediate implant placement. During the early post-implantation phase, there is a risk of local acute inflammation and bacterial contamination [2,16], necessitating rapid release of high doses of CeO2 to achieve anti-inflammatory and antibacterial effects; conversely, during the middle and late stages of bone healing, the inflammatory response tends to subside, necessitating a sustained, slow release of CeO2 to facilitate osteoblast adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation [17,18].

Micro- and nanostructures of specific dimensions can inherently influence the immune microenvironment and facilitate bone regeneration. Arrays of nanocolumns with a diameter of approximately 300 nm can inhibit the TLR4/NF-κB pathway, inducing macrophages to secrete TGF-β instead of IL-6, thereby fostering an immune homeostasis conducive to bone regeneration [7,19]. Optimizing the arrangement of lamellar nanostructures: attaining the dual objective of effectively eradicating bacteria and promoting osteogenesis [20]. By modifying processing parameters, femtosecond lasers can accurately and consistently produce highly reproducible and uniform periodic micro- and nanostructures on zirconia surfaces [21], without inducing phase transitions in the zirconia or introducing other impurity elements [22,23]. In previous studies, we employed a self-engineered femtosecond laser microfabrication platform to achieve micrometer-level precise ablation of human tissue through optimized equipment and parameters and uniformly fabricated multi-scale micro-nano structures on the non-smooth threaded surface of titanium implants, thereby promoting osseointegration efficiency [24,25].

The mismatch between the spatial arrangement of single-function components and their release kinetics complicates the attainment of synergistic anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, and osteogenic effects [26]. This study developed a bilayer sandwich structure inspired by the concept of “time-controlled release.” The outer layer functions as a rapid-release layer, delivering high CeO2 concentrations within hours to days post-implantation to prioritize anti-inflammatory and antibacterial effects. The inner layer, prepared using high-repetition-rate femtosecond laser-fabricated micro-nano structures and loaded with CeO2, functions as a slow-release layer, continuously releasing low CeO2 concentrations for several weeks after the outer layer has been depleted, thereby synergistically enhancing long-term osteogenesis alongside the zirconium substrate’s micro-nano structures. Materials and methods

Synthesis of CeO2 NPs and their polydopamine (PDA) modification

CeO2 NPs were synthesized using the hydrothermal method through the following steps: First, CeO2 NPs were prepared by dissolving cetyltrimethylammonium bromide in ultrapure water and stirring the dissolution using a magnetic stirrer (MS-2A, Amphenol, China). The mixture was incubated in a 90°C water bath for 3 h. Subsequently, the mixture was centrifuged to isolate the product, which was subsequently transferred to a hydrothermal reaction system and reacted at 110 °C for 24 h. After the hydrothermal reaction, the product was dehydrated at 70 °C. The dried sample was subsequently placed into a KSL-1200X muffle furnace (Hefei Kejing, China) and calcined at 400 °C for 4 h. To prepare CeO2 NPs with PDA modification, solid CeO2 NPs were dispersed with dopamine hydrochloride in Tris-HCl buffer at pH 8.5. The reaction was stirred at room temperature and shielded from light for 12 h. Subsequently, the product was collected using centrifugal washing and vacuum-dried to obtain CeO2 NPs with PDA modification, which were stored in a light-protected environment.

Loading of Trbs-CeO2

Zirconia sheets (∅10 × 2 mm) and implants (M 4.0 × 6 mm) were fabricated from 3 mol% Yttria-Stabilized Zirconia Polycrystals (3Y-TZP, Wieland, Germany). Zirconia was milled via CAD/CAM, sintered at 1560 °C for 16 h, and subsequently polished. These samples were divided into four groups and processed as follows:

P group: Polished surface group.

La group: A fiber femtosecond laser system with a central wavelength of 1030 nm, pulse duration of 300 fs, repetition rate of 200 kHz, and average power of 27 W was utilized. The scanning system was configured with a speed of 1000 mm/s and an interval of 30 μm; the scanning length was set to 2000 μm along the X-axis and 3000 μm along the Y-axis, with the complete scanning process repeated twice. Upon completion of the scanning, the implant was rotated 60°, and the above operation was repeated. After the implant surface was completely processed, the samples were ultrasonically cleaned with acetone, anhydrous alcohol, and ultrapure water for 10 min.

CeO2 group: The initial preparation procedure was similar to that of the La group. Subsequently, 0.1 mL of a cerium oxide ethanol suspension was applied to the surface. After evaporation of the ethanol, the laser power was adjusted to 3.3 W, and one additional scan was performed, simultaneously keeping the scanning speed and spacing unchanged (Supplementary Table S1).

Trbs-CeO2 group: The initial preparation procedure was identical to that of the cerium oxide group. Subsequently, 0.1 mL of PDA-modified CeO2 NP solution was applied to the zirconia surface of the Trbs-CeO2 group and frozen at −20 °C for at least 12 h. Subsequently, the sample was dried at −80 °C for 12 h utilizing a vacuum freeze dryer (Bilang, China).

Characterization of Trbs-CeO2

The micro/nanomorphologies on the zirconia surface induced by femtosecond laser were examined using an optical microscope (Leica, Germany) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Hitachi, Japan). The CeO2 NPs microstructure was examined using transmission electron microscopy (TEM, FEI, America). The surface roughness of each group was analyzed using atomic force microscopy (AFM, Shimadzu, Japan) for nanoscale assessment. The elemental composition and chemical bonding structure were analyzed using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Fisher, USA) characterization, and the surface phase composition of the materials was characterized using X-ray diffraction (XRD, Shimadzu, Japan). Hydrophilicity was evaluated using the stopped-drop method with a contact angle meter (Dataphysics, Germany). The compressive stress of zirconia implants was assessed pre- and post-femtosecond laser treatment using a testing machine (WANCE, China) at a crosshead speed of 1 mm/min.

Zirconia sheets were immersed in 5 mL of ultrapure water for 1, 3, 5, 7, 10, 14, 21, and 28 days, with three replicates at each time interval. The solution was collected at the specified time points and digested with nitric acid and hydrogen peroxide at 180 °C for 30 min. Inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES, ThermoFisher, USA) was utilized to analyze the collected samples.

Cell and bacterial cultures

Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus strain 252 (MRSA 252) was cultured in Mueller-Hinton agar (MHA, AOBOX, 02-051) and Mueller-Hinton broth (MHB, AOBOX, 02-052), and Escherichia coli (E. coli, ATCC 25922) was cultured in Luria-Bertani broth (LB, AOBOX, 02-137) and its liquid medium (LB, AOBOX, 02-136). Cultivation was performed in a constant-temperature incubator maintained at 37 °C. This study utilized a mouse-derived macrophage cell line (RAW264.7) and a mouse-derived osteogenic precursor cell line (MC3T3-E1) supplied by the Chinese Academy of Sciences. RAW 264.7 was cultured using Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM, Gibco, C11995500BT), and MC3T3-E1 cells were cultured using α-Minimum Essential Medium complete medium (α-MEM, Gibco, C12571500BT). Furthermore, 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, 11011-8611) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Cytiva, SH40003.01) were incorporated to supplement the medium. Cell culture at 37 °C, 5% CO2, and 95% humidity.

Antibacterial properties of Trbs-CeO2

After the bacteria matured, the optical density (OD) value at 595 nm was calibrated to 1.0. The solution was subsequently diluted to 1 × 106 CFU/mL density and inoculated into 48-well plates containing zirconia sheets and incubated at 37 °C for 16 h to facilitate subsequent experiments. To detect bacterial adhesion, phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was washed twice to remove non-adherent bacteria. Subsequently, each sample was placed in a clean tube, 5 mL of PBS was introduced, and sonicated to detach bacteria adhering to the surface. A 1000-fold diluted solution of the collected bacteria was prepared, and 100 μL of the dilution was inoculated onto solid medium. The colonies were photographed following a 12 h incubation at 37 °C. To detect bacterial survival, the supernatant was collected after 16 h of incubation at 37 °C, and the OD value was measured at 595 nm. To ascertain the ratio of dead to live bacteria, the bacteria were stained with SYTO9/PI live and dead bacteria stain kit (MKbio, MX4234) and incubated in the absence of light for 15 min.

To characterize the microscopic morphology of bacteria, the bacteria were fixed on the surface of zirconia sheets using 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4 °C overnight. Subsequently, they underwent a gradient dehydration treatment utilizing alcohol solution (50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, and 100%), with each immersion lasting 5 min. Hexamethyldisilane was subsequently added, and the reaction was allowed to proceed for 40 min before the sample was completely dried. The platinum layer was uniformly sputtered on the sample surface using the vacuum sputtering method. The sample was fixed on the sample tray with conductive silver adhesive and examined using SEM.

Bacteria from zirconia sheets were washed twice with PBS, sonicated to dislodge the supernatant, and subsequently, the supernatant was collected by centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant was removed, and the cell precipitate was immersed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde for at least 2 days. The samples were subsequently sent to a central laboratory for dehydration, embedding, sectioning, and staining, followed by TEM observation.

Macrophage polarization

RAW264.7 cells were cultured for 7 days, and cell morphology was observed using SEM. The SEM method was similar to that of a bacterium. Cytoskeleton staining was performed using 4% paraformaldehyde (Biosharp, BL539A) fixed for 30 min, washed twice with PBS, and stained with TRITC-Phalloidin (Yeasen, 40734ES75) and 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, Biosharp, BL105B) as specified. The final observation was performed using fluorescence microscope (Bio-Rad, USA).

Immunofluorescence (IF): Samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde before being permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100, and subsequently treated with 5% BSA for blocking. Subsequently, the cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with arginase-1 (Arg1, Proteintech Group, 66129-1-Ig) and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS, Proteintech Group, 18985-1-AP). After the primary antibody incubation, cells were co-incubated with Alexa Fluor 594 (Beyotime, A1185) and Alexa Fluor 488 (Beyotime, A0423) for 1 h. DAPI staining was applied for 5 min to label the nuclei, and the results were subsequently examined using fluorescence microscope.

ROS scavenging capacity: RAW264.7 cells were cultured for 24 h and stimulated by LPS (1 μg/mL, ACMEC, AC12037). The experiment was conducted precisely following the instructions of the Reactive Oxygen Species Detection Kit (Beyotime, S0033S). Fluorescence microscope was utilized to observe the fluorescence intensity of ROS in RAW264.7 cells, and flow cytometry was employed to detect the ROS production level from different groups. The entire experiment was conducted under strict light-proof conditions.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA): Inflammatory factor levels in cell supernatants were evaluated using ELISA kits following the manufacturer’s instructions. ELISA kits used included IL-1β (BOSTER, EK0394), IL-6 (BOSTER, EK0411), and IL-10 (BOSTER, EK0417).

Molecular mechanisms research

RAW264.7 were incubated in 12-well plates containing zirconia sheets for 7 days, subsequently washed twice with RNase-free saline (Biosharp, BL510B) at 4 °C, and lysed using Trizol for 5 min. The samples were subsequently transferred to a −80 °C refrigerator for long-term storage. RNA sequencing services were rendered by Wayen Biotechnologies (Shanghai), Inc.

Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR): The expression levels of Arg1, iNOS, TLR4, MyD88, and NF-κB in RAW264.7 cells were evaluated on day 7 of culture. GAPDH was selected as an internal reference gene. Total RNA was extracted from the samples using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) and subsequently converted to cDNA using a reverse transcription kit (Bio-Rad, RR047A). The cDNA was mixed with SYBR Green premix (Bio-Rad, RR820A) and primers, followed by quantitative analysis of gene expression using a CFX96 real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR instrument (Bio-Rad, USA). Supplementary Table S2 presents the primer sequences for the genes involved in this study.

Western Blot: RAW264.7 cells were inoculated in 6-well plates containing zirconia sheets for 7 days and subsequently lysed by radioimmunoprecipitation lysates (RARI, Beyotime, P0013B) containing 1% protease inhibitor (Beyotime, P1045-1), 1% EDTA (Beyotime, P1045-3), and 1% phosphatase inhibitor (Beyotime, P1045-2). The supernatant was obtained after centrifugation and subsequently stained with Caumas Brilliant Blue (Beyotime, BL502A). Following sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and electrotransfer to a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane (PVDF, Merck, PR05507), the membranes were treated with BSV (Biosharp, BL535A) closure. Primary antibodies were incubated with the membrane overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-coupled secondary antibodies (Beyotime, A0208) for 1 h at room temperature. Furthermore, SDS-PAGE and Western Blot images were acquired using an ECL chemiluminescent substrate kit (Biosharp, BL520A) and a ChemiDoc XRS instrument (Bio-Rad, USA). Primary antibodies utilized included Arg1 (Proteintech Group, 16001-1-AP), iNOS (Proteintech Group, 18985-1-AP), TLR4 (Proteintech Group, 19811-1-AP), MyD88 (HUABIO, HA723139), NF-κB p65 (HUABIO, HA721307), phospho-NF-κB p65 (p-NF-κB p65, HUABIO, HA723223), and GAPDH (Proteintech Group, 81640-5-RR).

Flow cytometry: RAW264.7 cells were seeded in 6-well plates containing zirconia sheets for 24 h. The P + LPS group and Trbs-CeO2+ LPS group were treated with LPS (1 μg/mL) for 2 h. Cells were harvested and centrifuged. According to the manufacturer’s instructions, the cells were sequentially incubated with CD16/CD32 monoclonal antibody (BD Pharmingen, 567021) for 10 min and APC-labeled anti-TLR4 antibody (BioLegend, SA15-21) for 30 min. Following two washes with PBS, the fluorescence intensity was measured using flow cytometer analyzer (Beckman Coulter Gallios, USA) in the FL6-A channel.

Biocompatibility of Trbs-CeO2

Preliminary cell adhesion experiment. MC3T3-E1 were cultured at 37 °C for 0.5, 1, and 2 h, subsequently fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 15 min, and stained using DAPI for 30 s. Five images per group were captured using a fluorescence microscope and analyzed using ImageJ software. MC3T3-E1 cells were cultured separately for 1 day for SEM observation and for 24 and 48 h for cytoskeleton staining, respectively, to evaluate cellular morphology. Cell Counting Kit 8 (Beyotime, C0038) was utilized to assess cytotoxicity. 5000 MC3T3-E1 cells were cultured on zirconia sheets in 48-well plates. After 12, 24, 48, and 72 h, the samples were washed twice with PBS. Subsequently, 20 μL of CCK8 with 180 μL of culture medium was added to each well and placed at 37 °C for 1 h of incubation, protected from light. Additionally, 100 μL of sample per well was transferred to a 96-well plate, and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm.

In vitro osteogenic differentiation of MC3T3-E1

MC3T3-E1 cells were seeded in 48-well plates with zirconia sheets and subsequently replaced with osteogenic induction medium (OriCell, MUXMX-90021) on the following day, with the medium refreshed every 3 days. Enzyme activity was measured on days 7 and 14 utilizing an Alkaline Phosphatase (ALP) Test Kit (Beyotime, P0321S), and ALP staining was performed according to the instructions provided with the ALP chromogenic kit (Beyotime, C3206). Alizarin red staining was performed on day 21. First, 4% paraformaldehyde was employed to fix cells for 15 min, and the cells were stained with 1% alizarin red staining solution (OriCell, MUXMX-90021) for 30 min. For quantitative analysis, the samples were eluted with PBS containing 10% 16-alkylpyridinium chloride (Macklin, H811089), and the absorbance was measured at 540 nm.

MC3T3-E1 cells were seeded into 12-well plates containing zirconia sheets. After 7 and 14 days of culture, the mRNA expression levels of ALP, Runx2, Osterix, and COL1A1 in MC3T3-E1 cells were detected using RT-qPCR (SupplementaryTable S2).

Co-culture of MC3T3-E1 and RAW264.7 cells

Indirect co-culture model: RAW264.7 cells were stimulated with LPS (1 μg/mL) for 24 h, and the supernatant was obtained by centrifugation. The resultant supernatant was subsequently mixed with osteogenic induction medium in equal proportions to formulate LPS-conditioned medium. MC3T3-E1 cells in each group were cultured using LPS-conditioned medium, with the medium changed every two days. The control group was cultured in osteogenic induction medium on polished sheets. After 7 days, the ALP activity was quantitatively measured using an ALP assay kit.

Direct co-culture model: MC3T3-E1 cells and RAW264.7 cells were cultured on zirconia sheets in 12-well plates and cultured using LPS-conditioned medium, with the medium changed every other day. The ALP activity was detected after 7 days, and the expression levels of IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-10 in the supernatant were evaluated using ELISA.

Animal in vivo studies

The Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Army Medical University reviewed and approved this study (Approval No. AMUWEC20255399). Six-month-old New Zealand-type large white rabbits weighing 2.5–3 kg were utilized for the experiment. Each rabbit was individually housed and randomly assigned to one of four groups (n = 8 per group). The experimental animals received sterile drinking water and sterilized feed in a constant temperature environment (50% humidity, 23 °C, and 12 h of light daily). 3% sodium pentobarbital (Pentobarbital sodium, China) was utilized for anesthesia to alleviate pain before surgery. All experiments employed the 3R principle (Reduce, Replace, and Optimize) to prevent or alleviate nonessential pain.

The skin was prepared and sterilized in the operative area following anesthesia, after which the skin was incised layer by layer, and the muscles were separated to expose the distal femur. Afterward, holes were drilled perpendicular to the lateral wall of the epiphysis of the rabbit distal femur utilizing a drill (D = 4 mm, H = 6 mm) and cooled with saline. Four groups (n = 8 per group) of zirconia implants were inserted into the bone cavities. The joint cavity and skin were carefully sutured.

Rabbit femurs were excised at 4 and 12 weeks and preserved using 4% paraformaldehyde fixation. A high-resolution micro-CT system (Always, China) was set with a resolution of 8.6 μm and an exposure duration of 500 ms. The rabbit femurs were scanned, followed by a 3D reconstruction technique of a 300 μm circumferential region surrounding the femoral implant using the VG Studio Max software. The bone volume fraction/bone volume ratio (BV/TV), mean number of trabeculae (Tb.N), and mean trabecular spacing (Tb.Sp) were quantitatively analyzed. The rabbit femurs were subsequently ground to a thickness of 30-50 μm using a grinder and stained with Vancouver Gleason (VG) to evaluate the bone-to-implant contact (BIC). Fresh femurs were dehydrated and embedded, sectioned into 3 μm thick paraffin sections, stained with hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining and Masson’s trichrome stain, and sealed with neutral gum for microscopic examination.

Statistical analysis

The Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (version 20.0; IBM Corp., USA) and GraphPad Prism (version 8.0; GraphPad Software, USA) software were utilized for data analyses. The Shapiro–Wilk test was employed to assess the normality of data distribution. Data with normal distribution were analyzed using an independent-sample t-test or one-way analysis of variance, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons, and expressed as mean ± standard deviation. A p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Preparation and characterization of Trbs-CeO2

Optical microscopic examination revealed that uniform, periodic, and parallelly arranged groove structures covered the entire threaded surface of the zirconia implant (Fig. 2A). SEM revealed that these nanopores were consistently dispersed within the submicron corrugated laser-induced periodic surface structures (LIPSS) (Fig. 2B). The compressive strength of the P group was 1.44 ± 0.37 GPa, whereas that of the La group was 1.28 ± 0.24 GPa. No statistically significant difference was observed between them (P = 0.561, p> 0.05). CeO2 NPs were synthesized via the hydrothermal method and wrapped with PDA. TEM analysis of the nanocrystal structure of CeO2 NPs revealed that they were uniformly sized spherical nanoparticles measuring 11.55 ± 1.79 nm and peripherally modified with a PDA layer (Fig. 2C). XPS analysis of CeO2 and Trbs-CeO2 groups confirmed the presence of Ce elements on the zirconia surface, with the relative ratios of Ce4+ to Ce3+ determined to be 72.14% and 27.86% for the CeO2 group and 67.35% and 32.65% for the Trbs-CeO2 group, respectively (Fig. 2D and E). XRD results in CeO2 and Trbs-CeO2 groups revealed that the strongest characteristic peak of CeO2 NPs, along with the distinctive diffraction peaks of zirconia, was observed (Fig. 2F). The remaining peaks are overwhelmed by the strong signals of the main phases of zirconia. XPS and XRD results confirmed the successful loading of CeO2 NPs on the zirconia surface. The CeO2 NPs time-release profile indicates that the CeO2 group demonstrated a rapid release of CeO2 NPs during the first 7 days, subsequently reaching a plateau on day 7. However, the Trbs-CeO2 group exhibited rapid release within the first 5 days, followed by a slower release rate from days 5 to 21, and stabilized on day 21 (Fig. 2G). The water contact angle of each group was 59.5 ± 2.98°, 51.9 ± 4.57°, 24.83 ± 5.45°and 12.4 ± 1.11°, revealing that the Trbs-CeO2 group exhibited the lowest water contact angle, signifying a substantial enhancement in hydrophilicity (Fig. 2H). AFM images revealed micrometer grooves approximately ∼3 μm deep (Fig. 2I). For each group, the surface arithmetic mean height (Sa) values were 79.7 ± 18.2 nm, 710.3 ± 29.4 nm, 845.3 ± 108.6 nm, and 912.3 ± 65.5 nm, respectively; the surface quadratic mean height (Sq) values were 117.23 ± 36.2 nm, 810.3 ± 41.3 nm, 974.7 ± 75.8 nm, and 1065.3 ± 70.8 nm, respectively. The results revealed a gradual increase in Sa and Sq from P, La, CeO2, to Trbs-CeO2, proving that the successful loading of Trbs-CeO2 increased zirconia roughness (Fig. 2J).

Antibacterial effects of Trbs-CeO2

We utilized MRSA as a representative of Gram-positive bacteria and E. coli as a representative of Gram-negative bacteria to investigate the antibacterial properties of Trbs-CeO2 on the surface. The bacterial adhesion experiment revealed a significant decrease in the number of bacteria on zirconia sheets in CeO2 and Trbs-CeO2 groups (Fig. 3A). Decreased survival rates of MRSA and E. coli were identified when bacteria were cultured in 48-well plates containing sheets (Fig. 3B). Notably, the La group surface markedly decreased the E. coli survival rate but not MRSA, which may be attributable to the unfavorable nature of this laser-induced porous morphology for E. coli. SEM revealed that MRSA cultured in CeO2 and Trbs-CeO2 groups exhibited numerous ruptured bacteria fragments, and the morphology of the surviving bacteria was significantly altered, adopting a non-spherical shape. Except for group P, the morphology of E. coli in the other three groups exhibited significant elongation and even cell membrane rupture. Bacteria were obtained from each group of sheets, and TEM revealed that the bacteria in the CeO2 and Trbs-CeO2 group exhibited cell membrane rupture and content exudation (Fig. 3C). SYTO.9 and PI staining revealed a significant number of dead bacteria stained red in Trbs-CeO2 groups (Fig. 3D). Micro-CT revealed that the quantity of bone tissue within 300 μm of the implant was the highest in the Trbs-CeO2 group (Fig. 8B and E). This demonstrated that the Trbs-CeO2 group exhibited greater and denser bone trabeculae, potentially enhancing implant osseointegration. Additionally, hard tissue and paraffin sections of the rabbit femur were prepared to investigate the osseointegration effect on the longitudinal section. VG, HE, and Masson staining revealed that at the Trbs-CeO2 group demonstrated richer bone tissue, characterized by denser new bone trabeculae, a tighter interface between the implant and bone tissue, a larger area of new bone coverage, and higher bone density (Fig. 8C and F). Discussion

This is the first study to use a combination of physical modification through a high-repetition-rate femtosecond laser and chemical modification with CeO2 NPs to fabricate a Trbs-CeO2 on irregular, threaded zirconia implants. Such multiple surface modifications can synergistically enhance their anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, and osteogenic properties. This synergistic effect is mainly manifested in the following two aspects. First, the structural synergy, micro/nano-topography provides an enlarged contact area and mechanical interlocking sites for nanoparticles and PDA, thereby facilitating coating adhesion and enabling time-sequential release of CeO2 NPs. Second, functional synergy The “bilayer sandwich” design of Trbs-CeO2 facilitates the time-sequential release of CeO2 NPs: in the early stage, CeO2 NPs are rapidly released into the peri-implant microenvironment via PDA degradation to exert their anti-inflammatory and antibacterial effects; in the middle and late stages, CeO2 NPs embedded in the implant surface and femtosecond laser-prepared micro-nano structure contribute to osteogenesis.

Compared to a nanosecond laser, a femtosecond laser achieves superior machining precision and reduced thermal damage through its vaporization-ablation mechanism [28,29]. This study utilized this technique to construct rounded groove structures with a periodicity of ∼30 μm and a depth of ∼3 μm, along with nanopores, on the zirconia surface. This topography did not significantly impair the compressive strength of the material, which is consistent with findings from most studies on femtosecond laser surface treatment of zirconia and represents one of the significant advantages of this technique [[30], [31], [32]]. Additionally, the introduction of micro-nano topography, PDA, and CeO2 NPs significantly enhanced the surface roughness and hydrophilicity of zirconia. Wettability exhibits a significant correlation with surface morphology type and roughness scale [[33], [34], [35]]. Previous studies have demonstrated that 3 μm-wide grooves impede water molecule movement, thereby increasing the hydrophobicity of zirconia surfaces [35]. This study specifically designed ∼30 μm-wide grooves to facilitate water diffusion and penetration. Combined with the intrinsic hydrophilicity of CeO2 NPs and PDA, these factors collectively enhanced the hydrophilicity of Trbs-CeO2.

High surface roughness and hydrophilicity have been demonstrated to promote early cell attachment and protein adsorption [[36], [37], [38]]. Meanwhile, micron-scale grooves and nanomorphology has been demonstrated to enhance extension of cellular filamentous pseudopods and osteoblast differentiation with elongated cytoskeletons [25,[39], [40], [41], [42]]. Consistent with previous studies, this study observed that MC3T3-E1 cells in La, CeO2, and Trbs-CeO2 groups increased filamentous pseudopod numbers and enlarged cytoskeletons. thereby facilitating robust mechanical anchoring to the substrate. Furthermore, the partially demonstrated osteogenic potential of CeO2 in previous studies, which significantly enhanced extracellular matrix mineralization in MC3T3-E1 cells with concurrent Runx2 and CoL1A1 upregulation [43]. This study observed elevated mRNA expression levels of ALP, Osterix, Runx2, and COL1A1, as well as significantly enhanced in vivo osseointegration, further elucidating the pro-osteogenic mechanism of CeO2 NPs.

Typically, high-roughness surfaces promote bacterial adhesion [44,45]. This study, however, revealed diminished adhesion of E. coli on such surfaces, attributable to the synergistic effects of bacterial type and topographical scale. In contrast to spherical MRSA, rod-shaped E. coli is spatially constrained by curved grooves, with impeded growth orientation consequently hindering surface colonization [34,46]. Previous studies have demonstrated that CeO2 NPs can adhere to bacterial cell membranes, interfering with material exchange between bacteria and the external environment, exerting antibacterial effects [47]. We examined damage to MRSA and E. coli cell membranes, and decreased bacterial survival rates and increased dead-to-live ratios both indicated the antibacterial efficacy of Trbs-CeO2.

A prolonged inflammatory microenvironment is detrimental to osseointegration, thereby impeding the regeneration and repair of bone tissue at the implant surface [48]. Macrophages function as essential regulators in bone immunomodulation and provide critical support for successful osseointegration by regulating the inflammatory microenvironment [13,49]. Studies have demonstrated that M1 macrophages markedly accumulate in inflammatory tissues, promoting inflammatory responses through pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion and consequent tissue injury. Conversely, a higher M2/M1 ratio favors tissue repair, thereby facilitating successful osseointegration [50,51]. Therefore, the essential strategy for treating inflammation is to decrease macrophage differentiation to the M1 phenotype and increase the M2 phenotype. Recent studies have demonstrated that the physicochemical properties of biomaterial surfaces can significantly regulate the polarization phenotypes of macrophages [17,52,53]. This study found that Trbs-CeO2 inhibited the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling pathway, which constitutes the core upstream pro-inflammatory signaling axis that drives macrophage M1 polarization. Downregulation of this pathway inhibits the M1 transcriptional program and related inflammatory cascades, thereby ameliorating the inflammatory microenvironment and polarizing macrophages more easily toward an M2 phenotype and exerting anti-inflammatory effects. These effects were manifested in three aspects in this study: First, it downregulated pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6 and IL-1β) and upregulated anti-inflammatory IL-10. Second, it reduced intracellular ROS levels. Third, it decreased TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB expression at mRNA and protein levels. Furthermore, under LPS stimulation, Trbs-CeO2 accelerated TLR4 endocytosis and significantly attenuated NF-κB pathway activation. These findings are consistent with those of a previous study that reported that CeO2 NPs exerted an anti-inflammatory activity by modulating the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling pathway to alleviate inflammatory bowel disease in mice [54]. Moreover, indirect and direct co-culture models revealed that the inflammatory microenvironment significantly suppressed ALP enzymatic activity, whereas the Trbs-CeO2 coating reversed this effect. This reversal is primarily attributed to the dual functionality of Trbs-CeO2 coating in simultaneously exerting anti-inflammatory and pro-osteogenic effects.

This study has several limitations. First, unlike the oral environment, which is inherently exposed to bacterial challenges, a rabbit femoral implant model cannot adequately simulate pathological conditions associated with infection or inflammation. Future research should focus on developing animal models with clinical relevance infection or inflammation models. Second, to thoroughly validate the functional regulatory effects of Trbs-CeO2 on the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB pathway, subsequent studies will employ Co-Immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) and NF-κB reporter cell line. Moreover, pathway-specific activation studies, along with loss- and gain-of-function assays targeting key molecules, should be conducted to comprehensively clarify the underlying molecular mechanisms in the future.

Conclusion

In this study, a novel zirconia implant surface (Trbs-CeO2) with the properties of promoting osseointegration, anti-inflammation, and antibacterial activity was developed by synergistically combining high-repetition-rate femtosecond laser physical modification with CeO2 NPs chemical modification. The micro-grooves and nanoporous structures formed on the zirconia surface by the femtosecond laser could not only promote the early adhesion and osteogenic differentiation of osteoblasts but also form a mechanical interlocking structure with CeO2 NPs, thereby improving the stability of the coating. The “bilayer sandwich” design of CeO2 NPs/PDA/CeO2 NPs enabled the time-sequential release of CeO2 NPs, which could exert their anti-inflammatory effects via suppression of the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling pathway and antibacterial effects by disrupting the cell membranes. Typically, “physics + chemistry” treatments produced a double synergistic effect to promote osteogenesis multidimensionally. Trbs-CeO2 provided a novel and effective strategy for the surface modification of zirconia implants and exhibited potential application in poor osteogenesis caused by inflammation and infection.
