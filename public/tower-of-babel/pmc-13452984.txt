Radiological follow-up after surgery for a giant pulmonary artery aneurysm
Authors: Santiago A. Endara, Gerardo A. Davalos, Jaime R. Pinto, Vladimir E. Ullauri, Veronica M. Tapia, Juan A. Benitez, Daniel A. Freire
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pulmonary artery aneurysm (PAA) is a rare cardiovascular condition, with an incidence of 1 per 14,000 autopsies. It is defined as a dilation of the pulmonary artery trunk exceeding 4 cm. Due to the risk of lethal complications such as dissection thrombosis or spontaneous rupture, surgical management is imperative for “giant” or symptomatic aneurysms, although postoperative follow-up protocols are not standardized. A 68-year-old Asian female presented with a 6-month history of progressive chest pain and dyspnea. Computed tomography angiography (CTA) revealed a giant fusiform aneurysm of the pulmonary trunk (PT), measuring 5.17 cm, and the right pulmonary artery branch (RPA), measuring 3.26 cm. Resection and repair of the PT and the RPA were performed via median sternotomy and cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), using 28 mm and 20 mm Dacron grafts, respectively. Following a favorable postoperative course, the patient was discharged in stable condition, at follow-up, she remains in good health. Surveillance CTA performed 6 weeks postoperatively demonstrated excellent graft patency, restored pulmonary hemodynamics, and the absence of peri-graft collections or anastomotic complications. Surgical resolution of giant fusiform aneurysm of the PT and the RPA is an effective intervention to prevent catastrophic outcomes. Close imaging surveillance is essential during the postoperative period to monitor dynamic changes not only in the reconstructed pulmonary bed but also in other arterial segments, which is decisive for long-term therapeutic success.

Introduction

PAA is an infrequent clinical entity, with a reported incidence in autopsy series of approximately 1 case per 14,000 individuals [1,2]. Histopathologically, it is defined as a focal dilation involving all 3 layers of the vascular wall. Radiologically, it is diagnosed when the diameter of the pulmonary artery trunk exceeds 4 cm or is 1.5 times greater than the patient's expected baseline diameter [1]. When these dimensions reach critical thresholds, generally above 5-6 cm, it is classified as a giant aneurysm [3,4], a condition that exponentially increases the risk of life-threatening complications.

The pathogenesis of PAAs is diverse, ranging from congenital causes such as left to right shunts and valvular heart disease, to acquired causes, including pulmonary arterial hypertension, infectious processes, systemic vasculitis, and connective tissue diseases [2,5]. Although many patients remain asymptomatic for long periods, the natural history of giant aneurysms is marked by the risk of dissection, in situ thrombosis, or spontaneous rupture, the latter carrying a reported mortality rate near 100% [6,7].

Due to its low prevalence, standardized clinical practice guidelines defining optimal management are lacking. However, there is growing consensus on the necessity of surgical intervention in cases of giant or symptomatic aneurysms to prevent catastrophic outcomes [8]. In this context, advanced imaging techniques, such as CTA and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), have become the gold standard for both initial anatomical characterization and surgical planning [5].

Following surgical resolution, radiological follow-up becomes a fundamental pillar in evaluating the integrity of the repair, vascular patency, and early detection of long-term postoperative complications. Nonetheless, evidence regarding postoperative surveillance protocols in giant aneurysms remains limited. This case report aims to describe the management of a patient with a symptomatic giant pulmonary artery aneurysm, with a specific emphasis on the importance of longitudinal radiological follow up after surgical repair to ensure hemodynamic stability and long-term therapeutic success.

Case presentation

A 68-year-old Asian female presented history of hypertension and dyslipidemia, both under pharmacological treatment. Additionally, she had a surgical history of lumbar spine instrumentation and right radius osteosynthesis in 2017. The patient reported a clinical course of approximately 6 months characterized by sporadic progressive chest pain and dyspnea. Upon physical examination, she was hemodynamically stable with adequate oxygen saturation on room air. Cardiovascular examination revealed no audible murmurs. During the diagnostic protocol, chest radiography showed enlargement of the pulmonary artery segment, with an increased cardiothoracic silhouette. The right hilum was prominent, and increased pulmonary vascularity was observed. CTA revealed a giant fusiform aneurysm of the PT, measuring 5.17 cm in transverse diameter. The RPA measured 3.26 cm, and the left pulmonary artery (LPA) measured 2.0 cm (Fig. 1). The ascending aorta was recorded at 3.7 cm with parietal atherosclerotic calcifications. Transthoracic echocardiography showed preserved left ventricular systolic function, with an LVEF of 69%. The right ventricle was not dilated and showed preserved systolic function, with a TAPSE of 24 mm. Estimated right ventricular systolic pressure/pulmonary artery systolic pressure was within normal limits at 35 mmHg. Mild tricuspid regurgitation and mild central aortic regurgitation were present. The main pulmonary artery was markedly dilated, measuring 4.9 cm (Fig. 4 and Table 1). Coronary angiography showed normal coronary arteries with left dominance. Extensive laboratory evaluation and systemic screening for connective tissue disorders, autoimmune vasculitis, chronic infectious etiologies, history of chest trauma and valvular or congenital cardiac anomalies were entirely negative, leading to a diagnosis of an idiopathic giant pulmonary artery aneurysm. Given the patient’s clinical presentation with pain and the previously described findings, elective surgical resolution was planned. The patient underwent resection and repair of the PT aneurysm approximately 5 cm, and resection and repair of the RPA aneurysm approximately 3.0 cm, via a median sternotomy approach. The procedure was performed under CPB; anterograde cardioplegia was administered every 15 minutes. Temperature was maintained at 34 degrees Celsius. Resection of the aneurysms and replacement with Dacron grafts 20 mm FlowWeave Bioseal (Artivion, Kennesaw, GA) tube for the RPA and 28 mm Dacron graft (VUP Medical A.S., Brno, Czech Republic) for the PT. Anastomoses were executed using 4/0 Prolene suture (Ethicon Inc., Johnson & Johnson, Somerville, NJ) and reinforced with linear Cortex felt (VUP Medical A.S., Brno, Czech Republic). The functionality of the new anastomoses and the absence of leaks were verified by filling the right ventricle and pulmonary arteries. Transesophageal echocardiography confirmed the functionality of the PT graft and its bifurcation into the right branch, showing a native pulmonary valve with preserved function. Epicardial pacemaker electrodes and 3 pericardial drains were placed. Surgical times were: CPB time of 279 minutes, aortic cross-clamp time of 218 minutes, and reperfusion time of 32 minutes, procedure without intraoperative events. Postoperative course in the ICU, the patient achieved successful weaning from mechanical ventilation and vasoactive support (Norepinephrine) within the first postoperative days. She experienced transient episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation with instability, requiring amiodarone loading, synchronized electrical cardioversion (270 Joules), and subsequent management with beta-blockers (Bisoprolol), achieving and maintaining sinus rhythm. Following a favorable clinical course and progressive removal of drains without complications, she was discharged 10 days after surgery in stable clinical condition. Discharge instructions included Apixaban, Amiodarone and Bisoprolol, analgesia and strict outpatient follow-up. Postoperative follow-up was uneventful; control studies were performed at 6 weeks to evaluate anatomical and functional integrity. A surveillance thoracic CTA was conducted, postoperative changes at the level of the PT and the origin of the main branches, with hyperdense prosthetic material at this level, PT diameter of 2.7 cm, LPA diameter of 1.7 cm, and RPA diameter of 2.1 cm (Fig. 2, Fig. 3). Normal pulmonary valve with normal function, left ventricular chamber dimensions and wall thickness within normal limits, minimal amount of pericardial fluid and minimal pericardial thickening were observed, with no evidence of pleural effusion or parenchymal consolidations. A surveillance transthoracic echocardiography showed preserved left ventricular systolic function, with an LVEF of 68%. The right ventricle remained nondilated with preserved systolic function, with a TAPSE of 21 mm. Estimated right ventricular systolic pressure/pulmonary artery systolic pressure was within normal limits at 26 mmHg. Mild tricuspid regurgitation and mild central aortic regurgitation persisted. Histopathological evaluation showed several membranous soft-tissue fragments measuring 3.0 - 4.4 cm in length on gross examination. One surface was smooth and whitish, whereas the opposite surface contained a firmly adherent blood clot. Microscopically, the specimen consisted of an arterial wall of variable thickness, with a focally thickened area characterized by degradation and disorganization of the elastic layer. There was associated intimal fibrosis and thickening. The muscular layer was attenuated and showed myxoid degeneration, while the adventitia demonstrated hemorrhage and sparse lymphocytic infiltration. Elastic staining confirmed dissociation of the elastic lamina. Alcian blue staining highlighted myxoid degeneration of the fibers, and trichrome staining demonstrated interstitial fibrosis. Discussion

PAA is an infrequent condition that poses significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges because of its low prevalence and the absence of standardized management guidelines [1]. In the present case, the patient had progressive chest pain and a 5.13-cm dilation of the pulmonary trunk, meeting radiological criteria for PAA by exceeding both the 4-cm threshold and the 1.5-fold expected baseline diameter criterion [1,6]. Considering the patient’s symptoms and radiological findings, surgical treatment was recommended.

Resection and replacement of the aneurysmal segment with a Dacron graft through median sternotomy and cardiopulmonary bypass is consistent with the definitive surgical approach described for giant aneurysms [9]. Although there are no established imaging schedules specifically for surgically repaired PAA, the 6-week CTA in this case was selected as an early postoperative baseline after resolution of the immediate postoperative period. This timing is also supported by thoracic vascular graft imaging literature, in which homogeneous low-attenuation perigraft fluid, soft-tissue stranding, and small isolated gas bubbles may be expected during the first 6 weeks, whereas new, enlarging, or persistent gas or collections beyond this interval should raise concern for complications [10].

The 6-week CTA confirmed graft patency and excluded early anastomotic complications. Echocardiographic follow-up complemented cross-sectional imaging by documenting preserved pulmonary valve function and normal pulmonary artery pressures. Subsequent surveillance should be individualized according to symptoms, pulmonary pressures, right-ventricular function, graft and native-vessel dimensions, renal function, and prior imaging findings. CTA provides high spatial resolution for graft and anastomotic assessment, whereas magnetic resonance angiography may reduce cumulative radiation and iodinated contrast exposure in selected patients.

This case highlights the importance of structured postoperative radiological follow-up after surgical repair of giant PAA. Even after successful elective surgery, serial imaging is necessary to monitor graft patency, anastomotic integrity, pulmonary artery branches, and possible adaptive changes in adjacent vascular structures. Given the limited evidence regarding postoperative surveillance in PAA, further clinical reporting is needed to better define optimal follow-up strategies.

Conclusion

PAA represents a highly complex clinical challenge due to its potential for lethal complications and the absence of standardized management protocols. This clinical case reaffirms that surgical intervention via synthetic graft substitution is a safe and effective strategy to prevent rupture or dissection in patients with critical diameters or persistent symptomatology.

The significance of this report lies in emphasizing that therapeutic success does not end with surgical resolution. Postoperative imaging follow-up is an indispensable tool, not only to verify the integrity and patency of the vascular reconstruction but also to monitor the dynamic evolution of the pulmonary arterial branches and other vascular segments, which is crucial for long-term outcomes.
