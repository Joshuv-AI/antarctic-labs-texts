Machine learning model of clinical laboratory data for 30-day mortality of patients with hodgkin’s lymphoma in ICU: a retrospective study based on MIMIC-IV database
Authors: Minghui Chang, Zheng Xu, Lingyu Xu, Chenyu Li, Xingguo Song, Limin Niu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrognostic stratification of Hodgkin lymphoma (HL) patients in ICU remains challenging, with conventional scoring systems often overlooking pathophysiological biomarkers. This retrospective cohort study analyzed 1,908 HL patients from the MIMIC-IV database. Multivariate logistic regression and machine learning (ML, gradient-boosting (GBM) was optimized with LASSO regularization) were employed to identify 30-day mortality predictors, validated through SHAP interpretability, calibration curves, and decision curve analysis. Multi-organ dysfunction (AST, BUN, T-Bil), systemic inflammation (NLR, WBC) and APTT emerged as critical mortality determinants, and selected for model construction. GBM achieved superior discrimination (training AUC = 0.89; test AUC = 0.75), SHAP analysis, calibration curve and decision curve analysis (DCA) confirmed clinical utility, outperforming empirical intervention strategies. This study establishes a biomarker-driven ML framework for HL prognosis, integrating renal, hepatic, and inflammatory markers into actionable risk stratification. thereby providing a scientific basis for comprehensive HL management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10238-025-01973-9.Keywords: Hodgkin’s lymphoma (HL), Machine learning model, Survival prognosis, 30-day mortality, MIMIC database

Introduction

Hodgkin’s lymphoma (HL), the common lymphoid malignancy that originates from lymphoid hematopoietic tissue, is characterized by the presence of Reed-Sternberg cells and exhibits distinct histopathological subtypes including nodular sclerosis and mixed cellularity [1]. Its incidence rate in China is about 5/100,000, lower than the world average level accounting for approximately 1% [2]. A majority of HL patients (80–90%) can be cured with conventional chemoradiotherapy [3–5]. However, some patients may still experience relapse or progression, even intensive care after first line treatment, due to the treatment’s effectiveness and aggressiveness, combined with poorly understood factors [6–9]. And relapse of HL is generally associated with unfavorable prognosis, such as secondary malignancies, heart disease. This not only seriously affects the quality of life but also places a sustained economic burden on families and society [10, 11]. Therefore, it is of great clinical significance to explore the prognostic factors to identify the HL patients with poor prognosis, especially those in intensive care unit (ICU).

Due to the multifactorial etiology and heterogeneous pathogenesis of HL, numerous clinical and biological factors can significantly influence patient outcomes. Large-scale retrospective studies are of great significance for a deep understanding of HL [1]. The Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC) is a large single-center database that provides clinical data to researchers from around the world for free [12]. It collected clinical data from 14,742 HL patients admitted to Intensive Care, providing a relatively reliable source for assessing the prognosis of HL patients in ICU. The dataset encompasses comprehensive clinical parameters such as laboratory tests, disease diagnoses, and survival status, which, unfortunately, are not included in any ICU-related scores. Hence, it is more accurate and meaningful to evaluate the outcome of HL patients in ICU using clinical laboratory characteristics.

The prognostic evaluation of HL in critically ill patients admitted to the ICU requires integrating traditional prognostic markers with ICU-specific parameters, though significant challenges exist. ICU-related scores such as SOFA, APS III, LODS are widely used in patients with severe infections, but they may be less accurate and meaningful in the case of lymphoma in the ICU [13]. Laboratory parameters can serve as prognostic indicators for HL patients in the ICU because they capture both tumor biology and critical illness pathophysiology. A retrospective study utilizing the MIMIC III database demonstrated that LDH serves as a prognostic indicator for non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma patients in the ICU [13, 14]. Meanwhile, Jing Yang et al. also demonstrated the correlation between VEGF overexpression and the survival and clinical pathological characteristics of NHL patients [15].

Machine learning (ML) mimics biological neural processes by adaptively optimizing hidden layer parameters during training, effectively addressing classification problems and minimizing prediction errors [16]. This technology demonstrates significant predictive efficacy in healthcare, capable of processing high-dimensional laboratory parameters (e.g., Cr, LDH, NLR) through regularization and feature selection to identify nonlinear interactions (e.g., hypoalbuminemia-lactate synergy) while autonomously extracting dynamic feature patterns (e.g., WBC-platelet ratios) [17, 18]. To address ICU data gaps, imputation methods are employed to integrate oncology and critical care biomarkers, enhancing prognostic accuracy.

In this study, we explored the correlation between laboratory parameters and prognosis in HL patients using the MIMIC database. More importantly, this investigation employed demographic variables (age, gender) and laboratory parameters to develop 10 ML architectures for 30-day mortality prediction in HL. The validated computational framework demonstrates potential for enhancing risk stratification protocols, thereby providing a scientific basis for comprehensive HL management.

Methods

Data selection and collection

This retrospective study leveraging critical care data (2020–2022) extracted from the MIMIC-IV database (https://physionet.org/content/mimiciv/3.1/), an open-access critical care database documenting patient encounters at a tertiary academic medical center [19]. This database is comprised of extensive and high-quality medical records of patients who were admitted to the intensive critical care units of the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center, two of our authors (Lingyu Xu, Zheng Xu) obtained the necessary permissions to utilize the MIMIC-IV database and was responsible for the data extraction.

The study population comprised two distinct cohorts: one for HL, and one for non-Hodgkin lymphoma (non-HL) used for an external validation attempt. Inclusion criteria: patients who were diagnosed with HL or non-HL admitted to the ICU; ≥ 18 years of age; stable vital signs and complete clinical and follow-up data. Exclusion criteria: unknown survival time; missing or unclear basic information, survival status, and treatment information; unclear tumor pathological characteristics; and incomplete clinical laboratory data. We performed 1:3 propensity score matching (PSM) without replacement for the HL cohort. Propensity scores were estimated based on age and gender, and matches were identified using the nearest-neighbor algorithm. The overall methodological workflow is detailed in Fig. 1. The variables selected from the MIMIC-IV database for this study included gender, age, survival status, Creatinine (Cr), Blood urea nitrogen (BUN), Alanine transaminase (ALT), Aspartate aminotransferase (AST), Total bilirubin (T-Bil), Hemoglobin (Hb), Total calcium (T-Ca), White blood cells (WBC), Lymphocytes (LYM), Neutrophils (NEUT), Neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), Hematocrit (HCT), Activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), Alkaline phosphatase (ALP), and Prothrombin time (PT).

Machine learning (ML) model Building strategy

To address collinearity concerns among candidate predictors identified through Lasso regression-based selection, 6 features including AST, BUN, NLR, WBC, APTT, T-Bil were subjected to model development. The refined variable subset was subsequently employed to establish optimized prediction frameworks. We constructed an integrative predictive model combining laboratory parameters to systematically evaluate 10 ML classifiers (including naïve bayes, K-nearest Neighbor (KNN), logistic regression, random forest (RF), decision tree (DT), Artificial Neural Networks (ANN), Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM), Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LGBM), Adaptive Boosting (AdaBoost), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), for 30-day mortality among HL patients in ICU. Implemented in Python 3.10 with standardized libraries, the computational framework employed GridSearchCV for hyperparameter tuning and rigorous 7:3 dataset partitioning. Model performance was assessed through AUC analysis, calibration validation, supplemented by SHAP analysis, calibration curve as well as decision curve analysis (DCA) revealed critical feature contributions. This critical feature was then rigorously validated using 70/30 bootstrap-based cross-validation to ensure robust and unbiased performance estimates.

Internal validation was performed using the bootstrap method, which involved 1000 iterations of resampling with replacement to generate distinct training sets. Each resampled dataset was split into 70% training and 30% test subsets. In each iteration, ROC curve metrics were calculated on the test set. AUC values were collected, and the aggregate True Positive Rate (TPR) was computed as the mean TPR across all iterations. Model discriminative performance was summarized by the mean AUC, and its variability was quantified by the standard deviation.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 23.0. Demographic and clinical characteristics were analyzed using chi-square tests for categorical variables. As the clinical data did not follow a normal distribution, median (M) and quartiles (QL, QU) were used for expression, and non-parametric tests were used for between-group comparisons. Univariate logistic regression identified predictors of survival in HL patients, followed by multivariate logistic regression to adjust for confounders (variables with P < 0.05 in univariate analysis). Prognostic performance of biomarkers was evaluated via receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 for all analyses.

Results

Demographic data and general clinical characteristics

Using the “mimic” module in the PostgreSQL10 software, 14,742 patients diagnosed with HL from MIMIC-IV were consulted and 1908 patients were enrolled in the current study according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. We retrospectively analyzed the medical records of these 1908 patients and divided them into the survivor group (1431 cases) and the non-survivor group (477 cases) based on their survival status at the last follow-up. As shown in Fig. 1, this respective data was first subjected to analysis the association between clinical laboratory characteristics and survival of HL patients in ICU, and then to construction of an ML model of laboratory data in predicting 30-day mortality of these patients.

The baseline characteristics of survivors and non-survivors are presented in Table 1. Patients in the survivor group had lower levels of Cr, BUN, ALT, AST, T-Bil, WBC, NEUT, NLR, APTT, ALP, and PT, HCT, but higher levels of T-Ca, LYM than those in the non-survivor group. The between-group differences were statistically significant (P < 0.05). There were no statistically significant differences in age, and Hb levels between the two groups (P > 0.05) (Table 1).

Construction and validation of ML model of clinical laboratory data for 30-day mortality of HL patients

To mitigate potential multicollinearity effects during model refinement, our ML optimization process incorporated L1-regularized regression (LASSO) for dimensionality reduction (Fig. 2A and B). Utilizing the one standard error rule for penalty parameter (λ) selection by choosing the most parsimonious model within one standard error of the minimum cross-validation error, we successfully screened out six variables with the most predictive power including WBC, BUN, AST, T-Bil, APTT, NLR.

Next, we developed an innovative computational architecture synergistically integrating above 8 clinical laboratory features to systematically benchmark 10 ML-based predictive models. During model development, XGBoost exhibited flawless discriminatory capacity (AUC = 1.00), outperforming GBM (AUC = 0.89) and light gradient boosting machine implementations (LGBM, AUC = 0.79) in the training cohort (Fig. 2C). In test cohort, GBM achieved the highest AUC score of 0.75 during validation. This exceeded the performance of competing algorithms XGBoost (0.70) (Fig. 2D). Based on these comprehensive evaluations, the evaluation framework ultimately identified GBM as the preferred algorithm due to its consistent top-tier performance in both training and test cohort. We further evaluated the GBM model using SHAP, feature importance quantification identified AST as the primary determinant, followed by BUN and NLR (Fig. 2E). Discussion

HL is a common malignant blood disease, and its incidence has been increasing year by year in China. Despite the high cure rate with first-line therapy, 5% to 10% of patients are refractory and 10% to 30% of patients relapse who experience poor outcomes [20, 21]. In order to better stratify the risk of adverse outcome in HL, a number of factors, including clinical and demographic (age, sex, Ann Arbor stage bulky peripheral or mediastinal disease, International Prognostic Score), have been tested in a number of previous studies. Despite the incorporation of several such parameters in a prognostic model, a sizeable fraction of high-risk patients still could not be identified in one study [22, 23]. Therefore, identification of reliable risk factors is crucial to guide treatment over the course of disease [24]. Previous studies have reported on the risk factors that affect the prognosis of HL patients [25], but most of them were limited to small sample sizes from single centers. This article analyzes the clinical characteristics of HL patients and explores the diagnostic efficacy of their survival prognosis based on the MIMIC database, providing effective evidence for clinical treatment.

This retrospective study analyzed 1908 HL patients to identify ICU mortality predictors. According to the survival status of the patients at the last follow-up, they are divided into the survivor group and the non-survivor group. Survivor group exhibited lower markers of organ dysfunction (AST, BUN, T-Bil), inflammation (WBC, NEUT, NLR), and coagulopathy (APTT), but higher T-Ca and LYM. There was no statistical significance in age, and Hb level between the two groups of patients. Multivariate analysis identified eight independent predictors (elevated BUN, T-Bil, NLR, WBC, NEUT, APTT, AST, HCT; reduced T-Ca, LYM), aligning with HL’s pathophysiological links to metabolic disturbances and immune dysregulation. The reasons for these findings may be that high BUN and T-Bil levels can lead to kidney and liver dysfunction, increasing the difficulty of treatment and worsening the prognosis [26]. Reduced T-Ca levels can induce abnormal calcium metabolism in patients, promoting cancer cell proliferation and invasion, and increasing resistance to radiation and chemotherapy, which can adversely affect treatment and prognosis. Elevated WBC, NEUT, and NLR levels can exacerbate immune and inflammatory responses in patients, affecting treatment efficacy and prognosis [27, 28]. Changes in HCT and APTT levels can lead to coagulation dysfunction in patients, making them more prone to circulatory system problems, which can affect treatment and prognosis.

Our study establishes a robust ML framework for prognosticating 30-day mortality of HL patients in ICU, addressing critical gaps in conventional ICU scoring systems. The GBM algorithm emerged as the optimal predictor (test AUC = 0.75), outperforming 9 other classifiers including XGB (AUC = 0.70) and LGBM (AUC = 0.74), through its balanced handling of complex biomarker interactions—a finding consistent with ML applications in hematologic malignancies [16–18]. The model’s superiority stems from two methodological innovations: (1) LASSO regularization effectively reduced collinearity among 14 candidate biomarkers to 6 clinically interpretable predictors (AST, BUN, NLR, WBC, APTT, T-Bil), and (2) SHAP-based interpretability analysis quantified non-linear relationships, revealing AST as the dominant feature attribution weight), followed by BUN and NLR.

The model’s generalizability is evidenced by minimal performance divergence between training (AUC = 0.89), cross-validation (AUC = 0.84) and test cohorts (AUC = 0.75). This aligns with prior studies advocating GBM for clinical datasets due to its built-in regularization and subsampling mechanisms [17]. Calibration curves further validated prediction reliability, demonstrating a critical improvement over traditional logistic regression models that often exhibit miscalibration in critical care settings [16]. DCA quantified the model’s clinical utility, showing consistent net benefit over “treat-all” strategies across 15–85% risk thresholds. This operational advantage stems from the model’s focus on actionable biomarkers: elevated BUN and NLR levels, indicative of renal dysfunction and systemic inflammation, align with known HL pathophysiology, while AST and WBC reflect hepatic stress and immune activation.

Despite these advances, several limitations should be acknowledged. The retrospective, single-center design may introduce selection bias. Although internal cross-validation demonstrated good performance, the absence of external validation in independent HL cohorts remains a notable constraint. Our attempt to validate the model in a non-HL cohort revealed suboptimal performance, confirming its disease-specific nature but also highlighting its limited generalizability beyond the intended population. Methodologically, while nonspecific biomarkers like AST and BUN contributed to prediction, their lack of specificity for HL progression complicates clinical interpretation, reflecting a trade-off between model performance and practical utility. Moreover, despite the use of SHAP to enhance interpretability, the inherent complexity of the GBM algorithm could still impede adoption in time-sensitive ICU environments, where simpler scoring systems are often favored. Future studies should focus on validating the model in multi-center HL cohorts and incorporating dynamic parameters such as real-time treatment response to enhance clinical applicability.

In conclusion, these findings suggest that multi-organ failure and systemic inflammation as pivotal determinants of 30-day mortality in critically ill HL patients. A GBM model, optimized via LASSO regularization, demonstrated robust prognostic performance with SHAP analysis highlighting AST, BUN, and NLR as dominant predictors. The model’s clinical utility was validated through calibration and decision curve analysis, outperforming empirical strategies. While limited by retrospective design, these findings bridge data-driven ML frameworks with pathophysiological insights, providing prognostic biomarkers for ICU risk stratification.
