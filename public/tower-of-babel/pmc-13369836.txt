Incidence and predictors of diabetic retinopathy among patients with type 2 diabetes and hypertension: a 10-year retrospective follow-up study
Authors: Mulat Belay Simegn, Habtamu Guaguahu Feleke, Muluye Gebrie Mengie, Desalew Mulu Gebey, Mekuant Dessie Bitewa, Aysheshim Asnake Abneh, Werkneh Melkie Tilahun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Diabetes-related blindness creates a major challenge for the healthcare system due to its high treatment cost. The presence of type 2 diabetes has been associated with an increased risk of hypertension, and vice versa, and their coexistence increases the risk of diabetic retinopathy. Despite this fact, to date, most of the research on diabetic retinopathy in Northwestern Ethiopia has been limited and has not considered the diabetic patient comorbid with hypertension. The study aims to determine the incidence and its predictors among patients with type-2 diabetes and hypertension at Debre Markos comprehensive and specialized hospitals. An institution-based retrospective follow-up study was conducted among 600 patients with type 2 diabetes and hypertension between June 30/2014, and July 01/2023. Data were entered using Epidata version 4.6 and analyzed using STATA version 17. All variables with P-values less than 0.25 in the bi-variable analysis were considered for multivariable analysis. Parametric survival models were fitted, and the Weibull model was selected based on its lowest AIC and BIC. Multicollinearity was checked using VIF. Statistical significance was declared at 95% CI with a P-value less than 0.05. Patients were followed for different follow-up times: with a median follow-up period of 85.2 months (IQR: 25.1). The overall incidence density of diabetic retinopathy was 5.16 per 1,000 person-months (95% CI 4.33–6.14). Injection medication (AHR = 0.24, 95% CI [0.18, 0.31]) and treatment duration ≥ 6 years (AHR = 0.30, 95% CI [0.15, 0.65]) decreased the hazard of developing the event. Whereas, poor glycemic control (AHR = 1.87, 95% CI [1.66, 2.31]), age ≥ 60 years (AHR = 1.51, 95% CI [1.09, 2.12]), obesity (AHR = 2.04, 95% CI [1.63, 2.71]), glycated hemoglobin (AHR = 1.88, 95% CI [1.42, 2.71]), and family history of diabetes (AHR = 2.12, 95% CI [1.16, 4.11]) had increased the hazard of developing diabetic retinopathy. The findings indicate a substantial burden and earlier occurrence of diabetic retinopathy. Therefore, early and regular screening could be prioritized, particularly for high-risk groups such as older patients, those with poor glycemic control, obesity, and a family history of diabetes. Clinically, targeting HbA1c reduction, blood pressure control, and weight reduction interventions should be recommended at all follow-up visits.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at10.1038/s41598-026-53165-4.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a chronic disease caused by either lack of enough insulin production by pancreas or when the body fails to use the produced insulin effectively1,2. Globally the number of patients with diabetes rose from 108 million in 1980 to 422 million in 20142. It is estimated to be 463 million people by 2019, and expected to rise to 578 million by 2030 and 700 million by 20453. However, Low and middle income countries takes a huge share of rapid rise in prevalence. Between 2000 and 2016 it causes a 5% increase in premature mortality2. An estimated 1.5 million deaths are directly caused by diabetes in 20194.

Diabetic retinopathy (DR) is emerging as global public health issue that may results in visual impairment5. It becomes the leading cause of blindness among working age adults globally, despite there are established treatment that can reduce the risk by 60%6. DR has a substantial and negative impact on patients’ emotional well-being7 and increase in financial costs in health care systems. it is estimated that diabetes account for 11.6% of the annual health-care budgets in most countries, and DR makes a big contribution to this figure8 For example in United States of America (USA) the direct annual costs of DR were estimated to be United States Dollar (USD) $490 million in 20049 and USD $93.6 per patient in Sweden10.

According to a study conducted in japan the incidence of diabetic retinopathy was 38.3/1000 person-years with a progression rate of 21.1/1000 person-years11. While in China12, Spain13, England14 and Australia15 the prevalence of DR was 1.81%, 2.43%, 36% and 14.4% respectively. In Africa the incidence of diabetic retinopathy was 225 per 1000 person years16. In Ethiopia the cumulative incidence of DR among patients with diabetes ranges from 16.017 to 20.15% (36.9 per 1000 person years (PY). The incidence rate was 9.66 and 50.42 per 1000 PY among type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) respectively18. Previous studies conducted across the world indicated that factors such as longer duration of type 2 DM (≥ 6 years)19,20, no education21–23, family history of DM24, presence of comorbidities like hypertension25–27, marital status28,29, inadequate physical exercise21,26, alcohol intake and smoking21 have been associated with incidence of diabetic retinopathy19. Additionally, hyperglycemia, cataract surgery30, high total cholesterol18 increased age, higher body mass index (BMI), socioeconomic status, urban resident16, lower hemoglobin level17,31, increased creatinine level, Male sex, high triglyceride levels, proteinuria12,32 taking more than one oral anti diabetic agents20 had an association with an incidence.

The incidence of hypertension in patients with type 2 diabetes is twofold higher than without the disease33. Their coexistence leads to a serious vascular complication and often devastating and remains one of the leading causes of blindness34. In addition to treating the case, various strategies such as more frequent patient visits35, home blood pressure and blood glucose monitoring36 have been reported to improve achievement of blood pressure and blood glucose targets and correspondingly to decrease the occurrence of diabetic retinopathy. Unlike most previous studies that focused exclusively on T2 DM patients, the current study included both T2DM and hypertensive patients, reflecting real-world multi-morbidity patterns in chronic disease participants. For instance, indicated that the co-occurrence of diabetes and hypertension synergistically increases micro vascular and macro vascular complications compared to either condition alone37. To date, the research on DR in Northwestern Ethiopia has been limited to prevalence estimates from cross-sectional studies and some used cox proportional hazards regression, a semi-parametric model that does not fully assume a specific baseline hazard distribution. The Weibull model is best to interpreted the proportional hazards model, allowing estimation with considering the baseline hazard function, which yield best estimate by minimize the standard error38. Thus, we determined time to DR, incidence of retinopathy and its predictors among T2DM and hypertensive patients at Debre Markos comprehensive and specialized hospital (DMCSH), Northwest Ethiopia.

Method and materials

Study area, design, period, and population

An institution-based retrospective follow-up study was conducted at DMCSH among type 2 diabetes and hypertensive patients having follow-up between June 30/2014, and July 01/2023. It is located in the Misraq Gojjam Zone, Debre Markos town of the Amhara region. Debre Markos Comprehensive Specialized Hospital is the only specialized hospital found in East Gojjam Zone; it serves more than 3.5 million people in its catchment area. The source population was all type 2 diabetes patients concomitant with hypertension having a follow-up at DMCSH. Among those, all diagnosed type 2 patients concomitant with hypertension diagnosed between June 30/2014, and July 01/2023, and available during the study period were considered as the study population.

Eligibility criteria

All type 2 diabetes mellitus patients with concomitant hypertension who were diagnosed between June 30, 2014, and July 01, 2023 and available during the study period were included in the study. However, dates of initiation were not recorded, the status of the outcome variable (DR) was not recorded, and patients who developed DR at the time of diagnosis (beginning of the study) were excluded.

Sampling technique and procedures

After identifying patients who fulfilled the selection criteria, a sampling frame was prepared by collecting their medical record numbers (MRN) from the hospital’s chronic illness clinic registration book. This registry contains comprehensive records of all patients diagnosed with both type two diabetes and hypertensive patients within a specified timeframe (June 30/2014, to July 01/2023). Then, study subjects were selected using a simple random sampling technique via a random number generator. This sampling procedure was further supported by the schematic diagram (Fig. 1).

Data collection tool

Data for this study is secondary data, and it was obtained from diabetic and hypertensive patients under follow-up from June 30/2014, to July 01/2023. A structured checklist was prepared and used for the extraction of relevant information from patient charts. Data was extracted from the patient’s intake form, follow-up card, chronic disease registration book, any electronic data sources, and clinical records, including laboratory results of biomarkers. Health professionals working at the chronic and medical outpatient department (OPD) were assigned as data collectors. Each data collector follows the prepared checklist to extract specific data points, including socio-demographic information, clinical details, treatment history, behavior factors, and outcomes. The process involves finding each patient’s medical chart, verifying the patient’s identity using their medical card number, and thoroughly extracting the required information on the checklist.

Data quality assurance

In order to maintain the quality of the data, well-experienced data collectors and supervisors were recruited. Two-day training was given for the data collectors on the objective of the study and how to review the documents as per the data extraction checklist. Before actual data collection time, the checklist (tool) was pretested on 5% of the sample size in the samples, thereby possible adjustments or modifications could be made to the tool. The variables like glucometer usage, physical activity status, and self-monitoring of glucose were excluded from the checklist due to not being recorded at any of the registers. Besides, daily monitoring of data collection for completeness and consistency was made, and the principal investigator was supervising the data collectors closely during data entry into EpiData, and there was a double-check process for the entered data.

Data process and analysis

The extracted data was entered into Epidata version 4.6 and was exported to R and STATA statistical software Version 17 for further management and analysis. All Descriptive statistics was computed considering the nature (categorical/continuous) and distribution (normal vs. skewed) and presented using tables, graphs, and texts. The categorical variables was described using frequency with percentages while mean with standard deviation and median with interquartile range was used to summarize normal and skewed distributed continuous variables, respectively. During data management, missing value was treated before the model was fitted for covariates that have more than 5% missing (Occupation 11%, alcohol use 6%, smoking 9%, neuropathy 18%, education 8%, and marital status 14%). Multiple imputations by chained equations (MICE) were performed with 20 imputations assuming missing at random. A sensitivity analysis was conducted by comparing the imputed datasets with the complete-case dataset. The parameter estimate between complete case analysis and multiple imputation is not that much different.

The incidence density rate (IDR) was calculated for the entire follow-up period. The survival experience was assessed using the Kaplan-Meier survivor function. The log-rank test was used to test the presence of significant differences among survival curves. Bi-variable analysis was conducted and variables had a p value of 0. 25 and less were considered for multivariable survival regression. Proportional hazard assumptions were checked using Schoenfeld residual global test (P-Value was 0.94), showed that proportional hazard assumption was meet. The shape parameter for the baseline hazard function was determined, which was approximately 1 (γ = 1.002), indicating that the hazard of developing diabetic retinopathy is approximately constant over time with a very slight increasing trend. This supports the appropriateness of the Weibull distribution, as it is flexible enough to accommodate constant, increasing, or decreasing hazards. The Weibull parametric model was preferred because, once the baseline hazard distribution is appropriately specified, it provides more efficient and precise estimates with smaller standard errors compared to the semi-parametric Cox model. Furthermore, different parametric survival models (Exponential, Weibull, Log-Normal, Log-Logistic, and Gompertz) and cox proportional hazard model were tested and the Weibull survival model had the lowest AIC/BIC value (Supplementary Table 1). Finally, variables with a P-value of less than 0.05 in multivariable regression analysis were considered to be a significant predictor of the time to diabetic retinopathy. Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF). The results indicated no evidence of multicollinearity, with a maximum VIF of 3.75 and a mean VIF of 2.47.

Model fitness: Goodness of fit of the model was assessed by Cox–Snell residual for the selected Weibull survival regression. Cox-snail residual test of the hazard function followed a 45-degree line showing that it approximately had an exponential distribution with a hazard rate of one confirming the model fitness of the data (Fig. 2). The association between predictors and hazard of developing DR was summarized using adjusted hazard ratio (AHR), and statistical significances were tested at P < 0.05.

Result

Descriptive statistics

From the registry of medical and chronic departments, 633 patient cards were selected. Due to the exclusion criteria, 33 patient cards were removed from the analysis due to outcome variable was not recorded, develop retinopathy at the time of diagnosis, or time was not register during follow-up was started. The mean age of participants at baseline was 56.2 (SD ± 15.96) years, and of the total study subjects, 34.5% were within the age category ≥ 60 years; among these patients, 42.5% develop diabetic retinopathy. More than half (62.7%) of the patients were male. On the other hand, less than one-third (26.2%) of the patients have no education at all. Similarly, more than half (56.2%) was also from urban residences. Nearly one-third (29.8%) of patients were single. Of all patients, 27% had no occupation, of which 25.3% develop retinopathy (Table 1).

Clinical and behavioral related characteristics

The mean baseline HbA1c was 8.37 with a standard deviation of SD ± 1.19, indicating the existence of individual variation at the initiation of the study. About 55.8% had diabetes duration of ≥ 5 years, of which 25.4% developed DR. Poor glycemic control was common, observed in 67.3% of participants; among these, 27% developed the event. Nearly half of the participants had normal BMI (49%), whereas 19.5% were obese, of which 37.6% developed retinopathy. Out of 252 patients who developed neuropathy, 34.9% developed DR. Among ever cigarette-smoking and alcohol-drinking use patients, 29% and 24.2% were developing diabetic retinopathy, respectively (Table 2).

Incidence

The findings indicated that about 126 cases (21%, 95% CI 17.9, 24.4) of study participants developed DR, making the overall incidence density rate 5.16 cases (95% CI 4.33–6.14) per 1000 person-months. The total time at risk was 24, 427 person months. The cumulative survival probabilities at 24, 48, and 72 months were 90%, 78%, and 60% respectively. The probability of developing diabetic retinopathy was increased when the survival time also increased (Fig. 3). Additionally, Kaplan-Meier survivor cures were taken across the age category, indicating significant differences in survival experience over time. Age ≥ 60 years exhibits a steeper decline in survival probability, indicating a higher hazard of experiencing the event and shorter event-free survival time (Fig. 4). Patients with a family history of diabetes show a lower survival probability over time compared to those without a family history. The survival curve for the Yes group declines more steeply, indicating a higher and earlier occurrence of the event (Fig. 5). Poor glycemic control exhibits the lower survival probability, indicating the highest risk of the event. Those with good glycemic control maintain the higher survival probability throughout the follow-up period (Fig. 6). Furthermore, those patients with uncontrolled hypertension status had a lower survival probability over time compared to those with controlled hypertension status. In contrast, patients with controlled hypertension status maintain relatively higher survival probabilities throughout the follow-up time (Fig. 7).

Discussion

In our study, 21% [95% CI 17.9, 24.4] of the participants had developed diabetic retinopathy with the incidence density of 5.16 cases per 1000 person month of observation. This finding is consistent with other cohort studies in sub-Saharan Africa 15–25%48, Jimma 20.15%18, Tikur Anbessa 18.6%32, and Gondar 20.4%49. The consistency of our findings with previous studies conducted in Ethiopia may be attributed to similarities in population characteristics, health system settings, diagnostic criteria, and study designs. Most of the studies, including ours, were conducted in similar public hospital environments where diagnostic and treatment protocols for diabetes and its complications such as retinopathy are relatively standardized. Additionally, comparable socio-demographic factors, and healthcare-seeking behaviors could have contributed to the similarity in outcomes. However this finding was higher than studies conducted at China 1.81%11, Spain 2.43%13, England 3.6%14, and Australia 14.4%15. In many low- and middle-income countries like Ethiopia, patients often present late, after complications have already begun. A 2023 study in Africa highlighted that insufficient access to medical care and inadequate healthcare infrastructure contribute significantly to accelerated DR development50. Additionally, Studies in Ethiopia consistently show that poor glycemic control, and coexisting hypertension, are strong independent predictors of DR51. On the other hand our result was lower than findings from Malawi ranging from 38.9% to 48.4% %31,52, South Africa 63%53. The disparity might be in our study there is relatively younger age (mean age of 56.2years) compared to population in Malawi and South Africa. Individual age strongly predict DR risk, in our case only 34.5% were aged ≥ 60, which likely reduced the overall incidence. Furthermore, Malawian/South African studies recruit from tertiary eye clinics, where patients typically present with visual complaints leading to inflated DR prevalence.

Patients taking injection medication decrease the hazard of developing diabetic retinopathy by 76%. This finding is consistent with study conducted in United States54, and in Amhara region of Gondar55. This could be due to patients using more intensive treatment regimens whether by injecting insulin or more than one oral medication enhances glycemic control and reduces the speed of DR development56. Similarly, Patients having treatment duration ≥ 6 years decrease the hazard of developing the event by 70%. This is congruent with study conducted at Iran57. This consistent finding might be long-term medication use improves sustained glycemic control, a key mechanism delaying micro vascular damage and benefits of treatment take time to manifest in retinal health. The predictor poor glycemic control increases the hazard of developing diabetic retinopathy by 1.87 times. This is evidenced by study conducted at Korea58. The reason for this consistency is continuously exposing retinal tissues to high glucose levels; poor glycemic control accelerates biochemical damage, vascular dysfunction, and neurodegeneration. This not only raises the probability but also shortens the time to onset of diabetic retinopathy59. Patients being obesity (BMI > = 30) increase the hazard of developing the event by 2.04 times compared to normal BMI. This result was supported by study conducted ant Kenya16 and a systematic review and meta-analysis study done at China60. This association is likely mediated through obesity-driven mechanisms such as insulin resistance, suboptimal glycemic control, hypertension, and systemic inflammation, all of which accelerate micro vascular damage. Individuals having family history of diabetes also increase the hazard of developing diabetic retinopathy. This finding is supported by studies from Ethiopia61,62, prospective study done at United Kingdom63 and Pakistan64. This may be due to both genetic predisposition and shared environmental risk factors leading to micro vascular endothelial dysfunction. This underscores the need for proactive screening in patients with diabetes with such family backgrounds49. Identifying patients with a family history of hypertension can help clinicians target those at elevated risk for DR, enabling earlier retinal screening and intervention. On the other hand, a unit increase in HbA1c increases the hazard of developing diabetic retinopathy by 1.88 times, keeping the other variable constant. This is due to the fact that high HbA1c levels reflect poor long-term glycemic control and are strongly associated with the development and progression of diabetic retinopathy. The pathophysiology behind this association lies in chronic hyperglycemia causing micro-vascular damage in the retina through mechanisms such as increased oxidative stress, inflammation, and formation of advanced glycation end products. This vascular damage leads to capillary leakage, ischemia, and neovascularization hallmarks of DR62,65.

Strength and limitation of study

This study was conducted using the parametric approach, using the Weibull distribution, better estimation improving inference accuracy compared to the Cox proportional hazard. It also considered a long follow-up time up to 10 years, which enhances the study’s reliability. However, it is not without limitations. Study limits causal inference due to its retrospective nature and depends on data accuracy recorded in medical charts, and some important factors, such as lipid profile, inflammatory markers, diet, and physical activity, were not available in records. Because this was a retrospective observational study based on medical records, residual confounding and bias may remain despite random sampling and multivariable adjustment. The study was conducted at a single hospital, not a multicenter study, which may limit the generalizability of the results to other settings.

Conclusion and recommendation

In this study, the incidence of diabetic retinopathy was somewhat high when compared to prior similar studies in Ethiopia. This study revealed that injection medication, age ≥ 60 years, poor glycemic control status, obesity, having a family history of diabetes, baseline HbA1c level, and ≥ 6 years’ treatment duration were significant predictors of time to diabetic retinopathy. Therefore, early and regular screening could be prioritized, particularly for high-risk groups such as older patients, those with poor glycemic control, obesity, and a family history of diabetes. Clinically, targeting HbA1c reduction, blood pressure control, and weight reduction interventions should be reinforced at all follow-up visits. Furthermore, strengthening patient education on lifestyle modification, medication adherence, and regular eye examination is also recommended to reduce the progression and incidence of diabetic retinopathy. Additionally, we recommend that researchers conduct prospective multicenter studies including lifestyle and genetically related factors.
