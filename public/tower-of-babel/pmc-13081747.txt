Blastocystis sp. induces neurocognitive deficits in mice via p38 MAPK-mediated downregulation of NR4A1
Authors: Xin Yang, Huizhu Nan, Yan Zhao, Huan Li, Xiangyun Zhang, Mu He, Lei Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBlastocystis sp. is a zoonotic protozoan with a broad host range, and its pathogenicity in humans has remained controversial. In this study, we observed neurocognitive impairments in mice following Blastocystis sp. infection, prompting us to investigate the mechanisms underlying these changes. Transcriptomic analysis of brain tissue revealed significant changes in the expression of several Neurocognitive Deficits -associated genes, most notably a marked downregulation of the nuclear receptor NR4A1. These findings were validated and confirmed using Western blot, immunofluorescence, and immunohistochemical analyses. While in vitro infection of BV2 microglial cells with Blastocystis sp. revealed activation of the IL-17-p38 MAPK-NR4A1 signaling axis, in vivo administration of a p38 inhibitor in infected mice ameliorated motor and cognitive deficits, demonstrating that this signaling cascade mediates neuropathology in mouse brain following the parasite infection. This finding indicates that Blastocystis sp. infection induces IL-17 overexpression in both the gut and brain, resulting in the activation of the p38 MAPK pathway. This subsequently leads to significant NR4A1 downregulation and, ultimately, results in Neurocognitive Impairment behavioral phenotypes in mice. This study reveals a novel mechanism whereby Blastocystis sp. infection induces cognitive and motor deficits through activation of the p38 MAPK-NR4A1 pathway in brain tissue, offering a potential therapeutic target for future interventions.Keywords: Blastocystis sp., neurocognitive deficits, p38 MAPK, NR4A1

Introduction

Blastocystis sp. is a zoonotic protozoan commonly found in the gut and is known to infect a wide range of hosts, including humans, birds, and other mammals.1,2 Its prevalence varies depending on geographic location and lifestyle factors, and its pathogenicity in hosts remains a subject of ongoing debate. To date, 42 subtypes of Blastocystis sp. have been identified,3 with substantial variation in pathogenicity observed among different subtypes within host organisms.4

Some subtypes of Blastocystis sp. appear to confer beneficial metabolic and immunological effects. For example, colonisation by Blastocystis sp. ST4 in mice has been shown to promote faster recovery from dextran sulphate sodium (DSS)-induced colitis.5 ST1 colonisation has been associated with increased abundance of the beneficial bacterium Akkermansia,6 while long-term colonisation by ST3 has been found to mitigate the loss of bacterial diversity following colitis induction.7 Additionally, Blastocystis sp. has been reported to support gut microbial homoeostasis8 and has been linked to healthy dietary patterns and favourable cardiometabolic biomarkers,9 suggesting that it may act as a commensal organism rather than a pathogen.10 Conversely, certain subtypes of Blastocystis sp. have been shown to induce inflammatory responses in the host’s skin and intestinal tract11‐13 and are often considered potential pathogens. For instance, ST7 disrupts the balance between regulatory T lymphocyte (Tregs) and T-helper 17 (Th17) cells by promoting pro-inflammatory immune responses.14 This subtype has been linked to gut dysbiosis and intestinal inflammation. Moreover, multiple studies have demonstrated that Blastocystis sp. infection induces intestinal oxidative stress, characterised by elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and diminished antioxidant enzyme activity, ultimately promoting a systemic inflammatory state.14‐16

Regarding neurocognitive impacts, Blastocystis sp. infection has been associated with various neurological symptoms. Firstly, clinical studies have reported significant correlations between this protozoan infection and several neuropsychiatric disorders, including schizophrenia, anxiety, depression, and hepatic encephalopathy.17,18 Animal studies have further demonstrated that Blastocystis sp. infection alters gene expression profiles in the prefrontal cortex of mice, leading to impaired cognitive function and reduced learning and memory capabilities15, while also inducing anxiety and depression-like behaviours in rats.19 These findings implicate Blastocystis sp. infection in cognitive dysfunction and possibly in the development of neurodegenerative diseases.

Secondly, currently known mechanisms by which gut microbiota—including bacteria and fungi—affect neurocognitive function include the production of microbial metabolites, immune modulation, and neuroinflammation. Dysbiosis may compromise the integrity of the intestinal barrier, allowing inflammatory mediators to enter systemic circulation and cross the blood–brain barrier, thereby triggering neuroinflammation.20 Alternatively, microbial metabolites such as propionate or short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) may induce oxidative stress and neurotoxicity, impair cognitive function, and contribute to the pathogenesis of neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease and Parkinson’s disease.21 The mechanisms by which Blastocystis sp. induces neurological impairments may involve alterations in gut microbial diversity resulting in changes in pyrimidine, one-carbon, and aromatic amino acid metabolism. These pathways are closely linked to folate-mediated one-carbon metabolism, which plays a critical role in neurocognitive executive functions.22 Additionally, Blastocystis sp. may affect gut-derived tryptophan metabolites associated with depression,23‐25 thereby indirectly contributing to memory loss, anxiety, and behavioural abnormalities in the host. These symptoms align with behavioural phenotypes observed in several neurodegenerative diseases.

The nuclear receptor NR4A1(also known as Nur77) plays a pivotal role in regulating neuroinflammation and cognitive function, which is expressed in macrophages and dopaminergic neurons, including those located in the dorsal striatum, nucleus accumbens, olfactory tubercle, prefrontal cortex and cingulate cortex.26,27 Its expression can be induced by various stimuli, such as inflammatory mediators and growth factors.28 In various models of neurodegenerative diseases—including Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, and HD—activation of the p38 MAPK pathway has been strongly linked to neuronal damage and cognitive dysfunction.29,30 Notably, the p38 MAPK signalling pathway negatively regulates NR4A1 expression, wherein inhibition of p38 MAPK attenuates inflammation-induced reduction of Nur77.31 Loss of NR4A1 has been shown to result in excessive activation of microglia,32 increased infiltration of inflammatory cells, and rapid downregulation of anti-inflammatory gene transcription,33 consequently exacerbating the disruption of the blood–brain barrier and accelerating the onset and progression of disease symptoms.34 Based on these findings, we hypothesise that Blastocystis sp. infection may modulate NR4A1 expression in brain tissue through an inflammation-related pathway, leading to neuronal dysfunction and behavioural deficits.

Previous studies have reported a strong association between Blastocystis sp. infection and elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines in the host,35 particularly the upregulation of interleukin-17 (IL-17). IL-17 can enter the central nervous system (CNS) via systemic circulation36 and has been implicated in the onset and progression of neuroinflammatory conditions such as Alzheimer's disease, epilepsy, and depression.37,38 It is well-established that IL-17 and other pro-inflammatory cytokines enhance the phosphorylation of p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), subsequently activating downstream inflammatory cascades within the p38 MAPK signalling pathway.39,40 This activation significantly promotes the stimulation of BV2 microglial cells and regulates the expression and release of downstream inflammatory mediators, exacerbating neuroinflammation and impairing normal neuronal function.41 We hypothesise that Blastocystis sp. infection may induce IL-17 overexpression, subsequently activating the p38 MAPK signalling pathway, which in turn negatively regulates NR4A1 expression, ultimately leading to neuronal dysfunction and cognitive impairment. Given our previous observation of Neurocognitive behavioural phenotypes in mice infected with Blastocystis sp., we designed this study to investigate whether such neurological alterations are mediated through IL-17-induced activation of the p38 MAPK signalling pathway, ultimately leading to the downregulation of NR4A1 and the development of neurodegenerative pathology.

Materials and methods

Animals and infection model

Male and female BALB/c mice (6 weeks old, 19–22 g) were purchased from Hebei Experimental Animal Centre and housed under SPF conditions (12 h light/dark cycle, 22 ± 2 °C, 55 ± 5% humidity) with free access to food and water. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. After a 7-day acclimation period, experimental groups received 1 × 106 Blastocystis sp. by oral gavage, while control mice received phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The establishment, colonisation, and load quantification methods for the Blastocystis sp. ST2 infection mouse model used in this study are detailed in our previously published work.42,43

Drug treatment

Inhibition of the p38 MAPK signalling pathway was achieved using its specific inhibitor, SB203580 (Biochemical Reagent, Catalogue No. A8254). SB203580 powder was dissolved in DMSO to prepare a 10 mg/mL stock solution, which was aliquoted and stored at −20 °C protected from light. Prior to daily injection, the stock solution was diluted to the desired concentration with sterile saline, ensuring that the final concentration of DMSO did not exceed 1% (v/v). Drug treatment was initiated 24 hours after establishing the Blastocystis sp. infection model. Mice in the intervention group received intraperitoneal injections of the SB203580 solution daily at a fixed time (10:00 AM), at a dosage of 2 mg/kg body weight. Mice in the solvent control group were injected with an equal volume of saline containing 1% DMSO in the same manner. This treatment regimen was continued for a total of 14 days.

Behavioural assays

Behavioural performance was assessed on day 7 post-infection. Animals were randomly assigned to experimental groups using computer-generated random numbers. All behavioural tests were conducted and scored by investigators blinded to group allocation. The randomisation scheme and blinding protocol were maintained throughout the study. There are no significant differences in body weight were observed between infected and control groups during the behavioural testing period. All animals maintained normal hydration status through free access to water, and no overt signs of systemic illness or temperature dysregulation were noted.

Morris water maze (MWM)

Spatial learning and memory were assessed using a circular water maze (120 cm diameter, 22 ± 1°C). A submerged platform was placed in the northeast quadrant. Mice underwent 4 trials per day for 5 days. Latency, path length, and swim speed were tracked. A probe trial (platform removed) on day 6 evaluated memory retention by recording time in the target quadrant and platform crossings. Data from the Morris water maze test were analysed by two-way ANOVA, with treatment group (control vs. infected) and time (training days) as the two independent factors. The interaction between group and time was also examined. For spatial memory parameters such as platform crossings and time spent in the target quadrant, post-hoc Tukey's test was applied for between-group comparisons.

Open field test (OFT)

Spontaneous locomotion and anxiety-like behaviour were measured in a 100 × 100 × 40 cm arena. Mice were allowed to freely explore for 10 min. Total distance travelled and time spent in the centre zone were recorded via tracking software (SMART v3.0, RWD Life Science Co., Ltd, Shenzhen, China).

Elevated plus maze (EPM)

Anxiety-like behaviour was assessed using a plus-shaped maze elevated 50 cm above the floor. Mice were allowed to explore freely for 5 min. Time and entries into open and closed arms were recorded via tracking software (SMART v3.0, RWD Life Science Co., Ltd, Shenzhen, China).

Tissue collection and processing

Following behavioural testing, mice were anaesthetised with 10% chloral hydrate and perfused with saline. Brains were removed on ice. For biochemical analyses, hippocampal and cortical tissues were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C. For histological analyses, half-brains were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), cryoprotected in 30% sucrose, embedded in OCT, and sectioned into 20 μm slices using a Leica CM1950 cryostat.

Western blotting

Tissues were lysed in radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer supplemented with protease/phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined by bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay. Samples (40 μg) were denatured, resolved by sodium dodecyl sulphate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. Membranes were blocked in 5% milk, incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, and then incubated in horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualised using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system. The intensity of each protein band was quantified using ImageJ. For densitometric analysis, a uniform local background subtraction method was applied to all blots to ensure consistency.To correct for variations in protein loading and transfer efficiency, the signal intensity of each target protein was first normalised to the intensity of the corresponding GAPDH (or β-actin) loading control band from the same sample. All samples were analysed under identical conditions with standardised parameters. Data are presented as the normalised fold change relative to the control group. Each experiment was independently repeated at least three times, and data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical significance was determined by unpaired t-test (for two-group comparisons) or one-way ANOVA (for multiple groups), with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Primary antibodies

Anti-IBA1, 1:1000 (Nature Biosciences, A30311); Anti-GFAP, 1:1000 (Nature Biosciences, A7334); Anti-Huntingtin, 1:1000 (Nature Biosciences, A67072); Anti-P-Tau, 1:1000 (Nature Biosciences, A56315); Anti-Amyloid beta, 1:1000 (Nature Biosciences, A34089); Anti-IL-17, 1:1000 (Biodragon, BD-PT7835); Anti-p38 MAPK, 1:1000 (Biodragon, RM4573); Anti-p38 (phosphor Thr180/Y182), 1:1000 (Biodragon, BD-PP0338); Beta Actin Mouse, 1:10,000 (Nature Biosciences, RA0001); Anti-GADPH, 1:10,000 (Coherion, CPA9165); Goat Anti-Mouse (H + L) Antibody, 1:10,000 (Nature Biosciences, M00001).

RNA extraction and qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from hippocampus and cortex sections using Trizol (TransGen Biotech), quantified by spectrophotometry, and reverse-transcribed into cDNA. qPCR was performed using SYBR Green (TransGen Biotech) on a real-time PCR platform. GAPDH served as the internal control. Relative expression was analysed using the 2−ΔΔCT method.

Immunofluorescence (IF) and immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Brain sections were permeabilized in 0.3% Triton X-100, blocked in 5% goat serum, and incubated overnight at 4 °C in 1:100 IBA1, GFAP, Huntingtin, and NR4A1 primary antibodies. Sections at Bregma + 0.5 mm were selected for immunostaining to target the dorsal caudoputamen (CPu). This region was identified by its anatomical boundaries: dorsally by the corpus callosum, medially by the lateral ventricle, and laterally by the external capsule. Images were acquired from the central CPu (1.5–2.0 mm lateral to the midline) using a 20 × objective (S4). This specific stereotaxic level was chosen because it captures the corticostriatal interface critical for cognitive-motor integration and allows standardised comparison with existing literature.44‐46 For IF, slides were incubated with Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies and mounted with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). Confocal imaging was performed. For IHC, HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies and diaminobenzidine (DAB) detection were used, followed by hematoxylin counterstaining and light microscopy. ImageJ was used for quantification. Immunofluorescence and immunohistochemistry sections were captured at 200 × magnification, with five non-overlapping fields randomly selected per group. Positive signals within fixed areas were automatically counted using ImageJ software with uniform threshold settings. Cell counting was performed independently by two investigators blinded to experimental groupings, and the average values were used for subsequent analysis.

Experimental design

Randomisation

Animals were randomly assigned to four experimental groups: female control group, female Blastocystis sp.-infected group, male control group, and male Blastocystis sp.-infected group. The randomisation procedure was carried out using a computer-generated random number sequence to ensure unbiased allocation across all groups.

Blinding procedure

All behavioural assessments, including the rotarod test, open field test, and other relevant behavioural paradigms, were conducted by investigators blinded to the experimental conditions. The entire testing process was video-recorded, and subsequent scoring was performed by evaluators who had no knowledge of the group assignments to eliminate potential scoring bias.

Transcriptomic analysis

RNA sequencing of brain tissues generated approximately 47 million reads per sample on the Illumina platform. After quality control and alignment to the reference genome, differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 with negative binomial testing. Gene counts were normalised by the BaseMean method, and significantly differentially expressed protein-coding genes were identified using thresholds of |log2FoldChange| > 1 and adjusted p-value < 0.05. The raw transcriptomic sequencing data have been deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under the accession number PRJNA1365050.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as means ± SEM. Two-group comparisons were performed using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests. For multi-group analysis, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Potential outliers were objectively identified using Grubbs’ test, applied only after confirming data normality via the Shapiro-Wilk test.

Results

Mice injected with Blastocystis sp. exhibit cognitive and behavioural impairments

To evaluate the effects of Blastocystis sp. infection on neurocognitive behaviour in mice, we conducted experiments using 6-week-old male and female BALB/c mice divided into control and infected groups. Spatial learning and memory were assessed using the Morris water maze (MWM) test. However, considering that female mice may be more affected by hormonal influences or have greater individual differences in exploratory behaviour,39 male mice were subsequently selected for additional behavioural assessments to ensure the rigour of the results, including open field test (OFT) and elevated plus maze (EPM) to evaluate anxiety-like behaviours and rotarod test to assess balance and motor coordination (Figure 1). Three days post-infection, both male and female mice exhibited significantly prolonged escape latencies during the MWM test from day 2 to day 6 compared to control mice (Figure 1A). On day 7, following removal of the platform, infected mice crossed the former platform location fewer times than the controls (Figure 1B), indicating impaired spatial learning and memory.

To investigate whether the duration of infection influenced behavioural outcomes, the same cohort of mice was subjected to a second MWM test following a 14-day infection period. Results showed a further significant increase in escape latency (Figure 1C), and spatial learning and memory continued to decline in the infected group compared to the control group. Notably, on days 5 and 6, escape latencies were even more prolonged compared to those observed during the earlier testing period, suggesting that extended infection exacerbates cognitive deficits (Figure 1D). These findings indicate that prolonged Blastocystis sp. infection may lead to more severe neurocognitive impairments.

To assess the extent of neurocognitive impairments, we conducted OFT, EPM, and rotarod tests to evaluate the effects of Blastocystis sp. infection on anxiety-like behaviour and motor coordination in mice.47,48 In the OFT, mice in the infected group spent significantly less time and travelled far less distance in the central zone compared to those in the control group (Figure 1E and F). Locomotor trajectory analysis revealed a marked reduction in activity within the open area with movement paths that appeared more confined and less exploratory (Figure 1G and H), indicating elevated levels of anxiety-like behaviour in Blastocystis sp.-infected mice. In the EPM test, infected mice spent significantly less time and travelled shorter distances in the open arms compared to control mice (Figure 1I and J). Locomotor trajectory analysis also indicated that activity in the infected group was predominantly confined to the closed arms (Figure 1K and L), further supporting the finding that anxiety-like behaviour was increased in Blastocystis sp.-infected mice. To evaluate balance and motor coordination, we performed the rotarod test. Infected mice exhibited a significantly shorter latency to fall compared to controls (Figure 1M), indicating impaired balance and coordination. Together, these findings demonstrate that Blastocystis sp. infection significantly impaired memory function, increased anxiety-like behaviour, and compromised motor coordination in mice.

To determine whether Blastocystis sp. infection induces neuroinflammation and pathological changes associated with neurodegenerative diseases, we examined the expression of the neuroinflammatory markers IBA1, a marker of microglial activation,49 and GFAP, a marker of astrocyte activation,42 in the cortex and hippocampus using Western blot (WB) analysis. Results showed that the expression levels of both IBA1 and GFAP were significantly elevated in the cortical region of infected mice compared to controls, whereas no significant differences between the two groups were observed in the hippocampus (Figure 2B and E). These findings suggest that Blastocystis sp. infection preferentially induces neuroinflammatory responses in the cortex.

Given that neuroinflammation is a common pathological feature of neurodegenerative diseases, we assessed the expression of key neurodegeneration-related proteins in the cortical region using WB analysis. Specifically, we examined β-amyloid (Aβ), phosphorylated Tau (P-Tau, a marker of neurofibrillary tangles), and HTT to investigate whether Blastocystis sp. infection is associated with pathological features resembling those of Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, and HD.50‐52 The results showed elevated expression of these proteins in the infected group compared to controls (Figure 2C and F), further supporting the notion that Blastocystis sp. infection induces neurocognitive dysfunction via neuroinflammatory mechanisms that promote neurodegeneration.

To visualise the spatial distribution and localisation of these molecular markers within neural tissue, we performed histological validation using immunohistochemistry (IHC) and immunofluorescence (IF) assays. The results showed a significant increase in GFAP- and IBA1-positive cells in the cortex of the infected group (Figure 2A and D), providing additional evidence that Blastocystis sp. infection may activate inflammatory responses that lead to glial cell activation. This glial activation likely contributes to the observed neurocognitive impairments, although the specific neurological disorder involved remains undefined at this stage.

Mice infected with Blastocystis sp. exhibit gene expression profiles consistent with neurocognitive disorders

Based on behavioural findings indicating neurocognitive dysfunction in Blastocystis sp.-infected mice, we performed transcriptomic analysis of the whole brain tissues from both infected and uninfected groups to further investigate the underlying molecular mechanisms and explore the impact of Blastocystis sp. infection on the CNS. Differential gene expression analysis identified a total of 436 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the infected and control groups, including 385 upregulated and 51 downregulated genes (Figure 3A). Kyoto Encyclopaedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis revealed that these DEGs were significantly enriched in pathways related to signal transduction (24%), the nervous system (4%), the immune system (21%), and neurodegenerative diseases (21%) (Figure 3B). Significantly, disease enrichment analysis revealed marked enrichment in neurodegenerative disease pathways. This observation, coupled with our prior serum transcriptomic findings showing enrichment for neurological disorder pathways (e.g., Alzheimer's and Parkinson's disease), indicates a potential connection between Blastocystis sp. infection and cognitive impairment-related neurological conditions (Figure 3C). Further analysis of neural-related DEGs revealed that NR4A1 exhibited the most substantial downregulation among all candidates (Figure 3D), indicating that it may play a critical role in Blastocystis sp.-induced neurodegeneration.

NR4A1 is a key factor mediating Blastocystis sp. infection-induced neurological symptoms in mice

To investigate the spatial distribution of NR4A1 within brain tissue, we performed IHC and IF staining. The results showed a marked reduction in NR4A1-positive cells in the infected group compared to controls (Figure 4B and C), supporting the observation that glial activation increases and NR4A1 expression decreases during Blastocystis sp. infection. These immunohistological findings are consistent with our earlier WB and qPCR results (Figure 4A), indicating that Blastocystis sp. infection may trigger inflammatory responses that activate glial cells and suppress NR4A1 expression, ultimately contributing to neurocognitive impairment (Figure 4).

Blastocystis sp. infection modulates NR4A1 expression via the IL-17-p38 MAPK signalling pathway

In a previous study by our group, transcriptomic analysis of mouse serum revealed a significant increase in IL-17 expression following Blastocystis sp. infection, suggesting that IL-17 may reach brain tissue via the circulatory system and activate inflammatory signalling pathways.53 IL-17 is a well-characterised pro-inflammatory cytokine known to play a pivotal role in host immune responses against microbial pathogens. Among the downstream effectors of IL-17, the p38 MAPK signalling pathway is a prototypical and critical mediator of inflammatory responses.54 In our current study, transcriptomic data indicated that NR4A1 expression was significantly downregulated in the brains of infected mice. Previous evidence suggests that NR4A1 is regulated by inflammatory signalling, particularly through the activation of MAPK family members such as p38 MAPK.55 Based on these findings, we propose that the downregulation of NR4A1 may be closely linked to IL-17-mediated activation of the p38 MAPK pathway, contributing to inflammation-driven neurocognitive impairment in Blastocystis sp.-infected mice.

To verify whether this pathway is indeed activated by Blastocystis sp. infection, we first measured the expression levels of IL-17 in both intestinal and brain tissues using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (Figure 5). The results showed that IL-17 levels were significantly elevated in the infected group compared to the control group in both the gut and brain (P < 0.0001; Figure 5A and B), indicating that Blastocystis sp. infection robustly induces IL-17-mediated inflammatory responses. We then performed WB analysis to assess the expression levels of IL-17, phosphorylated p38 (P-p38), total p38, and NR4A1 in brain tissue to evaluate the activation status of these key components within the pathway. The results showed that IL-17 and P-p38 levels were significantly elevated in the infected group compared to the control group, whereas total p38 protein expression remained unchanged between the two groups (Figure 5C, D and F). These findings indicate that Blastocystis sp. infection specifically induces phosphorylation of p38 MAPK without altering total protein levels. Moreover, NR4A1 protein levels were markedly reduced in the infected group relative to the control group (Figure 5E and F), supporting the hypothesis that NR4A1 downregulation is a downstream consequence of IL-17-mediated p38 MAPK activation.

p38 MAPK inhibition alleviates Blastocystis sp.-induced neuroinflammation by restoring NR4A1

To elucidate the role of the p38 MAPK-NR4A1 signalling axis in Blastocystis sp.-induced neuropathology, we administered the specific p38 inhibitor SB203580 in vivo and analysed key molecular and cellular markers. Mice were divided into four experimental groups: Control, Blastocystis sp.-infected, Infected + SB203580, and Control + SB203580. The Water Maze experiment revealed that the infected group had a significantly longer escape latency compared to the other three groups, indicating impaired spatial learning ability. When infected mice were treated with the SB203580 inhibitor, their escape latency was also shortened relative to the infected group, suggesting that the p38 inhibitor could ameliorate infection-induced cognitive dysfunction (Figure 6A). The number of platform crossings further showed that the p38 inhibitor partially restored spatial memory in infected mice (Figure 6B). Additionally, measurements of movement distance and time in the open arm further confirmed that the p38 inhibitor improved infection-related anxiety-like behaviours (Figure 6C–E).

Western blot and immunofluorescence analyses consistently demonstrated that SB203580 treatment in infected animals effectively suppressed p38 phosphorylation, reversed NR4A1 downregulation, and significantly attenuated the activation of both microglia and astrocytes. Notably, while SB203580 treatment restored NR4A1 expression and normalised glial cell activation, it did not significantly alter the infection-induced elevation in IL-17 levels. This key observation positions IL-17 upstream of p38 MAPK activation in the signalling cascade (Figure 7A and B​​​​​​). Furthermore, our subsequent immunohistochemical and immunofluorescence analyses further validated the above findings. As shown in Figure 7C, immunohistochemical results for NR4A1 and GFAP demonstrated that in the infected group, NR4A1 expression was reduced while GFAP-positive astrocytes were significantly increased. SB203580 treatment reversed these changes, restoring NR4A1 expression and normalising the number of GFAP-positive cells. Meanwhile, immunofluorescence analysis of IBA1 in Figure 7D similarly revealed that Blastocystis sp. infection induced marked activation of microglia (IBA1 + cells), which was significantly suppressed following SB203580 intervention. Together, these results confirm that the p38 MAPK-NR4A1 signalling pathway plays a critical role in the infection-mediated activation of neuroglia, and this effect operates independently of the upstream IL-17 signal.

In summary, our results demonstrate that Blastocystis sp. infection activates the IL-17–p38 MAPK–NR4A1 pathway in the brain; and downstream of IL-17, p38 MAPK serves as a critical regulator of NR4A1 expression and glial cell activation. These findings establish a direct mechanistic link between enteric protozoan infection and cerebral neuroinflammatory responses, highlighting the IL-17-p38 MAPK-NR4A1 as a central pathway mediating these effects.

In vitro validation of the IL-17-p38 MAPK-NR4A1 pathway

Given that our in vivo findings indicated significant glial cell activation and neuroinflammation in the brain of Blastocystis sp.-infected mice, BV2 microglial cell line was used to validate whether the IL-17-p38 MAPK-NR4A1 signalling axis is functional in microglial cells. To elucidate the causal relationship between IL-17 stimulation, p38 MAPK signalling, and NR4A1 expression, we set up four treatment groups: (1) Control, BV2 cells maintained under standard culture conditions without any treatment; (2) IL-17, BV2 cells stimulated with IL-17 alone to mimic elevated IL-17 levels following infection; (3) IL-17 + p38 MAPK inhibitor, BV2 cells co-treated with IL-17 and a p38 MAPK inhibitor to assess the necessity of p38 signalling downstream of IL-17; and (4) p38 MAPK activator, BV2 cells treated with a p38 MAPK activator to simulate direct activation of the pathway independent of IL-17. WB analysis showed that total p38 was expressed across all four groups, indicating that p38 protein abundance was not affected by the stimulation conditions. However, P-p38 levels were markedly elevated in both the IL-17 stimulation and p38 activator groups compared to the control and the IL-17 + p38 inhibitor groups. These results confirm that IL-17 effectively activates the p38 MAPK pathway and that this activation can be specifically blocked by a p38 inhibitor. In parallel, we examined the expression of NR4A1, a key downstream target of this pathway. NR4A1 protein levels were substantially higher in the control and IL-17 + p38 inhibitor groups compared to the IL-17 stimulation and p38 activator groups (Figure 8). These findings closely mirror our in vivo observations, in which Blastocystis sp. infection led to increased IL-17 expression, enhanced p38 MAPK phosphorylation, and reduced NR4A1 expression in mouse brain tissue. Collectively, these in vitro results indicate that IL-17 drives p38 MAPK phosphorylation and subsequent NR4A1 downregulation in microglial cells, establishing a potential relationship between IL-17 stimulation and activation of this signalling cascade. These in vitro findings, together with our in vivo observations that p38 inhibition ameliorated neurocognitive deficits in infected mice, suggest that this signalling cascade may plays a critical role in mediating neurocognitive pathology following Blastocystis sp. infection (Figure 8).

Discussion

Blastocystis sp. is a widely distributed opportunistic fungal pathogen that typically resides in the intestinal tract.56 Previous research has predominantly focused on its role in gastrointestinal disease and host immune responses.57 However, growing evidence has suggested a potential link between Blastocystis sp. infection and abnormalities in the host nervous system.58,59 Notably, infection with other microorganisms such as Toxoplasma gondii has been shown to induce cognitive deficits in mice, characterised by synaptic structural impairments and hippocampal neuroinflammation60‐65 Furthermore, the infection induced gut microbiota dysbiosis and colonic inflammation. The intricate link between gut dysbiosis and cognitive impairment is well-established, with specific pathogens including Candida albicans, Toxoplasma gondii, Streptococcus spp., and Clostridium perfringens all associated with poorer performance in cognitive assessments. These findings provide additional compelling evidence supporting the microbial-gut-brain axis hypothesis.66,67 In our previous study, transcriptomic analysis of serum from Blastocystis sp.-infected mice revealed significant enrichment of KEGG pathways related to behavioural and cognitive dysfunction, as well as inflammation. These findings suggest that Blastocystis sp. infection may indirectly impair CNS function through peripheral inflammatory responses, ultimately leading to cognitive deficits. Based on this hypothesis, we first evaluated the cognitive function of infected mice through a series of behavioural tests, including the MWM, OFT, EPM, and rotarod tests. These experiments confirmed that Blastocystis sp. infection results in marked impairments in memory and motor coordination and an increase in anxiety-like behaviour. To assess whether the observed behavioural deficits could be attributed to differences in motivation or task engagement, we also conducted a sucrose preference test. No significant differences in sucrose preference were observed between groups, indicating that the infected mice did not exhibit anhedonia-like behaviour or reduced reward-seeking motivation. Thus, the impairments detected in the MWM, OFT, EPM, and rotarod tasks were unlikely to be driven by generalised deficits in task engagement. The infection also led to the upregulation of Aβ, P-Tau, and HTT, which represent hallmark molecular phenotypes of behavioural and cognitive impairments such as Alzheimer's disease and Parkinson's syndrome. Notably, although the precise role of wild-type Htt upregulation in this context requires further investigation, it may represent a compensatory cellular stress response or be part of a broader disruption of neural homoeostasis. Our serum transcriptome analysis revealed significantly elevated IL-17 expression and concurrent activation of the inflammation-related p38 MAPK signalling pathway. To establish the causal relationship within the p38 MAPK-NR4A1 pathway, we administered the specific p38 inhibitor SB203580 via in vivo injection. The experimental results demonstrated that inhibitor treatment effectively reversed the downregulation of NR4A1. This finding directly confirms the crucial role of p38 MAPK activation in mediating NR4A1 suppression. Thus, the present study focused on whether IL-17, once elevated in the peripheral circulation, could activate the p38 MAPK pathway within the CNS and subsequently induce neurocognitive impairment.

Neuropsychiatric disorders represent a category of conditions characterised by cognitive deficits, anxiety, and other behavioural abnormalities [54]. In the present study, Blastocystis sp.-infected mice exhibited behavioural symptoms that closely resemble those observed in neuropsychiatric disorders, including memory impairment, increased anxiety, and motor dysfunction. Transcriptomic analysis further revealed that the altered signalling pathways in infected brain tissues were closely associated with neurocognitive disorders-related molecular signatures. An important methodological consideration is that our transcriptomic analysis was performed on whole-brain tissues. Although this strategy offers a global perspective on gene expression, it potentially overlooks specific changes in functionally distinct regions like the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex, areas fundamentally involved in cognitive processing. Previous studies have demonstrated that NR4A1, a nuclear receptor-type transcription factor, plays a crucial anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective role in the CNS.34 Downregulation of NR4A1 has been strongly associated with neuroinflammation and neurodegenerative diseases such as HD68 and is believed to contribute to neuronal dysfunction. However, it should be emphasised that the downregulation of NR4A1 may represent a downstream consequence of neuroinflammatory processes rather than a primary driver of pathology. NR4A1 is known to be sensitive to a variety of inflammatory signals,69‐71 and its suppression may thus represent a convergence point in the pathogenic cascade, ultimately leading to neuronal dysfunction. Future studies employing cell-specific knockout or overexpression of NR4A1 are warranted to definitively establish its causal role. Our transcriptomic data indicated robust enrichment of the p38 MAPK signalling pathway in the brain tissues of infected mice, accompanied by a significant downregulation of NR4A1. To determine whether the IL-17-p38 MAPK-NR4A1 axis plays a central role in Blastocystis sp.-induced neurocognitive dysfunction, we conducted comprehensive validation experiments in both in vivo (mouse brain) and in vitro (BV2 microglial cells) models. Consistent results from both systems confirmed that Blastocystis sp. infection leads to a significant elevation of IL-17 in the gut and brain, which in turn activates p38 MAPK signalling and ultimately results in the downregulation of NR4A1. While our findings establish an association between Blastocystis sp. infection, neurocognitive deficits, and alterations in the IL-17–p38 MAPK–NR4A1 signalling axis, alternative explanations must be considered. For instance, systemic inflammation induced by the infection could lead to generalised sickness behaviour, such as lethargy and reduced motivation, which might inherently impair performance in behavioural tests like the Morris water maze and open field test, rather than reflecting specific deficits in learning and memory. Additionally, the infection itself, as a chronic stressor, might independently provoke anxiety-like behaviours and cognitive dysfunction via activation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis. However, several observations suggest that the observed phenotypes are not solely attributable to generalised malaise or non-specific stress. Notably, our intervention with a p38 MAPK inhibitor specifically reversed NR4A1 downregulation and ameliorated behavioural deficits, indicating that a defined signalling pathway—rather than a general sickness state—plays a central role. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that potential contributions from systemic inflammation and stress cannot be entirely ruled out. Future studies should directly monitor acute-phase proteins or corticosterone levels during infection to better evaluate and distinguish the roles of these alternative mechanisms.

This study provides the first comprehensive evidence that Blastocystis sp. infection contributes to neurocognitive impairment through activation of the IL-17-p38 MAPK-NR4A1 signalling pathway. This mechanism not only explains the molecular cascade by which peripheral inflammatory cytokines such as IL-17 influence CNS function, but also highlights the critical role the IL-17-p38 MAPK-NR4A1 axis plays in mediating infection-induced neurodegeneration.

This study also has several limitations and unresolved questions that warrant further investigation.

First, our initial hypothesis proposed that IL-17 upregulation in the brain was triggered by peripheral inflammatory signals via the gut-brain axis following Blastocystis sp. infection. However, we later observed a significant increase in IL-17 protein expression within the cortical region of the brain, suggesting that IL-17 may also be locally activated. Given that Blastocystis sp. colonisation primarily occurs in the gut, the precise mechanism by which it might influence IL-17 expression within the brain—particularly how it may cross or signal through the blood-brain barrier—remains unclear. Second, the classification of Blastocystis sp. as a commensal or pathogenic organism remains controversial and appears to be subtype-dependent.13 In this study, we used the ST2 subtype, which has been suggested to exhibit pathogenic potential.56 However, whether other subtypes can similarly induce effects associated with other neurocognitive disorders, remains to be determined. Moreover, both microglia and astrocytes play critical roles in neuroinflammation. Under inflammatory conditions, microglia become activated and initiate inflammatory signalling cascades, resulting in the secretion of pro-inflammatory mediators. These mediators have been identified as key inducers of neurotoxic astrocyte phenotypes across various disease states.72 While our study has clearly established the involvement of microglia in the IL-17-p38 MAPK-NR4A1 pathway, the interactions between astrocytes and neurons in this context remain largely unexplored. It should be noted that this study utilised whole-brain tissue for transcriptomic sequencing. This approach enabled a comprehensive assessment of the impact of Blastocystis sp. infection on the overall transcriptional landscape of the brain and successfully identified core signalling pathways (such as the IL-17/p38 MAPK/NR4A1 axis) that may be commonly dysregulated across broad brain regions. However, this method also has inherent limitations: the use of whole-brain tissue may dilute localised or cell type-specific gene expression changes occurring in particularly susceptible brain areas (e.g., the striatum or subcortical regions). Consequently, while our findings reveal global molecular perturbations induced by the infection, they do not preclude the possibility of more pronounced or specific transcriptional responses in particular brain regions. Future studies employing RNA sequencing of specific brain areas could more precisely dissect the region-specific molecular pathology in this infection model. Finally, as this study was conducted primarily in animal models, the role of Blastocystis sp. infection in human neurocognitive disorders requires further clinical validation.

In conclusion, this study elucidates a molecular mechanism by which Blastocystis sp. infection induces neurocognitive impairment, highlighting the central role of the IL-17-p38 MAPK-NR4A1 signalling pathway (Figure 9). These findings provide new theoretical insight and a potential therapeutic target for neuroinflammation and cognitive dysfunction associated with Blastocystis sp. infection, offering important biomedical implications for future translational research.

This schematic illustrates the potential mechanisms by which Blastocystis sp. infection triggers neuroinflammation and cognitive-behavioural impairments via the gut-brain axis. Oral gavage establishes intestinal colonisation by Blastocystis sp., which may disrupt the intestinal epithelial barrier and activate gut immune responses through pathways involving enterochromaffin cells (EECs) among others. These processes promote Th17 cell differentiation and the release of inflammatory cytokines such as IL-17. Peripheral inflammatory signals are then conveyed to the central nervous system via vagal nerve transmission and/or circulatory routes, leading to the activation of the p38 MAPK signalling pathway and downregulation of NR4A1 expression in the brain. Ultimately, these molecular changes contribute to cortical neuroinflammation and neurocognitive abnormalities, including depression-like behaviours.

Conclusion

For a long time, the pathogenicity of Blastocystis sp. has been debated. Most of the research focuses on the intestinal inflammation caused by Blastocystis sp., but ignores whether it will affect brain function. We found that this common intestinal parasite can induce memory loss and dyskinesia in mice, showing symptoms similar to neurodegenerative diseases. Further transcriptome analysis showed that the down-regulation of the key protective protein NR4A1 was closely related to neurocognitive decline. Mechanistic studies have shown that Blastocystis sp. activates the IL-17-p38 MAPK-NR4A1 signalling pathway, drives inflammation in the brain and impairs neuronal function. This finding not only reveals a new way for parasites to affect the central nervous system, but also provides a potential intervention target for the prevention and treatment of infection-related neurodegenerative diseases.
