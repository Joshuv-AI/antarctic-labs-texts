Abstract
. We study the quantum dynamics of a large number of interacting fermionic particles in a constant magnetic field. In a coupled mean-field and semiclassical scaling limit, we show that solutions of the many-body Schrödinger equation converge to solutions of a non-linear Hartree–Fock equation. The central ingredient of the proof are certain semiclassical trace norm estimates of commutators of the position and momentum operators with the one-particle density matrix of the solution of the Hartree–Fock equation. In a first step, we prove their validity for non-interacting initial data in a magnetic field by generalizing a result by Fournais and Mikkelsen [ FM20b ] . We then propagate these bounds from the initial data along the Hartree–Fock flow to arbitrary times.



1.
Introduction

A wide variety of condensed matter systems can be quantum-mechanically described as gases of interacting fermionic particles. This includes ordinary metals, as well as semiconductors. Of particular interest is the description of fermions in two-dimensional materials subject to a homogeneous external magnetic field, due to the possible emergence of (fractional) quantization of the conductivity in the quantum Hall effect. The topological nature of this phenomenon reflects in its robustness against changes in the microscopic details of the model, a form of universality. Of particular relevance is the fact that the Hall conductivity stays quantized even in presence of weak interactions
[
GMP17
,
BBDRF18
,
MT19
,
Teu20
,
WMM+24
]
. Fractionalization of the Hall charge is believed to be a consequence of strong correlations between particles and continues to be a topic of active investigation, despite recent important progress in its mathematical understanding
[
BBDRF21
,
BCFO24
]
. To narrow the range of prerequisites for the emergence of the quantum Hall effect, we consider the widely studied mean-field scaling regime and prove that it gives rise only to weak correlations between particles, i.e., the time evolution of the system’s state remains well approximated by a Slater determinant evolving by a Hartree–Fock equation. Our proof adapts the many-body analysis in Fock space of
[
BPS14b
]
; this method, however, relies crucially on certain semiclassical properties of the solution of the Hartree–Fock equation, which turn out to be challenging to establish for anything but non-interacting fermions on the torus. At the core of our proof is therefore the task of establishing the semiclassical properties of non-interacting fermions in a magnetic field as initial data, followed by the propagation of these semiclassical properties to arbitrary times along the Hartree–Fock flow which includes both magnetic field and interaction. The proof of the semiclassical properties of the initial data generalizes the method of
[
FM20b
]
, whereas the propagation generalizes
[
BPS14b
]
.

To describe the interacting many-body evolution of the fermionic system, we introduce the

By

(Different fermionic scaling regimes, which are simpler in that the semiclassical scaling is not coupled to the mean-field scaling, have been studied by

In the non-interacting case

can be obtained as antisymmetric tensor products (

) of the eigenfunctions of

(1.4)

\Psi_{N}:=\bigwedge_{n=1}^{N}\psi_{n}\;,\qquad\Psi_{N}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N!}}\det\left(\psi_{i}(x_{j})\right)_{i,j=1,\ldots,N}\;,

is a normalized eigenvector of

are also called the

is a trace-class operator on the one-particle Hilbert space

defined (up to the normalization by

) as the partial trace over

, i. e.,

\gamma^{(1)}_{\Psi_{N}}:=N{\rm tr}_{N-1}\lvert\Psi_{N}\rangle\langle\Psi_{N}\rvert

or equivalently in terms of its integral kernel

\gamma^{(1)}_{\Psi_{N}}(x;y):=N\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{(N-1)}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{2}\ldots\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{N}\Psi_{N}(x,x_{2},\ldots,x_{N})\overline{\Psi_{N}(y,x_{2},\ldots,x_{N})}\;.

If

is the Slater determinant

1.4

is the Slater determinant obtainable from the spectral projection
\mathds{1}_{(-\infty,\mu]}({H_{\hbar,b}})
where the

General elements of
L^{2}_{\textnormal{a}}(\mathbb{R}^{dN})
can always be written as linear combinations of Slater determinants. The central principle of Hartree–Fock theory is to restrict attention to the submanifold of single Slater determinants, i. e., not admitting any linear combinations. The remaining degree of freedom is then the choice of the orbitals
\psi_{n}
. In time-independent Hartree–Fock theory, this gives rise to a variational problem over the orbitals of the expectation value of
H_{\hbar,b}^{N}
as an approximation to the ground state energy; in time-dependent Hartree–Fock theory one aims to find a trajectory in the submanifold of Slater determinants that stays as close as possible to the true many-body evolution in
L^{2}_{\textnormal{a}}(\mathbb{R}^{dN})
. Since Slater determinants can be identified with rank-
N
projections, time-dependent Hartree–Fock theory provides a (non-linear) equation for the evolution of a rank-
N
projection, the time-dependent Hartree–Fock equation

(1.5)

i\hbar\partial_{t}\omega_{N,t}=\Big[h_{\rm HF}(t),\omega_{N,t}\Big],\quad\omega_{N,t=0}=\omega_{N}\;.

The operator
h_{\rm HF}(t)=H_{\hbar,b}+W\ast\rho_{t}-X_{t}
is the Hartree–Fock Hamiltonian, which depends itself on the solution of the equation through the density
\rho_{t}(\mathbf{x})=N^{-1}\omega_{N,t}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x})
and the exchange operator, i. e., the operator
X_{t}
with the integral kernel
X_{t}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=N^{-1}\omega_{N,t}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})W(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})
for
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}
.

We are particularly interested in the description of a
quantum quench
: an initial Slater determinant is prepared for the non-interacting system with
W=0
, then, at time
t=0
, the interaction is abruptly switched on (
W\not=0
) and the system evolves by the many-body Schrödinger equation

(1.6)

i\hbar\partial_{t}\Psi_{N}(t)=H_{\hbar,b}^{N}\Psi_{N}(t)

Since the interaction in the mean-field scaling
Eq.

1.3
is weak, we can expect the time-dependent Hartree–Fock equation to provide a good approximation to the one-particle reduced density matrix
\gamma^{(1)}_{\Psi_{N}(t)}
obtained from the many-body Schrödinger evolution. This expectation is confirmed by
3
. Its proof depends crucially on the presence of a subtle semiclassical structure in the initial data, which we believe is of independent interest, also in view of the broader context discussed below in
Section

1.3

1
. The propagation of the semiclassical structure along the Hartree–Fock flow is also non-trivial; we discuss it in
2

1.2.
First Main Result: Semiclassical Structure of Spectral Projections

Let us recall how to define more formally the magnetic Schrödinger operator (
1.1
) and present the setting of our first main result.

Assumption 1.1
.

Let
a:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
V:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be such that

a_{j}\in L^{2}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for any

V_{+}\in L^{1}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
V_{-}\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})+L^{\infty}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with

(1.7)

p=1\ \text{ if }\ d=1\,,\qquad p>1\ \text{ if }\ d=2\,,\qquad p\geq\frac{d}{2}\ \text{ if }\ d\geq 3\,.

Moreover, we define
{H_{\hbar,b}}
as the Friedrichs extension of a minimal operator
H_{\hbar,b}^{\min}:u\mapsto(-i\hbar\nabla-ba(x))^{2}u+V(x)
of domain
{\rm Dom}(H_{\hbar,b}^{\min})=\mathcal{C}_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and associated to the domain

\displaystyle{\rm Dom}({H_{\hbar,b}}):=\{u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\>:

\displaystyle\>(-i\hbar\nabla-ba(x))u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\>\sqrt{V_{+}}u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

\displaystyle\>\text{and }(-i\hbar\nabla-ba(x))^{2}u+V(x)u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\}\;\;,

and for any
u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
(-i\hbar\nabla-ba(x))u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\>\sqrt{V_{+}}u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\overline{(i\hbar\nabla+ba(x))u(x)}(i\hbar\nabla+ba(x))v(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}V(x)\overline{u(x)}v(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\left\langle u,{H_{\hbar,b}}v\right\rangle.

The operator

is semibounded from below and is essentially self-adjoint.
When the potential is confining, i. e.,

[

Lew22
,
FLW23
]

]

Assumption 1.2
.

Let

,

, and

We prove that the spectral projector of the magnetic one-body operator (or equivalently, the one-body reduced density matrix of the Slater determinant obtained as the ground state of the corresponding non-interacting many-body system) satisfies commutators bounds, to which we refer as the

Theorem 1

(Semiclassical Structure of Initial Data)
.

Let

,

Remark 1.3
.

(1)

The assumptions on the regularity of

,

, and

are due to the method of our proof. As in

]
, the idea is to write, for

\displaystyle\qquad\leq{\left|\hskip-0.50003pt\left|[\mathds{1}_{(-\infty,\mu]}({H_{\hbar,b}}),R\chi(x)]\right|\hskip-0.50003pt\right|}_{1}\ +{\left|\hskip-0.50003pt\left|[\mathds{1}_{(-\infty,\mu]}({H_{\hbar,b}}),R(1-\chi(x))]\right|\hskip-0.50003pt\right|}_{1}.

for a well chosen bump function

supported on a bounded neighborhood of the set

We control the first summand by local commutator bounds (

and that

is finite for any

and the polynomial growth of

stated in

(2)

1.3.
Commutator Estimates: State of the Art and Applications

Estimates similar to the ones stated in
1
have been proved for spectral projections of standard Schrödinger operators with
f(x)=x_{j}
for any
j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
. This has been done for the harmonic oscillator
[
Ben22
, Section 3.1]
and
[
Laf24b
, Section 3]
, where the proof relies on explicit calculations with creation and annihilation operators. The general case has been treated in
[
FM20b
]
in presence of smooth confining potentials, and more recently in
[
CL25
]
for confining potentials of class

. We reproduce the spirit of the proof of
[
FM20b
]
that relies on the knowledge of the optimal Weyl law and multiscale techniques. The techniques presented in
[
CL25
]
rely on the Birman–Solomyak double integral approach.

As was mentioned above, this kind of bounds is motivated by the derivation of the Hartree–Fock equations for interacting fermions and usually constitute the assumption on the initial data for these equations (see
Section

1.4
); many recent results have been achieved in this respect for Schrödinger operators in the non-relativistic case
[
BPS14b
,
BJP+16
]
, in the relativistic case
[
BPS14a
]
, and in large-volume limits
[
FPS23
,
FPS24
]
. One of the main steps is the propagation of the bound
Eq.

1.9
for
f(x)=\frac{1}{1+\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert}e^{i\alpha\cdot x}
uniformly with respect to
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Our estimates can be useful to study the same question in the magnetic case for sufficiently regular interaction potentials
W
(compare (
1.12
)).

Let us now mention other natural settings where this type of estimate appears, although
it is not possible to directly deduce applications of our results. Indeed, they require less
regularity on the function
f
or that require replacing the trace norm by more general Schatten

We recall that the

of an operator

to a function on phase space is defined by

\mathcal{W}_{\gamma}:(x,\xi)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}y\cdot\xi}\gamma\Big(x+\frac{y}{2},x-\frac{y}{2}\Big)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\,,

where
\gamma(x,y)
denotes the integral kernel of

(provided it exists). One observes that

(1.11)

\mathcal{W}_{[x,\gamma]}=-i\hbar\nabla_{\xi}\mathcal{W}_{\gamma}\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathcal{W}_{[-i\hbar\nabla,\gamma]}=-i\hbar\nabla_{x}\mathcal{W}_{\gamma}\;,

which relates the commutator estimates
[\mathds{1}_{(-\infty,\mu}({H_{\hbar,b}}),x]
and
[\mathds{1}_{(-\infty,\mu]}({H_{\hbar,b}}),P]
to the regularity of the Wigner transform
[
CL25
,
Laf24b
]
. Estimates for norms of the gradients

Furthermore, bounds on Schatten norms of commutators of spectral projectors

{\left|\hskip-0.50003pt\left|[\mathds{1}_{(-\infty,\mu]}({H_{\hbar,b}}),\mathds{1}_{\Omega}]\right|\hskip-0.50003pt\right|}_{\rm HS}^{2}={\rm tr}(g(\mathds{1}_{(-\infty,\mu]}({H_{\hbar,b}})\mathds{1}_{\Omega}\mathds{1}_{(-\infty,\mu]}({H_{\hbar,b}})))\;.

In particular, when

[

]
from 1982, and have been related in
[

]
to the entanglement entropy of the non-interacting Fermi gas.
In the context of entanglement entropy, the asymptotics in the limit

of the trace of operators

are still a subject of active investigation for the non-interacting Fermi gas in various settings: for rectangular domains
\Omega
in
[

]
, for analytic

in
[
Sob13
]
, in a magnetic field without trapping potential
[

,
LSS21
,
PS24

[

]
for continuous

1.4.
Second Main Result: Validity of the Hartree–Fock Evolution

Remark 1.5
.

The trace norm of the one-particle density matrix is conserved both by the many-body Schrödinger evolution and the Hartree–Fock evolution. Thus by
2.21
for the initial data, we conclude that the density matrices separately are of order

(1.16)

\lVert\omega_{N,t}\rVert_{\rm tr}\sim\hbar^{-d}\;,\qquad\lVert\gamma^{(1)}_{\Psi_{N}(t)}\rVert_{\rm tr}\sim\hbar^{-d}\;.

Since

is a projection operator, by Hölder’s inequality for Schatten norms, we obtain that the Hilbert–Schmidt norm can be trivially estimated by

(1.17)

{\left|\hskip-0.50003pt\left|\omega_{N,t}\right|\hskip-0.50003pt\right|}_{\rm HS}=\sqrt{{\rm tr}(\omega_{N,t}^{*}\omega_{N,t})}\leq\sqrt{\lVert\omega_{N,t}\rVert_{\rm op}\lVert\omega_{N,t}\rVert_{\rm tr}}\leq\hbar^{-d/2}\;,

Thus

shows that the trace norm estimate gains a factor

as

1.16

over

1.17
. In particular, our estimates are significantly stronger than those obtained earlier in

]
for fermions in a magnetic field in two dimensions: the bounds proved there would correspond in our notation to an estimate of the form

.

Lemma 2.18

(Agmon formula)
.

Let
{H_{\hbar,b}}=(-i\hbar\nabla-ba)^{2}+V
be a magnetic Schrödinger operator acting on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
where
b\geq 0
,
a\in L^{2}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d})
and where
V\in L^{1}_{{\rm loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Let
\psi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be an eigenfunction of
{H_{\hbar,b}}
associated to the eigenvalue
E\in\mathbb{R}
. Then, for any Lipschitz function
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
and any smooth function
\chi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\chi e^{\phi}\psi\in{\rm Dom}({\mathfrak{q}_{\hbar,b}})
we have

{\mathfrak{q}_{\hbar,b}}(\chi e^{\phi}\psi)=E\left\lVert\chi e^{\phi}\psi\right\rVert_{L^{2}{(\mathbb{R}^{d})}}^{2}+\hbar^{2}\left\lVert e^{\phi}\psi(\nabla\chi+\chi\nabla\phi)\right\rVert_{L^{2}{(\mathbb{R}^{d})}}^{2}\;.

Here the quadratic
({\mathfrak{q}_{\hbar,b}},{\rm Dom}({\mathfrak{q}_{\hbar,b}}))
is defined in
Appendix

A
.

Proof.

Let
\phi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
{H_{\hbar,b}}\psi=E\psi
. We have

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{q}_{\hbar,b}}(\chi e^{\phi}\psi)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\lvert(i\hbar\nabla+ba(x))(\chi e^{\phi}\psi)(x)\right\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}V(x)\left\lvert(\chi e^{\phi}\psi)(x)\right\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\lvert(e^{\phi}\chi)(i\hbar\nabla+ba(x))\psi+i\hbar e^{\phi}\psi(\nabla\chi+\chi\nabla\phi)\right\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}V(x)\left\lvert(\chi e^{\phi}\psi)(x)\right\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(e^{\phi}\chi)^{2}\left\lvert(i\hbar\nabla+ba(x))\psi\right\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}V(x)\left\lvert(\chi e^{\phi}\psi)(x)\right\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\qquad+2{\rm Re}\,\Big(\left\langle(e^{\phi}\chi)(i\hbar\nabla+ba(x))\psi,i\hbar e^{\phi}\psi(\nabla\chi+\chi\nabla\phi)\right\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad+\hbar^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\lvert e^{\phi}\psi(\nabla\chi+\chi\nabla\phi)\right\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\;.

Let us write the first term

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(e^{\phi}\chi)^{2}

\displaystyle\left\lvert(i\hbar\nabla+ba(x))\psi\right\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}V(x)\left\lvert(\chi e^{\phi}\psi)(x)\right\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\left\langle(i\hbar\nabla+ba(x))\psi,\chi^{2}e^{2\phi}(i\hbar\nabla+ba(x))\psi\right\rangle+\left\langle V\psi,\chi^{2}e^{2\phi}\psi\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle(i\hbar\nabla+ba(x))\psi,(i\hbar\nabla+ba(x))(\chi^{2}e^{2\phi}\psi)\right\rangle+\left\langle V\psi,\chi^{2}e^{2\phi}\psi\right\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad-\left\langle(i\hbar\nabla+ba(x))\psi,2i\hbar\chi e^{2\phi}(\nabla\chi+\chi\nabla\phi)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle(i\hbar\nabla+ba(x))\psi,(i\hbar\nabla+ba(x))(\chi^{2}e^{2\phi}\psi)\right\rangle+\left\langle V\psi,\chi^{2}e^{2\phi}\psi\right\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad-2\left\langle(e^{\phi}\chi)(i\hbar\nabla+ba(x))\psi,i\hbar e^{\phi}(\nabla\chi+\chi\nabla\phi)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=E\left\langle\psi,\chi^{2}e^{2\phi}\psi\right\rangle-2\left\langle(e^{\phi}\chi)(i\hbar\nabla+ba(x))\psi,i\hbar e^{\phi}(\nabla\chi+\chi\nabla\phi)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=E\left\lVert\chi e^{\phi}\psi\right\rVert_{L^{2}{(\mathbb{R}^{d})}}^{2}-2\left\langle(e^{\phi}\chi)(i\hbar\nabla+ba(x))\psi,i\hbar e^{\phi}(\nabla\chi+\chi\nabla\phi)\right\rangle\;.

We remember that it is real, then

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(e^{\phi}\chi)^{2}

\displaystyle\left\lvert(i\hbar\nabla+ba(x))\psi\right\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}V(x)\left\lvert(\chi e^{\phi}\psi)(x)\right\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=E\left\lVert\chi e^{\phi}\psi\right\rVert_{L^{2}{(\mathbb{R}^{d})}}^{2}-2{\rm Re}\,\left\langle(e^{\phi}\chi)(i\hbar\nabla+ba(x))\psi,i\hbar e^{\phi}(\nabla\chi+\chi\nabla\phi)\right\rangle\;.

Hence, we obtain the desired inequality

{\mathfrak{q}_{\hbar,b}}(\chi e^{\phi}\psi)=E\left\lVert\chi e^{\phi}\psi\right\rVert_{L^{2}{(\mathbb{R}^{d})}}^{2}+\hbar^{2}\left\lVert e^{\phi}\psi(\nabla\chi+\chi\nabla\phi)\right\rVert_{L^{2}{(\mathbb{R}^{d})}}^{2}\;.\qed

Lemma 2.19

(Agmon estimates)
.

(2.32)

\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\>:\>V(x)-\mu<\varepsilon\}\subset U.

Let

\tilde{U}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\>:\>{\rm dist}(x,U)<1\}\quad\text{ and }\quad\phi_{\varepsilon}:x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\varepsilon\,{\rm dist}(x,\tilde{U})\;.

For the sake of completeness, we provide a proof of these estimates.

Proof of
Lemma

2.19
.

Let
E<\varepsilon/4
and
\psi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be a normalized eigenfunction of
{H_{\hbar,b}}
such that
{H_{\hbar,b}}\psi=E\psi
. Let
\chi\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d},[0,1])

\chi=1
on
\Omega_{\varepsilon}^{c}
and such that
{\rm supp}(\chi)\subset U^{c}
. By construction,
{\rm supp}(\left\lvert\nabla\chi\right\rvert)\subset\tilde{U}\setminus U
.
For
\gamma\in(0,1]
, we define the function

\displaystyle\phi_{\varepsilon,\gamma}:x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\frac{\phi_{\varepsilon}(x)}{1+\gamma\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{2}}\;.

By construction,
\phi_{\varepsilon,\gamma}
is bounded and almost everywhere differentiable in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Moreover, for almost all

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}\phi_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}(x)\nabla_{x}{\rm dist}(x,U)\;,

and

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}\phi_{\varepsilon,\gamma}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+\gamma\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{2}}\nabla_{x}\phi_{\varepsilon}-\frac{2\gamma\phi_{\varepsilon}(x)}{(1+\gamma\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{2})^{2}}x=\frac{1}{1+\gamma\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{2}}\nabla_{x}\phi_{\varepsilon}-\phi_{\varepsilon,\gamma}(x)\frac{2\gamma}{1+\gamma\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{2}}x\;.

Up to a translation, let us assume that
0\in U
so that
{\rm dist}(x,U)\leq\left\lvert x\right\rvert
for any

By the triangle inequality, one has

\displaystyle\left\lVert e^{\phi_{\varepsilon,\gamma}/\hbar}\right\rVert_{L^{2}{(\mathbb{R}^{d})}}\leq 1+\left\lVert\chi e^{\phi_{\varepsilon,\gamma}/\hbar}\right\rVert_{L^{2}{(\mathbb{R}^{d})}}\;.

On the one hand, by

2.32

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{q}_{\hbar,b}}(\chi e^{\phi_{\varepsilon,\gamma}/\hbar}\psi,\chi e^{\phi_{\varepsilon,\gamma}/\hbar}\psi)

On the other hand, given the supports of
\nabla\chi
,
\nabla\phi_{\varepsilon,\gamma}
and
\phi_{\varepsilon,\gamma}

\displaystyle\hbar^{2}\left\lVert\nabla(\chi e^{\phi_{\varepsilon,\gamma}/\hbar})\psi\right\rVert_{L^{2}{(\mathbb{R}^{d})}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\Big(\left\lVert(\chi e^{\phi_{\varepsilon,\gamma}/\hbar}\psi)\nabla\phi_{\varepsilon,\gamma}\right\rVert_{L^{2}{(\mathbb{R}^{d})}}^{2}+\hbar^{2}\left\lVert(e^{\phi_{\varepsilon,\gamma}/\hbar}\psi)\nabla\chi\right\rVert_{L^{2}{(\mathbb{R}^{d})}}^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(4\varepsilon^{2}+\left\lVert\nabla\chi\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}{(\Omega_{\varepsilon}\setminus U)}}^{2}\right)\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert_{L^{2}{(\Omega_{\varepsilon}\setminus U)}}^{2}

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}-E\Big)\left\lVert\chi e^{\phi_{\varepsilon,\gamma}/\hbar}\psi\right\rVert_{L^{2}{(\mathbb{R}^{d})}}^{2}\leq 2\left(4\varepsilon^{2}+\left\lvert\tilde{U}\setminus U\right\rvert^{2}\right)\;.

which is uniform with respect to

4.
Derivation of the Time-Dependent Hartree–Fock Equation (
3
)

Sketch of the Proof of

.

We follow the strategy of
[
BPS14b
]
with some simplification due to
[
BD23
]
. The first step is lifting the many-body evolution to Fock space, so that the tools of second quantization can be employed. The fermionic Fock space is defined as

\mathcal{F}:=\mathbb{C}\oplus\bigoplus_{n=1}^{\infty}L^{2}_{\textnormal{a}}(\mathbb{R}^{dN})\;.

Elements of the Fock space are sequences
\Psi=(\Psi^{(n)})_{n=0}^{\infty}
with
\Psi^{(n)}\in L^{2}_{\textnormal{a}}(\mathbb{R}^{dN})
such that

\lVert\Psi\rVert^{2}:=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\lVert\Psi^{(n)}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{dn})}<\infty\;.

On Fock space, the annihilation operators
a_{x}
for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
can be introduced as densely defined operators by

(a_{x}\psi)^{(n)}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}):=\sqrt{n+1}\psi^{(n+1)}(x,x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\;.

Moreover, for a one-particle wave function
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, one defines the corresponding annihilation and creation operators, respectively, by

a(f):=\int\overline{f(x)}a_{x}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\;,\qquad a^{*}(f):=a(f)^{*}\;.

One may also introduce
a^{*}_{x}
, but this is only defined within inner products where it can be moved to the other argument as
a_{x}
, or when integrated with a one-particle wave function
f
. It is well-known that in the fermionic case
a(f)
and
a^{*}(f)
are bounded operators, and satisfy the canonical anticommutator relations (CAR), i. e., for
f,g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we have

\{a(f),a^{*}(g)\}:=a(f)a^{*}(g)+a^{*}(g)a(f)=\langle f,g\rangle\;,\quad\{a(f),a(g)\}=0\;,\quad\{a^{*}(f),a^{*}(g)\}=0\;.

If
O
is an operator on the one-particle space
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, one defines

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Gamma(O)\psi^{(n)}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}O_{i}\psi^{(n)}\;,

where
O_{i}
acts on the
i
-th particle. If
O
has a (distributional) integral kernel
O(x;y)
one can verify that as a sesquilinear form

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Gamma(O):=\int O(x;y)a^{*}_{x}a_{y}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\;.

An example is the particle number operator as the second quantization of the identity

\mathcal{N}=\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Gamma(\mathds{1})=\int a^{*}_{x}a_{x}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\;.

The vector
\Omega:=(1,0,0,\ldots)\in\mathcal{F}
is called the vacuum vector; on this special vector one defines
a_{x}\Omega=0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In particular it has vanishing particle number in the sense that
\mathcal{N}\Omega=0
.

The first important observation for us is that, when restricted to the subspace
L^{2}_{\textnormal{a}}(\mathbb{R}^{dN})
of Fock space, the operator

\mathcal{H}_{\hbar,b}:=\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Gamma(H_{\hbar,b})+\frac{\lambda}{2}\int W(x-y)a^{*}_{x}a^{*}_{y}a_{y}a_{x}

is identical to the operator
H^{N}_{\hbar,b}
defined in
Eq.

1.2
; moreover the
N
-particle subspace
L^{2}_{\textnormal{a}}(\mathbb{R}^{dN})
is invariant under the action of
\mathcal{H}_{\hbar,b}
. Therefore, if we consider initial data
\Psi_{N}(0)
in the
N
-particle subspace, the solution
\Psi_{N}(t):=e^{-it\mathcal{H}_{\hbar,b}/\hbar}\Psi_{N}(0)
of the Fock-space Schrödinger equation
i\hbar\partial_{t}\Psi_{N}(t)=\mathcal{H}_{\hbar,b}\Psi_{N}(t)
agrees with the solution of the
N
-body Schrödinger equation
Eq.

1.6
.

The second important observation is that Slater determinants can be written as the application of a unitary transformation in Fock space to the vacuum vector
\Omega
. In fact, if
\Psi_{N}(0)
has the orbitals
\psi_{n}
, for
n=1,\ldots,N
, we can define the unitary particle–hole transformation
R_{N}(0):\mathcal{F}\to\mathcal{F}
by

R_{N}(0):=\prod_{n=1}^{N}\left(a^{*}(\psi_{n})+a(\psi_{n})\right)\;.

One easily verifies that, up to a sign that can be absorbed in the ordering of the factors in the definition, we have

R_{N}(0)\Omega=\prod_{n=1}^{N}a^{*}(\psi_{n})\Omega=\Psi_{N}(0)\;.

It is convenient to define the operators

V_{N}:=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\lvert\psi_{n}\rangle\langle\overline{\psi_{n}}\rvert\;,\qquad U_{N}:=\mathds{1}-\sum_{n=1}^{N}\lvert\psi_{n}\rangle\langle\psi_{n}\rvert

(notice that
U_{N}
is a projection while
V_{N}
contains an additional complex conjugation).
One can then show that

R^{*}_{N}a_{x}R_{N}=(-1)^{N}(a(U_{N,x})-a^{*}(V_{N,x}))\;,\qquad R^{*}_{N}a^{*}_{x}R_{N}=(-1)^{N}((a^{*}(U_{N,x})-a(V_{N,x}))\;,

where in terms of their (distributional) integral kernels we wrote
U_{N,x}(y)=U_{N}(y;x)
and
V_{N,x}(y)=V_{N}(y;x)
. Analogously
R_{N}(t)
is defined as the particle-hole transformation such that
R_{N}(t)\Omega
is the Slater determinant associated to the orbitals obtainable from the spectral decomposition of the solution of the time-dependent Hartree–Fock equation
Eq.

1.5
. (The spectral decomposition is not unique, giving rise to a phase ambiguity of the Slater determinant, which may even be non-differentiable. It is well-known that this ambiguity can be resolved by decomposing the initial data to obtain initial orbitals and then evolving these orbitals by an orbital-wise Hartree–Fock equation
[
Lub08
]
.) In the same way, employing the orbitals of the solution of the Hartree–Fock equation, one defines
U_{N,t}
,
V_{N,t}
, and the corresponding

,

The advantage of having introduced the Fock space formalism and in particular the particle-hole transformation is that we can now introduce the so-called fluctuation dynamics tracking the deviation between many-body and Hartree–Fock evolution by

\mathcal{U}_{N}(t,s):=R^{*}_{N}(t)e^{-i(t-s)\mathcal{H}_{\hbar,b}/\hbar}R_{N}(s)\;.

By the method of
[
BD23
]
it is straightforward to compute the generator of fluctuations

\mathcal{L}_{N}(t):=(i\hbar\partial_{t}R^{*}_{N}(t))R_{N}(t)+R^{*}_{N}(t)\mathcal{H}_{\hbar,b}R_{N}(t)\;.

More precisely, one is interested in computing
\mathcal{L}_{N}(t)
as a normal-ordered expression, i.e.  one use the CAR to write all its summands with all creation operators to the left of all annihilation operators. Moreover, in view of the proof below it is sufficient to compute only the terms of
\mathcal{L}_{N}(t)
that do not commute with the particle number operator
\mathcal{N}
. The obtained summands of
\mathcal{L}_{N}(t)
can be grouped into two categories, quadratic in creation or annihilation operators, and quartic in creation or annihilation operators. The key observation is that all the quadratic terms cancel out if the Hartree–Fock equation is inserted in the contribution of
(i\hbar\partial_{t}R^{*}_{N}(t))R_{N}(t)
, independent of the choice of the one-particle operator (this was already observed in
[
BPS14a
]
in the context of fermions with pseudo-relativistic kinetic energy). One is left with

(4.1)

\mathcal{L}_{N}(t)=\mathcal{A}_{N}(t)+\mathcal{B}_{N}(t)+\mathcal{C}_{N}(t)+\mathcal{M}_{N}(t)+\textnormal{h.c.}

where

(4.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{N}(t)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2N}\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}yW(x-y)a^{*}(U_{N,t,x})a^{*}(U_{N,t,y})a^{*}(V_{N,t,y})a^{*}(V_{N,t,x})\;,

(4.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{N}(t)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{N}\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}yW(x-y)a^{*}(U_{N,t,x})a^{*}(U_{N,t,y})a^{*}(V_{N,t,x})a(U_{N,t,y})\;,

(4.4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{N}(t)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{N}\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}yW(x-y)a^{*}(U_{N,t,x})a^{*}(V_{N,t,x})a^{*}(V_{N,t,y})a(V_{N,t,y})\;.

and
\mathcal{M}_{N}(t)
is commutes with the particle number operator,
[\mathcal{M}_{N}(t),\mathcal{N}]=0

To estimate the difference of
\gamma^{(1)}_{\Psi_{N}(t)}
and
\omega_{N,t}
in terms of the fluctuation dynamics, one uses the fact that the one-particle reduced density matrix can also be computed as

\gamma^{(1)}_{\Psi_{N}(t)}(x;y)=\langle\Psi_{N}(t),a^{*}_{y}a_{x}\Psi_{N}(t)\rangle

and then uses the unitarity of the particle-hole transformation to obtain

(4.5)

\begin{split}\gamma^{(1)}_{\Psi_{N}(t)}(x;y)&=\langle R^{*}_{N}(t)e^{-it\mathcal{H}_{\hbar,b}/\hbar}R_{N}(0)\Omega,R^{*}_{N}(t)a^{*}_{y}a_{x}R_{N}(t)R^{*}_{N}(t)e^{-it\mathcal{H}_{\hbar,b}/\hbar}R_{N}(0)\Omega\rangle\\
&=\langle\mathcal{U}_{N}(t,0)\Omega,\Big(a^{*}(U_{N,t,y})a(U_{N,t,x})-a^{*}(\overline{V_{N,t,x}})a(\overline{V_{N,t,y}})+\langle\overline{V_{N,t,y}},\overline{V_{N,t,x}}\rangle\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\ \ +a^{*}(U_{N,t,y,})a^{*}(\overline{V_{N,t,x}})+a(\overline{V_{N,t,y}})a(U_{N,t,x})\Big)\mathcal{U}_{N}(t,0)\Omega\rangle\;.\end{split}

Here one observes that

, the solution of the Hartree–Fock equation, which can be moved to the left side, leaving the remaining four terms, quadratic in creation or annihilation operators, to be estimated.

The Hilbert–Schmidt norm of the difference can be obtained by pairing

with the integral kernel of a Hilbert–Schmidt operator

The estimates to obtain the trace norm have also been shown in

is obtained by pairing with a trace-class operator

and taking the supremum over all such operators. The pairing is explicitly given by

(4.6)

where

I

\displaystyle:=\langle\Omega,\mathcal{U}^{*}_{N}(t,0)\left(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Gamma(U_{N,t}OU_{N,t})-\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Gamma(\overline{V_{N,t}}\overline{O^{*}}V_{N,t})\right)\mathcal{U}_{N}(t,0)\Omega\rangle

\displaystyle:=2{\rm Re}\,\langle\Omega,U^{*}_{N}(t,0)\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r_{1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r_{2}(V_{N,t}OU_{N,t})(r_{1};r_{2})a_{r_{1}}a_{r_{2}}\mathcal{U}_{N}(t,0)\Omega\rangle\;.

The first line is easily estimated using the standard bound
\lvert\langle\Psi,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Gamma(A)\Psi\rangle\rvert\leq\lVert A\rVert_{\rm op}\langle\Psi,\mathcal{N}\Psi\rangle
for any
\Psi\in\mathcal{F}
and any bounded operator
A
acting on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, followed by
\lVert U_{N,t}\rVert=1
and
\lVert V_{N,t}\rVert=1
:

(4.7)

\lvert\textnormal{I}\rvert\leq C\lVert O\rVert\langle\Omega,\mathcal{U}^{*}_{N}(t,0)\mathcal{N}\mathcal{U}_{N}(t)\Omega\rangle\;.

The estimate of the second term is a little more involved if we want to get the optimal bound. We define an auxiliary generator by dropping the
\mathcal{A}_{N}(t)
–term from
Eq.

4.1
, i. e.,

(4.8)

\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{N}(t):=\mathcal{B}_{N}(t)+\mathcal{C}_{N}(t)+\mathcal{M}_{N}(t)+\textnormal{h.c.}

and we define
\mathcal{U}^{(0)}_{N}(t,s)
as the evolution generated by
\mathcal{U}^{*}_{N}(t,0)\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{N}(t)\mathcal{U}_{N}(t,0)
, more explicitly the evolution solving the initial value problem

i\hbar\partial_{t}\mathcal{U}^{(0)}_{N}(t,s)=-\mathcal{U}^{*}_{N}(t,0)\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{N}(t)\mathcal{U}_{N}(t,0)\mathcal{U}^{(0)}_{N}(t,s)\;,\qquad\mathcal{U}^{(0)}_{N}(s,s)=\mathds{1}\;.

Next we define

\mathcal{U}^{(1)}_{N}(t,s):=\mathcal{U}_{N}(t,s)\mathcal{U}^{(0)}_{N}(t,s)

and verify that its generator is

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}^{(1)}_{N}(t)&=\mathcal{L}_{N}(t)-\mathcal{L}^{(0)}_{N}(t)=\mathcal{A}_{N}(t)+\textnormal{h.c.}\\
&=\frac{1}{2N}\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}yW(x-y)a^{*}(U_{N,t,x})a^{*}(U_{N,t,y})a^{*}(V_{N,t,y})a^{*}(V_{N,t,x})+\textnormal{h.c.}\end{split}

Note that
\mathcal{L}^{(1)}_{N}(t)
creates or annihilates four particles at a time, and therefore
[\mathcal{L}^{(1)}_{N}(t),i^{\mathcal{N}}]=0
. By Duhamel’s formula one concludes that
\mathcal{U}^{(1)}_{N}(t,0)i^{\mathcal{N}}\mathcal{U}^{(1)}_{N}(t,0)=i^{\mathcal{N}}
. Next, one expands

(4.9)

II

\displaystyle=2{\rm Re}\,\left(\textnormal{II}_{1}+\textnormal{II}_{2}+\textnormal{II}_{3}\right)

with

(4.10)

\displaystyle\textnormal{II}_{1}

\displaystyle:=\langle\Omega,\mathcal{U}^{(1)*}_{N}(t,0)\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r_{1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r_{2}(V_{N,t}OU_{N,t})(r_{1};r_{2})a_{r_{1}}a_{r_{2}}\mathcal{U}^{(1)}_{N}(t,0)\Omega\rangle\;,

(4.11)

\displaystyle\textnormal{II}_{2}

\displaystyle:=\langle\Omega,\left(\mathcal{U}^{*}_{N}(t,0)-U^{(1)*}_{N}(t,0)\right)\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r_{1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r_{2}(V_{N,t}OU_{N,t})(r_{1};r_{2})a_{r_{1}}a_{r_{2}}\mathcal{U}^{(1)}_{N}(t,0)\Omega\rangle\;,

(4.12)

\displaystyle\textnormal{II}_{3}

\displaystyle:=\langle\Omega,\mathcal{U}^{*}_{N}(t,0)\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r_{1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r_{2}(V_{N,t}OU_{N,t})(r_{1};r_{2})a_{r_{1}}a_{r_{2}}\left(\mathcal{U}_{N}(t,0)-\mathcal{U}^{(1)}_{N}(t,0)\right)\Omega\rangle\;.

The key point of this expansion is the observation that
\textnormal{II}_{1}=0
; this can be seen by inserting
1=i^{0}=i^{\mathcal{N}}
before the vacuum vector in the second argument of the inner product, commuting it through
\mathcal{U}^{(1)}_{N}(t,0)
, pulling it to the left of the operators
a_{r_{1}}a_{r_{2}}
where it becomes
i^{\mathcal{N}+2}
, commuting it through
\mathcal{U}^{(1)*}_{N}(t,0)
, and having it act on the vacuum vector in the first argument of the inner product as
i^{2}=-1
, so that
\textnormal{II}_{1}=-\textnormal{II}_{1}
.

It remains to estimate
\textnormal{II}_{2}
and
\textnormal{II}_{3}
; both work analogously, so we explain the estimate only for
\textnormal{II}_{2}
. We expand
\mathcal{U}_{N}(t,0)
by the Duhamel formula as

\mathcal{U}_{N}(t,0)-\mathcal{U}^{(1)}_{N}(t,0)=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\mathcal{U}(t,s)\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{N}(s)\mathcal{U}^{(1)}_{N}(s,0)

and insert this formula in
II
to get

\displaystyle\textnormal{II}_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{\hbar}\left\lvert\left\langle\Omega,\int_{0}^{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\mathcal{U}^{(1)*}_{N}(s,0)(\mathcal{B}_{N}(t)+\textnormal{h.c.})\mathcal{U}^{*}_{N}(t,s)\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r_{1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r_{2}(V_{N,t}OU_{N,t})(r_{1};r_{2})a_{r_{1}}a_{r_{2}}\mathcal{U}^{(1)}_{N}(t,0)\Omega\right\rangle\right\rvert

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{4}{\hbar}\left\lvert\left\langle\Omega,\int_{0}^{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\mathcal{U}^{(1)*}_{N}(s,0)(\mathcal{C}_{N}(t)+\textnormal{h.c.})\mathcal{U}^{*}_{N}(t,s)\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r_{1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r_{2}(V_{N,t}OU_{N,t})(r_{1};r_{2})a_{r_{1}}a_{r_{2}}\mathcal{U}^{(1)}_{N}(t,0)\Omega\right\rangle\right\rvert\;.

The estimate for the term with
\mathcal{C}_{N}(t)
can be obtained similarly to the estimate for the term containing
\mathcal{B}_{N}(t)
, which we shall now discuss. By expanding the potential into Fourier modes,

, we can write

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{N}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}yW(x-y)a^{*}(U_{N,t,x})a^{*}(U_{N,t,y})a^{*}(V_{N,t,x})a(U_{N,t,y})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}p\hat{W}(p)\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}xa^{*}(U_{N,t,x})e^{ip\cdot x}a^{*}(V_{N,t,x})\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}ya^{*}(U_{N,t,y})e^{ip\cdot y}a(U_{N,t,y})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}p\hat{W}(p)\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r_{1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r_{2}(U_{N,t}e^{ip\cdot x}V_{N,t})(r_{1},r_{2})a^{*}_{r_{1}}a^{*}_{r_{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Gamma\left(U_{N,t}e^{ip\cdot x}\overline{U_{N,t}}\right)\;,

where on the last line
x
is to be read as a multiplication operator. Using the Cauchy–Schwartz inequality and the well-known estimates
[
BPS14b
, Lemma 3.1]
for
\Psi\in\mathcal{F}
and
A
a bounded or Hilbert–Schmidt operator

\displaystyle\lVert\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Gamma(A)\Psi\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\lVert A\rVert_{\rm op}\lVert\mathcal{N}\Psi\rVert

\displaystyle\lVert\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r_{1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r_{2}A(r_{1};r_{2})a_{r_{1}}a_{r_{2}}\Psi\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\lVert A\rVert_{\rm HS}\lVert\mathcal{N}^{1/2}\Psi\rVert

one obtains that

II

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{\hbar N}\int_{0}^{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}p\lvert\hat{W}(p)\rvert\lVert U_{N,t}e^{ip\cdot x}\overline{U_{N,t}}\rVert_{\rm op}\lVert U_{N,t}e^{ip\cdot x}V_{N,t}\rVert_{\rm HS}\lVert V_{N,t}OU_{N,t}\rVert_{\rm HS}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\langle\mathcal{U}^{(1)*}_{N}(t,0)\Omega,\mathcal{N}^{2}\mathcal{U}^{(1)}_{t}(t,0)\Omega\rangle\;.

Two of these norms are straightforward to estimate, namely

\lVert U_{N,t}e^{ip\cdot x}\overline{U_{N,t}}\rVert_{\rm op}\leq\lVert U_{N,t}\rVert_{\rm op}\lVert e^{ip\cdot x}\rVert_{\rm op}\lVert\overline{U_{N,t}}\rVert_{\rm op}\leq 1

and

\lVert V_{N,t}OU_{N,t}\rVert_{\rm HS}\leq\lVert V_{N,t}\rVert_{\rm HS}\lVert O\rVert_{\rm op}\lVert U_{N,t}\rVert_{\rm op}\leq\lVert O\rVert_{\rm op}N^{1/2}\;.

The semiclassical structure is crucial to estimate the third of these norms; in fact, recalling
U_{N,t}=1-\omega_{N,t}
and
U_{N,t}V_{N,t}=0
, by the Hölder inequality for Schatten norms we obtain

\begin{split}\lVert U_{N,t}e^{ip\cdot x}V_{N,t}\rVert_{\rm HS}^{2}&=\lVert[U_{N,t},e^{ip\cdot x}]V_{N,t}\rVert_{\rm HS}^{2}\leq\lVert[\omega_{N,t},e^{ip\cdot x}]\rVert_{\rm HS}^{2}\lVert V_{N,t}\rVert_{\rm op}^{2}\\
&=\lVert[\omega_{N,t},e^{ip\cdot x}]^{*}[\omega_{N,t},e^{ip\cdot x}]\rVert_{\rm tr}\leq\lVert[\omega_{N,t},e^{ip\cdot x}]\rVert_{\rm op}\lVert[\omega_{N,t},e^{ip\cdot x}]\rVert_{\rm tr}\\
&\leq 2\lVert\omega_{N,t}\rVert_{\rm op}\lVert e^{ip\cdot x}\rVert_{\rm op}\lVert[\omega_{N,t},e^{ip\cdot x}]\rVert_{\rm tr}=2\lVert[\omega_{N,t},e^{ip\cdot x}]\rVert_{\rm tr}\;.\end{split}

This trace norm is exactly the one controlled by the propagated semiclassical commutator estimate
Eq.

1.14
. (The corresponding bound with the momentum operator in the exponent is not needed for the many-body analysis, it is only required to be able to close the Grönwall estimate in the proof of
2

II

\displaystyle\leq C\lVert O\rVert_{\rm op}\hbar^{-1/2}\langle\mathcal{U}^{(1)*}_{N}(t,0)\Omega,\mathcal{N}^{2}\mathcal{U}^{(1)}_{t}(t,0)\Omega\rangle\;.

Finally, by a Grönwall argument
[
BPS14b
, Theorem 3.2]
using similar estimates to those employed up to here, one proves that
there exist constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
we have for all
k\in\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{U}^{(1)*}_{N}(t,0)\Omega,(\mathcal{N}+1)^{k}\mathcal{U}^{(1)}_{N}(t,0)\Omega\rangle\leq\exp(c_{1}\exp(c_{2}\lvert t\rvert))\langle\Omega,(\mathcal{N}+1)^{k}\Omega\rangle

uniformly in particle number

and in

. This completes the sketch of the proof; for details we refer to
[
BPS14b
]
with the simplification of the computation of the generator due to
[
BD23
]
.
∎

\displaystyle{\left|\hskip-0.50003pt\left|\chi_{0,\hbar}^{-}({H_{\hbar,b}})\varphi P_{j}\chi_{0,\hbar}^{+}({H_{\hbar,b}})\right|\hskip-0.50003pt\right|}_{1}={\left|\hskip-0.50003pt\left|\chi_{0,\hbar}^{-}({H_{\hbar,b}})\varphi P_{j}f_{\hbar}({H_{\hbar,b}})\chi_{0,\hbar}^{+}({H_{\hbar,b}})\right|\hskip-0.50003pt\right|}_{1}

We control the first right-hand side term by the Hölder inequality, by the functional calculus and

\displaystyle{\left|\hskip-0.50003pt\left|\chi_{0,\hbar}^{-}({H_{\hbar,b}})[\varphi P_{j},f_{\hbar}({H_{\hbar,b}})]\chi_{0,\hbar}^{+}({H_{\hbar,b}})\right|\hskip-0.50003pt\right|}_{1}

\displaystyle\leq{\left|\hskip-0.50003pt\left|\chi_{0,\hbar}^{-}({H_{\hbar,b}})\right|\hskip-0.50003pt\right|}_{{\rm op}}{\left|\hskip-0.50003pt\left|[\varphi P_{j},f_{\hbar}({H_{\hbar,b}})]\right|\hskip-0.50003pt\right|}_{1}{\left|\hskip-0.50003pt\left|\chi_{0,\hbar}^{+}({H_{\hbar,b}})\right|\hskip-0.50003pt\right|}_{{\rm op}}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\langle b\right\rangle^{r}\hbar^{1-d}\;.

Furthermore, by the cyclicity of the trace and by
2.15
instead of

\displaystyle{\left|\hskip-0.50003pt\left|\chi_{0,\hbar}^{-}({H_{\hbar,b}})f_{\hbar}({H_{\hbar,b}})\varphi P_{j}\chi_{0,\hbar}^{+}({H_{\hbar,b}})\right|\hskip-0.50003pt\right|}_{1}

\displaystyle\leq{\left|\hskip-0.50003pt\left|\chi_{0,\hbar}^{-}({H_{\hbar,b}})\right|\hskip-0.50003pt\right|}_{\rm op}{\left|\hskip-0.50003pt\left|\varphi f_{\hbar}({H_{\hbar,b}})\right|\hskip-0.50003pt\right|}_{1}{\left|\hskip-0.50003pt\left|\chi_{0,\hbar}^{+}({H_{\hbar,b}})\right|\hskip-0.50003pt\right|}_{\rm op}

\displaystyle\leq C\hbar^{1-d}\;.

Hence, there exists
C>0
such that

\displaystyle{\left|\hskip-0.50003pt\left|\chi_{0,\hbar}^{-}({H_{\hbar,b}})\varphi P_{j}\chi_{0,\hbar}^{+}({H_{\hbar,b}})\right|\hskip-0.50003pt\right|}_{1}\leq C\left\langle b\right\rangle^{r}\hbar^{1-d}.

For the cases (ii), (iii) ,
m\geq n
and

, let us assume that

By the same calculation in

, one has

(B.3)

{\left|\hskip-0.50003pt\left|\chi_{n,\hbar}^{\mp}({H_{\hbar,b}})\varphi P_{j}^{r}\chi_{m,\hbar}^{\pm}({H_{\hbar,b}})\right|\hskip-0.50003pt\right|}_{1}\leq\frac{1}{4^{2(m-1)}\hbar^{2}}{\left|\hskip-0.50003pt\left|\chi_{n,\hbar}^{\mp}({H_{\hbar,b}})[[\varphi P_{j}^{r},{H_{\hbar,b}}],{H_{\hbar,b}}]\chi_{m,\hbar}^{\pm}({H_{\hbar,b}})\right|\hskip-0.50003pt\right|}_{1}\;.

The idea consists in inserting
({H^{\rm loc}_{\hbar,b}}+2^{2n-1}\hbar)
