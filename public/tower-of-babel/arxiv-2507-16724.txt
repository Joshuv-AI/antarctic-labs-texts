SALM: Spatial Audio Language Model with Structured Embeddings for Understanding and Editing
Jinbo Hu, Yin Cao, Ming Wu, Feiran Yang, Jun Yang

Abstract
Spatial audio understanding is essential for accurately perceiving and interpreting acoustic environments. However, existing audio-language models struggle with processing spatial audio and perceiving spatial acoustic scenes. We introduce the Spatial Audio Language Model (SALM), a novel framework that bridges spatial audio and language via multi-modal contrastive learning. SALM consists of a text encoder and a dual-branch audio encoder, decomposing spatial sound into semantic and spatial components through structured audio embeddings. Key features of SALM include seamless alignment of spatial and text representations, separate and joint extraction of spatial and semantic information, zero-shot direction classification and robust support for spatial audio editing. Experimental results demonstrate that SALM effectively captures and aligns cross-modal representations. Furthermore, it supports advanced editing capabilities, such as altering directional audio using text-based embeddings.

Introduction

Humans naturally interpret spatial acoustic scenes and describe them using language. For instance, the instruction “When the bell on the left rings, enter the room” can be understood by most humans. This is because humans can semantically interpret the audio signal “ringing bell”, comprehend the spatial concept of “left”, and integrate these elements to align the semantic and spatial information of the sound source. Analogously, a machine must also bridge linguistic information with spatial audio to accurately identify, localize, and respond to sounds based on given conditions.

The audio encoder contains semantic and spatial branches, decoupling spatial audio into semantic and spatial representations. We conduct experiments to evaluate the alignment between spatial audio and language representations through spatial audio-text retrieval. Furthermore, we assess the structure of audio embeddings by zero-shot direction classification and spatial audio editing. SALM demonstrates strong capabilities in effectively capturing and aligning cross-modal embeddings, producing well-structured audio embeddings. Notably, SALM facilitates spatial audio editing by manipulating the structured audio embeddings using text embeddings derived from textural descriptions. For example, arbitrary spatial audio directions can be generated by substituting Audio Spatial Embeddings (in Figure

3
Method

3.1
Paired Spatial Audio-Language Datasets

Training a model capable of interpreting spatial audio through natural language requires datasets containing spatial audio annotated with textual spatial descriptions, e.g., “The bell on the left rings”. Popular audio captioning datasets, including AudioCaps
[
23
]
and Clotho
[
24
]
, provide annotations of textural descriptions for sound event samples from AudioSet
[
25
]
and Freesound
[
26
]
. However, these datasets are limited to monophonic audio signals and lack spatial information about sound sources. Furthermore, existing spatial audio-language datasets
[
19
]
are not publicly available. Therefore, we construct a paired dataset for spatial audio-language by utilizing Clotho and AudioCaps datasets through two pipelines: spatial audio simulation and spatial caption generation.

Following the procedure for generating synthetic spatial sound event samples in PSELDNets
[
13
]
, we synthesize first-order Ambisonics (FOA) audio samples by convolving monophonic audio signals with simulated FOA-format spatial room impulse responses (SRIRs). FOA, well-known as an array-agnostic format, is characterized by its flexibility and adaptability across diverse microphone arrays. The simulated SRIRs are generated by simulating diverse shoe-box rooms, each parameterized by specific dimensions and frequency-dependent absorption coefficients assigned to their surfaces
[
13
]
. A microphone is placed at the centre of each simulated room to capture SRIRs from various locations within the room.

The spatial caption generation pipeline involves rephrasing textural descriptions by incorporating terms that refer to spatial audio attributes. This pipeline first converts numerical spatial attributes, such as azimuth angles, into natural language descriptions. These descriptions correspond to directional classes defined at
45^{\circ}
intervals, resulting in eight distinct directions (e.g., “south” or “northeast”). Subsequently, the original caption and its corresponding spatial information are input to LLAMA 3.2-3B
[
27
]
with an inference temperature of 0.2. The step ensures the generation of spatially enriched captions that precisely describe the sound sources and their position. An example of the original and generated spatial captions can be found in Fig.
1
. The prompt used for this process is:

The sound:
<
original caption
>
is coming from the
<
direction
>
. Rephrase the sentence in English to concisely describe the sound detail and the direction of its source.

3.2
Model Architecture

The Spatial Audio-Language Model (SALM) integrates a text encoder and an audio encoder, as illustrated in Fig.
1
. The text encoder, following the design of the LAION-CLAP
[
2
]
, utilizes the RoBERTa
[
28
]
model. RoBERTa is a general-purpose language model built upon bidirectional transformer
[
29
]
architecture. The pre-trained checkpoint from the LAION-CLAP text encoder is employed to initialize the text encoder.

The audio encoder employs the EINV2 version from PSELDNets
[
13
]
. This encoder comprises two branches: an audio semantic branch and an audio spatial branch, which process inputs from the omni-direction channel and all channels of FOA-format signals, respectively. This configuration enables two branches to independently capture the semantic and spatial information of spatial audio. The two branches are connected through a soft-parameter sharing strategy
[
9
]
, e.g., multiple sets of trainable parameters, as indicated by the dotted lines connecting Audio Spatial Branch and Audio Semantic Branch in Fig.
1
. The audio encoder decomposes spatial audio into Audio Semantic Embeddings and Audio Spatial Embeddings, which can subsequently be merged into Joint Audio Embeddings using the weighted sum operation. Both audio branches adopt the HTSAT
[
30
]
architecture, the Swin-Transformer-based
[
31
]
model. For initialization, the Audio Semantic Branch utilizes the pre-trained checkpoint from the LAION-CLAP audio encoder, while the Audio Spatial Branch uses the pre-trained checkpoint from the PSELDNets DOA branch.

The Audio Spatial Branch, Audio Semantic Branch, and Text Encoder produce 768-dimensional embeddings. To unify these embeddings, three separate two-layer multi-layer perceptions (MLPs) are applied to each output. These MLPs project 768-dimensional embeddings into 512-dimensional embeddings, ensuring that the final output embeddings from all encoders have the same dimensionality.

Following LAION-CLAP
[
2
]
and ELSA
[
19
]
, the model aims at learning aligned representations using a batched contrastive loss. This loss encourages the alignment of embeddings from the same sample across different modalities while penalizing the alignment between embeddings of different samples. Additionally, given that the simulated rooms used for synthesizing spatial audio are parameterized, we have accurate labels associated with the spatial locations of sound sources. To leverage these spatial labels, DOA loss is introduced into the training process. Specifically, the generated 512-dimensional Audio Spatial Embeddings are fed into another two-layer MLP designed to predict the Cartesian DOA of sound sources within the 3D sound scene. The overall loss is defined as follows:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SALM}}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathcal{L}_{\text{CL}}+\mathcal{L}_{\text{sCL }})+\mathcal{L}_{\text{DOA}},

(1)

where
\mathcal{L}_{\text{CL}}
is the contrastive loss between Audio Semantic Embeddings and Text Embeddings and evaluates the retrieval performance,
\mathcal{L}_{\text{sCL}}
is the contrastive loss between Joint Audio Embeddings and Spatial Text Embeddings and evaluates the spatial retrieval capability, and
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DOA}}
is the cosine distance and quantifies the angular distance between the predicted and target DOAs for sound sources.

3.3
Embedding Structure

The SALM text encoder generates output text embeddings based on input textual descriptions. Specifically, Text Embeddings are derived from the original captions, while Spatial Text Embeddings are produced from the spatial captions, as illustrated in the Text Input of Fig.
1
.

The SALM audio encoder utilizes two decoupled branches to generate output audio embeddings, i.e., Audio Spatial Embeddings (
E_{\mathtt{ASp}}
) and Audio Semantic Embeddings (
E_{\mathtt{ASe}}
). These two embeddings are subsequently merged into Joint Audio Embeddings (
E_{\mathtt{JA}}
) using the following operation:

E_{\mathtt{JA}}=E_{\mathtt{ASe}}+\mathbf{s}\odot E_{\mathtt{ASp}},

(2)

where
\mathbf{s}
is a set of learnable weights with 512 dimensions and
\odot
denotes the Hadmard product. The formulation allows the model to adaptively integrate semantic and spatial information for comprehensive spatial audio representation.

Furthermore, we can edit spatial audio by manipulating the structured audio embeddings using text embeddings derived from target textual descriptions. For example, arbitrary directions of spatial audio can be structured by replacing
E_{\mathtt{ASp}}
with text embeddings derived from descriptions about directions, such as “The sound is coming from the southwest.”, denoted as
E_{\mathtt{TDi}}
. The process is formalized as follows:

\tilde{E_{\mathtt{JA}}}=E_{\mathtt{ASe}}+\mathbf{s}\odot\frac{\lVert E_{\mathtt{ASp}}\rVert\cdot E_{\mathtt{TDi}}}{\lVert E_{\mathtt{TDi}}\rVert},

(3)

where
\lVert\cdot\rVert
denotes the L2 norm, which normalizes
E_{\mathtt{TDi}}
to ensure proper alignment with
E_{\mathtt{ASp}}
. The approach allows for flexible directionality in spatial audio based on textual descriptions.

Experiments

4.1
Experimental Setups

The synthetic spatial datasets are built upon Clotho
[
24
]
and AudioCaps
[
23
]
, containing 5,929 and 50,956 audio clips, respectively. Each audio clip is spatially augmented by simulating three distinct locations within the same room, enabling the model to see the same audio with various locations in each epoch and integrating audio semantic and spatial information for comprehensive evaluation. Finally, we synthesize 17,787 spatial audio clips using the Clotho dataset, amounting to roughly 111 hours, denoted as sClotho, and 152,868 audio clips using the AudioCaps dataset, totaling approximately 419 hours, denoted as sAudioCaps.

In line with LAION-CLAP
[
2
]
and ELSA
[
19
]
, we evaluate spatial retrieval performance through computing cross-modal audio-to-text and text-to-audio matching across all samples. The retrieval metrics are based on the cosine similarity of embeddings from two modalities. Specifically, recall at rank 1 (R@1), rank 5 (R@5), and rank 10 (R@10) are utilized to measure whether the correct match appears among the top 1, 5, and 10 closest samples, respectively. Additionally, the angular distance between the predicted and target DOAs is calculated to quantify the localization error.

The sampling rate of synthetic spatial audio samples is 24 kHz. We extract the 64-dimensional log mel spectrograms and intensity vectors
[
13

from four-channel FOA signals with a Hanning window of 1,024 points and a hop size of 240 as input audio features. The input segments to the model have a ten-second fixed duration for training and inference. For audio clip duration shorter than 10 seconds, the signal is repeated and subsequently padded with zero values to meet the required duration. Conversely, audio clips exceeding 10 seconds are randomly chunked to 10 seconds. A batch size of 64 and the Adam optimizer are employed for training. Spatial Semantic Retrieval

We conduct experiments to evaluate spatial audio understanding capabilities of various models and loss functions, using spatial retrieval metrics and localization errors, as shown in Table
1
.

SALM-s is a variant of the SALM and only utilizes the Audio Spatial Branch, as opposed to the dual-branch architecture employed in the original audio encoder. A key distinction between LAION-CLAP
[
2
]
and SALM-s lies in the handling of input audio channels. LAION-CLAP utilizes only one omni-direction channel of FOA audio signals, while SALM-s incorporates all channels. Experimental results indicate that SALM-s significantly outperforms LAION-CLAP across all metrics. This is primarily attributed to the absence of spatial information in the audio input to LAION-CLAP, which limits its capacity to find accurate spatial audio-text matching, relying solely on semantic information.

Introducing an additional audio semantic branch into SALM-s brings performance improvement, especially in localization error, which achieves an improvement of approximately 60%, as illustrated in the middle two rows of Table
1
. It demonstrates SALM’s ability to integrate spatial and semantic information effectively. Additionally, incorporating
\mathcal{L}_{\text{CL}}
into optimization yields further performance improvement across all metrics. The proposed loss functions contain an aggregated item (
\mathcal{L}_{\text{sCL}}
) and two decoupled items (
\mathcal{L}_{\text{CL}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DOA}}
), explicitly designed to enable the model to enhance semantic understanding and spatial perception jointly and independently.

4.3
Zero-shot Direction Classification

Zero-shot direction classification accuracy of SALM is reported in Table
2
. We generate captions by the template “
The sound is coming from the <direction>
”, where
direction
represents one of the eight-direction descriptions, such as “south” or “northeast”. Text embeddings for these captions are extracted using the pre-trained SALM and compared with audio embeddings from spatial audio samples based on cosine similarity. A match is classified as correct if the spatial attribute of audio samples is the closest to the corresponding caption about their directions. Experimental results demonstrate that Audio Spatial Embeddings and Joint Audio Embeddings exhibit a strong alignment with text embeddings associated with directional descriptions.

4.4
Spatial Audio Editing

We manipulate the direction of spatial audio by modifying the Audio Spatial Embeddings item (detail in Sec.
3.3
) and present results in Table
3
. The term “None” indicates no operation in audio embeddings, “Swap” refers to replacing Audio Spatial Embeddings with text embeddings from the templates about their corresponding directional description, while preserving the original directions of Joint Audio Embeddings, and “Change” involves changing directions of the sound through substituting Audio Spatial Embeddings with text embeddings from different directional descriptions. Overall, we find that “Swap” and “Change” operations minimally impact the spatial semantic information of sound. Experimental results show Joint Audio Embeddings are well-structured and can be edited without compromising the underlying semantics of the sound.

5
Conclusion

We introduced the Spatial Audio Language Model (SALM), designed to align spatial audio with natural language through multi-modal contrastive learning on paired spatial audio-language datasets. SALM integrates a text encoder and a dual-branch audio encoder, wherein the latter decomposes spatial audio into semantic and spatial representations and produces structured and flexible audio embeddings. Experimental results show that SALM can capture spatial and semantic features and achieve notable performance in spatial audio-text retrieval tasks. Additionally, SALM produces well-structured audio embeddings, enabling tasks such as zero-shot direction classification and spatial audio-text retrieval with editing capabilities. Specifically, SALM performs spatial audio editing by modifying structured embeddings, allowing alterations to spatial audio directions based on text embeddings derived from directional descriptions. This capability underscores SALM’s potential for applications in augmented and virtual reality environments. Future work will focus on handling overlapping sound sources and incorporating additional spatial and acoustic attributes, such as reverberation, noise, and source distance.
