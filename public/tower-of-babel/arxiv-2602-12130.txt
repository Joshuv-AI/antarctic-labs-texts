On minimal pattern-containing inversion sequences


Abstract
We introduce the notion of minimal inversion sequences for a pattern ρ \rho , which form the smallest set of inversion sequences whose avoidance is equivalent to the avoidance of ρ \rho for inversion sequences. We give a characterization of ρ \rho -minimal inversion sequences based on the occurrences of the pattern ρ \rho they contain, and use it to find upper and lower bounds on the lengths of ρ \rho -minimal inversion sequences. We provide some enumerative results on the exact number of minimal inversion sequences for some patterns, through a bijection with increasing trees, and some exhaustive generation. Lastly, we enumerate inversion sequences which are equal to their reduction, and find an interesting connection with poly-Bernoulli numbers.

1.2
Motivation and outline

The study of patterns in inversion sequences began in
[
11
]
and
[
5
]
, by analogy with permutation patterns. We begin with a short introduction to some classical concepts of the permutation patterns literature, which may be found in
[
3
]
. The set of patterns which may appear in a permutation is (up to reduction

1

1

The reduction of a sequence is defined in Section
2
.
) itself the set of permutations, yielding a nice correspondence between permutations and their patterns. More precisely, the pattern containment relation is a partial order on the set of all permutations. A
permutation class
is a down-set in this partial order: if
\sigma
and
\tau
are two permutations such that
\sigma
contains
\tau
as a pattern, then any permutation class which contains
\sigma
also contains
\tau
. Every permutation class can be characterized by the minimal set of permutations that it avoids; this set is called the
basis
of the class. The basis of a permutation class is an antichain (a set of pairwise incomparable elements) under the pattern containment order, and may be infinite.

In the context of inversion sequences, we can observe patterns that are not inversion sequences (even after reduction). For instance, the inversion sequence
0021401
contains the pattern 120. It is not difficult to see that the patterns which may appear in inversion sequences form (up to reduction) the set of nonempty Cayley permutations, and this set uniquely represents every way in which the values of an integer sequence may be ordered.
We may therefore use the term “pattern” instead of “nonempty Cayley permutation” from here on, as it is usual in the literature.

As we observed, Cayley permutations are not always inversion sequences, so one might wonder whether the set
\mathcal{I}(\rho)
of inversion sequences avoiding a pattern
\rho
can be described as the set of inversion sequences
\mathcal{I}(S_{\rho})
avoiding some set of
inversion sequences

S_{\rho}\subseteq\mathcal{I}
. If we allow infinite sets, the obvious answer is yes: a possible choice for
S_{\rho}
is the set of all inversion sequences which contain the pattern
\rho
. A more interesting refinement of this question is to ask whether there is a
finite
set (or equivalently, a finite antichain)
S_{\rho}
satisfying the above, and how small can such a set be?

This question already surfaced in
[
10
, page 3]
, although it is incorrectly answered. We recall their definition and approach, before introducing our own in the next section.

Definition 1

(From
[
10
]
)
.

Given a pattern
\tau=\tau_{1}\dots\tau_{k}
, let
L_{\tau}
be the set of all inversion sequences of the form
\theta^{(1)}\tau_{1}\theta^{(2)}\tau_{2}\dots\theta^{(k)}\tau_{k}
such that the length of the inversion sequence
\theta^{(1)}\tau_{1}\theta^{(2)}\tau_{2}\dots\theta^{(j)}\tau_{j}
is minimal for each

Claim 2

(From
[
10
]
)
.

For any set of patterns

, an inversion sequence
\sigma
avoids

if and only if
\sigma
avoids every pattern in

.

Our first contribution is the simple remark that Claim

, we have

, and Claim

then states that an inversion sequence avoids the pattern 10 if and only if it avoids 010. Clearly this is not true: the sequence

In
[

, Claim

We give in Corollary

This change of bound does not invalidate the algorithmic approach of
[

[
10

A summary of our work is as follows.

•

In Section

•

In Section

In Section

In Section

•

In Section

Definition 3
.

Let
\rho\in\mathcal{P}\backslash\{\varepsilon\}

Let

be an inversion sequence, and let

•

\rho\preceq\sigma

\rho

•

,

and
\rho\preceq\tau\preceq\sigma

By the first bullet point above,
\sigma

\rho

-minimal inversion sequence, although we provide such results for some patterns in Section

Structure of minimal inversion sequences

Given a pattern
\rho
, a
\rho
-minimal inversion sequence

Proposition 4
.

Let
n,k\geq 1
, let
\rho\in\mathcal{P}_{k}
, and let
\sigma\in\mathcal{I}_{n}\cap\mathcal{P}_{n}
be a sequence containing the pattern
\rho
. Then
\sigma
is
\rho
-minimal if and only if for any set
R\subseteq[1,n]
such that
\rho^{\prime}:=(\sigma_{i})_{i\in R}
is an occurrence of
\rho
, both conditions below are satisfied:

1.

[\mathbf{max}(\mathbf{Sat}(\sigma)),n]\subseteq R
,

2.

any value of
\sigma
which does not appear in
\rho^{\prime}
appears at least twice in
\sigma
.

Proof.

Let
s:=\mathbf{max}(\mathbf{Sat}(\sigma))
. First, we prove by contrapositive that both conditions are necessary. Let
R\subseteq[1,n]
be such that
\rho^{\prime}:=(\sigma_{i})_{i\in R}
is an occurrence of
\rho
.

1.

Assume that
u\in[s,n]
satisfies
u\notin R
. Then
(\sigma_{i})_{i\in[1,n]\backslash\{u\}}
is an inversion sequence which contains the pattern
\rho
, hence
\sigma
is not
\rho
-minimal.

2.

Assume that
u\in[1,n]\backslash R
is such that the value
\sigma_{u}
appears only once in
\sigma
, and let
\sigma^{\prime}:=\mathbf{red}((\sigma_{i})_{i\in[1,n]\backslash\{u\}})
. Clearly,
\sigma^{\prime}
contains the pattern
\rho
. We next show that
\sigma^{\prime}
is an inversion sequence. For each
i\in[1,n]\backslash\{u\}
, the transformation from
\sigma
to
\sigma^{\prime}
shifts the diagonal difference of each entry
\sigma_{i}
as follows:

•

if
i>u
, then the removal of
\sigma_{u}
increases the diagonal difference of
\sigma_{i}
by 1,

•

if
\sigma_{i}>\sigma_{u}
, then the reduction decreases the diagonal difference of
\sigma_{i}
by 1.

It follows that the only entries whose diagonal difference is actually increased are those
\sigma_{i}
such that
i>u
and
\sigma_{i}<\sigma_{u}
. Since
\sigma_{u}<u
, such entries satisfy
\sigma_{i}<i-2
, so they remain below the diagonal in
\sigma^{\prime}
. We conclude that
\sigma^{\prime}
is an inversion sequence, therefore
\sigma
is not
\rho
-minimal.

Now assume that both conditions are satisfied by every occurrence of
\rho
in
\sigma
. To complete our proof, we show that there is no sequence
\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}\cap\mathcal{P}
such that
\sigma^{\prime}\neq\sigma
and
{\rho\preceq\sigma^{\prime}\preceq\sigma}
.
Let
J\varsubsetneq[1,n]
be such that
\sigma^{\prime}:=\mathbf{red}((\sigma_{i})_{i\in J})
contains the pattern
\rho
, and let
{J^{c}:=[1,n]\backslash J}
. By condition 1, we have
J^{c}\subseteq[1,s-1]
, therefore
\sigma_{i}\leq s-2
for all
i\in J^{c}
. Let
V:=\{v\in\{\sigma_{i}\}_{i\in J^{c}}\;:\;v\notin\{\sigma_{i}\}_{i\in J}\}
be the set of values of
\sigma
which do not appear in
\sigma^{\prime}
. By condition 2, we have
|J^{c}|\geq 2\,|V|
. Since
J^{c}\neq\emptyset
, this implies that
|J^{c}|>|V|
.
Recall that
\sigma_{s}=s-1
, and for all

we have both

and
\sigma_{i}<\sigma_{s}
. By looking at the value to which
\sigma_{s}
is mapped in

, we obtain

\sigma^{\prime}_{s-|J^{c}|}=s-1-|V|>s-1-|J^{c}|,

therefore
\sigma^{\prime}
is not an inversion sequence. We conclude that

is
\rho
-minimal.

Proposition 5
.

Let
n,k\geq 1
, let
\rho\in\mathcal{P}_{k}
, let
\sigma\in\mathcal{I}_{n}
, let
{\ell\in[0,k-1]}
be such that
k-\ell\in\mathbf{Sat}(\rho)
, let
{r_{1}<\dots<r_{k}\in[1,n]}
be such that
\rho^{\prime}:=(\sigma_{r_{i}})_{i\in[1,k]}
is an occurrence of
\rho
, and let

. If
\sigma
is
\rho
-minimal, the following holds:

1.

for all

, if
\sigma_{i}\geq\sigma_{r_{k-\ell}}
, then

,

2.

for all
i\in[0,\ell]
, we have
r_{k-i}=n-i
,

3.

n-\ell\in\mathbf{Sat}(\sigma)
.

Proof.

First, we prove items 1 and 2 by contrapositive. Note that item 2 is equivalent to
[r_{k-\ell},n]\subseteq R
. Assume that
\sigma
does not satisfy item 1 or item 2 (or both), i.e.

U:=\{i\in[1,n]\backslash R\;:\;\sigma_{i}\geq\sigma_{r_{k-\ell}}\;\text{ or }\;i\geq r_{k-\ell}\}\neq\emptyset.

In particular, we have
\mathbf{min}(U)>\sigma_{r_{k-\ell}}
since every

satisfies either
{i>\sigma_{i}\geq\sigma_{r_{k-\ell}}}
or

because

is an inversion sequence.
Let
\sigma^{\prime}:=\mathbf{red}(\{\sigma_{i}\}_{i\in[1,n]\backslash U})

, let
{r^{\prime}_{i}:=r_{i}-|\{j\in U\;:\;j<r_{i}\}|}

, and let

By construction,
r^{\prime}_{k-i}=n^{\prime}-i

, and

\rho

is an inversion sequence.

•

Let

be such that
\sigma^{\prime}_{i}\leq\sigma_{r_{k-\ell}}
, and let
\sigma_{j}
be the entry of
\sigma

\sigma^{\prime}_{i}
. Since
\mathbf{min}(U)>\sigma_{r_{k-\ell}}
, the entry
\sigma_{j}
either stays at position

\sigma_{r_{k-\ell}}+1

, or

\sigma^{\prime}_{i}<i
.

•

Let

be such that
\sigma^{\prime}_{n^{\prime}-i}>\sigma_{r_{k-\ell}}
. By definition of
\sigma^{\prime}
and

, we have
{n^{\prime}-i\in R^{\prime}}
. Because

, we have
{n^{\prime}-i>r^{\prime}_{k-\ell}=n^{\prime}-\ell}
, hence

.
By definition of

. Since

{\sigma^{\prime}_{n^{\prime}-i}-\sigma^{\prime}_{n^{\prime}-\ell}=\rho_{k-i}-\rho_{k-\ell}}

-minimal. This concludes the proof of items 1 and 2.

Now we prove item 3 by contrapositive.
Assume that
\sigma
satisfies items 1 and 2, that

-minimal), and that

does not satisfy item 3. Since

is an inversion sequence and

\sigma

. Since

are not saturated.
Now, assume that

is such that

In particular, we have
\sigma_{r_{i}}-r_{i}>\sigma_{r_{k-\ell}}-r_{k-\ell}

, and in particular
\rho_{i}<\rho_{k-\ell}

is an occurrence of

\sigma_{r_{k-\ell}}-\sigma_{r_{i}}\geq\rho_{k-\ell}-\rho_{i}\geq k-\ell-i

that is part of

must be to the left of some entry of

Lemma 6
.

Let
\rho
be a pattern, and let
\sigma
be a
\rho
-minimal inversion sequence. Then

|\sigma|-\mathbf{dist}(\sigma)=|\rho|-\mathbf{dist}(\rho)+\mathbf{mdd}(\rho).

Proof.

Let

, let
k:=|\rho|
, let
\ell\in[0,k-1]
be such that

, let

be such that

is an occurrence of
\rho
, and let
\tau:=(\sigma_{i})_{i\in[1,n]\backslash R}
be the sequence obtained by removing
\rho^{\prime}
from
\sigma

By Proposition
5
, we know that
\sigma_{n-\ell}
is
\rho^{\prime}_{k-\ell}
, and every entry of
\tau
has value less than
\sigma_{n-\ell}
. Since
\sigma
is a Cayley permutation, this implies that
{\sigma_{n-\ell}=\rho_{k-\ell}+\mathbf{dist}(\sigma)-\mathbf{dist}(\rho)}
, where the number
\mathbf{dist}(\sigma)-\mathbf{dist}(\rho)
counts the values of

\sigma_{n-\ell}=n-\ell-1

, and

•

\rho_{k-\ell}=\mathbf{mdd}(\rho)+k-\ell-1

hence

∎

Corollary 7
.

Let
\rho
be a pattern, and let
\sigma
be a
\rho
-minimal inversion sequence. Then
|\rho|+\mathbf{mdd}(\rho)\leq|\sigma|\leq|\rho|+2\,\mathbf{mdd}(\rho)
. In particular,
|\sigma|\leq 3\,|\rho|-2
.

Proof.

The lower bound is satisfied because
|\mathbf{dist}(\sigma)|\geq|\mathbf{dist}(\rho)|
, since
\sigma
contains the pattern
\rho
.

To prove the upper bound, let
\rho^{\prime}
be an occurrence of
\rho
in
\sigma
, and let
\tau
be the sequence obtained by removing
\rho^{\prime}
from
\sigma
. Let
t:=|\tau|
,
m:=\mathbf{mdd}(\rho)
, and let
v
be the number of values of
\tau
which do not appear in
\rho^{\prime}
. In particular,
t=|\sigma|-|\rho|
, and
v=\mathbf{dist}(\sigma)-\mathbf{dist}(\rho)
. By item 2 of Proposition
4
, any value of
\tau
which is not in
\rho^{\prime}
must appear at least twice in
\tau
, therefore
t\geq 2v
. By Lemma
6
, we have
t=v+m
, hence
2v\leq v+m
, hence
v\leq m
, hence
t\leq 2m
, and finally
|\sigma|=|\rho|+t\leq|\rho|+2m
.

To see that
|\sigma|\leq 3\,|\rho|-2
, simply notice that
\mathbf{mdd}(\rho)\leq\mathbf{max}(\rho)
, and
\mathbf{max}(\rho)\leq|\rho|-1
since
\rho
is a Cayley permutation.
∎

For any pattern
\rho
, we remark that all inversion sequences of the form
\alpha\cdot\rho
for some
{\alpha\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{mdd}(\rho)}}
are
\rho
-minimal. Indeed, these are
\rho
-containing inversion sequences of minimal length, and they are Cayley permutations since
\mathbf{max}(\alpha)<\mathbf{mdd}(\rho)\leq\mathbf{max}(\rho)
. In particular, the total number of
\rho
-minimal inversion sequences is at least
\mathbf{mdd}(\rho)!
.

We conclude this section with a very simple but important consequence of Corollary
7
on pattern avoidance in inversion sequences.

Theorem 8
.

Let
P\subset\mathcal{P}\backslash\{\varepsilon\}
be a finite set of patterns. There exists a finite set
Q\subset\mathcal{I}\cap\mathcal{P}
such that
\mathcal{I}(P)=\mathcal{I}(Q)
.

Proof.

Let
Q
be the set obtained by replacing each pattern
\rho\in P
by the set of
\rho
-minimal inversion sequences. By definition of
\rho
-minimal inversion sequences, we have
Q\subset\mathcal{I}\cap\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{I}(Q)=\mathcal{I}(P)
. By Corollary
7
,
Q
contains only inversion sequences of length at most
\mathbf{max}(3\,|\rho|-2\;:\;\rho\in P)
, therefore

is finite.
∎

5.2
Exhaustive generation for small patterns

In this subsection, we use computer programs to generate every
\rho
-minimal inversion sequence for a pattern
\rho
of small length. We know from Corollary
7
that the length of every
\rho
-minimal inversion sequence is between
|\rho|+\mathbf{mdd}(\rho)
and
|\rho|+2\,\mathbf{mdd}(\rho)
, so an easy approach would be to generate each sequence of
\mathcal{I}\cap\mathcal{P}
whose length lies in this interval, and then test whether it is
\rho
-minimal using either the definition of
\rho
-minimal sequences (checking whether there is a smaller element in the poset
(\mathcal{I}\mathcal{P}[\rho],\preceq)
), or the characterization of Proposition
4
(checking whether every occurrence of
\rho
satisfies some conditions). While this approach works for very small patterns, its limits are already noticeable for some patterns of length 5: their minimal inversion sequences can reach a length of 13, and there are 363 674 407 sequences in
\mathcal{I}_{13}\cap\mathcal{P}_{13}
to test (see Section
6
for the enumeration of
\mathcal{I}\cap\mathcal{P}
).

Instead, we make use of some properties of minimal inversion sequences from Section
3
to generate them more efficiently.
Assume that we are given a nonempty pattern
\rho
with
m:=\mathbf{mdd}(\rho)
and
s:=\mathbf{min}(\mathbf{Sat}(\rho))
, and that we wish to generate every
\rho
-minimal inversion sequence.
The general idea of our approach is to start from the pattern
\rho
, and build an inversion sequence
\sigma
around it by inserting some new entries, possibly shifting some values of
\rho
in the process.
This way, we are guaranteed to construct an inversion sequence which contains at least one occurrence of
\rho
(we later refer to it as the
starting occurrence
of
\rho

Additionally, Section
3
gives several conditions that the newly inserted entries must satisfy for
\sigma
to be
\rho
-minimal. In practice, we generate all such sequences
\sigma
which belong to
\mathcal{I}\cap\mathcal{P}
and satisfy the following conditions.

•

By items 1 and 2 of Proposition
5
, each new entry inserted must be to the left and below the leftmost saturated entry of the starting occurrence of
\rho
(that is
\rho_{s}
at the beginning of our construction).

•

By Lemma
6

n:=|\sigma|-|\rho|
is the number of new entries inserted in
\rho

is the number of new values introduced by these entries, then
n-v=m

•

By Corollary
7

n

[m,2m]

•

By item 2 of Proposition
4
, each of the
v
new values is inserted at least twice.

If

11

\rho

\rho

Question 3
.

Does Wilf-equivalence between two patterns always imply they have the same inversion sequence basis type?

A natural extension of our work is to study other
minimal
combinatorial objects defined in a similar way for some notion of pattern containment. Some obvious choices of objects to consider are well-known subsets of inversion sequences in which patterns have also been studied, for the same pattern containment relation. We briefly discuss two such cases below.

•

Restricted growth functions.
A
record
(or strict left-to-right maximum) of an integer sequence
\sigma
is an entry
\sigma_{i}
such that
\sigma_{i}>\sigma_{j}
for all
j<i
.
Let

\mathbf{REC}(\sigma):=\{\sigma_{i}\;:\;\forall j<i,\;\sigma_{i}>\sigma_{j}\}

be the set of record values of
\sigma
. A
restricted growth function
(see e.g.
[
1
]
), or RGF for short, can be defined as an inversion sequence
\sigma
such that
\mathbf{REC}(\sigma)=[0,\mathbf{max}(\sigma)]
. In particular, RGFs are always Cayley permutations.

Given a pattern
\rho
, it is much easier to determine all
\rho
-minimal RGFs than
\rho
-minimal inversion sequences.
Let
m:=|[0,\mathbf{max}(\rho)]\backslash\mathbf{REC}(\rho)|
be the number of values of
\rho
which do not occur as a record. The value
m
has a role analogous to
\mathbf{mdd}(\rho)
in the inversion sequence case, since it serves as a measure of how much
\rho
differs from a RGF. We claim that the
\rho
-minimal RGFs are precisely the sequences obtained by inserting one occurrence of each value of
[0,\mathbf{max}(\rho)]\backslash\mathbf{REC}(\rho)
in
\rho
, in positions such that the resulting sequence is a RGF (each value must be inserted far enough to the left to be a record, and far enough to the right to avoid overtaking other records). Indeed,

–

it is clearly necessary to insert at least these entries in order to create a RGF which contains
\rho
,

–

inserting any extra entries (and possibly shifting values in the process) is unnecessary and would create a RGF that is not
\rho
-minimal, since removing those extra entries (and taking the reduction of the resulting sequence) still yields a RGF which contains
\rho
.

In particular,
\rho
-minimal RGFs always have length
|\rho|+m
, they contain a single occurrence of
\rho
, and they have a much simpler structure than
\rho
-minimal inversion sequences.

•

Ascent sequences.
An
ascent
(or consecutive pattern
01
) of an integer sequence
\sigma
is a pair of entries
(\sigma_{i},\sigma_{i+1})
such that
\sigma_{i}<\sigma_{i+1}
.
Let

\mathbf{asc}(\sigma):=|\{i\in[1,|\sigma|-1]\;:\;\sigma_{i}<\sigma_{i+1}\}|

be the number of ascents of
\sigma
. An
ascent sequence

[
4
]
is a nonnegative integer sequence
\sigma
such that
\sigma_{i}\leq\mathbf{asc}((\sigma_{j})_{j<i})
for all
i
. In particular, ascent sequences are a subset of inversion sequences, and a superset of restricted growth functions.

Given a pattern
\rho
, the set of
\rho
-minimal ascent sequences appears to be more difficult to study than its inversion sequence counterpart. We now make a few remarks about some of their differences.

–

Some patterns that are not ascent sequences only have a single minimal ascent sequence. Consequently, some patterns having different lengths are Wilf-equivalent for ascent sequences. For instance, 010 is the only 10-minimal ascent sequence.

–

Some patterns have “gaps” in their ascent sequence basis type. For instance, there exist some 210-minimal ascent sequences of length 11 and 13, but none of length 12.

–

We believe that a result on the length of
\rho
-minimal ascent sequences analogous to Corollary
7
could be given, albeit with an upper bound that is
quadratic
in the length of
\rho
. More precisely, if
\rho
is the decreasing pattern of length
k\geq 3
, we can construct a
\rho
-minimal ascent sequence of length
k^{2}+2k-2
, and we conjecture this is the greatest achievable length.
Such a sequence can be formed by concatenating
k-1
factors
4
4

4

To be precise, the factor containing the values
[a,b]
is obtained by interleaving the two nonincreasing sequences
(b-1,b-2,\dots,a+1)\cdot(a,a)
and
(b,b)\cdot(b-1,b-2,\dots,a+1)
.
containing two occurrences of each value in the
k-1
sets
[0,1],[2,4],[5,8],\dots,[(k^{2}-k-2)/2,(k^{2}+k-4)/2]
(of respective sizes
2,3\dots,k
), then appending an occurrence of
\rho
using the values
[(k^{2}+k-2)/2,(k^{2}+3k-4)/2]
.
For instance, with the notation
\texttt{A}:=10
,
\texttt{B}:=11
, and
\texttt{C}:=12
,

0101\cdot 342423\cdot 78685756\cdot\texttt{CBA}9

is a
3210
-minimal ascent sequence.

We hope to study
\rho
-minimal ascent sequences more thoroughly in the future.
