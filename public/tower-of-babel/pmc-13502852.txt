Feasibility of a culturally tailored traction alopecia prevention curriculum in a diverse urban middle school: a pilot study
Authors: Aliyyat Afolabi, Edra K. Ha, Oreoluwa Fawole, Beth Hoffman, Melissa Pugliano-Mauro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Traction alopecia (TA) is a preventable form of hair loss that disproportionately affects individuals of African descent because of high-tension hairstyling practices. TA often begins in childhood, yet few interventions target early education. This pilot study aimed to evaluate the feasibility and preliminary impact of a culturally tailored TA prevention curriculum for middle school students.Objective:To develop and pilot a culturally tailored traction alopecia prevention curriculum for middle school students and evaluate feasability, acceptability and preliminary education impact.Methods:A 45-minute interactive curriculum on TA causes, prevention, and healthy hair care was delivered to students at an urban middle school. Pre- and postsurveys assessed knowledge, hair care practices, and attitudes toward scalp health. Data were analyzed descriptively because of variations in survey question phrasing.Results:Of 73 participants, 35 completed both surveys. Before the program, only 11.4% had heard of TA. After the session, 91.4% reported improved understanding of TA, and 34.3% were “very likely” to change hairstyling practices. Most participants (74.3%) rated the session 7/10 or higher in value, and 82.9% intended to share the information with family.Limitations:Findings are limited by the small sample size, single-school setting, and lack of long-term follow-up to assess sustained behavior change.Conclusion:This pilot curriculum was feasible, well-received, and increased awareness of TA. Future studies should refine the curriculum, standardize evaluation tools, and examine long-term behavior change to support equitable hair health education in at-risk communities.

Introduction

Traction alopecia (TA) is a preventable form of hair loss caused by prolonged tension on the hair shaft and follicles, typically resulting from high-tension hairstyles such as braids, weaves, extensions, and ponytails. Though it can affect individuals of all backgrounds, TA disproportionately impacts women of African descent, particularly those with afro-textured, tightly coiled hair types, because of culturally prevalent hairstyling practices.1–3 Without early intervention, TA can progress from reversible hair loss to irreversible scarring alopecia. TA often begins during childhood or adolescence, coinciding with the early adoption of high-risk hairstyling practices.1,4,5 For instance, repeated use of tight braids or weaves, commonly worn for aesthetic or cultural reasons, has been associated with early-onset hair thinning and scalp discomfort in pediatric populations.6 Despite this early onset, public health strategies and dermatologic education on TA remain limited, especially for young people in communities of color. In African and African American populations, studies estimate TA prevalence between 17.1% in school-aged girls and 31.7% in adult women,7 though these figures have not been updated in over a decade—underscoring a lack of contemporary data and continued under-recognition in clinical and public health settings.5,7

Schools offer a promising venue for delivering early, culturally relevant health education.8,9 However, to our knowledge, no standardized TA prevention curricula currently exist for middle school populations. This study aimed to address this gap by developing and piloting a school-based TA education program tailored to a racially and socioeconomically diverse student population.

Materials and methods

Curriculum development

Given the lack of existing school-based curricula for TA awareness and prevention, we developed a 45-minute, interactive curriculum tailored to middle school students. The curriculum was informed by literature on TA and health education principles and refined with feedback from dermatologists, school nurses, and health educators with expertise in hair loss and skin of color conditions to ensure it was culturally appropriate and age-appropriate.1,10,11

The curriculum consisted of a slide-based presentation covering the causes, symptoms, and prevention of TA, as well as healthy hair practices and available treatments. To encourage engagement, the session included interactive elements such as pop quizzes and open discussion prompts about students’ personal experiences with hair care. The session began with prior knowledge checks and concluded with a Q&A segment to promote student dialogue. The full presentation is available in the Supplementary materials.

Study procedure and participants

This pilot study was conducted through a community academic partnership between the University of Pittsburgh and a local urban middle school (grades 6-8) serving a diverse population in terms of race, gender, and socioeconomic status. In alignment with community-engaged research principles, school leadership advised delivering the curriculum during both a science class and a lunchtime health series.

Students were invited to complete a brief precurricular survey assessing baseline knowledge, hair care practices, and awareness of TA. After the session, students completed a postcurricular survey evaluating changes in knowledge, attitudes toward TA prevention, and intention to modify hair practices. This project was deemed exempt by the University of Pittsburgh Institutional Review Board.

Outcome measures

Surveys included multiple-choice items addressing 3 primary domains: (1) TA knowledge, (2) hair care practices, and (3) attitudes toward healthy hair behaviors. The presurvey additionally captured demographic data, while the postsurvey assessed engagement, perceived value of the session, and intentions to share information learned. Full survey instruments are provided in Supplementary Appendix A, https://links.lww.com/IJWD/A100.

Statistical analysis

Survey data were cleaned and analyzed using RStudio. Responses were matched using a self-generated “secret code,” and only paired pre/post responses were included in the analysis. However, because of differences in question wording between pre- and postsurveys, formal statistical comparisons were limited, and descriptive statistics (frequencies and percentages) were used to summarize participant responses.

Results

Participant characteristics

Of the 73 responses collected, 38 were excluded because of incomplete data, leaving 35 participants with matched pre- and postsurvey responses. Of these, 34.3% (n = 12) were in seventh grade and 65.7% (n = 23) were in eighth grade. Nearly half (45.7%) identified as non-Hispanic Black or African American, and 60% identified as female. Based on U.S. Census Bureau data, most participants resided in ZIP codes with median household incomes below the state average (Table 1).

Before the session, 88.6% of students had never heard of the term “traction alopecia.” Despite this, 71.4% rated themselves as “very” or “somewhat” knowledgeable about hair and hairstyles. When asked about behavior change, 28.6% said they would be “very likely” to modify their hairstyle habits if informed of potential risks. In terms of the curriculum’s impact on perception, 91.4% of students reported a positive shift in how they valued hair and scalp health. Specifically, 37.1% indicated a significant change, and 54.3% a moderate one.

The session was generally well-received: 74.3% rated it 7 or higher on a 10-point scale for engagement and informativeness. In addition, 51.4% rated the session as “very valuable” and 22.9% as “extremely valuable.” Most students (82.9%) planned to share what they learned with family, 42.9% with friends, and 11.4% via social media (Table 3).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to develop and evaluate the feasibility of a school-based TA prevention program tailored to middle school students, a population particularly vulnerable to TA because of early adoption of high-tension hairstyling practices. Recent clinical studies demonstrate that TA disproportionately affects women and individuals of African descent, underscoring the need for targeted prevention strategies in these communities.11

Consistent with prior research among adolescents in Nigeria,8 precurricular survey results revealed limited awareness of TA, despite students’ self-reported confidence in their general hair knowledge. This gap highlights the importance of targeted education in communities where tight hairstyles such as braids, cornrows, and ponytails are culturally embedded and recognized contributors to the development of TA in individuals with afro-texture hair.12

Postcurricular responses showed a marked improvement in both awareness and understanding of TA. Notably, 60% of students reported a “very clear” understanding of how certain hairstyles contribute to hair loss, and over 90% reported a positive shift in their perception of hair and scalp health. Improving recognition of TA remains important, as recent studies continue to refine diagnostic criteria and emphasize earlier identification of the disease to prevent progression to permanent scarring.13 The results from our studies align with findings from other school-based dermatology interventions, such as sun safety curricula, which have demonstrated knowledge gains and attitudinal shifts.6 While those programs often spanned several weeks, our single-session intervention yielded encouraging short-term outcomes, suggesting that brief, focused education can still be impactful.

Importantly, the curriculum’s effects appeared to extend beyond individual knowledge. A large majority of students (82.9%) reported plans to share what they learned with family, indicating potential for broader community engagement and ripple effects beyond the classroom.

However, increased awareness did not consistently translate into behavioral intent. Although 34.3% of participants expressed a strong likelihood of changing their hair practices, 31.4% remained unlikely to do so. This underscores the complexity of hair-related behavior, which is shaped not only by knowledge but also by cultural norms, aesthetic preferences, and social influences. Future interventions may need to incorporate culturally sensitive strategies, such as offering protective style alternatives, engaging family members, and involving local hairstylists, to effectively support behavior change.

These findings demonstrate the feasibility of delivering a culturally relevant, school-based TA curriculum to middle school students. The program was well-received and led to increased awareness, with potential for broader community dissemination through student engagement. However, translating knowledge into behavioral change remains a challenge. Future research should explore longitudinal impacts, integrate protective style alternatives, and involve key community stakeholders—such as families and hairstylists—to reinforce sustainable hair health behaviors. Standardized survey instruments and larger, more diverse cohorts will be essential for assessing generalizability and refining intervention strategies.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the small sample size and specific demographic characteristics limit the generalizability of the findings. Although the results suggest increased awareness and engagement, the small cohort size may overestimate the likelihood of behavioral change. A total of 38 surveys were excluded because of incomplete responses, leaving only 35 matched pre- and postcurricular surveys for analysis.

The study was conducted at an English as a second language-designated middle school, where language barriers may have influenced participants’ ability to fully comprehend the curriculum or complete the surveys-particularly among students whose primary language was not English. Additional factors, such as time constraints, classroom disruptions, and variable student engagement, may have further contributed to survey noncompletion. Providing more time for survey administration and incorporating verbal or visual supports could improve data completeness in future studies.

A third limitation is the brief duration of the intervention and the immediate timing of the postcurricular assessment. As outcomes were measured directly following the session, we could not assess knowledge retention or long-term behavior change. Since modifying hair care habits likely requires repeated reinforcement, future longitudinal studies are needed to evaluate sustained impact.

Lastly, there was some inconsistency in survey design, as several outcome variables were not measured using matched pre- and postsession items. This limited our ability to perform formal statistical comparisons or assess change over time. Incorporating standardized, validated survey instruments in future iterations will enhance the reliability of findings and improve comparability across studies.

Conclusion

This pilot study highlights the promise of early, culturally sensitive education in raising awareness about TA among students at elevated risk. While the curriculum effectively increased knowledge and student engagement, sustained efforts are needed to promote long-term behavioral change. Integrating this intervention into broader dermatologic and public health frameworks may help reduce the burden of TA and promote equity in scalp and hair health for historically underserved populations.
