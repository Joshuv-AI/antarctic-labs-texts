A machine-learning approach to predict additional treatment after Bacillus Calmette-Guérin induction in non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer
Authors: Philippe Pinton, Haruna Kawano, Oliver Patschan, Arjun Ravi, Atsushi Nakano, Philippe Auvaro, Apurba Mukherjee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNon-muscle invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) comprises ~ 75% of newly diagnosed bladder cancer, with high-risk NMIBC associated with high rates of recurrence and progression. Nearly 40% of patients experience a lack of efficacy with gold standard Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) therapy and current methods for predicting BCG response are limited. This multicentre real-world study developed and evaluated machine learning (ML) models using data (April 2008–March 2024) from the Japan Medical Data Vision database to predict whether patients with NMIBC who received BCG induction would require additional treatment (cystectomy). In total, 7962 patients were identified based on NMIBC diagnosis and BCG. After processing, 1524 patients with 56 features were used for ML model development. Each ML model employed distinct feature selection, classification algorithms, and class imbalance strategies. Final ML models used either a nine-feature set plus repeat transurethral resection of bladder tumour (TURBT) obtained using a data-driven approach, or a clinically-informed eight-feature set plus repeat TURBT chosen for clinical relevance. Subsequent model performance suggested that factors other than feature selection, such as data imbalance, were key limitations. These findings demonstrate the feasibility of assembling a real-world dataset and performing exploratory ML modelling, although clinically meaningful prediction remains limited.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55917-8.Keywords: Non-muscle invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC), Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG), Machine learning, Treatment response prediction, Real-world data, Clinical decision supportSubject terms: Cancer, Oncology, Urology

Introduction

Bladder cancer is the ninth most common cancer and the thirteenth leading cause of cancer death globally1. In 2022, an estimated 614,298 new cases were diagnosed, and incidence is expected to increase1,2. Approximately 75% of newly diagnosed bladder cancer is non-muscle invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC). High-risk NMIBC is associated with high rates of recurrence after initial treatment and progression to muscle-invasive or metastatic disease3–5.

Transurethral resection of bladder tumour (TURBT) remains the primary approach for both diagnosis and initial treatment of bladder cancer, enabling tumour staging, grading, and symptom relief6. To reduce the risk of tumour recurrence and progression in patients with high-risk NMIBC, TURBT is typically followed by intravesical therapy such as Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG)6,7.

BCG has been the standard of care for high-risk NMIBC for the past two decades, reducing both recurrence and progression rates4. However, the therapeutic landscape for high-risk NMIBC has evolved substantially in recent years. Several agents have obtained regulatory approval for BCG-unresponsive disease, including pembrolizumab (anti-PD-1) approved in 2020 for patients with carcinoma in situ who are ineligible for or decline cystectomy, nadofaragene firadenovec (adenoviral IFN-α2b gene therapy) approved in 2022, and nogapendekin alfa inbakicept (N-803, IL-15 superagonist) approved in 20248,9. These developments highlight the need for improved risk stratification and early identification of patients who may require treatment escalation. BCG stimulates the immune system by inducing cytokine release and enhancing the cytotoxicity of T-cells and natural killer cells10,11. BCG induction consists of weekly bladder instillations for 6 weeks to stimulate a robust immune response against any residual cancer cells, although there is some variation internationally12. BCG induction in Japan is commonly administered as 6–8 weekly instillations in routine clinical practice, although the Japanese Urological Association (JUA) 2019 guideline itself recommends BCG induction followed by BCG maintenance for high‑risk NMIBC and does not specify a particular number of induction doses13,14. BCG maintenance involves additional bladder instillations every 3–6 months for 1–3 years to sustain the immune response and reduce the recurrence risk15,16.

Despite the utility of BCG, many patients discontinue treatment due to severe adverse effects17. In addition, nearly 40% of patients receiving BCG experience a lack of efficacy, necessitating further medical interventions18. “Recent systematic reviews confirm substantial variability in recurrence-free and progression-free survival after BCG in high-risk NMIBC, reflecting heterogeneity in treatment strategies and patient selection19. The duration of BCG is a critical factor influencing its efficacy, with clinical evidence showing that BCG maintenance yielded higher recurrence-free survival rates at five years20. A recent network meta-analysis also highlights persistent recurrence rates even with optimized BCG dosing strategies, underscoring the need for improved risk-stratification tools21.

Accurately predicting which patients will respond to BCG remains an important clinical challenge22–24. Current predictive models lack precision, but integrating machine learning (ML) with real-world data might offer a solution. Such an approach might also identify other clinical features that can predict the treatment response to BCG besides (for example) tumour stage, grade, size, and/or number of tumours. ML use in other cancers has shown promise in forecasting outcomes and predicting treatment responses25,26. The likely BCG response can be inferred from (but not directly measured by) whether additional treatment is required after BCG induction.

To evaluate the effectiveness of an ML model in predicting whether patients with NMIBC are likely to require additional treatment after BCG induction, a sufficiently large and representative dataset is required. The Japan Medical Data Vision (MDV) database is a widely used, commercially available, anonymised real-world dataset comprising administrative data from over 540 hospitals and 50 million patients in Japan27.

It includes detailed information on diagnoses, procedures, prescriptions, and laboratory results, while its longitudinal structure and extensive scale make it a useful resource for evaluating clinical decision-making and outcomes. The database is accessible through a licensing agreement27.

In Japan, the annual bladder cancer incidence is expected to increase from 25,132 cases in 2022 to 28,282 cases by 205028. The JUA guidelines set national standards for BCG in patients with NMIBC. In 2019, the JUA updated and expanded its recommendations to include both BCG induction and BCG maintenance for patients with intermediate- and high-risk NMIBC, due to the superior efficacy in preventing recurrence compared with BCG induction alone or intravesical chemotherapy14. BCG maintenance is recommended after 6–8 courses of BCG induction14. The BCG Tokyo strain is commonly used for this treatment29,30. Treatment patterns in the MDV database align with these recommendations.

This multicentre real-world study used MDV data to develop and evaluate multiple ML models. Each model was based on one of two distinct clinical feature selection strategies (data-driven or clinically-informed) and used different combinations of classifying algorithms (random forest [RF] or logistic regression [LR]) and class imbalance strategies (class weighting or synthetic minority over-sampling technique [SMOTE]). The aim was to predict whether patients in an NMIBC cohort who received BCG induction would subsequently require additional treatment, using cystectomy as a proxy for treatment escalation. This selection was made because cystectomy is a reliably coded and specific indicator of major treatment escalation in MDV. However, cystectomy is not a sensitive marker of biological BCG failure and is influenced by patient fitness, preferences, and institutional practice patterns.

Results

Patients with NMIBC in the MDV database and their characteristics

Inclusion and exclusion criteria for the NMIBC cohort are shown in Supplementary Fig. S1. Among the 275,051 patients with bladder cancer recorded in the MDV database between April 2008–March 2024, 257,430 had their diagnosis confirmed by an ICD-10 code (International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision). Of these, 39,070 patients had NMIBC. Both the annual number of newly recorded cases and the cumulative total of NMIBC cases in the MDV database have progressively risen (Supplementary Fig. S2).

Beginning with the complete group of 275,051 patients with bladder cancer in the MDV database, cohort selection criteria were applied to filter down to 11,818 patients with NMIBC who received BCG (Fig. 1). These were further subdivided into patients who had received complete BCG induction (n = 10,228 of 11,818; 87%) and patients who had received incomplete BCG induction (n = 1590 of 11,818; 13%). The complete BCG induction subgroup was further divided into patients who had received either complete intensive induction (n = 6226 of 10,228; 61%) or complete non-intensive induction (n = 4002 of 10,228; 39%).

Disposition of patients and clinical features for the ML model

Of the 11,818 patients in the NMIBC and BCG cohort, a total of 7962 patients and 98 clinical features were selected for the ML approach. The 98 features were selected because they were consistently available in the MDV dataset and captured demographics, comorbidities, laboratory parameters, and treatment history relevant to the clinical management of patients with NMIBC. The MDV database does not contain structured TNM staging or histopathology fields for bladder cancer, and no validated proxy exists to identify muscle-invasive (stage II+) disease. Therefore, exclusion of T2 + cases beyond the predefined criteria was not technically feasible. Although predefined exclusion criteria removed patients with procedure codes or diagnostic patterns strongly suggestive of muscle-invasive disease, residual misclassification is possible. A small number of patients with stage II or higher disease may remain due to coding inconsistencies or incomplete documentation in MDV, which does not provide structured TNM fields. The median follow-up period for the 7962 patients included in the ML analysis was 28 months, with an interquartile range (IQR) of 13–45 months. The 7962 patients were then filtered to generate two datasets for respectively training and testing the ML models (Fig. 2). First, 6398 patients were excluded due to missing laboratory records as these variables were essential for both feature selection strategies. Next, clinical features with > 40% missing data (n = 42 features) were excluded, with a 40% threshold chosen to balance data completeness with retention of clinically relevant variables, consistent with common practice in real-world database analyses. Patients with > 40% missing data (n = 40) were then excluded to ensure adequate feature completeness for reliable model training. The resulting dataset used for the ML approach comprised 1524 patients and 56 clinical features (Fig. 2). Continuous variables were standardised, categorical variables were one-hot encoded, and missing values within retained features were imputed using the median (continuous variables) or mode (categorical variables). Of the 1524 patients, 1490 were classified as receiving BCG induction without additional treatment (no cystectomy post BCG induction and/or first intravesical BCG), and 34 were classified as receiving BCG induction with additional treatment (cystectomy post BCG induction and/or first intravesical BCG). The patients were divided in a 70:30 ratio into a data training group (n = 1066; 24 patients classified as receiving BCG induction with additional treatment) and a test group (n = 458; 10 patients classified as receiving BCG induction with additional treatment) (Fig. 2). Baseline characteristics of the training and test groups were summarised descriptively. Because the split into training and test sets was generated randomly for model development rather than for inferential comparison, formal hypothesis testing between the two sets was not recommended and was therefore not performed. The distributions of baseline variables appeared comparable across the two subsets, consistent with stratified random sampling. Clinical feature selection for development of the ML approach

Two parallel approaches were used for feature selection on the baseline data of 56 clinical features: (1) a data-driven feature selection approach to identify the clinical features that were most strongly associated with the need for additional treatment (cystectomy) after BCG induction and (2) a clinically-informed feature selection approach to ensure variables commonly used in clinical practice were not overlooked and to compensate for the class imbalance in the dataset.

In the data-driven feature selection approach, highly correlated positive feature pairs (Spearman’s correlation coefficient ρ > 0.7) were filtered by removing the feature with more missing data. This led to the elimination of eight features, leaving 48 in total. Stratified five-fold cross-validation was then performed to prevent overfitting. Balanced RF was used to identify and rank the positive and negative predictors from the list of 48 features. For this, the correlation between each feature and the need for additional treatment (cystectomy) after BCG induction was computed. The importance of a feature was defined as its association with the need for additional treatment after BCG induction. The feature ‘repeat TURBT’ was defined strictly based on claims codes occurring prior to the index date. To address potential leakage, we performed a sensitivity analysis excluding repeat TURBT; results are provided (Supplementary Table S5).The nine clinical features with the highest contributions (importance of > 0.03), listed in descending order of importance as determined by the RF feature contribution plot, were total bilirubin, eosinophils, C-reactive protein, glucose, triglycerides, urea nitrogen (serum), cholinesterase, body-mass index (BMI), and monocytes (Fig. 3).

The second approach involved clinically-informed feature selection, as the data-driven feature selection method might overlook features of clinical importance or be overly affected by the imbalance in the dataset (1490 patients received BCG induction without additional treatment, while 34 received BCG induction with additional treatment). Repeat TURBT and additional eight clinical features from the baseline set of 56 were selected based on their prevalence in the literature: C-reactive protein, urea nitrogen (serum), estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), leukocyte count, age, smoking status, tumour-node-metastasis (TNM) cancer stage, and sex (Fig. 3). These eight clinically‑informed features are consistently reported in the NMIBC literature as predictors of recurrence, progression, or BCG response, including inflammation‑related markers (CRP, leukocyte count), renal function (eGFR, urea nitrogen), demographic factors (age, sex, smoking status), and tumour stage. Their relevance was supported by prior real‑world analyses such as Miyake et al. and by established prognostic frameworks described in international guidelines31,32. Two clinical features (C-reactive protein and urea nitrogen [serum]) were present in the outputs of both feature selection approaches.

Evaluation of the ML models

The aim of the ML models was to predict whether a patient with NMIBC who received BCG induction would require additional treatment (cystectomy). The models were developed using the training data (n = 1066) and subsequently assessed using the test data (n = 458). The data-driven and clinically-informed models both utilised (1) RF with class weighting, (2) RF with SMOTE, (3) LR with class weighting, and (4) LR with SMOTE. Hyperparameters for the RF and LR models were tuned using grid search within the stratified cross-validation framework. The performance of the eight models on the training data and the test data was compared (Table 2).

Both RF models using the nine clinical features identified in the data-driven approach plus repeat TURBT performed well on the training data but less well on the test data (Table 2, rows 1 and 2), as reflected in their low F1 scores, recall, precision, and the area under the precision-recall curve (PR AUC). The models achieved remarkably high accuracy (> 96%), but this likely reflects class imbalance as most patients in the dataset did not require additional treatment (Figs. 2 and 1490 not requiring additional treatment, 34 requiring additional treatment). The RF model with class weighting was outperformed by the RF model with SMOTE on the training data (F1 score of 0.53 compared with 0.99). The RF model with SMOTE showed near-perfect performance on the training data but showed severe overfitting, with an F1 score on the test data of 0.07. Both RF models had the area under receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.71 and PR AUC < 0.05, indicating that the models were biased toward the patients not requiring additional treatment (the majority class). LR with SMOTE demonstrated better performance than the RF models on the test data (F1 score 0.13, recall 1, and AUC 0.86; Table 2, row 4). While this represented an improvement, the precision remained extremely low (0.06), suggesting that the model predicted nearly all patients as requiring additional treatment. A similar trend towards improved performance in terms of F1 score, recall, and AUC was also observed with LR with class weighting compared with RF with class weighting (Table 2, rows 1 vs. 3).

The models using the eight clinically-informed features plus repeat TURBT performed similarly to the models using the nine features identified in the data-driven approach and displayed the same overall trends (Table 2, rows 5–8). Performance was much better on the training data (highest F1 score 0.98) than the test data (highest F1 score 0.16). Both the RF model with class weighting and the RF model with SMOTE achieved high AUC scores on the training data (0.97 and 1, respectively), indicating good overall discrimination (Table 2, rows 5–6). Despite this, the models still struggled to generalise to the patients requiring additional treatment, with exceptionally low respective PR AUC scores on the test data of 0.06 and 0.05.

Discussion

In this study, an ML approach using real-world data was used to predict whether patients with NMIBC who received BCG induction would subsequently undergo cystectomy as a marker of treatment escalation. Utilising two feature selection strategies for model development to balance predictive accuracy with practical utility in a clinical setting, this study provides some encouragement that ML models trained on real-world data might be able to predict whether patients with NMIBC will require BCG induction only, or if additional treatments (besides BCG maintenance) after BCG induction are needed.

The MDV database provided a large real-world dataset for developing and validating the ML models. Its large scale, clinical depth, and alignment with the JUA treatment guidelines for NMIBC made it well-suited for this study, particularly in ensuring the data were clinically representative and enabling longitudinal tracking of patient outcomes. However, limitations such as potential underrepresentation of non-Diagnosis Procedure Combination (DPC) hospitals, incomplete longitudinal data, and lack of certain clinical variables such as clinical response data should be considered when interpreting the results. In addition, because MDV does not include structured TNM staging, a small number of patients with possible stage II or higher disease may remain despite the exclusion criteria. This reflects known variability in claims-based coding and should be considered when interpreting the cohort composition. Future studies should incorporate electronic health records for BCG dosing/completion. The data (2008–2024) also encompass periods both before and after the JUA’s 2019 update and expansion of its treatment recommendations for patients with intermediate- and high-risk NMIBC14.

This study used a large, representative sample, with the initial cohort consisting of 11,818 patients with NMIBC treated with BCG. This substantial sample size reflects real-world clinical practice in Japan. The cohort of 1524 patients included a wide age range (median age 74 years), with 82% males, aligning with known demographic patterns of NMIBC (Supplementary Table S1). The detailed data on patient and clinical characteristics as well as treatment history supported robust feature selection and model development. Patients were stratified into complete-intensive, complete-non-intensive, and incomplete induction groups, enabling a more nuanced analysis of treatment patterns and outcomes. Overall, 27,250 patients with NMIBC were excluded for not receiving BCG, and 2 for unknown pregnancy (Fig. 1). While necessary, these exclusions may have introduced selection bias, thereby limiting applicability to all patients with NMIBC.

The treatment patterns observed in this study reflect high adherence to JUA guideline recommendations, which specify 6–8 instillations for BCG induction14: 87% of patients completed a course of ≥ 6 doses (Fig. 1). The number of BCG induction doses aligns closely with previous real-world data from Japan, specifically the Miyake et al. study of real-world patterns and clinical outcomes of Japanese patients with NMIBC receiving intravesical BCG31. The authors reported a median of eight doses in the complete induction cohort and four in the incomplete cohort, consistent with the findings presented here. Treatment definitions were comparable across the studies, underscoring the representative nature of this dataset and supporting the applicability of this model to clinical practice in Japan. The current study features a larger, more recent cohort with a higher rate of complete induction, potentially reflecting improved adherence or broader inclusion criteria.

This study additionally introduced ML-based stratification and predictive modelling. In the incomplete induction cohort, 13% of patients received fewer than six BCG doses. The median time to recurrence across the cohort was 58.9 months. Notably, patients with incomplete induction had a shorter median follow-up (24 months vs. 29 months in the complete cohort) and experienced a shorter time to recurrence (41.4 months vs. 60.4 months; Supplementary Fig. S4). This supports the hypothesis that incomplete induction is associated with worse clinical outcomes, suggesting that adherence to the complete regimen may delay disease recurrence and improve patient outcomes. The recurrence trends are consistent with those reported by Miyake at al.31, though the present study shows slightly shorter recurrence-free durations, possibly due to the shorter follow-up period compared with Miyake et al.

Following BCG induction, 37% of patients received TURBT alone, while 29% underwent TURBT combined with a second BCG course. A smaller proportion received chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or cystectomy, either as standalone treatments or in combination. These treatment patterns highlight that TURBT remains a cornerstone of post-induction management of NMIBC, and that BCG re-treatment is a common strategy in clinical practice. These real-world patterns are consistent with clinical guidelines and offer a solid foundation for predictive modelling. These observations align with recent evidence showing persistent variability in oncological outcomes after BCG, even in contemporary cohorts, underscoring the need for improved risk-stratification approaches19,21.

Almost 6400 patients (more than half of the BCG-treated cohort) were excluded from the ML analysis due to missing laboratory data (Fig. 2). This raises concerns about the completeness of the data and the potential for bias within the ML subset. Furthermore, the ML dataset exhibited a severe class imbalance: 1490 patients were classified as BCG induction without additional treatment (cystectomy) vs. 34 classified as BCG induction with additional treatment (cystectomy). This is not unusual for real-world data but limited minority class learning, evidenced in the low precision/recall scores despite AUC values of up to 0.86 (Table 2). Despite applying class weighting and SMOTE, the models struggled to generalise to the minority class, with consistently low PR AUC scores (Table 2). This imbalance, seen in the high accuracy scores despite the low scores in other metrics, likely skewed the development of the ML models and probably contributed to their substandard performance on the test data. The rarity of cystectomy in real-world NMIBC practice reflects true clinical epidemiology rather than a modelling artefact, and this structural imbalance inherently limits model learning despite the use of class weighting and SMOTE. This interpretation is further supported by the supplementary analyses. The confusion matrices and threshold-based metrics (Supplementary Table S7) show that even the best-performing model (LR + SMOTE) achieved perfect recall at the cost of extremely low precision, reflecting systematic over-prediction of the minority class. Calibration curves (Supplementary Figs. S5–S6) also demonstrated instability, consistent with the very small number of events in the test set (n = 10). Decision curve analyses (Supplementary Figs. S7–S8) confirmed that none of the models provided meaningful net benefit across clinically relevant threshold probabilities. Together, these findings indicate that the primary limitation was structural class imbalance rather than feature selection or model architecture.

The high prevalence of comorbidities within this patient population, such as 78% with other cancers and 66% with type 2 diabetes (Supplementary Table S2), may have influenced treatment adherence and outcomes. The missingness and imputation patterns (Supplementary Table S6) further illustrate the heterogeneity of laboratory data availability in real-world settings. Although imputation allowed model training, the high proportion of imputed values for several biochemical parameters likely contributed to noise and reduced discriminative performance. Furthermore, the presence of comorbidities and concomitant therapies likely influenced haematological and biochemical parameters, such as diabetes affecting glucose levels, autoimmune diseases influencing C-reactive protein, dialysis impacting eGFR, and the use of anti-inflammatory agents or corticosteroids altering leukocyte counts. These potential confounders were not explicitly adjusted for in this analysis. In addition, missing data in key variables such as smoking status (approximately 9% of patients with unknown status), may have reduced model robustness despite the use of imputation strategies. In addition, several of the top-ranked features identified by this model such as total bilirubin, urea nitrogen (serum), and C-reactive protein may not be directly associated with bladder cancer pathophysiology but rather reflect broader systemic processes such as inflammation and organ health. For instance, elevated bilirubin levels may indicate liver dysfunction33 or diseases generally associated with increased oxidative stress, inflammation, or metabolic disorders34, all of which can influence immune response and treatment tolerance. Similarly, C-reactive protein is a serum inflammatory biomarker, which has been shown to be associated with poor response to therapy when elevated35. GFR is used for evaluating kidney function and determining drug dosing requirements36. Although these associations are largely indirect, they may reflect underlying patient frailty or immune competence, suggesting the need for further investigation. In addition, key tumor-specific variables such as grade, CIS status, tumor size, multiplicity, and recurrence history are not available in MDV, which structurally limits biological interpretability.

The nine clinical features identified in the data-driven approach plus repeat TURBT were selected through a two-step ML pipeline using RF feature importance, representing the most statistically influential variables in the training data. Importantly, the sensitivity analysis excluding repeat TURBT (Supplementary Table S9) showed that model performance remained poor even when removing this potentially leakage-prone variable. Nevertheless, the potential for information leakage cannot be fully excluded. Repeat TURBT may reflect physician practice patterns, institutional protocols, or surveillance intensity rather than tumour biology. These factors could introduce confounding and should be interpreted with caution. This suggests that the limited predictive performance was not driven by leakage but by the underlying data structure. Top-ranked parameters (e.g., C-reactive protein, bilirubin, urea nitrogen, cholinesterase) likely reflect systemic inflammation and organ function rather than tumour biology per se. Such factors may influence clinical decision‑making (e.g., eligibility/tolerance for maintenance or re‑induction) and correlate with the proxy outcome (cystectomy). Given the claim’s nature of MDV, causal pathways cannot be inferred here, however. In contrast, the eight clinically-relevant features were chosen based on clinical judgment and established associations with NMIBC outcomes to ensure inclusion of features known to correlate with inflammation, renal function, cancer stage, and lifestyle37,38, and their availability and completeness in the dataset. These features, which are commonly reported in the literature as predictors of response to BCG, emphasized routinely available clinical and laboratory variables. This enhanced the applicability of the models in real-world settings by improving accessibility and interpretability; the absence of tumour-specific and molecular data within this model may have limited predictive performance, however.

The comparison between data-driven and clinically-informed feature selection strategies was intentionally included to explore the trade-off between statistical performance and clinical interpretability. The data-driven approach identified features with the highest predictive contribution based on RF importance scores. In contrast, the clinically-informed set was developed based on established relevance in the NMIBC literature and routine clinical availability. The set should not be regarded as an optimised collection of clinical variables with proven prognostic value however, as choices were limited to the set of 56 features available after filtering—their inclusion primarily reflects availability. Although the models developed using the clinically-informed features did not outperform those based on the data-driven set in terms of raw predictive metrics, they offered greater transparency and alignment with clinical practice, key factors for real-world adoption. This dual approach reflects the overarching aim of this study to balance predictive accuracy with practical utility in a clinical setting.

Neither the data-driven nor the clinically-informed set of features led to strong generalisation to the test data (Table 2). The consistently suboptimal performance, regardless of the feature set used, suggests that feature selection alone is not the primary limiting factor—rather, the challenges likely stem from the extreme class imbalance and limitations deriving from model architecture and a lack of established clinical and morphologic risk-factors. No clinical cut-offs were applied to determine parameter thresholds but could be added in the future for interpretability.

Overall, this study demonstrates the feasibility of assembling a large real-world cohort and performing exploratory ML modelling, but the predictive performance remains insufficient for clinical use. Future work incorporating tumor-specific variables and richer clinical data will be necessary to improve clinical relevance. Class weighting proved particularly effective in addressing dataset imbalance by enhancing the ability of the models to detect patterns in the minority class (patients requiring additional treatment). Similarly SMOTE helped balance the dataset without duplicating existing samples, thereby reducing the risk of overfitting while preserving the integrity of the majority class. However, despite applying both class weighting and SMOTE to address the data imbalance, the performance of the models on the test data remained suboptimal, especially in predicting outcomes for the minority class (patients requiring additional treatment; Table 2). The RF models showed evidence of overfitting, achieving high training scores with both class weighting and SMOTE but were unable to effectively generalise to the test data (Table 2, rows 1, 2, 5, 6). While LR models are less expressive than more complex algorithms, when combined with SMOTE they demonstrated slightly better recall and AUC on the test data (Table 2, rows 4 and 8). These findings suggest that, while RF is powerful, it may be less suitable than LR for highly imbalanced datasets without further tuning. In contrast, SMOTE, as a data-level oversampling approach, may offer more stable generalisation as seen in the results. However, neither method fully resolved the data imbalance. This highlights the need for models that may offer greater robustness or alternative imbalance strategies such as focal loss, more adaptive oversampling techniques (e.g., adaptive synthetic sampling), ensemble methods, or cost-sensitive learning approaches.

This study has several additional limitations. The model underwent only internal validation, leaving its applicability to external populations untested. To strengthen the findings, further prospective validation in a larger sample is required. In addition, several of the parameters identified as influencing the likelihood of additional treatment may have limited clinical relevance to bladder cancer pathophysiology and instead appear to reflect broader indicators of general health status (e.g., triglyceride levels). The model used the need for cystectomy as a high-specificity proxy for additional treatment, which may be an oversimplification. Although cystectomy represents a treatment escalation and therefore appears a reasonable proxy for non-response to BCG, it reflects a clinical decision rather than a direct measure of BCG treatment failure (the MDV dataset does not capture clinical decision‑making factors such as tumour grade at recurrence, progression to muscle‑invasive disease, patient preference, intolerance to intravesical therapy, or institutional practice patterns). Furthermore, cystectomy is only one of the additional treatments available and broader escalation pathways exist in practice (Supplementary Fig. S3). In the group of 1524 patients used in the ML approach, 1490 did not require cystectomy while 34 did, but 1064 had no additional treatments while 460 had some form of additional treatment. In turn, the use of additional treatment as a proxy for therapeutic response may not fully reflect clinical outcomes such as recurrence or disease progression. These generalisations could limit the clinical interpretability and applicability of the predictions. As the models are exploratory and intended to be hypothesis-generating rather than guiding clinical decision-making, they illustrate both the feasibility and limitations of ML-based prediction using administrative real-world data.

Conclusion

This exploratory analysis shows that ML models can be constructed from large real-world datasets to investigate treatment escalation after BCG induction, although their predictive performance remains limited by the structural constraints of claims data. These findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than clinically applicable. As richer datasets incorporating tumour-specific variables and post-treatment response information become available, more refined ML approaches may help unlock the full potential of real-world data for predictive modelling in NMIBC.

Methods

NMIBC cohort selection

Retrospective data on bladder cancer patients in the MDV database from April 2008–March 2024 were collected, based on NMIBC diagnosis (ICD-10 code) and BCG, with additional filtering based on TNM classification. Patients with muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC), defined as stage T2 or higher, N1+, or M1+, were systematically excluded during cohort selection based on TNM staging criteria (Version 8), using structured filters in the MDV database. In the first step, a broadly inclusive NMIBC cohort was identified using a wide range of early-stage tumour categories: T0, T0a, T0A, T0is, T1, T1a, T1a1, T1a2, T1b, T1b1, T1b2, T1c, T1c1, T1c2, T1c3, T1d, T1mi, T1mic, Ta, TA, Tis, TIS, Tis pd, Tis pu, Tis(DCIS), Tis(LCIS), Tis(Paget), Tispd, Tispu along with M0 and N0, ensuring all potential NMIBC cases were captured while excluding MIBC. This cohort was then refined using a second filter to include only patients with certain TNM categories (Ta, Tis, T1, T1a, T1a1, T1a2, T1b, T1b1, T1b2, T1c, T1c2, T1d, T1mi, T1mic) along with M0 and N0, aligning with the standard clinical definition of NMIBC. While a small number of patients (< 3%) in the final dataset were recorded as stage II or higher, these likely reflect residual coding inconsistencies or data entry errors rather than true MIBC. Given their low prevalence, these cases were not expected to materially impact the model’s performance or the conclusions of the study. Inclusion and exclusion criteria for the NMIBC cohort are shown in Supplementary Fig. S1.

Data on patient demographics were extracted from the discharge summary, a structured clinical document within the MDV database that summarises a patient’s hospital stay. The version recorded at or closest to the index date was used to ensure temporal relevance to the start of BCG.

Patient comorbidities and treatment history were collected at the index date or earlier. Laboratory values, such as peripheral blood haematology, biochemistry, coagulation, and immunology, were extracted at baseline, defined as the most recent available values within 30 days prior to the first BCG dose.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using Python (version 3.12.4) and standard machine‑learning libraries. Continuous variables were summarised using medians and IQRs, and categorical variables using counts and percentages. Patients were randomly assigned to the training and test sets using stratified sampling to preserve the proportion of patients who required additional treatment. Because this split was generated randomly, formal hypothesis testing to compare baseline characteristics between the two sets was not recommended and was therefore not performed.

Model performance was evaluated using accuracy, precision, recall, F1 score, the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), and the area under the precision–recall curve (PR AUC). Class imbalance was addressed using class weighting and the synthetic minority oversampling technique (SMOTE), depending on the model configuration. Internal validation was performed using stratified five‑fold cross‑validation on the training dataset.

Identifying key baseline features for predictive modelling

Patients included for the ML approach were selected from the full NMIBC cohort who received BCG (both complete BCG induction and incomplete BCG induction), based on the availability and completeness of clinical and laboratory data. Inclusion criteria for the ML approach were based on TNM classification, data completeness, and laboratory records. Clinical features and patients with > 40% missing data were sequentially excluded. This threshold was chosen to retain sufficient sample size while limiting the influence of imputation. Sensitivity checks at 30% and 50% yielded materially smaller training groups or excessive imputation, respectively; 40% was therefore adopted as a compromise. For the remaining clinical features, any missing values were imputed using median values. Median values were chosen to minimise the influence of outliers. Alternative imputers (k-Nearest Neighbours/Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations) were not used due to the severe class imbalances in the data (see Results). No clinical cut-offs were imposed.

The training data were used to develop the ML models, while the test data were utilised to evaluate their subsequent performance and whether they could generalise to new, unseen data.

Clinical feature selection

Clinical features (including lab values) were extracted at a single timepoint defined as the most recent available measurement < 30 days before first BCG dose. After exclusion of the features with > 40% missing data from the ML approach, two parallel strategies were followed to perform feature selection on the baseline data.

The first strategy was a data-driven clinical feature selection method aimed at identifying the clinical features with the strongest contribution to predicting the need for additional treatment (cystectomy) after BCG induction. This method involved a two-step process.

Step 1: Spearman’s correlation coefficient was used to measure the strength of association between pairs of clinical features. For feature pairs with a correlation coefficient > 0.7, the feature with more missing data was removed to mitigate multi-collinearity.

Step 1: Spearman’s correlation coefficient was used to measure the strength of association between pairs of clinical features. For feature pairs with a correlation coefficient > 0.7, the feature with more missing data was removed to mitigate multi-collinearity.

Step 2: A balanced-weight RF approach was then used for feature importance ranking to identify the clinical features that were most strongly associated with patients not requiring additional treatment. These features were selected for the prediction tool using the Python library SciKit®. No clinical cut-offs were applied to determine parameter thresholds. This approach balanced statistical robustness with data completeness, ensuring that highly correlated features did not bias the model. Stratified five-fold cross validation was performed on the training data to optimise model parameters and prevent overfitting. During this step, the model’s hyperparameters (including the number of trees, maximum tree depth, and the minimum number of samples required to split a node or to be a leaf node) were optimised using grid search to enhance predictive performance.

The second approach was a clinically-informed feature selection, designed to ensure that features commonly used in clinical practice were not overlooked by the data-driven methods. Clinically informed features were selected based on clinical relevance and prevalence in the published literature14,31,32. Priority was given to routinely-available laboratory variables.

Approach to handle imbalance during ML development

To mitigate biased model predictions deriving from unequal numbers of patients in the different BCG groups, two techniques (chosen for their interpretability and robustness in handling imbalanced datasets) were applied to address class imbalance: class weighting and SMOTE. Class weighting was applied to adjust the importance of each class in the model, ensuring the minority class received greater emphasis. In RF, the class weight parameter was set to ‘balanced’, allowing the algorithm to assign weights based on class frequency in the training data. In parallel, SMOTE was applied to generate synthetic samples for the minority class by interpolating between existing samples.

ML model evaluation

Both RF and LR modelling were employed in the data-driven and clinically-informed feature selection strategies, allowing a comparison of the different approaches. The RobustScalar method was used to scale the features for LR.

To evaluate the ML models, the following measures were reported for both the training and test data: accuracy, precision for the minority class (BCG induction with additional treatment cohort), recall for the minority class (BCG induction with additional treatment cohort), F1 score for the minority class (BCG induction with additional treatment cohort), PR AUC, and AUC.

The ML models were trained to predict whether a patient with NMIBC who received BCG induction would require additional treatment (cystectomy) after induction (BCG induction with additional treatment) or not (BCG induction without additional treatment), i.e., a binary outcome. Cystectomy was chosen as a proxy for additional treatment as it represents a substantial escalation.
