1  Relativistic equation of Motion

Let us first study the basics of the relativistic dynamics of a charge particle interacting with the ultra-intense laser pulse.

1.1  Lagrangian Formulation

The Lagrangian for a relativistic charged particle moving in an electromagnetic field is given by:

\mathcal{L}=-mc^{2}\sqrt{1-\frac{\mathbf{v}^{2}}{c^{2}}}+q\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{A}-q\phi,

(1)

where:

-

m
: Mass of the particle,

-

c
: Speed of light in vacuum,

-

\mathbf{v}
: Velocity of the particle,

-

q
: Electric charge of the particle,

-

\phi
: Scalar potential (electric potential),

-

\mathbf{A}
: Vector potential (associated with the magnetic field).

The term
-mc^{2}\sqrt{1-\frac{\mathbf{v}^{2}}{c^{2}}}
represents the relativistic kinetic term, derived from the proper time interval and the relativistic energy of the particle.
The term
q\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{A}
represents the interaction with the magnetic field (via the vector potential
\mathbf{A}
).
The term
-q\phi
represents the interaction with the electric field (via the scalar potential
\phi
).

The Euler-Lagrange equations derived from this Lagrangian yield the Lorentz force equation :

\frac{d\mathbf{p}}{dt}=q(\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}),

(2)

where:

•

\mathbf{p}=\gamma m\mathbf{v}
is the relativistic momentum,

•

\mathbf{E}=-\nabla\phi-\frac{\partial\mathbf{A}}{\partial t}
is the electric field,

•

\mathbf{B}=\nabla\times\mathbf{A}
is the magnetic field.

This formulation is especially useful in relativistic mechanics and field theory, as it respects the principles of Lorentz invariance and provides a compact way to describe the motion of a charged particle in electromagnetic fields.

1.2  Relativistic Lorentz force equation in covariant form

The relativistic Lorentz force equation in 4-vector notation (SI units) is:

\frac{dp^{\mu}}{d\tau}=qF^{\mu\nu}u_{\nu}

(3)

where:

•

p^{\mu}
is the 4-momentum of the particle:
p^{\mu}=\left(\mathcal{E}/c,\mathbf{p}\right)

•

\mathcal{E}=\gamma mc^{2}
being the total relativistic energy,

•

\mathbf{p}
the 3-momentum, and
c
the speed of light.

•

\tau
is the proper time of the particle.

•

q
is the charge of the particle.

•

F^{\mu\nu}
is the electromagnetic field tensor, encapsulating the electric field
\mathbf{E}
and the magnetic field
\mathbf{B}
:

F^{\mu\nu}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-E_{x}/c&-E_{y}/c&-E_{z}/c\\
E_{x}/c&0&-B_{z}&B_{y}\\
E_{y}/c&B_{z}&0&-B_{x}\\
E_{z}/c&-B_{y}&B_{x}&0\end{pmatrix}.

(4)

•

u_{\nu}
is the 4-velocity of the particle:
u_{\nu}=\gamma(c,-\mathbf{v})
, where
\gamma=1/\sqrt{1-v^{2}/c^{2}}
.

This equation describes the relativistic motion of a charged particle under the influence of electromagnetic fields. The term
qF^{\mu\nu}u_{\nu}
represents the force on the particle in 4-dimensional spacetime.

1.3  0th Component of the Lorentz Force

We are interested in the 0th component of the Lorentz force law, so we set
\mu=0
:

\frac{dp^{0}}{d\tau}=qF^{0\nu}u_{\nu}

(5)

Substitute the components of
F^{0\nu}
and
u_{\nu}
into the above equation .

From the field tensor
F^{\mu\nu}
, we know that:

F^{00}=0,\quad F^{01}=-\frac{E_{x}}{c},\quad F^{02}=-\frac{E_{y}}{c},\quad F^{03}=-\frac{E_{z}}{c}

(6)

Now, for the covariant 4-velocity:

u_{\nu}=\gamma(c,-\mathbf{v})

(7)

Thus, the components are:

u_{0}=\gamma c,\quad u_{1}=-\gamma v_{x},\quad u_{2}=-\gamma v_{y},\quad u_{3}=-\gamma v_{z}

(8)

Now, compute
F^{0\nu}u_{\nu}
:

F^{0\nu}u_{\nu}=F^{00}u_{0}+F^{01}u_{1}+F^{02}u_{2}+F^{03}u_{3}

(9)

Substituting the components:

F^{00}u_{0}=0\cdot\gamma c=0

(10)

F^{01}u_{1}=-\frac{E_{x}}{c}\cdot(-\gamma v_{x})=\gamma\frac{E_{x}v_{x}}{c}

(11)

F^{02}u_{2}=-\frac{E_{y}}{c}\cdot(-\gamma v_{y})=\gamma\frac{E_{y}v_{y}}{c}

(12)

F^{03}u_{3}=-\frac{E_{z}}{c}\cdot(-\gamma v_{z})=\gamma\frac{E_{z}v_{z}}{c}

(13)

Thus, we have:

F^{0\nu}u_{\nu}=\gamma\left(\frac{E_{x}v_{x}}{c}+\frac{E_{y}v_{y}}{c}+\frac{E_{z}v_{z}}{c}\right)

(14)

Substituting this expression into the Lorentz force equation :

\frac{dp^{0}}{d\tau}=q\gamma\left(\frac{E_{x}v_{x}}{c}+\frac{E_{y}v_{y}}{c}+\frac{E_{z}v_{z}}{c}\right)

(15)

This simplifies with
p^{0}=\mathcal{E}/c
:

\frac{1}{c}\frac{d\mathcal{E}}{d\tau}=q\gamma\left(\mathbf{E}\cdot\frac{\mathbf{v}}{c}\right)

(16)

\frac{d\mathcal{E}}{d\tau}=q\gamma\left(\mathbf{E}\cdot\mathbf{v}\right)\implies\gamma\frac{d\mathcal{E}}{dt}=q\gamma\left(\mathbf{E}\cdot\mathbf{v}\right)\implies\frac{d\mathcal{E}}{dt}=q\left(\mathbf{E}\cdot\mathbf{v}\right)

(17)

1.4  Interpretation

•

The left-hand side,
d\mathcal{E}/dt
, represents the rate of change of the particle’s energy.

•

The right-hand side represents the power delivered to the particle by the electric field. The term
\mathbf{E}\cdot\mathbf{v}
is the instantaneous power from the electric field.

2  Electromagnetic Fields of a Moving Charge

The electromagnetic fields of a moving charge, particularly an accelerating charge, are fully described using the
Lienard-Wiechert potentials
. These fields depend on the position, velocity, and acceleration of the charge and provide a complete solution to Maxwell’s equations for a moving point charge.

2.1  Lienard-Wiechert Potentials

The electromagnetic fields of a moving point charge are derived from the following potentials:

•

Scalar Potential (
\phi
)
:

\phi(\mathbf{r},t)=\frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}}\frac{1}{|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}(t_{r})|(1-\mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\beta})}

(18)

•

Vector Potential (
\mathbf{A}
)
:

\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{r},t)=\frac{q\boldsymbol{\beta}}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}c}\frac{1}{|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}(t_{r})|(1-\mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\beta})}

(19)

Here:

•

\mathbf{r}^{\prime}(t_{r})
is the position of the charge at the retarded time
t_{r}
(the time when the signal was emitted from the charge).

•

\boldsymbol{\beta}=\frac{\mathbf{v}}{c}
is the charge’s velocity in units of
c
.

•

\mathbf{n}=\frac{\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}{|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|}
is the unit vector from the charge to the observation point.

•

1-\mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\beta}
is the relativistic correction factor due to the motion of the charge.

2.2  Fields from Lienard-Wiechert Potentials

Using these potentials, the electric and magnetic fields of the moving charge are:

The total electric field is a combination of two terms: the
Coulomb-like field
and the
radiation field
.

\mathbf{E}=\frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}}\left[\frac{(1-\beta^{2})\mathbf{n}}{(1-\mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\beta})^{3}R^{2}}+\frac{\mathbf{n}\times\{(\mathbf{n}-\boldsymbol{\beta})\times\dot{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\}}{c(1-\mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\beta})^{3}R}\right]

(20)

Here:

•

The
first term
represents the Coulomb-like field, which depends on the velocity (
\boldsymbol{\beta}
) of the charge.

•

The
second term
represents the radiation field, which arises from the charge’s acceleration (
\dot{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
).

The magnetic field is related to the electric field as:

\mathbf{B}=\frac{\mathbf{n}\times\mathbf{E}}{c}

(21)

2.3  What is
R
?

In the context of the electromagnetic fields of a moving charge,
R
represents the
retarded distance
between the charge and the observation point. It is defined as:

R=|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}(t_{r})|

(22)

Explanation

1.

Observation Point (
\mathbf{r}
):

The position in space where the electromagnetic field is being measured or observed.

2.

Position of the Charge (
\mathbf{r}^{\prime}(t_{r})
):

The location of the charge at the
retarded time

t_{r}
, which accounts for the time it takes for the electromagnetic signal to propagate to the observation point at the speed of light.

3.

Retarded Distance:

The distance between the charge at its position at the retarded time (
\mathbf{r}^{\prime}(t_{r})
) and the observation point (
\mathbf{r}
).

Mathematically:

R=|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}(t_{r})|=\sqrt{(x-x^{\prime}(t_{r}))^{2}+(y-y^{\prime}(t_{r}))^{2}+(z-z^{\prime}(t_{r}))^{2}}.

(23)

Retarded Time (
t_{r}
)

The retarded time
t_{r}
is defined implicitly by the relation:

t_{r}=t-\frac{R}{c},

(24)

where:

•

t
is the time of observation,

•

c
is the speed of light.

This ensures causality, as the fields at the observation point depend on the charge’s state at an earlier time, accounting for the finite speed of electromagnetic signal propagation.

2.4  Physical Insights

1.

Near Field (Coulomb-like term):

•

Dominates at distances close to the charge.

•

Falls off as
1/R^{2}
.

•

Depends on the instantaneous velocity of the charge.

2.

Far Field (Radiation term):

•

Dominates at large distances.

•

Falls off as
1/R
, indicating radiative energy transfer.

•

Depends on the instantaneous acceleration of the charge.

3.

Angular Dependence:

•

The radiation field is strongest in directions perpendicular to the acceleration vector.

•

The fields are zero along the direction of motion for linear acceleration.

2.5  Special Cases

1.

Uniform Motion (
\mathbf{a}=0
):

•

If the charge moves at a constant velocity, there is no radiation field.

•

The fields simplify to a Coulomb-like field modified by relativistic effects (contracted in the direction of motion).

2.

Accelerated Motion (
\mathbf{a}\neq 0
):

•

Radiation fields appear.

•

The fields are time-dependent and carry energy away as electromagnetic waves.

3.

Oscillatory Motion:

•

Oscillating charges produce periodic radiation, such as dipole or quadrupole radiation patterns, depending on the symmetry of the motion.

3  Power Radiated Per Unit Solid Angle (Relativistic Case)

3.1  Relativistic Electromagnetic Field

The far-field electric field
\mathbf{E}_{\text{rad}}
for a relativistically moving charge
q
is given by:

\mathbf{E}_{\text{rad}}=\frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}c}\frac{\mathbf{n}\times\left[(\mathbf{n}-\boldsymbol{\beta})\times\dot{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\right]}{\left(1-\mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\beta}\right)^{3}R},

(25)

where:

•

\mathbf{n}
is the unit vector pointing from the charge to the observation point,

•

\boldsymbol{\beta}=\frac{\mathbf{v}}{c}
is the velocity of the charge (in units of
c
),

•

\dot{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\frac{\mathbf{a}}{c}
is the acceleration of the charge (in units of
c
),

•

1-\mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\beta}
is the Doppler factor, which accounts for relativistic corrections,

•

R
is the distance to the observation point.

The corresponding magnetic field is:

\mathbf{B}_{\text{rad}}=\frac{\mathbf{n}\times\mathbf{E}_{\text{rad}}}{c}.

(26)

3.2  Poynting Vector

The Poynting vector, representing the radiated energy flux per unit area, is:

\mathbf{S}=\frac{1}{\mu_{0}}(\mathbf{E}_{\text{rad}}\times\mathbf{B}_{\text{rad}}).

(27)

Substituting
\mathbf{B}_{\text{rad}}
into
\mathbf{S}
, we get:

\mathbf{S}=\frac{1}{\mu_{0}c}|\mathbf{E}_{\text{rad}}|^{2}\mathbf{n}.

(28)

The energy per unit area per unit time detected at the obsrevation point at time
t
when
the radiation is emitted at
t_{r}=t-R/c
.

[\mathbf{S\cdot\mathbf{n}]_{\text{ret}}}=\frac{1}{\mu_{0}c}|\mathbf{E}_{\text{rad}}|^{2}.

(29)

Substituting the relativistic
\mathbf{E}_{\text{rad}}
, we obtain:

|\mathbf{E}_{\text{rad}}|^{2}=\left(\frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}c}\right)^{2}\frac{|\mathbf{n}\times\left[(\mathbf{n}-\boldsymbol{\beta})\times\dot{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\right]|^{2}}{\left(1-\mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\beta}\right)^{6}R^{2}}.

(30)

The energy radiated per unit area from
t_{r1}
to
t_{r2}
is:

\mathcal{E}_{radiated}=\int_{t_{r1}}^{t_{r2}}[\mathbf{S\cdot\mathbf{n}]_{\text{ret}}}dt=\int_{t_{r1}}^{t_{r2}}[\mathbf{S\cdot\mathbf{n}]_{\text{ret}}}\frac{dt}{dt_{r}}dt_{r}

(31)

as
t_{r}=t-R/c\implies dt/dt_{r}=1-\mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\beta}
above equation reduces to (please note that
\mathbf{R}
is the vector from the source to the observation point, however when we are doing the measurement all the quantites are calculated in Lab frame and hence a negative sign will come in the expression):

\mathcal{E}_{radiated}=\int_{t_{r1}}^{t_{r2}}\Big\{[\mathbf{S\cdot\mathbf{n}]_{\text{ret}}}[1-\mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\beta}]\Big\}dt_{r}

(32)

Now the term in the
\{\cdots\}
is actually the power radiated per unit area.

3.3  Power Radiated Per Unit Solid Angle

From the above analysis the power radiated per unit solid angle (at retarded time) is related to the Poynting vector by:

\frac{dP(t_{r})}{d\Omega}=R^{2}|\mathbf{S}\cdot\mathbf{n}|_{\text{ret}}[1-\mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\beta}]\quad\quad[\because dA=R^{2}d\Omega=R^{2}\sin\theta d\theta d\phi].

(33)

Using the expression fo
r
|\mathbf{E}_{\text{rad}}|^{2}
, we get:

\frac{dP}{d\Omega}=\frac{1}{\mu_{0}c}\left(\frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}c}\right)^{2}\frac{|\mathbf{n}\times\left[(\mathbf{n}-\boldsymbol{\beta})\times\dot{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\right]|^{2}}{\left(1-\mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\beta}\right)^{5}}.

(34)

Simplifying further using
\mu_{0}c^{2}=\frac{1}{\epsilon_{0}}
:

\frac{dP(t_{r})}{d\Omega}=\frac{q^{2}}{16\pi^{2}\epsilon_{0}c}\frac{|\mathbf{n}\times[(\mathbf{n}-\boldsymbol{\beta})\times\dot{\boldsymbol{\beta}}]|^{2}}{\left(1-\mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\beta}\right)^{5}}.

(35)

However, for the power radiated per unit solid angle as evaluated at the time
t
is then given by:

\frac{dP(t)}{d\Omega}=\frac{q^{2}}{16\pi^{2}\epsilon_{0}c}\left|\frac{\mathbf{n}\times[(\mathbf{n}-\boldsymbol{\beta})\times\dot{\boldsymbol{\beta}}]}{\left(1-\mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\beta}\right)^{3}}\right|^{2}\equiv|V(t)|^{2}\quad\text{say}

(36)

3.4  Angular Dependence

The term
|\mathbf{n}\times[(\mathbf{n}-\boldsymbol{\beta})\times\dot{\boldsymbol{\beta}}]|^{2}
describes the angular dependence of the radiation:

•

The factor
\left(1-\mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\beta}\right)^{-5}
leads to strong collimation of radiation along the direction of motion (relativistic beaming).

•

The radiation is strongest perpendicular to the direction of acceleration, as in the non-relativistic case.

Figure 1:
Angular distribution of the power radiated for
\beta\ll 1
and
\beta\rightarrow 1
.

In the special case
\boldsymbol{\beta}||\boldsymbol{\dot{\beta}}
and
\mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\beta}=\beta\cos\theta
, the above equation simplifies to:

\boxed{\frac{dP(t_{r})}{d\Omega}=\frac{q^{2}\dot{\beta}^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}{16\pi^{2}\epsilon_{0}c(1-\beta\cos\theta)^{5}}}

(37)

From this expression it is clear that for non-relativistic cases
\beta\ll 1
the maximum power will be emitted along
\theta=\pi/2
, however for relativistic cases
\beta\sim 1
the radiation will be emitted along the direction of the electron motion.

3.5  Total power radiated : Larmor Formula

Total power radiated can be calcuated by integrating the
dP/d\Omega
as:

P(t_{r})=\frac{q^{2}\dot{\beta}^{2}}{16\pi^{2}\epsilon_{0}c}\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{\sin^{2}\theta}{(1-\beta\cos\theta)^{5}}\sin\theta d\theta=\frac{q^{2}\dot{\beta}^{2}}{16\pi^{2}\epsilon_{0}c}\times 2\pi\times\frac{4}{3}\frac{1}{(1-\beta^{2})^{3}}

(38)

As we know
\gamma=1/\sqrt{1-\beta^{2}}
and hence above equations simplifies to give us the total power radiated, which is refererred as the Larmor Formula.

P(t_{r})=\frac{q^{2}\dot{\beta}^{2}\gamma^{6}}{6\pi\epsilon_{0}c}=\frac{\mu_{0}q^{2}}{6\pi c}\dot{v}^{2}\gamma^{6}

(39)

4  Frequency distribution of the emitted radiation

Earlier we obtained the power radiated per unit solid angle as:

\frac{dP(t)}{d\Omega}=\frac{q^{2}}{16\pi^{2}\epsilon_{0}c}\left|\frac{\mathbf{n}\times[(\mathbf{n}-\boldsymbol{\beta})\times\dot{\boldsymbol{\beta}}]}{\left(1-\mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\beta}\right)^{3}}\right|^{2}\equiv|V(t)|^{2}\quad\text{say}

(40)

Hence the energy radiated per unit solid angle is then given by using Parseval’s theorem:

\frac{dW}{d\Omega}=\int|V(t)|^{2}\,dt=\int|\tilde{V}(\omega)|^{2}\,d\omega,

where
\tilde{V}(\omega)
is the Fourier transform of
V(t)
, defined as:

\tilde{V}(\omega)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int V(t)e^{i\omega t}\,dt.

V(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int\tilde{V}(\omega)e^{-i\omega t}\,d\omega.

The energy radiated per unit frequency per unit solid angle is then given by:

\frac{d^{2}I}{d\omega d\Omega}=|\tilde{V}(\omega)|^{2}.

The energy radiated per unit frequency and unit solid angle becomes after using previous expressions:

\frac{d^{2}I}{d\omega d\Omega}=\frac{q^{2}}{16\pi^{3}\epsilon_{0}c}\left|\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\hat{n}\times\left[(\hat{n}-\vec{\beta})\times\dot{\vec{\beta}}\right]}{(1-\hat{n}\cdot\vec{\beta})^{3}}e^{i\omega t}\,dt\right|^{2}.

(41)

Substituting,
t=t_{r}+R/c
and
\int dt=\int dt_{r}(dt/dt_{r})=\int(1-\hat{n}\cdot\vec{\beta})dt_{r}
the above expression simplifies to,

\frac{d^{2}I}{d\omega d\Omega}=\frac{q^{2}}{16\pi^{3}\epsilon_{0}c}\left|\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\hat{n}\times\left[(\hat{n}-\vec{\beta})\times\dot{\vec{\beta}}\right]}{(1-\hat{n}\cdot\vec{\beta})^{2}}e^{i\omega(t_{r}+R/c)}\,dt_{r}\right|^{2}.

(42)

4.1  Explanation of Terms

•

The cross-product
\hat{n}\times\left[(\hat{n}-\vec{\beta})\times\dot{\vec{\beta}}\right]
describes the angular and polarization dependence of the radiation.

•

The term
(1-\hat{n}\cdot\vec{\beta})^{2}
accounts for relativistic time dilation and Doppler shifts.

•

The exponential factor
e^{i\omega(t_{r}+R/c)}
introduces the frequency dependence, linking the particle’s motion to the observed radiation.

•

The integration over
t_{r}
connects the particle’s trajectory to the radiation field.

5  Introduction to the Radiation Reaction

Radiation reaction is a phenomenon that occurs when an accelerating charged particle emits electromagnetic radiation. As the particle radiates energy, it experiences a recoil force that alters its motion. This concept lies at the intersection of classical electrodynamics and modern physics and is essential for understanding processes involving high-energy particles, astrophysics, and accelerator physics.

5.1  When RR is important

As we saw previously, the total power radiated by the charge particle is given by:

P(t_{r})=\frac{\mu_{0}q^{2}}{6\pi c}a^{2}\gamma^{6}

(43)

where,
a
is the acceleration of the particle. The energy radiated by the charge particle in time
T
is then
given by:

\mathcal{E}_{rad}=\frac{\mu_{0}q^{2}}{6\pi c}a^{2}\gamma^{6}\quad T

(44)

If,
\mathcal{E}_{rad}\ll\mathcal{E}_{0}
where
\mathcal{E}_{0}
is the relevant energy scale of the particle then
in that case the RR is not relevant and one can ignore the same for all practical purposes. The specification of the relevant energy scale
\mathcal{E}_{0}
needs a small care. If we assume the particle is going through the continuous acceleration, in that case the energy of the particle after time
T
would be:

\mathcal{E}_{0}\sim m_{0}(aT)^{2}

(45)

Therefore, the RR is not important if :

\frac{\mu_{0}q^{2}}{6\pi c}a^{2}\gamma^{6}\quad T\ll m_{0}(aT)^{2}\implies T\gg\frac{\mu_{0}q^{2}}{6\pi cm_{0}}\gamma^{6}\equiv\tau

(46)

The characteristic time-scale where RR is important for
electron dynamics
is then given by:

\tau=\frac{\mu_{0}}{6\pi c}\frac{q^{2}}{m_{0}}\gamma^{6}\sim 6.27\times 10^{-24}\gamma^{6}\ \ \text{sec}

(47)

5.2  Radiation Reaction Force from Conservation of Energy

The radiation reaction force can be derived from the principle of conservation of energy, considering the power radiated as electromagnetic waves by an accelerating charge (Larmor power).

Radiated Power (Larmor Formula)

The power radiated by a non-relativistic charge is given by the Larmor formula:

P=\frac{\mu_{0}q^{2}a^{2}}{6\pi c}

(48)

where:

•

a
is the acceleration of the charge,

•

q
is the charge,

•

\mu_{0}
is the permeability of free space,

•

c
is the speed of light.

5.3  Work-Energy Principle and Abraham-Lorentz equation

The equation of motion with the RR is given by:

m\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{F}_{\text{ext}}+\mathbf{F}_{\text{rad}}

In order to determine the form of the
\mathbf{F}_{\text{rad}}
, we can say that the work done by this
force is equal to the negative of the energy radiated away:

The work done by a force is related to the rate of change of the particle’s kinetic energy:

\text{Work done by forces}=\text{Change in kinetic energy}.

(49)

\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\mathbf{F}_{\text{rad}}\cdot\mathbf{v}\ \ dt=-\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\frac{\mu_{0}q^{2}}{6\pi c}(\dot{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\dot{\mathbf{v}})\ \ dt=-\frac{\mu_{0}q^{2}}{6\pi c}\Big\{\big[\dot{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\mathbf{v}\big]_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}-\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\ddot{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\mathbf{v}\ dt\Big\}

(50)

For the circular motion or a periodic motion
\mathbf{\dot{v}}\perp\mathbf{v}
and hence the first term in the […] is zero. For periodic motion the value in the bracket would be same at
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
and hence it will be zero. Now comparing the RHS and LHS, the form of the
\mathbf{F}_{\text{rad}}
can be deduced as:

\mathbf{F}_{\text{rad}}=\frac{\mu_{0}q^{2}}{6\pi c}\frac{d\mathbf{a}}{dt}

(51)

where
d\mathbf{a}/dt
is the time derivative of acceleration, often called the “jerk.”

The radiated power represents the energy lost by the charge due to radiation. To conserve energy, this must come from an additional force opposing the motion, called the
radiation reaction force
(
\mathbf{F}_{\text{rad}}
).

•

The force depends on the rate of change of acceleration, which is a third-order time derivative of position.

•

It is very small in magnitude because the prefactor
\mu_{0}q^{2}/(6\pi c)
is extremely small, reflecting the fact that radiation reaction effects are typically negligible unless accelerations are very large (e.g., in particle accelerators or astrophysical processes).

Now, the equation of the motion will be then modified as:

\boxed{m(\mathbf{\dot{v}}-\tau\mathbf{\ddot{v}})=\mathbf{F}_{ext}}

where,
\tau=\mu_{0}e^{2}/(6\pi cm_{0})
for non-relativisitc case
\gamma\sim 1
. This equation is referred as the

Abraham-Lorentz equation
of motion which approximates the effect of RR.

The Abraham–Lorentz equation describes the radiation reaction force on a charged particle due to the electromagnetic radiation it emits when it is accelerated. This reaction arises because the particle loses energy to radiation, which must impact its motion.

5.4  Key Features of the Abraham–Lorentz equation

1.

Radiation Reaction Force
: The term
\frac{\mu_{0}q^{2}}{6\pi c}\dddot{\mathbf{x}}
represents the radiation reaction force. It shows that the particle’s own emission of radiation feeds back into its motion.

2.

Runaway Solutions
: The equation predicts unphysical “runaway" solutions, where the particle’s acceleration grows exponentially even in the absence of an external force. This is a fundamental issue of the equation in its classical form.

3.

Preacceleration
: Another issue is the prediction of “preacceleration," where the particle seems to anticipate an external force and begins moving before the force is applied.

5.5  What do we mean by Runaway Solutions and Pre-Acceleration?

As we learned the Abraham-Lorentz equation is given by:

m(\mathbf{\dot{v}}-\tau\mathbf{\ddot{v}})=\mathbf{F}_{ext}

Now, in the absence of any externally applied force
\mathbf{F}_{ext}=0
the above equation is modified as:

\mathbf{\dot{v}}-\tau\mathbf{\ddot{v}}=0\implies\frac{d\mathbf{a}}{dt}=\frac{\mathbf{a}}{\tau}\implies\mathbf{a}(t)=\mathbf{a_{0}}\text{e}^{t/\tau}

where,
\mathbf{a}_{0}
is the acceleration of the particle at
t=0
. The above equations shows that the acceleration of the particle
grows exponentially without application of any applied force, and hence it is referred as the “Runaway" solutions.

The Runaway solutions can be remedied by setting
\mathbf{a}_{0}=0
but it gives rise to the so called “Pre-Acceleation".

Example

With the inclusion of the radiation reaction force, Newton’s second law for a charged particle becomes

\mathbf{a}=\tau\dot{\mathbf{a}}+\frac{\mathbf{F}}{m},

(52)

where
\mathbf{F}=\mathbf{F}_{ext}
is the external force acting on the particle.

(a)
A particle is subjected to a constant force
F
, beginning at time
t=0
and lasting until time
T
. Find the most general solution
a(t)
to the equation of motion in each of the three periods:

(i)

t<0
,

(ii)

0<t<T
,

(iii)

t>T
.

The equation of motion is:

a-\tau\dot{a}=\frac{F}{m}.

(53)

This is a first-order linear differential equation . The general solution is:

a(t)=Ae^{t/\tau}+\frac{F}{m},

(54)

where
A
is an integration constant.

We now consider the three regions:

(i) Region
t<0
:
Before
t=0
, no force acts on the particle (
F=0
). The equation reduces to:

a-\tau\dot{a}=0.

(55)

The solution is:

a(t)=A_{1}e^{t/\tau},

(56)

where
A_{1}
is a constant.

(ii) Region
0<t<T
:
A constant force
F
acts on the particle. The equation is:

a-\tau\dot{a}=\frac{F}{m}.

(57)

The solution is:

a(t)=A_{2}e^{t/\tau}+\frac{F}{m},

(58)

where
A_{2}
is a constant.

(iii) Region
t>T
:
After
t=T
, the force is removed (
F=0
). The equation reduces to:

a-\tau\dot{a}=0.

(59)

The solution is:

a(t)=A_{3}e^{t/\tau},

(60)

where
A_{3}
is a constant.

(b)
Impose the continuity condition
a(t)
at
t=0
and
t=T
. Show that you can
either
eliminate the runaway in region (iii)
or
avoid preacceleration in region (i), but not both.

At
t=0
:
The acceleration
a(t)
must be continuous, so:

A_{1}e^{0/\tau}=A_{2}e^{0/\tau}+\frac{F}{m}.

(61)

Simplifying:

A_{1}=A_{2}+\frac{F}{m}.

(62)

At
t=T
:
The acceleration
a(t)
must again be continuous:

A_{2}e^{T/\tau}+\frac{F}{m}=A_{3}e^{T/\tau}.

(63)

Simplifying:

A_{3}=A_{2}+\frac{F}{m}e^{-T/\tau}.

(64)

Runaway and Preacceleration:

- Runaway: If
A_{3}\neq 0
, the acceleration
a(t)
for
t>T
diverges exponentially as
t\to\infty
.
- Preacceleration: If
A_{1}\neq 0
, there is nonzero acceleration before the force is applied (
t<0
).

To avoid these behaviors:
- Eliminate runaway (
t>T
): Set
A_{3}=0
, which gives:

A_{2}=-\frac{F}{m}e^{-T/\tau}.

(65)

- Avoid preacceleration (
t<0
): Set
A_{1}=0
, which gives:

A_{2}=-\frac{F}{m}.

(66)

However, both conditions cannot be satisfied simultaneously, as they impose conflicting requirements on
A_{2}
. This highlights the paradox of the radiation reaction force.

Velocity in the Three Regions

To calculate the velocity
v(t)
in each region, we integrate the acceleration
a(t)
over time.

Region (i):
t<0

In this region,
F=0
, so the acceleration is given by:

a(t)=A_{1}e^{t/\tau}.

(67)

The velocity
v(t)
is obtained by integrating
a(t)
:

v(t)=\int a(t)\,dt=\int A_{1}e^{t/\tau}\,dt.

(68)

This gives:

v(t)=A_{1}\tau e^{t/\tau}+C_{1},

(69)

where
C_{1}
is a constant of integration.

Region (ii):
0<t<T

In this region,
F\neq 0
, so the acceleration is:

a(t)=A_{2}e^{t/\tau}+\frac{F}{m}.

(70)

Integrating
a(t)
to find
v(t)
:

v(t)=\int\left(A_{2}e^{t/\tau}+\frac{F}{m}\right)\,dt.

(71)

This gives:

v(t)=A_{2}\tau e^{t/\tau}+\frac{F}{m}t+C_{2},

(72)

where
C_{2}
is a constant of integration.

Region (iii):
t>T

In this region,
F=0
, so the acceleration is:

a(t)=A_{3}e^{t/\tau}.

(73)

The velocity
v(t)
is:

v(t)=\int a(t)\,dt=\int A_{3}e^{t/\tau}\,dt.

(74)

This gives:

v(t)=A_{3}\tau e^{t/\tau}+C_{3},

(75)

where
C_{3}
is a constant of integration.

It should be noted that for the uncharged particle
\tau=0
and hence we retrieve the Newton’s equation
of motion.

5.6  Lorentz-Abraham-Dirac (LAD) equation

The Lorentz-Abraham-Dirac (LAD) equation describes the motion of a charged particle under the influence of external forces and its self-radiation (radiation reaction). The equation is derived from classical electrodynamics and models the recoil force on a charged particle as it radiates energy due to acceleration. This LAD equation is the relativistic generalization of the Abraham-Lorentz equation.

LAD Equation : Relativistic Form of the Abraham-Lorentz equation

Earlier we obtained the non-relativistic version of the RR as is given as:

m\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{F}_{\text{ext}}+\mathbf{F}_{\text{rad}}

(76)

where,

\mathbf{F}_{\text{rad}}=\frac{q^{2}}{6\pi\epsilon_{0}c^{3}}\frac{d\mathbf{a}}{dt}

(77)

The relativistic equation of motion can then be written as:

m\frac{du^{\mu}}{d\tau}=F^{\mu}_{\text{ext}}+F^{\mu}_{\text{rad}}

(78)

It should be more general and hence in the limit
v\ll c
we should get results from AL equation. It can be easily proved that in the non-relativistic limit the expression,

F^{\mu}_{\text{rad}}=\frac{q^{2}}{6\pi\epsilon_{0}c^{3}}g^{\mu}\quad;\quad g^{\mu}\equiv\frac{d^{2}u^{\mu}}{d\tau^{2}}

(79)

is same as equation
77
.

In the relativistic scenario the 4-acceleration (force) should always be orthogonal to the 4-velocity i.e.
a^{\mu}u_{\mu}=0
. This comes from the fact that
u^{\mu}u_{\mu}=c^{2}
and hence

\frac{d(u^{\mu}u_{\mu})}{d\tau}=u_{\mu}\frac{du^{\mu}}{d\tau}+u^{\mu}\frac{du_{\mu}}{d\tau}=2a^{\mu}u_{\mu}=0

(80)

However, the
g^{\mu}
as defined is not satisfying this condition, and hence we can modify the
g^{\mu}
as:

g^{\mu}=\frac{d^{2}u^{\mu}}{d\tau^{2}}+\delta u^{\mu}

(81)

and then demand
g^{\mu}u_{\mu}=0

g^{\mu}u_{\mu}=\frac{d^{2}u^{\mu}}{d\tau^{2}}u_{\mu}+\delta u^{\mu}u_{\mu}=0

(82)

\delta=-\frac{1}{c^{2}}\frac{d^{2}u^{\nu}}{d\tau^{2}}u_{\nu}

(83)

where,
u^{\nu}u_{\nu}=c^{2}
is used. Let us simplify this further and consider:

\frac{d}{d\tau}\Big(u_{\nu}\frac{du^{\nu}}{d\tau}\Big)=u_{\nu}\frac{d^{2}u^{\nu}}{d\tau^{2}}+\frac{du_{\nu}}{d\tau}\frac{du^{\nu}}{d\tau}=0

(84)

because
a^{\nu}u_{\nu}=0
is anyway satisfied (LHS of above equation), this implies:

\frac{1}{c^{2}}\frac{du_{\nu}}{d\tau}\frac{du^{\nu}}{d\tau}=-\frac{1}{c^{2}}u_{\nu}\frac{d^{2}u^{\nu}}{d\tau^{2}}=\delta

(85)

In the relativistic framework, the equation is then finally written as:

m\frac{du^{\mu}}{d\tau}=F^{\mu}_{\text{ext}}+\frac{q^{2}}{6\pi\epsilon_{0}c^{3}}\left(\frac{d^{2}u^{\mu}}{d\tau^{2}}+\frac{u^{\mu}}{c^{2}}\frac{du_{\nu}}{d\tau}\frac{du^{\nu}}{d\tau}\right),

(86)

where:

•

m
is the rest mass of the particle,

•

q
is the charge of the particle,

•

u^{\mu}=\gamma(c,\vec{v})
is the 4-velocity (
\gamma
is the Lorentz factor),

•

\tau
is the proper time of the particle,

•

F^{\mu}_{\text{ext}}
is the external 4-force acting on the particle.

The radiation reaction term consists of:

1.

\frac{d^{2}u^{\mu}}{d\tau^{2}}
, which accounts for changes in the particle’s acceleration due to radiated energy.

2.

Projection term:

u^{\mu}\frac{du_{\nu}}{d\tau}\frac{du^{\nu}}{d\tau}
, which ensures orthogonality of the 4-velocity and 4-acceleration, maintaining consistency with relativity.

Non-Relativistic Limit

The non-relativistic limit of the Lorentz-Abraham-Dirac (LAD) equation is achieved by simplifying the relativistic form under the assumption that the particle’s velocity
v
is much smaller than the speed of light
c
(
v\ll c
).

The relativistic LAD equation is:

m\frac{du^{\mu}}{d\tau}=F^{\mu}_{\text{ext}}+\frac{q^{2}}{6\pi\epsilon_{0}c^{3}}\left(\frac{d^{2}u^{\mu}}{d\tau^{2}}+\frac{u^{\mu}}{c^{2}}\frac{du_{\nu}}{d\tau}\frac{du^{\nu}}{d\tau}\right),

(87)

where:

•

u^{\mu}=\gamma(c,\vec{v})
is the 4-velocity,

•

\frac{du^{\mu}}{d\tau}
is the 4-acceleration,

•

\tau
is the proper time.

The two main steps to achieve the non-relativistic limit involve simplifying:

1.

Proper time
\tau
in terms of coordinate time
t
,

2.

Components of the 4-velocity
u^{\mu}
and 4-acceleration
\frac{du^{\mu}}{d\tau}
.

The proper time
\tau
relates to the coordinate time
t
via the Lorentz factor
\gamma
:

d\tau=\frac{dt}{\gamma},\quad\gamma=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-v^{2}/c^{2}}}.

(88)

In the non-relativistic limit (
v\ll c
):

\gamma\approx 1,\quad d\tau\approx dt.

(89)

This allows us to replace derivatives with respect to
\tau
with derivatives with respect to
t
:

\frac{d}{d\tau}\approx\frac{d}{dt}.

(90)

The 4-velocity
u^{\mu}=(\gamma c,\gamma\vec{v})
simplifies in the non-relativistic limit:

u^{\mu}\approx(c,\vec{v}),

(91)

since
\gamma\approx 1
.

The 4-acceleration
\frac{du^{\mu}}{d\tau}
has components:

\frac{du^{\mu}}{d\tau}=\left(\frac{d(\gamma c)}{d\tau},\frac{d(\gamma\vec{v})}{d\tau}\right).

(92)

For
v\ll c
, we approximate:

•

The temporal component
\frac{d(\gamma c)}{d\tau}
becomes negligible.

•

The spatial component reduces to the usual 3-acceleration,
\frac{d\vec{v}}{dt}
.

The radiation reaction term in the relativistic LAD equation is:

F^{\mu}_{\text{rad}}=\frac{q^{2}}{6\pi\epsilon_{0}c^{3}}\left(\frac{d^{2}u^{\mu}}{d\tau^{2}}+\frac{u^{\mu}}{c^{2}}\frac{du_{\nu}}{d\tau}\frac{du^{\nu}}{d\tau}\right)

(93)

In the non-relativistic limit:

1.

The dominant contribution comes from
\frac{d^{2}u^{\mu}}{d\tau^{2}}
, as the projection term
u^{\mu}\frac{du_{\nu}}{d\tau}\frac{du^{\nu}}{d\tau}
involves
v^{2}/c^{2}
, which is small and can be neglected.

2.

The spatial component of
\frac{d^{2}u^{\mu}}{d\tau^{2}}
simplifies to the second derivative of velocity,
\frac{d^{2}\vec{v}}{dt^{2}}
.

Thus, the radiation reaction force simplifies to:

\vec{F}_{\text{rad}}=\frac{q^{2}}{6\pi\epsilon_{0}c^{3}}\frac{d^{2}\vec{v}}{dt^{2}}.

(94)

Final Non-Relativistic LAD equation

Substituting the simplified expressions into the relativistic LAD equation yields:

m\frac{d\vec{v}}{dt}=\vec{F}_{\text{ext}}+\frac{q^{2}}{6\pi\epsilon_{0}c^{3}}\frac{d^{2}\vec{v}}{dt^{2}}.

(95)

This is the non-relativistic form of the LAD equation which is nothing but the
Abraham-Lorentz equation
, where:

•

The first term
\vec{F}_{\text{ext}}
is the external force,

•

The second term accounts for the radiation reaction.

•

LAD equation is the relativistic generalization of the Abraham-Lorentz equation of motion, and hence have the same limitations of Runaway/Pre-Acceleration solutions.

Key Challenges

1.

Runaway Solutions:

The LAD equation permits solutions where the particle’s acceleration grows exponentially even in the absence of external forces, which is unphysical.

2.

Preacceleration:

The equation predicts that a particle may start accelerating before the external force is applied, violating causality.

3.

Third-Order Nature:

The presence of a third time derivative (
\frac{d^{3}x}{dt^{3}}
) makes the LAD equation challenging to interpret as a standard Newtonian equation of motion.

5.7  Resolution: Landau-Lifshitz Approximation

To address the problems of the LAD equation , the
Landau-Lifshitz equation
is often used. It approximates the radiation reaction force by treating it perturbatively. This removes the runaway and preacceleration problems while maintaining agreement with the LAD equation in physical scenarios. They realized that if the second (RR) term were much smaller than the first in the instantaneous rest frame of the charge, it would be possible to reduce the order of the LAD equation by substituting
\frac{du}{d\tau}\to qF^{\mu\nu}u_{\nu}/m
in the RR term. The result, called the Landau–Lifshitz equation , is first-order in the electron momentum and free from the pathological solutions of the LAD equation
Burton and Noble 2014
.

As we know the LAD equation is given as:

m\frac{du^{\mu}}{d\tau}=F^{\mu}_{\text{ext}}+\frac{q^{2}}{6\pi\epsilon_{0}c^{3}}\left(\frac{d^{2}u^{\mu}}{d\tau^{2}}+\frac{u^{\mu}}{c^{2}}\frac{du_{\nu}}{d\tau}\frac{du^{\nu}}{d\tau}\right),

(96)

m\frac{du^{\mu}}{d\tau}=F^{\mu}_{\text{ext}}+\frac{q^{2}}{6\pi\epsilon_{0}c^{3}}\frac{d^{2}u^{\mu}}{d\tau^{2}}+\frac{u^{\mu}}{c^{2}}\boxed{\frac{q^{2}}{6\pi\epsilon_{0}c^{3}}\frac{du_{\nu}}{d\tau}\frac{du^{\nu}}{d\tau}},

(97)

The term in the box is actually the Power radiated or in another word the amount of the energy lost and hence can be written as:

\frac{d\mathcal{E}}{dt}=-\frac{q^{2}}{6\pi\epsilon_{0}c^{3}}a^{\mu}a_{\mu}

where,
a^{\mu}=du^{\mu}/d\tau
is the four-acceleration. So the term in the box makes sense as the main damping term.

5.7.1
Classical Electron Radius and Compton Wavelength

The classical electron radius
r_{e}
is a quantity that characterizes the effective size of the electron in classical electrodynamics, derived by equating the electron’s electrostatic potential energy with its rest energy. It represents the scale at which classical electrodynamics reaches its limits for describing the electron, as it assumes a point particle with an associated electric field.

The classical electron radius
r_{e}
is derived by equating the electron’s electrostatic potential energy to its rest energy. The rest energy of an electron is given by:

E_{\text{rest}}=m_{e}c^{2},

(98)

where
m_{e}
is the electron’s rest mass and
c
is the speed of light. The electrostatic potential energy of a uniformly charged sphere with radius
r_{e}
and charge
e
is:

U_{\text{electrostatic}}=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}}\frac{e^{2}}{r_{e}}.

(99)

Equating these two energies gives:

m_{e}c^{2}=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}}\frac{e^{2}}{r_{e}}.

(100)

Solving for
r_{e}
, we get:

r_{e}=\frac{e^{2}}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}m_{e}c^{2}}.

(101)

Substituting known constants:

r_{e}\approx 2.817\times 10^{-15}\,\text{m}.

(102)

The classical electron radius represents a characteristic length scale for the electron’s electric field, and is used in classical theories like Thomson scattering.

The Compton wavelength
\lambda_{C}
is a quantum mechanical property of a particle. It is the wavelength of a photon whose energy is equal to the rest energy of the particle. The Compton wavelength gives a scale below which quantum effects become significant in describing a particle:

\lambda_{C}=\frac{\hbar}{m_{e}c}\approx 3.8616\times 10^{-13}\,\text{m}.

(103)

The Compton wavelength
\lambda_{C}
is about 2 order larger than the classical electron radius
r_{e}
:

\frac{\lambda_{C}}{r_{e}}\approx\frac{3.8616\times 10^{-13}}{2.817\times 10^{-15}}\approx 137=\frac{1}{\alpha}.

(104)

5.7.2
Classical and Quantum Critical fields

The
Classical Critical Field
refers to the electromagnetic field strength at which the energy of the electromagnetic field acting on a charged particle becomes comparable to the rest energy of the particle. This is a critical threshold in classical electrodynamics, indicating when the particle can no longer be treated as a non-relativistic point particle without significant relativistic effects.

The critical field is given by the expression:

E_{\text{crit}}=\frac{m_{e}c^{2}}{er_{e}}

(105)

Interpretation
:

•

Field Strength
: The critical field
E_{\text{crit}}
represents the strength of an external electric field at which the energy associated with the field acting on a particle becomes comparable to the rest energy of the particle itself. This implies that the particle would begin to exhibit significant relativistic or quantum mechanical behavior if subjected to such a field.

•

Physical Meaning
: If a particle with charge
e
(such as an electron) is placed in an electric field of strength
E_{\text{crit}}
, the energy gained by the particle due to the field is equal to the rest energy of the particle. This is the point at which the effects of the field become comparable to the particle’s intrinsic energy, and classical treatment breaks down.

Substituting the classical electron radius
r_{e}\approx 2.817\times 10^{-15}\,\text{m}
into the expression for
E_{\text{crit}}
gives the
Classical critical field
value:

E_{\text{crit}}=\frac{m_{e}c^{2}}{er_{e}}\approx\,1.813\times 10^{20}\ \text{V/m}\approx 1.813\times 10^{18}\ \text{V/cm}.

(106)

Similary, the
Quantum Critical field
is defined as:

E_{\text{qm,crit}}=\frac{m_{e}c^{2}}{e\lambda_{C}}\approx 1.32\times 10^{18}\ \text{V/m}\approx 1.32\times 10^{16}\ \text{V/cm}

(107)

where,
\lambda_{C}=\hbar/m_{e}c
is the Compton wavelength.

5.7.3
Argument by Landau-Lifshitz

The Landau and Lifshitz showed that if the electric field experieced by the electron in it’s instanteneous rest frame is smaller than the Classical Critical field, i.e.
|E_{IRF}|\ll E_{crit}
then in such scenarios the Radiation Reaction can be considered as the small perturbation on the zeroth order term, implying:

\frac{du^{\mu}}{ds}\approx\frac{F^{\mu}_{\text{ext}}}{m_{e}}

So we again rewrite the LAD equation as:

m_{e}\frac{du^{\mu}}{ds}=F^{\mu}_{\text{ext}}+m_{e}\tau\left(\frac{d^{2}u^{\mu}}{ds^{2}}+\frac{u^{\mu}}{c^{2}}\frac{du_{\nu}}{ds}\frac{du^{\nu}}{ds}\right),

(108)

and using the LL approximation it can be written as:

m_{e}\frac{du^{\mu}}{ds}=F^{\mu}_{\text{ext}}+\tau\frac{dF^{\mu}_{\text{ext}}}{ds}+\mathcal{O}(\tau^{2})

(109)

Here,
\tau=q^{2}/(6\pi\epsilon_{0}c^{3}m_{e})\approx 6.26\times 10^{-24}\ \text{sec}
is the characteristic time and
s
is the proper time. One can ignore the
\mathcal{O}(\tau^{2})
as this term will be even smaller that the quantum-corrections. Anyways the Classical critical field is itself smaller than 2 order of magnitude from the quantum critical field, so in the purview of the classical ED we are anyway ignoring the terms which are way below the quantum limit. In this sense the LL equation is not the approximation but rather the classically exact solution for the radiation reaction.

So if we substitute
F^{\mu}_{ext}=qF^{\mu\nu}u_{\nu}
for the Lorentz force responsible for the motion of the electron in the intense electromagnetic field, then eventually the LL equation will be then given by after neglecting
\mathcal{O}(\tau^{2})
as:

m_{e}\frac{du^{\mu}}{d\tau}=qF^{\mu\nu}u_{\nu}+\tau\left[\frac{q}{m_{e}}(\partial_{\alpha}F^{\mu\nu})u^{\alpha}u_{\nu}-\frac{q^{2}}{m_{e}^{2}}F^{\mu\nu}F_{\alpha\nu}u^{\alpha}+\frac{q^{2}}{m_{e}^{2}c^{2}}(F^{\alpha\nu}u_{\nu})(F_{\alpha\lambda}u^{\lambda})u^{\mu}\right]

(110)

It should be noted that the though the LL equation is an approximation but the difference between LAD and LL equations are smaller than the quantum effects which we have already neglected. In that sense classicaly it is an exact equation of motion which takes into account the Radiation Reaction without any issues of “Runaway” solutions, i.e. in the absence of the external force all the RHS becomes zero and we have just a particle moving with constant velocity.

We will be now looking at the numerical examples and for that it is better to define dimensionless units.

6.1  Normalized variables

The typical Lorentz force equation is written as:

m\frac{d^{2}\mathbf{r}}{dt^{2}}=q(\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B})

We can make this equation dimensionless as:

•

\beta=v/c
: velocity

•

m^{\prime}=m/m_{e}
: mass

•

q^{\prime}=q/e
: charge

•

r^{\prime}=kr
: position

•

t^{\prime}=\omega t
: time (
\omega=kc
is frequency of laser)

•

p^{\prime}=p/m_{e}c
: momentum

•

a_{0}=eE_{0}/m_{e}\omega c=eB_{0}/m_{e}\omega
: dimensionless electric and magnetic field amplitude

•

\varphi=e\phi/m_{e}c^{2}
: scalar potential

•

a_{0}=eA_{0}/m_{e}c
: vector potential

6.2  Intensity normalization

The intensity
I
is related to the peak electric field
E_{\text{peak}}
through the Poynting vector:

\mathbf{S}=\frac{1}{\mu_{0}}\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{B}

(111)

Since
|\mathbf{B}|=|\mathbf{E}|/c
, we have:

|\mathbf{S}|=\frac{1}{\mu_{0}c}E_{\text{peak}}^{2}

(112)

The time-averaged intensity is:

I_{0}=\langle I\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{\mu_{0}c}E_{\text{peak}}^{2}

(113)

I_{0}=\langle I\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{\mu_{0}c}\left(\frac{a_{0}m_{e}c\omega}{e}\right)^{2}

(114)

Now, using
\omega=\frac{2\pi c}{\lambda}
:

I_{0}=\langle I\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{\mu_{0}c}\left(\frac{a_{0}m_{e}c\frac{2\pi c}{\lambda}}{e}\right)^{2}

(115)

Simplifying:

I_{0}=\frac{a_{0}^{2}m_{e}^{2}c^{3}(2\pi)^{2}}{2e^{2}\mu_{0}\lambda^{2}}\implies I_{0}\lambda^{2}=\Big(\frac{m_{e}^{2}c^{3}(2\pi)^{2}}{2e^{2}\mu_{0}}\Big)a_{0}^{2}\ \quad\text{W/m}^{2}\ \text{m}^{2}

(116)

I_{0}\lambda^{2}=10^{-4}10^{12}\Big(\frac{m_{e}^{2}c^{3}(2\pi)^{2}}{2e^{2}\mu_{0}}\Big)a_{0}^{2}\quad\ \text{W/cm}^{2}\ \mu\text{m}^{2}\approx 1.3681671\times 10^{18}\ \ a_{0}^{2}\quad\ \text{W/cm}^{2}\ \mu\text{m}^{2}

(117)

If
a_{0}>1
, relativistic dynamics will play an important role. Working with dimensionless units is very useful; not only can we get rid of numerical errors during calculations, but the problem also becomes scalable. For example, if the wavelength of the laser is varied, the main characteristics of the problem will scale accordingly.

7  Fig 8 Trajectory Problem

Now let us consider the interaction of an electron with a linearly polarized plane pulse, whose vector potential is given by:

\mathbf{A}=a_{0}\cos(\phi)~~\hat{x}

where,
\phi=\omega t-kz
is the phase. We have not considered any field envelope at this moment to keep the derivation simple to follow.

The corresponding electric and magnetic fields of the laser pulse can be obtained to be:

\mathbf{E}=-\frac{\partial\mathbf{A}}{\partial t}=a_{0}\omega\sin(\phi)~~\hat{x}\quad;\quad\mathbf{B}=\nabla\times\mathbf{A}=a_{0}k\sin(\phi)~~\hat{y}

The total energy of the electron is given as

8  Canonical Momenta and Constants of motion

Given the Lagrangian for a relativistic charged particle in an electromagnetic field:

\mathcal{L}=-mc^{2}\sqrt{1-\frac{\mathbf{v}^{2}}{c^{2}}}+q\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{A}-q\phi,

(149)

the canonical momentum
\mathbf{p}
is defined as:

\mathbf{p}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\mathbf{v}}.

(150)

8.1  Relativistic Kinetic Term

The derivative of the relativistic kinetic term is:

\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{v}}\left(-mc^{2}\sqrt{1-\frac{\mathbf{v}^{2}}{c^{2}}}\right)=\frac{m\mathbf{v}}{\sqrt{1-\frac{\mathbf{v}^{2}}{c^{2}}}},

(151)

which is the relativistic mechanical momentum.

8.2  Interaction with the Vector Potential

The derivative of the term involving the vector potential is:

\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{v}}\left(q\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{A}\right)=q\mathbf{A},

(152)

since the vector potential
\mathbf{A}
does not depend on
\mathbf{v}
.

8.3  Electric Scalar Potential

The scalar potential term
-q\phi
does not depend on
\mathbf{v}
, so its derivative is:

\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{v}}\left(-q\phi\right)=0.

(153)

8.4  Final Expression

Combining these results, the canonical momentum is:

\mathbf{p}=\frac{m\mathbf{v}}{\sqrt{1-\frac{\mathbf{v}^{2}}{c^{2}}}}+q\mathbf{A}=\gamma m\mathbf{v}+q\mathbf{A}.

(154)

8.5  Key Points

•

The term
\frac{m\mathbf{v}}{\sqrt{1-\frac{\mathbf{v}^{2}}{c^{2}}}}
is the
relativistic mechanical momentum
.

•

The term
q\mathbf{A}
is the contribution due to the interaction with the electromagnetic field, arising from the vector potential.

This distinction between the canonical momentum and the mechanical momentum is essential in the Hamiltonian formulation and quantum mechanics.

8.6  Cyclic Coordinates and Conservation Laws

In Lagrangian mechanics, a
cyclic coordinate
(or
ignorable coordinate
) is a generalized coordinate that does not explicitly appear in the Lagrangian. If a coordinate
q_{i}
is cyclic, the corresponding conjugate momentum
p_{i}
is conserved.

8.7  Definition of Cyclic Coordinates

Consider a Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}(q_{1},q_{2},\dots,q_{n};\dot{q}_{1},\dot{q}_{2},\dots,\dot{q}_{n};t)
, where
q_{i}
are the generalized coordinates and
\dot{q}_{i}
are the corresponding velocities. A coordinate
q_{i}
is said to be cyclic if:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial q_{i}}=0.

(155)

This means the Lagrangian does not explicitly depend on
q_{i}
.

8.8  Conservation of Conjugate Momentum

For any coordinate
q_{i}
, the conjugate momentum is defined as:

p_{i}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\dot{q}_{i}}.

(156)

If
q_{i}
is cyclic, the Euler-Lagrange equation :

\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\dot{q}_{i}}-\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial q_{i}}=0

(157)

reduces to:

\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\dot{q}_{i}}\right)=0.

(158)

Thus, the conjugate momentum
p_{i}
is conserved:

p_{i}=\text{constant}.

(159)

8.9  Physical Significance

•

A cyclic coordinate represents a symmetry in the system.

•

The conservation of the conjugate momentum
p_{i}
is a direct consequence of Noether’s theorem, which relates symmetries to conservation laws.

•

For example:

–

If the Lagrangian does not depend on a coordinate representing position, the conjugate momentum corresponds to linear momentum, which is conserved.

–

If the Lagrangian does not depend on an angular coordinate, the conjugate momentum corresponds to angular momentum, which is conserved.

can be obtained by knowing the initial conditions.

10  Ponderomotive scattering by paraxial laser beams

So far we have used a plane wave without any temporal envelope and study how the figure 8 shaped trajectory emerge in the instantenous rest frame of the particle. Next, we incorporated the gaussian temporal envelope and solve the problem analytically. The results are then compared with the numerical results where the effect of the RR is also studied. Now let us consider a scenario when the laser is modelled more realistically using the paraxial beam approximation, in such case we will see how the Ponderomotive scattering angle can be calculated analytically and how this process can be used as a potential diagnostic tool to measure the physical parameters associated with the ultra-intense lasers and also as a test tool to validate the theoritical framework of Radiation Reaction. This section is based on our previous work
Holkundkar and Mackenroth 2022
.

We model the laser field as,

\mathbf{A}=\frac{a_{0}}{\sqrt{1+z^{2}/z_{r}^{2}}}\exp\left[\frac{-r^{2}}{w_{0}^{2}(1+z^{2}/z_{r}^{2})}\right]g(\eta)\cos(\eta)\ \mathbf{e_{x}},

(199)

which closely resembles the paraxial beam model used for the numerical results as discussed in
Salamin, Mocken, and Keitel 2002
. For now we do not consider RR, such that the relativistic equations of motion of an electron under the influence of these laser fields is given by (using naturalized units
e=m_{e}=c=1
),

d\mathbf{p}/dt=-\mathbf{E}-\mathbf{\upsilon}\times\mathbf{B}
. The force equation along the polarization direction can be written as:

\frac{d\tilde{p}_{x}}{dt}=-\upsilon_{x}\frac{\partial A_{x}}{\partial x}=\frac{p_{x}}{\gamma}\frac{2x}{w_{0}^{2}(1+z^{2}/z_{r}^{2})}A_{x},

(200)

where we noted
A_{y}=A_{z}=0
,
\tilde{p}_{x}\equiv p_{x}-A_{x}
and
\upsilon_{x}=p_{x}/\gamma
is the electron’s velocity along the polarization direction. There will be no external force on the electron once the laser is over or it leaves the focus. Hence in a plane wave laser field it holds
p_{x}\sim A_{x}
. In a finite, non-plane-wave field, as considered in equation
199
, however,
\tilde{p}_{x}
arises as a correction term due to the gradient of the laser’s radial intensity profile. The displacement along the polarization direction can easily be estimated to be
x\sim x_{0}+p_{x}\eta/2\gamma
, with
x_{0}
being the initial position of the electron in the bunch. Using these simple approximations equation
200
simplifies to,

\frac{d\tilde{p}_{x}}{d\eta}\sim\frac{2}{\gamma\frac{d\eta}{dt}}\frac{A_{x}^{2}}{w_{0}^{2}(1+z^{2}/z_{r}^{2})}\Big[x_{0}+\frac{A_{x}\eta}{2\gamma}\Big].

(201)

Similarly, the longitudinal component of the force can be written as:
dp_{z}/dt=-\upsilon_{x}\partial A_{x}/\partial z
, however the rate change of energy is given by
d\gamma/dt=-\upsilon_{x}\partial A_{x}/\partial t
. It can be shown that in a plane wave
\zeta_{z}\equiv\gamma-p_{z}
is a constant of motion, whose value can be obtained by imposing the condition that initially
p^{i}_{z}=-\gamma_{i}\upsilon^{i}_{z}\sim-\gamma_{i}
, where
\gamma_{i}
is the
initial
energy of the electron bunch and minus indicates the counter-propagation of the electron bunch. Next, using the definition of
\eta=t-z+\phi_{0}
, one can write
d\eta/dt=1-p_{z}/\gamma
, which implies
\gamma d\eta/dt=\langle\zeta_{z}\rangle\sim\gamma_{i}+\gamma_{f}
. There will be no prominent transverse deflection of the electron until it is near the Rayleigh range of the focused laser. So effectively the
x_{0}
can be considered as the initial position of the particle at
z=z_{r}
. With these approximations, equation
201
is simplified to be

\frac{d\tilde{p}_{x}}{d\eta}\sim\frac{2}{(\gamma_{i}+\gamma_{f})}\frac{A_{x}^{2}}{2w_{0}^{2}}\Big[x_{0}+\frac{A_{x}\eta}{2\gamma}\Big]

(202)

Terms with
\eta A_{x}^{3}
vanish upon integration whence equation
202
can be easily integrated over
\eta
to obtain
\tilde{p}_{x}

\tilde{p}_{x}\sim\frac{a_{0}^{2}}{(\gamma_{i}+\gamma_{f})}\frac{x_{0}}{2w_{0}^{2}}\exp\Big[\frac{-r_{0}^{2}}{w_{0}^{2}}\Big]\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\Big[\frac{-8\ln(2)\eta^{2}}{\tau_{0}^{2}}\Big]\cos^{2}(\eta)d\eta

(203)

\tilde{p}_{x}\sim\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{32\ln(2)}}\frac{a_{0}^{2}\tau_{0}}{(\gamma_{i}+\gamma_{f})}\frac{x_{0}}{2w_{0}^{2}}\exp\Big[\frac{-r_{0}^{2}}{w_{0}^{2}}\Big].

(204)

There are manifold scattering angles associated with each individual electron in the bunch, but the width of the electron bunch on the detector, viz. the most easily accessible signal, is set by the largest scattering angle. This largest scattering depends on the maximum transverse momentum. From, equation
204
it can be deduced that the
\tilde{p}_{x}
would maximize for an electron with initial position
x_{0}=w_{0}/\sqrt{2}
by setting
d\tilde{p}_{x}/dx_{0}=0
, and hence the maximum net momentum along
x
can be written as
\tilde{p}_{x;max}=\tilde{p}_{x}|_{x_{0}=w_{0}/\sqrt{2}}
and is given by,

\tilde{p}_{x;max}\sim\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{256\ \ln(2)\ \text{e}}}\ \ \frac{a_{0}^{2}}{(\gamma_{i}+\gamma_{f})}\frac{\tau_{0}}{w_{0}}.

(205)

The maximum scattering angle of the electron bunch (as estimated from the
z
axis) would be;
\tan\Theta\propto\tilde{p}_{x;max}/p_{z}
. As discussed earlier, after the scattering
p_{z}\sim\gamma_{f}
and hence the scattering angle is estimated to be

\tan\Theta\sim\kappa\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{256\ \ln(2)\ \text{e}}}\ \ \frac{a_{0}^{2}}{\gamma_{f}(\gamma_{i}+\gamma_{f})}\frac{\tau_{0}}{w_{0}}.

(206)

where,
\kappa
is a proportionality constant, to be obtained by fitting the equation
206
with the simulation results. The fitting parameter
\kappa
accounts for stronger scattering in a realistic laser field due to higher order field corrections, unaccounted for in our simple model along with other approximations. This value of the
\kappa
is obtained by rigorous benchmarking of the analytical results against our realistic simulations with paraxial laser field profiles.

As we have seen, for a focused laser pulse we only require the final energy
\gamma_{f}
of the most strongly scattered electrons to estimate the maximal scattering angle of the overall electron bunch. Generally,
\gamma_{f}\sim\gamma_{i}
when there is no loss of energy through RR. However, For ultra-intense laser beams RR implies radiative energy loss of the electrons. Consequently, when RR can not be ignored, the energy of the outgoing electron bunch is always smaller than the energy of the incident electron bunch. In the context of ponderomotive scattering of a relativistic electron bunch, the detailed account of the interaction dynamics is not crucial, but the knowledge of final energy of the electron (
\gamma_{f}
) after the exit from the laser focus suffices to estimate the extent of the scattering and radiative energy loss. The dominance of RR in the interaction dynamics is conventionally measured in terms of the parameter
R(a_{0},\gamma_{i})\sim 4r_{eo}a_{0}^{2}\gamma_{i}/3
, where
r_{eo}=kr_{e}\sim 2.21\times 10^{-8}
(for
\lambda=800
nm) is a dimensionless rescaling of the classical electron radius
r_{e}
. Physically, the parameter
R(a_{0},\gamma_{i})
corresponds to the average energy loss due to radiation per laser cycle in units of the electron rest mass energy. Generally, the quantum effects in RR are prevalent when the quantum efficiency parameter
\chi\sim\gamma_{i}E_{Laser}/E_{S}>1
, where
E_{Laser}
is the peak laser field and
E_{S}=m_{e}^{2}c^{3}/|e|\hbar\simeq 1.32\times 10^{18}
V/m is the Schwinger critical field. In terms of dimensionless electric field
a_{0}=eE/m_{e}\omega c
, the critical field can be expressed as
a_{S}\sim m_{e}c\lambda/h\sim 329718
for
\lambda=800
nm laser. For the extreme case of
a_{0}=100
,
\gamma_{i}=900
used in this work the parameter
\chi\sim 0.27<1
and hence quantum RR would not be crucial for the results presented in our manuscript.

We wish to stress, on the other hand, that the full electron dynamics in a focused laser field with the inclusion of the RR was found too involved for an analytical solution. However, the dynamics of an electron in a plane wave with RR has been extensively studied in the past. Consequently, an accurate estimate of the most strongly scattered electrons’ final energy can be found by approximating this final energy as that of an electron scattered by a plane wave with an intensity equal to the intensity an electron would experience inside the focused laser pulse at
(r,z)=(w_{0}/\sqrt{2},z_{r})
. This peak intensity can be read off from equation
199
as

A^{\text{p.w.}}=\left|\mathbf{A}\left(\frac{w_{0}}{\sqrt{2}},z_{r}\right)\right|=\frac{a_{0}}{\sqrt{2}}\exp[-0.25],

(207)

where we assumed the field to be at the peak of its temporal field oscillations
g(\eta)\cos(\eta)=1
.

The final energy
\gamma_{f}
is then estimated by
numerically
solving the LL model dynamics of a single electron inside a plane wave of intensity
A^{\text{p.w.}}
. The energy loss due to the RR is typically linear with the laser amplitude
a_{0}
, and the RR effect can be quantified using the dimensionless parameter
R(a_{0},\gamma_{i})
. In view of this in we fitted the outgoing energy of the electron through the function
\gamma_{f}(a_{0},\tau_{0},\gamma_{i})=C\sqrt{R(a_{0},\gamma_{i})\tau_{0}}+D
[here
C
and
D
are the fitting parameters]. It should be understood that the fitting parameter
C
and
D
are not the part of the our analytical framework which predicted the scattering angle as in equation
206
. However, the final exit energy of the particle is just an input to equation
206
. There is no straightforward way to calculate
\gamma_{f}
(apart from experimentally measuring the same) and hence we relied on the plane wave ansatz and calculated through numerical simulations by fitting the function
\gamma_{f}(a_{0},\tau_{0},\gamma_{i})=C\sqrt{R(a_{0},\gamma_{i})\tau_{0}}+D
. We note, that in a real focused laser field, however, the electron would scatter away, thus experiencing lower RR and hence the final exit energy would be slightly higher than estimated via this simple plane wave model. We will see, however, that this does not affect the predictive power of our model.
