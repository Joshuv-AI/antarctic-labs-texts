Associations between serum uric acid and risk of depressive symptoms in East Asian populations
Authors: Menghui Yao, Xinxin Han, Yue Yin, Shuolin Wang, Yingdong Han, He Zhao, Hong Di, Juan Wu, Yun Zhang, Xuejun Zeng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite the fact that observational studies have reported associations between serum uric acid (SUA) and depressive symptoms risk in East Asian populations, there is a lack of evidence demonstrating a causal relationship between them. This study aimed to perform a comprehensive assessment of the relationship between SUA and depressive symptoms.MethodsThis study included two cohort studies and a two-sample Mendelian randomization study. The cross-sectional cohort was derived from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) wave 3 (in 2015), and the baseline data of participants were extracted from the CHARLS wave 1(in 2011), excluding those with depressive symptoms in 2011 and forming a longitudinal cohort from 2011 to 2015. Logistic multiple regression was performed to investigate the cross-sectional and longitudinal associations of SUA with depressive symptoms in the two cohorts. Furthermore, we performed two-sample Mendelian randomization analyses to explore the potential causal relationships between them.ResultsWe included two cohorts of 9056 and 3177 individuals respectively. Logistic regression showed that individual with higher SUA levels had a lower risk of depressive symptoms (OR = 0.921; 95%CI: 0.886–0.957) in the cross-sectional cohort. However, neither the baseline SUA level (OR = 1.055, 95%CI: 0.961–1.157) nor the change in SUA level (OR = 0.945, 95%CI: 0.656–1.363) affected the depressive symptoms 4 years later in the longitudinal cohort. The two-sample MR showed that there was no genetic causal relationship between SUA and depression (ORIVW_MRE= 1.071, 95% CI: 0.926–1.238).ConclusionsSUA and depressive symptoms are associated, with lower SUA levels observed in middle-aged and older participants with depressive symptoms; however, no causal evidence supports their relationship.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-024-06343-5.Keywords: Depressive symptoms, Serum uric acid, Cohort study, Mendelian randomization study, Causal relationship

Introduction

Depression is a prevalent mental disorder with the main feature of persistent and severe low mood [1, 2]. It can also lead to feelings of worthlessness, hopelessness, or even suicidal thoughts or actions [3]. A systematic review reported the median prevalence of depressive symptoms in older people living in an institution was 29% [4]. Another study showed that approximately 50% of elderly persons in long-term care suffer from depressive symptoms and 20% from major depression [5]. Since older persons constitute the fastest growing part of the population, depression has become the most common psychiatric disorder around the world [6]. In China, depression has become an increasingly serious public health issue [7]. It is estimated that the prevalence of depression in older adults in China is as high as 23.6%, and this rate is expected to rise not only with age but also due to a growing trend of earlier onset in middle-aged individuals [8, 9]. Previous studies have reported that the prevalence of depression among middle-aged and older adults in China ranges from 17.4–46.15% [10]. This clearly highlights that depression in this demographic has become a significant public health concern. Given the rising prevalence and its profound impact on health, it is urgent to investigate the factors contributing to depression in middle-aged and older populations.

While current studies have not identified the specific mechanisms of depressive disorders, previous studies have implicated oxidative stress (OS) to be a cause of depression [11, 12]. OS can reduce neurogenesis and increase neuronal apoptosis, as well as affect the activity of serotonin (5-HT) neurotransmission and the metabolic pathways of monoamine neurotransmitters [13]. In patients with depression, increased OS and lipid peroxidation, alongside reduced total antioxidant capacity, have been observed, which may contribute to various depressive symptoms [13]. Targeting these alterations with appropriate antioxidants may offer a potential strategy for treating depression [14]. Serum uric acid (SUA), the end-product of purine metabolism, has been reported as a well-documented critical natural anti-oxidant in organisms [15, 16]. Several studies have indicated that lower SUA levels may be associated with an increased risk of neurodegenerative diseases, such as Alzheimer’s disease and Parkinson’s disease [17, 18]. Therefore, it could be speculated that SUA might be associated with depression. The negative associations between SUA level and depressive symptoms have been proven in several cross-sectional studies, with patients showing lower SUA levels relative to corresponding healthy controls [19, 20]. However, the relationship between SUA levels and psychiatric comorbidities is complex. Due to uncontrolled confounding factors and the potential for reverse causality, these cross-sectional studies are insufficient to fully explain the link between SUA levels and depressive symptoms. Therefore, large-scale longitudinal studies are lacking to corroborate the causal link between SUA and depressive symptoms.

Mendelian randomization (MR) is a commonly applied method in epidemiology, utilizing genetic variations as instrumental variables (IVs) to estimate the causal relationship between modifiable environmental exposures and medically relevant traits or diseases [21, 22]. There are only a few related MR studies about SUA and depression. MR analyses based on the European population revealed no genetic causal relationship between SUA and depression [23, 24]. However, a recent meta-analysis of genome-wide association studies (GWASs) on depression in East Asians revealed that the genetic architecture of depression differs between European and East Asian populations. Only 11% of the loci associated with depression in Europeans reached nominal significance levels in East Asians [25]. Given these disparities, it is plausible that genetic variants influencing SUA levels and depression may differ in frequency or effect size between these populations, which could lead to distinct causal relationships. Therefore, it is necessary to re-evaluate the genetic causal relationship between SUA and depression in East Asian populations.

In this study, based on a large-scale cross-sectional and longitudinal cohort from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), we performed a logistic multiple analysis to assess the associations between SUA and depressive symptoms. Furthermore, we performed a 2-sample MR study to explore whether SUA is causally associated with depressive symptoms (Fig. 1). We hypothesize that low SUA levels contribute to the development of depressive symptoms, suggesting a causal relationship between SUA and depressive symptoms. The study aims to provide large-scale longitudinal and MR evidence, addressing the gap in East Asian-specific genetic data on SUA and depression.

Methods

Data and samples

The CHARLS is a national population-based survey among Chinese adults aged 45 years or older conducted by the National School of Development of Peking University. The participants were selected through a four-stage, stratified, cluster random sampling method from 150 counties distributed in 28 provinces [26]. To date, CHARLS has conducted four waves of surveys nationwide, including wave 1 in 2011–2012, wave 2 in 2013, wave 3 in 2015, and wave 4 in 2018. Blood tests were only performed in wave 1 and wave 3. The study was approved by the ethics committee of Peking University (IRB00001052–11015). All participants provided their signed informed consents.

In our study, we constructed two cohorts. The cross-sectional cohort, derived from wave 3, was used to explore the horizontal association between SUA and depressive symptoms. The exclusion criteria for participants were as follows: (1) missing data on CESD-10 scores; (2) missing data on SUA; (3) aged less than 45 years or missing data on age; (4) having cancer or unable to determine whether having cancer; and (5) missing data on sex, BMI, or other covariates. A total of 9056 participants were enrolled in the analyses. The longitudinal cohort was constructed to examine the predictive effect of SUA on depressive symptoms. Participants who had no depressive symptoms in wave 1, but manifested depressive symptoms in wave 3, were enrolled in the cohort. The other exclusion criteria were the same as those for the cross-sectional cohort. More details regarding the eligibility and selection of participants in two cohorts are shown in Fig. 2.

Flow diagram for participants included in the study

Assessment of depressive symptoms

The 10-term CES-D questionnaire was used to measure the degree of depressive symptoms in CHARLS. It included 10 questions, and each question was scored from 0 to 3 with the CES-D score ranging from 0 to 30 [27, 28]. Previous studies have proved that the CES-D-10 was a valid tool for measuring depressive symptoms in older participants in China [29, 30]. In our study, the binary depressive symptoms variable was generated according to whether the CESD score is greater than or equal to 10. The specific 10-term CES-D questionnaire is shown in additional file1: Table S1.

Assessment of serum uric acid

The CHARLS website (https://charls.charlsdata.com) had given a detailed description of the blood sample collection, processing, transportation, and storage. Briefly, the staff from local Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) stations collected fasting venous blood samples and sent them, under low-temperature storage, to the local CDC and Beijing CDC laboratories for analysis. The level of SUA was analyzed using the UA Plus method [31]. The SUA levels in wave 3 were also categorized into low(≤ 4.30 mg/dl), medium(4.31–5.40 mg/dl), and high(≥ 5.41 mg/dl) groups based on their tertile levels for subsequent regression analysis.

Covariates

Some covariates were selected in our study. The sociodemographic variables included age, sex (male and female), body mass index [BMI (kg/m2)], education level (illiterate, junior high school or below, and high school or above), marital status [married/cohabitated, other (separated/divorced/ widowed/never married)], and residence (rural and urban). Health behaviors (history of smoking and drinking), and medical history [including arthritis, cardiovascular diseases, pulmonary diseases, metabolic diseases, and other diseases (kidney disease, digestive disease, emotional, nervous, or psychiatric problems, memory-related disease)] were collected by well-trained interviewers. Blood test data included total cholesterol (TC), triglyceride (TG), glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c), C-reactive protein (CRP), and creatinine (Cr).

Data sources for 2-sample MR analysis

Summary-level GWAS data for SUA (expressed as mg/dl) variants were obtained from IEU OpenGWAS project database (ebi-a-GCST90018757). The data comprised 129,405 individuals from BioBank Japan (BBJ), the mean value of SUA level was 5.5 mg/dl [32]. The depression data were derived from the largest and most recent meta-analysis of GWASs conducted among East Asians. This analysis included 98,502 individuals, comprising 12,588 cases and 85,914 controls. The study utilized data from several sources, including the China Kadoorie Biobank (CKB), the CONVERGE Consortium, the Taiwan-MDD study, and publicly available sub cohorts from the U.S. and U.K. The outcome of depression was defined by symptom questionnaires, structured clinical interviews, medical health care records, and self-completed surveys [25].

We used MR to analyze genetic variants that had a significant genome-wide correlation with SUA (GWAS p-value < 5 × 10− 8). Independent variants were defined by a linkage disequilibrium r2<0.001. A total of 59 single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with SUA levels were selected as independent IVs. Of the 59 SNPs, 55 were directly matched in the summary data of the SNP–outcome (depression) GWAS. The variance explained by each SNP was calculated as R2 = 2 × β2 × MAF (1-MAF), where β and MAF represent the effects of the SNP on exposure and minor allele frequency, respectively. F-statistic = (β/SE)2, where SE is the standard error of the genetic effect. The SNPs of SUA used to construct the IVs were presented in additional file 1: Table S2. All the SNPs had an F-statistic > 10 and explained 5.6% of the variance in SUA concentrations. We then excluded 6 SNPs (rs16856823, rs34315986, rs3775948, rs477138, rs6026578, and rs9468498) for being palindromic.

An assumption of MR analysis is that the IVs are not associated with confounders. Body fat and BMI were considered to be the most likely important confounders between SUA and depression [23]. We used the PhenoScanner V2 to check the associations of these SNPs with body fat and BMI. We found five SNPs (rs9837316, rs2231142, rs11525583, rs4966019, and rs8062941) were related to body fat and BMI; therefore, we excluded them and performed MR analysis again with the remaining 54 SNPs as IVs.

Statistical analysis

In cross-sectional analysis, binary regression analysis was used to evaluate the association between SUA and depressive symptoms. Two models were constructed. The crude model included only SUA, while the adjusted model controlled for age, sex, BMI, educational level, residence, smoking history, drinking history, TC, TG, Cr, CRP, HbA1c, and chronic diseases (arthritis, respiratory diseases, cardiovascular diseases, metabolic diseases and other diseases). The odds ratios (ORs) and their 95% confidence interval (CI) were presented in all the models. SUA was included in the model as continuous and categorical variable to test the stability of the results.

In longitudinal analysis, we performed logistic multiple regression again to explore the impact of baseline SUA in 2011 and its changes (from 2011 to 2015) on further depressive symptoms in 2015. As the continuous variable, the changes of SUA were calculated by the formula: ΔSUA= (SUA2015-SUA2011)/SUA2011. As the categorical variable, the changes in SUA from 2011 to 2015 were divided into five groups: stable(including low2011→low2015, medium2011→medium2015, high2011→high2015), slight increase(low2011→medium2015, medium2011→high2015), significant increase(low2011→high2015), slight decrease(medium2011→low2015, high2011→medium2015), and significant decrease(high2011→low2015). We used the same model-building methods as described above and adjusted for the same covariates in wave 1 in the binary regression analysis.

We applied various MR methods with different assumptions regarding horizontal pleiotropy, including inverse-variance weighted (IVW), weighted median, simple median, MR-Egger, and weight mode methods, to determine MR estimates of SUA for depression. The primary outcome was the IVW meta-analysis of the Wald ratio for each SNP. Additionally, the IVW based on multiplicative random effects(MRE) method provides a concise estimation and takes into account potential heterogeneity among the Wald ratio estimates from SNPs [33]. Thus, if there was heterogeneity, random-effects IVW models were used; otherwise, the fixed-effect IVW model were used。MR-Egger, weighted median and other methods were used to complement IVW estimates as these approaches could provide more robust estimates but less efficient (wider CIs) under certain scenarios [34].

The distribution of continuous variables was described by the median and interquartile range (IQR), since they were not normally distributed. The Wilcoxon rank sum test was used to test the difference between two groups for continuous variables. For categorical variables, the chi-square test was applied to compare two groups. Logistic multiple regression was performed using SPSS 25.0. Two-Sample MR analyses were implemented by the package TwoSampleMR in R (version 4.0.0), a p-value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Sensitivity analysis

We performed a series of sensitivity analyses to ensure the robustness of our findings. First, given the differential effects of hyperuricemia (HUA) on the human body compared to normal SUA)level [35], we excluded patients with HUA (SUA>7 mg/dl)from further analysis. Second, we included adjustments for time-varying covariates, including BMI, TC, TG, Cr, CRP, HbA1c, to further investigate the associations between SUA and its changes and depressive symptoms. Finally, to thoroughly address missing data and minimize potential selection bias, we re-included patients with cancer who were previously excluded, applied Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) to handle the missing data, and reanalyzed the relationship between SUA and depressive symptoms across both the cross-sectional and longitudinal cohorts. Further details are provided in Supplementary File 1: Figure S1.

For MR analysis, we utilized heterogeneity markers (Cochran Q-derived p < 0.05) from the IVW method to signify potential horizontal pleiotropy. The intercept from the MR-Egger regression indicated directional pleiotropy (p < 0.05). The MR-Pleiotropy Residual Sum and Outlier methods (MR-PRESSO) were also performed to assess and correct horizontal pleiotropy. Leave-one-out analysis was also conducted to examine whether the MR estimate was affected or biased by a single SNP.

MR results of serum uric acid on depression

The MR results from different methods used to assess of the causal effect of genetically predicted SUA levels on depression risk are presented in Fig. 3A. There was no association between SUA and depression risk (ORIVW_MRE = 1.071, 95% CI: 0.926–1.238, p = 0.259). Using the weighted median, simple median, MR-Egger, and weight mode methods, we still haven’t found any associations between SUA and depression risk. The estimated effect sizes of the SNPs on both the SUA and depression outcomes are displayed in scatter plots (Fig. 3B). MR-Egger regression indicated that there was no notable directional pleiotropy (intercept = 0.009, p = 0.153). The leave-one-out analysis showed that the results estimate was not driven by elimination of any single SNP (Fig. 3C). Cochran’s Q test showed certain heterogeneity among the SUA instrumental variable estimates based on the 49 SNPs(p = 0.030). We then performed MR-PRESSO analysis to identify outlier variants. However, MR-PRESSO results showed that there were no outliers. More importantly, the results were not significantly different after removing the SNPs that were confounded by body fat and BMI (Fig. 3D-F).

Sensitivity analysis

In the cross-sectional cohorts, excluding individuals with HUA (n = 738) did not alter the results (Additional File 1: Table S4), with the odds ratios (ORs) and p-values remaining consistent. Imputing incomplete data using the multiple imputation method and including cancer patients also yielded consistent results (Additional File 1: Table S5). In the longitudinal cohorts, additional adjustment for time-dependent variables again produced results consistent with the main analysis (Additional File 1: Table S6). Excluding individuals with HUA (n = 325) or including cancer patients, along with imputation of incomplete data, still resulted in similar findings, although the p-value for the significant increase in SUA changes (low 2011 → high 2015) became statistically significant (Additional File 1: Table S7-8). Furthermore, we compared the baseline characteristics of the cohorts before and after imputation, and found no statistical differences, indicating that the multiple imputation method performed well and did not introduce bias (Additional File 1: Table S9).

Discussion

To our best knowledge, this is the first study to provide a comprehensive assessment of the relationship between SUA and depressive symptoms in East Asian populations, using cross-sectional, longitudinal, and genetic evidence. In the cross-sectional study, the results revealed a negative correlation between SUA levels and depressive symptoms in middle-aged older adults from China, which is in agreement with previous studies. In a large Taiwanese population study, SUA levels significantly declined in the depression group, and multivariable logistic regression analysis suggested that the high SUA level was a protective factor against depressive symptoms [36]. A cohort study from southwestern China reported the level of SUA in patients with depression was lower than that in controls [13]. A retrospective cohort study based on the Korean population also reported similar results [37]. These studies were all cross-sectional, so causal inferences could not be drawn. In the longitudinal study, we found that baseline SUA levels and its changes were not related to the occurrence of depressive symptoms during the follow-up period, suggesting that SUA and depressive symptoms were correlated, but not causally related. However, a single SUA measurement and a short follow-up duration may have limited our ability to detect the long-term effects of SUA on depressive symptoms, highlighting the need for studies with repeated measurements and longer follow-up.

To corroborate our findings, we performed two sample MR analysis to investigate whether SUA and depression have a potential genetic causal association. However, all the methods employed in this study failed to reveal any genetic causal relationship between SUA and depression. Despite the potential heterogeneity in this study, which may arise from factors such as differences in population, genetic measurement platforms, and detection methods, several studies have pointed out that the results obtained using the IVW method based on the MRE model remain reliable [38–40]. Similarly, a meta-analysis and MR study reported that there is no evidence of causal effects between SUA and major depression in European and South American populations, suggesting the absence of causality between SUA and depression across different populations [23].

Previous studies have shown that OS was involved in the development of major depression, as the brain is more vulnerable to oxidative free radicals than other tissues due to its high oxygen consumption [23], and SUA has been proposed as a reliable indicator of OS [41]. Several epidemiological studies have also indicated a negative association between SUA and depressive symptoms [13, 36, 37, 42], leading some scholars to propose that SUA plays a role in the etiology of depression. However, our study showed that SUA is insufficient to induce depressive symptoms, and further research is required to elucidate the negative association between SUA levels and depressive symptoms. Here, we think there are several possibilities to explain this phenomenon. First, a potential third variable may influence both SUA and depressive symptoms, creating a spurious causal relationship between them, such as reactive oxygen species (ROS). Studies have shown that elevated levels of ROS and the upregulation of OS-related genes are associated with the progression of depression [43]. The brain, as a major consumer of oxygen and rich in oxidizable lipids, is more vulnerable to ROS-induced damage compared to other organs. The increase in ROS can lead to neuronal dysfunction and disrupted signaling, potentially contributing to the pathogenesis of depression [44]. At the same time, SUA, a key antioxidant in humans, plays a crucial role in scavenging extracellular free radicals. However, evidence suggests that in individuals with high ROS levels, there is often a depletion of SUA [45, 46]. Thus, ROS may simultaneously influence both SUA levels and depressive symptoms, potentially creating a spurious association between SUA and depressive symptoms. Second, there may be reverse causality, whereby depressive symptoms lead to a reduction in SUA, but not vice versa. Therefore, we constructed a new longitudinal cohort based on the original cross-sectional cohort, consisting of participants with depressive symptoms in wave 1, followed up until wave 3. Using generalized linear models, we analyzed the impact of changes in depressive status (persistent depressive symptoms or recovery) on SUA levels. However, no association was still found in the results (Additional File 1: Table S10). We also performed a reverse MR analysis (from depression to SUA) and still showed that depression was not related to SUA (data not shown). Third, the epidemiological evidence may be subject to recall bias or publication bias, but we think this possibility is small, as consistent findings have been reported in populations from different regions. Further studies still need to be performed.

The primary strength of this study lies in its comprehensive approach, incorporating cross-sectional, prospective, and MR analyses to explore the associations between SUA and depressive symptoms. Moreover, this study represents the first assessment of the causal relationship between SUA and depressive symptoms within East Asian populations. However, several limitations should be taken into consideration. First, SUA was measured only once, which may not accurately reflect long-term levels. This is a common limitation in large cohort studies due to the challenges of continuous biomarker monitoring. We mitigated this by categorizing SUA levels (low, medium, high) to capture relative status, as SUA typically fluctuates within a small range under normal conditions. Second, some potential confounding factors, such as dietary intake and drug intake, were not adjusted in the model due to the lack of related data in the CHARLS. Third, as the CES-D measures depressive symptoms rather than clinically diagnosed depression, its use may introduce classification bias. However, the CES-D 10 is a commonly used tool to evaluate clinically significant depressive symptoms. Lastly, we limited our MR analysis to East Asian participants to avoid population structure bias, so our findings may not apply to other ethnic groups.

Conclusions

SUA and depressive symptoms are associated, with lower SUA levels observed in middle-aged and older participants with depressive symptoms; however, no causal evidence supports their relationship.
