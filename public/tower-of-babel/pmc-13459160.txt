Behavioral intentions toward a mobile mental health screening and support tool suite for healthcare professionals in Ethiopia
Authors: Berhanu Boru Bifftu, Susan J. Thomas, Khin Than Win
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMobile mental health applications (MMHAs) enable healthcare professionals to efficiently screen, identify, and refer individuals with mental health conditions, while also providing access to evidence-based educational resources. Although MMHAs are increasingly recognised as promising tools for improving mental healthcare delivery, little is known about healthcare professionals’ intentions to use these applications in routine clinical practice. Evidence on the factors influencing healthcare professionals’ intended use of MMHAs also remains limited. Therefore, this study aimed to assess healthcare professionals’ intentions to use MMHAs and associated factors.MethodsThis cross-sectional study involved 427 health professionals, with data collected online using quota sampling. The single population proportion formula was used to calculate the sample size. Behavioral intention to use MMHAs was assessed using a modified e-Delphi study. Associated factors were identified through bivariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to determine the strength and significance of associations.ResultsOverall, 63% (95% CI: 58.5%–67.7%) of participants expressed an interest in using mobile mental health applications. Age (35–44 years) (AOR = 2.73, 95% CI: 1.390–5.367), perceived usefulness (AOR = 4.89; 95% CI: 2.765–8.661), favorable attitudes (AOR = 2.37; 95% CI: 1.375–4.089), and supportive subjective norms (AOR = 2.06; 95% CI: 1.035–4.134) were factors associated with intentions to use MMHAs.ConclusionOverall, 63% of participants expressed intention to use MMHAs to support mental health screening, referral, and access to evidence-based resources. Age, perceived usefulness, favorable attitudes, and subjective norms significantly predicted intention to use. These findings highlight the importance of improving perceived usefulness through practical training and demonstration of clinical benefits, promoting positive attitudes through user-centred app design, and strengthening supportive professional and organisational environments to improve subjective norms. Additionally, tailored capacity-building initiatives aimed at various age groups may boost adoption and engagement with MMHAs in routine clinical practice.

Introduction

Globally, there are approximately 450 million people who suffer from mental illness, with a 29.1% prevalence rate over a one-year period [1, 2]. Among these, depression, anxiety, and medically unexplained somatic symptoms are particularly widespread [3, 4]. Community-based surveys across various countries have revealed high rates of common mental health conditions [5, 6], with some variation by location. For instance, studies have reported the prevalence of common mental health conditions as ranging from 20.8% to 28.8% in the United States [6], 6.6% to 18.1% in Australia [7], 14% to 17.2% in Europe [8] and up to 34.9% in Africa [9]. Mental health conditions are a major contributor to the global disease burden, accounting for roughly 15% of Years of Life Lost (YLLs) [10] and ranking among the top ten preceding causes of disability worldwide [2, 11]. Additionally, mental health conditions account for 14.3% of annual deaths and contribute to 13% of the total global disease burden [11].

Despite these high prevalence rates and consequences, the recognition of mental health disorders in healthcare settings remains low, ranging from 0 to 28% in low-income countries [12] and around 48% in high-income countries [13]. Contributing factors include a shortage of health professionals trained in mental healthcare, insufficient resources, perceptions about mental health conditions, and limited access to relevant technologies [14, 15]. Accordingly, the World Health Organization (WHO) has recommended integrating mental health into primary healthcare (PHC) systems, especially in low- and middle-income countries like Ethiopia [16, 17]. PHC refers to essential health care that is universally accessible to individuals and families in the community, delivered through their full participation and at a cost the community and country can afford. Given that PHC serves as the first point of entry for most individuals with mental health conditions, this approach enables broader, decentralised, and sustainable access to mental healthcare facilities [17].

In alignment with WHO recommendations and drawing lessons from successful international examples, Ethiopia’s Federal Ministry of Health has launched a National Mental Health Strategy [16, 18]. A key focus of this strategy is capacity building and the expanded involvement of a broad range of health professionals in mental health care delivery [16, 18]. In particular, empowering health professionals to efficiently screen, identify, and refer mental health conditions is crucial, especially in communities with limited access to trained mental healthcare professionals [19]. The scarcity of trained mental healthcare professionals [20], compounded by increasing challenges since the COVID-19 pandemic, and rapid technological advancements, underscore the growing need and opportunities to supplement traditional mental health care with Digital Mental Health Interventions (DMHIs) [21, 22].

Digital tools such as smartphones, telepsychiatry, and online support communities, can enhance access to mental health assistance, specifically in low-resource settings like Ethiopia [20, 23, 24]. Among this, mobile apps are one of the fastest-growing DMHI used in addressing mental health interventions and barriers such as transportation, time, cost, and geographic location [25, 26]. Mobile mental health applications (MMHAs) offer digital tools that empower primary healthcare providers to efficiently screen, identify, and refer individuals with mental health conditions, deliver remote support, and enhance public awareness thereby addressing critical gaps in mental health service delivery [20, 24, 27]. Despite these promising opportunities, several barriers hinder health professionals’ engagement with mobile app-based mental health interventions. These include concerns about potential harm, doubts about the reliability and appropriateness, limited understanding of their functionalities, language barriers, costs, privacy, and doubts about the trustworthiness of digital content [28, 29]. Moreover, a recently published systematic review and meta-analysis highlights the critical role of users’ attitudes, perceived usefulness, and willingness to adopt digital mental health interventions [30]. However, most of the included studies have focused on populations in developed countries, as well as general populations, students, and clinical patients, with very few specifically involving healthcare professionals. This highlights the need for more evidence from health professionals in resource-limited settings like Ethiopia.

In Ethiopia, where mental health professionals are scarce, and the healthcare infrastructure is inadequate [31–33], alternative approaches, such as mobile mental health applications, have the potential to fill these critical service gaps. Despite this, there is a notable shortage of research examining health professionals’ intentions to use MMHAs and the factors influencing their intentions. Therefore, this study aimed to assess health professionals’ intentions to use MMHAs and its associated factors in Ethiopia. These insights are vital for guiding the design and development of culturally appropriate mobile mental health solutions that align with the needs and realities of Ethiopia’s healthcare workforce.

Methods

Study design, period and context

A cross-sectional study was conducted from March 7 to May 13, 2025. This study was carried out in Gondar City, situated in the Amhara Regional State of Northwest Ethiopia, approximately 747 km from Addis Ababa. The Amhara Regional State covers about 170,752 square kilometres and has a population of approximately 21,134,988, with a nearly equal male-to-female ratio. Administratively, the region is divided into eleven zones. Ethiopia is the second most populous country in the Horn of Africa and is characterised by considerable linguistic, cultural, and ethnic diversity, with more than 80 languages, including Amharic, the official working language. Ethiopia is classified as a low-income country, with an economy largely dependent on agriculture. According to the Ethiopian Ministry of Health (2019), the mental health workforce in Ethiopia represents just 0.26% of the national health workforce, far below WHO recommendations [34]. Ethiopia’s healthcare system follows a three-tier structure comprising primary (health posts, health centres, and primary hospitals), secondary (general hospitals), and tertiary (specialised hospitals) levels of care [35].

Participant eligibility criteria

Healthcare professionals from different disciplines, including psychiatrists, general practitioners, mental health professionals, nurses, midwives, public health officers, clinical psychologists, and social workers were included. Participants were required to be aged 18 years or older, had at least six months of work experience, and voluntarily agreed to participate in the study.

Sample size determination

The required sample size for this study was determined using the single population proportion formula, based on the following assumptions: a 95% confidence level (Z = 1.96), a 5% margin of error, a 20% non-response rate, and an assumed proportion of 50%, as there were no previous similar studies conducted in Ethiopia. Participant recruitment

Recruitment was conducted using multiple strategies: a recruitment poster was displayed at the entrances of primary healthcare facilities in Gondar, and online advertisements were shared via social media platforms. Participants who expressed interest in response to these advertisements were sent an email invitation, which included information about the study’s objectives, a Participant Information Sheet, and an electronic consent form. Electronic informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their involvement in the study. We then recruited 427 eligible study participants using a quota sampling method.

Instruments and measurements of variables

A previously developed and validated instrument, constructed through an eDelphi methodology, was utilised in this study (anonymised for the review process). In this study, instruments were developed and validated across eight key domains: sociodemographic factors, mobile phone use, perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, attitudes, subjective norms, facilitating conditions, and willingness to use mobile mental health apps. The instrument includes items based on the following operational definitions: Perceived usefulness refers to the degree to which a health professional believes that using mobile mental health applications will assist in screening and supporting individuals with mental health conditions. Perceived ease of use is defined as the extent to which the use of such applications is free from physical and mental effort. Attitude reflects healthcare professionals’ overall positive or negative evaluation of using mobile mental health apps for screening and support. Intention to use captures the degree to which healthcare professionals plan or intend to adopt these applications in practice. Social norms represent the impact of opinions from important others, such as peers, colleagues, or family members, on their decision to use mobile mental health apps. Facilitating conditions refer to the availability of enabling resources and infrastructure, including device compatibility, internet access, technical support, and organizational readiness. All constructs, except for sociodemographic and mobile phone-related factors, were measured using a five-point Likert-type agreement scale: Strongly disagree [1], Disagree [2], Neutral [3], Agree [4], and Strongly agree [5]. To define the intention to use the MMHAs, responses were collected using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “Strongly disagree” [1] to “Strongly agree” [5]. For analysis purposes, a common threshold used in behavioral adoption studies was applied: responses of 4 (“Agree”) or 5 (“Strongly agree”) were classified as indicating high intention to use, while responses of 1 (“Strongly disagree”), 2 (“Disagree”), or 3 (“Neutral”) were considered as low intention. Perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, attitudes, subjective norms, and facilitating conditions were measured using Likert scale items. For the purpose of analysing associated factors, responses were grouped into three categories: Disagreed/Strongly disagreed, Neutral, and Agreed/Strongly agreed. Socio-demographic variables, including age, sex, educational status, profession, and work experience, were also included.

Reliability of the instruments

Cronbach’s alpha was calculated for each construct to evaluate the consistency of items within the scale. In this study, the internal consistency of the constructs was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha. The results were as follows: perceived usefulness (9 items) – 0.923; perceived ease of use (3 items) – 0.768; attitudes (5 items) – 0.868; subjective norms (2 items) – 0.765; facilitating conditions (4 items) – 0.866; and intention to use mobile mental health apps (9 items) – 0.938.

Data quality control

To ensure data quality, an online pilot test was conducted three weeks prior to the main data collection period among 46 healthcare professionals working in Bahir Dar, using the previously validated Amharic version of the questionnaire adapted for the Ethiopian context during our earlier Delphi study.

Data analysis

The collected data were imported into SPSS version 26 for analysis. Data cleaning was performed by checking for completeness and consistency of variables. Descriptive statistics, including frequency, mean, median, proportion, and standard deviation (SD), were calculated where appropriate and presented using tables, charts, and figures. Associated factors were examined using both bivariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses. Variables significantly associated with the intention to use mobile mental health applications were identified based on their Odds Ratios (ORs), 95% Confidence Intervals (CIs), and p-values less than 0.05. The model’s goodness-of-fit was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test.

Results

A total of 427 participants completed the survey, resulting in a 93% response rate. The remaining 7% of the returned questionnaires contained incomplete or incorrectly completed responses.

Socio-demographic characteristics

Among the participants, 306 were male, representing 71.7% of the sample. Most of the participants were in the 18–24 age group (159 participants, 37.2%). Regarding educational status, 236 participants (55.3%) held a bachelor’s degree. In terms of professional background, the most common profession was nursing (231 participants, 54.1%). Additionally, 134 participants (31.4%) had between 5 and 9 years of employment skill (Table 1).

Mobile phone-related characteristics

Among the participating health professionals, 376 (88%) reported using Android devices. A total of 216 participants (50.6%) reported having heard of smartphone applications related to mental health. Most, 306 (71.6%) had never received training on the use of mobile mental health applications for patient care, and 241 (56.4%) had never used such apps for patient screening or support. Participants reported using their mobile phones for different purposes. The most frequent uses were for education and learning (332 participants) and text messaging (283 participants). Fewer participants reported using their phones for gaming (195) and social media (178), while only a small proportion (65 participants) used their phones for patient management tasks, such as screening and support (Table 2).

Barriers and perceived benefits of using mobile mental health applications

Participants identified several barriers to the use of mobile mental health applications in patient care. The most frequently cited barrier was a lack of awareness, with 211 participants indicating they were not familiar with the existence of such applications. Despite these challenges, participants also recognised several perceived benefits. The most commonly reported benefit was the ability to monitor clients’ symptoms, noted by 257 participants (Table 2).

Factors associated with the intention to use mobile mental health applications

Bivariate analysis revealed that age, educational status, work experience, perceived helpfulness, perceived ease of use, attitudes, subjective norms, and facilitating conditions were significantly associated with the willingness to use mobile mental health applications. These variables were subsequently included in the multivariate analysis. In contrast, gender and professional category were not significantly associated with intention and were therefore ignored in the advanced analysis. The results of the multivariate analysis revealed that age (35–44 years) (AOR = 2.73, 95% CI: 1.390–5.367), perceived usefulness (agreed or strongly agreed) (AOR = 4.89; 95% CI: 2.765–8.661), favorable attitudes (AOR = 2.37; 95% CI: 1.375–4.089), and supportive subjective norms (agreed or strongly agreed) (AOR = 2.06; 95% CI: 1.035–4.134) were factors significantly associated with the intention to use mobile mental health applications (Table 4).

Discussion

In resource-limited settings like Ethiopia, where there is a critical shortage of mental health professionals and inadequate healthcare infrastructure, adjunctive approaches such as mobile mental health applications are urgently needed. Despite this, there is a notable shortage of research examining health professionals’ intentions to use and the factors influencing their adoption of these applications. Therefore, this study aimed to assess health professionals’ intentions to use MMHAs and its associated factors in Ethiopia.

Overall, 63% (95% CI; 58.5%–67.7%) of participants expressed an intention to use mobile mental health applications. However, levels of interest varied across specific intention-related items. Notably, a large majority (356 participants, 83.4%) agreed or strongly agreed with the statement: “I intend to use mobile mental health apps to encourage people to seek appropriate professional assistance and other supports.” Although the intended outcome was not exclusive to mental health apps, the overall prevalence of intention in our study (63%) is similar with previous research carried out among health professionals in Ethiopia, which reported similar levels of willingness to use mobile-based clinical guidelines [61.1% [36] and 63.3% [37] ].

However, our reported rate is substantially higher than findings from studies conducted in Saudi Arabia (42.3%) [38] and Ethiopia (28%) for clinical guidelines in general [39], and lower than that reported in a recently published evidence, which found a pooled intention rate of 73% (95% CI: 69%–76%) [30]. These variations may be due to sample size, the focus of health issues, and the study setting. In contrast, studies among clinical populations report markedly higher levels of intention. For example, a study among clinical patients in Ethiopia found that 94.7% were interested in using mental health applications [40]. Another study among pregnant women in Ethiopia reported a 77.3% intention rate to use mobile phones for mental health support [41]. Similarly, higher intention rates were observed among clinical patients in Bangladesh (73%) [42]. In addition, a systematic review and meta-analysis conducted specifically among clinical patients reported a pooled intention rate of 78.97% (95% CI: 74.48%–83.46%) [43]. These variations may be due to sample size, the population surveyed, and the study setting. Evidence indicates that participants’ intention to use DMHIs varies across countries, study areas, populations, and digital platforms, highlighting the importance of generating context-specific evidence that addresses the unique needs of diverse user groups and technological environments.

Among the associated factors, health professionals aged 35–44 years had more than twice the likelihood of intending to use mobile mental health applications compared to those aged 18–24 years (AOR = 2.73, 95% CI: 1.390–5.367), supporting evidence that age significantly influences technology adoption [29, 44]. Although younger individuals are generally more comfortable with technology, this trend varies depending on context, motivation, and support systems. In healthcare settings, younger professionals may have higher digital literacy but often lack clinical experience and autonomy, limiting mHealth adoption [45]. In contrast, mid-career professionals may be less digitally fluent but possess greater clinical experience and decision-making authority, enhancing perceived usefulness and intent to use such tools [46]. These results suggest the need for age- specific mHealth training strategies: older professionals may benefit from targeted digital skills support, while younger professionals may require better integration of mHealth into clinical practice and more autonomy to appreciate its value in care delivery.

In the present study, health professionals who perceived mobile mental health applications as useful were more than four times as likely to intend to utilise them compared to those who did not (AOR = 4.89, 95% CI: 2.765–8.661). In healthcare, perceived usefulness often refers to the extent to which a digital tool enhances clinical decision-making, improves efficiency, or contributes to better patient care. Empirical evidence consistently shows that when health professionals recognise the practical value of mobile health (mHealth) applications, they are significantly more likely to adopt and integrate them into their practice [26, 30, 47]. This finding underscores the importance of clearly demonstrating the real-world benefits of mHealth tools and designing systems that align with clinical workflows to enhance perceived usefulness and facilitate successful adoption. The finding that perceived usefulness emerged as the strongest predictor of intention to use mobile mental health applications (AOR = 4.89) suggests that cognitive evaluations of clinical benefit play a more decisive role than infrastructural enabling factors. This indicates that healthcare professionals are primarily motivated by the perceived ability of the application to improve clinical decision-making, efficiency, and patient care outcomes. This supports technology acceptance models, showing that perceived usefulness is a key factor influencing behavioral intention. It often surpasses other factors when users see clear benefits in work efficiency, decision-making, or service quality. In healthcare, professionals tend to focus on the clinical and practical benefits of mobile mental health apps such as improved screening, decision support, and workflow—rather than on structural or environmental barriers. The finding indicates that emphasising the clinical relevance and practical benefits of mobile mental health tools may encourage adoption more effectively than just improving infrastructure.

Health professionals with favorable attitudes toward mobile mental health applications had over twice the likelihood of intending to use them compared to those without such attitudes (AOR = 2.37, 95% CI: 1.375–4.089). Attitudes play a critical role in determining the intention to use technology, as emphasised in theoretical models such as the TPB [48], and the TAM [49]. A favourable attitude reflects an individual’s favourable evaluation of a technology’s usefulness and relevance, which strongly influences their willingness to adopt it. In the context of mobile mental health applications, participants with favourable attitudes are more likely to perceive these tools as beneficial and compatible with their clinical practice. This finding highlights the need to foster positive attitudes through targeted awareness campaigns, training programs, and demonstrations of real-world clinical benefits [30]. Interventions that improve familiarity and confidence with mobile mental health tools may enhance attitudes and, in turn, increase adoption and integration into routine healthcare delivery [29, 47].

Participants who perceived a supportive subjective norm were nearly three times more likely to intend to use mobile mental health applications compared to those who did not (AOR = 2.06, 95% CI: 1.035–4.134), underscoring the significant influence of perceived social pressure or approval from important others, such as colleagues, supervisors, or peers on behavioral intentions [29, 50]. A supportive social environment fosters encouragement and validation, enhancing the likelihood of adoption [47, 48]. This finding highlights the importance of leveraging opinion leaders or champions within healthcare institutions to positively shape subjective norms. Furthermore, the broader socio-cultural environment significantly affects the acceptance and use of mobile mental health tools [29, 30]. Cultural values, beliefs, trust in technology, privacy concerns, language preferences, internet access, and the availability of culturally relevant content all influence perception and adoption [30, 48, 50]. In settings where health professionals exhibit favorable attitudes, perceive social support, recognise the tools’ usefulness, and have high digital mental health literacy, the trust and adoption of mobile mental health applications are more likely. Therefore, the design and development of mobile mental health applications need to incorporate the cultural context and the specific needs of health professionals to ensure relevance, trust, and higher adoption rates among health professionals.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study offers several strengths, including its contextual relevance as the first of its kind in Ethiopia to examine healthcare professionals’ intentions to use mobile mental health applications and the associated influencing factors, thereby providing culturally appropriate and context-specific insights. The results have practical implications for app developers, policy-makers, and program designers, and they contribute to capacity building through the identification of areas for targeted digital literacy initiatives. Nonetheless, this study is subject to certain limitations. The cross-sectional nature of the research limits causal inferences, and the use of self-reported measures may be prone to social desirability and recall bias. The sample may not fully represent all healthcare professionals in Ethiopia, particularly those in underrepresented or rural areas, which may affect the generalizability of the findings. Use of quota sampling and online recruitment may have excluded individuals with limited internet access or lower digital literacy. As a result, the findings may not be fully generalizable to all healthcare professionals, and the results should be interpreted with this limitation in mind. Although binary categorisation is common in technology adoption research, dichotomising intention to use the MMHA into high and low categories may result in a loss of nuanced information. In addition, the limited availability of comparable published literature in the Ethiopian context constrained the depth of the discussion. Another limitation of this study is the uneven distribution of participants by profession and gender, with the majority being male and nurses. This could limit how widely the findings can be generalised to other healthcare professional groups, especially those less represented in the sample. Consequently, the results should be interpreted cautiously, and future research should aim for more balanced and diverse samples across professional categories and gender groups. Finally, the absence of qualitative data limits the ability to capture the socio-cultural factors that may shape participants’ intentions to use a mobile mental health application.

Conclusion

Overall, 63% of healthcare professionals surveyed in this Ethiopian study expressed an intention to use mobile mental health applications in their clinical practice. Age (35–44 years), perceived usefulness, attitudes, and supportive subjective norms were statistically significant predictors of this intention. These results have several important implications for future endeavours seeking to increase the adoption of mobile mental health tools. Intervention strategies should be tailored to specific age groups, particularly the 35–44 age group, and emphasise the app’s usefulness in clinical settings. Additionally, fostering positive attitudes through awareness campaigns and training, along with cultivating a supportive social and professional environment, is essential. App developers and public health communicators should prioritise user-centred design, clearly communicate practical benefits, and incorporate features that support digital literacy, such as interactive tutorials and in-app guidance. As the first study of its kind in this context, this research provides culturally appropriate and context-specific evidence to inform the design and development of mobile mental health solutions tailored to the needs of Ethiopian healthcare professionals. By addressing contextual factors that influence technology adoption, the study contributes to ongoing efforts to reduce the mental health treatment gap caused by the shortage of professionals. It also lays the groundwork for future research and practical applications in digital mental health, supporting improved service delivery, workforce engagement, and the integration of technology into Ethiopia’s mental health care system. Further research using qualitative methods is recommended to explore the socio-cultural factors shaping participants’ intentions to use a mobile mental health application for efficiently screening, identifying, and referring individuals with mental health conditions, as well as providing access to evidence-based educational resources.
