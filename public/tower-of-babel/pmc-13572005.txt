Post stenotic pulmonary artery aneurysm mechanism and management
Authors: Anis Sergouchni, Mamoudou Diawara, Hatim Yousfi, Hasna Leghlimi, El Wartiti Loukman, Mehdi Bamous
Published: 2026

Introduction

Poststenotic dilatation of the pulmonary artery (PA) trunk and its left branch is a common feature of pulmonary valve stenosis (PVS) but typically does not reach aneurysmal proportions [1]. PA aneurysm may develop in patients with PVS independently of the severity of the stenosis.These aneurysms are probably the result of a combination of intrinsic arterial wall weakness and haemodynamic stress, especially when congenital malformation of the valve leads to stenosis. The paucity of PA aneurysm cases impedes the understanding of pathological progression and treatment options of this disease. We describe a patient presenting with chest pain and PA aneurysm, the diagnostic approach and therapeutic intervention are emphasized with a review of the literature.

Case report

A 27-year old woman with an unremarkable past medical history. Three weeks before admission the patient experienced multiple episode of chest pain, described as moderate to severe associated with dyspnoea, and palpitations on exertion. Her physical examination showed distended jugular veins and a holosystolic murmur on the upper left parasternal border.Her ECG revealed a complete right bundle branch block and her chest X-ray (Fig. 1) showed cardiomegaly and leftward mediastinal enlargement suggestive of pulmonary artery dilatation. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) was subsequently performed and revealed a severe pulmonary valve stenosis with a peak transvalvular pressure gradient of 74 mmHg, in association with a main and left pulmonaries arteries aneurysms. There were no signs of pulmonary arterial hypertension and right ventricular systolic function was preserved. A CT scan demonstrated a dilatation of the main pulmonary artery that continued into the left pulmonary artery (LPA) 53mm and 57mm respectively (Fig. 2). The right pulmonary artery was normal.Testing upon admission included myocardial enzymes, auto-immune antibodies, virology, and tuberculosis testing. The negative results ruled out the possibility of myocarditis, syphilis, tuberculosis, Behcet's disease and vasculitis. Given to recent onset chest pain and the size of the PA aneurysm (57mm), the decision was made to proceed with surgical intervention.Fig. 1Chest X-ray demonstrating a prominent pulmonary trunk (arrow).Fig. 2CT scan demonstrating aneurysmal dilatation of the pulmonary trunk and its left branch (arrows).

The patient underwent surgery through a median sternotomy, a 6 cm aneurysm involving the pulmonary trunk and extending to the left pulmonary artery was seen after opening the pericardium (Fig. 3).The patient was placed on cardiopulmonary bypass and on beating heart a longitudinal incision was made on the anterior wall of the pulmonary trunk and the LPA.The pulmonary valve was tricuspid and a commissurotomy was made in all three fused commissure. An aneurysmorrhaphy was done by resecting the pulmonary arterial wall and suturing the pulmonary trunk and LPA on a 25 mm probe with 5/0 polypropylene sutures.Postoperative recovery was uneventful. Chest radiography in the postoperative period showed normal cardiac shadows and lung fields. Prior to discharge, an echocardiogram revealed good aneurysm repair and a transvalvular pressure gradient of 20 mmhg in association with a mild to moderate pulmonary regurgitation. Pathological examination of the resected artery wall revealed myxoid degeneration, and fragmentation of elastic fibres in the tunica media. At six months’ follow up, her symptoms had improved significantly and her right ventricle was only moderately dilated with a systolic pressure of 40mmhg.Fig. 3Surgical view highlighting pulmonary artery aneurysm and stenotic pulmonary valve (arrows).

Discussion

Deterling and Clagett reported only eight cases of pulmonary artery aneurysm (PAA) in a review of 109 571 necropsies [2]. Currently, in adults patients a PA diameter > 4 cm is considered a cut-off point that distinguishes a true PAA from a PA dilatation. The characteristic PA dilatation in the setting of PVS is diffuse and usually involves its left branch, our patient had the following diameters 53 mm and 57 mm on the pulmonary trunk and LPA respectively. The PA dilatation doesn't correlate with the degree of pulmonary valve stenosis or valve morphology, it is not rare to see it with modest degree of valve stenosis. The preferential dilatation of the left PA is related to the anatomy and flow characteristics of the bifurcation. The poststenotic PA trunk dilatation reach aneurysmal proportions in these patients because of an associated structural abnormality of the PA wall [3]. Our patient had mucoid degeneration of the media and fragmentation of the elastic fibers, these structural abnormalities have been described in aneurysmal hypertensive and non-hypertensive pulmonary trunks of patients with different congenital heart defect [4]. Whether and to what degree hemodynamic stress plays a facilitating role or whether wall abnormalities are inherent or acquired remain uncertain. Progression to dissection or rupture has been documented in association with severe pulmonary arterial hypertension, Eisenmenger syndrome and the pulmonary artery diameter alone was not associated with adverse outcomes [5]. Spontaneous PA dissection is rare, in contrast to the aortic aneurysm associated with bicuspid aortic valve, because of the low PA pressures distally to valve stenosis as opposed to the systemic circulation. In a large cohort of 1673 patients, the incidence of dissection was 14 per 100 000 patient-years [6].Our patient had new onset chest pain associated with effort related dyspnea and according to the management algorithm proposed by Reisenauer et al. when the diameter is 5 cm < PAA < 8 cm we should operate.For the same PAA diameter without chest pain and in the absence of severe pulmonary arterial hypertension, the risk of aneurysm rupture is small, and the PAA should be observed with serial imaging by CT and echocardiography to rule out progression. There are no clear guidelines for management. Conservative treatment was advocated when PAA are secondary to medical diseases such as tuberculosis, syphilis, Behcet's disease and vasculitis. For PAA associated with pulmonary hypertension, anatomical anomalies, surgery is recommended to diminish the risks of fatal complications.Surgical options include aneurysmorrhaphy, replacement with a Dacron graft or pulmonary allograft, and combined use of a stentless bioprosthesis.In post stenotic PAA aneurysmorrhaphy and relief of pulmonary valve stenosis is the preferred option because by establishing normal pulmonary blood flow and excluding the haemodynamic stress on the arterial wall the PA size remained stable for years after surgery. For PAA induced by inflammatory disease and pulmonary hypertension, complete replacement of the entire pulmonary trunk with allograft or synthetic graft is recommended because of potential recurrent dilatation due to the residual aneurysmal wall.

Conclusion

Collective review of the literature reveals that PAA may develop in patients with PVS independently of the severity of the stenosis and also supports the hypothesis that the poststenotic dilatation becomes aneurysmal owing to an associated intrinsic abnormality of the PA wall.
