On the Fréchet Root Kernel of Certain Wave Equations
Rafael Abreu

Abstract
We extend the adjoint method to complex-valued PDEs and introduce the Fréchet root sensitivity kernel , as the most fundamental kernel from which all other material-sensitivity kernels can be derived. We apply this framework to four representative equations: two real-valued PDEs (the second-order wave equation and the Euler–Bernoulli beam equation) and two complex-valued PDEs (the complex transport equation and the Schrödinger equation with zero potential). We compute and analyze the Fréchet root kernels for all four PDEs and show that, for constant material parameters, the kernel exhibits a consistent structure across systems, while its instantaneous form propagates as a wave whose shape depends on the initial conditions. For the Schrödinger equation, we find an especially notable result: the integrand of the Fréchet root kernel coincides with the Born rule of quantum mechanics, suggesting that the probabilistic interpretation of the wavefunction may arise naturally from a general sensitivity-analysis framework rather than from an independent postulate. Our results establish a unified approach to sensitivity analysis for real- and complex-valued PDEs, provide a new perspective on the origin of the Born rule.



Keywords:
Adjoint Method; Fréchet Kernels; Complex PDEs; Schrödinger Equation; Born Rule

1
Introduction

The Adjoint Equation Method and the Adjoint State Method are closely related mathematical frameworks used to analyze systems governed by partial differential equations (PDEs). Although the two are sometimes used interchangeably, they emphasize different aspects of the same underlying idea.

The Adjoint Equation refers to the differential equation obtained from the variational or weak formulation of a forward problem. It expresses how perturbations in the output of a system relate to perturbations in its inputs or parameters, through an adjoint (dual) field that satisfies a specific PDE with corresponding boundary and terminal conditions. The adjoint equation is therefore a mathematical construct, derived to quantify sensitivities and reciprocity properties of the forward model
(
34
, e.g.)
.

By contrast, the Adjoint State Method is a computational procedure that employs the adjoint equation within an optimization or inverse problem framework. In this approach, one defines a cost functional that depends on model parameters and on state variables constrained by a PDE. The adjoint state—introduced as a Lagrange multiplier—enforces the PDE constraint. Solving both the forward and adjoint problems enables the efficient computation of gradients of the cost functional with respect to model parameters, independently of the number of parameters
(
36
;
24
;
10
, e.g.)
. In short, the Adjoint Equation Method emphasizes the derivation of the adjoint equations themselves, while the Adjoint State Method highlights their algorithmic use in optimization and sensitivity analysis.

This connection between the adjoint equation and gradient computation naturally leads to the concept of Fréchet sensitivity kernels. They provide a systematic way to quantify how perturbations in model parameters affect observables. In other words, the Fréchet sensitivity kernel encapsulates the (linearized) influence of the changes of the PDE parameters on the output functional (observations). This can be efficiently computed using the solution of the adjoint equation. In this sense, the Adjoint State Method provides the computational mechanism to obtain these kernels, enabling gradient-based optimization and inverse analysis.

In this work, we generalize the adjoint framework to complex-valued PDEs, extending classical formulations that are typically restricted to real-valued systems. We demonstrate this generalization through four representative cases:
(i) two real-valued PDEs—the second-order wave equation and the Euler–Bernoulli beam equation; and
(ii) two complex-valued PDEs—the complex transport equation and the Schrödinger equation with zero potential.
The real-valued PDEs can be viewed as the real components of the complex ones, establishing a direct correspondence between the two classes. This design allows us to interpret complex-valued PDEs as natural extensions of well-understood real-valued systems: decomposing a complex-valued PDE into real and imaginary parts recovers the studied real-valued models.

We introduce the Fréchet root kernel, which we define as the most fundamental Fréchet sensitivity kernel from which all others can be derived. We compute and analyze the Fréchet root kernel for each of the four PDEs considered. Our results show that understanding the kernels in real-valued systems provides the theoretical foundation for their complex-valued analogues. Interestingly, or the particular case of the Schrödinger equation with zero potential, the integrand of the Fréchet root kernel coincides with the Born rule of quantum mechanics
(
15
;
29
;
42
;
28
, e.g.)
, offering a direct link between sensitivity analysis and the probabilistic interpretation of wavefunctions.

All PDEs and adjoint problems are solved numerically using the Finite Difference Method
(
26
;
19
)
. The analysis is carried out in one spatial dimension, which suffices to capture the essential theoretical behavior while maintaining clarity.
Finally, we discuss the implications of our findings for sensitivity analysis and inverse theory, and outline possible extensions of the complex-valued adjoint framework to multidimensional and nonlinear systems.

(\delta_{\textbf{m}}\chi+\delta_{\mathsf{L}}\chi+\delta_{u}\chi=0)
if the following condition holds:

\displaystyle\rho\partial^{2}_{t}\mathsf{L}-\lambda\partial_{x}^{2}\mathsf{L}=\sum_{r}\delta(x^{r}-x)(d-u),\hskip 20.00003pt\text{with}\hskip 20.00003pt\mathsf{L}(x,0)=\partial_{t}\mathsf{L}(x,0)=0.

(34)

Note that the expression involving the Lagrange multiplier
(\mathsf{L})
is equal to the initial wave equation (eq. (
27
)) with a different source term, and that without having previous information on the adjoint wavefield, it seems reasonable to assume

\displaystyle\mathsf{L}(x,T-t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}u(x,T-t)=u^{\dagger}(x,t),

(35)

thus, the adjoint wavefield
u^{\dagger}
is equal to the time-reversed wavefield
u(x,T-t)
. Note that we name the adjoint wavefield
u^{\dagger}
to keep the original notation used by
27
. Note also that we could have defined the adjoint wavefield in any other way

\displaystyle\mathsf{L}(x,T-t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\mathcal{F}u(x,T-t)=u^{\dagger}(x,t)

(36)

where
\mathcal{F}
is
any
certain linear functional, and the properties previously explained will still hold. For example, we could have rightfully chosen
\mathsf{L}(x,T-t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\partial_{t}u(x,T-t)
.

The new defined adjoint wavefield must satisfy the equations of motion given for
\mathsf{L}
(eq. (
34
)), which becomes:

\displaystyle\rho\partial^{2}_{t}u^{\dagger}-\lambda\partial_{x}^{2}u^{\dagger}=\sum_{r}\delta(x^{r}-x)(d-u).

(37)

where the adjoint source term is determined by the difference between the data and the synthetic wavefield,
(d-u)
, at the receiver locations
\sum_{r}\delta(x^{r}-x)
.

4.1.2
1D Fréchet Derivatives

In the next, the dependence of the adjoint variable
u^{\dagger}
on the model is ignored, i.e., we assume that the adjoint wavefield
u^{\dagger}
perfectly satisfies the adjoint equation of motion (eq. (
34
)) and perturbations in the model parameters exist
(\delta\rho\neq\delta\lambda\neq\delta f\neq 0)
.

The Material Parameter and Source Fréchet Kernels

It follows that we can write the variation in the action
\chi
(eq. (
31
)) as follows

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \delta_{\textbf{m}}\chi&=-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}u^{\dagger}\left[\delta\rho\partial_{t}^{2}u-\delta\lambda\partial_{x}^{2}u-\delta f\right]\dif x\dif t,\\
&=-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\left[u^{\dagger}\delta\rho\partial_{t}^{2}u-\delta\lambda\partial_{x}u^{\dagger}\partial_{x}u-u^{\dagger}\delta f\right]\dif x\dif t,\\
&=\int_{\Omega}\left(\delta\ln\rho\,K_{\rho}+\delta\ln\lambda\text{K}_{\lambda}+\delta\ln f\,K_{f}\right)\dif x=0,\end{aligned}

(38)

where we have defined the following sensitivity kernels

\displaystyle K_{\rho}=-\rho\int_{0}^{T}u^{\dagger}\partial^{2}_{t}u\dif\tau=\rho\int_{0}^{T}\partial_{t}u^{\dagger}\partial_{t}u\dif\tau,\hskip 20.00003pt\text{K}_{\lambda}=-\lambda\int_{0}^{T}\partial_{x}u^{\dagger}\partial_{x}u\dif\tau,\hskip 20.00003ptK_{f}=\int_{0}^{T}u^{\dagger}f\dif\tau.

(39)

The kernels given in eqs. (
39
) describe the change in the misfit function due to changes in the model parameters
(\rho,\lambda)
and source
(f)
, in terms of the original
(u)
and adjoint
(u^{\dagger})
wavefields. These are well known expressions used within the context of geophysical full-waveform inversion
(
10
;
22
;
36
;
32
, e.g.)
.

The Fréchet Root Kernel

Note that we can write eq. (
38
) as follows

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \delta_{\textbf{m}}\chi=\int_{\Omega}\left(\delta\ln\rho\left(\rho\delta^{2}\star_{t}K_{R}\right)+\delta\ln\lambda\left(\lambda\delta^{2}\star_{x}K_{R}\right)+\delta\ln f\,K_{f}\right)\dif x=0,\end{aligned}

(40)

where we have defined the Fréchet root kernel
K_{R}
defined as follows:

\displaystyle K_{R}=\int_{0}^{T}(uu^{\dagger})\dif\tau,

(41)

in addition to the source kernel
K_{f}
defined in eq. (
39
).

4.2
The Euler-Bernoulli Beam Equation

The Euler-Bernoulli Beam equation reads as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}u+V=\rho A\partial_{t}^{2}u+\partial_{x}^{2}\left(EI\partial_{x}^{2}u\right)+V\delta(x-x^{s})=0,

(42)

where
u=u(x,t)
, is the transverse displacement,
\rho
is the mass density,
A
is the cross-sectional area of the beam,
E
is the Young’s modulus,
I
is the area moment of inertia,
x^{s}
the source location and
V=V(t)
is an external time-dependent force
(
33
;
21
)
.

4.2.1
The 1D Adjoint Euler-Bernoulli Beam Equation

Once again, we assume now that we would like to minimize the difference between some data waveforms
d
(obtained from certain experiment) and the synthetic waveforms
u
(obtained using eq. (
42
)). We express this optimization problem as a constrained Lagrange minimization problem (eq. (
21
)), given by the following expression:

\displaystyle\chi=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\left\{\frac{1}{2}(d-u)^{2}\sum_{r}\delta(x-x^{r})-\mathsf{L}\left(\rho A\partial_{t}^{2}u+\partial_{x}^{2}\left(EI\partial_{x}^{2}u\right)+V\delta(x-x^{s})\right)\right\}\dif x\dif t,

(43)

where we aim to minimize the waveforms differences
(d-u)
at certain locations
x^{r}
and the vector Lagrange multiplier
\mathsf{L}(x,t)
(which minimizes the functional
\chi
) remains to be determined.

Taking the first variation
\delta
of the
\chi
functional (eq. (
43
)) with respect to model parameters
\textbf{m}=(\rho A,EI)
, the force term
V
and displacement
u
, and using KKT conditions (eqs. (
23
)) and assuming that the data are accurately enough described by the wave equation and following the detailed procedure described in Sec.
4.1.1
, leads to the following adjoint equations

\displaystyle\rho A\partial^{2}_{t}u^{\dagger}+\partial_{x}^{2}\left(EI\partial_{x}^{2}u^{\dagger}\right)=\sum_{r}\delta(x^{r}-x)(d-u),

(44)

where the adjoint source term is determined by the difference between the data and the synthetic wavefield,
(d-u)
, at the receiver locations
\sum_{r}\delta(x^{r}-x)
. Note that, for simplification purposes, we have considered the material parameters
(\rho A,EI)
as single parameters.

4.2.2
1D Fréchet Derivatives

If we assume that the adjoint wavefield
u^{\dagger}
perfectly satisfies the adjoint equation of motion (eq. (
44
)) and perturbations in the model parameters exist, we can find expression for the different Fréchet kernels.

The Material Parameter and Source Fréchet Kernels

It follows that we can write the variation in the action
\chi
(eq. (
43
)) as follows

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \delta_{\textbf{m}}\chi=\int_{\Omega}\left(\delta\ln\rho A\,K_{\rho A}+\delta\ln EI\text{K}_{EI}+\delta\ln V\,K_{V}\right)\dif x=0,\end{aligned}

(45)

where we have defined the following sensitivity kernels

\displaystyle K_{\rho A}=\rho A\int_{0}^{T}\partial_{t}u^{\dagger}\partial_{t}u\dif\tau,\hskip 20.00003pt\text{K}_{EI}=EI\int_{0}^{T}\partial_{x}^{2}u^{\dagger}\partial_{x}^{2}u\dif\tau,\hskip 20.00003ptK_{V}=\int_{0}^{T}u^{\dagger}V\dif\tau.

(46)

The kernels given in eqs. (
39
) describe the change in the misfit function due to changes in the model parameters
(\rho A,EI)
and source
(V)
, in terms of the original
(u)
and adjoint
(u^{\dagger})
wavefields.

The Fréchet Root Kernel

Note that we can write eq. (
45
) as follows

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \delta_{\textbf{m}}\chi=\int_{\Omega}\left(\delta\ln\rho A\left(\rho A\delta^{2}\star_{t}K_{R}\right)+\delta\ln EI\left(EI\delta^{4}\star_{x}K_{R}\right)+\delta\ln V\,K_{V}\right)\dif x=0,\end{aligned}

(47)

where we have defined the Fréchet root kernel
K_{R}
as in eq. (
41
), in addition to the source kernel
K_{V}
defined in eq. (
46
).

5
Applications to Complex Valued PDEs

We next extend the presented theory to two time-dependent 1D complex PDEs: (1) the complex transport equation and (2) the zero potential Schrödinger equation.

5.1
The 1D Complex Transport Equation

We now consider that the displacement
u
is a complex valued vector,
(\textbf{u}\in\mathbb{C})
and it is governed by the complex 1D transport equation given by the following expression

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\textbf{u}-\textbf{f}=\textbf{i}\sqrt{\rho}\partial_{t}\textbf{u}-\sqrt{\lambda}\partial_{x}\bar{\textbf{u}}-\textbf{f}\delta(x-x^{s})=0,\hskip 20.00003pt\text{with}\hskip 20.00003pt\textbf{u}(x,0)=\partial_{t}\textbf{u}(x,0)=0,

(48)

where
\bar{\textbf{u}}
is the complex conjugate of
u
,
\lambda
is a Lamé parameter,
\rho
the density,
x^{s}
the source location and
f
certain time dependent function. The spatial
x
and temporal
t
coordinates are considered to be real-valued variables as well as the propagation velocity
c=\sqrt{\lambda/\rho}
, i.e.,
x,t,\lambda,\rho\in\mathbb{R}
.

As we will next see, the physics of eq. (
48
) is similar to the second-order wave equation (eq. (
27
)) with a slight difference. To illustrate this, we write the complex displacement vector
u
as

\displaystyle\textbf{u}=u_{R}+\textbf{i}u_{I},

(49)

where
u_{R},u_{I}
are the real and imaginary parts respectively. Assuming constant density
\rho
, we can write eq. (
48
), after a little algebra, as follows

\displaystyle\rho\partial_{t}^{2}u_{R}-\lambda\partial_{x}^{2}u_{R}

\displaystyle=f_{R}\delta(x-x^{s}),

(50)

\displaystyle\rho\partial_{t}^{2}u_{I}-\lambda\partial_{x}^{2}u_{I}

\displaystyle=f_{I}\delta(x-x^{s}),

(51)

where
f_{R}
and
f_{I}
are the real and imaginary parts of the source term
f
and we have assume initial conditions at rest, ie.e,
\textbf{u}(x,0)=\partial_{t}\textbf{u}(x,0)=0
. Note that the real and imaginary parts of the displacement
(u_{R},u_{I})
are completely decoupled, and the physics governing each of them is simply the same as the second-order wave equation described before.

5.1.1
The 1D Adjoint Complex Transport Equations

We can write the constrained Lagrange minimization problem eq. (
21
) as follows

\displaystyle\chi=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\left\{\frac{1}{2}(\textbf{d}-\textbf{u})^{2}\sum_{r}\delta(x-x^{r})-\mathsf{L}\left(\sqrt{\rho}\textbf{i}\partial_{t}\textbf{u}-\sqrt{\lambda}\partial_{x}\bar{\textbf{u}}-\textbf{f}\right)\right\}\dif x\dif t,

(52)

where the vector Lagrange multiplier
\mathsf{L}(x,t)
(which minimizes the functional
\chi
) remains to be determined.

Taking the first variation
\delta
of the
\chi
functional given in eq. (
52
) with respect to model parameters
\textbf{m}=(\rho,\lambda)
, the force term
f
and displacement
u
, and using KKT conditions eq. (
23
) and assuming that the data are accurately enough described by the complex transport equation (eq. (
48
)), i.e.,
\partial_{\mathsf{L}}\chi=\mathcal{L}\textbf{u}-\textbf{f}=0
, we can write the following

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \delta_{\textbf{m}}\chi=&-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\mathsf{L}\left[\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\rho}}\delta\rho\textbf{i}\partial_{t}\textbf{u}-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\lambda}}\delta\lambda\partial_{x}\bar{\textbf{u}}-\delta\textbf{f}\right]\dif x\dif t=0,\\
\delta_{u}\chi=&\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\sum_{r}\delta(x-x^{r})(\textbf{d}-\textbf{u})\delta\textbf{u}\dif x\dif t-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\mathsf{L}\left[\sqrt{\rho}\textbf{i}\partial_{t}\delta\textbf{u}-\sqrt{\lambda}\partial_{x}\delta\bar{\textbf{u}}\right]\dif x\dif t=0.\end{aligned}

(53)

In the absence of perturbations in the model parameters
(\delta\rho=\delta\lambda=\delta\textbf{f}=0)
, i.e.,
\delta_{\textbf{m}}\chi=0
, and following the same procedure above, the variation in the action
\chi
is stationary
(\delta_{\textbf{m}}\chi+\delta_{\mathsf{L}}\chi+\delta_{\textbf{u}}\chi=0)
if

\displaystyle\sqrt{\rho}\textbf{i}\partial_{t}\mathsf{L}-\sqrt{\lambda}\partial_{x}\mathsf{L}=\sum_{r}\delta(x^{r}-x)(\textbf{d}-\textbf{u}),\hskip 20.00003pt\text{with}\hskip 20.00003pt\mathsf{L}(x,0)=\partial_{t}\mathsf{L}(x,0)=0,

(54)

where again
\mathsf{L}=\mathsf{L}(x,T-t)
. Without having previous information of the Lagrange multiplier
\mathsf{L}
, it seems reasonable to assume
\mathsf{L}(x,T-t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\textbf{u}(x,T-t)=\textbf{u}^{\dagger}(x,t)
(eq. (
35
)), thus, the adjoint wavefield
\textbf{u}^{\dagger}
is equal to the time-reversed complex wavefield
\textbf{u}(x,T-t)
. The new defined adjoint wavefield must satisfy the equations of motion given for
\mathsf{L}
(eq. (
54
)), that is,

\displaystyle\sqrt{\rho}\partial_{t}\textbf{i}\textbf{u}^{\dagger}-\sqrt{\lambda}\partial_{x}\bar{\textbf{u}}^{\dagger}=\sum_{r}\delta(x^{r}-x)(\textbf{d}-\textbf{u}).

(55)

5.1.2
1D Fréchet Derivatives

In the following, the dependence of
u^{\dagger}
on the model is ignored, i.e., we assume that the adjoint wavefield
u^{\dagger}
perfectly satisfies the adjoint equation of motion eq. (
54
) and there are perturbations in the model parameters
(\delta\rho\neq\delta\lambda\neq\delta f\neq 0)
.

The Material Parameter and Source Fréchet Kernels

We can write the variation in the action eq. (
53
) as follows

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \delta_{\textbf{m}}\chi&=-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\textbf{u}^{\dagger}\left[\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\rho}}\delta\rho\textbf{i}\partial_{t}\textbf{u}-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\lambda}}\delta\lambda\partial_{x}\bar{\textbf{u}}-\delta\textbf{f}\right]\dif x\dif t,\\
&=\int_{\Omega}\left(\delta\ln\rho\,K_{\rho}+\delta\ln\lambda\text{K}_{\lambda}+\delta\ln\textbf{f}\,K_{\textbf{f}}\right)\dif x=0,\end{aligned}

(56)

where we have defined the following sensitivity kernels

\displaystyle K_{\rho}=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\sqrt{\rho}\textbf{u}^{\dagger}\textbf{i}\partial_{t}\textbf{u}\dif\tau,\hskip 20.00003pt\text{K}_{\lambda}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\sqrt{\lambda}\textbf{u}^{\dagger}\partial_{x}\bar{\textbf{u}}\dif\tau,\hskip 20.00003ptK_{\textbf{f}}=\int_{0}^{T}\textbf{u}^{\dagger}\textbf{f}\dif\tau.

(57)

Once again, the kernels given in eqs. (
57
) give us the change in the misfit function due to changes in the model parameters
(\rho,\lambda)
and source
(\textbf{f})
, in terms of the original
(\textbf{u})
and adjoint
(\textbf{u}^{\dagger})
wavefields. Note that we can change the position of the spatial derivative in the expression for
\text{K}_{\lambda}
. By placing the spatial derivative on the Fréchet kernel
\text{K}_{\lambda}
, we change the nature of the contribution to the kernel: instead of directly multiplying the wavefield
u^{\dagger}
with the spatial derivative of
\bar{u}
, we are considering how
\rho
itself changes in space and how those changes affect the sensitivity
\text{K}_{\lambda}
.

The Fréchet Root Kernel

We can write the variation in the action eq. (
53
) as follows

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \delta_{\textbf{m}}\chi=\int_{\Omega}\left(\delta\ln\rho\left(\frac{\textbf{i}\sqrt{\rho}}{2}\delta\star_{t}K_{R_{\rho}}\right)+\delta\ln\lambda\left(\frac{\sqrt{\lambda}}{2}\delta\star_{x}K_{R_{\lambda}}\right)+\delta\ln\textbf{f}\,K_{\textbf{f}}\right)\dif x=0,\end{aligned}

(58)

where we have defined the Fréchet root kernels
K_{R_{\rho}},K_{R_{\lambda}}
as follows

\displaystyle K_{R_{\rho}}=\int_{0}^{T}(\textbf{u}^{\dagger}\textbf{u})\dif\tau,\hskip 10.00002ptK_{R_{\lambda}}=\int_{0}^{T}(\textbf{u}^{\dagger}\bar{\textbf{u}})\dif\tau,

(59)

in addition to the source kernel
K_{\textbf{f}}
defined in eq. (
57
).

5.2
The Zero Potential Schrödinger Equation

The time-dependent Schrödinger equation (TDSE) for a free particle in one dimension (with no potential) reads as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\psi}+J=\textbf{i}\hbar\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\psi}+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\partial^{2}_{x}\boldsymbol{\psi}+J=0,

(60)

where
\psi(x,t)
is the complex-valued wavefunction,
\hbar
is the reduced Planck constant,
m=m(x)
is the particle effective mass and
J(x,t)
is a source term that may represent an external force or interaction acting on the particle
(
42
;
29
;
15
;
28
)
.

Note that we have considered a variable mass
(m=m(x))
, which accounts for how the kinetic energy operator changes from one material to another.
If we split the wavefunction
\boldsymbol{\psi}
into its real (
\psi_{R}
) and imaginary (
\psi_{I}
) parts, that is,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\psi}=\psi_{R}+\textbf{i}\psi_{I},

(61)

and assume
J=0
, after some algebra, one can show that if we decouple real and imaginary parts, the Schrödinger equation (eq. (
60
)) can be written as follows

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{t}^{2}+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{4m^{2}}\partial_{x}^{4}\right)(\psi_{R},\psi_{I})=0.

(62)

One can clearly see that the decoupled Schrödinger equation has the same mathematical structure as the Euler-Bernoulli Beam equation (eq. (
42
)).

5.2.1
The 1D Adjoint (Zero Potential) Schrödinger Equation

We can write the constrained Lagrange minimization problem eq. (
21
) as follows

\displaystyle\chi=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\left\{\frac{1}{2}(\textbf{d}-\boldsymbol{\psi})^{2}\sum_{r}\delta(x-x^{r})-\mathsf{L}\left(\textbf{i}\hbar\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\psi}+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\partial^{2}_{x}\boldsymbol{\psi}+J\right)\right\}\dif x\dif t,

(63)

where the vector Lagrange multiplier
\mathsf{L}(x,t)
(which minimizes the functional
\chi
) remains to be determined.

Taking the first variation
\delta
of the
\chi
functional given in eq. (
63
) with respect to model parameters
\textbf{m}=(m)
, the force term
J
and the wavefunction
\boldsymbol{\psi}
, and using KKT conditions eq. (
23
) and assuming that the data are accurately enough described by the zero potential Schrödinger equation (eq. (
60
)), we can write the following

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \delta_{\textbf{m}}\chi=&\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\mathsf{L}\left[\textbf{i}\delta\hbar\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\psi}+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m^{2}}\delta m\partial^{2}_{x}\boldsymbol{\psi}-\delta J\right]\dif x\dif t=0,\\
\delta_{\psi}\chi=&\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\sum_{r}\delta(x-x^{r})(\textbf{d}-\boldsymbol{\psi})\delta\boldsymbol{\psi}\dif x\dif t-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\mathsf{L}\left[\textbf{i}\hbar\partial_{t}\delta\boldsymbol{\psi}+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\partial^{2}_{x}\delta\boldsymbol{\psi}\right]\dif x\dif t=0.\end{aligned}

(64)

In the absence of perturbations in the model parameters
(\delta m=\delta J=0)
, i.e.,
\delta_{\textbf{m}}\chi=0
, and following the same procedure above, the variation in the action
\chi
is stationary
(\delta_{\textbf{m}}\chi+\delta_{\mathsf{L}}\chi+\delta_{\psi}\chi=0)
if

\displaystyle\textbf{i}\hbar\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\dagger}+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\partial^{2}_{x}\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\dagger}=\sum_{r}\delta(x^{r}-x)(\textbf{d}-\boldsymbol{\psi}).

(65)

5.2.2
1D Fréchet Derivatives

In the following, the dependence of
\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\dagger}
on the model is ignored, i.e., we assume that the wavefunction
\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\dagger}
perfectly satisfies the adjoint equation of motion eq. (
65
) and there are perturbations in the model parameters
(\delta\hbar\neq\delta m\neq\delta J\neq 0)
.

A change in
\hbar
affects the spread of the wavefunction and the energy level sand the spatial distribution of the wavefunction are directly affected by
\hbar
.

The Material Parameter and Source Fréchet Kernels

We can write the variation in the action eq. (
64
) as follows

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \delta_{\textbf{m}}\chi&=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\dagger}\left[\textbf{i}\delta\hbar\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\psi}+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m^{2}}\delta m\partial^{2}_{x}\boldsymbol{\psi}+\delta J\right]\dif x\dif t,\\
&=\int_{\Omega}\left(\delta\ln\hbar K_{\hbar}+\delta\ln mK_{m}+\delta\ln J\,K_{J}\right)\dif x=0,\end{aligned}

(66)

where we have defined the following sensitivity kernels

\displaystyle K_{\hbar}=\textbf{i}\hbar\int_{0}^{T}\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\dagger}\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\psi}\dif\tau,\hskip 20.00003ptK_{m}=\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\int_{0}^{T}\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\dagger}\partial_{x}^{2}\boldsymbol{\psi}\dif\tau,\hskip 20.00003ptK_{J}=\int_{0}^{T}\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\dagger}J\dif\tau.

(67)

The Fréchet Root Kernel

We can write the variation in the action eq. (
64
) as follows

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \delta_{\textbf{m}}\chi=\int_{\Omega}\left(\delta\ln\hbar\left(\textbf{i}\hbar\delta\star_{t}K_{R}\right)+\delta\ln m\left(\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\delta^{2}\star_{x}K_{R}\right)+\delta\ln JK_{J}\right)\dif x=0,\end{aligned}

(68)

where we have defined the Fréchet root kernel
K_{R}
as follows

\displaystyle K_{R}=\int_{0}^{T}(\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\psi})\dif\tau,

(69)

in addition to the source kernel
K_{J}
defined in eq. (
67
).

Fréchet Kernels Gallery

We next analyze the root Fréchet kernels previously obtained for each PDE. For the numerical solution of the wave equations, we apply the finite-difference method (see

Selection of the source time functions

we consider, in all cases, initial conditions at rest, i.e.,
u(x,0)=\partial_{t}u(x,0)=0
, and to generate motion we consider a time dependent source time function
f(t)
located at certain position
x=x_{0}
. Two cases for
f(t)
are assumed:

1.

We assume that
f(t)
is a real valued function, i.e.,
f\in\mathbb{R}
, and given by a Gaussian pulse centered at
t=t_{0}
with certain dominant frequency
f_{0}
and amplitude
A
, given by the following expression

\displaystyle f(t)=A\exp\left(-\omega_{0}^{2}\frac{(t-t_{0})^{2}}{2}\right),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{with}\hskip 10.00002pt\omega_{0}=2\pi f_{0}.

(70)

2.

We assume that
f(t)
is a complex valued function, i.e.,
f\in\mathbb{C}
, and given by a modulated Gaussian pulse centered at
t=t_{0}
with certain dominant frequency
f_{0}
and amplitude

, given by the following expression

\displaystyle f(t)=A\exp\left(-\omega_{0}^{2}\frac{(t-t_{0})^{2}}{2}\right)\exp\left(\textbf{i}n\omega_{0}t\right),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{with}\hskip 10.00002ptn\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\omega_{0}=2\pi f_{0}.

(71)

The difference between a Gaussian (eq. (
70
)) and a modulated Gaussian (eq. (
71
)) is that the word
modulated
means a pulse (Gaussian in this case) oscillates in time. The Gaussian envelope (eq. (
70
)) determines the time duration of the wave packet and the oscillatory term (complex exponential in (eq. (
71
)) represents the central frequency of oscillation, dictating how fast the Gaussian pulse oscillates in time.

To develop some intuition, let us we assume a Gaussian pulse centered at
t_{0}=2
s and with a dominant period of 2s (

Hz, see Fig.

Hz, see Fig.
1
–b). The resulting real part of the modulated Gaussian pulse does not resemble anymore a Gaussian like wavelet (see Fig.
1
–c) and the imaginary part resembles the first-order derivative of a Gaussian (see Fig.
1
–d).

6.1
Fréchet Root Kernels of the Second-Order Wave Equation

We only analyze the Fréchet root kernel (eq. (

7
Discussions

7.1
The Fréchet Sensitivity Kernels

Fréchet sensitivity kernels quantify how small changes of a parameter in the chosen PDE affect the corresponding outputs (e.g. measurements of physical observables). In seismological inverse problems, sensitivity kernels describe how an observable responds to changes in underlying parameters of the elastic wave equation.

Different Fréchet sensitivity kernel can be defined/derived depending on the chosen perturbed material parameter such as density and/or elastic parameters. They include interactions between the a modified version of the forward and adjoint wavefields. By modified version we mean that often one needs to take into consideration temporal/spatial derivatives of the forward wavefield interacting with the adjoint.

The definition of the adjoint wavefield depends on the misfit functional that we choose to evaluate. In seismology, for example, one is often interested into differences in travel times and/or waveforms. These differences define the source of motion of the adjoint wavefield.

The adjoint wavefield is by definition is backward in time propagated wavefield that follows the same physical rules of the forward wavefield (same PDE). One can emphasize that the physical nature of this backward in time propagated PDE is avoided. This is because, at least within the seismological community, the adjoint wavefield is simply considered a mathematical object with not physical justification. This simply allows seismologist to obtain information about the interior of the Earth without asking too many question of the mathematical machinery used. Same scenario is presented in the understanding of quantum mechanics. One does not need to understand quantum mechanics in order to make reliable predictions wit the theory.

After the selection of the misfit functional and calculation of the adjoint wavefield we are able to have access to the different Fréchet sensitivity kernels, which are nothing more that a particular case of the Fréchet root kernel.

7.2
The Fréchet Root Kernel

We have defined the Fréchet root kernel as the simplest Fréchet kernel from which all others material sensitivities can be derived. Based on this definition, we have analyzed the root kernel obtained for four different PDEs: the first group of two PDEs are real valued and the second group complex valued.

The group of real valued PDEs is simply the real part of the complex valued PDEs. In this sense, understanding Fréchet root kernels for the real valued scenarios gives us the fundamentals to understand the complex valued Fréchet root kernels. This is because we have considered for the real valued PDEs the (i) second-order wave and (ii) the Euler-Bernoulli beam equations and for the complex-valued PDEs: (i) the complex-transport and (ii) Schrödinger (with zero potential) equations, that when we decompose into real and imaginary parts the are simply the second-order wave and Euler-Bernoulli beam equations.

As one can expect, in all cases the Fréchet root sensitivity is a constant between the source and receiver when constant material parameters are chosen. Interestingly, in all cases, depending of the initial condition of motion chosen, different sensitivity (in waveform and amplitude) is evidenced at the source and receiver locations.

In order to understand this effect at the source and receiver locations, we analyzed the integrand of the Fréchet root kernel by separate for each considered PDE. We found that the instant sensitivity, i.e., the Fréchet root kernel evaluated at a certain instant, travel like a wave (propagated by the considered PDE) with a waveform depending on the initial condition chosen.

While for real-valued PDEs, this may not seem an striking result, for complex-valued PDEs it is. This is because, as mentioned by
1
, we are free to choose the source of motion that generates the adjoint wavefield. Thus for the particular selection that the source of motion of the adjoint wavefield is the complex conjugate of the recorded forward wavefield we recover always a Gaussian like shape for the Fréchet root kernel evaluated at a any instant. This is the well known Born rule of quantum mechanics, derived from Fréchet sensitivity kernels instead of making ad-hoc assumptions.

7.3
The Born Rule

In quantum mechanics, the Born rule is an ad-hoc rule and a fundamental principle that describes a mathematical relationship between the wavefunction and measurement outcomes. It is essential for understanding what we observe using the Schrödinger equation
(
15
;
29
;
42
;
28
, e.g.)
.

In simple terms, the Born rule relates the wavefunction
(\psi)
to the probability of different measurement outcomes: It states that the probability
P(\textbf{x})
of measuring a particle at position
x
is given by the modulus of the wavefunction as follows

\displaystyle P(\textbf{x},t_{n})=|\boldsymbol{\psi}(\textbf{x},t_{n}))|^{2}=\boldsymbol{\psi}(\textbf{x},t_{n})\bar{\boldsymbol{\psi}}(\textbf{x},t_{n}),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{with}\hskip 10.00002ptn\in\mathbb{N},

(72)

where
\bar{\boldsymbol{\psi}}
is the complex conjugate of the wavefunction
(\boldsymbol{\psi})
. The wave function itself is complex, but the square modulus,
|\boldsymbol{\psi}(\textbf{x})|^{2}
, gives a real, non-negative probability density for finding the particle at a specific location. The, adhoc-proposed, Born rule provides the link between before and after measurements in quantum mechanics.

The collapse of the wavefunction is, however, yet not understood. This collapse is non-deterministic and instantaneous, leading to a definite outcome. Simply, a probability distribution where the particle is most likely to be located.

7.4
The Born Rule from Sensitivity Kernels

Although the Born rule has never been derived from Fréchet sensitivity kernels, both concepts can be viewed as addressing a form of sensitivity: the Born rule characterizes the sensitivity of measurement outcomes, while Fréchet sensitivity kernels quantify how a functional responds to perturbations in the parameters on which it depends. Both therefore reflect a common abstract idea—how small changes in a
state
influence the observable behavior of a system—even though they arise from distinct mathematical and physical frameworks.

By applying the adjoint method to the Schrödinger equation and computing the associated Fréchet root sensitivity kernel, we find that the resulting sensitivity of a measurement (i.e., the probability density) to perturbations in the wavefunction takes exactly the same form as the Born rule. Specifically, the Fréchet root kernel for the Schrödinger equation describes how infinitesimal changes in the wavefunction influence the probability distribution, and for position measurements this sensitivity coincides with the squared modulus of the wavefunction.

Thus, the Born rule can be interpreted as a special case of a more general sensitivity-analysis framework, in which the adjoint method provides the system’s response (here, the probability distribution) to small variations of the wavefunction.

8
Conclusions

In this work, we extended the adjoint method to complex-valued PDEs and applied it to four representative equations. We introduced the
Fréchet root sensitivity kernel
, defined as the most fundamental Fréchet kernel from which all other material-sensitivity kernels can be derived.

We demonstrated how this framework clarifies the structure of Fréchet sensitivity kernels for both real- and complex-valued PDEs. In all considered cases with constant material parameters, the Fréchet root kernel is constant between source and receiver. However, its instantaneous form—evaluated at a fixed time—propagates like a wave governed by the underlying PDE, with a waveform determined by the chosen initial condition. While this behavior is expected for real-valued PDEs, its implications for complex-valued PDEs are particularly notable: for the Schrödinger and complex-transport equations, choosing the adjoint source as the complex conjugate of the forward wavefield leads to a Gaussian-shaped instantaneous sensitivity, consistent with previous findings.

We further established a direct connection between the
Born rule
of quantum mechanics and the
Fréchet root sensitivity kernel
of the Schrödinger equation with zero potential. Our analysis suggests that the Born rule is not merely an ad-hoc postulate; instead, it naturally emerges from the sensitivity of quantum systems to perturbations of the wavefunction. Under this interpretation, a measurement corresponds to the system’s response to small changes in the wavefunction, and the resulting sensitivity kernel reproduces the probability density given by the Born rule.

This connection between Fréchet root kernels and the Born rule provides a promising foundation for a more unified understanding of quantum measurements, linking concepts from quantum theory with adjoint-based sensitivity analysis commonly used in optimization and inverse problems. A complete mathematical derivation and extension of these results will be presented in a forthcoming contribution.
