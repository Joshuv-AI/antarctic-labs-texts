The effect of rotor airflow from plant protection UAV on pesticide application in rice
Authors: Hao Wang, Cunnian Fu, Sujie Zhou, Zhuan Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Multi-rotor plant protection drones are extensively utilized in rice field operations. However, the airflow generated by the rotors not only disturbs the rice canopy but also affects the deposition of pesticide droplets. The investigation of the impact of rotor wind fields on the protection of rice crops has the potential to enhance the effectiveness of operations of this nature. The present study firstly acquired aerial imagery of plant protection drone operations via the utilization of aerial photography, and subsequently employed machine vision technology to investigate the disturbance patterns of rotor wind fields on rice canopies. Notably, there is currently no effective experimental method to observe the disturbance of rice canopies caused by the wind field generated by plant protection drone rotors, and machine vision processing is proven to be an effective approach, which is a key innovation of this study. This study innovatively adopts an integrated experimental–numerical approach, a distinct advancement over existing Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (UAV) spraying and airflow research that typically focuses on single experimental or simulation methods. Aerial photography and machine vision were used to explore canopy disturbance patterns, while reverse engineering combined with thrust tests and numerical simulations verified rotor model precision and analyzed droplet deposition. Core findings show that flight speeds of 3–4 m/s enable effective overlap between canopy disturbance and droplet deposition zones, achieving optimal plant protection effects; higher speeds cause droplet drift and zone misalignment, reducing efficacy. In addition, this study explores the spray deposition of plant protection drones based on numerical simulation methods, and assists in judging the effect of plant protection operations through the coincidence of droplet deposition and canopy disturbance zones. This integrated approach and related findings provide a reliable technical basis for optimizing UAV flight parameters, and have certain reference significance for the technical research and development of plant protection drones and the setting of operation parameters.

Introduction

Rice is widely regarded as one of the world’s most significant staple crops, alongside wheat and corn, which are collectively considered to be the three primary staple crops. It is estimated that over half of the global population relies on rice as their primary food source, particularly in Asia where more than 60% of people derive the majority of their daily caloric intake from rice. However, the cultivation of rice is susceptible to a wide variety of pests and diseases, which cause annual grain losses amounting to approximately 5% to 30% of total production. Consequently, the proactive implementation of rice pest and disease control measures is imperative to mitigate yield losses and ensure food security. Presently, chemical control remains the primary method for managing rice pests and diseases on a global scale. The application of pesticides can be categorized into three methods: manual spraying, ground-based mechanical spraying, and aerial spraying. Among these, the utilization of UAV for the purpose of aerial spraying represents a novel approach to the management of rice pests and diseases1.

It is evident that plant protection UAV offers a number of significant advantages2–4. Firstly, it possesses a high degree of manoeuvrability, is straightforward to operate, requires minimal labor intensity, and does not result in crop damage. Secondly, the necessity for operators to enter the treatment area during operations is eliminated, thereby effecting a substantial reduction in pesticide exposure to humans, a minimization of hazards, and the assurance of maximum safety. Thirdly, the implementation of these systems ensures high operational efficiency, thereby facilitating the expeditious implementation of pest control measures during sudden large-scale outbreaks, thus ensuring the mitigation of economic losses. Fourthly, they achieve high rates of pesticide utilization. The employment of plant protection UAV has been demonstrated to result in a 20–40% reduction in pesticide usage when compared with traditional methods, whilst simultaneously conserving over 90% of water. The employment of UAV operations has been demonstrated to enhance efficiency by a factor of more than 40 in comparison with manual labor, with the capacity to cover a range of 80 to 100 mu per hour, whilst concomitantly effecting a reduction in pesticide usage by an average of 30%. The utilization of multi-rotor plant protection UAV spraying technology has witnessed a substantial increase in adoption across China, thereby establishing itself as a predominant technique within the realm of crop protection1.

Multi-rotor plant protection UAV exhibit two novel characteristics in their pesticide application methods. Firstly, the airflow generated by the rotating rotors is relatively intense5. Secondly, the droplet size of the atomized pesticide solution is very small. The efficacy of pesticide application is closely related to the interaction between these two characteristics. Consequently, researchers have conducted extensive studies on the application technology of plant protection UAV. A series of experiments have been conducted by researchers in order to investigate the effects of plant protection UAVs on crops and fruit trees6,7. The focus of these experiments was to examine the distribution of rotor wind fields and their effect on droplet deposition. The investigation explored key influencing factors including droplet size (ranging from 150 to 300 μm), drone operating altitude (1.5–3.0 m above the rice canopy), flight speed (2–6 m/s), and spray application rate per acre (15–30 L/acre), with particular focus on their effects on droplet deposition (evaluated as 0.5–3.2 µg/cm2 on leaf surfaces), canopy penetration (measured as 35–82% droplet coverage on middle-lower stems), spray drift (accounting for 5–23% of total sprayed volume at wind speeds < 2 m/s), and pest control efficacy (72–91% pest mortality in target rice crops 7 days after application)8. The majority of these studies utilize experimental methods. However, given the complexity of natural field environments, the numerous random factors that are in play, and the constraints imposed by crop growth cycles, it is challenging to derive consistent research findings through experimental approaches alone.

The development of Computational Fluid Dynamics(CFD) and the enhancement of computing capabilities has precipitated the emergence of numerical simulation as a seminal research methodology for investigating the distribution patterns of rotor wind fields and the deposition mechanisms of fog droplets in UAVs. A preliminary investigation into the rotor wind field structures of single-rotor, quad-rotor and hexa-rotor plant protection UAVs has been conducted by researchers9–11. This investigation has employed a combination of experimental methods and numerical simulation techniques, with the objective of achieving a more profound comprehension of the rotor wind field structures during both hovering and operational states. The wind field patterns of these three drone models and their temporal variation characteristics have been obtained. These studies have enhanced researchers’ understanding of the rotor wind field distribution characteristics of plant protection UAVs to a certain extent. Furthermore, they furnish pertinent references for the selection of operational parameters when undertaking analogous crop protection tasks on specific crops using particular drone models.

The movement patterns of droplets from plant protection UAVs represent a significant research area, as the stable flight of these devices generates a strong rotor wind field that exerts a certain coercive effect on droplet motion12,13. Researchers are investigating the distribution characteristics and developmental patterns of drone rotor wind fields with the ultimate goal of more accurately exploring and understanding the impact of these wind fields on droplet movement. It is evident from the extant research findings that an increase in the flight altitude of drones has a detrimental effect on the distribution of the rotor flow field asymmetry14. This phenomenon results in a more asymmetric distribution of droplets as the drone ascends, leading to a reduction in droplet coverage on the left side of the drone. Concurrently, as altitude rises, the coefficient of variation in droplet deposition declines, leading to more uniform deposition but diminished deposition mass. A range of studies have analyzed the effects of factors such as droplet size, droplet velocity, adjuvant solution properties, and application equipment on droplet drift15,16. These analyses have established droplet drift energy models and developed droplet drift testing systems and methodologies specifically for plant protection UAVs. The majority of existing studies have focused on analyzing the effects of factors such as drone rotor wind fields, fog droplet size, and natural meteorological conditions on fog droplet drift, yielding significant research outcomes17,18.

However, despite the advancements in multi-rotor plant protection UAVs, several challenges persist during the execution of aerial spraying operations for rice. The primary reasons for this phenomenon can be identified in two key aspects: Firstly, due to the relatively weak rigidity of rice stems, the downwash airflow generated by the rotors disturbs the rice canopy, creating a lodging zone within the airflow field. This zone demonstrates a certain degree of lag relative to the moving drone. While the formation of this lodged canopy zone is inevitable, the exposed undersides of leaves and the middle-to-lower sections of stems within this area actually enhance the penetration and coverage of pesticide droplets on the rice plants19,20. Secondly, the interaction mechanisms between the rotor wind field and spray droplets, as well as their combined interaction with crops, remain unclear. The drift and deposition characteristics of spray droplets are not well defined, and further investigation is required to determine whether the deposition zone under wind field influence aligns with the rice canopy disturbance zone21,22. Consequently, the employment of specific technical methodologies to investigate the spatial patterns of rice canopy disturbance zones and droplet deposition areas under rotor wind fields can facilitate the development of precision application techniques integrated with intelligent control systems.

This study has certain innovations for the application of plant protection UAVs in rice field operations, addressing the aforementioned research gaps and limitations. Firstly, aiming at the lack of effective experimental methods for observing rice canopy disturbance caused by UAV rotor wind fields, this study proposes and verifies that machine vision processing combined with aerial photography is an effective observation approach. Secondly, unlike most existing studies that rely solely on experiments or numerical simulations23–25, this study adopts an integrated experimental–numerical approach, combining machine vision observation, reverse engineering, thrust tests, and CFD numerical simulations to systematically explore the interaction between rotor wind fields, rice canopy disturbance, and droplet deposition. Thirdly, this study innovatively takes the coincidence degree between the canopy disturbance zone and the droplet deposition zone as a key index to evaluate the effect of plant protection operations, providing a new perspective for the optimization of UAV operation parameters. Finally, the research findings clarify the influence of flight speed on the spatial distribution of disturbance and deposition zones, providing direct technical support for the precise setting of UAV operation parameters and the improvement of plant protection efficiency.

Materials and methods

The present study proposes an innovative approach to address the aforementioned issue. The study employs image processing methodologies to investigate the disturbance patterns of rotor wind fields on rice canopies. Secondly, numerical simulations are conducted based on reverse modelling of plant protection UAV rotors, with the aim of validating the reliability of the rotor model. Thirdly, the flight operations of plant protection UAVs are simulated in order to obtain the deposition patterns of fog droplets under the influence of rotor wind fields. Finally, the spatial relationship between rice canopy disturbance zones and droplet deposition zones is investigated, leading to conclusive findings.

The disturbance effects of rotor airflow on rice canopy

During the course of drone spraying operations, the location of the canopy disturbance zone is subject to continuous fluctuation, and crop swaying exhibits dynamic characteristics, thereby rendering it challenging to obtain direct and precise characteristic information of this area through physical measurement methods. The present study collected drone operation imagery data via the utilization of aerial photography, with the subsequent analysis concentrating on the characteristics of the canopy disturbance zone. Furthermore, machine vision technology was utilized to investigate the variation patterns of the aforementioned zone at varying operating speeds.

Imaging acquisition

The impact of rotor wind fields on rice canopies is influenced by three primary factors: the aircraft model, the operating altitude, and the operating speed. The DJI Agras T20 was selected as the operational model. During the operation, the drone’s flight altitude was maintained at a consistent level; the operating altitude was set to 2 m in accordance with standard operational parameters. In order to enhance operational efficiency, flight speeds varied significantly, with reported ranges spanning from 1 to 8 m/s26,27. In relation to the disruption of crop canopy integrity, it has been demonstrated that excessively high flight speeds result in weaker downward wash airflow. The drone sweeps over the canopy in a strip-like pattern, causing minimal plant sway and flipping only a small number of leaves. This results in a restriction of the promotion of droplet deposition on the middle and lower plant layers and leaf undersides. This study utilizes flight speed as the variable of interest, with a particular focus on the commonly employed operational speeds of 3 m/s, 4 m/s, 5 m/s, and 6 m/s.

Aerial imagery of crop protection drone operations was captured at the grain production functional zone in Guo Xi Subdistrict, Ouhai District, on July 10, 2025. At this stage, rice plants were in the panicle initiation and jointing phase, with an average plant height of approximately 80 centimeters. The drone employed for the purpose of plant protection in the present study was the DJI Agras T20, which was manufactured by DJI Innovation Technology Co., Ltd, as illustrated in Fig. 1a. The model under consideration features an effective spray width of 7 m, with four nozzles mounted directly beneath the rotors. The spray rate was set to 6 L/min per unit area, producing droplet sizes ranging from 50 to 300 μm. Tap water was used as the spray solution.

The drone model employed for the purpose of video recording in this study is the DJI Mavic 3, which possesses an approximate hovering time of 40 min, as illustrated in Fig. 1b. The maximum wind speeds to which the system can withstand are 12 m/s, and it is capable of maintaining hovering accuracy of ± 0.1 m vertically and ± 0.3 m horizontally. The camera lens specifications include a field of view (FOV) of 84°, an equivalent focal length of 24 mm, and an effective pixel count of 20 million. During the experiment, natural wind speeds measured by an anemometer ranged from 0 to 0.6 m/s, indicating relatively stable meteorological conditions that met operational requirements.

The methodology employed in the context of aerial photography is delineated in Fig. 1c. The plant protection UAV performs spraying operations along a pre-planned single path, while the aerial photography drone hovers alongside it at a height of 2 m to capture images. In order to ensure consistent image depth of field and operational safety for both drones, it is imperative to maintain a fixed distance of 30 m during filming. Multiple image data acquisitions are conducted at each operational speed, with a video frame rate of 120 frames per second. The positioning of yellow markers between the plant protection UAV and the aerial photography drone is a key observation of this study. In order to mitigate calculation errors caused by oblique viewing angles, video frames were selected from the straight line connecting the marker, the crop protection drone, and the aerial photography drone when calculating the lag distance of canopy disturbance areas.

Keyframe image processing

Initially, the drone operation imagery data is subjected to a process of pre-processing in order to extract video segments that are both clear and stable. In order to circumvent computational errors engendered by oblique viewing angles, the frame encompassing the crop protection drone positioned at the precise center of the image is designated as the key frame for calculation. Key frames at four specified speeds were captured as required, as demonstrated in the Fig. 2.

Given that both the canopy disturbance areas and the UAV are moving within the video frame sequence, the problem of extracting feature regions in images is transformed into identifying and extracting moving objects within the video frame sequence. The primary method for object tracking employs optical flow techniques. Optical flow is a method that calculates object motion information between adjacent frames by utilizing the temporal changes in pixels across image sequences and the correlation between consecutive frames21,22. Optical flow methods can be further categorized into two distinct types: sparse optical flow and dense optical flow. Sparse optical flow selects a feature of interest (e.g. edges or corners) to track its velocity vector, finding greater application in trajectory tracking30. Dense optical flow is a process which calculates the optical flow vector for every pixel in each frame31. Despite its comparatively reduced computing speed, it delivers more accurate and comprehensive results, rendering it suitable for applications involving motion object segmentation. The objective of this study is to identify areas of disturbance within the ever-changing crop canopy; consequently, the selection of dense optical flow methods is employed. As illustrated in Fig. 3, the keyframe images obtained through the utilization of dense optical flow processing are presented. The colored areas represent pixels that shift position as the video frame sequence changes, while the black areas indicate pixels with no displacement, i.e. the background region. At speeds of 3 m/s and 4 m/s, the canopy disturbance zone exhibits greater oscillation amplitude and higher pixel displacement intensity. At speeds of 5 m/s and 6 m/s, the oscillation amplitude in the disturbance zone diminishes, the oscillation zone elongates, and pixel displacement intensity weakens.

In images processed by the dense optical flow method, static objects are largely filtered out, effectively preserving the flying drone and the swaying rice canopy area in the image (Fig. 4a). In order to ascertain the relative positions between the canopy disturbance area and the drone, further processing is required. Initially, the image undergoes a conversion to grayscale, as illustrated in Fig. 4b. The image is subsequently subjected to binarisation, with a threshold range of 15 to 255 applied (Fig. 4c). The Canny edge detection algorithm is then applied in order to calculate the contours of feature regions. When the drone moves rapidly forward, the canopy disturbance area exhibits an approximately elliptical distribution. In order to ascertain the relative positional relationship between the specified area and the drone, an ellipse is fitted to the canopy disturbance area and the drone area. During the fitting process, a threshold is established to filter out noise points and extract the ellipse’s centroid (Fig. 4d).

Reverse modeling and numerical simulation of rotor blades

Reverse modeling

The extant research suggests that the utilization of CFD numerical simulation methods in the study of droplet deposition characteristics is a practice that yields favourable outcomes. A critical step in this process is the acquisition of a three-dimensional model of the rotor blades on the DJI Agras T20. This issue was addressed by employing a reverse engineering-based modelling approach to reconstruct the three-dimensional rotor blade model, as seen in Fig. 5. The EinScan-SP 3D scanner from Shining 3D was utilized in order to perform a 3D scan of the DJI Agras T20 rotor. The scanned point cloud data were imported into Geomagic Wrap software (Version 2017, URL: https://www.artec3d.com/fr/3d-software/geomagic-wrap ) to facilitate its processing and the subsequent generation of the rotor’s reconstructed surface. The construction of a 3D model of the rotor blade was subsequently facilitated using SolidWorks software (Version 2018, URL: https://www.solidworks.com ).

Fig. 5The reverse modeling workflow for rotor blades.

The reverse modeling workflow for rotor blades.

The 3D scanner utilized is an optical, non-contact scanning apparatus comprising three components: a computer, a scanning head and a tripod, as seen in Fig. 6a. The device uses a white LED light source and possesses a camera resolution of 1.3 megapixels, with a scanning depth of field ranging from 290 millimeters to 480 millimeters. In order to achieve optimal light reflection, an overhead scanning approach is adopted. The DJI Agras T20 rotor blades are composed of carbon fiber. It is imperative to note that, owing to the low reflectivity of black material, the blades require coloring with a white imaging agent. The scanner’s single-pass scanning range is 200 mm × 150 mm. However, the dimensions of the rotor are 382.4 mm, which necessitates the stitching of multiple segmented scans into a complete image. Consequently, it is imperative to establish feature points prior to scanning in order to guarantee that each segmented scan area comprises a minimum of three overlapping reference points, as seen in Fig. 6b. It has been demonstrated that the greater the number of overlapping reference points, the higher the accuracy of point cloud registration. The rotor structure is distinguished by a flat and elongated configuration. The blade thickness is measured at 8 mm at the hub, with a length of 382.4 mm and a width of less than 15 mm. The substantial curvature of the leading and trailing edges poses a considerable challenge for scanning. The acquisition process involved a total of 15 scans, thus ensuring comprehensive coverage of the rotor’s intricate details. The results of the point cloud acquisition process are presented in Fig. 6c.

The point cloud was imported into Geomagic Wrap, where the sampling on the point cloud can be refined. It is imperative that the data points are preserved in areas of high curvature and at the boundaries, whilst ensuring that the points on flat surfaces are uniformly reduced. Following a series of iterations of sampling, the final point cloud dataset comprises approximately 300,000 points, as seen in Fig. 7a. The processed point cloud was subsequently merged and encapsulated to generate polygonal data for the rotor. Subsequently, surface reconstruction was performed based on this polygonal data. The deviation cloud map of the reconstructed 3D model is shown in the Fig. 7b. The analysis revealed that the rotor deviations primarily ranged from − 0.0494 mm to 0.0494 mm, with maximum deviations of 0.3589 mm and − 0.1288 mm concentrated in areas of high curvature. The mean deviation ranged from − 0.0032 to 0.0085 mm, the standard deviation was 0.0204 mm, and the root mean square (RMS) was estimated at 0.0206 mm. The integration of the aforementioned data into the SolidWorks software, coupled with the utilization of the measured blade clamp data for the purpose of mirroring, facilitated the acquisition of the rotor’s three-dimensional geometric model, as seen in Fig. 7c.

Propeller performance test

In order to validate the application of the rotor’s inverse model to engineering simulations, thrust tests were conducted at different rotational speeds in pursuit of obtaining thrust values. The thrust testing apparatus is illustrated in Fig. 8. The Mayatech MT10PRO force tester was utilized to measure tensile force. The maximum load capacity of the apparatus is 10 kg, and the measurement error is 1 g. The UNI-T UT330 tachometer was utilized to measure the speed of the motor. The measurement range of the tachometer extends from 10 revolutions per minute (rpm) to 9999 rpm, with a measurement error margin of 0.1 rpm. The experimental motor is the DJI Agras T20 motor, with a KV rating of 75 rpm/V. The electronic speed controller (ESC) employed was the Haoying Skywalker ESC, which is capable of a maximum current of 60 A. The battery employed in the experiment was a 6 S lithium battery with a capacity of 16,000 mAh.

Fig. 8The thrust testing apparatus.

The thrust testing apparatus.

The total weight of the motor and rotor assembly was 846 g, and the measuring apparatus was positioned vertically. Following a thorough verification process, the horizontal rotor plane was selected to mitigate the influence of gravity on the measurement outcomes. Standard weights of 100 g, 500 g and 1000 g were utilized for the calibration of the force gauge. Multiple measurements were taken, showing deviations from the standard weights of less than 1%, ensuring experimental accuracy. Numerical simulation

In the present simulation, the rotor rotational speed ranges from 200 rpm to 1600 rpm, which belongs to the low‑speed operation range of UAV rotors. The corresponding Reynolds number ranges from 1.6 × 104 to 1.27 × 105. The corresponding Mach number is far below 0.3, so air compressibility can be completely neglected. Therefore, the flow field around the rotor can be accurately described as a three‑dimensional, incompressible, viscous, and turbulent flow. Given the moderate Reynolds number regime, fully developed turbulence, and the requirement for efficient and reliable prediction of time‑averaged flow characteristics such as thrust, downwash velocity, and pressure distribution, the Reynolds‑Averaged Navier–Stokes (RANS) method is selected. The RANS approach is widely adopted and well‑validated in numerical studies of plant protection UAV rotors, as it provides a good balance between computational cost, convergence stability, and accuracy for engineering applications. The MRF model is employed to segment the computational domain into rotational and stationary domains, facilitating the simplification of the flow field of the propeller into the instantaneous flow field of the propeller at a specific position, thereby enhancing the computational efficiency36.

The computational domain is comprised of a rotational domain and a stationary domain, as illustrated in Fig. 10. Let D denote the rotational diameter of the rotor. The rotating domain is constituted by the inner cylindrical region, characterized by a diameter of 1.05D and a height of 0.15D, which encompasses the rotor surface. The remaining portion constitutes the stationary domain. The upper surface of the rotating domain is located 6D from the inlet and 6D radially from the wall. The lower surface is located 12D from the outlet.

It is imperative to note that the required mesh resolution increases in proportion to the propeller’s rotational speed. Subsequent to verifying mesh independence at a rotor speed of 1500 rpm, the maximum mesh size on the rotor surface was determined to be 0.005 m, with a local minimum of 0.001 m. The first layer of the rotor boundary layer was set to 0.00025 m with a total thickness of 0.002 m. The mesh size at the interface between the rotational and stationary domains was set to 0.025 m. Once the total number of meshes had reached approximately two million, the rotor thrust simulation results were essentially independent of the mesh. The mesh parameters under consideration were adopted for the numerical simulation of the flow field around the rotor at different speeds.

The rotor surface was set as a smooth wall in the numerical model. Although surface roughness may affect aerodynamic performance at low Reynolds numbers37, the carbon fiber rotor used in this study has a very smooth surface with negligible roughness. Furthermore, the present study focuses on global thrust, downwash velocity field, and droplet deposition characteristics, where the influence of wall roughness is minor compared with rotor geometry, rotational speed, and pitch angle. A smooth wall assumption is widely adopted in UAV rotor simulations to ensure computational stability and efficiency, which is consistent with similar published studies. Assuming a sufficiently extensive computational domain, pressure inlet conditions are applied to the inlet boundary and pressure outlet conditions to the outlet boundary, with the reference pressure set to standard atmospheric pressure. The wall surfaces are defined as fixed, while the rotor surface is designated as a sliding wall with zero rotational velocity relative to the rotating domain. The calculation has been configured as a steady-state analysis, with the SIMPLE algorithm being employed to solve for pressure and velocity. It is imperative to note that all parameters have been set to second-order accuracy, with a convergence criterion of 0.00001.

PRE-PROCESSING

The numerical simulation of plant protection UAV spraying is comprised of three distinct steps: pre-processing, computation, and post-processing. The preliminary processing stage involves the simplification of the plant protection UAV model, with the retention of a mere six rotors whose spatial distribution is aligned with that of the DJI Agras T20. During the hovering phase, rotors 1 and 4 are positioned horizontally, while rotors 2 and 6 are tilted backward by 5° and rotors 3 and 5 are tilted forward by 5°. Given that the focus is on the drift behavior of droplets at specific flight speeds (3 m/s, 4 m/s, 5 m/s, and 6 m/s), it is imperative to ascertain the pitch angle of the plant protection UAV at these respective velocities. During uniform-speed operations, the pitch angle of the plant protection UAV remains stable and unchanged. External sensors measured its pitch angles at speeds of 3 m/s, 4 m/s, 5 m/s, and 6 m/s to be 4.5°, 6°, 7.5°, and 9°, respectively. Figure 12 shows the state of the simulation model under these operating conditions.

Fig. 12The state of the simulation model under different operating conditions.

The state of the simulation model under different operating conditions.

The implementation of simulations that employ the MRF method necessitates the delineation of two distinct computational domains: a rotating domain and a stationary domain, as illustrated in Fig. 13. The rotational domain spans 5% of the rotor diameter, while the stationary domain measures 10 m × 6 m × 20 m. Given that the operational altitude of the plant protection UAV is 2 m (the distance from the radar to the rice canopy), the rotor plane rotates 2.6 m from the crop canopy. In this instance, the rice canopy is approximated as a stationary wall surface in order to facilitate analysis of the rotor wind field and droplet motion. The plant protection UAV is positioned at a distance of 16 m from the outlet in order to ensure that fully developed airflow is achieved.

Fig. 13The computational domain of the DJI Agras T20.

The computational domain of the DJI Agras T20.

To ensure computational accuracy and minimize the required total mesh quantity, three refinement regions are arranged along the flight direction around the periphery of the computational domain. This phenomenon results in a reduction of mesh density from dense to sparse, from the interior to the exterior of the domain. The inlet boundary condition is designated as a pressure inlet, while the outlet is set as a pressure outlet; all walls are also assigned to specific wall types. The interface between rotating and stationary domains adopts a common-node arrangement to minimize interpolation errors and is defined as an interior surface. The surface mesh size for the propeller is set to 0.003 m. The rotation domain grid size was set to 0.006 m. The Boi1 domain grid size was set to 0.06 m. The Boi2 domain grid size was set to 0.12 m. The Boi3 domain grid size was set to 0.24 m. The turbulence model that has been selected for this study is the SST k-ω turbulence model. The initial layer of the rotor boundary layer mesh is set to 7.5 × 10⁻⁵ meters, with a growth rate of 1.1, thus resulting in a total of 20 layers. As illustrated in Fig. 14, polyhedral meshing is employed for the delineation of boundary surfaces, whilst hexahedral meshing is utilized for the internal core region. While ensuring computational accuracy, the total mesh number is minimized to approximately 4,000,000.

Several key assumptions were adopted in the numerical framework to balance computational efficiency and physical rationality, and their validity and influence on real-field applicability are justified as follows. First, the rice canopy was treated as a rigid stationary wall. This simplification was widely used in UAV spraying simulations21,22. Using a rigid wall avoids strong fluid–structure interaction oscillations that impede numerical convergence, while allowing accurate prediction of the downwash flow field and droplet impact behavior. In real fields, this approximation slightly underestimates local flow buffering but does not change the overall distribution of canopy disturbance and droplet deposition. Second, steady-state flow conditions were assumed for forward flight at constant speed. This treatment is standard for simulating UAV operations with fixed pitch angle and rotor speed. The steady-state assumption greatly reduces computational cost while reliably capturing time-averaged airflow patterns, droplet trajectories, and lag distances.

The operation process of the DJI Agras T20 with constant flight speed can be regarded as a steady-state process. The solution method adopts a coupled approach, utilizing a second-order upwind discretization scheme with the convergence residual set to 0.00001. Once a steady state has been achieved through calculation by the numerical simulation, the wind field velocity vectors are displayed at differing cross-sections. As illustrated in Fig. 15, the velocity vectors within the longitudinal cross-section are depicted, with the nozzle positioned at the center. As demonstrated in the accompanying figure, the downwash from rotors 1, 2, and 6 exhibits a vertical downward trajectory, while the downwash from rotors 3, 4, and 5 demonstrates a downward and rearward movement tendency. It is important to note that, since only the rear nozzles are activated when the agricultural drone moves forward, the wind fields generated by rotors 3, 4, and 5 will dominate the transport of spray droplets.

Discussion

The present findings reveal a strong coupling between rotor downwash airflow, rice canopy disturbance, and pesticide droplet deposition, which directly determines the efficacy of UAV-based plant protection in rice fields. Flight speed was confirmed as the dominant operational parameter governing the spatial alignment between the canopy disturbance zone and the droplet deposition zone. At 3–4 m/s, the lag distances of the two zones differed by only 0.02–0.06 m, indicating near-perfect overlap. This alignment allowed droplets to penetrate the opened rice canopy and deposit onto leaf undersides and middle-lower stems, which are typically inaccessible under natural conditions. Such a matching effect maximized droplet utilization and reduced pesticide loss.

Limitations of this study

Due to the seasonal growth characteristics and timeliness of rice cultivation, additional field experiments and validations could not be supplemented within the current crop cycle, leading to several limitations that are explicitly acknowledged as follows.

First, field validation of the CFD simulation results remains insufficient. Although the rotor model was verified through thrust tests, direct comparisons between simulated droplet deposition distributions and field measurements using water‑sensitive paper, dye tracers, or direct sampling were not conducted. Deposition in the upper and lower rice canopy layers was not separately analyzed either. Therefore, the current simulation results are largely theoretical and should be interpreted with caution.

Second, the rice canopy was simplified as a static rigid wall in the CFD model. Since the core focus of this study involves canopy movement and dynamic disturbance, this assumption does not fully represent the actual physical interaction between the downwash flow and the flexible rice canopy. Although this treatment ensures numerical convergence and stability, it may slightly underestimate flow buffering and real‑time canopy deformation. All simulation results are interpreted within the constraints of this simplification.

Third, the statistical analysis is constrained by limited data. The regression models were established based on only four flight speed levels, and only mean values were originally reported. Standard deviation, standard error, and confidence intervals were supplemented in the revision, but significance tests and advanced methods such as bootstrap resampling were not applied owing to the small dataset. The regression relationships are therefore used only to reflect trends within the tested conditions and should not be over‑interpreted.

Fourth, several key influencing factors were not fully considered in the experimental design, including droplet size distribution (VMD), nozzle type, canopy density (e.g., LAI), and real‑time meteorological variability (wind, humidity, temperature). These factors are recognized as important variables that may affect deposition and disturbance characteristics and will be systematically incorporated in future research.

Within the scope of the aforementioned limitations, this study still provides a feasible technical approach and quantitative reference for understanding canopy disturbance and droplet deposition coupling during UAV plant protection operations. Future studies will expand the dataset across different days, plots, growth stages, and environmental conditions to enhance statistical robustness and field applicability.

Conclusion

This study combined aerial photography, machine vision, reverse engineering, thrust testing, and CFD numerical simulations to investigate the effects of plant protection UAV rotor airflow on rice canopy disturbance and pesticide droplet deposition. The research developed an effective non-contact method to quantify dynamic canopy deformation, established a high-precision rotor model validated by experimental data, and revealed the quantitative relationship between flight speed, lag distance of canopy disturbance, and lag distance of droplet deposition. Within the experimental conditions and parameter range adopted in this study, the flight speed of 3–4 m/s shows a relatively good matching effect between canopy disturbance range and droplet deposition lag distance. It can be taken as a preferred reference range for plant protection operation parameter setting under similar rice field environments, rather than a universally optimal speed applicable to all scenarios.

Future research should develop a real-time adaptive control system for plant protection UAVs that dynamically adjusts spray timing, nozzle angle, and rotor rotational speed according to flight speed to maintain consistent alignment between canopy disturbance and droplet deposition zones. Field validation should be expanded across multiple rice growth stages, environmental wind conditions, and UAV models to improve the universality of the optimized parameters. Machine vision algorithms should be further enhanced to enable on-board, real-time identification of canopy disturbance during operations, supporting closed-loop intelligent spraying. In addition, new nozzle layouts and rotor-spray cooperative control strategies should be designed to reduce droplet drift and improve deposition uniformity at higher operational speeds.
