The formation of gradient-driven singular structures of codimension one and two in two-dimensions: The case study of ferronematics Part I: Energy estimates and compactness results
Giacomo Canevari, Federico Luigi Dipasquale, Bianca Stroffolini

Abstract
We study a two-dimensional variational model for ferronematics — composite materials formed by dispersing magnetic nanoparticles into a liquid crystal matrix. The model features two coupled order parameters: a Landau-de Gennes 𝐐 \mathbf{Q} -tensor for the liquid crystal component and a magnetisation vector field 𝐌 \mathbf{M} , both of them governed by a Ginzburg-Landau-type energy. The energy, the largest part of which is carried by the 𝐐 \mathbf{Q} -component, includes a singular coupling term favouring alignment between 𝐐 \mathbf{Q} and 𝐌 \mathbf{M} . In this article and in the companion paper [ 18 ] , we analyse the asymptotic behaviour of (not necessarily minimizing) critical points as a small parameter ε \varepsilon tends to zero. In this paper, we prove that the (rescaled) energy density for the 𝐐 \mathbf{Q} -component, concentrates, to leading order, on a finite number of singular points. Moreover, we prove energy estimates and compactness results that will be crucially used in [ 18 ] to determine the structure of the energy concentration set for the 𝐌 \mathbf{M} -component as well as the relationship between the two singular sets. Keywords: Ginzburg-Landau functional, Allen-Cahn equation, vectorial problems, topological singularities, rectifiable sets. 2020 Mathematics Subject Classification: 35Q56 ⋅ \cdot 76A15 ⋅ \cdot 49Q15 ⋅ \cdot 26B30



The formation of gradient-driven singular structures of codimension one and two has been the object of intensive investigation over the past few decades. Singular structures of codimension one, i.e. interfaces, arise in both scalar and vectorial problems, with notable examples in the literature
on diffusion-reaction equations, such as the Allen-Cahn equations for instance.
While the mathematical theory on the scalar Allen-Cahn equation is well-advanced, much less is known in the vectorial case. The available results on Allen-Cahn systems focus on the asymptotic analysis, via
\Gamma
-convergence, of minimising solutions
[
3
,
22
]
— with the exception of a recent paper
[
11
]
, which obtains convergence results for non-minimising solutions of Allen-Cahn systems in dimension two.
On the other hand, singular objects of codimension two arise only in vectorial problems. A prototypical example is the Ginzburg-Landau functional for superconductivity, which has been extensively studied since the seminal monograph
[
6
]
. There is now quite a well-established theory on Ginzburg-Landau solutions, encompassing both minimising and non-minimising ones as well as solutions to evolution problems and, to a lesser degree, generalisations to different functionals with a similar structure.

In this work, we investigate a two-dimensional problem
where singularities of both codimension one and two emerge,
due to the coupling between two different components of the problem.
The physical motivation for this model
is the study of
ferronematics
,
a class of composite materials obtained as suspensions
of magnetic nanoparticles in a nematic liquid crystal host
[
14
,
26
]
.
According to the approach proposed in
[
12
]
, ferronematics are described by two order parameters.
The orientation of the liquid crystal molecules
is described by the Landau-de Gennes
\mathbf{Q}
-tensor,
which is a map from the physical domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}

to the space
\mathscr{S}_{0}^{2\times 2}
of
2\times 2
, symmetric, real matrices
with trace equal to zero. Nonzero values of
\mathbf{Q}
correspond
to liquid crystal configurations with a well-defined direction of
molecular alignment, represented by the
eigenspace of
\mathbf{Q}
associated with the positive eigenvalue,
while
\mathbf{Q}=0
indicates an isotropic state,
where all the directions of molecular alignment are equally likely.
The distribution of magnetic nanoparticles is
described by the average magnetisation vector,

\mathbf{M}\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
. The system is governed by a
free energy functional which depends on both
\mathbf{Q}
and
\mathbf{M}
:

(1)

\mathscr{F}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{M}):=\int_{G}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\left|\nabla\mathbf{Q}\right|^{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left|\nabla\mathbf{M}\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}f_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{M})\right\}{\mathrm{d}}x,

where
\varepsilon
is a non-dimensional parameter.
The interaction between the liquid crystal host and
the magnetic inclusions is mediated by the potential
f_{\varepsilon}
,
which takes the form

(2)

f_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{M}):=\frac{1}{4}\left(1-\left|\mathbf{Q}\right|^{2}\right)^{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\left(1-\left|\mathbf{M}\right|^{2}\right)^{2}-\varepsilon\beta\,\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M}\cdot\mathbf{M}+\kappa_{\varepsilon}.

Here
\beta>0
is given and
\kappa_{\varepsilon}
is an additive
constant, depending on
\varepsilon
and
\beta
only,
uniquely determined by imposing that
\inf f_{\varepsilon}=0
.
Potential energies similar to (
2
) have been derived via suitable homogenisation limits
[
17
]
.
For positive values of
\beta
, the potential
f_{\varepsilon}

promotes alignment between the liquid crystal molecules
and the magnetisation vector.
Indeed, the potential
f_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{M})
is minimised
by pairs
(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{pot}},\,\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{pot}})

that satisfy the conditions

(3)

\left|\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{pot}}\right|=\lambda_{\varepsilon,\beta},\qquad\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{pot}}=\sqrt{2}s_{\varepsilon,\beta}\left(\frac{\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{pot}}\otimes\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{pot}}}{\lambda_{\varepsilon,\beta}^{2}}-\frac{\mathbf{I}}{2}\right)

where
\mathbf{I}
is the
2\times 2
identity matrix
and
\lambda_{\varepsilon,\beta}
,
s_{\varepsilon,\beta}
are
positive constants, uniquely determined by
\varepsilon
and
\beta
,
such that

\lambda_{\varepsilon,\beta}\to\left(\sqrt{2}\beta+1\right)^{1/2},\qquad s_{\varepsilon,\beta}\to 1

as
\varepsilon\to 0
(see
[
20
, Lemma B.2]
).
We are interested in the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
,
which is physically motivated as the “large domain limit”
(i.e., the size of the domain is much larger than
the typical correlation length for the liquid crystal molecules).
The asymptotic analysis of the Ginzburg-Landau functional
shows that the energy for the
\mathbf{Q}
-component concentrates
around a finite number of points,
which correspond to
non-orientable
singularities
of the limiting liquid crystal configuration.
In particular, the
\mathbf{Q}
-component of minimisers
converges to a matrix-valued map that admits no
continuous orthonormal frame of eigenvectors.
However, as the coupling promotes alignment between
\mathbf{M}

and the eigenvectors of
\mathbf{Q}
, the energy for the
\mathbf{M}
-component
should concentrate on singular lines, corresponding to
jumps in the eigenvector frame.

The asymptotic analysis of minimisers for
\mathscr{F}_{\varepsilon}
,
subject to Dirichlet boundary conditions,
has been carried out
in the paper
[
20
]
.
In the present paper, instead, we consider general

critical points

(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon},\,\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon})

of the functional
\mathscr{F}_{\varepsilon}
, that is,
finite-energy solutions of the
Euler-Lagrange system of equations

(4)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\Delta\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}+\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}(\left|\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}-1)\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}-\dfrac{\beta}{\varepsilon}\left(\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\otimes\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}-\dfrac{\left|\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}}{2}\mathbf{I}\right)=0

(5)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\Delta\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}+\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}(\left|\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}-1)\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}-\dfrac{2\beta}{\varepsilon^{2}}\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}=0.

At first sight, both (
4
) and (
5
) look like
perturbed Ginzburg-Landau systems. However,
(
5
) can be regarded as the Euler-Lagrange equation of
a ‘perturbed’ vectorial Allen-Cahn-type energy functional
with wells depending on
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}
.
Indeed, the coupling term favours
\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}
to align with the eigenvector of
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}
corresponding to the positive eigenvalue, so that for each nozero value of
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}
, there are exactly two values of
\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}
that minimise the potential.
We will consider Dirichlet boundary conditions
for
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}
and either Dirichlet or Neumann boundary conditions for
\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}
(see (
7
), (
9
) below for details).

There are physical motivations behind the analysis of general critical points, including both stable and unstable ones.
In setting up variational models for physical systems, there is some
consensus that observable configurations (often also referred to as
stable
configurations
, according to Gibbs) should correspond
to minimisers or local minimisers
of the energy, subject to boundary conditions as appropriate.
However, there are experimentally and numerically observed
cases
[
15
,
16
,
24
]
in which, before
decaying into a state of minimal or locally minimal energy
an unperturbed system remains in a state of
unstable equilibrium
(also called a

metastable phase
) for quite a long time, with respect to the
typical time-scale of the overall dynamics.
It is been argued in
[
24
]
that
such unstable equilibria should correspond
to general critical points, so that their
mathematical analysis is physically justified.
These general remarks are particularly relevant
to the functional (
1
) we are considering,
since numerical results
[
12
,
20
]
show
an abundance of critical points, stable and unstable.

In the study of the system (
4
), (
5
), a major difficulty is
provided by the coupling term.
Roughly speaking, while we expect the integral of
\frac{\beta}{\varepsilon}\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}

in the free energy functional to be relatively small
because of the alignment between
\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}
and
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}
,
the coupling terms in the equations (
4
), (
5
)
have large prefactors, of order
\frac{1}{\varepsilon}

or
\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}
. To deal with these issues,
here and in the companion paper
[
18
]
, we
adapt and combine methods from the literature
on the Ginzburg-Landau functional
[
6
,
7
]

with the approach introduced in the recent
paper
[
11
]
for non-minimising solutions
of the vectorial Allen-Cahn problem.

Assumptions and setting of the problem

We consider two alternative sets of boundary conditions
for
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}
and
\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}
.
One option is to impose
Dirichlet boundary conditions for both
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}
and
\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}
:

(6)

\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}=\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{bd}},\quad\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}=\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{bd}}\qquad\textrm{on }\partial\Omega.

We assume the boundary data
\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{bd}}\in C^{1}(\partial\Omega,\,\mathscr{S}_{0}^{2\times 2})
,

\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{bd}}\in C^{1}(\partial\Omega,\,\mathbb{R}^{2})
are
\varepsilon
-independent
maps that satisfy

(7)

\left|\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{bd}}\right|=\left(\sqrt{2}\beta+1\right)^{1/2},\qquad\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{bd}}=\sqrt{2}\left(\frac{\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{bd}}\otimes\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{bd}}}{\sqrt{2}\beta+1}-\frac{\mathbf{I}}{2}\right)

at any point of
\partial\Omega
.
This particular form of the boundary datum
approximates the set of minimisers (
3
) for the potential and
ensures that the potential energy is small,
i.e.
f_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{bd}},\,\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{bd}})\leq C\varepsilon^{2}

for some
\varepsilon
-independent constant
C

(see Lemma
1.1
below).
Alternatively to (
6
), we consider
‘mixed’ boundary conditions, i.e. Dirichlet boundary
conditions for
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}
and homogeneous Neumann boundary
conditions for
\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}

(8)

\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}=\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{bd}},\quad\partial_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}=0\qquad\textrm{on }\partial\Omega,

where
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
is the exterior unit normal to
\partial\Omega
.
We then assume the boundary datum
\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{bd}}

(does not depend on
\varepsilon
and) takes the form

(9)

\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{bd}}=\sqrt{2}\left(\mathbf{n}_{\mathrm{bd}}\otimes\mathbf{n}_{\mathrm{bd}}-\frac{\mathbf{I}}{2}\right)

on
\partial\Omega
, for some
map
\mathbf{n}_{\mathrm{bd}}\in C^{1}(\partial\Omega,\,\mathbb{R}^{2})

which, a priori, is completely independent of the
values of
\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}
on the boundary.

Regardless of our choice of boundary conditions,
we will always
assume
that there exists a
constant
{\rm C}_{\rm pot}>0
, independent of
\varepsilon
, such that

(10)

\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega}f_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon},\,\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon})\leq{\rm C}_{\rm pot}

for any
\varepsilon
small enough.

Convergence results: Theorem
A

The main result of this paper is the following theorem.

Theorem A
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a bounded, simply connected domain of class
C^{2}
.
Let
\{(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon},\,\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon})\}\subset W^{1,2}(\Omega,\,\mathscr{S}_{0}^{2\times 2})\times W^{1,2}(\Omega,\,\mathbb{R}^{2})
be a sequence of critical
points of
\mathscr{F}_{\varepsilon}
subject to either (
6
)–(
7
)
or to (
8
)–(
9
).
Assume that the condition (
10
)
is satisfied. Then, as
\varepsilon\to 0
,

1.

\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\to\mathbf{Q}_{\star}
strongly in
W^{1,p}(\Omega)
for any
p<2

and
\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\to\mathbf{M}_{\star}
strongly in
L^{p}(\Omega)
for any
p<+\infty
.

2.

The limiting maps
\mathbf{Q}_{\star}
,
\mathbf{M}_{\star}

satisfy

\left|\mathbf{Q}_{\star}(x)\right|=1,\quad\left|\mathbf{M}_{\star}(x)\right|=\left(\sqrt{2}\beta+1\right)^{1/2},\quad\mathbf{Q}_{\star}(x)=\sqrt{2}\left(\frac{\mathbf{M}_{\star}(x)\otimes\mathbf{M}_{\star}(x)}{\sqrt{2}\beta+1}-\frac{\mathbf{I}}{2}\right)

for a.e.
x\in\Omega
and

(14)

\div j(\mathbf{Q}_{\star})=0

in the sense of distributions in
\Omega
.

3.

\mu_{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup\mu_{\star}
and

\nu_{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup\nu_{\star}
weakly*
as measures in
\Omega
.

4.

\spt\mu_{\star}
is finite.

5.

\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\to\mathbf{Q}_{\star}
strongly in

W^{1,p}_{\rm loc}(\Omega\setminus\spt\mu_{\star})
for any
p<+\infty
.

Theorem
A
shows, in particular, that the energy of a sequence
of critical points concentrates on a finite set
\spt\mu_{\star}

of singular points, arising from the Ginzburg-Landau terms
in
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}
.
We also emphasise that, by (
14
)
and item
5
,

\mathbf{Q}_{\star}
is a smooth harmonic map in
\Omega\setminus\spt\mu_{\star}
.

As we are going to discuss in more detail
later on, the proof of Theorem
A
is
fundamentally rooted in the classical Ginzburg-Landau theory,
as developed in
[
6
,
7
]
,
although the presence of the coupling terms in
the Euler-Lagrange equations (
4
), (
5
) calls
for special care and non-trivial refinements of the arguments
in
[
6
,
7
]
.
The situation changes completely when coming to the analysis
of the energy densities
\nu_{\varepsilon}
, the reason being that their
behaviour is basically controlled by the system (
5
),
which, as mentioned above, should be
properly considered as a perturbed Allen-Cahn system.
Correspondingly, the arguments
should be completely different from
those for the measures
\mu_{\varepsilon}
, and follow instead the lines of
the asymptotic analysis of critical points of the vectorial
Allen-Cahn functional, as developed in the recent
breakthrough paper
[
11
]
.
Nonetheless, since the arguments in
[
11
]
are
ultimately based on refined energy estimates and since the
systems (
4
), (
5
) are coupled,
the estimates and compactness properties obtained
in the present paper will be crucial to cope with the consequences of
the presence of the coupling term in (
5
).
However, because of the significantly different framework, we deferred the
study of the asymptotic behaviour of energy densities
\nu_{\varepsilon}

to the companion paper
[
18
]
.

Structure of the proof

The proof of Theorem
A
is substantially more complex than the analogous results for minimising solutions.
Many of the arguments of
[
20
,
19
]
do not carry over to this context, because they are based
on energy minimality.
Instead, our proofs combine different elements.
In order to obtain compactness for the
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}
-component, we adapt arguments from the Ginzburg-Landau literature, mostly from
[
6
]
and
[
7
]
, with some modifications due to the presence of coupling terms in equations (
4
) and (
5
). The same arguments also show that
\mu_{\varepsilon}
concentrates on a finite number of singular points. To prove compactness for the
\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}
-component, we perform the same change of variables that is used in
[
20
]
(see Section
3.1
), which enables us to apply compactness results for the vectorial Modica-Mortola problem
[
22
]
.

The proof of the strong convergence
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\to\mathbf{Q}_{\star}

in
W^{1,2}_{\rm loc}(\Omega\setminus\spt\mu_{\star})
is
more delicate and, once again, the main source of difficulty is the coupling term.
The heart of the matter consists in proving that, for any ball

B\subset\!\subset\Omega\setminus\spt\mu_{\star}
, there holds

(15)

\int_{B}\left\{\left|\nabla\left|\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\right|\right|^{2}+\left(\frac{\left|\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\right|-1}{\varepsilon}-\kappa_{\star}\right)^{2}\right\}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\to 0,\qquad\mbox{as }\varepsilon\to 0,

where
\kappa_{\star}=\frac{\beta}{2\sqrt{2}}\left(\sqrt{2}\beta+1\right)
is
a constant representing the ground level of the ‘modified’ Ginzburg-Landau potential
arising because of the interaction between the
\mathbf{Q}
and
\mathbf{M}
(i.e., the term in
round brackets in the left-hand side of (
15
)).
The analogous statement in the classical Ginzburg-Landau theory,
formally corresponding to
\beta=0
, is well-known, see
[
6
, Theorem X.2]
.
However, the argument of
[
6
]
does not work directly in our
case, exactly because of the coupling term. The proof of (
15
),
detailed in Lemma
3.7
below,
is therefore trickier than its counterpart
[
6
, Equation (127)]
. Once (
15
)
is proved, the rest of the argument in
[
6
]
, based on a suitable
Hodge decomposition of the pre-Jacobian
j(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon})
of
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}
, carries on
with small modifications (see Proposition
3.5

for full details).

The importance of (
15
) is not limited to
the proof of the locally strong convergence away from
\spt\mu_{\star}
.
As detailed in the companion article
[
18
]
, it is also a key
ingredient in the asymptotic analysis of the energy measures
\nu_{\varepsilon}
as well as
in the description of the support of the corresponding limiting measure and of its
relationship with the support of
\mu_{\star}
.

Plan of the paper

The paper is organised as follows. Section
1
contains some notation and preliminary results on the space of
\mathbf{Q}
-tensors, on the potential
f_{\varepsilon}
, and some first consequences of the equations (
4
)–(
5
) (such as the maximum principle, the Pohozaev identity, and a “clearing-out” property
[

]
).
Section

\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}
and
\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}
. For instance, we prove uniform

-estimates on
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}
, for

, as well as uniform

.
In Section

, we will apply these estimates to prove compactness for both

. As a key tool, we use a change of coordinates,

]
, allowing us to write the functional as an almost decoupled one: a Ginzburg-Landau type term,

).
The proof of Theorem

is presented in Section
4
.
Finally, Appendix
A

Notation
.

We use the symbol
\{X_{\varepsilon}\}
to denote a family of
objects indexed by the parameter
\varepsilon>0
, almost always
used as a shorthand to denote
the sequence
\left\{X_{\varepsilon_{k}}\right\}_{k}
, where
\varepsilon_{k}\to 0
as

k\to+\infty
.
Usually, for the sake of a lighter notation, we do not relabel subsequences.

In inequalities like
A\lesssim B
, the symbol
\lesssim
means that there
exists a constant
C
, independent of
A
and
B
, such that
A\leq CB
.
In particular,
dealing with sequences indexed by
\varepsilon
, we use

\lesssim
to denote inequality up to a constant independent of
\varepsilon
.
Whenever it is relevant, we keep track of the dependences
of the implicit constants. Whenever possible without inducing
ambiguities, for the sake of a lighter notation, we avoid writing
explicitly the measure of integration in the integrals.

1
Preliminary estimates

1.1
The space of
\mathbf{Q}
-tensors

In the Introduction, we defined the space of
\mathbf{Q}
-tensors

\mathscr{S}_{0}^{2\times 2}
as

\mathscr{S}_{0}^{2\times 2}:=\{\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{M}_{2\times 2}(\mathbb{R})\,:\,\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{Q}^{\rm t},\,\,\tr(\mathbf{Q})=0\}.

It follows immediately that
\mathscr{S}_{0}^{2\times 2}
is a linear space of dimension
2
whose elements
are
2\times 2
, real matrices of the form

\mathbf{Q}=\begin{pmatrix}Q_{11}&Q_{12}\\
Q_{12}&-Q_{11}\end{pmatrix}.

We endow
\mathscr{S}_{0}^{2\times 2}
with the usual Frobenius norm of matrices, i.e.,

\left|\mathbf{Q}\right|:=\sqrt{\tr(\mathbf{Q}^{\rm t}\mathbf{Q})},\qquad\mbox{for any }\mathbf{Q}\in\mathscr{S}_{0}^{2\times 2}.

The map

(1.1)

\mathscr{S}_{0}^{2\times 2}\ni\mathbf{Q}\mapsto\mathbf{q}:=\sqrt{2}\left(Q_{11},\,Q_{12}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

establishes an isometric isomorphism between
\mathscr{S}_{0}^{2\times 2}
and
\mathbb{R}^{2}

(the latter provided with the standard scalar product).
Of course, we can also identify any
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathscr{S}_{0}^{2\times 2}
with the complex number

Q_{11}+iQ_{12}
, and thus we have the further identification

\mathscr{S}_{0}^{2\times 2}\simeq\mathbb{C}
.

We denote as
\mathscr{N}
the unit sphere in
\mathscr{S}_{0}^{2\times 2}
, i.e.,

\mathscr{N}:=\left\{\mathbf{Q}\in\mathscr{S}_{0}^{2\times 2}\,:\,\left|\mathbf{Q}\right|=1\right\},

which can be identified with the unit circle
\mathbb{S}^{1}
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}

(or, equivalently, in
\mathbb{C}
). Moreover, in view of (
1.1
),
we may also identify, equivalently,

\mathscr{N}=\left\{\sqrt{2}\left(\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}-\frac{\mathbf{I}}{2}\right)\,:\,\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}\right\}.

Representing
\mathbf{Q}
-tensors in “polar coordinates”.

Let
G\subseteq\Omega
be a simply connected,
smooth subdomain, and let
\mathbf{Q}\colon G\to\mathscr{S}_{0}^{2\times 2}

be a smooth map such that
\left|\mathbf{Q}\right|>0
on
G
.
The spectral theorem implies that
\mathbf{Q}

can be written in the form
\sqrt{2}\mathbf{Q}=\left|\mathbf{Q}\right|(\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}-\mathbf{m}\otimes\mathbf{m})
,
where
(\mathbf{n},\,\mathbf{m})
is an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors of
\mathbf{Q}
.
Both
\mathbf{n}
and
\mathbf{m}
are uniquely determined by
\mathbf{Q}
,
up to their sign. (In other words, either
\mathbf{m}=\mathbf{n}^{\perp}
or

\mathbf{m}=-\mathbf{n}^{\perp}
.)
Similarly,
\mathbf{n}
can be written as
\mathbf{n}=(\cos\varphi,\,\sin\varphi)

for some scalar function
\varphi\colon G\to\mathbb{R}
.
As a consequence, setting
\rho:=\left|\mathbf{Q}\right|
, we can write

(1.2)

\mathbf{Q}=\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\begin{matrix}\cos(2\varphi)&\phantom{-}\sin(2\varphi)\\
\sin(2\varphi)&-\cos(2\varphi)\end{matrix}\right)

The function
\varphi
is uniquely determined by
\mathbf{Q}
,
up to a constant multiple of
\pi
. An explicit computation
shows that

(1.3)

\left|\nabla\mathbf{Q}\right|^{2}=\left|\nabla\rho\right|^{2}+4\rho^{2}\left|\nabla\varphi\right|^{2}

Moreover, if we only assume that
\mathbf{Q}
is bounded
away from zero on
\partial G
, but not necessarily
in the interior of
G
, we can still write
\mathbf{Q}

as in (
1.2
), except that the function
\varphi

can be discontinuous. However, we can always choose
\varphi

to be continuous on
\partial G
, except for one
jump discontinuity at most (see, e.g.,
[
13
, Proof of Lemma 1.3 and the remarks thereafter]
). If
x_{0}\in\partial G

is the unique jump point of
G

and
\varphi(x_{0}^{+})
,
\varphi(x_{0}^{-})
are the
traces of
\varphi
on either side of
x_{0}

(oriented in the anticlockwise direction), then we define

(1.4)

\deg(\mathbf{Q},\,\partial G):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\left(\varphi(x_{0}^{+})-\varphi(x_{0}^{-})\right)

The number
\deg(\mathbf{Q},\,\partial G)
is the topological
degree of
\mathbf{Q}
on
\partial G
, and is a half-integer.
If
\mathbf{Q}
admits a continuous lifting on
\partial G
,
then
\deg(\mathbf{Q},\,\partial G)=0
(and vice-versa).
Similar considerations apply when
\mathbf{Q}\in W^{1,2}(\Omega,\,\mathscr{S}_{0}^{2\times 2})
,
so long as
\rho
is bounded away from zero
(by lifting results for
\mathbf{Q}
-tensors — see, e.g.,
[
4
,
8
]
— and
degree theory in
W^{1/2,2}
— see, e.g.,
[
13
, Proposition 12.1]
).

Pre-Jacobians.

We recall some notation from
[
20
]
.
We define the vector product of
two matrices
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathscr{S}_{0}^{2\times 2}
,
\mathbf{P}\in\mathscr{S}_{0}^{2\times 2}
as

(1.5)

\mathbf{Q}\times\mathbf{P}:=Q_{11}P_{12}-Q_{12}P_{11}+Q_{21}P_{22}-Q_{22}P_{21}=2\left(Q_{11}P_{12}-Q_{12}P_{11}\right)

For any
\mathbf{Q}\in(L^{\infty}\cap W^{1,1})\left(\Omega,\,\mathscr{S}_{0}^{2\times 2}\right)
,
we define the vector field
j(\mathbf{Q})\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
as

(1.6)

j(\mathbf{Q}):=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{Q}\times\partial_{1}\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{Q}\times\partial_{2}\mathbf{Q}\right).

For notational convenience, we shall often write (
1.6
)
in the form

(1.7)

j(\mathbf{Q})=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{Q}\times\nabla\mathbf{Q}.

By analogy with the Ginzburg-Landau literature,
j(\mathbf{Q})

could be called the “pre-Jacobian” of
\mathbf{Q}
.
This terminology is justified because, for smooth maps
\mathbf{Q}
,
we have

(1.8)

\frac{1}{2}\curl j(\mathbf{Q})=\partial_{1}Q_{11}\,\partial_{2}Q_{12}-\partial_{2}Q_{11}\,\partial_{1}Q_{12}

(and the same remains true if
\mathbf{Q}\in W^{1,2}(\Omega,\,\mathscr{S}_{0}^{2\times 2})
,
by a density argument).
In particular, for any
\mathbf{Q}
-tensor field

\mathbf{Q}\in\left(L^{\infty}\cap W^{1,1}\right)\left(\Omega,\,\mathscr{S}_{0}^{2\times 2}\right)

it makes sense to define

(1.9)

J(\mathbf{Q}):=\curl j(\mathbf{Q}),

if we agree that the derivatives are taken in the sense of distributions.
The distribution
J(\mathbf{Q})
is called the
distributional Jacobian

of
\mathbf{Q}
.

If
\mathbf{Q}
can be represented in the form (
1.2
), then

(1.10)

j(\mathbf{Q})=\rho^{2}\,\nabla\varphi.

As a consequence, for any smooth
\mathbf{Q}\colon\Omega\to\mathscr{S}_{0}^{2\times 2}
, there holds

(1.11)

\left|\mathbf{Q}\right|^{2}\left|\nabla\mathbf{Q}\right|^{2}=\left|\mathbf{Q}\right|^{2}\left|\nabla(\left|\mathbf{Q}\right|)\right|^{2}+4\left|j(\mathbf{Q})\right|^{2}

The equality (
1.11
) holds at any point
of
\{\mathbf{Q}\neq 0\}
because of (
1.3
), (
1.10
),
and is satisfied trivially at almost any point of
\{\mathbf{Q}=0\}
.
Another consequence of (
1.10
) is the equality

(1.12)

\curl\left(\frac{j(\mathbf{Q})}{\left|\mathbf{Q}\right|^{2}}\right)=0,

which holds at any point
x\in\Omega
such that
\mathbf{Q}(x)\neq 0
.
Moreover, if
G\subseteq\Omega
is a simply connected,
smooth subdomain such that
\left|\mathbf{Q}\right|>0
on
\partial G

and
{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
is the unit tangent vector to
\partial G

oriented anti-clockwise direction, then

(1.13)

\int_{\partial G}\frac{j(\mathbf{Q})\cdot{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}{\left|\mathbf{Q}\right|^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}s=\pi\deg(\mathbf{Q},\,\partial G)

because of (
1.4
) and (
1.10
).

Remark 1.4
.

The distributional pre-Jacobian and Jacobian are continuous with respect to the
weak
W^{1,p}
-convergence, for any
p>1
. In other words,
given a sequence of uniformly bounded
\mathbf{Q}
-tensor fields

\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\in W^{1,p}\left(\Omega,\,\mathscr{S}_{0}^{2\times 2}\right)

which converges weakly in
W^{1,p}\left(\Omega,\,\mathscr{S}_{0}^{2\times 2}\right)
to
some
\mathbf{Q}
-tensor field
\mathbf{Q}_{\star}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, then

j(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon})\to j(\mathbf{Q}_{\star})
and

J(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon})\to J(\mathbf{Q}_{\star})
as
\varepsilon\to 0
in the sense of
distributions in
\Omega
. (Cf., e.g.,
[
1
, Remark 3.6(iii)]
.)

Notation
.

Besides the pre-Jacobian of
\mathbf{Q}
-tensor fields,
occasionally we shall also consider the pre-Jacobian of a vector
field
\mathbf{u}\in\left(L^{\infty}\cap W^{1,1}\right)(\Omega,\,\mathbb{R}^{2})
.
Denoted
j(\mathbf{u})
, such a pre-Jacobian can be identified with the
vector field with components

(1.14)

\left(j(\mathbf{u})\right)_{k}=\left(u_{1}\partial_{k}u_{2}-u_{2}\partial_{k}u_{1}\right)_{k},\qquad\mbox{for }k\in\{1,\,2\},

i.e.,

j(\mathbf{u})=\mathbf{u}\times\nabla\mathbf{u},

exactly as in the standard Ginzburg-Landau theory.

1.2
Properties of the potential
f_{\varepsilon}
.

We summarise below some properties of the potential
f_{\varepsilon}
.
As in
[
20
, Equation (3.1)]
,
we define

(1.15)

\kappa_{\star}:=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}\beta\left(\sqrt{2}\beta+1\right).

Lemma 1.1

(
[
20
, Lemma B.3]
)
.

The potential
f_{\varepsilon}
satisfies the following properties.

(i)

The constant
\kappa_{\varepsilon}
in (
2
),
uniquely defined by imposing
the condition
\inf f_{\varepsilon}=0
, satisfies

\kappa_{\varepsilon}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\beta^{2}+\sqrt{2}\beta\right)\varepsilon+\kappa_{\star}^{2}\varepsilon^{2}+\mathrm{o}(\varepsilon^{2})

as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

(ii)

If
(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{M})\in\mathscr{S}_{0}^{2\times 2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}
is such that

\left|\mathbf{M}\right|=\left(\sqrt{2}\beta+1\right)^{1/2},\qquad\mathbf{Q}=\sqrt{2}\left(\frac{\mathbf{M}\otimes\mathbf{M}}{\sqrt{2}\beta+1}-\frac{\mathbf{I}}{2}\right)

then
f_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{M})=\kappa_{\star}^{2}\,\varepsilon^{2}+\mathrm{o}(\varepsilon^{2})

as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

(iii)

If
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small, then
for any
(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{M})\in\mathscr{S}_{0}^{2\times 2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}
we have

(1.16)

\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}f_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{M})\geq\frac{1}{8\varepsilon^{2}}\left(\left|\mathbf{Q}\right|^{2}-1\right)^{2}-\beta^{2}\left|\mathbf{M}\right|^{4}\!.

For any
(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{M})\in\mathscr{S}_{0}^{2\times 2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we can write

(1.17)

\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}f_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{M})=\frac{1}{4\varepsilon^{2}}\left(\left|\mathbf{Q}\right|^{2}-1\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\ell(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{M})+\chi_{\varepsilon},

where

(1.18)

\ell(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{M}):=\frac{1}{4}\left(\left|\mathbf{M}\right|^{2}-1\right)^{2}-\beta\,\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M}\cdot\mathbf{M}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\beta^{2}+\sqrt{2}\beta\right)

and

(1.19)

\chi_{\varepsilon}:=\frac{\kappa_{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon^{2}}-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\left(\beta^{2}+\sqrt{2}\beta\right)\!.

Note that

(1.20)

\chi_{\varepsilon}\to\kappa_{\star}^{2}\qquad\mbox{as }\varepsilon\to 0.

In the next lemma, we consider minimisers of the
function
\ell(\mathbf{Q},\,\cdot\,)
, for a given
\mathbf{Q}
.
We recall that any
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathscr{S}_{0}^{2\times 2}\setminus\{0\}

has exactly one positive and one negative eigenvalues,
because of the constraint
\tr\mathbf{Q}=0
.

Lemma 1.2
.

For any
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathscr{S}_{0}^{2\times 2}\setminus\{0\}
, the function
\ell(\mathbf{Q},\,\cdot\,)

has exactly two minimisers, given by

(1.21)

\mathbf{M}_{\pm}:=\pm\left(\sqrt{2}\beta\rho+1\right)^{1/2}\mathbf{n}

where
\rho:=\left|\mathbf{Q}\right|
and
\mathbf{n}
is a unit eigenvector of
\mathbf{Q}
corresponding to its positive eigenvalue.
Moreover, there holds

(1.22)

\begin{split}\min\ell(\mathbf{Q},\,\cdot\,)=\ell(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{M}_{\pm})=\frac{\beta}{2}(1-\rho)\left(\sqrt{2}+\beta+\beta\rho\right)\!.\end{split}

Proof.

The gradient and the Hessian of
\ell
with respect to
the
\mathbf{M}
-variable are given by

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbf{M}}\ell(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{M})

\displaystyle=\left(\left|\mathbf{M}\right|^{2}-1\right)\mathbf{M}-2\beta\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M}

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}^{2}_{\mathbf{M}}\ell(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{M})

\displaystyle=\left(\left|\mathbf{M}\right|^{2}-1\right)\mathbf{I}+2\mathbf{M}\otimes\mathbf{M}-2\beta\mathbf{Q}.

We immediately see that any critical point of
\ell(\mathbf{Q},\,\cdot\,)

must be an eigenvector of
\mathbf{Q}
.
Since
\tr\mathbf{Q}=0
and
\tr(\mathbf{Q}^{2})=\rho^{2}
,
the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{Q}
must be equal to
\pm\rho/\sqrt{2}
.
Let
(\mathbf{n},\,\mathbf{m})
be an orthonormal basis
of eigenvectors of
\mathbf{Q}
, such that
\mathbf{n}
is associated with
the positive eigenvalue of
\mathbf{Q}
and
\mathbf{m}
is associated with
the negative one. Then, substituting
\mathbf{M}=\lambda\,\mathbf{n}
,

\mathbf{M}=\mu\,\mathbf{m}
in the condition
\nabla_{\mathbf{M}}\ell=0
, we find
that critical points (with respect to the
\mathbf{M}
-variable only)
occur for

\mathbf{M}=0,\qquad\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{M}_{\pm}:=\pm\left(1+\sqrt{2}\beta\rho\right)^{1/2}\mathbf{n},\qquad\mathbf{M}=\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{\pm}:=\pm\left(1-\sqrt{2}\beta\rho\right)^{1/2}\mathbf{m}

— the latter pair,
\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{\pm}
,
being defined only when
\sqrt{2}\beta\rho\leq 1
.
However,
\mathrm{D}^{2}_{\mathbf{M}}\ell(\mathbf{Q},\,0)=-\mathbf{I}-2\beta\mathbf{Q}
cannot
be positive semi-definite, because its trace is equal to
-2
.
Moreover, we have

\ell(\mathbf{Q},\,\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{+})=\ell(\mathbf{Q},\,\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{-})
,

\ell(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{M}_{+})=\ell(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{M}_{-})
and

\ell(\mathbf{Q},\,\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{\pm})-\ell(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{M}_{\pm})=\sqrt{2}\beta\rho>0.

Therefore, the minimisers of
\ell(\mathbf{Q},\,\cdot\,)
are exactly those given
in (
1.21
), and (
1.22
) follows
by an explicit computation.
∎

Corollary 1.3
.

For any
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathscr{S}_{0}^{2\times 2}\setminus\{0\}
and any
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
there holds

(1.23)

\ell(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{M})-\ell(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{M}_{\pm})=\frac{1}{4}\left|\mathbf{M}-\mathbf{M}_{+}\right|^{2}\left|\mathbf{M}-\mathbf{M}_{-}\right|^{2}+\sqrt{2}\beta\rho(\mathbf{M}\cdot\mathbf{m})^{2},

where
(\mathbf{n},\,\mathbf{m})
is an eigenframe for
\mathbf{Q}
and
\mathbf{n}
is a unit
eigenvector of
\mathbf{Q}
corresponding to its positive eigenvalue.

Proof.

Since
\rho=\left|\mathbf{Q}\right|>0
, we can write

\mathbf{Q}=\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{2}}(\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}-\mathbf{m}\otimes\mathbf{m})
, where

\mathbf{n}
is an eigenvector of
\mathbf{Q}
corresponding to its positive eigenvalue
and
\mathbf{m}
is orthogonal to
\mathbf{n}
. Furthermore, we can decompose
\mathbf{M}

along
(\mathbf{n},\,\mathbf{m})
, i.e, we can write

\mathbf{M}=(\mathbf{M}\cdot\mathbf{n})\mathbf{n}+(\mathbf{M}\cdot\mathbf{m})\mathbf{m},

and it follows that

\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M}\cdot\mathbf{M}=\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{2}}\left((\mathbf{M}\cdot\mathbf{n})^{2}-(\mathbf{M}\cdot\mathbf{m})^{2}\right)

On the other hand, we may obviously rephrase

\left(\left|\mathbf{M}\right|^{2}-1\right)^{2}=\left(\left|\mathbf{M}\right|^{2}-\left(1+\sqrt{2}\beta\rho\right)\right)^{2}+2\sqrt{2}\beta\rho\left(\left|\mathbf{M}\right|^{2}-1-\sqrt{2}\beta\rho\right)+2\beta^{2}\rho^{2}.

Combining the above formulas with the definition (
1.18
) of
\ell

and with (
1.22
), we obtain

\begin{split}\ell(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{M})&=\frac{1}{4}\left(\left|\mathbf{M}\right|^{2}-\left(1+\sqrt{2}\beta\rho\right)\right)^{2}+\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\beta\rho\left(2(\mathbf{M}\cdot\mathbf{m})^{2}-\left(1+\sqrt{2}\beta\rho\right)\right)\\
&\quad+\frac{1}{2}\beta^{2}\rho^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\beta^{2}+\sqrt{2}\beta\right)\\
&=\ell(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{M}_{\pm})+\frac{1}{4}\left(\left|\mathbf{M}\right|^{2}-\left(1+\sqrt{2}\beta\rho\right)\right)^{2}+\sqrt{2}\beta\rho(\mathbf{M}\cdot\mathbf{m})^{2}.\end{split}

After rearrangement, the conclusion follows by noticing that,
in view of (
1.21
),

\left(\left|\mathbf{M}\right|^{2}-\left(1+\sqrt{2}\beta\rho\right)\right)^{2}=\left|\mathbf{M}-\mathbf{M}_{+}\right|^{2}\left|\mathbf{M}-\mathbf{M}_{-}\right|^{2}.

∎

For
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathscr{S}_{0}^{2\times 2}\setminus\{0\}
, let
\Sigma(\mathbf{Q}):=\{\mathbf{M}_{+},\,\mathbf{M}_{-}\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
be the set of minimisers
of
\ell(\mathbf{Q},\,\cdot\,)
, as given by (
1.21
).
If
\mathbf{Q}\neq 0
and
\dist(\mathbf{M},\,\Sigma(\mathbf{Q}))\leq 1
, we define

(1.24)

\pi(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{M}):=\begin{cases}\mathbf{M}_{+}&\textrm{if }\left|\mathbf{M}-\mathbf{M}_{+}\right|\leq 1\\
\mathbf{M}_{-}&\textrm{if }\left|\mathbf{M}-\mathbf{M}_{-}\right|\leq 1.\end{cases}

\pi(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{M})
is the closest projection of
\mathbf{M}

to
\Sigma(\mathbf{Q})
. It is defined in a non-ambiguous way,
because
\left|\mathbf{M}_{+}-\mathbf{M}_{-}\right|=2(\sqrt{2}\beta\rho+1)^{1/2}>2
.

Remark 1.5
.

Let
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathscr{S}_{0}^{2\times 2}\setminus\{0\}
be arbitrary.
As an obvious consequence of (
1.23
),
and since
\left|\mathbf{M}_{+}-\mathbf{M}_{-}\right|>2
, letting
\mathbf{N}=\pi(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{M})
,
for any
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
\dist(\mathbf{M},\,\Sigma(\mathbf{Q}))\leq 1
,
there holds

(1.25)

\ell(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{M})-\ell(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{N})\geq\frac{1}{4}\left|\mathbf{M}-\mathbf{N}\right|^{2}.

Lemma 1.4
.

For any
\Lambda>1
, there exist positive
constants
\delta_{0}(\Lambda)
and
c_{0}(\Lambda)

(depending on
\Lambda
and
\beta
only)
such that the following statement holds:
for any
(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{M})\in\mathscr{S}_{0}^{2\times 2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}
such
that
\Lambda^{-1}\leq\left|\mathbf{Q}\right|\leq\Lambda

and
\dist(\mathbf{M},\,\Sigma(\mathbf{Q}))\leq\delta_{0}(\Lambda)
, there holds

\nabla_{\mathbf{M}}\ell(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{M})\cdot(\mathbf{M}-\pi(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{M}))\geq c_{0}(\Lambda)\Big(\ell(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{M})-\ell(\mathbf{Q},\,\pi(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{M}))\Big)\geq 0.

Proof.

Let
X(\Lambda)\subseteq\mathscr{S}_{0}^{2\times 2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}
be the set of pairs
(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{M})

such that
\Lambda^{-1}\leq\left|\mathbf{Q}\right|\leq\Lambda

and
\dist(\mathbf{M},\,\Sigma(\mathbf{Q}))\leq 1
.
Let
(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{M})\in X(\Lambda)
and
\mathbf{N}:=\pi(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{M})
.
Since
\left|\mathbf{Q}\right|>0
, we may write
\sqrt{2}\mathbf{Q}=\rho(\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}-\mathbf{m}\otimes\mathbf{m})
,
where
\rho:=\left|\mathbf{Q}\right|
and
(\mathbf{n},\,\mathbf{m})

is an orthonormal set of eigenvectors, with
\mathbf{n}
corresponding to the positive
eigenvalue of
\mathbf{Q}
.
In view of Corollary
1.3
, of the immediate inequality

\left|\mathbf{M}-\mathbf{N}\right|^{2}\geq(\mathbf{M}\cdot\mathbf{m})^{2}
, and of the
assumption
\dist(\mathbf{M},\,\Sigma(\mathbf{Q}))\leq 1
, we obtain

(1.26)

0\leq\ell(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{M})-\ell(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{N})\leq L(\Lambda)\left|\mathbf{M}-\mathbf{N}\right|^{2},

where the first inequality holds because
\mathbf{N}
is a minimiser of
\ell(\mathbf{Q},\,\cdot\,)
,
and
L(\Lambda)
can be taken of the form

(1.27)

L(\Lambda)=\sqrt{2}\beta\Lambda+C,

form some
C
that does not depend on
\Lambda
, nor on
\beta
.

On the other hand, by Taylor-expanding
\nabla_{\mathbf{M}}\ell

around the point
(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{N})
, we obtain

\begin{split}\nabla_{\mathbf{M}}\ell(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{M})\cdot(\mathbf{M}-\mathbf{N})&\geq\mathrm{D}^{2}_{\mathbf{M}}\ell(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{N})(\mathbf{M}-\mathbf{N})\cdot(\mathbf{M}-\mathbf{N})-L^{\prime}\left|\mathbf{M}-\mathbf{N}\right|^{3},\end{split}

where
L^{\prime}
is an upper bound for the third
derivatives of
\ell(\mathbf{Q},\,\cdot\,)
on
X(\Lambda)
.
(The third derivatives of
\ell(\mathbf{Q},\,\cdot)

do not depend on
\mathbf{Q}
, so we can choose
L^{\prime}

to be independent of
\Lambda
.)
We evaluate explicitly the second
derivatives of
\ell
.
Recalling (
1.21
), we obtain

(1.28)

\begin{split}\mathrm{D}^{2}_{\mathbf{M}}\ell(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{N})=\sqrt{2}\beta\rho\,\mathbf{I}+(\sqrt{2}\beta\rho+2)\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}+\sqrt{2}\beta\rho\,\mathbf{m}\otimes\mathbf{m}=2\sqrt{2}\beta\rho\,\mathbf{I}+2\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}\end{split}

and hence,

(1.29)

\begin{split}\mathrm{D}^{2}_{\mathbf{M}}\ell(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{N})(\mathbf{M}-\mathbf{N})\cdot(\mathbf{M}-\mathbf{N})\geq 2\sqrt{2}\,\beta\rho\left|\mathbf{M}-\mathbf{N}\right|^{2}\geq\frac{2\sqrt{2}\,\beta}{\Lambda}\left|\mathbf{M}-\mathbf{N}\right|^{2}\end{split}

for any
(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{M})\in X(\Lambda)
.
Combining (
1.26
), (
1.27
), and (
1.29
),
and choosing, for instance,
\delta_{0}(\Lambda):=\frac{\sqrt{2}\beta}{\Lambda L^{\prime}}
,
the lemma follows.
∎

Lemma 1.5
.

For any
\delta>0
there exist positive
constants
\varepsilon_{1}(\delta)>0
and
c_{1}(\delta)>0

(depending on
\beta
and
\delta
, but not on
\varepsilon
)
such that the following statement holds:
for any
\varepsilon\in(0,\,\varepsilon_{1}(\delta)]
and
any
(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{M})\in\mathscr{S}_{0}^{2\times 2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
\left|\mathbf{Q}\right|\leq 3/4

or
\dist(\mathbf{M},\,\Sigma(\mathbf{Q}))\geq\delta
, there holds

(1.30)

f_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{M})\geq c_{1}(\delta)\,\varepsilon.

Proof.

Let
\delta>0
be fixed.
Should the lemma be false, there would exist sequences

(\varepsilon_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
,
(\mathbf{Q}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
,
(\mathbf{M}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}

such that
\varepsilon_{k}\to 0
as
k\to+\infty
,

(1.31)

\left|\mathbf{Q}_{k}\right|\leq\frac{3}{4}\quad\textrm{or}\quad\dist(\mathbf{M}_{k},\,\Sigma(\mathbf{Q}_{k}))\geq\delta\qquad\textrm{for any }k\in\mathbb{N},

and

(1.32)

\frac{f_{\varepsilon_{k}}(\mathbf{Q}_{k},\,\mathbf{M}_{k})}{\varepsilon_{k}}=\frac{1}{4\varepsilon_{k}}\left(\left|\mathbf{Q}_{k}\right|^{2}-1\right)^{2}+\ell(\mathbf{Q}_{k},\,\mathbf{M}_{k})+\varepsilon_{k}\,\chi_{\varepsilon_{k}}\to 0\qquad\textrm{as }k\to+\infty.

We can give an estimate for
\left|\mathbf{Q}_{k}\right|

by combining (
1.32
) with Equation (
1.22
),
which provides a lower bound for
\ell_{\varepsilon_{k}}(\mathbf{Q}_{k},\,\mathbf{M}_{k})
,
and Lemma
1.1
, which shows that
the sequence
(\chi_{\varepsilon_{k}})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}

is bounded. We obtain

(1.33)

\begin{split}\frac{1}{4\varepsilon_{k}}\left(\left|\mathbf{Q}_{k}\right|^{2}-1\right)^{2}&\leq-\min\ell(\mathbf{Q}_{k},\,\cdot)+\mathrm{o}_{k\to+\infty}(1)\\
&\leq\frac{\beta}{2}\left(\left|\mathbf{Q}_{k}\right|-1\right)\left(\sqrt{2}+\beta+\beta\left|\mathbf{Q}_{k}\right|\right)+\mathrm{o}_{k\to+\infty}(1).\end{split}

This inequality implies that the sequence
(\mathbf{Q}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}

is bounded. Then, from (
1.32
) we deduce

\begin{split}\limsup_{k\to+\infty}\left(\frac{1}{4}\left(\left|\mathbf{M}_{k}\right|^{2}-1\right)^{2}-\beta\,C\left|\mathbf{M}_{k}\right|^{2}\right)\leq\limsup_{k\to+\infty}\ell(\mathbf{Q}_{k},\,\mathbf{M}_{k})\leq 0,\end{split}

where
C
is an upper bound for
\left|\mathbf{Q}_{k}\right|
.
Therefore,
(\mathbf{M}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, too, is bounded.
Up to extraction of a subsequence, we can assume that
\mathbf{Q}_{k}\to\mathbf{Q}
,

\mathbf{M}_{k}\to\mathbf{M}
. By passing to the limit in (
1.33
)
and (
1.32
), we see that
\left|\mathbf{Q}\right|=1
and

\ell(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{M})\leq 0
.
On the other hand, (
1.22
)
implies that
\ell(\mathbf{P},\,\mathbf{N})\geq 0
for any
(\mathbf{P},\,\mathbf{N})

such that
\left|\mathbf{P}\right|=1
, with strict inequality unless
\mathbf{N}\in\Sigma(\mathbf{P})
.
Therefore, we must have
\left|\mathbf{Q}\right|=1
and
\mathbf{M}\in\Sigma(\mathbf{Q})
,
but this contradicts (
1.31
). The lemma follows.
∎

The maximum principle.

From now on, we denote by
(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon},\,\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon})

a solution to (
4
)–(
5
),
subject to either Dirichlet or mixed boundary conditions,
as in (
6
)–(
7
) or (
8
)–(
9
)
respectively. Either way, we assume
that (
10
) is satisfied.
We will denote the energy density as

(1.34)

e_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{M}):=\frac{1}{2}\left|\nabla\mathbf{Q}\right|^{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left|\nabla\mathbf{M}\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}f_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{M})

for
(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{M})\in W^{1,2}(\Omega,\,\mathscr{S}_{0}^{2\times 2})\times W^{1,2}(\Omega,\,\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

Lemma 1.6
.

The maps
\mathbf{Q}^{*}_{\varepsilon}
,
\mathbf{M}^{*}_{\varepsilon}
are smooth inside
\Omega

and of class
C^{1}
up to the boundary of
\Omega
.
Moreover, there exist a constant
C_{\beta}
, depending only on
\beta
,
and a constant
C_{\beta,\Omega}
, depending only on
\beta
and
\Omega
,
such that

(1.35)

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{Q}^{*}_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}+\left\|\mathbf{M}^{*}_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq{C_{\beta}}

(1.36)

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla\mathbf{Q}^{*}_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}+\left\|\nabla\mathbf{M}^{*}_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{{C_{\beta,\Omega}}}{\varepsilon}.

Proof of Lemma
1.6
.

Elliptic regularity theory (both for the Dirichlet and the Neumann
problems) implies that
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}
and
\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}
are smooth in
\Omega

and of class
C^{1}
up to
\partial\Omega
.
We focus on the proof of (
1.35
); once (
1.35
) is proved,
the gradient bounds (
1.36
) will follow
from elliptic estimates, by reasoning along the lines
of
[
5
, Lemma A.2]
.
The bounds (
1.35
) are a consequence of the maximum principle.
For the problem with Dirichlet boundary conditions,
the details can be found in
[
20
, Lemma 4.1]
.
Here, we focus on the problem with mixed boundary conditions.

As an intermediate step towards (
1.35
), we show that

(1.37)

\max_{\overline{\Omega}}\left|\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}\leq m_{\varepsilon}:=1+{\sqrt{2}}\beta\max_{\overline{\Omega}}\left|\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\right|

Indeed, by taking the scalar product of (
5
)
against
\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}
, we obtain

-\frac{1}{2}\Delta\left(\left|\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}\right)+\left|\nabla\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}+\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}(\left|\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}-1)\left|\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}-\dfrac{2\beta}{\varepsilon^{2}}\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}=0.

and hence (observing that
\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M}\cdot\mathbf{M}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left|\mathbf{Q}\right|\left|\mathbf{M}\right|^{2}
for any
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathscr{S}_{0}^{2\times 2}
and
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
)

(1.38)

-\frac{1}{2}\Delta\left(\left|\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}\right)\leq-\dfrac{\left|\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\left(\left|\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}-1-{\sqrt{2}}\beta\left|\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\right|\right)

at every point of
\Omega
.
Now, let
x_{0}\in\overline{\Omega}

be a maximum point for
\left|\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}

and suppose, towards a contradiction,
that
\left|\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})\right|>m_{\varepsilon}
.
Then, the right-hand side of (
1.38
) would attain
a strictly negative value at the point
x_{0}

and, by continuity of
(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon},\,\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon})
,
it would be strictly negative in a (smooth)
neighbourhood
V\subseteq\overline{\Omega}
of
x_{0}
.
If
x_{0}
lies in the interior of
\Omega
,
we obtain a contradiction
because
-\Delta(\left|\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2})(x_{0})\geq 0
.
On the other hand, if
x_{0}\in\partial\Omega
,
then Hopf’s lemma (applied on

) implies
that
\partial_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}(\left|\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2})(x_{0})>0
,
which contradicts the boundary conditions (
6
).
Therefore, (
1.37
) is proved.

Now, (
1.35
) follows by (
1.37
)
by the maximum principle. Indeed, by taking the scalar
product of (
4
) against
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}
, we deduce

(1.39)

-\frac{1}{2}\Delta\left(\left|\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}\right)\leq-\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\left(\left|\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{4}-\left|\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}-{\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{2}}}\,\varepsilon\left|\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\right|\left|\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}\right)

inside
\Omega
. Let
x_{1}\in\overline{\Omega}

be a maximum point for
\left|\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}
.
If
x_{1}\in\partial\Omega
, then
\left|\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}(x_{1})\right|^{2}=1

because of the assumption (
7
) on the boundary datum.
If
x_{1}\in\Omega
, then (
1.39
) and (
1.37
)
imply that

(1.40)

\left|\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}(x_{1})\right|^{3}\leq\left|\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}(x_{1})\right|+{\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{2}}}\,\varepsilon\left|\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}(x_{1})\right|^{2}\leq\left(1+{\beta^{2}\,\varepsilon}\right)\left|\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}(x_{1})\right|+{\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{2}}}\,\varepsilon

and hence,
\left|\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\right|
is uniformly bounded, in terms of
\beta
only.
∎

Remark 1.6
.

The arguments above apply, with minor modifications, to the problem
with Dirichlet boundary conditions.
In both cases, from (

) and (

\left|\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\right|\leq s_{*}(\beta,\,\varepsilon),\quad\left|\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}\leq 1+{{\sqrt{2}}}\beta\,s_{*}(\beta,\,\varepsilon)\qquad\textrm{in }\Omega,

P(X)=X^{3}-(1+{\beta^{2}\varepsilon})X-{\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{2}}}\varepsilon

Elementary calculus shows that

as

, for any given value of

the constraint

with respect to
\varepsilon

we deduce

(1.42)

s_{*}(\beta,\,\varepsilon)=1+{\varepsilon\kappa_{\star}+{\rm O}(\varepsilon^{2})}

.

The Pohozaev identity.

We define the
stress-energy tensor
associated
to
(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon},\,\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon})
as

(1.44)

T_{jk}^{\varepsilon}:=\partial_{j}\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\partial_{k}\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon\,\partial_{j}\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\partial_{k}\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}-e_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon},\,\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon})\,\delta_{jk}

for
(j,\,k)\in\{1,\,2\}^{2}
.
Here
e_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon},\,\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon})
is the energy density,
defined by (
1.34
).

Lemma 1.7
.

The stress-energy tensor satisfies
\partial_{j}T_{jk}^{\varepsilon}=0
in
\Omega
.

Proof.

For ease of notation, we will write
\mathbf{Q}
,
\mathbf{M}

instead of
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}
,
\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}
.
Let
G
be a smooth subdomain of
\Omega
and
let
\mathbf{X}\in C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{c}}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\,\mathbb{R}^{2})

be a test vector field, which may or may
not be supported in
\overline{\Omega}
.
We test the equation (
4
)
against
X_{k}\,\partial_{k}\mathbf{Q}
, test (
5
)
against
\varepsilon\,X_{k}\,\partial_{k}\mathbf{M}
, then integrate over
G
. We obtain

-\int_{G}X_{k}\left(\partial_{j}\partial_{j}\mathbf{Q}\cdot\partial_{k}\mathbf{Q}+\varepsilon\,\partial_{j}\partial_{j}\mathbf{M}\cdot\partial_{k}\mathbf{M}\right)+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{G}X_{k}\left(\nabla_{\mathbf{Q}}f_{\varepsilon}\cdot\partial_{k}\mathbf{Q}+\nabla_{\mathbf{M}}f_{\varepsilon}\cdot\partial_{k}\mathbf{M}\right)=0,

where
\nabla_{\mathbf{Q}}f_{\varepsilon}
,
\nabla_{\mathbf{M}}f_{\varepsilon}

denote the (partial) gradients of
f_{\varepsilon}

with respect to the variables
\mathbf{Q}
and
\mathbf{M}
,
evaluated at
(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon},\,\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon})
. By the chain rule,
the previous equality can also be written as

(1.45)

-\int_{G}X_{k}\left(\partial_{j}\partial_{j}\mathbf{Q}\cdot\partial_{k}\mathbf{Q}+\varepsilon\,\partial_{j}\partial_{j}\mathbf{M}\cdot\partial_{k}\mathbf{M}\right)+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{G}X_{k}\,\partial_{k}\left(f_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{M})\right)=0,

Now, we integrate by parts repeatedly. The potential term can be written as

(1.46)

\int_{G}X_{k}\,\partial_{k}\left(f_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{M})\right)=-\int_{G}(\div\mathbf{X})\,f_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{M})+\int_{\partial G}(\mathbf{X}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\nu}})\,f_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{M})\,\mathrm{d}s,

where
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
is the outward unit normal to
G
.
We rewrite the first term by integrating by parts twice:

(1.47)

\begin{split}-\int_{G}X_{k}\,\partial_{j}\partial_{j}\mathbf{Q}\cdot\partial_{k}\mathbf{Q}&=\int_{G}\partial_{j}X_{k}\,\partial_{j}\mathbf{Q}\cdot\partial_{k}\mathbf{Q}+\int_{G}X_{k}\,\partial_{j}\mathbf{Q}\cdot\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\mathbf{Q}-\int_{\partial G}({\boldsymbol{\nu}}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{Q})\cdot(\mathbf{X}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{Q})\,\mathrm{d}s\\
&=\int_{G}\partial_{j}X_{k}\,\partial_{j}\mathbf{Q}\cdot\partial_{k}\mathbf{Q}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{G}(\div\mathbf{X})\left|\nabla\mathbf{Q}\right|^{2}\\
&\hskip 56.9055pt-\int_{\partial G}({\boldsymbol{\nu}}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{Q})\cdot(\mathbf{X}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{Q})\,\mathrm{d}s+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial G}({\boldsymbol{\nu}}\cdot\mathbf{X})\left|\nabla\mathbf{Q}\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\end{split}

A similar computation applies to the term
containing the derivatives of
\mathbf{M}
. By combining (
1.45
), (
1.46
) and (
1.47
), we obtain

(1.48)

\begin{split}\int_{G}T_{jk}^{\varepsilon}\,\partial_{j}X_{k}&=\int_{\partial G}\left(({\boldsymbol{\nu}}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{Q})\cdot(\mathbf{X}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{Q})+\varepsilon\,({\boldsymbol{\nu}}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{M})\cdot(\mathbf{X}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{M})\right)\mathrm{d}s\\
&\hskip 42.67912pt-\int_{\partial G}(\mathbf{X}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\nu}})\,e_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{M})\,\mathrm{d}s\end{split}

By choosing
G=\Omega
and taking
\mathbf{X}
compactly
supported in
\Omega
,
the boundary terms vanish, and the lemma follows.
∎

Remark 1.8
.

Completely analogous computations to those above return
the following fact, which will be used
later on. Suppose that
\mathbf{Q}_{\star}\colon G\to\mathbb{S}^{1}

is a smooth harmonic map, i.e., a
smooth solution to the equation

\Delta\mathbf{Q}_{\star}+\left|\nabla\mathbf{Q}_{\star}\right|^{2}\mathbf{Q}_{\star}=0\qquad\mbox{in }G,

and let
\mathbf{X}
be any smooth vector field with compact support in

. Then,

(1.49)

\int_{G}\left\{\partial_{j}\mathbf{Q}_{\star}\cdot\partial_{k}\mathbf{Q}_{\star}\partial_{j}X_{k}-\frac{1}{2}(\div\mathbf{X})\left|\nabla\mathbf{Q}_{\star}\right|^{2}\right\}\,{\mathrm{d}}x=0.

Lemma 1.8
.

For each ball
B=B(x_{0},\,R)\subseteq\Omega
,
there holds

(1.50)

\begin{split}\frac{2}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{B}f_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon},\,\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon})\,{\mathrm{d}}x&+\frac{R}{2}\int_{\partial B}\left(\left|\partial_{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}+\varepsilon\left|\partial_{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}s\\
&=\frac{R}{2}\int_{\partial B}\left(\left|\partial_{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}+\varepsilon\left|\partial_{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}+\frac{2}{\varepsilon^{2}}f_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon},\,\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon})\right)\mathrm{d}s\end{split}

where
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
is the outward unit normal and
{\boldsymbol{\tau}}

is the unit tangent field on
\partial B
, oriented in such a way
that
({\boldsymbol{\nu}},\,{\boldsymbol{\tau}})
is a positive basis.

Proof.

The lemma follows by taking
G=B(x_{0},\,R)

and
\mathbf{X}(x):=\varphi(x)(x-x_{0})
in (
1.48
), where
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
is a smooth cut-off function such that
\varphi\equiv 1
in a neighbourhood of
\Omega
.
∎

The clearing-out lemma.

This version of the “clearing-out lemma” is inspired
by
[
7
, Theorem 2]
, but its proof is much simpler,
because we are working in a two-dimensional domain. Roughly stated,
it implies that, if the energy of
(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon},\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon})
on a ball
of radius
R
is sufficiently small with respect to
\log(R/\varepsilon)
,
then
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}
is nonzero at the centre of the ball.

Lemma 1.9

(Clearing-out)
.

Let
B=B(x_{0},\,R)
be a ball contained in
\Omega

and let
\varepsilon
be such that
0<\varepsilon<R
. Then,

\left|\left|\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})\right|-1\right|\lesssim\left(\frac{\mathscr{F}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon},\,\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon};\,B)}{\log(R/\varepsilon)}\right)^{1/4}+\varepsilon^{1/2}

The implicit constant on the right-hand side
depends only on
\beta
and
\Omega
.

Proof.

First of all, we claim that there exists
\rho\in(\varepsilon,\,R)

such that

(1.51)

\int_{\partial B(x_{0},\,\rho)}e_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon},\,\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon})\,\mathrm{d}s\leq\frac{\mathscr{F}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon},\,\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon};B)}{\rho\,\log(R/\varepsilon)}

For otherwise, we would have

\begin{split}\mathscr{F}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon},\,\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon};\,B)\geq\int_{\varepsilon}^{R}\left(\int_{\partial B(x_{0},\,\rho)}e_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon},\,\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon})\,\mathrm{d}s\right)\,\mathrm{d}\rho&>\int_{\varepsilon}^{R}\frac{\mathscr{F}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon},\,\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon};B)}{\rho\,\log(R/\varepsilon)}\mathrm{d}\rho\\
&=\mathscr{F}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon},\,\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon};\,B),\end{split}

which is a contradiction. For such a value of
\rho
,
Lemma
1.8
gives

(1.52)

\begin{split}\frac{2}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{B(x_{0},\,\varepsilon)}f_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon},\,\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon})\leq\frac{2}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{B(x_{0},\,\rho)}f_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon},\,\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon})&\leq\rho\int_{\partial B(x_{0},\,\rho)}e_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon},\,\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon})\,\mathrm{d}s\\
&\leq\frac{\mathscr{F}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon},\,\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon};\,B)}{\log(R/\varepsilon)}\end{split}

(see
[
20
, Lemma B.1]
).
Therefore, taking (
1.16
)
and Lemma
1.6
into account,
from (
1.52
) we deduce

(1.53)

\begin{split}\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{B(x_{0},\,\varepsilon)}\left(\left|\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}-1\right)^{2}&\lesssim\int_{B(x_{0},\,\varepsilon)}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}f_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon},\,\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon})+\beta^{2}\left|\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{4}\right)\\
&\lesssim\frac{\mathscr{F}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon},\,\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon};\,B)}{\log(R/\varepsilon)}+\beta^{2}\varepsilon^{2}\end{split}

Finally, the gradient estimate (
1.36
)
implies that

(1.54)

\left|\left|\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})\right|-1\right|\lesssim\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{B(x_{0},\,\varepsilon)}\left(\left|\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}-1\right)^{2}\right)^{1/4}

— see
[
7
, Lemma III.3]
for the details.
(The statement in
[
7
]
only provides
a one-sided bound for
1-\left|\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\right|
,
but the very same argument can be used to
prove the opposite inequality.)
The lemma follows by combining (
1.53
)
with (
1.54
).
∎

2

\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}

In this section, we derive several

and

Our arguments follow a classical path and

The goal of this section is to prove the following result.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon},\,\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon})
be a sequence of solutions

)–(
5
), subject to
either (
6
)–(
7
) or (
8
)–(
9
).

) is satisfied.

C
, depending only on
\Omega
,
\beta
,

, and the boundary data,

\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}\leq C\left|\log\varepsilon\right|\!.

,

\Omega
,

,

,

Remark 2.1
.

More precisely, the constants on the right-hand side of (

)
and of (
2.2
) depend on the boundary data only through
the
L^{1}(\partial\Omega)

As in
[

7
]
, the main step in the proof of
Proposition
2.1
is to obtain bounds on
j(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon})
.
To this end, we write the equation satisfied by
j(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon})

by taking the vector product of
Equations (
4
)–(
5
)
against
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}
and
\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}
respectively:

(2.3)

-\div\left(j(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon})\right)=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\,\partial_{k}\left(\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\times\partial_{k}\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\right)\qquad\textrm{in }\Omega

(see
[
20
, Lemma 4.8]
for details).
Lemma
1.6
implies that the right-hand side of
Equation (
2.3
) is bounded in
W^{-1,\infty}(\Omega)
:
indeed,

(2.4)

\varepsilon\left\|\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\times\partial_{k}\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq\varepsilon\left\|\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\left\|\partial_{k}\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq C

for
k\in\{1,\,2\}
and some constant
C
that
does not depend on
\varepsilon
but only on
\beta
. This allows us to
obtain
\varepsilon
-independent bounds on
j(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon})
.

Lemma 2.2
.

For any
p\in[1,\,2)
, there exists an

\varepsilon
-independent constant
C_{p}>0
such that

\left\|j(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon})\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq C_{p}

for any
\varepsilon
small enough.
The constant
C_{p}
depends only on
p
,
\beta
,
{\rm C}_{\rm pot}
,
and the
L^{1}(\partial\Omega)
-norm of
\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{bd}}\times\partial_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{bd}}
.

Proof.

As in
[
6
,
7
]
, the strategy is to consider
(a suitable variant of) the Helmoltz, or Hodge,
decomposition of
j(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon})
. As a preliminary step,
we will decompose
j(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon})
in a suitable way.
We use the notation
\rho_{\varepsilon}:=\left|\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\right|
,

\mathbf{j}_{\varepsilon}:=j(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon})
.

Step 1

(Splitting
\curl\mathbf{j}_{\varepsilon}
)
.

Let
A\colon[0,\,+\infty)\to[0,\,2]

be a smooth function such that

(2.5)

A(0)=1,\qquad A(t)=\frac{1}{t}\qquad\textrm{if }t\geq\frac{1}{2},\qquad\left|A^{\prime}(t)\right|\leq 4\qquad\textrm{for any }t\geq 0,

and let
\alpha_{\varepsilon}:=A(\rho_{\varepsilon})
. We write

\curl\mathbf{j}_{\varepsilon}=\curl(\alpha^{2}_{\varepsilon}\,\mathbf{j}_{\varepsilon})+\curl((1-\alpha_{\varepsilon}^{2})\,\mathbf{j}_{\varepsilon}).

The first term,
\curl(\alpha^{2}_{\varepsilon}\,\mathbf{j}_{\varepsilon})
,
is supported in
\{\rho_{\varepsilon}\leq 1/2\}
, because
of (
1.12
) and (
2.5
).
Lemma
1.6
implies the pointwise inequality

(2.6)

\left|\curl(\alpha^{2}_{\varepsilon}\,\mathbf{j}_{\varepsilon})\right|\lesssim\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{\{\rho_{\varepsilon}\leq 1/2\}}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\lesssim\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\left(\rho_{\varepsilon}^{2}-1\right)^{2},

where
\mathbbm{1}_{\{\rho_{\varepsilon}\leq 1/2\}}
is the indicator function
of the set
\{\rho_{\varepsilon}\leq 1/2\}
. Then, recalling (
1.43
),
we obtain the
\varepsilon
-independent bound

(2.7)

\left\|\curl(\alpha^{2}_{\varepsilon}\,\mathbf{j}_{\varepsilon})\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\leq C.

In fact, the constant
C
depends only on
\beta
and
{\rm C}_{\rm pot}
.

As for the second term,
\curl((1-\alpha_{\varepsilon}^{2})\,\mathbf{j}_{\varepsilon})
,
Lemma
1.6
and (
2.5
) imply the pointwise bound

\left|(1-\alpha^{2}_{\varepsilon})\,\mathbf{j}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}\lesssim\frac{(\rho^{2}_{\varepsilon}-1)^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}\,\rho_{\varepsilon}^{4}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\rho_{\varepsilon}\geq 1/2\}}+\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{\{\rho_{\varepsilon}\leq 1/2\}}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\lesssim\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}(\rho_{\varepsilon}^{2}-1)^{2}.

Therefore, (
1.43
) gives

(2.8)

\left\|(1-\alpha^{2}_{\varepsilon})\mathbf{j}_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C,

where
C
does not depend on
\varepsilon
but only on
\beta
and
{\rm C}_{\rm pot}
.

Step 2

(Hodge decomposition of
\mathbf{j}_{\varepsilon}
)
.

We consider the following elliptic problems:

(2.9)

\begin{cases}\Delta\phi_{\varepsilon}\hskip 2.27621pt=\curl(\alpha_{\varepsilon}^{2}\,\mathbf{j}_{\varepsilon})&\textrm{in }\Omega\\
\partial_{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}\phi_{\varepsilon}=\dfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{bd}}\times\partial_{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{bd}}&\textrm{on }\partial\Omega\end{cases}

and

(2.10)

\begin{cases}\Delta\xi_{\varepsilon}\hskip 2.27621pt=\curl((1-\alpha_{\varepsilon}^{2})\,\mathbf{j}_{\varepsilon})&\textrm{in }\Omega\\
\partial_{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}\xi_{\varepsilon}=0&\textrm{on }\partial\Omega\end{cases}

The right-hand sides of (
2.9
) and (
2.10
)
satisfy the solvability conditions for Neumann problems.
Indeed, if
{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
denotes the unit tangent vector
to
\partial\Omega
in the anti-clockwise direction, we have

(2.11)

\alpha_{\varepsilon}^{2}\,\mathbf{j}_{\varepsilon}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\tau}}=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{bd}}\times\partial_{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{bd}},\quad(1-\alpha^{2}_{\varepsilon})\,\mathbf{j}_{\varepsilon}=0\qquad\textrm{on }\partial\Omega

because our assumptions on the boundary conditions
(either (
7
) or (
9
))
imply
\left|\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\right|=\left|\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{bd}}\right|=1
on
\partial\Omega
.
As a consequence, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\curl\left(\alpha_{\varepsilon}^{2}\,\mathbf{j}_{\varepsilon}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial\Omega}\alpha_{\varepsilon}^{2}\,\mathbf{j}_{\varepsilon}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\tau}}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial\Omega}\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{bd}}\times\partial_{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{bd}}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\curl\left((1-\alpha_{\varepsilon}^{2})\,\mathbf{j}_{\varepsilon}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial\Omega}(1-\alpha_{\varepsilon}^{2})\,\mathbf{j}_{\varepsilon}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\tau}}=0,

and the solutions
\phi_{\varepsilon}
,
\xi_{\varepsilon}

exist and are unique up to additive constants.
Moreover, writing
\nabla^{\perp}:=(-\partial_{2},\,\partial_{1})
,
we have

\curl\left(\mathbf{j}_{\varepsilon}-\nabla^{\perp}\phi_{\varepsilon}-\nabla^{\perp}\xi_{\varepsilon}\right)=\curl\mathbf{j}_{\varepsilon}-\Delta\phi_{\varepsilon}-\Delta\xi_{\varepsilon}=0\qquad\textrm{in }\Omega.

As
\Omega
is assumed to be simply connected,
there exists a function
H_{\varepsilon}\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
such that

(2.12)

\mathbf{j}_{\varepsilon}=\nabla H_{\varepsilon}+\nabla^{\perp}\phi_{\varepsilon}+\nabla^{\perp}\xi_{\varepsilon}\qquad\textrm{in }\Omega.

By taking the divergence of both sides of (
2.12
),
and keeping (
2.3
) into account,
we obtain

(2.13)

-\Delta H_{\varepsilon}=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\,\partial_{k}\left(\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\times\partial_{k}\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\right)\qquad\textrm{in }\Omega.

In a similar way, by taking the
tangential component

of the trace at the boundary in both sides of (
2.12
),
and using (
2.9
), (
2.10
), (
2.11
),
we deduce

\partial_{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}H_{\varepsilon}=0\qquad\textrm{on }\partial\Omega.

The boundary of
\Omega
is connected, because
\Omega

is simply connected. Therefore, up to an additive constant,
we can assume that

(2.14)

H_{\varepsilon}=0\qquad\textrm{on }\partial\Omega.

Step 3

(Elliptic estimates on
\phi_{\varepsilon}
,
\xi_{\varepsilon}
,
H_{\varepsilon}
)
.

We estimate
\phi_{\varepsilon}
by applying
estimates for the Poisson problem with integrable right-hand side
(see e.g.
[
7
, Proposition A.2]
).
Keeping (
2.7
) into account, we obtain

(2.15)

\begin{split}\left\|\nabla{\phi_{\varepsilon}}\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq C_{p}\left(\left\|\curl(\alpha_{\varepsilon}^{2}\,\mathbf{j}_{\varepsilon})\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}+\left\|\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{bd}}\times\partial_{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{bd}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\partial\Omega)}\right)\leq C_{p},\end{split}

for any
p\in[1,\,2)
and some constant
C_{p}

that does not depend on
\varepsilon
but only on
p
,
\beta
,

{\rm C}_{\rm pot}
, and the boundary data. As for
\xi_{\varepsilon}
,
we test Equation (
2.10
) against
\xi_{\varepsilon}

and integrate by parts, recalling
that
(1-\alpha_{\varepsilon}^{2})\,\mathbf{j}_{\varepsilon}=0
on
\partial\Omega
:

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla\xi_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}=-\int_{\Omega}(1-\alpha_{\varepsilon}^{2})\,\mathbf{j}_{\varepsilon}\times\nabla\xi_{\varepsilon}.\end{split}

Then, Young’s inequality and (
2.8
) give

(2.16)

\begin{split}\left\|\nabla\xi_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\left\|(1-\alpha_{\varepsilon}^{2})\,\mathbf{j}_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C,\end{split}

for the same constant
C
as in (
2.8
).
In a similar way, testing (
2.13
)–(
2.14
)
against
H_{\varepsilon}
, integrating by parts,
and applying (
2.4
), we obtain

(2.17)

\begin{split}\left\|\nabla H_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\varepsilon\sum_{k=1}^{2}\left\|\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\times\partial_{k}\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C.\end{split}

Combining (
2.15
), (
2.16
)
and (
2.17
), the lemma follows.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

Assume (
10
) holds.
For any
\varepsilon
small enough, we have

\left\|j(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon})\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\lesssim\left|\log\varepsilon\right|^{1/2},

where the implicit constant on the right-hand side
depends only on
\left|\Omega\right|
,
\beta
,
{\rm C}_{\rm pot}
, and
the
L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
-norm of
\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{bd}}\times\partial_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{bd}}
.

Proof.

We consider again the decomposition of
j(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon})

given in (
2.12
). In view of (
2.16
) and (
2.17
),
the lemma will follow if we prove that the solution
\phi_{\varepsilon}

to (
2.9
) satisfies

(2.18)

\left\|\nabla\phi_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\lesssim\left|\log\varepsilon\right|^{1/2}.

We follow the same strategy as in
[
6
, Lemma X.5]
.
To simplify the notation, we
set
h_{\varepsilon}:=\curl(\alpha_{\varepsilon}^{2}\,j(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}))

and
2g:=\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{bd}}\times\partial_{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{bd}}
.
By testing (
2.9
) against
\phi_{\varepsilon}
,
we obtain

(2.19)

\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla\phi_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}=\int_{\partial\Omega}g\,\phi_{\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{d}s-\int_{\Omega}h_{\varepsilon}\,\phi_{\varepsilon}

We estimate the boundary term first. Since the
solution
\phi_{\varepsilon}
is only identified up to an
additive constant, we can assume without loss of generality
that
\phi_{\varepsilon}
has zero average in
\Omega
. Then,
the trace inequality and the Poincaré inequality give

(2.20)

\left|\int_{\partial\Omega}g\,\phi_{\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{d}s\right|\leq\left\|g\right\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}\left\|\phi_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}\lesssim\left\|\phi_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\lesssim\left\|\nabla\phi_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

(the boundary datum
g
depends only on
\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{bd}}
,
not on
\varepsilon
, and is continuous).
Now, we estimate the term that depends on
h_{\varepsilon}
.
Let
Z_{\varepsilon}:=\{\left|\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\right|\leq 1/2\}
.
We recall (see (
2.6
)) that
\left|h_{\varepsilon}\right|\lesssim\varepsilon^{-2}

and
h_{\varepsilon}
is supported in
Z_{\varepsilon}
. Moreover,
the estimate (
1.43
)
(which holds thanks to the assumption (
10
))
for the Ginzburg-Landau potential
implies

(2.21)

\left|Z_{\varepsilon}\right|\lesssim\int_{\Omega}\left(\left|\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}-1\right)^{2}\lesssim\varepsilon^{2}.

As a consequence, we obtain

(2.22)

\begin{split}\left|\int_{\Omega}h_{\varepsilon}\,\phi_{\varepsilon}\right|\lesssim\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{Z_{\varepsilon}}\left|\phi_{\varepsilon}\right|\lesssim\left\|\phi_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(Z_{\varepsilon})}\end{split}

We can further estimate the
L^{\infty}(Z_{\varepsilon})
-norm
of
\phi_{\varepsilon}
by applying Trudinger’s inequality
(see e.g
[
23
, Theorem 7.15]
),
which gives

\int_{\Omega}\exp\left(\frac{\left|\phi_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}}{\sigma_{1}^{2}\left\|\nabla\phi_{\varepsilon}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}\right)\leq\sigma_{2}\left|\Omega\right|

for some universal constants
\sigma_{1}
,
\sigma_{2}
.
It follows that

\exp\left(\frac{\left\|\phi_{\varepsilon}\right\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(Z_{\varepsilon})}}{\sigma_{1}^{2}\left\|\nabla\phi_{\varepsilon}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}\right)\left|{Z_{\varepsilon}}\right|\leq\sigma_{2}\left|\Omega\right|

and, hence,

(2.23)

\left\|\phi_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{\infty}({Z_{\varepsilon}})}\leq\sigma_{1}\left\|\nabla\phi_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\left(\log\left(\frac{\sigma_{2}\left|\Omega\right|}{\left|{Z_{\varepsilon}}\right|}\right)\right)^{1/2}\lesssim\left\|\nabla\phi_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\left(\left|\log\varepsilon\right|^{1/2}+1\right)

for any
i\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,N_{\varepsilon}\}
.
Combining (
2.20
) with (
2.22
)
and (
2.23
), we obtain

\left\|\nabla\phi_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\lesssim\left(\left|\log\varepsilon\right|^{1/2}+1\right)\left\|\nabla\phi_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

and (
2.19
) follows.
∎

Next, we need to estimate
\rho_{\varepsilon}:=\left|\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\right|
.
To this end, we write an equation for
\rho_{\varepsilon}

by taking the scalar product of both sides of (
4
)
against
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}
. We obtain

(2.24)

-\frac{1}{2}\Delta(\rho_{\varepsilon}^{2})+\left|\nabla\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\left(\rho_{\varepsilon}^{2}-1\right)\rho_{\varepsilon}^{2}-\frac{\beta}{\varepsilon}\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}=0\qquad\textrm{in }\Omega.

From this equation, we deduce the following bound:

Lemma 2.4
.

For any
\delta\in(0,\,1/2)
, any
p\in[1,\,2]

and any
\varepsilon>0
small enough, there holds

\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla\rho_{\varepsilon}\right|^{p}\lesssim\left(\delta\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}\right)^{p/2}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2-p}}{\delta^{2}}+1,

where the implicit constant in front of the
right-hand side is independent of
\delta
,
p
,
\varepsilon

and depends only on
\Omega
,
\beta
,
{\rm C}_{\rm pot}
,
and the
L^{1}(\partial\Omega)
- and the
L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
-norm of

\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{bd}}\times\partial_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{bd}}
.

Proof.

For a given value of
\delta\in(0,\,1/2)
, let

E_{\varepsilon}:=\left\{x\in\Omega\colon\left|\rho_{\varepsilon}(x)-1\right|\leq\delta\right\}\!.

The bound (
1.43
) implies

(2.25)

\left|\Omega\setminus E_{\varepsilon}\right|\lesssim\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}\int_{\Omega}{\left(\rho_{\varepsilon}-1\right)^{2}}\lesssim\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\delta^{2}},

where the implicit constant on the right-hand side depends
only on
\beta
and
{\rm C}_{\rm pot}
,
and hence, recalling (
1.36
),

(2.26)

\int_{\Omega\setminus E_{\varepsilon}}\left|\nabla\rho_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}\lesssim\frac{1}{\delta^{2}},

where, again, the implicit constant on the right-hand side depends
only on
\beta
and
{\rm C}_{\rm pot}
.

It remains to estimate
\nabla\rho_{\varepsilon}

on
E_{\varepsilon}
. To this purpose, we define

\overline{\rho}_{\varepsilon}:=\begin{cases}1+\delta&\textrm{if }\rho_{\varepsilon}\geq 1+\delta\\
\rho_{\varepsilon}&\textrm{if }1-\delta<\rho_{\varepsilon}<1+\delta\\
1-\delta&\textrm{if }\rho_{\varepsilon}\leq 1-\delta.\end{cases}

We have
\overline{\rho}_{\varepsilon}=1
on
\partial\Omega
,
because
\left|\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\right|=\left|\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{bd}}\right|=1
on
\partial\Omega

by (
7
), (
9
).
To estimate
\nabla\rho_{\varepsilon}
on
E_{\varepsilon}
,
we test Equation (
2.24
)
against
1-\overline{\rho}_{\varepsilon}
and integrate by parts.
We obtain

(2.27)

\begin{split}\int_{E_{\varepsilon}}\rho_{\varepsilon}\left|\nabla\rho_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}&=\int_{\Omega}\left(\left|\nabla\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}(\rho_{\varepsilon}^{2}-1)\rho_{\varepsilon}^{2}-\frac{\beta}{\varepsilon}\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\right)(1-\overline{\rho}_{\varepsilon})\\
&\leq\delta\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}+\mathrm{I}_{\varepsilon}+\mathrm{II}_{\varepsilon},\end{split}

where

\mathrm{I}_{\varepsilon}:=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\left|\rho_{\varepsilon}^{2}-1\right|\left|1-\overline{\rho}_{\varepsilon}\right|,\qquad\mathrm{II}_{\varepsilon}:=\frac{\beta}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}\left|1-\overline{\rho}_{\varepsilon}\right|\,\left|\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\right|

To estimate
\mathrm{I}_{\varepsilon}
, we observe that
\left|1-\overline{\rho}_{\varepsilon}\right|\leq\left|1-\rho_{\varepsilon}\right|
and hence,

(2.28)

\begin{split}\left|\mathrm{I}_{\varepsilon}\right|\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\frac{(\rho_{\varepsilon}^{2}-1)^{2}}{1+\rho_{\varepsilon}}\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega}(\rho_{\varepsilon}^{2}-1)^{2}\leq C,\end{split}

because of (
1.43
). To estimate
\mathrm{II}_{\varepsilon}
,
we apply the
L^{\infty}
-bound (
1.35
),
the Hölder inequality, and (
1.43
) again:

(2.29)

\begin{split}\left|\mathrm{II}_{\varepsilon}\right|\lesssim\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}\left|1-\rho_{\varepsilon}\right|\lesssim\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega}(\rho_{\varepsilon}^{2}-1)^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leq C,\end{split}

where the constant
C
depends only on
\Omega
,
\beta
, and
{\rm C}_{\rm pot}
.
From (
2.27
), (
2.28
) and (
2.29
),
we deduce

(2.30)

\int_{E_{\varepsilon}}\left|\nabla\rho_{\varepsilon}\right|^{p}\lesssim\left(\int_{E_{\varepsilon}}\rho_{\varepsilon}\left|\nabla\rho_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}\right)^{p/2}\lesssim\left(\delta\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}\right)^{p/2}+C

(we have used that
\rho_{\varepsilon}\geq 1-\delta\geq 1/2
on
E_{\varepsilon}
).
Taking (
2.26
) into account, the lemma follows.
∎

We can now complete the proof of Proposition
2.1
.

2.1
.

\rho_{\varepsilon}:=\left|\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\right|

\int_{F_{\varepsilon}}\left|\nabla\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}=\int_{F_{\varepsilon}}\left(\left|\nabla\rho_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}+\frac{\left|j(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon})\right|^{2}}{\rho_{\varepsilon}^{2}}\right)\lesssim\int_{F_{\varepsilon}}\left(\left|\nabla\rho_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}+\left|j(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon})\right|^{2}\right)

(2.32)

\int_{F_{\varepsilon}}\left|\nabla\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}\lesssim\delta\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}+\delta^{-2}+\left|\log\varepsilon\right|

).

Step 1

(Proof of (

.

Then, for any

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
B=B(x_{0},\,R)
be a ball. Let
B^{\prime}
be the ball concentric with
B

and with radius
r<R
. Assume that
f
is a continuous function that
belongs to
L^{p}(B)
for any
p\in[1,+\infty)
and that

v\in C^{2}(B)
is a non-negative function satisfying

(2.44)

-\frac{1}{2}\Delta v+\frac{1}{4\varepsilon^{2}}v\leq f\qquad\mbox{in }B

and

\left\|v\right\|_{L^{\infty}(B)}<+\infty.

Then,

(2.45)

\left\|v\right\|_{L^{p}(B^{\prime})}\lesssim\varepsilon^{2},\qquad\mbox{for any }p\in[1,+\infty),

where the implicit constant in front of the right-hand side depends only on

p
,
r
,
R
,
\left\|v\right\|_{L^{\infty}(B)}
, and
\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p}(B)}
.

Proof.

We fix
\zeta\in C^{\infty}_{c}(B)
,
a smooth cut-off function satisfying

(2.46)

0\leq\zeta\leq 1\,\,\mbox{ in }B,\qquad\zeta\equiv 1\,\,\mbox{ in }B^{\prime},\qquad\left|\nabla\zeta\right|\lesssim\frac{1}{R-r}\,\,\mbox{ in }B.

We observe that, by Hölder’s inequality, it is enough
to prove (
2.45
) for any
p
large enough.
Therefore, we can assume that
p>2
. We define

\psi:=\zeta^{2p}v^{p-1}.

The function
\psi
belongs to
C^{1}_{c}(B)
and we can
test the inequality (
2.44
) against
\psi
.
Since
\psi\geq 0
, this yields

(2.47)

\int_{B}\frac{1}{2}\nabla\psi\cdot\nabla v\,{\mathrm{d}}x+\frac{1}{4\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{B}\zeta^{2p}v^{p}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\leq\int_{B}\zeta^{2p}v^{p-1}f\,{\mathrm{d}}x.

Since
0\leq\zeta\leq 1
, we have
\zeta^{2p}\leq\zeta^{2(p-1)}
pointwise
and therefore, by Hölder’s inequality,

(2.48)

\int_{B}\zeta^{2p}v^{p-1}f\,{\mathrm{d}}x\leq\int_{B}\zeta^{2(p-1)}v^{p-1}\left|f\right|\,{\mathrm{d}}x\leq\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p}(B)}\left(\int_{B}\zeta^{2p}v^{p}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\right)^{1-1/p}.

On the other hand, we have

\int_{B}\nabla\psi\cdot\nabla v\,{\mathrm{d}}x=\int_{B}\left(2p\zeta^{2p-1}v^{p-1}\nabla\zeta\cdot\nabla v+(p-1)\zeta^{2p}v^{p-2}\left|\nabla v\right|^{2}\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}x,

so that, by (
2.47
), we obtain

(2.49)

\begin{split}\int_{B}&\frac{1}{2}(p-1)\zeta^{2p}v^{p-2}\left|\nabla v\right|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}x+\frac{1}{4\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{B}\zeta^{2p}v^{p}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\\
&\leq\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p}(B)}\left(\int_{B}\zeta^{2p}v^{p}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\right)^{1-1/p}+\int_{B}p\zeta^{2p-1}v^{p-1}\left|\nabla\zeta\right|\left|\nabla v\right|\,{\mathrm{d}}x.\end{split}

Since
p>2
, we may rewrite the integrand in the
last term on the right-hand side as

p\zeta^{2p-1}v^{p-1}\left|\nabla\zeta\right|\left|\nabla v\right|=p\left(\zeta^{p-1}v^{p/2}\left|\nabla\zeta\right|\right)\left(\zeta^{p}v^{p/2-1}\left|\nabla v\right|\right)

Thus, by applying Young’s inequality and using (
2.46
),
we obtain the bound

(2.50)

p\zeta^{2p-1}v^{p-1}\left|\nabla\zeta\right|\left|\nabla v\right|\leq\frac{C^{\prime}_{p}}{(R-r)^{2}}\zeta^{2(p-1)}{v}^{p}+\frac{1}{2}(p-1)\zeta^{2p}v^{p-2}\left|\nabla v\right|^{2},

where
C^{\prime}_{p}
is a positive constant that depends only on
p
.
Plugging (
2.50
) into (
2.49
)
and recalling that
v^{p}\leq\left\|v\right\|_{L^{\infty}(B)}v^{p-1}
pointwise, we arrive at

\frac{1}{4\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{B}\zeta^{2p}v^{p}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\leq C_{p}\left(r,\,R,\,\left\|v\right\|_{L^{\infty}(B)},\,\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p}(B)}\right)\left[\left(\int_{B}\zeta^{2p}v^{p}\,{\mathrm{d}}{x}\right)^{1-1/p}+\int_{B}\zeta^{2(p-1)}v^{p-1}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\right],

whence, by a further application of Hölder’s inequality on the last
term on the right-hand side, we obtain

\frac{1}{4\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{B}\zeta^{2p}v^{p}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\leq C_{p}\left(r,\,R,\,\left\|v\right\|_{L^{\infty}(B)},\,\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p}(B)}\right)\left(\int_{B}\zeta^{2p}v^{p}\,{\mathrm{d}}{x}\right)^{1-1/p},

for a possibly larger constant
C_{p}\left(r,\,R,\,\left\|v\right\|_{L^{\infty}(B)},\,\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p}(B)}\right)

(still depending only on
p
,
R
, and
\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p}(B)}
),
so that

\left(\int_{B}\zeta^{2p}v^{p}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\right)^{1/p}\leq C_{p}\left(r,\,R,\,\left\|v\right\|_{L^{\infty}(B)},\,\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p}(B)}\right)\varepsilon^{2},

and, recalling that
\zeta\equiv 1
in
B^{\prime}
, the
desired inequality (
2.45
) follows.
∎

and then, by (
2.42
) and (
2.58
),
we obtain

\left\|\nabla\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(B)}\leq C_{p}\varepsilon^{-2/p}\leq C_{\alpha}\varepsilon^{-\alpha},

where the constant
C_{\alpha}
in the right-hand side depends only on
\alpha
,

\beta
,
x_{0}
, and
R
.∎

2.3
An estimate for the energy of
\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}

Proposition 2.9
.

Assume that (
10
) holds.
There exists a constant
C>0
,
depending only on
\beta
,
{\rm C}_{\rm pot}
, and the boundary data,
such that, for any
\varepsilon
small enough, there holds

(2.62)

\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}\leq C.

Proof.

The strategy for the proof is to test Equation (
5
)
against a suitable test function, then apply the assumption (
10
) on the potential
and the estimate for
\nabla\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}
given by Proposition
2.1
.
Before going to the details, let us recall some notation.
Let
\ell
be defined as in (
1.18
).
We recall from Lemma
1.2
that,
for any
x\in\Omega
such that
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}(x)\neq 0
,
the function
\ell(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}(x),\,\cdot\,)
has exactly
two minimisers in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, that is

(2.63)

\mathbf{M}_{\pm}(x):=\pm\left(\sqrt{2}\beta\rho_{\varepsilon}(x)+1\right)^{1/2}\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}(x)

where
\rho_{\varepsilon}(x):=\left|\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}(x)\right|

and
\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}(x)
is a unit eigenvector corresponding to the (unique)
positive eigenvalue of
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}(x)
.
Let
\Sigma_{x}:=\Sigma(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}(x)):=\{\mathbf{M}_{\pm}(x)\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
For any
\mathbf{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
\dist(\mathbf{N},\,\Sigma_{x})\leq 1
,
let
\pi_{x}(\mathbf{N}):=\pi(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}(x),\,\mathbf{N})
be the projection
of
\mathbf{N}
to
\Sigma_{x}
, as defined in (
1.24
).

Step 1

(Construction of
\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon}
)
.

Let
\delta\in(0,\,1/2)
be a fixed parameter,
to be chosen later (independently of
\varepsilon
).
Let
\xi
,
\zeta\colon[0,\,+\infty)\to[0,\,1]
be
smooth functions such that

(2.64)

\displaystyle\xi(t)=0\ \textrm{ if }0\leq t\leq\frac{1}{2},\qquad\xi(t)=1\ \textrm{ if }t\geq\frac{3}{4}

(2.65)

\displaystyle\zeta(t)=1\ \textrm{ if }0\leq t\leq\delta,\qquad\zeta(t)=0\ \textrm{ if }t\geq 2\delta

For
x\in\Omega
, we define

\eta_{\varepsilon}(x):=\xi\left(\rho_{\varepsilon}(x)\right)\zeta\left(\dist(\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}(x),\,\Sigma_{x})\right)

and

(2.66)

\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon}(x):=\left(1-\eta_{\varepsilon}(x)\right)\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}(x)+\eta_{\varepsilon}(x)\,\pi_{x}(\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}(x))

The value
\eta_{\varepsilon}(x)
is non-zero only if
\rho_{\varepsilon}(x)\geq 1/2

and
\dist(\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}(x),\,\Sigma_{x})\leq 2\delta<1
,
therefore
\pi_{x}(\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}(x))

is well-defined if
\eta_{\varepsilon}(x)\neq 0
.

We use
\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon}
as a test function in Equation (
5
).
Assume for a moment we are imposing mixed boundary conditions,
as in (
8
), (
9
).
Then, by testing Equation (
5
)
against
\varepsilon\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}-\varepsilon\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon}
, integrating by parts,
and applying Young’s inequality, we obtain

(2.67)

\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}\leq\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left|\nabla\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\nabla_{\mathbf{M}}\ell(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon},\,\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon})\cdot(\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon}-\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon})\right)\!,

where
\nabla_{\mathbf{M}}
denotes the (partial) gradient with respect
to the
\mathbf{M}
-variable. Equation (
2.67
) remains true if we
are considering Dirichlet boundary conditions as
in (
6
), (
7
). Indeed, the assumption (
7
)
implies that the boundary data satisfy
\left|\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{bd}}(x)\right|=1
,

\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{bd}}(x)\in\Sigma_{x}
and, hence,
\eta_{\varepsilon}(x)=1
,

\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon}(x)=\pi_{x}(\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{bd}}(x))=\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{bd}}(x)
for each
x\in\partial\Omega
.
As a consequence,
\varepsilon\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}-\varepsilon\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon}=0
on
\partial\Omega

and (
2.67
) follows, irrespective of whether we
are imposing Dirichlet or mixed boundary conditions.

Step 2

(Estimate on
\nabla\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon}
)
.

We claim that

(2.68)

\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}\lesssim\int_{\Omega}\left(\varepsilon\left|\nabla\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}f_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon},\,\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon})\right)\!.

Here (and in the rest of this proof, wherever we use the notation
\lesssim
)
the implicit constant in front of the right-hand side
depends on
\delta
, but not on
\varepsilon
.
In order to prove this claim, we consider the (open) set

(2.69)

E_{\varepsilon}:=\left\{x\in\Omega\colon\rho_{\varepsilon}(x)>\frac{3}{4},\ \dist(\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}(x),\,\Sigma_{x})<\delta\right\}\!.

By Lemma
1.5
, there exists
a constant
c_{1}(\delta)>0
, depending on
\delta
and
\beta

but not on
\varepsilon
, such that

f_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon},\,\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon})\geq c_{1}(\delta)\,\varepsilon

on
\Omega\setminus E_{\varepsilon}
.
As a consequence, we have

(2.70)

\left|\Omega\setminus E_{\varepsilon}\right|\leq\frac{1}{c_{1}(\delta)\,\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}f_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon},\,\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}).

On the other hand, the bound (
1.36
)
implies the pointwise estimate
\left|\nabla\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon}\right|\lesssim\varepsilon^{-1}
.
Therefore, from (
2.70
) we deduce

(2.71)

\varepsilon\int_{\Omega\setminus E_{\varepsilon}}\left|\nabla\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}\lesssim\frac{{\left|\Omega\setminus E_{\varepsilon}\right|}}{\varepsilon}\lesssim\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega}f_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon},\,\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}).

It remains to estimate
\left|\nabla\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon}\right|
on
E_{\varepsilon}
.
For any
x\in E_{\varepsilon}
, we have
\eta_{\varepsilon}(x)=1

(because of (
2.64
), (
2.65
)) and
\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon}(x)=\pi_{x}(\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}(x))
.
By definition of
\pi_{x}
(see (
1.24
)),

\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon}(x)
is a nonzero eigenvector of
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}(x)

corresponding to the (unique) positive eigenvalue.
We claim that
\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon}
is smooth in the open set
E_{\varepsilon}
.
Indeed, let
B\subseteq E_{\varepsilon}
be an arbitrary open ball.
The spectral theorem implies that
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}
can be written in the form

\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}=\frac{\rho_{\varepsilon}}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}\otimes\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}-\mathbf{m}_{\varepsilon}\otimes\mathbf{m}_{\varepsilon}\right)\qquad\textrm{in }B,

where
(\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon},\,\mathbf{m}_{\varepsilon})
is a smooth orthonormal
frame of eigenvectors for
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}
, and (see (
2.63
))

(2.72)

\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon}(x)=\pi_{x}(\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}(x))=\pm\left(\sqrt{2}\beta\rho_{\varepsilon}(x)+1\right)^{1/2}\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}(x)\qquad\textrm{at any point }x\in B,

By definition of
\pi_{x}
, the sign of
\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon}(x)\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}(x)

is the same as the sign of
\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}(x)\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}(x)

and is nonzero, because
\dist(\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}(x),\,\Sigma_{x})\leq 1/2
.
Therefore, the sets
B^{+}:=\{x\in B\colon\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon}(x)\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}(x)>0\}

and
B^{-}:=\{x\in B\colon\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon}(x)\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}(x)<0\}

are open, disjoint, and their union contains
B
.
As
B
is connected, it follows that either
B=B^{+}

or
B=B^{-}
— that is, the sign in (
2.72
) is constant.
Either way,
\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon}
is smooth in
B
and there holds

\left|\nabla\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon}\right|\lesssim\left|\nabla\rho_{\varepsilon}\right|+\left|\nabla\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}\right|\lesssim\left|\nabla\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\right|

pointwise in
B
. As the ball
B\subseteq E_{\varepsilon}

is chosen arbitrarily, we have

(2.73)

\int_{E_{\varepsilon}}\left|\nabla\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}\lesssim\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}.

Now, (
2.68
) follows from (
2.71
)
and (
2.73
).

Step 3

(Estimate on the potential term)
.

We claim that

(2.74)

\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}\nabla_{\mathbf{M}}\ell(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon},\,\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon})\cdot(\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon}-\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon})\lesssim\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega}f_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon},\,\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}).

To this end, we consider again the
set
E_{\varepsilon}
defined by (
2.69
) above.
The
L^{\infty}
-bound (
1.35
) and
the estimate (
2.71
) imply

(2.75)

\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega\setminus E_{\varepsilon}}\nabla_{\mathbf{M}}\ell(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon},\,\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon})\cdot(\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon}-\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon})\lesssim\frac{\left|\Omega\setminus E_{\varepsilon}\right|}{\varepsilon}\lesssim\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega}f_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon},\,\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}).

To estimate the contribution from
E_{\varepsilon}
,
we observe that

\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon}(x)=\pi_{x}(\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}(x))

at each point
x\in E_{\varepsilon}
. As a consequence,
Lemma
1.4
and the
L^{\infty}
-bound (
1.35
) imply

(2.76)

\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{E_{\varepsilon}}\nabla_{\mathbf{M}}\ell(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon},\,\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon})\cdot(\mathbf{N}_{\varepsilon}-\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon})\leq 0,

so long as we choose
\delta
small enough.
(We can choose a suitable value of
\delta
that depends
only on
\beta
and the
L^{\infty}
-norm
of
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}
; however, the latter is bounded uniformly
with respect to
\varepsilon
, so
\delta
can be chosen
independently of
\varepsilon
.)
Combining (
2.75
) with (
2.76
),
we obtain (
2.74
).

Step 4

(Conclusion)
.

From (
2.67
), (
2.68
) and (
2.74
),
we deduce

(2.77)

\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}\lesssim\int_{\Omega}\left(\varepsilon\left|\nabla\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}f_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon},\,\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon})\right)\!,

The terms at the right-hand side are bounded
uniformly with respect of
\varepsilon
, because of
Proposition
2.1
and
Assumption (
10
).
The lemma follows.
∎

Remark 2.6

More precisely, like in Proposition

From Proposition

Corollary 2.10
.

Assume that (
10
) holds. Then, for any

\varepsilon>0
small enough,

(2.78)

\left\|j(\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon})\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\lesssim\varepsilon^{-1/2}

for any
p\in[1,2]
. As a consequence,

(2.79)

\varepsilon\left\|\div j(\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon})\right\|_{W^{-1,p}(\Omega)}\lesssim\varepsilon^{1/2}

for any
p\in[1,2]
and any
\varepsilon
small enough.

Proof.

Let
p\in[1,2]
be arbitrary and assume
\varepsilon
is so small
that (
2.62
) holds.
Since
j(\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon})=\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\times\nabla\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}
, by the uniform bound

\left\|\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\lesssim 1
, the
Hölder inequality, and (

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega}\left|j(\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon})\right|^{p}\,{\mathrm{d}}x&=\int_{\Omega}\left|\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\times\nabla\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{p}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\leq\int_{\Omega}\left|\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{p}\left|\nabla\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{p}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\\

\varepsilon

\begin{split}\left|\left\langle\varepsilon\div(j(\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon})),\,\Psi\right\rangle_{W^{-1,p}(\Omega),W^{1,p^{\prime}}_{0}(\Omega)}\right|&=\varepsilon\left|\int_{\Omega}j(\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon})\cdot\nabla\Psi\,{\mathrm{d}}x\right|\\

3
Compactness

In this section, we prove compactness properties, in appropriate norms,
for both the
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}
-component and the
\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}
-component of
sequences
\{(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon},\,\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon})\}
of critical
points of
\mathscr{F}_{\varepsilon}
, subject to boundary condition either
as in (
6
)–(
7
) or as in (
8
)–(
9
)
and satisfying assumption (
10
).
We first prove convergence (up to subsequences) for the

\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}
-component to a limiting map

\mathbf{Q}_{\star}:\Omega\to\mathscr{N}
. For this task, we employ methods
reminiscent of the standard Ginzburg-Landau theory but appropriately
modified to take the coupling term into account. A key step consists in
proving that the energy densities
\mu_{\varepsilon}
defined in (
12
)
converge to a limiting measure
\mu_{\star}
whose support is a finite set of points.
Then, as in
[
20
]
,
we observe that, away from the (finite) energy-concentration set
and up to a suitable change of variable (presented in Section
3.1
below),
we can rewrite the functional
\mathscr{F}_{\varepsilon}
as
the sum of a Ginzburg-Landau type functional (with a slightly modified potential)
and a vectorial Allen-Cahn functional including a perturbation term which is small
in energy.
Taking advantage of this fact and
of classical compactness results for sequences with bounded Allen-Cahn energy
from
[
3
,
22
]
, we eventually show that a subsequence of

\{\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\}
converges in
L^{p}(\Omega)
, for any finite
p\geq 1
.

3.1
Changes of variables: from
\mathbf{Q}
to
\mathbf{q}
and from
\mathbf{M}
to
\mathbf{u}

Following
[
20
, Section 3]
,
we introduce suitable types of “change of variables” for both the
\mathbf{Q}
-component
and the
\mathbf{M}
-component of a pair
(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{M})\in\mathscr{S}_{0}^{2\times 2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

As for the
\mathbf{Q}
-component, we recall from (
1.1
) that the map

\mathscr{S}_{0}^{2\times 2}\ni\mathbf{Q}\mapsto\mathbf{q}:=\sqrt{2}\left(Q_{11},\,Q_{12}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

provides an isometric isomorphism between
\mathscr{S}_{0}^{2\times 2}
(endowed with
the Frobenius scalar product) and
\mathbb{R}^{2}
(with the standard
scalar product). Of course, we can also view
\mathbf{q}
as a complex
number, in the obvious way.
This correspondence obviously extends to an isometric
correspondence between
\mathbf{Q}
-tensor fields and vector fields.
With the help of Lemma
1.1
, we deduce that

(3.1)

\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{1}{2}\left|\nabla\mathbf{q}\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{8\varepsilon^{2}}\left(\left|\mathbf{q}\right|^{2}-1\right)^{2}\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}x\leq\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{M})+\beta^{2}\int_{\Omega}\left|\mathbf{M}\right|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}x.

Thus, in view of Theorem
2.11
and of the uniform bound (
1.35
),
the Ginzburg-Landau energy of a sequence
\{\mathbf{q}_{\varepsilon}\}
associated with a sequence
of critical points
\{(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon},\,\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon})\}
is logarithmically bounded.
This allows us to apply to
\mathbf{Q}
-tensor fields
several results typical of the Ginzburg-Landau theory with essentially no changes.
Usually,
we will understood the usage of the above correspondence, whenever
convenient, for the rest of this paper.

Let
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathscr{S}_{0}^{2\times 2}\setminus\{0\}
. Then, by the spectral theorem, we may write

\mathbf{Q}=\frac{\left|\mathbf{Q}\right|}{\sqrt{2}}(\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}-\mathbf{m}\otimes\mathbf{m})

for orthogonal vectors
\mathbf{n}
,
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
, where
\mathbf{n}
denotes
the eigenvector of
\mathbf{Q}
relative to the positive eigenvalue while

\mathbf{m}
, because of the zero-trace constraint, is relative to the
negative eigenvalue. In turn,
we may decompose any
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
as

\mathbf{M}=(\mathbf{M}\cdot\mathbf{n})\,\mathbf{n}+(\mathbf{M}\cdot\mathbf{m})\,\mathbf{m}.

As in
[
20
, Equation (3.6)]
, we define

u_{1}:=(\mathbf{M}\cdot\mathbf{n}),\qquad u_{2}:=(\mathbf{M}\cdot\mathbf{m}).

Upon setting
\mathbf{u}:=(u_{1},\,u_{2})
, we clearly have

\left|\mathbf{M}\right|=\left|\mathbf{u}\right|.

The above discussion generalises to
\mathbf{Q}
-tensor field and
vector fields as follows.
Let
G\subseteq\Omega
be a smooth,
simply connected domain. Let

\{(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon},\,\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon})\}\subset W^{1,2}\left(G,\,\mathscr{S}_{0}^{2\times 2}\right)\times W^{1,2}(G,\,\mathbb{R}^{2})

be a sequence satisfying, for any
\varepsilon>0
,

(3.2)

\displaystyle\int_{G}\left(\frac{1}{2}\left|\nabla\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{4\varepsilon^{2}}\left(\left|\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}-1\right)^{2}\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}x\lesssim\left|\log\varepsilon\right|,

(3.3)

\displaystyle\left|\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}(x)\right|\geq\frac{1}{2},\quad\left|\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}(x)\right|\leq A\qquad\mbox{for any }x\in G,

where the implicit constant on the right-hand side
of (
3.2
) does not depend on
\varepsilon
and

A
is some positive constant that does not depend on
\varepsilon
as well.
By (
1.35
) and Proposition
2.1
,
these assumptions are verified for critical points
under either (
6
)-(
7
) or (
8
)-(
9
),
provided that assumption (
10
) is in force.
Then, since
G
is simply connected and smooth, we may coherently decompose

(3.4)

\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}=\frac{\left|\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\right|}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}\otimes\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}-\mathbf{m}_{\varepsilon}\otimes\mathbf{m}_{\varepsilon}\right)\qquad\mbox{in }G,

where
(\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon},\,\mathbf{m}_{\varepsilon})
is an orthonormal frame of eigenvectors
of
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}
in
G
, with
\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}\in W^{1,2}(G,\,\mathbb{S}^{1})
and

\mathbf{m}_{\varepsilon}\in W^{1,2}(G,\,\mathbb{S}^{1})
— in fact, since
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}
is
smooth,
\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}
,
\mathbf{m}_{\varepsilon}
are also smooth. We will always denote with

\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}(x)
the eigenvector of
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}(x)
relative to its
(strictly) positive eigenvalue.
As in the above, we then let

By Lemma

Moreover, by the uniform bound in

\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\to\mathbf{Q}_{\star}

Next, we state an additional convergence property for
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}
.
To this purpose, we first recall that

writes as a sum of Dirac deltas,
whose multiplicities are the degrees of
\mathbf{Q}
computed on small circles
around the singular points. The singularities with degree different from

of
\mathbf{Q}

these are captured, in location and charge, by

,

[

Also, we recall that,

well-defined as an element of

(i.e., it is either
an integer or a half-integer). In particular, being

\mathbf{Q}_{\star}\in W^{1,1}(\Omega,\,\mathscr{N})
, it has a well-defined topological degree
around each point in
\spt\mu_{\star}
, its Jacobian has the structure
described above, and since its topological singular set is the support of

J(\mathbf{Q}_{\star})
, it also follows that the sum of the degrees of its topological
singularities equals the degree of
\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{bd}}
.

Without loss of generality, we may suppose that first

N_{\rm top}\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
points in
\spt\mu_{\star}

denotes the number of topological singularities
of
\mathbf{Q}_{\star}
and the numbers

\mathbf{Q}_{\star}

3.3

Here, we prove that any sequence

of functions

\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\mathcal{AC}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon};\,G)\leq C<+\infty

p

The argument relies on the following specialisation to our case
of a classical compactness and lower-semicontinuity theorem
for sequences with bounded Allen-Cahn energy.

Theorem 3.12

(
[
22
, Theorem 4.1 and Theorem 3.4]

Any family
\{\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}\}

\mathcal{AC}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon};\,G)\leq C<+\infty

\mathbf{u}_{\star}\in L^{1}(G,\,\mathbb{R}^{2})
and a (non-relabelled subsequence) such that

\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}\to\mathbf{u}_{\star}
a.e. in
G

where

In fact,
[
22
, Theorem 3.4]
is a full
\Gamma
-convergence
statement in the

-topology.
Similar results, allowing for more general energies of Allen-Cahn type
having an arbitrary (finite) number of
wells of equal depth, can be found in
[
3
]
.

Remark 3.9

In particular,

Using Lemma
3.3

-compactness in

Appendix A
The pure Dirichlet case:
W^{1,2}_{\loc}
-convergence up to the boundary for
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}

In this appendix, we assume that
\{\left(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon},\,\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\right)\}
is a sequence
of critical points of
\mathscr{F}_{\varepsilon}
subject to the pure Dirichlet boundary
conditions (
6

More precisely, we have the following proposition.

Proposition A.1
.

Let
x_{0}\in\partial\Omega
and let

be any ball satisfying

(A.1)

\overline{B(x_{0},\,2R)}\cap\spt\mu_{\star}=\emptyset.

W^{1,2}\left(B(x_{0},\,R)\cap\Omega\right)

As a first step towards the proof of
Proposition

the Pohozaev identity in Lemma
1.8
.

Lemma A.2
.

Assume that
\Omega
is star-shaped about a point
x_{\star}\in\Omega
.
Then, there holds

(A.2)

\begin{split}\frac{2}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega}f_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon},\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon})\,{\mathrm{d}}x&+\frac{R}{2}\int_{\partial\Omega}\left(\left|\partial_{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}+\varepsilon\left|\partial_{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}s\\
&\lesssim\frac{R}{2}\int_{\partial\Omega}\left(\left|\partial_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}+\varepsilon\left|\partial_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}+\frac{2}{\varepsilon^{2}}f_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon},\,\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon})\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}s\end{split}

where the implicit constant on the right-hand side depends only on
\Omega

and the boundary data.

Proof.

Take
\mathbf{X}:=x-x_{\star}
in (
1.48
). Since
\Omega
is star-shaped, there
is a constant
\alpha_{\Omega}>0
such that

(x-x_{\star})\cdot{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\geq\alpha_{\Omega},\qquad\forall x\in\partial\Omega.

On the other hand, precisely as in
[

, (12) of Chapter III]

({\boldsymbol{\nu}}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon})\cdot(\mathbf{X}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon})=\left((x-x_{\star})\cdot{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\right)\left|{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}+\left((x-x_{\star})\cdot{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\right)(\partial_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\partial_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon})

and the same holds for
\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}
.
Combing these facts and applying suitably Young’s inequality,
we obtain (
A.2
), with a constant depending only on

\alpha_{\Omega}

and the boundary data.
