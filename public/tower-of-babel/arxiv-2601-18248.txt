A particle on a ring or: how I learned to stop worrying and love θ \theta -vacua
Mohammad Aghaie, and Ryosuke Sato

Abstract
Recently, Ai, Cruz, Garbrecht, and Tamarit (ACGT) 2 ; 4 ; 3 ; 5 claimed that there is no strong CP problem by adopting a new order of limits in the volume and topological sector. We critically examine this proposal by focusing on simple one-dimensional quantum mechanics on a ring. We demonstrate that consistent results are obtained only when one sums over all topological sectors before taking the large T T limit. This observation justifies the conventional path integral formulation of gauge theories and implies that the strong CP problem does exist in QCD.

According to them, if the infinite spacetime volume limit (

This proposal has ignited a significant debate in the community and there have been a few responses from different perspectives. Benabou
et al.

\eta^{\prime}
meson, while Khoze

Moreover, Bhattacharya
13
argues that in a gapped quantum field theory such as QCD, the effect of boundary conditions on local observables is exponentially suppressed with the distance from the boundary. Consequently, although the total topological charge
Q
need not be exactly quantized at finite volume with open boundaries, physical observables depend only on bulk quantities such as local correlation functions, the vacuum energy density, and the response to
\bar{\theta}

Q

Q
becomes sharply peaked around integer values. Topological quantization therefore re-emerges as an emergent bulk property rather than a boundary condition, explaining why open and zero-field boundary conditions are physically equivalent for locally defined observables in the infrared limit, despite being mathematically distinct. This work might be the most direct response to the shortcoming suggested by ACGT.

More recently, Gamboa
et al.

27
challenged the ACGT claim by stressing the distinction between local and global observables, arguing that the disappearance of
\theta
from certain fermionic correlators does not signal CP conservation but rather reflects the insensitivity of local probes to global holonomy. In contrast, genuinely global quantities like topological susceptibility remain
\theta
-dependent. Also, Ringwald
44
argued that the vacuum energy of one-flavor QCD should be a real-analytic function of the quark mass due to the existence of a mass gap, a property that is incompatible with the chiral Lagrangian proposed by ACGT.

While the original arguments are formulated in four-dimensional gauge theory, their essential logic can be tested in simpler quantum-mechanical models where calculations are fully controllable. Two standard examples are particularly instructive: a free particle on a ring (the quantum rotor) and a particle on a ring subject to a periodic potential (the quantum pendulum). Both exhibit nontrivial topology and a
\theta
dependence, but they emphasize complementary aspects of the problem and come with distinct limitations.

The quantum rotor is the simplest exactly solvable system with nontrivial topology: its full spectrum and propagator can be derived analytically, and the latter requires an explicit sum over winding numbers, making the
\theta
dependence manifest. However, the absence of a potential means that the wavefunctions are completely delocalized, obscuring the notion of distinct “
n
-vacua.” By contrast, the quantum pendulum provides a more direct semiclassical analogy to gauge theory, with potential minima at
\phi=2\pi n
representing classical vacua and instanton tunneling producing a
\theta
-dependent Bloch ground state. This picture is physically transparent but not exactly solvable, as analytic control typically relies on semiclassical or WKB approximations rather than a fully explicit path-integral construction.

Here, we revisit the issue in these fully controlled quantum-mechanical systems where all relevant observables can be computed analytically via two equivalent methods: canonical quantization and the Euclidean path integral. While the canonical formulation provides an unambiguous benchmark, the path-integral approach allows us to explicitly implement the ACGT prescription and directly examine its consequences for important quantities such as energy spectrum, propagator, partition function, and topological susceptibility. We show that taking the Euclidean time extent
\beta\to\infty
prior to summing over topological sectors removes the contribution of nontrivial winding configurations, leading to physically inconsistent results. In particular, both systems exhibit explicit
\theta
dependence in their spectra, in agreement with standard quantum-mechanical expectations and in contradiction with the ACGT prescription.

We also compute the topological susceptibility in both formulations and explicitly verify their equivalence when the limits are taken consistently. This allows us to refute the claim of ref.
5
that the susceptibility originates from a contact term. Instead, we show that the susceptibility is an immediate consequence of contributions from topological sectors. We show that if these sectors are not summed before taking the infinite-time limit, the susceptibility vanishes identically, contradicting both canonical quantum mechanics and lattice results. Finally, we trace the origin of this discrepancy to an inconsistent implementation of time-translation invariance in ref.
5
, as discussed in section
2.4
.

As a direct consequence, the order-of-limits proposed by ACGT leads to the phenomenological inconsistencies emphasized by Benabou
et al.

11
. In particular, it implies a vanishing topological susceptibility in pure Yang–Mills theory (and in full QCD), in contradiction with lattice results and established phenomenology. Moreover, in the large-
N_{c}
limit the Witten–Veneziano relation directly ties the
\eta^{\prime}
mass to the Yang–Mills topological susceptibility, so a vanishing susceptibility is also incompatible with the observed
\eta^{\prime}
mass.

2
A ring without potential: quantum rotor

The quantum rotor is the simplest exactly solvable quantum-mechanical system whose compact, multiply connected configuration space gives rise to distinct winding sectors. Importantly, its dynamics is simple enough to be exactly solvable in both the canonical and path-integral formulations. Therefore, we use the quantum rotor as a toy model to investigate the conceptual issues raised by ACGT in the context of QCD, focusing in particular on the order of limits proposed in
2
, where the spacetime volume is taken to infinity before summing over all winding sectors.

To this end, we begin by solving the Schrödinger equation for this toy model in order to set the stage and fix our notation. We then compute the propagator using both approaches and demonstrate their equivalence. Finally, we use this framework to analyze the topological susceptibility and examine the consequences of taking the infinite-time limit prior to summing over topological sectors, thereby clarifying how different prescriptions affect the recovery of standard quantum-mechanical results.

Throughout this paper, we work in natural units and set
\hbar=1
. The real-time and Euclidean time extents are denoted by
T
and
\beta
, respectively.

2.1
Canonical quantization and energy spectrum

Let us consider a free particle of mass
m
constrained to move on a ring of radius
R
. The Lagrangian for this system is

L(\phi,\dot{\phi},t)=\frac{1}{2}I\dot{\phi}^{2}+\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\dot{\phi}+V(\phi,t),

(1)

where
I=mR^{2}
is the moment of inertia and
\theta
is a vacuum angle conjugate to the winding number. As is well known, the second term is a total derivative and therefore does not contribute to the classical equations of motion. Nevertheless, when the configuration space satisfies
\phi\sim\phi+2\pi
, this term has nontrivial quantum-mechanical consequences.
As shown in the appendix
A
, the effect of
\theta
can be understood as Aharonov-Bohm effect on a charged particle.

In order to isolate the role of topology, we focus in this section on the simplest case with vanishing potential and, for the moment, ignore the explicit
\theta
-term in the Lagrangian. Using the canonical momentum
p=I\dot{\phi}
, the Hamiltonian of the system takes the form

H=-\frac{1}{2I}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\phi^{2}}.

(2)

The time-independent Schrödinger equation (TISE) is therefore

-\frac{1}{2I}\frac{\partial^{2}\psi}{\partial\phi^{2}}=E\psi(\phi).

(3)

Since the angular coordinate
\phi
is periodic,
\phi\sim\phi+2\pi
, the most general boundary condition consistent with a single-valued probability density is

|\psi(\phi+2\pi)|^{2}=|\psi(\phi)|^{2}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\psi(\phi+2\pi)=e^{i\theta}\psi(\phi),

(4)

which is known as the twisted boundary condition. The parameter
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
plays the role of a vacuum angle, analogous to the
\theta
-parameter in Yang–Mills theory.

Solving eq. (
3
) subject to the boundary condition (
4
), we obtain the normalized eigenfunctions

\psi_{n}(\phi)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{ik_{n}\phi},\qquad k_{n}=n+\frac{\theta}{2\pi},\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z},

(5)

with corresponding energy eigenvalues

E_{n}(\theta)=\frac{k_{n}^{2}}{2I}=\frac{1}{2I}\left(n+\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\right)^{2}.

(6)

An equivalent description is obtained if we keep the
\theta
-term in the Lagrangian,
\mathcal{L}\supset\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\dot{\phi}
. In this case, the canonical momentum is shifted by
p\to p+\theta/2\pi
, and the wavefunctions are strictly periodic,
\psi(\phi+2\pi)=\psi(\phi)
, while the Hamiltonian is modified by a corresponding momentum shift. The resulting energy spectrum is identical to (
6
), demonstrating the equivalence between twisted boundary conditions and the inclusion of a topological
\theta
-term at the canonical level.

2.2
Propagator

The propagator of the quantum rotor can be computed exactly in two complementary ways. Canonical quantization, via the Schrödinger equation, yields the well-known energy spectrum and its explicit
\theta
dependence. Independently, the path-integral formulation expresses the propagator as a sum over winding sectors with an explicit
\theta
term, making the role of topology and the order of limits manifest. This allows a direct test of the ACGT order-of-limits prescription against the exact canonical result. In the following, we present both approaches.

Method A: Canonical Quantization

The propagator is defined as

\displaystyle K(\phi_{f},t_{f};\phi_{i},t_{i})

\displaystyle=\langle\phi_{f}|e^{-iH(t_{f}-t_{i})}|\phi_{i}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\langle\phi_{f}|n\rangle\langle n|\phi_{i}\rangle e^{-iE_{n}(t_{f}-t_{i})}.

(7)

Using the eigenfunctions
\psi_{n}(\phi)=e^{in\phi}/\sqrt{2\pi}
, this expression becomes

\displaystyle K(\phi_{f},t_{f};\phi_{i},t_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}e^{in(\phi_{i}-\phi_{f})}e^{-iE_{n}T},\qquad T=t_{f}-t_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\!\left[in(\phi_{i}-\phi_{f})-\frac{iT}{2I}\left(n+\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\exp\left(-\frac{i\theta^{2}T}{8\pi^{2}I}\right)\vartheta\left(-\frac{\phi_{f}-\phi_{i}}{2\pi}-\frac{\theta T}{4I\pi^{2}};~-\frac{T}{2\pi I}\right),

(8)

where
\vartheta(z;\tau)
is Jacobi theta function defined as

\vartheta(z;\tau)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left(i\pi\tau n^{2}+2\pi inz\right)
.
See also refs.
35
;
11
.

Method B: Path Integral

We now compute the same propagator using the path integral formulation. Setting
t_{i}=0
and
t_{f}=T
for convenience, the propagator can be written as

K(\phi_{f},T;\phi_{i},0)=\int_{\phi(0)=\phi_{i}}^{\phi(T)=\phi_{f}}\mathcal{D}\phi\;e^{iS[\phi]}.

(9)

The action, including a topological
\theta
-term, is

S_{\theta}[\phi]=\int_{0}^{T}dt\,\left[\frac{I}{2}\dot{\phi}^{2}+\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\dot{\phi}\right].

(10)

Then, the path integral becomes

\displaystyle K(\phi_{f},T;\phi_{i},0)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{\phi(0)=\phi_{i}}^{\phi(T)=\phi_{f}+2\pi k}\mathcal{D}\phi\;\exp\!\left[iS_{0}[\phi]+\frac{i\theta}{2\pi}(\phi_{f}-\phi_{i}+2\pi k)\right]

\displaystyle=\exp\!\left[\frac{i\theta}{2\pi}(\phi_{f}-\phi_{i})\right]\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}e^{ik\theta}\int_{\phi(0)=\phi_{i}}^{\phi(T)=\phi_{f}+2\pi k}\mathcal{D}\phi\;\exp\!\left[iS_{0}[\phi]\right].

(11)

The remaining path integral corresponds to a free particle on the real line,

\langle\phi_{f}+2\pi k,T|\phi_{i},0\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}=\sqrt{\frac{I}{2\pi iT}}\exp\!\left[\frac{iI(\phi_{f}-\phi_{i}+2\pi k)^{2}}{2T}\right].

(12)

where we need to include the contribution of all winding sectors,
\phi(T)=\phi_{f}+2\pi k
. Substituting this result into eq. (
11
), the propagator on the ring becomes

\displaystyle K(\phi_{f},T;\phi_{i},0)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{I}{2\pi iT}}\exp\!\left[\frac{i\theta}{2\pi}(\phi_{f}-\phi_{i})\right]\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}e^{ik\theta}\exp\!\left[\frac{iI(\phi_{f}-\phi_{i}+2\pi k)^{2}}{2T}\right]

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{I}{2\pi iT}}\exp\left(\frac{iI(\phi_{f}-\phi_{i})^{2}}{2T}+\frac{I\theta(\phi_{f}-\phi_{i})}{2\pi}\right)\vartheta\left(\frac{I(\phi_{f}-\phi_{i})}{T}+\frac{\theta}{2\pi};\frac{2I\pi}{T}\right).

(13)

By using the modular transformation,
\vartheta(z/\tau;-1/\tau)=(-i\tau)^{1/2}\exp\left(i\pi z^{2}/\tau\right)\vartheta(z;\tau)
, we can rewrite the propagator in eq. (
8
) as

\displaystyle K(\phi_{f},T;\phi_{i},0)|_{\rm eq.~\eqref{eq:propagator_Sch}}=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{I}{2\pi iT}}\exp\left(\frac{iI(\phi_{f}-\phi_{i})^{2}}{2T}+\frac{I\theta(\phi_{f}-\phi_{i})}{2\pi}\right)

\displaystyle\times\vartheta\left(-\frac{I(\phi_{f}-\phi_{i})}{T}-\frac{\theta}{2\pi};\frac{2I\pi}{T}\right).

(14)

The equivalence between eqs. (
8
) and (
13
) can be verified using
\vartheta(z;\tau)=\vartheta(-z;\tau)
.

Here we have focused on the propagator
K(\phi_{f},T;\phi_{i},0)
with finite
T
and we can see that eq. (
13
) cannot be equivalent to eq. (
8
) if we take a summation over
k
with a finite upperbound in eq. (
13
).
Importantly, our results are consistent with the remark in Schulman’s textbook
49
that indicates for a particle on a ring, the propagator,
K(\phi_{f},T;\phi_{i},0)
, must be a periodic function of both the initial and final angles,
\phi_{i}
and
\phi_{f}
. This periodicity, and hence the equivalence between the canonical and path-integral representations of the propagator, is guaranteed only when contributions from all integer winding sectors are included. Prescriptions that effectively restrict the sum over winding numbers therefore do not reproduce the full quantum-mechanical propagator.

2.3
The partition function and energy spectra

The partition function of the quantum rotor is defined in Euclidean time as

Z(\theta,\beta)\equiv\mathrm{Tr}\!\left(e^{-\beta H}\right),

(15)

where
\beta
denotes the Euclidean time extent. This function can be evaluated equivalently using canonical quantization or the path-integral formalism.
In the canonical approach, inserting a complete set of energy eigenstates of the Hamiltonian, one finds

Z(\theta,\beta)=\sum_{n}\langle n|e^{-\beta H}|n\rangle=\sum_{n}e^{-\beta E_{n}(\theta)}.

(16)

This representation makes the fact that the energy spectrum can be extracted from the exponential decomposition of
Z(\theta,\beta)
explicit. Alternatively, the same quantity can be computed using the Euclidean path integral. For the quantum rotor, the Euclidean action in the presence of a
\theta
term is

S_{E}[\phi;\theta]=\frac{I}{2}\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau\,\dot{\phi}^{2}(\tau)-i\theta\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau\,q(\tau),

(17)

where
q(\tau)=\dot{\phi}(\tau)/2\pi
is the topological charge density as it is defined in ref.
5
.

Then, the partition function takes the form

\displaystyle Z(\theta,\beta)=\int\mathcal{D}\phi\,\exp\Big( - S_E[\phi;\theta] \Big)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi I}{\beta}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\exp\left[-\frac{2\pi^{2}I}{\beta}\,n^{2}+in\theta\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\exp\!\left[-\frac{\beta}{2I}\left(n+\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\right)^{2}\right],

(18)

where we have used the results from
2.2
in the first line and we have used
the modular transformation
\vartheta(z/\tau;-1/\tau)=(-i\tau)^{1/2}\exp\left(i\pi z^{2}/\tau\right)\vartheta(z;\tau)
with
\tau=2\pi iI\beta
and
z=\theta/2\pi

in the second line.
This way, we can explicitly demonstrate that the path integral formalism can reproduce the results of the canonical quantization when the contribution of all winding sectors is summed over.

We can use this framework to examine the order of limits discussed by ACGT. Consider instead a truncated partition function in which only a finite range of winding numbers is included. This truncation should be viewed purely as a technical device to make the order of limits explicit, and its rationale and interpretation are discussed in detail in section
2.4
. So, let’s define a truncated partition function as,

Z_{\Delta}(\theta)=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi I}{\beta}}\sum_{n=-\Delta}^{\Delta}\exp\!\left[in\theta-\frac{2\pi^{2}In^{2}}{\beta}\right],

(19)

where
\Delta\in\mathbb{N}
is held fixed. For fixed
\Delta
and large
\beta
, the Gaussian factor varies slowly over
|n|\leq\Delta
and may be neglected at leading order,

Z_{\Delta}(\theta)\;\xrightarrow[\beta\to\infty]{}\;\sqrt{\frac{2\pi I}{\beta}}\sum_{n=-\Delta}^{\Delta}e^{in\theta}=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi I}{\beta}}\frac{\sin\!\left[(\Delta+\tfrac{1}{2})\theta\right]}{\sin(\theta/2)}.

(20)

This truncated expression fails to reproduce the correct energy spectrum of the quantum rotor. Moreover, the prefactor
\beta^{-1/2}
, is analogous to the
(VT)^{-1/2}
factor in ref.
33
.

From eq. (
16
), for
\beta\to\infty
the partition function is dominated by the ground state, and one defines

E_{0}=-\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\frac{1}{\beta}\log Z(\theta,\beta).

(21)

Using the truncated partition function, however, one obtains

E_{0}\sim\frac{1}{\beta}\log Z_{\Delta}\;\xrightarrow[\beta\to\infty]{}\;0\qquad(\text{for fixed }\Delta).

(22)

This result is equivalent to the claim by ACGT
2
;
5
that
\theta
-dependence in the partition function enters only through a normalization factor,
Z_{\theta}=N(\theta)Z_{0}
. However, it is in clear disagreement with the well
-
known and solid results in quantum mechanics.

2.4
Topological susceptibility

We now discuss the topological susceptibility of the quantum rotor and reexamine the discussions by Albandea
et al.

6
, Benabou
et al.

11
, and Ai
et al.

5
. Our goal is to clarify the precise origin of the nonvanishing susceptibility and the role played by the order of limits in its evaluation. To be concrete, first we compute the topological susceptibility using the following definition,

\chi=-\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\frac{1}{\beta}\left.\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta^{2}}\Big(\log Z(\theta,\beta)\Big)\right|_{\theta=0}.

(23)

We will calculate
\chi
using three different methods and show that they will give
\chi=1/4\pi^{2}I
. Also, we clarify the role of topological sectors.

Method A: Euclidean correlation function with semiclassical expansion

Taking two derivatives of (
18
) and evaluating the result at
\theta=0
, we obtain

\chi=\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\frac{-1}{\beta}\frac{1}{Z(0,\beta)}\int\mathcal{D}\phi\,\left(i\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau\,q(\tau)\right)\left(i\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau^{\prime}\,q(\tau^{\prime})\right)e^{-S_{E}[\phi;0]}.

(24)

This equation can be rewritten as an expectation value of the squared integrated topological charge,
\chi=\beta^{-1}\langle Q^{2}\rangle
with
Q=\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau\,q(\tau)
as it is done in ref.
11
. Moreover, using time-translation invariance, and taking the integral over
\tau^{\prime}
, it can be written as
\chi=\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau\,\langle q(\tau)q(0)\rangle
, as in eq. (1) of ref.
5
. However, in this form the
\beta\to\infty
limit is implicit, and its interplay with the temporal integrations is not manifest. As we show below, the two prescriptions are equivalent only when the order of limits is specified.

In contrast to the approach adopted in ref.
5
, here, we will proceed by evaluating the path integral directly. This is quite straight forwards as
\int d\tau\,q(\tau)=\int d\tau\;\dot{\phi}(\tau)/2\pi=n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Therefore the susceptibility is simply,

\displaystyle\chi=\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\frac{1}{\beta}\frac{1}{Z(0,\beta)}\int\mathcal{D}\phi\,n^{2}\,e^{-S_{E}[\phi;0]}=\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\frac{1}{\beta}\frac{\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}n^{2}\,\exp\!\big(-\frac{2\pi^{2}I}{\beta}n^{2}\big)}{\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\exp\!\big(-\frac{2\pi^{2}I}{\beta}n^{2}\big)}.

(25)

which is equivalent to the eq. (37) in ref.
11
. To connect with the correlator-based approach of ref.
5
, we return to eq. (
24
) and write

\chi=\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}\beta}\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau\,\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau^{\prime}\,\frac{1}{Z(0,\beta)}\int\!\mathcal{D}\phi\,\dot{\phi}(\tau)\dot{\phi}(\tau^{\prime})\,e^{-S_{E}[\phi;0]}.

(26)

We now expand the path integral semiclassically around classical winding solutions,

\phi(\tau)=\phi_{\rm cl}(\tau)+\eta(\tau),\qquad\phi_{\rm cl}(\tau)=\phi_{0}+\frac{2\pi n}{\beta}\tau,\qquad\eta(0)=\eta(\beta)=0.

(27)

As a result, the measure factorizes into winding and fluctuation parts and substituting
\dot{\phi}(\tau)
,

\displaystyle\chi

\displaystyle=\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}\beta}\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau^{\prime}\,\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{1}{Z(0,\beta)}\int\mathcal{D}\eta\,\left(\frac{2\pi n}{\beta}+\dot{\eta}(\tau)\right)\left(\frac{2\pi n}{\beta}+\dot{\eta}(\tau^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle\quad\times\exp\!\left[-\frac{I}{2}\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau^{\prime\prime}\,\dot{\eta}^{2}\right]\exp\!\left[-\frac{2\pi^{2}I}{\beta}n^{2}\right].

(28)

The cross terms between the classical configuration and the fluctuation field vanish identically due to the Dirichlet boundary conditions imposed on
\eta
. As a result, the topological susceptibility can be written as

\displaystyle\chi

\displaystyle=\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}\beta}\Bigg[\frac{\int d\tau d\tau^{\prime}\,\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\,\left(\frac{2\pi n}{\beta}\right)^{2}\,\exp\!\left[-\frac{2\pi^{2}I}{\beta}n^{2}\right]}{\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\exp\!\left[-\frac{2\pi^{2}I}{\beta}n^{2}\right]}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad~+\frac{\int d\tau d\tau^{\prime}\,\int\mathcal{D}\eta\;\dot{\eta}(\tau)\dot{\eta}(\tau^{\prime})\,\exp\!\left[-\frac{I}{2}\int d\tau^{\prime\prime}\,\dot{\eta}^{2}\right]}{\int\mathcal{D}\eta\,\exp\!\left[-\frac{I}{2}\int d\tau^{\prime\prime}\,\dot{\eta}^{2}\right]}\Bigg]

(29)

Using the identity,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Z[0]}\int{\cal D}\eta~\dot{\eta}(\tau)\dot{\eta}(\tau^{\prime})e^{-S_{E}[\eta;0]}=\frac{d}{d\tau}\frac{d}{d\tau^{\prime}}\frac{1}{Z[0]}\int{\cal D}\eta~\eta(\tau)\eta(\tau^{\prime})e^{-S_{E}[\eta;0]}\equiv\frac{d}{d\tau}\frac{d}{d\tau^{\prime}}\langle{\rm T}\,\eta(\tau)\eta(\tau^{\prime})\rangle.

(30)

The susceptibility simplifies to

\displaystyle\chi=\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\left[\frac{\langle n^{2}\rangle}{\beta}+\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}\beta}\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau\,\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau^{\prime}\,\frac{d}{d\tau}\frac{d}{d\tau^{\prime}}\langle{\rm T}\,\eta(\tau)\eta(\tau^{\prime})\rangle\right].

(31)

Then, defining the fluctuation propagator as
G(\tau,\tau^{\prime};\beta)=\langle\eta(\tau)\eta(\tau^{\prime})\rangle
that
satisfies Dirichlet boundary conditions,
G(0,\tau^{\prime};\beta)=G(\beta,\tau^{\prime};\beta)=0,
we obtain (see, e.g., chapter 3 of
34
)

\displaystyle G(\tau,\tau^{\prime};\beta)=\frac{\tau+\tau^{\prime}-|\tau-\tau^{\prime}|}{2I}-\frac{\tau\tau^{\prime}}{\beta I},

(32)

So,

\frac{d}{d\tau}\frac{d}{d\tau^{\prime}}G(\tau,\tau^{\prime};\beta)=\frac{1}{I}\,\delta(\tau-\tau^{\prime})-\frac{1}{\beta I}.

(33)

Substituting this result in eq. (
31
), we obtain

\displaystyle\chi=\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\left[\frac{\langle n^{2}\rangle}{\beta}+\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}\beta}\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau\,\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau^{\prime}\left(\frac{1}{I}\,\delta(\tau-\tau^{\prime})-\frac{1}{\beta I}\right)\right].

(34)

Eq. (
34
) makes explicit that the Gaussian fluctuation contribution vanishes identically for any finite
\beta
, due to an exact cancellation between the local
\delta
-function term and the
\beta
-dependent boundary contribution imposed by Dirichlet conditions.
The nonzero result reported in ref.
5
for a fixed topological sector stems from interchanging the limit
\beta\to\infty
with the temporal integrations which is not valid as the integrand contains a
\beta
-dependent constant whose integral exactly cancels the
\delta
-function contribution.

Moreover, eq. (
34
) makes the validity of our argument explicit, as the susceptibility is evaluated without assuming time-translation invariance. In this formulation, the Gaussian contribution vanishes regardless of the order in which infinite-time limit and temporal integrations are performed. A nonzero susceptibility emerges only upon summing over all winding sectors, demonstrating that the result is a genuine consequence of the full topological sum rather than an artifact of integration order or symmetry assumptions.

Method B: Euclidean correlation function of momentum operator

The derivative of the partition function
Z
given in eq. (
23
) can be written as the correlation function of the momentum operators.
Promoting
\theta
to a
\tau
-dependent source
\theta(\tau)
, functional derivatives of
\log Z
generate correlation functions of the momentum operator.
Then, eq. (
24
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\chi=\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\frac{-1}{\beta}\frac{1}{Z(0,\beta)}\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau^{\prime}\frac{\delta}{\delta\theta(\tau)}\frac{\delta}{\delta\theta(\tau^{\prime})}Z\biggr|_{\theta(\tau)=0}

(35)

and we find,

\displaystyle\frac{\delta}{\delta\theta(\tau)}\frac{\delta}{\delta\theta(0)}\log Z\biggr|_{\theta(\tau)=0}

\displaystyle=\left[-\left(\frac{1}{Z}\frac{\delta Z}{\delta\theta(0)}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{Z}\frac{\delta}{\delta\theta(\tau)}\frac{\delta}{\delta\theta(0)}Z\right]_{\theta(\tau)=0}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}I^{2}}\langle 0|p|0\rangle^{2}-\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}I}\delta(\tau)+\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}I^{2}}\langle 0|Tp(\tau)p(0)|0\rangle,

(36)

where
p(\tau)=e^{H\tau}p(0)e^{-H\tau}
and
|0\rangle
denotes the ground state. Using the same prescription for extracting
\chi
as in the previous subsection, we integrate over
\tau
to obtain

\displaystyle\chi\big|_{\theta=0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}I}-\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}I^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\tau\left[\langle 0|Tp(\tau)p(0)|0\rangle-\langle 0|p|0\rangle^{2}\right].

(37)

Using the spectral representation of the Euclidean correlator,

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\tau\left[\langle 0|Tp(\tau)p(0)|0\rangle-\langle 0|p|0\rangle^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tau\left[\sum_{n}\langle 0|p(\tau)|n\rangle\langle n|p(0)|0\rangle-\langle 0|p|0\rangle^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tau\sum_{n\neq 0}\left[|\langle n|p(0)|0\rangle|^{2}\exp\left(-(E_{n}-E_{0})\tau\right)\right]

\displaystyle=2\sum_{n\neq 0}\frac{1}{E_{n}-E_{0}}|\langle n|p(0)|0\rangle|^{2}.

(38)

we recover

\displaystyle\chi\big|_{\theta=0}=\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}I}-\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}I^{2}}\sum_{n\neq 0}\frac{|\langle n|p|0\rangle|^{2}}{E_{n}(0)-E_{0}(0)}.

(39)

Since the ground state of the quantum rotor satisfies
p|0\rangle=0
, we find that this result in exact agreement with Method A.

Method C: Ground-state energy

In the above two method, we have calculated
\chi
from the Euclidean path integral formulation. We can also derive the equivalent results from
\theta
-dependence of the spectrum of the system.
For fixed
\theta
, the system has a discrete spectrum
E_{n}(\theta)
.
In large
\beta
limit,
\log Z(\theta,\beta)
behaves as
-\beta E_{0}(\theta)
.
Using the definition of the susceptibility already introduced in
eq. (
23
), we may write

\displaystyle\chi\big|_{\theta=0}=\left.\frac{\partial^{2}E_{0}(\theta)}{\partial\theta^{2}}\right|_{\theta=0}.

(40)

For small
\theta
,
E_{0}(\theta)
can be calculated by using perturbative expansion as

\displaystyle E_{0}(\theta)=E_{0}(0)+\frac{\theta}{2I\pi}\langle 0|p|0\rangle+\theta^{2}\left[\frac{1}{8I\pi^{2}}-\frac{1}{4I^{2}\pi^{2}}\sum_{n\neq 0}\frac{|\langle n|p|0\rangle|^{2}}{E_{n}(0)-E_{0}(0)}\right]+\cdots,

(41)

Taking two derivatives with respect to
\theta
and evaluating the result at
\theta=0
, we obtain

\displaystyle\chi|_{\theta=0}=\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}I}-\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}I^{2}}\sum_{n\neq 0}\frac{|\langle n|p|0\rangle|^{2}}{E_{n}(0)-E_{0}(0)}.

(42)

We obtained the result equivalent to eq. (
39
).
Again, by using
p|0\rangle=0
, we obtain
\chi=1/4\pi^{2}I
.

Comments on the ACGT Order-of-Limits

We have demonstrated that all three approaches yield the same result for the topological susceptibility,
\chi=1/4\pi^{2}I
. This result, together with its interpretation, is in full agreement with the findings of Albandea
et al.

6
and Benabou
et al.

11
. Now let us discuss the implications on ACGT proposal on the order of the limits in the path integral formulation and the discussion on the topological susceptibility in ref.
5
.

First, we introduce a technical device that allows us to address the order-of-limits issue in a direct and transparent way and to pinpoint the origin of the nonvanishing susceptibility in the full path-integral formulation. Specifically, we impose a finite cutoff
\Delta
on the winding number. This cutoff allows us to cleanly distinguish between the order in which the summation over all winding sectors
(\Delta\to\infty)
and the infinite-time limit
(\beta\to\infty)
are taken. The same strategy was employed in the analysis of the propagator (section
2.2
) and of the partition function and energy spectrum (section
2.3
), and will be used again in the next section for the quantum pendulum. We emphasize that truncating the winding sum is purely a technical device, introduced solely to test and clarify the order-of-limits prescription proposed by ACGT.

We now return to eq. (
34
), where the nonzero contribution to
\chi
appears to arise from winding configurations. Introducing the finite cutoff
\Delta
, eq. (
34
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\chi(\Delta)=\lim_{\Delta\to\infty}\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\frac{1}{\beta}\,\frac{\sum_{|n|\leq\Delta}n^{2}\exp\!\left(-\frac{2\pi^{2}I}{\beta}n^{2}\right)}{\sum_{|n|\leq\Delta}\exp\!\left(-\frac{2\pi^{2}I}{\beta}n^{2}\right)},

(43)

Here
\Delta
denotes the maximal winding number allowed to contribute to the path integral. For any fixed, finite
\Delta
, the explicit
1/\beta
prefactor suppresses the result as
\beta\to\infty
, and
\chi(\Delta)=0
. In contrast, performing the winding sum first yields

\chi=\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\lim_{\Delta\to\infty}\chi(\Delta)=\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}I},

(44)

This result is in full agreement with standard quantum-mechanical expectations, lattice simulations, and ref.
11
.
In particular, as shown in method C, the ground-state energy of the quantum rotor in the presence of a
\theta
term is
E_{0}(\theta)=\frac{1}{2I}\left(\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\right)^{2}
, which in turn implies a nonvanishing topological susceptibility,
\chi=\left.\partial_{\theta}^{2}E_{0}\right|_{\theta=0}=1/(4\pi^{2}I)
.
The conclusion is therefore unambiguous: if the limit
\beta\to\infty
is taken at fixed winding number (or within any finite subset of winding sectors), the susceptibility vanishes, whereas a nonzero topological susceptibility arises only when the sum over all winding sectors is performed prior to the infinite-time limit. This feature lies at the core of the Euclidean path-integral formulation and reflects the necessity of including all allowed paths in the functional integral.

Second, our method B is equivalent to the calculation of
\chi
in eq. (4) of Ai
et al.

5
.
For the quantum rotor,
\langle 0|p|0\rangle=0
and
\langle 0|Tp(\tau)p(0)|0\rangle=0
are satisfied because of
p|0\rangle=0
. Thus, eq. (
36
) tells us that

\displaystyle\frac{\delta}{\delta\theta(\tau)}\frac{\delta}{\delta\theta(0)}\log Z

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}I^{2}}\delta(\tau),

(45)

and we obtain

\displaystyle\chi=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\tau\frac{\delta}{\delta\theta(\tau)}\frac{\delta}{\delta\theta(0)}\log Z=\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}I}.

(46)

Although we have explicitly verified the correctness of the calculation of
\chi
presented in ref.
5
, this agreement at the level of the final result does not, by itself, justify the ACGT order-of-limits prescription. The reason is that the order of the limits in the path integral does not appear directly in ref.
5
calculation.
The crucial point is that both our analysis in Method B and that of ref.
5
rely on an implicit assumption about the ground state, namely
p|0\rangle=0
, which is sufficient to obtain
\chi=1/(4\pi^{2}I)
, independently of any discussion of the path integral. In particular the condition
p|0\rangle=0
immediately implies
\exp\left(2\pi ip\right)|0\rangle=|0\rangle
. Since
p
is the generator of translations, the operator
e^{2\pi ip}
implements a
2\pi
shift of the angular variable, corresponding to a large gauge transformation in the quantum rotor. Thus, assuming
p|0\rangle=0
is equivalent to selecting a large-gauge-invariant ground state. If such a gauge-invariant state is imposed as a boundary condition in the path-integral formulation, the functional integral necessarily includes a sum over all topological sectors. Consequently, the calculation of the topological susceptibility in ref.
5
, while correct, is not equivalent to the path-integral prescription proposed by ACGT refs.
2
;
4
;
3
;
5
. The latter requires a gauge-variant boundary condition at finite spacetime volume, with gauge invariance recovered only after taking the
VT\to\infty
limit.

Third, we comment on the order of limit
\beta\to\infty
and the temporal integration. Ref.
5
claimed that
\chi=1/(4\pi^{2}I)
can be obtained even within a fixed topological sector; however, this derivation relies on interchanging the
\beta\to\infty
limit with the temporal integration as

\displaystyle\chi=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\tau\left(\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\frac{d}{d\tau}\frac{d}{d\tau^{\prime}}G(\tau,\tau^{\prime};\beta)\right),

(47)

which is not justified as the integrand contains a
\beta
-dependent constant whose integral exactly cancels the
\delta
-function contribution.
As shown explicitly in eq. (
34
) in the method A, the fluctuation contribution to
\chi
vanishes identically for any finite
\beta
, leaving only the winding-sector term
\langle n^{2}\rangle/\beta
, which goes to zero as
\beta\to\infty
for any fixed winding number.
A nonzero susceptibility arises only when the sum over all winding sectors is performed
before
the infinite-time limit. In that case,
\langle n^{2}\rangle\sim\beta
at large
\beta
, yielding
\chi=1/(4\pi^{2}I)
.
Thus, the nonvanishing topological susceptibility is a global property of the theory, stemming from the unrestricted sum over topological sectors, and cannot be attributed to a contact term within an individual sector.

Finally, we emphasize that nonzero
\chi
directly means that
\theta
is a physical parameter because it affects
E_{0}(\theta)
, which is a physical observable. As illustrated in the appendix
A
, nonzero
\theta
on a ring can be understood as the Aharonov-Bohm effect due to a magnetic flux inside the ring. Also, for small
\theta
, we can show that
\langle 0|p|0\rangle\simeq\theta\chi
, which is analogous to
\langle(g_{s}^{2}/32\pi^{2})G_{\mu\nu}\tilde{G}^{\mu\nu}\rangle\simeq\theta\chi_{t}
in QCD
50
.

3
A ring with potential: quantum pendulum

In the previous section, we have discussed quantum rotor system which is equivalent to a particle on a ring without potential.
In this section, we discuss the system with nonzero potential. This potential can be either electric potential or gravitational potential. A particle on a ring with gravitational potential can be interpreted as a quantum pendulum, which has been discussed in the literature such as refs.
46
;
9
.

Now the Schrödinger equation of this system is given as

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2I}\frac{\partial^{2}\psi}{\partial\phi^{2}}+V(\phi)\psi(\phi)=E\psi(\phi)

(48)

with the twisted boundary condition for
\psi
:

\displaystyle\psi(\phi+2\pi)=e^{i\theta}\psi(\phi).

(49)

Without loss of generality, we can assume
V(\phi)
has the global minimum at
\phi=0
and
V(\phi)
around the minimum can be expanded as

\displaystyle V(\phi)\simeq\frac{1}{2}I\omega^{2}\phi^{2}.

(50)

The ground state wave function is localized around
\phi\simeq 2\pi n
with integer
n
, and the wave function should satisfy the boundary condition eq. (
49
) at the same time. Thus, the construction of the ground state in this system is quite similar to
\theta
-vacua in gauge theories. See, e.g.,
46
.

3.1
The partition function

As section
2
, we calculate the partition function
Z
.
The partition function
Z
is defined as similar to eq. (
16
), and we obtain

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}e^{-E_{n}\beta}.

(51)

Let us calculate
Z
by using two different methods.

Method A: Schrödinger equation with WKB approximation

First, let us solve Schrödinger equation (
48
) directly.
Since the ground state gives the dominant contribution for large
\beta
as
Z\simeq\exp(-E_0 \beta),
we calculate the energy of the ground state
E_{0}
by utilizing the WKB approximation. To this end, it is useful to define the following dimensionless variables:

\displaystyle z\equiv\sqrt{I\omega}\phi,\qquad v(z)\equiv\frac{V(\sqrt{1/I\omega}z)}{\omega},\qquad\nu\equiv\frac{E}{\omega}-1/2.

(52)

v(z)
is a periodic function as
v(z)=v(z+p)
where the period
p
is

\displaystyle p=2\pi\sqrt{I\omega}.

(53)

Then, we obtain the reduced Schrödinger equation and boundary condition as

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\psi^{\prime\prime}(z)+v(z)\psi(z)=\left(\frac{1}{2}+\nu\right)\psi(z),\qquad\psi(z+p)=e^{i\theta}\psi(z).

(54)

The reduced potential
v(z)
has minima at
z=np
with integer
n
, corresponding to a periodic array of identical wells. We assume that the potential is sufficiently deep and smooth so that, in the vicinity of each minimum, it may be approximated by a harmonic oscillator. More precisely, there exists a small parameter
\epsilon
such that

v(z)\simeq\frac{(z-np)^{2}}{2},\qquad np-\epsilon<z<np+\epsilon,

(55)

while in the region
np+\epsilon\leq z\leq(n+1)p-\epsilon
the potential is large enough to justify the WKB approximation. In this regime, the low-energy states are localized near individual minima, and for the ground state the parameter
\nu
appearing in the parabolic cylinder functions is expected to satisfy
\nu\simeq 0
. Without loss of generality, we focus on the minimum at
z=0
.

Hence, in the region
-\epsilon<z<\epsilon
, the wavefunction can be written as a linear combination of parabolic cylinder functions,

\psi(z)\equiv\xi\,D_{\nu}(-\sqrt{2}\,z)+(1-\xi)\,D_{\nu}(\sqrt{2}\,z),

(56)

where
\xi
is a coefficient to be fixed by the boundary condition. For
\nu\simeq 0
, the asymptotic behavior of (
56
) is

\displaystyle\psi(z)

\displaystyle\to e^{-z^{2}/2}+(1-\xi)\frac{\nu\sqrt{\pi}}{z}e^{z^{2}/2},

\displaystyle(z\to-\infty)

(57)

\displaystyle\psi(z)

\displaystyle\to e^{-z^{2}/2}-\xi\frac{\nu\sqrt{\pi}}{z}e^{z^{2}/2}.

\displaystyle(z\to+\infty)

(58)

Using (
57
), the wavefunction in the region
-p+\epsilon\leq z\leq-\epsilon
can be matched to
\psi(-\epsilon)
via the WKB approximation,

\displaystyle\psi(z)\simeq\left(\frac{v(z)-1/2}{v(-\epsilon)-1/2}\right)^{1/4}\Biggl[

\displaystyle e^{-\epsilon^{2}/2}\exp\left(\int_{-\epsilon}^{z}dz^{\prime}\sqrt{2v(z^{\prime})-1}\right)

\displaystyle+(1-\xi)\frac{\nu\sqrt{\pi}}{-\epsilon}e^{\epsilon^{2}/2}\exp\left(-\int_{-\epsilon}^{z}dz^{\prime}\sqrt{2v(z^{\prime})-1}\right)\Biggr].

(59)

Similarly, using (
58
), the wavefunction in the region
\epsilon\leq z\leq p-\epsilon
is

\displaystyle\psi(z)\simeq\left(\frac{v(z)-1/2}{v(\epsilon)-1/2}\right)^{1/4}\Biggl[

\displaystyle e^{-\epsilon^{2}/2}\exp\left(-\int_{\epsilon}^{z}dz^{\prime}\sqrt{2v(z^{\prime})-1}\right)

\displaystyle-\xi\frac{\nu\sqrt{\pi}}{\epsilon}e^{\epsilon^{2}/2}\exp\left(+\int_{\epsilon}^{z}dz^{\prime}\sqrt{2v(z^{\prime})-1}\right)\Biggr].

(60)

To determine the tunneling-induced energy splitting, we impose the twisted boundary condition,
\psi(z+p)=e^{i\theta}\psi(z)
. For this purpose, it is convenient to rewrite (
60
) as

\displaystyle\psi(z)\simeq\left(\frac{v(z)-1/2}{v(\epsilon)-1/2}\right)^{1/4}\Biggl[

\displaystyle ke^{-S}\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{\epsilon}e^{\epsilon^{2}/2}\exp\left(-\int_{-\epsilon+p}^{z}dz^{\prime}\sqrt{2v(z^{\prime})-1}\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{\xi\nu}{ke^{-S}}e^{-\epsilon^{2}/2}\exp\left(+\int_{-\epsilon+p}^{z}dz^{\prime}\sqrt{2v(z^{\prime})-1}\right)\Biggr],

(61)

where
S
and
k
are defined as

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{0}^{p}dz^{\prime}\sqrt{2v(z^{\prime})},

(62)

\displaystyle k

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{\pi}}\exp\left(-\epsilon^{2}+\int_{0}^{p}dz^{\prime}\sqrt{2v(z^{\prime})}-\int_{\epsilon}^{-\epsilon+p}dz^{\prime}\sqrt{2v(z^{\prime})-1}\right).

(63)

Solving the twisted boundary condition using (
59
) and (
61
), we obtain

\nu=-2ke^{-S}\cos\theta,\qquad\xi=\frac{e^{i\theta}}{2\cos\theta}.

(64)

Defining
K\equiv k\omega
, the ground-state energy takes the form

E_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\omega-2Ke^{-S}\cos\theta.

(65)

This expression explicitly exhibits the tunneling-induced band structure. As we will show in the next subsection, the quantities
S
and
K
defined in
eqs. (
62
) and (
63
)
coincide with the bounce action and the associated functional determinant
computed in the semiclassical path-integral formalism.

Using the ground-state energy defined as eq. (
65
), the partition function
Z
for large
T
behaves as

\displaystyle Z\simeq\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\omega\beta+2K\beta e^{-S}\cos\theta\right).

(66)

where the
\theta
-dependence originates from tunneling between adjacent minima. Moreover, the topological susceptibility
\chi
is calculated as

\displaystyle\chi=-\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\frac{1}{\beta}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta^{2}}\log Z=\frac{\partial^{2}E_{0}}{\partial\theta^{2}}=2Ke^{-S}\cos\theta.

(67)

which shows that the susceptibility is entirely controlled by the tunneling amplitude and is exponentially suppressed in the semiclassical limit.

Method B: Path integral with dilute instanton gas approximation

We now calculate the partition function
Z(\theta,\beta)
from the Euclidean path integral, which provides a complementary perspective to the Schrödinger approach. For a finite Euclidean time interval
\beta
, the action is

\displaystyle S_{E}[\phi]=\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau\,\Bigg[\frac{1}{2}I\left(\frac{d\phi}{d\tau}\right)^{2}+V(\phi(\tau))+\frac{i\theta}{2\pi}\frac{d\phi}{d\tau}\Bigg].

(68)

For large
\beta
, the path integral is dominated by configurations around classical solutions, i.e., saddle points of
S_{E}
. One such solution is the
bounce
(instanton)
\phi_{B}(\tau)
, which satisfies the classical equation of motion and interpolates between physically equivalent vacua:

\displaystyle I\frac{d^{2}\phi_{B}}{d\tau^{2}}=V^{\prime}(\phi_{B}(\tau)),\qquad\lim_{\tau\to-\infty}\phi_{B}(\tau)=0,\quad\lim_{\tau\to+\infty}\phi_{B}(\tau)=2\pi.

(69)

with
\phi=0
and
\phi=2\pi
identified. Using the dilute instanton gas approximation
19
, we can sum over configurations with arbitrary numbers of instantons (
n_{+}
) and anti-instantons (
n_{-}
) to obtain

\displaystyle Z(\theta,\beta)=\int_{\phi(0)=0}^{\phi(\beta)=0}\mathcal{D}\phi\,e^{-S_{E}[\phi]}

\displaystyle=e^{-\omega\beta/2}\sum_{n_{+},n_{-}=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n_{+}!\,n_{-}!}\left(K_{B}\beta\,e^{-S_{B}}\right)^{n_{+}+n_{-}}e^{in_{+}\theta}e^{-in_{-}\theta}

\displaystyle=\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\omega\beta+2K_{B}\beta\,e^{-S_{B}}\cos\theta\right].

(70)

where the summation goes over all possible winding configurations. Here,
S_{B}
is the bounce action, and
K_{B}
arises from the functional determinant associated with small fluctuations around the instanton:

\displaystyle S_{B}

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi\sqrt{2IV(\phi)},

(71)

\displaystyle K_{B}

\displaystyle\equiv\sqrt{\frac{S_{B}}{2\pi}}\left|\frac{\det(-\partial_t^2 + \omega^2)}{\det^{\prime}(-\partial^{2}+V^{\prime\prime}(\phi_{B})/I)}\right|^{1/2}.

(72)

where
\det^{\prime}
indicates that the zero mode is omitted in the determinant.

We now show that
S_{B}
and
K_{B}
correspond precisely to
S
and
K=k\omega
defined in the semiclassical Schrödinger analysis (eqs. (
62
) and (
63
)).
Clearly,
S_{B}
is equivalent to
S
up to a rescaling of variables. For
K_{B}
, the determinant can be expressed as
28
;
19

\displaystyle K_{B}=\sqrt{\frac{I\omega}{\pi}}A,

(73)

You can refer to appendix
B
for a detailed calculation of
K_{B}
. Here,
A
is a constant related to the behavior of
\phi_{B}(\tau)
as discussed in
28
;
19
and characterizes the asymptotic decay of the instanton:

\displaystyle\frac{d\phi_{B}}{d\tau}\to Ae^{-\omega|\tau|}.\qquad(\tau\to\pm\infty)

(74)

On the other hand, the Schrödinger-based quantity
K
, eq. (
63
), can be rewritten as

\displaystyle K=k\omega

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{\epsilon\omega}{\sqrt{\pi}}\exp\left(\int_{\epsilon}^{p/2}dz^{\prime}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2v(z^{\prime})}}\right).

(75)

Introducing the inverse of the bounce,
\tau_{B}(\phi)
such that
\tau_{B}(\phi_{B}(\tau))=\tau
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\epsilon}^{p/2}dz^{\prime}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2v(z^{\prime})}}~=~\omega\left[\tau_{B}\left(\sqrt{\frac{1}{I\omega}}\frac{p}{2}\right)-\tau_{B}\left(\sqrt{\frac{1}{I\omega}}\epsilon\right)\right]~\simeq~\frac{A}{\epsilon}\log\sqrt{I\omega}.

(76)

which reproduces the same prefactor as
K=\sqrt{\frac{I\omega}{\pi}}A=K_{B}
.
Thus we have shown that this
K_{B}
is equivalent to
K
in eq. (
63
).

Finally, the ground-state energy and partition function derived from the path integral are

\displaystyle E_{0}=-\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\frac{1}{\beta}\log Z(\theta,\beta)=\frac{1}{2}\omega-2K_{B}e^{-S_{B}}\cos\theta.

(77)

which is fully consistent with the result obtained from the semiclassical Schrödinger method (eq. (
65
)). Similarly, the topological susceptibility
\chi
obtained from this path integral agrees with eq. (
67
).

3.2
The partition function and topological susceptibility by ACGT method

It is important to emphasize that the ground-state energy
E_{0}
in eq. (
77
) is obtained because all possible instanton and anti-instanton configurations have been summed in
Z
(eq. (
70
)). In contrast, the approach of refs.
2
;
4
;
3
;
5
considers the limit
\beta\to\infty
before summing over all winding numbers. This subtle difference leads to different conclusions for the ground-state energy and topological susceptibility.

The partition function in eq. (
70
) can be decomposed into contributions with finite winding number
\Delta
:

\displaystyle Z(\theta,\beta)=\sum_{\Delta=-\infty}^{\infty}Z_{\Delta}(\beta)e^{i\Delta\theta},

(78)

where
Z_{\Delta}(\beta)
is the contribution from paths with winding number
\Delta
. Using the series representation of the modified Bessel function of the first kind,

I_{n}(x)=\sum_{m}\frac{(2x)^{n+2m}}{[m!(m+n)!]},

(79)

one finds
2
;
3
;
33

\displaystyle Z_{\Delta}(\beta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-\omega\beta/2}}{|\Delta|!(|\Delta|+m)!}\left(K\beta e^{-S}\right)^{|\Delta|+2m}=e^{-\omega\beta/2}I_{|\Delta|}\left(2K\beta e^{-S}\right).

(80)

We define a truncated partition function including only winding numbers
|\Delta^{\prime}|\leq\Delta
:

\displaystyle Z(\theta,\beta;\Delta)\equiv\sum_{\Delta^{\prime}=-\Delta}^{\Delta}Z_{\Delta^{\prime}}(\beta)e^{i\Delta^{\prime}\theta}.

(81)

The full partition function is recovered as
\lim_{\Delta\to\infty}Z(\theta,\beta;\Delta)
. The ACGT proposal corresponds to first taking the limit
\beta\to\infty
with fixed
\Delta
before letting
\Delta\to\infty
.

From eq. (
78
), each
Z_{\Delta}(\beta)
can be expressed as

\displaystyle Z_{\Delta}(\beta)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\theta\,e^{-i\Delta\theta}Z(\theta,\beta).

(82)

This allows rewriting the truncated partition function as

\displaystyle Z(\theta,\beta;\Delta)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\theta^{\prime}\,f(\theta^{\prime};\theta,\Delta)\,Z(\theta^{\prime},\beta),

(83)

where

\displaystyle f(\theta^{\prime};\theta,\Delta)\equiv\sum_{\Delta^{\prime}=-\Delta}^{\Delta}e^{i\Delta^{\prime}(\theta-\theta^{\prime})}.

(84)

Applying the dilute gas instanton approximation to eq. (
83
), we obtain

\displaystyle Z(\theta,\beta;\Delta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\theta^{\prime}\,f(\theta^{\prime};\theta,\Delta)\,\exp[-\tfrac{1}{2}\omega\beta+2K\beta e^{-S}\cos\theta^{\prime}\Big].

(85)

For large
\beta
with finite
\Delta
, the integral is dominated by
\theta^{\prime}\simeq 0
. Expanding around this saddle point gives

\displaystyle Z(\theta,\beta;\Delta)

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{f(0;\theta,\Delta)}{2\pi}\int d\theta^{\prime}\,\exp[-\tfrac{1}{2}\omega\beta+2K\beta e^{-S}\big(1-\tfrac{1}{2}\theta^{\prime 2}\big)+\frac{f^{\prime}(0;\theta,\Delta)}{f(0;\theta,\Delta)}\theta^{\prime}\Big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{K\beta e^{-S}}}\exp[-\tfrac{1}{2}\omega\beta+2K\beta e^{-S}+\frac{(f^{\prime}(0;\theta,\Delta)/f(0;\theta,\Delta))^{2}}{4K\beta e^{-S}}\Big].

(86)

We observe that the functional form of the prefactor
\sqrt{\pi/KTe^{-S}}
closely resembles the result presented in ref.
33
. From the above, the effective large-
\beta
behavior of
-(1/\beta)\log Z
becomes

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\beta}\log Z\;\to\;\frac{1}{2}\omega-2Ke^{-S}-\frac{1}{2\beta}\log\frac{\pi}{K\beta e^{-S}}.

(87)

The last term vanishes slowly as
\beta\to\infty
but introduces a
\log\beta
correction, which prevents a well-defined extraction of the ground-state energy. Moreover, the
\theta
-dependence disappears, leading to a vanishing topological susceptibility in this procedure.

Let us discuss the result in eq. (
87
). First, we note that this result does not agree with eq. (
65
). This suggests that the ACGT procedure does not correctly reproduce the quantum mechanics of a particle on a ring with a potential in the presence of a nonzero
\theta
parameter.
Second, the physical interpretation of eq. (
83
) appears to be subtle. The first two terms on the right-hand side in eq. (
87
) coincide with the ground state energy at
\theta=0
. Technically, this behavior can be traced to the fact that, for finite
\Delta
,
Z(\theta,T;\Delta)
can be written in the form of eq. (
83
), and that
Z(\theta^{\prime},\beta)
with
\theta^{\prime}
close to zero provides the dominant contribution in the large
T
limit. However, eq. (
83
) represents a sum over partition functions
Z(\theta,\beta)
with different values of
\theta
. Since each value of
\theta
corresponds to a distinct superselection sector, it is not obvious how to assign a direct physical meaning to such a summation.
Furthermore, as already pointed out in ref.
33
, the expression in eq. (
87
) contains a
\log\beta
term, which prevents taking a well-defined limit
\beta\to\infty
. The appearance of this logarithmic term can be understood from the fact that the
\theta
states form a continuous spectrum, so that states with
\theta
near zero contribute dominantly to the partition function
Z
.

4
Order of Limits, Topological Sectors, and
\theta
-vacua

In sections
2
and
3
, we studied simple examples of one-dimensional quantum mechanics and showed that summation over all possible configurations is required to obtain consistent results for a finite time interval
T
. We also found that imposing a restriction on the winding number leads to a mixture of different
\theta
states. Note that a similar argument has been done in QCD
14
.
Since only the lowest-energy state contributes to the partition function in the limit
T\to\infty
, the two limits
T\to\infty
and
\Delta\to\infty
do not commute.

These observations are useful for understanding the arguments presented in the ACGT papers
2
;
4
;
3
;
5
.
Let us denote the state localized at
\phi\simeq 2\pi n
by
|2\pi n\rangle
and the shift transformation
\phi\to\phi+2\pi
by
G
, which is equivalent to
\exp(2\pi i p)
used in section
2.4
.
The states
|2\pi n\rangle
, the transformation
G
, and the instantons discussed in section
3
correspond to the
n
vacua, large gauge transformations, and instantons in gauge theory, respectively.
The transformation generated by
G
is a gauge transformation in this system, because the physical region of
\phi
is
[0,2\pi)
and extending
\phi
to
\mathbb{R}
merely introduces a redundancy in the description of the physics.
The operator
G
commutes with the Hamiltonian
H
, and the Hilbert space decomposes into subspaces in which
G
has eigenvalue
e^{i\theta}
.
A superselection rule applies in each sector, and one must choose a single such subspace.
The states
|2\pi n\rangle
themselves are not invariant under
G
, since

\displaystyle G|2\pi n\rangle=|2\pi(n+1)\rangle.

(88)

Instead, one can construct “
\theta
states,” which satisfy
G|\theta\rangle=e^{i\theta}|\theta\rangle
, as

\displaystyle|\theta\rangle\equiv\sum_{n}e^{in\theta}|2\pi n\rangle.

(89)

This state has the lowest energy within the Hilbert subspace characterized by
G=e^{i\theta}
, and this construction is consistent with the twisted boundary condition given in eq. (
49
).
We have seen that, in order to define the partition function for a given sector, one must sum over all topological sectors
before
taking the limit
T\to\infty
.
The construction of
\theta
vacua in gauge theory proceeds in a parallel manner.
If, instead, one performs the sum over topological sectors
after
taking the limit
T\to\infty
, one is led to consider the partition function
Z
with finite
\Delta
, as discussed in sections
2
and
3
.
For finite
\Delta
, the partition function
Z
can be expressed as a linear combination of partition functions associated with different values of
\theta
, even though these sectors cannot communicate due to the superselection rule.
In the limit
VT\to\infty
, the dominant contribution arises from the
\theta
vacuum with
\theta\simeq 0
.
The prefactor
(VT)^{-1/2}
found in ref.
33
can also be understood as a consequence of the fact that the
\theta
vacua form a continuous spectrum.

The conventional formulation of the path integral for a finite time interval
T
is obtained by inserting a large number of completeness relations,

\displaystyle\langle\phi_{f}|e^{-HT}|\phi_{i}\rangle

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\to\infty}\left(\prod_{k^{\prime}=1}^{N-1}\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi_{k^{\prime}}\right)\left(\prod_{k^{\prime\prime}=1}^{N}\sum_{p_{k^{\prime\prime}}=-\infty}^{\infty}\right)\prod_{k=1}^{N}\langle\phi_{k}|e^{-HTN}|p_{k}\rangle\langle p_{k}|\phi_{k-1}\rangle,

(90)

where we identify
\phi_{0}=\phi_{i}
and
\phi_{N}=\phi_{f}
.
After integrating over the momentum variables
p_{k^{\prime\prime}}
, we obtain the path-integral expression, such as eq. (
70
).
This formulation shows that one must sum over
all
possible paths
\phi(\tau)
satisfying
\phi(0)=\phi_{i}
and
\phi(T)=\phi_{f}
.
This implies that, once
\phi=0
and
\phi=2\pi
are identified as physically equivalent points, one must sum over
all
topological sectors.
In sections
2
and
3
, we explicitly demonstrated that consistent results for quantum mechanics on a ring are obtained only by summing over all topological sectors.
The correct large
T
behavior is therefore obtained by consistently calculating at finite
T
and then taking the limit
T\to\infty
.

5
Conclusion

In this work we critically examined the proposal of Ai, Cruz, Garbrecht, and Tamarit that the
\theta
dependence of gauge theories can be eliminated by a particular order-of-limits prescription in the Euclidean path integral. Using fully controlled examples in one-dimensional quantum mechanics, namely the quantum rotor and the quantum pendulum, we employed canonical quantization as a benchmark, whose results are exact and well established, and contrasted them with the path-integral formulation, which allows one to explicitly distinguish the order in which limits are taken. This framework enabled us to directly test the ACGT prescription and show that it does not reproduce the quantum mechanical observables correctly.

For the quantum rotor, we showed that canonical quantization and the Euclidean path-integral formulation all yield the same result for the ground-state energy and the topological susceptibility,
\chi=1/(4\pi^{2}I)
. This nonvanishing susceptibility follows directly from the
\theta
dependence of the ground-state energy. To probe the ACGT order-of-limits prescription, we introduced a finite cutoff on the winding number as it makes the order of performing the summation over topological sectors and the infinite-time limit, explicit. We found that taking the limit
\beta\to\infty
at fixed winding number (or within any finite subset of winding sectors) forces the susceptibility to vanish, whereas performing the sum over all winding sectors prior to the infinite-time limit reproduces the exact quantum-mechanical result. The two limits do not commute, and the physically correct order is uniquely fixed by consistency with the exact spectrum.

We also clarified that the calculation of the susceptibility in ref.
5
, while correct at the level of the final result, implicitly assumes a large-gauge-invariant ground state. This assumption already enforces a sum over all topological sectors and is sufficient to determine the
\theta
dependence of the ground-state energy independently of any path-integral considerations. Consequently, that calculation cannot be used to justify the ACGT path-integral prescription, which instead corresponds to imposing gauge-variant boundary conditions at finite spacetime volume and restoring gauge invariance only after taking the infinite-volume limit. Finally, we showed that attempts to obtain a nonzero susceptibility within a fixed topological sector rely on an unjustified interchange of the infinite-time limit with the temporal integration; when treated correctly, such contributions vanish identically. The nonzero susceptibility of the quantum rotor is therefore a genuinely global effect, originating from the unrestricted sum over winding sectors.

The quantum pendulum provides a complementary test of the ACGT prescription. In this system, the ground-state energy and its
\theta
dependence arise from tunneling between distinct minima of the potential and are correctly reproduced only when
all
instanton and anti-instanton configurations are included in the path integral. Implementing the ACGT order of limits leads to a truncated partition function that effectively mixes different
\theta
superselection sectors. This procedure yields an expression dominated by contributions from
\theta^{\prime}\simeq 0
, eliminates the physical
\theta
dependence, and introduces a logarithmic dependence on
\beta
that obstructs the extraction of a well-defined ground-state energy. The resulting expression disagrees with both the exact solution of the Schrödinger equation and the standard path-integral result.

The failure can be traced to the fact that truncating the winding sum amounts to averaging over distinct quantum theories labeled by
\theta
, which have no direct physical meaning when combined. As a result, the ACGT prescription does not describe the quantum mechanics of a particle on a ring with a potential in the presence of a nonzero
\theta
parameter. The quantum pendulum thus makes explicit that the order-of-limits issue is not a technical subtlety but leads to a qualitatively incorrect theory.

Appendix A
Aharonov-Bohm effect for a charged particle on a ring

The Lagrangian of a particle with mass
m
and charge
e
is written as

\displaystyle L=\frac{1}{2}m(\dot{x}^{2}+\dot{y}^{2}+\dot{z}^{2})-eV(\vec{x})+e[A_{x}(\vec{x})\dot{x}+A_{y}(\vec{x})\dot{y}+A_{z}(\vec{x})\dot{z}],

(91)

where
V(\vec{x})
and
\vec{A}(\vec{x})
are the electromagnetic scalar and vector potential.
If a particle is constrained to move on a ring with radius
R
as
(x,y,z)=(R\cos\phi,R\sin\phi,0)
, then, the Lagrangian becomes

\displaystyle L=\frac{1}{2}mR^{2}\dot{\phi}^{2}-eV(\phi)+eRA_{\phi}(\phi)\dot{\phi},

(92)

where
V(\phi)
and
A_{\phi}(\phi)\equiv-A_{x}\sin\phi+A_{y}\cos\phi
are the scalar and vector potential on the ring.
Let us define
\Phi
as the magnetic flux inside the ring with radius
R
. Then, by choosing an appropriate gauge, we can take
A_{\phi}
as a
\phi
-independent constant as
A_{\phi}=\Phi/2\pi R
. Then, by defining the moment of inertia
I=mR^{2}
, we can write the Lagrangian as

\displaystyle L=\frac{1}{2}I\dot{\phi}^{2}-eV(\phi)+\frac{e\Phi}{2\pi}\dot{\phi}.

(93)

Then, we obtain the Hamiltonian as
H=(1/2I)\left(p_{\phi}-e\Phi/2\pi\right)^{2}+eV(\phi)
and the Schrödinger equation becomes,

\displaystyle\left[\frac{1}{2I}\left(-i\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}-\frac{e\Phi}{2\pi}\right)^{2}+eV(\phi)\right]\psi(\phi)=E\psi(\phi).

(94)

The wave function
\psi(\phi)
can be understood as a part of wavefunction in three-dimensional space as
\Psi(x,y,z)\sim\psi(\phi)\delta(\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}-R)\delta(z)
.
Then, it is convenient to regard
\psi(\phi)
as a function defined in
\phi\in\mathbb{R}
with the following boundary condition:

\displaystyle\psi(\phi)=\psi(\phi+2\pi).

(95)

It is convenient to remove the explicit flux dependence from the Hamiltonian by defining

\displaystyle\tilde{\psi}(\phi)\equiv\exp\!\left(-i\frac{e\Phi}{2\pi}\phi\right)\psi(\phi).

(96)

This corresponds to a (multi-valued) gauge transformation. The Schrödinger equation then takes the free-particle form

\displaystyle\left[-\frac{1}{2I}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\phi^{2}}+eV(\phi)\right]\tilde{\psi}(\phi)=E\tilde{\psi}(\phi),

(97)

while the effect of the magnetic flux is encoded in the twisted boundary condition

\displaystyle\tilde{\psi}(\phi+2\pi)=\exp(- i e \Phi)\,\tilde{\psi}(\phi).

(98)

If
V(\phi)
is independent on
\phi
as
V(\phi)=V
, the Scrhödinger equation with twisted boundary conditions can be easily solved.
Then, we find

\displaystyle\tilde{\psi}_{n}(\phi)=e^{ik_{n}\phi},\qquad{\rm with}\quad k_{n}=n-\frac{e\Phi}{2\pi},\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z}.

(99)

The corresponding energy eigenvalues are

\displaystyle E_{n}(\Phi)=\frac{1}{2I}\left(n-\frac{e\Phi}{2\pi}\right)^{2}+eV.

(100)

Under a shift
\Phi\to\Phi+2\pi/e
, the spectrum is invariant up to a relabeling
n\to n-1
, and is therefore periodic in the flux with period
2\pi/e
,
which is the hallmark of the Aharonov–Bohm effect.
