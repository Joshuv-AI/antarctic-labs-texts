AI Infrastructure Sovereignty
Sergio Cruzes

Abstract
Artificial intelligence has shifted from a software-centric discipline to an infrastructure-driven system. Large-scale training and inference increasingly depend on tightly coupled data centers, high-capacity optical networks, and energy systems operating close to physical and environmental limits. As a result, control over data and algorithms alone is no longer sufficient to achieve meaningful AI sovereignty. Practical sovereignty now depends on who can deploy, operate, and adapt AI infrastructure under constraints imposed by energy availability, sustainability targets, and network reach. This tutorial-survey introduces the concept of AI infrastructure sovereignty, defined as the ability of a region, operator, or nation to exercise operational control over AI systems within physical, environmental, and infrastructural limits. The paper argues that sovereignty emerges from the co-design of three layers: AI-oriented data centers, optical transport networks, and automation frameworks that provide real-time visibility and control across both. We analyze how AI workloads reshape data center design, driving extreme power densities, advanced cooling requirements, and tighter coupling to local energy systems, with sustainability metrics such as carbon intensity and water usage acting as hard deployment boundaries. We then examine optical networks as the backbone of distributed AI, showing how latency, capacity, failure domains, and jurisdictional control define practical sovereignty limits. Building on this foundation, the paper positions telemetry, agentic AI, and digital twins as enablers of operational sovereignty, enabling validated, closed-loop control across compute, network, and energy domains. The tutorial concludes with a reference architecture for sovereign AI infrastructure that integrates telemetry pipelines, agent-based control, and digital twins, framing sustainability as a first-order design constraint rather than a policy add-on.

In this work, it is useful to distinguish between legal sovereignty and operational sovereignty. Legal sovereignty concerns jurisdiction, data protection, compliance, and formal control over ownership and access rights. While necessary, these mechanisms do not by themselves determine whether AI systems can be deployed, operated, or adapted under real-world constraints. Operational sovereignty refers instead to the ability to observe infrastructure state, make decisions based on local conditions, and execute actions within locally defined policies and limits. It is exercised through visibility, control authority, and validated execution across compute, network, and energy systems. Telemetry, automation, and closed-loop control are therefore not merely operational tools, but core enablers of operational sovereignty in AI infrastructure.

At the same time, recent studies emphasize a fundamental boundary. Complete end-to-end control of the AI stack is unrealistic, even for large economies, due to global interdependence in semiconductors, platforms, talent, and supply chains. A region may rely on foreign-designed accelerators while still achieving a meaningful level of AI sovereignty if it controls how, when, and where those accelerators operate. By unifying AI-oriented data centers, optical networks, sustainability limits, and telemetry-driven agentic control within a single framework, this tutorial reframes AI sovereignty as an infrastructure and control problem rather than a purely legal or software-defined one.

From AI Models to Physical Infrastructure

AI infrastructure cannot be understood as a purely digital stack. At large scale, it is shaped by a hierarchy of physical constraints that begin with power availability and propagate upward through cooling, networking, and operations. Higher layers may optimize utilization or efficiency, but they cannot bypass limits imposed by electrical capacity, thermal rejection, or physical space. As AI systems scale, infrastructure design becomes a process of managing these constraints rather than abstracting them away.

The rapid progress of artificial intelligence is often framed in terms of model size, training data, and algorithmic efficiency. Yet these advances are inseparable from a deeper transformation in the nature of AI workloads themselves. Modern AI systems, particularly large-scale foundation models, rely on tightly synchronized computation across thousands of accelerators operating as a single logical machine
[
37
]
. Training phases concentrate extreme compute demand into relatively short and intense intervals, while inference phases distribute sustained demand continuously across global platforms. Together, these characteristics shift AI from a flexible, location-agnostic software workload into one that is strongly bound to physical infrastructure
[

]
.

As a result, AI deployment is increasingly determined not by model availability alone, but by whether a given site can satisfy these physical and operational constraints.

Sovereignty often emerges unevenly across layers. A cloud operator may control data locality and access governance while remaining dependent on external power grids or cooling water sources. Conversely, a public research AI facility may fully control its physical infrastructure and energy supply but rely on external platforms for model tooling. These cases illustrate sovereignty as a layered condition rather than an all-or-nothing property, shaped by which layers are locally controlled and which are externally coupled.

Synchronized compute demand is a defining feature of contemporary AI. Training clusters require thousands of GPUs or similar accelerators to operate in lockstep, exchanging gradients and parameters under strict latency and bandwidth constraints. This coordination amplifies stress across supporting systems. Power draw is not only high in absolute terms but also highly dynamic, with rapid fluctuations that challenge conventional power distribution, protection, and backup designs

As these pressures accumulate, power, cooling, and space emerge as the dominant constraints shaping AI deployment. The limiting factor is no longer the availability of accelerators alone, but the ability of a site to supply sufficient electrical capacity, reject heat efficiently, and accommodate dense installations safely and reliably

Infrastructure therefore becomes a primary determinant of who can run AI at scale. Operators with access to abundant, reliable, and increasingly low-carbon energy, combined with advanced cooling architectures and high-capacity optical connectivity, can sustain large AI clusters and evolve them over time
[
26

. Others face structural barriers that cannot be mitigated through software optimization or cloud abstraction alone. Even when compute hardware is available, insufficient power or cooling capacity can render it effectively unusable.
This reality marks a departure from earlier cloud computing paradigms, in which workloads could be shifted relatively freely across data centers. AI workloads are far less portable. They bind computation to specific physical environments capable of meeting strict electrical, thermal, and networking requirements, and they expose operators directly to local grid, cooling, and facility constraints
[
42

Understanding AI through this infrastructure lens is essential for discussions of sovereignty and sustainability. Control over models and data remains necessary, but it is insufficient on its own. The capacity to build, operate, and continuously optimize the physical systems that sustain AI ultimately determines who can deploy AI at scale, under what conditions, and for how long

AI-Oriented Data Centers and Sustainability Limits

A key challenge in AI infrastructure planning is the mismatch between the speed of digital scaling and the pace of physical infrastructure development. Compute capacity can be procured and deployed rapidly, while power grids, substations, cooling plants, and permitting processes evolve over much longer timescales. This asymmetry means that capital availability alone does not determine deployment feasibility. Physical infrastructure becomes the pacing element that defines where and when AI capacity can realistically be built.

AI-oriented data centers differ fundamentally from facilities designed for traditional enterprise or general-purpose cloud workloads. The concentration of accelerators in large training and inference clusters drives rack power densities far beyond historical norms, forcing a shift toward high-density compute layouts and redesigned mechanical and electrical infrastructures
[
42
,
5
]
. Whereas conventional data centers were optimized for moderate and relatively uniform heat loads, AI facilities must accommodate sustained high heat flux and rapid load variation. As a result, air-based cooling, while still present in hybrid configurations, becomes increasingly constrained as rack densities rise. Liquid-based approaches, including direct-to-chip cooling and immersion techniques, are therefore adopted to remove heat closer to the source and to maintain stable operating conditions under continuous AI workloads
[
26
]
. These transitions reshape facility design, influencing floor layouts, power distribution architectures, redundancy strategies, and maintenance practices.

At AI rack densities, power delivery, cooling architecture, and water usage form a tightly coupled system. Choices made in one domain immediately affect the others. Increasing electrical density raises thermal load, which in turn constrains cooling technology and water sourcing. Once air cooling limits are exceeded, infrastructure decisions shift from component optimization to system-level trade-offs, where efficiency gains in one area may increase exposure or risk in another.

Once local environmental or grid constraints are reached, additional AI capacity cannot be deployed without fundamental changes in energy systems, cooling technologies, or regulatory frameworks. Sustainability therefore acts not as a secondary optimization objective, but as a hard boundary shaping where and how AI-oriented data centers can be built and operated.

Sustainability considerations now act as hard boundaries on AI deployment rather than downstream reporting metrics. Power sourcing, water availability, heat rejection, and community acceptance directly affect whether projects can proceed at all. At scale, sustainability functions as a form of risk management, shaping site selection and architectural choices long before operational efficiency metrics are evaluated.

In some regions, sustainability constraints define sovereignty boundaries more strongly than regulation. An AI deployment may meet legal sovereignty requirements yet be infeasible because of water stress, carbon intensity, or community opposition. In such cases, sovereignty is effectively constrained by environmental limits. Regions that can integrate clean energy, efficient cooling, and sustainability-aware automation gain practical autonomy to deploy and evolve AI systems over time.

Optical Networks as the Backbone of AI Sovereignty

Optical networks provide the connective fabric that links AI-oriented data centers into operational systems. While computation and storage remain physically anchored to specific sites, optical transport determines how these resources can be accessed, coordinated, and scaled across space. In the AI era, sovereignty is shaped not only by the location of data centers, but by the structure, capacity, and governance of the metro, regional, and long-haul optical networks that interconnect them
[
30
]
. While optical capacity and topology define the physical limits of sovereign AI, realizing control over these limits requires real-time visibility and coordinated automation, discussed in Sections 5 and 6.

As AI workloads become more distributed and interdependent, network infrastructure shifts from a role focused on raw throughput to one centered on control and predictability. Reliability, routing policy, repairability, and jurisdictional control increasingly matter as much as aggregate bandwidth. Control points within the network, such as routing decisions and interconnection boundaries, become key levers for enforcing operational and sovereignty requirements.

Beyond performance and resilience, optical fiber infrastructure plays a direct role in enforcing data sovereignty through jurisdictional control. The physical routes followed by optical signals determine which legal frameworks apply to data in transit, who has authority over access and monitoring, and which entities are responsible for maintenance and repair. Even when AI compute and storage are hosted within national borders, reliance on externally governed fiber routes can expose AI workloads to foreign regulatory reach, interception obligations, or delayed restoration following failures. As a result, sovereignty depends not only on the location of data centers, but on ownership, governance, and operational control of the optical fiber infrastructure itself

1
]

These links support distributed inference, data aggregation, and proximity-sensitive services where response time is critical. Regional optical networks extend this reach, allowing AI workloads to be balanced across multiple sites within a geographic area, supporting redundancy, load sharing, and operational flexibility. An AI system may be legally sovereign yet operationally dependent if its training or inference pipelines rely on external optical paths. For instance, a country hosting large inference clusters may still depend on international submarine cables for model updates, cross-border data flows, or distributed training synchronization. In this case, sovereignty is constrained not by data ownership but by latency budgets, failure domains, and repair control in optical networks that cross foreign jurisdictions.

This architectural constraint links network physics directly to the geographic concentration of AI capability.

Submarine cables introduce an additional layer of technical and geopolitical exposure. Many AI ecosystems depend on international connectivity carried by a limited number of high-capacity submarine systems that concentrate traffic across shared physical routes

Optical reach therefore acts as a multiplier of AI capability. Dense, well-managed optical meshes allow operators to pool compute capacity across sites, adapt workload placement in response to energy or sustainability constraints, and contain failures without widespread service disruption

Telecommunications operators play a distinctive role in this context, as they already control large portions of the optical infrastructure that underpins AI connectivity. Beyond providing transport capacity, telcos operate metro aggregation, interconnection points, and regulated network assets that directly shape routing policy, data locality, and jurisdictional enforcement. This positions optical networks not merely as enablers of AI workloads, but as practical instruments for implementing sovereign AI infrastructure. In this model, sovereignty is realized through integrated control of fiber assets, interconnection governance, and network-aware AI deployment, rather than through isolated ownership of compute resources alone

In this sense, control over optical networks is as consequential as control over data centers themselves. Fiber routes, spectrum availability, routing policies, and operational governance define the boundaries within which AI systems can function reliably and autonomously

Telemetry as the Foundation of Control and Visibility

As AI infrastructure grows in scale and coupling, effective control increasingly depends on continuous and accurate visibility across multiple layers. Telemetry provides this foundation by exposing the real-time state of data centers, optical networks, and supporting energy systems, transforming physical infrastructure into an observable and manageable system
[
14
]
. In the absence of telemetry, operators are forced to rely on static models, coarse assumptions, or delayed alarms, approaches that are ill-suited to environments characterized by rapid load variation, tight interdependencies, and narrow operating margins
[
4
]
.

Operational sovereignty can be strengthened through telemetry and agentic control even in heterogeneous environments. For example, a multi-vendor AI data center that exposes real-time power, cooling, and network telemetry into a unified control plane can enforce local policies regardless of equipment origin. The ability to observe, validate, and act locally reduces dependence on vendor-specific management systems and external intervention, shifting sovereignty from asset ownership to operational capability.

Cross-layer telemetry spans compute, power, cooling, and network domains. In AI-oriented data centers, this includes measurements such as rack- and cluster-level power draw, thermal gradients, coolant flow rates, and accelerator utilization
[
42
]
. In optical networks, telemetry captures signal quality indicators, spectrum occupancy, latency, loss, and fault conditions along fiber paths
[
14
]
. When these streams are combined, they reveal interactions that remain hidden within siloed monitoring systems, such as how power or cooling constraints influence workload placement decisions, or how network congestion and latency affect the efficiency of distributed training and inference
[
21
]
.

Streaming architectures are essential to make telemetry operationally useful. Rather than relying on periodic polling or coarse-grained summaries, modern infrastructures push measurements continuously into distributed monitoring and control platforms
[
14
]
. This shift enables near real-time observability, where changes in load, failures, or environmental conditions are detected within the timescales at which corrective actions remain effective. Streaming telemetry therefore underpins closed-loop control, allowing automation systems and agents to react promptly and adjust configurations before local degradations escalate into system-wide disruptions
[
47
]
.

As AI infrastructure spans multiple operators, vendors, and jurisdictions, telemetry itself becomes a sovereignty boundary. While automation and agentic control require sharing fine-grained telemetry across domains, unrestricted data exchange risks exposing sensitive operational and regulatory information. Recent work on governed telemetry data sharing shows that sovereignty can be preserved through policy-enforced data access, anonymization, and context-aware controls, rather than through isolation. By attaching usage policies directly to telemetry streams and enforcing stakeholder-specific, time-bound, and location-aware access rules, it becomes possible to support cross-domain automation while retaining operational authority and regulatory compliance. This approach reinforces the view that sovereignty is exercised through control mechanisms embedded in infrastructure workflows, not through static ownership alone
[
33

9
Conclusion

The rapid expansion of artificial intelligence has shifted the meaning of sovereignty from a question of data and algorithms to a question of infrastructure. As AI systems scale, their feasibility and impact are increasingly determined by access to power, cooling, water, and optical connectivity, as well as by the operational capability to manage these resources within environmental and geopolitical limits. AI sovereignty is no longer an abstract policy goal but an outcome shaped by engineering choices and control mechanisms.

This tutorial has framed AI sovereignty through the lens of physical infrastructure, emphasizing the interdependence of AI-oriented data centers, optical networks, and energy systems. It has shown that sustainability constraints act as hard boundaries on AI deployment and that optical networks define the practical reach and resilience of AI systems. Control over these layers, rather than ownership of software alone, ultimately determines who can deploy AI at scale and under what conditions.

A central takeaway is the role of telemetry and agentic AI as enabling technologies for sovereign operation. Telemetry transforms infrastructure into an observable system, while agent-based control and digital twins enable coordinated, validated action across domains. Together, they provide a foundation for automation that respects physical limits, enforces sustainability objectives, and reduces operational dependence on external actors.

For the engineering community, the implications are clear. Designing AI systems now requires cross-layer thinking that integrates compute, networking, energy, and control from the outset. Performance, efficiency, and resilience must be evaluated alongside sustainability and autonomy, using real-time observability and closed-loop control as core design principles.

For policymakers and regulators, this perspective highlights the need to align governance frameworks with operational reality. Effective sovereignty and sustainability policies must account for infrastructure constraints and support mechanisms that enable dynamic, verifiable control rather than static compliance. By grounding policy in measurable system behavior, it becomes possible to balance innovation, environmental responsibility, and autonomy.

In redefining AI sovereignty through infrastructure, this work underscores that the future of AI will be shaped as much by fibers, transformers, and cooling systems as by models and code. Understanding and governing these foundations is essential for building AI ecosystems that are sustainable, resilient, and genuinely sovereign.
