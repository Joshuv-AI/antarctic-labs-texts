Social determinants of intersectional inequalities in multimorbidity and its burden in China: a multilevel analysis of individual heterogeneity and discriminatory accuracy (MAIHDA)
Authors: Zhili Chen, Yaoda Hu, Huijing He, Qiong Ou, Yawen Liu, Tan Xu, Ji Tu, Zhiming Lu, Zichao Wang, Da Xu, Qing Wang, Zhujie Ran, Mengwei Zhang, Guangliang Shan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundConventional epidemiological studies often rely on average associations for single social determinants, which may obscure heterogeneity across jointly defined social positions. This study applied an intersectionality-informed Multilevel Analysis of Individual Heterogeneity and Discriminatory Accuracy (MAIHDA) approach to examine disparities in multimorbidity prevalence and burden among Chinese adults.MethodsWe analyzed 25,409 participants from the 2023–2024 China National Health Survey. Intersectional strata were defined a priori by six social-position dimensions: age, sex, region, residence, educational attainment, and occupational status, yielding 127 observed strata. Multimorbidity was defined as having two or more of 14 chronic conditions, and burden was assessed using the Chinese Multimorbidity Weighted Index (CMWI). MAIHDA models were fitted separately for multimorbidity and CMWI to estimate stratum-specific predictions, variance partition coefficients (VPCs), proportional changes in variance (PCVs), and discriminatory accuracy. Sensitivity analyses examined age restriction, outcome definition, survey-site clustering, lifestyle adjustment, and birthplace-based residence classification.ResultsOverall, multimorbidity prevalence was 22.96%, and mean CMWI was 1.32. Predicted multimorbidity prevalence ranged from 3.36% to 53.37%, and predicted CMWI ranged from 0.19 to 2.95. Higher predicted values were concentrated among older adults, northern residents, participants with lower education, and those who were farmers, unemployed, or retired. Intersectional strata accounted for 16.92% of total variation in multimorbidity and 13.74% in CMWI; discriminatory accuracy for multimorbidity was moderate (AUC = 0.75). Age explained the largest share of between-stratum variance, followed by occupational status and region. Additive main effects explained most between-stratum heterogeneity (PCVs: 82.69% for multimorbidity and 88.37% for CMWI), although residual stratum-specific deviations persisted in a subset of strata. Sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of the findings.ConclusionsMultimorbidity prevalence and burden were unevenly distributed across intersectionally defined social positions in China. Most heterogeneity reflected additive social-position gradients, particularly age, occupation, and region, with smaller residual deviations beyond additivity. These findings support proportionate universalism: universal chronic disease prevention and management should be strengthened, with additional support directed toward socially and regionally disadvantaged groups with greater burden.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12939-026-02880-5.

Introduction

Multimorbidity, commonly defined as the coexistence of two or more chronic conditions in an individual, is associated with increased mortality risk and functional decline [1, 2]. A meta-analysis estimated that 34.9% to 39.4% of adults worldwide have multimorbidity [3], while estimates in China range from about 15.1% to 35.7% [4]. However, a simple presence or absence measure does not capture its public health impact, because diseases differ in their consequences for health. To better quantify burden, weighted indices such as the Charlson Comorbidity Index and the Multimorbidity Weighted Index have been developed [5, 6]. Considering both multimorbidity prevalence and burden can therefore provide more policy-relevant evidence.

Multimorbidity is socially patterned. Key social determinants of health, such as age, sex, educational attainment, occupational status, residence, and region, have been shown to shape both the occurrence and burden of multimorbidity [7–9]. In China, these dimensions are embedded in distinctive social and institutional contexts, including gendered differences in social roles, caregiving responsibilities and healthcare use, urban–rural differences, regional inequalities, and variation in employment, social protection, healthcare access, and environmental exposures. North–South variation is particularly relevant, as it may reflect not only climate and dietary patterns, but also broader contextual and socioeconomic conditions [10, 11]. These contexts may influence multimorbidity partly through behavioural and health-related pathways; however, in this study, lifestyle-related factors were conceptualized as potential downstream mechanisms rather than primary dimensions defining social position. Understanding multimorbidity therefore requires attention not only to individual behaviours, but also to how multiple social positions jointly shape exposure, vulnerability, diagnosis, and care.

Despite growing evidence on social inequalities in multimorbidity, many epidemiological studies still rely on single-dimension average associations, such as odds ratios (ORs). Although useful for identifying broad gradients, such approaches may obscure heterogeneity within social groups [9, 12, 13]. This “tyranny of averages” can label many unaffected individuals as high risk while overlooking high-risk individuals in groups with lower average risk, potentially weakening targeting and distorting resource allocation [14]. Intersectionality theory addresses this limitation by emphasizing that social positions are mutually constituted rather than merely separate or additive categories [15]. In health research, this means that social determinants may combine to create distinct contexts of risk and protection.

Multilevel Analysis of Individual Heterogeneity and Discriminatory Accuracy (MAIHDA) provides a practical approach for this purpose. By nesting individuals within intersectional strata defined by combinations of sociodemographic and socioeconomic characteristics, MAIHDA estimates between-stratum differences, quantifies within-stratum heterogeneity, and evaluates discriminatory accuracy using measures such as the Variance Partition Coefficient (VPC) and the Area Under the Curve (AUC) [16]. Compared with single-level models requiring many interaction terms, MAIHDA scales more readily to multiple dimensions and yields more stable estimates for small strata through shrinkage or precision weighting [17, 18]. It has been increasingly used to study intersectional inequalities in outcomes such as diabetes [19], cardiometabolic multimorbidity [20], and depression [21]. Nevertheless, MAIHDA should be understood as an intersectionality-informed empirical strategy: it can identify where inequalities are concentrated and assess whether additive main effects explain between-stratum differences, but it cannot by itself establish the structural processes underlying those inequalities [22].

Despite this progress, MAIHDA-based evidence on multimorbidity in China remains limited. We therefore applied an intersectionality-informed MAIHDA approach in which strata were defined a priori by six theoretically and contextually relevant social-position dimensions: age, sex, region, residence, educational attainment, and occupational status. Specifically, we aimed to map multimorbidity prevalence and burden across these strata; quantify between-stratum heterogeneity and discriminatory accuracy using VPC and AUC; and distinguish additive main effects from residual stratum-specific deviations to assess whether some strata showed non-additive patterning beyond what would be expected from the six social-position dimensions alone.

Methods

Study design and participants

The data for this study were derived from the China National Health Survey (CNHS), conducted between April 2023 and October 2024 in two southern provinces (Guangdong and Jiangsu) and two northern provinces (Liaoning and Jilin). The CNHS is an ongoing, large-scale, community-based cross-sectional survey launched in 2012, with detailed design and methodology described elsewhere [23]. Briefly, a multi-stage stratified cluster sampling strategy was used to recruit permanent residents aged 20 years or older from urban and rural communities. Participants completed standardized questionnaires and physical examinations to collect information. A total of 25,618 adults participated in the 2023–2024 survey. After excluding individuals younger than 20 years and those with missing information on key study variables, 25,409 participants from 31 survey sites across the four provinces were included in the primary analysis (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Sampling weights and complete survey design variables were unavailable in the analytic dataset; therefore, the primary analyses were conducted as unweighted model-based MAIHDA analyses. To account for potential within-site correlation, we conducted a site-clustering sensitivity analysis using the available survey-site identifier.

Dimensions of intersectional strata (exposure)

The intersectional strata were defined using six social-position dimensions selected a priori based on prior evidence on social determinants of multimorbidity and their relevance to the Chinese context [7–9]: age (< 60 years/≥60 years), sex (male/female), region (South/North), residence (urban/rural), educational attainment (below high school/high school or above), and occupational status (employed/farmer/unemployed/retired), with sex retained as a stratum-defining dimension rather than used as a separate stratification variable. All possible combinations of these six variables yielded 128 intersectional strata (2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 4 = 128), each representing a unique intersectional social position defined by age, sex, region, residence, education, and occupation. Because the stratum defined by < 60 years, male, North, rural, high school or above, and retired had a sample size of zero, 127 strata were included in the final analysis. The overall distribution of participants and stratum-specific sample sizes are presented in Supplementary Table S1. Overall, approximately 83% of strata included ≥ 20 participants and 72% included ≥ 30, indicating that most strata had sufficient sample sizes to support reliable estimation [24].

Outcome assessment

The chronic conditions considered in this study included hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, cancer, stroke, heart disease (e.g., coronary heart disease, angina, or congestive heart failure), chronic lung disease (e.g., chronic bronchitis or emphysema), asthma, liver disease (excluding fatty liver and excluding tumors/cancer), kidney disease (excluding tumors/cancer), stomach or other digestive diseases (excluding tumors/cancer), emotional, nervous, or psychiatric problems, memory-related diseases (e.g., dementia, Parkinson’s disease, or brain atrophy), and arthritis or rheumatism. All conditions were obtained by self-report and were required to have been diagnosed at a county-level or higher hospital. Multimorbidity was defined as having two or more of these 14 conditions.

Multimorbidity burden was quantified using the Chinese Multimorbidity Weighted Index (CMWI), which was developed in a Chinese population and incorporates the impact of multiple chronic conditions on physical functioning [25]. Compared with simple disease counts, the CMWI better captures differences in how specific conditions affect health. The CMWI includes the same 14 conditions listed above and assigns each condition a weight based on its functional impact; an individual’s CMWI score is calculated as the sum of the weights of all diagnosed conditions. Among the 14 conditions, weights ranged from 0.20 to 5.10, with the three highest weights for stroke (5.1), memory-related diseases (4.3), and cancer (3.4). Weights for the remaining conditions are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Lifestyle-related variables and other social determinants

Smoking, alcohol consumption, body mass index (BMI), and physical activity were assessed as lifestyle-related variables [26]. Smoking was classified as never smoker, current smoker, or former smoker. Alcohol consumption was categorized as never drinker, current drinker, or former drinker. BMI < 18.5 or ≥ 28 kg/m² was considered high-risk. Physical activity combined occupational and leisure-time levels: low (both light), moderate (moderate/high in either), and high (moderate/high in both), with moderate and high combined for analysis. These variables were not used to construct the intersectional strata or included in the primary MAIHDA models, because they may be downstream of social position and may partly mediate associations between social determinants and multimorbidity. They were included only in a supplementary lifestyle-adjusted sensitivity analysis to evaluate the robustness of the primary findings.

Due to limited sample size, we could not include additional social-position dimensions such as ethnicity, marital status, and birthplace in the primary intersectional strata. Within an intersectional framework, these factors are not simply covariates to adjust for but may represent constitutive dimensions of social positioning and structural inequality. Therefore, ethnicity and marital status were reported descriptively, and birthplace was examined in a sensitivity analysis by replacing current residence in the construction of intersectional strata.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics included frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and means with standard deviations (SD) for continuous variables. We applied MAIHDA, treating individuals as level-1 units nested within intersectional strata as level-2 units [22]. The strata were defined by all observed combinations of the six social-position dimensions described above: age, sex, region, residence, educational attainment, and occupational status. The same three-model sequence was fitted separately for multimorbidity and CMWI. For clarity, models for multimorbidity were labelled B1–B3, and corresponding models for CMWI were labelled C1–C3.

Multimorbidity was analyzed using multilevel logistic regression with a random intercept for intersectional strata, whereas CMWI was analyzed using linear mixed-effects models with a random intercept for strata. Model B1/C1 was a null intersectional model including only the overall intercept and the stratum random intercept. Model B2/C2 added each social-position dimension separately as a fixed effect to examine its contribution to between-stratum variance. Model B3/C3 included all six dimensions simultaneously as additive main effects while retaining the stratum random intercept. Between-stratum heterogeneity was quantified using the VPC, and the proportional change in between-stratum variance (PCV) was used to assess how much of the initial stratum-level variance was explained by each dimension and by the full additive model. For multimorbidity, discriminatory accuracy was assessed using the AUC. Residual stratum-specific deviations from Model B3/C3 were interpreted as evidence of non-additive patterning beyond additive main effects, rather than as direct proof of underlying intersectional mechanisms. Full model specifications, variance calculations, and estimation details are provided in Supplementary Methods.

We conducted five sensitivity analyses: restriction to participants aged ≥ 45 years, alternative outcome definition excluding emotional, nervous, or psychiatric problems and memory-related diseases, addition of a survey-site random intercept to account for within-site clustering, additional adjustment for lifestyle-related variables, and replacement of current residence with birthplace in the construction of intersectional strata. All analyses were performed using R version 4.4.3, with multilevel logistic and linear mixed-effects models fitted using the lme4 package.

Results

Table 1 summarizes the 25,409 participants. Overall, multimorbidity prevalence was 22.96% and mean CMWI was 1.32. Both outcomes were higher in older adults (≥ 60 years), northern China, and those with less than a high school education. Prevalence and burden did not always coincide: urban residents had slightly higher prevalence than rural residents (23.58% vs. 22.02%) but lower burden among those with multimorbidity (CMWI 3.23 vs. 3.64), and although retired participants had the highest prevalence (38.27%), the highest multimorbidity burden was seen in the unemployed (CMWI 3.80 vs. 3.38).

Participant characteristics, multimorbidity, and CMWI in the study population

Across the 127 intersectional strata, predicted outcomes from the primary MAIHDA models showed substantial between-stratum heterogeneity (Fig. 1; Tables S3 and S4). Predicted multimorbidity prevalence ranged from 3.36% in the lowest-risk stratum (< 60 years, female, South, urban, high school or above, employed) to 53.37% in the highest-risk stratum (≥ 60 years, female, North, urban, below high school, unemployed). Predicted CMWI ranged from 0.19 (< 60 years, female, South, rural, high school or above, employed) to 2.95 (≥ 60 years, male, North, urban, below high school, unemployed). High predicted prevalence and burden were generally concentrated in strata characterized by older age, northern region, lower educational attainment, and unemployment, whereas the lowest predicted values were mostly observed among younger, southern, higher-educated, employed groups. Detailed stratum-specific sample sizes and corresponding observed and predicted values are provided in Tables S3 and S4.

Table 2 presents the primary MAIHDA results for multimorbidity and CMWI. In the null models (B1/C1), clear between-stratum variation was observed, with between-stratum variances of 0.67 for multimorbidity and 0.38 for CMWI. The corresponding VPCs showed that 16.92% and 13.74% of the total variation, respectively, was attributable to differences between the 127 intersectional strata. Discriminatory accuracy for multimorbidity was moderate (AUC = 0.75). In the one-dimension-at-a-time models (B2/C2), age explained the largest share of between-stratum variance for both outcomes (PCV = 53.03% for multimorbidity; 58.50% for CMWI), followed by occupational status and region. Sex, residence, and educational attainment explained smaller proportions. In the additive main-effects models (B3/C3), higher multimorbidity and CMWI were observed among adults aged ≥ 60 years, northern residents, and those who were farmers, unemployed, or retired, whereas lower values were observed among women and participants with high school education or above. After accounting for all six additive main effects, between-stratum variance decreased to 0.12 for multimorbidity and 0.04 for CMWI, corresponding to PCVs of 82.69% and 88.37%. Thus, most between-stratum heterogeneity was explained by additive social-position gradients, although a smaller residual component remained beyond additivity.

Sensitivity analyses generally supported the robustness of the primary findings (Table S7). In the age-restricted analysis among participants aged ≥ 45 years, between-stratum variation remained evident despite lower VPCs than in the primary analysis (11.27% for multimorbidity; 9.36% for CMWI). Additive main effects still explained most between-stratum variance (PCVs 81.60% and 87.43%), and age, region, and occupational status remained the main contributing dimensions (Tables S7 and S9). High predicted values remained concentrated among older adults in northern regions and socioeconomically disadvantaged groups (Tables S10 and S11). Results were also robust to alternative outcome definition, site-clustering adjustment, lifestyle adjustment, and birthplace-based residence classification. Excluding emotional and memory-related diseases produced VPCs and PCVs similar to the primary analysis (Tables S14–S19). Adding a survey-site random intercept attenuated stratum-level VPCs but they remained meaningful (12.81% for multimorbidity; 11.09% for CMWI), while site-level variation accounted for 5.95% and 2.74%, respectively; the regional contribution was also attenuated, suggesting partial overlap between regional patterning and site-level context (Tables S20–S24). Lifestyle-adjusted models yielded comparable VPCs (15.98% and 12.40%) and PCVs (81.01% and 86.67%) (Table S25). Replacing current residence with birthplace produced similar overall patterns, although some stratum-specific rankings shifted (Tables S26–S35). Across all sensitivity analyses, most between-stratum heterogeneity was still explained by additive social-position gradients, while a smaller residual component persisted beyond additivity.

Discussion

In this large community-based sample of Chinese adults, we used an intersectionality-informed MAIHDA approach to examine how multimorbidity prevalence and burden were distributed across socially defined strata. Multimorbidity was common, affecting 22.96% of participants, with a mean CMWI of 1.32. Both outcomes varied substantially across the 127 intersectional strata: predicted multimorbidity prevalence ranged from 3.36% to 53.37%, and predicted CMWI ranged from 0.19 to 2.95. Higher values were concentrated among older adults, northern residents, individuals with lower education, and those who were farmers, unemployed, or retired. Intersectional strata accounted for a meaningful share of total variation, with VPCs of 16.92% for multimorbidity and 13.74% for CMWI, while discriminatory accuracy for multimorbidity was moderate. Most between-stratum variation was explained by additive main effects of the six social-position dimensions, although a smaller residual component persisted beyond additivity.

These findings should be interpreted through intersectionality theory and with attention to the role of MAIHDA as an empirical tool. Intersectionality, as developed by Crenshaw and extended in social epidemiology, emphasizes that social positions are mutually constituted rather than merely separate or additive categories [27]. Consistent with Krieger’s ecosocial theory, these positions shape health through material, psychosocial, environmental, and institutional pathways that may become embodied over time [28]. In this study, age, sex, region, residence, education, and occupation were therefore treated as jointly defining intersectional social positions, not simply as independent risk factors. MAIHDA is useful because it can model many such strata simultaneously and compare additive with residual components of inequality. However, statistical interaction is not equivalent to intersectionality. The residual stratum-specific deviations observed here should be understood as evidence of non-additive patterning and as a basis for further hypothesis generation, rather than as direct proof of the structural mechanisms producing intersectional inequality [16].

The predominance of additive main effects is substantively important. It suggests that inequalities in multimorbidity and CMWI were driven mainly by the cumulative layering of familiar social gradients rather than by widespread large departures from additivity. Placed in the growing MAIHDA literature, our findings are consistent with prior intersectional applications. For instance, a Swedish MAIHDA study of cardiometabolic multimorbidity reported a similarly high VPC (around 15%) alongside only modest discriminatory accuracy (AUC around 0.71), with the highest prevalence concentrated among adults aged 60–64 years with low education; in that study, age and education were also the leading contributors to between-stratum differences [20]. In our data, age contributed most to between-stratum differences in both outcomes, while region and occupational status also played important roles. This is expected given the strong age-related nature of multimorbidity. Yet age is not only a biological process; it is also a life-course marker of accumulated social, occupational, environmental, and healthcare exposures [29, 30]. The age-restricted sensitivity analysis among participants aged ≥ 45 years showed that, although VPCs were attenuated, between-stratum variation remained evident and age, region, and occupation remained the main contributing dimensions. Thus, the findings were not simply an artifact of including younger adults, while confirming that age is central to the patterning of multimorbidity.

The roles of region and occupation should be understood within China’s broader social and institutional context. Regional inequalities in China are shaped by long-standing differences in economic development, climate, environment, dietary practices, healthcare infrastructure, and public health resources [31, 32]. Northern residence was consistently associated with higher multimorbidity prevalence and burden, which may reflect geographically patterned cardiometabolic risk, colder climate and seasonal constraints on physical activity, higher-salt dietary habits, and differences in healthcare access and chronic disease management [33, 34]. Occupation also emerged as a major structuring dimension. In the Chinese context, occupation may capture not only employment status but also material resources, employment stability, insurance arrangements, pension access, physical exposures, psychosocial stress, and the ability to maintain continuous care [35]. Farmers may face physically demanding work and more limited access to high-quality chronic disease management, while unemployed adults may represent a particularly vulnerable group affected by both poor health and socioeconomic disadvantage [36]. Retired adults had high prevalence, likely reflecting age and greater healthcare contact, but their burden was lower than that of unemployed participants, possibly because retirement is accompanied by more stable social protection than unemployment [35, 37].

Although additive gradients explained most between-stratum heterogeneity, residual deviations identified strata whose outcomes were higher or lower than expected under additivity. Positive residual deviations for both outcomes were repeatedly concentrated among older northern rural women, especially those who were unemployed or farmers and had lower education. From an intersectionality-informed perspective, these strata represent the convergence of older age, female sex, rural residence, northern context, limited education, and disadvantaged occupational position. Although causal mechanisms cannot be established in this cross-sectional study, their excess burden may reflect mutually reinforcing constraints, including cumulative biological risk, physically demanding work histories, limited access to timely diagnosis and management, gendered caregiving responsibilities, weaker economic protection, and regional exposures [38–40]. Conversely, negative residual deviations in selected urban or retired strata may suggest that contextual resources, such as urban healthcare access, retirement benefits, pension income, or regular medical contact, partially buffer other disadvantages [41, 42].

Our sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of the findings. Because lifestyle-related variables may lie downstream of social position, smoking, alcohol consumption, BMI, and physical activity were not included in the primary MAIHDA model sequence, avoiding potential overadjustment. The lifestyle-adjusted analysis produced comparable results. The outcome-definition analysis excluding emotional, nervous, or psychiatric problems and memory-related diseases also yielded similar findings. The site-clustering analysis showed that within-site correlation contributed to variation but did not materially alter the main conclusions, and replacing current residence with birthplace supported the robustness of residence-related patterning.

These findings support proportionate universalism [43]. The meaningful VPCs indicate that intersectional strata capture important contextual patterning, but the moderate discriminatory accuracy shows that they should not be used for narrow individual risk classification. Universal chronic disease prevention, early detection, functional assessment, and integrated management should be strengthened for the whole adult population, while additional support should be directed toward groups with greater predicted burden, particularly older adults in northern, rural, and socioeconomically disadvantaged settings. Health planning should also move beyond simple disease counts and consider functional burden.

Several limitations should be noted. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and cannot establish temporal ordering between social position, lifestyle-related factors, multimorbidity, and burden. Second, all chronic conditions were self-reported and required diagnosis at a county-level or higher hospital. Differential detection may therefore exist because access to diagnosis may vary by region, residence, education, occupation, and other social resources. As a result, some observed differences in diagnosed multimorbidity may partly reflect unequal diagnostic opportunity rather than differences in underlying disease burden alone. Third, although outcome-definition sensitivity analyses supported robustness, the multimorbidity definition did not capture disease severity, duration, treatment, or timing. Fourth, sampling weights and complete survey design variables were unavailable, so fully design-weighted MAIHDA models could not be implemented; estimates should therefore be interpreted as unweighted model-based estimates for the analytic sample. Finally, the strata were limited to six dimensions with relatively coarse categories. Ethnicity and marital status were not included because of sample size or data limitations. This is particularly relevant in China, where ethnicity may overlap with geography, rural residence, socioeconomic disadvantage, cultural practices, and access to healthcare and public resources. Therefore, some regional or residence-related patterns may partly reflect unmeasured ethnic heterogeneity, and residual stratum-specific deviations may have been under- or overestimated in strata with uneven ethnic composition. Future studies with larger and more diverse samples should incorporate ethnicity as a constitutive social-position dimension.

Conclusions

Multimorbidity prevalence and multimorbidity burden were unevenly distributed across intersectionally defined social positions in China. Most between-stratum heterogeneity was explained by additive gradients of age, region, occupation, education, residence, and sex, while a smaller residual component persisted beyond additivity. These findings suggest that multimorbidity inequalities largely reflected the cumulative layering of social disadvantage, but that some groups, particularly older northern rural women with socioeconomic disadvantage, experience burdens that exceed additive expectations. An intersectionality-informed MAIHDA approach can help identify where such burdens are concentrated, while also reminding us that statistical models cannot replace theoretical and contextual explanation. Reducing multimorbidity inequalities will require universal chronic disease prevention and management, strengthened proportionately according to social and regional need.
