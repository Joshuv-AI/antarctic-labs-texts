Vision At Night: Exploring Biologically Inspired Preprocessing For Improved Robustness Via Color And Contrast Transformations
Lorena Stracke, Lia Nimmermann, Shashank Agnihotri, Margret Keuper, Volker Blanz

Abstract
Inspired by the human visual system’s mechanisms for contrast enhancement and color-opponency, we explore biologically motivated input preprocessing for robust semantic segmentation. By applying Difference-of-Gaussians (DoG) filtering to RGB, grayscale, and opponent-color channels, we enhance local contrast without modifying model architecture or training. Evaluations on Cityscapes, ACDC, and Dark Zurich show that such preprocessing maintains in-distribution performance while improving robustness to adverse conditions like night, fog, and snow. As this processing is model-agnostic and lightweight, it holds potential for integration into imaging pipelines, enabling imaging systems to deliver task-ready, robust inputs for downstream vision models in safety-critical environments.

2
Related Work

Biological Vision As A Model For Robustness.

Biologically inspired neural computation has a long tradition in machine learning
[
28
,
14
,
25
]
. Many efforts focus on modeling the early visual system. Neural activations in trained CNNs resemble center-surround receptive fields
[
32
]
.
Kim et al. 2016
introduced ON and OFF ReLU units to mimic the behavior of bipolar cells.
Babaiee et al. 2021
incorporated center-surround filters and fed them as additional inputs to deeper layers, improving robustness on NORB
[
26
]
.
Hasani et al. 2019
applied DoG filters directly to the first activation maps in CNNs, simulating retinal filtering.
Tsitiridis et al. 2019
extended this by using double-opponent cells for opponent-channel processing in face biometrics. Building on this, our work integrates DoG-style preprocessing into the input pipeline for segmentation, targeting robustness to nighttime and weather-based lighting shifts.

Grayscale Input In Neural Networks.

Grayscale input has been explored in various contexts
[
38
]
.
Bui et al. 2016
showed that grayscale images can outperform RGB in object recognition under challenging conditions. For facial expression and identity recognition, grayscale performs comparably to RGB
[
10
,
43
]
, although shallow models rely more on color
[
10
]
. RGB remains superior in color-sensitive tasks such as plant disease classification
[
33
]
. Given the dominance of luminance-based perception in the HVS under low-light conditions
[
39
]
, grayscale variants serve as a promising robustness baseline in our study.

Robustness To Lighting Variations.

Several works have highlighted the vulnerability of neural networks to lighting changes
[
21
]
.
Sivaraman and Murthy 2018
demonstrated performance drops under illumination shifts for ImageNet-trained CNNs.
Hu et al. 2023
showed that brightness and shadow variations significantly degrade model performance. The large-scale benchmark by
Hendrycks and Dietterich 2019
further confirmed that models trained on clean data are sensitive to a wide range of corruptions. Our work complements these by targeting robustness through preprocessing mechanisms that mimic biologically grounded visual filters.

3
Image Preprocessing

We explore biologically inspired preprocessing techniques that enhance neural network robustness in adverse lighting and weather conditions, with a focus on nighttime scenarios.
We model the HVS’s center-surround and double-opponent retinal cells through two components:
(1) color reparameterization using biologically plausible color space transforms
and
(2) spatial contrast extraction via a DoG-like process
.
Both are combined into a lightweight, fixed module applied directly before the network.

3.1
Color Reparameterization

The color reparameterization is carried out by forming linear combinations of the RGB channels of the original image. These combinations are obtained using
Eq.

1
, and are dependent on the
3\times 3
matrix
\mathrm{M}
:

\scriptstyle\begin{bmatrix}\text{channel}_{1}\\
\text{channel}_{2}\\
\text{channel}_{3}\end{bmatrix}=\mathrm{M}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}R\\
G\\
B\end{bmatrix}

(1)

We examine three types of color reparameterizations: grayscale, color-opponency, and single-color.

Grayscale.

The grayscale variant is motivated both by human night vision
[
39
]
and findings that show how grayscale images can perform comparably to RGB images (see
Section

2
).
For this variant, all coefficients of the corresponding linear combination are set to
\frac{1}{3}
.
In addition, we examine an alternative grayscale variant, referred to as “
Grayscale green bias
”, whose coefficients are:

\scriptstyle\mathrm{M}_{\text{grayscale green bias}}=\begin{bmatrix}0.299&0.587&0.114\\
0.299&0.587&0.114\\
0.299&0.587&0.114\end{bmatrix}.

(2)

Grayscale green bias
is commonly adopted by many RGB to Grayscale conversion methods
[
17
]
with the intuition that it approximates an average person’s relative perception of the brightness of red, green, and blue light
[
31
]
.

Grayscale images can be represented either as a single-channel image or replicated across all three channels. In the case of single-channel,
\mathrm{M}
is a
1\times 3
matrix.

Color-opponency.

For the color-opponency reparameterization, we draw inspiration from human double-opponent cells
[
31
,
23
,
39
]
.
We mimic their processing using the color reparameterization matrix given in
Eq.

3
:

{\scriptstyle\mathrm{M}_{co}=\begin{bmatrix}\phantom{-}\frac{1}{3}&\phantom{-}\frac{1}{3}&\phantom{-}\frac{1}{3}\\
\phantom{-}\frac{1}{2}&-\frac{1}{2}&\phantom{-}0\\
-\frac{1}{6}&-\frac{1}{6}&\phantom{-}\frac{1}{3}\end{bmatrix}.}

(3)

This transformation produces three output channels corresponding to black-white, red-green, and blue-yellow.

Single Color.

To assess whether color reparameterization influences component
(2) spatial contrast extraction
, we also consider the single-color variant, in which the reparameterization matrix is the identity matrix.
In this case, the output channels remain red, green, and blue, analogous to the case without reparameterization.

3.2
Spatial Contrast Extraction

To model the center-surround structure, we approximate the Difference of Gaussians (DoG), which serves as a simplified form of the Marr-Hildreth kernel
[
27
]
.
While proposed as an edge detector, it functions similarly to the center surround cells in early vision
[
27
,
31
,
23
,
39
]
.

Here, we progressively blur the original image with a uniform 3
\times
3 box blur kernel, resulting in multiple images
\mathrm{img}_{\mathrm{d}}
, where
\mathrm{d}
denotes the number of times the progressive blurring was applied.
Multiple box blurring approximates the Gaussian blur
[
16
]
.
We subtract the blurred images from the original image with weights that sum up to
0
.
This results in a similar effect to a convolution with a Marr-Hildreth kernel, effectively simulating a center-surround cell.
This also discards absolute luminances, so only local contrast information is left.
The final input for the networks is then calculated using
Eq.

4
, where
\mathrm{M}
denotes the color reparameterization weights of the used variant and
\mathrm{d}
the depth (number of steps) of the progressively blurring:

\scriptstyle\mathrm{img}_{\text{contrast}}=\mathrm{M}\cdot\mathrm{img}_{\text{original}}\;-\;\sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{\mathrm{d}}\tfrac{1}{\mathrm{d}}\cdot\mathrm{M}\cdot\mathrm{img}_{\mathrm{i}}.

(4)

When
\mathrm{d}=0
, only the color reparameterization is applied and no spatial contrast extraction, functioning as the control.
In combination with single-color reparameterization, this results in not performing any processing.
Thus, serving as a baseline.

4.1
Experimental Setup

We study semantic segmentation as a downstream task, since it requires correct pixel-wise predictions and is therefore well-suited for analyzing preprocessing techniques. Models are trained on Cityscapes
[
13
]
and evaluated on Cityscapes, Dark Zurich
[
34
]
, and ACDC
[
35
]
(night, fog, and rain subsets). Performance is measured using mean IoU (mIoU), mean class accuracy (mAcc), and overall pixel accuracy (aAcc), averaged over three seeds. We evaluate three representative architectures: DeepLabV3+
[
12
]
, SegFormer
[
42
]
, and InternImage
[
41
]
. Further experimental details, including backbone choices and hyperparameters, are provided in
Section

A.2
.

4.2
Robustness Under Nighttime Conditions

We evaluate 3 biologically inspired preprocessing variants: grayscale, color-opponency, and single-color, each with depth
\mathrm{d}=5
.
Fig.

2
shows the mIoU results on the Cityscapes dataset and the nighttime test datasets.
As expected, we observe a drop in performance on the nighttime images, compared to the i.i.d. Cityscapes test set.
However, we observe that on the i.i.d. test set, all preprocessing variants perform on par with the baseline,
while on the nighttime images, all preprocessing variants outperform the baseline.
Among them, the grayscale preprocessing consistently achieves the best results,
indicating a slight edge for luminiscence-based preprocessing for nighttime images.
This observation, although intuitive, has not been demonstrated previously for semantic segmentation with real-world nighttime images.

Our results show that contrast-based preprocessing is particularly effective in nighttime scenarios and further strengthens robustness when incorporated into the training pipeline. Grayscale conversion, in particular, enforces invariance to color while emphasizing luminance, which explains the slight drop in i.i.d. performance. At the same time, this color invariance improves generalization under distribution shifts by simplifying the input representation and reducing sensitivity to illumination changes.

4.3
Ablation Studies

Grayscale In Nighttime Situations.

Given the relatively superior nighttime performance of grayscale preprocessing, we investigate it further, independent of the
spatial contrast extraction
(that is, with
\mathrm{d}=0
).
We tested the two grayscale versions: one, the already discussed version with all coefficients of
\mathrm{M}=\frac{1}{3}
, second,
grayscale green bias
from
Eq.

2
.
Each as single-channel and three-channel (duplicated) inputs, as shown by
Fig.

3
. Consistent with the main experiments, all grayscale versions outperform the baseline at night.
Interestingly, all grayscale versions perform on par with each other, across architectures.

This observation that grayscale preprocessing performs well in nighttime settings aligns with the human vision in low-light situations (see
Section

2
).

Depth Parameter Search.

To analyze the impact of the depth parameter
\mathrm{d}
, we trained a DeepLabv3+ with a smaller backbone (ResNet-18) to enable an effective study under resource constraints.
We ablate over
\mathrm{d}\in[0,10]
on the same datasets and report the results in
Fig.

4
.
We observe that the depth significantly influences the performance, making it challenging to identify a single optimal value.
The performance also varies across different preprocessing variants.
Balancing overall robustness and accuracy on i.i.d. data, we observe that
\mathrm{d}=5
is the best candidate across preprocessing variants.
We report additional results in
Appendix

B
.

Going Beyond Nighttime Robustness.

For nighttime robustness, in our evaluations, luminance emerged to be more important than color information.
However, in other robustness scenarios, especially when low luminance is not a concern, color might play an important role.
To test this hypothesis, we evaluate the preprocessing effects under different weather conditions.
For this purpose, we trained a DeepLabv3+ (ResNet-50) and an InternImage network on Cityscapes, and validated them on the fog, rain, and snow splits of the ACDC dataset.
The results are presented in
Fig.

5
.
For DeepLabv3+, all preprocessing variants consistently improved performance across all weather subsets.
Whereas, for InternImage, all preprocessing variants showed gains on the fog subset.
In the rain subset, color emerged as more important, with only grayscale leading to a slight decline in performance.
For the snow subset, all variants were compared at par with the baseline.

These findings demonstrate that the proposed preprocessing not only benefits nighttime images but also generalizes to some diverse adverse weather conditions.
While the gains might be architecture dependent, color information is more important when low luminance is not a concern.

5
Conclusion

This study demonstrates that biologically inspired preprocessing, grounded in the human visual system’s center-surround and color-opponent mechanisms, can substantially improve semantic segmentation robustness under adverse conditions without sacrificing in-distribution accuracy.
Across multiple architectures and datasets, luminance-driven transformations, particularly grayscale preprocessing, consistently enhanced performance in low-light scenarios. Meanwhile, color-inclusive preprocessing improved resilience under challenging weather.
Given that the approach is model-agnostic and computationally lightweight, it can be readily integrated into imaging pipelines.
Embedding such processing in future imaging sensors could deliver task-ready, robust input, which would advance reliable vision in safety-critical applications.

Appendix A
Implementation Details

A.1
More Detailed Description Of The Preprocessing

The implemented preprocessing mimics aspects of the human visual system by applying color conversion and contrast computation to the input image. As described in the main paper (see
Section

3
), we investigate three preprocessing variants: single-color, grayscale, and color-opponency.

The preprocessing is applied immediately before feeding the input images into the neural network. Depending on the configuration, it consists of two or three convolutional layers.

The first layer is a fixed, non-trainable
3\times 3
averaging filter applied independently to each input channel, producing a uniform blur. This operation is repeated
d
times recursively, generating progressively blurred versions of the input. The original image is then stacked with the
d
blurred versions, resulting in a tensor with
3(d+1)
channels: the first three correspond to the RGB channels of the original image, followed by the RGB channels of the increasingly blurred images.

The second convolutional layer performs the color space conversion and subtraction between the original and blurred images. Like the first layer, it is non-trainable. Depending on the configuration, this layer outputs either a single-channel or a three-channel representation. The calculations for this are provided in the main paper (see
Section

3.1
).

For the grayscale comparison, we evaluate the two grayscale variants: grayscale and grayscale_green_bias. They are both evaluated with the depth set to
d=0
and with either one or three output channels after the second layer.

To maintain compatibility with networks expecting three-channel input, a third convolutional layer is added when only a single channel is produced. This layer has one input channel, three output channels, a
1\times 1
kernel, stride 1, and no padding. It acts as a pointwise convolution, expanding the single channel to three identical channels.

A.2
Experimental Setup

All experiments were conducted on NVIDIA Tesla V100 GPUs (16 GB) for the task of semantic segmentation, using a single GPU for training in all cases.

When performing image classification, a model can still correctly classify an image, even if it fails to extract features from most pixels

9
]
.
Thus, we explore the pixel-wise classification task of semantic segmentation, since here all pixels need to be correctly classified, thus allowing for a better study of the image processing techniques.

Appendix D
Limitations.

In our experiments, we aimed to identify the best-performing depth (
Section

4
). While it is possible to determine a single overall best-performing depth, the optimal value can vary depending on the color-reparameterization used in the preprocessing. Moreover, the results from the depth search in the range of 0 to 10 may not be directly transferable to other architectures. This is illustrated by the depth search conducted on Deeplabv3+ (ResNetV1c-50), as shown in
Fig.

8
: in this case, the grayscale preprocessing with depth
d=5
yields better performance than grayscale with depth
d=0
(i.e., grayscale without contrast). By contrast, for all other architectures, grayscale preprocessing with depth
d=0
surpasses depth
d=5
in nighttime scenarios.

Consequently, future research should focus on assessing how different architectures influence the performance across varying depths, or on developing methods that avoid using a fixed depth value during training.
