Countering postoperative immune suppression with a self-assembling dendritic cell nanovaccine
Authors: Qiang Yang, Tongge Wang, Hongrui Zhang, Yanteng Xu, Shuheng Huang, Yanyun He, Runxiu Wei, Na Yin, Yifei Gao, Liya Yang, Shenyao Jin, Linjing Mi, Ruilin Chao, Mingqiang Li, Min Feng, Ling Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Postoperative immune suppression, driven by transforming growth factor β (TGFβ)-mediated dendritic cell (DC) dysfunction, significantly impairs antigen-specific T cell responses and elevates cancer recurrence risk. A key mechanism is TGFβ-mediated lysosomal incompetence in DCs, resulting from loss of lysosomal prosaposin (pSAP). Herein, we developed an in vivo-generated DC vaccine, BAIT (boosting antigen-delivering immunovaccine for T cell priming), which integrated DC recruitment, antigen delivery, and immune activation within a single platform. Inspired by arginine metabolism-driven lysosomal biogenesis and acidification, BAITs were designed with an arginine-enriched self-assembling peptide backbone, in which tumor lysate antigens and CCL20 co-assembled via coordination interactions. When administered early post-surgery, BAITs recruited DCs, promoted antigen internalization, and activated the mTOR pathway to upregulate pSAP expression. This BAIT-driven DC modulation reversed TGFβ-induced impairments in antigen digestion and MHC-peptide complex formation. To optimize precision immunotherapy, we employed a functional precision medicine approach by customizing tumor lysate preparations induced via ferroptosis, necroptosis, or apoptosis. In a murine postoperative cancer model, Apo-BAITs formulated with apoptotic lysates achieved an 85.5% reduction in tumor burden. These findings demonstrate that BAITs target the lysosomal mTOR-pSAP axis to restore DC function, providing a personalized postoperative cancer vaccine strategy to counter surgery-induced immune suppression.

Introduction

Surgical excision of primary or metastatic tumors can be life-saving or life-extending. However, mounting evidence indicates that the surgical trauma itself may inadvertently promote the outgrowth of micrometastases and facilitate the formation of new metastatic lesions [1,2]. The systemic response to surgery can override pre-existing tumor-specific T cell surveillance, permitting dormant tumor cells to escape immune control and resume growth [3]. This vulnerability is closely linked to the postoperative “immune suppression window”, during which surgical stress, anesthesia, and other perioperative factors, while essential for wound healing, concurrently induce systemic immune suppression [4].

A central feature of this postoperative immune vulnerability is impaired DC function. Both our previous work and prior studies have demonstrated that dysfunctional DCs exhibit reduced antigen uptake and diminished T-cell priming capacity, thereby compromising antitumor immunity and potentially facilitating metastatic progression [5,6]. Several surgery-related changes converge to disrupt DC function. First, surgical removal of the tumor also dismantles its microenvironment, depleting the pool of tumor antigens and abolishing chemokine gradients that guide DC migration [7]. Second, surgical stress triggers the release of mediators such as prostaglandins and transforming growth factor β (TGFβ) that directly impair and disrupt DC function [8,9]. These factors suppress the key steps of antigen processing and presentation, leaving DCs unable to effectively display tumor antigens. The combined loss of DC migration and antigen presentation critically weakens the initiation of tumor-specific CD8+ T cell responses, thereby allowing residual tumor cells to escape detection and drive postoperative metastatic outgrowth. Indeed, this recovery period is increasingly recognized as a “window of opportunity” for residual tumor cells to establish metastases [10].

Lysosomes are indispensable for DC antigen processing, as they contain the hydrolytic enzymes necessary to digest internalized antigens into peptides for subsequent loading onto major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules [11,12]. However, post-surgical patients exhibit elevated levels of TGFβ, a pleiotropic cytokine primarily involved in tissue repair and wound healing, which directly disrupts the DC lysosomal function and thus compromises their antigen-processing ability [13]. Consistent with this, preclinical studies validate that loss of TGFβ signaling in DCs (e.g., conditional deletion of TGFβ receptor Ⅱ) enhances MHC Ⅰ/Ⅱ-mediated antigen presentation, promotes robust CD8+ T cell priming, and suppresses tumor growth [14,15]. Mechanistically, sustained TGFβ exposure drives excessive glycosylation of the lysosomal precursor protein prosaposin (pSAP) [16]. This aberrant modification prevents pSAP from binding to sortilin, the receptor that traffics it to lysosomes. Instead, hyperglycosylated pSAP is misdirected to the secretory pathway and released extracellularly, causing a severe deficiency of functional saposins (SAPs) inside DC lysosomes. The loss of SAPs compromises lysosomal membrane integrity and peptide generation, and in particular, it diminishes cross-presentation of antigens from apoptotic cells [17]. Supporting this mechanistic cascade, targeted delivery of pSAP to DCs using anti-DEC205 antibodies in tumor-bearing mice replenished lysosomal SAPs, improved antigen cross-presentation, and significantly reduced tumor burden [16]. These findings implicate TGFβ-driven lysosomal dysfunction in DCs as a major contributor to postoperative immune suppression, highlighting the potential of strategies that restore functional pSAP.

Arginine metabolism plays a critical role in regulating lysosomal function in DCs by activating the mTOR signaling pathway, which promotes lysosomal biogenesis and acidification [18,19]. Notably, mTOR activity is tightly linked to pSAP expression, underscoring the importance of this axis in maintaining lysosomal competence [20]. Given that TGFβ signaling impairs lysosomal function by causing aberrant pSAP glycosylation and mislocalization (reducing SAP availability and antigen cross-presentation), activation of mTOR through arginine supplementation may counteract these effects. Based on this rationale, we developed BAIT (boosting antigen-delivering immunovaccine for T cell priming), an in vivo-generated DC vaccine featuring an arginine-enriched self-assembling peptide nanogel that integrated DC recruitment, antigen delivery, and lysosome restoration within a single platform (Scheme 1). Tumor lysate antigens co-assemble within the nanogel to form an immunogenic reservoir, while the chemokine CCL20 is embedded to recruit DCs. When administered in the early postoperative period, BAITs gradually release CCL20 to establish a local chemokine gradient, attracting migratory DCs to the injection site. The recruited DCs internalized the nanosized BAITs, which in turn activate the mTOR pathway and upregulate pSAP and saposins to restore lysosomal function. Energized by BAITs, DCs efficiently process the tumor lysates into antigenic peptides and present them on MHC class Ⅰ and Ⅱ molecules, resulting in robust T cell priming and amplified antitumor responses. To enhance precision immunotherapy, we adopted a functional precision medicine (FPM) approach to customize the tumor lysate component of BAITs. We systematically evaluated lysates generated through distinct immunogenic cell death modalities (ferroptosis, necroptosis, and apoptosis) and identified an apoptosis-derived formulation (Apo-BAITs) as the most effective for promoting DC maturation. This enhanced DC maturation led to efficient antigen presentation and vigorous priming of tumor‐specific T cells, underscoring the potential for customizing BAITs according to a patient's tumor characteristics or treatment regimen. Overall, integrating BAIT vaccination with clinical FPM could provide a personalized postoperative immunotherapy strategy tailored to individual patients to directly counter surgery-induced immunosuppression.Scheme 1BAIT, an arginine-enriched peptide nanogel co-assembling antigens and CCL20, acts as an in vivo-generated DC vaccine. Results

Fabrication and mechanical properties of self-assembling BAITs

To counteract lysosomal dysfunction driven by elevated TGFβ following tumor resection, we developed a DC-attracting nanovaccine (BAIT) by co-assembling tumor lysate antigens, which provide a broad repertoire of tumor-associated proteins [21], together with the chemokine CCL20 into arginine-enriched peptide nanogels (Fig. 1A). The peptide was synthesized with the sequence Arg-Ala-Asp-Ala-Arg-Ala-Asp-Ala-Arg-Ala-Asp-Ala-Arg-Ala-Asp-Ala, which could self-assemble into β-sheet nanofibers through electrostatic interactions between alternating arginine and aspartic acid residues, further stabilized by inter-strand hydrogen bonding. Ca2+ was employed as a linker to anchor tumor lysate antigens and CCL20 onto the peptide backbone. Molecular dynamics simulations confirmed the strong affinity between the peptides and Ca2+. The consistently persistent root mean square deviation (RMSD) fluctuations and stable radius of gyration (Rg) values suggested negligible peptide-peptide interactions, implying that the overall size and compactness of the peptides were largely unaffected during the simulation (Fig. S1A). In contrast, the radial distribution function (RDF) of Ca2+ around the peptides revealed a sharp peak at approximately 0.25 nm, indicating a strong and preferential accumulation of Ca2+ near the peptide surface (Fig. 1B). This finding highlights stable calcium binding, likely mediated by specific interaction sites or binding motifs that facilitated Ca2+ coordination. Visual molecular dynamics (VMD) analysis illustrated conformational changes in the peptide-Ca2+ complex over a 100 ns simulation, with dynamic structural shifts occurring as the simulation progressed (Fig. 1C). These changes highlight the evolving interactions between the peptides and Ca2+, further emphasizing the importance of calcium binding in modulating peptide structure. Representative snapshots of three peptide instances further revealed the role of specific Asp, Ala and Arg residues in calcium coordination, driving localized conformational changes such as stabilization or destabilization of helical regions (Fig. 1D). Specifically, Asp3, Asp7, Asp11, Asp15 and Ala16 coordinated with Ca2+ in the first peptide, Asp3, Arg5, Asp7, Asp11 and Ala16 in the second, and Asp11 for two peptides’ coordination in the third.

Circular dichroism spectroscopy confirmed that these self-assembling peptides bind strongly to antigens and co-assemble into an integrated hydrogel network in the presence of Ca2+, as evidenced by significant conformational changes (Fig. 1E). Consistently, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) of the pre-nanostructured hydrogel revealed that incorporation of tumor lysate antigens led to a denser and thickened fibrous network compared to the blank peptide hydrogel, indicating successful integration of antigens into the peptide scaffold (Fig. S1B). With Ca2+ serving as a stabilizing linker to prevent aggregation of tumor lysate antigens and CCL20, the resulting BAIT nanogels exhibited excellent water dispersibility (Fig. 1F). Upon nanostructuring, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) further showed that the initially unstable and irregular antigen dispersions were transformed into well-defined, monodisperse nanogels (Fig. 1G). Confocal laser scanning microscopy, supported by Pearson's correlation analysis (0.908 ± 0.009), confirmed strong spatial colocalization of peptides (green) and antigens (red) within BAITs (Fig. 1H). Quantitative analysis further showed that BAITs achieved a high antigen encapsulation efficiency of 94.3% ± 6.0%, indicating efficient incorporation of tumor lysates into the nanogel network. Dynamic light scattering measured an average hydrodynamic diameter of 110.4 ± 5.7 nm for the BAITs (Fig. 1H), slightly larger than that of blank nanogels, consistent with antigen loading (Fig. S1C). Correspondingly, the zeta potential decreased after antigen incorporation, reflecting the presence of negatively charged protein components (Fig. S1D). Notably, rheological analysis revealed a three-step sol-gel transition during BAIT formation (Fig. 1I). Initially, the tumor antigen alone behaved as a fluid, with the loss modulus greater than the storage modulus (G’ < G″). Upon mixing with the peptide, the storage modulus and loss modulus flipped (G’ > G″), indicating the formation of an integrated network hydrogel. After adding Ca2+ and applying ultrasound, the system transitioned into a nanoparticulate gel (both storage and loss moduli decreased but maintained G’ > G″, consistent with a gel-like state). We next quantified BAITs' injectability using a texture analyzer. The force required to inject the nanogel was below 2.8 N, substantially lower than the clinically acceptable threshold of 10 N (Fig. 1J) [22]. These favorable mechanical properties, combined with the nanoscale size of BAITs, ensure easy injectability for potential clinical applications and facilitate efficient DC uptake. Importantly, the nanogel formulation also enhances tissue retention, creating a stable local niche for DC accumulation and activation while preventing rapid antigen clearance.

To further evaluate stability, antigen retention within BAIT nanogels remained at 93.1% and 87.7% of the initial levels after 3 and 7 days at 4 °C, respectively (Fig. S1E), indicating good storage stability. In addition, in vivo imaging showed that BAITs exhibited prolonged retention at the injection site compared to free antigens. The fluorescence of free antigens declined rapidly to 16.4% within 6 h and became nearly undetectable by 12 h, whereas BAIT-treated sites maintained strong fluorescence for over 24 h (Fig. 1K and S1F). Together, these results demonstrate that BAIT nanogels are structurally well-defined, exhibit stable antigen incorporation, and possess favorable physicochemical and mechanical properties, supporting their application as a robust platform for DC-targeted immunotherapy.

BAITs enable efficient antigen uptake and digestion by DCs

The first step in initiating an antigen-specific immune response is recruiting DCs to the vaccine site. BAITs establish a local chemokine gradient through sustained release of CCL20, thereby promoting DC recruitment via the CCL20/CCR6 axis [23]. Consistent with this design, BAITs exhibited sustained CCL20 release kinetics, enabling the formation of a stable chemokine gradient over time (Fig. S1G). To assess the DC-recruiting capability of BAITs, we performed real-time transwell migration assays (Fig. 2A). BAITs or free antigens were placed in the lower chambers, and bone marrow-derived DCs were seeded into the upper chamber, separated by a porous membrane that allowed cell migration. As shown in Fig. 2B, DCs migrated significantly more toward BAITs than toward free antigens. Once recruited, DCs must internalize sufficient antigen to initiate effective antigen presentation [24,25]. However, in a TGFβ-rich postoperative environment, DC antigen uptake is limited (Fig. 2D and S2B). In our model, DC antigen uptake reached a maximum at an antigen concentration of 80 μg/mL, with no further increase beyond this threshold. Consistently, we observed that after antigen uptake, DCs upregulated immunostimulatory molecules, including MHC molecules (H2-D1, H2-L, H2-K1, H2-Aa, H2-Ab1, H2-Ea), co-stimulation molecules (Cd80, Cd86, Cd40, etc.) and proinflammatory cytokines (Il12, Ifna1, Ifna2), while downregulating immunosuppressive factors (Cd274, Ido1, etc.) (Fig. 2E and S2C). Notably, the expression of these activation markers plateaued at the same antigen concentration (80 μg/mL), consistent with the observed saturation in antigen uptake. Hence, under TGFβ exposure, DC antigen internalization hits a ceiling, which in turn constrains DC activation.

To determine why antigen uptake saturates under TGFβ, we examined the fate of internalized antigens. DCs were allowed to internalize fluorescent antigens, then were washed and incubated in fresh medium for 24 h (Fig. 2F). In the absence of TGFβ, the intracellular antigen signal nearly vanished over this chase period, indicating efficient antigen degradation. By contrast, DCs exposed to TGFβ retained strong antigen fluorescence after 24 h, indicating that TGFβ suppresses lysosomal antigen digestion. Poor antigen breakdown can lead to antigen accumulation inside DCs, which would impair their capacity to take up additional antigens. We next tested whether BAITs could relieve this block and enhance antigen digestion in DCs. Indeed, BAIT treatment greatly improved antigen uptake. After 6 h of incubation, DCs treated with BAITs showed a 4.4-fold higher intracellular antigen signal than DCs given free antigens (Fig. 2G). Flow cytometry confirmed this increased antigen uptake efficiency with BAITs (Fig. 2H). To directly assess antigen processing, we performed a sequential uptake experiment. DCs were first incubated with Cy5-antigens for 12 h, then washed and exposed to a second wave of Cy3-labeled antigens (either free or BAIT-formulated) for an additional 6 h. In DCs that received BAITs, the residual Cy5 signal from the first uptake was markedly lower than in DCs that received free antigens, indicating more complete digestion of the initially internalized antigens. Concurrently, BAIT-treated DCs showed a significantly higher Cy3 signal from the second uptake, reflecting an enhanced capacity to acquire new antigens (Fig. 2I and S2D). In contrast, DCs exposed to free antigens retained a strong Cy5 signal and took up little of the Cy3-antigens. These findings suggested that BAITs counteract TGFβ-induced suppression of lysosomal antigen processing, allowing DCs to fully digest internalized antigens and continuously uptake new ones.

BAITs restore lysosomal function in DCs via mTOR-mediated pSAP upregulation

Having observed that BAITs improve antigen digestion in DCs, we next explored the underlying mechanism. We pulsed bone marrow-derived DCs with Cy3-labeled antigens (either free or BAIT-formulated) and tracked antigen persistence in the endolysosomal compartment. After a 12-h uptake period in the presence of TGFβ, cells were washed, and intracellular fluorescence was monitored over time (Fig. 3A). DCs that internalized BAITs cleared the antigen far more efficiently than those given free antigens (Fig. 3B and C). By 6 h after the wash, BAIT-treated DCs had reduced their antigen fluorescence to 28.9% of the initial level, whereas DCs that took up free antigen still retained 90.5%. By 18 h, BAIT-treated DCs had nearly no detectable antigen signal, while antigen-treated DCs continued to exhibit strong fluorescence even at 24 h. Prosaposin (pSAP) is a lysosomal precursor protein essential for maintaining lysosomal integrity and function. Within lysosomes, pSAP is cleaved by cathepsins into saposins A–D, which are nonenzymatic cofactors required for sphingolipid degradation. Loss or deficiency of pSAP results in impaired saposin generation, defective sphingolipid catabolism, and consequently severe lysosomal dysfunction [26]. Surgical stress-induced TGFβ is thought to trigger this cascade by causing pSAP hyperglycosylation and misrouting out of DCs, leading to lysosomal pSAP deficiency and impaired antigen processing [8,16]. Consistent with this, we found that exogenous TGFβ markedly reduced pSAP expression in DCs, whereas neutralization of TGFβ with a blocking antibody fully restored pSAP levels and rescued MHC class Ⅰ and Ⅱ expression, confirming a direct inhibitory role of TGFβ in DC lysosomal competence and antigen presentation (Fig. S3). Since mTOR signaling is known to regulate pSAP expression [20], we investigated whether the arginine-enriched BAIT nanogel could restore DC lysosomal function by activating the mTOR pathway and elevating pSAP levels in DC lysosomes (Fig. 3D). To determine whether this effect is mediated by arginine release, we quantified intracellular free arginine and observed a significant, time-dependent increase following BAIT treatment (Fig. S4A). We then profiled the expression of 40 mTOR-pathway-related genes in DCs. BAIT-treated DCs showed significant upregulation of 33 out of 40 genes relative to antigen-treated DCs, indicating robust activation of the mTOR pathway (Fig. 3E). Among them, we found that genes of the Ragulator complex (Lamtor1–5), which are critical for mTORC1 activation and have been linked to pSAP expression [20], were also markedly elevated. Consistently, RT-qPCR analysis confirmed that BAIT-treated DCs had significantly higher Psap mRNA levels than DCs treated with antigens alone (Fig. 3F).

We next assessed pSAP protein levels within DC lysosomes via immunofluorescence staining. As shown in Fig. 3G and S4B, in control DCs (without TGFβ stimulation), confocal microscopy and quantitative analysis showed pSAP co-localized with the lysosomal marker LAMP-1, whereas TGFβ exposure dramatically reduced lysosomal pSAP staining in the model group. Adding free antigens did not restore lysosomal pSAP, whereas BAIT-treated DCs showed a strong restoration of pSAP within LAMP-1+ lysosomal compartments, approaching the levels in untreated control cells. Co-localization analysis confirmed the significant overlap of LAMP-1 and pSAP signals in BAIT-treated DCs (Fig. 3H), indicating that BAITs markedly enhanced pSAP accumulation within lysosomes. Western blotting further corroborated that total pSAP protein in TGFβ-exposed DCs was greatly reduced compared to the control group, but BAIT treatment restored pSAP to baseline levels (Fig. 3I and S4C). Consistently, the levels of single saposins (SAPs, cleavage products of pSAP) were likewise markedly increased by BAIT treatment. Importantly, pharmacological inhibition of mTOR abolished the BAIT-induced restoration of both pSAP and mature saposins, supporting the mechanistic requirement for mTOR signaling in BAIT-mediated activation of the pSAP-lysosome axis (Fig. 3J and S4D, E).

Because lysosomal proteolytic activity depends on an acidic pH [27], we used LysoSensor Green to evaluate lysosomal acidity after BAIT treatment. TGFβ exposure (model) significantly decreased lysosomal fluorescence compared to untreated DCs, indicating a more neutral (less acidic) lysosomal pH (Fig. 3K and S4F). In contrast, BAIT-treated DCs maintained bright LysoSensor fluorescence, suggesting that BAITs normalized lysosomal pH, markedly differing from the elevated pH observed in the model and antigen-only groups. Together, these results demonstrate that arginine-enriched BAITs restore lysosomal function in TGFβ-impaired DCs by activating the mTOR pathway and enhancing pSAP expression, thereby rejuvenating the cells’ antigen-processing capability.

BAITs enhance antigen-presenting capacity and maturation of DCs

With lysosomal function restored, we next examined whether BAIT treatment improves antigen presentation by DCs. DCs were first incubated with Cy3-antigens (free or BAIT) for 12 h, then washed and incubated for another 24 h with continued TGFβ exposure (Fig. 4A). We then stained the cells to detect MHC Ⅰ/Ⅱ-peptide complexes on the surface of DCs. BAIT-treated DCs showed substantially higher surface MHC Ⅰ levels and had almost no remaining intracellular Cy3-antigen signal (Fig. 4B and D), indicating efficient antigen processing and presentation on MHC Ⅰ. In contrast, DCs treated with free antigens retained substantial intracellular antigens and showed weaker MHC Ⅰ surface staining, consistent with impaired lysosomal function and inefficient antigen presentation. A similar trend was observed for MHC Ⅱ. BAIT-treated DCs displayed greater MHC Ⅱ surface localization and little intracellular antigen, whereas free antigen-treated DCs showed lower surface MHC Ⅱ and more antigen retention (Fig. 4C and E). Flow cytometry further validated that BAITs enhanced the antigen-presenting and maturation status of DCs. The proportion of MHC Ⅰ+ DCs increased from 29.7% (TGFβ-only model) to 50.6% with BAIT treatment, and MHC Ⅱ+ DCs increased from 29.2% to 51.1% (Fig. 4F and G). In addition, BAIT-treated DCs showed higher surface CD80 expression, a key marker of DC maturation (Fig. 4H and I). To profile broader immune changes, we performed RT-qPCR on DCs for genes related to immune activation and suppression. BAIT-treated DCs exhibited marked upregulation of genes associated with DC maturation, antigen processing and presentation, along with pronounced downregulation of immunosuppressive genes, compared to DCs treated with antigens alone (Fig. 4J). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that arginine-enriched BAITs activate the mTOR-pSAP pathway to reverse TGFβ-induced lysosome dysfunction, enabling DCs to efficiently process antigens and upregulate critical costimulatory and MHC molecules. This suggests that BAIT-treated DCs should be more effective at priming tumor-specific T cell responses.

DCs were pre-treated with PBS, free antigens, or BAITs for 24 h, then co-cultured with splenic T cells under TGFβ stimulation (Fig. 5A). BAIT-treated DCs induced significantly greater differentiation of naïve T cells into cytotoxic CD8+ T cells, compared to DCs treated with PBS or antigens alone (Fig. 5B and S5A). In parallel, T cells co-cultured with BAIT-primed DCs secreted higher levels of the key effector cytokines IFNγ and TNFα (Fig. 5C). We then evaluated the therapeutic potential of BAIT vaccination in a postoperative tumor setting. We established a murine postoperative lung cancer model (Fig. 5D). After primary tumor resection, mice were inoculated with a small number of LLC tumor cells at the surgery site to mimic residual disease, and then treated with a subcutaneous injection of either tumor lysate antigens alone or BAITs (formulated with the same lysates). Control mice received saline. Over a 16-day observation period, BAIT-treated mice showed significantly delayed and reduced tumor recurrence compared to saline-treated controls (Fig. 5E and F). Additionally, the average tumor weight at endpoint was markedly lower in the BAIT group than both the model and antigen-only groups, underscoring the superior therapeutic efficacy of BAITs. To determine if BAITs were counteracting surgery-induced immune suppression, we analyzed the immune composition of the tumor microenvironment. Flow cytometry revealed that BAIT-treated tumors had a much higher infiltration of antigen-presenting cells, with CD45+MHC Ⅰ+ cells increasing from 33.3% (model) to 53.7% in the BAIT group (Fig. 5G). BAIT treatment also significantly boosted effector T cell infiltration. Intratumoral CD8+ increased by 1.5-fold and CD4+ T cells by 1.4-fold relative to the model group (Fig. 5H and I). Notably, BAIT therapy caused no adverse effects, as treated mice maintained normal body weight and major organ weights (Fig. S5B–G). Taken together, these data demonstrate that BAIT vaccination after surgery attracts DCs and reverses TGFβ-induced immune dysfunction, leading to enhanced DC maturation and antigen presentation. These activated DCs then migrate to draining lymph nodes and prime robust tumor-specific T cell responses, ultimately driving strong T cell infiltration into tumors and potent postoperative antitumor immunity.

Optimizing BAITs via a clinical functional precision medicine approach

Encouraged by the efficacy of BAITs, we sought to further improve their therapeutic performance using a functional precision medicine (FPM) approach. Immunogenic cell death (ICD) has garnered attention in cancer therapy because dying tumor cells undergoing ICD release damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) that potently activate DCs [28,29]. We hypothesized that inducing ICD in tumor cells prior to lysate preparation could endow the BAIT antigens with endogenous adjuvants, thereby boosting vaccine immunogenicity. To test this, we triggered three cell death conditions with distinct dominant ICD-associated features in tumor cells (ferroptosis-dominant stress using cisplatin, necroptosis-dominant stress using citronellol, and apoptosis-dominant stress using doxorubicin), as illustrated in Fig. 6A. We first determined sub-lethal concentrations of each inducer that reliably caused ICD-relevant cellular stress and death (Fig. S6A). We then used SEM to visualize the characteristic morphological changes associated with each death mode (Fig. 6B). Untreated tumor cells exhibited intact membrane structures and no structural damage. Cisplatin-treated cells displayed ferroptosis-associated morphological changes, notably multiple circular membrane protrusions indicative of lipid peroxidation [30]. Citronellol-treated cells displayed necroptosis-associated features, including cellular detachment, swelling, and membrane disruption with variably sized pores [31]. To assess whether each inducer preferentially activated its expected cell death pathway, we examined representative molecular markers for ferroptosis, necroptosis, and apoptosis by Western blot (Fig. 6C and D). Cisplatin treatment reduced the expression of GPX4 (a key ferroptosis regulator) to ∼47.2% of the control level, consistent with induction of ferroptosis-dominant signaling activation [33]. Citronellol treatment greatly increased the level of phosphorylated RIP1 (p-RIP1) to ∼213.9% of control, confirming activation of the necroptosis-associated signaling [34]. Doxorubicin-treated cells showed a 2.1-fold increase in the pro-apoptotic protein BAX compared to untreated controls, with no significant change in GPX4 or p-RIP1, indicating apoptosis as the predominant death program under these conditions [35]. Together, these data indicate that while the induced cell death modalities are not mutually exclusive, each treatment condition is characterized by a dominant ICD-associated pathway capable of providing distinct immunostimulatory cues.

We next investigated whether these different ICD-associated death conditions enriched the tumor cells with DAMPs. One crucial DAMP in ICD is the exposure of calreticulin on the cell surface, which serves as an “eat me” signal to DCs [36]. Confocal laser scanning microscopy images revealed that doxorubicin-treated cells had a striking increase in surface calreticulin staining compared to other groups (Fig. 6E and S6B), indicating robust translocation of calreticulin to the cell membrane during immunogenic apoptosis. Cisplatin- and citronellol-treated cells also exhibited elevated calreticulin exposure relative to untreated cells, though to a lesser extent than doxorubicin. We also measured the transcript levels of various DAMPs (HMGB1, calreticulin, heat shock proteins, and S100 proteins) induced by each treatment [37]. All three ICD inducers significantly upregulated DAMP gene expression compared to untreated cells (Fig. 6F). Notably, doxorubicin (apoptosis) led to the highest increases in DAMP mRNAs (e.g., Hmgb1 up by 329.6%, Calr by 477.1%, Hsp60 by 346.9%, and S100a4 by 453.1%). These results indicate that apoptosis induced by doxorubicin in this context is highly immunogenic and generates a particularly DAMP-enriched tumor lysates.

We then formulated three optimized BAIT vaccines using the ICD-modified lysates (Fer-BAITs, ferroptosis-associated lysates; Nec-BAITs, necroptosis-associated lysates; Apo-BAITs, apoptosis-associated lysates) (Fig. 6G). All three formulations showed minimal cytotoxicity and favorable biocompatibility in vitro (Fig. S6C). We compared their ability to stimulate DC maturation. Among the variants, Apo-BAIT was most effective, inducing the highest upregulation of maturation markers on DCs within 24 h (Fig. 6H). Specifically, Apo-BAIT-treated DCs had 1.9-fold higher surface MHC Ⅰ and CD86 compared to untreated DCs, outperforming the increases seen with BAIT- (1.2-fold), Fer-BAIT- (1.2-fold), or Nec-BAIT-treated (1.1-fold) DCs. Similarly, Apo-BAITs expanded the fraction of MHC Ⅱ+CD80+ mature DCs by 1.8-fold over the model (untreated) group, versus 1.3-, 1.2-, and 1.2-fold, for BAITs, Fer-BAITs, and Nec-BAITs, respectively (Fig. 6I). Thus, incorporating immunogenically dying tumor cells into BAITs enhanced DC activation, with Apo-BAITs (immunogenic apoptosis-derived lysates) conferring the strongest effect. These data suggest that, in the context of ICD, using an apoptosis-derived lysates provides superior DC maturation signals, which may translate to improved T cell priming [38].

We next verified that the enhanced DC maturation by Apo-BAITs indeed leads to stronger T cell responses. DCs were treated with BAIT or each of the three ICD-BAIT formulations for 24 h (under TGFβ), then co-cultured with splenic T cells isolated from murine spleens. Flow cytometry showed that CD3+ splenic T cells stimulated by Apo-BAIT-primed DCs had the highest expression of CD8 and CD4 (Fig. 7A). Notably, the portion of CD8+CD4+ T cells rose from 2.9% in the control to 30.0% with Apo-BAITs, far exceeding the levels seen with the other formulations. To assess T cell function, we measured their antitumor activity. T cells activated by Apo-BAIT-treated DCs exhibited the strongest cytotoxic profile (Fig. 7B). These T cells expressed significantly higher levels of effector cytokine production (Ifng, Tnf, Lta, and Il2) and cytotoxic molecules (Lamp1, Prf1, Gzma, and Gzmb) compared to T cells activated by the other BAIT formulations. Finally, to determine if these T cells secreted soluble factors that can kill tumor cells, we collected T cell culture supernatants and applied them to LLC tumor cells (Fig. 7C). Supernatant from the Apo-BAIT group most effectively inhibited LLC cell proliferation over 48 h, whereas supernatants from the other groups had significantly weaker effects (Fig. 7D and S7). This indicates that Apo-BAIT-primed T cells release high levels of antitumor cytokines. Marked tumor suppression following surgical resection by Apo-BAIT treatment

We next evaluated the in vivo therapeutic efficacy of our optimized BAITs in a murine postoperative lung cancer model. Two days after surgical resection of the primary tumor, we re-inoculated 1 × 106 LLC cells at the surgical site to mimic residual tumor, then administered either BAITs, Fer-BAITs, Nec-BAITs, or Apo-BAITs subcutaneously (the model group received saline) (Fig. 8A). Apo-BAIT treatment produced the most dramatic suppression of tumor recurrence, achieving an 85.5% reduction in tumor weight compared to the saline-treated mice (Fig. 8B). BAITs, Fer-BAITs, and Nec-BAITs conferred only moderate tumor inhibition and were markedly less effective than Apo-BAITs. Tumor growth curves over the 16-day treatment period further confirmed that Apo-BAITs had a sustained and potent antitumor effect, whereas the other formulations showed only partial tumor growth delay (Fig. 8C). Histological examination reinforced these findings. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining of tumor sections from Apo-BAIT-treated mice revealed greatly reduced cellularity compared to other groups (Fig. 8D). Correspondingly, TUNEL staining indicated a significantly higher apoptotic index in Apo-BAIT-treated tumors (Fig. 8E and F), while immunofluorescent staining for Ki67 showed far fewer proliferating tumor cells in the Apo-BAIT group (Fig. 8G and H). (F, G) Serum biochemical parameters for liver (F) and kidney (G) functions (n = 3; n.s. is P > 0.05 by one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni post-hoc test). Data are presented as mean ± SD.

Safety was also assessed across the treatment groups. No treatment-related mortality or overt signs of toxicity were observed throughout the 16-day study, and all mice maintained stable body weights (Fig. 10D). Additionally, the weights of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) in treated mice showed no significant differences from controls, suggesting no gross organ toxicity (Fig. 10E). Serum biochemistry revealed no abnormalities in hepatic function markers (ALT, TBIL, ALB) or renal function indicators (BUN, CREA, UA) (Fig. 10F and G). Histological analysis (H&E staining) of major organs from BAIT-treated mice showed no pathological changes or evidence of cytokine storm (Fig. S8). These results demonstrated that all four BAIT formulations exhibited excellent biocompatibility and systemic safety in vivo. Overall, our results establish Apo-BAITs as a safe and highly effective platform for reprogramming the postoperative immune microenvironment, providing a personalized cancer vaccine strategy with strong translational potential.

Discussion

DC vaccines are among the earliest cancer immunotherapies approved by the FDA, but their clinical efficacy has been modest, especially in preventing postoperative tumor recurrence [39,40]. A major limitation is the disconnect between ex vivo preparation of these vaccines and the in vivo conditions they encounter [41,42]. Producing a DC vaccine involves multiple ex vivo procedures, including isolating a patient's monocytes, differentiating them into DCs, pulsing with tumor antigens to load MHC, and inducing maturation, before infusing the cells back into the patient [43]. Once reinfused, however, these DCs face a postoperative environment replete with immunosuppressive factors (e.g., TGFβ) that can undermine their function. For instance, surgical stress elevates TGFβ levels that disrupt DC lysosomal activity [8,16]. Since efficient antigen processing in lysosomes is a prerequisite for T cell priming [11,12], this disruption likely contributes to the limited efficacy of conventional DC vaccines in the postoperative setting.

To address these limitations, and building on our previous work on remodeling the in vivo milieu to facilitate immune cell recruitment [6,44], we developed an in vivo-generated DC vaccine (BAIT) that sidesteps ex vivo manipulation by empowering host DCs directly at the site of vaccination. After surgical tumor resection, a BAIT injection creates a local milieu that recruits DCs, delivers tumor antigens to them, and counteracts TGFβ-induced dysfunction. The arginine-enriched BAIT nanogel activates DC mTOR signaling via arginine metabolism, which upregulates pSAP and saposins in lysosomes and restores antigen-processing capacity. In this way, BAITs reprogram DCs in situ, enabling them to effectively process and present tumor antigens and thereby initiate robust T cell activation and antitumor immunity.

Another critical factor for vaccine efficacy is the antigen repertoire and immunogenicity of the tumor lysates. This can vary widely among patients and treatment regimens, influencing the strength of the immune response [45,46]. We addressed this variability by implementing a functional precision medicine approach: we tested BAITs formulated with tumor lysates obtained from different immunogenic cell death pathways. ICD modalities (such as ferroptosis, necroptosis, and apoptosis) provoke the release of DAMPs that heighten DC activation [28,29]. By incorporating these ICD-derived lysates, we aimed to boost the inherent immunostimulatory content of the vaccine. Indeed, our optimized Apo-BAIT, using an apoptosis-derived lysate, showed superior performance in activating DCs and suppressing tumor growth. This underscores the benefit of customizing vaccine content based on the most immunogenic form of tumor cell death for a given context.

Our in vivo DC vaccine platform (BAIT) addresses a key limitation of conventional DC vaccines by restoring DC lysosomal function during the critical postoperative period of immune suppression. By integrating DC recruitment, antigen delivery, and lysosomal activation in one system, BAITs effectively prime anti-tumor immunity when and where it is most needed. Furthermore, by coupling BAITs with functional precision medicine (through ICD-tailored lysates), we can personalize the vaccine to maximize efficacy for individual patients. Although this study used only the LLC lung cancer model, the mTOR-pSAP axis targeted by BAIT is a general feature of postoperative immune suppression. In addition, while mTOR activation was supported by transcriptional analysis and pharmacological inhibition, phosphorylation of canonical downstream targets (e.g., S6K or 4EBP1) was not directly assessed and will be examined in future studies to further strengthen mechanistic resolution. The tumor lysate component can be readily adapted from different tumor types, suggesting that the platform could be broadly applicable across solid tumors. Nonetheless, this strategy holds promise for improving postoperative cancer immunotherapy outcomes when translated to the clinical setting.

Conclusion

We have developed an in vivo-generated DC vaccine (BAIT) that integrates DC recruitment, antigen delivery, and immune activation into a single platform to overcome postoperative immune suppression caused by TGFβ-mediated DC dysfunction. When administered shortly after surgery, BAITs effectively recruit DCs via controlled CCL20 release. The recruited DCs internalize BAIT nanogels, which activate the mTOR-pSAP lysosomal pathway and restore cells’ antigen-processing function. These “recharged” DCs then present tumor-derived peptides on MHC Ⅰ and Ⅱ, initiating strong T cell priming and anti-tumor responses. To further enhance efficacy and enable personalized therapy, we created three advanced BAIT formulations using tumor lysates from cells undergoing ferroptosis, necroptosis, or apoptosis (Fer-BAITs, Nec-BAITs, and Apo-BAITs). Among these, Apo-BAITs elicited the highest DC maturation and T cell activation. Notably, subcutaneous Apo-BAIT administration after tumor resection resulted in significant tumor suppression and a reprogramming of the tumor immune microenvironment toward an anti-tumor state. Therefore, Apo-BAITs, especially when combined with a functional precision medicine approach, offer a promising personalized strategy for preventing postoperative tumor recurrence.

Cell lines: LLC cells were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). LLC cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and maintained in a 37 °C humidified incubator (Thermo, USA) with 5% CO2.

Isolation and culture of bone marrow-derived DCs: To prepare bone marrow-derived DCs, C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old, both sexes) were euthanized, and femurs and tibias were harvested. Bone marrow cells were flushed out with RPMI 1640 medium using a 26G needle, and the resulting cell suspension was filtered through 70-μm cell strainers (Corning) to remove debris. Cells were collected by centrifugation at 500×g for 5 min, subjected to red blood cell lysis for 3 min, and washed with PBS (7 × volume) to terminate lysis. After a centrifugation at 500×g for 5 min, 2 × 107 bone marrow cells were seeded into 10 cm tissue-culture dishes and cultured with GM-CSF (20 ng/mL) and IL4 (10 ng/mL) in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. After 7 days of culture at 37 °C and 5% CO2, non-adherent cells were collected, plated out, and subjected to subsequent experimental analysis. No mycoplasma contamination was detected in the cell culture.

Molecular dynamics simulations: Initial peptide conformations were generated via the PEP-FOLD4 web server (https://bioserv.rpbs.univ-paris-diderot.fr/services/PEP-FOLD4/) [47]. Molecular dynamics simulations were implemented in GROMACS 2021.6 using the CHARMM36 force field with TIP3P explicit solvent [48]. The system involved three identical peptides,which were embedded in a cubic solvation box extending 1.0 nm beyond the solute. Ionic conditions were established through stoichiometric addition of Ca2+ ions (5:1 M ratio relative to peptide) using the GROMACS genion module, with charge neutralization achieved by Cl− counterions. The system's total energy was minimized via steepest descent algorithm (STEEP). Subsequent equilibration involved the NVT ensemble for 100 ps at 300 K, followed by the NPT ensemble for 100 ps at 1 bar. Finally, 100 ns production MD simulations were performed on a supercomputing platform. Energy and trajectory analyses were conducted using GROMACS tools, PyMOL software (http://www.pymol.org), and VMD for advanced visualization and structural analysis.

Preparation and characterization of BAITs: LLC cells were harvested by trypsinization, washed with PBS, and collected by centrifugation at 500×g for 10 min. The cell pellets were resuspended in 0.25 × PBS and subjected to five freeze/thaw cycles (−80 °C/+37 °C) to obtain tumor antigens. 4 mg of tumor antigens, 20 μg of CCL20, and 2.5 mg of arginine-enriched self-assembling peptides were added into ultrapure water to a final volume of 0.5 mL and then incubated at 4 °C overnight to form an antigen hydrogel. Subsequently, the antigen complex was mixed with 0.5 mL CaCl2 solution (15 mM). After ultrasonication and extrusion through a 0.22 μm filter, BAITs were obtained. The size distribution of BAITs was measured by dynamic light scattering (DLS) using a Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern, UK).

For SEM, BAITs samples were submerged in 2.5% glutaraldehyde/phosphate buffer and washed with 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.0). Samples were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (30% to 100%) and dried, then sputter-coated with gold. SEM images were obtained with a Gemini 500 field-emission scanning electron microscope (Zeiss, Germany). For TEM characterization, 10 μL of BAIT sample was applied onto formvar/carbon-coated copper grids (300 mesh, Elektron Technology UK Ltd.). After 2 min of adsorption, the excess liquid was blotted with filter paper. Subsequently, the samples were negatively stained with 3 wt% phosphotungstic acid, blotted again, and allowed to air-dry. TEM images were obtained using a 120 kV TEM (HT7800, Hitachi, Japan). The rheological properties were analyzed using an advanced rheometer (Kinexus Lab+, Malvern, UK). Time sweeps (1% strain at 1 Hz) were performed to monitor changes in the storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) of BAITs.

Circular dichroism spectroscopy: The complexes of the arginine-enriched peptides and antigens were prepared with varying CaCl2 concentrations. Samples were placed in a micro cuvette with a 1 cm path length. Circular dichroism (CD) spectra were recorded in the wavelength range of 200–250 nm with a 1 nm step size, and data acquisition time was set to 0.5 s per point. The data were processed as previously described for the dimer.

In vivo stability assay: Cy3-antigens or Cy3-BAITs were administered to mice via subcutaneous injection. For in vivo imaging, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane, and fluorescent signals were monitored at designated time points using the NightOWL Ⅱ LB983 imaging system (NightOWL, Germany). All images were acquired with a 5 s exposure time and analyzed using indiGO software (Berthold Technologies).

Real-time cell migration test: DC migration was assessed in a CIM-16 well plate using an xCELLigence real-time cell analyzer (RTCA) instrument (ACEA Biosciences, San Diego, CA, USA). Each well was composed of an upper and a lower chamber separated by a microporous membrane with randomly distributed 8 μm pores. A dimensionless parameter was measured as a cell index, which was used to evaluate the ionic environment at an electrode/solution interface and integrate information on cell numbers. As illustrated in Fig. 2A, 165 μL medium containing PBS, antigens, or BAITs was loaded into the lower chamber of the CIM-16 plate. After assembling the upper chamber, 45 μL of prewarmed medium was added to it, and the plate was left for 30 min at room temperature for equilibration. Bone marrow-derived DCs were seeded in the upper chamber at 4 × 104 cells in 100 μL of medium per well, and then incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO2. The assembled plate was transferred to the RTCA data processing instrument, and data were collected. The instrument measures electrical impedance, which is reflected in the cell index. Rising cell impedance correlates with increasing numbers of DCs migrating and adhering to the lower chamber.

Cellular uptake: Antigens were fluorescently labeled with Cy3-NHS ester or Cy5-NHS ester according to established protocols [49], and excess dye was removed using 10 kDa molecular weight cutoff Amicon Ultra centrifugal filters (Sigma Millipore). Cy3-BAITs and Cy5-BAITs were then prepared from the corresponding labeled antigens. For internalization assays, DCs were stained with Hoechst 33342 for 15 min, washed three times with PBS, and seeded into 12-well glass-bottom plates pre-coated with poly-L-lysine at 5 × 105 cells per well. Cells were stimulated with TGFβ (2.5 ng/mL) for 48 h and then treated with Cy3-antigens or Cy3-BAITs and incubated for 2, 4, or 6 h under TGFβ stimulation. Antigen uptake was analyzed by confocal microscopy (FV3000, Olympus, Japan) and flow cytometry (CytoFLEX-S, Beckman Coulter, USA). To evaluate the efficiency of antigen uptake and digestion, DCs were stimulated with TGFβ (2.5 ng/mL) for 48 h and subsequently incubated with Cy5-antigens for 12 h in the continued presence of TGFβ. After replacing the culture medium, the cells were further exposed to either Cy3-antigens or Cy3-BAITs for 6 h under TGFβ. Intracellular fluorescence was visualized by a laser scanning confocal microscope (FV3000, Olympus, Japan).

Antigen digestion test: To evaluate antigen digestion in DCs, a time-course assay was conducted. DCs were stimulated with TGFβ (2.5 ng/mL) for 48 h, then incubated with Cy3-antigens for 12 h. Cells were washed, and fresh TGFβ-containing medium was added. Prior to imaging, the cells were incubated with LysoTracker Green (50 nM) at 37 °C for 30 min. Intracellular fluorescence intensity was subsequently quantified at predetermined time points (0, 6, 12, 18, and 24 h) using a laser scanning confocal microscope (FV3000, Olympus, Japan).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA): The secretion levels of TNFα and IFNγ in the supernatants from stimulated T cells were measured using ELISA kits according to the manufacturer's instructions. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured with a microplate spectrophotometer (Synergy H1, Bio-Tek, USA). Cytokine concentrations were calculated using a 4-parameter fit standard curve.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR): Total RNA was extracted from cell samples using TRIzol reagent (Accurate Biology), following the manufacturer's instructions. The RNA concentration and quality were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). For each sample, 1000 ng of total RNA was reverse transcribed using a Reverse Transcription Kit (Accurate Biology). Quantitative real-time PCR was carried out in a 10 μL reaction volume using the SYBR Green Premix RT-qPCR Kit (Accurate Biology) on a LightCycler 480 Ⅱ system (Roche, Switzerland). Gene expression levels were normalized to Actb. PCR cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30 s, and a final dissociation stage. Primer sequences are provided in Table S1.

Immunofluorescence analysis of pSAP: DCs were seeded on poly-L-lysine-coated glass coverslips placed in 12-well plates and cultured with TGFβ stimulation. After treatment, the cells were washed twice with PBS and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min. For intracellular protein staining, fixed cells were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 at room temperature for 10 min. Next, the cells were blocked with 2% BSA and incubated with rat CoraLite Plus 488 anti-mouse LAMP1 antibody (1:200) and rabbit anti-mouse pSAP (1:200) antibody overnight at 4 °C. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with a Cy5-conjugated anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:200) for 1 h at 37 °C. Nuclei were stained with DAPI for 15 min. Fluorescence images were acquired using a laser scanning confocal microscope (FV3000, Olympus, Japan).

Lysosomal pH imaging: LysoSensor Green was employed to monitor lysosomal pH changes under different treatments. DCs were seeded in culture dishes with thin glass bottoms coated with poly-L-lysine with TGFβ stimulation. Following treatment, DCs were incubated with 1 μM LysoSensor Green in culture medium for 30 min at 37 °C. Subsequently, the DCs were washed twice with PBS, replaced with fresh culture medium, and immediately subjected to imaging. Fluorescence images were acquired using a laser scanning confocal microscope (FV3000, Olympus, Japan).

Western blot analysis: Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail and PMSF. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit. Samples were denatured by boiling at 95 °C for 5 min in sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) sample buffer containing 5% β-mercaptoethanol. Equal amounts of total protein were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% (w/v) skim milk in PBS containing 0.1% Tween-20 for 1 h, followed by incubation with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence reagents and imaged with the Tanon 4600 Imaging System (Tanon, China).

Flow cytometry analysis: Single-cell suspensions were prepared and filtered through a cell strainer. For surface marker detection, cells were washed with cell staining buffer, and then incubated with the indicated fluorophore-conjugated antibodies for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark. Cells were washed and analyzed on a flow cytometer (CytoFLEX-S, Beckman Coulter, USA). Data were processed with FlowJo software version 7.6.2 (Tritar Inc., San Carlos, CA, USA). For tumors, the tissue samples were mechanically minced in RPMI-1640 medium and subjected to enzymatic dissociation. The resulting cell suspensions were filtered through 40 μm cell strainers and resuspended in complete RPMI-1640 medium. Cells were then centrifuged at 500×g for 5 min (2 times) to obtain a single-cell suspension. Surface marker expression was assessed by staining with the indicated antibodies at 4 °C, followed by analysis using a flow cytometer (CytoFLEX-S, Beckman Coulter, USA).

Immunofluorescence staining for MHC molecules in DCs: DCs were seeded on poly-L-lysine-coated glass coverslips placed in 12-well plates and cultured under TGFβ stimulation. After treatment with PBS, Cy3-antigens, or Cy3-BAITs for 12 h, the cells were washed twice with PBS and incubated with fresh TGFβ-containing medium for another 24 h. Then, the cells were washed twice with PBS and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min. For intracellular protein staining, the fixed cells were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 at room temperature for 10 min and blocked with 2% BSA. For MHC Ⅰ analysis, the cells were incubated with rat CoraLite Plus 488 anti-mouse LAMP1 antibody (1:200) and rabbit anti-mouse MHC Ⅰ antibody (1:100) overnight at 4 °C, followed by Cy5-conjugated anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:200) for 1 h at 37 °C. For MHC Ⅱ detection, the cells were incubated with rat CoraLite Plus 488 anti-mouse LAMP1 antibody (1:200) and rat CoraLite Plus 647 anti-mouse MHC Ⅱ antibody (1:200) overnight at 4 °C. Nuclei were stained with DAPI for 15 min. Fluorescence images were acquired using a laser scanning confocal microscope (FV3000, Olympus, Japan).

SEM analysis of treated LLC cells: To determine the morphology of treated LLC cells, the cells were submerged in 2.5% glutaraldehyde and washed with 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) for 10 min three times. The samples were then dehydrated in a graded ethanol series (30%, 50%, 70%, 80%, and 95%) for 15 min at each step, followed by three washes in 100% ethanol for 15 min each. Finally, the samples were critical point dried using CO2 and sputtered with 30 nm gold. SEM images were obtained on a Gemini 500 field emission scanning electron microscope (Zeiss, Germany).

Determination of surface-exposed calreticulin: LLC cells seeded on poly-L-lysine-coated glass coverslips in 12-well plates were treated with PBS, cisplatin (240 μM), citronellol (1200 μM), or doxorubicin (1.5 μM) for 24 h. Then, the cells were washed twice with PBS and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min. After fixation, cells were blocked with 2% BSA and incubated with primary anti-calreticulin antibody (1:50) overnight at 4 °C. Subsequently, cells were incubated with a Cy3-conjugated secondary antibody (1:200) for 1 h at 37 °C. Nuclei and cell membranes were stained with DAPI and DiD, respectively, for 15 min. Fluorescence images were acquired using a laser scanning confocal microscope (FV3000, Olympus, Japan).

Cell viability analysis: For cell viability analysis, cells were seeded into 96-well plates at 1 × 104 cells per well. After cell attachment, a series of concentrations of BAITs, Fer-BAITs, Nec-BAITs, and Apo-BAITs were added. Following a 24 h incubation, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well. Plates were incubated for 4 h at 37 °C, and the absorbance at 450 nm was detected using a microplate spectrophotometer (Synergy H1, Bio-Tek) to determine cell viability.

In vivo therapeutic efficacy against postoperative tumor recurrence: LLC tumor-bearing C57BL/6 mice (tumor volume ≈ 50 mm3) were subjected to complete tumor resection. Two days after surgery, mice were subcutaneously rechallenged with 1 × 106 LLC cells to establish a postoperative tumor recurrence model. Then, the mice were randomly divided into five groups and treated with subcutaneous injections of BAITs, Fer-BAITs, Nec-BAITs, or Apo-BAITs prepared from the resected tumors, while the model group received an equal volume of saline as a control. Tumor volume and body weight were monitored every other day. Tumor volume was calculated using the formula: L × B2/2, where L is the longest diameter and B the shortest diameter, measured with a caliper. At the study endpoint, mice were euthanized, and tumors were excised, photographed, and weighed.

Histological analysis: Tumors and organs from mice in each group were harvested, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h, and embedded in paraffin. The embedded tissues were then sectioned at a thickness of 8 μm and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). All stained sections were examined and imaged using a SLIDEVIEW VS200 slide scanner (Olympus, Japan).

Immunofluorescence assay: Immunofluorescence was conducted on paraffin-embedded tumor sections. After deparaffinization and rehydration, the slides were incubated with primary antibodies against Ki67 (1:300) or CD3 (1:500) at 4 °C overnight. Afterwards, the tumor sections were washed and incubated with FITC-conjugated anti-mouse secondary antibodies (1:100) or Cy5-conjugated anti-mouse secondary antibodies (1:100) in the dark. Nuclei were stained with DAPI (1 μg/mL). Immunofluorescence images were captured on an Olympus FV3000 confocal microscope.

Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) assay: Apoptotic cells in tumor tissues were detected using a TUNEL assay kit. The staining was performed according to the manufacturer's instructions. Stained sections were imaged using an EVOS M7000 Auto Cell Imaging System (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Serum biochemical analysis: Blood was collected from euthanized mice through the orbital venous plexus (approximately 1 mL per mouse). Samples were allowed to clot for 15–30 min at room temperature and then centrifuged at 3000×g for 20 min to separate serum. Serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT), total bilirubin (TBIL), and albumin (ALB) were measured to evaluate liver function. Blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine (CREA), and uric acid (UA) were measured to assess the kidney function, using an automatic biochemical analyzer (Hitachi, Japan).

Statistical analysis: Data were analyzed using Prism 9.0 software (GraphPad Software). Comparisons were made using two-tailed Student's t-test or one-way/two-way ANOVA with Bonferroni post-hoc test, as appropriate. Data were approximately normally distributed, and variance was similar between the groups. Statistical significance is indicated as n.s. P > 0.05, ∗P < 0.05, ∗∗P < 0.01, ∗∗∗P < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001.
