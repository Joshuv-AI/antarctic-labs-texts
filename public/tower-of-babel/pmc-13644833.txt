Fasting proinsulin as a predictor of future glucose tolerance deterioration in Japanese adults
Authors: Nobuhiro Kajihara, Tomonori Tsuyama, Kazuki Fukuda, Ai Maekawa, Eiichi Araki, Naoto Kubota, Kazuya Yamagata
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAims/IntroductionProinsulin is associated with β‐cell stress and has been linked to subsequent deterioration in glucose metabolism in Western populations, but its predictive value in Asian populations remains unclear. We investigated whether proinsulin is associated with future glucose tolerance deterioration in community‐dwelling Japanese adults.Materials and MethodsOf 160 individuals who underwent a 75‐g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) in 2020–2021, 88 completed a follow‐up OGTT in 2024–2025. Fasting proinsulin concentrations were measured using stored baseline serum samples from 84 participants. Associations with glucose tolerance deterioration were examined using Firth's penalized logistic regression, and discriminatory performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis.ResultsDuring the 4‐year follow‐up, glucose tolerance deteriorated in 16 participants. Fasting proinsulin concentrations were higher in progressors than in non‐progressors (5.8 vs. 3.6 pmol/L; P = 0.009). Each 1‐standard deviation increase in fasting proinsulin was associated with glucose tolerance deterioration (odds ratio 2.11, 95% confidence interval 1.31–3.72; P = 0.002). In exploratory ROC analysis, the area under the curve was 0.710, and a cutoff of 5.1 pmol/L yielded 68.8% sensitivity and 83.8% specificity. Participants with elevated fasting proinsulin (≥ 5.1 pmol/L) and a low disposition index (≤ 1.27) had the highest odds of deterioration.ConclusionsFasting proinsulin may help identify Japanese adults at risk of future glucose tolerance deterioration, particularly when combined with indices of β‐cell compensation.

INTRODUCTION

Type 2 diabetes is a growing global health burden, with more than 800 million adults worldwide reported to be living with the disease. 1 Because lifestyle modification and pharmacological intervention can prevent or delay type 2 diabetes, 2 identifying individuals at high risk is essential for implementing targeted prevention programs.

Proinsulin, the precursor of mature insulin and C‐peptide, has lower metabolic activity than mature insulin. 3 , 4 Proinsulin concentrations increase under conditions of greater β‐cell secretory demand, such as insulin resistance and hyperglycemia, whereas the fasting proinsulin‐to‐insulin ratio reflects β‐cell dysfunction and is elevated in type 2 diabetes. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 Prospective studies in Western populations have linked elevated fasting proinsulin concentrations and/or an increased fasting proinsulin‐to‐insulin ratio to subsequent deterioration in glucose tolerance. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 However, insulin resistance and impaired insulin secretion, the two major pathophysiological mechanisms underlying type 2 diabetes, appear to contribute differently in Western and Asian populations 14 , 15 ; therefore, the predictive value of proinsulin requires specific evaluation in Asian populations. In the present study, we examined whether proinsulin and related indices were associated with glucose tolerance deterioration in Japanese individuals. We also assessed whether fasting proinsulin concentrations were related to insulin resistance and whether an exploratory fasting proinsulin threshold could discriminate glucose tolerance deterioration during a 4‐year follow‐up in community‐dwelling Japanese adults.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and participants

This prospective study was conducted as a follow‐up investigation of the Kumamoto Koshi Study. 16 At baseline, in fiscal years 2020–2021, 160 residents of Koshi City, a rural area in Kumamoto Prefecture, Japan, underwent a 75‐g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT). Two participants were diagnosed with type 2 diabetes based on OGTT results and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) levels and were excluded, leaving 158 individuals eligible for follow‐up.

For the follow‐up survey, conducted in fiscal years 2024–2025, invitation letters were mailed to all eligible participants. Of the 158 eligible individuals, 70 either declined participation or could not be contacted, and 88 underwent a repeat 75‐g OGTT. Of the 88 participants who completed the follow‐up OGTT, three were excluded because they had diabetes mellitus at both baseline and follow‐up and were therefore unsuitable for evaluating glucose tolerance deterioration. Consequently, 85 participants were included in the final analysis (Figure 1).

Changes in glucose tolerance status from baseline to follow‐up. The remaining 85 participants were classified as non‐progressors (n = 69, 81.2%) or progressors (n = 16, 18.8%) according to changes in glucose tolerance status from baseline to follow‐up. Before undergoing the follow‐up OGTT, participants were asked to self‐report any current regular medications. One of the authors (N.K.) subsequently confirmed that no participants were taking medications that could affect glucose metabolism, insulin sensitivity, or insulin secretion, such as oral hypoglycemic agents or corticosteroids.

The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The baseline survey and proinsulin measurement using stored serum samples were approved by the Ethics Committee of Kumamoto University (approval number: Rinri 2602). The follow‐up survey was approved by the Human Genome and Gene Analysis Research Ethics Committee of Kumamoto University (approval number: Genome 570). All participants provided written informed consent.

Biochemical measurements and calculations

At the follow‐up examination, a 75‐g OGTT was performed after an overnight fast of 10–14 h. Blood samples were collected at 0, 30, 60, and 120 min after glucose loading. Participants were classified as having normal glucose tolerance (NGT), isolated impaired fasting glucose (IFG), impaired glucose tolerance (IGT), or diabetes mellitus (DM) according to World Health Organization criteria, based on plasma glucose levels. 17 Plasma glucose, HbA1c, and insulin concentrations were measured using GLU‐HL (Serotec, Hokkaido, Japan), Determiner‐L HbA1c (Canon Medical Diagnostics, Tokyo, Japan), and ARCHITECT Insulin (Abbott Japan, Tokyo, Japan), respectively. All measurements were performed at Falco Biosystems (Kyoto, Japan). HOMA‐IR (homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance), the Matsuda index, HOMA‐β (homeostasis model assessment of β‐cell function), the insulinogenic index, and the disposition index were calculated as described previously. 16 Serum proinsulin levels in the baseline Kumamoto Koshi Study cohort were measured at the Kumamoto University laboratory using a Human Proinsulin ELISA Kit (Mercodia; product code 10–1118‐01; Mercodia AB, Uppsala, Sweden). Proinsulin could not be measured in one participant because no residual serum sample was available; this participant remained classified as having NGT at follow‐up. Therefore, analyses involving proinsulin and proinsulin‐related indices included 84 participants. The antibody used in this assay exhibits no cross‐reactivity with human insulin or C‐peptide and measures total proinsulin, including des 31–32, split 32–33, des 64–65, and split 65–66 proinsulin forms.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are summarized as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) or the median [interquartile range (IQR)], whereas categorical variables are reported as counts and percentages. Because the number of events was small, associations between fasting proinsulin levels and outcomes were assessed using Firth's penalized maximum‐likelihood logistic regression. Odds ratios (ORs) are reported per 1‐SD increase in fasting proinsulin, and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and P values were calculated using the profile penalized‐likelihood method.

Between‐group comparisons were performed using the two‐tailed Student's t‐test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and Fisher's exact test for categorical variables. A two‐sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The discriminatory performance of each parameter for glucose tolerance deterioration was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) was used to assess how well each continuous variable discriminated worsening glucose tolerance. Exploratory cutoff values and their corresponding sensitivity and specificity were determined using the Youden index.

Spearman's rank correlation analysis was used to evaluate associations between baseline fasting proinsulin concentrations and baseline metabolic parameters and the 4‐year change in the disposition index (calculated as the follow‐up value minus the baseline value). In cutoff‐based analyses, crude ORs and 95% CIs were calculated using the reference category specified in the table; the overall P value was calculated using Fisher's exact test.

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R version 4.1.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria); the logistf package was used for penalized logistic regression analyses.

RESULTS

Baseline and follow‐up glucose tolerance status

At baseline, 65, 15, 1, and 7 of the 88 participants were classified as having NGT, IGT, IFG, and DM, respectively (Figure 1). As described in the Materials and Methods section, three participants who had DM at both baseline and follow‐up were excluded from the analysis. During the 4‐year follow‐up, glucose tolerance did not deteriorate in 69 (81.2%) of the remaining 85 participants, whereas it deteriorated in 16 (18.8%) participants (NGT to IGT, n = 10; NGT to DM, n = 2; IGT to DM, n = 4) (Figure 1).

Proinsulin concentrations were also significantly higher in progressors than in non‐progressors at fasting (5.8 vs. 3.6 pmol/L; P = 0.009) and 60‐min (36.2 vs. 26.6 pmol/L; P = 0.038). Previous prospective studies have associated the proinsulin‐to‐insulin ratio with subsequent development of type 2 diabetes. 10 , 18 In the present cohort, however, the proinsulin‐to‐insulin ratio did not differ significantly between groups.

Table 2 presents the logistic regression analyses of glucose tolerance deterioration. In the unadjusted model, fasting proinsulin concentration was significantly associated with glucose tolerance deterioration (OR 2.11; 95% CI 1.31–3.72; P = 0.002). To evaluate the robustness of this association, we performed additional models in which fasting proinsulin was adjusted for each covariate separately. Fasting proinsulin remained significantly associated with glucose tolerance deterioration after adjustment for age, sex, BMI, HbA1c, or fasting insulin. However, the association was attenuated and was no longer statistically significant after adjustment for fasting plasma glucose or the disposition index (Table 2). Because the number of progressors was limited (n = 16), multivariable models including two or more covariates were not fitted. It remains unclear whether the attenuation of the association after adjustment for fasting plasma glucose reflects limited statistical power or true confounding. These findings should therefore be interpreted with caution and considered exploratory.

Previous studies have associated both increased insulin resistance and impaired insulin secretion with the development of type 2 diabetes. 19 , 20 , 21 Consistent with these findings, HOMA‐IR, an index of insulin resistance, was significantly higher in progressors than in non‐progressors (P = 0.022), whereas the Matsuda index, an index of insulin sensitivity, was significantly lower in progressors (P = 0.003). Although HOMA‐β and the insulinogenic index did not differ significantly between groups, the disposition index, which reflects the ability of β‐cells to compensate for reduced insulin sensitivity by increasing insulin secretion, was significantly lower in progressors than in non‐progressors (P = 0.003) (Table 1).

Discriminatory performance of fasting proinsulin concentration for glucose tolerance deterioration

We next evaluated the discriminatory performance of fasting proinsulin concentration using ROC curve analysis (Table 3). Previous studies have demonstrated the utility of 60‐min and 120‐min plasma glucose concentrations during the 75‐g OGTT and of indices of insulin secretion and insulin resistance for identifying individuals at risk of developing type 2 diabetes. 22 , 23 The AUCs for 60‐min and 120‐min plasma glucose concentrations were 0.776 and 0.653, respectively. Fasting proinsulin concentration yielded an AUC of 0.710, which was higher than those for 120‐min plasma glucose concentration (0.653), HOMA‐IR (0.691), HOMA‐β (0.469), and the insulinogenic index (0.530). An exploratory fasting proinsulin cutoff of 5.1 pmol/L provided 68.8% sensitivity and 83.8% specificity. These findings suggest that fasting proinsulin may have relatively high specificity for discriminating glucose tolerance deterioration in this cohort.

Association between proinsulin and conversion to diabetes

We next investigated whether proinsulin was associated with conversion to DM. Because all participants who developed a diabetic pattern on the 75‐g OGTT had either NGT (n = 2) or IGT (n = 4) at baseline, we separately analyzed participants with baseline NGT or IGT (Table 4). Compared with non‐converters (NGT to NGT, n = 53; IGT to NGT, n = 6; IGT to IGT, n = 5), converters to DM (NGT to DM, n = 2; IGT to DM, n = 4) were older and had higher HbA1c levels, whereas BMI was similar between groups. Fasting, 30‐min, 60‐min, and 120‐min plasma glucose concentrations during the OGTT were significantly higher in converters than in non‐converters. Fasting (3.5 vs. 9.7 pmol/L; P = 0.018) and 120‐min (32.7 vs. 62.4 pmol/L; P = 0.006) proinsulin concentrations were significantly higher in converters than in non‐converters. The proinsulin‐to‐insulin ratio was also significantly higher in converters at fasting (12.4% vs. 17.8%; P = 0.037) and 30‐min (6.5% vs. 9.4%; P = 0.043).

Together, these findings suggest that elevated fasting proinsulin concentrations are associated with subsequent glucose tolerance deterioration and progression to type 2 diabetes in Japanese individuals.

Combined association of fasting proinsulin and disposition index with glucose tolerance deterioration

Previous studies have demonstrated that fasting proinsulin levels are elevated under conditions of increased β‐cell secretory demand and serve as a marker of insulin resistance in Western populations. 8 , 24 Consistent with these findings, fasting proinsulin concentrations were strongly associated with indices of insulin resistance, including the Matsuda index (ρ = −0.678) and HOMA‐IR (ρ = 0.664). By contrast, associations with indices of insulin secretion were weaker, including the insulinogenic index (ρ = 0.169), HOMA‐β (ρ = 0.313), and the disposition index (ρ = −0.430) (Table 6). Although an inverse correlation was observed between fasting proinsulin levels and the disposition index at baseline, baseline fasting proinsulin was not significantly associated with changes in the disposition index over the 4‐year follow‐up period (Table 6). These observations suggest that fasting proinsulin primarily reflects insulin resistance in this Japanese cohort, while also reflecting the level of β‐cell compensation at baseline rather than subsequent changes in β‐cell function.

Finally, we investigated whether participants with both elevated fasting proinsulin levels and a low disposition index were at the highest odds of glucose tolerance deterioration during the 4‐year follow‐up period. Elevated fasting proinsulin levels and a low disposition index were defined as ≥ 5.1 pmol/L and ≤ 1.27, respectively, based on the exploratory cutoff values presented in Table 3. Participants with low fasting proinsulin levels and a high disposition index served as the reference group (Table 7). Compared with the reference group, participants with elevated fasting proinsulin levels and a high disposition index had higher odds of glucose tolerance deterioration (OR 7.86; 95% CI 1.60–38.67). The odds were highest among participants with both elevated fasting proinsulin levels and a low disposition index (OR 24.06; 95% CI 4.89–118.42) (Table 7). Thus, the combination of fasting proinsulin concentration and the disposition index may help identify Japanese adults at higher risk of future glucose tolerance deterioration.

DISCUSSION

Both insulin resistance and reduced insulin secretory capacity predict glucose tolerance deterioration. 25 Insulin resistance can be quantified using the euglycemic–hyperinsulinemic clamp technique or frequently sampled intravenous or oral glucose tolerance tests; however, these methods are costly, labor‐intensive, and impractical for large‐scale clinical and epidemiological studies. Hyperproinsulinemia is thought to result from the secretion of immature proinsulin‐rich granules by β‐cells in response to the increased insulin demand associated with insulin resistance and may therefore serve as an indicator of β‐cell stress. 24 , 26 , 27 Type 2 diabetes has also been classified into eight clusters, one of which is characterized by elevated fasting proinsulin levels. 28 In this study, fasting proinsulin concentration was strongly associated with insulin resistance, and elevated fasting proinsulin levels (≥ 5.1 pmol/L) were associated with subsequent glucose tolerance deterioration in a Japanese cohort. These findings suggest that fasting hyperproinsulinemia may serve as a practical marker of insulin resistance and subsequent glucose tolerance deterioration, although it cannot yet replace more complex clinical assessments.

We also found that elevated fasting proinsulin and a low disposition index were jointly associated with glucose tolerance deterioration. Fasting proinsulin concentrations were inversely correlated with the baseline disposition index (Table 6). In addition, when both variables were included in the same model, the association between fasting proinsulin and glucose tolerance deterioration was attenuated and was no longer statistically significant (Tables 2 and 5). Therefore, we do not claim that fasting proinsulin independently predicts glucose tolerance deterioration. Instead, our findings suggest that fasting proinsulin and the disposition index may provide complementary information for risk stratification. Among participants with a high disposition index (>1.27), glucose tolerance deteriorated in 4 of 11 participants (36.4%) with elevated fasting proinsulin (≥ 5.1 pmol/L), compared with 4 of 59 participants (6.8%) with lower fasting proinsulin, corresponding to an OR of 7.86 (95% CI 1.60–38.67; P = 0.018). Thus, fasting proinsulin may identify individuals at increased risk who might not be identified by the disposition index alone. Although the difference in the proportion of progressors between the high‐proinsulin/high‐disposition‐index group and the high‐proinsulin/low‐disposition‐index group did not reach statistical significance (36.4% [4 of 11] vs. 63.6% [7 of 11]; P = 0.395, Fisher's exact test), the proportion of progressors was highest among participants with both elevated fasting proinsulin and a low disposition index, with an OR of 24.06 (95% CI, 4.89–118.42) compared with the reference group (Table 7). Because cutoffs were derived from the same dataset and the number of events was small, these category‐based findings should be considered exploratory. Nevertheless, because proinsulin can be quantified from a single fasting blood sample, elevated fasting proinsulin may represent a practical marker for identifying individuals who may warrant further assessment of β‐cell function using an OGTT. This possibility warrants confirmation in larger, independent cohorts.

An elevated proinsulin‐to‐insulin ratio has been reported to be a marker of β‐cell dysfunction and to be predictive of the development of type 2 diabetes in Western populations. 10 , 18 , 29 Consistent with these findings, the proinsulin‐to‐insulin ratio differed significantly between participants who converted to DM and those who did not (Table 4). However, the ratio did not differ significantly between progressors (NGT to IGT, NGT to DM, and IGT to DM) and non‐progressors (Table 1). The reasons for the lack of a significant difference between these groups remain unclear; however, the fact that most progressors developed IGT (62.5%) rather than diabetes (37.5%) may partly explain this observation. The proinsulin‐to‐C‐peptide ratio has been reported to be a stronger predictor of diabetes than the proinsulin‐to‐insulin ratio in Western populations, 30 suggesting that the choice of denominator may influence the predictive value of proinsulin‐based indices. Differences in proinsulin assay methods and cross‐reactivity with different proinsulin forms may also contribute to discrepancies among studies. Further studies are warranted to investigate the predictive value of proinsulin‐based indices for glucose tolerance deterioration in Japanese populations.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was small, limiting statistical power for some analyses and reducing the precision of estimates of the association between fasting proinsulin and glucose tolerance deterioration. Second, 32,33‐split proinsulin is the predominant proinsulin form contributing to hyperproinsulinemia in diabetes. 12 However, specific proinsulin forms were not measured in this study. Third, fatty acids regulate proinsulin processing, 31 but free fatty acid concentrations were not measured. Fourth, medication use was assessed based on participants' self‐reports rather than being verified against prescription records. Therefore, the unreported use of medications that could affect insulin sensitivity or insulin secretion cannot be completely excluded. Fifth, the present study was underpowered to determine whether fasting proinsulin predicts glucose tolerance deterioration independently of the disposition index. Finally, the reproducibility of the 75‐g OGTT is limited, 32 however, its reproducibility was not assessed in this study.

Despite these limitations, this prospective study showed that fasting proinsulin was associated with subsequent glucose tolerance deterioration in community‐dwelling Japanese adults. Fasting proinsulin may complement conventional glycemic markers for risk stratification, particularly when combined with measures of β‐cell compensation, such as the disposition index. Larger studies in independent cohorts are needed to validate the clinical utility of fasting proinsulin and determine whether it can support early identification of individuals at risk of glucose tolerance deterioration and type 2 diabetes.
