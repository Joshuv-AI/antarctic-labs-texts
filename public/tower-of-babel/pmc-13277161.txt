Cognitive and emotional traits as mediators in the relationship between family factors and obsessive-compulsive disorder in adolescents
Authors: Meiying Lei, Miuyu Yu, Qinji Su, Yongmei Jiang, Congwen Huang, Fangjie Wen, Jiatong Li, Zhikun Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundObsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) is a multifaceted psychiatric disease requiring more in-depth mechanism research to improve treatment outcomes, with family-related variables playing a crucial role, particularly in adolescent populations.MethodsA total of 113 adolescents with OCD were included in this study. All patients were assessed by the Yale-Brown Obsessive-Compulsive Scale (Y-BOCS), Family Environment Scale (FES), Parenting Style Assessment Scale (EMBU), Obsessive Beliefs Questionnaire (OBQ-44) and State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI). The correlation between family factors (dysfunctional family environment and negative parent rearing), cognitive traits (obsessive beliefs), emotional traits (anxiety traits), and obsessive-compulsive symptoms (OCS) were analyzed. Moreover, the mediating effect of cognitive and emotional traits between family factors and OCS was explored. Finally, 31 parents of patients and 20 non-clinical adolescents and their parents completed the FES, EMBU, OBQ-44, and STAI.ResultsThe results showed that dysfunctional family environment, negative parent rearing, obsessive beliefs and anxiety traits were positively correlated with OCS in adolescents with OCD. Parallel mediation analyses revealed that obsessive beliefs fully mediated the relationship between family factors and OCS, whereas anxiety traits did not show a significant unique mediating effect after controlling for obsessive beliefs. Compared with parents of non-clinical adolescents, parents of OCD patients showed significant obsessive beliefs and anxiety traits.ConclusionCognitive traits (obsessive beliefs), but not emotional traits (anxiety traits), served as a unique mediating mechanism between family factors and OCS. Both cognitive and emotional traits demonstrated familial aggregation. These findings provide an empirical basis for family-based interventions targeting obsessive beliefs in adolescents with OCD.Keywords: Obsessive-compulsive disorder, Adolescent, Family factors, Obsessive beliefs, Anxiety traits

Introduction

Obsessive-compulsive disorder is a complex and chronic disabling psychiatric disorder characterized by intrusive thoughts and compulsive behaviors, imposing significant distress and functional impairment on patients and their families. The lifetime prevalence is approximately 2–3%, with higher rates in children and adolescents [1]. Pharmacological and psychotherapeutic are the primary therapeutic approaches for managing OCD, but the majority of patients derive limited benefit from these interventions, with only 60–70% experiencing clinically meaningful improvements. Moreover, patients showing a response to conventional therapy reported persistent residual symptoms [2]. Research focusing on adolescent cases of OCD resistant to cognitive behavioral therapy and/or drug therapy has reported that family-related variables, including the family environment, parenting attitudes, and parental psychological state, significantly influence the onset and progression of the disease [3] and can predict the response to treatment [4]. Furthermore, family-based psychotherapy has been shown effective for OCD. Therefore, further research on the family-related variables of OCD in adolescents holds great practical implications.

The influence of family environment and parents’ rearing style on adolescent OCD

As the primary growth environment of adolescents, the family exhibits a more pronounced relationship and interaction with adolescent OCD compared to adult patients [5]. Adolescents with OCD reported higher family control, overprotection, anxious parenting, rejection, and lower emotional warmth, and parenting attitudes involving over-intervention have also been linked to adolescent OCD [6]. Moreover, for adolescents with moderate to severe OCD, more rejection and less emotional warmth were associated with severity of symptoms and functional impairment [7]. Family cohesion and family intimacy were significantly associated with OCD and seemed to be potential protective factors. However, the specific ways in which these family-related factors affect OCD, along with the extent and mechanisms of such influence, remain not fully understood.

The influence of family factors also plays a significant role in the efficacy and prognosis of adolescent OCD. Despite initial relief following first-line treatment, the persistence of dysfunctional parental rearing style and family environment may result in symptom recurrence and treatment failure. Contributing factors include heightened family adaptability, increased denial and rejection, parental over-protection or control, insufficient emotional support and intimacy within the family, and the presence of anxiety, obsessive-compulsive, and depressive symptoms of family members [8, 9]. Children from families with lower levels of parental blame, less family conflict, and higher family cohesion at the start of treatment showed more positive responses to family-based cognitive behavioral therapy compared to those from families with higher levels of dysfunction [10]. This highlights the importance of considering family-related factors when addressing adolescent OCD and developing treatment strategies aimed at modifying these factors.

The influence of family members’ mental state on adolescent OCD

Pedigree studies revealed that the risk of developing OCD is approximately 10–20% among first-degree relatives of patients, which represents a 5- to 10-fold increase compared to the general population [11]. The presence of a familial history of mental illness has been linked to unfavorable treatment outcomes [12]. Moreover, certain characteristics commonly observed in nonpsychiatric parents of patients, such as excessive perfectionism, meticulous attention to cleanliness and detail, avoidance of harm, low tolerance for pain, and imposing excessive responsibility on children, are associated with obsessive-compulsive symptoms in their children [3]. Parents with high anxiety or depression often have a lower tolerance for their child’s distress. When a child with OCD experiences the anxiety triggered by exposure and response prevention therapy, the parent’s own distress escalates. To alleviate their own discomfort and the child’s immediate crying or panic, they are likely to interrupt the exposure or give reassurance, thereby preventing the child from learning that the anxiety will pass on its own [4, 13].

The influence of cognitive and emotional traits on adolescent OCD

The cognitive and emotional characteristics play essential roles in the pathogenesis and maintenance of OCD. Obsessive beliefs are the core cognitive factor of adolescent OCD, which mainly includes three dimensions: excessive responsibility/threat assessment, perfectionism/uncertainty intolerance, and thinking importance/control belief [14, 15]. A prospective cohort study (n = 754) showed that higher perfectionism/ uncertainty intolerance scores at baseline were associated with more severe OCD symptoms in adolescents 1 year later, and threat assessment scores were also predictive [16]. Clinical studies further found that the scores of perfectionism/uncertainty intolerance dimension in adolescents with OCD were significantly higher than those in patients with anxiety disorder and healthy controls [14].

The presence of anxiety traits, characterized by an internal affective vulnerability, serves as a fundamental feature of OCD [17]. Individuals with high levels of anxiety traits are inclined to perceive environmental stimuli as potential threats and experience difficulties in coping with uncertainty, thereby increasing their susceptibility to developing OCD [18]. Moreover, patients with OCD who possess anxiety traits are more likely to exhibit depressive symptoms, thereby complicating treatment efforts and tending to have a less favorable prognosis [19].

The interaction between obsessive beliefs and anxiety traits can aggravate OCD symptoms in adolescents. For example, anxiety sensitivity can enhance the predictive effect of obsessive beliefs on OCD symptoms: when the anxiety sensitivity score is higher than the median, the regression coefficient of over responsibility/threat assessment on OCD symptoms is 0.42, while it is only 0.21 when it is lower than the median [20]. However, there are relatively few studies that examine both of these characteristics simultaneously, especially in clinical samples.

The mechanisms through which family-related and cognitive-emotional vulnerability variables impact OCD in adolescents remain insufficiently understood. Existing mediation models propose that certain cognitive variables mediate the influence of familial factors on the development of OCD [21]. However, there are insufficient research examining mediation models specifically within adolescent OCD populations. A significant portion of existing studies have utilized non-clinical samples, such as college students, or employing the obsessive-compulsive subscale scores of the Symptom Checklist-90 (SCL-90) as diagnostic indicators. This approach may limit the applicability of findings to clinical populations, necessitating further investigation [16, 22]. Furthermore, evaluations of patients’ parents frequently concentrate exclusively on their direct impact on the patients, thereby overlooking the intrinsic characteristics of the parents themselves. The detection of these intrinsic susceptibility factors may enhance our understanding of familial dynamics and inform targeted interventions involving family members in treatment.

Drawing on previous research, we conducted a study to test the following hypotheses: (1) the family environment and parental rearing style are associated with OCD in adolescents; (2) obsessive beliefs and anxiety traits are related to OCD in adolescents; (3) family factors may influence OCD through the mediation of obsessive beliefs and anxiety traits in adolescents; and (4) parents of adolescents with OCD exhibit a certain degree of obsessive beliefs and anxiety traits. The novelty of this study lies in its exploration of the mediating role of cognitive and emotional traits in the relationship between family environment, parenting style, and adolescent OCD. Additionally, this research extends the focus beyond the patients themselves to include the previous generation and assesses the possible influence of parental characteristics on the patients, thereby providing a more robust foundation for family-based therapeutic interventions.

Methods

Participants

Adolescent patients with OCD were recruited from both outpatient and inpatient settings at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University between April 2019 and December 2022. All the recruited patients were diagnosed by an experienced psychiatrist. The inclusion criteria for the study were individuals aged 12–17 years who met the diagnostic criteria for OCD according to the ICD-10, with a Y-BOCS total score of ≥ 16. The exclusion criteria encompassed individuals with a history of organic brain disease or serious physical illnesses, evidence of drug dependence or psychoactive substance use, diagnosis of schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, major depression, ADHD, or other mental disorders, as well as those who were uncooperative or unable to complete testing.

Unaffected biological parents of patients were recruited. The inclusion criteria consisted of individuals aged 32–60 years with a primary school education or higher, and who were the primary caregivers of patients. Exclusion criteria encompassed a history of organic brain diseases, serious physical illnesses, evidence of drug dependence or psychoactive substance use, reported current or past mental illness, and individuals who were uncooperative or unable to complete the tests.

Furthermore, non-clinical adolescents (NCA) were recruited as the control group. The inclusion criteria for the NCA group included individuals aged 12–17 years with no diagnosed mental disorders according to the ICD-10. The exclusion criteria comprised a history of organic brain diseases, serious physical illnesses, evidence of drug dependence or psychoactive substance use, and individuals who were uncooperative or unable to complete the tests.

Non-clinical biological parents of the NCA were also recruited in this study. The inclusion criteria consisted of biological parents aged 32–60 years old with an education level of primary school or higher, and who were the main caregivers of NCA. Exclusion criteria were the same as in the unaffected biological parents of patients.

The research was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University and was conducted in compliance with pertinent ethical standards and regulations. All participants and their legal guardians provided written informed consent.

Questionnaires

Yale-brown obsessive compulsive scale

The Yale-Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale (Y-BOCS) was used to assess the severity of OCD symptoms. In this scale, the first 5 items are aggregated to yield an obsession severity score (OS), while the next 5 items contribute to the compulsion severity score (CS). Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the Y-BOCS were calculated to be 0.90 in non-clinical samples and 0.80 in clinical samples. The Chinese version of the Y-BOCS has been validated by a previous study, demonstrating strong convergent and divergent validity, with satisfactory levels of test-retest reliability and internal consistency [23].

Family environment scale

The Family Environment Scale (FES) was developed by Moss in 1981 and comprises 90 binary questions. This scale assesses various family characteristics, including family cohesion, expressiveness, conflict, independence, achievement orientation, intellectual–cultural orientation, active–recreational orientation, moral–religious emphasis, organization, and control. Phillips et al. revised and redeveloped the Chinese version of FES (FES -CV), which demonstrated satisfactory internal consistency and retest reliability within the Chinese population [24]. Dysfunctional family environment (DFE) includes emotional expression, conflict, and control [25].

Parents rearing style assessment

Parental rearing style was assessed by Egna Minnen Beträffande Uppfostran (EMBU), an 81-item self-report inventory in which individuals rate their parents’ behaviors and attitudes towards them. The Chinese version of the scale was revised by Yue Dongmei et al. in 1993 [26]. The paternal rearing style included emotional warmth, punitive, over-involvement, favoritism, rejection, and overprotection. The maternal rearing style included emotional warmth, punitive, over-involved, favoritism, and rejection. Parental rearing is generally divided into positive rearing (emotional warmth) and negative rearing (rejection, controlling, over-involvement, and overprotection) [21].

Obsessive beliefs questionnaire

The Obsessive Beliefs Questionnaire (OBQ-44), a self-report instrument developed by the Obsessive Compulsive Cognitions Working Group (OCCWG) in 2005, assesses dysfunctional beliefs associated with the development and perpetuation of OCD. The OBQ-44 consists of three sub-scales, namely responsibility/threat estimation (RT), perfectionism/certainty (PC), and importance/control of thoughts (ICT). The overall OBQ demonstrated high internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.96, indicating strong reliability. The Chinese version of OBQ-44 was validated by Wang et al. and demonstrated good reliability and validity [27].

State-trait anxiety inventory

The State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI), originally developed by Charles DS et al., was translated into Chinese in 1980 with a revised version known as STAI-FormX. This inventory comprises 40 items divided into two subscales: anxiety state (S-AI, items 1–20) and anxiety traits (T-AI, items 21–40). The anxiety state subscale assesses an individual’s immediate or recent experience of fear, tension, anxiety, and neuroticism in a specific time or situation, whereas the anxiety traits subscale evaluates stable anxiety and tension personality traits. The S-AI and T-AI questionnaires have good reliability and validity [28].

Procedure

A total of 113 adolescent patients diagnosed with OCD were included in the study. Patients were required to complete the following scales: Y-BOCS, FES, EMBU, OBQ-44, and STAI. Additionally, thirty-one parents who voluntarily participated in the study of patients and 20 non-clinical adolescents and their parents were requested to complete FES, EMBU, OBQ-44, and STAI.

Data analysis

Data were sorted and analyzed using SPSS 22.0 (IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 22.0. IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Quantitative data were described as mean (standard deviation). Categorical data were described with number and percentage. Data were tested for normality with the Shapiro-Wilk test and Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. To compare the differences between the two groups, the Chi-square test was performed for categorical variables, the t test for continuous variables with normal distribution versus discrete, as well as the non-parametric Wilcoxon rank sum test for continuous variables without normal distribution versus discrete variables. A false discovery rate (FDR)-corrected statistical threshold of P (FDR) < 0.05 was used to control for multiple comparisons. Effect size was calculated using Cohen’s d (d). Subsequently, Pearson correlation coefficients of obsessive beliefs, anxiety traits, dysfunctional family environment, negative rearing, and OCD symptoms were calculated.

Mediation analyses were performed employing the PROCESS model 4 in SPSS 22.0 to investigate the mediating influence of cognitive traits and emotional traits on the relationship between family factors and OCD. The models were estimated using a bootstrapping method with 5,000 samples and 95% percentile confidence intervals (CIs). Associations were considered statistically significant when the Bootstrap CIs excluded zero. Two distinct sets of mediation models were evaluated. Initially, simple mediation models were tested, dysfunctional family environment (DFE) and parental negative rearing (PNR) either identified as the independent variables, obsessive beliefs (OB) and anxiety traits (T-AI) either as the mediators, and OCD symptoms (OCS) as the dependent variable. These models assess the indirect effect of each mediator in isolation, facilitating direct comparison with prior studies that utilized analogous analytical methodologies. Subsequently, parallel mediation models were implemented with both OB and T-AI simultaneously included as parallel mediators. This approach estimates the unique indirect effect of each mediator while controlling for their shared variance, thereby providing a more conservative and rigorous assessment of distinct mediating pathways. All models included gender and age as covariates.

A post-hoc statistical power analysis was conducted utilizing GPower version 3.1.9.7. The power (1-β) was calculated based on the actual sample size (N = 113), a significance level (α) of 0.05, and an observed effect size (Cohen’s f² = 0.15, indicative of a small effect). The resulting power was determined to be 0.983. A significance level of 0.05 was employed for all statistical procedures in the study.

Mediation analysis

Simple mediation analyses showed that OB fully mediated the relationships of both DFE and PNR with OCD symptoms (95% bootstrap CIs for the indirect effect did include zero; and the direct effects were non-significant). The mediating effect of OB accounted for 77.22% and 85.89% of the total effect of DFE and PNR, respectively, with effect sizes of 0.488 and 0.067, compared to total effects of 0.632 and 0.078. In contrast, T-AI partially mediated these relationships (95% CIs for both direct and indirect effects excluded zero), indicating that family factors were associated with OCD symptoms both directly and indirectly through T-AI. The direct effects of DFE and PNR on OCS were quantified as 0.356 and 0.060, constituting 56.33% and 76.91% of their respective total effects (see Fig. 1; Table 3).

Parallel mediation analyses were performed with OB and T-AI simultaneously included as mediators, while controlling for age and gender. When DFE served as the predictor variable, the total effect of DFE on OCS was significant (total effect = 0.632, 95% CI [0.293, 0.971]). Upon inclusion of both mediators, the direct effect of DFE on OCS was not significant (direct effect = 0.118, 95% CI [− 0.171, 0.408]), accounting for 18.67% of the total effect. The indirect effect mediated through OB was significant (indirect effect = 0.460, 95% CI [0.262, 0.678]), constituting 72.78% of the total effect. Conversely, the indirect effect mediated through T-AI was not significant (indirect effect = 0.054, 95% CI [− 0.061, 0.182]), explaining only 8.54% of the total effect. These findings suggest that OB fully mediate the relationship between DFE and OCS, while T-AI does not function as a unique mediator when controlling for OB. When PNR serves as a predictor in this analysis, the overall impact of PNR on OCS was found to be significant, with a total effect size of 0.078 and a 95% CI ranging from 0.054 to 0.102. Within the parallel mediation model, the direct effect of PNR on OCS was not statistically significant, with a direct effect size of 0.011 and a 95% CI of [− 0.018, 0.040], accounting for 14.10% of the total effect. Conversely, the indirect effect mediated through OB was significant, with an effect size of 0.062 and a 95% CI of [0.041, 0.087], representing 79.49% of the total effect. The indirect effect mediated through T-AI was not significant, with an effect size of 0.005 and a 95% CI of [− 0.005, 0.017], contributing only 6.41% to the total effect. These findings indicate that OB fully mediate the relationship between PNR and OCS, whereas T-AI does not exhibit a significant unique mediating effect (see Fig. 2; Table 4).

In summary, the results of the parallel mediation analysis indicate that, after controlling for the shared variance between OB and T-AI, OB alone serve as a robust and comprehensive mediator in the associations between family factors and adolescent OCD symptoms. Consequently, the partial mediation previously attributed to T-AI in simpler models can be explained by its overlap with OB.

The obsessive beliefs, anxiety state/traits and family factors in adolescents with OCD and non-clinical adolescents

The results showed that adolescents with OCD exhibited significantly higher levels of responsibility/threat estimation, perfectionism/certainty, importance/control of thoughts, and anxiety state/traits compared to non-clinical adolescents (P < 0.001). In terms of family factors, the moral-religious subscale score was significantly lower in the OCD group (t = -3.384, P = 0.001, d = 0.87), and maternal over-involvement was significantly higher (t = 2.111, P = 0.040, d = 0.55). There were no significant differences in family cohesion, emotional expression, conflict, independence, success, cultural, recreational, organization, control, father rearing style, mother emotional warmth, punitive, favoritism, and rejection between the two groups. After false discovery rate (FDR) correction, the differences in OBQ and STAI remained significant (FDR-adjusted P < 0.001), as did the moral-religious subscale (FDR-adjusted P = 0.042). However, the previously significant difference in maternal over-involvement did not survive FDR correction (FDR-adjusted P > 0.05), although its effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.55) suggested a moderate practical difference (see Tables 5 and 6).

3.5. The obsessive beliefs, anxiety state/traits and family factors in relatives of adolescents with OCD and NCA.

Compared to relatives of NCA, relatives of adolescents with OCD showed significantly higher scores of responsibility/threat estimation, perfectionism/certainty, importance/control of thoughts, and anxiety state/traits (P < 0.001). Additionally, the OCD patients’ relatives reported significantly higher scores on family moral-religious, organization, and control than those of the relatives of NCA (t = 2.049, P = 0.046, d = 0.59; t = 2.742, P = 0.009 d = 0.79; t = 2.334, P = 0.024, d = 0.67, respectively), as well as higher maternal over-involvement (t = 2.978, P = 0.005, d = 0.85). No significant differences were found between the two groups in family cohesion, emotional expression, conflict, independence, success, cultural, recreational, father rearing style, mother emotional warmth, punitive, favoritism, and rejection. After FDR correction, the differences in in OBQ and STAI remained significant (FDR-adjusted P < 0.001). However, none of the family-related differences (moral-religious, organization, control, maternal over-involvement) survived FDR correction (all adjusted P > 0.05). Nevertheless, their effect sizes were all above 0.55 (range: d = 0.59–0.85), indicating moderate to large practical differences (See Tables 7 and 8).

Discussion

This study aims to investigate the family factors, obsessive beliefs, and anxiety traits associated with adolescent obsessive-compulsive disorder, as well as the interrelationships among these variables. Additionally, it examines these factors within the relatives of patients, with a particular emphasis on family-related influences. We yielded several significant findings. Firstly, it explored the mediating roles of cognitive and emotional traits between familial factors and OCD. Parallel mediation analyses showed that obsessive beliefs fully mediated the relationship between family factors and adolescent OCD symptoms, whereas trait anxiety did not exhibit a unique mediating effect after controlling for obsessive beliefs. This suggests that cognitive traits operate as a specific mechanism linking family environment to OCD, while anxiety traits may be a non‑specific correlate. Second, elevated obsessive beliefs and anxiety traits co-occurred in both patients and their relatives, indicating familial aggregation of these characteristics. This underscores the importance of developing family-specific treatment strategies for adolescents with OCD.

Obsessive beliefs constitute the fundamental pathological cognitions associated with OCD. Our research indicates that obsessive beliefs are linked to OCD and are significantly influenced by familial factors, corroborating previous findings [16, 29, 30]. Adolescence represents a pivotal stage in cognitive development, during which detrimental family environment and negative parenting styles may impact cognitive processing patterns, leading to maladaptive beliefs such as overgeneralization and catastrophic thinking. Adolescents may also acquire maladaptive behaviors and attitudes, such as perfectionism and excessive worry, through the observation and imitation of parental behaviors and attitudes. Thus, obsessive beliefs are considered a crucial mediating variable influenced by family dynamics in the context of adolescent OCD. Empirical studies have demonstrated that parents’ threatening interpretations of adolescents’ intrusive thoughts are positively correlated with the adolescents’ obsessive beliefs, interpretative biases, and the severity of OCD symptoms [31]. Halvaiepour et al. identified that external criticism from parents indirectly influenced OCS via adolescents’ beliefs in excessive responsibility, with the mediating effect constituting 35% of the total effect [32]. Another study demonstrated that parental overprotection indirectly exacerbated symptoms through beliefs in perfectionism, with the mediating effect accounting for 28% of the total effect [16]. In our investigation, parallel mediation analyses showed that obsessive beliefs fully mediated the association between family factors and OCS, accounting for 72.78% and 79.49% of the total effects for dysfunctional family environment and negative parental rearing, respectively. These relatively high proportions likely resulted from our specific clinical sample of Chinese adolescents with OCD. In Chinese culture, family interdependence and parental expectations strongly influence obsessive beliefs. Our use of the comprehensive Obsessive Beliefs Questionnaire may capture more belief domains affected by family dynamics than previous methods. The clinical nature of our sample, unlike community samples in earlier studies, may also show stronger associations due to greater variability in family factors and obsessive beliefs. However, cross-sectional designs might overestimate these effects, suggesting the need for longitudinal studies.

In our simple mediation models, anxiety traits initially appeared to partially mediate the relationship between family factors and OCD. However, when we conducted parallel mediation analyses, which controlled for shared variance between anxiety traits and obsessive beliefs, anxiety traits no longer exhibited a significant unique mediating effect. This finding contrasts with previous reports. Zhang et al. found that adolescents with OCD exhibited significantly higher neuroticism scores compared to healthy controls, with neuroticism serving as a mediating factor between parenting styles and OCD symptoms. Specifically, paternal emotional warmth reduces OCD symptoms by reducing neuroticism levels, while maternal over-intervention exacerbates symptoms by increasing neuroticism levels [21]. Based on previous studies, anxiety characteristics, such as neuroticism and anxiety sensitivity may not only be risk factors for OCD, but also important mediating factors on OCD in adolescents [17, 18]. Importantly, those studies did not simultaneously control for obsessive beliefs. Our parallel mediation results suggest that the mediating role of anxiety traits may be at least partly accounted for by their overlap with obsessive beliefs.

In this study, we observed different patterns of mediating effects between simple and parallel models, with parallel models providing a more conservative estimate. Based on these results, we speculate that obsessive beliefs are more specific to OCD than anxiety traits. This speculation is supported by previous research demonstrating that particular subtypes of obsessive beliefs are uniquely associated with distinct OCD symptom dimensions [5, 33]. For example, Piras et al. reported that over-conscientiousness and threat assessment are linked to contamination symptoms, perfectionism and intolerance of uncertainty are associated with symmetry symptoms, and beliefs about the importance and control of thoughts are related to unacceptable thoughts [34]. Furthermore, the influence of family factors on OCD is more substantiated through their impact on obsessive beliefs. For instance, family conflict influences checking symptoms through over-conscientiousness beliefs and symmetry symptoms through perfectionism beliefs [35]. In contrast, anxiety traits serve as a general risk factor for both anxiety spectrum disorders and obsessive-compulsive spectrum disorders. As emotional traits, anxiety functions more as a susceptibility background and, in our parallel mediation results, did not independently transmit the effect of family factors to OCD symptoms.

The reciprocal relationship between obsessive beliefs and anxiety traits remains unclear, despite hypotheses suggesting shared neurobiological substrates [35]. Existing research has established that anxiety sensitivity is associated with OCS through metacognitive processes [36]. Building on these findings, we propose that obsessive beliefs and anxiety traits may influence each other during development, possibly sharing similar cognitive and neural pathways. Our observation that the unique mediating effect of anxiety traits diminishes when obsessive beliefs are accounted for supports the notion that anxiety traits may be a correlate or consequence of obsessive beliefs, rather than an independent pathway. Long-term studies are required to determine whether obsessive beliefs lead to anxiety traits or the other way around. Recognizing these psychological traits in teenagers with OCD underscores the need to explore early childhood cognitive growth and its interaction with family relationships. Longitudinal studies following these constructs from childhood to adolescence could clarify the exact mechanisms behind these associations and guide interventions that are sensitive to developmental stages.

Our research indicated that relatives of adolescents diagnosed with OCD exhibited higher scores in domains such as moral-religiosity, organization, control, and maternal overinvolvement when compared to relatives of NCA, with effect sizes ranging from moderate to large (Cohen’s d = 0.59–0.85). However, these differences did not retain statistical significance following correction for false discovery rate. Despite this, the moderate-to-large effect sizes suggest potential clinical relevance. Maternal overinvolvement and control, which are recognized as correlates of family accommodation, a predictor of poorer treatment outcomes, may necessitate intervention strategies aimed at reducing accommodation and promoting adolescent autonomy [37]. Additionally, the observed family organization and moral-religiosity may contribute to the reinforcement of perfectionism and excessive responsibility. Consequently, family interventions could benefit from incorporating psychoeducation to enhance flexibility in expectations and diminish the reinforcement of obsessive beliefs. Future research with larger sample sizes is essential to validate these family-level patterns.

Obsessive beliefs and anxiety traits, similar to those observed in adolescent patients, were also elevated in their relatives, suggesting familial aggregation. Research on adolescents with OCD undergoing Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) demonstrates that parental anxiety levels can influence treatment outcomes, with higher parental anxiety associated with less favorable improvement [38]. Our previous studies have also shown that parental anxiety traits correlate with symptom severity and treatment efficacy [39]. These findings highlight the importance of including family members in both assessment and intervention for adolescent OCD.

Limitations

The limitations of this study are as follows: (1) The study employs a cross-sectional design, precluding the inference of causal relationships. Longitudinal studies with larger samples are needed to clarify the causal relationships and dynamic interactions among family factors, cognitive traits, anxiety traits, and OCD symptoms. (2) Given the limited sample size, a post-hoc statistical power analysis was conducted, revealing a statistical power of 0.983, which significantly exceeds the recommended threshold of 0.80. This high power indicates a strong sensitivity for detecting the specified mediation effect (f²=0.15) with a minimal risk of Type II error. Despite the small effect size (f²=0.15), the high power suggests a robust capacity for identifying subtle mediation effects. Nonetheless, further prospective studies with expanded sample sizes are required to obtain additional evidence. (3) The subsample of 31 patients came from the families who voluntarily participated in the study, which may introduce selection bias. For instance, parents who agreed to participate might have greater willingness to cooperate or higher educational levels. Also, the small subsample limited statistical power for detecting family-level differences after multiple correction. Thus, the generalizability of family-related findings is limited, and larger, more systematically recruited samples are needed. (4) Adolescents with OCD who were undergoing treatment, including medication and psychotherapy, at the time of enrollment were not excluded, potentially affecting the assessment of OCS. Medications may alleviate the severity of OCD symptoms, potentially diminishing the observed associations between familial factors, cognitive variables, and OCS. Conversely, psychotherapy might directly influence obsessive beliefs, thereby modifying the mediation pathways explored in this study. Anxiety and depression symptoms were not assessed at the time of enrollment, and the influence of these factors was not controlled, which may also affect the mediating effect. Enrolling medication-naive patients and more detailed data collection would be more appropriate in future protocols.

Conclusions

In summary, our findings emphasize the connections between familial factors, cognitive and emotional characteristics, and OCD. They provide insights into the familial clustering of obsessive beliefs and anxiety traits closely associated with OCD and underscore the persistent influence of family environment and parenting attitudes in the context of OCD. These results indicate that family-based interventions for adolescent OCD should prioritize modifying maladaptive obsessive beliefs rather than solely targeting general anxiety. Furthermore, intervention studies that investigate whether altering family environments or parental behaviors directly alleviates OCS in adolescents would offer critical evidence for causal inference and inform targeted treatment strategies.
