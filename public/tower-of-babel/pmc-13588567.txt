Double-Port Totally Endoscopic Mitral Valve Replacement Through a Prosthesis-Sized Working Incision with Posterior Subvalvular Preservation
Authors: Cao D Khang, Tran D Quang, Phan Q Thuan, Vuong N Minh, Pham TV Chuong, Nguyen H Dinh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the feasibility and early outcomes of totally endoscopic non-robotic mitral valve replacement using a double-port DCT approach with posterior subvalvular preservation.MethodsIn this single-center retrospective observational study, 76 consecutive patients underwent totally endoscopic non-robotic mitral valve replacement between November 2023 and December 2025. The DCT approach was defined by exclusive endoscopic visualization, no rib spreading, a camera port and prosthesis-sized working incision placed in the same intercostal space, and thoracoscopic intracardiac instrumentation. Mean skin incision length was 3.8 ± 0.5 cm. Mean cardiopulmonary bypass and aortic cross-clamp times were 148.3 ± 38.6 and 101.9 ± 32.0 minutes, respectively. There was 1 early non-cardiac death (1.3%; 95% CI, 0.03%–7.1%), no late death, and no mitral valve reoperation (0%; 95% CI, 0%–4.7%). No moderate or severe paravalvular regurgitation was observed during early echocardiographic follow-up. Mean follow-up was 9.7 ± 6.0 months. 1. Introduction

Mitral valve replacement remains necessary when mitral valve pathology is unsuitable for durable repair, particularly in patients with advanced rheumatic involvement, leaflet restriction, fibrosis, or calcification [1]. During replacement, preservation of the mitral subvalvular apparatus is an important surgical principle because the continuity between the annulus, chordae, papillary muscles, and left ventricular wall contributes to left ventricular geometry and systolic performance [2–4]. However, complete posterior leaflet preservation may not be appropriate in every patient, especially in rheumatic disease, bulky leaflet tissue, annular or leaflet calcification, or restricted leaflet mobility, because retained tissue can interfere with prosthetic seating, prosthetic leaflet motion, or the left ventricular outflow tract [5,6].

Minimally invasive mitral valve surgery through a right mini-thoracotomy has become widely adopted in experienced centers and has been associated with favorable early recovery and acceptable long-term results [7–9]. Totally endoscopic and robotic platforms have further reduced the dependence on direct vision through the thoracic incision by shifting visualization to a camera-based operative field [10–12]. Nevertheless, mitral valve replacement is technically distinct from mitral valve repair. In replacement surgery, the surgeon must perform annular suturing, subvalvular management, prosthesis delivery, seating, and knot tying in a confined operative field while avoiding prosthetic obstruction and paravalvular leak. Therefore, a small incision alone does not adequately define the technical character of totally endoscopic mitral valve replacement.

The novelty of the present DCT approach is the combination of a double-port, non-robotic, totally endoscopic configuration with a prosthesis-sized working incision. The camera port and main working incision are placed in the same intercostal space to improve coaxial alignment of the camera, instruments, mitral annulus, and prosthesis. Visualization is maintained exclusively by three-dimensional endoscopy, no rib spreader is used, and the working incision is intentionally limited to the minimum size required for prosthetic valve delivery rather than enlarged for direct manual exposure. In this setting, posterior ventricular-annular continuity is preserved using an anatomy-driven strategy: posterior leaflet preservation when feasible, or posterior leaflet resection with native chordal preservation or artificial chordal reimplantation when leaflet preservation is unsuitable.

The present study aimed to describe this double-port totally endoscopic non-robotic technique for mitral valve replacement with posterior subvalvular preservation and to evaluate early clinical and echocardiographic outcomes in a consecutive single-center retrospective observational cohort. Because the study did not include a comparator group, the primary scientific objective was to assess technical feasibility and early safety, not superiority over other minimally invasive, totally endoscopic, or robotic approaches.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design and patients

This was a single-center retrospective observational study of consecutive patients who underwent totally endoscopic non-robotic mitral valve replacement using the DCT approach at University Medical Center Ho Chi Minh City. Between November 2023 and December 2025, 76 patients were included.

Patients were considered for totally endoscopic mitral valve replacement after preoperative clinical assessment, transthoracic echocardiography, and computed tomographic evaluation when required to assess peripheral cannulation suitability. The indication for mitral valve replacement rather than repair was based on valve pathology, leaflet quality, calcification, rheumatic involvement, restricted leaflet motion, or the surgeon’s judgment regarding the feasibility of durable repair.

Patients who underwent concomitant tricuspid valve repair, atrial fibrillation ablation, or left atrial appendage management were included. Patients requiring emergency conversion before mitral valve exposure, redo sternotomy, active infective endocarditis, or concomitant major aortic procedures were excluded.

For descriptive technical reporting, patients were categorized according to the posterior subvalvular management strategy used during valve replacement: posterior leaflet preservation, in which the posterior leaflet and chordal attachments were retained; or posterior leaflet resection with posterior subvalvular preservation, in which the posterior leaflet was resected because of severe calcification, fibrosis, thickening, or restricted mobility while ventricular-annular continuity was maintained by native chordal preservation or artificial chordal reimplantation. These categories were anatomy-driven and were not intended to represent randomized or protocol-assigned treatment groups.

2.2. Definition of totally endoscopic DCT mitral valve replacement

For mitral valve replacement, the DCT approach was defined as a double-port, totally endoscopic, non-robotic technique characterized by exclusive endoscopic visualization without direct vision through the skin incision, no rib spreading, thoracoscopic port-based intracardiac instrumentation, and a primary working incision less than 4 cm.

In contrast to totally endoscopic mitral valve repair, in which a primary incision less than 3 cm may be sufficient, totally endoscopic mitral valve replacement requires delivery of a rigid prosthetic valve through the working incision [13]. Therefore, in the present study, the primary incision was intentionally limited to the minimum size required for prosthetic valve insertion, usually 3–4 cm, rather than enlarged for direct visualization or manual exposure.

The DCT configuration consisted of a main working incision and an endoscopic camera port placed in the same intercostal space to improve coaxial alignment of the operative field, camera, instruments, and prosthesis (Fig. 1). Additional small ports were used when necessary for aortic cross-clamping, left ventricular venting, atrial retraction, or auxiliary instrumentation.

2.3. Operative technique

All procedures were performed under general anesthesia with double-lumen endotracheal intubation and single-lung ventilation. The patient was positioned supine with the right hemithorax elevated. Cardiopulmonary bypass was established after systemic heparinization, usually through peripheral femoral arterial and venous cannulation. Venous drainage was assisted as needed to optimize exposure.

A right anterior mini-thoracotomy was created in the appropriate intercostal space near the nipple line. The working incision was protected with a soft-tissue retractor, and no rib spreader was used. A three-dimensional endoscopic camera was introduced through a separate port in the same intercostal space as the main working incision. A transthoracic aortic cross-clamp was applied through a small auxiliary incision, and myocardial protection was achieved using antegrade cardioplegia. Left ventricular venting was performed through the right superior pulmonary vein when indicated.

After cardioplegic arrest, the left atrium was opened through the interatrial groove. A left atrial retractor was inserted through an auxiliary port to expose the mitral valve. All mitral valve assessment, leaflet excision or preservation, annular suturing, prosthetic valve seating, knot tying, and sub-valvular management were performed under exclusive endoscopic visualization (Fig. 1).

2.4. Posterior leaflet and subvalvular management

The posterior leaflet was preserved whenever leaflet quality and mobility permitted safe prosthetic valve implantation without risk of left ventricular outflow tract obstruction, prosthetic interference, or excessive tissue bulk. In these patients, the posterior leaflet was detached only as needed, trimmed when necessary, and retained with its chordal attachments to preserve papillary muscle-annular continuity.

When the posterior leaflet was severely calcified, fibrotic, thickened, retracted, or immobile, complete posterior leaflet preservation was considered unsuitable. In such cases, the diseased posterior leaflet tissue was resected to facilitate prosthetic valve seating and avoid prosthetic obstruction. However, preservation of posterior subvalvular continuity remained a technical priority. Native posterior chordae were preserved whenever feasible. If native chordal preservation was not possible because of leaflet resection or chordal involvement, artificial chordal reimplantation was performed using expanded polytetrafluoroethylene sutures anchored to the papillary muscle and secured to the posterior annular region or residual leaflet tissue to restore ventricular-annular continuity (Video 1 (https://www.j-saudi-heart.com/cgi/editor.cgi?article=1517&window=additional_files&context=jsha)).

Annular sutures were then placed circumferentially under endoscopic visualization. Care was taken to avoid injury to the circumflex artery near the posterolateral annulus and to prevent entrapment or distortion of preserved chordal structures. The prosthetic valve was introduced through the main working incision, which was limited to the size required for valve delivery. After the prosthesis was seated and secured, valve motion was inspected endoscopically, and prosthetic function was assessed by intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography after weaning from cardiopulmonary bypass.

2.5. Outcomes and follow-up

The primary outcomes were technical feasibility, early clinical safety, and early echocardiographic prosthetic valve function after totally endoscopic DCT mitral valve replacement. Early mortality was defined as death within 30 days after surgery or during the index hospitalization. Late mortality was defined as death occurring after discharge during the follow-up period.

Secondary outcomes included conversion to sternotomy, cardiopulmonary bypass time, aortic cross-clamp time, ventilation time, intensive care unit stay, hospital length of stay, reoperation for bleeding, low cardiac output syndrome, stroke, acute kidney injury requiring dialysis, wound complications, permanent pacemaker implantation, thromboembolic events, prosthetic valve thrombosis, major bleeding, paravalvular leak, infective endocarditis, mitral valve reoperation, and echocardiographic prosthetic valve function.

Transthoracic echocardiography was performed before discharge and during follow-up. Follow-up echocardiographic assessment included left ventricular ejection fraction, left ventricular dimensions, left atrial size, right ventricular function when available, estimated pulmonary artery pressure, prosthetic valve mean gradient, prosthetic regurgitation, paravalvular leak, and evidence of prosthetic valve dysfunction. Prosthetic valve function was interpreted according to contemporary echocardiographic recommendations [14].

Prosthetic valve-related adverse events were defined as the first occurrence of prosthetic valve dysfunction, valve thrombosis, operated-valve endocarditis, thromboembolic events, major bleeding, mitral valve reoperation, or valve-related death. Prosthetic valve dysfunction was defined as moderate or severe transprosthetic or paravalvular mitral regurgitation, or significant prosthetic mitral stenosis on Doppler echocardiography. Significant prosthetic mitral stenosis was diagnosed using integrated Doppler criteria, including a mean transmitral gradient >10 mm Hg at a normal heart rate, pressure half-time >200 ms, Doppler velocity index >2.5, or effective orifice area <1.0 cm2, together with supportive imaging findings when available. For echocardiography-based events, the event date was defined as the date of the first study demonstrating dysfunction. Time-to-event outcomes included overall survival, freedom from mitral valve reoperation, and freedom from prosthetic valve-related adverse events during follow-up.

2.6. Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were assessed for distributional characteristics using visual inspection of histograms and the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, whereas non-normally distributed variables are presented as median with interquartile range. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. For key binary outcomes, 95% confidence intervals were calculated using the exact binomial method. For continuous outcomes, 95% confidence intervals were calculated using the t distribution when appropriate.

Comparisons between posterior subvalvular management strategies, when performed, were exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Continuous variables were compared using the Student t test or Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher exact test, as appropriate. No multivariable regression modelling was performed because of the modest sample size and low number of clinical events.

Time-to-event outcomes were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier product-limit method and are reported with 95% confidence intervals calculated using Greenwood’s formula. Patients were censored at the date of last clinical or echocardiographic follow-up. Numbers at risk were displayed below the Kaplan–Meier curve. Because mean follow-up duration was relatively short and the number of patients remaining at risk decreased substantially at later time points, estimates beyond 12 months were considered descriptive and were interpreted cautiously. All analyses were performed using Stata version 17.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA).

2.7. Ethics statement

This study was conducted in accordance with institutional ethical standards and the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of University of Medicine and Pharmacy at Ho Chi Minh City (Approval No 1817/ĐHYD/HĐĐĐ). Because of the retrospective observational design, the requirement for individual informed consent was waived by the Institutional Review Board.

3. Results

The study included 76 patients, with a predominance of female patients (65.8%). The median age was 53.5 years (IQR, 45.0–62.0), and 11.8% of patients were in NYHA class III/IV. Rheumatic disease was the predominant mitral valve pathology (90.8%), and mixed mitral stenosis and regurgitation was the most common lesion (80.3%). Atrial fibrillation was present in 47.4% of patients. Preoperative echocardiography showed preserved left ventricular systolic function, with a mean left ventricular ejection fraction of 54.5 ± 6.1%, while moderate-to-severe pulmonary hypertension was observed in 59.2% of patients. Moderate or severe tricuspid regurgitation was present in 68.4% of patients (Table 1).

Echocardiographic follow-up was available in all patients before discharge, in 74 patients (97.4%) at 1 month, and in 69 patients (90.8%) at 3 months. No moderate or severe paravalvular mitral regurgitation was observed at any follow-up time point, whereas mild paravalvular regurgitation was identified in 1.3%, 1.4%, and 4.3% of patients before discharge, at 1 month, and at 3 months, respectively. Left ventricular end-diastolic dimension and systolic pulmonary artery pressure decreased significantly after surgery and remained lower during follow-up. The prevalence of moderate-to-severe pulmonary hypertension decreased from 59.2% preoperatively to 21.1% before discharge, 18.9% at 1 month, and 15.9% at 3 months. Similarly, moderate-to-severe tricuspid regurgitation decreased from 68.4% preoperatively to 17.1%, 14.9%, and 8.7% at the respective follow-up time points. Left ventricular ejection fraction was preserved overall and was significantly higher than the preoperative value at 3 months (Table 4).

The mean follow-up duration was 9.7 ± 6.0 months (range, 1.3–27.5 months). No additional deaths occurred during follow-up beyond the 1 early postoperative death, and no patient underwent mitral valve reoperation (0%; 95% CI, 0%–4.7%). Using the predefined definition of prosthetic valve-related adverse events, Kaplan–Meier analysis showed freedom from prosthetic valve-related adverse events of 98.6% (95% CI, 90.6%–99.8%) at 6 months and 95.2% (95% CI, 80.1%–98.9%) at 12 months. The 24-month estimate was also 95.2% (95% CI, 80.1%–98.9%), but only 7 patients remained at risk near 24 months; therefore, this later estimate should be interpreted as descriptive rather than as a robust durability estimate (Fig. 2).

4. Discussion

In this single-center retrospective observational study, totally endoscopic non-robotic mitral valve replacement using the double-port DCT approach was technically feasible and was associated with acceptable early clinical and echocardiographic outcomes. The procedure was performed through a prosthesis-sized working incision without rib spreading and under exclusive three-dimensional endoscopic visualization. There was 1 early noncardiac death, no additional death during follow-up, and no mitral valve reoperation. Major postoperative complications were infrequent, and early follow-up echocardiography demonstrated satisfactory prosthetic valve function without moderate or severe paravalvular mitral regurgitation.

The main contribution of this study is technical rather than comparative. Minimally invasive mitral valve surgery has been well described through right mini-thoracotomy, endoscopic, and robotic platforms [7,8,10–12]. However, many reports combine repair and replacement or focus mainly on repair. Mitral valve replacement imposes additional constraints because a rigid prosthesis must be delivered, seated, and secured within a limited operative field. The DCT approach addresses this problem by separating visualization from the working incision while keeping the camera and main incision in the same intercostal space. This configuration is intended to improve coaxial alignment and instrument maneuverability during annular suturing, subvalvular management, and prosthetic valve delivery.

The concept of a prosthesis-sized working incision is also important. In totally endoscopic mitral valve replacement, the minimum incision cannot be defined independently of prosthetic valve delivery. A 3- to 4-cm incision in the present series represented the smallest functional access that allowed safe insertion of the selected prosthesis while preserving the principle of exclusive endoscopic visualization. Thus, the goal was not simply to minimize skin incision length, but to avoid enlargement of the incision for direct vision or manual exposure. This distinction helps clarify the novelty of the DCT technique relative to conventional right mini-thoracotomy approaches.

Posterior ventricular-annular continuity was preserved in all patients, but the specific technique was anatomy-driven. Previous literature supports preservation of the mitral subvalvular apparatus during mitral valve replacement to maintain left ventricular geometry and function [2–6]. Nevertheless, complete leaflet preservation may be difficult or undesirable in rheumatic disease, calcified leaflets, thickened subvalvular tissue, or restricted leaflet motion. In such settings, native chordal preservation or artificial chordal reimplantation after posterior leaflet resection may provide a pragmatic alternative to complete posterior leaflet retention. The present series suggests that these anatomy-driven subvalvular strategies can be performed within a double-port endoscopic platform. However, because the posterior management strategy was selected according to pathology and operative anatomy, our study cannot determine whether one preservation strategy is superior to another.

The early echocardiographic findings support the functional adequacy of the technique in the short term. No moderate or severe paravalvular regurgitation was observed, and pulmonary pressure, tricuspid regurgitation severity, and left ventricular dimensions improved during early follow-up. These findings suggest effective early prosthetic function and hemodynamic recovery. However, the short follow-up period limits any inference about long-term durability, prosthetic valve-related complications, or late ventricular remodeling.

The Kaplan–Meier estimates should therefore be interpreted carefully. Although freedom from prosthetic valve-related adverse events was high at 6 and 12 months, the mean follow-up duration was only 9.7 months. The 24-month Kaplan–Meier estimate is reported for completeness because some patients had follow-up extending beyond 2 years, but the number of patients remaining at risk at that time point was small. Consequently, the 24-month estimate should be considered descriptive and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive evidence of mid-term durability.

Finally, the absence of a comparator group precludes conclusions regarding superiority. The present study does not establish that the DCT approach is better than sternotomy, right minithoracotomy, robotic mitral valve surgery, or other totally endoscopic methods. Instead, it provides an early technical experience demonstrating that double-port totally endoscopic non-robotic mitral valve replacement with posterior subvalvular preservation can be performed with acceptable early outcomes in a selected single-center cohort.

5. Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations. First, it was retrospective and conducted at a single center, which limits generalizability. Second, the sample size was modest and the number of adverse events was low; therefore, the precision of estimated event rates is limited despite the addition of confidence intervals. Third, there was no comparator group, and no conclusion can be drawn regarding superiority over conventional sternotomy, right minithoracotomy, robotic surgery, or other totally endoscopic approaches. Fourth, posterior subvalvular management was anatomy-driven rather than randomized or protocol-assigned. Therefore, the posterior leaflet preservation, native chordal preservation, and artificial chordal reimplantation categories should be interpreted as technical descriptions, not comparative treatment groups. Fifth, follow-up was relatively short. Kaplan–Meier estimates beyond 12 months, particularly the 24-month estimate, were based on a limited number of patients at risk and should be interpreted cautiously. Longer follow-up, external validation, and comparative studies are required to define durability, reproducibility, and broader applicability.

6. Conclusion

In this single-center retrospective observational series, totally endoscopic non-robotic mitral valve replacement using the double-port DCT approach was technically feasible and was associated with acceptable early clinical and echocardiographic outcomes. The technique allowed mitral valve replacement through a prosthesis-sized working incision without rib spreading while maintaining posterior ventricular-annular continuity using anatomy-driven posterior leaflet preservation, native chordal preservation, or artificial chordal reimplantation. Because of the lack of a comparator group, modest sample size, and short follow-up, these findings should be interpreted as feasibility data rather than evidence of superiority. Longer follow-up and comparative studies are required to determine durability and broader applicability.
