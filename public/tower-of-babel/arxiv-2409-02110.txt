Estimating the coherence of noise in mid-scale quantum systems
Pedro Figueroa-Romero, Miha Papič, Adrian Auer, Inés de Vega

Abstract
While the power of quantum computers is commonly acknowledged to rise exponentially, it is often overlooked that the complexity of quantum noise mechanisms generally grows much faster. In particular, quantifying whether the instructions on a quantum processor are close to being unitary has important consequences concerning error rates, e.g., for the confidence in their estimation, the ability to mitigate them efficiently, or their relation to fault-tolerance thresholds in error correction. However, the complexity of estimating the coherence, or unitarity, of noise generally scales exponentially in system size. Here, we obtain an upper bound on the average unitarity of Pauli noise and develop a protocol allowing us to estimate the average unitarity of operations in a digital quantum device efficiently and feasibly for mid-size quantum systems. We demonstrate our results through both experimental execution on IQM Spark TM , a 5-qubit superconducting quantum computer, and in simulation with up to 10 qubits, discussing the prospects for extending our technique to arbitrary scales.



The main obstacle for quantum computing undeniably remains the presence of noise, causing a multitude of errors that limit the usefulness of currently available quantum processors. That is to say, not all quantum noise is made equal, and ironically, figures of merit that are efficient to access in an experiment –such as the fidelity of a quantum operation– are often by definition insensitive to many of the details of such noise. It is thus imperative to have a plethora of protocols with certain practical desiderata that can probe different aspects of noise, and that can then, in turn, provide actionable information to existing methods to suppress, mitigate, or fully correct potential errors.

Quantum noise can be described as an undesirable quantum transformation mapping the ideal noiseless output state of a quantum computation into some other valid, albeit unexpected, output quantum state. Focusing on the regime where such noise is
effectively
Markovian, i.e., it only depends on the input state and no other external context variables, a comprehensive classification of all possible errors can be made

Generally, Markovian quantum noise will be described by a quantum operation that contains both coherent and stochastic noise contributions. 1
Average coherence of noise and its relation with purity and fidelity

It is well known that no physical system can be perfectly isolated. In particular, the loss of coherence of quantum states is one of the fundamental practical problems facing any quantum technology. In general, the dissipation of information to an external environment results in an increasingly mixed classical probability distribution over a set of possible quantum states. Such mixedness or uncertainty can be quantified by the purity of the respective density matrix
\rho
, given by
\tr(\rho^{2})
. The purity takes extremal values of 1 for a pure state, and
2^{-n}
for a maximally mixed state on
n
-qubits, and it can also be written as the Schatten 2-norm
\|\rho\|_{2}^{2}
, where
\|X\|_{2}=[\tr(XX^{\dagger})]^{1/2}
.

Logical operations or gates
\mathcal{G}
in a quantum computer are described by unitary operators, which by definition preserve the purity of quantum states, i.e., for
\mathcal{G}(\rho_{\mathsf{in}})=\rho_{\mathsf{out}}
, we have
\tr(\rho_{\mathsf{in}}^{2})=\tr(\rho_{\mathsf{out}}^{2})
. The corresponding, real noisy operation,
\mathcal{G}_{\mathsf{noisy}}
, however, is described by a more general
Completely Positive (CP)
map, such that for
\mathcal{G}_{\mathsf{noisy}}(\rho_{\mathsf{in}})=\rho_{\mathsf{out}}
, we have
\tr(\rho_{\mathsf{out}}^{2})\leq\tr(\rho_{\mathsf{in}}^{2})
. The reduction in purity of states when acted on by a noisy channel is related to the
unitarity
of such channel, a measure of “
how far
”
\mathcal{G}_{\mathsf{noisy}}
is from being unitary. Because locally we can always relate a noisy channel with an ideal gate by
\mathcal{G}_{\mathsf{noisy}}:=\mathcal{E}\circ\mathcal{G}
(
[
1
]
) for some
CP
map
\mathcal{E}
, the unitarity of the noisy gate equivalently measures how far
\mathcal{E}
is from being unitary.

The average unitarity of a quantum channel can be defined as in
[
12
]
, by

\displaystyle\overline{\mathsf{u}}(\mathcal{E})

\displaystyle:=\frac{2^{n}}{2^{n}-1}\expect_{\psi\sim\mathsf{Haar}}\|\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\psi)\|_{2}^{2},

(1)

where
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\cdot):=\mathcal{E}(\,\cdot-\mathds{1}/2^{n})
, with
\mathds{1}
being an
n
-qubit identity operator, and the normalization
2^{n}/(2^{n}-1)
ensuring that
0\leq\overline{\mathsf{u}}(\mathcal{E})\leq 1
, and with
\overline{\mathsf{u}}(\mathcal{E})=1
if and only if
\mathcal{E}
—and hence the noisy operation
\mathcal{G}_{\mathsf{noisy}}
— is unitary. Since the purity of a noisy output state may be written in the form
\tr(\rho_{\mathsf{out}}^{2})=\|\mathcal{E}(\rho_{\mathsf{in}})\|_{2}^{2}
, the definition in Eq. (
1
) implies that the average purity of a noisy state is, in general, a combination of average unitarity and trace-decrease and/or non-unital terms
1
1

1

See, e.g., Eq. (
26
) for an explicit expression.
.

The unitarity only quantifies
how unitary

\mathcal{G}_{\mathsf{noisy}}
is, but not whether it is the target unitary, i.e., it does not measure whether
\mathcal{G}_{\mathsf{noisy}}
is far from the ideal
\mathcal{G}
. A figure of merit aiming to quantify this distinction is the average gate fidelity,

\displaystyle\overline{\mathsf{F}}(\mathcal{G}_{\mathsf{noisy}},\mathcal{G})

\displaystyle:=\expect_{\psi\sim\mathsf{Haar}}\tr[\mathcal{G}_{\mathsf{noisy}}(\psi)\mathcal{G}(\psi)]

\displaystyle=\expect_{\psi\sim\mathsf{Haar}}\langle\psi|\mathcal{E}(\psi)|\psi\rangle:=\overline{\mathsf{F}}(\mathcal{E}),

(2)

where implicitly we denote
\overline{\mathsf{F}}(\mathcal{E}):=\overline{\mathsf{F}}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{I})

\mathcal{I}

\psi=|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|

\overline{\mathsf{F}}(\mathcal{E})=1
if and only if
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{I}
(equivalently if and only if
\mathcal{G}_{\mathsf{noisy}}=\mathcal{G}
). With respect of
\overline{\mathsf{F}}

[
12

it is shown that the average unitarity satisfies
\overline{\mathsf{u}}(\mathcal{E})\geq\overline{f}(\mathcal{E})^{2}

(3)

is the so-called average polarization of
\mathcal{E}
, and with saturation occurring for a depolarizing (purely incoherent) channel.

The unitarity can also be written in terms of fidelity as
\overline{\mathsf{u}}(\mathcal{E})=(2^{n}/(2^{n}-1))\overline{\mathsf{F}}(\mathcal{E}^{\prime\,\dagger}\mathcal{E}^{\prime})
, where
\mathcal{E}^{\prime\,\dagger}
is the adjoint map of
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
2
2

2

The adjoint map

\mathcal{E}^{\prime\,\dagger}\mathcal{E}^{\prime}

\mathsf{C}_{m}=\mathsf{L}_{m}\mathsf{L}_{m-1}\cdots\mathsf{L}_{1},

6
Scaling bottlenecks

6.1
Sample Complexity

The main roadblock for estimating the average coherence of noise in large systems (or even just the purity of quantum states) with
RM
s, is the required number of measurements to have an estimation within a given error, i.e., its sample complexity, which is then by definition manifested by the variance associated to such
RM
s. In Protocol
4.1
, statistical uncertainty stems from the sampling of the
\Omega
-distributed circuits, that of the
RM
elements, and the number of measurements per
RM
element; these quantities correspond to
N_{\mathsf{C}_{m}^{*}}
and
N_{\mathsf{W}}
in the estimators of Eq. (
12
) and Eq. (
11
), and to
N_{\mathsf{meas}}
of Eq. (
10
), respectively.

The main limitation to scalability is imposed by
N_{\mathsf{meas}}
: using local
RM

5
) to estimate purity to precision
1/\sqrt{N_{\mathsf{W}}}
, it was observed numerically in
[
33
]
that it scales approximately as
N_{\mathsf{meas}}\sim{2}^{0.75n}
in number of qubits
n
. Generally, within the classical shadows framework, the required number of measurements can be seen to scale exponentially in a similar fraction of
n
and linearly in the actual purity of the state
[
26
,
58
,
59
]
.

Mixedness reduces the sample complexity, as also observed numerically in
[
33
]
, and with
\Omega
-distributed circuits, quantum states will naturally get increasingly mixed in increasing depth. Coincidentally, thus, a high percentage of coherent error contributes to a higher variance not only in average fidelity estimation but also in average unitarity estimation itself.

When it comes to
\Omega
-distributed circuit samples when estimating average fidelity, the variance associated with it can be directly linked to the average amount of coherent noise
[
12
,
22
]
. How to control this uncertainty, as well as guarantees when choosing the number of sample circuits and number of measurements, has been widely studied for Clifford
RB

[

,
60
,
10
]
; furthermore,
[

]
obtained a bound on the variance for the purity in circuits within (an improved version of) Clifford

(namely employing
\Omega
-distributed circuits), it has been observed numerically that similar behavior follows (at least when layers are made up only of either generators-of or Clifford gates themselves)
[
22
,
23
]
, and that
Pauli twirling

[
61

15

generally suppresses the variance of expectation values under any sequence of operations, regardless of whether noise is correlated (either spatially or temporally) or not

A prospect could therefore be to benchmark average Pauli-dressed gates, aiming to obtain a unitarity within Ineq. (
4

There are two other

7
Conclusions

We have derived an upper bound on the average unitarity of Pauli-twirled noise solely in terms of the average fidelity of the respective bare noise, and we have established a protocol enabling the estimation of the average unitarity for a broad class of quantum circuits in digital systems, independent of technology platform or architecture, with tenths of qubits. Our results have been inspired both by novel scalable
Randomized Benchmarking (RB)
techniques, as well as
Randomized Measurements (RM)
methods. We have shown that reliable estimation of both average operation coherence and fidelity in such systems can be done under mild conditions and with a reasonable sampling overhead. Finally, we demonstrated our results in experiment on up to 5 qubits and numerically in simulation with up to 10 qubits.

A fully scalable estimation of the average coherence of noise will almost surely rely on modularity, e.g., by using smaller-scale unitarity information extracted from smaller qubit subsets and then combined with a Simultaneous
RB
-like protocol.

A promising way of moving forward in this direction is that of
[
59
]
, where the authors establish a method to estimate entropy and entanglement of a quantum state in polynomially-many measurements, relying on conditions essentially satisfied when spatial correlation lengths in the system remain finite and for systems in a one-dimensional topology. Their main result suggests that generally one could partition
any
large
n
-qubit system into smaller subsystems and reconstruct the global
n
-qubit purity from the
local
purity of pairs of contiguous subsystems, like
stitching
together the bigger purity with a number of measurements that overall grow polynomially in
n
. As a direct consequence, if such results hold more generally, our protocol could be used to estimate global average noise coherence with a similar sample complexity.

Nevertheless, several things remain unclear, e.g., what classes of (Markovian) noise such a method would hold for, how it could be phrased beyond one-dimensional qubit arrays, or how one would certify the global purity estimations, among others. As we have argued, however, the average coherence of noise is a crucial figure of merit for benchmarking the error mechanisms limiting the performance of quantum computers, perhaps only standing after average layer fidelity in terms of importance as a benchmark, so the aforementioned hurdles must soon be overcome.

A.3
The output states of
\Omega
-distributed circuits

We will consider a set of
\Omega
-distributed circuits of depth
m
, acting on a fiducial initial state
|0\rangle
, which in turn we randomize with a layer
\mathsf{V}\in\mathds{L}(\mathds{G}_{1})
. That is, we will have circuits of the form
\mathsf{C}_{m}\mathsf{V}|0\rangle
, which will always give a pure state output in the noiseless case. In the noisy case, however, we will have states of the form

\tilde{\varrho}_{m}:=\expect_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathsf{L}_{i}\sim\Omega\\
\mathsf{V}\sim\Omega_{1}\end{subarray}}\phi(\mathsf{L}_{m})\phi({\mathsf{L}_{m-1}})\cdots\phi(\mathsf{L}_{1})\phi(\mathsf{V})(|0\rangle\!\langle{0}|),

(17)

averaged independently over each of the
\Omega
-distributed layers
\mathsf{L}_{i}
, and uniformly over the initial single-qubit gate layer
\mathsf{V}\in\mathds{L}(\mathds{G}_{1})
with
\Omega_{1}
. This noisy output can of course now be mixed,
2^{-N}\leq\tr(\tilde{\varrho}_{m}^{2})\leq 1
.

Our protocol focuses on efficiently estimating the purities of the states
\tilde{\varrho}_{m}
, to in turn estimate the average unitarity of noise in the circuits
\mathsf{C}_{m}
. In the following, we first study the behavior of the average purity of
\tilde{\varrho}_{m}
in increasing circuit depth

B.1
Purity of the initial randomization

Let us consider first the case of no gate layers but just the noisy initial single-qubit Clifford layer,
m=0
, then we have

\displaystyle\tr(\tilde{\varrho}_{0}^{2})

\displaystyle=\expect_{\mathsf{V}\sim\Omega_{1}}\tr[\phi(\mathsf{V})(|0\rangle\!\langle 0|)\phi(\mathsf{V})(|0\rangle\!\langle 0|)^{\dagger}]

\displaystyle:=\expect_{\psi\sim\Omega_{1}}\tr[\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{spam}}(|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|)\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{spam}}(|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|)^{\dagger}],

(18)

where we defined the noisy implementation of
\mathsf{V}
as
\phi(\mathsf{V}):=\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{spam}}\,\mathcal{U}_{\mathsf{V}}
for some noise channel
\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{spam}}
explicitly standing for
State Preparation and Measurement (SPAM)
noise, and where we defined the initial randomized pure state as
\mathcal{U}_{\mathsf{V}}(|0\rangle\!\langle 0|):=|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|
.

Noticing that the noisy outputs
\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{spam}}(|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|)
are quantum states and thus Hermitian, and that we can define the self-adjoint map
\mathcal{X}^{\dagger}
of a quantum channel
\mathcal{X}
by the property
\tr[A\mathcal{X}(B)]:=\tr[\mathcal{X}^{\dagger}(A)B]
, we can write

\displaystyle\tr(\tilde{\varrho}_{0}^{2})

\displaystyle=\expect_{\psi\sim\Omega_{1}}\langle\psi|\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{spam}}^{\dagger}\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{spam}}(|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|)|\psi\rangle.

(19)

Equivalently, the self-adjoint map of a quantum channel can be defined in terms of its Kraus operators as
\mathcal{X}^{\dagger}(\cdot):=\sum{K}_{\mu}^{\dagger}(\cdot)K_{\mu}
, where
\{K_{\mu}\}
the Kraus operators of
\mathcal{X}
, so Eq. (
19
) in a sense is already measuring how different
\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{spam}}
is from a unitary.

In general, the purity of a quantum state,
\rho:=\mathcal{X}(|\varphi\rangle\!\langle\varphi|)
can be written as
\tr(\rho^{2}):=\langle\varphi|\mathcal{X}^{\dagger}\mathcal{X}(|\varphi\rangle\!\langle\varphi|)|\varphi\rangle
, which corresponds to the gate fidelity of
\mathcal{X}^{\dagger}\mathcal{X}
with respect to the identity on the state
|\varphi\rangle
. Thus, we can write Eq. (
19

\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{spam}}^{\dagger}\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{spam}}
with respect to the identity, averaged over all possible initial states
|\psi\rangle
,

\displaystyle\tr(\tilde{\varrho}_{0}^{2})

\displaystyle=\expect_{\psi\sim\Omega_{1}}\mathsf{F}_{\psi}(\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{spam}}^{\dagger}\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{spam}}),

(20)

where here

\mathsf{F}_{\psi}(\mathcal{X}):=\langle\psi|\mathcal{X}(|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|)|\psi\rangle,

(21)

is the gate-fidelity of the map
\mathcal{X}
with respect to the identity map on the state
|\psi\rangle
.

B.2
Relation to average unitarity and average trace-decrease

Ultimately, however, the average loss of purity in the average state
\tilde{\varrho}_{0}
must be related to some amount of
non-unitarity
of the noise, i.e., it is noise that decreases purity. This can be quantified by the average unitarity,
\expect_{|\psi\rangle}\mathsf{u}_{\psi}
, defined for a
CP
map
\mathcal{E}
as

\expect_{\psi\sim\Omega_{1}}\mathsf{u}_{\psi}(\mathcal{E}):=\left(\frac{2^{n}}{2^{n}-1}\right)\expect_{\psi\sim\Omega_{1}}\mathsf{F}_{\psi}(\mathcal{E}^{\prime\,\dagger}\mathcal{E}^{\prime}),\quad\text{where}\quad\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\cdot):=\mathcal{E}(\cdot-\mathds{1}/2^{n}),

(22)

which is so defined to account for the case of
\mathcal{E}
being trace-decreasing. When
\mathcal{E}
is
Trace Preserving (TP)
we have the relation
\mathcal{E}^{\dagger\,\prime}\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\cdot)=\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}\mathcal{E}(\cdot-\mathds{1}/2^{n})
, otherwise, however, for any quantum state
\rho
,

\mathcal{E}^{\dagger\,\prime}\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\rho)=\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}\mathcal{E}\left(\rho-\frac{\mathds{1}}{2^{n}}\right)-\tr\left[\mathcal{E}\left(\rho-\frac{\mathds{1}}{2^{n}}\right)\right]\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}\left(\frac{\mathds{1}}{2^{n}}\right),

(23)

thus

\displaystyle\left(\frac{2^{n}-1}{2^{n}}\right)\expect_{\psi\sim\Omega_{1}}\mathsf{u}_{\psi}(\mathcal{E})

\displaystyle=\expect_{\psi\sim\Omega_{1}}\mathsf{F}_{\psi}(\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}\mathcal{E})

\displaystyle\qquad-\expect_{\psi\sim\Omega_{1}}\langle\psi|\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}\mathcal{E}(\mathds{1}/2^{n})|\psi\rangle-\expect_{\psi\sim\Omega_{1}}\tr\left[\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\left(|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|\right)\right]\langle\psi|\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}(\mathds{1}/2^{n})|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=\expect_{\psi\sim\Omega_{1}}\mathsf{F}_{\psi}(\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}\mathcal{E})-2^{-n}\expect_{\psi\sim\Omega_{1}}\left\{\mathsf{S}_{\psi}(\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}\mathcal{E})+\mathsf{S}_{\psi}(\mathcal{E})\left[\mathsf{S}_{\psi}(\mathcal{E})-\tr[\mathcal{E}(\mathds{1}/2^{n})]\right]\right\},

(24)

where

\mathsf{S}_{\psi}(\mathcal{E}):=\tr[\mathcal{E}(|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|)],

(25)

is a trace-preservation measure of the map
\mathcal{E}
for a given sample
|\psi\rangle
.

We can then write the average purity of
\tilde{\varrho}_{0}
as

\displaystyle\tr(\tilde{\varrho}_{0}^{2})

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{2^{n}-1}{2^{n}}\right)\expect_{\psi\sim\Omega_{1}}\mathsf{u}_{\psi}(\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{spam}})

\displaystyle\quad+2^{-n}\expect_{\psi\sim\Omega_{1}}\left\{\mathsf{S}_{\psi}(\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{spam}}^{\dagger}\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{spam}})+\mathsf{S}_{\psi}(\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{spam}})\left[\mathsf{S}_{\psi}(\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{spam}})-\tr[\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{spam}}(\mathds{1}/2^{n})]\right]\right\},

(26)

which we recall is defined as averaged over the states
|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|=\mathcal{U}_{\mathsf{V}}(|0\rangle\!\langle 0|)
, which are distributed uniformly and independently on the
n
-qubits according to a distribution
\Omega_{1}
on single-qubit gates
\mathds{G}_{1}
.

When the noise channel
\mathcal{E}
is
TP
, the rightmost summand in Eq. (
24
) becomes a term equal to
-2^{-n}
, and in that case the average unitarity is just a re-scaling of the average gate-fidelity of the form

\expect_{\psi\sim\Omega_{1}}\mathsf{u}_{\psi}(\mathcal{E})\quad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathcal{E}\,\text{is TP})}}}{{=}}\quad\frac{2^{n}\expect_{\psi\sim\Omega_{1}}\mathsf{F}_{\psi}(\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}\mathcal{E})-1}{2^{n}-1}.

(27)

and thus also the average purity in Eq. (
20

\displaystyle\tr(\tilde{\varrho}_{0}^{2})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{spam}}\,\text{is TP})}}}{{=}}\left(\frac{2^{n}-1}{2^{n}}\right)\expect_{\psi\sim\Omega_{1}}\mathsf{u}_{\psi}(\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{spam}})+\frac{1}{2^{n}}.

(28)

B.3
The case of global depolarizing noise

A particular case of a quantum channel for modeling noise that is both easy mathematically as in interpretation is that of global depolarizing channel,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{p}(\cdot):=p\,(\cdot)+(1-p)\tr(\cdot)\,\frac{\mathds{1}}{2^{n}},

(29)

where
0\leq{p}\leq 1
is the probability for the input state to remain the same, and which can be directly defined in terms of the channel by the so-called polarization
\gamma
,

\gamma(\mathcal{E}):=\frac{4^{n}\mathrm{F}_{\mathsf{ent}}(\mathcal{E})-1}{4^{n}-1},

(30)

where here

\mathrm{F}_{\mathsf{ent}}(\mathcal{E}):=\langle\Psi|(\mathcal{I}\otimes\mathcal{E})[|\Psi\rangle\!\langle\Psi|]|\Psi\rangle,\qquad\text{where}\quad|\Psi\rangle:=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{2^{n}}|ii\rangle}{\sqrt{2^{n}}},

(31)

is the so-called entanglement fidelity (equivalent to the purity of the so-called Choi state of
\mathcal{E}
). Because maximally entangled states can be written in the Pauli basis as
|\Psi\rangle\!\langle\Psi|=4^{-n}\sum_{P\in\mathsf{Pauli}_{n}}P\otimes{P}^{\mathrm{T}}
, this is equivalent to

\mathrm{F}_{\mathsf{ent}}(\mathcal{E})=\frac{1}{4^{n}}\sum_{P\in\mathsf{Pauli}_{n}}\frac{1}{2^{n}}\tr[P\mathcal{E}(P)]=\frac{1}{4^{n}}\tr(\bm{\mathcal{E}}),

(32)

where we identified the matrix
\bm{\mathcal{E}}
, with elements
\bm{\mathcal{E}}_{ij}=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\tr[P_{i}\mathcal{E}(P_{j})]
, as the Pauli Transfer Matrix representation of
\mathcal{E}
.

For a depolarizing channel, in particular,

\gamma(\mathcal{E}_{p})=p.

(33)

Also, a composition of depolarizing channels with the same polarization is just another depolarizing channel as

\underbrace{\mathcal{E}_{p}\cdots\mathcal{E}_{p}}_{k\,\text{times}}=\mathcal{E}_{p^{k}}.

(34)

Given the above property, for a depolarizing channel, we also have

\displaystyle\mathsf{u}_{\psi}(\mathcal{E}_{p})

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{n}\mathsf{F}_{\psi}(\mathcal{E}_{p^{2}})-1}{2^{n}-1}

\displaystyle=\gamma(\mathcal{E}_{p})^{2}

\displaystyle:=\mathsf{u}(\mathcal{E}_{p}),

(35)

for any state
|\psi\rangle
. To stress this independence of the state
\psi
, we drop the subindex in the last line, which simply means
\mathsf{u}(\mathcal{E}_{p}):=\mathsf{u}_{\varphi}(\mathcal{E}_{p})
for an arbitrary pure state
|\varphi\rangle
.

Notice that,
\mathcal{E}_{p}\,\mathcal{U}_{\mathsf{C}}=\mathcal{U}_{\mathsf{C}}\,\mathcal{E}_{p}
, or more generally,

\mathcal{E}_{p}\,\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{X}\mathcal{E}_{p},

(36)

whenever
\mathcal{X}
is a unital and
TP
quantum channel. Furthermore, for any unital and
TP
channel
\mathcal{X}
, the unitarity
\mathsf{u}_{\psi}(\mathcal{E}_{p}\mathcal{X})
only depends on
\mathsf{F}_{\psi}(\mathcal{X}^{\dagger}\mathcal{E}_{p^{2}}\mathcal{X})
, which satisfies

\displaystyle\mathsf{F}_{\psi}(\mathcal{X}^{\dagger}\mathcal{E}_{p^{2}}\mathcal{X})

\displaystyle=\langle\psi|\mathcal{X}^{\dagger}\mathcal{E}_{p^{2}}\mathcal{X}(|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|)|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=p^{2}\mathsf{F}_{\psi}(\mathcal{X}^{\dagger}\mathcal{X})+(1-p^{2})2^{-n},

\displaystyle=\mathsf{u}(\mathcal{E}_{p})\left(\mathsf{F}_{\psi}(\mathcal{X}^{\dagger}\mathcal{X})-2^{-n}\right)+2^{-n},

(37)

and so

\mathsf{u}_{\psi}(\mathcal{E}_{p}\mathcal{X})=\frac{2^{n}\mathsf{u}(\mathcal{E}_{p})\left(\mathsf{F}_{\psi}(\mathcal{X}^{\dagger}\mathcal{X})-2^{-n}\right)}{2^{n}-1}=\mathsf{u}(\mathcal{E}_{p})\mathsf{u}_{\psi}(\mathcal{X}),

(38)

i.e. the unitarity this way for depolarizing channels factorizes.

Appendix C
Operational estimation of the purity

Now we know how the purity of the noisy average state

in Eq. (

C.1
Purity estimation through randomized measurements

RM
s found some of their first applications in estimating the purity of the quantum state of a subsystem
[
30
,
31
]
. Specifically, here we will focus on the results of
[
32
,
33
]
, whereby it is shown that for a reduced quantum state
\rho_{A}=\tr_{B}(\rho_{AB})
, from some composite closed quantum system

, where here we assume
A
to be a
n
-qubit quantum system, its purity can be expressed as

\tr(\rho_{\mathsf{A}}^{2})=2^{n}\sum_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{{s}^{\prime}}}(-2)^{-h(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{s}^{\prime})}\expect_{\mathsf{U}\sim\mathsf{Haar}}\left[\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{U}}(\mathbf{s})\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{U}}(\mathbf{s}^{\prime})\right],

(46)

where
\mathbf{s}
and
\mathbf{s}^{\prime}
are
n
-bit strings,
h(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{s}^{\prime})
is the Hamming distance (number of distinct bits) between them,
\expect_{\mathsf{U}\sim\mathsf{Haar}}
denotes uniform averaging with the Haar measure over the local unitaries
\mathsf{U}=\Motimes_{i=1}^{n}\mathsf{U}_{i}
,
10
10

10

That is,
\mathds{E}_{\mathsf{Haar}}
here refers to a uniform and independent average over all the local unitaries
U_{i}
with the Haar measure, i.e.,
\mathds{E}_{\mathsf{Haar}}\sim\mathds{E}_{U_{1}}\mathds{E}_{U_{2}}\cdots\mathds{E}_{U_{N}}
and the

\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{U}}(\mathbf{s}):=\langle\mathbf{s}|\,\mathcal{U}_{\mathsf{U}}(\rho_{\mathsf{A}})|\mathbf{s}\rangle,

(47)

are noiseless probabilities of observing the
n
-bit string
\mathbf{s}
upon applying the unitary
\mathsf{U}
on the reduced state
\rho_{\mathsf{A}}
.

The main advantage here is that the quantum component of estimating purity this way is just the probabilities in Eq. (
47
), which simply means applying a product of random unitaries and measuring each on a user-specified basis. The rest, i.e., fully estimating Eq. (
46

n
qubits, the number of distinct terms in the sum over
n
-bit strings in Eq. (
46

; however, this sum is only a function of the probabilities
