Recyclable tannic acid modified magnetic nanoparticles for ciprofloxacin adsorption
Authors: Mona Hamelian, Hosna Janjani, Roya Moradi, Sanaz Fathi, Tooraj Massahi, Nazir Fattahi, Masoud Moradi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The rapid expansion of human and industrial activities has intensified antibiotic consumption, such as Ciprofloxacin (CIP), leading to the release of resistant compounds into aquatic systems and posing significant human health and environmental risks. In this study, tannic acid–functionalized magnetite nanoparticles (TA/Fe3O4 NPs) were synthesized via chemical precipitation and evaluated as efficient, magnetically separable, and recyclable adsorbents for CIP removal. Structural and morphological features were confirmed by XRD, BET, and SEM-EDS analyses. Key operational parameters, including pH, contact time, initial CIP concentration, and adsorbent dosage, were systematically optimized using response surface methodology (RSM). Under optimized conditions (pH = 10.47, adsorbent dosage = 1.43 g/L, contact time = 89.4 min, initial CIP concentration = 2 mg/L), the maximum removal efficiency reached 87.43%. Equilibrium modeling indicated that the Langmuir isotherm (R2 = 0.9922) best described the adsorption process, with a maximum capacity of 4.27 mg/g, suggesting uniform monolayer coverage of CIP molecules. Kinetic analysis revealed that the Elovich model (R2 = 0.9775) provided the most accurate fit, consistent with heterogeneous active sites and a physicochemical adsorption mechanism. Reusability tests demonstrated only an 8% decline in efficiency after five adsorption–desorption cycles, confirming the material’s stability and durability. Mechanistic insights highlighted the role of tannic acid’s hydroxyl and carboxyl groups, hydrogen bonding, π–π interactions, coordination effects, and ternary Fe–TA–CIP complexes in facilitating adsorption. Overall, TA/Fe3O4 NPs represent a cost-effective, sustainable, and environmentally benign strategy for antibiotic removal from aqueous environments, offering strong potential for practical application.

Introduction

Recently, significant quantities of various antibiotics have been introduced into aquatic environments, including surface water, groundwater, and wastewater treatment plant effluent. CIP is among the most significant antibiotics introduced into aquatic environments, owing to its efficacy against gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria. Reports indicate the different concentration ranges of CIP in sewage treatment plant effluents (0.2 to 1 µg/L), surface water (0.0018 to 19.617 nmol/L), hospital effluents (up to 14 mg/L), and drug manufacturers (up to 31 mg/L)1–3. CIP exhibits long-term stability in aqueous environments, which is attributed to its high chemical stability. This property hinders biological activity and diminishes the efficacy of conventional treatment processes aimed at its removal4. Consequently, the accumulation of CIP in aquatic environments is associated with the emergence of antimicrobial resistance (AMR), which poses a significant threat to public health and the environment. Therefore, the development of effective strategies for the removal of antibiotics from contaminated water sources has emerged as a critical research topic in environmental chemistry5,6.

Various approaches have been proposed for CIP removal, including biological treatment, advanced oxidation processes, ozonation, etc. However, these methods often encounter challenges such as inefficient removal, complex procedures, and high energy demands. In contrast, the adsorption method utilizing various adsorbents presents greater potential due to its low cost, high performance, and flexibility2,3.

Numerous adsorbents have been explored for the removal of ciprofloxacin, encompassing activated carbon, biochar, municipal and industrial residues, graphene- and polymer-based composites, zeolites, aerogels, metal–organic frameworks (MOFs), and carbon nanotubes. While these materials demonstrate strong adsorption capacities toward various contaminants, they are constrained by several drawbacks inherent to high-performance adsorbents, such as prolonged activation procedures, substantial energy demands, and reliance on complex or costly separation techniques. These limitations have stimulated the search for novel and more efficient alternatives. Nanomaterials represent a novel approach to enhancing the efficiency of processes to remove organic and inorganic pollutants. These materials offer significant promise for pollutant removal due to their unique properties, which include a high volume-to-surface ratio, magnetic characteristics, catalytic activity, and increased reactivity compared to traditional materials. The application of nanomaterials in environmental remediation has garnered considerable attention from researchers, as they can effectively address the limitations associated with conventional treatment methods7. To prevent generating secondary waste and facilitate the recovery and reuse of nanoparticles, the use of magnetic materials presents a promising approach for solid–liquid phase separation techniques8. Magnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (MNPs) have emerged as particularly promising candidates for the adsorption of recalcitrant pollutants, owing to their exceptionally high specific surface area, intrinsic magnetic responsiveness, facile mobility, efficient post-adsorption recovery, and straightforward synthesis9.

Harres et al. have reported that magnetite-based catalysts have garnered considerable attention owing to their remarkable features, including biocompatibility, facile synthesis, minimal toxicity, superparamagnetic properties, and outstanding chemical and physical stability. These attributes render magnetite-based catalysts highly effective nanomaterials for the remediation of both organic contaminants10.

However, the inherent limitations of raw Fe3O4, such as low surface reactivity and susceptibility to aggregation, necessitate a surface modification to enhance its adsorption capacity and selectivity11. Previous studies have shown that humic-like compounds are widely used in the modification of various adsorbents because a key feature of these materials is the presence of strong functional groups that can form complexes with metal species and influence the mobility of metals as well as their oxidation and reduction states. In addition, these materials regulate the adsorption rate of metals on the adsorbent12.

Tannic acid, as a humic-like compound, is widely used in the adsorption process due to its environmental friendliness, cost-effectiveness, and sustainability. Tannic acid, a natural polyphenol, can be extracted from readily available sources like the bark, fruits, and seeds of various plants. A key characteristic of this biopolymer is its high abundance of hydroxyl groups and phenolic rings, which significantly enhances its ability to chelate metal ions. As a result, tannin has become a promising adsorbent for the separation of metal ions13,14. Zou et al. have reported that Tannic acid molecules contain five 1,2,3-trihydroxyphenyl groups, which facilitate the formation of strong bonds between the pollutant and the tannic acid-modified adsorbent. These structural features enhance the adsorption capacity of the modified adsorbent, promoting effective pollutant removal in various applications15.

In previous studies, tannic acid has been employed to modify adsorbents such as activated carbon, coconut shell, and graphene. The findings indicate that tannic acid significantly enhances the adsorption capacity of these materials, leading to improved removal of pollutants16–19.

Therefore, the application of low-cost, magnetically recoverable adsorbents has emerged as a promising strategy for the removal of persistent and hard-to-degrade pollutants from aqueous environments. Owing to their strong multi-dentate bonding capabilities, these materials facilitate efficient pollutant separation and recovery while minimizing the risk of secondary contamination. In this study, we synthesized Fe3O4 nanoparticles functionalized with tannic acid and investigated their performance in the adsorption and removal of the antibiotic ciprofloxacin, a widely used pharmaceutical and a common environmental contaminant. To maximize adsorption efficiency, the operating parameters were systematically optimized using response surface methodology (RSM). Furthermore, the influence of tannic acid modification on the physicochemical properties of the nanoparticles was examined, and the underlying mechanisms governing ciprofloxacin adsorption were elucidated. The findings provide valuable insights into the design of sustainable, magnetically recoverable adsorbents for advanced wastewater treatment applications.

Materials and methods

Preparation of Fe3O4 NPs

The magnetic Fe3O4 nanoparticles were prepared using the chemical precipitation method, and the detailed procedure is described as follows. FeCl2·4H2O (2 g) and FeCl3·6H2O (5.2 g) were dissolved in 25 mL of deoxygenated water, and then 0.85 mL of concentrated hydrochloric acid was added. The resulting solution was carefully dropped into 250 mL of 1.5 M NaOH solution under vigorous stirring and nitrogen (N2) protection at 353 K. The obtained magnetic nanoparticles were separated from the solution using a powerful magnet and rinsed with 200 mL of deionized water three times. Finally, the products were dried at 40 °C to yield Fe3O4 nanoparticles.

Preparation of the TA/Fe3O4 NPs

For the synthesis of TA/Fe3O4 NPs, in the first step, magnetite nanoparticles (500 mg) were dispersed in 200 mL of water and sonicated for 20 min. Subsequently, tannic acid (0.5 g) dissolved in 500 mL of water was added to the mixture. The solution was then stirred for 12 h at 40 °C. The TA/Fe3O4 NPs nanoparticles precipitate was separated by magnetic decantation and washed several times with deionized water. Finally, the TA/Fe3O4 NPs were dried in a vacuum oven at 40 °C for 12 h.

Nanocomposite characterization

The nanocomposite was characterized using various techniques, including FE-SEM with EDS, XRD, and BET.

Adsorption experiments

Batch experiments were conducted in a series of 150 mL glass conical flasks. Each flask was charged with TA/Fe3O4 powder at concentrations ranging from 0.6 to 2 g/L and 50 mL of an aqueous antibiotic solution at initial concentrations ranging from 0.5 to 4 mg/L. The flasks were shaken for preselected time intervals. The pH of the working solutions, buffered with 10 mmol/L sodium borate, was adjusted to range from 4.6 to 11 using HCl or NaOH solutions. The residual antibiotic concentration was determined after filtering the supernatant through 0.22 μm PES syringe filters (Pall, USA). Preliminary experiments without adsorbent indicated that the loss of antibiotic due to adsorption on the flask walls and filter interception was negligible under the experimental conditions applied. After adsorption, the TA/Fe3O4 nanocomposite was regenerated with a 60% (v/v) methanol solution. The used TA/Fe3O4 suspension and methanol solution were shaken at 125 r/min at ambient temperature for 30 min. The TA/Fe3O4 was then magnetically separated, dried in an oven at 65 °C for 1 h, and reused for antibiotic adsorption. The reusability test was performed over five cycles in total.

The study of isotherms and kinetics

Isotherm data were generated at a constant adsorbent dose of 1 g/L, using initial CIP concentrations spanning 0.25–10 mg/L and a fixed contact time of 180 min to approximate equilibrium. For each concentration, the equilibrium liquid-phase concentration Ce and corresponding adsorbed amount qe were determined and subsequently used to fit appropriate isotherm models. Triplicate runs were performed to assess reproducibility, and blank controls without adsorbent were included to account for any non-adsorptive losses. Reaction kinetics were measured at an adsorbent dose of 1 g/L with an initial CIP concentration of 2 mg/L, monitoring the time course from 0 to 180 min. Time‐dependent concentrations Ct were obtained at multiple intervals to construct adsorption uptake profiles qt and evaluate kinetic rate constants. The sampling frequency was selected to resolve both the initial rapid uptake phase and the subsequent approach to pseudo‐equilibrium. Langmuir isotherm

The Langmuir isotherm model assumes that adsorption takes place on a structurally homogeneous adsorbent surface, where each active site is energetically equivalent and accommodates only a single adsorbate molecule, thereby forming a uniform monolayer without lateral interactions. Freundlich isotherm

The Freundlich isotherm model describes adsorption on energetically heterogeneous surfaces, where multilayer adsorption can occur and uptake capacity increases with solute concentration. The equation of the Freundlich isotherm model is presented as follows (Eq. 5):5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{q}_{e}={K}_{F}{{C}_{e}}^{\frac{1}{n}}$$\end{document}

Values of n < 1 indicate cooperative adsorption behavior, whereas n > 1 suggests a heterogeneous surface with energetically diverse sites and a generally favorable adsorption process.

Tmekin isotherm

The proposed isotherm incorporates a parameter that directly accounts for the interactions between the adsorbent surface and the adsorbed species. To simplify the analysis, the model disregards extremely low and excessively high concentration ranges. Under this assumption, the heat of adsorption, considered as a temperature-dependent property, decreases in a linear fashion with surface coverage, rather than following a logarithmic trend as in other models. The mathematical formulation further implies that adsorption sites are characterized by a uniform distribution of binding energies, up to a defined maximum value. In this model, the parameters AT and BT (RT/bT) were obtained by nonlinear fitting of the qe and Ce data, and the value of BT was subsequently used to calculate the adsorption energy. Reusability and desorption test

To assess the recyclability and stability of the tannic acid–modified magnetite adsorbent, desorption and reusability experiments were conducted over 5 consecutive adsorption–desorption cycles. After completing the adsorption process under optimal conditions (initial ciprofloxacin concentration = 2 mg/L, adsorbent dose = 1.43 g/L, pH = 10.47, and contact time = 90 min), the adsorbent was separated from the aqueous solution using a strong neodymium magnet. The collected adsorbent was then transferred to 20 mL of a dilute methanol solution (80% v/v) without drying and subjected to stirring at 200 rpm for 60 min to desorb the adsorbed CIP. Following desorption, the adsorbent was magnetically separated and washed three times with distilled water to remove residual methanol and ciprofloxacin. The regenerated adsorbent was subsequently reused for ciprofloxacin adsorption under identical conditions. This process was repeated for 5 consecutive cycles. After each cycle, the CIP removal efficiency and desorption were calculated according to Eqs. Results and discussion

Adsorbent characterization

XRD analysis

The XRD pattern of TA/Fe3O4 shows peaks at the 2θ values of 18.3° (111), 30.1° (220), 35.5° (311), 37.1° (222), 43.1° (400), 47.2° (331), 53.5° (422), 57.0° (511), 62.6° (440), 65.8° (531), 66.9° (442), 71.0° (620), 74.1° (533), 75.1° (622), 79.1° (444), 82.0° (551), 86.9° (642), and 89.8° (731), which matches the reference cubic structure of CCDC 1,645,11927 (Fig. 1a). The findings reveal that this structure maintains the inverted spinel arrangement typical of magnetite. Furthermore, the absence of additional peaks suggests that impurity phases like γ-Fe2O3 or α-Fe2O3 are not present in the sample28.

The XRD results also revealed that the diffraction peaks of TA/Fe3O4 nanoparticles exhibited high intensity, suggesting a high degree of crystallinity. This characteristic is crucial for preserving the nanoparticles’ magnetic properties and structural integrity. Furthermore, the analysis showed no shift or broadening of the peaks, indicating that TA had no impact on the lattice structure or defect concentration within the Fe3O4 nanoparticles. These findings support the stability and compatibility of TA with Fe3O4 in nanostructured forms. The XRD analysis conducted by Rasheed et al. demonstrated that the modification of bentonite using tannic acid did not significantly alter the intensity or the position of its characteristic diffraction peaks29.

BET analysis

Figure 1b presents the N2 adsorption-desorption isotherm for TA/Fe3O4, which exhibits a type IV isotherm characteristic of mesoporous materials30. The isotherm displays an H1 hysteresis loop, indicative of cylindrical pores, consistent with the material’s morphology. Further analysis using the BET model yielded a specific surface area of 29.22 m2/g and a specific volume of 6.71 cm3 (STP)/g for the TA/Fe3O4 nanoparticles31. The Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) model was employed to determine the average pore diameter, which was calculated to be 2.45 nm. A study conducted by Singh et al. (2020) utilized BET analysis to evaluate the structural properties of Fe3O4 adsorbent. The analysis revealed a surface area of 25.37 m2/g and a pore volume of 0.16 cm3 (STP/g. Additionally, the study identified that the majority of pore diameters fell within the 2 to 50 nm range, with a predominant diameter of 11 nm. These findings suggest that the adsorbent exhibits a mesoporous structure, which is consistent with its intended application in adsorption processes32. The surface area of TA/Fe3O4 is observed to be marginally higher than that of Fe3O4 alone. This enhancement can be attributed to the influence of TA on the adsorbent’s properties. Previous research has demonstrated that the presence of TA introduces functional groups, such as hydroxyl and carboxyl moieties, which significantly enhance the adsorption capacity of the material29.

This relatively low coverage is primarily attributed to the low initial ciprofloxacin concentration, which reduces the number of molecules available to occupy the active sites. FSEM-EDS analysis

The morphological characteristics of the adsorbent were investigated using the FSEM-EDS technique. The analysis revealed that the surface of the TA/Fe3O4 adsorbent exhibits significant roughness, suggesting its potential to provide an enhanced surface area for adsorption processes (Fig. 1c). Additionally, the FSEM images demonstrated a semi-spherical morphology, consistent with observations reported in prior studies on similar materials37. In addition, the quantitative and qualitative composition of the TA/Fe3O4 nanoparticle was assessed using EDS analysis (Fig. 1d). The results revealed that this nanoparticle consists of Fe, O, and C, with atomic percentages of 42.86%, 31.48%, and 25.66%, respectively. The presence of carbon in this composite is attributed to the TA component. Figure 1e presents the spatial elemental mapping of the TA/Fe3O4 nanoparticle surface, illustrating the distribution of various elements. As evident from Fig. 1e, the intensity of the color corresponding to Fe is more pronounced compared to that of other constituent elements within the adsorbent (O & C). This observation aligns with and corroborates the atomic percentage data obtained from the EDS analysis.

The study of isothermal and kinetics

Adsorption isotherms serve as indispensable tools for elucidating the equilibrium partitioning of contaminants between aqueous and solid phases, thereby offering critical insights into adsorption mechanisms, sorbent capacity, and the rational design of treatment systems. In the present work, four classical models, including Langmuir, Freundlich, Temkin, and Dubinin–Radushkevich (D–R), were assessed using equilibrium data expressed as Ce and qe (Fig. 6a). Previous studies have indicated that nonlinear regression is more suitable for determining isotherm and kinetic parameters than linear regression because linearization can lead to inaccurate interpretations and the coefficient of determination (R2) alone is insufficient to identify the optimal model. Therefore, nonlinear regression was employed for parameter estimation, and model performance was compared using statistical indices including the correlation coefficient (R2), root mean square error (RMSE), chi-square (χ2), and the sum of squared errors (SSE).

The Langmuir isotherm posits that sorption proceeds via a single molecular layer on uniform binding sites, devoid of lateral interactions among adsorbates, as captured by Eq. (4)​​. Our analysis yielded parameters of qmax = 4.2705 mg/g and KL = 0.53 L/mg. Notably, a KL​ value exceeding 0.1 underscores the adsorbent’s pronounced affinity for CIP; conversely, the modest qmax​ likely arises from constraints in surface area or experimental variables, such as optimized pH. These observations align with contemporary investigations, wherein the Langmuir framework optimally fitted CIP uptake onto microwave-alkali-modified fly ash, delivering qmax ≈ 12.5 mg/g and R2 > 0.99, while accentuating the exothermic character of the reaction38.

The Freundlich isotherm, adept at characterizing adsorption on heterogeneous surfaces with potential multilayer formation, is articulated by the Eq. (5)​. In this investigation, parameters KF​ = 1.39 and n = 2.05 were derived, where n > 1 n > 1 n > 1 signifies favorable adsorption underpinned by a synergy of physical and chemical interactions. Nevertheless, elevated error metrics relative to the Langmuir model suggest that heterogeneous mechanisms are not predominant. This trend echoes findings in recent literature, such as the adsorption of CIP onto activated carbon from compressed wood, where the Freundlich model achieved an R2 = 0.99, with KF​=21.73 and n = 1.97, underscoring physisorption driven by hydrogen bonding and van der Waals forces39. Similarly, activated carbon from rice husk exhibited Freundlich dominance with a capacity of 2272 mg/g, highlighting the contrast with our study’s lower capacity, likely due to the adsorbent’s limited specific surface area40. Consequently, while the Freundlich model elucidates potential heterogeneity in adsorption sites, the Langmuir model’s superior fit reinforces the prevalence of a homogeneous adsorption mechanism.

The Temkin isotherm, predicated on a linear diminution of adsorption energy as surface coverage increases, is articulated by the Eq. (6). In this study, parameters AT = 11.96 L/g and bT = 3.5 J/mol were determined, with the constant B = RT/bT ≈ 708 (where R = 8.314 J/mol.K, T = 298 K), signifying a physicochemical adsorption process marked by substantial energy variations and potentially stronger interactions. The relatively modest R2 = 0.9379 underscores the model’s limitations in systems with uniform energy distribution. These findings resonate with prior research on CIP adsorption onto graphene-like carbon oxide, where the Temkin model, with R2 = 0.98 and bT ≈ 2000–3000 J/mol, highlighted a progressive decline in adsorption energy with surface occupancy41,42. The Temkin model in this context accentuates the presence of notable lateral interactions among adsorbates, providing critical insights for refining adsorbent design to optimize efficacy in real-world applications.

The Dubinin–Radushkevich (D–R) isotherm, designed to elucidate adsorption within microporous structures, is represented by Eq. (8). Analysis yielded parameters qDR=3.1179 mg/g, β = 2.2093 × 10− 7 mol2/kJ− 2, and E = 1.5044 kJ/mol. The low E value (< 8 kJ/mol) underscores a predominantly physical adsorption mechanism, driven by van der Waals forces or pore-filling processes. However, the suboptimal fit (R2 = 0.9372) suggests that pore filling is not the primary mechanism governing CIP uptake. This observation aligns with prior research on a graphene oxide/chitosan composite, where the D-R model, with E ≈ 2–5 kJ/mol and R2 = 0.96, similarly affirmed a physical adsorption process43.

The temporal evolution of adsorption was analyzed by fitting the experimental qt–t data to four widely employed kinetic models: pseudo-first-order (PFO), pseudo-second-order (PSO), Elovich, and intraparticle diffusion (IPD). Nonlinear least-squares regression was adopted to avoid distortions introduced by linearization (Fig. 6b). From these fits, model parameters, 95% confidence intervals, and statistical indices of goodness-of-fit (R2, RMSE, SSE, and χ2) were obtained. Among the tested models, the Elovich equation yielded the most accurate description of the data (R2 = 0.9775, RMSE = 0.0523, SSE = 0.0355, χ2 = 0.2536), marginally outperforming both PSO and PFO. The Elovich framework presumes a heterogeneous adsorbent surface with sites of varying activation energies, a premise consistent with the polyphenol-functionalized Fe3O4 (tannic acid–Fe3O4) system, where energetically diverse binding domains are expected. The Elovich constants (α = 0.0159, β = 1.1940) corroborate that adsorption proceeds rapidly at the outset but progressively slows as active sites become saturated. Comparable kinetic behavior has been documented in related adsorption systems44.

Although the Elovich model provided the best overall fit, both PSO and PFO also achieved high correlation coefficients (R2 ≈ 0.976). The PSO model (k2 = 0.00261, qe = 2.35 mg/g) is often interpreted as evidence of chemisorption-driven uptake (Table 7). Nevertheless, it is important to note that a strong PSO fit does not unequivocally confirm chemisorption; in some cases, it merely reflects a mathematical approximation of the data trend45.

Effect of pH

The optimum pH for CIP adsorption was obtained at 10.47 (Fig. 5). The reason for the high efficiency at alkali pH is that CIP contains a secondary alkylamine, two tertiary arylamines (similar to aniline), and a carboxylic acid. This compound has a fluorine atom attached to the carboxylic acid functional group. Consequently, it has two pKa values: pKa1 = 6.1 and pKa2 = 8.2, and it exists in zwitterionic form at neutral pH (5.5–8). At acidic pH, the concentration of H+ ions is high, leading to competition between the positively charged ions of the CIP molecule and H+ ions, which results in decreased adsorption of CIP by the adsorbent. Additionally, the amine groups acquire hydrogen atoms at low pH, resulting in a net positive charge. Consequently, since the catalyst surface carries a positive charge at acidic pH, the positive charge of CIP reduces its adsorption due to repulsion between the two. In contrast, as pH increases, the amount of CIP adsorption rises because, up to pH 8, CIP molecules are neutral. Beyond this point, the adsorption efficiency does not change significantly since the carboxylic functional group becomes negatively charged, leading to reduced repulsion48,49.

Figure 7 shows the point of zero charge (pHzpc) of Fe3O4 and Fe3O4/TA nanoparticles. As results show, pHzpc is 7.07 for Fe3O4/TA and 7.45 for pristine magnetite. The phenolic and carboxylic groups of tannic acid shift the surface charge toward more acidic values, rendering the surface more positive under acidic conditions and more deprotonated under alkaline conditions. At first glance, this behavior appears counterintuitive, since both the adsorbent surface (negatively charged above pHzpc) and CIP (predominantly anionic at alkaline pH, with pKa1 ≈ 6.1 for the carboxyl group and pKa2 ≈ 8.7 for the piperazinyl group) should experience strong electrostatic repulsion. Nevertheless, the enhanced adsorption observed at pH 10.7 indicates the dominance of specific surface interactions beyond simple electrostatics.

Tannic acid can coordinate with Fe ions to form metal–phenolic networks, generating new Fe–TA complexation sites and providing aromatic, hydrogen-bonding, and π–π interaction domains. Recent studies have shown that TA–Fe(III)-based adsorbents are highly effective for fluoroquinolone removal, with Fe-mediated chelation playing a key role50. Under alkaline conditions, the fully deprotonated carboxylate group of CIP acts as a strong ligand for Fe2+/Fe3+ centers on magnetite or Fe–TA sites, forming robust inner-sphere coordination complexes that effectively overcome electrostatic repulsion51. Moreover, ternary Fe–TA–CIP complexes, π–π stacking between the quinolone ring of CIP and the galloyl units of TA, and hydrogen bonding between hydroxyl groups of TA and heteroatoms in CIP further enhance adsorption50,52.

Partial deprotonation of phenolic hydroxyls in TA at alkaline pH strengthens Fe binding and stabilizes the metal–phenolic framework, thereby creating additional coordination sites capable of capturing CIP carboxylates. This mechanism has been confirmed in recent studies on metal–phenolic networks and TA–Fe systems for pollutant removal53.

Overall, although both the Fe3O4/TA surface and CIP molecules are negatively charged at pH 10.7, strong coordination, chelation, π–π stacking, hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, and intraparticle diffusion collectively govern the adsorption process. These synergistic mechanisms explain the unexpectedly high adsorption efficiency under alkaline conditions and align with prior reports showing that even when electrostatic repulsion is expected, multiple mechanisms, including electrostatic attraction at lower pH, hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic effects, and pore diffusion, act simultaneously to enhance overall adsorption capacity9.

Tang et al.‘s (2018) research demonstrated that with rising pH levels, there is an enhancement in the deprotonation of oxide-containing groups, such as the phenolic hydroxyls found in TA. This results in two notable effects: firstly, the surface obtains a greater negative charge, thereby intensifying electrostatic attraction, which favors the adsorption of adsorbates. Secondly, the formation of additional water clusters on the adsorbent surface occurs, obstructing access and diminishing the number of sites available for adsorption by the adsorbate. As a result, the overall adsorption capacity initially rises but subsequently declines54.

Numerous investigations have elucidated that variations in the physicochemical characteristics of pollutants, influenced by differing pH levels, profoundly impact the efficacy of organic pollutant removal processes. Research findings diverge on the optimal pH conditions for adsorption. Certain studies suggest that acidic environments enhance pollutant removal, while others indicate superior adsorption efficiency under alkaline conditions. In contrast to the present analysis, Vargas GO et al. and da Rosa Salles et al. reported that the removal of furosemide using CNT-Fe3O4 adsorbents was maximized in acidic pH environments. This phenomenon is likely due to the pollutant’s pKa value of 3.8, which facilitates adsorption in acidic conditions through mechanisms such as hydrogen bonding (including Yoshida-type hydrogen bonds), π-π interactions, and cation-π interactions55,56.

Conversely, Nunes FB et al. observed that for the pollutant Citalopram, characterized by a pKa of approximately 9.78 and predominantly cationic at alkaline pH, adsorption is enhanced due to electrostatic attraction to the negatively charged surface of GO-Fe3O4 adsorbents, favoring alkaline conditions57.

Effect of adsorbent dosage

Results indicated that the removal efficiency of CIP was raised with the increase of the TA/Fe3O4 dosage. The initial increase in adsorption with increasing dosage can be attributed to the enhanced mass transfer of CIP molecules. This occurs because the number of active binding sites increases with dosage, allowing the same number of CIP molecules to bind more efficiently. However, using an excessively high dosage of adsorbent can lead to particle coagulation, which reduces the effective interactions between the adsorbent and CIP, ultimately resulting in decreased adsorption efficiency29. da Rosa Salles et al. reported that the removal efficiency of pollutants increases with increasing catalyst dosage. They attributed this enhancement to the greater available surface area and the higher number of active binding sites, which allow for stronger interactions with furosemide molecules. However, at higher adsorbent dosages, the removal efficiency decreases, which may be attributed to the aggregation of nanoparticles caused by van der Waals forces and intermolecular interactions among nanoadsorbent particles, thereby hindering the adsorption process56.

Moreover, the adsorption efficiency declines when the catalyst dosage surpasses an optimal threshold. This phenomenon arises from the underutilization of available surface area at elevated adsorbent quantities, where a higher proportion of active sites remains unoccupied despite increased material quantity. A contributing factor to this reduced capacity is interparticle aggregation, which limits accessibility to binding sites and disrupts adsorbate-adsorbent interactions52. da Rosa Salles et al. have suggested that the decline in adsorption efficiency observed at elevated adsorbent dosages may be attributed to the pronounced disparity between the concentration of adsorbate molecules and the available adsorbent surface sites, which in turn hinders effective mass transfer and reduces overall uptake performance56.

Effect of CIP concentration

Results indicated that the adsorption capacities of TA/Fe3O4 increased with higher initial concentrations of CIP, eventually reaching saturation at elevated concentrations. Ozmen et al. (2010) investigated the impact of the initial Cu(II) ion concentration on the adsorption process. Their results indicate that the initial concentration of the adsorbate serves as a crucial driving force that facilitates the overcoming of mass transfer resistances encountered by Cu(II) ions as they move between the aqueous and solid phases. Consequently, an increase in the initial concentration of Cu(II) ions can enhance the adsorption capacity8. Vargas GO et al. demonstrated that increasing the initial pollutant concentration enhances adsorption by providing a greater number of adsorbate molecules, thereby promoting stronger interactions with the adsorbent surface. However, at higher concentrations, the removal efficiency declines due to the saturation of active adsorption sites. Similarly, da Rosa Salles et al. reported that pollutant removal efficiency initially increases with concentration as a result of reduced mass transfer resistance, but further increases lead to a decline in efficiency, which is attributed to strong interactions among pollutant molecules that hinder their access to adsorption sites55,56.

Proposed mechanism of adsorption

The functional groups present in the structure of the adsorbent and adsorbate are important factors in the adsorption process. Electronegative oxygen atoms in the furan ring and carbonyl groups in ciprofloxacin help establish hydrogen bonds and electrostatic interactions. Since the surface charge of TA/Fe3O4 is negative, the positive charge of the amine functional groups in CIP enhances the electrostatic attraction of CIP molecules to the adsorbent19. Yasmin et al. (2024) identified three main mechanisms for CIP adsorption: hydrogen bonding, π-π interactions, and electrostatic forces. While electrostatic interactions are minimal due to the neutral surface charge of the adsorbent, π-π interactions between CIP and the adsorbent occur consistently across all pH levels. Hydrogen bonding involves electronegative atoms in CIP (e.g., F and O) interacting with partially electropositive hydrogen atoms on the adsorbent’s functional groups. When the pH surpasses the adsorbent’s point of pHpzc, the surface becomes negatively charged, enhancing electrostatic attraction to cationic CIP and reinforcing π-π interactions, thereby improving adsorption capacity52. Wang et al. (2018) provided insight into the adsorption mechanism, revealing that the primary mode of interaction is coulombic in nature. This involves the carboxylic or phenolic groups on TA interacting with a positively charged adsorbate. Additionally, the number of available binding sites increases with pH. Moreover, the enhancing effect of TA on the removal of adsorbate has been documented across various adsorbents, including magnetite nanoparticles and iron-TA nanocomposites58. Rasheed et al. (2021) have reported that the π-π interactions between the phenyl and furan rings of TA and aflatoxin (AF) may improve AF adsorption onto the adsorbent29. The cavities formed by aminosilane-modified bentonite could aid in the transport and capture of AFs. These traits distinguish the adsorbent’s enhanced AF adsorption from unmodified bentonite, which only has OH– groups for adsorbate capture59(Fig. 8).

Reusability and desorption

Efficient magnetic separation and reusability of heterogeneous adsorbents are essential parameters for achieving sustainable and economically viable water treatment processes. In this study, the reusability and desorption of the TA/Fe3O4 adsorbent were evaluated through five consecutive adsorption–desorption cycles for CIP removal. The adsorption efficiency decreased gradually from 87.43% in the first cycle to 80.48% in the fifth cycle, representing an overall reduction of approximately 7.95% (Fig. 9). This modest decline indicates that TA/Fe3O4 retains most of its adsorption capacity after repeated use, demonstrating high structural and functional stability. The decrease in adsorption efficiency can be primarily attributed to the partial degradation or detachment of tannic acid functional groups, particularly phenolic hydroxyl and aromatic moieties, during the desorption process. Moreover, the use of ethanol in the desorption process reduces the number of active sites available for hydrogen bonding and π–π stacking interactions with the fluoroquinolone rings of CIP. Furthermore, repeated regeneration may induce minor oxidation or rearrangement of the tannic acid coating, altering the surface chemistry of the adsorbent. In addition, slight aggregation of magnetite nanoparticles over successive cycles could reduce the effective surface area and restrict access to adsorption sites. However, the tannic acid coating acts as a stabilizing layer that minimizes extensive particle aggregation, thereby maintaining the material’s dispersibility and integrity60,61.

Overall, the limited loss in adsorption efficiency after multiple reuse cycles confirms that TA/Fe3O4 is a stable and reusable adsorbent suitable for removing fluoroquinolone antibiotics from aqueous environments.

Desorption efficiency quantifies the proportion of contaminants recovered from the adsorbent surface after each regeneration cycle. The desorption efficiencies observed, ranging from 96.56% to 99.32%, unequivocally demonstrate the process’s exceptional efficacy, achieving nearly complete recovery of the adsorbed contaminants throughout multiple cycles. These results highlight the sustained effectiveness of the desorption mechanism even after repeated usage. This outstanding performance is attributed to the high solubility of antibiotics in methanol, both being organic compounds. Based on the similarity–miscibility principle, antibiotics dissolve readily in organic solvents like methanol, facilitating effective contaminant removal from the adsorbent62. Consequently, this robust desorption efficiency enables facile regeneration of the adsorbent, promoting its repetitive use in water treatment applications.

In a related investigation, Sacko et al. demonstrated the high reusability potential of TA/Fe3O4 nanoparticles for recovering microplastics from aquatic systems. Their findings indicated that the removal efficiency for polystyrene (PS) and polyethylene terephthalate (PET) remained largely stable after two regeneration cycles, with efficiencies decreasing only marginally from 84% to 77% for PS and from 98.1% to 95% for PET. A more pronounced decline was observed between the third and fifth cycles, with efficiencies reducing to 69% for PS and 87.3% for PET. The authors attributed this reduction to factors associated with the regeneration process, particularly the degradation of functional groups during desorption. Furthermore, they suggested that the diminished magnetic properties of the adsorbent under acidic conditions during regeneration could weaken electrostatic interactions, thereby contributing to the observed decrease in performance61.

Conclusion

In this work, magnetic iron oxide nanoparticles functionalized with tannic acid (TA/Fe3O4 NPs) were synthesized and evaluated as a novel and efficient adsorbent for the removal of the CIP from aqueous media. Under optimized operational conditions, including pH: 10.47, adsorbent dosage: 1.43 g/L, contact time: 89.4 min, and initial CIP concentration: 2 mg/L, the system achieved a maximum removal efficiency of 87.83%, confirming the high affinity of TA/Fe3O4 toward CIP molecules. Among the isotherm models, the goodness of fit followed the sequence Langmuir > Freundlich > Temkin > D–R, with the Langmuir model exhibiting the highest R² and the lowest RMSE, signifying monolayer adsorption of CIP molecules onto a homogeneous distribution of active sites on the nanoparticle surface. Kinetic analysis revealed that the Elovich model provided the most accurate representation of the adsorption behavior, as reflected by its superior R2 and minimal RMSE values. This model, which accounts for heterogeneous activation energies across surface sites, indicates a physicochemical adsorption process characterized by rapid initial uptake followed by gradual attenuation as surface sites become saturated. Breakpoint partitioning analysis demonstrated greater conformity with the IPD model, suggesting that boundary-layer mass transfer and surface adsorption dominate the early phase. In contrast, intraparticle diffusion governs the later stages of the process. The adsorption mechanism involves multiple synergistic interactions, including hydrogen bonding, π–π stacking between the aromatic rings of tannic acid and the quinolone moieties of CIP, and coordination complexation between CIP’s carboxyl groups and Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺ centers on the nanoparticle surface. Owing to their pronounced magnetic responsiveness, the nanoparticles can be rapidly separated from the aqueous phase, and regeneration experiments confirmed excellent stability, retaining approximately 92% of the initial adsorption efficiency after five consecutive reuse cycles. Collectively, the findings demonstrate that surface modification of Fe3O4 with tannic acid markedly enhances both adsorption capacity and selectivity toward antibiotic molecules. The developed system represents a cost-effective, structurally stable, and recyclable adsorbent suitable for large-scale application in the treatment of pharmaceutical effluents.
