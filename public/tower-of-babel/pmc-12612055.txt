Genetic underpinnings and causal effects of brain structure and function on chronic pain intensity
Authors: Xiuzhi Wang, Jinyu Liu, Xichen Wang, Jin Yang, Yipeng Le, Yingchao Song, Qian Su, Xiaoxiao Xiao, Yifan Li, Wen Qin, Chunshui Yu, Ping Wang, Meng Liang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Chronic pain represents a major clinical burden, with its intensity being a key measure of its severity. However, the genetic and neural underpinnings of chronic pain intensity remain unraveled. Here, we identified six genetic loci (including a novel discovery) significantly associated with chronic pain intensity using a genome-wide association study in the UK Biobank (n = 134,627). We then systematically investigate its relationship with 3924 brain imaging-derived phenotypes. Phenotypic correlation analyses revealed 29 imaging-derived phenotypes predictive of future chronic pain intensity. Genetic correlation and polygenic risk score analyses showed 13 of these imaging-derived phenotypes share a genetic basis with chronic pain intensity. Mediation analyses indicated that 12 imaging-derived phenotypes mediate genetic effects on chronic pain intensity. Mendelian randomization further supported a causal influence of three imaging-derived phenotypes, including brain structure and functional network connectivity, on chronic pain intensity. Our findings elucidate the genetic architecture and neurobiological pathways of chronic pain intensity, highlighting the brain’s central role in chronic pain pathophysiology.

Introduction

Chronic pain poses a significant global health challenge1–4. It affects over 20% of the population and its prevalence is expected to grow with the increasingly aging society5. Despite its profound impact, effective clinical management strategies remain limited, underscoring the urgent need to elucidate the biological mechanisms underlying chronic pain to inform targeted treatments6–9.

Previous research has explored the biological underpinnings of chronic pain from genetic and neuroimaging perspectives10–18. A twin study has demonstrated that chronic pain is heritable, with heritability estimates ranging from 25 to 50%19, and a few genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified many genetic loci associated with chronic pain across various anatomical regions, including headache20, neck/shoulder pain21, low back pain22, knee pain23, multisite pain7,24,25, and even the number of chronic pain sites14. However, most of these GWAS studies have focused on the susceptibility of chronic pain at specific anatomical regions, with very limited investigations into chronic pain intensity (CPI)—another clinically very relevant measure of pain severity. To date, only one GWAS examined CPI and identified 125 genetic loci in a multi-ancestry American cohort26. Further exploration of the genetic architecture of CPI remains necessary to validate previous findings and uncover novel genetic loci. Notably, these existing GWAS studies found that the genes associated with chronic pain were highly enriched in brain tissues11,14,22,25, suggesting a potential role of the brain in the origination and perpetuation of chronic pain.

Indeed, a number of neuroimaging studies have linked alterations in brain structure and function to chronic pain. For example, reduced gray matter volume (GMV) in the insular cortex has been linked to higher pain intensity8, and some of these changes were reversible with effective treatments27–30, highlighting the critical role of the brain in the emergence and persistence of chronic pain. However, the inconsistencies of results across previous neuroimaging studies, small sample sizes, and limited brain imaging-derived phenotypes (IDPs) have prevented the establishment of a clear consensus on the brain mechanisms underlying CPI. Large-scale studies that encompass a broader range of brain phenotypes are needed to validate and expand upon these preliminary discoveries, and to investigate whether these brain alterations share a genetic basis with or even exert causal effects on CPI.

This study leverages the UK Biobank cohort to address these gaps by comprehensively examining the interplay between genetic factors, brain, and CPI. Specifically, we aimed to address the following four questions (Fig. 1): First, we conducted a GWAS of CPI in a white British population (n\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\,=\,$$\end{document}=134,627) to elucidate the genetic risk factors for CPI, with the aim of validating previously identified genetic loci and potentially identifying novel loci. Second, we conducted association analyses between 3924 brain IDPs at baseline and CPI at follow-up to pinpoint specific brain IDPs that were predictive of future pain intensity. Third, we used genetic covariance analyzer (GNOVA)31, polygenic risk scores (PRS) and mediation analyses to test whether these neuroimaging markers shared a genetic basis with CPI. Lastly, we performed Mendelian randomization (MR) to infer the causal effect of brain IDPs on CPI. Results

GWAS of chronic pain intensity

We conducted a GWAS involving 12,675,140 autosomal single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in a sample of 134,627 individuals of white British ancestry. The analysis was adjusted for sex, age, quadratic age term (age²), age-by-sex interaction terms (age × sex, age² × sex), genotype array, and the first 40 genetic principal components (PCs) to account for potential confounding factors. A total of 80 genome-wide significant SNPs were identified (Fig. 2; p < 5 × 10−8), which were consolidated into six distinct genomic loci (ranging in size from 31.15 kb to 1013.48 kb) and represented by six lead SNPs (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Data 2). Among the six lead SNPs, all but one were successfully replicated in three validation datasets with pain-related phenotypes (“Pain [limb, back, neck, head, abdominally]” in FinnGen32, “pain intensity” in MVP26, “multisite chronic pain” in UK Biobank14) (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Data 2), supporting the robustness of these associations. Notably, one locus (rs2867932) was not replicated in any of the three validation datasets and has not been reported in previous pain GWAS studies, and thus was considered as novel. In addition to the autosomal GWAS, we conducted genome-wide association analyses on the X chromosome, including both pseudoautosomal (PAR) and non-pseudoautosomal (non-PAR) regions, but no genome-wide significant associations were observed. The most significant variant was rs140630250 (non-PAR region), with a p value of 9.02\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\,\times \,$$\end{document}×10−6 (Fig. 2).

In the above GWAS (primary GWAS), all participants with CPI data collected at the follow-up were included. A second GWAS was conducted focusing solely on the participants with CPI data but without brain MRI data (n\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\,=\,$$\end{document}=106,697) as the base data to derive the PRS for the subsequent PRS-based cross-trait prediction analyses and the instrumental variables of the exposure in the subsequent MR analyses. We observed that, although the sample size decreased, 5 out of the 6 genetic loci remained significant (Supplementary Fig. 2 and Supplementary Data 2). Furthermore, the effect directions of the six lead SNPs in the second GWAS were consistent with those in the primary GWAS. These findings suggest a high stability of the identified risk loci associated with CPI.

Post-GWAS analyses: functional annotation, gene mapping, and gene-set enrichment

To further explore the GWAS results for CPI, we employed Functional Mapping and Annotation (FUMA) for downstream analyses, which included risk locus characterization, gene mapping, and gene-set enrichment analysis. A total of 1148 candidate SNPs were identified based on their linkage disequilibrium (LD, r2 > 0.6) with the independent significant SNPs within the identified risk loci. All candidate SNPs underwent functional annotation, revealing that the majority were located within intronic (82.2%) or intergenic regions (8.8%), while 24 SNPs (2.1%) were located within exonic regions (Fig. 3a). Notably, seven of these exonic SNPs were nonsynonymous variants, indicating that these nucleotide mutations result in amino acid sequence alterations. These seven nonsynonymous SNPs were mapped to the following genes: BSN (rs34762726), MST1 (rs3197999), MST1R (rs1062633, rs2230590), SLC45A4 (rs3739238), and FAM120AOS (rs10821128, rs1055710). A small proportion (3.0%) of the 1148 candidate SNPs were categorized into RegulomeDB classes 1–2 (Fig. 3b), suggesting potential regulatory roles. A total of 3.4% of these SNPs had a CADD score greater than 12.37 (Fig. 3c), indicating potential pathogenicity. Additionally, 82.8% of the candidate SNPs fell within chromatin states 1–7 (Fig. 3d), which are indicative of open chromatin regions. Using positional, expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL), and chromatin interaction data, we mapped the 1148 candidate SNPs to 183 genes (Supplementary Data 4). Multi-marker analysis of genomic annotation (MAGMA) identified 24 genes (Supplementary Data 4). Together, these analyses identified a total of 183 unique genes, with 23 genes consistently implicated by all four mapping methods (Fig. 3e).

We also performed gsMap35 analysis to explore significant enrichment of CPI heritability in different tissue regions (Fig. 3f and Supplementary Data 5). Gene-set enrichment analysis revealed that 183 unique genes associated with CPI were significantly enriched in several gene sets related to brain structure (Fig. 3g and Supplementary Data 6), including those involved in subcortical volume, brain morphology, and cortical surface area. Collectively, these findings not only support the robustness of our GWAS results but also establish strong links between CPI-related genes and brain structure. Most importantly, these results underscore the critical need to investigate the neural mechanisms underlying CPI and the potential pathways linking genes, brain, and CPI.

Phenotypic correlations between 3924 baseline brain IDPs and follow-up chronic pain intensity

To investigate the longitudinal association between 3924 brain structural and functional IDPs (Supplementary Data 7) with future CPI, we performed regression analyses for each IDP using generalized linear models (GLMs) on a cohort of 14,519 to 18,098 participants from the UK Biobank dataset (Supplementary Data 8 and Supplementary Fig. 3). The median interval between baseline and follow-up was 1.6 years, allowing for a predictive analysis of the relationship between baseline brain IDPs and follow-up CPI.

Mediation analysis

The above observed phenotypic correlation and shared genetic basis between brain IDPs and CPI raised the possibility that the genetic effects on CPI could be mediated by brain IDPs. To test this possibility, we conducted mediation analyses for the pathways of “genetic factors-brain IDPs-CPI” using structural equation modeling (SEM). Specifically, the 13 brain IDPs successfully predicted by PRSCPI served as mediators, PRSCPI (or the six lead SNPs significantly associated with CPI) as independent variables, and CPI as the dependent variable. Discussion

In this study, we first identified the genetic factors of CPI and then explored the relationships between 3924 brain IDPs and CPI from four perspectives: phenotypic correlation, shared genetic basis, mediation analyses and causal relationship. We obtained the following main results: First, six distinct genetic loci were identified to be significantly associated with CPI through a GWAS in 134,627 individuals of white British ancestry, one of which was a novel discovery. Second, we identified 29 baseline brain IDPs that were phenotypically associated with follow-up CPI, mainly including the GMV of the bilateral insular, subcallosal and central operculum cortices, as well as several intrinsic functional network connectivities. Third, GNOVA and PRS-based cross-trait prediction analyses indicated that 13 of the 29 identified phenotypic associations had a shared genetic basis with CPI. Fourth, mediation analyses revealed that 12 brain IDPs (corresponding mainly to the GMV of the bilateral insular and central operculum cortices) partially mediated the genetic effects on CPI. Finally, two-sample MR analyses showed that the surface area of the superior frontal cortex, the volume of left insula, and the functional connectivity (ICA100 edge 384) exhibited significant causal effects on CPI.

The genetic mechanisms underlying CPI appear to be relatively conserved across different populations and ancestries. The SNP-based heritability (9.8%) for CPI identified in this study was similar with a recent cross-ancestry Meta-GWAS study (8.4% for European population)26. Moreover, most of the loci identified in the present study were consistent with those reported in previous studies14,26,32 (Supplementary Data 2). For example, the variant rs2247036 on chromosome 3, identified as most significantly associated with CPI in the previous study26, was linked (r²\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\,=\,$$\end{document}=0.66) to the lead variant rs9815930 identified in our present study. Additionally, the association between CPI and the risk locus on chromosome 3 remained significant even with a much smaller sample size after excluding the participants without brain MRI data (Supplementary Data 2), further highlighting the robustness of this genetic factor. The nearest gene of rs2247036 is TRAIP, which has been reported to be related to chronic widespread musculoskeletal pain. Importantly, despite a much smaller sample size used in the present study, one novel variant associated with CPI was identified: rs2867932 (WDR61). The WDR61 gene has previously been reported to be highly expressed in brain tissues and associated with cortical volume changes in individuals with Alzheimer’s disease38,39, which may contribute to the higher incidence of dementia in patients with chronic pain16. Most genes identified through the four different mapping methods in the present study have also been reported to be linked to chronic pain mechanisms. For example, DCC, the most significant gene in the 23 genes identified by all 4 methods (Supplementary Data 4), has been reported to contribute to multisite chronic pain conditions via corticolimbic circuits25. Similarly, RNF123 and TRAIP have been associated with chronic widespread musculoskeletal pain12. In addition, we leveraged a recently developed method gsMap to spatially integrate the genetic architecture of CPI with transcriptomic features across tissue regions, and showed that, among all examined annotations, the brain showed the strongest associations with genetic risk for chronic pain (Fig. 3f and Supplementary Data 5), underscoring its central role in the etiology of persistent pain. This finding is consistent with the emerging view that chronic pain involves dysfunction in the central nervous system, including those related to sensory integration, affective modulation, and neuroplasticity40–42. In addition, enrichment signals were also observed in the spinal cord and sympathetic nerves, suggesting that both central sensitization and peripheral neuronal contributions may underlie pain persistence43,44. The results of gene-set enrichment analyses further revealed that many of the genes associated with CPI were also related to brain structure, particularly regional volume and surface area (Fig. 3g and Supplementary Data 6), implying an important role of the brain in the biological pathways of pain processing. In sum, our results revealed a genetic architecture of CPI in a white British population and highlighted the potential role of the brain in the biological mechanisms underlying CPI.

To formally test whether brain structure and function were associated with CPI and identify the specific brain IDPs exhibiting significant associations, we conducted association analyses between 3924 brain structural/functional IDPs and CPI using the large cohort of UK Biobank data. It is worth noting that, as the CPI data were acquired only in the follow-up online data acquisition after the neuroimaging data acquisition (the interval ranging between 3 months and 5.1 years, with a median of 1.64 years), we were allowed to test whether future CPI could be predicted by brain IDPs at baseline by performing the phenotypic association analyses between them. We identified 29 brain IDPs that were significantly correlated with future CPI after adjusting for relevant covariates and correcting the number of effective phenotypes (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Data 8): 22 were brain structural IDPs (mainly including the GMV of the bilateral insular, central operculum and subcallosal cortices) and the other 7 were brain functional IDPs (mainly including functional network connectivities). We found that a smaller GMV of insular, central operculum and subcallosal cortices at baseline was associated with higher CPI at the follow-up. The insular and central operculum cortices have been indicated to be key regions involved in pain perception45–47. Task-fMRI study showed that these regions were crucial for encoding acute pain intensity48,49. Cross-sectional studies have also linked GMV reduction in the insular cortex to higher CPI8, while longitudinal studies have further demonstrated that the shrunken structure of the insula was restored when the intensity of pain decreased after treatment27–29. Our results were consistent with these previous findings, highlighting the important role of the insular cortex in pain processing. In addition to brain structural abnormalities, functional alterations in brain IDPs have also been reported in chronic pain patients. In the present study, the ICA25 edge 180 and ICA100 edge 916 showed the most significant associations with CPI (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Data 8). Interestingly, both connectivities were associated with DMN – ICA25 edge 180 represents cross-network connectivity between the DMN and the visual network, and ICA100 edge 916 reflects within-network connectivity of the DMN. The locations and network definitions of each node and edge in the UK Biobank can be found in the study by Hongtu Zhu et al.50. Disruption of the DMN in chronic pain states has been observed in a number of neuroscience studies51–53. Importantly, the connectivity between the DMN and insula in fibromyalgia has been identified as a predictor of pain intensity at the time of the MRI scan54. These findings highlight the crucial role of the DMN in chronic pain susceptibility and severity. Taking together, the findings from our present study and previous studies suggest that atrophy in the bilateral insular cortex, central operculum cortex and alterations of some intrinsic brain functional connectivities may reflect key neural mechanisms underlying chronic pain intensity. It should be noted that these results were obtained from all individuals with and without chronic pain at the baseline stage. To assess whether the observed significant associations were driven by only one of the two subgroups (with vs. without chronic pain at baseline), we repeated the IDP-CPI association analyses for each subgroup separately. We found that the observed IDP-CPI associations were highly consistent across the two subgroups and independent of their pain status at baseline (Supplementary Data 9), suggesting the robustness of these results.

Having established the phenotypic association between brain IDPs and CPI, we further investigated the genetic basis underlying these phenotypic associations using GNOVA and PRS-based cross-trait prediction method. The GNOVA approach identified a total of 15 brain IDPs showing both phenotypic and genetic associations with CPI, of which 13 could be successfully predicted by the PRSCPI. These brain IDPs mainly include the GMV of the bilateral insula, central operculum, as well as the total surface area of both cerebral hemisphere (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Data 10). A previous study demonstrated a negative genetic correlation between insular volume and chronic pain conditions7, and our findings are consistent with this evidence. These findings suggest a shared genetic basis between the structure of these brain regions and CPI.

Considering the shared genetic basis between the brain and CPI, we conducted mediation analyses to further clarify whether genetic factors influence CPI through influencing brain structure. After controlling for a wide range of covariates and applying a rigorous multiple comparison correction, we identified 12 brain IDPs as significant mediators of the genetic effects (PRSCPI) on CPI, mainly related to the GMV, volume and surface area of the insular cortex (Fig. 6 and Supplementary Data 11). These findings suggest that the insular cortex may have an important role in mediating the genetic influence on CPI. Although the observed mediation effect sizes seemed small, such effect size is common in large-scale neuroimaging studies16,55,56. When using each of the six lead SNPs identified in the GWAS for CPI in the mediation analyses, none of the mediation effects survived Bonferroni correction. Given the polygenic nature of chronic pain, it suggests that a single SNP could not capture enough variance of the brain IDPs (i.e., the mediators) to allow for the detection of significant mediation effects.

The causal effects of brain IDPs on CPI were also tested using MR analyses, which identified three significant causal effects (Fig. 7 and Supplementary Data 12), involving both structural and functional IDPs. The structural IDPs included the surface area and volume of the superior prefrontal and insular cortices whose reduction were found to have a causal effect on the increase of CPI. The two regions have been suggested to be critical in the processing of chronic pain in previous studies57–59. Decreased cortical surface area and volume in these regions might reflect weakened resilience, potentially increasing the risk of heightened CPI by impairing the brain’s ability to modulate pain signals effectively. A recent MR study60 also reported a causal relationship between white matter microstructure of insula and migraine, further underscoring the pivotal role of insula in the development of chronic pain. In addition to these two structural IDPs, we also found a significant causal effect of a functional network connectivity on CPI. Specifically, a decrease of a resting-state functional connectivity between the sensorimotor network (precentral & postcentral regions) and the central executive network (supramarginal & inferior parietal regions) was found to be causally associated with an increase of CPI. The disrupted connectivity between the sensorimotor network and the central executive network may reflect abnormalities in somatosensory information processing, contributing to the persistence and exacerbation of chronic pain61,62. These findings suggest that the integrity of both brain structure and function might confer resilience against the CPI. Understanding these causal pathways could pave the way for more targeted interventions and therapeutic strategies to alleviate chronic pain.

This study has several limitations. First, we used the highest reported pain intensity over the past 24 h as the CPI phenotype due to the lack of data covering longer periods. Although this measure is the only measure that could be used to define CPI in the UK Biobank dataset and has been commonly used in large cohorts, it might not accurately represent CPI and thus reduce power to detect genetic and neuroimaging associations. Second, to maximize the sample size and statistical power, those participants without chronic pain were also included and coded as 0 in the present study, which may have introduced noise and the results may partly reflect differences between the presence and absence of chronic pain. Third, we did not stratify chronic pain by body sites and thus only the common genetic and/or neural associations with CPI across body sites could be identified, but those specific to a particular body site may be missed in the present study. Fourth, although brain imaging preceded CPI assessment, the GLM and mediation analyses remain correlational and cannot confirm causality. While MR was applied to support causal inference, it relies on a few assumptions (e.g., valid instruments, no pleiotropy) that may not be fully testable. Fifth, our analyses were restricted to individuals of white British ancestry, which may limit generalizability to other populations. Sixth, although our results are internally robust, independent replication in external cohorts is warranted in future studies. Finally, we were unable to use the GWAS summary statistics from the MVP dataset with a much larger sample size due to data access restrictions, and thus the statistical power might not be optimal in our present study.

In summary, we identified 1 novel genetic locus associated with CPI and 29 brain IDPs predictive of future CPI. We further provided evidence for a shared genetic basis underlying the phenotypic associations between 13 brain IDPs and CPI, and also showed that 12 brain IDPs, mainly related to the structure of the insula, partially mediated the genetic influences on CPI. The volume of the left insula, the surface area of the right superior frontal cortex and the functional connectivity between sensorimotor network and central executive network exhibited causal effects on CPI. These findings provide new insights into the complex relationship among genetic factors, brain structure and function, and CPI and offer new avenues for developing new treatment and optimizing pain management strategies for chronic pain patients in clinical practice.

Chronic pain intensity

The CPI used in the present study was obtained from the UK Biobank “Experience of Pain” questionnaire (https://biobank.ndph.ox.ac.uk/showcase/ukb/docs/pain_questionnaire.pdf). During the online follow-up data acquisition, participants were asked, “Are you troubled by pain or discomfort, either all the time or on and off, that has been present for more than 3 months?” (field ID: 120019). Those who responded “yes” were classified as having chronic pain and were further instructed to specify the body site affected by chronic pain and to report the pain intensity in that region over the preceding 24 h on a scale of 0 to 10 (0 indicating “no pain” and 10 representing “the worst pain imaginable”). To enhance statistical power, participants without chronic pain were also included in the analysis. Specifically, for individuals reporting chronic pain, the CPI phenotype was defined as the maximum pain intensity reported across all affected body sites, whereas those without chronic pain were assigned a CPI value of 0, the similar strategy adopted in a previous study for defining CPI64.

MRI data

In the present study, we utilized all 3924 brain IDPs publicly available from the UK Biobank to assess the potential phenotypic and genetic correlations between brain structure/function and CPI. These brain IDPs were derived from the MRI data of the UK Biobank collected from four scanning centers: Cheadle, Reading, Newcastle, and Bristol. All scans were performed using identically configured Siemens Skyra 3 T MRI scanners equipped with 32-channel radiofrequency receive head coils. Detailed scanning protocols and procedures can be found in the UK Biobank documentation (https://biobank.ndph.ox.ac.uk/showcase/showcase/docs/brain_mri.pdf).

GWAS

Linear regression model of PLINK(v.2.0)66 was used to perform GWAS of CPI. The model included age, age2, sex, age×sex, age2 × sex, genotype array, and the first 40 PCs as covariates for autosomal and PAR region. For the non-PAR region of the X chromosome, the model included age, age², genotype array, and the 40 genetic principal components. Autosomal and PAR region were analyzed assuming diploidy in both sexes, with standard 0/1/2 genotype coding. Non-PAR region were analyzed under PLINK2 X-inactivation model: males were coded as 0/2 and females as 0/1/2. Prior to conducting the linear regression analysis, quantitative covariates were standardized to have a mean of 0 and a variance of 1.

Heritability

Following the acquisition of GWAS summary statistics, LDSC regression67 was employed to estimate the SNP-based heritability of CPI. Additionally, the LD interception and attenuation ratio were calculated to reflect potential confounding factors in the results. Pre-computed European LD scores from the 1000 Genomes Project Phase 368 were utilized to align with the population’s LD structure.

Post-GWAS analyses

FUMA(v1.5.2)69 was employed to conduct post-GWAS analyses, which included risk locus characterization, gene mapping, and gene-set enrichment. Default parameters were applied throughout the process unless otherwise specified.

Genetically informed spatial mapping

Genetically informed spatial mapping (gsMap)35 was employed to identify spatially resolved cell populations associated with CPI by integrating the CPI GWAS summary statistics and high-resolution spatial transcriptomics data. gsMap first uses a graph neural network to model both spatial coordinates and transcriptome-wide gene expression profiles of each spot to compute gene specificity scores (GSS). These GSSs are mapped to SNPs based on proximity to gene transcription start and end sites, as well as epigenomic SNP-to-gene annotations. Using stratified linkage disequilibrium score regression (S-LDSC), the method estimates trait heritability enrichment for SNPs linked to each spatial spot. Finally, a Cauchy combination test is applied to aggregate statistical significance across spots within a region. This framework enables high-resolution mapping of trait–cell associations across tissues.

Gene set analyses

All mapped genes resulted from the GWAS were input for gene set enrichment analyses, utilizing GWAS catalog to report gene sets based on the hypergeometric test, as implemented in GENE2FUNC of FUMA.

Association analyses between 3924 brain IDPs and chronic pain intensity

Brain MRI data quality control and preprocessing

After removing participants who had withdrawn from the study, manual quality control was performed on the brain structural images, leading to the exclusion of participants with notable structural abnormalities, artifacts, or incomplete scans. In addition, participants diagnosed with major neurological or neurodegenerative disorders prior to imaging were excluded based on ICD-10 codes, to avoid potential confounding effects on brain structure and function. These disorders included stroke, Parkinson’s disease, Alzheimer’s disease, multiple sclerosis, dementia, schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, other demyelinating diseases (excluding multiple sclerosis), epilepsy, cerebral palsy, neurological trauma or injury, essential tremor, and cognitive impairment. To further mitigate potential interference from population stratification, we also excluded participants who were not of white British ancestry. Next, we filtered out participants who completed the online “Experience of Pain” questionnaire before or within 3 months following the collection of imaging data. This step was crucial to ensure that the imaging data were collected prior to the assessment of CPI. Additionally, participants with missing covariate data were excluded from the analyses. Participants with IDP values greater than six times the median absolute deviation from the median were also excluded to minimize the impact of outliers on the models. We then applied ComBat harmonization70 and normal score transformation71 to each brain IDP to eliminate data heterogeneity between scanners and ensure a Gaussian distribution of the IDP values. To maximize the sample size for each model, the above quality control procedure was performed separately for each brain IDP. (Details of participant exclusion across each quality control step for the 3924 IDPs are provided in Supplementary Fig. 3).

Genetic correlation analyses

GNOVA31 was used to explore the genome-wide genetic correlations between IDPs and CPI. GNOVA estimates the genetic covariance and correlation between two traits using GWAS summary statistics, and has the potential to provide more accurate estimates of genetic correlation than LDSC in certain scenarios75,76. Specially, we applied GNOVA to each of the 29 predictive brain IDPs identified by the “Phenotypic Association Analyses”, using GWAS summary statistics from the secondary CPI GWAS (see PRS-based cross-trait prediction analyses section) and the corresponding brain IDP GWAS. As these two GWAS were conducted in non-overlapping samples, the resultant genetic correlations were expected to be more accurate and less prone to sample overlap bias. The LD scores for the European population were calculated based on the 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3 reference panel, and overlapping SNPs were aligned and quality-controlled following GNOVA’s recommended pipeline. To account for multiple comparisons in the GNOVA analyses, we applied Bonferroni correction across the results of 29 IDPs (p\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\, < \,$$\end{document}<3.19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\,\times \,$$\end{document}×10−3 (0.05/15.68 (Meff for 29 predictive IDPs)).

PRS-based cross-trait prediction analyses

To further verify the robustness of the shared genetic architecture between CPI and the 15 brain IDPs identified by GNOVA, we conducted PRS-based cross-trait prediction analyses, i.e., using the PRS for CPI (PRSCPI) to predict each of the 15 brain IDPs. The PRS-CS77 was utilized for calculating PRSCPI. We first divided the participants with CPI data (n\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\,=\,$$\end{document}=165,876) into two groups based on the availability of brain MRI data: the participants without MRI data were used as the base dataset (n\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\,=\,$$\end{document}=131,946) and the participants with MRI data were used as the target dataset (n\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\,=\,$$\end{document}=33,930). To avoid potential bias introduced by genetic relatedness between the base and target datasets, we first identified participants with high relatedness using the combined sample (i.e., the union of the base and target groups), applying a kinship coefficient cutoff of 0.088465. The identified individuals were excluded from the whole dataset to ensure that any individuals included in the present study were mutually unrelated. Then, the same quality control procedures for samples and variants as described in the GWAS section were applied to both datasets separately. Ultimately, 106,697 participants remained in the base dataset and 27,931 participants in the target dataset for the PRSCPI calculation.

In the base dataset, a secondary GWAS for CPI (n\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\,=$$\end{document}= 106,697) was conducted to generate GWAS summary statistics, which were then used to calculate PRSCPI for each participant in the target dataset using the PRS-CS, which is a Bayesian regression framework that applies continuous shrinkage priors to estimate SNP effect sizes from GWAS summary statistics while accounting for LD patterns. We used the PRS-CS auto model, which automatically learns the global shrinkage parameter from the data without the need for validation data. LD information was derived from the European ancestry LD reference panel provided by PRS-CS, based on the 1000 Genomes Project. The resulting posterior effect size estimates were used to calculate PRS for each individual in the target dataset via PLINK 2.0. The quality control and processing procedures described in the “Brain MRI data quality control and preprocessing” section were applied for each brain IDP, resulting in a total of 24,503 to 25,566 participants included in these prediction analyses. All covariates listed in the “Phenotypic association analyses” section were controlled for each GLM model. p values were corrected using Meff of the 15 predictive IDPs (7.10): p\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\, < \,$$\end{document}<0.05\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$/$$\end{document}/7.10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\,=\,$$\end{document}=7.04\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\,\times \,$$\end{document}×10⁻3.

Mediation analyses

The Lavaan78 package in R was utilized to conduct SEM for mediation analyses. Specifically, the PRSCPI or each of the six lead SNPs identified in the GWAS for CPI was used as the independent variable, each of the 13 brain IDPs exhibiting both phenotypic associations and shared genetic basis with CPI was used as the mediator, and the CPI was used as the outcome variable.

The same quality control and preprocessing steps for brain IDPs as detailed in the “Brain MRI data quality control and preprocessing” section were applied to exclude unqualified participants for each mediation analysis. As many covariates were not collected in the online follow-up data acquisition, the dependent variable (i.e., CPI) was only adjusted for age, age2, sex, age × sex, age2 × sex, and the first 10 PCs. In contrast, the mediator (i.e., IDPs) was adjusted for the full set of covariates as in the “Phenotypic Association Analyses”. Ultimately, 16,995 ~ 16,996 participants were included in the mediation analyses. The maximum likelihood algorithm was employed for model estimation. p values of indirect effects (a × b) were corrected by Meff (5.17) of the 13 PRSCPI predictive IDPs. Specifically, when using the PRSCPI as the independent variable, the p threshold was set to 9.67 × 10−3 (0.05/5.17), and when using a single SNP as the independent variable, the p threshold was set to 1.61 × 10−3 (p<0.05\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$/$$\end{document}/5.17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$/$$\end{document}/6 lead SNPs).

MR

The TwoSampleMR package (version 0.6.8)79 in R was employed to test the causal effects of brain IDP on CPI for each of the 29 significant IDP-CPI associations. As the brain IDPs were acquired before the acquisition of CPI data, we only considered the direction of causality from brain IDPs to CPI, that is, each brain IDP was considered as the exposure and the CPI was considered as the outcome. The GWAS summary statistics for the 29 brain IDPs were previously published and obtained from the UK Biobank cohort (n\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\,=\,$$\end{document}=33,224)37. The GWAS summary data for CPI was derived from a secondary GWAS conducted in the present study (n\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\,=\,$$\end{document}=106,697), in which the participants with brain MRI data were excluded to eliminate any sample overlap between exposure and outcome. Instrumental variants (IVs) were defined as the SNPs associated with the exposure (i.e., brain IDPs) at p\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\, < \,$$\end{document}<1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\,\times \,$$\end{document}×10−5 and not associated with outcome (i.e., p\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\, > \,$$\end{document}>5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\,\times \,$$\end{document}×10−8), ensuring sufficient strength across all exposures—a threshold commonly used in previous MR studies26,60,80,81. Independent IVs were identified through LD clumping with a window size of 10,000 kb and an LD threshold of r²\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\, < \,$$\end{document}<0.001, ensuring that the selected IVs were not in high LD with one another. Subsequently, the effect alleles of the IVs were harmonized between the exposure and outcome datasets, with palindromic IVs excluded to avoid strand ambiguity. The F-statistics for each IVs were calculated using their Beta values and standard errors (SE) from the GWAS of CPI, following the formula: F\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\,=\,$$\end{document}=Beta\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$/$$\end{document}/SE. IVs with F < 10 were removed to minimize the risk of weak instrument bias82. Additionally, Steiger’s test83 was performed to exclude IVs that exhibited a stronger correlation with the outcome than the exposure. Finally, outliers were identified and excluded using the RadialMR method84.

Five MR approaches, including inverse variance weighting (IVW), maximum likelihood, MR-Egger, weighted median, and weighted mode, were used to investigate the causal effect of brain IDPs on CPI. The IVW method was chosen as the primary approach for MR analyses, as it provides the most efficient causal estimates under the assumption that all IVs are valid, offering the highest statistical power85. A causal effect was considered significant if the IVW estimate had a p value\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\, < \,$$\end{document}<0.05/15.68 (Meff for 29 predictive brain IDPs) and if its direction was consistent with the results obtained from the other four MR methods. To ensure the reliability of the findings, Cochran’s Q test was used to investigate heterogeneity in the MR estimates across IVs, and MR-Egger intercepts were assessed to evaluate bias due to weak IVs and the potential for horizontal pleiotropy.
