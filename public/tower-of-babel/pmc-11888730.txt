Comparative Evaluation of Shear Bond Strength of Orthodontic Metal Bracket Bonded with the Conventional Adhesive System and Moisture Insensitive Primer System on a Tooth Surface Which has Undergone Bleaching with 35% Hydrogen Peroxide – An In Vitro Study
Authors: Praveen Roy, KK Manjusha, A Nishad, Ashitha Aravindan, JK Krishnajith, KA Jameema
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and Objectives:Today, orthodontic bracket bonding on the tooth surface is a vital phase in any orthodontic therapy as strong tooth-bracket bonding is essential for success and efficiency. Tooth whitening is popular due to tooth discolouration and patient need for esthetic changes. This in vitro study compared the shear bond strength (SBS) and adhesive remnant index (ARI) of brackets bonded with a conventional and moisture-insensitive primer system on tooth surfaces bleached with 35% hydrogen peroxide in dry and moisture conditions.Methods:Fifty-six healthy humans with extracted maxillary first premolars were cleaned using a periodontal curette, rinsed, treated with 35% hydrogen peroxide, and kept in a 0.1% thymol aqueous solution at room temperature. These teeth were embedded in orthodontic acrylic resin in four groups: the conventional primer system in dry conditions, the conventional primer system in moist conditions using artificial saliva, the moisture insensitive primer system in dry conditions, and the moisture insensitive primer system in moist conditions. After bonding metal brackets to tooth surfaces bleached with 35% hydrogen peroxide, universal testing machine and a stereomicroscope were used to calculate SBS and ARI.Results and Discussion:SBS and ARI values were the lowest in the conventional primer system in moist conditions, with statistically significant differences between groups. The study’s findings match earlier research. In vivo research should evaluate SBS and ARI to support in vitro findings.Conclusion:In the current study, moisture was found to be a factor affecting SBS and ARI. The moisture-insensitive bonding system was found to be resistant to effects of moisture compared with the conventional system.

INTRODUCTION

The evolution of orthodontic adhesive systems has been marked by a continuous quest for materials and techniques that provide optimal bond strength while addressing practical challenges encountered during clinical procedures. Traditional adhesives, commonly known as conventional adhesive systems, have been widely employed in orthodontic practice for decades. These systems typically consist of a multistep application process involving etching, priming, and bonding. However, conventional adhesive systems have shown limitations, particularly in maintaining bond strength in the presence of moisture. The oral environment, with its inherent challenges of saliva and humidity, poses difficulties in achieving and sustaining a dry field during bonding procedures.[12]

In response to these challenges, moisture-insensitive primer systems were introduced to orthodontics. The moisture-insensitive primer typically contains hydrophilic and hydrophobic components. These components work together to ensure effective bonding even under the influence of moisture such as saliva or blood, which could be encountered during the bonding procedure. This can lead to increased efficiency during orthodontic appointments.[34]

The impact of bleaching on the SBS of orthodontic brackets (OBs) is a topic of ongoing research. Some investigations have found that bleaching can reduce the SBS of OB by up to 50%. These studies have attributed this reduction to the removal of enamel minerals by the bleaching agent, which can weaken the adhesion between the OB and the tooth. Additional studies have indicated that bleaching has no substantial influence on the SBS of brackets. These investigations have demonstrated that the enamel surface may be able to remineralize after bleaching, restoring the bond between the OB and the tooth.[567]

By undertaking a comparative assessment of SBS between conventional adhesive primer systems (CAPSs) and moisture-insensitive primer systems (MIPS) on teeth that have undergone bleaching, this study seeks to contribute valuable insights to the existing body of orthodontic literature. Understanding the performance of these adhesive systems under the specific conditions of bleached teeth is crucial for clinicians who must navigate the delicate balance between esthetic demands and the mechanical stability of orthodontic appliances. The current study was done to contrast SBS and ARI of OB bonded with a CAPS and MIPS on tooth surfaces bleached with 35% hydrogen peroxide in dry and moist conditions.

METHODS

SBS and ARI of OB bonded with a CAPS and MIPS on tooth surfaces treated with 35% hydrogen peroxide were compared in vitro. The trial lasted 6 months. Fifty-six healthy maxillary first premolars were extracted for orthodontics used for the study.

The teeth were cleaned with a periodontal curette, washed with water, and bleached with 35% hydrogen peroxide. The teeth were kept in a 0.1% thymol aqueous solution at an ambient temperature. Teeth are divided into four groups of equal size (n = 14) and mounted in orthodontic acrylic resin. Color coding was done to differentiate the samples in later stages of analysis.

GROUP 1: CAPS (3M Unitek Transbond XT: Primer and adhesive) in dry conditions; color-coded as blue.

GROUP 2: CAPS (3M Unitek Transbond XT: Primer and adhesive) in moist conditions using artificial saliva; color-coded as orange.

GROUP 3: MIPS (Transbond moisture-insensitive Primer and Transbond XT adhesive) in dry conditions; color-coded as black.

GROUP 4: MIPS (Transbond moisture-insensitive Primer and Transbond XT adhesive) in moist conditions using artificial saliva; color-coded as white.

All the samples were etched for 30 seconds using 37% phosphoric acid on the outer enamel surfaces of the teeth. Afterward, the teeth were rinsed by applying water and dried for 10 seconds. The designated adhesive was applied under dry and contaminated conditions (artificial saliva) according to the group’s specifications. The tooth surface was subjected to LED light (Woodpecker) for 40 seconds to initiate the light-curing process. Subsequently, a 0.022 slot metal bracket with a mesh base was securely attached to the tooth surface using a Transbond XT light cure composite and then cured.

Following 48 hours, starting with the completion of thermocycling, the samples underwent SBS testing in the occluso-cervical plane, with the chisel placed at the tooth–bracket contact.

The quantity of adhesive left on the tooth surface was evaluated by ARI proposed by Artun and Bergland.[8] Each tooth was examined under a stereomicroscope, after debonding the brackets, and was scored as:

0 – No composite material remains on the enamel’s surface.1 – The enamel’s surface contains no more than 50% of the composite material.2 – More than 50% composite material remains on the enamel surface.3 – The entire composite substance remains on the enamel surface.

0 – No composite material remains on the enamel’s surface.

0 – No composite material remains on the enamel’s surface.

1 – The enamel’s surface contains no more than 50% of the composite material.

1 – The enamel’s surface contains no more than 50% of the composite material.

2 – More than 50% composite material remains on the enamel surface.

2 – More than 50% composite material remains on the enamel surface.

3 – The entire composite substance remains on the enamel surface.

3 – The entire composite substance remains on the enamel surface.

ANOVA was employed to contrast the SBS and ARI scores between the groups, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test to compare pairs. Fisher’s exact test compared ARI score frequencies between groups at a 5% significance level.

RESULTS

Groups 1 through 4 had the maximum shear bond strength (Mpa) of 8.11, 4.22, 7.85, and 7.21, respectively. The minimum SBS (Mpa) of groups 1 through 4 was found to be 4.93, 0.44, 6.14, and 5.19, respectively. The mean SBS (Mpa) of groups 1 through 4 was 6.54 ± 1.21, 1.85 ± 1.54, 6.89 ± 0.58, and 6.44 ± 0.91, respectively. The group differences were statistically significant (P = 0.001). The pairwise comparisons between group 2 and all other groups was statistically significant (P = 0.001) in the post hoc test.Among 14 samples of group 1, 5 (36%) were observed to have score 1, while 9 (64%) were observed to have score 2. Among 14 samples of group 2, 5 (36%) were observed to have scored 0, while 9 (64%) were observed to have scored 1. Among 14 samples of group 3, 4 (29%) were observed to have scored 1, while 10 (71%) were observed to have scored 2. Among 14 samples of group 4, 6 (43%) were observed to have score 1, while 8 (57%) were observed to have score 2. The difference between the proportion of scores of the groups regarding ARI score was found to be statistically significant (P = 0.001).

DISCUSSION

Since at least as early as Ancient Greece, medical practitioners have been exploring ways to improve tooth positions. By the 16th century, medical students in France were specializing in oral healthcare and treatment. Pierre Fauchard published “The Surgical Dentist” in 1669 and crafted a U-shaped iron appliance called “the Bandeau” to expand the palate and create room for more teeth.[910] Fixed appliances are an important aspect of the contemporary orthodontic treatment approach. Bonding uses sticky resins to connect attachments directly to the tooth enamel. Correct bracket placement is crucial to orthodontic treatment success and prognosis. The binding strength between the enamel and orthodontic brackets is crucial for bracket-bonding systems.[11]

Adhesive and attachments should be strong enough to endure mastication, arch wire loads, and tooth movement in all three planes. Additionally, the SBS should enable OB debonding without affecting the enamel surface. Orthodontic therapy involves tensile, shear, and torsion pressures on bonded brackets. Shear bond strength resists occlusion forces on bonded connectors. Shear and tensile loading modes can test orthodontic bonding. SBS was assessed in the present study because it best resembles clinical conditions.[1213]

The mean SBS was higher for MIPS in dry conditions than for CAPS and MIPS in moisture conditions. The CAPS in moisture conditions had the lowest SBS between groups. Group differences were statistically significant. This supports earlier research by Evangelina et al.,[14] Marusamy et al.,[15] Shukla et al.,[16] and Goswami et al.[17]

Rangaswamy Rajagopal et al.[18] found that the conventional primer fails to adhere under moisture contamination. Previous research found that salivary contamination decreased the binding strength of hydrophilic Assure adhesive resin, supporting the present investigation.[1920]

Preserving a healthy and undamaged enamel surface after removing brackets after orthodontic treatment is a top priority for the practitioner. OB failure happens at the adhesive enamel or OB junction during the debonding process. Because the enamel surface is relatively intact, OB failure at this junction is favorable. However, apart from the potential harm to the enamel surface during cleaning, a significant amount of time is necessary to eliminate the remaining adhesive. Enamel fractures may result from bond failure at the enamel adhesive junction, which happens when the bond between the adhesive and bracket is overly strong.[212223]

The current study used Artun and Bergland’s (1984) method to evaluate the ARI score. This method accounts for adhesive left on the tooth surfaces. Numerous investigations on bond strength assessed the ARI ratings put out by Bishara, wherein the scoring was carried out according to a certain gradation pattern representing the amount of adhesive remaining on the OB base. Digital photos, scanning electron micrographs, and high-precision elemental maps of the adhesive remnant as evaluated using energy dispersed X-ray spectrometry can all be used to assess the quantitative measurement of the residual adhesive. Cehreli S. B claims that qualitative visual scoring utilizing the ARI can produce outcomes that are comparable to those determined by quantitative image analysis methods.[24]

Similarly, from the results obtained regarding the shear bond strength, ARI scores were found to be least favorable in the group where the conventional primer in moisture conditions was tested. The difference between the ARI scores of this group with other groups was found to be statistically significant. Similar results were reported in a previous study conducted by Bianca Mota Santos et al.[25]

Comparable levels of ARI scores were reported for the other groups (moisture-insensitive primer in dry conditions, conventional primer in dry conditions, and moisture-insensitive primer in moisture conditions). The moisture-insensitive primer in dry conditions had the most favorable ARI score, followed by the conventional primer in dry conditions and the moisture-insensitive primer in moist conditions. Similar findings were noted in the study carried out by Rima Hadrous et al.[26]

For proper bracket retention, conventional bonding adhesives often require a contamination-free enamel surface. Nevertheless, the shear bond strength (SBS) of orthodontic brackets may be lowered by saliva contamination of the enamel, which is typically present when bonding posterior or partially erupted teeth.[27]

In the current study, moisture was found to be a factor affecting shear bond strength and adhesive remnant index. The moisture-insensitive bonding system was found to be resistant to effects of moisture compared with the conventional system. Many studies conducted in the past reported similar findings.

The nature of the study design is in vitro. There is no risk or discomfort to living subjects in the in vitro models. The inherent complexity of organ systems and the inside environment of the human body, however, cannot be captured. Therefore, applying the study’s findings directly to clinical settings should be done with utmost caution. To support the findings from the in vivo experiments, it is advised to evaluate the ARI and SBS.
