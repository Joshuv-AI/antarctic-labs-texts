Isolation and Antibiogram of Staphylococcus aureus Isolated From Bovine Mastitis in Bardaghat Municipality, Nepal
Authors: Biswash Ghimire, Shambhu Shah, Akash Adhikari, Sujan Adhikari, Asmita Regmi, Sirjan Bastola
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundMastitis in dairy animals is a major economic and public health concern in Nepal, exacerbated by the emergence of antimicrobial‐resistant pathogens. This study aimed to determine the prevalence and antibiotic resistance patterns of Staphylococcus aureus isolated from bovine milk in Bardaghat Municipality, Nepal.ResultsA cross‐sectional survey screened 196 milk samples from lactating cows and buffaloes using the California Mastitis Test (CMT). The overall prevalence of S. aureus was 39.79%. Strikingly, 100.00% of isolates exhibited resistance to oxacillin, indicating the widespread presence of methicillin‐resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA). Moreover, 25.64% of isolates were multidrug‐resistant (resistant to n 3 antibiotic classes), and 18% had a multiple antibiotic resistance (MAR) index > 0.2. No statistically significant associations were found between the prevalence of S. aureus and evaluated risk factors.ConclusionsThese findings underscore an urgent need for improved mastitis management, including routine bacterial screening, judicious antibiotic use and public health measures to mitigate transmission of resistant S. aureus to humans.Keywords: antimicrobial resistance, bovine mastitis, MRSA, Nepal, Staphylococcus aureus

Background

Mastitis, an affliction of the mammary gland, is typically accompanied by pathological changes in glandular tissue and physical, chemical and bacterial abnormalities in milk (Haxhiaj et al. 2022). It is the most economically significant disease affecting dairy cows worldwide, accounting for the majority of direct expenditures associated with common production disorders (Moschovas et al. 2023). Mastitis reduces milk yield and quality, increases veterinary costs and predisposes cows to infertility and reproductive issues. In Nepal, milk losses due to mastitis are substantial, with an estimated NPR 8320 million (around 64 million USD) for subclinical mastitis and NPR 4430 (around 34 million USD) for clinical mastitis (Kharel et al. 2023). Mastitis not only causes significant financial losses but also poses a serious threat to cattle welfare (Girma and Tamir 2022).

Clinical mastitis is characterised by visible signs such as a swollen, hot udder and clotted milk, often accompanied by systemic symptoms, making it easier to diagnose. Subclinical mastitis (SCM), however, lacks obvious external signs and is diagnosed through elevated somatic cell counts or tests like the California mastitis test (CMT) (Stanek et al. 2024). Field studies indicate a higher prevalence of SCM in dairy herds, which is harder to diagnose and control due to the need for regular screening and bacteriological culture. Subclinical cases can impose more than double the financial burden of clinical cases (Khasapane et al. 2023).

Bacterial agents are the primary cause of mastitis, with approximately 70.00% of cases attributed to bacterial toxins. Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus), Streptococci, E. coli, Corynebacterium spp., and Klebsiella spp. are the main pathogens (Wieland 2024). In Nepal, Staphylococcus species are the most commonly isolated mastitis pathogens (Shrestha et al. 2021; Tiwari et al. 2022). Other causes, including fungal, viral, algal or mycoplasma infections, as well as non‐infectious factors like trauma or environmental irritants, account for the remaining cases.

In Nepal, antibiotics are often used indiscriminately to treat mastitis, without proper prescription or adherence to withdrawal periods, fostering resistant organisms (Rijal et al. 2021). The prevalence of drug‐resistant pathogens, such as methicillin‐resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) and multidrug‐resistant E. coli, is well‐documented (Dahal and Chaudhary 2018), showing high resistance to commonly used antibiotics. These resistant bacteria can transfer to humans via direct contact or food, posing significant public health risks (Hashempour‐Baltork et al. 2019).

Agriculture accounts for 27.10 % of Nepal's GDP, with the livestock sector contributing 11.50% to total GDP and 25.70% to agricultural GDP (Dhakal 2022; Poudel et al. 2020). Nepal produced 2.3 million metric tons of milk in 2020; the total milk production for Nepal in the fiscal year 2079/80 (2022/23) was 2,729,198 metric tons. In Nawalparasi (East) district, the 2022 cattle and buffalo population were 75,548 and 66,739, respectively, with 11,540 cattle and 20,410 buffaloes lactating (Ministry of Agriculture and Livestock Development 2022). Despite the high prevalence of SCM in Nepal, there is limited research on this issue in Bardaghat, where farmers lack access to reliable animal health services, leading to inadequate diagnosis and treatment.

Nepal lacks comprehensive epidemiological data on mastitis, with most information derived from isolated surveys or retrospective lab records. The absence of systematic surveillance hinders targeted control and policy planning. Rural farmers have limited access to professional veterinary care and low awareness of mastitis detection and prevention, particularly subclinical cases. Many are unfamiliar with proper milking hygiene or dry‐cow therapy and rely on para‐veterinarians or informal drug sellers for treatment, leading to inappropriate drug use and failure to observe milk withdrawal periods (Rame Hau et al. 2025). This exacerbates public health concerns, including antibiotic resistance. Effective mastitis control requires identifying pathogens like S. aureus and conducting antibiotic resistance testing to address both animal health and public health challenges in Nepal.

Materials and Methods

Study Area

The study was conducted in the [Blinded for Peer Review], which is located in the [Blinded for Peer Review]. The laboratory work was conducted at the Veterinary Microbiology Laboratory, [Blinded for Peer Review].

Sample Size

The sample size in particular was generated from EpiTools—Epidemiological Calculators (Ausvet), assuming the prevalence of SCM in Bardaghat Municipality to be 15.00% and an expected precision of 5.00% and confidence level of 95.00%. The sample size for true prevalence was estimated to be 196. Samples from separate quarters were collected and CMT was done at the farm and samples were collected in the labelled universal sample container for cultural examination in the lab.

Sampling Method and Transportation

Purposive sampling was employed to select cows and buffaloes, and milk samples were collected aseptically in accordance with the National Mastitis Council guidelines (Middleton et al. 2014). Teat ends were scrubbed with cotton swabs soaked in 70% ethanol after pre‐dipping and drying. The first three to four streams of milk were fore‐stripped and discarded to reduce canal contaminants. Approximately 10 mL of milk was collected into sterile, labelled vials held at a 45‐degree angle to prevent debris entry. Samples were immediately placed in an icebox and transported to the laboratory for processing within 4 h of collection.

California Mastitis Test (CMT)

The CMT was used as a screening test for subclinical mastitis. It was carried out according to a procedure described by Quinn et al. (1994). A squirt of milk, about 2 mL from each quarter was placed in each of four shallow cups in the CMT paddle. An equal amount of the commercial CMT reagent was added to each cup. A gentle circular motion was applied to the mixtures in a horizontal plane for 15 s. The CMT results were scored as 0 (negative), trace, 1(weak positive), 2 (distinct positive) and 3 (strong positive) based on gel formation. All CMT scores of 0 and trace were considered negative while CMT scores of 1, 2 and 3 were considered indicators of SCM.

Isolation and Identification of S. aureus

A 10 µL calibrated loop was used to inoculate milk directly onto trypticase soy agar supplemented with 5% sheep blood (blood agar) and mannitol salt agar (MSA). Plates were incubated aerobically at 37°C and examined at 24 and 48 h.

S. aureus was identified based on colony morphology (creamy, greyish‐white to golden colonies, mannitol fermentation on MSA), the presence of haemolysis (beta‐haemolysis or double‐zone haemolysis) and Gram staining (Gram‐positive cocci in clusters). Biochemical confirmation included a positive catalase test and a positive tube coagulase test. For the tube coagulase test, suspect colonies were emulsified in rabbit plasma and incubated at 37°C. Clot formation was assessed at 4 h and, if negative, re‐assessed after 24 h of incubation.

Antibiotic Sensitivity Test (AST)

AST was carried out using Kirby–Bauer disc diffusion method (Hudzicki 2009). For AST, the Mueller–Hinton Agar (MHA) plate was streaked with bacterial suspension with a turbidity of 0.5 McFarland standard, and the antibiotic discs were applied to the plate. After a 24‐h incubation at 37°C, zone of inhibition for each test antibiotic was measured and susceptibility of each isolate of S. aureus to different antibiotics was assessed in accordance with the CLSI 2024 guidelines (CLSI 2024) (Table 1). The isolates showing resistance to three or more antimicrobial classes were classified as MDR (Magiorakos et al. 2012). Resistance to multiple antibiotics within the same class was counted as resistance to a single antimicrobial category.

Calculation of Multiple Antibiotic Resistance (MAR) Index

The MAR index is calculated as the ratio of the number of antibiotics to which the isolate is resistant (a) to the total number of antibiotics to which the isolate was tested (b) and is given by:

MAR Index = a / b where a is the number of antibiotics to which the organism is resistant and b is the total number of antibiotics to which the organism was tested.

This represents the degree of resistance of each isolate and its value ranges from 0 to 1.

Data Analysis

Data were entered into Microsoft Excel 2016 and analysed using IBM SPSS version 25. The descriptive statistics were summarised using frequency and proportions. The univariate logistic regression analysis (Chi‐squared test) was used to test for association between independent variables (factors) and outcome variable (S. aureus in milk) at a 95.00% confidence interval. The p‐value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of Sampled Animals

A total of 196 animals were sampled from all 16 wards to determine the prevalence of S. aureus in Bardaghat Municipality, Parasi District. Out of these animals, lactating cows were higher (51.02%, n = 100/196) than that of lactating buffaloes (48.98%, n = 96/196). Most of the animals (69.38%, n = 136/196) sampled were between 1st and 3rd parity and the remaining were above the 4th parity (30.62%, n = 60/196). In the sampled animals, most of them were in early lactation (56.12%, n = 110/196) and the remaining were in late lactation period (43.88%, n = 86/196). The average milk yield per day per animal was 8 L and most animals (56.12%, n = 110/196) produced this amount of milk while the remaining animals (43.88%, n = 86/196) produced more than 8 L per day. Among the sampled animals, teat dipping was practised in less number of animals (27.55%, n = 54/196) and it was not practised in the majority of animals (72.45%, n = 142/196) (Table 2).

Discussion

In this study, Staphylococcus aureus was isolated from 39.79% (78/196) of bovine milk samples. This finding is comparable to the 39.92% prevalence reported by (Tiwari et al. 2022) in Chitwan. A slightly higher rate of 45% was observed by (Limbu et al. 2020) in Dharan, while (Joshi et al. 2014) documented a lower prevalence of 29.7% in Pokhara. Furthermore, similar findings (41.94%) were reported of water buffalo milk samples in the Philippines (Badua et al. 2020). In contrast, lower rates were observed by (Khanal and Pandit 2013) and (Shrestha et al. 2021) reporting 24.6% and 15.2%, respectively. These variations in prevalence may be attributed to differences in animal age, physiological condition, stress levels, parity, milk yield and farm‐specific factors such as management and hygiene practices (Zigo et al. 2021).

In this study, none of the evaluated factors such as species, lactation stage, parity, SCM, milk yield and teat dipping showed a statistically significant association with S. aureus. However, animals in early lactation and those not subjected to post‐milking teat dipping exhibited slightly higher infection rates. Similar findings were reported in Ethiopia (Girma and Tamir 2022) where early lactation was identified as a critical period due to physiological stress and compromised immune function. A study from Pakistan also noted an increased risk of S. aureus infection during early lactation (Ahmed et al. 2023). Furthermore, poor post‐milking teat disinfection practices have been consistently associated with higher rates of intramammary infections (Abe et al. 2020) and the absence of teat dipping was significantly linked to increased mastitis. These might include the specific characteristics of the Bardaghat dairy population, the sample size being insufficient to detect smaller effect sizes, or the influence of unmeasured confounding variables. This highlights the need for future research employing larger cohorts or multivariate statistical models. Such studies could provide a more definitive understanding of these relationships.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing of Staphylococcus aureus isolates demonstrated high sensitivity to tetracycline (84.62%), levofloxacin (84.62%), chloramphenicol (82.05%) and ciprofloxacin (70.51%). These findings align with studies in central Nepal, which reported comparable susceptibility of S. aureus isolates from mastitic milk to fluoroquinolones and chloramphenicol. Specifically, tetracycline resistance was observed in 15.49% of isolates, notably lower than the 36.8% resistance documented by (Tiwari et al. 2022). Conversely, 84.50% of the isolates were sensitive to tetracycline, closely mirroring the 83% sensitivity reported by (Dhakal et al. 2007). Similarly, ciprofloxacin resistance was 15.49%, considerably lower than the 69% reported by (Shrestha et al. 2021) with 81.69% of isolates exhibiting sensitivity, closely reflecting the 82% reported by (Dhakal et al. 2007). Regarding gentamicin, 35.21% of isolates were resistant, a rate higher than the 23.2% resistance documented by (Tiwari et al. 2022).

Notably, all isolates in the present study exhibited complete resistance (100%) to oxacillin, indicating the presence of MRSA. The prevalence of β‑lactam resistance observed in our isolates aligns with previous findings. The prevalence of widespread multi‐antibiotic resistance, including notable β‑lactam insensitivity among Staphylococcus aureus isolates from mastitic cattle, with several confirmed as MRSA carrying the mecA gene is already reported (Shrestha et al. 2021). Likewise, high resistance rates in SCM pathogens isolated in Kathmandu, reaffirming β‑lactam inefficacy in local herds is also documented (Paudel et al. 2023).

The finding of 100% oxacillin resistance among all S. aureus isolates is of critical importance. This indicates the widespread presence of MRSA in the study area. This high prevalence of β‐lactam resistance aligns with previous reports from Nepal (Joshi et al. 2014; Rajbhandari et al. 2024) and signifies a major challenge for mastitis treatment, potentially leading to therapeutic failures. The presence of such highly resistant strains also highlights a critical ‘One Health’ concern. More critically, it poses a substantial public health threat given the zoonotic potential of MRSA transmission to humans through direct contact with infected animals, farm environments, or the consumption of raw milk. Recent on‐farm epidemiological studies emphasize that livestock settings act as critical reservoirs for the dissemination of these pathogens. Similar molecular insights into antimicrobial‐resistant S. aureus strains in goats have demonstrated significant virulence profiling and highlighted the potential cross‐species zoonotic threat originating from livestock (Javed et al. 2023; Javed et al. 2024). Consequently, combating mastitis requires a comprehensive ‘One Health’ approach that monitors resistance dynamics at the human–animal–environment interface.

Multidrug resistance (MDR), defined as resistance to three or more classes of antibiotics, was observed in 25.64% of S. aureus isolates in this study. This finding is consistent with the results from Kumar et al. (2024) in Haryana, India, who reported MDR in 27% of S. aureus strains isolated from mastitis cases. The occurrence of MDR strains may be attributed to the widespread misuse of antibiotics in veterinary practices in Nepal, where informal drug sellers and para‐veterinarians often serve as the primary providers of animal healthcare (Rame Hau et al. 2025).

While this study provides critical baseline phenotypic data on antimicrobial resistance, a notable limitation is the absence of advanced molecular characterisation. Phenotypic screening is highly valuable for immediate clinical decision‐making; however, integrating molecular approaches is essential to fully understand the molecular dynamics and β‐lactam resistance mechanisms driving these phenotypes. For instance, the identification of specific resistance genes, such as the mecA gene, and virulence profiling, including coagulase gene identification, are critical for mapping the evolutionary trajectory of these pathogens in livestock settings (Rolo et al. 2017; Aqib et al. 2021). Future studies in this region should employ genotypic profiling to validate phenotypic resistance patterns and provide deeper molecular insights.

The MAR index, with 18% of isolates having an index greater than 0.2, strongly indicates exposure to high‐risk environments with frequent antibiotic exposure (Woh et al. 2023). This empirical evidence supports the contextual factors highlighted in our introduction, such as limited access to professional veterinary care, low awareness of mastitis prevention, and the reliance on para‐veterinarians or informal drug sellers for treatment. These factors often lead to inappropriate drug use and contribute to the observed antibiotic pressure in Nepal's dairy sector (Arnold et al. 2021).

Overall, the growing antimicrobial resistance among mastitis pathogens has serious implications not just for animal health but also for public health. This study highlights the need for improved mastitis control strategies, including farmer training, routine screening using tests like CMT and judicious use of antibiotics guided by culture and sensitivity results. Furthermore, given the high prevalence of multi‐drug resistant strains and potential therapeutic failures, there is a pressing need to explore alternative therapeutic strategies. Approaches such as drug repurposing are emerging as promising and cost‐effective strategies to identify potential novel therapeutics for the treatment of bovine mastitis when conventional antibiotics fail (Muzammil et al. 2022).

Conclusion

Our study identified the widespread presence of multidrug‐resistant S. aureus including MRSA, in bovine milk from Bardaghat Municipality, Nepal. This contamination, likely exacerbated by unhygienic milking practices and indiscriminate antibiotic use, poses a significant public health risk, particularly to consumers of raw or unpasteurized milk. Urgent measures are imperative, including the implementation of mandatory milk pasteurization at the farm level. Furthermore, comprehensive farmer training programs are essential to enhance awareness regarding hygienic practices, proper mastitis screening and the judicious use of antibiotics guided by sensitivity testing, thereby mitigating the spread of these resistant pathogens.
