Phenotype Switching From Delayed Cutaneous Reactions to Immediate Anaphylaxis Upon Rechallenge With Paclitaxel
Authors: Hwa-Jeong Seo, Su-Jin Park, Woo-Jung Song, Hyouk-Soo Kwon, You Sook Cho, Tae-Bum Kim
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

Paclitaxel is a well-established cause of immediate hypersensitivity reactions (HSRs), which are traditionally attributed to non-immunoglobulin E (IgE)-mediated mast cell activation related to the Cremophor EL vehicle.1 In contrast, delayed cutaneous HSRs occurring several days after paclitaxel administration are considered type IV reactions mediated by T cells.2 Here, we describe a clinically important hypersensitivity phenotype in which delayed maculopapular eruptions accompanied by fever preceded severe immediate anaphylaxis upon paclitaxel rechallenge.

We report 5 patients (mean age 43 ± 8.3 years) with breast, ovarian, cervical, or unknown primary cancer who developed delayed maculopapular rashes with fever 6–8 days after their first exposure to paclitaxel. The timing and clinical characteristics of these delayed reactions are consistent with previously reported delayed paclitaxel HSRs.3,4 These symptoms improved with supportive care or systemic corticosteroids. However, all patients experienced acute immediate reactions, including anaphylaxis, during subsequent paclitaxel administration. These reactions occurred within minutes of re-exposure and were characterized by respiratory compromise, urticaria (n = 2), and severe facial erythema (n = 1), with hypotension occurring in three cases (Table). In all cases, paclitaxel was the only agent administered at the time of anaphylaxis, supporting drug causality.

A striking and consistent feature of the initial delayed reactions was the presence of fever, suggesting systemic immune activation rather than isolated cutaneous involvement. This clinical sequence suggests that delayed type IV reactions and immediate anaphylaxis, while traditionally classified as mechanistically distinct,2 are not necessarily independent phenomena. Instead, our observations support the concept of phenotype switching, in which an initial delayed HSR may predispose patients to subsequent immediate, potentially life-threatening reactions upon rechallenge. An association between delayed and immediate HSRs to paclitaxel has been suggested in prior observational studies.3,4 Moreover, a fatal case of paclitaxel-induced anaphylaxis preceded by a delayed HSR has been reported, underscoring the potential severity of this pattern.5

Notably, all four patients who underwent rapid drug desensitization experienced recurrent breakthrough reactions (BTRs), which occurred in 8 of 13 total desensitization cycles (61.5%). These BTRs often occurred at early or low-dose steps despite intensified premedication. This unusually high rate of BTRs contrasts with previously reported outcomes of taxane desensitization, in which BTRs occurred in a minority of patients.6,7 These findings suggest that the delayed-to-immediate phenotype may represent a high-risk endophenotype characterized by persistent immune activation. Clinical manifestations of delayed cutaneous reactions and immediate hypersensitivity during rechallenge or desensitization are shown in the Supplementary Fig. S1.

The precise immunologic mechanisms underlying this phenotype remain unclear. While immediate paclitaxel reactions have long been considered predominantly non-IgE-mediated,1 IgE-mediated mechanisms have been demonstrated in a subset of patients with taxane hypersensitivity, even during early treatment cycles.1,7 Alternatively, immune activation associated with the T-cell–mediated delayed reaction may lower the threshold for mast cell activation upon re-exposure.

Delayed maculopapular eruptions accompanied by fever after paclitaxel administration should not be dismissed as minor adverse events. Rather, they may serve as a sentinel warning for immediate anaphylaxis upon re-exposure. Patients exhibiting this pattern warrant prompt allergy referral, careful risk stratification, and cautious consideration of rechallenge strategies, including modified desensitization protocols or alternative therapies.
