Abstract
During the Cosmic Dawn (CD), the HI 21-cm optical depth ( τ \tau ) in the intergalactic medium can become significantly large. Consequently, the second and higher-order terms of τ \tau appearing in the Taylor expansion of the HI 21-cm differential brightness temperature ( δ ​ T b \delta T_{\rm b} ) become important. This introduces additional non-Gaussianity into the signal. We study the impact of large τ \tau on statistical quantities of HI 21-cm signal using a suite of standard numerical simulations that vary X-ray heating efficiency and the minimum halo mass required to host radiation sources. We find that the higher order terms suppress statistical quantities such as skewness, power-spectrum and bispectrum. However, the effect is found to be particularly strong on the non-Gaussian signal. We find that the change in skewness can reach several hundred percent in low X-ray heating scenarios. For moderate and high X-ray heating models changes are around ∼ 40 % \sim 40\% and 60 % 60\% , respectively, for M h , min = 10 9 ​ M ⊙ M_{\rm h,min}=10^{9}\,{\rm M}_{\odot} . This change is around ∼ 75 % \sim 75\% , 25 % 25\% and 20 % 20\% for low, moderate and high X-ray heating models, respectively, for M h , min = 10 10 ​ M ⊙ M_{\rm h,min}=10^{10}\,{\rm M}_{\odot} . The change in bispectrum in both the halo cutoff mass scenarios ranges from ∼ 10 % \sim 10\% to ∼ 300 % \sim 300\% for low X-ray heating model. However, for moderate and high X-ray heating models the change remains between ∼ 10 % \sim 10\% to ∼ 200 % \sim 200\% for both equilateral and squeezed limit triangle configuration. Finally, we find that up to third orders of τ \tau need to be retained to accurately model δ ​ T b \delta T_{\rm b} , especially for capturing the non-Gaussian features in the H i 21-cm signal.

2.1
Basic equations of H
i
21-cm signal

The differential brightness temperature corresponding to redshifted H
i

21
-cm signal relative to the CMBR can be written as
(
Rybicki & Lightman 1986
)
,

\delta T_{\rm b}({\bf n},z)=\frac{T_{S}({\bf n},z)-T_{\gamma}(z)}{1+z}\left[1-\exp\{-\tau({\bf n},z)\}\right],

(1)

where
T_{S}({\bf n},z)
is the H
i
spin temperature corresponding to its hyperfine transition and
T_{\gamma}
is the CMBR temperature.
\tau({\bf n},z)
is the optical depth that quantifies the level of absorption or emission of H
i
21-cm line within a medium (here IGM) and can be written as
(
Pritchard & Loeb 2008
)
,

\displaystyle\tau({\bf n},z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 27\hskip 2.84544pt\frac{x_{\rm HI}(\textbf{n},z)}{T_{S}(\textbf{n},z)}\left[1+\delta_{\rm b}(\textbf{n},z)\right]\left(\frac{\Omega_{\rm b}h^{2}}{0.023}\right)

(2)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\left(\frac{0.15}{\Omega_{\rm m}h^{2}\cdot 10}\right)^{1/2}(1+z)^{3/2}\,\mathrm{mK},

where
x_{\rm HI}(\textbf{n},z)
and
\delta_{\rm b}(\textbf{n},z)
are neutral hydrogen fraction and baryonic density contrast respectively along the line of sight direction
n
and redshift
z
.
\Omega_{\rm b}
and
\Omega_{\rm m}
are the cosmological baryon density and total matter density parameters at present, respectively.
h
is the present Hubble constant in units of
100
km/s/Mpc. We do not consider the effects of the peculiar velocity in this study.

Normally, it is assumed that
\tau({\bf n},z)\ll 1
, and under this assumption the equation (
1
) can be approximated as,

\delta T_{\rm b}({\bf n},z)=\frac{T_{S}({\bf n},z)-T_{\gamma}(z)}{1+z}\tau({\bf n},z),

(3)

which can be further written as,

\displaystyle\delta T_{\rm b}(\textbf{n},z)=27x_{\rm HI}(\textbf{n},z)\big[1+\delta_{\rm b}(\textbf{n},z)\big]\left(\frac{\Omega_{\rm b}h^{2}}{0.023}\right)

\displaystyle\times\left(\frac{0.15}{\Omega_{\rm m}h^{2}}\frac{1+z}{10}\right)^{1/2}\left[1-\frac{T_{\gamma}(z)}{T_{\rm S}(\textbf{n},z)}\right]\,\mathrm{mK}.

(4)

The above equation is widely used for modelling the signal, both analytically and numerically. However, the optical depth can be very large at high redshifts in places where the spin temperature is low and the H
i
density is large. During CD, the spin temperature can be very low before the onset of X-ray heating when it is coupled to the IGM kinetic temperature. This leads to a large H
i
optical depth, particularly in regions with high H
i
density.
For larger values of
\tau
, it is necessary to include higher-order terms, such as
\tau^{2}
,
\tau^{3}
, and beyond, in equation (
3
) to accurately compute
\delta T_{\rm b}(\textbf{n},z)
. However, for moderate
\tau
values equation (
1
) can be approximated as,

\delta T_{\rm b}(\textbf{n},z)=\frac{T_{S}(\textbf{n},z)-T_{\gamma}(z)}{1+z}\left[\tau-\tau^{2}/2+\tau^{3}/6-\mathcal{O}(\tau^{4})\right].

(5)

This inclusion of higher-order terms would influence various statistics of the signal.

shows the percentage difference between the two estimates, which quantifies the effect of higher-order terms. We find that all models show significant deviations in skewness. These deviations tend to occur at the redshifts where
\tau
is large.

At the beginning of CD, skewness is largely negative around redshift
z=18
. As redshift decreases, its magnitude gradually reduces until the heating of the IGM begins. However, during the initial stages of X-ray heating the skewness starts becoming more negative again. As redshift decreases further, heated regions around sources grow significantly and percolates through the IGM. As a result, skewness becomes positive and continues to increase. Eventually, when the entire IGM is nearly heated, the skewness decreases again.
For example, in the model with
M_{\rm h,min}=10^{10}

{\rm M}_{\odot}
and
f_{\rm x}=1000

\approx-30
at redshift
z\approx 18
. Its magnitude gradually decreases until redshift
z\sim 14
as the
\rm{Ly{\alpha}}
coupling spreads through the IGM and nears completion. Following this, X-ray heating starts, and skewness tends towards more negative values until redshift
z\approx 12.5
. However, as the heated regions expand and percolates through the IGM, the skewness quickly becomes positive, reaching a peak around
z\approx 11.5
. Later, skewness decreases again as almost the entire IGM gets heated. A similar pattern is observed in other scenarios. However, the actual timings of these processes differ depending on the values of
M_{\rm h,min}
and
f_{\rm x}
. In general, higher values of
M_{\rm h,min}
and/or lower values of
f_{\rm x}
delay these processes.

We can further see from the left column of Fig.
4
that the initial trend in the redshift evolution of skewness is almost similar for a fixed
M_{\rm h,min}
value when
\rm{Ly{\alpha}}
coupling dominates. This redshift range is from the beginning up to
z\approx 16.5
for
M_{\rm h,min}=10^{9}

{\rm M}_{\odot}
and upto
z\sim 14
for
M_{\rm h,min}=10^{10}

{\rm M}_{\odot}
models. For larger value of
f_{\rm x}
, X-ray heating starts earlier and dominates the
\rm{Ly{\alpha}}
coupling, shortening its redshift window (lower panel of Fig.

).

Based on the results above, we conclude that large heated and Ly-
\alpha
coupled regions enhance the non-Gaussianity of the signal. The completion of
\rm{Ly{\alpha}}
coupling and IGM heating, however, leads to a gradual decrease in non-Gaussianity.

3.4
Impact of large optical depth on Skewness

We now shift our focus to the impact of high H
i
21-cm optical depth on
\delta T_{\rm b}
and its statistics. If we choose a model where heating is inefficient as in
f_{\rm x}=0.1
,
M_{\rm h,min}=10^{9}

{\rm M}_{\odot}
,
\tau
would be sufficiently large throughout the entire CD as the spin temperature remains very low. As a result,
\delta T_{\rm b}
calculated using exact equation is considerably suppressed compared to
\delta T_{\rm b}
calculated using the approximated equation (see lower panel of fig.
1
). Further, the skewness of
\delta T_{\rm b}
is suppressed compared to that estimated using the approximated
\delta T_{\rm b}
.

During the initial stages of CD, the skewness of the approximated and exact
\delta T_{\rm b}
takes large negative values. This is because
\rm{Ly{\alpha}}
radiation couples the H
i
21-cm spin temperature to the IGM kinetic temperature within regions surrounded by the sources, whereas the background IGM remains coupled with the CMBR temperature. This makes the H
i
21-cm signal negative inside the sources and consequently the overall signal negatively skewed. We do not see any significant difference between the exact and approximated
\delta T_{\rm b}
, mainly because H
i
21-cm optical depth is small during the initial stages of CD.

However, in Fig.
4
we see that the magnitude of skewness decreases as we go from redshift
z=18
to lower redshifts. During this period, the
\rm{Ly{\alpha}}
coupling percolates through the IGM and eventually results into a uniform
\rm{Ly{\alpha}}
coupled IGM. The average spin temperature decreases and
\tau
increases until the redshift around which the heating starts. This makes the higher-order terms of
\tau
in equation (
1
) important. Consequently, the difference in skewness between the approximated and the exact
\delta T_{\rm b}
also increases with decreasing redshift. This change can go up to
\sim 100\%
in some cases considered here.

Once X-ray heating starts, kinetic and spin temperatures start increasing with decreasing redshift. Consequently, the optical depth starts to decrease with redshift. This makes the contribution of higher-order terms less important. Therefore, the exact
\delta T_{\rm b}
is very similar to the approximated one toward the end of CD.

Due to very inefficient heating in models corresponding to
f_{\rm x}=0.1
, the IGM, including the
\rm{Ly{\alpha}}
coupled regions near the sources, remains cold for an extended period of time. Therefore, the average
\delta T_{\rm b}
remains negative and the PDF of
\delta T_{\rm b}
remains negatively skewed up to redshift
z\sim 11
. Cold IGM in these models also lead to a larger optical depth making higher-order terms more impactful on the global signal as well as on the skewness. We see that (first two top-right panels in Fig.
4
) the differences in skewness go up to
\sim 500\%
and
\sim 75\%
for models with
(f_{\rm x},M_{\rm h,min})=(0.1,10^{9}\,{\rm M}_{\odot})
and
(0.1,10^{10}\,{\rm M}_{\odot})
, respectively.

X-ray heating is moderate in the model with
f_{\rm x}=46.4
,
M_{\rm h,min}=10^{9}\,{\rm M}_{\odot}
. Therefore, the change in the skewness between approximated and exact
\delta T_{\rm b}
goes up to
\sim 40\%
at redshift
z\sim 14
when the average optical depth becomes maximum in the model. Similarly, we see that the change is only up to
\approx 25\%
at redshift
z\approx 12
for
M_{\rm h,min}=10^{10}\,{\rm M}_{\odot}
for the same X-ray efficiency parameter.
Models with
f_{\rm x}=1000
exhibit similar features, but these appear at relatively earlier redshifts. Here, the maximum difference in skewness between the exact and approximated models is
\approx 60\%
and
\approx 20\%

respectively for
M_{\rm h,min}=10^{9}\,{\rm M}_{\odot}
and
\rm 10^{10}\,{\rm M}_{\odot}

3.5
Redshift evolution of bispectrum

Bispectrum is the lowest order many-point statistics that is sensitive to non-Gaussianity. Bispectrum of a
\delta T_{\rm b}
field within a volume
V
can be written as,

\delta^{\rm K}_{3\rm D}(\textbf{{k}}_{1}+\textbf{{k}}_{2}+\textbf{{k}}_{3})~B(\textbf{{k}}_{1},\textbf{{k}}_{2},\textbf{{k}}_{3})=V^{-1}\langle\Delta_{\rm b}(\textbf{{k}}_{1})\Delta_{\rm b}(\textbf{{k}}_{2})\Delta_{\rm b}(\textbf{{k}}_{3})\rangle,

where
\Delta_{\rm b}(\textbf{{k}})
is the Fourier conjugate of
\delta T_{\rm b}(\textbf{{x}})
,
\langle\cdots\rangle
denotes an ensemble average. The Kronecker delta function
\delta^{\rm K}_{3\rm D}
in the definition is an implication of statistically homogeneity of the signal which ensures that
k
-triplets must form a closed triangle for a non zero bispectrum. We computed bispectrum of the simulated
\delta T_{\rm b}
maps using an FFT-based fast estimator
(
Shaw et al. 2021
)
where the Kronecker delta function is replaced by summation of plane waves over grid points in real space. To determine the shape of the triangle formed by the three
k
-modes, this algorithm uses the parametrization:
\mu=-(\textbf{{k}}_{1}\cdot\textbf{{k}}_{2})/(k_{1}~k_{2})
and
t=k_{2}/k_{1}
, where
k_{i}=|\textbf{{k}}_{i}|
. The cosine of the angle between
\textbf{{k}}_{1}
and
\textbf{{k}}_{2}
is denoted as
\mu
, whereas
t
is the ratio between them and each set of
(k_{1},\mu,t)
represents a different triangle configuration. For unique triangle configuration, order of three
k
sides and the range of
\mu
and
t
values are prescribed in
(
Bharadwaj et al. 2020
)
as,

\displaystyle k_{1}\geq k_{2}\geq k_{3};\quad{\rm}0.5\leq t,\,\mu\leq 1\quad{\rm and}\quad 2\mu t\geq 1.\

We have divided the
k
-space into
20
logarithmic spherical shells for bispectrum estimation. Each combination of three shells forms a particular bin in the triangle shape and size space
(k_{1},\mu,t)
. The average size of the bin corresponds to
k_{1}
. Sampling of (
\mu,t
) space for a fixed
k_{1}
represents different triangle shape configurations. We suggest the reader to
Bharadwaj et al. 2020
and
Shaw et al. 2021
for the details about the triangle shape and the bispectrum framework. In this work, we consider only the equilateral (
k_{1}=k_{2}=k_{3}
) and the squeezed limit triangles (
\textbf{{k}}_{1}\approx-\textbf{{k}}_{2}
,
\textbf{{k}}_{3}\approx 0
) for our bispectrum analysis.

for both the equilateral and squeezed limit triangles. The bispectrum corresponding to the equilateral triangles finds the correlation between equal length scales and that corresponds to the squeezed limit configuration calculates the correlation between two equal length scales and one very large length scale, respectively.

\Delta^{3}(k_{1},\mu,t)

\delta T_{\rm b}

5

\approx 12

We see in this study that, for squeezed limit configuration, all the features appear at later stages compared to the equilateral one. The reason is that, in the squeezed-limit triangle configuration, there is a correlation involving one very large length scale, and astrophysical processes begin to affect such large scales at later times, i.e., at lower redshifts.

As the X-ray heating progresses, it gradually percolates through the IGM. This raises the entire IGM temperature above the CMBR temperature. The widespread heating again reduces the bispectrum. We see this effect in Fig.

f_{\rm x}=46.4
and

For
f_{\rm x}=1000

3.6
Impact of large optical depth on bispectrum

The right columns of Figures
5
and
6
show the corresponding percentage change in the exact bispectra compared to the approximated bispectra calculated as,
\frac{\Delta^{3}_{\rm approx}-\Delta^{3}_{\rm exact}}{\Delta^{3}_{\rm exact}}\times 100\%
as a function of redshift. We observe that, for most of the CD period, the magnitude of the exact bispectrum is suppressed compared to the approximate one. This suppression is mainly caused by the term
-\tau^{2}/2
in the expression for the differential brightness temperature (equation
5
), which reduces the value of
\delta T_{\rm b}
.

Since
\tau^{2}\propto\rho^{2}_{\rm HI}/T^{2}_{S}
, the suppression is not uniform across the
field. It is stronger in regions with a higher neutral hydrogen density and lower spin temperature. As a result, the
\tau^{2}/2
term affects the non-Gaussian signal (i.e., bispectrum and skewness) more than the global or Gaussian component of the signal. For example, the percentage change in the bispectrum is found to be
\sim 10\%
at redshift
z=18
across all models and triangle configurations. As redshift decreases, this change increases, reaches a maximum at an intermediate redshift, and then declines again. This trend can be explained if we look at the redshift evolution of H
i
21-cm optical depth. As shown in Fig.
1
, the average optical depth increases, peaks at a certain redshift, and then decreases at lower redshifts. The percentage difference in the bispectrum becomes largest when the optical depth is highest, consistent with what is observed in the right columns of Figs.
5
and
6
. We find that the percentage change in the bispectrum increases up to
\sim 250\%
at redshifts
z\approx 14
and
\approx 12
for
f_{\rm x}=0.1
, for both the equilateral and squeezed triangles at
k_{1}=0.16\,{\rm Mpc}^{-1}
. A similar trend is observed on smaller scales, e.g., at
k_{1}=0.8\,{\rm Mpc}^{-1}
(see Fig.
6
) where the change reaches up to
\sim 300\%
. This large change is due to the fact that both the IGM kinetic temperature and hence the spin temperature remain very low in this case because heating is highly inefficient. In models with moderate heating (
f_{\rm x}=46.4
), the percentage change is smaller, typically lies between
\sim 10\%
to
\sim 200\%
. This is because heating is more efficient than in the low
f_{\rm x}
case. This results in higher IGM and spin temperatures. As a result, the H
i
optical depth becomes lower which reduces the impact of higher-order terms in
\tau
. For models with very efficient heating (
f_{\rm x}=1000
), the IGM and spin temperatures are large from the beginning of the CD. This makes the contribution of higher-order terms less significant. Consequently, the change in the bispectrum is small, around
10\%
, in these cases. However, we find that the change reaches up to
\sim 200\%
at a few redshifts. We also find that the percentage change can sometimes be negative. This typically occurs when the bispectrum transitions from negative to positive or vice versa.

6
Summary and Discussions

The H
i
21-cm optical depth, which is proportional to the neutral hydrogen density and inversely proportional to the spin temperature, can become quite large during the Cosmic Dawn (CD). This is because the spin temperature is expected to remain very low before X-ray heating begins. Our simulations show that, in some CD models, the optical depth can reach values as high as
\approx 0.55
, with nearly
50\%
of the simulation pixels showing optical depths greater than
0.1
. Therefore, the commonly used approximation that the optical depth is much less than one (
\tau<<1
) which simplifies the expression
[1-\exp(-\tau)]\approx\tau
may not hold during this epoch.

To revisit the impact of this assumption on
\delta T_{b}
and its associated statistics, we performed a suite of numerical simulations spanning a wide range of astrophysical parameters, including the X-ray heating efficiency (
f_{\rm x}
) and the minimum halo mass (
M_{\rm h,min}
). These simulations model
\rm{Ly{\alpha}}
coupling, X-ray heating, spin temperature evolution, and ionization using a 1D radiative transfer code, named
grizzly
. We used them to study how large optical depths affect the statistical properties of the 21-cm signal, including the power spectrum, skewness, and bispectrum of the differential brightness temperature
\delta T_{\rm b}
.

We find that
\delta T_{\rm b}
is significantly suppressed when the full expression with
[1-\exp(-\tau)]
is used, particularly in regions with high neutral hydrogen density and low spin temperature. This suppression is not uniform across the volume but is stronger in regions where the ratio
\rho_{\rm HI}/T_{s}
is higher. As a result, a new kind of non-Gaussianity (due to higher order terms of
\tau
) emerges with a sign opposite to that of the existing bispectrum. In the model with
M_{\rm h,min}=10^{9}\,{\rm M}_{\odot}
and
f_{\rm x}=0.1
, the skewness changes by up to
\sim 500\%
. For the same halo mass, the change is about
40\%
and
60\%
for
f_{\rm x}=46.4
and
f_{\rm x}=1000
, respectively. For a higher halo mass threshold of
M_{\rm h,min}=10^{10}\,{\rm M}_{\odot}
, the skewness changes by approximately
75\%
,
25\%
, and
20\%
for
f_{\rm x}=0.1
,
46.4
, and
1000
, respectively.

We also studied the impact on the bispectrum in both equilateral and squeezed triangle configurations across all redshifts during CD. We analyzed two different scales,
k_{1}=0.16\,{\rm Mpc}^{-1}
and
0.8\,{\rm Mpc}^{-1}
, representing large and small scales. For low-heating models (
f_{\rm x}=0.1
) with both
M_{\rm h,min}=10^{9},{\rm M}_{\odot}
and
10^{10},{\rm M}_{\odot}
, where the optical depth remains high throughout the CD, the change in the bispectrum is consistently large—reaching up to
\sim 300\%
at both scales and triangle configurations. However, in models with moderate and high X-ray heating efficiency (
f_{\rm x}=46.4
and
1000
), the changes are smaller—typically around
\sim 10\%
to
\sim 200\%
, regardless of halo mass or triangle shape.

Further, we investigated how many terms in the
\tau
-expansion are needed to accurately model
\delta T_{\rm b}
, especially to capture its non-Gaussian features. We find that including terms up to third order in
\tau
reduces the difference to less than
\sim 1\%
. This result holds across all

k
-values, redshifts, models, and triangle configurations considered in our study.

Finally, we note that our entire analysis has been carried out in real space. Including the effect of peculiar velocities would further enhance the optical depth. This could amplify the impact on the 21-cm signal.
