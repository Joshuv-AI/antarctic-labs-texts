A multilayer level-set method for eikonal-based traveltime tomography
Wenbin Li, Ken K.T. Hung, Shingyu Leung

Abstract
We present a novel multilayer level-set method (MLSM) for eikonal-based first-arrival traveltime tomography. Unlike classical level-set approaches that rely solely on the zero-level set, the MLSM represents multiple phases through a sequence of i n i_{n} -level sets ( n = 0 , 1 , 2 , ⋯ n=0,1,2,\cdots ). Near each i n i_{n} -level set, the function is designed to behave like a local signed-distance function, enabling a single level-set formulation to capture arbitrarily many interfaces and subregions. Within this Eulerian framework, first-arrival traveltimes are computed as viscosity solutions of the eikonal equation, and Fréchet derivatives of the misfit are obtained via the adjoint state method. To stabilize the inversion, we incorporate several regularization strategies, including multilayer reinitialization, arc-length penalization, and Sobolev smoothing of model parameters. In addition, we introduce an illumination-based error measure to assess reconstruction quality. Numerical experiments demonstrate that the proposed MLSM efficiently recovers complex discontinuous slowness models with multiple phases and interfaces.

Introduction

Traveltime tomography is a widely used method in geophysical and medical imaging that determines the spatial distribution of a medium’s wave-propagation velocity (or its reciprocal, slowness) by inverting measured wave traveltimes
[
4
,
40
,
19
,
11
,
13
]
. This technique is broadly applicable to different wave phenomena, including seismic, electromagnetic, and acoustic waves. The method is predicated on the principle that the traveltime between a source and a receiver represents a path integral of the medium’s slowness, such that variations in the measured traveltimes reveal heterogeneity within the medium being imaged. Due to the multiple ray paths that can connect a source and receiver, traveltime tomography can be formulated using either first-arrival
[
18
,
36
]
or multi-arrival data
[
8
,
19
]
. For this work, we focus specifically on the inverse problem of first-arrival traveltime tomography.

Traditional traveltime tomography methods primarily rely on the Lagrangian ray-tracing technique, solving a set of ordinary differential equations derived from Fermat’s principle to calculate the trajectory of wave rays and their traveltime
[
27
]
. The technique is termed Lagrangian because it explicitly tracks the motion of rays through space, in contrast to Eulerian methods which observe properties at fixed spatial points.
A major drawback of the ray-tracing approach is its non-uniform resolution, where ray paths are highly sensitive to initial conditions and the medium’s velocity structure. In media with strong heterogeneity, the ray-tracing technique inevitably produces an irregular distribution of ray paths. This uneven coverage leaves portions of the model poorly constrained (e.g., forming shadow zones), and may result in an ill-conditioned system of equations in the traveltime tomography
[
2
,
3
]
.

An alternative approach is to compute the traveltime in an Eulerian framework. This is achieved by solving the eikonal equation across a computational grid, where typical approaches include the fast marching method
[
34
]
and the fast sweeping method
[
41
,
32
,
22
]
. Based on the fast sweeping method,
[
18
]
develops a PDE-based Eulerian approach to traveltime tomography using first arrivals.
In that work, the first-arrival traveltime is modeled as the viscosity solution
[
6
]
of the eikonal equation, which is computed efficiently by the fast sweeping method. The traveltime tomography is then performed by minimizing the data misfit under the constraint of eikonal equation, where an adjoint state method
[
31
]
is proposed to solve the PDE-constrained optimization problem. To stabilize the gradient flow, a Sobolev regularization is imposed on the velocity perturbations, and so the algorithm of
[
18
]
leads to a smooth velocity model.

In practice, many important velocity/slowness models are characterized by sharp discontinuities representing faults, layer boundaries, or interfaces between distinct units. For discontinuous velocity/slowness models,
[
30
]
has introduced the concept of an extended viscosity solution to justify the well-posedness of the eikonal equation. This solution exists and is Lipschitz continuous when the slowness function is piecewise Lipschitz continuous, and it corresponds to the first-arrival traveltime. Moreover, any finite difference scheme that is monotonic in slowness and converges to the viscosity solution for continuous slowness must also converge to the extended viscosity solution for discontinuous slowness
[
17
]
.
In
[
20
]
, we have developed a level-set adjoint state method for first-arrival traveltime tomography where the underlying slowness is discontinuous. In that work, the level-set method is employed to represent a piecewise continuous slowness function. The extended viscosity solution of the eikonal equation is computed to match the first-arrival traveltime, and an adjoint state method similar to that of
[
18
]
is proposed to solve the tomography problem. In
[
21
]
, we extend the level-set adjoint state method to solve crosswell transmission-reflection traveltime tomography problems, where the level-set function describes slowness discontinuities and the geometry of reflectors.

The level-set method
[
28
,
29
]
has been widely used in various fields involving topological changes and interface structures, including multiphase flow simulation, shape optimization, computational geometry, and computer graphics. In terms of inverse problems, the level-set method is first used for inverse obstacle problems in
[
33
]
, and thereafter, it is applied to a variety of inverse problems. For instance, in
[
26
]
, it is used to reconstruct 2-D binary obstacles; in
[
14
]
and
[
9
]
, it is used for inverse scattering to determine the geometry of extended targets; in
[
1
]
for electrical resistance tomography in medical imaging; in
[
16
,
15
,
23
]
for inverse gravimetry; and in
[
24
,
25
]
for magnetic inverse problems.

Classical level-set methods represent interfaces via the zero-level set of a single level-set function, where the interfaces (connected or disjointed) partition the domain into two phases. However, many practical problems involve heterogeneous structures with multiple phases and interacting interfaces. For instance, the Dirichlet
k
-partition problem seeks to partition a domain into
k
distinct subdomains to minimize the sum of their smallest eigenvalues
[
5
]
. In tomography problems, there is significant interest in recovering media with multi-component and multilayered architectures. To represent such multi-phase structures, the multiple level-set method has been developed
[
42
,
38
,
7
,
37
]
. This approach introduces more than one level-set functions to define the interfaces between distinct components. For instance, in
[
42
]
, it associates to each sub-region a level-set function, requiring
n
level-set functions for
n
different phases. In
[
38
,
7
,
37
]
, a compact formulation is developed that allows
n
level-set functions to represent up to
2^{n}
distinct phases. The use of multiple level-set functions complicates the interface representation, and significantly increases the computational cost for inverse problems.

In this paper, we propose a novel multilayer level-set method (MLSM) for eikonal-based first-arrival traveltime tomography. The proposed MLSM uses a single level-set function to represent multiple subregions of distinct phases. Unlike conventional level-set methods that only utilize the zero-level set, the MLSM defines interfaces of multiple phases using a series of
i_{n}
-level-sets (
n=0,1,2\cdots
). Near each
i_{n}
-level-set, the multilayer level-set function is designed to behave as a local signed-distance function. This framework allows for the delineation of arbitrarily many distinct subregions using only one level-set function. We then utilize the MLSM to reconstruct multi-phase heterogeneous structures in traveltime tomography.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section 2 develops the formulation of the multilayer level-set function, and explains how to use it to represent piecewise structures with multiple interfaces. Section 3 proposes the multilayer level-set method (MLSM) for eikonal-based traveltime tomography. In this section, we adopt the Eulerian framework of
[
18
,
20
]
to compute the Fréchet derivatives of the traveltime data misfit, develop comprehensive regularization techniques for the MLSM formulation, and propose an illumination-based error measure to evaluate the performance of the inversion algorithm. Section 4 presents numerical results to demonstrate the algorithm’s efficacy, and Section 5 draws conclusions.

2
A novel Multilayer level-set method (MLSM)

2.1
Formulation and interface representation

The multilayer level-set method (MLSM) aims to represent interfaces of multiple phases by a single level-set function. Considering a real function
\phi(\mathbf{x})
and a series of real numbers
i_{0}<i_{1}<\cdots<i_{N-1}
such that the following relation is satisfied.

\{\phi(\mathbf{x})<i_{0}\}\subset\{\phi(\mathbf{x})<i_{1}\}\subset\cdots\subset\{\phi(\mathbf{x})<i_{N-1}\}.

(2.1)

This implies a multilayer structure,
\Omega_{0}\subset\Omega_{1}\subset\cdots\subset\Omega_{N-1}
, where

\Omega_{n}=\{\mathbf{x}\mid\phi(\mathbf{x})<i_{n}\},\quad\forall n=0,1,\cdots,N-1.

(2.2)

Then the interface
\partial\Omega_{n}
can be represented by the
i_{n}
-level-set,

\partial\Omega_{n}=\{\mathbf{x}\mid\phi(\mathbf{x})=i_{n}\},\quad\forall n=0,1,\cdots,N-1.

(2.3)

With this formulation, a single level-set function can represent a series of interfaces of different phases. It is natural to take the numbers
\{i_{n}\}_{n=0}^{N-1}
as an arithmetic sequence, i.e.,
\Delta i=i_{n}-i_{n-1}
is constant; for example,
i_{n}=n
, or
i_{n}=\frac{n}{2}
in a small computational domain.

Like the typical level-set method, the level-set function
\phi
is expected to have a smooth transition through the interfaces
\partial\Omega_{n}
. As a result, we design the level-set function
\phi(\mathbf{x})
to behave as a signed-distance function near each
\partial\Omega_{n}
. Let
d_{n}(\mathbf{x})
denote the signed distance function to
\partial\Omega_{n}
,

d_{n}(\mathbf{x})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}-\mathrm{dist}(\mathbf{x},\partial\Omega_{n})\,,&\mathbf{x}\in\Omega_{n}\\
\mathrm{dist}(\mathbf{x},\partial\Omega_{n})\,,&\mathbf{x}\in\Omega^{c}_{n}\end{array}\right.,

(2.4)

where
\mathrm{dist}(\mathbf{x},\partial\Omega_{n}):=\inf_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\in\partial\Omega_{n}}\|\mathbf{x}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\|_{2}
,
\forall n=0,1,\cdots,N-1
. The multilayer level-set function is then designed in the following way:

\phi(\mathbf{x})=i_{n_{\mathbf{x}}}+\min\left(\max\left(d_{n_{\mathbf{x}}}(\mathbf{x}),-\frac{\Delta i}{2}\right),\frac{\Delta i}{2}\right)\,,\quad\mathrm{where}\ n_{\mathbf{x}}:=\mathrm{argmin}_{n}|d_{n}(\mathbf{x})|\,.

(2.5)

With formula (
2.5
),
\partial\Omega_{n}
is represented by the
i_{n}
-level-set of
\phi(\mathbf{x})
; around
\partial\Omega_{n}
,
\phi(\mathbf{x})
has the form of
\phi(\mathbf{x})=i_{n}+d_{n}(\mathbf{x})
. Moreover, we impose a truncation on each signed-distance function
d_{n}(\mathbf{x})
, making it between
-\frac{\Delta i}{2}
and
\frac{\Delta i}{2}
,
\forall n=0,1,\cdots,N-1
. Then the multilayer level-set function
\phi(\mathbf{x})
lies in the region
\left[i_{0}-\frac{\Delta i}{2},i_{N-1}+\frac{\Delta i}{2}\right]
.

Alternatively, if one is interested in representing two disjoint regions
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
, then formula (
2.5
) can be used to construct a corresponding multilayer level-set function.
The signed distance function
d_{1}
is however defined in the following way

d_{1}(\mathbf{x})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\mathrm{dist}(\mathbf{x},\partial\Omega_{1})\,,&\mathbf{x}\in\Omega_{1}\\
-\mathrm{dist}(\mathbf{x},\partial\Omega_{1})\,,&\mathbf{x}\in\Omega^{c}_{1}\end{array}\right..

\phi(\mathbf{x})
in (
2.5
).

.

It is the signed-distance function of the circle
\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}=0.5
. Considering
i_{n}=n
and so
\Delta i=1
, it is also a mutilayer level-set function satisfying equation (
2.5
). Here,
\Omega_{n}=\{(x,y)\mid\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}<n+0.5\}
, and
\partial\Omega_{n}=\{(x,y)\mid\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}=n+0.5\}
. Figure

in

, where we indicate its 0-level-set and 1-level-set, respectively.

Let
d_{0}(x,y)=\sqrt{(x+1)^{2}+y^{2}}-0.3
and
d_{1}(x,y)=0.3-\sqrt{(x-1)^{2}+y^{2}}
. We define the level-set function,

\phi(x,y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\min\left(\max\left(d_{0}(x,y),-\frac{1}{2}\right),\frac{1}{2}\right)\,,&|d_{0}(x,y)|<|d_{1}(x,y)|\\
1+\min\left(\max\left(d_{1}(x,y),-\frac{1}{2}\right),\frac{1}{2}\right)\,,&|d_{0}(x,y)|\geq|d_{1}(x,y)|\end{array}\right.\,.

It is a multilayer level-set function in the form of equation (

). Here,
N=2
,

, and so
\Delta i=1
. Considering equations (
2.2
) and (
2.3
), the represented regions and interfaces are as follows:

\displaystyle\Omega_{0}=\{(x,y)\mid\sqrt{(x+1)^{2}+y^{2}}<0.3\},

\displaystyle\partial\Omega_{0}=\{(x,y)\mid\sqrt{(x+1)^{2}+y^{2}}=0.3\}\,;

\displaystyle\Omega_{1}=\{(x,y)\mid\sqrt{(x-1)^{2}+y^{2}}>0.3\},

\displaystyle\partial\Omega_{1}=\{(x,y)\mid\sqrt{(x-1)^{2}+y^{2}}=0.3\}\,.

\phi(x,y)
in
[-2,2]\times[-2,2]
, where we indicate its 0-level-set and 1-level-set, respectively.

2.2
Representing piecewise structures with multiple interfaces

Consider a piecewise function as follows,

S(\mathbf{x})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}S_{n}(\mathbf{x})&,&\mathbf{x}\in D_{n},\ n=0,1,\cdots,N-1\\
S_{N}(\mathbf{x})&,&\mathbf{x}\in\left(\bigcup_{n=0}^{N-1}D_{n}\right)^{c}\end{array}\right.\,.

(2.6)

We explain how to represent
S(\mathbf{x})
using the multilayer level-set function
\phi(\mathbf{x})
proposed above. Considering the definition of
\Omega_{n}
in equation (
2.2
), Let
D_{0}=\Omega_{0}=\{\mathbf{x}\mid\phi(\mathbf{x})<i_{0}\}
, and
D_{n}=\Omega_{n}\setminus\Omega_{n-1}=\{\mathbf{x}\mid i_{n-1}\leq\phi(\mathbf{x})<i_{n}\}
,
\forall n=1,\cdots,N-1
. The multilayer level-set representation of
S(\mathbf{x})
reads as follows,

S(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}p_{n}\left(1-H(\phi-i_{n})\right)+p_{N}H\left(\phi-i_{N-1}\right)\,,

(2.7)

where
H(\cdot)
denotes the Heaviside function:
H(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&,&x<0\\
1&,&x\geq 0\end{array}\right.
. In equation (
2.7
), the parameters
p_{n}
are related to the function values
S_{n}
in the following way,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}S_{n}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{k=n}^{N-1}p_{k}(\mathbf{x}),&n=0,1,\cdots,N-1\\
S_{N}(\mathbf{x})=p_{N}(\mathbf{x})&\end{array}\right.\,,

(2.8)

and so

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}p_{n}(\mathbf{x})=S_{n}(\mathbf{x})-S_{n+1}(\mathbf{x}),&n=0,1,\cdots,N-2\\
p_{N-1}(\mathbf{x})=S_{N-1}(\mathbf{x})&\\
p_{N}(\mathbf{x})=S_{N}(\mathbf{x})&\end{array}\right.\,.

(2.9)

For example, when
N=2
, we have
p_{0}(\mathbf{x})=S_{0}(\mathbf{x})-S_{1}(\mathbf{x})
,
p_{1}(\mathbf{x})=S_{1}(\mathbf{x})
, and
p_{2}(\mathbf{x})=S_{2}(\mathbf{x})
.

2.3
Reinitialization of the multilayer level-set function

In a typical level-set method, reinitialization aims to maintain the signed-distance property of the level-set function near its 0-level set, which represents the interface structure. Since the multilayer level-set function
\phi(\mathbf{x})
represents a series of interfaces
\partial\Omega_{n}
using its
i_{n}
-level-sets,
\forall n=0,1,\cdots,N-1
, the reinitialization is expected to maintain its signed-distance property around all these interfaces.

Firstly, solve the following reinitialization equation,
\forall n=0,1,\cdots,N-1
,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\frac{\partial\psi_{n}}{\partial\xi}+\mathrm{sign}(\phi-i_{n})\,\left(|\nabla\psi_{n}|-1\right)=0\\
\psi_{n}(\mathbf{x},\xi=0)=\phi-i_{n}\end{array}\right.\,.

(2.10)

\psi_{n}(\mathbf{x}):=\psi_{n}(\mathbf{x},\xi=\infty)
will be a signed-distance function to the
i_{n}
-level-set of
\phi(\mathbf{x})
. In practice, because the reinitialization is repeatedly performed during the iterations of
\phi
, one only needs to evolve the equation for several
\Delta\xi
steps, e.g. 5 steps.

Then, for every grid point
\mathbf{x}
, find

n_{\mathbf{x}}=\mathrm{argmin}_{n}|\psi_{n}(\mathbf{x})|\,.

(2.11)

And the multilayer level-set function is reinitialized as

\phi(\mathbf{x})=i_{n_{\mathbf{x}}}+\min\left(\max\left(\psi_{n_{\mathbf{x}}}(\mathbf{x}),-\frac{\Delta i}{2}\right),\frac{\Delta i}{2}\right)\,.

(2.12)

Equations (
2.11
) and (
2.12
) exactly follow the definition of
\phi(\mathbf{x})
in equation (
2.5
).

For example, when
N=2
, we have that

\phi(\mathbf{x})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}i_{0}+\min\left(\max\left(\psi_{0}(\mathbf{x}),-\frac{\Delta i}{2}\right),\frac{\Delta i}{2}\right)\,,&|\psi_{0}(\mathbf{x})|<|\psi_{1}(\mathbf{x})|\\
i_{1}+\min\left(\max\left(\psi_{1}(\mathbf{x}),-\frac{\Delta i}{2}\right),\frac{\Delta i}{2}\right)\,,&|\psi_{0}(\mathbf{x})|\geq|\psi_{1}(\mathbf{x})|\end{array}\right.\,.

3
Eikonal-based traveltime tomography

Now we propose the multilayer level-set algorithm for eikonal-based traveltime tomography, where the multilayer level-set function is employed to represent discontinuous slowness structures with multiple phases and interfaces.

3.1
Problem setup and level-set representation

Consider the eikonal equation with a point-source boundary condition,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}|\nabla T(\mathbf{x})|=S(\mathbf{x})\,,\ \ \ \mathbf{x}\in\Omega\\
T(\mathbf{x}_{s})=0\end{array}\right.

(3.1)

where the viscosity solution
T(\mathbf{x})
represents the first-arrival traveltime,
S(\mathbf{x})
denotes the slowness parameter which is the reciprocal of wave velocity, and
\mathbf{x}_{s}
denotes the location of point source.
The traveltime tomography reads as an inverse problem: Given the traveltime data
T^{*}
on
\Gamma\subset\partial\Omega
, recover the slowness
S(\mathbf{x})
in
\Omega
.

In practice, the measurement of traveltime is performed many times with a set of point sources. Let
\{\mathbf{x}_{s,j}\}_{j=1}^{J}
denote the set of point sources, and
T_{j}(\mathbf{x})
be the corresponding traveltime,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}|\nabla T_{j}(\mathbf{x})|=S(\mathbf{x})\,,\ \ \ \mathbf{x}\in\Omega\\
T_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{s,j})=0\end{array}\right.\,,\quad j=1,\cdots,J\,.

(3.2)

Then the inverse problem of traveltime tomography reads: Given the measurement traveltime data
T_{j}^{*}
(
j=1,\cdots,J
) on
\Gamma\subset\partial\Omega
, recover the slowness
S(\mathbf{x})
in
\Omega
.

To reconstruct a discontinuous slowness structure with multiple phases and interfaces, we consider
S(\mathbf{x})
to be a piecewise continuous function as shown in equation (
2.6
), where
S_{n}(\mathbf{x})
is assumed to be continuous,
\forall n=0,\cdots,N
. The multilayer level-set formulation is employed to represent
S(\mathbf{x})
, as shown in equation (
2.7
).
Here, we have the Fréchet derivatives of
S(\mathbf{x})
in the following way,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial S}{\partial\phi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}p_{n}\,\delta(\phi-i_{n})+p_{N}\,\delta\left(\phi-i_{N-1}\right)

(3.3)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial S}{\partial p_{n}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-H(\phi-i_{n}),\qquad n=0,1,\cdots N-1

(3.4)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial S}{\partial p_{N}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H\left(\phi-i_{N-1}\right)

(3.5)

where
\delta(\cdot)
denotes the Dirac delta function arising from the derivative of Heaviside. The inverse problem of traveltime tomography reduces to recovering the level-set function
\phi(\mathbf{x})
and the parameters
p_{n}(\mathbf{x})
.

3.2
Adjoint state methods

We use an
L^{2}
-norm misfit function to evaluate the discrepancy between simulated and measurement traveltime data,

E=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\int_{\Gamma}\left|T_{j}-T_{j}^{*}\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\,,

(3.6)

where
T
depends on
S
and so the level-set function
\phi
and parameters
p_{n}
. The tomography problem is solved by considering an optimization problem,

(\phi(\mathbf{x}),p_{0}(\mathbf{x}),\cdots,p_{N}(\mathbf{x}))=\arg\min\,E\big(T\big(S((\phi(\mathbf{x}),p_{0}(\mathbf{x}),\cdots,p_{N}(\mathbf{x}))\big)\big)\,.

(3.7)

We propose the gradient descent algorithm to solve (
3.7
), and like our previous works, we use an adjoint state method to calculate the Fréchet derivative
\frac{\partial E}{\partial S}
.

Proposition 1
.

The Fréchet derivative
\frac{\partial E}{\partial S}
is given by

\frac{\partial E}{\partial S}=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\lambda_{j}\,S\,,

(3.8)

where
\lambda_{j}
is the adjoint state variable satisfying the following adjoint state equation,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot(\lambda_{j}\nabla T_{j})=0&,&\mathrm{in}\ \Omega\\
\lambda_{j}\frac{\partial T_{j}}{\partial\mathbf{n}}=T_{j}-T_{j}^{*}&,&\mathrm{on}\ \Gamma\\
\lambda_{j}=0&,&\mathrm{on}\ \partial\Omega\setminus\Gamma\end{array}\right.\,,\quad j=1,\cdots,J\,.

(3.9)

We refer readers to our previous works
[
18
,
21
]
for the derivations of (
3.8
) and (
3.9
). Moreover, we mention that the eikonal equations (
3.2
) and the adjoint state equations (
3.9
) are solved by the fast sweeping method with a Godunov upwind scheme
[
41
,
18
]
.

Combining equation (
3.8
) and equations (
3.3
)-(
3.5
), we have that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial E}{\partial\phi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(-\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}p_{n}\,\delta(\phi-i_{n})+p_{N}\,\delta\left(\phi-i_{N-1}\right)\right)\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{J}\lambda_{j}\,S

(3.10)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial E}{\partial p_{n}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(1-H(\phi-i_{n})\right)\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{J}\lambda_{j}\,S,\qquad n=0,1,\cdots N-1

(3.11)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial E}{\partial p_{N}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H\left(\phi-i_{N-1}\right)\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{J}\lambda_{j}\,S

(3.12)

Numerically, we use a smooth version of the Heaviside function,

H_{\tau}(\phi)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\tanh\frac{\phi}{\tau}+1\right)\,,

(3.13)

and the numerical Delta function is

\delta_{\tau}(\phi)=H_{\tau}^{\prime}(\phi)=\frac{1}{2\tau\cosh^{2}\frac{\phi}{\tau}}\,.

(3.14)

Then the level-set function
\phi(\mathbf{x})
and the parameters
p_{n}(\mathbf{x})
are updated according to their gradient descent directions.

3.3
Regularization

3.3.1
Reinitialization of
\phi

Firstly, the multilayer level-set reinitialization is imposed on
\phi
during its updating. As discussed in Section
2.3
, it helps to maintain the signed-distance property of
\phi
near its
i_{n}
-level-sets. This is a regularization on
\phi
making it well-behaved around the interface structure. In our multilayer level-set formulation, the reinitialization is performed according to equations (
2.10
)-(
2.12
). Ideally the reinitialization does not change the
i_{n}
-level-set of
\phi
,
\forall n=0,\cdots,N-1
, and so it will not affect the Fréchet derivatives of
E
as shown in equations (
3.10
)-(
3.12
).

3.3.2
Penalization on
\phi

A further penalization can be imposed on
\phi
to regularize the shape of interfaces. In a single level-set method, the
0
-level-set represents the interface, and its arc-length or area can be evaluated by
\int_{\Omega}\delta(\phi)|\nabla\phi|\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

[
42
]
, where
\delta(\cdot)
denotes the Dirac delta function. In the multilayer level-set method, the interfaces are represented by
i_{n}
-level-sets as shown in equation (
2.3
). As a result, we consider the following term,

E_{r}(\phi)=\int_{\Omega}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\delta(\phi-i_{n})|\nabla\phi|\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,.

(3.15)

Here,
E_{r}(\phi)
evaluates the length or area of all the interfaces represented by the multilayer level-set function. Penalizing
E_{r}(\phi)
has the effect of shrinking the interfaces, and thus avoiding forming sharp corners in the shape of interfaces. We add the penalization term
E_{r}(\phi)
to the data misfit function
E
, and form the total-energy functional,

E_{\mathrm{total}}=E+\gamma_{\phi}\,E_{r}(\phi)\,,

(3.16)

where
E
is defined in equation (
3.6
), and
\gamma_{\phi}>0
is a parameter that controls the amount of penalization applied. Using the calculus of variations, the Fréchet derivative of
E_{r}(\phi)
is,

\frac{\partial E_{r}}{\partial\phi}=-\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\delta(\phi-i_{n})\,\nabla\cdot\left(\frac{\nabla\phi}{|\nabla\phi|}\right)\,.

(3.17)

The Dirac delta function can be approximated as
[
42
]

\delta(\phi-i_{n})\doteq\chi(T_{\hat{\tau},i_{n}})|\nabla\phi|\,,

(3.18)

where
\chi(\cdot)
is the indicator function,

\chi(D)(\mathbf{x})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcr}1&,&\mathbf{x}\in D\\
0&,&\mathbf{x}\notin D\end{array}\right.\,,

(3.19)

and
T_{\hat{\tau},i_{n}}
denotes a small tube containing the
i_{n}
-level-set,

T_{\hat{\tau},i_{n}}=\{\mathbf{x}:\ |\phi(\mathbf{x})-i_{n}|<\hat{\tau}\}\,.

(3.20)

Substituting (
3.18
) into (
3.17
), we have that

\frac{\partial E_{r}}{\partial\phi}\doteq-\chi(T_{\hat{\tau}})\,|\nabla\phi|\,\nabla\cdot\left(\frac{\nabla\phi}{|\nabla\phi|}\right)\,,

(3.21)

where
T_{\hat{\tau}}
denotes the union of all the tubes containing the
i_{n}
-level-sets,

T_{\hat{\tau}}=\bigcup_{n=0}^{N-1}\{\mathbf{x}:\ |\phi(\mathbf{x})-i_{n}|<\hat{\tau}\}\,.

(3.22)

A drawback of formula (
3.21
) is that it consists of a complicated curvature term
\nabla\cdot\left(\frac{\nabla\phi}{|\nabla\phi|}\right)
. Since the multilayer level-set reinitialization is performed on
\phi
, one can expect that
|\nabla\phi|\doteq 1
near the
i_{n}
-level-sets. Then formula (
3.21
) is simplified to be

\frac{\partial E_{r}}{\partial\phi}\doteq-\chi(T_{\hat{\tau}})\,\Delta\phi\,,

(3.23)

And

\frac{\partial E_{\mathrm{total}}}{\partial\phi}=\frac{\partial E}{\partial\phi}-\gamma_{\phi}\cdot\chi(T_{\hat{\tau}})\,\Delta\phi\,.

(3.24)

The added penalization effectively introduces a viscosity term near the
i_{n}
-level-sets.

We mention that one should be very careful when using this penalization. Firstly, according to equation (
2.5
),
\phi
can be discontinuous away from its
i_{n}
-level-sets, where computing
\Delta\phi
may introduce blowup. Therefore, the tube width
\hat{\tau}
should be taken small; for example, we take
\hat{\tau}=3\tau
in computations, where
\tau
is the parameter of our numerical Heaviside function as shown in equation (
3.13
). Secondly, one should keep in mind that the penalization has the effect of shrinking the interfaces. To prevent over-smoothing of the interface structure, this penalization is used only when necessary.

3.3.3
Regularization of
p_{n}

To update
p_{n}
smoothly,
\forall n=0,\cdots,N
, we consider a Sobolev regularization on the perturbations of
p_{n}
. Denoting
\tilde{p}_{n}=-\frac{\partial E}{\partial p_{n}}
, we solve the following equation to regularize
\tilde{p}_{n}
,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle(I-\gamma\Delta)\tilde{P}_{n}(\mathbf{x})=\tilde{p}_{n}(\mathbf{x})\,,\ \ \mathbf{x}\in\Omega\\
\displaystyle\frac{\partial\tilde{P}_{n}}{\partial\mathbf{n}}\mid_{\partial\Omega}=0\end{array}\right.\,,

(3.25)

where
\mathbf{n}
denotes the outer normal direction of
\partial\Omega
, and
\gamma>0
is a parameter controlling the amount of regularization. Then
\tilde{P}_{n}
is used to replace
\tilde{p}_{n}
in the updating of
p_{n}
.
To illustrate the effect of (
3.25
), we include the following proposition, which corrects some minor issues of Theorem 3.2 in
[
23
]
.

Proposition 2
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded domain with boundary of class
C^{1,1}
(or alternatively, a bounded convex domain), and
\tilde{p}_{n}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. The functional

J(\tilde{P})=\|\tilde{P}-\tilde{p}_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\gamma\|\nabla\tilde{P}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)},\ \ (\gamma>0)

has a unique minimizer in
H^{1}(\Omega)
; this minimizer, denoted
\tilde{P}_{n}
, satisfies equation (
3.25
).

Proof.

(1)
J(\tilde{P})
has a unique minimizer in
H^{1}(\Omega)
.

Since
J(\tilde{P})
is convex and Gâteaux differentiable, in
H^{1}(\Omega)
the minimizer of
J
is equivalent to the solution of its Euler-Lagrange equation:

\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\epsilon}J(\tilde{P}+\epsilon v)\right|_{\epsilon=0}=0\ \iff\ \int_{\Omega}\left(\tilde{P}-\tilde{p}_{n}\right)v\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}+\gamma\int_{\Omega}\nabla\tilde{P}\cdot\nabla v\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=0\,,\ \forall v\in H^{1}(\Omega)\,.

Define the bilinear form
a:H^{1}(\Omega)\times H^{1}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}
, and the linear functional
L:H^{1}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}
as follows,

a(u,v)=\int_{\Omega}uv\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}+\gamma\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,,\quad L(v)=\int_{\Omega}\tilde{p}_{n}v\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,.

It is direct to verify that
a(u,v)
is bounded and coercive, and
L(v)
is bounded; in fact,
a(u,v)\leq(1+\gamma)\|u\|_{H^{1}}\|v\|_{H^{1}}
,
a(u,u)\geq\min\{1,\gamma\}\|u\|_{H^{1}}^{2}
, and
L(v)\leq\|\tilde{p}_{n}\|_{L^{2}}\|v\|_{H^{1}}
. Then by the Lax-Milgram theorem,

\exists\mid u\in H^{1}(\Omega)\,,\quad\mathrm{such\ that}\ \ a(u,v)=L(v)\,,\ \forall v\in H^{1}(\Omega)\,.

(3.26)

This
u
is the unique minimizer of
J
in
H^{1}(\Omega)
, denoted
\tilde{P}_{n}
.

(2) The minimizer
\tilde{P}_{n}\in H^{2}(\Omega)
, and satisfies equation (
3.25
).

By (
3.26
),
\tilde{P}_{n}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
satisfies

\int_{\Omega}\tilde{P}_{n}v\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}+\gamma\int_{\Omega}\nabla\tilde{P}_{n}\cdot\nabla v\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=\int_{\Omega}\tilde{p}_{n}v\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,,\ \forall v\in H^{1}(\Omega)\,;

(3.27)

it is a weak solution of the elliptic equation (
3.25
). Since
\Omega
is a bounded domain with boundary of class
C^{1,1}
(or a bounded convex domain), elliptic regularity theories, e.g. Theorem 2.2.2.5 and Theorem 3.2.1.3 in
[
12
]
, imply that
\tilde{P}_{n}\in H^{2}(\Omega)
. Then integration by parts leads to equation (
3.25
).
∎

Moreover, we mention that
\tilde{P}_{n}
is a gradient descent direction of
p_{n}
.

\displaystyle E(p_{n}+\epsilon\tilde{P}_{n})-E(p_{n})

\displaystyle\doteq

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial E}{\partial p_{n}}\,\epsilon\tilde{P}_{n}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=-\epsilon\int_{\Omega}\tilde{p}_{n}\tilde{P}_{n}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=-\epsilon\int_{\Omega}(I-\gamma\Delta)\tilde{P}_{n}\cdot\tilde{P}_{n}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

(3.28)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\epsilon\int_{\Omega}\left|\tilde{P}_{n}\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}+\epsilon\gamma\left(\int_{\Omega}-\left|\nabla\tilde{P}_{n}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}+\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{\partial\tilde{P}_{n}}{\partial\mathbf{n}}\tilde{P}_{n}\,\mathrm{d}s\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\epsilon\int_{\Omega}\left(\left|\tilde{P}_{n}\right|^{2}+\gamma\left|\nabla\tilde{P}_{n}\right|^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\leq 0\,.

Now, we summarize the inversion algorithm as follows.

Algorithm 1. Multilayer level-set algorithm for eikonal-based traveltime tomography.

1:

Prescribe the number
N
and the arithmetic sequence
\{i_{n}\}_{n=0}^{N-1}
.

2:

Initialize the multilayer level-set function
\phi
and the parameters
p_{n}
,
n=0,\cdots,N
.

3:

Construct the slowness function
S(\mathbf{x})
according to the multilayer level-set formulation (
2.7
).

4:

Compute the simulated traveltime
T_{j}
,
j=1,\cdots,J
, by solving the eikonal equation (
3.2
), and evaluate
E
according to (
3.6
).

5:

Solve the adjoint state variable
\lambda_{j}
from equation (
3.9
),
j=1,\cdots,J
.

6:

Evaluate the Fréchet derivatives according to equations (
3.10
)-(
3.12
) and equation (
3.23
); set
\tilde{\phi}=-\partial E/\partial\phi+\gamma_{\phi}\cdot\chi(T_{\hat{\tau}})\,\Delta\phi
and
\tilde{p}_{n}=-\partial E/\partial\tilde{p}_{n}
,
n=0,\cdots,N
.

7:

Regularize
\tilde{p}_{n}
according to equation (
3.25
), use
\tilde{P}_{n}
to replace
\tilde{p}_{n}
.

8:

Update the model parameters:
\phi^{\mathrm{new}}=\phi+\epsilon\tilde{\phi}
,
p_{n}^{\mathrm{new}}=p_{n}+\epsilon\tilde{P}_{n}
,
n=0,\cdots,N
.

9:

Perform the multilayer level-set reinitialization for
\phi
according to equations (
2.10
)-(
2.12
).

10:

Go back to step 3 until the stopping criterion is achieved:
E<\epsilon_{stop}
, or the number of iterations exceeds the specified value.

3.4
An error measure considering illumination

We propose a novel measure to evaluate the performance of the inversion algorithm for first-arrival traveltime tomography. A direct index is the error between recovered slowness
S(\mathbf{x})
and its true value
S_{\mathrm{true}}(\mathbf{x})
, e.g.
|S-S_{\mathrm{true}}|
. (In test examples, the true values are known.)
However, in first-arrival traveltime tomography, the rays passing through the detection area are not uniformly distributed. For instance, rays bend away from regions with large slowness, and information from such low-illumination regions is poorly detected by boundary measurements; when evaluating the performance of inversion algorithm, one should allow larger mistakes in the low-illumination regions, which is the nature of the first-arrival traveltime tomography.

Similar to what we did in
[
21
]
, we propose a labeling function,
F(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{x}_{s})
, to indicate the illumination of the location
\mathbf{x}\in\Omega
by rays emanating from the point source
\mathbf{x}_{s}
. Specifically, when a first-arrival ray emitted from
\mathbf{x}_{s}
is received by a boundary measurement at
\Gamma\subset\partial\Omega
, we set
F(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{x}_{s})\equiv 1
along the ray; denoting
\{\mathbf{x}:\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}(\xi)\}
the arc-length parametrization of the ray, it holds that

\frac{\mathrm{d}F\left(\mathbf{x}(\xi);\mathbf{x}_{s}\right)}{\mathrm{d}\xi}=0\,,

(3.29)

where
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\xi}
denotes the total derivative along
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}(\xi)
. Equation (
3.29
) implies that,

\nabla F(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{x}_{s})\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}(\xi)}{\mathrm{d}\xi}=0\,.

(3.30)

Considering the characteristics of the eikonal equation, we have the ray direction
\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}(\xi)}{\mathrm{d}\xi}
as follows,

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}(\xi)}{\mathrm{d}\xi}=\frac{\nabla T(\mathbf{x})}{S(\mathbf{x})}\,,

(3.31)

where
T(\mathbf{x})=T(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{x}_{s})
is the first-arrival traveltime with the point source
\mathbf{x}_{s}
.
Substituting (
3.31
) into (
3.30
), the labeling function
F(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{x}_{s})
satisfies the following equation,

\nabla F(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{x}_{s})\cdot\nabla T(\mathbf{x})=0\,.

(3.32)

In practice,
F(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{x}_{s})
is computed by propagating its value at the measurement boundary
\Gamma\subset\partial\Omega
back into the domain
\Omega
,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}-\nabla F(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{x}_{s})\cdot\nabla T(\mathbf{x})=0&,&\mathbf{x}\in\Omega\\
F(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{x}_{s})=1&,&\mathbf{x}\in\Gamma\\
F(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{x}_{s})=0&,&\mathbf{x}\in\partial\Omega\setminus\Gamma\end{array}\right.\,.

(3.33)

The advection equation (
3.33
) is solved by a fast sweeping method with the Godnov upwind scheme, where
F(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{x}_{s})
is initialized to be
0
in
\Omega
.

For multiple point sources
\{\mathbf{x}_{s,j}\}_{j=1}^{J}
, denoting
T_{j}(\mathbf{x}):=T(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{x}_{s,j})
the first-arrival traveltime corresponding to the point source
\mathbf{x}_{s,j}
, we introduce a set of labeling functions,
F_{j}(\mathbf{x}):=F(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{x}_{s,j})
,
j=1,\cdots,J
, according to the advection equation (
3.33
), i.e.,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}-\nabla F_{j}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\nabla T_{j}(\mathbf{x})=0&,&\mathbf{x}\in\Omega\\
F_{j}(\mathbf{x})=1&,&\mathbf{x}\in\Gamma\\
F_{j}(\mathbf{x})=0&,&\mathbf{x}\in\partial\Omega\setminus\Gamma\end{array}\right.\,.

(3.34)

Then the total illumination function is defined as follows,

F(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}F_{j}(\mathbf{x})\,.

(3.35)

In (
3.35
), we compute the total illumination at
\mathbf{x}
by summing contributions from all point sources, and we normalize it to make
F(\mathbf{x})\in[0,1]
; an
F(\mathbf{x})
close to
1
means the location
\mathbf{x}
is of high illumination.

In test examples where the true model
S_{\mathrm{true}}
is known, the total illumination
F(\mathbf{x})
should be computed using the first-arrival traveltime
T(\mathbf{x})
calculated from
S_{\mathrm{true}}
.
Lastly, we define the error considering illumination,

e_{F}(\mathbf{x}):=F(\mathbf{x})\,\big|S(\mathbf{x})-S_{\mathrm{true}}(\mathbf{x})\big|\,,

(3.36)

where
F(\mathbf{x})
is the total illumination function,
S(\mathbf{x})
is the recovered slowness, and
S_{\mathrm{true}}(\mathbf{x})
denotes its true value. To summarize,
e_{F}(\mathbf{x})
tolerates large mistakes
|S(\mathbf{x})-S_{\mathrm{true}}(\mathbf{x})|
in regions with low illumination.

5
Conclusions

We have developed a multilayer level-set method (MLSM) for eikonal-based first-arrival traveltime tomography. In contrast to conventional level-set methods, which use only the zero-level set for interface representation, the MLSM defines the interfaces of multiple phases using a series of
i_{n}
-level-sets. Near each
i_{n}
-level-set, the multilayer level-set function is designed to behave as a local signed-distance function. Within this framework, a single level-set function can delineate arbitrarily many distinct interfaces and sub-regions. We have detailed the representation of piecewise structures with multiple interfaces using the MLSM, and developed a reinitialization strategy for the multiplayer level-set function. After demonstrating the performance of the MLSM through various representative examples of moving interfaces, we propose it for eikonal-based first-arrival traveltime tomography. The multilayer level-set function is employed to represent discontinuous slowness structures with multiple phases and interfaces.
To solve the tomography problem, we adopt an Eulerian framework, modeling the first-arrival traveltime via the viscosity solution of the eikonal equation, and calculating the Fréchet derivatives of the data misfit by the adjoint state method. Comprehensive regularization techniques are proposed for the MLSM formulation, including reinitialization, arc-length penalization of the multilayer level-set function, and a Sobolev regularization for updating the MLSM parameters. In addition, we introduce an illumination-based error measure to evaluate the performance of the inversion algorithm. Extensive numerical examples illustrate the method’s efficacy. The results demonstrate that the MLSM is an effective and promising tool for reconstructing multiphase structures in traveltime tomography.
