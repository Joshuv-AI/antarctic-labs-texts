Evaluation of risks associated with PAHs, pesticides, and heavy metals in the red sea at Jazan harbors and nearby marine regions including halophile analysis
Authors: Hassien M Alnashiri, S Areshi, A El-Shabasy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe current study assess the degree of pollution in two harbors (Madaia and Saha) located in the southwestern Red Sea, Saudi Arabia with potential impacts on the intervening control coastline. This study deals with the integration between polluted and non-polluted areas. The general physicochemical study, PAHs determination, Pesticide residue evaluation, the most important heavy metals analysis and halophiles investigation are accomplished. 1) According to PAHs determination, 1-methyl-Naphthalene was predominant at Madaia Harbor, 2-methyl-Naphthalene at Saha Harbor, and Acenaphthene at the control coastline. 2) According to pesticide residue evaluation, twenty-one pesticide residues were analyzed, with terbufos, methyl parathion, and malathion detected at the highest concentrations across all sites. 3) According to heavy metal analysis, As, Hg and Pb record the highest concentration at Control coastline. 4) According to halophiles investigation, slight to moderate levels of halophilic bacteria were observed at both harbors, while halophilic yeasts were present at all study sites. Correlation and simple linear regression analyses indicated that pollution at the control coastline was negatively influenced by discharges from both harbors, with Saha Harbor being the primary source, although Madaia Harbor also had significant impacts.Keywords: Boat leak, Surrogate PAH specification, Integration marine pollution, Prevalent heavy metals, RCSP, Fungal countsSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Ocean sciences

Introduction

The marine environment of the Red Sea, particularly along the southwestern Saudi Arabian coastline, has been heavily impacted due to its unique geographic and environmental characteristics. The Red Sea serves as a major navigation corridor between the East and West, while its semi-enclosed nature, narrow structure, and intense human activities exacerbate its ecological vulnerability1. It connects maritime trade routes between the Atlantic and Indian Oceans, making it a vital route for shipping, international trade, and naval operations on a global scale2.

One of the most important pollutants, hydrocarbons, can linger for years and have detrimental impacts on coastal ecosystems, as well as on the ecosystem and marine species. Furthermore, because different species exhibit varying degrees of sensitivity to the same congener or compounds within the same family, the presence of PAHs in the environment can have unanticipated ecological effects3. Among the major pollutants, hydrocarbons are of particular concern due to their persistence in marine environments and their detrimental impacts on coastal ecosystems and marine species. Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), in particular, are considered the most hazardous due to their carcinogenic, mutagenic, and bioaccumulate properties4. There are several ways that PAHs can find their way into the marine environment, such as through direct spills of petroleum or petroleum derivatives, residential and industrial emissions, river runoff, and atmospheric deposition5. Usually found in marine sediments at very low concentrations (0.01–1 ng/g dry weight), PAHs are produced naturally as byproducts of biosynthesis or enter the environment due to natural upwelling6. Combustion method is the best choice where heat process breaks down organic molecules completely to produce carbon dioxide and water. Nevertheless, many minor organic molecules that recombine to form new substances are produced by incomplete combustion7.

Another significant source of marine contamination is pesticide residues, which are widely dispersed across seas and oceans. Pesticide pollution is closely linked to overuse and improper application. Direct and aerial spraying, agricultural runoff, and soil erosion contribute to contamination of aquatic habitats. Uncontrolled agricultural practices, especially involving pesticides and mineral fertilizers, have become major sources of pollution in rivers, groundwater, and marine systems8. Consequently, they can be carried away from their application sites and deposit in regions far away from their intended source regions and therefore act as transboundary pollutants via spray drift, volatilization, or wind erosion9. The classical analytical approache (LC or GCMS) is used to measure actually the pesticide molecule instead of metolachlor method that fail in determine active groups10.

The third contribution in marine pollution is accumulation of heavy metals. They have great biological toxicity, widespread distribution, and persistent accumulating properties make them the main cause of pollution in the marine environment. Heavy metals have been continuously circulating in the marine environment through river runoff and atmospheric deposition as a result of the fast industrial expansion and urbanization in coastal areas11. The primary sources of heavy metals in water and sediments as a result of industrial urbanization and human activity along coastlines are waste discharges and untreated sewage12. For Saudi Arabian marine water (Red Sea/Arabian Gulf), the standard limits for the three major toxic heavy metals; Arsenic (As), Mercury (Hg), and Lead (Pb) are typically set with international guidelines based on the particular study and regulation as being below 1 μg/l for both Hg and Pb and below 10 μg/l for As1,13. Two crucial phases of analysis are sample dissolution and measurement. The standard addition method is an analytical technique that aims to equate the sample solution matrix with the standard. It can be accomplished by several methods like AAS, spectrophotometry, XRF or ASV. Atomic Absorption Spectroscopy (AAS) is generally superior to conventional molecular analysis for heavy metals due to the highest sensitivity, specificity, and lowest detection limits especially trace metals like heavy metals14.

Halophiles are microorganisms that can multiply and flourish in marine environments. They are able to withstand the denaturing effects of salt and balance the osmotic pressure of their surroundings, allowing them to exist in saline settings. These microbes may be vulnerable to or resistant to adverse conditions despite these tough environments. Fungi and bacteria that are halophilic are thought to be natural markers that can be affected by any level of pollution15.

No research has been published that focused on different categories of toxicological marine environmental chemical ingredients besides environmental microbiology to estimate the degree of pollution. Moreover, the pollution impacts are not comprehended on other unpolluted areas.

Hence, this study aims to evaluate the integration of pollution from two harbors in the southwestern Red Sea, Saudi Arabia, on the adjacent control coastline. Specifically, it investigates the extent and effects of hydrocarbon, pesticide, and heavy metal contamination, as well as halophile dynamics, on the physicochemical and biological characteristics of the marine environment.

Materials and methods

The studying area

The sites that have the highest concentration of people in addition to machinery are chosen based on economic goals. The most well-known location in the Jazan region of Saudi Arabia is Madaia Harbor, which is home to numerous fishing boats, pontoon boats, bass boats, and deck boats. In a similar vein, Saha Harbor is home to numerous fishermen who use traditional fishing techniques in addition to a variety of boats with medium- to long-term constructed lifespans. There are a number of modest tourist resorts and a bathing area in the space between two harbors.

The harbors of Madaia and Saha, which are located at 16.7658138°N 42.7418148°E and 16.501542°N 42.755227°E, respectively, are thought to be the most likely to be polluted due to human activity, boat waste, and leaks. Conversely, the 37.2-km-long control area between the former harbors is the ideal location for tourism (Fig. 1).

During March 2024, disposable high-density polyethylene (HDPE), polytetrafluoroethylene (Teflon), or polycarbonate bottles with a capacity of 500 ml are used to collect 10 replica of water samples from the seashore at a distance of 50 m and inside sea water that is 1 m deep at each studied region. Until further analysis, the samples are stored at a temperature between 4 and 10 °C.

They emphasize the use of technology, trained collectors, standard equipment, disposable instruments, strategy plans, avoiding issues and dangers, protecting the environment, and handling unforeseen circumstances16.

The goal of this study is to pave the enthusiastic results and interpretative discussion to comprehend the impact of pollution from both harbors on the coastline sea shore between them through chemical, physical and biological analysis.

Physicochemical sea water analysis

At the locations of collection, some physical and chemical analyses of seawater samples are performed right away. Digital thermometers, digital S400-Temu pH/mV benchtop meters, and digital Detachable Probe Dissolved Oxygen Sensors are used to measure the temperature, pH, and dissolved oxygen, respectively. The LinJie Water Tester meter is used to dilute the TDS (Total Dissolved Solids) and EC (Electric Conductivity) values to a 1: 1000 ratio.

PAH’s detection method

At the Environmental and Food Pollutant Analysis Laboratory at Fayoum University in Fayoum, Egypt, PAHs were extracted, cleaned, and determined by GC–MS.

Before approaching any PAHs sample preparation methods, it is important to highlight several precautions that must always be applied. Since polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons are ubiquitous, all the containers should be rinsed with high purity acetone or hexane before use. To avoid cross-contamination, the samples analyzed have to come in contact only with inert materials, such as aluminum, glass or stainless steel, plastic materials must be completely avoided, especially polyethylene, since analytes adsorption may occur. Since polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons are light sensitive, samples and extracts have to be protected from light by using amber or foil-wrapped vials. Finally, to avoid loss of the lighter compounds during drying, it is a good practice to avoid drastic evaporation processes and to leave the last drop to evaporate naturally.

QA/QC procedures for Polycyclic Aromatic Hydrocarbon (PAH) analysis using Internal Standards (IS) ensure accuracy by monitoring extraction efficiency, instrumental performance, and contamination. All details of surrogate and internal mixture compounds of PAHs are listed at Appendix.

16 different PAH standards with 10 mg/liter of each of the following: Naphthalene, anthracene, benzo, acenaphthene, and acenaphthyelene Indeno[1,2,3-cd], chrysene, benzo[a]pyrene, benzo[a]anthracene, benzo[g,h,i]perylene, benzo[k]fluoranthene, and benzo[b]fluoranthene Dibenzo and pyrene [a,h] According to the producing company’s instructions, anthracene, fluoranthene, fluorene, phenanthrene, and pyrene (dissolved in cyclohexane) were bought from Supelco (Bellefonte, PA, USA), kept in their original sealed bottle, tightly covered, and shielded from light and moisture at −20°C.

The recovery of internal standards was monitored to ensure accurate sample results. Each sample is spiked with Internal Standards before extraction to evaluate the accuracy and determine how the sample matrix affects recovery. Surrogate Recovery Rates should be ranged between 70–120% for PAHs to obtain acceptable data. Analyze solvent blank samples alongside field samples was necessary to identify any contamination from reagents, glassware or laboratory air.

The aforementioned procedural blank, internal standard and matrix spike standard were carried out for every 10 replica for each studied area to ensure the whole covering of the region without any bias and guarantee random sampling.

The QuEChERS purification technique in combination with GC–MS identification and quantification offers high instrumental performance in PAH analysis because it is fast, selective, efficient and precise method for determination of PAHs. It verifies sensitivity, separation efficiency, and mass accuracy. Key checks include ensuring daily calibration, monitoring IS response stability, and assessing chromatographic resolution.

Agilent Technologies (Palo Alto, CA, USA) will supply the QuEChERS kits needed for the extraction and purification of PAHs from samples to validate the entire analytical procedure.

A Barnstead Easypure II water purification system (Thermo Scientific Co., Waltham, MA, USA) was used to obtain deionized water, while Merck (Darmstadt, Germany) was the supplier of acetonitrile17.

Extraction of PAHs

After taking the samples out of the freezer and thawing them at 4 °C, 20 ml of water samples were placed in a centrifuge tube, 10 mL of hexane was added, and the mixture was violently agitated for one minute. After that, the mixture will be put onto a QuEChERS column that has 1.5 g of sodium acetate and 6 g of magnesium sulfate. The column will be shaken for one minute and centrifuged for five minutes at 4000 rpm18.

Clean-up by QuEChERS clean-up column

The supernatant was then separated into 6 mL aliquots, which were then put into a QuEChERS clean-up column with 400 mg of PSA, 1200 mg of magnesium sulfate, and 400 mg of end-capped octadecylsilane silica gel particles for purification. After centrifuging the eluate for five minutes at 4000 rpm, a 1µl aliquot of the supernatant was added to a gas chromatograph and mass spectrometer for the measurement of PAHs19.

Calibration curve of PAHs

In addition to 13 concentrations (0.2–120 ng/ml) of benzo[a]anthracene and chrysene, 10 concentrations (1.0–120 ng/ml) of benzo[k]fluoranthene and benzo[a]pyrene, 11 concentrations (0.5–120 ng/ml) of indeno[1,2,3-c,d]pyrene and dibenzo[a,h]anthracene, and 12 concentrations (0.3–120 ng/ml) of benzo[g,h,i]perylene, 14 concentrations (0.1–120 ng/ml) of each PAH (including naphthalene, acenaphthyelene, acenaphthene, fluorene, phenanthrene, and benzo[b]-fluoranthene. Plotting concentration against integrated peak area yielded the 16 PAH standard curves, and the amount of each PAH was determined using the corresponding calibration curve (Figs. 2, 3).

Quantitative determination of PAHs

In order to prolong the life of an Agilent HP-5MS column (30m in length, 0.25 mm in inner diameter, and 0.25 μm in film thickness), the extract (1µl) was injected into the column and attached to a 5 m guard column. With helium as the carrier gas, a flow rate of 1 ml per minute, an injector temperature of 290 °C, and spitless mode, 16 PAHs were separated in 40 min under the following temperature programming conditions: Starting at 70 °C, the temperature increased to 195 °C at 15°C/min for 2.5 min, then to 240 °C at 15°C/min for 17 min, then to 270 °C at 5°C/min, and finally to 310 °C at 15°C/min for 10 min when maintained. Retention durations and mass spectra of unknown peaks were compared to reference standards in order to identify the different PAHs present in samples20.

Pesticide residue determination

Pesticide residues were determined both qualitatively and quantitatively using a 7890B gas chromatograph connected to an Agilent 5977 A mass detector (Agilent Technologies, Wilmington, USA). An analytical column HB-5MS Cross bond (30 m, 0.25 mm internal diameter, 0.25 mm film thickness) from Phenomenex, Torrance, CA, USA, was used to accomplish the chromatographic separation. To offer superior surface inertness for the detection of trace pesticides. The splitless single taper liner with glass wool (p/n 5190–2293) from Agilent was utilized. An analytical balance (type ME 104, Mettler Toledo, Greifensee, Switzerland), a centrifuge (Sigma, Germany), a vortex mixer (VELP scientific, Usmate Velate, Italy), a laboratory blender (Waring, Stamford, USA), and an ultrasonic tabletop cleaner (type P 230, CREST Ultrasonics, Penang, Malaysia).

GC–MS for pesticide residue analysis

20 ml of water sample mixed with 10 ml of dichloromethane in a 50 ml centrifuge tube. Vigorous shaking is done for 3 min using vortex mixer. 4 g magnesium sulfate is added with 1 g sodium chloride in the centrifuge tube (QuEChERS). Shaking is performed for 1 min then centrifuging at 4000 rpm for 5 min. 6 ml dichloromethane layer is collected and poured into another centrifuge tube containing 400 mg PSA, 1200 mg magnesium sulfate and 400 mg C18EC for purification. Finally, shaking and centrifugation are done at 4000 rpm for 5 min then the supernatant is collected and injected in GC–MS for pesticide residue analysis21.

From 60 °C (hold 1 min) to 170 °C at 2 °C/min, and finally to 285 °C at 5 °C/min, the column temperature was increased. Helium with purity grade 6 was utilized as the carrier gas at a continuous flow of 1 ml/min, the inlet temperature was kept at 250 °C, 2ll was injected in splitless mode, and the purge flow was 50 ml/min for 0.75 min. At 70 eV, the electron impact (EI) ionization mode was used to operate the mass detector. The quadrupole, ion source, and transfer line were all adjusted at temperatures of 150, 250, and 285 °C, respectively. Following the acquisition of the total ion chromatogram for stock standard solutions in scan mode, which were identified by their retention time (RT) and mass spectra, mass spectra were obtained in the full-scan mode at a rate of 1.5 scans/s spanning the mass range (m/z) of 40–550. For the quantitative goal, the Selected Ion Monitoring (SIM) method was employed.

Monitoring of heavy metals by atomic absorption spectroscopy

Five milliliters of concentrated HNO3 and two milliliters of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) were introduced to the polytetrafluoroethylene digesting vessel along with three milliliters of the sample. A two-step temperature procedure was then used to digest the samples. In the first step, the revolving magnetron’s maximum power was 1000 W, and the temperature was raised linearly to 190 °C over ten minutes. The temperature was kept at 190 °C for 30 min in the second step. Following digestion and cooling, each solution was diluted with deionized water in a 50 mL volumetric flask and evaporated to around 2 mL for the AAS analysis. All digestions were carried out in triplicate, and the results were presented as the average of three repeated measurements.

Determination of heavy metals (As, Hg and Pb)

Arsenic (As), Mercury (Hg), and Lead (Pb) are considered primary toxic heavy metals in environmental and health studies due to their high degree of toxicity, extreme environmental persistence, bioaccumulation capabilities, and widespread anthropogenic sources. They cause severe multi-organ damage—affecting nervous, reproductive, and cardiovascular systems—even at low levels of exposure22.

An Agilent atomic absorption spectrometer equipped with Agilent single-element hollow cathode lamps and a 10-cm air–acetylene burner was used for the determination of the most common metal ions that reflected the degree of contamination; As, Hg and Pb. Validation of the methods was carried out by using the following certified reference materials; Institute for Reference materials and Measurements (IRMM)—European Reference Materials ERM-EC 681 Polyethylene 19 besides National Metrology Institute of Japan (NMIJ) Certified Reference Material CRM 8102a ABS resin1023.

The most prevalent metal ions, As, Hg, and Pb, which indicated the level of contamination, were identified using an Agilent atomic absorption spectrometer fitted with Agilent single-element hollow cathode lamps and a 10-cm air-acetylene burner. The following approved reference materials were used to validate the procedures: European Reference Materials Institute for Reference Materials and Measurements (IRMM) In addition to the National Metrology Institute of Japan (NMIJ) (Ritter et al. 2004).

The presented signals do not represent chromatographic peaks but reflect continuous signal acquisition over the measurement period. To ensure clarity, the absorbance-time (Abs/Time) profiles generated during mercury analysis represent continuous signal monitoring rather than chromatographic separation. Unlike chromatographic techniques, the hydride generation method produces a steady-state signal proportional to analyte concentration. Therefore, the recorded fluctuations correspond to instrumental noise and signal stability over time.

Instrument performance was evaluated based on signal consistency, low standard deviation, and absence of significant baseline drift. The mean absorbance values obtained from these time-resolved measurements were used to construct the calibration curve.

The absence of sharp peaks and the presence of stable signal plateaus further confirm that the analytical response is governed by steady-state vapor generation rather than transient chromatographic elution, which is consistent with hydride generation-based atomic absorption techniques.

Risk Characterization of a Single Pesticide (RCSP).

RCSP is the ecological risk assessment for a single pesticide target. It can be determined by dividing DT50 to MAO LD50. DT50 is the days required for 50% of a pesticide to dissipate from sediment. MAO LD50 is Mammal acute oral lethal dose by 50% mortality27.

MPC values, which indicate a substance’s possible ecological risk, are calculated using environmental and ecotoxicology physicochemistry data. It is acknowledged as a reliable scientific resource for assessing the damage that PAHs cause to the environment and ecology.

PAhs are categorized on possible, probable or non-carcinogenic chemicals in terms of two different international agencies; International Agency for Research on Cancer 1973, 1983, 2010 (IARC) and United States Environmental Protection Agency Integrated Risk Information System (IRIS) besides California Environmental Protection Agency Proposition 65 List of Chemicals Known to the State to Cause Cancer or Reproductive Toxicity The risk assessment is undertaken in the use of toxic equivalence factors (TEFs). This allows the toxicity of a certain PAH to be expressed as a single number related to the equivalent concentration of the most toxic or carcinogenic congener. Discussion

Indications of different Polycyclic Aromatic Hydrocarbon (PAH) types in seawater pinpoint to different causes about their origins like dissolved, particulate or unresolved phases besides their sources like natural internal petroleum wells, anthropogenic activities such as external petroleum transporters, vehicular emissions, industrial operations, sea sewage spillage or waste incineration52.

Crude oil and other petroleum products can be seeped into the Red Sea during ships or boat movements besides sailing of oil tankers. Human activities can play a great role by incomplete combustion of biomass that contributes in increasing PAHs levels at sea water.

PAHs and Pesticides are designed to be stable in slightly acidic water but they are hydrolyzed in sea water as acidic agents causing remarkable slightly decreasing pH values to reach 7 at close water basin as harbor. They are not the only drivers of harbor sea acidification but also heavy metals do inherently shift seawater pH especially at close one. Fractions of heavy metals are continuously transformed from different phases leading to lowering alkalinity of sea water53,54.

Although methyl-naphthalene scores high concentrations at all studied regions, its coefficient of variations (C.V.) are more noticeable with low values to confirm that they harms marine life effectively through toxicity in terms of high RQ. Furthermore, they can build up in the tissues of aquatic animals, and may have an impact on immune systems. It is dominant at Madaia and Saha harbors where it originates not only from oil deposits but also from industrial and urban runoff. It is one example of a lower molecular weight PAH that can be extremely harmful to aquatic life. It can reduce the efficiency of immune system of human beings besides accumulate in the tissues of fish and other aquatic creatures.

However, the highest concentration of Acenaphthene is found by the Control coastline. It comes from industrial effluent and wastewater treatment facilities. Once in the water, some of it dissolves, volatilizes, and disperses on the surface due to its low water solubility and propensity to adsorb to organic matter and silt. Hence, the increasing of Acenaphthene concentration is an indication on the increasing of human activities due to touristic resorts interference55. Reported that with the help of land and coastal anthropogenic activities, the quantities of dissolved PAHs in seawater are progressively diluted and distributed into the offshore and far seas.

The main ways that Terbufos enters seawater are through spills during handling or transportation, incorrect chemical disposal, and agricultural runoff from treated areas. Rainwater can carry pesticides like Terbufos into streams from the land, eventually making their way to the open ocean and coastline. When environmental experts protect mangrove aggregations from insect invasions, it has even happened in gardens and close to agricultural regions. Terbufos can dissolve in organic molecules and move through the water cycle, despite being almost insoluble in water56.

Additionally, unintentional ship spills and discharges from wastewater containing the pesticide are two ways that Methyl parathion spreads at sea. It can also reach coastal waterways by air deposition from aerial spraying and runoff from agricultural regions where it is employed. Surface runoff from agricultural fields, off-target drift during aerial spraying, and the release of contaminated wastewater from terrestrial sources are the main ways that Malathion enters seawater. Once in the sea, wind, fog, and precipitation can all carry it. Triphenyl phosphate, Diazinone, Cyprodinil, Kresoxim-methyl and Epoxiconazole are not recognized with World Health Organization (WHO) as well as Globally Harmonized System of Classification and Labelling of Chemicals (GHS) to confirm that there are some pesticide residues should be studied scientifically with great attention under environmental agencies commandments8. stated that inadequate application techniques may cause the pesticide to be distributed unevenly, resulting in higher quantities in some locations. Furthermore, weather can affect how pesticides spread and degrade, which could result in higher quantities in unfavorable circumstances.

Seawater analysis requires monitoring of the most essential heavy metals; Arsenic (As), Mercury (Hg), and Lead (Pb) that are very toxic, non-biodegradable and accumulative in marine life and then spread to people through the food chain, resulting in serious health problems such organ damage. Their existence, even in trace amounts, is a sign of pollution generated by humans, which poses a major risk to marine ecosystems and public health. Arsenic, Mercury and Lead levels must be measured for seafood safety and public health protection. Ecotoxicological data is a prime requisite that monitor the degree of persistence and long-term accumulation for such chemical residue like PAH or pesticide in the surrounding environment. It is noteworthy to mention that TPQ ecological marine risk assessment is the highest at Saha harbor, RPQ ecological marine risk assessment is high at all studied regions and Probable ecological risk index (RI) for heavy metals is the highest at control coastline. These outcomes reflect the degree of integration that occurs on the control region. The variation of either antagonistic or synergistic effects of PAHs and pesticides leads to less integration than heavy meal impacts because they are not degradable like PAHs and pesticides57,58.

Quantification is necessary to conduct ecological risk assessments and understand the potential impacts of these three heavy metals on fisheries and marine biodiversity11. observed that the spatial distribution of the main monitor heavy metals allocate the center of origin and the direction of diffusion.

The existence of moderate halophilic bacteria in seawater is indicated by certain microbial traits and environmental variables rather than by direct observations. Moderate halophiles can be linked to marine settings, especially those affected by sun salterns and evaporating saltwater, which raise the salt content. They flourish best in salt concentrations between 0.5 and 2.5 M (about 3–15% salt). The presence of a diverse microbial community, which frequently includes obligate and halotolerant halophilic bacteria, indicates that the natural marine environment has been modified on a physical and chemical basis, leading to the creation of more tolerant bacteria halophiles, even though seawater’s natural salt concentration of about 0.6 M (3.5%) falls into the slight halophile range. Because the Control coastline is located distant from the primary sources of contamination, there are fewer disturbances there, which indicate the lack of typical slight bacterial halolophiles. Presence of highest moderate total bacterial counts at Saha harbor refers that the most invading site for environmental contamination is localized at Saha harbor not Madaia harbor. The percentage of moderate bacterial halophiles increases from Saha harbor to Control coastline ending to Madaia harbor to clarify the direction of environmental pollution track. Certainly, the highest percentage of bacterial biodiversity ends at Madaia harbor. Moreover, presence of highest percentage of non-halophilic fungi and halophilic yeasts at Control coastline and Saha harbor respectively confirms the previous interpretations.

Aforementioned aftermaths show that the integration between two harbors appears at Control coastline where it presents between them. Wind directions play a great role in PAHs distribution in assistance of closely the Red Sea and presence of many petroleum wells near to Yemen. It causes the increment of total PAHs concentrations at Control coastline4. revealed that the same distribution of hydrocarbon concentrations for the sea water samples from different polluted areas. This analogy attains the effect of winds and other environmental strains. After assessing the Red Sea state characteristics near southwestern Saudi Arabia59, looked into the seasonal and monthly variations in waves in the basin caused by wind jets blowing through mountain gaps along the basin beaches as well as the monsoonal winds. According to the mean and extreme climatologies, the basin’s waves are at their peak during the December–March period, with the southernmost region seeing the most active circumstances. Orographic features and sea-land borders affect the circulation patterns of the atmospheric conditions over the RS and, consequently, the wave climatic behaviors.

Diversity and high concentration of studied pesticides at all sampling sites reflect invading another terrestrial parameter besides sea water ones. The highest moderate ecological risk shifts to Control coastline for Hg heavy metal detection from both harbors to indicate that the cumulative integration is occurred at high levels. No integration appears for moderate bacterial halophiles distribution at Control coastline refers to different affinity for biological effects than physiochemical ones. Knowing the origin and the most pollution source is determined biologically not physicochemically by studying halophiles. The homogeneity of general physicochemical analysis obeys the importance of biological parameters as the main indicator for environmental pollution. Eventually, Correlation and Simple Linear Regression (SLR) equations settled the situation to evaluate the degree of integration between two harbors on Control coastline by expressing homogeneity non-scattered plot graphs for Madaia vs Control and Saha vs Control but scattered points are observed at Madaia vs Saha. Finally Pearson’s correlation coefficient analysis records the highest values between Madaia vs Control to state that Nonetheless, the direction of environmental pollution comes from Saha harbor however the impact effects are great obvious from Madaia harbor on Control coastline.

Conclusion

This investigation gives a brilliant spot on the role of integration at environmental sciences. Certainly, most of environmental studies rely on specific impacts on specific areas no other adjacent areas where can be influenced directly or indirectly. Integration can predict the origin, the effects and the direction of environmental impacts. Kinetic mass transfer coefficients and thermodynamic distribution coefficients play vital role in enhancing the idea of integration. Heavy metals have different integrated impacts than any other chemicals like PAHs or pesticides because they have different characteristic isomeric structures than elementary nature of heavy metals. At this study, the physicochemical and biological analysis at two harbors denote the negatively results on adjacent control coastline with different levels. The study recommends that when environmental analysis is performed at any impact area, scientists should take into consideration other safe ecological habitats in their studies.

The current study confirms on the concept of integration from polluted areas to non-polluted ones. This concept will open the field to researchers to show more studies on this concept to interpret not only local impacts from a region to adjacent one but also global impacts from country to country or from continent to continent like air pollution from developed countries causes flooding or drought at other developing countries. This study is a step toward understanding the continuous fluctuations of global climate changes.
