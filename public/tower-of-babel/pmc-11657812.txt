Development and validation of attitudes toward inclusive education scale among Chinese normal universities students’ attitudes
Authors: Jin-liang Ding, You-juan Hong
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
PurposeInclusive education plays a crucial role in integrating students with special educational needs (SEN) into mainstream schools, where the attitudes of pre-service teachers are pivotal. Despite recognizing the significance of these attitudes, there is currently a gap in understanding the perspectives of students attending normal universities in China regarding inclusive education practices. This study aims to bridge this gap by creating the Chinese version of the Normal Universities Students’ Attitudes Toward Inclusive Education Scale (NUSATIES) and examining its psychometric properties.MethodsIn the context of China, this study validated and assessed the reliability of the Chinese adaptation of the NUSATIES among a sample of 4,091 students from normal universities. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was employed to confirm the scale’s structural integrity, discriminant validity, and convergent validity. Additionally, Cronbach’s alpha and composite reliability (CR) were used to evaluate the scale’s internal consistency and reliability.ResultsThe findings indicated that the Chinese version of the NUSATIES possessed robust validity and reliability, which comprises three factors (i.e., cognitive, affective, and behavioral intention). The findings revealed that normal university students’ attitudes significantly varied based on specialty; students specializing in special education exhibited more positive attitudes towards inclusive education than those in general education.ConclusionThese findings indicate that the scale is effective in measuring attitudes toward inclusive education in Chinese-speaking countries. This study significantly contributes to the field by emphasizing the importance of positive attitudes among normal university students in fostering inclusive education and by offering a validated tool for assessing these attitudes.

Introduction

Over the past 30 years, a growing number of countries have emphasized inclusive education in both policy and practice [1], coupled with a significant increase in the number of children identified with complex needs in recent years [2]. Inclusive education involves the practice of integrating students with special educational needs (SEN) into mainstream classrooms or schools. It ensures these students have equal access to educational resources and services as their peers without SEN, achieved through restructuring of mainstream educational settings. It underscores the integration of all children and youth, irrespective of individual characteristics such as race, ethnicity, disability, gender, sexual orientation, language, or socioeconomic background [3].

Inclusive education is a non-discriminatory form of quality education for all, signifying not only respect for students’ human rights and equal educational opportunities but also respect for their diversity and varied needs [4]. Existing research indicates that inclusive education facilitates the realization of educational equity and diversity, as well as improvement in the academic and social competencies of students with special needs.

One of the main barriers to the successful practice of inclusive education is the teachers’ attitudes towards inclusion and its principles. Specifically, studies have shown that when teachers possess highly positive attitudes towards inclusive education, they more readily adapt and transform their teaching methods to meet the learning needs of all students [4]. Conversely, educators with apprehensive or negative attitudes may employ practices that foster exclusion rather than fostering inclusion in their classrooms [5]. The attitudes of pre-service teachers are particularly pivotal for the effectiveness of inclusive programs designed for students with SEN [6–9]. As Avramidis and Norwich suggest, new teachers need to master the skills and knowledge required in an inclusive classroom [9]. More importantly, the formation of positive attitudes towards inclusive education contributes to ensuring an inclusive future in their classrooms. The WHO World Report on Disability emphasizes the critical role of adequately training mainstream teachers to ensure they are confident and competent in meeting the diverse educational needs of children. The report underscores that teacher training programs should integrate principles of inclusion, focusing on attitudes and values in addition to knowledge and skills [8].

In China, the Second Phase of the Special Education Promotion Plan (2017–2020) suggests developing the skills, knowledge, and positive attitudes toward inclusive education among normal college students, aiming to comprehensively improve the quality of inclusive education. Further highlighting the need for positive attitudes, Murphy states that if pre-service teachers graduate with negative attitudes, these tend to become difficult to change [10]. This means pre-service training may be the optimal time to modify these passive attitudes about inclusive education [11]. Consequently, it’s no surprise that most teacher training institutions are now required to cultivate graduates who can respond to different student populations and needs in their mainstream courses [12].

Through an analysis of the literature on attitudes toward inclusive education (ATIE), it is evident that the scales used to measure it were often simplistic and flawed. Traditionally, attitudes have been divided into three main factors: cognitive, affective, and behavioral intention [13]. Specifically, the ATIE scale should include individual psychological structures of attitude in addition to inclusion-related measurements [14]. The scale of teachers’ ATIE was mainly based on the concept of inclusion as a value but seldom considered viewpoints from the three critical dimensions of attitude. For example, Saloviita and Timo divided the ATIE scale into four dimensions: expected outcomes, rights of the child, workload of the teacher, and inclusion as a value [15]. The Inclusive Educational Practices Scale by Moberg mainly contained items about facts, but not values [16].

The Multidimensional Attitudes toward Inclusive Education Scale (MATIES) developed by Mahat (2008), the Sentiments, Attitudes, and Concerns about Inclusive Education Revised Scale (SACIE-R) developed by Forlin et al. (2011), and the the Attitudes Towards Inclusive Education Scale (AIES, 2024) developed by Sakız et al.(2024) were the questionnaires with adequate psychometric properties that addressed the affective, cognitive, and behavioral components of teachers’ attitudes [14, 17, 18]. However, the low response rate and acceptability of the MATIES, the unclear acceptability of the SACIE-R, and their focus on disability rather than SEN may restrict the utility of these questionnaires [18]. Though the AIES demonstrated robust psychometric properties, indicating its reliability and validity, it mainly focus on the three key aspects of IE: school culture, educational policy, and school practices.

Secondly, some studies overlook the psychometric properties of their measurements, and complete statistics are not reported verbatim. For example, research began to question the representativeness of the sample of the My Thinking about Inclusion scale (MTAI) [19], which was developed on populations containing both parents and teachers and, hence, did not purely represent the perspective of the latter. Similarly, no reliability statistics and only weak to moderate construct validity were reported for the Teacher Questionnaire [20]. Conversely, the Impact of Inclusion Questionnaire was reported to show good internal consistency [21], but unfortunately, no tests of validity were reported. There are similar conditions for other instruments developed by Sharma and Desai [22], as well as those by McLeskey et al. [23].

The lack of reliability statistics and the limited construct validity of this questionnaire present challenges in endorsing its use for evaluating teachers’ attitudes toward inclusive education [24]. Thirdly, some questionnaires use outdated terminology. For instance, the Opinions Relative to Integration of Students with Disabilities (ORI) uses the term “integration” rather than “inclusion” [25]. Similarly, the PATIE [26], SACIE-R [17], and MATIES [14], along with the ORI and CIES, predominantly emphasize “disability” over SEN. This emphasis could restrict the applicability of these questionnaires for assessing educators’ attitudes toward the inclusion of children facing behavioral, emotional, or learning challenges, rather than solely focusing on physical disabilities. Lastly, due to previous dramatic changes in special education concepts, the scales measuring attitudes towards inclusion that were created in the 80s, 90s, and early 2000s, such as the Opinions Relative to Mainstreaming Scale [23], the Interaction with People with Disabilities Scale [25], and the Attitudes toward Mainstreaming Scale, would now be deemed obsolete [27, 28].

In conclusion, careful consideration is essential when choosing from the aforementioned questionnaires. Moreover, most of these instruments were originally used in English-speaking nations, making it challenging to ascertain their applicability in diverse linguistic and cultural settings [24]. A suitable assessment scale tailored to the context in China must be formulated to effectively gauge teachers’ attitudes towards inclusion. This scale should reflect current perspectives, policies, and practices specific to China, acknowledging that cultural factors can significantly influence attitudes on such matters [29]. A psychometrically-sound Chinese Normal College Students’ Attitudes Toward Inclusive Education Scale (NUSATIES) would benefit both inclusive education studies and the field, facilitating international data comparisons. Our study aims to evaluate the validity and reliability of the Chinese version of the NUSATIES scale, specifically investigating normal college students’ attitudes toward the inclusion of students with disabilities.

Method

Participants and procedure

Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) was conducted using a preliminary sample of 320 students from two normal universities from Fuzhou, a southeast coastal city in China, obtained through facilitated sampling. All participants completed pen-and-paper survey anonymously in or after class. Ultimately, 300 valid questionnaires were collected (267 females), with a mean age of 19.89 years (SD = 1.83, range 17–22). According to self-reported specialty, 260 participants (86.7%) were from general education (see Table 1).

Demographic of respondents

Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) was performed using a sample selected from 44 universities employing convenience sampling and snowball sampling. Firstly, the 31 provinces/municipalities/autonomous regions of China are divided into three regions—first-tier cities, second-tier cities, and third-tier cities and above—based on variations in natural conditions, economic resources, and political factors. Second, in each of the three regions, two first-tier cities (i.e. Peking, Guangzhou), five second-tier cities (i.e. Nanjing, Chongqing, Xiamen, Nanchang et al.), seven third-tier cities and above (i.e. Quanzhou, Hefei, Taizhou et al.) are selected. Finally, snowball sampling was used to recruit the potential study participants. Investigators from the seven cooperative institutions were initially invited, and they distributed the questionnaire to participants by an online platform named Wenjuanxing, where the study website contained a description of the study, procedures, and preamble informed consent. In total, 4,091 valid responses (670 male) from 44 universities in China participate in the study, specifically, 484 students were recruited from 8 universities in first-tier cities, 1,221 students from 14 universities in second-tier cities, and 2,386 students from 22 universities in third-tier cities and above. Participants’ ages ranged from 18 to 21 years (Mean = 19.02, SD = 1.63). Among the participants, 3,644 (89.1%) specialized in general education, while 447 (10.9%) were from special education programs. Additionally, 155 normal college students from a university were randomly selected after four weeks to assess the test-retest reliability of the scale. The average age of this subgroup was 19.09 years (SD = 0.67, range 17–21), with 56 males and 65 specializing in special education (see Table 1). In general, the sample of this study comprehensively represents the vast population of teacher candidates in the region, ensuring the validity and reliability of the statistical analysis.

The questionnaires were distributed in electronic form, and participation was anonymous and voluntary, either during or after class. We sent the participants a link to an online questionnaire, which included the items and consent form. After completing the questionnaires, all participants had an opportunity to enter a lottery.

Instruments

Normal university student’s attitudes toward inclusive education scale

The primary items were selected and generated by combining a comprehensive examination of the literature and interviews on attitudes toward inclusive education. First, the literature suggests that attitudes have been divided into three camps: cognitive, affective and behavioural [13]. In considerations of attitudes towards inclusive education, the cognitive component would contain statements on the proposed positive or negative consequences of inclusion as well as its technical feasibility. The affective component would contain statements on the desirability of inclusion and on its fit with other important values. Finally, the behavioural component has to do with the readiness of the teacher to act according to the expressed cognitive and affective beliefs and views and promote inclusion in practice. In order to understand attitudes of towards inclusive education more comprehensively, an open-ended questionnaire with some questions (e.g., “Are you for or against inclusive education? Why? Would you like to accept special children into your class?” etc.) was developed by the authors on the basis of three dimensions of inclusive education, and interviewed 85 normal college students.

Subsequently, items were refined and compiled to comprehensively and accurately assess the attitudes toward inclusive education of normal college students. Experts were asked to make a choice on how relevant (or representative) each item was to the corresponding content dimension, and to suggest items that needed to be added or adjusted. A scale comprising 35 items was developed for preliminary analysis. The items included 25 from the ATIES by Saloviita et al. [15] and ten items from individual interviews.The original ATIES scale was translated into Mandarin Chinese by the two separate and bilingual researchers, who have systematic training. Then, the authors modified the translated words. A team of six university faculty (excluding the authors) working in the field of special and inclusive education and educational psychology checked and determined the better version. Furthermore, the better Chinese version of SSE was translated back into English and compared with the original scale. Modifications are made until the acceptable Chinese version is agreed. After reviewing the logic and typo errors, ten native Chinese speakers from different background were asked to complete the draft and point out any ambiguities or difficulties in understanding. Furthermore, ten items from individual interviews were evaluated by a panel of experts (2 professors of psychology, 4 professors of special education, and 4 front-line teachers of inclusive education) to ensure content validity. Additionally, four doctors of education were invited to discuss the items in an evaluative manner.

Ultimately, the initial scale comprised 25 items rated on a six-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 6 (“strongly agree”). Each score corresponded directly with the degree of agreement with NUSATIES. Cognitive aspects were measured using 12 items, with a sample item being: “Inclusive education can benefit all children, including those with disabilities and typical students”. Affective aspects were measured using five items, with a sample item being: “I feel very pleased to assist students with physical and mental disabilities in solving their learning and adaptation challenges”. Lastly, behavioral intentions were measured using eight items, with a sample item being: “I will adjust teaching strategies and evaluation methods to facilitate inclusive education”.

Teacher efficacy to implement inclusive practices scale

The Teacher Efficacy to Implement Inclusive Practices Scale consists of 18 items measuring three aspects of efficacy: inclusive instruction, collaboration, and dealing with disruptive behaviors [30]. This questionnaire uses a 5-point scoring system (1 = strongly disagree; 5 = strongly agree). Higher scores on the scale indicate greater levels of TEIP. Example items include: “I can effectively manage disruptive behavior in the classroom”. This scale has demonstrated reliable validity and reliability in China [30]. The Cronbach’s alpha for the total scale and its three dimensions ranged from 0.88 to 0.96.

Concerns about inclusive education scale

The Concerns about Inclusive Education Scale was used to assess CIES, which comprises 21 items reflecting four concerns: resources, acceptance, academic standards, and workloads [11]. Respondents rate items on a 4-point Likert scale (1 = not concerned at all, 4 = extremely concerned). Higher scores indicate a stronger level of CIES. A representative item is “It will be difficult to maintain discipline in class.” The scale demonstrates excellent validity and reliability in the Chinese population [31]. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the scale and its four factors were 0.95, 0.92, 0.83, 0.89, and 0.83, respectively.

Data analysis

All data analysis was performed using SPSS 24.0 and Amos 23.0 [32]. First, item analysis was conducted to examine the suitability and reliability of the items. The criteria for deleting items in the analysis were as follows: the correlation coefficient between the items of the scale and the total score was less than 0.40; items showed no significant difference between high and low groups (p > 0.001); the alpha coefficient of the scale remained higher than that of the total scale after deleting the item.

Then, an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) with varimax rotation was performed on a preliminary sample (n = 300) to determine the factor structure. Next, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was carried out to validate the factorial model on the formally tested samples (n = 4091). Afterward, the internal consistency and split-half reliability of the NUSATIES were determined using Cronbach’s α on the CFA samples (n = 4091), and the test-retest reliability was examined using data from 155 participants.

The Pearson correlation coefficients of NUSATIES and its dimensions with TEIP and CIES were computed to demonstrate the convergent and discriminant validity of the scale. Furthermore, the composite reliability (CR) and average variance extracted (AVE) of each dimension of the scale were calculated.

Following this, multi-group CFA was performed to evaluate the measurement invariance of NUSATIES across gender (male/female) and specialty (general education/special education) groups, respectively. A series of nested models with increasing constraints were employed to assess configural invariance (comparing loading patterns), metric invariance (equal constraints on factor loadings), scalar invariance (equal constraints on intercepts), and residual invariance (strict invariance) sequentially. Since the chi-square test (Δχ²) can be influenced by sample size, larger samples tend to show significant differences in Δχ². Therefore, other indices were considered to evaluate model fit. ΔCFI and ΔRMSEA were used to assess measurement equivalence, following recommendations by Cheung and Rensvold [33]. Generally, measurement equivalence is considered acceptable when |ΔCFI| < 0.01 and |ΔRMSEA| < 0.015 [33].

Results

Items analysis

According to the item deletion criteria from the analysis, a total of 5 items (items 7, 9, 12, and 17) were removed. Specifically, four items (items 7, 9, 12, and 17) had correlation coefficients with the total score of less than 0.4 and were thus deleted. The remaining 21 items were retained based on both the high and low grouping method and by examining the item’s alpha value. These items were kept for subsequent analysis.

Exploratory factor analysis

The results of the EFA are presented in Table 2. Items with factor loadings less than 0.40 and/or cross-loadings exceeding 0.40 were considered for deletion. The three factors were derived through four iterations of EFA, adhering to predefined criteria such as the scree plot and eigenvalues > 1. Ultimately, 15 items remained, with six items from the NUSATIES being deleted. The results of exploratory factor analysis (n = 300)

The eigenvalues of the three extracted factors were 8.98, 1.07, and 1.06, explaining 57.56%, 4.97%, and 1.84% of the total variance, respectively (together, these factors explained 64.37% of the total variance). The three factors were labeled according to item interpretation and the outcomes derived from the rotated factor loading matrix (see Table 2).

The first factor was named “cognitive”, reflecting the perceived positive or negative outcomes of inclusion and its technical feasibility. The second factor was named “affective”, capturing one’s emotional responses to inclusion in practice. The third factor was labeled “behavioral intention”, signifying teachers’ readiness to act based on their cognitive and affective beliefs, thereby promoting inclusion in practice.

Confirmatory factor analysis

The CFA demonstrated a satisfactory model fit for the proposed 3-factor structure (χ²= 1329.12, df = 78, χ²/df = 17.04, CFI = 0.95, TLI = 0.95, SRMR = 0.03, RMSEA = 0.07 [0.065, 0.072]). The standardized path estimates ranged from 0.62 to 0.84 (all p < 0.001), and the three factors correlated significantly with each other (see Fig. 1). Therefore, this study supports the three-factor model of NUSATIES [35].

Furthermore, Fornell and Larcker indicated that a scale demonstrating satisfactory convergent validity should possess an Average Variance Extracted (AVE) exceeding 0.5 [38], and the composite reliability (CR) should be at least 0.6. Measurement invariance test

The goodness-of-fit statistics for the three-factor model of NUSATIES were analyzed in subsamples categorized by specialty (general education/special education) [39]. Favorable results were obtained in both subsamples: general education (n = 3644, χ2 = 1120.542, df = 73, p < 0.001, CFI = 0.973, RMSEA = 0.063, SRMR = 0.029); special education (n = 447, χ2 = 369.185, df = 73, p < 0.001, CFI = 0.927, RMSEA = 0.098, SRMR = 0.045), indicating robust baseline model fits for both groups. Subsequently, measurement invariance by specialty group was assessed using configural, metric, and scalar models (see Table 5). In Step 1, the configural invariance model was executed, demonstrating satisfactory fit indices (CFI = 0.967, TLI = 0.952, RMSEA = 0.049, SRMR = 0.049). In Step 2, a metric invariance model (factor cross-group) was employed, constraining factor loadings to equality across specialty groups. The model demonstrated favorable fit indices (CFI = 0.966, TLI = 0.955, RMSEA = 0.047), and the changes in fit indices indicated acceptable metric invariance for specialty (ΔCFI = 0.001, ΔRMSEA = 0.002). In Step 3, a scalar invariance (strong invariance) model was applied, with constraints on factor loadings and thresholds across specialty groups. Results showed excellent fit indices (CFI = 0.966, TLI = 0.956, RMSEA = 0.047), and changes in fit indices indicated acceptable scalar invariance for specialty (ΔCFI = 0.001, ΔRMSEA = 0.002). Step 4 tested residual invariance (strict invariance), achieving preferable results (CFI = 0.942, TLI = 0.937, RMSEA = 0.056). However, ΔCFI (0.024) and ΔRMSEA (0.007) exceeded critical values, indicating lack of specialty-related invariance at the residual level.

Gender and specialty -related measurement invariance of the NUSATIES

Measurement invariance by gender was also analyzed. First, baseline models for different genders were constructed. The model fit for the male group (n = 670, χ2 = 235.82, df = 73, p < 0.001, CFI = 0.977, RMSEA = 0.058, SRMR = 0.025) and the female group (n = 3421, χ2 = 1243.86, df = 73, p < 0.001, CFI = 0.967, RMSEA = 0.068, SRMR = 0.031) were satisfactory. For the configural model, the results indicated that the fit indices were satisfactory (CFI = 0.969, TLI = 0.955, RMSEA = 0.047, SRMR = 0.025). The metric invariance model suggested a good model fit (RMSEA = 0.046, CFI = 0.969, TLI = 0.958) and acceptable fit indices (ΔCFI = 0.000, ΔRMSEA = 0.001). Therefore, metric invariance across gender was confirmed. Additionally, the scalar invariance model demonstrated excellent fit (RMSEA = 0.046, CFI = 0.968, TLI = 0.959), with fit indices within acceptable ranges (ΔCFI = 0.000, ΔRMSEA = 0.001). Therefore, scalar invariance across gender was validated. However, the residual invariance model demonstrated a good model fit (RMSEA = 0.050, CFI = 0.953, TLI = 0.949), but the change in model fit indices was unacceptable (ΔCFI = 0.015, ΔRMSEA = 0.003). Therefore, residual invariance for gender was not supported. In summary, gender and specialty showed configural, metric, and scalar invariances.

Next, the study examined whether there were significant differences in the NUSATIES scores based on gender and specialty. As shown in Table 6, the total score on the NUSATIES and its dimensions exhibited significant differences based on specialty; Specifically, students within the special education groups exhibited significantly more positive attitudes toward inclusive education compared to those in the general education groups (p < 0.001). However, there was no significant difference based on gender.

Discussion

The aim of this study was to develop a Chinese version of the Attitudes Toward Inclusive Education Scale for university teacher candidates and to investigate its psychometric properties. Additionally, this study represents the first attempt to develop an Attitudes Toward Inclusive Education Scale specifically for pre-service educators, which is crucial for assessing the attitudes of future teachers towards inclusive education and predicting the success of inclusive education practices.It is essential for policymakers to explore potential impediments to inclusive education and pinpoint specific training requirements to bolster the effective implementation of inclusive policies.

The present study determined that the Chinese version of the NUSATIES exhibits good construct validity. CFA indicated that the three-factor model of the scale adequately fits the data. The scale aimed to capture attitudes related to three key aspects of inclusive education: cognition, affect, and behavior. The factors mentioned above have significant implications for policymakers and practitioners involved in promoting inclusive education. For example, grasping the advantages of inclusion can guide the development of evidence-based strategies to support students with SEN in inclusive settings. Similarly, understanding the factors that shape principals’ attitudes toward inclusive education can inform the development of policies and interventions aimed at addressing challenges encountered in inclusive environments.

The high level of internal consistency observed in this study (Cronbach’s α = 0.95 ) offers robust evidence regarding the reliability of the Chinese version of the NUSATIES. Moreover, the NUSATIES and its subscale positively correlated with TEIP while it is negatively correlated with CIES, which is consistent with previous results of research [40, 41], which supports the sound criterion-related validity of the NUSATIES. This study also found the CR of the scale exceeded 0.7 and the AVE was more than 0.5, showing that the NUSATIES had high convergent and discriminant validity [42].

The current investigation found no statistically significant gender disparities in attitudes towards inclusive education. This outcome aligns with previous studies by O’Toole and Burke (2013) among Irish pre-service teachers and Carroll et al. (2003) in Australia [43, 44], both of which similarly reported no gender differences in attitudes towards inclusive education. However, it is important to acknowledge that these findings diverge from results reported in specific studies. For example, Sharma et al. (2015) identified that male pre-service teachers in Pakistan demonstrated more favorable attitudes towards inclusive education compared to their female counterparts [45]. Similarly, Saloviita (2020) and Alghazo and Naggar Gaad (2004) in Finland and the United Arab Emirates observed significant gender disparities favoring female teachers in their attitudes toward inclusive education [46, 47]. Moreover, Forlin et al. (2007) reported mixed findings concerning gender disparities across Canada, Hong Kong, Australia, and Singapore [48]. These variations across studies are likely influenced by methodological as well as cultural, theoretical, and personal factors.

While prior research has acknowledged potential cultural and disciplinary variations in responses to ATIES [49], many studies addressing these questionnaires have not provided sufficient details on measurement invariance. Establishing measurement invariance is crucial for ensuring consistent scale measurement across diverse groups and assessing the reliability of score comparisons among population subgroups [50]. The current study provided critical preliminary proof regarding the measurement invariance of the NUSATIES, reflecting actual differences in attitude toward inclusion rather than measurement artifacts.

In the current study, participants with a specialization in special education demonstrated significantly higher levels of positive attitudes towards inclusive education than those in general education. These findings align with Mergler et al. (2016) and Zhao and Li (2015), who observed that pre-service teachers specializing in special education exhibit more favorable attitudes toward inclusive education compared to those lacking experience in inclusive education. Moreover, well-trained teachers demonstrate greater readiness to implement effective educational practices [51–53]. It is suggested that as students in special education acquire enhanced understanding through training in inclusive education, they are more inclined to cultivate positive beliefs, attitudes, and behaviors towards inclusive practices, thereby fostering inclusivity within their classrooms and educational institutions.

These findings carry substantial implications for research and practice, emphasizing the significance of positive attitudes among students in mainstream education to advance inclusive education. Training in inclusive education and fostering positive attitudes are both indispensable for achieving the goal of inclusion [54]. This study underscores the importance, within the realm of educational psychology practice, of investigating potential barriers to inclusive education and pinpointing specific training requirements critical to effectively implementing inclusive policies for students in mainstream education.

Implication

This study carries substantial implications for future research and practice in the field of inclusive education. The findings indicate that the NUSATIES scale can serve as a valuable tool for measuring the attitudes of Chinese teacher candidates toward inclusive education. This instrument facilitates the assessment of pre-service teachers’ attitudes and provides empirical evidence for interventions. The three-factor model identified in this study can offer specific guidance to educators on intervening with pre-service teachers regarding inclusive education concepts. Researchers can utilize the Chinese version of the NUSATIES scale to explore potential variables contributing to enhancing university teacher candidates’ attitudes toward including students with disabilities. Educators can reconsider and reform undergraduate courses based on measurement results to ensure better training in inclusive education for teacher candidates. Consequently, the positive attitudes of these teacher candidates towards inclusive education can expedite their implementation of inclusive practices in future workplaces, fostering greater inclusivity and a more positive attitude towards students with special needs. This approach can promote the effective implementation of inclusive education on a larger scale.

Limitations and future directions

There are some limitations in this study that require further improvement. First, this study only involved Chinese Normal College Students, which limits the generalizability of our results. As suggested by Cullen et al. [54], attitudes toward inclusive education may vary across cultures. Therefore, this scale needs to be tested with different cultures in future research. Second, although large samples were used, achieving a balanced male-female ratio in sampling was challenging. The proportion of female students was very high (83.7%), whereas male students constituted a smaller percentage. This disparity is due to the small number of male students enrolled in teacher training programs at all levels and in all specializations. Consequently, findings may be limited in terms of generalizability. It needs to conduct a qualitative study to more comprehensively understand female and male normal students’ attitude toward inclusive education in future. Additionally, the NUSATIES was developed as a self-report measure. The sensitivity of inclusion as a topic may lead respondents to choose options that reflect social desirability, potentially distorting survey data. It is suggested that data should be collected from multiple sources in the future, such as parent or student ratings. Additional qualitative research methods, such as interviews, to examine attitudes towards inclusive education can also be beneficial.

Conclusion

In summary, this study describes the development of the Normal College Students’ attitudes toward inclusive education scale (NUSATIES), which comprises three factors (i.e., cognitive, affective, and behavioral intention), meeting psychometric standards. The final 15-item NUSATIES is now available for use in identifying the attitudes of pre-service teachers toward inclusive education.
