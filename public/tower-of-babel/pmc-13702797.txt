Wildlife trade as a driver of pandemic emergence: a post-COVID alliance’s legacy of response
Authors: Jamie K Reaser, Ann-Kristen Konzak, Johannes Keil, Beate Henrichfreise, Michael Nagel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHuman health is inextricably linked to the health of wildlife populations and ecological systems. One Health science offers a multi-faceted approach to pandemic prevention, holistically investigating the interrelatedness of environmental, animal, and human health. Wildlife trade is a vital context for this work; zoonotic disease transmission along the wildlife trade pathway has had devastating social, cultural, and economic consequences. In 2021, in response to the COVID-19 pandemic, the German government launched the International Alliance against Health Risks in Wildlife Trade to enhance awareness, knowledge, and policies that narrow the gap between transdisciplinary science and the implementation of wildlife trade risk-reduction measures. Alliance operations have transitioned to the World Organisation for Animal Health. Herein, we establish a historical record for the Alliance, reflecting on first-phase achievements and lessons learned in the scientific, policy, and community empowerment contexts. We emphasize Alliance advancement of One Health, making recommendations for even greater returns on programmatic investments in pandemic prevention.Keywords: capacity building, International Alliance against Health Risks in Wildlife Trade, One Health, science-policy, zoonoses

Human health is inextricably linked to the health of wildlife populations and ecological systems. In recent years, this has been most widely and aptly demonstrated when human-driven impacts on ecological systems facilitate disease outbreaks in wildlife, and susceptible people sicken after direct or indirect contact with infected animals, leading to epidemics (localized disease outbreaks) and pandemics (widespread disease outbreaks). The microorganisms that cause disease are known as pathogens. When pathogens are transmitted from wildlife to people, sometimes through intermediary domestic animals, the resultant infections are referred to as zoonotic diseases or zoonoses (Plowright et al. 2021, Reaser et al. 2022). One Health science offers a multi-faceted approach to pandemic prevention, holistically investigating the ways science, technology, and social systems interface with environmental, animal, and human health (One Health High-Level Expert Panel et al. 2022, Danasekaran 2024). The advent of the COVID-19 (SARS-CoV-2) pandemic in March 2020 raised the profile of One Health as a transdisciplinary philosophy and practice applicable to understanding and mitigating the root causes of zoonotic disease emergence (Bonilla-Aldana et al. 2020, Henley 2020). Calls have been made to integrate a comprehensive One Health approach across scientific communities as the most effective way to achieve multilateral policy goals aimed at pandemic prevention, spurring necessary innovations in science and policy (Zhang et al. 2025a, Reaser and Lieberman 2026). In this Special Report, we provide a comprehensive overview of the International Alliance against Health Risks in Wildlife Trade’s (hereafter, the Alliance) response to such calls, detailing how this innovative global partnership has employed a One Health approach to empower a wide range of stakeholders—from multilateral frameworks to local communities—to mitigate pandemic risks in wildlife trade.

The wildlife trade is part of a complex, dynamic arrangement of legal and illegal commerce networks hallmarked by human–animal interactions. Throughout the wildlife trade pathway (Arroyo-Quiroz et al. 2025, see figure 1 therein), there are numerous opportunities for zoonotic disease transmission. Disease outbreaks resulting from wildlife trade have impacted public health and a wide range of other sectors, leading to hundreds of billions of dollars of economic damage globally (Karesh et al. 2005, Swift et al. 2007, Pavlin et al. 2009). Not surprisingly, One Health scholars have identified wildlife trade as a key factor in the COVID-19 pandemic and a primary concern for subsequent zoonoses outbreaks (Wang et al. 2022, Gallo-Cajiao et al. 2023, Karesh et al. 2025). The Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora (CITES) reported (CITES Secretariat 2022) that approximately 82 million wild animals were in direct trade, with an additional distribution of 86 million kg of wildlife by weight. Because these calculations only reflect the relatively small number of species regulated by CITES and illegal trade is not readily documented, total wildlife trade volumes are likely orders of magnitude higher. For example, a 22-year retrospective (2000–2022) of wildlife imports into the United States (US) identified nearly 30,000 species in legal trade, representing over 2.85 billion individual animals (Marshall et al. 2025). Given the high economic value of wildlife commerce, wildlife trade activity is more likely to accelerate than diminish; legal wildlife trade was conservatively estimated to be worth between US$2.9 and 4.4 trillion from 1997 to 2016 (Andersson et al. 2021), and illegal wildlife entry into the US alone at US$3.2 billion/year (Tow et al. 2021).

In September 2021, the German Federal Ministry for Economic Cooperation and Development (BMZ), supported by the German development assistance agency, Deutsche Gesellschaft für Internationale Zusammenarbeit, responded to the zoonotic disease risks associated with wildlife trade by investing approximately 12 million EUR in a global framework of cooperation. The Alliance was launched at the IUCN World Conservation Congress in Marseille, France, with a mission to forge “an open, inclusive and collaborative space where stakeholders join forces to better understand and reduce the threat of pathogen spillover from wildlife trade and markets, providing and communicating evidence, supporting interventions, thereby, reducing the risk of future outbreaks, epidemics and pandemics, while concurrently improving health, equity, and well-being for all species—through a One Health approach.” Two goals were established: (a) reduce the risks of zoonotic spillover and step up responses (including behavioral changes) to human and animal health risks caused by direct and indirect contact with wildlife and their products along the wildlife trade chain and (b) enhance international and national awareness, knowledge, and policies regarding the first goal, thus narrowing the gap between science and implementation. At the outset, more than 180 national and international political, academic, and civil society organizations joined the Alliance, committing to a shared vision of “a world of healthy and safe coexistence of people, animals, and the ecosystems that they are part of—underpinned by the recognition of their close links and interdependence” (International Alliance against Health Risks in Wildlife Trade 2025a).

The Alliance recently transitioned to a new administrative host—the World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH)—and a second phase of operations. In this Special Report, we establish a historical record for the Alliance; reflect on contributions made in the scientific, policy, and community empowerment contexts; and call for further action consistent with the Alliance’s mission, building on the legacy of the Alliance’s founding years. There are numerous technical, logistical, and sociocultural challenges to the effective implementation of a One Health approach. In the context of the Alliance’s mission, the need to wrestle with the complexities of disease and wildlife trade dynamics, separately and in concert, may be obvious but is not readily tractable. For example, the wildlife trade is catalyzed by a wide range of human needs and values, ranging from wildlife harvested and sold locally for sustenance (bushmeat) to wildlife traded internationally as luxury items—“exotic” live animals, as well as wildlife parts and products in the décor and fashion industries. The Alliance set out to address the overlooked risks of wildlife-to-human pathogen transmission (spillover), while also recognizing that wildlife trade is foundational to local communities throughout much of the world for food, medicines, crafts, and ceremonial practices tied to cultural identity and that globalization of wildlife trade supports various industries underpinning national economies (Hughes et al. 2021, Paudel et al. 2025). The Alliance thus adopted a participatory, stakeholder-driven approach to operations to ensure inclusiveness and balanced governance (figure 1).

A highly diverse Steering Committee elected by Alliance membership formulated the Alliance’s strategic plan and, in an ongoing manner, sets targets for further development and orientation, identifying topics and priorities for the Alliance’s focus. Internally, Steering Committee members serve as representatives of stakeholder communities by region, sector, and discipline (knowledge communities). In part, this is accomplished through thematic working groups led by Steering Committee members to implement Alliance priorities (table 1). Externally, Steering Committee members further the Alliance’s mission and programs by engaging in multilateral negotiations, making presentations at scientific and technical conferences, and collaborating with other relevant initiatives. A Secretariat based in Bonn and funded by the German government served as the primary focal point for the Alliance, managing its day-to-day Secretariat operations (International Alliance against Health Risks in Wildlife Trade 2025a). Further information on Alliance governance is available at: https://alliance-health-wildlife.org/. The remainder of the manuscript focuses on how the Alliance has advanced One Health at the zoonoses prevention-wildlife trade nexus, with recommendations made for the way forward—how the Alliance can achieve even greater returns on investments in pandemic prevention.

Advancing One Health science

In the context of the Alliance’s mission, advancing One Health science is crucial to understanding, promoting, and implementing pandemic prevention, preparedness, and response along the wildlife trade pathway—the chain of human–wildlife interactions proceeding from wildlife sourcing through wildlife end use (Gallo-Cajiao et al. 2023, Fa et al. 2025). One Health science provides insights into the anthropogenic degradation of ecological systems that drives zoonotic disease emergence and the dynamics of zoonotic diseases in wildlife populations (Plowright et al. 2021, Caceres-Escobar et al. 2023). One Health science can also help us understand how environmental conditions influence why and how people engage in wildlife trade, as well as the information needs, roles, and responsibilities of practitioners across environmental, animal, and human health care professions (Woldehanna and Zimicki 2015, Reaser et al. 2025b). Because One Health science is a transdisciplinary practice that takes a systems approach, the field has the potential to generate zoonoses risk mitigation measures in the wildlife trade context that benefit ecological and socio-cultural systems in an integrated, sustainable manner (Aguirre et al. 2021, Kuhn et al. 2024).

Here, we provide examples of how the Alliance has employed and advanced One Health science consistent with its science-based risk reduction plan (figure 2). We focus on four topic areas: integrating state-of-the-art research into the Alliance’s programming, funding pilot projects to develop and test science-based risk mitigation measures, establishing platforms for exchanging scientific and technical knowledge globally, and hosting a transdisciplinary experts pool to assist national governments through scientific missions.

State-of-the-art research. The Alliance strengthens the One Health scientific community by integrating state-of-the-art research into its programming. Taking a transdisciplinary approach, this has included research on emerging and novel pathogens, risk analysis, and strategies for human behavior change. For example, research teams supported by Alliance funding (see pilot projects, next section) investigated linkages between wild meat handling and zoonoses risks (Tumelty et al. 2023, Akpan et al. 2025, Masudi et al. 2025, Soto et al. 2025, Akpan et al. 2026a), assessed zoonoses risks associated with animal-derived products used for medicinal and belief-based purposes (Akpan et al. 2025b), and tested methods for assessing whether the use of wild animal species is safe, legal, and sustainable, consistent with a One Health approach (Timoshyna et al. 2025). The Alliance strengthened the social sciences at the interface of wildlife trade and zoonoses risk mitigation by providing in-kind sponsorship for two article collections hosted by Frontiers in Conservation Science. Supported by members of the Alliance’s Human Dimensions Working Group, the first collection explored opportunities for advancing the science of environmental justice in the international wildlife trade (Reaser et al. 2025a), whereas the second collection addressed the need for zoonoses public health messaging to simultaneously promote biodiversity conservation to prevent harm to wildlife believed to host zoonotic pathogens (Reaser et al. 2025b). The Alliance routinely translated scientific findings for policy development and community capacity building. For example, the co-chairs of the Science-Policy Working Group outlined a wildlife trade agenda for Pandemic Agreement implementation (Reaser and Lieberman 2026), and an assessment of Alliance-funded transdisciplinary projects was published to catalyze effective on-the-ground risk management (Li et al. 2026). Pilot projects. The Alliance funded 18 transdisciplinary pilot projects aimed at mitigating zoonotic spillover risks in wildlife trade. The geographic reach of the project portfolio spans 23 countries of Africa, Asia, and the Americas, along with one global-scale activity. A project map, with links to project descriptions, is available at: https://alliance-health-wildlife.org/projects/.

The projects filled scientific information gaps and helped bridge science into One Health education and policy programs. They prioritized upstream prevention and tailored risk-reduction efforts to local knowledge systems, cultural values, gender roles, and governance structures (International Alliance against Health Risks in Wildlife Trade 2025b). In boxes 1 and 2, we spotlight One Health projects in Vietnam and Liberia that cut across science, policy, and capacity building to make substantive advances in zoonoses risk mitigation at source. Li and colleagues (2026) provide an overview of the collective pilot projects, outlining key lessons and insights to strengthen the prevention of future pandemics at the human–animal–environment interface. Despite the relatively small financial input, substantial returns on investment were demonstrated. For example, in Guatemala, over 700 wild animals were screened for viruses, conservation rangers were trained to report wildlife mortality and morbidity events for diagnostic analysis, and advances were made to pathogen collection and storage capacities. In the Democratic Republic of the Congo, wild meat (bushmeat) sampling revealed the presence of multiple pathogens of human health concern; 800 samples collected in wildlife markets demonstrated predominance of high-risk species for sale, and unhygienic practices for handling wildlife parts were widely documented. The study also revealed that wildlife hunting practices are financially motivated, whereas wildlife consumption stems from a lack of economic alternatives. These observations underscore the need for interventions differentially targeted among stakeholders. By assessing lessons learned across these projects, the Alliance gained transferable insights that can accelerate the design and implementation of research programs elsewhere (International Alliance against Health Risks in Wildlife Trade 2025a). Collectively, the projects reveal that zoonoses risk mitigation requires more than scientific evidence; it depends on enabling political environments, trusted relationships, and context-sensitive strategies (Li et al. 2026). These factors are discussed in other sections of this article.

Spotlight on national wildlife-farming regulations to reduce spillover risk in Vietnam.

Vietnam has rapidly expanded its commercial wildlife farming, with over 7000 registered farms, creating hotspots for pathogen spillover. To mitigate this risk, the government—supported by the Alliance—has prioritized the One Health approach to strengthen biosecurity, enhance applied research, and tighten regulatory oversight. A collaborative project was initiated to support and improve the national policy system, strengthen the basis for wildlife farm management, and raise knowledge and awareness of the zoonoses risks associated with wildlife farming. Through a research initiative and associated multi-stakeholder consultations, project participants pushed for critical updates to Vietnam’s wildlife-farming framework, producing actionable recommendations to improve wildlife farm regulation. The project identified legal and regulatory gaps in wildlife-farm monitoring, disease surveillance, veterinary oversight, and biosecurity. It then developed policy recommendations and technical standards that could be incorporated into government management systems. The project also generated detailed analyses of veterinary legislation, wildlife-farm management, and implementation challenges across six provinces. Major outcomes of the project include:

Spotlight on implementation of wildlife disease surveillance guidelines in Liberia.

In 2024, with financial support from the Alliance, IUCN (including its Wildlife Health Specialist Group), WOAH, and EcoHealth Alliance took a One Health approach in developing General Guidelines for Surveillance of Diseases, Pathogens and Toxic Agents in Free-ranging Wildlife (hereafter, Guidelines). In Liberia, the Alliance supported IUCN to build capacity for guideline implementation. From 8 to 10 March 2025, in Tubmanburg City, Bomi, Liberia, IUCN and the Forestry Training Institute of Liberia convened a workshop for 31 participants from key national and international organizations, enhancing technical knowledge on wildlife disease surveillance and catalyzing multistakeholder collaboration among government representatives, academia, non-governmental organizations, and international institutions. The initiative also led to Liberia's first-ever sampling for avian influenza in wild birds, revealing the presence of avian influenza RNA among the 202 wild bird samples collected in Mesurado wetland habitats. Overall, the project established the foundations for a national wildlife disease surveillance system in Liberia and demonstrated how One Health collaboration can strengthen early detection and prevention of zoonotic disease risks. Knowledge exchange. The World Organisation for Animal Health’s (WOAH) report, Rapid Review of Evidence on Managing the Risk of Emerging Diseases in the Wildlife Trade, led by an Alliance Steering Committee member, calls for improved global knowledge sharing and knowledge brokering (Stephen 2021). The World Health Organization (WHO) also recognizes the need for collaborative knowledge sharing to co-create and crowdsource solutions to pandemic risks (WHO 2025). The Alliance is a leading international venue for One Health knowledge sharing, regularly enabling experts from around the world to give voice to health risks in the wildlife trade context. In just the first few years of the Alliance’s operations, more than 500 knowledge products were produced with and by our members. The Alliance has facilitated knowledge exchange by creating spaces for information sharing and dialogue among relevant stakeholders. In its first phase of operations, the Alliance hosted over 30 online events, attracting more than 1400 participants and, ultimately, reaching 12 million people (International Alliance against Health Risks in Wildlife Trade). Technical webinars have been offered in three formats: Expert Talks, Special Issues, and Voices from the Ground. Expert Talks bring cutting-edge science and policy to Alliance members, empowering them to advance the Alliance’s mission. Special Issues are timely “pop-up” webinars that provide information and guidance on emerging issues (e.g., Mpox outbreaks in progress). Voices from the Ground webinars report on Alliance-funded projects and CONTACT missions (discussed below). Online interactive workshops are designed to deliver mission-relevant science for practical uptake in the field. For example, the Alliance held a workshop on behavior change strategies for mitigating zoonoses risk at the human-wildlife interface in wildlife trade. The Alliance’s five open and inclusive working groups (table 1) provided an ongoing opportunity for members to drive the knowledge exchange process, prioritizing discussions and activities according to need and associated motivation. Fundamentally, the working groups were dedicated to knowledge-driven capacity building and practical applications (e.g., developing science-based positions for multilateral negotiations). Across these frameworks, Alliance programming has enabled knowledge sharing among One Health practitioners who would otherwise rarely interact due to geographic, sector, discipline, and cultural divides. By promoting transparent evidence sharing amongst its members, the Alliance proactively filled information gaps while constructively addressing misinformation and disinformation campaigns. It has provided a platform for co-learning, accelerating the growth and diffusion of knowledge and innovation. Ultimately, the knowledge exchange programming proved effective in translating the best available information into timely, effective action. For information on Alliance events, see: https://alliance-health-wildlife.org/events/.

Expert consultancies. Through the Government Consultancy Facility (CONTACT), Alliance-member expertise supports government implementation of primary prevention measures and interventions at the wildlife-human interface. Interdisciplinary teams respond to requests for support from national governments, providing context-specific services in knowledge transfer and capacity building. CONTACT’s overarching goal is to empower evidence-based interventions targeting risks and behavioral prevention measures throughout the wildlife trade chain, strengthening the scientific underpinnings of regulation, legislation, enforcement, and international cooperation. CONTACT uniquely specializes in medium-term, preventive government advisory services aimed at minimizing health risks associated with wildlife trade and consumption. During the first phase of the Alliance’s programming, CONTACT missions were executed in four countries: Benin, Bolivia, Guatemala, and Peru. Programming led to substantive scientific advancements. For example, in Bolivia, a collaborative effort with the country’s National Museum of Natural History produced a platform for Bolivia’s first health surveillance system and data systematization of confiscated wildlife. In Benin, a close collaboration with the German government facilitated access to innovative diagnostic tools (e.g., Pan-Orthopox PCR test), significantly improving the country’s zoonoses early detection and rapid response capacities. In addition, the project’s social science research provided vital insights into the motivations and practices of those involved in wild trade, enabling the design of strategically targeted and culturally sensitive health communication strategies (International Alliance against Health Risks in Wildlife Trade 2025a). CONTACT missions helped institutionalize national commitments to safeguarding public health and biodiversity conservation. They have catalyzed and accelerated further investments in zoonoses risk mitigation by governments and their stakeholders by, for example, making them more competitive for international funding. For further CONTACT information, see: https://alliance-health-wildlife.org/for-governments/.

Informing and implementing policy decisions and regulatory frameworks

Typically, regulatory measures to minimize zoonotic disease are not promulgated until after outbreaks occur (Anderson et al. 2025). However, the COVID-19 pandemic underscored the need for nations to take a precautionary approach—to proactively institute policies to prevent zoonotic spillover along the wildlife trade pathway (Daszak et al. 2020, Biggs et al. 2023). Although there have been calls for a complete ban on commercial wildlife trade, at least for certain taxonomic groups (D’Cruze et al. 2020) research has revealed that trade prohibitions can drive illegal trade and, thereby, potentially increase spillover risks (Roe and Lee 2021, Kubo et al. 2025). The Alliance recognizes that wildlife trade is at least as much about people as it is about wild animals; wildlife trade interfaces with the lives and livelihoods of millions of people globally. It is closely tied to complex, dynamic, interrelated socioeconomic, cultural, and ecological factors (Liew et al. 2021, Biggs et al. 2023, Hughes et al. 2023). Thus, the Alliance has hallmarked holistic, system-oriented engagement in policy processes intended to prevent future pandemics stemming from wildlife trade. In addition to providing the science needed by decision-makers to formulate effective policy from a One Health perspective, the Alliance has promoted policy agendas that are inclusive, participatory, culturally sensitive, equitable, and just. In this context, environmental justice spans social justice, wildlife species justice, and ecological justice (Reaser et al. 2025a, Arroyo-Quiroz et al. 2025)

Multilateral conventions and related forums

Four multilateral bodies are leading the way in pandemic prevention at the human-wildlife interface. These include the United Nations Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD), Convention on the Conservation of Migratory Species of Wild Animals (CMS), Convention on the International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora (CITES), and the World Health Organization Pandemic Agreement. The Alliance has played a pivotal role in delivering One Health science into the post-COVID negotiating processes under each of these forums, as well as supporting the scientific and technical capacity of governments to implement the decisions made by the governing Conferences of Parties (COPs). For example, at the Convention on Biological Diversity’s 15th Conference of Parties in Montreal, Canada, the Alliance co-hosted a high-level roundtable, “Pandemic prevention: protecting nature and safeguarding human health,” including senior officials from the German and French governments. The Alliance has used less formal international mechanisms to motivate national government participation in the multilateral negotiations aimed at pandemic prevention. For example, in 2022, the Alliance hosted sessions at the World Health Summit in Berlin, Germany, combined with gatherings of experts from 20 nations, to pave tangible pathways for governments to invest in primary prevention as a cost-effective strategy with conservation and social benefits. In 2023, at a Breaking Barriers Conference, also in Berlin, the Alliance convened decision-makers, scientists, and biodiversity conservation practitioners for a session entitled “Advancing the One Health agenda with a focus on environment.” Following a 2024 call with the Alliance, the International Union of Biological Sciences joined forces with 143 organizations, co-signing a letter to the Intergovernmental Negotiating Body tasked with negotiating what would become the Pandemic Agreement under the WHO. This letter underscored the need to prioritize pathogen spillover prevention and address the root causes of zoonotic disease emergence (Li et al. 2026).

National and subnational governance

Sharp and colleagues (2021) found that post-COVID, policymakers' most significant challenges to future pandemic prevention included a lack of financing and assessment capacity for outbreak preparedness and response, coordinating across the diverse array of stakeholders and disciplines, building capacity for outbreak-related research and development, and reinforcing measures for infection prevention and control. The Alliance has helped address these shortcomings at the national and subnational levels across the science-policy interface. For example, through the CONTACT country advisory missions and pilot project funding, the Alliance has helped countries prioritize and synchronize risk management activities in accordance with international agreements and expectations (supplementary tables 1, 2). A biosecurity-oriented, Alliance-funded project in Vietnam helped to align national strategies with global best practices. Project leaders produced guidance to help the country assess and improve national legislation that directly or indirectly governs wildlife trade. At national and provisional levels in China, an Alliance-funded project informed government legislators and other decision-makers of regulatory loopholes and public health risks along the wildlife trade chain, providing them with recommendations for strengthening governance frameworks. Implementation of the recommendations is ongoing. An Alliance-funded project engaging the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), Cameroon, and Namibia focused on improving national-level One Health governance capacities. In the DRC, the project advanced the country’s first comprehensive wildlife policy through a participatory, cross-sectoral process, leading to the creation of a national steering committee and technical task force. A sectoral diagnostic and community-based surveillance guide was drafted to improve integration of One Health governance. In Cameroon, national efforts focused on aligning wildlife laws and policies with regional frameworks and strengthening legal reform processes through knowledge-sharing and coordination among ministries. Subnational governance improvements were made by harmonizing legal frameworks and encouraging future implementation that is inclusive of local communities and decentralized actors. In Namibia, a comprehensive national legal analysis of wildlife and related sectors was undertaken, identifying key governance gaps (e.g., traceability, coordination, and stakeholder inclusion) and further enabling ongoing legal reform. At the subnational level, One Health governance capacities were strengthened through multi-sector engagement; local, private, and community actors engaged in workshops, reinforcing the country’s community-based conservation model and improving cross-sector coordination (International Alliance against Health Risks in Wildlife Trade 2025b, Li et al. 2026).

Empowering local communities

Because pandemics start at ground level as localized events (Plowright et al. 2021), local communities are the first to be impacted by zoonotic disease outbreaks and have the potential to serve as first responders. Community-led initiatives can uniquely foster the most imperative conditions for effective zoonotic risk mitigation—trust, equity, effective and timely communication—and reach marginalized groups (Ayala et al. 2021, Yuan et al. 2022, Byanyima et al. 2023). However, the Independent Panel for Pandemic Preparedness and Response has observed that “the potential for communities to shape the [pandemic] response at the decision-making table has been severely neglected” (The Independent Panel for Pandemic Preparedness and Response 2021). Likewise, Byanyima and colleagues recognize that a strong community infrastructure is vital to pandemic prevention, preparedness, and response, yet acknowledge that it has been insufficiently addressed. The Alliance has thus made strong commitments to local community empowerment, supporting the development of context-specific, culturally sensitive, and inclusive initiatives that align with the on-the-ground complexity of zoonoses outbreaks, as well as the diverse socio-ecological systems in which wildlife trade occurs.

The Alliance set out to support local communities by design; the Alliance’s inclusive network ensures that local organizations and underrepresented actors gain visibility and opportunities to contribute alongside larger, typically better-resourced institutions. Many of the pilot projects funded by the Alliance reached beyond scientific advancements to catalyze new cross-sectoral partnerships, strengthen local capacity, facilitate behavior change through trust-building and inclusive dialogues, and generate practical tools for community application. For example, in Thailand and Vietnam, Alliance-funded projects employed social marketing approaches with local communities to reduce the risk of their exposure to zoonoses during bat guano collection. Bat guano is used locally and sold into commercial markets as fertilizer. The project engaged over 70 bat guano workers and community leaders, fostering cross-sectoral collaboration among 58 stakeholders. An Alliance-funded project carried out in Cameroon and the Congo Basin piloted a social and behavioral change campaign to reduce zoonoses risks associated with wild meat handling. Community members engaged in multiple campaign workshops. The Alliance has partnered with various communities to generate a wide range of outputs tailored to their communication desires and needs (supplementary table 2). This has, for example, included locally contextualized and developed children's books that raise awareness of wildlife health risks and conservation (International Alliance against Health Risks in Wildlife Trade 2025a, 2025b). Through the aforementioned knowledge exchange platforms, the Alliance has reinforced and amplified locally developed innovations, facilitating their diffusion and adaptation in other settings (International Alliance against Health Risks in Wildlife Trade 2025b). The Alliance has consistently demonstrated that community-led, locally grounded, and collaborative approaches to pandemic prevention, planning, and response are feasible and effective despite the resource limitations and logistical challenges faced by many local communities (Li et al. 2026).

Conclusion: a call to further action

As the Alliance moves into its next phase of operations, we have reflected on the program’s mission, goals, challenges, and accomplishments, asking ourselves: What is the most vital and impactful niche for the Alliance? How can the Alliance leverage its current structure and accomplishments for the greatest returns on investment? In conclusion, we offer the following succinct observations and recommendations. Although these points are directed at future Alliance operations, we believe they will prove beneficial to donors supporting pandemic prevention from a One Health perspective. These points complement and contextualize recommendations recently made by the co-chairs of the Alliance Science-Policy Working Group (Reaser and Lieberman 2026).

Be member-driven. The Alliance’s greatest strength is its diverse, inclusive, proactive membership. Build upon existing platforms to enable Alliance members to set programmatic vision, direction, and priorities. At a minimum, this should include a) greater authority and accountability for the Alliance Steering Committee and associated working groups and b) a formalized, yet flexible, peer-learning network or laboratory that catalyzes innovative thinking and practical, fit-to-context solutions consistent with the Alliance’s mission.

Be needs-driven. Orient the Alliance toward the needs of national governments as set forth in multilateral agreements and national planning strategies. Focus on prioritizing applied risk-based science, strengthening policy and regulatory frameworks, and aligning national governance with tailor-made solutions that serve and empower local communities.

Be integrative. The Alliance’s mission is explicit to wildlife trade, and the Alliance must continue promoting pandemic prevention through a biodiversity conservation lens. However, to be effective over the long term, the Alliance needs to proactively broaden its membership and work program, better integrating the human health and agricultural sectors, the diversity of disciplines across One Health sciences, and Indigenous and local knowledge. The first phase of Alliance operations aptly demonstrated the value of integrating the social sciences in project planning and execution.
