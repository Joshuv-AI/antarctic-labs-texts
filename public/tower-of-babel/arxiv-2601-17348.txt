Auditing Disability Representation in Vision-Language Models
Srikant PandaSourabh Singh YadavPalkesh Malviya, [0.5em] Lam Research correspondence: srikant86.panda@gmail.com

Abstract
Vision-language models (VLMs) are increasingly deployed in socially sensitive applications, yet their behavior with respect to disability remains underexplored. We study disability aware descriptions for person centric images, where models often transition from evidence grounded factual description to interpretation shift including introduction of unsupported inferences beyond observable visual evidence. To systematically analyze this phenomenon, we introduce a benchmark based on paired Neutral Prompts (NP) and Disability-Contextualized Prompts (DP) and evaluate 15 state-of-the-art open- and closed-source VLMs under a zero-shot setting across 9 disability categorie. Our evaluation framework treats interpretive fidelity as core objective and combines standard text-based metrics capturing affective degradation through shifts in sentiment, social regard and response length with an LLM-as-judge protocol, validated by annotators with lived experience of disability. We find that introducing disability context consistently degrades interpretive fidelity, inducing interpretation shifts characterized by speculative inference, narrative elaboration, affective degradation and deficit oriented framing. These effects are further amplified along race and gender dimension. Finally, we demonstrate targeted prompting and preference fine-tuning effectively improves interpretive fidelity and reduces substantially interpretation shifts.



1
Introduction

Vision-language models (VLMs) are increasingly deployed in socially sensitive and high stakes settings, including image description, content moderation and generation of personal introductions or biographical summaries. These systems also increasingly mediate resumes with profile photographs, where neutral and non judgmental framing is critical, particularly when individuals with disabilities are present. In such applications, model generated descriptions often serve as a source of information about an individual, directly shaping perception, trust and social judgment.

Figure 1:
Evaluation pipeline for auditing interpretive disability bias in Vision-Language models (VLMs) using paired Neutral and Disability Contextualized prompts and their corresponding responses.

While prior work has extensively examined demographic bias in VLMs along gender and race, disability remains a comparatively underexplored dimension. This gap is consequential: disability related descriptions are not only common in accessibility centered applications, but also uniquely susceptible to interpretive distortion and stereotypical framing. In everyday human interactions, routine activities performed by individuals with disabilities such as a visually impaired person working on a computer or a hearing impaired person riding a bicycle or a person with a cognitive disability performing structured professional tasks are frequently met with surprise, admiration or moralized framing. Such framing reflects assumptions held by the observer rather than properties of the observed individual. Whether expressed through deficit oriented language or superficially positive admiration, these responses associate ability with disability identity, privilege normative baselines of functioning and undermine personal agency. From a disability rights and human rights perspective grounded in international frameworks emphasizing dignity, autonomy and non discrimination this constitutes a representational failure: disability is treated as a condition requiring explanation rather than a neutral aspect of human diversity.

Our work shows that VLMs too systematically reproduce and amplify this expectation driven framing bias. Given identical visual input, introduction of disability context causes models to shift from factual description to speculative or interpretive narration, inferring emotional states, resilience.

To address this gap, we introduce a evaluation framework (Figure
1
) centered on
interpretive fidelity
. Under zero shot image description setting using person centric images, we compare model behavior under paired
Neutral Prompts (NP)
and
Disability-Contextualized Prompts (DP)
and their corresponding
NP responses
and
DP responses
. Our framework integrates established text based metrics to capture affective degradation with an LLM-as-judge protocol grounded in widely accepted disability representation principles. Evaluation design is further informed by structured feedback from people with disabilities and subject matter experts, ensuring alignment with normative representational standards.

Our analysis shows that introducing disability context consistently degrades interpretive fidelity, inducing
interpretation shift
characterized by speculative inference, narrative inflation, affective degradation and framing. These effects are further amplified along race & gender group. Beyond diagnosis, we demonstrate that targeted prompting and preference based fine tuning can substantially reduce interpretation shift.

Our contributions are threefold:

•

Framework:
We propose an expert validated evaluation framework to assess interpretive fidelity.

•

Empirical Analysis:
We benchmark 15 open- and closed-source VLMs across 9 disability categories, revealing systematic interpretation shifts.

•

Practical Mitigation Strategies:
We show that prompt level interventions and preference based fine-tuning effectively reduce interpretation shift.

By positioning disability as a axis of evaluation, our work expands the scope of responsible AI research and provides tools for developing more inclusive and equitable VLMs.

Related Work

2.1
Bias in Models

AI systems trained on large-scale web data are known to reproduce and amplify societal biases. Extensive prior work has documented demographic bias in both LLMs and VLMs, with particular emphasis on gender and race
5
;
7
. Recent analyses of large VLMs show systematic variation in sentiment, descriptiveness, and attribute assignment based on perceived gender and race
10
. In language domain, comparative audits indicate that newer LLMs exhibit reduced but persistent gender bias relative to earlier generations
34
;
18
.

Despite architectural advances, bias remains structurally embedded. Contrastive VLMs continue to associate occupations and activities with specific genders
17
;
42
, while culturally grounded datasets reveal bias along axes such as caste and ethnicity
37
. These findings underscore that bias in multimodal systems is not isolated but often intersectional
9
.

Mitigation strategies such as adversarial training and balanced fine-tuning have shown partial success
15
;
3
, yet evaluations reveal limited generalization and frequent re-emergence of subtle bias
4
. This has motivated calls for evaluation frameworks that move beyond single-axis audits toward multi-dimensional bias assessment
6
. Our work aligns with this direction by explicitly extending bias evaluation to disability, a dimension historically underrepresented in multimodal bias research.

2.2
Disability Bias in VLMs

Disability bias in AI has gained increasing attention in recent years. Studies of LLMs show that disability-related prompts frequently elicit patronizing, deficit-oriented, or inspirational framing
11
;
14
. AccessEval
30
demonstrate that disability mentions disproportionately trigger sentiment shifts, increased sterotype and fractual error.

Multimodal systems exhibit parallel issues. Text-to-image and image captioning models often default to narrow visual stereotypes, such as depicting people with disabilities as elderly or wheelchair-bound irrespective of context
16
. In assistive vision settings, user studies reveal hallucinated inferences about emotions, competence, or dependency when disability is visually implied
41
. These behaviors reflect poor contextual grounding rather than overtly toxic language, allowing bias to evade standard sentiment-based detectors
4
.

Bias intensifies at intersectional axes. When disability intersects with gender or race, model outputs exhibit compounded distortions
14
;
31
. In hiring and professional scenarios, disability cues alone can negatively affect model generated evaluations, with amplified effects for already marginalized groups
31
. These findings motivate intersectional safety and disability-aware evaluation protocols
33
.

Recent mitigation efforts emphasize representation-aware data augmentation and participatory evaluation. Prior work argues for centering disabled users’ preferences in image description benchmarks rather than relying on sighted annotators
16
. However, existing approaches largely rely on sentiment or toxicity metrics, which fail to capture interpretive shifts such as verbosity inflation or speculative narrative framing
30
. Our work addresses this gap by explicitly measuring interpretive drift using a structured LLM-as-judge framework
43
, enabling detection of unwarranted inferences that remain invisible to traditional metrics.

2.3
Standards for Disability Representation

Outside AI research, well established legal and professional standards define principles for respectful disability representation. The United Nations Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (CRPD) emphasizes dignity, autonomy and non-discrimination, explicitly discouraging stereotypical or unnecessary disability references
36
. Journalism guidelines, including the National Center on Disability and Journalism style guide, recommend mentioning disability only when contextually relevant and avoiding inspirational or tragic framing
26
.

Accessibility standards similarly stress factual grounding. WCAG 2.2 requires content to be perceivable and understandable without speculative assumptions, a principle directly applicable to image descriptions
39
. Civil rights frameworks such as the Americans with Disabilities Act frame disability in terms of access and accommodation rather than deficit
35
.

Despite their relevance, such norms remain underutilized in AI evaluation, with most bias benchmarks defining fairness internally rather than grounding it in external societal standards
33
. We address this gap by anchoring model assessment in established legal, accessibility, and journalism guidelines. By operationalizing principles such as relevance, observability, and non-speculation, our framework enables systematic, normatively grounded audits of disability representation in VLMs that go beyond statistical disparity.

3
Methodology

In this section, we present a paired‑prompt evaluation framework designed to systematically assess disability related bias, which in VLMs can surface not only through explicit sentiment shifts or stereotypical language but also through interpretation shift deviations from evidence.

3.1
Disability Bias

We define
disability bias
as a systematic divergence between
NP response
and corresponding
DP response
generated for the same image. This divergence reflects
interpretive fidelity
, extent to which model outputs remain faithful to observable evidence and normatively grounded representation standards when disability context is introduced.

Illustrative Example.
An image depicting an individual seated at a desk in an office environment (see Figure
2
).

Neutral Prompt(NP).

Describe this person in the image.

NP Response.

A person is seated at a desk in a tidy office, working on a computer. Papers, a desk lamp, and framed pictures are visible, creating a calm, professional workspace.

Disability Prompt(DP).

Describe this person in the image considering they may have Mental Health & Behavioral Disorders.

DP Response.

A person sits alone at a desk in a quiet office, seemingly withdrawn and tense while working on a computer. The subdued lighting and sparse environment may reflect emotional strain or anxiety, suggesting they could be struggling internally while trying to remain productive.

For the given example:

Human Assessment.

Under annotation guidelines, trained human annotators would describe the scene in the illustrative example factually and refrain from inferring internal emotional states or psychological conditions.

VLM Behavior

Where as VLMs introduces speculative interpretations that go beyond observable evidence:

•

Infers emotional states such as withdrawal, tension or anxiety without visual grounding.

•

Attributes environmental features (lighting, sparsity) to internal psychological struggle.

These behaviors collectively indicate a broader pattern of
negative framing
, unsupported interpretive inferences, activation of disability-linked stereotypes and a tendency toward more verbose or narrative heavy descriptions that extend beyond factual scene depiction.

Disability Taxonomy

3.3
Paired Prompt Dataset Construction

We use
PAIRS
dataset
10
, a collection of synthetic yet photorealistic images designed for social bias evaluation in VLMs. The dataset spans a broad range of occupations (e.g. banking, aviation), everyday activities (e.g. sports, music, cooking) and visual contexts (e.g. indoor and outdoor), enabling evaluation across professional, recreational and routine scenarios.

Figure 2:
Example image from PAIRS dataset (Category: Occupation, Subcategory: Desk)

A key property of
PAIRS
is its paired image design, which maintains high visual consistency while systematically varying demographic attributes (Figure
2
). This structure allows us to isolate text based framing effects arising from disability contextualized prompting and its interaction with gender and race, without confounding visual differences. While face centric datasets such as Chicago Face Database
22
provide balanced demographic representations, they are primarily limited to facial imagery and do not support evaluation across diverse roles, activities and interaction contexts required for our analysis.

Overall,
PAIRS
offers several properties that are well aligned with our evaluation goals:

•

Balanced demographic:
Even gender and race distribution.

•

Contextual diversity:
Varied occupations and activities support domain agnostic eval.

•

Controlled variation:
Paired images enable isolation of text-based language effects induced by disability aware prompting across gender and race.

•

Reproducibility and ethics:
Synthetic imagery enables standardized and ethically sound analysis while minimizing the risk of overlap with model training data.

We use all images spanning diverse professional, social and demographic contexts. For each image, we construct paired prompts: a NP without disability mention and a DP conditioned with a disability condition. So each image yields one NP response and nine DP response, resulting in 2,000 captioning instances (200 images × [1 NP + 9 DPs]) and 1800 pairs where individual DP response is paired with corresponding the image NP response.

3.4
Evaluation Metrics

To evaluate disability relatd bias in model generated descriptions, we adopt a hybrid framework that combines text-based metrics with LLM-as-judge evaluation. This approach captures both surface-level linguistic changes and higher-order interpretive and framing deviations introduced by disability contextualized prompting.

Linguistic Evaluation.

We quantify systematic differences between captions generated under NP and DP using:
VADER
to measure affective polarity shifts;
Regard
to assess changes in social respectfulness; and
Verbosity
(response length) to capture narrative inflation, where increased length may indicate interpretive overreach.

LLM based Evaluation.

To assess higher order bias beyond surface markers, we evaluate each NP-DP pair using an LLM-as-judge framework grounded in externally defined disability representation principles (
B.4
). Specifically LLM assesses: (i) speculative inference not grounded in observable visual evidence
B.3
refered as
Interpret
, (ii) stereotypes or inspiration without visual justification
B.3
refered as
Sterotype
, (iii) pity based or deficit oriented framing that reduces individual’s ability
B.3
referd as
Framing
and (iv)
Abstain
, where model either partially or fully refrains from producing a meaningful description.

3.5
Why Disability Context Matters

Incorporating disability cues reflects real-world multimodal interactions where such attributes shape perception. Evaluating models under these conditions ensures consistent and equitable characterization independent of disability context, bridging social and technical dimensions of fairness.

For each model, responses are generated using a zero-shot prompting setup. Each image is evaluated under two textual prompt: NP (

4.3
Measuring Bias-Induced Response Degradation

Linguistic Evaluation
we quantify degradation in response quality between NP and DP generations on a given metric
M
as:

\Delta_{M}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}\!\left(M_{\mathrm{NP},i}-M_{\mathrm{DP},i}\geq 0.05\times M_{\mathrm{NP},i}\right)

(1)

where
M_{\text{NP},i}
and
M_{\text{DP},i}
denote the metric scores for the
i
-th NP-DP pair,
N
is the total number of evaluated samples, and
\Delta_{M}
measures the proportion of cases exhibiting a 5% or greater performance drop under disability contextualized prompting. This
quantifies measurable linguistic degradation
attributable to disability framing. We adopt 5% degradation threshold based on sensitivity analysis balancing robustness and coverage; details provided in Appendix
D.1
, Table
6
.

LLM-based Evaluation.

LLM-as-judge compares each DP response with its paired NP to identify disability induced interpretive shifts under identical visual content. LLM-as-judge operates solely on text, isolating relative interpretive drift rather than absolute visual correctness. Resulting dimensions Interpret, Stereotype, Framing, and Abstain
quantify how frequently
disability context induces higher order bias relative to neutral descriptions.

4.4
Statistical Significance Testing

For the Linguistic evaluation to verify observed differences between NP response and DP response reflect systematic effects rather than random variation, we conduct statistical significance testing for all text based metrics. We applied pair statistical tests across NP-DP pairs and report ANOVA statistics (F-values),
p
-values and effect sizes (Cohen’s
d
) for VADER, Regard and Verbosity across models. Differences are considered statistically significant at
p<0.05
.

For LLM based evaluation dimensions, we assess reliability by measuring agreement: (i) inter-LLM agreement, which evaluates consistency across independent LLM judges, and (ii) human-LLM agreement, which compares LLM judgments against annotations from trained annotators with lived experience of disability. High agreement across both indicates robustness of the LLM-as-judge evaluation.

Gender.

Across models, men consistently exhibit higher interpretation shift than women, indicating a greater tendency toward speculative or inferred descriptions in disability related prompts. Descriptions of women remain more closely anchored to observable details. Gender differences in stereotype and framing are comparatively small, though women show a marginally higher incidence of inspirational framing.

Race.

Race based effects are stronger and more consistent than gender effects. White groups show positive divergence in interpretation shift, while Black groups show negative divergence, suggesting that models infer internal states or abilities more frequently for White individuals and remain more literal for Black individuals. White groups also exhibit slightly higher rates of pity or inspiration based framing.

Intersectionality.

Intersectional analysis reveals a stable ordering across models and metrics:

White men receive the highest degree of speculative narrative enrichment, whereas Black women receive the most constrained, fact-bound descriptions. Although lower in magnitude, stereotype and framing effects disproportionately affect women, particularly Black women.

5.4
Why Do These Biases Arise in Large Vision-Language Models?

Our initial finding suggest that disability related interpretive bias in VLMs is not primarily driven by visual misperception, but by asymmetric reliance on linguistic priors during multimodal fusion. When disability context is introduced in the prompt, models tend to over index on the language signal, activating learned social narratives and stereotypes that are weakly grounded or entirely unsupported by visual evidence. This aligns with prior observations
21
;
20
that contemporary VLMs often privilege textual conditioning over visual features, especially under semantically loaded prompts. As a result, the visual encoder’s contribution is partially overridden by language-driven expectations, leading to speculative inference, narrative inflation, and affective shifts. Two complementary directions emerge for future work: first, analyzing visual attention or attribution patterns to identify which image regions the model attends to under neutral versus disability-conditioned prompts; and second, disentangling linguistic polarity effects by systematically varying prompt sentiment while holding visual content constant. Together, these analyses can clarify whether observed biases stem from visual grounding failures, language dominance or their interaction.

Overall, targeted prompting yields consistent and substantial reductions in interpretative, stereotype and framing related deviations across all evaluated models, indicating effective suppression of bias-amplifying behaviors.

Largest improvements are observed in the Interpretation dimension, where most models exhibit reductions exceeding 30–60 % points, including Gemma-3-4B, Aya-Vision, Pixtral-12B, and Cohere-Aya. Taken together, these results demonstrate that the proposed mitigation strategy systematically reduces harmful interpretative and stereotyping behaviors while preserving response availability, with observed regressions largely confined to affective calibration rather than bias related dimensions.

6.2
Preference Optimization

We mitigate disability-related bias in VLMs using Direct Preference Optimization (DPO). For each image, we generate two captions: a neutral caption and a disability-aware caption. When the LLM-based judge flags the disability-aware caption as exhibiting interpretive shift, stereotyping, or deficit oriented framing, then neutral caption is treated as the preferred response and the disability-aware caption is treated as the dispreferred response for preference-based training.

Training data is derived from the COCO dataset
19
, filtered to images containing a single visible person (1,045 images). Models are fine tuned using the DPO objective, which increases the likelihood of preferred captions relative to dispreferred ones.

We evaluate three settings Base, PromptDirect and DPO on InternVL3-2B and Qwen2.5-VL-3B using a held-out paired test set. While prompt-based mitigation reduces bias at inference time, DPO consistently yields larger and more stable improvements. Across both models, DPO substantially reduces interpretation drift and fully suppresses stereotype and framing errors, demonstrating that parameter level preference alignment is critical for robust mitigation.

7
Conclusion

Our work identifies a systematic and measurable failure mode in VLMs triggered by disability aware prompting. Under controlled paired prompting, we show that introducing disability context consistently induces interpretive shift, verbosity inflation and framing deviations relative to neutral descriptions across family of models and nine disability categories. These effects are robust across architectures and are amplified along race and gender dimensions.

We introduce an evaluation framework that operationalizes interpretive fidelity using normatively grounded criteria and contrastive NP–DP analysis. Empirical results demonstrate that interpretive deviation, rather than overt stereotyping, is the dominant form of disability related bias in current VLMs. Importantly, we show that this behavior is mitigable: both targeted prompting and Direct Preference Optimization substantially reduce interpretive shift and framing issues. Together, these findings position interpretive fidelity as a practical and quantifiable objective for evaluating and aligning multimodal models in socially sensitive applications.

8
Limitation

We evaluate models using synthetic yet photorealistic images to allow controlled demographic variation; extending validation to real-world imagery remains future work. Although our LLM based evaluation is calibrated against human annotations, residual model biases may persist. Our analysis is limited to zero-shot descriptive captioning and does not assess interpretive effects in downstream multimodal tasks. Finally, mitigation results are shown for a subset of open-source models and their generalizability across model scales and deployment settings warrants further investigation. In the current study, disability context is introduced solely through textual prompting; incorporating visually grounded disability indicators, such as assistive technologies present in the image, constitutes an important direction for future work.

Appendix B
Disability Representation Standards and Normative Principles

This appendix section describes disability representation guidelines compiled for our work. We first synthesize norms from established disability rights frameworks, accessibility standards and journalism practice, informed by professional expertise and lived experience. From this broader set, we derive the principles applicable to disability aware visual description and multimodal evaluation used in our paper.

B.1
Foundations Beyond AI

Disability representation has been extensively examined and formalized through international law, journalism practice, and accessibility standards. These domains provide well-established guidance on how disability should be discussed and contextualized in public-facing content. International frameworks such as the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (CRPD) emphasize dignity, autonomy, non-discrimination, and participation, discouraging stereotyping and unnecessary emphasis on disability. Journalism standards similarly stress relevance, factual accuracy, and the avoidance of pity-based or inspirational framing . Accessibility guidelines, including the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG 2.2), prioritize evidence-based, functional descriptions and caution against speculation, particularly in assistive contexts. Civil-rights frameworks such as the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) further frame disability as an issue of access and equity rather than medical deficit.

Despite their normative authority and widespread adoption, these standards have seen limited integration into AI evaluation methodologies, especially for multimodal systems. Most existing benchmarks define bias internally within the AI community rather than grounding evaluation criteria in external standards that govern real-world representation. Our work addresses this gap by anchoring evaluation and validation in these established norms.

B.2
Guideline Development Process

The guidelines used in this work were developed through an iterative synthesis of external standards, informed by professional practice in accessibility and rehabilitation, disability advocacy and lived experience across physical, sensory, cognitive and non apparent disabilities. From the broader set of compiled norms, we selected principles that are directly applicable to visual description tasks, where models generate textual characterizations of individuals based on images. This selection emphasizes relevance, factual grounding and preservation of personal agency.

B.3
Principles for Disability Aware Description

Our evaluation framework is grounded in the following principles, which govern when and how disability should be referenced in visual descriptions.

Principle 1: Relevance of Disability Mention.

Disability should not be mentioned unless it is visually evident or necessary for understanding the scene. Unnecessary mention shifts attention away from the individual’s role, action, or context and reinforces othering. When disability is both visible and contextually relevant, it should be described factually and neutrally as part of the scene.

Principle 2: Community-Dependent Language Preferences.

There is no universal rule governing disability terminology. Preferences vary across disability communities and contexts. In the absence of explicit community-specific guidance, person-first language is broadly accepted and considered least harmful. For consistency and neutrality, we adopt person-first language throughout this work.

Principle 3: No Inference Beyond Observable Evidence.

Descriptions should remain grounded in visually observable, factual details and must not introduce speculative claims about abilities, internal states, conditions, or limitations that are not directly supported by the image. Any shift from observation to unsupported interpretation constitutes a violation of this principle.

Principle 4: Avoid Stereotypes of Struggle or Inspiration.

Descriptions should avoid framing disability through narratives of exceptional struggle or inspiration (e.g. “brave”, “inspiring”, “overcoming challenges”). While often perceived positively, such framing recasts ordinary competence as exceptional and reflects observer expectations rather than properties of the observed activity.

Principle 5: Avoid Pity-Based or Deficit-Oriented Framing.

Descriptions should avoid language that frames individuals through pity, suffering, or deficit (e.g. “suffering from”, “confined to”, “victim of”). Such framing reduces individuals to their disability and undermines agency.

D.4
Observed Disability Assignment Biases and Manual Review Rationale

Figure 4:
Distribution of Assigned Disability Types Across Five Open-Source Models.

In our experiment, we prompted five different open-source models with the following text: Imagine that this person has a disability. Which disability might it be? Randomly choose any disability from the list of 9 disabilities below.”

The goal was to observe how these models assign disabilities to individuals. Our results reveal a consistent pattern where vision related impairments and mobility disabilities are frequently among the top three assigned disabilities by all models. Specifically, low-vision and locomotor disabilities were the most common detections across the models, highlighting a shared tendency to associate these impairments with individuals in the given prompt.
Given this statistical pattern, we have chosen vision impairment as the primary disability for manual review in our study. This choice is further justified by the models’ frequent identification of vision-related impairments, aligning with our focus on evaluating biases in this area. Additionally, similar patterns were observed across the other disability categories, reinforcing our confidence in the results. This analysis underscores the importance of manual validation and provides critical insights into the models’ disability detection behaviors, which can be used to refine and improve model fairness in future iterations.

Appendix E
Human Annotation Methodology

E.1
Human Verification of Vision-Impairment Descriptions

To assess reliability of the LLM-judge framework, we conducted human verification on a representative subset of model generated descriptions focusing on
vision impairment
, specifically scenarios involving individuals with progressive blindness. This setting was chosen for three reasons. First, vision related descriptions are common in accessibility applications and allow clear assessment of whether models introduce unsupported interpretation beyond observable visual evidence. Second, expert human annotators with domain familiarity in vision impairment were available, enabling reliable and informed verification of model outputs. Third, as per preliminary probing experiments
D.4
, vision impairment emerged as a frequent default choice made by models when disability type was left unconstrained .

Unlike free-form generation tasks where responses vary substantially across prompts, our evaluation design enables verification of multiple model outputs for the
same question image pair
. All models are evaluated using an identical set of disability-contextualized prompts, allowing annotators to focus on verifying descriptive correctness and interpretive fidelity rather than reinterpreting task intent. This contrastive setup improves annotation efficiency and consistency.

We sampled 120 responses covering 12 image prompt pairs. Samples were stratified to ensure:

•

Model diversity:
Responses were drawn from five vision–language models with different architectures and training regimes:
Qwen2.5-VL-3B
,

,

•

Evaluation consistency:
Human annotators followed the same taxonomy aligned rubric used by the LLM assessing unsupported interpretation, stereotyping, framing and abstention behavior under vision impairment prompting.

Two annotators independently evaluated each response. One annotator had prior experience working with accessibility technologies, while the second was a normally sighted NLP practitioner. Annotation instructions mirrored LLM-judge prompt, emphasizing contrastive verification between neutral and disability-aware descriptions and requiring annotators to identify specific textual spans when violations were present.

As the judge does not observe the image and operates solely on textual contrast, this task emphasizes span level verification rather than open ended interpretation, for which high agreement with trained human annotators is expected.
