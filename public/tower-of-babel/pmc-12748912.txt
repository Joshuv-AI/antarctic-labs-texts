Differential grain quality responses of rice varieties under combined salt, cadmium and arsenic stresses
Authors: Sulthan Rifasa, Muhammed Ramzan Khan, Muhammed Uzair, Amir Mumtaz, Muhammad Zeeshan Hyder, Sumaira Farrakh, Ayesha Tahir, Tayyaba Yasmin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRice production, a cornerstone of universal food security, is increasingly threatened by abiotic stresses. Among these, heavy metals and salinity have emerged as particularly severe constraints, significantly reducing yield potential and compromising grain quality. Owing to the limited understanding of the synergistic effects of these stresses, the grain quality in genetically distinct rice varieties from two key rice growing countries: Pakistan (Super Basmati [SB], Kala Shahkaku 282 [KS]) and Sri Lanka (Ambalanthota AT 362 [AT], Bathalagoda BG 94/1 [BG])was assessed. All the varieties were subjected to optimized concentrations of abiotic stress treatments like, salt (S; 150 mM NaCl), cadmium, (CD; 300 µM CdCl₂), arsenic (AS; 250 µM Na₂AsO₃), and their combinations (COM, S + AS, S + CD, CD + AS). The head rice yield was significantly reduced to 40–45% in KS and AT under combined stress, whereas the degree of milling increased to 42% in AT under combined stress. Among physical traits, average length (r = -0.90) and arithmetic mean of grain (r = -0.83) showed very strong negative correlations with sphericity, while L/B ratio (r = -0.92) exhibited a very strong negative correlation with aspect ratio. High susceptibility was observed in SB and BG for cooking and functional property traits. An increase in volume expansion from 1.6 to 4.5 cm3 and an elongation ratio 1.30 were observed under AS treatments in SB whereas COM increased elongation ratio to 1.35 in BG. Amylose content was strongly reduced by S + CD treatment to 8 mg/ml but increased to 40 mg/ml by S + AS compared with the control value of 30 mg/ml. Gel consistency in BG was also altered, shifting from soft (120 mm) to hard (30 mm) by COM, S + CD, S + AS, CD + AS compared with individual stress. Our study demonstrated that heavy metal and salinity stress differentially impaired the grain quality in economically significant rice varieties. Introduction

Rice (Oryza sativa), a staple crop for nearly half of the global population1, is primarily grown in Asia, with cultivation expanding over 40% area since 1961 from 115 million hectares2. The rising rice demand, driven by socioeconomic growth and health awareness3, has reinforced the need for effective strategies to improve grain quality as a long-standing research priority4. The grain quality in rice comprises nutritional components, milling, appearance, and cooking-eating attributes, which vary across regions and among different varieties2,3. Key indicators include amylose content, alkali spreading score and gel consistency5. Milling, essential for producing edible grains, affects both nutritional value and quality traits such as yield, head rice recovery, and appearance6. Commercial popularity in rice depends on cooking quality, sensory traits such as aroma, texture, taste, and consistency of grains, which are influenced by environmental and cultivation factors5,6. Importantly, the abiotic stresses have been reported to affect these quality attributes negatively2–5.

Salt stress is a leading factor that limits rice crop production worldwide reducing the yield by 30–50% annually in salty lands5. It particularly impacts the reproductive stages and the rice grain quality traits like amylose content, alkalinity stress, gel consistency which are highly sensitive to salinity7. Salinity levels exceeding 3.0 dS/m significantly reduce rice yield, particularly by decreasing panicle number, and can lead to a 50% reduction in grain yield at 6.0 dS/m7–9. Salt-tolerant cultivars mitigate yield loss through higher grain filling and weight and maintain superior milling, appearance, and cooking quality compared to susceptible cultivars10.

In addition to salinity, heavy metal contamination is another major factor impacting rice crop health and productivity6. Rice tends to accumulate higher concentrations of arsenic (AS) and cadmium (CD) in its tissues compared to other crops11 with reported grain concentrations exceeding the permissible limits12. Cadmium is absorbed via roots and translocated to grains, phloem mediated transport and ultimately affects the grain quality5,13,14. Cadmium negatively impacts aromatic and non-aromatic rice grain quality, significantly by reducing protein, amylose content, milled rice percentage and brown rice rates6,15,16.

Arsenic, a toxic non-essential metalloid, poses serious risks to rice yield, quality, and human health, potentially affecting 5.5 billion consumers worldwide7,17. Rice can accumulate up to ten times more AS than other cereals, particularly under flooded conditions that enhance AS solubility and uptake18. Silicon transporters inadvertently facilitate arsenite uptake, making rice a key dietary source of arsenic even in areas with low AS in water. Grain AS varies with variety and environmental condition, with genetic factors playing a significant role19. Arsenic contamination in rice significantly alters grain quality attributes, elemental profiles, and cooking properties. High-yielding and indigenous varieties respond differently due to strong genotype environment interactions18.

The literature indicates that studies on rice grain quality are ranked second in frequency. Mainly, all such studies have analyzed grain physiology and mineral accumulation while fewer have focused on the effects of abiotic stress on rice grain quality. However, a few papers have analyzed grain quality under salinity21 and heavy metal stress, particularly involving cadmium and arsenic. Although the detrimental impacts of salinity18, cadmium, and arsenic stresses on rice grain quality have been widely reported in isolation21, their combined effects, particularly during the sensitive growth phase from panicle initiation to maturity, remain poorly understood. With climate change and soil degradation increasingly exposing rice crops to multiple concurrent stresses, there is a pressing need to investigate how these interactions affect grain quality. To address this gap, the present study systematically evaluated the individual and combined impacts of salinity, cadmium, and arsenic on milling, physical, cooking, and functional quality traits in economically important rice cultivars. The findings provide novel insights into the interplay between abiotic stress and rice grain quality and highlight the synergistic effects of heavy metals and salinity in rice yield attributes.

Varietal performance assessment under stress conditions

All experimental findings were incorporated to support informed preferences for variety selection following abiotic stress exposure.

The KS variety demonstrated the highest overall adaptability, scoring four in milling quality, physical properties, and functional traits. In contrast, SB was the most adversely affected variety, receiving scores of two for milling and cooking properties, one for physical properties, and two for functional traits. The Sri Lankan variety AT received the highest rating (4) for cooking properties but scored three across the remaining traits. BG was ranked third overall in adaptability under stress conditions (Fig. 5).

Abiotic stress risk assessment of the selected rice varieties

Distinct variations among treatments were observed, suggesting that stress combinations influenced quality parameters in unique ways.

Based on the findings, the COM treatment exerted a highly adverse effect on all varieties in terms of milling properties, physical attributes, and cooking quality, while its impact on functional traits was moderate. AS treatment significantly affected milling and physical properties but showed moderate effects on cooking and functional trait. The S treatment strongly affected cooking and functional traits but had only mild effects on milling and physical properties. Lastly, CD treatment had a pronounced impact on functional traits, moderate influence on cooking properties, and mild effects on milling and physical parameters. Discussion

Rice grain quality is a complex trait that involves the interaction of genetics and environmental factors. The major components, milling, physical attributes, nutritional and cooking properties are particularly important which are affected by the stress factors6. It is crucial to investigate these components and identify the most damaging stress. This study evaluated rice grain quality across key parameters, including milling, physical (appearance), cooking, functional (processing), and sensory traits under salt, cadmium, arsenic, and their combined effects in economically important rice cultivars. All treatments led to an increase in the degree of milling across varieties compared to their controls. Previous studies have shown that a higher degree of milling can reduce heavy metal concentrations in rice grains, with reductions observed in Cd (3%), Pb (29%), Cu (6%), and Zn (15%)20. Additionally, under 51.3 mM NaCl, significant decreases in brown rice rate, milled rice rate, and head rice rate have been reported9,21. In present study, all treatments led to an increase in the degree of milling across varieties compared to their controls. The highest degree (41%) of milling was observed under COM in the AT. The lowest milling degree (6.0%) was recorded in SB under SCD conditions. All stress treatments significantly reduce paddy weight, with cadmium having a more pronounced effect in BG (5.6 g to 4.12 g) and KS (5.4 g to 4.14 g). Brown rice weight was also significantly reduced under all treatments in all varieties. Individual arsenic and its combination with salt stress strongly affected the brown rice weight in BG grains probably because of the presence of DMA in endosperm which might have aggravated by the As treatment6. The recently characterized peptide transporter OsPTR7 has been implicated in arsenic detoxification, suggesting a mechanistic role in mitigating heavy metal stress in rice22. Similarly, the strong effects of CD and SCD treatments on KS and SB appear to be mediated through the waxy gene pathway, which contributes to reducing white belly formation in brown rice under Cd stress23, as also supported by our findings. Furthermore, during grain filling, Cd-induced suppression of pyruvate kinase genes (OsPK1, OsPK4, OsPK9, and OsPK10) disrupts energy metabolism and alters the ABA/GA hormonal balance, ultimately compromising grain yield and quality24. A similar trend was observed in SB under Salt stress, KS under cadmium, AT under COM where significant polished rice weight loss occurred. However, significant reductions occurred under AS in BG (43%), because during early grain filling, arsenic mainly enters the grain directly but later moves circumferentially. Arsenite accumulates in the bran aleurone layer near the ovular vascular trace, where it is sequestered as arsenite glutathione complexes in vacuoles, limiting further movement. At higher arsenic levels, this capacity can be exceeded, allowing arsenic to reach the sub-aleurone layer24 that might have resulted in reduced grain yield in our study. The findings of current study are consistent with prior studies where salt stress led to a marked reduction in head rice25.

Grain appearance, a key determinant of rice quality and marketability, is the most visibly affected trait under abiotic stress because it primarily alters the physical properties of the grain through changes in the spikelet hull and endosperm tissues26. In the present study, grain length and width were significantly reduced under all stress treatments across varieties compared to controls. This could be regulated by the ubiquitin proteasome system, where TUD1-D1/RGA1, OsGRX8/WG1, GW6a/OsIHA1, and HDR3 promote grain length, while CLG1 represses GS3 to reduce length26. In this study, the observed grain length reduction in all varieties might possibly be due to both Cd transport and its accumulation in roots. Rice varieties have been reported to be different in Cd uptake and involve various mechanisms, like sequestration through cell wall binding, vacuolar storage, and regulation of OsHMA3 gene. Indica rice, lacking functional OsHMA3, has shown higher Cd levels in roots and shoots27. These insights highlight root-based detoxification and OsHMA3 role in limiting Cd transfer to edible grains28 and grain reduction29.

Salinity stress markedly reduced grain thickness through the auxin signaling pathway (via OsAUX3 and OsYUC11)5. The gibberellin (GA) pathway, mediated by GW6, has also been reported to reduce grain thickness, while the cytokinin pathway, involving OsPUP4 and BG3, can contribute further to reducing the grain thickness30. Our observation showed that all stress treatments led to a reduction in thickness. The most significant decrease was observed in KS, SB, and BG varieties as compared to control, but the AT variety was comparatively less affected. Among all treatments and varieties, the COM, S treatment consistently resulted in a significant reduction in thickness might have occurred through the involvement of cross talk between auxin and gibberellin as in one of our related experiments the genes related to these hormones were significantly higher in these treatment groups (data not published). Furthermore, heavy metals and salt stress have been reported to affect these factors22,31–33. However, AT grain size was affected significantly by salt stress as this is a salt sensitive variety34.

Cooking quality attributes are known to be affected by both genetic and environmental factors. It has been reported that salinity stress up-regulates rice storage proteins (glutelin, prolamin, globulin, and albumin), consequently impacting cooking qualities35. Modulation of storage protein composition and starch accumulation alters significantly under stress conditions, critically influencing rice elongation ratio and volume expansion through MARK signaling pathway36. Findings from the current study further highlight how environmental conditions can significantly alter cooking-related traits. Our results are consistent with the findings of Obata et al36., confirming that the elongation ratio increased significantly under all treatments in all varieties, with S, COM and CDAS showing the most pronounced effects and volume expansion increased markedly in SB varieties compared to all varieties.

Arsenic is known to affect silicon transport and cooking properties such as increasing curling grains, bursting grains, reducing elongation ratio37. Differences between varieties in aquaporin expression affect how much arsenic is absorbed, while the plant nodes help block As (III) from moving into the grains, reducing its buildup38. The observed change in our study might be linked with this phenomenon.

Environmental stress disrupts starch deposition, reducing amylose content and other grain qualities39. Amylose synthesis is mediated by granule-bound starch synthase I (GBSSI), encoded by reduction40. Interestingly, amylose content of the study grains was significantly reduced by stress treatments, particularly S and COM in SB and S and CD in KS, while AS had a moderate effect across varieties except BG. According to Umadevi et al.43., gel consistency shows a significant negative correlation with both volume expansion ratio and water uptake41. Rice varieties with low gelatinization exhibit poor kernel elongation, volume expansion, and water uptake, leading to decreased consumer preference42. The observed intra- and inter-varietal variations in gel consistency (GC) values across locations may stem from either starch polymer aggregation forming rigid rice gels9 or the interactive effects of minor genes (waxy, BEIII, pullulanase) and potential arsenic exposure, though this requires further validation43. Additionally, OsBadh2 suppresses 2-acetyl-1-pyrroline (2-AP) production, consequently reducing gel consistency under cadmium stress conditions27. Salt treatment increased gel hardness in SB and AT, while it softened the gel in KS. In BG, gel consistency was altered only by the treatment CDAS, shifting from soft to hard through above mentioned pathway in study grain. Rice aroma is primarily regulated by the Osbadh2 gene, which functions under homozygous recessive conditions and enhances 2-acetyl-1-pyrroline (2AP) production but reduces salt stress tolerance due to its dual role in arsenic stress response27. The aromatic variety SB lost aroma by all treatments while COM induced aroma to BG and COM, CD induced aroma in KS while COM reduced aroma in AT.

Overall, our finding revealed that during genotypic performance KS variety showed best performance by showing tolerance to other except CD and COM. Therefore, we hypothesize that the salt tolerant variety KS-282 maintains salinity tolerance through coordinated regulation of ROS and homeostasis. Under salt stress, OsRbohA and OSCA genes (OsOSCA1.2, OsOSCA3.1) mediate Ca²⁺ influx and ROS/NO signaling, while higher activities of ascorbate peroxidase and catalase prevent prolonged ROS accumulation during grain formation. Stress-responsive transcription factors OsSERF1 and OsDREB2A are activated exclusively in KS-282, supporting adaptive gene expression. In addition, early and strong induction of genes maintaining a high K⁺/Na⁺ ratio stabilizes ionic balance. Together, these mechanisms suggest that KS-282 tolerance is driven by controlled ROS signaling, efficient antioxidant defense, transcriptional regulation, and potassium homeostasis. However, this hypothesis is matching with author Fukuda et al35. in study. The variations in genotypes in response to salt stress could be due to the accumulation and changes in activities of starch synthetic enzymes. In KS-282, salt tolerance may be mediated by the upregulation of starch-synthetic enzymes such as granule-bound starch synthase and starch branching enzymes. This enhances starch and amylopectin accumulation, supporting grain filling under salt stress. Further, our findings align with the hypothesized mechanisms of KS-282 tolerance to As. The genes AsTol6.1, AsTol6.2, and AsTol10.1 act epistatically to confer arsenic tolerance. Plasma membrane intrinsic proteins OsPIP2;4, OsPIP2;6 and OsPIP2;7 enhance As³⁺ tolerance during short-term exposure. These transporters may also play an important role in overall tolerance. In addition, exogenous auxin improves As³⁺ tolerance in rice, suggesting its role in reducing arsenic stress.

In contrast, the finding for the susceptibility of KS-282 to Cd stress is matching with Obata et al.36 to uncontrolled Cd uptake and poor detoxification. In this variety, Cd entry through transporters such as OsNRAMP5, OsIRT1, and OsZIP family members may not be well restricted. Weak sequestration in root vacuoles and reduced binding to cell wall components or thiol compounds (phytochelatins, glutathione) allow more Cd to move to shoots and grains via OsHMA2. Once accumulated, Cd triggers excessive ROS production. The low activity of antioxidant enzymes such as SOD, CAT, and APX fails to prevent oxidative damage. Cd also disturbs nutrient balance and suppresses key metabolic genes like OsPKs, disrupting energy metabolism. This process is influenced by MAPK signaling through auxin variation and changes in cell cycle-related and hormonal regulation during grain filling. Together, these mechanisms likely explain the reduction in grain weight, size, and overall quality under Cd stress. Due to Cd susceptibility KS might have shown susceptibility to COM stress. In addition, stress risk assessment showed that COM stress had a much more severe effect, as multiple mechanisms were triggered when stresses were combined. However, further molecular experiments are needed to explore this in detail.

Conclusion

This study reveals that abiotic stresses S, CD, AS, SAS, SCD, CDAS, COM significantly impair rice quality traits, including milling yield, grain physical properties, cooking behavior, and functional attributes. The COM having the most severe impact, particularly on BG and SB varieties, which showed high susceptibility to quality degradation. The KS and AT variety demonstrated greater resilience, with KS maintaining stability in milling and physical traits and AT exhibiting tolerance in cooking and functional properties. Physical grain traits were severely compromised under COM, CDAS, and SCD, though AT showed relative stability, while cooking traits varied stress-dependently, displaying negative correlations between volume expansion and bursting grains but positive links to stickiness. Functional properties were most affected by COM and CDAS, with variety-specific responses revealing SB as most impacted by SCD, BG by AS, KS by CD, and AT by COM across all traits. Ultimately, KS emerged as the most adaptable variety across all quality domains, followed by AT (excelling in cooking traits), whereas, SB, despite being aromatic and texturally desirable under control conditions, were the most vulnerable to stress-induced quality deterioration. Further research should focus on molecular and physiological investigations to elucidate the underlying mechanisms conferring resilience in KS and AT. Integrating omics approaches can accelerate the key biomarkers associated with grain quality maintenance and can provide genetic resources for future breeding programs.

Material and methodology

Plant material and growth conditions

Four indica rice varieties were used in this study, comprising two Pakistani varieties Super Basmati (SB), Kala Shahkaku 282 (KS), and two Sri Lankan varieties Ambalanthota AT 362 (AT), Bathalagaoda BG 94/1 (BG). The seeds of Pakistani and Sri Lankan rice varieties were provided by the National Agriculture Research Center (NARC) and Rice Research Station, Sammanthurai, Sri Lanka respectively. Seeds were sown in June 2024 at the National Institute of Genomics and Advanced Biotechnology, NARC, Islamabad, Pakistan. Pots of 2′ height and 2′ diameter size, (200 in number) were filled with 2 kg of peat moss and soil mixture in a 1:1 ratio. Seeds were soaked and transferred to Hoagland based hydroponic media and after 14 days, 6 seedlings were transferred into each pot30. The plants were grown in natural field conditions. Panicle initiation was monitored, and abiotic stress treatments were imposed when approximately 50% of the plants had reached panicle formation stage. From four rice varieties line, 24 healthy pots were selected per variety, and three pots were allocated for each treatment. Eight pots, including control, were used per replicate, with each treatment replicated three times. Stress treatments were prepared in liquid form, including 150 mM sodium chloride (NaCl),300µM cadmium (CdCl2), 250µM sodium arsenite (Na2AsO3), as well as dual combinations (S + AS), (S + CD), (CD + AS), and a triple stress combination (COM). One liter of each solution was directly applied to designated pots. Plants were allowed to reach maturity, and grain samples were harvested from three plants in each pot, in early October 2024. Three replicates were used for each treatment group. The harvested grains were air dried and stored at room temperature for three months. Subsequently, milling quality, physical quality, cooking properties, functional traits, and sensory attributes were evaluated with modifications44.

Milling analysis of rice grains

The harvested control and treated paddies (250 in number) were weighted and milled at Food Science Research Institute (FSRI), National Agricultural Research Centre (NARC), Islamabad, Pakistan using a Paddy Husker Machine, Model KHL-250, Mitsubishi Electric Automation (Thailand) Co, Ltd. The weight of brown rice milled rice, and head rice were determined according to the standards of FSRI. The degree of milling and head rice yield were calculated based on the method described with slightly modifications20.

DoM=(1 – (Wt. of polished or milled rice/Wt. of brown rice)*100.

HRY= (Wt.of head rice/Wt. of paddy rice)*100.

Determination of physical properties

The physical parameters, like average length(L), width(W), whiteness index, length/breadth(B) (L/B) ratio, colour index (CI), chalkiness(C) of the control and treated paddy and head rice(kernel) were determined by placing grains in the Micron Epson perfection v600 photo scanner at FSRI, NARC, Islamabad, Pakistan and following FSRI standards. Grain thickness (T) was measured for ten randomly selected grains from each variety by using digital Vernier caliper. The geometric mean diameter (GMD), arithmetic means diameter (AMD), and equivalent diameter (ED), grain volume (V), surface area (Sa), breadth(B) sphericity (Sp), aspect ratio (Ra), size (S), and free space were calculated using the formulas provided by45. The moisture was measured using Moisture analyzer PM-450 at FSRI and classification of rice were by followed by the46. The bulk density (BD) of paddy and kernel were determined using the protocol outlined47. A pre-weight cylinder filled with one hundred grains or paddy were filled and leveled. The bulk density of paddy and kernels were calculated as the ratio of mass to volume occupied. The protocol for measuring true density using 20cm3 of toluene displaced by the kernels was followed as described by Qadir and Wani 2023. The porosity of kernels and paddy was calculated using the specified formula47.

Geometric Mean Diameter, GMD= (LWT)1/3

Arithmetic Mean Diameter, AMD= (L + W + T)/3

Equivalent Diameter, ED=[L(W + T)2/4]1/3

Volume, V = 0.25[π/6) L(W + T)2]

Surface area, Sa = πBL/(2 L-B)

Breadth, B= (WT)1/2

Sphericity, Sp= (LWT)1/3/L

Aspect ratio, Ra = W/L

Size, (S)= (L*B*L)1/3

Free space = Surface area of paddy – Surface area of kernel

Bulk density (BD) = Mass of sample/Total volume

True density (TD) = Mass of sample/Volume of displaced toluene (cm3)

Porosity= (1-BD/TD) *100

Assessment of cooking properties

Volume expansion ratio

The water displacement method was used to determine the volume expansion by measuring cylinder. About 3 g of rice grains were poured into the measuring cylinder containing 30 ml water and the total volume was observed (A). Then an initial increased volume was recorded (A-30). After that, the same grains were soaked for 20 min and cooked for 60 min in a water bath at 90 °C. Then the 3 g cooked rice were placed in 30 ml water taken in 100 ml measuring cylinder. Subsequently, an increase in volume of water (B) was measured and a volume raise was recorded (B-30). The volume expansion ratio was calculated as described by slight modifications48.

Elongation ratio

The initial length of ten grains was measured using a scalar or slide caliper. The same grains were then soaked for 20 min and subsequently cooked for 30 min. After cooking, the length of each intact grain was measured again. Then the average length was calculated by dividing the total length by number of grains. The elongation ratio was determined by divining the average length of cooked grains by the average length of the raw grains. Additionally, the percentage of grains exhibiting bursting, curling and stickiness was calculated by multiplying the number of affected grains by49.

Aroma

Freshly milled rice powder (1 g) from each variety was mixed with 10 mL of 1.7% KOH, covered, kept at room temperature for 1 h and then assessed for aroma by an expert panel50.

Evaluation of cooking processing functional properties

Gel consistency

Gel consistency was measured following a modified method51. Rice flour (0.1 g) was mixed with 0.026 ml of 95%ethanol containing 0.025% thymol blue was added to each tube and mixed thoroughly. The mixture was gently vortexed, followed by the addition of 2 ml of 0.2 N KOH, vortexed again. The tubes were then placed in a boiling water bath at 92 °C for 7–10 min, cooled at room temperature for 5 min, and subsequently transferred to an ice bath for 15–20 min. After cooling, the tubes were placed horizontally on clean graph paper on a flat laboratory bench for 30–60 min to allow gel formation. Gel length was measured and categorized (Supplementary table S6). All tests were performed in triplicate to ensure accuracy52.

Amylose content

Amylose content was determined using the modified method of52. Rice powder (0.1 g) was gelatinized with 1 ml, 95% ethanol and 9 ml of 1 N NaOH were added. The mixture was boiled in the water bath for 10 min to gelatinize the starch and allowed it to cool, diluted to volume, and reacted with 1 ml of 1 N acetic acid and 2 ml of iodine. After 20 min, absorbance at 620 nm was measured, and amylose content was calculated from a standard curve with modified method48.

Alkali spread digestion

Alkali digestion was evaluated following Prakash et al.48 with minor modifications. Ten head rice grains per variety were incubated in 1.7% KOH at 30°C for 24–25 h. Disintegration was visually rated using IRRI standard evaluation system (Supplementary table S7) with tests performed in triplicate for accuracy.

Performance test and risk assessment

A preference test was conducted to evaluate the overall adaptability of paddy varieties under multiple abiotic stress conditions. The questionnaire was designed to assess which variety performed best based on integrated results from milling properties, physical appearance, cooking characteristics, and functional traits. The relative risk associated with each abiotic stress treatment was assessed for all varieties to identify the most detrimental conditions. A rating system was applied to all seven stress treatments across the evaluated parameters, including milling properties, physical characteristics, cooking quality, and functional traits27.

Data analysis

All experiments were conducted in triplicate. Data were analyzed using two-way ANOVA with repeated measures and multiple comparisons, employed by GraphPad Prism (version 10.4.2(633) and R language (version 4.3.3). This approach was chosen due to the inclusion of four varieties and eight including control and treatment and varying numbers of traits with a significant level (α) of 0.05. Tukey’s test (α = of 0.05) was used to identify significant differences among samples29. All the graphs were generated using R and the data reduction was performed through principal component analysis (PCA), heatmap, cluster dendrogram. Additionally, Pearson correlation analyses were conducted to assess relationships between varieties and treatments53.
