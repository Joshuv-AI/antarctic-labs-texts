Intraplaque Hemorrhage in Chronic Anterior Circulation Arterial Occlusion: Associated Factors and Vessel‐Specific Patterns on High‐Resolution Magnetic Resonance Imaging
Authors: Yaping Meng, Lingmin Zhao, Tao Chen, Shoufeng Liu, Shenna Niu, Chengcheng Cui, Yajing Wang, Wei Wang, Yilin Ma, Yue Zhang, Yuxin Xing, Kai Zheng, Ying Cai, Jieli Chen, Jialing Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic anterior circulation arterial occlusion, particularly involving the internal carotid artery or middle cerebral artery (MCA), carries a persistent risk of ischemic events. Intraplaque hemorrhage (IPH) is a marker of plaque instability, but its prevalence, vessel‐specific distribution, and clinical correlates in chronic anterior circulation arterial occlusion remain unclear.MethodsWe retrospectively enrolled 210 patients with chronic anterior circulation arterial occlusion who underwent high‐resolution magnetic resonance imaging between January 2024 and March 2025. IPH was identified by T1‐weighted hyperintensity within the vessel wall. Clinical and vascular risk factors, imaging, and laboratory factors associated with IPH were analyzed using logistic regression, including analyses stratified by occlusion site.ResultsIPH was detected in 86 (41.0%) and was more frequent in internal carotid artery than MCA occlusion (60.5% versus 38.4%). Patients with IPH were more frequently male, had more smoking history, lower platelet counts, higher creatinine levels, and more recent acute ischemic stroke. In multivariable analysis, recent acute ischemic stroke (adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 2.08 [95% CI, 1.10–3.94]) and occlusion location (MCA versus internal carotid artery, aOR, 0.51 [95% CI, 0.27–0.95]) were independently associated with IPH. Intracranial internal carotid artery occlusion showed a lower likelihood of IPH in univariable analysis, with directionally consistent associations after adjustment (OR, 0.26 [95% CI, 0.09–0.79]), whereas recent acute ischemic stroke remained independently associated with IPH in MCA occlusion (aOR, 3.12 [95% CI, 1.19–8.20]).ConclusionsIPH is common in chronic anterior circulation arterial occlusion and shows vessel‐specific patterns. Its association with recent ischemic stroke, particularly in MCA occlusion, suggests persistent plaque activity in some chronically occluded arteries. High‐resolution magnetic resonance imaging may help characterize plaque phenotype and improve risk assessment in chronic occlusive cerebrovascular disease.

Chronic anterior circulation arterial occlusion (ACAO), most commonly involving the internal carotid artery (ICA) or middle cerebral artery (MCA), represents an advanced manifestation of intracranial atherosclerotic disease and is associated with a substantial risk of recurrent ischemic events. Ischemic stroke (IS) in the setting of chronic artery occlusion may arise from hemodynamic insufficiency, artery‐to‐artery embolism, or mixed mechanisms, with atherosclerosis constituting the predominant underlying pathology. 1 , 2 Nevertheless, randomized clinical trials, including the COSS (Carotid Occlusion Surgery Study) 3 and the CMOSS (Chinese Middle Cerebral Artery Occlusion Stenting Study), 4 have consistently demonstrated that revascularization strategies fail to reduce the risk of recurrent stroke compared with optimized medical therapy. Even among patients with impaired collateral compensation, the risk of ipsilateral recurrent IS may reach 22.7% within 2 years. 3 Collectively, these findings underscore that hemodynamics alone is insufficient to explain stroke vulnerability in chronic occlusion, highlighting the need to identify additional structural and biological factors of risk in ACAO.

Intraplaque hemorrhage (IPH) is a defining feature of advanced atherosclerosis and an important marker of plaque activity and instability. 5 , 6 , 7 Extensive evidence from studies of carotid artery stenosis demonstrates that IPH occurs across all degrees of luminal narrowing, is enriched in symptomatic mild‐to‐moderate stenosis, 8 and independently predicts ipsilateral recurrent stroke or transient ischemic attack (TIA), 9 , 10 , 11 often outperforming stenosis severity in identifying high‐risk lesions. 12 Mechanistically, IPH is thought to arise from rupture of fragile intraplaque neovessels, leading to hemoglobin extravasation, erythrocyte membranes accumulation, cholesterol deposition, macrophage infiltration, and progressive expansion of the necrotic core, which collectively promote plaque destabilization. 13 , 14 , 15 However, existing evidence has largely focused on stenotic lesions, whereas the prevalence, associated factors, and clinical relevance of IPH in the chronic occlusions remain insufficiently characterized.

High‐resolution magnetic resonance imaging (HR‐MRI) provides a sensitive and noninvasive modality for detecting IPH and characterizing plaque components, with imaging showing strong concordance with histopathological. 16 , 17 , 18 Hyperintensity on T1‐weighted fat‐suppressed sequences (HST1) reliably reflects recent IPH (<6 weeks) and has been linked to subsequent ischemic events. 19 To date, the vessel‐specific characteristics of intracranial occlusions on HR‐MRI remain incompletely characterized, particularly regarding differences between extracranial and intracranial ICA segments and segment‐specific MCA occlusions.

Atherosclerosis is a dynamic and multifactorial process driven by endothelial dysfunction, lipid oxidation, inflammation, and oxidative stress. 20 , 21 Clinical factors including recent acute IS (AIS), metabolic abnormalities, and hematologic biomarkers may be related to plaque activity and instability; however, their role in the context of chronic occlusion—and whether such associations differ between ICA and MCA territories—remain unclear. Notably, the reported prevalence of IPH in intracranial arteries, particularly the MCA is substantially lower than that observed in the extracranial carotid circulation. 22 , 23 Yet, comparative risk evaluations of IPH burden and associated clinical profiles between ICA and MCA occlusions in the chronic occlusive disease are lacking, limiting accurate phenotyping and risk assessment in ACAO.

Therefore, this study aimed to characterize the prevalence and distribution of HR‐MRI‐detected IPH in patients with chronic ACAO, to compare vessel‐specific patterns between ICA and MCA occlusions, and to identify clinical factors independently associated with IPH.

METHODS

Data Availability

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study Population

We retrospectively enrolled patients with chronic ACAO admitted to Tianjin Huanhu Hospital between January 2024 and March 2025, applying a consecutive‐case design to minimize selection bias. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board, and the requirement for informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective nature of the study.

All patients underwent HR‐MRI during index hospitalization for evaluation of arterial occlusion and vessel wall characteristics. Brain computed tomography/MRI was reviewed to determine the occurrence of AIS or TIA within the vascular territory corresponding to the occluded artery.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) chronic arterial occlusion defined as occlusion duration ≥4 weeks based on prior vascular imaging and well‐documented clinical history; (3) chronic occlusion of the ICA, MCA, or anterior cerebral artery confirmed by HR‐MRI; (4) for symptomatic patients, ischemic symptoms (stroke or TIA) attributable to the vascular territory of the occluded artery; and (5) absence of large territorial infarction, defined as involvement of >50% of the MCA territory on computed tomography or MRI.

Exclusion criteria included (1) acute occlusion (<4 weeks), including patients who had undergone intravenous thrombolysis or mechanical thrombectomy; (2) imaging features suggestive of acute intraluminal thrombus, fresh intramural hematoma, or arterial dissection; (3) nonatherosclerotic vasculopathies, including suspected cardioembolic sources (eg, atrial fibrillation, rheumatic heart disease, congenital heart disease), vasculitis, arterial dissection, or moyamoya disease; (4) active malignancy or severe dysfunction/failure of major organs; and (5) incomplete clinical or laboratory data, or contraindications to MRI. Definition of Chronic Occlusion

CAO was defined as an occlusion duration ≥4 weeks, based on a standardized integrated assessment combining prior vascular imaging and predefined clinical‐imaging criteria. When available, prior vascular imaging (including carotid ultrasound, MR angiography, computed tomography angiography, or digital subtraction angiography) performed ≥4 weeks before or after HR‐MRI, demonstrating persistent arterial occlusion, was considered direct evidence of chronicity. In the absence of definitive imaging confirmation, chronic occlusion was adjudicated based on clinical presentation and imaging features suggestive of chronic hypoperfusion, as detailed in the Data S1.

Clinical Variables, Demographics, and Laboratory Data Collection

Demographic characteristics, vascular risk factors, clinical variables, and laboratory parameters were systematically extracted from electronic medical records. Definitions of vascular risk factors adhered to current international guidelines. Prior cerebrovascular disease was defined as any history of IS, hemorrhagic stroke, or TIA confirmed by neuroimaging. Hypertension and diabetes were defined by a documented medical history or ongoing treatment. Coronary artery disease was defined as a history of angina or myocardial infarction. Smoking was defined as current or prior tobacco use for ≥6 months. Patients were classified as symptomatic if they experienced TIA or IS within the preceding 12 months attributable to the vascular territory of the occluded artery. Patients with isolated dizziness and no focal neurological symptoms were considered asymptomatic. 2 Recent AIS was defined as an IS occurring within 2 weeks before admission.

Neurological severity at admission was assessed using the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale. Laboratory testing was performed during index hospitalization by certified laboratory technicians who were blinded to imaging and clinical outcomes. Laboratory parameters included hematologic indices, biochemical markers, and lipid profiles.

HR‐MRI Screening and Image Analysis

All HR‐MRI examinations were performed using a 3.0‐T scanner (Prisma or Skyra, Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany) equipped with a 64‐channel head coil. The imaging protocol included high‐resolution vessel wall imaging, time‐of‐flight MR angiography, and conventional brain MRI sequences such as T1‐weighted imaging, T2‐weighted imaging, and diffusion‐weighted imaging. Detailed imaging parameters are provided in Table S1.

Time‐of‐flight MR angiography was used to assess luminal occlusion, whereas high‐resolution vessel wall imaging and T1WI were used for vessel wall evaluation. The ICA was anatomically classified into extracranial (C1) and intracranial (C2–C7) segments. IPH was defined as hyperintense signal within the plaque on T1‐weighted imaging, exceeding 150% of the signal intensity of reference tissue, consistent with established criteria. 21 , 23 , 24 Plaque regions of interest (ROIs) were manually placed at the site of maximal wall thickening or peak intraplaque signal. Reference ROIs were positioned in ipsilateral adjacent cortical gray matter for intracranial arteries and in the sternocleidomastoid muscle for extracranial carotid plaques. To reduce variability related to coil sensitivity and spatial signal inhomogeneity, reference ROIs were located as close as anatomically feasible to the plaque, and plaque‐to‐reference mean signal intensity (SI) ratios were calculated. ROI placement and lesion localization were confirmed across multiple imaging planes. Representative IPH‐positive and IPH‐negative examples across vascular territories, including ROI placement and signal intensity measurements, are shown in Figures S1 to S5.

Conditions associated with T1 hyperintensity unrelated to IPH, including acute intraluminal thrombus, intraluminal hematoma, or arterial dissection, were carefully excluded based on characteristic imaging features. 24 Morphological criteria, including lesion location, eccentricity, and limited longitudinal extent, were used to differentiate IPH from intraluminal thrombus. Detailed imaging criteria and representative examples are provided in the Data S2 and Figure S6.

All HR‐MRI images were independently reviewed by 2 experienced neuroradiologists who were blinded to clinical and laboratory data. Interobserver agreement for IPH identification was excellent, with a Cohen's κ coefficient of 0.91. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY). Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and are presented as mean±SD or median (interquartile range [IQR]) as appropriate. Between‐group comparisons were performed with the independent‐samples t test for normally distributed variables and the Mann–Whitney U test for nonnormally distributed variables. Categorical variables were summarized as counts (percentages) and compared using the chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate.

Variables with P<0.10 in univariable analyses, together with clinically relevant covariates, were entered into multivariable logistic regression models to identify factors independently associated with IPH. Subgroup analyses were performed stratified by occlusion site (ICA versus MCA). Hierarchical multivariable models were constructed: Model 1 adjusted for age, sex, and subgroup‐specific clinical factors (ICA subgroup: occlusion segment; MCA subgroup: recent AIS); Model 2 further adjusted for a composite vascular risk burden score (range 0–3; hypertension, diabetes, and coronary artery disease) and smoking history to reduce model complexity and minimize the risk of overfitting in subgroup analyses. Sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the main findings under alternative model specifications and analytic assumptions. In subgroup analyses, additional full multivariable models including all variables with P<0.10 in univariable analyses were constructed as sensitivity analyses. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs were reported. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors. Interobserver agreement for IPH identification was assessed using Cohen's κ statistic. Before analysis, 5 patients were excluded due to incomplete data for variables included in the primary analyses (Figure 1). The final analytic data set contained no missing values; therefore, no imputation was performed. A 2‐sided P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines.

Within the ICA territory, IPH was more commonly detected in extracranial ICA occlusions compared with intracranial ICA occlusions (48.1% versus 13.5%, P=0.041). Multivariable Analysis of Factors Associated With IPH

Univariate logistic regression identified qualifying artery, recent AIS, sex, smoking history, platelet count, and creatinine as variables associated with IPH (Table 3; Figure 3). In multivariable logistic regression analysis, 2 variables remained independently associated with IPH. Recent AIS was associated with a higher likelihood of IPH (adjusted OR [aOR], 2.08 [95% CI, 1.10–3.94]). Occlusion location was also independently associated with IPH, with MCA occlusion conferring a lower likelihood of IPH compared with ICA occlusion (aOR, 0.51 [95% CI, 0.27–0.95]). No statistically significant interaction was detected between occlusion site and recent AIS (P for interaction >0.05, Table S2). No evidence of multicollinearity was observed, with all variance inflation factor values below accepted thresholds.

Sensitivity Analyses

Given the potential bidirectional relationship between IPH and AIS, sensitivity analyses were performed by excluding patients with recent AIS and those with ACAO. After these exclusions, the association between occlusion site and IPH remained directionally consistent and statistically significant in both univariable and multivariable analyses, as shown in Table S3.

In a sensitivity analysis restricted to imaging‐confirmed chronic occlusion (n=177), the association between recent AIS and IPH remained similar in magnitude, although statistical significance was attenuated (P=0.056 versus 0.024), with a slightly wider CI (95% CI, 0.98–4.42 versus 1.10–3.94), likely reflecting reduced statistical power. The association between MCA occlusion and lower IPH prevalence remained statistically significant and directionally consistent across analyses (aOR, 0.43 [95% CI, 0.22–0.86]), as shown in Table S4.

Subgroup Comparisons According to Occlusion Site

Subgroup analyses stratified by occlusion site are presented in Table S5. Among patients with ICA occlusion, demographics, vascular risk factors, clinical presentation, or laboratory parameters did not differ significantly between the groups with and without IPH. In contrast, within the MCA occlusion subgroup, the group with IPH was more likely to have a smoking history (48.5% versus 20.0%, P=0.004) and recent AIS (63.6% versus 32.3%, P=0.003). The group with IPH also exhibited higher levels of hemoglobin (143.0 versus 137.0 g/L, P=0.024), creatinine (77.0 versus 63.9 μmol/L, P=0.012), and uric acid (348.0 versus 285.0 μmol/L, P=0.032). A higher proportion of patients in the group with IPH had baseline modified Rankin Scale scores of 3to 6, although this difference did not reach statistical significance (modified Rankin Scale 3–6: 30.3% versus 13.8%, P=0.051). Other demographic, clinical, and anatomical characteristics were comparable between groups.

Multivariable Factors Associated With IPH in ICA and MCA Occlusions

Multivariable analyses stratified by occlusion site are shown in Table 4 and Figure 3. In the subgroup with ICA occlusion, intracranial ICA occlusion was associated with a lower likelihood of IPH in univariate analysis (OR, 0.26 [95% CI, 0.09–0.79]). The association remained directionally consistent after multivariable adjustment. Platelet count, total cholesterol, and triglyceride levels were not independently associated with IPH after adjustment.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we systematically characterized demographic, clinical, anatomical, and circulating biomarker correlations of IPH in patients with chronic ACAO. Three principal findings emerged. First, IPH was common in ACAO and occurred more frequently in ICA occlusion than in MCA occlusion. Second, recent AIS was independently associated with IPH, with a more consistent association observed in the subgroup with MCA occlusion. Third, subgroup analyses revealed distinct patterns of factors associated with IPH between ICA and MCA territories, underscoring vascular bed‐specific heterogeneity in plaque features within chronic occlusive disease.

Mechanisms Considerations and Systemic Associations of IPH

The presence of IPH in chronically occluded arteries is biologically plausible and consistent with prior evidence demonstrating that IPH occurs across the full spectrum of luminal disease severity and reflects ongoing plaque activity rather than a quiescent end‐stage lesion. 25 IPH is widely regarded as a key driver of plaque destabilization through hemoglobin‐mediated oxidative stress, macrophage recruitment, endothelial activation, and neovascular proliferation, processes that promote necrotic core expansion and increase vascular permeability. 13 , 26

In the present cohort, patients with IPH were more frequently male and more likely to have a history of smoking, findings consistent with prior studies linking male sex and tobacco exposure to heightened inflammatory burden, increased macrophage infiltration, and rupture‐prone carotid plaques. 27 , 28 , 29 We also observed lower platelet counts and higher creatinine levels in patients with IPH. Reduced platelets may reflect enhanced platelet consumption in the setting of plaque‐associated microthrombosis, 30 whereas elevated creatinine may reflect oxidative stress and endothelial dysfunction linked to renal impairment, potentially promoting microvascular fragility and increasing susceptibility to IPH. 31 , 32 , 33 Although lower platelet counts and higher creatinine levels were observed in univariable analyses, these associations did not remain independent after multivariable adjustment and should be interpreted with caution. Their potential biological relevance warrants further investigation in future prospective and mechanistic studies.

Notably, traditional systemic markers such as serum lipid profiles and circulating inflammatory cell counts, did not differ significantly between the IPH and NIPH groups. This observation suggests that, in the context of chronic occlusion, IPH may be more closely related to local vascular microenvironmental and structural plaque characteristics rather than systemic inflammatory burden alone.

No statistically significant interaction was observed between occlusion site and recent AIS, suggesting that the association between occlusion site and IPH was not modified by recent ischemic events. Estimates involving ACAO should be interpreted with caution due to the limited sample size. Nevertheless, the consistent vessel‐specific patterns observed across sensitivity analyses suggest underlying biological differences between extracranial and intracranial occlusions rather than spurious findings driven by acute ischemic events.

Vessel‐Specific Differences Between ICA and MCA Occlusions

A notable finding of this study is the marked difference in IPH prevalence between ICA and MCA occlusion. IPH was substantially more frequent in the ICA, whereas NIPH was more common in the MCA, highlighting vessel‐specific vulnerability. These differences are likely attributable to fundamental anatomical and hemodynamic distinctions between extracranial and intracranial arteries. 23 The extracranial ICA is characterized by a well‐developed adventitial vasa vasorum network that can extend into atherosclerotic plaques under inflammatory conditions, providing a structural substrate for fragile neovessels prone to leakage and hemorrhage. 34 In contrast, intracranial arteries such as the MCA lack a substantial vasa vasorum, possess a thinner media and denser internal elastic lamina, 35 and are therefore less susceptible to neovascular‐driven hemorrhagic transformation. 36

Hemodynamic factors may further modulate these differences. The extracranial ICA, particularly along its tortuous proximal course, is exposed to complex flow patterns and pulsatile shear stress that favor eccentric plaque formation and microvascular fragility. By contrast, the intracranial MCA is characterized by relatively uniform laminar flow, potentially conferring relative protection against intraplaque neovascular rupture. 37 , 38 , 39 Consistent with these anatomical and physiological conditions, we observed a higher prevalence of IPH in extracranial compared with intracranial ICA occlusions, whereas no segmental differences were detected within the MCA territory.

Divergent Factors Associated With IPH in ICA Versus MCA Occlusions

Subgroup‐specific regression analyses further revealed divergent factors associated with IPH between ICA and MCA occlusions. In ICA occlusion, IPH appeared to be primarily associated with local anatomical factors, with intracranial ICA involvement conferring a lower likelihood of IPH. This pattern suggests that plaque vulnerability in the ICA may be influenced predominantly by structural and biomechanical stressors rather than systemic influences.

In contrast, among patients with MCA occlusion, recent AIS was the most consistently associated factor with IPH. Notably, systemic and metabolic factors, including smoking status and selected hematologic and biochemical markers, were associated with IPH in univariable analyses within the subgroup with MCA occlusion. This finding suggests that intracranial plaques may be more sensitive to systemic metabolic stressors. 23 This is consistent with prior HR‐MRI studies demonstrating that T1 hyperintensity within MCA plaques correlates with ipsilateral ischemic events. 34 , 40

In patients with mild carotid stenosis (<30%), IPH has been shown to independently predict ipsilateral ischemic events, whereas the association is less consistent in moderate‐to‐severe stenosis. 9 , 25 , 41 , 42 , 43 Our findings extend this concept to chronic occlusion by revealing that the relationship between IPH and ischemic events persists, though modified by vascular territory.

Interpretation of the Association Between AIS and IPH

The observed association between recent AIS and IPH should be interpreted with caution given the cross‐sectional design, in which ischemic events and plaque characteristics were assessed at approximately the same time point, precluding determination of temporal sequence. In addition, patients with recent neurological symptoms may have been more likely to undergo detailed vessel wall imaging, introducing potential referral or detection bias. Such mechanisms may increase the likelihood of identifying plaque vulnerability features in symptomatic patients compared with those with more stable or asymptomatic diseases. Consequently, the observed association may partly reflect differences in clinical presentation and imaging referral patterns rather than a strictly causal relationship.

From a biological perspective, the relationship between AIS and IPH may be bidirectional. IPH may represent an active plaque phenotype that predisposes to embolic events, whereas postischemic hemodynamic alterations or inflammatory responses may also influence plaque characteristics and enhance the detectability of IPH. 44 The heterogeneity in the association between IPH and AIS across vascular territories further supports a context‐dependent effect. ICA occlusion is often accompanied by more robust collateral circulation, which may buffer the clinical impact of plaque instability, whereas the MCA has less developed collateral pathways and may therefore be more vulnerable to ischemic events once plaque destabilization occurs. Accordingly, cross‐territory comparisons should be interpreted cautiously, and the primary emphasis should be placed on within‐territory associations.

The robustness of these findings was supported by sensitivity analyses. In analyses restricted to imaging‐confirmed chronic occlusion, the direction and magnitude of the association between AIS and IPH were preserved despite attenuation of statistical significance, likely reflecting reduced statistical power rather than a true null association. In addition, the vessel‐specific patterns remained consistent across analyses, arguing against a purely event‐driven explanation.

We acknowledge that the exact timing of arterial occlusion is often difficult to determine in clinical practice, which may introduce some degree of misclassification and selection bias. However, such misclassification is likely to be nondifferential and would tend to attenuate observed associations rather than generate spurious findings.

Taken together, these findings support a stable association between IPH and AIS while highlighting the limitations of cross‐sectional inference. Prospective longitudinal studies incorporating serial HR‐MRI, particularly in asymptomatic populations, are warranted to clarify the temporal relationship between IPH and AIS. Multicenter studies across diverse clinical settings will also be important to validate the generalizability of these findings.

Strengths and Limitations

This study provides a comprehensive evaluation of IPH in chronic ACAO by integrating clinical characteristics, anatomical features, and circulating biomarkers, with systematic comparison between ICA and MCA occlusions. By demonstrating vessel‐specific differences in IPH prevalence and associated factors, our findings indicate heterogeneity in plaque features across vascular territories in chronic occlusive disease.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective, cross‐sectional design precludes causal inference, and the precise timing of arterial occlusion could not always be determined. Second, although HR‐MRI enabled IPH detection, technical challenges remain for small‐caliber arteries due to spatial resolution limitation and partial volume effects. In addition, signal intensity measurements may be influenced by reference tissue selection and coil inhomogeneity, and the time‐dependent T1 signal of blood products may lead to residual misclassification with subacute thrombus, particularly after recent ischemic events. Other vessel wall features (eg, contrast enhancement, fibrous cap integrity) were not assessed. Third, circulating biomarkers were measured at a single time point, and this was a single‐center study with a modest sample size, therefore, validation in larger, multicenter prospective cohorts is required.

CONCLUSIONS

IPH is a common vessel wall feature in chronic ACAO and occurs more frequently in ICA occlusion than in MCA occlusion, particularly in the extracranial segment. The independent association between IPH and recent AIS, particularly in MCA occlusion, suggests persistent plaque activity in a subset of chronically occluded arteries. These findings highlight the heterogeneity of chronic occlusive cerebrovascular disease and support the potential role of HR‐MRI in characterizing plaque phenotype.
