The zinc–nitric oxide axis in acute brain injury: Convergent oxidative–nitrative stress, peroxynitrite signaling, and therapeutic targets
Authors: Hyun Wook Yang, Min Kyu Park, Hyun Ho Jeong, Sang Won Suh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute brain injuries are characterized by secondary cascades driven by oxidative and nitrative stress. While the roles of excitotoxicity and neuroinflammation in acute brain injury are well recognized, emerging evidence indicates that the interaction between dysregulated zinc (Zn2+) signaling and nitric oxide (NO) biology forms a central metal–redox system that amplifies neuronal injury. Here, we propose the zinc–nitric oxide (Zn–NO) axis as a unified mechanistic framework linking synaptic dysfunction, mitochondrial failure, and neuroinflammatory escalation. Pathological Zn2+ accumulation, arising from vesicular release, intracellular mobilization, and disrupted transporter homeostasis, impairs mitochondrial respiration, enhances reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, and disrupts redox buffering capacity. In parallel, NO derived from neuronal, endothelial, and inducible nitric oxide synthases reacts with superoxide to form peroxynitrite (ONOO−), a potent mediator of oxidative and nitrative damage. Zn2+ and NO converge primarily within mitochondria, where their interaction promotes superoxide production, ONOO− formation, and bioenergetic collapse. This coupling further disrupts glutathione homeostasis, activates NADPH oxidase, destabilizes lysosomal function, and amplifies inflammatory signaling pathways governing microglial activation and blood–brain barrier integrity. These processes drive neuronal death through apoptosis, ferroptosis-associated lipid peroxidation, and inflammation-linked degeneration. In this review, we integrate experimental and translational evidence supporting the finding that the Zn–NO–ONOO- axis is a central driver of redox-dependent injury, and highlight therapeutic strategies targeting this network, including zinc modulation, NO pathway regulation, peroxynitrite scavenging, and mitochondrial protection. In conclusion, targeting the Zn–NO axis as a unified redox hub may limit secondary injury propagation and improve neuroprotection in acute brain injury.Keywords: Zinc–nitric oxide axis, Peroxynitrite, Oxidative and nitrative stress, Zinc dyshomeostasis, Mitochondrial dysfunction, Neuroinflammation

Introduction

Acute brain injuries—such as epilepsy, hypoglycemia, and traumatic brain injury (TBI)—trigger a complex interplay of biochemical cascades that ultimately determine neuronal survival or death [[1], [2], [3]]. Among the many signaling molecules involved, zinc (Zn2+) and nitric oxide (NO) have emerged as central regulators whose interactions shape pathological degeneration and potential avenues for recovery [[4], [5], [6]]. Although each mediator has long been recognized for its individual roles in synaptic transmission, metabolism, and vascular regulation, recent advances have revealed that Zn2+ and NO function not in isolation but as components of an integrated biochemical axis [4]. This Zn–NO axis acts as a decisive determinant of neuronal fate under metabolic and excitotoxic stress.

Under physiological conditions, Zn2+ participates in neuromodulation and synaptic plasticity through tightly regulated vesicular release, whereas NO contributes to neuronal signaling and neurovascular coupling [[7], [8], [9]]. However, in case of homeostatic imbalance, these normally beneficial signals converge into a self-amplifying toxic cascade [9]. Excess Zn2+ released from synaptic vesicles or metallothioneins enters vulnerable neurons and accumulates intracellularly. At moderate concentrations (approximately 10–50 μM), Zn2+ has been shown to impair mitochondrial respiration and enhance reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, partly through disruption of electron transport chain activity and activation of NADPH oxidase [5,10]. As intracellular Zn2+ levels further increase, higher concentrations (approximately 100–300 μM) induce pronounced neuronal injury and cell death [[11], [12], [13]]. Thus, Zn2+ toxicity exhibits a concentration-dependent profile, in which early mitochondrial dysfunction and ROS generation precede overt neuronal degeneration.

Simultaneously, pathological NO production—arising from neuronal nitric oxide synthase (nNOS) activation during hyperexcitation or inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) induction during inflammation—reacts rapidly with superoxide at sites of mitochondrial and enzymatic superoxide generation to form peroxynitrite (ONOO−) [[14], [15], [16]]. This reaction occurs with near diffusion-limited kinetics, resulting in localized accumulation of ONOO−. Notably, ONOO−primarily induces oxidative and nitrative modifications of proteins, lipids, and DNA, rather than nitrosative signaling, thereby exacerbating mitochondrial dysfunction, DNA damage, and lysosomal destabilization [[14], [15], [16]].

These findings demonstrate that Zn2+ and NO do not merely converge on overlapping injury pathways; rather, they interact through recursive feedback loops in which Zn2+ promotes oxidative conditions that enhance NO toxicity, whereas NO- and ONOO−-mediated oxidative and nitrative damage liberates additional Zn2+ from intracellular stores [5,14,17,18]. This cyclical amplification transforms localized biochemical disturbances into widespread neuronal injury, contributing to delayed cell death, persistent inflammation, and long-term functional deficits.

This review integrates current mechanistic insights into Zn2+ and NO biology, emphasizing the emerging concept of the Zn–NO axis as a unified driver of injury across diverse neurological disorders. We discuss how the interplay between Zn2+ dyshomeostasis and aberrant NO signaling contributes to excitotoxicity, oxidative and nitrative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, neuroinflammation, and lysosomal impairment. In addition, we highlight therapeutic strategies targeting this axis, including activation of AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), a key regulator of cellular energy homeostasis; inhibition of phosphodiesterase-4 (PDE4), which modulates cAMP-dependent inflammatory signaling; and modulation of C1q/tumor necrosis factor-related protein 9 (CTRP9), an adiponectin paralog with anti-inflammatory and metabolic regulatory functions. We also discuss emerging approaches such as hexokinase 2 (HK2) modulation, zinc chelation, and mechanogenetic stimulation, which collectively target multiple nodes of the Zn–NO network. By integrating these mechanistic and therapeutic perspectives, this review provides a comprehensive framework to better understand the Zn–NO axis as a central regulator of neuronal injury and highlights it as a promising target for disease-modifying interventions in acute brain injury.

Molecular basis of Zn–NO interactions

The relationship between Zn2+ and NO is best conceptualized not as parallel signaling pathways, but as a tightly coupled metal–redox network. Under pathological conditions, Zn2+ and NO engage in bidirectional interactions that mutually amplify redox stress, forming a nonlinear system that drives neuronal injury. Rather than acting within isolated compartments, coupled Zn–NO operates across synapses, cytosol, mitochondria, and lysosomes, where localized interactions generate self-reinforcing redox feedback loops that determine whether neurons adapt or undergo degeneration [[19], [20], [21]].

Zn2+ mobilization as a trigger for NO-dependent redox amplification

Under physiological conditions, Zn2+ is tightly buffered and compartmentalized [22]. In contrast, pathological stress rapidly increases the intracellular labile Zn2+ pool through vesicular release, metallothionein oxidation, and impaired zinc transport [23,24]. The elevated Zn2+ levels directly prime NO-dependent redox toxicity by disrupting mitochondrial function and enhancing superoxide generation. This creates a permissive redox environment in which NO is efficiently converted into ONOO− [[25], [26], [27], [28]]. Thus, Zn2+ mobilization functions as a critical initiating event that sensitizes neurons to NO-driven oxidative and nitrative injury.

NO as a Zn-sensitive amplifier of redox injury

NO signaling is inherently context-dependent but becomes pathologically amplified in the presence of Zn2+ dysregulation. Under physiological conditions, NO primarily signals through the sGC–cGMP pathway, with S-nitrosylation serving as a secondary regulatory mechanism [16,[29], [30], [31], [32], [33]]. However, under pathological conditions, excess NO reacts rapidly with superoxide—particularly within Zn2+-loaded mitochondria—to generate ONOO−, a potent mediator of oxidative and nitrative damage [25]. Notably, Zn2+ accumulation enhances mitochondrial superoxide production, thereby accelerating ONOO− formation [8,19,34]. This establishes a feed-forward Zn–NO amplification loop, in which Zn2+ promotes NO toxicity, and NO-derived oxidants further exacerbate Zn2+ dyshomeostasis.

Zn–NO coupling as a driver of ferroptotic vulnerability

Zn–NO interactions converge on redox systems that govern ferroptotic susceptibility. Zn2+ accumulation impairs mitochondrial respiration and disrupts glutathione (GSH) homeostasis, while ONOO− induces oxidative and nitrative damage that further compromises antioxidant defenses [3,[35], [36], [37], [38], [39]]. Together, these processes destabilize the GSH/glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) axis, promoting lipid peroxidation [24,40,41]. Thus, ferroptotic neuronal death emerges not from isolated oxidative stress but from integrated Zn–NO redox coupling that drives lipid peroxidation and membrane damage.

Mitochondria as the primary site of Zn–NO convergence

Zn–NO interactions primarily occur in the mitochondria [34,42,43]. Zn2+ inhibits mitochondrial dehydrogenases and increases electron leakage, generating superoxide, while NO inhibits cytochrome c oxidase and slows electron transport [34,[42], [43], [44]]. These combined effects favor rapid ONOO− formation. Consequently, mitochondria transition from bioenergetic centers to sources of redox amplification, driving oxidative and nitrative damage and energetic collapse [19,36].

Lysosomes as amplifiers of Zn–NO-driven redox feedback

Lysosomes act as secondary amplifiers of Zn–NO coupling [45,46]. ONOO−-mediated oxidative damage, together with elevated Zn2+, destabilizes lysosomal membranes, resulting in the release of cathepsins and labile Zn2+ into the cytosol [47,48]. This process reinforces the Zn–NO axis, increasing intracellular Zn2+ burden and further promoting mitochondrial dysfunction and ONOO− generation [25,47,49,50]. In parallel, Zn2+ impairs lysosomal acidification and autophagic flux, preventing the clearance of damaged mitochondria and sustaining redox stress [50,51].

Metallothioneins as redox-sensitive mediators of Zn–NO coupling

Metallothioneins function as redox-sensitive regulators at the Zn–NO interface [52]. ONOO−-mediated oxidation releases bound Zn2+, converting metallothioneins from protective buffers into sources of secondary Zn2+ [47,52], which reinforces the bidirectional nature of Zn–NO coupling.

Context-dependent Zn–NO interactions in plasticity and repair

Despite their central role in injury, Zn2+ and NO also mediate adaptive signaling [20]. At physiological levels, Zn2+ modulates NOS activity, while NO regulates Zn2+ trafficking and redox-sensitive signaling pathways, contributing to synaptic plasticity and neurovascular function [6,8,22,52]. Thus, Zn–NO interactions are inherently context-dependent and require selective therapeutic strategies to suppress pathological redox amplification while preserving physiological signaling (Fig. 1).

Therapeutic targeting of the Zn–NO axis in acute brain injury

The recognition that Zn2+ and NO form an interdependent and self-amplifying injury axis has fundamentally reshaped therapeutic strategies for acute brain injury [5,13,17,53,54]. Zn2+ and NO interact through tightly coupled feedback loops involving mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative and nitrative stress, neuroinflammation, and neurovascular impairment [36,43,45]. This systems-level framework suggests that effective therapies must not simply block individual pathways but rather disrupt or recalibrate the Zn–NO–ONOO- network that drives neuronal vulnerability.

Core mechanistic targets within the Zn–NO axis

Mitochondrial dysfunction lies at the center of Zn–NO-mediated injury [45]. Excess Zn2+ accumulates within mitochondria, inhibiting key dehydrogenases and impairing the electron transport chain, ultimately resulting in ATP depletion and increased superoxide generation [43,45]. Concurrently, pathological NO production, driven by sustained nNOS activation and iNOS induction, reversibly inhibits cytochrome c oxidase and promotes the formation of ONOO− [38,44,55]. This convergence amplifies the oxidative and nitrative damage to mitochondrial proteins, lipids, and DNA, ultimately triggering apoptotic and ferroptotic cell death pathways [4,38,55].

In parallel, Zn2+ disrupts antioxidant defenses by impairing GSH recycling and reducing GPX4)activity, thereby promoting lipid peroxidation [24,41,56]. ONOO− further oxidizes metallothioneins, releasing additional Zn2+ and reinforcing intracellular zinc overload [52,57,58]. This creates a self-propagating cycle linking redox imbalance, mitochondrial collapse, and metal dyshomeostasis [52].

Beyond neurons, the Zn–NO axis also drives neuroinflammation and neurovascular dysfunction [54,55,59]. Elevated Zn2+ promotes microglial activation and biases immune responses toward a pro-inflammatory phenotype, increasing iNOS expression and ROS production [55]. Meanwhile, NO and ONOO− compromise endothelial tight junctions and degrade extracellular matrix components via MMP activation, leading to blood–brain barrier (BBB) disruption [12,60]. Together, these processes extend injury from cellular to network and tissue levels.

Zinc-targeted therapeutic strategies

Given that Zn2+ dysregulation represents an early and pivotal event in acute brain injury, several strategies have focused on limiting its toxic accumulation. Chelators such as CaEDTA have demonstrated efficacy in reducing extracellular zinc levels and attenuating neuronal death in experimental models [12] (Fig. 2). However, indiscriminate zinc removal may disrupt physiological synaptic signaling and plasticity. Consequently, subsequent refined approaches aimed to modulate zinc homeostasis without abolishing its physiological roles. These include enhancing vesicular sequestration through ZnT3, stabilizing metallothioneins to prevent redox-induced zinc release, and limiting zinc entry through Ca2+-permeable AMPA receptors and voltage-gated channels [3,57,61,62] (Fig. 2). Such strategies preserve the regulatory functions of Zn2+ while preventing its pathological accumulation.

Nitric oxide and redox-modulating interventions

Selective inhibition of nNOS or iNOS can reduce excessive NO production and limit ONOO− formation [26,38,55]. However, complete suppression of NO may impair physiological processes such as neurovascular coupling and synaptic plasticity. An alternative strategy involves modulating downstream redox pathways rather than NO itself. Scavengers of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species, as well as inhibitors of NADPH oxidase, can reduce superoxide availability and thereby limit ONOO− formation [17,63,64]. These approaches uncouple the toxic interaction between NO and superoxide while preserving beneficial NO signaling.

Metabolic and signaling-based therapeutic approaches

Recent advances highlight the importance of targeting metabolic resilience and intracellular signaling pathways that regulate the Zn–NO axis. AMPK serves as a central energy sensor that restores mitochondrial function, enhances autophagy, and suppresses inflammatory signaling [[65], [66], [67], [68]]. Activation of AMPK has been shown to mitigate Zn2+-induced mitochondrial dysfunction and reduce oxidative stress [69,70] (Fig. 2). CTRP9, an upstream activator of AMPK, exerts pleiotropic protective effects by improving metabolic homeostasis, suppressing neuroinflammation, and preserving neurovascular integrity [71,72]. CTRP9 also reduces Zn2+ accumulation and attenuates BBB disruption, suggesting that it modulates multiple nodes of the Zn–NO network simultaneously [72] (Fig. 2). PDE inhibitors, particularly PDE4 and PDE5 inhibitors, enhance cAMP and cGMP signaling pathways, respectively [2,13,73] (Fig. 3, Fig. 2). These pathways improve mitochondrial function, inhibit inflammatory cascades, and stabilize lysosomal integrity. By restoring intracellular signaling balance, PDE inhibitors indirectly disrupt the Zn–NO amplification loop. In addition, metabolic regulators such as HK2 modulators may protect against Zn2+-induced bioenergetic failure by maintaining glycolytic flux and mitochondrial coupling [74,75]. These interventions highlight the importance of preserving cellular energy homeostasis in limiting Zn–NO-mediated injury (Fig. 2).

Convergent regulatory network linking PDE4, 5–cAMP and cGMP signaling to the Zn–NO–ONOO- axis in neuronal injury

This figure shows the intersection between Zn2+dysregulation, NO signaling, and PDE4 and 5-dependent cAMP and cGMP homeostasis. Pathological Zn2+ accumulation impairs mitochondrial respiration and enhances superoxide production, while excessive NO reacts with superoxide to form ONOO−, amplifying oxidative and nitrative stress. ONOO− further disrupts cellular homeostasis by destabilizing lysosomes, releasing additional Zn2+, and suppressing cAMP–PKA signaling, in part through activation of PDE4B. Pharmacological inhibition of PDE4 (e.g., rolipram, roflumilast, amlexanox) restores intracellular cAMP levels, activates PKA, and improves mitochondrial efficiency, thereby reducing ROS availability for ONOO− formation. Collectively, PDE4 and 5 inhibition disrupts the Zn–NO–ONOO- amplification loop and restores neuronal metabolic and redox homeostasis.

Integrated therapeutic framework across disease contexts

Although epilepsy, hypoglycemia, and traumatic brain injury differ in their initiating events, they shared a Zn–NO-driven injury cascade (Fig. 4). In epilepsy, excessive neuronal firing triggers rapid Zn2+ release and NO overproduction, promoting excitotoxic and redox-mediated damage [76]. In hypoglycemia, energy failure and reperfusion-induced oxidative stress initiate Zn–NO interactions that lead to delayed neuronal death [1,77,78]. In TBI, mechanical disruption rapidly evolves into a biochemical cascade dominated by Zn2+ mobilization, NO production, and BBB breakdown [13,79]. This convergence underscores the importance of therapeutic strategies that target common downstream mechanisms rather than disease-specific triggers. Consequently, interventions that stabilize mitochondria, reduce oxidative and nitrative stress, regulate zinc homeostasis, and suppress neuroinflammation are likely to have broad applicability across multiple forms of acute brain injury [13,22,62].

The Zn–NO axis across epilepsy, hypoglycemia-induced brain injury, and traumatic brain injury

This figure illustrates how the shared Zn–NO– peroxynitrite (ONOO−) amplification loop operates across distinct acute brain injury paradigms. In epilepsy, seizure-induced zinc (Zn2+) release and neuronal nitric oxide synthase (nNOS) activation promote mitochondrial superoxide production and ONOO− formation, contributing to hippocampal neuronal loss and epileptogenesis. In hypoglycemia, energy failure and reperfusion enhance Zn2+ accumulation and NO in vulnerable neuronal populations, leading to PARP activation and delayed neuronal death. In traumatic brain injury (TBI), mechanical damage, blood–brain barrier (BBB) disruption, and neuroinflammation drive Zn2+ mobilization and multi-source NO production (nNOS, eNOS, iNOS), resulting in sustained oxidative stress, vascular dysfunction, and white matter injury. Despite different initiating insults, these conditions converge on Zn–NO-dependent mitochondrial dysfunction, lysosomal impairment, and microglial activation.

Toward network-based therapeutic strategies

Collectively, these findings support a paradigm shift from single-target interventions toward network-based therapeutic strategies. Rather than focusing on isolated molecules, effective treatments must address the dynamic and interconnected nature of the Zn–NO axis. Notably, timing and context of the intervention are critical. Early-phase interventions may prioritize zinc chelation and redox suppression, whereas later-phase therapies may focus on metabolic recovery, autophagy enhancement, and neurovascular repair [59,65,72,[80], [81], [82]]. This unified framework positions the Zn–NO axis not only as a central driver of neuronal injury but also as a strategic target for developing disease-modifying therapies. Integrated therapeutic can disrupt the self-amplifying loops that link mitochondrial dysfunction, redox imbalance, and inflammation, offering the potential to improve acute outcomes and long-term neurological recovery [15,[83], [84], [85]].

Conclusions and perspectives

Over the past two decades, Zn2+ and NO have emerged as central regulators of redox-dependent neuronal fate in acute brain injury. Rather than acting as independent mediators, accumulating evidence supports the concept that Zn2+ and NO form a tightly coupled metal–redox axis that governs the transition from adaptive signaling to irreversible neurodegeneration [[4], [5], [6],19]. Under pathological conditions, dysregulated Zn2+ mobilization and excessive NO production converge at the mitochondrial level, where they amplify reactive oxygen and nitrogen species generation and drive ONOO− formation [5,45,86,87]. This Zn–NO–ONOO- cascade disrupts mitochondrial bioenergetics, impairs antioxidant defenses, and promotes lipid peroxidation, thereby linking excitotoxicity and neuroinflammation to ferroptotic and apoptotic cell death [4,45].

Notably, the impact of Zn–NO interactions extends beyond neuronal injury to encompass neurovascular dysfunction and immune activation [7,53,59,88]. The same redox mechanisms that damage mitochondria also destabilize lysosomal integrity, activate microglia, and compromise BBB function, highlighting the Zn–NO axis as a system-level regulator of brain injury progression [7,55,89,90]. Collectively, these findings support a unifying model in which redox imbalance, metal dyshomeostasis, and inflammatory signaling are integrated through a self-amplifying network.

From a therapeutic perspective, these insights argue against single-target approaches and instead favor strategies that modulate the Zn–NO axis at multiple levels. Interventions such as zinc chelation, NOS modulation, AMPK activation, PDE inhibition, CTRP9 signaling, and metabolic reprogramming each target distinct nodes within this network [13,38,72,73,75,81,91] (Table 1). However, their efficacy likely depends on precise temporal and spatial control, reflecting the dualistic nature of Zn2+ and NO as physiological regulators and pathological drivers.

In addition to the Zn–NO axis, emerging evidence suggests that other gasotransmitters, particularly hydrogen sulfide (H2S), may play an important modulatory role in zinc homeostasis and redox signaling [98,101]. H2S can bind free Zn2+ to form relatively insoluble complexes, thereby reducing labile zinc availability and attenuating zinc-mediated neurotoxicity [101] (Table 1). This interaction positions H2S as a potential counter-regulatory component within the Zn–NO redox network and highlights the therapeutic potential of H2S-releasing compounds as novel modulators of metal–redox imbalance.

Despite this evidence, a major challenge lies in resolving the spatiotemporal dynamics of Zn2+ and NO interactions in vivo. Advances in genetically encoded sensors, redox imaging, and single-cell multi-omics are expected to provide critical insights into how this axis operates across different cell types and disease contexts. In parallel, the identification of reliable biomarkers of Zn–NO dysregulation will be essential for translating mechanistic findings into clinically actionable strategies.

Ultimately, we propose that the Zn–NO axis represents a central redox hub that integrates metabolic failure, oxidative and nitrative stress, neuroinflammation, and neurovascular dysfunction in acute brain injury. Targeting this axis offers a promising avenue for the development of disease-modifying therapies that move beyond symptomatic control toward restoration of cellular and network homeostasis. Therefore, a deeper understanding of this metal–redox interface—potentially expanded to include additional regulators such as H2S—may redefine therapeutic strategies for a broad spectrum of neurological disorders.
