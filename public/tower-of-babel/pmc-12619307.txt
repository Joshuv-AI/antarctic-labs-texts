Development of convenient potyvirus-based vectors for simultaneous expression of two heterologous proteins in solanaceous plants
Authors: Na Li, Changhui Deng, Yubo Zhu, Li Qin, Zhaoji Dai, Hangjun Sun, Changjian Xia, Hongguang Cui, Xiaohua Xiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Plant virus-based vectors can deliver foreign nucleic acid sequences into host plants for expression, serving as powerful biotechnological tools in agricultural applications, such as plant functional genomic study, agronomic trait improvement, pest management, genome editing. Viruses in the genus Potyvirus represent the largest group of plant-infecting RNA viruses, employing polyprotein processing as the genome expression strategy. A variety of potyvirus-based vectors have been documented, whereas the ones with a simple cloning strategy for simultaneous expression of two heterologous proteins await to be exploited. In this study, we developed an infectious cDNA clone of a potato virus Y (PVY) isolate PVY-N, termed pPVY. Next, we engineered Golden Gate-compatible derivatives of pPVY and pPVMV (an infectious cDNA clone of an attenuated strain of pepper veinal mottle virus) by incorporating Type IIS restriction enzyme sites into the P1/HCPro and NIb/CP junctions of the viral genomes. The resulting recombinant clones, pPVY-2×GoldG and pPVMV-2×GoldG, are highly infectious in their natural Solanaceae hosts. Both vectors allow simultaneous insertion of two reporter genes (mCherry and GFP) for robust expression in Nicotiana benthamiana and N. tabacum (for PVY) and N. benthamiana and pepper plants (for PVMV). Serial passage revealed that the recombinant viruses harboring two heterologous genes were genetically stable. Considered the fact that both viruses have a wide host range in Solanaceae plants, the PVY- and PVMV-based vectors would serve as valuable biotechnological tools and have a promising application prospect.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07576-6.

Background

Plant viruses evolved a variety of ingenious tactics to optimize the coding capacity of their compact genomes, such as subgenomic RNA synthesis, polyprotein processing, ribosomal frameshifting, stop codon read-through, and leaky scanning initiation [18, 34, 42]. Understanding these strategies aids the development of plant virus-based vectors, which can deliver non-viral sequences into host plants for efficient expression. Viral vectors serve as the powerful biotechnological tools in agricultural applications, mainly including:

i) Functional Genomics. When a recombinant virus carrying a partial sequence of a candidate host gene infects host plants, cellular RNA interference (RNAi) machinery would slice viral genome molecules, generating virus-derived small interference RNAs (vsiRNAs) and foreign insert-derived siRNAs. The latter would target the host gene, binding its promoter region or cleaving its transcripts to inhibit or downregulate its expression, so-called virus-induced gene silencing (VIGS) [16, 32, 52]. If a recombinant virus harbors a complete coding sequence of a host gene, viral infection would drive its robust expression along with viral genome translation [1, 9]. ii) Agronomic Trait Improvement. Numerous genes engaging in regulating important agronomic traits such as flowering and plant architecture have been characterized. Their complete coding sequences were engineered into plant virus-based vectors for robust expression to improve crop performance without genetic modification [1, 47]. iii) Pest Management. If a foreign sequence in a recombinant virus is from a key gene of an insect pest or pathogen, the foreign insert-derived siRNAs will be formed along with viral infection. The siRNAs could be delivered into the pest or pathogen during its infestation, resulting in the silencing of the target gene (so-called host-induced gene silencing, HIGS) and achieving the purpose of pest management [1, 20, 51]. iv) Gene Editing. An increasing number of plant virus-based vectors have been launched for the delivery of gene editing components (Cas or/and gRNA) into host plants to modify plant genetic materials [9, 35, 67].

Potyvirus is the largest genus in the kingdom of plant RNA viruses, including many agriculturally and economically important virus species such as Plum pox virus (PPV), Soybean mosaic virus and Potato virus Y (PVY). Members in the genus (potyviruses) are flexuous filamentous in particle morphology, and share a single-stranded positive-sense RNA genome (+ ssRNA; 9.7 ~ 11 kb) [11, 23, 38, 48, 70]. Their genomes contain one genome-spanning open reading frame (ORF) and another short ORF (termed PIPO) embedded in the P3 cistron, with the exception of sweetpotato-infecting potyviruses that have one more ORF (termed PISPO) embedded in the P1 cistron [6, 8, 30]. Owing to viral RNA polymerase slippage at a conserved motif G1−2A6−7 in P3 or P1 cistron during viral replication, a small portion of frame-shifted genomic RNAs are generated. For them, PIPO or PISPO becomes translational in frame with its upstream sequence [41, 44, 50, 59, 62, 68]. Along with viral genome expression, two polyproteins (a large one P1-to-CP, and a small one P1-to-P3N-PIPO), and P1N-PISPO (for sweetpotato viruses only), are generated, and immediately processed by virus-encoded proteases into ten viral units (starting from N-termini): P1, HCPro, P3, 6K1, CI, 6K2, NIa-VPg, NIa-Pro, NIb, and CP [11, 38, 48, 70]. Within this genus, Euphorbia ringspot virus, has a unique cistron Maf/ham1-like protein between NIb and CP [27], which functions in host adaptation likely via partnering with viral polymerase (NIb) [61].

Substantial evidence shows that either P1/HCPro or NIb/CP junction in potyvirus can tolerate a foreign insert for robust expression in a host plant along with viral infection [2, 46]. A large number of potyvirus-based vectors such as tobacco etch virus (TEV) and PPV have been launched to express a heterologous protein for plant biotechnology and synthetic biology [26, 46]. However, only a few potyviruses have been exploited as VIGS vectors, applying in functional genomic studies [65]. A likely explanation is that potyvirus encodes a strong RNA silencing suppressor (HCPro) that heavily blocks cellular RNAi-mediated antiviral response [63, 65]. Several studies revealed that two foreign genes engineered into P1/HCPro and NIb/CP junctions allow their simultaneous expression from a single potyviral vector, showing great potentials in complex metabolic engineering and coordinated production of multi-subunit proteins [3, 4, 25, 31, 36, 54]. However, the integration of a foreign fragment into a potyvirus-based vector consists of complex procedures, and is tedious. It should comply with the two criteria: (i) the addition of foreign insert does not result in the frame-shifting; (ii) the sequence coding for cleavage peptide (recognized by viral protease P1 or NIa-Pro) should be included at each foreign insert/viral cistron junction, which is a must for the release of heterologous protein from virus-encoded polyprotein.

Therefore, the development of potyvirus-based vectors that are compatible with a simple cloning strategy is needed. This study aims to develop potyvirus-based vectors that are compatible with Golden Gate cloning for simultaneous expression of two heterologous proteins in Solanaceous plants. We successfully generated two potyvirus-based vectors, and each of them harbors type IIS restriction enzyme sites at both P1/HCPro and NIb/CP junctions. We demonstrated that both vectors allow simultaneous expression of two reporter genes in the corresponding Solanaceae hosts. The resultant recombinant viruses are genetically stable. Altogether, this study provides valuable biotechnological tools for heterologous protein expression and functional genomic studies in Solanaceae plants.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and virus source

Nicotiana benthamiana seeds were propagated and collected by Dr. Hongguang Cui’s group. N. benthamiana seedlings were grown in a growth chamber with the setup as 16-h light (6500 Lx) at 25℃ and 8-h darkness at 23℃, 70% relative humidity. Tobacco (N. tabacum cv. K326) and pepper (Capsicum chinense cv. ‘Yellow Lantern’) plants were maintained in a plant-growing rack under 16-h light and 8-h darkness at a range of 25 ~ 28 °C. Tobacco seeds were obtained from Haikou Cigar Research Institute, and pepper seeds were purchased from Maoming Dading Seed Science & Technology Co., Ltd. The pEarleygate100-PVY, an infectious cDNA clone of PVY isolate PVY-N (NCBI GenBank accession no. MG591487) was developed by Dr. Hangjun Sun, with the binary plant expression vector pEarleygate as the backbone [55]. The pPVMV-GoldG, a pepper veinal mottle virus (PVMV)-based viral vector that harbors a single Golden Gate cloning site at P1/HCPro junction was constructed in a recent study [12].

PVY-based constructs

A low-copy mini-binary T-DNA vector, pCB301-35 S-Nos [10], was used as the backbone to re-construct the full-length cDNA clone of PVY-N. The complete genomic sequence of PVY-N was amplified from pEarleygate100-PVY by using a pair of primers PVY-F/PVY-NOS-SalI-R (Additional file 1: Table S1). The resulting amplicon was ligated with Pst I/Stu I-treated pCB301-35 S-Nos via seamless cloning to generate the full-length cDNA clone of PVY-N, termed pPVY.

pPVY served as the backbone to create a GFP-tagged clone. The GFP sequence was amplified from pVPH-GFP [10] with primer pair PVY-NIb-GFP-F/PVY-CP-GFP-R (Additional file 1: Table S1). Another two fragments, upstream and downstream of NIb/CP junction, were amplified with corresponding primer pairs 6K2-AatII-F/PVY-GFP-NIb-R and PVY-GFP-CP-F/PVY-NOS-SalI-R (Additional file 1: Table S1). The three obtained products were mixed as the template for overlapping PCR with primer set 6K2-AatII-F/PVY-NOS-SalI-R. The resulting fragment was ligated with Aat II/Sal I-treated pPVY to generate the GFP-tagged clone, pPVY-GFP. For PVY-GFP, the original cleavage peptide TYEVHHQ/AND at NIb/CP junction (proteolytically processed by NIa-Pro) was engineered into NIb/GFP and GFP/CP junctions for the release of free GFP from virus-encoded polyprotein. In case that the GFP-coding region was removed via a likely recombination event by viral polymerase, the nucleotide sequences coding for the two cleavage peptides have ten nucleotide differences, ACTTATGAAGTACACCATCAA/GCTAACGAT versus ACCTACGAGGTTCATCACCAG/GCAAATGAC (the nucleotides in italics are artificially introduced).

pPVY-2×GoldG has two Golden Gate cloning sites at P1/HCPro and NIb/CP junctions, respectively. A tandem of elements (two Bsa I/Sap I sites with a 9-nt/7-nt spacer, flanked by two short sequences coding for cleavage peptides by P1 or NIa-Pro) were engineered into P1/HCPro and NIb/CP junctions of pPVY to generate the pPVY-2×GoldG. The detailed information for the construction of pPVY-2×GoldG was provided in Additional file 1: Supplementary Text. Golden Gate cloning [12] was employed to construct pPVY-2×GoldG-mCh, pPVY-2×GoldG-G, and pPVY-2×GoldG-mCh-G. For pPVY-2×GoldG-mCh, the mCherry-coding sequence was amplified from pCaMter-mCherry [22] with primer set BsaI-mcherry-F/BsaI-mcherry-R (Additional file 1: Table S1), followed by the integration into pPVY-2×GoldG. As the GFP-coding sequence contains a Bsa I site, we performed overlapping PCR to destroy it. Two GFP fragments were amplified from pPVY-GFP with primer pairs SapI-GFP-F/GFP-disBsaI-R and GFP-disBsaI-F/SapI-GFP-R (Additional file 1: Table S1), respectively. The two PCR products was mixed as the template for overlapping PCR with primer set SapI-GFP-F/SapI-GFP-R. The resulting amplicon was individually cloned into pPVY-2×GoldG and pPVY-2×GoldG-mCh, thus generating pPVY-2×GoldG-G and pPVY-2×GoldG-mCh-G.

PVMV-based constructs

In a previous study by our group, pPVMV-GoldG harboring Bsa I sites at P1/HCPro junction for Golden Gate cloning was developed [12]. In this study, we aimed to engineer Sap I sites into NIb/CP junction to generate a dual-expression vector. Sequence analysis revealed the existence of three Sap I sites in pPVMV-GoldG, which are located in HCPro-, P3-, and CP-coding regions, respectively. Overlapping PCRs were performed to introduce synonymous mutations to destroy the three sites. Briefly, four fragments were amplified from pPVMV-GoldG with primer sets PV-MluI-inf-F/PV-HCPro-disSapI1-R, PV-HCPro-disSapI1-F/PV-P3-disSapI2-R, PV-P3-disSapI2-F/PV-CP-disSapI3-R, and PV-CP-disSapI3-F/PV-C-inf-R (Additional file 1: Table S1), respectively. First and last two amplicons were respectively mixed as the templates for overlapping PCRs with primer sets PV-MluI-inf-F/PV-P3-disSapI2-R and PV-P3-disSapI2-F/PV-C-inf-R to amplify two long fragments. The two long amplicons were mixed as the template for another overlapping PCR with primer set PV-MluI-inf-F/PV-C-inf-R. The final product was integrated into Sal I/Mlu I-treated pPVMV-GoldG via seamless cloning to generate an intermediate clone pPVMV-GoldG-disSapI. Subsequently, a tandem of elements, two Sap I sites with a 5-bp spacer flanked by two short sequences encoding DDFVLHQ/AGE (cleavage peptide by NIa-Pro), were inserted into NIb/CP junction pPVMV-GoldG-disSapI to produce the pPVMV-2×GoldG. Briefly, two fragments, upstream and downstream of NIb-CP junction, were amplified from pPVMV-GoldG-disSapI by primer pairs PV-C-inf-F/PV-addSapI-R and PV-addSapI-F/PV-C-inf-R (Additional file 1: Table S1), respectively. A mixture of two obtained amplicons were used as the template for overlapping PCR with primer set PV-C-inf-F/PV-C-inf-R. The obtained amplicon was inserted back into Aat II/Sal I-treated pPVMV-GoldG-disSapI to generate the pPVMV-2×GoldG.

pPVMV-2×GoldG-mCh-G was constructed via Golden gate cloning. The complete mCherry sequence was amplified from pCAMter-mCherry [22] with a pair of primers mCherry-BsaI-F/mCherry-BsaI-R (Additional file 1: Table S1). The obtained product was cloned into pPVMV-2×GoldG to produce the pPVMV-2×GoldG-mCh. The complete GFP sequence was amplified from pHNu-GFP [22] with the primer set GFP-SapI-F/GFP-SapI-R (Additional file 1: Table S1), followed by the integration into pPVMV-2×GoldG-mCh to generate the pPVMV-2×GoldG-mCh-G. All developed plasmids in this study were verified by Sanger sequencing.

RNA extraction, RT-PCR, and real-time RT-qPCR

Total RNAs were extracted from leaf tissues of N. benthamiana or N. tabacum plants by using TRNzol reagent (TIANGEN), and from pepper leaves with FastPure Plant Total RNA Isolation Kit (Vazyme). The first-strand cDNAs were synthesized by using RevertAid First Strand cDNA synthesis kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) was performed for the detection of PVY and its derivatives: primer pair PVY-CP-F/PVY-CP-R were used for PVY, PVY-GFP and PVY-2×GoldG, and primer pairs F1/R1 and F2/R2 for PVY-2×GoldG-G, PVY-2×GoldG-mCh and PVY-2×GoldG-mCh-G. Two pairs of primers F3/R3 and F4/R4 were used for the detection of PVMV-2×GoldG and PVMV-2×GoldG-mCh-G (Additional file 1: Table S1).

Real-time RT quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed with SuperReal PremixPlus (TIANGEN) by the utility of Applied Biosystems QuantStudio 5 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Primer set PVMV-CP-F/PVMV-CP-R (Additional file 1: Table S1), targeting CP cistron in PVMV-2×GoldG and PVMV-2×GoldG-mCh-G, were designed. Another pair of primers q-CP-F/q-CP-R (Additional file 1: Table S1) were designed to target CPs of PVY and its derivatives, including PVY-2×GoldG, PVY-2×GoldG-G, PVY-2×GoldG-mCh, and PVY-2×GoldG-mCh-G). The Actins in N. benthamiana (NbActin) and pepper (CcActin), and 18 S ribosomal RNA in N. tabacum [17, 22] were used as reference genes for the assays. The relative gene expression levels were calculated by the software provided by the manufacturer.

Agrobacterial infiltration and Sap rub-inoculation

Agrobacterium (strain GV3101)-mediated transformation and sap rub-inoculation in N. tabacum, N. benthamiana, and pepper plants were performed by following previously-described protocols [12, 49, 66].

Immunoblot and antibodies

Total proteins were extracted from upper non-inoculated leaves with reference to previous protocols [12, 22], separated by 12% sodium dodecyl-sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membrane (Millipore). Two polyclonal antibodies against GFP (Sangon Biotech) or mCherry (Proteintech), and a goat anti-rabbit immunoglobulin antibody conjugated to horseradish peroxidase (Sangon Biotech), were used as the primary and secondary antibodies, respectively. The hybridization signals were detected with an enhanced chemiluminescence detection reagent kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and visualized by the utility of a multifunctional chemiluminescence imager apparatus (JingYi Technology).

GFP and mCherry imaging

The GFP and mCherry signals in inoculated plants were monitored in a dark room by using a UV lamp (LUYOR) and a Fluorescence Protein Lamp (LUYOR), respectively. Excitation wavelengths were 365 nm for GFP and 540 nm for mCherry.

Results

Development of infectious cDNA clones of PVY-N with a low-copy mini-binary vector as the backbone

A low-copy mini-binary vector pCB301-35 S-Nos was used to re-construct the infectious cDNA clone of PVY-N, mainly considered that: (i) the cDNAs of potyviral genomes are usually toxic to Escherichia coli, and the unexpected deletions, additions or mutations are likely introduced during their multiplication in E. coli; (ii) virus clones in a small size is convenient for genetic manipulation. The complete genomic sequence of PVY-N (Fig. 1a) was amplified from pEarleygate100-PVY, followed by the integration into pCB301-35 S-Nos to generate a mini-clone, termed pPVY (Fig. 1b). To visually monitor viral infection in planta, a complete GFP sequence was engineered into NIb/CP intercistronic junction of pPVY to generate a GFP-recombinant clone, pPVY-GFP (Fig. 1b). To test the infectivity of pPVY and pPVY-GFP, N. tabacum seedlings at 1- or 2-leaf-stage were inoculated with pPVY or pPVY-GFP via agroinfiltration (optical density at 600 nm [OD600] of 0.6, 8 plants per clone). All plants inoculated with pPVY or pPVY-GFP exhibited typical vein clearing and mosaic symptoms in newly expanded leaves at 10 days post agroinfiltration (dpai). At 25 dpai, mottling symptoms with alternating dark green and light green were observed (Fig. 1c). These symptoms were absent in the plants inoculated with empty vector (Fig. 1c). Obvious green fluorescence signals were observed in upper non-inoculated leaves of all plants inoculated with pPVY-GFP at 10 dpai and 25 dpai (Fig. 1c). The presence of PVY or PVY-GFP in these plants was validated by RT-PCR assays targeting viral CP (Fig. 1d).

Engineering type IIS restriction enzyme sites into both P1/HCPro and NIb/CP junctions in PVY- and PVMV-based vectors

Potyvirus-based vectors are widely launched for the expression of heterologous proteins. In most cases, foreign genes or fragments were integrated into P1/HC-Pro or NIb/CP intercistronic position [3, 37]. For cloning convenience, Type IIS restriction enzyme sites were engineered into the two junctions. Based on the analysis of the cutting profiles of all known Type IIS restriction enzymes in PVY- and PVMV-vector sequences, both Bsa I and Sap I sites were respectively integrated into P1-HCPro and NIb-CP junctions in pPVY to generate pPVY-2×GoldG (Fig. 2a). For each position, a tandem of elements, two Bsa I or Sap I sites with a 9- or 7-nt spacer flanked by two short sequences encoding cleavage peptide by P1 or NIa-Pro were introduced. The cassette facilitates not only Golden Gate cloning, but also the release of heterologous proteins from virus-encoded polyprotein.

To test the infectivity of PVY-2×GoldG, agrobacterial culture harboring pPVY-2×GoldG or pPVY (the parallel control) at OD600 of 0.6 was inoculated into N. tabacum seedlings at 2- or 3-leaf stage (eight plants per clone). PVY-2×GoldG showed similar infectivity to PVY, and all inoculated plants exhibited typical vein clearing symptoms at 10 dpai, and mottling and dwarfish symptoms at 20 dpai (Fig. 2b). The presence of PVY or PVY-2×GoldG in upper non-inoculated leaves was validated by RT-PCR (Additional file 2: Figure S1a). Real-time RT-qPCR was performed to determine viral genome accumulation. As shown in Fig. 2c, the genomic accumulation level of PVY-2×GoldG was not significantly different from that of PVY at 10 dpai and 20 dpai. Sanger sequencing confirmed that the introduced cassettes were stably present in virus progeny at 20 dpai (Additional file 2: Figure S1b).

Similarly, the PVMV-based vector, pPVMV-2×GoldG, was generated via the addition of Sap I sites into NIb-CP junction of pPVMV-GoldG (Fig. 3a). During the process, an intermediate clone, pPVMV-GoldG-disSapI, for which the three inherent Sap I sites in pPVMV-GoldG were destroyed via synonymous mutations, was generated. The infectivity of the two clones together with PVMV-GoldG (the parallel control) was tested in N. benthamiana and pepper. The results showed that all clones induced similar symptoms in N. benthamiana at 8 dpai (severe leaf curling) or pepper plants at 20 days post rub-inoculation (dpri) (mild mottling). Real-time RT-qPCR assays revealed that both PVMV-GoldG and PVMV-GoldG-disSapI shared a comparable genome accumulation level with that of PVMV-2×GoldG (Fig. 3c). Altogether, the modified clone pPVMV-2×GoldG was highly infectious in its natural and experimental hosts (Fig. 3b).

pPVY-2×GoldG allows individual or simultaneous expression of two reporter genes in N. benthamiana or N. tabacum

We investigated whether pPVY-2×GoldG can support individual or simultaneous expression of two heterologous genes in N. benthamiana or N. tabacum. For this purpose, two reporter genes GFP and mCherry were individually or simultaneously engineered into pPVY-2×GoldG via Golden Gate cloning to obtain three recombinant virus clones, pPVY-2×GoldG-G, pPVY-2×GoldG-mCh, and pPVY-2×GoldG-mCh-G (Fig. 4a). Agrobacterial culture harboring each clone at OD600 of 0.6 was infiltrated into eight N. tabacum (1- or 2-leaf-stage) and ten N. benthamiana seedlings (3- or 4-leaf-stage). Infectivity test showed that all inoculated plants with pPVY-2×GoldG-mCh manifested obvious red fluorescence signals in upper non-inoculated leaves at 10 dpai and 25 dpai, whereas the plants with pPVY-2×GoldG-G showing green fluorescence signals (Fig. 4b; Additional file 2: Figure S2a). As anticipated, both red and green fluorescence signals were observed in all plants inoculated with pPVY-GoldG-mCh-G at the two time points (Fig. 4b; Additional file 2: Figure S2a), indicating that the two reporter genes were co-expressed along with viral infection. RT-PCR corroborated that the foreign inserts were retained in corresponding recombinant viruses (Fig. 4c; Additional file 2: Figure S2b). Immunoblot analysis further confirmed the co-expression of GFP and mCherry in infected leaf tissues (Fig. 4d; Additional file 2: Figure S2c). These observations indicate that free GFP and mCherry are efficiently processed and released from virus-encoded polyprotein along with viral genome expression.

Next, we tested the pPVMV-2×GoldG for the simultaneous expression of two heterologous proteins in N. benthamiana and pepper. Similar with the section above, mCherry and GFP cistrons were integrated into P1/HCPro and NIb/CP junctions, respectively, thus giving rise to the recombinant clone pPVMV-2×GoldG-mCh-G (Fig. 5a). pPVMV-2×GoldG-mCh-G, together with pPVMV-2×GoldG (the parallel control), were each inoculated into five N. benthamiana seedlings at 3- to 5-leaf-stage by agrobacterial infiltration (OD600 of 1.0). At 10 dpai, all plants inoculated with pPVMV-2×GoldG-mCh-G exhibited both red and green fluorescence signals in upper non-inoculated leaves (Additional file 2: Figure S3a). RT-PCR confirmed the presence of both foreign cistrons in the recombinant virus, PVMV-2×GoldG-mCh-G (Additional file 2: Figure S3b). Immunoblot analysis validated the co-expression of mCherry and GFP along with viral infection (Additional file 2: Figure S3c). Each virus-containing homogenate was rub-inoculated into eight pepper plants at 2- or 3-leaf-stage. The similar results with that obtained in N. benthamiana were produced: (i) all pepper plants infected by PVMV-2×GoldG-mCh-G displayed both red and green fluorescence signals at 10 dpri and 20 dpri (Fig. 5b); (ii) both foreign genes were retained in the recombinant virus (Fig. 5c); (iii) mCherry and GFP were immuno-detected (Fig. 5d). Altogether, the obtained data support that pPVMV-2×GoldG can co-deliver two heterologous genes for expression in its experimental and natural hosts.

Both PVY- and PVMV-based Recombinant clones, harboring two heterologous genes, are genetically stable

Genetic stability is an important criterion in assessing the virus-based expression vectors. To evaluate the genetic stability of PVY-based recombinant clone for co-expressing two heterologous proteins, we designed the following experiments: (i) pPVY-2×Gold-mCh-G was inoculated into N. tabacum plants at 1- or 2-leaf-stage by agrobacterial infiltration, and the inoculated plants were maintained for 50 days; (ii) at 50 dpai, the virus-containing homogenate, prepared from newly infected leaves, was rub-inoculated into N. tabacum plants. Thirty days later, the newly-expanded leaves were sampled for fluorescence observation, RT-PCR, and immunoblot analysis. Fluorescence observation showed that the infected plants still displayed strong red and green fluorescence signals in upper non-inoculated leaves (Fig. 6a), an indication of the co-expression of GFP and mCherry. As expected, RT-PCR analysis validated the presence of both foreign inserts in virus progeny at this time point (Fig. 6b). Immunoblot analysis further confirmed the co-expression of mCherry and GFP (Fig. 6c). Using an equivalent strategy, we evaluated the genetic stability of PVMV-GoldG-mCh-G. The pPVMV-GoldG-mCh-G was agro-inoculated into pepper plants, and at 60 dpai, the virus-containing homogenate was rub-inoculated healthy pepper seedlings. At 30 dpri, the pepper plants infected with PVMV-2×GoldG-mCh-G exhibited strong red and green fluorescence signals in upper non-inoculated leaves (Fig. 6d). RT-PCR revealed the existence of two foreign inserts in PVMV-2×GoldG-mCh-G (Fig. 6e). Immunoblot analysis confirmed the co-expression of mCherry and GFP in virus-infected leaf tissues (Fig. 6f). Therefore, the PVMV-based dual expression vector is genetically stable.

Discussion

Potyviruses employ polyprotein processing as their main genome expression strategy. With the aid of this strategy, numerous potyvirus-based vectors have been developed for heterologous protein expression and functional genomic studies. In this study, we developed two potyvirus-based dual expression vectors, in which type IIS restriction enzyme sites were engineered into both P1/HCPro and NIb/CP junctions for convenient cloning. Both of them can tolerate two heterologous genes for simultaneous expression in corresponding Solanaceous hosts. Importantly, the resulting recombinant viruses are genetically stable, allowing continuous co-expression of heterologous proteins in planta along with viral infection. In nature, PVMV and PVY can infect a wide range of host plants mainly distributing to the Solanaceae family [12, 53, 56]. Theoretically, pPVY-2×GoldG and pPVMV-2×GoldG should be also applicable in other Solanaceae hosts for simultaneous expression of two heterologous proteins.

As we stated in introduction, the integration of foreign sequence into potyvirus-based vector involves tedious design and complex procedures [12]. There have been several strategies to modify potyvirus-based vectors for cloning convenience: (i) multiple cloning sites (restriction endonuclease sites) are introduced into NIb/CP junction, such as TEV and PPV [15, 24]; (ii) a long sequence module for other cloning strategies is engineered into NIb/CP junction, such as Nimble Cloning site for papaya leaf distortion virus (PLDMV)-based vector and Gateway Cloning site for telosma mosaic virus (TelMV) vector [58, 65]. For them, additional residues, besides the ones deriving from viral protease cleavage sites (for the release of heterologous protein form virus-encoded polyprotein) would be added to the ends of the expressed heterologous protein, which might result in its dysfunctionality. As type IIS restriction enzyme cleaves a sequence outside its recognization site [12, 28, 40, 45], both PVY- and PVMV-based vectors possess potential advantages for cloning the desired genes: (i) operation convenience. One-step digestion-ligation allows an integration of gene-of-interest into the vector; (ii) protein fidelity. The resultant protein has only a few extra residues at its ends, which derives from viral protease cleavage site, and could not be cleared by using any other potyvirus vector. In view of all potyviruses sharing a consistent genome expression strategy, it is feasible to develop more and more potyvirus-based vectors having Golden Gate cloning feature for dual expression of heterologous proteins in different potyvirus-host combinations.

A variety of plant virus-based vectors have been developed for multiple biotechnological applications in Solanaceae plants. VIGS has been used to characterize gene functions in fruit development and ripening [21, 52], plant immune responses [14, 43], and metabolite biosynthesis [29, 33, 69, 71]. Plant virus-based vectors allow overexpression of key biosynthetic enzymes or transcription factors to improve agronomic traits in tomato and pepper [47]. For instance, early flowering trait was achieved via overexpression of FLOWERING LOCUS T orthologues [13, 57]. Virus-mediated overexpression enhanced crop performance in drought stress, dwarfism, and anthocyanin accumulation [57]. In addition, VIGE is playing increasing roles in agronomic trait improvement for Solanaceae crops, such as anthocyanin synthesis regulation and fruit coloring in tomato [5, 60]. Both PVY- and PVMV-based vectors are in particular suited for characterization of two genes cooperatively regulating a cellular pathway to enhance crop performance. For instance, they could be used for simultaneous expression of two interactive transcriptional factors (Delila and Rosea1) inducing anthocyanin accumulation, as in the case of TEV-based vector [4].

In recent years, several potyviruses, such as PLDMV, sugarcane mosaic virus, and TelMV, have been developed into VIGS vectors [7, 58, 65], which greatly broaden the applications of potyvirus-based vectors in functional genomic studies. In particular, in comparison with wild-type TelMV isolate, an attenuated strain with decreased RSS activity of HCPro, when harboring a partial sequence of a host gene, achieved better efficiency in downregulating the expression of target gene [65]. Based on these observations, we propose that both PVMV- and PVY-based vectors might be exploited into VIGS vectors for simultaneous silencing of two endogenous genes in different Solanaceae crops. Remarkably, a recent study demonstrated that short RNA inserts (24–32 nt) in viral vector can trigger robust VIGS [19], which may also facilitate future use of pPVY and pPVMV for functional genomics. As mentioned above, VIGE serves as a powerful biotechnology in agricultural applications. Remarkably, a compact RNA-guided endonuclease TnpB (~ 400 amino acids) and its guide RNA were loaded into tobacco rattle virus vector, achieving excellent editing efficiency in Arabidopsis thaliana [67]. Both PVY and PVMV-derived recombinant viruses, harboring two heterologous genes (GFP and mCherry), are genetically stable. It is speculated that potyvirus-based vectors including the ones of PVY and PVMV could be used to deliver the small TnpB into host plants for gene editing. It will be a promising research direction.

Solanaceae plants are susceptible to a huge number of viruses across different families. Viral agents have become a major threat to the healthy development of Solanaceae crops [47]. For instance, up to 164 viruses have been identified in peppers worldwide, and, in particular, PVY, CMV, and TMV, are prevalent worldwide [12]. It is urgently needed to deploy a broad resistance to multiple devastating viruses in Solanaceae. Pathogen-derived resistance (PDR) is an efficient strategy to combat viruses [51, 64]. Plant virus-based vector can be used to deliver a genomic segment of a notorious virus into host plant, conferring robust resistance against the target virus [12]. When two arrays of sequences from multiple viruses are engineered into P1-HCPro and NIb-CP junctions of PVY or PVMV vector, the resulting recombinant virus would protect plants against the infections by these viruses. Meanwhile, the attenuated strain can cross-protect plants against the severe strains of the same virus or its close relatives; therefore, the PVY-based vector developed in this study awaits to be optimized via virulence attenuation, as in the case of PVMV vector [12]. In addition, the DAG motif in potyviral CP mediates aphid transmission [39], and, thus, mutating the motif is needed to improve the biosafety of potyviral vectors.
