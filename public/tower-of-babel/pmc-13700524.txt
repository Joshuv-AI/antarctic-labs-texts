Characteristics of injurious falls among hospitalized patients: a retrospective descriptive study
Authors: Yanfei Ma, Chengcheng Xie, Miyan Wang, Haili Long
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo describe the characteristics of injurious falls among hospitalized patients from 2023 to 2025 in our hospital and to provide descriptive evidence to inform fall prevention and patient safety management.MethodsA retrospective descriptive study was conducted using data extracted from the hospital information system. Injurious fall cases among hospitalized patients from 2023 to 2025 were identified. Two independent researchers collected data on patient characteristics, event characteristics (including location, shift, low nurse staffing periods, clinical department, classification based on a hospital-based nursing adverse event grading system, and injury severity), and reporting nurse characteristics (including gender, age, educational background, professional title, job position, and number of reports). Data consistency was verified, and descriptive statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 25.0. The unit of analysis was hospitalization encounters, and fall events were analyzed at the event level.ResultsA total of 341,959 hospitalization encounters were recorded during 2023–2025. A total of 257 fall-related adverse events were reported during this period, of which 130 (50.58%) were injurious falls. Most affected patients were older adults, with those aged 61–80 years accounting for 52.31%, and 56.15% were male. Falls most frequently occurred in patient rooms (49.23%) and bathrooms (20.00%), followed by ward public areas (16.92%) and outside the ward or during inter-ward movement (13.08%). The majority of events were classified as grade III according to a hospital-based nursing adverse event grading system (88.46%), and most injuries were grade I severity (80.00%). Departments with higher numbers of reported injurious falls included hematology, oncology, gastroenterology, neurosurgery, and general medicine. A total of 66.92% of events occurred during low nurse staffing periods, with a relatively balanced distribution across shifts. A total of 111 reporting nurses were involved; most were female (90.99%), aged 31–40 years (55.86%), held a bachelor’s degree (78.38%), and were mainly nurse practitioners or senior nurses (72.98%). Most nurses reported only one event (87.39%).ConclusionInjurious falls were more frequently observed among older patients and occurred most often in patient rooms and bathrooms, with a higher proportion of events observed in several clinical departments and during periods defined as having limited nursing resources. Reporting nurses were predominantly young to middle-aged nurses with intermediate professional titles, and most reported only a single event. This study provides a systematic descriptive summary of injurious fall characteristics from the perspectives of patients, events, and reporting staff, which may provide information for hospital-based patient safety management.

Introduction

Falls are defined as an unexpected event in which an individual comes to rest on the ground, floor, or a lower level due to loss of balance without intentional action [1]. In hospitalized patients, falls are among the most common adverse events and remain a major concern for patient safety in clinical practice [2]. Previous studies have reported considerable variation in inpatient fall rates across healthcare settings. A multicenter study in Switzerland involving 35,998 patients from 138 hospitals reported an overall fall incidence of 3.4%, with marked inter-hospital variability ranging from 0.0% to 11.2% [3]. Similarly, a study conducted in Japan reported a fall incidence of 2.5%, corresponding to 1.99 falls per 1,000 patient-days [4]. These findings suggest that inpatient falls are common, while their incidence varies substantially across institutions.

Inpatient falls have been reported to be associated with multiple adverse outcomes, including prolonged hospital stay, increased healthcare costs, and serious injuries such as fractures and traumatic brain injuries, which may adversely affect patient recovery and quality of life [5–7]. Therefore, fall prevention has become an essential component of patient safety and nursing management.

Compared with general falls, injurious falls represent fall events involving more severe physical consequences. Although no unified definition exists, injurious falls are generally defined as falls resulting in varying degrees of physical harm, ranging from minor soft tissue injuries to severe outcomes such as fractures [8, 9]. Previous studies have reported that approximately one-quarter to one-third of inpatient falls result in injuries [7, 10]. Injurious falls most commonly occur in patient rooms and during toileting activities, and are also associated with prolonged hospitalization [7]. These previous findings highlight that injurious falls are clinically significant adverse events.

However, existing studies have primarily focused on the incidence of falls and the identification of factors associated with fall occurrence, such as advanced age, comorbidities, medication use, and impaired mobility. Systematic descriptions of the characteristics of injurious falls remain limited. In addition, differences in patient composition, ward structure, and nursing practice across institutions may lead to heterogeneity in event characteristics. Moreover, little attention has been given to the characteristics of reporting nurses, which may limit a comprehensive understanding of patient safety management systems. Therefore, based on hospital information system data, this study retrospectively analyzed injurious fall events among hospitalized patients from 2023 to 2025. We systematically described patient characteristics, event characteristics, and reporting nurse characteristics, aiming to provide a descriptive overview of the distribution of injurious fall events.

Methods

Study design and aim

This was a single-center retrospective descriptive study. The study used data from hospitalized patients with injurious falls between 2023 and 2025, which were routinely recorded in the hospital information system during standard clinical care. All data were retrospectively extracted after ethical approval and analyzed to describe the characteristics of injurious falls and to provide descriptive information to inform patient safety management.

The study was conducted at Mianyang Central Hospital, a tertiary Grade A general hospital located in Sichuan Province, China. The hospital serves as a regional medical center for northwest Sichuan and provides comprehensive medical services for a large population. It comprises 47 clinical departments and offers a wide range of multidisciplinary services. Annually, the hospital records approximately 2.54 million outpatient and emergency visits and more than 116,000 inpatient admissions. During the study period (2023–2025), a total of 341,959 inpatient admission encounters were recorded.

The hospital functions as a regional referral center for complex and critically ill patients transferred from surrounding healthcare institutions. The study hospital has an established electronic adverse event reporting system, which was used as the primary data source for this study. The unit of analysis was hospitalization encounters rather than individual patients, and fall events were recorded at the event level. Repeated admissions of the same patient were treated as independent hospitalization encounters for analysis purposes.

Study population and eligibility criteria

This study included injurious fall events occurring among hospitalized patients in our hospital from 2023 to 2025.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) fall events occurring between 2023 and 2025; (2) events recorded in the hospital adverse event reporting system; (3) complete documentation of key information, including event type, time of occurrence, and related details; and (4) falls resulting in clinically confirmed physical injury with documented severity grading.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) falls without evidence of physical injury at the time of clinical assessment and throughout the hospitalization period; (2) duplicate or erroneous records (e.g., repeated reporting of the same event); and (3) records with missing key information that precluded analysis.

Injury status was determined based on immediate post-fall clinical assessment and was continuously monitored during hospitalization. If new clinical evidence of injury (e.g., imaging-confirmed fracture or delayed symptom onset considered related to the fall based on clinical assessment) emerged during the same hospitalization, the case was reclassified as an injurious fall.

Definitions of variables

The classification system used in this study was a hospital-based nursing adverse event grading system established within the institutional quality management framework.

Nursing adverse events were classified as follows: Grade I refers to severe adverse outcomes or sentinel events, including unexpected death or serious outcomes beyond the natural course of disease, such as permanent functional impairment. Grade II refers to adverse outcomes occurring during medical or nursing care that result in physical or functional impairment attributable to nursing activities rather than the underlying disease, or harm to healthcare workers. Grade III refers to mild adverse outcomes related to nursing factors, which fully recover after treatment without lasting functional impairment. Grade IV refers to near-miss or potential adverse events in which errors or risks were identified in time and no actual patient harm occurred.

Patient injury severity was categorized as follows: no injury indicates no physical harm identified after clinical assessment. Grade I injury refers to minor injuries requiring no or minimal treatment, such as mild abrasions or contusions without the need for dressing or suturing. Grade II injury refers to injuries requiring medical treatment, such as extensive abrasions, contusions, or lacerations requiring dressing or suturing. Grade III injury refers to severe injuries requiring further medical management, such as fractures, loss of consciousness, severe tissue damage, or functional impairment. Death refers to cases in which the event directly or indirectly resulted in patient death.

Nursing professional titles and management positions were classified according to the hospital human resources management system, which follows the standardized nursing professional title framework in China. Nursing professional titles are categorized into five levels: nurse, staff nurse, supervisor nurse, associate chief nurse, and chief nurse. Only categories observed in the study population are reported, as some levels were not represented. Management positions were classified according to the hospital administrative structure. Among the included nurses, only two categories were identified—non-managerial nurses and nursing team leaders—and were therefore reported in the Results section.

This classification system is routinely applied in hospital quality management and adverse event reporting.

Data collection

Data were extracted from the hospital information system by two trained researchers using authorized accounts. The collected data included patient characteristics, event-related variables, and reporting nurse characteristics.

Patient and event variables included gender, age, year of occurrence, shift, nursing low-staffing period, location of fall, department, event classification, and injury severity. Reporting nurse variables included gender, age, educational background, professional title, job position, and number of reported events.

Data extraction was independently performed by the two researchers, followed by cross-checking and verification. A double-entry procedure was applied to ensure data accuracy and consistency.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Mianyang Central Hospital (Approval No. S20260389-01). All data were anonymized prior to analysis. No personal identifiers such as patient names or identification numbers were included. The data were used solely for research purposes.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0. Continuous variables were described as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Only descriptive statistical analyses were conducted.

Results

Characteristics of injurious fall events among hospitalized patients

From 2023 to 2025, a total of 341,959 hospitalization encounters were recorded in our hospital. During this period, 257 fall-related adverse events were reported, of which 130 were classified as injurious falls.

Among patients with injurious falls, 73 (56.15%) were male and 57 (43.85%) were female. A large proportion of cases occurred among patients aged 61 years and above, with patients aged 61–80 years accounting for 52.31%, followed by those aged ≥ 81 years (16.92%) and 41–60 years (23.85%).

Regarding annual distribution, 2023 accounted for the highest proportion of cases (40.77%), followed by 2025 (33.85%) and 2024 (25.38%). The distribution across shifts was similar among day shift, early night shift, and late night shift, accounting for 36.15%, 32.31%, and 31.54%, respectively. A total of 66.92% of events occurred during nursing low-staffing periods.

According to the hospital-based nursing adverse event classification system, grade III events accounted for 88.46%, whereas grade II events accounted for 11.54%. Regarding injury severity, grade I injuries were the most common (80.00%), followed by grade II (10.77%) and grade III injuries (9.23%). The top five departments involved were hematology (13.08%), oncology (11.54%), gastroenterology (10.00%), neurosurgery (8.46%), and general medicine (4.62%).

Regarding location of occurrence, most events took place in patient rooms (49.23%) and bathrooms (20.00%). Ward public areas accounted for 16.92%, mainly corridors, with a small number occurring in treatment rooms (n = 2) and water rooms (n = 1). Falls outside wards or across areas accounted for 13.08%, including off-ward activities (n = 14), and isolated cases occurring near elevators, corridors, and external steps. Examination-related areas accounted for 0.77% (Table 1).

Regarding temporal distribution, injurious falls occurred in all months from 2023 to 2025. Although some fluctuations were observed across years, no obvious monthly concentration was observed (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of reporting nurses involved in injurious fall events

This section describes nurses involved in reporting injurious fall events only and does not represent the characteristics of the overall nursing workforce. A total of 111 nurses were involved in reporting 130 injurious fall events among hospitalized patients. The majority of reporting nurses were female (90.99%). Most reporting nurses were aged 31–40 years (55.86%), followed by those aged ≤ 30 years (34.23%), while nurses aged ≥ 41 years accounted for a smaller proportion.

Most reporting nurses held a bachelor’s degree (78.38%), followed by junior college (15.32%) and master’s degrees (5.41%). Regarding professional titles, supervisor nurses (37.84%) and staff nurses (35.14%) constituted the largest proportions, followed by junior nurses (24.32%), whereas associate chief nurses accounted for a small proportion (1.80%). Most nurses did not hold managerial positions (92.79%), while nursing team leaders accounted for 7.21%.

Most nurses reported only one event (87.39%), while 8.11% and 4.50% reported two and three events, respectively. Detailed information is presented in Table 2.

Discussion

Characteristics of patients with injurious fall events

The present study showed that injurious fall events among hospitalized patients from 2023 to 2025 were predominantly concentrated in older adults, with patients aged 61 years and above accounting for more than half of all cases. In this study, a higher proportion of injurious fall cases was observed among older patients. Previous systematic reviews have consistently identified advanced age as an important risk factor for falls in both hospital and community settings. In addition, prior fall history, comorbid conditions, and impairments in cognition, gait, and vision have been reported to be associated with an increased risk of falls [11]. These factors have been discussed in previous studies as possible contributors to increased vulnerability among older inpatients. A meta-analysis has identified muscle weakness, gait instability or impaired mobility, visual impairment, and a history of falls as important risk factors for inpatient falls [12]. However, the present study did not collect or analyze these variables; therefore, no association could be evaluated.

In terms of occurrence settings, injurious falls mainly occurred in patient rooms and bathrooms, followed by ward public areas, while relatively fewer events were observed in examination-related areas and outside the ward. This distribution suggests that injurious falls were frequently observed in areas where patients perform activities of daily living. Patient rooms and bathrooms are areas with frequent patient activity, where transfers, toileting, and bedside movements are commonly performed. During these activities, factors such as balance instability or mobility limitations may contribute to vulnerability to falls, although these factors were not directly assessed in the present study. Similar observations have been reported in previous studies. Öztürk et al. found that most inpatient falls occurred in patient rooms, followed by bathrooms and examination areas [2]. Furthermore, it has been reported that routine activities such as toileting, ambulation, bedside transfers, and bathing are associated with an increased risk of falls, and that activity context is closely related to injury severity [13]. In addition, patients requiring assistance with mobility or toileting have been shown to be at higher risk of injurious or severe fall-related outcomes [4]. Taken together, findings from previous studies suggest that injurious falls in hospitalized patients are more likely to occur during activities of daily living, particularly among individuals with limited functional capacity or increased care dependency.

Injurious fall events were relatively evenly distributed across shifts, whereas a higher proportion occurred during nursing resource–limited periods. According to the predefined definition in this study, these periods mainly included midday and nighttime hours, which are typically characterized by relatively lower nursing staffing levels according to institutional scheduling practices. These time periods may also coincide with changes in patient activity patterns, such as nighttime ambulation, toileting, and unassisted movement. However, this study did not further examine specific patient behaviors or nursing workload; therefore, the underlying mechanisms require further investigation in future studies.

With regard to departmental distribution, injurious fall events were more frequently observed in hematology, oncology, gastroenterology, and neurosurgery units. Patients in these departments may differ in disease characteristics, treatment exposure, and care needs, which could partly explain variations in the distribution of injurious fall events across departments. Previous studies have reported that falls in patients with hematological diseases are associated with multiple factors, including high comorbidity burden, intensive chemotherapy, use of sedative-hypnotics and diuretics, nocturnal toileting, dizziness, and impaired functional status [14]. Moreover, advanced age, prolonged hospitalization, and neurological conditions have also been identified as important factors associated with fall risk [15]. These factors have been discussed as possible contributors to fall vulnerability in previous studies. Overall, the observed distribution of injurious fall events may reflect differences in patient characteristics and treatment exposure across clinical departments; however, the specific mechanisms remain to be further clarified.

Regarding severity, most events were classified as Grade III nursing adverse events (88.46%), while Grade II events accounted for a relatively small proportion (11.54%). In terms of patient injury severity, Grade I injuries were the most common (80.00%), although approximately 20% of patients experienced Grade II or higher injuries. The observed distribution indicates that although most injurious falls resulted in mild harm, a non-negligible proportion involved moderate to severe injury. Previous studies have reported that approximately one-quarter to one-third of inpatient falls result in varying degrees of physical injury, with some progressing to fractures or head injuries [7–10]. Compared with findings from previous studies, the proportion of moderate to severe injuries in the present study was relatively lower, but still highlights that injurious falls should not be considered low-risk events. It is also important to note that differences exist between nursing adverse event grading and patient injury severity classification: the former focuses on the nature of the event and its impact on clinical processes, whereas the latter reflects the actual harm experienced by patients. Therefore, a single classification system may not fully capture the complexity of fall-related events in clinical practice, and a multidimensional assessment approach is warranted. Overall, although injurious falls were predominantly associated with mild outcomes in this study, their potential for serious harm should not be overlooked, and prevention strategies should consider both high-frequency low-severity events and low-frequency high-severity events in patient safety management.

Characteristics of nurses reporting injurious fall events

In this study, injurious fall events were predominantly reported by younger and early-to-mid-career nurses, with the highest proportion observed among nurses aged 31–40 years. Nurses with a bachelor’s degree and intermediate professional titles accounted for the majority of reporters. This distribution may reflect the characteristics of the reporting nurses included in this study; however, the underlying reasons for this pattern cannot be determined from the available data. In addition, as this study did not further analyze ward-level staffing patterns or workload distribution, more detailed interpretation of this distribution remains limited within the scope of the present data.

Regarding reporting behavior, most nurses reported only one injurious fall event during the study period. The reasons underlying this reporting pattern require further investigation in future studies. Notably, as an essential component of nursing quality management, the reporting of adverse events plays a critical role in patient safety surveillance and risk identification [16, 17]. Therefore, strengthening nurses’ awareness of fall events and improving their reporting practices remains important for enhancing the overall effectiveness of inpatient safety management systems.

Practical implications for fall prevention and nursing management

Based on existing evidence and the descriptive findings of this study, preventive strategies for injurious inpatient falls may benefit from a comprehensive, system-based, and context-specific multidimensional approach.

At the level of risk assessment, previous studies have suggested that early fall risk assessment and stratified management can help identify patients at different risk levels and provide a foundation for targeted interventions [18]. On this basis, clinical practice should further strengthen dynamic reassessment throughout hospitalization, integrating patients’ functional status, mobility capacity, and history of falls into a continuous monitoring system, which may facilitate ongoing risk identification and updating rather than relying solely on a single admission-based assessment.

Previous studies on environmental and situational management have indicated that inpatient falls commonly occur in patient rooms and bathrooms, which are common locations for routine patient activities [19]. From a behavioral–environmental interaction perspective, movements such as turning, grasping, pushing, and pulling in these settings may interact with environmental features and may contribute to fall occurrence, highlighting the importance of optimizing spatial design in fall prevention [20]. Furthermore, studies on environmental design and risk modeling have shown that factors such as lighting conditions, flooring characteristics, and spatial layout may all influence fall risk, while conventional fall risk assessment tools tend to focus primarily on intrinsic patient factors and insufficiently integrate environmental determinants [21]. Accordingly, fall prevention strategies may extend beyond warning signs or basic infrastructural arrangements and instead be integrated into a context-specific management framework, including standardized rounding procedures, targeted assistance in high-risk areas such as bathrooms, and timely support during patient mobility to reduce situational exposure to risk.

At the level of human resources and organizational management, previous studies have shown that inpatient falls may be associated with staffing levels, workload, and organizational factors, suggesting that fall prevention relies not only on individual risk assessment tools but also on system-level resource allocation and integrated management strategies [22]. Therefore, nursing management practice may consider strengthening staffing allocation and targeted surveillance during periods with relatively higher occurrence of events, which may support patient safety management and promote a shift toward risk-oriented and fine-grained scheduling strategies.

Systematic education and training interventions may improve nurses’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding fall prevention, thereby enhancing their ability to recognize and respond to risk [23]. In addition, simulation-based structured training (e.g., SBAR communication training) has been shown to improve nurses’ knowledge, technical skills, and communication clarity in patient safety scenarios [24]. Accordingly, fall prevention should not rely solely on institutional regulations, but should also be reinforced through continuous education, simulation training, and case-based review to strengthen clinical judgment and safety awareness.

Overall, inpatient fall prevention should be regarded as an ongoing system optimization process, the core of which lies in integrating risk assessment, environmental management, human resource allocation, and competency development to establish a multi-level and coordinated patient safety prevention system.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, this was a single-center retrospective study, and the findings may be influenced by institutional characteristics, which may limit the generalizability of the results. Second, the data were primarily extracted from the hospital information system, and important variables such as patients’ functional status, medication use, and environmental factors were not available for analysis. Third, this study did not further classify types of injuries, and the assessment of injury severity remains limited. In addition, as with most retrospective studies relying on routine reporting systems, there is a possibility of underreporting of injurious fall events, which may have affected the completeness of case capture. Importantly, this study was designed as a descriptive analysis; therefore, it was not intended to examine causal relationships or identify independent predictors associated with injurious falls. Future multicenter and prospective studies are needed to validate and extend these findings.

Conclusion

This study described the characteristics of injurious fall events among hospitalized patients in a tertiary hospital from 2023 to 2025. The results showed that such events were predominantly observed in older adults and mainly occurred in patient rooms and bathrooms. Certain clinical departments had relatively higher numbers of reported events, and a higher proportion of events was observed during periods defined as having limited nursing resources. At the nurse level, reporting was mainly performed by younger nurses and those with intermediate professional titles, and most nurses reported only a single event.

Overall, this study provides a systematic descriptive overview of injurious fall events from multiple perspectives, including patient characteristics, occurrence settings, temporal distribution, and nursing staff characteristics, which may provide information to support inpatient safety management.
