snRNA-seq of human ovaries reveals heat shock proteins are associated with obesity related cancer risk
Authors: Yuhan Zhang, Yonghui Jiang, Yunhai Yu, Gengchen Feng, Zihe Zhao, Weihan Zhang, Shumin Li, Yimeng Li, Ziyi Yang, Xueqi Yan, Xueying Gao, Zi-Jiang Chen, Han Zhao, Shigang Zhao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundObesity significantly impacts female reproductive health and increases the risk of gynecological tumors. However, the specific transcriptional changes that occur in the ovarian microenvironment during obesity-induced stress and the relationship between obesity and ovarian cancer remain unclear.MethodsOur study investigated the single-cell landscape of the ovarian cortex in individuals with varying BMI levels by snRNA-seq, revealing weight-stage related cellular composition deviations and expression profile irregularities. Results: Using single-cell high-dimensional Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (hdWGCNA), we identified distinct obesity-related gene modules within various subpopulations of stroma cells and blood vascular endothelial cells. Notably, we observed a negative correlation between BMI and heat shock protein (HSP) family genes. Specifically, we found that HSPD1 might function as a potential regulator of ovarian carcinogenesis and progression under conditions of obesity, as supported by our co-analysis with data from three bulk RNA-seq ovarian cancer databases. Our findings suggested that lower expression of HSPD1 indicated a poorer prognosis for ovarian cancer.ConclusionsOur study identified a cluster of genes in ovarian cells that are suppressed by obesity, including those belonging to HSP family genes. These findings provide valuable insights for investigating the link between obesity and ovarian diseases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-024-05898-x.Keywords: Obesity, Ovary, snRNA-seq, Heat shock proteins, HSPD1

Background

Obesity represents a significant public health concern on a global scale. Statistics indicate that more than 1.97 billion adults and over 340 million adolescents are affected by either overweight or obesity [1]. In recent years, a growing body of studies has demonstrated the detrimental effects of obesity on the female reproductive system, leading to hormonal disorders and ovulation abnormalities, which are significantly linked to infertility and adverse fertility outcomes [2]. Our recent work on single-cell sequencing of ovaries from obese mice found that obesity could have an influence on ovary microenvironment. Additionally, the study suggested that obesity promotes the emergence of ovarian tumor-like cells [3].

There is a relationship between obesity and tumors exactly [4–6]. Amount of clinical observation statistics reveals that obesity has been associated with various types of tumors, including many tumors of reproductive system such as endometrial cancer, ovarian cancer, and prostate cancer [4]. High BMI is related to various metabolic abnormalities [2, 7]. Excessive adipose tissue secretes pro-inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, reactive oxygen species (ROS) and improve immune cell activation, thereby causes the inflammation. These disorders can impact tumor proliferation and metastasis, leading to more aggressive types [6, 7]. However, the specific transcriptional alterations occurring in human ovaries under the influence of obesity stress have not been fully elucidated. Whether there is a connection between these changes and carcinogenesis is still uncertain.

This study explored the transcriptional impact of obesity on ovarian cortical tissues obtained from three women suffering from resection of benign ovarian tumors with varying BMI values, ranging from normal to obese. With the application of single-cell high-dimensional Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (hdWGCNA) [8], gene modules associated with obesity phenotype were identified. We further investigated functions of core genes from co-expression network during progression of tumor by integrating three ovarian cancer databases. This study complements the ovarian transcriptional atlas by providing a detailed cellular landscape of the ovarian cortex at the single-cell level, across varying obesity levels, and offers insights into the cancer risk associated with obesity.

Methods

Tissue collection

The ovarian tissue samples used in this study were obtained from the normal cortical region of three patients who underwent surgical resection for benign ovarian tumors. Detailed clinical information is provided in Fig. S1. All patients had signed informed consent for sample collection and data analysis prior to surgery.

Histological analysis

Three sets of ovarian samples were fixed in 4% formalin overnight at 4 °C, followed by dehydration in graded ethanol solution and xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Sectioning paraffin-embedded ovaries at a thickness of 5 μm and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) reagent. Finally, images of the tissue sections were captured using a light microscope.

Tissue processing, isolation and dissociation

The obtained ovarian samples were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) buffer, then cut into pieces. The tissue pieces were digested with 1.5 ml GEXSCOPE Tissue Dissociation Solution (Singleron Biotechnologies) at 4 °C. After digestion, a 40-micron sterile strainer was used to purify the nuclei. We centrifuged the processed nuclei, and resuspended the samples in PBS (HyClone). Finally, the samples were centrifuged and again resuspended in PBS.

snRNA-seq library preparation and sequencing

Single-nucleus RNA-seq libraries were constructed following the Singleron GEXSCOPE protocol (Singleron Biotechnologies). Then, individual libraries were diluted to 4 nM and pooled for sequencing. The cDNA libraries were sequenced using the HiSeq X Ten platform (Illumina), with a paired-end read length of 150 bp [9].

snRNA-seq data processing

The 10 × Chromium single-nuclei RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) data was preprocessed using the CellRanger pipeline. Write CellRanger basic steps and STAR reference genome. Downstream analyses were mainly conducted by the Seurat R package (4.2.1). To filter out low-quality cells, the following criteria were applied: cells with 200 < features < 5000, 300 < gene counts < 15,000, mitochondrial gene percentage < 10%, erythrocyte gene ratio < 10%. Counts were normalized using default settings (Function ‘NormalizeData’) with log-normalized algorithms. After that, cell cycle effects were removed by scaling cycle-related genes with ‘ScaleData’.

Data integration and dimensionality reduction

Batch effects of three sets of data were mitigated using Harmony R package. After that, the top 3000 highly variable genes (HVG) were obtained using ‘FindVariableFeatures’ function. ‘ScaleData’ and ‘RunPCA’ (extraction of the first 20 principal components) were sequentially applied to these genes to obtain a preliminary downscaling matrix. Cell clustering was done using the ‘FindClusters’ function (resolution = 0.5) resulting in a total of 13 clusters of cells. The function ‘RunUMAP’ was then implemented to downscale the data and the results were presented as 2D images using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP).

Identification of genes characteristics in each population

In order to describe the biological definition and function of the cell populations, marker genes for the initial 13 clusters and the annotated 7 types of cells were computed by the ‘FindAllMarkers’ function. Expression log2 fold change > 1 and adjusted p-value < 0.05 were as the filter criterion for gene lists. Differentially expressed genes (DEG) of stroma cells and blood vascular endothelial cells across different BMIs were calculated by the ‘FindMarkers’ function with the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Significant DEGs were defined with expression log2 fold change > 1 and adjusted p-value < 0.05.

Cell trajectory analysis

The Monocle3 R package was used to predict the differentiation trajectories of stroma cells and blood vascular endothelial cells. The parameters used were the official recommended values. Cell trajectories were simulated using the ‘learn_graph’ function. The starting point of the trajectory was determined with reference to the cell differentiation potential score calculated by the Cytotrace R package (https://cytotrace.stanford.edu/).

Analysis of co-expressed gene modules

The hdWGCNA R package was employed to analyze characteristic modules of stroma cells and blood vascular endothelial cells. The top 3000 highly variable genes were selected, and the metacell matrix was transposed using the KNN algorithm via the ‘MetacellsByGroups’ function. The ‘SetDatExpr’ function was used to build the expression matrix, selecting the optimal soft-power (4 for SC, 5 for BEC). Co-expression networks were then built using the ‘ConstructNetwork’ function, resulting in a total of four SC gene modules and eight BEC gene modules. The eigengenes in the modules were obtained with the ‘ModuleEigengenes’ function and the top 50 hub genes of the modules were listed with the ‘GetHubGenes’ function. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to assess the correlation between module genes and traits. The change in expression of module genes along differentiation trajectories was determined by calculating the expression linkage at each point after dividing the trajectory time into 50 equal parts.

Gene set-based HSP scoring

Cell expression levels at different BMIs were scored using the ‘AddModuleScore’ function. A list of HSP family genes obtained from the Enrichr database was used as a reference for scoring.

Protein interaction network analysis

The top 50 and top 100 marker genes from SC3 were imported into the STRING database (http://string-db.org/) to construct the interconnections of the corresponding proteins of each gene. The node relationships were imported into Cytoscape 3.10.1 software. Cytohubba plug-in was used to calculate the Maximum Clique Centrality (MCC) value, and top 10 genes were taken as the key targets to construct the protein–protein interaction (PPI) network.

Enrichment analysis

To identify the potential biological functions and corresponding pathway information of genes, the Enrichr website (https://maayanlab.cloud/Enrichr) was used to enrich marker genes, DEGs, and hub genes obtained from WGCNA analysis. The reference databases used were Gene Ontology Biological Processes (GO BP) and Reactome.

Survival analysis

Kaplan–Meier plot assessment (http://kmplot.com/analysis/) and the OncoLnc database (http://www.oncolnc.org) were utilized to obtain information on high/low expression (cutoff = 50%) of HSP family genes in serous ovarian cancer and clinical information. Then, the correlation between patients' survival status and the expression of selected genes was calculated with Cox univariate analysis. Similarly, the survival curve of HSPD1 was plotted with HSPD1 expression cutoff set at 75% using the GEPIA2 website (http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn/).

Immunohistochemistry

To investigate the expression of HSPD1, we performed immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining on formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue sections. The staining was carried out by a trained pathologist utilizing a standardized protocol. Tissue sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval and quenching of endogenous peroxidase activity. Non-specific protein binding was blocked prior to incubation with primary antibodies. The sections were incubated with primary antibodies (Anti-HSPD1, Cell Signaling Technology, 12165) at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubation with corresponding secondary antibodies at room temperature. Diaminobenzidine (DAB) was used for chromogenic detection, and the sections were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, and mounted. Images were captured using KFBIO Digital Pathology Slide Scanner.

Statistics

DEGs were identified by Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Differences were considered significant if p-value was 0.05 or less. Enrichment analysis was calculated by Fisher’s exact test followed by Benjamini–Hochberg correction with an adjusted p-value threshold of 0.05.

Results

Characterization of cellular composition in the human ovarian cortex in response to obesity

In this study, we conducted a single-cell atlas analysis on the human ovarian cortex to investigate the impact of obesity. Ovarian cortical samples were obtained from normal part of three patients with benign ovarian tumor surgery, each with varying BMI, including one non-obese individual (BMI 19.6), one with class I obesity (BMI 33.9) and one with class III obesity (BMI 42.9) according to WHO criteria (Fig. 1A) [10]. The clinical information of the patients and the histological morphology of the ovarian cortex were presented in Fig. S1A and S1C. Pathological images of primary lesions are presented in Fig. S1B, showing no signs of invasion or metastasis. Single-nucleus libraries were prepared, resulting in 21,755 nuclei for downstream analysis, with an average of 1617 genes per single-cell nucleus. To ensure high-quality data, we filtered out dead cells and hematopoietic cells based on mitochondrial gene (MT gene) expression ratio (< 10%) and erythrocyte gene (RB gene) expression ratio (< 10%) (Fig. S1D). Subsequently, unsupervised clustering by UMAP revealed 13 distinct clusters of cells (res = 0.5, Fig. S1E), which were manually annotated into 7 cell types, including stroma cells (SC), blood vascular endothelial cells (BEC), lymphatic endothelial cells (LEC), granulosa cells (GC), steroidogenic cells (StC), immune cells (IMC) and smooth muscle cells (SMC) (Fig. 1B). Further analysis of marker genes using 'FindAllMarkers' to identify the top 50 markers for each cell type (Fig. 1C, Table S1).

To elucidate the relationship between ovarian cellular composition and obesity, we examined the distribution characteristics of each cell type across the different BMI individuals. Notably, all seven cell types were present in the samples, albeit with significant variability in numbers. Our analysis revealed two major cellular changes: a decrease in the percentage of SCs and an increase in the percentage of BECs with increasing BMI (Fig. 1D). Moreover, the variability of ovarian cellular composition increased with severity of obesity, particularly in class III obesity where the variability was most pronounced (Fig. 1D). These findings shed light on the altered cellular profiles in the ovarian cortex in response to obesity stress.

Reduction of HSP gene expression in ovarian stroma cells caused by obesity

As Fig. 1D shown, stroma cells (SC) are the predominant cell type in the ovarian cortex. We identified six clusters of SCs at the initial resolution shown in Fig. 1A, which were further clustered into three distinct subpopulations based their correlations (Figs. 2A, S2A). Notably, the cell ratio of the largest cluster SC1 decreases gradually with increasing BMI. These cells expressed high levels of genes such as HSP90AA1, HSP90AB1, DNAJB1, HSPB1, HSPH1, with enrichment in the HSF1-associated pathway (Figs. 2B, C, S2B). In contrast, the SC2 cell ratio increased with BMI, and its marker genes, such as KCNIP4, GRIK2, LAMA4, were enriched in glycosylation and transcription-promoting pathways (Figs. 2B, C, S2B). SC3 was specifically presented in the stroma cells of the obesity III individual, with characteristic genes such as CENPE, TOP2A, and MKI67 (Fig. 2B, C). These genes were mainly involved in the regulation of cell division and cell cycle overexpressed in aggressive tumors leading to the progression and metastasis of tumor cells (Fig. S2C) [11]. Notably, the increasing expression of proliferation-associated marker genes in ovarian stroma cells, particularly in SC3, suggested potential malignant activities associated with obesity. Furthermore, our analysis revealed that the top markers in SC3 were significantly overexpressed in a cohort of transcriptome data of tumor tissues from ovarian cancer patients from the TCGA database, indicating a potential malignant transformation in SC3 (Figs. 2D, S2D). To infer the core genes of SC3, we also constructed a PPI network using the STING database and screened a set of strongly associated networks by Cytoscape (Fig. 2E). Top 10 core genes regulating the malignant potential were found.

To further investigate the characteristics altered gene expression patterns and potential regulatory genes, we employed the new analysis method, single-cell hdWGCNA, which combines complex clinical information including BMI, age, gender, tumor type accurately and constructs trait-associated gene sets. As Fig. 2F shown, we constructed four SC co-expression modules based on top 3000 highly variable genes (Table S2). Notably, module 2 (SC_M2) showed a strong positive correlation with BMI while module 4 (SC_M4) demonstrated a highly negative correlation with BMI in all SCs (Fig. 2F). Subsequently, the trajectory mapping of all SCs with Monocle 3 revealed a differentiation trend from SC2 to SC1, indicating that the SC2 population was naiver (Figs. 2G, S2E). Furthermore, through jointly analysis with the SCs cell differentiation trajectory, we found that SC2 differentiated towards SC1 cells. Genes in SC_M2 were notably upregulated in individuals classified as obese III (Fig. 2H). The hub genes, including KCNIP4, GPC6, GRIK2, LAMA4, FAM155A, were mainly related to the mesenchymal epithelial transition (MET) pathway, cellular connectivity, and signal transduction (Figs. 2I, S2F). On the other hand, SC_M4, whose representative genes including HSP90AA1, HSPA8, DNAJB1, HSPD1, and HSPH1, were downregulated in these two obese individuals (Figs. 2H, I, S2F). The reduced expression of HSP-related genes in the obese individuals suggested that obesity impaired the normal HSP response during stroma cells differentiation. Consistently, differential analysis of all stroma cells also confirmed the abnormal down-regulation of HSP-related genes in the obese groups (Fig. 2J). Besides, module 1 (SC_M1), including genes associated with cell cycle such as TOP2A, NCAPG, KIF18B, was specially highly expressed in the sever obesity individual and had positive correlation with BMI in all SCs (Fig. 2H, I). This module might be related to carcinogenesis and was in accordance with the SC subgroup having malignant potential (Fig. S2F) [11].

Reduction of HSP gene expression in ovarian blood vascular endothelial cells under obesity stress

In the ovary, blood vessels play a critical role in transporting oxygen, hormones, and other substances, as well as responding to external stimuli for nutrient demand. Therefore, understanding the changes in blood vasculature in the over-nourished environment induced by obesity is particularly crucial [12–14]. In our investigation, two clusters of BECs were identified and annotated as VWFhigh BECs and ENPP2high BECs. The nomenclature for these clusters was based on the characteristic expression of these two marker genes, which are recognized as specific markers for BECs (Fig. 3A, B) (Table S3). The DEGs for VWFhigh cluster revealed high expression of DNM3, PDE7, NOSTRIN, ADGRG6. These genes mainly enriched in the intracellular activation of G-protein-coupled-receptor-mediated signaling, indicating an active response to external stimuli (Figs. 3C, S3A). On the other hand, DEGs for the ENPP2high cluster, showing more significance than VWFhigh cluster, were enriched in the VEGF and NOTCH pathways, pivotal for arterial endothelium formation, and contributing to platelet activation (Figs. 3C, S3A). Pseudotime analysis using Monocle3 with Cytotrace unveiled a differentiation trajectory with the ENPP2high subpopulation at the anterior and the VWFhigh subpopulation at the terminal end (Figs. 3D, S3B).

Further exploration of the alteration in blood vascular endothelial mapping under obesity stress revealed a positive correlation between BECs and BMI, which differed from the changing pattern observed in stroma cells (Fig. 3E). Utilizing hdWGCNA analysis, eight expression-correlation modules were constructed based on the top 3000 highly variable genes in BECs (Fig. 3F) (Table S2). Notably, we focused on the module 2 (BEC_M2), which exhibited a positive correlation with BMI. Moreover, along the BECs differentiation trajectory, BEC_M2 showing an upward trend and significant up-regulation in the obese III individual (Fig. 3F, G). The BEC_M2 included signature genes such as KIAA1549L, PDE7B, MTMR10, ADGRG6, ADAMTS18, which were associated with myofiber contraction, coronary angiogenesis, ECM organization, and cellular connectivity, mostly observed in VWFhigh BEC (Figs. 3H, S3C, S3D, S4A), suggesting their response to obesity-induced pressure and promotion of angiogenesis. Conversely, module 5 (BEC_M5) showed a negative correlation with BMI. Genes of BEC_M5 were highly expressed in the transitional state of the BEC trajectory and down-regulated in all obese individuals (Fig. 3F, G). Transcription factors, including NR4A1, BTG2, ATF3, and EGR3, were identified in BEC_M5, and may play a role in regulating cell proliferation and prompted the tumor progression. (Fig. S3D) [15–20]. Notably, representative members of the HSP family genes were found in BEC_M5 (Figs. 3H, S3D), potentially indicating the tendency of blood vascular endothelium toward differentiated cell states and contributing to abnormal endothelial types and functions in response to obesity. The HSP expression score in all blood vascular endothelial cells and the DEG analysis further indicated a significant reduction in HSP family genes in the obese individuals, particularly noticeable in ENPP2high BECs (Fig. 3I, J).

Implications of low HSPD1 expression in ovarian cancer prognosis

The above findings revealed a decrease in HSP gene expression within both stroma cells and blood vascular endothelial cells in the high BMI individuals. Furthermore, we confirmed expression level of HSP-related genes among all types of ovarian cells both in human and obesity animal models, then obtained a consistent downregulation trend of HSP score (Figs. S5A–C).

Apart from this, obesity is known to be a risk factor for tumor progression [4]. To further investigate the association of these trait genes with tumor prognosis, we conducted Cox regression analysis to assess the correlation between the expression of HSP family genes in cancer tissues and survival risk in serous ovarian cancer using the K–M Plot (http://kmplot.com/analysis/) and the Oncolnc database (http://www.oncolnc.org). We found lower HSPD1 expression was linked to poorer survival in patients with ovarian cancer both in K-M Plot and the Oncolnc database (Fig. 4A). This association was further validated using transcriptomic data of ovarian cancer from the TCGA database obtained from GEPIA2 website (http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn/). It showed that patients with low HSPD1 expression in ovarian cancer experienced significantly poorer survival outcomes compared to those with high HSPD1 expression (p-value = 0.028), confirming the adverse impact of low HSPD1 expression on ovarian cancer outcomes (Fig. 4B). Additionally, HSPD1 expression was consistently reduced in overall level of obese patients (Fig. 4C, D). In the meanwhile, the same differential expression was validated in our previous snRNA-seq dataset from obese mice ovarian tissue [3], where Hspd1 was down-regulated in both diet-induced obese mice and genetically-induced obese mice (Fig. 4E).

To verify the above findings, we performed immunohistochemical experiments of HSPD1, which revealed a reduced expression pattern in obese groups (Fig. 4F). This result at protein level was consistent with our omics analysis findings. Additionally, four ovarian samples were included for further validation at the protein level (two from non-obese individuals and two from overweight individuals). The immunohistochemical results demonstrated consistent alterations, indicating that HSPD1 expression decreased under obesity (Fig. S6).

Discussion

Obesity disrupts normal endocrine rhythms, affecting ovulatory behavior and oocyte quality [21, 22]. Previous studies have shown functional abnormalities of oocytes in obese women, such as apoptosis activation and abnormal mitochondrial function [21–23]. However, there is limited understanding of the role of components in the extrafollicular ovarian microenvironment tissues initiated by obesity. Our study used snRNA-seq analysis to create a single-cell depiction of ovarian cortex from individuals with varying obesity classes. We focused on analyzing alterations at the transcriptional level in the two main cell types, stroma cells and blood vascular endothelial cells, which exhibit opposite trends with BMI.

The dominant cell types in ovary are altered in obesity, with a positive correlation observed between the degree of alteration in cellular proportions and features and the severity of obesity. Available studies point the functions of stroma cells that they mainly synthesize collagen and other extracellular matrix components, involve in steroidogenesis, and repair ovarian structures [23–25]. We discovered subgroups newly enriched in regulating transcription and assisting in ovary homeostasis. The number of stroma cells decreases with obesity, particularly in a subpopulation identified by HSP-related genes, which exhibits lower differentiation levels. Activating genes of obesity in stroma cells are comprised of LAMA4, GRIK2, FAM155A, and KCNIP4, which have been established a connection with BMI, involve in signal transduction and decrease cell proliferation [26–29]. It suggests that the growth and proliferation of stroma cells in obese individuals are inhibited, and may cause disruption to the structure of ovaries.

The principle vasculature of the ovary is located in the medulla, whereas the cortex comprises predominantly small, branching arterioles and micro-vessels [12]. Blood vessels in cortex play a vital role in supporting the nutrition during follicular growth and development [12–14]. Single-cell mapping of endothelial alterations within several organs including brain, heart, lung, spleen, liver, and adipose tissue of obese individuals has been previously described, but ovarian tissue is missing [30]. We identified two subpopulations of ovarian blood vascular endothelial cells within the cortex, with different functional characteristics related to obesity reactivity. Cells of obese individuals suffer from undernourished and hypoxic conditions, causing the secretion of pro-vascular factors that promote blood vascular endothelial proliferation [31]. We found a positive correlation between cell number of BECs and BMI. Moreover, the gene module activated by obesity, which comprises ADGRG6, PDE7B, KIAA1549L, was found to specifically impact blood vascular endothelial cells, thus influencing the VEGF pathway and contributing to the aberrant activation of the angiogenic pathway under the stress of high BMI, consistent with the observed alterations in cell number. This heightened proliferation aligns with previous findings [32–34].

Furthermore, obesity modifies the typical transcriptional expression pattern of cells in the ovaries, resulting in the emergence of abnormally operating cell populations. HSP family genes, which are significantly downregulated in obesity, are presented in stroma cells and blood vascular endothelial cells. To further investigate these obesity-related changes at protein level, we conducted immunohistochemical analyses. The results were consistent with sequencing and reinforced the reliability of our findings. HSP family genes encode heat shock proteins constituting a highly conserved family of proteins, which can be categorized into six distinct groups based on their molecular size, including HSP100, HSP90, HSP70, HSP60, HSP40, and small HSPs [35]. Heat shock proteins are involved in essential processes, serving as molecular chaperones for proper folding and repair of proteins, contributing to cytoskeleton formation [36]. These proteins are transcriptionally activated in response to diverse physical, chemical, and biological stressors, playing a crucial role in maintaining intracellular homeostasis [35]. In the ovary, they participate in reproductive hormone responses [36], and are widely expressed throughout all stages of germ cell development, ensure ovarian microenvironment stability, therefore significantly influence the process of ovulation [24, 36]. In obese individuals, a long-term chronic inflammatory environment due to metabolic disorders and inflammatory factors secreted by excess adipose tissue results in elevated ROS, ER activation, and impaired mitochondrial function [37]. The pathological extracellular environment leads to abnormal stress response and biosynthesis of body tissues [38, 39], then causes the reaction of stress genes like HSP family genes. Further, the expression of HSP family genes may be reduced linked to the adaptation and the production of autoantibodies in obese patients experiencing prolonged stress [36]. Existing studies have shown that abnormal glucose lipid metabolism and inflammation can lead to reduced HSP expression in the ovary, resulting in decreased oocyte and granulosa cell viability [40]. HSP family genes, known for regulating key factors in the activation of the mitochondrial apoptotic pathway, play a pivotal role in cellular senescence, autophagy processes, and also impact angiogenesis and EMT progression. Consequently, HSP family genes have been the subject of extensive research in the field of oncology [41]. A cluster of cells discovered in stroma cells with cancerous potential, which were exclusively found in the obese III individual, possibly resulting from the down-regulation of expression of HSP genes.

Specifically, HSPD1, which encodes the HSP60 family of proteins serving as molecular chaperones for mitochondrial proteins to ensure normal cellular energy metabolism and oxidation–reduction reaction [41], is a bi-directional participant in tumor progression [42]. It plays a protective role in ovarian cancer through the ability to induce apoptosis through caspases and mediate downstream MAPK activation of ROS to hinder tumor cell proliferation [41, 43]. HSPD1 manifests impaired expression under conditions of obesity stress, thus contributing to adverse clinical outcomes subsequent to tumorigenesis.

However, some limitations must be acknowledged. Due to the specificity of ovarian sampling, our study included a limited sample size, which may restrict the generalizability of the results. Despite this constraint, the reliability of our conclusions was enhanced through advanced analytical techniques and verification of external datasets. Additionally, our data only encompassed partial characteristics of the ovarian cortex, preventing us from examining changes and functions in the medulla during the obesity process. Further investigation into the altered pathways associated with obesity, particularly concerning HSP family genes, is warranted.

In conclusion, our study developed a transcriptional profile of obese ovarian cortical tissues at single-cell level and identified a potential mechanism for cellular carcinogenesis caused by obesity, probably by the suppression of HSP family genes. Especially, HSPD1 deficiency was associated with a poor prognosis in ovarian cancer. These findings suggested potential avenues for preventing obesity-related ovarian carcinogenesis.

Conclusions

Our study of transcriptional profile of obese ovarian cortical tissues identified a cluster of genes in ovarian cells that are suppressed by obesity, including those belonging to the HSP family genes. Notably, we observed HSPD1 as a potential biomarker for poor prognosis in obesity-related ovarian cancer.
