Embedding H ∞ ​ ( 𝔻 ) H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D}) into L ∞ ​ ( 𝕋 ) L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}) : a proof without non-tangential limits
Mario P. Maletzki

Abstract
The purpose of this note is to show in an accessible and self-contained way the existence of an isometric algebra embedding from H ∞ ​ ( 𝔻 ) H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D}) into L ∞ ​ ( 𝕋 ) L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}) , without appealing to Fatou’s classical theorem on non-tangential limits of analytic functions, and relying only on results from complex and functional analysis that are typically covered in a standard undergraduate course.



1
Introduction

Beyond its intrinsic interest, the existence of an isometric embedding
H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})
has proven to be useful in the study of the classical Hardy space
H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
of bounded analytic functions on
\mathbb{D}
, and for its applications (such as the identification of the
Shilov boundary
of
H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
or the study interpolating sequences for this space in terms of Poisson integrals) the reader is referred to
Garnett (1981)
. For background and notation on functional and complex analysis see
Conway (1990)
and
Rudin (1987)
respectively, and for basic facts on the theory of Hardy spaces see for instance
Duren (1970)
.

We first recall that the Poisson kernel in the unit disk is given by

P_{r}(\theta):=\frac{1-r^{2}}{1-2r\cos(\theta)+r^{2}}=\frac{1-|re^{i\theta}|^{2}}{|1-re^{i\theta}|^{2}},\quad\mbox{ for }0\leq r<1,

and that it is
an approximate identity for
L^{1}(\mathbb{T})
, which means that viewing the Poisson kernel as a net
(P_{r})_{r}\subset L^{1}(\mathbb{T})
, we have that
||P_{r}\ast f-f||_{1}\rightarrow 0
as
r\rightarrow 1
. The last fact follows from the following well-known properties:

i)

P_{r}(\sigma)
is decreasing on
0<\sigma<\pi
,

ii)

P_{r}(\sigma)\geq 0
for every
\sigma
,

iii)

P_{r}(\sigma)=P_{r}(-\sigma)
for every
0<\sigma<\pi
,

iv)

It holds

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}P_{r}(\sigma)\ d\sigma=1.

v)

For every
\delta>0
,

\lim_{r\rightarrow 1}\sup_{|t|\geq\delta}\{P_{r}(t)\}=0.

Given a harmonic function
u
in a disk
D(a,R)
of the complex plane that is also continuous in
\overline{D(a,R)}
, we have that for each
z=a+\rho e^{i\sigma}\in D(a,R)
the following equality holds

u(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}u(e^{it})P_{\frac{\rho}{R}}(\sigma-t)\ dt,

(1)

which is called the
Poisson integral formula for harmonic functions
. To derive this equality, we first prove that as a consequence of both Cauchy’s integral theorem and Cauchy’s integral formula, we have that (
1
) holds for analytic functions, and from there it will be straightforward that
1
holds for harmonic functions.

Lemma 1
.

If
f
is an analytic function on
D(0,1+\varepsilon)
for some
\varepsilon>0
, then

f(re^{i\sigma})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(e^{it})P_{r}(\sigma-t)\ dt\quad\mbox{ for every }\ re^{i\sigma}\in\mathbb{D}

(2)

Proof.

By Cacuhy’s integral formula we have

f(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}\frac{f(w)}{w-z}\ dw\quad\mbox{ for every }\ z\in\mathbb{D},

and since for any
z\in\mathbb{D}
the function
w\mapsto\frac{f(w)}{w-1/\overline{z}}
is holomorphic on an open set containing
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
, by Cauchy’s integral theorem we have
\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}\frac{f(w)}{w-1/\overline{z}}\ dw=0
, and thus

f(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}f(w)\left(\frac{1}{w-z}-\frac{1}{w-1/\overline{z}}\right)\ dw.

Now, if
w\in\partial\mathbb{D}
then
\frac{1}{w}=\overline{w}
, and computing

\frac{1}{w-z}-\frac{1}{w-1/\overline{z}}=\frac{(w-1/\overline{z})-(w-z)}{w^{2}-w(1/\overline{z}+z)+z/\overline{z}}=\frac{z-1/\overline{z}}{w^{2}-w(1/\overline{z}+z)+z/\overline{z}}=

=\frac{1}{w}\frac{|z|^{2}-1}{w\overline{z}-(1+|z|^{2})+z/w}=\frac{1}{w}\frac{1-|z|^{2}}{1-2\Re(z\overline{w})+|z|^{2}},

and we conclude

f(re^{i\sigma})=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}\frac{f(w)}{w}\frac{1-|z|^{2}}{1-2\Re(z\overline{w})+|z|^{2}}\ dw=

=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{f(e^{it})}{e^{it}}\frac{1-r^{2}}{1-2r\cos(\sigma-t)+r^{2}}ie^{it}\ dw=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(e^{it})P_{r}(\sigma-t)\ dt.

∎

Remark
.

From this we see that if
f
is holomorphic on
\mathbb{D}
and
re^{i\sigma}\in\mathbb{D}
, then for
r<\rho<1
, the function
f_{\rho}(z):=f(\rho z)
is holomorphic on
D(0,1/\rho)
and therefore

f(re^{i\sigma})=f_{\rho}((r/\rho)e^{i\sigma})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f_{\rho}(e^{it})P_{r/\rho}(\sigma-t)\ dt.

To simplify the notation, for a point
z=re^{i\sigma}\in\mathbb{D}
we will write
P_{z}(t):=P_{r}(\sigma-t)
, and for a function
f
defined on
\mathbb{T}
, we may consider the function defined on
[-\pi,\pi]
and write
f(t)
instead of
f(e^{it})
.

2
Existence of isometric embedding

We now proceed to show how the existence of the isometric embedding
H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})
can be deduced from Banach-Alaoglu’s theorem, which assures that any bounded sequence in the dual of a separable normed space has a
w^{*}
-convergent subsequence.

Theorem 1
.

Given
f\in H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
, there is a function
f^{*}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})
such that for every
re^{i\sigma}\in\mathbb{D}
it holds

f(re^{i\sigma})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f^{*}(e^{it})P_{r}(\sigma-t)\ dt.

The mapping
f\mapsto f^{*}
defines an isometric algebra homomorphism from
H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
into
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})
.

For the proof of the theorem we will need the following lemma:

Lemma 2
.

The linear space generated by
\{P_{z}\}_{z\in\mathbb{D}}
is dense in
L^{1}(\mathbb{T})
.

Proof.

If
\overline{\langle\{P_{z}\}_{z\in\mathbb{D}}\rangle}\neq L^{1}(\mathbb{T})
, by the Hahn-Banach theorem we would find a non-zero function
f\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})
such that

(f\ast P_{r})(\sigma)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(e^{it})P_{z}(t)\ dt=0\quad\mbox{ for every }z=re^{i\sigma}\in\mathbb{D},

and therefore for a fixed
0<r<1
it is
f\ast P_{r}\equiv 0
. Now, since
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})\subset L^{1}(\mathbb{T})
and
(P_{r})_{r}
is an approximate identity for
L^{1}(\mathbb{T})
, we have
||P_{r}\ast f-f||_{1}\rightarrow 0
as
r\rightarrow 1
, and thus that
f=0
, which is a contradiction.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1
.

Let
f_{r}(t):=f(rt)\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})
for
0<r<1
.

The Maximal Modulus Principle implies that
||f_{r}||\leq||f_{s}||
if
0<r\leq s<1
, and thus
||f_{r}||\leq\lim_{s\rightarrow 1}||f_{s}||=||f||
. Since
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})\cong(L^{1}(\mathbb{T}))^{*}
and
L^{1}(\mathbb{T})
is separable, we have that
\overline{B_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}(0,||f||)}
is
w^{*}
-sequentially compact, and therefore for any sequence
(f_{r_{n}})_{n}
where
(r_{n})\subset(0,1)
with
r_{n}\rightarrow 1
, there is a subsequence
(f_{r_{n_{k}}})_{k}
and a function
f^{*}\in\overline{B_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}(0,||f||)}
such that
f_{r_{n_{k}}}\xrightarrow{w^{*}}f^{*}
.

For a fixed
re^{i\sigma}\in\mathbb{D}
, considering any
r<r_{n_{k}}<1
, we have that

f(re^{i\sigma})-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f^{*}(e^{it})P_{r}(\sigma-t)\ dt=

\frac{1}{2\pi}\left(\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f_{r_{n_{k}}}(e^{it})P_{\frac{r}{r_{n_{k}}}}(\sigma-t)\ dt-\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f^{*}(e^{it})P_{r}(\sigma-t)\ dt\right)=

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f_{r_{n_{k}}}(e^{it})(P_{\frac{r}{r_{n_{k}}}}-P_{r})(\sigma-t)\ dt-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}(f_{r_{n_{k}}}-f^{*})(e^{it})P_{r}(\sigma-t)\ dt

and thus

\left|f(re^{i\sigma})-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f^{*}(e^{it})P_{r}(\sigma-t)\ dt\right|\leq

\left|\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f_{r_{n_{k}}}(e^{it})(P_{\frac{r}{r_{n_{k}}}}-P_{r})(\sigma-t)dt\right|+\left|\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}(f_{r_{n_{k}}}-f^{*})(e^{it})P_{r}(\sigma-t)dt\right|

\leq||f||_{\infty}||P_{\frac{r}{r_{n_{k}}}}-P_{r})||_{1}+\left|\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}(f_{r_{n_{k}}}-f^{*})(e^{it})P_{r}(\sigma-t)\ dt\right|,

and taking limits as
k\rightarrow\infty
, since
r_{n_{k}}\rightarrow 1
and
f_{r_{n_{k}}}\xrightarrow{w^{*}}f^{*}
, we conclude that

f(re^{i\sigma})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f^{*}(e^{it})P_{r}(\sigma-t)\ dt.

To see that
f\mapsto f^{*}
defines an isometric algebra homomorphism from
H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
into
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})
, we first check that
i:H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})\rightarrow L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})
with
i(f):=f^{*}
is well-defined, that is, it does not depend on the sequence
(r_{n})_{n}
. Considering another sequence
(r_{n}^{\prime})_{n}\subset(0,1)
with
r_{n}^{\prime}\rightarrow 1
, this would lead to another function
g^{*}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})
such that

f(re^{i\sigma})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}g^{*}(e^{it})P_{r}(\sigma-t)\ dt\quad\mbox{ for every }re^{i\sigma}\in\mathbb{D},

but then we would have for every
z=re^{i\sigma}\in\mathbb{D}
that

(f^{*}-g^{*})(P_{z})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}(f^{*}-g^{*})(e^{it})P_{r}(\sigma-t)\ dt=f(z)-f(z)=0,

and by the previous lemma we would conclude that
f^{*}=g^{*}
. That
i
is a linear homomorphism is straightforward, and since
||f^{*}|\leq||f||
, it is only left to see that
||f||\leq||f^{*}||
, but this is clear because

|f(z)|\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}|f^{*}(e^{it})|P_{r}(\sigma-t)\ dt\leq\frac{||f^{*}||}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}P_{r}(\sigma-t)\ dt=||f^{*}||,

and thus
||f||=\sup_{z\in\mathbb{D}}|f(z)|\leq||f^{*}||

∎

3
Extending the embedding to harmonic function

Since in the proof of Theorem
1
we only used the fact that
f
is holomorphic on
\mathbb{D}
when we claimed that

f(re^{i\sigma})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f_{r_{n_{k}}}(e^{it})P_{r/{r_{n_{k}}}}(\sigma-t)\ dt,

and the same equality holds for complex-valued harmonic function
\mathbb{D}
, it is straightforward that the isometric algebra embedding can actually be extended to
h^{\infty}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{D})
, the Hardy space of bounded complex-valued harmonic functions on
\mathbb{D}
.

Moreover, the isometric embedding
h_{\mathbb{C}}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})
turns out to be surjective:

Theorem 2
.

If
f
is a bounded complex-valued harmonic function on
\mathbb{D}
, then there is a function
f^{*}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})
such that for every
re^{i\sigma}\in\mathbb{D}
it holds

f(re^{i\sigma})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f^{*}(e^{it})P_{r}(\sigma-t)\ dt.

The mapping
f\mapsto f^{*}
defines an isometric algebra isomorphism from
h^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
onto
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})
.

To prove this, we first recall that for an integrable function
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T})
its
Poisson integral
is defined by

F(z):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(e^{it})P_{z}(t)\ dt,

and that it satisfies the following:

Proposition 1
.

For any real-valued function
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T})
, its Poisson integral
F(z)
is an harmonic function on
\mathbb{D}
.

Proof.

Consider the function
g:[-\pi,\pi]\times\mathbb{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
defined by
g(t,z):=\frac{1+e^{-it}z}{1-e^{-it}z}
, which for each fixed
t
is holomorphic on
\mathbb{D}
, and define

G(z):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}g(t,z)f(t)\ dt.

Since
f
is real-valued, we have that
\Re(G)(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\Re(g(t,z))f(t)\ dt
, and because

g(t,re^{i\sigma})=\frac{1+re^{-i(\sigma-t)}}{1-re^{-i(\sigma-t)}}\frac{1-re^{-i(t-\sigma)}}{1-re^{-i(t-\sigma)}}=

=\frac{1-re^{-i(t-\sigma)}+re^{-i(\sigma-t)}-r^{2}}{1-re^{-i(t-\sigma)}-re^{-i(\sigma-t)}-r^{2}}=\frac{(1-r^{2})+i2\sin(\sigma-t)}{1-2r\cos(\sigma-t)+r^{2}},

we have that
\Re(G)(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}P_{z}(t)f(t)\ dt
, so it is enough to prove that
G(z)
is holomorphic on
\mathbb{D}
to show that the Poisson integral of
f
is harmonic on
\mathbb{D}
.

Now, considering a closed triangle
\Delta\subset\mathbb{D}
, the function
g(t,z)
is bounded on
\partial\mathbb{D}\times\partial\Delta
and
f(t)
is integrable in
\partial\mathbb{D}
, so the function
g(t,z)f(t)\in L^{1}(\partial\mathbb{D}\times\partial\Delta)
and thus by Fubini’s theorem

\int_{\partial\Delta}G(z)\ dz=\int_{\partial\Delta}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}g(t,z)f(t)\ dt\right)\ dz=

=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(t)\left(\int_{\partial\Delta}g(t,z)\ dz\right)\ dt,

but since for a fixed
t

g(t,z):\mathbb{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is holomorphic, we have that
\int_{\partial\Delta}g(t,z)\ dz=0
, and therefore
\int_{\partial\Delta}G(z)\ dz=0
for every triangle
\Delta\subset\mathbb{D}
, and an application of Morera’s theorem is enough to claim that
G(z)
is holomorphic on
\mathbb{D}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2
.

From the comments at the beginning of this section it is clear that
f\mapsto f^{*}
is an isometric algebra homomorphism from
h^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
into
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})
, and by the previous proposition we also have that it is onto.
∎

Remark
.

Observe that all this results could be adapted to functions on
\mathbb{D}^{n}
with the corresponding Poisson kernels on the polydisks.
