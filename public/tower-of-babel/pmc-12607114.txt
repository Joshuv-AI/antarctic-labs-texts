NnDREB2 regulated by NnWOX1-1 is involved in adventitious root formation in lotus root (Nelumbo nucifera Gaertn.)
Authors: Libao Cheng, Zhen Xu, Quan Liu, Lin Wang, Jiexia Liu, Shuyan Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLotus root, a crucial aquatic cash crop, demonstrates primary root atrophy, while developing adventitious roots critically mediate nutrient acquisition and product organ development. Previous investigation reveals the functional involvement of NnWOX1-1 in root development through transgenic Arabidopsis models.ResultsIn this study, we demonstrated nuclear and membrane co-localization of NnWOX1-1 fluorescence signals in tobacco leaf epidermal cells. NnWOX1-1 overexpression significantly enhanced root elongation and increased root primordia formation in transgenic lotus root and Sagittaria trifolia plants. We identified NnDREB2C as a downstream target gene of NnWOX1-1. The 615 bp nucleotide sequence of NnDREB2C encoded a 205-amino acid polypeptide containing a conserved AP2 domain. Subcellular localization assays revealed exclusive nuclear accumulation of NnDREB2C-GFP fusion proteins in tobacco mesophyll cells. NnDREB2C transcription was significantly upregulated under 200 mg/L ethephon treatment and 20 g/L sucrose exposure, while remaining unresponsive to 10 µM indole-3-acetic acid (IAA). Tissue-specific expression analysis showed maximum transcript abundance in leaves, floral organs, and internodal segments, with minimal expression in fruits and petioles. Transgenic manipulation experiments established that NnDREB2C overexpression enhanced the number and length of adventitious root in both lotus and S. trifolia plants. Conversely, RNAi-mediated knockdown lines exhibited significant reductions in the adventitious root development compared to wild-type counterparts.ConclusionsThrough functional analysis of NnWOX1-1, NnDREB2C and the measurement of ethylene content in transgenic plants, we demonstrated that NnDREB2C regulated by NnWOX1-1 was involved in the formation of adventitious roots in lotus seedling. Additionally, NnWOX1-1 and NnDREB2C affected synthesis of ethylene which was also an important regulator of adventitious roots.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07525-3.Keywords: Lotus roots, NnWOX1-1, NnDREB2C, Adventitious roots, Ethylene, Expression

Introduction

Lotus root is an important aquatic vegetable and economic crop in China. Its underground stem is rich in starch, dietary fiber, and various vitamins, and so is widely used in food processing and medicinal applications [1]. Lotus root cultivation is primarily concentrated in Asian regions, particularly in China, India, Japan, and South Korea. As the world’s largest producer of lotus roots, China leads in both cultivation area and yield. The main domestic production areas are distributed along the middle and lower reaches of the Yangtze River basin, including Hubei, Hunan, Jiangxi, Anhui, and Jiangsu provinces. In recent years, the cultivation area of lotus roots has been annually expanded. The production model has gradually transitioned from traditional extensive paddy field cultivation relying on natural conditions to facility-based cultivation and integrated three-dimensional planting and aquaculture models, thus making the regulation of plant growth and development is particularly important. The primary root of lotus root undergoes degeneration, so adventitious roots play crucial roles during plant growth. These roots not only absorb water and nutrients, anchor the plant, and provide structural support, but directly influence the yield and quality of lotus roots. Therefore, in-depth research on the molecular mechanisms regulating adventitious root development in lotus seedlings is essential for advancing the lotus root industry and driving innovation in lotus.

Based on the timing of root primordium formation, adventitious roots can be classified into latent root primordia and induced root primordia [2]. Adventitious roots derived from latent root primordia refer to those formed through external stimulation activating cell division based on pre-existing root primordia within plant tissues. These root primordia exist in non-root tissues of plants and begin developing into adventitious roots under specific conditions. Conversely, adventitious roots originating from induced root primordia are directly initiated by external stimuli, where plant cells start dividing without pre-existing latent primordia to form new root primordia that subsequently develop into adventitious roots [3]. As a dicotyledonous plant, lotus root [4] has been shown to possess latent root primordia, which has been testified in previous study [5]. The formation of adventitious roots in plants results from the coordinated regulation of multiple internal and external factors, primarily involving environmental regulation, hormonal regulation, and genetic expression control [3, 6–8]. Environmental factors such as temperature, light, water availability, gas composition, and soil conditions show significant regulatory roles at various stages of adventitious root development [9–11]. The formation of adventitious roots is coordinately regulated by multiple phytohormones, including auxin (IAA), ethylene (ETH), cytokinin (CK), methyl jasmonate (MeJA), gibberellin (GA), abscisic acid (ABA), and salicylic acid (SA) [8, 12–17]. These hormones regulate metabolic processes such as cell division, expansion, and differentiation through signal transduction pathways, and ultimately influence adventitious root formation. Genetic regulation plays a decisive role in adventitious root initiation through complex network controls. Recent advancements in molecular biology and transcriptomic technologies have enabled further investigation into the functions of WOX transcription factor family members during adventitious root formation in plant.

The WOX (WUSCHEL-related homeobox) transcription factor family, a plant-specific group of transcription factors, plays critical roles in diverse plant biological processes, including embryonic development, organ differentiation, root development, regeneration, tissue repair, and environmental adaptation [18, 19]. These proteins are central to regulate cell differentiation and determining cell fate. Members of the WOX family are categorized into three clades: the WUS clade, the intermediate clade, and the ancient clade. The members of WUS clade including WOX1-7 regulate organ meristem and embryogenesis of plant. WOX8, 9, 11and 12 belong to intermediate clade, and their expression are related with embryogenesis. In addition, they also participate development of adventitious root. The ancient clade (WOX10, 13, and WOX14), which originates from green plants, remains poorly characterized function [20]. In Arabidopsis, petunia, alfalfa, and tobacco, WOX1 has been identified as a key regulator of lateral organ development [21]. Overexpression of Arabidopsis WOX1 leads to lateral leaf expansion, and subsequent studies demonstrate that WOX1 and WOX3 cooperatively regulate this process [22]. Additionally, EupWOX1 has been shown to mediate root primordium induction, and modulates root elongation and lateral root growth [23].

It has been reported that WOX can interact with AP2/ERF transcription factors to regulate growth and development of plant. Rice OsWOX3B interacts with HL6, a member of the AP2/ERF family, to modulate leaf trichome formation through the regulation of auxin-responsive gene expression [24]. The AP2/ERF transcription factor family, which includes the DREB subfamily, is ubiquitously distributed across plant tissues and organs. DREB transcription factors regulate root growth through multifaceted mechanisms. These genes enhance drought and salt stress tolerance by modulating dehydration-responsive elements (DREs). At the same time, some members of DREB family also influence root elongation, emergence angle, and root hair differentiation. It is shown that DREBs regulate lateral root formation by altering auxin distribution via transcriptional control of PIN family members in the auxin signaling pathway. DREB transcription factors can collaborate with ethylene response factors (ERFs) to coordinate root hair development and gravitropic responses [25]. They also suppress cytokinin (CK) sensitivity to modulate root tillering and branching [26].

Preliminary studies in our laboratory have shown that NnWOX1-1 can significantly increase the number and length of roots in transgenic Arabidopsis plants. Based on these findings, this study further investigated the function of NnWOX1-1 by constructing overexpression and RNAi interference vectors, and transforming into lotus root and S. trifolia plants. Additionally, yeast library screening was used to identify downstream target genes (DREB2B) of the NnWOX1-1 transcription factor, followed by cloning, expression, and functional validation of this gene.

Materials and methods

Subcellular localization of NnWOX1-1 and NnDREB2C transcription factors

The pCAMBIA1300 vector containing green fluorescent protein (GFP) was used as the overexpression vector, and GFP fusion expression was employed to identify “positive” transgenic plants. The full-length CDS of NnWOX1-1 and NnDREB2C were cloned, and then inserted into the pCAMBIA1300 vector. The positive plasmid was then transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101 (Weidi Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). After verification by PCR method, bacterial cultures containing the target genes were obtained. Single colony was selected and cultured overnight to prepare the tobacco leaf infection solution. The pCAMBIA1300 vector carrying an empty plasmid was used as the control. The bacterial suspension containing the pCAMBIA1300 vector with the target gene was used to infect Nicotiana benthamiana leaves aging 35–45 days. Before infection, the tobacco plants were exposed to strong light for 10 min to promote stomatal opening. A 1 mL syringe was used to draw the infection solution, and the abaxial side of the tobacco leaves (avoiding the veins) was infiltrated. After 2–3 days of dark cultivation, the infiltrated epidermal tissue was sampled and examined under a super-resolution laser confocal microscope (LSM 880NLO, Carl Zeiss, Germany) to observe GFP fluorescence signals.

Transient transformation of NnWOX1-1 and NnDREB2C in lotus root and S. trifolia

Preparation of materials of lotus root and S. trifolia

Lotus seeds and S. trifolia corms of similar size were selected, and the plumules of lotus and S. trifolia buds were used as tissue culture materials. After rinsing the surface mucus with clean water, the materials were disinfected with 75% alcohol, and then soaked in sterilized water with Shannong No. 1 Type I for 12 h. Subsequently, these materials were inoculated onto MS medium (containing 30 g/L sucrose, 7 g/L agar, 0.15% Shannong No. 1 Type III, 1 g/L activated charcoal, pH 5.8) and cultured aseptically for one month. Tissue culture seedlings of lotus and S. trifolia with similar growth vigor were selected for infection experiment.

The steps of instantaneous transformation

The recombinant plasmids of the NnWOX1-1 and NnDREB2C overexpression vector and RNAi vector were transformed into Agrobacterium rhizogenes K599 competent cells using the freeze-thaw method. Subsequently, the plasmids were introduced into lotus root (Nelumbo nucifera) and S. trifolia following the protocol. The Agrobacterium strains carrying the pCAMBIA1300 empty vector, overexpression vectors and RNAi vectors were activated on YEB solid medium (containing 50 mg·L⁻¹ kanamycin and 50 mg·L⁻¹ rifampicin). Single colony was selected, and subcultured on fresh YEB solid medium for expansion. Colonies were collected into a 50 mL centrifuge tube, resuspended in 20 mL YEB liquid medium, and incubated overnight at 28 °C with shaking at 200 rpm. The overnight culture was centrifuged (5 min, 6000 rpm, 4 °C), and the supernatant was discarded. The pellet was resuspended in 2 mL MES buffer (20 mM MgCl₂ + 10 mM MES), centrifuged again under the same conditions, and the supernatant was discarded. The pellet was resuspended in 1 mL MS medium, and then transferred to a sterile 50 mL tube, adjusted to 20 mL with MS medium, and supplemented with 200 µL acetosyringone (AS, 20 mg/mL). The suspension was incubated overnight at 28 °C with shaking (200 rpm) until the OD₆₀₀ reached 0.7–0.9. Sterilized lotus root and S. trifolia seedlings were wounded at internodal regions and immersed in the Agrobacterium suspension for overnight infection. Infected plants were transferred to MS liquid medium (MS + 3% sucrose + 20 mg/L AS, pH 5.8), and cultured in a growth chamber under the following conditions: 28 °C/12 h (day) and 22 °C/12 h (night), with a light intensity of 30,000 lx. After 5 days, infected plants were rinsed three times with sterile water, and the medium was replaced. Three days later, plants were washed 3–5 times with cefotaxime solution (400 mg/L) to eliminate residual Agrobacterium. Plants were transferred to fresh MS liquid medium (MS + 3% sucrose + 300 mg/L carbenicillin, pH 5.8), and maintained in the growth chamber under the same conditions.

Identification downstream gene regulated by NnWOX1-1

Yeast one-hybrid

NnWOX1-1-pGADT7 was employed as bait to screen a yeast one-hybrid motif library. Following identification of “positive” clones through auxotrophic selection, DNA sequencing and multiple sequence alignment (Clustal Omega) revealed motifs interacting with NnWOX1-1. Three plasmid combinations included pGADT7-NnWOX1-1, pGADT7-NnWOX1-1 + pHIS2-p53, pGAD53m + pHIS2-p53 were independently transformed into Y187 competent cells and plated on SD/-Trp/-Leu selection medium. After 3–5 days incubation at 30 °C, six colonies were subjected to colony PCR verification. Three randomly selected clones were pooled, normalized to OD600 = 0.002, and serially diluted (0, 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 75, and 100 mM) for spot assay on 3-amino-1,2,4-triazole (3AT)-supplemented plates. For library screening, Y187 cells pre-transformed with NnWOX1-1-pGADT7 were co-transformed with motif library plasmids, and plated on SD/-Trp/-Leu/-His medium containing 100 mM 3AT. “positive” clones from 100 mM 3AT SD-TLH plates were submitted to Nanjing Ruiyuan Biotechnology Co., Ltd. for sequencing. Consensus motif sequences were subsequently mapped to the Nelumbo nucifera genome using BLASTn tool to retrieve full-length candidate gene (NnDREB2C).

EMSA binding assay validation

The electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) for protein-DNA interaction was conducted by Nanjing Ruiyuan Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The NnWOX1-1 was expressed and secreted using the Pichia pastoris secretory expression system, followed by purification and quantification via the BCA method. A biotin-labeled DNA probe was synthesized based on a specific sequence fragment of NnDREB2C (CAATTCTGTTCTCAAATTATCTCTCCTCTCCCAACAGCGTCACCTTATCCACGTGGGAAACGTCAACCAAACGAC). The interaction between NnWOX1-1 and NnDREB2C was further validated via a series of experimental steps, including 4% native polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE), electrophoretic transfer to a nylon membrane, membrane washing, and chemiluminescent detection of probe-protein binding signals.

Determination of ethylene and auxin content

Measurement of ethylene content in transgenic plants

NnWOX1-1 and NnDREB2 overexpression and RNAi plants were thoroughly rinsed with distilled water, and the plant materials were weighed. The samples were promptly placed into 50 mL glass vials, which were immediately tightly sealed and stored in a foam box to avoid light for 7 h. Prior to sample analysis, 500 µL of a 0.5 × 10−6 mol/L ethylene standard was drawn using a 1 mL gas-tight microsyringe, and injected into a gas chromatograph (GC-7890 A, Agilent, USA) for measurement. The samples were analyzed only after the peak height of the standard ethylene stabilized. The GC conditions were as follows: 45 °C, 120 °C and 200 °C of column temperature, injector temperature, and FID detector temperature respectively; column head pressure flow rate was 40 mL/min with 2.8 mL/min column flow rate, and makeup gas flow rate was 2.5 mL/min; split ratio was 10:1, and injection volume was 500 µL. Ethylene release rates was calculated according to following formula: Ethylene concentration (µL/L) = (sample peak height/standard ethylene peak height) × standard concentration. Ethylene release rate [nL/(g·h)] = [ethylene concentration (nL/L) × container volume (mL)]/[fresh weight of sample (g) × sealing time (h)].

Measurement of IAA content in transgenic plants

The overexpression and RNAi lines of NnWOX1-1 and NnDREB2B were washed in clean water before endogenous hormone determination. Empty vector-infected lines were used as controls. 0.5 g of freeze-dried sample was ground in liquid nitrogen, and powder was transferred to 2.5 ml centrifuge tubes. A total of 500 µl extraction buffer (isopropanol: water: formic acid = 2:1:0.002) was added, followed by incubation at −20 °C for 20 min. These tubes were treated with ice-water bath sonication for 30 min. After adding 1 ml chloroform, the samples were incubated at −20 °C for 20 min, and then sonicated in ice-water bath for 5 min. The tubes were vortexed for 1 min, and centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. The lower layer (900 µL, collected in two aliquots of 450 µL each into 1.5 ml centrifuge tubes) was freeze-dried. The residue was redissolved in 200 µl 80% methanol, followed by ice-water bath sonication for 1 min and vortexing for 1 min. The solution was filter-sterilized and 100 µL was transferred to insert tubes. IAA content was determined using liquid chromatography (Sigma, Shanghai, China) reported by Li et al., (2022).

Ethylene treatment

The coat of lotus root seeds was cracked and soaked in distilled water for germination. After germination, a sterile blade was used to make incisions at the hypocotyl of the seedlings. Interference vectors of NnWOLX1-1 and NnDREB2C were introduced into the lotus plants via Rhizobium rhizogenes-mediated transformation, which was followed with previously described method. Transgenic plants were treated with 300 mg/L ethephon for 48 h, and then transfered to distilled water for cultivation. The number and length of adventitious roots were measured 10 days later.

Determination of root number and length of transgenic plants

The overexpression and interference vectors were introduced into seedlings. Tissue sections were prepared, mounted on glass slides, and examined for eGFP green fluorescence using a confocal laser scanning microscope. Additionally, the relative gene expression level in “positive” seedlings was quantified by real-time quantitative PCR (qRT-PCR), and the data was analyzed and visualized using GraphPad Prism. For both the transgenic and control groups, about 6 eGFP-“positive” seedlings with comparable growth vigor were selected. The number and length of adventitious roots exceeding 1 mm were measured, and the mean values were calculated and recorded.

Analysis of fresh weight and dry weight in transgenic plants

After measuring the fresh weight of transgenic and control plants using an analytical balance, the samples had been oven-dried at 65 °C until the weight were not changed. Their dry weight was then measured to calculate the dry matter content, with averages determined.

Data analysis

The experimental data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA in GraphPad Prism 9, and bar graphs were generated. The results represent means ± SE from three independent biological experiments, and each with more than ten plants. Statistical significance was indicated as follows: “* ”represents p < 0.05; “** ”represents p < 0.01; “*** ” represents p < 0.001; “****” represents p < 0.0001.

Results

Subcellular localization of NnWOX1-1

To precisely investigate the subcellular localization of NnWOX1-1 in plant cells, tobacco organelles were analyzed through transient transformation assays. Leaves from eight-leaf-stage tobacco plants were transfected with the 35 S::NnWOX1-1-GFP vector, with empty GFP vector-transfected leaves as the control. Under confocal microscopy, strong GFP fluorescence signals were observed in the merged channel using a 488 nm excitation wavelength. Distinct cellular contours were also visible in the bright-field microscopy images (Fig. 1a). Co-localization analysis revealed that NnWOX1-1 was predominantly localized at the nucleus and plasma membrane, as evidenced by the eGFP fluorescence signals. These findings suggest that NnWOX1-1 may regulate plant growth and development as a nuclear-and membrane-associated protein.

Selection of “positive” transgenic plants

The NnWOX1-1 overexpression vector was introduced into lotus root and S. trifolia seedlings by Agrobacterium-mediated transformation, and the RNAi interference vector was transformed into lotus root seedlings. Initial screening of transgenic S. trifolia plants was conducted using fluorescence detection to identify “positive” seedlings. (Additional Fig. 1). Our results demonstrated detectable eGFP green fluorescence in all transgenic “positive” seedlings. In NnWOX1-1 overexpressing lotus plants, the relative expression levels of NnWOX1-1 were significantly higher than those in control plants (Additional Fig. 2a), whereas in RNAi-interfered plants, the expression levels were markedly lower compared to controls (Additional Fig. 2b). These findings confirm the successful genetic transformation of NnWOX1-1 in both lotus and S. trifolia plants.

The role of NnWOX1-1 on AR formation in lotus and S. trifolia

The function of NnWOX1-1 on AR formation was evaluated in transgenic lotus and S. trifolia plant. We found that transgenic lotus and S. trifolia plants exhibited earlier adventitious root formation, and the root length and number significantly were higher than those of the control plants (Fig. 1b, c, d, e, f, g, h, i, j). In NnWOX1-1-overexpressing plants, fresh weight, dry weight, ethylene, and auxin levels were significantly increased. By contrast, NnWOX1-1-knockdown plants showed significant reductions in fresh weight, dry weight, and ethylene. Whereas auxin content remained unchanged (Fig. 2a, b, c, d). The above results indicated that NnWOX1-1 could promote formation in lotus root and S. trifolia.

To assess the impact of NnWOX1-1 on phytohormone metabolism, we quantified ethylene emission and auxin levels in transgenic plants. NnWOX1-1-overexpressing plants exhibited higher ethylene production compared to empty vector controls, whereas RNAi-mediated silencing of NnWOX1-1 resulted in reduced ethylene release (Fig. 2e, f). This regulation strongly suggests NnWOX1-1functions as a positive regulator of ethylene biosynthesis. Regarding auxin metabolism, the overexpressors showed elevated IAA content relative to controls, no statistically significant difference was detected between RNAi lines and control plants (Fig .2g, h). This differential response implies complex regulatory mechanisms for auxin homeostasis in plant.

Identification of downstream target gene of NnWOX1-1

Preliminary screening of downstream target genes

Using the Y187 yeast strain harboring the pGADT7-NnWOX1-1 bait plasmid as the recipient, competent yeast cells were prepared. The motif library plasmids were subsequently transformed into these competent cells and plated onto SD-TLH screening plates supplemented with 100 mM 3-amino-1,2,4-triazole (3AT). “positive” yeast clones were selected from the SD-TLH + 100 mM 3AT plates. Forty-eight putative “positive” clones underwent PCR verification, revealing 29 distinct motifs capable of sustained growth on SD-TL, SD-TLH, and SD-TLH + 100 mM 3AT media. These 29 motifs were preliminarily identified as potential interaction partners of NnWOX1-1 (Fig. 3a). Functional annotation of the 29 motif sequences was performed using PlantCARE. Following analysis, six functionally characterized motifs were selected for genome-wide scanning in lotus root (Nelumbo nucifera. L). This screening yielded corresponding promoter sequences containing these conserved motifs. Notably, one identified motif was localized within the promoter region of NnDREB2C, a dehydration-responsive element binding transcription factor.

Further validation of the interaction between NnWOX1-1 and NnDREB2C

The “positive” control (pGAD53m + pHIS2-p53) showed normal growth on SD-TL, SD-TLH, and SD-TLH + 100 mM 3AT plates, whereas the negative control (pHIS2-NnDREB2C + pGADT7) failed to grow on the SD-TLH + 100 mM 3AT plate. In contrast, the experimental group (pHIS2-NnDREB2C + pGADT7-NnWOX1-1) exhibited robust growth on all tested plates (SD-TL, SD-TLH, and SD-TLH + 100 mM 3AT) (Fig. 3b), suggesting a protein-protein interaction between NnDREB2C and NnWOX1-1–52. To further validate this interaction, we performed an experiment of Electrophoretic Mobility Shift Assay (EMSA). The results confirmed that the NnDREB2C probe specifically binds to NnWOX1-1 (Fig. 3c), which indicated that NnDREB2C was a downstream target gene of the transcription factor NnWOX1-1. Furthermore, in lotus plants overexpressing NnWOX1-1, NnDREB2C expression was upregulated, and while decreased expression of NnWOX1-1 led to significant lower transcriptional level of NnDREB2C (Fig. 3d), suggesting that NnWOX1-1 positively regulates NnDREB2C transcriptional level.

Cloning and sequence analysis of NnDREB2C

Using cDNA from seedlings of the lotus root cultivar “Tai Konglian 36” as a template, we successfully obtained a clone containing the entire open reading frame (ORF) of NnDREB2C. The gene spanned 615 bp and encoded a protein of 205 amino acid residues (Fig. 4a). Bioinformatics analysis revealed that NnDREB2C contains a conserved AP2 domain. Notably, the AP2 domain of NnDREB2C exhibited a higher number of nucleotides compared to homologous domains of DREB2 from other species (Fig. 4b). Phylogenetic analysis using MEGAX software demonstrated that NnDREB2C formed a distinct clade in the evolutionary tree, with no close homologs (Fig. 4c), suggesting a distant genetic relationship with other species.

Expression analysis of NnDREB2C

The lotus seedlings were treated with 200 mg/L ethephon, 10 µM auxin, or 20 g/L sucrose. After three days of treatment, hypocotyl tissues were collected at 0 h, 18 h, and 36 h for expression analysis. Our results showed that ethephon treatment induced a bell-shaped expression pattern of NnDREB2C, peaking at 18 h, with significantly higher expression levels in treated plants than in controls (Fig. 5a). In contrast, auxin treatment did not produce a significant temporal expression pattern or notable differences between treated and control plants (Fig. 5b). Sucrose treatment similarly triggered a biphasic response in NnDREB2C expression, reaching its maximum at 18 h (Fig. 5c). These findings suggest that NnDREB2C expression is inducible by ethephon and sucrose, but not by auxin. Furthermore, tissue-specific expression analysis revealed organ-dependent regulation of NnDREB2C in lotus. The highest expression levels were detected in leaves, followed by petioles, flowers, and internodes, while seeds exhibited minimal expression (Fig. 5d).

Functional Validation of the NnDREB2C

Identification of “positive” transformants in transgenic plants

Overexpression and RNAi of NnDREB2C were performed in lotus and S. trifolia plants. “Positive” transgenic plants were identified through confocal fluorescence microscopy, which successfully detected eGFP fluorescence in S. trifolia (Additional Fig. 3a) and lotus (Additional Fig. 3b). qRT-PCR analysis revealed significantly elevated NnDREB2C expression levels in these lotus transgenic plants compared to control plants (Additional Fig. 4). In contrast, lotus RNAi lines showed detectable GFP fluorescence (Additional Fig. 5a), but exhibited substantially reduced NnDREB2C expression relative to control plants (Additional Fig. 5b).

Effect of NnDREB2C on adventitious root formation

To investigate the role of NnDREB2C in adventitious root formation, overexpression and RNAi vectors were constructed, and introduced into lotus and S. trifolia.

seedlings. Our results demonstrated that NnDREB2C-overexpressing plants exhibited significantly increased adventitious root number and root length compared to control plants (Fig. 6b, c, d, e, f, g). Conversely, RNAi-silenced plants showed a significant reduction in adventitious root length, root number, and biomass (fresh and dry weights) relative to controls (Fig. 6h, i. j).

Effect of NnDREB2C on plant growth and hormone synthesis in transgenic plants

Transgenic plants displayed markedly higher fresh and dry weights, suggesting that NnDREB2C not only enhanced adventitious root development, but promoted overall plant growth (Fig. 7a, b, c, d). Ethylene levels were analyzed in both NnDREB2C-overexpressing and NnDREB2C-silenced transgenic plants. Compared to the control, ethylene content was significantly elevated in NnDREB2C-overexpressing plants, and while markedly reduced in silenced lines (Fig. 7e, f). Auxin quantification revealed similarly enhanced levels in NnDREB2C-overexpressing plants relative to controls. However, no significant difference in auxin content was observed between NnDREB2C-silenced plants and the control group (Fig. 7g, h).

Disscussion

Adventitious roots do not develop at specific developmental stages or predefined sites within plants, while generally arise from vascular tissues in the hypocotyl, stems, or leaves. This biological process includes the induction stage, differentiation stage, and subsequent elongation of root primordia. These stages are governed by a complex interplay of internal and external factors, primarily environmental cues, phytohormonal signaling, and genetic regulation. Environmental influences include temperature fluctuations [28], light intensity/spectrum [29], and water availability [30]. Key phytohormones mediating this process are endogenous ethylene, auxin, and cytokinins [6, 8, 31].

Recent advances in molecular studies have elucidated the roles of specific gene families in root initiation. Notably, transcription factors such as WOX (WUSCHEL-related homeobox) [32], DREB (dehydration-responsive element binding) [25], and ARF (auxin response factor) [33] have been identified as central regulators of adventitious root development. Previous studies have established that adventitious root formation in lotus is modulated by multiple factors, particularly through the coordinated action of phytohormones and genetic regulation. Our current findings further elucidate that this developmental process involves a sophisticated regulatory network encompassing both gene-gene interactions and gene-hormone crosstalk. Our results showed that transgenic lines overexpressing NnWOX1-1 developed significantly longer and more numerous adventitious roots than wild-type plants (lotus and S. trifolia) (Fig. 1b, c, d, e, g, h), whereas RNAi knockdown lines (lotus) exhibited inhibited adventitious root development, produced fewer and shorter roots (Fig. 1f, i, g). These findings demonstrate that NnWOX1-1 plays an essential role in adventitious root formation in lotus, which aligns with previous reports in eucalyptus [32], poplar [34–36], and apple [37].

WOXs are a class of plant-specific transcription factors that play critical roles in lateral organ development, adventitious root formation, and stem cell maintenance. Research indicates that WOXs are crucial at various stages of adventitious root formation. For example, WOX11 is expressed in founder cells of adventitious root, and promotes the development of root primordium by regulating the expression of WOX5 and WOX7 [38]. In eucalyptus, EupWOX1 and EupWOX5 not only play key roles in induction of root primordium, but participate in root elongation [23].

These findings demonstrate that the WOX transcription factor family is essential for adventitious root formation in plants. Members of the WOXs family exhibit substantial structural divergence in their gene architecture, while maintaining functional conservation in regulating organogenesis [39]. Our findings corroborate this observation, as NnWOX1-1, NnWOX4-3, and NnWOX5-1 display significant structural variations, yet consistently enhance root formation when overexpressing in transgenic Arabidopsis plants [27]. WOXs can interact with plant hormones to modulate critical biological processes including root cell self-renewal and dedifferentiation [40]. We observed elevated ethylene levels in transgenic plants compared to controls (Fig. 2e), suggesting that NnWOX1-1 may regulate ethylene biosynthesis during adventitious root formation in lotus seedlings.

Research has demonstrated that some members of WOX family interact with genes from other families to regulate adventitious root formation [37] and control cell division during root development [41, 42]. It is shown that members of the WOX family can interact with AP2/ERF family members to cooperatively regulate plant growth and development. AP2/ERF transcription factors are widely distributed across diverse plant species and participate signal transduction pathways to govern various physiological and biochemical processes. Genes of AP2/ERF family play crucial roles in root system development [43]. In rice, overexpression of the AP2/ERF transcription factor ERF3 markedly promotes root cap development, whereas its suppression lead to significantly reduced root cap number and length. Further studies reveal that ERF3 interacts with WOX11 to regulate root cap formation by modulating the expression of the cytokinin response regulator RR2 [44]. In this study, we identified NnDREB2C, a downstream target gene of NnWOX1-1 using yeast one-hybrid (Y1H) and electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSA) (Fig. 3b, c). NnDREB2C belonged to the AP2/ERF transcription factor family and functioned in the ethylene signaling pathway during adventitous root formation in lotus (Figs. 4b and c and 5a).

The AP2/ERF transcription factor family is widely presented in plants, with each member harboring one or more conserved AP2 domains of approximately 60 amino acids in length. This family is categorized into five major subfamilies: ERF (ethylene-responsive element-binding factors), AP2 (APETALA2), DREB (dehydration-responsive element-binding proteins), RAV (related to ABI3/VP1), and Soloist (a small group of unclassified members) [24, 45]. The molecular mechanisms underlying DREB-mediated regulation of root growth involve coordinated physiological and molecular processes. First, DREBs modulate dehydration-responsive elements (DREs) to enhance drought and salt tolerance by influencing root elongation, growth angle, and root hair differentiation [25, 26]. Second, DREBs regulate lateral root formation by altering auxin distribution in roots through transcriptional control the members of PIN family in the auxin signaling pathway [25]. Furthermore, DREBs reduce sensitivity to cytokinin (CK), thereby affecting root tillering and branching [26]. Consistent with these findings, our study revealed that overexpression of NnDREB2C in transgenic lotus plants significantly enhanced adventitious root growth, resulting in longer and more numerous roots compared to wild-type plants (Fig. 6b, c, d, e, f, g). Conversely, RNAi mediated knockdown of NnDREB2C suppressed adventitious root formation (Fig. 6h, i, g), indicating its regulatory role in this process. Previous studies have demonstrated that ethylene regulates the expression of soybean DREB1 genes. Inhibition of ethylene signal markedly upregulates GmDREB1A;1 and GmDREB1A;2.

transcripts, whereas the activation of ethylene decreases their expression [46]. Our results align with these observations: ethephon treatment induced NnDREB2C expression, and ethylene levels were significantly higher in NnDREB2C overexpressing plants (Figs. 5a and 7e). These findings suggest that NnDREB2C contributes to ethylene biosynthesis during adventitious root formation in lotus. Furthermore, we observed that ethephon treatment restored adventitious root formation impaired by silencing of NnWOX1-1 and NnDREB2C (Fig. 8), suggesting that ethylene plays a regulatory role in this process in lotus roots.

Conclusion

Base on previous findings, this study investigated the function of NnWOX1-1 in lotus. The results showed that transgenic plants developed adventitious roots with significantly enhanced quantity and length relative to wild-type controls, concomitant with elevated endogenous ethylene levels. Through Yeast One-Hybrid screening and Electrophoretic Mobility Shift Assays, we identified NnDREB2C as a direct transcriptional target of NnWOX1-1. This transcription factor of AP2/ERF family, previously associated with ethylene-mediated growth regulation, demonstrated unique induction to ethylene and sucrose, but remaining unaffected by exogenous auxin.

Functional validation through NnDREB2C overexpression in two agriculturally significant species (lotus and S. trifolia) recapitulated the enhanced adventitious rooting phenotype. Conversely, RNAi-mediated suppression of NnDREB2C in lotus resulted in reduction in root primordia formation. Notably, both NnWOX1-1 and NnDREB2C overexpression lines exhibited similar ethylene level elevations, suggesting feedforward regulation within the ethylene signaling cascade during root organogenesis.
