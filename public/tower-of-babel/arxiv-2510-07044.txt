Robustness of Covariance Estimators with Application in Activity Detection
Hendrik Bernd Zarucha, Peter Jung, Giuseppe Caire

Abstract
The first part of this work considers a general class of covariance estimators. Each estimator of that class is generated by a real-valued function g g and a set of model covariance matrices ℋ \mathcal{H} . If 𝐖 \mathbf{W} is a potentially perturbed observation of a searched covariance matrix, then the estimator is the minimizer of the sum of g g applied to each eigenvalue of 𝐖 1 2 ​ 𝐙 − 1 ​ 𝐖 1 2 \mathbf{W}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{Z}^{-1}\mathbf{W}^{\frac{1}{2}} under the constraint that 𝐙 \mathbf{Z} is from ℋ \mathcal{H} . It is shown that under mild conditions on g g and ℋ \mathcal{H} such estimators are robust, meaning the estimation error can be made arbitrarily small if the perturbation of 𝐖 \mathbf{W} gets small enough. In the second part of this work the previous results are applied to activity detection in random access with multiple receive antennas. In activity detection recovering the large scale fading coefficients is a sparse recovery problem which can be reduced to a structured covariance estimation problem. The recovery can be done with a non-negative least squares estimator or with a relaxed maximum likelihood estimator. It is shown that under suitable assumptions on the distributions of the noise and the channel coefficients, the relaxed maximum likelihood estimator is from the general class of covariance estimators considered in the first part of this work. Then, codebooks based upon a signed kernel condition are proposed. It is shown that with the proposed codebooks both estimators can recover the large-scale fading coefficients if the number of receive antennas is high enough and S ≤ ⌈ 1 2 ​ M 2 ⌉ − 1 S\leq\left\lceil\frac{1}{2}M^{2}\right\rceil-1 where S S is the number of active users and M M is number of pilot symbols per user.

Introduction

This work considers a compressed sensing problem with

K
measurement processes of the form

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}_{k}=\mathbf{A}\sqrt{\textnormal{diag}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)}\mathbf{h}_{k}+\mathbf{e}_{k}\in\mathbb{C}^{M}

(1)

for
k=1,\dots,K
. Here
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is assumed to be
S
-sparse
and non-negative,

\mathbf{h}_{k}
are mutually independent, complex normal distributed random vectors with expectation
0
and identity
\mathbf{I}
as
covariance matrix,
\mathbf{e}_{k}
are mutually independent, complex normal distributed random vectors
with expectation
0
and covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}

and

\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times N}
has columns
\mathbf{a}_{n}
.

Activity detection in random access with multiple receive antennas
can be modeled as such a problem.
In this case
\mathbf{a}_{n}
are the pilot symbols assigned to the
n
-th user,

\mathbf{A}
is the codebook,
and the channel coefficient
of the
n
-th user transmitting to the
k
-th receive antenna
is given by
(\sqrt{\textnormal{diag}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)}\mathbf{h}_{k})_{n}
.
The variances of the channel coefficients are called large-scale fading coefficients
and are the entries of
\mathbf{x}
so that the entries of
\mathbf{x}
are non-negative.
Further,
N
is the total number of devices,
S
is the number of active devices,

K
is the number of receive antennas,
and the
k
-th receive antenna observes the signal
\mathbf{y}_{k}
.
Since devices only transmit sporadically and the large scale fading coefficient
vanishes if a device is inactive,
\mathbf{x}
is indeed a sparse vector.

Activity detection can be modeled in other ways, such as
[
1
,
2
]
, and the
validity of this model has been discussed frequently.
A further discussion about the validity of the model
is thus omitted. It suffices to say that it is justified to study it,
since this work focuses on mathematical properties instead of performance,
and since this model has been studied several times,
for instance in
[
3
,
4
,

,
6
,
7
,
8
]
.
Note that the measurement process (
1
) also appears in works about
unsourced random access, such as
[
9
,
10
]
. Unsourced random access differs in two aspects
from activity detection. In unsourced random access the columns
\mathbf{a}_{n}
represent the
codewords of a common codebook used by all users, and

will then be the
\ell_{2}
-norm
of the vector of all large-scale fading coefficients of users transmitting the
n

If the columns of
\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times K}
are
\mathbf{y}_{k}
,
the columns of
\mathbf{H}
are
\mathbf{h}_{k}
, the columns of
\mathbf{E}
are
\mathbf{e}_{k}
,
and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{Y}^{H}=

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}\textnormal{diag}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\mathbf{A}^{H}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}+\mathbf{A}\sqrt{\textnormal{diag}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)}\left(\frac{1}{K}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{H}^{H}-\mathbf{I}\right)\sqrt{\textnormal{diag}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)}\mathbf{A}^{H}+\frac{1}{K}\mathbf{E}\mathbf{E}^{H}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}

\displaystyle+\mathbf{A}\sqrt{\textnormal{diag}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)}\frac{1}{K}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{E}^{H}+\frac{1}{K}\mathbf{E}\mathbf{H}^{H}\sqrt{\textnormal{diag}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)}\mathbf{A}^{H}.

(2)

Due to (
2
) the sample covariance matrix

\frac{1}{K}\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{Y}^{H}
is a perturbed observation of
the covariance matrix

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{K}\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{Y}^{H}\right]=

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}\textnormal{diag}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\mathbf{A}^{H}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf{a}_{n}\mathbf{a}_{n}^{H}x_{n}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}

with the mean-zero perturbation

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}\sqrt{\textnormal{diag}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)}\left(\frac{1}{K}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{H}^{H}-\mathbf{I}\right)\sqrt{\textnormal{diag}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)}\mathbf{A}^{H}+\frac{1}{K}\mathbf{E}\mathbf{E}^{H}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}+\mathbf{A}\sqrt{\textnormal{diag}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)}\frac{1}{K}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{E}^{H}+\frac{1}{K}\mathbf{E}\mathbf{H}^{H}\sqrt{\textnormal{diag}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)}\mathbf{A}^{H}.

Thus, (
2
) describes a covariance estimation
problem where the searched covariance matrix
\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf{a}_{n}\mathbf{a}_{n}^{H}x_{n}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}

is from the structured model

\left\{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf{a}_{n}\mathbf{a}_{n}^{H}z_{n}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}:\mathbf{z}\geq 0\right\}
.
Several estimators can be considered to estimate
\mathbf{x}
.
One can consider the non-negative least squares estimator which
is given as any solution of

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{z}\geq 0}\left\|\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf{a}_{n}\mathbf{a}_{n}^{H}z_{n}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}-\frac{1}{K}\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{Y}^{H}\right\|_{2}

(3)

where the norm is the Frobenius norm.
The non-negative least squares can be considered a relaxed version
of more common
\ell_{1}
-regularized estimators in compressed sensing
[
12
]
.
It can recover sparse non-negative signals even without

\ell_{0}
-regularization or
\ell_{1}
-regularization
[
12
]
.
On the other hand, one can consider the maximum likelihood estimator which
can be found by solving

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{z}\geq 0\textnormal{ is $S$-sparse}}\textnormal{trace}\left(\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf{a}_{n}\mathbf{a}_{n}^{H}z_{n}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right)^{-1}\frac{1}{K}\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{Y}^{H}\right)+\ln\left(\textnormal{det}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf{a}_{n}\mathbf{a}_{n}^{H}z_{n}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right)\right),

see for instance
[
10
]
.
Due to the combinatorial nature of the constraints, one
often considers the relaxed maximum likelihood estimator
which is any minimizer of

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{z}\geq 0}\textnormal{trace}\left(\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf{a}_{n}\mathbf{a}_{n}^{H}z_{n}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right)^{-1}\frac{1}{K}\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{Y}^{H}\right)+\ln\left(\textnormal{det}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf{a}_{n}\mathbf{a}_{n}^{H}z_{n}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right)\right).

(4)

Since the relaxation removes the combinatorial constraints,
finding the minimizer is significantly easier, and one can use,
for example, coordinate-wise descent methods to approximate a minimizer.
Due to the
\ell_{0}
-regularizer being obsolete for the non-negative least squares,
one could hope that a similar result also holds
for the relaxed maximum likelihood estimator.
In particular, robust recovery guarantees are sought after.
Very generally speaking,
robustness in this work will refer to a property that bounds the estimation
error of an estimator as a function of the magnitude of the perturbation;
however, the exact statements will be specified in theorems below.

1.2
This Work’s Contribution

In the first part of this work a general class of covariance estimators is considered.
Given some closed set of positive definite matrices

\mathcal{H}
of structured covariance matrices and
g:\left(0,\infty\right)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

the covariance estimators considered in this work are minimizers of

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{Z}\in\mathcal{H}}\sum_{m=1}^{M}g\left(\lambda_{m}\left(\mathbf{W}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{Z}^{-1}\mathbf{W}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)\right),

(6)

where
\lambda_{m}\left(\mathbf{Z}\right)
is the
m
-th largest eigenvalue of
\mathbf{Z}

and
\mathbf{W}
is a Hermitian positive definite perturbed observation of a searched and unknown covariance matrix

\mathbf{X}
. For instance,
\mathbf{W}
could be a sample covariance matrix.
Note that, since
\mathbf{W}
is not necessarily in
\mathcal{H}
,
the minimization problem is non-trivial.
It is shown that if certain conditions on
g
are fulfilled, then this estimator is robust,
meaning that its minimizers are arbitrarily close to the searched and unknown covariance matrix
\mathbf{X}
as long as the perturbed
\mathbf{W}
is close enough to
\mathbf{X}
.

The second part of this work considers deterministic codebook constructions
from
[
15
]
with

\displaystyle M^{2}\asymp S

(7)

such that the linear operator
\mathcal{A}\left(\mathbf{z}\right)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf{a}_{n}\mathbf{a}_{n}^{H}z_{n}
satisfies a signed kernel condition
[
15
]
instead of
a restricted isometry property. It is shown that for such constructions the minimizers of
(
3
) and (
4
)
each converge in probability to the unknown
\mathbf{x}
.
It is discussed that this improvement comes with a trade-off. The number
of receive antennas has to increase significantly if
one improves from (
5
) to (
7
).

Further, it is proven that, if
\mathcal{A}\left(\mathbf{z}\right)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf{a}_{n}\mathbf{a}_{n}^{H}z_{n}
satisfies the signed kernel condition,
the optimization problem (
4
)
is robust, meaning that the estimation error of the minimizer (
4
)
can be controlled by making

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf{a}_{n}\mathbf{a}_{n}^{H}x_{n}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}-\frac{1}{K}\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{Y}

small enough.
This gives a direct relation between the number of receive antennas and the probability to
make the estimation error smaller than a given target.
This result is proven by applying the results of the first part of this work with

\mathbf{W}=\frac{1}{K}\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{Y}^{H}
,
\mathbf{X}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf{a}_{n}\mathbf{a}_{n}^{H}x_{n}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}

At last, it is shown that, if
\mathcal{A}\left(\mathbf{z}\right)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf{a}_{n}\mathbf{a}_{n}^{H}z_{n}

cluster points of a common coordinate descent method to solve

4

4
).

Given

set
\left[N\right]:=\left\{1,\dots,N\right\}
.
The set of Hermitian matrices is denoted by

\mathbb{H}^{M}:=\big\{\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times M}:\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{A}^{H}\big\}

and the set of Hermitian positive definite matrices by

\mathbb{HPD}^{M}:=\left\{\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{H}^{M}:\textnormal{$\mathbf{A}$ is positive definite}\right\}
.
The
m
-th largest eigenvalue of
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{H}^{M}
is denoted by

\lambda_{m}\left(\mathbf{A}\right)

For any
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times N}

\ell_{p}

\left\|\mathbf{A}\right\|_{p}

\left\|\mathbf{A}\right\|_{2}

For any
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times N}

\ell_{q}

\left\|\mathbf{A}\right\|_{p\rightarrow q}:=\sup_{\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|_{p}\leq 1}\left\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\right\|_{q}

The space
\mathbb{H}^{M}
is embedded with the topology induced by the frobenius norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{2}
.
Note that for any
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{H}^{M}
one has

\left\|\mathbf{A}\right\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\lambda_{m}\left(\mathbf{A}\right)^{2}
,
which will be used frequently.

\mathbb{HPD}^{M}\subset\mathbb{H}^{M}
is equipped with the subspace topology.
Note that sets are compact in
\mathbb{HPD}^{M}
if and only if they are compact in

\mathbb{H}^{M}
. However, sets that are closed in
\mathbb{HPD}^{M}
are not necessarily closed in

\mathbb{H}^{M}
, since
\mathbb{HPD}^{M}
is not closed in
\mathbb{H}^{M}
,
and thus not a complete metric space.
The same is true for any set
\mathcal{H}\subset\mathbb{HPD}^{M}

that is closed in
\mathbb{HPD}^{M}
which is always embedded with the subspace topology of

\mathbb{HPD}^{M}
.
The set of

-sparse vectors is denoted by
\Sigma_{S}^{N}:=\left\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:\textnormal{$\mathbf{x}$ has at most $S$ non-zero coordinates}\right\}
and the set of non-negative vectors is denoted by
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}:=\left\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:x_{n}\geq 0\right\}
.
By

\mathbf{x}

By

\mathbf{\mu}\in\mathbb{C}^{M}
and covariance
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times M}
.

2
Main Results

2.1
Robustness of Covariance Estimation

Given some
\mathcal{H}\subset\mathbb{HPD}^{M}
and

g:\left(0,\infty\right)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

the covariance estimators considered in this work are minimizers of

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{Z}\in\mathcal{H}}\sum_{m=1}^{M}g\left(\lambda_{m}\left(\mathbf{W}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{Z}^{-1}\mathbf{W}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)\right).

Here
\mathcal{H}
is any set of potential covariance matrices fitting a structured model
and
\mathbf{W}
is a perturbed observation of a searched and unknown
covariance matrix
\mathbf{X}
. For instance,
\mathbf{W}

could be a sample covariance matrix.
Certainly some restrictions on
\mathcal{H}
and
g
are required, because otherwise there might not
even be a solution to the optimization problem. In this work
\mathcal{H}
will be a closed set in

\mathbb{HPD}^{M}
and one of two different conditions on
g
is considered.
The following requirements on
g
are the minimal requirements for the proof of the main result.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
g:\left(0,\infty\right)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,

g_{1}:\left[g\left(1\right),\infty\right)\rightarrow\left(0,1\right]
,

g_{2}:\left[g\left(1\right),\infty\right)\rightarrow\left[1,\infty\right)

and

\delta_{1},\delta_{2}:\left(0,\infty\right)\rightarrow\left(0,\infty\right)
.

g
and the tuple
\left(g,g_{1},g_{2},\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\right)
are each called
sufficiently nice if the following properties are fulfilled.

1.

g
is sufficiently growing, namely

\lim_{x\rightarrow 0}g\left(x\right)=\infty=\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}g\left(x\right)
.

2.

g
is continuous everywhere.

3.

g
is continuous around
1
with

\left|x-1\right|\leq\delta_{1}\left(\epsilon\right)\Rightarrow\left|g\left(x\right)-g\left(1\right)\right|\leq\epsilon

for all
\epsilon>0
.

4.

The minimizer is sufficiently explicit, namely

g\left(x\right)-g\left(1\right)\leq\delta_{2}\left(\epsilon\right)\Rightarrow\left|x-1\right|\leq\epsilon

for all
\epsilon>0
.

5.

The almost inverse functions exist and are defined by

g_{1}\left(y\right)=\inf_{z\in\left(0,1\right]:g\left(z\right)\leq y}z

and

g_{2}\left(y\right)=\sup_{z\in\left[1,\infty\right):g\left(z\right)\leq y}z

for all
y\in\left[g\left(1\right),\infty\right)
.

Note that Property (
4
)
implies that
g\left(x\right)\geq g\left(1\right)
for all
x\in\left(0,1\right)

and that
1
is the unique global minimizer of
g
.
This further guarantees that the minimizer for

\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{HPD}^{M}
is always
\mathbf{W}
since the identity is the only matrix
with only
1
as eigenvalue. In this case the robustness is trivial.
Whenever
\mathcal{H}\neq\mathbb{HPD}^{M}
, solving the optimization problem
and proving robustness is not
trivial however. Due to Property (
1
) the almost inverse functions

g_{1},g_{2}
satisfying Property (
5
) always exist and are well defined.
Further, Property (
1
) and Property (
2
)
yield that they are strictly monotonic.
The following stricter condition on
g
can be used to improve the robustness.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
g:\left(0,\infty\right)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,

g_{1}:\left[g\left(1\right),\infty\right)\rightarrow\left(0,1\right]
,

g_{2}:\left[g\left(1\right),\infty\right)\rightarrow\left[1,\infty\right)
,

\nu>0
and
\epsilon_{0}\in\left(0,1\right)
.

g
and the tuple
\left(g,g_{1},g_{2},\nu,\epsilon_{0}\right)
are each called
sufficiently convex if the following properties are fulfilled.

1.

g
is sufficiently growing, namely

\lim_{x\rightarrow 0}g\left(x\right)=\infty=\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}g\left(x\right)
.

2.

g
is continuous everywhere.

3.

g
is strictly monotonically falling in
\left(0,1\right]
with inverse function

g_{1}
.

4.

g
is strictly monotonically increasing in
\left[1,\infty\right)
with inverse function

g_{2}
.

5.

g\left(1+\epsilon\right)\leq g\left(1-\epsilon\right)
for all
\epsilon\in\left(0,1\right)
.

6.

g
is convex on
\left[1,\infty\right)
.

7.

g
is differentiable everywhere with

g^{\prime}\left(x\right)\neq 0
for all
x\neq 1
and

-\frac{g^{\prime}\left(1+\epsilon\right)}{g^{\prime}\left(1-\epsilon\right)}\geq\nu

for all
\epsilon\in\left(0,\epsilon_{0}\right]
.

It is later proven that
g
being sufficiently convex is indeed a strictly stronger condition
than
g
being sufficiently nice.
Under any of these conditions, the corresponding covariance estimators are robust.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let the tuple
g
be sufficiently nice or sufficiently convex,

\mathcal{H}\subset\mathbb{HPD}^{M}
be closed in
\mathbb{HPD}^{M}
and
\mathbf{X}\in\mathcal{H}
.
Then, there exists a function
\delta:\left(0,\infty\right)\rightarrow\left(0,\infty\right)
such that
the following holds true:
For every
\epsilon>0
and
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{HPD}^{M}
with

\left\|\mathbf{W}-\mathbf{X}\right\|_{2\rightarrow 2}\leq\delta\left(\epsilon\right)
,
any minimizer
\mathbf{Z}
of

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{Z}\in\mathcal{H}}\sum_{m=1}^{M}g\left(\lambda_{m}\left(\mathbf{W}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{Z}^{-1}\mathbf{W}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)\right)

(8)

obeys
\left\|\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{Z}\right\|_{2\rightarrow 2}\leq\epsilon
.
If
g
is sufficiently convex, then
\delta
can be chosen such that it is
linear in a neighborhood around
0
.

Proof.

This is just a shortened version of Theorem
3.5
and Theorem
3.7
.

The robustness of Theorem
2.3
yields that any such covariance estimator indeed
recovers matrices correctly if
\mathbf{W}
is not perturbed.
If
\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{X}
, then
\left\|\mathbf{W}-\mathbf{X}\right\|_{2\rightarrow 2}\leq\delta\left(\epsilon\right)
and thus
\left\|\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{Z}\right\|_{2\rightarrow 2}\leq\epsilon
for all
\epsilon>0
.
It follows that
\mathbf{Z}=\mathbf{X}
.
Moreover, the robustness gives control over the estimation error.
If the estimation error
\left\|\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{Z}\right\|_{2\rightarrow 2}
is supposed to be
small, then one just needs to control the magnitude of the perturbation
\left\|\mathbf{W}-\mathbf{X}\right\|_{2\rightarrow 2}
.
This control implies that any function that maps
\mathbf{W}
to any
minimizer of (
8
)
is continuous in all
\mathbf{X}\in\mathcal{H}
.
Theorem
2.3
will be used with
g(x)=x-\ln\left(x\right)
,
\mathbf{W}=\frac{1}{K}\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{Y}^{H}
,
\mathbf{X}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf{a}_{n}\mathbf{a}_{n}^{H}x_{n}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}

and
\mathcal{H}=\left\{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf{a}_{n}\mathbf{a}_{n}^{H}z_{n}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\hskip 2.0pt\text{ for all }\hskip 2.0pt\mathbf{z}\geq 0\right\}

to get a result for the relaxed maximum likelihood estimator in Theorem
2.6
.
However, this will only be a part of the proof.

2.2
Application to Activity Detection

In this work codebooks that generate linear operators
\mathcal{A}\left(\mathbf{z}\right)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf{a}_{n}\mathbf{a}_{n}^{H}z_{n}

with a signed kernel condition are considered.
The signed kernel condition was introduced in
[
15
]
.

Definition 2.4

[

By

codebooks

Theorem 2.5

Algorithm 1

Coordinate Descent for Relaxed Maximum Likelihood Decoding

INPUT:

measurement
\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times K}
, measurement matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times N}

with columns
\mathbf{a}_{n}
for
n\in\left[N\right]
,

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{HPD}^{M}
,
permutation
\sigma:\left[N\right]\rightarrow\left[N\right]
,
initialization
\mathbf{x}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}

OUTPUT:

estimator
\mathbf{x}^{\#}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\prime}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}

WHILE

any suitable stopping condition is not yet fulfilled

DO

FOR

n^{\prime}=1,\dots,N
(ordered!)

DO

n:=\sigma\left(n^{\prime}\right)

t\leftarrow\max\left\{-x_{n},\left(\mathbf{a}_{n}^{H}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\prime}\frac{1}{K}\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{Y}^{H}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\prime}\mathbf{a}_{n}-\mathbf{a}_{n}^{H}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\prime}\mathbf{a}_{n}\right)\left(\mathbf{a}_{n}^{H}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\prime}\mathbf{a}_{n}\right)^{-2}\right\}

RETURN:

\mathbf{x}^{\#}\leftarrow\mathbf{x}

Lemma 3.1
.

Let the tuple
\left(g,g_{1},g_{2},\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\right)
be sufficiently nice
and
\mathcal{H}\subset\mathbb{HPD}^{M}
be closed in
\mathbb{HPD}^{M}

For all

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{Z}\in\mathcal{H}}\sum_{m=1}^{M}g\left(\lambda_{m}\left(\mathbf{W}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{Z}^{-1}\mathbf{W}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{1}\left(\mathbf{W}\right)}{g_{2}\left(\gamma-(M-1)g\left(1\right)\right)}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{m}\left(\mathbf{Z}\right)\leq\frac{\lambda_{M}\left(\mathbf{W}\right)}{g_{1}\left(\gamma-(M-1)g\left(1\right)\right)}\hskip 2.0pt\text{ for all }\hskip 2.0pt\mathbf{Z}\in\mathcal{G}\hskip 2.0pt\text{ and }\hskip 2.0ptm\in\left[M\right].

(12)

Proof.

Note that if
\gamma<Mg\left(1\right)
, then
\mathcal{G}
is empty and hence compact,

\gamma\geq Mg\left(1\right)
and

Let
\mathbf{v}

(

Lemma 3.3
.

(

\displaystyle\delta_{c}\left(\epsilon\right)=

4.2
Signed Kernel Condition

The next part of the proof of Theorem
2.6
is based on
the signed kernel condition from Definition
2.4
and the robustness constant from

[
15
, Definition 2.1]
.

Definition 4.3
.

Let
\mathcal{A}:\mathbb{C}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{M\times M}
be a linear operator,

S\in\mathbb{N}
and
\left\|\cdot\right\|
a norm on
\mathbb{C}^{M\times M}
.
The constant

\displaystyle\tau\left(\mathcal{A}\right):=\inf_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+},\mathbf{x}\in\Sigma_{S}^{N}\cap\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}\mathbf{z}\neq\mathbf{x}}\frac{\left\|\mathcal{A}\left(\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{x}\right)\right\|}{\left\|\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{x}\right\|_{2}}

is called robustness constant.

The general norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|
appearing in this definition is due to the
later explained relation of the robustness constant with the
non-negative least residual estimator which is any minimizer of

\displaystyle\underset{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}}{\textnormal{argmin}}\hskip 1.0pt\left\|\mathcal{A}\left(\mathbf{z}\right)-\mathbf{W}^{\prime}\right\|.

So the non-negative least residual is the non-negative least squares with
the
\ell_{2}
-norm being replaced by the general norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|
.
The constant
\tau\left(\mathcal{A}\right)
depends on the choice of the norm

\left\|\cdot\right\|
as well as the sparsity
S
. Its dependence is omitted for the sake of brevity.
Due to the following result from
[
15
, Theorem 3.2, Theorem 2.2, Proposition 2.8]

an signed kernel condition is the equivalent condition for robust recovery with the
non-negative least residual, and the robustness constant gives a relation
between the estimation error and the magnitude of the perturbation.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
\mathcal{A}:\mathbb{C}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{M\times M}

have signed kernel condition of order
S
and let
\left\|\cdot\right\|
be a norm on

\mathbb{C}^{M\times M}
. Then,
\tau\left(\mathcal{A}\right)>0

and

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{2}{\tau\left(\mathcal{A}\right)}\left\|\mathbf{W}^{\prime}-\mathcal{A}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\right\|\hskip 2.0pt\text{ for all }\hskip 2.0pt\mathbf{x}\in\Sigma_{S}^{N}\cap\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N},\mathbf{W}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times M},\textnormal{$\mathbf{z}$ minimizer of $\min_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}}\left\|\mathcal{A}\left(\mathbf{z}\right)-\mathbf{W}^{\prime}\right\|$}

(52)

holds true.

Proof.

By
[
15
, Theorem 3.2]
one has

\displaystyle\{\mathbf{x}\}=\min_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+}}\left\|\mathcal{A}\left(\mathbf{z}\right)-\mathcal{A}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\right\|\hskip 2.0pt\text{ for all }\hskip 2.0pt\mathbf{x}\in\Sigma_{S}^{N}\cap\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+}.

By
[
15
, Theorem 2.2]
together with
[
15
, Proposition 2.8]

it follows that
(
52
) holds true.

It should be noted that a linear operator can have the signed kernel condition of order

S
and a non-zero but very small robustness constant
\tau\left(\mathcal{A}\right)
.
This can cause the estimation error to be very large even for very small perturbations.
In implementations observations without perturbation can have small perturbations
due to machine precision, or estimators will solve optimization problems only up to
a predefined precision effectively causing a small perturbation.
This together with a small robustness constant
can cause recovery to seemingly fail in implementations
even if no perturbation is present.
The construction in (
9
) exactly has these problems
and is thus only of theoretical value. For implementations
constructions with better robustness constants are required.

Combining this robustness result with the robustness of the
trace-log-det covariance estimator
yields a robustness result for the relaxed maximum likelihood estimator.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
\mathcal{A}:\mathbb{C}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{M\times M}

have signed kernel condition of order
S
.
For all
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{HPD}^{M}
,
\mathbf{x}\in\Sigma_{S}^{N}\cap\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}
,

0<\beta<\lambda_{1}\left(\mathcal{A}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right)
,
\eta>0
with

\mathcal{H}:=\left\{\mathcal{A}\left(\mathbf{z}\right)+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}:\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}\right\}\subset\mathbb{HPD}^{M}

there exists a function
\delta:\left(0,\infty\right)\rightarrow\left(0,\infty\right)
such that
the following holds true:
For every
\epsilon>0
and
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{HPD}^{M}
with

\left\|\mathbf{W}-\mathcal{A}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right\|_{2\rightarrow 2}

\leq\delta\left(\epsilon\right)
,
any minimizer
\mathbf{z}
of

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}}\textnormal{trace}\left(\left(\mathcal{A}\left(\mathbf{z}\right)+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{W}\right)+\ln\left(\textnormal{det}\left(\mathcal{A}\left(\mathbf{z}\right)+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right)\right)

(53)

obeys
\left\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}\right\|_{2}\leq\epsilon
.
In particular,
\delta
can be chosen as

\displaystyle\delta_{skc}\left(\epsilon\right):=

\displaystyle\min\Bigg\{\frac{\left(1-\ln\left(2\right)\right)M^{-1}\tau\left(\mathcal{A}\right)}{\ln\left(2\right)}\frac{\lambda_{1}\left(\mathcal{A}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right)}{\lambda_{M}\left(\mathcal{A}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right)}\left(\frac{\lambda_{1}\left(\mathcal{A}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right)-\beta}{\lambda_{M}\left(\mathcal{A}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right)+\beta}\right)\frac{W_{0}\left(-\exp\left(-\left(1+\eta\right)\right)\right)}{W_{-1}\left(-\exp\left(-\left(1+\eta\right)\right)\right)}\epsilon,

\displaystyle\tau\left(\mathcal{A}\right)\epsilon,\left(\ln\left(4\right)-1\right)M\lambda_{M}\left(\mathcal{A}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right)\left(\frac{\lambda_{M}\left(\mathcal{A}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right)+\beta}{\lambda_{1}\left(\mathcal{A}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right)-\beta}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{W_{-1}\left(-\exp\left(-\left(1+\eta\right)\right)\right)}{W_{0}\left(-\exp\left(-\left(1+\eta\right)\right)\right)},

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}\left(\mathcal{A}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right)\left(\frac{\lambda_{1}\left(\mathcal{A}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right)-\beta}{\lambda_{M}\left(\mathcal{A}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right)+\beta}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(1+W_{0}\left(-\exp\left(-\left(1+\frac{\eta}{M}\right)\right)\right)\right),

\displaystyle\left(1-\ln\left(2\right)\right)\lambda_{1}\left(\mathcal{A}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right)\left(\frac{\lambda_{1}\left(\mathcal{A}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right)-\beta}{\lambda_{M}\left(\mathcal{A}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right)+\beta}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},\beta\Bigg\}.

(54)

Proof.

Choose the norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|:=\left\|\cdot\right\|_{2\rightarrow 2}
for the robustness constant.
Note that
\mathcal{H}
is closed since
\mathcal{A}
is linear and thus continuous on
the finite-dimensional space
\mathbb{C}^{N}
.
Set
\mathbf{X}:=\mathcal{A}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
and let
\delta_{tld}
be from
Theorem
4.2
so that

\delta_{skc}\left(\epsilon\right)=\delta_{tld}\left(\frac{\tau\left(\mathcal{A}\right)}{2}\epsilon\right)
.
Now let
\mathbf{W}
be such that

\left\|\mathbf{W}-\mathcal{A}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right\|_{2\rightarrow 2}\leq\delta_{skc}\left(\epsilon\right)
.
It follows

\left\|\mathbf{W}-\mathbf{X}\right\|_{2\rightarrow 2}\leq\delta_{skc}\left(\epsilon\right)=\delta_{tld}\left(\frac{\tau\left(\mathcal{A}\right)}{2}\epsilon\right)

and
0<\beta<\lambda_{1}\left(\mathbf{X}\right)
.
If
\mathbf{z}
is an optimizer of (
53
) then

\mathbf{Z}:=\mathcal{A}\left(\mathbf{z}\right)+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
is an optimizer of
(
51
). Thus, Theorem
4.2

yields that

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{Z}-\mathbf{X}\right\|_{2\rightarrow 2}\leq\frac{\tau\left(\mathcal{A}\right)}{2}\epsilon.

(55)

Now set
\mathbf{W}^{\prime}:=\mathbf{Z}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\mathcal{A}\left(\mathbf{z}\right)
so that

\mathbf{z}
is an optimizer of
\min_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}}\left\|\mathcal{A}\left(\mathbf{z}\right)-\mathbf{W}^{\prime}\right\|_{2\rightarrow 2}
.
By Theorem
4.4
one gets

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{x}\right\|_{2}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\tau\left(\mathcal{A}\right)}\left\|\mathbf{W}^{\prime}-\mathcal{A}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\right\|_{2\rightarrow 2}=\frac{2}{\tau\left(\mathcal{A}\right)}\left\|\mathbf{Z}-\mathbf{X}\right\|_{2\rightarrow 2}.

Applying (
55
) to this yields the claim.

To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this is the first robustness result for
the relaxed maximum likelihood estimator.
Note that (
52
)
is again a robustness result, and indeed one could choose

\delta\left(\cdot\right):=\frac{2}{\tau\left(\mathcal{A}\right)}\cdot
.
The robustness derived in Theorem
4.5
is weaker, since

\delta_{skc}
depends negatively on the
dimension

. Due to this, the non-negative least residual has better robustness properties
and better recovery guarantees. An interesting question would be whether Theorem
4.5

can be improved by removing all dimensional scaling parameters
to make
\delta_{skc}
scale like
\frac{1}{\tau\left(\mathcal{A}\right)}

and thus make the result as good as the recovery guarantee for the
non-negative least residual.
This is left for future investigation.

Now it is shown that the signed kernel condition is also an equivalent condition for successful recovery
with the relaxed maximum likelihood estimator
in the infinite antenna case.
This result is not required for the proof of Theorem
2.6
but is of independent interest.

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{HPD}^{M}
and

\mathcal{A}:\mathbb{C}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{M\times M}

be a linear operator such that

\mathcal{H}:=\big\{\mathcal{A}\left(\mathbf{z}\right)+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}:\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}\big\}\subset\mathbb{HPD}^{M}
.
Then, the following are equivalent.

1.

\mathcal{A}
has the signed kernel condition of order
S
.

2.

For all
\mathbf{x}\in\Sigma_{S}^{N}\cap\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}

the problem

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}}\textnormal{trace}\left(\left(\mathcal{A}\left(\mathbf{z}\right)+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathcal{A}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right)\right)+\ln\left(\textnormal{det}\left(\mathcal{A}\left(\mathbf{z}\right)+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right)\right)

(56)

has a unique minimizer and it is
\mathbf{x}
.

Proof.

By (
50
) from
Lemma
4.1
the minimizers of
(
56
) are exactly the minimizers of

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}}\sum_{m=1}^{M}g\left(\lambda_{m}\left(\left(\mathcal{A}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\mathcal{A}\left(\mathbf{z}\right)+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathcal{A}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)\right)

(57)

for some
g
of a sufficiently convex tuple.
Since
g
is part of a sufficiently convex tuple,

1
is the unique minimizer of
\min_{x\in\left(0,\infty\right)}g(x)
.
It follows that
\mathbf{z}
is a minimizer of (
57
)
if and only if

\displaystyle g\left(\lambda_{m}\left(\left(\mathcal{A}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\mathcal{A}\left(\mathbf{z}\right)+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathcal{A}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)\right)=g\left(1\right)

for all
m\in\left[M\right]
.
This, on the other hand, is equivalent to

\mathcal{A}\left(\mathbf{z}\right)+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\mathcal{A}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
.
It follows that

\mathbf{z}
is a minimizer of (
56
)
if and only if
\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{x}\in\ker\left(\mathcal{A}\right)

(58)

holds true.
Applying
[
15
, Theorem 3.2]
and
[
15
, Theorem 2.2]

yields that

\mathcal{A}
having the signed kernel condition of order
S

is equivalent to

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}-\Sigma_{S}^{N}\cap\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}\right)\cap\ker\left(\mathcal{A}\right)=\left\{0\right\}.

(59)

It is now shown that the latter is equivalent to the second condition of this theorem.

Assume that (
59
) holds true.
Let
\mathbf{x}\in\Sigma_{S}^{N}\cap\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}
and
\mathbf{z}
be a minimizer of
(
56
).
Then,
\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}-\Sigma_{S}^{N}\cap\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}

and by (
58
)
\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{x}\in\ker\left(\mathcal{A}\right)
.
By (
59
)
\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{x}

and
\mathbf{x}
is the unique minimizer of
(
56
).
On the other hand, assume that
for all
\mathbf{x}\in\Sigma_{S}^{N}\cap\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}
the problem
(
56
) has the unique minimizer
\mathbf{x}
.
To prove the converse implication, let
\mathbf{v}\in\left(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}-\Sigma_{S}^{N}\cap\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}\right)\cap\ker\left(\mathcal{A}\right)
.
Then, there exist

\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}
and
\mathbf{x}\in\Sigma_{S}^{N}\cap\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}
so that

\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{v}\in\ker\left(\mathcal{A}\right)
.
By (

)
\mathbf{z}
is a minimizer of
(

). By assumption

so that (

) holds true.

Note that the convergence of Theorem
2.6
can only
hold if the the unique minimizer property in

4.6

is fulfilled, and by Theorem
4.6

the conclusion and convergence of Theorem
2.6

can only hold if the operator

has the signed kernel condition of order
S
.
By
[
15
, Remark 3.14]
no other matrix

\mathbf{A}
can generate a linear operator

\mathcal{A}\left(\mathbf{z}\right)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf{a}_{n}\mathbf{a}_{n}^{H}z_{n}

with a higher order of the signed kernel condition than

2.5
.
Hence, the condition

in
Theorem
2.6
is optimal and can not be improved.

The unique identifiability condition in
[
13
, Theorem 5]

can only guarantee recovery of
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}
if its non-zero entries
are at specific positions.
Such recovery guarantees are called non-uniform.
The signed kernel condition, on the other hand, guarantees

.

4.3
Conclusion of Theorem
2.6
by a Concentration Argument

In order to prove Theorem
2.6
one can now use an operator
with signed kernel condition and apply Theorem
4.5
to the case of the relaxed maximum likelihood
estimator. For the non-negative least squares this will be even easier.
It remains to show that for
K
large enough,

\left\|\frac{1}{K}\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{Y}^{H}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}-\mathcal{A}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\right\|_{2\rightarrow 2}

is sufficiently small with arbitrarily high probability.
This can be done by applying a concentration inequality for
sub-exponential random variables often called Bernstein type inequality.
In this subsection a precise definition of sub-exponential and sub-Gaussian
random variables is required.

For a random variable
X
define

\left\|X\right\|_{\psi_{p}}:=\inf_{t>0:\frac{\exp\left(\left|X\right|^{p}\right)}{t^{2}}\leq 2}t
.
A random variable
X
is called sub-exponential if
\left\|X\right\|_{\psi_{1}}<\infty

and sub-Gaussian if
\left\|X\right\|_{\psi_{2}}<\infty
. See
[
20
]
for more information. Note that if

X\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\sigma^{2}\right)
, then

X
is sub-Gaussian with
\left\|X\right\|_{\psi_{2}}=2\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}\sigma
.

Lemma 4.7
.

There exists a constant
c>0
such that the following holds true:
Let the
K
columns of
\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times K}
be mutually independent

\mathcal{CN}\left(0,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\prime}\right)
random variables
for some
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{HPD}^{M}
and
\xi>0
.
Then

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{1}{K}\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{Y}^{H}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\prime}\right\|_{2}\leq\xi

(60)

holds true with probability of at least

\displaystyle p^{\prime}:=1-M\left(M+1\right)\exp\left(-cK\min\left\{\frac{9\xi^{2}}{128M^{2}\sup_{m^{\prime}\in\left[M\right]}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\prime}_{m^{\prime},m^{\prime}}\right)^{2}},\frac{3\xi}{8\sqrt{2}M\sup_{m^{\prime}\in\left[M\right]}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\prime}_{m^{\prime},m^{\prime}}}\right\}\right).

Proof.

Let
c>0
be the numerical constant from
[
20
, Theorem 2.8.1]
.
Let the columns of
\mathbf{Y}
be denoted by
\mathbf{y}_{k}
for
k\in\left[K\right]
.
Given
m_{1},m_{2}\in\left[M\right],k\in\left[K\right]
define

\displaystyle R_{m_{1},m_{2},k}:=

\displaystyle\textnormal{real}\left(y_{m_{1},k}\overline{y_{m_{2},k}}\right)-\textnormal{real}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\prime}_{m_{1},m_{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textnormal{real}\left(y_{m_{1},k}\right)\textnormal{real}\left(y_{m_{2},k}\right)+\textnormal{imag}\left(y_{m_{1},k}\right)\textnormal{imag}\left(y_{m_{2},k}\right)-\textnormal{real}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\prime}_{m_{1},m_{2}}\right)\hskip 2.0pt\text{ and }\hskip 2.0pt

\displaystyle I_{m_{1},m_{2},k}:=

\displaystyle\textnormal{imag}\left(y_{m_{1},k}\overline{y_{m_{2},k}}\right)-\textnormal{imag}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\prime}_{m_{1},m_{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textnormal{imag}\left(y_{m_{1},k}\right)\textnormal{real}\left(y_{m_{2},k}\right)-\textnormal{real}\left(y_{m_{1},k}\right)\textnormal{imag}\left(y_{m_{2},k}\right)-\textnormal{imag}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\prime}_{m_{1},m_{2}}\right).

Since

\mathbf{y}_{k}\sim\mathcal{CN}\left(0,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\prime}\right)
,
one gets

\textnormal{real}\left(y_{m,k}\right),\textnormal{imag}\left(y_{m,k}\right)\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\prime}_{m,m}\right)

and hence these are sub-Gaussian with

\displaystyle\left\|\textnormal{real}\left(y_{m,k}\right)\right\|_{\psi_{2}}=\left\|\textnormal{imag}\left(y_{m,k}\right)\right\|_{\psi_{2}}=2\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\prime}_{m,m}}\leq 2\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}\sup_{m^{\prime}\in\left[M\right]}\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\prime}_{m^{\prime},m^{\prime}}}.

Due to
[
20
, Lemma 2.7.7]
the random variables
R_{m_{1},m_{2},k}

and
I_{m_{1},m_{2},k}
are sub-exponential random variables with

\displaystyle\left\|R_{m_{1},m_{2},k}\right\|_{\psi_{1}}\leq\frac{8}{3}\sup_{m^{\prime}\in\left[M\right]}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\prime}_{m^{\prime},m^{\prime}}\geq\left\|I_{m_{1},m_{2},k}\right\|_{\psi_{1}}.

(61)

Since
\mathbf{y}_{k}\sim\mathcal{CN}\left(0,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\prime}\right)
,
it follows that

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{K}\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{Y}^{H}\right]=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{y}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{H}\right]=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\prime}

and thus,

6
Number Of Receive Antennas

In this section the scaling of the number of receive antennas
K
in
Theorem
2.6
is discussed. The number of receive antennas is dependent on which of the two
estimators (
10
) and
(
11
) is chosen
and needs to satisfy (
66
)
or (
68
) respectively.
The first part of the maximum in (
68
)
can simply be evaluated so that the second part of this maximum
is of interest.
The term
-\frac{8}{3}\ln\left(\frac{1-p}{M\left(M+1\right)}\right)>0
appears in both (
66
) and (
68
),
comes from the union bound, and can also be evaluated.
The term

\sup_{m\in\left[M\right]}\left(\mathcal{A}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right)_{m,m}
requires knowledge of the vector
of large scale fading coefficients that is unknown prior to choosing the number of receive antennas.
However, if additional box constraints for the large scale fading coefficients are known,
this value can be bounded and evaluated easily as well.
Such constraints are usually present and known in applications,
since devices can only send at a maximum power
or are considered inactive and absorbed in the noise if
their transmit power is too small.

Ignoring
\tau\left(\mathcal{A}\right)
,
\epsilon
,
\delta\left(\epsilon\right)
as
well as the logarithmic terms the number of receive antennas
K
needs to scale at least
in the order of
M^{2}
. However, the more insightful information is within terms

\tau\left(\mathcal{A}\right)
,
\epsilon
and
\delta\left(\epsilon\right)
.
According to (
66
)
in the case of the non-negative least squares
the number of receive antennas
depends directly on the product
\tau\left(\mathcal{A}\right)\epsilon
.
According to (
68
)
in the case of the relaxed maximum likelihood estimator
the number of receive antennas depends on

\delta\left(\epsilon\right)
from Theorem
4.5
which can be chosen as
\delta_{skc}
.
Due to (
54
)
\delta_{skc}
scales like the product

M^{-1}\tau\left(\mathcal{A}\right)\epsilon
for
\epsilon
small enough and
M
large enough.
Due to this, the relaxed maximum likelihood estimator requires significantly more receive
antennas to achieve the same estimation errors in Theorem
2.6
.
It remains open, whether this gap can be overcome by improving Theorem
4.5
.

Consider
K_{0}=K_{0}\left(\epsilon\right)
from
(
66
)
or (
68
) respectively
as a function of
\epsilon
.
If all factors but
\epsilon
are constant and
\epsilon
is small enough,

\delta_{skc}
is linear.
Thus, in both cases
K_{0}\left(\epsilon\right)
is also invertible for
\epsilon

small enough, and it scales like
K_{0}\left(\epsilon\right)=C\epsilon^{-2}
.
Hence, if all other factors are constant and
\epsilon=\left\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}\right\|_{2}
is small enough,

\displaystyle K\geq K_{0}\left(\epsilon\right)=C\left\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}\right\|_{2}^{-2}

(70)

is sufficient to achieve the estimation error
\left\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}\right\|_{2}
.

However, both estimators depend on the robustness constant
\tau\left(\mathcal{A}\right)
.
It should be noted that the robustness constants are different in each case because
they depend on different norms, see the proof of Theorem
2.6
with (
10
) and the proof of Theorem
4.5
respectively.
However, this is not that important since independent of the norm
the robustness constant of any normalized operator
\mathcal{A}
satisfies

\displaystyle\tau\left(\mathcal{A}\right)\leq 2\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}\left(\exp\left(\frac{S}{4M^{2}}\ln\left(\frac{N}{4S}\right)\right)-1\right)^{-1}

(71)

by

Thus, in both cases

can only stay constant
if the number of pilot symbols satisfies
M^{2}\asymp S\ln\left(\frac{N}{4S}\right)

or is higher.
If
M^{2}\asymp S\ln\left(\frac{N}{4S}\right)
is violated,
the robustness constant decreases exponentially in
\frac{S}{4M^{2}}\ln\left(\frac{N}{4S}\right)

and, ignoring other factors, the number of receive antennas also grows exponentially in

\frac{S}{4M^{2}}\ln\left(\frac{N}{4S}\right)
.
So Theorem
2.6
allows one to reduce the required number of pilot symbols
from
M^{2}\asymp S\left(\ln\left(\mathrm{e}\frac{N}{S}\right)\right)^{2}
to
M^{2}\asymp S
,
but it pays the price for a significantly increased number of receive antennas due to

\tau\left(\mathcal{A}\right)
decreasing.

Consider the choice
\epsilon:=\frac{1}{4}\min_{n:x_{n}\neq 0}\left|x_{n}\right|
in
Remark
2.7

and the ratio
\xi=\frac{\epsilon}{\sup_{m^{\prime}\in\left[M\right]}\left(\mathcal{A}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right)_{m^{\prime},m^{\prime}}}
,
and assume all other factors stay constant.
For large
\epsilon
and small
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
the ratio
\xi
stays constant,
and due to (
66
)
K
can be chosen
constant for the non-negative least squares.
However, for small
\epsilon
and large
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
the ratio
\xi
is small,
and according to (
66
)
K
needs to scale
like
\frac{1}{\xi^{2}}
for the non-negative least squares.
On the other hand, for small
\epsilon
and large
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}

the ratio
\xi^{2}
scales roughly like

\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{\sup_{m^{\prime}\in\left[M\right]}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right)_{m^{\prime},m^{\prime}}^{2}}

which is basically the signal-to-noise ratio of the weakest user.
Thus, the number of active users
K
for the non-negative least squares
has to scale like one over the signal-to-noise ratio of the weakest user
if the signal-to-noise ratio of the weakest user is small.

In the case of the relaxed maximum likelihood estimator one needs to consider
the denominator
\delta\left(\epsilon\right)
instead of
\epsilon

due to (
68
).

\delta\left(\epsilon\right)
can be chosen as
\delta_{skc}\left(\epsilon\right)
from
(
54
) which is scaling linearly in
\epsilon
for small

\epsilon
. Hence, the number of active users
K
for the relaxed maximum likelihood
estimator also has to scale like one over the signal-to-noise ratio of the weakest user
if the signal-to-noise ratio of the weakest user is small.
In any case, any change in the signal-to-noise ratio can be compensated by potentially
increasing the number of receive antennas.
