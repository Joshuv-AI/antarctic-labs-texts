Canakinumab dosing intervals in pediatric colchicine-resistant FMF: outcomes and predictors of successful extension in a real-world cohort
Authors: Nergis Akay, Kenan Barut, Betul Kosa, Ekin Nurhan Mert, Zeynep Torunoglu, Ece Aslan, Umit Gul, Elif Kilic Konte, Mehmet Yildiz, Sezgin Sahin, Ozgur Kasapcopur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesCanakinumab is widely used in colchicine-resistant familial Mediterranean fever (FMF), yet data on interval adjustment strategies and predictors of successful extension remain limited. This study evaluated clinical and inflammatory outcomes across dosing interval groups and identified factors associated with successful interval extension.MethodsThis retrospective single-centre study included 60 pediatric patients with colchicine-resistant FMF treated with canakinumab. Patients were stratified into stable interval (group 1, n = 14), successful interval extension (group 2, n = 24) and unsuccessful interval extension (group 3, n = 22). Longitudinal C-reactive protein (CRP) trajectories were analysed using linear mixed-effects models. Clinical outcomes were compared across groups, and predictors of successful interval extension were assessed using multivariable logistic regression with Firth’s penalized method as a sensitivity analysis.ResultsFollowing canakinumab initiation, CRP levels declined rapidly and stabilized after month 3, while the median annual attack rate decreased from 7 (4–12) to 1.1 (0.5–2.1) attacks per patient-year. Inflammatory trajectories did not differ across groups. Despite similar CRP dynamics, complete remission rates differed significantly (57.1%, 45.8% and 27.3% in Groups 1–3, respectively; P = 0.005), and all patients in group 3 experienced attacks. Arthritis at disease onset was independently associated with successful interval extension (OR = 7.14, 95% CI 1.26–40.41, P = 0.026), with consistent direction in Firth analysis (OR = 5.51, 95% CI 1.08–28.23, P = 0.081).ConclusionAlthough canakinumab achieves sustained inflammatory suppression, biochemical improvement does not ensure comparable clinical control. Clinical phenotype, particularly arthritis at onset, may guide individualized interval adjustment.

Introduction

Familial Mediterranean fever (FMF) is a common childhood-onset monogenic autoinflammatory disease inherited in an autosomal recessive manner and characterized by recurrent attacks of fever, serositis and arthritis that typically resolve within 3–4 days [1]. The disease is primarily caused by pathogenic variants in the MEFV gene, leading to dysregulated pyrin inflammasome activity and excessive IL-1β-mediated innate immune inflammation [2]. Despite its generally self-limited course, the broad and heterogeneous clinical presentation of FMF may result in delayed diagnosis and treatment, thereby increasing the risk of long-term complications, most notably AA amyloidosis [3]. The primary goals of treatment in FMF are to control acute inflammatory attacks, suppress ongoing inflammation, prevent attack recurrence and ultimately avoid inflammation-related complications [4]. Colchicine remains the first-line and most widely used treatment for FMF and is highly effective in suppressing inflammatory attacks, preventing amyloidosis and improving long-term outcomes. Nevertheless, despite optimal and continuous therapy, a subset of patients continues to exhibit colchicine resistance or intolerance, reported in ∼5–10% of cases [5, 6]. These patients represent a high-risk group with increased disease burden, impaired quality of life and a continued risk of inflammation-related organ damage. While colchicine remains the first-line therapy, patients who develop colchicine resistance require alternative targeted treatment strategies. Given that excessive interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β)-mediated inflammation represents the final common pathway in FMF pathogenesis, inhibition of IL-1 has emerged as a rational and effective therapeutic approach in this setting [7–11]. Canakinumab, a long-acting monoclonal antibody targeting IL-1β, is one of the available anti-IL-1 agents and received regulatory approval in 2016 for the treatment of colchicine-resistant FMF [12–14]. Its efficacy and safety have been demonstrated in multiple clinical trials and real-world studies, and its prolonged dosing interval provides a practical advantage, particularly in pediatric patients [12, 15–20]. However, despite increasing clinical experience, uncertainties remain regarding optimal dosing, interval adjustment and long-term treatment optimization, highlighting the need for real-world pediatric data. Accordingly, the present study aimed to describe the clinical characteristics of children with FMF treated with canakinumab at our centre and to evaluate treatment effectiveness and real-life management strategies.

Methods

Study design and settings

This single-centre retrospective cohort study was conducted at the Pediatric Rheumatology Department of Istanbul University–Cerrahpaşa, a tertiary referral centre. The study included children diagnosed with FMF who were treated with canakinumab in routine clinical practice between 2015 and 2025. Patient data were anonymized prior to analysis. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Istanbul University-Cerrahpasa Clinical Research Ethics Committee (approval date: 27 December 2022; approval number: 576619) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective design.

Study population

This retrospective single-centre study included patients diagnosed with FMF before 18 years of age who subsequently initiated canakinumab therapy. FMF diagnosis was established according to the classification criteria in use at the time of each patient’s diagnosis. Patients diagnosed prior to 2019 were classified using either the Tel Hashomer criteria or the Yalçınkaya criteria, depending on the criteria applied by the treating physician, while patients diagnosed from 2019 onward were classified according to the Eurofever/PRINTO classification criteria [21–23]. Eligible patients had well-defined colchicine-resistant FMF, received canakinumab for at least 12 months, and had sufficient clinical and laboratory follow-up data available after treatment initiation. Colchicine resistance was defined as the persistence of recurrent clinical attacks and/or ongoing subclinical inflammation despite maximally tolerated colchicine therapy [24]. Patients with incomplete medical records, use of canakinumab for indications other than FMF, or the presence of an alternative primary autoinflammatory or autoimmune disease that could confound treatment response assessment were excluded.

Data collection

Clinical data were retrospectively extracted from electronic medical records, outpatient clinic notes and hospitalization files. Recorded variables included demographic characteristics (age, sex, age at symptom onset, age at diagnosis and diagnostic delay); family-related factors (family history of FMF, parental consanguinity and geographic origin); clinical features (attack frequency and duration, manifestations such as fever, serositis, arthritis and exertional leg pain); genetic data (MEFV mutation status and genotype); laboratory parameters [C-reactive protein (CRP), serum amyloid A (SAA) when available]; treatment history (including colchicine and prior biologic therapies, with detailed information on treatment duration, dosing regimens and treatment response, particularly changes in attack frequency under each therapy); annualized attack rates during the 1-year period before and after canakinumab initiation, as well as the presence of subclinical inflammation prior to canakinumab therapy [defined as persistently elevated acute-phase reactants (CRP, SAA), particularly in during attack-free periods]; and canakinumab-related variables, including starting dose (mg/kg), dosing interval, treatment duration and dose or interval modifications.

Canakinumab treatment strategy and outcome definitions

Canakinumab was administered subcutaneously in routine clinical practice at a weight-based dose of 2–3 mg/kg (maximum 150 mg) every 4 or 8 weeks, according to disease severity and physician discretion. Patients were followed for ≥12 months. Based on longitudinal dosing patterns, patients were classified according to their final canakinumab dosing interval status at the last follow-up visit: (i) stable interval (no interval modification during follow-up), (ii) successful interval extension (a longer dosing interval than baseline maintained at last follow-up) and (iii) unsuccessful interval extension (interval extension attempted but not maintained, resulting in return to the baseline dosing interval at last follow-up). This classification was based on the final observed dosing interval rather than interim interval adjustments during follow-up. No standardized tapering protocol was applied. Successful interval extension was defined as maintenance of the extended interval for ≥2 consecutive doses without clinical relapse or elevation of inflammatory markers at last follow-up.

The primary outcome was treatment response, assessed by changes in annualized attack frequency and acute-phase reactants. Complete remission was defined as ≥6 months attack-free with normalization of inflammatory markers and no clinical disease activity; partial remission as ≥50% reduction in attack frequency with improvement in inflammatory markers; minimal disease activity as low attack burden with normal inflammatory markers outside attacks; and non-response as <50% reduction or persistent disease activity. Successful interval extension and treatment response categories were evaluated as distinct outcomes. While successful interval extension reflected the feasibility of maintaining a longer dosing interval without requiring interval re-shortening, complete remission represented a more stringent measure of disease control based on sustained absence of attacks and normalization of inflammatory markers.

Subclinical inflammation was defined as elevated inflammatory markers without clinical symptoms. Longitudinal changes in CRP values obtained during routine clinical assessments at canakinumab initiation and at ∼3, 6 and 12 months of follow-up were evaluated across dosing groups. These measurements were derived from retrospective medical records and were not restricted to attack-free visits. The secondary outcome was identification of factors associated with successful interval extension. Baseline characteristics, including age, sex, MEFV genotype, pre-treatment attack frequency, prior therapies and initial dosing interval, were compared between groups.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 26). Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov or Shapiro–Wilk tests and visual inspection. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± s.d. or median (interquartile range, IQR), as appropriate; categorical variables as frequencies (%). Comparisons were performed across dosing interval groups and according to interval extension outcome (successful vs. unsuccessful). Parametric (Student’s t test, one-way ANOVA) or non-parametric (Mann–Whitney U, Kruskal–Wallis) tests were applied as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using the χ2 or Fisher’s exact test. Variables with P < 0.10 in univariable analyses were entered into multivariable logistic regression to identify independent predictors. Model stability (events per variable) and multicollinearity were assessed. Given the small sample size, Firth’s penalized logistic regression was performed as a sensitivity analysis. CRP values were right-skewed and log-transformed for model-based analyses. Longitudinal changes were evaluated using the Friedman test with Bonferroni correction and a linear mixed-effects model with a first-order autoregressive [AR(1)] covariance structure, including time, group and their interaction as fixed effects and patient ID as a random intercept. Estimated marginal means with 95% confidence intervals were reported. A two-sided P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Artificial intelligence (Claude, Anthropic) was used solely for figure visualization; all analyses and interpretations were performed and verified by the authors.

Results

Baseline demographic, clinical and treatment characteristics of the study cohort

A total of 60 patients with colchicine-resistant FMF receiving canakinumab were included in the analysis. The median age at symptom onset was 24 (12–48) months, and the median age at diagnosis was 48 (27–72) months. Female patients constituted 53.3% of the cohort (n = 32). The most common MEFV genotype was M694V homozygosity, observed in 85% (n = 51) of patients. Before diagnosis, the median annual attack frequency was 12 (12–24) attacks per year. The most frequent initial clinical manifestations were fever (n = 54, 91.5%), abdominal pain (n = 52, 88.1%) and arthralgia (n = 35, 59.3%). Comparison of clinical and treatment characteristics across dosing interval groups

The study cohort (n = 60) was stratified into three groups based on dosing interval patterns: stable interval (group 1, n = 14), successful interval extension (group 2, n = 24) and unsuccessful interval extension (group 3, n = 22). Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were comparable across groups, with no significant differences observed in sex distribution, age at diagnosis, follow-up duration, family history, or MEFV genotype (all P > 0.05). However, the frequency of arthritis at disease onset differed significantly between groups (P = 0.008), driven primarily by a higher prevalence in the group 2 (Table 1). Pre-canakinumab treatment characteristics were comparable across dosing interval groups, with no significant differences observed between groups (Table 2). Among patients in whom interval extension was attempted, prior anakinra exposure was not associated with successful interval extension (50.0% vs. 45.5% in the successful and unsuccessful extension groups, respectively; χ² = 0.095, P = 0.758).

In contrast, significant differences emerged when canakinumab treatment characteristics and outcomes were compared (Table 3). Regarding initial dosing interval, a higher proportion of patients in the successful interval extension group (group 2) received canakinumab every 4 weeks (9/24, 37.5%), whereas most patients in the unsuccessful interval extension group (group 3) were treated every 8 weeks (21/22, 95.5%) (P = 0.026). Notably, all patients in group 3 (22/22, 100%) experienced attacks during canakinumab treatment, indicating a significantly higher rate of ongoing disease activity compared with the other groups (P = 0.029). Although not reaching statistical significance, a trend toward difference was observed for age at canakinumab initiation and total number of doses administered (P = 0.089 and P = 0.085, respectively). Importantly, canakinumab treatment duration differed significantly across groups (P = 0.048). Post hoc pairwise analysis revealed that this difference was driven by a significant difference between patients with stable dosing intervals and those with unsuccessful interval extension (group 1 vs. group 3, P = 0.013), whereas no significant differences were observed between the other group comparisons.

Longitudinal changes in CRP levels and comparison across interval strategies during canakinumab therapy

CRP levels changed significantly over time [Friedman χ²(3) = 10.6, P = 0.014], median CRP levels decreased markedly from 8.8 mg/l (IQR: 1.1–37) at baseline to 1.3 mg/l (IQR: 0.4–6.6) at month 3 and remained stable at month 6 [1.7 mg/l (IQR: 0.6–7.8)] and month 12 [1.1 mg/l (IQR: 0.4–4.5)]. Post hoc analysis confirmed that this change was driven by the baseline-to-month 3 reduction (P = 0.015), with no significant differences thereafter. These findings were consistent in linear mixed-effects modelling of log-transformed CRP, demonstrating a significant effect of time (P < 0.001), characterized by an early decline followed by a stable inflammatory profile. There was no significant effect of interval group on CRP levels (P = 0.657), and no time-by-group interaction (P = 0.904), indicating comparable CRP trajectories across dosing strategies (Fig. 1).

Clinical outcomes and predictors of successful interval extension

Among 60 patients, complete remission was achieved in 25 (41.6%), partial remission in 32 (53.3%) and 2 (3.3%) were non-responders. One patient (1.7%) had biopsy-confirmed amyloidosis that had been diagnosed before canakinumab initiation. Clinical outcomes differed significantly across interval groups (χ² = 15.8, Monte Carlo P = 0.005), driven by a lower rate of complete remission and a higher rate of partial remission in group 3 compared with Groups 1–2. Overall, 45.8% (n = 27) achieved low disease activity, with no significant difference across groups (P = 0.089), although rates were numerically lower in group 3 (Fig. 2A).

In univariable analyses, arthritis at disease onset (OR 9.50, 95% CI 1.80–50.07, P = 0.008) and longer initial dosing interval (OR 0.08, 95% CI 0.01–0.70, P = 0.022) were associated with successful interval extension. In multivariable analysis, arthritis at onset remained an independent predictor (OR 7.14, 95% CI 1.26–40.41, P = 0.026), while initial dosing interval showed a non-significant trend (P = 0.067). Findings were consistent in Firth’s penalized regression, with arthritis at onset retaining a borderline association (OR 5.51, 95% CI 1.08–28.23, P = 0.081) (Fig. 2B).

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the clinical and inflammatory trajectories of patients receiving canakinumab according to dosing interval strategies. Although CRP levels declined rapidly following treatment initiation and remained stable thereafter, the most clinically relevant finding was the marked reduction in disease burden, with the median annual attack rate decreasing from 7 (4–12) to 1.1 (0.5–2.1) attacks per patient-year. Inflammatory trajectories did not differ significantly across interval groups. Despite comparable CRP dynamics, clinically meaningful differences emerged between groups: patients with unsuccessful interval extension exhibited higher rates of ongoing disease activity and lower rates of complete remission. In contrast, successful interval extension was associated with more favourable clinical outcomes. Furthermore, baseline clinical characteristics appeared to influence the likelihood of successful interval extension, suggesting that patient phenotype may play a role in guiding treatment de-escalation decisions.

A particularly noteworthy observation was the association between the initial dosing interval and the likelihood of successful interval extension. In our cohort, a higher proportion of patients in the successful interval extension group had initiated canakinumab every 4 weeks, whereas nearly all patients in the unsuccessful group had started on an every-8-week schedule. This finding raises the hypothesis that achieving deeper and more sustained disease control through a more intensive initial dosing regimen may create a favourable immunological milieu for subsequent interval extension. Although causality cannot be established from our retrospective data, this observation suggests that the initial interval choice may not be inconsequential and warrants prospective evaluation.

One of the main findings of our study was the rapid decline in CRP levels after canakinumab initiation, with the largest reduction occurring between baseline and month 3, followed by relative stabilization through months 6 and 12. This pattern is biologically plausible and consistent with prior studies showing that canakinumab induces rapid suppression of IL-1-mediated inflammation in colchicine-resistant FMF and other autoinflammatory conditions [15, 16, 25–27]. In the pivotal CLUSTER program, canakinumab produced prompt control of inflammatory activity and maintained disease improvement over long-term follow-up, supporting the interpretation that the early CRP drop observed in our cohort reflects a true treatment response rather than random fluctuation [28]. Our longitudinal findings, therefore, reinforce the concept that the major anti-inflammatory benefit of canakinumab is achieved early, while later follow-up more likely reflects maintenance of remission than continued incremental improvement.

Our findings are in line with previous cohort studies showing that, despite rapid normalization of acute-phase reactants under canakinumab treatment, clinical relapses may still occur, particularly following dose interval extension or treatment withdrawal [29]. In the present study, however, we extend this observation by demonstrating a clear dissociation between biochemical and clinical outcomes across interval groups, with comparable CRP trajectories but significantly different remission rates. Together, these findings suggest that suppression of inflammatory markers alone may not adequately reflect disease control under IL-1 inhibition and highlight the need for a more comprehensive assessment integrating clinical and laboratory parameters.

The interval-extension analysis represents the most clinically actionable aspect of our study. Patients in the successful interval-extension group had more favourable outcomes, whereas all patients in the unsuccessful group experienced attacks during canakinumab treatment and had lower complete remission rates. This is broadly in line with emerging real-world paediatric FMF data showing that dose-interval extension is feasible in selected patients but not universally sustainable. In the PeRA experience, interval extension could be maintained in a substantial proportion of children, while other cohorts similarly showed that withdrawal or interval extension is achievable only in a subset of carefully selected patients [29, 30]. Taken together with our findings, these studies suggest that successful interval extension may serve as a surrogate of durable disease control, whereas failed extension likely identifies patients who still require tighter IL-1 blockade.

In this study, we identified a dissociation between inflammatory markers and clinical outcomes in canakinumab-treated FMF patients. Despite similar CRP trajectories, clinical responses differed across dosing strategies, potentially reflecting ongoing subclinical inflammation. Given the established role of persistent inflammation in long-term complications such as amyloidosis, these findings highlight the limitations of relying solely on biochemical markers to guide treatment decisions. Recent follow-up studies of canakinumab interval extension have explicitly shown that some patients required shortening of the interval not only because of clinical attacks but also because of subclinical inflammatory activity detected during apparently quiescent periods [31, 32]. Our data are compatible with this concept: patients in the unsuccessful interval-extension group may have harboured persistent disease activity that was not fully reflected by serial CRP alone but became clinically evident when treatment spacing was attempted. This interpretation supports careful longitudinal monitoring and suggests that interval extension decisions should consider the possibility of residual inflammation even when conventional biomarkers appear reassuring.

Another notable finding was that arthritis was independently associated with successful interval extension. This association remained directionally consistent in Firth’s penalized regression, although with borderline statistical significance (OR = 5.51, 95% CI 1.08–28.23, P = 0.081). Despite the limited sample size, the concordance across models supports the robustness of this finding. This observation suggests that clinical phenotype may influence the longitudinal response to IL-1 blockade and the feasibility of treatment de-escalation, although confirmation in larger studies is warranted. Although the biological basis of this association remains uncertain, arthritis may identify a subgroup with a more IL-1-driven inflammatory phenotype, resulting in a more sustained response to IL-1 blockade and facilitating successful interval extension. To our knowledge, this association has not been previously reported in FMF, and should, therefore, be considered hypothesis-generating. Current tapering literature in FMF has focused more on treatment protocols and relapse frequency than on robust baseline predictors of successful dose extension, so our finding may add a novel clinically relevant dimension [33]. Future multicentre studies with larger cohorts will be needed to validate whether arthritis truly predicts a more favourable de-escalation profile or whether this association reflects cohort-specific characteristics.

It is also notable that the two non-responders and the single patient with biopsy-confirmed amyloidosis diagnosed prior to canakinumab initiation were all observed in the stable interval group (group 1). Although the small numbers preclude definitive conclusions, this paradoxical distribution may reflect a clinically heterogeneous subgroup: patients who neither required nor tolerated interval adjustment may have represented cases with persistent subclinical disease activity that was insufficiently controlled at the stable dosing interval. This interpretation is consistent with the known risk of amyloidosis in FMF patients with ongoing inflammation despite treatment and underscores the importance of vigilant monitoring even in patients who do not undergo dose modifications.

This study has several notable strengths. It integrates comprehensive clinical and inflammatory assessments under canakinumab therapy, enabling evaluation of the relationship between biochemical response and real-world disease control. The longitudinal design with repeated measurements allowed characterization of CRP trajectories, demonstrating a rapid early decline followed by stabilization. Clinically relevant stratification based on interval-adjustment strategies provided a novel and underexplored perspective on IL-1 blockade, revealing discordance between inflammatory markers and clinical outcomes. The use of mixed-effects modelling and Firth’s penalized logistic regression further strengthens the analysis by appropriately addressing repeated measures and small-sample bias. Finally, the identification of arthritis as a potential predictor of successful interval extension offers a novel insight for individualized treatment approaches.

Several limitations should be considered. The retrospective single-centre design limits generalizability and introduces potential selection bias. Subgroup analyses were based on relatively small sample sizes, reducing statistical power and yielding wide confidence intervals. The reliance on CRP as the primary biomarker, without additional markers such as SAA, may have limited the detection of subclinical inflammation. Treatment decisions were not protocolized, introducing potential confounding related to clinical practice. In addition, the predominance of M694V homozygous patients may restrict applicability to broader FMF populations.

In conclusion, canakinumab induces rapid and sustained suppression of inflammation; however, normalization of CRP does not equate to optimal disease control. Despite similar inflammatory trajectories, clinically meaningful differences across interval strategies underscore a dissociation between biochemical response and clinical outcomes. Successful interval extension may serve as a marker of durable remission, whereas failure to extend intervals likely reflects persistent disease activity despite apparent biochemical improvement. These findings emphasize the need for a clinically integrated, individualized approach to treatment optimization under IL-1 inhibition.
