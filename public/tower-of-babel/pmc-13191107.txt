The Global Burden of Schizophrenia: Findings from the 2021 Global Burden of Diseases, Injuries, and Risk Factors Study
Authors: GBD 2021 Schizophrenia Collaborators
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and HypothesisWe utilized data from the 2021 Global Burden of Disease (GBD) Study to (i) provide estimates of the incidence, prevalence, and illness-related burden associated with schizophrenia; (2) examine how these rates have changed from 1990 to 2021; and (3) explore the association of these rates with several country-level variables (eg, access/quality of healthcare systems).Study DesignThe GBD study provides estimates for the incidence, prevalence, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) associated with 371 illness, injuries, and risk factors by age and sex across 204 countries and territories. We examined data from the GBD 2021 study to describe the global epidemiology of schizophrenia as well as the association between metrics of schizophrenia epidemiology and country-level variables previously associated with these metrics.Study ResultsIn 2021, the global age-standardized incidence, prevalence, and DALY rates were 15.4, 277.7, and 177.8 per 100 000 individuals, respectively. Between 1990 and 2021, global incidence rates (both age-standardized and crude) declined, whereas crude prevalence and DALY rates per 100 000 increased and age-standardized prevalence and DALYs remained unchanged. However, these findings varied by age, gender, and geography. Changes in country-level variables between 1990-2021 (eg, alcohol use and per capita income) were associated with changes in incidence, prevalence, and DALY rates over this same time period.ConclusionsSchizophrenia remains a significant health concern worldwide. Our results highlight the need for health systems to prepare to address the growing prevalence of schizophrenia which may be greatest among older adults, and especially older women.Keywords: schizophrenia, epidemiology, global health

Introduction

The Global Burden of Diseases, Injuries, and Risk Factors (GBD) Study represents the largest international effort to quantify the morbidity and mortality associated with negative health states worldwide. Comprised of an international collaboration of over 12 000 researchers, the GBD Study provides valuable data with regard to the global incidence, prevalence, and illness-related burden of schizophrenia that can guide policy makers in effectively allocating resources for mental health services. Previous analyses of GBD data have highlighted the global public health challenge of schizophrenia. For example, although schizophrenia was identified as the ninth most prevalent mental disorder among the 10 mental disorders examined in the 2019 iteration of the GBD study, it was the third leading cause of disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), trailing only anxiety and depressive disorders—mental disorders with a worldwide prevalence estimated to be ~13× and ~12× greater, respectively, as compared to schizophrenia.1

Although estimates of the global and/or regional burden of schizophrenia have been published using 2010,2 2016,3 2017,4 and 2019 GDB data,1,5,6 this article represents an important expansion upon these previous manuscripts for several reasons. First, all previous GBD schizophrenia publications were completed prior to the COVID-19 pandemic; thus the current article provides the first data with regard to the global epidemiology of schizophrenia before and after the COVID-19 pandemic. Second, estimates of illness-related burden develop for past GBD schizophrenia manuscripts have not included burden associated with the premature mortality rates commonly experienced by individuals with schizophrenia due to factors such as suicide and cardiovascular disease.1 Our article represents the first attempt to estimate the full effect of this premature mortality on GBD illness-related burden estimates for schizophrenia.

To facilitate global estimation of the epidemiology of illnesses and injuries, the GBD study utilizes a series of longitudinally-tracked, country-level covariates that are positively or negatively associated with illness/injuries within their modeling process.7 For example, with regard to schizophrenia specifically, the GBD 2021 study utilizes the covariate of lag distributed income to facilitate location-level estimates of schizophrenia prevalence.8 However, among the remaining GBD covariates are several variables previously suggested to be associated with the incidence, prevalence, and/or illness-related burden of schizophrenia—see Table 1. Of note, not all putative variables associated with schizophrenia epidemiology are included in the GBD covariates database (eg, cannabis use9). Our study is the first to examine the association of these variables and GBD estimates of schizophrenia epidemiology and is the most comprehensive global analysis of the association between population-level variables and epidemiological metrics of schizophrenia completed to date. Moreover, our analyses represent several important advancements in the study of schizophrenia epidemiology. First, while most research on schizophrenia epidemiology to date relies on cross-sectional analyses of epidemiological metrics (eg, incidence and prevalence) and putative risk factors, our study examines longitudinal associations between changes in schizophrenia epidemiology and changes in country-level risk factors from 1990 to 2021. Second, our analyses are the first to examine associations between schizophrenia epidemiology and risk factors across all countries worldwide; prior research has relied on examining such associations only within a single country or across a subset of countries. Third, to the best of our knowledge, our study simultaneously examines the largest number of putative risk factors for schizophrenia epidemiology in a single study to date. Finally, as growing research has highlighted that schizophrenia epidemiology is driven by the interaction of multiple variables, our study employs multivariate analyses to unpack the interplay of multiple factors on longitudinal changes in metrics of schizophrenia epidemiology.

Thus, the goal of the current study is to examine global incidence, prevalence, and illness-related burden associated with schizophrenia using data from the GBD 2021 study and examine whether these rates have changed over time. To contextualize these findings, we also examine the relationships between these rates and several country-level variables previously suggested to be associated with the epidemiology of schizophrenia.

Methods

The GBD Study provides estimates and 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs) for the incidence (ie, number of people who experience a first onset of schizophrenia in a given year), prevalence (ie, total number of people living with schizophrenia in given year), and DALYs associated with 371 illness, injuries, and risk factors by age and sex across 204 countries and territories that are groups into 21 regions (see Table S1). Data with regard to schizophrenia incidence and prevalence were obtained via a structured review of peer reviewed-publications, grey literature, and expert consultation with the majority of sources identifying schizophrenia based on DSM-IV-TR codes 295.10-295.30, 295.60, and 295.90 and ICD-10 code F20. Included sources were published no earlier than January 1, 1980, written in any language, provided sufficient information to assess the quality of study methods and sample characteristics, and included a sample representative of the general population. Final estimates of incidence and prevalence were generated using DisMod-MR 2.1—a Bayesian meta-regression tool that calculates internally consistent estimates of incidence and prevalence by age, location, sex, and year. Additional details with regard to the methodology used to develop these estimates are available elsewhere.8,10

Illness-related burden is quantified using DALYs— a well-established health metric designed to quantify the burden and loss of life associated with a given illness or injury calculated by summing the Years of Life Lost (YLLs: number of years of life lost due to premature death from an illness/injury) and Years Lived with Disability (YLDs: healthy years of life lost due to living with an illness/injury). To facilitate consistency in the calculation of YLLs within the GBD study, YLLs are only assigned to illnesses/injuries included in the ICD causes of death codes. As schizophrenia is not included among these codes, YLLs for individuals with schizophrenia are always zero in GBD calculations, and DALYs are calculated based solely on YLD estimates. Thus, although DALYs do not capture the premature mortality experienced by individuals with schizophrenia due to factors such as elevated rates of death from suicide,11 we utilize this metric to facilitate comparison with results from past iterations of the GBD study that also report DALYs.5,12 However, to address the limitation of this measure in fully capturing the premature mortality associated with schizophrenia, we completed additional analyses to estimate the increase in DALYs associated with schizophrenia if YLLs were included in the calculation of this metric.

For incidence, prevalence, and DALYs, we present both age-standardized and crude rates per 100 000 with 95% UIs. Crude rates represent the actual number of new emergent cases of schizophrenia in a given year (crude incidence), the total number of individuals living with schizophrenia in a given year (crude prevalence), and the total number of DALYs for all individuals living with schizophrenia in a given year (crude DALYs), respectively. Age-standardized rates, calculated using population estimates for the 2021 GBD study, are standardized versions of crude rates that control for the age distribution of the population under study. Such values allow for comparison of epidemiological metrics (eg, incidence, prevalence, and DALY) across time or geographical regions that are not affected by differences/changes in the underlying age structure of the populations being compared.13 Such adjustment is particularly important when comparing temporal changes or regional differences in the epidemiology of illnesses such as schizophrenia for which the first onset of symptoms typically occurs during a specific age range (ie, late teens to early 20s). For example, temporal changes in crude incidence rates for schizophrenia could stem from (1) true changes in incidence rates over time; (2) temporal changes in the age composition of the population (eg, increases/decreases in the percentage of the total population who are at greatest risk for the onset of schizophrenia—people in their late teens to early 20s), or (3) both. However, age-standardized metrics have been criticized for obscuring the true magnitude of health concerns (eg, not reflecting the actual incidence of a disease in a community) that may be better evidenced by crude rates, thereby hindering local efforts to prioritize and address health concerns.14 Consequently, we present both crude and age-standardized metrics in our results.

Statistical Analyses

Descriptive details for the incidence, prevalence, and DALYs associated with schizophrenia are presented for 2021 with results stratified by age, sex, and location. To compare sex differences in 2021 incidence, prevalence, and DALYs, we employed statistical simulation to generate 10 000 estimates of the incidence, prevalence, and DALYs for men and women in 2021. These estimates were obtained from a statistical distribution modeled to have a similar mean and 95% UI as GBD 2021 data for these epidemiological metrics. Between-group mean differences (Mdiff) and 95% percentile-based boot-strapped confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated based on 10 000 samples drawn with replacement from these simulated data. CIs that do not contain zero were considered to be evidence of a statistically significant mean difference. Temporal changes in incidence, prevalence, and DALYs from 1990 to 2021 were evaluated by determining the percent change in these values from 1990 to 2021. Percent changes with 95% UIs that do not contain zero were considered to be statistically significant changes.

To address the limitation that DALYs do not capture the premature mortality associated with schizophrenia, we complete additional analyses to estimate the increase in DALYs associated with schizophrenia if YLLs were included DALY calculations. For these analyses, we completed a simulation analysis in which we used GBD 2021 population and mortality data15,16 and recent meta-analytic data with regard to increased mortality risk11 and average number of reduced years of life among individuals with schizophrenia17 to generate 1 000 000 estimates of crude global YLL rates to capture the premature mortality typically experienced by individuals with schizophrenia. Consistent with GBD methodology, DALYs were calculated by summing YLLs and YLDs. To differentiate these values from the standard DALYs reported within the GBD study, we will refer to these values as DALY+s. Of note, as individuals who die in a given year contribute to population-level YLLs but not YLDs, prior to calculating DALY+s, we removed YLDs that would have been assigned to individuals with schizophrenia who we estimated to have died in 2021. YLDs removed for these individuals were estimated by multiplying the number of individuals with schizophrenia estimated to have died in 2021 within our simulation by the appropriate GBD disability weight for schizophrenia. Consistent with GBD methodology, 63% of these individuals were assigned the disability weight associated with the acute state of schizophrenia, and 37% were assigned the disability weight associated with the residual state of schizophrenia.8 As the GBD modeling process assumes that no incident cases of schizophrenia occur before age 10 or at age 80 or greater,8 we applied a similar restriction in our simulation analyses.

To examine the association between GBD covariates and country-level changes in schizophrenia incidence, prevalence, and DALYS from 1990 to 2021, we calculated change scores for each covariate by subtracting the 1990 covariate estimate from the 2021 estimate. Thus, positive values of these change scores reflect an increase in the covariate from 1990 to 2021, and negative values reflect a decrease in the covariate from 1990 to 2021. The one exception was the covariate of country latitude as this value is constant over time. Thus, latitude was examined using the 2021 GBD estimate. Robust regression was used to compare percent changes from 1990 to 2021 in incidence, prevalence, DALYs to change scores for GBD covariates. More specifically, each covariate change score was first examined as a univariate predictor in separate robust regression equations for incidence, prevalence, and DALYs, respectively. All covariates with univariate associations with P < .10 with a specific epidemiological variable (ie, incidence, prevalence, or DALYs) were then included in a multivariate robust regression analysis for that specific epidemiological variable.

Results

Schizophrenia Incidence 2021

In 2021, approximately 1.2 million individuals worldwide (~0.015% of the global population) experienced an incident episode of schizophrenia (95% UI: 1 008 000-1 473 000). The age-standardized incidence rate for schizophrenia worldwide was 15.4 per 100 000 individuals (95% UI = 12.7-18.6), and the crude was 15.5 per 100 000 individuals (95% UI = 12.8-18.7). Age-standardized [men: 16.4 per 100 000 (95% UI = 13.6-19.8); women: 14.4 per 100 000 (95% UI = 11.9-17.4)] and crude incidence rates [men: 16.7 per 100 000 (95% UI = 13.8-20.0); women: 14.3 per 100 000 (95% UI = 11.8-17.3)] were higher among men than women (age-standardized Mdiff = 2.07; 95% CI, 2.03-2.11; crude Mdiff = 2.29; 95% CI, 2.25-2.33). When examining age-specific crude incidence rates, rates were numerically higher for men than women for all age ranges, and incidence rates were highest among individuals ages 20-24 years for both sexes as opposed to all other age ranges—see Figure 1. Regional and country-level incidence rates are included in Table S2.

Trends in Schizophrenia Incidence 1990-2021

Between 1990 and 2021, the annual incidence rate of schizophrenia declined worldwide by 1.3% for age-standardized rates (95% UI = -2.6% to −0.2%) and 6.4% for crude rates (95% UI = −9.6% to −3.4%)—see Figure 2. However, there is considerable geographic variability with regard to this finding—see Table S2. For example, age-standardized incidence rates decreased by 4.2% in High-Income North America between 1990 and 2021 (95% UI = −5.8% to −2.6%) and increased in Eastern Europe by 5.0% (95% UI = 3.4%-6.5%) with no statistically significant change in other regions.

Schizophrenia Prevalence 2021

In 2021, there were approximately 23.2 million individuals worldwide living with schizophrenia (~0.3% of the global population). The worldwide age-standardized prevalence rate was 277.7 per 100 000 (95% UI = 229.8-329.1), and the crude rate was 293.8 per 100 000 individuals (95% UI = 243.4-347.5). Crude prevalence rates were highest among individuals ages 35-39 for both sexes as compared to all other age groups. Similar to the incidence rate, global age-standardized [men: 291.6 per 100 000 (95% UI = 241.6-345.0); women: 280.5 per 100 000 (95% UI = 232.3-331.0)] and crude prevalence rates [men: 306.9 per 100 000 (95% UI = 254.3-362.9); women: 263.6 per 100 000 (95% UI = 217.7-311.9)] were higher among men than women (age-standardized Mdiff = 17.8 per 100 000; 95% CI, 17.2-18.4; crude Mdiff = 26.4 per 100 000; 95% CI, 25.6-27.1). However, this finding was not true for all age groups; for individuals 80 and older, the crude prevalence rate was greater among women than men (Mdiff = −5.7 per 100 000; 95% CI, −6.0 to −5.3)—see Figure 1. Regional and country-level prevalence rates are included in Table S3.

Trends in Schizophrenia Prevalence 1990-2021

Between 1990-2021, there was a statistically significant increase in crude prevalence rates (15.0%; 95% UI = 12.6%-17.3%) whereas there was not a statistically significant change in age-standardized rates—see Figure 2. This finding varied by age group, with crude prevalence rates declining among individuals ages 15-24 and increasing among individuals ages 50 and older (all 95% UI do not contain zero). There is also considerable geographic variability with regard to this finding—see Table S3. For example, among GBD regions, age-standardized prevalence rates decreased in High-Income North America and increased in Western Sub-Saharan Africa, Central Europe, South Asia, Southeast Asia, East Asia, and Eastern Europe with no statistically significant changes in other GBD regions.

Schizophrenia DALYs 2021

In 2021, over 14 816 000 DALYs were attributable to schizophrenia worldwide. The age-standardized DALY rate was 177.8 per 100 000 (95% UI = 131.5-228.8), and the crude rate was 187.8 per 100 000 (95% UI = 138.5-242.0). The relationship between DALYs, age, and sex mirrored the relationship between schizophrenia prevalence, age, and sex (see Figure 1). Global age-standardized [men: 189.0 per 100 000 (95% UI = 139.9-243.7); women: 166.4 per 100 000 (95% UI = 122.8-213.9)] and crude prevalence rates [men: 198.8 per 100 000 (95% UI = 147.0-256.7); women: 176.6 per 100 000 (95% UI = 129.8-227.4)] were higher among men than women (age-standardized Mdiff = 23.7 per 100 000; 95% CI, 23.0-23.4; crude Mdiff = 20.9 per 100 000; 95% CI, 20.4-21.5). However, this finding was not true for all age groups; for individuals 80 and older, crude DALY rates were greater among women than men (Mdiff = −1.5 per 100 000; 95% CI, −1.7 to −1.2)—see Figure 1. Crude DALYs per 100 000 were greatest among both men and women for the age group of 35-39 years as compared to all other age groups. Regional and country-level DALYs are included in Table S4.

Trends in Schizophrenia DALYs 1990-2021

The change in DALYs from 1990 to 2021 also mirrored findings with regard to changes in illness prevalence during this time period. Between 1990 and 2021, there was a statistically significant increase in crude DALY rates (14.3%; 95% UI = 11.6%-16.8%) whereas there was not a statistically significant change in age-standardized rates—see Figure 2. This finding varied by age group, with crude incidence rates declining among individuals ages 20-24 and increasing among individuals ages 50 and older (all 95% UI do not contain zero). There is also considerable geographic variability with regard to this finding—see Table S4. For example, among GBD regions, age-standardized prevalence rates decreased in High-Income North America and increased in Western Sub-Saharan Africa, Central Europe, South Asia, Southeast Asia, East Asia, and Eastern Europe with no statistically significant changes in other GBD regions.

Estimated DALYs that Capture the Premature Mortality Associated with Schizophrenia

During 2021, we estimated that there were approximately 6 076 000 YLLs (95% UI = 4 895 000-7 433 000) experienced by individuals with schizophrenia worldwide due to premature mortality. Using this value, global DALY+s for individuals with schizophrenia were approximately 20 558 000 (95% UI = 16 565 000-25 085 000)—a value 39% greater than GBD 2021 DALY estimates for schizophrenia.

GBD 2021 Covariates

Univariate analyses revealed associations between changes in incidence rates from 1990 to 2021 and changes in several GBD 2021 covariates from 1990 to 2021: HAQIs, number of employed healthcare workers per 10 000, air pollution, alcohol use, receipt of antenatal care, per capita healthcare expenditures, negative life events, nicotine use, per capita income, and urbanicity. Country-level latitude was also associated with country-level changes in the incidence of schizophrenia from 1990 to 2021. Results of the multivariate analyses are summarized in Table 2. After controlling for all covariates found to be associated with changes in incidence rates from 1990 to 2021, increases in country-level incidence rates were associated with increases from 1990 to 2021 in country-level alcohol use, HAQIs, healthcare expenditures per capita, and increased per capita income. Conversely, increases in country level incidence rates were associated with decreases in (1) air pollution, (2) percentage of women receiving any antenatal care, and (3) urbanicity. With regard to latitude, countries farther from the equator experienced greater decreases in incidence rates from 1990 to 2021.

Discussion

In 2021, over 1.2 million individuals experienced a first episode of schizophrenia, and over 23 million individuals were living with schizophrenia worldwide, who, collectively, experienced nearly 15 million years of lost quality life (ie, DALYs) due to this illness. Consistent with past research, our results suggest that the global incidence rate of schizophrenia is higher among men than women,18,19 peaks between ages 20 and 24,4 and that age-standardized incidence rates have continued to decline worldwide since 1990.5 However, our results suggest important caveats to some of these findings. For example, although global age-standardized incidence rates declined between 1990 and 2021, there is considerable age and geographical variability in this finding. With regard to the former, crude incidence rates decreased between 1990 and 2021 but primarily among younger (15-24) and older (55 and older) individuals. Likewise, age-standardized incidence rates remained unchanged in most countries, with increases (n = 3) and decreases (n = 2) occurring in relatively few countries. Similar geographic variability was evidence when considering changes in crude incidence rates since 1990, with 72 countries experiencing no change, 68 experiencing a decrease, and 64 experiencing an increase. In total, these data suggest that global-level changes in age-standardized and crude incidence rates should be interpreted cautiously as they may mask demographic and geographical variability. A case in point may be Denmark, which is noteworthy in our data for experiencing relatively large increases in age-standardized and crude incidence rates of schizophrenia from 1990 to 2021 despite concurrent global decreases in these same metrics. Future research may wish to explore specific country-level factors that may have contributed to this, such as the robust literature of schizophrenia incidence in Denmark20–24 (which could facilitate improved incidence estimates) or Denmark’s longstanding focus on specialized care for individuals with first-episode psychosis25–29 (which could facilitate increased detection of individuals with incident episodes of schizophrenia).

Prevalence and DALY rates demonstrated similar trends within our study. Crude prevalence and DALY rates increased globally from 1990 to 2021, whereas age-standardized rates remained stable, suggesting that increases in crude prevalence and DALYs were driven primarily by increases in global population within this time frame.15 Consistent with past GBD analyses,3,5 we found a demographic shift in crude prevalence and DALY rates with rates increasing between 1990 and 2021 among older individuals (ie, individuals 50 and older). Given the stability of age-standardized prevalence and DALYs during this same period, these increases in crude rates likely stem from a growing population of older adults with schizophrenia.3 More specifically, although individuals with schizophrenia, on average, die earlier than individuals without schizophrenia,30,31 the average lifespan of individuals with schizophrenia may still be increasing over time resulting in a shifting demography of schizophrenia such more individuals are living into their later years despite having this illness.30–32 Moreover, although 2021 crude prevalence and DALY rates were greater among men than women, this pattern reversed among individuals 80 and older where crude prevalence and DALY rates were greater among women than men. Unfortunately, healthcare systems are often poorly equipped to meet the unique needs of older adults (in particular older women) with schizophrenia.33–38 Consequently, our findings suggest that developing specialized care programs for older adults with schizophrenia, and, in particular, older women with schizophrenia, may be an important healthcare focus worldwide.34,39,40 Building such a healthcare infrastructure will require not only improved access to mental health services but also strategies to address common challenges experienced by older adults (eg, multiple medical comorbidities,41,42 complex medication regimes,43,44 social isolation and loneliness,45,46 and economic and technological barriers to accessing care47,48) with special attention directed to the needs of older women specifically (eg, an increased risk for cardiovascular disease and osteoporosis relative to older men49,50).

The inability of DALYs as calculated within current GBD methodology to capture the premature mortality commonly experienced by individuals with schizophrenia has been noted as a key limitation in past GBD reports.1 Our study attempts to rectify this limitation by providing global estimates of YLLs and DALY+s associated with schizophrenia (ie, an adjusted value of GBD 2021 DALYs that accounts for premature mortality among individuals with schizophrenia). Our results suggest that in 2021 there were over six million YLLs experienced by individuals with schizophrenia worldwide due to premature mortality. Using this value, individuals with schizophrenia were estimated to have experienced over 20.5 million DALY+s in 2021—a value 39% greater than GBD 2021 DALY estimates for schizophrenia. Although these estimates suggest that the YLLs associated with schizophrenia are substantial, our estimates should likely be considered an estimate of the “upper range” of this metric as our modeling relied on an estimated relative of risk of all-cause mortality among individuals with schizophrenia that may capture deaths both related (eg, suicide) and unrelated to schizophrenia (eg, accidents). Ultimately, though, these analyses highlight the need for advancements in GBD methodology to capture the contribution of schizophrenia on premature mortality, and efforts are currently underway to account for suicide-related YLLs in future GBD estimates of mental illness epidemiology.1

To contextualize our findings with regard to incidence, prevalence, and DALYs, we examined the association of these variables with several country-level covariates collected as part of the GBD study. Our analyses advance past research on factors that are associated with the epidemiology of schizophrenia by examining such factors simultaneously so as clarify the association of each factor on incidence, prevalence, or DALYs after parsing out the effect of other factors included in the model. In our multivariate analyses, country-levels increases in age-standardized incidence rates from 1990 to 2021 were associated with increases in country-level alcohol use, HAQIs, healthcare expenditures and decreases in air pollution, percentage of women who received any antenatal care, and urbanicity over this same time period. Likewise, increases in prevalence and DALY rates between 1990 and 2021 were associated with increases in country-level alcohol use, per capita income, SDI and decreases in country level air pollution during this same time period. Although these associations should be interpreted cautiously due to the potential of an “ecological fallacy” (ie, using associations derived from group-level analyses to make inferences/predictions about individual-level outcomes51), several of our findings comport with well-replicated results in the schizophrenia literature (eg, the associations between incidence and alcohol use, HAQIs, and the receipt of antenatal care52–54). Future research may benefit from continued exploration of whether these variables may exert a causal influence on the epidemiology of schizophrenia. For example, increases in healthcare access and quality (assessed using HAQI), per capita healthcare expenditures, and percentage of women receiving any prenatal care were associated with decreases in country-level incidence rates. Such findings may reflect the ability of well-resourced, high quality, easily accessible healthcare systems to better mitigate factors that may contribute to risk of developing schizophrenia (eg, prenatal exposure to viral infection55,56). Likewise, the association between increasing prevalence rates with increasing per capita income may reflect the benefit of greater personal financial resources on reducing premature mortality and extending the lifespan for individuals with schizophrenia.

We found unexpected results with regard to the association of air pollution, urbanicity, and latitude and metrics of schizophrenia epidemiology. For example, although we found negative associations between country-level changes in these variables from 1990 to 2021 and changes in country-level schizophrenia incidence from 1990 to 2021, both air pollution, and urbanicity have been found to be positively associated with the incidence of schizophrenia in past studies.30,57,58 Several factors may account for these inconsistent findings. With regard to air pollution, our finding may stem from the metric of air pollution included in the GBD study (ie, population-weighted mean of outdoor particulate matter 2.5 μg/m3) which unlike other metrics of air pollution (eg, nitrogen dioxide and nitrogen oxides) shows inconsistent associations with schizophrenia incidence.57–59 Additionally, past studies of air pollution and schizophrenia incidence have come largely from China and high-income countries; our finding of a negative association between changes in these variables from 1990 to 2021 worldwide raises questions about whether past result may stem from unique features of the countries where the research was conducted that may not apply to other geographic locations. Similarly, the well-replicated positive association between urbanicity and schizophrenia incidence rates come primarily from research from a small number of high-income countries—data from studies conducted in low- and middle-income countries, which compromise 80% of the world population, have not found a similar association.60 For example, consistent with our results, a recent study in Brazil found a negative association between population density and schizophrenia incidence rates.61 With regard to latitude, previous research has demonstrated that countries farther from the equator experience greater prevalence rates of schizophrenia but no difference in incidence rates as compared to countries closer to the equator.30 However, these associations are highly nuanced as multiple variables have been suggested to moderate these relationships (eg, season of birth62 and sex63). Our results expand upon these findings by highlighting the longitudinal nature of the association between latitude and schizophrenia epidemiology. Specifically, we found that countries farther from the equator experienced a greater decline in incidence rates of schizophrenia from 1990 to 2021 than countries closer to the equator. This finding challenges previous hypotheses that the risk factors for schizophrenia risk present in higher latitude environments may overwhelm the protective factors typically present in these same environments (eg, access to better healthcare systems).64 In total, our findings with regard to air pollution, urbanicity, and latitude challenge and expand upon previous research findings and may contribute to efforts to shift from dogmatic to data-driven understandings of the “rich contours” of schizophrenia epidemiology.65,66

Of note, the current study did suffer from several limitations. For instance, although resources are improving, high quality epidemiological data on schizophrenia are limited in many countries, especially low- and middle-income countries, which may hinder accurate estimation of incidence, prevalence, and DALYs within the GBD study.3 Global investment in establishing surveillance systems in underrepresented countries, especially low- and middle-income countries, would assist in developing more accurate future estimates of the global epidemiology of schizophrenia. Likewise, future iterations of the GBD study would benefit from the incorporation of methodological strategies that better reflect the contribution of schizophrenia to premature mortality.

Schizophrenia remains a considerable health challenge worldwide. Although it is encouraging that incidence rates are declining globally, crude prevalences and DALY rates have increased since 1990-2021. Investing in the development and sustainability of high quality, easily accessible, and well-resourced healthcare systems may be critical in promoting the continued reduction of the incidence of schizophrenia and supporting the health needs of the growing number of individuals living with this illness. As the population of older adults with schizophrenia grows, including women with schizophrenia specifically, developing effective interventions and systems of care that address the unique and unmet needs of this population67,68 may be a key quality improvement target worldwide.
