SF3B6 promotes the oncogenic phenotypes of MDA-MB-231 cells by extensively interacting with and regulating transcripts expression and alternative splicing
Authors: Chunxiu Yuan, Yuanyuan Gao, Ying Huang, Yanjiao Zhao, Xuan Li, Nan Wang, Sheng Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SF3B6 functions as a splicing factor, and is closely associated with the malignant progression of multiple cancer types, including breast cancer. However, its underlying mechanism is largely unknown in breast tumor cells. In this study, we investigated the molecular mechanism and downstream targets of SF3B6 by transfecting the siRNA of SF3B6 (siSF3B6) into MDA-MB-231 cells and combining with high-throughput transcriptome sequencing (RNA-seq) and improved RNA immunoprecipitation sequencing (iRIP-seq) technology. We found siSF3B6 significantly repressed cellular proliferation and migration levels, but increased apoptosis levels of MDA-MB-231 cells. Downstream RNA-seq analysis revealed that SF3B6 silencing resulted in widespread changes in differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and regulatory alternative splicing events (RASEs) that were involved in inflammatory response and immune regulatory pathways, such as the NF-κB signaling pathway. The iRIP-seq analysis indicated that SF3B6 can regulate the gene expression and alternative splicing profile by directly interacting with its targeting mRNAs. Further analysis showed that SF3B6 potentially contributes to the malignant properties of TNBC by regulating the expression and splicing of key oncogenes, including PPM1F and FASN, and tumor suppressor genes, including RLF and RECQL4, which were validated by RT-qPCR experiment. In summary, this study highlights the crucial molecular role of SF3B6 in breast cancer cells, providing new insights into its potential as a biomarker or valuable target in breast cancer diagnosis and treatment in future.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23477-y.

Background

Breast cancer, including triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) as one serious type, is a heterogeneous group of tumors that includes multiple types. For TNBC, it is characterized by the absence of overexpression of estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) genes1. TNBC accounts for 15–20% of all breast cancer cases, and is the most aggressive breast cancer type. Compared with other breast cancer subtypes, TNBC carries the worst prognosis, and is sensitive to chemotherapy. And although its benefits are limited, the chemotherapy remains the standard treatment2. Meanwhile, TNBC treatment is challenging due to its heterogeneity, high invasiveness, and relatively poor treatment response3. For the underlying mechanisms, multiple pathways were involved in TNBC progression, including Notch signaling pathway, Wnt/β-catenin pathway, TGF-β signaling pathway, Hedgehog signaling pathway, PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, and pathways associated with epidermal growth factor receptor4. At the molecular level, it is reported that the alternative splicing (AS) pattern of genes can predict the prognosis and immune microenvironment signatures in TNBC5. While the molecular functions and regulatory factors of AS need to be further investigated in TNBC.

Among the AS regulation, splicing factors play the essential role6. The dysregulation of splicing factors can mediate the aberrant AS profile, which will further impact numerous human diseases, including cancers7. Through RNA interference screen by targeting the splicing factors, nine splicing factors were identified and can significantly modulate proliferation of TNBC cell lines by deregulation of sister chromatid cohesion8. Among the splicing factors, we focused on the splicing factor 3b subunit 6 (SF3B6), which is also known as SF3B14 and is an intrinsic component of functional U2 small nuclear ribonucleoprotein9. SF3B6 extensively participates in the AS regulation, thus involves in multiple biological processes10,11. In cancers, it has been demonstrated that SF3B6 promoted proliferation and migration of gastric cancer cells through MAPK signaling pathway12. Genome-wide RNAi screen for p53 activity identified seven splicing factors, including SF3B6, as the inhibitor of P53 activity in multiple non-small cell lung cancer cell lines, indicating the potential therapeutic role of these splicing factors13. In breast cancer cell lines, Gene co-expression network analysis also identified SF3B6 as one of the hub genes that may play a key role in the development and progression of breast cancer14. However, the molecular targets and functional pathways of SF3B6 remain largely unknown in breast cancer.

In this study, we speculate that SF3B6 can possibly promotes the malignant progression of TNBC by binding and regulating the expression or alternative splicing (AS) of genes related to the occurrence and development of TNBC. Thus, we extensively investigated the functions of SF3B6 in the TNBC MDA-MB-231 cell line by silencing its expression through the small interference RNA (siRNA) method. Then we explored its influence on the cell phenotypes, including apoptosis, proliferation, and migration. The transcriptome sequencing data (RNA-seq) of SF3B6 knockdown (siSF3B6) were obtained. In addition, potential target transcripts bound by SF3B6 were detected by improved RNA immunoprecipitation and sequencing (iRIP-seq) technology, Subsequently, the molecular mechanism of SF3B6 regulating the expression and alternative splicing of related genes in TNBC was analyzed. In summary, our results demonstrated that SF3B6 has a profound impact on the oncogenic phenotypes of MDA-MB-231 cells, which may be a consequence of the regulation on gene expression and AS of SF3B6.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and small interference RNA transfection

The MDA-MB-231 cells line (CL-0150B, Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd., China) were cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO2 in DMEM (PM150210, Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd., China) with 10% fetal bovine serum (10091148, Gibco, China), 100 µg/mL streptomycin, 100 U/mL penicillin (SV30010, Hyclone, USA). The siRNA duplexes were designed and constructed by Gemma (Suzhou, China), with 5’-UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT-3′ (sense) as negative control (siNC) sequences and 5′- GGAGGAACAGUUGAAGCUUTT- 3′ (sense) as targeting SF3B6 (siSF3B6) sequences. The Lipofectamine™ RNAi MAX Transfectn Reagent (13778030, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) was used for siRNA according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Transfected cells were harvested after 48 h for following experiments.

Cell proliferation assay

The Cell Counting kit-8 (CCK-8, 40203ES76, Yeasen, Shanghai, China) was used for cell proliferation assay following previous study and tested parameters15. Subsequent to the upstream steps, the optical density of the cells was measured with a Microplate Reader (ELX800, Biotek, USA) at an absorbance of 450 nm. The cell proliferation rate was calculated using the following formula: proliferation rate = (siSF3B6 OD value − blank OD value) / (siNC OD value − blank OD value) × 100%.

Apoptosis assay by flow cytometric analysis

The Annexin V-APC/7-ADD apoptosis detection kit (40304ES60, Yeasen, Shanghai, China) was used to evaluate the apoptosis level following previous study and tested parameters15. After the treatment of siNC and siSF3B6 cells with 5 µl Annexin V-APC incubated at room temperature in the dark for 5 min and 10 µl PI reagents incubated for 5 min, the samples were subjected to flow cytometry (FACSCanto, BD, USA) analysis to detect the apoptosis levels.

Cell migration assay by transwell experiment

The in vitro migration assay was conducted following previous study15, using transwell chambers (3422, Corning, USA) with 5*104 cells in 0.2 ml serum-free medium and 8 μm filter for each chamber. Then the chambers inserted in medium with 600 ul 10% FBS (10091148, Gibco, China) served as a chemoattractant in the lower chamber, and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C and 5% CO2. The total number of cells that migrated into the lower chamber were fixed by 4% paraformaldehyde (P0099, Beyotime, China) for 30 min, then stained with 0.1% crystal violet (C0121, Beyotime, China). The migration cells were observed and counted under inverted microscope (MF52-N, Mshot, China) at 200 × magnification.

RNA extraction and sequencing (RNA-seq)

Total RNA was treated with RQ1 DNase (Promega) to remove DNA, for quality assessment by measuring the absorbance at 260 nm/280nm (A260/A280) using smartspec plus (BioRad). RNA integrity was further verified by 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis.

For each sample, 1 µg of total RNA was used for mRNAs capture (Vazyme, N401) and directional RNA-seq library preparation by VAHTS® Universal V8 Kit for Illumina (N605) following previous study15. For next generation sequencing, the libraries were prepared following the manufacturer’s instructions and applied to Illumina Novaseq 6000 system for 150 nt paired-end sequencing.

RNA-Seq data analysis

Raw reads with two ambiguous (N) bases were first discarded. Then the reads were filtered by removing adaptors, low-quality bases, and short reads (< 16 nt) using FASTX-Toolkit (Version 0.0.13). After that, the filtered reads were aligned to the human genome (GRCh38) by HISAT216 allowing 4 mismatches. Gene expression levels were calculated following published study15 by FPKM calculation (fragments per kilobase of transcript per million fragments mapped)17. The R Bioconductor package DESeq218 was used to identify the differentially expressed genes (DEGs). The P value for correction < 0.05 and fold change ≥ 2 or ≤ 1/2 were set as the cut-off criteria for DEGs.

Alternative splicing analysis

The alternative splicing events (ASEs) and SF3B6-regulated ASEs (RASEs) were defined and quantified using the ABLas pipeline19. In brief, Ten ASE types were identified based on the splice junction reads, including exon skipping (ES), alternative 5’ splice site (A5SS), alternative 3′ splice site (A3SS), mutually exclusive exons (MXE), mutually exclusive 5’UTRs (5pMXE), mutually exclusive 3′UTRs (3pMXE), cassette exon, A3SS&ES, and A5SS&ES. To predict the RASEs, Student’s t test was performed to evaluate the statistical difference and significance of the ratio alteration of ASEs. Those events with P value corresponding to a false discovery rate cutoff of 5% were considered as RASEs.

Improved RNA Immunoprecipitation and sequencing (iRIP-seq)

The iRIP-seq experiment was conducted on wild-type MDA-MB-231 cells following previous study20. The cells were irradiated once for 400 mJ/cm2, and lysed in ice-cold wash buffer (1×PBS, 0.5% SDS, 0.5% NP-40 and 0.5% sodium deoxycholate) with a 400 U/mL RNase inhibitor (Takara) and protease inhibitor cocktail (Bimake) for 30 min. We added RQ I (Promega, 0.1 U/µl) and incubated it in a heat block for 30 min at 37 °C. The mixture was then vibrated vigorously and centrifuged at 13,000×g at 4 °C for 15 min to remove cell debris. Then RNA digestion by MNase was performed.

For immunoprecipitation, the supernatant was incubated overnight at 4 °C with 5 µg SF3B6 antibody (thermo: PA5-57077) and control IgG-antibody (AC005), and further incubated with protein A/G Dynabeads (Thermo Scientific) for 2 h at 4 °C. After removing the supernatants, the beads were sequentially washed with lysis buffer and high-salt buffer. We incubated the suspension for 30 min in a heat block at 70 °C to release the immunoprecipitated SF3B6 with crosslinked RNA and vortex. Then we added Proteinase K (Sangon Biotech) into the 10% input(without immunoprecipitated)and immunoprecipitated SF3B6 with crosslinked RNA, with final concentration of 1.2 mg/ml. The RNA was purified with Phenol: chloroform: isopentyl alcohol (25:24:1 pH < 5) reagent (Solarbio). The cDNA libraries were prepared with the KAPA RNA Hyper Prep Kit (KAPA, KK8544) according the manufacturer’s procedure. The libraries were sequenced with the same platform of RNA-seq.

Analysis of iRIP-seq dataset

Bioinformatics analysis of iRIP-seq was conducted following published study19. After reads were aligned onto the genome with HISAT2 16 and PCR duplicate was removed, two software programs, including Piranha21 and ABLIRC, were used to perform peak calling. “ABLIRC” strategy was used to identify the binding regions of SF3B6 using peak calling method19. After the random peak calculation, we conducted significance analysis on identified peak and screen out the peak with significant peak (p value < 0.05) or peak with maximum depth of a certain degree ( > = 10). Then, with the input samples as the control, the peak with IP abundance greater than 4 times (adjustable parameter) of input sample was screened as the final SF3B6-bound peak. The target genes of SF3B6 were finally determined by the peaks and the binding motifs of SF3B6 were called by HOMER software22.

RT-qPCR validation for selected genes

RT-qPCR was performed on the ABI QuantStudio 5 (Thermo, USA), followed by denaturing at 95 °C for 10 min, 40 cycles of denaturing at 95 °C for 15 s and annealing and extension at 60 °C for 1 min. Each group had two (iRIP-seq experiment) or three (RNA-seq experiment) biological replicates. And each sample had three technical replicates. The concentration of each transcript was then normalized to mRNA level of GAPDH using 2− ΔΔCT method to analysis23. Comparisons were performed by the two-way ANOVA or the paired Student’s t test by GraphPad Prism software (Version number8.0, San Diego, CA). Primers for qPCR analysis are presented in Table S1.

Functional enrichment analysis

To sort out functional categories of DEGs, RASGs, and peak associated genes (target genes), Gene Ontology (GO) pathways were identified using KOBAS 2.0 server24. Hypergeometric test and Benjamini-Hochberg FDR controlling procedure were used to define the enrichment of each pathway.

Results

Silencing SF3B6 regulated cell proliferation and gene expression in MDA-MB-231 cells

To explore the functions of SF3B6, we knocked down its expression in MDA-MB-231 cells using small interference RNA (siSF3B6). Four siRNAs were transfected into cells, and showed significant repression on SF3B6 expression by RT-qPCR experiment (Fig. 1A). Next, we selected the last siRNA (si-4) for following experiments due to its most repression level. By culturing the cells for 72 h after transfection, we found siSF3B6 significantly inhibited the proliferation of MDA-MB-231 cells compared with negative control (siNC) (Fig. 1B). At the same time, Apoptosis experiment demonstrated the obvious enhanced apoptosis level in siSF3B6 samples, although not statistically significant (Fig. 1C). Then we performed transwell experiment and found that siSF3B6 significantly inhibited the migration level of MDA-MB-231 cells (Fig. 1D). These results demonstrated that SF3B6 can promote the cancer phenotypes of MDA-MB-231 cells. To further explore how SF3B6 regulated the cellular phenotypes, we performed RNA-seq experiment to identify downstream targets of SF3B6. After obtaining the expression levels of all detected genes, principal component analysis (PCA) demonstrated that siSF3B6 samples can be clearly separated from the siNC samples (Fig. 1E). These results indicate that SF3B6 can affect the phenotypes of breast cancer cells probably by modulating the transcriptome profile.

Differentially expressed genes analysis by siSF3B6

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which SF3B6 contributes to the phenotype changes of breast cancer cells, we performed differentially expressed genes (DEGs) analysis and detected the significantly regulated genes by SF3B6. PCA for all DEGs demonstrated a clear separation between siSF3B6 and NC samples at the first component (Fig. 2A), indicating the profound regulation on gene expression of SF3B6. There were 296 up-regulated and 212 down-regulated DEGs in siSF3B6 samples compared to siNC samples (Fig. 2B). Heatmap presentation of the expression levels of DEGs showed they had consistent expression level among the three replicates (Fig. 2C). The results of GO-CC analysis shown the subcellular localization of DEGs, majority of which were located in the nucleus, cytoplasm, and extracellular regions (Fig. 2D-E), indicating that SF3B6 modulates the expression of genes widely distributed in cells. In summary, these results illustrated the regulatory role of SF3B6 on gene expression.

SF3B6-regulated DEGs were enriched in immune and inflammatory response pathways

Next, we performed Gene Ontology Biological Process (GO-BP) enrichment analysis to explore the enriched functional pathways of the DEGs. Notably, in addition to transcriptional and post-transcriptional regulation, the up DEGs were significantly enriched in pathways related to inflammation and immune responses, including neutrophil chemotaxis, cellular response to lipopolysaccharide, and cellular response to tumor necrosis factor (Fig. 3A), while the down DEGs were enriched in transmembrane transport, intracellular signal transduction, inflammatory response, and DNA repair associated pathways (Fig. 3B). To further investigate these findings, we selected several DEGs known to be associated with cancer or immune responses and analyzed their expression changes (Fig. 3C). Meanwhile, several tumor-associated DEGs were then validated by RT-qPCR experiment, which showed consistent change pattern with RNA-seq results (Fig. 3D). For instance, Adrenomedullin (ADM) is linked to tumor vessel hyperpermeability, metastasis and poor prognosis in cancer25,26. NR1D1 enhances anti-tumor immune response and inhibits breast cancer progression by activating cGAS-STING signaling pathway27. Inhibition of POLD1 has been shown to mediate apoptosis of MDA-MB-231 cells28. Together, these findings suggest that SF3B6 regulates immune response and inflammatory responses through diverse molecular mechanisms, ultimately affecting the progression of breast cancer.

SF3B6 regulates alternative splicing of immune-related genes

As a component of the spliceosome, SF3B6 participates in the regulation of alternative splicing (AS) pattern of pre-mRNAs. Thus, we detected the AS profile to identify the SF3B6-regulated AS events (RASEs). The total AS events results showed that intronR, ES, A3SS, and A5SS were top event types (Fig. 4A). By performing differential AS analysis between siSF3B6 and siNC samples, we found that there were 2706 RASEs (Fig. 4B, C). The distribution and frequency of different types of RASEs were shown in the Fig. 4B. Interestingly, intronR was the most prevalent type of RASEs, significantly surpassing other AS events. For the up- and down-regulated RASEs, the AS ratio showed high consistency among the three biological replicates for each of the two groups (Fig. 4C). GO-BP Enrichment analysis of the regulated alternative splicing genes (RASGs) identified the top 10 enriched pathways, which were closely related to transcription and post-transcriptional regulation, including RNA splicing, RNA processing, and regulation of transcription by RNA polymerase II (Fig. 4D). In addition, RASGs were significantly enriched in IκB/NF-κB signaling and innate immune response pathways (Fig. 4E). For example, NUP62, a component of the nuclear pore complex which regulates the transport of biomolecules between nucleus and cytoplasm29,30, has been reported to activate NF-κB signaling by stabilizing Nup88 31. These findings were consistent with the results shown in Fig. 2E. The changes in transcripts and the corresponding A5SS of NUP62, including the validation result by RT-qPCR, were shown in the Fig. 4F, which may result in a changed protein after translation. Together, these results suggest that SF3B6 plays a pivotal role in the alternative splicing of immune-related genes, particularly through mechanisms involving intron retention and the regulation of key pathways such as NF-κB signaling.

SF3B6 interacts with large number of RNAs by iRIP-seq experiment

SF3B6 is a canonical RNA binding protein (RBP) that can modulate gene expression and alternative splicing by interacting with RNA targets. To further explore its molecular mechanism, we performed iRIP-seq in MDA-MB-231 cells with input RNA as background (Figs. 5A, Fig. S1). The dominant genomic regions of SF3B6-bound peaks were antisense, CDS, intron, and intergenic regions of the two independent experiments, suggesting that SF3B6 protein has a broad binding region on RNAs (Fig. 5B). The results of motif analysis demonstrated that SF3B6 consistently bound to the GCCGG motif in both experiments (Fig. 5C). There are 75 peaks shared between the two experiments, and the corresponding genes may play an important role in the whole splicing process (Fig. 5D). The top 10 GO biological process analysis results showed that the SF3B6-bound genes in the first experiment were enriched in endocytosis, negative regulation of DNA-templated transcription, mRNA processing, and protein phosphorylation. The SF3B6-bound genes in the second experiment were mostly enriched in positive regulation of DNA-templated transcription (Fig. 5E). The heterogeneity of the two outcomes implies that SF3B6 may play a role in mRNA processing followed by transport and transcriptional regulation, respectively. Then, we identified the overlapped peaks and selected WDR74, which involved in pre-rRNA processing and ribosomal subunit biogenesis32. We found SF3B6 had obvious binding signal on the 5’ untranslated region of WDR74 (Fig. 5F), indicating the potential regulatory function of SF3B6 on WDR74 stability and translation.

SF3B6 has the ability to interact with mRNAs and modulate their expression levels

By integrating the analysis of the DEGs from the RNA-seq data and the identified genes from the iRIP-seq data, we found four overlapped genes (Fig. 6A). However, the fate of these mRNAs is not the same. Among them, the expressions of PPM1F and FASN were down-regulated, while the expression levels of RLF and RPS10 were up-regulated in siSF3B6 samples (Fig. 6B–E). The expression levels of PPM1F, FASN, and RLF were also validated by RT-qPCR experiment, showing consistent expression pattern with RNA-seq (Fig. 6C–E). The high expression of PPM1F promotes the migration and invasion of cancer cells and regulates apoptosis through dephosphorylation of TAK133,34. High expression of FASN promotes brain metastasis of breast cancer and lowers the survival rate of patients35–37. The binding site of PPM1F and FASN to SF3B6 in iRIP-seq data were shown in Fig. 6F, G. These results prove that SF3B6 protein can regulate part of gene expression through direct interaction with mRNA.

SF3B6 can regulate alternative splicing by directly binding to mRNAs

By integrating the analysis of the RASEs from the RNA-seq data and the binding peaks from the iRIP-seq data, we identified 39 overlapped genes (Fig. 7A). Among the overlapped genes, we found that RECQL4, which is a DNA helicase and plays a role in DNA replication and repair, and its mutation and abnormal expression can lead to genomic instability. It has been reported that RECQL4 plays an oncogenic role in breast cancer38,39. The A3SS event was observed in RECQL4 transcripts, which could form a longer transcript (Fig. 7B). Besides, the binding sites of SF3B6 protein on RECQL4 mRNA were detected around the alternative splicing region of RECQL4 transcript (Fig. 7C). These results suggest that SF3B6 protein interactions with these mRNAs can lead to abnormalities in transcripts.

Discussion

To clarify the mechanism of SF3B6 in promoting breast cancer, we performed high-throughput RNA sequencing by knocking down SF3B6 in MDA-MB-231 cells. The results of enrichment analysis of DEGs and RASGs showed that SF3B6 not only participated in gene transcription and post-transcriptional regulation as a regulatory factor, but also extensively affected the expression and alternative splicing of genes that were tightly associated with the development of breast cancer. Breafly speaking, SF3B6-regulated DEGs and RASGs were significantly enriched in immune/inflammatory associated pathways such as NF-κB signaling, which brought new insights into the regulatory signature of SF3B6. Further, the RNA binding profile of SF3B6 demonstrated that SF3B6 can modulate the expression and alternative splicing of a subset of genes probably through interacting with their transcripts. In summary, our results demonstrated the regulatory functions of SF3B6 and its potential influence on the development of breast cancer; the downstream targets of SF3B6 can be further explored to identify their potential as therapeutic targets in breast cancer in future.

As a canonical splicing factor, SF3B6 promotes pre-mRNA recognition, spliceosome formation, and helps to bind of introns with poor sequence complementarity40. In this study, we detected that thousands of AS events were dysregulated by siSF3B6 in MDA-MB-231 cells, among which the most abundant RASE type was intron retention (Fig. 4B), indicating that SF3B6 knockdown globally modulated the normal splicing profile of precursor mRNAs. Interestingly, we also observed that the most abundant bound region on RNAs of SF3B6 was intron, which may partly explain why intron retention events were so much more than other ASEs.

During the formation and function of the spliceosome, the subcellular localization of SF3B6 changes constantly41. This suggests that the molecules interacting with SF3B6 are likely to be dynamically changed as well, which is proved by the results of gene enrichment analysis identified from two RIP experiments. The binding peak and motif analysis of RIP-seq revealed the dynamic changes of SF3B6 regulation and RNA interaction during transcription and post-transcription. Through integrated analysis of RNA-seq and RIP-seq data, we found that SF3B6 can regulate gene expression or alternative splicing via directly bind to mRNA. Among them, PPM1F promotes cell migration and invasion in breast cancer42,43. FASN is highly expressed in breast cancer and facilitates tumor proliferation and metastasis44,45. The high expression of NCOR2 and its splicing body BQ323636.1 enhanced the drug resistance of tumors46,47. RLF and the tumor suppressor ANKRD11 are down-regulated in breast cancer48,49. In short, SF3B6 acts as a hub gene to widely regulate the expression and alternative splicing of oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes in breast cancer.

Meanwhile, our study also has some limitations. Our work partially explains the molecular mechanism of SF3B6 in promoting breast cancer, but we only used one cell line and did not perform in vivo animal studies to verify its effect on tumor growth. We will further investigate the function of SF3B6 in more cell lines, in vivo models, and clinical samples. We used high-throughput sequencing to obtain targets regulated or interacted with SF3B6, nevertheless, we did not deeply investigate the regulatory or underlying interaction mechanisms between SF3B6 and its potential targets, as well as the related signaling pathways and their influences on breast cancer. Definitely, this has become the key focus of subsequent research.

In conclusion, our research demonstrates that SF3B6 plays an important role in TNBC, and its cancer-promoting effect is due to the regulation of target genes at both transcriptional and post-transcriptional levels. In addition, SF3B6 may participate in immune/inflammatory responses and make a difference immune/tumor invasion. Our work serves as a crucial foundation to further investigate the function and underlying mechanisms of SF3B6 in breast cancer. Taken together, high expression of SF3B6 and its potential regulatory function may let it be a potential biomarker or have therapeutic value of breast cancer. Meanwhile, the mechanism by which it works needs to be further investigated.
