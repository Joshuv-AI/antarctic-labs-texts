Transient high-level DNA release mediated by cryo-thermal therapy promoted myeloid cell hyperactivation to induce CD4+ Th1 cell-dominant antitumor immunity
Authors: Junjun Wang, Yue Lou, Zelu Zhang, Shicheng Wang, Jiaqi You, Yongxin Zhu, Yichen Yao, Yuankai Hao, Ke Wang, Ping Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTumor patients often exhibit limited responses to immunotherapy owing to the low immunogenicity and immunosuppressive environment of tumors. Our previous studies revealed that cryo-thermal therapy caused tumor cell rupture due to mechanical compression, notably causing the release of a substantial amount of DAMPs (danger-associated molecular patterns), such as heat shock protein 70, calreticulin and high-mobility group box protein 1; the release of these DAMPs increased myeloid cell maturation, thereby reshaping the systemic immune environment and ultimately inducing durable CD4+ T helper type 1 (Th1) cell-dominated antitumor immunity. In fact, under conditions of mechanical stress and rapid temperature changes, the disruption of tumor cells caused by cryo-thermal therapy results in extensive deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) damage and the rapid release of substantial amounts of DNA. Consequently, tumor-derived DNA, which potently activates innate immunity by engaging multiple DNA sensors, plays a pivotal role in orchestrating antitumor immunity. We hypothesized that cryo-thermal therapy induces the transient release of high levels of DNA, which modulates CD11b+ myeloid cell function, subsequently influencing CD4+ Th1-cell dominated antitumor immune responses. In this study, a B16F10 melanoma model was established, and DNA concentrations were measured at different time points after cryo-thermal therapy. Deoxyribonuclease I (DNase I) was subsequently administered immediately following cryo-thermal therapy to deplete extracellular DNA, allowing an investigation of the role of DNA in regulating CD11b+ myeloid cell function and CD4+ T cell differentiation. The phenotype and function of CD11b+ myeloid cells and CD4+ T cells were assessed by flow cytometry, RNA sequencing, and cell culture in vitro. Our studies confirmed that cryo-thermal therapy triggered a transient release of high levels of DNA, which was internalized by CD11b+ myeloid cells via C-type lectin receptors and subsequently sensed by inflammasomes. Then, the intracellular sensing of DNA induced the production of the mature form of interleukin (IL)-18, ultimately promoting the Th1 differentiation of CD4+ T cells. This study highlights the pivotal role of DNA release after cryo-thermal therapy in driving CD4+ Th1 cell-dominant antitumor immunity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-025-02482-8.Keywords: Cryo-thermal therapy, High-level DNA, CD4+ Th1 cells, CD11b+ myeloid cells, C-type lectin receptor

Introduction

Tumor immunotherapy, in particular, immune checkpoint blockade and adoptive cell transfer, has facilitated significant progress in the clinical treatment of tumors. However, the overall clinical response rate of patients receiving tumor immunotherapy still needs to be improved. The major limitations to the success of immunotherapy are the low immunogenicity of tumor cells and the highly immunosuppressive tumor environment, the establishment of which is induced by the tumor itself. However, the challenges and limitations of immunotherapy can be addressed by promoting immunogenic cell death (ICD). ICD can be induced by various tumor therapies; increased tumor cell ICD leads to the release of DAMPs (danger-associated molecular patterns) and tumor-associated antigens to propagate antitumor immunity, thus improving cancer immunotherapy [1].

In situ tumor ablation techniques, including cryo- and heat-based thermal ablation, lead to the release of tumor debris and DAMPs via cellular destruction in order to elicit a systemic immune response [2]. DAMPs include various molecules, including nucleic acids, proteins, ions, glycans, and metabolites [3]. Tumor cell-derived deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA), which is a DAMP molecule, is recognized by DNA sensors in antigen-presenting cells (APCs) [4]. Upon detection by sensors in APCs, these DNA molecules initiate multiple signaling pathways, leading to the secretion of various cytokines. The cyclic GMP–AMP synthase (cGAS)–stimulator of interferon genes (STING) pathway, which senses and is activated by DNA, induces the transcription of type I interferon genes, which play key roles in increasing the antigen presentation capacity of APCs and mediating the reactivation of CD8+ T cell [5–8]. Toll-like receptor-9 (TLR9) can recognize foreign unmethylated CpG DNA to induce the production of proinflammatory cytokines and thus promote a strong T helper type 1 cells (Th1) response, thereby inducing a systematic antitumor response [9, 10]. Moreover, inflammasomes, such as NOD-, LRR- and pyrin domain-containing protein 3 (NLRP3) and absent in melanoma 2 (Aim2) inflammasomes, are also activated by nucleic acids and trigger the release of inflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin (IL)−1β and IL-18, which can stimulate T cell-dependent antitumor immunity [11–13]. Caspase-1 activation was triggered by the engagement of Aim2 and NLRP3 in DCs, leading to a potent enhancement of their antigen cross-presentation capacity, which ultimately resulted in the promotion of robust T cell-mediated antitumor immunity [14].

In our previous study, we developed an innovative cryo-thermal therapy for solid tumor ablation in situ, utilizing alternating liquid nitrogen cooling followed by radiofrequency (RF) heating. Compared with conventional clinical thermal ablation therapies, cryo-thermal therapy causes tumor tissue disruption because of mechanical stress and rapid temperature changes rather than extreme temperatures, resulting in unique cellular disruption. Our previous studies demonstrated that cryo-thermal therapy triggered the release of DAMPs, such as heat shock protein 70 (Hsp70), calreticulin (CRT) and high-mobility group box protein 1 (HMGB1), in situ and in peripheral circulation to induce CD11b+ myeloid cell maturation and promote the Th1 differentiation of CD4+ T cells [15–18]. Moreover, by systemically reversing the immunosuppressive microenvironment, cryo-thermal therapy enhanced the efficacy of cytokine-targeted therapy in a highly metastatic model [19]. These are key for the induction of long-lasting systemic antitumor immunity by cryo-thermal therapy [20]. Although tumor cells are disrupted by cryo-thermal therapy, whether tumor cells that are destroyed by cryo-thermal therapy can release damaged DNA to initiate CD11b+ myeloid cell maturation and ultimately lead to CD4+ Th1 cell-dominant immunity remains to be investigated.

In this work, a subcutaneous B16F10 tumor model was established, and deoxyribonuclease I (DNase I) was utilized to deplete DNA in vivo/in vitro after cryo-thermal therapy. The results demonstrated that cryo-thermal therapy induced the transient release of high levels of DNA, which promoted the release of IL-18, ultimately promoting CD4+ Th1 cell-dominant immunity. Mechanically, the DNA that was released due to cryo-thermal therapy-induced explosive cell lysis was engulfed by cells via the C-type lectin receptor; then, the DNA translocated to the cytoplasm, where it was sensed by inflammasomes and promoted inflammasome activation and IL-18 release, which is critical for the Th1 polarization of CD4+ cells. These studies indicate the key role of the transient release of high levels of DNA after cryo-thermal therapy in establishing long-term CD4+ Th1 cell-dominant immunity.

Methods

Cell culture and animal model

The B16F10 melanoma cell line was generously provided by Professor Weihai Yin from Med-X Research Institute, Shanghai Jiao Tong University. The cells were grown in complete Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, GE Healthcare, Logan, UT, USA), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gemini Bio-Products, West Sacramento, CA, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Hyclone, Logan, UT, USA), under 5% CO2 at 37 °C.

Female C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Shanghai Slaccas Experimental Animal Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) and housed in isolated cages under a 12-hour light/dark cycle, which were fed with sterile food and acidified water. To establish subcutaneously (s.c.) melanoma tumor model, 6–8 weeks mice were s.c. injected with approximately 5 × 105 B16F10 melanoma cells into the right flank.

All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Welfare Committee of School of Biomedical Engineering and Med-X Research Institute, Shanghai Jiao Tong University (2020017).

Cryo-thermal therapy procedures

The cryo-thermal therapy system was developed by our laboratory for local tumor treatment. On day 12 after tumor inoculation, when the diameter of the tumor reached approximately 10 mm, mice were randomly divided into different groups. To fully ablate the primary tumor, tumor-bearing mice were anesthetized with 1.6% pentobarbital sodium (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), followed by rapid LN2 freezing at −20 °C for 5 min, and subsequent radiofrequency heating at 50 °C for 10 min.

Quantification of DNA concentration

For in vivo DNA concentration measurement in the B16F10 model, B16F10 cells were inoculated subcutaneously into C57BL/6 mice. The serum from tumor-bearing mice or cryo-thermal therapy treated-mice at indicated times was collected, and DNA concentration was determined with the Picogreen double stranded DNA (dsDNA) Quantitation Reagent (Yeasen, Shanghai, China).

The tumor interstitial fluid from cryo-thermal therapy or cryo-thermal therapy with DNA depletion at indicated times was collected. The released DNA was extracted by MagMAX™ Cell-Free DNA Isolation Kit (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA,) and DNA concentration was determined with the Picogreen double stranded DNA (dsDNA) Quantitation Reagent (Yeasen, Shanghai, China).

DNA depletion in vivo/in vitro

For in vivo DNA depletion, mice were intravenously (i.v.) injected with 2000 UI DNase I (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) immediately after cryo-thermal therapy [21]. For in vitro DNA depletion, 0.5 units of DNase I was used for every 1 µg of DNA for digestion based on the concentration of serum DNA. The medium was incubated at 37 °C overnight [22].

Flow cytometry

The splenic single-cell suspension was obtained from indicated groups. In a brief, mice were sacrificed and splenocytes were collected. To remove red blood cells, the splenocytes were treated with erythrocyte lysis buffer (0.15 M NH4Cl, 1.0 M KHCO3, and 0.1 mM Na2EDTA) and then filtered through a 70 μm nylon cell strainer.

To exclude dead cells, the samples were first stained with Zombie Dye staining (Biolegend, San Diego, CA, USA). Following this, the cells were incubated with a panel of conjugated surface marker antibodies for 20 min at room temperature.

For detection of intracellular cytokines, the Cell Activation Cocktail (Biolegend, San Diego, CA, USA) was utilized to stimulate the cells for 4 h in an incubator with 5% CO2 at 37 °C. After surface staining, the cells were fixed and subsequently permeabilized with fixation buffer and intracellular staining permeabilization wash buffer (Biolegend, San Diego, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, respectively. Then the cells were incubated with antibodies for intracellular cytokines for 20 min.

For transcription factor staining, the cells were processed with a True-Nuclear Transcription Factor Buffer Set (Biolegend, San Diego, CA, USA). BD LSRFortessa (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) was used for collecting data and FlowJo V10 software (https://www.flowjo.com accessed on Sep 23, 2024) was used for analyzing the data. All reagents used were listed in Table S1 and the fluorochrome-conjugated monoclonal antibodies used were listed in Table S2.

Isolation of CD4+ T cell and CD11b+ myeloid cell

The splenocytes were collected 24 h after cryo-thermal therapy and subsequently prepared into the single-cell suspension. For isolation of CD4+ T cells, EasySep Mouse CD4 Positive Selection Kit II (StemCell Technologies, Vancouver, BC, Canada) was used to isolate CD4+ T cells. For isolation of the CD11b+ myeloid cells, the cells were labelled with an allophycocyanin-conjugated CD11b antibody (Biolegend, San Diego, CA, USA) and subsequently isolated using the EasySep Mouse Allophycocyanin Positive Selection Kit II (StemCell Technologies, Vancouver, BC, Canada) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

Enzyme linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

For serum IL-18 level detection, mice serum was collected 24 h post cryo-thermal therapy from indicated groups, and tested using the Mouse IL-18 Elisa Kit (Boster Biological Technology, Wuhan, China). A standard curve was generated and used for quantifying the concentration of IL-18 in the serum. All protocols were strictly obeying the instructions of the kit.

In vitro cell culture assay

To analyze the role of transient high-level DNA-reprogrammed CD11b+ myeloid cells in mediating the differentiation of CD4+ T cells, splenic CD11b+ myeloid cells were isolated 24 h post cryo-thermal therapy or cryo-thermal therapy with DNA depletion, and CD4+ T cells were collected from tumor -bearing control mice, respectively. CD11b+ myeloid cells and CD4+ T cells were cocultured in a 1:1 ratio for 48 h before phenotypical detection.

To analyze whether CD11b+myeloid cells which are activated by the released DNA after cryo-thermal therapy regulate the differentiation of CD4+ T cells through IL-18, splenic CD11b+ myeloid cells and CD4+ T cells were isolated 24 h after cryo-thermal therapy and tumor-bearing control respectively, and cocultured in a 1:1 ratio for 48 h. Anti-IL-18 monoclonal antibody (50 µg/mL) (Bio X Cell, West Lebanon, NH, USA) was supplied for IL-18 signaling blockade.

For further analyzing the role of C-type lectin receptor mediated internalization of released DNA after cryo-thermal therapy in stimulating the T cell differentiation-regulating function of CD11b+myeloid cells, splenic CD11b+ myeloid cells were isolated 24 h post cryo-thermal therapy or cryo-thermal therapy with blocking C-type lectin receptor which is blocked with Mannose, and cocultured with CD4+ T cells collected from tumor-bearing control mice in a 1:1 ratio for 48 h.

In all experiments, the CD3 agonist antibody (1 µg/mL) was added for the activation of CD4+ T cells. The subsets of CD4+ T cells were analyzed by flow cytometry.

Western blot

CD11b+ cells were ruptured on ice for 30 min in 1×RIPA lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) which contained phosphatase inhibitor (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and proteinase inhibitor (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). After being separated by 12.5% SDS-PAGE, proteins (15 µg per lane) were shuffled over to a PVDF membrane. This work made use of anti-ASC (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), anti-Cleaved Caspase-1 antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), anti-pro Caspase-1 antibody (Abcam, Cambridge, MA, UK), anti-β-actin (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), and HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (EpiZyme Biotech, Shanghai, China). These images are representative of three independent experiments.

Agarose gel electrophoresis

DNA from interstitial fluid were extracted by MagMAX Cell-Free DNA Isolation Kit (Thermo Fisher, USA) following the instruction manual and dissolved in DNA elution solution. Then samples from different treatment groups were then mixed with DNA loading buffer and separated on a 2.5% agarose gel (Biowest, Rue de la Caille, Nuaillé, France) with nucleic acid red (NA-Red) in 1× TBE buffer at room temperature. DNA ladder (100–600 bp with BeyoRed) was used for DNA fragment size marker. DNA loading buffer, NA-Red, TBE and DNA ladder were all purchased from Beyotime, Shanghai, China. Tanon 1200 (Tanon, Shanghai, China) was used for visualization capture.

RNA isolation and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

TRIzol (TaKaRa, Otsu, Shiga, Japan) was used for extracting total RNA of the isolated cells. And the PrimeScript RT reagent kit (TaKaRa, Otsu, Shiga, Japan) was used for reverse transcription. The absorbance at 260/280 nm of samples above 1.9 were used. For measuring the expression of genes, the ABI 7900HT sequence detection system (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA), and SDS 2.4 software were used for data collection. The amplified conditions comprised 40 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 60 °C for 31 s. The expression level of targeted genes was normalized as GAPDH (∆∆Ct method). All the primers used were purchased from (Thermo Scientific, USA) and listed in Table S3.

RNA sequencing

CD11b+ myeloid cells and CD4+ T cells were carefully isolated, as previously detailed. The TRIzol reagent (TaKaRa, Otsu, Shiga, Japan) was used for extracting total RNA from CD11b+ myeloid cells and CD4+ T cells, following strictly to the manufacturer’s guidelines. RNA purity and quantity were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA), while RNA integrity was evaluated with an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The VAHTS Universal V6 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit was used for constructing the RNA-seq libraries, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) was commissioned to conduct the subsequent transcriptome sequencing and analysis.

The heatmap analysis and the Volcano Plot was analyzed by OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). According to the relevant code, the dotplot was generated based on the results of the enrichment analysis. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed using the GSEA software. The software ranked the genes from a predefined gene set according to their differential expression across the two sample types. The goal was to test whether the predefined gene set showed significant enrichment at either the top or the bottom of the ranking list.

Statistical analysis

All data are shown as the mean ± SD. GraphPad Prism V.9.0 (La Jolla, California, USA, https://www.graphpad.com accessed on Sep 23, 2024) was used for statistical analyses. Two-sided the student’s t-test was used for measuring statistical distinctions in mean values between the two groups. For more groups, the differences were measured by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with the Holm-Sidak method. The survival rate of mice with different treatments was compared by the Kaplan-Meier method and the log-rank test. Values of *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001 and ****p < 0.0001 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Transient release of high levels of DNA after cryo-thermal therapy played a crucial role in promoting long-term survival in mice

In our previous studies, we revealed that cryo-thermal therapy can initiate tumor ICD and release immunostimulatory chaperone proteins [15, 18] and neoantigens [23] that contribute to the subsequent generation of a strong antitumor immune response, leading to the long-term survival of mice. In this study, we hypothesized that the chain of events from exposure to cryo-thermal therapy to tumor cell death involves substantial DNA damage and amplifies the ICD effect, thus improving the therapeutic effect of cryo-thermal therapy; therefore, we sought to evaluate the potential contribution of DNA damage that is triggered by cryo-thermal therapy to the induction of strong antitumor immunity. Therefore, the contents of serum DNA at different time points after cryo-thermal therapy were evaluated (Fig. 1A). The serum DNA concentrations were significantly increased at 6 h after cryo-thermal therapy and quickly returned to baseline at 24 h (Fig. 1B). After cryo-thermal therapy, the levels of DNA in tumor interstitial fluid (IF) were significantly reduced at 6 h compared to 0 h baseline , while the levels of serum DNA were concurrently elevated in the cryo-thermal therapy-treated mice (Fig. 1C). After cryo-thermal therapy with DNA depletion, although the levels of tumor interstitial DNA were effectively downregulated as compared to 0 h, no corresponding elevation in serum DNA was observed (Fig. 1C). These data demonstrated that tumor-derived DNA was rapidly mobilized into systemic circulation after cryo-thermal therapy. Further, the fragment size of DNA in tumor interstitial fluid was detected by agarose gel electrophoresis. A significant alteration in the DNA fragment size in tumor interstitial fluid was observed at 0 h after cryo-thermal therapy. (Fig. 1D). Compared with that from the control tumor tissues, IF from cryo-thermal therapy-treated tumor tissues contained a higher percentage of short DNA molecules (< 100 bp, Fig. 1D). To address the role of serum DNA that is released by dying cells in cryo-thermal therapy-mediated antitumor immunity, DNase I was intravenously injected immediately after cryo-thermal therapy to deplete serum DNA (Fig. 1E). The results revealed that the cryo-thermal therapy-induced increase in serum DNA levels was notably reversed by DNA depletion (Fig. 1B). In addition, the long-term survival of mice was significantly impaired when serum DNA was depleted in vivo via DNase I immediately after cryo-thermal therapy (Fig. 1E). These results suggest that transient high levels of DNA in serum following cryo-thermal therapy would play a crucial role in improving long-term survival of mice.

Transient release of high levels of DNA due to cryo-thermal therapy promoted CD4+ T cell Th1 differentiation via CD11b+ myeloid cells

CD11b+ myeloid cells (including monocytes, dendritic cells, tissue macrophages and granulocytes) bridge innate and adaptive immunity, mainly via antigen processing, antigen presentation and adaptive immune cell recruitment [24]. CD11b+ myeloid cells can induce CD4+ T cell differentiation into distinct functional subsets by presenting antigens and establishing a specific cytokine milieu [25, 26]. In our previous studies, cryo-thermal therapy triggered the release of proinflammatory cytokines and DAMPs, such as HSP70, HMGB1, and CRT, leading to CD11b+ myeloid cells activation and maturation [17, 18, 27]. Consequently, robust and long-lasting CD4+ Th1-mediated antitumor immunity was established [28]. To confirm whether the transient release of high levels of DNA after cryo-thermal therapy contributed to the induction of systemic Th1 cell-dominant CD4+ T cell-mediated antitumor immunity, the phenotype of CD11b+ myeloid cells and the percentage of CD4+ T-cell subsets were analyzed at different time points after cryo-thermal therapy via flow cytometry (Fig. 2A).

Compared with that in the tumor-bearing control group, the proportion of total CD11b+ myeloid cells in the spleen were lower 24 h after cryo-thermal therapy, but the expression level of major histocompatibility complex class II (MHC II) on total splenic CD11b+ myeloid cells was significantly greater (Fig. 2B). The proportion of total CD11b+ myeloid cells was significantly greater on days 5 and 14 after cryo-thermal therapy than in tumor-bearing control mice, although the expression levels of MHC II and CD86 on CD11b+ myeloid cells did not obviously change (Fig. 2C, D). When mice were treated with both cryo-thermal therapy and DNA depletion, there were no significant differences in the proportions or phenotypes of CD11b+ myeloid cells at different time points compared with those after cryo-thermal therapy alone (Fig. 2B-D). However, a decreased proportion of Th1 and T helper type 17 (Th17) cells and an increased proportion of regulatory T cells (Tregs) were observed 24 h after cryo-thermal therapy with DNA depletion compared with cryo-thermal therapy alone, suggesting that the dominant subset of CD4+ T cells changed from a Th1 subset to a suppressive Treg subset after cryo-thermal therapy with DNA depletion (Fig. 2E, S1A). Similarly, the increased proportion of CD4+ Th1 cells on day 5 after cryo-thermal therapy was also reversed when mice were subjected to cryo-thermal therapy combined with DNA depletion, although there was no difference in the proportion of Tregs (Fig. 2F). At the late stage (14 days after cryo-thermal therapy), an increased percentage of CD4+ Th1 cells and a decreased percentage of T follicular helper (Tfh) cells and Tregs were observed after cryo-thermal therapy, whereas the percentages of Tfh and Tregs were markedly increased after cryo-thermal therapy combined with DNA depletion (Fig. 2G, S1C). These findings suggest that the cryo-thermal therapy-induced transient release of high levels of DNA leads to persistent differentiation of CD4+ cells toward the Th1 lineage.

Myeloid cells are the major cell type that senses dsDNA [29, 30]. We wondered whether the transient release of high levels of DNA after cryo-thermal therapy could induce CD4+ Th1 cell differentiation via its effects on CD11b+ myeloid cells. CD4+ T cells were cocultured with CD11b+ myeloid cells that had been isolated by CD11b+ magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS) 24 h after cryo-thermal therapy combined with DNA deletion or with CD11b+ myeloid cells that had been isolated 24 h after cryo-thermal therapy alone (Fig. 2H). The proportions of CD4+ Th1 and Th17 cells were significantly lower and the population of Tfh were higher in the group cultured with CD11b+ myeloid cells after the combination treatment (Fig. 2I, S1D). There was no difference in the proportion of Treg when CD4+ T cells were cultured with CD11b+ myeloid cells from mice received cryo-thermal therapy combined DNA depletion compared with cultures of CD4+ T cells with CD11b+ myeloid cells from cryo-thermal therapy. These results indicated that transient DNA release-mediated CD4+ Th1 differentiation after cryo-thermal therapy depended on CD11b+ myeloid cells.

The DNA that is released after cryo-thermal therapy reprogrammed CD11b+ myeloid cells towards a proinflammatory phenotype via inflammasome activation

The results described above highlight the role of transient DNA release after cryo-thermal therapy in initiating CD4+ Th1 differentiation in a myeloid cell-dependent manner. However, no significant differences in the expression of markers that are associated with antigen presentation or costimulatory signaling pathways in CD11b+ myeloid cells were detected via flow cytometry between the group that was subjected to cryo-thermal therapy alone and the group that was subjected to cryo-thermal therapy combined with DNA depletion (Fig. 2B-D). To further investigate whether the expression of genes associated with antigen presentation and costimulatory signaling pathways in CD11b+ myeloid cells is affected by the transient release of high levels of DNA, RNA-seq was performed on splenic CD11b+ myeloid cells that were isolated by MACS 24 h after cryo-thermal therapy combined with DNA depletion (Fig. 3A).

Compared with CD11b+ myeloid cells from the cryo-thermal therapy alone group, CD11b+ myeloid cells from the cryo-thermal therapy combined with DNA deletion group presented an altered transcriptomic profile, in which a total of 773 differentially expressed genes were identified, including 527 upregulated genes and 246 downregulated genes (Fig. 3B, C). After cryo-thermal therapy combined with DNA depletion, the phagocytic capacity of CD11b+ myeloid cells was significantly reduced. Consequently, the abilities of these cells to present antigens, form immunological synapses, which provide a cellular microenvironment that is conducive to costimulatory signals [31], and the inflammatory response pathways were markedly impaired, leading to the inhibition of T cells (Fig. 3D). These findings indicate that the high levels of DNA that were transiently released after cryo-thermal therapy were engulfed by CD11b+ myeloid cells, resulting in reprogramming of CD11b+ myeloid cell function. However, CD11b+ myeloid cells sense this DNA after taking it up remains elusive. Among the DNA sensors, including cGAS, TLR9, NLRP3 and Aim2, the FPKM level of Aim2 was substantially lower than that observed with cryo-thermal therapy, whereas the expression of cGAS was slightly greater. (Fig. 3E). Moreover, after cryo-thermal therapy combined with DNA depletion, the expression level of Aim2 was measured via qRT‒PCR, and its expression was significantly decreased in CD11b+ myeloid cells (Fig. 3F). Upon recognition of intracellular or extracellular nucleic acids, Aim2 and NLRP3 initiate the assembly of a large multiprotein complex known as the inflammasome, subsequently leading to the secretion of bioactive IL-1β and IL-18[11, 12, 32]. Therefore, the expression levels of IL-18 and IL-1β were further confirmed via qRT‒PCR. A decreased level of IL-18 was observed in CD11b+ myeloid cells, and the level of IL-18 binding protein (IL-18BP) in CD11b+ myeloid cells was increased after cryo-thermal therapy combined with DNA depletion (Fig. 3F). No difference in the expression level of IL-1β was detected (Figure S2). IL-18 is a proinflammatory cytokine that was initially shown to function as an interferon (IFN)-γ-inducing factor that is involved in Th1 responses. IL-18BP, which is a natural inhibitor of IL-18, has high affinity for mature IL-18 and blocks its interaction with IL-18Rα, thus limiting IL-18-induced inflammation [33]. These findings indicate that the transient release of DNA induced by cryo-thermal therapy participates in the generation of inflammatory cytokines to promote the maturation of CD11b+ myeloid cells.

It is unclear whether the sensing of released DNA by CD11b+ myeloid cells induce the expression of IL-18 at the protein level. Therefore, IL-18 levels in the serum were measured via ELISA 24 h after cryo-thermal therapy. After cryo-thermal therapy, significantly higher serum IL-18 levels than those in the tumor-bearing controls was observed (Fig. 3G). Moreover, decreased serum levels of IL-18 were observed after cryo-thermal therapy combined with DNA depletion compared with those after cryo-thermal therapy alone (Fig. 3G). These findings suggest that the high level of DNA that was released after cryo-thermal therapy affects the secretion of the mature form of IL-18.

To investigate whether the released DNA was sensed by the inflammasome, serum samples were collected 6 h after cryo-thermal therapy and pre-treated with DNase I overnight. CD11b+ myeloid cells were isolated by MACS 24 h after cryo-thermal therapy and cultured in conditioned medium (Fig. 3H). When cultured in the serum conditional medium that had been pre-treated with DNase I, the expression level of Aim2 in CD11b+ myeloid cells were significantly decreased (Fig. 3I). Moreover, the expression level of IL-18 in CD11b+ myeloid cells was notably decreased after the cells were cultured in medium that had been conditioned with mouse serum 6 h after cryo-thermal therapy and in the presence of DNase I to deplete DNA in vitro (Fig. 3I). Additionally, there was an increased level of IL-18BP in the CD11b+ myeloid cells in the cryo-thermal therapy combined with DNA depletion group (Fig. 3I). To further investigate the activation of inflammasomes in myeloid cells, western blot analysis was performed in myeloid cells. After cryo-thermal therapy with DNA depletion, cleaved caspase-1 (p20/p10) levels were significantly elevated in myeloid cells compared to that of cryo-thermal therapy group, likely due to premature termination of caspase-1 signaling caused by the substantial reduction of the released DNA (Fig. 3J). Additionally, the supernatants from ex vivo cultured myeloid cells from cryo-thermal therapy and cryo-thermal therapy with DNA depletion were analyzed by ELISA. The secretion of IL-18 was markedly reduced in cryo-thermal therapy with DNA depletion group (Fig. 3K). These findings collectively indicated that cryo-thermal therapy-released DNA was essential for maintaining inflammasome activation in myeloid cells, highlighting the critical role of DNA-mediated signaling in regulating inflammasome activity and cytokine secretion. Together, these results indicated that the DNA that was transiently released after cryo-thermal therapy was sensed by the inflammasome, promoted the maturation of CD11b+ myeloid cells, and induced the production of high levels of the inflammatory cytokine IL-18.

Cryo-thermal therapy promoted the Th1 differentiation of CD4+ T cells via CD11b+ myeloid cells that were activated by released DNA

The results described above suggest that the transient release of high levels of DNA after cryo-thermal therapy influences the function of reprogrammed CD11b+ myeloid cells, which are characterized by high expression levels of IL-18. To investigate how transiently released DNA regulates the differentiation of CD4+ T cells through the maturation of CD11b+ myeloid cells, RNA-seq was performed to assess the changes in the gene expression profile of CD4+ T cells at the same time point after cryo-thermal therapy (Fig. 4A). Compared with that after cryo-thermal therapy alone, the transcriptomic profile of CD4+ T cells from cryo-thermal therapy combined with DNA depletion group was significantly altered, and a total of 1346 differentially expressed genes were identified, including 1228 upregulated and 118 downregulated genes (Fig. 4B, C). The response to IL-18 was decreased, and the expression level of IL-18R was obviously lower in CD4+ T cells after cryo-thermal therapy combined with DNA depletion than in those after cryo-thermal therapy alone (Fig. 4D). In addition, GSEA revealed that CD4+ T cell differentiation-related pathways, including negative regulation of the transforming growth factor β receptor signaling pathway and Th17, Th1 and Th2 differentiation (Fig. 4E), were significantly inhibited after cryo-thermal therapy combined with DNA depletion, indicating that the response to transforming growth factor (TGF)-β, which is crucial for Treg differentiation and immunosuppressive function [34, 35] in CD4+ T cells, was increased after cryo-thermal therapy combined with DNA depletion; thus, CD4+ T cells have the potential to differentiate into immunosuppressive regulatory T cells rather than CD4+ Th1 and Th17 cells after cryo-thermal therapy combined with DNA depletion, which is consistent with the findings of in vivo studies (Fig. 2E, S1A). Consistently, the expression levels of Ifng (encodes IFN-γ, the typical cytokine of CD4+ Th1 cells) and Tbx21 (encodes T-bet, the major transcription factor of CD4+ Th1 cells [36]) were downregulated in the cryo-thermal therapy combined with DNA depletion group (Fig. 4F). Collectively, these results suggest that CD4+ T cells respond to IL-18 that is secreted by CD11b+ myeloid cells that are activated by the DNA that is released after cryo-thermal therapy and initiate Th1-dominant differentiation.

To further validate the assumption described above, splenic CD11b+ myeloid cells and CD4+ T cells were isolated and cocultured in vitro with an anti-IL-18 neutralizing monoclonal antibody (Fig. 4G). The subsets of CD4+ T cells 48 h after cocultivation with splenic CD11b+ myeloid cells were detected via flow cytometry. The results revealed that the CD4+ Th1 subset was significantly increased, but there was no difference in the proportion of Treg when CD4+ T cells were cocultured with CD11b+ myeloid cells from mice that received cryo-thermal therapy compared with cultures of CD4+ T cells with CD11b+ myeloid cells from tumor-bearing controls (Fig. 4H). However, in the presence of anti-IL-18 neutralizing antibody in vitro, the population of CD4+ Th1 subset notably decreased when CD4+ T cells were cocultured with CD11b+ myeloid cells from mice that received cryo-thermal therapy (Fig. 4H). At the same time, the population of Th2 and Th17 was significantly upregulated after being cultured with CD11b+ myeloid cells from mice that received cryo-thermal therapy (Figure S3). After IL-18 neutralization in vitro, the upregulated level of Th2 and Th17 was reversed (Figure S3). These results indicated that cryo-thermal therapy promoted the secretion of IL-18 via the transient release of high levels of DNA, ultimately inducing the differentiation of Th1 cells.

CD11b+ myeloid cells engulfed released DNA via the C-type lectin receptor to induce the expression of IL-18, leading to CD4+ Th1 differentiation after cryo-thermal therapy

The results described above indicated that the transient release of high levels of DNA after cryo-thermal therapy induced the maturation of CD11b+ myeloid cells to produce IL-18, thereby promoting the differentiation of CD4+ T cells into Th1 cells. Because the phagocytic capacity was dramatically reduced after cryo-thermal therapy combined with DNA depletion (Fig. 3D), the mechanism by which the released DNA was taken up by CD11b+ myeloid cells remained still unknown. GSEA of the RNA-seq data revealed that the C-type lectin receptor signaling pathway was notably downregulated after cryo-thermal therapy combined with DNA depletion compared with that after cryo-thermal therapy alone (Fig. 5A). C-type lectin receptors play key roles in phagocytosis, cytokine production, and antigen presentation [37]. Furthermore, CD209A, which is a C-type lectin receptor, acts as a cell surface receptor for internalizing DNA, thereby playing a crucial role in facilitating and modulating immune responses [38]. These results suggest that transiently released DNA could be phagocytized by CD11b+ myeloid cells via the C-type lectin receptor following cryo-thermal therapy.

To identify whether the DNA that was released after cryo-thermal therapy was phagocytosed by CD11b+ myeloid cells via the C-type lectin receptor, CD11b+ myeloid cells were isolated 24 h after cryo-thermal therapy and pre-treated with mannose (C-type lectin receptor inhibitor) for 1 h, followed by culture in the medium supplemented with 6 h serum samples (Fig. 5B). After blocking the C-type lectin receptor with mannose, a decrease in Aim2 expression was detected via qRT‒PCR (Fig. 5C). Furthermore, the expression level of IL-18 was inhibited after blocking the C-type lectin receptor, accompanied by an increased level of IL-18BP (Fig. 5D). Moreover, the serum level of IL-18 was lower 24 h after cryo-thermal therapy combined with C-type lectin receptor blockade than after cryo-thermal therapy alone (Fig. 5E, F). These results indicate that CD11b+ myeloid cells phagocytose DNA after its transient release induced by cryo-thermal therapy in a C-type lectin receptor-dependent manner.

To study the role of C-type lectin receptor signaling in CD11b+ myeloid cell maturation and CD4+ T cell differentiation in vivo, mannose was i.v. injected immediately after cryo-thermal therapy, and the phenotypes of CD11b+ myeloid cells and subsets of CD4+ T cells were detected via qRT‒PCR and flow cytometry, respectively, 24 h after treatment (Fig. 5E). Compared with that after cryo-thermal therapy, the expression level of Aim2 was significantly downregulated after cryo-thermal therapy combined with C-type lectin receptor blockade (Fig. 5G). Moreover, IL-18 expression was decreased, although there was no difference in the expression level of IL-18BP (Fig. 5H). Additionally, the percentage of Th1-polarized CD4+ T cells (characterized by the expression of the typical cytokine IFN-γ) was significantly lower after blocking the C-type lectin receptor than after cryo-thermal therapy alone, whereas there were no differences in other CD4+ subsets (Fig. 5I, S4A). In accordance with the above in vivo studies, the differentiation of CD4+ Th1 cells was inhibited after they were cultured with CD11b+ myeloid cells from mice that received cryo-thermal therapy combined with C- type lectin receptor blockade compared with cells that were cultured with CD11b+ myeloid cells from mice that received cryo-thermal therapy alone (Fig. 5J). Meanwhile, the proportion of Th2 and Th17 cells were significantly lower and the population of Tfh were higher after being cultured with CD11b+ myeloid cells from mice that received cryo-thermal therapy combined with C-type lectin receptor blockade (Figure S4B). Moreover, the long-term survival of mice was notably impaired when the C-type lectin receptor was blocked in vivo after cryo-thermal therapy (Fig. 5K). These findings suggest that the C-type lectin receptor plays a key role in the uptake of the released DNA, thereby leading to the activation and assembly of the inflammasome and the release of the inflammatory factor IL-18, which contributes to efficient Th1 differentiation.

Discussion

In our previous study, we revealed that cryo-thermal therapy-induced early-phase IFN-γ responses maintained Th1-dominant CD4+ T cell differentiation in the late stage. However, the mechanism underlying the induction of Th1 differentiation in the early stage after cryo-thermal therapy has not yet been elucidated. In this study, cryo-thermal therapy induced the transient release of high levels of DNA into serum, where this DNA was sensed by CD11b+ myeloid cells; then, these myeloid cells were reprogrammed into mature APCs and promoted the Th1 differentiation of CD4+ T cells in the early stage. Mechanically, the released DNA was engulfed by the C-type lectin receptor into the cytoplasm of myeloid cells, where it was sensed by inflammasomes, ultimately inducing the release of IL-18, which plays a major role in promoting CD4+ Th1 differentiation. Our findings emphasized the vital role of cryo-thermal therapy-induced transiently high levels of DNA in the serum in promoting CD4+ Th1 differentiation at an early stage via the IL-18 that is produced by myeloid cells.

In our study, we determined that the transient release of high levels of DNA into the serum was induced by cryo-thermal therapy, which resulted from DNA damage after treatment. Cellular DNA is continuously threatened by damage from both exogenous and endogenous sources. Cell-intrinsic factors, such as cellular metabolism involving aldehydes and reactive oxygen species, lead to DNA breaks [39]. On the other hand, environmental, physical and chemical agents can also lead to exogenous DNA damage [40]. Therefore, when cells experience DNA damage, small fragments of DNA can leak from broken cells and may be detected by DNA sensors, thereby activating downstream signaling pathways to initiate the inflammatory immune response [41]. Cryo-thermal therapy disrupts the whole tumor through the synergistic effects of thermal and mechanical stresses [15], thus increasing the degree of DNA damage in tumor cells and ultimately resulting in massive leakage of DNA from these tumor cells.

Previous studies have suggested that p20/p10 was considered to represent the active form of caspase-1. However, the dominant active caspase-1 dimers induced by inflammasomes were primarily full-length p46 and the transient species p33/p10, rather than cleaved caspase-1 (p20/p10)[42]. In fact, the generation of p20/p10 disrupted caspase-1 structural integrity, thereby terminating caspase-1 signaling. Our study found that following cryo-thermal therapy with DNA depletion, the expression level of cleaved caspase-1 (p20/p10) in the myeloid cells was significantly higher compared to cryo-thermal therapy, suggesting that DNA depletion led to the premature termination of caspase-1 signaling in myeloid cells after the therapy. These results indicated that the released DNA after cryo-thermal therapy led to the activation of caspase-1 signaling in myeloid cells, thereby leading to the persistent secretion of IL-18.

cGAS is the major cytosolic sensor of dsDNA in the innate immune response and is crucial for facilitating type I IFN production in response to intracellular DNA [43, 44]. Aim2 is considered to be the other primary detector of cytosolic DNA and can form a molecular platform with caspase-1 and ASC to process the precursors of the proinflammatory cytokines IL-1β and IL-18 into their active forms [45]. In this study, the expression level of Aim2 was downregulated, but the expression level of cGAS was upregulated after cryo-thermal therapy combined with DNA depletion, which indicated that the DNA that was released after cryo-thermal therapy was sensed mainly by the Aim2 inflammasome instead of cGAS. Gasdermin D triggered by the Aim2 inflammasome induces K+ efflux, which is pivotal for inhibiting the cGAS-STING pathway [46]. In addition, once the inflammasome is activated, caspase-1 interacts with cGAS and eventually cleaves it, thereby inhibiting the type I IFN response [47]. Moreover, cGAS deficiency can enhance inflammasome activation in macrophages [48]. Therefore, cryo-thermal therapy triggers the release of high levels of DNA into the serum, where it is sensed by the inflammasome, thereby inhibiting the activation of the cGAS-STING pathway. In fact, inactive procaspase-1 is constitutively expressed in macrophages, and once the inflammasome is activated, it triggers the cleavage of caspase-1 into its mature form without transcriptional activation [49], which may explain why inflammasomes are rapidly activated when they sense the release of high levels of DNA into the serum after cryo-thermal therapy instead of cGAS. In addition, IF from Cryo-thermal therapy-treated tumor tissues contained a higher percentage of short DNA molecule (< 100 bp) as compared with that from the control tumor tissues. However, the cGAS-STING signaling pathway can be activated by long DNA molecules (>500 bp) at lower DNA concentration [50]. In contrast, Aim2 can be fully activated by DNA fragments as short as ~ 80bp [51], which were abundant in IF after cryo-thermal therapy. This finding explained why the Aim2 inflammasome is activated instead of the cGAS‒STING pathway after cryo-thermal therapy.

Owing to their notable ability to recognize pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and DAMPs, C-type lectin receptors constitute an important class of pattern recognition receptors and play crucial roles in inducing adaptive immunity [37, 52]. Additionally, C-type lectin receptors are currently known for their ability to mediate phagocytosis in myeloid cells [53]. Dendritic cells (DCs) internalize DNA, which is dependent on the C-type lectin receptor, thereby delivering DNA to promote the cytokine response [38]. In this study, after cryo-thermal therapy, the released DNA was internalized via C-type lectin receptors and translocated to the cytoplasm, where it was sensed by the inflammasome, which in turn assembled to process pro-IL-18 to mature IL-18. In this study, due to the lack of available anti-mouse CD209A antibodies, mannose was used as a broad-spectrum CLR inhibitor, which could simultaneously block the receptor(s) responsible for DNA endocytosis and other CLR members. This may explain why mannose treatment resulted in lower survival rates compared to DNase treatment.

Conclusion

In summary, we revealed that cryo-thermal therapy promoted DNA damage in tumor cells, which resulted in the transient release of high levels of DNA. The released DNA was phagocytosed by CD11b+ myeloid cells, translocated to the cytoplasm, and detected by the inflammasome. Then, the activated inflammasome cleaved the inactive form of IL-18 into the active form, thereby leading to long-term CD4+ Th1 immunity. This study highlighted the contribution of released DNA to the induction of systematic antitumor immunity after cryo-thermal therapy.
