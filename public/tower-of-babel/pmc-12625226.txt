Sequential omics analysis reveals molecular signatures of malignant transformation in recurrent meningiomas
Authors: Kenta Ohara, Satoru Miyawaki, Yu Teranishi, Daisuke Komura, Atsushi Okano, Hiroki Hongo, Shogo Dofuku, Daiichiro Ishigami, Yu Sakai, Daisuke Shimada, Hiroto Katoh, Genta Nagae, Masako Ikemura, Tetsuo Ushiku, Shinichi Morishita, Yutaka Suzuki, Masahiro Shin, Hirofumi Nakatomi, Shumpei Ishikawa, Hiroyuki Aburatani, Nobuhito Saito
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMeningiomas are the most common primary brain tumors in adults and have the potential for recurrence. Although most recurrent meningiomas retain their initial World Health Organization grade, a subset undergoes malignant transformation (MT). The molecular mechanisms underlying this transformation remain poorly understood. We aimed to characterize distinct recurrence subtypes—MT and grade 1-retained recurrence (GR)—using sequential multi-omic analyses. In this study, we reviewed meningioma patients with paired histological evaluations. Among these, 10 patients experienced MT and 25 showed GR. Patients with MT exhibited significantly higher Ki-67 proliferation indices and shorter overall survival. Comprehensive molecular profiling, including matched sequential recurrences, was performed on samples from six patients each with MT and GR meningiomas. Compared to GR tumors, MT tumors demonstrated a marked increase in tumor mutation burden and copy number alterations, with deletion of cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 2A emerging as a key acquired event. MT cases also showed selective upregulation of cell cycle-related genes, including Forkhead box M1, a feature absent in GR tumors. Notably, even prior to recurrence, MT tumors displayed distinct global DNA methylation patterns, particularly in regions targeted by the polycomb repressive complex 2 and H3K27me3 marks. Our findings suggest that molecular signatures evolve during MT and that certain intermediate aggressive meningiomas may progress toward malignancy. This study underscores the importance of DNA methylation and transcriptomic profiling in understanding tumor progression and recurrence. Introduction

Meningiomas are the most common primary tumors of the central nervous system, accounting for approximately 37.6% of all primary brain tumors [48]. Approximately 80% exhibit benign histological traits, and gross total resection remains the standard treatment, with 5-year local recurrence rates ranging from 7 to 23% [56]. However, a subset of World Health Organization (WHO) grade 1 meningiomas recur and follow an aggressive clinical course [41, 47]. High-grade meningiomas are associated with increased recurrence and reduced survival, in line with their histopathological characteristics [20, 43, 56, 60]. Although most recurrent meningiomas maintain their original histological grade, a subset progresses to higher grades, resembling the transformation pattern observed in gliomas [26].

Malignant transformation of meningiomas occurs in 0.16–2.1% of cases [2, 17, 44, 56]. Advanced age and non-skull base location are potential risk factors for progression [4, 40], yet predicting these cases remains challenging. The terminology for “malignant transformation (MT)” has varied across the literature, with some reports including upgrades to WHO grade 2 [2, 44]. However, focusing on the clinically most aggressive entity, secondary WHO grade 3 meningiomas arising from lower-grade tumors have a worse prognosis than de novo grade 3 cases [54, 57]. Even when originating from tumors initially classified as WHO grade 1, secondary WHO grade 3 meningiomas exhibit the poorest outcomes, underscoring the importance of elucidating their molecular mechanisms for timely detection and therapeutic strategies.

Recent studies have shed light on the genetic landscape of high-grade meningiomas and identified risk factors for recurrence [1, 2, 9, 10, 14, 15, 23, 68]. Large-scale molecular analyses have improved the classification and prediction of aggressive subtypes [12, 45, 46, 50, 59]. Key findings include Neurofibromatosis type 2 (NF2) inactivation and extensive chromosomal copy number alterations (CNAs), such as 1p loss and cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 2A (CDKN2A) homozygous deletion. Epigenetic and transcriptomic features, including global DNA methylation have been linked to tumor aggressiveness. Meningiomas with high proliferative activity frequently exhibit Forkhead box M1 (FOXM1) overexpression or hypermethylation at histone H3 lysine 27 trimethylation (H3K27me3) targeted sites [12, 45, 67]. Mutations (C228T and C250T) in the telomerase reverse transcriptase promoter (TERTp) region have also been identified as independent poor prognostic factors, regardless of WHO grade [41, 58, 63, 65].

The revised WHO classification has enhanced our understanding of the molecular features of anaplastic (grade 3) meningiomas. However, the mechanisms driving recurrence and MT remain poorly defined [23, 68]. While some studies have explored molecular alterations in recurrent meningiomas, few have analyzed paired primary and recurrent tumors—an approach critical for identifying acquired changes during progression [8, 23, 37]. Because malignant transformation is rare but associated with dismal prognosis, elucidating its molecular course is essential. In this study, we aimed to address this gap by characterizing sequential molecular alterations through comprehensive analyses of longitudinally collected, matched tumor samples, focusing on WHO grade progression. For clarity, we adopted a strict definition of malignant transformation (MT) as histological progression to WHO grade 3. Specifically, we investigated the MT pathway by comparing secondary WHO grade 3 meningiomas with their matched lower-grade precursors.

Materials and methods

Study participants

From an institutional cohort of 516 patients who underwent meningioma resection between January 1, 2000 and July 31, 2021, we identified 50 patients who underwent multiple resections for recurrence with paired histological evaluations available. Among these, 35 patients were included in the clinical analyses: 10 patients who experienced MT, defined as histological progression to WHO grade 3, and 25 with grade-retained recurrence, retained at WHO grade 1 (GR). Histopathological diagnoses were made at the Department of Pathology, University of Tokyo Hospital, in accordance with the 2021 WHO classification [38]. Clinical data were retrospectively reviewed and compared. We conducted comprehensive molecular analyses of samples from 12 patients (12 blood samples and 37 tumor samples; Table S1). Six patients were classified as having experienced MT and six as showing GR, according to the definitions described above.

Collection of blood and frozen tumor samples and extraction of DNA and RNA

After explaining the purpose of the study and obtaining informed consent, blood and tumor samples were collected from patients during surgery. Venous blood samples (2 mL) were drawn intraoperatively and stored at 4 °C in ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid-containing tubes. Tumor specimens resected during surgery were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C. DNA was extracted from both blood and tumor samples using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany). DNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit (QIAGEN) in combination with the RNase-Free DNase Set (QIAGEN). RNA quantity was measured using the NanoDrop spectrophotometer, and RNA integrity was evaluated with an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA).

Whole-exome sequencing (WES)

Library preparation was performed using the SureSelect XT Human All Exon Kit V6 (Agilent Technologies). Genomic DNA was fragmented with an acoustic ultrasonicator (Covaris, Woburn, MA, USA), and adapter sequences were ligated to both ends of the fragments. Target exons were captured by hybridization with biotinylated RNA probes and isolated using streptavidin-conjugated magnetic beads. The captured DNA was then amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) to construct sequencing libraries. Sequencing was performed on a NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), generating 150-base pair (bp) paired-end reads with a mean coverage depth of 133 × (Table S2). All DNA libraries contained unique molecular barcodes, and sequencing data were processed as previously described [30]. Molecular barcodes were annotated using AnnotateBamWithUmis (fgbio 1.1.0), and reads were aligned to the human reference genome using Burrows-Wheeler Aligner [35]. Consensus sequences were generated with CallMolecularConsensusReads (fgbio, v1.1.0). Somatic mutations and CNAs in tumor tissues were identified using WES [13]. Single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and small insertions/deletions (indels) were detected with two variant callers: Mutect2 (GATK v4.1.7.0) and Strelka2 (v2.9.10). Only variants detected by both tools were included in downstream analyses. Additional filtering of Mutect2 calls was performed with FilterMutectCalls (GATK v4.1.7.0). To minimize false positives, we excluded variants that were found in dbSNP build 138, detected in a panel of normals (derived from pooled peripheral blood samples), present in the matched blood sample, or supported by fewer than five reads in the tumor sample.

CNAs were analyzed using the CNV_facets package, with tumor and matched blood samples as inputs [61]. Germline mutations in peripheral blood samples were annotated using HaplotypeCaller (GATK v4.1.7.0) and Funcotator (GATK v4.1.7.0). SNVs and indels listed in the Database for Single Nucleotide Polymorphisms (dbSNP) build 138 were excluded [62]. Pathogenic mutations were identified based on clinical significance registered in the ClinVar database [32]. Phylogenetic tree analysis was manually performed for each case based on shared genetic mutations. Additionally relevant genetic alterations beyond mutations were manually annotated and incorporated into the phylogenetic framework.

RNA sequencing

Bulk RNA sequencing was performed on RNA extracted from 37 frozen tumor samples collected from 12 patients. Libraries were prepared using the TruSeq Stranded mRNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina). Polyadenylated mRNA was isolated from total RNA with magnetic beads conjugated to poly(T) oligonucleotides and subsequently fragmented by heat treatment. First-strand cDNA synthesis was performed using SuperScript II reverse transcriptase (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Adapter sequences were ligated to both ends of the cDNA, followed by PCR amplification to generate sequencing libraries. Libraries were sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 system (Illumina), producing 100-bp single-end reads (Table S3). Sequencing reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38/hg38) using the Spliced Transcripts Alignment to a Reference (STAR) aligner [19]. Raw gene expression levels were quantified with StringTie and normalized as both read counts and transcripts per million (TPM) [52].

Gene expression data analysis

Gene expression data were analyzed using iDEP (v0.93) [22]. Read count data were used for normalization, hierarchical clustering, principal component analysis (PCA), gene expression variation analysis, Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis, and pathway enrichment analysis–all performed with default settings. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was conducted to identify differentially expressed gene (DEG) sets between recurrent tumor types [66]. To compare gene expression profiles across recurrence types, we used the HALLMARK Gene Sets (v7.4) from the Molecular Signatures Database [36]. For hierarchical clustering, FOXM1 target genes were defined as previously described [67], and E2F target genes were obtained from the HALLMARK_E2F_TARGETS gene set (v7.4) [36]. Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) was applied to evaluate the expression of specific gene sets across individual tumor samples [24]. Additionally, gene sets characteristic of molecular meningioma subgroups were assessed based on the classifications reported by Nassiri et al. [45].

DNA methylation analysis

Genome-wide DNA methylation profiling was performed on 37 tumor samples from 12 patients using the Infinium MethylationEPIC BeadChip array (Illumina). Raw methylation data were preprocessed using the minfi package, and beta values and M values were calculated for each CpG probe [3]. Probes with poor performance (detection P > 0.01 in any sample) were excluded from downstream analyses. Additionally, probes containing common single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), located on sex chromosomes (X or Y), or identified as cross-reactive (i.e., mapping to multiple genomic loci) were removed [11]. Quantile normalization was applied during preprocessing [3]. Differentially methylated positions (DMPs) were identified using the limma package [55], and GO enrichment analysis was performed on these probes. Differentially methylated regions (DMRs) were then identified with the R package DMRcate using default parameters [53]. For hierarchical clustering, the 10,000 probes with the highest standard deviation in M values were selected. Clustering was conducted with Euclidean distance and complete linkage via Cluster 3.0 [18]. PCA was performed using the top 1,000 most variable probes across samples. To classify meningioma samples based on DNA methylation profiles, we applied the MNGv2.4 classifier, as described by Sahm et al. [59], using the MolecularNeuropathology. org server (https://www.molecularneuropathology.org/mnp).

Master transcription factor analysis

Master transcription factor analysis was conducted using ElmerV2 [64]. Based on DNA methylation β-values, differentially methylated distal probes (located in non-promoter regions) were identified between molecular tumor subtypes (MTs). For each differentially methylated probe, the 10 nearest upstream and 10 nearest downstream genes were assessed for inverse correlation between methylation and gene expression. Owing to the limited sample size, the analysis was conducted with the following parameters: genome = “hg38,” method = “unsupervised”; differential methylation thresholds: methylation difference (sig.diff) = 0.3, p value threshold = 0.05, minimum subgroup fraction (minSubgroupFrac) = 0.2; probe–gene pair association criteria: p-value threshold for association (Pe) = 0.05, raw p value threshold (raw.pvalue) = 0.05, filterProbes = TRUE, permutation size (permu.size) = 10, 000, and minimum subgroup fraction (minSubgroupFrac) = 0.4.

Histopathological analysis

All pathological diagnoses were confirmed by an expert neuropathologist according to the 2021 WHO Classification of Tumors of the Central Nervous System [38]. Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue sections were used for immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis. Whole-slide sections were stained for Ki-67, FOXM1, and MCM2 using the following primary antibodies: anti-Ki-67 rabbit polyclonal (clone 30–9; Ventana Medical Systems, Tucson, AZ, USA), anti-FOXM1 rabbit monoclonal (clone EPR17379; Abcam; dilution 1:200), and anti-MCM2 mouse monoclonal (Cell Signaling Technology, #12079S, dilution 1:200). IHC evaluation was performed on 90 tumor samples from 10 patients with MT and 25 patients with GR. The Ki-67 labeling index was assessed by identifying regions with the highest cellular density and determining the maximum percentage of positively stained nuclei. For FOXM1 and MCM2, the number of positively stained nuclei per high-power field was quantified with ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Statistical analyses

Categorical variables were compared between subgroups using Fisher’s exact test. The Mann–Whitney U test was applied to compare two non-parametric continuous variables. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated to assess overall survival, and comparisons between recurrence types were evaluated using the log-rank test. Differences in gene and protein expression were also analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test. The Benjamini–Hochberg method was applied to control the false discovery rate (FDR or q-value) in GO enrichment analysis, pathway analysis, and GSEA. All statistical analyses were performed using JMP Pro version 15 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). All reported p-values were two-sided, and values of < 0.05, including FDR-adjusted q-values, were considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical analysis was performed on 35 patients with paired histological evaluations, including 10 who experienced MT (progression to WHO grade 3) and 25 with GR (Fig. 1a). Clinical comparisons (Table S4) showed that patients with MT were significantly older at initial surgery (p = 0.0058), had more recurrences (p = 0.016), and more frequently presented with non–skull base tumors (p = 0.027, Fig. 1b). Notably, distant metastases were observed only in MT cases (5/10, 50%). The Ki-67 index remained stable in the GR group (3.2 ± 2.8% to 4.3 ± 2.9%) but increased significantly in MT cases (8.5 ± 4.5% to 34.3 ± 14.9%, p = 3.7 × 10⁻⁷, Fig. 1c). Overall survival following initial surgery was significantly shorter in patients with MT (p = 0.029, Fig. 1d). Genomic alterations by whole-exome sequencing analysis

Whole-exome sequencing (WES) was performed on 12 patients with recurrent meningiomas to identify key genetic drivers. Six experienced MT from WHO grade 1 or 2 to grade 3 due to increased mitotic activity and anaplasia, while the remaining six retained WHO grade 1 since diagnosis (Table S1). Genetic alterations and pathway-associated mutations were analyzed (Fig. 2a, Tables S5, S6).

Previously reported driver mutations were identified in four cases with NF2 mutations (13 tumor samples), one with smoothened receptor (SMO) mutations (four samples), one with RNA polymerase II subunit A (POLR2A) mutations (one sample), and one with AT-rich interaction domain 1B (ARID1B) mutations (four samples). These mutations were generally present from the early stages and persisted through recurrences, except for one case with a POLR2A mutation. Additional mutations affecting key pathways were detected in individual cases: salvador family WW domain–containing protein 1 (SAV1, Hippo pathway), glioma tumor suppressor candidate region gene 1 (GLTSCR1, SWI/SNF complex), integrator complex subunit 1 (INTS1, RNA polymerase), and contactin 2 (CNTN2, SHH pathway). No pathogenic germline variants were identified.

To clarify longitudinal changes within each recurrence type, we next evaluated genomic alterations over the course of recurrence in the MT and GR groups. According to the 2021 WHO classification, CDKN2A homozygous deletions or TERTp mutations define WHO grade 3 meningiomas. In our study, five of six histologically malignant cases harbored hemizygous or homozygous deletions of CDKN2A, four of which were associated with post-MT. CDKN2A deletion was significantly more frequent in MT cases (p = 0.0004), suggesting a pivotal role in malignant progression. To assess whether molecularly defined MT aligns with histological classification, we performed Sanger sequencing of the TERTp region. In one GR case, a TERTp mutation (C228T) emerged at recurrence, suggesting molecular MT (Fig. S1). Notably, this case also carried a CDKN2A hemizygous deletion. In contrast, only three of the six histological MT cases met the molecular criteria for MT, suggesting that histological progression may involve additional or alternative mechanisms. Therefore, subsequent analyses focused on changes unique to histological MT cases.

We next analyzed tumor mutation burden (TMB) across recurrences. The mean somatic mutation count was 19.4 per sample, with an average TMB of 0.32, placing meningiomas among the lowest across The Cancer Genome Atlas Program (TCGA) tumor types, consistent with prior reports (Fig. S2). Nevertheless, TMB significantly increased upon recurrence in MT cases (p = 0.0025; Fig. 2a, b).

CNAs were also assessed using WES data (Fig. 2a). MT cases exhibited more chromosomal CNAs even at the pre-malignant stage, frequently showing 22q and 1p deletions. Notably, all grade-progressive tumors showed co-deletion of 22q/1p. Recurrent deletions of 10q and 14 were also enriched in MT cases. Focusing on MT-associated CNAs, 9p deletions encompassing CDKN2A and CDKN2B were detected in four of six grade-progression cases. To quantify genomic instability, we calculated a score based on the proportion of regions affected by CNAs. This score significantly increased after MT (p = 0.0004, Fig. 2c), indicating CNA accumulation before recurrence and further escalation during MT.

Transcriptome analysis by RNA sequencing

To investigate gene expression alterations in recurrent meningiomas, we performed bulk RNA sequencing. One sample (case 5, T3; Table S1) was excluded due to poor mapping quality, leaving 35 samples from 12 patients for analysis. To capture the overall transcriptomic landscape, we first performed hierarchical clustering and PCA using all tumor samples. Hierarchical clustering of the top 1,000 most variable genes revealed two major clusters, with most MT tumors—particularly those post-MT—grouping together (Fig. 3a). In contrast, GR cases clustered stably, whereas MT cases showed marked transcriptomic shifts, indicating substantial molecular changes during malignant progression. PCA supported these findings, showing distinct expression profiles in post-MT tumors (Fig. 3b).

We next assessed longitudinal expression changes within each recurrence type. Differentially expressed gene (DEG) analysis revealed minimal changes in GR cases (52 upregulated, 17 downregulated), whereas MT cases exhibited large-scale transcriptomic reprogramming, with 1,940 upregulated and 518 downregulated genes (Fig. 3c). Gene Ontology (GO) and pathway enrichment analyses revealed that cell cycle–related genes were significantly upregulated in post-MT tumors (p = 2.7 × 10–24, Fig. 3d). Transcription factor analysis identified FOXM1-related genes, which regulate cell proliferation and the cell cycle, as significantly upregulated (p = 5.0 × 10–25, Fig. 3d). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) using the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) confirmed enrichment in cell cycle checkpoint regulation (p = 0.008, q = 0.031) and in target genes of E2F, a transcription factor involved in cell cycle progression (p = 0.015, q = 0.027) (Fig. S3). No significant gene clusters were identified when comparing primary and recurrent tumors in GR cases.

DNA methylation analysis

Genome-wide DNA methylation profiling was performed on 36 samples from 12 patients. To evaluate global methylation patterns, we first performed unsupervised clustering and PCA using all tumor samples. Clustering based on the top 10,000 most variable probes divided the samples into two distinct groups, clearly separating MT from GR cases (Fig. 4a). Unlike gene expression clustering, samples from the same patient consistently grouped together, indicating that DNA methylation patterns are largely case-specific. PCA further confirmed stable methylation profiles within each case, irrespective of MT or GR status (Fig. S4a). Based on the findings from clustering and PCA, we next analyzed alterations within and between the MT and GR groups. DMP analysis revealed no significant changes in GR cases, whereas MT cases showed substantial alterations, with 1,478 hypomethylated and 305 hypermethylated probes. However, GO analysis of DMPs did not yield any significantly enriched gene sets at a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 (Fig. S4b). A comparison of all tumors from MT versus GR cases revealed significant methylation differences at H3K27me3- and polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2)–associated sites (Fig. 4b). Additionally, DMR analysis comparing tumors before and after MT identified multiple regions, including several mapping to HOX genes (Supplementary Table S7). Correspondingly, HOX gene expression—regulated by PRC2—was upregulated in post-MT tumors (Fig. 4c).

FOXM1 overexpression and MTs

Given that earlier analyses indicated upregulation of cell proliferation-related genes in MT, we further investigated specific gene expression patterns and associated gene sets longitudinally within each recurrence type. Notably, FOXM1 and its transcriptional targets were significantly upregulated in post-MT tumors, with FOXM1 expression progressively increasing alongside meningioma grade advancement (Fig. 5a). TPM values for FOXM1 significantly increased following MT (p = 0.001, Fig. S5). Expression of FOXM1 target genes was particularly elevated in cases progressing from WHO grade 1 to grade 3 (Fig. 5b). To validate these transcriptomic findings, we performed immunohistochemistry (IHC) on tissue from 10 cases with MT and 25 with GR. IHC confirmed a significant increase in FOXM1 protein expression after grade progression, whereas GR cases showed no significant change (p = 4.0 × 10–5, Fig. 5c, d). We next investigated the relationship between transcription factor activation and enhancer hypomethylation during histological progression. The analysis revealed a strong correlation between FOXM1 overexpression and hypomethylated enhancer regions in MT tumors (Fig. 5e), suggesting an epigenetic mechanism contributing to FOXM1 activation. Additionally, oncogenic mutations and CNAs, as identified by WES, were predominantly enriched in the retinoblastoma (RB) signaling pathway, particularly in tumors undergoing MT (Figs. 5f, S6a). This prompted further evaluation of E2F, a key transcription factor downstream of the RB pathway and a major regulator of cell cycle progression. Expression of E2F target genes was significantly altered in MT cases, particularly in those progressing from WHO grade 1 to grade 3 (Fig. S6b). Consistent with this, cell cycle checkpoint–associated genes were also upregulated in MT tumors (Fig. 5f).

Association between meningioma molecular classification and MT

Previous studies have classified meningiomas using comprehensive molecular profiling. To assess how these classifications relate to recurrence, we analyzed our case series to determine longitudinal shifts in each patient. We first examined the relationship with DNA methylation–based classifications.

Notably, four of six patients with histological MT transitioned into a malignant methylation class (Fig. 6a), suggesting that DNA methylation profiles—used to define molecular subtypes—can change substantially during histological progression. However, only one patient was initially classified with a truly benign methylation profile. This implies that DNA methylation abnormalities likely arise early in tumor evolution, before the onset of overt MT, similar to the early appearance of chromosomal CNAs. By contrast, in GR cases, the methylation class assigned at the primary tumor remained stable at recurrence, indicating the absence of class transition. From a gene expression perspective, we next analyzed molecular subtypes in each case using previously defined gene sets via GSVA. Primary GR tumors retained gene expression signatures associated with favorable prognosis, including those related to immune response and angiogenesis (Fig. S7a). Conversely, MT cases exhibited elevated expression of gene sets linked to cell proliferation and metabolism. In particular, genes associated with cell cycle progression were markedly upregulated following MT, indicating a transition to a more aggressive molecular group as predicted by the expression signature. (Figs. 6b, S7b). Among these, the expression of minichromosome maintenance complex component 2 (MCM2), a known marker of aggressive meningiomas, showed a significant increase in paired comparisons between pre- and post-MT tumors (p = 0.001, Figs. 6c, S7c). These findings indicate that gene expression profiles, particularly those involved in cell proliferation pathways, are significantly altered following MT, reflecting dynamic changes in tumor biology. Molecular alterations in MT extend beyond genomic changes such as CNAs, which tend to precede histological grade progression, and include epigenetic and transcriptomic changes that contribute to distinct patterns of branched clonal evolution (Figs. 6d, S8). Although tumor progression begins before detectable MT, molecular classifications can shift throughout this process, reinforcing the utility of molecular prognostic models even for WHO grade 1 tumors. Therefore, rather than relying on isolated molecular features, a comprehensive and integrative molecular approach is essential to fully elucidate the mechanisms underlying meningioma progression (Fig. S9).

Discussion

Our study highlights the molecular alterations associated with meningioma recurrence, with a focus on MT. While large-scale genetic analyses have begun to elucidate the landscape of aggressive meningiomas [12, 45, 46, 50, 59], the molecular pathways driving histological MT remain poorly understood, largely because only a limited number of studies have analyzed serial tumor samples from the same patients [8, 37, 50, 59]. By examining matched recurrent specimens, we found that chromosomal CNAs often precede histological progression. Transcriptional dysregulation of cell proliferation and cell cycle pathways, particularly involving FOXM1 and E2F-related gene clusters, becomes increasingly prominent during MT. In contrast, tumors that retained WHO grade 1 throughout recurrence exhibited minimal molecular abnormalities, consistent with their indolent behavior. Our integrated analysis of DNA methylation, RNA expression, and genomic alterations across serial recurrences provides a longitudinal view of molecular evolution in meningioma.

NF2 gene alterations are more common in malignant meningiomas than in benign ones [14], whereas non-NF2 mutations, such as TRAF7, AKT1, SMO, KLF4, and POLR2A, are typically associated with grade 1 tumors and favorable outcomes [1, 14, 15]. These mutations persist with minor variations during recurrence [8, 37], and our findings confirm the stability of major driver mutations across recurrent samples.

These genetic alterations often correlate with anatomical site and histological subtype and may carry prognostic value [70]. However, NF2 -mutant meningiomas show greater heterogeneity in location and clinical behavior, underscoring the need for refined molecular subclassification [45, 71].

CDKN2A/2B deletion, especially homozygous loss, is a hallmark of WHO grade 3 meningiomas under the 2021 WHO classification [6, 63, 68, 69]. Consistently, most MT cases in our cohort harbored CDKN2A deletions. Meningiomas with NF2 mutations and high CNA burdens are prone to recurrence and exhibit distinct DNA methylation and transcriptomic profiles [23, 25]. MT is frequently associated with 1p loss and deletions of chromosomes 6, 10, and 14 [8, 16, 25, 59, 68], and the occurrence of NF2 loss and 1p deletion portends poor prognosis [34, 50]. Our MT cases universally showed NF2 inactivation, 1p loss, and elevated CNA burdens, alongside increasing TMB with grade progression. These findings support prior evidence that genomic instability intensifies during MT [8, 23, 45].

TERT promoter (TERTp) mutations (C228T and C250T) are associated with poor prognosis and are classified as WHO grade 3 under current criteria [41, 58, 65]. Both TERTp mutations and TERT methylation anomalies may drive MT [54], and their prognostic value appears independent of histological grade, suggesting the existence of NF2-agnostic molecular pathways [69]. Interestingly, one of our grade 1 cases acquired a TERTp mutation without histological progression, indicating that TERTp mutations do not always coincide with MT.

Upregulation of transcription factors such as FOXM1 and E2F is a recognized feature of aggressive meningiomas and recurrence risk, regardless of grade [45, 50, 51, 67]. However, most previous studies analyzed only one sample per patient, limiting insight into dynamic expression changes during recurrence or MT [29]. We demonstrated that the expression of FOXM1, E2F, and their downstream targets significantly increases during MT. CNAs affecting the RB pathway, including CDKN2A /2B deletion, may accumulate during MT, promoting cell cycle progression. FOXM1 overexpression, potentially driven by RB and p53 pathway dysregulation, may facilitate proliferation in transforming meningiomas [5, 31]. In contrast, GR tumors showed few DEGs, supporting the idea that “benign” recurrences reflect residual disease rather than molecular progression. This aligns with previous findings that meningiomas with low Ki-67 indices tend to recur indolently [42]. These distinctions highlight the need for molecularly tailored therapeutic strategies.

Genome-wide DNA methylation dysregulation is also an important prognostic marker [59]. Malignant meningiomas often exhibit hypermethylation at PRC2 and H3K27me3 target sites [16, 25], and H3K27me3 loss by immunostaining correlates with poor prognosis suggesting, suggesting the role of histone-related epigenomic alterations in progression and malignancy [7, 21, 27, 28]. HOX gene dysregulation, affecting H3K27me3 regulation, has also been observed in malignant methylation classes [49]. Our data show transcriptional dysregulation of HOX genes following MT. Although we did not identify specific methylation changes linked to recurrence or MT, our results suggest that methylation alterations likely precede recurrence. Further validation in larger cohorts is needed to confirm these findings.

Recent multimodal studies have proposed molecular classification schemes to predict meningioma prognosis independent of WHO grading [12, 45, 50, 59]. While more accurate, these schemes typically rely on single-sample analyses, which may not capture temporal molecular shifts. Some studies suggest DNA methylation and gene expression profiles remain stable across recurrences [50, 59], but this remains unclear. We found that molecular classification can shift with MT. Using the DKFZ classifier [59], we observed that four of six patients transitioned to a malignant class following MT. Similarly, pathway analyses revealed classification changes; for instance, MT cases transitioned from molecular group 3 to group 4 in the scheme proposed by Nassiri et al. [45]. These findings suggest that intermediate categories—such as the DKFZ intermediate class and Nassiri’s group 3—may serve as early indicators of tumors at risk for MT. Although cross-system comparisons are limited, our results indicate that molecular subtypes evolve with histological progression. This underscores the importance of identifying reliable early-stage biomarkers of malignant.

Our study has some limitations. The small sample size restricts the generalizability of our findings, particularly regarding the full molecular heterogeneity of meningiomas. Additionally, not all MT cases arose from WHO grade 1 (benign) tumors, complicating comparisons across progression stages. Moreover, WHO grade 1 meningiomas are genetically diverse, and the MT and GR cohorts may harbor different driver mutation profiles, limiting the validity of direct cross-sectional comparisons. Notably, one GR case harbored a TERTp mutation together with a hemizygous CDKN2A deletion, suggesting molecular malignant progression despite its histological classification as WHO grade 1. As TERTp-driven progression may represent an independent pathway from histological grade [41], we did not include this case in the histological MT group in our analyses. Furthermore, we acknowledge that the number of patients included in the molecular analysis (n = 12) was limited. Because of this small cohort size, our study did not include de novo WHO grade 3 meningiomas, preventing us from determining whether the observed alterations are transformation-specific or general features of grade 3 biology. In a prior study, an anaplastic meningioma subgroup enriched in progressive tumors, characterized by dysregulation of EMT networks and PRC2 activity including HOX genes, showed poorer prognosis than the de novo–enriched subgroup [16], highlighting the need to clarify how secondary MT biologically differs from de novo WHO grade 3 tumors. The availability of paired recurrent meningioma samples suitable for multi-omics profiling is inherently rare, and previous studies have similarly relied on small cohorts. Nevertheless, our results suggest that meningioma progression often follows a sequential molecular trajectory, especially at the genomic and transcriptomic levels. Consistent with that in prior reports, features such as FOXM1/E2F activation, CDKN2A deletion, and CNA accumulation were observed [6, 12, 25, 45, 49, 50, 67]; our contribution is to demonstrate their stepwise acquisition in longitudinally paired samples. We therefore view our study as an important exploratory step.

Another limitation relates to prior radiation exposure. In our cohort, all MT cases received radiotherapy—predominantly stereotactic radiosurgery—before histologic confirmation of MT, raising the possibility that radiation exposure may have influenced the transformation trajectory. Prior work shows that radiation-associated meningiomas often harbor NF2 structural variants and mutational signatures linked to defects in homologous recombination repair [49]. While a previous report suggested that radiosurgery did not increase the incidence of MT [44], we cannot exclude radiation effects as a contributor to the alterations observed here, given the treatment timing in our series. Addressing this will require future analyses in radiation-naïve cohorts to disentangle intrinsic progression from radiation-related effects.

A further limitation is that our analyses relied on bulk WES, RNA-seq, and DNA methylation profiling, which cannot resolve tumor-intrinsic versus microenvironmental contributions to malignant progression. Recent single-cell analyses have revealed extensive intratumoral heterogeneity, dynamic evolutionary trajectories, and immune–stromal interactions in recurrent meningiomas [33, 39], underscoring the value of these approaches in refining transformation trajectories in future work. Despite these limitations, our study offers valuable insights into the alterations associated with secondary WHO grade 3 MT.

Finally, our study did not establish predictive biomarkers of malignant transformation. This limitation stems from the factors noted above. Our longitudinal design was intended to highlight sequential molecular changes within paired tumors rather than to define predictive classifiers. Future studies with larger, multi-institutional cohorts will be required to identify reliable predictive features of malignant transformation.

In conclusion, we delineate key molecular events underlying histological MT in meningiomas. Genomic instability—emerges as a central feature of tumor evolution. While our study does not establish predictive biomarkers, it provides exploratory evidence that cell cycle dysregulation—particularly FOXM1 activation and RB pathway disruption, accumulates during progression. These findings lay the groundwork for future studies aimed at developing predictive models, which will require comprehensive analyses in larger, multi-institutional cohorts.
