CuO stabilized on magnetic decorated GO nanosheets for efficient synthesis of 3,4-dihydropyrimidine-2(1H)-one/thione and 1,4-dihydropyridine derivatives under green conditions
Authors: Mohammad G Dekamin, Mohammad Eslami, Elham Mahdavi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study presents a green, efficient and sustainable method for synthesizing biologically active 3,4-dihydropyrimidine-2(1H)-one/thione (3,4-DHPMs/3,4-DHPMTs) and 1,4-dihydropyridine (1,4-DHPs) heterocycles. The protocol involves a one-pot, three-component condensation of aldehydes, 1,3-dicarbonyl compounds, and urea or ammonium carbonate, catalyzed by heterogeneous CuO stabilized on magnetic decorated GO nanosheets (CuO/mGO-TA-Me-BTA) nanocomposite. The method adheres to green chemistry principles, offering advantages such as short reaction times, high yields, easy work-up, and the use of a recyclable and low-cost catalyst. The catalyst demonstrated remarkable stability and could be reused up to six times without significant loss of its activity. The CuO/mGO-TA-Me-BTA nanocomposite was extensively characterized using Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, X-ray diffraction (XRD), field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM), energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) spectroscopy, EDX elemental mapping, thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), atomic absorption spectroscopy (AAS), and vibrating sample magnetometry (VSM).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-34312-9.Subject terms: Cancer imaging, Cancer therapy, Chemical libraries, Small molecules, Drug screening, Medicinal chemistry, Pharmaceutics, Chemical biology, Drug discovery, Cardiology, Molecular medicine, Chemistry, Catalysis, Chemical biology, Coordination chemistry, Green chemistry, Inorganic chemistry, Materials chemistry, Medicinal chemistry, Organic chemistry, Physical chemistry, Supramolecular chemistry, Surface chemistry, Chemical synthesis, Materials science, Materials for devices, Materials for energy and catalysis, Nanoscale materials, Techniques and instrumentation, Nanoscience and technology, Graphene, Nanoscale devices, Nanoscale materials, Other nanotechnology, Techniques and instrumentation

Introduction

Nanoscience opens up new frontiers across disciplines such as chemistry, biology, physics, and engineering by enabling precise control over matter at the atomic scale. A key breakthrough from nanoscience is the creation of nanocatalysts1,2. Operating at the nanoscale, nanocatalysts offer enhanced catalytic performance, mainly due to their increased surface area and modified electronic properties, resulting in faster and more efficient chemical reactions. These catalysts play a pivotal role in industrial processes, environmental protection, and energy technologies. Hence, they help to develop more sustainable production methods, reduce pollution, and improve energy conversion systems such as in fuel cells and hydrogen production. Indeed, the integration of nanoscience and catalysis science and technology is revolutionizing industries, providing high-performance solutions that are both economical- and environmentally-benign3–6. On the other hand, graphene oxide (GO) is a highly effective support as well as nanocatalyst due to its high surface area, abundant oxygen-containing functional groups, good dispersion in polar solvents, moderate electron mobility, thermal and chemical stability, tunable surface properties, and environmental compatibility, making it valuable in green chemistry, energy production, and environmental remediation. Generally, GO-based nanomaterials demonstrate exceptional catalytic potential for synthesizing pharmacologically active nitrogen-heterocycles. In these systems, benzotriazole stabilizes CuO, through nitrogen coordination, effectively to reduce Cu(II) species leaching and nanoparticles aggregation. Therefore, the CuO stabilized on magnetic decorated GO nanosheets (mGO/CuO/Fe₃O₄) hybrid nanomaterial enhances its performance through GO’s conductive sp2 network that facilitates Cu redox cycle, suppresses charge recombination in photocatalysis, and incorporates Fe₃O₄ for magnetic recovery – collectively improving catalytic efficiency, stability, and recyclability7–9.

Nitrogen-containing heterocycles, especially 3,4-DHPM and 1,4-DHP have attracted considerable attention due to their versatile pharmacological activities10–15. They serve as important scaffolds in drug design and discovery16–18. Also, they have gained considerable attention due to their important role in the field of pharmaceuticals and medicines as well as their role in the synthesis of DNA and RNA19,20. Their ability to inhibit calcium influx through L-type calcium channels in cardiac and smooth muscle cells, makes them valuable therapeutic agents for managing cardiovascular diseases. Several 3,4-DHPM/3,4-DHPMTs and 1,4-DHPs derivatives have been explored for their potential therapeutic applications including as antihypertensive21, antihyperglycemic22, anti-inflammatory agents23, antimicrobial agents24, anticancer agents25, analgesic26, antibacterial27, calcium channel blockers (CCBs)28, antitubercular activity, and antiviral agents29. Exploring the detailed structures of the 3,4-DHPMs/3,4-DHPMTs and 1,4-DHPs uncovers valuable insights into their essential contributions to the complex molecular design of various medicinal compounds10,30. As illustrated in Fig. 1, (S)-L-771688 A, nitractin B, idoxuridine C, (R)-SQ 32,926 D, emivirine E, 5-Fluorouracil F, (S)-monastrol G, fluorostrol H, piperastrol I, nifedipine, amlodipine, nimodipine, cilnidipine, and nicardipine are widely recognized as significant members of the 3,4-DHPM/3,4-DHPMTs and 1,4-DHPs classes of pharmaceutical compounds.

3,4-DHPMs/3,4-DHPMTs and 1,4-DHPs derivatives can be synthesized through multicomponent reactions through the Biginelli and Hantzsch named reactions, respectively. Due to the wide range of therapeutic properties, scientists have been attracted to develop synthetic methods for these types of scaffolds30–32. In this context, multicomponent reactions (MCRs) have garnered significant attention for their ability to afford a diverse array of complex molecules with high efficiency, swiftness, step-economy, cost-effectiveness, and eco-friendliness33–38. The literature review reveals that a range of synthetic protocols, employing both heterogeneous and homogeneous catalysts, have been developed for the synthesis of 3,4-DHPMs/3,4-DHPMTs and 1,4-DHPs derivatives. These protocols commonly revolve around a three-component reaction involving aldehydes, β-ketoesters, and urea/thiourea or ammonia, respectively.

Magnetic nanocatalysts have emerged as highly efficient catalytic systems in organic reactions39,40, particularly in the Biginelli and Hantzsch reactions, showcasing remarkable catalytic performance, high conversion rates, selectivity, and recyclability. Their magnetic properties allow for easy separation and recovery using an external magnetic field, simplifying purification, reducing waste, and which enables multiple reuses without significant loss of activity, thus offering economic and environmental benefits41–44. Collaborations among chemists, materials scientists, and engineers may further advance the integration of magnetic nanocatalysts into cutting-edge catalytic systems for broader applications in organic synthesis and beyond. Given the abundance of review reports that cover diverse catalysts utilized in the Biginelli and Hantzsch reactions, ranging from traditional Lewis acids to modern organocatalysts and innovative nanocatalytic systems, we will focus on providing a comprehensive overview of applied various catalysts45–48.

Despite prior endeavors, chemists persistently innovate in the realm of organic reactions to tackle pressing challenges, unveil new synthetic pathways, and propel advancements in fields that span from pharmaceuticals to materials science. In line with our focus on crafting efficient magnetic nanocatalysts, we designed a CuO-based CuO/mGO-TA-Me-BTA, as a non-toxic magnetic nanocatalyst, for the synthesis of nitrogen-containing heterocyclic compounds via the Biginelli and Hantzsch reactions, all within the framework of green chemistry principles (Fig. 2)121–131. In this study, we successfully achieved several critical objectives of sustainable synthesis including green reaction conditions (utilizing mild and eco-friendly media), high product yields with exceptional selectivity, facile catalyst separation enabled by magnetic recovery, significantly reduced reaction times due to enhanced catalytic efficiency, and the ability to synthesize diverse products-demonstrating the system’s versatility and practical applicability.

Experimental

Materials

All starting materials, reagents and solvents are commercially available and were purchased from Merck or Aldrich and used without further purification, except for benzaldehyde, which was used as a fresh distilled sample. 1H NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker (Avance DRX-500) spectrometer using CDCl3 as the solvent at room temperature. Chemical shifts (δ) are reported in parts per million (ppm) relative to tetramethylsilane. Fourier Transform infrared (FTIR) spectra of the samples were measured using an ABB Bomem MB100 FTIR spectrophotometer. Scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) was performed on VEGA\\TESCAN-LMU. An energy dispersive detector (EDS) coupled to the microscope was used to identify chemical elements of the prepared catalyst. X-Ray diffraction (XRD) pattern was recorded on APD 2000, using Cu Kα radiation (50 kV, 150 mA) in the range of 2θ = 10–120°. The amounts of Cu(II) species inside the structure of CuO/mGO-TA-Me-BTA was determined by atomic absorption spectroscopy (AAS) analysis on a AA-6300 SHIMADZU. The thermal properties of the prepared nanoparticles were determined using differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) on the TA-LA model of Pishtaz Engineering Co device. The magnetic properties of nanoparticles were also measured using vibrating sample magnetometer (VSM) on a VSM MDKB device.

Synthesis of graphene oxide (GO)

The graphene oxide (GO) sheets were prepared by a modified Hummers´ method according to the procedure reported in the literature132. Initially, 3.0 g of sodium nitrate (NaNO₃) was dissolved in 140 ml of concentrated sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄, 98%) in an ice bath. Then, 15.0 g of potassium permanganate (KMnO₄) and 3.0 g of graphite powder were gradually added to the mixture under continuous stirring. The reaction mixture was magnetically stirred for 2 h while maintaining the temperature below 20 °C. Subsequently, the temperature was raised to 35 °C, and the mixture was stirred for an additional 12 h. After this period, 250 ml of deionized water was slowly added, followed by 30 min. of stirring. Next, 20 ml of H₂O₂ (35%) and 800 ml of distilled water were introduced, resulting in a noticeable color change from brown to bright yellow. To further purify the product, a solution of concentrated HCl (37%, 8 ml) in deionized water (10 ml) was added dropwise under stirring. Finally, the synthesized graphene oxide was allowed to settle, the supernatant was decanted, and the remaining GO slurry was dried in an oven at 80 °C. Then, an aqueous precursor solution was prepared by dissolving FeCl₃·6H₂O (4 mmol) and FeCl₂·4H₂O (2 mmol) in 50 ml deionized water, followed by pH adjustment to 4.0 using NaOH (1.0 M). Separately, graphene oxide (GO, 27.5 mg) was dispersed in water (20 ml) to form a stable suspension. This GO suspension was then slowly introduced into the iron precursor solution under continuous stirring for 30 min. The mixture’s pH was subsequently raised to 10.0 through dropwise addition of NaOH solution (1.0 M). After 1 h of vigorous stirring at alkaline conditions, the black magnetic precipitate was collected using an external magnet, thoroughly washed with deionized water and EtOH (five cycles each), and finally dried at 70 °C in a convection oven to obtain the final magnetic GO nanocomposite133.

Synthesis of benzotriazole (BTA)

O-phenylenediamine (5.4 g) was dissolved in a mixture containing glacial AcOH (6.0 ml) and deionized water (20 ml). The solution was then cooled to 15 °C. A separate solution of sodium nitrite (3.0 g) dissolved in water (7 ml), was then gradually introduced into the reaction mixture. Upon addition, a noticeable color change from deep red to pale brown was observed. The reaction mixture was further cooled in an ice bath for 30 min. The resulting benzotriazole product was isolated through filtration and subsequently washed with cold deionized water to ensure its purity.

Synthesis of 1-(prop-2-yn-1-yl)-1H-benzo[d][1,2,3]triazole

In the subsequent step, the synthesized benzotriazole (2.0 mmol), propargyl bromide (2.2 mmol), potassium carbonate (4.0 mmol), and DMF (5.0 ml) were introduced into a 10 ml reaction flask. The mixture was stirred at 45 °C for 12 h. The reaction progress was monitored using thin-layer chromatography (TLC) using a solvent system of EtOAc and n-hexane (1:3). Upon completion of the reaction, the product, 1-(prop-2-yn-1-yl)-1H-benzo[d][1,2,3]triazole, was isolated through extraction with EtOAc and then dried in an oven to obtain the final product.

Synthesis of mGO-TA-Me-BTA

The grafting of 1-(prop-2-yn-1-yl)-1H-benzo[d][1,2,3]triazole onto the magnetic graphene oxide (m-GO) was carried out via the Hueisgen click reaction. Initially, m-GO (300.0 mg) was dispersed in 80 ml of deionized water via ultrasonication. To this dispersion, 1-(prop-2-yn-1-yl)-1H-benzo[d][1,2,3]triazole (10.0 mmol), sodium azide (15.0 mmol), sodium ascorbate (20 mol%), copper sulfate (0.5 mmol), and tert-butyl alcohol (20 ml) were added. The reaction mixture was stirred at 60 °C for 24 h. Upon completion of the reaction, the nanocomposite was isolated using an external magnet and thoroughly washed with distilled water (3 × 10 ml) and EtOH (1 × 10 ml), then dried at 50 °C for 2 h.

Synthesis of the CuO/mGO-TA-Me-BTA nano-catalyst

In a 250 ml reaction vessel, magnetic graphene oxide nanocomposite grafted with benzotriazole (mGO-TA-Me-BTA,1.0 g) was dispersed in deionized water (100 ml) through ultrasonication. To this dispersion, CuSO4 (3.0 mmol) dissolved in deionized water (25 ml) was added and the obtained mixture was stirred at room temperature for 24 h. Afterward, the nanocomposite was isolated using an external magnet, washed with distilled water, and dried in a vacuum. The structure of the synthesized nanocomposite (CuO/mGO-TA-Me-BTA) was characterized by FTIR, XRD, FESEM, EDX, elemental mapping, TGA, VSM, and AAS analysis.

One-pot three-component synthesis of the 3,4-dihydropyrimidine-2(1H)-one/thione derivatives (4a-w) in the presence of CuO/mGO-TA-Me-BTA magnetic nanocomposite

For the synthesis of 3,4-dihydropyrimidine-2(1H)-one/thione derivatives, a mixture of aldehyde (1.0 mmol), urea/thiourea (1.1 mmol), ethyl acetoacetate (1.0 mmol) and the CuO/mGO-TA-Me-BTA nanocomposite (10.0 mg) was added to distilled water-EtOH (2.0 ml, v/v 3:1) in a 5 ml round-bottom flask. The reaction mixture was stirred using a magnetic stirrer and heated under reflux conditions in an oil bath for the required time, as indicated in Table 2. The progress of the reaction was monitored by thin-layer chromatography (TLC) using a 1:3 n-hexane:EtOAc solvent system. Upon completion of the reaction, the magnetic nanocatalyst was separated using an external magnet, and the 3,4-dihydropyrimidine-2(1H)-one/thione product was isolated by extraction with EtOAc. If necessary, the products were crystallized from a mixture of EtOAc-n-hexane or EtOAc:n-hexane. The final structures of the synthesized compounds were identified and confirmed by melting point (M.P) determination as well as 1H NMR and FTIR analysis.

For the synthesis of 1,4-dihydropyridine derivatives, a 5 ml round-bottom flask was charged with a mixture of aldehyde (1.0 mmol), ethyl acetoacetate (2.0 mmol), ammonium acetate (1.5 mmol), CuO/mGO-TA-Me-BTA nanocomposite (10.0 mg), and distilled water-EtOH (2.0 ml, v/v 3:1). After the reaction completion, the magnetic nanocatalyst was separated using an external magnet and the 1,4-dihydropyridine product was extracted with EtOAc. If needed, the products were crystallized from a mixture of EtOAc and n-hexane or EtOH. The final structures of the synthesized compounds were confirmed by melting point (M.P) determination as well as 1H NMR and FTIR spectroscopy.

Results and discussion

As shown in Fig. 3 and based on our previous experience, the synthesis pathway for the Cu(II) stabilized magnetic benzotriazole-grafted graphene oxide (CuO/mGO-TA-Me-BTA) nano-structured catalyst involves a series of sequential steps. Graphene oxide nanosheets were generated by oxidizing graphite using a modified Hummers’ method134–136. These nanosheets were subsequently been decorated with magnetic Fe3O4, leading to the formation of mGO. N-propargyl benzotriazole has been attached onto the pre-treated mGO, followed by further modification facilitated by the Huisgen 1,3-dipolar cycloaddition click reaction, culminating in the preparation of mGO-TA-Me-BTA. Lastly, CuSO4 was anchored to the surface of mGO-TA-Me-BTA through supramolecular coordination with the benzotriazole motif, exploiting its well-established effectiveness as a ligand for a diverse array of transition metals. The intriguing aspect of fixing copper sulfate onto the surface of mGO-TA-Me-BTA magnetic nanocomposite is the transformation that the nanoparticles undergo. Copper sulfate is deposited onto the nanoparticles, losing SO42− in the process and forming stable copper oxide. This transformation can occur as the nanocatalyst dries.

Step-by-step synthesis process of the supramolecular CuO/mGO-TA-Me-BTA nanocatalyst.

The synthesized CuO/mGO-TA-Me-BTA catalyst underwent comprehensive characterization to clarify its structural, morphological, and size-related attributes. Techniques including Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, X-ray diffraction (XRD), field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM), energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) spectrometry, elemental mapping, thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), atomic absorption spectroscopy (AAS), and vibrating sample magnetometry (VSM) were employed. For this purpose, Fig. 4 highlights the FTIR analysis of free graphene oxide (Fig. 4a), magnetic graphene oxide (Fig. 4b), benzotriazole (Fig. 4c), and the CuO/mGO-TA-Me-BTA nanocomposite (Fig. 4d). The broad signal observed at 3328 cm−1 corresponds to the stretching vibrations of hydroxyl groups on the magnetic graphene oxide surface. Additionally, the adsorption band at 570 cm1 signifies the characteristic Fe–O bond, confirming the presence of Fe3O4 magnetic nanoparticles in the CuO/mGO-TA-Me-BTA. Signals at 1207 and 1022 cm−1 are attributed to the stretching vibrations of C‒O and C‒OH groups, respectively. Moreover, sharp adsorption bands at 1735 and 1612 cm−1 are assigned to the C = O and C = C stretching vibrations of graphene oxide nano sheets. In the spectrum of 1-((1H-1,2,3-triazol-5-yl)methyl)-1H-benzo[d][1,2,3]triazole (Fig. 4c), new signals emerge at 1535 and 1396 cm−1, indicative of the C = C and C‒N stretching vibrations of the benzotriazole group. Additionally, adsorption bands at 3190 and 2920 cm−1 relate to the stretching vibrations of aromatic C‒H and aliphatic C‒H groups. These adsorption bands confirm the presence of 1-((1H-1,2,3-triazol-5-yl)methyl)-1H-benzo[d][1,2,3]triazoles as linkers on the mGO surface.

FTIR spectra of (a) GO, (b) mGO, and (c) BTA, and (d) the CuO/mGO-TA-Me-BTA.

In the next step, X-ray diffraction analysis was utilized to explore the crystalline structure, phase purity, and particle size of the CuO/mGO-TA-Me-BTA nanocatalyst, as illustrated in Fig. 5. The X-ray diffraction pattern of the copper-supported nanocatalyst was compared with reference cards for graphene oxide, Fe3O4, and copper oxide to facilitate comparison. In Fig. 5, the presence of two distinct peaks at 2θ = 8.8° (with a d-spacing of 1.00 nm) and 2θ = 26.7° (with a d-spacing of 0.33 nm) indicates the presence of crystallized graphene oxide. Additionally, peaks at 2θ = 30.8°, 35.7°, 45.3°, 55.4°, 57°, and 61.8° correspond to the (220), (311), (400), (422), (511), and (440) crystalline layers of Fe3O4 cubic spinel (as per JCPDS card No. 11-0614).130 This indicates the presence of highly crystalline magnetic Fe3O4 particles within the nanocatalyst structure, with no apparent diffraction peaks indicating the presence of hematite impurities. Additionally, the diffraction peaks observed at 2θ = 17.8°, 19.8°, 25.2°, 31.8°, 35.6°, 40°, and 53° can be attributed to the presence of immobilized CuO on the nano-composite (according to JCPDS card No. 5-0661).130 The average particle size, as determined by Scherrer’s equation, is approximately 15 nm.

FESEM analysis revealed that the CuO/mGO-TA-Me-BTA composite features nearly spherical nanoparticles evenly distributed across the GO sheets, as depicted in Fig. 6. The average nanoparticle size of 17 nm, determined from FESEM images, concurs well with the findings derived from XRD analysis.

Thermogravimetric/derivative thermal gravimetric analysis (TGA-DTG) was employed to evaluate the thermal stability of the synthesized nanocatalyst. As illustrated in Fig. S6, the TGA-DTG curves reveal four distinct stages of weight loss occurring within different temperature ranges: below 300 °C, 340–450 °C, 450–740 °C, and above 740 °C. During thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) for graphene oxide (GO), the initial weight gain is commonly attributed to the oxidation of double bonds situated on the basal plane of GO. This is followed by the absorption or adsorption of moisture through hydrogen bonding occurring at the both the edges and basal plane, or chemisorption via the ring opening-reaction of epoxide groups. It is apparent that the removal of these compounds from the GO surface led to a partial decrease in weight at temperatures below 300 °C. The subsequent step, occurring between approximately 340 °C and 450 °C, involves both the decomposition of the triazole motif and the generation of epoxy groups through the condensation of adjacent hydroxyl groups on the surface of the GO layers. Following this, the third stage occurs within the temperature range of 450–740 °C, primarily associated with the breakdown of carboxyl or epoxy functional groups. At temperatures attributed 740°C, the ultimate step of weight reduction occurs, primarily attributed to the thermal degradation of the carbon framework of graphene oxide nanosheets. Additionally, it involves the condensation of hydroxyl groups present on the surface of Fe3O4, and potentially, any residual CuO components. Moreover, apart from thermal analysis, DTG analysis also indicates the steps of weight loss. The DTG analysis highlights a significant drop in weight during the last stage, at a temperature of 740 °C.

In the next step, the magnetic properties of Fe3O4-GO nanosheets and CuO/mGO-TA-Me-BTA nanocatalyst were examined at room temperature using the VSM technique (Fig. S7, see SI). The hysteresis curves reveal that the maximum saturation magnetization values are 44.45 emu/g for Fe3O4-GO and 34.22 emu/g for CuO/mGO-TA-Me-BTA. The lower saturation magnetization value for CuO/mGO-TA-Me-BTA is attributed to the surface modifications and the inclusion of non-magnetic elements such as BTA and CuO on the catalyst’s surface. Additionally, both magnetization curves show a decrease from the plateau state to zero after removal of the magnetic field, indicating typical superparamagnetic behavior.

Ultimately, to determine the amount of Cu(II) loaded on the CuO/mGO-TA-Me-BTA nanocomposite, atomic absorption spectroscopy (AAS) elemental analysis was conducted. The analysis indicated that the copper concentration on the magnetic substrate was 0.1 mg/liter. From this concentration, it was calculated that the CuO loading on the magnetic substrate amounts to 0.32 mmol/g.

The performed analysis indicates that our objective in designing magnetic nanocatalysts is consistently achieved. In the following the synthesis of various 3,4-DHPMs/3,4-DHPMTs and 1,4-DHPs skeleton derivatives in the presence of CuO/mGO-TA-Me-BTA nanocomposite was investigated to demonstrate its efficacy. In this context, the three-component condensation involving benzaldehyde 1g (1.0 mmol), ethyl acetoacetate 2a (1.0 mmol), and urea 3a (1.2 mmol) was selected as the model reaction. Various parameters including solvent type, temperature, and catalyst loading were examined. Initially, to determine the best solvent, a systematic investigation of the model reaction was examined in the presence of various solvents including H2O, EtOH, H2O:EtOH (1:3), H2O:EtOH (3:1), ethylacetoacetate, acetonitrile, and dimethylformamide as well as under solvent-free conditions, with 5.0 mg of catalyst, at room temperature (Entries 1–8). As usual, the progress of the model reaction was monitored by TLC using a 3:1 mixture of hexane:EtOAc. As summarized in Table 1, the model reaction under ambient temperature conditions did not yield a high amount of the desired product after 2 h, and extending the reaction time did not significantly improve the yield. However, the H2O:EtOH mixture (3:1) (Entry 3) showed better performance compared with the other solvents. One of the important reasons for the better performance of the designed nanocatalyst in aqueous solvent is the high dispersibility of graphene oxide nanosheets in water. Graphene oxide (GO) exhibits excellent dispersibility due to its hydrophilic nature, with oxygen-containing functional groups such as hydroxyl, epoxy, and carboxyl groups increasing its affinity for water molecules. This enables GO to form stable a colloidal suspension without the need for additional stabilizing agents. The good dispersibility and colloidal stability of GO in water significantly enhance the efficiency and effectiveness of the nanocatalyst in aqueous environments. Moreover, ethanol’s capacity to efficiently dissolve organic compounds facilitates the rapid formation of the desired product. In the next experiments, to enhance the yield of the desired product, the reaction model in the solvent of H2O:EtOH under reflux conditions were investigated (Entry 9). Under reflux conditions, as expected, a satisfactory yield (58%) of the product was obtained within a 30-min timeframe. The model reaction was also repeated with different amounts of 7.5, 10, and 12.5 mg of CuO/mGO-TA-Me-BTA under reflux temperature conditions (Entries 10–12). Ultimately, 10.0 mg was determined as the most effective amount of catalyst (Entry 11). Increasing the catalyst amount from 5.0 to 10.0 mg resulted in a significant enhancement of the reaction yield from 58 to 90% and reduced the reaction time from 30 min. to 15 min. (Entries 10–11). However, increasing the catalyst amount from 10.0 to 12.5 mg did not show any significant impact on the reaction yield (Entries 11–12). It is noteworthy that altering other conditions did not significantly impact the reaction yield and reaction time. The results indicated that the magnetic part of the catalyst had a little influence on the catalytic activity, with the active site of the catalyst for the model reaction being copper oxide (Entries 13–14). Also, an acceptable yield of the desired product was not observed in the absence of catalyst (Entry 15).

As seen in Tables 2 and 3, the influence of substituents on the phenyl ring is crucial for determining the reaction rate. The nature of these substituents plays a significant role; aromatic aldehydes with electron-withdrawing groups such as nitro and halogens facilitate a faster reaction compared to those with electron-donating groups such as methyl, hydroxy and methoxy. In the case of substituents at the ortho- position on the phenyl ring, steric hindrance has a significant impactful on the reaction time and yield than the electronic nature of the substituent.

Reaction mechanisms illustrate the catalyst’s roles in providing alternative pathways with lower activation energies, enhancing efficiency, selectivity, and reducing by-products. Understanding these mechanisms is essential for designing green and sustainable catalytic systems. Therefore, the following section will examine the mechanism of the 3,4-dihydropyrimidine-2(1H)-one/thione derivative synthesis in the presence of CuO/mGO-TA-Me-BTA nanocomposite. As shown in Fig. 9, the synthesis of 3,4-dihydropyrimidine-2(1H)-one/thione begins with the activation of aldehyde carbonyl groups (1) by the Lewis acidic copper(II) sites on the CuO/mGO-TA-Me-BTA catalyst. This activation, facilitated by CuO active sites, creates an electrophilic center, which is subsequently attacked by the nitrogen atoms in urea or thiourea (3a-b). The nucleophilic attack, involving the lone electron pairs of nitrogen, followed by dehydration, forms an imine intermediate (9). In the next step, CuO plays a critical role in transforming the keto form of ethyl acetoacetate into its active enol form (2'). The nucleophilic enol then adds to the imine intermediate (9), resulting in the formation of intermediate 10. The final step involves an intramolecular nucleophilic attack by the amide nitrogens of urea or thiourea on the activated carbonyl group of ethyl acetoacetate, a process facilitated by CuO. This reaction, followed by dehydration, leads to the formation of the target 3,4-dihydropyrimidine-2(1H)-one/thione derivatives (4a-w)137.

Proposed mechanism for the synthesis of 3,4-dihydropyrimidine-2(1H)-one/thione derivatives in the presence of CuO/mGO-TA-Me-BTA nanocomposite.

In the proposed mechanism, the roles of electron-donating and electron-withdrawing groups in accelerating the reaction are evident. Aromatic aldehydes with electron-withdrawing groups at the ortho- or para- positions show high reactivity in forming intermediates 8 and 9. These groups reduce the electron density on the carbonyl carbon, facilitating and speeding up the nucleophilic attack by urea or thiourea. Electron-withdrawing groups increase the partial positive charge on the carbonyl carbon, making it more electrophilic and prone to nucleophilic addition. This stabilization lowers the energy barrier for forming the tetrahedral intermediate, a key step in the mechanism, thus enhancing the reaction rate. In the next step, electron-withdrawing groups further reduce the electron density on the imine carbon, enabling a faster enol (2´) attack by increasing its electrophilicity.

Conversely, electron-donating groups increase the electron density on the carbonyl carbon, reducing its electrophilicity and slowing down nucleophilic attack. This decrease in reactivity highlights the contrasting effects of electron-donating and electron-withdrawing groups on reaction kinetics.

As seen in the Fig. 10, the synthesis mechanism of 1,4-dihydropyridine derivatives (6a-m) is similar to that of the 3,4-dihydropyrimidine-2(1H)-one/thione, typically beginning with the activation of aromatic carbonyl aldehydes in the presence of copper oxide (CuO). In the first step, the activated carbonyl aldehyde, in the presence of an enol system created from ethyl acetoacetate using copper oxide, forms the benzylidene intermediate 11 via a Knoevenagel condensation, making it a Michael acceptor. Here, CuO active sites acts as a Lewis acid, activating the carbonyl group of the aldehyde and facilitating the condensation reaction. Meanwhile, the second mole of ethyl acetoacetate, once activated by copper oxide active sites, undergoes a nucleophilic attack by ammonia, leading to the formation of the enaminone intermediate, referred to as intermediate 12. CuO plays a key role in stabilizing the enol form of ethyl acetoacetate, enabling the nucleophilic attack by ammonia. Intermediate 13 is formed when enaminone 12 nucleophilically attacks the Michael acceptor, intermediate 11. The coordination of copper oxide as a Lewis acid with the carbonyl group of intermediate 11 is crucial in accelerating this reaction step. CuO enhances the electrophilicity of the carbonyl group, making it more susceptible to nucleophilic attack. Tautomerization of the imine-enamine and keto-enol forms takes place, converting the chemical structure of intermediate 13 into form 14. CuO facilitates this tautomerization by stabilizing the transition states involved in the process. Ultimately, the final product, 1,4-dihydropyridine skeletons 6a-m, are formed through intramolecular attack of the amine on the carbonyl group, cyclization, and elimination of one molecule of water. Similar to previous steps, copper oxide activates the carbonyl group, thereby accelerating the intramolecular attack of the amine and promoting cyclization137.

Proposed mechanism for the synthesis of 1,4-dihydropyridine derivatives in the presence of CuO/mGO-TA-Me-BTA nanocomposite.

A similar justification to the previous mechanism (Fig. 9) can be provided regarding the influence of electron-withdrawing and electron-donating groups on product 6a-m formation in this mechanism.

Heterogeneous catalysts are renowned for their recyclability, a property that significantly enhances their practical and economic value. The recyclability of heterogeneous catalysts reduces waste generation, minimizes the need for frequent replacements, and lowers overall production costs. Additionally, their ability to maintain performance over extended use aligns with sustainable practices, making them essential in modern, eco-efficient industrial processes. To demonstrate the recyclability and reusability of the magnetic CuO/mGO-TA-Me-BTA catalyst, a model reaction for the preparation of derivatives 4g and 6e was repeated under optimized conditions for 6 runs. After each cycle, the magnetic catalyst was separated using an external magnet and washed several times with distilled water (2 × 5 ml) and EtOH (2 × 5 ml). After drying in an oven, the catalyst was used again in the next cycle. As shown in Fig. 11, after 6 cycles, a slight decrease in the yield of both reactions is observed, indicating the catalyst’s robust stability and reusability. To ensure the purity and stability of the catalyst, a hot filtration test was performed during the reaction. The catalyst was removed from the reaction mixture at elevated temperature, and the filtrate was allowed to react further. The absence of significant reaction progress after catalyst removal confirmed that the catalysis was truly heterogeneous and not due to leached active species. XRD of the recycled catalyst confirms its excellent structural and stability after multiple reaction cycles (Fig. S17, see SI).

Conclusion

This study establishes CuO/mGO-TA-Me-BTA magnetic nanocomposite as a highly efficient, reusable catalyst for the synthesis of 3,4-dihydropyrimidine and 1,4-dihydropyridine derivatives under mild and environmentally benign conditions. The CuO/mGO-TA-Me-BTA catalytic system affords high yields and selectivity while preserving sensitive functional groups, with the magnetic properties enabling facile catalyst recovery and multiple reuse cycles without significant activity loss. By combining operational simplicity with green chemistry principles, this method offers a sustainable alternative to conventional synthetic approaches. The structural diversity of the produced derivatives highlights its potential in medicinal chemistry for developing novel bioactive compounds, while the catalyst’s robustness and scalability underscore its utilization for both academic research and industrial applications. This work advances the design of multifunctional nanocatalysts for sustainable heterocyclic synthesis, bridging materials science and pharmaceutical development.
