Learning via short videos impairs memory accuracy and reduces brain synchrony
Authors: Meiting Wei, Yandan Li, Haosen Ni, Zhenglong Li, Jiang Liu, Guang-Heng Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The rapid rise of short videos (SVs), particularly social media–style formats characterized by rapid switching and fragmented content, has led to their increasing integration into learning environments. However, their efficacy and neurocognitive impact remain contentious. The present study examined whether SVs are superior or inferior to long videos as tools for learning and memory. Across three experiments, memory performance and forgetting rate were assessed under both incidental and intentional encoding conditions, and inter-subject correlation (ISC) analysis was employed to investigate neural response patterns during SV viewing. Behaviorally, participants learning with SVs showed significantly lower immediate memory accuracy across encoding conditions and exhibited a higher rate of forgetting when explicitly instructed to remember. At the neural level, ISC analyses revealed that SVs elicited reduced neural synchrony in key brain regions supporting visuospatial attention, episodic memory, and cognitive control, including the superior parietal lobule, precuneus, and middle occipital gyrus. In contrast, SVs evoked higher synchrony in temporal and frontal regions associated with bottom-up attentional processing. Furthermore, functional connectivity analyses indicated that SVs weakened coupling between visual, attentional, and cognitive control networks. Together, these findings suggest that the fragmented and rapidly switching nature of typical social media short videos enhances bottom-up attentional capture at the expense of top-down cognitive processes critical for deep learning and long-term memory consolidation. This study provides converging neurobehavioral evidence suggesting that SVs viewing in social media contexts is associated with reduced neural synchronization and poorer memory performance.

Introduction

Short video (SV) platforms have become one of the most popular internet applications globally1. It is not only popular among the young but also increasingly favored by the elderly2. According to a survey, American adults spend an average of 58.4 min per day on TikTok, while Chinese users spend an average of 151 min per day, far exceeding other applications3. The surge in popularity has also led to growing attention from researchers to its potential impacts.

SVs are typically defined as a new type of video content generated by users, with a duration ranging from a few seconds to five minutes4. SVs have several characteristics: First, SV applications have rich content and strong visual impact5, and this audio-visual experience can easily immerse users6,7. Second, the short duration of SV is in line with the fast-paced lifestyle of modern society, enabling users to quickly obtain information and enjoy themselves8. Third, the brevity and diversity of SV content led to the fragmentation of information, time, and space, and viewing is not restricted by time or space. Fourth, SVs are driven by powerful algorithmic recommendations, which can provide personalized content suggestions based on user behavior and preferences9, and can more effectively meet user needs8. These characteristics, including brevity, high sensory salience, fragmentation, and algorithm-driven personalization, not only make SVs highly entertaining but also render them increasingly appealing as a tool for informal learning.

Reflecting these affordances, a growing number of users have begun to treat SVs as a means of acquiring knowledge. On social media platforms, so-called “educational” SVs have proliferated, seemingly catering perfectly to the fragmented learning rhythm of modern life. Before evaluating the effectiveness of such practices, however, a critical conceptual distinction must be drawn. In educational research, a substantial body of research has focused on instructional video segmentation, in which comprehensive instructional content is deliberately divided into logically organized, coherent short units, often in combination with active learning strategies such as retrieval practice. Accumulating evidence indicates that such pedagogically guided segmentation effectively sustains attention and facilitates learning10–13. Furthermore, smaller units of information have been proven to help reduce cognitive load14. By contrast, the SVs encountered on social media are decidedly different in nature: consumption is typically incidental, passive, and largely devoid of instructional scaffolding that supports deep cognitive processing. It is precisely this consumptive, everyday SV format that the present study seeks to isolate and examine.

Given the inherently audiovisual and often emotionally engaging nature of social-media SVs, one might intuitively expect them to be well suited to learning. Indeed, studies have shown that SVs can enhancing students’ learning motivation, engagement, and interest15–17, and their conciseness, visualization, and contextualization have made them applicable in language learning and skill training18–20. From the perspective of learning mechanisms, the stimulus-reward-action association models provide a theoretical framework for understanding the reinforcing effect of SVs on learning motivation21,22. However, a closer look at the underlying cognitive architecture reveals a different picture. Otto23 pointed out that the use of SVs promotes surface learning strategies and leads to a decline in quiz scores. SV users may have a lower motivation to engage in academic tasks that require sustained effort and delayed rewards due to their habituation to instant gratification24. From the perspective of cognitive schema construction, learning essentially relies on the process of information being encoded from sensory memory through working memory and ultimately into long-term memory. According to the stage model proposed by Atkinson and Shiffrin25, the content of SVs first forms sensory memory. When learners pay attention to the parts that interest them, the relevant information is transferred to the limited-capacity working memory, and only through rehearsal can it possibly enter long-term storage. However, the fast pace and high-density information characteristics of SVs can easily lead to working memory overload and increased cognitive load14. According to cognitive load theory, excessive load will directly impair learning efficiency26. Barrouillet and Camos27 further revealed based on their time-based resource-sharing model of working memory that attention shifts can cause the decay of working memory representations over time. SVs, through their rapid and frequent attention-switching, constantly reset the cognitive processing, making it difficult for working memory to form stable knowledge representations28. This ultimately limits their effectiveness in deep knowledge learning.

Notably, most existing empirical studies provide only indirect evidence regarding the relationship between SV use and cognitive functions. For example, significant association between SV use and a decline in memory function, including a positive correlation with memory loss29, damage to short-term memory30, and negative impacts on working memory and language ability, which in turn lead to poorer academic performance31. In addition, it has been found that watching SVs can impair prospective memory, attention, and task performance28,32,33. However, these findings do not directly test how much information is retained when individuals intentionally learn from short videos. To address this, the present study will directly investigate the cognitive effects of the SV itself when used as a learning tool, with a particular focus on its impact on memory accuracy and forgetting rate, thereby providing more direct empirical evidence for the educational value of short videos.

Although most existing studies have focused on the learning behavioral consequences of SV use, research on the underlying neural mechanisms is still in its infancy. Existing evidence indicates that watching SVs typically requires the coordinated action of multiple brain networks, including the default mode network (DMN), salience network (SN), attention network, and frontoparietal network (FPN)34–36. The dynamic interactions of these networks in natural stimulus processing (such as movies, music, and narratives) have been shown to be crucial for cognitive processing37–39. In terms of reward processing, studies have found that personalized SV viewing can cause abnormal activation in brain regions such as the ventral tegmental area and the amygdala40, which is closely related to the reinforcement learning mechanism. The pleasure experienced by users during viewing can reinforce their usage behavior, forming a positive emotional maintenance cycle41. This explains at the neural mechanism level why SVs may enhance learning interest and motivation. However, preferred SV content may suppress the regulatory function of the prefrontal cortex42, which, as the core of working memory and executive control, can directly affect an individual’s cognitive control ability when impaired. At the same time, personalized SVs also enhance the functional coupling between the DMN and the sensory processing pathway as well as the FPN40, leading to bottom-up limbic system processes dominating attention allocation and further weakening top-down cognitive regulation. Therefore, from a neural perspective, learning through SVs also shows obvious duality: while enhancing learning motivation, it may impair working memory function and reduce executive control ability.

Inter-subject correlation (ISC) is a data-driven analysis method43 that is widely used to study neural responses to natural stimuli (such as movies and videos)44,45. Unlike the traditional general linear model, ISC does not rely on predefined task models; instead, it reveals shared brain responses to complex stimuli by examining the similarity of neural activity between subjects46. Higher ISC means that responses among individuals are more “synchronized” or “consistent”47. Dikker et al. found that students’ neural synchronization can predict real-world classroom engagement48. In other words, by effectively capturing the neural synchronization of subjects while watching SVs and long videos (LVs), and comparing the differences in ISC under the stimulation of these two types of videos, it is expected to reveal their different impacts on brain cognitive processing and memory formation mechanisms.

Based on the research background outlined above, this study will conduct three consecutive experiments to systematically investigate the effectiveness of SV as a learning tool from both behavioral and neural perspectives. When SVs are used as a learning tool, Experiment 1 will examine participants’ natural memory performance under no memory prompt conditions; Experiment 2 will assess intentional memory effects through explicit memory instructions. These behavioral experiments will collectively focus on the differences between SVs and LVs in terms of memory accuracy and forgetting rate. Experiment 3 will employ functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) technology, combined with ISC analysis and functional connectivity (FC) methods, to delve into the neural mechanisms underlying the impact of SVs on memory processing and to provide neuroscientific evidence for the behavioral findings by comparing the ISC differences under SV and LV conditions. The core hypotheses of this study are as follows: (1) When used as a learning tool, SVs will lead to lower memory accuracy and higher subsequent forgetting rate under both unintentional and intentional memory conditions; (2) At the neural level, the SV group will expected to be associated with reduced inter-subject neural synchronization and weakened FC within and across large-scale brain systems supporting visuospatial attention, episodic memory, and cognitive control. These neural alterations are predicted to reflect disrupted integrative processing during fragmented learning and to be related to individual differences in memory performance.

Methods

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Yunnan Normal University (approval number 20230318) and followed the principles of the Helsinki Declaration (2008 version). All participants provided written informed consent and received financial compensation. The studies reported here were not preregistered.

Pilot study - production and selection of video materials

Design and participants

A total of 72 valid participants were recruited offline from a higher education institution in Yunnan Province, including 37 males and 35 females, as self-reported by participants, with an average age of 21.77 years (SD = 2.51).

Materials

This study implemented strict control measures for the SVs and LV used in the experiment, as follows.

(i) Duration control: The total duration of both types of videos was set to 10 min. The LV consisted of a single, continuous 10-minute excerpt drawn from a complete 30 min source video. The SV condition was constructed by concatenating 5 to 7 independent short clips, with each clip lasting between 30 s and 2.5 min to simulate the typical rhythm of popular short-form platforms, resulting in a total duration of 10 min.

(ii) Content and coherence control: All source materials were travelogues focusing on scenic spots. To minimize the influence of prior knowledge and emotional arousal, the videos featured lesser-known overseas destinations and maintained a neutral narrative style without strong emotional cues or rapid visual flickering.

(iii) Information density control: SVs are often characterized by a faster pace and higher information density. To ensure that any observed effects were attributable to the presentation format rather than the sheer volume of verbal information, we strictly controlled and matched the core informational content between conditions. The primary metric for information density was the total word count of spoken narration. All audio content was transcribed, and the word counts for the experimental materials were as follows: SV1, 2929; LV1, 2926; SV2, 3016; LV2, 3012. This near-identical matching confirms that the effective verbal information load was equated. To operationally achieve this balance within the fragmented SV format, we interspersed non-informative filler segments (e.g., sustained aerial landscape shots without any narration or textual overlay) among the informational clips. In other words, the SV sequence included 2 to 3 non-informative filler segments (e.g., aerial landscape shots without narration) among its 5–7 clips. This design ensured that the core verbal information load was effectively equated between the SV and LV conditions.

(iv) Language and presentation: The spoken narration in all videos was the original dialogue from the content creators (native Mandarin speakers). Standard Chinese subtitles matching the audio were added to all videos to ensure clarity and control for potential auditory comprehension differences.

Two distinct versions (Set 1 and Set 2) of both SV and LV were produced. The current study employed SV2 and LV2; therefore, all subsequent references to SV and LV specifically denote these versions.

Exp.1. Memory performance of SV content under no-prompt conditions

Design and participants

This study employed a one-way between-subjects design, with two experimental conditions: SVs and LVs. The dependent variables included memory accuracy and forgetting rate, where memory accuracy was the score obtained in the immediate test after learning; the forgetting rate was calculated using the following formula: (immediate test accuracy − subsequent test accuracy)/ immediate test accuracy. Importantly, in the present study, the term forgetting rate is used as an operational label rather than a formal estimate of forgetting dynamics. Because memory was assessed at only two time points, this measure is not intended to model the temporal function of forgetting, but instead serves as a descriptive index of relative memory decline, allowing comparison across learning conditions over an identical delay interval.

To determine the required sample size, we used G*Power (version 3.1.9.7) for a priori sample size estimation51,52. The power analysis (d = 0.5) indicated that a minimum of 128 participants were needed for a one-way between-subjects design to achieve 80% statistical power (1-β) at a significance level of α = 0.05.

A total of 200 college students were recruited for the experiment. After excluding 20 participants who did not complete the subsequent test, had a gap of more than 30 hours between tests, or had a subjective memory effort rating higher than 5 (indicating possible deliberate memorization behavior), a final valid sample of 180 participants were obtained (Mage = 20.95, SD = 2.26). Sex was self-reported, with 80 males and 100 females in the final sample. Participants were randomly assigned to the SV group (n = 89, 38 males) and the LV group (n = 91, 42 males), with specific demographic information shown in Supplementary Table 1. Within each condition, all participants viewed the same 10-minute video materials, and this stimulus-consistency principle was applied across all subsequent experiments.

Materials

Video materials

The specific production standards and control measures for the video materials are detailed in the pilot study section.

Scales

Demographic information: Participants’ gender, age, grade level, as well as the average daily duration of smartphone use and SV browsing in the past week were collected.

Short Video Dependency Scale (SVDS): This scale was adapted from the diagnostic criteria for Internet gaming disorder in the DSM-553 and consists of 20 items covering four dimensions: preoccupation and perseverance, withdrawal and tolerance, impairment and hindrance, and concealment and avoidance. The scale uses a 5-point rating (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree), with higher total scores indicating greater dependency on short video social media. The internal consistency reliability (Cronbach’s α) of this scale in this study was 0.895.

Test questions

To assess the learning effect of the video, we extracted information from the full text content of the two videos and compiled 20 multiple-choice questions. These questions were then randomly divided into two sets of tests, labeled as Test A and Test B, each containing 10 questions. Each question had four options, with only one being the correct answer explicitly mentioned in the video. The specific questions and options can be found in the Set of Test Questions section of the Supplementary Methods.

To control for potential errors due to test order, participants were randomly divided into two groups: one group completed Test A in the immediate test and Test B in the delayed test; the other group completed Test B in the immediate test and Test A in the delayed test, thus balancing test order.

Manipulation check

To test the effectiveness of the experimental manipulation, participants were asked to rate the degree of memory effort they exerted while watching the video on a 9-point scale, ranging from 1 (not at all) to 9 (very hard).

Experimental procedure

The experimental procedure is shown in Fig.1 and the specific steps are as follows: (1) Preparation phase: Researchers first explained the experimental precautions to the participants. After obtaining informed consent, participants filled out the demographic questionnaire and the SVDS. (2) Video viewing phase: Before the formal experiment, participants were informed that they would watch a video next and were emphasized that this was a “relaxation session” to help them adapt to the experimental environment and relieve tension. Researchers did not mention anything related to memory or testing, and asked participants to watch the corresponding video in a naturally relaxed state. (3) Immediate testing phase: After the video playback ended, the screen presented the A/B test questions (a total of 10 questions), and participants were required to select the correct answers based on the content of the video they had watched. If they could not recall the relevant information, they could choose the option “5=can’t remember”. (4) Subjective assessment phase: After completing the test, participants were required to evaluate the degree of memory effort they had exerted while watching the video. (5) Delayed test control and implementation: To eliminate the interference of participants actively reviewing the video content after the experiment ended, the experimenter only informed them that “the experiment is over” after the immediate test, without mentioning that there would be a subsequent test the next day. At the same time the next day, the experimenter contacted the participants and provided the link for the delayed test. Participants completed the test through the Credamo platform, with the questions set as another equivalent questionnaire. Exp.2. Memory performance of SV content under prompt conditions

Design and participants

The design is the same as that in Exp.1. The final sample comprised 185 participants (Mage = 20.62, SD = 1.91), with sex self-reported, including 78 males and 107 females. Participants were randomly assigned to the SV group (n = 94, 42 males) and the LV group (n = 91, 36 males) (see Supplementary Table 2).

Procedure and materials

The procedure and materials were almost identical to those of Exp.1. The only difference was that the instruction page explicitly prompted participants to remember the content and informed them that a test would be conducted, thereby putting participants into a true learning state. In this experiment, the Cronbach’s α of the SVDS was 0.858.

Exp.3. Differences in neural synchronization during memory processing between LV and SV

Design and participants

The fMRI data in Exp.3 and our prior study54 were collected from the same participants during a single scanning session. The prior study focused on neural activity and FC during memory retrieval. In contrast, the present study addresses a distinct research question by examining ISC during video viewing, thereby focusing on neural synchrony during encoding rather than retrieval. This separation of analyses allows for a more targeted investigation of distinct cognitive stages.

We initially recruited 173 college students through offline advertisements and online questionnaires. After preliminary screening, 76 participants met the inclusion criteria. 11 participants were unable to participate in the experiment due to personal scheduling conflicts, and 6 participants were excluded from data analysis due to excessive head movement. The final sample comprised 59 participants (Mage = 20.95, SD = 2.21), with sex self-reported, including 22 males and 37 females.

The remaining participants were divided into the SV group (n = 28) and the LV group (n = 31), with an average age of 20.95 (SD = 2.21). All participants were right-handed, aged between 18 and 27 years, in good mental health, and had experience using SVs. Detailed demographic information is shown in Supplementary Table 3.

Inclusion criteria were: a score of less than 5 on the diagnostic items of the SV Addiction Scale (SVAS)55; a score of less than 49 on the Self-Rating Anxiety Scale56; and a score of less than 53 on the Self-Rating Depression Scale56. Exclusion criteria included: a personal or family history of mental illness; acute or chronic diseases such as heart disease, vascular disease, respiratory disease, or endocrine system disorders; metal implants in the body (e.g., cranial implants or pacemakers); use of hearing aids; common infectious diseases such as hepatitis, AIDS, or tuberculosis; and a history of epilepsy or related conditions.

Procedure and materials

Participants who met the inclusion criteria were required to complete a demographic questionnaire (including age, gender, SV use history, and average daily viewing duration over the past two weeks), followed by a set of questionnaires assessing SVAS, SVDS, Short Video Use Self-Control Failure Scale (SVUSCF)57, Self-Rating Anxiety Scale, and Self-Rating Depression Scale) before the experiment. All participants then underwent a standard MRI safety screening, and those who passed proceeded to the scanning session. Participants were randomly assigned to the SV or the LV group (All participants had previously taken part in the experiment and had not viewed the experimental video). The video materials were identical to those used in Exp.2; however, unlike Exp.1 and 2, no delayed follow-up test was administered on the following day.

During fMRI acquisition, video stimuli were presented via a projection screen positioned at the rear of the scanner bore and viewed through a mirror mounted on the head coil, and auditory stimuli were delivered synchronously through MRI-compatible headphones. Importantly, participants were explicitly informed prior to video presentation that their memory for the video content would be tested immediately after viewing (prompt conditions). After the video playback ended, a prompt interface appeared on the screen for 5 s, marking the beginning of the retrieval stage. Subsequently, 20 memory test questions were presented in random order. Each question automatically jumped to the next one after the participant recorded their answer, with a 20 s response time for each question (determined by response time based on Exp.1 and 2). Failure to answer within the specified time was considered a retrieval failure.

fMRI acquisition and preprocessing

Data were collected on a Siemens 3 T Prisma scanner equipped with a 64-channel head coil at the Magnetic Resonance Imaging Research Center of Yunnan Normal University.

Functional images were acquired using an echo-planar imaging pulse sequence with the following parameters: repetition time (TR) = 2000 ms, echo time (TE) = 30 ms, slice thickness = 3.2 mm, interslice gap = 0 mm, flip angle (FA) = 90°, number of slices = 54, field of view (FOV) = 220 × 220 mm2, matrix size = 64 × 64, voxel size = 1 × 1 × 1 mm3. Structural images were acquired using a T1-weighted three-dimensional spoiled gradient echo sequence with the following parameters: TR = 2200 ms, TE = 3.42 ms, slice thickness = 1.0 mm, interslice gap = 0 mm, FA = 9°, inversion time = 1100 ms, FOV = 224 × 224 mm2, in-plane resolution = 256 × 256.

Data preprocessing was performed using the DPABI toolbox6, following the standard procedure. First, the first 10 volumes of each functional run were discarded to allow for signal equilibration and magnetic field stabilization. Slice timing correction was then performed to adjust for differences in acquisition time between slices. Next, head motion correction was conducted by realigning all functional volumes to the mean image. Participants with excessive head motion (translation >2.5 mm or rotation >2.5°) were excluded. In addition, mean framewise displacement (mFD) was calculated for each participant, and a threshold of 0.2 mm was applied to further ensure data quality. For spatial normalization, each participant’s T1-weighted structural image was co-registered to the corresponding mean functional image. The resulting images were then normalized to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) standard space (voxel size: 3 × 3 × 3 mm3) using the unified segmentation approach combined with Diffeomorphic Anatomical Registration Through Exponentiated Lie Algebra (DARTEL). Subsequently, linear detrending was applied to remove low-frequency signal drift, followed by temporal band-pass filtering (0.01–0.08 Hz) to reduce physiological noise and high-frequency artifacts. Finally, spatial smoothing was performed using an isotropic Gaussian kernel with a full width at half maximum (FWHM) of 4 mm, enhancing the signal-to-noise ratio and spatial consistency of the functional images.

Data analysis

ISC analysis: ISC analysis was performed on the preprocessed functional imaging data using Python 3.9. In the MNI152 standard space, images from the 60th to 90th TR (120-180 s after video onset) of all subjects were extracted, as this time window captured the critical periods of SVs content switching while maintaining comparability with the LVs condition. For each participant, we employed a leave-one-out approach58 to calculate voxel-wise correlation coefficients. Specifically, for each subject, the BOLD time series was correlated with the average time series of all other subjects. This produced one Fisher’s Z-transformed ISC map per subject. Subsequently, one-sample t-tests were conducted on these individual maps separately for the SV group and the LV group, and independent samples t-tests were further used to compare the differences in ISC values between the two groups. Multiple comparisons were corrected using the Gaussian Random Field (GRF) method (voxel p < 0.001, cluster p < 0.05, two-tailed). Unless otherwise specified, all neuroimaging results were corrected using this standard. Brain regions showing significant ISC effects were subsequently identified as seed regions, and their mean ISC values were extracted for further analyses.

FC analysis: We defined spherical regions of interest (ROIs) with a radius of 6 mm centered on the peak coordinates of the clusters and performed FC analysis in DPABI.

Partial correlation analysis: We further extracted the ISC mean values of the brain regions with significant clusters as seed points and conducted partial correlation analysis with SVDS, SVAS, and SVUSCF scores (controlling for gender, age, grade, and mFD), and the results were corrected using the False Discovery Rate (FDR).

Results

Results for pilot study

ANOVA analysis was conducted using JASP 0.19 to compare the four video materials in terms of emotional dimensions and familiarity, and the results are shown in Supplementary Table 4. No significant main effects of video material were observed for positive emotions, negative emotions, pleasure, arousal, and familiarity (all ps > 0.05), indicating that the four videos were comparable in their emotional and familiarity profiles. In this study, we used the two videos SV2 and LV2.

Results for Exp.1

The results of the homogeneity test of baseline information for the two groups of participants showed that there were no significant differences in any variables except for SVDS (See Supplementary Table 5). Therefore, it was included as a control variable in the subsequent analysis. Additionally, the manipulation checks results revealed that the memory effort levels for both groups were lower than 5 (MSV = 3.798, SD = 1.582; MLV = 3.648, SD = 1.722), and there was no significant difference between groups, t(178) = 0.606, p = 0.545, Cohen’s d = 0.090, 95%CI = [-0.202, 0.383]. Finally, to exclude the error of tests, we conducted independent sample t-tests on the difficulty of each test. Results revealed no significant difference in results whether participants in the SV group or the LV group completed the A set first or the B set first. This finding indicates that the difficulty of the tests was consistent for participants (see Supplementary Table 6).

Given the significant difference in SVDS between the two groups, we included SVDS as a covariate in subsequent analyses to control for its potential confounding effect. First, an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was conducted on memory accuracy.

We further examined the relationship between self-reported memory effort and memory performance. In the SV group, memory effort was not significantly correlated with accuracy (r = 0.069, p = 0.520) or with forgetting rate (r = -0.030, p = 0.779). In the LV group, effort was also unrelated to accuracy (r = -0.119, p = 0.260) but showed a significant negative correlation with forgetting rate (r = -0.293, p = 0.005), indicating that higher effort was associated with slower forgetting in the LV condition.

The independent samples t-test results indicate that the two groups of participants are homogeneous (see Supplementary Table 7). Correlational analyses between self-reported memory effort and memory performance yielded distinct patterns across groups. In the SV group, higher memory effort was significantly associated with better immediate accuracy (r = 0.419, p = 0.001) and with a lower forgetting rate (r = -0.252, p = 0.014). In the LV group, memory effort showed a marginal positive correlation with accuracy (r = 0.202, p = 0.054) and a significant positive correlation with forgetting rate (r = 0.264, p = 0.011).

Discussion

As a learning tool, short videos are less effective and have higher forgetting rate

Exp.1 and 2 together explored whether SVs could be more effective learning tools than LVs. The results showed that, whether under unintentional or intentional memory conditions, SVs as a learning medium led to lower memory accuracy and higher subsequent forgetting rate. This finding is completely consistent with our research hypothesis and highlights the core dilemma faced by SVs as learning tools: SVs not only fail to enhance learning outcomes but also accelerate memory loss and increase the likelihood of recall failure.

This conclusion is highly consistent with existing research. Tamir et al60. pointed out that media use may weaken individuals’ ability to remember target information; Xu et al31. reported that SV use has a negative impact on adolescents’ academic performance. We posit that a key underlying mechanism may be the distinctive “rapid, passive content-switching pattern” inherent to SVs, which could impair the core cognitive process of event segmentation61, thereby causing cognitive overload40. Direct empirical support for this view comes from a recent study by Li et al62., which found that watching randomly sequenced SVs impaired subsequent event segmentation ability and memory for narrative films. Moreover, although intentional memory prompts may enhance attention engagement, the brief duration and rapid content switching of SVs may still limit opportunities for effective encoding.

Amalric et al63. further pointed out that SVs learning hardly induces meaningful adaptive changes in brain response networks. In contrast, LVs usually have richer contextual information, more detailed explanations, and more ample space for reflection, all of which are crucial for understanding and long-term retention of complex information. The shortcomings of SVs in content depth and logical coherence make it difficult for learners to integrate new knowledge meaningfully. In addition, SVs often contain numbers of dynamic visuals, sound effects, and rapid switching of high-intensity sensory stimuli. Although these stimuli can attract attention, they may interfere with the brain’s processing and retention of core educational content, resulting in very limited knowledge being retained in memory63. The cognitive cost may involve a bias toward processing salient sensory inputs, potentially constraining resources available for deep semantic processing.

These findings have important implications for educational practice and digital content design. While SVs are vivid and engaging, their effectiveness as a learning tool should be evaluated with caution. Correlation analyses showed that greater effortful encoding was associated with higher memory accuracy and lower forgetting, indicating that subjective effort remains beneficial even under SV conditions. However, despite this relationship, overall memory performance in the SV group was significantly lower than in the LV group. This dissociation suggests that the fast-paced and highly stimulating characteristics of SVs may bias cognitive processing toward continuous sensory tracking, thereby limiting the resources available for deeper semantic encoding. Such a format-related constraint may constitute an implicit cognitive cost, which may be difficult to fully compensated for by increased subjective effort alone.

Reduced neural synchronization in the brain during short video learning and memory processing

Importantly, while our prior work54 focused on neural activity and functional connectivity during memory retrieval, the present study extends these findings by examining neural synchronization during the encoding stage using ISC. This allows us to characterize how fragmented short-video input shapes shared neural dynamics at the point of initial information processing, thereby complementing prior evidence from the retrieval phase.

The results of the ISC analysis showed that, regardless of the group, the brain regions with significant synchronization were mainly concentrated in the VSN, DAN, DMN, and FPN. The DAN is typically involved in goal-directed and top-down attentional regulation64. The DMN is not only associated with self-referential processing, episodic memory, and introspection but also plays an important role in watching movies and social cognition38,65,66. Meanwhile, the FPN is primarily responsible for top-down cognitive control67. This pattern is highly consistent with neuroimaging studies using natural stimuli (such as movies, narrative materials)37,39,40, indicating that, watching indeed requires the mobilization of distributed brain networks to support complex cognitive processing.

By comparing the groups, we found that the LV group induced stronger neural synchronization in brain regions such as the SPG, precuneus, and MOG. Specifically, the SPG plays a key role in spatial attention, selection of visual information, and task-oriented attentional control68, and is associated with episodic memory retrieval, contextual integration, and conscious experience. As a key component of the DMN, the PCUN is not only involved in self-referential processing and explicit memory retrieval69,70 but also exerts top-down control over visual processing, attention, and tracking of moving stimuli71,72. In addition, the PCUN is involved in segmenting sensory information flow into meaningful events73. Meanwhile, the MOG, plays a crucial role in processing the position, shape, and orientation of visual objects74. Previous studies have shown that increasing attentional load alters the activity patterns in the visual cortex75,76. These regions, which showed stronger synchronization in the LV group, form a network supporting sustained attention, contextual integration, and event segmentation. Higher neural synchrony may reflect more stable and shared cognitive states across viewers when processing coherent narratives.

In contrast, the SV group showed higher ISC values in the STG, MTG, and MFG. The STG and MTG are brain regions commonly associated with semantic processing, phonetic perception, and social cognition77,78. The MFG, a key node within the VAN, is implicated in bottom-up control processes, including responding to novel stimuli and attentional reorientation79. Collectively, this distinct pattern of heightened synchronization aligns with the stimulus characteristics and viewing dynamics typical of short-video formats. It may signify a shift in cognitive processing state, wherein neural resources are preferentially allocated toward the immediate detection of and response to rapidly changing, highly salient stimuli, rather than toward the deep integration of a global narrative.

This observed neural pattern is consistent with prior findings suggesting that SV viewing is associated with elevated attentional demands and cognitive load80,81, as well as evidence that random SV viewing attenuates eye-movement synchronization at event boundaries62. In addition, the ISC value of the MFG was positively correlated with SV dependency and self-control failure, suggesting that the neural activity pattern in this region may be associated with the decline in cognitive control ability in SV use82. In addition, the SV group also showed increased synchronization within the broader VAN and the cerebellum. The VAN is recognized for its role in detecting behaviorally relevant stimuli and facilitating attentional shifts68,83, while the cerebellum contributes to various higher cognitive functions84. For example, short video addiction is positively correlated with increased gray matter volume in the cerebellum82. These co-occurring increases in synchronization may reflect a broader neural adaptation supporting the rapid attentional and information-processing demands elicited by the short-video format.

These findings suggest that SVs viewing in social media contexts may be associated with reduced sustained cognitive processing, highlighting a potential trade-off between attention capture and deeper information processing.

Reduced coupling between brain regions related to attention and cognitive control when watching short videos

In the subsequent FC analysis, we found that the SV group had significantly lower FC strength between several key brain region pairs compared to the LV group, including SPG-MOG, SPG-MFG, SPG-STG, MOG-calcarine, MOG-SFG, and STG-MFG. This pattern of results suggests that SV viewing is associated with reduced functional integration within brain networks, particularly along key pathways involved in visual processing, attentional regulation, and cognitive control.

Specifically, the SPG is a core area for spatial attention, sensorimotor integration, and visuospatial working memory68, and together with the MOG, which is mainly responsible for higher-level visual spatial analysis, and the calcarine, which handles primary visual information processing, they form a neural network for visuospatial information processing85. The observed reduction in connectivity between the SPG and the MOG is consistent with the rapid visual transitions characteristic of short videos and may be associated with a reduced demand for deep integration of visual information. Similarly, the weakened connection between the MOG and calcarine is consistent with the possibility that the fragmented nature of SVs influences the functional dialogue between different levels of the visual hierarchy.

The SFG, a key node of the FPN in cognitive control, decision-making, and task execution68,86, also showed reduced coupling with the MOG. Notably, individuals with addictive disorders typically exhibit abnormal functional activity and connectivity in the SFG87. This pattern aligns with the limited attentional resources theory88, wherein high-information-flow environments may shift resource allocation toward external stimulus processing, a state that could be associated with altered connectivity between sensory and cognitive control regions.

Additionally, the observed decoupling between the STG—responsible for auditory and cross-modal integration78,89, and regions like the MFG and SPG, suggests a functional reorganization that differs from the more integrated processing seen during LV viewing. This is in line with previous reports that short-video consumption can be associated with altered cognitive processing patterns28,31.

Together, these findings outline a potential neural basis that may help explain why short videos, despite their strong attention-capturing properties, are less effective in supporting deep learning and durable memory formation. Importantly, by integrating the present results with our prior work (Wei et al., 2026), a more complete picture begins to emerge. While earlier findings highlighted impairments during memory retrieval, the current study demonstrates that such disruptions may already arise during encoding, as reflected in reduced neural synchronization and weakened functional integration. This cross-stage perspective provides a more comprehensive account of how short-video formats disrupt memory processing, suggesting that deficits observed during retrieval may, at least in part, originate from suboptimal encoding dynamics.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the sample group was limited to college students, while the use of SVs is increasingly common among adolescents and other age groups. Future research should expand the sample range to verify the universality of the results. Second, although the emotional factors of the video content were controlled in the experiment, individual differences in emotional responses may still affect cognitive processing. Subsequent studies need to further explore the role of emotion regulation in SV learning. Third, while our interpretations regarding attentional allocation, cognitive load, and depth of processing are informed by prior experimental and theoretical work, these psychological constructs were not directly measured in the present study. Accordingly, such interpretations should be viewed as theory-driven and inferential rather than as direct empirical evidence. In addition, this study did not involve the design and evaluation of intervention measures. Future research could attempt to develop targeted intervention programs to mitigate the potential negative impact of SV use on cognitive functions, or explore the optimal combination of SVs and other digital media in educational settings to improve learning efficiency. Finally, the video materials used in the experiment inevitably involved trade-offs between experimental control and ecological validity. To approximate real-world SV consumption, the SV stimuli could not be perfectly matched to the LV condition in terms of content structure. As a result, the independent contributions of presentation format and content coherence could not be fully disentangled in the present design. Moreover, constraints of the fMRI environment prevented the replication of naturalistic browsing behaviors, such as finger-based scrolling and self-paced video switching. These factors may have influenced memory encoding and retrieval. Future studies should adopt more refined experimental designs to orthogonally manipulate video format and content coherence, and employ more ecologically valid paradigms, to more comprehensively examine the role of SVs in authentic learning contexts.

Conclusions

The results of this study indicate that social media-style SVs perform significantly worse as learning tools compared to traditional LVs, a phenomenon that is consistently present under both intentional and unintentional memory conditions. Under intentional memory conditions, the SV group exhibited a significantly higher forgetting rate, revealing that their shallow processing attributes and high-intensity sensory stimuli may hinder deep information processing and effective encoding, leading to a decline in memory retention capabilities. Neurologically, SVs induce lower neural synchronization in brain regions related to visual processing, spatial attention, and self-referential processing, while showing higher synchronization in areas associated with semantic processing, phonetic perception, and bottom-up attentional control. FC analysis further demonstrates that the rapid switching and fragmented information characteristics of SVs interfere with visual integration, sustained attention, and top-down cognitive regulation. Importantly, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence against the effectiveness of well-designed instructional short videos embedded within structured educational contexts. Rather, the present results highlight the potential cognitive and neural costs of passive or semi-passive short-video consumption in environments characterized by minimal pedagogical scaffolding and frequent attentional shifts, such as social media platforms. In summary, this study reveals the potential negative impact of SVs on memory encoding and retention from both behavioral and neural perspectives, providing scientific evidence for assessing their suitability as learning tools.
