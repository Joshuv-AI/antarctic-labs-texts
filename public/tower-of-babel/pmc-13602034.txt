Orthoplastic limb salvage in 12 days: Integrated reconstruction of a severe open tibial fracture
Authors: Roberto Herrera, Agustin Davies, Jesica Presas, Mariano Codesido, Macarena Otero
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis case illustrates how coordinated orthoplastic management integrating staged biological optimization, definitive skeletal stabilization, and microsurgical reconstruction may improve outcomes in devastating lower-extremity trauma while reducing delays, complications, and reconstructive burden.Keywords: Open tibial fracture, Orthoplastic surgery, Limb salvage, Free flap reconstruction, Intramedullary fixation, Gustilo IIIB

Introduction

Severe Gustilo-Anderson type IIIB open tibial fractures remain among the most challenging injuries in orthopaedic trauma because successful limb salvage requires simultaneous management of skeletal instability, extensive soft-tissue loss, contamination, and infection risk. Modern orthoplastic care is based on the integration of radical debridement, stable skeletal fixation, timely soft-tissue reconstruction, and coordinated multidisciplinary decision-making to restore both biological and mechanical conditions for healing [1], [2], [3], [4].

Increasing evidence supports structured orthoplastic pathways as a means of reducing complications and improving coordination of definitive reconstruction in severe open tibial fractures. Their implementation has become increasingly important across different trauma systems, where close collaboration between orthopaedic and reconstructive surgeons, together with early infection-control strategies, is fundamental to the management of complex lower-extremity injuries [1], [4].

Although the principles of orthoplastic reconstruction are well established, their successful application depends on appropriate timing and coordination of each treatment stage. Demonstrating how these principles are integrated into routine clinical practice remains educationally relevant, particularly for centres seeking to establish or refine structured orthoplastic pathways [2], [3].

This report describes the staged orthoplastic management of a severe Gustilo-Anderson type IIIB open tibial fracture, illustrating the coordinated integration of biological optimization, definitive skeletal stabilization, and microsurgical soft-tissue reconstruction that resulted in successful limb salvage within a 12-day treatment pathway.

Case presentation

A previously healthy 21-year-old male sustained a high-energy distal tibial open fracture following a motorcycle accident. Clinical examination demonstrated extensive medial soft-tissue loss with exposed tibia and severe contamination, consistent with a Gustilo-Anderson type IIIB injury (Fig. 1).

Initial traumatic soft-tissue injury and preoperative radiographs.

DAY 0

Urgent surgical management included radical debridement of devitalized tissue, extensive irrigation, temporary external fixation (Fig. 2), and initiation of broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics. Temporary external fixation restored limb alignment while preserving access for serial debridement and subsequent definitive soft-tissue reconstruction. Local antibiotic delivery was performed using antibiotic-loaded polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA), followed by negative-pressure wound therapy (VAC) (Fig. 3).

Temporary external fixation after initial debridement associated with postoperative radiographic evaluation.

Local antibiotic delivery using antibiotic-loaded PMMA followed by negative-pressure wound therapy (VAC).

DAY 2

A second-look procedure was performed with repeat debridement, reassessment of tissue viability, and renewal of negative-pressure wound therapy. Progressive biological optimization of the wound bed was achieved while maintaining temporary skeletal stability.

DAY 4

Definitive skeletal reconstruction was performed using intramedullary fixation after restoration of length, alignment, and rotational profile. Careful management of fracture comminution and preservation of the biological environment were prioritized to create a stable reconstructive platform for definitive soft-tissue coverage (Fig. 4). Local antibiotic delivery was repeated using antibiotic-loaded PMMA, and negative-pressure wound therapy was reapplied to further optimize the wound bed prior to definitive soft-tissue reconstruction. Following definitive fixation, a residual medial soft-tissue defect measuring approximately 20 × 6 cm remained with exposed bone and implant.

Definitive intramedullary fixation followed by local antibiotic delivery, negative-pressure wound therapy reapplication, and residual soft-tissue defect measurement.

DAY 7

Definitive soft-tissue reconstruction was performed using a free anterolateral thigh flap measuring 24 × 8 cm, selected based on the defect characteristics and reconstructive requirements.

Recipient vessels were identified through proximal dissection of the posterior tibial neurovascular bundle outside the zone of injury. Arterial inflow was established using an end-to-side anastomosis to the posterior tibial artery. Venous drainage was achieved with two end-to-end venous anastomoses to the posterior tibial concomitant veins. All microvascular anastomoses were performed under optical magnification using interrupted 9-0 nylon sutures (Fig. 5).

A) Free fasciocutaneous flap planning. B) Preparation and dissection of the posterior tibial vessels proximal to the zone of injury.

Total ischemia time was less than 40 minutes. Postoperative flap monitoring was performed clinically according to the institutional microsurgical protocol and supported by bedside thermography and sodium fluorescein assessment (Fig. 6). Following confirmation of adequate perfusion and venous drainage, the flap was inset without tension. The donor site was closed primarily.

Immediate postoperative appearance following free ALT flap inset and thermographic monitoring.

DAY 12

The patient was discharged after confirmation of stable flap perfusion, satisfactory wound healing, and an uneventful early postoperative course, without postoperative complications or the need for additional procedures, within 12 days from the initial injury (Fig. 7).

Clinical appearance at hospital discharge on postoperative day 12.

Results

Definitive limb salvage was successfully completed within a coordinated 12-day orthoplastic treatment pathway. Stable skeletal alignment and fixation were achieved, and the free flap remained completely viable, providing durable soft-tissue coverage without vascular compromise or deep infection.

The postoperative course was uneventful, with no flap compromise, vascular complications, or requirement for additional surgical procedures following definitive reconstruction.

Stable soft-tissue coverage was maintained throughout follow-up. Radiographic evaluation demonstrated progressive fracture healing, with complete fracture union at 9 months (Fig. 8). The patient subsequently returned to recreational basketball without evidence of reconstructive failure.

Clinical and radiographic evolution at 9-month follow-up demonstrating stable soft-tissue coverage, complete flap integration, and complete radiographic fracture union.

Discussion

The coordinated orthoplastic strategy described in this report resulted in successful limb salvage within a short, structured treatment pathway. Although each component of treatment is well established individually, the educational value of this report lies in demonstrating their timely integration into a structured reconstructive pathway rather than presenting a novel surgical technique. This case illustrates how coordinated implementation of established orthoplastic principles may facilitate efficient reconstruction while preserving the biological and mechanical conditions required for healing [5], [6].

Timing and infection prevention

The timing of soft-tissue coverage in Gustilo-Anderson type IIIB fractures remains one of the principal determinants of infection prevention and successful limb salvage. In the present case, definitive flap coverage was achieved within the first week after injury, consistent with contemporary orthoplastic recommendations favoring early biological reconstruction. Cochonat et al. demonstrated significantly lower infection rates in patients treated with flap coverage within 7 days compared with delayed reconstruction [7]. Similarly, the SPARTA multicenter study identified progressive increases in deep infection risk as the interval between definitive fixation and soft-tissue coverage increased [2].

Conover et al. further reported that each additional day between fixation and flap coverage increased rates of surgical site infection, wound complications, and reoperation in Gustilo IIIB tibial fractures [8]. These findings reinforce the importance of minimizing delays between skeletal stabilization and definitive soft-tissue reconstruction.

Orthoplastic integration and biological reconstruction

Beyond timing alone, successful management of severe open tibial fractures depends on adherence to fundamental orthoplastic principles, including aggressive debridement, staged biological optimization, stable skeletal fixation, local infection control, and timely definitive coverage.

Failure to achieve coordinated biological, mechanical, and reconstructive integration in these injuries has historically been associated with prolonged hospitalization, repeated reoperations, chronic infection, flap failure, nonunion, and secondary amputation. Schnetz et al. identified infection and nonunion as major complications following severe open tibial fractures, particularly in cases requiring multiple staged procedures and delayed reconstruction [9]. Likewise, large orthoplastic series have demonstrated that coordinated orthoplastic management substantially reduces deep infection rates, nonunion incidence, and overall number of surgical procedures compared with fragmented treatment pathways [3], [10].

In the present case, serial debridement, local antibiotic delivery, temporary stabilization, definitive intramedullary fixation, and microsurgical coverage were planned as interconnected stages of the same reconstructive process. Definitive fixation was not considered an isolated orthopaedic procedure, but rather a fundamental component required to optimize the biological environment before definitive soft-tissue reconstruction.

An anterolateral thigh free flap was selected because it provides reliable vascular anatomy, a long vascular pedicle, a versatile skin paddle, and minimal donor-site morbidity, making it particularly suitable for extensive lower-extremity defects requiring durable soft-tissue coverage [11], [12], [13].

Staged versus single-stage orthoplastic reconstruction

While this case employed a staged strategy with a short interval between fixation and flap coverage, emerging evidence suggests potential advantages of integrated 'fix-and-flap' orthoplastic models. Mathews et al. demonstrated significantly lower infection rates when definitive fixation and flap coverage were coordinated within a single orthoplastic strategy [14]. Nevertheless, the staged approach utilized in the present case represented a pragmatic balance between early definitive reconstruction and progressive biological optimization of the wound bed.

Although a formal orthoplastic board was not convened, treatment planning was performed collaboratively between trauma and reconstructive surgeons throughout the staged reconstruction, reflecting the multidisciplinary decision-making advocated in contemporary orthoplastic care [6].

The present case also highlights the growing importance of orthoplastic training within modern trauma surgery. In many high-energy trauma environments, severe lower-extremity injuries require not only fracture fixation skills, but also understanding of soft-tissue management, reconstructive timing, recipient vessel preparation, and microsurgical planning. Recent evidence supports the feasibility of training orthopaedic surgeons to perform simple pedicled flaps within an orthoplastic collaboration model, reducing time to soft-tissue coverage [15].

Although multidisciplinary collaboration remains essential, increasing familiarity of trauma surgeons with orthoplastic principles may facilitate earlier reconstructive decision-making, reduce treatment delays, and improve continuity of care in complex limb salvage scenarios. These same principles extend to war- and crisis-related extremity injuries, where similarly extensive contamination often requires close collaboration with microbiology and infectious disease specialists alongside coordinated orthoplastic care [16].

Clinical impact and functional recovery

Beyond the objective outcomes reported above, this favorable clinical evolution contrasts with the historically high complication burden associated with severe Gustilo IIIB tibial fractures, underscoring how coordinated orthoplastic management — integrating biological optimization, definitive skeletal stabilization, and early soft-tissue reconstruction — may translate into meaningful functional recovery in devastating lower-extremity trauma [9].

Limitations

A limitation of this report is the absence of prospectively collected validated patient-reported outcome measures. Nevertheless, long-term follow-up confirmed durable soft-tissue coverage, complete fracture union, and return to recreational sports following coordinated orthoplastic reconstruction.

Conclusion

Successful orthoplastic management of severe lower-extremity trauma depends on coordinated integration of biological optimization, definitive skeletal stabilization, infection control, and timely soft-tissue reconstruction within a structured limb salvage pathway.

Although none of the individual reconstructive steps are novel, this report demonstrates how their coordinated implementation within a 12-day orthoplastic treatment pathway may facilitate efficient reconstruction and favorable functional recovery in complex Gustilo-Anderson type IIIB tibial fractures.
