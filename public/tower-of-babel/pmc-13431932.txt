Propofol‐induced hypertriglyceridemia in critically ill trauma patients requiring nutrition therapy: A descriptive cohort study
Authors: Emma S. Lemke, Jonathon K. Walker, Julie E. Farrar, Saskya Byerly, Dina M. Filiberto, Roland N. Dickerson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to assess the incidence and identify risk factors associated with the development of propofol‐induced hypertriglyceridemia in critically ill trauma patients who required nutrition therapy.MethodsAdult patients (aged >17 years) admitted to the trauma intensive care unit who received propofol for >24 h and had a serum triglyceride concentration determination during the infusion were retrospectively evaluated. Hypertriglyceridemia was defined as a serum triglyceride concentration > 400 mg/dL. Potential risk factors for hypertriglyceridemia as well as linear and nonlinear correlative relationships between propofol‐associated lipid injectable emulsion dose and serum triglyceride concentration were evaluated.ResultsOne hundred and ninety‐five serum triglyceride determinations were obtained from 100 patients. Twenty‐five patients (25%) developed hypertriglyceridemia. The greatest risk factor for hypertriglyceridemia was lipid injectable emulsion dose (0.48 [0.36, 0.64] vs 0.28 [0.20, 0.49] g/kg/day; P < 0.01). Those who exhibited hypertriglyceridemia had a higher C‐reactive protein (P < 0.02), lower prealbumin (P = 0.001), higher serum creatinine (P < 0.04), and required more days of nutrition therapy (P < 0.03). The hypertriglyceridemia group exhibited a nonlinear concave lipid dose–serum triglyceride concentration response relationship (r = 0.731, P = 0.001) whereas those without hypertriglyceridemia had a flattened and variable linear relationship (r = 0.115, P = 0.34).ConclusionHypertriglyceridemia is common during propofol therapy, reflective of lipid injectable emulsion dose, and is associated with greater inflammation, organ dysfunction, and severe critical illness requiring prolonged nutrition therapy.

INTRODUCTION

Propofol is recommended for mechanically ventilated patients who require sedation and for the management of intracranial pressure for those with severe traumatic brain injury. 1 , 2 It is considered an appropriate therapeutic option because of its rapid onset of action and short half‐life. Because propofol is formulated in a 10% soybean oil‐based lipid injectable emulsion, aggressive doses of propofol can lead to significant increases in serum triglyceride concentrations, 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 particularly if lipoprotein lipase is saturated. 10 Hypertriglyceridemia from lipid injectable emulsion administration may result in adverse effects including pancreatitis, 5 , 6 , 8 decreased reticuloendothelial clearance, 11 impaired immune function, 12 and cholestasis. 13 One of the first reported cases in the United States of severe hypertriglyceridemia associated with propofol was three decades ago in a medical intensive care unit patient receiving a lipid injectable emulsion‐free parenteral nutrition regimen. 9 Since that time, investigators have sought to ascertain the incidence and risk factors for developing hypertriglyceridemia during propofol therapy. Most of the literature examining this complication was conducted in mixed medical and surgical intensive care units, with the majority of patients being medical patients. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 14 However, data from critically ill trauma patients are lacking, and the influence of fat‐containing enteral nutrition has been ignored. We sought to assess the incidence and identify risk factors associated with the development of propofol‐induced hypertriglyceridemia in critically ill trauma patients who require nutrition therapy.

METHODS

We performed a descriptive cohort study in 100 critically ill trauma patients at Regional One Health from January 1, 2020 to May 31, 2021. Adult patients aged >17 years old, who were admitted to the trauma intensive care unit, received a continuous intravenous propofol infusion for ≥24 h, and had at least one serum triglyceride concentration determination were available for study inclusion. Patients were retrospectively identified from the hospital and nutrition support service electronic medical records. The patients' electronic medical records were reviewed for data retrieval. Injury Severity Score, 15 an anatomical scoring system used to assess the severity of injuries for trauma patients, was obtained from the institution‐based trauma registry. All patients were referred to the nutrition support service for management of enteral nutrition or parenteral nutrition. The data collection period encompassed 1 day prior to receiving propofol if available and up to 7 days while receiving a propofol infusion or until discontinuation of propofol, whichever came first. Serum triglyceride concentration determinations were ordered at the discretion of the primary trauma service or the nutrition support service. The lumen used for administration of propofol or parenteral nutrition was avoided for blood collection. Prior to obtaining the blood specimen, infusions were paused, the first blood sample was discarded, the line was flushed with normal saline, and the blood sample was then obtained. A second confirmatory serum triglyceride concentration was usually obtained for those with hypertriglyceridemia. Patients were excluded if they received <24 h of propofol, did not have a serum triglyceride concentration determination during propofol administration, did not require enteral nutrition or parenteral nutrition during their trauma intensive care unit stay, or if it was deemed that the electronic nursing intake and output documentation was incomplete.

Our primary aim was to determine the incidence of hypertriglyceridemia for those patients who received an intravenous propofol infusion and nutrition therapy. Our secondary aims were to determine the incidence of severe hypertriglyceridemia, risk factors for hypertriglyceridemia, lipid injectable emulsion dose–serum triglyceride concentration relationship, and assess clinical outcomes. Hypertriglyceridemia was defined as a serum triglyceride concentration >400 mg/dL. 4 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 16 Severe hypertriglyceridemia was defined as a serum triglyceride concentration >1000 mg/dL. The amount of propofol and nutrition received in 24 h was recorded from 00:00 to 23:59 h daily. Serum chemistries were obtained between 00:00 to 02:00 h. The amount of propofol given in the previous 24 h prior to the serum triglyceride concentration determination was used for evaluating the lipid injectable emulsion dose–serum triglyceride concentration response relationship. Characterization of triglyceride elimination kinetics following discontinuation of propofol for those with hypertriglyceridemia was evaluated when post‐propofol serum triglyceride concentration determinations were available.

Pertinent risk factors for hypertriglyceridemia included a medical history of hyperlipidemia, documentation of antihyperlipidemic medications prior to admission, human immunodeficiency virus disease, alcohol use disorder, liver disease, pancreatitis, end‐stage or chronic kidney disease, pregnancy, obesity, hyperglycemia (serum glucose concentration >180 mg/dL), sepsis, diabetes by medical history or a baseline hemoglobin A1c > 6.5%, or corticosteroid therapy. Concomitant medications that can increase serum triglyceride concentration, including thiazide diuretics, levothyroxine, cyclosporine, tacrolimus, estrogen, beta‐blockers, isotretinoin, protease inhibitors, and atypical antipsychotics, were also recorded. Medications that can decrease serum triglyceride concentration by activation of lipoprotein lipase (e.g., insulin and heparin infusion) were also recorded. Duration and dosage of propofol therapy, day of maximum serum triglyceride concentration, other serum triglyceride concentration determinations, total daily dose of injectable lipid emulsion, and caloric intakes received from propofol, enteral nutrition and parenteral nutrition were obtained.

Pre‐resuscitation body weight was used to determine target energy and protein goals. Target caloric intake was 30–32 kcal/kg/day. 17 For those with obesity (body mass index > 29.9 kg/m2), a total caloric goal of 22–25 kcal/kg ideal body weight/d was assigned. 18 Caloric intake from propofol was calculated from the volume of propofol received per day multiplied by the caloric density (1.1 kcal/mL). Lipid injectable emulsion was eliminated from the parenteral nutrition prescription and caloric intake was adjusted to avoid overfeeding during propofol infusion. Enteral nutrition was adjusted for calories received by propofol by use of a high protein‐containing enteral formula (1 kcal/mL, 92 g protein/L) with concurrent administration of liquid protein supplement boluses as needed. 19 Any patient who received parenteral nutrition as the sole source for nutrition therapy or as supplemental therapy for those with gastric feeding intolerance and concurrent enteral nutrition were identified as receiving parenteral nutrition. A target blood glucose concentration range of 70–150 mg/dL 20 , 21 was maintained by use of intermittent sliding‐scale regular human insulin, Neutral Protamine Hagedorn insulin with sliding‐scale regular human insulin, or a continuous regular human insulin infusion when required. 21 The nutrition regimen was usually initiated within the first 3 days following trauma intensive care unit admission and advanced daily to achieve goal caloric and protein intakes within 2–4 days, depending on feeding tolerance, surgical procedures, hemodynamic stability, glycemic excursions, or electrolyte perturbations.

Statistical analysis was conducted using SigmaPlot for Windows version 11.2 (Systat Software). Distribution of the data was evaluated for normality by the Shapiro–Wilk test. Data were summarized using medians and interquartile ranges (IQR) for continuous variables and frequencies and proportions for categorical variables. Comparison of continuous variables between two independent groups was performed by the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical data were analyzed by Chi‐square or Fisher's Exact analysis. Regression analysis using the Pearson correlation coefficient was used to assess a linear relationship between serum triglyceride concentration and lipid injectable emulsion dosage. Nonlinear regression analysis for the dose–response relationship was interpolated using TableCurve 2D for Windows version 5.01 (Systat Software). The linear or nonlinear model with the best fit, as assessed by the strongest correlation coefficient, was used for interpretation of the data. Significance testing was performed with the use of a two‐sided alpha level of 0.05.

Our study was approved by the University of Tennessee Health Science Center Investigational Review Board (protocol # 24‐10244‐XM) and Regional One Health's Office of Medical Research, adhering to the Declaration of Helsinki principles. Written informed consent was waived as the research poses no more than minimal risks to participants.

RESULTS

Patient characteristics

A total of 302 critically ill trauma patients were screened for study inclusion to achieve 100 evaluable patients. In 78% of ineligible patients, exclusion was attributed to receiving <24 h of propofol or lack of serum triglyceride concentration determination during propofol infusion. None of the patients was pregnant, or had human immunodeficiency disease or pancreatitis. Other reasons for excluding patients from the study are depicted in Figure 1. Patients had multiple severe injuries as reflected by an injury severity score of 29 (20, 34), markedly elevated C‐reactive protein concentration of 22.7 (16.0, 29.5) mg/dL, depressed serum prealbumin concentration 8.0 (3.0, 11.0) mg/dL), prolonged trauma intensive care unit and hospital lengths of stay (18 [11, 26] days and 25 [18, 35] days, respectively) and required nutrition support therapy for 14 (8, 19) days. Those with hypertriglyceridemia tended to have more ventilator‐dependent days and longer trauma intensive care unit and hospital length of stays (Table 1); however, no significant differences were observed. Prevalence and risk factors for hypertriglyceridemia

The maximum serum triglyceride concentration for the entire population ranged from 42 to 1429 mg/dL with a median concentration of 241 (141, 399) mg/dL. Twenty‐five patients (25% of the population) experienced a serum triglyceride concentration greater than 400 mg/dL during propofol infusion (Table 2). Four patients (4% of the population) experienced severe hypertriglyceridemia with a serum triglyceride concentration greater than 1000 mg/dL. The maximum serum triglyceride concentration occurred on Day 4 after 3 days of propofol therapy for the hypertriglyceridemia and non‐hypertriglyceridemia groups (P = 0.35, Table 2). Nine patients had a baseline serum triglyceride concentration determination prior to propofol infusion. There was no significant difference in pre‐propofol serum triglyceride concentrations between those who experienced hypertriglyceridemia (n = 5) compared to four patients without an elevated serum triglyceride concentration (159 [102, 427] vs 136 [112, 210] mg/dL, respectively; P = 0.56). Two of the five patients with pre‐propofol serum triglyceride concentration determinations from the hypertriglyceridemia group had a baseline serum triglyceride concentration greater than 300 mg/dL.

Median and maximum serum triglyceride concentration for those with hypertriglyceridemia were over twice that of those who did not experience an elevated serum triglyceride concentration (Table 2; P = 0.001). Those with hypertriglyceridemia received twice as much propofol (2 vs 1 mg/kg/h; Table 2, P < 0.01) and more hours per day of propofol infusion (20 vs 18 h, respectively; Table 2, P < 0.05). Both groups underwent a similar duration of propofol therapy (4 days; Table 2, P = 0.35). Daily lipid injectable emulsion intake from propofol was significantly greater for those who experienced hypertriglyceridemia when normalized to body weight (0.48 vs 0.28 g/kg/day, respectively; Table 2, P < 0.01) or when expressed as absolute intakes (35 vs 21 g/day, respectively; Table 2, P < 0.01).

Examination of the propofol‐associated lipid injectable emulsion dose relative to serum triglyceride concentration in 195 determinations from all patients indicated that the dose–response relationship between lipid injectable emulsion and serum triglyceride concentration was highly variable, with an overall poor correlative linear relationship (Figure 2; r = 0.358) across the broad dosage range of 0–2.5 g/kg/day. Serum triglyceride concentrations ranged from 32 to 388 mg/dL with a median concentration of 169 (116, 241) mg/dL in 138 determinations from patients without hypertriglyceridemia. Fifty‐seven serum triglyceride concentration determinations from patients who experienced hypertriglyceridemia ranged from 57 to 1429 mg/dL with a median of 439 (358, 590) mg/dL.

Influence of propofol lipid injectable emulsion dose on serum triglyceride concentration from 195 observations from all patients. The linear regression was serum triglyceride concentration (mg/dL) = 188 + 201 × lipid dose (g/kg/day); r = 0.358; P = 0.001.

To examine whether there was a different dose–response relationship in those who experienced hypertriglyceridemia, serum triglyceride concentration determinations during the propofol infusion for both the hypertriglyceridemia and non‐hypertriglyceridemia groups were evaluated. The dose–response relationship for the non‐hypertriglyceridemia group was flattened and highly variable (Figure 3; r = 0.115, P < 0.33). In contrast, nonlinear analysis for the hypertriglyceridemia group revealed a significant concave relationship between maximum serum triglyceride concentration and lipid injectable emulsion dose from propofol (r = 0.731; P = 0.001) as a rapid rise in serum triglyceride concentration occurred as the lipid injectable emulsion dose from propofol increased (Figure 3).

Regression analysis of lipid injectable emulsion dose on serum triglyceride response in those who did or did not experience hypertriglyceridemia. Without hypertriglyceridemia, the linear regression was serum triglyceride concentration (mg/dL) = 193 + 25 × lipid injectable emulsion dose (g/kg/day); r = 0.115; P = 0.33. For those with hypertriglyceridemia, the nonlinear regression was serum triglyceride concentration (mg/dL) = 29 + 292 × elipid dose(g/kg/day); r = 0.731, P = 0.001.

There were no differences observed between the hypertriglyceridemia and non‐hypertriglyceridemia groups with respect to the incidence of disease conditions that more commonly predispose patients to lipid dysregulation, nor differences in administration of medications associated with hyperlipidemia (Table 3). However, those with hypertriglyceridemia experienced a greater serum C‐reactive protein concentration (P = 0.02), greater serum creatinine concentration (P < 0.04), lower serum prealbumin concentration (P = 0.001), and were younger (P < 0.04; Table 1). There were no differences observed between the hypertriglyceridemia and non‐hypertriglyceridemia groups with respect to severity of injury via injury severity score, incidence of traumatic brain injury, or other patient characteristics (Table 1). The hypertriglyceridemia group tended to have more patients with obesity (56% vs 35%); however, these differences were not statistically significant (P < 0.10).

Triglyceride elimination characteristics

Serial post‐propofol serum triglyceride concentration determinations were available in four patients with hypertriglyceridemia. The serum triglyceride concentration fell from 996 (565, 1207) to 300 (197, 638) mg/dL over 36 (24, 66) h for an estimated logarithmic elimination half‐life of 54.9 (27.2, 85.4) h or 13.8 (5.4, 22.8) mg/dL/h decline in serum triglyceride concentration. The propofol infusion was discontinued and alternative sedative therapy with dexmedetomidine or midazolam was initiated upon discovery of hypertriglyceridemia for many patients. Propofol was urgently discontinued for all patients exhibiting severe hypertriglyceridemia. None of the patients developed propofol infusion syndrome, pancreatitis, or other overt adverse clinical events.

DISCUSSION

These data indicated that one fourth of critically ill trauma patients who received propofol therapy and required nutrition therapy developed hypertriglyceridemia with a serum triglyceride concentration greater than 400 mg/dL, and 4% of patients exhibited severe hypertriglyceridemia with a serum triglyceride concentration greater than 1000 mg/dL. Observations from all patients demonstrated a poor linear relationship between propofol‐associated lipid injectable emulsion dose and serum triglyceride concentration. Those who developed hypertriglyceridemia received significantly more lipid injectable emulsion than those without hypertriglyceridemia. Patients who developed hypertriglyceridemia also had greater serum C‐reactive protein and creatinine concentrations, lower serum prealbumin concentration, required a longer duration of specialized nutrition support, and had trends toward longer requirement for days of ventilatory support, trauma intensive care unit, and hospital length of stays. These data suggest that patients with hypertriglyceridemia had worse inflammation, more organ dysfunction, and greater severity of illness irrespective of similar injury severity scores. Those who developed hypertriglyceridemia exhibited a nonlinear concave relationship between lipid injectable emulsion dose and serum triglyceride concentration, which suggested lipoprotein lipase saturation.

Previous studies indicated that the incidence of hypertriglyceridemia (defined as a serum triglyceride concentration of ≥400 mg/dL) associated with propofol therapy in critically ill patients ranged from 4.5% to 27.9%. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 14 Only one of these studies had a higher incidence of hypertriglyceridemia than that observed in this study. 14 Two studies reported a 3.8% 5 and 7.3% 14 incidence of severe hypertriglyceridemia, which was similar to our observed 4% incidence. Patients who experienced hypertriglyceridemia received about 67% more lipid injectable emulsion than those without hypertriglyceridemia (Table 2) and is confirmatory to other propofol studies indicating those who developed hypertriglyceridemia received greater doses. 5 , 6 , 8 , 14 However, the propofol‐associated lipid injection emulsion dose that resulted in hypertriglyceridemia in our study was lower than doses given in past studies. 5 , 6 , 8 , 14 These previous studies indicated that the doses of propofol at the time of the maximum serum triglyceride concentration ranged from 3 5 , 8 , 14 to 3.6 mg/kg/h 6 (or from 0.72 to 0.86 g/kg/day of lipid injectable emulsion). The lipid injectable emulsion median dose 24 h prior to the maximum serum triglyceride concentration for our critically ill trauma patients with hypertriglyceridemia was 2.8 mg/kg/h (or 0.67 g/kg/day of lipid injection emulsion). Data from this study indicated that the lipid injectable emulsion dose‐serum triglyceride concentration response had a poor correlative relationship overall (Figure 2) and was generally unpredictable, as some patients who received lipid injectable emulsion doses >1.5 g/kg/day did not exhibit hypertriglyceridemia (Figures 2 and 3). These data indicate that the critically ill trauma patient receiving nutrition therapy with hypertriglyceridemia had multiple influencing factors beyond lipid injectable emulsion dosage alone that resulted in hypertriglyceridemia.

Exploration of risk factors for developing dysregulation in intravenous lipid injectable emulsion clearance showed no difference between groups (Table 3). The cumulative number of risk factors were similar at a median of 2 risk factors per group (p < 0.46; Table 3). Obesity is a common risk factor for the pathogenesis of hypertriglyceridemia, especially in those with excess visceral adipose tissue. 22 Although there was a trend in an increased prevalence of obesity for the hypertriglyceridemia group, differences between groups were not significant.

Another predisposing condition for hypertriglyceridemia in critically ill trauma patients who received propofol and nutrition support is the presence of critical illness itself. Critically ill patients with infection, trauma, shock, and sepsis exhibit increased net fat oxidation with lipolysis, increased plasma free fatty acids, and an elevation in serum triglyceride concentration. 23 , 24 Other studies have observed increases in serum triglyceride concentration by as much as 25%–40% from baseline in the absence of lipid injectable emulsion administration. 24 , 25 , 26 Increased availability of free fatty acids from adipose tissue lipolysis 25 may provide the substrates leading to an upregulation of very‐low‐density lipoprotein synthesis by the liver, thus raising serum triglyceride concentration.

Studies that investigated the influence of critical illness upon intravenous triglyceride elimination are conflicting, as some indicate decreased clearance and a longer half‐life, 26 , 27 whereas others indicate increased elimination and a shorter half‐life. 28 , 29 With larger doses of lipid injectable emulsion, lipoprotein lipase can be saturated. Once lipoprotein lipase saturation occurs, elimination shifts from a logarithmic first‐order elimination to a zero‐order elimination whereby lipid injectable emulsion removal is at a constant rate. Thus, during zero‐order elimination, if the intake exceeds the constant maximal amount of lipid injectable emulsion that can be cleared, substantial rises in serum triglyceride concentration will occur. To evaluate this phenomenon, Lindholm examined soybean oil lipid injectable emulsion clearance in 10 postoperative patients who underwent elective surgery and 25 critically ill trauma and surgical patients. 27 Both groups were given a small bolus dose followed by a constant intravenous infusion of 100 g of 20% lipid injectable emulsion over 4 h. The unstressed control group's serum triglyceride concentration increased from 96 to about 245 mg/dL post bolus and remained stable throughout the infusion period. Conversely, the critically ill patients showed a gradual rise in serum triglyceride concentration throughout the infusion from a baseline of 166 to 592 mg/dL, reflective of lipoprotein lipase saturation. 10 Following discontinuation of the lipid injectable emulsion infusion, the serum triglyceride concentration returned to pre‐infusion baseline concentrations within 40 min for all patients in the control group. In contrast, about half of the critically ill patients' serum triglyceride concentration determinations returned to baseline within 40 min, about 30% returned to baseline within 1.5 h, and all returned to baseline concentrations by 6 h. 27 Triglyceride concentration elimination data from only four patients with hypertriglyceridemia in our study indicated an unlikely triglyceride logarithmic half‐life of 52 h upon discontinuation of propofol in those with hypertriglyceridemia. It may be possible that some of the critically ill trauma patients with hypertriglyceridemia during propofol infusion had an increased baseline serum triglyceride concentration prior to propofol infusion. If true, then these patients would be at greater risk for saturation of lipoprotein lipase, since saturation occurs at a serum triglyceride concentration of about 300–400 mg/dL. 10 If the patients with hypertriglyceridemia had an elevated baseline serum triglyceride concentration prior to infusion, then the return of serum triglyceride concentration to baseline following discontinuation of propofol may have occurred sooner than when the actual follow‐up serum triglyceride concentration determination was obtained, resulting in an exaggerated prolonged half‐life. Further study with measurement of pre‐propofol serum triglyceride concentration and multiple serial serum triglyceride concentration measurements following propofol discontinuation for patients with hypertriglyceridemia is warranted.

A commonly overlooked risk factor in the pathogenesis of hypertriglyceridemia associated with propofol by most published studies is the influence of concurrent administration of enteral nutrition. The fat content of enteral nutrition competes with lipid injectable emulsion for endothelial lipoprotein lipase binding and subsequent triglyceride hydrolysis. Evidence for this phenomenon has been demonstrated in healthy volunteers. 30 Using tracer‐labeled intravenous soybean oil‐based lipid injectable emulsion and a high‐fat diet, these data indicated that the lipid injectable emulsion elimination half‐life was 22% longer during the fed state when compared to fasting. 30 Patients in this study were prescribed a hypocaloric, high‐protein nutrition regimen to avoid overfeeding from the addition of propofol calories. 19 The enteral nutrition regimen consisted of a formula that provided 1 kcal/mL and protein at 92 g/L with additional liquid, sugar‐free protein supplementation to meet the high nitrogen demands of severe traumatic injuries. 31 The fat content of the enteral nutrition formula provided total fat of only 9.5 g/L, of which nearly half comprised medium‐chain triglycerides, which do not require lipoprotein lipase for metabolism. With this enteral nutrition feeding strategy, the amount of daily fat intake from enteral nutrition was low during propofol therapy and equivalent for both groups (Table 4). Therefore, it is unlikely that enteral nutrition was a major contributor to hypertriglyceridemia by competing for lipoprotein lipase with resultant saturation.

Other factors to consider for reduced triglyceride clearance is the increased phospholipid‐to‐triglyceride ratio with 10% soybean oil lipid injectable emulsion compared to 20% and 30% lipid injectable emulsions. This increased phospholipid‐to‐triglyceride ratio potentially interferes with triglyceride clearance. 32 Increased production of phospholipid–apolipoprotein E complexes and free cholesterol–phospholipid complexes can decrease the enzymatic activity of lipoprotein lipase and endocytic uptake of triglyceride remnants, resulting in an increased serum triglyceride concentration. 30

A final consideration is the presence of carnitine depletion. 33 Lack of l‐carnitine results in decreased carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 activity, which is the main regulating factor for transporting long‐chain fatty acids from the cytosol into the mitochondrial matrix for β‐oxidation. Carnitine depletion has occurred in chronically critically ill patients, particularly in those with kidney failure who received prolonged hemodialysis or continuous renal replacement therapy. 33 Although plasma carnitine concentrations were not obtained in these patients, it is less likely that carnitine depletion occurred as patients were studied within the first several days of trauma intensive care unit stay and did not require prolonged renal replacement therapy during the observation period. Propofol has been implicated in inhibiting carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 when given in toxic doses mimicking carnitine depletion. 34 However, none of the patients had clinical evidence of propofol‐related infusion syndrome.

Limitations of this study include its single‐center design, which reduces its generalizability to other trauma centers, and the limited number of subjects to achieve statistical significance for some supporting trending clinical data to indicate that the hypertriglyceridemia group was likely a “sicker population” or included a greater proportion of patients with obesity. Its retrospective study design, lack of pre‐propofol serum triglyceride concentration determinations, and omission of serial serum triglyceride concentration determinations following discontinuation of propofol limit our interpretation for risk factors for hypertriglyceridemia and reduced clearance. Finally, its appropriate use in practice as a nursing‐directed titration‐based dosing strategy likely reflects non‐steady‐state serum triglyceride concentration determinations during the propofol infusion.

CONCLUSIONS

Hypertriglyceridemia occurred in one fourth of critically ill trauma patients who received propofol and nutrition therapy. Prospectively identifying critically ill trauma patients who are at risk of developing significant hypertriglyceridemia is difficult due to the contributory effects of numerous factors beyond propofol dose. We recommend that serum triglyceride concentration be determined prior to administration of propofol and followed with serial serum triglyceride concentration determinations, preferably at least every 48 h. More frequent monitoring may be necessary in critically ill patients with marked inflammation, organ dysfunction and severe critical illness, those with a pre‐propofol baseline serum triglyceride concentration >300 mg/dL, and patients who are prescribed a high‐fat‐containing enteral nutrition regimen.
