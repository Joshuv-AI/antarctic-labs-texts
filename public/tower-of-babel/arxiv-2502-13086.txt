The u u -invariant of function fields in one variable
Karim Johannes Becher, Nicolas Daans, Vlerë Mehmeti

Abstract
. The u u -invariant of a field is the largest dimension of an anisotropic quadratic torsion form over the field. In this article we obtain a bound on the u u -invariant of function fields in one variable over a henselian valued field with arbitrary value group and with residue field of characteristic different from 2 2 . This generalises a theorem due to Harbater, Hartmann and Krashen and its extension due to Scheiderer. Their result covers the special case where the valuation is discrete. We further give a new proof of a theorem due to Parimala and Suresh bounding by 8 8 the u u -invariant of a function field in one variable over any henselian discretely valued field of characteristic 0 0 with perfect residue field of characteristic 2 2 . Keywords: Quadratic form, henselian valued field, function field in one variable, local-global principle, residue form, real field, torsion form, general u u -invariant, dyadic valuation Classification (MSC 2020): 11E04, 11E81, 12D15, 12J10, 14H05

Introduction

The study of numerical field invariants in quadratic form theory was initiated by Irving Kaplansky in 1953.
In
[
18
]
,
he introduces three quantities in
\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
attached to an arbitrary field of characteristic different from
2
and suggests to study and compare them.
One of them was later called the
u
-invariant
, a term which goes back to Hanfried Lenz’ article
[
24
]
from 1963.

A field
K
is called
nonreal
if
-1
is a sum of squares in
K
, and otherwise
K
is called
real
.
For this introduction, let
K
denote a nonreal field of characteristic different from
2
.

A quadratic form over
K
is called
universal
if it represents all elements of
K
.
Following Kaplansky and Lenz, the
u
-invariant of
K
is defined as the smallest natural number
n
such that any regular quadratic form in
n
variables over
K
is universal provided that such a natural number exists, and
\infty
otherwise.
This quantity in
\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
is denoted by
u(K)
.

In the early 1970ies, Richard Elman and Tsit-Yuen Lam continued the study of this invariant in a series of articles.
They modified the definition in a way that makes no difference for nonreal fields, but makes it more meaningful for real fields. We will recall their definition in
Section

4
.

As pointed out in
[
18
, Theorem 3]
, one always has
u(K(\!(t)\!))=2u(K)
, where
K(\!(t)\!)
denotes the field of Laurent series in
t
over
K
.
Consequently, and since
u(\mathbb{C})=1
, examples of fields of
u
-invariant
2^{r}
for any given
r\in\mathbb{N}
are easy to obtain.
Incited by this observation, Kaplansky wonders in
[
18
]
whether
u(K)
is always a power of
2
or
\infty
.
This question was answered in the negative in 1989 by Alexander Merkurjev.
After giving in
[
29
]
a first construction of a field of
u
-invariant
6
, he showed in
[
30
]
that every positive even integer is the
u
-invariant of some field.
Merkurjev’s construction of examples involves direct limits and produces fields which are not finitely generated over any proper subfield.
When restricting to fields which are of finite transcendence degree over some familiar base field, no examples are known with a
u
-invariant that is not a power of
2
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and let
F/K
be a field extension of transcendence degree
n
.
One may try to determine or at least bound the
u
-invariant of
F
in terms of
n
and properties of
K
.
Well-known statements of this sort are the following:

•

If
K
is algebraically closed, then
u(F)\leqslant 2^{n}
.

•

If
K
is finite, then
u(F)\leqslant 2^{n+1}
.

•

If
K
is a complete discretely valued field with finite residue field, then
u(F)\leqslant 2^{n+2}
.

The first two bounds were established in 1936 by Chiungtze C. Tsen
[
39
]
and independently in 1952 by Serge Lang
[
22
, Theorem 6, Corollary]
.
The last bound was proven by David Leep
[
23
]
in 2013, based crucially on a result on systems of quadratic forms due to Roger Heath-Brown
[
16
]
. (When
\mathsf{char}(K)\neq 0
, that bound also follows from

[
22
, Theorem 6 and Theorem 8]
.)

In each of the three cases above, the upper bound becomes an equality if
F/K
is finitely generated. This follows by a classical valuation argument; see
Corollary

4.5
below.
Hence, in those situations, we actually have
u(F)=2^{d}
where
d
is the cohomological dimension of
F
.
This might suggest that
u(F)=2^{n+2}
would hold as well when
K
is a nonreal number field and
F/K
a finitely generated extension of transcendence degree
n
.
However, even for
n=1
this is still an open problem.
A more general open question is whether
u(F)
can be bounded in terms of
u(K)
and the transcendence degree of
F/K
.

We will now focus on function fields in one variable over a given base field.
By a
function field in one variable over
K
, we mean a finitely generated field extension of transcendence degree
1
.
We can ask for sufficient conditions on
K
for a certain number
u
to serve as a common upper bound on
u(F)
for all function fields in one variable
F/K
.

Our main result provides a transfer principle for this common upper bound between a henselian valued field
K
and its residue field, assuming the latter to be of characteristic different from
2
.
In particular, for any odd prime number
p
, applying
Theorem

I
below to the field of
p
-adic numbers
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
with its natural valuation, one retrieves the result from
[
33
]
and
[
17
]
that
u(F)=8
for every function field in one variable
F/\mathbb{Q}_{p}
.

Our standard reference for the theory of valued fields is
[
15
]
.
For a valuation
v
on
K
, we denote
by
Kv
the residue field and by
vK
the value group of
v
, and we call
v

discrete
if
vK\simeq\mathbb{Z}
.
Given a valuation
v
on
K
, we call the pair
(K,v)
a
valued field
.
A valuation
v
on
K
is called
henselian
if,
for every finite field extension
L/K
, there is a unique extension of
v
to a valuation
w
on
L
;
note that in this case
w
is henselian, too.
By
[
15
, Theorem 1.3.1]
, every complete rank-
1
valuation is henselian.

The characteristic of a ring
R
is denoted by
\mathsf{char}(R)
.
Our main result can be stated as follows:

Theorem I

(
Theorem

6.2
)
.

Assume that
(K,v)
is a henselian valued field with
\mathsf{char}(Kv)\neq 2
.
For
u\in\mathbb{N}
, the following are equivalent:

(i)

u(F)\leqslant[vK:2vK]\cdot u
for every function field in one variable
F/K
.

(ii)

u(E)\leqslant u
for every function field in one variable
E/Kv
or
[vK:2vK]=\infty
.

Furthermore, if
u(E)=u
for every function field in one variable
E/Kv
, then
u(F)=[vK:2vK]\cdot u
for every function field in one variable
F/K
.

If
v
is a discrete valuation, then
[vK:2vK]=2
.
Hence
Theorem

I
generalises
[
17
, Theorem 4.10]
, where the equivalence of
(i)
and
(ii)
was obtained for the case where
(K,v)
is a complete discretely valued field with
\mathsf{char}(Kv)\neq 2
.
Under the same hypothesis on
(K,v)
,
a valuation theoretic local-global principle for isotropy of quadratic forms over function fields in one variable over
K

was established in
[
8
, Cor. 3.5]
, from which
[
17
, Theorem 4.10]
is easily retrieved.
This local-global principle
[
8
, Theorem 3.1]
was extended in
[
28
, Cor. 3.19]
by the last author of the present article to the situation where the valuation
v
on
K
is complete and the value group
vK
is an arbitrary ordered subgroup of
\mathbb{R}
.
It is illustrated in
[
28
, Section 6]
how this local-global principle applies to establish upper bounds on the
u
-invariant of function fields in one variable over complete valued fields with value group contained in
\mathbb{R}
.
By
[
3
, Theorem 4.4]
, the local-global principle from
[
28
, Cor. 3.19]
extends to the case where
v
is henselian but not necessarily complete, keeping the assumption that
vK
is contained in
\mathbb{R}
.
Nevertheless, this version of the local-global principle turns out to be applicable to the study of field invariants in the case of function fields in one variable over henselian valued fields without assumption on the value group, by using reduction arguments concerning the rank of the valuation.
Such reduction steps have been applied in
[
3
]
to the study of the Pythagoras number of function fields, and in
[
9
]
to the study of linkage of Pfister forms over function fields. Here we apply them to the study of the
u
-invariant, to prove
Theorem

I
.

Unlike any previous results in the same direction,
Theorem

I
holds for an arbitrary value group
vK
.
Independently from our work, a weaker version of the implication from
(ii)
to
(i)
was obtained in
[
26
, Theorem 4.1]
for the case where
vK\subseteq\mathbb{R}
(see also
Remark

6.3
).

In any case, the applicability of a local-global principle to a problem on field invariants in quadratic form theory depends on the control over the same problem in the local situation, here specifically on the ability to bound the
u
-invariant of a henselian valued field in terms of the
u
-invariant of the residue field.
The study of quadratic forms over a complete valued field with residue characteristic different from
2
and value group contained in
\mathbb{R}
goes back to William Hetherington Durfee
[
10
]
.
Some of the results were reproven for complete discrete valuations by Tonny Albert Springer
[
37
]
.
The results from
[
10
]
extend to henselian valuations without any condition on the value group.
While these statements and techniques are essentially well-known, to the best of our knowledge the literature does not cover them in the desirable generality or under minimal hypotheses.
We therefore include in
Section

3
a self-contained and elementary presentation of those statements which are relevant to our topic. We indicate in
Remark

3.13
how this compares to the existing literature.

Next to removing any restricting hypothesis on the value group, another aspect giving
Theorem

I
a wider scope compared to preceding results on
u
-invariants of function fields is that it applies for the definition of the ‘general
u
-invariant’ introduced by Elman and Lam
[
12
]
, which is meaningful also for real fields.
In this setting, Claus Scheiderer had already extended
[
17
, Cor. 4.12]
to
[
36
, Theorem 3]
, thus covering
Theorem

I
in the case of a discrete valuation.
In
Section

4
we revisit this definition in a way that applies to all fields, also regardless of their characteristic, and we prove some basic bounds for valued fields.
In dealing with the
u
-invariant of function fields in one variable, the ‘strong
u
-invariant’ of a field was introduced in
[
17
, Sect. 5]
as a short-hand notation.
In
Section

5
, we briefly revisit this notion, basing it on the general
u
-invariant as to fit the study of real and nonreal function fields of any characteristic.

In
Section

6
, we present the proof of
Theorem

I
and then provide some examples of fields where it applies.
The obvious challenge left by
Theorem

I

is that of removing the restriction on the residue field to be of characteristic different from
2
.
A valuation whose residue field has characteristic
2
is also called
dyadic
.
In
Section

7
we do a first step on the problem in the dyadic case.
For the local-global principle from
[
28
]
, this is perfectly possible, as is explained in
[
2
, Theorem 7.1]
.
The study of quadratic forms over the completion

F^{w}
of the function field
F
with respect to a dyadic valuation
w
on
F
, however, poses additional problems.
The
u
-invariant of dyadic complete discretely valued fields was studied in
[
25
]
.
However, the valuations
w
appearing in the local-global principle are not all discrete.
Using ground work from
[
14
]
on quadratic forms over dyadic henselian valued fields, we achieve an alternative proof of the following theorem due to Raman Parimala and Suresh Venapally from
[
34
]
.

Theorem II

(
Theorem

7.11
or
[
34
, Theorem 4.7]
)
.

Let
(K,v)
be a henselian discretely valued field such that
\mathsf{char}(K)=0
,
\mathsf{char}(Kv)=2
and
Kv
is perfect.
Let
F/K
be a function field in one variable.
Then
u(F)=8
if
K
has a finite field extension of even degree, and otherwise
u(F)=4
.

2.
Valuations and torsion-free abelian groups

In the following statements we consider torsion-free abelian groups.
We will denote the group operation additively.

Consider an abelian group
G
. The value
\mathsf{rrk}(G)=\mathsf{dim}_{\mathbb{Q}}(G\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Q})
in
\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
is called the
rational rank of
G
.
Hence,
\mathsf{rrk}(G)=|S|
for any maximal
\mathbb{Z}
-linearly independent subset
S
in
G
.
In particular,
G
is torsion if and only if
\mathsf{rrk}(G)=0
.

The following is an extension of
[
5
, Lemma 3.4]
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
G
be a torsion-free abelian group and
H
a subgroup of
G
with
r=\mathsf{rrk}(G/H)<\infty
.
Let
n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
.
Then
[G:nG]\leqslant n^{r}[H:nH]
.
Furthermore, if
G/H
is finitely generated, then
[G:nG]=n^{r}[H:nH]
.

Proof.

For any
s\in\mathbb{N}
and elements
f_{1},\dots,f_{s}\in G
representing different classes in
G/nG
, denoting by
F
the subgroup of
G
generated by
H\cup\{f_{1},\dots,f_{s}\}
, we have that
F/H
is finitely generated and
f_{1},\dots,f_{s}
represent distinct classes in
F/nF
, whereby
s\leqslant[F:nF]
.
Therefore, to prove that
[G:nG]\leqslant n^{r}[H:nH]
,
we may assume without loss of generality that
G/H
is finitely generated.

We choose
g_{1},\dots,g_{r}\in G
such that
(g_{i}+H\mid 1\leqslant i\leqslant r)

is a maximal
\mathbb{Z}
-linearly independent family in
G/H
and we denote by
G_{0}
the subgroup of
G
generated by
H\cup\{g_{1},\dots,g_{r}\}
.
The choice of
g_{1},\dots,g_{r}
implies that
G/G_{0}
is torsion.

Assume that
G/H
is finitely generated.
Then
G/G_{0}
is finite and
G_{0}\simeq\mathbb{Z}^{r}\times H
, whereby

[G_{0}:nG_{0}]=n^{r}[H:nH]
.
Since
G
is torsion-free, multiplication by
n
induces an isomorphism
G/G_{0}\to nG/nG_{0}
. In particular
[G:G_{0}]=[nG:nG_{0}]
,
whereby
[G_{0}:nG_{0}]=[G:nG]
.
Therefore
[G:nG]=n^{r}[H:nH]
.
∎

By an
ordered abelian group
we mean an abelian group
(G,+,0)
endowed with a total order relation
\leqslant
such that, for any
x,y,z\in G
with
x\leqslant y
, one has
x+z\leqslant y+z
.
Note that any ordered abelian group is torsion-free as a group.
When
(G,+,0,\leqslant)
is an ordered abelian group, a
convex subgroup
is a subgroup
H
of
G
such that for all
x\in H
and
y\in G
with
0\leqslant y\leqslant x
, we have
y\in H
.
One sees that the set of convex subgroups of
G
is totally ordered by inclusion.
The cardinality of the set of proper convex subgroups of
G
will be called the
rank of
G
and denoted by
\mathsf{rk}(G)
.
By
[
15
, Prop. 3.4.1]
, one has
\mathsf{rk}(G)\leqslant\mathsf{rrk}(G)
.

Given a valuation
v
on a field
K
, we refer to the rank of the value group
vK
also as the
rank of
v
and denote it by
\mathsf{rk}(v)
.
Note that an ordered abelian group can be embedded into
\mathbb{R}
if and only if it is of rank at most
1
. (Consequently, the rank-
1
valuations on
K
correspond precisely to the non-trivial absolute values on
K
.)

When
L/K
is an extension of fields and
v
and
w
are valuations on
K
and
L
respectively, we say that
w

extends

v
if
vK\subseteq wL
and
v=w|_{K}
, and we call
(L,w)/(K,v)
an
extension of valued fields
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
F/K
be a function field in one variable and let
w
be a valuation on
F
and
v=w|_{K}
.
Then one of the following holds:

(1)

Fw/Kv
is a finite field extension and
wF/vK
is a finitely generated
\mathbb{Z}
-module with
\mathsf{rrk}(wF/vK)=1
.

(2)

Fw/Kv
is a function field in one variable and
wF/vK
is finite.

(3)

Fw/Kv
is an algebraic field extension and
wF/vK
is a torsion group.

Proof.

This follows from
[
15
, Theorem 3.4.3]
.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
(K,v)
be a henselian valued field and
K_{0}
a subfield of
K
.
There exists a subfield
K_{1}
of
K
containing
K_{0}
such that
vK_{1}/vK_{0}
is torsion,
Kv/K_{1}v
is algebraic and purely inseparable,
v|_{K_{1}}
is henselian and the order of any non-trivial torsion element in
vK/vK_{1}
is a power of
\mathsf{char}(Kv)
.

Proof.

Using Zorn’s Lemma, we choose
K_{1}
as an extension of
K_{0}
inside
K
which is maximal with respect to the property that the quotient
vK_{1}/vK_{0}
is torsion.

Consider
t\in K
algebraic over
K_{1}
. Then
{[vK_{1}(t):vK_{1}]\leqslant[K_{1}(t):K_{1}]<\infty}
,
so
vK_{1}(t)/vK_{1}
is torsion, and it follows by the choice of
K_{1}
that
t\in K_{1}
.
Therefore,
K_{1}
is relatively algebraically closed in
K
.

Consider
t\in K
transcendental over
K_{1}
. Then
K_{1}(t)/K_{1}
is a rational function field in one variable,
and hence it follows by
Proposition

2.2
from the choice of
K_{1}
that the extension
K_{1}(t)v/K_{1}v
is finite. Having this for any
t\in K
, we conclude that the extension
Kv/K_{1}v
is algebraic.

Since
(K,v)
is henselian and
K_{1}
is relatively algebraically closed in
K
, it follows that
(K_{1},v|_{K_{1}})
is henselian as well.

Consider
\xi\in Kv
separable over
K_{1}v
. Since
(K,v)
is henselian,
\xi
is the residue of some element
t\in\mathcal{O}_{v}
algebraic over
K_{1}
.
Since
K_{1}
is algebraically closed in
K
, we obtain that
t\in K_{1}
, whereby
\xi\in K_{1}v
.
This shows that
Kv/K_{1}v
is purely inseparable.

It remains to be shown that the order of any torsion element of
vK/vK_{1}
is either
1
or a power of
\mathsf{char}(Kv)
.
Hence, for
q\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
not divisible by
\mathsf{char}(Kv)
and
t\in{K}^{\times}
such that
qv(t)\in vK_{1}
, we need to show that
v(t)\in vK_{1}
.
Given any such
q
and
t
, we choose
y\in K_{1}
such that
v(y)=qv(t)=v(t^{q})
, whereby
yt^{-q}\in{\mathcal{O}}^{\times}_{v}
.
Since
Kv/K_{1}v
is purely inseparable, there exists a positive integer
m
which is either
1
or a power of
\mathsf{char}(Kv)
such that the residue of
(yt^{-q})^{m}
lies in
K_{1}v
.
We fix
u\in{K}^{\times}_{1}
such that
(yt^{-q})^{m}\in u+\mathfrak{m}_{v}
.
Since
(K,v)
is henselian and
q\neq\mathsf{char}(Kv)
, one has
1+\mathfrak{m}_{v}\subseteq K^{\times q}
. Hence, there exists
z\in{\mathcal{O}}^{\times}_{v}
with
(yt^{-q})^{m}=uz^{q}
.
Hence
(t^{m}z)^{q}=u^{-1}y^{m}\in{K}^{\times}_{1}
.
Since
K_{1}
is relatively algebraically closed in
K
, it follows that
t^{m}z\in{K}^{\times}_{1}
.
Since
v(z)=0
, we obtain that
mv(t)=v(t^{m}z)\in vK_{1}
. Since
qv(t)\in vK_{1}
and
q
is coprime to
m
, we conclude that
v(t)\in vK_{1}
, as desired.
∎

3.
Valued fields and residue forms

We assume familiarity with standard facts from quadratic form theory over fields as covered in particular by the first chapters of
[
21
]
and
[
11
]
.
On this basis, we will give a succinct introduction to the theory of quadratic forms over valued fields with residue characteristic different from
2
.
While in the case of a complete discrete valuation all these results are well-known, we have not found a presentation of this theory in the literature which would provide the statements in this generality.
Our presentation resembles that
of
[
10
]
, a pioneer article on the topic of quadratic forms over valuation rings.
The terminology used in
[
10
]
is meanwhile a bit off the standard, and the statements there are formulated for a valuation that is assumed to be complete of rank-
1
, while the arguments mostly work in more generality.
Our presentation highlights that large parts of the theory may be developed without recourse to a henselization or completion of the valuation ring.
To introduce the concepts and formulate a first few facts, we deal with quadratic forms over a local ring in which
2
is invertible.

Let
R
be a commutative ring, with unity.
A
quadratic form over
R
is a homogeneous polynomial
\varphi
of degree
2
in a prescribed finite tuple of variables whose length is called the
dimension of
\varphi
and denoted by
\mathsf{dim}(\varphi)
.
Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and consider an
n
-dimensional quadratic form
\varphi
over
R
.
It gives rise to a map
R^{n}\to R
, defined by evaluating
\varphi
on
n
-tuples,
as well as to an
R
-bilinear form

\mathfrak{b}_{\varphi}:R^{n}\times R^{n}\to R:(x,y)\mapsto\varphi(x+y)-\varphi(x)-\varphi(y).

There is a unique symmetric
n\times n
-matrix
A
over
R
such that

\mathfrak{b}_{\varphi}
is given by
(x,y)\mapsto xAy^{\mathsf{t}}
, and we call
\varphi

nonsingular
if
\mathsf{det}(A)\in{R}^{\times}
.
We say that
\varphi

represents
a\in R
if
a=\varphi(x)
for some
x\in R^{n}
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
.
Given elements
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in R
, we denote the
n
-dimensional quadratic form
a_{1}X_{1}^{2}+\ldots+a_{n}X_{n}^{2}
over
R
by
\langle a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\rangle_{R}
, or simply by
\langle a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\rangle
if the ring
R
is clear from the context. A quadratic form of this shape (that is, where the terms
X_{i}X_{j}
with
i\neq j
all have coefficient
0
) is called
diagonalized
or a
diagonal form
.
Let

\varphi,\psi\in R[X]
be
n
-dimensional quadratic forms over
R
.
We call
\varphi
and
\psi

isometric
if
\psi(X)=\varphi(X\cdot C)
for some invertible matrix
C\in\mathbb{M}_{n}(R)
, and we write
\varphi\simeq\psi
to indicate this.
If
\varphi
and
\psi
are quadratic forms over
R
,
m=\mathsf{dim}(\varphi)
and
n=\mathsf{dim}(\psi)
, then
we denote by
\varphi\perp\psi
the
(m+n)
-dimensional quadratic form
\varphi(X_{1},\dots,X_{m})+\psi(X_{m+1},\dots,X_{m+n})
over
R
.
We further denote by
\mathbb{H}_{R}
or by
\mathbb{H}
the quadratic form
X_{1}X_{2}
over
R
.

The next few observations are under the assumption that
2\in{R}^{\times}
.
Note that this implies that
\mathbb{H}_{R}\simeq\langle 1,-1\rangle
.
Consider an
n
-dimensional quadratic form
\varphi
in
X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
over
R
.
Then
\varphi=XSX^{\mathsf{t}}
for a unique symmetric matrix
S\in\mathbb{M}_{n}(R)
.
We set
\mathsf{det}(\varphi)=\mathsf{det}(S)
and call this the
determinant of
\varphi
.
Note that
\mathfrak{b}_{\varphi}:R^{n}\times R^{n}\to R
is given by
(x,y)\mapsto x(2S)y^{\mathsf{t}}
, and hence
\varphi
is nonsingular if and only if
\mathsf{det}(S)\in{R}^{\times}
, and we simply call
\varphi

regular
in this case. (We avoid to use the term ‘regular’ without assuming
2\in{R}^{\times}
, as it then occurs with different possible meanings in the literature.)
Note that
\mathsf{det}(\varphi\perp\psi)=\mathsf{det}(\varphi)\cdot\mathsf{det}(\psi)
for any quadratic form
\psi
over
R
.

While our main attention is to the case where
R
is a valuation ring with
2\in{R}^{\times}
, the next few statements hold more generally when
R
is local and
2\in{R}^{\times}
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Assume that
R
is local and
2\in{R}^{\times}
.
Let
\varphi
be a quadratic form over
R
and
b\in{R}^{\times}
.
Then
\varphi
represents
b
if and only if
\varphi\simeq\langle b\rangle_{R}\perp\psi
for a quadratic form
\psi
over
R
.

Proof.

Clearly,
b
is represented by
\langle b\rangle_{R}
and hence also by
\varphi
if
\varphi\simeq\langle b\rangle_{R}\perp\psi
for some quadratic form
\psi
over
R
.
To prove the converse, assume that
\varphi(x)=b
for some
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in R^{n}
.
Since
b\in R^{\times}
and
R
is local, there exists
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
with
x_{i}\in R^{\times}
. We may without loss of generality assume that
x_{1}\in R^{\times}
.
Let
(e_{1},\ldots,e_{n})
be the canonical basis of
R^{n}
. We set
e_{1}^{\prime}=x
and further

e_{i}^{\prime}=e_{i}-(2b)^{-1}\mathfrak{b}_{\varphi}(e_{1}^{\prime},e_{i})e_{1}^{\prime}\in R^{n}
for
2\leqslant i\leqslant n
.
Then
(e_{1}^{\prime},\dots,e_{n}^{\prime})
is also an
R
-basis of
R^{n}
.
Let
C\in\mathbb{M}_{n}(R)
be the matrix of the base change from
(e_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,e_{n}^{\prime})
to
(e_{1},\ldots,e_{n})
.
Then
C
is invertible in
\mathbb{M}_{n}(R)
.
Using that
\mathfrak{b}_{\varphi}
is
R
-bilinear, one computes that
\mathfrak{b}_{\varphi}(e_{1}^{\prime},e_{i}^{\prime})=0
for
2\leqslant i\leqslant n
.
Thus, letting
X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
, we have that

\varphi(X)\simeq\varphi(X\cdot C)=bX_{1}^{2}+\psi(X_{2},\dots,X_{n})
for an
(n-1)
-dimensional quadratic form
\psi
over
R
.
∎

Corollary 3.2
.

Assume that
R
is local and
2\in{R}^{\times}
.
Let
\varphi
be a quadratic form over
R
and
n=\mathsf{dim}(\varphi)
.
Then
\varphi
is regular if and only if
\varphi\simeq\langle a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\rangle_{R}
for certain
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in{R}^{\times}
.

Proof.

Clearly, if
\varphi\simeq\langle a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\rangle_{R}
for certain
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in{R}^{\times}
, then
\varphi
is regular.

The converse is proven by induction on
n
.
For
n=0
, the statement holds trivially.
Assume that
n>0
.
Let
\mathfrak{m}
be the maximal ideal of
R
.
Then
{R}^{\times}=R\smallsetminus\mathfrak{m}
and, since
\varphi
is regular, the entries of the associated matrix cannot all lie in
\mathfrak{m}
.
So there exist
x,y\in R^{n}
with
\mathfrak{b}_{q}(x,y)\notin\mathfrak{m}
.
As

\mathfrak{b}_{q}(x,y)=\varphi(x+y)-\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)
, we conclude that
\varphi(z)\notin\mathfrak{m}

for some
z\in\{x,y,x+y\}
.
Letting
a_{1}=\varphi(z)
, we have that
a_{1}\in R\smallsetminus\mathfrak{m}={R}^{\times}
and obtain by
Proposition

3.1
that
\varphi\simeq\langle a_{1}\rangle\perp\psi

for some
(n-1)
-dimensional quadratic form
\psi
over
R
.
As
\varphi
is regular, so is
\langle a_{1}\rangle\perp\psi
. Hence
a_{1}\mathsf{det}(\psi)\in{R}^{\times}
, which implies that
\mathsf{det}(\psi)\in{R}^{\times}
.
Hence
\psi
is regular.
By the induction hypothesis, we obtain that
\psi\simeq\langle a_{2},\dots,a_{n}\rangle_{R}
for certain
a_{2},\dots,a_{n}\in{R}^{\times}
, whereby

\varphi\simeq\langle a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\rangle_{R}
.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Assume that
R
is local with
2\in{R}^{\times}
.
Let
\mathfrak{m}
denote the maximal ideal of
R
.
Let
x\in{R}^{\times}
and
y,c\in R
.
If
|R/\mathfrak{m}|=3
, then assume that
c\notin 1+\mathfrak{m}
.
Then there exist
x^{\prime}\in R
and
y^{\prime}\in{R}^{\times}
such that
x^{2}-cy^{2}=x^{\prime 2}-cy^{\prime 2}
.

Proof.

If
y\in{R}^{\times}
, then we may take
x^{\prime}=x
and
y^{\prime}=y
.
Assume now that
y\notin{R}^{\times}
.
In view of the hypotheses, there exists
\lambda\in R
such that
1-\lambda^{2}c\in{R}^{\times}
and
2\lambda x+(1+\lambda^{2}c)y\in{R}^{\times}
.
Set
x^{\prime}=(1-\lambda^{2}c)^{-1}((1+\lambda^{2}c)x+2\lambda cy)
and
y^{\prime}=(1-\lambda^{2}c)^{-1}(2\lambda x+(1+\lambda^{2}c)y)
.
Then

\displaystyle(1-\lambda^{2}c)^{2}(x^{\prime 2}-cy^{\prime 2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle((1+\lambda^{2}c)x+2\lambda cy)^{2}-c(2\lambda x+(1+\lambda^{2}c)y)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-\lambda^{2}c)^{2}(x^{2}-cy^{2})\,,

whereby
x^{2}-cy^{2}=x^{\prime 2}-cy^{\prime 2}
.
∎

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, a vector
x\in R^{n}
is called
primitive
if its coordinates generate
R
as an ideal; as
R
is local, this just says that one of the coordinates of
x
lies in
{R}^{\times}
.

For valuation rings, the following statement is
[
6
, Theorem 4.5]
, where it is attributed to J.-L. Colliot-Thélène and M. Kneser. We give a different proof.

Proposition 3.4
.

Assume that
R
is local with
2\in{R}^{\times}
.
Let
\varphi
be a regular quadratic form over
R
,
n=\mathsf{dim}(\varphi)
and
b\in R
. If
x_{0}^{2}b=\varphi(x)
for some
x_{0}\in R
and some primitive vector
x\in R^{n}
, then

b
is represented by
\varphi
.

Proof.

Let
\mathfrak{m}
denote the maximal ideal of
R
.
By
Corollary

3.2
, we may assume without loss of generality that
\varphi=\langle a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\rangle_{R}
with
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in{R}^{\times}
.
Let
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in R^{n}
be primitive and
x_{0}\in R
such that
x_{0}^{2}b=\varphi(x)
.
We may permute
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
and assume without loss of generality that, for some
m\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, we have

x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\in{R}^{\times}
and
x_{m+1},\dots,x_{n}\in\mathfrak{m}
.

Assume first that
|R/\mathfrak{m}|>3
or

a_{1}\not\equiv b\,\,\mathsf{mod}\,\,\mathfrak{m}
.
Set
c=a_{1}^{-1}b
.
Using
Lemma

3.3
, we obtain that

x_{1}^{2}-cx_{0}^{2}=z_{1}^{2}-cz_{0}^{2}
for certain
z_{0}\in{R}^{\times}
and
z_{1}\in R
.
We set
y_{1}=z_{0}^{-1}z_{1}
to obtain that

a_{2}x_{2}^{2}+\dots+a_{n}x_{n}^{2}=bx_{0}^{2}-a_{1}x_{1}^{2}=bz_{0}^{2}-a_{1}z_{1}^{2}=z_{0}^{2}(b-a_{1}y_{1}^{2})\,.

Letting
y_{i}=z_{0}^{-1}x_{i}
for
2\leqslant i\leqslant n
, we obtain that
b=a_{1}y_{1}^{2}+\ldots+a_{n}y_{n}^{2}
, as desired.

Consider now the case where
|R/\mathfrak{m}|=3
and
a_{1}\equiv b\,\,\mathsf{mod}\,\,\mathfrak{m}
.
If
x_{0}\in{R}^{\times}
, then
we may set
y_{i}=x_{0}^{-1}x_{i}
for
1\leqslant i\leqslant n

to obtain the desired equality.
Assume now that
x_{0}\in\mathfrak{m}
.
Since
a_{1}x_{1}^{2}\in{R}^{\times}
and
a_{1}x_{1}^{2}+\ldots+a_{n}x_{n}^{2}=bx_{0}^{2}\in\mathfrak{m}
, we obtain that
m\geqslant 2
, whereby
x_{2}\in{R}^{\times}
.
If
a_{2}\not\equiv b\,\,\mathsf{mod}\,\,\mathfrak{m}
, then we switch the roles of
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
and conclude by the previous case.
So we are left to consider the case
where
a_{1}\equiv a_{2}\equiv b\,\,\mathsf{mod}\,\,\mathfrak{m}
.
We set
c=a_{1}x_{1}^{2}+a_{2}x_{2}^{2}
.
Then
c\equiv-b\not\equiv b\,\,\mathsf{mod}\,\,\mathfrak{m}
, and
we conclude by the previous case that there exist
z,y_{3},y_{4},\ldots,y_{n}\in R

such that
b=cz^{2}+a_{3}y_{3}^{2}+\ldots+a_{n}y_{n}^{2}
. Then letting
y_{1}=zx_{1}
and
y_{2}=zx_{2}
, we obtain that
b=a_{1}y_{1}^{2}+\ldots+a_{n}y_{n}^{2}
.
∎

We collect some standard facts and definitions for quadratic forms over fields, referring to
[
11
, Chap. II]
for more details.
Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and consider an
n
-dimensional quadratic form
\varphi
over a field
K
.
We call
\varphi

isotropic
if there exists
x\in K^{n}\smallsetminus\{0\}
with
\varphi(x)=0
,
anisotropic
otherwise.
We have that
\varphi
is nonsingular if and only if it is isometric to
(m\times\mathbb{H}_{K})\perp\psi
for some natural number
m
and a nonsingular anisotropic quadratic form
\psi
over
K
, where
m\times\mathbb{H}_{K}
denotes the
m
-fold orthogonal sum
\mathbb{H}_{K}\perp\ldots\perp\mathbb{H}_{K}
.
This
\psi
is uniquely determined up to isometry and called the
anisotropic part of
\varphi
.
A nonsingular quadratic form whose anisotropic part is trivial (
0
-dimensional) is called
hyperbolic
.
If
\mathsf{char}(K)\neq 2
, then every anisotropic quadratic form over
K
is nonsingular, and by
Corollary

3.2
isometric to
\langle a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\rangle_{K}
for some
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in{K}^{\times}
.

In the sequel, let
K
be a field and
v
a valuation on
K
.
Recall that we denote by
\mathcal{O}_{v}
the valuation ring of
v
, by
\mathfrak{m}_{v}
its maximal ideal, by
Kv
the residue field
\mathcal{O}_{v}/\mathfrak{m}_{v}
and by
vK
the value group
v({K}^{\times})
. We assume in the sequel that
\mathsf{char}(Kv)\neq 2
. This means that
2\in{\mathcal{O}}^{\times}_{v}
.
In particular,
\mathsf{char}(K)\neq 2
.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
a_{1},\dots,a_{n},b\in{\mathcal{O}}^{\times}_{v}
.

(a)

If
\langle a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\rangle_{K}
represents
b
, then
n\geqslant 1
and there exist
b_{2},\dots,b_{n}\in{\mathcal{O}}^{\times}_{v}
such that
\langle a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\rangle_{\mathcal{O}_{v}}\simeq\langle b,b_{2},\dots,b_{n}\rangle_{\mathcal{O}_{v}}
.

(b)

If
\langle a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\rangle_{K}
is isotropic, then
n\geqslant 2
and there exist
b_{3},\dots,b_{n}\in{\mathcal{O}}^{\times}_{v}
such that

\langle a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\rangle_{\mathcal{O}_{v}}\simeq\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{O}_{v}}\perp\langle b_{3},\dots,b_{n}\rangle_{\mathcal{O}_{v}}
.

Proof.

We set
\varphi=\langle a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\rangle_{\mathcal{O}_{v}}
and
\varphi_{K}=\langle a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\rangle_{K}
.

(a)
Assume that
\varphi_{K}
represents
b
. Then
bx_{0}^{2}=a_{1}x_{1}^{2}+\ldots+a_{n}x_{n}^{2}
for certain

x_{0},\dots,x_{n}\in\mathcal{O}_{v}
, not all contained in
\mathfrak{m}_{v}
.
It follows that
x_{i}\in{\mathcal{O}}^{\times}_{v}
for some
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
.
We obtain by
Proposition

3.4
that
b=a_{1}y_{1}^{2}+\ldots+a_{n}y_{n}^{2}
for certain
y_{1},\dots,y_{n}\in\mathcal{O}_{v}
.
We conclude by
Proposition

3.1
that
\varphi\simeq\langle b\rangle_{\mathcal{O}_{v}}\perp\psi
for some
(n-1)
-dimensional quadratic form
\psi
over
\mathcal{O}_{v}
.
Using
Corollary

3.2
we obtain that
\psi\simeq\langle b_{2},\dots,b_{n}\rangle_{\mathcal{O}_{v}}
for certain
b_{2},\dots,b_{n}\in{\mathcal{O}}^{\times}_{v}
.
Then

\varphi\simeq\langle b,b_{2},\dots,b_{n}\rangle_{\mathcal{O}_{v}}
.

(b)
Assume that
\varphi_{K}
is isotropic. Hence
a_{1}x_{1}^{2}+\ldots+a_{n}x_{n}^{2}=0
for certain
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in K
which are not all zero.
If
x_{1}=0
, then
\langle a_{2},\dots,a_{n}\rangle_{K}
is isotropic.
As every regular isotropic form over
K
represents all elements of
K
, we obtain in any case that
-a_{1}
is represented by
\langle a_{2},\dots,a_{n}\rangle_{K}
.
Applying part
(a)
to
\langle a_{2},\dots,a_{n}\rangle_{K}
, we obtain that

\langle a_{2},\dots,a_{n}\rangle_{\mathcal{O}_{v}}\simeq\langle-a_{1},b_{3},\dots,b_{n}\rangle_{\mathcal{O}_{v}}
for certain
b_{3},\dots,b_{n}\in{\mathcal{O}}^{\times}_{v}
.
As
\langle a_{1},-a_{1}\rangle_{\mathcal{O}_{v}}\simeq\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{O}_{v}}
, we conclude that
\varphi\simeq\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{O}_{v}}\perp\langle b_{3},\dots,b_{n}\rangle_{\mathcal{O}_{v}}
.
∎

A quadratic form
\varphi
over
K
is called
v
-unimodular
if
\varphi\simeq\langle a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\rangle_{K}
for
n=\mathsf{dim}(\varphi)
and certain
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in{\mathcal{O}}^{\times}_{v}
.

Corollary 3.6
.

A regular quadratic form over
K
is
v
-unimodular if and only if its anisotropic part is
v
-unimodular.

Proof.

This follows by induction from
Proposition

3.5

(b)
.
∎

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
a_{1},\dots,a_{n},c_{1},\dots,c_{n}\in{\mathcal{O}}^{\times}_{v}
. Assume that we have
\langle a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\rangle_{K}\simeq\langle c_{1},\dots,c_{n}\rangle_{K}
. Then

\langle a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\rangle_{\mathcal{O}_{v}}\simeq\langle c_{1},\dots,c_{n}\rangle_{\mathcal{O}_{v}}
and

\langle\overline{a}_{1},\dots,\overline{a}_{n}\rangle_{Kv}\simeq\langle\overline{c}_{1},\dots,\overline{c}_{n}\rangle_{Kv}
.

Proof.

An isometry over
\mathcal{O}_{v}
induces an isometry over
Kv
, so we only need to prove the first part. The proof is by induction on
n
.
For
n=0
there is nothing to prove.
Assume that
n\geqslant 1
.
As
c_{1}
is represented by
\langle a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\rangle_{K}
,

Proposition

3.5
yields that

\langle a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\rangle_{\mathcal{O}_{v}}\simeq\langle c_{1},b_{2},\dots,b_{n}\rangle_{\mathcal{O}_{v}}
for certain
b_{2},\dots,b_{n}\in{\mathcal{O}}^{\times}_{v}
.
Then
\langle c_{1},\dots,c_{n}\rangle_{K}\simeq\langle a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\rangle_{K}\simeq\langle c_{1},b_{2},\dots,b_{n}\rangle_{K}
.
Witt’s Cancellation Theorem
[
21
, Chap. I, Theorem 4.2]
yields that

\langle c_{2},\dots,c_{n}\rangle_{K}\simeq\langle b_{2},\dots,b_{n}\rangle_{K}
.
Now, we obtain from the induction hypothesis, that

\langle c_{2},\dots,c_{n}\rangle_{\mathcal{O}_{v}}\simeq\langle b_{2},\dots,b_{n}\rangle_{\mathcal{O}_{v}}
.
Then
\langle a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\rangle_{\mathcal{O}_{v}}\simeq\langle c_{1},b_{2},\dots,b_{n}\rangle_{\mathcal{O}_{v}}\simeq\langle c_{1},c_{2}\dots,c_{n}\rangle_{\mathcal{O}_{v}}
.
∎

Assume that
\varphi
is
v
-unimodular.
Let
n=\mathsf{dim}(\varphi)
and
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in{\mathcal{O}}^{\times}_{v}
such that
\varphi\simeq\langle a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\rangle_{K}
.
We associate to
\varphi
the quadratic form
\overline{\varphi}^{v}=\langle\overline{a}_{1},\dots,\overline{a}_{n}\rangle_{Kv}
over
Kv
. By
Proposition

3.7
, the quadratic form
\overline{\varphi}^{v}
is uniquely determined up to isometry by
\varphi
. We call
\overline{\varphi}^{v}
the
residue form of
\varphi
(
with respect to
v
). We often simply write
\overline{\varphi}
for
\overline{\varphi}^{v}
, if the context clarifies the reference to the valuation
v
.

Corollary 3.8
.

Let
\varphi
be a
v
-unimodular quadratic form over
K
. If
\varphi
is isotropic, then
\overline{\varphi}^{v}
is isotropic.

Proof.

This follows directly from
Proposition

3.5

(b)
.
∎

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
\varphi
be a regular quadratic form over
K
.
Let
\mathcal{C}
be a set of representatives of
{K}^{\times}/v^{-1}(2vK)
.
There exist
r\in\mathbb{N}
, distinct
c_{1},\dots,c_{r}\in\mathcal{C}
,
and
v
-unimodular quadratic forms
\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{r}
over
K
such that

\varphi\simeq c_{1}\varphi_{1}\perp\ldots\perp c_{r}\varphi_{r}
.

Proof.

By the hypothesis on
\mathcal{C}
we have
vK=\bigcup_{c\in\mathcal{C}}(v(c)+2vK)
.
We fix a diagonalization of
\varphi
and then choose

c_{1},\dots,c_{r}\in\mathcal{C}
such
that

\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}(v(c_{i})+2vK)
contains the values of all entries of this diagonalization.
For
i\in\{1,\dots,r\}
and
a\in{K}^{\times}
with
v(a)\in v(c_{i})+2vK
, we have
\langle a\rangle\simeq\langle c_{i}u\rangle=c_{i}\langle u\rangle
for some
u\in{\mathcal{O}}^{\times}_{v}
.
Hence, any of the diagonal entries can be replaced by
c_{i}u
for some
i\in\{1,\dots,r\}
and
u\in{\mathcal{O}}^{\times}_{v}
.
This leads to a presentation
\varphi\simeq c_{1}\varphi_{1}\perp\ldots\perp c_{r}\varphi_{r}
where
\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{r}
are
v
-unimodular quadratic forms over
K
.
∎

We come to the main statements of this section.
They show that, if the valuation
v
on
K
is henselian, then unimodular quadratic forms over
K
are characterised by their residue forms.
We will formulate those statements under the hypothesis that
1+\mathfrak{m}_{v}\subseteq{K}^{\times 2}
.
This hypothesis holds in particular when
v
is henselian. (It is equivalent to
v
being
2
-henselian
; see
Remark

7.3
.)

Theorem 3.10
.

Assume that
1+\mathfrak{m}_{v}\subseteq{K}^{\times 2}\!
.
Let
\varphi
be a
v
-unimodular quadratic form over
K
and
b\in{\mathcal{O}}^{\times}_{v}
.
The following equivalences hold:

(a)

\varphi
represents
b
if and only if
\overline{\varphi}^{v}
represents
\overline{b}
.

(b)

\varphi
is isotropic if and only if
\overline{\varphi}^{v}
is isotropic.

(c)

\varphi
is hyperbolic if and only if
\overline{\varphi}^{v}
is hyperbolic.

Proof.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in{\mathcal{O}}^{\times}_{v}
be such that

\varphi\simeq\langle a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\rangle_{K}
, whereby
\overline{\varphi}\simeq\langle\overline{a}_{1},\dots,\overline{a}_{n}\rangle_{Kv}
.

(a)
If
\varphi
represents
b
over
K
, then it represents
b
over
\mathcal{O}_{v}
, by
Proposition

3.5
, and hence
\overline{\varphi}^{v}
represents
\overline{b}
over
Kv
.
For the converse, assume that
\overline{\varphi}^{v}
represents
\overline{b}
over
Kv
.
Hence, there exist
x\in\mathcal{O}_{v}^{n}
such that
\varphi(x)\equiv b\,\,\mathsf{mod}\,\,\mathfrak{m}_{v}
.
It follows that
\varphi(x)b^{-1}\in 1+\mathfrak{m}_{v}\subseteq{K}^{\times 2}
, whereby
\varphi(x){K}^{\times 2}=b{K}^{\times 2}
.
Hence
\varphi
represents
b
.

(b)

If
\varphi
is isotropic, then so is
\overline{\varphi}^{v}
, by
Corollary

3.8
.
If
\overline{\varphi}
is isotropic, then

\overline{\varphi}^{\prime}=\langle\overline{a}_{2},\dots,\overline{a}_{n}\rangle_{Kv}
represents
-\overline{a}_{1}
, and
it follows by
(a)
that
\varphi^{\prime}
represents
-a_{1}
, whereby
\varphi
is isotropic.

(c)
We prove the equivalence by induction on
n
. If
n=0
, then
\varphi
and
\overline{\varphi}^{v}
are trivially hyperbolic.
Assume that
n>0
.
If
\varphi
is anisotropic, then so is
\overline{\varphi}^{\prime}
, by
(b)
, and as
n>0
, it follows that neither
\varphi
nor
\overline{\varphi}
is hyperbolic.
Assume that
\varphi
is isotropic. By
Proposition

3.5

(b)
, there exist
b_{3},\dots,b_{n}\in{\mathcal{O}}^{\times}_{v}
such that
\varphi\simeq\mathbb{H}_{K}\perp\langle b_{3},\dots,b_{n}\rangle_{K}
and
\overline{\varphi}\simeq\mathbb{H}_{Kv}\perp\langle\overline{b}_{3},\dots,\overline{b}_{n}\rangle_{Kv}
.
Now, by Witt’s Cancellation Theorem
[
21
, Chap. I, Theorem 4.2]
,
\varphi
is hyperbolic if and only if
\rho=\langle b_{3},\dots,b_{n}\rangle_{K}
is hyperbolic, and
\overline{\varphi}^{v}
is hyperbolic if and only if
\overline{\rho}^{v}=\langle\overline{b}_{3},\dots,\overline{b}_{n}\rangle_{Kv}
is hyperbolic.
By the induction hypothesis,
\rho
is hyperbolic if and only if
\overline{\rho}^{v}
is hyperbolic.
From this together we conclude that
\varphi
is hyperbolic if and only if
\overline{\varphi}^{v}
is hyperbolic.
∎

Let
\varphi
be a quadratic form over
K
.
For
m\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote by
m\times\varphi
the
m
-fold orthogonal sum
\varphi\perp\ldots\perp\varphi
.
We say that
\varphi
is
torsion
if
m\times\varphi
is hyperbolic for some
m\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
.

Part of the following statement recovers
[
10
, Theorem 1]
and its corollary.

Theorem 3.11

(Durfee)
.

Assume that
1+\mathfrak{m}_{v}\subseteq{K}^{\times 2}
.
Let
\varphi
be a quadratic form over
K
.
Let
r\in\mathbb{N}
,
c_{1},\dots,c_{r}\in{K}^{\times}
with
v(c_{i})\not\equiv v(c_{j})\,\,\mathsf{mod}\,\,2vK
for
1\leqslant i<j\leqslant r
and let
\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{r}
be

v
-unimodular quadratic forms over
K
such that we have

\varphi\simeq c_{1}\varphi_{1}\perp\ldots\perp c_{r}\varphi_{r}
.
Then
\varphi
is anisotropic, hyperbolic or torsion if and only if
all residue forms
\overline{\varphi}_{1}^{v},\dots,\overline{\varphi}_{r}^{v}
have the corresponding property.

Proof.

Suppose that
\overline{\varphi}_{i}^{v}
is isotropic for some
i\in\{1,\dots,r\}
.
Then
\varphi_{i}
is isotropic, by
Theorem

3.10

(b)
, and hence
\varphi
is isotropic.

Suppose now that
\overline{\varphi}_{i}^{v}
is anisotropic for
1\leqslant i\leqslant r
.
Then
\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{r}
are anisotropic and represent only elements
of
{K}^{\times}
that lie in
v^{-1}(2vK)
.
Consider
x\in K^{n}\smallsetminus\{0\}
for
n=\mathsf{dim}(\varphi)
and let
a=\varphi(x)
.
As
\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{r}
are anisotropic and
\varphi\simeq c_{1}\varphi_{1}\perp\ldots\perp c_{r}\varphi_{r}
, we obtain that
a=c_{1}a_{1}+\ldots+c_{r}a_{r}
for some
(a_{1},\dots,a_{r})\in K^{r}\smallsetminus\{0\}
with
v(a_{1}),\dots,v(a_{r})\in 2vK\cup\{\infty\}
.
For
1\leqslant i<j\leqslant r
, we have
v(c_{i})\not\equiv v(c_{j})\mod 2vK
, so either
v(c_{i}a_{i})\neq v(c_{j}a_{j})
or
a_{i}=a_{j}=0
. As
(a_{1},\dots,a_{r})\neq 0
, we conclude that

v(a)=\mathsf{min}\{v(a_{i}c_{i})\mid 1\leqslant i\leqslant r\}<\infty
, whereby
a\neq 0
.
This argument shows that
\varphi
is anisotropic.

Having thus shown the equivalence for anisotropy, we will use it now to establish the equivalence for hyperbolicity.
For
i\in\{1,\dots,r\}
, let
\psi_{i}
denote the anisotropic part of
\varphi_{i}
and let
m_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
\varphi_{i}\simeq m_{i}\times\mathbb{H}\perp\psi_{i}
.
Set
m=m_{1}+\ldots+m_{r}
and
\psi=c_{1}\psi_{1}\perp\ldots\perp c_{r}\psi_{r}
.
Then
\varphi\simeq m\times\mathbb{H}\perp\psi
.
By
Corollary

3.6
,
\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{r}
are
v
-unimodular.
It follows by the above that
\psi
is anisotropic.
Therefore
\varphi
is hyperbolic if and only if
\psi
is trivial, if and only if

\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{r}
are all trivial.
Furthermore, for
1\leqslant i\leqslant r
, we have by part
(c)
of
Theorem

3.10
that
\overline{\varphi}_{i}^{v}
is hyperbolic if and only if
\varphi_{i}
is hyperbolic, which is if and only if the anisotropic part
\psi_{i}
is trivial.
This establishes the equivalence for hyperbolicity.

Consider now
m\in\mathbb{N}
.
Note that
m\times\varphi\simeq c_{1}(m\times\varphi_{1})\perp\ldots\perp c_{r}(m\times\varphi_{r})
and
that
m\times\varphi_{i}
is
v
-unimodular for
1\leqslant i\leqslant r
.
Having this for all positive integers
m
, the equivalence for being torsion follows from that for hyperbolicity.
∎

Preceding treatments of quadratic forms over complete nondyadically discretely valued (or more generally
2
-henselian nondyadically valued) fields often focussed more on describing the structure of the Witt ring of quadratic forms than on determining isotropy etc. of individual quadratic forms. While we do not use the Witt ring in the present article, we wish to mention the following direct consequence of
Theorem

3.11
, which relates to the Witt ring point of view.

Two regular quadratic forms
\varphi
and
\psi
over a field of characteristic different from
2
are called
Witt equivalent
if their
anisotropic parts are isometric, which is if and only if

\varphi\perp-\psi
is hyperbolic.

Corollary 3.12
.

Assume that
1+\mathfrak{m}_{v}\subseteq{K}^{\times 2}
.
Let
r\in\mathbb{N}
,
c_{1},\dots,c_{r}\in{K}^{\times}
with
v(c_{i})\not\equiv v(c_{j})\,\,\mathsf{mod}\,\,2vK
for
1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n
and let
\vartheta_{1},\psi_{1},\dots,\vartheta_{r},\psi_{r}
be

v
-unimodular quadratic forms over
K
.
Then the forms
c_{1}\vartheta_{1}\perp\ldots\perp c_{r}\vartheta_{r}
and
c_{1}\psi_{1}\perp\ldots\perp c_{r}\psi_{r}
over
K
are Witt equivalent if and only if, for
1\leqslant i\leqslant r
, the forms
\overline{\vartheta}_{i}
and
\overline{\psi}_{i}
over
Kv
are Witt equivalent.

Proof.

Letting
\varphi_{i}=\vartheta_{i}\perp-\psi_{i}
for
1\leqslant i\leqslant r
, the statement follows from the criterion for the hyperbolicity of the quadratic form
\varphi=c_{1}\varphi_{1}\perp\ldots\perp c_{r}\varphi_{r}
over
K
which is contained in
Theorem

3.11
.
∎

Remark 3.13
.

When restricting attention to complete discrete valuation rings and their fraction fields, most (if not all) of the results in this section are well-known.
To the best of our knowledge these first appeared explicitly in Durfee’s doctoral research
[
10
]
for fields with a complete rank-
1
valuation.
Inspection reveals that Durfee’s proofs (and other versions found later) mostly work without changes for general valued fields
(K,v)
with
1+\mathfrak{m}_{v}\subseteq K^{\times 2}
and
2\in\mathcal{O}_{v}^{\times}
, thus in particular for non-dyadic henselian valued fields, which would be sufficient for the purposes in the rest of this article.
For our own and the reader’s convenience, we have included here an elementary and self-contained exposition which postpones adding the assumption
1+\mathfrak{m}_{v}\subseteq K^{\times 2}
until the very end.

4.
The generalised
u
-invariant

By the Artin-Schreier Theorem, a field
K
admits a field ordering if and only if
-1
is not a sum of squares in
K
; see e.g.
[
21
, Chap. VIII, Theorem 1.10]
.
This motivates to call the field
K

real
in this case, and
nonreal
otherwise.
Given a field ordering, a quadratic form is assigned a corresponding signature, and there is a notion of being positive or negative definite at this ordering.
A quadratic form which is positive definite at some ordering cannot be universal, since it cannot represent
-1
. Therefore the definition of the
u
-invariant formulated in the introduction for nonreal fields would have value
\infty
for any real field.
Elman and Lam
[
12
]
suggested a variation to the definition of the
u
-invariant that is meaningful also for real fields while not changing its meaning for nonreal fields.
We refer to
[
32
, Chap. 8]
for a general discussion of the
u
-invariant, including several variations on how to define it for real fields on the one hand and for fields of characteristic
2
on the other hand.

In this section, we discuss relations between the values of the
u
-invariant for a given valued field and its residue field.
There seems a want in the literature for such a treatment covering both real fields and fields of characteristic
2
in a compatible way.
Most of the statements here are well-known in the case where the valued field is nonreal and the residue field is of characteristic different from
2
.
Including the case of real fields in the discussion leads to some technicalities, which can be skipped if one restricts the attention to nonreal fields.

Let
K
be a field.
Recall that, if
\mathsf{char}(K)\neq 2
, we defined a quadratic form
\varphi
over
K
to be a
torsion form
if
m\times\varphi=\varphi\perp\ldots\perp\varphi
is hyperbolic for some
m\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
.
When
\mathsf{char}(K)=2
, we take the convention that every quadratic form over
K
is torsion.
The
general
u
-invariant of
K
as defined by Elman and Lam in
[
12
]
is given by

u(K)\,=\,\mathsf{sup}\{\mathsf{dim}(\varphi)\mid\varphi\mbox{ anisotropic torsion form over }K\}\,\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}.

Note that, if
K
is nonreal, then every anisotropic quadratic form over
K
is a torsion form, and hence in this case the definition can be simplified to

u(K)\,=\,\mathsf{sup}\{\mathsf{dim}(\varphi)\mid\varphi\mbox{ anisotropic quadratic form over }K\},

which corresponds to how the
u
-invariant was originally defined by Lenz.
This further agrees with the definition mentioned in the introduction when
K
is nonreal of characteristic different from
2
.

Pfister’s Local-Global Principle
[
21
, Chap. VIII, Sect. 3]
states that, over a real field, a regular quadratic form is torsion if and only if it has signature zero at every field ordering. Therefore, if
K
is real, then the
u
-invariant of
K
has to be even or infinite.

The following construction will be used to produce certain torsion forms in characteristic different from
2
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Assume that
\mathsf{char}(K)\neq 2
.
Let
\vartheta
be a regular quadratic form over
K
.
Let
c\in{K}^{\times}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
c
is a sum of
2^{k}
squares in
K
.
Then
\vartheta\perp-c\vartheta
is a torsion form. More precisely,
2^{k}\times(\vartheta\perp-c\vartheta)
is hyperbolic.

Proof.

The hypothesis implies that the form
2^{k}\times\langle 1,-c\rangle_{K}
is isotropic, and since it is a Pfister form, it follows by
[
21
, Theorem X.2.9]
that it is hyperbolic.
Consequently,
2^{k}\times\langle a,-ac\rangle
is hyperbolic for every
a\in{K}^{\times}
.
Let
n=\mathsf{dim}(\vartheta)
.
By
Corollary

3.2
, there exist
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in{K}^{\times}
such that
\vartheta\simeq\langle a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\rangle_{K}
.
Then
2^{k}\times(\vartheta\perp-c\vartheta)\simeq\mathop{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{2.1}{$\displaystyle\perp$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.5}{$\textstyle\perp$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.5}{$\scriptstyle\perp$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.5}{$\scriptscriptstyle\perp$}}}}}\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n}(2^{k}\times\langle a_{i},-ca_{i}\rangle_{K})
, which is hyperbolic.
∎

Let
v
in the sequel be a valuation on
K
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\psi
be an
n
-dimensional quadratic form over
Kv
.
Then there exists an
n
-dimensional quadratic form
\Psi
over
\mathcal{O}_{v}
which reduces modulo
\mathfrak{m}_{v}
to
\psi
.
Moreover, if
\psi
is anisotropic, then any such form
\Psi
is anisotropic over
K
and satisfies
v(\Psi(x))\in 2vK
for every
x\in K^{n}\smallsetminus\{0\}
.

Proof.

See
[
2
, Proposition 4.2]
.
∎

In some cases where we will use
Lemma

4.2
, we want the lifted form
\Psi
in addition to be a torsion form over
K
.
To achieve this some extra considerations are necessary when
K
is real.

For a ring
R
we denote by
{\mathsf{\Sigma}{R}^{(2)}}
the set of elements of
R
which are sums of squares in
R
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Assume that
Kv
is nonreal and
\mathsf{char}(K)\neq 2
.
The following hold:

(a)

Every element of
Kv
is the residue modulo
\mathfrak{m}_{v}
of an element of
\mathcal{O}_{v}\cap{\mathsf{\Sigma}{K}^{(2)}}
.

(b)

Every anisotropic even-dimensional quadratic form over
Kv
is the reduction modulo
\mathfrak{m}_{v}
of a
quadratic form defined over
\mathcal{O}_{v}
which is an anisotropic
torsion form over
K
.

Proof.

(a)

If
\mathsf{char}(Kv)=2
, then for
x\in\mathcal{O}_{v}
we have
x+2(x^{2}+1)\equiv x\,\,\mathsf{mod}\,\,\mathfrak{m}_{v}
and
x+2(x^{2}+1)=2(x+\frac{1}{4})^{2}+\frac{15}{8}\in{\mathsf{\Sigma}{K}^{(2)}}
.
If
Kv
is nonreal with
\mathsf{char}(Kv)\neq 2
, then there exists
z\in{\mathsf{\Sigma}{\mathcal{O}}^{(2)}}
with
z\equiv-1\,\,\mathsf{mod}\,\,\mathfrak{m}_{v}
, and
for
x\in\mathcal{O}_{v}
we obtain that
\frac{1}{4}((x+1)^{2}+z(x-1)^{2})\in{\mathsf{\Sigma}{\mathcal{O}_{v}}^{(2)}}
and

x\equiv\frac{1}{4}((x+1)^{2}+z(x-1)^{2})\,\,\mathsf{mod}\,\,\mathfrak{m}_{v}
.

(b)

Consider an arbitrary anisotropic
2
-dimensional form
\beta
over
Kv
.
By
(a)
, we may choose
a,b,c\in\mathcal{O}_{v}\cap{\mathsf{\Sigma}{K}^{(2)}}
such that
the
2
-dimensional quadratic form
B=aX_{1}^{2}+bX_{1}X_{2}-cX_{2}^{2}
over
\mathcal{O}_{v}
reduces modulo
\mathfrak{m}_{v}
to
\beta
.
Since
\beta
is anisotropic over
Kv
, so is
B
over
K
.
Note that

B\simeq a\langle 1,-(b^{2}+4ac)\rangle
over
K
and
b^{2}+4ac\in{\mathsf{\Sigma}{K}^{(2)}}
, whence it follows by
Lemma

4.1
that
B
is a torsion form.
This shows the claim for
2
-dimensional quadratic forms.
By
[
11
, Propositions 7.29 and 7.31]

every even-dimensional quadratic form is an orthogonal sum of
2
-dimensional quadratic forms. Hence the statement follows in general.
∎

The following is a partial extension of
[
5
, Theorem 5.2]
, including now the case of residue characteristic
2
.

Proposition 4.4
.

We have

u(K)\geqslant[vK:2vK]\cdot 2\lfloor\mbox{$\frac{u(Kv)}{2}$}\rfloor\,.

Moreover,

u(K)\geqslant[vK:2vK]\cdot u(Kv)

holds under any of the following conditions:

(i)

K
is nonreal;

(ii)

Kv
is real;

(iii)

u(Kv)
is even;

(iv)

v({\mathsf{\Sigma}{K}^{(2)}}\smallsetminus\{0\})\not\subseteq 2vK
.

Proof.

In the case where
Kv
is real, the statement is fully covered by
[
5
, Theorem 5.2]
.
Hence we only consider the complementary situation and assume from now on that
Kv
is nonreal.

Let
n,r\in\mathbb{N}
arbitrary with
2^{r}\leqslant[vK:2vK]
and
n\leqslant u(Kv)
, and assume that
n
is even in the case where
K
is real and
v({\mathsf{\Sigma}{K}^{(2)}}\smallsetminus\{0\})\subseteq 2vK
.
We will show that
u(K)\geqslant 2^{r}n
.
This will establish the statement.

By the choice of
n
there exists an anisotropic
n
-dimensional quadratic form
\varphi
over
Kv
.
Then
\varphi
is the reduction modulo
\mathfrak{m}_{v}
of
an
n
-dimensional quadratic form
\Phi
over
\mathcal{O}_{v}
, and by
Lemma

4.3
we may choose
\Phi
to be a torsion form over
K
when
n
is even.
Also if
K
is nonreal,
\Phi
is torsion.
In any case, since
\varphi
is anisotropic, it follows by
Lemma

4.2
that
v(\Phi(x))\in 2vK
for every
x\in K^{n}\smallsetminus\{0\}
.

We may assume that
r\geqslant 1
unless
vK=2vK
.
By the choice of
r
, we may choose
c_{1},\ldots,c_{r}\in{K}^{\times}
such that
v(c_{1}),\dots,v(c_{r})
represent
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-linearly independent classes in
vK/2vK
, and we may choose to have
c_{1}\in-{\mathsf{\Sigma}{K}^{(2)}}
except when
v({\mathsf{\Sigma}{K}^{(2)}}\smallsetminus\{0\})\subseteq 2vK
.
We denote by
\mathcal{P}
the power set of
\{1,\dots,r\}
, and for
I\in\mathcal{P}
we set
c_{I}=\prod_{i\in I}c_{i}
.
We consider the quadratic form
\Psi=\mathop{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{2.1}{$\displaystyle\perp$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.5}{$\textstyle\perp$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.5}{$\scriptstyle\perp$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.5}{$\scriptscriptstyle\perp$}}}}}\displaylimits_{I\in\mathcal{P}}c_{I}\Phi
over
K
.
It follows by the properties of
\Phi
and
c_{1},\dots,c_{r}
that
\Psi
is anisotropic.
We claim that
\Psi
is torsion.
This is obvious when
\Phi
is torsion.
Assume that
\Phi
is not torsion. In this case
n
is odd,
vK\neq 2vK
,
r\geqslant 1
and
-c_{1}\in{\mathsf{\Sigma}{K}^{(2)}}
, and since
\Psi=\Theta\perp c_{1}\Theta
with
\Theta=\mathop{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{2.1}{$\displaystyle\perp$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.5}{$\textstyle\perp$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.5}{$\scriptstyle\perp$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.5}{$\scriptscriptstyle\perp$}}}}}\displaylimits_{I\in\mathcal{P^{\prime}}}c_{I}\Phi
where
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
is the power set of
\{2,\dots,r\}
, we conclude by
Lemma

4.1
that
\Psi
is torsion.
Hence
u(K)\geqslant\mathsf{dim}(\Psi)=2^{r}n
.
Having this for all
n
and
r
as above, the statement follows.
∎

We obtain the following lower bound for the growth of the
u
-invariant under finitely generated field extensions in terms of the transcendence degree.
The argument is well-known at least for diagonal quadratic forms over nonreal fields, see e.g.
[
35
, Cor. 2
(ii)
]
.

Corollary 4.5
.

Let
F/K
be a finitely generated field extension and let
n
be its transcendence degree.
Then there exists a finite field extension
L/K
such that
u(F)\geqslant 2^{n}u(L)
.

Proof.

The statement is trivial when
n=0
.
Consider the case where
n=1
.
In this case
F/K
is a function field in one variable.
We pick any discrete valuation
v
on
F
which is trivial on
K
.
We set
L=Fv
.
Then
L/F
is a finite field extension and
[\mathbb{Z}:2\mathbb{Z}]=2
.
Moreover, if
F
is real but
Fv
is nonreal, then by
[
4
, Proposition 4.2]
, there exists
x\in{\mathsf{\Sigma}{F}^{(2)}}
with
v(x)=1
.
Hence, in any case we obtain by
Proposition

4.4
that
u(F)\geqslant 2u(L)
.
This proves the statement for
n=1
. The general statement follows by induction on
n
.
∎

Determining the
u
-invariant of a valued field in terms of the value group and the
u
-invariant of the residue field is possible in residue characteristic different from
2
provided that roots of quadratic equations can be lifted from the residue field.
This is also well-known over nonreal fields, see e.g.
[
35
, Theorem 4]
.

Proposition 4.6
.

Assume that
1+\mathfrak{m}_{v}\subseteq{K}^{\times 2}
and
\mathsf{char}(Kv)\neq 2
.
Then

u(K)=[vK:2vK]\cdot u(Kv)\,.

Proof.

In view of
Theorem

3.10

(a)
, the hypothesis implies that
K
is real if and only if
Kv
is real.
In particular,
Proposition

4.4
yields that

u(K)\geqslant[vK:2vK]\cdot u(Kv)\,.

It remains to show the converse inequality.

For this, we set
s=[vK:2vK]
and
u=u(Kv)
and assume without loss of generality that
s<\infty
.
Note that
v^{-1}(2K)={\mathcal{O}}^{\times}_{v}{K}^{\times 2}
.
We fix representatives
c_{1},\dots,c_{s}\in{K}^{\times}
of the different classes of
{K}^{\times}/{\mathcal{O}}^{\times}_{v}{K}^{\times 2}
.
Let
\varphi
now be an anisotropic torsion form over
K
.
We write
\varphi\simeq c_{1}\varphi_{1}\perp\ldots\perp c_{s}\varphi_{s}
where
\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{s}
are
v
-unimodular forms over
K
.
Then
\overline{\varphi}_{1}^{v},\dots,\overline{\varphi}_{s}^{v}
are anisotropic torsion forms over
K
, by
Theorem

3.11
.
Hence
\mathsf{dim}(\varphi_{i})=\mathsf{dim}(\overline{\varphi}_{i}^{v})\leqslant u
for
1\leqslant i\leqslant s
, whereby
\mathsf{dim}(\varphi)=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\mathsf{dim}(\varphi_{i})\leqslant s\cdot u
.
This shows that
u(K)\leqslant s\cdot u
.
∎

A similarly general statement covering the case of residue characteristic
2
seems out of reach.
Only the following statement is known to hold without restriction on the residue characteristic.
One may trace it back to Kaplansky
[
18
]
, who observes that
u(K)=2u(k)
in the case where
K=k(\!(t)\!)
for a field
k
with
\mathsf{char}(k)\neq 2
.

If
\mathsf{char}(K)=2
, we denote by
[K:K^{(2)}]
the degree of imperfection of
K
, i.e. the degree of the field extension
K/K^{(2)}
where
K^{(2)}=\{x^{2}\mid x\in K\}
.

Theorem 4.7

(Kaplansky; Mamone-Moresi-Wadsworth)
.

Assume that
v
is discrete and henselian.
If
\mathsf{char}(K)=2
, then assume further that
[K:K^{(2)}]=2[Kv:Kv^{(2)}]
.
Then
u(K)=2u(Kv)
.

Proof.

When
\mathsf{char}(Kv)\neq 2
this follows from
Proposition

4.6
.
See
[
25
, Theorem 2]
for the case when
\mathsf{char}(Kv)=2
.
∎

Note that, if
\mathsf{char}(K)=2
and
v
is complete and discrete, then
[K:K^{(2)}]=2[Kv:Kv^{(2)}]
holds, whereby
Theorem

4.7
applies.
In a more general context, when
\mathsf{char}(Kv)=2
, one may have an inequality
u(K)>[vK:2vK]\cdot u(Kv)
, even when
(K,v)
is henselian and complete.
We sketch an example, where we omit the verification of valuation-theoretic details.

Example 4.8
.

Let
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{2}
denote the algebraic closure of
\mathbb{F}_{2}
and
K
the perfect closure (maximal purely inseparable extension) of
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{2}(t)
or of
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{2}(\!(t)\!)
. That is,
K=\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{2}(t^{2^{-n}}|\,n\in\mathbb{N})
or
K=\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{2}(\!(t)\!)(t^{2^{-n}}|\,n\in\mathbb{N})
, respectively.
Let
v
denote the unique extension of the
t
-adic valuation on
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{2}(t)
and
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{2}(\!(t)\!)
to
K
.
We also denote the natural extension of this valuation to the completion
K^{v}
by
v
.
Note that
(K^{v},v)
is a complete rank-
1
valued field. In particular, it is henselian.
Since
K
is perfect, so is
K^{v}
, whereby trivially
1+\mathfrak{m}_{v}\subseteq{(K^{v})}^{\times 2}
.
The value group
vK
is
2
-divisible, and the residue field
Kv=\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{2}
is algebraically closed, whereby
[vK:2vK]=1=u(Kv)
.
But
u(K)>1
, because the quadratic form
X_{1}^{2}+X_{1}X_{2}+t^{-1}X_{2}^{2}
is anisotropic over
K^{v}
.

With a bit more work, an example can also be constructed of a complete henselian valued field
(K,v)
with
\mathsf{char}(K)=0
,
\mathsf{char}(Kv)=2
, and
1+\mathfrak{m}_{v}\subseteq{K}^{\times 2}
, whereas
u(K)>[vK:2vK]\cdot u(Kv)
.

5.
The generalised strong
u
-invariant

We are now prepared to study the
u
-invariant of function fields in one variable.
Let
K
be a field.
Extending
[
4
, Sect. 5]
(by assuming nothing further on
K
),
we define

\widehat{u}(K)\,=\,\mbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}\mathsf{sup}\,\{u(F)\mid F/K\mbox{ function field in one variable}\}\,\in\mbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}.

We call
\widehat{u}(K)
the
generalised strong
u
-invariant of
K
.
For nonreal fields of characteristic different from
2
,
\widehat{u}
coincides with the ‘strong
u
-invariant’ defined in
[
17
]
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
L/K
be a finite field extension.
Then

u(L)\leqslant\hbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}u(L(X))\leqslant\widehat{u}(K)\,.

If
L/K
is a simple extension or
\mathsf{char}(K)\neq 2
, then

u(L)\leqslant\hbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}u(K(X))\leqslant\widehat{u}(K)
.

Proof.

Since
L
is the residue field of a discrete valuation on
L(X)
and since
L(X)
is real if and only if
L
is real, the first inequality follows from
Proposition

4.4
.
The second inequality is clear from the definition of
\widehat{u}(K)
.
The refinement under the assumption that
L/K
is simple or
\mathsf{char}(K)\neq 2
now follows by
[
5
, Proposition 6.1]
and its proof.
∎

Example 5.2
.

In
[
7
]
, an example is constructed of a field
K
such that
u(L)=2
holds for every finite field extension
L/K
, while there exists a pair of quadratic forms in
5
variables over
K
having no nontrivial common zero. As explained in
[
1
, Introduction]
, this implies that
u(K(X))\geqslant 6
.
Hence in this case we obtain that
\widehat{u}(K)\geqslant\frac{1}{2}u(K(X))\geqslant 3>2=\mathsf{sup}\{u(L)\mid L/K\mbox{ finite field extension}\}
.

Proposition 5.3
.

Assume that
u(L)=\widehat{u}(K)
for every finite field extension
L/K(\sqrt{-1})
.
Then
u(F)=2\,\widehat{u}(K)
for every function field in one variable
F/K
.

Proof.

Consider a function field in one variable
F/K
.
By the definition of
\widehat{u}(K)
, we have
u(F)\leqslant 2\widehat{u}(K)
.
We fix
x\in F
transcendental over
K
.
There exists a discrete valuation
w
on
F
which is trivial on
K
and with
w(x^{2}+1)>0
. Then
Fw
is a finite field extension of
K(\sqrt{-1})
, whence
u(Fw)=\widehat{u}(K)
by the hypothesis.
Since
[wF:2wF]=2
, we conclude by
Proposition

4.4
that
u(F)\geqslant 2\widehat{u}(K)
. Therefore
u(F)=2\widehat{u}(K)
.
∎

The following is a partial extension of
[
13
, Theorem]
with no assumption on
\mathsf{char}(K)
. It is also a variation of
[
2
, Theorem 7.3]
.

Proposition 5.4
.

The following are equivalent:

(i)

K(\sqrt{-1})
has no finite field extension of even degree,

(ii)

u(F)=2
for every function field in one variable
F/K
,

(iii)

u(F)<4
for some function field in one variable
F/K
.

If these equivalent conditions hold, then
\widehat{u}(K)=1
, otherwise
\widehat{u}(K)\geqslant 2
.

Proof.

Since any nontrivial torsion form over a field
F
is indefinite at every field ordering of
F
, the implication

({\it\ref{it:herquacl1}\Rightarrow\ref{it:herquacl2}})
follows from
[
2
, Theorem 7.3]
.
Since

({\it\ref{it:herquacl2}\Rightarrow\ref{it:herquacl3}})
is obvious, it therefore remains to prove
({\it\ref{it:herquacl3}\Rightarrow\ref{it:herquacl1}})
.

Let
F/K
be an arbitrary function field in one variable.
Assume that
K(\sqrt{-1})
has a finite extension of even degree.
Then the proof of
[
2
, Theorem 7.3]
shows that there exists an anisotropic totally indefinite
2
-fold Pfister form
\pi
over
F
.
Then
\pi
has signature zero at every ordering of
F
.
Using Pfister’s Local-Global Principle
[
21
, Chap. VIII, Theorem 6.14]
if
K
is real, it follows that
\pi
is a torsion form.
We conclude that
u(F)\geqslant\mathsf{dim}(\pi)\geqslant 4
.
∎

Note that by its definition,
\widehat{u}(K)
is always in
\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
, but we do not know whether it is always in
\mathbb{N}
.
On the other hand, we do not know of any example where
\widehat{u}(K)
is not a power of

.

In the following example and the subsequent statement, we use the concept of

-fields (where

) introduced by S. Lang in
[

]
.

Example 5.5
.

Let
i\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
K
is a
\mathcal{C}_{i}
-field. Then
u(K)\leqslant 2^{i}
.
Moreover, for any
j\in\mathbb{N}
and any field extension
L/K
of transcendence degree

,
[
31
, Theorem 2a]
(which is
[
22
, Theorem 6]
without the hypothesis on ‘normic forms’) yields that
L
is a
\mathcal{C}_{i+j}
-field, whereby
u(L)\leqslant 2^{i+j}
.
Applying this with

, we obtain that
\widehat{u}(K)\leqslant 2^{i}
.

Theorem 5.6

(Lang)
.

Let
(K,v)
be a henselian discretely valued field with
\mathsf{char}(K)=0
and algebraically closed residue field
Kv
.
Then
K
is a
\mathcal{C}_{1}
-field.
In particular, for any
i\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
u(F)\leqslant 2^{i+1}
for any field extension
F/K
of transcendence degree
i
.
Furthermore
\widehat{u}(K)=2
.

Proof.

That
K
is a
\mathcal{C}_{1}
-field is proven in
[
22
, Theorem 10]
in the case where
(K,v)
is complete.
Let
K^{v}
denote the completion of
K
with respect to
v
.
By
[
20
, Theorem 5.9]
, the hypotheses on
(K,v)
imply that
K
is existentially closed in
K^{v}
.
In other terms, every multivariate polynomial over
K
has a zero over
K
provided that it has a zero over
K^{v}
.
Since
K^{v}
is a
\mathcal{C}_{1}
-field, we conclude that
K
is a
\mathcal{C}_{1}
-field.

As
K
carries a discrete valuation,
K(\sqrt{-1})
has a quadratic field extension, so
Proposition

5.4
yields that
\widehat{u}(K)\geqslant 2
.
The rest follows by
Example

5.5
.
∎

Proposition 5.7
.

Let
v
be a valuation on
K
.
Then

\widehat{u}(K)\geqslant[vK:2vK]\cdot\widehat{u}(Kv)\,.

Proof.

Consider an arbitrary function field in one variable
E/Kv
.
By
[
3
, Lemma 5.6]
, there exists a function field in one variable
F/K
and an extension
w
of
v
to
F
such that
Fw\simeq E
, and if
E
is nonreal, then so is
F
.
By
Proposition

4.4
, we obtain that

u(F)\geqslant[wF:2wF]\cdot u(Fw)
.
By
Proposition

2.2
, since
{Fw=E}
and
E/Kv
is transcendental, the quotient group
wF/vK
is finite.
Hence
Proposition

2.1
yields that
[wF:2wF]=[vK:2vK]
.
Therefore

[vK:2vK]\cdot u(E)=[wF:2wF]\cdot u(Fw)\leqslant u(F)\leqslant 2\widehat{u}(K)\,.

Having this for all function fields in one variable
E/Kv
, the statement follows.
∎

In
Theorem

6.2
we will see that the inequality in
Proposition

5.7
becomes an equality when
v
is henselian.

Proposition 5.8
.

Let
v
be a valuation on
K
and
let
F/K
be a function field in one variable.
Then there exists a function field in one variable
E/Kv
such that
u(F)\geqslant 2\cdot[vK:2vK]\cdot\lfloor\frac{u(E)}{2}\rfloor
, and
if
F
is nonreal, then
u(F)\geqslant[vK:2vK]\cdot u(E)
.

Proof.

We choose a valuation
w
on
F
extending
v
such that

Fw/Kv
is a function field in one variable.
(This can be done by fixing
x\in F
transcendental over
K
,
taking the Gauss extension of
v
to
K(x)
with respect to
x
and then extending this valuation further to
F
.)
By
Proposition

2.2
, the quotient group
wF/vK
is finite. Therefore we have
[wF:2wF]=[vK:2vK]
, by
Proposition

2.1
.
We now conclude by
Proposition

4.4
.
∎

The following proposition considers the behaviour of the invariant
\widehat{u}
under algebraic field extensions.
It recovers the fact that
\widehat{u}(K)
is also an upper bound on
u(K^{\prime})
for all finite field extensions
K^{\prime}/K
.

Proposition 5.9
.

Let
K^{\prime}/K
be an algebraic field extension. Then

u(K^{\prime})\leqslant\widehat{u}(K^{\prime})\leqslant\widehat{u}(K)\,.

Moreover, if
K^{\prime}/K
is a direct limit of finite normal extensions of odd degree of
K
, then

\widehat{u}(K^{\prime})=\widehat{u}(K)\,.

Proof.

By
Proposition

5.1
, we have
u(K^{\prime})\leqslant\widehat{u}(K^{\prime})
.
To show that
\widehat{u}(K^{\prime})\leqslant\widehat{u}(K)
, consider
a function field in one variable
F^{\prime}/K^{\prime}
and an anisotropic torsion form
\varphi
over
F^{\prime}
.
There exists a finitely generated subextension
F/K
of
F^{\prime}/K
such that
\varphi
is defined over
F
and is a torsion form over
F
. Moreover, we may choose
F
to be transcendental over
K
.
Then
F/K
is a function field in one variable and
\varphi
is an anisotropic torsion form over
F
.
Hence
\mathsf{dim}(\varphi)\leqslant u(F)\leqslant 2\widehat{u}(K)
.
Since this holds for any anisotropic torsion form
\varphi
over
F^{\prime}
, it follows that
u(F^{\prime})\leqslant 2\widehat{u}(K)
.
Having this for any function field in one variable
F^{\prime}/K^{\prime}
, we conclude that
\widehat{u}(K^{\prime})\leqslant\widehat{u}(K)
.

Assume now that
K^{\prime}/K
is a direct limit of finite normal extensions of odd degree.
It remains to show that in this case
\widehat{u}(K)\leqslant\widehat{u}(K^{\prime})
.
Consider a function field in one variable
F/K
and an anisotropic torsion form
\varphi
over
F
.
There exists a function field in one variable
F^{\prime}/K^{\prime}
such that
F\subseteq F^{\prime}
and
F^{\prime}=K^{\prime}F
.
Since
K^{\prime}/K
is a direct limit of finite normal extensions of odd degree, so is
F^{\prime}/F
.
Therefore, Springer’s Theorem for odd degree extensions
[
21
, Chap. VII, Theorem 2.7]
yields that
\varphi
remains anisotropic over
F^{\prime}
. Since it also remains a torsion form over
F^{\prime}
, we obtain that
\mathsf{dim}(\varphi)\leqslant u(F^{\prime})\leqslant 2\widehat{u}(K^{\prime})
. Since this holds for all anisotropic torsion forms
\varphi
over
F
, we see that
u(F)\leqslant 2\widehat{u}(K^{\prime})
.
Having this for all function fields in one variable
F/K
, we conclude that
\widehat{u}(K)\leqslant\widehat{u}(K^{\prime})
.
∎

For a general algebraic extension
K^{\prime}/K
, a strict inequality
\widehat{u}(K^{\prime})<\widehat{u}(K)
may occur.
For example, if
K^{\prime}
is an algebraic closure of
K
while
K(\sqrt{-1})
has a finite field extension of even degree, then
Proposition

5.4
yields that
\widehat{u}(K^{\prime})=1<\widehat{u}(K)
.

Question 5.10
.

Does
\widehat{u}(K^{\prime})=\widehat{u}(K)
hold for every finite field extension
K^{\prime}/K
?

In most cases where we have any knowledge on the
u
-invariant of function fields over
K
, we have in particular that
u(K(X,Y))=4u(K)
.

Corollary 5.11
.

Assume that
\mathsf{char}(K)\neq 2
and
u(K(X,Y))=4u(K)
. Then
\widehat{u}(K)=u(K)
.

Proof.

By
Proposition

5.9
, we have
u(K)\leqslant\widehat{u}(K)
.
To show the opposite inequality, consider an arbitrary function field in one variable
F/K
.
Then
F
is isomorphic to a finite field extension of
K(X)
, so that
Proposition

5.1
and the hypothesis yield that
u(F)\leqslant\frac{1}{2}u(K(X,Y))=2u(K)
.
This shows that
\widehat{u}(K)\leqslant u(K)
.
∎

The inequality

has an analogue for the strong

-invariant.

Proposition 5.12
.

We have
\widehat{u}(K(X))\geqslant 2\widehat{u}(K)
.

Proof.

Consider a function field in one variable
F/K
.
Then
F(X)/K(X)
is a function field in one variable and
u(F(X))\geqslant 2u(F)
, by
Proposition

5.1
.
Therefore we have
2\widehat{u}(K(X))\geqslant u(F(X))\geqslant 2u(F)
.
Having this for every function field in one variable

yields the claimed inequality.
∎

Question 5.13
.

Is
\widehat{u}(K(X))=2\widehat{u}(K)
?

For the

-invariant, the corresponding question would have a negative answer.

Example 5.14
.

Let

denote the quadratic closure of

.
Choose an arbitrary proper finite field extension

(e.g.

).
Then

is nonreal, and by
[

, Chap. VII, Cor. 7.11]

. In particular

.
It follows by

that

.

One may wonder whether
\widehat{u}(K)
is characterised in terms of the

-invariant by a single field extension of

. For example, is

for every function field in one variable

? In other terms:

Is

?

6.
Function fields in one variable over a henselian valued field

We will now prove our main result and subsequently present various situations where it can be applied.
We need a last step of preparation.
For a valuation
w
on a field
F
, we denote by
F^{w}
the corresponding completion.
Let
K
be a field with
\mathsf{char}(K)\neq 2
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
v
be a henselian rank-
1
valuation on
K
and
\mathsf{char}(Kv)\neq 2
.
Let
{F/K}
be a function field in one variable and
w
a rank-
1
valuation on
F
with
\mathcal{O}_{v}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{w}
.
Then

u(F^{w})=[wF:2wF]\cdot u(Fw)\leqslant[vK:2vK]\cdot 2\widehat{u}(Kv)\,.

Proof.

Since
2\in{\mathcal{O}}^{\times}_{v}\subseteq{\mathcal{O}}^{\times}_{w}
, we have that
\mathsf{char}(Fw)\neq 2
.
Note that

w
extends naturally to a complete valuation
w^{\prime}
on
F^{w}
with
w^{\prime}F^{w}=wF
and
F^{w}w^{\prime}=Fw
. Hence
w^{\prime}
is a complete rank-
1
valuation on
F
and
\mathsf{char}(F^{w}w^{\prime})\neq 2
. It follows by
[
15
, Theorem 1.3.1 (Hensel’s Lemma)]
that
1+\mathfrak{m}_{w^{\prime}}\subseteq{(F^{w})}^{\times 2}
.
The equality on the left follows now from
Proposition

4.6
applied to
(F^{w},w^{\prime})
.
It remains to show that
[wF:2wF]\cdot u(Fw)\leqslant[vK:2vK]\cdot 2\widehat{u}(Kv)
.

The situation where
w|_{K}
is trivial will be treated below as case
(4)
.
We first consider the situation where
w|_{K}
is nontrivial.
Then
\mathcal{O}_{v}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{w}\cap K\subsetneq K
.
Since
v
has rank
1
, this implies that
w|_{K}
is equivalent to
v
.
We may then assume that
v=w|_{K}
.
We follow the case distinction given in
Proposition

2.2
.

(1)
Suppose that
Fw/Kv
is a finite field extension and
wF/vK
is a finitely generated
\mathbb{Z}
-module with
\mathsf{rrk}(Fw/Kv)=1
.
It follows by
Proposition

2.1
that
[wF:2wF]=2\cdot[vK:2vK]
.
By
Proposition

5.9
we have that
u(Fw)\leqslant\widehat{u}(Kv)
.
From this the claimed inequality follows.

(2)
Suppose that
Fw/Kv
is a function field in one variable and
wF/wK
is finite.
Then by
Proposition

2.1
,
[wF:2wF]=[vK:2vK]
, and
u(Fw)\leqslant 2\widehat{u}(Kv)
, which implies the claimed inequality.

(3)
Suppose that
Fw/Kv
is an algebraic field extension and
wF/vK
is a torsion group.
Then
u(Fw)\leqslant\widehat{u}(Kv)
and
[wF:2wF]\leqslant[vK:2vK]
by
Proposition

2.1
.
In this case we obtain even that
[wF:2wF]\cdot u(Fw)\leqslant[vK:2vK]\cdot\widehat{u}(Kv)
.

(4)
Assume finally that
w|_{K}
is trivial.
In this case
w
is a discrete valuation, so we may assume that
wF=\mathbb{Z}
, and
the residue field
Fw
is a finite extension of
K
.
Hence
v
extends to a valuation
v^{\prime}
on
Fw
, and as
v
is henselian, so is
v^{\prime}
.
It follows by
Proposition

4.6
that
u(F^{w})=[wF:2wF]\cdot u(Fw)=2u(Fw)

and
u(Fw)=[v^{\prime}(Fw):2v^{\prime}(Fw)]\cdot u((Fw)v^{\prime})
.
Since the extension
(Fw)v^{\prime}/Kv
is finite, we have that
u((Fw)v^{\prime})\leqslant\widehat{u}(Kv)
, by
Proposition

5.9
.
Furthermore,
[v^{\prime}(Fw):vK]\leqslant[Fw:K]<\infty
, whence
[v^{\prime}(Fw):2v^{\prime}(Fw)]=[vK:2vK]
by
Proposition

2.1
.
Thus

u(Fw)=[vK:2vK]\cdot u((Fw)v^{\prime})\leqslant[vK:2vK]\cdot\widehat{u}(Kv)
.
∎

We come to our generalisation of
[
36
, Theorem 3]
and
[
4
, Cor. 6.4]
.
The proof crucially depends on the local-global principle for isotropy from
[
28
, Cor. 3.9]
, or more precisely, on the slightly extended version provided in
[
3
, Theorem 4.4]
, where the valuation on the base field is assumed of rank
1
and henselian, but not necessarily complete.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
v
be a henselian valuation on
K
with
\mathsf{char}(Kv)\neq 2
. Then

\widehat{u}(K)=[vK:2vK]\cdot\widehat{u}(Kv).

Furthermore, if
u(E)=2\widehat{u}(Kv)
for every function field in one variable
E/Kv
, then
u(F)=2[vK:2vK]\cdot\widehat{u}(Kv)
for every function field in one variable
F/K
.

Proof.

By
Proposition

5.7
we have that
\widehat{u}(K)\geqslant[vK:2vK]\cdot\widehat{u}(Kv)
.
We now show the converse inequality.

We first consider the case where
v
has rank
1
.
Consider an arbitrary function field in one variable
F/K
and an anisotropic torsion form
\varphi
over
F
. We claim that
\mathsf{dim}(\varphi)\leqslant[vK:2vK]\cdot 2\widehat{u}(Kv)
.
If
\mathsf{dim}(\varphi)\leqslant 2
, then this is trivial, because
\widehat{u}(Kv)\geqslant 1
.
Suppose now that
\mathsf{dim}(\varphi)\geqslant 3
.
Since
\varphi
is anisotropic over
F
and
v
is henselian of rank
1
, it follows by
[
3
, Theorem 4.4]
that there exists a rank-
1
valuation
w
on
F
with
\mathcal{O}_{v}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{w}
and such that
\varphi
remains anisotropic over
F^{w}
.
We obtain by
Lemma

6.1
that
\mathsf{dim}(\varphi)\leqslant[vK:2vK]\cdot 2\widehat{u}(Kv)
, as claimed.
Having this established for all function fields
F/K
and all anisotropic torsion forms
\varphi
over
F
, we conclude that
\widehat{u}(K)=[vK:2vK]\cdot\widehat{u}(Kv)
.
We have now established that
\widehat{u}(K)=[vK:2vK]\cdot\widehat{u}(Kv)
whenever
v
is of rank
1
.

In the next step, we assume that
v
has arbitrary finite rank
n
and show the statement in this situation.
If
n=0
, then
v
is trivial, whereby
Kv=K
,
[vK:2vK]=1
, and hence trivially
\widehat{u}(K)=\widehat{u}(Kv)=[vK:2vK]\cdot\widehat{u}(Kv)
. The case where
n=1
is already established, and we will use it in the induction step.
Assume that
n>0
.
Then the value group
vK
contains a unique maximal proper convex subgroup
\Delta
.
It follows that the quotient group
vK/\Delta
is a torsion-free group of rank
1
,
\Delta
has rank
n-1
, and the ordering on
vK
induces an ordering on
\Gamma=vK/\Delta
. Mapping
x\in K
to
v(x)+\Delta
defines a valuation
v^{\prime}
on
K
of rank
1
with
v^{\prime}K=\Gamma
.
The residue field
Kv^{\prime}
of
v^{\prime}
carries a valuation
\overline{v}
with value group
\Delta
and hence of rank
n-1
. Furthermore,
\mathcal{O}_{v}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{v^{\prime}}
, and
\mathfrak{m}_{v^{\prime}}
is an ideal of
\mathcal{O}_{v}
such that
\mathcal{O}_{v}/\mathfrak{m}_{v^{\prime}}\simeq\mathcal{O}_{\overline{v}}
.
Since
v
is henselian, the valuations
v^{\prime}
on
K
and
\overline{v}
and
Kv^{\prime}
are both henselian.
By the induction hypothesis, we obtain that
\widehat{u}(Kv^{\prime})=[\overline{v}(Kv^{\prime}):2\overline{v}K^{\prime}(Kv^{\prime})]\cdot\widehat{u}((Kv^{\prime})\overline{v})
,
and by the case where
n=1
, we have that
\widehat{u}(K)=[v^{\prime}K:2v^{\prime}K]\cdot\widehat{u}({Kv^{\prime}})
.
Furthermore,
(Kv^{\prime})\overline{v}=Kv
,
v^{\prime}K=\Gamma
and
\overline{v}(Kv^{\prime})=\Delta
.
Therefore
\widehat{u}(K)=[\Gamma:2\Gamma]\cdot\widehat{u}(Kv^{\prime})=[\Delta:2\Delta]\cdot[\Gamma:2\Gamma]\cdot\widehat{u}(Kv)
.
Finally, since
vK
and
vK/\Delta
are torsion-free, it readily follows that
[vK:2vK]=[\Gamma:2\Gamma]\cdot[\Delta:2\Delta]
.
We conclude that
\widehat{u}(K)=[vK:2vK]\cdot\widehat{u}(Kv)
, as desired.

We now consider the general case without any assumption on the value group
vK
.
Let
\mathbb{F}
denote the prime field of
K
.
Consider an arbitrary function field in one variable
F/K
and an anisotropic torsion form
\varphi
over
F
.
We fix a positive integer
m
such that
m\times\varphi
is hyperbolic.
We choose a finite subset
S
of
F
such that
\varphi
is defined over
K(S)
and is a torsion form over
K(S)
, and further such that

F=K(S)
and
\mathbb{F}(S)/\mathbb{F}(S\cap K)
is of transcendence degree
1
.
For any subfield
F_{1}
of
F
containing
S
, we have
u(F_{1})\geqslant\mathsf{dim}(\varphi)
because
\varphi
is an anisotropic torsion form over
F_{1}
.

We set
K_{0}=\mathbb{F}(S\cap K)
.
Since the absolute transcendence degree of
K_{0}
is bounded by
|S|
, it follows by
[
15
, Theorem 3.4.3]
that
\mathsf{rk}(v|_{K_{0}})\leqslant|S|+1<\infty
.
By
Lemma

2.3
, there exists a subfield
K_{1}
of
K
containing
K_{0}
such that
vK_{1}/vK_{0}
is torsion,
vK/vK_{1}
has no elements of order
2
,
Kv/K_{1}v
is algebraic and purely inseparable and
v|_{K_{1}}
is henselian.
As the extension
Kv/K_{1}v
is purely inseparable and
{\mathsf{char}(Kv)\neq 2}
, it is in particular a direct limit of normal extensions of odd degree.
We conclude by
Proposition

5.9
that
\widehat{u}(Kv)\leqslant\widehat{u}(K_{1}v)
.
As
vK/vK_{1}
has no elements of order
2
, we have
[vK_{1}:2vK_{1}]\leqslant[vK:2vK]
.
Since
vK_{1}/vK_{0}
is torsion, we conclude that
\mathsf{rk}(v|_{K_{1}})=\mathsf{rk}(v|_{K_{0}})<\infty
.
Therefore, in view of the previous part, we have
\widehat{u}(K_{1})=[vK_{1}:2vK_{1}]\cdot\widehat{u}(K_{1}v)\leqslant[vK:2vK]\cdot\widehat{u}(Kv)
.
Letting
F_{1}=K_{1}(S)
, we have that
F_{1}/K_{1}
is a function field in one variable and obtain that

\mathsf{dim}(\varphi)\leqslant u(F_{1})\leqslant 2\widehat{u}(K_{1})\leqslant[vK:2vK]\cdot 2\widehat{u}(Kv)
.
Having this for any anisotropic torsion form
\varphi
defined over any function field in one variable
F/K
, we conclude that
\widehat{u}(K)\leqslant[vK:2vK]\cdot\widehat{u}(Kv)
.
Hence, we have established the equality
\widehat{u}(K)=[vK:2vK]\cdot\widehat{u}(Kv)
in all generality.

For the last part of the statement, assume now that, for every function field in one variable
E/Kv
we have
u(E)=2\widehat{u}(Kv)
, and hence also
u(E)=u(Kv(T))
.
Consider an arbitrary function field in one variable
F/K
.
If
K
is real, then it follows by
Theorem

3.10

(a)
that
Kv
is real, whereby
Kv(T)
is real and
u(Kv(T))
is even.
Therefore,
Proposition

5.8
yields that there exists a function field in one variable
E/Kv
such that
u(F)\geqslant[vK:2vK]\cdot u(E)
.
Since
u(E)=2\widehat{u}(Kv)
and we already established that
u(F)\leqslant 2\widehat{u}(K)=2[vK:2vK]\cdot\widehat{u}(Kv)
, we conclude that
u(F)=2[vK:2vK]\cdot\widehat{u}(Kv)
.
∎

Remark 6.3
.

Assume that
v
is a complete rank-
1
valuation on
K
such that
\mathsf{char}(Kv)\neq 2
and let
r=\mathsf{rrk}(vK)
.
In this setting, in
[
26
, Theorem 4.1]
(independently from this work) the bound
\widehat{u}(K)\leqslant 2^{r}\widehat{u}(Kv)
is shown, which
strengthens
[
28
, Cor. 6.2]
,
where the same bound is given for the special case where
vK\simeq\mathbb{Z}^{r}
, while otherwise achieving the weaker bound
\widehat{u}(K)\leqslant 2^{r+1}\widehat{u}(Kv)
.

The equality
u(K)=[vK:2vK]\cdot u(Kv)
obtained through
Theorem

6.2
is an improvement to the bound
\widehat{u}(K)\leqslant 2^{r}\widehat{u}(Kv)
.
Indeed, using
Proposition

2.1
with
G=vK
and
H=0
, we obtain that
[vK:2vK]\leqslant 2^{r}
. Note also that this inequality can be strict (e.g. when
vK=\mathbb{R}
, we have
r=\infty
and
[vK:2vK]=1
).

We now recall three well-known constructions of valued fields
(K,v)
with given residue field
Kv
and a given value group
vK
, which provides us with examples where we can apply
Theorem

6.2
.

Example 6.4
.

Let
k
be a field with
\mathsf{char}(k)\neq 2
and let
K
be the field of formal Puiseux series in one variable
t
over
k
, that is,
K=\bigcup_{r\in\mathbb{N}}k(\!(t^{1/r})\!)
.
The
t
-adic valuation on
k(\!(t)\!)
extends uniquely to a valuation
v
on
K
, and this extension has residue field
Kv=k
and value group
vK=\mathbb{Q}
. In particular,
\mathsf{rrk}(vK)=1
.
Note that
v
is henselian, but not complete.
As
\mathbb{Q}
is divisible, we have that
[vK:2vK]=1
. Hence
we obtain by
Theorem

6.2
that
\widehat{u}(K)=\widehat{u}(k)
.
Denoting by
(K^{\prime},v^{\prime})
the completion of
(K,v)
, we obtain similarly that

\widehat{u}(K^{\prime})=\widehat{u}(k)
.

Example 6.5
.

Let
k
be a field with
\mathsf{char}(k)\neq 2
and
\Gamma
an ordered abelian group.
Let
K
denote the field of Hahn series with respect to
\Gamma
and with coefficients in
k
.
(The elements of

are series
\sum_{\gamma\in S}a_{\gamma}t^{\gamma}
with well-ordered support
S\subseteq\Gamma
.)
Then
K
carries a natural valuation

with value group
\Gamma
and residue field
k
, and
(K,v)
is henselian, by
[
27
, Lemma 2.40]
.
By
Theorem

6.2
, we obtain that
\widehat{u}(K)=[\Gamma:2\Gamma]\cdot\widehat{u}(k)
.

Example 6.6
.

Consider a valued field
(k,v)
with
\mathsf{char}(kv)\neq 2
and value group
vk
contained in
\mathbb{R}
as an ordered group.
In particular
\mathsf{rk}(v)\leqslant 1
.
Let
G
denote the divisible closure of
vk
, which is still a subgroup of
\mathbb{R}
, and in fact a
\mathbb{Q}
-linear subspace of
\mathbb{R}
. We consider the quotient space
\mathbb{R}/G
.
Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and choose
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\overline{\gamma}_{1},\dots,\overline{\gamma}_{n}
are
\mathbb{Q}
-linearly independent in

Let
K=k(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
.
By
[
15
, Theorem 2.2.1]
,

extends to a valuation
w
on
K
such that
w(X_{i})=\gamma_{i}
for

and with
Kw=kv
and

.
In particular, we have
[wK:2wK]=2^{n}\cdot[vk:2vk]
.

Let
(K^{\prime},w^{\prime})
be either the henselization or the completion of

.
Then in particular
(K^{\prime},w^{\prime})
is a henselian valued field with

and

By

6.2
, one obtains that
\widehat{u}(K^{\prime})=2^{n}[vk:2vk]\cdot\widehat{u}(kv)
.

We now recall how examples of valuations to which

6.2
is applicable are obtained naturally from a classical geometric setting.

Example 6.7
.

Let
(k,v)
be a discretely valued field, and
\mathcal{O}_{v}
its valuation ring.
Let us take a regular arithmetic surface
\mathcal{X}
over
\mathcal{O}_{v}
, i.e. a regular irreducible flat model of a regular irreducible curve
X
over

. Let

be an irreducible divisor in
\mathcal{X}
, and
\eta_{D}
its generic point. Then
\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{X},\eta_{D}}
is a

, hence a discrete valuation ring. It induces a discrete valuation
v_{1}
on the function field
K
of

, with residue field
\kappa(D)
-the function field of

. One can then take a closed point

, and similarly, it induces a discrete valuation
v_{2}
on
\kappa(D)
of residue field
\kappa(x)
. By composing the valuations

and

, we obtain a rank-

on

\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}

and
\kappa(x)/kv

.
This can for instance be applied to models of
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}}^{1}
over

Example 6.8
.

Let
k
be a field in which

is not a square.
Let us fix coordinate functions
{X,Y}
on
\mathbb{P}_{k}^{2}
.
Let

\mathbb{P}_{k}^{2}

We note that

and

is the residue field of

in

.
Its generic point

corresponds to the ideal
(X^{2}+1)
in

Now let us take a closed point

of

Let us choose e.g.

By composing

and

on

In the following cases,

.

.

7.
Dyadic discrete valuations with perfect residue field

In this final section, we take a look at the dyadic case and provide an alternative proof of
[
34
, Theorem 4]
which is based on the local-global principle from
[
28
]
.

Let
K
be a field.
Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\varphi
be an
n
-dimensional quadratic form over
K
.
Recall from
Section

3
that there exists a unique
n\times n
-matrix
A
over
K
such that

\mathfrak{b}_{\varphi}(x,y)=\varphi(x+y)-\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)=xAy^{\mathsf{t}}
for all
x,y\in K^{n}
, and
\varphi
is called
nonsingular
if
\mathsf{det}(A)\in{K}^{\times}
.
Consider now
m\in\mathbb{N}
with
m\leqslant n
and an
m
-dimensional quadratic form
\psi
over
K
.
We call
\psi
a
subform of
\varphi
if
\psi(X)=\varphi(X\cdot C)
for
X=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{m})
and some
m\times n
-matrix
C
of rank
m
over
K
, or equivalently, if there exist
K
-linearly independent vectors
e_{1},\dots,e_{m}\in K^{n}
such that
\psi(c_{1},\dots,c_{m})=\varphi(c_{1}e_{1}+\ldots+c_{m}e_{m})
for all
c_{1},\dots,c_{m}\in K
.
If
\psi
is nonsingular, then by
[
11
, Prop. 7.22]
,
\psi
is a subform of
\varphi
if and only if
\varphi\simeq\psi\perp\vartheta
for some quadratic form
\vartheta
.

A
2
-dimensional quadratic form will be called a
binary form
.
Given a valuation
v
on
K
, a binary form
\beta
over
K
will be called
v
-
tame
if
\beta\simeq c(X_{1}^{2}+X_{1}X_{2}+dX_{2}^{2})
for some
c\in{K}^{\times}
and
d\in\mathcal{O}_{v}
with
1-4d\in{\mathcal{O}}^{\times}_{v}
.
Note that any
v
-tame binary form is nonsingular.

Proposition 7.1
.

Let
\varphi
be a quadratic form over
K
and
n=\mathsf{dim}(\varphi)
.
Let
v
be a valuation on
K
.
The following two conditions are equivalent:

(i)

\varphi
contains a
v
-tame binary subform.

(ii)

There exist
x,y\in K^{n}
and
d\in\mathcal{O}_{v}
with
1-4d\in{\mathcal{O}}^{\times}_{v}
such that
\mathfrak{b}_{\varphi}(x,y)=1
and
\varphi(x)\varphi(y)=d
.

If
\mathsf{char}(Kv)=2
, then
(i)
and
(ii)
are further equivalent to the following:

(iii)

There exist
x,y\in K^{n}
with
v(\mathfrak{b}_{\varphi}(x,y))\leqslant\mathsf{min}\{v(\varphi(x)),v(\varphi(y))\}<\infty
.

Proof.

(i\Rightarrow ii,iii)

Assume that
\varphi
contains a
v
-tame binary subform.
This subform is isometric to
c(X_{1}^{2}+X_{1}X_{2}+dX_{2}^{2})
for some
c\in{K}^{\times}
and
d\in\mathcal{O}_{v}
with
1-4d\in{\mathcal{O}}^{\times}_{v}
.
Hence, there exist
x,y\in K^{n}
such that
\varphi(x)=c
,
\varphi(y)=cd
and
\mathfrak{b}_{\varphi}(x,y)=c
.
Then
\mathfrak{b}_{\varphi}(x,c^{-1}y)=1
and

\varphi(x)\varphi(c^{-1}y)=c(c^{-2}cd)=d
.
Furthermore,

v(\mathfrak{b}_{\varphi}(x,y))=v(c)=\mathsf{min}\{v(\varphi(x)),v(\varphi(y))\}<\infty
.

(ii\Rightarrow i)

Let
d\in\mathcal{O}_{v}
and
x,y\in K^{n}
be such that
1-4d\in{\mathcal{O}}^{\times}_{v}
,
\mathfrak{b}_{\varphi}(x,y)=1
and
d=\varphi(x)\varphi(y)
.
For
\lambda\in K
, we have

\mathfrak{b}_{\varphi}(x,\lambda y)^{2}=4\varphi(x)\varphi(\lambda y)
and hence
\lambda x\neq y
.
Thus
x
and
y
are
K
-linearly independent.
If
\varphi(x)=\varphi(y)=0
, then we replace
x
by
x+y
, and
if
\varphi(y)\neq 0=\varphi(x)
, then we switch the roles of
x
and
y
.
Hence, we may assume that
\varphi(x)\neq 0
.
We set
c=\varphi(x)
and
y^{\prime}=cy
.
Then
x
and
y^{\prime}
are
K
-linearly independent,
\varphi(y^{\prime})=cd
and
\varphi(x_{1}x+x_{2}y^{\prime})=x_{1}^{2}c+cx_{1}x_{2}+cdx_{2}^{2}
for any
x_{1},x_{2}\in K
.
Therefore
c(X_{1}^{2}+X_{1}X_{2}+dX_{2}^{2})
is a subform of
\varphi
.

(iii\Rightarrow ii)

Here we need to assume that
\mathsf{char}(Kv)=2
.
Let
x,y\in K^{n}
be such that
v(\mathfrak{b}_{\varphi}(x,y))\leqslant\mathsf{min}\{v(\varphi(x),v(\varphi(y))\}<\infty
.
Without loss of generality,
v(\varphi(x))\leqslant v(\varphi(y))
and
\varphi(x)\neq 0
.
Set
y^{\prime}=\mathfrak{b}_{\varphi}(x,y)^{-1}y
and
d=\varphi(x)\varphi(y^{\prime})
. Then
\mathfrak{b}_{\varphi}(x,y^{\prime})=1
and
v(d)=v(\varphi(x))+v(\varphi(y))-2v(\mathfrak{b}_{\varphi}(x,y))\geqslant 0
, whereby
d\in\mathcal{O}_{v}
.
Since
\mathsf{char}(Kv)=2
, it follows that
1-4d\in{\mathcal{O}}^{\times}_{v}
.
∎

Example 7.2
.

In the setting of
Proposition

7.1
, assume that
\varphi
is nonsingular and isotropic. Hence there exist
x,y\in K^{n}
such that
\varphi(x)=0
and
\mathfrak{b}_{\varphi}(x,y)=1
. Then
\varphi(x)\varphi(y)=0\in\mathcal{O}_{v}
, so
Proposition

7.1
yields that
\varphi
contains a
v
-tame binary form.
More precisely, in this case
\varphi
contains the hyperbolic form
\mathbb{H}=X_{1}X_{2}
as a subform, which is isometric to
X_{1}(X_{1}+X_{2})
and hence
v
-tame.

A valuation
v
on
K
is called
2
-henselian
if every quadratic polynomial in
\mathcal{O}_{v}[X]
having a simple root in
Kv
also has a root in
K
.

Remark 7.3
.

If
\mathsf{char}(Kv)\neq 2
, then

is
2
-henselian if and only if
1+\mathfrak{m}_{v}\subseteq{\mathcal{O}}^{\times 2}_{v}
, by
[

, Cor. 4.2.4]
.

The following statement clarifies the role of tame binary subforms in determining isotropy over a
2
-henselian valued field.

Lemma 7.4
.

Let
v
be a
2
-henselian valuation on
K
.
Let
c,d\in\mathcal{O}_{v}
be such that
1-4d\in{\mathcal{O}}^{\times}_{v}
.
If
\overline{c}
is nontrivially represented by
X_{1}^{2}+X_{1}X_{2}+\overline{d}X_{2}^{2}
over
Kv
, then
c
is represented nontrivially by
X_{1}^{2}+X_{1}X_{2}+dX_{2}^{2}
over
K
.
In particular, if
X_{1}^{2}+X_{1}X_{2}+\overline{d}X_{2}^{2}
is isotropic over
Kv
, then
X_{1}^{2}+X_{1}X_{2}+dX_{2}^{2}
is isotropic over
K
.

Proof.

Assume that
\overline{c}
is nontrivially represented by
X_{1}^{2}+X_{1}X_{2}+\overline{d}X_{2}^{2}
over
Kv
.
Hence there exist elements
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathcal{O}_{v}
which are not both contained in
\mathfrak{m}_{v}
such that
x_{1}^{2}+x_{1}x_{2}+dx_{2}^{2}-c\in\mathfrak{m}_{v}
.
If
x_{2}+2x_{1}\in{\mathcal{O}}^{\times}_{v}
, then
\overline{x}_{1}\in Kv
is a simple root of the polynomial
T^{2}+Tx_{2}+dx_{2}^{2}-c\in\mathcal{O}_{v}[T]
, and since
v
is
2
-henselian, there exists
x_{1}^{\prime}\in K
with
x_{1}^{\prime 2}+x_{1}^{\prime}x_{2}+dx_{2}^{2}-c=0
.
If
x_{2}+2x_{1}\in\mathfrak{m}_{v}
, then
x_{1}\in{\mathcal{O}}^{\times}_{v}
, and as
1-4d\in{\mathcal{O}}^{\times}_{v}
, it follows that
x_{1}+2dx_{2}\equiv(1-4d)x_{1}\not\equiv 0\,\,\mathsf{mod}\,\,\mathfrak{m}_{v}
, whereby the residue
\overline{x}_{2}\in Kv
is a simple root of the polynomial
x_{1}^{2}+x_{1}T+dT^{2}-c\in\mathcal{O}_{v}[T]
. As
v
is
2
-henselian, we conclude that there exists
x_{2}^{\prime}\in K
with
{x_{1}^{2}+x_{1}x_{2}^{\prime}+d{x_{2}^{\prime}}^{2}-c=0}
.
∎

Whether a given quadratic form possesses a tame binary subform is invariant under certain extensions of valued fields. In this context, we state two crucial reductions by certain extensions of the base field.

Following
[
14
]
, given a valuation
v
on
K
, a field extension
K^{\prime}/K
will be called
v
-inertial
if it is algebraic and
v
extends to a valuation
v^{\prime}
on
K^{\prime}
in such way that

is separable with

for every finite subextension

of

.

Theorem 7.5

(Elomary-Tignol)
.

Let
v
be a henselian valuation on
K
with
\mathsf{char}(Kv)=2
and
\varphi
a nonsingular quadratic form over

.
Then
\varphi
contains a
v
-tame binary subform if and only if there exists a
v
-inertial extension
L/K
such that
\varphi_{L}
is isotropic.

Proof.

If
\varphi
is isotropic, then the equivalence holds trivially, in view of
Example

7.2
.
Assume that
\varphi
is anisotropic.
Let
n=\mathsf{dim}(\varphi)
.
It follows by
Proposition

7.1
that

\varphi
possesses no
v
-tame binary subform if and only if we have
v(\mathfrak{b}_{\varphi}(x,y))>\mathsf{min}\{v(\varphi(x)),v(\varphi(y))\}
for all nonzero vectors
x,y\in K^{n}
, which is condition
(S^{\prime})
in

.
Therefore the statement follows from

∎

Lemma 7.6
.

Let
(K,v)
be a valued field.
Let
k
be a subfield of
K
which is dense in
K
with respect to the
v
-adic topology.
Let
\varphi
be a quadratic form over
k
.
Then
\varphi
contains a
v
-tame binary subform over
K
if and only if
\varphi
contains a
v
-tame binary subform over
k
.

Proof.

Note that

\mathcal{O}_{v}^{\#}=\{d\in\mathcal{O}_{v}\mid 1-4d\in{\mathcal{O}}^{\times}_{v}\}
is open in
K
.
We endow
K^{n}\times K^{n}
with the
v
-adic topology.
Then

U=\{(x,y)\in K^{n}\times K^{n}\mid\mathfrak{b}_{\varphi}(x,y)\neq 0\}
is open in
K^{n}\times K^{n}
.
The map

\Phi:U\to K,(x,y)\mapsto\varphi(x)\varphi(y)\mathfrak{b}_{\varphi}(x,y)^{-2}

is continuous.
We set
S=\{\varphi(x)\varphi(y)\mid x,y\in K^{n},\mathfrak{b}_{\varphi}(x,y)=1\}
.
For
(x,y)\in U
and
\lambda=\mathfrak{b}_{\varphi}(x,y)^{-1}
, we have
\mathfrak{b}_{\varphi}(\lambda x,y)=1
, whereby
\Phi(x,y)=\Phi(\lambda x,y)\in S
.
Therefore
S=\Phi(U)
.
The hypothesis that
k
is dense in
K
implies that
k^{n}\times k^{n}
is dense in
K^{n}\times K^{n}
. It follows that
(k^{n}\times k^{n})\cap\Phi^{-1}(\mathcal{O}_{v}^{\#})
is dense in
\Phi^{-1}(\mathcal{O}_{v}^{\#})
.
By
Proposition

7.1
, this yields the claimed statement.
∎

Lemma

7.6
can typically be applied for the completion of a valued field.
Recall that we denote the completion of a field
F
with respect to a valuation
w
by
F^{w}
.

Theorem 7.7
.

Let
(K,v)
be a discretely valued field such that
\mathsf{char}(K)=0
,
\mathsf{char}(Kv)=2
and
Kv
is perfect.
Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and let
F/K
be a field extension of transcendence degree
n
and
\varphi
a quadratic form over
F
with
\mathsf{dim}(\varphi)>2^{n+1}
.
Let
w
be a valuation on
F
extending
v
and assume that
w
is henselian or of rank
1
.
Then
\varphi
contains a
w
-tame binary subform over
F
.

Proof.

Assume first that
w
is henselian.
Let
(F^{\prime},w^{\prime})
be a maximal inertial algebraic extension of
(F,w)
. (It is unique up to isomorphism of valued fields, and called
the
inertia field of
(F,w)
; see e.g.
[
15
, Section 5.2]
.)
Since
w
is henselian, so is
w^{\prime}
, and it follows that
F^{\prime}w^{\prime}
is separably closed.
We choose a maximal algebraic extension
K^{\prime}/K
inside
F^{\prime}/K
with
w^{\prime}K^{\prime}=vK=\mathbb{Z}
.
Set
v^{\prime}=w^{\prime}|_{K^{\prime}}
.
Since
(F^{\prime},w^{\prime})
is henselian and
F^{\prime}w^{\prime}
is separably closed, it follows that
K^{\prime}v^{\prime}
is separably closed.
Since
Kv
is perfect, we obtain that
K^{\prime}v^{\prime}
is algebraically closed.
Hence
(K^{\prime},v^{\prime})
is a henselian discretely valued field with algebraically closed residue field.
Since
F^{\prime}/K^{\prime}
is a field extension of transcendence degree
n
, it follows by
Theorem

5.6
that
u(F^{\prime})\leqslant 2^{n+1}
.
Hence
\varphi
is isotropic over
F^{\prime}
.
Since
F^{\prime}/F
is a
w
-inertial extension, we conclude by
Theorem

7.5
that
\varphi
contains a
w
-tame binary form.
This proves the statement under the assumption that
w
is henselian.

Let us now consider the case where
\mathsf{rk}(w)=1
while
w
is not assumed to be henselian.
Let
\hat{w}
denote the natural valuation extension of
w
to
F^{w}
.
Since
\mathsf{rk}(w)=1
, the valuation
\hat{w}
is henselian.
Let
F^{\prime}
denote the relative algebraic closure of
F
inside
F^{w}
and
w^{\prime}=\hat{w}|_{F^{\prime}}
.
Then
(F^{\prime},w^{\prime})
is henselian, too, and
F^{\prime}/K
is of transcendence degree
n
over
K
.
Hence it follows from the case which is already established that
\varphi
contains a
w^{\prime}
-tame binary form over
F^{\prime}
.
Since
F
is dense in
F^{w}
with respect to the
\hat{w}
-adic topology, and hence as well in
F^{\prime}
, we conclude by

Lemma

7.6
that
\varphi
contains a
w
-tame binary form over
F
.
∎

to be henselian or of rank

.

Proposition 7.9
.

Let
v
be a
2
-henselian valuation on
K
with
\mathsf{char}(Kv)=2
and such that
Kv
is perfect.
Let
\beta
be an anisotropic
v
-tame binary form over
F
.
Then the nonzero elements represented by
\beta
over
K
form a coset of
{K}^{\times}/{K}^{\times 2}{\mathcal{O}}^{\times}_{v}
.

Proof.

By the hypothesis,
\beta\simeq c(X_{1}^{2}+X_{1}X_{2}+aX_{2}^{2})
for some
a\in\mathcal{O}_{v}
and
{c\in{K}^{\times}}
.
Since
c
is represented by
\beta
, we need to show that the nonzero elements represented by
c(X_{1}^{2}+X_{1}X_{2}+aX_{2}^{2})
over
K
are precisely the elements of
c{K}^{\times 2}{\mathcal{O}}^{\times}_{v}
.
For this, we may assume without loss of generality that
c=1
.
Since
v
is
2
-henselian and
\beta
is anisotropic,
by
Lemma

7.4
, the quadratic form
X_{1}^{2}+X_{1}X_{2}+\overline{a}X_{2}^{2}
over
Kv
is anisotropic, too.
This implies that, for any
x_{1},x_{2}\in K
, we have

v(x_{1}^{2}+x_{1}x_{2}+ax_{2}^{2})=2\mathsf{min}\{v(x_{1}),v(x_{2})\}
and hence
x_{1}^{2}+x_{1}x_{2}+ax_{2}^{2}\in\{0\}\cup{K}^{\times 2}\cdot{\mathcal{O}}^{\times}_{v}
.
On the other hand, since
Kv
is perfect and
\mathsf{char}(Kv)=2
, the quadratic form
X_{1}^{2}+X_{1}X_{2}+\overline{a}X_{2}^{2}
over
Kv
represents all elements of
Kv
.
Since
v
is
2
-henselian, we conclude by
Lemma

7.4
that

X_{1}^{2}+X_{1}X_{1}+aX_{2}^{2}
over
K
represents all elements of
{\mathcal{O}}^{\times}_{v}
, hence also all elements of
{K}^{\times 2}{\mathcal{O}}^{\times}_{v}
.
∎

Lemma 7.10
.

Let
v
be a
2
-henselian valuation on
K
such that
Kv
is perfect,
\mathsf{char}(Kv)=2
and
[vK:2vK]<\infty
.
Let
\varphi
be an anisotropic quadratic form over
K
.
Let
r\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{r}
be
v
-tame binary forms over
K
and
\psi
a quadratic form over
K
containing no
v
-tame binary subform and such that

(
\ast
)

\varphi\simeq\beta_{1}\perp\ldots\perp\beta_{r}\perp\psi.

Set
m=[vK:2vK]
.
Then
r\leqslant m
and the nonzero elements represented by
\psi
over
K

are contained in a union of
m-r
cosets of
{K}^{\times}/{\mathcal{O}}^{\times}_{v}{K}^{\times 2}
.

Proof.

We denote by
B_{i}
be the set of elements of
{K}^{\times}
represented by
\beta_{i}
, for
1\leqslant i\leqslant r
.
It follows by

7.9
that

are cosets of
{K}^{\times}/{{\mathcal{O}}}^{\times}_{v}{K}^{\times 2}
.
Note that
{K}^{\times}/{\mathcal{O}}^{\times}_{v}{K}^{\times 2}\simeq vK/2vK
, whereby
m=|{K}^{\times}/{\mathcal{O}}^{\times}_{v}{K}^{\times 2}|
.
If
B_{i}=B_{j}
for some distinct
i,j\in\{1,\dots,r\}
,
then
\beta_{i}\perp\beta_{j}
is isotropic.
If
\psi
represents an element of

for some

, then

Hence in view of
(\ast)
, in either of these two situations,

is isotropic.
In the remaining case, the cosets

are distinct, and
the elements of
{K}^{\times}
represented by

are contained in

, which is a union of

cosets of
{K}^{\times}/{\mathcal{O}}^{\times}_{v}{K}^{\times 2}
.
∎

Recall that, by

11
, Prop. 7.22]
, given a nonsingular subform

for another quadratic form

.
Therefore, given a valuation

on

and a quadratic form

over

, a decomposition of

-tame binary forms are nonsingular.

Theorem 7.11

(Parimala-Suresh)
.

Let
(K,v)
be a henselian discretely valued field with
\mathsf{char}(K)=0
,
\mathsf{char}(Kv)=2
and
Kv
perfect.
Then
\widehat{u}(K)=2\widehat{u}(Kv)
with

\widehat{u}(Kv)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }Kv\text{ has no finite extension of even degree, }\\
2&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Furthermore,
u(F)=4\widehat{u}(Kv)
for every function field in one variable
F/K
.

Proof.

Consider first the case where
Kv
has no finite extension of even degree. Then
\widehat{u}(Kv)=1
, by
Proposition

5.4
, using that
-1=1\in{(Kv)}^{\times 2}
.
It further follows that there exists an inertial extension of valued fields
(K^{\prime},v^{\prime})/(K,v)
which is a direct limit of odd degree normal finite extensions and such that
K^{\prime}v^{\prime}
is algebraically closed.
Since
(K^{\prime},v^{\prime})
is henselian as well, it follows by
Theorem

5.6
that
\widehat{u}(K^{\prime})=2
. We conclude by
Proposition

5.9
that
\widehat{u}(K)=2
.
By
Proposition

5.4
, this implies that
u(F)=4
for every function field in one variable
F/K
.

We now assume that there exists a finite field extension of even degree
\ell/Kv
.
By
Proposition

5.4
we have that
u(E)\geqslant 4
for every function field in one variable
E/Kv
, and by
Proposition

5.8
this yields that
u(F)\geqslant 8
for every function field in one variable
F/K
.
It therefore remains only to show that
\widehat{u}(K)\leqslant 4
.

Consider a function field in one variable
F/K
and an anisotropic quadratic form
\varphi
over
F
.
We need to show that
\mathsf{dim}(\varphi)\leqslant 8
.

By
[
3
, Theorem 4.4]
, there exists a rank-
1
valuation
w
on
F
with
\mathcal{O}_{v}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{w}
and such that
\varphi
remains anisotropic over
F^{w}
.
In particular
\mathsf{dim}(\varphi)\leqslant u(F^{w})
.

Consider first the case where
K\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{w}
.
Then
\mathsf{char}(Fw)=\mathsf{char}(K)=0
, and since
F/K
is a function field in one variable,
w
is discrete.
It follows that
w
extends naturally to a discrete valuation on
F^{w}
with residue field
Fw
.
Therefore
u(F^{w})=2u(Fw)
, in view of
Proposition

4.6
.
Since
Fw/K
is a finite field extension,

v
extends to a discrete valuation
v^{\prime}
on
Fw
. Since
v
is henselian and
Kv
is perfect, we obtain that
v^{\prime}
is henselian and
(Fw)v^{\prime}
is perfect.
Since
\mathsf{char}((Fw)v^{\prime})=\mathsf{char}(Kv)=2
, we obtain by

[
25
, Cor. 1]
that
u((Fw)v^{\prime})\leqslant 2
.
By
Theorem

4.7
it follows that
u(Fw)=2u((Fw)v^{\prime})\leqslant 4
.
Therefore,
\mathsf{dim}(\varphi)\leqslant u(F^{w})\leqslant 8
.

Hence we are left with the case where
K
is not contained in
\mathcal{O}_{w}
. Since the valuation
v
on
K
is discrete,
\mathcal{O}_{v}
is a maximal proper subring of
K
, so we obtain that

K\cap\mathcal{O}_{w}=\mathcal{O}_{v}
.
Hence, after rescaling values,
w
is an extension of
v
from
K
to
F
.
Then
Fw/Kv
is either an algebraic extension or a function field in one variable.
Since
Kv
is perfect and
Fw/Kv
has transcendence degree at most
1
, we have
[Fw:(Fw)^{(2)}]\leqslant 2
, by
[
38
, Satz 15]
, where
(Fw)^{(2)}
is the subfield of
Fw
of squares.
It follows by
[
25
, Cor. 1]
that
u(Fw)\leqslant 2[Fw:(Fw)^{(2)}]\leqslant 4
.
If
w
is discrete,
then we obtain using
Theorem

4.7
that
u(F^{w})=2u(Fw)\leqslant 8
, whereby

\mathsf{dim}(\varphi)\leqslant u(F^{w})\leqslant 8
.

It remains to consider the case where
w
is not discrete.
In this case, it follows by

Proposition

2.2
that
Fw/Kv
is an algebraic extension and the group
wF/vK
is either torsion or finitely generated with
\mathsf{rrk}(wF/vK)=1
.
In particular,
Fw
is perfect and, since
vK\simeq\mathbb{Z}
, we obtain that
[wF:2wF]\leqslant 4
.
We write

\varphi\simeq\beta_{1}\perp\ldots\perp\beta_{r}\perp\psi

for some
r\in\mathbb{N}
,
w
-tame binary forms
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{r}
over

and a quadratic form
\psi
over
F
that contains no
w
-tame binary subform.
Since

has rank
1
,

7.7
yields that
\mathsf{dim}(\psi)\leqslant 4
.
Therefore

\mathsf{dim}(\varphi)=2r+\mathsf{dim}(\psi)\leqslant 2r+4\,.

We set
m=[wF:2wF]
and obtain from
Proposition

2.1
that
m\leqslant 4
.
Since
\varphi
is anisotropic over
F^{w}
, we obtain from

7.10
that
r\leqslant m\leqslant 4
and the nonzero elements represented by

over

m-r

{F^{w}}^{\times}/{\mathcal{O}}^{\times}_{w}{F^{w}}^{\times 2}
.
In particular, if
r=4
, then
\psi
is trivial.

Hence
\mathsf{dim}(\varphi)\leqslant 10
. Moreover, if
r\leqslant 2
or
r=4
, then we get that
\mathsf{dim}(\varphi)\leqslant 8
, as desired.
Consider now the case where
r=3
and
\psi
is nontrivial.
Then
m=4
,

\mathsf{dim}(\varphi)=6+\mathsf{dim}(\psi)\leqslant 10
and

\psi
over

are contained in a single coset of

.

.
Hence

∎
