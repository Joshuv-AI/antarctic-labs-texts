Development and External Validation of an Explainable Machine Learning Model to Identify Positive Dysphagia Screening Results in People with Dementia: A Multicenter Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Jie Du, Huipin Zhang, Xiaomeng Wen, Xianwei Guo, Juanjuan Hu, Zhunzhun Liu, Xiaobao Li, Claire Shuiqing Zhang, Yun Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo develop and externally validate an interpretable machine learning model for identifying the likelihood of a positive Standardized Swallowing Assessment (SSA) result in people with dementia, particularly in settings where instrumental swallowing assessments are not readily available.MethodsCandidate variables included demographic, physical, oral-health, and mental health variables routinely available in clinical practice. Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was used for variable selection. Four machine learning models were developed and compared: logistic regression, support vector machines, random forest, and AdaBoost. Model performance was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), sensitivity, calibration, and decision curve analysis. SHAP analysis was applied to enhance model interpretability. A web-based likelihood assessment tool was developed to estimate the individual probability of a concurrent positive SSA result.ResultsAll four models showed good discrimination. AdaBoost achieved an AUC of 0.918 in internal cross-validation and AUCs of 0.900 and 0.889 in the domain and temporal external validation cohorts, respectively, with sensitivities ranging from 0.879 to 0.901. Since sensitivity was prioritized for case finding, AdaBoost was selected for model interpretation and online-tool development. SHAP analysis ranked body mass index as the leading contributor to the AdaBoost output, followed by number of teeth, eating ability, dietary type, and Clinical Dementia Rating score.ConclusionThe model showed good performance for identifying people likely to have a concurrent positive SSA screening result. The web-based tool may support case finding and referral for further swallowing assessment, but it should not be used as a standalone diagnostic or prognostic instrument. Prospective and geographically diverse validation is required before routine clinical implementation.

Introduction

Along with the rapid aging of the global population, dementia has become a major public health challenge. According to the World Health Organization,1 approximately 57 million people currently live with dementia, with nearly 10 million new cases diagnosed annually. Dementia ranks as the seventh leading cause of death worldwide, primarily affecting older adults. In China alone, more than 330,000 dementia-related deaths occur each year.2 Importantly, mortality in this population is often driven by severe complications rather than dementia itself, with pneumonia representing the leading direct cause of death.3 Dysphagia is a key contributor to pneumonia and is highly prevalent among individuals with dementia,4 with an estimated average prevalence of 58%.5 Impairment of pharyngeal and laryngeal function often results in food entering the airway instead of the stomach,6 thereby increasing the likelihood of pneumonia.7 Beyond pneumonia, dysphagia is associated with malnutrition, dehydration, asphyxia,8 and a substantially increased likelihood of mortality. Timely recognition and management of co-existing dysphagia have been shown to mitigate these adverse outcomes.9

Despite its clinical importance, diagnosing dysphagia in people with dementia remains challenging. Cognitive impairment renders self-reporting unreliable, and swallowing impairment may not be recognized until complications occur. Common bedside screening tools, such as the Water Swallow Test developed by Toshio Okada,10 have imperfect sensitivity,11 which may lead to an underestimation of the prevalence of dysphagia. The Standardized Swallowing Assessment (SSA) used in the present study is also a screening instrument and cannot characterize swallowing physiology or reliably exclude silent aspiration. Instrumental assessments, including videofluoroscopic swallowing study and fiberoptic endoscopic evaluation of swallowing, may be feasible for some people with dementia but can be challenging or unavailable in routine practice because of limited access, cognitive impairment, or difficulty sustaining cooperation.12 An accessible case-finding model may therefore help identify people who warrant further swallowing assessment, particularly where instrumental assessment is not readily available. This objective differs from prognostic prediction: prognostic models estimate future risk, whereas the present cross-sectional model estimates the likelihood of a concurrent positive screening result. Although dysphagia models have been developed in stroke and cancer populations,13,14 few studies have focused on screening support in dementia. Dysphagia in dementia is commonly progressive in the context of neurodegeneration,15,16 although its clinical mechanisms vary across subtypes. In Alzheimer disease, cortical degeneration may contribute to eating apraxia and sensory-perceptual impairment; vascular dementia may involve pseudobulbar dysfunction and impaired pharyngeal reflexes; and dementia with Lewy bodies may involve parkinsonian bradykinesia and rigidity affecting swallowing efficiency.17 These differences may influence clinical presentation and model transportability across dementia subtypes.

This study aimed to address this knowledge gap by developing and externally validating an explainable machine learning model for identifying the likelihood of a concurrent positive SSA screening result in people with dementia. SHAP analysis was used to provide individualized estimates and transparent descriptions of variable contributions. The model was additionally evaluated in a nursing-home cohort because many people with advanced dementia reside in long-term care settings where access to instrumental swallowing assessment is limited.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis Or Diagnosis reporting guidelines, extended for artificial intelligence (TRIPOD+AI).18

Materials and Methods

This multicenter cross-sectional study used a development cohort and two independent validation cohorts to develop and evaluate machine learning models for identifying the likelihood of a concurrent positive SSA screening result in people with dementia. Supplementary Figure 1 summarizes model development, validation, evaluation, and interpretation.

Study Population

We included participants aged 60 years or older who met the diagnostic criteria for dementia defined by the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) (F00–F03).19 Participants were enrolled from a tertiary hospital and a nursing home in China. The development cohort and temporal external validation cohort were collected from the tertiary hospital, whereas the domain external validation cohort was obtained from the nursing home. The nursing home represents a long-term care environment where screening for concurrent dysphagia is particularly important. This design allowed evaluation of model performance across both acute care and long-term care environments. Exclusion criteria were: (1) presence of other mental disorders aside from dementia; (2) other medical conditions directly causing dysphagia (eg, head and neck cancer, trauma, or acute stroke); and (3) medical conditions requiring fasting or fluid restriction. The flowchart for participant recruitment is provided in Supplementary Figure 2. The development cohort was recruited from March 1 to September 20, 2024, in the tertiary hospital. The domain external validation cohort was recruited from the nursing home between October 1 and 17, 2024, and the temporal external validation cohort was recruited from the same hospital from November 1, 2024, to January 27, 2025. The three datasets contained completely non‑overlapping participants and followed identical data collection procedures.

Sample size was calculated according to Riley.20 Assuming a dysphagia prevalence of 58% among individuals with dementia,5 an anticipated C-index of 0.95, and 23 candidate variables, a minimum sample size of 411 participants, including at least 239 with dysphagia, was required. The required minimum sample size was exceeded. Calculations were performed using R software (version 4.4.3).

Data Collection and Variables

Data were extracted from electronic medical records and supplemented with structured bedside assessments. Candidate variables were identified through literature review and expert consultation, spanning four domains: Demographics: age, education, marital status, living arrangement, family functioning, and caregiver information;Oral health: number of teeth, oral chronic disorders, and Kayser-Jones Brief Oral Health Status Examination (BOHSE) score;21Physical health: comorbidities, polypharmacy, dietary type, eating ability, appetite, prealbumin levels, white cell count, body mass index (BMI), Mini Nutritional Assessment-Short Form (MNA-SF) score; and Barthel Index;Mental health: dementia subtype, disease duration, psychiatric medication, Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) score.22

Dysphagia screening status was assessed using the Standardized Swallowing Assessment (SSA), a tool with established sensitivity and specificity.23,24 A total score greater than 18 was classified as a positive screening result. It is important to note that the SSA is a bedside screening instrument rather than an instrumental diagnostic reference standard; it does not characterize swallowing physiology and may not detect silent aspiration, although previous studies have reported that the SSA can achieve an accuracy of 97% and a specificity of 90%.24 Assessments were performed by trained assessors who were blinded to candidate-variable data. Candidate variables and SSA assessments were collected within one week to minimize temporal separation. A full inventory of assessed likelihood factors is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Statistical Analysis

Data Preprocessing

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.3). Outliers were detected using the interquartile range (IQR) method, erroneous entries were corrected after source verification, and extreme values were transformed when clinically and statistically appropriate. DataWig (v0.3.1, MXNet) was applied separately to the development, domain validation, and temporal validation cohorts to impute missing values (all <30%) without data leakage. Each variable was predicted from all others using a two‑layer MLP (64, 32 units, ReLU; Adam, lr=0.001, batch size=64, early stopping). Continuous variables were assessed for normality. Those approximately normally distributed are summarized as mean ± standard deviation; non‑normally distributed variables are reported as median (interquartile range). Between‑group comparisons were performed using Student’s t‑test or Mann–Whitney U-test for continuous variables, and the chi‑square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. The standardized mean difference (SMD) was reported to quantify the magnitude of the imbalance. Of note, as the proportion of positive SSA screening results was essentially balanced (56.9%), no class imbalance handling techniques were applied. Prior to model fitting, all variables were preprocessed to ensure comparability across datasets. Ordinal categorical variables, including Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) score, eating ability, and dietary type, were numerically encoded based on the degree of deviation from normal aging, with higher values indicating greater impairment. Nominal categorical variables were converted into dummy variables, while continuous variables such as BMI and number of teeth were standardized to Z-scores. Factor levels were harmonized across all datasets to ensure consistency in model training and validation. Detailed preprocessing procedures for each variable are summarized in Supplementary Table 2.

Model Development

Variable selection was performed using LASSO regression with ten-fold cross-validation. To ensure parsimony and generalizability, the lambda.1se criterion was applied.25 Selected variables were then entered into each model without additional transformation. Four machine learning algorithms were evaluated: logistic regression (LR), support vector machine (SVM), random forest (RF), and AdaBoost. Hyperparameters of machine learning models were optimized using grid search within cross-validation. Hyperparameters for all models were optimized via grid search with 5‑fold cross‑validation on the development cohort, and the combination that maximized the Youden index was retained for each final model. For the SVM, a radial basis function kernel was employed, with the regularization parameter (C) searched over (0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, 100) and the kernel width (gamma) uniformly sampled from a reasonable interval estimated by the sigest function. The RF was tuned by evaluating the number of variables considered at each split (mtry) over (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6); after the optimal (mtry) was identified, the number of trees (ntree) was determined by monitoring the out‑of‑bag (OOB) error curve and stopping when the error stabilized. For AdaBoost, the number of iterations (50, 100, 150, 200, 250, 300, 350), the maximum depth of the base decision trees (1, 2, 3, 4), and the weight updating strategy (Freund, Zhu, Breiman) were jointly tuned. In all cases, the hyperparameter set yielding the highest Youden index during the cross‑validation procedure was selected. The final hyperparameters selected via grid search were SVM: C = 1, γ = 0.046; random forest: mtry = 3, ntree = 56; and AdaBoost: iterations = 250, maximum tree depth = 3, weight updating strategy = Freund.

Model Evaluation

Model performance was evaluated in terms of discrimination (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve [AUC], sensitivity, specificity, precision, F1 score), calibration (calibration plots, Brier score, calibration intercept, calibration slope), and clinical utility (decision curve analysis [DCA]). The 95% confidence intervals for AUC were estimated using the DeLong method. AUC and sensitivity were prespecified as the primary performance metrics for model comparison because missed positive screens were considered clinically important. Calibration was assessed using calibration plots and Hosmer-Lemeshow test, and the aforementioned calibration metrics. Internal validation was performed using five-fold cross-validation in the development cohort; external validation was performed in both the temporal and domain validation cohorts. External validation was performed using both temporal and independent cohorts to assess model robustness and generalizability.25 The optimal cutoff value was established at the maximum Youden index in the development cohort and applied directly to the validation cohorts. To assess the potential influence of dietary type and eating ability on model performance, a sensitivity analysis excluded these two variables and re-evaluated the AdaBoost model using BMI, number of teeth, and CDR score.

Model Interpretation

SHAP is a model-agnostic framework for interpreting machine learning predictions.26 It can provide local explanations for individual estimates and global summaries of variable contributions. In this study, SHAP was used to quantify how each variable contributed to the AdaBoost model output. SHAP values were interpreted as explanations of model behavior, not as evidence of biological causation.

Two independent validation cohorts were established: a temporal validation cohort from the same tertiary hospital (November 2024–January 2025) and a domain validation cohort recruited from a nursing home in October 2024.

Variable Selection

Ordinal categorical variables, including Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) score, eating ability, and dietary type, were assigned numerical values according to the degree of deviation from normal aging, with higher scores indicating greater impairment. Continuous variables such as BMI and number of teeth were standardized using Z-score normalization.

Ten-fold cross-validation was applied to determine two optimal lambda values: lambda.min (0.024), corresponding to the minimum prediction error, and lambda.1se (0.118) (Supplementary Figure 3). To achieve a balance between accuracy and simplicity, the lambda.1se model was selected. This model retained five variables with non-zero coefficients. The relative importance of these variables was evaluated based on their behavior in the LASSO solution path and the absolute values of their standardized coefficients. According to the absolute values of the regression coefficients, the variables were ranked in the following order: BMI, CDR score, number of teeth, eating ability, and dietary type.

Model Performance

All four models demonstrated good discrimination performance and maintained relatively consistent results across validation cohorts. AdaBoost achieved the highest cross‑validation AUC (0.918) and consistently high sensitivities (0.879–0.901) across validation cohorts; because sensitivity was prioritized for case finding, it was selected as the final model, even though random forest had slightly higher external AUCs. Calibration was assessed using Brier score, intercept, and slope. Brier scores were low across cohorts (0.120–0.291), indicating small prediction errors. Intercepts were near zero in cross‑validation but deviated in external cohorts, suggesting slight risk overestimation. Slopes for LR, RF, and AdaBoost were close to 1, while SVM showed markedly elevated slopes (2.916–4.864), reflecting overconfident probability estimates (Table 2). The Hosmer–Lemeshow test indicated acceptable calibration (p > 0.05) (Supplementary Figure 4). Slightly lower discrimination and sensitivity were observed in the temporal validation cohort. The performance fluctuation observed in the temporal validation cohort may be related to the counterintuitive data distribution within this cohort. The optimal cutoff values were derived from the development cohort and applied unchanged to the external validation cohorts (LR is 0.670, SVM is 0.722, RF is 0.705, AdaBoost is 0.904). On decision curve analysis, the net benefit was similar among the four models across most clinically relevant threshold probabilities. When the threshold probability was high, the net benefit decreased for all models, implying that additional resource allocation may not substantially improve outcomes in patients classified as high risk. Overall, the models offer comparable clinical utility for dysphagia screening. Sensitivity Analysis

Because dietary type and eating ability may reflect clinical adaptations to existing swallowing impairment and thus introduce circularity, a sensitivity analysis was performed by excluding these two variables and re‑evaluating the AdaBoost model with the remaining three variables (BMI, number of teeth, and CDR score). The simplified model retained good discrimination and overall accuracy. However, the elevated calibration slope and reduced sensitivity observed in the temporal validation cohort indicate a tendency toward extreme probability estimates and highlight the need for attention to cross‑scenario generalizability (Supplementary Table 5). In cross‑validation, the reduced model achieved an AUC of 0.921 (95% CI 0.884–0.942) with a sensitivity of 0.858; for the domain external validation, the AUC was 0.930 (95% CI 0.878–0.982) and sensitivity increased slightly to 0.894; in the temporal external validation, the AUC was 0.877 (95% CI 0.809–0.944) with a sensitivity of 0.800. The consistently high performance indicates that the core discriminative power is driven by nutritional status, oral health, and dementia severity, meaning the model’s discriminatory ability does not entirely rely on the excluded variables; however, residual information leakage or circularity cannot be ruled out. These findings suggest that discrimination was not solely dependent on dietary type and eating ability, although sensitivity was lower in cross‑validation and temporal validation, and potential circularity cannot be completely excluded.

Variable Importance and Interpretation

SHAP analysis ranked variables according to their contributions to the AdaBoost model output: (1) BMI, (2) number of teeth, (3) eating ability, (4) dietary type, and (5) CDR score (Figure 2A). This ranking differed from the LASSO coefficient ranking because the two methods describe different models and different aspects of variable contribution. SHAP plots further revealed complex, non-linear relationships between these variables and SSA-defined dysphagia likelihood, indicating that their influence is not straightforward (Figure 2B). Detailed associations for each variable are shown in Supplementary Figure 5, BMI showed the steepest SHAP response pattern, suggesting that it contributed substantially to the model’s likelihood estimation. We then investigated how the influence of the top four variables changed as dementia advanced (Supplementary Figure 6). For participants with mild or moderate dementia, these factors remained significant. However, their impact diminished substantially in participants with severe dementia. Importantly, given the cross-sectional nature of the data, these variables’ importance rankings should not be interpreted as evidence that these factors causally contribute to dysphagia development; rather, they should be viewed as markers associated with concurrent dysphagia status. Development of an Online Likelihood Assessment Tool

To facilitate both clinical and home-based application, we further developed a publicly accessible web-based likelihood assessment tool based on the AdaBoost model (dusonlinecomputer.shinyapps.io/Positive_SSA_Screening_Result_Likelihood_Tool/). This tool provides an individualized estimate and displays variable contributions. Detailed instructions are presented in Supplementary Figure 7. The interface should be interpreted as a decision-support aid: it does not establish a diagnosis, cannot exclude silent aspiration, and should not replace professional assessment.

Discussion

In this multicenter cross-sectional study involving 620 people with dementia, we developed and externally validated an explainable machine learning model for identifying individuals who were likely to have a concurrent positive SSA screening result. Five routinely available variables were selected using LASSO regression and evaluated using four modeling approaches. All four models showed good discrimination. AdaBoost had the highest cross-validated AUC and maintained high sensitivity in both external cohorts, although random forest produced slightly higher AUCs in the external cohorts. Because missed positive screens were considered clinically important, sensitivity was prioritized and AdaBoost was selected for subsequent SHAP interpretation and online-tool development. The model was evaluated in both hospital and nursing-home populations, which is clinically relevant because long-term care facilities often rely on bedside assessment and may have limited access to instrumental swallowing evaluation. Importantly, the model estimates the likelihood of a concurrent positive SSA result; it does not diagnose dysphagia or predict its future onset.

The final model retained BMI, number of teeth, eating ability, dietary type, and CDR score. These variables represent complementary aspects of nutritional and muscular status, oral function, functional dependence, dietary adaptation, and dementia severity. BMI contributed most strongly to the AdaBoost output. Low BMI may reflect poor nutritional status and sarcopenia, which have been associated with impaired swallowing-related muscle function.27 Dysphagia may also reduce food intake and worsen malnutrition and muscle loss, creating a bidirectional relationship. Thus, the observed association should not be interpreted as evidence that low BMI independently causes swallowing impairment. The nonlinear SHAP pattern for BMI further suggests that model contribution did not change in a simple linear manner across the observed range. This finding supports careful assessment of nutritional and body-composition status rather than assuming that indiscriminate nutritional supplementation will continuously reduce the probability of a positive screen.

Number of teeth was the second most influential variable in the final model. Tooth loss can reduce masticatory efficiency, impair bolus formation, and increase the demands placed on oral-phase control.28 Poor chewing may produce a less cohesive bolus and make swallowing more difficult, particularly in people with impaired cognition or motor planning. Previous studies have also linked masticatory function with cerebral perfusion and cognitive health, although these pathways were not measured in our study.29,30 Dentures may partially restore chewing function, but their benefit depends on fit, tolerance, oral care, and the person’s ability to use them safely.31 Cognitive impairment may therefore limit the effectiveness of prosthetic rehabilitation and increase the need for supervision. These observations support incorporating oral-health assessment into swallowing case finding, while recognizing that tooth count is a marker of oral function and accumulated health status rather than a direct measure of swallowing physiology.

Higher CDR scores were associated with a greater model-estimated probability of a positive SSA result. This association is clinically plausible because cognition and swallowing depend on overlapping distributed neural networks, and progressive neurodegeneration may impair motor planning, sensorimotor integration, attention, and protective airway responses.32 Eating ability and dietary type also contributed to the model, but their interpretation requires particular caution. Dependence during feeding may reflect broader functional decline, frailty, reduced motor control, or the consequences of existing swallowing difficulty. Likewise, semi-liquid and liquid diets are frequently introduced in response to suspected dysphagia rather than preceding it. Prolonged texture modification may also reduce dietary variety, energy density, and oropharyngeal stimulation.33 Swallowing-muscle loss may be both a consequence of inadequate intake and a contributor to further impairment.34,35 Dietary type and eating ability can therefore function simultaneously as correlates, consequences, and indirect contributors. This bidirectionality explains the potential for circularity and precludes causal inference from the present cross-sectional data.

The sensitivity analysis excluding dietary type and eating ability directly addressed this concern. The reduced three-variable model retained good discrimination in internal and external validation, indicating that model discrimination was not solely driven by variables that may represent clinical adaptations to existing swallowing impairment. However, sensitivity declined in internal and temporal validation, and the analysis cannot fully eliminate information leakage or reverse causation. The five-variable model may be more useful for pragmatic case finding because it incorporates information already available during routine care, whereas the reduced model may better isolate less downstream markers. These two perspectives are complementary: the full model prioritizes bedside utility, while the reduced model provides a more conservative assessment of robustness.

SHAP analysis improved transparency by showing how individual variables contributed to the AdaBoost output. Higher BMI and greater tooth count were generally associated with lower model-estimated likelihood, whereas lower BMI and fewer teeth were associated with higher likelihood. Severe dementia, complete feeding dependence, and liquid diets tended to contribute toward a positive screen, whereas mild dementia, independent feeding, and a regular diet tended to contribute in the opposite direction. Overlapping color distributions and the dependence plots indicated that these associations were not strictly linear. BMI and tooth count showed S-shaped patterns, and the intervals between categories of dietary type, eating ability, and CDR score were unequal. These findings illustrate complex model behavior rather than biological dose-response relationships. SHAP values quantify contributions to the prediction function and must not be interpreted as causal effects on dysphagia development.

Stratified SHAP analyses suggested that relative variable contributions differed by dementia severity. BMI contributed most strongly among participants with mild dementia, whereas tooth count was more prominent in moderate dementia. In severe dementia, the contributions of the other variables became less distinct. One possible interpretation is that advanced neurodegeneration and generalized functional deterioration increasingly dominate the clinical presentation, reducing the relative contribution of nutritional or oral-health variables. However, subgroup sample size and the high prevalence of positive screens in severe dementia may also reduce the stability of these estimates. The stage-specific patterns should therefore be viewed as hypothesis-generating. Prospective studies are needed to determine whether nutritional, dental, or functional interventions modify swallowing outcomes at different stages of dementia.

The variables identified by the model are consistent with recognized mechanisms of dementia-related dysphagia, although the model does not directly measure those mechanisms. Progressive neurodegeneration can affect bilateral cortical and subcortical networks involved in swallowing initiation, sensorimotor integration, and airway protection.36 Reduced oropharyngeal sensation may delay swallow initiation, while impaired laryngeal elevation and incomplete airway closure can increase aspiration risk. Silent aspiration is particularly important because impaired sensation and cough response may allow aspiration without overt clinical signs, and bedside screens such as the SSA may fail to detect it.37 Oral-phase impairment, including reduced mastication and poor bolus control, is also common in dementia and is reflected indirectly by tooth loss and feeding dependence.38 Imaging and physiological studies have described tongue atrophy, reduced tongue-palate contact, altered tongue-base position, and reduced laryngeal elevation in older adults with cognitive impairment.39 Low BMI and sarcopenia may represent downstream consequences of these deficits and may further weaken swallowing-related muscles.40 These mechanisms provide clinical context for the selected variables but should not be interpreted as mechanisms proven by the present analysis.

The nursing-home validation cohort is a notable strength because it tested model performance in a long-term care environment that differs from the hospital development setting. In many nursing homes, routine dysphagia case finding is performed by nursing staff, and access to VFSS or FEES may be limited. The required variables can be obtained without specialized equipment, which may make the model suitable for supporting decisions about who should undergo more detailed swallowing evaluation. Nevertheless, the tool should not provide false reassurance. A low estimated likelihood does not exclude swallowing impairment or silent aspiration, and clinical signs such as recurrent coughing, unexplained respiratory infection, weight loss, wet voice, or prolonged mealtimes should prompt assessment regardless of the model output. Similarly, a high estimate should support referral and multidisciplinary evaluation rather than automatically trigger dietary or therapeutic changes.

The slightly lower AdaBoost performance in the temporal validation cohort may reflect differences in case mix, sample size, seasonal recruitment, or unmeasured changes in clinical practice. The development cohort was recruited mainly between spring and autumn, whereas the temporal cohort included winter recruitment. Seasonal infection, nutritional status, and functional decline could have influenced participant characteristics and model performance. This interpretation remains speculative because these factors were not directly evaluated. Broader prospective validation across seasons, institutions, healthcare systems, and dementia subtypes is required to establish transportability and determine whether recalibration is needed in new settings.

The publicly accessible online tool translates the model into a practical format and displays individualized estimates together with variable contributions. Its principal role should be case finding and support for referral, not diagnosis, prognosis, or unsupervised treatment selection. Users should be clearly informed that the outcome is an SSA-based screening result, that a negative estimate cannot rule out silent aspiration, and that clinical decisions must incorporate history, examination, caregiver observations, and instrumental testing when indicated. Prospective implementation studies are needed to determine whether the tool improves referral pathways, timeliness of swallowing assessment, interprofessional communication, or patient outcomes. Claims that it reduces pneumonia, hospitalization, caregiver burden, or resource use would require direct evaluation and cannot be inferred from the present study.

This study has several strengths. It used a multicenter design with internal, temporal, and domain external validation; included a clinically relevant nursing-home cohort; used routinely available variables; and applied SHAP to improve model transparency. The sensitivity analysis also examined the influence of two variables that could introduce circularity. These features strengthen confidence that the model captures reproducible patterns associated with SSA screening status, although they do not overcome the limitations of the outcome definition or cross-sectional design.

Several limitations should be considered. First, the outcome was defined by the SSA rather than VFSS or FEES. The SSA is a validated bedside screening tool but cannot characterize swallowing physiology or reliably identify silent aspiration. Some participants with negative SSA results may therefore have had clinically important impairment, introducing outcome misclassification. Future studies should include instrumental assessment in all participants or in a representative subsample to quantify misclassification and evaluate calibration against confirmed dysphagia. Second, the cross-sectional design means that the model estimates concurrent screening status and cannot predict incident dysphagia, progression, or response to intervention. The selected variables should be interpreted as associated markers, not causal risk factors. Third, dietary type and eating ability may partly reflect adaptations to pre-existing swallowing difficulty. Although the reduced-model analysis supported robustness of discrimination, residual circularity cannot be excluded. Fourth, the sample size met the prespecified calculation, but overfitting remains possible, particularly for flexible machine learning algorithms. Only two external cohorts were included. Fifth, all participants were recruited from one region of China, which may limit generalizability across ethnic, cultural, and healthcare contexts. Finally, the online tool has not undergone prospective clinical-impact evaluation and should not replace professional judgment.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we developed and externally validated an explainable machine learning model for identifying people with dementia who were likely to have a concurrent positive SSA screening result. The model used five routinely obtainable variables and demonstrated good discrimination and high sensitivity across hospital and nursing-home cohorts. The web-based tool may support case finding and referral for further swallowing evaluation, but it is not a standalone diagnostic or prognostic instrument and cannot exclude silent aspiration. Prospective, geographically diverse, and instrumentally validated studies are needed before routine clinical implementation.
