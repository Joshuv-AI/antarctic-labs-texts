Peer review of the pesticide risk assessment of the active substance Beauveria bassiana strain R444
Authors: European Food Safety Authority (EFSA), Fernando Álvarez, Maria Arena, Domenica Auteri, Sofia Batista Leite, Marco Binaglia, Anna Federica Castoldi, Arianna Chiusolo, Angelo Colagiorgi, Mathilde Colas, Federica Crivellente, Chloe De Lentdecker, Isabella De Magistris, Mark Egsmose, Gabriella Fait, Franco Ferilli, German Giner Santonja, Varvara Gouliarmou, Alessio Ippolito, Frederique Istace, Dimitra Kardassi, Aude Kienzler, Anna Lanzoni, Roberto Lava, Renata Leuschner, Alberto Linguadoca, Mariano Lopez Romano, Jochem Louisse, Christopher Lythgo, Oriol Magrans, Iris Mangas, Silvia Mazzega, Andrea Mioč, Ileana Miron, Tunde Molnar, Laura Padovani, Vincenzo Padricello, Martina Panzarea, Juan Manuel Parra Morte, Simone Rizzuto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe conclusions of the EFSA following the peer review of the initial risk assessments carried out by the competent authority of the rapporteur Member State, Denmark, for the pesticide active substance Beauveria bassiana strain R444 and the considerations as regards the inclusion of the substance in Annex IV of Regulation (EC) No 396/2005 are reported. The context of the peer review was that required by Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009 of the European Parliament and of the Council. The conclusions were reached on the basis of the evaluation of the representative uses of Beauveria bassiana strain R444 as an insecticide on tomatoes and as an acaricide on stone fruits. The reliable end points, appropriate for use in regulatory risk assessment, are presented. Missing information identified as being required by the regulatory framework is listed. Issues not finalised are identified.

SUMMARY

Beauveria bassiana strain R444 is a new active substance for which, in accordance with Article 7 of Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009 of the European Parliament and of the Council, the rapporteur Member State (RMS), Denmark, received an application from Andermatt PHP (Pty) Ltd. on 22 October 2021 for approval. In addition, the applicant submitted a proposal for inclusion of the substance in Annex IV of Regulation (EC) No 396/2005 within the approval dossier. Complying with Article 9 of the Regulation, the completeness of the dossier was checked by the RMS and the date of admissibility of the application was recognised as being 1 March 2023.

An initial evaluation of the dossier on Beauveria bassiana strain R444 was provided by the RMS in the draft assessment report (DAR), and subsequently, a peer review of the pesticide risk assessment on the RMS evaluation was conducted by EFSA in accordance with Article 12 of Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009. The following conclusions are derived.

The uses of Beauveria bassiana strain R444 according to the proposed representative uses as an insecticide on tomatoes in field and in greenhouse (permanent) and non‐permanent protected structures via spray and as an acaricide on stone fruits in field via spray result in sufficient insecticidal and acaricidal efficacy against the target organisms.

The assessment of the data package revealed no issues that could not be finalised or that need to be included as critical areas of concern with respect to the identity of the microbial active substance and the physical, chemical and technical properties of the representative formulation and analytical methods.

In the area of mammalian toxicology, beauvericin was identified as a secondary metabolite of potential concern. A data gap was identified as regards the general toxicity profile of beauvericin. Risk from non‐dietary exposure to the metabolites is considered unlikely.

In the area of residues, without data addressing the occurrence of beauvericin (non‐viable residues) including its potential in situ formation by Beauveria bassiana strain R444 (viable residues) following the representative use conditions, the consumer risk assessment cannot be finalised.

None of the five criteria outlined in the guidance (SANCO/11188/2013 Rev. 2) were considered met for Beauveria bassiana strain R444.

In the environmental fate and behaviour section, information was not provided on the exposure levels of the secondary metabolite of potential concern, beauvericin, though its level in the formulation for representative uses is low and in situ production other than in infected arthropods, the rhizosphere or in plant tissues was considered as being excluded.

In the area of ecotoxicology, low risk was concluded for mammals, aquatic organisms, bees and soil organisms. However, the risk assessment could not be finalised for birds regarding oral exposure to secondary metabolites from infected insects, for non‐target arthropods other than bees concerning infectivity, pathogenicity and toxicity from secondary metabolites.

BACKGROUND

Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009 of the European Parliament and of the Council 1 (hereinafter referred to as ‘the Regulation’) lays down, inter alia, the detailed rules as regards the procedure and conditions for approval of active substances. This regulates for the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) the procedure for organising the consultation of Member States and the applicant(s) for comments on the initial evaluation in the draft assessment report (DAR), provided by the rapporteur Member State (RMS), and the organisation of an expert consultation, where appropriate.

In accordance with Article 12 of the Regulation, EFSA is required to adopt a conclusion on whether an active substance can be expected to meet the approval criteria provided for in Article 4 of the Regulation (also taking into consideration recital (10) of the Regulation) within 120 days from the end of the period provided for the submission of written comments, subject to an extension of 30 days where an expert consultation is necessary, and a further extension of up to 150 days where additional information is required to be submitted by the applicant(s) in accordance with Article 12(3).

Beauveria bassiana strain R444 is a new active substance for which, in accordance with Article 7 of the Regulation, the RMS, Denmark (hereinafter referred to as the ‘RMS’), received an application from Andermatt PHP (Pty) Ltd. on 22 October 2021 for approval of the active substance Beauveria bassiana strain R444. In addition, the applicant submitted a proposal for inclusion of the substance in Annex IV of Regulation (EC) No 396/2005 within the approval dossier. Complying with Article 9 of the Regulation, the completeness of the dossier was checked by the RMS and the date of admissibility of the application was recognised as being 1 March 2023.

The RMS provided its initial evaluation of the dossier on Beauveria bassiana strain R444 in the DAR, which was received by EFSA on 9 February 2024 and updated on March 2024 (Denmark, 2024). The peer review was initiated on 20 June 2024 by dispatching the DAR to the Member States and the applicant, Andermatt PHP (Pty) Ltd., for consultation and comments. EFSA also provided comments. In addition, EFSA conducted a public consultation on the DAR. The comments received were collated by EFSA and forwarded to the RMS for compilation and evaluation in the format of a reporting table. The applicant was invited to respond to the comments in column 3 of the reporting table. The comments and the applicant's response were evaluated by the RMS in column 3.

The need for expert consultation and the necessity for additional information to be submitted by the applicant in accordance with Article 12(3) of the Regulation were considered in a teleconference between EFSA and the RMS on 14 November 2024. On the basis of the comments received, the applicant's response to the comments and the RMS's evaluation thereof, it was concluded that additional information should be requested from the applicant, and that EFSA should conduct an expert consultation in the areas of potential effects on human/mammalian health and potential effects on non‐target organisms.

The outcome of the teleconference, together with EFSA's further consideration of the comments, is reflected in the conclusions set out in column 4 of the reporting table. All points that were identified as unresolved at the end of the comment evaluation phase and which required further consideration, including those issues to be considered in an expert consultation, were compiled by EFSA in the format of an evaluation table.

The conclusions arising from the consideration by EFSA, and as appropriate by the RMS, of the points identified in the evaluation table, together with the outcome of the expert consultation and the written consultation on the assessment of additional information, where these took place, were reported in the final column of the evaluation table.

In accordance with Article 12 of the Regulation, EFSA should adopt a conclusion on whether Beauveria bassiana strain R444 can be expected to meet the approval criteria provided for in Article 4 of the Regulation, taking into consideration recital (10) of the Regulation.

A final consultation on the conclusions arising from the peer review of the risk assessment took place with Member States via a written procedure in June 2026.

This conclusion report summarises the outcome of the peer review of the risk assessment on the active substance and the formulation for representative uses evaluated on the basis of the representative uses of Beauveria bassiana strain R444 as an insecticide on tomatoes and acaricide on stone fruits as proposed by the applicant. In accordance with Article 12(2) of Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009, risk mitigation options identified in the DAR and considered during the peer review, if any, are presented in the conclusion.

THE MICROBIAL ACTIVE SUBSTANCE AND THE FORMULATION FOR REPRESENTATIVE USES

Beauveria bassiana strain R444 is a wild‐type, indigenous fungus deposited at South African National Fungal Culture Collection under accession number 8132/7861 and in Leibniz Institute DSMZ – German Collection of Microorganisms and Cell Cultures GmbH’ (DSMZ) under accession number DSM 32777.

There is not any area of concern with respect to the identity of the microorganism.

The formulated product for the representative uses for the evaluation was ‘Bb‐Protec’, a wettable powder (WP) containing 3.0 × 107 CFU/g – 3.0 × 108 CFU/g (nominal 1 × 108 CFU/g) of Beauveria bassiana strain R444.

The information on the microbial active substance and the formulation for representative uses, including the co‐formulants in this formulation, was considered in the overall assessment during the peer review. None of the co‐formulants is an unacceptable co‐formulant listed in Annex III of Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009; 2 however, a co‐formulant is a non approved active substance under Regulation (EC) 1107/2009. 3 , 4 Details on the composition of the formulations cannot be reported in conclusions because of the provisions in Article 63(2)(d) of Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009; however, this information was fully available and evaluated during the peer review. A proposal for classification of the formulation(s) according to Regulation (EC) 1272/2008 was provided by the applicant and assessed by the RMS (see Volumes 3 MP of the DAR).

The representative EU uses evaluated were professional and non‐professional foliar spray application for the control of white fly on tomato in field, greenhouse (permanent) and non‐permanent protected structures and for the control of spider mites on stone fruits in the field. Full details of the GAP can be found in the list of end points in Appendix A.

Data were submitted to conclude that the use of Beauveria bassiana strain R444 according to the representative uses proposed at EU level results in sufficient insecticidal and acaricidal efficacy against targeted pests, following the guidance document SANCO/10054/2013‐rev. 3 (European Commission, 2013).

CONCLUSIONS OF THE EVALUATION

General aspects

With regard to the mammalian toxicity, the toxicological profile of the formulation for representative uses was discussed in an experts' meeting. 5 Acute toxicity studies were available with the formulation for representative uses ‘Bb‐Protec’. With regard to the co‐formulants contained in ‘Bb‐Protec’, sufficient toxicological data were available for all components, and it is possible to conclude that they do not impact on the toxicity/classification and safety of the proposed formulation. The collected information, including information from the existing uses other than plant protection products under regulated EU frameworks, did not highlight any concern.

The availability of ecotoxicity data with the formulation for representative uses and the available data for the individual co‐formulants were discussed in an experts' meeting. 6 A potential concern for one individual co‐formulant was identified for non‐target arthropods other than bees, and a data gap was set (see Sections 6.4 and 10).

SPECIFICATION OF THE MICROORGANISM, PHYSICAL AND TECHNICAL PROPERTIES AND METHODS OF ANALYSIS

The following guidance documents were followed in the production of the conclusion of this section: EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2018.

The microbial pest control agent as manufactured (MPCA‐AM) is not isolated and purified but directly formulated to microbial pest control product (MPCP) in a continuous process; however, it was proposed the dry spore powder intermediate to be considered as MPCA‐AM. The minimum and maximum content of Beauveria bassiana strain R444 in MPCA‐AM was 5.0 × 1010–5.0 × 1011 spores/g (minimum viability 75%). The specification was based on batch data from industrial scale plant production. Available data demonstrated that the microorganism could produce secondary metabolites (see Section 2). It was demonstrated that the metabolite of potential concern beauvericin remained below the LOQ in the MPCP. The content of microbial contaminants in MPCA‐AM and MPCP were below the limits defined in the Working Document on Microbial Contaminant Limits (OECD, 2011).

Multilocus phylogenetic analysis based on internal transcriber spacer ITS1, partial sequences of the elongation factor tef, of the RNA polymerases rpb1 and rpb2 and of the intergenic region bloc was used to identify the microorganism as belonging to the Beauveria bassiana species. A method based on PCR amplification and restriction enzyme analysis can be used for unequivocal identification of Beauveria bassiana strain R444 at strain level.

For the formulation of the representative uses, a storage for 24 months at temperature between 4 and 10°C was proposed. However, the provided shelf‐life study had some deficiencies; therefore, a data gap for a new shelf‐life study or proposal for shorter storage at this temperature range was identified (see Section 10).

Acceptable methods were available for the determination of the microorganism in the MPCA‐AM and MPCP and for the determination of the content of contaminating microorganisms in MPCA‐AM and MPCP. Residue definitions were not set for Beauveria bassiana strain R444 (see Sections 4 and 6); therefore, monitoring methods are not required.

BIOLOGICAL PROPERTIES OF THE MICRO‐ORGANISM

The following guidance document was followed in the production of the conclusion of this section: European Commission 2020b.

Beauveria bassiana strain R444 is a naturally occurring, non‐modified fungus originally isolated from soil in the Clanwilliam region of the Western Cape of South Africa in October 2003.

Historical background Beauveria species are well‐studied microorganisms with a long history of use as biological control agents against pest insects such as the European corn borer (Ostrinia nubilalis), the codling moth (Carpocaspa pomonella) and the chinch bug (Blissus leucopterus). Beauveria bassiana is an entomopathogenic fungus (parasitic to insects) which grows naturally in soils throughout the world.

The target organisms for the representative uses are white flies (Bemisia tabaci, Trialeurodes vaporariorum) and spider mites (Tetranychus spp.).

Beauveria bassiana strain R444 is not considered host specific.

The main mode of action of Beauveria bassiana strains is percutaneous infection, which leads to fungus outgrowths and subsequent insect death. The putative mode of action includes synthesis of fungal‐produced microbial metabolites and immunosuppression of the insect host as well as induction of plant defence response.

Beauveria bassiana has two phases in its life cycle, the parasitic and the saprophytic phase. The parasitic phase consists of the adhesion and germination of the spores on the cuticle of arthropods for the later penetration in the tegument. Once the tegument has been penetrated the conidium attaches to the host cuticle, each spore germinates, and a germ tube is produced starting the colonisation with the formation of the blastospores. After total colonisation, the hyphae exit the tegument under favourable environmental conditions, starting the saprophytic phase with the release of the spores that are able to survive in soil and be disseminated. B. bassiana is well adapted for soil survival, either as conidia or as saprophytic mycelia which can grow when nutrients are available in the rhizosphere. The species has been reported to be epiphytic and or endophytic, having been recovered from plant tissues.

Genetic material transfer: Being a mitotic asexual fungus (no sexual recombination or meiosis having been observed in its life cycle), Beauveria bassiana is not expected to have the potential for transfer of genetic material to other organisms.

Infectiveness, dispersal and colonisation ability: Non‐GLP study showed that Beauveria bassiana strain R444 displayed mycelial growth at temperature between 10°C and 33°C with an optimal growth temperature of 30°C and did not grow at temperatures below 8°C and above 36°C. The optimal spore viability is achieved at temperatures from 16 to 30°C where germination is above 95%. At temperatures of 10°C and lower, spore viability is limited and germination is slower; at temperatures higher than 33°C, spore viability is reduced, and at 38°C, no germination was recorded.

Antifungal susceptibility: Testing of Beauveria bassiana strain R444 with several antimycotic agents following EUCAST reference method E. DEF.9.3 7 allowed the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) for amphotericin B, anidulafungin, caspofungin, isavuconazole, itraconazole, micafungin, posaconazole and voriconazole to be determined. These antimycotic agents correspond to three different modes of action. Based on these data, it was suggested that, in case of need, azoles and polyenes are probable antimycotic agents for treatment (with the lowest MIC being for posaconazole). It is noted that EUCAST breakpoints for Beauveria species together with the tested antifungal agents were not available, to allow the strain to be clearly categorised as resistant or susceptible to these substances.

Secondary metabolites of (potential) concern: Based on a literature search, the species B. bassiana can produce bassiacridin, bassianin, bassianolide, beauvericin, beauveriolide, beauvetetraon, pyridovericin, pyridomacrolidin, oosporein and tenellin. Whole genome sequencing (WGS) analysis on B. bassiana strain R444 excluded the potential production of bassianin, confirmed the potential production of bassianolide, beauvericin, beauveriolide, oosporein and tenellin while did not explore the genetic capacity to produce bassiacridin, beauvetetraon, pyridovericin and pyridomacrolidin. The (eco)toxicological properties of the above metabolites were investigated (see Sections 3.2 and 6). Significant in situ formation of beauvericin (and other secondary metabolites) is only considered to occur in infected arthropods and possibly in the rhizosphere and within plants (see Sections 4 and 5.2). Data gaps related to exposure levels have been identified in Sections 4 and 6.

POTENTIAL EFFECTS ON HUMAN/MAMMALIAN HEALTH

The toxicological profile of Beauveria bassiana strain R444 and its metabolites was discussed at the Pesticides Peer Review Teleconference 177 in June 2025.

Mammalian infectivity and pathogenicity of the microorganism and sensitisation effects

As regards medical information, there is no evidence that Beauveria bassiana species cause infection in immuno‐competent healthy humans. No adverse health effects have been reported in workers exposed to Beauveria bassiana strain R444 during production and handling.

Beauveria bassiana species are not included in the EFSA qualified presumption of safety (QPS) list, as filamentous fungi are currently excluded from QPS evaluation due to safety and complexity considerations (EFSA BIOHAZ Panel, 2025).

Current methods for testing dermal sensitisation are not suitable for testing microorganisms and no validated test methods exist for assessing sensitisation via inhalation. Based on their characteristics, microorganisms such as Beauveria bassiana strain R444 shall be regarded as potential sensitisers.

While there are no known specific human or animal pathogens within the genus Beauveria, Beauveria bassiana can act as an opportunistic human pathogen.

No signs of toxicity or pathogenicity have been detected in rats after single exposure to the technical product by oral and intratracheal routes. Microbial analysis of faeces, fluids and tissues/organs was investigated and demonstrated full clearance (no infectivity) of the active substance after 21 days.

In vivo studies showed that the technical product is not a skin irritant, and minimally irritant to the eye.

The use of personal protective equipment (PPE) and respiratory protective equipment (RPE) for the operators and workers is recommended as non‐specific risk mitigation measure.

It is concluded that Beauveria bassiana strain R444 has no potential for infectivity/pathogenicity in humans based on the available data.

Mammalian toxicity of the metabolites of (potential) concern

Based on the available peer reviewed scientific literature, only beauvericin was identified as a metabolite of potential toxicological concern for human health among the metabolites potentially produced by this strain (see Section 2). Recently, it was concluded that beauvericin is not genotoxic and is of low toxicity after acute and repeated administration in rodents; however, no firm conclusion could be drawn with respect to chronic exposure (EFSA CONTAM Panel, 2024). Therefore, the general toxicity profile of beauvericin cannot be concluded on the basis of the available information to allow for a quantitative risk assessment (data gap). Beauvericin has been measured in the analysis of batches of ‘Bb‐Protec’ and found to be present below the LOQ (see Section 1).

The RMS and Member States (MSs) supported the view that no metabolites of concern for human health associated with Beauveria bassiana strain R444 under the proposed conditions of use of the formulation for representative uses ‘Bb‐Protec’ are identified.

EFSA is of the view that no concerns are identified for the non‐dietary exposure to ‘Bb‐Protec’, based on the available toxicological information and the outcome of the analytical determination (see Sections 1 and 2). In situ production of secondary metabolites of potential toxicological concern (such as beauvericin) cannot be excluded based on the available data and, if demonstrated, further toxicological investigations might be needed (see above and Section 4).

RESIDUES

The assessment in the residue section is based on the following guidance documents: Commission guidance SANCO/11188/2013 Rev. 2 (European Commission, 2015). Beauveria bassiana strain R444 is not approved as a basic substance under Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009 (Criterion I). It is also not listed in Annex I of Regulation (EC) No 396/2005 (Criterion II).

Notably, Beauveria bassiana strain R444 harbours the genes encoding for beauvericin production. For this metabolite, adverse health effects cannot be excluded, and it is identified as a metabolite of potential toxicological concern (see Section 3.2). Therefore, criterion III of the guidance cannot be considered as met. Substantial information was provided on the potential of the species Beauveria bassiana to grow endophytically after inoculation of plant surfaces, which could lead to the accumulation of secondary metabolites in plant tissues.

The RMS expressed the view in the updated DAR that there are no data gaps linked to beauvericin relevant for the residue section because the estimated concentrations of beauvericin on edible commodities linked to the representative uses under assessment are expected to be very low in comparison with measured naturally highly contaminated food commodities, as reported in the literature. This view was supported by one MS.

However, EFSA notes that beauvericin is an emerging mycotoxin and that, given the current uncertainties regarding its occurrence and toxicological relevance, comparison with naturally occurring levels cannot, on its own, support a conclusion on consumer safety for the additional exposure potentially resulting from the representative uses.

Notably, an occurrence and potential in situ formation of beauvericin by Beauveria bassiana strain R444 in tomatoes and stone fruits intended for consumption in line with the representative uses was not investigated. This information is required and assumptions of low anticipated beauvericin levels are considered insufficient.

A data gap was set for qualitative and quantitative information on the residue behaviour of identified toxicologically relevant metabolite (beauvericin) under conditions of the representative uses on edible commodities at BBCH 89, for tomatoes (indoor and outdoor uses) and stone fruits (see Section 9.1).

The representative uses are foliar treatments of tomatoes (in and outdoor) and of stone fruits up to a BBCH of 89 representing the growth stage of ripe fruits. Some information on viable residues of Beauveria bassiana strain R444 and/or of the residue behaviour following treatment is available; however, uncertainties remain in particular to its endophytic growth potential which may lead to in situ production of beauvericin.

There is insufficient information to conclude whether exposure to viable and/or non‐viable residues linked to the representative uses of Beauveria bassiana strain R444 is lower, equal to, or higher than concentrations naturally occurring, and criterion IV of the guidance cannot be considered to be met. In addition, consumer exposure cannot be excluded, and criterion V of the guidance cannot be considered to be met.

A data gap is set for adequate experimental evidence on the behaviour of viable residues, including potential endophytic growth of Beauveria bassiana strain R444 under conditions of the representative uses on tomatoes (indoor), which is considered as most critical, however, also on tomatoes and stone fruits outdoor (see Section 9.1).

Based on the available data and information, it cannot be concluded that beauvericin residues in treated produce would only occur at acceptable levels for the consumer. In addition, none of the five assessment criteria according to the Commission guidance SANCO/11188/2013 Rev. 2 (European Commission, 2015) for potential inclusion in Annex IV of Regulation (EC) No 396/2005 are met.

Consequently, the consumer risk assessment cannot be finalised (see Section 9.1).

ENVIRONMENTAL FATE AND BEHAVIOUR

Information has been provided in relation to the potential interference of Beauveria bassiana strain R444 with the analytical systems for the control of the quality of drinking water provided for in Directive 98/83/EC11 (see specific Annex VI decision‐making criteria in Directive 2005/25/EC12). As these methods require pathogenic bacteria to be identified and confirmed as absent, it is concluded unlikely that filamentous fungi or their conidia would interfere with methodologies used for such determinations.

Fate and behaviour in the environment of the microorganism and any other forms

A non‐GLP laboratory study investigating the persistence and multiplication in soil of Beauveria bassiana strain R444 was available in the dossier. The Bb‐Protec product applied contained B. bassiana strain R444 in the experiment and colony counts showed that the strain can persist in soil for the duration of the study, which was 35 days after the last application. However, the use of sterilised soil and the limited study duration do not allow for a comprehensive characterisation of the environmental behaviour and ecological dynamics of the strain for the representative uses. A weight of evidence approach based on the open literature on Beauveria bassiana species was presented in the dossier. A range of laboratory and field investigations examining the soil persistence and multiplication of different B. bassiana strains revealed substantial variability among studies. Despite this variation, the overall pattern indicated a gradual reduction in B. bassiana populations following soil inoculation, with concentrations returning to natural background levels (830 CFU per gram of soil) within approximately 0.5–1.5 years. This suggests that Beauveria Bassiana strain R444 spores under representative uses of open field, and greenhouses (permanent and not permanent protected structures) are expected to persist in soil in concentrations considerably higher than the natural background levels, considering repeated applications over the years, as spores might only be expected to germinate when infecting arthropods or in the rhizosphere where nutrient levels are higher and epiphytic growth might occur (see Sections 2 and 4). For infectivity and pathogenicity to non‐target organisms, see Section 6. Predicted environmental densities (PED) in soil have been calculated (see Appendix A).

A GLP laboratory experiment was submitted to investigate the persistence and multiplication in water of Beauveria bassiana strain R444. The applied product Bb‐Protec containing B. bassiana strain R444 was used to sterile deionised water and incubated (T = 10–14°C) for 10 days in the dark. In the absence of competing microorganisms, Beauveria bassiana strain R444 decreased to 60% of the initially applied amount by the end of the study. The dossier did not contain any literature data on the persistence or multiplication of Beauveria bassiana spp. in surface water. However, Beauveria bassiana is a soil‐adapted fungus whose natural habitat is the soil, and its germination is triggered by the presence of arthropods (including insects and arachnids), on which its life cycle depends. Maximum initial PED surface water values are presented in Appendix A.

With respect to the microorganism mobility, a non‐GLP study was presented in the dossier demonstrating that the vertical mobility of conidia is limited to 5 cm of soil depth. Information retrieved from literature search showed that the species B. bassiana is moved horizontally via insects and heavy rainfall, or as a result of the colonisation of plants' root systems.

Specific studies on the Beauveria bassiana strain R444 were not conducted to evaluate its persistence in air. Its degradation in the air has been related in the literature for B. bassiana species to exposure to ultraviolet (UV) irradiation, and different technologies have been developed to protect the fungus from this kind of exposure to UV, to maintain longer control of target pests. Overall, taking all the observations into consideration, it has been concluded that B. bassiana is expected to become non‐viable in air, and accumulation is not expected.

Fate and behaviour in the environment of any relevant metabolite formed by the microorganism under relevant environmental conditions

Strain‐specific investigation on in situ secondary metabolites' production in the environment (soil, surface water or possibility for movement to groundwater) following applications of ‘Bb‐Protec’ was not available. Though secondary metabolites might be present in the MPCA‐AM, only beauvericin is identified as a metabolite of potential concern and this was not present in the MPCP above its LOQ (see Section 1). Considering the available information on the biology and life cycle of the species, in‐situ production of secondary metabolites in the environmental compartments can be concluded as minimal, with the exception of within infected arthropods or in the rhizosphere or plant tissues (see Sections 2, 4 and 5.1). Consequently, PEC for secondary metabolites for the formulation for representative uses and these representative uses were concluded as not being necessary.

POTENTIAL EFFECTS ON NON‐TARGET ORGANISMS

Several aspects related to the ecotoxicological risk assessment of Beauveria bassiana strain R444 were discussed at the Pesticides Peer Review Teleconference 178 (June 2025).

Birds and mammals

Dietary studies were available with birds (Bobwhite quail, 5‐day exposure) and mammals (rat, single exposure) using the formulation for representative uses ‘Bb‐Protec’. No mortality or clinical signs of toxicity were observed, and no indication of infectivity or pathogenicity was detected during the post‐exposure observation periods (30 days for birds) and 21 days (for rats).

The systematic literature search identified no peer‐reviewed publications reporting adverse effects of B. bassiana strain R444 in birds or mammals under environmentally relevant exposure conditions.

Based on all available evidence, it was considered that B. bassiana strain R444 is neither infective nor pathogenic to birds and mammals.

Beauveria bassiana strain R444 has the potential to produce various secondary metabolites (see Section 2). No safety concerns were identified for the secondary metabolite beauvericin present in the formulation Bb‐Protect. However, this did not address the separate exposure scenario of birds orally exposed to secondary metabolites potentially produced in situ within infected arthropods under representative field conditions. For this exposure scenario, the available information was insufficient to conclude a low risk, resulting in a data gap (see Section 9.1). This data gap does not apply to uses in permanent structures, where exposure is considered minimal. Consequently, for field uses and uses in non‐permanent protected structures, the risk assessment for birds regarding secondary metabolites potentially produced in situ by B. bassiana strain R444 could not be finalised. The RMS had a diverging view.

Aquatic organisms

Acute and chronic aquatic studies with the Bb‐Protec formulation were conducted in fish species (rainbow trout Oncorhynchus mykiss and zebra fish Danio rerio) and aquatic invertebrates (Daphnia magna). The acute studies, due to their short duration, were not suitable for assessing infectivity or pathogenicity. Although infectivity and pathogenicity were not directly evaluated in the 30‐day chronic fish study, the exposure duration was considered sufficient for any such effects to manifest.

No adverse effects were reported in the fish studies or in the acute D. magna study. In the chronic D. magna study, mortality effects were observed in both the active test item (100% cumulative mortality by day 21) and attenuated test item (52%) treatment groups, but not in the media control (4%) or the test item sterile filtrate (4.1%) groups. It was concluded that these adverse effects were attributed to physical entrapment associated with the presence of the bacterial cells, rather than to an adverse biological effect of the microorganism (i.e. infectivity or pathogenicity). No evidence of infectivity or pathogenicity to fish and daphnids were observed in the chronic studies.

The applicant provided several lines of evidence supporting a low risk conclusion to aquatic invertebrates: (i) effects were due to physical entrapment rather than toxic or pathogenic mechanisms, (ii) test concentrations exceeded realistic environmental exposure levels, (iii) B. bassiana rapidly declines in aquatic systems, leading to only transient exposure and (iv) the systematic literature search identified one publication showing no adverse effects of B. bassiana (a strain different than R444) on the fish Cyprinus carpio and the green algae Selenastrum capricornutum, after 96 and 72 h of exposure, respectively.

Based on all the available data, it was concluded that B. bassiana strain R444 is neither infective nor pathogenic to aquatic organisms and that secondary metabolites potentially produced by B. bassiana strain R444 are of no safety concern. Therefore, a low risk to aquatic organisms was concluded for all representative uses of B. bassiana strain R444.

Bees

Acute contact and oral studies on honey bees with the formulation for representative uses Bb‐Protec were available, with prolonged post‐exposure observation periods of 27 days (contact) and 30 days (oral). No chronic adult studies or larval studies were available.

Contact exposure resulted in no increased mortality compared with the control group during the observation period. Oral exposure produced cumulative mortality of 17% (active test item) and 24% (attenuated test item) by day 30, with no mortality observed in the control. The onset of mortality was delayed (from approximately day 15 onwards) and progressed gradually, and no clinical signs indicative of infection or pathogenicity were reported in any treatment group. A quantitative exposure assessment showed that the concentrations tested in the oral and contact studies were above the calculated worst‐case field exposure (margins of safety of 4 and 2, respectively).

The systematic literature search identified 17 peer‐reviewed publications investigating effects of B. bassiana on honey bees and bumblebees, although none of them used the strain under assessment. Reported effects were variable across strains and concentrations/doses but generally indicated low risk under field realistic exposure levels. Several publications demonstrated that natural hive temperatures inhibit B. bassiana conidial germination, preventing sustained exposure of honey bee larvae or in‐hive adults to viable conidia.

Based on the absence of infectivity or pathogenicity in the available regulatory studies (where the tested exposure levels were exceeding the realistic field exposure) and on the literature evidence indicating limited exposure of honey bees under hive conditions due to temperature constraints on B. bassiana growth, it was concluded that B. bassiana strain R444 poses a low risk to honey bees for all representative uses.

Based on all the available data, it was concluded that B. bassiana strain R444 is neither infective nor pathogenic to bees and that secondary metabolites potentially produced by B. bassiana strain R444 are of no safety concern. Therefore, a low risk to bees was concluded for all representative uses of B. bassiana strain R444.

Non‐target arthropods other than bees

Three 10‐day oral studies were available with the formulation for representative uses to assess the effects of B. bassiana strain R444 on non‐target arthropods other than bees. Infectivity and pathogenicity were not investigated in those studies.

A data gap was identified for contact exposure studies with non‐target arthropods and the formulation for representative uses, considering that a co‐formulant may exert effects via a physical mode of action (e.g. suffocation) and a concern could not be dismissed 8 (see Section 10). A quantitative risk assessment was performed for the oral exposure route using margin of safety (MoS) values calculated from the three regulatory studies. MoS values calculated using mortality endpoints ranged between 0.5 and 156 for the representative uses on stone fruits.

The systematic literature search identified 15 peer‐reviewed publications investigating effects of B. bassiana on non‐target arthropods other than bees, although none used strain R444. Reported effects were variable across strains and concentrations/doses, with most studies showing no or low effects on non‐target arthropods. The limited field studies indicated minor and transient effects under field conditions. Several publications demonstrated that effects on non‐target arthropods, when observed, were consistently lower than effects on target pest species, and that strain‐specific differences play a crucial role in determining impact levels.

The available studies, including those from the literature search and the risk assessment for non‐target arthropods other than bees, were discussed at the meeting. 9

The species tested in the regulatory studies (i.e. the coccinellid Lindorus lopanthae, the lacewing Chrysoperla rufilabris and the was Pediobius foveolatus) were considered representative of natural enemies of pests relevant to the proposed uses described in the GAP. The experts agreed that a contact study was needed to assess that exposure route.

The experts concluded that, considering the available data, the outcome of the quantitative risk assessment and the literature search, a low risk via the oral route of exposure to non‐target arthropods from B. bassiana strain R444 could not be demonstrated for the representative field uses and for the uses in non‐permanent protective structures. This conclusion was reached considering the low MoS value for the parasitic wasp P. foveolatus (0.5), that repeated applications (up to 20) may increase exposure and the general uncertainty around the outcome of the findings from non‐strain‐specific literature studies. Additionally, the risk to non‐target arthropods via contact exposure could not be addressed with the available data. The RMS had a diverging view, considering that a low risk to non‐target arthropods could be finally concluded based on the available toxicity data, the outcome of the literature search and the weight‐of‐evidence approach. Therefore, for the representative uses in the field and in non‐permanent structures, the risk assessment for non‐target arthropods other than bees from infectivity and pathogenicity, and toxicity from secondary metabolites potentially produced by B. bassiana strain R444 could not be finalised (data gap leading to issue not finalised, see Section 9.1). For the representative uses in permanent structures, however, a low risk to non‐target arthropods other than bees could be concluded considering the minimal exposure.

Soil organisms

A 14‐day study with the earthworm Eisenia fetida using the formulation for representative uses Bb‐Protec was available. The duration of the study was considered sufficient for assessing potential infectivity and pathogenicity. No adverse effects were observed.

A quantitative assessment showed that the predicted environmental density in soil was substantially lower than the endpoint from the study, resulting in a high margin of safety.

The systematic literature search identified a peer‐reviewed publication investigating effects of B. bassiana on earthworms, although the study did not use strain R444 and focused primarily on mortality. The study demonstrated that B. bassiana did not affect survival of the earthworm E. eugeniae.

Based on the absence of mortality or behavioural effects in the study and the high MoS, a low risk to earthworms from pathogenicity, infectivity and toxicity from secondary metabolites was concluded for all representative uses of B. bassiana strain R444.

No individual studies for toxicity to soil microorganisms were available for B. bassiana strain R444. However, several lines of evidence were presented to address the risk. These were: (i) B. bassiana is a common inhabitant of soils and a natural part of soil microbial communities; therefore, its use in agricultural settings is not expected to cause adverse effects, and (ii) soil microbial communities show good resilience with natural populations well adapted to fluctuations and able to recover even from major disturbances. Based on these considerations, a low risk to soil microorganisms was concluded for all representative uses of B. bassiana strain R444.
