Distribution characteristics and influencing and controlling mechanisms of sediment/pore water metals in the intermountain basin of the Northwest Pacific Ocean
Authors: Yang Wang, Yang Liu, Hongyi Wang, Haoran Li, Jianxin Xia, Huaiming Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The sediment-water interface and shallow-surface sediment are the “factory” for early diagenesis.Research on diagenesis of the Eastern Pacific and Atlantic Ocean suggests that the solid phase metals are enriched above the oxic-suboxic boundary, while the dissolved phase metals are enriched below it. However, there is still limited research of the Northwest Pacific Ocean, especially on the small-scale metal synergistic variations above the boundary and the influence of oligotrophic conditions on metal distribution. This research gives a closer examination into the vertical variations of the two phases metal (Mn、Fe、Co、Ni、Cu、Mo、Cd、Cr、Pb、Zn) from the Western Pacific Intermountain Basin (such as Block M2 in the southern contract area of polymetallic nodules exploration of the Beijing Pioneer Hi-tech. Development Co., Ltd. (the BPC)), and achieves the following scientific knowledge: The boundaries in eutrophic regions such as the Eastern Equatorial Pacific is located at a depth of a few to over ten centimeters in the sediment, while the oligotrophic Western Pacific have relatively deeper boundaries. The relatively weaker biological activity results in smaller amount of metal entering the sediment, as well as the lower content of montmorillonite leads to lower amount of carrier for metal fixation, make the shallow-surface sediment in the study area have lower solid and dissolved metal. Above the boundary, the relatively enriched solid phase metal and the little remaining dissolved phase metal exhibit consistent small-scale vertical variation characteristics. This suggests that similar environmental conditions such as redox conditions, or the simultaneous supply of reactants/ carriers at the same depth, constrain the variations of the two metal phases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54998-9.

Introduction

The intersection of bottom water and sediment is a critical interface for metal consolidation and dissolution, which is a “factory” for metal mineralization evolution and early diagenesis. Therefore, studying the metal distribution and its influencing and controlling mechanisms can help enhance our understanding of these processes. The vertical attenuation of oxygen concentration from the bottom water to the sediment-water surface leads to changes in redox conditions, which resulted in vertical differential distribution characteristics of metals such as Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, and Cu1. The demonstration of these characteristics is also related to factors such as energy production during reactions1, flux of reactants/carriers2, intensity of biological respiration3, intensity of carrier adsorption4, and past climate change5. The most pronounced characteristics of metal vertical variation in the shallow-surface sediment appear at the oxic-suboxic boundary (where dissolved oxygen sudden decrease1. Above this boundary, the solid phase metal often exhibits enrichment, while below this boundary, the dissolved phase metal shows enrichment characteristics6. Under these macroscopic characteristics, researchers have also reported some small-scale vertical variation characteristics, such as enrichment of dissolved phase metal in the uppermost surface layer7, synergistic variations among solid phase metals5, sequential peak formation8, traces of metal migration6, and changes caused by the participation of oxidants at deeper depths9. The sequential reaction of metals with changes in redox conditions which controls vertical variation characteristics are only ideal states. However, there are obviously differences in metal vertical distribution characteristics among different regions or even at different stations within the same region10. These exceptions may be related to the change such as the flux of organic carbon input, bottom water environmental conditions such as temperature and pH, and differences in metal properties and behaviors11.

Based on researches on the metal vertical distribution characteristics in the shallow-surface sediment/pore water of the Eastern Pacific Ocean (CCZ, i.e. Clarion-Clipperton Fraction Zone10,12–15, Guatemala Basin4,16, Peru Basin5,6,17–19, Eastern Pacific Continental Margin20,21, the Western Pacific Ocean22–24, the Atlantic Ocean (Northeast Atlantic Basin8,25–28, the Equatorial Atlantic Ocean6,29, Atlantic Continental Margin11, as well as zonation reaction models established based on measured data1,2,9,30–33, the metal variation characteristics near the boundaries and in deep layers have preliminarily demonstrated34–36. However, scientific knowledge of the distribution characteristic and its mechanisms is still in shortage. In addition, previous researches have mainly focused on large-scale variations in metals vertical distribution around the boundaries and in more deeper layers, especially on the mechanism of redox condition, with the lack of research on the small-scale and synergistic variations above the boundaries, especially on the mechanisms of biological activities as well as carrier fixation. As a contractor of the International Seabed Authority, Beijing Pioneer Hi-tech. development Co. Ltd. (the BPC) has been continuously conducting resource and environmental surveys in its polymetallic nodule exploration contract area in the North-Western Pacific Ocean in recent years. This research, based on box-corer and multi-corers sampling data from 6 stations in the southern contract area (Fig. 1), analyzes the regional differences of solid metal content in sediment/dissolved metal concentration in pore water, and furtherly demonstrates their vertical variation characteristics in the shallow-surface sediment (above the oxic-suboxic boundary) of the oligotrophic North-Western Pacific intermountain basin, as well as studying the mechanisms.

Regional background & sampling information

The North-Western Pacific Ocean is a typical distribution area of seamount chains and intermountain basins. This area is located in a subtropical oligotrophic zone, with extremely low organic carbon content in surface sediment37. Pelagic clay is the main type of sediment of this area which is composited with mainly terrestrial debris (silt and clay, 75 ~ 98%), siliceous biogenic debris (< 1 ~ 20%), and volcanic debris (1 ~ 10%)38. The bottom flow is mainly controlled by the Antarctic bottom water (AABW), which generally flows from south to north in this area39. Polymetallic nodules are widely distribution in this area, with an average abundance of 26.16 kg/m2 and an average coverage of 61.13% in Block M238.

Samples were collected during the Cruise DY69 in the southern contract areas of the BPC, namely Block M1 and M2, located in the intermountain basins of the Magellan, Marshall, and Marcus Wake seamount areas in the North-Western Pacific Ocean (Fig. 1). The stations deployed in cross-section to obtain data along a certain latitude and longitude to study the horizontal differential distribution characteristics of the metals. Samples were collected at stations located near the foothills of the seamounts and within the intermountain basins to study the differential distribution characteristics of the metals between the basins and the mountains. The thickness of sediment cores is between 20 ~ 40 cm (stratified sampled by 1–2 cm intervals, see the vertical coordinate of the profile for details) in order to study the vertical distribution of the metals. Samples were collected by box-corer or multi-corers, and all samples are collected without sediment leakage, with clear overlying water, and with a total weight of no less than 10 kg.

Results

Metal content and concentration of the two phases

The samples in this research have not yet reached the boundary within their sampling depth (within 50 cm, this will be explained later in the text). Above the oxic-suboxic boundary, most of the shallow-surface sediment in the intermountain basins of the North-Western Pacific have low levels of solid phase metals (Fig. 2), as same as the concentration of dissolved phase metals, which is also a low level or even depleted (Fig. 3). The solid phase Mn content does not exceed 1% in the study area, similar to the maxima observed above the boundary in the CCZ12 and the Atlantic Ocean11, but relatively lower than the maximum in the Peru Basin (approximately 1 ~ 3%, 283MC5 and the Guatemala Basin (approximately 1.5 ~ 6.5%, 9BC26)4. Except for station DY69-BC17, the solid phase Ni content does not exceed 200 mg/kg in the study area, similar to the situation in the Atlantic Ocean25, but relatively lower than the CCZ (up to approximately 350 mg/kg14, the Peru Basin (approximately 400 ~ 800 mg/kg, 283MC)5 and the Guatemala Basin (up to approximately 400 ~ 1900 mg/kg, 9BC26)4. The solid phase Cu, Mo, Cd and Zn also exhibit significant differences as mentioned above. The concentration of dissolved metal in the depletion zone above the boundary was not obviously lower. The concentration of dissolved Mn in this area generally does not exceed 0.4 µmol/L, which is lower than that of Peru Basin and Guatemala Basin (generally higher than 0.4 µmol/L) above the boundary. The concentrations of dissolved Cu, Fe, Cd and Pb in this area also showed similar differences (especially for sample 283MC at the Peru basin), while the concentrations of dissolved Co and Zn are consistent with those of other sea areas mentioned above.

The station DY69-BC17 is located near the foothill area of a seamount and the metal contents in solid phase of this station are relatively higher (Fig. 2). For instance, the comparison of the solidMn, Ni, and Cu content between this station and other stations are as follows: Mn: about 0.6 ~ 1.0% and about 0.5 ~ 0.7%, Ni: about 150 ~ 400 mg/kg and about 120 ~ 200 mg/kg, and Cu: about 300 ~ 400 mg/kg and about 180 ~ 260 mg/kg. Other solid phase metal contents at this station, including Fe, Co, Zn, Mo, Pb, and Cd, exhibit the aforementioned characteristics, while that of the dissolved Co, Cu, Cd, Pb concentrations are not obvious. The other seamount-near station, DY69-BC09, exhibit more pronounced enrichment characteristics in dissolved phase metals concentrations (Fig. 3). For instance, the comparison of the dissolved phase Co and Cu concentrations between this station and other stations are as follows: Co: up to about 0.01 ~ 0.035 µmol/L and 0.005 ~ 0.010 µmol/L; Cu: up to about 0.05 ~ 0.25 µmol/L and 0.02 ~ 0.06 µmol/L. Dissolved phase Mn, Cd, Pb, and Zn concentrations also exhibit the aforementioned differences, while that of the dissolved phase Fe concentration, and most solid phase metal contents are not obvious.

Synergistic vertical variation and the small-scale profile characteristics of the two phases

The metals exhibit three levels of the synergistic variations vertically including: variations between solid phase metals, variations between dissolved phase metals, and variations between metals in two phases (Figs.4,5,6,7,8,9,10,11,12). For examples: the correlation coefficients between the solid phase Mn content and the solid phase Ni contents at station DY69-BC09, DY69-BC20 and DY69-BC22 are 0.91 (p < 0.01), 0.94 (p < 0.01), and0.96 (p < 0.01), respectively (Fig. 4A and D, and 4E); the correlation coefficients between the solid phase Co content and the solid phase Cd contents at station DY69-BC09, DY69-BC20 and DY69-BC22 are 0.82 (p < 0.01), 0.90 (p < 0.01), and 0.84 (p < 0.01), respectively (Fig. 5A and D, and 5E); the correlation coefficients between the dissolved phase Mn concentration and the dissolved phase Co, Cu, and Pb concentrations at station DY69-BC22 are 0.74 (p < 0.01), 0.65 (p < 0.01), and 0.65(p < 0.01), respectively (Fig. 6E, and 7E); the correlation coefficients between the solid phase Mn content and the dissolved phase Mn concentration at station DY69-MC02 are as high as 0.96 (p < 0.01) (Fig. 8B); the correlation coefficients between the solid phase Zn content and the dissolved phase Zn concentration at station DY69-BC22 are as high as 0.93 (p < 0.01) (Fig. 9E). More correlation data in detail can be found in Supplementary Material 1. We have conducted a comprehensive correlation analysis (including integration and averaging process between different stations and metals) on the currently available data, which will cause a decrease in the correlation coefficient, but this correlation is still clear in the profile features. Inevitably, due to the complexity of influencing and controlling factors, there are a few exceptions exist (like some poor correlation between some metal profiles at some stations such as solid phase Fe profile in Station DY69-BC09 and DY69-BC17, see Fig. 5A and C).

The metal content/concentration profiles of samples from various stations in the study area, whether in the solid or dissolved phase, exhibit relatively similar small-scale vertical variation characteristics from the surface to shallower(Figs. 4,5,6,7,8,9,10,11,12), including: ① enrichment above about 2 cm; ② minima at about 3 cm; ③ maxima at about 5 cm; ④ low value range between about 5 ~ 10 cm; ⑤ first increasing and then decreasing range between about 10 ~ 25 cm; and ⑥ first increasing and then decreasing range between about 25 ~ 40 cm.

We also compared the vertical distribution profiles of the solid and dissolved metals at each station (Figs. 10,11,12). Among them, the vertical variations of Mn, Co, Ni, Cu, Cd, Pb, and Zn were highly comparable with the unified small-scale characteristics, while Fe, Cr, and Mo showed a gradually increasing trend with burial depth. We found that the vertical variation of solid phase metals expect Cr was significantly positively correlated with montmorillonite content (r = 0.55 ~ 0.83, p < 0.03 (except Mo), as shown in Figs. 4, 5 and 13 and Supplementary Material 2, original data sources: ref. 38), while the vertical variation of Solid Cr was strongly correlated with illite content (r = 0.56, p < 0.001, see Fig. 14 and Supplementary Material 2, original data sources: ref. 38). At the same time, the correlation between the content of solid phase metals (Mn, Co, Ni, Cu) and montmorillonite content at each station is extremely high (r = 0.94 ~ 0.98, p < 0.03, see Supplementary Material 3, original data sources: ref. 38).

Discussion

Differences in boundary depth and metal distribution due to nutritional condition and amount of carrier

The dissolved oxygen concentration of the bottom water in the study area can reach 200µmol/L38, which is obviously higher than that in the Peru Basin (about 135 ~ 145 µmol/L)9,19 and North-Eastern and the tropic Pacific Ocean (about 100 ~ 180 µmol/L)7,10,12–15,18,21,40,41, while lower than the South Pacific Ocean (usually at about 180–240 µmol/L)3,42(Figs, 15B, 16B and 16 C). This high oxygen concentration is attributed to the traverse of oxygen-rich AABW from south to north39,43. The North-Western and South Pacific Oceans are oligotrophic (see the base map of Fig. 15), where low biological productivity, low organic carbon flux, and low sedimentation rates result in extremely low organic carbon content in the shallow-surface sediment37,42,44–46, indicating that biological activities consume less oxygen. In oligotrophic regions such as North-Western and South Pacific Ocean), the relatively abundant oxygen supply and extremely low biological consumption result in the unconsumed dissolved oxygen being able to penetrate down into deeper depths (up to tens of meters)3,42. In contrast, in the relatively eutrophic Peru Basin and East Pacific Ocean, the depths oxic-suboxic boundaries are at only a few to tens of centimeters depth9, while in the mesotrophic CCZ and North-Eastern Atlantic Basin, these depths are at a few meters’ depth12,15,28(Figs. 15C and 16B C). The average concentration of NO3− in the pore water of samples in the study area is 0.417 µmol/L38, and is relatively uniform in profile (Fig. 17A), and the TOC (Total Organic Carbon) concentration continuously decreases from the surface to the deeper layers but remains in surplus (Fig. 17B)38, which not only manifesting an oxidized environment1,47, but also suggesting that the samples in this research have not yet reached the boundary within their sampling depth (within 50 cm) (Fig. 16B).

Compared to the eutrophic and mesotrophic regions, the North-Western Pacific Ocean exhibits lower metal content/concentration in both the solid and dissolved phases above the boundary (Figs. 2, 3 and 15A). This is explained by the low organic carbon content and biomass in oligotrophic regions, which restricts the oxidation reactions related to organisms20, limiting metal fixation from water to sediment (Fig. 16B and C). In addition, the relatively lack of montmorillonite carriers in the study area compared to the Eastern Pacific (Fig. 15D and E) is also a reason for the relatively low content of solid phase metals. The relatively high montmorillonite content in some high-value areas of the Eastern and Western Pacific Ocean, as well as in the near-seamount stations in this study, may be related to their proximity to volcanic material sources48,49. Moreover, due to the close relationship between montmorillonite’s involvement in early diagenesis and biological processes50, higher productivity also contributes to the fixation of metals. The regional differences of the content of solid Fe, Co, Pb, and Cd are not as significant as those in Mn, Ni, Cu, Zn, and Mo. This suggests that the Mn and Fe group metals have different enrichment preferences similar to those in polymetallic nodules51. This also suggests that some of the metal in sediment also exist in the form of Fe-Mn oxide particles, even micro nodules52,53.

The 2 stations, DY69-BC17 and DY69-BC09, near the seamounts exhibit respectively high solid phase metal content and dissolved phase metal concentration (Figs. 2 and 3). On the one hand, it is related to the relatively high organic carbon flux and biological processes near seamount where organic carbon fixes more metals into sediments. On the other hand, it is related to the abundant supply of montmorillonite carriers near the seamount (Fig. 16A). The sediment collected in station DY69-BC17 is relatively high consolidated, with high sand content (38.98%), characterized by a high montmorillonite content (14.19%, see Fig. 15E, sources: ref. 38), which also suggests that montmorillonite is the carrier of metals4. At the same time, the high consolidation as well as relatively large particle size suggests the older sediment age at its lower layer (below 4 cm)63. However, according to the view of5, the vertical distribution of metals is dynamically changed, meaning that the disturbances of changes of the environments can quickly lead it into a new balance. This also explain why the vertical distribution characteristics of metals at this station are not significantly different from those at other stations. In addition, station DY69-BC17 is the only station without nodule coverage, while the nodule abundance and coverage at other stations range from 6.8 to 42.3 kg/m2 and 36% to 81%, respectively38 There is a negative correlation between the solid phase metal content and nodule abundance/coverage, as well as the nodule metal content, with average correlation coefficients of −0.42 (p < 0.06), −0.68 (p < 0.10), and − 0.46 (p < 0.05) between stations, respectively (Supplementary Material 3), indicating a tendency for metal in sediment to migrate and enrich in nodules. The concentration of dissolved metals at another near-seamount station, DY69-BC09, is relatively high, which is the result of biological process (mainly associated with the decomposition of organic matter by microorganisms4) producing more dissolved metals. Deng et al23. also found that the concentrations of dissolved Mn, Al, and REE in pore water located in the seamount area are much higher than those in the abyssal plain, attributing this to the dissolution of elements from detrital materials near the seamounts and their extensive diffusion towards the seafloor. In this study, due to the high biological productivity in seamount areas, the supply of organic carbon is abundant, leading to strong oxidation reactions of organic carbon, which results in a positive correlation between dissolved phase metal concentration and water nitrate concentration (r = 0.76, p < 0.02) and water phosphate concentration (r = 0.69, p < 0.09)1,38 (see Supplementary Material 3). Stations DY69-BC09 and DY69-BC17 also exhibit significant differences in some physical and chemical environments features, characterized by relatively lower bottom water temperature (1.465 ~ 1.491℃, compared to 1.498 ~ 1.516 ℃ at other stations), salinity (34.690 ~ 34.694 psu, compared to around 34.695 psu at other stations), and higher bottom water pH (7.91, compared to 7.88 ~ 1.89 at other stations)38. The results of the correlation analysis (Supplementary Material 3) indicate that dissolved phase metal concentrations are negatively correlated with water temperature (r = −0.78, p < 0.11) and water salinity (r = −0.66, p < 0.01). These factors may comprehensively control the differential distribution of metal by promoting or limiting redox reactions2,32,35. The two near-seamount stations separately characterized by high solid metal content and dissolved metal concentration, indicating that carrier fixation and biodegradation process played controlling roles, respectively.

In typical scenarios, some dissolved-phase metals are enriched beneath the oxic-suboxic boundary6. Disturbances such as deep-sea mining, if they breach this boundary, may cause dissolved metals to release into seawater, and these metals are toxic to marine organisms64, potentially causing environmental impacts. For current mining technologies, the disturbance depth is mostly around ten to several tens of centimeters38,65. Thus, if deep-sea mining occurs in eutrophic areas, due to the shallow depth of oxic-suboxic boundaries, the disturbance may cause the release of a large amount of dissolved-phase metal. However, there is almost no such risk sine the deeper depth of the boundaries in the north-western Pacific intermountain basin area. Disturbances of the deep-sea mining inevitably cause sediment suspension and redistribution above the boundary, and thus the lower content of solid phase metals above the boundary in this area also mitigates this process and its resulting environmental impacts. Therefore, the above two advantages reduce the environmental impact risk of heavy metal release from deep-sea mining activities if it is happened in the north-western Pacific intermountain basin, especially in the plain areas far from seamounts where the content/concentration of solid and dissolved phase metals is lower.

Synergistic vertical variation and the small-scale profile characteristics of the two phases and its control mechanisms

The synergistic variation in vertical distribution of metal represented by solid phase Mn5,14,66 is primarily due to the adsorption of related metals by Mn solid phase35 or fixation with the same carrier8. When solid metal dissolves, the metals adsorbed are mobilized and dissolved at the same time8,16, resulting in synergistic variation in the concentration of metals in the dissolved phase14,20. However, under the macro-scale variation characteristics, i.e. metal enrichment in the solid phase and depletion in the dissolved phase above the oxic-suboxic boundary5,6,11, there is a synergistic variation between the little remaining dissolved phase metal concentration and the solid phase metal content. This indicates that during the conversion reaction between the two phases, the suitable environmental conditions the both phases favored exist, the reaction products are continuously accumulated, meanwhile the reactants or carriers are continuously supplied.

According to existing early diagenetic models1,2,9,30–33, the main redox processes occurring above the oxic-suboxic boundary are respiration and nitrification. The mechanisms for the small-scale vertical distribution characteristics of solid and dissolved metals in surface-shallow sediment and pore water in the North-Western Pacific intermountain basin (oligotrophic region) can be summarized as follows (Fig. 16): The enrichment of metals in the uppermost layer (see profile DY69-BC09, Fig. 4A ~ Fig. 9A) is the result of organic matter degradation5 and at least 70% of the organic carbon consumption within this process occurs within the upper 1 ~ 2 cm of the sediment (Fig. 17B)7,16. This process also resulted in mild enrichment of NO3 (Fig. 17A)− as well as a correlation between dissolved metals and bottom water nutrient concentration (see Supplementary Material 3)16,18. However, the enrichment degree of metals is not as significant as in regions such as the Peru Basin5, which is also related to the oligotrophic condition in the study area. The synergistic variation between the two phases indicate that this mechanism continuously supplies dissolved-phase metals, which are then continuously fixed into solid phase metal by diagenesis1,67, i.e. the more dissolved phase supply, the richer the solid-phase products. After metals exhibit enrichment at the surface, a decay (expressed as a minimum value) generally occurs in the subsurface layer first, followed by a second maxima. The maxima or minima values of metals at 3–5 cm (such as DY69-MC02, Fig. 4B ~ Fig. 9B) can be explained by metal migration20. This migration is also dominated by Mn, due to its high mobilization ability, so after the first maxima of Mn, a second maxima will appear below it66; although Fe has relatively low mobilization ability, it can combine with carriers such as clay minerals, and it also shows a small peak above the boundary (at about 3 cm)68. Many metal such as Cu, also migrate simultaneously with Mn and Fe, showing a similar second maxima in the profile20. The consistent surface variation characteristics in the metal profiles of each station indicate that surface disturbances were effectively avoided during the sampling process. The significant changes in the metal profiles of each station indicate limited biological disturbance (as this effect can lead to uniform metal distribution28). The significant correlation between solid metal content and montmorillonite content between stations and profiles (Figs. 4, 5 and 13, and Supplementary Materials 2 and 3) indicates that montmorillonite provides a useful carrier for solid metals4. At about 5 ~ 10 cm, due to the lack of suitable reaction conditions as well as the amount of the carriers (Figs. 4 and 5), metals migrate out of this layer, resulting in low metal content/concentration (see profile DY69-BC09, Fig. 4A ~ 9 A). Below this, at about 10 ~ 25 cm and 25 ~ 40 cm, the metals exhibit first increasing and then decreasing (see profiles DY69-BC22 and DY69-BC25, Fig. 4E ~ 9E and Fig. 4F ~ 9 F), which is mainly related to the enrichment of carrier and the fixation process (Figs. 4 and 5). As reported, the relevant metals with respectively high mobilization ability, such as Co, Zn, and Cu, are partially fixed in the sub-oxidized environment by adsorbing onto carries like clay, organic, or Fe compounds4,5,68. In addition, there is a certain similarity between the changes in NO3− profile (Fig. 17A) and the changes in solid/dissolved metal, manifested as mild enrichment at 2 cm, low values range between 5 ~ 10 cm, first increasing and then decreasing range between 10 ~ 25 cm and 25 ~ −40 cm. This is because the nutrients in pore water solidify or dissolve at the same time as the dissolved metals. The overall performance of the TOC% profile is a consumption process from surface to depth, which is due to the gradual consumption of organic carbon by oxidation reactions. Organic carbon is oxidized by oxidants (dissolved oxygen and nitrate) along with metals above the boundary27, which results in a certain similarity between its profile variation and variations in metal content (Fig. 17B), manifested in enrichment in the surface layer and low value range between 5 ~ 10 cm.

Metals with different behaviors also exhibit differences in vertical variations (Figs. 10,11, and 12). Mn, Ni, and Cu generally have relatively typical profile characteristics20 (see profile DY69-BC09, Figs. 4 and 6, and 8A) and often display the aforementioned small-scale variations. This is due to the strong mobilization ability of these metals, which are sensitive to change of redox conditions and so that are favored reactants5. Metals such as Fe, Mo and Cr require some kinds of carrier for fixation or special redox condition in order to enrichment or transformation20, resulting in less pronounced profile characteristics1 (see profile DY69-BC09, Figs. 5 and 7, and 9A). Some researchers have reported the sequentially appearing of metals peaks in the profile and explained it as competition for28 or different sensitivities to8 redox conditions. In this research, the inconsistent depth of metal peaks is also common, like the first peak of dissolved Cd and Zn appeared earlier than Fe and Pb, located at 2 cm and 3 cm, respectively (see profile DY69-BC22, Fig. 7E), but no regular pattern in the sequence of metal peaks was found. Additionally, although the metal peaks appear varying in depth, the shape of the peaks and the whole profile are basically comparable.

Conclusions

In summary, this study suggests that there are regional differences in the distribution of solid and dissolved metals of surface-shallow ocean sediments and pore water (Fig. 16): the metal content in sediment varies regionally due to the carrier supply and nutritional condition. For oligotrophic regions (such as the North-Western Pacific intermountain basin where this study is located, Fig. 16B), moderate oxygen rich condition and lower biological productivity result in relatively deep sediment oxic-suboxic boundary (The boundary is inferred and illustrated based on the characteristics of metal and NO3− profiles in this study). The lower biological activity and montmorillonite carrier content result in relatively lower metal content in sediment. For the near-seamount area (Fig. 16C), relatively high biological productivity and montmorillonite carrier content result in relatively high sediment metal content. For eutrophic areas such as the Peru and Guatemala basin in the Eastern Equatorial Pacific Ocean (Fig. 16C), poor-oxygen condition and higher biological oxygen consumption result in shallow oxic-suboxic boundary, which combined with stronger biological processes, and higher levels of montmorillonite carriers, leading to higher sediment metal content.

There are similar vertical distribution characteristics and mechanisms of metals under regional differences in metal content, which are controlled by the amount of carrier and influenced by factors such as redox conditions and biological activity intensity (Fig. 16). Both oligotrophic and eutrophic regions conform to the overall characteristics, i.e. solid phase metals enrichment and dissolved phase metal depletion above the oxic-suboxic boundary; while solid phase metals depletion and dissolved phase metals enrichment below the boundary. This characteristic is determined by specific reactions under specific redox conditions, while special profiles that do not exhibit such overall characteristics may have undergone disturbance processes such as biological disturbance or turbidity current impact, or metal re-migration. Under the constraints of redox conditions, the vertical distribution of metals is mainly controlled by the amount of montmorillonite carrier, while biological processes and metal migration are also the impact factors. The intense biological activity on the surface leads to metal enrichment, while the peak on the subsurface is caused by the migration of metals, and the vertical variation of metals below it depends on the amount of montmorillonite carrier. The vertical synergistic variation of solid phase metals is due to their common tendency to be fixed by the montmorillonite carrier, while vertical synergistic variation of dissolved metals is due to the simultaneous release caused by the dissolution of the fixed solid metals. The vertical synergistic variation between the two phases is due to the same reaction or conditions under which they participate. When conditions are favorable, the supply of metals in both phases increases, and the reaction increases, and vice versa. In a word, the synergistic variation in the content/concentration of the two phases are caused by the similar redox states favored by the conversion reactions between various solid and dissolved phase metals based on montmorillonite carriers through biological interactions.

In areas unaffected by volcanic activity, the vertical distribution of montmorillonite carriers in shallow sediments is generally consistent, which explains why the metal profiles in eutrophic and oligotrophic areas are comparable (such as the vertical distribution characteristics of metals in this study being comparable to Koschinsky’s results5). When strong volcanic activity causes changes in the vertical distribution of montmorillonite, it breaks this typical vertical distribution of metals. The strong biological effect leads to the formation of features above and below the boundary, but does not break the vertical distribution characteristics of metals controlled by the montmorillonite carrier, but just makes more metals enriched.

Methods

Preprocessing and analysis of sediment

Dry the sediment sample in oven at 100 ℃, then grind the sample and enable it to pass through 200 mesh sieves using an agate mortar. Weigh 4.00 g samples and pour them into a mold. Edge the substrate with boric acid powder and press it with a pressure of 20 MPa on a press. Label the compressed sample on the non-measuring surface or write a sample number with a marker pen. Store it in a dryer to prevent moisture absorption for XRF testing. The content of Mn and Fe in sediment was analyzed at Lanzhou University using the PW4400/40X XRF manufactured by Panalytical Company (reference standards: GB17378-2007 and GB/T 14506.28–2010).

Weigh 0.1 g sample and place it in a 50 mL polytetrafluoroethylene beaker. Wet it with a small amount of water, add 8 mL of nitric acid, 8 mL of hydrofluoric acid, and 1mL of perchloric acid. Place the polytetrafluoroethylene beaker in an electric heating plate at 130 ℃ for digestion for 1 min, adjust the temperature of the electric heating plate to 250 ℃, and evaporate the sample to smoke from perchloric acid for about 3 min, then remove and cool it. Add 5 mL of nitric acid, 5 mL of hydrofluoric acid, and 0.5 mL of perchloric acid in sequence, heat on an electric heating plate for 10 min, then turn off the power and leave overnight. After heating again until the perchloric acid smoke is completely emitted, remove. Add 8 mL of aqua regia (freshly prepared) while still hot, heat on an electric heating plate until 2 mL ~ 3 mL of the solution is left, rinse the cup wall with about 10 mL of deionized water, and gently heat for 5 ~ 10 min until the solution is clear, then remove. After cooling, transfer the solution into a 50 mL polyethylene test tube with a scale and stopper, dilute to the mark with deionized water, shake well, and let it stand to clear for ICP-MS analysis. The contents of Co, Ni, Cu, Cd, Cr, Pb, Zn, and Mo in sediments were analyzed at Lanzhou University using an X-Series 2 ICP-MS manufactured by Thermo Fisher Scientific (reference standards: GB17378-2007 and GB/T 20260 − 2006). Parallel sample test was conducted, and the RSD between samples ranged from 0.67% to 9.71%. The standard material GBW07316 (for deep-sea sediment composition analysis, national first-class standard material, within the validity period of verification) was synchronized tested during analysis process for quality control, and the test results met the quality control requirements.

Preprocessing and analysis of pore water

Pore water samples were extracted from sediments using a Rhizon CCS (Rhizosphere Research Products, Netherlands. pore size: 0.12 ~ 0.18 μm, the left volume of the sampler is less than 0.4mL). Before sampling, immerse the sampler in ultrapure water for 5 s and keep it moist throughout the sampling process. During sampling, try to insert the sampler horizontally into the core as much as possible. After sampling, the pore water sample was immediately acidified with ultrapure hydrochloric acid to pH = 2, and then transferred to a polyethylene tube for storage under sealed and low-temperature (4 ℃) conditions. The concentrations of Mn, Fe, Co, Cu, Cd, Pb, and Zn in the pore water were tested using a 7700x ICP-MS manufactured by Agilent at Third Institute of Oceanography, Ministry of Natural Resources (reference standard: HJ 700–2014 and HJ 776–2015). The detection limits for Mn, Fe, Co, Cu, Cd, Pb, and Zn concentrations are 0.01 µg/L, 0.82 µg/L, 0.05 µg/L, 0.12 µg/L, 0.03 µg/L, 0.07 µg/L, 0.10 µg/L, respectively. Parallel sample test was conducted, and the RSD between samples ranged from 1.56% to 9.78%. The standard material GBW (E) 080040 (for seawater composition analysis, national first-class standard material, within the validity period of verification) was synchronized tested during analysis process for quality control, and the test results met the quality control requirements.
