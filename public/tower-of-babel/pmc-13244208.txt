Slow deep breathing reduces muscle sympathetic nerve activity and improves breathing efficiency in pulmonary hypertension

To the Editor:

Excessive muscle sympathetic nervous activity (MSNA) in pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) has been associated with impaired exercise capacity and risk of clinical deterioration [1]. In animal models of PAH and chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH), sympathetic nerve activity promotes deleterious pulmonary vascular and right ventricular remodelling [2, 3]. Despite this, no therapies target sympatho-excitation in pulmonary hypertension. MSNA cycles with respiration such that it is suppressed by high lung volumes [4]. Slow deep breathing, by enhancing within-breath suppression of MSNA, could reduce sympatho-excitation. Small uncontrolled trials of home device-guided slow deep breathing in pulmonary hypertension (PAH and CTEPH) for 2–3 months have shown improvements in World Health Organization functional class, health-related quality of life, and N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide [5, 6]. However these two studies have not assessed the physiologic implications (acute or longer term) of this therapy. We hypothesised that slow deep breathing would acutely reduce MSNA in pulmonary hypertension.

12 patients with clinically stable treated pulmonary hypertension (9 PAH, 3 CTEPH; 7 female; age 56±15 years) underwent measurement of MSNA (n=10; satisfactory recording was not obtained in two patients) alongside cardiovascular (heart rate and blood pressure) and respiratory (ventilation, end-tidal gas partial pressures and peripheral oxygen saturation) physiologic variables during resting spontaneous breathing (10 min) and during slow deep breathing (5 min), after the stable establishment of volitional breathing at 6 breaths·min−1, guided by a metronome. Tidal volume (VT) remained spontaneously determined. This study was approved by the Health and Disability Ethics Committee (HDEC 2022 FULL 12454), was prospectively registered (Australia New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry, ACTRN12622000494730) and conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki (2013).

Microneurography was performed at the common peroneal nerve to record post-ganglionic multi-unit MSNA [7]. In brief, a tungsten microelectrode was inserted and manipulated until an appropriate MSNA signal was obtained, showing pulse-synchronous spontaneous burst activity, a 3:1 signal-to-noise ratio, and augmentation by apnoea but not by arousal or touching of the skin. The MSNA signal was amplified, band-pass filtered (700–2000 Hz), rectified and integrated (0.1 s time constant) using a nerve traffic analyser (University of Iowa Bioengineering, Iowa City, IA, USA) to produce a mean voltage neurogram. MSNA was analysed using an open-source standardised application [8], and was expressed as bursts per minute (burst frequency), bursts per 100 heart beats (burst incidence), burst size relative to the largest baseline period burst (burst amplitude), and the product of burst frequency and amplitude (total activity).

Respiratory frequency, VT and minute ventilation were measured by pneumotachometer (with mouthpiece and nose-clip; Hans Rudolph Shawnee, KS, USA). End-tidal carbon dioxide tension (PETCO2) and end-tidal oxygen tension (PETO2) were measured at the mouth (gas analyser; ADInstruments, Bella Vista, NSW, Australia). Beat-to-beat non-invasive blood pressure (BP) was measured by finger photoplethysmography (ADInstruments) and heart rate by lead II electrocardiogram (ADInstruments).

Within-breath respiratory modulation of MSNA was calculated as the percentage change in MSNA from low lung volume (below participant's mean VT for that period) to high lung volume (above mean VT). Arterial baroreflex control of MSNA was determined as the relationship by linear regression between burst occurrence probability per cardiac cycle and diastolic BP (DBP), in 3 mmHg bins [9]. The slope was taken as sympathetic baroreflex sensitivity. The DBP corresponding to a 50% probability of burst occurrence was calculated (T50) representing the mid-point of the MSNA–DBP relationship [9, 10].

Baseline and slow deep breathing measures were compared using Student's paired t-test and Wilcoxon signed-rank test for non-normal data. Statistical significance was defined as p<0.05. Values are expressed as means±sd unless otherwise stated. Statistical analyses were performed using R Statistical Software (version 4.2.1, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) within Rstudio (version 2024.04.2, Posit, Boston, MA, USA).

Participants had idiopathic (n=6), drug-induced (n=2), congenital heart disease associated (n=1) PAH and CTEPH (n=3). From most recent right heart catheterisation, mean pulmonary artery pressure was 40±13 mmHg, pulmonary vascular resistance was 5.5±3.9 Wood units, and cardiac output was 6.1±1.0 L·min−1. Eight participants were on dual pulmonary hypertension therapy (phosphodiesterase type 5 inhibitors (PDE5i) with endothelin receptor antagonists (ERA)), one participant was on triple therapy (PDE5i, ERA and nebulised iloprost), and one participant was on calcium channel blocker monotherapy. Two CTEPH participants were treated with anticoagulation without pulmonary hypertension therapy. World Health Organization functional class was I in seven, II in two, and III in three participants.

During slow deep breathing, MSNA burst frequency decreased (24±10 during baseline versus 21±12 bursts·min−1 during slow deep breathing; p=0.037) as did burst incidence (39±19 versus 34±20 bursts·100 HB−1; p=0.004). No change was observed in burst amplitude nor total activity with slow deep breathing (figure 1a–d). Respiratory modulation of MSNA was enhanced by slow deep breathing (figure 1e). Arterial baroreflex T50 was shifted downwards with slow deep breathing (figure 1f) without a change in sensitivity (−3.7±1.8 versus −3.1±2.0 bursts·mmmHg−1; p=0.275). VT increased with slow deep breathing (figure 1h) such that minute ventilation was unchanged (figure 1i). PETCO2 reduced, whilst PETO2 and peripheral oxygen saturation increased with slow deep breathing (figure 1j–k).

This study primarily demonstrated that acute slow deep breathing in stable treated pulmonary hypertension patients reduces MSNA burst occurrence. That slow deep breathing reduced MSNA burst frequency is important, as burst frequency has been strongly associated with deleterious outcomes in PAH [1]. The acute reductions in MSNA with slow deep breathing could contribute to previously observed reductions in N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide with longer-term slow deep breathing [6], as sympatho-excitation is known to adversely impair right ventricular function [2].

Mechanistically, this study suggests reduction of MSNA by slow deep breathing in pulmonary hypertension could result from the enhanced respiratory modulation of MSNA with greater within-breath suppression of MSNA at high lung volumes triggered by the Hering–Breuer reflex. Secondly, the mid-point of the MSNA–DBP relationship (T50) was lower with slow deep breathing, without a change in sensitivity, having the effect of reducing MSNA at a given DBP, indicative of a downward shift in the MSNA–DBP relationship. However, other studies to examine arterial baroreflex control of MSNA with slow deep breathing have observed inconsistent results, leaving the role of the arterial baroreflex in slow deep breathing uncertain [11, 12]. Finally, improvements in gas exchange could reduce central and/or peripheral chemoreflex activation and subsequent sympathetic outflow.

Whilst MSNA burst size and total activity were unchanged, both burst occurrence and size, determine the amount of neurotransmitter released from sympathetic nerve terminals, but are differentially controlled [10], potentially indicative of independent functions. Though MSNA total activity integrates both factors, how burst frequency and amplitude interact to produce end-organ consequences of MSNA remains poorly understood. As such, whether the re-distribution of MSNA activity to larger but less frequent bursts seen in some patients, is physiologically significant, is unclear.

Secondly, we observed that a slow deep breathing pattern enhanced breathing efficiency, despite pulmonary vascular impairments to gas exchange. This raises the possibility that the opposing, rapid and shallow breathing pattern frequently adopted by pulmonary hypertension patients contributes to ventilatory inefficiency [13]. Slow deep breathing likely improves breathing efficiency by reducing dead space volume as a proportion of VT, thereby enhancing alveolar ventilation. However, without arterial blood gas measurements we are unable to confirm this. Improvements in pulmonary blood flow due to potentiated venous return induced by larger intrathoracic pressure swings during slow deep breathing may also contribute to improvements in ventilation–perfusion matching [14].

This was a small sample, particularly for MSNA data (n=10), of generally well and stable pulmonary hypertension patients with lower basal MSNA, which may limit the generalisability of findings to patients with more advanced disease and greater sympatho-excitation. However, acute slow deep breathing appears to have similar efficacy in populations ranging from healthy young individuals with low basal resting MSNA and chronic cardiorespiratory conditions with higher MSNA [11, 15, 16]. Secondly, by including both PAH and CTEPH patients, some specificity of the insights gained may be limited. Thirdly, we did not assess the pulmonary vascular or cardiac haemodynamic effects of slow deep breathing. Whilst increased venous return to the right ventricle (RV) may be advantageous in those with compensated RV function, its effects in those with a decompensated or overloaded RV warrants consideration. Finally, we only observed the acute physiologic effects of slow deep breathing. Whether these are sustained and underlie relevant clinical benefits with long-term practise of slow deep breathing remains unclear, both in pulmonary hypertension but also more broadly in chronic cardiorespiratory disease.

Acute slow deep breathing in treated and stable PAH and CTEPH patients has promising physiological effects, reducing MSNA and improving breathing efficiency. Randomised sham-controlled trials are warranted to assess the effects of long-term slow deep breathing on both physiologic and clinical outcomes in pulmonary hypertension.
