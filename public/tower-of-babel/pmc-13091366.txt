Synergistic engineering of monoterpenoid synthase and metabolic pathways to enhance α-terpineol production in Saccharomyces cerevisiae
Authors: Shuai Wang, Xinxin Wang, Ying Wang, Chun Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPlant-derived monoterpenoid α-terpineol has gained significant attention due to its versatile applications in the food, cosmetic and pharmaceutical industries. Microbial biosynthesis offers an environmentally sustainable approach for its commercial-scale production. Here, we developed a combinatorial engineering strategy to enhance α-terpineol production in Saccharomyces cerevisiae. A truncated Vitis vinifera α-terpineol synthase (tVvTS) was optimized through alanine scanning, site-directed mutagenesis, and N-terminal truncation, yielding a variant with improved whole-cell production performance, tVvTSM522K/13aa, which increased α-terpineol titer by 4.8-fold. Further enhancement of mevalonate pathway flux through enzyme fusion and mitochondrial compartmentalization increased the titer by 26.3-fold relative to the parental strain. Subsequent optimization of tolerance-associated genes expression, diploid construction, and fed-batch fermentation enabled the final strain WB11 to produce 81.78 mg/L α-terpineol in a 5-L bioreactor. This study provides an efficient combinatorial strategy for microbial production of natural terpene alcohols.Keywords: α-Terpineol, Terpenoid biosynthesis, Metabolic engineering, Enzyme engineering, Saccharomyces cerevisiae

Introduction

α-Terpineol is a valuable monoterpenoid widely used in the flavor, fragrance, pharmaceutical, and cosmetic industries because of its pleasant aroma and diverse bioactivities [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6]]. At present, α-terpineol is mainly obtained from plant extraction, which is constrained by seasonal variation, low recovery, and environmental concerns. Chemical synthesis is feasible but often requires toxic solvents and harsh reaction conditions. Therefore, microbial production has emerged as a promising alternative for the sustainable manufacture of α-terpineol and other “natural” terpenoids [7,8].

In microorganisms, α-terpineol is synthesized from geranyl diphosphate (GPP), which is derived from the native isoprenoid pathway [9]. In Saccharomyces cerevisiae, expression of a truncated α-terpineol synthase from Vitis vinifera (tVvTS), together with pathway engineering of the mevalonate pathway, enabled de novo α-terpineol production, but the reported titers remained low (3.32 mg/L in shake flasks and 21.88 mg/L in a 5-L bioreactor) [10]. These results indicate that microbial α-terpineol biosynthesis is still far from industrially relevant levels. Three major unresolved bottlenecks appear to limit this process: the low catalytic efficiency and poor heterologous performance of plant-derived VvTS/tVvTS in yeast, severe competition for the common precursor GPP with endogenous sterol biosynthesis, and the cytotoxicity of α-terpineol that impairs cell growth and production [11,12].

Plant-derived terpene synthases often exhibit low catalytic efficiency and poor performance in heterologous hosts [13,14], making enzyme optimization a key strategy for improving monoterpene production. For example, structure-guided protein engineering has been successfully applied to enhance the activities of geraniol synthase and borneol synthase, leading to markedly improved product titers in microbial hosts [15,16]. However, such strategies have not yet been applied to optimize VvTS for α-terpineol biosynthesis, and the key residues affecting its functional performance in yeast remain poorly understood. Therefore, engineering tVvTS is a critical step toward improving α-terpineol production and may also provide insight into its catalytic mechanism.

S. cerevisiae is an excellent host for terpenoid biosynthesis [7,11], but α-terpineol production is further constrained by insufficient effective GPP supply and product toxicity. In yeast, GPP is a transient intermediate that is readily converted into FPP for sterol biosynthesis, thereby limiting flux toward monoterpene formation. Previous studies have shown that redirecting metabolic flux, strengthening precursor pathways, and exploiting mitochondrial compartmentalization can substantially improve terpene production in yeast [17,18]. In addition, optimizing gene copy number and fermentation parameters has proven effective for further increasing product titers in engineered strains [19]. Although these strategies have shown clear benefits in other terpenoid systems, their combined application to α-terpineol biosynthesis has not been systematically explored. Meanwhile, the cytotoxicity of α-terpineol may compromise membrane integrity, inhibit cell growth, and reduce production robustness, indicating that improving host tolerance is also important for efficient biosynthesis.

Therefore, improving α-terpineol biosynthesis in S. cerevisiae requires a coordinated strategy that addresses three key bottlenecks: inefficient α-terpineol synthase, limited GPP availability caused by branch competition with endogenous FPP/sterol biosynthesis, and product toxicity. Here, we hypothesized that simultaneous optimization of tVvTS in vivo performance, precursor partitioning, and host tolerance would synergistically enhance α-terpineol production. To test this, we first engineered tVvTS through structure-guided mutagenesis and N-terminal truncation to improve apparent in vivo functional performance. We then increased carbon flux toward α-terpineol by combining enzyme fusion, mitochondrial compartmentalization, and gene copy-number optimization. Finally, we improved production robustness through efflux engineering, diploid construction, and fed-batch fermentation. Using this stepwise strategy, the final strain produced 81.78 mg/L α-terpineol in a 5-L bioreactor. This represents a 1635.6-fold increase over the initial unmodified strain. Overall, this study establishes an effective combinatorial engineering strategy for improving α-terpineol production in S. cerevisiae and may inform the production of other monoterpenoids.

Materials and methods

Strains and media

Strains and primers used in this study are listed in Table S3 and Table S4, respectively. Saccharomyces cerevisiae BY4741 and BY4742 were used as parental strains and maintained in our laboratory collection. The VvTS gene (GenBank accession no.: NM_001281287) from Vitis vinifera was chemically synthesized by GENEWIZ (Suzhou, China) with codon optimization for expression in S. cerevisiae. Escherichia coli DH-5α was used for plasmid construction and cultivated in LB medium (5 g/L yeast extract, 10 g/L tryptone, 10 g/L NaCl) supplemented with 100 mg/L ampicillin at 37 °C. Yeast extract-peptone-dextrose (YPD) medium (20 g/L peptone, 10 g/L yeast extract, 20 g/L glucose) and synthetic defined (SD) medium (20 g/L glucose, 6.7 g/L yeast nitrogen base with ammonium sulfate, 2 g/L synthetic complete amino acid dropout medium, plus 20 g/L agar powder) were used for the growth and selection of S. cerevisiae, with appropriate auxotrophic selection.

Homology modeling and molecular docking

As the crystal structure of VvTS has not been experimentally resolved, a three-dimensional (3D) model was generated via homology modeling using SWISS-MODEL (https://swissmodel.expasy.org/). The crystal structure of limonene synthase (PDB ID: 5ua0.1.A) was selected as the modeling template. The quality of the generated VvTS model was assessed using two key parameters: sequence identity (i.e., amino acid sequence similarity) and Global Model Quality Estimation (GMQE). Models are generally considered reliable when sequence identity exceeds 30%. The GMQE score ranges from 0 to 1, where higher values indicate greater model reliability. We also retrieved the 3D structure of the small-molecule ligand geranyl diphosphate (GPP) from the NCBI PubChem database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubchem/). Both the VvTS protein and GPP substrate were preprocessed with AutoDock software, involving water molecule removal, hydrogen atom addition, and charge assignment. We then performed molecular docking calculations using AutoDock Vina 1.1.2. Among the generated docking conformations, we selected the one with the lowest binding energy for subsequent analysis and visualized the docking results using PyMOL software.

Monoterpene production in shake-flask cultures

Engineered S. cerevisiae strains were first cultured in SD medium supplemented with appropriate nutritional deficiencies for 24 h. We then inoculated the culture into Erlenmeyer flasks containing 20 mL YPD medium and 2 mL isopropyl myristate. Cultures were incubated at 30 °C with shaking at 200 rpm for 5 days. IPM was selected as the organic overlay in this study because of its relatively low volatility and good biocompatibility during long-term cultivation. All shake-flask fermentations were performed in biological triplicate. Statistical analyses were performed using Student's t-test, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Localization of MLS-GFP by fluorescence microscopy

To evaluate the mitochondrial targeting ability of the mitochondrial localization signal (MLS) from subunit IV of yeast cytochrome c oxidase, we observed yeast cells co-expressing ATM1-mCherry (an ATP-binding cassette Fe/S cluster precursor transporter, used as a mitochondrial marker) and MLS-GFP via fluorescence microscopy. We performed epifluorescence microscopy using a confocal fluorescence microscope (Olympus FV1000).

Batch and fed-batch cultivations

Strain WB11 was used for batch and fed-batch fermentations in a 5-L bioreactor (Shanghai Baoxing Bio-Engineering, China) containing 2 L YPD medium. The fed-batch protocol was implemented as follows: The initial bioreactor medium was YPD with a carbon-to-nitrogen (C/N) ratio of 7:1 (i.e., 20 g/L tryptone, 10 g/L yeast extract, 62.03 g/L glucose). An activated single colony of strain WB11 was inoculated into 4 mL of YPD medium and incubated at 30 °C (200 rpm) for 16 h to generate the primary seed culture. We then transferred the primary seed culture to a 100 mL flask containing 20 mL YPD medium at a 10% (v/v) inoculum ratio and incubated it for 20 h to prepare a mature secondary seed culture. Next, the secondary seed culture was transferred to a 500 mL flask with 200 mL YPD medium (10% v/v inoculum ratio) and incubated for 20 h to yield a mature tertiary seed culture. The tertiary seed culture was inoculated into the 5-L bioreactor (containing 2 L medium) at 10% (v/v), resulting in an initial OD600 of 4.3. Fermentation parameters were controlled as follows: temperature, 30 °C; air flow rate, 3 vvm; dissolved oxygen (DO), maintained below 30% (with agitation speed coupled to DO); and pH, stabilized at ∼5.5. At the onset of fermentation (0 h), 15 mL of a metal ion-vitamin mixture was added. We supplemented 250 mL isopropyl myristate at both 12 h and 24 h of fermentation. Since glucose and ethanol were depleted by 24 h, glucose feeding was performed, with the feeding rate coupled to OD600. A total of 1 L glucose feed medium was added over the 168 h fermentation period.

α-Terpineol extraction, identification, and quantification

After fermentation of S. cerevisiae strains, 2 mL of the upper organic phase was transferred to a 2 mL Eppendorf (EP) tube and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min. The clear upper organic phase was carefully aspirated (to avoid impurities) into a clean 2 mL EP tube and recentrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 5 min. The organic phase was extracted using a 1 mL syringe and filtered through a 0.22 μm organic membrane. The clear filtrate was transferred to an HPLC vial insert and analyzed by gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) on the same day.

α-Terpineol detection was conducted on a Shimadzu GCMS-QP2010 Plus GC-MS system equipped with a DB-5MS capillary column (Agilent; 30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm). Detection conditions were set as follows: column oven temperature, 250 °C; injector temperature, 300 °C; carrier gas, helium (flow rate, 1.0 mL/min); split injection mode (injection volume, 1 μL; split ratio, 10:1). The GC temperature program was initiated at 80 °C, ramped at 20 °C/min to 280 °C, and held at 280 °C for 28 min. The MS scan range was 45–750 m/z. A calibration curve was constructed by preparing α-terpineol standard solutions with different concentration gradients, correlating α-terpineol concentration with peak area.

Results

Structure-guided engineering of tVvTS for enhanced α-terpineol production

We introduced tVvTS (N-terminal 28-amino-acid signal peptide removed) [10] into S. cerevisiae BY4742 to generate strain WB01. Fermentation of WB01 confirmed that tVvTS is functional in α-terpineol biosynthesis (Fig. S1). However, the initial titer was low, reaching only 0.05 mg/L (Fig. 1B). To overcome the low apparent in vivo performance of heterologous terpene synthases, a semi-rational design approach was employed. Due to the absence of a crystallographic structure for VvTS, we constructed a high-confidence homology model using SWISS-MODEL. The resulting model exhibited 55.91% sequence identity and a GMQE score of 0.82, suggesting high reliability. We preprocessed the tVvTS protein and GPP as the ligand using AutoDock software, then performed molecular docking with AutoDock Vina and visualized the best docking pose using PyMOL.

(B) α-Terpineol production of tVvTS alanine scanning mutants, targeting residues within 4 Å. (C) α-Terpineol production of tVvTS saturation mutants at six identified key positions: K90, Q127, P215, V288, F290, and M522. Key amino acid residues critical for enzyme function are typically located in or near the binding pocket [20,21]. We thus identified potential key residues as those within 4 Å of the tVvTS ligand-binding pocket. From the docking pose, we selected amino acid residues in the substrate binding site, binding pocket entrance, and other related regions for further characterization (Fig. 1A). The identified residues were Q81, Y86, K90, Q127, H128, L210, P215, V288, F290, Y294, S448, I451, I520, M522, and M527. We engineered tVvTS by mutating these residues. First, we performed alanine scanning to assess the role of these selected residues in the whole-cell production phenotype of tVvTS. Each residue was individually substituted with alanine, and the resulting mutant constructs were transformed into S. cerevisiae BY4742. Fermentation results are presented in Fig. 1B α-terpineol titer was used as a whole-cell functional output of the tested tVvTS variants under identical host and cultivation conditions, rather than a direct measure of intrinsic enzyme activity. The wild-type tVvTS served as a control. Alanine scanning results showed that 6 out of 15 mutants enhanced α-terpineol titer: K90A, Q127A, P215A, V288A, F290A, and M522A. Among the alanine-substitution variants, the F290A mutant exhibited the most dramatic enhancement, boosting α-terpineol titer by 120% compared to strain WB01 (Fig. 1B). Because all enzyme variants were evaluated in the same host background under identical cultivation conditions, the relative titers provide a comparative readout of their apparent in vivo functional performance, although they do not distinguish intrinsic catalytic effects from differences in expression, stability, or cellular context. These residues were thus identified as key positions for improving α-terpineol titer and were selected for a second round of saturation mutagenesis. We conducted random saturation mutagenesis at these 6 key positions. Among the resulting mutants, 6 additional variants further improved α-terpineol biosynthesis. The M522K mutant performed the best, achieving an α-terpineol titer 1.77-fold higher than that of the F290A mutant and 4.60-fold higher than that of strain WB01 (Fig. 1C). Structural inspection of the docking model suggests that residue M522 lies at the periphery of the active-site cavity near the pyrophosphate moiety of GPP. Substituting a neutral hydrophobic methionine with a positively charged lysine may increase local polarity and strengthen electrostatic interactions with the diphosphate group, which could be favorable for productive cyclization. Furthermore, the M522K substitution appears to alter the spatial relationship between residue 522 and the docked GPP molecule, which may influence substrate positioning and/or product release (Fig. S2).

Structural analysis based on the homology model revealed that the N-terminal region (the first 15 amino acids) exhibited low model confidence scores (high predicted local error), suggesting significant structural flexibility or disorder (Fig. 2A). In crystallography, such disorder often implies that these regions are not essential for the stability of the catalytic core. We hypothesized that this disordered N-terminal tail might destabilize the protein or sterically hinder substrate access in the yeast cytosol. Therefore, we designed progressive truncations (15aa, 13aa, 11aa, 9aa, and 7aa) to avoid impairing the whole-cell production performance of the full-length protein. Progressive N-terminal truncation revealed that removing 13 amino acids (tVvTS13aa) resulted in the best whole-cell production phenotype, achieving an α-terpineol titer of 0.18 mg/L, which is 3-fold higher than that of strain WB01 (Fig. 2B). However, no α-terpineol was detected upon truncation of 15 amino acids. Notably, truncation of 15 amino acids resulted in complete loss of detectable α-terpineol production, whereas truncation of 13 amino acids yielded the highest activity. This sharp boundary effect suggests that residues 13–15 likely reside at the structural interface between the flexible N-terminal tail and the catalytic core. The abrupt loss of detectable production upon excessive truncation suggests that these residues may be important for maintaining a productive structural context, rather than acting as merely flexible extensions.

(B) α-Terpineol production in N-terminal gradient-truncated strains of tVvTS. (C) α-Terpineol production in tVvTS combinatorial mutant strains. We then performed combinatorial mutagenesis by combining the two most effective variants (tVvTSM522K and tVvTS 13-amino-acid truncation) to construct strain WB02. This combinatorial mutant further increased the α-terpineol titer to 0.24 mg/L, representing a significant improvement over mutants generated by individual mutagenesis strategies. The titer was 4.8-fold higher than that of strain WB01 (Fig. 2C).

Strengthening precursor supply improves α-terpineol production

HMG-CoA reductase is a rate-limiting enzyme in terpenoid biosynthesis [22], and expressing its truncated variant (tHMG1) has been shown to improve terpenoid production [23]. To boost α-terpineol precursor supply, we expressed tHMG1 in yeast to redirect metabolic flux toward terpenoid synthesis (Fig. 3A). GPP is the direct precursor of α-terpineol and an intermediate in farnesyl pyrophosphate (FPP) synthesis catalyzed by Erg20. Erg20 has a higher affinity for GPP than tVvTSM522K/13aa, thereby limiting tVvTSM522K/13aa apparent in vivo performance. To mitigate Erg20-mediated GPP consumption and ensure sufficient precursor for monoterpenoid synthase, we engineered the Erg20F96W/N127W mutant (with reduced GPP affinity) [24,25]. Spatial proximity of consecutive enzymes in metabolic pathways facilitates substrate channeling and enhances flux efficiency [26,27]. We thus investigated whether fusing ERG20F96W/N127W with tVvTSM522K/13aa would improve α-terpineol production. The fusion strategy was designed to potentially promote more efficient intermediate transfer between the GPP-generating enzyme (ERG20F96W/N127W) and the GPP-consuming α-terpineol synthase, thereby reducing cytoplasmic diffusion and competitive consumption of GPP by endogenous pathways. Prior studies in S. cerevisiae have shown that fusion expression can, in some cases, outperform separate co-expression for monoterpenoid production. We fused ERG20F96W/N127W to the N-terminus and C-terminus of tVvTSM522K/13aa via linkers (GS)5, respectively. These fusion constructs were integrated into the multi-copy rDNA locus of the BY4742 genome to generate strains WB03 and WB04 (Fig. 3A). These strategies collectively improved α-terpineol production. Strain WB04 showed a more pronounced increase in α-terpineol titer, reaching 0.84 mg/L. This titer was 3.5-fold higher than that of the parental strain WB02 (Fig. 3B). Notably, this 3.5-fold titer improvement is consistent with a possible benefit of the fusion design, although the present study does not include a strict matched separate-expression control to directly resolve the underlying mechanism [10,28]. Structural analysis of the tVvTS homology model indicated that the N-terminus is located near the flexible lid region of the active site. Fusing ERG20F96W/N127W to the N-terminus (as in WB03) may impose significant steric hindrance on the active pocket, potentially interfering with substrate entry or product release. In contrast, C-terminal fusion in WB04 may reduce steric interference relative to N-terminal fusion and is therefore more compatible with productive function of tVvTS in vivo.

In addition to the fusion expression of key enzymes that enhances substrate channeling, reduces energy loss during substrate transport, and improves GPP utilization, increasing the levels of precursors IPP and DMAPP to enhance GPP supply is also crucial for α-terpineol biosynthesis [8]. The mitochondrial matrix offers a distinct advantage for terpene biosynthesis due to its high concentration of Acetyl-CoA and a high NADPH/NADP+ ratio, which favors the reduction steps in the MVA pathway [29]. Compared to cytosolic overexpression of the MVA pathway, mitochondrial compartmentalized overexpression of the MVA pathway exerts a more significant effect on IPP production [30]. To target MVA pathway genes to S. cerevisiae mitochondria, we used a 26-amino-acid N-terminal mitochondrial localization signal (MLS) from yeast cytochrome oxidase subunit IV (CoxIV) [30], which we fused to the N-terminus of each target gene. The subcellular targeting efficiency of the MLS was validated using fluorescence microscopy. We constructed strain WB05, which co-expressed the mitochondrial marker ATM1-mCherry and the MLS-GFP fusion protein. Colocalization of the fused GFP and mCherry signals confirmed that the MLS correctly targeted GFP to mitochondria (Fig. S3). We fused the MLS to seven MVA pathway enzymes and cloned these into two expression cassettes (Fig. 3C). We first integrated the X-1 locus-targeting cassette into WB04's genome to generate WB07. Using WB07 as the chassis, we then integrated the GDP1 locus-targeting cassette to create the mitochondrial compartmentalized strain WB08 (Fig. 3C and D). Fermentation assays confirmed that compartmentalization significantly increased α-terpineol titer in WB08: titers were 7.4-fold higher than in WB04 and 26.3-fold higher than in the parental strain WB02 (Fig. 3E).

Redirecting GPP flux toward α-terpineol biosynthesis

Endogenous Erg20 in yeast converts GPP to FPP, which limits α-terpineol production. To maximize α-terpineol production, we targeted deletion of the endogenous ERG20 gene. We integrated the ERG20F96W/N127W and tVvTSM522K/13aa fusion protein into the ERG20 locus of S. cerevisiae WB08, achieving endogenous ERG20 knockout and constructing strain WB09 (Fig. 4A). An alternative strategy was also developed: a mitochondrial-targeted ERG20F96W/N127W and tVvTSM522K/13aa fusion construct was used to knock out endogenous ERG20, generating strain WB10 (Fig. 4A). Fermentation results demonstrated that both WB09 and WB10 (with endogenous ERG20 knocked out) exhibited decreased α-terpineol titer and impaired cell growth (Fig. 4B). This was likely because reduced intracellular FPP synthesis lowered ergosterol levels (a downstream product of FPP) [31,32]. To mitigate this, we exogenously supplemented 20 mg/L ergosterol into the fermentation medium. While this significantly improved the growth of α-terpineol-producing strains, α-terpineol titer remained notably low (Fig. 4C). Insufficient α-terpineol synthase expression emerged as a potential bottleneck. We hypothesized that increasing its gene copy number to boost expression would redirect metabolic flux toward α-terpineol biosynthesis and improve titer.

We therefore optimized the copy number of the α-terpineol synthase mutant gene (tVvTSM522K/13aa) by integrating 1, 2, and 3 copies into the delta loci of strains WB08 and WB09 genomes, respectively (Fig. 4D). In strain WB08 (intact endogenous ERG20), increasing the copy number of tVvTS negatively impacted the titer. Conversely, in the ERG20-deficient strain WB09, the titer was positively correlated with the gene copy number (Fig. 4E). This suggests that in the absence of competition from endogenous Erg20, the abundance of synthase becomes the limiting factor for converting the accumulated GPP pool.

Enhancing tolerance and mitigating growth-production trade-offs via diploid engineering

α-Terpineol possesses potent antibacterial activity, and its intracellular accumulation can induce significant cytotoxicity even at relatively low titers [33]. To mitigate α-terpineol toxicity, we selected seven endogenous S. cerevisiae tolerance-associated genes for overexpression screening (Table S1). The candidates were obtained by combining NCBI/database annotations with literature survey. The selection rationale encompassed three protective dimensions: (1) canonical multidrug ABC transporters for extracellular efflux (Pdr5) and vacuolar transporter (Ycf1) of lipophilic xenobiotics [34,35]; (2) mitochondrial membrane transporters and structural components (Atm1, Mic12, Mmr1) to potentially alleviate local toxicity and membrane stress caused by our mitochondrial compartmentalization strategy [[36], [37], [38]]; and (3) putative stress-responsive clearance proteins (Mga2, Ups1) to evaluate the mitigation of terpene-induced proteotoxic stress [39,40]. These seven candidates were identified and integrated into the HO locus of strain WB02 (Table S1). Among the candidates, overexpression of the ABC transporter Pdr5 yielded the most significant improvement, increasing the α-terpineol titer by 1.22-fold compared to the control (Fig. 5A). Notably, compared to the parental strain WB02, all strains overexpressing the tested tolerance-related genes, particularly Pdr5, exhibited improved cell growth. This suggests that even at low production titers, the metabolic burden or inherent cytotoxicity of pathway intermediates can subtly impair cell fitness, and overexpression of tolerance-associated genes overexpression alleviates this stress, thereby promoting better growth. PDR5 is known to enhance β-carotene efflux and improve β-carotene tolerance in S. cerevisiae [41], and its overexpression may alleviate terpene-associated stress by reducing intracellular toxicity, either through direct export of α-terpineol or through broader detoxification-related effects, thereby enhancing strain robustness.

While efflux engineering partially alleviated product toxicity, the haploid strain lacking endogenous ERG20 (e.g., WB09-3) still exhibited severe growth retardation due to the depletion of FPP, an essential precursor for ergosterol biosynthesis (Fig. 4B). This presented a classic trade-off between cell growth and product synthesis. To reconcile this, we employed a diploid engineering strategy designed to balance the metabolic flux between the essential ergosterol pathway and the heterologous α-terpineol production pathway.

To construct these diploid strains, we utilized two distinct haploid backgrounds with well-defined genotypes (detailed in Table S3). Strain WY02 (carrying the validated PDR5 expression module and wild-type ERG20) is an engineered haploid that contains multiple copies of the optimized α-terpineol synthase fusion cassette and overexpresses the efflux pump Pdr5, but it retains the wild-type ERG20 gene. In contrast, strain WY03 shares the same multi-copy synthase and PDR5 overexpression features but has its endogenous ERG20 completely knocked out. These two strains were then used to create heterozygous diploids with different genetic configurations. Specifically, we mated WY02 (ERG20) with the ERG20-knockout haploid WB09-3 (which also carries the synthase fusion) to generate the diploid strain WB11. As a control, we also constructed strain WB12 by mating WY03 (ERG20Δ) with WB08 (ERG20-intact, without the multi-copy synthase integration).

As shown in Fig. 5B, both diploid strains (WB11 and WB12) exhibited superior growth and α-terpineol production compared to their haploid parents. Notably, WB11 achieved the highest titer (9.97 ± 0.86 mg/L), a nearly 200-fold increase relative to the initial strain WB01. This improvement probably resulted from the combined effects of reduced ERG20 dosage, increased synthase copy number, and Pdr5 expression. Because these variables were not independently deconvoluted in the current strain set, their individual contributions cannot be strictly assigned. The superior performance of WB11 over WB12 is consistent with the importance of the specific allelic combination, particularly the heterozygous ERG20 status together with high synthase copy number, in balancing growth and production.

Medium optimization and fed-batch fermentation boost α-terpineol titer

Culture medium components affect α-terpineol biosynthesis, and medium optimization is a routine approach to boosting biosynthetic titers [42]. Key parameters for optimization include adjusting the carbon-to-nitrogen (C/N) ratio and initial pH. Additionally, the α-terpineol synthase used in this study harbors magnesium ion-binding motifs, and the volume fraction of organic extractant also impacts α-terpineol titer. We thus optimized four parameters for WB11 (the highest-titer strain): C/N ratio, Mg2+ concentration, organic extractant volume fraction, and initial pH. A uniform design using DPS software was used to develop the medium optimization protocol for WB11 (Table S2). The experimental results demonstrated that a C/N ratio of 7:1, pH 6.4, combined with 10 mM Mg2+ and 25% (v/v) organic phase, yielded the maximum α-terpineol titer of 28.83 ± 2.94 mg/L (Fig. 6A). This condition provided an optimal balance between cell growth and terpene synthesis, serving as the basis for the subsequent fed-batch fermentation.

We conducted fed-batch fermentation of the highest-titer strain WB11 in a 5-L bioreactor. The initial medium was YPD adjusted to a C/N ratio of 7:1. Fed-batch fermentation data included cell density, glucose and ethanol concentrations, and WB11's α-terpineol titer over time (Fig. 6B). Maximum α-terpineol titer reached 81.78 ± 2.94 mg/L at 132 h of cultivation. Following fed-batch fermentation, WB11's α-terpineol titer was 1635.6-fold higher than that of the parental strain WB01.

Discussion

Synthetic biology has enabled the production of various plant-derived monoterpenoids in S. cerevisiae [18,43,44]; however, titers for α-terpineol have historically lagged behind those of other terpenes such as limonene and linalool [18,43]. This discrepancy is largely attributed to the complex cyclization mechanism of α-terpineol synthase and the strict regulation of the precursor GPP branch point [10]. In this study, we established a combinatorial engineering framework addressing three synergistic bottlenecks: catalytic inefficiency, precursor diversion, and cellular toxicity. The resulting strain, WB11, achieved a titer of 81.78 mg/L in a 5-L bioreactor, representing a 1635.6-fold improvement over the initial strain.

In nature, monoterpenoids are predominantly synthesized in plant plastids, and monoterpene synthases typically harbor a plastid-targeting transit peptide at their N-terminus [45]. Previous studies have demonstrated that truncation of the transit peptide of VvTS is indispensable for its catalytic activity in S. cerevisiae [10]. Building upon this prior truncation strategy, we further identified redundant sequences within the tVvTS. Subsequent truncation experiments on tVvTS revealed that removing an additional 13 N-terminal amino acids resulted in a two-fold increase in α-terpineol production, representing the best-performing truncation variant under the tested conditions. Notably, truncation of 15 amino acids resulted in an undetectable level of α-terpineol production. We hypothesize that residues 13-15 are located near a structurally important boundary between the flexible N-terminus and the catalytic core. Excessive truncation (i.e., 15 amino acids) is postulated to disrupt the conformational integrity of the catalytic core, thereby abolishing detectable whole-cell production [46]. This abrupt loss of activity suggests that residues 13–15 may be important for maintaining the structural integrity of the catalytic region. This observation may help guide future truncation design and structural analysis of related monoterpene synthases.

Although multiple beneficial single mutations were identified, extensive combinatorial mutagenesis was not pursued due to the exponential expansion of library size. The observed strong positive effect of M522K suggests that this substitution makes a major contribution to the improved production phenotype. Future work employing computational epistasis modeling may further refine combinatorial design. It should be noted that a limitation of this study is that enzyme performance was evaluated based on cellular production output rather than purified in vitro kinetics. Future work will benefit from purified-enzyme kinetics and expression/stability quantification to disentangle intrinsic catalytic effects from cellular context. However, while in vitro characterization (e.g., kcat and Km determination) would provide direct catalytic constants, the in vivo production phenotype integrates enzyme expression level, folding efficiency, substrate availability, and intracellular stability. Therefore, the observed improvements should be interpreted as improved whole-cell functional performance under the tested cellular conditions, rather than as a direct measurement of intrinsic catalytic constants.

Beyond enzyme engineering, the competition for the precursor GPP between the heterologous terpene synthase and the endogenous ergosterol pathway (via Erg20) represents the most significant metabolic constraint. Previous attempts to downregulate ERG20 often resulted in severe growth defects due to sterol deficiency. Our study resolved this “growth-production trade-off” by employing a novel diploid engineering strategy. By constructing a heterozygous diploid strain (ERG20/erg20Δ), we reduced the dosage of the competing FPP synthase. The resulting phenotype is consistent with a more favorable balance between sterol synthesis required for growth and GPP availability for α-terpineol formation. These results suggest that gene-dosage balancing may be a useful strategy when essential native functions compete with heterologous product formation.

To further maximize production capacity, we addressed the issue of product toxicity. Monoterpenoids like α-terpineol are potent membrane disruptors, and their accumulation can trigger stress responses that stall fermentation [33]. The overexpression of the efflux pump Pdr5 proved effective in enhancing strain tolerance, likely by actively exporting α-terpineol and reducing its intracellular concentration below toxic thresholds [47]. Efflux engineering may complement mitochondrial compartmentalization by reducing intracellular product-associated stress during fermentation. The observed growth improvement in efflux-engineered strains, even in a low-production background, highlights a critical but often overlooked aspect of metabolic engineering: the “metabolic health” of the chassis. Stress-related proteins like Pdr5 may contribute to this by not only exporting the target product but also clearing a broad range of endogenous toxic metabolites, thus enhancing overall cellular robustness and creating a more favorable environment for subsequent pathway engineering. Furthermore, although the 25% (v/v) IPM overlay improved product recovery, its effect on oxygen transfer and process economics should be considered during scale-up. Future optimization may reduce the required solvent fraction through improved strain robustness and alternative in situ recovery strategies.

However, the production of α-terpineol (81.78 mg/L) remains significantly lower than the gram-scale titers achieved for linear or simpler cyclic terpenes [18,28,43]. This production gap is likely intrinsic to the catalytic mechanism of α-terpineol synthase, which involves a complex carbocation cascade prone to premature quenching or side reactions [48]. Furthermore, the lack of a dedicated GPP synthase in yeast remains a bottleneck [49]. Future efforts should focus on three areas: (1) structure-guided evolution targeting the stabilization of the α-terpinyl cation intermediate to improve turnover rates or mining novel terpene synthases with higher specific activities from uncharacterized plant sources for α-terpineol synthesis in S. cerevisiae [12]; (2) computational design-enabled reprogramming of monoterpene synthase branch-point selectivity, as exemplified by recent structure- and QM/MM-guided engineering studies, to improve both product specificity and catalytic efficiency [50]; and (3) the implementation of dynamic regulatory circuits (e.g., metabolite-responsive biosensors) to autonomously decouple growth and production phases [17,24]. In conclusion, these results provide a basis for further optimization of yeast-based terpenoid production.

Conclusions

In this study, we performed semi-rational engineering of α-terpineol synthase and identified a tVvTS variant with an improved whole-cell production performance for α-terpineol production in S. cerevisiae. Building on this enzyme variant, we further improved production through fusion-protein design, mitochondrial compartmentalization, efflux-mediated tolerance engineering, diploid construction, and fermentation optimization. The final strain WB11 produced 28.83 mg/L α-terpineol in shake-flask culture and 81.78 mg/L in fed-batch fermentation in a 5-L bioreactor. These results provide a practical engineering strategy for improving monoterpenoid production in yeast.
