Repeated Eigenvalues Imply Nodes? A Problem of Planar Differential Equations
Kenzi Odani

Abstract
Poincaré gave a criterion which determines the shape of equilibrium for planar differential equations. In his statement, he excluded the case of repeated eigenvalues. In fact, in such a case, we can give a C 1 C^{1} counter-example to his assertion. In this note, we show that if we strengthen the condition to C 1 , α C^{1,\alpha} ( 0 < α < 1 0<\alpha<1 ), his assertion becomes true even in case of repeated eigenvalues.



1
Introduction.

Consider a planar differential equation below:

\displaystyle\dfrac{d\bm{x}}{dt}=\bm{f}(\bm{x}),\quad\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2},

(1)

where
\bm{f}
is a 2-dimensional function.
We call a point
\bm{p}
an
equilibrium
if
\bm{f}(\bm{p})=\bm{0}
.
We call an equilibrium
repelling (attracting)
if all
nearby trajectories are outgoing (incoming).
We call a repelling or an attracting equilibrium
\bm{p}

a
node (focus)
if the direction

\bm{d}(t)=\bm{f}\big(\bm{x}(t)\big)/|\bm{f}\big(\bm{x}(t)\big)|

converges to a constant vector (rotates infinite times)
as
\bm{x}(t)\to\bm{p}
.
We call an equilibrium a
saddle
if it has two outgoing and
two incoming trajectories, which appear alternately.
We give their phase portraits below.

repelling node

attracting node

saddle

repelling focus

attracting focus

The notions such as node, saddle and focus were introduced by
H. Poincaré
[
2
]
.
He gave a criterion which determines the shape of equilibrium.
Let
\bm{p}
be an equilibrium of Eq.(
1
),
and
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
the eigenvalues of Jacobian matrix.
He stated that when
\bm{f}
is a polynomial vector and

\lambda_{1}\not=\lambda_{2}
, the following assertions hold:

(i)

If
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
are real with the same sign,
then
\bm{p}
is a node.

(ii)

If
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
are real with opposite signs,
then
\bm{p}
is a saddle.

(iii)

If
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
are imaginary with
nonzero real part, then
\bm{p}
is a focus.

In case of linear systems, we can prove his statement
even when
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}
. The proof can be found in many
textbooks. See
[
1
, §20.4]
, for example.

In case of nonlinear systems, he did not provide any complete proofs.
However, we can prove his statement for nonlinear

C^{1}
systems.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose that Eq.(
1
) is of class
C^{1}
.
If
\lambda_{1}\not=\lambda_{2}
, then the assertions
(i), (ii) and (iii) hold.

The condition
\lambda_{1}\not=\lambda_{2}
is critical.
In fact, if it is dropped, the following counter-example appears.

Example
.

Consider a
C^{1}
nonlinear system below:

\displaystyle\dfrac{d}{dt}\begin{bmatrix}\ x\ \\
\ y\ \end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\ -1\!&\!0\ \\
\ \varepsilon\!&\!-1\ \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\ x\ \\
\ y\ \end{bmatrix}-\dfrac{2}{\ln(x^{2}+y^{2})}\begin{bmatrix}\,-y\,\\
\,x\,\end{bmatrix},

(2)

where
0\leqq\varepsilon<2
. Then the equilibrium
\bm{0}
is a focus.

The above example shows that if we drop the condition

\lambda_{1}\not=\lambda_{2}
, Theorem 1 becomes false.
However, such an example is very fragile.
If we slightly strengthen the
C^{1}
condition, Theorem 1
becomes true even when
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}
.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose that Eq.(
1
) is of class
C^{1,\alpha}
(
\alpha>0
).
Then the assertions (i), (ii) and (iii) hold.

Here, we say that the system (
1
) is of class
C^{1,\alpha}

if
\bm{f}
are of class
C^{1}
and their first partial derivatives
are
\alpha
-Hölder continuous.
The
C^{1,\alpha}
condition (
0<\alpha<1
) is stronger than

C^{1}
and weaker than
C^{2}
.

2
Proofs.

Regarding the proof of Theorem 1, the assertion (ii) is guaranteed
by Grobman-Hartman Theorem, which is found in
[
3
, §5]
,
for example.
Unfortunately, the theorem can not distiguish (i) and (iii) because
nodes and foci are topologically equivalent. See
[
1
, §22.6]
.
Instead, we can prove (i) and (iii) by diagonalizing Jacobian matrix
and by transforming it to a form of polar coordinates.
We can carry out the proof without any difficulties.
So we leave the proof to the reader.

In this section, we shall prove Example and Theorem 2.

Proof of Example.

We transform Eq.(
2
) to a form of polar coordinates:

\displaystyle r^{\prime}(t)=-r+\varepsilon r\cos\theta\sin\theta,\quad\theta^{\prime}(t)=\varepsilon\cos^{2}\theta-\dfrac{1}{\ln r},

(3)

By evaluating the first of Eq.(
3
), we have that

\displaystyle-k_{1}r\leqq r^{\prime}(t)\leqq-k_{2}r,\quad k_{1}:=1+\tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon,\ k_{2}:=1-\tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon.

Take
r_{0}=r(0)
so small that
r_{0}\in[0,1)
.
By integrating the above, we have that

\displaystyle r_{0}e^{-k_{1}t}\leqq r(t)\leqq r_{0}e^{-k_{2}t}<1.

(4)

Therefore,
r(t)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
.
Hence
\bm{0}
is attracting.

By taking logarithm of Eq.(
4
), we have that

\displaystyle-(k_{1}t-\ln r_{0})\leqq\ln r(t)<0.

(5)

By applying Eq.(
5
) to the second of Eq.(
3
),
we have that

\displaystyle\theta^{\prime}(t)\geqq-\dfrac{1}{\ln r(t)}\geqq\dfrac{1}{k_{1}t-\ln r_{0}}>0,

By integrating it, we have that

\displaystyle\theta(t)-\theta(0)\geqq\dfrac{1}{k_{1}}\big\{\ln(k_{1}t-\ln r_{0})-\ln(-\ln r_{0})\big\}.

Therefore,
\theta(t)\to\infty
as
t\to\infty
.
Hence
\bm{0}
is a focus.
∎

Proof of Theorem 2.

By translating
\bm{p}
to
\bm{0}
, we make Eq.(
1
)
the following form:

\displaystyle\dfrac{d}{dt}\begin{bmatrix}\ x\ \\
\ y\ \end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\ a_{11}\!&\!a_{12}\ \\
\ a_{21}\!&\!a_{22}\ \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\ x\ \\
\ y\ \end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}\ u(x,y)\ \\
\ v(x,y)\ \end{bmatrix},

(6)

where
u
,
v
are nonlinear terms.
Remark that the square matrix of Eq.(
6
) is Jacobian matrix
and denote it by
J
.

Consider only the case
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}
. This is because other
cases are already established in Theorem 1.
Put
\lambda=\lambda_{1}
, and assume that
\lambda<0
.
(It does not hurt the generality.)
We devide the proof into two cases.

1.
The case when
J
is diagonalizable.
In such a case, there is a matrix
P
such that

P^{-1}JP=\Big[\begin{matrix}\ \lambda\!&\!0\ \\[-1.00006pt]
\ 0\!&\!\lambda\ \end{matrix}\Big]
.
So we can transform Eq.(
6
) into the following form:

\displaystyle\dfrac{d}{dt}\begin{bmatrix}\ x\ \\
\ y\ \end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\ \lambda\!&\!0\ \\
\ 0\!&\!\lambda\ \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\ x\ \\
\ y\ \end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}\ p(x,y)\ \\
\ q(x,y)\ \end{bmatrix}.

Also, we transform it to a form of polar coordinates:

\displaystyle r^{\prime}(t)=\lambda r+r\varphi(r,\theta),\quad\theta^{\prime}(t)=\psi(r,\theta),

(7)

Since both
\varphi,\psi
are
\alpha
-Hölder continuous,
there are reals
c>0,m>0
such that

\displaystyle\big|\,\varphi(r,\theta)\,\big|<mr^{\alpha},\quad\big|\,\psi(r,\theta)\,\big|<mr^{\alpha}\quad\textrm{on }[0,c]\times\mathbb{R}.

(8)

We take a real
d
so small that
md^{\alpha}<\tfrac{1}{2}|\lambda|

and
d\leqq c
.
If we take
r(t)\in[0,d]
, then we evaluate the first of
Eq.(
7
) as follows:

\displaystyle r^{\prime}(t)<\big(\!-\!|\lambda|+\tfrac{1}{2}|\lambda|\,\big)r=-kr,\quad k:=\tfrac{1}{2}|\lambda|.

If we take
r_{0}=r(0)
so small that
r_{0}\in[0,d]
,
then we have that

\displaystyle r(t)<r_{0}e^{-kt}.

(9)

Therefore,
r(t)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
.
Hence
\bm{p}
is attracting.

By applying Eqs.(
8
), (
9
) to the second
of Eq.(
7
), we have that

\displaystyle\big|\,\theta^{\prime}(t)\,\big|<m\big(r_{0}e^{-kt}\big)^{\alpha}=mr_{0}^{\,\alpha}e^{-lt},\quad l:=\alpha k.

(10)

By integrating Eq.(
10
) from
0
to
t
(
0\leqq t
),
we have that

\displaystyle\big|\,\theta(t)-\theta(0)\,\big|\leqq-\dfrac{m}{l}r_{0}^{\,\alpha}\Big(e^{-lt}-1\Big)<\dfrac{m}{l}r_{0}^{\,\alpha}.

Since
\theta(t)
is bounded, there is a sequence
\{t_{n}\}
diverging to
\infty
such that

\theta(t_{n})
converges to a limit
\theta^{*}
.
By integrating Eq.(
10
) from
t
to
t_{n}
(
t\leqq t_{n}
),
we have that

\displaystyle\big|\,\theta(t_{n})-\theta(t)\,\big|\leqq-\dfrac{m}{l}r_{0}^{\,\alpha}\Big(e^{-lt_{n}}-e^{-lt}\Big)<\dfrac{m}{l}r_{0}^{\,\alpha}e^{-lt}.

By taking
n\to\infty
, we have that

\displaystyle\big|\,\theta^{*}-\theta(t)\,\big|\leqq\dfrac{m}{l}r_{0}^{\,\alpha}e^{-lt}.

Therefore,
\theta(t)
converges to
\theta^{*}
.
Hence
\bm{p}
is a node.

2.
The case when
J
is not diagonalizable.
In such a caes, there is a matrix
P
such that

P^{-1}JP=\Big[\begin{matrix}\ \lambda\!&\!0\ \\[-1.00006pt]
\ \varepsilon\!&\!\lambda\ \end{matrix}\Big]
(Jordan normal form).
Put
\varepsilon=-\lambda
.
So we can transform Eq.(
6
) into the following form:

\displaystyle\dfrac{d}{dt}\begin{bmatrix}\ x\ \\
\ y\ \end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\ \lambda\!&\!0\ \\
\ -\lambda\!&\!\lambda\ \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\ x\ \\
\ y\ \end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}\ p(x,y)\ \\
\ q(x,y)\ \end{bmatrix}.

Also, we transform it to a form of polar coordinates:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} r^{\prime}(t)&=\lambda r-\lambda r\sin\theta\cos\theta+r\varphi(r,\theta),\quad\theta^{\prime}(t)=-\lambda\cos^{2}\theta+\psi(r,\theta).\end{aligned}

(11)

Since both
\varphi,\psi
are
\alpha
-Hölder continuous,
there are reals
c>0,m>0
such that

\displaystyle\big|\,\varphi(r,\theta)\,\big|<mr^{\alpha},\quad\big|\,\psi(r,\theta)\,\big|<mr^{\alpha}\quad\textrm{on }[0,c]\times\mathbb{R}.

(12)

We take a real
d
so that
md^{\alpha}<\tfrac{1}{4}|\lambda|

and
d\leqq c
.
If we take
r(t)\in[0,d]
, then we evaluate the first of
Eq.(
11
) as follows:

\displaystyle r^{\prime}(t)\leqq\big(\!-\!|\lambda|+\tfrac{1}{2}|\lambda|+\tfrac{1}{4}|\lambda|\,\big)r=-kr,\quad k:=\tfrac{1}{4}|\lambda|.

If we take
r_{0}=r(0)
so small that
r_{0}\in[0,d]
,
then we have that

\displaystyle r(t)\leqq r_{0}e^{-kt}.

(13)

Therefore,
r(t)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
.
Hence
\bm{p}
is attracting.

By applying Eqs.(
12
), (
13
) to the second
of Eq.(
11
), we have that

\displaystyle a\cos^{2}\theta-be^{-lt}\leqq\theta^{\prime}(t)\leqq a\cos^{2}\theta+be^{-lt},\quad

(14)

\displaystyle a:=|\lambda|,\ b:=mr_{0}^{\,\alpha},\ l:=\alpha k.

Take a real
t_{0}\in[0,\infty)
so large that

e^{lt_{0}}>16ab/l^{2}
.

Firstly, consider the case when
h(t)<0
for every
t\in[t_{0},\infty)
,
where

\displaystyle h(t)=a\cos^{2}\theta(t)-be^{-lt}.

In this case, we have that
\cos\theta(t)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
.
Therefore, there is an integer
n
such that

\theta(t)\to(n+\tfrac{1}{2})\pi
as
t\to\infty
.
Hence
\bm{p}
is a node.

Secondly, consider
the case when
h(t_{1})\geqq 0
for some
t_{1}\in[t_{0},\infty)
.
In this case, we shall prove that
h(t)\geqq 0
for every

t\in[t_{1},\infty)
.
To prove it, assume that there is a real
t_{2}\in(t_{1},\infty)

satisfying
h(t_{2})<0
.
Take the largest
s\in[t_{1},t_{2})
satisfying
h(s)=0
.
This implies that
h(t)<0
on
(s,t_{2}]
.
By the definition of derivative, we have that

\displaystyle h^{\prime}(s)=\lim_{t\to s+0}\dfrac{h(t)-h(s)}{t-s}\leqq 0.

(15)

By differentiating
h(t)
, and by putting
t=s
, we have that

\displaystyle h^{\prime}(s)=-2a\cos\theta(s)\sin\theta(s)\,\theta^{\prime}(s)+lbe^{-ls}.

(16)

By putting
h(s)=0
to Eq.(
14
), we have that

\displaystyle 0\leqq\theta^{\prime}(s)\leqq a\cos^{2}\theta(s)+be^{-ls}.

(17)

By using Eqs.(
15
), (
16
), we have that

\displaystyle\cos\theta(s)\sin\theta(s)\,\theta^{\prime}(s)=\dfrac{1}{2a}\big\{lbe^{-ls}-h^{\prime}(s)\big\}>0.

(18)

By applying Eq.(
17
) to Eq.(
18
), we have that

\displaystyle\cos\theta(s)\sin\theta(s)>0,\quad\theta^{\prime}(s)>0.

(19)

By applying Eqs.(
17
), (
19
) to Eq.(
16
),
we have that

\displaystyle h^{\prime}(s)\geqq-2a\cos\theta(s)\sin\theta(s)\big(a\cos^{2}\theta(s)+be^{-ls}\big)+lbe^{-ls}.

(20)

Since
a\cos^{2}\theta(s)=be^{-ls}
, we have that

\displaystyle\hskip-20.00003pt\big(lbe^{-ls}\big)^{2}-\big\{2a\cos\theta(s)\sin\theta(s)\big(a\cos^{2}\theta(s)+be^{-ls}\big)\big\}^{2}

\displaystyle=\big(lbe^{-ls}\big)^{2}-4a^{2}\dfrac{be^{-ls}}{a}\Big(1-\dfrac{be^{-ls}}{a}\Big)\big(be^{-ls}+be^{-ls}\big)^{2}

\displaystyle=l^{2}b^{2}e^{-3ls}\big(e^{ls}-16ab/l^{2}\big)+16b^{4}e^{-4ls}.

Since
e^{ls}\geqq e^{lt_{0}}>16ab/l^{2}
,
we have that Eq.(
21
)
>0
.
By applying it to Eq.(
20
), we have that
h^{\prime}(s)>0
.
It contradicts to Eq.(
14
). Therefore, we have that
h(t)\geqq 0
for every
t\in[t_{1},\infty)
.

By using Eq.(
14
), we have that
\theta(t)
is monotone
increasing on
[t_{1},\infty)
.
Moreover,
\theta(t)
can not cross
(n+\tfrac{1}{2})\pi

for any integer
n
.
This is because if
\theta(t_{2})=(n+\tfrac{1}{2})\pi
for some real

, a contradiction.
Therefore,
\theta(t)
is bounded.
Since
\theta(t)
is monotone increasing and bounded, it converges
to a limit. Hence
\bm{p}
is a node.
∎
