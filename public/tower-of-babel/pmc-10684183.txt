Dietary vitamins modified the association of dietary iron with type 2 diabetes: the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, 2003–2014
Authors: Xiaoyan Wu, Jian Gao, Lin Zhu, Wenjie Wang, Yunlong Zhang, Jing Ni, Xuehong Zhang
Published: 2023

To the Editor: The epidemic of diabetes mellitus poses a major public health threat worldwide. In the United States, the prevalence of type 2 diabetes (T2D) affected 13.9% of adults and would continually increase.[1] Diet is one of the major lifestyle risk factors for T2D, in which dietary iron has generated substantial interest over the past 20 years.[2,3] It is to be noted that dietary vitamins A, B2, B6, E, C, and folic acid were reported to affect the absorption or oxidation of iron in body.[4] However, whether these dietary vitamins play a role in iron-related T2D remains unclear. Further research revealing the holistic role of these dietary vitamins in the association between iron and T2D is needed, which would provide important guidance for decreasing the risk of iron-related T2D. The present study aimed to examine the association between dietary iron and T2D in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) and evaluate whether holistic dietary vitamins affect this relationship.

The present investigation included six surveys (2003–2004, 2005–2006, 2007–2008, 2009–2010, 2011–2012, and 2013–2014), and were conducted among adults who were aged older than 18 years, were not pregnant among females, did not intake extreme total energy (<500 kcal/d or >4500 kcal/d), and did not have missing or unknown information on any dietary nutrient intake, body mass index (BMI), current drinking, current smoking, hypertension, and coronary heart disease. At last, a total of 30,904 subjects were analyzed [Supplementary Figure 1, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B832].

Mean values of dietary nutrients from the two non-consecutive days through 24-h dietary recall interviews were used in this analysis. Vitamin score was developed by summing the specific decile of dietary vitamins A, B2, B6, C, E, and folic acid together, which was ranged from 6 to 60, representing the lowest to highest holistic intake level for these vitamins.

T2D was defined as hemoglobin A1c ≥6.5%, and/or fasting glucose ≥7.0 mmol/L, and/or having diagnosed T2D.

All analyses incorporated sample weights to account for the complex survey design according to NHANES analytic guidelines. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression was applied to describe the relationship between dietary iron and T2D, and multivariable logistic regression models were employed to evaluate the association of dietary vitamin score (holistic intake of vitamin A, B2, B6, E, C, and folic acid) and iron with T2D, with the lowest quintile serving as the reference. A two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed by statistical software SPSS, version 28.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), except that RCS plots were performed in R project 3.0.3 (https://cran.r-project.org/package=plotRCS).

In this study, a total of 4625 T2D cases were ascertained. Characteristics of participants with or without T2D were described in Supplementary Table 1, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B832. Compared with participants without T2D, participants with T2D tended to be older, males, with hypertension and coronary heart disease, had greater BMI, fasting glucose, and hemoglobin A1c, but lower education level and annual household income.

After adjustment for potential confounders, dietary total and non-heme iron were related to the prevalence of T2D with a inverted U-shape [Figure 1]. Compared to the participants in the 1st quintile of iron, the odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals (ORs [95%CIs]) for participants in the 2nd, 3rd, 4th, and 5th quintiles were 1.07 (0.87–1.23), 1.34 (1.12–1.62), 1.27 (1.05–1.55), and 1.42 (1.22–1.75) for dietary total iron (P quadratic trend = 0.002), 1.11 (0.90–1.29), 1.27 (1.04–1.49), 1.35 (1.13–1.59), and 1.42 (1.22–1.72) for dietary non-heme iron (P quadratic trend = 0.004), respectively. No evident associations of dietary total heme iron or dietary heme iron derived from red meat with T2D was observed in the present study [Supplementary Table 2, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B832].

Associations of the energy-adjusted dietary (A) total iron, (B) non-heme iron, and (C) heme iron with prevalence of T2D by RCS in the US population. Solid line: Odds ratio; Rotted lines: 95% confidence intervals. CI: Confidence interval; RCS: Restricted cubic spline; T2D: Type 2 diabetes.

The association of dietary total and non-heme iron with T2D was affected by the holistic level of dietary vitamins [Supplementary Figure 2, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B832]. The significant inverted U-shaped relationships remained among participants in the 2nd or 4th quartile of vitamin score but disappeared among participants in the 1st or 3rd quartile of vitamin score [Supplementary Figure 3, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B832]. Among participants in the 2nd quartile of vitamin score, compared to the participants in the 1st quintile of iron, the ORs (95% CIs) for participants in the 2nd, 3rd, 4th, and 5th quintiles were 1.04 (0.78–1.40), 1.33 (0.95–1.87), 1.51 (1.12–2.04), and 1.34 (0.96–1.86) for dietary total iron (P quadratic trend = 0.017), 1.15 (0.83–1.59), 1.45 (1.06–1.98), 1.41 (1.04–1.90), and 1.47 (1.08–2.00) for dietary non-heme iron (P quadratic trend = 0.014), respectively. Among participants in the 4th quartile of vitamin score, compared to the participants in the 1st quintile of iron, the ORs (95% CIs) for participants in the 2nd, 3rd, 4th, and 5th quintiles were 0.79 (0.57–1.08), 1.01 (0.74–1.37), 1.41 (1.01–1.97), and 1.15 (0.81–1.63) for dietary total iron (P quadratic trend = 0.031), 0.96 (0.71–1.29), 1.08 (0.77–1.51), 1.54 (1.07–2.23), and 1.11 (0.76–1.63) for dietary non-heme iron (P quadratic trend = 0.014), respectively. No influence was observed in the association between dietary heme iron and T2D [Supplementary Table 3, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B832].

When the analysis was conducted in each dietary vitamin subgroup with dietary iron divided in their original quintiles and not based on dietary vitamin subgroups, all results were consistent with previous finding except that inverted U-shaped relationships turned to be only significant linear trend among participants in the 2nd quartile of vitamin score, but the value for P quadratic trend still approached statistically significant, P linear trend = 0.079 and P quadratic trend = 0.025 for dietary total iron, P linear trend = 0.008 and P quadratic trend = 0.014 for dietary non-heme iron, respectively [Supplementary Table 3, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B832]. Dietary supplement products were used in order to improve or maintain health. In addition, after excluding these participants reported iron supplement, both the inverted U-shaped relationship between iron and T2D and the influence of dietary vitamins in this association remained [Supplementary Table 4, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B832].

The energy-adjusted dietary total iron being 19.06 mg/day corresponded to the maximum OR of T2D risk through outputting the coordinates of each point on the predicted RCS [Supplementary Figure 4, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B832]. Based on this cut-off point, participants were classified into increasing and decreasing trend risk of T2D groups, respectively. Compared to the participants in the increasing trend risk of T2D group, participants in the decreasing trend risk of T2D group tended to have higher dietary intakes of fruit, vegetables, legumes, berries, soy products, grains, and citrus but lower intakes of red meat, poultry, eggs, and cheese. No statistically significant differences between these two groups were observed in seafood and yogurt. In addition, as presented in Supplementary Table 3, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B832, higher dietary vitamin A, B2, B6, E, C, and folic acid were observed among participants in the decreasing trend risk of the T2D group than those among participants in the increasing trend risk of T2D group.

The flow diagram of patient recruitment was shown in Supplemental Figure 4, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B832.

In the nationally representative samples of United States adults, an inverted U-shaped relationship between dietary total iron and the prevalence of T2D was observed, which was further modified by holistic dietary intake of vitamin A, B2, B6, E, C, and folic acid (indicated by vitamin score). Dietary non-heme iron, represented 97.8% of dietary total iron, contributed to this relationship, while heme iron even if from red meat sources showed no statistically significant association with T2D in the present study. The present results of the influence of holistic dietary vitamins level on the association between iron and T2D would provide important diet guidance on delaying the development of T2D.

The influence of holistic intake of dietary vitamin C, E, and folic acid on the association between iron and T2D might be determined by the property of vitamins enhancing iron absorption or preforming antioxidant action, and this might partly depend on vitamins intake level.[4] Lower vitamins intake level could increase iron absorption, possibly by preventing the formation of insoluble and unabsorbable iron compounds, and reduction of ferric to ferrous iron, and consequently increased the risk of T2D. On the contrary, higher vitamins intake level might be enough to exhibit their antioxidant action and overcome the pro-oxidant action of iron.

Nevertheless, this study was limited by its cross-sectional study design, which hindered making inferences regarding the causality between dietary iron and T2D. Despite the comprehensive adjustment for confounders in the present analyses, we could not exclude the possibility of residual confounding by dietary or other variables, for example, chromium played an important role in glyco-metabolism and affected the absorption of iron, but the data were not provided in the NHANES database.

In conclusion, the inverted U-shaped association of dietary total and non-heme iron with T2D was modified by holistic dietary vitamins intake of vitamin A, B2, B6, E, C, and folic acid. Adequate intake of these vitamins should be recommended to decrease the risk of iron-related T2D. Future studies, including cohort studies and clinical trials, are necessary to confirm the findings.
