Design and application of a magnetically recoverable citric acid-functionalized graphene oxide nanocomposite for ultrasound-assisted green synthesis of naphthopyrimidines
Authors: Raziyeh Molla-Mohammadi, Javad Safaei-Ghomi, Hourieh Sadat Oboudatian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study reports the synthesis of a novel acidic and magnetic nanocatalyst based on graphene oxide (GO), iron oxide (Fe₃O₄), and silica (SiO₂), functionalized with citric acid (CA), which was successfully synthesized and characterized by FT-IR, XRD, SEM, TEM, EDX, TGA, VSM, and Raman spectroscopy. The resulting nanocomposite (GO/Fe₃O₄@SiO₂@CA) exhibited a well-defined core–shell structure, high thermal stability, and superparamagnetic properties. This material was employed as a recyclable and green catalyst for the one-pot multicomponent synthesis of naphthopyranopyrimidines derivatives in ethanol under ultrasonic conditions. The catalyst showed excellent efficiency, product yields (88–97%) within remarkably short times (10–20 min), and was easily recoverable by an external magnet. Additionally, it was able to retain its catalytic activity over seven consecutive cycles with negligible loss in performance. These results highlight GO/Fe₃O₄@SiO₂@CA as a sustainable and high-performance nanocatalyst for green organic synthesis. In this study, we synthesized a mild, non-toxic, green, and multi-acid catalyst, and were able to increase the product formation efficiency and reduce the time with cavitation energy. Introduction

Nanocomposites have gained considerable attention in recent years due to their remarkable physicochemical properties, such as high surface area, tunable chemical functionality, and excellent stability. These features make them suitable for a wide range of applications, including catalysis, energy conversion, drug delivery, and environmental remediation1–3. Among nanostructured supports, graphene oxide (GO), a two-dimensional oxidized form of graphite, is particularly appealing because of its large surface area, strong mechanical properties, and abundant oxygenated functional groups (–OH, –COOH, and epoxy), which allow strong interactions with both organic and inorganic molecules4–9. The rich surface chemistry of GO provides multiple anchoring sites for metal and metal oxide nanoparticles through coordination and hydrogen bonding. Oxygen groups on GO act as nucleation centers for Fe₃O₄ nanoparticles, promoting uniform dispersion and preventing magnetic aggregation. Coating Fe₃O₄ with silica (SiO₂) forms a protective shell that enhances thermal and chemical stability while maintaining access to active sites10–14. Functionalization of the composite with citric acid (CA), a biocompatible organic acid, introduces additional –COOH and –OH groups, increasing polarity, hydrophilicity, and surface reactivity. The selection of citric acid was made based on the higher density of acidic sites, controlled acidity compared to strong acids Includes of sulfonic group and green chemistry considerations. Citric acid contains three carboxylic acid groups, through which multiple Brønsted acidic sites can be introduced onto the catalyst surface. As a result, enhanced surface acidity and possible cooperative catalytic interactions are provided compared to mono-functionalized systems. As citric acid is recognized as an inexpensive, biodegradable, non-toxic, and industrially abundant organic acid. Therefore, improved sustainability and better alignment with green chemistry principles are achieved through its incorporation.

The resulting nanocomposite, GO/Fe₃O₄@SiO₂@CA, combines the electronic conductivity and mechanical strength of GO, the magnetic responsiveness of Fe₃O₄, and the chemical stability of SiO₂, producing a multifunctional catalyst with high activity and recyclability15–20. GO also plays a central role as an electron mediator. Its two-dimensional π-conjugated structure enables rapid electron delocalization, enhancing charge transfer between active sites and reactants. Strong π–π stacking and hydrogen bonding interactions further allow efficient adsorption of aromatic molecules such as β-naphthol and aldehydes, improving catalytic efficiency even under mild ultrasonic irradiation21–27.

Multicomponent reactions (MCRs) have emerged as highly sustainable strategies for constructing complex heterocycles with high atom economy and minimal waste. Pyranopyrimidines and their fused derivatives, including naphthopyranopyrimidines, are bioactive heterocycles with diverse pharmacological properties, including anticancer, antimicrobial, and anti-inflammatory effects. Traditional synthetic methods often require long reaction times, toxic solvents, and non-recyclable catalysts28–36. In this study, we report the synthesis and characterization of a novel citric acid-functionalized magnetic nanocomposite, GO/Fe₃O₄@SiO₂@CA, prepared via a modified Hummers’ method. Its catalytic potential was evaluated in the green, one-pot multicomponent synthesis of naphthopyranopyrimidine derivatives under ultrasonic irradiation in ethanol. Efficient magnetic separation and good recyclability are exhibited by the prepared nanocomposite. Catalytic performance is maintained over multiple reaction cycles, further distinguishing the developed system from previously reported catalysts. The cooperative effects of GO, Fe₃O₄, SiO₂, and CA resulted in high catalytic activity, excellent yields, easy magnetic recovery, and remarkable structural stability, offering a sustainable and efficient approach for the synthesis of bioactive heterocycles. We also investigated the synergistic effect with ultrasonic. The combination of polycarboxylic acid functionality, magnetic graphene oxide support, and ultrasonic irradiation provides a synergistic catalytic system. Ultrasound-induced cavitation enhances mass transfer and accessibility of acidic sites, resulting in shorter reaction times and improved yields compared to conventional conditions. The use of ultrasonic irradiation significantly reduced reaction time while maintaining high yield and selectivity. Also, we managed to synthesize some new derivatives (six compounds) of naphthopyranopyrimidines.

Experimental section

Chemicals and methods

All materials, including solvents and reagents, met analytical-grade specifications and were applied directly in the procedures without any pre-treatment. Natural graphite powder, ferric chloride hexahydrate (FeCl₃·6 H₂O), ferrous sulfate heptahydrate (FeSO₄·7 H₂O), tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS), citric acid (CA), and ammonia solution were purchased from Merck and Sigma-Aldrich. Deionized water was employed throughout all experiments. Graphene oxide and all nanocomposites were characterized using FT-IR (Shimadzu 8400 s), ¹H and ¹³C NMR (DMSO), XRD (Philips PW1730), SEM and TEM (ZEISS), VSM (Meghnatis Daghigh Kavir), and TGA (STA504).

Preparation of graphene oxide (GO)

Graphene oxide (GO) was synthesized from natural graphite powder using a modified Hummers’ method, chosen for its simplicity, reproducibility, and ability to produce high-quality GO with abundant oxygen-containing functional groups. In a typical procedure, 2 g of natural graphite and 1 g of sodium nitrate (NaNO₃) were added to 46 mL of concentrated sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄, 98%) in an ice bath. Sulfuric acid provides a strong acidic medium that facilitates intercalation between graphite layers and controls the oxidation process. Sodium nitrate promotes layer separation, ensures uniform oxidation, prevents aggregation, and improves the overall quality of GO. Next, 6 g of potassium permanganate (KMnO₄) was added gradually under continuous stirring while keeping the temperature below 35 °C to avoid excessive heat generation. KMnO₄ acts as a strong oxidizing agent that converts graphite into graphene oxide and introduces oxygen-containing functional groups (–OH, –COOH, epoxy) onto the surface. The reaction mixture was stirred at this temperature for 2 h to ensure complete oxidation. Afterwards, 92 mL of deionized water was slowly added, and the reaction continued for 30 min. To further dilute the mixture and terminate the oxidation, 280 mL of deionized water was added, followed by 20 mL of hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂, 30 wt%).

The addition of H₂O₂ turned the suspension golden, indicating the removal of residual KMnO₄ and MnO₂. Hydrogen peroxide stops the oxidation reaction, removes remaining oxidants, stabilizes the GO suspension, and enhances surface oxygen functionalities. The resulting black solid was repeatedly washed with warm deionized water and ethanol to remove residual acids and impurities. The suspension was ultrasonicated for 45 min to ensure uniform dispersion of the GO sheets. Finally, the mixture was centrifuged, and the obtained product was dried in oven at 50 °C for 12 h, yielding high-quality graphene oxide powder. The entire procedure ensures the formation of GO with high surface area, abundant functional groups, and good dispersibility, suitable for subsequent nanocomposite synthesis in Scheme 137–42.

Preparation of GO/Fe₃O₄ nanocomposite

Fe₃O₄ nanoparticles were deposited onto GO sheets via a co-precipitation method. Briefly, GO (0.5 g) was dispersed in 100 mL of deionized water and sonicated for 30 min to form a homogenous suspension. FeCl₃·6 H₂O (2.7 g) and FeSO₄·7 H₂O (1.0 g) were added to the GO suspension under inert nitrogen conditions. Ammonia solution was added dropwise to adjust pH ~ 10. The mixture was stirred at 80 °C for 2 h, resulting in the formation of Fe₃O₄ nanoparticles on the GO sheets. The black product was magnetically separated, thoroughly washed with water and ethanol, and dried at 60 °C in Scheme 137,43.

Coating of GO/Fe₃O₄ with silica (SiO₂)

The synthesized GO/Fe₃O₄ composite was dispersed in ethanol–water mixture under ultrasonication to achieve a uniform suspension. Tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS, 1 mL) and NH₄OH were then added dropwise, and the reaction was allowed to proceed under continuous stirring at room temperature for 24 h. The GO/Fe₃O₄@SiO₂ nanocomposite was recovered by magnetic separation, followed by repeated washing and drying in Scheme 144–47.

Functionalization with citric acid (CA)

For surface functionalization, 0.5 g of GO/Fe₃O₄@SiO₂ was treated with 1 g of citric acid dissolved in 50 mL of water and stirred at 60 °C for 4 h. After the reaction, the GO/Fe₃O₄@SiO₂@CA nanocatalyst was isolated using an external magnet, repeatedly washed with ethanol and water, and dried under vacuum at 60 °C in Scheme 1.

General procedure for synthesis of naphthopyranopyrimidines

In a 10 mL ethanol solution, β-naphthol (1 mmol), benzaldehyde derivatives (1 mmol), barbituric acid (1 mmol), and 20 mg of GO/Fe₃O₄@SiO₂@CA were mixed and stirred at 60 °C. In some experiments, ultrasonic irradiation was used. Reaction progress was monitored via TLC. Upon completion, the catalyst was magnetically separated, washed, and reused for subsequent runs. The crude product was filtered, washed with cold ethanol, and recrystallized to obtain pure naphthopyranopyrimidine derivatives in Scheme 2.

Results and discussion

Structural and morphological characterization of GO/Fe₃O₄@SiO₂@CA nanocomposite

The structural characteristics and chemical functionalities of the synthesized nanocomposite were investigated using various analytical techniques. The FT-IR spectra of graphite and of GO-based nanocomposites (GO, GO/Fe₃O₄, GO/Fe₃O₄@SiO₂, GO/Fe₃O₄@SiO₂@CA) were acquired and shown in Fig. 1. In Fig. 1b, the broad absorption band at 3430 cm⁻¹ corresponds to O–H stretching vibrations. Distinct peaks at 1720 and 1625 cm⁻¹ are attributed to C = O and C = C bonds, respectively. A new peak observed at 580 cm⁻¹ indicates the Fe–O vibration (Fig. 1c), while characteristic Si–O–Si bands appear around 1100 cm⁻¹ (Fig. 1d). In the final catalyst, peaks near 2925 cm⁻¹ (sp³–CH) and 1750 cm⁻¹ (carboxylic acid C = O) confirm the effective functionalization with citric acid (Fig. 1e). The data obtained from FT-IR spectra indicated the correct deposition and formation of GO@Fe₃O₄@SiO₂@CA nanocomposite.

The powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) of (a) graphite, (b) GO, (c) GO@Fe₃O₄, and (d) GO@Fe₃O₄@SiO₂@CA displays in Fig. 2. XRD patterns showed a sharp peak at 2θ ≈ 27° for pristine graphite (Fig. 2a) and a broad peak at ≈ 14° after oxidation, confirming GO formation (Fig. 2b). Peaks at 2θ = 30.1°, 35.6°, 43.3°, 53.5°, 57.2°, and 62.8° correspond to Fe₃O₄ spinel structure (JCPDS No. 75–0449)(Fig. 2c). In the final pattern, a broad background around 20°–25° indicates amorphous groups of SiO₂ and citric acid (Fig. 2d). These results and the constant of Fe₃O₄ pattern in Fig. 2d, confirm the successful stepwise assembly of the nanocomposite (Fig. 2).

As it is obvious in Fig. 3b, the FE-SEM images demonstrated that spherical Fe₃O₄ nanoparticles, with an average size of about 63 nm, were uniformly distributed across the surface of GO sheets. TEM analysis further confirmed the presence of Fe₃O₄@SiO₂@CA at their interface with GO (Fig. 3c).

Besides, the presenting elements along with weight% can be clearly seen in the structure of graphene oxide and GO@Fe₃O₄@SiO₂@CA nanocomposite using energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) (Fig. 4c, d). Elemental mapping by EDX revealed a homogeneous distribution of carbon, oxygen, iron, and silicon elements throughout the catalyst structure.

The magnetic properties of GO@Fe₃O₄ and GO@Fe₃O₄@SiO₂@CA have been measured with the help of a vibrating sample magnetometer (VSM) (Fig. 5). Magnetization measurements revealed that GO/Fe₃O₄ had a saturation magnetization of ~ 60 emu/g, while GO/Fe₃O₄@SiO₂@CA showed ~ 40 emu/g due to surface modifications and silica shell. These results illustrate that the magnetization property decreases by coating and functionalization. Besides, the nanocomposite retained sufficient superparamagnetism for efficient magnetic separation (Fig. 5).

VSM magnetization curves of (a) GO@Fe₃O₄ and (b) GO@Fe₃O₄@SiO₂@CA nanocomposite.

Raman spectroscopy was employed to further confirm the structural features of GO and the final nanocomposite GO/Fe₃O₄@SiO₂@CA. The GO spectrum showed two major peaks: At ~ 1357 cm⁻¹, the D band appears, attributed to the A₁g breathing vibration of disordered sp³ carbons, whereas the G band at ~ 1594 cm⁻¹ corresponds to the E₂g mode of sp² carbon domains. The intensity ratio (I_D/I_G) was calculated to be ~ 1.02, indicating a moderate degree of disorder and oxidation in the GO structure. After the functionalization and coating steps, the Raman spectrum of GO/Fe₃O₄@SiO₂@CA maintained both D and G bands with minor shifts and broadening, suggesting successful integration without significant degradation of the GO framework. A broad peak at ~ 2650 cm⁻¹ (2D band) was also observed, which is characteristic of few-layered graphene oxide (Fig. 6).

Raman spectra of (a) GO and (b) GO@Fe₃O₄@SiO₂@CA nanocomposite.

The thermal behavior of the (a) GO@Fe₃O₄, (b) GO@Fe₃O₄@SiO₂ and (c) GO@Fe₃O₄@SiO₂@CA nanoparticles was estimated by TGA analysis (Fig. 7). Results depict appropriate thermal stability with no significant decline in weight. TGA curves revealed initial weight loss below 200 °C due to moisture and solvent removal. Further loss (~ 23%) between 200 and 600 °C is attributed to the decomposition of grafted citric acid and labile groups (Fig. 7c).

Catalytic performance in naphthopyranopyrimidine synthesis

The catalytic performance of GO/Fe₃O₄@SiO₂@CA was evaluated in the three-component reaction of β-naphthol, barbituric acid, and various aromatic aldehydes in ethanol under ultrasonic irradiation. The reaction conditions were mild (60 °C, 80 W), and the products were obtained in excellent yields (88–97%) within 10–20 min. The nanocatalyst enabled rapid C–C and C–N bond formation through a one-pot condensation pathway. Electron-donating and electron-withdrawing groups on the aldehydes did not significantly affect the yields, indicating the broad applicability of the catalyst. Turnover number (TON) and turnover frequency (TOF)

To evaluate the intrinsic catalytic efficiency of the prepared nanocomposite, the turnover number (TON) and turnover frequency (TOF) were calculated based on the amount of active acidic sites estimated from thermogravimetric analysis (TGA). TGA analysis indicated that approximately 23 wt% of the catalyst corresponds to immobilized citric acid. The calculation of active sites was performed assuming that each citric acid molecule provides three Brønsted acidic (–COOH) groups. Based on the molar mass of citric acid (192.12 g mol⁻¹), the number of moles of citric acid incorporated into the catalyst was estimated from the TGA weight loss, and the total acidic sites were determined accordingly. In a typical reaction, 25 mg of catalyst was used for the conversion of 1 mmol of barbituric acid (limiting reagent in the model reaction between β-naphthol, barbituric acid, and 4-chlorobenzaldehyde), affording 97% yield within 10 min under ultrasonic irradiation. Proposed reaction mechanism

The plausible mechanism for the synthesis of naphthopyranopyrimidines using β-naphthol, barbituric acid, and aromatic aldehydes in the presence of the acidic nanocatalyst GO/Fe₃O₄@SiO₂@CA is illustrated in Scheme 3. In the first step, the carbonyl group of the aldehyde is activated through protonation by the acidic sites of the nanocatalyst. The activated aldehyde undergoes a Knoevenagel-type condensation with barbituric acid to form intermediate (I). Subsequently, β-naphthol attacks the activated carbon of intermediate (I) via a Michael addition, generating intermediate (II). This intermediate then undergoes intramolecular cyclization, in which the hydroxyl group of β-naphthol attacks the carbonyl group of the barbituric acid moiety, affording a six-membered heterocyclic ring. Finally, dehydration of the intermediate produces the desired naphthopyranopyrimidine derivative under ultrasonic conditions (Scheme 3). A second mechanism has also been predicted in other articles. The first step involves the formation of intermediate which was formed by the nucleophilic attack of 2-naphthol to aldehyde and elimination of one H2O molecule. Adsorption of the aldehyde on the catalyst surface enhances the electrophilicity of the carbonyl group which is followed by the nucleophilic attack of 2-naphthol to them. Then, Michael addition of intermediate is formed with cyclic 1,3-dicarbonyl compound followed by cyclodehydration yield the desired final product50.

Catalyst recyclability

An essential feature of heterogeneous nanocatalysts is their structural stability and reusability over multiple cycles without significant loss in activity. To evaluate the reusability of the GO@Fe₃O₄@SiO₂@CA nanocatalyst, recycling experiments were conducted using the model reaction between β-naphthol, barbituric acid, and 4-chlorobenzaldehyde under the optimized ultrasonic conditions. After each cycle, the catalyst was magnetically separated, thoroughly washed with ethanol and water, and dried under vacuum before reuse. The yield of the reaction was measured after each run to assess catalytic efficiency. As shown in Fig. 9, the catalyst maintained high activity over seven consecutive cycles. As it can be viewed, the catalytic activity of nanocomposite declined from 97% in the fresh run to 89% after the completion of seven run.

Conclusion

In summary, we successfully developed a green, efficient, and magnetically recoverable nanocatalyst based on GO@Fe₃O₄@SiO₂ functionalized with citric acid. The nanocatalyst was thoroughly characterized using FT-IR, XRD, FE-SEM, TEM, EDX, TGA, VSM, and Raman spectroscopy, confirming its core–shell structure, functional groups, thermal stability, and magnetic behavior. The catalyst showed excellent performance in the one-pot ultrasound-assisted synthesis of naphthopyranopyrimidine derivatives under mild and environmentally friendly conditions. A wide range of products was obtained in high yields (88–97%) within short times (10–20 min), and the catalyst was efficiently recycled for at least five consecutive runs with minimal loss in activity. Compared to other catalytic systems, the GO@Fe₃O₄@SiO₂@CA nanocatalyst offered a superior combination of high efficiency, operational simplicity, recyclability, and green chemistry principles. These findings suggest that this system is a promising candidate for sustainable synthesis of biologically active heterocyclic compounds.
