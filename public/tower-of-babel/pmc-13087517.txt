Introduction

Pheochromocytomas and paragangliomas (PPGLs) are catecholamine‐producing neuroendocrine tumors arising from chromaffin cells. Tumors confined to the adrenal medulla are termed pheochromocytomas, whereas extra‐adrenal ones are called paragangliomas. The 2017 WHO classification considers all PPGLs potentially malignant, warranting appropriate treatment and long‐term follow‐up [1]. Because no reliable biological, molecular, or histological markers can predict metastatic potential, malignancy is defined by the presence of distant metastases in tissues normally devoid of chromaffin cells [1].

Recent studies estimate that 30%–40% of PPGLs have a hereditary basis, with > 20 causative genes having been identified thus far [2, 3]. Tumors that arise from germline variants are classified as hereditary pheochromocytoma‐paraganglioma syndrome (HPPS) [4]. Early‐onset PPGLs (in patients < 35 years of age), multifocality, bilaterality, or malignant behavior strongly suggest causes related to germline variants, even in the absence of known family history or other classical features [5]. Reflecting this high heritability, current guidelines recommend genetic testing for all patients with PPGLs [6]. Genetic information is crucial for diagnosis, surveillance, and prognostication. Herein, we report two PPGL cases in siblings, wherein the younger brother's diagnosis led to genetic evaluation and early detection in his older sister.

Case Reports

Case 1

An 11‐year‐old boy presented with abdominal pain. Contrast‐enhanced abdominal computed tomography (CT) revealed a retroperitoneal mass (Figure 1a). Physical examination revealed a blood pressure of 130/78 mmHg. Endocrine evaluation revealed elevated levels of plasma norepinephrine (7200 pg/mL), 24‐h urinary normetanephrine (3.6 mg/day), and urinary vanillylmandelic acid (VMA; 12.2 mg/day). Both 123I‐Meta‐iodobenzylguanidine (MIBG) scintigraphy and 18F‐Fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography‐CT (PET‐CT) showed uptake in the right adrenal gland, along the dorsal inferior vena cava, and near the splenic artery (Figure 1b–d), consistent with PPGL. The tumors were resected via midline laparotomy with both adrenal glands preserved. Histopathology confirmed pheochromocytoma and paraganglioma with a grading system for adrenal pheochromocytoma and paraganglioma (GAPP) score of 4 (moderately differentiated; Figure S1a,b). Multiple PPGL lesions, along with the early age at onset, raised suspicion of hereditary PPGL. As the patient was a minor, genetic counseling was provided to the parents, and genetic testing was subsequently performed with parental consent. Genetic testing was conducted using PCR‐direct sequencing. Germline testing using peripheral blood DNA revealed no pathogenic variants in SDHB, RET, or VHL. Subsequent analysis of DNA derived from both peripheral blood and tumor tissue identified a novel SDHD variant, c.74dupT (p.Val26GlyfsTer43) (NM_003002.4) in exon 2, codon 25 (Figure 2a), confirming HPPS. According to the guidelines [7], this variant fulfilled the criteria for Pathogenic Very Strong criterion 1 (PVS1) and athogenic Moderate criterion 2 (PM2). This variant was absent from public databases (e.g., ClinVar [8] and gnomAD [9]). Genetic counseling was offered, but the parents declined further testing.

The patient's HPPS diagnosis and possibility of multifocal recurrence prompted a surveillance strategy involving endocrinological and imaging evaluations every 6–12 months, with treatments guided by symptom development.

One year postoperatively, MRI revealed a recurrent lesion near the left adrenal gland. As the patient was asymptomatic with normal catecholamine levels, conservative follow‐up was chosen (Figure 3). The lesion gradually enlarged over the next seven years. At eight years postoperatively, 123I‐MIBG scintigraphy showed uptake in the aortocaval region, and cervical MRI detected a carotid body tumor. Elevated plasma norepinephrine and urinary normetanephrine levels prompted reoperation. The aortocaval mass was resected with minimal adhesions after pancreatic mobilization. Pathology confirmed paraganglioma with a GAPP score of 6 (moderately differentiated; Figure S1c–e). Catecholamine levels normalized postoperatively, and the carotid body tumors remain under surveillance for potential future intervention.

Case 2

The elder sister of case 1, a 17‐year‐old girl, presented with night sweats and exertional palpitations. HPPS was suspected, given her family history. Endocrine studies revealed elevated plasma norepinephrine (5100 pg/mL), 24‐h urinary normetanephrine (5.0 mg/day), and urinary VMA (13.2 mg/day). 123I‐MIBG scintigraphy and 18F‐FDG PET‐CT showed uptake in both adrenal glands and para‐aortic paraganglia, consistent with PPGL.

The patient underwent laparotomy with complete tumor resection. Histopathology confirmed pheochromocytoma and paraganglioma with GAPP scores of 3 (moderately differentiated; Figure S1f,g). SDHD sequencing from blood and tumor samples revealed the same c.74dupT insertion found in her brother (Figure 2b). Genetic counseling was provided, and both parents were tested. The variant was detected only in the father, confirming paternal HPPS inheritance (Figure 2c). The family pedigree is shown in Figure 4.

Discussion

The proportion of familial PPGLs is now recognized to be 30%–40% [2, 3]. SDHD variants account for ~9% of PPGL cases [10]. The SDHD c.74dupT insertion that was identified in this family represents a novel pathogenic variant. Carriers of SDHD variants frequently develop multifocal PPGLs (reported in up to 75% of cases), although most present as nonfunctional head and neck paragangliomas (HNPGLs). Metastasis occurs in < 10% of cases [11]. The overall prognosis for SDHD variant carriers is generally favorable, with no increased mortality [12]. The c.74dupT variant is a truncating variant, and previous reports have demonstrated that cases with truncating variants tend to present at a younger age and are more frequently associated with thoracoabdominal paragangliomas than those with missense [13]. This finding is consistent with our case, which developed at a young age and in the abdominal region.

Notably, both patients in the present report developed PPGL during adolescence. Current international consensus recommends genetic counseling and clinical surveillance for individuals carrying SDHD pathogenic variants, particularly paternal‐inherited SDHD variants (SDHD‐pi), with tumor screening starting around 10–15 years of age [14]. These cases highlight the importance of appropriate surveillance strategies for SDHD‐pi carriers.

Given the risks of recurrence and metastasis, lifelong surveillance is essential for patients with HPPS. Annual evaluations—including blood pressure measurement, clinical assessment, and biochemical testing—are also recommended for SDHD variant carriers [15]. Both siblings in this report underwent regular endocrine and imaging follow‐ups. In case 1, asymptomatic lesion growth during the patient's university years prompted the resection of functional retroperitoneal tumors before addressing the accompanying HNPGLs, reflecting the higher hormonal activity and clinical priority of pheochromocytomas [16].

HPPS caused by abnormal SDHD follows an autosomal‐dominant inheritance pattern with maternal imprinting, whereby the disease typically manifests if the variant is paternally inherited [14]. However, exceptions occur as well [17], with maternally inherited SDHD variants carrying an estimated 5% lifetime PPGL risk [18]. Paternally inherited variants lead to HNPGLs in > 97% of carriers, with a penetrance of 86% by 50 years of age [16]. PPGL transmission is paternal in 60%–80% of affected families [19].

SDH‐deficient paragangliomas frequently demonstrate increased 18F‐FDG uptake, reflecting enhanced glycolytic activity related to mitochondrial dysfunction. In this context, FDG‐PET is considered a useful complementary imaging modality to MIBG scintigraphy in the evaluation of SDHx‐related PPGLs [20].

We recently treated two siblings with HPPS caused by a novel SDHD variant. Clinicians should suspect HPPS and recommend genetic counseling in any PPGLs that present with early onset, multifocality, or other high‐risk features. Elucidating genotype–phenotype correlations is critical for optimizing surveillance and treatment strategies.
