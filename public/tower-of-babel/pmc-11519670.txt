Predictions of species distributions based only on models estimating future climate change are not reliable
Authors: Spyros Tsiftsis, Zuzana Štípková, Marcel Rejmánek, Pavel Kindlmann
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractChanges in climate and land use are the most often mentioned factors responsible for the current decline in species diversity. To reduce the effect of these factors, we need reliable predictions of future species distributions. This is usually done by utilizing species distribution models (SDMs) based on expected climate. Here we explore the accuracy of such projections: we use orchid (Orchidaceae) recordings and environmental (mainly climatic) data from the years 1901–1950 in SDMs to predict maps of potential species distributions in 1980–2014. This should enable us to compare the predictions of species distributions in 1980–2014, based on records of species distribution in the years 1901–1950, with real data in the 1980–2014 period. We found that the predictions of the SDMs often differ from reality in this experiment. The results clearly indicate that SDM predictions of future species distributions as a reaction to climate change must be treated with caution.Subject terms: Biodiversity, Ecological modelling, Macroecology

Introduction

Species are disappearing worldwide, and it is expected that this will get worse in the future1. The most important factors responsible for this are commonly assumed to be changes in climate and land use2,3, with land use often ranked as the most influential change4. Land use change - mainly associated with destruction of undisturbed or partially disturbed habitats - is understood to be driving force of the biodiversity decline5. It is assumed that broad-scale global land-cover transformation will increase species extinction rates soon6–9. Climate change is, however, often cited as one of the major driving forces1,10–16. To reduce the effect of climate change on the decline in biodiversity, its effects should be moderated by adopting proactive actions - “climate smart conservation”17. For this, we need to know, how climate change will affect species distributions and abundances in the future18,19.

The interactions between the negative effects of climate and land use changes are often adressed20–23, but attempts to integrate climate change and land use change in projections of future trends in biodiversity, are still in their rudimentary stage24,25. Nevertheless, it is becoming clear that the interaction between climate changes and habitat loss might be disastrous26. While it is sometimes believed that current land use change is more important than climate change in reduction of biodiversity8,27,28, it is expected that accelerated climate change will play an increasingly important role in the future14,29–31. In addition, we must admit that there are many other contributing factors: e.g., overexploitation, eutrophication, and the introduction of pathogens and herbivorous mammals32–35.

Future species distributions are usually presented on habitat suitability maps. These maps are generated by species distribution models (SDMs), whose inputs are locations of species in nature and climatic variables generated by climate models36–38, abiotic factors such as geological substrate and soil39,40, and in few cases also biotic factors such as magnet species or pollinator potential distributions41,42,42,43,]. The use of SDMs recently became very popular. According to the Web of Science, more than 6,000 papers using SDMs were published over the last 25 years and this number is rapidly increasing38. Many of these studies predict, how the distribution of species will be affected by climate change e.g42,44–46. However, it is impossible to verify the accuracy of such predictions, because they refer to scenarios 30, 50, or more years from now and include lots of uncertainties: (i) future climatic scenarios, (ii) adaptation capacity, (iii) speed of organisms’ migration to more favourable areas47–50, (iv) effect of the spatial scale of the analyses28, (v) extinction rates associated with increased temperature47, (vi) life history and population dynamics of the studied species29,48, etc.

Thus, it is worth questioning the reliability of these predictions. The answers are very important in practice: e.g., according to Araújo et al.51, it is estimated that 63% of the species of European concern will lose climatically suitable habitats in the Natura 2000 network by 2080. But are all the predicted shifts and losses of these species realistic? Santini et al.52 assessed the reliability of species distribution projections in climate change research. They conclude that our ability to predict future species distribution is low on average and they call for caution in the application and interpretation of SDM projections under different climates. This only underlines the necessity for determining the reliability of SDM predictions.

Here the accuracy and the uncertainties of future species distributions are explored by “going back in time” procedure: records of orchid species and environmental (mainly climatic) data from the years 1901–1950 and SDMs are used to predict maps of potential distributions of species in 1980–2014. This enables the predictions of species distributions in 1980–2014 based on records of species in the years 1901–1950 to be compared with actual data on species distributions for 1980–2014.

Inevitably, even the data for 1980–2014 may be inaccurate, because any records of species, even the most comprehensive ones, are only a subset of the real distribution of the species at the time of the study. Therefore, an additional comparison was made: the predictions of the species distribution for 1980–2014 based on the data from the years 1901–1950 were compared with the map of species distribution predicted by the SDMs for 1980–2014 based on species records from the same years (1980–2014).

We found that the predictions of the species distribution models often differ from reality in this “time-shifted” world. Thus, even if the analyses are carried out for only one plant family, the results are worrying and clearly indicate that SDM predictions of future species distributions of organisms as a reaction to climate change must be treated with caution, especially when considering conservation priorities and policies.

Materials and methods

Plant group selected

Orchids (family Orchidaceae) are especially suitable as a model group for this study, because orchid flora is very well recorded across Europe53, many orchids are close to extinction and/or their distribution is very restricted54–57. Therefore, predictions of their survival at their present sites, and/or of their possible spread to sites where orchids are likely to occur in the future, are vital for survival of these species.

The orchid data used as inputs for the SDMs originate from the Czech Republic, whose orchid flora is very well studied58. The dataset used here is a part of the database of the Nature Conservation Agency of the Czech Republic (NCA CR) and contains more than 100,000 records. To select orchid taxa for the SDM analyses, first all the duplicate presence records were deleted from the NCA CR database to reduce the effect of spatial autocorrelation: all records in one 1 × 1 km grid cell were considered as one. This way, a 1 × 1 km grid of presence/absence species records, was obtained. Then only orchid taxa with at least 50 such records in each of the two time periods, P1 and P2, were included: P1 covers the interval 1901–1950 and P2 1980–2014. This resulted in a set of 25 species.

Models used

We employed Maxent (ME), which is the most used algorithm for predictions of species distributions36,37,59,60. To avoid possible biases caused by using an inappropriate modelling method, an additional commonly used algorithm was also implemented: Random Forests (RF)36,61–63 and the results from ME and RF were compared.

Definitions and predictors selected

To avoid confusion, the three types of data used in the analyses were defined: (1) A record is an observation of the presence of a particular species at a site, defined by the species name, time of observation and X and Y coordinates of the site. However, in the analyses the site is not a point, but a 1 × 1 km grid cell. Orchid sites falling within the same 1 × 1 km grid cell l were considered as one record. (2) Environmental variable is a characteristic of the environment, which may affect the likelihood of the presence of the species studied at the site. These may be climatic variables (temperature, precipitation etc.), substrate characteristics etc. (3) Habitat suitability map is a three-dimensional function (independent variables are X and Y coordinates of the site, and time), whose values (dependent variables) indicate the likelihood (a number between 0 and 1) of the presence of the species studied at a site (grid cell) with coordinates and time defined by this function. It is viewed like a topographic map with the likelihood of species presence instead of altitude as its values. The habitat suitability map is estimated using SDM (ME or RF) with (1) and (2) as inputs and is usually referred to as a potential habitat suitability map (but referred to here as potential species distribution). Because the input values for the SDM analysis to estimate (3) are (1) and (2), the latter is also often referred to as a predictor.

The geological map of the Czech Republic and a set of climatic variables (generated based on the mean monthly maximum temperatures, mean monthly minimum temperatures, and monthly precipitations) were used as predictors, as these are used in most predictive models. The climatic data were obtained from (a) the EuMedClim database64(available for the period 1901–2014) and (b) from the CHELSA database65,66(available for the period 1901–2016). Their resolution was 30-sec (∼ 1 km2). Then the results obtained from the two databases were compared.

Both P1 and P2 cover more than 30 years and P2 begins 30 years after the end of P1, which are sufficient lengths of time to expect significant changes. Nineteen climatic variables were obtained by calculating the mean monthly climatic variables for P1 and P2 using two databases (EuMedClim and CHELSA), using the “biovars” function of “dismo” R package. To account for multicollinearity between these variables and avoid overfitting, Pearson’s correlation coefficients were calculated for all pairwise interactions. To eliminate highly correlated variables, only one was selected for any pair with a correlation coefficient r > |0.70|. This resulted in the choice of 6 climatic variables: Bio1 (annual mean temperature), Bio2 (mean diurnal range), Bio5 (max temperature in warmest month), Bio6 (min temperature in coldest month), Bio12 (annual precipitation), and Bio14 (precipitation in the driest month). The geological substrate, obtained from the geological map of the Czech Republic (based on the digital geological map 1:500,000 - GEOČR500 by Czech Geological Survey67) was added to this set of predictors. The geological map of the Czech Republic consists of 6 types of geological substrates, which were represented by an additional 6 variables, with values from 0 to 1 (absent-present).

Model parameterization

ME can be used when only presence-only distribution data are available, whereas RF can be used when either presence-absence or presence-only data are available36. When data are missing, the generation of background samples constitutes the best solution68. Thus, the default number of background samples (10,000 points) was used to characterize the environmental conditions in the area of interest69. Moreover, as the number of records included in P1 differs from that in P2, and thus the sampling effort in the two periods differed, a bias file for each period was created70. Bias files were created based on all the orchid records in each period using the geographic distance method. Specifically, orchid records for each period were used and the area for the random generation of background samples was set to be within a 5 km radius around the orchid records.

Based on the above-mentioned procedure, for each of the orchid species included in this study, for each period and for each method of modelling, ten models were run. At each run, species records were randomly divided into training and testing datasets using the 80:20 ratio. Each set of data (training, testing and background samples) was used to run ME and RF using the two sets of environmental predictors (CHELSA and EuMedClim climatic variables) (Fig. 1). ME was run using the default settings, as suggested by Phillips and Dudík69, whereas the number of trees in RF was set to 1000. However, it is known that extrapolation is a common problem of RF, as the model cannot make predictions outside the range of the training data. To overcome this problem, a regression model was used as suggested by Zhang et al.71 and then the RF constructed using the residuals of the regression. Thus, the generalized linear model (GLM), and then the residuals of the GLM were used as input in the RF. Finally, the average values of the ten replicates, separately for each modelling method, were used in further analyses.

Algorithm of model construction and creation of maps of potential distribution. The algorithm was run 10 times after randomly splitting species presence data (into training and testing datasets) and randomly selecting 10,000 background samples.

To convert the habitat suitability maps obtained into presence/absence (binary) maps, i.e., to transform the predictions of likelihood from the interval < 0;1 > of the species to be found in a grid cell to the presence/absence (0–1 binary prediction) for each grid cell, the Maximum Sensitivity plus Specificity (MaxSSS) threshold72was used for each orchid and period. This threshold was selected, as it provides better results than other thresholds independent of the data used (either presence/absence or presence-only data72. This was done for ME and RF separately.

Tests performed

First each of the SDMs was run separately, using records, geological substrate and the selected 6 climatic variables from P1, which yielded the potential habitat suitability map of the species considered in P1. Models were trained using data from P1 and then projected on to P2 by using the corresponding predictors from P2 (prediction of future). Then, these SDMs were run using records from P2, geological substrate and the selected 6 climatic variables from P2 to predict the potential habitat suitability map of the species considered in P2. In this way, for each modelling method and each species, one set of predictions was obtained of the potential habitat suitability maps for P1, and 2 sets of predictions of the potential habitat suitability maps for P2: (i) one based on orchid records from P1 and predictors (geological substrate and climatic variables) from P2 (prediction of future), and (ii) another based on orchid records and predictors, both from P2.

Simply stated, (i) what people are usually doing when predicting the future distributions of species (from P1 to P2 in our case, in which “time was shifted backwards”), while (ii) predicting distribution of species based on contemporary records and predictors (from P2 to P2). If (i) provides a good prediction, then the habitat suitability maps resulting from (i) and (ii) must strongly overlap. This was tested using the niche equivalency test that provide Schoener’s D and Hellinger distance I73. These statistics use habitat suitability scores and were widely used in the past e.g.74,75. D and I measure niche overlap using different calculations and their values range from 0 (no overlap between predictions of the two SDMs) to 1 (complete overlap). To calculate the niche overlap, the habitat suitability values below the mean MaxSSS threshold for each species were set to zero.

An addition, alternative assessment of the accuracy of SDM predictions was calculated using the percentage of orchid records from P2 that were in the grid cells of P2, determined as “presence cells” by the SDMs and MaxSSS that were obtained when orchid records of P1 were used. If this percentage is large, then the prediction of the future by the SDM is good.

The niches equivalency test was also used for testing how large a shift in the species distribution from P1 to P2 is predicted by each of the SDMs. If D and/or I calculated for the species potential distributions in P1 and P2 are small, then the species distributions changed greatly over time and vice versa.

All these calculations were done four times: separately for ME and RF and separately for CHELSA vs. EuMedClim (Fig. 1). The non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test was used to test whether differences between the corresponding D and I were statistically significant.

Figures 2 and 3 show the species predicted to move away from their initial distribution over time. However, the patterns obtained using the different models (ME and RF) and climate databases (CHELSA or EuMedClim) are not always the same. For the Maxent model using the CHELSA climatic variables (Fig. 2), the predictions into the future for 15 orchid species based on the data from P1 (green bars) and the predictions for P2 based on the data from P2 (red bars), showed similar trends. Ten species (Anacamptis morio, A. pyramidalis, Cephalanthera damasonium, Coeloglossum viride, Neottia ovata, Ophrys holoserica, Orchis militaris, Platanthera bifolia, P. chlorantha and Traunsteinera globosa) increased their distribution compared to the predictions based on P1 (green bars longer than blue bars), whereas for 9 species (Cephalanthera longifolia, Corallorhiza trifida, Dactylorhiza sambucina, Epipactis microphylla, E. purpurata, Neotinea ustulata, Neottia cordata, Orchis mascula and O. pallens) decreased over time (green bars shorter than blue bars). However, the observed trends (increasing or decreasing) were more pronounced in the predictions of the future for P2 (green bars) than the actual distribution recorded in P2 (red bars); the green bars are longer than the red bars. This means that the distributions predicted by the models for (P2) are much larger than those recorded in P2.

In general, the results of the RF using the CHELSA climatic variables were like those predicted by the ME model. However, one major difference is that RF predictions (green bars) of distributions are in general much larger than those generated by ME, as for certain species the whole area of the Czech Republic appears to be potentially suitable in terms of climate (e.g., Anacamptis morio, Neottia ovata, Platanthera bifolia).

When EuMedClim variables were included in the ME model (Fig. 3), 12 species had similar patterns in the future (green bars) and to that predicted for P2 (red bars) based on the data for P2 compared with the area of the Czech Republic that was potentially suitable in P1. Suitable areas for 5 species (Cephalanthera rubra, Corallorhiza trifida, Cypripedium calceolus, Epipactis purpurata and Orchis mascula) decreased, those for 7 others (Coeloglossum viride, Epipactis microphylla, Neotinea ustulata, Neottia ovata, Ophrys holoserica, Platanthera bifolia and Traunsteinera globosa) increased, and the remaining species showed opposite trends. The suitable area for Dactylorhiza sambucina decreased slightly when predicted for P2, whereas it increased slightly when its potential distribution was predicted for P2 using data for P2. The suitable areas for the remaining 12 orchid species increased when predicted for P2, whereas they decreased when their predicted potential distribution for P2 was obtained using data for P2.

As in the case of the CHELSA data (Fig. 3), RF showed similar patterns as ME, when EuMedClim variables were used. However, even in this case, the predictions of the future (green bars) revealed broader potential distributions than ME, but were more restricted compared to the distributions obtained when RF and the CHELSA data were used.

In general, species trends were similar when the models built with the CHELSA and the EuMedClim data, were compared, but not always. For example, ME predicted increases in distribution for a few species (e.g., Anacamptis morio, Platanthera chlorantha) between P1 and P2 (blue and red bars) when the CHELSA data were used, but a decrease when EuMedClim data were used. Although such differences appeared, they were not very common.

Discussion

As follows from our analyses, the distributions of orchids have changed significantly. The choice of the model and the climatic database used to build the model influenced the results. Based on the AUC values, all predictions are acceptable (AUC ≥ 0.75) and a high percentage of the P2 records are located in areas that were not occupied (Table 3) when data for P1 were predicted for P2. This demonstrates the rather low performance of the predictions of the “future”. Thus both SDM models failed in the predictions of habitat suitability for several dozens of orchid species in the Czech Republic in P2, based on orchid records from P1, no matter which climatic data from the period from P1 to P2 were used. The problem is evidently in the choice of the input data. First, there is a possibility that our results are not robust enough to be generalized to many (maybe all) regions and groups of organisms. If this is the case, then the only solution is to perform the predictions of species distributions for each region and group of organisms separately.

Second, the choice of environmental variables might have been wrong. Although it is expected that plants will tend to move towards areas with suitable environmental conditions76–81, the dispersal ability of their seeds is likely to determine whether they have the time to change their distribution82. For example, many species of trees are characterized by a low dispersal ability, and thus it is unlikely that forests can compensate for the erosion of their range by colonizing new areas83,84. In our case, the spread of forest orchids to higher altitudes is therefore determined by the slow upward movement of forests85,86. Although orchids have small seeds and thus are capable of long-distance dispersal87,88, the slow movement of trees will indirectly influence the future distribution of forest orchids (e.g., species of the genera Cephalanthera, Epipactis and Platanthera), which rarely occur outside forested areas89. E.g., Geppert et al.86, report that more than 50% of the orchids occurring in the Italian Alps have been unable to fully track climate change since 1990.

Third, there are many other predictors that may affect habitat suitability (and consequently species distribution) much more than climate change and therefore should not be neglected: e.g., topography, geology, vegetation47,98,99or biotic interactions42,100.

Kougioumoutzis et al.101, mention that most of the taxa analyzed are expected to continue to be considered as critically endangered under all land-cover/land-use scenarios, GCM/RCP and time-period combinations, while two are projected to become extinct in most future climate change scenarios. However, when they included land-cover/land-use data in the analyses, these negative effects were less pronounced101. In our case, land use might have been a much stronger driver of changes in habitat suitability for orchids, than climatic data. It is likely that the land cover changes that took place in the Czech Republic after the second world war, and more specifically after 1948, had a marked affect28, as they resulted in the abandonment of traditional management. Mosaics of grasslands and small fields were converted into huge fields and the application of artificial fertilizers increased dramatically, both of which resulted in the loss of natural habitats102,103 and a decline in many orchid species28. Although for most species studied here the number of records during P2 is considerably larger than the number recorded during P1, their spatial distribution is restricted to the remaining subset of the natural habitats that existed in the Czech Republic during P1.

In conclusion: from our study it follows that for predictions of species distribution in the future it is not sufficient to use only climatic models as environmental drivers. For each species and region considered, one must carefully choose all potential environmental variables and test the importance of all of them as factors responsible for species distribution. This may be a very difficult task, but using only easily available variables may lead to misleading predictions.
