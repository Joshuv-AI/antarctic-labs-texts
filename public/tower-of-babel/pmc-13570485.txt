A Case of Lentigo Maligna Presenting as Repigmentation of Hair in a Scalp That Previously Underwent Radiotherapy for Tinea Capitis
Authors: Madeleine Louise Kelly, Ken Low, Robert M Miller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLentigo maligna has occasionally been noted to have the capacity to invade and repigment grey hairs. We present a case of a 78‐year‐old male who presented with a patch of brown hair on the vertex of the scalp in an area of previously white hair. The patient had previous radiotherapy to the site as a child for the treatment of tinea capitis. Initial histology revealed a lentigo maligna in situ subtype and the patient underwent surgical excision with split thickness skin graft. An invasive component was identified on histological examination of the entire lesion. This case highlights not only the rare entity of hair repigmentation as a sign of malignancy but also highlights past scalp radiotherapy as a possible risk factor in the development of melanoma.Keywords: lentigo maligna, scalp irradiation, tinea capitis

Introduction

Lentigo maligna has occasionally been noted to have the capacity to cause repigmentation of pale hair [1]. One of the major risk factors for melanoma in Australia and specifically for lentigo maligna is sun exposure. It is important to also consider other associated risk factors such as past radiotherapy [1, 2].

Case

A 78‐year‐old male presented with a slowly darkening patch of previously white hair to light brown on the vertex of the scalp. He had marked actinic skin damage and multiple previous non‐melanoma skin cancers. An in‐situ melanoma was surgically removed in 2020 from the left back. Other medical history hypertension was well controlled with Irbesartan 300 mg/hydrochlorothiazide 25 mg for a decade. He had radiotherapy to the scalp at age 8 years for tinea capitis. It is unknown the dose the patient personally received but most protocols from the time suggested a range of 300 to 400 Roentgens (R) which is equivalent to 2.6 to 3.5 Grey [3].

On physical examination, there was a 70 × 90 mm area of tan pigment with 5 spots of darker pigment within, on the vertex of the scalp (Figure 1). The hair arising from this patch showed light brown pigmentation in contrast to his otherwise white hair. Excisional biopsy of two of the darker lesions was performed. Histology of both lesions exhibited solar elastosis, junctional melanocytic proliferation of single cells/small nests with atypia and architectural asymmetry. Atypical cells involved the hair follicular epithelium. SOX10/PRAME immunohistochemistry was positive, and the overall picture was diagnostic of melanoma in situ of lentigo maligna subtype (Figure 2a,b).

(a) SOX10 staining highlights adnexal structure (hair follicle) involvement as well as overlying junctional melanocytic component (with some Confluence). (b) PRAME IHC showing the adnexal extension.

The patient underwent a wide local excision with a split thickness skin graft of the vertex of the scalp. Histology showed predominantly lentigo maligna type melanoma in situ with high and low‐density areas and marked adnexal colonisation including extension into the germative portion of the hair follicle (Figure 3). There were tiny dermal nests of melanocytes that had undergone partial regression compatible with an invasive component limited to the papillary dermis (Clark level 2, Breslow Thickness 0.25 mm, Figure 4). Regional lymph nodes were not palpable and on follow‐up to date there has been no local or regional recurrence.

Discussion

One feature that should raise suspicion for melanoma on the scalp is the rare phenomenon of repigmentation of previously white or fair hair. This phenomenon is the reverse of poliosis.

Prominent melanocyte hyperplasia in the hair bulb occurring within an in situ or invasive melanoma, as present in this case, is a rare entity [4, 5]. It has been described in only a handful of cases. Most of the published case reports describe hair repigmentation in the setting of scalp melanoma without melanocytic invasion into the hair follicles [4, 5]. Both our case and a case by Inzinger et al. reported invasion of the hair follicles on histology [4]. It has been hypothesised in other cases where there is a lack of follicular bulb invasion that the repigmentation may have occurred by paracrine secretion of melanocyte activating factors by the surrounding melanoma [4, 5, 6].

This patient has been taking hydrochlorothiazide as part of his hypertension treatment for many years. It is uncertain whether the photosensitising properties of this drug have played a role in this patient developing lentigo maligna of the scalp.

X‐rays were discovered in 1895 and used for the treatment of many dermatological ailments including tinea capitis in the 1930s to 1940s [7, 8]. The patient in this case had radiotherapy for the treatment of tinea capitis at age 8, and with the increasing age of this irradiated population, it is important to ask patients if they have a history of radiation treatment and to monitor for skin malignancies. Approximately 10% of patients who received irradiation for benign disease develop non‐melanoma skin malignancy [6, 7, 8, 9, 10]. It's even rarer for melanoma to occur, with very few cases reported after tinea capitis irradiation treatment [8]. We are not suggesting a definite causal relationship in this case, but we consider it a possible contributing factor. Clinicians should be suspicious of the possibility of melanoma when previously white or grey hair repigments. We should also remember to consider past radiation for benign conditions as a possible risk factor for non‐melanoma and melanoma skin cancer.
