Fragmented QRS duration as a marker of cardiac and hepatic iron overload: across-sectional study
Authors: Seyyed Bozorgmehr Hedayati, Soodeh Hooshmandi, Raheb Ghorbani, Mohammad Kamali, Mohammad Hashem Arabkhani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThalassemia is a group of hematological disorders brought about by defective globin chains synthesis, which can indirectly culminate in hepatic and myocardial cellular damages through iron deposition. The purpose of this study is to examine the relationship between fragmented QRS duration and liver T2* value. This cross-sectional study was implemented on 36 beta thalassemia major patients in 2024, in Semnan, Iran. The relevant information, including serum ferritin and hemoglobin levels, and T2* values of heart and liver, along with fQRS durations of patients, saved in hospital records, was statistically analyzed using IBM ™ SPSS Statistics application (version 27.0), pursuing a relationship between fQRS duration and liver T2* value. The patients included 23 females and 13 males of Persian ethnicity, holding a mean age of 31.36 ± 7.93 years. Both of hepatic and cardiac T2* values were significantly associated with fQRS duration via a negative linear relationship, with the hepatic T2* value possessing smaller correlation coefficient. In addition, serum ferritin level demonstrated a positive correlation with heart and liver T2* values, as well as fQRS duration. fQRS duration can be used as a non-invasive and convenient marker in patients suffering from beta thalassemia major to assess cardiac and hepatic involvement due to ongoing iron deposition.Keywords: Thalassemia, Iron overload, Magnetic resonance imaging, Electrocardiography, FerritinSubject terms: Biomarkers, Cardiology, Diseases, Medical research

Introduction

Thalassemia refers to hematological disorders characterized by a decline in or absence of production of the globin chains. These inherited disorders are categorized into two major groups, depending on the type of globin chain affected: alpha- and beta thalassemia. Beta thalassemia major (β-TM) is a severe variant of thalassemia resulting in the complete absence or a significant reduction in beta-globin chain synthesis1. Ineffective erythropoiesis and the resultant anemia, along with iron overload, are among the leading causes of morbidity and mortality in these patients2.

In iron overload, the oxidative stress and inflammation brought on by the deposited excessive iron in the myocardium lead to cellular damage and fibrosis3. Interestingly, myocardial scar can manifest as fragmented QRS (fQRS) complexes on the 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG)4. Moreover, T2*-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) offers a non-invasive method for quantifying the iron deposited in a tissue. Given that excess iron in organs causes local magnetic field disturbances, which shorten MRI relaxation times (T2 and T2*), the resulting reduction in signal intensity is leveraged to detect iron overload. From a clinical perspective, a cardiac T2* value < 20 milliseconds (ms) denotes a considerable cardiac iron overload which is associated with an abating left ventricular function5. Quantitative T2* MRI also serves as the best non-invasive technique to evaluate iron deposition in the liver6. The deposited iron in the liver cells can result in liver fibrosis and cirrhosis, and importantly, can aggravate the pre-existing liver disorders7.

Buyukkaya and colleagues conducted a study on 96 subjects, of which 66 were β-TM patients (mean age: 23 ± 6 years) and 30 were healthy controls (mean age: 23 ± 4 years), to evaluate the prevalence of fQRS in β-TM patients and to investigate the relationship between the frequency of fQRS and Doppler-derived indices. The study unveiled that fQRS had a significantly higher prevalence in β-TM patients. Also, ferritin level tended to show a statistically meaningful increment in β-TM patients developing fQRS. Consequently, the authors concluded that fQRS may serve as a noninvasive marker for myocardial scarring in those afflicted by β-TM8.

Bayar et al. accomplished a study on 103 patients with β-TM with the purpose of examining the importance of fQRS as well as its relation to the myocardial T2* value. The subjects’ median age was 22.6 ± 6.6 years. The results were indicative of a significant association between fQRS and cardiac T2* value. What is more, no difference was observed in left ventricular ejection fraction of patients with and without fQRS. The study deduced that the presence of fQRS offers a high predictive value for involvement of the heart by iron overload9.

Another cross-sectional study by Alizadeh Sani and colleagues on 71 β-TM patients aged 19.84 ± 7.54 years revealed a statistically meaningful association between cardiac T2* value and fQRS. The study inferred that a considerable proportion of β-TM patients present fQRS in ECGs who also have a shortened myocardial T2* value. In addition, the liver and myocardial T2* values were significantly associated in patients presenting fQRS10.

A cross-sectional study by Ghandi et al. was carried out on 40 patients (mean age 22.50 ± 6.75 years) suffering from β-TM in order to investigate the relation between cardiac iron overload and fQRS. The study disclosed that there was a significant association between cardiac iron overload and fQRS. Interestingly, univariate analysis showed that subjects with fQRS had five times higher odds of cardiac iron overload. Furthermore, patients with fQRS were remarkably older11.

Soltanpour and Davari accomplished a cross-sectional study on 60 patients with average age of 17.5 ± 9.1. A robust inverse correlation was found between ferritin level and hepatic T2*-weighted MRI values; however, a weak correlation existed between the former and the cardiac T2*-weighted MRI values12.

A study by Suthar et al. was undertaken on 73 β-TM children with the age of 9.06 ± 4.68 years with the aim of discovering the association between heart and liver T2* value and serum ferritin level. The study showed a negative correlation between serum ferritin level and liver T2* values. Conversely, serum ferritin was not significantly correlated with heart T2* value13.

We conducted the current study in order to assess the relationship between fQRS duration and liver T2* value, as an indirect measurement of liver iron concentration.

Method

This cross-sectional study was conducted in Semnan Amir Al-Momenin Hospital in 2024 as a part of a thesis project. The study was intended to include all eligible β-TM patients hospitalized in the thalassemia ward of the hospital during the solar year 1403 (March 2024 to March 2025). Accordingly, 36 β-TM cases participated in the study.

The inclusion criteria consisted of holding a diagnosis of β-TM, being otherwise healthy, being 1 year of age or older, presenting a fQRS pattern in surface ECG, undergoing liver and heart T2*-weighted MRI during the hospitalization course and currently receiving iron chelation therapy. The exclusion criteria included the presence of liver disease or heart disease, a history of splenectomy, or any uncertainty in the medical records. Participants were enrolled according to the exclusion and inclusion criteria and underwent screening via reviewing medical records (documents).

The required data, including the demographic data (patients’ ages and genders), the administered iron-chelating drug, levels of serum hemoglobin and ferritin, ECG records and T2*-weighted MRI of heart and liver, was gleaned from patients’ records which were saved in the hospital.

The ECG records were visually assessed by two physicians to detect fQRS based on the following criteria:

Narrow QRS complex (duration < 120 ms) must exhibit one of the various RSR’ patterns, like an extra R wave (R’) in the S wave’s nadir, notched R wave, or more than one R’. This pattern must involve no fewer than two contiguous leads14.

We categorized the severity of cardiac iron overload based on the measured cardiac T2* values: mild (15 ms < T2* value < 20 ms), moderate (10 ms < T2* value < 15 ms), and severe (T2* value < 10 ms). Furthermore, T2* value beyond 20 ms was deemed indicative of insignificant cardiac overload15. Additionally, the severity of hepatic iron overload was also stratified regarding the measured liver T2* values: normal (T2* value > 16 ms), borderline mild (8 ms < T2* value < 16 ms), mild (4 ms < T2* value < 8 ms), moderate (2 ms < T2* value < 4 ms), and severe (T2* value < 2 ms)16.

Both hepatic and cardiac T2*-weighted MRI were performed leveraging a 1.5-Tesla Philips MRI. In addition, serum ferritin levels were measured employing human ELISA (enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay) kits produced by Pishtaz Teb Zaman Diagnostics company, Iran. The changes in color were captured (at 450-nanometer wavelength) with the 2100 Stat Fax ELISA microplate reader produced by Awareness Technology Inc., USA. We also leveraged the Sysmex XP-300 automated differential hematology analyzer to measure serum hemoglobin levels.

Statistical analyses were performed employing the IBM ™ SPSS Statistics application (version 27.0). The qualitative variables were expressed with frequency and percentages, whereas the quantitative ones were reported as mean and standard deviation. The Shapiro–Wilk test indicated that although ages, serum hemoglobin levels, and heart T2* values follow normal distribution, fQRS durations, hepatic T2* values, and serum ferritin levels are not normally distributed. Due to the small number of patients in groups with varying degrees of cardiac or hepatic iron overload, we conducted the Kruskal–Wallis test to compare the measured variables—T2* values of the liver and heart, serum levels of ferritin and hemoglobin, and fQRS durations—among the groups. We also used the Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient to assess the correlation between the measured variables. Moreover, multiple linear regression was implemented to evaluate the linear relationship between T2* values of heart and liver, serum levels of ferritin and hemoglobin, and fQRS duration. Significance was considered at P < 0.05.

Discussion

According to the study, cardiac iron overload in β-TM patients can prolong the fQRS duration, as demonstrated by a negative correlation between cardiac T2* value and fQRS duration. Such a relationship was also observed between hepatic T2* value and fQRS duration. Correspondingly, a linear relationship exists between these variables (T2* values of heart and liver and fQRS duration).

Eghbali and colleagues accomplished a cross-sectional study on 60 individuals afflicted by β-TM and aged 17.65 ± 9.28 years. The study exhibited a statistically-significant correlation between serum ferritin level and hepatic T2* value despite no significant correlation between the former and cardiac T2* value17. While our study and the Eghbali’s one correspond in terms of results and type of study, they differ considering their sample size and the subject’s age.

Majd et al. carried out a cross-sectional study on 85 Iranian thalassemia patients, either major or intermedia type, aiming to examine the association between T2*-weighted MRI of heart and liver and serum ferritin content. The patients’ mean age was 22.7 ± 7 years. They found that serum ferritin level and hepatic iron concentration had a statistically meaningful positive correlation, whereas a significant negative correlation existed between the former and T2* values of the heart and liver18. This study and ours share the common result that serum ferritin level is negatively correlated with hepatic T2* value. Furthermore, both studies are cross-sectional; however, the sample sizes and subjects’ mean age were dissimilar. In addition, our study solely focused on β-TM.

Suthar et al. performed a study in 2018 on 73 β-TM children aged 9.06 ± 4.68 years. A negative correlation was discovered between serum ferritin level and liver T2* values. In contrast, serum ferritin did not show a significant correlation with heart T2* value13. These findings are consistent with those of our study. However, these studies are different regarding their sample size and subjects’ mean age.

Amini and colleagues implemented a cross-sectional study on 40 patients diagnosed with β-TM. The patients’ mean age was 22.45 ± 6.75. The purpose of the study was to examine the correlation between cardiac iron overload, evaluated with T2*-weighted MRI, and fQRS. A positive correlation was seen between T2*-weighted MRI and serum content of ferritin and hemoglobin; however, T2*-weighted MRI and fQRS were negatively correlated19. Our study is inconsistent with the former and consistent with the latter. Both studies share common attributes, including the type of study. However, their methods are different.

A study by Aggarwal et al. was conducted on 48 transfusion-dependent thalassemia patients aged 10.7 ± 3.4 years. The study’s purpose was discovering a more easy-to-access tool to evaluate myocardial iron overload. Notably, the study disclosed that high cardiac iron overload was significantly associated with increased QRS duration. The finding was consistent with the current study. Furthermore, their study showed that high serum ferritin level is a predictor of diminution in cardiac T2* value which was not shown by our study. Additionally, they found no correlation between QRS duration and cardiac T2* values, whereas our study indicated a strong negative correlation20. The two studies resemble each other regarding the measured variables, while their sample sizes and subjects’ ages contrast.

Following blood transfusion, senescent transfused red blood cells are engulfed by macrophages within the reticuloendothelial system. This process causes unstable intracellular iron to be released into the bloodstream, where it attaches to transferrin. Once transferrin binding capacity is surpassed, excess non–transferrin-bound iron (NTBI) is transported via calcium channels into various types of cells, including the hepatocytes and myocytes. The deposition of iron in these tissues contributes to the diverse clinical manifestations associated with iron overload. The oxidative stress brought on by the metabolism of NTBI can result in cellular damage, including necrosis and apoptosis21. Ferritin is a protein constituted by 24 subunits, which serves as an iron-storage protein. This protein is secreted into the blood by hepatocytes and macrophages, and normally resides inside the cells22. Serum ferritin level proved to change in parallel to the amount of stored iron in the body23. Given that liver is often the organ where iron storage starts first compared with other organs24, combined with the fact that fQRS can indicate iron overload-associated myocardial scarring11, it would not be surprising if fQRS can indirectly represent the hepatic iron overload.

Given the negative correlation between cardiac T2* value and fQRS duration, and also between liver T2* value and fQRS duration, fQRS duration can be leveraged as a convenient marker to monitor patients already diagnosed with cardiac and/or hepatic iron overload. In addition, the negative correlation between serum ferritin level and hepatic T2* value suggests considering serum ferritin as another accessible monitoring marker for hepatic iron overload.

This study was completed on a small sample size, which was rooted in the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Actually, passing the screening with strict inclusion criteria, like undergoing T2*-weighted MRI of the heart and liver, confined the sample size. The small sample size restricts statistical power and reduces generalizability. Importantly, limiting inclusion to solely patients with fQRS can cause selection bias while preventing evaluation of whether fQRS predicts iron overload compared to those without fQRS. It should not be overlooked that the absence of any control or comparison group further weakens the conclusion of the study. Additionally, the higher proportion of female participants may have introduced bias, potentially affecting the generalizability of the results. Hence, we recommend further studies with larger sample sizes including both cases and controls, and patients with and without fQRS.

In conclusion, considering the significant negative correlation of fQRS duration and T2* values of heart and liver, along with the linear relationship existing between them, fQRS duration could serve as a convenient marker to monitor heart or liver iron overload in the context of β-TM.
