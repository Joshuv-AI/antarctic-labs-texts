GLP-1 receptor agonists in elective spine surgery: a systematic review of fusion outcomes, perioperative safety, and procedure-specific risk
Authors: Vinicius Ricieri Ferraz, Bashar Dawoud, Philippe A. Mercier, Tobias A. Mattei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGlucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) are increasingly used in patients with obesity and type 2 diabetes mellitus undergoing elective spine surgery. Although these agents may improve metabolic risk profiles, their associations with spinal fusion, perioperative complications, and procedure-specific outcomes remain incompletely defined.MethodsWe conducted a systematic review and qualitative synthesis of comparative clinical studies evaluating adults undergoing spine surgery with documented preoperative or perioperative GLP-1 RA exposure. Eligible studies assessed fusion-related outcomes, perioperative medical complications, surgical site infection, readmission, length of stay, healthcare utilization or costs, and musculoskeletal outcomes. The review was conducted in accordance with PRISMA 2020 principles, with eligibility criteria defined using a PICOS framework.ResultsTwenty-three studies were included, consisting predominantly of retrospective cohort studies and propensity-matched database analyses. Lumbar fusion studies generally reported favorable or neutral associations between GLP-1 RA exposure and pseudarthrosis, infection, readmission, and healthcare utilization, although some transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion cohorts reported adverse associations. Cervical fusion outcomes were more heterogeneous and appeared to vary by surgical approach. Anterior cervical fusion studies generally reported neutral or favorable outcomes, whereas posterior cervical fusion studies yielded conflicting findings. Gastrointestinal safety data were sparse, no included study reported postoperative pancreatitis, and preoperative treatment duration and longitudinal BMI or weight change were incompletely characterized.ConclusionsCurrent evidence suggests that GLP-1 RA exposure is associated with procedure-specific differences in outcomes after elective spine surgery. Because the available evidence is predominantly observational, these findings should be interpreted as associations rather than evidence of direct causal benefit or harm. Perioperative GLP-1 RA management should be individualized according to surgical procedure, metabolic status, gastrointestinal symptoms, renal and hydration risk, and nutritional or sarcopenia-related vulnerability.

Introduction

Obesity and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) are increasingly prevalent among patients undergoing elective spine surgery and are associated with impaired wound healing, infection, altered spinal biomechanics, and potentially compromised bone health. These factors may adversely affect perioperative outcomes and spinal fusion, making metabolic optimization an important component of surgical planning [1].

Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs), including semaglutide, liraglutide, and dulaglutide, have become widely used for the treatment of T2DM and obesity. These agents improve glycemic control and can produce substantial weight loss, resulting in an increasing number of patients presenting for spine surgery with current or recent GLP-1 RA exposure [2,3]. GLP-1 RAs may therefore represent an increasingly relevant component of preoperative metabolic optimization, particularly in patients whose obesity or metabolic comorbidities increase surgical risk [4].

However, the implications of GLP-1 RA therapy for spine surgery remain uncertain. Weight reduction and improved glycemic control may favorably affect perioperative risk, whereas loss of lean skeletal muscle mass has raised concerns regarding sarcopenia and functional recovery [[5], [6], [7]]. Delayed gastric emptying has also prompted perioperative concerns regarding aspiration risk and anesthetic management [8]. In addition, experimental and metabolic studies have suggested potential effects of GLP-1 signaling on bone metabolism, although the clinical relevance of these mechanisms to spinal arthrodesis remains unclear [9,10].

Emerging clinical studies have reported heterogeneous associations between GLP-1 RA exposure and spine surgery outcomes, with findings varying across lumbar and cervical procedures [[11], [12], [13]]. Several systematic reviews and meta-analyses have recently evaluated GLP-1 receptor agonist use in patients undergoing spine surgery. However, the evidence base has expanded rapidly, with multiple additional studies published across lumbar, anterior cervical, posterior cervical, and adult spinal deformity procedures. Moreover, aggregation of heterogeneous spinal procedures may obscure clinically relevant differences according to anatomical region and surgical approach. An updated procedure-specific synthesis may therefore provide additional insight into whether associations between GLP-1–based therapy and surgical outcomes differ across distinct spine procedures. Therefore, this systematic review aimed to provide an updated, procedure-specific synthesis of the available clinical evidence on GLP-1 RA exposure in elective spine surgery, with particular emphasis on differences across lumbar, anterior cervical, and posterior cervical procedures, as well as fusion-related outcomes, perioperative safety, surgical site infection, renal complications, healthcare utilization, and musculoskeletal considerations.

Methods

Protocol and reporting framework

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) guidelines. Search strategy and information sources

We systematically searched PubMed/MEDLINE and Ovid for studies published from January 2000 through June 2026. Search terms combined concepts related to GLP-1–based therapies and spine surgery, including “GLP-1 receptor agonist,” “glucagon-like peptide-1,” “semaglutide,” “liraglutide,” “dulaglutide,” “tirzepatide,” “spine surgery,” “spinal fusion,” “arthrodesis,” “pseudarthrosis,” and “nonunion.” Boolean operators and database-specific syntax were adapted accordingly. Reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews were manually screened to identify additional eligible studies.

Eligibility criteria

Eligible studies included adults undergoing elective cervical, thoracic, or lumbar spine surgery with documented preoperative or perioperative exposure to GLP-1–based therapy and an appropriate comparator group. GLP-1–based therapies included selective GLP-1 receptor agonists, such as semaglutide, liraglutide, and dulaglutide, as well as the dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonist tirzepatide.

Outcomes of interest included pseudarthrosis or nonunion, revision surgery, hardware-related complications, surgical site infection, perioperative medical complications, readmission, length of stay, healthcare utilization or costs, and musculoskeletal outcomes when available.

Comparative clinical studies, including retrospective and prospective observational studies and propensity-matched database analyses, were eligible. Case reports, noncomparative case series, editorials, commentaries, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and preclinical studies were excluded from the primary clinical synthesis. Non-spine perioperative studies and preclinical or review literature were used only to provide contextual evidence regarding aspiration risk, renal outcomes, bone metabolism, and body composition.

Study selection and data extraction

Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts, followed by full-text assessment of potentially eligible studies. Disagreements were resolved by consensus or adjudication by a third reviewer.

Data were extracted using a standardized form and included study design, data source, sample size, surgical procedure and spinal region, GLP-1-based therapy, medication indication, comparator characteristics, follow-up duration, outcome definitions, fusion-assessment methods, and reported effect estimates. A targeted supplementary extraction recorded BMI or body weight at treatment initiation and before surgery, longitudinal anthropometric measurements, actual or prescription-derived treatment duration, administrative exposure-definition windows, and perioperative gastrointestinal or aspiration-related outcomes, including pancreatitis. For database studies, medication-exposure windows, coding strategies, and methods of outcome ascertainment were recorded when available.

Assessment of overlapping populations

Because several studies used large administrative claims databases or electronic health-record networks, potential cohort overlap was assessed according to data source, study period, procedure type, exposure definition, and outcome definition. Studies with potentially overlapping populations were not interpreted as fully independent confirmation of the same outcome. When substantial overlap was suspected, greater emphasis was placed on the more comprehensive and clinically specific analysis.

Risk-of-bias assessment

Risk of bias in the included observational studies was assessed using the Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) framework. Relevant domains included confounding, participant selection, exposure classification, deviations from intended exposure, missing data, outcome measurement, and selective reporting. Particular attention was given to potential confounding related to diabetes severity, body mass index, glycemic control, smoking, frailty, renal disease, and medication indication.

Data synthesis

Because of substantial heterogeneity in surgical procedures, patient populations, medication-exposure definitions, comparator groups, follow-up periods, and outcome ascertainment, a quantitative meta-analysis was not performed. Findings were synthesized narratively and grouped by anatomical region and procedure type, including lumbar, anterior cervical, and posterior cervical surgery. Perioperative and healthcare-utilization outcomes were summarized separately. Findings from non-spine perioperative, metabolic, and musculoskeletal literature were used only for contextual interpretation and were distinguished from the primary spine-surgery evidence. To avoid obscuring clinically relevant procedure-specific heterogeneity, findings from lumbar and thoracolumbar, anterior cervical, and posterior cervical procedures were interpreted separately rather than collapsed into a single spine-wide effect estimate.

Results

Search and study selection

The database search identified 1,143 records. After removal of 124 duplicates, 1,019 records underwent title and abstract screening. Of these, 823 were excluded, and 196 full-text reports were assessed for eligibility. A total of 173 reports were excluded because they did not involve spine surgery (n = 32), were preclinical studies (n = 50), reviews (n = 10), did not evaluate GLP-1–based therapy (n = 53), or were abstracts or conference proceedings (n = 28). Twenty-three studies met the eligibility criteria and were included in the qualitative synthesis (Figure).

Study characteristics

The included studies consisted predominantly of retrospective cohort studies, propensity-matched analyses, and large administrative or electronic health-record database studies. No randomized clinical trials evaluating GLP-1-based therapy in spine surgery were identified. Studies included lumbar fusion, cervical fusion, adult spinal deformity surgery, and other elective spine procedures. Exposure definitions varied by medication, indication, duration of therapy, and perioperative timing.

No included study reported BMI or body weight at GLP-1-based therapy initiation together with a subsequent preoperative measurement. Limited longitudinal anthropometric information was available in Wiener et al. [3] and Parker et al. [24]; however, the measurement time points did not represent a defined pretreatment-to-preoperative trajectory. Parker et al. [24] reported a mean 12-month preoperative weight change of −6.0 ± 15.0 lb among GLP-1 users versus −0.8 ± 14.2 lb among nonusers (p = .012), but the initial measurement was not explicitly obtained before GLP-1 initiation [24]. These data therefore do not permit assessment of the magnitude or rate of GLP-1-associated preoperative weight loss.

Preoperative GLP-1 treatment duration was reported in only a small subset of studies. Parker et al. [24] reported a mean duration of 14.2 ± 14.3 months [24], and 2 semaglutide cohorts by Khalid et al. [17,18] provided prescription-derived exposure categories using a 9-month threshold [17,18]. Most other studies used administrative prescription windows ranging from approximately 90 days to 1 year, which indicate exposure ascertainment rather than continuous treatment, adherence, or cumulative dose. This heterogeneity precluded a reliable duration-response analysis.

The principal outcomes included pseudarthrosis or nonunion, revision surgery, hardware-related complications, surgical site infection, readmission, length of stay, healthcare utilization, and perioperative medical complications.

Lumbar fusion outcomes

Lumbar and thoracolumbar studies generally reported neutral or favorable associations between GLP-1–based therapy and surgical outcomes, although findings were not uniform.

Wiener et al. [3] reported lower postoperative infection and early revision rates among GLP-1 RA users in a matched spine-fusion cohort [3]. Tummala et al. [11] reported lower pseudarthrosis rates at multiple postoperative intervals without an increase in major 90-day medical complications or hardware failure [11]. Similarly, Agrawal et al. [14], Chang et al. [15], and Ghali et al. [16] reported favorable or neutral fusion-related outcomes among GLP-1 RA or semaglutide users [[14], [15], [16]].

In contrast, 2 TLIF-focused studies by Khalid et al. [17,18] reported adverse associations, including a higher likelihood of subsequent lumbar fusion surgery and increased overall clinical complications in selected semaglutide-exposed cohorts [17,18].

More recent studies generally reported neutral or favorable findings. Kishan et al. [19] observed lower perioperative and long-term complications after thoracic and/or lumbar fusion, particularly in higher-BMI groups [19]. In comparative analyses, Wang et al. [20] and Sacks et al. [21]reported lower pseudarthrosis and selected mechanical complications with tirzepatide compared with single-receptor GLP-1 agonists [20,21]. Ng et al. [22] reported shorter length of stay and lower episode-of-care costs after anterior lumbar interbody fusion without an increase in complications [22]. Baidya et al. [23] and Parker et al. [24] reported generally comparable surgical and patient-reported outcomes [23,24], while D’Amico et al. Cervical fusion outcomes

Cervical fusion outcomes were more heterogeneous and appeared to vary by surgical approach. Among posterior cervical fusion cohorts, Ng et al. [13] reported higher pseudarthrosis at 2 years among semaglutide users (OR 4.79), together with increased postoperative dysphagia [13]. In contrast, Rajkovic et al. [26] reported lower pseudarthrosis after matching but a higher incidence of postoperative radiculopathy [26]. These conflicting findings preclude a definitive conclusion regarding posterior cervical fusion.

Anterior cervical studies were generally neutral or favorable. Tao et al. [12] reported no significant difference in short-term surgical or early fusion-related outcomes after cervical decompression and fusion [12]. Vatsia et al. [27] reported lower pseudarthrosis in a multilevel cervical fusion cohort [27], and Amasa et al. [28] reported lower pseudarthrosis after single-level ACDF [28].

Perioperative and healthcare-utilization outcomes

Spine-specific perioperative outcomes were generally neutral or favorable. Heo et al. [31] reported no increase in aspiration after ACDF and lower rates of acute kidney injury, venous thromboembolism, and extended length of stay [31]. Wahid et al. [32] reported lower rates of sepsis, surgical site infection, deep vein thrombosis, wound dehiscence, and mortality among overweight adults receiving long-term preoperative GLP-1 RA therapy before elective laminectomy [32]. Ng et al. [22] reported shorter length of stay and lower same-day and 90-day costs after anterior lumbar interbody fusion [22].

Gastrointestinal safety outcomes were sparsely reported. Heo et al. [31] directly assessed postoperative pulmonary aspiration after ACDF and found no significant difference between GLP-1 users and controls (0.81% vs. 1.25%; OR 0.70, 95% CI 0.32–1.56), with similar overall dysphagia rates [31]. Baidya et al. [23] reported isolated inpatient nausea and Clostridioides difficile colitis events and similar time to flatus (1.77 ± 1.09 vs. 1.95 ± 1.13 days; p = .586) [23], while Kishan et al. [19] reported 90-day ileus and dysphagia by BMI stratum [19]. No included study reported postoperative pancreatitis, and nausea, vomiting, gastroparesis or delayed gastric emptying, regurgitation, and bowel obstruction were not systematically assessed.

Large non-spine perioperative studies did not demonstrate a consistent increase in postoperative aspiration pneumonia associated with GLP-1 RA exposure [33]. Bone, body composition, and musculoskeletal outcomes

Direct clinical evidence evaluating bone metabolism, fusion-bed biology, or body composition in patients receiving GLP-1–based therapy during spine surgery was limited. Most included studies did not directly assess bone mineral density, bone turnover markers, paraspinal muscle mass or quality, nutritional status, or longitudinal changes in body composition. Consequently, potential effects of GLP-1–based therapy on bone and skeletal muscle remain mechanistically plausible but cannot be established from the available spine-surgery evidence.

Discussion

Principal findings

This systematic review identified procedure-specific associations between GLP-1-based therapy and outcomes after elective spine surgery. Lumbar and thoracolumbar studies generally reported neutral or favorable associations with pseudarthrosis, postoperative complications, readmission, length of stay, and healthcare utilization [3,11]. Cervical outcomes were more heterogeneous. Anterior cervical studies were predominantly neutral or favorable [27,30], whereas posterior cervical studies reported conflicting findings, including increased pseudarthrosis and dysphagia in one cohort and lower pseudarthrosis but increased radiculopathy in another [13,26]. Recent spine-specific systematic reviews evaluated a smaller body of predominantly retrospective evidence available through 2025 and early 2026 [2,34]. In contrast, the present review includes 23 comparative spine-surgery studies identified through June 2026 and emphasizes separate interpretation of lumbar and thoracolumbar procedures, anterior cervical fusion, and posterior cervical fusion. This distinction is clinically relevant because the updated literature suggests predominantly neutral or favorable associations in lumbar and anterior cervical surgery, whereas posterior cervical fusion outcomes remain heterogeneous. The present review therefore complements prior syntheses by providing an updated, procedure-specific assessment rather than treating spine surgery as a homogeneous procedural category. Overall, spine-specific perioperative data did not demonstrate a consistent increase in aspiration or acute kidney injury.

Preoperative optimization and surgical candidacy

The increasing use of GLP-1–based therapies should be considered within the broader shift toward preoperative metabolic optimization in spine surgery. Contemporary optimization strategies increasingly address obesity, glycemic control, smoking, frailty, nutrition, and functional status before elective surgery. In this setting, GLP-1–based therapy may represent one component of a multidisciplinary optimization strategy, particularly for patients whose obesity or metabolic comorbidities increase surgical risk.

Improved weight and metabolic control could potentially expand surgical candidacy for selected high-risk patients; however, this remains a hypothesis rather than an established clinical benefit. Prospective studies are needed to determine whether GLP-1–based preoperative optimization improves surgical candidacy, perioperative recovery, or long-term outcomes [4].

The current evidence cannot determine whether the magnitude or rate of preoperative GLP-1-associated weight loss modifies perioperative or fusion-related outcomes. No study captured a defined pretreatment-to-preoperative BMI or weight trajectory; the partial longitudinal measurements reported by Wiener et al. [3]and Parker et al. [24] do not establish when treatment began relative to the first anthropometric measurement.

Interpretation of procedure-specific heterogeneity

The heterogeneity observed across lumbar, anterior cervical, and posterior cervical procedures should not be interpreted as evidence of a direct biological effect of GLP-1–based therapy on spinal fusion. Although experimental and metabolic literature suggests potential effects of GLP-1 signaling on bone metabolism, current clinical evidence does not establish a causal relationship between these therapies and arthrodesis success [10].

Several alternative explanations may account for the observed differences, including residual confounding, diabetes severity, body mass index, frailty, nutritional status, renal function, medication indication, duration of therapy, surgical approach, and differences in outcome ascertainment across database studies [2,34]. Actual or prescription-derived treatment duration was available in only a few cohorts [17,18,24]; administrative lookback windows in the remaining studies should not be interpreted as evidence of continuous therapy or adherence.

The generally favorable lumbar findings may reflect improved glycemic control, weight reduction, reduced perioperative risk, or differences in patient selection rather than a direct effect on fusion biology. Conversely, adverse findings reported in selected TLIF and posterior cervical cohorts may reflect procedure-specific factors or unmeasured differences in patient characteristics [13,17,18]. Loss of lean skeletal muscle mass during substantial pharmacologic weight loss represents an additional potential concern, particularly in patients at risk for sarcopenia [5,6]. Whether these changes meaningfully affect spinal stability, rehabilitation, or fusion outcomes remains unknown.

Cardiovascular considerations

Potential cardiovascular benefit represents an additional consideration that is not captured by fusion outcomes alone. Large cardiovascular outcome trials have demonstrated cardiovascular benefit with GLP-1–based therapy in selected high-risk populations. In the SELECT trial, semaglutide reduced major adverse cardiovascular events in adults with overweight or obesity and established cardiovascular disease without diabetes [35]. Although these findings are not specific to spine surgery and cannot be extrapolated directly to postoperative cardiovascular outcomes, they highlight the broader cardiometabolic implications of perioperative treatment decisions. Future spine-specific studies should evaluate cardiovascular outcomes, including myocardial injury, myocardial infarction, and stroke.

Perioperative medication management

Perioperative management of GLP-1–based therapy should be individualized rather than based on routine discontinuation. Contemporary multi-society guidance emphasizes assessment of factors associated with delayed gastric emptying, including active gastrointestinal symptoms, dose escalation, higher medication doses, and underlying disorders of gastric motility, while also considering the metabolic consequences of withholding therapy [8].

In patients at increased risk of retained gastric contents, strategies may include temporary dietary modification, adjustment of the anesthetic plan, and point-of-care gastric ultrasound when appropriate and available. Decisions regarding continuation or interruption should be coordinated among the surgical, anesthesia, and prescribing teams and tailored to the individual patient and medication [8].

Perioperative hydration and renal function also warrant attention, particularly in patients experiencing nausea, vomiting, or reduced oral intake. Major spine surgery may further increase susceptibility to hemodynamic and renal stress. Assessment of volume status and renal function, avoidance of unnecessary nephrotoxins, and appropriate intraoperative hemodynamic management are therefore reasonable components of perioperative care. Importantly, current spine-specific studies do not demonstrate a consistent increase in acute kidney injury associated with GLP-1–based therapy [21,31].

Clinical implications

Current evidence does not support considering GLP-1–based therapy a contraindication to elective spine surgery or routinely discontinuing therapy solely because of concerns regarding spinal fusion.

For lumbar and anterior cervical procedures, available evidence is predominantly neutral or favorable, although the observational nature of the data precludes causal conclusions. Posterior cervical fusion requires greater caution in interpretation because available studies have reported conflicting outcomes [13,26]. Rather than recommending procedure-specific discontinuation, these findings support individualized assessment of metabolic health, nutritional status, frailty, and potential sarcopenia in higher-risk patients. These differences further support interpreting GLP-1–based therapy according to surgical region and approach rather than extrapolating a single overall association across heterogeneous spine procedures.

Patients experiencing substantial weight loss may also benefit from attention to adequate nutrition, protein intake, preservation of lean mass, and physical conditioning, particularly when sarcopenia or frailty is present [[5], [6], [7]].

Strengths and limitations

This review addresses a rapidly evolving topic in spine surgery and emphasizes procedure-specific interpretation rather than treating all spinal procedures as a homogeneous group. It also distinguishes observed clinical associations from proposed biological mechanisms and highlights the limitations inherent in large retrospective database studies.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Most included studies were retrospective and observational, frequently relying on administrative claims or electronic health-record data. Medication indication, dose, adherence, dose-escalation phase, and timing of the last dose relative to surgery were generally unavailable. Treatment duration was reported in only a few studies, whereas most cohorts relied on administrative prescription-based exposure definitions. Longitudinal preoperative BMI change and gastrointestinal adverse events were also incompletely characterized. In particular, no included study reported postoperative pancreatitis, preventing estimation of its incidence in patients undergoing spine surgery while receiving GLP-1-based therapy. Fusion and complication outcomes were often identified through diagnostic or procedural coding rather than standardized radiographic or prospectively adjudicated assessment.

Residual confounding is also likely, particularly from differences in diabetes severity, glycemic control, body mass index, renal disease, smoking, frailty, nutritional status, and access to healthcare. In addition, potential overlap among large database cohorts may limit the independence of apparently confirmatory findings. Finally, few studies directly evaluated bone density, body composition, sarcopenia, paraspinal muscle quality, nutritional status, or fusion biology, limiting mechanistic interpretation [2,5,34].

Future research

Future prospective studies should characterize GLP-1-based therapy by medication type, indication, dose, duration of therapy, dose-escalation phase, cumulative preoperative exposure, adherence, and timing of the last dose relative to surgery. Studies should report BMI and body weight at treatment initiation, immediately before surgery, and at prespecified longitudinal time points, together with the magnitude and rate of weight loss, changes in glycemic control, renal and nutritional status, body composition, paraspinal muscle quality, bone density, and standardized radiographic fusion outcomes. Future studies should also systematically capture perioperative gastrointestinal symptoms and complications, including nausea, vomiting, delayed gastric emptying or gastroparesis, regurgitation, aspiration events, ileus, bowel obstruction, and pancreatitis, together with perioperative continuation or interruption of GLP-1-based therapy.

Procedure-specific analyses will be particularly important for TLIF, posterior cervical fusion, long-segment deformity correction, and anterior column procedures. Prospective studies should also distinguish metabolic optimization effects from direct medication-related effects and evaluate whether preoperative GLP-1–based therapy meaningfully influences surgical candidacy, postoperative recovery, and long-term functional outcomes.

Conclusions

GLP-1–based therapies are increasingly relevant to preoperative optimization in elective spine surgery. Current evidence suggests predominantly neutral or favorable associations in lumbar and anterior cervical procedures, whereas posterior cervical fusion outcomes remain heterogeneous and require further investigation.

Because the available evidence is largely observational, these findings should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects. Perioperative management should be individualized according to surgical procedure, metabolic status, gastrointestinal and aspiration risk, renal function, nutritional status, and potential sarcopenia-related vulnerability.
