A multi-year campus-level smart meter database
Authors: Mingchen Li, Zhe Wang, Yao Qu, Kin Ming Chui, Marcus Leung-Shea
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
With the growing need for precise campus electricity management, understanding load patterns is crucial for improving energy efficiency and optimizing energy use. However, detailed electricity load data for campus buildings and their internal equipment is often lacking, hindering research. This paper introduces an energy consumption monitoring dataset from The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology (HKUST) campus in Hong Kong, comprising data from over 1400 meters across more than 20 buildings and collected over two and a half years. Using the Brick Schema curation strategy, raw data was curated into a research-ready format. This dataset supports various research tasks, including load pattern recognition, fault detection, demand response strategies, and load forecasting.

Background & Summary

In Hong Kong, residential energy consumption accounts for 24% of total energy use, while commercial sectors (including offices, healthcare, retail, etc.) account for 43%, indicating that building energy consumption has exceeded half of the total energy usage1. Utilizing smart meters to monitor different buildings and spaces within them can effectively identify load patterns and manage loads to enhance energy efficiency2. University campus areas are comprised of various types of buildings, including classrooms, libraries, dormitories, offices, restaurants, laboratories, and retail spaces, each with its unique energy consumption characteristics3. These buildings effectively represent the typical range of architectural types.

Open-source databases that encompass various building types are critically important as they provide researchers and engineers with a rich resource of data for analyzing different patterns of energy consumption. Moreover, they enable further detecting unnecessary energy wastes, profiling customer behaviors, benchmarking and optimizing building energy efficiency, and forecasting of building loads. As demonstrated by Panapakidis and others4, Pan and colleagues5 conducted pattern recognition on a campus’s buildings and residential buildings based on electrical load. Furthermore, an increasing number of machine learning algorithms, such as Artificial Neural Network (ANN)6, Recurrent Neural Network (RNN)7, Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM)8, etc., are being used for load forecasting9–11. Datasets covering multiple types of buildings can significantly enhance the robustness of these models.

However, currently, datasets on building electricity load often focus on single types of buildings, such as residential12–17 or office buildings18–20. This narrow focus provides an incomplete picture of the energy landscape, as it excludes a wide variety of other building types, such as retail, restaurant, and laboratory, each with distinct energy needs and consumption patterns. Even among the datasets that include multiple building types3, they lack detailed data on the electricity load of internal devices, such as Heating, Ventilation, and Air Conditioning (HVAC) systems, lighting, and office equipment. This omission hinders a comprehensive understanding of energy consumption and the identification of targeted energy conservation strategies.

In 2020, an unprecedented global lockdown was implemented worldwide to control the spread of COVID-19 in many countries19. As of March 1, 2023, Hong Kong officially lifted the mask mandate, becoming one of the last regions in the world to do so, marking the end of its pandemic prevention policies21. During the pandemic, changes in travel frequency, epidemic prevention policies, and working behaviors significantly affected the energy consumption patterns of different types of buildings. A comparative study of energy consumption data collected during and after COVID-19 can reveal how occupant behaviors and energy usage patterns change as a result of the pandemic22.

This paper presents a dataset on the electricity load of a campus, along with the development of its associated metadata using the semantic Brick23 model. Methods

Campus

The dataset utilized in this study originates from real-world electricity consumption data from 1432 meters at the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, situated in the rural coastal area of Sai Kung District, Hong Kong (22.3375 N, 114.263E). The campus spans 60 hectares, and the data is overseen by the University Sustainability Office. The detailed collection and analysis of electricity data aim to scrutinize current usage patterns across various building types and uncover potential energy efficiencies within the campus. For better management and analysis, the campus is segmented into three zones as depicted in Fig. 1.Fig. 1Overview of HKUST Campus.

Overview of HKUST Campus.

The Main Campus comprises the Academic Building and the Cheng Yu Tung Building. The Academic Building is a multifaceted complex that includes offices, classrooms, a library, dining facilities, and more. The Cheng Yu Tung Building is integrated with the Academic Building through a heat exchange link and is also part of the Main Campus. The Lee Shau Kee (LSK) Campus hosts the Lee Shau Kee Business Building, the HKUST Jockey Club Institute for Advanced Study/Lo Ka Chung Building, and the Li Dak Sum Yip Yio-Chin Kenneth Li Conference Lodge. The remainder of the buildings are categorized under the Rest Campus. By understanding the building types, one can predict electricity usage trends and identify areas with higher consumption. This categorization enables more accurate and targeted analysis of electricity meter data, facilitating efficient energy management and conservation strategies across the campus.

Zone

To better manage and optimize energy consumption across the campus, the school has been divided into 104 Energy Zones. This approach offers several benefits. Firstly, by segmenting the campus into smaller areas, energy usage in each zone can be more precisely monitored and analyzed, enabling the identification and resolution of energy wastage in specific areas. Secondly, this granular management allows for the implementation of targeted energy-saving measures based on the unique consumption characteristics of each zone, thereby enhancing the overall energy efficiency of the campus. Lastly, by managing the campus in smaller sections, any issues of abnormal energy consumption can be quickly located and addressed, facilitating timely interventions.

Within these Energy Zones, varying numbers of electricity meters have been deployed to monitor energy consumption. Some zones have one or more meters that directly measure the entire zone’s energy usage, while others do not have such direct measurements. The university utilizes meters from two main providers: MunHean and Hensen & CET Electric Technology. MunHean meters are installed in academic buildings and remote buildings, specifically employing the SKYDER-MH E63-L model, which communicates via the Low-power, Long-range Wide Area Network (LoRaWAN) protocol. In contrast, Hensen & CET Electric Technology meters are deployed in staff quarters and student halls. Additionally, the PMC-53A model communicates through the Modbus RTU protocol and meets the IEC 62053-22 Class 0.5S accuracy standard, providing a resolution of 0.1 kWh. However, the maximum reading capacities for both Hensen & CET Electric Technology meters were not specified.

Data curation

In preparing the dataset, we encountered variations in the sampling frequencies due to the electricity meters being sourced from different suppliers and batches. Thus, we identified four distinct sampling frequencies: 15 minutes, 30 minutes, 1 hour, and 1 day. Missing values were replaced with the placeholder ‘NAN’. It is important to note that these missing values were neither imputed nor subjected to outlier detection. This approach was adopted due to the absence of reliable standards for guiding the imputation of missing values or the detection of outliers. Consequently, we decided to provide unaltered raw data, allowing researchers to apply their preferred data-cleaning techniques. Considering that the best methods can vary across different research or practical contexts, this flexibility is crucial.

Data Records

The dataset is meticulously organized into two main categories: time-series data and metadata. As illustrated in Fig. 3, the time-series data is stored in Excel (.xlsx) format, while the metadata is in Turtle (.ttl) format. This structured organization facilitates efficient data management and analysis. The raw dataset, comprising various equipment and spaces within the HKUST campus, totals approximately 773 MB. After resampling, the dataset size is 830 MB. The total compressed size of the dataset is approximately 1.43 GB. The choice of Excel for time-series data allows for easy manipulation and analysis due to its widespread use and compatibility with various analytical tools. The Turtle format for metadata, adhering to the Brick schema, ensures semantic richness and interoperability, enabling detailed, standardized descriptions and advanced data queries. This dual-format approach enhances the dataset’s accessibility and usability, supporting comprehensive analysis and effective energy management across the campus. The dataset is hosted on the Dryad website31 (10.5061/dryad.k3j9kd5h6).Fig. 3Organization of Raw and Clean Time-Series Datasets.

Time-series data

The dataset comprises time-series data, meticulously organized into raw and cleaned formats, as shown in Fig. 3. The raw dataset includes multiple Excel files labeled GUI.NO.xxxxx.xlsx, such as GUI.NO.D0001.xlsx, GUI.NO.D0002.xlsx, and GUI.NO.M0604.xlsx, all housed within a ‘Time-series data’ folder. Accompanying these files is a metadata file, HKUST_Meter_Metadata.ttl, providing essential contextual information. The cleaned dataset involves resampling the raw data into specified intervals and categorizing them into separate directories: T15, T30, T60, and T1440, representing different time intervals of 15 minutes, 30 minutes, 60 minutes, and 1440 minutes (1 day) respectively. Each directory contains resampled Excel files maintaining the original naming convention. For instance, GUI.NO.D0001.xlsx appears within the T15, T30, T60, and T1440 directories, reflecting the resampled data at 15-minute, 30-minute, 60-minute, and daily intervals respectively. The metadata file, HKUST_Meter_Metadata.ttl, is also included in the cleaned dataset to ensure comprehensive documentation of the processed data. This structured approach facilitates ease of access and analysis, ensuring that both raw and resampled data are systematically categorized and well-documented. This organization is critical for reproducibility and further analysis in time-series research, enabling detailed exploration and utilization of the dataset.

Brick metadata

Along with the building’s time-series dataset, we used the semantic model as metadata to comprehensively describe the building and its smart meter systems. This model is based on the Brick schema, an open-source framework for standardizing the semantic description of building assets. The schema provides an extensible dictionary of terms and concepts, a set of relationships for linking these concepts, and a flexible data model leveraging semantic web technologies.

The detailed Brick model is stored in .ttl file format, facilitating integration with other building management systems and smart analytics applications. This structured metadata is essential for optimizing building operations and developing intelligent control systems. As illustrated in Fig. 4, the model is composed of three key components: the Brick class (in green), Brick entities (in blue), and Entity Properties (in red). Entities are instances of classes, typically named to suit specific projects. At the same time, types ensure both humans and machines can correctly identify each instance, enabling rapid query responses — such as identifying various Sites or Buildings. Generally, properties are consistent across instances; for example, the Normal_Meter_M0270’s properties shown in the figure demonstrate that its unit is kWh and its resolution is 30 minutes, alongside the name of its associated time-series data file. We employ a dynamic location methodology in our modeling process to accommodate the varying measurement locations of each electricity meter. Initially, we convert these locations into Brick format and then add specific location information for each meter, as shown in yellow.Fig. 4Brick model for one Electrical Meter.

Figure 6 shows the percentage of missing data points for different sampling intervals and quarters from Q1 2022 to Q2 2024. The y-axis lists the sampling intervals: 15 minutes, 30 minutes, 1 hour, and 1 day, while the x-axis covers the different quarters. When calculating missing rates, we included not only meters sampled at each interval but also higher-frequency meters resampled to lower frequencies. For instance, the 15-minute interval includes all 15-minute meters, the 30-minute interval includes all 30-minute meters plus 15-minute meters resampled to 30 minutes, and so on.Fig. 6Missing Rates in Electrical Meter Data Across Different Sampling Times and Quarters.

Missing Rates in Electrical Meter Data Across Different Sampling Times and Quarters.

The 15-minute interval shows the highest missing rates, ranging from 23.467% to 52.417%. The peak is in 2022 Q4, indicating challenges in data collection at this granularity during this period. As the sampling interval increases, the missing rates decline. The 30-minute interval ranges from 18.548% to 36.680%, the 1-hour interval from 13.988% to 29.656%, and the 1-day interval from 3.377% to 13.584%. This trend highlights the difficulties in maintaining complete data at finer temporal resolutions. Several reasons could explain these missing rates. Higher frequency data collection (15 and 30 Minutes) might face technical issues such as sensor malfunctions or data transmission errors. These issues are less pronounced in lower frequency collections (1 Hour and 1 Day), where data transmission and storage are more manageable. Despite these missing rates, the data retains significant value. The lower missing rates at 1-hour and 1-day intervals still provide comprehensive insights for long-term trends and patterns. Additionally, data imputation techniques can mitigate the impact of missing values, ensuring the dataset remains robust for analysis. Furthermore, the decreasing trend in missing rates over time suggests improvements in data collection processes, reflecting the dataset’s growing reliability.

Educational and Research buildings, such as the Academic Building, Cheng Yu Tung Building, IASR Plant, LSK Business Buildings, and NFF2 Lab, generally show good data quality in most quarters, with missing rates usually ranging from 0% to 20%. In the case of Residential buildings, the Staff Quarters SSQ APT1-49 and Student Hall GGT exhibit remarkable data quality, with missing rates in most quarters not exceeding 2%. Energy consumption discrepancy analysis

Some zones have main meters measuring total area electricity consumption, allowing us to validate our data. For these zones, we compare main meter values with sub-meter values. We first resample all meters to daily data and calculate daily consumption for each meter. We then compare the total consumption of the main meter with the summed consumption of the sub-meters. The relative error is computed by dividing the Mean Absolute Error (MAE) between the two by the larger of the two means. Table 4 shows that relative error values across different zones vary significantly, ranging from 9.40% to 30.75%. Several factors could contribute to these discrepancies. Some devices are likely not connected to sub-meters while still being recorded by the main meter, leading to potential discrepancies since sub-meters do not capture all energy usage within a zone. Additionally, data loss or interruptions during long-term monitoring could result in mismatches between sub-meter and main meter readings. Differences in meter accuracy also play a role; sub-meters with lower precision may not accurately record small fluctuations in electricity consumption, causing cumulative discrepancies. These factors together likely contribute to the relative errors.

Usage Notes

The time-series data is in xlsx format and can be loaded into any compatible software. The dataset is 1.43GB. Researchers can use Python, Java, MATLAB, or R to process it. Detailed information on data preprocessing, analysis, missing data rates, and query examples is available on the dataset’s GitHub page (https://github.com/LiMingchen159/HKUST_Meter_Brick). This dataset is also useful for research in load pattern recognition, fault detection, demand response strategies, and load forecasting.
