Spin susceptibility in a pseudogap state with fluctuating spiral magnetic order
Paulo Forni, Pietro M. Bonetti, Henrik Müller-Groeling, Demetrio Vilardi, Walter Metzner

Abstract
We compute the electron spin susceptibility in the pseudogap regime of the two-dimensional Hubbard model in the framework of a SU(2) gauge theory of fluctuating magnetic order. The electrons are fractionalized in fermionic chargons with a pseudospin degree of freedom and bosonic spinons. The chargons are treated in a renormalized mean-field theory and order in a Néel or spiral magnetic state in a broad range around half-filling below a transition temperature T ∗ T^{*} . Fluctuations of the spin orientation are captured by the spinons. Their dynamics is governed by a non-linear sigma model, with spin stiffnesses computed microscopically from the pseudospin susceptibility of the chargons. The SU(2) gauge group is higgsed in the chargon sector, and the spinon fluctuations prevent breaking of the physical spin symmetry at any finite temperature. The electron spin susceptibility obtained from the gauge theory shares many features with experimental observations in the pseudogap regime of cuprate superconductors: the dynamical spin susceptibility S ⁡ ( 𝐪 , ω ) S(\mathbf{q},\omega) has a spin gap, the static uniform spin susceptibility κ s \kappa_{s} decreases strongly with temperature below T ∗ T^{*} , and the NMR relaxation rate T 1 − 1 T_{1}^{-1} vanishes exponentially in the low temperature limit if the ground state is quantum disordered. At low hole doping, S ⁡ ( 𝐪 , ω ) S(\mathbf{q},\omega) exhibits nematicity below a transition temperature T nem < T ∗ T_{\rm nem}<T^{*} , and at larger hole doping in the entire pseudogap regime below T ∗ T^{*} .



I
Introduction

A peculiar feature of cuprate high temperature superconductors is their pseudogap behavior above the critical temperature for superconductivity, observed in a broad doping range from the underdoped into the optimally doped regime
Keimer et al. 2015
;
Proust and Taillefer 2019
.
It is characterized by a reduction of the charge carrier concentration, a spin gap, and a reconstructed Fermi surface which in photoemission looks like a collection of Fermi arcs. It is often accompanied by electronic nematicy, breaking the tetragonal symmetry of the crystal.

There is convincing numerical evidence for pseudogap behavior in the two-dimensional Hubbard model, in particular from quantum cluster calculations
Qin et al. 2022
, and fluctuation diagnostics of the contributions to the self-energy has revealed that the pseudogap is generated mainly by antiferromagnetic spin fluctuations
Gunnarsson et al. 2015
.
While the guidance provided by such numerical simulations is very valuable, a deeper understanding of the pseudogap phenomenon and data with a higher momentum resolution remain desirable.

Approximate analytic theories can provide further insights on the structure of the pseudogap state, and information on its fine-structure in momentum space. Early theories of the pseudogap phenomenon were based on weak-coupling expansions, such as Moriya’s theory
Moriya and Ueda 2000
and the two-particle self-consistent approach by Vilk and Tremblay
Vilk and Tremblay 1996
. The Mermin-Wagner theorem on the absence of spin symmetry breaking at finite temperatures is respected in these theories, but the pseudogap forms only for large magnetic correlation lengths and thus in a limited parameter regime, while the numerical results show that strong short-ranged correlations are actually sufficient.
Noteworthy previous theories involving only short-ranged correlations are based on Anderson’s resonanting valence bond (RVB) idea
Lee et al. 2006
, and on dynamical gap generation by umklapp processes, as proposed by Yang, Rice, and Zhang
Yang et al. 2006
.

More recently it was shown that the pseudogap behavior observed in cuprates can be captured by SU(2) gauge theories of fluctuating magnetic order
Sachdev and Chowdhury 2016
;
Chatterjee et al. 2017
;
Scheurer et al. 2018
;
Wu et al. 2018
;
Sachdev et al. 2019
;
Bonetti and Metzner 2022a
. Formally this approach is based on a fractionalization of the electron into a fermionic
chargon
with a pseudospin degree of freedom and a charge neutral
spinon
, that is, a SU(2) matrix providing a space and time dependent local reference frame
Schulz 1995
. The local spin rotations can be parametrized by a SU(2) gauge field, and the fractionalization leads to a gauge redundancy. One can then construct states where the chargons exhibit some sort of magnetic order (Néel, spiral, or stripe), while the spinon fluctuations restore the SU(2) spin symmetry and prevent magnetic long-range order of the physical spin-carrying electrons
Dupuis 2002
;
Borejsza and Dupuis 2004
;
Sachdev et al. 2009
, at least at finite temperatures. Quantities involving only charge degrees of freedom behave essentially as in a conventional magnetically ordered state, and the Fermi surface gets correspondingly reconstructed.
Recently a more sophisticated gauge theory of the pseudogap regime with additional auxiliary degrees of freedom (“ancilla qubits”) was proposed
Zhang and Sachdev 2020a
;
Zhang and Sachdev 2020b
;
Mascot et al. 2022
;
Nikolaenko et al. 2023
;
Bonetti et al. 2024
.

In this paper we analyze the dynamical spin susceptibility as obtained from the SU(2) gauge theory of fluctuating magnetic order in a pseudogap state. Our analysis builds on the formalism of Ref.
Bonetti and Metzner 2022a
, which is designed to quantitatively compute physical observables for a microscopic model such as the two-dimensional Hubbard model. We focus on regions in the phase diagram where the chargons order in a circular spiral or in a Néel state. Pseudogap behavior emerging from stripe states will be analyzed in subsequent work. We present results for the dynamical spin susceptibility
S(\mathbf{q},\omega)
and its static uniform limit, and for the NMR relaxation rate.

The remainder of the article is structured as follows. In Sec. II we review the SU(2) gauge theory of fluctuating spiral/Néel order. General expressions for the electron spin susceptibility are derived in Sec. III, while concrete results are presented and discussed in Sec. IV. A conclusion with a summary and outlook in Sec. V closes the presentation.

II
SU(2) gauge theory

We consider the Hubbard model on a square lattice
Arovas et al. 2022
;
Qin et al. 2022
for the sake of concreteness. Extensions to extended Hubbard models (with non-local interactions) and other lattice geometries are straightforward.
Our analysis is based on the functional integral formalism
Negele and Orland 1987
.
We use natural units so that
\hbar=k_{B}=1
.
The Hubbard action has the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}[c,c^{*}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\beta}\!d\tau\sum_{j,j^{\prime},\sigma}c_{j\sigma}^{*}(\tau)\left[(\partial_{\tau}-\mu)\delta_{jj^{\prime}}+t_{jj^{\prime}}\right]c_{j^{\prime}\sigma}(\tau)

(1)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\beta}\!d\tau\;U\sum_{j}n_{j\uparrow}(\tau)\,n_{j\downarrow}(\tau),

where
c_{j\sigma}(\tau)
and
c_{j\sigma}^{*}(\tau)
are imaginary time Grassmann fields corresponding to the annihilation and creation, respectively, of an electron with spin orientation
\sigma
at site
j
, and

n_{j\sigma}(\tau)=c_{j\sigma}^{*}(\tau)c_{j\sigma}(\tau)
.
The hopping amplitudes
t_{jj^{\prime}}
are translation invariant.
The chemical potential is denoted by
\mu
, and
U>0
is the strength of the Hubbard interaction.
The Hubbard action is invariant under
global
SU(2) spin rotations

c_{j}(\tau)\to\mathcal{U}c_{j}(\tau)
,

c_{j}^{*}(\tau)\to c_{j}^{*}(\tau)\,\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}
,
where
c_{j}(\tau)
and
c^{*}_{j}(\tau)
are two-component spinors composed from
c_{j\sigma}(\tau)
and
c^{*}_{j\sigma}(\tau)
with
\sigma\in\{\uparrow,\downarrow\}
, respectively, while
\mathcal{U}
is an arbitrary SU(2) matrix.

II.1
Electron fractionalization

To separate spin orientation fluctuations from the charge and spin amplitude degrees of freedom, we fractionalize the electron fields as
Schulz 1995
;
Dupuis 2002
;
Borejsza and Dupuis 2004
;
Sachdev et al. 2009

c_{j}(\tau)=R_{j}(\tau)\,\psi_{j}(\tau)\,,\quad c_{j}^{*}(\tau)=\psi_{j}^{*}(\tau)\,R_{j}^{\dagger}(\tau),

(2)

where
R_{j}(\tau)\in\mbox{SU(2)}
, to which we refer as
spinon
, is composed of bosonic fields, and the components
\psi_{js}
of the
chargon
spinor
\psi_{j}
are fermionic.
The factorization in Eq. (
2
) introduces a redundant SU(2) symmetry associated with the pseudospin of the chargons.

Using Eq. (
2
), the Hubbard action can be expressed in terms of the spinon and chargon fields. The quadratic part of Eq. (
1
) can be written as
Borejsza and Dupuis 2004

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{0}[\psi,\psi^{*},R]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\beta}\!d\tau\bigg\{\sum_{j}\psi^{*}_{j}(\tau)\left[\partial_{\tau}-\mu-\phi_{j}(\tau)\right]\psi_{j}(\tau)

(3)

\displaystyle+\,\sum_{j,j^{\prime}}t_{jj^{\prime}}\,\psi_{j}^{*}(\tau)\,e^{iA_{jj^{\prime}}(\tau)}\,\psi_{j^{\prime}}(\tau)\bigg\},

with an SU(2) gauge field
(\phi,A)
related to the spinon rotations by

\phi_{j}(\tau)=-R_{j}^{\dagger}(\tau)\partial_{\tau}R_{j}(\tau)
and

e^{iA_{jj^{\prime}}(\tau)}=R_{j}^{\dagger}(\tau)R_{j^{\prime}}(\tau)
.
Both
\phi_{j}(\tau)
and
A_{jj^{\prime}}(\tau)
are elements of the Lie algebra of SU(2), and can therefore be written as linear combinations of the Pauli matrices
\sigma^{a}
with
a=1,2,3
:

\phi_{j}(\tau)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{a}\phi_{j}^{a}(\tau)\sigma^{a}
and

A_{jj^{\prime}}(\tau)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{a}A_{jj^{\prime}}^{a}(\tau)\sigma^{a}
.
In the interaction part of the Hubbard action (
1
), the SU(2) rotations drop out
Bonetti and Metzner 2022a
, so that

\mathcal{S}_{I}[\psi,\psi^{*},R]=\mathcal{S}_{I}[\psi,\psi^{*}]=U\int_{0}^{\beta}\!d\tau\sum_{j}n^{\psi}_{j\uparrow}(\tau)\,n^{\psi}_{j\downarrow}(\tau),

(4)

with
n^{\psi}_{js}(\tau)=\psi^{*}_{js}(\tau)\,\psi_{js}(\tau)
.
The action
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}_{0}+\mathcal{S}_{I}
represents the Hubbard model action where the physical electrons have been replaced by chargons coupled to an SU(2) gauge field.

II.2
Spinon effective action

Our strategy is to treat the chargons in an approximation that ignores fluctuations of the spin orientation, and treat the latter by the spinon degrees of freedom.
Assigning only long wavelength fluctuations to the rotation matrices
R_{j}(\tau)
, we can assume that they vary slowly in space. Under this assumption a continuum limit and a gradient expansion can be applied. The latter corresponds to an expansion in powers of the gauge fields.

Expanding to second order in the gauge fields, and integrating out the chargon fields, one can derive an effective action for the spinons of the form
Dupuis 2002
;
Bonetti and Metzner 2022a

\mathcal{S}[A]=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{a,b}J_{\mu\nu}^{ab}\int_{q}A_{\mu}^{a}(q)\,A_{\nu}^{b}(-q)\,,

(5)

where
A_{\mu}^{a}(q)
with
\mu\in\{0,x,y\}
and
q=(q_{0},q_{x},q_{y})
is the gauge field in momentum representation.
We use Einstein’s summation convention for repeated space-time indices
\mu
and
\nu
, but not for the SU(2) indices
a
and
b
. The integral is written in a shorthand notation

\int_{q}=T\sum_{q_{0}}\int\frac{d^{2}\mathbf{q}}{(2\pi)^{2}}
, where
q_{0}
is a bosonic Matsubara frequency.
The coefficients
J_{\mu\nu}^{ab}
in Eq. (
5
) are the
spin stiffnesses
associated with the Goldstone modes of the magnetically ordered chargon state.
They can be computed either from the spin susceptibility of the chargon state, or from its SU(2) gauge field response kernel
Bonetti and Metzner 2022a
;
Bonetti and Metzner 2022b
;
Bonetti 2022
;
Goremykin and Katanin 2024
.

Fourier transforming to position space
x=(\mathbf{r},\tau)
, the action (
5
) can be mapped to a non-linear sigma model of the form
Bonetti and Metzner 2022a

\mathcal{S}[\mathcal{R}]=\frac{1}{2}\int dx\,{\rm tr}\left\{\mathcal{P}_{\mu\nu}[\partial_{\mu}\mathcal{R}^{T}(x)][\partial_{\nu}\mathcal{R}(x)]\right\}\,,

(6)

where
\mathcal{R}
is the
adjoint
representation of the SU(2) rotation
R
, defined by

R^{\dagger}\sigma^{a}R=\sum_{b}\mathcal{R}^{ab}\sigma^{b}\,,

(7)

and
\mathcal{P}_{\mu\nu}=\frac{1}{2}{\rm tr}(\mathcal{J}_{\mu\nu})\,I_{3}-\mathcal{J}_{\mu\nu}
, with the
3\times 3
identity matrix
I_{3}
, and the
3\times 3
matrix
\mathcal{J}_{\mu\nu}
formed by the matrix elements
J_{\mu\nu}^{ab}
.

II.3
Chargons

II.3.1
Renormalized mean-field theory

We treat the chargons in a renormalized mean-field theory
Wang et al. 2014
;
Yamase et al. 2016
. Mean-field equations for ordered phases are thereby solved with renormalized instead of bare interactions as input parameters. The renormalized interactions are obtained from a functional renormalization group flow
Metzner et al. 2012
, which takes charge, spin, and pairing channels into account on equal footing. The coupled fluctuations of the various channels have two important effects: i) magnetic and charge interactions are reduced, and ii) d-wave pairing interactions are generated by fluctuations
Metzner et al. 2012
. Since we are interested in the normal (non-superconducting) state, we ignore the pairing instability by assuming that the temperature is above the transition temperature for pairing, or that superconductivity has been suppressed by an external magnetic field.

The effective interactions are momentum and frequency dependent, but these dependences are rather weak for the
two-particle irreducible

Wang et al. 2014
effective magnetic interaction which enter the mean-field equation for the magnetic order parameter. Hence we compute the effective interaction in the magnetic channel at zero frequency and at the wave vector where the magnetic interaction is maximal, and use this as a momentum and frequency independent coupling constant
\bar{U}
.

II.3.2
Spiral magnetic order

Numerous static and dynamical mean-field calculations for the two-dimensional Hubbard model yield planar spiral magnetic order in a sizable portion of the phase diagram
Frésard et al. 1991
;
Chubukov and Frenkel 1992
;
Chubukov and Musaelian 1995
;
Igoshev et al. 2010
;
Bonetti 2020
;
Goremykin and Katanin 2023
.
Choosing the spins to be aligned in the
xy
plane (all planes in spin space are energetically degenerate), the spin expectation value in a spiral state has the time-independent form

\langle\mathbf{S}_{j}^{\psi}(\tau)\rangle=m\left[\cos(\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{j})\,{\bf e}_{x}+\sin(\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{j})\,{\bf e}_{y}\right]\,,

(8)

where
\mathbf{Q}
is a fixed wave vector,
m
is the spin amplitude,
\mathbf{r}_{j}
is the position vector of the lattice site
j
, and
{\bf e}_{\alpha}
are unit vectors with directions
\alpha=x,y,z
.
The components of the pseudospin field of the chargons
\mathbf{S}_{j}^{\psi}(\tau)
can be expressed in terms of the chargon fields as

S^{\psi a}_{j}(\tau)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{s,s^{\prime}}\psi_{js}^{*}(\tau)\sigma_{ss^{\prime}}^{a}\psi_{js^{\prime}}(\tau)\,,

(9)

where
\sigma^{a}
are the Pauli matrices.

Diagonalizing the (renormalized) mean-field Hamiltonian for the spiral state yields two quasiparticle bands of the form

E_{\mathbf{k}}^{\pm}=\frac{1}{2}(\xi_{\mathbf{k}}+\xi_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{Q}})\pm\sqrt{\frac{1}{4}(\xi_{\mathbf{k}}-\xi_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{Q}})^{2}+\Delta_{m}^{2}}\,,

(10)

where
\Delta_{m}
is the energy gap associated with the spiral magnetic order.
Extracting an effective (renormalized) interaction
\bar{U}
from the functional renormalization group flow
Wang et al. 2014
;
Yamase et al. 2016
, the gap is determined by the self-consistency equation

\Delta_{m}={\bar{U}}\,m={\bar{U}}\,\Delta_{m}\int_{\mathbf{k}}\frac{f(E_{\mathbf{k}}^{-})-f(E_{\mathbf{k}}^{+})}{E_{\mathbf{k}}^{+}-E_{\mathbf{k}}^{-}}\,,

(11)

where
f(x)=(e^{x/T}+1)^{-1}
is the Fermi function.
The optimal wave vector
\mathbf{Q}
is determined by minimizing the free energy

F(\mathbf{Q})=-T\int_{\mathbf{k}}\sum_{\ell=\pm}\ln\big[1+e^{-E_{\mathbf{k}}^{\ell}(\mathbf{Q})/T}\big]+{\bar{U}}^{-1}\Delta_{m}^{2}(\mathbf{Q})+\mu(\mathbf{Q})n\,,

(12)

where the chemical potential
\mu(\mathbf{Q})
is determined by keeping the electron density
n
fixed.
For our choice of model parameters, the optimal
\mathbf{Q}
always has one component equal to
\pi
, that is, the wave vector has the form
\mathbf{Q}=(\pi-2\pi\eta,\pi)
, or symmetry related, with
\eta\geq 0
.

It is convenient to introduce rotated fermion fields
Kampf 1996

\begin{split}\tilde{\psi}_{j}(\tau)&=e^{-\frac{i}{2}\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{j}}e^{\frac{i}{2}\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{j}\sigma^{3}}\psi_{j}(\tau)\,,\\
\tilde{\psi}_{j}^{*}(\tau)&=\psi_{j}^{*}(\tau)\,e^{-\frac{i}{2}\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{j}\sigma^{3}}e^{\frac{i}{2}\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{j}}\,.\end{split}

(13)

In this rotated basis, the spins appear aligned ferromagnetically in the
x
direction, so that the system remains translation invariant.
In momentum space, the transformation (
13
) corresponds to

\tilde{\psi}_{\mathbf{k}}=(\tilde{\psi}_{\mathbf{k}\uparrow},\tilde{\psi}_{\mathbf{k}\downarrow})=(\psi_{\mathbf{k}\uparrow},\psi_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{Q}\downarrow})
.
The rotated chargon Green function is diagonal in momentum space (corresponding to translation invariance in real space), and has the form

\widetilde{G}^{-1}(\mathbf{k},ik_{0})=\left(\begin{matrix}ik_{0}-\xi_{\mathbf{k}}&-\Delta_{m}\\
-\Delta_{m}&ik_{0}-\xi_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{Q}}\end{matrix}\right)\,,

(14)

where
k_{0}
denotes a fermionic Matsubara frequency.
Using a partial fraction decomposition, the Green function can be written as
Bonetti and Metzner 2022b

\widetilde{G}(\mathbf{k},ik_{0})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell=\pm}\frac{u_{\mathbf{k}}^{\ell}}{ik_{0}-E_{\mathbf{k}}^{\ell}}\,,

(15)

with the matrix coefficients

u_{\mathbf{k}}^{\ell}=\sigma^{0}+\ell\frac{h_{\mathbf{k}}}{e_{\mathbf{k}}}\sigma^{3}+\ell\frac{\Delta_{m}}{e_{\mathbf{k}}}\sigma^{1}\,,

(16)

where
h_{\mathbf{k}}=\frac{1}{2}(\xi_{\mathbf{k}}-\xi_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{Q}})
and

e_{\mathbf{k}}=\sqrt{h_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}+\Delta_{m}^{2}}
.

II.3.3
Chargon susceptibility

The dynamical pseudospin susceptibility of the chargons is defined as the connected expectation value

\chi_{jj^{\prime}}^{ab}(\tau)=\langle S_{j}^{\psi a}(\tau)S_{j^{\prime}}^{\psi b}(0)\rangle_{c}=\langle S_{j}^{\psi a}(\tau)S_{j^{\prime}}^{\psi b}(0)\rangle-\langle S_{j}^{\psi a}(\tau)\rangle\langle S_{j^{\prime}}^{\psi b}(0)\rangle\,,

We evaluate the spin susceptibility in random phase approximation (RPA), which is a conserving approximation consistent with the mean-field treatment of the free energy and the single-particle Green function.
The spin susceptibility is thereby coupled to the charge susceptibility

Hence, we extend the definition Eq. (

II.4
Spin stiffnesses

In this section we provide the formulae for the pseudospin stiffnesses in the spiral state of the chargons, which are the key parameters entering the non-linear sigma model, Eq. (
6
) for the spinon dynamics. Various derivations of expressions for the stiffnesses can be found in Refs.
Bonetti and Metzner 2022b
;
Bonetti and Metzner 2022a
;
Bonetti 2022
;
Goremykin and Katanin 2024
. They can be obtained either by expanding the inverse spin susceptibility around the Goldstone poles
Bonetti and Metzner 2022b
, or from a suitable low energy-momentum limit of the SU(2) gauge field response kernel
Bonetti and Metzner 2022a
;
Bonetti 2022
;
Goremykin and Katanin 2024
.

The stiffness matrix
\mathcal{J}_{\mu\nu}
is diagonal in the spin indices. For a spiral state aligned in the
xy
plane,

\mathcal{J}_{\mu\nu}={\rm diag}\left(J_{\mu\nu}^{\perp},J_{\mu\nu}^{\perp},J_{\mu\nu}^{\Box}\right),

(27)

where
J_{\mu\nu}^{\Box}
and
J_{\mu\nu}^{\perp}
describe the stiffness of in-plane and out-of-plane fluctuations, respectively. We denote their temporal components
J_{00}^{a}
by
Z^{a}
, and their spatial components by
J_{\alpha\beta}^{a}
with
\alpha,\beta\in\{x,y\}
.
We ignore spatio-temporal components
J_{\alpha 0}^{a}
and
J_{0\beta}^{a}
, which are imaginary and related to Landau damping. For the out-of-plane stiffness they actually vanish, since the out-of-plane Landau damping is of cubic order in momentum and frequency
Bonetti and Metzner 2022b
. The in-plane Landau damping is quadratic in momentum and frequency, and depends also on the ratio between frequency and momentum, so that the coefficient of the quadratic behavior cannot be parametrized by a single number.
We now present expressions for the stiffnesses in the form obtained from an expansion of the susceptibility.

For the spatial in-plane stiffness one obtains

III
Electron spin susceptibility

In this section we derive expressions for the spin susceptibility of the electrons

S_{jj^{\prime}}^{ab}(\tau)=\langle S_{j}^{a}(\tau)S_{j^{\prime}}^{b}(0)\rangle_{c}=\langle S_{j}^{a}(\tau)S_{j^{\prime}}^{b}(0)\rangle\,,

(41)

where
S_{j}^{a}(\tau)=\frac{1}{2}c_{j}^{*}(\tau)\sigma^{a}c_{j}(\tau)
is now the spin field formed from the
electron
fields (instead of chargons).
We consider only the thermally or quantum disordered case, where the disconnected term

\langle S_{j}^{a}(\tau)\rangle\langle S_{j^{\prime}}^{b}(0)\rangle
vanishes.

III.1
Fractionalization

Inserting the electron fractionalization Eq. (
2
) into Eq. (
41
), we obtain

\displaystyle S_{jj^{\prime}}^{ab}(\tau)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\langle\psi_{j}^{*}(\tau)R_{j}^{\dagger}(\tau)\sigma^{a}R_{j}(\tau)\psi_{j}(\tau)\psi_{j^{\prime}}^{*}(0)R_{j^{\prime}}^{\dagger}(0)\sigma^{b}R_{j^{\prime}}(0)\psi_{j^{\prime}}(0)\rangle

(42)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\langle\psi_{j}^{*}(\tau)\mathcal{R}_{j}^{ac}(\tau)\sigma^{c}\psi_{j}(\tau)\psi_{j^{\prime}}^{*}(0)\mathcal{R}_{j^{\prime}}^{bd}(0)\sigma^{d}\psi_{j^{\prime}}(0)\rangle\,,

where in the second step we have passed to the adjoint representation Eq. (
7
). Neglecting correlations between chargons and spinons, we factorize the above expectation value in chargon and spinon expectation values as

S_{jj^{\prime}}^{ab}(\tau)=\langle\mathcal{R}_{j}^{ac}(\tau)\mathcal{R}_{j^{\prime}}^{bd}(0)\rangle\bar{\chi}_{jj^{\prime}}^{cd}(\tau)\,,

(43)

where
\bar{\chi}_{jj^{\prime}}(\tau)
is the pseudospin susceptibility of the chargons as defined in Eq. (
17
), but with the disconnected contributions included.
An analogous factorization approximation has been used in calculations of the single-electron propagator
Scheurer et al. 2018
;
Wu et al. 2018
;
Bonetti and Metzner 2022a
.

III.2
Spinon correlator

We now evaluate the spinon correlator in Eq. (
43
),

\mathcal{D}_{jj^{\prime}}^{acbd}(\tau)=\langle\mathcal{R}_{j}^{ac}(\tau)\mathcal{R}_{j^{\prime}}^{bd}(0)\rangle\,,

(44)

where, in the continuum limit, the expectation value is determined by the spinon action Eq. (
6
).
The SO(3) matrix
\mathcal{R}
can be expressed as a triad of orthonormal unit vectors,

\mathcal{R}=\big({\bf\hat{n}}^{1},{\bf\hat{n}}^{2},{\bf\hat{n}}^{3}\big),

(45)

which are subject to the local constraints

{\bf\hat{n}}_{j}^{c}(\tau)\cdot{\bf\hat{n}}_{j}^{d}(\tau)=\delta_{cd}\,.

(46)

Note that the index labeling the three unit vectors corresponds to the column index of the matrix
\mathcal{R}
.
For a spin-diagonal stiffness matrix
\mathcal{J}_{\mu\nu}
, the spinon action in Eq. (
6
) can then be written as

\mathcal{S}[\mathcal{R}]=\frac{1}{2}\int\!dx\sum_{c}P_{\mu\nu}^{cc}\,[\partial_{\mu}{\bf\hat{n}}^{c}(x)]\cdot\partial_{\nu}{\bf\hat{n}}^{c}(x)\,,

(47)

with Einstein’s summation convention for the repeated space-time indices
\mu
and
\nu
.

Inserting the stiffness matrix (
27
) of a spiral state into the relation

\mathcal{P}_{\mu\nu}=\frac{1}{2}{\rm tr}(\mathcal{J}_{\mu\nu})\,I_{3}-\mathcal{J}_{\mu\nu}
, we obtain the relations

P_{\mu\nu}^{11}=P_{\mu\nu}^{22}={\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}J_{\mu\nu}^{\Box}\,,\;P_{\mu\nu}^{33}=J_{\mu\nu}^{\perp}-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}J_{\mu\nu}^{\Box}\,.

(48)

For the Néel state, Eq. (
40
) yields

P_{\mu\nu}^{11}=J_{\mu\nu}\,,\;P_{\mu\nu}^{22}=P_{\mu\nu}^{33}=0\,.

(49)

In frequency-momentum representation, the spinon correlator defined in Eq. (
44
) is given by

\mathcal{D}^{acbd}(q)=\langle\hat{n}_{a}^{c}(q)\hat{n}_{b}^{d}(-q)\rangle\,.

(50)

Using the CP
1
representation of the non-linear sigma model, we show in Appendix
B
that the propagator has the diagonal form

\mathcal{D}^{acbd}(q)=\delta_{ab}\delta_{cd}\,\mathcal{D}^{c}(q)\,.

(51)

Neglecting the current-current interaction in the CP
1
action, which can be justified in a large
N
limit, all spinon correlators are equal:

\mathcal{D}^{1}=\mathcal{D}^{2}=\mathcal{D}^{3}\equiv\mathcal{D}
. This is also shown in Appendix
B
.
The electron spin susceptibility from Eq. (
43
) can then be written in the form

S_{jj^{\prime}}^{ab}(\tau)=\mathcal{D}_{jj^{\prime}}(\tau)\left[\bar{\chi}_{jj^{\prime}}^{11}(\tau)+\bar{\chi}_{jj^{\prime}}^{22}(\tau)+\bar{\chi}_{jj^{\prime}}^{33}(\tau)\right]\delta_{ab}\,,

(52)

where

\mathcal{D}_{jj^{\prime}}(\tau)=\int_{q}\mathcal{D}(q)\,e^{i\mathbf{q}\cdot(\mathbf{r}_{j}-\mathbf{r}_{j^{\prime}})}e^{-iq_{0}\tau}\,.

(53)

Inserting the spin expectation value of the chargons, Eq. (
8
), into the disconnected contributions to
\bar{\chi}^{aa}(\tau)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\bar{\chi}_{jj^{\prime}}^{11}(\tau)=\chi_{jj^{\prime}}^{11}(\tau)+m^{2}\cos(\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{j})\cos(\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{j^{\prime}})

(54a)

\displaystyle\bar{\chi}_{jj^{\prime}}^{22}(\tau)=\chi_{jj^{\prime}}^{22}(\tau)+m^{2}\sin(\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{j})\sin(\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{j^{\prime}})

(54b)

\displaystyle\bar{\chi}_{jj^{\prime}}^{33}(\tau)=\chi_{jj^{\prime}}^{33}(\tau)\,.

(54c)

Using

\cos(\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{j})\cos(\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{j^{\prime}})+\sin(\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{j})\sin(\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{j^{\prime}})=\cos[\mathbf{Q}\cdot(\mathbf{r}_{j}-\mathbf{r}_{j^{\prime}})]
, Eq. (
52
) yields

S_{jj^{\prime}}^{ab}(\tau)=\mathcal{D}_{jj^{\prime}}(\tau)\left[m^{2}\cos[\mathbf{Q}\cdot(\mathbf{r}_{j}-\mathbf{r}_{j^{\prime}})]+{\rm tr}\,\chi_{jj^{\prime}}(\tau)\right]\delta_{ab}\,,

(55)

with
{\rm tr}\,\chi_{jj^{\prime}}(\tau)=\chi_{jj^{\prime}}^{11}(\tau)+\chi_{jj^{\prime}}^{22}(\tau)+\chi_{jj^{\prime}}^{33}(\tau)
.
This function is manifestly spin SU(2) and translation invariant. The translation invariance follows from the translation invariance of the chargon susceptibility
\tilde{\chi}_{jj^{\prime}}
in the rotated spin basis and the invariance of the trace under a change of basis.
The SU(2) symmetry of the spinons thus restores the broken symmetries of the chargon susceptibility.

We now evaluate the spinon correlator
\mathcal{D}(q)
in a saddle point approximation, which becomes exact in a suitable large
N
limit.
We begin with the Néel case, where the action
\mathcal{S}[\mathcal{R}]
depends only on
{\bf\hat{n}}^{1}
,

\mathcal{S}[\mathcal{R}]=\frac{1}{2}\int dx\,J_{\mu\nu}\,[\partial_{\mu}{\bf\hat{n}}^{1}(x)]\cdot\partial_{\nu}{\bf\hat{n}}^{1}(x)\,.

(56)

The dynamics of
{\bf\hat{n}}^{2}
and
{\bf\hat{n}}^{3}
is exclusively determined by the orthonormality constraints.
The propagator for
{\bf\hat{n}}^{1}
is fully determined by the action Eq. (
56
) and the normalization
{\bf\hat{n}}^{1}(x)\cdot{\bf\hat{n}}^{1}(x)=1
. Implementing the normalization constraint by a Lagrange multiplier field
\lambda(x)
, we obtain the action

\mathcal{S}[\mathcal{R},\lambda]=\frac{1}{2}\int dx\left\{J_{\mu\nu}\,[\partial_{\mu}{\bf\hat{n}}^{1}(x)]\cdot\partial_{\nu}{\bf\hat{n}}^{1}(x)+i\lambda(x)\left[{\bf\hat{n}}^{1}(x)\cdot{\bf\hat{n}}^{1}(x)-1\right]\right\}\,.

(57)

In a saddle point approximation we replace the fluctuating field
\lambda(x)
by a space-time independent number
\lambda
. The propagator for
{\bf\hat{n}}^{1}
can then be read off directly from the action as

\langle\hat{n}_{a}^{1}(q)\hat{n}_{b}^{1}(-q)\rangle=\delta_{ab}\mathcal{D}^{1}(q)=\frac{\delta_{ab}}{Z(m_{s}^{2}+q_{0}^{2})+J\mathbf{q}^{2}}\,,

(58)

with
m_{s}^{2}=i\lambda/Z
. Since
\mathcal{D}^{1}=\mathcal{D}^{2}=\mathcal{D}^{3}\equiv\mathcal{D}
, we have found

\mathcal{D}(q)=\frac{1}{Z(m_{s}^{2}+q_{0}^{2})+J\mathbf{q}^{2}}\,.

(59)

The dynamically generated spinon mass
m_{s}
is determined by the normalization condition

\langle{\bf\hat{n}}^{1}(x)\cdot{\bf\hat{n}}^{1}(x)\rangle=1
, corresponding to

\int_{q}\mathcal{D}(q)=\int_{q}\frac{1}{Z(m_{s}^{2}+q_{0}^{2})+J\mathbf{q}^{2}}=\frac{1}{3}\,.

(60)

The above integral is ultraviolet divergent. It thus requires an ultraviolet cutoff which we will specify below.

We now turn to the case of spiral order. Neglecting the current-current interaction in the CP
1
representation (
97
) of the non-linear sigma model, as before,
the action becomes independent of
J^{\Box}
. Hence, setting
J^{\Box}=0
doesn’t affect the results obtained from the CP
1
representation, and the saddle point solution for the spiral case has the same form as for the Néel case.
The action (
47
) obtained from the adjoint representation of the rotation matrices can also be brought to the Néel form by setting
J^{\Box}=0
, such that

\mathcal{S}[\mathcal{R}]=\frac{1}{2}\int dx\,J_{\mu\nu}^{\perp}\,[\partial_{\mu}{\bf\hat{n}}^{3}(x)]\cdot\partial_{\nu}{\bf\hat{n}}^{3}(x)\,.

(61)

This action has the same form as the action for the Néel case, Eq. (
56
), with
{\bf\hat{n}}^{1}
replaced by
{\bf\hat{n}}^{3}
. It depends only on the out-of-plane stiffness
J^{\perp}
. Applying the same saddle point approximation as for the Néel case above, we obtain the spinon correlator

\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}^{1}=\mathcal{D}^{2}=\mathcal{D}^{3}
as

\mathcal{D}(q)=\frac{1}{Z^{\perp}(m_{s}^{2}+q_{0}^{2})+J_{\alpha\beta}^{\perp}q_{\alpha}q_{\beta}}\,,

(62)

where the spinon mass
m_{s}
is again determined by the condition
\int_{q}\mathcal{D}(q)=\frac{1}{3}
.

The spinon correlators in the Néel and in the spiral state have thus the same form. The same is true for the spinon propagator
D(q)=\langle z_{\sigma}^{*}(q)z_{\sigma}(q)\rangle
on the saddle point level in the CP
1
representation
Bonetti and Metzner 2022a
, see Appendix
C
.

Fourier transforming Eq. (
55
) yields the electron spin susceptibility as a function of momentum and (Matsubara) energy,
S^{ab}(q)=\delta_{ab}S(q)
, with

\displaystyle S(\mathbf{q},iq_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}m^{2}\left[\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{Q},iq_{0})+\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{Q},iq_{0})\right]

(63)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle T\sum_{q^{\prime}_{0}}\int_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime},iq^{\prime}_{0})\,{\rm tr}\chi(\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{q}^{\prime},iq_{0}-iq^{\prime}_{0})\,.

IV.2
Electron spin susceptibility

We now evaluate the electron spin susceptibility
S(\mathbf{q},\omega)
. More specifically, we will compute and discuss the spin structure factor, the static spin susceptibility, and the NMR relaxation rate. Most expressions have the same form for the cases of Néel and spiral order. In those expressions, we denote the spatial and temporal out-of-plane stiffnesses in case of spiral order by the same symbols
J
and
Z
, respectively, as the stiffnesses in the Néel case.

We impose an ultraviolet cutoff
\Lambda
on the support of the spinon propagators in momentum space by multiplying it with the Gaussian cutoff function
e^{-\mathbf{q}^{2}/(2\Lambda^{2})}
. The choice of the cutoff function is quite arbitrary, but
\Lambda
is in any case much smaller than
|\mathbf{Q}|
.
For the sake of consistency, the same cutoff has to be chosen in the evaluation of the sum rule
\int_{q}\mathcal{D}(q)=\frac{1}{3}
, which determines the spinon mass, and for the convolution of chargons and spinons as in Eq. (
63
).
The contributions from the disconnected chargon susceptibility (the first line in Eq. (
63
)) to
{\rm Im}S(\mathbf{q},0)
vanish rapidly for
\mathbf{q}
outside circles of radius
\Lambda
around
\mathbf{Q}
and
-\mathbf{Q}
. In particlular, they are negligible for small
\mathbf{q}
.
We choose
\Lambda=0.3
in all the following results. With this choice, the ground state remains magnetically ordered at half-filling (implying
m_{s}=0
), and becomes quantum disordered (with
m_{s}>0
) for any degree of hole doping considered in this work.

IV.2.1
Spin structure factor

The dynamical spin structure factor, which can be measured in particular by inelastic neutron scattering, is essentially (up to a Bose factor) determined by the imaginary part of the dynamical spin susceptibility.
Continuing Eq. (
63
) to real frequencies we obtain

is the Bose function.
The imaginary part of the spinon correlator

(67)

(68)

Performing the frequency integral in Eq. (

{\rm Im}S_{c}(\mathbf{q},\omega)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{s=\pm}\int_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}\frac{s}{Z\omega_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}}{\rm Im}\,{\rm tr}\chi(\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{q}^{\prime},\omega-s\omega_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}})\left[b(s\omega_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}})-b(s\omega_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}-\omega)\right]\,.

At zero temperature the difference of Bose functions in Eq. (

{\rm Im}S_{c}(\mathbf{q},\omega)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}\frac{1}{Z\omega_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}}{\rm Im}\,{\rm tr}\chi(\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{q}^{\prime},\omega-\omega_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}})\,\Theta(\omega-\omega_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}})\quad\mbox{for}\quad\omega\geq 0\,,

and
{\rm Im}S_{c}(\mathbf{q},-\omega)=-{\rm Im}S_{c}(\mathbf{q},\omega)
.
Hence,
{\rm Im}S(\mathbf{q},\omega)
vanishes for all
|\omega|<m_{s}
, that is, the electron spin susceptibility inherits the
spin gap
from the spinons.

IV.2.3
NMR relaxation rate

The relaxation rate of nuclear spins due to the coupling to the electron spins can be obtained from the imaginary part of the spin susceptibility in the form

T_{1}^{-1}=\lim_{\omega\to 0}\frac{T}{\omega}\int_{\mathbf{q}}F(\mathbf{q})\,{\rm Im}S(\mathbf{q},\omega)\,,

(73)

where
F(\mathbf{q})
is a form factor related to the coupling between nuclear and electron spins. Note that we use natural units so that
\hbar=k_{B}=1
.
We will see that the dominant contributions to
T_{1}^{-1}
come from momenta near
\pm\mathbf{Q}
, so that we can approximate
F(\mathbf{q})
by a momentum independent number
F_{Q}
.

Due to the spinon mass, the first contribution to
{\rm Im}S(\mathbf{q},\omega)
in Eq. (
65
), originating from the disconnected chargon susceptibility, vanishes for
\omega\to 0
.
Inserting
{\rm Im}\,S_{c}(\mathbf{q},\omega)
from Eq. (
69
), we obtain

T_{1}^{-1}=\lim_{\omega\to 0}\frac{T}{2\omega}F_{Q}\sum_{s=\pm}\int_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}\frac{s}{Z\omega_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}}{\rm Im}\,{\rm tr}\chi_{\rm loc}(\omega-s\omega_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}})\left[b(s\omega_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}})-b(s\omega_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}-\omega)\right]\,,

(74)

with the trace of the local susceptibility

{\rm tr}\chi_{\rm loc}(\omega)=\int_{\mathbf{q}}{\rm tr}\chi(\mathbf{q},\omega)
. For
\mathbf{q}\neq\pm\mathbf{Q}
, the imaginary part of
{\rm tr}\chi(\mathbf{q},\omega)
vanishes linearly for
\omega\to 0
. However, there is a larger contribution to
{\rm Im}\,{\rm tr}\chi_{\rm loc}(\omega)
from the Goldstone poles at
\mathbf{q}=\pm\mathbf{Q}
. Here, we need to discuss the spiral and the Néel cases separately.

For
\mathbf{q}
near
\pm\mathbf{Q}
and low frequencies, the chargon spin susceptibilities in the spiral state exhibit the Goldstone poles
Bonetti and Metzner 2022b

\chi^{a}(\mathbf{q},\omega)\sim\frac{m^{2}/2}{J_{\alpha\beta}^{a}(q_{\alpha}\mp Q_{\alpha})(q_{\beta}\mp Q_{\beta})-Z^{a}(\omega+i0^{+})^{2}}\,,

(75)

with
a\in\{\Box,\perp\}
,
where we have defined
\chi^{\Box}=\chi^{11}+\chi^{22}
and
\chi^{\perp}=\chi^{33}
.
The imaginary parts of these poles are given by

{\rm Im}\chi^{a}(\mathbf{q},\omega)\sim\frac{\pi}{4}\frac{m^{2}}{Z^{a}\Omega_{\mathbf{q}\mp\mathbf{Q}}^{a}}\left[\delta(\omega-\Omega_{\mathbf{q}\mp\mathbf{Q}}^{a})-\delta(\omega+\Omega_{\mathbf{q}\mp\mathbf{Q}}^{a})\right]\,,

(76)

where

\Omega_{\mathbf{q}}^{a}=\sqrt{J_{\alpha\beta}^{a}q_{\alpha}q_{\beta}/Z^{a}}\,.

(77)

Performing the elementary momentum integration, we obtain the contribution from the Goldstone modes to the imaginary part of the local chargon susceptibility

{\rm Im}\chi_{\rm loc}^{a}(\omega)=\frac{m^{2}}{4\sqrt{\det J^{a}}}s(\omega)\,,

(78)

where
s(\omega)
is the sign of
\omega
, and
J^{a}
is the
2\times 2
matrix composed of
J_{\alpha\beta}^{a}
.
This Goldstone contribution remains finite for
\omega\to 0
, and thus dominates over all other contributions, which are linear in the frequency.

Inserting
{\rm Im}\chi_{\rm loc}^{a}(\omega)
from Eq. (
78
) into Eq. (
74
), and performing the sum over
s=\pm
, we obtain

T_{1}^{-1}=\lim_{\omega\to 0}\frac{T}{2\omega}F_{Q}\sum_{a\in\{\Box,\perp\}}\int_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}\frac{m^{2}}{4Z^{a}\sqrt{\det J^{a}}\,\omega_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}}\left[b(\omega_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}-\omega)-b(\omega_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}+\omega)\right]\,.

(79)

Using

\lim_{\omega\to 0}\omega^{-1}\left[b(\omega_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}-\omega)-b(\omega_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}+\omega)\right]=-2b^{\prime}(\omega_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}})\,,

(80)

and the spinon density of states

N_{s}(\omega)=\int_{\mathbf{q}}\delta(\omega-\omega_{\mathbf{q}})=\frac{Z^{\perp}}{\sqrt{\det J^{\perp}}}\frac{\omega}{2\pi}\Theta(\omega-m_{s})\,,

(81)

the momentum integral in Eq. (
79
) can be carried out exactly, yielding the remarkably simple formula

T_{1}^{-1}=TF_{Q}\frac{m^{2}\,b(m_{s})}{4\pi\sqrt{\det J^{\perp}}}\sum_{a\in\{\Box,\perp\}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det J^{a}}}\,.

(82)

In case of Néel order, there is no Goldstone pole in
\chi^{11}
, while
\chi^{\perp}=\chi^{22}+\chi^{33}
has the form

\chi^{\perp}(\mathbf{q},\omega)\sim\frac{2m^{2}}{J(\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{Q})^{2}-Z(\omega+i0^{+})^{2}}\,,

(83)

with
\mathbf{Q}=(\pi,\pi)
. The imaginary part of the local chargon susceptibility is then obtained from this single pole as

{\rm Im}\,{\rm tr}\chi_{\rm loc}(\omega)=\frac{m^{2}}{2\sqrt{\det J}}s(\omega)\,,

(84)

and the NMR relaxation rate becomes

T_{1}^{-1}=TF_{Q}\frac{m^{2}\,b(m_{s})}{2\pi\det J}\,.

(85)

V
Conclusions

We have computed the electron spin susceptibility in the pseudogap regime of the two-dimensional Hubbard model at a moderate interaction strength. Approximate expressions for the dynamical spin susceptibility
S(\mathbf{q},\omega)
have been derived in the framework of a recently formulated SU(2) gauge theory of fluctuating magnetic order
Bonetti and Metzner 2022a
.
The gauge structure results from a fractionalization of the electron operators in fermionic chargons with a pseudospin degree of freedom and bosonic spinons
Schulz 1995
;
Dupuis 2002
;
Borejsza and Dupuis 2004
;
Sachdev et al. 2009
. The chargons are treated in a renormalized mean-field theory. They order in a Néel or spiral magnetic state in a broad doping range around half-filling below a doping-dependent transition temperature
T^{*}
. Fluctuations of the spin orientation are captured by a non-linear sigma model. The spin stiffnesses parametrizing the action of the sigma model are obtained from a renormalized RPA for the chargon susceptibility in the magnetic phases
Bonetti and Metzner 2022b
. The non-linear sigma model has been solved in a saddle point approximation.
Previous applications of the above
Bonetti and Metzner 2022a
and related
Sachdev and Chowdhury 2016
;
Chatterjee et al. 2017
;
Scheurer et al. 2018
;
Wu et al. 2018
;
Sachdev et al. 2019
gauge theories of fluctuating magnetic order have focused on the single-electron propagator, yielding a low-energy fermionic excitation spectrum concentrated on Fermi arcs.

The SU(2) spin symmetry is conserved by the spinon fluctuations at any finite temperature, in agreement with the Mermin-Wagner theorem. Whether the ground state is magnetically ordered or quantum disordered depends on the size on the stiffnesses, and on an ultraviolet cutoff required to regularize the non-linear sigma model. This cutoff remains a free parameter which we cannot compute within our theory.
Our results for the spin susceptibility share many features with experimental observations in the pseudogap regime of cuprates:
i) The dynamical spin susceptibility has a spin gap at finite temperatures, which suppresses
{\rm Im}S(\mathbf{q},\omega)
at temperatures and energies below this gap,
ii)
{\rm Im}S(\mathbf{q},\omega)
exhibits nematicity below a transition temperature
T_{\rm nem}
,
iii) The static uniform spin susceptibility
\kappa_{s}
decreases substantially upon lowering the temperature below
T^{*}
, and
iv) the NMR relaxation rate
T_{1}^{-1}
vanishes exponentially for
T\to 0
if the ground state is quantum disordered.

At low hole doping the chargons undergo Néel order at
T^{*}
, which transforms into spiral order at a lower transition temperature
T_{\rm nem}<T^{*}
. By contrast, at larger hole doping, for our parameters above
p=0.1
(see Fig.
1
), the chargons enter into a spiral state directly at
T^{*}
. Hence, in the former case the nematic transition occurs at a temperature below
T^{*}
, and in the former right at
T^{*}
. This dichotomy is in perfect agreement with experimental observations in cuprate compounds: A nematic transition right at
T^{*}
has been detected in underdoped YBCO with hole concentrations between
p=0.11
and
p=0.15

Sato et al. 2017
, and also in moderately underdoped
\rm HgBa_{2}CuO_{4+\delta}

Murayama et al. 2019
, while a nematic transition at a temperature
T_{\rm nem}
well below
T^{*}
has been found in strongly underdoped YBCO
Hinkov et al. 2008
.

While the qualitative agreement of our results with the pseudogap behavior observed in experiments is encouraging, the finding of a finite residual uniform static spin susceptibility in the presence of a hard spin gap is suspicious. Indeed, the uniform susceptibility obtained from our approximations remains finite even at finite frequencies, violating an exact property following from the SU(2) spin symmetry
Bonetti 2022
. This implies that our theory is not conserving in the sense of Baym and Kadanoff
Baym and Kadanoff 1961
. The source of this deficiency is probably the factorization of the electron spin susceptibility in chargon and spinon expectation values.

Our treatment of the chargons via a renormalized static mean-field theory is applicable only for weak or moderate interaction strengths. It is important to realize that pseudogap behavior does not require particularly strong interactions. An extension of our theory to strong coupling is possible by computing the chargon phase diagram and the corresponding spin stiffnesses from dynamical mean-field theory.

B.2
Spinon correlator

To evaluate the spinon correlator
\langle\mathcal{R}_{j}^{ac}(\tau)\mathcal{R}_{j^{\prime}}^{bd}(0)\rangle
in Eq. (
43
), we now use the CP
1
representation of the rotation matrices, Eqs. (
45
) and (
94
). The spinon correlator thus becomes a 4-point function of Schwinger bosons.
We consider only the case without a spinon condensate, which applies at any non-zero temperature, and also to a quantum disordered ground state. In this case only “normal” expectation values with an equal number of
z
and
z^{*}
bosons contribute, that is, only

\langle\hat{\bf n}_{j}^{\pm}(\tau)\hat{\bf n}_{j^{\prime}}^{\mp}(0)\rangle
and

\langle\hat{\bf n}_{j}^{3}(\tau)\hat{\bf n}_{j^{\prime}}^{3}(0)\rangle
need to be computed.

The SU(2) symmetry imposes the following general spin structure on normal boson 4-point functions:

\langle z_{s_{1^{\prime}}}^{*}(1^{\prime})z_{s_{1}}^{\phantom{*}}(1)z_{s_{2^{\prime}}}^{*}(2^{\prime})z_{s_{2}}^{\phantom{*}}(2)\rangle=\Gamma(1^{\prime}2^{\prime};12)\delta_{s_{1}s_{1^{\prime}}}\delta_{s_{2}s_{2^{\prime}}}+\Gamma(2^{\prime}1^{\prime};12)\delta_{s_{1}s_{2^{\prime}}}\delta_{s_{2}s_{1^{\prime}}}\,,

(105)

where
1,2,1^{\prime},2^{\prime}
is a shorthand for the space and time variables.
This symmetry relation is the bosonic analogue of a well-known property of the fermion 4-point vertex in SU(2) symmetric Fermi systems
Salmhofer and Honerkamp 2001
;
Eberlein and Metzner 2010
.
Using this relation, the contributing expectation values can be written as

\displaystyle\langle n_{a}^{+}(1)n_{b}^{-}(2)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle z_{s_{1^{\prime}}}^{*}(1)z_{s_{1}}^{*}(1)z_{s_{2^{\prime}}}^{\phantom{*}}(2)z_{s_{2}}^{\phantom{*}}(2)\rangle(i\sigma^{2}\sigma^{a})_{s_{1^{\prime}}s_{1}}^{\dagger}(i\sigma^{2}\sigma^{b})_{s_{2^{\prime}}s_{2}}^{\phantom{{\dagger}}}

(106)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Gamma(11;22)\left(\delta_{s_{1}s_{2}}\delta_{s_{1^{\prime}}s_{2^{\prime}}}+\delta_{s_{1}s_{2^{\prime}}}\delta_{s_{1^{\prime}}s_{2}}\right)(\sigma^{a}\sigma^{2})_{s_{1^{\prime}}s_{1}}(\sigma^{2}\sigma^{b})_{s_{2^{\prime}}s_{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4\Gamma(11;22)\delta_{ab}\,,

and, analogously,
\langle n_{a}^{-}(1)n_{b}^{+}(2)\rangle=4\Gamma(22;11)\delta_{ab}
, such that

\displaystyle\langle n_{a}^{1}(1)n_{b}^{1}(2)\rangle=\langle n_{a}^{2}(1)n_{b}^{2}(2)\rangle=\left[\Gamma(11;22)+\Gamma(22;11)\right]\delta_{ab}\,,

(107a)

\displaystyle\langle n_{2}^{a}(1)n_{1}^{b}(2)\rangle=-\langle n_{1}^{a}(1)n_{2}^{b}(2)\rangle=i\left[\Gamma(11;22)-\Gamma(22;11)\right]\delta_{ab}=0\,.

(107b)

The last term vanishes since
\Gamma(11;22)=\Gamma(22;11)
due to time reversal and spatial reflection symmetry.
For the third component we obtain

\displaystyle\langle n_{a}^{3}(1)n_{b}^{3}(2)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle z_{s_{1^{\prime}}}^{*}(1)z_{s_{1}}^{\phantom{*}}(1)z_{s_{2^{\prime}}}^{*}(2)z_{s_{2}}^{\phantom{*}}(2)\rangle\sigma_{s_{1^{\prime}}s_{1}}^{a}\sigma_{s_{2^{\prime}}s_{2}}^{b}

(108)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\Gamma(12;12)\delta_{s_{1}s_{1^{\prime}}}\delta_{s_{2}s_{2^{\prime}}}+\Gamma(21;12)\delta_{s_{1}s_{2^{\prime}}}\delta_{s_{2}s_{1^{\prime}}}\right]\sigma_{s_{1^{\prime}}s_{1}}^{a}\sigma_{s_{2^{\prime}}s_{2}}^{b}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Gamma(12;12){\rm tr}(\sigma^{a}){\rm tr}(\sigma^{b})+\Gamma(21;12){\rm tr}(\sigma^{a}\sigma^{b})=2\Gamma(21;12)\delta_{ab}\,.

The relations derived above can be summarized as

\mathcal{D}^{acbd}(1,2)=\langle n_{a}^{c}(1)n_{b}^{d}(2)\rangle=\delta_{ab}\delta_{cd}\,\mathcal{D}^{c}(1,2)\,,

(109)

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{1}(1,2)=\mathcal{D}^{2}(1,2)=2\Gamma(11;22)\,,

(110a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{3}(1,2)=2\Gamma(21;12)\,.

(110b)

Due to the symmetry relation Eq. (
105
), we can express
\Gamma(11;22)
and
\Gamma(21;12)
by various, at first sight distinct expectation values.
Inserting
1=(j,\tau)
and
2=(j^{\prime},0)
, we choose

\displaystyle\Gamma(11;22)=\langle z_{j\uparrow}^{*}(\tau)z_{j\downarrow}^{*}(\tau)z_{j^{\prime}\downarrow}^{\phantom{*}}(0)z_{j^{\prime}\uparrow}^{\phantom{*}}(0)\rangle\equiv\Gamma_{jj^{\prime}}(\tau)\,,

(111a)

\displaystyle\Gamma(21;12)=\langle z_{j\uparrow}^{*}(\tau)z_{j\downarrow}^{\phantom{*}}(\tau)z_{j^{\prime}\downarrow}^{*}(0)z_{j^{\prime}\uparrow}^{\phantom{*}}(0)\rangle\equiv\Gamma^{\prime}_{jj^{\prime}}(\tau)\,.

(111b)

An advantage of this choice is that in a perturbative expansion of these expectation values no disconnected Feynman diagrams contribute, since the spinon propagators are spin conserving.

Inserting the above results for the spinon correlator into the factorized susceptibility, Eq. (
43
), we obtain

S_{jj^{\prime}}^{ab}(\tau)=2\Gamma_{jj^{\prime}}(\tau)\left[\bar{\chi}_{jj^{\prime}}^{11}(\tau)+\bar{\chi}_{jj^{\prime}}^{22}(\tau)\right]\delta_{ab}+2\Gamma^{\prime}_{jj^{\prime}}(\tau)\bar{\chi}_{jj^{\prime}}^{33}(\tau)\delta_{ab}\,.

(112)

This result is manifestly spin rotation invariant. The SU(2) symmetry of the spinons thus restores the broken symmetry of the chargon susceptibility.
Inserting the disconnected contributions to
\bar{\chi}^{aa}(\tau)
as in Eq. (
54
), we find, for a spiral state aligned in the
xy
plane,

S_{jj^{\prime}}^{ab}(\tau)=2\Gamma_{jj^{\prime}}(\tau)\left[\chi_{jj^{\prime}}^{11}(\tau)+\chi_{jj^{\prime}}^{22}(\tau)+m^{2}\cos(\mathbf{Q}(\mathbf{r}_{j}-\mathbf{r}_{j^{\prime}}))\right]\delta_{ab}+2\Gamma^{\prime}_{jj^{\prime}}(\tau)\chi_{jj^{\prime}}^{33}(\tau)\delta_{ab}\,.

(113)

S_{jj^{\prime}}^{ab}(\tau)
is translation invariant, unlike
\bar{\chi}_{jj^{\prime}}^{11}
and
\bar{\chi}_{jj^{\prime}}^{22}
.
For the disconnected contributions this is obvious from Eq. (
113
).
For the connected parts, the translation invariance follows from the translation invariance of the chargon susceptibility
\tilde{\chi}_{jj^{\prime}}
in the rotated spin basis, in combination with
\chi_{jj^{\prime}}^{33}=\tilde{\chi}_{jj^{\prime}}^{33}
and the invariance of the trace of the susceptibility under a change of basis.

It is instructive to see what happens if we were to average only over
global
, space and time independent, rotations in Eq. (
43
). In this case the spinon correlators in Eq. (
111
) can be calculated exactly.
One can show directly via a complex integration over
z_{\uparrow}
and
z_{\downarrow}
that
\Gamma_{jj^{\prime}}(\tau)=\Gamma^{\prime}_{jj^{\prime}}(\tau)=\frac{1}{6}
for global rotations, so that

S_{jj^{\prime}}^{ab}(\tau)=\frac{1}{3}{\rm tr}\left[\bar{\chi}_{jj^{\prime}}(\tau)\right]\delta_{ab}
,
which is the intuitively expected result.

The quadratic part of the CP
1
action (
97
) is symmetric under
z_{\downarrow}\leftrightarrow z_{\downarrow}^{*}
. Moreover, the transformation
z_{\downarrow}\leftrightarrow z_{\downarrow}^{*}
transforms
\Gamma_{jj^{\prime}}(\tau)
into
\Gamma^{\prime}_{jj^{\prime}}(\tau)
, and vice versa. Hence, neglecting the current-current interaction in Eq. (
97

\Gamma_{jj^{\prime}}(\tau)=\Gamma^{\prime}_{jj^{\prime}}(\tau)
.

Saddle point solution in CP

As an alternative to the saddle point solution of the non-linear sigma model in the adjoint representation, as used in the main text, here we discuss another saddle point solution, which is based on the

1
representation of the model
Auerbach 1994
;
Chubukov et al. 1994

C.1
Large
N
limit and saddle point

The current-current interaction in Eq. (
97
) can be decoupled by a Hubbard-Stratonovich transformation with a U(1) gauge field
\mathcal{A}_{\mu}
, and the constraint (
95
) can be implemented by a Lagrange multiplier field
\lambda
. The resulting action describes the massive
\rm CP^{1}
model
Azaria et al. 1995

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\text{CP}^{1}}[z,z^{*},\mathcal{A}_{\mu},\lambda]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int dx\Big[2J^{\perp}_{\mu\nu}(D_{\mu}z)^{*}(D_{\nu}z)

(114)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}M_{\mu\nu}\mathcal{A}_{\mu}\mathcal{A}_{\nu}+i\lambda(z^{*}z-1)\Big]\,,\hskip 14.22636pt

where
D_{\mu}=\partial_{\mu}-i\mathcal{A}_{\mu}
is the covariant derivative. We have suppressed the space-time dependence of the fields to shorten the expression. The coefficients
M_{\mu\nu}
are the matrix elements of the mass tensor of the U(1) gauge field,

{\rm M}=4\left[({\rm J}^{\Box})^{-1}-({\rm J}^{\perp})^{-1}\right]^{-1}\,,

(115)

where
{\rm J}^{\Box}
and
{\rm J}^{\perp}
are the stiffness tensors built from the matrix elements

J_{\mu\nu}^{\Box}
and
J_{\mu\nu}^{\perp}
, respectively.
In case of Néel order this mass tensor vanishes and the CP
1
action is U(1) gauge invariant.

To perform a large
N
expansion, we extend the two-component field
z=(z_{\uparrow},z_{\downarrow})
to an
N
-component field
z=(z_{1},\dots,z_{N})
, and rescale it by a factor
\sqrt{N/2}
so that it now satisfies the local constraint

z^{*}(x)z(x)=\sum_{\sigma=1}^{N}z^{*}_{\sigma}(x)z_{\sigma}(x)=\frac{N}{2}\,.

(116)

To obtain a nontrivial limit
N\to\infty
, we rescale also the stiffnesses
J^{\perp}_{\mu\nu}
and
J^{\Box}_{\mu\nu}
by a factor
2/N
, yielding the massive CP
N-1

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\text{CP}^{N-1}}[z,z^{*},\mathcal{A}_{\mu},\lambda]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int dx\Big[2J_{\mu\nu}^{\perp}(D_{\mu}z)^{*}(D_{\nu}z)

(117)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{N}{4}M_{\mu\nu}\mathcal{A}_{\mu}\mathcal{A}_{\nu}+i\lambda\left(z^{*}z-N/2\right)\Big].\qquad

In the large
N
limit, the partition function of the CP
N-1
model is dominated by a saddle point for the fields
\mathcal{A}
and
\lambda

\mathcal{A}_{\mu}(x)=0

\lambda(x)=\lambda

;
Azaria et al. 1995
.
One can then read off the spinon propagator directly from the action (
117

D(q)=\langle z_{\sigma}^{*}(q)z_{\sigma}(q)\rangle=\frac{1}{2Z^{\perp}q_{0}^{2}+2J_{\alpha\beta}^{\perp}q_{\alpha}q_{\beta}+2Z^{\perp}m_{s}^{2}}\,,

(118)

where
2m_{s}^{2}=i\lambda/Z^{\perp}
.
The Lagrange multiplier
\lambda
and thus the spinon mass
m_{s}
are fixed by taking the average of the constraint, leading to
\langle z^{*}(x)z(x)\rangle=N/2
, and thus

\int_{q}D(q)=\frac{1}{2}
.
This relation for the spinon mass has the same structure as the corresponding condition

\int_{q}\mathcal{D}(q)=\frac{1}{3}
with
\mathcal{D}(q)
from Eq. (
62

C.2

We still need to evaluate the correlation functions

Ultimately we are interested in the Fourier transform

\Pi^{s}(\mathbf{q},iq_{0})=T\sum_{q^{\prime}_{0}}\int_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}D(\mathbf{q}^{\prime},iq^{\prime}_{0})D(\mathbf{q}^{\prime}+\mathbf{q},iq^{\prime}_{0}+iq_{0})\,,
