Salvage of a 5‐Year Neglected Distal Femur Nonunion in a Refugee: A Case Report
Authors: Brian Prigmore, Mackenzie Kelly, Graham J. DeKeyser, Darin M. Friess, Zachary M. Working
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
CaseA 38‐year‐old woman was found to have a closed, chronically displaced left distal femur fracture sustained 5 years prior while living in South America. Prompt surgical intervention in her home country was inaccessible, leading to non‐union, functional impairment, leg shortening, and soft tissue contractures. Dual‐plate fixation, bone grafting, and quadricepsplasty were performed and the patient′s function and mobility were restored to a degree commensurate with daily requirements.ConclusionChronically displaced distal femur fractures with nonunion, shortening, and soft tissue contractures can be safely and effectively managed with dual‐plating, bone grafting, and quadricepsplasty.

1. Introduction

Distal femur fractures are relatively common injuries that occur from low‐energy mechanisms in elderly patients or high‐energy mechanisms in younger patients [1, 2]. Prompt treatment is critical given the high morbidity associated with these fractures and the functional restrictions they may impose. Open reduction internal fixation (ORIF) is the mainstay of treatment, though nonoperative management may be considered for stable, minimally displaced, nonarticular fractures [3]. Various ORIF techniques can be employed, including fixed‐angle blade plates, sliding barrel condylar plates, condylar buttress plates, locking plates, or intramedullary (IM) nails. The technique performed is determined by fracture configuration and surgeon preference, but, regardless, it should restore length, alignment, and rotation [1, 3]. After ORIF, nonunion rates of distal femur fractures are as high as 13.8%, with rates influenced by fracture characteristics (e.g., comminution, bone loss), patient comorbidities (e.g., diabetes, anemia), soft tissue/vascular injuries, and implant failure [4–6].

Operative management of femur fractures that have progressed to nonunion can be particularly challenging due to the possibility of distorted anatomy, soft tissue contractures, bone loss, arthrofibrosis, and neurovascular entrapment, which can all challenge safe restoration of the normal mechanical axis and complicate postoperative rehabilitation [7, 8]. Previous studies have shown that the ideal fixation construct for most distal femur nonunions is dual‐column plating with bone grafting, which has shown superior union rates compared to single‐column plating alone, IM nailing, and bone grafting alone [9, 10]. Additionally, if contractures and arthrofibrosis are not adequately addressed during non‐union surgery, they can severely limit postoperative range of motion (ROM) and overall recovery of the patient. Various techniques may improve knee ROM in this scenario, namely manipulation under anesthesia, lysis of adhesions, or quadricepsplasty [11]. Quadricepsplasty, using Judet, Thompson, or modified methods, is especially useful for severe extension contractures where release of soft tissue constituents is necessary to restore motion, reduce risk of quadriceps injury, and prevent knee joint extensor lag [12]. Regardless of the quadricepsplasty technique chosen, this procedure is known to yield functional improvements in patients with extension contractures and may be necessary to perform during ORIF of chronically displaced fractures [8].

In this case report, we highlight our approach to a distal femur nonunion with notable soft tissue contractures and arthrofibrosis using combined dual‐plating, bone grafting, and quadricepsplasty, adding to a growing body of literature that supports the use of dual‐plate fixation in the management of this challenging‐to‐treat fracture pattern.

2. Case Presentation

A 38‐year‐old woman was discovered in a refugee center to have a chronically displaced left distal femur fracture that had progressed to non‐union. Her injury, a closed fracture, occurred 5 years prior after being pinned between two trucks in a severe trauma while living in another country. Despite her injury severity, she was unable to access surgical care in her home country. The first medical facility she visited was unable to perform the necessary operation. The second facility required her to purchase her own implants, but despite fundraising enough to do so, the facility confiscated the implants but never completed the surgery.

In the years since her injury, she has been unable to bear weight on her injured extremity due to severe pain and instability. She subsequently became wheelchair‐ and crutch‐reliant for ambulation, challenging her ability to secure stable housing and care for her children as she could only ambulate very short distances and relied completely on assistive devices. She eventually came to the United States as a refugee, and through her discovery at a community outreach program was referred to our orthopaedic trauma clinic for further evaluation and management.

At her initial clinic visit, her left leg was visibly shortened, deformed, and held in flexion (Figure 1A,B). She was 4 ′11 ′′ tall, weighed 128 lbs, and had a BMI of 26 kg/m2. She was destitute in appearance, but participated in no vices (no drugs, alcohol, or smoking). There was notable tenderness about the lateral aspect of her left distal thigh/knee. Sensation to light touch was intact but subjectively diminished in all nerve distributions below the knee. Physical examination demonstrated an extremely limited ROM arc of approximately 0°–20°, but it was challenging to determine whether this was knee or fracture motion. The patella appeared nonmobile and all exam measures were limited by pain.

(A and B) Clinical photographs obtained during initial emergency department consult showing gross deformity of the lower extremity.

Radiographs demonstrated a significantly displaced, nonunited distal femur fracture. The distal metaphyseal fragment was positioned in a flexed/lateral position relative to the femoral shaft, with complete bony discontinuity between the two (Figure 2A,B). A CT angiogram of the extremity revealed an intact popliteal artery with adequate three‐vessel runoff to the foot. However, the popliteal artery was seen to drape directly behind the posterior aspect of the dominant distal fracture fragment (Figure 3), appearing adherent to the superior margin of the distal femur fracture surface exposed to chronic flexion forces. This imaging was instrumental in committing to a medial accessory approach for management of the vasculature.

(A) AP radiograph of the left knee showing an oblique distal femoral diametaphyseal fracture with lateral translation of the proximal fragment and nonunion at the fracture site itself; a prior healed fracture of the fibular neck is also seen. (B) Lateral radiograph of the left distal femur demonstrating extension and shortening of distal fragment; the knee joint itself is held in a flexed position.

Computed tomography angiography (CTA) of the left lower extremity showed an intact popliteal artery without evidence of traumatic vessel injury or extravasation. However, the popliteal artery was observed to acutely change course as it passed around the posterior corner of the distal fracture fragment. No change in vessel caliber was appreciated. There was adequate three vessel runoff to the foot.

After discussion of various management options, it was decided the patient′s function could be safely improved with surgery. Though she was uninsured and living in a refugee camp, the treatment team and hospital agreed upon a moral obligation to treat her surgically.

On the day of surgery, the patient was induced under general anesthesia and placed supine on the operating room table. Her leg was prepped and draped in the usual sterile fashion with a sterile triangle placed under the operative extremity. The operation began with an anterolateral incision to the distal femur and knee. The knee joint was entered and found to be severely encased in scar tissue. Intra‐articular debridement was performed to enable knee and patella motion. The distance between the patella and proximal tibia was minimal and chronically shortened with tremendous tension on the extensor mechanism. This, in combination with the patient′s short stature, confirmed that standard adult retrograde IM nailing would be impossible. As such, the decision was made to proceed with dual plating and bone grafting via subvastus approaches medially and laterally to try to give this patient the best chance of achieving fracture union and the lowest chance of needing revision surgery for a failed construct based on an understanding of the contemporary literature [9, 10, 13, 14].

The medial dissection followed the intermuscular septum and the insertion of vastus medialis. The septum was incompetent, from either the initial trauma or years of femur motion. The neurovascular bundle was mobilized meticulously and the artery was picked off the fracture edges with blunt dissection in painstaking detail. Based on intraoperative assessment of severely limited knee ROM in part due to soft tissue contractures, a modified quadricepsplasty was deemed necessary to restore femur length and joint motion. To achieve this, the vastus lateralis, medialis, and intermedius were released in an extraperiosteal fashion up to the proximal third of the femur and separated from the rectus femoris tendon using the existing subvastus approaches. Once the vastus musculature was released off the femur, substantial improvement in knee motion and fracture fragment mobilization was observed. In terms of the rectus femoris, the decision was made not to perform a rectus femoris tenotomy to permit more aggressive early rehabilitation and functioning based on a preoperative discussion with the patient about rehabilitation goals.

Following quadricepsplasty and wide release of scar tissue, attention was turned back to plating. Before the wide releases, we experienced a bony overlap of approximately 6 cm, but this was lessened to approximately 4 cm after wide debridement. We trimmed off the cortical ends to ensure the fracture ends could come together with appropriate rotation and alignment. The small size of the femur produced some challenges obtaining adequate fixation, so a dual implant construct was pursued. Double‐straight clamps were used to apply compression between the distal femur and shaft, and a 2.7 mm plate was utilized temporarily on the lateral side to lessen the degrees of freedom in the reduction and to apply additional compression. On the medial side, an upside‐down lateral tibial plateau plate was contoured for contact and provision of distal fixation for the short articular segment. Locking screws were used distally because of the short segment, as well as the bone density caused by 5 years of nonweight bearing. The degree of plate contouring required the locking screws to be oriented towards rather than parallel to the joint. On the lateral side, a 4.5 mm distal femur plate was contoured to fit along the length of the femur. This lateral plate was similarly secured with locking and nonlocking screws, with the proximal screws traversing just inferior to the lesser trochanter and the distal screws angled diagonally towards, but not into, the joint. Bone autograft from rongeured pieces of femur and demineralized bone matrix were then applied to the seam.

Vancomycin and tobramycin powder were applied after irrigation. Following layered closure, incisional wound vacuum‐assisted devices and a hinged knee brace were applied. Twenty‐four hours of gram‐positive coverage was administered to defend the hardware. Immediate weight‐bearing as tolerated was allowed in a hinged knee brace limited to 45° of flexion. The patient was admitted for 6 days and discharged following pain control, physical therapy (PT) evaluation, and arrangement of outpatient social services. Six weeks of aspirin was prescribed for deep vein thrombosis (DVT) prophylaxis, and the patient was started on vitamin D supplementation and a high protein diet for optimal bony healing.

At follow‐up visits, her incisions appeared well‐healed (Figure 4A,B). Strength testing and sensation were intact throughout the operative extremity, including 5/5 strength in active hip flexion, knee flexion/extension, ankle plantarflexion/dorsiflexion, and extensor hallucis longus, as well as intact sensation to light touch in the sural, saphenous, tibial, superficial peroneal, and deep peroneal nerve distributions. This marked an improvement from presurgery baseline. Intraoperative tissue cultures returned negative for infection.

Clinical radiographs showing well‐healed (A) lateral and (B) medial incisions without signs of infection.

Serial radiographs revealed slow but progressive union with restoration of a portion of the total lost femur length. At 16 months, a persistent fracture line was observed but there was ongoing evidence of bone healing (Figures 5A–F). A leg length discrepancy of approximately 6 cm was noted clinically and radiographically (Figures 6A,B). We suspect that the degree of femoral shortening is contributing at least in part to her patella baja seen on lateral radiographs, though her patella positioning and any effect this may or may not have on her extensor mechanism do not seem to limit her strength, ability to ambulate, or perform daily activities and thus no corrective operations have been considered. We have addressed her leg length discrepancy with a 2 cm shoe lift, allowing the patient to successfully ambulate with a cane without an obvious antalgic gait pattern. She achieved a ROM arc of 0°–75° at the 14‐month post‐op time point despite significant yet predictable delays in postoperative PT. She has some residual knee pain that is well‐controlled with celecoxib 200 mg twice daily as needed and is overall satisfied with her outcome. Due to language barriers and her residence in a refugee camp, unprecedented challenges have been encountered in terms of gathering validated patient‐reported outcome metrics, though she reports great satisfaction with her outcome as she can now care for her children independently and is pursuing employment. Although additional interventions have been considered to address her leg length discrepancy, no further surgery is planned as this discrepancy is not bothersome enough for her to consider contralateral femoral shortening.

Medial and lateral locking plates are in appropriate position without evidence of hardware failure. A persistent transverse fracture line is visualized in the distal femur metaphysis, though bony healing is seen in a progressive fashion with each subsequent radiograph.

3. Discussion

This case illustrates a unique, chronically displaced distal femur fracture nonunion with significant shortening and soft tissue contractures that was successfully treated with dual plating, quadricepsplasty, and bone grafting. At 16 months post‐op, the patient′s ambulatory status has been restored and delayed union continues to progress. Although patient‐reported outcome metrics were unable to be obtained due to language barriers and unique social circumstances, the patient remains immensely satisfied with her outcome and is now able to care for her children and join the workforce.

We suspect that our patient′s ongoing delayed union reflects the extreme chronicity of her fracture and the challenges of restoring biology to severely compromised bone and soft tissues. Despite this, we are reassured by the existing literature supporting the use of dual plating with bone grafting for managing distal femur nonunions and therefore remain hopeful her fracture will fully heal with time. The multicenter study by Gavaskar et al. [9] investigated outcomes of 33 patients with aseptic distal femur nonunions following dual‐plating and bone grafting. Their results showed that 91% of patients in their cohort (30 of 33) achieved radiographic union by 36 weeks, with 22 of these patients achieving union by 24 weeks. Roddy et al. [10] investigated outcomes in a cohort of 102 patients with aseptic distal femur nonunions. Their study showed that use of a dual‐column construct was significantly associated with the lowest rate of recalcitrant non‐union. More specifically, in Roddy et al.′s cohort, only 1 of 36 patients (3%) who underwent dual‐column constructs went on to recalcitrant nonunion, which was significantly lower compared to the nonunion rate of patients who underwent single‐plating (8 of 29 patients, or 29%), IM nailing (4 of 10 patients, or 40%), or grafting alone (9 of 25 patients, or 36%). Though not a direct comparison, clinical studies of acute distal femur fractures, also provide a rationale for dual‐plate fixation. For instance, Bologna et al. [13] in their study of 21 patients with complex distal femur fractures, demonstrated that dual plating with medial and lateral locking plates demonstrated a higher union rate and possibly lower revision rates compared to single lateral plates. Beyond the use of dual plating for purposes of achieving union, a study by Sanders et al. [14] also supports this technique over single‐plate fixation to ensure stability of the construct and to prevent collapse into varus angulation. In their case series of nine patients with distal femur fractures who failed to achieve stable fixation and collapsed into varus with a lateral plate alone, all nine patients were successfully treated (i.e., achieved radiographic union) with the addition of a medial plate and bone grafting. Thus, the decision for dual‐plating with bone grafting is likely to give this patient the highest probability of healing and the lowest probability of requiring revision surgery for a failed construct.

Although not directly related to nonunion repair, the role of modified quadricepsplasty with sparing of the rectus femoris tendon proved essential to improving knee motion while enabling rehabilitation. Despite delays in PT, our patient achieved an additional 55° of motion after quadricepsplasty, an improvement comparable to published cohorts in the literature [8]. This ROM improvement has restored her functional status to the point where she is ambulatory despite a noteworthy leg length discrepancy of approximately 6 cm that is being managed with a shoe lift alone. The decision to manage this with a shoe lift rather than femoral shortening of the contralateral side was made based on a shared discussion of her preferences and desire to avoid additional surgical risk.

Overall, this case demonstrates that chronically displaced distal femur fractures, even those sustained several years prior and that have progressed to nonunion, can be successfully managed with dual plating, bone grafting, and quadricepsplasty. Despite a persistent leg length discrepancy being managed with shoe lifts, the combination of these procedures has restored her ambulatory function and independence in daily activities.
