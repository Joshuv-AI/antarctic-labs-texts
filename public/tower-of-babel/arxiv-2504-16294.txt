Ultimate quantum sensitivity in the 3D relative localisation of two single-photon emitters via two-photon interference Preprint: APS/123-QED
Luca Maggio, Vincenzo Tamma

Abstract
We present a quantum sensing protocol for the simultaneous estimation of the difference in the localization parameters of two single-photon sources, paving the way to single-photon 3D imaging and 3D nanoscopy beyond the diffraction limit. This is achieved by exploiting two-photon interference of the two emitted photons at a beam splitter via sampling measurements in the frequency and transverse momenta at the output. We prove theoretically that this technique reaches the ultimate sensitivity in the 3D relative localization of two emitters, already with a number of sampling measurements of ∼ 1000 \sim 1000 and a bias in the three localization parameters below 1 % 1\% . These results are independent of the values of the localization parameters to estimate.



I
Introduction

Two-photon interference at a beam splitter is a fundamental phenomenon at the heart of quantum optics and quantum technologies
Hong et al. 1987
;
Shih and Alley 1988
. If the two-photons fully overlap in their wave packets the probability amplitudes associated with coincidence events interfere destructively, and therefore the photons always bunch at the same detector at the beam splitter output. Therefore, by counting the coincidence events, it is possible to estimate the overlap between the two-photon wave packets, encoding specific parameters such as a temporal delay
Lyons et al. 2018
, a frequency shift
Gerrits et al. 2015
;
Jin et al. 2015
;
Gianani et al. 2018
;
Fabre and Felicetti 2021
, or polarization
Harnchaiwat et al. 2020
;
Sgobba et al. 2023
. This is relevant for applications in quantum computing
Kok et al. 2007
;
Barz et al. 2012
, quantum key distribution
Tang et al. 2014
;
Guan et al. 2015
, quantum repeaters
Sangouard et al. 2011
;
Hofmann et al. 2012
, and quantum coherence tomography
Teich et al. 2012
.

However, such a scheme is limited both in the precision and in the range of the values of the given parameter to estimate. More recently, such constraints were overcome by exploiting sampling measurements that resolve inner photonic parameters for the quantum estimation of temporal delays and spatial displacements
Triggiani et al. 2023
;
Triggiani and Tamma 2024
;
Muratore et al. 2024
. Despite such results, the ability to estimate multiple parameters with ultimate quantum precision has remained so far challenging. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, even though several 3D imaging and 3D nanoscopy protocols have been developed, the achievement of an optimal protocol, i.e. that can reach the ultimate sensitivity, has never been proven so far. Highly sensitive protocols include fusion modules
Tragakis et al. 2024
, adaptive optical imaging
Cameron et al. 2024
, and imaging based on time-of-flight measurements
Piron et al. 2021
. However, these sensing schemes measure directly the localization parameters and, therefore, they are limited by the diffraction and by the precision of the detectors employed in the direct measurements.

In this Letter, we demonstrate a technique which allows one to simultaneously estimate with ultimate quantum precision the three parameters for the localization of a probe single-photon emitter with respect to a reference one. This is possible by observing the quantum interference of both photons at a beam splitter when measurements which resolve the three components of the momenta of the two photons are employed in the far field. This protocol can pave the way to new quantum technologies in 3D imaging and 3D nanoscopy
Koskinen et al. 1992
;
Kolb et al. 2010
;
Horaud et al. 2016
. Applications in biological sensing can include cancer biology
Chen et al. 2016
, signaling
Dudok et al. 2015
, immunology
Williamson et al. 2011
, bacteriology
Gahlmann and Moerner 2014
, virology
Hanne et al. 2016
and in medical diagnostic
Benda et al. 2016
, since this protocol does not require exposure to a large number of photons that could damage the analyzed sample
Schermelleh et al. 2019
.

We show that, in general, the sensitivity of the scheme decreases when one ‘classically’ averages over the information associated with the momenta of the detected photons, and it is completely lost when there is no spatial overlap between the two input photons. Only in the limit of full spatial overlap between the photons, i.e. almost equal localization parameters for both photons, it is possible to achieve the same precision by employing non-resolving measurements. On the other hand, the proposed momentum-resolved technique allows us to achieve ultimate quantum precision for any values of such parameters already with
\sim 1000
sampling measurements, independently of the value of the localization parameters.

II
Experimental setup

In this section we describe the experimental setup in FIG.
1
. Two independent photons impinge on a balanced beam splitter, each of them from a different input port. The two photons are prepared in such a way that their probability distributions in the momentum domain are identical, i.e.
\left|\psi_{1}(\vec{k})\right|^{2}=\left|\psi_{2}(\vec{k})\right|^{2}=\left|\psi(\vec{k})\right|^{2}
, where
\vec{k}=(\omega,k_{x},k_{y})
. Here,
k_{x}
and
k_{y}
refer to the transverse momenta along the x and y coordinates,
\omega
to the frequency, and the indices 1 and 2 refer to the photon in inputs 1 and 2, respectively. From now on, we will consider the single-photon distribution in the momentum space as a Gaussian distribution that is factorable as a product of three Gaussian distributions in
k_{x}
,
k_{y}
and
\omega
, each of them with variance
\sigma^{2}_{k_{x}}
,
\sigma^{2}_{k_{y}}
and
\sigma^{2}_{\omega}
, respectively. However, the results discussed in this work can be generalized for every given distribution in the momentum domain.

Each photon is centered in its transverse position along the x and y coordinates in
x_{j}
and
y_{j}
, and it is emitted at a time such that its temporal distribution is centered in
t_{j}
, where
j=1,2
. As a consequence, the amplitude of each photon is
\psi_{j}(\vec{k})=|\psi(\vec{k})|\mathrm{e}^{i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{x_{j}}}
, where
\vec{x_{j}}=(t_{j},-x_{j},-y_{j})
. The bosonic operators in the momentum domain, i.e.
\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{1}\left(\vec{k}\right)
and
\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{2}\left(\vec{k}\right)
are defined in such a way they can take also into account the inner modes of the photons that are not related to their momentum or location. Therefore, their commutation relation is
[\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{1}\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right),\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{2}\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)]=\sqrt{\nu}\delta^{3}(\vec{k}_{1}-\vec{k}_{2})
, where
\nu\in[0,1]
represents the degree of indistinguishability of the photons when momentum-resolved measurements are performed. The two-photon state thus reads (for a full description of the input state, see Appendix
A
)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left|\Phi\right\rangle&=\int d^{3}k_{1}\psi_{1}\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{1}\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\left|0\right\rangle\\
&\otimes\int d^{3}k_{2}\psi_{2}\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{2}\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\left|0\right\rangle.\end{split}

(1)

After impinging at the beam splitter, the two photons can be reflected or transmitted. The combination of reflection or transmission of each photon can lead to a bunching event or a coincidence event for which the photons exit the same output port or two different output ports, respectively. Then two cameras, namely
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
in FIG.
1
, resolve the three components of the momenta of the two photons by measuring both their frequency and transverse momenta. We measure their transverse momenta by measuring their transverse position in the far field where they are detected by either of the two cameras. This position, defined in the transverse coordinates
(x^{\prime}_{j},y^{\prime}_{j})
(where
j
refers to the
j
-th output port,
j=1,2
) is linked to the transverse momentum since in the far field regime,
(k_{x},k_{y})=\omega(x^{\prime}_{j},y^{\prime}_{j})/cd
holds, where
d
is the distance of the detectors from the beam splitter (
d
is equal for both the detectors), and
c
is the speed of light (for a mathematical description of the evolution of the probe in the sensing protocol, see Appendix
B.1
).

In order to evaluate the output probabilities of having bunching, labeled with the letter B, or anti-bunching, labeled with the letter A, it is useful to introduce the following parameters

\displaystyle\begin{split}\xi=\frac{\Delta\omega}{2\sigma_{\omega}}&,\qquad\tau=2\sigma_{\omega}\Delta t,\\
\kappa_{x}=\frac{\Delta k_{x}}{2\sigma_{k_{x}}}&,\qquad\lambda_{x}=2\sigma_{k_{x}}\Delta x,\\
\kappa_{y}=\frac{\Delta k_{y}}{2\sigma_{k_{y}}}&,\qquad\lambda_{y}=2\sigma_{k_{y}}\Delta y,\end{split}

(2)

where
\Delta\omega=\omega_{2}-\omega_{1}
,
\Delta k_{x}=k_{x,2}-k_{x,1}
,
\Delta k_{y}=k_{y,2}-k_{y,1}
,
\Delta t=t_{2}-t_{1}
,
\Delta y=y_{2}-y_{1}
and
\Delta x=x_{2}-x_{1}
. Such probabilities thus read (for detailed derivations, see Appendices
B.2
,
B.3
and
B.4
)

\displaystyle\begin{split}P_{\nu}\left(X;\vec{\kappa}|\vec{\lambda}\right)=\frac{\gamma^{2}}{(\pi)^{3/2}}\mathrm{e}^{-|\vec{\kappa}|^{2}}\zeta_{X;\nu}(s\vec{\kappa}\cdot\vec{\lambda}).\end{split}

(3)

Here,
\zeta_{X;\nu}(s\vec{\kappa}\cdot\vec{\lambda})
represents the quantum beating,

\zeta_{X;\nu}(s\vec{\kappa}\cdot\vec{\lambda})=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\alpha\left(X\right)\nu\cos\left(s\vec{\kappa}\cdot\vec{\lambda}\right)\right),

(4)

where
\vec{\kappa}=(\xi,\kappa_{x},\kappa_{y})
,

\vec{\lambda}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\tau^{2}+\lambda_{x}^{2}+\lambda_{y}^{2}}}\begin{pmatrix}\tau\\
-\lambda_{x}\\
-\lambda_{y}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\theta)\\
\sin(\theta)\cos(\phi)\\
\sin(\theta\sin(\phi))\end{pmatrix},

(5)

s=\sqrt{\tau^{2}+\kappa_{x}^{2}+\kappa_{y}^{2}}
,
X=A,B
,
\alpha(A)=-1
,
\alpha(B)=1
and
\gamma\in[0,1]
is the detector efficiency.

This sensing protocol can estimate the independent parameters
s,\theta,\phi
simultaneously. The precision in measuring the transverse momenta in the far field, therefore the precision in the output transverse position, namely
(\delta x^{\prime},\delta y^{\prime})
, as well as the frequency precision
\delta\omega
, must be small enough to resolve the distribution in the momentum space and the quantum beatings, namely

\delta\vec{\kappa}\ll 1,\qquad\delta\vec{\kappa}\cdot\vec{\lambda}\ll\frac{1}{s}.

(6)

Such conditions are independent of the wavelength of the probes. Therefore, the estimation is not affected by the diffraction limit
Rayleigh 1879
;
Born and Wolf 2013
.

If the experimental setup does not resolve in the momentum space, we can retrieve only one parameter. In this case, the output of the sensing scheme can be defined only as a bunching event or a coincidence event. We can find the probability of the outputs by integrating Eq.
3
with respect of the three components of
\vec{\kappa}
, obtaining

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{P}_{\nu}\left(X|s\right)=\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}\left(1+\alpha(X)\nu\exp\left[-s^{2}/4\right]\right).\end{split}

(7)

Therefore, with the non-resolving scheme, we can estimate only
s
.

Plot of the probability distribution in Eq.
3
in the momentum space for photons with Gaussian momentum distribution
|\psi(\vec{k})|^{2}
with vector

\vec{\kappa}\cdot\vec{\lambda}
and a Gaussian envelope that depends on

. For simplicity, we impose
\gamma=1
,

.

III
Ultimate 3D quantum sensitivity

A set of unbiased estimators
(\tilde{s},\tilde{\theta},\tilde{\phi})
of the localization parameters is affected by an error described by the covariance matrix
\mathrm{Cov}[\tilde{s},\tilde{\theta},\tilde{\phi}]
. The covariance matrix is bounded below by the Cramér-Rao bound, which is inversely proportional to the number of sampling measurements
N
, and directly proportional to the inverse of the Fisher information matrix
F_{\nu}(s,\theta,\phi)

Cramér 1999
;
Rohatgi and Saleh 2015
. This bound represents the maximum precision achievable by the sensing scheme. The inequality between the covariance matrix and the Cramér-Rao bound means that the difference between these matrices is positive semi-definite. Ultimately, the Cramér-Rao bound is bounded below by the quantum Cramér-Rao bound, which is inversely proportional to the number of sampling measurements
N
, and directly proportional to the inverse of the quantum Fisher information matrix
Q(s,\theta,\phi)

Helstrom 1969
;
Holevo 2011
. Instead, this bound represents the maximum precision achievable in nature for the estimation of the parameters
(s,\theta,\phi)
. Similarly as before, this inequality means that the difference between Cramér-Rao bound and quantum Cramér-Rao bound is positive semi-definite. To summarize, this set of inequalities hold

\mathrm{Cov}[\tilde{s},\tilde{\theta},\tilde{\phi}]\geq\frac{F^{-1}_{\nu}\left(s,\theta,\phi\right)}{N}\geq\frac{Q^{-1}\left(s,\theta,\phi\right)}{N},

(8)

where, given the state
\left|\Phi\right\rangle
in Eq.
1
, the quantum Fisher information matrix is (for a detailed derivation, see Appendix
C
)

Q(s,\theta,\phi)=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&s^{2}&0\\
0&0&s^{2}\sin^{2}\theta\end{pmatrix}.

(9)

The Fisher information matrix can be obtained from the probabilities in Eq.
3
. In particular, by considering
\nu=1
, we obtain

\displaystyle F_{\nu=1}\left(s,\theta,\phi\right)=\gamma^{2}Q\left(s,\theta,\phi\right)

(10)

This means that in the limit of perfect detectors and identical photons in all degrees of freedom but the localization parameters, it is possible to achieve the ultimate precision for the estimation of
(s,\theta,\phi)
. Since the Fisher information matrix is diagonal, we notice that in the asymptotic limit of large
N
there is no correlation between the estimation of the parameters. When we do not resolve in the momentum space, we can obtain indistinguishability only if
s\sim 0
. Under this assumption and while having perfect detectors, the Fisher information for the non-resoling protocol is

\mathcal{F}_{\nu=1}(s)\xrightarrow{s\rightarrow 0}\gamma^{2}Q_{ss}(s,\theta,\phi)=\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}.

(11)

Therefore, in this limit
s\rightarrow 0
it is possible to achieve ultimate precision in the estimation of only the parameter
s
without resolving the photonic momenta at the detection.

In the case of photons that differ in other parameters apart from the localization ones, that is, when
\nu\neq 1
, this sensing protocol is still able to estimate the parameters
(s,\theta,\phi)
although without saturating the quantum Cramér-Rao bound. Indeed, in this scenario, the quantum beats lose visibility, as shown in FIG.
2
. The Fisher information matrix is an integral with respect to the resolved parameters that defines the vector
\vec{\kappa}
. In particular, if we rename the parameter of integration
\vec{\lambda}\cdot\vec{\kappa}=\rho
, we have

\displaystyle F_{\nu}(s,\theta,\phi)

\displaystyle=\gamma^{2}\int d\rho f_{\nu}(\rho;s,\theta,\phi),

(12)

where
f_{\nu}(\rho;s,\theta,\phi)
is the contribution for each value
(s,\theta,\phi)
to the Fisher information as plotted in FIG.
3
(for the evaluation of the Fisher information matrix as a function of the distinguishability parameter
\nu

f_{\nu}(\rho;s,\theta,\phi)=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\rho^{2}}\beta_{\nu}(s\rho)}{(\pi)^{1/2}}\begin{pmatrix}\rho^{2}&0&0\\
0&\frac{s^{2}}{2}&0\\
0&0&\frac{s^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}{2}\end{pmatrix},

(13)

where the function

\displaystyle\begin{split}\beta_{\nu}(s\rho)&=\sum_{X=A,B}\frac{1}{\zeta_{X;\nu}(s\rho)}\left(\frac{\partial\zeta_{X;\nu}(x)}{\partial x}\right)_{x=s\rho}^{2}\\
&=\frac{\nu^{2}\sin^{2}(s\rho)}{1-\nu^{2}\cos^{2}(s\rho)}\end{split}

(14)

describes the contributions to the fisher information from the quantum beats emerging in the expression of
\zeta_{X;\nu}(s\rho)
in Eq.
4
.

If
\beta_{\nu}(s\rho)=0
, no parameters can be estimated (which happens for
\nu=0
, and therefore when the two photons are fully distinguishable at the detectors). Instead, for
\beta_{\nu}(s\rho)=1
, the precision in the estimation is maximum (that is, when
\nu=1
, meaning that all the degrees of freedom of the photons but the ones to estimate are identical). These properties of
\beta_{\nu}(s\rho)
are linked to the fact that
\beta_{\nu}(s\rho)
is a function of
(\partial\zeta_{X;\nu}(x)/\partial x)_{x=s\rho}
, for
X=A,B
, which represents the degree of sensitivity at small variation of the value
s\rho
defined by the localization parameters to estimate.

Another important property of
\beta_{\nu}(s\rho)
is that it oscillates with half the period and in the same direction as the beatings of the probabilities in Eq.
3
as emerging from the dependence on the function
(\partial\zeta_{X;\nu}(x)/\partial x)_{x=s\rho}^{2}=\nu^{2}\sin^{2}(s\rho)/4=\nu^{2}(1-\cos(2s\rho))/8
which describes the momentum-dependent sensitivity of our protocol. As expected, the sensitivity is lost for
\nu\neq 1
at the stationary points of
\zeta_{X;\nu}(s\rho)
for values of
s\rho
multiple of
\pi
.

N

(s,\theta,\phi)

\nu

V
Conclusion

In this work, we present a 3D quantum sensing technique which allows us to simultaneously estimate with ultimate quantum precision the three parameters for the localization of a probe single-photon emitter with respect to a reference one, paving the way to single-photon 3D imaging and 3D nanoscopy beyond the diffraction limit. We show that the ultimate precision can be achieved by resolving the parameters in the 3D momentum space. Instead, by employing non-resolved measurements, only a single parameter can be estimated, and the maximum precision for its estimation is achieved under a specific condition, that is, for almost equal localization parameters for both photons.

The efficiency of the presented protocol saturates the quantum Cramér-Rao bound, by employing non-entangled photons differing only in the localization parameters. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this is the first time that the achievement of the ultimate precision limit has been proven for a 3D imaging protocol. We also demonstrate that 3D localization can also be achieved in the case of partial distinguishability of the photons at the detectors in parameters other than the ones we wish to estimate.

Remarkably, unbiased estimation can be achieved with any number
N
of sampling measurements and the Cramér-Rao bound in sensitivity is saturated already for
N\sim 1000
sampling measurements for different values of photonic distinguishability at the detectors. Therefore, this work can pave the way to 3D quantum imaging technology at the nanoscale for engineering and biomedical applications.

B.1

The two input photons impinge on a balance beam splitter, whose effect on the state
\left|\Phi\right\rangle

\hat{U}_{BS}

\hat{U}_{BS}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{i}\hat{U}_{BS}=\sum_{j}U_{BS,ij}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{j}

\hat{U}_{BS}\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{i}\hat{U}_{BS}=\sum_{j}U_{BS,ij}\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{j}

U_{BS}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}.

(19)

\displaystyle\hat{U}_{BS}\left|\Phi\right\rangle=\int d^{3}k_{1}\psi_{1}\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\frac{\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{1}\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)-\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{2}\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)}{\sqrt{2}}\left|0\right\rangle\otimes\int d^{3}k_{2}\psi_{2}\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\frac{\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{1}\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)+\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{2}\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)}{\sqrt{2}}\left|0\right\rangle.

This state is defined by three contributes. In fact we can write

\displaystyle\begin{split}\hat{U}_{BS}\left|\Phi\right\rangle&=\frac{1}{2}\int d^{3}k_{1}d^{3}k_{2}\psi_{1}\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\psi_{2}\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{1}\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{1}\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\left|0\right\rangle+\\
&-\frac{1}{2}\int d^{3}k_{1}d^{3}k_{2}\psi_{1}\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\psi_{2}\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{2}\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{2}\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\left|0\right\rangle+\\
&+\frac{1}{2}\int d^{3}k_{1}d^{3}k_{2}\psi_{1}\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\psi_{2}\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\left(\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{1}\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{2}\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)-\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{2}\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{1}\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\right)\left|0\right\rangle,\end{split}

(21)

where the first and second rows represent, respectively, a bunching (B) on the first and second channel, and the third row represents an anti-bunching (A). After the beam splitter, the photons reaches the detectors, that are at a fixed distance
d
from the beam splitter. We impose that the transverse momentum of each photon is much smaller than its longitudinal momentum. In the far field regime, i.e. when the photons reach the detectors, the amplitude of each photon becomes
\psi_{j}^{FF}\left(\vec{k}\right)
by taking an extra phase. We obtain

\psi_{j}^{FF}\left(\vec{k}\right)=\mathrm{e}^{i\omega d/c}\psi_{j}\left(\vec{k}\right)=\mathrm{e}^{i\omega d/c+i\vec{x}_{j}\cdot\vec{k}}\left|\psi\left(\vec{k}\right)\right|,

(22)

where the transverse momentum
(k_{x},k_{y})
is linked to the position on the detection plane
(x^{\prime}_{j},y^{\prime}_{j})
by the relation
(k_{x},k_{y})=(x^{\prime}_{j},y^{\prime}_{j})\omega/cd
,
and the two-photon state in proximity of the detectors is:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left|\Phi^{FF}\right\rangle&=\frac{1}{2}\int d^{3}k_{1}d^{3}k_{2}\psi_{1}^{FF}\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\psi_{2}^{FF}\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{1}\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{1}\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\left|0\right\rangle+\\
&-\frac{1}{2}\int d^{3}k_{1}d^{3}k_{2}\psi_{1}^{FF}\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\psi_{2}^{FF}\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{2}\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{2}\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\left|0\right\rangle+\\
&+\frac{1}{2}\int d^{3}k_{1}d^{3}k_{2}\psi_{1}^{FF}\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\psi_{2}^{FF}\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\left(\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{1}\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{2}\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)-\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{2}\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{1}\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\right)\left|0\right\rangle.\end{split}

(23)

B.2
Coincidence-event probability

The detectors are sensitive to the frequency and the position of each photon. For each couple of data-frequency and position- extracted from the i-th photon, only one transverse momentum is possible. For the evaluation of the output probabilities, we first consider no losses in detection and perfect coupling efficiency between the elements of the setup. The probability of having an anti-bunching event, while measuring
\left(\vec{k}_{1},\vec{k}_{2}\right)
, is

\displaystyle\begin{split}P\left(A;\vec{k}_{1},\vec{k_{2}}|\vec{x}_{1},\vec{x_{2}}\right)&=\left|\left\langle 0\right|\hat{a}_{1}\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\hat{a}_{2}\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\left|\Phi^{FF}\right\rangle\right|^{2}+\\
&+\left|\left\langle 0\right|\hat{a}_{1}\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\hat{b}_{2}\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\left|\Phi^{FF}\right\rangle\right|^{2}+\\
&+\left|\left\langle 0\right|\hat{b}_{1}\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\hat{a}_{2}\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\left|\Phi^{FF}\right\rangle\right|^{2}+\\
&+\left|\left\langle 0\right|\hat{b}_{1}\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\hat{b}_{2}\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\left|\Phi^{FF}\right\rangle\right|^{2}.\end{split}

(24)

We evaluate each term of this probability as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left|\left\langle 0\right|\hat{a}_{1}\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\hat{a}_{2}\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\left|\Phi^{FF}\right\rangle\right|^{2}&=\left|\frac{\sqrt{\nu}}{2}\left(\psi_{2}^{FF}\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\psi_{1}^{FF}\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)-\psi_{2}^{FF}\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\psi_{1}^{FF}\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\right)\right|^{2}\\
&=\frac{\nu}{4}\left|\psi\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\psi\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\right|^{2}\left|\mathrm{e}^{i\vec{x}_{1}\cdot\vec{k}_{1}}\mathrm{e}^{i\vec{x}_{2}\cdot\vec{k}_{2}}-\mathrm{e}^{i\vec{x}_{1}\cdot\vec{k}_{2}}\mathrm{e}^{i\vec{x}_{2}\cdot\vec{k}_{1}}\right|^{2}\\
&=\frac{\nu}{2}\left|\psi\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\psi\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\right|^{2}\left[1-\cos\left(\Delta\vec{x}\cdot\Delta\vec{k}\right)\right],\end{split}

(25)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left|\left\langle 0\right|\hat{a}_{1}\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\hat{b}_{2}\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\left|\Phi^{FF}\right\rangle\right|^{2}&=\left|\frac{\sqrt{1-\nu}}{2}\psi_{2}^{FF}\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\psi_{1}^{FF}\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\right|^{2}\\
&=\frac{1-\nu}{4}\left|\psi\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\psi\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\right|^{2},\end{split}

(26)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left|\left\langle 0\right|\hat{b}_{1}\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\hat{a}_{2}\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\left|\Phi^{FF}\right\rangle\right|^{2}&=\left|\frac{\sqrt{1-\nu}}{2}\psi_{1}^{FF}\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\psi_{2}^{FF}\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\right|^{2}\\
&=\frac{1-\nu}{4}\left|\psi\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\psi\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\right|^{2},\end{split}

(27)

\left|\left\langle 0\right|\hat{b}_{1}\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\hat{b}_{2}\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\left|\Phi^{FF}\right\rangle\right|^{2}=0.

(28)

The sum of all the contributions is

P\left(A;\vec{k}_{1},\vec{k_{2}}|\vec{x}_{1},\vec{x_{2}}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left|\psi\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\psi\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\right|^{2}\left[1-\nu\cos\left(\Delta\vec{x}\cdot\Delta\vec{k}\right)\right].

(29)

B.3
Bunching-event probability

The probability of having a bunching event, while measuring
\left(\vec{k}_{1},\vec{k_{2}}\right)
, is

\displaystyle\begin{split}P\left(B;\vec{k}_{1},\vec{k_{2}}|\vec{x}_{1},\vec{x_{2}}\right)&=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1,2}\left|\left\langle 0\right|\hat{a}_{i}\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\hat{a}_{i}\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\left|\Phi^{FF}\right\rangle\right|^{2}+\\
&+\sum_{i=1,2}\left|\left\langle 0\right|\hat{a}_{i}\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\hat{b}_{i}\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\left|\Phi^{FF}\right\rangle\right|^{2}+\\
&+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1,2}\left|\left\langle 0\right|\hat{b}_{i}\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\hat{b}_{i}\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\left|\Phi^{FF}\right\rangle\right|^{2}\end{split}

(30)

In analogy to the previous subsection, we evaluate each term involved in the evaluation of the probability, obtaining

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1,2}\left|\left\langle 0\right|\hat{a}_{i}\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\hat{a}_{i}\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\left|\Phi^{FF}\right\rangle\right|^{2}&=\left|\frac{\sqrt{\nu}}{2}\left(\psi_{2}^{FF}\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\psi_{1}^{FF}\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)+\psi_{2}^{FF}\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\psi_{1}^{FF}\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\right)\right|^{2}\\
&=\frac{\nu}{4}\left|\psi\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\psi\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\right|^{2}\left|\mathrm{e}^{i\vec{x}_{1}\cdot\vec{k}_{1}}\mathrm{e}^{i\vec{x}_{2}\cdot\vec{k}_{2}}+\mathrm{e}^{i\vec{x}_{1}\cdot\vec{k}_{2}}\mathrm{e}^{i\vec{x}_{2}\cdot\vec{k}_{1}}\right|^{2}\\
&=\frac{\nu}{2}\left|\psi\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\psi\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\right|^{2}\left[1+\cos\left(\Delta\vec{x}\cdot\Delta\vec{k}\right)\right],\end{split}

(31)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sum_{i=1,2}\left|\left\langle 0\right|\hat{a}_{i}\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\hat{b}_{i}\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\left|\Phi^{FF}\right\rangle\right|^{2}&=\sum_{i=1,2}\left|\frac{\sqrt{1-\nu}}{2}\psi_{i}^{FF}\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\psi_{i}^{FF}\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\right|^{2}\\
&=\frac{1-\nu}{2}\left|\psi\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\psi\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\right|^{2},\end{split}

(32)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1,2}\left|\left\langle 0\right|\hat{b}_{i}\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\hat{b}_{i}\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\left|\Phi^{FF}\right\rangle\right|^{2}=0.\end{split}

(33)

Therefore, the bunching probability is

P\left(B;\vec{k}_{1},\vec{k_{2}}|\vec{x}_{1},\vec{x_{2}}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left|\psi\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\psi\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\right|^{2}\left[1+\nu\cos\left(\Delta\vec{x}\cdot\Delta\vec{k}\right)\right].

(34)

B.4
Evaluation of the output probabilities under the condition of non-unitary efficiency detection

To summarize, the probability of having bunching or anti-bunching while measuring
\left(\vec{k}_{1},\vec{k}_{2}\right)
is

P\left(X;\vec{k}_{1},\vec{k_{2}}|\vec{x}_{1},\vec{x_{2}}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left|\psi\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\psi\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\right|^{2}\left[1+\alpha\left(X\right)\nu\cos\left(\Delta\vec{x}\cdot\Delta\vec{k}\right)\right],

(35)

where
\alpha\left(A\right)=-1
and
\alpha\left(B\right)=1
. We observe quantum beats in
\Delta\vec{k}
with a visibility that depends on
\nu
. In fact, for
\nu=0
, i.e. for two orthogonal photon states, we cannot observe quantum beats, since there are no interference effects. The visibility of the quantum beats is not related to the parameters
\vec{K}=\frac{\vec{k}_{1}+\vec{k}_{2}}{2}
. Therefore, detecting only
\Delta\vec{k}
is necessary for the estimation of
\Delta\vec{x}
. The probability of having bunching or anti-bunching, while measuring
\left(\vec{k}_{1},\vec{k}_{2}\right)
is

P\left(X;\vec{k}_{1},\vec{k_{2}}|\vec{x}_{1},\vec{x_{2}}\right)=\frac{1}{2}C\left(\Delta\vec{k}\right)\left[1+\alpha\left(X\right)\nu\cos\left(\Delta\vec{x}\cdot\Delta\vec{k}\right)\right],

(36)

where the beats envelope
C\left(\Delta\vec{k}\right)
is

C\left(\Delta\vec{k}\right)=\int d^{3}K\left|\psi\left(\vec{K}+\frac{\Delta\vec{k}}{2}\right)\psi\left(\vec{K}-\frac{\Delta\vec{k}}{2}\right)\right|^{2}.

(37)

Therefore, if we consider a Gaussian distribution in the momentum space centered in
\vec{k}_{0}
and with variance
\sigma^{2}_{\omega},\sigma^{2}_{k_{x}},\sigma^{2}_{k_{y}}
for each component of the vector
\vec{k}=(\omega,k_{x},k_{y})
,

\left|\psi\left(\vec{k}\right)\right|^{2}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3/2}\sigma_{\omega}\sigma_{k_{x}}\sigma_{k_{y}}}\exp\left[-\frac{(\omega-\omega_{0})^{2}}{2\sigma_{\omega}^{2}}\right]\exp\left[-\frac{(k_{x}-k_{x,0})^{2}}{2\sigma_{k_{x}}^{2}}\right]\exp\left[-\frac{(k_{y}-k_{y,0})^{2}}{2\sigma_{k_{y}}^{2}}\right],

(38)

the beats envelope is

C\left(\Delta\vec{k}\right)=\frac{1}{(4\pi)^{3/2}\sigma_{\omega}\sigma_{k_{x}}\sigma_{k_{y}}}\exp\left[-\frac{\Delta\omega^{2}}{4\sigma_{\omega}^{2}}\right]\exp\left[-\frac{\Delta k_{x}^{2}}{4\sigma_{k_{x}}^{2}}\right]\exp\left[-\frac{\Delta k_{y}^{2}}{4\sigma_{k_{y}}^{2}}\right].

(39)

By remembering that a photon with transverse momentum defined by
(k_{x},k_{y})
is detected by the camera
C_{j}
at
(x^{\prime}_{j},y^{\prime}_{j})
, such that
(k_{x},k_{y})=(x^{\prime}_{j},y^{\prime}_{j})\omega/cd
, the error in the detection for
(k_{x},k_{y})
, namely
\delta(k_{x},k_{y})
, is related to the errors of the position
\delta(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
and the frequency
\delta\omega
as follows

\delta k_{x}^{2}=k_{x}^{2}\left(\frac{\delta x^{\prime 2}}{x^{\prime 2}_{j}}+\frac{\delta\omega^{2}}{\omega^{2}}\right),\qquad\delta k_{y}^{2}=k_{y}^{2}\left(\frac{\delta y^{\prime 2}}{y^{\prime 2}_{j}}+\frac{\delta\omega^{2}}{\omega^{2}}\right).

(40)

The estimation of the parameters
\Delta\vec{x}
is possible only under the condition that the errors in the measurement of the parameters are small enough to resolve the beating oscillations and to resolve both the transverse-momentum and frequency distributions of the two photons. These constraints are here summarized for the case of Gaussian probability distribution in frequency and transverse momentum:

\delta\omega\ll\sigma_{\omega},\qquad\delta k_{x}\ll\sigma_{k_{x}},\qquad\delta k_{y}\ll\sigma_{k_{y}},\qquad|\Delta\vec{x}\delta\vec{k}|\ll 1.

(41)

We consider now the presence of losses in the detection. We assume that both the efficiencies of the two detectors are equal to
\gamma\in\left[0,1\right]
. Each photon can be lost in the detection with probability
1-\gamma
. Therefore, the probabilities of detecting zero, one, or two photons are, respectively,

\displaystyle\begin{split}&P_{0}=\left(1-\gamma\right)^{2},\\
&P_{1}=2\gamma\left(1-\gamma\right),\\
&P\left(X;\Delta\vec{k}|\Delta\vec{x}\right)=\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}C\left(\Delta\vec{k}\right)\left[1+\alpha\left(X\right)\nu\cos\left(\Delta\vec{x}\cdot\Delta\vec{k}\right)\right].\end{split}

(42)

The zero-photon and one-photon events do not carry any information for the estimation of
\Delta\vec{x}
. In fact, only the two-photon events have a probability that is function of these two parameters. This probability is proportional to
\gamma^{2}
. In particular, for
\nu=1
, the probabilities of detecting both photons in a bunching or an anti-bunching event are

\displaystyle\begin{split}P_{\nu=1}\left(A;\Delta\vec{k}|\Delta\vec{x}\right)&=\gamma^{2}C\left(\vec{k}\right)\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\Delta\vec{x}\cdot\Delta\vec{k}}{2}\right),\\
P_{\nu=1}\left(B;\Delta\vec{k}|\Delta\vec{x}\right)&=\gamma^{2}C\left(\vec{k}\right)\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\Delta\vec{x}\cdot\Delta\vec{k}}{2}\right).\end{split}

(43)

We define

\displaystyle\begin{split}\xi=\frac{\Delta\omega}{2\sigma_{\omega}}&,\qquad\tau=2\sigma_{\omega}\Delta t,\\
\kappa_{x}=\frac{\Delta k_{x}}{2\sigma_{k_{x}}}&,\qquad\lambda_{x}=2\sigma_{k_{x}}\Delta x,\\
\kappa_{y}=\frac{\Delta k_{y}}{2\sigma_{k_{y}}}&,\qquad\lambda_{y}=2\sigma_{k_{y}}\Delta y,\end{split}

(44)

recalled in the main text with Eq.
2
. Also, we introduce
\vec{\kappa}=(\xi,\kappa_{x},\kappa_{y})
,

s=\sqrt{\tau^{2}+\lambda_{x}^{2}+\lambda_{y}^{2}}=2\sqrt{\sigma_{\omega}^{2}\Delta t^{2}+\sigma_{k_{x}}^{2}\Delta x^{2}+\sigma_{k_{y}}^{2}\Delta y^{2}},

(45)

and the unitary vector

\vec{\lambda}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\tau^{2}+\lambda_{x}^{2}+\lambda_{y}^{2}}}\begin{pmatrix}\tau\\
-\lambda_{x}\\
-\lambda_{y}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\theta)\\
\sin(\theta)\cos(\phi)\\
\sin(\theta\sin(\phi))\end{pmatrix},

(46)

that in the main text is introduced in Eq.
5
. Here,
\theta\in[0,\pi/2]
and
\phi\in[0,2\pi[
, since we are fixing the sign of
\tau
as positive. This choice is made because it is not possible to determine all the signs of
\tau,\lambda_{x},\lambda_{y}
simultaneously, because both
\vec{\lambda}
and
-\vec{\lambda}
give the same output probabilities. The parameters
\theta
is

\displaystyle\theta=\arctan\frac{\sqrt{\lambda_{x}^{2}+\lambda_{y}^{2}}}{\tau}=\arctan\frac{\sqrt{\sigma_{k_{x}}^{2}\Delta x^{2}+\sigma_{k_{y}}^{2}\Delta y^{2}}}{\sigma_{\omega}\Delta t},

(47)

while
\phi
is

\displaystyle\phi=\arctan\frac{\lambda_{y}}{\lambda_{x}}=\arctan\frac{\sigma_{k_{y}}\Delta y}{\sigma_{k_{x}}\Delta x},

(48)

where the signs of
\lambda_{x}
and
\lambda_{y}
are appropriately considered in order to define in which quadrant
\phi
is located.

By using these new definitions, we can write the output probabilities as

P_{\nu}\left(X;\vec{\kappa}|\vec{\lambda}\right)=\frac{\gamma^{2}}{(\pi)^{3/2}}\mathrm{e}^{-|\vec{\kappa}|^{2}}\zeta_{X;\nu}(s\vec{\kappa}\cdot\vec{\lambda}),

(49)

which is Eq.
3
in the main text, where

\zeta_{X;\nu}(s\vec{\kappa}\cdot\vec{\lambda})=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\alpha\left(X\right)\nu\cos\left(s\vec{\kappa}\cdot\vec{\lambda}\right)\right),

(50)

which is Eq.
4
in the main text.
Instead, the conditions in Eq.
41
can be written in the form

\delta\vec{\kappa}\ll 1,\qquad\delta\vec{\kappa}\cdot\vec{\lambda}\ll\frac{1}{s}.

(51)

These conditions are reported as Eq.
6
in the main text.

By using Eq.
49
, we can find the output probabilities for the non-resolving scheme by integrating with respect of the three components of
\vec{\kappa}
, obtaining

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{P}_{\nu}\left(X|s\right)=\int d^{3}\kappa P_{\nu}\left(X;\vec{\kappa}|\vec{\lambda}\right)=\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}\left(1+\alpha(X)\nu\exp\left[-s^{2}/4\right]\right),\end{split}

(52)

which is Eq.
7
in the main text.

Appendix C
Quantum Fisher information in Eq.
9

In this section we evaluate the quantum Fisher information matrix, which will assume the form

Q(s,\theta,\phi)=\begin{pmatrix}Q_{ss}&Q_{s\theta}&Q_{s\phi}\\
Q_{s\theta}&Q_{\theta\theta}&Q_{\theta\phi}\\
Q_{s\phi}&Q_{\theta\phi}&Q_{\phi\phi}\end{pmatrix},

(53)

where, the elements of the matrix are by definition

\displaystyle\begin{split}Q_{\zeta\mu}=4\mathrm{Re}\left[\left\langle\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\zeta}\bigg|\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\mu}\right\rangle-\left\langle\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\zeta}\bigg|\Phi\right\rangle\left\langle\Phi\bigg|\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\mu}\right\rangle\right],\end{split}

(54)

where
\zeta,\mu=s,\theta,\phi
.
For clarity, we recall the expression of the input state, which is

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left|\Phi\right\rangle&=\int d^{3}k_{1}\mathrm{e}^{i\vec{x}_{1}\cdot\vec{k}_{1}}\left|\psi\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\right|\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{1}\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\left|0\right\rangle\otimes\int d^{3}k_{2}\mathrm{e}^{i\vec{x}_{2}\cdot\vec{k}_{2}}\left|\psi\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\right|\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{2}\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\left|0\right\rangle\\
&=\int d^{3}k_{1}d^{3}k_{2}\mathrm{e}^{i\vec{x}_{1}\cdot\vec{k}_{1}}\mathrm{e}^{i\vec{x}_{2}\cdot\vec{k}_{2}}\left|\psi\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\psi\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\right|\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{1}\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{2}\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\left|0\right\rangle.\end{split}

(55)

Here, only the phase depends on
\vec{x}_{j}
,
j=1,2
, therefore, only the phase will depend on
s,\theta,\phi
. We can manipulate the argument of the phase in order to explicit the dependence on these parameters

\displaystyle\begin{split}\vec{x}_{1}\cdot\vec{k_{1}}+\vec{x}_{2}\cdot\vec{k_{2}}&=\frac{1}{2}\Delta\vec{x}\cdot\Delta\vec{k}+\vec{x}\cdot\vec{K}\\
&=\frac{s}{2}\vec{\kappa}\cdot\vec{\lambda}+\vec{x}\cdot\vec{K}.\end{split}

(56)

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\left|\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial s}\right\rangle=\frac{i}{2}\int d^{3}k_{1}d^{3}k_{2}\vec{\kappa}\cdot\vec{\lambda}\mathrm{e}^{i\vec{x}_{1}\cdot\vec{k}_{1}}\mathrm{e}^{i\vec{x}_{2}\cdot\vec{k}_{2}}\left|\psi\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\psi\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\right|\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{1}\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{2}\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\left|0\right\rangle,\\
&\left|\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\theta}\right\rangle=\frac{is}{2}\int d^{3}k_{1}d^{3}k_{2}\vec{\kappa}\cdot\frac{\partial\vec{\lambda}}{\partial\theta}\mathrm{e}^{i\vec{x}_{1}\cdot\vec{k}_{1}}\mathrm{e}^{i\vec{x}_{2}\cdot\vec{k}_{2}}\left|\psi\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\psi\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\right|\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{1}\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{2}\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\left|0\right\rangle,\\
&\left|\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\phi}\right\rangle=\frac{is}{2}\int d^{3}k_{1}d^{3}k_{2}\vec{\kappa}\cdot\frac{\partial\vec{\lambda}}{\partial\phi}\mathrm{e}^{i\vec{x}_{1}\cdot\vec{k}_{1}}\mathrm{e}^{i\vec{x}_{2}\cdot\vec{k}_{2}}\left|\psi\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\psi\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\right|\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{1}\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{2}\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\left|0\right\rangle.\end{split}

(57)

Therefore, it is possible to evaluate the inner products that define the quantum Fisher information matrix. We obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}\begin{pmatrix}\left\langle\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial s}\bigg|\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial s}\right\rangle&\left\langle\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial s}\bigg|\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\theta}\right\rangle&\left\langle\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial s}\bigg|\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\phi}\right\rangle\\
\left\langle\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\theta}\bigg|\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial s}\right\rangle&\left\langle\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\theta}\bigg|\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\theta}\right\rangle&\left\langle\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\theta}\bigg|\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\phi}\right\rangle\\
\left\langle\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\phi}\bigg|\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial s}\right\rangle&\left\langle\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\phi}\bigg|\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\theta}\right\rangle&\left\langle\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\phi}\bigg|\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\phi}\right\rangle\end{pmatrix}&=\frac{1}{4}\int d^{3}k_{1}d^{3}k_{2}\left|\psi\left(\vec{k}_{1}\right)\psi\left(\vec{k}_{2}\right)\right|^{2}\times\\
&\times\begin{pmatrix}(\vec{\kappa}\cdot\vec{\lambda})^{2}&s(\vec{\kappa}\cdot\vec{\lambda})\left(\vec{\kappa}\cdot\frac{\partial\vec{\lambda}}{\partial\theta}\right)&s(\vec{\kappa}\cdot\vec{\lambda})\left(\vec{\kappa}\cdot\frac{\partial\vec{\lambda}}{\partial\phi}\right)\\
s(\vec{\kappa}\cdot\vec{\lambda})\left(\vec{\kappa}\cdot\frac{\partial\vec{\lambda}}{\partial\theta}\right)&s^{2}\left(\vec{\kappa}\cdot\frac{\partial\vec{\lambda}}{\partial\theta}\right)^{2}&s^{2}\left(\vec{\kappa}\cdot\frac{\partial\vec{\lambda}}{\partial\theta}\right)\left(\vec{\kappa}\cdot\frac{\partial\vec{\lambda}}{\partial\phi}\right)\\

Appendix E
Likelihood estimation for the realization of FIG
4

In this section, a derivation of the likelihood estimators for the estimation of the parameters
(s,\theta,\phi)
follows. The sensing scheme presented in this work is based on resolved-sampling measures. By considering that the i-th element of the sample has a probability
P_{\nu}\left(X_{i};\vec{\kappa}_{i}|s,\vec{\lambda}\right)
to occurs, we define the likelihood as

\mathcal{L}=\prod_{i}P_{\nu}\left(X_{i};\vec{\kappa}_{i}|s,\vec{\lambda}\right),

(79)

and the Log-Likelihood, as it logarithm, i.e.

\log\mathcal{L}\propto\sum_{i}\log\left[1+\alpha\left(X_{i}\right)\nu\cos\left(s\vec{\kappa}_{i}\cdot\vec{\lambda}\right)\right].

(80)

In order to estimate the parameters
(s,\theta,\phi)
, we define three estimators such that the Log-Likelihood, as a function of these estimators, is maximized. Therefore, we impose the stationariety condition, for which

\displaystyle\begin{split}\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\partial\log\mathcal{L}}{\partial s}=0\\
\\
\displaystyle\frac{\partial\log\mathcal{L}}{\partial\theta}=0\\
\\
\displaystyle\frac{\partial\log\mathcal{L}}{\partial\phi}=0\end{cases}\Longrightarrow\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{i}\frac{\alpha\left(X_{i}\right)\nu\sin\left(s\vec{\kappa}_{i}\cdot\vec{\lambda}\right)}{1+\alpha\left(X_{i}\right)\nu\cos\left(s\vec{\kappa}_{i}\cdot\vec{\lambda}\right)}\vec{\kappa}_{i}\cdot\vec{\lambda}=0\\
\\
\displaystyle\sum_{i}\frac{\alpha\left(X_{i}\right)\nu\sin\left(s\vec{\kappa}_{i}\cdot\vec{\lambda}\right)}{1+\alpha\left(X_{i}\right)\nu\cos\left(s\vec{\kappa}_{i}\cdot\vec{\lambda}\right)}\vec{\kappa}_{i}\cdot\frac{\partial\vec{\lambda}}{\partial\theta}=0\\
\\
\displaystyle\sum_{i}\frac{\alpha\left(X_{i}\right)\nu\sin\left(s\vec{\kappa}_{i}\cdot\vec{\lambda}\right)}{1+\alpha\left(X_{i}\right)\nu\cos\left(s\vec{\kappa}_{i}\cdot\vec{\lambda}\right)}\vec{\kappa}_{i}\cdot\frac{\partial\vec{\lambda}}{\partial\phi}=0\end{cases}.\end{split}

(81)

Since
\vec{\lambda}
,
\frac{\partial\vec{\lambda}}{\partial\theta}
and
\frac{\partial\vec{\lambda}}{\partial\phi}
are three orthogonal vectors, solving this system is equivalent to solve

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\frac{\alpha\left(X_{i}\right)\nu\sin\left(s\vec{\kappa}_{i}\cdot\vec{\lambda}\right)}{1+\alpha\left(X_{i}\right)\nu\cos\left(s\vec{\kappa}_{i}\cdot\vec{\lambda}\right)}\vec{\kappa}_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

(82)

By numerically solving this system for
s,\theta,\phi
as functions of
\{X_{i},\vec{\kappa}_{i}\}
, it is possible to find the likelihood estimators.
