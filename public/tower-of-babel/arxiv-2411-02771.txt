Automatic doubly robust inference for linear functionals via calibrated debiased machine learning
Lars van der Laan, Alex Luedtke, Marco Carone

Abstract
In causal inference, many estimands of interest can be expressed as a linear functional of the outcome regression function; this includes, for example, average causal effects of static, dynamic and stochastic interventions. For learning such estimands, in this work, we propose novel debiased machine learning estimators that are doubly robust asymptotically linear, thus providing not only doubly robust consistency but also facilitating doubly robust inference (e.g., confidence intervals and hypothesis tests). To do so, we first establish a key link between calibration, a machine learning technique typically used in prediction and classification tasks, and the conditions needed to achieve doubly robust asymptotic linearity. We then introduce calibrated debiased machine learning (C-DML), a unified framework for doubly robust inference, and propose a specific C-DML estimator that integrates cross-fitting, isotonic calibration, and debiased machine learning estimation. A C-DML estimator maintains asymptotic linearity when either the outcome regression or the Riesz representer of the linear functional is estimated sufficiently well, allowing the other to be estimated at arbitrarily slow rates or even inconsistently. We propose a simple bootstrap-assisted approach for constructing doubly robust confidence intervals. Our theoretical and empirical results support the use of C-DML to mitigate bias arising from the inconsistent or slow estimation of nuisance functions.



Keywords:
Debiasing, isotonic calibration, double robustness, targeted learning, causal inference.

1
Introduction

1.1
Motivation

Nonparametric inference on the causal effects of dynamic and stochastic treatment interventions using flexible statistical learning techniques is a fundamental problem of causal inference. These inferential targets fall within a broader class of target parameters that can be expressed as linear functionals of the outcome regression, that is, the conditional mean of the outcome given treatment assignment and baseline covariates
(
Chernozhukov et al. 2018b
;
Rotnitzky et al. 2021
;
Hirshberg and Wager 2021
)
. To achieve inference for such parameters, various debiased machine learning frameworks are available, including one-step estimation
(
Pfanzagl and Wefelmeyer 1985
;
Bickel et al. 1993
)
, estimating equations and double machine learning
(
Robins et al. 1995
;
Robins et al. 1994
;
van der Laan and Robins 2003
;
Chernozhukov et al. 2018a
)
, and targeted minimum loss estimation (TMLE)
(
van der Laan and Rubin 2006
;
van der Laan and Rose 2011
)
. When performing inference on linear functionals, debiased machine learning methods frequently produce estimators that demonstrate robustness against inconsistent or slow estimation of certain nuisance functions, a property known as double robustness
(
Bang and Robins 2005
;
Smucler et al. 2019
)
. For example, when estimating the average treatment effect (ATE), doubly robust estimators remain consistent as long as either the outcome regression or the propensity score is consistently estimated, even if the other is inconsistently estimated. This robustness property extends more generally to linear functionals of the outcome regression, necessitating consistent estimation of only the outcome regression itself or the Riesz representer of the linear functional
(
Chernozhukov et al. 2018b
;
Rotnitzky et al. 2021
)
.

Extending the double robustness property of debiased machine learning estimators to statistical inference, that is, to uncertainty quantification, is a challenging problem. Achieving asymptotic linearity for such estimators typically requires consistent estimation of both the outcome regression and the Riesz representer at sufficiently fast rates. When one of the nuisance estimators is inconsistent or estimated too slowly, desirable properties such as asymptotic linearity, asymptotic normality, and
n^{\frac{1}{2}}
–consistency can be lost, compromising the validity of inferences
(
Benkeser et al. 2017
)
. In particular, in such cases, typical confidence intervals and p-values can be expected to be poorly calibrated. One approach to achieving doubly robust inference is through the use of parametric methods, such as maximum likelihood estimation, for estimating nuisance parameters. These methods exhibit model double robustness, maintaining asymptotic linearity when at least one of the nuisance parametric models is correctly specified
(
Vermeulen and Vansteelandt 2015
;
Vermeulen and Vansteelandt 2016
)
. However, the applicability of these methods for nonparametric inference is limited due to their reliance on strong assumptions, as both models are then likely to be misspecified in practice
(
Kang and Schafer 2007
)
.

To overcome the limitations of parametric methods, several estimators exhibiting sparsity double robustness, a weaker form of model double robustness, have been proposed
(
Avagyan and Vansteelandt 2017
;
Athey et al. 2018
;
Bradic et al. 2019
;
Smucler et al. 2019
;
Tan 2020
)
. For instance, in the context of high-dimensional generalized linear regression models,
Avagyan and Vansteelandt 2017
and
Bradic et al. 2019
introduced doubly robust
\ell_{1}
–regularized regression estimators of the ATE. These estimators achieve asymptotic linearity when either the outcome regression or the propensity score exhibits sufficient sparsity in the covariates, allowing for misspecification of one of the nuisance models. Additionally,
Athey et al. 2018
and
Tan 2020
proposed singly robust estimators that are robust to misspecification of the outcome regression model, assuming sufficient sparsity in the propensity score. However, a significant drawback of these estimators is their reliance on strong sparsity assumptions for the nuisance functions, which may fail to hold in practice.
Smucler et al. 2019
developed a unifying framework for constructing sparsity doubly robust estimators using
\ell_{1}
–regularization techniques. They relax the strong sparsity assumptions by requiring that nuisance estimators based on possibly misspecified models converge to approximately sparse functions sufficiently quickly. However, as the authors note, there is limited justification to expect an inconsistent nuisance estimator derived from a misspecified model to tend to a sparse or approximately sparse limiting function, even if the true nuisance function depends on only a small subset of covariates. Furthermore, these methods are constrained by their reliance on
\ell_{1}
–regularized generalized linear models for the nuisance functions, limiting their ability to fully leverage the advantages offered by modern learning techniques.

The seminal work of
van der Laan 2014
introduced a debiasing technique, based on the TMLE framework
(
van der Laan and Rose 2011
)
, for constructing estimators with which it is possible to perform doubly robust inference using generic machine learning techniques. The resulting estimators are doubly robust asymptotically linear (DRAL), in the sense that they remain asymptotically linear even when one of the nuisance functions is estimated too slowly or even inconsistently. Their approach has since been applied to nonparametric inference of the ATE
(
Benkeser et al. 2017
)
, mean outcomes under informative missingness
(
Díaz and van der Laan 2017
)
, counterfactual survival curves
(
Díaz 2019
)
, and semiparametric inference on the ATE in partially linear regression models
(
Dukes et al. 2021
)
. For inference on the ATE, these estimators involve iteratively debiasing initial estimators of the propensity score and outcome regression to linearize the doubly robust remainder term of the resulting parameter estimator. However, the TMLE-based framework for doubly robust inference has certain limitations. First, the iterative debiasing procedure used to obtain doubly robust inference is challenging to justify theoretically and can be computationally demanding to implement, posing challenges for its application to large-scale data and complex data structures. Second, when considering new parameters of interest, a new debiasing algorithm must be derived on a case-by-case basis, requiring careful analysis of the relevant remainder terms and novel targeting algorithms. Notably, to date, there is no unified approach for constructing DRAL estimators or obtaining doubly robust inference for the entire class of linear functionals of the outcome regression.

1.2
Contributions of this work

In this work, we aim to derive doubly robust inferential procedures for linear functionals of the outcome regression. Specifically, these procedures should facilitate calibrated inference even when one nuisance estimator converges to its intended target arbitrarily slowly or even inconsistently. We introduce a novel estimation framework, which we refer to as
calibrated debiased machine learning
(C-DML), that allows for the construction of DRAL estimators and confidence intervals that provide doubly robust inferences. To accomplish this, we rely on cross-fitted estimators of the outcome regression and Riesz representer obtained through any generic supervised learning algorithms. Within the C-DML procedure, calibrated nuisance estimators may be constructed, for example, using a generalization of isotonic calibration — a technique commonly used in prediction and classification tasks
(
Zadrozny and Elkan 2001
;
Niculescu-Mizil and Caruana 2005
;
van der Laan et al. 2023b
)
— based on loss functions tailored for the task at hand. These calibrated estimators are then used to create a calibrated one-step debiased estimator, which we refer to as a C-DML estimator. For inference on counterfactual means, an augmented inverse probability weighted estimator in which the cross-fitted propensity score and outcome regression estimators are calibrated using isotonic calibration provides an example of a C-DML estimator. We derive the closed-form influence function of our proposed estimators, allowing us to construct confidence intervals without needing to know which, if any, of the nuisance functions is possibly estimated too slowly or inconsistently. Moreover, to avoid the estimation of additional nuisance functions that arise in
van der Laan 2014
and related works, we propose an automated bootstrap-assisted procedure for doubly robust inference, which is both computationally efficient and valid as long as at least one of the nuisance estimators is consistent at a fast enough rate.

Our approach is motivated by the DRAL TMLE estimators of the ATE proposed in
van der Laan 2014
and
Benkeser et al. 2017
. While we share the objective of constructing nuisance estimators to achieve doubly robust asymptotic linearity by solving certain score equations, our strategy and implementation for doing so differs significantly, even when focusing on estimating the ATE. Specifically, our approach is a noniterative and computationally efficient two-step procedure that can be easily implemented using commonly available software for isotonic regression. Moreover, our method is not dependent on the specific form of the linear functional but rather on the availability of initial nuisance estimators and the ability to evaluate an empirical estimator of the linear functional as a black box. Furthermore, even in the special case of the ATE, our theoretical results refine those presented by
van der Laan 2014
and
Benkeser et al. 2017
, establishing that our estimator achieves asymptotic linearity not only under consistent estimation of one nuisance function alone but also when both nuisance functions are estimated consistently, with one estimated too slowly.

Prior work has empirically investigated the finite-sample benefits of calibrating propensity score estimates in IPW and AIPW estimators of the ATE
(
Gutman et al. 2022
;
Deshpande and Kuleshov 2023
;
Ballinari and Bearth 2024
)
. However, these studies have focused exclusively on the calibration of a single nuisance function — the propensity score — and a single parameter, the ATE. Additionally, none of these works has provided theoretical justification for the improved performance achieved through propensity score calibration beyond the observation that calibration can reduce the estimation error of nuisance functions. Our contribution extends this line of research by demonstrating that, for the general class of linear functionals, calibration of both the outcome regression and the Riesz representer is essential for further debiasing, both in finite samples and asymptotically, and for constructing DRAL estimators. In the special case of the ATE, we find that the outcome regression should also be calibrated and that it is the inverse propensity score, rather than the propensity score itself, that must be calibrated.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce the class of linear functional parameters we consider and provide background on the standard efficient one-step debiased estimator and its rate double robustness properties. In Section
3
, we formulate the problem of doubly robust inference, introduce a novel model-agnostic linear expansion for the bias of one-step debiased estimators, and demonstrate how further debiasing of nuisance functions can be employed to construct DRAL estimators. In Section
4
, we present a novel link between nuisance estimator calibration and the doubly robust inference properties of debiased machine learning estimators, use this link to build our general calibrated debiased machine learning framework, and propose our novel isotonic-calibrated DML estimator. In Section
5
, we establish the theoretical properties of our proposed C-DML estimators and bootstrap-assisted confidence intervals. Finally, in Section
6
, we investigate the empirical performance of C-DML in synthetic and semi-synthetic experiments. We provide concluding remarks in Section
7
.

Problem setup

2.1
Data structure and target parameter

Suppose that we have at our disposal a sample of
n
independent and identically distributed observations,
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}
, of the data-structure
Z:=(W,A,Y)
drawn from a probability distribution
P_{0}
. In this data-structure,
W\in\mathcal{W}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a vector of baseline covariates,
A\in\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{R}
is a potentially multi-valued treatment assignment, and
Y\in\mathbb{R}
is a bounded outcome. For any given distribution
P
, we define pointwise the outcome regression
\mu_{P}(a,w):=E_{P}(Y\,|\,A=a,W=w)
for any realization
(a,w)
in the
P
–support of
(A,W)
, and denote by
Pf
the integral
\int f(z)dP(z)
for any
P
–integrable real-valued function
f
.
When
\mathcal{A}
is discrete, we also define the (generalized) propensity score
\pi_{P}(a|w):=P(A=a\,|\,W=w)
. We denote by
P_{0,A,W}
the marginal distribution of
(A,W)
induced by
P_{0}
, by
\mathcal{H}:=L^{2}(P_{0,A,W})
the Hilbert space of real-valued
P_{0,A,W}
–square integrable functions on
\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{W}
equipped with the inner product
(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})\mapsto\langle\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\rangle:=\iint\mu_{1}(a,w)\mu_{2}(a,w)P_{0,A,W}(da,dw)
, and by
\|\cdot\|
the induced norm. Similarly, for any given distribution
P
, we denote by
\|\mu\|_{P}
the norm
\{\iint\mu(a,w)^{2}P_{A,W}(da,dw)\}^{\frac{1}{2}}
of
\mu
, where
P_{A,W}
is the marginal distribution of
(A,W)
induced by
P
. We suppose that
\mathcal{M}
is a locally nonparametric model for
P_{0}
satisfying the following variation independence property: for every
\mu\in\mathcal{H}
, there exists
P\in\mathcal{M}
such that
P_{A,W}=P_{0,A,W}
and
\mu_{P}=\mu
. To simplify notation, we write
S_{0}
for any summary
S_{P_{0}}
of the true data-generating distribution
P_{0}
.

Our objective is to obtain doubly robust nonparametric inference for a feature
\tau_{0}:=\psi_{0}(\mu_{0})
of the outcome regression
\mu_{0}
arising from a continuous linear functional
\psi_{0}:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Here,
\psi_{0}
represents the evaluation at
P_{0}
of a smooth mapping
P\mapsto\psi_{P}(\cdot)
defined on the statistical model
\mathcal{M}
. In other words, we explicitly allow the functional of interest to itself depend on the unknown data-generating distribution
P_{0}
. We require
\psi_{0}
to satisfy the following conditions:

A1)

(linearity and boundedness)
it holds that
\psi_{0}(c\mu_{1}+\mu_{2})=c\psi_{0}(\mu_{1})+\psi_{0}(\mu_{2})
for each
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in\mathcal{H}
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
and that
\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{H}}|\psi_{0}(\mu)|/\|\mu\|<\infty
;

A2)

(Hellinger continuity):

P\mapsto\psi_{P}
is a continuous functional at
P_{0}
with domain
\mathcal{M}
equipped with the Hellinger distance and range
\{\psi_{P}:P\in\mathcal{M}\}
equipped with the operator norm;

A3)

(pathwise differentiability)
for each
\mu\in\mathcal{H}
,
P\mapsto\psi_{P}(\mu)
is pathwise differentiable at
P_{0}
relative to
\mathcal{M}
with efficient influence function
\widetilde{\phi}_{0,\mu}:=\phi_{0,\mu}-P_{0}\phi_{0,\mu}
, where
\phi_{0,\mu}\in\mathcal{H}
is such that, for some
L\in(0,\infty)

|\phi_{0,\mu_{1}}(A,W)-\phi_{0,\mu_{2}}(A,W)|\leq L|\mu_{1}(A,W)-\mu_{2}(A,W)|
holds
P_{0,A,W}
–almost surely for each
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in\mathcal{H}
;

We also assume that we can construct a well-behaved estimator
\psi_{n}
of
\psi_{0}
based on the available data, such that
\psi_{n}(\mu)=\psi_{0}(\mu)+(P_{n}-P_{0})\widetilde{\phi}_{0,\mu}+o_{p}(n^{-\frac{1}{2}})
locally uniformly around each
\mu\in\mathcal{H}
— this requirement is stated precisely as Condition
C7
later.

The target parameter mapping
P\mapsto\Psi(P):=\psi_{P}(\mu_{P})
we consider encompasses various interesting parameters that exhibit linear dependence on the outcome regression. Notably, many estimands of interest in causal inference, such as counterfactual means and the ATE, correspond to functionals of the form

\mu\mapsto\psi_{0}(\mu)=E_{0}\left\{m(A,W,\mu)\right\}

(1)

for a known map
m:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{W}\times\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. For example, the counterfactual mean outcome under an intervention that sets treatment to level
a_{0}\in\mathcal{A}
is obtained by taking
m:(a,w,\mu)\mapsto\mu(a_{0},w)
, whereas the ATE is obtained by taking
m:(a,w,\mu)\mapsto\mu(1,w)-\mu(0,w)

\psi_{0}
satisfies
A1

is linear in its last argument and if, for example, there exists some
M\in(0,\infty)

\mu\in\mathcal{H}

|m(A,W,\mu)|\leq M|\mu(A,W)|

\phi_{0,\mu}:=m(\cdot,\mu)

\psi_{0}(\mu)

\psi_{n}(\mu):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}m(A_{i},W_{i},\mu)

\mu\mapsto\psi_{n}(\mu)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\{\mu(1,W_{i})-\mu(0,W_{i})\}

Chernozhukov et al. 2018b

Rotnitzky et al. 2021

By the Riesz representation theorem, there exists a unique nonparametric Riesz representer
\alpha_{0}\in\mathcal{H}

\psi_{0}

\mu\mapsto\psi_{0}(\mu)=\langle\alpha_{0},\mu\rangle

\alpha\mapsto\|\alpha\|^{2}-2\psi_{0}(\alpha)

\mathcal{H}

Chernozhukov et al. 2021

\tau_{0}
. In particular,
\alpha_{0}
appears in the efficient influence function of the parameter
P\mapsto\psi_{P}(\mu_{P})
, viewed as a functional on the nonparametric statistical model
\mathcal{M}
, and this plays a critical role in the debiasing of plug-in estimators based on the functional
\psi_{n}
. Below, for any
\mu,\alpha\in\mathcal{H}
, we denote by
D_{\mu,\alpha}
the mapping
z\mapsto\alpha(a,w)\left\{y-\mu(a,w)\right\}
.

Theorem 1
.

Under conditions
A1
–
A3
,
P\mapsto\psi_{P}(\mu_{P})
is pathwise differentiable at
P_{0}
under the model
\mathcal{M}
and has nonparametric efficient influence function
D_{0}:=\widetilde{\phi}_{0,\mu_{0}}+D_{\mu_{0},\alpha_{0}}
with
\widetilde{\phi}_{0,\mu_{0}}
defined in
A3

\mu,\alpha\in\mathcal{H}

\psi_{0}

\psi_{0}(\mu)-\psi_{0}(\mu_{0})=-P_{0}D_{\mu,\alpha}+\langle\mu-\mu_{0},\alpha_{0}-\alpha\rangle\,.

As indicated above, our framework covers various pathwise differentiable parameters in semiparametric and nonparametric statistics of particular interest in causal inference. Below, we provide various examples.

Example 1

(counterfactual means under general interventions)
.

Let
Y^{a_{0}}
denote the potential outcome that would be observed if treatment
a_{0}\in\mathcal{A}
were administered. Under certain causal assumptions
(
Rubin 1974
)
, the mean of
Y^{a_{0}}
is identified by
\tau_{0}:=E_{0}\{\mu_{0}(a_{0},W)\}
, which corresponds to the linear functional
\psi_{0}:\mu\mapsto E_{0}\{\mu(a_{0},W)\}
. Doubly robust inference for this parameter was considered in
van der Laan 2014
and
Benkeser et al. 2017
. More generally, the mean of the counterfactual outcome under a stochastic intervention that, given
W=w
, draws a treatment value
A

a\mapsto\omega_{0}(a|w)
depending only on
P_{0,A,W}
can be identified by
\tau_{0}:=\int\mu_{0}(a,w)\omega_{0}(a|w)P_{0,A,W}(da,dw)
. If
(a,w)\mapsto\alpha_{0}(a,w):=\omega_{0}(a|w)/\pi_{0}(a|w)

A1
–
A3
hold and
\alpha_{0}
is the nonparametric Riesz representer of
\tau_{0}
.

Example 2

(average treatment effect under covariate-dependent outcome missingness)
.

Suppose that the observed data unit consists of
(W,S,\Delta,\Delta Y)
, where
S\in\{0,1\}
is a binary treatment,
\Delta\in\{0,1\}
is a missingness indicator taking value
1
if
Y
is observed, and
\Delta Y
is the observed outcome that equals
Y
if
\Delta=1
and
0
otherwise. Under certain causal assumptions, the ATE under covariate-dependent outcome missingness is identified by the estimand
\tau_{0}:=E_{0}\{E_{0}(\Delta Y\,|\,S=1,\Delta=1,W)-E_{0}(\Delta Y\,|\,S=0,\Delta=1,W)\}
. Doubly robust inference for this estimand was considered in
Díaz and van der Laan 2017
. Using the notation of this section, we can write
Z:=(W,A,Y^{\prime})
, where
A:=(S,\Delta)
is a multi-valued interventional variable and
Y^{\prime}:=\Delta Y
is an outcome with missingness. The linear functional corresponding with
\tau_{0}
is
\psi_{0}:\mu\mapsto E_{0}\{\mu(1,1,W)-\mu(0,1,W)\}

is given by
\alpha_{0}:(s,\delta,w)\mapsto(2s-1)\delta/P_{0}(\Delta=1,S=s\,|\,W=w)

Example 3

(projection onto semiparametric working model)
.

Suppose now that the parameter of interest is
\tau_{0}=E_{0}\{m(A,W,\mu_{0})\}
for a known function
m:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{W}\times\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. If we believe that
\mu_{0}
can be approximated well by elements in a closed linear subspace
\Theta\subset\mathcal{H}
, we could consider instead of
\tau_{0}
the working estimand
E_{0}\{m(A,W,\Pi_{0}\mu_{0})\}
with
\Pi_{0}:\mu\mapsto\argmin_{\theta\in\Theta}\|\mu-\theta\|
defined on
\mathcal{H}
. For example, we may take
\Theta
to be a partially linear regression model that assumes a known parametric form for the conditional average treatment effect (CATE). Doubly robust inference for the ATE in partially linear regression models was considered in
Dukes et al. 2021
. The nonparametric Riesz representer of the working functional
\mu\mapsto E_{0}\{m(A,W,\Pi_{0}\mu)\}
is given by
\Pi_{0}\alpha_{0}
, where
\alpha_{0}
is the nonparametric Riesz representer of the original functional
\mu\mapsto E_{0}\{m(A,W,\mu)\}
. For a finite dimensional sieve
\Theta_{n}\subset\Theta

\mu\mapsto\psi_{n}(\mu):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}m(A_{i},W_{i},\Pi_{n}\mu)

\Pi_{n}\mu:=\argmin_{\mu\in\Theta_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\{Y_{i}-\mu(A_{i},W_{i})\}^{2}
.

2.2
One-step debiased estimation and rate double robustness

Suppose we are given arbitrary estimators
\mu_{n}
and
\alpha_{n}
of the outcome regression
\mu_{0}
and Riesz representer
\alpha_{0}
. For example, as in
Chernozhukov et al. 2018b
;
Chernozhukov et al. 2021
, the estimators
\mu_{n}
and
\alpha_{n}
can be learned directly using any supervised machine learning algorithm based on the least-squares loss
(z,\mu)\mapsto\{y-\mu(a,w)\}^{2}
and the Riesz loss
(z,\alpha)\mapsto\alpha^{2}(a,w)-2\psi_{n}(\alpha)
, respectively. Given these nuisance estimators, the plug-in estimator
\psi_{n}(\mu_{n})
could be used to estimate
\tau_{0}
. However, when
\mu_{n}
is obtained using flexible statistical learning tools, this estimator typically lacks both
n^{\frac{1}{2}}
–consistency and asymptotic normality due to excessive bias that arises from the first-order dependence of
\psi_{n}(\mu_{n})-\psi_{0}(\mu_{0})
on the nuisance estimation error
\mu_{n}-\mu_{0}

(
van der Laan and Rose 2011
;
Chernozhukov et al. 2018b
)
. To overcome this sensitivity, debiasing methods must typically be used to eliminate the first-order bias of the plug-in estimator
\psi_{n}(\mu_{n})
. For example, the one-step debiased estimator
(
Chernozhukov et al. 2018b
;
Chernozhukov et al. 2021
)

\tau_{n}:=\psi_{n}(\mu_{n})+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{n}(A_{i},W_{i})\{Y_{i}-\mu_{n}(A_{i},W_{i})\}

could be used. The incorporation of this debiasing step renders the resulting one-step debiased estimator
\tau_{n}
insensitive not only to the nuisance estimation error
\mu_{n}-\mu_{0}
but also to
\alpha_{n}-\alpha_{0}
, thereby allowing for fast estimation rates and inference for
\tau_{0}
, even when
\alpha_{0}
and
\mu_{0}
are estimated at comparatively slow rates. The above estimator is a generalization of the well-known augmented inverse probability weighted estimator
(
Robins et al. 1994
;
Robins et al. 1995
;
Bang and Robins 2005
)
and has also been referred to as an augmented balancing weighted (ABW) estimator
(
Bruns-Smith et al. 2023
)
.

Suppose that both
\alpha_{n}
and
\mu_{n}
tend to possibly incorrect limits
\overline{\alpha}_{0}
and
\overline{\mu}_{0}
, in the sense that
\|\alpha_{n}-\overline{\alpha}_{0}\|=o_{p}(1)
and
\|\mu_{n}-\overline{\mu}_{0}\|=o_{p}(1)
, with either
\overline{\mu}_{0}=\mu_{0}
or
\overline{\alpha}_{0}=\alpha_{0}
. To understand the asymptotic properties of the one-step debiased estimator
\tau_{n}
, it is useful to scrutinize the expansion in Theorem
1
, from which the doubly robust decomposition of the estimation error

\tau_{n}-\tau_{0}=(P_{n}-P_{0})(\widetilde{\phi}_{0,\mu_{n}}+D_{\mu_{n},\alpha_{n}})+\text{Rem}_{\mu_{n}}(\psi_{n},\psi_{0})+\langle\alpha_{0}-\alpha_{n},\mu_{n}-\mu_{0}\rangle

(2)

can be derived, where

D_{\mu_{n},\alpha_{n}}
is an estimator of the efficient influence function component
D_{\mu_{0},\alpha_{0}}
in Theorem
1
,
\text{Rem}_{\mu_{n}}(\psi_{n},\psi_{0}):=\psi_{n}(\mu_{n})-\psi_{0}(\mu_{n})-(P_{n}-P_{0})\widetilde{\phi}_{0,\mu_{n}}
is a linearization remainder, and
\widetilde{\phi}_{0,\mu_{n}}
is as defined in
A3
. Under weak conditions, the first term in the decomposition is asymptotically linear, satisfying
(P_{n}-P_{0})(\widetilde{\phi}_{0,\mu_{n}}+D_{\mu_{n},\alpha_{n}})=(P_{n}-P_{0})\overline{D}_{0}+o_{p}(n^{-\frac{1}{2}})
, where the influence function
\overline{D}_{0}:=\widetilde{\phi}_{0,\overline{\mu}_{0}}+D_{\overline{\mu}_{0},\overline{\alpha}_{0}}
reduces to the efficient influence function in Theorem
1
when both
\overline{\mu}_{0}=\mu_{0}
and
\overline{\alpha}_{0}=\alpha_{0}
. The asymptotic linearity of
\psi_{n}
in a locally uniform sense implies that the second term is asymptotically negligible, in that
\text{Rem}_{\mu_{n}}(\psi_{n},\psi_{0})=o_{p}(n^{-\frac{1}{2}})
, and is, in fact, identically zero for functionals of the form given in (
1
). Consequently, even in the case of inconsistent estimation of one nuisance function, it can typically be established, under regularity conditions, that

\tau_{n}-\tau_{0}=(P_{n}-P_{0})\overline{D}_{0}+o_{p}(n^{-\frac{1}{2}})+\langle\alpha_{0}-\alpha_{n},\mu_{n}-\mu_{0}\rangle\,.

(3)

As a result, the bias of the one-step debiased estimator is primarily driven by the cross-product (or mixed) bias term
\langle\alpha_{0}-\alpha_{n},\mu_{n}-\mu_{0}\rangle
, whose absolute value is bounded above by
\|\alpha_{n}-\alpha_{0}\|\|\mu_{n}-\mu_{0}\|
in view of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. Most of the existing literature aims to show that
\tau_{n}
is asymptotically linear with influence function
\overline{D}_{0}
. When
\tau_{n}
satisfies the above, this is only possible if
\langle\alpha_{0}-\alpha_{n},\mu_{n}-\mu_{0}\rangle=o_{p}(n^{-\frac{1}{2}})
. To ensure that this rate condition holds, the product rate condition
\|\alpha_{n}-\alpha_{0}\|\|\mu_{n}-\mu_{0}\|=o_{p}(n^{-\frac{1}{2}})
is often assumed, which typically requires consistent estimation of both nuisance functions at sufficiently fast rates
(
Robins et al. 1994
;
Robins et al. 1995
;
van der Laan and Robins 2003
;
Bang and Robins 2005
;
Chernozhukov et al. 2018a
)
. In particular, this condition holds if both
\|\alpha_{n}-\alpha_{0}\|=o_{p}(n^{-\frac{1}{4}})
and
\|\mu_{n}-\mu_{0}\|=o_{p}(n^{-\frac{1}{4}})
.

As a consequence of (
2
), the one-step debiased estimator
\tau_{n}
is said to be rate doubly robust
(
Rotnitzky et al. 2021
)
in the nuisance estimation errors in that asymptotic linearity and efficiency can be achieved if
\|\alpha_{n}-\alpha_{0}\|=o_{p}(1)
,
\|\mu_{n}-\mu_{0}\|=o_{p}(1)
and
\|\alpha_{n}-\alpha_{0}\|\|\mu_{n}-\mu_{0}\|=o_{p}(n^{-\frac{1}{2}})
. Moreover,
\tau_{n}
is doubly robust in terms of consistency: it converges in probability to
\tau_{0}
as long as
\|\alpha_{n}-\alpha_{0}\|=o_{p}(1)
or
\|\mu_{n}-\mu_{0}\|=o_{p}(1)
. While it maintains consistency under inconsistent estimation of either the outcome regression
\mu_{0}
or Riesz representer
\alpha_{0}
, the one-step debiased estimator typically lacks desirable properties necessary for inference, such as
n^{\frac{1}{2}}
–consistency and asymptotic normality, in such scenarios. This is also the case when both nuisance estimators are estimated consistently but at insufficiently fast rates so that the condition
\|\alpha_{n}-\alpha_{0}\|\|\mu_{n}-\mu_{0}\|=o_{p}(n^{-\frac{1}{2}})
does not hold. Consequently, despite rate double robustness,
\tau_{n}
is typically only asymptotically linear when
\mu_{0}
and
\alpha_{0}
are sufficiently structured (e.g., smooth or sparse) to be estimated at fast enough rates, and this represents a stringent condition that may fail to hold in practice.

3
Preliminaries on doubly robust inference

3.1
Statistical objective: doubly robust asymptotic linearity

In this work, we seek to construct estimators of
\tau_{0}
that are doubly robust asymptotically linear (DRAL), in the sense that they are asymptotically linear if any of the following hold:
\|\alpha_{n}-\alpha_{0}\|^{2}=o_{p}(n^{-\frac{1}{2}})
,
\|\mu_{n}-\mu_{0}\|^{2}=o_{p}(n^{-\frac{1}{2}})
or
\|\alpha_{n}-\alpha_{0}\|\|\mu_{n}-\mu_{0}\|=o_{p}(n^{-\frac{1}{2}})
. The DRAL property substantially improves upon rate double robustness as it only requires that
\|\alpha_{n}-\alpha_{0}\|\wedge\|\mu_{n}-\mu_{0}\|=o_{p}(n^{-\frac{1}{4}})
and therefore allows one of the nuisance estimators to converge arbitrarily slowly to its target or even be inconsistent. To illustrate this improvement, suppose that
\mu_{0}
and
\alpha_{0}
are known only to be Hölder smooth with respective exponents
\beta>0
and
\gamma>0
. In such cases, achieving asymptotic linearity under rate double robustness generally requires that
\beta+\gamma>d
, where
d
is the dimension of the covariate vector
W

(
Robins et al. 2008
)
. Thus, under rate double robustness, if one of the nuisance functions is insufficiently smooth so that the minimum nuisance smoothness satisfies
\beta\wedge\gamma\ll d/2
, achieving asymptotic linearity typically necessitates the other nuisance function to exhibit a high degree of smoothness, which may not be satisfied by the data-generating mechanism in many applications. In contrast, achieving asymptotic linearity under the DRAL property only requires that the maximum nuisance smoothness satisfy
\beta\vee\gamma>d/2
. As a result, DRAL estimators can maintain asymptotic linearity even when one of the nuisance functions is arbitrarily non-smooth, as long as the other nuisance function has smoothness exponent larger than
d/2
.

As illustrated in the previous section, the lack of normality of the one-step debiased estimator under inconsistent or insufficiently fast estimation of one of the nuisance functions occurs because in such case the cross-product remainder term
\langle\alpha_{0}-\alpha_{n},\mu_{n}-\mu_{0}\rangle
becomes sensitive to the estimation rate of the consistent nuisance estimator. This sensitivity leads to the cross-product remainder term contributing to the asymptotic bias and variance of
n^{\frac{1}{2}}(\tau_{n}-\tau_{0})
. Therefore, to construct a DRAL estimator, it is critical to ensure that the bias from the cross-product term
\langle\alpha_{0}-\alpha_{n},\mu_{n}-\mu_{0}\rangle
in (
2
) is corrected for, making it either asymptotically linear or negligible, even if one of the nuisance estimators converges to its target too slowly or inconsistently, in the sense that
\|\alpha_{n}-\alpha_{0}\|\|\mu_{n}-\mu_{0}\|\neq o_{p}(n^{-\frac{1}{2}})
. In the remainder of this section, we examine this bias term in greater detail, illustrating how additional debiasing efforts can render it asymptotically linear in such cases.

3.2
General strategy: debiasing to linearize the cross-product remainder term

In this section, we illustrate how additional debiasing of the nuisance estimators
\alpha_{n}
and
\mu_{n}
linearizes the cross-product remainder term
\langle\alpha_{0}-\alpha_{n},\mu_{n}-\mu_{0}\rangle
. Our key idea, rooted in
van der Laan 2014
, is to express the cross-product remainder term
\langle\alpha_{0}-\alpha_{n},\mu_{n}-\mu_{0}\rangle
solely in terms of the
most favorable
nuisance estimator and the corresponding nuisance function. Here, we use
most favorable
to refer to the nuisance estimator that estimates its intended target at the fastest rate. We can then show that if the most favorable nuisance estimator satisfies certain empirical orthogonality (or score) equations, the cross-product remainder term is debiased and asymptotically linear under relatively weak conditions. This strategy was originally used in
van der Laan 2014
and
Benkeser et al. 2017
to construct DRAL estimators of the ATE for the special case where one of the nuisance functions is estimated inconsistently. However, the important regimes in which both nuisance functions are consistently estimated but one is estimated too slowly were not studied. In this section, in addition to studying a broad class of statistical parameters, we establish the asymptotic linearity of the remainder in general, and provide a refined analysis to establish the asymptotic negligibility of the remainder in such regimes.

To provide intuition and elucidate key ideas, we first present an informal analysis of the cross-product remainder term for the case where
\mu_{n}
is most favorable, which will be useful when the outcome regression is consistently estimated but the Riesz representer is estimated inconsistently or at a slower rate. We first introduce the following notation. For any vector-valued function
v:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{W}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{k}
with
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we define the conditional expectation projection operator
\Pi_{v}:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}
as the mapping
f\mapsto\Pi_{v}f:=\argmin_{\theta\in\Theta_{v}}\|f-\theta\|
, where
\Theta_{v}
is the set of functions of the form
h\circ v
for any
h:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Whenever
v
and
f
are nonrandom functions, we have that
\Pi_{v}f:(a,w)\mapsto E_{0}[f(A,W)\,|\,v(A,W)=v(a,w)]
. For brevity, we denote the orthogonal projections of the error terms
\alpha_{0}-\alpha_{n}
and
\mu_{0}-\mu_{n}
onto
\mu_{n}
and
\alpha_{n}
, respectively, as
s_{n,0}:=\Pi_{\mu_{n}}(\alpha_{0}-\alpha_{n})
and
r_{n,0}:=\Pi_{\alpha_{n}}(\mu_{0}-\mu_{n})
.
Following the strategy of
van der Laan 2014
, when
\mu_{n}
is consistent for
\mu_{0}
, and
\alpha_{n}
is inconsistent or converges to
\alpha_{0}
slower than
\mu_{n}
tends to
\mu_{0}
, we aim to express the cross-product remainder
\langle\alpha_{0}-\alpha_{n},\mu_{n}-\mu_{0}\rangle
solely in terms of
\mu_{n}
and
\mu_{0}
by applying the law of total expectation to condition on the random vector
(\mu_{n}(A,W),\mu_{0}(A,W))
within the
P_{0}
–expectation. Doing so, we find that the cross-product remainder term can be written as

\displaystyle\langle\alpha_{0}-\alpha_{n},\mu_{n}-\mu_{0}\rangle\

\displaystyle=\ \langle\Pi_{(\mu_{n},\mu_{0})}(\alpha_{0}-\alpha_{n}),\mu_{n}-\mu_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=\ \langle\Pi_{\mu_{n}}(\alpha_{0}-\alpha_{n}),\mu_{n}-\mu_{0}\rangle+\text{ higher-order terms}

\displaystyle=\ \langle s_{n,0},\mu_{n}-\mu_{0}\rangle+\text{ higher-order terms}\,,

where, informally, the higher-order terms arise because
\Pi_{(\mu_{n},\mu_{0})}\approx\Pi_{\mu_{n}}
whenever
\mu_{n}\approx\mu_{0}
under regularity conditions. The key bias term precluding asymptotic linearity of the one-step debiased estimator under inconsistent or insufficiently fast estimation of
\alpha_{0}
is driven by the population moment expression
\langle s_{n,0},\mu_{n}-\mu_{0}\rangle
for the outcome nuisance estimator
\mu_{n}
. To analyze this term further, we note that

\displaystyle\langle s_{n,0},\mu_{n}-\mu_{0}\rangle\

\displaystyle=\ -\int s_{n,0}(a,w)\{y-\mu_{n}(a,w)\}\,dP_{0}(z)

\displaystyle=\ (P_{n}-P_{0})A_{n,0}-P_{n}A_{n,0}\,,

where
A_{n,0}
is defined as the map
z\mapsto s_{n,0}(a,w)\{y-\mu_{n}(a,w)\}
. The first term is a so-called drift term that is asymptotically linear under conditions, satisfying
(P_{n}-P_{0})A_{n,0}=P_{n}A_{0}+o_{p}(n^{-\frac{1}{2}})
with
A_{0}:z\mapsto s_{0}(a,w)\{y-\mu_{0}(a,w)\}
and
s_{0}:=\Pi_{\mu_{0}}(\alpha_{0}-\overline{\alpha}_{0})
. The second term is typically a first-order bias term that precludes the asymptotic linearity of the cross-product remainder in this case.

As a consequence, to debias the cross-product remainder with respect to the estimation error of
\alpha_{n}
, it suffices that the residuals
Y_{1}-\mu_{n}(A_{1},W_{1}),\ldots,Y_{n}-\mu_{n}(A_{n},W_{n})
for
\mu_{0}
be orthogonalized in
L^{2}(P_{n})
to the projected residuals
s_{n,0}(A_{1},W_{1}),\ldots,s_{n,0}(A_{n},W_{n})
for
\alpha_{0}
, in the sense that

P_{n}A_{n,0}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{n,0}(A_{i},W_{i})\{Y_{i}-\mu_{n}(A_{i},W_{i})\}=0\,.

(4)

A similar analysis can be applied to the cross-product remainder term for the case where the Riesz representer estimator
\alpha_{n}
is most favorable by instead conditioning on
(\alpha_{n},\alpha_{0})
. In particular, when
\alpha_{n}
is consistent for
\alpha_{0}
but
\mu_{n}
is either inconsistent or converges to
\mu_{0}
slower than
\alpha_{n}
tends to
\alpha_{0}
, the estimator
\alpha_{n}
must satisfy the empirical orthogonality condition

\psi_{n}(r_{n,0})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{n,0}(A_{i},W_{i})\alpha_{n}(A_{i},W_{i})=0

(5)

for the cross-product remainder term to be debiased with respect to the estimation error of
\mu_{n}
.
To understand why this also represents an orthogonality condition, we note that (
5
) can be rewritten as
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{n,0}(A_{i},W_{i})\{V_{n,i}-\alpha_{n}(A_{i},W_{i})\}=0,
where
V_{n,1},\ldots,V_{n,n}
together form the empirical Riesz representer of
\psi_{n}
, defined implicitly by the equation
\psi_{n}(h)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(A_{i},W_{i})V_{n,i}
for each
h\in\mathcal{H}
.

We now formalize the heuristic arguments above and establish that properties (
4
) and (
5
) indeed suffice to debias the cross-product remainder term with respect to the error in the Riesz representer and outcome regression estimators, respectively. In the next section, we provide a simple method for post-hoc construction of nuisance estimators that satisfy properties (
4
) and (
5
) exactly. For the moment, we take for granted that nuisance estimators
\mu_{n}
and
\alpha_{n}
satisfying these properties can be constructed. We define
B_{0}:=\psi_{0}(r_{0})+\widetilde{\phi}_{r_{0}}-r_{0}\alpha_{0}
with
r_{0}:=\Pi_{\alpha_{0}}(\mu_{0}-\overline{\mu}_{0})
, and
B_{n,0}:=\psi_{0}(r_{n})+\widetilde{\phi}_{r_{n}}-r_{n}\alpha_{n}
, and recall the definitions
A_{0}:z\mapsto s_{0}(a,w)\{y-\mu_{0}(a,w)\}
and
A_{n,0}:z\mapsto s_{n,0}(a,w)\{y-\mu_{n}(a,w)\}
. We also denote by
\Delta_{\alpha,n}:=\alpha_{n}-\alpha_{0}
and
\Delta_{\mu,n}:=\mu_{n}-\mu_{0}
the nuisance estimation errors. The theorem we now state relies on the following condition to relate the estimation error in the projection operators to the nuisance estimation errors
\|\mu_{n}-\mu_{0}\|
and
\|\alpha_{n}-\alpha_{0}\|
:

B1)

Coupling between errors:

i)

\|(\Pi_{\mu_{n}}-\Pi_{\mu_{0}})\Delta_{\alpha,n}\|+\|(\Pi_{\mu_{n},\mu_{0}}-\Pi_{\mu_{n}})\Delta_{\alpha,n}\|=O_{p}(\|\mu_{n}-\mu_{0}\|)
;

ii)

\|(\Pi_{\alpha_{n}}-\Pi_{\alpha_{0}})\Delta_{\mu,n}\|+\|(\Pi_{\alpha_{n},\alpha_{0}}-\Pi_{\alpha_{n}})\Delta_{\mu,n}\|=O_{p}(\|\alpha_{n}-\alpha_{0}\|)
.

Theorem 2
.

If
\mu_{n}
satisfies (
4
) and condition
B1i
holds, the outcome regression-favorable decomposition

\displaystyle\langle\alpha_{0}-\alpha_{n},\mu_{n}-\mu_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=P_{n}A_{0}+(P_{n}-P_{0})(A_{n,0}-A_{0})+O_{p}\left(\|\mu_{n}-\mu_{0}\|^{2}\wedge\|\mu_{n}-\mu_{0}\|\|\alpha_{n}-\alpha_{0}\|\right)

holds. If
\alpha_{n}
satisfies (
5
) and condition
B1ii
holds, the Riesz representer-favorable decomposition

\displaystyle\langle\alpha_{0}-\alpha_{n},\mu_{n}-\mu_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=P_{n}B_{0}+(P_{n}-P_{0})(B_{n,0}-B_{0})+O_{p}\left(\|\alpha_{n}-\alpha_{0}\|^{2}\wedge\|\mu_{n}-\mu_{0}\|\|\alpha_{n}-\alpha_{0}\|\right)+Rem_{\psi_{n}}(r_{n})

holds with

Rem_{\psi_{n}}(r_{n}):=\psi_{n}(r_{n})-\psi_{0}(r_{n})-(P_{n}-P_{0})\widetilde{\phi}_{r_{n}}
.

Importantly, the second-order terms in both decompositions above are second-order in the estimation error of their respective favorable nuisance function. As a consequence, when all conditions of the theorem hold, we find that the bias term is second-order in the estimation rate of whichever nuisance function estimator converges fastest to its intended target. When
\|\mu_{n}-\mu_{0}\|=o_{p}(1)
, the outcome-favorable decomposition establishes that asymptotic linearization of the cross-product remainder requires that either
\|\mu_{n}-\mu_{0}\|^{2}=o_{p}(n^{-\frac{1}{2}})
or
\|\mu_{n}-\mu_{0}\|\|\alpha_{n}-\alpha_{0}\|=o_{p}(n^{-\frac{1}{2}})
. Similarly, when
\|\alpha_{n}-\alpha_{0}\|=o_{p}(1)
, the Riesz representer-favorable decomposition establishes that asymptotic linearization of the cross-product remainder requires that either
\|\alpha_{n}-\alpha_{0}\|^{2}=o_{p}(n^{-\frac{1}{2}})
or
\|\mu_{n}-\mu_{0}\|\|\alpha_{n}-\alpha_{0}\|=o_{p}(n^{-\frac{1}{2}})
. Notably, if both nuisance functions are consistently estimated, that is,
\overline{\alpha}_{0}=\alpha_{0}
and
\overline{\mu}_{0}=\mu_{0}
, then
A_{0}=B_{0}=0
and the cross-product remainder can be expressed purely in terms of second-order remainders that are
o_{p}(n^{-\frac{1}{2}})
under usual rate conditions. As a consequence, provided that
\mu_{n}
and
\alpha_{n}
simultaneously satisfy (
4
) and (
5
) and that the remainders are
o_{p}(n^{-\frac{1}{2}})
, Theorem
2
establishes that the cross-product remainder
\langle\alpha_{0}-\alpha_{n},\mu_{n}-\mu_{0}\rangle
is a DRAL estimator of 0 with a possibly degenerate influence function
1(\overline{\alpha}_{0}\neq\alpha_{0})A_{0}+1(\overline{\mu}_{0}\neq\mu_{0})B_{0}
. Furthermore, if the estimation error for
\tau_{n}
satisfies the asymptotic expansion in (
3
), then the one-step debiased estimator
\tau_{n}
of
\tau_{0}
with suitably orthogonalized nuisance estimators
\mu_{n}
and
\alpha_{n}
will be DRAL with influence function
\overline{D}_{0}+1(\overline{\mu}_{0}\neq\mu_{0})A_{0}+1(\overline{\alpha}_{0}\neq\alpha_{0})B_{0}
.

To formally establish that the cross-product remainder is DRAL, we need to show that the remainder terms in the outcome regression- and Riesz representer-favorable decompositions are asymptotically negligible, that is, of rate
o_{p}(n^{-\frac{1}{2}})
, even under inconsistent or insufficiently fast estimation of
\mu_{0}
or
\alpha_{0}
. Asymptotic negligibility holds for the empirical process remainders
(P_{n}-P_{0})(A_{n,0}-A_{0})
and
(P_{n}-P_{0})(B_{n,0}-B_{0})
, as long as
\|A_{n,0}-A_{0}\|=o_{p}(1)
,
\|B_{n,0}-B_{0}\|=o_{p}(1)
, and the functions
A_{n,0}-A_{0}
and
B_{n,0}-B_{0}
fall, for example, in a Donsker function class with probability tending to one. Consistency of
A_{n,0}
and
B_{n,0}
only requires consistent estimation of the associated favorable nuisance functions, as under
B1
and boundedness of the nuisance estimators, we have that
\|A_{n,0}-A_{0}\|=O_{p}(\|\mu_{n}-\mu_{0}\|)
and
\|B_{n,0}-B_{0}\|=O_{p}(\|\alpha_{n}-\alpha_{0}\|)
.

High-level conditions similar to
B1
have appeared in prior work on doubly robust inference, such as
van der Laan 2014
,
Benkeser et al. 2017
, and
Dukes et al. 2021
. To illustrate this condition heuristically, we may expect the projection
\Pi_{\mu_{n},\mu_{0}}\Delta_{\alpha,n}
to be approximated well by
\Pi_{\mu_{n}}\Delta_{\alpha,n}
as long as
\mu_{n}
converges to
\mu_{0}
in an appropriate sense. This is because conditioning on the ‘information’ contained in
\{\mu_{n}(A,W),\mu_{0}(A,W),Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}\}
should be similar to conditioning on the ‘information’ contained in
\{\mu_{n}(A,W),Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}\}
. The following lemma establishes that Lipschitz continuity of two bivariate functions is sufficient for
B1
to hold, a novel result that is relevant also to prior works requiring a similar condition:

B1
enumi

Lipschitz continuity:
Denoting
\mathcal{D}_{n}:=\{Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}\}
, it holds that, for all
n
large enough:

i)

(\widehat{m},m)\mapsto E_{0}[\Delta_{\alpha,n}(A,W)\,|\,\mu_{n}(A,W)=\widehat{m},\mu_{0}(A,W)=m,\mathcal{D}_{n}]
is
P_{0}^{n}
–almost surely Lipschitz continuous with fixed constant
L<\infty
;

ii)

(\widehat{a},a)\mapsto E_{0}[\Delta_{\mu,n}(A,W)\,|\,\alpha_{n}(A,W)=\widehat{a},\alpha_{0}(A,W)=a,\mathcal{D}_{n}]
is
P_{0}^{n}
–almost surely Lipschitz continuous with fixed constant
L<\infty
.

Lemma 1
.

Conditions
B1i
and
B1ii
are implied, respectively, by conditions
enumii
and
enumiii
.

Conditions analogous to
enumii
and
enumiii
were also imposed in
Bonvini et al. 2024
to construct a variant of the DRAL estimator of
van der Laan 2014
for the ATE parameter, and in
Wang et al. 2023
to analyze regressions involving estimated features. Condition
enumi
implies that a small change in the value of one nuisance function at a point should, on average, lead to a small change in the estimation error for the other nuisance function at that point.
Bonvini et al. 2024
verified this condition in a toy example in which
\mu_{n}(A,W)
,
\mu_{0}(A,W)
,
\alpha_{n}(A,W)
and
\alpha_{0}(A,W)
jointly follow a multivariate normal distribution conditional on
\mathcal{D}_{n}
. Lipschitz continuity is a fairly mild smoothness condition, and so, we hypothesize that these conditions or the higher-level conditions of
B1
are plausibly satisfied by real data-generating processes. Our experimental results support this hypothesis empirically.

3.3
Debiasing via targeted minimum loss-based estimation

For the special case of doubly robust inference on the average treatment effect,
van der Laan 2014
and
Benkeser et al. 2017
propose a debiasing procedure based on targeted minimum loss estimation (TMLE) to refine given nuisance estimators to asymptotically satisfy (
4
) and (
5
). The idea behind the TMLE-based approach is to iteratively debias the initial nuisance estimators
\mu_{n}
and
\alpha_{n}
using a reweighted generalized linear regression algorithm with offset to obtain
targeted
estimators
\mu_{n}^{*}
and
\alpha_{n}^{*}
that satisfy the empirical score equations

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{n}^{*}(A_{i},W_{i})\{Y_{i}-\mu_{n}^{*}(A_{i},W_{i})\}\,=\,o(n^{-\frac{1}{2}})\,;

(6)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{n}^{*}(A_{i},W_{i})\alpha_{n}^{*}(A_{i},W_{i})-\psi_{n}(r_{n}^{*})\,=\,o(n^{-\frac{1}{2}})\,.

(7)

Here,
s_{n}^{*}
and
r_{n}^{*}
are estimators of the (data-dependent) nuisance functions
s_{n,0}^{*}:=\Pi_{\mu_{n}^{*}}(\alpha_{0}-\alpha_{n}^{*})
and
r_{n,0}^{*}:=\Pi_{\alpha_{n}^{*}}(\mu_{0}-\mu_{n}^{*})
, which can be obtained, for example, using univariate empirical risk minimization with features
\mu_{n}^{*}(A_{1},W_{1}),\ldots,\mu_{n}^{*}(A_{n},W_{n})
and
\alpha_{n}^{*}(A_{1},W_{1}),\ldots,\alpha_{n}^{*}(A_{n},W_{n})
, respectively — we provide details in Appendix
B.1
. Under certain regularity conditions, it can be shown that (
6
) and (
7
) imply that (
4
) and (
5
) are satisfied asymptotically, so that the one-step debiased estimator based on
\mu_{n}^{*}
and
\alpha_{n}^{*}
is indeed DRAL.

As discussed in the Introduction, the TMLE-based algorithm for doubly robust inference involves estimating two univariate nuisance functions at each iteration, with the final iteration involving estimation of nuisances
r_{n,0}^{*}
and
s_{n,0}^{*}
; the process can be computationally quite prohibitive. In addition, the final estimation of
r_{n,0}^{*}
and
s_{n,0}^{*}
possibly induces additional (unnecessary) finite-sample biases on the one-step debiased estimator
\tau_{n}
by only approximately solving the empirical moment equations (
4
) and (
5
). We also note that neither
van der Laan 2014
nor
Benkeser et al. 2017
formally demonstrate that their iterative refinement procedures maintain the convergence properties of the original nuisance estimators in pursuit of satisfying the empirical orthogonality equations. Such a formal analysis is important to ensure that, under consistent estimation of both nuisance functions, their refinement procedure does not harm the quality of estimators and inferences based on the one-step debiased estimator. In the following section, we propose a novel procedure for constructing one-step debiased estimators that is free from these issues and, unlike prior works, simultaneously tackles a whole class of target parameters.

4
Calibrated debiased machine learning

4.1
Link between calibration and doubly robust inference

We now establish a fundamental link between doubly robust inference and calibration, a machine learning technique commonly used in prediction and classification tasks
(
Zadrozny and Elkan 2001
;
Gupta et al. 2020
)
. In the previous section, we argued that constructing nuisance estimators that satisfy the empirical orthogonality equations
(
4
)
and
(
5
)
is crucial for obtaining debiased machine learning estimators that are DRAL. The key insight of this paper is that these equations can be reformulated in terms of empirical calibration properties of nuisance estimators
\mu_{n}
and
\alpha_{n}
.

To formalize this claim, let
(z,\nu)\mapsto\ell(z,\nu)
be a valid loss function for some nuisance function
\nu_{0}\in\mathcal{H}
. We say that an estimator
\nu_{n}
is
\ell
–empirically calibrated
for
\nu_{0}
if it satisfies the self-consistency property

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(Z_{i},\nu_{n})=\min_{\theta}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(Z_{i},\theta\circ\nu_{n})\,,

(8)

where the minimization is over the class of all real-valued functions on
\mathbb{R}
.
In other words, an estimator
\nu_{n}
is

4.2
Isotonic-calibrated debiased machine learning

We refer to the implementation of debiased machine learning with calibrated nuisance functions as C-DML. Theorem
3
establishes that calibration of nuisance functions bestows DML estimators with the DRAL property. We now describe a particular implementation of our general framework for C-DML, which outlines how to construct DRAL estimators using isotonic calibration and one-step debiased estimation.

Our post-hoc procedure employs isotonic regression
(
Barlow and Brunk 1972
)
to empirically calibrate any given set of nuisance estimators,
\mu_{n}
and
\alpha_{n}

Our procedure is automatic, requiring no knowledge of the specific form of the linear functional

4.3
Automated bootstrap-assisted confidence intervals

In previous work, doubly robust confidence intervals were obtained through a standard Wald construction based on the asymptotic normality of the corrected estimator using an adjusted estimator of the asymptotic variance. This estimator is complex because it involves the estimation of additional, complicated nuisance functions. Here, we propose an automated approach for the construction of confidence intervals associated with the estimator odescribed in Algorithm
1
using a variant of the bootstrap
(
Efron and Tibshirani 1994
)
. Our proposed bootstrap-assisted inference approach, which we refer to as boot-IC-DML, is straightforward to describe, computationally efficient, and valid if either of the nuisance estimators is consistent at a sufficiently fast rate. Importantly, boot-IC-DML can be fully automated since, unlike existing approaches for doubly robust inference, it does not require the estimation of additional nuisance functions beyond those already used to compute the IC-DML estimator. Additionally, boot-IC-DML uses the same initial cross-fitted nuisance estimators, namely
(\alpha_{n,1},\mu_{n,1}),\ldots,(\alpha_{n,J},\mu_{n,J})
, across all bootstrap samples. As a result, the initial nuisance estimators only need to be computed once using the original data, greatly saving computational time. Boot-IC-DML only requires recomputation of the isotonic regression problems outlined in (
10
) for each bootstrap sample, which can be efficiently solved in linear time using standard software.

For simplicity, we focus the description of our automated algorithm on the case where the linear functional
\psi_{0}
is of the form

for a known map
m:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{W}\times\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
although a similar algorithm can be devised for the general case by appropriately bootstrapping the estimation of the functional
\psi_{n}
. In such cases, we can consider the empirical plug-in estimator
\psi_{n}
of
\psi_{0}
defined as

As in the previous subsection, we first describe boot-IC-DML in the simpler case where the initial estimators are not cross-fitted. 5.1
Doubly robust asymptotic linearity and inference

We now present our main theorem on the doubly robust asymptotic linearity of the IC-DML estimator. Before doing so, we provide an informal lemma that establishes that the isotonic calibrated nuisance estimators perform as well as the best monotone non-decreasing transformation of the uncalibrated, cross-fitted nuisance estimators, up to a typically negligible error of order
O_{p}(n^{-\frac{1}{3}})
. The formal results and regularity conditions are provided in Section
D.5
.

Lemma 2
.

Suppose that
\psi_{0}
has the form (
1
) with mapping
m:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{W}\times\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, and that
\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\psi_{n,j}
is taken to be the plug-in estimator
\mu\mapsto\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}m(A_{i},W_{i},\mu)
. Under regularity conditions, it holds that

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{J}\|\mu_{n,j}^{*}-\mu_{0}\|^{2}\,

\displaystyle=\,\min_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{\textnormal{iso}}}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\|f\circ\mu_{n,j}-\mu_{0}\|^{2}+O_{p}(n^{-\frac{2}{3}})\,;

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{J}\|\alpha_{n,j}^{*}-\alpha_{0}\|^{2}\,

\displaystyle=\,\min_{g\in\mathcal{F}_{\textnormal{iso}}}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\|g\circ\alpha_{n,j}-\alpha_{0}\|^{2}+O_{p}(n^{-\frac{2}{3}})\,.

Since the identity transform is itself monotone non-decreasing, the above lemma implies that the isotonic calibrated nuisance estimators typically perform no worse than the uncalibrated ones, with potentially improved performance when the uncalibrated estimators are poorly calibrated or inconsistent up to a monotone transformation. As such, in addition to providing doubly robust asymptotic linearity, the use of isotonic calibration weakens nuisance estimator consistency requirements by only requiring that the initial cross-fitted nuisance estimators be consistent up to a monotone transformation.

We now present our main result. The limiting distribution of the scaled and centered version of the cross-fitted IC-DML estimator involves the additional nuisance functions
s_{0}
and

\displaystyle s_{0}(a,w)

However, these estimators are usually not model-agnostic, relying, for example, on strong Hölder smoothness assumptions on the nuisance functions, and require the selection of tuning parameters for which there is limited practical guidance.

As detailed in the next section, the regularity and efficiency of the IC-DML estimator under the consistency of both nuisance estimators are important, as they address a potential criticism of DRAL estimators — namely, that they are irregular
(
Dukes et al. 2021
)
. In particular, provided one nuisance function is estimated consistently at a sufficiently fast rate, Theorem
4
finds that irregularity only ever occurs when the other nuisance function is inconsistently estimated. In other words, if the other nuisance function is consistently estimated but at a slow rate, the IC-DML estimator remains regular and asymptotically linear, in contrast to standard doubly robust estimators, which are then neither regular nor asymptotically linear.
Since many machine learning methods — including random forests, neural networks, and gradient-boosted trees — are known to be universal approximators
(
Hornik et al. 1989
;
Biau et al. 2008
)
, while slow nuisance estimation may well occur, inconsistent nuisance estimation is less likely to arise. Even in the special case of the ATE parameter, Theorem
4
assumes substantially weaker conditions than prior works on doubly robust inference
(
van der Laan 2014
;
Benkeser et al. 2017
;
Dukes et al. 2021
)
. Specifically, these previous works imposed that the nuisance estimators converge to their (possibly incorrect) limits quickly, requiring that
\|\mu_{n,j}^{*}-\overline{\mu}_{0}\|\|\alpha_{n,j}^{*}-\overline{\alpha}_{0}\|=o_{P}(n^{-\frac{1}{2}})
, which offers no improvement over rate double robustness when both nuisance estimators are consistent.

It can be shown that Theorem
4
remains true even when only the consistent nuisance estimators are calibrated. In particular, if
\overline{\mu}_{0}=\mu_{0}
and we take
\alpha_{n,j}^{*}=\overline{\alpha}_{0}:=0
for each

\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\psi_{n,j}(\mu_{n,j}^{*})
is asymptotically linear under similar conditions. In the case of the ATE, this debiased plug-in estimator is asymptotically equivalent to the superefficient TML estimator that leverages the outcome regression for confounding bias adjustment, as proposed in
Benkeser et al. 2020
. Similarly, if
\overline{\alpha}_{0}=\alpha_{0}
and we take
\mu_{n,j}^{*}=\overline{\mu}_{0}:=0

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{n,j(i)}^{*}(A_{i},W_{i})Y_{i}
is asymptotically linear, leveraging the Riesz representer for confounding bias adjustment. As a result, isotonic calibration offers a simple and automated debiasing approach for constructing asymptotically linear (although irregular) inverse-probability-of-treatment weighting and G-computation plug-in estimators, eliminating the requirement to derive the efficient influence function of the target parameter.

We now discuss the conditions of the above theorem. Condition
C2
ensures that the (possibly inconsistent) nuisance estimators have in-probability limits in
\mathcal{H}
and is commonly required for doubly robust inference
(
Benkeser et al. 2017
;
Smucler et al. 2019
;
Dukes et al. 2021

C3
requires bounded nuisance functions and estimators. The boundedness of the Riesz representer
\alpha_{0}

is a general form of the strong positivity assumption
(
Chernozhukov et al. 2022

P_{0}
–support of the outcome

\mu_{0}
and its estimator. Condition
C4
imposes a weak upper bound on the number of plateaus in the (piecewise constant) isotonic regression solution; this is expected to hold in any setting with noisy outcomes where isotonic regression cannot perfectly fit the data. In view of
Deng et al. 2021
, continuous differentiability of

implies that the number of observations in a given plateau of an isotonic regression solution grows at a rate

with probability tending to one; heuristically, this suggests that the number of plateaus grows at rate
n^{\frac{1}{3}}=o(n^{\frac{1}{2}})

xgboost

5.3
On estimator irregularity under inconsistent nuisance estimation

As emphasized before, Theorem
4
establishes that the isotonic calibrated one-step debiased estimator is DRAL, and that it is regular and efficient when both nuisance functions are consistently estimated, even if one nuisance function is estimated insufficiently fast. However, under inconsistent estimation of one of the nuisance functions, the resulting estimator is typically irregular under a nonparametric model, as it is asymptotically linear with an influence function differing from the nonparametric efficient influence function of the target parameter
(
van der Vaart 2000
;
Dukes et al. 2021
)
. This irregularity implies that inference for
\tau_{0}
is not uniformly valid when considering sampling from local perturbations of the data-generating distribution. Irregularity is typically viewed as undesirable due to its potential to cause poor performance in finite samples, since it is possible to find a distribution such that an arbitrarily large number of samples may be required for the asymptotic limiting distribution to accurately approximate finite-sample behavior
(
Leeb and Pötscher 2005
)
. This criticism is arguably less relevant in our case since standard one-step debiased estimators are, under inconsistent estimation of one nuisance function, typically neither regular nor
n^{\frac{1}{2}}
–consistent, let alone asymptotically linear
(
Dukes et al. 2021
)
. We stress here that the set of laws under which the IC-DML estimator is regular and asymptotically efficient is a strict superset of the corresponding set of laws for standard one-step debiased estimators, and furthermore, that the IC-DML estimator is asymptotically linear and permits valid pointwise (as opposed to uniform) inference at
n^{\frac{1}{2}}
–rate at laws at which the standard estimators neither converge at
n^{\frac{1}{2}}
–rate nor readily allow even pointwise inference.

The following theorem characterizes the local asymptotic behavior of the IC-DML estimator in the irregular case where one of the nuisance functions is inconsistently estimated. It establishes that the estimator is a locally regular and efficient estimator of a projection-based oracle parameter
\Psi_{0}
, which agrees with the original target parameter
P\mapsto\Psi(P):=\psi_{P}(\mu_{P})
at
P_{0}
. As a result, the IC-DML procedure offers regular and locally uniformly valid inference for the corresponding projection estimand, even when sampling from least favorable local perturbations of
P_{0}
.

To define this oracle parameter, we first introduce additional notation. We define the affine spaces
\overline{\Theta}_{0,P}:=\{\overline{\mu}_{0}+f\circ\alpha_{0}:f\}\cap L^{2}(P)
and
\overline{\Theta}_{0,P}^{*}:=\{\overline{\alpha}_{0}+g\circ\mu_{0}:g\}\cap L^{2}(P)
, where the indexing functions
f
and
g
vary over all one-dimensional, real-valued transformations. We denote the orthogonal projection of
\mu_{P}
onto
\overline{\Theta}_{0,P}
by
\overline{\Pi}_{0,P}\mu_{P}:=\argmin_{\mu\in\overline{\Theta}_{0,P}}\|\mu_{P}-\mu\|_{P}
, and the orthogonal projection of
\alpha_{P}
onto
\overline{\Theta}_{0,P}^{*}
by
\overline{\Pi}_{0,P}^{*}\alpha_{P}:=\argmin_{\alpha\in\overline{\Theta}_{0,P}^{*}}\|\alpha_{P}-\alpha\|_{P}
. We may then define the projection-based oracle parameter
\Psi_{0}
, which varies depending on which nuisance function is estimated inconsistently, as

\displaystyle P\mapsto\Psi_{0}(P)

\displaystyle:=1(\overline{\mu}_{0}=\mu_{0})\overline{\psi}_{0,P}(\mu_{P})+1(\overline{\alpha}_{0}=\alpha_{0})\psi_{P}(\overline{\Pi}_{0,P}\mu_{P})\ ,

where
\overline{\psi}_{0,P}:h\mapsto\langle\overline{\Pi}_{0,P}^{*}\alpha_{P},h\rangle_{P}
is an oracle approximation of the linear functional
\psi_{P}:h\mapsto\langle\alpha_{P},h\rangle_{P}
. The orthogonality conditions characterizing the projections
\overline{\Pi}_{0}^{*}\alpha_{0}
and
\overline{\Pi}_{0,P}\mu_{0}
imply that
\Psi_{0}(P_{0})=\Psi(P_{0})
, so that the oracle parameter agrees with the target parameter at
P_{0}
. In the case where
\mu_{0}
is consistently estimated,
\Psi_{0}
reduces to the parameter
P\mapsto\overline{\psi}_{0,P}(\mu_{P})
, which leverages the outcome regression
\mu_{0}(A,W)
as a dimension reduction of
(A,W)
. Alternatively, in the case where
\alpha_{0}
is consistently estimated,
\Psi_{0}
reduces to the parameter
P\mapsto\psi_{P}(\overline{\Pi}_{0,P}\mu_{P})
, which leverages the Riesz representer
\alpha_{0}(A,W)
as a dimension reduction of
(A,W)
.

Theorem 6

(Behavior under local perturbations)
.

Suppose that the conditions of Theorem
4
hold and that either
\overline{\mu}_{0}=\mu_{0}
or
\overline{\alpha}_{0}=\alpha_{0}
but not both. Then,
\tau_{n}^{*}
is a locally regular and asymptotically linear at
P_{0}
for the oracle projection parameter
\Psi_{0}
with influence function
\chi_{0}
. Consequently, under sampling from any local perturbation
P_{0,hn^{-\frac{1}{2}}}
of
P_{0}
, it holds that
n^{\frac{1}{2}}\{\tau_{n}^{*}-\Psi_{0}(P_{0,hn^{-\frac{1}{2}}})\}
tends to a mean-zero normal random variable with variance
\sigma_{0}^{2}
as defined in Theorem
4
.

Theorem
6
demonstrates that even under sampling from least favorable local perturbations, the IC-DML estimator still provides regular and locally uniformly valid inference for the projection-based oracle parameter
\Psi_{0}
. Notably, the magnitude of the oracle bias
\Psi_{0}(P)-\Psi(P)
for
\Psi
is driven by the degree of nuisance estimator inconsistency via projection distances
\|\overline{\Pi}_{\mu_{0}}\mu_{P}-\mu_{P}\|_{P}
and
\|\overline{\Pi}_{\alpha_{0}}\alpha_{P}-\alpha_{P}\|_{P}
. Consequently, under sampling from a local perturbation, when the nuisance functions are only mildly inconsistently estimated, the local asymptotic bias of the IC-DML estimator for the target parameter
\Psi
may still be relatively small. A similar result was derived in
van der Laan et al. 2023a
for superefficient and irregular, adaptive debiased machine learning estimators obtained using data-driven model selection techniques. As a special case, Theorem
6
implies that
\tau_{n}^{*}
is regular and asymptotically linear for the actual target parameter
\Psi
over all local perturbations that do not perturb the consistently estimated nuisance function, which agrees with Theorem 2 of
Dukes et al. 2021
.

Numerical experiments

6.1
Experiment 1: performance under inconsistent nuisance estimation

We now evaluate the numerical performance of the IC-DML estimator through simulation studies. First, we conduct an experiment based on synthetic data to empirically verify the theoretical properties of the IC-DML estimator introduced in Algorithm
1
for inference on the ATE
\tau_{0}=E_{0}\{\mu_{0}(1,W)-\mu_{0}(0,W)\}
. The estimator we consider is defined as
\tau_{n}^{*}:=\tau_{n}^{*}(1)-\tau_{n}^{*}(0)
, where
\tau_{n}^{*}(a)
is an IC-DML estimator of the counterfactual mean
E_{0}\{\mu_{0}(a,W)\}
obtained, as in Example
1
, using Algorithm
1
. As a benchmark, we compare the performance of the proposed estimator with the standard cross-fitted AIPW estimator, outlined in Section
2.1
, as well as the DR-TMLE proposed in
Benkeser et al. 2017
, described in Section
3.3
and implemented in the
R
package
drtmle

(
Benkeser and Hejazi 2023
)
. Notably, the IC-DML estimator differs from the AIPW estimator solely through the additional step of isotonic calibration of the cross-fitted propensity score and outcome regression estimators. To quantify uncertainty, we utilize the bootstrap-assisted confidence intervals outlined in Algorithm
2
for the IC-DML estimator, and Wald-type confidence intervals derived from the influence function-based variance estimator for both the AIPW estimator and DR-TMLE.

We consider the same data-generating process used in the experiments of
Benkeser et al. 2017
. The covariate vector
W=(W_{1},W_{2})
is first drawn as independent
W_{1}\sim\text{Uniform}(-2,2)
and
W_{2}\sim\text{Bernoulli}(0.5)
. Then, given
(W_{1},W_{2})=(w_{1},w_{2})
, the treatment assignment variable
A
is drawn from a Bernoulli distribution with success probability
\pi_{0}(w_{1},w_{2}):=\text{expit}(-w_{1}+2w_{1}w_{2})
. Given
A=a
and
(W_{1},W_{2})=(w_{1},w_{2})
, the outcome variable
Y
is drawn from a Bernoulli distribution with success probability
\mu(a,w_{1},w_{2}):=\text{expit}(0.2a-w_{1}+2w_{1}w_{2})
. To evaluate the doubly robust inference property, we examine three scenarios of inconsistent nuisance estimation for both the IC-DML, AIPW and DR-TMLE estimators:

(a)

when both the outcome regression and treatment mechanism are estimated consistently;

(b)

when only the treatment mechanism is estimated consistently;

(c)

when only the outcome regression is estimated consistently.

In settings (b) and (c), to induce inconsistent estimation of the outcome regression and treatment mechanisms, we estimate the propensity score
\pi_{0}
and outcome regressions
\mu_{0}
using (misspecified) main-term logistic regression models. In all settings, any consistently estimated nuisance function is estimated using a Nadaraya–Watson kernel smoother with bandwidth selected via cross-validation and with stratification by both
A
and
W_{2}
; the implementation available in
R
package
fksum
was used
(
Hofmeyr 2020
)
. For the DR-TMLE estimator, we used this same kernel estimator to estimate the nuisance functions
r_{0}
and
s_{0}
. For both the IC-DML, DR-TMLE, and AIPW estimators, we employ 5-fold cross-fitting when estimating the initial nuisance functions. To avoid positivity issues, the cross-fitted propensity score estimators used to construct the AIPW estimator were truncated to lie in
[c_{n},1-c_{n}]
, where
c_{n}:=25/(n^{\frac{1}{2}}\log n)
, as suggested in
Gruber et al. 2022
. Details for reproducing these analyses can be found in the associated GitHub repository, accessible through a link provided in the Appendix.

For each scenario and sample sizes ranging from 250 to 10,000, a total of 5,000 datasets were randomly generated and analyzed using all estimation procedures considered. Empirical estimates of the standard error of the IC-DML estimator against the AIPW estimator as well as the empirical coverage of nominal 95% confidence interval are displayed in Figure
1
. Both the IC-DML and DR-TMLE estimators appeared to exhibit DRAL, showing less bias and better coverage than the AIPW estimator in settings with inconsistent estimation. Despite being easier and faster to implement, across all inconsistent settings, the IC-DML estimator showed less bias and resulted in confidence intervals with better coverage than DR-TMLE, while also having smaller or equal variance. In the setting where both nuisance functions were consistently estimated, all estimators performed similarly.

7
Conclusion

In this work, we introduced a unified machine learning framework for doubly robust inference on linear functionals of the outcome regression. These functionals belong to a subset of parameters characterized by the mixed bias property
(
Rotnitzky et al. 2021
)
. For the case of linear functionals, our framework provides an affirmative answer to the open question raised by
Smucler et al. 2019
, regarding the feasibility of achieving doubly robust inference for mixed-bias parameters using black-box nuisance function estimators. Another significant contribution of this work lies in establishing a close relationship between calibration and the doubly robust inference properties of debiased machine learning estimators. Importantly, our proof techniques rely solely on the nuisance estimators satisfying the calibration score equations stated in Section
5.1
. While our IC-DML framework uses isotonic calibration, these score equations can also be satisfied when nuisance functions are calibrated using other histogram-type estimators, such as (honest) regression trees
(
Wager and Athey 2018
)
, the highly adaptive lasso
(
van der Laan 2017
;
Benkeser and van der Laan 2016
;
Fang et al. 2021
)
, and Venn-Abers calibration
(
Vovk and Petej 2012
;
van der Laan and Alaa 2024
)
. In general, both our theoretical and empirical results support the use of calibrated nuisance estimators in causal inference to mitigate bias arising from inconsistent nuisance estimation and to ensure calibrated inferences.

There remain several open questions along this line of research. First, whether our framework can be extended to handle general parameters characterized by the mixed bias property
(
Rotnitzky et al. 2021
)
remains to be established. Preliminary research we have done on this question suggests that this may indeed be possible, although further work is required. Second, it may be of interest to develop an isotonic calibrated targeted minimum loss estimator that inherits both the plug-in property and doubly robust asymptotic linearity, as considered in
van der Laan 2014
and
Benkeser et al. 2017
. Such an estimator could outperform non-plug-in alternatives such as one-step debiased estimators
(
Porter et al. 2011
)
. A potential approach for this could be to iteratively apply the isotonic calibration and plug-in debiasing steps until the relevant score equations for both DRAL and the plug-in property are solved. Lastly, extending our framework to accommodate multiple and sequentially robust estimators in longitudinal data structures presents further exciting research opportunities
(
Rotnitzky et al. 2017
;
Luedtke et al. 2017
)
.

Additional details on IC-DML estimator

B.1
Estimation of nuisance functions in limiting distribution

Using the bootstrap-assisted inference method of Algorithm
2
, it is not necessary to obtain estimates of the univariate nuisance functions
f_{0,\overline{\mu}_{0}}
and
g_{0,\overline{\alpha}_{0}}
, which appear in the limited distribution of Theorem
4
. Nonetheless, if desired, we note that these nuisance functions can be estimated using univariate empirical risk minimization. To see this, observe that
f_{0,\overline{\mu}_{0}}
is the Riesz-representer of the linear functional
\alpha\mapsto\langle\alpha_{0}-\overline{\alpha}_{0},\alpha\rangle_{P_{0}}=\psi_{0}(\alpha)-\langle\overline{\alpha}_{0},\alpha\rangle_{P_{0}}
with respect to the linear space
\left\{f\circ\overline{\mu}_{0}:f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\|f\circ\mu\|<\infty\right\}
. Hence, as a consequence of the Riesz-defined above Theorem
1
, the function
f_{0,\overline{\mu}_{0}}
can be obtained via univariate empirical risk minimization, after substituting
\overline{\mu}_{0}
and
\overline{\alpha}_{0}
with their respective estimators. Specifically, the function
f_{0,\overline{\mu}_{0}}
can be directly estimated via offset univariate empirical risk minimization over
\{f\circ\mu_{n,\diamond}^{*}\in L^{2}(\overline{P}_{0,A,W})\}
based on an empirical estimator of the Riesz-loss risk:

f\mapsto\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\left\{\alpha_{n,j(i)}^{*}-(f\circ\mu_{n,j(i)}^{*})\right\}^{2}(A_{i},W_{i})-2\left\{(f\circ\mu_{n,j(i)}^{*}\cdot\alpha_{n,j(i)}^{*})(A_{i},W_{i})-\psi_{n,j(i)}\left(f\circ\mu_{n,j(i)}^{*}\right)\right\}\right].

Similarly, noting that
\overline{\mu}_{0,\alpha}(a,w)=E_{0}\left[Y-\overline{\mu}_{0}(A,W)|\alpha(A,W)=\alpha(a,w)\right]
, the function
g_{0,\overline{\alpha}_{0}}
can be estimated by regressing the residual
\{Y_{i}-\mu_{n,j(i)}^{*}(A_{i},W_{i})\}_{i\in[n]}
on
\{\alpha_{n,j(i)}^{*}(A_{i},W_{i})\}_{i\in[n]}
using, for instance, locally adaptive regression splines
(
Mammen and Van De Geer 1997
)
or random forests
(
Breiman 2001
)
.

B.2
Extension to parameters with the mixed bias property

In this section, we present a brief overview of how our doubly robust inference framework can be extended to a general class of parameters with the mixed bias property, as introduced in
Rotnitzky et al. 2021
. While our primary focus in this paper remains on the class of linear functionals discussed in Section
2.1
, it is important to highlight that our results can be easily generalized to parameters exhibiting the mixed bias property.

To this end, let
Z\sim P_{0}
be an arbitrary data-structure drawn from a distribution
P_{0}
belonging to a nonparametric statistical model
\mathcal{M}
. We consider doubly robust inference for a feature
\tau_{0}:=\Psi(P_{0})
for a parameter
\Psi:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. We assume that
\Psi
is pathwise differentiable with efficient influence function
\chi_{0}\in L^{2}_{0}(P_{0})
at
P_{0}
. We further assume that
\Psi
has the mixed bias property at
P_{0}
meaning that, for all
\overline{P}\in\mathcal{M}
, the following condition holds:

\Psi(\overline{P})-\Psi(P_{0})+E_{0}[\chi_{\overline{P}}(Z)]=E_{0}[S(Z)\{\overline{\nu}(Z)-\nu_{0}(Z)\}\{\overline{\eta}(Z)-\eta_{0}(Z)\}],

(13)

where
z\mapsto S(z)
is a fixed function,
\overline{\nu}:=\nu_{\overline{P}}
,
\nu_{0}:=\nu_{P_{0}}
,
\overline{\eta}:=\eta_{\overline{P}}
, and
\eta_{0}:=\eta_{P_{0}}
are nuisance functions. Under the regularity conditions of Theorem 2 in
Rotnitzky et al. 2021
, it can be shown that

\displaystyle(\nu_{0},\eta_{0})=\argmin_{\nu,\eta\in L^{2}(P_{0})}E_{0}\left[S(Z)\left\{\nu^{2}(Z)+\eta^{2}(Z)\right\}-2\{m_{1}(Z,\nu)+m_{2}(Z,\eta)\}\right],

for fixed functions
(z,\nu)\mapsto m_{1}(z,\nu)
and
(z,\eta)\mapsto m_{2}(z,\eta)
. In the special case, where
S=1
, the optimization problem can be decoupled as follows:

\nu_{0}=\argmin_{\nu\in L^{2}(P_{0})}E_{0}\left[\nu^{2}(Z)-2m_{1}(Z,\nu)\right];\;\eta_{0}=\argmin_{\eta\in L^{2}(P_{0})}E_{0}\left[\eta^{2}(Z)-2m_{2}(Z,\eta)\right].

We note that the setup of Section
2.1
is recovered by taking
Z=(W,A,Y)
,
P\mapsto\Psi(P)=\psi_{P}(\mu_{P}):=E_{P}\{m(A,W,\mu_{P})\}
,
\mu\mapsto\psi_{n}(\mu):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{m}m(W_{i},A_{i},\mu)
,
(\nu,\eta,z)\mapsto S:=1
,
P\mapsto\nu(P):=\mu_{P}
,
P\mapsto\eta(P):=\alpha_{P}
,
(z,\nu)\mapsto m_{1}(z,\nu):=y\nu(a,w)
, and
(z,\eta)\mapsto m_{2}(z,\eta):=m(a,w,\eta)
.

We now introduce the generalization of the calibrated one-step debiased estimator proposed in Section
4
. Given initial estimators
\nu_{n}
and
\eta_{n}
of
\nu_{0}
and
\eta_{0}
, we define the calibrated estimators
\nu_{n}^{*}=f_{n}\circ\nu_{n}
and
\eta_{n}^{*}=g_{n}\circ\eta_{n}
where
(f_{n},g_{n})
are obtained as:

\displaystyle(f_{n},g_{n})

\displaystyle\in\argmin_{f,g\in\mathcal{F}_{iso}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[S(Z_{i})\left\{(f\circ\nu_{n})^{2}(Z_{i})+(g\circ\eta_{n})^{2}(Z_{i})\right\}-2\{m_{1}(Z_{i},f\circ\nu_{n})+m_{2}(Z_{i},g\circ\eta_{n})\}\right].

(14)

Notably, assuming suitable smoothness in the loss function, the first order conditions of the minimizing solutions guarantees that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[S(Z_{i})\left\{(f\circ\nu_{n})(Z_{i})\nu_{n}^{*}(Z_{i})-\frac{m_{1}(Z_{i},f\circ\nu_{n})}{S(Z_{i})}+(g\circ\eta_{n})(Z_{i})\eta_{n}^{*}(Z_{i})-\frac{m_{2}(Z_{i},g\circ\eta_{n})}{S(Z_{i})}\right\}\right]=0,

for all transformations
f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
g:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.

Given the calibrated nuisance estimators
\nu_{n}^{*}
and
\eta_{n}^{*}
, a calibrated one-step debiased estimator of
\Psi(P_{0})
can be constructed as in
Rotnitzky et al. 2021
(see also the companion paper
Smucler et al. 2019
). As in Algorithms
1
and
2
, the initial nuisance estimators can be cross-fitted and confidence intervals can be constructed using the bootstrap. While our focus in this work is on linear functionals, our proof techniques can be readily extended to establish rate and inference double robustness for the proposed calibrated one-step debiased estimator of
\Psi(P_{0})
, subject to conditions.

R
package
CDML

Python
code implementing CDML can be found on GitHub at the following link:

.

The following subsections provide
R
and
Python
code for calibrating inverse propensity weights and outcome regressions. These calibrated models can be directly used within DML frameworks to construct IC-DML estimators of the average treatment effect. Here, we implement isotonic regression using

C.1
R Code

# Function: isoreg_with_xgboost
# Purpose: Fits isotonic regression using XGBoost.
# Inputs:
# - x: A vector or matrix of predictor variables.
# - y: A vector of response variables.
# - max_depth: Maximum depth of the trees in XGBoost (default = 15).
# - min_child_weight: Minimum sum of instance weights (Hessian)
# needed in a child node (default = 20).
# Returns:
# - A function that takes a new predictor variable x
# and returns the model’s predicted values.
isoreg_with_xgboost <- function(x, y, max_depth = 15, min_child_weight = 20) {
# Create an XGBoost DMatrix object from the data
data <- xgboost::xgb.DMatrix(data = as.matrix(x), label = as.vector(y))

# Set parameters for the monotonic XGBoost model
params <- list(max_depth = max_depth,
min_child_weight = min_child_weight,
monotone_constraints = 1, # Enforce monotonic increase
eta = 1, gamma = 0,
lambda = 0)

# Train the model with one boosting round
iso_fit <- xgboost::xgb.train(params = params,
data = data,
nrounds = 1)

# Prediction function for new data
fun <- function(x) {
data_pred <- xgboost::xgb.DMatrix(data = as.matrix(x))
pred <- predict(iso_fit, data_pred)
return(pred)
}
return(fun)
}

# Function: calibrate_inverse_weights
# Purpose: Calibrates inverse weights using isotonic regression
# with XGBoost for two propensity scores.
# Inputs:
# - A: Binary indicator variable.
# - pi1: Estimated propensity score for treatment group A = 1.
# - pi0: Estimated propensity score for control group A = 0.
# Returns:
# - A list containing calibrated inverse weights for each group:

# - alpha0_star: Calibrated inverse weights for A = 0.

import xgboost as xgb
import numpy as np

def isoreg_with_xgboost(x, y, max_depth=15, min_child_weight=20):
"""
Fits isotonic regression using XGBoost with monotonic constraints to ensure
non-decreasing predictions as the predictor variable increases.

Args:
x (np.array): A vector or matrix of predictor variables.
y (np.array): A vector of response variables.
max_depth (int, optional): Maximum depth of the trees in XGBoost.
Default is 15.
min_child_weight (float, optional): Minimum sum of instance weights
needed in a child node. Default is 20.

Returns:
function: A prediction function that takes a new predictor variable x
and returns the model’s predicted values.

Example:
>>> x = np.array([[1], [2], [3]])
>>> y = np.array([1, 2, 3])
>>> model = isoreg_with_xgboost(x, y)
>>> model(np.array([[1.5], [2.5]]))
"""

# Create an XGBoost DMatrix object from the data
data = xgb.DMatrix(data=np.asarray(x), label=np.asarray(y))

# Set parameters for the monotonic XGBoost model
params = {
’max_depth’: max_depth,
’min_child_weight’: min_child_weight,
’monotone_constraints’: "(1)", # Enforce monotonic increase
’eta’: 1,
’gamma’: 0,
’lambda’: 0
}

# Train the model with one boosting round
iso_fit = xgb.train(params=params, dtrain=data, num_boost_round=1)

# Prediction function for new data
def predict_fn(x):
"""
Predicts output for new input data using the trained isotonic regression model.

Args:
x (np.array): New predictor variables as a vector or matrix.

Returns:
np.array: Predicted values.
"""
data_pred = xgb.DMatrix(data=np.asarray(x))
pred = iso_fit.predict(data_pred)
return pred

return predict_fn

. By Lipschitz continuity, we have that

\displaystyle\left|g(\mu_{n}(x),\mu_{0}(x))-E[g(\mu_{n}(X),\mu_{0}(X))|\mu_{n}(X)=\mu_{n}(x)]\right|

\displaystyle=\left|E[g(\mu_{n}(x),\mu_{0}(x))-g(\mu_{n}(x),\mu_{0}(X))|\mu_{n}(X)=\mu_{n}(x)]\right|

\displaystyle\leq E[\left|g(\mu_{n}(x),\mu_{0}(x))-g(\mu_{n}(x),\mu_{0}(X))\right||\mu_{n}(X)=\mu_{n}(x)]

\displaystyle\leq LE[\left|\mu_{0}(x)-\mu_{0}(X)\right||\mu_{n}(X)=\mu_{n}(x)].

On the event
\{\mu_{n}(X)=\mu_{n}(x)\}
, we know

\displaystyle|\mu_{0}(x)-\mu_{0}(X)|

\displaystyle\leq|\mu_{0}(x)-\mu_{n}(x)|+|\mu_{n}(x)-\mu_{n}(X)|+|\mu_{0}(X)-\mu_{n}(X)|

\displaystyle\leq|\mu_{0}(x)-\mu_{n}(x)|+|\mu_{0}(X)-\mu_{n}(X)|.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\left|g(\mu_{n}(x),\mu_{0}(x))-E[g(\mu_{n}(X),\mu_{0}(X))|\mu_{n}(X)=\mu_{n}(x)]\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim E[\left|\mu_{0}(x)-\mu_{0}(X)\right|\mid\mu_{n}(X)=\mu_{n}(x)]

\displaystyle\lesssim E|\mu_{0}(x)-\mu_{n}(x)|+E[|\mu_{0}(X)-\mu_{n}(X)|\mid\mu_{n}(X)=\mu_{n}(x)].

Now, for some function
f\in L^{2}(P_{X})
, suppose that
(\mu_{n}(x),\mu_{0}(x))\mapsto(\Pi_{\mu_{n},\mu_{0}}f)(x)
is Lipschitz continuous. Then, defining
g:(\widehat{m},m)\mapsto E_{0}[f(X)|\mu_{n}(X)=\widehat{m},\mu_{0}(X)=m,\mathcal{D}_{n}]
and noting by the law of iterated expectation that
\Pi_{\mu_{n}}\Pi_{\mu_{n},\mu_{0}}f=\Pi_{\mu_{n}}f
,
we obtain the following pointwise error bound:

\left|\Pi_{\mu_{n},\mu_{0}}f-\Pi_{\mu_{n}}f\right|\lesssim\left|\mu_{n}-\mu_{0}\right|+\Pi_{\mu_{n}}(|\mu_{n}-\mu_{0}|).

Since
\|\Pi_{\mu_{n}}(|\mu_{n}-\mu_{0}|)\|_{L^{2}(P_{0})}\leq\|\mu_{n}-\mu_{0}\|_{L^{2}(P_{0})}
by the properties of projections, it follows that

\|\Pi_{\mu_{n},\mu_{0}}f-\Pi_{\mu_{n}}f\|_{L^{2}(P)}\lesssim\|\mu_{n}-\mu_{0}\|_{L^{2}(P)}.

Taking
f
to be
\Delta_{\alpha_{n}}
, we conclude that Condition
B1i
implies Condition
enumii
. By an identical argument, Condition
B1ii
implies Condition
enumiii
. The result then follows.

∎

Lemma 5
.

For

\mathcal{J}(\delta,\mathcal{R}_{n,j})+\mathcal{J}(\delta,\mathcal{S}_{n,j})\lesssim\sqrt{\delta}\left(1+\sqrt{k(n)\delta\log(1/\delta)}\right)

where
Q\circ\mu_{n,j}^{-1}

\mu_{n,j}(W)
conditional on

consists of univariate functions with at most

\mathcal{J}(\delta,\mathcal{F}_{n})\lesssim\delta\sqrt{k(n)\log(1/\delta)}

Lemma 6
.

For each
j\in[J]
, let
\mathcal{H}_{n,j}=\mathcal{F}_{n,j}\times\mathcal{F}_{n,j}\times\mathcal{F}_{n,j}\times\mathcal{F}_{n,j}
for a function class
\mathcal{F}_{n,j}
that is deterministic conditional on the
j
-th training set
\mathcal{D}_{n}\backslash\mathcal{C}^{(j)}
. Assume that
\mathcal{J}(\delta,\mathcal{F}_{n,j})\leq t_{n}(\delta)
for some map
\delta\mapsto t_{n}(\delta)
. For each
j\in[J]
, let
T_{n,j}:\mathbb{R}^{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a bounded Lipchitz function that is deterministic conditional on
\mathcal{D}_{n}\backslash\mathcal{C}^{(j)}
. Then, for all
\delta>0
,

E_{0}^{n}\left[\sup_{h_{\diamond}\in\mathcal{H}_{n,\diamond}:\|h_{\diamond}\|_{\overline{P}_{0}}\leq\delta}\left(\overline{P}_{n}-\overline{P}_{0}\right)T_{n,\diamond}(h_{\diamond})\right]\lesssim n^{-\frac{1}{2}}t_{n}(\delta)\left(1+\frac{t_{n}(\delta)}{\sqrt{n}\delta^{2}}\right).

Furthermore, for any
\sqrt{n}\delta^{2}>t_{n}(\delta)
,

E_{0}^{n}\left[\sup_{h_{\diamond}\in\mathcal{H}_{n,\diamond}:\|h_{\diamond}\|_{\overline{P}_{0}}\leq\delta}\left(\overline{P}_{n}^{\#}-\overline{P}_{n}\right)T_{n,\diamond}(h_{\diamond})\right]\lesssim n^{-\frac{1}{2}}t_{n}(\delta)\left(1+\frac{t_{n}(\delta)}{\sqrt{n}\delta^{2}}\right).

Proof.

To establish the first bound, by the triangle inequality, it suffices to show, for each
j\in[J]
, that

E_{0}^{n}\left[\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{n,j}:\|h\|\leq\delta}\left(P_{n,j}-P_{0}\right)T_{n,j}(h)\right]\lesssim n^{-\frac{1}{2}}t_{n}(\delta)\left(1+\frac{t_{n}(\delta)}{\sqrt{n}\delta^{2}}\right).

By Theorem 2.10.20 of
van der Vaart and Wellner 1996
on preservation of entropy integrals under Lipschitz transformation and Lemma
5
, we have that
\mathcal{H}_{n,j}
is a uniformly bounded function class with uniform entropy integral satisfying
\mathcal{J}(\delta,\mathcal{H}_{n,j})\lesssim 4\mathcal{J}(\delta,\mathcal{F}_{n,j})\lesssim t_{n}(\delta)
. Hence, by Theorem 2.1 of
van der Vaart and Wellner 2011
,

E_{0}^{n}\left[\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{n,j}:\|h\|\leq\delta}\left(P_{n,j}-P_{0}\right)T_{n,j}(h)\right]\lesssim n^{-\frac{1}{2}}t_{n}(\delta)\left(1+\frac{t_{n}(\delta)}{\sqrt{n}\delta^{2}}\right).

Next, noting that
P_{n,j}^{\#}
is the empirical distribution of
O(n/J)
i.i.d. samples from
P_{n,j}
, we can apply the same argument conditional on
P_{n,j}
with
\delta_{n}:=\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{n,j}:\|h\|_{P_{0}}\leq\delta}\|h\|_{P_{n,j}}
to find:

\displaystyle E_{0}^{n}\left[\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{n,j}:\|h\|_{P_{0}}\leq\delta}\left(P_{n,j}^{\#}-P_{n,j}\right)T_{n,j}(h)|P_{n,j}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\{\mathcal{J}(\delta_{n},\mathcal{S}_{n,j})+\mathcal{J}(\delta_{n},\mathcal{R}_{n,j})\}\left(1+\frac{\mathcal{J}(\delta_{n},\mathcal{S}_{n,j})+\mathcal{J}(\delta_{n},\mathcal{R}_{n,j})}{\sqrt{n}\delta_{n}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim n^{-\frac{1}{2}}t_{n}(\delta_{n})\left(1+\frac{t_{n}(\delta_{n})}{\sqrt{n}\delta_{n}^{2}}\right)

We can show that
\varepsilon\mapsto t_{n}(\sqrt{\varepsilon})\left(1+\frac{t_{n}(\sqrt{\varepsilon})}{\sqrt{n}\varepsilon}\right)
is a concave function. Thus, taking the expectation over both sides of the previous display and applying Jenson’s inequality, we find for
\widetilde{\delta}_{n}:=\sqrt{E\delta_{n}^{2}}
that

E_{0}^{n}\left[\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{n,j}:\|h\|_{P_{0}}\leq\delta}\left(P_{n,j}^{\#}-P_{n,j}\right)T_{n,j}(h)\right]\lesssim n^{-\frac{1}{2}}t_{n}(\widetilde{\delta}_{n})\left(1+\frac{t_{n}(\widetilde{\delta}_{n})}{\sqrt{n}\widetilde{\delta}_{n}^{2}}\right)

Moreover, by Theorem 2.2 of
van de Geer 2014
, we have
\widetilde{\delta}_{n}^{2}=E\delta_{n}^{2}\lesssim\delta^{2}
for any
\delta>0
with
\sqrt{n}\delta^{2}>t_{n}(\delta)
. Combining this with the previous display, we obtain

E_{0}^{n}\left[\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{n,j}:\|h\|_{P_{0}}\leq\delta}\left(P_{n,j}^{\#}-P_{n,j}\right)T_{n,j}(h)\right]\lesssim n^{-\frac{1}{2}}t_{n}(\delta)\left(1+\frac{t_{n}(\delta)}{\sqrt{n}\delta^{2}}\right),

for any
\delta^{2}>n^{-\frac{1}{2}}t_{n}(\delta)
.
∎

Lemma 7
.

For each
j\in[J]
, let
T_{n,j}:\mathbb{R}^{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a Lipschitz continuous function that is deterministic conditional on
\mathcal{D}_{n}\backslash\mathcal{C}^{(j)}
. Moreover, let
\rho_{n,j}:=T_{n,j}(\mu_{n,j}^{*},s_{n,j}^{*},\alpha_{n,j}^{*},r_{n,j}^{*})

to the estimated nuisance functions

. Suppose
\|\rho_{n,\diamond}\|_{\overline{P}_{0}}=O_{P}(\varepsilon_{n})
for a deterministic sequence
\varepsilon_{n}\rightarrow 0
. Then,
\left(\overline{P}_{n}-\overline{P}_{0}\right)\rho_{n,\diamond}=o_{p}(n^{-\frac{1}{2}})
. Moreover, when
\overline{P}_{n},\alpha_{n,j}^{*}
,
\mu_{n,j}^{*}
,

are replaced with the bootstrap estimators

,

, and

, we also have
\left(\overline{P}_{n}^{\#}-\overline{P}_{n}\right)\rho_{n,\diamond}^{\#}=o_{p}(n^{-\frac{1}{2}})

.

Proof.

Since
\|\rho_{n,\diamond}\|_{\overline{P}_{0}}=O_{P}(\varepsilon_{n})
for some

D.5
Convergence Rates for Isotonic Calibrated Nuisance Estimators

Let
\alpha_{n,\diamond}^{*}:=\{\alpha_{n,j}^{*}:j\in[J]\}
and
\mu_{n,\diamond}^{*}:=\{\mu_{n,j}^{*}:j\in[J]\}
be the isotonic calibrated nuisance estimators obtained via Algorithm
1
. Let
\overline{\alpha}_{0}
and
\overline{\mu}_{0}
be their respective potentially misspecified limits, as in
C2
. In this section, we determine the mean square error rates for the calibrated nuisance estimators
\alpha_{n,j}^{*}
and
\mu_{n,j}^{*}
with respect to
\alpha_{0}
and
\mu_{0}
. We focus our convergence rate results on the case where the linear functional
\psi_{0}
takes the form
\mu\mapsto E_{0}\{m(A,W,\mu)\}
for a known map
m:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{W}\times\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Additionally, we assume that the linear functional estimator
\psi_{n}
is taken as the empirical plug-in estimator
\mu\mapsto\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}m(A_{i},W_{i},\mu)
. Our convergence rate results can be extended to general estimators
\psi_{n}
, provided that certain regularity conditions hold for the second-order remainder.

We begin by providing a result for a more general setting that holds for general loss functions. Consider a loss function
\ell(x,y,\nu):=\nu^{2}(x)-m(x,y,\nu)
for some mapping
m
. We assume
|m(x,y,\nu_{1})-m(x,y,\nu_{2})|\leq L|\nu_{1}(x)-\nu_{2}(x)|
for some
L>0
, and that, for any bounded, convex function class
\mathcal{F}
, the risk is locally quadratic around its minimizer in that
\|\nu-\nu_{0}(\mathcal{F})\|^{2}_{P_{0}}\lesssim P_{0}\{\ell(\cdot,\nu)-\ell(\cdot,\nu_{0}(\mathcal{F})\}
, where
\nu_{0}(\mathcal{F}):=\argmin_{\nu\in\mathcal{F}}\|\ell(X,Y,\nu)\|_{P_{0}}
. Let
\{\nu_{n,j}:j\in[J]\}
be cross-fitted estimators of the population risk minimizer
\nu_{0}:=\argmin_{\nu\in L^{2}(P_{0})}P_{0}\ell(\cdot,\nu)
. Define
\nu_{n,\diamond}^{*}:=h_{n}\circ\nu_{n,\diamond}
and
\nu_{n,\diamond}^{*\#}:=h_{n}^{\#}\circ\nu_{n,\diamond}
where we define the isotonic regression solutions:

\displaystyle h_{n}

\displaystyle:=\argmin_{h\in\mathcal{F}_{iso}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(X_{i},Y_{i},h\circ\nu_{n,j(i)});

\displaystyle h_{n}^{\#}

\displaystyle:=\argmin_{h\in\mathcal{F}_{iso}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(X_{i}^{\#},Y_{i}^{\#},h\circ\nu_{n,j(i)});

\displaystyle h_{n,0}

\displaystyle:=\argmin_{h\in\mathcal{F}_{iso}}\|h\circ\nu_{n,\diamond}-\nu_{0}\|_{\overline{P}_{0}}.

Theorem 7
.

Suppose that
h_{n}
,
h_{n}^{\#}
, and
h_{n,0}
fall in a uniformly bounded subset of
\mathcal{F}_{iso}
with probability tending to one. Then,
\|h_{n}\circ\nu_{n,\diamond}-h_{n,0}\circ\nu_{n,\diamond}\|_{\overline{P}_{0}}=O_{p}(n^{-1/3})
. Hence,

\displaystyle\|h_{n}\circ\nu_{n,\diamond}-\nu_{0}\|_{\overline{P}_{0}}

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{h\in\mathcal{F}_{iso}}\|h\circ\nu_{n,\diamond}-\nu_{0}\|+O_{p}(n^{-1/3})

Moreover, the bootstrap calibrated nuisance estimators satisfy, for any function
\overline{\nu}_{0}\in L^{2}(P_{0})
and as
n\rightarrow\infty
, the following root mean square error bounds:

\displaystyle\|h_{n}^{\#}\circ\nu_{n,\diamond}-\overline{\nu}_{0}\|_{\overline{P}_{0}}

\displaystyle\leq\|h_{n}\circ\nu_{n,\diamond}-\overline{\nu}_{0}\|_{\overline{P}_{0}}+O_{p}(n^{-1/3}).

Proof.

Let
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{iso}
be a uniformly bounded subset of
\mathcal{F}_{iso}
that contains
h_{n}
,
h_{n}^{\#}
, and
h_{n,0}
with probability tending to one. Define
\mathcal{F}_{n,j}:=\{f\circ\nu_{n,j}:f\in\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{iso}\}
. Since
h_{n}
and
h_{n,0}
fall in
\mathcal{F}_{iso}
with probability tending to one, it holds that
\nu_{n,j}^{*}
and
\nu_{n,0}
fall in
\mathcal{F}_{n,j}
with probability tending to one. The property
|m(x,y,\nu_{1})-m(x,y,\nu_{2})|\leq L|\nu_{1}(x)-\nu_{2}(x)|
for some
L>0
implies that
|\ell(x,y,\nu_{1})-\ell(x,y,\nu_{2})|\leq|\nu_{1}(x,y)-\nu_{2}(x,y)|\left\{\|\nu_{1}+\nu_{2}\|_{\infty}+L\right\}
.

We prove the convergence rates for the bootstrap estimators, as the rates for the non-bootstrap estimators follow along similar lines. To this end, for
j\in[J]
, we define the random rate
\delta_{n,j}^{\#}:=\|h_{n}^{\#}\circ\nu_{n,j}-h_{n,0}\circ\nu_{n,j}\|_{P_{0}}
and let
(\delta_{n}^{\#})^{2}:=\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}(\delta_{n,j}^{\#})^{2}
. By convexity of
\mathcal{F}_{iso}
, the locally quadratic assumption on
\ell
, and
h_{n,0}
being a population risk minimizer, we have that

(\delta_{n}^{\#})^{2}\lesssim\overline{P}_{0}\ell(\cdot,h_{n}^{\#}\circ\nu_{n,\diamond})-\overline{P}_{0}\ell(\cdot,h_{n,0}\circ\nu_{n,\diamond}).

Furthermore, since
h_{n}^{\#}\circ\nu_{n}
is the bootstrap empirical risk minimizer, it holds that

\overline{P}_{n}^{\#}\left\{\ell(\cdot,h_{n}^{\#}\circ\nu_{n,\diamond})-\ell(\cdot,h_{n,0}\circ\nu_{n,\diamond})\right\}\leq 0.

Combining the above two displays, we obtain that:

\displaystyle\overline{P}_{0}\ell(\cdot,h_{n}^{\#}\circ\nu_{n,\diamond})-\overline{P}_{0}\ell(\cdot,h_{n,0}\circ\nu_{n,\diamond})

\displaystyle=(\overline{P}_{0}-\overline{P}_{n}^{\#})\ell(\cdot,h_{n}^{\#}\circ\nu_{n,\diamond})+\overline{P}_{n}^{\#}\left\{\ell(\cdot,h_{n}^{\#}\circ\nu_{n,\diamond})-\ell(\cdot,h_{n,0}\circ\nu_{n,\diamond})\right\}

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt-(\overline{P}_{0}-\overline{P}_{n}^{\#})\ell(\cdot,h_{n,0}\circ\nu_{n,\diamond})

\displaystyle\leq(\overline{P}_{0}-\overline{P}_{n}^{\#})\left\{\ell(\cdot,h_{n}^{\#}\circ\nu_{n,\diamond})-\ell(\cdot,h_{n,0}\circ\nu_{n,\diamond})\right\},

and, therefore,

\displaystyle(\delta_{n}^{\#})^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim(\overline{P}_{0}-\overline{P}_{n}^{\#})\left\{\ell(\cdot,h_{n}^{\#}\circ\nu_{n,\diamond})-\ell(\cdot,h_{n,0}\circ\nu_{n,\diamond})\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\left[\gamma_{n,j}(\delta_{n,j}^{\#})+\gamma_{n,j}^{\#}(\delta_{n,j}^{\#})\right].

where we define:

\displaystyle\gamma_{n,j}(\delta)

\displaystyle:=\sup_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\in\mathcal{F}_{n,j}:\|\nu_{1}-\nu_{2}\|\leq\delta}\left|({P}_{0}-{P}_{n,j})\left\{\ell(\cdot,\nu_{1})-\ell(\cdot,\nu_{2})\right\}\right|;

\displaystyle\gamma_{n,j}^{\#}(\delta)

\displaystyle:=\sup_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\in\mathcal{F}_{n,j}:\|\nu_{1}-\nu_{2}\|\leq\delta}\left|(P_{n,j}-P_{n,j}^{\#})\left\{\ell(\cdot,\nu_{1})-\ell(\cdot,\nu_{2})\right\}\right|.

By our assumptions on the loss
\ell
, we have, for each
j\in[J]
and
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\in\mathcal{F}_{n,j}
, that
\ell(\cdot,\nu_{1})-\ell(\cdot,\nu_{2})
falls in a fixed bounded Lipschitz transformation of
\mathcal{F}_{n,j}\times\mathcal{F}_{n,j}
. Hence, from the proof of Lemma
6
with
t_{n}(\delta):=\sqrt{\delta}
, we have for any fixed
\delta>0
and conditional on
\mathcal{E}_{n,j}:=\mathcal{D}_{n}\backslash\mathcal{C}_{n,j}
that:

\displaystyle E_{0}^{n}[\gamma_{n,j}(\delta)|\mathcal{E}_{n,j}]

\displaystyle\lesssim n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{\delta}\left(1+\frac{\sqrt{\delta}}{\sqrt{n}\delta^{2}}\right);

\displaystyle E_{0}^{n}[\gamma_{n,j}^{\#}(\delta)|\mathcal{E}_{n,j}]

\displaystyle\lesssim n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{\delta}\left(1+\frac{\sqrt{\delta}}{\sqrt{n}\delta^{2}}\right),

where the bound on the right-hand side is deterministic.

We are now ready to obtain a convergence rate bound for
\delta_{n}^{\#}=\|h_{n}^{\#}\circ\nu_{n,\diamond}-h_{n,0}\circ\nu_{n,\diamond}\|_{\overline{P}_{0}}
. To do so, we use a peeling argument similar to the proof of Theorem 3.2.5 in
van der Vaart and Wellner 1996
. We will also use the following fact: if, for given vectors
x,y,u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{J}
, both
\sum_{j=1}^{J}x_{j}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{J}y_{j}
and
\sum_{j=1}^{J}u_{j}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{J}v_{j}
hold then there exists some
j^{*}\in[J]
such that both
x_{j^{*}}\leq y_{j^{*}}
and
u_{j^{*}}\leq v_{j^{*}}
. To verify this claim, suppose the contrapositive that
x_{j}>y_{j}
and
u_{j}>v_{j}
for all
j\in[J]
. Then, we must have
\sum_{j=1}^{J}x_{j}>\sum_{j=1}^{J}y_{j}
and
\sum_{j=1}^{J}u_{j}<\sum_{j=1}^{J}v_{j}
, which is a contradiction. The claim follows. Proceeding with the proof and letting
\varepsilon_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
be a deterministic rate to be set later, note

\displaystyle P(2^{k+1}\varepsilon_{n}\geq\delta_{n}^{\#}\geq 2^{k}\varepsilon_{n})

\displaystyle=P\left(2^{k+1}\varepsilon_{n}\geq\delta_{n}^{\#}\geq 2^{k}\varepsilon_{n},\sum_{j\in[J]}(\delta_{n,j}^{\#})^{2}\leq\sum_{j\in[J]}\left[\gamma_{n,j}(\delta_{n,j}^{\#})+\gamma_{n,j}^{\#}(\delta_{n,j}^{\#})\right]\right)

\displaystyle\leq P\left(2^{k+1}\varepsilon_{n}\geq\delta_{n,j_{n}}\geq 2^{k}\varepsilon_{n},\delta_{n,j_{n}}^{2}\leq\left[\theta_{n,j_{n}}(\delta_{n,j_{n}})+\theta_{n,j_{n}}^{\#}(\delta_{n,j_{n}})\right]\text{ for some }j_{n}\in[J]\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j\in[J]}P\left(2^{k+1}\varepsilon_{n}\geq\delta_{n,j}^{\#}\geq 2^{k}\varepsilon_{n},(\delta_{n,j}^{\#})^{2}\leq\left[\gamma_{n,j}(\delta_{n,j}^{\#})+\gamma_{n,j}^{\#}(\delta_{n,j}^{\#})\right]\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j\in[J]}P\left(2^{k+1}\varepsilon_{n}\geq\delta_{n,j}^{\#}\geq 2^{k}\varepsilon_{n},2^{2k}\varepsilon_{n}^{2}\leq\left[\gamma_{n,j}(2^{k+1}\varepsilon_{n})+\gamma_{n,j}^{\#}(2^{k+1}\varepsilon_{n})\right]\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j\in[J]}E_{0}^{n}\left[P\left(2^{2k}\varepsilon_{n}^{2}\leq\left[\gamma_{n,j}(2^{k+1}\varepsilon_{n})+\gamma_{n,j}^{\#}(2^{k+1}\varepsilon_{n})\right]\right)|\mathcal{E}_{n,j}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim E_{0}^{n}\left[\frac{E_{0}^{n}[\widetilde{\gamma}_{n,j}^{\#}(2^{k+1}\varepsilon_{n})|\mathcal{E}_{n,j}]+E_{0}^{n}[\widetilde{\gamma}_{n,j}^{\#}(2^{k+1}\varepsilon_{n})|\mathcal{E}_{n,j}]}{2^{2k}\varepsilon_{n}^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{2^{k+1}\varepsilon_{n}}(1+\frac{\sqrt{2^{k+1}\varepsilon_{n}}}{\sqrt{n}\varepsilon_{n}^{2}})}{2^{2k}\varepsilon_{n}^{2}},

for some fixed constant
C>0
.
Taking
\varepsilon_{n}:=n^{-1/3}
in the above, it is straightforward to show, for some other fixed constant
C^{\prime}>0
, that

\displaystyle P(2^{k+1}n^{-1/3}\geq\delta_{n}^{\#}\geq 2^{k}n^{-1/3})

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{2^{k+1}\varepsilon_{n}}(1+\frac{\sqrt{2^{k+1}\varepsilon_{n}}}{\sqrt{n}\varepsilon_{n}^{2}})}{2^{2k}\varepsilon_{n}^{2}}\leq C^{\prime}\frac{1}{2^{k}}.

Therefore, for any constant

Let

The following lemma establishes that the isotonic calibrated nuisance estimators are empirically calibrated for their respective loss functions.

Lemma 8

(Empirical calibration via isotonic calibration)
.

Then, for all
h_{1},h_{2}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(h_{1}\circ\mu_{n,j(i)}^{*})(A_{i},W_{i})\left\{Y_{i}-\mu_{n,j(i)}^{*}(A_{i},W_{i})\right\}

\displaystyle=0;

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\{(h_{2}\circ\alpha_{n,j(i)}^{*})(A_{i},W_{i})\alpha_{n,j(i)}^{*}(A_{i},W_{i})-\psi_{n,j(i)}(h_{2}\circ\alpha_{n,j(i)}^{*})\right\}

\displaystyle=0.

Proof.

The proof of this lemma follows from a simplification of the proof of Lemma 4 in
van der Laan et al. 2023b
. We give the proof for
\alpha_{n,\diamond}^{*}
as the proof for
\mu_{n,\diamond}^{*}
follows along similar lines. Recall that
\alpha_{n,\diamond}^{*}=g_{n}\circ\alpha_{n,\diamond}
for the empirical risk minimizer
g_{n}\in\mathcal{F}_{iso}
defined in Algorithm
1
. Also recall, following
Groeneboom and Lopuhaa 1993
, that we took
g_{n}
to be the unique càdlàg piece-wise constant solution of the isotonic regression problem that can only take jumps at observed values
\{\alpha_{n,j(i)}(A_{i},W_{i}):i\in[n]\}
. Hence,
g_{n}
is a piece-wise constant. We claim, for any function
h:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and all
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}
sufficiently close to zero, that the perturbed function
g_{n}+\varepsilon h\circ g_{n}\in\mathcal{F}_{iso}
. To see this, observe that if
g_{n}
is constant in some interval of its domain if and only if the same holds for
h\circ g_{n}
. Hence, the function
g_{n}+\varepsilon h\circ g_{n}
is piece-wise constant and jumps at the same points as
g_{n}
. Moreover, by taking
\varepsilon
sufficiently small, we can ensure that the maximal jump change
\varepsilon\sup_{i\in[n]}|(h\circ g_{n})(\mu_{n,j(i)}(A_{i},W_{i})|
of
h\circ g_{n}
is strictly smaller than the maximal jump change of
g_{n}
. Therefore, for this choice of
\varepsilon
, the perturbed function
g_{n}+\varepsilon h\circ g_{n}\in\mathcal{F}_{iso}
must be nondecreasing and, therefore, an element of
\mathcal{F}_{iso}
. The claim now follows.

Proceeding with the proof of the Lemma, we denote
\xi_{n}(\varepsilon,\alpha_{n,j}):=g_{n}\circ\alpha_{n,j}+\varepsilon(h\circ g_{n})(\alpha_{n,j})
and, in a slight abuse of notation, let

R_{n}(\xi_{n}(\varepsilon)):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\{\xi_{n}(\varepsilon,\alpha_{n,j})^{2}(A_{i},W_{i})-2\psi_{n,j(i)}(\xi_{n}(\varepsilon,\alpha_{n,j}))\right\}.

Now, because
g_{n}
minimizes
g\mapsto R_{n}(g)
over
\mathcal{F}_{iso}
and
g_{n}+\varepsilon h\circ g_{n}\in\mathcal{F}_{iso}
for
\varepsilon
sufficiently close to
0
, it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}[R_{n}(\xi_{n}(\varepsilon))-R_{n}(g_{n})]\Big|_{\varepsilon=0}=0.

Computing the derivative, using linearity of the functional
\psi_{n,\diamond}
and that
h\circ g_{n}\circ\alpha_{n,\diamond}=h\circ\alpha_{n,\diamond}^{*}
, this further implies:

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\{(h\circ\alpha_{n,j(i)}^{*})(A_{i},W_{i})\alpha_{n,j(i)}^{*}(A_{i},W_{i})-\psi_{n,j(i)}(h\circ\alpha_{n,j(i)}^{*})\right\}=0.

The result for
\alpha_{n,\diamond}^{*}
now follows, noting that
h
was an arbitrary function.
