Application of the extended parallel process model to assess the predictors of physical exercise in preventing non-communicable diseases among civil servants in Ethiopia, a mixed-method study
Authors: Natnael Kebede, Mulugeta Desalegn Kasaye, Amare Muche, Yawkal Tsega
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionDespite the importance of physical exercise in preventing non-communicable diseases, a research gap exists regarding its predictors among civil servants in Ethiopia's South Wollo Zone. Few studies have used theoretical frameworks like the Extended Parallel Process Model (EPPM) to investigate threat, efficacy, and exercise behavior in this population. This study applied the EPPM in a mixed-methods design to assess these predictors. It provides insights into how perceived threat, perceived efficacy, and response efficacy influence behavior, combining numerical data with subjective experiences to comprehensively understand factors affecting exercise for NCD prevention in 2025.MethodsA concurrent mixed-methods approach was employed in conducting the study, combining qualitative and quantitative research methods simultaneously between February 10–28, 2025, GC. The quantitative study employed a stratified sampling technique to select 422 study participants, while the qualitative study utilized purposive sampling with the rule of saturation. Data were collected using self-administered questionnaires. For the qualitative study, in-depth interviews were conducted with the assistance of an audio recorder and an interview guide, led by the investigators. Data completeness was verified, and it was collected and entered using the KoboTool software before being exported to Stata version 17 for analysis. Descriptive statistics were computed, and logistic regression analysis was used to assess the significance of the association between the outcome response of physical exercise for NCD prevention and independent variables. Variables with a p-value less than 0.05 were considered predictors of outcome response of physical exercise in preventing non-communicable diseases, and the adjusted odds ratio with 95% CI was used to determine the strength and significance of the association.ResultsThe outcome response of physical exercise for NCD prevention revealed that less than half of the civil servants, 47.57% (CI: 42.66%-52.52%), were engaged in adaptive Danger Control. Key predictors of this protective outcome response were identified; self-efficacy (AOR = 2.18, 95% CI: 1.68, 2.83) and response efficacy (AOR = 2.48, 95% CI: 1.92, 3.20) were the strongest predictors of being in the Danger Control category. Conversely, a higher perceived severity of threat significantly reduced the odds of this adaptive exercise behavior (AOR = 0.43, 95% CI: 0.32, 0.58). Among socio-demographic factors, marital status was a significant predictor. Single civil servants had four times higher odds of engaging in regular physical exercise for NCD prevention compared to their married counterparts (AOR = 4.03, 95% CI: 1.32, 12.36). The qualitative exploration provided crucial context, revealing that the transition to protective physical exercise is primarily driven by a strong belief in one's capabilities and the solution's effectiveness, while overwhelming fear acts as a significant barrier.ConclusionsThis study found a lower proportion of civil servants engaged in preventive physical exercise for NCDs compared to previous studies. Self-efficacy and response efficacy were strong positive predictors of this adaptive behavior, whereas perceived threat severity was a significant negative predictor. Single civil servants showed higher exercise engagement than their married counterparts. Qualitative data revealed that protective behavior was driven by belief in personal capability and exercise effectiveness, while overwhelming fear of severity acted as a key barrier, explaining the counterproductive threat-behavior relationship. Interventions should build self-efficacy, demonstrate benefits, avoid fear-messaging, and implement supportive workplace policies for married staff.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-26097-3.

Introduction

The Extended Parallel Process Model (EPPM) is a theoretical framework that explains how individuals respond to health threats through the interplay of perceived threat (severity and susceptibility) and perceived efficacy (self-efficacy and response efficacy). When efficacy beliefs outweigh threat perceptions, individuals engage in the adaptive "Danger Control" process and adopt protective behaviors. Conversely, when threat perceptions dominate, a maladaptive "Fear Control" response occurs, characterized by defensive avoidance [1, 2].

Non-communicable diseases (NCDs) have become a significant public health concern worldwide, including in Ethiopia. The increasing prevalence of NCDs, such as cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, and obesity, has highlighted the need for effective preventive strategies. Regular physical exercise has been identified as a crucial factor in preventing NCDs and promoting overall health and well-being [3, 4]. Physical exercise has been widely recognized as a critical preventive measure and management strategy for NCDs. Engaging in regular physical exercise has been shown to reduce the risk of developing NCDs, enhance overall health and well-being, and improve quality of life [5].

Physical exercise is a powerful modifiable risk factor that can significantly reduce the burden of NCDs. Regular exercise helps to maintain a healthy body weight, control blood pressure, improve lipid profiles, enhance insulin sensitivity, and boost overall cardiovascular fitness. Furthermore, physical exercise has been linked to reducing the risk of certain types of cancer, such as colon and breast cancer. Exercise also plays a crucial role in managing and improving mental health, including reducing stress, anxiety, and depression [5–7].

Non-communicable diseases have reached epidemic proportions globally, causing approximately 41 million deaths each year, accounting for 71% of global mortality [8]. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that physical inactivity is responsible for around 3.2 million deaths annually, making it the fourth leading risk factor for mortality worldwide [9]. Africa bears a substantial burden of non-communicable diseases, with an increasing prevalence over the past few decades. Urbanization, lifestyle changes, and a shift towards sedentary behaviors have contributed to the rise of NCDs in African countries. The lack of physical exercise among African populations exacerbates the problem, making them more susceptible to NCDs [10].

Ethiopia has witnessed a rapid epidemiological transition in recent years, with NCDs accounting for a substantial proportion of the disease burden. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), NCDs were responsible for approximately 33% of all deaths in Ethiopia in 2018. Cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, cancer, and chronic respiratory diseases were the leading causes of mortality [11, 12]. The consequences of low physical exercise rates include an increased burden of NCDs such as cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, and certain types of cancer. Additionally, reduced productivity and increased absenteeism due to health-related issues impact both individual and societal levels. The strain on the healthcare system due to the treatment of NCDs further exacerbates the problem [10, 13].

Various factors can influence the likelihood of civil servants engaging in physical exercise for NCD prevention. These factors may include socio-demographic characteristics, personal beliefs, knowledge about NCDs and exercise, perceived barriers and benefits of physical activity, social support, and environmental factors. Understanding these predictors can help identify target areas for intervention and develop strategies to promote physical exercise among civil servants [14–16]. Civil servants constitute a substantial proportion of the workforce in Ethiopia and play vital roles in the public sector. However, due to the nature of their work, civil servants often lead sedentary lifestyles, spending extended periods sitting at desks or engaging in minimal physical activity. This lack of exercise poses a significant risk for the development of NCDs, not only affecting the individuals themselves but also impacting their productivity and overall quality of life [17].

Despite the importance of physical exercise in preventing non-communicable diseases, there is a research gap in understanding the predictors of exercise engagement among civil servants in the South Wollo Zone of Ethiopia. Limited studies have utilized theoretical frameworks such as the Extended Parallel Process Model (EPPM) to investigate the relationships between threat, efficacy, and exercise behavior in this specific population. The Extended Parallel Process Model (EPPM) was chosen for this study as it uniquely accounts for emotional and fear-based responses to health threats, which is critical in the Ethiopian context where non-communicable diseases are often perceived as severe and fatal. Unlike other models focused primarily on rational decision-making, the EPPM's distinction between adaptive danger control and maladaptive fear control provides a more nuanced framework for understanding why high threat perceptions may not translate into protective physical exercise behavior in this population, making it particularly suitable for investigating the complex cognitive and emotional processes influencing exercise adoption among Ethiopian civil servant.By incorporating this theoretical framework, the study will provide valuable insights into factors such as perceived threat, perceived efficacy, and response efficacy that influence exercise behavior. Additionally, the use of mixed methods will provide comprehensive insights into the factors influencing exercise behavior, incorporating both numerical data and subjective experiences.

Theoretical framework

Based on the Extended Parallel Process Model (EPPM), the theoretical framework proposes that an individual's response to the health threat of non-communicable diseases (NCDs) is determined by the balance between their perceived threat (comprising severity and susceptibility) and their perceived efficacy (comprising self-efficacy and response efficacy). If the perceived efficacy outweighs the perceived threat, the individual will engage in the adaptive "Danger Control" process, leading to the recommended protective behavior of physical exercise. Methods and materials

Study design, area, and period

A concurrent mixed-methods approach was employed, combining quantitative and qualitative research components simultaneously. The study was conducted among civil servants in the South Wollo Zone of Ethiopia from February 10–28, 2025. The overall methodology, including aspects of the sampling procedure, data collection, and quality assurance, was adapted from a previous study by our team investigating weight management behaviors, with modifications made to align with the specific focus on physical exercise and the Extended Parallel Process Model (EPPM) [18].

Study population and eligibility

The source population was all civil servants in the South Wollo Zone. The study population consisted of all currently employed civil servants aged 18 years or older. Those who were unable to respond due to illness were excluded.

Variables

Dependent variable

Physical exercise engagement among civil servants (fear control or danger control).

Independent variables

Socio-demographic factors (age, sex, educational status, marital status, and income of parents).

Extended parallel process model constructs (Severity of threat, Susceptibility to threat, Self-efficacy, and Response efficacy).

Sample size and sampling

The quantitative sample size was calculated using the single population proportion formula, yielding a minimum of 384 participants. Accounting for a 10% non-response rate, the final sample size was 422. A stratified sampling technique was used. From a sampling frame of all 22 woredas (districts) in the zone, five were randomly selected. The sample size was proportionally allocated to these woredas, and participants were chosen via simple random sampling.

For the qualitative component, a purposive sampling technique was used. Thematic saturation was reached after 8 in-depth interviews.

Data collection procedure

Quantitative Data

Data were collected using a structured, self-administered questionnaire. This questionnaire was newly developed for the current study based on the EPPM constructs. It covered socio-demographic variables and EPPM components: Severity of Threat, Susceptibility to Threat, Self-efficacy, and Response Efficacy. Each construct was measured with four items on a 5-point bipolar Likert scale. The tool is provided as Supplementary File 1.

Qualitative data

In-depth interviews (IDIs) were conducted by the principal investigator in a private setting. An interview guide was used to explore participants' perceptions in depth. Interviews lasted approximately 30–45 min, were audio-recorded with permission, and continued until thematic saturation was reached, which occurred after 8 interviews.

Measurement

Severity of threat

The perceived seriousness of the potential negative consequences associated with non-communicable diseases resulting from a lack of physical exercise was measured using four items on a 5-point bipolar Likert scale, summed to obtain a score, and used as a continuous variable for further analysis.

Susceptibility to threat

The perceived personal vulnerability or likelihood of experiencing non-communicable diseases due to a lack of physical exercise was measured using four items on a 5-point bipolar Likert scale, summed to obtain a score, and used as a continuous variable for further analysis.

Self-efficacy

The belief in one's ability to engage in and maintain regular physical exercise to prevent non-communicable diseases was measured using four items on a 5-point bipolar Likert scale, summed to obtain a score, and used as a continuous variable for further analysis.

Response efficacy

The belief that engaging in physical exercise would effectively reduce the risk of non-communicable diseases was measured using four items on a 5-point bipolar Likert scale, summed to obtain a score, and used as a continuous variable for further analysis.

Outcome response (Danger Control or Fear Control)

The response was measured using the Sum Threat Score (TS) and Efficacy Score (ES) separately. Subsequently, the Discriminating Value (DV) was computed as the difference between the Efficacy Score (ES) and the Threat Score (TS). If the Discriminating Value was greater than zero, it indicated that efficacy outweighed the threat, leading to the classification of the response as Danger Control. Conversely, if the Discriminating Value was less than zero, the response was classified as Fear Control, indicating that the threat was stronger than the efficacy. This classification follows the EPPM's theoretical premise that protective behavior is only initiated when efficacy definitively exceeds threat; when threat is equal to or greater than efficacy (DV ≤ 0), the individual remains in a state of fear control where defensive responses dominate [19].

Data quality assurance

The questionnaire was initially prepared in English and then translated into Amharic. To ensure consistency, it was subsequently back-translated into English by another person. A pretest was conducted on 5% of the study population in Kombolcha town, and necessary amendments were made based on any issues related to understanding, clarity, and vocabulary. Throughout the data collection period, close supervision was maintained by supervisors. Data collectors and supervisors received one day of training on the questionnaire and the data collection process. The collected data underwent thorough checks for completeness and consistency. To enhance data quality, a double data entry method using Epidata was employed. The face validity of the questionnaire was assessed by considering the views and comments of experts in the field of health promotion. Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) was conducted to assess convergent validity, and the results demonstrated acceptable factor loadings across the measurement items. For the constructs, factor loadings ranged from 0.60 to 0.71, with eigenvalues supporting its unidimensional structure. Internal consistency of the constructs was evaluated using Composite Reliability (CR) and Cronbach’s alpha (α), with alpha values ranging from 0.59 to 0.77, which are considered acceptable for newly adapted or context-specific behavioral measures. Composite reliability values also met the recommended threshold, indicating that the measurement demonstrated adequate reliability and construct validity.

Rigors and trustworthiness of the study

After data collection began, investigators engaged in discussions concerning the study objectives, the confidentiality of information, the contents of the open-ended semi-structured questionnaire, and data quality management. To verify transcription and ensure accuracy, a subset of participants was enlisted to review the transcribed data and assess whether the investigators had accurately captured their perspectives. To maintain authenticity, all data were included in the final report, providing readers with a transparent understanding of the basis for the conclusions drawn. Rigor was upheld through meticulous attention to detail, adherence to procedures, and consistent accuracy throughout the research process. Peer review was conducted to enhance credibility, with trained qualitative researchers reviewing the data, analysis, interpretations, and conclusions. A session with peer researchers from similar backgrounds was held to ensure consistency between the data analysis and thematic development.

Throughout the data collection process, an audit trail was established, which remained intact until the member check and transcription verification were completed. Additionally, reflexivity was practiced through self-awareness and bracketing techniques. The study employed an explorative approach to gain a comprehensive understanding of the core phenomenon being investigated. To ensure dependability, meticulous documentation was maintained, minimizing spelling errors and including all relevant documents in the final report. Transferability was addressed by providing evidence and offering a detailed description of the study, encompassing the sampling process, data analysis, and results. This comprehensive approach allowed for the evaluation of the generalizability of the study's findings.

Data management and analysis

The collected data were checked for completeness and consistency. Subsequently, the data were entered into the Kobo Tool software and exported to Stata version 17 for analysis, which was conducted by the investigators. Descriptive analysis was employed, calculating measures such as minimum, maximum, means, and standard deviations. Logistic regression analysis was used to assess the significance of the association between dependent and independent variables. Bivariable and multivariable analyses were computed to examine the association between the response outcome and selected independent variables. In bivariable logistic regression analysis, variables with a p-value of 0.25 or less were entered into the multivariable logistic regression analysis. Variables with a p-value less than 0.05 were considered predictors of physical exercise in preventing non-communicable diseases, and the adjusted odds ratio with 95% confidence interval was used to determine the strength and significance of the association. The adequacy of the final model was checked using the Hosmer and Lemeshow Goodness-of-Fit test.

For qualitative data analysis

The audio-recorded interviews were transcribed verbatim and translated into English by the investigators, with the translated data subsequently verified for accuracy by an independent reader. Thematic analysis was then conducted using a rigorous approach. The principal investigators meticulously reviewed the transcriptions multiple times, listening to the audio recordings repeatedly to ensure data integrity and gain a comprehensive understanding of the participants' perspectives. Each unit of meaning was systematically labeled with a code through open coding, and similar codes were organized into meaningful categories. The data analysis process was facilitated manually. The categories underwent a rigorous peer-review process and were cross-checked by the co-lead author, leading to the final creation of categories and themes. Subsequently, the report was crafted based on these categories and predefined themes aligned with the study objectives. Quotes were selectively employed to highlight each category and establish their association with respective themes. Finally, a triangulation approach was applied to integrate the quantitative findings with the qualitative findings, ensuring a comprehensive and well-rounded analysis.

The outcome response of physical exercise for preventing non-communicable diseases

Factors associated with physical exercise behavior for NCD prevention

The analysis identified key predictors influencing whether civil servants adopted an adaptive exercise behavior (Danger Control) to prevent NCDs or a non-adaptive response (Fear Control). Bivariable analysis indicated that age, marital status, monthly income, severity of threat, self-efficacy, and response efficacy were candidate variables for the final model (p-value < 0.25). The Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test (p = 0.7162) confirmed the model's adequacy.

After adjustment, four variables significantly predicted the outcome of engaging in physical exercise for NCD prevention. Efficacy beliefs were the strongest facilitators: for every one-unit increase in self-efficacy, the odds of practicing protective physical exercise (Danger Control) more than doubled (AOR = 2.18, 95% CI: 1.68, 2.83). Likewise, a one-unit increase in response efficacy nearly tripled the odds of this positive exercise outcome (AOR = 2.48, 95% CI: 1.92, 3.20).

Conversely, the severity of threat demonstrated a significant negative association with exercise behavior; a one-unit increase was associated with a 57% reduction in the odds of being in the Danger Control category (AOR = 0.43, 95% CI: 0.32, 0.58), suggesting that high fear can inhibit the intended preventive action.

Qualitative findings: thematic analysis of in-depth interviews

The analysis identified four central themes. First, High Perceived Susceptibility Tempered by Invincibility revealed participants acknowledged general NCD risks but often minimized their personal vulnerability. Second, Severity as a Double-Edged Sword showed that while the serious consequences of NCDs motivated some, it caused paralyzing fear and fatalism in others. Third, the Pivotal Role of Self-Efficacy emerged as the key differentiator, where confidence and practical strategies to overcome barriers were essential for consistent exercise. Finally, Unshakable Belief in Response Efficacy underscored a universal, often experientially-based, conviction in exercise's effectiveness, forming the critical foundation for initiating and sustaining preventive behavior.

Theme 1: high perceived susceptibility tempered by a sense of invincibility

Participants frequently expressed an intellectual understanding of their vulnerability to NCDs, often linking it directly to their sedentary occupations. However, this was frequently accompanied by a psychological distancing from the immediate risk.A 28-year-old male participant reported that, "I know that sitting all day in the office is not good, and that diseases like hypertension can come. Discussion

This study applied the Extended Parallel Process Model (EPPM) to investigate the predictors of physical exercise for non-communicable disease (NCD) prevention among civil servants in Ethiopia. The findings reveal a complex interplay of cognitive processes that both confirm and challenge established theoretical expectations, offering valuable insights for public health intervention. The finding that nearly half, 47.57% (95% CI: 42.66%–52.52%) of the civil servants were engaged in adaptive Danger Control indicates a significant portion of the population is effectively translating health threats into protective action. This proportion is notably lower than the 63.8% reported in a similar EPPM study on diabetes self-care in Jimma, Ethiopia [20], suggesting that the majority may, in fact, be in the maladaptive Fear Control pathway. This discrepancy could be attributed to the nature of the recommended behavior; while diabetes self-care often involves structured medication regimens, incorporating regular physical exercise requires more substantial lifestyle changes, facing greater barriers like time, access to facilities, and cultural norms around leisure-time activity [21].

The finding that self-efficacy was the strongest predictor robustly validates the EPPM framework. The model posits that self-efficacy is a critical determinant for danger control, a premise confirmed by our finding that with each unit increase in self-efficacy, the odds of adaptive behavior more than double. This result is highly consistent with a study on skin cancer prevention, which found self-efficacy to be the strongest predictor of behavioral intention [22]. Our qualitative data provide the "why" behind this statistic, revealing that individuals with high self-efficacy employed practical strategies (e.g., exercising immediately after work) to proactively overcome environmental and motivational barriers. This confirms that fostering a belief in one's capability to perform a behavior is a universally essential component of successful health promotion, transcending specific cultural contexts.

Similarly, the finding that response efficacy was the strongest predictor robustly validates the EPPM framework. This suggests that the fundamental belief that "exercise works" is the most powerful engine for motivating behavior in this population. This aligns with meta-analytic findings that response efficacy is a consistent and potent predictor of adaptive outcomes in fear appeal research [23]. The high prevalence of this belief, as echoed in our qualitative themes where participants cited personal and observed benefits, provides a solid foundation for public health messaging. It indicates that the message about the effectiveness of exercise for NCD prevention is being received and believed, which is a crucial first step that should not be undermined.

The most striking and theoretically divergent finding is that higher perceived severity significantly reduced the odds of adaptive exercise behavior. This directly contradicts a baseline EPPM assumption that high perceived threat is a necessary motivator for action [19]. The negative association between perceived severity and exercise behavior, though inconsistent with the classical EPPM assumption, is supported by recent evidence showing that very high severity can trigger fear-control processes, including defensive avoidance and fatalistic beliefs, especially in low-resource settings. Studies demonstrate that when threats are perceived as overwhelming or uncontrollable, individuals disengage rather than take protective action [24]. In Sub-Saharan Africa, heightened severity of NCDs has been linked to fatalism, where individuals view chronic disease as inevitable, reducing motivation for preventive behaviors. Our qualitative findings confirm this mechanism: participants often described severe NCD consequences as “too frightening,” leading to avoidance, fatalistic thinking, or emotional shutdown. Thus, the inverse relationship between severity and exercise behavior reflects the shift from danger control to fear control, consistent with updated interpretations of fear appeal theory in LMIC contexts. While the model allows that high threat with low efficacy leads to fear control, our results suggest that in this context, high severity itself can be inhibitory, even when controlling for efficacy. This discrepancy can be justified by contextual factors. The qualitative data revealed themes of fatalism (e.g., "if it is my destiny to get sick") and defensive avoidance. In a setting with limited access to healthcare resources, perceptions of severe, life-altering NCDs may not induce a sense of control but rather a state of helplessness and psychological reactance, where the recommended behavior feels futile against an overwhelming threat [25]. This finding warns against fear-mongering campaigns in similar settings, as they may inadvertently promote maladaptive fear control responses instead of the intended danger control.

The significant demographic predictor, where single civil servants were four times more likely of being in the Danger Control category compared to their married counterparts, is consistent with broader literature on physical activity. Married individuals, particularly in a context with strong family responsibilities, often report greater time constraints and competing priorities, such as childcare and household duties, which act as significant barriers to regular exercise [26]. This highlights the need for targeted interventions that address the specific logistical and social barriers faced by married civil servants, such as promoting family-inclusive physical activities or time-management strategies.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study possesses notable strengths, including its application of the robust Extended Parallel Process Model (EPPM) theoretical framework, the use of a concurrent mixed-methods design, which provided both quantitative breadth and qualitative depth, a high response rate enhancing the representativeness of the findings, and rigorous data quality control measures. However, it is also subject to limitations, such as the cross-sectional design, which prevents the establishment of causal relationships, and the reliance on self-reported measures for physical exercise behavior, which may be subject to social desirability bias. The study’s logistic regression results should be interpreted cautiously because some predictors, such as marital status, had wide confidence intervals due to small subgroup sample size.

Policy and practice implications

Based on the EPPM framework and our findings, public health initiatives should be strategically designed to promote Danger Control responses. For message framing, health authorities and campaign designers must avoid fear appeals that lack efficacy scaffolding. Messaging should minimize emphasis on disease severity and instead focus on building self-efficacy through clear, achievable goals (e.g., "Start with a 15-min walk") and reinforcing response efficacy by highlighting the proven benefits of exercise. Programmatically, this involves creating structured exercise programs with gradual progression, skill-building workshops, and peer support networks. Workplace policies must specifically address the logistical barriers faced by married employees through flexible scheduling, on-site facilities, and family-inclusive activities to effectively encourage sustainable exercise habits for NCD prevention.

Conclusions

This study revealed that the proportion of civil servants engaged in adaptive physical exercise behavior for NCD prevention was notably lower than reported in previous studies. The outcome response was predicted by several key factors, with self-efficacy and response efficacy identified as strong positive predictors that facilitated adaptive behavior. Conversely, perceived threat severity served as a significant negative predictor, inhibiting protective action. Among socio-demographic factors, marital status emerged as a predictor, with single civil servants demonstrating higher engagement in preventive exercise compared to their married counterparts. These quantitative findings were further illuminated by qualitative data, which revealed that individuals' transition to protective behavior was primarily driven by strong beliefs in their personal capabilities and the effectiveness of exercise. Meanwhile, overwhelming fear of disease severity frequently acted as a substantial barrier to action, potentially explaining the counterproductive relationship between threat perception and behavioral outcomes observed in the quantitative analysis.

Recommendations

Program developers should design initiatives that enhance self-efficacy through structured exercise programs with gradual progression, skill-building workshops, and peer support networks to strengthen individuals' confidence in their exercise capabilities. Simultaneously, response efficacy should be reinforced through clear communication of exercise benefits, success stories from peers, and evidence-based information about NCD prevention. For message framing, authorities should minimize emphasis on disease severity and instead focus on achievable health gains and positive outcomes. Workplace policies should particularly address the needs of married employees through flexible scheduling, family-inclusive activities, and time management support to overcome identified participation barriers.
