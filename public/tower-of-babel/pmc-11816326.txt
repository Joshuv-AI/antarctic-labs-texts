Influence of demographic, socioeconomic and geographical variability on children under-five mortality in The Gambia: an application of survival analysis
Authors: Amadou Barrow, Musa Nget, Modou Lamin Jammeh, Ramatoulie M Ceesay, Fatou Jankeh Jawara, Sheikh Omar Sillah
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThere has been a notable decline in child mortality globally, yet Gambia continues to face a high mortality rate among children under five, which poses a serious public health challenge. This research delves into the differences in demographic, socioeconomic and geographic aspects that affect the survival rates of children under five in The Gambia.MethodsIn this study, a retrospective cross-sectional approach was adopted, which is quantitative. The researchers used data from the Gambia Demographic Health Survey conducted in 2019–2020. To analyse the impact of demographic, socioeconomic and geographical factors on the survival of children under 5 years of age in The Gambia, we applied Kaplan-Meier survival estimates along with the Cox proportional hazards model.ResultsThe weighted under-five mortality (U5M) rate was 52 per 1000 live births. Our Kaplan-Meier survival estimates illustrate that most under-five mortalities occur within the first 48–60 months after birth, with children from the poorest families facing the highest risk. Conversely, children from the wealthiest families are least affected by geographical regions and the household wealth index. At the multivariable level, the results suggest that fathers with secondary education levels had 36% (HR=0.64; 95% CI 0.42 to 0.97) lower risk of U5M than those with tertiary educational levels. Among the eight regions, Janjanbureh (HR=1.93; 95% CI 1.02 to 3.63), Kuntaur (HR=2.13; 95% CI 1.13 to 4.03) and Mansakonko (HR=1.96; 95% CI 1.03 to 3.74) reported higher risks of U5M compared with those from Banjul after controlling for confounders.ConclusionThe research highlights considerable differences based on demographics, socioeconomic conditions and geography in the mortality rates among children under five in The Gambia. This underscores the urgent need for specific strategies targeting the most at-risk areas and socioeconomic groups to improve child survival outcomes.

Introduction

Global disparities in under-five mortality (U5M), strongly influenced by demographic, socioeconomic and geographic variations, pose a significant challenge to public health, predominantly in lower- to middle-income nations.1 This inequality is especially pronounced in geographical distribution, contributing significantly to the variance in child mortality due to demographic and socioeconomic factors.2 However, there has been substantial progress globally in reducing the mortality rate of children under 5 years old, which has been regarded as slower than expected. Globally, between 2000 and 2021, the mortality rate dropped by half, from 76 to 38 deaths per 1000 live births. The total number of under-five deaths dropped significantly during this period, from 9.9 million in 2000 to 5 million in 2021.1 3 Two regions, sub-Saharan Africa and Southern Asia, are responsible for more than 80% of the 5 million under-five deaths in 2021.1 3 According to the UNICEF and WHO reports of 2020, children from developing regions face a 10-fold mortality risk compared with those in developed countries.4

The U5M rate (U5MR) serves as a key indicator of a population’s health, influenced by many factors, including economic circumstances, social welfare and environmental conditions.58 Sadly, progress in survival rates has significantly slowed since 2010, and 54 countries will not meet the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) goal for U5M, and 63 countries will fail to reach the SDG target for neonatal mortality.3 U5M is an essential benchmark for the SDGs. In particular, SDG Goal 3.2 targets eradicating preventable deaths in under-five children, aiming to bring down the mortality rate to <25 per 1000 live births across all nations.3 This objective stands as a crucial measure for evaluating child health levels across populations and in turn, reflecting the overall development status of each nation. The U5MR offers a demographic evaluation of a country’s populace and is a significant indicator of a nation’s socioeconomic development and quality of life.911

In The Gambia, the infant mortality ratio is estimated at 39 per 1000 live births, with majority of the deaths occurring in the neonatal period (26 deaths per 1000 live births in 2018), which is well over 12 deaths per 1000 live births set in the SDG 3.2.12 Infant and U5MRs for the 5-year period before the survey are 42 and 56 deaths per 1000 live births, respectively. The neonatal mortality rate is 29 deaths per 1000 live births. Based on these mortality levels, about 1 in 18 children in The Gambia does not survive to their fifth birthday (GBOS and ICF, 2021). These statistics underscored the importance of focusing on under-five survival through the designing of responsive and scalable strategies to reduce the burden of under-five mortalities. In The Gambia, socioeconomic factors such as poverty and the lack of a middle class have considerably impacted child mortality, with little difference observed between the poorest and the middle wealth groups but a significant disparity with the wealthiest category.13 Furthermore, cultural and religious issues like child marriage have severely influenced child mortality rates, particularly evident in the reduced survival rates of children born to mothers under 20 years old.8 13 Geographical variability also plays a role in child mortality in The Gambia. There are differences in prevalence based on location (urban vs rural) and within rural communities.10 12

Despite the significance of previous studies, they did not account for the analysis of time-to-event censored data. To bridge this knowledge gap, the present research leverages the Kaplan-Meier survival estimates and the Cox proportional-hazards regression model instead of the conventional regression model to explore socioeconomic and geographical disparities in U5MR.1416 Both the Kaplan-Meier and the Cox proportional-hazards regression models offer robust alternatives to the logistic regression, which fails to incorporate survival time and censoring information.17 Considering that a significant portion of U5M causes are preventable, this paper seeks to examine the influence of demographic, socioeconomic and geographical disparities on U5MRs. By doing so, this study aims to enhance efforts towards reducing U5M in The Gambia. The results of this analysis will offer evidence-based insights to guide the design and implementation of targeted interventions and strategies for improvement.

Methods

Setting of the study

The study focuses on The Gambia, a culturally, ethnically and geographically diverse country with an estimated population of around 2.0 million people with an annual growth rate of 3.3%.18 The Gambia is found on the western coast of Africa lying between latitudes 130 and 140 N and longitude 130 and 140 W.18 It is one of the smallest countries in mainland Africa with a land size of 10 689 km2 making it the fourth-most densely populated country in Africa.19 It has a long narrow shape stretching a distance of 487 km inland from the west and a width of 24 km.20 The nation’s population is young, with about 55% under the age of 25, and has a high total fertility rate of nearly four children per woman.21 22 The Gambia’s population is predominantly rural, with significant urbanisation occurring, particularly along the Gambia River and its largest city, Serekunda. The economy largely depends on agriculture, but economic challenges have led to migration, both within the country and internationally. In terms of healthcare, the current health expenditure is around 2.6% of GDP as of 2020, with a low physician density of 0.08 physicians per 1000 population and a hospital bed density of 1.1 beds per 1000 population.21 The country has over eight identifiable ethnic groups, with the Mandinka and Wollof being the largest and politically dominant tribes. Cultural values and practices unique to these ethnic groups significantly influence child health outcomes. Moreover, there are significant ethnic variations in healthcare utilisation due to the cultural differences among Gambians.

Study design and data source

The research adopted a retrospective cross-sectional quantitative approach, leveraging data from the latest Gambia Demographic and Health Survey (GDHS) for the period of 2019/2020, which was conducted from November 2019 until March 2020.23 Carried out every half-decade, the GDHS is a comprehensive and nationally representative health survey that uses a layered sampling strategy, standardised tools and proficient interviewers. The survey, which stands as the largest of its kind globally, garners information on demographic factors and rates of infant and child mortality from the birth histories given by the interviewed mothers. Randomly chosen samples from clusters formed the survey’s principal sample unit. Besides, the GDHS collects data on geographic and socioeconomic attributes like the household wealth index, parental education levels and rural/urban living conditions. The 2019/2020 GDHS Birth Recode data file was employed in this study, given that the assessment of disparities in child mortality necessitates details on the socioeconomic status and the age of demise of children aged five or under. A total of 7025 households were successfully interviewed as part of the survey, as 25 households were systematically picked from each cluster across the 48 districts, 120 wards and 8 local government areas.23 The survey recorded a response rate of 95% from a sample size of 31 665. Nonetheless, the current study’s analysis was limited to a weighted sample of children aged 0–59 months (n=29 565).

Study variables

The dependent variable consists of two components: a binary indicator and time duration. The binary indicator is set to 1 if a child who is born alive passes away before reaching the age of 5, and is set to 0 otherwise. The time duration is measured in months and represents the time that elapses until the child’s death occurs, if applicable.7 11 14 16 24 The explanatory variables include demographic variables: child gender (male, female), mother’s age (at first birth), mother’s age at first sex (sexual debut), marital status (married, unmarried), religion (Islam, Christian), ethnicity (Mandinka/Jahanka, Wollof/Serere, Fula/Tukulur/Lorobo, Jola/Sarahule, non-Gambian/others); socioeconomic variables: maternal and paternal education (no formal education, primary, secondary, higher), household wealth index (poorest, poorer, middle, richer, richest); geographical variables: place of residence (urban, rural) and region (Banjul, Basse, Brikama, Janbureh, Kanifing, Kerewan, Kuntaur, Mansakonko).7 11 14 16 24

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis method adopted for this study is survival analysis. It involves examining survival time-to-event data.14 The three survival analysis techniques reviewed and considered were parametric modelling, semi-parametric modelling and non-parametric analysis. The study adopted the non-parametric technique for analysing censored data,14 with merits in trend detection, robustness and partially known (right-censored data where the event of interest (eg, death, failure) has not yet occurred for some subjects at the time of analysis).25 We use one of the most common non-parametric methods in survival analysis, Kaplan-Meier survival estimates, to assess the survival function and pattern of U5M.17 25 26 A log-rank test was used to assess for differences in survival between comparison groups using Kaplan-Meier curves. A survival curve is presented graphically based on the Kaplan-Meier survival estimates that plot the probability of survival over time. As a child grows in age, his/her conditional survival probability increases.6 In order to estimate measures such as median survival time, Kaplan-Meier summarises the data in a useful manner.

In order to model survival data, two related functions are used: a survivor function and a hazard function.25 Assume that T represents the overall survival time of subjects in a population, and that t represents the realisation of T. We can express the cumulative distribution function of T as follows:

(1) F(t)=P(T§lt;t)

In this context, F(t) is the probability density function that represents a child’s survival time. Here, ‘t’ stands for the actual survival duration, while ‘T’ symbolises the random variable connected to that survival time.

Using the distribution function of T and the survival function S(t), we can determine the proportion of children who have lived for more than t days since their birth as follows:

(2) F(t)=P(T§gt;t)=∫0tf(u)du

(3) S(t)=P(T≥t)=1−F(t)

The hazard function, symbolised as h(t), is an indicator of the probability of an individual’s death at a specific time point, provided that the individual has survived until that moment. Essentially, this function reveals the immediate death rate for a person who has lived up to a particular time, t:

(4) h(t)=limΔ→0P(t §lt; T ≤t + Δt ∖T §gt; t)Δt=f(t)S(t)

In this context, h(t) signifies the hazard function, T represents the time of survival, S(t) is the function for survival and ∆ denotes the immediate shift or change.14

The Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was used to determine the influence of different covariates on the survival periods of individuals via the hazard function. This technique presents an HR that aids in comparing the survival durations among various population groups. In this model, it is important to note that at the time of the survey, an individual’s survival status may remain unknown, a concept known as right-censoring. Furthermore, the exponential linear regression element within the model helps interpret the impact of explanatory variables on HR. The formulation of the Cox hazard was as such:

(5) h(t)=h0(t)exp.(b1X1+b2X2+b3X3+⋯bkXk)

In this context, X1 through Xk stand for k different explanatory variables, while h0(t) symbolises the baseline hazard at a specific point in time, t. This baseline hazard signifies the risk for someone whose values for all explanatory variables are zero. To simplify equation (5), we divide both sides by h0(t) and then apply logarithm operation as shown here:

(6) ln⁡h(t)h0(t)=b1X1+b2X2+b3X3+⋯+bkXk

Where ln⁡h(t)h0(t) is the HR. The coefficients b1 to bk are estimated by the Cox regression.24

In an attempt to understand the effect of different predictor variables on the mortality of children under the age of 5, four distinct models were integrated into the Cox proportional hazards model for analysis. Before running the analysis, we confirmed the Cox proportional hazards model assumptions, including hazard proportionality, independence of survival times and non-informative censoring. We also evaluated multicollinearity among predictors using variance inflation factors, ensuring all values were below the acceptable threshold of 5, thus confirming the absence of problematic multicollinearity in our model. Model 1 solely included demographic factors as predictor variables. Model 2 exclusively incorporated socioeconomic variables. Model 3 was limited to geographical variables. Finally, model 4, often referred to as the final adjusted model and visually depicted using a forest plot, combined all variables previously included in models 1, 2 and 3 for comprehensive analysis. The association measurements were articulated in terms of HRs, supported by 95% CIs.17 All evaluations were weighted to guarantee the accurate representation of the survey sample. The results were deemed statistically significant if the p value was less than 0.05. The statistical software used to carry out these analyses were SAS V.9.4 (SAS Institute, NC, Cary, USA) and R V.4.0.2.27

Results

The research findings indicated that child sex, maternal age at first sexual experience, maternal age at childbirth and ethnicity were significantly associated with child death rates, as shown in table 1. More males (9.4%) than females (7.9%) were found in the deceased group, suggesting a higher mortality risk in male children (p<0.001). A higher proportion of child deaths occurred among those whose mothers had their first sexual experience at a younger age (8–15 years, 10.4%; p<0.001). Furthermore, child death rates were higher among mothers younger at childbirth (12–19 years, 9.3%; p<0.001). There was also a significant association between death rate and ethnicity (p=0.004), although no clear trend was observed. Nearly all the individuals in the study were married, and 99% were of Muslim faith, showing no significant impact on death rates (p=0.77 and p=0.46, respectively). The weighted U5MR was 52 per 1000 live births.

The results further depicted a significant association between maternal and paternal education, wealth index, residence and region with the rate of survival and death in a population of 29 565 individuals. It reveals that a higher percentage of individuals who are alive have higher education (95.5% maternal, 93.0% paternal), belong to a wealthier index (richer 92.7%, richest 94.1%) and reside in urban areas (92.4%). Conversely, those who died had a higher rate of no education (9.6% maternal, 9.3% paternal), belonged to the poorest wealth index (9.6%) and were predominantly from rural areas (9.5%). The results also showed regional variations, suggesting geographical disparities in survival and mortality rates. Notably, the p values were <0.001 for each characteristic, indicating statistically significant differences. In contrast, no significant differences in death rates based on marital status or religion were noted.

Table 2 shows the results of the Cox proportional hazard regression model. The model investigates the relationship between survival time and demographic predictors and presents both univariate and multivariable analyses; meaning that it looks at the effect of each predictor variable individually and collectively. There was a statistically significant higher risk of U5M in children born to mothers who had their first sexual intercourse at age 8–15 (HR=1.27, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.59, p=0.038), compared with those who had it at 16–19 years after controlling for other demographic factors in the multivariable analysis. Jola/Sarahule/Others had a lower risk of U5M (HR=0.73, 95% CI 0.64 to 0.98, p=0.039) compared with Fula/Tukulur/Lorobo after controlling for other demographic factors. Regarding socioeconomic predictors, for fathers with secondary level of education, in the multivariable HR was 22% (HR=1.78, 95% CI 0.64 to 0.95, p=0.012) lower risk of U5M than those with higher education. In terms of region, compared with Banjul, all other regions had an HR of more than 1, indicating an increased risk of U5MRs. Regions such as Janjanbureh (HR=1.97, 95% CI 1.09 to 3.54, p=0.024), Kerewan (HR=2.06, 95% CI 1.16 to 3.66, p=0.014), Kuntaur (HR=2.28, 95% CI 127 to 4.12, p=0.006) and Mansakonko (HR=2.02, 95% CI 1.22 to 3.69, p=0.021) had higher risk of U5M compared with those from Banjul after controlling for residence in the multivariable models.

Figure 1 presents the results of the Cox proportional hazards regression of the final (full/adjusted) model. It assesses the influence of different factors (demographic, socioeconomic and geographical characteristics) on the survival time of children under five. With regards to paternal education, when compared with the reference group (higher education), fathers with secondary education had a lower hazard of their children experiencing U5M (HR=0.64; 95% CI 0.42 to 0.97). In terms of region, when compared with Banjul, all other regions had higher risks of U5M, with Janjanbureh (HR=1.93; 95% CI 1.02 to 3.63), Kuntaur (HR=2.13; 95% CI 1.13 to 4.03) and Mansakonko (HR=1.96; 95% CI 1.03 to 3.74) after adjusting for confounders.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that the U5MR is high in Gambia, especially in rural areas. Demographic, socioeconomic and regional factors that contribute to the occurrence of these deaths can be addressed to some extent to prevent their occurrence.15 Our study examines the demographic, socioeconomic and regional survival differentials of the under five in Gambia.

A Kaplan-Meier survival analysis demonstrated that most mortality incidents among children under 5 years of age tend to occur within the fourth and fifth year after birth. It was observed from our study that in the first 60 months, there are significant differences in U5MRs between children from the poorest households and those from the richest, or between rural and urban areas. However, as children grow older, these survival probabilities become slightly different across different groups. This could imply that certain early-life interventions or conditions in poorer or rural households have a more significant impact on child survival in the 3 years, but their effect diminishes as children grow older. Similarly, reports from UNICEF highlight an increase trend in U5MRs compared with any other growth period of the child.4 5 28 This corroborates the findings of Lartey and his team, which indicate that a child’s likelihood of survival increases with age and is also significantly influenced by their family’s wealth status; lower for the poorest and higher for the richest.6 Based on these findings, strategies aimed at reducing U5M should prioritise the entire children under 60 months of age, considering their vulnerable immune systems. These interventions should be designed to shield them from persistent environmental hazards to their health, such as malaria, unclean water and inadequate sanitation.29

The research indicates that regional differences significantly influence U5M, with particular emphasis on regions within Gambia’s inland territories, such as Basse, Janjanbureh and Kuntaur. These regions demonstrate a higher risk than Banjul, the country’s capital. This disparity suggests that access to referral hospitals across provinces is limited especially when it comes to handling a large volume of complex maternal and labour cases, which has led to a surge in recorded U5M cases in rural areas of The Gambia. This centralisation of specialised healthcare services in urban areas as compared with rural areas, along with the management of complicated maternal and delivery cases in rural general hospitals, contributes to the recorded high mortality rates for under-fives. Subsequently, the uneven distribution of healthcare resources and infrastructure throughout the country’s regions exacerbates the situation.8 10 30

The findings from the Cox proportional hazards regression models suggest an inverse link between a father’s level of education and the mortality rates of children under 5 years of age; a rise in paternal education corresponds to a decrease in U5M, and vice versa. This observation aligns with the belief that the likelihood of a child’s survival is enhanced by higher levels of parental education.26 Contrary to what one might expect, the level of education of the mother did not present a significant statistical impact, even though previous research has shown that the mother’s education generally exerts a greater influence on child mortality than the father’s education and numerous other socioeconomic aspects.63134 A potential reason for this could be the high rates of illiteracy or elementary-level education among mothers in rural Gambia due to practices such as early marriage (<16 years), in combination with the cultural norm that assigns husbands the roles of main decision-makers and breadwinners, particularly in these areas.12 13 30 35 The non-significant observed in the model could be due to potential social desirability bias and recall bias which could lead to differential reporting of child death owing to the cross-sectional nature of this study design. It is also reflective of the complex interplay of factors that might be associational but not main predictors of U5M in the context of Gambia. Thus, the lack of significance does not equate to a lack of importance in real-world scenarios. Many factors contribute to U5M, including healthcare access, sanitation and nutrition, which were not included in our model.

According to the Cox model in the study, the household wealth index shows that the rich have a lower risk of U5M than the poor. A study in Ethiopia affirmed that the risk of child mortality was the highest among the poor.15 Targeted intervention is required to close the gap between the rich and the poor. The study also showed that younger mothers had increased risks of U5M, especially in rural settings and parents with no formal education. This is consistent with a study conducted in Ethiopia, which reported that having a child at a young age increased the chance of U5M.36 A possible explanation for this is the prevalence of early marriages in rural Gambia. Consequently, since education is a crucial component of child survival, irrespective of region, formal education sensitisation in rural Gambia would make a significant contribution to reducing childhood mortality.8 13 37

Strengths and limitations

The robustness of this study is credited to the utilisation of Kaplan-Meier survival estimates and the Cox proportional hazards model for evaluating time-to-event data and addressing the issue of censored observations. However, it is essential to note that this study had certain constraints. To begin with, even though DHS is well known for its reliability in providing data on child mortality, we recognise that there could be potential difficulties with data collection. This may arise from inaccuracies in reporting the child’s age or the age at which they passed away in months. Second, our research centred on U5M over the past 5 years, using the household wealth index created for the survey year as a socioeconomic status indicator. However, changes in household wealth generally transpire over a long period, which means that current wealth index measurements can effectively represent past values. However, ideally, the current measure of both dependent and independent variables would have been perfect for our analysis. Finally, our research is based on retrospective cross-sectional data, which means that we could not establish temporality or chronological relationships between variables, thereby hindering causal inference.

Conclusion

The research highlights considerable differences based on demographics, socioeconomic conditions and geography in the mortality rates among children under 5 years old in The Gambia. This underscores the urgent need for specific strategies targeting the most at-risk areas and socioeconomic groups to improve child survival outcomes. The results also suggest that region, paternal education and household wealth index are significantly associated with U5M in Gambia. Such findings could be helpful in targeting interventions to increase access to healthcare to reduce U5M, particularly in regions with higher hazards. This suggests that educational attainment, socioeconomic status and place of residence considerably impact survival rates, indicating potential areas of intervention to improve outcomes. Furthermore, improved formal education, especially in rural Gambia, is essential for reducing U5M in the country, since education is a key factor in child survival, regardless of the area.
