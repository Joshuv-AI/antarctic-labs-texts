Smoking and its Role in Oral Cancer
Authors: Prabhu Manickam Natarajan, Bhuminathan Swamikannu, Leela Bhavani, Bindu Kamaraj, Loganathan Kamaraj, Ramachandran Veerakumar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTOral cancer remains a significant public health concern globally, with smoking identified as a primary risk factor. This abstract reviews the role of smoking in the etiology of oral cancer, emphasizing its contribution to carcinogenesis through the induction of genetic mutations, oxidative stress, and inflammation. Tobacco smoke contains carcinogenic compounds such as polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, nitrosamines, and heavy metals, which are implicated in the transformation of oral mucosal cells. Epidemiological studies consistently demonstrate a dose–response relationship between smoking intensity and oral cancer risk. Despite advances in prevention and treatment, smoking cessation remains a critical component in reducing oral cancer incidence. This review underscores the importance of public health initiatives and targeted interventions aimed at mitigating smoking-related oral cancer risk.

INTRODUCTION

Smoking has long been associated with a wide range of health problems, but its role in the development of oral cancer is particularly concerning. Oral cancer, which includes cancers of the mouth, lips, tongue, cheeks, and throat, is a serious and potentially life-threatening condition. The relationship between smoking and oral cancer is well-documented, with smoking being one of the leading causes of this disease.[1]

Smoking is a major public health issue with profound implications for overall health, including a well-established link to oral cancer. This form of cancer, which affects the tissues of the mouth, including the lips, gums, tongue, and the roof and floor of the mouth, has been strongly associated with tobacco use. The carcinogens present in tobacco smoke, such as benzene, formaldehyde, and nitrosamines, are known to damage the DNA in oral cells, leading to mutations that can result in cancer. In addition, smoking contributes to a range of other adverse effects, such as chronic inflammation and compromised immune response, which further exacerbate the risk of cancer development.[23] Understanding the relationship between smoking and oral cancer is crucial for prevention and treatment strategies, highlighting the importance of reducing tobacco use to mitigate this serious health threat.

Link between smoking and oral cancer

Smoking tobacco exposes the mouth and throat to carcinogenic chemicals that can damage the DNA in cells, leading to mutations that cause cancer. Cigarettes contain over 7,000 chemicals, many of which are toxic and at least 70 are known to cause cancer. The act of inhaling smoke means that these harmful substances come into direct contact with the tissues of the mouth and throat, increasing the risk of developing oral cancer.[4]

Studies have shown that smokers are significantly more likely to develop oral cancer than nonsmokers. According to the American Cancer Society, people who smoke are six times more likely to develop oral cancer than those who do not. The risk increases with the number of cigarettes smoked per day and the duration of smoking over a lifetime. Heavy smokers, defined as those who smoke more than 40 cigarettes a day, are at the highest risk.[5]

How smoking contributes to oral cancer

The process by which smoking leads to oral cancer is complex and multifaceted. Here are some of the key ways smoking contributes to the development of this disease[6]:

Direct Exposure to Carcinogens: The carcinogens in tobacco smoke directly damage the cells in the mouth and throat. Repeated exposure causes mutations in the DNA of these cells, which can eventually lead to the formation of cancerous tumors.Weakened Immune System: Smoking weakens the immune system, making it harder for the body to fight off infections and diseases, including cancer. A weakened immune system also reduces the body’s ability to repair damaged DNA, increasing the risk of cancerous changes in cells.[7]Inflammation and Cell Damage: Smoking causes chronic inflammation in the tissues of the mouth and throat, leading to the breakdown of healthy cells and the promotion of cancerous growth. The constant irritation from smoke can cause cells to multiply rapidly in an attempt to repair the damage, increasing the likelihood of mutations and the development of cancer.[8]Synergistic Effect with Alcohol: The risk of oral cancer is even higher for individuals who both smoke and consume alcohol. Alcohol acts as a solvent, enhancing the absorption of carcinogens in tobacco smoke into the tissues of the mouth and throat. This synergistic effect greatly amplifies the risk of developing oral cancer.

Impact of smoking cessation on oral cancer risk

The good news is that quitting smoking can significantly reduce the risk of developing oral cancer. The body begins to heal as soon as a person stops smoking, and over time, the risk of cancer decreases. Within 5 years of quitting, the risk of developing oral cancer is reduced by half compared to that of a current smoker. After 10 to 15 years, the risk approaches that of a nonsmoker.

Smoking cessation not only lowers the risk of oral cancer but also improves overall oral health. Former smokers are less likely to develop gum disease, tooth loss, and other oral health problems. They also experience better outcomes if they are diagnosed with oral cancer, as nonsmokers tend to respond better to treatment.[7]

Smoking cessation plays a crucial role in reducing the risk of oral cancer, a disease closely linked to the use of tobacco products. Smoking introduces carcinogenic chemicals into the body, which damage the cells lining the mouth, leading to mutations that can result in cancerous growths. Quitting smoking significantly decreases the likelihood of developing oral cancer, as it allows the body to begin repairing the damage caused by tobacco. Even for those who have smoked for many years, cessation can dramatically lower their risk over time. Furthermore, quitting smoking also enhances the effectiveness of treatments for those already diagnosed with oral cancer, improving overall survival rates and quality of life. Thus, smoking cessation is a vital preventive measure and an essential component of oral cancer management and recovery.[9]

CONCLUSIONS

Smoking is a major risk factor for oral cancer, contributing to the development of the disease through direct exposure to carcinogens, immune system suppression, chronic inflammation, and a synergistic effect with alcohol. However, quitting smoking can dramatically reduce the risk of oral cancer and improve overall health outcomes. Public health efforts aimed at reducing smoking rates, along with education on the risks of tobacco use, are crucial in preventing oral cancer and saving lives. The message is clear: to protect your oral health and reduce your risk of cancer, it is never too late to quit smoking.
