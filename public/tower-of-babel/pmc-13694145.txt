Artificial Intelligence in Medical Imaging: A Review of Radiodiagnosis in Indian Perspectives
Authors: Emily Das, Rasel Mondal, Ashim Dhor, Tanmay Basu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Artificial intelligence (AI) has become the most transformative development in radiology since digital imaging, with deep learning algorithms demonstrating validated—and in controlled settings superior—performance across pulmonary nodule detection, breast cancer screening, tuberculosis (TB) triage, acute stroke identification, and glioma segmentation. Over 950 AI/machine learning–enabled medical devices have received clearance from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration as of 2024, ∼75% within radiology, yet a consequential knowledge gap persists: fewer than 30% of practising radiologists can critically evaluate an AI study and fewer than 20% have received formal AI training. This review addresses that gap by providing a clinically accessible account of core AI concepts, major neural network architectures—including convolutional neural networks, U-Net, generative adversarial networks, vision transformers, and foundation models—the complete model development and validation workflow, and validated clinical applications across all major imaging modalities. We give dedicated attention to the Indian context, where an acute radiologist deficit of 1:50,000 against the World Health Organization-recommended ratio of 1:10,000, a 26% share of the global TB burden, and heterogeneous imaging infrastructure make AI both urgently necessary and uniquely demanding of local validation; we discuss the Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission, federated learning, and the domestic ecosystem of Qure.ai, Niramai, and Sigtuple as structural enablers, as well as algorithmic bias, explainability, and regulatory frameworks. We conclude that AI literacy is no longer optional: the radiologist's role is not threatened by AI but transformed by it—from film reader to AI-augmented clinical imaging physician capable of extending world-class diagnostic care to underserved populations across India and globally.

Introduction

Radiology has always been a discipline defined by its willingness to embrace transformative technology. 1 2 3 From Wilhelm Röntgen's serendipitous discovery of X-rays in 1895 to the successive development of computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), picture archiving and communication systems, and Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine, each technological revolution has reshaped the specialty while expanding its clinical reach. 1 The contemporary wave of artificial intelligence (AI) represents not a departure from this trajectory but its most sophisticated expression—one that, unlike prior hardware-driven innovations, strikes directly at the cognitive core of the specialty: pattern recognition, probabilistic reasoning, and the synthesis of complex visual and clinical information. 2 3 4

The AI renaissance in medical imaging is propelled by three convergent forces: the exponential accumulation of digitized imaging data, advances in graphics processing unit, and tensor processing unit computing hardware that made large-scale neural network training tractable, and deep learning (DL) algorithms—particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs) that learn complex hierarchical representations directly from raw image data without the need for manual feature engineering. 8 9 The pivotal demonstration came at the ImageNet 2012 Large Scale Visual Recognition Challenge, 10 where AlexNet reduced the top-5 classification error from 26 to 15.3%, 11 proving for the first time that data-driven DL could decisively outperform carefully hand-engineered computer vision systems. The medical translation of this insight followed rapidly: CheXNet (2017) surpassed the mean performance of four attending radiologists in pneumonia detection from chest X-rays 12 ; Ardila et al (2019) achieved an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.944 for lung cancer detection on low-dose CT, outperforming six radiologists on single-scan interpretation 13 ; and McKinney et al (2020) reduced mammography false positives by 5.7% and false negatives by 9.4% relative to the average of six radiologists, with performance noninferior to double reading. 14

India constitutes perhaps the most compelling case study for AI in radiology anywhere in the world. The rapid expansion of AI-enabled medical devices globally, with radiology accounting for the majority of regulatory clearances, underscores the increasing integration of AI into routine imaging practice. 5 6 For India, this rapid adoption presents both an opportunity to address workforce shortages and a need to ensure that deployed systems are appropriately validated for local populations and clinical settings. With ∼25,000 to 30,000 radiologists serving a population of 1.44 billion—a ratio of roughly 1:50,000, compared with the World Health Organization (WHO)-recommended ratio of 1:10,000—with 70 to 80% of that workforce concentrated in major urban centres, 15 and with 65% of the population living in rural areas, the workforce deficit is both acute and geographically skewed. India carries 26% of the global tuberculosis (TB) burden (more than 2.1 million new cases annually), the world's second-largest cancer burden, and high rates of cardiovascular disease—all conditions with major imaging components. 16 These structural realities are compounded by a documented knowledge gap: fewer than 30% of practising radiologists can critically evaluate an AI study and fewer than 20% have received formal AI training. 7 We believe that AI literacy is no longer optional—it is as fundamental to modern radiology practice as anatomy, medical physics, and pharmacology.

In this review, our goal is to equip practicing radiologists, residents, and trainees with the conceptual framework they need to be intelligent users—rather than passive consumers—of AI in medical imaging. We have written this article with deliberate attention to clinical relevance: every architectural concept we describe is anchored to a real imaging application, every performance metric is explained in terms of its clinical consequences, and every cautionary note about AI failure is illustrated with documented cases from the literature.

Search Strategy

We searched PubMed, Scopus, and IEEE Xplore for articles published between January 2017 and March 2025 using the following search terms: “artificial intelligence,” “deep learning,” “machine learning,” “medical imaging,” “radiology,” “convolutional neural network,” “Vision Transformer,” “foundation model,” “large language model,” “radiomics,” “federated learning,” “algorithmic bias,” “explainability,” “India,” and “tuberculosis.” Terms were combined using Boolean operators (AND, OR) as appropriate. We included peer-reviewed original research articles, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, regulatory documents, and clinical trial reports that reported validated AI performance in clinical radiology with quantitative outcomes. We excluded conference abstracts, editorials, opinion pieces, and studies that lacked external validation or quantitative performance metrics. Landmark studies published before 2017 (e.g., AlexNet, U-Net, generative adversarial network [GAN]) were included where foundational to the review. Snowball citation searching was applied to identify additional relevant references from the bibliographies of included papers. This is a narrative review; no formal Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses flow diagram is presented, consistent with the journal's scope for review articles.

Artificial Intelligence, Machine Learning, and Deep Learning

The three terms most commonly encountered in clinical AI discussions are nested concepts, not synonyms.

AI is the broadest concept: any computational system designed to mimic human cognitive functions such as perception, reasoning, and decision-making. 4 17 Early AI systems were rule-based expert systems—brittle, requiring exhaustive manual specification of every possible scenario, and failing predictably outside those narrow conditions.

Machine learning ( ML ) introduced the paradigm shift from explicit programming to learning from data. Classical algorithms—support vector machines (SVMs), random forests, logistic regression—dominated computational medicine through the early 2010s but required feature engineering : human experts had to manually construct task-relevant numerical descriptors before any learning could occur. 18 This bottleneck fundamentally limited the complexity of problems these methods could address.

DL resolves this bottleneck through multilayered neural networks that discover feature hierarchies automatically from raw data—edges and textures in early layers, anatomical shapes in intermediate layers, and complex pathological patterns such as nodule speculation or ground-glass opacity in deep layers. 8 9 This automatic representation learning, combined with the ability to leverage massive datasets, is DL's decisive advantage over classical ML in nearly every imaging task.

Five learning paradigms underpin radiology AI: supervised learning (labeled input–output pairs; underpins most deployed clinical tools 12 ); unsupervised learning (structure discovery without annotation; useful for anomaly detection 19 ); semisupervised learning (small labeled set combined with large unlabeled pool; critical given the high cost of expert annotation in medical imaging); self-supervised learning (no human labels required; the basis of foundation models such as MedSAM 20 21 ); and reinforcement learning (reward-signal training; applied to landmark detection and acquisition protocol optimization 22 ).

CNNs remain the workhorse of imaging AI, exploiting local connectivity, weight sharing, and translational equivariance to extract spatial features efficiently. 23 The architectural lineage—AlexNet, 11 VGGNet, 24 ResNet (residual skip connections enabling training of 50–152-layer networks 25 ), and DenseNet (layer-to-layer connections for parameter-efficient feature reuse 26 )—defines modern radiology AI, with ResNet underpinning CheXNet and numerous Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-cleared tools.

For segmentation, U-Net 27 is the gold standard. Its encoder-decoder design with skip connections elegantly resolves the tension between localization and context, enabling accurate boundary delineation of organs, tumors, and lesions with modest training data. Extensions—V-Net (3D volumes), nnU-Net (self-configuring), Attention U-Net, and TransUNet—dominate radiology segmentation benchmarks.

GANs pit a generator against a discriminator in a min-max game, driving synthesis of realistic images. 28 Clinical applications include data augmentation for scarce pathological cases, MR-to-CT synthesis for radiotherapy planning, image super-resolution, and ultra-low-dose CT denoising. 29

Vision transformers ( ViTs ) divide images into 16 × 16 patch tokens processed simultaneously via multihead self-attention, 30 31 capturing global anatomical context from the first layer rather than requiring many stacked convolutions. The Swin Transformer adds hierarchical windowed attention for high-resolution medical images. ViTs excel at multiorgan segmentation, whole-body CT analysis, and cross-modal alignment. 20

Large language models ( LLMs ) and foundation models represent the most ambitious frontier. GPT-4V, Med-PaLM 2, CheXagent, and LLaVA-Med demonstrate near-radiologist quality in structured report generation 32 33 ; MedSAM segments anatomical structures across 10 modalities from 1.57 million image-mask pairs. 21 Three critical, well-documented failure modes demand awareness: hallucination (fluent but factually wrong outputs—Lee et al documented fabricated drug interactions and clinical guidelines in GPT-4 medical queries 32 ), calibration failure (spurious confidence in incorrect outputs), and temporal knowledge cutoff (unawareness of posttraining evidence, necessitating retrieval-augmented generation 33 ). All LLM outputs require robust human oversight before clinical deployment.

The AI Model Development Workflow

Data, Ground Truth, and Representativeness

Data quality, quantity, and representativeness determine AI performance at least as much as architectural choices—and often more. 34 35 Ground truth derives from one of three sources, each with distinct trade-offs 36 : radiological labels (free-text report mining; scalable but susceptible to reporting conventions and retrospective bias); structured reannotation by expert readers (research-grade; interannotator agreement—Cohen's kappa or intraclass correlation coefficient—must always be reported); and histopathological ground truth (most reliable for oncology but introduces verification bias, as biopsied cases differ systematically from the full clinical population).

Models trained at a single institution reliably exhibit degraded performance under distribution shift on different scanners, protocols, or patient demographics—the most common documented cause of real-world AI failure. 15 37 Transfer learning, data augmentation, and GAN-based synthesis can partially compensate but cannot fully substitute for genuine clinical diversity. 23 29 Federated learning (FL)—sharing model gradients rather than patient images—offers a privacy-preserving path to multi-institutional collaboration well-suited to India's fragmented data landscape and the Digital Personal Data Protection Act (DPDP Act) 2023. 38 39

Validation Hierarchy

The validation framework is more important to clinical credibility than peak reported performance. Internal validation (held-out test from the same institution) is the minimum necessary step but susceptible to dataset-level overfitting. External validation on independent, demographically distinct cohorts is essential; most published AI studies show 10 to 30 percentage-point AUC degradation at this step 35 36 and a systematic review by Liu et al found that in adequately matched comparisons, AI performance was broadly equivalent to that of clinicians, not superior. 35 Prospective RCTs represent the gold standard; the Mammography Screening with Artificial Intelligence (MASAI) trial 42 and SCREEN-TB trial 43 are current exemplars. Studies without external validation should be regarded with skepticism.

Clinical Applications across Imaging Modalities

Chest Radiography and Tuberculosis

Chest X-ray (CXR) is the highest-volume AI application globally, anchored by the National Institutes of Health (NIH) ChestX-ray14 (112,120 images) 44 and Stanford CheXpert (224,316 images). 45 CheXNet (2017) reported AUC 0.769 for pneumonia detection, surpassing four attending radiologists (mean AUC 0.693). 12 AI triage tools now automatically flag time-critical findings—pneumothorax, large effusion, mediastinal shift, cardiomegaly—for priority work list review. 23 For TB—India's most critical AI application, given >2.1 million new cases annually—qXR (Qure.ai) achieves sensitivity 88 to 96% and specificity 75 to 90% across validation cohorts, comparable to experienced radiologists. 43 WHO formally endorsed AI-powered CXR analysis as an alternative to human interpretation for TB screening in individuals aged 15 years and older in 2021. 46

Computed Tomography

For lung nodule detection, Ardila et al (2019) achieved AUC 0.944 on National Lung Screening Trial data, outperforming six radiologists and showing particular superiority on subsolid nodules. 13 For acute stroke, AI systems for intracranial hemorrhage and large-vessel occlusion detection achieve sensitivity >97%, with a mean processing time of 7 seconds versus a 52-minute median radiologist report time 47 48 —critical given that 1.9 million neurons die per minute of untreated ischaemia. 47 For Indian hospitals with limited after-hours coverage, stroke AI is a high-impact, immediately deployable application. Abdominal organ segmentation achieves Dice similarity coefficient (DSC) of 0.93 to 0.97 for the liver and kidneys. 49

MRI and Mammography

AI PI-RADS systems for prostate cancer on mpMRI achieve pooled sensitivity of 0.85 and specificity of 0.79 for clinically significant cancer (Gleason ≥3 + 4), comparable to subspecialty uroradiologists. 50 BraTS glioma segmentation achieves DSC of 0.88 to 0.92 for whole-tumor volume, enabling automated Response Assessment in Neuro-Oncology response assessment. 50 The MASAI RCT (Lång et al, 2023; n = 80,033)—an interim clinical safety analysis—demonstrated AI-supported screening detected 20% more cancers with 44% radiologist workload reduction and no safety compromise. 42 McKinney et al (2020) demonstrated AI noninferiority to double reading in a UK cohort, 14 although Haibe-Kains et al raised important reproducibility concerns that the field has partially addressed. 51 Niramai's Thermalytix (thermal imaging combined with AI; CDSCO-approved) was designed for Indian women with dense breast tissue, extending screening access without ionizing radiation. 52

Ultrasound and Nuclear Medicine

Validated ultrasound AI tools exist for thyroid nodule ACR TI-RADS classification, POCUS guidance, fetal biometry, and liver fibrosis assessment. 53 In positron emission tomography/CT, DL reconstruction enables fourfold radiotracer dose reduction, and AI-based whole-body lesion segmentation computes total metabolic tumor volume as a validated prognostic biomarker in lymphoma and lung cancer. 49

Radiomics and Radiogenomics

Radiomics extracts quantitative features from segmented regions of interest—shape, first-order statistics, and texture (GLCM, GLRLM, NGTDM)—capturing tumor heterogeneity invisible to qualitative reporting. 54 55 Aerts et al (2014) demonstrated that CT radiomic features independently predict lung cancer prognosis. 56 Radiogenomics extends this to noninvasive virtual biopsy, with validated applications including EGFR mutation status in non–small cell lung cancer and MGMT methylation status in glioblastoma. 5 Adherence to IBSI standardization 57 and the radiomic quality score 54 should be required for any radiomics study submitted for peer review.

Limitations of Current Evidence

Despite this evidence base, several limitations constrain clinical translation. The majority of published AI studies rely on retrospective, single-institution datasets; external validation consistently demonstrates AUC degradation of 10 to 30 percentage points. 35 36 Prospective RCT evidence is currently limited to mammography screening 42 and TB triage. 43 Real-world performance in Indian public-sector settings—where scanner heterogeneity and variable image quality differ markedly from Western academic trial conditions—remains inadequately studied. 15 The generalization problem remains unsolved; domain adaptation, test-time augmentation, and continual learning with elastic weight consolidation 58 offer partial mitigation. Foundation models (MedSAM, Med-PaLM multimodal 20 21 ) represent the most promising path to robust generalization, though Indian-cohort validation is yet to be reported.

Bias, Ethics, and the Regulatory Landscape

Algorithmic Bias Taxonomy

Four distinct bias mechanisms require different mitigation strategies. Dataset bias : training populations unrepresentative of deployment populations—NIH ChestX-ray14 is single-center U.S. data; TB models trained on it may fail on young adults in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) who bear the greatest burden; mammography AI trained on Western women degrades for South Asian women with higher breast density. 15 59 Label bias : ground truth inherits annotator errors systematically—if radiologists consistently underreport a finding, the model learns to as well. Confounding bias (shortcut learning): models exploit correlates of disease rather than pathological features—demonstrated in dermatology (Winkler et al showed models learning dermoscopic rulers as a malignancy proxy, 60 a phenomenon replicated in radiology by Zech et al. 37 ). Algorithmic amplification : systematic underperformance on underrepresented subgroups widens health disparities at scale. 34 For Indian radiology, the absence of Indian patient data from most commercial AI training sets is a specific, well-documented, and actionable concern.

Ethics and Accountability

Three ethical dimensions require explicit attention. Informed consent : patients should know when AI is assisting diagnosis; institutional frameworks must reflect this. Medico-legal accountability : the reporting radiologist retains full clinical and legal responsibility for any AI-assisted diagnosis 61 —AI is a decision-support tool, not a decision-making agent. Data sovereignty : the DPDP Act 2023 raises unresolved concerns about cloud storage of Indian patient data outside India, 62 a dimension procurement teams must address explicitly.

Regulatory Landscape

United States : FDA regulates AI/ML SaMD via 510(k), De Novo, or premarket approval pathways; the Predetermined Change Control Plan (PCCP) framework permits prespecified model retraining without new submissions. 5 EU : MDR 2017/745 supplemented by the EU AI Act (2024)—the world's first comprehensive AI governance law—classifies diagnostic AI as “high-risk,” mandating conformity assessment, transparency, and human oversight. 63 India : CDSCO Medical Devices Rules 2017 classify AI diagnostic software as Class B or C; AI-specific guidance remains under development. 15 64 We recommend Indian procurement teams require: external validation on Indian patient cohorts, full training dataset demographic disclosure, and explicit documentation of intended use and known failure modes. Rigorous AI study reporting requires adherence to CLAIM (42-item checklist for AI imaging studies 65 ), TRIPOD + AI (prediction model reporting standards 66 ), or FUTURE-AI (30 guidelines spanning fairness, universality, traceability, usability, robustness, and explainability 67 ).

Overview of the AI Model in Indian Radiology

India's radiologist deficit (1:50,000 vs. WHO-recommended ratio of 1:10,000), geographic maldistribution (70–80% urban, serving 35% of the population), distinctive epidemiology (TB, dense-breast cancer, late-stage presentations, tropical infections overlapping with malignancy), and heterogeneous equipment infrastructure create both the most compelling AI use cases globally and the most stringent validation requirements. 15 AI tools validated on Western equipment empirically fail on older scanners common in Indian public facilities—this is a documented, not theoretical, risk. 15

ABDM provides the foundational digital infrastructure—Ayushman Bharat Health Account (ABHA) identifiers, the Health Information Exchange and Consent Manager (HIE-CM), and interoperable electronic health records—enabling federated AI research on nationally representative Indian imaging data while satisfying DPDP Act requirements. 62 FL , 38 sharing model gradients rather than patient images, is the appropriate privacy-preserving architecture; the FeTS study demonstrated federated glioma segmentation across 71 institutions on six continents, achieving performance noninferior to centralized training without sharing patient data. 68 We propose a federated AI consortium coordinated through IRIA and the National Informatics Centre under the ABDM framework as a realistic path to India-validated public-good AI models. The domestic ecosystem is already contributing. Qure.ai 's qXR—NTEP-deployed, FDA/CE/CDSCO-cleared, and WHO-endorsed for TB screening 43 —is the global exemplar of AI validated on and for an LMIC context. Niramai 's Thermalytix (thermal imaging combined with AI, CDSCO-approved) was designed for younger Indian women with dense breast tissue, extending screening access without ionizing radiation. 52 Sigtuple develops AI for hematology and pathology on Indian data. AI deployments at AIIMS centers and the NITI Aayog National AI Strategy 69 further demonstrate that India has the technical capability, clinical insight, and policy infrastructure to be a global contributor to equitable AI in health—not merely a consumer of Western tools.

Conclusion

AI augments radiologist accuracy, efficiency, and consistency while preserving the radiologist as the accountable decision-maker. Validated DL performance across lung cancer detection, 13 breast cancer screening, 14 42 TB triage, 43 and acute stroke 48 is well-established; the MASAI RCT's simultaneous 20% gain in cancer detection and 44% reduction in radiologist workload 42 exemplifies the complementary, not competitive, nature of this relationship. Alertness to documented risks—10- to 30-percentage-point AUC degradation at external validation, algorithmic bias, black-box opacity, and LLM hallucination—is equally mandatory. A model lacking external validation on Indian patients, undisclosed training demographics, or adequate explainability is not an acceptable clinical tool regardless of its internal AUC. AI literacy—critical evaluation of performance metrics, bias identification, and regulatory navigation—is the defining new competency for modern radiology practice. 61 70 Its integration into NBEMS residency curricula and IRIA continuing professional development is urgent. For India, the imperative is uniquely acute. A 1:50,000 radiologist-to-population ratio, a 26% share of the global TB burden, and geographically skewed workforce distribution make AI not a convenience but a necessity. 15 The domestic ecosystem—Qure.ai's NTEP-deployed qXR, Niramai's Thermalytix designed for Indian women, and Sigtuple's hematology AI—demonstrates that India is already a producer, not merely a consumer, of globally relevant health AI. 43 52 ABDM's digital infrastructure and FL offer a privacy-preserving path to nationally representative, India-validated models. 38 62 What remains is deliberate institutional commitment: mandating Indian-cohort validation in procurement, integrating AI governance into CDSCO frameworks, 64 and educating the next generation of radiologists as AI-literate clinical leaders capable of extending world-class diagnostic care to every district hospital and primary health center across the country.
