Role of estimated salt intake and nutritional status in patients undergoing hemodialysis: a longitudinal analysis
Authors: Takeo Ishii, Yasuhiro Hagiwara, Yutaka Matsuyama, Hiromichi Wakui, Kouichi Tamura, Yoshihiro Arimura, Ashio Yoshimura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn patients undergoing dialysis, increased salt intake is associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD) events, including a higher incidence of congestive heart failure. We calculated the estimated salt intake (eSALT) using a salt intake estimation formula based on the amount of sodium removed by dialysis and investigated its relationship with patients’ prognosis.MethodsWe included 6,180 patients undergoing dialysis who attended the Zenjinkai Outpatient Clinic. We used Watson’s body water and Ramdeen’s salt estimation equations, utilizing pre- and post-dialysis blood samples collected once a month at the beginning of the week, with a 2-day interval. The eSALT was measured repeatedly in all patients, 36 times in 3 years, starting on April 1, 2016. The observational outcomes included all-cause mortality and CVD events. Statistical analyses were performed using Cox proportional hazard analysis, cubic spline curves, and Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE). Baseline and previous time-dependent repeated-measured eSALT were used in the GEE for exposure factor. The outcomes included normalized protein catabolic rate (nPCR), Geriatric Nutritional Risk Index (GNRI), and body mass index (BMI), as well as percentage change in creatinine clearance rate, blood pressure, creatinine, albumin, and uric acid.ResultsSalt intake was 8.15 g/day over 3 years. The eSALT did not predict CVD events in adjusted Cox proportional hazard analysis. Spline curves showed an increased hazard ratio for heart failure with eSALT ≥ 6 g, but not for all-cause mortality. The GEE model indicated that eSALT was associated with nutritional indices, including the GNRI, %CGR, nPCR, and BMI, from longitudinal data.ConclusioneSALT can be used to estimate patients’ prognosis and nutritional status. Although eSALT > 6 g/day increases the risk of congestive heart failure, it improves nutritional status. Background

High salt intake is associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular events, and the World Health Organization dietary guidelines recommend a sodium intake of 4.5 g, corresponding to a lower salt intake [1]. A dose-dependent decrease in systolic blood pressure was observed when daily salt intake was reduced to ≤4.0 g/day in patients with hypertension [2]. However, studies evaluating the association between salt intake and cardiovascular events have reported inconsistent results. Many studies investigating the association between a single baseline salt measurement have reported a J-shaped association [3–7]. A study calculating sodium intake from spot urine samples reported a J-shaped mortality rate [8]. In Japan, the rate of decline in the estimated glomerular filtration rate was reportedly lower in individuals whose daily sodium intake was < 107.0 mEq/day and potassium intake was > 29.2 mEq/day [9]. Conversely, many randomized controlled trials have reported a reduction in all-cause mortality and cardiovascular disease (CVD) events with a salt intake of ≤6.0 g/day [7, 10, 11]. Salt distribution is divided into extracellular and intracellular in the interstitium with inflammation and muscle wasting [12, 13]. There have been reports of increased catabolism, including in skeletal muscle, during dialysis sessions [14]. However, the effects of sodium loading in end-stage renal disease and its subsequent removal during dialysis sessions remain poorly understood.

Instead of estimating salt intake from urinary collection [7, 15–19], we used estimated salt intake (eSALT), a formula for estimating salt intake based on the amount of sodium removed by dialysis in patients undergoing dialysis. Approximately 90% of ingested salt is excreted through urine in healthy individuals [20]. Given the necessity to excrete ingested sodium, 24-h sodium output in the urine is assumed to provide a robust estimate of approximately contemporaneous sodium intake and accordingly serves as a reference standard. As 95% of ingested sodium is removed via the urine, dialysis represents the sole route of urine elimination in anuric dialysis patients [21]. In patients undergoing dialysis, the removal of eSALT is comparable to salt excretion in individuals with normal kidney function. We thus assumed that eSALT would serve as a viable proxy for dietary behavior and nutritional phenotype, and not merely sodium exposure [22–24].

In this study, we aimed to calculate the eSALT using a formula derived from the amount of sodium removed during dialysis and to examine its association with patient outcomes. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the relationship between salt intake and outcomes in a longitudinal analysis of a large cohort of patients undergoing dialysis for >36 times over 3 years.

Methods

Participants

This retrospective cohort study used the laboratory and clinical data of 6,883 patients as of March 2016 who underwent hemodialysis at Yokohama Daiichi Hospital, Zenjinkai, and its affiliated dialysis clinics located in the Kanagawa and Tokyo metropolitan areas of Japan. Follow-up was performed for 3 years, from April 2016 to March 2019, with March 2016 as the baseline. Of the 6,883 patients initially considered, 111 were excluded owing to missing data on height and diabetes status, whereas 592 were excluded for having baseline eSALT scores outside the 1st and 99th percentiles.

Thus, the final cohort consisted of 6,180 patients. This research was conducted after approval by the Institutional Review Board of Yokohama City University (approval number B200700018) and the Local Ethics Committee of Zenjinkai Group (approval number 0001116288).

The eSALT levels in patients undergoing dialysis were measured at the start of each month using pre-dialysis and post-dialysis blood samples from sessions performed at 2-day intervals at the beginning of the week. This estimation was conducted using Watson’s body water estimation formula [25] and Ramdeen’s salt estimation formula [26], based on previous studies [27]. eSALT was calculated as follows:

eSALT (g/day) = ((TBW_post * (Na_pre - Na_post) + (BW_pre - BW_post) * Na_pre)) * 23 × 2.54/1,000)/2.75

Where TBW is Total body water, BW is Body weight, Male TBW = 2.447 - 0.09516 * age + 0.1074 * height + 0.3362 * weight, and Female TBW = −2.097 + 0.1069 * height + 0.2466 * weight

To convert from mEq/L to g/L, the value was multiplied by 23 (the atomic weight of Na) and divided by 1,000. To convert Na to NaCl, the result was further multiplied by 2.54 (the atomic weight of Na = 23, Cl = 35.5). To account for the 66-h interval between dialysis sessions—from the end of dialysis on Friday to the initiation of dialysis on Monday (equivalent to 2.75 days)—the estimated daily NaCl intake (grams) was divided by 2.75 to normalize for this interval.

The baseline eSALT was calculated as the average of the 3 months from January to March 2016 and classified into five groups (Table 1). In the subsequent observation period, eSALT was measured 36 times over 3 years, starting from April 1, 2016. The eSALT was measured repeatedly every month for all patients.

Patient profile: laboratory data, demographics, and complications

Monthly average blood pressure, creatinine (Cr), albumin (Alb), uric acid (UA), normalized protein catabolic rate (nPCR), Geriatric Nutritional Risk Index (GNRI), body mass index (BMI), and percentage change in Cr clearance rate (%CGR) were set as outcomes for continuous variables in the Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE). The formula is as follows:

BMI: Weight (kg)/(Height (m))2

%CGR (male): CGR z = 0.8+(BW pre ×Cr pre - BW post ×Cr post) ×(1-RCr)/(-ln(RCr)x BMI x 152), where RCr = Cr post/Cr pre.

%CGR (female): CGR = 0.8+(BW pre ×Cr pre - BW post ×Cr post) ×(1-RCr)/(-ln(RCr)x BMI x 172.7), where RCr = Cr post/Cr pre [28].

nPCR (g/kg/day): nPCR = 5:42 ×G/V + 0.17 [29]where G is urea generation rate, and V is total body water (Watson’s formula), G = (BUNnext pre-BUNpost)×(V/△ t). △t = Time from post-dialysis to next dialysis [30].

GNRI = (1.489 ×albumin (g/L)) + (41.7 ×(weight/WLo)) [31, 32].

Statistical analysis

Cox proportional hazard analysis

Analysis was adjusted for demographic and laboratory data, and fully adjusted analyses were performed using Cox proportional hazards analysis. All covariates in Table 1 and Additional Table 1 were used for the adjusted analysis [33, 34].

Restricted cubic spline curve

The SAS cubic spline curve program from the Yale School of Public Health [35] was used to plot the hazard ratio (HR) for the relationship between hard endpoints and eSALT, fully adjusted with confidence intervals by demographic data and laboratory data derived from dialysis data system. The covariates of the baseline data was provided in Table 1 and Additional Table 1.

To assess the eSALT response and its outcomes, correlations between eSALT and various time-dependent repeated measures of clinical data, along with a large number of covariates, were tested. Two types of GEE were employed [36–39]:

Baseline-categorized eSALT data, correlated with time-dependent clinical indicators as covariates.

Baseline-categorized eSALT data, correlated with time-dependent clinical indicators as covariates.

Previous month’s categorized eSALT, correlated with currently time-dependent clinical indicators.

Previous month’s categorized eSALT, correlated with currently time-dependent clinical indicators.

Both types of GEE analyses were performed, adjusting for demographic and laboratory data, and fully adjusted models were created. In both analyses, covariates included baseline complications and time-dependent clinical data from the previous month (Table 1, Additional Table 1). The outcomes included repeated monthly measurements over 3 years, such as blood pressure, Cr, Alb, BMI, nPCR, CGR%, UA, and GNRI. For each month, outcomes were defined as the current month’s measurements of these parameters.

The same covariates were used in the Cox proportional hazards models, with monthly averages applied (Table 1, Additional Table 1) [37, 40, 41]. Any covariate that overlapped with an outcome was excluded from the adjusted GEE analysis. Unadjusted analyses for all parameters are provided in the additional materials.

Hard endpoints, including all-cause mortality, were obtained from medical records, whereas CVD events were identified from insurance claims data, using International Classification of Diseases-10 codes for diseases of the circulatory system (I00–I99), along with the corresponding date and time of occurrence.

The same covariates were used in the adjusted analyses for Cox proportional hazards, restricted cubic spline curves, and GEE; however, GEE models incorporated time-dependent data from the previous month’s repeated measures to estimate association effects. Covariates strongly influenced by eSALT were excluded from all analyses. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 or p < 0.005.

All analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Results

eSALT calculation

The 3-year mean eSALT was 8.15 g/day (standard deviation: 3.27, minimum: 1.06 g/day, maximum: 18.89 g/day). The 1st percentile was 1.72 g/day, the 99th percentile was 16.91 g/day, and the interquartile range was 5.80–10.11 g/day. Histograms of salt intake over time are shown in Additional Fig. 1.

The mean and standard deviation of the baseline eSALT values in the five categories were as follows: category 1, 3.99 ± 0.98 g/day; category 2, 6.08 ± 0.42 g/day; category 3, 7.46 ± 0.40 g/day; category 4, 9.04 ± 0.53 g/day; and category 5, 11.86 ± 1.44 g/day (Table 1).

Cox proportional hazard model

Cox proportional hazard analysis for all-cause mortality showed an increase in the HR for all-cause mortality with increasing salt intake in the unadjusted analysis. However, in the adjusted analysis, the HR was 1.00 (0.99–1.02), showing no significant correlation. CVD events were significantly correlated with salt intake in the unadjusted analysis (HR: 1.02, 1.01–1.00) but not in the adjusted analysis. New-onset heart failure (HF) was positively correlated, with an HR of 1.04 (1.01–1.07) in the unadjusted analysis, but not in the adjusted analysis. The incidence of acute myocardial infarction (AMI) and stroke showed similar patterns (Table 2, Additional Table 2).

As the eSALT–CVD event relationship may be non-linear based on the results of the adjusted Cox proportional hazard analysis, a spline curve was plotted to examine HR values with continuous baseline eSALT data on the horizontal axis. The HR for all-cause mortality showed no correlation between eSALT and decreased or increased salt intake (Fig. 1a, Additional Figure 3a).

Spline curve analysis for new-onset HF demonstrated a J-shaped relationship in adjusted models, with the risk of HF increasing at eSALT ≥ 6.0 g/day and modestly elevated at eSALT < 6.0 g/day (Fig. 1c, Additional Figure 3c). GEE

For blood pressure, baseline categorical eSALT and repeated measures of systolic blood pressure showed that higher eSALT persistently increased systolic and diastolic blood pressure in unadjusted and adjusted models (Additional Figure 4a, b, e, f). In the short term, previous categorical eSALT and repeated measures of systolic blood pressure, the highest eSALT category (Category 5) showed significantly reduced systolic and diastolic blood pressures (Additional Figure 4c, d, g, h).

Concerning the serum Cr levels, baseline eSALT significantly increased Cr levels in unadjusted and adjusted models. Repeated measurements of eSALT in the previous month were significantly associated with elevated Cr in unadjusted and adjusted models (Additional Figure 5a–d).

Regarding serum Alb levels, the baseline eSALT significantly increased Alb levels in unadjusted and adjusted models. However, repeated measurements of eSALT in the previous month significantly increased Alb levels in unadjusted analyses, but no correlation was found in the adjusted analysis (Additional Figure 5e–h). Regarding serum UA levels, baseline eSALT significantly increased the UA levels in unadjusted analysis but not in the adjusted models. Repeated measurements of eSALT in the previous month showed a significant increase in the UA levels in unadjusted analysis, although the differences were small in the adjusted analysis (Additional Figure 5i–l).

Nutritional markers, including nPCR (Fig. 2a, b), CGR% (Fig. 2c, d), and GNRI (Fig. 2 e, f), and BMI (Fig. 2 g, h) were strongly correlated with baseline and repeatedly measured eSALT in adjusted models. Discussion

In this study, 6,180 outpatients were followed over 3 years to investigate the correlation between salt intake, CVD events, and clinical parameters. During the follow-up period, 1,209 patients died, and the mean daily salt intake was 8.15 g (Additional Fig. 1), indicating that daily salt intake was relatively well controlled. Unadjusted Kaplan–Meier curves showed that survival was longer in the high eSALT group than in the low eSALT group (Additional Fig. 2). We hypothesized that this unadjusted result might have been confounded by nutritional factors.

Cox proportional hazard analysis

Cox proportional hazard analysis of all-cause mortality at baseline revealed that the HR increased with increasing salt intake in the unadjusted analysis (Table 2), but this association was not statistically significant in the adjusted analysis. No unadjusted correlations were found for the occurrence of AMI or stroke (Table 2, Additional Table 2), suggesting that hard endpoints did not increase with higher salt intake in Cox models. To explore these relationships, cubic spline curves were used to estimate the risk of hard endpoints. The cubic spline curve for HF showed an increased risk for eSALT ≥ 6 g/day (Fig. 1 c), which could be attributed to a slightly increased risk of CVD events (Fig. 1 b).

To evaluate nutritional status, we used GEE to estimate the relationship between salt intake and nutritional parameters. History of increased salt intake was associated with prompt higher levels of the GNRI, nPCR, CGR, and BMI, suggesting that patients with higher salt consumption may consume more protein or calories (Fig. 2 a-h). As has previously been demonstrated by Yaprak et al., the risk of mortality in dialysis patients is determined by multiple factors, among which, it has been established that patients with a favorable nutritional status, as indicated by GNRI and BMI, have been reported to have a lower risk. In dialysis patients, an increased salt intake may be associated with greater food consumption and improved nutritional factors, and even if such increases in intake elevate the risk of cardiovascular events, it is sugested that improved nutrition could offset the the risk of mortality [23]. Mente et al. estimated salt intake from urinary sodium excretion in the general population and found that the risk of a composite endpoint increased with intake above 12 g, but not with intake below 12 g [42]. This differs from our study, where the lowest risk was associated with approximately 6 g of salt in patients undergoing dialysis. In our Cox proportional hazard model, the relationship between eSALT and hard endpoints did not appear to be linear in the adjusted analysis.

Spline curves

The eSALT and all-cause mortality appeared not to be related, this result is suspected to be confounded with nutritional status. For CVD events, HF, and AMI, the adjusted cubic spline curves showed an increasing trend in risk with eSALT ≥ 6 g/day (Fig. 1a–d). However, the increased risk of congestive HF in the adjusted analysis (Fig. 1c) suggested that higher salt intake, combined with other nutritional factors and water intake, increased the risk of HF during dialysis. In the study conducted by Yaprak et al., dialysis patients were divided into survival and non-survival groups, the latter of which contained a higher proportion of obese patients or those with higher weight gain, who also had higher salt intake, with an eSALT value > 6 g/day being found to be associated with increased heart failure. Furthermore, it has been reported that the risk of mortality is largely determined by multiple risk factor categories. Moreover, a high intake of salt has also been found to be associated with nutritional improvement, and has thus been suggested to counteract the increase in mortality due to heart failure [23]. These spline curve results are consistent with those of a previous Japanese study on eSALT and outcomes in a large cohort of patients undergoing hemodialysis, where higher salt intake correlated with increased nutrient intake and reduced mortality from malnutrition [27]. Accordingly, we used GEE to explore the association between eSALT and time-dependent nutritional covariates and to investigate the correlation effects of eSALT on nutritional status. However, Alataş et al. [22] have reported that dietary protein, beta carotene, vitamin C, vitamin E, and polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) constitute the dietary total antioxidant capacity (dTAC), the values of which have been established to show significant negative correlations with CRP, LDL-cholesterol, the lymphocyte/monocyte ratio, and the physical measurement BMI. These findings accordingly indicate that dietary patterns associated with strong antioxidant effects that increase dTAC may contribute to reducing oxidative stress and improve the prognosis of hemodialysis patients. Consequently, although an increase in salt intake may contribute to a higher risk of cardiovascular events, higher dietary intakes may include foods with antioxidant effects, which may mitigate and offset the heightened risks associated with a higher salt intake.

GEE analysis

eSALT was associated with increased blood pressure at baseline (Additional Figures 4a, b, e, f). However, in the short term, higher eSALT led to a decrease in systolic and diastolic blood pressure (Additional Figure 4c, d, g, h). This discrepancy is likely due to the effect of water removal during dialysis sessions, as salt intake from the previous month correlates with lower current blood pressure in the GEE analysis (Additional Figure 4c, d, g, h). Salt intake in the previous month likely reflects the amount of water removed during a dialysis session. The mechanism underlying this blood pressure decrease could be comparable to the action of diuretic antihypertensive drugs, which lower blood pressure by excreting sodium through urine.

However, long-term salt accumulation may contribute to an increase in blood pressure and a higher risk of CVD ultimately [43, 44]. Nutritional indices, including GNRI, nPCR, CGR%, and BMI—were elevated in the GEE analysis and associated with a history of higher eSALT (Fig. 2a–h), and these improvements were linked to better all-cause mortality outcomes. In their study, in which they conducted linear regression analysis, with cardiometabolic risk as the outcome and anthropometric measurements to predict cardiometabolic risk in hemodialysis patients, Arslan et al. found that among BMI, the fat tissue index, and the visceral adiposity index, the latter was more closely correlated with cardiometabolic risk than changes in BMI [24]. Consequently, whenever possible, body composition indices should be measured. Another important relationship between salt and nutritional factors is that excessive salt intake leads to an increase in urine volume as the kidneys excrete sodium. To prevent dehydration, the urea cycle is activated, primarily involving the consumption of skeletal muscle. This results in the accumulation of urea in the interstitial space, which maintains osmotic pressure and facilitates water reabsorption [45, 46]. However, this suggests the opposite: muscle mass is maintained without the need for skeletal muscle consumption if the urea cycle is not necessary, as sodium removal is facilitated by dialysis through electrical forces. The GEE results indicated that increased salt intake increases each of the nutritional indices (Fig. 2 a-h).

We estimated salt intake using pre- and post-dialysis blood samples, accounting for fluid removal, to assess prognosis in patients undergoing hemodialysis. This approach is similar to the estimation of salt intake through urine collection that removes sodium from urine, typically used in patients with normal kidney function. eSALT > 6 g/day increases the risk of cardiovascular events, particularly congestive HF, and is assumed to serve as a proxy for dietary behavior and nutritional phenotype, not merely salt exposure. However, this is consistent across patients undergoing hemodialysis and those with hypertension or chronic kidney disease. Therefore, early intervention for salt reduction is recommended for healthy individuals.

Limitations

The timing of blood collection, performed both before and after dialysis during the facilities’ routine monthly session, is a limitation of the study. According to Ramdeen et al.’s protocol [26], blood should be collected immediately after a dialysis session, and immediately before the subsequent dialysis session 3 days later. All our facilities conduct blood collection at the beginning of each month; consequently, obtaining the required post-dialysis samples from the prior month would not have been practical.

Moreover, the medical records that we had lacked causes of death. Since patients with infectious diseases or malnutrition were more likely to be included in the study, we speculated on the relationship between eSALT and cardiovascular-related mortality based on the frequency of cardiovascular events and further nutritional data.

The GEE analysis using time-dependent data was based on retrospective observational data rather than a prospective randomized controlled trial. Therefore, it is challenging to definitively confirm the relationship between hard endpoints and eSALT within the GEE framework. However, we assessed the relationship between eSALT and time-dependent nutritional markers; these results provide insight into the prognostic impact of eSALT on hard endpoints. Furthermore, eSALT, as an exposure factor, was characterized across five levels, and unlike propensity analysis, where exposure factors were framed in a binary fashion (i.e., either 1 or 0), making it difficult to adjust GEE for hard endpoints with propensity scores.

Finally, another limitation of the study is related to the eSALT calculations not being conducted for some visits where a negative value was recorded. It is likely that older patients undergoing dialysis had a lower dietary intake of salt, thereby not impacting the results. However, since these patients were only a small subset of the broader cohort, their impact on the broader trends is likely negligible.

Conclusions

in dialysis patients, a high intake of salt is associated with an elevated risk of congestive HF. However, a higher intake of salt combined with other nutrients was found to be conducive to an improvement in nutritional status. An eSALT intake of <6 g/day, combined with an adequate intake of other nutrients, may contribute to an improved prognosis. Given that this study was of a purely observational nature and that eSALT served as an exposure factor rather than an intervention, further studies integrating salt intake, diet quality, and body composition assessments are warranted.
