Dual-phase eutectic ceramics with improved hardness and toughness via nano-coherent high-entropy oxides
Authors: Xu Wang, Yujie Zhong, Huadong Li, Ye Yuan, Xiangyu Huang, Cui Zhou, Detian Wan, Yuan Tian, Xingyue Zhai, Jihong Zhu, Zied Moumni, Jian Lu, Weihong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Alumina-based eutectic ceramics exhibited high high-temperature strength but their intrinsic brittleness constrained broad structural applications. Here, we broke this limitation by introducing high-entropy rare-earth aluminate (REAlO3, RE = Gd0.25Eu0.25Nd0.25Sm0.25) into the Al2O3 matrix via directional solidification. The resulting dual-phase eutectics exhibited a unique architecture where single-crystalline Al2O3 was interlocked with bicrystalline high-entropy REAlO3, forming nanoscale coherent grain boundaries (~57.5 nm) and semi-coherent phase boundaries (lattice misfit <5.1%). The tailored microstructure and orientation relationship enabled a synergistic enhancement of Vickers hardness (19.4 GPa) and fracture toughness (5.5 MPa·m1/2), outperforming all reported binary alumina-based counterparts. The property synergy originated from a cascaded strengthening mechanism that spanned atomic-scale lattice distortion to nanoscale coherent interfaces, coupled with multi-mode toughening via crack deflection, bifurcation, and bridging. Our work establishes a high-entropy eutectic design strategy for engineering ceramics with exceptional mechanical performance under extreme conditions.

Introduction

Directionally solidified alumina-based eutectic ceramics, formed from eutectic liquid-phase solidification, resulting in a unique three-dimensional interlocking topological structure of two or three limited-solubility solid phases1–3. The liquid-solid transition during processing leads to a refined microstructure, reduced porosity, and the absence of amorphous phases at the interfaces4,5. These ceramics demonstrate an 8-fold increase in flexural strength and a 13-fold improvement in creep resistance compared to their sintered counterparts6. They exhibit an ultra-high temperature capability exceeding 1700 °C while maintaining a density below 5 g/cm3, along with microstructural stability and resistance to oxidation and creep7. These properties make them suitable for high-temperature components, such as next-generation combustor liners and nozzle guide vanes8. Furthermore, they also show promise for thermal protection systems on hypersonic vehicles, such as leading-edge parts like wings and nose cones, as well as thermal structural panels1.

However, directionally solidified dual-phase eutectic ceramics (e.g., Al2O3/Y3Al5O12 (YAG), Al2O3/ZrO2, Al2O3/GdAlO3 (GAP)) exhibit limited fracture toughness (2–4 MPa·m1/2)1, which significantly constrained their widespread applications. Considerable efforts have been devoted to enhancing their fracture toughness, primarily through the following approaches: (1) seed-induced orientation control9–11, (2) third-phase addition (e.g., ZrO2)12, (3) interface engineering13, and (4) low-energy grain boundary introduction for microstructural refinement14. While these methods have led to a modest improvement in fracture toughness, the achieved values still remain inadequate for practical application requirements. Therefore, further exploration of novel strategies for enhancing fracture toughness remains imperative.

In the present work, we focus on dual-phase eutectic ceramics comprising Al2O3 and high-entropy REAlO3 (RE = Gd0.25Eu0.25Nd0.25Sm0.25). The directional solidification technique enables the fabrication of Al2O3/REAlO3 dual-phase eutectic ceramics with a three-dimensionally interlocking topology, wherein Al2O3 and high-entropy REAlO3 grow along the preferred growth directions and well-defined interface planes that form thermodynamically stable low-strain-energy configurations. In contrast to traditional enhancement strategies, we have developed an approach, that is, employing nano-coherent high-entropy REAlO3, to overcome the intrinsic fracture toughness limitations and improve the hardness of alumina-based dual-phase eutectic ceramics. Here are several considerations: (i) The introduction of high-entropy REAlO3 may enhance the mechanical performance of Al2O3/REAlO3 dual-phase eutectic ceramics because high-entropy oxides typically exhibit high hardness, strength, and toughness due to the cocktail effect, valence electron distribution, lattice distortion, and sluggish diffusion15,16; (ii) The lattice distortion characteristics of high-entropy oxides can be utilized to tailor the eutectic interfaces, thereby enhancing mechanical properties. This study employs the directional solidification technique to fabricate Al2O3/REAlO3 dual-phase eutectic ceramics, where Al2O3 and high-entropy REAlO3 are interpenetrated into each other forming a three-dimensional interpenetrating network. Researches have demonstrated that the eutectic interfaces play critical roles in determining mechanical performance of alumina-based eutectic ceramics5; (iii) the coherent grain boundaries (GBs) of high-entropy REAlO3 and the semi-coherent phase boundaries (PBs) between Al2O3 and high-entropy REAlO3 can ensure microstructural stability, and facilitate the achievement of conflicting mechanical properties, such as the hardness and fracture toughness. Low-strain-energy plane defects, such as twin boundaries, coherent GBs, and semi-coherent PBs were demonstrated to enable synergistic optimization of conflicting properties, for instance, nanotwinned Cu with ultrahigh strength and retained conductivity17; ultra-strong and ductile steel produced via high-density coherent Ni(Al,Fe) nanoparticles in martensite (σUTS: 2.2 GPa, ε: ≥8%)18; Ni-based single-crystal superalloys with near-coherent γ/γ‘ interfaces enabling superior high-temperature strength-ductility19; (iv) Nanosized high-entropy REAlO3 grains may strengthen the Al2O3/REAlO3 dual-phase eutectic ceramics. According to the Hall–Petch relation20,21, the smaller the grain size, the better the performance. The nano-coherent high-entropy REAlO3 grains could enhance the mechanical properties of the Al2O3/REAlO3 dual-phase eutectic ceramics.

To fundamentally transcend this strength-toughness dilemma, a paradigm shift in material design is required—one that moves beyond conventional composite strategies and instead engineers the atomic and interfacial structures across multiple length scales. High-entropy oxides, with their inherent lattice distortion and sluggish diffusion, offer a promising way to tailor phase stability and interface cohesion. However, integrating high-entropy oxides into eutectic systems to create structurally ordered, nano-coherent composites remains an uncharted territory. Here, we propose and demonstrate a high-entropy eutectic ceramic design by incorporating a single-phase REAlO3 into the Al2O3 matrix. We envision that the high-entropy-induced lattice distortion and nanoscale domain formation will work in concert with the intrinsically low-energy eutectic interfaces, thereby activating simultaneous strengthening and toughening mechanisms. This approach not only breaks the property trade-off in alumina-based eutectics but also establishes a generalizable materials design strategy potentially applicable to other eutectic systems.

Results

Phase constitution and eutectic microstructures

Figure 1a shows the X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern of the prepared Al2O3/REAlO3 eutectic ceramic powder, compared with those of four other comparative eutectic ceramics (CECs) Al2O3/XAlO3 (X = Gd, Sm, Eu, Nd). The results indicate that the microstructure consists only of Al2O3 and REAlO3 phases, with no intermediate or amorphous phases present. A magnified view (Fig. 1b) of the 2θ range between 33–35°, 48–56° reveals that the diffraction peak of the high-entropy REAlO3 exhibits a right shift compared to those of the Al2O3/GdAlO3 and Al2O3/EuAlO3 eutectics, while it shows a left shift relative to the Al2O3/SmAlO3 and Al2O3/NdAlO3 eutectics. The peak position of the high-entropy REAlO3 lies between these two groups. Fig. S2 shows the Rietveld refinement XRD data of the five eutectics. The results confirm excellent agreement between observed and calculated XRD patterns, validating successful phase identification and structural modeling. Fig. S3 compares the lattice parameters (a, b, c) and unit-cell volume of high-entropy REAlO3 with those of XAlO3 (X = Gd, Sm, Eu, Nd), revealing deviations from both the XAlO3 (X = Gd, Sm, Eu, Nd) and the Vegard-predicted values. These results are further supported by molecular dynamics (MD) simulations (Supplementary information). Comparison of Fig. S3d and Table S2 shows that the unit-cell volume of the high-entropy REAlO3 is smaller than those of NdAlO3 and SmAlO3, but larger than those of GdAlO3 and EuAlO3. Notably, the diffraction peaks of Al2O3 remain unchanged across all five eutectics. Actually, the lattice distortions of REAlO3 can be directly visualized in the subsequent atomic-resolution analysis. Figure 1c shows the transverse and longitudinal scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of the prepared Al2O3/REAlO3 dual-phase eutectic ceramics, where the black area represents Al2O3 and the white area represents high-entropy REAlO3. It can be observed that the eutectic morphology grows in a colony form with distinct colony boundaries. For comparison, the microstructure of Al2O3/REAlO3 dual-phase eutectic ceramics is compared with four CECs, as presented in Fig. S4. The eutectic morphology is similar to that of Al2O3/GdAlO3 and Al2O3/SmAlO3 eutectic ceramics, exhibiting irregular Chinese Script patterns both within the colonies and at the colony boundaries. However, it differs from that of Al2O3/EuAlO3 and Al2O3/NdAlO3 eutectics, which display some regular fibrous or lamellar morphology in both intra-colonial and inter-colonial regions. A common feature is that the colony boundary regions consistently exhibit coarser microstructures, while the intra-colonial regions show finer microstructures. Additionally, at the same withdrawal rate, the Al2O3/REAlO3 dual-phase eutectic ceramics demonstrate larger colony sizes. These larger colony dimensions result in a higher proportion of fine eutectic structures and a reduced number of coarse boundary structures, leading to an overall finer eutectic microstructure. Figure 1d shows the X-ray computed tomography (XCT) image of the prepared Al2O3/REAlO3 eutectic ceramics. It can be observed that the two phases interpenetrate into each other, forming a three-dimensionally interconnected topology. The 3D morphology of the single-phase and micro-pores can be observed in Fig. S5. Quantitative analysis indicates a near-theoretical Al2O3/REAlO3 mass ratio of 45:55. The resulting eutectic ceramics exhibited densification with a low porosity of 0.29 vol%, showing a maximum equivalent pore diameter of 24.8 μm and an average equivalent pore diameter of 10.6 μm. To verify the successful fabrication of high-entropy REAlO3, comprehensive energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping and line scanning analyses are conducted. The results reveal that all rare earth elements (REEs: Gd, Sm, Eu, Nd) are homogeneously distributed within the white regions of Fig. S6, while Al and O are detected across the entire area with significantly strong signals in the dark regions. Further in-depth scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) characterization is performed to elucidate the high-entropy REAlO3 in greater detail. Fig. 1e presents the high-angle annular dark-field STEM (HAADF-STEM) image of the high-entropy REAlO3 along the [010] direction, with the corresponding diffraction patterns inset in the lower left corner. Combined with diffraction patterns acquired along eight additional zone axes (Fig. S7), the crystal structure of high-entropy REAlO3, with lattice parameters measured as a = 5.288 Å, b = 7.490 Å, c = 5.284 Å, is identified as GdFeO3 (GFO) type. This crystal structure follows the general ABO3 stoichiometry and crystallizes in the Pbnm space group (No. 62). Deep potential MD simulations (Table S2) also verify that high-entropy REAlO3 is GFO-type structure. The lattice parameters differ from those of XAlO3 (X= Gd, Sm, Eu, Nd). Owing to the lattice strains exerted in multiple directions, its lattice parameters exhibit intermediate values. HAADF-EDS mapping is employed to further investigate the atomic sublattice site-occupation of the high-entropy REAlO3. Fig. 1e present the elemental distribution mapping of high-entropy REAlO3, with incident direction along the [010] zone axis. Atomic-resolution EDS mapping confirms the high-entropy REAlO3 exhibits identical atomic occupancy of all four REEs, with Gd/Sm/Eu/Nd sharing equivalent Wyckoff positions. Al element occupies distinct positions, alternating regularly with REEs. The Sm-Al elemental mapping clearly demonstrates this ordered atomic column arrangement (where Sm is shown as a representative substitute for REEs for clarity).

Orientation relationships

Figure 2 shows the electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) results of the prepared Al2O3/REAlO3 dual-phase eutectic ceramics. From the inverse pole figure (IPF) mapping of Al2O3 (Fig. 2a), it can be observed that the preferred growth direction of Al2O3 is 〈10-10〉. Note that although Al2O3 exhibits two distinct colors, they represent the same crystallographic orientation. The reason for this dual-color labeling in EBSD is that the Channel 5 software indexed Al2O3 as pseudo-hexagonal structure, where 〈10-10〉 and 〈01-10〉 are treated as distinct. GD-I: 〈101〉REAlO3-I || 〈010〉REAlO3-II. (purple circles in Fig. S8)

OR-I: {101} REAlO3-I || {101} REAlO3-II (the green);

OR-II: {100} REAlO3-I || {121} REAlO3-II (the pink);

OR-III: {010} REAlO3-I || {101} REAlO3-II (the gray);

OR-IV: {001} REAlO3-I || {121} REAlO3-II (the black);

OR-V: {121} REAlO3-I || {001} REAlO3-II (the yellow).

Some of the above potential orientation relationships are mutually contradictory. For example, when {100} REAlO3-I is parallel to {121} REAlO3-II, {001} REAlO3-I cannot simultaneously be parallel to {121} REAlO3-II, as {100} REAlO3-I and {001} REAlO3-I are mutually perpendicular. Note that since REAlO3 has an orthorhombic structure (a ≠ b ≠ c, α = β = γ = 90°), {100} ≠ {010} ≠ {001}. Due to the presence of two coherent high-entropy REAlO3 grain types, it is necessary to investigate the crystallographic orientation relationships between Al2O3 and both REAlO3-I and REAlO3-II. By combining IPFs (Fig. 2) and PFs (Figs. S9, S10), it is evident that the preferred growth direction of Al2O3 is parallel to those of high-entropy REAlO3 (indicated by pink and blue squares in Fig. S9, S10):

GD-II: 〈10-10〉 Al2O3 || 〈101〉 REAlO3-I || 〈010〉 REAlO3-II.

Considering that EBSD results only reflect the crystallographic information from a limited region of the sample cross-section, high-energy XRD was employed to characterize the crystallographic orientation across the entire cross-section. As shown in Fig. S11, Al2O3 exhibits only one diffraction peak of (30-30) plane, while REAlO3 shows two distinct peaks of (101) and (020) planes. Therefore, the XRD results demonstrate that the crystallographic orientation of the sample cross-section follows 〈30-30〉 Al2O3 || 〈101〉 REAlO3-I || 〈020〉 REAlO3-II, which is identical to GD-II. Notably, these two peaks share identical crystallographic information as their 2θ values exhibit a doubling relationship. The comparison reveals that the d-spacings of (101) and (020) planes do not coincide. Their 2θ values differs slightly (23.879° vs. 23.829°). This minor discrepancy results in a single diffraction peak corresponding to two distinct crystallographic indices.

From Fig. S9, two possible orientation relationships between REAlO3-I and Al2O3 are identified (marked by pairs of identically colored squares in the PFs):

OR-VI: {121} REAlO3-I || {0001} Al2O3;

OR-VII: {121} REAlO3-I || {11-20} Al2O3.

Since the {0001} and {11-20} planes in Al2O3 are mutually perpendicular, OR-VI and OR-VII cannot coexist; only one can be valid.

Similarly, Fig. S10 reveals four possible orientation relationships between REAlO3-II and Al2O3.

OR-VIII: {100} REAlO3-II || {0001} Al2O3;

OR-IX: {001} REAlO3-II || {0001} Al2O3;

OR-X: {100} REAlO3-II || {11-20} Al2O3;

OR-XI: {001} REAlO3-II || {11-20} Al2O3.

However, due to its orthogonal structure, the {100} and {001} planes in REAlO3-II are perpendicular. Therefore, OR-VIII and OR-IX are mutually exclusive, as are OR-X and OR-XI. Consequently, only two of these relationships can be valid simultaneously. Thus, HRTEM experiments are required to further investigate the orientation relationship between REAlO3-I and Al2O3, REAlO3-II and Al2O3.

GBs and PBs structures

GBs and PBs are critical microstructural features in Al2O3/REAlO3 dual-phase eutectic ceramics, where their distribution, density, and orientation relationships play a decisive role in mechanical properties. As shown in Fig. S12, densely distributed nanoscale black GBs and red PBs can be observed, with GB-to-PB area ratio of 76.5: 23.5%. To accurately determine the crystallographic orientation relationships of GBs and PBs from EBSD data, HRTEM and STEM investigations were performed.

Figure 3a presents a low-magnification STEM image of REAlO3, with a GB indicated by white arrows. The corresponding selected area electron diffraction (SAED) patterns (insets) reveal zone axes of [10-1] and [010], corresponding to grain REAlO3-I and grain REAlO3-II, respectively. Fig. 3b shows a magnified HAADF-STEM image of the GB region box in white from Fig. 3a. It can be seen that the GB is coherent. Unfortunately, due to the small lattice mismatch, it is difficult to determine the specific GB locations at this magnification. This conclusion is further supported by Geometric phase analysis (GPA) of Fig. 3b, which reveals no detectable strain at the GB, as marked by the white dotted lines in Fig. 3e. Crystallographic orientation relationships between grain the REAlO3-I and grain REAlO3-II are determined as GD-I, OR-I, OR-III, and OR-V from the SAED patterns of the GB (Fig. 3c), identifying (101) as the coherent plane. Figure 3d shows the difference in atomic column arrangement across the GB, as marked by the yellow boxes in the HAADF-STEM image. The coherent GB can be clearly seen in the bright field (BF) imaging (Fig. S13a). In addition, the live changes in the SAED patterns on both sides of the GB can be clearly observed in the video (Movie S1), and it is evident that the patterns differ across the REAlO3 GB. The GFO-type structural model viewed along the [10-1] and [010] zone axes (partially overlapping with the HAADF-STEM image on the right side of Fig. 3d) was used for comparative analysis. The model reveals atomic-scale details that are not discernible in the HAADF-STEM image. The simulated HAADF-STEM images (Fig. S13) generated using quantitative scanning transmission electron microscopy (QSTEM) software also agree well with experimental observations. Notably, Fig. S14 presents the local enlarged GPA results from Fig. 3b, in which lattice distortion in the high-entropy REAlO3 can be observed in the magnified view. This result validates the XRD pattern in Fig. 1a and the Rietveld refinement result in Fig. S3.

Discussion

Formation of the high-entropy solid solution

Synthesizing high-entropy REAlO3 is the key to integrating it into alumina-based eutectic ceramics and improving the mechanical performance. Experiment results demonstrate the successful synthesis of high-entropy REAlO3 through liquid-solid transition processing, establishing semi-coherent interfaces with the Al2O3 matrix. This study investigates the thermodynamic stabilization mechanism of high-entropy REAlO3. Strengthening and toughening mechanisms

The improved mechanical properties of Al2O3/REAlO3 dual-phase eutectic ceramics originates from multiscale synergistic strengthening and toughening mechanisms, in which high-entropy-induced lattice distortion and the Hall-Petch effect play critical roles. The disordered multi-cationic configuration in the high-entropy REAlO3 phase generates local lattice distortion and internal strain fields, which provide prominent high-entropy strengthening. Meanwhile, the nanostructure satisfies the typical conditions for the Hall-Petch effect; the refined grain size significantly increases GB density, which suppresses crack nucleation and enhances hardness. These atomic‑ and nanoscale‑strengthening effects also lay a solid foundation for the improved fracture toughness by suppressing premature crack propagation and facilitating crack deflection instead of catastrophic failure.

The superior hardness of Al2O3/REAlO3 dual-phase eutectic ceramics stems from three multiscale strengthening pathways. Multi-principal ions (e.g., Nd³⁺(1.163 Å), Gd³⁺(1.107 Å), CN = 9) induce local distortion stress fields within REAlO3 lattices due to ionic radius mismatch (ζ = 1.2%), significantly suppressing shear deformation during indentation, and enhances bond strength by optimizing Al-O-RE covalent character; Concurrently, the high-entropy REAlO3 grains are refined to an average size of 57.5 nm, markedly increasing GB density, where these nanoscale GBs act as rigid obstacles to block microcrack initiation via the classical Hall-Petch mechanism20,21. Beyond nanoscale effects, the three-dimensional interpenetrating network enables Al2O3 and high-entropy REAlO3 to form a three-dimensional interlocked topology, where load transfer through strong PBs eliminates stress concentration in individual phases.

The improvement fracture toughness of the eutectic ceramic is derived from a hierarchical multi-mechanism toughening system, dominated by interface-mediated crack regulation strategies. The semi-coherent PBs (mismatch \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\delta }_{I}$$\end{document}δI = 5.1% and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\delta }_{{II}}$$\end{document}δII = 4.9%) and modulus difference across the PB force crack deflection > 50° at Al2O3/REAlO3 interfaces (Fig. 5d), extending propagation path by 50–100%, or trigger crack bifurcation with branch angles ~15°, effectively dispersing fracture energy. Additionally, the three-dimensional interlocked structure forces non-planar crack propagation, where bridging ligaments with a size of 1 ~ 2 μm (comparable to the crack-opening displacement) generate crack closure stresses through elastic constraint and grain boundary sliding-induced micro-plasticity. This synergistic toughening effect results in a fracture toughness (KICb) of 5.5 MPa·m1/2, representing a 103% improvement relative to the weakest binary eutectic in this study (Al2O3/NdAlO3) and a notable 22% enhancement compared to the relatively tough Al2O3/GdAlO3 binary eutectic, exceeding the typical toughness range (2 ~ 4 MPa·m1/2) of conventional alumina-based binary eutectic ceramics. First-principles calculations show that the coexistence of several rare-earth elements causes obvious spatial differences in charge accumulation and depletion in high-entropy REAlO3, as shown in Fig. S20. A heterogeneous bonding network with different bond strengths and mixed ionic features is formed, which could give REAlO3 higher fracture toughness. This can also explain the improved fracture toughness observed in Al2O3/REAlO3.

The improved properties breakthrough of Al2O3/REAlO3 dual-phase eutectic ceramics relies on a decoupling strategy to break the inherent strength-toughness trade-off, achieved by two parallel design principles: A cascaded strengthening chain evolves across multiple scales. It initiated by high-entropy lattice distortion at the atomic scale, progresses to nano-coherent GB formation at the nanoscale, and culminates in low-strain-energy PB design at the nano-microscale. Concurrently, the multi-mechanistic toughening system integrates sequential energy-dissipation processes, including crack deflection for path extension, bifurcation for energy dispersion, and ligament bridging for closure stress generation. The experimentally observed crack morphology within a 5 μm scale shows clear crack deflection, bifurcation, and ligament bridging, which serves as direct visual evidence to confirm the synergistic effect of semi-coherent interfaces and the three-dimensional interpenetrating network in achieving the exceptional synergy of mechanical properties.

In summary, we fabricate dual-phase eutectic ceramics comprising Al2O3 and nano-coherent high-entropy REAlO3 by directional solidification. The single-crystalline Al2O3 interlocks with bicrystalline high-entropy REAlO3 in three-dimensional space, forming coherent REAlO3 GBs (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\gamma }_{{GB}}$$\end{document}γGB = 0 J/m²), semi-coherent PBs (lattice misfit δ < 5.1%) and nanoscale REAlO3 domains (λavg = 57.5 nm). This unique configuration delivers unprecedented property synergy of Vickers hardness (HV = 19.4 GPa) and fracture toughness (KICv = 5.5 MPa·m1/2). The values exceed reference binary eutectics by 10–37% and 22–103%, respectively, shattering the strength-toughness trade-off via multiscale mechanisms—high-entropy strengthening (lattice distortion and Hall-Petch effect) and multi-mechanistic toughening mechanisms (crack deflection/bifurcation at interfaces and bridging). The improved properties make it a prime candidate for extreme environment applications such as next-generation wear-resistant coatings and plasma-facing components in fusion reactors. Our work establishes high-entropy eutectic design as a paradigm for engineering ceramics with atomic-precision interfaces and exceptional load-bearing capability.

Methods

Material design

The material design of this work was based on a systematic review of Al2O3-based eutectic ceramics and their binary phase diagrams. Al2O3-based eutectic ceramics are composed of Al2O3 and lanthanide aluminates. The phase diagrams and relevant literatures for binary systems of Al2O3 and 14 X2O3 (X= [La→Lu]) were first reviewed, excluding Pm due to its radioactivity. This review revealed two distinct groups of Al2O3-based eutectic ceramics: one group consists of Al2O3 and perovskite-phase REAlO3 (orthorhombic structure), where RE refers to the first seven lanthanide elements (La, Ce, Pr, Nd, Sm, Eu, Gd); the other group comprises Al2O3 and garnet-phase RE3Al5O12 (cubic structure), with RE corresponding to the latter seven lanthanide elements (Tb, Dy, Ho, Er, Tm, Yb, Lu). The present work focused on the Al2O3/perovskite-phase REAlO3 eutectics. Further analysis of the Al2O3/X2O3 binary phase diagrams for La, Ce, Pr, Nd, Sm, Eu, and Gd showed that only Nd, Sm, Eu, and Gd can form stable Al2O3/REAlO3 eutectic structures (see Fig. S21). In contrast, La, Ce, and Pr form intermediate phases (e.g., LaAl11O18), which hinder the direct formation of Al2O3-based eutectic ceramics. For this reason, four-element high-entropy phase (Gd0.25Eu0.25Nd0.25Sm0.25)AlO3 for our investigation was selected.

Fabrication of dual-phase eutectic ceramics

High-purity ceramic powders of Al2O3 (99.99% purity with particle size below 5 μm) and gadolinium oxide (Gd2O3), neodymium oxide (Nd2O3), europium oxide (Eu2O3), and samarium oxide (Sm2O3) powders (99.99% purity with particle size below 5 μm) were procured from Beijing Yosoc Science & Technology Co., Ltd. To prepare Al2O3/REAlO3 dual-phase eutectic ceramics, Al2O3 powder was first mixed with Gd2O3, Sm2O3, Eu2O3, and Nd2O3 and powders individually according to the eutectic molar ratios of 78: 22, 74: 26, 75: 25 and 79: 21 and as shown in Fig. S21. Subsequently, these four mixtures were blended again to obtain the final mixture. Then mix the four powders in a 1:1:1:1 molar ratio to obtain the final mixture. For comparative studies, these four distinct mixed powders were separately reserved for the preparation of Al2O3/GdAlO3, Al2O3/NdAlO3, Al2O3/EuAlO3, and Al2O3/SmAlO3 eutectic ceramics.

The mixture was placed in an agate jar with anhydrous ethanol and subjected to 12 h of planetary ball milling (QM-3SP2, Nanjing Chi Shun Technology Development Co., Ltd) to ensure homogeneous mixing. The resulting slurry was thoroughly dried at 70 °C in a drying oven (DZ-2BCDZ-2BC, Shenyang Kejing Auto-instrument Co., Ltd), followed by separation of the powder from agate balls using an 80-mesh sieves. The dried mixture was combined with a polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) binder in a 3:1 mass ratio and loaded into a 316 L stainless steel cold-pressing die with internal dimensions of 10 × 10 × 150 mm3. The green body was formed under 30 MPa pressure, held for 5 min, and then demolded. Subsequently, the compacted rods underwent cold isostatic pressing at 220 MPa for 15 min. Finally, the rods were sintered in a muffle furnace (KSL-1700X-A1, Hefei Kejing Materials Technology Co., Ltd) at 1550 °C for 2 h.

The directional solidification experiment was conducted using an optical floating zone furnace (FZ-T-2000-X-I-VPOPC, Crystal System, Japan) equipped with four xenon lamps (each with a power of 3 kW). This furnace can achieve a maximum temperature of 2500 °C, which meets the requirements of this study. The eutectic point of the ceramics, as determined by the phase diagram, is approximately 1720 °C. Although the temperature during the experiment could not be directly measured, the melting state of the ceramic was monitored through a CCD (charge coupled device) camera via a quartz tube.

During the process, the feed rod was rotated clockwise at 15 revolutions per minute (rpm), while the seed rod remained stationary. The withdrawing rate was maintained at 20 mm/h. To minimize air containing in the sample, the experiment was performed under a vacuum of 10-1 Pa. Ultimately, a eutectic ceramic rod with a diameter of ~9 mm and a length of ~85 mm was successfully fabricated.

Microstructure characterization and simulation

The directionally solidified eutectic ceramic rods were sectioned using a diamond wire saw (STX-202A, Shenyang Kejing Auto-instrument Co., Ltd). All samples were progressively ground with 400-, 800-, and 1500-grit diamond abrasive discs, followed by grounding with 2000-grit SiC abrasive paper and final polishing using 2.5 μm diamond polishing paste for 5 min. For scanning electron microscopy (SEM) observation, the sample surfaces were sputter-coated with a gold layer. For electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) sample preparation, an additional polishing step using colloidal silica suspension is required, followed by deposition of a 5-nm-thick carbon coating on the surface. Specimens for transmission Kikuchi diffraction (TKD) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis were prepared using a dual-beam focused ion beam (FIB) system (Thermo Scientific Scios 2, United States).

The phase composition was analyzed via X-ray diffraction (XRD-7000, Shimadzu, Japan) and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS). The morphology was observed by SEM (JSM-6700F, JEOL, Japan). The three-dimensional morphology and micro-pores distribution were characterized using a high-resolution 190 kV nano-focus X-ray computed tomography (XCT, Diondo d2, Germany) system. The key testing parameters were as follows: X-ray voltage of 90 kV, tube current of 90 μA, integration time of 2000 ms; 1795 projection images were acquired with a reconstruction matrix of 4000 × 4000 and a voxel size of 2 μm. Three-dimensional visualization and analysis were performed using VGStudio MAX 3.4 software. Crystallographic orientation relationships were investigated using EBSD with a NordlysNano detector (Oxford Instruments, UK). TKD testing was performed using a Thermo Fisher Scios 2 equipped with a Bruker OPTIMUS detector on TEM samples. The step size was set to 10 nm. After the EBSD and TKD testing, the data was subsequently post-processed by the Oxford Instrument software Channel 5. Pole figures (PFs) and inverse pole figure (z-axis) (IPFs) maps were calculated automatically by the software. PBs and GBs were examined by high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) and high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) using a Thermo Fisher Speatra microscope operating at 200 kV. Nano-scale elemental distributions in the Al2O3/REAlO3 dual-phase eutectic ceramics samples were also examined by STEM-EDS analysis. Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) patterns generated through Digital Micrograph software (Gatan Inc., USA).

To enhance the atomic-scale contrast in HAADF-STEM images, selected micrographs underwent digital processing through combined application of band-pass frequency filtering and Wiener restoration algorithms to suppress both high-frequency noise and low-frequency amorphous background signals. Lattice distortion quantification was performed via Geometric Phase Analysis (GPA) using an open-source plugin integrated in the DM platform. GPA was performed on a HAADF-STEM image of the GB region in Fig. 3d. The FFT of the HAADF-STEM image was first calculated to obtain diffraction spots in reciprocal space, and the diffraction peaks corresponding to the parallel crystal planes (the red and the blue squares in Fig. 3e) were selected to define the analysis orientations. The selected spots were then filtered and inverse FFT to reconstruct the lattice phase field in real space. Finally, the lattice strain fields (e.g., εxx and εyy) were derived by spatial differentiation of the phase field. Simulation of the HAADF-STEM images was conducted using the quantitative scanning transmission electron microscopy (QSTEM) software developed by Koch35, and the parameters used for the simulation are high voltage = 200 kV, Scherzer focus df = -13.7 nm, spherical aberration Cs = 0.05 mm, chromatic aberration coefficient Cc = 1.4 mm, and thermal diffuse scattering (TDS) runs = 10 times. Both the electron probe convergence semi-angle and detector collection semi-angles were precisely matched to that in experiment.

Mechanical property testing

The nanohardness (Hn) and elastic modulus (E) of the samples were conducted on a Nano Indenter® G200 system (KLA, USA) equipment fitted with a 20 nm-radius Berkovich diamond tip. A standardized procedure involving 49 indentations at 8 mN load with 20 µm inter-indentation spacing was implemented. Data processing followed the Oliver-Pharr method36, incorporating a Poisson’s ratio (ν) of 0.23 estimated from averaged values of 0.22 for Al2O3 and 0.25 for GdAlO325.

To ensure measurement reliability of nanohardness and elastic modulus, the specimens underwent vibratory polishing combined with tri-solvent ultrasonic cleaning (acetone/ethanol/deionized water). A 7 × 7 grid indentation pattern was implemented for each specimen to mitigate microstructural heterogeneity effects. Testing parameters were strictly controlled with a loading rate of 0.8 mN/s, peak load of 8 mN, and 2-second dwell duration. The acquired dataset underwent Weibull24 statistical analysis to evaluate dispersion characteristics, with mean values and standard deviations calculated through rigorous statistical processing.

Vickers hardness (Hv) was evaluated using an HVS-10AT Vickers tester (China) across seven load levels (0.98, 1.96, 2.94, 4.9, 9.8, 29.4, and 49 N).
