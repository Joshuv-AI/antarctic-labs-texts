Safety assessment of the substance 1,3‐benzenedicarboxamide, 5‐[[[cis‐4‐(1,1‐dimethylethyl)cyclohexyl]carbonyl]amino]‐N1,N3‐bis[cis‐4‐(1‐methylethyl)cyclohexyl]‐ for use in food contact materials
Authors: EFSA Panel on Food Contact Materials (FCM), Claude Lambré, Riccardo Crebelli, Maria João da Silva, Konrad Grob, Ester Heath, Evgenia Lampi, Maria Rosaria Milana, Marja Pronk, Mario Ščetar, Georgios Theodoridis, Els Van Hoeck, Nadia Waegeneers, Ronan Cariou, Laurence Castle, Emma Di Consiglio, Roland Franz, Eric Barthélémy, Remigio Marano, Gilles Rivière
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe EFSA Panel on Food Contact Materials assessed the safety of the 1,3‐benzenedicarboxamide, 5‐[[[cis‐4‐(1,1‐dimethylethyl)cyclohexyl]carbonyl]amino]‐N1,N3‐bis[cis‐4‐(1‐methylethyl)cyclohexyl]‐, to be used as a clarifying agent in polypropylene at up to 0.08% w/w. The final articles are intended for contact with all types of food except foods containing more than 10% ethanol, infant formula or mineral water intended to be used to reconstitute infant formula. They are intended for long‐term storage at room temperature and below, including hot fill and heating conditions. The migration of the substance was below the limit of quantification of 0.05 mg/kg food in 10% ethanol, with values estimated up to 0.031 mg/kg in olive oil. The migration of the trans isomers can be estimated up to 0.0006 mg/kg food. The total mass transfer of all impurities was individually below 0.05 mg/kg food. The Panel excluded genotoxicity concerns for the substance based on in vitro studies. Trans isomers and relevant impurities were assessed using in silico and literature data and did not raise concerns for genotoxicity. The Panel concluded that the substance is not of safety concern for the consumer if it is used at up to 0.08% w/w in polypropylene homo‐ and co‐polymers, and the manufactured materials and articles are: (i) for single and repeated use, in contact up to and above 6 months at room temperature and below, including hot‐fill conditions and/or heating up to 70°C ≤ T ≤ 100°C for maximum t = 120/2^((T − 70)/10) min; and (ii) are used in contact with all types of food, excluding beverages with an ethanol content above 10%, infant formula, including reconstituted infant formula and human milk.Keywords: 1,3‐Benzenedicarboxamide, 5‐[[[cis‐4‐(1,1‐dimethylethyl)cyclohexyl]carbonyl]amino]‐N1,N3‐bis[cis‐4‐(1‐methylethyl)cyclohexyl]‐; CAS no. 2649373‐38‐4; evaluation; FCM substance no. 1100; food contact materials; safety assessment

INTRODUCTION

Background and Terms of Reference as provided by the requestor

Before a substance is authorised to be used in food contact materials (FCM) and is included in a positive list, an opinion of the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) on its safety is required. This procedure has been established in Articles 8, 9 and 10 of Regulation (EC) No 1935/2004 1 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 27 October 2004 on materials and articles intended to come into contact with food.

According to this procedure, the industry submits applications to the Member States' competent authorities which transmit the applications to the EFSA for their evaluation.

In this case, EFSA received an application from the Belgian competent authority (FPS Public Health, Food Chain Safety and Environment), requesting the evaluation of the substance 5‐[[[cis‐4‐(1,1‐dimethylethyl)cyclohexyl]carbonyl]amino]‐N1,N3‐bis[cis‐4‐(1‐methylethyl)cyclohexyl]‐1,3‐benzenedicarboxamide, with the CAS number 2649373‐38‐4 and the FCM substance No 1100. The dossier was submitted by Keller and Heckman LLP on behalf of Milliken & Company.

According to Regulation (EC) No 1935/2004 of the European Parliament and of the Council on materials and articles intended to come into contact with food, EFSA is asked to carry out an assessment of the risks related to the intended use of the substance and to deliver a scientific opinion.

Methodologies

The assessment was conducted in line with the principles laid down in Regulation (EC) No 1935/2004 on materials and articles intended to come into contact with food. This regulation requires applicants to submit an application accompanied by a technical dossier containing the information specified in the guidelines for the safety assessment of a substance to be published by EFSA. In practice, the technical dossier should contain the information required in the EFSA Note for guidance for the preparation of an application for the safety assessment of a substance to be used in food contact materials (EFSA, 2008).

The methodology is based on the characterisation of the substance that is the subject of the request for safety assessment prior to authorisation, its impurities and reaction and degradation products, the evaluation of the exposure to those substances through migration and the definition of minimum sets of toxicity data required for safety assessment.

To establish the safety from ingestion of migrating substances, the toxicological data indicating the potential hazard and the likely human exposure data need to be combined. Exposure is estimated from studies on migration into food or food simulants and considering that a person may consume daily up to 1 kg of food in contact with the relevant FCM.

As a general rule, the greater the exposure through migration, the more toxicological data are required for the safety assessment of a substance. Currently, there are three tiers with different thresholds triggering the need for more toxicological information as follows:

In case of high migration (i.e. 5–60 mg/kg food), an extensive data set is needed.

In case of high migration (i.e. 5–60 mg/kg food), an extensive data set is needed.

In case of migration between 0.05 and 5 mg/kg food, a reduced data set may suffice.

In case of migration between 0.05 and 5 mg/kg food, a reduced data set may suffice.

In case of low migration (i.e. < 0.05 mg/kg food), only a limited data set is needed.

In case of low migration (i.e. < 0.05 mg/kg food), only a limited data set is needed.

The above tiered approach to toxicity testing applies to all migrating substances.

For impurities, oligomers and reaction and degradation products which migrate into foods, non‐testing methods may be taken into account to assess the genotoxic potential (EFSA CEF Panel, 2016).

In case of applications on mixtures of natural origin, the EFSA CEP Panel updated the risk assessment of ‘wood flour and fibres, untreated’ and set criteria for future applications of materials from plant origin as additives for plastic food contact materials (EFSA CEP Panel, 2019). This was completed by an EFSA Technical Report on principles that could be applicable to the safety assessment of the use of mixtures of natural origin (EFSA, 2023a, 2023b), which details and sets specific data requirements.

Finally, relevant cross‐cutting documents from the EFSA Scientific Committee 6 should be followed.

More detailed information on the above data requirements is available in the respective above‐mentioned EFSA documents.

The assessment was conducted in line with the principles described in the EFSA Guidance on transparency in the scientific aspects of risk assessment (EFSA, 2009).

ASSESSMENT

According to the applicant, the substance 5‐[[[cis‐4‐(1,1‐dimethylethyl)cyclohexyl]carbonyl]amino]‐N1,N3‐bis[cis‐4‐(1‐methylethyl)cyclohexyl]‐1,3‐benzenedicarboxamide, renamed by the Panel as 1,3‐benzenedicarboxamide, 5‐[[[cis‐4‐(1,1‐dimethylethyl)cyclohexyl]carbonyl]amino]‐N1,N3‐bis[cis‐4‐(1‐methylethyl)cyclohexyl]‐ is intended to be used at up to 0.08% w/w as clarifying agent in the production of polypropylene (PP) homo‐ and co‐polymer materials and articles. The final articles are intended for contact with all types of food except foods containing more than 10% ethanol, infant formula or mineral water intended to be used to reconstitute infant formula. Examples of typical articles include packaging for foods and beverages, e.g. bottles, cups, bowls or tubs as well as repeated‐use applications like containers and eating utensils. The final FCMs may be used for storage above 6 months at room temperature and below, including hot‐fill conditions and/or heating up to 70°C ≤ T ≤ 100°C for maximum t = 120/2^((T − 70)/10) min.

The safety of the substance has not been evaluated in the past by the SCF or EFSA.

Physical and chemical properties 11

The solubility of the substance in water at 20°C was 33 ng/L; the log Po/w at pH 5 and 50°C was 6.72. Solubility data in ethanol or iso‐octane were not provided. Solubility in NMP at 25°C and 100°C was reported to be 1 and 18 g/L, respectively, while in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) at 100°C, it was 3 g/L. The melting point was 312–313°C.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) showed thermal degradation of the substance starting from 300°C and 320°C in air and under nitrogen, respectively. Hence, no thermal degradation of the substance is expected under maximum PP processing conditions (i.e. max 240°C).

Since the substance is added to the plastic as a powder, the potential presence of a fraction of small particles should be established in accordance with the guidance on technical requirements for regulated food and feed product applications to establish the presence of small particles, including nanoparticles (EFSA Scientific Committee, 2021a). As the substance is incorporated and processed under polymer melting conditions, it will dissolve into the polymer with the loss of the particulate nature. Hence, no additional assessment for the fraction of small particles was needed (EFSA Scientific Committee, 2021a).

Specific migration of the substance and the impurities 12

Specific migration of the substance

Migration was tested using plaques of 0.2 cm thickness made of random copolymer PP with nominally 0.08% w/w of the substance. Confirmation of the use level by measurement of the content in the PP plaques was not provided, but target loading of 0.08% w/w in the polymer was declared. The test specimens were brought into contact with 10% ethanol (simulant A), 3% acetic acid (simulant B) and olive oil (simulant D2). They were totally immersed for 10 days at 60°C at a surface/volume ratio of 6.19 dm2/L. Prior to analysis, the simulants 10% ethanol and 3% acetic acid were diluted 1:1 with isopropanol. Olive oil was extracted with ethanol before analysis. Ultra performance liquid chromatography (UPLC) with triple quadrupole detection (QQQ) operated in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode was used to analyse the substance. It was shown that the applied retention time window covers all six isomers.

In all food simulants, the migration of the substance was below the limits of quantification (LOQ) of 0.05 mg/kg food, which were set at the lowest calibration point. That into olive oil was estimated between 0.023 and 0.031 mg/kg. The Panel noted that, due to the low solubility of the substance in water, the migration into the aqueous food simulants is expected to be substantially lower than the levels in olive oil.

Specific migration of the impurities

Concerning the composition of the migrated species, the analytical method described in Section 3.1.3.1 covered the total of all six stereoisomers, but it can be assumed from the initial compositional pattern that only the main isomer, the all‐cis isomer (ccc′), may migrate in detectable amounts. Considering that trans isomers are present at up to 2%, their possible migration can be anticipated below 0.0006 mg/kg food.

The migration of the other impurities was calculated by the applicant considering the maximum residual level of each impurity measured either in the commercial batches or in the worst‐case batch as reported in Table 1 and assuming 100% migration from the final article. For this calculation, the following parameters and scenarios were used: (i) intended maximum use level of the substance in PP at 0.08% w/w; (ii) polymer density 0.9 g/cm3; (iii) contact ratio 6 dm2/kg food; (iv) for total mass transfer calculations, relevant polymer thicknesses of 520, 1700 or 5000 μm (JRC, 2016) were assigned to the particular molecular weight of the impurity from which exhaustive migration can be assumed at test conditions 10 days/60°C. For two impurities, AIPA and TEA, further refinements were made by the applicant, based on overconservative quantification or volatilisation during the two heat processing steps of the polymer material production, respectively. Toxicological data 14

The migration of the substance was up to 0.031 mg/kg food. Therefore, in accordance with the EFSA Note for Guidance (EFSA AFC Panel, 2008), the applicant submitted only data on its genotoxic potential: a bacterial reverse mutation assay (Ames test) and an in vitro mammalian cell micronucleus test, following the EFSA Scientific opinion on genotoxicity testing strategies applicable to food and feed safety assessment (EFSA Scientific Committee, 2011). In addition, an in vitro mammalian chromosome aberration test and an in vivo mammalian erythrocyte micronucleus test were provided.

The migration of 14 impurities was estimated using total mass transfer; individual results were all below 0.05 mg/kg food. Possible migration of the eight impurities 1–6, 12 and 13 would not exceed 0.13 μg/kg food, leading to a potential exposure below the threshold of toxicological concerns (TTC) for genotoxic carcinogens of 0.0025 μg/kg body weight per day. Toluene (No. 14) was assessed with literature data, while the remaining four impurities (Nos. 7–10) were assessed with in silico data.

Genotoxicity potential of the substance

In all four genotoxicity studies submitted by the applicant and described in the sections below, the concentration, homogeneity and stability of the test item formulations were not determined. This constitutes a GLP deviation as it may impact on the tested or administered doses and, hence, the results. However, this deviation was justified by the study directors and reported in the respective study reports for three out of the four studies. For the in vivo mammalian erythrocyte micronucleus test, this GLP exempted phase was not mentioned in the GLP compliance statement, but the information on characterisation of the test item in the vehicle was provided in the section that describes the test item formulation. The Panel noted that fresh formulations (within either 2 or 4 h for in vitro tests or on each day, before in vivo dosing) were prepared in each experiment following the sponsor's recommendation. In addition, the Panel considered that the OECD Test Guideline (TG) recommendations regarding the testing of insoluble concentrations were followed. Given that precipitation occurred at the highest concentrations tested in vitro, the test system was exposed to the highest concentration that could be reached. In conclusion, the Panel considered that the referred observations did not compromise the reliability of the results obtained and did not affect the integrity of the studies.

Moreover, the Panel noted that the genotoxicity tests were carried out with two batches reported as different from the commercial ones by the applicant. However, one batch (used for the in vitro and the in vivo mammalian cell micronucleus tests) showed a similar purity and impurity profile as well as trans isomer content to the commercial lots, and analytical data were within the provided specifications. The other (used for the Ames and the in vitro mammalian chromosome aberration test) contained a higher level of trans isomers (ca. 10%) and a correspondingly lower level of the ccc′ isomer (ca. 90%), as compared to the commercial lots (ccc′, ≥ 98% w/w). Due to the low solubility of the substance, the highest concentration for testing ccc′ was reached, so the 10% of the trans isomers is not expected to affect the study results for the substance (ccc′).

Bacterial reverse mutation assay

The Ames test was performed following the OECD Test Guideline (TG) 471 (OECD, 2020) and in compliance with GLP principles. The tested batch (SG1393) was not a commercial one and consisted of approximately 90% of the substance (ccc′ isomer), 10% of trans isomers and 0.26% of other process‐related impurities. It was dissolved in dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO) and tested within 4 h in the bacterial reverse mutation assay (Ames test) using Salmonella Typhimurium strains TA98, TA100, TA1535, TA1537 and Escherichia coli WP2 uvrA.

A plate incorporation assay and a pre‐incubation assay were carried out in the absence and presence of metabolic activation by phenobarbital/β‐naphthoflavone‐induced rat liver S9 fraction (S9 mix). For the plate incorporation assay, the selected concentrations were 1.5, 5, 15, 50, 150, 500, 1500 or 5000 μg/plate, and for the pre‐incubation assay, it was 15, 50, 150, 500, 1500 or 5000 μg/plate. Negative (vehicle) and strain‐specific positive controls were added to both experiments, as recommended. All treatments were done using triplicate cultures.

In both assays, no cytotoxicity was noted, as assessed by the background growth of the bacteria in the plates, irrespectively of the strain and treatment condition. Precipitation of the test substance was observed at and above 500 μg/plate, with and without metabolic activation. Precipitation did not affect the scoring of revertant colonies. No concentration‐related increase and no duplication of the mean number of revertant colonies for any tested concentration was reported for any of the bacteria strains, in the presence or absence of metabolic activation. The numbers of revertant colonies on both vehicle and positive control plates were within the laboratory historical control ranges.

The results of the test are considered negative, indicating that under the test conditions, the test substance is not mutagenic. According to the EFSA's ‘Harmonised approach for reporting reliability and relevance of genotoxicity studies’ (EFSA, 2023a, 2023b), the Panel considered the study reliable without restrictions and the results of high relevance.

In vitro mammalian cell micronucleus test

The in vitro mammalian cell micronucleus test using the TK6 human lymphoblastoid cell line was performed following the OECD TG 487 (OECD, 2023) and in compliance with GLP principles. The used batch (MLKN0100123585) was not a commercial one and differed from the one used in the Ames test. It consisted of 99.85% of the ccc′ isomer, 0.15% of trans isomers and other impurities present at low ppm level, dissolved in DMSO.

TK6 cells were exposed to the test item for a short (3 h) period in the absence and in the presence of metabolic activation by S9 mix and for a continuous (24 h) period without metabolic activation; duplicate cultures were used for each exposure condition. No cytotoxicity was noted for any of the tested conditions in a preliminary range‐finding experiment and the selection of the concentration‐range was limited by the solubility of the substance. The test item was evaluated up to the lowest concentration at which precipitation was visible in cultures, corresponding to 29.63 μg/mL and 19.75 μg/mL for the 3‐h and 24‐h treatments, respectively. Three lower concentrations were additionally included for each treatment schedule: 5.85, 13.17 and 19.75 μg/mL for the 3‐h treatment (with and without S9 mix) and 3.90, 8.78 and 13.17 μg/mL for the 24‐h treatment. Micronucleated cells were scored in a total of 2000 mononuclear cells (1000 cells per culture) for all test item treatments, vehicle and positive controls. The cytotoxicity was measured through the determination of the relative population doubling time (RPD).

No or low cytotoxicity was observed for the highest concentrations tested for micronuclei, at the end of the 3‐h incubation time (26.49% and −0.30% with and without S9 treatment, respectively) and at the end of the 24‐h incubation time (9.46%). For the highest concentrations tested, precipitation of the test item was noted at the end of both treatment periods. No significant differences were observed in the frequency of micronucleated cells between treated cultures and the vehicle control in any treatment conditions. The frequencies of micronucleated cells for negative and positive controls were within the historical control limits.

The results of the test were considered negative, indicating that the test item does not induce clastogenic or aneugenic effects under the test conditions. The Panel considered the study reliable without restrictions and the results of high relevance.

In vivo mammalian erythrocyte micronucleus test

The test was conducted following the OECD TG 474 (OECD, 2016a, 2016b) and in compliance with GLP principles. The test item corresponded to the batch tested in the in vitro micronucleus assay (Section 3.2.1.2), consisting of 99.85% of the ccc′ isomer.

Groups of five male Swiss albino mice were exposed by gavage to the test substance dissolved in corn oil at dose levels of 0, 500, 1000 or 2000 mg/kg body weight (bw) for two consecutive days, at 24‐h interval. Given the absence of sex‐related toxicity in the preliminary toxicity test, only males were treated in the main experiment; the maximum recommended dose of 2000 mg/kg bw was reached. The solubility characteristics of the test item limited the concentration of the formulation in corn oil and, therefore, split dosing (2–2.5 h apart) was performed for the vehicle and test item groups. A concurrent vehicle control group of five male mice received 10 mL/kg of corn oil and a positive control group of five male mice received cyclophosphamide monohydrate at the dose of 15 mg/kg bw. Blood samples were collected 47 h after the final administration. The frequency of micronucleated polychromatic erythrocytes (MNPCE) was determined by flow cytometry analysis of approximately 20,000 PCE per animal. In addition, the proportion of PCE relative to the total erythrocyte (TE) count was assessed for each animal, as a measure of the potential toxicity of the test item.

No clinical signs of toxicity and no bone marrow cytotoxicity, as measured by a reduction in the PCE/TE ratio, were observed in the groups of treated animals compared with the vehicle control group. The mean frequency of MNPCE was not significantly different between any of the groups of treated mice and the concurrent vehicle control group.

According to the evaluation criteria of the OECD TG 474, to consider a test substance as clearly negative and the results as relevant, in addition to the lack of micronucleated PCE induction at the maximum recommended or applicable dose, bone marrow exposure must be demonstrated. Without confirmation of bone marrow exposure, the results are inconclusive because potential genotoxic effects at other body sites (e.g. the site of first contact) cannot be ruled out. In the present study, given that neither evidence of bone marrow toxicity nor any other proof of systemic exposure was reported, the negative micronuclei results cannot be considered as demonstrating a lack of clastogenic or aneugenic activity of the test item. The Panel considered the study reliable without restrictions and the inconclusive results of low relevance.

However, based on the negative results obtained in the in vitro micronucleus test (see Section 3.2.1.2), the Panel concluded that no in vivo clastogenic/aneugenic activity is anticipated.

In vitro mammalian chromosome aberration test

The test was conducted following the OECD TG 473 (OECD, 2016a, 2016b) and in compliance with GLP principles. The same batch used in the Ames test was used and consisted of approximately 90% of the substance (ccc′ isomer), 10% of trans isomers and 0.26% of other impurities.

The test item was dissolved in DMSO and evaluated for the induction of chromosome aberrations in human lymphocytes in a range of concentrations selected in a preliminary toxicity test, based on mitotic index (MI) inhibition. The selected concentration range was limited by the solubility of the test item and, for each treatment condition, the highest concentration considered for chromosome aberration analysis corresponded to the lowest precipitating concentration, as observed at the end of the exposure period; no cytotoxicity was observed in the range‐finding experiment. In the short treatment, cells were exposed for 4 h to the test substance at concentrations of 4, 8 and 16 μg/mL in the absence of metabolic activation, and to 2, 4 and 8 μg/mL in the presence of phenobarbital/β‐naphthoflavone‐induced rat liver S9 fraction (S9 mix), followed by a 20‐h recovery period. In the continuous treatment, cells were exposed for 24 h, without S9 mix, to 8, 16 and 32 μg/mL of the test substance. Concurrent vehicle and positive (mitomycin C or cyclophosphamide) controls were added. Whenever possible, for each treatment condition, 300 consecutive metaphases (150 per duplicate culture) were scored for the quantification of chromosome aberrations, and 600 were scored for the solvent control, unless 15 cells with aberrations (excluding gaps) were firstly reached. Polyploid cells were additionally counted.

The test item did not induce cytotoxicity in any condition tested. Precipitation at the end of the incubation period was noted at the highest concentration used for each condition. The frequency of cells with structural chromosomal aberration and polyploidy in treated cultures was not significantly different from negative controls in any treatment condition and, thereby, no concentration‐related increases were observed. The frequencies of chromosomal aberrations in negative and positive controls were within the historical limits.

The results of the study were considered negative, i.e. the test item did not induce the formation of structural chromosomal aberrations and polyploidy under the tested conditions. The Panel considered the study reliable without restrictions and the results of high relevance.

Conclusions on the genotoxic potential of the substance

The Panel concluded that the available results of the in vitro studies allow to rule out concern for genotoxicity of 1,3‐Benzenedicarboxamide, 5‐[[[cis‐4‐(1,1‐dimethylethyl)cyclohexyl]carbonyl]amino]‐N1,N3‐bis[cis‐4‐(1‐methylethyl)cyclohexyl]‐.

Impurities

With regard to the trans stereoisomers, their physico‐chemical properties and toxicological profiles were assessed by the applicant, comparatively to those of the substance (ccc′), using the OECD QSAR Toolbox. Based on these predictions, the Panel concluded that trans isomers do not raise concern for genotoxicity.

In silico data from the OECD QSAR Toolbox and Leadscope were provided for four impurities (Nos. 7–10): cTBCHA, ■■■■■, ■■■■■, ■■■■■. The in silico assessment of the molecules indicated no alerts for genotoxicity.

Regarding toluene genotoxicity, literature data and other available information have generally reported negative results. The EPA – U.S. Environmental Protection Agency/IRIS – Integrated Risk Information System Summary (2005) summarises the findings from several in vitro mutation assays and chromosome aberration studies in vitro, and in vivo, and also data on micronuclei analysed in human lymphocytes of exposed workers, mainly reporting negative results. Overall, the available data indicate that toluene is not genotoxic.

DISCUSSION

The substance is a powder and will dissolve into the PP during the formulation and subsequent processing of the plastic. Therefore, no additional assessment for the presence of a fraction of small particles is needed.

The substance is the all‐cis stereoisomer (ccc′, ≥ 98% w/w), and its migration into the tested food simulants was below the respective LOQs (0.05 mg/kg food) that were set at the lowest calibration points. The Panel considered the negative results of the available in vitro genotoxicity studies as highly relevant for the assessment of the substance (ccc′) and concluded that the substance does not raise concern for genotoxicity.

The migration of the trans isomers (present below 2%) is below 0.0006 mg/kg food, hence well below 0.05 mg/kg food. Based on in silico data, the Panel concluded that they do not raise concern for genotoxicity.

The Panel considered the provided list of impurities to be complete and did not expect other reaction or degradation products. Impurities 15–18 are authorised without restrictions; impurity 11 is authorised with an SML of 60 mg/kg food, and its migration remains below this limit. Hence, it does not raise safety concerns. Possible migration of impurities 1–6, 12 and 13 would not exceed 0.13 μg/kg food, leading to a potential exposure below the threshold of toxicological concerns (TTC) for genotoxic carcinogens of 0.0025 μg/kg body weight per day. Based on in silico and literature data, the Panel concluded that impurities 7–10 and 14 do not raise a concern for genotoxicity.

The applicant excluded the use of the final PP articles in contact with infant formula, human milk and mineral water that could be used to reconstitute infant formula. However, the substance is intended to be used to manufacture bottles that could, in principle, come into contact with drinking water that could be used to reconstitute infant formula. The available toxicological data do not cover food for infants below 16 weeks of age. Thus, in principle, contact with water is not covered by the assessment. However, considering the limited solubility of the substance in water (33 ng/L), the migration of the substance arising from the use in bottles in contact with water is expected to be well below 3 μg/kg food. Potential migration of the impurities for which the total mass transfer was above 3 μg/kg food (ref SC Op) was estimated by modelling to be below 3 μg/kg food under foreseeable uses (see Section 3.1.3). Therefore, the Panel anticipated that, if the situation were to occur, the use of the substance in bottles does not raise a safety concern, including bottles in contact with water used to reconstitute infant formula.

CONCLUSIONS

The FCM Panel concluded that the substance 1,3‐benzenedicarboxamide, 5‐[[[cis‐4‐(1,1‐dimethylethyl)cyclohexyl]carbonyl]amino]‐N1,N3‐bis[cis‐4‐(1‐methylethyl)cyclohexyl]‐ is not of safety concern for the consumers if:

the substance is used at up to 0.08% w/w in polypropylene homo‐ and co‐polymers; and

the substance is used at up to 0.08% w/w in polypropylene homo‐ and co‐polymers; and

the manufactured materials and articles are for single and repeated use, used in contact up to and above 6 months at room temperature and below, including hot‐fill conditions and/or heating up to 70°C ≤ T ≤ 100°C for maximum t = 120/2^((T − 70)/10) min; and

the manufactured materials and articles are for single and repeated use, used in contact up to and above 6 months at room temperature and below, including hot‐fill conditions and/or heating up to 70°C ≤ T ≤ 100°C for maximum t = 120/2^((T − 70)/10) min; and

the manufactured materials and articles are used in contact with all types of food, excluding beverages with ethanol content above 10%, infant formula including reconstituted infant formula, and human milk.
