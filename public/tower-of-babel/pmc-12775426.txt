Association of post-liposuction edema on body composition and weight reduction efficiency
Authors: Seoyoung Park, Jiyoung Hwang, Selin Woo, Jaewon Kim, Sunyoung Kim, Ho Geol Woo, Jeongeun Kim, Kyuhee Chae, Dong Keon Yon, Hyunji Sang, Sang Youl Rhee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Edema following liposuction can obscure surgical outcomes and increase the risk of complications, emphasizing the need for quantitative evaluation. This study aimed to evaluate the association between postoperative edema severity and changes in weight and body composition, as well as to assess fat reduction efficiency across different edema severity groups in patients undergoing local anesthetic minimally invasive liposuction (LAMS). This study analyzed multicenter cohort data collected in 2024 from 20 branches of the 365MC Hospital in South Korea, including 3832 patients who underwent LAMS. Postoperative edema severity was assessed using a standardized self-report questionnaire and patients were classified into three groups: no edema, mild, and moderate to severe edema. The primary outcome was postoperative changes in body weight and total body water (TBW), using bioelectrical impedance analysis. The Kruskal-Wallis and Dunn’s tests were used to compare changes across edema groups, while multivariable linear regression evaluated associations between aspirated fat volume and postoperative outcomes. Weight reduction efficiency (per 50 cc of aspirated fat) was also assessed. Subgroup analyses were conducted by seven anatomical regions: abdomen, arms, back, buttocks, calves, flanks, and thighs. Of the 3832 patients (3825 females [99.82%]), the majority were classified into the mild edema group (90.34%), followed by moderate to severe (5.61%) and no edema (4.04%). Aspirated fat volume was associated with reductions in body weight across all groups, although the strength of this association declined with increasing edema severity, from an adjusted β of -0.06 (95% CI, -0.07 to -0.05) kg/cc in the no edema group to − 0.05 (-0.09 to -0.01) kg/cc in the moderate to severe group. In contrast, TBW showed the weakest and non-significant association, suggesting that postoperative fluid retention may obscure the weight-reducing effect of aspirated fat. Regional analysis indicated that the buttocks showed the highest efficiency (-11.41 [95% CI, -26.76 to 3.93] kg/50 cc in the no edema group; -5.89 [-17.64 to 5.87] kg/50 cc in moderate to severe), while the thighs showed the lowest (-5.74 [-9.82 to -1.68] kg/50 cc and -2.02 [-3.85 to 0.19] kg/50 cc, respectively). This study investigates the potential association of edema severity on postoperative body composition changes and fat reduction efficiency following LAMS. These findings highlight the clinical impact of edema on surgical outcomes and the importance of tailored postoperative management to enhance recovery and patient satisfaction.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-29903-5.

Introduction

Liposuction is a widely performed cosmetic surgical procedure to remove localized fat deposits and enhance body contours1. According to the International Society of Aesthetic Plastic Surgery (ISAPS), liposuction consistently ranks among the top five global cosmetic procedures, with over 5.1 million body contouring surgeries, including liposuction, performed in 20232. Despite its efficacy, liposuction can lead to various complications3. Hematomas are the most common, but edema remains an unavoidable consequence due to fluid infiltration4,5. Edema is a normal physiological response to trauma from cannula use, typically peaking within 4 days and subsiding over 4 to 6 weeks. Mild edema may persist for up to six months and can increase the risk of contour deformities and complications such as skin necrosis6.

While previous studies have described general postoperative outcomes following liposuction, few have critically examined how variations in edema severity affect specific clinical outcomes, such as body composition changes or surgical efficiency7,8. These studies have primarily relied on descriptive analyses, lacking rigorous quantitative evaluations9. Additionally, body composition measurements, including body fat mass (BFM) and total body water (TBW), immediately after surgery can be distorted by edema and anatomical characteristics, potentially underestimating the true effect of the procedure. Given these limitations, more precise, quantitative analyses of surgical efficiency by anatomical region and edema severity are needed to accurately interpret patient outcomes and guide optimal postoperative management strategies.

To address these gaps, this study aimed to evaluate the impact of postoperative edema severity on clinical outcomes following liposuction, focusing on body composition changes and surgical efficiency. Using a large multicenter cohort from 20 clinics of 365MC Hospital, all treated with a standardized local anesthetic minimally invasive liposuction (LAMS) technique, we examined how edema severity influences postoperative body composition across anatomical regions. By incorporating precise measures of surgical efficiency and accounting for variations in edema severity, this study provides a clearer understanding of the clinical implications of postoperative edema and contributes to refining postoperative management strategies. Based on these considerations, we hypothesized greater edema severity to attenuate reductions in body weight and total body water and to alter weight-reduction efficiency across anatomical sites.

Methods

Patient and data collection

We analyzed anonymized and de-identified data from a multicenter cohort of liposuction procedures conducted in 2024 across 20 branches of 365MC liposuction hospitals in South Korea10. Among the initial 4182 patients (7046 cases), we excluded 343 patients (622 cases) due to missing data, 7 patients (10 cases) with a fat removal volume less than 50 cc (1 bottle), and those without recorded postoperative edema information. After applying these criteria, 3832 patients (6414 cases) were included in the final analysis (Figure S1). Missingness was assessed for each variable, and since no variable had 30% or more missing values, multiple imputation by chained equations was applied to address missing data and minimize potential bias.

The research protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Kyung Hee University Medical Center (KHUH 2024-04-002). The requirement for informed consent was waived by the Institutional Review Board of Kyung Hee University Medical Center because de-identified data were used in the analyses. This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was reported in accordance with the STrengthening the Reporting of OBservational studies in Epidemiology guidelines11.

Local anesthetic minimal-invasive liposuction

LAMS, or local anesthetic minimally invasive liposuction, is a proprietary technique developed by 365MC Hospital. This procedure is performed without general anesthesia or intravenous sedation, utilizing only local anesthesia and requiring no surgical incisions. Fat is removed through small puncture sites using a low-pressure LockBar syringe and a manual cannula, minimizing tissue disruption. The technique involves tunneling to break down fibrous septa within the adipose layer, followed by aspiration of liquefied fat. A tumescent solution is administered to aid in fat emulsification, minimize bleeding, and reduce patient discomfort. The standard formulation consists of 1 L of 0.45% normal saline, 1 cc of epinephrine, 16 cc of lidocaine, and 20 cc of sodium bicarbonate. Although the proportion of tumescent fluid and fat varies by region and patient factors, each 50 cc syringe contains approximately 50% fat on average. The total infiltration volume is not fixed but is adjusted according to the size of the treated region and the thickness of the subcutaneous fat layer. LAMS follows a functional endpoint approach in which tumescent solution is infused until adequate tumescence and hydrodissection are achieved, rather than using a predetermined volume for all patients. As a result, infiltration amounts are not recorded individually in the electronic medical record.

Treatment indications and extent of intervention

Before the procedure, patients undergo an in-person consultation conducted by clinicians, during which regional adiposity is evaluated using circumferential measurements, visual inspection, and palpation to assess subcutaneous fat thickness. Based on this assessment, together with the patient’s contouring goals and the absence of contraindications to tumescent anesthesia, a standardized treatment package is selected. These packages correspond to predefined anatomical regions and establish the clinical indication and target area for LAMS. The extent of the intervention is also standardized: LAMS uses a bottle-based system in which aspirated fat is collected in 50 cc syringes (“bottles”), and the scope of treatment follows a uniform protocol across centers. After package selection, the electronic medical record system automatically assigns each case to one of seven representative anatomical regions (abdomen, arms, back, buttocks, calves, flanks, and thighs), and all region-level analyses in this study were conducted based on this standardized mapping.

Assessment of postoperative edema

Postoperative edema was assessed using a subjective, self-report method implemented through the 365MC mobile application. Patients received a push notification following the procedure prompting them to report their current level of swelling in response to the question, “Do you currently have edema?” Responses were recorded using a three-point ordinal scale: “No edema,” “Mild swelling,” and “Moderate to severe swelling causing functional impairment.”9 This assessment was administered uniformly to all surgical patients, with identical questions and response options provided. Any unusual or concerning cases regarding edema were additionally documented in the nursing records as separate comments. These responses were categorized into three groups for analysis: no edema, mild, and moderate to severe edema for analysis12. No further follow-up assessments were conducted.

Outcome of interest

The primary outcomes were postoperative changes in body weight and TBW. Additional body composition parameters, including SMM and BFM, were assessed as secondary outcomes. All measures were obtained using bioelectrical impedance analysis with the InBody 370S (InBody Co., Seoul, South Korea)13. Each parameter was measured twice: once on the day of the procedure and again at a mean follow-up of 3.82 weeks (0.88 months). Weight, SMM, and BFM were recorded in kilograms, and TBW was recorded in liters. Changes were calculated as the difference between postoperative and preoperative measurements (Δ = postoperative - preoperative) and compared across three edema severity groups. To further evaluate surgical efficiency, weight reduction per 50 cc of aspirated fat (kg/50 cc) was calculated for each body region and edema group. Analyses were conducted both overall and stratified by the seven anatomical regions. These outcomes were selected as the primary endpoints because they directly align with the study aim and reflect our expectation that greater edema severity would mitigate observable postoperative reductions.

Statistical analyses

Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables as means with standard deviations (SD). The Anderson-Darling test was used to assess the normality of outcome variables (Table S1). Based on the results, the Kruskal-Wallis test was applied to compare differences across edema severity groups (Table S2). For variables with significant group differences, post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using Dunn’s test with adjusted p-values. Prior to multivariable linear regression, variance inflation factors (VIFs) were calculated to assess multicollinearity, and covariates were selected accordingly14. Regression models were then fitted to examine associations between aspirated fat volume and postoperative outcomes by edema group. Results were reported using R², regression coefficients (β), p-values, and 95% confidence intervals (CIs)15. All analyses were conducted using SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) and Python (version 3.12.4; Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE, USA).

Results

Patients’ characteristics

A total of 3832 patients (3825 females [99.82%] and 7 males [0.18%]) who underwent LAMS were included in the analysis, as detailed in Table 1. The mean age of the overall cohort was 34.43 (SD = 8.41) years, and the mean BMI was 22.92 (2.74) kg/m² at baseline. The mean aspirated fat volume was 17.08 (SD = 8.13) cc overall, and was highest in the moderate to severe edema group at 20.56 (7.76) cc, compared to 14.04 (8.49) cc in the no edema group. BFM was also higher in the moderate to severe group at 20.53 (SD = 5.62) kg, compared to 19.69 (5.46) kg in the no edema group, while SMM did not differ substantially across groups.

Edema-stratified changes in postoperative body composition

Table 2 summarizes postoperative body composition changes according to edema severity. Across the cohort, the most frequently treated surgical sites were the arms (n = 1814, 47.34%), followed by the abdomen and thighs (n = 805, 21.01% for each). Postoperative reductions in body weight, TBW, and BFM varied notably across edema groups, as illustrated in Fig. 1A. For both body weight and TBW, the magnitude of postoperative reduction decreased as edema severity increased. Specifically, mean weight reduction was -1.49 (SD = 1.85) kg in the no edema group, compared to -1.15 (2.23) kg in the moderate to severe group. A similar trend was observed in TBW, with mean changes of -0.29 (SD = 0.97) L and -0.01 (1.10) L, respectively. These group-level differences were statistically significant (Fig. 1B and Table S3). In contrast, BFM decreased by -1.07 (SD = 1.61) kg in the no edema group and -1.11 (1.76) kg in the moderate to severe group, although the difference between groups was not significant (Fig. 2).

Association between aspirated fat volume and postoperative outcomes by edema severity

Multicollinearity was assessed prior to model fitting, and all VIFs were confirmed to be below 2, indicating stable covariate structure (Table S4). To evaluate the association between aspirated fat volume and postoperative body composition, multivariable linear regression analyses were then conducted within each edema severity group, adjusting for age, sex, height, branch, attending physician, and surgical site in (Fig. 2 and Table S5). Aspirated fat volume was significantly associated with reductions in body weight across all edema groups, although the strength of this association decreased with increasing edema severity, from the no edema group (adjusted β = -0.06 [95% CI, -0.07 to -0.05] kg/cc) to the moderate-to-severe group (-0.05 [-0.09 to -0.01] kg/cc). Notably, TBW showed the weakest association, which was not statistically significant. This attenuation indicates that postoperative fluid retention in severe edema may obscure the weight-reducing effect of aspirated fat. In the moderate to severe group, no significant associations were observed for other body composition measures.

Postoperative weight reduction efficiency per aspirated fat volume

Table 3; Fig. 3 present weight reduction efficiency, defined as postoperative body weight change (kg) per 50 cc of aspirated fat, stratified by anatomical site and edema severity. The buttocks consistently exhibited the greatest efficiency across all edema groups, with reductions of -11.41 (95% CI, -26.76 to 3.93) kg/50 cc in the no edema group, -11.18 (-19.87 to -2.48) kg/50 cc in the mild group, and -5.89 (-17.64 to 5.87) kg/50 cc in the moderate to severe group, despite relatively small aspirated volumes. The abdomen indicated the second highest efficiency, while the thighs, which had the largest aspirated volumes (19.39 [SD = 9.23] cc in the mild group), showed markedly lower efficiency, ranging from -5.74 (95% CI, -9.82 to -1.68) kg/50 cc in the no edema group to -2.02 (-3.85 to -0.19) kg/50 cc in the moderate to severe group. This inverse relationship between aspirated volume and efficiency suggests that greater fat removal, particularly from the thighs, may not translate into proportional weight reduction.

Discussion

Key findings

This multicenter cohort study of 3832 patients undergoing LAMS showed that the severity of postoperative edema influenced body composition outcomes. With increasing edema severity, the magnitude of postoperative reductions in both body weight and TBW progressively declined. In analyses of associations with aspirated fat volume, body weight showed significant reductions across all groups, although the strength of this association diminished as edema severity increased. By contrast, TBW exhibited the weakest and non-significant association, suggesting that postoperative fluid retention in severe edema may obscure the physiological effects of fat removal. Furthermore, analysis of weight reduction efficiency revealed that the buttocks and abdomen achieved the greatest weight loss per unit of aspirated fat, whereas the thighs, despite receiving the largest aspirated volumes, presented the lowest efficiency. These findings likely reflect anatomical and physiological variability in the body’s response to liposuction.

Plausible underlying mechanisms and comparison with prior work

Liposuction, also referred to as suction lipectomy or lipoplasty, has become the most frequently performed aesthetic surgical procedure worldwide, particularly among female patients16. Among the various anatomical sites, the arms, abdomen, and thighs are most commonly targeted for fat reduction, as also observed in our multicenter cohort study across 20 centers. Despite its widespread use and refinement through modalities such as ultrasound- or laser-assisted liposuction17, the procedure remains susceptible to complications, including postoperative edema18. Edema results from cannula-induced trauma that disrupts adipose tissue and compromises lymphatic drainage, causing interstitial fluid accumulation9. This process leads to visible swelling and temporarily fills the dead space created by aspiration, potentially masking the immediate physiological effects of fat removal9. In line with these mechanisms, our findings underscore the importance of evaluating the extent to which edema severity influences the interpretation of postoperative body composition changes. By examining real-world data from a large longitudinal cohort, this study provides novel evidence linking edema severity to both measurable surgical outcomes and their potential misrepresentation in early postoperative assessments.

Our findings indicate that postoperative edema acts as a key modifier of observable changes in body composition following liposuction-assisted procedures. In the no edema group, aspirated fat volume was consistently associated with significant reductions in body weight, TBW, and BFM, suggesting that when fluid retention is minimal, surgical effects are more accurately reflected in compositional metrics19,20. In contrast, the moderate to severe edema group exhibited no significant changes in body composition despite having the highest mean fat removal. Notably, TBW changes were minimal across all groups. These results support the hypothesis that early postoperative fluid retention and interstitial accumulation can distort the physiological signals of fat loss21,22. This interpretation is further reinforced by regression models, which showed reduced explanatory power of fat volume on body composition change in the presence of higher edema severity. Physiologically, postoperative edema is triggered by lymphatic vessel damage and increased capillary permeability, both of which impair fluid clearance and delay recovery23.

The thighs, despite receiving the largest aspirated fat volumes, showed the lowest weight reduction efficiency, whereas the buttocks and abdomen exhibited higher efficiency even with moderate fat removal. This regional variability may reflect anatomical and physiological differences in fibrous density, lymphatic drainage, and interstitial fluid dynamics24. The thighs are characterized by dense fibrous septa and relatively sluggish lymphatic drainage, which can promote persistent interstitial fluid accumulation and attenuate the visible effects of fat removal25. Moreover, extensive liposuction in this region may disrupt lymphatic vessels, delaying fluid clearance until collateral pathways or lymph angiogenesis compensate26. In contrast, the abdomen and buttocks have more favorable tissue architecture and efficient lymphatic circulation, facilitating faster fluid resolution and more apparent compositional changes27. These mechanisms underscore the importance of interpreting postoperative outcomes with caution, as regional fluid dynamics can mask the true physiological impact of fat removal. Nonetheless, as several CIs crossed zero, the results should be interpreted with caution, likely reflecting both regional differences and the limited sample sizes, highlighting the need for larger studies to establish more definitive conclusions.

Clinical and policy implications

Postoperative edema is a key determinant of how early outcomes following liposuction-assisted micro-cannula suction are perceived and evaluated. Edema can obscure the actual physiological effect of fat removal, particularly in low-efficiency regions such as the thighs, where fluid retention may attenuate visible changes. Therefore, clinicians may need to interpret early changes in weight or body composition with caution. Early management strategies such as compression therapy, manual lymphatic drainage, cryotherapy, and kinesiology taping may facilitate fluid clearance and improve the accuracy of postoperative evaluation9,28. The site-specific efficiency data from this study can help surgeons set realistic, region-tailored expectations, select anatomical targets with the greatest yield per aspirated volume, and explain to patients why visible results may be delayed in the presence of significant edema. Incorporating these approaches into routine practice may enhance patient communication, support individualized care, and improve satisfaction.

The observed variation in surgical efficiency and edema-related distortion suggests the potential value for standardized postoperative care protocols that reflect anatomical and physiological diversity. Previous studies also highlight how edema and surgical technique can influence both outcomes and measurement accuracy29,30. Building initiatives from organizations such as the ISAPS highlight the importance of surgeon education to enhance patient safety and improve surgical outcomes2,31. These programs offer evidence-based training on complication mitigation and recovery optimization. Developing globally aligned standards for edema-sensitive postoperative care would help reduce variability in outcomes, improve patient communication, and provide consistent benchmarks for quality assurance in aesthetic surgery2,32–34.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, postoperative edema was assessed using a subjective, patient-reported three-level scale, which may introduce perception-related variability and recall bias. Although this scale was applied uniformly across all patients within the 365MC network and consistently recorded at the standardized postoperative follow-up visit, it does not represent a validated patient-reported outcome measure such as the BODY-Q35. Second, reliance on subjective patient-reported measures rather than objective modalities (e.g., ultrasound or MRI) may limit the precision and reproducibility of edema quantification36–39. Third, edema was evaluated at a single postoperative time point, which precluded assessment of temporal patterns or resolution trajectories. Fourth, missing data necessitated the use of multiple imputation, introducing the possibility of residual and selection bias. Fifth, the observed associations between edema severity and postoperative outcomes may be confounded by unmeasured variables. For example, individual inflammatory responses, pre-existing lymphatic dysfunction, adherence to postoperative care protocols, or undocumented comorbidities could independently influence both edema severity and recovery trajectories. Finally, although this multicenter cohort included 20 clinics, all clinics were within a single national healthcare system in South Korea, and racial or ethnic data were not collected. Moreover, because the cohort was overwhelmingly female (99.82%), with fewer than ten male patients and none in the moderate-to-severe edema group, sex-stratified analyses were not feasible. Therefore, the generalizability of these findings to broader or more diverse populations is limited.

Conclusion

This study is the first to evaluate postoperative changes in body composition and weight reduction efficiency following liposuction in a large multicenter cohort treated with a standardized LAMS technique. The findings indicate that postoperative edema can obscure the physiological effects of fat removal, emphasizing the importance of assessing outcomes using body composition metrics such as TBW rather than weight alone. Given the regional variability in surgical efficiency, early outcomes should be interpreted with consideration of both edema severity and anatomical site. These insights may enhance patient counseling regarding delayed visible changes and support individualized, edema-informed postoperative management in aesthetic surgery.
