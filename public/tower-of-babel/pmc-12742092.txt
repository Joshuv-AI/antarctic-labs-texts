Are we ready for the feedback? A qualitative exploratory study on residents’ awareness and readiness for multisource (360°) feedback in medical residency
Authors: Sumera Nisar, Ayesha Jamal, Husna Irfan Thalib, Sariya Khan, Sayeeda Mehveen, Aseef Rehman, Ghala AlSughayyir, Ammar Yassir Bafarat, Hiba Hisham Alshikh Ali
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveIn the constantly advancing, dynamic and competitive fields of residency programmes, continuous professional development is crucial for fostering competent and well-rounded health professionals. Multisource (MS) (360°) feedback is widely adopted internationally as a comprehensive tool to assess residents’ performance, yet it is not commonly practised in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA). Therefore, this study aims to explore awareness and readiness among residents in KSA to receive 360° feedback.DesignA qualitative exploratory study using in-depth semistructured interviews.SettingSecondary and tertiary care hospitals in major cities, recognised for postgraduate training by the Saudi Commission for Health Specialties of KSA.ParticipantsFor the study, medical residents of diverse specialties were recruited by the convenience sampling method. Informed written consent for participation was taken. Audio recorded interviews data were transcribed, and a thematic analysis technique was used to extract the themes.ResultA total of 7 female and 2 male residents participated. The subthemes were categorised under the following major themes: lack of awareness of the concept, acknowledging the value of multisource feedback (MSF), receptivity/readiness and challenges.ConclusionThe study underscores the significant potential of MS (360°) feedback in fostering the personal and professional development of medical residents in KSA. Introduction

In the constantly advancing, dynamic and competitive field of residency programmes, continuous professional development is crucial to foster competent and well-rounded health professionals who can match the demands of this field successfully. An impactful improvement in the skills of the healthcare team requires a detailed review and feedback from the different stakeholders involved in the professional life of a resident. This can be accomplished by adopting an innovative approach gaining popularity in the medical field called multisource (MS) or 360° feedback. This approach offers a comprehensive and holistic view of a resident’s performance, incorporating feedback from various perspectives, including peers, supervisors, paramedic team and self-assessment. The 360° evaluation assesses a wide range of competencies beyond technical or clinical expertise. Communication, professionalism, teamwork, leadership, integrity, empathy, accountability and collaboration with peers, nursing staff and patients are some of the crucial elements of this multidimensional assessment, which helps identify not only how well residents perform clinical tasks, but also how effectively they engage in communication and collaboration within the healthcare team. These key attributes play an important role in influencing patient safety and the quality of care provided.1

Traditionally, evaluations were primarily conducted by supervisors or attending physicians. However, 360° feedback is a MS assessment tool that collects feedback from various stakeholders involved in a resident’s professional life, thereby broadening the evaluation scope by including input from peers, nursing staff and even patients. The 360° feedback approach provides a comprehensive and holistic overview of a resident’s abilities, skills and competencies by taking into consideration the evaluation of the superiors, colleagues, healthcare team members and patients. It is an effective and balanced method to evaluate an individual’s skills as it gathers information from the perceptions of everyone interacting with the resident and not just the supervisor. This approach not only increases the self-awareness of the resident but also contributes greatly to identifying areas of improvement, thereby contributing greatly to the resident’s overall development.2 Hence, it provides a more accurate and well-rounded representation of a resident’s strengths, weaknesses and overall performance.3 Self-assessment is also a component of the 360° feedback process.4 Residents gain insights into their own behaviours which can help them to actively work on enhancing their skills and addressing areas that may need improvement, thereby leading to both personal and professional development.5 6 In a medical setting, collaboration is essential for delivering high-quality patient care. 360° feedback fosters a culture of open communication and collaboration among residents.7 By involving peers and colleagues in the evaluation process, residents learn to appreciate diverse perspectives, develop effective communication skills and build stronger collaborative relationships within the healthcare team.8 9 The 360° feedback approach also helps to identify blind spots by shedding light on areas that might be overlooked in a single-source evaluation conducted by a supervisor.10

Despite its advantages, the 360° feedback has its own set of challenges. The process may lead to conflicts if the feedback is not delivered constructively, creating tension among team members. Moreover, the anonymity of responses may encourage overly critical or biased evaluations, reducing the tool’s effectiveness. Implementing this method also requires significant time, training and administrative resources, which may not be feasible in all healthcare settings with limited resources.4

In the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA), postgraduate medical education and training is governed by the Saudi Commission for Health Specialties (SCFHS), which sets the standards for residency training programmes. Currently, resident evaluations in the Kingdom primarily rely on supervisor assessments, in-training evaluation reports and performance in standardised exams.

While these methods provide a strong foundation for assessing knowledge and clinical skills, they may not comprehensively evaluate interpersonal competencies, teamwork and professionalism.3

A descriptive-analytic study conducted at the Shiraz University of Medical Sciences in Iran demonstrated the effectiveness of 360° feedback for evaluating ENT residents. The findings emphasised that comprehensive assessment should extend beyond supervisors to include diverse evaluators.6

Another descriptive study conducted at a tertiary care hospital in India evaluated interpersonal skills, communication skills and professionalism among 26 resident doctors from four different specialties using the 360° evaluation method. Residents were evaluated by teachers, peers, nursing staff, undergraduate students and patients using a 5-point Likert scale. The results indicated that while peers and consultants provided slightly lower ratings compared with other groups, overall there was good agreement among the evaluators. The mean scores for all skills showed an increasing trend over the years of residency. The study concluded that regular orientation programmes and feedback using the 360° evaluation method could bring about significant behavioural changes in residents.7

Similarly, a cross-sectional study conducted at the Aga Khan Medical University in Pakistan assessed communication and interpersonal skills among Internal Medicine residents using the 360° evaluation method. 49 residents were evaluated by eight stakeholders, including physicians, nurses and unit staff, alongside self-assessments. The study found significant differences in the scores provided by the various stakeholders, with self-assessment scores being significantly lower than those provided by faculty.8

This practice is widely adopted internationally to assess the performance of residents holistically.11,14 However, it is not a common practice in the KSA.15 Therefore, the present study aims to explore in-depth the awareness and readiness among the residents of KSA to undergo a 360-feedback.

Methodology

Study design and setting

A qualitative exploratory study was carried out among the medical residents of KSA to explore their awareness and readiness to undergo an MS (360°) feedback from December 2023 to June 2024. The residents were recruited from public and private hospitals from all major cities that provided residency training programmes for various specialties registered under the SCFHS. Residents from major cities of each of the four provinces of KSA, including Jeddah, Riyadh, Tabuk, Dammam and Al Baha, participated in the interviews.

Study subjects and sampling techniques

A total of 9 residents, 7 women and 2 men, underwent in-depth semistructured interviews for the study with each interview lasting for a duration of 45–50 min on average. The participants were enrolled through convenience sampling from various hospitals in different regions and from a variety of specialties registered under the SCFHS recognised for postgraduate training. Five participants were from the second year of their residency programme while others were from the first, third and fifth years. The mean age of the participants was 27 years. Participants from various specialties were enrolled to ensure a holistic perspective of different residency programmes. The demographic data of the participants are given in table 1.

Instrument development

The interview items were developed by AJ and SM following a comprehensive literature search, and 15 questions with accompanying probes were finalised. Prior to the interview, the participant’s demographics, for example, gender, age, year of residency and specialty, were noted. The interview items were categorised into two parts. The first part of the interview mainly consisted of five questions inquiring about the resident’s awareness of the multisource feedback (MSF). The second part of the interview consisted of questions assessing the readiness of the residents in-depth in addition to factors that may encourage or limit them to undergo the MSF. One pilot interview was conducted before proceeding to the main interviews to ensure the correct interpretation of the questions by the participant. The interview guide is provided in online supplemental file 2.

Data collection method

A total of nine interviews were conducted. Four members of the research team, AJ, HIT, SM and SK, conducted the interviews. The interview guide was shared with the participants by email a week before the interview. Due to the diverse location of the study participants from different regions of KSA, two interviews were conducted in person by AJ and HIT, while the rest of the seven interviews were conducted online through Google Meet and Zoom from (14 October 2023 to 25 March 2024). All interviews were conducted in English. They were recorded and transcribed by SK and GA. To ensure the anonymity and to maintain the confidentiality, a number was assigned to each participant in place of their names.

None of the participants withdrew their participation and consent. Total duration for each interview was about 45–50 min. Open-ended questions were asked from the participants to know about their thoughts on MSF. Field notes during the interview were collected manually and electronically and shared with the team members by email. Data collection was stopped when no new perspective was found (data saturation).

Data management and analysis

All recorded interviews were transcribed and organised by SK and GA. Thematic analysis was carried out by SN after initial familiarisation with the data. The initial familiarisation and exploration of the transcribed data used the inductive approach while working intensively with the data line by line.

Two cycles of open and axial coding were run by AJ, HIT, SK and SM under the supervision of SN. Considerable overlapping of codes was apparent, which was dealt with by merging them to arrange into overarching subthemes and themes. The initial draft of thematic analysis was shared and discussed with the team members for their agreement. GA, HHAA and AYB were involved in writing, reviewing and paraphrasing the manuscript. The final draft was concluded after mutual consensus of the team members (member check) and sent to three faculty members for peer debriefing to check personal and interpersonal reflexivity.

Patient and public involvement

Patient and/or public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

The study explored the awareness of MSF and readiness of medical residents to undergo this holistic evaluation. A total of 9 residents, 7 women and 2 men, from various specialties and diverse regions of KSA participated in the study as mentioned in table 1.

The study extracted four major themes with 31 subthemes accordingly. The subthemes were categorised under the following major themes: lack of awareness of the concept, acknowledging the value of MSF, receptivity and challenges. A comprehensive summary of the results is available as online supplemental file 1.

The first theme, ‘Lack of awareness of the concept’, indicates that medical residents were unfamiliar with the principles of MSF as indicated by phrases such as ‘It’s my first time hearing this term’, ‘Nothing comes to my mind when I hear the term 360°/MS feedback’. Within this theme, several subthemes were identified focusing on the ambiguity and uncertainties residents face in understanding and comprehending the concept of MSF process. Most of the participants were clueless about the goals of MSF and uncertain about the key stakeholders. They were also unacquainted with the competencies assessed through this feedback. The study findings reveal a lack of conceptual understanding among most medical residents regarding 360° feedback.

The second theme, ‘Acknowledging the value of MS feedback’, identifies medical residents’ recognition of the benefits and positive outcomes associated with engaging in MSF processes as indicated by phrases such as ‘It will help me in my overall professional and personal development and enhance the work environment’, ‘I think it will help me identify the hidden problems and my deficiencies’. Within this central theme, various subthemes emerged, reflecting the different ways in which residents perceive and appreciate the value of comprehensive feedback mechanisms in their professional development and workplace environment. Residents acknowledged the significance of receiving holistic feedback from multiple sources, including peers, supervisors and patients.

The third theme of ‘Receptivity’ examines the mindfulness of medical residents in KSA regarding the MS (360°) feedback concept, assessing their preparedness and willingness to incorporate this evaluation method into their training. Their readiness is reflected in sentiments like ‘I like the idea as it can help us reflect on our strengths and weaknesses’. Several subthemes emerged within this overarching theme, highlighting the various motivations behind the residents’ readiness to embrace this feedback process. Most residents perceive the evaluation as holistic, offering a comprehensive view of their performance.

The final theme, ‘Challenges’, addresses the setbacks and complexities involved in implementing the MS evaluation for medical residents. Residents expressed concerns about various factors, including time management issues, over-criticism and the perceived complexity due to the involvement of numerous stakeholders.

Discussion

In highly competitive training environments of residency programmes, professional development of residents extends beyond clinical knowledge and skills. The behaviours such as empathy, collaboration, teamwork and communications are all equally critical in improving the quality of patient care and enhancing residents’ professional skills. To access professional growth, feedback is an essential mean of providing information about the performance, progress, strengths and weaknesses of any process. Timely, meaningful, constructive and relevant feedback is an effective way to improve and promote engagement, motivation and outcome.16 The essence of MS (360°) feedback is to assess not only a resident’s clinical competence, but also a wider range of behaviours that define excellence in professional medical practice and directly impact patient care. While clinical proficiency is essential, a resident’s ability to interact constructively, communicate effectively and contribute to a collaborative work environment is equally vital for ensuring patient safety and high-quality care. Therefore, continuous professional development should combine clinical performance and interpersonal competencies. This is crucial in the process of clinical teaching and learning, as it motivates residents to improve during their clinical placements and enhances their learning experience. The main goal of providing this feedback is to bridge the gap between current and desired performance. Even though feedback is essential for improvement, residents often perceive it as biased and demotivating.17 The lack of awareness of the potential benefits of this invaluable tool further increases hostility against it.18 This can be addressed by enhancing feedback literacy to drive the maximum benefit from it and improve knowledge and skill.19 MSF provides a structured method to achieve this balance.20

The Accreditation Council of Graduate Medical Education developed a 360° feedback toolkit in anticipation to provide residents with holistic constructive feedback from multiple stakeholders working with and around them to know their shortcomings for further improvement.9 This MSF or evaluation helps the residents to know the perspective of all those working around and with him. Moreover, it enhances motivation for personal and professional development of the residents, improves interprofessional teamwork and builds raters’ confidence as a useful team member in improving the healthcare.14

This exploratory study comprehensively illustrates and assesses the awareness and readiness of medical residents in KSA to participate in MS (360°) feedback. The insights gained highlight both the potential benefits and the challenges of implementing this all-inclusive feedback method. The study identifies a general lack of awareness among residents about the concept of MSF. Despite the well-documented benefits of MS (360°) feedback in medical education, many residents were unfamiliar with its purpose, key stakeholders and the competencies it assesses. This lack of awareness can be attributed to the traditional evaluation systems predominantly used in KSA, where feedback is primarily provided by supervising consultants. Most of the study participants expressed uncertainty and confusion regarding the key stakeholders. Additionally, residents demonstrated uncertainty regarding the competencies assessed through 360° feedback.

The lack of awareness among the residents necessitates the implementation of key educational initiatives in medical colleges and training programmes to familiarise residents with the MSF process, its key stakeholders and its professional and personal development goals.21 Workshops, seminars and informational sessions could play a crucial role in bridging this knowledge gap and promoting a more receptive attitude towards MS evaluations. Moreover, addressing the ambiguity regarding the competencies assessed during MSF requires a transparent communication between feedback providers and residents. The inclusive nature of 360° feedback, which incorporates input from various sources such as peers, supervisors and patients, can be overwhelming for residents dealing with uncertainty about whose feedback holds weight and relevance. Providing guidance and clarification regarding the roles and contributions of different feedback providers can lessen residents’ uncertainty and enhance trust in the feedback process.22

Furthermore, in the current study, despite the initial unfamiliarity, after a brief introduction to the MSF concept, residents readily recognised its value. One of the study participants expressed, ‘This feedback will help me to identify what I lack and how I can perform better’. They acknowledged that receiving feedback from multiple sources, including peers, supervisors, nurses, attending staff and, most importantly, patients, offers a more balanced and comprehensive view of their overall performance. Residents further added that it could help them identify their strengths and areas for improvement that might not be apparent through traditional feedback methods as the consultants might not view their performance most of the time.23

The study participants acknowledged that the MSF is superior to traditional feedback due to several key reasons. First, MSF collects input from a variety of sources including peers, supervisors, nurses and patients, providing comprehensive and well-rounded assessments of residents’ performance. This has been demonstrated in another study where 16 residents were evaluated across six competencies, with both groups having comparable scores at the entry point. Group A, after receiving MSF, demonstrated significant improvement in all six competencies (p<0.01), whereas no significant improvement was observed in Group B following traditional feedback. These findings further support that MSF provides a more holistic understanding compared with traditional feedback, which may only come from one or two sources.24 Additionally, MSF fosters a culture of continuous improvement by offering residents a diverse perspective and insight into their strengths and areas of improvement.25 It also promotes a culture of accountability and fairness by ensuring that assessments are not solely based on the perspective of a single evaluator. It minimises biases and encourages a more objective evaluation, thereby providing residents with a more accurate representation of their progress and performance. This is evidenced by a previous study, where patients’ parents rated residents’ communication skills significantly higher than nurses and physicians, while no significant difference was observed between nurse and physician ratings. This highlights the value of incorporating multiple perspectives, such as parents’ unique insights into residents’ communication, into the evaluation process to provide more comprehensive feedback for residents.26

Although many residents in our study were not fully aware of the core concept of MSF and had concerns towards feedback in general, as expressed by residents previously in a similar study where the inability to present sensitive subjects in a constructive manner and superficial relationships between the evaluator and resident physician were identified as key barriers to the feedback process.27 However, it is interesting to note that all of the residents in this study were willing to undergo and looking forward to an implementation of the MS (360°) feedback in their hospitals and residency programmes. A key drive for residents’ readiness to embrace feedback is their eagerness to improve professionally and the ultimate goal of improving patient care.28 Residents who seek to enhance these skills understand that feedback offers valuable insights into their interactions with others, both patients and colleagues. Recognising the holistic nature of feedback, they view feedback not as criticism but as a catalyst for personal and professional development.

In a system where MSF is not a routine process, its implementation in the residency programmes can present several challenges. The current study further explored the concerns expressed by the residents, such as the discouragement due to over-criticism, the time required to provide and receive feedback and the complexity of involving multiple stakeholders in the evaluation process, making it lengthy, time-consuming and cumbersome. Additionally, there were concerns about the potential biases of evaluators and the need for adequate training to ensure that feedback is fair and effective. These findings align with another study, where both residents and Allied Healthcare Professionals (AHCPs) showed interest in MSF, but deeper discussions revealed barriers to its acceptance and provision. Issues such as roles and responsibilities, perceptions of expertise, hospital culture, interprofessional and power dynamics were highlighted as key obstacles.29 Evaluators lacking proper training may struggle to provide balanced, constructive and actionable feedback. The residents also highlighted the importance of ensuring that feedback is constructive.30 The feedback delivered in a supportive and professional manner avoids negative impacts on morale and professional relationships. Addressing these challenges is crucial for the successful implementation of MSF in residency programmes.

Implications for practice

The findings of this study have significant implications for medical practice. First, there is a clear necessity for medical training programmes in KSA to incorporate and elucidate the concept of MSF to foster the professional development of residents and align them with international standards. The primary focus of these programmes should include the objectives, stakeholders and competencies related to MSF. Second, recognising the value of MS input from residents highlights the need for initiatives to integrate these systems into medical education. This includes promoting a supportive feedback environment, offering guidance for feedback interpretation and action planning and incorporating regular MSF into routine clinical practice to enhance resident learning and patient care. Creating organised, time-efficient feedback systems that effortlessly integrate into residents’ routines is essential. Lastly, addressing the concerns raised by residents necessitates a comprehensive approach that includes support systems to manage regular conduction of the feedback process, along with training on providing and receiving feedback. Limitations

Our study has few limitations. The use of convenience sampling may have resulted in participation by residents who were available or interested in feedback processes and therefore may be prone to selection bias. Additionally, this study was limited to self-reported perceptions that may be susceptible to recall inaccuracy or confidence levels, stress or burnout, which may not be fully representative of actual awareness or readiness for MSF.

Conclusion

The study underscores the significant potential of MS (360°) feedback in fostering the personal and professional development of medical residents in KSA. Initially unfamiliar with this approach, many residents swiftly acknowledged its benefits in offering holistic and equitable evaluation. The study emphasises the necessity for educational campaigns aimed at increasing awareness and tackling obstacles such as excessive criticism, time limitations and evaluator’s biases. By pinpointing the effective strategies to surmount these challenges, residency programmes can successfully incorporate MSF, thereby cultivating more proficient and versatile healthcare professionals.
