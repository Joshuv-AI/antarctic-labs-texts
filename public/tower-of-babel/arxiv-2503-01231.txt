Change of basis for the tridiagonal pairs of type II
Nicolas Crampé, Julien Gaboriaud, Satoshi Tsujimoto

Abstract
We study tridiagonal pairs of type II. These involve two linear transformations A A and A ⋆ A^{\star} . We define two bases. In the first one, A A acts as a diagonal matrix while A ⋆ A^{\star} acts as a block tridiagonal matrix, and in the second one, A A acts as a block tridiagonal matrix while A ⋆ A^{\star} acts as a diagonal matrix. We obtain the change of basis coefficients between these two bases. The coefficients are special functions that are written as a nested product of polynomials that resemble Racah polynomials but involve shift operators in their expression.



Keywords:
Tridiagonal pairs; Leonard pairs; orthogonal polynomials; multivariable special functions

Introduction

Leonard pairs.

Consider two finite-dimensional
matrices
A
and
A^{\star}
.
An elementary result in linear algebra is:

“Two diagonalizable matrices
A
and
A^{\star}
commute with each other if and only if they can be
diagonalized simultaneously.”

The simplest departure from this trivial case is perhaps the situation where
upon diagonalizing
A
, the matrix
A^{\star}
takes a tridiagonal form.
Now suppose that the opposite also holds—that is,
A
takes a tridiagonal form
when diagonalizing
A^{\star}
—and in addition that both matrices are irreducible.
This is the setting of
Leonard pairs

[
1
,
2
]
.

In this looser but more general setting, the matrices
A
and
A^{\star}
do not
commute.
Instead, they obey well-studied algebraic relations known as the

Askey–Wilson relations

[
3
]
:

\displaystyle A^{2\phantom{\star}}A^{{\star}}-\beta A^{\phantom{\star}}A^{{\star}}A^{\phantom{\star}}+A^{{\star}}A^{2\phantom{\star}}-\gamma^{\phantom{\star}}\{A,A^{\star}\}-\rho^{\phantom{\star}}A^{{\star}}=\gamma^{\star}A^{2\phantom{\star}}+\omega A^{\phantom{\star}}+\eta^{\phantom{\star}}\,,

(1a)

\displaystyle A^{{\star 2}}A^{\phantom{\star}}-\beta A^{{\star}}A^{\phantom{\star}}A^{{\star}}+A^{\phantom{\star}}A^{{\star}2}-\gamma^{\star}\{A,A^{\star}\}-\rho^{\star}A^{\phantom{\star}}=\gamma^{\phantom{\star}}A^{{\star}2}+\omega A^{{\star}}+\eta^{{\star}}\,,

(1b)

where
\beta
,
\gamma
,
\gamma^{\star}
,
\rho
,
\rho^{\star}
,
\omega
,
\eta
,

\eta^{\star}

are some parameters
(this has been shown by Terwilliger in
[
1
]
).
There are two natural bases in this situation:
the one where
A
is diagonal, and the other one where
A^{\star}
is diagonal.
These two natural bases are related by a change of basis.
The matrix that gives the change of basis is remarkable:
in the most general case
[
4
]
,
its entries contain
q
-Racah polynomials, and various
limiting cases yield other families of the Askey scheme of hypergeometric
orthogonal polynomials
[
5
]
.

This is a striking result.
From a simple linear algebra problem, one is led to the (finite)
orthogonal polynomials families of the Askey scheme.
And the matrices
A
and
A^{\star}

encode fundamental properties of these families:
their recurrence and difference equations.

Tridiagonal pairs.

The generalization from commuting matrices to Leonard pairs was valuable.
It makes sense to look for further extensions.
One generalization of Leonard pairs is the concept of
tridiagonal pairs
.
In this looser setting (see Definition
2.1
), one demands that
in the basis where
A
is diagonal,
A^{\star}
is block tridiagonal, and vice-versa.
The algebraic relations between
A
and
A^{\star}
have been obtained in the general
case
[
6

7
]
and are called

:

\displaystyle\big[\,A^{\phantom{\star}},\ A^{2\phantom{\star}}A^{{\star}}-\beta A^{\phantom{\star}}A^{{\star}}A^{\phantom{\star}}+A^{{\star}}A^{2\phantom{\star}}-\gamma^{\phantom{\star}}\{A,A^{\star}\}-\rho^{\phantom{\star}}A^{{\star}}\,\big]=0\,,

(2a)

\displaystyle\big[\,A^{\star},\ A^{{\star 2}}A^{\phantom{\star}}-\beta A^{{\star}}A^{\phantom{\star}}A^{{\star}}+A^{\phantom{\star}}A^{{\star}2}-\gamma^{\star}\{A,A^{\star}\}-\rho^{\star}A^{\phantom{\star}}\,\big]=0\,,

(2b)

where

,
\gamma
,
\gamma^{\star}
,

These relations, introduced for the study of the association scheme

[

[

,
9
]
and of the circular

[

. They also generalize the
q
-Dolan–Grady
relations
[
11

or the cubic
q
-Serre relations

[
12
]
.
Inspired by the story of Leonard pairs, it would be natural to consider two bases
(one where
A
is diagonal and another one where
A^{\star}
is diagonal)
and look for the change of basis coefficients between the two bases.
In these should appear special functions which are generalizations of the
polynomials of the Askey scheme.

The goal.

To the best of our knowledge, so far such results have not been obtained in general.
The question of what special functions arise in the study of tridiagonal pairs
still remains.
This is what we will clarify in this paper.

We will explicitly provide these special functions for tridiagonal
pairs of type II.

The precise definition of tridiagonal pairs of type II is given in
Definition
2.2
.
Roughly speaking they correspond to a
q\to 1
limit of the most general case
of tridiagonal pairs.

The bases.

There are a number of challenges to face in order to solve this change of basis
problem.
One is the fact that there are multiple choices of bases
where
A
is diagonal and
A^{\star}
is block tridiagonal (and vice-versa).
This is due to the degeneracy of the eigenvalues of
A
and
A^{\star}
.
Thus, from the start, one cannot speak of “two natural bases”.
Fortunately, there exists another basis called the
split basis
.
The split basis, given in equation (
12
), corresponds to a basis
where
A
takes an upper triangular, block bidiagonal form
while
A^{\star}
takes a lower triangular, block bidiagonal form
(or upper bidiagonal and lower bidiagonal form in the case of Leonard pairs).
This basis was used to great effect by Terwilliger and proved to be a key
ingredient in his study of Leonard pairs.

The split basis provides a well-defined basis and we use it as our starting point.
From there, we obtain two natural bases.
One basis is such that
A
is diagonal,
A^{\star}
is block tridiagonal
and the change of basis from the split basis to this basis is given by a lower
triangular matrix with
1
s on the diagonal.
The other basis is obtained similarly by a change of basis involving an upper
triangular matrix with
1
s on the diagonal and leads to
A^{\star}
being
diagonal while
A
is block tridiagonal.
Equipped with two well-defined bases, the problem is now well posed and we solve it
in this paper.

2
Tridiagonal pairs of type II

We first recall the definition and main properties of tridiagonal pairs.

Definition 2.1

(
[
37
]
)
.

Let
\mathbb{F}
denote a field, and let
{\mathcal{V}}
denote a vector space
over
\mathbb{F}
with finite positive dimension.
By a
tridiagonal pair
(or TD-pair) on
{\mathcal{V}}
, we mean
an ordered pair
(A,A^{\star})
, where
A
and
A^{\star}
are linear
transformations on
{\mathcal{V}}
that satisfy the following four conditions:

(i)

A
and
A^{\star}
are both diagonalizable on
{\mathcal{V}}
.

(ii)

There exists an ordering
{\mathcal{V}}_{0},{\mathcal{V}}_{1},\dots,{\mathcal{V}}_{d}
of the
eigenspaces of
A
such that

\displaystyle A^{\star}\,{\mathcal{V}}_{i}\subseteq{\mathcal{V}}_{i-1}+{\mathcal{V}}_{i}+{\mathcal{V}}_{i+1}\,\qquad(0\leq i\leq d)

(3)

where
{\mathcal{V}}_{-1}=0,\ {\mathcal{V}}_{d+1}=0
.

(iii)

There exists an ordering

{\mathcal{V}}^{\star}_{0},{\mathcal{V}}^{\star}_{1},\dots,{\mathcal{V}}^{\star}_{\delta}
of the eigenspaces of

A^{\star}
such that

\displaystyle A\,{\mathcal{V}}^{\star}_{i}\subseteq{\mathcal{V}}^{\star}_{i-1}+{\mathcal{V}}^{\star}_{i}+{\mathcal{V}}^{\star}_{i+1}\,\qquad(0\leq i\leq\delta)

(4)

where
{\mathcal{V}}^{\star}_{-1}=0,\ {\mathcal{V}}^{\star}_{\delta+1}=0
.

(iv)

There is no subspace
{\mathcal{W}}
of
{\mathcal{V}}
such that both

A{\mathcal{W}}\subseteq{\mathcal{W}}
,
A^{\star}{\mathcal{W}}\subseteq{\mathcal{W}}
,
other than
{\mathcal{W}}=0
and
{\mathcal{W}}={\mathcal{V}}
(irreducibility).

In this paper, we restrict ourselves to the case when the field
\mathbb{F}
is
the field of complex numbers
\mathbb{C}
.
For a TD-pair, it is well-known that
A
and
A^{\star}
have the same number of
eigenspaces
i.e.

d+1=d^{\star}+1
(
d
is called the
diameter
of
the TD-pair) and that the following conditions hold

[
37
]
:

\displaystyle\rho_{i}=\text{dim}{\mathcal{V}}_{i}=\text{dim}{\mathcal{V}}^{\star}_{i}=\text{dim}{\mathcal{V}}_{d-i}=\text{dim}{\mathcal{V}}^{\star}_{d-i}\qquad(0\leq i\leq d)\,.

(5)

The tuple
(\rho_{0},\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{d})
is called the
shape
of a
TD-pair.
Because we restrict ourselves to a closed field (the complex numbers), the
TD-pair is sharp
i.e.

\rho_{0}=1
.

It is conjectured that, for any TD-pair, there exist
N
integers

\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\dots,\ell_{N}\geq 0
,
N\geq 1
such that the shape satisfies
the
character formula

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{d}\rho_{i}\lambda^{i}=\prod_{p=1}^{N}\frac{1-\lambda^{\ell_{p}+1}}{1-\lambda}\,.

(6)

In particular, the diameter
d
satisfies
d=\ell_{1}+\dots+\ell_{N}
.
The case
N=1
and
\boldsymbol{\ell}=(\ell)
corresponds to

\rho_{0}=\rho_{1}=\dots=\rho_{\ell}=1

i.e.
the spectra of
A
and
A^{\star}
are simple.
Then, a TD-pair becomes a Leonard pair.

In this paper, we provide and study families of TD-pairs
for any choice of the
N
-tuple

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\ell}=(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{N})\,.

(7)

It is well-established that the eigenvalues
\theta_{i}
and
\theta_{i}^{\star}

of
A
and
A^{\star}
defining a TD-pair can take only few particular forms.
In this paper, we focus on the TD-pairs of type II.

Definition 2.2
.

A tridiagonal pair (see Definition
2.1
) such that
the eigenvalues
\theta_{i}
,
\theta^{\star}_{i}
of
A
,
A^{\star}

can be expressed as

\displaystyle\theta_{i}

\displaystyle=c_{0}+c_{1}i+c_{2}i^{2}\,,

(8a)

\displaystyle\theta^{\star}_{i}

\displaystyle=c^{\star}_{0}+c^{\star}_{1}i+c^{\star}_{2}i^{2}\,,

(8b)

for some scalars
c_{0},c_{1},c_{2},c^{\star}_{0},c^{\star}_{1},c^{\star}_{2}\in\mathbb{C}

is called a
tridiagonal pair of type II
.

&+\sum_{p=1}^{N}\sum_{1\leq q\leq r\leq N}\sum_{{\mathbf{n}}={\mathbf{i}}-{\mathbf{e}}_{q}-{\mathbf{e}}_{r}}^{{\mathbf{i}}}\xi^{{\scriptscriptstyle}(N)}_{{\mathbf{n}}+{\mathbf{e}}_{p},p}{{{D}}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle}(N)}_{{\mathbf{n}},{\mathbf{i}}}\overline{{{D}}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle}(N)}_{{\mathbf{i}}+{\mathbf{e}}_{p}-{\mathbf{e}}_{q}-{\mathbf{e}}_{r},{\mathbf{n}}+{\mathbf{e}}_{p}}V_{{\mathbf{i}}+{\mathbf{e}}_{p}-{\mathbf{e}}_{q}-{\mathbf{e}}_{r}}\,.\end{aligned}

(53)

∎

5
Change of basis and special functions

In the theory of Leonard pairs, two natural bases are those where each linear
map acts diagonally.
Orthogonal polynomials of the (
q
-)Askey scheme appear as entries of the change
of basis matrices between these two natural bases.
Similarly, here we are interested in the special functions that appear as change
of basis coefficients between two natural bases for tridiagonal pairs.

Concretely, we want to compute the elements
T_{\mathbf{i}}({\mathbf{x}})
of the change
of basis between
V({\mathbf{x}})
and
V_{\mathbf{i}}
:

\displaystyle V_{\mathbf{i}}=\sum_{{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{0}}^{\boldsymbol{\ell}}T_{\mathbf{i}}({\mathbf{x}})\,V({\mathbf{x}})\,.

(55)

Remarking that relations (
41
) and (
49
) imply

\displaystyle V_{\mathbf{i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\mathbf{n}}=\mathbf{0}}^{\mathbf{i}}{{D}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle}(N)}_{{\mathbf{n}},\mathbf{i}}\sum_{{\mathbf{x}}={\mathbf{n}}}^{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\overline{{{C}}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle}(N)}_{{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{n}}}V({\mathbf{x}})=\sum_{{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{0}}^{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\;\sum_{{\mathbf{n}}=\mathbf{0}}^{\text{min}(\mathbf{i},{\mathbf{x}})}{{D}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle}(N)}_{{\mathbf{n}},\mathbf{i}}\overline{{{C}}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle}(N)}_{{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{n}}}\;V({\mathbf{x}})\,,

(56)

where
\text{min}(\mathbf{i},{\mathbf{x}})\equiv(\text{min}(i_{1},x_{1}),\dots,\text{min}(i_{N},x_{N}))
,
one gets

\displaystyle T_{\mathbf{i}}({\mathbf{x}})=\sum_{{\mathbf{n}}=\mathbf{0}}^{\text{min}(\mathbf{i},{\mathbf{x}})}{{D}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle}(N)}_{{\mathbf{n}},\mathbf{i}}\;\overline{{{C}}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle}(N)}_{{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{n}}}\,.

(57)

The previous expression of
T_{\mathbf{i}}({\mathbf{x}})
can be simplified and expressed
in terms of generalized hypergeometric functions.
For
i,x,\ell
nonnegative integers satisfying
i,x\leq\ell
, we introduce the
function
R
as follows:

\displaystyle{R\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{0}{i,x,a_1,a_2}{\ell,b_1,b_2}\middle|Z\right)}

\displaystyle=\binom{\ell}{i}(b_{1})_{i}(b_{2})_{x}\;{{}_{4}F_{3}\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{0}{-i,\;-x,\; a_1,\; a_2}{b_1,\; b_2,\;-\ell}\middle|Z\right)}

\displaystyle=\binom{\ell}{i}(b_{1})_{i}(b_{2})_{x}\sum_{k=0}^{\text{min}(i,x)}\frac{(-i,-x,a_{1},a_{2})_{k}}{(1,b_{1},b_{2},-\ell)_{k}}\,Z^{k}\,.

(58)

As the
Z
might be an operator that does not commute with the coefficients in
the sum (see below), we need to fix a convention.
Here we use the convention that
Z^{k}
is on the far right of each term in
the sum.
We also introduce the shift operator
e^{\partial_{x}}
acting as follows:

e^{\partial_{x}}f(x)=f(x+1)\,,

(59)

and the function identity:
\mathbf{1}:x\mapsto 1
such that
e^{\partial_{x}}\mathbf{1}(x)=1
.

Theorem 5.1

(
Change of basis between
V({\mathbf{x}})
and
V_{\mathbf{i}}
)
.

The elements
T_{\mathbf{i}}({\mathbf{x}})
can be expressed as the following product:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} T_{\mathbf{i}}({\mathbf{x}})&=\frac{(-1)^{|\mathbf{i}|}}{(|\mathbf{i}|+\omega^{\star})_{|\mathbf{i}|}(|{\mathbf{x}}|+\omega)_{|{\mathbf{x}}|}}\\

{\overrightarrow{\prod}}_{p=1}^{N}X_{p}=X_{1}X_{2}\dots X_{N}

Proof.

Using properties of the Pochhammer symbol, the coefficients

\overline{{{C}}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle}(N)}_{{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{n}}}
and
{{D}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle}(N)}_{{\mathbf{n}},\mathbf{i}}

\displaystyle\overline{{{C}}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle}(N)}_{{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{n}}}=\frac{(-1)^{|{\mathbf{n}}|}}{(|{\mathbf{x}}|+\omega)_{|{\mathbf{x}}|}}\prod_{p=1}^{N}\frac{(-x_{p},\ |{\mathbf{x}}|+|{\mathbf{n}}|_{1}^{p-1}+\omega)_{n_{p}}(|{\mathbf{n}}|_{1}^{p-1}+|{\mathbf{x}}|_{1}^{p-1}+|\boldsymbol{\ell}|_{p}^{N}+a_{p}+\omega+1)_{x_{p}}}{(1,\ |{\mathbf{n}}|_{1}^{p-1}+|{\mathbf{x}}|_{1}^{p-1}+|\boldsymbol{\ell}|_{p}^{N}+a_{p}+\omega+1)_{n_{p}}}

(61)

\displaystyle{{D}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle}(N)}_{{\mathbf{n}},\mathbf{i}}=\frac{(-1)^{|{\mathbf{n}}|-|\mathbf{i}|}}{(|\mathbf{i}|+\omega^{\star})_{|\mathbf{i}|}}\prod_{p=1}^{N}\binom{\ell_{p}}{i_{p}}\frac{(-i_{p},\ |\mathbf{i}|+|{\mathbf{n}}|_{1}^{p-1}+\omega^{\star})_{n_{p}}(|\mathbf{i}|_{1}^{p-1}+|{\mathbf{n}}|_{1}^{p-1}+|\boldsymbol{\ell}|_{p+1}^{N}-a_{p}+\omega^{\star})_{i_{p}}}{(-\ell_{p},\ |\mathbf{i}|_{1}^{p-1}+|{\mathbf{n}}|_{1}^{p-1}+|\boldsymbol{\ell}|_{p+1}^{N}-a_{p}+\omega^{\star})_{n_{p}}}\,.

(62)

Putting these together, we can write
T_{{\mathbf{i}}}({\mathbf{x}})

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} T_{{\mathbf{i}}}({\mathbf{x}})=\sum_{{\mathbf{n}}=\mathbf{0}}^{\min({\mathbf{i}},{\mathbf{x}})}\prod_{p=1}^{N}&\ \frac{(-x_{p})_{n_{p}}(-i_{p})_{n_{p}}(-\ell_{p})_{i_{p}}}{(1)_{n_{p}}(-\ell_{p})_{n_{p}}(1)_{i_{p}}}\frac{(|{\mathbf{x}}|_{1}^{p-1}+|{\mathbf{n}}|_{1}^{p}+|\boldsymbol{\ell}|_{p}^{N}+a_{p}+\omega+1)_{x_{p}-n_{p}}}{(|{\mathbf{x}}|+|{\mathbf{n}}|+|{\mathbf{x}}|_{1}^{p-1}-|{\mathbf{n}}|_{1}^{p-1}+\omega)_{x_{p}-n_{p}}}\\

(63)

This expression can be reworked into a more familiar form using the following trick.
Using the shift operators
e^{\partial_{x_{p}}}
, all the
dependence on
n_{p}
of the expression (
61
) can be gathered in

e^{n_{p}\partial_{x_{p}}}
as shown in the following expression:

\displaystyle\overline{{{C}}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle}(N)}_{{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{n}}}=\frac{(-1)^{|{\mathbf{n}}|}}{(|{\mathbf{x}}|+\omega)_{|{\mathbf{x}}|}}{\overrightarrow{\prod}}_{p=1}^{N}

\displaystyle\ \frac{(-x_{p},|{\mathbf{x}}|+\omega)_{n_{p}}(|{\mathbf{x}}|_{1}^{p-1}+|\boldsymbol{\ell}|_{p}^{N}+a_{p}+\omega+1)_{x_{p}}}{(1,|{\mathbf{x}}|_{1}^{p-1}+|\boldsymbol{\ell}|_{p}^{N}+a_{p}+\omega+1)_{n_{p}}}e^{n_{p}\partial_{x_{p}}}\cdot\mathbf{1}(x_{N})\,.

(64)

We recall that the product is ordered, as mentioned at the end of the theorem.
Similar expressions can be obtained for
{{D}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle}(N)}_{{\mathbf{n}},\mathbf{i}}
using
the shift operator
e^{\partial_{i_{p}}}
. The result of the theorem is then proven
inserting these expressions in (
57
).

∎

It is immediate to see that the case

yields univariate Racah polynomials.

6
Limiting cases

Tridiagonal pairs of type II are divided in three categories: the first, second and third kind.
The kind of a tridiagonal pair is determined according to the degrees
of the eigenvalues
\theta_{i}
,
\theta^{\star}_{i}
.
The first kind is the most general case, and the second and third kinds can be reached from it
through successive limits.
We detail this below.

6.1
Racah-type case (First kind)

This is the general case that was introduced in this paper.
For
i=0,1,\dots,|\boldsymbol{\ell}|
, the eigenvalues of the first kind can be written as follows:

\displaystyle\theta_{i}=\theta_{0}+hi(i+\omega)\,,{\quad\qquad}\theta^{\star}_{i}=\theta^{\star}_{0}+h^{\star}i(i+\omega^{\star})\,,

(74)

for some free constants

\theta_{0},\theta_{0}^{\star},h,h^{\star},\omega,\omega^{\star}\in\mathbb{C}
.

The special functions
T_{\mathbf{i}}({\mathbf{x}})
are given in (
60
)
and resemble a nested product of Racah polynomials.

6.2
Hahn-type case (Second kind)

For
i=0,1,\dots,|\boldsymbol{\ell}|
, the eigenvalues of the second kind can be written as follows:

\displaystyle\theta_{i}=\theta_{0}+hi(i+\omega)\,,{\quad\qquad}\theta^{\star}_{i}=\theta^{\star}_{0}+h^{\star}i\,,

(75)

for some free constants

\theta_{0},\theta_{0}^{\star},h,h^{\star},\omega\in\mathbb{C}
.
The second kind case is obtained from the first kind case by rewriting

h^{\star}\mapsto h^{\star}t
,
\omega^{\star}\mapsto 1/t
and letting
t\to 0
.

The special functions giving the change of basis in this case become

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} T_{\mathbf{i}}({\mathbf{x}})=\frac{(-1)^{|\mathbf{i}|}}{(|{\mathbf{x}}|+\omega)_{|{\mathbf{x}}|}}{\overrightarrow{\prod}}_{p=1}^{N}{H\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{0}{i_p,\; x_p,\; |\bx|+\omega}{\ell_p,\; |\bx|_{1}^{p-1}+|\Bell|_{p}^{N}+\omega+a_p+1}\middle|e^{\partial_{x_{p}}}\right)}\cdot\mathbf{1}(x_{N})\,,\end{aligned}

(76)

with

\displaystyle{H\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{0}{i,\; x,\; a}{\ell,\; b}\middle|Z\right)}=\binom{\ell}{i}(b)_{x}\ {{}_{3}F_{2}\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{0}{-i,\; -x,\; a}{-\ell,\; b}\middle|Z\right)}\,.

(77)

These functions resemble a nested product of Hahn polynomials.

6.3
Krawtchouk-type case (Third kind)

For
i=0,1,\dots,|\boldsymbol{\ell}|
, the eigenvalues of the third kind can be written as follows:

\displaystyle\theta_{i}=\theta_{0}+hi\,,{\quad\qquad}\theta^{\star}_{i}=\theta^{\star}_{0}+h^{\star}i\,,

(78)

for some free constants

\theta_{0},\theta_{0}^{\star},h,h^{\star}\in\mathbb{C}
.
The third kind case is obtained from the second kind case by rewriting

h\mapsto ht
,
\omega\mapsto 1/t
and letting
t\to 0
.

The special functions giving the change of basis in this case become

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} T_{\mathbf{i}}({\mathbf{x}})=\prod_{p=1}^{N}\frac{(-\ell_{p})_{i_{p}}}{(1)_{i_{p}}}{{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{0}{-i_p,\; -x_p}{-\ell_p}\middle|1\right)}\end{aligned}

(79)

This is a simple product of Krawtchouk polynomials

K_{i_{p}}(x_{p};\phi;N)
with parameter
\phi=1
.

7
Outlooks

In this paper, we provided an explicit expression for the change of basis between
the two bases in the definition of tridiagonal pairs of type II.
It involves special functions which generalize Racah polynomials.
It would be interesting to study if these special functions possess additional
properties such as contiguity relations, duality, etc.

The monovariate Racah polynomials have been used extensively and appear in
numerous contexts.
To cite a few examples,
they provide an explicit expression of the
6j
-symbol introduced in the problem
of the recoupling of three spins in quantum mechanics
[
41
]
,
they characterize
P
- and
Q
- polynomial association scheme

[
4
,
42
]
,
they are closely related to the generic second-order superintegrable model on
the 2-sphere
[
43
]
,
they define interesting models of free fermions
[
44
]

and also describe exactly solvable birth and death processes
[
45
]
.
Our generalization of the Racah polynomials introduced in this paper could also
have similar uses in these different contexts and we are exploring such
potential applications.

In addition, the bispectrality property of the Racah polynomials leads to the
definition of the eponym algebra, the
Racah algebra
(see

e.g.

[
46
]
for a recent review).
This corresponds to the case
N=1
in this paper.
For
N=1
, the operators
A
and
A^{\star}
satisfy the relations of
the Racah algebra which are more constrained than the ones of the tridiagonal
algebra (see (
21
)).
A detailed understanding of the algebraic properties of the functions for

N\geq 2
is the next step.
It would be interesting to obtain supplementary relations—in addition to the
ones of the tridiagonal algebra—satisfied by
A
and
A^{\star}
for any given
shape
(\rho_{0},\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{d})
.
This would provide an algebraic interpretation of the shape of tridiagonal
pairs.

Clearly, results similar to the ones obtained in this paper ought to exist
for tridiagonal pairs of type I or type III and we plan to pursue these in
upcoming work.
As mentioned in the introduction, a change of basis for tridiagonal pairs of
type I has been already computed in
[
31
]
. To be
precise, the irreducibility of these pairs was not discussed in this latter
cited paper and cannot be called tridiagonal pairs. However, the constraints on
the parameters such that they become really tridiagonal pairs can be computed
thanks to
[
38
]
.
It would be very interesting to understand the connection between the change of
basis obtained in
[
31
]
and the one obtained by
generalizing the results of the present paper. Both are not necessarily
identical since the bases where
A
or
A^{\star}
are diagonal are not unique.
To the best of our knowledge, the computation of the change of basis for
tridiagonal pairs of type III does not appear in the literature.

Hypergeometric functions.

The generalized hypergeometric function is defined by

\displaystyle{{}_{r}F_{s}\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{0}{a_1,\; a_2,\;\dots,\; a_r}{b_1,\; \dots,\;b_s}\middle|z\right)}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(a_{1},\;a_{2},\;\dots,\;a_{r})_{k}}{(1)_{k}(b_{1},\;\dots,\;b_{s})_{k}}z^{k}\,,

(83)

with the Pochhammer symbol
(x)_{k}
given by

\displaystyle(x)_{k}=(x)(x+1)\dots(x+k-1)\,,\qquad(x)_{0}\equiv 1\,,

(84)

and

\displaystyle(a_{1},\;\dots,\;a_{n})_{k}=(a_{1})_{k}\dots(a_{n})_{k}\,.

(85)
