When injury costs too much: the catastrophic costs for spinal cord injury in Iran
Authors: Shahrokh Yousefzadeh-Chabok, Diana Pacheco Barzallo, Zahra Mohtasham-Amiri, Enayatollah Homaie Rad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe main objective of this study was to determine the percentage of families experiencing catastrophic costs as a result of spinal cord injury. Patients with spinal cord injuries often encounter significant expenses related to treatment, rehabilitation, and caregiving. Catastrophic expenditures are the direct consequence of the lack of effective health insurance. This financial burden, along with the potential for catastrophic costs, presents a serious challenge for these individuals.MethodsData were obtained from the International Spinal Cord Injury Survey in Iran (InSCI-IR), which included 291 patients from Guilan Province. The study collected detailed information on direct and indirect costs, changes in household income after spinal cord injury, coping strategy earnings, and the household wealth index. Catastrophic costs were calculated using multiple approaches, including the household budget share method, disease-specific cost calculations, analyses excluding coping earnings, income estimation based on asset ownership, and Xu’s method based on capacity to pay. In addition, regression models and sensitivity analyses were conducted.ResultsThe prevalence of households experiencing catastrophic costs due to spinal cord injury (SCICC) was 45.0%, ranging from 22.0% to 49.4% depending on the calculation method. Factors associated with catastrophic costs included gender (female patients were less likely to experience catastrophic costs, β = -0.75), presence of complete SCI (β = 1.53) or incomplete SCI (β = 1.46), household size (larger households were less likely to experience catastrophic costs, β = -0.27), higher activity participation levels (β = 0.29), greater independence scores (associated with a lower likelihood of catastrophic costs, β = -0.16), and the occurrence of pressure ulcers (β = 0.90).ConclusionsHealth insurance strategies that enhance cost-sharing arrangements and broaden coverage for underrepresented services, along with preventive measures aimed at managing pressure ulcers and pain, can significantly mitigate the incidence of catastrophic financial burdens faced by patients with spinal cord injuries.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13706-5.Keywords: Spinal cord injury, Health care costs, Poverty, Financial hardship, Iran

Introduction

Spinal cord injury (SCI) is a life-altering and debilitating condition that significantly impacts not only the affected individuals but also their caregivers and the healthcare system as a whole [1]. The consequences of SCI can vary based on the level of injury, often resulting in persistent neurological deficits that manifest as sensory, motor, and autonomic dysfunctions [2]. In 2019, it was estimated that approximately 0.9 million people worldwide were affected by SCI [3]. The leading causes of SCI are traumatic incidents, including road traffic crashes, falls, and violence. However, there is a concerning trend of increasing rates of non-traumatic SCI [4]. In lower income countries, road traffic crashes remain the most common cause of spinal cord injury (SCI). In contrast, higher income countries countries are witnessing a decline in traffic-related injuries due to improved transportation infrastructure. Nevertheless, with an aging population in these regions, fall-induced SCI is on the rise [1]. 10.5 cases per 100,000 people is the global estimated of SCI [5].

For individuals who experience complete injuries resulting in paraplegia and tetraplegia, the process of care and rehabilitation extends into the post-discharge period, primarily managed by caregivers [6]. Moreover, patients with spinal cord injuries (SCI) are vulnerable to secondary health complications and neurological comorbidities [7]. Consequently, the costs associated with the rehabilitation of these patients can be prohibitively high, especially when they lack health insurance coverage [8]. Paraplegia and tetraplegia are prevalent among spinal cord injury patients; research indicates that, a decade after their injury, only 32.4% of patients with paraplegia and 24.2% with tetraplegia are employed [9].

In many cases of spinal cord injury, income loss is an unavoidable reality. The financial burden imposed by spinal cord injury (TSCI) places significant strain on households, often leading to increased poverty. Today, providing financial protection against healthcare expenses is essential to prevent families from facing financial hardship. The assessment of catastrophic costs serves as a critical methodology for quantifying the financial burden experienced by households. These costs may be evaluated in a general context or with specificity to particular diseases. The Iranian literature is replete with studies addressing general catastrophic costs. A meta-analysis conducted in 2014, which synthesized the findings of 42 studies, indicated that 7.1% of the Iranian population is subjected to catastrophic costs [10]. Furthermore, another investigation identified a general catastrophic cost prevalence of 4.7%, while disease-specific catastrophic costs displayed considerable variability, ranging from 9% for multiple sclerosis to an alarming 54.5% for cancer [11].

There is a growing consensus that health systems must provide financial protection for individuals and households facing economic hardships due to out-of-pocket health expenditures. The absence of such financial protection is widely regarded as a shortcoming of health systems. The catastrophic expenditure approach quantifies the financial burden of health expenses on households [12]. The rate of households exposed to catastrophic health expenditures (CHE) illustrates the potential number of families that fall into poverty due to healthcare costs. This index is crucial for health policymakers, as it can guide efforts to assist households dealing with the catastrophic costs.

Estimations has shown that SCI prevalence is 921 cases per 100,000 population in 2016 [13]. While several studies have been conducted to estimate the worldwide costs of SCI, there is a lack of information regarding the percentage of catastrophic costs. This study was conducted in Guilan Province, located in northern Iran. Guilan has a high incidence of spinal cord injuries, primarily due to falls from heights and pedestrian-related traffic crashes. These characteristics make Guilan a particularly relevant setting for the InSCI study.

Methods

Data

This study employed a cross-sectional design, utilizing data from the International Spinal Cord Injury Survey in Iran (InSCI-IR), which was conducted exclusively in Guilan Province. The primary objective of the survey was to evaluate the living conditions and costs of patients with spinal cord injuries (SCI) in this province. The initial phase of InSCI-IR, conducted in 2024, included a total of 291 SCI patients from Guilan Province. The International Spinal Cord Injury Survey in Iran (InSCI-IR) was conducted exclusively in Guilan Province as part of the global InSCI initiative. The survey included two modules: the international InSCI questionnaire, covering sociodemographic, clinical, and living conditions, and a local module on catastrophic expenditures. Patient data were obtained from the Poursina Hospital trauma registry and the National Spinal Cord Injury Registry of Iran (NSCIR-IR), and information was collected through structured telephone interviews with eligible adults (≥ 18 years) with traumatic SCI sustained at least one year earlier. For this analysis, we focused on cost-related variables including direct and indirect costs, income, coping strategies, and wealth indices [14]. The survey incorporated clinical data obtained from a hospital Health Information System and conducted telephone interviews. Participants included those with traumatic SCI who had surpassed one-year post-injury and were over 18 years of age. Individuals with neurodegenerative disorders, peripheral nerve damage, or congenital etiologies were excluded from the study. Relevant clinical data, treatment costs, and socioeconomic and demographic information were extracted from the survey databank and cleaned for analysis. The study protocol and its related survey is available in the article published in 2024 [14].

Indirect cost

Income loss

In addition to treatment costs, patients were surveyed regarding income loss related to spinal cord injury (SCI). This included income lost due to unemployment, early retirement due to disability, income changes resulting from job modifications, and shifts from full-time to part-time work.

Debt

Moreover, patients may resort to selling assets, borrowing funds, incurring interest, or receiving loans and compensation related to SCI to manage treatment expenses. These avenues for generating income are referred to as coping strategies and were also incorporated into the survey questions (see Table 1).

Calculation of income and other variables

A self-reported household income from the year preceding the spinal cord injury (SCI) was collected to assess household income levels. Given the prevalence of a predominantly informal sector in Iran, self-reported household income may not accurately reflect the actual earnings of these households. To mitigate bias, household income was estimated based on household asset ownership. Information regarding household assets was extracted from the database, and a predictive model was developed using data from the Household Income and Expenditure Survey (HIES) for Iran in 2022, as provided by the Iranian Statistical Center. Utilizing the asset data from InSCI-IR, income was estimated for each household (see Appendix 1). Additionally, a wealth score was computed for each household based on asset ownership, with principal component analysis (PCA) applied for this purpose. The asset variables used to calculate the wealth index included access to a car, laptop, health insurance, dishwasher, television, personal computer, complementary health insurance, washing machine, refrigerator, phone, microwave, radio, vacuum cleaner, air conditioner, smartphone, bicycle, internet, motorcycle, various types of drinking water (such as piped, spring, packed, etc.), and the number of rooms in the residence. The results of the PCA are presented in Appendix 2.

Independence score

The database included various questions regarding the dependency of spinal cord injury (SCI) patients on activities such as eating and drinking, washing the upper body, head, and lower body, dressing the upper and lower body, grooming, breathing, as well as managing bladder and bowel functions. It also covered using the toilet, turning the upper and lower body in bed, sitting up in bed, performing push-ups in a chair or wheelchair, transferring to and from bed and wheelchair, and moving around moderate distances. We combined these variables to create a new metric called the “independency score.” Higher values of this score indicate that patients have greater independence in their daily living activities.

Furthermore, we assessed patient participation in activities using a 1 to 5 Likert scale. This scale evaluated the level of difficulty patients experienced in performing daily routines, managing stress, utilizing their hands and fingers, and using both public and private transportation, among other factors. A higher score in activity participation indicates that the patient faced greater challenges in engaging in routine social life.

There are two primary methods for calculating catastrophic health spending. The first approach concentrates on the out-of-pocket health expenditures (OOPs) incurred by families, emphasizing the role of health insurance in controlling these costs and preventing households from descending into poverty [15]. This method is frequently used to evaluate the performance of health and insurance systems, aligning with one of the targets of the third Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) related to Universal Health Coverage (UHC). Another approach examines the costs associated with a specific disease, focusing on the household members affected by that condition. This is referred to as disease-related catastrophic costs and encompasses the indirect costs, such as job loss and caregiving, associated with certain diseases. These types of costs are not classified as health expenditures because they are not directly tied to medical treatment. They arise when a household confronts a disease. Consequently, relying solely on the first approach may not adequately assess the financial protection of households. This method is commonly applied to chronic diseases like tuberculosis, cancer, and HIV.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis was conducted using various approaches to income estimation in this study. Catastrophic costs were calculated based on income derived from the Household Income and Expenditure Survey (HIES) data, which was linked to household asset ownership through regression estimators (see Appendix 3). Additionally, the analysis focused on direct costs to illustrate variations in catastrophic costs related specifically to these expenses. To better understand the changes in thresholds, catastrophic costs were assessed at 10%, 40%, and 60% thresholds, and the results were compared. Furthermore, the Xu method was employed to determine catastrophic costs by assessing the capacity to pay, defined as the difference between income and subsistence expenditures. Utilizing the 2022 Iranian HIES data, we calculated subsistence expenditures based on the average food costs of households in the 45th to 55th income quintiles (adjusted for household size). If the costs associated with Serious Catastrophic Illness (SCI) exceeded 40% of the capacity to pay, the household was classified as experiencing catastrophic expenditures.

Table 5 illustrates the relationship between various variables and the incidence of SCICC related to spinal cord injury (SCI). Due to high collinearity among the degree of SCI, independence score, activity participation, and chronic diseases, we developed three models to minimize the impact of collinearity on the significance of the explanatory variables. We employed the variance inflation factor (VIF) test for this purpose, adjusting the variables within the models based on changes in the VIF results. The findings indicate that females experienced a lower risk of incurring SCICC (β = -0.75), with this result being significant at the 95% confidence interval. Both complete SCI (β = 1.53) and incomplete SCI (β = 1.46) were associated with a significantly increased risk of facing such costs. Additionally, household size demonstrated an inverse relationship with the risk of SCICC; households with a higher number of members were less likely to incur these costs (β = -0.27). Furthermore, activity participation had a direct correlation with SCICC (β = 0.29) in Model II. The independence score, on the other hand, displayed an inverse relationship with the probability of experiencing SCICC (β = -0.16). The presence of bedsores significantly increased the risk of catastrophic costs (β = 0.90). While some chronic diseases, including hypertension and diabetes, were examined for their association with SCICC, no significant relationships were established. However, a direct correlation was found between the presence of pain and the risk of SCICC (β = 0.30). Notably, being a current smoker also heightened the likelihood of SCICC (β = 0.79). Other variables did not show significant relationships with SCICC.

Discussion

This study aimed to employ a comprehensive methodology for calculating the percentage of catastrophic costs (SCICC) incurred by patients with spinal cord injury (SCI). The findings indicated that the annual cost for an SCI patient in Iran is approximately $3,000. Depending on the methodology applied, between 20% and 45% of Iranian households experience catastrophic costs when a family member suffers from SCI.

In a study conducted in the United States, the annual costs varied significantly based on the type of SCI, ranging from $28,334 for complete cervical injuries to $16,792 for incomplete thoracic injuries. In Canada, the cost of inpatient care for SCI was reported to be $11,193, the highest among various types of injuries. A systematic review also assessed the lifetime costs associated with SCI, revealing that these costs can range from $0.7 million to $2.5 million. Furthermore, another study in the United States focused on the rehabilitation of paraplegic patients, finding that their daily care expenses fluctuated between $1,133 and $1,313, depending on the patient’s age.

In Iran, a developing country, the percentage of catastrophic costs associated with chronic diseases is notably high. A meta-analysis revealed that these costs were 54.5% for cancer patients, 37.3% for dialysis patients, and 9.1% for those with multiple sclerosis [11]. Additionally, the pooled estimate of catastrophic health expenditures in the general population was 4.7% [11].

Research on the costs of spinal cord injury (SCI) primarily focuses on lifetime and average costs. However, these studies employ various methodologies, and there is currently no standardized approach that facilitates comparison across studies. Furthermore, no studies have been identified that calculate the catastrophic costs specifically for SCI patients.

In the United States, a study examined catastrophic costs among non-neurologic patients resulting from motor vehicle crashes. The findings indicated that 91.5% of uninsured patients and 10.1% of privately insured patients were at risk of incurring catastrophic costs [16]. In Vietnam, a hospital-based study using the subsistence expenditures method found that 26% of households experienced catastrophic costs due to any type of injury [17]. Utilizing a 40% threshold, a cohort study in the United States determined that 33% of patients with lower-extremity orthopedic trauma faced the risk of catastrophic health expenditures [18].

Furthermore, a modeling study from 2015 estimated that 3.7 billion individuals worldwide could encounter catastrophic expenses if surgery is required [19]. By analyzing data from the United States Medical Expenditure Panel Survey (MEPS) from 2010 to 2017, another study found that 12.2% of adults with traumatic injuries faced catastrophic expenditures [20].

The results of the regression analysis indicated that households with female spinal cord injury (SCI) patients face a lower risk of incurring SCICC. This can be attributed to the cultural context of Iran, where females typically do not hold the position of head of household and are not the primary earners. As a result, the presence of SCI in females may not lead to a loss of income, thereby reducing the likelihood of facing catastrophic costs. Similar findings have been observed in tuberculosis patients in Iran, Afghanistan, Tanzania, Bangladesh, and Bangalore and India [21, 22].

Patients with spinal cord injury (SCI) who maintain independence in their daily routines exhibit a lower risk of incurring SCICC. Conversely, those with limited social participation and individuals experiencing complete SCI face a heightened risk of such costs. These findings underscore the impact of disability and the severity of injuries on the likelihood of catastrophic expenses. Additionally, the presence of bedsores significantly increases the risk of these costs. The substantial financial burden of bedsores, particularly during the acute stages, has been corroborated by studies conducted in the United Kingdom and Iran [23, 24].

The findings of the current study indicate a notable peak in the relationship between wealth score and SCICC, with middle-income households being more affected than both the poorest and the wealthiest groups. One possible explanation is that households in the lowest wealth quintiles may simply forgo or delay healthcare due to lack of resources, thereby incurring lower absolute expenditures despite significant need. In contrast, middle-income families are more likely to seek care but may lack comprehensive insurance coverage, exposing them to higher out-of-pocket payments. In addition, these households often have limited coping strategies compared to wealthier groups, such as savings, access to credit, or social support networks [25]. Insurance gaps may also play a role, since many middle-class individuals are employed in the informal sector or self-employed, making them less likely to benefit from robust insurance packages [26]. Finally, cultural and behavioral differences in health-seeking patterns may amplify this effect, with middle-class families more willing to pursue costly rehabilitation and non-medical services, leading to a higher risk of catastrophic expenditures [27, 28]. An Iranian study revealed that catastrophic expenditures were higher among the poor compared to the poorest, as well as middle- and high-income groups [12]. Similar trends were observed in Pakistan during the years 2007 to 2008 [29]. However, the relationship between catastrophic costs and socioeconomic status is intricate and closely linked to the country’s health and insurance systems. For instance, research conducted in South Korea demonstrated that catastrophic expenditures are significantly associated with employment status [30]. Additionally, a Chinese study categorized participants into five income quintiles, showing that higher income quintiles experienced fewer catastrophic costs, while the opposite was true for lower [31].The study found a nonlinear concave relationship between catastrophic costs and the number of years since a spinal cord injury (SCI). Catastrophic costs are highest in the initial years following the SCI and gradually decrease afterward. However, these costs begin to rise again after the fourth year. It is important to recognize that the financial implications of SCI resemble those of chronic diseases, as households must provide ongoing support for the SCI patient until death. Treatment expenses are elevated during the initial years as the patient and their family work towards stability but tend to increase again in the final years leading up to death. For instance, the cost of treatment two months post-spinal cord injury (SCI) was 30,500 US$. This cost rose to 7,400 US$ during the period from two to eleven months after the injury. Subsequently, it decreased to 1,500 US$ during the rehabilitation phase but increased again to 6,200 US$ in the five months leading up to death [32].

In Iran, catastrophic health costs for SCI patients are significant. Despite high basic insurance coverage for these patients in this study, the high prevalence of catastrophic costs is attributed to insufficient cost-sharing by health insurance providers and a lack of coverage for certain services, such as home care and rehabilitation. Health insurance policies must expand their coverage and enhance cost-sharing to reduce out-of-pocket expenses for SCI patients. Achieving Universal Health Coverage (UHC) is essential for managing the catastrophic costs associated with such conditions.

Furthermore, implementing preventive strategies to control bedsores and pain can be effective in diminishing the catastrophic costs borne by households. A systematic review has indicated that the cost of preventing bedsores is relatively low, and such prevention strategies are both cost-effective and capable of saving significant amounts of money for patients. Thus, preventative interventions can help alleviate the catastrophic expenses faced by SCI patients.

Additionally, early detection of bedsores can lead to lower treatment costs and reduce catastrophic financial burdens. The findings of this study revealed that coping strategies aimed at preventing catastrophic costs were largely ineffective within households. Only four households managed to avoid catastrophic costs through these strategies. It is noteworthy that out of the total sample, only four households did not utilize coping strategies, while thirty-two earned less than 500 US$.

Study limitations

The study encountered some limitations. Firstly, the methodologies used for calculating costs and catastrophic costs differ among costing studies, which creates a challenge in comparing our results to other researches. To address this issue, we adopted a different approach for calculating catastrophic costs. Secondly, the data available for analyzing the relationship between catastrophic costs and the number of years since the onset of spinal cord injury (SCI) were insufficient. A more robust analysis is needed, particularly with a dynamic examination of these two factors. Thirdly, recall bias may have influenced the cost data, as patients and their family members might not accurately remember the amounts paid for various items. However, in the InSCI-IR study, efforts were made to minimize this bias by collecting cost data for the preceding 30 days whenever possible. Additionally, we utilized patients’ medical records, health insurance documents, and registered data from hospitals to further mitigate this type of bias. Another limitation is related to the estimation of household income and wealth. While this approach helps reduce some reporting bias, recent evidence suggests that variance in self-reporting of assets and wealth indicators can still affect the accuracy of household classification and wealth scores [33]. This issue should be considered when interpreting our results. Moreover, the data are derived exclusively from Guilan Province in northern Iran. Given the socioeconomic and health system differences across provinces, the results may not be generalizable to the entire country. Future studies covering multiple regions in Iran are needed to confirm whether these findings apply nationally.

Conclusion

The burden of catastrophic costs is significant for households with spinal cord injury (SCI) patients in Guilan province, Iran. Current coping strategies have proven ineffective in shielding these families from financial strain. Enhancing Universal Health Coverage and implementing preventative measures against bedsores may alleviate the financial burdens faced by these patients. Future research should consider conducting a dynamic analysis to track the lifetime changes in catastrophic costs associated with SCI.
