Regulation of mitochondrial autophagy by lncRNA MALAT1 in sepsis-induced myocardial injury
Authors: Guangqing Huang, Xu Zhao, Yong Bai, Jie Liu, Wei Li, Yongquan Wu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSepsis-induced myocardial injury (SIMI) is a severe complication of sepsis, contributing significantly to mortality. Mitochondrial dysfunction and dysregulated autophagy are implicated in SIMI pathogenesis. Long non-coding RNA MALAT1 has been associated with various diseases, including sepsis, but its role in SIMI remains unclear.ObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the role of lncRNA MALAT1 in SIMI, specifically in the regulation of mitochondrial autophagy.MethodsA sepsis-induced cardiomyopathy model was established in mice, and the cardiac tissues were analyzed. The expression of lncRNA MALAT1 was modulated and its effects on mitochondrial autophagy, myocardial injury, inflammation, and apoptosis were assessed. Furthermore, the interaction between MALAT1 and miR-146a was explored, as well as the involvement of the TLR4/NF-kB/MAPK signaling pathway.ResultsActivation of mitochondrial autophagy by urolithin A (UA) alleviated SIMI, inflammation, and cardiac dysfunction. Downregulation of MALAT1 enhanced mitochondrial autophagy, stabilized the mitochondrial membrane potential, and inhibited mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, leading to improved cell viability and reduced myocardial injury. Furthermore, MALAT1 interacted with miR-146a, and their modulation influenced mitochondrial autophagy, myocardial injury, and inflammation. The TLR4/NF-kB/MAPK signaling pathway was implicated in these processes.ConclusionOur findings suggest that lncRNA MALAT1 plays a crucial role in SIMI by modulating miR-146a-mediated mitochondrial autophagy and the TLR4/NF-kB/MAPK signaling pathway. These results provide new insights into the pathogenesis of SIMI and potential therapeutic targets.Keywords: LncRNA MALAT1, Sepsis, Myocardial injury, Mitophagy, MiR-146a

Introduction

Sepsis is a severe systemic inflammatory response caused by infection, which can lead to multiple organ dysfunction, including myocardial (heart muscle) injury. Sepsis-induced myocardial injury (SIMI) is one of the main manifestations of sepsis, characterized by reversible myocardial suppression. Inflammatory influences on myocardial cells can result in infiltration, edema, and mitochondrial damage. Studies have shown that the incidence of cardiac dysfunction caused by sepsis can be as high as 70%, making it a major predictor of sepsis-related mortality [1]. The mortality rate of septic patients with cardiovascular impairment is 70–90% higher than that of septic patients without cardiovascular injury, with the latter having a mortality rate of 20% [2]. The pathogenesis of SIMI is not yet fully understood, but research suggests that it is associated with multiple factors, such as inflammatory storm, cell apoptosis, autophagy, oxidative stress, necrosis, and mitochondrial damage.

Mitochondrial dysfunction plays a critical role in SIMI. Research has shown that mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS) contribute to cardiac inflammation in sepsis [3]. Approaches have been proposed to address the energy crisis in sepsis by eliminating dysfunctional mitochondria or recruiting functionally normal mitochondria from other systems [4]. Mitophagy, a key process in maintaining cellular homeostasis, is particularly important in mitochondrial quality control and cellular health. It selectively identifies, degrades, and clears damaged or excessive mitochondria through autophagic mechanisms [5]. Mitophagy plays a significant role in various physiological and pathological conditions, including tumors, Parkinson's disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, and atherosclerosis [6–10]. Mitophagy is considered to be involved in protecting cells from ischemia/reperfusion injury and oxidative stress [11, 12]. Recent studies have found an association between mitochondrial damage in sepsis-induced cardiomyopathy and reduced mitochondrial autophagy, leading to the accumulation of damaged mitochondria [13]. Impaired mitophagy results in the accumulation of dysfunctional mitochondria, exacerbating oxidative stress and inflammation in SIMI [14]. Shi et al. found that Levosimendan alleviates LPS-induced cardiac dysfunction and oxidative stress in septic mice by inducing mitophagy through the activation of the PINK1-Parkin pathway [15]. Moreover, Chen et al. found that hydrogen enhances mitophagy by activating the PINK1/Parkin pathway, thereby reducing cell damage and acute lung injury in sepsis models [16]. This suggests that activation of mitochondrial autophagy may be an effective approach to reducing SIMI [17]. Minocycline has been shown to enhance cardiomyocyte autophagy, improving mitochondrial and cardiac function [18]. Valproic acid (VPA) alleviates sepsis-induced myocardial dysfunction (SIMD) by enhancing autophagy and reducing mitochondrial damage, oxidative stress, and cardiac inflammation [19]. However, the specific mechanisms warrant further investigation.

MALAT1 (Metastasis-associated lung adenocarcinoma transcript (1) is a long non-coding RNA that plays a crucial regulatory role in various biological processes. MALAT1 is a key regulatory factor in the metastatic phenotype of lung cancer cells [20]. Its overexpression is associated with tumor progression and poor prognosis in clear cell renal cell carcinoma [21]. In colorectal cancer, MALAT1 promotes cell proliferation and inhibits apoptosis [22]. In sepsis models, MALAT1 levels are upregulated [23, 24]. Knockdown of MALAT1 improves SIMI, inflammation, and cell apoptosis in animal and cell models [25, 26]. However, it remains unclear whether MALAT1 is involved in the mitigation of SIMI through mitochondrial autophagy. Therefore, this study aims to investigate whether the lncRNA MALAT1 participates in the regulation of SIMI by modulating mitochondrial autophagy. By delving into this mechanism, we can gain a better understanding of the pathogenesis and progression of SIMI, providing new targets and strategies for its treatment.

Materials and methods

Animals and treatment

Male mice (C57BL/6 J) aged 6–8 weeks were randomly assigned to 3 groups (n = 6/group): CTRL, LPS, and LPS + UA. LPS were dissolved with sterile normal saline. To activate mitophagy, urolithin A (UA; 30 mg/kg) was intraperitoneally injected in sterile saline with 0.1% DMSO prior to LPS treatment. Then the mice were injected intraperitoneally with a single dose of LPS (5 mg/kg) for 48 h to induce septic cardiomyopathy. After treatment, blood samples were collected to measure the myocardial injury markers, such as creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB), troponin T, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH). In the experiment involving the administration of the mitophagy inhibitor mdivi-1, mdivi-1 (10 mg/kg) was injected intraperitoneally 24 h after LPS induction. The animals were euthanized via pentobarbital 48 h after LPS injection. Heart tissues were then isolated and used for subsequent experiments.

Echocardiography and evaluation of cardiomyocyte shortening/re-lengthening

The heart performance was measured by Echocardiography in all mice 48 h after LPS injection (14.0 MHz transducer, Sequoia C512; Acuson, Siemens, Erlangen, Germany). The cardiomyocytes mechanical properties were assessed with SoftEdge Myocam system (IonOptix, Milton, MA, USA). The changes of cardiomyocyte length were monitored using SoftEdge software and evaluated through the indices as follows: peak shortening (PS), time-to-PS (TPS), time-to-90% re-lengthening (TR90), maximal velocity of shortening (+ dL/dt), and re-lengthening (-dL/dt).

ELISA

The concentrations of inflammatory cytokines, including tumor-necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin (IL)-6, IL-1β, and cardiac injury markers, including creatine kinase-MB, LDH, and troponin T, were determined with ELISA kits in accordance with manufacturer's protocols. The concentrations of creatine kinase (CK; Nanjing Jiangcheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China) in supernatants were detected following the instructions of the corresponding kit.

HE staining

Myocardial tissues were fixed, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned (4 μm). Afterward, the paraffin sections were dewaxed in xylene, and dehydrated through a graded series of ethanol, followed by hematoxylin staining for 5 min. After being rinsed in running water, the sections were differentiated in acid ethanol (30 s), soaked in tap water (15 min), and stained in eosin (2 min). After a series of treatment (dehydration, xylene clearing, and neutral resin sealing), the tissues were observed under a microscope (Olympus, USA).

TUNEL staining

The paraffin sections obtained from the above experiment were dewaxed with xylene for 10 min, again 10 min with fresh xylene, and dehydrated through a serial alcohol gradient. Twenty μg/mL DNase-free protease K was added into tissue slides and incubated at 20–37 °C for 15–30 min, followed by a PBS wash for three times. Then, 50-μL TUNEL solution was added into tissue slides and incubated at 37 °C for 60 min in the dark. After washing by PBS for three times, tissue slides were treated with anti-fluorescence quenching agent and then observed by fluorescence microscopy at excitation wavelength of 450–500 nm and emission wavelength of 515–565 nm.

Determination of mitochondrial transmembrane potential (ΔΨm)

Tetrachloro-tetraethyl benzimidazol carbocyanine iodide (JC-1) was employed to evaluate the changes in ΔΨm. Cardiomyocytes were cultured in disposable confocal dishes at a density of 5 × l04 cells/dish. The cells were incubated with 5 μM JC-1 (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology) for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark and washed twice with PBS. JC-1-labelled cells were visualized using an Olympus FV1000 laser confocal microscope. Cellular mitochondria with normal ΔΨm emitted red fluorescence (J-aggregate), while those with abnormal ΔΨm showed green fluorescence (J-monomer).

ATP measurements

ATP production of chondrocyte was investigated using ATP Assay Kits (ab83355, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), according to manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, 5 × 105 cells were homogenized in ATP Assay Buffer. Aliquots of cell lysates were incubated with ATP Reaction Mix for 30 min. Absorbance of the mixtures was quantified using a spectrophotometer at a 570 nm wavelength. ATP levels were calculated from a standard curve plotted by the serial dilution of authentic ATP.

Mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS) measurements

The mitochondrial ROS concentration in cells was measured using the cell-permeant MitoSOX Red mitochondrial superoxide indicator (Molecular Probes, USA).

mt-Keima assays

Mitophagy activity was observed using the mt-Keima assay, which was performed using a mitochondria-targeted mKeima-Red expression plasmid (pMT-mKeima-Red, #AM-V-251, MBL Medical & Biological Laboratories, Co., Ltd.. Woburn, MA).

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, CA, USA) was used to analyze the data, and the significance between the two groups was analyzed via Student’s t-test. Significance between multiple groups was analyzed via one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). P < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Results

Activation of mitochondrial autophagy alleviates sepsis-induced myocardial injury

To investigate the cardioprotective role of mitochondrial autophagy in sepsis-induced myocardial disease, we administered a mitochondrial autophagy inducer, urolithin A (UA), to mice prior to lipopolysaccharide (LPS) treatment. Compared to the control group, LPS-mediated sepsis significantly increased the levels of cardiac injury markers, such as cardiac troponin T (Fig. 1A), CK-MB (Fig. 1B), and LDH (Fig. 1C). In contrast, administration of UA, which activated mitochondrial autophagy, suppressed the levels of myocardial injury biomarkers. The LPS-induced sepsis also led to a significant increase in pro-inflammatory cytokines in cardiac tissue compared to the control group, including TNF-α (Fig. 1D), IL-6 (Fig. 1E), and IL-1β (Fig. 1F). However, UA administration significantly reduced the levels of these inflammatory factors compared to the LPS group. Histological examination of cardiac tissue using HE staining revealed disrupted myocardial cells and membrane damage in the LPS group. Conversely, these pathological changes were mitigated in the UA-treated group (Fig. 1G). The percentage of TUNEL-positive cells, indicating apoptosis, was higher in the LPS-treated group compared to the control group, whereas UA administration inhibited LPS-induced cardiomyocyte apoptosis (Fig. 2A). Western blot analysis showed elevated levels of Bax and cleaved caspase-3 proteins, along with decreased Bcl-2 levels, in myocardial tissue of the LPS-treated group, indicating LPS-induced cardiomyocyte apoptosis. However, UA administration inhibited LPS-induced cardiomyocyte apoptosis by modulating the expression levels of these proteins (Fig. 2B). These results suggest that activation of mitochondrial autophagy alleviates sepsis-induced myocardial injury.

Activation of mitochondrial autophagy alleviates sepsis-induced cardiac dysfunction

Compared to the control group, LPS treatment decreased left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), left ventricular fractional shortening (LVFS), and left ventricular end-systolic pressure (LVESP), while increasing left ventricular end-diastolic pressure (LVEDP). However, administration of UA reversed the changes in cardiac functional parameters induced by LPS (Fig. 3A–E). LPS impaired the contractile and diastolic function of myocardial cells, as evidenced by decreased peak shortening (PS), maximal rates of contraction and relaxation (± dL/dt), and prolonged time to PS (TPS) and time to 90% relengthening, compared to the control group. The LPS-induced myocardial dysfunction was attenuated or reversed by UA treatment (Fig. 3F–J). Overall, the activation of mitochondrial autophagy protected the cardiac function of mice with sepsis-induced myocardial disease.

Activation of mitochondrial autophagy attenuates sepsis-induced mitochondrial damage

Compared to the control group, LPS treatment reduced the mitochondrial membrane potential of myocardial cells. However, treatment with UA stabilized the mitochondrial membrane potential to levels close to normal (Fig. 4A). LPS treatment increased mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS) production in myocardial cells, while UA treatment effectively inhibited mitochondrial ROS production (Fig. 4B). LPS treatment decreased cellular ATP generation, whereas UA treatment reversed this effect (Fig. 4C). The levels of LC3II, Parkin, and PINK1 were elevated after LPS treatment, and compared to the LPS group, the LPS + UA group exhibited significantly higher levels of LC3II, Parkin, and PINK1. The expression of p62 was upregulated in the LPS group but downregulated in the LPS + UA group (Fig. 5A). LPS moderately induced mitochondrial autophagy, while UA strongly activated mitochondrial autophagy (Fig. 5B). LPS induction reduced the viability of myocardial cells, which was reversed by UA treatment (Fig. 5C). These results indicate that although mitochondrial autophagy in myocardial cells was mildly activated by LPS, endogenous activation failed to prevent sepsis-mediated myocardial injury. However, UA administration significantly reduced sepsis-induced cardiac dysfunction and myocardial damage by restoring mitochondrial function.

Downregulation of MALAT1 induces activation of mitochondrial autophagy in sepsis-induced cardiomyocytes

The expression level of MALAT1 in cardiac tissues was higher in the LPS-treated group compared to the control group, while administration of UA suppressed the expression of MALAT1 (Fig. 6A). LPS treatment promoted the expression of MALAT1 in myocardial cells compared to the control group, whereas UA administration inhibited MALAT1 expression (Fig. 6B). Thus, the cardioprotective effects of mitochondrial autophagy may be associated with the inhibition of MALAT1 expression. We knocked down and examined the expression of MALAT1 in cardiomyocytes (Fig. 7A). Knockdown of MALAT1 was performed in LPS-induced cardiomyocytes. Additionally, to further investigate the role of MALAT1 in mitochondrial autophagy, the mitochondrial autophagy inhibitor Mdivi-1 was used (Fig. 7B). Knockdown of MALAT1 enhanced the mitochondrial membrane potential in LPS-induced cardiomyocytes, while Mdivi-1 blocked the effect of MALAT1 silencing on the mitochondrial membrane potential (Fig. 7C). Knockdown of MALAT1 inhibited LPS-induced mitochondrial ROS production, while Mdivi-1 promoted ROS production (Fig. 7D). Knockdown of MALAT1 enhanced ATP generation in LPS-induced cardiomyocytes, while Mdivi-1 blocked the effect of MALAT1 silencing on ATP production (Fig. 7E). Furthermore, we found that knockdown of MALAT1 induced the activation of mitochondrial autophagy in cardiomyocytes, while Mdivi-1 inhibited MALAT1-knockdown-induced mitochondrial autophagy (Fig. 8A, B). These results suggest that knockdown of MALAT1 induces the activation of mitochondrial autophagy in sepsis-induced cardiomyocytes.

Downregulation of MALAT1 inhibits LPS-induced cardiomyocyte damage through inducing mitochondrial autophagy

Through the detection of cardiomyocyte damage-related markers, we found that knockdown of MALAT1 alleviated LPS-induced cardiomyocyte damage, while Mdivi-1 reversed the protective effect of MALAT1 knockdown on LPS-induced H9c2 cell damage (Fig. 9A–E). The inhibitory effect of MALAT1 silencing on LPS-induced H9c2 cell apoptosis was reversed by Mdivi-1 (Fig. 10A, B). Similarly, the inhibitory effect of MALAT1 knockdown on LPS-induced H9c2 cell inflammation was reversed by Mdivi-1 (Fig. 10C–F). These results indicate that knockdown of MALAT1 improves LPS-induced cardiomyocyte damage by activating mitochondrial autophagy.

LncRNA MALAT1 regulates sepsis-induced cardiomyocyte damage via targeting miR-146a-mediated mitochondrial autophagy

Previous studies have shown that the long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) MALAT1 can regulate the immune response in lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced septic rats by targeting the miR-146a/NF-κB P65 pathway, thereby affecting the function of cardiac cells [27]. We predicted the binding site of MALAT1 to miR-146a (Fig. 11A). The luciferase activity of MALAT1-WT was inhibited by the miR-146a mimic, while no effect was observed on MALAT1-MUT, confirming the interaction between MALAT1 and miR-146a (Fig. 11B). The expression levels of miR-146a in the heart tissues and cardiomyocytes of the LPS-treated group were lower than those in the control group, while treatment with UA increased the expression of miR-146a (Fig. 11C, D). Knockdown of MALAT1 promoted the expression of miR-146a in cardiomyocytes. Knockdown of MALAT1 enhanced the expression of miR-146a in LPS-induced cardiomyocytes, while Mdivi-1 inhibited the promoting effect of MALAT1 knockdown on miR-146a expression (Fig. 11E, F). To further investigate whether MALAT1 regulates the progression of septic cardiomyopathy through targeting miR-146a, a rescue experiment was conducted using a miR-146a inhibitor. The decreased expression of miR-146a confirmed the successful transfection of the miR-146a inhibitor (Fig. 11G). Transfection of si-MALAT1 and miR-146a inhibitor into H9c2 cells resulted in the corresponding expression changes of miR-146a (Fig. 11H). Moreover, it was found that the inhibition of miR-146a expression reversed the alleviating effect of MALAT1 knockdown on LPS-induced H9c2 cell damage (Fig. 11I–M).

Silencing of MALAT1 inhibits the TLR4/NF-kB/MAPK signaling pathway via targeting miR-146a

Previous studies have demonstrated that MALAT1 can modulate the function of cardiac cells in lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced septic rats by targeting the miR-146a/NF-κB pathway [27]. Therefore, we further investigated this pathway. We found that knockdown of MALAT1 suppressed the activation of the TLR4/NF-kB/MAPK signaling pathway in LPS-induced H9c2 cells by upregulating the expression of miR-146a (Fig. 15A–C). Knockdown of MALAT1, by upregulating the expression of miR-146a, inhibited the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome in LPS-induced H9c2 cells (Fig. 15D). These results suggest that silencing of MALAT1 targets the miR-146a-mediated TLR4/NF-kB/MAPK signaling pathway, inhibits inflammation, and thereby alleviates sepsis-induced cardiomyocyte damage.

Discussion

Cardiac injury markers primarily include cardiac troponin T (cTnT), myoglobin (Myo), creatine kinase (CK), creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB), and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH). They are elevated in the bloodstream during myocardial tissue damage or necrosis, and are used for diagnosing and assessing myocardial injury [28, 29]. Bhardwaj et al. found that LPS significantly increased the production of LDH and CK-MB in the heart tissue of male rats [30]. Furthermore, several studies have reported increased levels of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), cardiac troponin T (cTnT), and creatine kinase MB (CK-MB) in LPS-induced sepsis models [31, 32]. Inflammatory response in cardiac cells leads to upregulation of various inflammatory factors, such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-1β, and interleukin-6 (IL-6). Myocardial cell inflammation induces elevation of various inflammatory mediators, including TNF-α, interleukin-1β, and interleukin-6 (IL-6) [33, 34]. In our study, we also observed elevated levels of cardiac injury-related markers and increased levels of inflammatory factors TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β in mice following LPS treatment. These findings indicate that LPS induces cardiac injury in mice, leading to inflammation.

Urolithin A (UA) is a natural dietary and gut microbiota-derived metabolite that has been shown to activate mitochondrial autophagy [35, 36]. Mice pre-treated with UA exhibited significantly reduced levels of cardiac injury markers compared to the group treated with LPS alone, along with a significant downregulation of various inflammatory factors. These findings suggest that UA activation of mitochondrial autophagy alleviates LPS-induced cardiomyocyte damage in mice. Multiple studies have also demonstrated that UA can decrease the expression levels of autophagy proteins p62, LC3 II, and LC3 II/I ratio in cardiomyocytes stimulated by high glucose, enhance autophagic flux, alleviate mitochondrial damage, and mitigate mitochondrial swelling, thereby alleviating cardiomyocyte injury [37, 38]. In addition to assessing cardiac injury markers and inflammatory factors, we also analyzed the impact of UA treatment on cell viability and cardiac function. In the group treated with LPS alone, the proportion of apoptotic cardiomyocytes significantly increased, accompanied by a significant elevation in pro-apoptotic protein levels, and a marked decline in cardiac function. Conversely, UA treatment significantly inhibited LPS-induced cell apoptosis and significantly improved cardiac function. These results indicate that UA suppresses LPS-induced cardiomyocyte damage by activating autophagy.

To further confirm whether UA alleviates myocardial injury through the activation of mitochondrial autophagy, we examined mitochondrial autophagy-related indicators. LC3 is a hallmark protein involved in the formation and expansion of autophagosomes during autophagy. LC3 exists in the cytoplasm as an unmodified form called LC3-I. During autophagy, LC3-I is converted to LC3-II by enzymes on the membrane of autophagosomes. LC3-II associates with the outer membrane of autophagosomes, facilitating their expansion and closure. Therefore, the detection of LC3-II levels is commonly used as an indicator to evaluate cellular autophagic activity [39, 40]. p62 is an important protein cargo that promotes the clearance of damaged proteins through both proteasomes and autophagosomes. During autophagy, p62 acts as a bridge between LC3 (a membrane protein that plays a crucial role in cellular autophagy) and ubiquitinated proteins, selectively enveloping them into autophagosomes for subsequent degradation by proteolytic enzymes in autolysosomes [41, 42]. Hence, the expression level of p62 is negatively correlated with autophagic activity. Parkin is an E3 ubiquitin ligase primarily involved in the process of mitophagy, which is the selective autophagy of damaged mitochondria. Parkin plays a critical role in the autophagic process of impaired mitochondria by recruiting PINK1 and promoting mitochondrial autophagy through PINK1's kinase activity [43, 44]. We found that UA treatment significantly increased the level of mitochondrial autophagy and reduced the autophagic substrate p62 compared to the group treated with LPS alone. Keima is a pH-sensitive fluorescent protein with dual excitation spectra dependent on the surrounding pH, being activated at 440 nm and 586 nm in neutral and acidic environments, respectively. Keima's characteristic fluorescent signals in neutral and acidic pH can intuitively reflect the degree of autophagy [45]. Through mt-Keima labeling of mitochondrial autophagy, we also observed that UA significantly activated the process of mitochondrial autophagy. These studies collectively indicate that UA indeed alleviates myocardial injury through the activation of mitochondrial autophagy.

In exploring the role of MALAT1, a long non-coding RNA known to be upregulated in sepsis [46], we found that MALAT1 knockdown alleviated LPS-induced myocardial injury by promoting mitochondrial autophagy. This aligns with previous studies showing that MALAT1 knockdown improves inflammation and cell apoptosis [47]. Our data further reveal that MALAT1 regulates mitochondrial autophagy and myocardial injury through its interaction with miR-146a, a key regulator of inflammation and autophagy. The luciferase assays and expression analyses confirm that MALAT1 directly targets miR-146a, influencing mitochondrial function and myocardial damage.

The interaction between MALAT1 and miR-146a is particularly significant as miR-146a is involved in regulating the TLR4/NF-κB/MAPK signaling pathway, which is critical for the inflammatory response in sepsis [48, 49]. Our results indicate that MALAT1 knockdown, through its modulation of miR-146a, suppresses the activation of this pathway, thereby reducing inflammation and myocardial damage. The reversal of protective effects by miR-146a inhibition further underscores the pivotal role of miR-146a in mediating the benefits of MALAT1 knockdown.

While these findings provide a comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms through which mitochondrial autophagy and MALAT1 affect sepsis-induced myocardial injury, it is important to acknowledge the study’s limitations. The reliance on animal models and cell experiments, while insightful, may not fully replicate the complexity of human sepsis and myocardial injury. Future research should aim to validate these results in clinical settings and explore additional therapeutic strategies for targeting mitochondrial autophagy and MALAT1 in sepsis.

Conclusion

In summary, our study demonstrates that activation of mitochondrial autophagy through UA and MALAT1 knockdown alleviates sepsis-induced myocardial injury by enhancing mitochondrial function and modulating inflammatory responses via the miR-146a/TLR4/NF-κB/MAPK signaling pathway. These findings offer valuable insights into potential therapeutic approaches for managing sepsis-related cardiac dysfunction.
