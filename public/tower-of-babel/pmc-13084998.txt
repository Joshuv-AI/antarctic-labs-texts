Effect of secukinumab dose escalation on MRI-detected inflammation and structural damage in axial spondyloarthritis: a clinical, investigator-initiated, treat-to-target study (TRACE)
Authors: Nora Vladimirova, Mikkel Østergaard, Sengül Seven, Jakob M Møller, Bente Jensen, Rene Østgård, Rene Poggenborg, Ole Rintek Madsen, Oliver Hendricks, Lau Brix, Stylianos Georgiadis, Susanne Juhl Pedersen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionEvidence of treat-to-target strategies including secukinumab dose escalation in patients with axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA) remains limited. We investigated the effect of a treat-to-target approach aiming for Axial Spondyloarthritis Disease Activity Score (ASDAS) remission, assessed by clinical and MRI outcomes over 24 weeks.MethodsTRACE was a multicentre study including biologic-naïve patients with active axSpA. Sixteen weeks after secukinumab 150 mg initiation, patients in ASDAS remission (<1.3)(week 16 remitters) continued the same dose, while non-remitters (week 16 non-remitters) escalated to 300 mg/month. Sacroiliac joint (SIJ) and spine MRIs were performed at baseline, weeks 4, 16 and 24, and centrally scored using Spondyloarthritis Research Consortium of Canada (SPARCC) and Canada-Denmark scores for inflammation and structural lesions. The primary end point was total MRI-inflammation improvement ≥2 (weeks 16–24) comparing week 16 remitters and week 16 non-remitters.ResultsOf 90 patients, 68% were male, mean age was 35.4 years. At week 16, 30 (35%) patients achieved ASDAS remission. At week 24, 36 (49%) patients were in ASDAS remission, including 10 (22%) week 16 non-remitters, who responded after dose escalation (dose-escalation responders).Already at week 4, marked reductions in total MRI inflammation (SPARCC spine+SIJ) (–14.7), and structural lesion remodelling (reduced SIJ erosion; increased SIJ fat and backfill), were observed. These changes continued through week 16, with only minor improvements thereafter.The primary end point occurred in 14 (52%) of week 16 remitters versus 19 (41%) of week 16 non-remitters (p=0.53). Dose-escalation responders had a numerically larger mean total MRI inflammation reduction (–6.1) than dose-escalation non-responders (–2.2).ConclusionNo statistically significant effect of secukinumab dose escalation on reducing MRI-detected inflammation was observed, except in a selected subgroup of patients. However, 22% achieved ASDAS remission after dose escalation. Notably, substantial MRI-detected inflammation reduction and structural remodelling occurred within the first 4 weeks.Trial registration numberNCT03639740.Keywords: Axial Spondyloarthritis, Biological Therapy, Magnetic Resonance Imaging, Inflammation

Introduction

Axial spondyloarthritis (AxSpA) is a chronic inflammatory disease characterised by entheseal and subchondral bone inflammation primarily affecting the sacroiliac joints (SIJs) and the spine, leading to chronic back pain, impaired function and varying degrees of progressive spine deformity.1

MRI is the gold standard for detecting inflammation in the SIJs and the spine and also allows assessment of structural damage progression.2 Validated MRI-scoring systems provide objective evaluation of lesion evolution and are the most sensitive tools for capturing early effects of anti-inflammatory treatment in axSpA.3

Current management guidelines for axSpA recommend a treat-to-target approach to improve disease outcomes and reduce structural progression,4 although few studies have evaluated the effectiveness of this approach.5 6 Secukinumab, a fully human monoclonal antibody targeting interleukin-17A (IL-17A), a cytokine central to both inflammation and structural damage in axSpA,7 8 is currently approved for treatment at both 150 mg, in both radiographic (r-) and non-radiographic axSpA, and 300 mg in r-axSpA when active symptoms persist, with comparable safety profiles.9 10 At the time the study was designed, secukinumab was only approved for r-axSpA at a 150 mg dose, and alternative biologic treatment options were more limited. Therefore, it remained unclear whether dose escalation could benefit selected patients, making secukinumab a relevant candidate for dose escalation within a treat-to-target strategy.6 11 However, no clear guidelines exist regarding secukinumab dose escalation in axSpA, and evidence on its impact—particularly on objective outcome measures such as MRI-detected inflammation—remains limited, representing a key knowledge gap that this study intended to address.12

The aim of this study was to evaluate the effect of secukinumab dose escalation (from 150 mg to 300 mg) on MRI-detected SIJ and spine inflammation in biologic-naïve patients with axSpA who did not achieve remission (defined as Axial Spondyloarthritis Disease Activity Score (ASDAS) <1.3) at week 16, as compared with patients who achieved ASDAS remission at week 16 and continued secukinumab 150 mg to week 24. In addition, we aimed to investigate the longitudinal changes in clinical and MRI outcomes, including MRI inflammation and structural damage of the SIJ and spine, over a 24-week follow-up period.

Methods

Study design

This investigator-initiated, open-label, multicentre observational study (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT03639740; treat-to-target with secukinumab in axSpA (TRACE)) was conducted to evaluate treatment outcomes. Identification of MRI and biochemical biomarkers for disease activity, treatment response and structural damage progression evaluated the effects of a treat-to-target strategy with secukinumab in patients with axSpA. Patients were enrolled between February 2019 and May 2022 from five Danish rheumatology centres: Center for Rheumatology and Spine Diseases at Rigshospitalet (Glostrup, Frederiksberg and Gentofte); Rheumatology Department at Silkeborg Regional Hospital and the Danish Hospital for Rheumatic Diseases, Sønderborg.

Study population

Patients, diagnosed with axSpA with inadequate response to ≥2 different non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and clinical indication for biological drug initiation, were considered eligible for the study. Inclusion criteria included (1) age 18–70 years; (2) fulfilment of the Assessment of SpondyloArthritis international Society (ASAS) classification criteria for axSpA and/or the modified New York criteria (mNYc) for ankylosing spondylitis (AS)13 14 judged by a rheumatologist; (3) active inflammation on SIJ or spine MRI (fulfilled by all patients) or fulfilment of mNYc as evaluated by a central SpA imaging expert (MØ or SJP)14 15; (4) high disease activity with ASDAS ≥2.1, (5) total back pain ≥40 assessed by Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Disease Activity Index (BASDAI) question 2 and (6) sufficient contraception for women. Exclusion criteria included prior biologic use, glucocorticoids within 4 weeks before baseline MRI and contraindications to secukinumab or MRI (online supplemental table S1).

Intervention and treatment protocol

All enrolled patients initiated secukinumab 150 mg at baseline, which was received as subcutaneous injections of secukinumab 150 mg weekly for 4 weeks, followed by secukinumab 150 mg monthly until week 16. At week 16, patients were evaluated for clinical remission, that is, ASDAS <1.3, which was the target of the treatment algorithm. Patients in remission (w16REM group) continued with 150 mg monthly until week 24, while patients not in remission (w16NON-REM group) increased the dose to 300 mg monthly from week 16. Concomitant treatment with paracetamol, NSAIDs and conventional synthetic disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drug was permitted in a fixed dose from 4 weeks before the first MRI; glucocorticoids, other DMARDs and opioid analgesics were not allowed.

Clinical, laboratory and patient-reported outcomes

At each visit (weeks 0, 4, 16 and 24), patients completed validated questionnaires and clinical assessments as described in the ASAS core outcome set for axSpA16 (details in table 1). Biochemical analyses included safety laboratory tests and high-sensitivity C reactive protein (CRP).

Imaging

MRI scans included sagittal short tau inversion recovery (STIR) and T1-weighted images of the spine, and coronal oblique STIR and T1-weighted images of SIJs, acquired using 3 T systems (Ingenia Elition, Philips, the Netherlands; and Vida, Siemens, Germany) and 1.5 T systems (Avanto and Sola, Siemens, Germany), in accordance with existing international recommendations.17 Radiography of the pelvis (anteroposterior projection) and lumbar and cervical spine (lateral projections) was also obtained. Imaging was performed at the radiology departments of participating centres.

Baseline MRIs and radiographs were performed within 2 weeks prior to initiation of secukinumab to confirm inclusion criteria fulfilment. Follow-up MRIs of the SIJs and spine were acquired at weeks 4, 16 and 24.

Imaging assessment

All images were anonymised and read in unknown chronological order by an experienced reader (SJP), blinded to clinical and other imaging data. MRI of SIJs was assessed for the presence of inflammation (ie, bone marrow oedema (BME)) and structural lesions (ie, fat lesion, erosion, backfill and ankylosis) according to the Spondyloarthritis Research Consortium of Canada (SPARCC) MRI SIJ inflammation score18 and SPARCC MRI SIJ structural score.19 Correspondingly, the spine was assessed according to the SPARCC MRI spine inflammation score20 and by the Canada-Denmark (CANDEN) MRI spine score for inflammation (ie, BME) and structural lesions (ie, fat lesion, erosion, new bone formation (bone spur/ankylosis)) at distinct anatomical locations in the cervical, thoracic and lumbar segments.21 22 For CANDEN, inflammation subscores were calculated (details in table 2). Total MRI inflammation score was calculated as the sum of SPARCC SIJ and SPARCC spine inflammation scores. MRI inflammation improvement was defined as a ≥2-point reduction in the total MRI inflammation score. A ≥2-point reduction in the total MRI inflammation score was selected because prior work has shown that improvements of this magnitude (specifically the established minimally important change of 2.5 points for SIJ inflammation) correlate with clinically meaningful patient-reported improvement.23

End points

To compare reductions in MRI inflammation in the SIJs and spine from week 16 to 24 in patients who at week 16 were in ASDAS remission versus not in ASDAS remission, the primary end point was predefined as the proportion of patients with a decrease ≥2 in total MRI inflammation score from week 16 to 24 in the w16REM versus the w16NON-REM groups.

Secondary end points included the following comparisons between the w16REM versus the w16NON-REM groups: (1) the proportion of patients in ASDAS remission versus not in remission at week 24; (2) changes in ASDAS score from weeks 0–16 to 16–24; (3) proportions of patients improving from week 16 to 24 measured by a reduction ≥2 in the CANDEN spine, SPARCC SIJ and SPARCC spine inflammation scores; (4) changes in MRI inflammation scores from weeks 0–16 to 16–24 measured by the CANDEN spine, SPARCC SIJ and SPARCC spine inflammation scores and (5) changes in MRI structural scores from week 16 to 24 measured by the CANDEN spine and SPARCC SIJ structural scores. Finally, in all patients, changes in SIJ and spine MRI inflammation and structural scores between weeks 0, 4, 16 and 24 were assessed.

Safety evaluation

Safety monitoring for adverse events (AEs) and serious AEs (SAEs) was performed for all included patients who received secukinumab treatment during the study period and were defined and reported in accordance with the European and Danish Medicines Agency regulations.

Statistical analysis

Data were characterised by mean (SD) for continuous variables and number (percentage) for categorical variables. Between-group comparisons were made using the Mann-Whitney-Wilcoxon test for continuous variables, and χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. The number of patients enrolled in the study followed a predefined power calculation (details in online supplemental material).

Results

In total, 90 patients (male 68%, mean age 35.4 years) were enrolled in the study and initiated treatment with secukinumab 150 mg (table 1). The mean symptom duration was 7.7 years (SD 7.0), mean time since diagnosis was 2.4 years (SD 4.0). A total of 72% of patients were human leucocyte antigen-B27 positive and 39% had radiographic axSpA according to the mNYc. At baseline, the mean total MRI inflammation score (SPARCC SIJ+SPARCC spine) was 42.5 (SD 37.0).

Eighty-five patients completed the week 16 follow-up (figure 1). Of these 85 patients, 30 (35%) had achieved ASDAS remission (w16REM) and continued secukinumab 150 mg monthly, while 55 patients (65%) had not reached remission (w16NON-REM). In four cases, dose escalation was not pursued due to patient/physician preference, as patients experienced substantial benefit and were unwilling to change a well-tolerated 150 mg secukinumab regimen. These patients were excluded from further analysis due to protocol violation. Secukinumab dose was escalated to 300 mg monthly in 51 of the w16NON-REM patients.

Patient disposition week 0–16: five patients discontinued the study—one due to non-compliance; three due to adverse events, including severe urticaria, recurrent infections and Campylobacter infection with diarrhoea; and one due to elective pilonidal cyst surgery. Week 16: in four cases within the group of patients not in Axial Spondyloarthritis Disease Activity Score (ASDAS) remission at the week 16 visit, secukinumab dose was not escalated due to patient/physician decision (patients had experienced substantial benefit and were unwilling to escalate dose). These patients were excluded from the subsequent analysis due to protocol violation. Week 16–24: four patients discontinued the study—two due to non-compliance (missed MRI or missed secukinumab dose) and two due to adverse events, including one case of severe urticaria and one myocardial infarction. Week 24: among patients who completed the week 24 visit (n=77), MRI data from both week 16 and week 24 were available for 27 of 29 patients in the week 16 ASDAS remission group and for 46 of 48 patients in the week 16 ASDAS non-remission group. ASDAS could not be calculated in four cases due to missing components (patient-reported outcomes or C reactive protein), and these patients were excluded from the analysis.

Seventy-seven patients completed the week 24 follow-up. ASDAS could be calculated in 73 patients, of these 36 (49%) were in ASDAS remission, 26 (36%) had sustained remission since week 16 and 10 (14%) achieved remission following dose escalation to 300 mg at week 16. At baseline, patients who achieved ASDAS remission by week 16 were younger and had lower disease activity measures compared with those who did not, while no differences were observed in other clinical measures, including CRP, or in MRI scores for BME and structural lesions (table 1).

Improvement in MRI inflammation from week 16 to week 24

The primary end point, comparing the proportion of patients with total MRI inflammation improvement from week 16 to 24 between those in ASDAS remission (who remained on secukinumab 150 mg/month) and those not in remission (who escalated to 300 mg/month) at week 16, was not met. MRI inflammation improvement was observed in 14 (52%) of w16REM and in 19 (41%) of w16NON-REM patients (p=0.5), among those who were followed to week 24 and had available MRIs (figure 2). The reduction of MRI inflammation scores also did not differ significantly between the two groups (w16REM vs w16NON-REM) (table 3). The mean reduction in the total MRI score was −2.7 (SD 5.9) in w16REM and −2.8 (SD 8.7) in w16NON-REM patients (p=0.4). In the subgroup of patients not in remission at week 16 (w16NON-REM), who achieved remission by week 24 following dose escalation (n = 10), the mean reduction was numerically larger at –6.1 (SD 11.6), compared with –2.2 (SD 8.0) in patients still not in remission in week 24 (n=35).

Changes in MRI inflammation and structural lesions from baseline

In the total population, reductions in MRI inflammation were evident as early as week 4, with a significant decrease in the mean total MRI inflammation score (−14.7 (SD 15.5)). Decreases in active inflammation (BME) were recorded by the SPARCC SIJ inflammation score (−6.9 (SD 9.2)), SPARCC spine score (−7.8 (SD 11.5)) and CANDEN spine inflammation score (−6.0 (SD 12.1)). All CANDEN subscores also declined, including the anterior vertebral corners (−3.2 (SD 5.7)) and the posterior spine segments (−2.1 (SD 5.7)) (table 2). This reduction in SIJ and spine inflammation occurred most rapidly within the first 4 weeks, continued steadily through week 16 and showed smaller incremental decreases up to week 24 (figures3 4).

Changes in MRI structural lesions were evident already by week 4 and continued at a slower pace through weeks 16 and 24 (table 2). In the SIJs, there was a decrease in SPARCC erosion score, an increase in SPARCC fat and backfill scores, while ankylosis remained unchanged. Similarly, in the spine, the CANDEN fat score increased over time (figure 4), while both erosion and ankylosis scores remained unchanged. The pattern of structural lesion changes over time was similar in w16REM and w16NON-REM groups, across all lesion types.

Effect of secukinumab on clinically assessed disease activity

A rapid decline in clinical disease activity was observed after treatment initiation with early improvements in BASDAI, back pain, fatigue and patient global, alongside sustained improvements in functioning and overall health status (ASAS Health Index, BASFI and BASMI). Changes in clinical parameters and patient-reported outcomes from baseline to week 24 are presented in table 2, while figure 3 illustrates the change in ASDAS scores over the same period.

By week 16, 52 (61%) of patients achieved low disease activity (ASDAS <2.1), and 48 (66%) by week 24. In contrast, the more stringent threshold of ASDAS remission (<1.3) was met by 30 (35%) at week 16 and 36 (49%) at week 24.

Between week 16 and week 24, patients not in ASDAS remission at week 16 who escalated to secukinumab 300 mg experienced significant additional improvements in clinical disease activity measures and function, including BASDAI, patient global, total back pain, morning stiffness, fatigue, BASFI, BASMI and ASAS Health Index (table 3). Among these patients, 20 (44%) reached ASDAS low disease activity and 10 (22%) achieved ASDAS remission by week 24 (figure 1). In contrast, patients already in ASDAS remission at week 16 who continued on 150 mg showed minimal further change, reflecting maintained low disease activity.

Safety

In all patients included in the study (n=90), a total of 88 AEs were reported during the study period, most commonly infections (including COVID-19), allergic reactions and gastrointestinal symptoms (table 4). No suspected unexpected serious adverse reactions occurred. SAEs included pyelonephritis (n=1), diarrhoea (n=2) and myocardial infarction (n=1). Treatment was discontinued due to AEs in six patients (figure 1). One uveitis flare, one case of new-onset Crohn’s disease and one case of new-onset psoriasis were recorded. No new safety signals were identified.

Discussion

This is the first study to evaluate inflammatory and structural MRI changes in the SIJs and spine of biologic-naïve patients with axSpA treated with secukinumab using a treat-to-target approach aiming for ASDAS remission (<1.3). After 16 weeks on the secukinumab 150 mg dose, one-third of patients achieved ASDAS remission, whereas two-thirds did not and increased the dose to 300 mg/month. No statistically significant effect of secukinumab dose escalation on reducing MRI inflammation was observed and the prespecified primary end point was therefore not met. However, patients not in ASDAS remission at week 16 achieved further improvements in clinical activity and function after dose escalation. Furthermore, this study for the first time demonstrated that significant reductions in MRI SIJ and spine inflammation and MRI SIJ bone erosion can be observed as early as 4 weeks after secukinumab treatment initiation, and that these improvements and the structural remodelling occur much faster in this first month than later in the course (weeks 4–24).

The overall MRI improvement in inflammation did not differ between those who continued 150 mg secukinumab and those who escalated to 300 mg at week 16. However, 22% of the patients who increased dose due to not being in remission (w16NON-REM group) achieved ASDAS remission following dose escalation and 44% reached ASDAS low disease activity. This subgroup also showed a numerically greater reduction in total MRI inflammation between weeks 16 and 24 compared with patients who were already in remission at week 16 and those who never achieved remission, suggesting a potential benefit of dose escalation in selected individuals. These findings align with preliminary results from the Treat-to-target in Axial Spondyloarthritis (AScalate) trial, where a similar proportion (28%) of patients with axSpA responded—achieving a clinically important improvement in ASDAS (ie, an absolute reduction ≥1.1)—to secukinumab dose escalation within a treat-to-target strategy compared with standard-of-care strategy.11 Similar findings were also reported in the secukinumab dose‑escalation study in AS (ASLeap) study by Deodhar et al, where patients with axSpA not in ASDAS remission (<1.3) after 16 weeks of secukinumab 150 mg were randomised to continue the same dose or escalate to 300 mg.6 Although overall remission rates were comparable at weeks 24 and 52, dose escalation improved outcomes in patients with a moderate initial response, leading the authors to propose its use in selected subsets.6 Altogether, these findings support an individualised approach to dose intensification within a treat-to-target strategy.

This is the first study to demonstrate that changes in both inflammatory and structural MRI findings can be detected as early as 4 weeks after initiating secukinumab in patients with axSpA. We particularly observed early reductions in BME in the SIJs, vertebral body corners and postero-lateral spine segments. Interestingly, approximately half of the improvement from baseline to week 24 occurred within the initial 4 weeks of treatment, continued steadily throughout week 16 with minimal additional changes thereafter. MRI inflammation improvement at such an early time point has not been reported previously for IL-17 inhibitors. Previous secukinumab trials have typically reported MRI changes from week 16 onwards.9 25 While prior studies consistently support the effect of IL-17A inhibition on MRI-detected inflammation, variations in MRI scoring methods limited direct comparisons.26 27 Similarly, an effect of tumour necrosis factor (TNF) inhibitors on MRI-detected inflammation, as assessed by the SPARCC SIJ score, has generally been described after 6 and 12 weeks of treatment or later.28 29 One previous study reported a reduction in MRI inflammation scores after 4 weeks of golimumab treatment in patients with axSpA, as detected by whole-body MRI.30 Notably, our findings extend existing evidence by showing continued MRI inflammation reduction even in patients who did not achieve the stringent threshold of ASDAS remission (<1.3) by week 16.

Structural MRI changes, specifically reductions in SPARCC SIJ erosion scores and increases in fat and backfill scores, were also evident as early as week 4, with the fastest changes occurring within the first month of therapy and continuing, although less markedly, through week 24. To our knowledge, this represents the earliest documented structural remodelling following IL-17A inhibition. These results align with findings from a randomised trial in ixekizumab-treated patients with axSpA, where similar structural responses were reported at weeks 16 and 52.8 31 Comparable effects have also been reported with TNF inhibitors and tofacitinib, typically from week 12 onwards32 33 and as early as week 4 in the abovementioned study.30 Thus, these early changes likely reflect an MRI-detected post-inflammatory remodelling response typical of axSpA, although alternative explanations related to the natural course of disease cannot be excluded.34 Our findings highlight the potential of MRI to assess such structural modifications at a much earlier stage, compared with radiography, which typically detects changes only after years of follow-up,35 potentially enabling more sensitive monitoring of disease progression and earlier interventions.

Strengths of this study include the use of MRI as early as week 4 and the comprehensive imaging of both the SIJ and spine, allowing for detailed assessment of treatment effects on total axial inflammation and structural remodelling. This is particularly relevant given the central role of spinal lesions in long-term functional impairment,36,38 which is an important treatment outcome from the patient perspective.39

Several limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting the study results. First, the absence of randomisation and control group continuing secukinumab at 150 mg, even if not in ASDAS remission at week 16, weakens our conclusions about the specific effect of dose escalation and does not allow us to draw causal inferences regarding structural lesion remodelling, which may partly reflect natural post-inflammatory processes. However, this would require a much larger sample size, which was not feasible. Instead, we chose a design directly applicable in routine clinical practice.

Second, using ASDAS ≥1.3 as a criterion for treatment escalation may have been overly stringent, potentially excluding patients with good clinical improvement and acceptable disease activity level without formally reaching ASDAS remission. Accordingly, a treat-to-target approach targeting ASDAS <1.3 was not endorsed by the recent 2019 American College of Rheumatology/Spondylitis Association of America/ Spondyloarthritis Research and Treatment Network recommendations because it was considered too stringent.40 41 Notably, several patients (n=5) discontinued our study, primarily because they experienced substantial benefit at 150 mg secukinumab and reached ASDAS low disease activity, but not ASDAS remission, and were unwilling to escalate or alter a well-tolerated regimen. The withdrawal of these patients reduces the effective population in the escalation group and may have led to an underestimation of the potential benefit of dose escalation. This reflects the concerns regarding ASDAS-driven treat-to-target strategies raised by the Treat‑to‑Target Trial in axSpA (TICOSPA) study, in which high protocol-violation rates in the treat-to-target arm often stemmed from reluctance to intensify therapy according to the ASDAS-based algorithm, and no clear superiority of a treat-to-target strategy over usual care in axSpA was observed.5 42

Third, the non-randomised escalation approach may have introduced confounding by indication, as patients requiring escalation could represent a more difficult-to-treat or difficult-to-manage subgroup. Lastly, the early (week 4) MRI findings were not a primary end point and therefore require confirmation in independent studies. Further research is needed to define optional treatment targets and identify patients most likely to benefit from dose intensification.

In conclusion, we did not observe a statistically significant effect of secukinumab dose escalation on reducing MRI inflammation within a treat-to-target approach. Nevertheless, dose escalation reduced several clinical measures of disease activity, with 22% reaching ASDAS remission after escalation, and a numerical reduction in MRI-inflammation was observed in a selected subgroup of patients.

Moreover, this is the first study to show that the initial 4 weeks of treatment provides the fastest and most pronounced reduction of MRI-detected inflammation and bone erosion, of a similar magnitude as the cumulative reductions with the subsequent 5 months. Other documentation of structural remodelling, such as changes in fat lesions and backfill, was also demonstrated within the initial 4 weeks. These findings highlight the potential of MRI to sensitively assess treatment effects on inflammation and structural progression in clinical trials, even of very short duration.
