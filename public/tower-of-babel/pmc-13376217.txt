Digital archive of ephemerally preserved sedimentary structure in surf zone uplifted by the 2024 Noto Peninsula Earthquake
Authors: Masatoshi Yuhi, Noritaka Endo, Hiroko Okazaki, Yusuke Suzuki, Fujio Masuda, Shinya Umeda, Junichi Ninomiya, Mamoru Arita, Keisuke Futagi, Tetsuji Konno, Tsuyoshi Haraguchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe surf zone is a dynamic coastal area characterising the physical environment of sandy beaches. However, direct, non-disturbing field observations of the highly variable sedimentary history of surf zones have been almost impossible in the past. The lack of on-site observations has limited our understanding of the morphological behaviour and inherent dynamics. In contrast, surf-zone stratigraphy was uplifted onto land on a time scale of seconds at the Kaiso Coast, Japan, by the Noto Peninsula Earthquake on 1 January 2024. The structure of unsolidified sediments covering the bar-trough zone to the foreshore (hereafter referred to as the surf zone) has been instantaneously preserved as it was without post-event interference except for the top layer. This extraordinary natural experiment enables us to explore the present-day sedimentary architecture formed beneath the surf zone under high-wave conditions. Here, we show the results of a large-scale trenching survey of the uplifted stratigraphy that covers the surf zone, including the breaker zone. A high-resolution photogrammetric digital archive of the sedimentary structure of the ephemerally preserved stratigraphy that was instantaneously uplifted by the earthquake was constructed, which provides an ideal basis for interpreting the spatiotemporal evolutionary history of bedforms within the surf zone. The spatially continuous orthoimages, covering a total of over 500 m, identified the development and transition of various sedimentary structures that were previously estimated but not fully evidenced in the real field. The combination of multiple trench surveys, aligned in both cross-shore and alongshore directions, allows for quasi-three-dimensional observations. This pioneering archive provides a unique opportunity for anyone to freely observe in detail the recent sedimentary structures as if they were there. Introduction

The evolution of surf-zone morphology characterizes the physical environment of sandy beaches and has a significant influence on coastal preservation, disaster prevention, and ecosystems. Therefore, it has attracted widespread interest in various fields related to ocean and coastal science/engineering, such as sedimentology1,2, coastal geomorphology3,4, wave dynamics5, sediment transport6, coastal disasters7, coastal ecosystems8, fisheries9, coastal management10, and others.

In the field of sedimentology, various facies models have been proposed for coasts and shallow seas. Correspondingly, interpretation of various bedform evolution and the estimation of the formative conditions for individual sedimentary structures have also been conducted for unidirectional, oscillatory, and combined flow environments. Clifton (1971)11 proposed a model for the sedimentary structures on non-barred coasts. Subsequently, the model for barred-coast facies was constructed by Davidson-Arnott & Greenwood12. Clifton (2006)2 revisited the basic models of coastal-facies and pointed out their limitations. The development of the models is summarized in several textbooks13,14. To date, sedimentary structures at various coastal sites have been examined in relation to these models. Despite these efforts, the present coastal-facies models for oscillatory/combined-flow environments are not as mature as the river-facies models for unidirectional-flow environments. Correspondingly, the physical understanding of the complex morphological behaviour and its inherent dynamics remains incomplete, especially in the surf zone, where intensive or large-scale morphological changes occur continuously. In addition, further refinement is needed to account for the influences of anthropogenic interference, such as the construction of breakwaters.

One of the main reasons that has limited the progress of the coastal-facies model in the past decades is the lack of direct field observations of the sedimentary structure beneath the surf zone, where the intensive actions of breaking waves continuously alter the bedform. On-site observations that do not disturb the bedforms, facies, waves, or coastal currents are almost impossible in practice. Consequently, existing observations of submarine stratigraphy are primarily based on ancient facies recorded on land outcrops15,16, which lack relevant information on the environmental conditions responsible for their formation, and are likely to have been affected by various events over a long period. Modern underwater stratigraphy can be observed by collecting core samples12,17,18, which provide close, but discrete and narrow, views that preclude continuous, multiscale, and three-dimensional observations. Recently, the rapid progress of exploration technologies offered additional opportunities for coastal-facies observation, such as ground-penetrating radar (GPR)19,20 and high-resolution seismo-acoustic profiling (parametric echo sounding system: PES)21. The spatial coverage of these techniques is large and continuous, while their spatial resolution (in the order of 10 cm in the vertical direction) is not necessarily sufficient. The use of X-ray computed tomography22, which can detect the detailed structure of the specimen, was attempted, but the observable area is very limited in both the horizontal and vertical directions. The applications of these emerging techniques are still limited.

Surprisingly, an extraordinary phenomenon enabled us to explore the non-disturbed stratigraphy beneath the surf zone. An underwater sedimentary structure emerged onto land almost instantaneously on the Kaiso Coast, Japan (Fig. 1), due to the sudden uplift of the ground induced by the Noto Peninsula Earthquake on 1 January 202423–25. Because multiple faults with a total length of 100 km ruptured in approximately 40 s, it was inferred that uplift occurred over just a few seconds in the local area around the Kaiso Coast. A comparison of airborne LiDAR surveys before and after the earthquake indicated that the magnitude of the uplift reached approximately 4 m (Supplementary Fig. S1), i.e., a pre-earthquake seafloor up to a depth of 4 m suddenly emerged on land. This natural experiment provided an unparalleled opportunity for on-site, undisturbed observations of the present-day stratigraphy beneath the surf zone.

To best utilise this ideal opportunity, we conducted a large-scale trenching survey of the emerged seafloor, covering an area of over 100 m alongshore and 130 m cross-shore distance (Fig. 2; Table 1). A high-resolution digital archive of orthophotographs of the ephemerally preserved stratigraphy was seamlessly constructed over the surf zone. The combination of cross-shore and alongshore images of multiple trenches allows for quasi-three-dimensional observation. We anticipate that the constructed orthoimage archive will provide an ideal basis for interpreting the spatiotemporal evolutionary history of bedforms within the surf zone from multiple perspectives.

Field site

Overview of the study area

The Kaiso Coast is located in Monzen Town, Wajima City, Ishikawa Prefecture, Japan, at the northwestern tip of the Noto Peninsula (Fig. 1a). A sandy pocket beach has formed at the field site between the Kaiso and Kuroshima ports (Fig. 1b and c). The orientation of the shoreline at the surveyed area was generally in the south-southeast (SSE) to north-northwest (NNW) direction. The beach consists mainly of medium sand with a representative grain size of 0.2 to 0.5 mm.

According to available geoinformation of the northern coastal zone of the Noto Peninsula26,27, the area around the field site is primarily composed of the Early Miocene sandstone, mudstone, and conglomerate. Unconsolidated Holocene sediments are distributed in the low-lying areas near the Hakkagawa River, which flows into the middle of the coast. This river is considered the principal source of sediment discharge into the local littoral system at the Kaiso Coast, but the contribution of the river to the total sediment budget of the coast is not yet quantitatively known.

The beach had experienced high winter waves in the past. Between 1980 and 1993, four detached breakwaters (BW1-BW4 in Fig. 1c) were constructed as countermeasures. Subsequently, salients developed behind the breakwaters, and the width of the beach recovered (Supplementary Fig. S2). Comparison of aerial photographs between 1975 and 2023 revealed that the shoreline advanced approximately 50 m in that period. The shoreline location before the uplift was estimated to be approximately 10–20 m landward of the breakwaters (Figs. 1b and 3).

The Kaiso Coast is in the immediate vicinity of the western segment of the active faults that triggered the Noto Peninsula Earthquake on 1 January 2024 (Fig. 1a). A comparison of airborne LiDAR surveys before and after the earthquake revealed that the earthquake uplifted the area by approximately 4 m (Supplementary Fig. S1). The observed coseismic uplift is among the largest in modern observations. Over the spatial scale of trench length, the coseismic uplift is nearly uniform. The uplift resulted in the expansion of the beach width in the 150–250-m range (Fig. 1c).

Unfortunately, no bathymetric data were available before the earthquake. Moreover, to the authors’ knowledge, no scientific literature exists that focuses on the morphological characteristics of the Kaiso Coast. Accordingly, the morphological features at the Kaiso Coast were examined based on past aerial photographs (Supplementary Fig. S2) and the post-event Lidar survey, including underwater bathymetry (Supplementary Fig. S3). Aerial photographs taken at different times before the uplift often revealed the development of gently curved multiple sandbars seaward of the breakwaters. Although the location and shape of the bars substantially varied over time and space, the typical cross-shore positions of the inner and outer bar crests were inferred to be approximately 50–100 and 150–250 m seaward from the breakwaters, respectively.

The aerial photograph taken three months after the earthquake (6 April 2024) already showed the presence of newly formed multiple sandbars (Supplementary Fig. S3). The bathymetric profile obtained from the LiDAR survey conducted on the same day was also examined to further understand the bar properties. The cross-shore bathymetries of the Kaiso Coast after the earthquake for several cross-shore transects (Supplementary Fig. S3) indicated that the location, size, and number of the bars vary substantially alongshore. The bar height, defined as the difference in elevation between the crest and trough of the outermost bar, is in the range of 1 to 2 m. On average, the mean slope from the foreshore to the bar-trough area with water depth up to 7 m is approximately 1/50.

The above observation is expected to help understand the bar quantities before the uplift, as the wave climate remains the same, and the change in sediment diameter is likely to be small before and after the earthquake. However, care should be taken because the pre- and post-earthquake influences of breakwaters are significantly different. The reflected wave from the new beach is expected to be substantially weaker than that from the breakwaters before the earthquake. Correspondingly, before the earthquake, the breakwaters exerted substantial influences on the alongshore and cross-shore currents, including the rip currents originating from the gap between them. After the earthquake, in contrast, there is no influence of breakwaters. The variations in the local wave field and resulting circulation patterns around the bar-trough zone are likely to affect the bar properties. The details of the pre-earthquake bathymetry and the mechanisms of three-dimensional unsteady flow fields and sediment transport patterns in the littoral cell remain unclear.

Wave climate

Since the coast faces the Sea of Japan, whose tidal range is microtidal (as small as 0.5 m around the Kaiso Coast), the morphological process is wave-dominated. Because the Sea of Japan is semi-enclosed, the fetch for wave development is limited. Accordingly, wind waves govern the wave climate, whereas well-developed swells rarely affect the coast. Another distinct feature of the wave climate in the Sea of Japan area is the significant seasonal variation28. According to wave observations at Kanazawa and Wajima ports, located approximately 75 km southwest and 20 km northeast of the study area, respectively (Fig. 1a), waves are calm in summer, with significant wave heights of typically less than 1 m. In contrast, high and steep waves with significant wave heights exceeding 1 m are often observed during winter. The annual maximum significant wave height ranges from 5 to 10 m29,30. It should be noted here that, at the survey area, high waves incident from the northwest to north-northwest generated by winter monsoons are partly shielded owing to the topography of the Noto Peninsula and the breakwater of Kaiso Port.

Methods

Trench survey

The surf zone before the earthquake emerged on land almost instantaneously, enabling direct observation of the stratigraphy that had formed underwater before the earthquake but was not affected by waves afterwards, being preserved after uplift until the trench excavation on land.

The trench area was located approximately 400–500 m north of the mouth of the Hakkagawa River. The excavation was conducted from 3 to 5 June 2024, five months after the earthquake. The survey was concentrated over a short time because the unconsolidated sand layers were prone to collapse. The trench was initially excavated using heavy machinery, followed by manual adjustments.

The trenches were excavated sufficiently long to allow continuous observation of the transition in sedimentary structures (Table 1; Fig. 2). The trenches covered both cross-shore and alongshore directions, enabling quasi-three-dimensional observations. Three trenches (Cs-1–Cs-3) were dug in a cross-shore direction (Fig. 2b). Trench Cs-1 was positioned in front of the third breakwater (BW3) and covered the outer and inner bar areas. Trench Cs-2 was located at the gap between the second and third detached breakwaters (BW2 and BW3) and covered the inner bar-trough area and foreshore before uplift. Trench Cs-3 was positioned in front of the northwestern edge of BW3, between Cs-1 and Cs-2, and covered both the outer and inner bar areas. In addition, several trenches (As-1–As-7) were excavated alongshore (Fig. 2b). A relatively long alongshore trench (As-1) was located approximately 100 m seaward of the breakwater. It was located at the outer bar-trough position before the earthquake, as inferred from post-event images and ground elevation surveys. In addition, six short trenches (As-2–As-7) were excavated in the alongshore direction. As-2 was connected to the landward end of Cs-3 to enable quasi-three-dimensional observation of the sedimentary structure. The total lengths of the cross-shore and alongshore trenches were approximately 340 and 195 m, respectively. The depths of the trenches were adjusted according to the groundwater level. The trench bottom was set as deep as possible, unless groundwater seepage interrupted the survey. The depth was approximately 0.2 to 0.3 m in the outer bar zone, while it was approximately 1 m close to the breakwaters. The cross-sections of the representative trenches (Cs-1, Cs-2, Cs-3, and As-1) are shown in Fig. 3.

Photogrammetry

The cross-sections of the trench walls were recorded as a series of photographs using a Sony α7R V (effective pixels: 61 million) and a Sony FE 28 mm F2 lens (Sony Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). Three-dimensional photogrammetric models were constructed using Structure from Motion and Multi-View Stereo (SfM-MVS) in Agisoft Metashape (version 2.1.0; Agisoft LLC, Saint Petersburg, Russia) from overlapping multiple snapshots taken from different angles. Orthophotographs were then constructed in a direction normal to the vertical trench surface, while correcting for lens distortion and minor surface irregularities. The orthophotographs were seamlessly combined at 30 m intervals. The resulting photographs were named according to the trench number and distance from the base points of the survey. For the alongshore trenches, base points were set at the north-northwestern edges of the trench. For the cross-shore trenches, the base points were set at the seaward edge of the trenches, except for Cs-1, whose base point was taken on the ridge at the time of the survey and roughly corresponded to the location of As-1 (Fig. 3). Finally, the joint orthophotographs were manually examined to analyse the stratigraphy.

The digital archive included orthophotographs at three resolutions. The highest- and high-resolution photographs in TIFF format have resolutions of 0.15 and 0.30 mm/pixel, respectively. These photographs can be used when a detailed inspection is required. Moderate-resolution photographs suitable for practical use were produced in JPG format with a 0.60 mm/pixel resolution. Moreover, three-dimensional photogrammetric models were constructed for the vertical walls crossing at the corner of the trench, where three-dimensional observations from two directions were possible. These models were constructed in “tls” format, which can be handled by Agisoft Viewer.

It is noted that there were a few locations where photogrammetric operations did not perform well. The distortion remained unresolved at the 60–90-m interval in Trench As-1. Photogrammetric models could not be constructed at 0–20 m intervals in Trench As-1 and at 30–55 m intervals in Trench Cs-1, where the trenches were very shallow.

Results

Photogrammetric digital archive

We constructed a high-resolution photogrammetric digital archive of the ephemerally preserved stratigraphy covering the surf zone at the time of the earthquake. The principal features of the archive can be summarised as follows. First, it covers various present-day sedimentary structures in the surf zone, which are extremely difficult to observe in the field. Second, it provides easy-to-use orthophotographs that display a continuous view of intact strata, without deformation or diagenesis after uplift, over sufficiently long spatial spans to observe the spatial transitions of sedimentary structures. Third, users can freely observe sedimentary structures down to a very fine scale at any time in a digital archive. Fourth, the combination of cross-shore and alongshore images allows for quasi-three-dimensional analysis. Finally, the environmental conditions at the stratigraphic formation are available.

The digital archive comprises seamlessly joined orthophotographs of vertical walls at 30-m intervals across 10 trenches. Orthophotos with different resolutions (0.15, 0.30, and 0.60 mm/pixel) can be effectively utilised for various purposes, such as detailed inspection of local sedimentary structures at high resolution or rapid confirmation of the overall picture at moderate resolution. Moreover, three-dimensional photogrammetric models were constructed at the connections of the trenches where observations from two directions were possible. The comprehensive, epoch-making archive can provide vital information for obtaining essential physical insights into evolutionary sequences and their interactions with environmental factors.

The trenches were located in a highly dynamic surf zone environment, where bed elevation can vary by tens of centimetres during a single storm event. Because the depth of the trench is up to 1 m, the observed stratigraphy was considered to be formed over a relatively short time span. In addition, because the observed sedimentary structure is a snapshot of highly transient morphological change, it may capture the formation of a fragile sedimentary structure that may disappear within a relatively short period after subsequent events. Accordingly, the archive is suitable for the detailed estimation of bedform formation during relatively short time scales.

Identified sedimentary structures

The observed section revealed instantaneous snapshots of the ever-changing sedimentary structures, including distinct types of cross and planar lamination. Orthoimages along the cross-shore trenches reveal the transition of diverse sedimentary structures that comprise typical beach systems (Fig. 4). Individual sedimentary structures reflect a set of environmental conditions such as flow velocity and direction, water depth, and sediment grain size.

In the foreshore area of the cross-shore trench (Cs-2), low-angle seaward-dipping planar-parallel lamination and short foreset lamination conformable to topsets (structure similar to the cross section of Gilbert-type deltas) were confirmed as characteristic sedimentary structures. Similar to the observations in existing studies, these sedimentary structures are considered to be formed by swash-backwash motions of waves and related to the seaward migration of the foreshore and beach step (Fig. 4a and b)31–34. In the offshore part of the trench area, hummocky cross-stratification (HCS)35,36, which has been interpreted as a storm deposit at the lower shoreface in existing studies (Fig. 4c), was identified. In the most offshore part of the trench area, namely, in the longshore trench located in the pre-earthquake outer bar-trough zone (As-1), planar-parallel lamination was dominant (Fig. 4d). It might be interpreted as outer bar12 or post-earthquake deposits.

In the entire trench areas, especially in the central region (inner bar area), various sedimentary structures caused by oscillatory and combined flow are observed in the surf zone, influenced by the breakwaters. In the following, we provide descriptions of representative laminations observed at the survey site (Figs. 5 and 6). The in-depth analysis of their distribution and formative conditions will be discussed in separate publications.

Wave ripple cross-lamination (e.g., Fig. 5a) and HCS (e.g., Fig. 5b) were identified at several locations on the trenches. Wave ripple cross-lamination37 is considered to be formed under low-energy oscillatory flow conditions. Another example related to oscillatory flows is shown in Fig. 5c, which illustrates the formation of large-scale ripple cross-lamination with wavelengths exceeding several tens of centimetres. In the existing literature, the formation of large-scale ripple cross-lamination has been reported in several studies38–40. It is considered that this type of structure is difficult to observe by discrete core sampling for present-day deposits because the wavelength is much larger than the horizontal core size. Accordingly, the findings in this study provided a rare opportunity to observe the formation of large-scale ripples. HCS is formed under an oscillatory or combined flow with a long wave period and relatively large oscillatory velocities41.

The next two examples illustrate the observed beach components that are considered to have developed under the influence of currents in the intense-wave environment of the surf zone. The lower part of Fig. 5b shows an example of cross-stratification characterised by the downstream migration of the brink point, where an abrupt change occurs in the inclination of the cross-lamination. The observed structure is similar to the humpback cross-stratification in fluvial contexts42. Although humpback cross-stratification has often been reported in unidirectional-flow environments, commonly in river deposits, a few cases have been documented within marine strata. For example, humpback cross-stratification has been observed in delta deposits43,44. While river flow dominates the delta front, wave influence is also present, as indicated by the term “wave-dominated delta”. It is possible that humpback cross-stratification also formed under the conditions where unidirectional current was dominant over wave action. In this study, humpback cross-stratifications were identified at several locations on the trench surface.

Figure 5d shows an example of herringbone cross-stratification45. Alternating cross-laminations dipping in opposite directions were clearly developed under the alternating action of bidirectional flows. Although the term “herringbone cross-stratification” has been primarily used for tidal deposits, it describes a morphology formed by counter-currents in a broader sense. Such a structure can also be formed if bidirectional currents other than tides exist at the same location. Therefore, this term is occasionally applied to offshore deposits20. In this sense, we used the term “herringbone cross-stratification” here.

Figure 6 shows the formation of a scour-fill structure with backset lamination. The observed structure had features similar to those of the deposits conventionally attributed to chutes-and-pools34 in the high-flow regime of the unidirectional flow domain. To the best of our knowledge, it is a remarkable new finding that such sedimentary structures can be formed and preserved in wave-dominated environments. However, the flow conditions responsible for the formation of the detected structures are unclear.

The alongshore trenches in the inner bar area (As-2 to 7) exhibited various types of laminations and alongshore variability (e.g., Supplementary Fig. S4). Combining observations from orthogonal cross-sections is expected to yield valuable insights for understanding sedimentary structures and inferring their formation processes. However, the detailed depositional environment remains unclear and should be elucidated, considering the three-dimensional migration of bedforms and multi-directional flows. Three-dimensional views of the stratigraphic images in the present study (e.g., Fig. 7) will be helpful for testing theoretical and experimental studies.

Relation to external forces

Additional information on the environmental conditions in the study area is beneficial for reading the history recorded in sedimentary structures, such as water depth, external forces (waves and tides), the existence of characteristic reliefs (e.g., sand bars and troughs), and coastal/port structures (breakwaters, groins, etc.). One advantage of this archive set is that the environmental nature of its formation is available. First, the morphological features before the uplift, such as the location of the bar or trough and the distance to the breakwaters or river mouth, are known. We are confident that the observed sedimentary structures were generated in coastal environments covering the surf zone, including the breaker zone. Moreover, field records of storm waves and tides before the uplift are available near the study area. Wave records at Kanazawa and Wajima Ports have been available for over the last 50 years in the Nationwide Ocean Wave Information Network for Ports and HarbourS (NOWPHAS) by the Port and Airport Research Institute, Japan46. Tidal records are provided by the Japan Meteorological Agency47.

In particular, the properties of incident waves just before uplift are crucial for interpreting sedimentary structures. According to the NOWPHAS observations at the Kanazawa and Wajima stations, waves with significant-wave heights exceeding 3 m hit the coast several times in December 2023 (Supplementary Fig. S5). The highest significant wave height of 6.8 m was recorded on 17 December 2023. Immediately prior to the earthquake, wave heights exceeded 3 m from 31 December 2023 to 1 January 2024. Previous studies have revealed that the bed level in the surf zone may vary by several tens of centimetres during a high-wave event48,49. At water depths up to 4 m before the uplift, these high-energy wave events likely substantially influenced the stratigraphic sequence. Using external force information, a detailed interpretation of the formation process of the observed stratigraphy is possible based on the flow regime concept of sedimentology. However, detailed collations and interpretations are beyond the scope of this study and are left for future research.

Discussion

Comparison with existing studies

The archive constructed in this study provides clear, high-resolution images of the development of various sedimentary structures. As expected, the detected structures include various types of laminations, which have been well documented in the existing literature and are commonly predicted in the surf zone under oscillatory or combined flow environments. The wave-ripple cross-lamination and HCS identified at several places within the trenches are typical examples. Moreover, planar-parallel lamination and short foreset lamination identified in the foreshore area are considered to be formed under swash-backwash motions. These structures are likely related to the seaward migration of the foreshore and beach step, which have been commonly observed in many other studies.

In addition, remarkably, the detected field evidence included sedimentary structures that had previously been predicted from laboratory experiments or outcrops in a current-dominated domain but were not fully confirmed in the real field of surf zone environments susceptible to wave influence. One of the novel examples is the formation of large-scale ripple cross-lamination. The incidence of long-lasting swells with a very long wave period, exceeding 21–30 s, has been speculated to be necessary for developing large-scale ripple cross-lamination in fine sand38. However, this requirement was far beyond the wave (swell) period observed in the study area. The collation of existing knowledge from wave-flume experiments and the expected swell period at the study area implies that the observed large-scale ripples were formed by combined flows and not due to pure wave effects.

An additionally remarkable example is the formation of a unique trough-fill structure with backset lamination. It is a novel finding that sedimentary structures like chutes-and-pool deposits can be formed and preserved even in wave-dominated environments50. Conventional studies generally consider that chute-and-pool deposits are formed under a high-flow regime in unidirectional flows. In coastal environments, chute-and-pool deposits have been found in tsunami deposits, likely due to the intense backwash51,52. They have also been identified in storm deposits, where a strong offshore-directed flow likely created a hydraulic jump53,54. The findings in the present study raise open questions about the detailed formative conditions of sedimentary structures and the dynamics underlying morphological changes.

Possible influence of post-earthquake external forces

Because the trench excavation was conducted 5 months after the earthquake, the possible influence of post-earthquake events is briefly examined here. After the uplift, several natural forces acted on the coast, including tsunamis immediately after the earthquake, run-ups of high winter waves, and strong winds.

To examine the tsunami behaviour on the Kaiso Coast, numerical simulations were conducted using a numerical tsunami model (JAGURS)55 based on a nonlinear shallow-water equation. The fault model proposed by the Geospatial Information Authority of Japan (GSI)23 was applied to describe the tsunami source, but the fault model could not fully reproduce the ground uplift at the Kaiso Coast. The numerical results revealed that tsunami waves with heights exceeding 1 m intermittently hit the coast several times after the initial uplift of the water. The tsunami lasted approximately 2 h, but the intermittent nature of wave incidence limited its duration, with water surface variations exceeding 1 m. Additionally, after a ground uplift of approximately 4 m, the bed elevation of the study area reached more than 1 m above mean sea level (Fig. 3), except for a limited northwestern portion of As-1. In addition, a field survey of the tsunami run-up indicated that the debris likely carried by the tsunami was found approximately 100 m seaward of the breakwaters56(close to As-1 and seaward of trenches Cs-2, 3, As-2–7, and the main portion of Cs-1). Accordingly, we concluded that the influence of tsunamis was insignificant in most of the study area. For Trench As-1 and the seaward portion of trench Cs-1, where the post-event ground elevation was approximately 1 m or less above mean sea level, tsunamis may have influenced the top layer.

According to NOWPHAS observations, wind waves with significant wave heights greater than 3 m hit the coastline of Ishikawa Prefecture near Kanazawa and Wajima Port more than 10 times between January and May 2024. However, a comparison of satellite images captured the day after the earthquake, subsequent aerial photographs, and UAV observations during the survey revealed that the shoreline position in the study area remained relatively stable during this period. This is partly because the surveyed area has been protected from the high winter waves from the northwest to the north-northwest by the geometry of the peninsula and the port breakwater. Thus, the influence of wind-wave run-up was considered small at trench locations (mostly 1 m or more above mean sea level). In summary, the effects of post-event tsunamis and wind waves were limited to the surface layer over a small portion of the survey area (Trench As-1 and the seaward portion of Trench Cs-1). These effects were minimal over the principal part of the study area.

On the Kaiso Coast, the sand on the surface layer is transported landward by strong winds from the west during winter. In relation to this, non-negligible sand deposition was recognised at the location immediately seaward of the breakwaters during the survey. This observation implies the influence of post-earthquake winds on the surface layer. In addition, a comparison of the aerial photograph captured on 11 January 2024 (Fig. 1c) with that during the survey suggested that sand had accumulated over part of the pre-earthquake trough area (close to the location of As-1), likely due to the combined effects of waves and winds. It is also noted that a small drainage canal outlet is located behind the excavated area. Drainage water likely flowed on the surface around the survey area after heavy rain, which might have influenced the post-event surface topography. Care should be taken when interpreting the layer immediately beneath the bed surface. However, the influences of wind and drainage are limited to vertical locations very close to the land surface.

Although the possible influences of post-earthquake physical processes deserve to be closely examined, we did not address the above issues in detail in the present paper because their impacts were estimated to be limited to the layers immediately beneath the surface or the most seaward portion of the trenches. This study focused on the sedimentary structures formed by wave action before the earthquake, located at a certain depth below the surface, and unaffected by post-earthquake events such as tsunamis, waves, tides, and winds.

Limitations

Although vast amounts of information can be extracted from the archive, it requires a detailed, extensive analysis of the images to study the interrelations among the various sedimentary structures in the surf zone and the variability of the corresponding bedforms within a complex geomorphological system. Since this study primarily focused on constructing a data archive and verifying its applicability, it lacks a comprehensive interpretation of the observed sedimentary structures. Interpretations of the details of individual structures, their interrelationships, and the physical environments that created them are limited in the present study. Efforts are underway to develop these interpretations with the help of numerical analysis of fluid motion and post-earthquake bathymetry analysis. Additionally, to fully utilize the developed archive, it is desirable to develop efficient methods for detailed analysis of the entire system using advanced image-processing techniques.

Summary and future scope

This study investigated sedimentary structures that formed in the surf zone and emerged almost instantaneously on land during intense seismic motion. Based on a large-scale trenching survey of the ephemerally preserved stratigraphy covering the surf zone, a photogrammetric digital archive was constructed comprising high-resolution, seamless orthoimages. By achieving both long-distance continuity and high resolution, the archive enabled the precise observation of sedimentary structures across diverse spatial scales. The archived images included several sedimentary structures that had previously been predicted from laboratory experiments but had not been fully confirmed in the real-world field settings of the surf zones.

It is well known that bar-trough relief is an essential constituent of surf-zone bedforms57,58 and plays an important role in the conservation of coastal ecosystems and in disaster prevention in coastal areas. However, the physical understanding of the characteristics and inherent dynamics of the formation, evolution, migration, and decay processes of sandbars59 as well as their relationships to internal sedimentary structures40 remains limited. Because this data archive was constructed for a barred coast with artificial defence structures, it can provide a basis for a detailed physical interpretation of the development sequences and their relationship to environmental factors. Careful and detailed collation and analysis will help clarify the behaviour of bars interacting with high wind waves, rip currents, and tsunamis under the influence of detached breakwaters. We anticipate that this digital archive will serve as a solid benchmark for future flume experiments, numerical analyses, and field observations.
