Interrater reliability of nutritional risk screening using NRS-2002 in hospitalized patients with cancer
Authors: Lu Zhang, Rong Guo, Yinyin Ou, Yuqing Zhuang, Mengxiang Li, Zhijun Li, Peijuan Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeMalnutrition is common among hospitalized cancer patients and has been linked to adverse clinical outcomes. Accurate nutritional risk screening is essential for rapid nutritional intervention. Although the NRS-2002 tool is widely used in clinical practice, its interrater reliability in oncologic settings has not been adequately examined. This study aimed to evaluate the interrater reliability of nutritional risk screening among hospitalized patients with cancer using the Nutritional Risk Screening 2002 (NRS-2002) tool.DesignRetrospective cross-sectional study.MethodsThirty-five trained clinical nurses and one registered dietitian independently performed NRS-2002 assessments within 24 h of admission among 434 hospitalized cancer patients. Cohen’s kappa statistics were used to evaluate interrater reliability for each NRS-2002 component, including nutritional status, disease severity, and risk classification derived from the total score.ResultsNurses identified fewer patients at nutritional risk than the dietitian (73 vs. 211). In addition, the overall agreement between the registered dietitian and clinical nurses using the NRS-2002 was fair (kappa = 0.343, 95% CI: 0.275 to 0.411). Interrater reliability was fair for nutritional status (kappa = 0.241) but poor for disease severity (kappa = 0.198), whereas perfect agreement was observed for the age component (kappa = 1.000). Among cancer types, patients with breast cancer (n = 11) showed complete agreement (kappa = 1.000), though this finding should be interpreted cautiously because of the small sample size, followed by those with gastrointestinal tumors (kappa = 0.489, 95% CI: 0.289 to 0.689) and esophageal cancer (kappa = 0.408, 95% CI: 0.212 to 0.604). Nurses with junior professional titles (kappa = 0.347) showed slightly higher consistency with the dietitian than those with intermediate titles (kappa = 0.339).ConclusionOverall, this study demonstrated fair interrater reliability of the NRS-2002 for assessing nutritional risk among hospitalized patients with cancer. To improve the reliability of nutritional risk screening, we recommend standardizing the operational definitions of disease severity scores, implementing structured and periodic training programs for clinical nurses, and establishing regular audit and supervision mechanisms. These measures are essential for improving screening consistency and, in turn, patient assessment and subsequent care.

Introduction

Malnutrition is a common complication in patients with cancer, with an incidence of 40% to 80% [1]. Cancer-related malnutrition increases treatment-related adverse effects, prolongs hospitalization [2, 3], and significantly impairs quality of life and prognosis [4, 5–6]. Approximately 10% to 20% of cancer patients die from malnutrition rather than the tumor itself [7]. These findings highlight the importance of early nutritional risk identification.

The Nutritional Risk Screening 2002 (NRS-2002) is an internationally recognized tool widely used for identifying nutritional risk and predicting prognosis among hospitalized patients [7–9–10]. However, its effectiveness in oncology settings remains limited. Studies have reported that its sensitivity among cancer populations is only 0.531, with a missed diagnosis rate of up to 49% [11]. Meta-analyses further confirm that its sensitivity is significantly lower than that of the Patient-Generated Subjective Global Assessment (PG-SGA) [12]. This discrepancy largely stems from the subjective judgment involved in evaluating nutritional impairment and disease severity within the NRS-2002 [12]. Moreover, differences in patients’ educational levels and factors such as feeding assistance may further contribute to information bias [13].

In routine clinical practice, nutritional risk screening is typically performed by clinical nurses at admission, while dietitians conduct more comprehensive nutritional assessments and provide specialized interventions. As a result, the accuracy and consistency of NRS-2002 scoring may be influenced by evaluators’ professional training, clinical experience, and familiarity with nutritional assessment tools. Previous studies have reported variability in NRS-2002 scoring among healthcare professionals, suggesting that interrater reliability may affect the effectiveness of nutritional screening and subsequent clinical decision-making [14, 15]. Therefore, quantifying interrater reliability among multidisciplinary evaluators using the NRS-2002 is essential for optimizing nutritional management in oncology care.

This study retrospectively analyzed data from 434 hospitalized cancer patients to assess interrater reliability by comparing NRS-2002 screening outcomes between nurses and dietitians. To further identify potential sources of variation, each component of the NRS-2002 was examined separately.

By clarifying the reliability and influencing factors of nutritional risk screening in hospitalized cancer patients, this study aims to provide evidence for improving screening quality, enabling earlier nutritional intervention, optimizing nutritional status, and promoting recovery.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective cross-sectional study assessed the interrater reliability of the NRS-2002 nutritional screening tool in hospitalized cancer patients. Each patient was independently assessed by the responsible ward nurse and a senior registered dietitian who served as the sole dietitian participating in the study. Both evaluators performed the assessments independently and were blinded to each other’s results. The dietitian’s assessment was used as the professional reference for comparison with the nurses’ screening outcomes.

Each patient was independently assessed by the responsible ward nurse and by a senior registered dietitian who served as the professional reference evaluator. Both assessors performed the NRS-2002 screening independently and were blinded to each other’s scores. Because the design compared nurses’ assessments with a single dietitian rather than evaluating agreement among multiple independent raters, the analysis focused on pairwise agreement using Cohen’s kappa.

Setting and participants

The study was conducted in the Department of Radiotherapy at a tertiary hospital in Guangzhou, China. Medical records of hospitalized cancer patients admitted between January 2021 and December 2023 were retrospectively reviewed. Inclusion criteria were: (1) age 18–90 years; (2) hospital stay longer than 24 h; and (3) completion of both nurse- and dietitian-performed NRS-2002 screenings within 24 h of admission. Exclusion criteria were: (1) records containing inconsistencies in key clinical information, including mismatched patient identity between nursing and dietitian screening records or screening timestamps that were clearly inconsistent with the documented admission time; and (2) incomplete data required for NRS-2002 evaluation. After applying these criteria, 434 patients were included in the final analysis.

Instruments

Nutritional risk screening

The data (body weight, height and BMI) of the patients were recorded by the ward nurse in the hospital information system when they were placed in the standing position while facing forward using an ultrasonic measuring device (Zhengzhou Shanghe Electronic Technology Co., Ltd.; model SH-200G), with all values rounded to one decimal place. Within 2 h of admission, the responsible nurse conducted nutritional risk screening using the NRS-2002, which comprises three components: impaired nutritional status (e.g., BMI, weight loss, and reduced food intake), disease severity (e.g., cancer, diabetes, or acute exacerbation of chronic disease), and age ≥ 70 years. The total NRS-2002 score ranges from 0 to 7, with a score < 3 indicating no nutritional risk, and a score ≥ 3 indicating nutritional risk. The screening results were documented in the hospital information system.

Procedure

Within 24 h after admission, the dietitian performed nutritional risk screening using the NRS-2002 and entered the results into a third-party data platform. During this process, the dietitian was blinded to the nurses’ scores. All participating nurses completed one 20-minute face-to-face training session led by a specialist nutrition nurse using PowerPoint slides. The training introduced the importance of nutritional risk screening and explained how to use the NRS-2002, including BMI calculation and the assessment of weight change. Before the study assessments, the registered dietitian attended a 40-minute practical training session organized by the head of the Nutrition Department. This session included a demonstration of the NRS-2002 procedure, an explanation of the scoring rules, discussion of common problems, and brief case examples. The difference in training reflected the different roles of the two evaluator groups in clinical practice. Nurses were trained to follow the screening steps in a consistent way, while the dietitian’s training focused on applying the scoring rules consistently in more complex situations. However, the purpose of both training sessions was the same: to ensure that all evaluators used the same NRS-2002 criteria before the independent blinded assessments.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 20.0. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, and normally distributed continuous variables as mean ± standard deviation (x̄ ± s). Interrater agreement for the binary nutritional risk classification derived from the NRS-2002 total score (≥ 3 vs. < 3) was assessed using Cohen’s kappa based on 2 × 2 contingency tables. Since this endpoint represents the clinically relevant decision threshold for nutritional risk screening, unweighted kappa was considered appropriate for evaluating agreement on this classification. Kappa values were interpreted as follows: <0.20, poor; 0.21–0.40, fair; 0.41–0.60, moderate; 0.61–0.80, substantial; and 0.81–1.00, almost perfect agreement.

Although 35 nurses participated in the study, the unit of analysis was the independent nurse-dietitian assessment pair for each patient. A total of 434 patients were included; therefore, kappa statistics were calculated from 434 paired assessments. In this analysis, the registered dietitian’s assessment was used as the professional reference standard to evaluate the overall consistency of the nurse group with that standard.

For the ordinal NRS-2002 total score (0–7), agreement between nurse and dietitian ratings was additionally examined using Bland-Altman analysis. Bland-Altman plots were generated in Excel to visualize the distribution and magnitude of score differences between the two evaluators across the measurement range.

Results

General characteristics of participants

A total of 434 cancer patients were included, aged 20–86 years (mean 53.7 ± 12.6). Among them, 263 (60.60%) were male and 171 (39.40%) were female. Regarding BMI, 58 patients (13.36%) had a BMI < 18.5 kg/m², 254 (58.53%) had 18.5–23.9 kg/m², 92 (21.20%) had 24–27.9 kg/m², and 30 (6.91%) had ≥ 28 kg/m². By diagnosis, there were 17 cases (3.92%) of central nervous system malignancies, 135 (31.11%) of head and neck cancer, 11 (2.53%) of breast cancer, 45 (10.37%) of lung cancer, 46 (10.60%) of esophageal cancer, 69 (15.90%) of reproductive system tumors, 59 (13.59%) of gastrointestinal tumors, 23 (5.30%) of liver cancer, and 29 (6.68%) of other tumors. Based on the Barthel Index (BI), 2 patients (0.46%) scored ≤ 40, 4 (0.92%) scored 41–60, 30 (6.91%) scored 61–99, and 398 (91.71%) scored 100. Detailed characteristics are presented in Table 1.

Nutritional risk screening

According to the NRS-2002 results, nurses classified 361 patients as not being at nutritional risk and 73 as being at nutritional risk, whereas the dietitian, who served as the professional reference, classified 223 patients as not being at nutritional risk and 211 as being at nutritional risk (Fig. 1). Detailed NRS-2002 scores assigned by nurses and the dietitian are presented in Fig. 2. Bland-Altman analysis showed a mean difference of -0.823 between nurse and dietitian scores (Fig. 3), with 95% limits of agreement from − 2.977 to 1.332. Most scores were within this range, and although the kappa statistic indicated only fair agreement, nurses consistently assigned lower scores than dietitians.

Interrater reliability

Cohen’s kappa analysis showed an overall interrater consistency of 0.343 (P < 0.001), indicating fair agreement between nurses and dietitians in NRS-2002 assessments (Table 2). The kappa values for individual NRS-2002 components (A-C) ranged from 0.198 to 1 (P < 0.001), showing poor agreement for disease severity, fair agreement for nutritional status, and perfect agreement for age scoring. Stratified analysis

Stratified analysis showed the highest agreement between nurses and dietitians in NRS-2002 screening among patients with breast cancer (kappa = 1.000, P = 0.001; potentially due to the small sample size, n = 11), followed by those with gastrointestinal tumors, esophageal cancer, head and neck cancer, lung cancer, and reproductive system tumors, with kappa values ranging from 0.208 to 0.489. Overall agreement between dietitians and nurses with junior professional titles was slightly higher than that of nurses with intermediate titles. Detailed results are shown in Table 4.

Discussion

The findings revealed notable differences in interrater consistency in NRS-2002 screening results between nurses and the dietitian among hospitalized patients with cancer. Nurses identified only about one-third as many patients at nutritional risk as the dietitian (73 vs. 211), indicating that a substantial proportion of at-risk patients may not have been recognized through routine nursing screening alone. This finding has important clinical implications, as nurse-led screening usually represents the first step in the inpatient nutritional care pathway. Under-identification at this stage may delay referral for comprehensive nutritional assessment and timely nutritional intervention, thereby potentially contributing to poorer clinical outcomes. For instance, a recent study supports the prognostic importance of integrated nutritional assessment approaches, demonstrating that combining SARC-F, GLIM criteria and biochemical parameters can significantly improve short-term mortality prediction in hospitalized older adults with advanced cancer [16]. We believe that this discrepancy may be ·related to differences in professional training, clinical focus, and interpretation of more subjective components of the tool. The agreement between the two evaluator groups was 67.74% (Table 2), and after adjustment for chance, the overall interrater reliability was fair (κ = 0.343, P < 0.001). Among the individual components, agreement was highest for age, fair for nutritional status, and lowest for disease severity. Higher screening consistency was observed in patients with breast cancer, followed by those with gastrointestinal and esophageal cancers. However, the breast cancer result should be interpreted cautiously because the subgroup was small (n = 11), despite the perfect agreement observed (κ = 1.000). In addition, nurses with junior professional titles showed slightly higher agreement with the dietitian than those with intermediate titles.

Paulsen et al. [14] reported low interrater agreement (κ = 0.27) between nurses and dietitians using the NRS-2002 in a hematology ward, consistent with the present findings and illustrating the common difficulty of achieving cross-professional consistency. However, their study was limited to patients with hematologic disorders and did not investigate the causes of scoring discrepancies between nurses and dietitians. In contrast, Tevik et al. [17] examined patients with chronic heart failure and found an agreement rate of 80% with a mean kappa value of 0.733, indicating substantial consistency. That study, however, included only two nurses and applied standardized disease severity scoring. The present study focused on hospitalized cancer patients and involved all clinical nurses in the ward. Similar to Paulsen et al. [14], it identified challenges in maintaining interrater consistency but further analyzed the detailed sources of variation, providing a more comprehensive understanding of the issue and strategies for improvement. Although Tevik et al. demonstrated high agreement, the limited number of participants and standardized scoring may restrict the generalizability of their results. By including a broader nursing cohort and omitting prior score standardization, this study more accurately reflects variability encountered in real clinical settings.

After stratification, this study found higher interrater consistency among patients with breast cancer, gastrointestinal tumors, and esophageal cancer, which may partly relate to the clinical manifestations of these diseases and their influence on key NRS-2002 scoring components. For instance, in breast cancer, a substantial proportion of patients are overweight or obese and may experience relatively stable nutritional intake during certain treatment stages [18]. Therefore, BMI and recent weight change, the two important elements of the NRS-2002 nutritional status domain, are often easier to identify and interpret consistently across evaluators. In contrast, gastrointestinal and esophageal cancers frequently involve the digestive tract and are commonly associated with dysphagia, reduced food intake, and treatment-related gastrointestinal symptoms [19]. These features often lead to more evident reductions in dietary intake and weight loss, which correspond directly to the intake and weight-change criteria within the NRS-2002 scoring system [20]. When these nutritional indicators are more clinically apparent, evaluators may reach more consistent judgments, thereby contributing to higher interrater agreement.

In this study, the lowest consistency was observed for disease severity scoring (k = 0.198), followed by nutritional status scoring (k = 0.241). This may reflect both the subjectivity and ambiguity of assessment criteria and differences in evaluators’ nutrition-related training. The assessment of nutritional status primarily involves evaluating weight loss and reduced food intake, which in clinical practice are often based on patients’ or families’ recollections, introducing subjectivity and reducing interrater reliability [21, 22]. The disease severity section of the NRS-2002 includes only 12 disease categories with suggested scoring guidelines, yet it remains one of the most debated components in clinical use. Several studies have shown that insufficient nutrition education results in limited nutritional knowledge among nurses, while structured training and effective supervision are often lacking in practice [23–28]. Consequently, nurses’ ability to perform accurate nutritional risk screening may be compromised.

Another factor that may contribute to differences between nurses and dietitians is the time available for screening in routine practice [15, 29]. In hospital settings, nurses often perform nutritional screening alongside multiple clinical tasks, which may limit the time spent on each assessment. Previous studies have reported that completing the NRS-2002 generally requires only a few minutes when performed as part of routine nursing workflow, whereas dietitians may spend longer reviewing medical records, confirming weight history, and clarifying recent dietary intake with patients or family members [30]. This difference in available time and depth of information gathering may influence the interpretation of certain items, particularly those requiring patient recall, such as recent food intake or weight loss. As a result, dietitians may identify nutritional risk more frequently because they have greater opportunity to verify information and explore borderline cases. Although screening time was not formally recorded in the present study, this factor may partially explain the discrepancy observed between the two evaluator groups and should be considered in future studies that aim to optimize the implementation of nutritional screening in busy clinical environments.

Nutritional risk screening is the first step in standardized nutritional care. Accurate risk identification provides the basis for subsequent nutritional assessment and intervention, ultimately improving clinical outcomes. As the primary executors of routine screening [29], clinical nurses play a pivotal role in ensuring accuracy. Disease-specific guidelines, such as the NRS-2002 Severity Grading Manual for Patients with Cancer, should be developed to standardize disease severity scoring and reduce variability. Structured education programs are also necessary to strengthen nurses’ knowledge, confidence, and self-efficacy in nutritional assessment and counseling [24], thereby minimizing subjectivity. For junior nurses, emphasis should be placed on operational accuracy, such as proper BMI calculation and precise documentation of weight loss, to reduce procedural errors. For intermediate-level nurses, simulation-based decision-making training using case analyses can help reduce reliance on experience-driven judgments. Additionally, hospitals should conduct regular audits of nutritional nursing quality and implement continuous improvement programs to enhance nurses’ practical competence in nutritional care.

This study has several limitations. First, nutritional risk was assessed using the clinician-reported NRS-2002 rather than patient-reported outcome (PRO) instruments such as the Patient-Generated Subjective Global Assessment (PG-SGA) or the Malnutrition Universal Screening Tool (MUST), which are also well-recognized in oncology nutrition research. NRS-2002 was used as it reflects routine clinical practice in many Chinese hospitals (including our institution), where this tool is integrated into electronic health record systems and used by nursing staff for rapid inpatient screening. Herein, our present study focused on inter-clinician agreement in the initial screening stage rather than incorporating patient-reported nutritional experiences. Differences in screening pathways between healthcare systems, particularly regarding the first-line use of PRO-based tools in some regions, should therefore be considered when interpreting the findings. Second, this was a single-center study conducted in one department, which may limit the generalizability of the results. Third, only one registered dietitian was used as the reference evaluator in this study. Therefore, the observed agreement may partly reflect the scoring characteristics of that individual dietitian and may not fully represent agreement with dietitians more generally, to a certain extent. In addition, since multiple nurses were compared against the same dietitian, the paired assessments may not have been fully independent, which could have affected the agreement estimates. Fourth, the retrospective design precluded direct observation of assessment procedures. Lastly, although standardized training was provided, its effectiveness was not formally evaluated. Future studies including multiple dietitians are needed to provide a more robust evaluation of interrater reliability, could adopt multicenter prospective designs and compare clinician-administered tools such as the NRS-2002 with PRO-based instruments (e.g., PG-SGA) to better define optimal nutritional screening pathways in oncology practice.

Conclusion

This study reveals fair interrater reliability of the NRS-2002 in assessing nutritional risk among hospitalized patients with cancer, with the agreement being high for more objective items, such as age, but was lower for domains requiring greater subjective judgment, particularly nutritional status and disease severity. These findings highlight the need for clearer clinical guidelines, standardized training, and regular supervision to improve assessment consistency, facilitate early identification of nutritional risk, and ultimately enhance clinical outcomes in oncology nursing practice.
