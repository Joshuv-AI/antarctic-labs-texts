A Rare Presentation of Asymmetrical Jaundice
Authors: Timothy Chan, Almothana Manasrah, Usama Sakhawat, Ahmed Shehadah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTJaundice typically reflects elevated serum bilirubin and manifests as a symmetric yellow discoloration of the skin and sclera. We describe a rare case of a young man who developed asymmetrical jaundice and edema days after receiving right lower quadrant paracenteses. The distribution of his jaundice and edema was anatomically correlated and favored his left side in both intensity and severity. Previous reports of asymmetrical jaundice speculated peritoneal-subcutaneous fistula formed by invasive abdominal procedures, but this incompletely explains this phenomenon. Asymmetrical jaundice is an extremely rare finding with scant representation in the literature, and its pathophysiology is likely multifactorial.

INTRODUCTION

Jaundice is a clinical manifestation of hyperbilirubinemia and may reflect various underlying diseases.1 Bilirubin, an orange-yellow heme metabolite, accumulates in the circulation when its production exceeds elimination, sometimes seen in hemolysis, hepatitis, cirrhosis, or biliary obstruction.2 Once elevated, bilirubin is distributed systemically through the vasculature, typically resulting in symmetric yellowish discoloration of the skin and sclera. Asymmetrical cases of jaundice are extremely uncommon and seldom discussed in the literature. We present a case of asymmetrical jaundice in a young man, followed by a review of the literature and discussion of its possible pathophysiology.

CASE REPORT

A 31-year-old man with a history of alcohol use disorder, hepatitis C carrier (untreated), and cirrhosis presented to the hospital with vomiting, poor appetite, fatigue, chills, and multiple episodes of syncope. He reported a 2-day alcohol binge before presentation and was feeling increasingly weak. He had a distant history of a motor vehicle accident and previously denied intravenous drug use. On presentation, he was tachycardic and hypotensive, and his laboratory test results showed hyponatremia, acute kidney injury (creatinine 4.8 mg/dL), and hepatitis (aspartate aminotransferase 971, alanine transaminase 158, alkaline phosphatase 467, total bilirubin 13.1 mg/dL, direct bilirubin 11.1 mg/dL).

Shortly after presentation, the patient experienced rapid clinical deterioration and was admitted to the critical care unit, where he was treated for toxic metabolic encephalopathy, shock, respiratory distress, Gram-negative bacteremia, and kidney failure. On the first day of admission, paracentesis was performed to the right lower quadrant (RLQ), and 1.9 L of fluid was collected without complication. That same day, computed tomography of the abdomen/pelvis with intravenous contrast demonstrated numerous small retroperitoneal lymph nodes. RLQ paracentesis was repeated on day 3, with 3 L of fluid taken. Peritoneal fluid analysis from both procedures showed no evidence of bacterial peritonitis. Ascitic bilirubin levels were not collected.

On hospital day 4, the patient developed noticeably asymmetric jaundice favoring the left side. The involvement of jaundice was extensive, involving his left upper and lower extremities, torso, face, and proximal right arm (Figures 1–4). The jaundice was most intense to the left distal arm, which happened to be significantly edematous as well (Figure 2). The distal right upper extremity and his entire right lower extremity were spared of both jaundice and edema (Figures 1 and 4). Upper extremity venous duplex ultrasound was performed and did not show deep vein thrombosis. This clinical change was associated with a dramatic rise in total bilirubin from 9.9 (day 4) to 21.8 mg/dL (day 5). His peak creatinine was 4.8 mg/dL requiring renal replacement therapy, peak total protein 5.4 g/dL, and albumin nadir 1.9 g/dL.

Unfortunately, the patient's condition did not improve, and with multiorgan failure his family switched him to comfort care. He died 8 days after admission. His presentation of unilateral jaundice and edema remained stable until his dying.

DISCUSSION

To our knowledge, only 2 cases of non-transient asymmetrical jaundice have been reported in the literature:

In 1967, Conn described a 65-year-old man with cirrhosis who underwent an emergency portacaval shunt placement for bleeding esophageal varices.3 Approximately 1 week later, he developed right-sided ascites, edema, and jaundice from the axilla to the inguinal ligament. In 1972, a 44-year-old man with decompensated cirrhosis developed jaundice and edema to his right leg and abdomen, 3 days after receiving right-sided paracentesis.4

These reports share similarities with our case: all involved men with cirrhosis who developed irregularly distributed jaundice and edema days after receiving an invasive right-sided procedure. Conn proposed that these procedures created a peritoneal-subcutaneous fistula, allowing for ascites to invade the subcutaneous space and spread asymmetrically.3 Although this hypothesis may explain asymmetrical edema, it does not fully account for the jaundice as ascitic bilirubin levels are typically low (<0.8 mg/dL) in the absence of choleperitoneum.5,6 Although concurrent abdominal wall fistulization and choleperitoneum could conceivably produce these symptoms, we have very low suspicion of this because a RLQ fistulization is pathologically inconsistent with the preferential accumulation of jaundice and edema to the left distal arm. In addition, computed tomography imaging showed no clear fistulization, and clinical suspicion for choleperitoneum was so low that ascitic bilirubin was not assessed.

A third case of asymmetrical jaundice from 1974 involved a young man with acute hepatitis with a transient unilateral jaundice due to an asymmetric rate of clearance.7 That patient had longstanding left-sided edema since childhood, attributed to be from aberrant lymph drainage related to lymphangiectasia. This suggests that aberrant vascular or lymphatic circulation may contribute to asymmetrical jaundice.

Our patient's complex hospital course included profound hypoalbuminemia and shock, both of which may have promoted interstitial third-spacing and peripheral vasoconstriction. His distant history of trauma from a motor vehicle accident raises the possibility of preexisting asymptomatic aberrant circulation that was exacerbated by his severe condition. Although venous doppler study did not detect deep vein thrombosis, other causes of impaired drainage from the left extremities may explain the preferential accumulation of jaundice and edema to that extremity.

Notably, our patient demonstrated hyperbilirubinemia of 21.8 mg/dL. At this level, diffuse jaundice would be expected, making the relative sparing of the right-sided extremities particularly striking. Concurrent afferent arterial compromise of the right extremities, due to a combination of shock-related vasoconstriction, vasopressor use, and distant trauma, may explain such sparing. Unfortunately, the presence and symmetry of scleral icterus was not documented, which could have contributed to this hypothesis.

Asymmetrical jaundice is an exceptionally rare clinical finding and has not been well-described in over 5 decades. Its mechanism remains unclear; although peritoneal-subcutaneous fistula has been proposed in the literature, it incompletely explains the preferential accumulation of edema and jaundice to the left distal arm. We suspect that our patient's pathophysiology is likely multifactorial, involving a combination of hyperbilirubinemia, shock, vasoconstriction, interstitial third-spacing, and/or abnormal circulation of blood or lymph. However, in the absence of additional confirmatory tests and dynamic imaging, such speculation remains hypothetical. We hope to increase awareness of this unusual phenomenon and encourage consideration of both local and systemic mechanisms when asymmetric jaundice is encountered.
