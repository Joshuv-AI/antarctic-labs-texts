Bedside peripherally inserted central catheter placement in trauma intensive care unit in South Korea: a comparative analysis of safety and tip positioning accuracy
Authors: Jung Rae Cho, Hancheol Jo, Dae Sung Ma, Dong Hun Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTransporting critically ill trauma patients for fluoroscopy-guided peripherally inserted central catheter (PICC) placement poses substantial risks. This study evaluated the clinical outcomes of bedside PICC insertion using a surface anatomical landmark-based measurement in a trauma intensive care unit (ICU).MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included trauma ICU patients who underwent PICC insertion between September 2023 and August 2024 at a regional trauma center. Insertion location was categorized as bedside or fluoroscopy-guided. Outcomes included optimal tip positioning, aberrant-course malposition, complication rates, and catheter dwell time. Multivariable linear regression identified factors associated with PICC duration.ResultsAmong 282 PICC procedures (171 bedside, 111 fluoroscopy), the bedside group had significantly higher injury severity scores and more frequent mechanical ventilation. Using the cm-based carina-distance criterion, optimal tip positioning was achieved in 73.1% of bedside and 65.5% of evaluable fluoroscopy-guided insertions (P=0.184). However, using the vertebral body unit-based radiologic criterion, target-zone achievement was lower in the bedside group than in the fluoroscopy group (48.5% vs. 64.9%). Initial aberrant-course malposition occurred in nine bedside insertions (5.3%) and in no fluoroscopy-guided insertions, and all were corrected before clinical use. Overall complication rates were comparable (17.5% vs. 18.0%, P=0.919). ConclusionsBedside PICC insertion using a surface anatomical measurement method may be a feasible transport-free option for selected critically ill trauma ICU patients.

INTRODUCTION

Peripherally inserted central catheters (PICCs) are essential for establishing reliable long-term venous access in critically ill patients and serve as safer alternatives to traditional central venous catheters for prolonged intravenous therapy [1-3]. In trauma intensive care units (ICUs), PICCs are particularly valuable for patients requiring extended courses of vasopressors, antibiotics, or parenteral nutrition, while minimizing the complications associated with central line placement [4].

Conventional PICC insertion under fluoroscopic guidance provides real-time visualization for optimal tip positioning [5]. However, transporting critically ill patients with trauma to interventional radiology suites poses substantial risks, particularly for those dependent on multiple life-supporting devices, including mechanical ventilation, continuous renal replacement therapy, and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation. Such transfers may increase the likelihood of hemodynamic instability, device disconnection, and delayed emergency responses [6,7].

Bedside PICC insertion has emerged as a compelling alternative to eliminate transport-related hazards while maintaining procedural safety. When performed under ultrasound guidance by experienced operators, bedside techniques achieve high success rates with minimal insertion complications [8-11]. However, this approach has inherent limitations, primarily the inability to verify the catheter tip position in real time and the challenges in accurately estimating the insertion length without fluoroscopic confirmation [5,12].

Various anatomical landmark-based methods have been developed to overcome these limitations [8,11,13-15]. At our institution, we developed a standardized surface landmark-based anatomical measurement approach: the puncture site, the mid-clavicular point, sternoclavicular junction, and right parasternal border of the third intercostal space [16].

Through this study, we aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of our standardized bedside measurement technique in trauma ICU patients by comparing tip positioning accuracy, procedural characteristics, and complication rates with those of fluoroscopy-guided insertion, thereby assessing its potential as a safe alternative when patient transport is undesirable.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The Institutional Review Board of Dankook University Hospital approved this retrospective study prior to data collection and analysis (No. DKUH 2025-05-016), and the requirement for informed consent was waived.

Study Design and Population

This retrospective study included patients with trauma admitted to our regional trauma center ICU who underwent PICC placement between September 2023 and August 2024. Patients aged ≥18 years and those with complete procedural and radiographic documentation were eligible for inclusion. Patients aged <18 years or those with insufficient clinical data were excluded from the analysis.

PICC Insertion Techniques

PICC placement was performed either at the bedside in the trauma ICU by four trauma surgeons or under fluoroscopic guidance in an interventional radiology suite by two interventional radiologists. Given the retrospective nature of this study, there was no predefined protocol for assigning patients to a specific method. Instead, the choice between bedside and fluoroscopy-guided placement was made case-by-case based on the real-time clinical judgment of the attending physician, primarily considering the patient's hemodynamic stability, severity of injuries, and the potential risks associated with intra-hospital transport. All procedures were conducted under maximal barrier precautions. Throughout the study period, four 5-Fr, double‑lumen PICC systems were used: Arrow Advantage 5 pressure injectable 2 lumen PICC (Teleflex); Hartz Raum double‑lumen 5‑Fr PICC (Sungwon Medical Co., Ltd.); Turbo‑Ject Power‑Injectable 5‑Fr double‑lumen PICC (Cook Medical); and PowerPICC 5‑Fr double‑lumen catheter (Bard Access Systems Inc.). At our institution, indications for PICC placement included the need for prolonged intravenous therapy (e.g., medications or parenteral nutrition), poor peripheral venous access, or the proactive exchange of a conventional central line maintained for over a week to mitigate catheter-related infection risks.

For bedside insertions, catheter length was estimated using our standardized surface landmark-based anatomical measurement technique: from the puncture site to the mid-clavicular point, from the mid-clavicle to the ipsilateral sternoclavicular junction, and from the sternoclavicular junction to the right parasternal border of the third intercostal space. For the left-sided insertions, an additional segment from the ipsilateral sternoclavicular junction to the contralateral parasternal border of the first intercostal space was included [16]. Once the ideal catheter length was estimated using the surface landmark method, the PICC was trimmed to the corresponding length prior to insertion. The patient was placed in the supine position with the arms abducted to greater than 90 degrees, the head rotated toward the ipsilateral arm, and the chin tucked. If any cervical spine abnormality was known or suspected, the head and neck were immobilized. All venous access was obtained under real-time ultrasound guidance. The guidewire was advanced, and the catheter was inserted. During catheter advancement, gentle manual compression was applied near the confluence of the ipsilateral jugular vein and the subclavian vein to facilitate catheter advancement toward the cavoatrial junction, while minimizing the risk of migration into the internal jugular vein. Post-procedural portable chest radiography was performed to confirm the tip position before clinical use. For fluoroscopy-guided insertions, following ultrasound-guided venous puncture, the catheter length was measured to position the tip approximately two vertebral body units (VBUs) below the carina, and the procedure was performed under continuous fluoroscopic visualization to ensure accurate tip placement.

Outcome Definitions

The primary outcome was the optimal tip positioning rate. Given that the bedside and fluoroscopy-guided groups utilized different procedural standards for catheter placement, we evaluated tip position using both the cm-based distance from the carina and VBU-defined criteria to ensure a comprehensive and balanced assessment of procedural outcomes. Accordingly, optimal positioning was dually defined: as the catheter tip located between the inferior border of the carina and up to 5 cm below it (for the cm-based measurement) [17], and two VBUs below the carina (for the fluoroscopy-guided criterion) [13]. To further elucidate the relationship between absolute distance and anatomic landmarks, the cm-based tip positions were subcategorized into four distinct ranges: above the carina (<0 cm), 0 to ≤3 cm, >3 to ≤6 cm, and >6 cm below the carina. These subcategories were then cross-tabulated against VBU-based classification, categorized as short (≤1 VBU), optimal (2 VBUs), or long (≥3 VBUs) for both groups. This subgroup analysis was performed to assess the concordance between the two measurement standards and to evaluate how the distribution of tip positions differed between the bedside and fluoroscopy-guided insertion methods. Both definitions are consistent with current guideline recommendations to position the tip in the lower superior vena cava or at the cavoatrial junction [1,13,18-21]. The secondary outcomes included the frequency of aberrant-course catheter malposition, complications related to PICC, and catheter dwell time. Procedure time was defined as the interval from the initiation of puncture to the application of the final dressing.

Initial aberrant-course malposition was defined as catheter deviation from the expected venous pathway detected immediately after insertion and requiring correction before clinical use (e.g., entry into the ipsilateral internal jugular vein, contralateral subclavian vein, or kinking within the subclavian vein). Delayed tip migration was defined as a newly identified catheter course abnormality, including J-shaped curling or migration into other veins, on follow-up radiography after initially acceptable catheter placement. Tips located above the carina but following the normal venous pathway were clinically maintained if functionally patent due to the limited access typical in severe trauma patients, and were thus evaluated separately from aberrant-course malpositions.

Complications were systematically categorized into infectious and mechanical adverse events. Infectious complications were categorized as suspected catheter-related fever, blood culture positivity, and catheter tip culture positivity. Suspected catheter-related fever was defined as fever without another identifiable source of infection leading to catheter removal. Because this retrospective study was not designed as a formal central line-associated bloodstream infection (CLABSI) surveillance study, confirmed CLABSI was not adjudicated as a separate outcome. Mechanical complications were strictly defined as irreversible catheter occlusion. Additionally, it was examined whether insertion-related factors, including the side of insertion (right vs. left) and tip location (above the carina, between the carina and up to 5 cm below, and >5 cm below the carina), were associated with complication rates.

Statistical Analysis

For demographic and baseline characteristics, the analysis was performed at the patient level; for patients requiring multiple PICCs, only data from the initial insertion were included to avoid duplication. For procedural characteristics and clinical outcomes, the analysis was performed at the procedure level because malposition, delayed migration, complications, and catheter dwell time were specific to each PICC procedure. Continuous variables were expressed as mean and standard deviation and compared between groups using the Student t-test. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages and compared using the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Subgroup analyses were performed to compare outcomes according to insertion and tip location categories. The incidence of complications was compared between the groups. To identify factors associated with clinical outcomes, regression analyses were performed for catheter removal due to complications using logistic regression and for catheter dwell time using linear regression. Because these outcomes were procedure-specific, the primary analyses were performed at the procedure level. To account for potential within-patient correlation from repeated PICC procedures, patient-level cluster-robust standard errors were used. A sensitivity analysis restricted to the first PICC procedure per patient was also performed. Variables associated with outcomes in univariate analysis or considered clinically relevant were considered for multivariable modeling. Final multivariable models were constructed based on clinical relevance, model stability, and avoidance of overfitting. All statistical analyses were conducted using the R software version 4.5.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Statistical significance was set at P<0.05.

RESULTS

Patient Characteristics

A total of 294 PICCs were placed in trauma ICU during the study period. After excluding 12 procedures because of insufficient data or patient age <18 years, 282 procedures were included in the final analysis (Figure 1). Of these, 171 (60.6%) were bedside PICC insertions and 111 (39.4%) were fluoroscopy-guided placements. These procedures were performed on 250 patients, as some underwent more than one PICC placement during their ICU stays. Patients in the bedside group exhibited significantly higher injury severity scores than those in the fluoroscopy-guided group (26.1±10.5 vs. 23.4±9.7, P=0.048). The overall distribution of injury patterns is presented in Table 1.

Procedural Outcomes

The procedural characteristics and outcomes are summarized in Table 2. The bedside group had higher rates of mechanical ventilation, targeted temperature management, and chest tube drainage than the fluoroscopy group. Right-sided insertion was predominant in both groups (80.7% vs. 84.7%, P=0.392). Basilic vein access was used in all fluoroscopy-guided cases, whereas it was used in 71.3% of bedside insertions (P<0.001). The mean procedure time did not differ significantly between the two groups (14.9±11.6 vs. 11.9±18.1 minutes, P=0.128). In the bedside group, the estimated surface anatomical landmark-based catheter length averaged 38.6±3.2 cm overall, with right-sided insertions measuring 38.1±2.7 cm, while left-sided insertions measured 40.8±3.9 cm.

Initial aberrant-course malposition occurred in nine bedside insertions (5.3%) and in no fluoroscopy-guided insertions (P=0.013). All nine initial malpositions in the bedside group were identified on post-procedural portable chest radiography and were corrected before clinical use, with proper placement reconfirmed on repeat radiography. Delayed tip migration occurred in 4 bedside insertions (2.3%) and 1 fluoroscopy-guided insertion (0.9%) (P=0.651) on follow-up chest radiography. In a sensitivity analysis that expanded initial aberrant-course malposition to include functionally patent but high-positioned tips above the carina, the combined rate of non-optimal/aberrant placements was 14.0% (24/171; 16 non-optimal and 9 aberrant placements, with 1 overlapping case) in the bedside group and 1.8% (2/111) in the fluoroscopy group (P=0.0003).

The mean tip-to-carina distance was significantly shorter in bedside insertions than in the fluoroscopy group (2.6±2.5 vs. 4.1±2.1 cm, P<0.001). Using the cm-based carina-distance criterion, optimal tip positioning was achieved in 125 of 171 bedside insertions (73.1%) and 72 of 110 evaluable fluoroscopy-guided insertions (65.5%) (P=0.184). One fluoroscopy-guided case could not be assessed using the cm-based distance due to technical limitations. Using this VBU-based criterion, 72 of 111 fluoroscopy-guided insertions (64.9%) were classified as successfully reaching the intended target zone, compared to 83 of 171 (48.5%) in the bedside group (P=0.007). Sub-category analysis revealed that the relationship between cm-based and VBU-based criteria was highly consistent across both groups (Figure 2). The 3 to 6 cm range showed the highest concordance with the VBU-defined optimal zone in both groups (88.9% vs. 90.0%), whereas the 0 to 3 cm range showed low VBU-optimal concordance (38.1% vs. 38.7%) and was predominantly classified as VBU-short.

Safety and Complications

The overall complication rates were comparable between the groups (17.5% vs. 18.0%, P=0.919). Suspected catheter-related fever occurred in 28 of 171 bedside insertions (16.4%) and 20 of 111 fluoroscopy-guided insertions (18.0%). Blood cultures were performed in all cases and the blood culture positivity rate was 39.3% (11/28) in the bedside group and 25.0% (5/20) in the fluoroscopy group (P=0.363). Catheter tip culture was performed in 40 of 48 cases (83.3%). Tip culture positivity was observed in 5 of 23 tested bedside cases (21.7%) and in none of the 17 tested fluoroscopy-guided cases (P=0.061). Catheter occlusion occurred in only two bedside cases (1.2%). While left-sided insertions showed a higher overall complication rate than right-sided insertions, there was no statistically significant difference (24.0% vs. 16.4%, P=0.201). Logistic regression analysis did not identify any statistically significant predictors of complications. Mean catheter dwell time was significantly longer in the bedside group (20.2±10.4 vs. 17.7±9.4 days, P=0.032). In the multivariable linear regression analysis, prolonged ICU stay and bedside insertion were associated with longer PICC duration, whereas in-hospital mortality and overall complications were associated with shorter PICC duration (Table 3). A sensitivity analysis restricted to the first PICC procedure per patient showed consistent results.

DISCUSSION

This study suggests that bedside PICC insertion using a standardized surface anatomical landmark-based measurement can be a feasible approach for selected trauma ICU patients in whom transport to the fluoroscopy suite is undesirable. Although bedside insertion lacks real-time fluoroscopic tip visualization, the observed initial aberrant-course malposition rate was within the range reported in previous studies using anatomical landmark-based or bedside techniques [8,9,11,13-15,22]. In addition, all initial aberrant-course malpositions in the bedside group were detected by post-procedural portable chest radiography and corrected before clinical use.

Transporting critically ill trauma patients to radiology suites poses substantial risks, including hemodynamic deterioration and complications related to multiple life-sustaining therapies [6,7]. Bedside PICC insertion therefore offers a practical advantage by avoiding transport while providing central venous access at the point of care. The surface landmark-based measurement used in this study was simple and reproducible, and the estimated catheter lengths were consistent with those reported in previous cadaveric and fluoroscopic studies [14,23]. However, tip-positioning outcomes should be interpreted cautiously because the bedside and fluoroscopy-guided groups were not targeted using identical anatomical criteria.

The optimal catheter tip position was initially assessed using a cm-based carina-distance criterion, defined as the zone between the inferior border of the carina and 5 cm below it, which has been used as a practical radiographic surrogate for the lower superior vena cava or cavoatrial junction [19,24-26]. Using this criterion, bedside insertion showed an acceptable optimal positioning rate. However, when the VBU-based radiologic criterion [13] was applied to both groups, bedside catheters tended to be positioned more shallowly than fluoroscopy-guided catheters. This discrepancy indicates that cm-based and VBU-based criteria are related but not interchangeable, and that the apparent performance of each technique depends on the definition used.

To further clarify this issue, we performed a subcategory analysis of cm-based tip positions. The bedside group clustered more frequently in this shallower 0 to 3 cm range, whereas the fluoroscopy-guided group clustered more frequently in the 3 to 6 cm range. Thus, the observed difference in tip-positioning outcomes is best understood as a difference in tip distribution related to different target standards, rather than as evidence of procedural superiority or inferiority. These findings suggest that future refinement of the bedside surface landmark method should aim to reduce shallow placement while preserving the transport-free advantage of bedside insertion.

The higher rate of initial aberrant-course malposition in the bedside group may be attributable to the inherent limitation of catheter advancement without real-time fluoroscopic visualization. Although ultrasound guidance enables safe venous puncture, it does not provide continuous visualization of the guidewire or catheter course during advancement. Therefore, the catheter may deviate into unintended venous pathways, such as the ipsilateral internal jugular vein or subclavian vein, owing to venous anatomy, valve resistance, patient positioning, or limited ability to optimize positioning in critically ill trauma patients. This finding underscores the importance of systematic post-procedural radiographic confirmation after bedside insertion, as all initial aberrant-course malpositions in our cohort were detected on portable chest radiography and corrected before clinical use.

Delayed tip migration was another clinically relevant radiographic observation in this study. Five delayed migration events were identified after initially acceptable catheter placement; all occurred after left-sided insertion. This finding is anatomically plausible because left-sided PICCs traverse a longer and more angulated course through the brachiocephalic vein, which may increase the risk of tip instability, endothelial irritation, or malposition [19,24]. However, these delayed migration events were uncommon and did not translate into a statistically significant difference in overall documented catheter-related complications between the bedside and fluoroscopy-guided groups. Because the number of events was small and follow-up radiography was not standardized, this result should be interpreted as exploratory. Nevertheless, left-sided PICCs may require more careful length estimation and radiographic follow-up during catheter dwell time.

Documented catheter-related complication rates were comparable between the two groups. Most complications were related to suspected catheter-related fever, whereas irreversible catheter occlusion was rare. Blood culture positivity and catheter tip culture positivity were numerically higher in the bedside group but did not reach statistical significance. These findings suggest that bedside insertion was not associated with a statistically significant increase in documented infectious or mechanical catheter-related complications in this cohort. However, this interpretation should be cautious because catheter tip cultures were not performed in all suspected infection cases, and routine Doppler ultrasound screening was not performed to detect asymptomatic catheter-related thrombosis. Therefore, clinically silent thrombosis, particularly in high-positioned or unstable tips, may have been underestimated.

Catheter dwell time was longer in the bedside group, but this finding should be interpreted in the context of patient selection and ongoing vascular access needs. PICC duration was evaluated as a secondary outcome to provide contextual information on whether bedside PICCs, including those requiring correction of initial aberrant-course malposition, could remain in clinical use after successful placement. It was not intended as a directional measure of procedural superiority. In the multivariable linear regression analysis using patient-level cluster-robust standard errors, prolonged ICU stay and bedside insertion were associated with longer PICC duration, whereas in-hospital mortality and overall complications were associated with shorter PICC duration. These findings should be interpreted cautiously. Catheter dwell time is influenced by multiple competing clinical factors, including ongoing vascular access needs, treatment completion, discharge, mortality, and complication-related removal. The association between bedside insertion and longer dwell time may therefore reflect the clinical context in which bedside PICC was selected, particularly among patients requiring prolonged intensive care and venous access. Accordingly, the absence of a shorter dwell time in the bedside group may provide indirect contextual evidence that bedside PICCs could remain in clinical use after appropriate correction of the initial catheter position. However, this finding should not be interpreted as evidence of superior catheter durability or procedural efficacy.

Despite acceptable procedural outcomes, the inability to verify the tip position in real time remains an important limitation of bedside PICC insertion. Intracavitary electrocardiography (IC-ECG) has been used in many bedside PICC insertion protocols, and previous studies have reported high diagnostic performance [27-29]. However, IC-ECG was not available at our institution during the study period because dedicated equipment had not been introduced. Moreover, its applicability may be limited in some critically ill trauma patients with rhythm disturbances, unclear P-wave changes, severe tachyarrhythmia, or pacing. Therefore, in this cohort, post-procedural portable chest radiography remained the standard method for tip confirmation after bedside insertion. Future adoption of adjunctive tools, such as portable handheld X-ray confirmation, may improve positioning accuracy while preserving the transport-free advantage of bedside PICC placement.

This study had some limitations. First, its retrospective, single-center design inherently introduces selection bias and residual confounding because the choice between bedside and fluoroscopy-guided insertion was based on clinical judgment rather than random allocation. Second, the analysis was based on completed PICC procedures with available procedural and radiographic documentation; therefore, aborted insertion attempts and insertion-related complications may not have been fully captured. Third, the two insertion strategies used different target standards. Although additional cm-based and VBU-based analyses were performed, these criteria are not fully interchangeable, and direct comparison of tip-positioning accuracy should be interpreted cautiously. Fourth, complication assessment was limited by retrospective documentation, non-standardized culture practices, and the absence of routine Doppler ultrasound screening; thus, asymptomatic catheter-related thrombosis may have been underestimated. Finally, the small number of events for some outcomes limited statistical power.

In conclusion, bedside PICC insertion using a standardized surface anatomical landmark-based measurement method may be a feasible transport-free option for selected critically ill trauma ICU patients. Although documented catheter-related complication rates were comparable to those of fluoroscopy-guided insertion, bedside placement tended to result in a shallower tip-position distribution and required careful post-procedural radiographic confirmation. Future adjunctive bedside tip-confirmation strategies may further improve the accuracy of bedside PICC placement while preserving its advantage of avoiding patient transport.

KEY MESSAGES

▪ Bedside peripherally inserted central catheter (PICC) insertion using a surface anatomical measurement method may provide a feasible transport-free vascular access option for selected high-risk trauma intensive care unit patients.

▪ Bedside insertion showed comparable documented catheter-related complication rates but required careful post-procedural radiographic confirmation because of shallower tip distribution and initial aberrant-course malposition.

▪ Adjunctive bedside tip-confirmation strategies may further improve positioning accuracy while preserving the transport-free advantage of bedside PICC placement.
