Bioluminescent sentinel plants enable autonomous diagnostics of viral infections
Authors: Camilo Calvache, Marta Rodriguez-Rodriguez, Victor Vazquez-Vilriales, Elena Garcia-Perez, Aubin Fleiss, Mustafá Ezzeddin-Ayoub, Fabio Pasin, José-Antonio Daròs, Karen S. Sarkisyan, Diego Orzaez, Marta Vazquez-Vilar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Plants engineered with synthetic genetic programs can transform how we monitor and manage the extension of crop pests and diseases. Here, we establish a bioluminescent platform in Nicotiana benthamiana for autonomous viral sensing based on the fungal bioluminescence pathway (FBP). We first demonstrate that recombinant viruses can deliver missing pathway components, establishing a methodological framework for spatially resolved tracking of infection dynamics. Leveraging this starting point, we develop a dual-output sentinel circuit that uses a protease-responsive bioluminescence resonance energy transfer (BRET) module to report infection through a virus-triggered spectral shift in luminescence. In the absence of infection, plants emit a stable yellow glow indicating system integrity. Upon infection with potyviruses, cleavage of the BRET fusion by the virus-encoded NIa-protease activates a distinct colour change detectable with low-cost imaging. This modular design is compatible with other pathogens carrying specific proteases and supports future multiplexing strategies. Our results highlight the potential of synthetic sentinel gene circuits as autonomous biosensors for precision crop protection.

Introduction

The integration of continuous sensing technologies into production systems enables real-time monitoring of plant health, facilitating data-driven decision making and optimized crop management1. Synthetic biology provides a framework for equipping plants with novel functions, including the ability to sense and respond to environmental cues through engineered genetic circuits2–6. A particularly compelling application lies in engineering autonomous sentinel plants, or phytosensors, that sense environmental threats and communicate them via remotely detectable, ideally quantifiable, outputs7–10. While certain plant species have long been used in agriculture as indicators of pests or diseases, and more recently for monitoring invasive plant pathogens in the context of climate change11,12, these traditional sentinel systems often suffer from low specificity, inconsistent performance, and a reliance on late-stage visible symptoms.

Recent advances in plant synthetic biology have enabled autonomous light emission in plants by expressing the fungal bioluminescence pathway (FBP) from Neonothopanus nambi, without the need for external substrates or excitation13. This pathway has since been repurposed as a versatile reporter for transcriptional circuit design14,15, with recent improvements including the successive enhancements of the pathway efficiency leading to the enhanced fungal bioluminescence pathway (eFBP)16,17, and the miniaturization of the system through the replacement of hispidin synthase (HispS) with smaller, plant-derived alternatives18. These developments support in vivo, substrate-free monitoring of plant processes at high spatiotemporal resolution19,20.

Here, we assess autobioluminescent plants as living biosensors for viral infection. We propose that light-emitting genetic circuits can be deployed to create responsive sentinel systems, capable of translating viral presence into detectable light signals. Unlike luciferase or fluorescent reporters, autobioluminescence permits non-invasive imaging in real time using standard, low-cost optical equipment, making it suitable for field-based phytodiagnostics.

We develop two mechanistically distinct strategies. First, as a methodological approach to establish and validate whole-plant bioluminescence detection and viral infection tracking, we engineer recombinant plant viruses carrying a mini-HispS gene to complement transgenic Nicotiana benthamiana lines lacking this pathway component. Upon infection, the complete bioluminescent circuit is reconstituted, producing light specifically in infected tissues. Second, we design a virus-responsive bioluminescence resonance energy transfer (BRET) circuit that integrates a luciferase–fluorescent protein fusion21 cleavable by NIa proteases (NIa-Pro) encoded by members of the Potyviridae, the largest group of plant RNA viruses22. This dual-output system (Yellow-OFF/Green-ON) mimics electronic status indicators: uninfected plants emit yellow light via sustained BRET, while infection triggers a spectral shift towards green due to NIa-Pro-mediated cleavage, providing a virus-specific optical signature.

Together, these strategies provide a modular framework for phytosensing. By coupling synthetic bioluminescent circuits with viral sensing mechanisms, we show the feasibility of creating programmable, substrate-free sentinel plants capable of reporting infections remotely with specificity, autonomy, and spatiotemporal precision.

Results

Spatiotemporal viral tracking with autobioluminescence supports whole-plant infection monitoring and reveals the uncoupling between local and systemic responses

We first assessed whether luminescence could be used to monitor viral spread at the organ and whole-plant level using minimal imaging equipment. With this methodological aim, recombinant viruses were employed as tools to probe infection dynamics rather than as final sensing devices. Hispidin synthesis has been reported as the main rate-limiting step of the FBP15, which makes its restoration suitable for establishing a direct and quantitative relationship between viral cargo expression and luminescence output. However, the original fungal HispS is too large to be efficiently propagated by recombinant plant RNA viruses. We therefore employed compact, plant-derived HispS substitutes, namely HmS, ASCL and PKS2, previously described by Palkina et al.18 and delivered them using viral vectors in FBPΔHispS N. benthamiana plants stably transformed with the remaining three pathway components: hispidin-3-hydroxylase (H3H), luciferase (Luz) and caffeylpyruvate hydrolase (CPH). Virus-associated luminescence levels were assessed in darkness using consumer-grade Digital Single-Lens Reflex (DSLR) cameras optimized for low-light detection linked to a customized lab management platform25. A software tool was developed to quantify luminescent signals within defined regions of the captured images. To monitor organ-level dynamics, fully agroinfected leaves were detached post-infiltration, placed in Petri dishes under long-day photoperiod conditions, and imaged every 24 h. Under these settings, clear differences in luminescence kinetics were observed among the three viral systems tested (Fig. 1b, quantified in Fig. 1c). The BeYDV replicon reached peak luminescence at 3 days post-inoculation (dpi), whereas the PVX and TMVΔCP systems peaked three and four days later, respectively. All three mini-HispS variants were functional across the systems, with the exception of ASCL, which exhibited undetectable local activity when mobilized via a TMVΔCP infectious clone. Although ASCL failed to produce detectable luminescence in this context, its functionality in other expression systems indicates that this effect is unlikely to arise from intrinsic protein properties and may instead reflect vector-specific constraints. Analysis of viral load in locally infected tissues carrying BeYDV-HmS, PVX-HmS, or TMV-HmS revealed correlation with luminescence intensity (Supplementary Fig. 1), indicating that mini-HispS-based reporters can serve as quantitative proxies.

We next evaluated the ability to trace and quantify the systemic spread of luminescence following local agroinoculation of basal leaves with TMV and PVX clones (Fig. 1d, e, and Supplementary Movie 1). When delivered via a PVX vector, all three HispS substitutes induced detectable luminescence in systemic tissues by 4 dpi, just 24 h after light emission first appeared in locally infected leaves (Fig. 1d, upper panel). The infection dynamics were comparable across the three PVX clones, as illustrated by the time-course of normalized luminescence (Fig. 1d, lower panel). Detailed inspection of systemic PVX-infected tissues revealed that luminescence consistently preceded the onset of visible symptoms (Supplementary Fig. 2). As expected, HmS and PKS2, but not ASCL, triggered local luminescence when delivered via TMV (Fig. 1e, upper panel). Unexpectedly, and in contrast to initial expectations, only the TMV-ASCL infectious clone induced detectable luminescence in systemic tissues. The time-course analysis of normalized luminescence shows this divergent behaviour, clearly distinguishing ASCL from HmS and PKS2 (Fig. 1e, lower panel). These results indicate a functional uncoupling between local and systemic responses, suggesting that strong local activation does not necessarily predict the establishment of systemic infection.

Engineering of a BRET-based spectral switch via targeted proteolytic cleavage

Having developed a methodological framework for viral-associated bioluminescence detection with minimal instrumentation, we next shifted from infection tracking to the implementation of an autonomous, potyvirus-responsive sentinel system (Fig. 2a). This transition required moving from viral complementation assays to a fully genome-encoded configuration, in which all components necessary for light emission and signal processing are stably integrated in the plant. Central to this design was the development of a bioluminescent colour switch based on BRET, using Luz as the energy donor (emission range: 460–625 nm). Accordingly, the BRET module was built on an FBPΔLuz background, with Luz supplied in cis as part of a protease-responsive fusion protein. The fungal HispS-based pathway was retained for the remaining steps of the bioluminescent reaction, as it provides higher brightness in planta when combined with Aspergillus nidulans 4′-phosphopantetheinyl transferase (NPGA), as previously reported18. To evaluate energy transfer, we generated fusion constructs by placing each acceptor at either the N- or C-terminus of Luz, connected via a flexible GGGGS linker (GS1X). These constructs were transiently expressed in N. benthamiana alongside the remaining components of the FBP lacking LUZ (eFBPΔLuz) and visualized using a DSLR camera. Only the C-terminal fusions, particularly Luz:DsRed, produced a visible shift in luminescence from green to a yellow colour (resulting from the combined red and green emissions caused by partial BRET efficiency), which was more readily detected in DSLR images by using a purple dichroic filter (Fig. 2c). This orientation dependence is consistent with the presence of a native transmembrane domain at the N-terminus of Luz, whose disruption by N-terminal fusion likely interferes with proper membrane association and catalytic activity.

BRET was accompanied by a modest reduction in total luminescence except for Luz:DsRed (Fig. 2d). Upon external excitation, DsRed fluorescence was observed exclusively in C-terminal fusions, as confirmed by confocal microscopy (Fig. 2e) and fluorometric analysis (Fig. 2f), and displayed a punctate pattern consistent with endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and Golgi apparatus endomembrane association (Supplementary Fig. 3). Based on the distinct and robust spectral shift, we selected the Luz:DsRed fusion for further BRET optimization. Spectral emission profiles were confirmed using a charge-coupled device (CCD) camera (Fig. 2g), and a series of alternative linker sequences were tested to improve energy transfer (Fig. 2h). Among these, the so-called OD and GGS linkers yielded the greatest BRET efficiency (Fig. 2i, j) and were retained for subsequent experiments.

We next introduced an optimized tobacco etch virus (TEV) NIa-Pro cleavage site (CS) into the BRET linker to generate a colour switch responsive to site-specific proteolysis (Fig. 3a). Insertion of the CS did not significantly affect BRET efficiency in any of the tested linkers (Supplementary Fig. 4). All Luz:linker-CSTEV:DsRed constructs were then co-infiltrated in N. benthamiana leaves with or without co-expression of the TEV NIa-Pro, following the experimental design outlined in Fig. 3b. A shift in luminescence from yellow to green was observed exclusively in the presence of the protease, indicating cleavage and release of DsRed. This spectral switch was detectable both by filtered DSLR camera and with a CCD camera equipped with spectral analysis system (Fig. 3c). To investigate the spatial dynamics of the cleavage event, the subcellular localization of DsRed was examined by confocal microscopy (Fig. 3d). In the absence of the protease, DsRed localized to endomembrane-associated compartments, as confirmed by colocalization with green fluorescent organelle markers. Upon NIa-Pro co-expression, DsRed signal redistributed to the nucleo-cytoplasm, consistent with protease-mediated release of the fluorescent moiety.

To further validate the functionality of the spectral switch, we replaced the recombinant protease with an infectious viral clone. To assess specificity, we also included an infectious clone of the tobacco vein mottling virus (TVMV), a potyvirus carrying a protease with a distinct cleavage site, in the assay (see Supplementary Fig. 5). As shown in Fig. 3e, agroinoculation with the TEV infectious clone, but not with TVMV, induced a statistically significant shift in the 520/570 nm luminescence ratio in plants expressing the Luz:GGS-CSTEV:DsRed sentinel construct. This colour change was readily detectable using both a CCD and DSLR camera systems (Fig. 3e). Conversely, the TVMV-responsive construct displayed equivalent specificity, responding only to TVMV and not to TEV (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Genomic integration of the BRET-based spectral switch enables autonomous detection of TEV infection in N. benthamiana

To establish a bona fide glowing sentinel (Fig. 4a), we generated two independent transgenic N. benthamiana lines, both co-expressing (i) the output luminescence module eFBPΔLuz expressing HispS, H3H, CPH and NPGA and (ii) a BRET-based switch module, incorporating either the Luz:GGS-CSTEV:DsRed, or the Luz:OD-CSTEV:DsRed construct (GGSTEV or ODTEV lines, respectively). The performance and specificity of the switch were then tested by transient expression of the TEV NIa-Pro by agroinfiltration with TEV, using TVMV as a negative control (Fig. 4b). Next, we assessed activation of the green-ON state in systemic tissues following agroinoculation of basal leaves with the viral construct. In ODTEV#15 sentinel plants, this systemic response was first assessed by spectral analysis of distal tissues using CCD camera imaging. As shown (Fig. 4c), distal tissues exhibited a green-shifted yellow luminescence exclusively upon infection with TEV, but not with TVMV, confirmed by quantification of the G/R ratio in each plant (Fig. 4d). The presence or absence of each virus in systemic tissues was validated by reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) (Fig. 4d). To evaluate sentinel performance using low-cost equipment, luminescence was then monitored over time in TEV- or TVMV-infected ODTEV#15 plants using an unfiltered DSLR camera (Fig. 4e). Under these conditions, a faint yellow glow, corresponding to the yellow-OFF state, was visible from the start of the experiment, primarily in the stem, and was independent of infection status. Upon TEV infection, a green-ON luminescent signal first appeared in locally inoculated leaves at 3 dpi, followed several days later by robust green luminescence in apical tissues. The signal then propagated basipetally, following a stereotypical vasculature-first spread pattern (Supplementary Movies 2, 3). This dynamic produced a characteristic time-course profile that could be captured using simple luminescence measurements (Fig. 4f).

The correct functioning of the sentinel was further assessed by analyzing selected tissue samples from TEV- and TVMV-infected plants (depicted in Fig. 4g). The presence of each virus was first verified by RT-PCR (Fig. 4h), followed by assessment of the subcellular distribution of the Luz:DsRed fusion partners. The systemic switch to the green-ON state correlated with the expected molecular and subcellular signatures, including the translocation of DsRed signal from microsomal to soluble fractions as detected by Western blot (Fig. 4i) and a redistribution of red fluorescence from endomembrane-associated regions to nucleocytoplasmic compartments observed by confocal microscopy (Fig. 4j).

In late-stage TEV infections, basal leaves occasionally displayed luminescence patterns that deviated from the typical vasculature-first spread (Fig. 4k, upper panel), raising questions about their infection status. As shown in Fig. 4k (lower panel), the yellow-OFF/green-ON dual-output logic, captured using a DSLR camera with a purple filter, enabled discrimination of infection states, independent of absolute luminescence intensity. TVMV-infected tissues showed the lowest G/R ratios, while TEV-infected apical tissues showed the highest (Fig. 4l). Basal leaves from TEV-infected plants exhibited significantly lower G/R ratios than apical tissues, consistent with low but RT-PCR-detectable viral loads and predominant microsomal localization of DsRed (Fig. 4m, n).

To further evaluate sentinel specificity, we challenged plants with two phylogenetically distant viruses, PVX and TMV (Fig. 5a; Supplementary Movie 4). As with TVMV, neither PVX nor TMV triggered the green-ON state; only TEV induced the characteristic luminescence time-course associated with infection (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Fig. 6). As previously observed in late-stage TEV infections, elevated total luminescence occasionally appeared in necrotic basal leaves during advanced TMV infection (Fig. 5c). However, filtered DSLR images (Fig. 5c) distinguished infection states, revealing a yellow-OFF output (low G/R ratios) in TMV-infected basal leaves, in contrast to the green-ON signature (high G/R ratios) in apical leaves of TEV-infected plants (Fig. 5d). Molecular diagnostics confirmed the infection status of all samples (Fig. 5e, f). Notably, the reproducibility of the G/R ratios across experiments allow the definition of an empirical threshold, with values below 0.7 corresponding to non-TEV-infected plants and values above 0.7 consistently indicating TEV infection (see Figs. 4i, 5d and Supplementary Fig. 6). RT-PCR analysis independently confirmed the presence of PVX or TEV in the corresponding tomato and sentinel plants, with a higher proportion of positive samples detected at the highest TEV inoculum (Fig. 6g). These results indicate that luminescence activation in sentinel plants correlates with viral infection and that TEV doses sufficient to infect tomato plants also trigger reporter activation in sentinel plants, preceding the visual appearance of disease symptoms in tomato. Consistent with viral infection status, sentinel plants that tested negative by RT-PCR (e.g. plants 6C and 8B) did not show any increase in bioluminescence over time, whereas RT-PCR-confirmed infected plants (e.g. plant 8D, 10E and 12C) displayed an increase in luminescence, as shown in Fig. 6e, f. Although a marginal increase in total luminescence was occasionally observed at late stages of PVX infection, this signal remained weak and close to background levels (Fig. 6f) and was readily distinguishable from TEV-induced activation. In contrast, only TEV infection produced a reproducible shift in the G/R ratio, clearly above the 0.7 arbitrary threshold, consistent with protease-dependent cleavage of the Luz:DsRed reporter (Fig. 6h). Western blot analysis corroborated these findings, revealing accumulation of free DsRed exclusively in TEV-infected samples, while PVX-infected plants retained the intact fusion protein (Fig. 6i).

Discussion

Early and reliable detection of crop pathogens is increasingly critical for sustainable agriculture, particularly under the pressures of climate change and reduced pesticide use. Yet conventional diagnostic methods such as PCR and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), though accurate, are invasive, offline, and reliant on laboratory infrastructure, limiting their scalability for in-field use. Passive spectral techniques (e.g., near-infrared (NIR), thermal imaging) offer non-invasive alternatives but typically rely on indirect physiological signatures and complex interpretation models. Biosensors and nanomaterials have been proposed to monitor plant health by detecting metabolites such as H2O2 and the phytohormone salicylate26,27, which are known to accumulate at high levels during biotic stress, including viral infections. However, physiological or metabolic signatures may not be reliable indicators of pathogen presence, as some infections can remain asymptomatic or latent28. For example, negative autoregulation in potyviruses has been reported as an immune evasion strategy that modulates infection kinetics to avoid the activation of phytohormone signaling pathways29.

Active phytosensing systems report defined molecular events, thus offering greater specificity and interpretability. Our work addresses this need by providing a modular platform for autonomous pathogen detection in plants, integrating synthetic signal processing with visible optical outputs. By embedding a protease-responsive BRET circuit into a bioluminescent chassis, we engineered N. benthamiana plants that act as living sentinels, capable of reporting viral infections through both spectral and intensity-based cues. This illustrates an application of plant synthetic biology in a greenhouse-compatible setting, and a step toward the development of deployable sentinel plants for precision crop protection in defined agricultural settings.

A key feature of the system is its ability to detect virus-specific proteolytic activity in vivo. The TEV-responsive switch distinguishes infected from non-infected plants and even resolves infection boundaries within a single plant under the conditions tested. This detection was achieved using low-cost imaging tools, supporting its use for real-time, remote monitoring without external reagents or invasive sampling. A key conceptual advance is the implementation of a dual-output design that mimics status-indicator logic in electronic alarm systems: not only does the system report infection through a spectral switch, but it also provides a stable signal in healthy plants, confirming system integrity. This “pilot light” feature is useful for minimizing false negatives (e.g. due to eventual transgene silencing) and ensure confidence in the absence of infection, an often-overlooked but essential attribute for practical deployment. In our experiments we observed that protease activation led not only to a spectral shift but to a modest increase in luminescence intensity. This may reflect improved enzymatic performance upon cleavage of the Luz:DsRed fusion, possibly due to relief of steric hindrance or quenching. Alternatively, the activation of defense mechanisms and/or necrotic reactions could enhance flux through the phenylpropanoid pathway, increasing availability of caffeic acid, the FBP substrate. This later mechanism would also explain the increase in yellow luminescence observed during late stages of both specific and non-specific infections. Either mechanism is consistent with the sensitivity of bioluminescence to intracellular context and stress physiology.

Although initially developed for TEV and extended to TVMV, the approach may be generalizable. Numerous plant pathogens, including viruses, bacteria, and fungus, deploy proteases or intracellular effectors with defined cleavage specificities. BRET linker peptides can be redesigned for specificity toward the over 200 known potyviruses22, including potato virus Y (PVY), one of the most economically damaging plant viruses worldwide30. Conversely, NIa-Pro cleavage motifs themselves can be engineered to tune the switch’s sensitivity, ranging from highly specific recognition, as demonstrated here and by others31, to broader-spectrum responsiveness, as recently shown by Wang et al.32. Moreover, bacterial effectors such as AvrRpt233 and AvrPphB34 offer opportunities for tailored biosensing beyond viral targets. These effectors cleave host proteins at well-defined motifs, yet their deployment in synthetic circuits similarly requires balancing efficient cleavage with avoidance of unintended activation by host or pathogen-derived proteases with overlapping specificities35. Together, these features position protease-activated switches as a versatile and modular platform for real-time plant health surveillance across a broad spectrum of pathogen types.

A particularly attractive deployment scenario for bioluminescent sentinels is an intercropping model, as exemplified in our results, in which sentinel plants are grown alongside the target crop. In such a configuration, sentinels can be either the crop species itself or a closely related, highly susceptible host for the pathogen of interest, enabling early and localized detection before symptom emergence in the crop. In this context, the LAB ecotype of N. benthamiana, possibly as a result of the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase 1 (RDR1) mutation, represents a highly advantageous sentinel chassis due to its exceptional susceptibility to a broad range of plant pathogens, making it a well suited candidate for broad-spectrum biosentinel applications.

In their current configuration, bioluminescent sentinel plants are best suited for environments where ambient light can be controlled, such as growth chambers or greenhouse compartments. We performed quantitative measurements of nocturnal ambient illumination across multiple outdoor locations, which were directly compared with experimentally defined detection thresholds, and found that infection-associated luminescence remains distinguishable under moderate light levels typical of low-light-pollution rural settings, particularly in shaded microenvironments, but not under peri-urban conditions with high artificial light contamination (see Supplementary Fig. 7). Under suitable low-light conditions, luminescence therefore provides an early-warning signal, which can be confirmed by quantitative G/R ratio analysis or optical filtering. Future developments may focus on increasing the brightness and versatility of the system. In this context, recent implementation of the FBP as a single polycistronic RNA simplifies genetic design while improving stoichiometric control and expression stability36. Additional gains may be achieved through the use of enhanced luciferase variants37, BRET acceptors with higher quantum yield, and pathways emitting at shorter wavelengths, such as the five-colour Nano-lanternX (NLX) system38, to boost signal strength and enable expanded multiplexing. Integration of orthogonal sensors into multicolours sentinel lines offers a route toward simultaneous detection of diverse pathogens or environmental cues. While N. benthamiana is a widely used host for viral detection due to its broad susceptibility39, its utility as a dedicated phytosensor chassis could be further improved through targeted engineering, such as suppressing immune responses to enhance sensitivity and maintain signal integrity40.

Altogether, this work provides a proof-of-concept for whole-plant biosensors capable of decoding pathogen-derived molecular signals into visible readouts. By leveraging synthetic biology to make infections visible, bioluminescent sentinels may help shift plant disease management from reactive to predictive strategies, contributing to sustainable agriculture.

Methods

Bacterial strains, plant material and material availability

Agrobacterium tumefaciens strains GV3101, EHA105 and LBA4404, and Escherichia coli TOP10 were obtained from pre-existing laboratory-maintained glycerol stocks and used for cloning and plant transformation procedures. Nicotiana benthamiana plants used in this study were grown from laboratory-maintained seed stocks. The FBPΔHispS lines used in the experiments shown in Fig. 1 were previously generated by the Sarkisyan laboratory and are available from Karen Sarkisyan for academic research use upon request at karen.s.sarkisyan@gmail.com. Newly generated sentinel N. benthamiana lines described in this study are available from the corresponding authors for academic research use, subject to completion of a material transfer agreement where required. Requests will be processed within 4 weeks.

Modular cloning

DNA constructs used in this work were assembled using GoldenBraid (GB) modular cloning system41. Coding sequences of proteins used in this work were codon optimized for N. benthamiana using IDT Codon Optimization Tool (https://eu.idtdna.com/CodonOpt) and subsequently domesticated using the GB Domesticator tool (https://goldenbraidpro.com/do/synthesis/). Level 0, Level 1 and Level >1 assemblies were performed using the GoldenBraid typeIIS restriction-ligation protocol described by Sarrion-Perdigones et al.41, using the appropriate GB destination vectors and restriction enzymes for each assembly level. Level 0 parts were confirmed by restriction analyses and Sanger sequencing, and Level 1 and Level >1 assemblies were verified by restriction analyses. All plasmids used in this work are listed in Supplementary Data 1.

N. benthamiana transient expression

Plasmids were transferred to Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 or EHA105 (see Supplementary Data 1 and Supplementary Table 1 for details). Five-to-six week-old N. benthamiana plants grown under controlled conditions (24 °C, 16 h light/20 °C, 8 h dark) were used for agroinfiltration. Overnight A. tumefaciens cultures were pelleted and resuspended in infiltration buffer (10 mM MES, pH 5.6; 10 mM MgCl2; 200 µM acetosyringone) to an optical density of 0.1 at 600 nm (OD600). Bacterial suspensions were incubated for 2 h at room temperature on a horizontal rolling mixer in darkness. For co-expression experiments different Agrobacterium cultures harboring different plasmids were mixed prior to infiltration (see Supplementary Data 3). Finally, infiltration was performed using a 1-ml needleless syringe, applied to the abaxial side of the three youngest fully expanded leaves per plant. Detailed information on the experimental design can be found in the Supplementary Data 3 file.

N. benthamiana viral agroinoculation

Binary vectors of BeYDV, PVX, TMV, TEV and TVMV clones were reported23,42–45. Plasmids were transferred to the A. tumefaciens strains EHA105 or GV3101. Single colonies were grown at 28 °C for 24 h, pelleted and resuspended in agroinfiltration buffer to an OD600 of 0.1 for PVX, TMV and BeYDV, and OD600 of 0.3 for TEV and TVMV. Agroinoculations for BeYDV, TMV and PVX tracking were performed in N. benthamiana transgenic lines constitutively expressing three out of the four genes of the FBP (CPH, H3H and Luz) under cauliflower mosaic virus (CaMV) 35S promoter. For local infection assays, infiltrated leaves were detached at 1 dpi for PVX and BeYDV, and at 2 dpi for TMV, and placed on Petri dishes containing water. For systemic infection assays with PVX, TMV, TEV and TVMV, one or two basal leaves per plant were agroinoculated at a single infiltration site with an approximate diameter of 2 cm. Plants and leaves were maintained under 16 h light / 8 h dark photoperiod and photographed every 2, 6 or 24 h, depending on the experiment.

N. benthamiana and tomato mechanical inoculation

Systemically infected N. benthamiana leaves displaying PVX or TEV symptoms were harvested and used to prepare crude extracts for mechanical inoculation of sentinel plants and Solanum lycopersicum var. Moneymaker. For inoculum preparation, infected tissue was frozen in liquid nitrogen and ground to a fine powder. For each gram of infected tissue, 20 mL of inoculation buffer (50 mM potassium phosphate, pH 8.0, 1% polyvinylpyrrolidone-10, 1% polyethylene glycol-6000, 10 mM 2-mercaptoethanol) were added, resulting in a 1:20 (w/v) extract. Serial dilutions (1:200 and 1:2000 (w/v)) were subsequently prepared in inoculation buffer. For mechanical inoculation, a 10 µL drop of 10% (w/v) carborundum prepared in inoculation buffer was applied to the adaxial surface of a fully expanded leaf of 5-week-old sentinel plants and 7-week-old tomato plants. Virus inoculum was applied by gently rubbing the leaf surface with a cotton swab soaked in the extract. Virus inoculum was applied by gently rubbing the leaf surface with a cotton swab soaked in the corresponding extract. For the intercropping experiment shown in Fig. 6, sentinel and tomato plants were arranged in trays in defined positions, and each plant position was assigned a unique row and column identifier. After inoculation, plants were maintained in a greenhouse under controlled conditions at 25 °C with a 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod.

RT-PCR, qRT-PCR and qPCR analyses for virus detection and quantification

Virus detection and quantification were performed by RT-PCR, quantitative RT-PCR (qRT-PCR), and quantitative PCR (qPCR). For RNA viruses, total RNA was extracted from either locally inoculated or systemically infected leaves, depending on the experiment, using the GeneJET Plant RNA Purification Kit (Thermo Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For qRT-PCR analyses, total RNA was treated with the DNA-free™ DNA Removal Kit (Invitrogen) to eliminate residual genomic DNA contamination. First-strand cDNA synthesis was carried out using 1 µg of total RNA and the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara Bio) with random hexamer primers, following the manufacturer’s protocol. RT-PCR amplifications were performed using MyTaq DNA Polymerase (Bioline, Meridian Bioscience), whereas qRT-PCR reactions were conducted using TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ (Takara Bio) according to the manufacturers’ instructions. For DNA virus quantification, total DNA was extracted using the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method to assess episomal viral DNA accumulation. Quantitative PCR was subsequently performed under the same cycling and reaction conditions used for qRT-PCR. Amplification of the housekeeping gene actin was used as an internal reference for normalization in all quantitative analyses. qRT-PCR and qPCR reactions were carried out using a QuantStudio™ 3 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) with three technical replicates per sample. For the intercropping experiment shown in Fig. 6g, RT-PCR was used as a qualitative assay to confirm PVX or TEV infection in the indicated sentinel N. benthamiana and tomato samples. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2–ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences used for the detection and quantification of all viruses are listed in Supplementary Data 2.

Protoplasts isolation

N. benthamiana protoplasts were isolated from agroinfiltrated leaves harvested 4 dpi. Protoplast isolation was performed essentially as described by Yoo et al.46, with minor modifications. Briefly, leaf tissue was cut into small strips and vacuum-infiltrated for 10 min in freshly prepared enzyme solution containing 20 mM MES, pH 5.7, 1.5% (w/v) cellulase R10, 0.4% (w/v) macerozyme R10, 0.4 M mannitol, 20 mM KCl, 10 mM CaCl₂, 0.1% BSA and 5 mM β-mercaptoethanol. Leaf strips were incubated in the dark for 3 h at room temperature with gentle agitation to release protoplasts. The enzyme/protoplast suspension was diluted with an equal volume of W5 solution containing 2 mM MES, pH 5.7, 154 mM NaCl, 125 mM CaCl₂ and 5 mM KCl, and filtered through nylon mesh to remove undigested material. Intact protoplasts were further purified from dead protoplasts and cellular debris by sucrose flotation on 20% (w/v) sucrose, washed, and resuspended in WI solution containing 4 mM MES, pH 5.7, 0.5 M mannitol and 20 mM KCl.

Image acquisition and processing

Bioluminescence pictures were taken in a dark room with a Sony Alpha ILCE-7M3 camera (DSLR camera) with a Sony SEL20F18G objective (ISO 51200, f/2.0 and 30 s of exposure). The same acquisition parameters were used to obtain the images included in Supplementary Movies 1–4. Images were acquired in uncompressed RAW (ARW) format and processed using Sony Imaging Edge Desktop software to convert them to 8-bit TIFF images for visualization and 16-bit TIFF images for quantification. Color separation for BRET-based experiments was achieved by using Permacolor dichroic ROSCO filters: R343, R346, R349 and R49. The R343, R346, and R349 filters transmit ~33%, 22%, and 25–30% of incident light, respectively, corresponding to light losses of –1.6 to –2.2 f-stops. The R49 filter transmits about 15% (occasionally estimated as low as 25%), resulting in a loss of –2.5 to –3.0 f-stops. All filters allow significant transmission in the violet (380–450 nm) and deep red (600–700 nm) regions, while strongly attenuating green to yellow wavelengths (500–580 nm). Filter details are available on ROSCO’s MyColor Selector: https://emea.rosco.com/es/node/1706. Luminescence pictures of stable plants with R346 filter displayed in Figs. 4, 5 and 6 were taken with 5 min of exposure time (ISO 51200, f/1.8). For the image shown in Fig. 6c, imaging was performed in complete darkness using a 30-s exposure time. During acquisition, a brief pulse of white light was applied solely to visualize plant positions within the setup.

Luminescence and fluorescence quantification

For the luminescence quantification of full leaves and plants displayed in Fig. 1, images were processed using Fiji software. Green channel was selected for bioluminescence quantification. Total plant tissue was defined as region of interest (ROI) for total intensity measurements, and area selected was used for normalization. Mean values are reported.

For luminescence and fluorescence measurements from leaf discs (Fig. 2), they were collected at 72 h post infiltration using a cork borer with a diameter of 0.5 cm. Two or three discs per agroinfiltrated leaf were excised. Leaf discs were transferred to a black 96-well microplate. Bioluminescence measurements were carried out with a GloMax®-Multi Detection System (Promega) by using an integration time of 10 s. DsRed2 (λ_ex max = 561 and λ_em max = 587) and PdaC1 (λ_ex max = 480 and λ_em max = 492) fluorescence activities were determined with Perkin Elmer Victor X2 Microplate Reader.

For the quantification of luminescence in plants depicted in Figs. 4, 5 and 6, image analysis was performed using FIJI. Total bioluminescence was quantified by defining the entire visible plant as the ROI in Fig. 4f and by defining ROIs at the apex of visible N. benthamiana plants from Fig. 6e. Green-to-red (G/R) ratios were calculated using total integrated intensities of the green and red channels in Fig. 4g and from apex-defined ROIs in Fig. 6h. For Figs. 4l and 5d, 10-12 square ROIs (10 × 10 pixels) were randomly selected within the indicated areas to quantify G and R channel intensities. Both individual and average ROI values are shown. For Fig. 6, dashed lines in panels d and e delineate independent experimental blocks. For Fig. 6f, luminescence values were normalized per plant by calculating a baseline as the average signal from 1 to 3 dpi, when no infection-associated increase in luminescence was detected. For each subsequent time point, the baseline value was subtracted from the measured signal and the result was divided by the baseline. Values that could not be reliably quantified because sentinel plants were partially or fully obscured by tomato plants were annotated as no data.

For subcellular localization experiments displayed in Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 2, leaf samples were collected at 72 h post infiltration. BRET efficiency calculation

Leaf samples were collected at 72 h post infiltration. Image acquisition and spectral unmixing was done in an IVIS Lumina-X5 Imaging System (Revvity) following manufacturer indications. Luminescence at 520 nm (donor channel) and 570 nm (acceptor channel) were used to determine BRET efficiency. BRET ratio was calculated using the equation described by Dimri et al.47. All luminescence images from IVIS X5 Lumina Imaging System were analysed using Revvity’s Living Imaging software and ImageJ. For Revvity’s Living Imaging software, the automatic spectral unmixing tool was employed to decompose and process both luminescence (520–570 nm) channels. From the resulting images, three ROIs of equal dimensions were defined using FIJI and used to quantify the total counts per replicate per channel for subsequent data analysis.

Computational analysis of viral bioluminescence

For the automated quantification of viral bioluminescence kinetics, a pipeline was implemented in Python 3.x with a graphical interface (PyQt5) that integrates OpenCV for image reading and pre-processing, NumPy for array handling, and Matplotlib for graph and colormap generation. The software, called LumTrack, is capable of generating time courses of bioluminescence images with or without overlay with brightfield images, generating color maps for plants using a standardized intensity scale where minimum and maximum values were calculated globally from all regions of interest across all time course images to ensure visual consistency between frames, and performing comparative analysis through the extraction of absolute luminescent intensity values in predefined regions of interest. From 16-bit TIFF files (see Image acquisition and processing section), each bioluminescence frame was automatically converted to 8-bit grayscale (0–255) by applying the standard luminance formula (Y = 0.299 R + 0.587 G + 0.114 B) with R, G, and B taken in their original 16-bit range and internally scaled to the 8-bit range. ROIs are defined as rectangles of constant dimensions that are configured directly in the interface and are extracted from each image by cropping the NumPy array according to their spatial boundaries. For quantification, in each ROI and frame, the values of all pixels in grayscale are summed, providing an absolute value of luminosity. Both processes utilized pixel value extraction from grayscale images in predefined regions of interest. To ensure visual comparability between all frames, the minimum (vmin) and maximum (vmax) intensity values are calculated globally considering all pixels from the selected ROIs throughout the entire time course. From these data, the system automatically generates temporal series of luminescent intensity, time courses, for each ROI. For the spatial representation of bioluminescence, the ‘jet’ colormap from Matplotlib is used, scaled with the global vmin/vmax values. Finally, all quantitative information is exported in CSV format with the corresponding luminescent intensity values for each analyzed ROI, and colormaps in PNG format, fully automated from the GUI. For comparison between viral treatments displayed in Fig. 5 (TEV, PVX and TMV), comparative graphs were generated representing the temporal evolution of luminescent intensity for each virus.

N. benthamiana stable transformation and selection of sentinel lines

A. tumefaciens strain LBA4404 was co-transformed with two compatible plasmids carrying HygR-Luz:ODTEV/GGSTEV:DsRed and nptII-eFBPΔLuz48 (see Supplementary Data 1). A. tumefaciens culture was grown in MGL medium to exponential phase (OD600 0.2–0.5) with plasmid-selective antibiotics and acetosyringone. Cells were then pelleted and resuspended in TY medium. Discs from five- to six-week-old N. benthamiana full-expanded leaves were cut and placed onto co-cultivation medium (MS medium, pH 5.7, supplemented with 1 mg/L 6-benzylaminopurine and 0.1 mg/L naphthalene acetic acid) for 24 h. Discs were then immersed in the A. tumefaciens suspension for 15 min and returned to co-cultivation medium for 48 h. Then, discs were transferred to selection medium (containing 100 mg/L kanamycin, 20 mg/L hygromycin and 200 mg/L carbenicillin) for shoot regeneration. Regenerated shoots were transferred to rooting medium (hormone-free) for root development. Rooted plantlets were transplanted to soil and grown in a greenhouse under 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod at 24 °C. A total of 19 GGSTEV and 25 ODTEV T0 plants were recovered. These plants were screened for the presence of both T-DNAs using the primers listed in Supplementary Data 2 and subsequently agroinfiltrated with either an empty vector or the TEV NIa-Pro construct (Supplementary Fig. 8) to assess color change. From the positive lines, three GGSTEV and three ODTEV lines exhibiting strong luminescence throughout the entire plant were selected and self-pollinated to obtain the T1 generation. T1 seeds were germinated in MS selective media containing kanamycin (100 mg/L) and hygromycin (20 mg/L) and screened for luminescence at the seedling stage. Luminescence was detected only in two GGSTEV (GGSTEV#5 and GGSTEV#11) and one ODTEV (ODTEV#15) lines. However, after two weeks, approximately half of the GGSTEV#5 seedlings lost luminescence, and this line was discarded for further experiments. Silencing was also observed in some T1 and T2 ODTEV#15 plants grown on selective media. Silenced segregants were discarded and only luminescent plants were used for subsequent experiments.

Protein extraction and microsomal fractionation

Collected plant tissue was flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, ground to a fine powder, and stored at –80 °C until use. For soluble protein extraction, tissue was homogenized in a 1:2 (w:v) ratio using phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; 137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 10 mM Na₂HPO₄, 1.8 mM KH₂PO₄, pH 7.4). The homogenate was centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected as the soluble protein fraction. For microsomal fractionation, 200 mg of frozen tissue were processed following the small-scale microsomal fractionation protocol described by Abas and Luschnig49. Briefly, frozen tissue was homogenized in ice-cold extraction buffer containing 100 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 25% (w/w; 0.81 M) sucrose, 5% (v/v) glycerol, 10 mM EDTA, pH 8.0, 10 mM EGTA, pH 8.0, 5 mM KCl and 1 mM DTT. The homogenate was clarified by centrifugation at 600 × g for 3 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected. The remaining pellet was re-extracted with extraction buffer and centrifuged again under the same conditions. The combined supernatants were then centrifuged at 21,000 × g for 90 min at 4 °C to pellet microsomal-type membranes. The pellet was washed with extraction buffer and centrifuged again at 21,000 × g for 45 min at 4 °C. Final pellets were resuspended in SDS sample buffer containing 62.5 mM Tris-HCl, pH 6.8, 2% (w/v) SDS, 10% (v/v) glycerol, 0.01% (w/v) bromophenol blue and 100 mM DTT, and incubated at 95 °C for 5 min prior to analysis.

SDS-PAGE and Western blot analysis

Proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) using MES-SDS running buffer (pH 7.3) on NuPAGE 10% Bis-Tris gels (Invitrogen) under reducing conditions. Then, proteins were transferred to a polyvinylidine difluoride (PVDF) membrane (Amersham HybondTM-P, GE healthcare) using a semi-wet blotting system (XCell IITM Blot Module, Invitrogen). Membranes were blocked with 2% enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) Prime blocking reagent (GE Healthcare) in PBS containing 0.1% (v/v) Tween-20 for 30 min with agitation at room temperature. For DsRed detection, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with 1:500 dilution of anti-DsRed primary antibody (OriGene Technologies, Cat# TA180084) on a shaker, followed by incubation with 1:10,000 dilution of horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-labelled anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (GE Healthcare, Cat# NA931) for 1 h at room temperature. Signal detection was performed using ECL Prime Western blotting detection reagent (GE Healthcare) and visualized using a Quant500 imager for chemiluminescent detection with exposure times ranging from 1 min (for soluble fraction detection) to 30 min (for microsomal fraction detection). For Fig. 6i, microsomal and soluble protein fractions were analysed separately to assess the subcellular distribution and protease-dependent processing of the Luz-DsRed reporter in PVX- and TEV-infected sentinel plants. A positive control expressing cytosolic DsRed was included for comparison in all the blots.

Statistics and reproducibility

No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. Sample sizes were chosen based on standard practice in plant transient expression and infection assays, previous experience with comparable experimental setups, and the availability of independent plants or leaves. No data were excluded from the analyses, except for specific time points in Fig. 6f where reliable luminescence quantification was not possible because some N. benthamiana sentinel plants were partially or fully obscured by tomato plants in the images. These excluded values are indicated as ND in the figure. The experiments were not randomized. The investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment. Statistical analyses were performed as indicated in the figure legends. Biological replicates correspond to independent plants or independent agroinfiltrated leaves, as specified in each legend, whereas technical replicates correspond to repeated measurements or samples derived from the same biological unit. Western blot and RT-PCR analyses were used as qualitative molecular assays to assess Luz:DsRed processing and viral infection status, respectively, and no statistical analysis was applied to these data. Across the study, western blots were performed on 18 independent biological samples derived from 15 individual sentinel plants: 10 TEV-infected plants and 5 specificity controls infected with other viruses (2 TVMV, 1 TMV and 2 PVX). These samples were also analysed by RT-PCR. For Fig. 6, RT-PCR analyses comprised 15 TEV-infected and 5 PVX-infected independent plants, including samples already analysed by western blot and 12 additional biological replicates. The molecular analyses shown in Figs. 4, 5 and 6 were consistent across independent biological samples. The experiments shown in Figs. 4 and 5 were each repeated twice with additional independent plants from the same sentinel line, with comparable results. Figure 6 was not independently repeated, but included biological replication within the experiment. Where applicable, statistical analyses were performed as indicated in the corresponding figure legends and in this section. Statistical tests were two-sided unless otherwise stated. One-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was used for the experiments specified in the legends. P values ≤ 0.05 were considered significant. Graphs and statistical analyses were generated using GraphPad Prism. For Fig. 2d, statistical analyses were performed using two-tailed unpaired t-tests. For the DsRed/Luz panel, P values for significant comparisons were: Luz vs EV, P < 0.0001; DsRed:Luz vs EV, P < 0.0001; Luz:DsRed vs EV, P < 0.0001; and DsRed:Luz vs Luz, P = 0.0492. For the PdaC1/Luz panel, P values for significant comparisons were: Luz vs EV, P < 0.0001; PdaC1:Luz vs EV, P < 0.0001; Luz:PdaC1 vs EV, P < 0.0001; PdaC1:Luz vs Luz, P = 0.0007; and Luz:PdaC1 vs Luz, P = 0.0029. For Fig. 2f, statistical analyses were performed using two-tailed unpaired t-tests. For the DsRed fluorescence panel, P values for significant comparisons were: Luz:DsRed vs EV, P < 0.0001; Luz:DsRed vs Luz, P < 0.0001; and Luz:DsRed vs DsRed:Luz, P < 0.0001. For the PdaC1 fluorescence panel, P values for significant comparisons were: Luz:PdaC1 vs EV, P < 0.0001; Luz:PdaC1 vs Luz, P < 0.0001; and Luz:PdaC1 vs PdaC1:Luz, P < 0.0001. For Fig. 2j, statistical analyses were performed using two-tailed unpaired t-tests. P values for significant comparisons were: GGS vs Acr, P = 0.0437; Luz vs Acr, P = 0.0268; GGS vs GS3x, P = 0.0331; Luz vs GS3x, P = 0.0083; Luz vs GS1x, P = 0.0101; Luz vs GGS, P = 0.0233; and Luz vs OD, P = 0.0242. For Fig. 3c, statistical analyses were performed using two-tailed unpaired t-tests. For GS1xTEV, P values for significant comparisons were: +TEVPro vs −TEVPro, P < 0.0001; Luz vs −TEVPro, P < 0.0001; and Luz vs +TEVPro, P < 0.0001. For ODTEV, P values for significant comparisons were: +TEVPro vs −TEVPro, P < 0.0001; Luz vs −TEVPro, P < 0.0001; and Luz vs +TEVPro, P = 0.0083. For GGSTEV, P values for significant comparisons were: +TEVPro vs −TEVPro, P < 0.0001; and Luz vs −TEVPro, P < 0.0001. The comparison Luz vs +TEVPro for GGSTEV was not significant, P = 0.4698. For Fig. 3e, statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. The overall ANOVA was significant, F(3,32) = 245.2, P < 0.0001. Adjusted P values for Tukey’s test were: Luz vs TVMV, P < 0.0001; Luz vs TEV, P < 0.0001; Luz vs Luz:DsRed, P < 0.0001; TVMV vs TEV, P = 0.0043; TVMV vs Luz:DsRed, P = 0.9973; and TEV vs Luz:DsRed, P = 0.0073. For Figs. 4l and 5d, no inferential statistical analysis was performed because the quantified ROIs correspond to technical replicates within the same biological sample or image, rather than independent biological replicates. These data are therefore reported descriptively as mean values ± SD, and no P values are provided.
