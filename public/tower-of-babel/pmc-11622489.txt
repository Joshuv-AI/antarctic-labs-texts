Treatment outcome of community acquired pneumonia among pediatric patients admitted to pediatrics wards at university of Gondar comprehensive and specialized hospital, northwest Ethiopia: a Retrospective cohort study
Authors: Eshete Tadege Belay, Hiluf Maldey Hayelom, Boressa Adugna, Nigussie Birhan, Kifle Asmamaw
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPneumonia is inflammation of the lung parenchyma and is a substantial cause of childhood morbidity and mortality in developing countries. Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) is a type of pneumonia termed when patients who develop pneumonia in the outpatient setting and have not been in any health care facilities within 90days.ObjectiveThe objective of this study was to determine treatment outcome of community acquired pneumonia among hospitalized pediatric patients.MethodA retrospective cohort study was conducted from April 15, 2019 to July 15, 2019 and included patients who were admitted and hospitalized in pediatrics wards from September 1, 2015 to March 30, 2019. The study included pediatric age groups between one month and fifteen years old. Study Participants were selected based on the diagnosis of Community acquired pneumonia. Systematic sampling technique was used. All the statistical data were carried out using Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS 20) and descriptive statistics were presented using means with standard deviation and percentages. Binary logistic regression model was fitted to measure the association between independent and dependent variables including duration of signs and symptoms. 95% Confidence interval was used. Statistically significant at P < 0.05.ResultsA total of 385 patients with Community Acquired Pneumonia were included in this study of whom 368(95.65%) were discharged and 17(4.4%) of patients were dead. Drug therapy change (AOR 20.308(3.666-112.501), P = 0.001), Prescribing and taking of large number of drugs (above 5 drugs) (AOR 0.067, CI (0.015–0.313), P = 0.001), Loss of appetite (AOR 38.641, CI (5.454-273.769), P = 0.000), and Blood transfusion (AOR 10.514, CI (1.752–63.113), P = 0.01) have significant association with the treatment outcome of death.ConclusionPoor treatment outcome (death) was accounted for 4.4% of the pediatric patients hospitalized with community acquired pneumonia in this study setting. Based on the findings of this study Community acquired pneumonia is still a cause of substantial mortality of children under 15years old while on standardized treatment strategies. Antibiotic drug therapy change, taking increased number of drugs, loss of appetite and blood transfusion are the factors that contribute to this poor treatment outcome of CAP among children under 15years old.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-024-05280-2.

Background

Pneumonia is inflammation of the lung parenchyma and is a substantial cause of childhood morbidity and mortality in developing countries. It is a severe form of acute lower respiratory tract infection that specifically affects the lungs [1, 2].

Recent well-designed epidemiologic studies in developed countries indicated pneumonia is the leading cause of death in children aged < 5 years worldwide and when we see the causative agents respiratory viruses are detected in 30–70%, atypical bacteria in 7–17%, and pyogenic bacteria in 2–8% of children hospitalized with community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) [3, 4].

Risk factors for fatal pneumonia include poor socioeconomic status such as (lack of proper education of parents, incomplete immunization schemes and malnutrition), young age of the child, late hospitalization with cyanosis, altered sensorium, grunting, associated chest in drawing, hepatomegaly, inability to drink, associated loose stools or heart disease, anemia, rickets, lack of breast feeding and inadequate treatment [2, 5].

Based on pocket book of pediatrics WHO 2013 guide line and revised WHO classification and treatment of childhood pneumonia 2014, pneumonia is classified as severe or non-severe pneumonia and no pneumonia (common cold) on the basis of clinical features [6–8].

Sever pneumonia is characterized by cough or difficulty in breathing, plus at least one from central cyanosis or oxygen saturation < 90% on pulse oxymetry, severe respiratory distress (e.g. grunting, very severe chest in drawing), signs of pneumonia with a general danger sign such as inability to breastfeed or drink, lethargy or unconscious, convulsions and pneumonia/non-sever pneumonia is characterized by fast breathing ≥ 50 breaths/min in a child aged 2–11 months, ≥ 40 breaths/min in a child aged 1–5 years and Chest in drawing and no pneumonia is characterized by no signs of pneumonia or severe pneumonia and The management of pneumonia is based on the classification [6].

Ampicilline or penicilline G should be administered to the fully immunized infant or school-aged child admitted to a hospital ward with CAP when local epidemiologic data document lack of substantial high-level penicillin resistance for invasive S. pneumonia and third-generation parenteral cephalosporin (Ceftriaxone or Cefotaxime) should be prescribed for hospitalized infants and children who are not fully immunized [9]. Intravenous Ampicilline (or Benzylpenicillin) and Gentamicine (Ampicilline 50 mg/kg or Benzylpenicillin 50 000 U/kg IM or IV every 6 h for at least 5 days and Gentamicin 7.5 mg/kg IM or IV once a day for at least 5 days. If the child does not show signs of improvement within 48 h and staphylococcal pneumonia is suspected, switch to Gentamicin 7.5 mg/kg IM or IV once a day and Cloxacillin 50 mg/kg IM or IV every 6 h. Use Ceftriaxone (80 mg/kg IM or IV once daily) in cases of failure of first line treatment [6].

Hence, treatment outcome of pneumonia is still poor. Studies had showed that there are high rates of complications and death. Though pneumonia is a treatable and preventable infectious disease, it is the number one infectious killer of children globally, contributing to the death of 1.1 million (18%) of children under the age of five every year worldwide. It is a major cause of global morbidity with an estimated 156 million episodes and 14.9 million hospitalizations per year in pediatrics [10, 11].

The vast majority of those killed by pneumonia are poor and living in low and middle income countries. Globally 880,000 children under five died of pneumonia in 2016, that’s almost two fatalities every minute of every day more than diarrhea, malaria and measles combined. Most of the deaths happened in South Asia and sub-Saharan Africa [12].

In Ethiopia pneumonia is responsible for about 21% of all deaths in children aged less than 5 years and the number of new clinical cases of pneumonia per year is estimated to be 35% which puts the country among the 15 highest mortality countries due to pneumonia in the world. In line with this WHO 2018 Pneumonia and Diarrhea reports Ethiopia ranks fifth among the 15 top countries and approximately 4 million cases and 21% childhood pneumonia deaths are occurring in Ethiopia annually and majority of these children have limited access to quality facility based clinical care [13].

Based on 2018 report, in Ethiopia pneumonia kills 30,733(16%) of all under five years children only in 2016 which means there were a mortality rate of four children every hour. In 2017 there were 59per 1000 live births under five mortality rate and 995,457children aged 12–23 months were not fully immunized [12]. In Ethiopia Antibiotics which could prevent 70% of all pneumonia deaths are frequently not accessible and often unavailable. The access of effective antibiotic treatment is < 10% [13].

There are different patient and non-patient related factors that affect treatment outcome of pneumonia which include patient related factors like early age, late presentation of the child which may due to poor parents’/care givers knowledge/awareness about hospital disease management, economic impact, long distance residency and lack of transportation, poor knowledge about preventive approaches like personal hygiene and vaccination, poor patient and parents/care givers adherence to the drugs and treatment process as a whole. Disease factors like pneumonia severity and health care related factors like inappropriate and ineffective drug selection, unavailability of effective medicines [2, 14, 15].

Most of the previous studies on childhood pneumonia in Ethiopia have focused on risk factors for the acquisition of and recurrence of pneumonia on under 5years children and studied pneumonia as a general. But this study attempted to assess treatment outcome and identifies factors that associated with poor treatment outcome among pediatric patients age between one month and 15 years old hospitalized with CAP and treated with current treatment modalities.

Therefore this study will be important to determine the treatment outcome, to identify risk factors for poor outcome and determining their impact and to determine current community acquired pneumonia treatment modalities associated with poor treatment outcome of Community Acquired Pneumonia [2, 16, 17].

Methodology

Study setting and period

Study setting

The study was conducted at Pediatrics wards of University of Gondar Comprehensive and Specialized Hospital (UGCSH), Northwest Ethiopia. It is a government comprehensive and specialized hospital with more than 500 beds serving for a population of about 5 million. It has a range of specialties, including internal medicine, pediatrics, surgery, gynecology, psychiatry, HIV care, and an outpatient clinic.

University of Gondar Comprehensive and Specialized Hospital Pediatrics ward has one emergency unit and two in patients, ward A and ward B. Ward B is served for pediatric patients admitted with SAM and oncology and Ward A for pediatric patients’ admitted with the rest pediatric diseases. Ward A has 39 beds and ward B with 31 beds. There is also Pediatric Intensive Care Unit which accommodates only 4 beds. Emergency unit has 21 beds and patients can stay there for more than 24 h.

Study period

Data was taken from April 15, 2019 to July 15, 2019 from medical records of patients who were hospitalized from September 01, 2015 to March 30, 2019 retrospectively.

Study design

A Retrospective cohort study design was used.

Population

Source population

All pediatric patients who were admitted to pediatrics wards and hospitalized with the diagnosis of community acquired pneumonia.

Study population

All pediatric patients who were admitted to pediatrics wards and hospitalized with the diagnosis of community acquired pneumonia from September 1, 2015 to March, 30, 2019.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

All pediatric patients age 1month to fifteen years old who were admitted to pediatrics wards and hospitalized with the diagnosis of community acquired pneumonia from September 1, 2015 to March 30, 2019.

Patients who took treatment (drug and non-drug treatments) for community acquired pneumonia at least for 48 h.

Exclusion criteria

Patients whose outcome is not recorded or not known.

Patients who transferred to other health facilities irrespective of reasons for transfer.

Patients with incomplete records of independent variables like age, sex, drug therapy etc.

Sample size determination and sampling technique

Sample size determination

The required sample size was calculated by using the principle of number of cases/charts per variable. This study has 25 independent variables from which the 10 variables have lists or classifications. Considering these variables sample size was determined by taking 15-16charts per variable. This means for the ten variables with lists 16 charts per variable and for the rest 15 variables 15charts per variable was used. It gives 160 plus 225 which is a total of 385 charts.

Sampling technique

Systematic sampling technique was used. The total number of patients admitted to the hospital during the study period (record of patients hospitalized from September 01, 2015 to March 30, 2019) was recorded from the hospital’s patients’ log book with the patients’ card number, address and admission and discharge dates for the simplicity of contacting patients’/patient care givers and to exclude patients who took treatment less than two days. Excluding patients treated for less than two days record of total number of patients admitted was 4000. Obtaining this (4000) K is calculated by N/n (4000/385) = > 10, one number was selected by lottery from 1 to 10 numbers. And therefore every 10 patient’s chart was taken by identifying numbers from the record of the log book. For patients with recurrent admissions only the last encounter was considered and the recent data was taken if the chance of selecting in to the sample is occurred but number of admissions, past medical history and past medication history were recorded from the previous admissions. For incomplete documents the next patient’s document was taken and K was updated accordingly.

Study variables

Independent variables

Socio-demographic characteristics such as age, sex, residence and clinical conditions of pneumonia such as very sever, sever, pneumonia, duration of illness before admission, signs and symptoms at admission, duration of signs and symptoms, co-morbid conditions, vaccination status, nutritional status, drug and non-drug treatments, length of hospital stay and laboratory abnormalities.

Dependent variable

Treatment outcome.

Ethical considerations

Written Informed consent for inclusion in the study and publication of their details was provided by the Parents or Legal Guardians of all the study participants before their children participated in the study after discussed on the purpose and relevance of the study, the contents of the questioner and reached an agreement.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Helsinki declaration and was conducted after obtaining ethical clearance from the ethics review committee of Department of Clinical Pharmacy, school of pharmacy, college of medicine and health sciences University of Gondar (Ref. no Sops// 042//2019). A written permission was obtained from the administrative office (the Hospital clinical director) of University of Gondar Comprehensive Specialized Hospital and wrote a letter of permission to head of the pediatric ward and Chart room of University of Gondar Comprehensive Specialized Hospital before starting of conducting data collection.

Data quality control technique

There were two data collectors and one supervisor who were blinded to the study hypothesis and to the dependent variable. They were trained intensively on contents of questionnaire, data collection methods and ethical concerns. After obtaining ethical approval and before data collection was started the prepared questionnaire was pre-tested in 10% (39 patient medical documents) of the calculated sample size from pediatric patients who were hospitalized for community acquired pneumonia in the hospital and discharged before August 30, 2015 to check the completeness and quality of the questionnaire and data collection methods. The collected data for pretesting of the questionnaire is not included in the study. Collected data were checked daily by the supervisor for completeness.

Data collection process

Patients’ medical records were traced from patients log book and patients’ medical charts were drawn from the chart room. Patient medical chart were reviewed and data were extracted starting from the date of admission until patient was discharged or dead step by step. A structured format that contains questions for data extraction was used to address the events that were occurred in each participant during hospitalization. The questions were containing socio-demographic variables such as age, gender, residence. Patient disease status like clinical features, duration of illness before admission, diagnosis including pneumonia and co-morbid diseases, drug and non-drug treatments the patient received, patients immunization status, clinical investigations and Chest x- ray, pneumonia severity at admission, nutritional status, previous admissions, feeding status, intravenous fluid use, history of blood transfusion and type of outcome.

Data entry and analysis

After completion of each day data collection it was reviewed, cleaned, filtered and organized. Then it was entered into and analyzed using Statistical Package for Social science (SPSS version 20) software. Descriptive statistics such as frequency (%), mean and SD were calculated. Frequency (%) calculated for all variables and mean and SD were calculated for age, duration of illness from onset to admission, duration of signs and symptoms, number of hospital admissions, total number of drugs prescribed and taken, length of hospital stay and date of starting improvement. Binary logistic regression model was fitted (Husmer-lemeshow goodness of fit, 95% CI) to measure the strength of association between independent and dependent variables. Back ward likelihood ratio (LR) were used for multivariable regression analysis, P-value less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Operational definitions

Treatment outcome

The result obtained after the treatment. It might be improved or died [18].

Good treatment outcome (improved, discharged)

Represents discharge from the hospital on the grounds of clinical improvement [18].

Poor treatment outcome (death)

defined as the occurrence of death while on treatment [18].

Treatment

Include pharmacologic and non-pharmacologic therapies given to the patient during hospitalization [4, 9].

Standard treatment strategy

A treatment strategy in the treatment of Community acquired pneumonia for pediatric patients hospitalized with community acquired pneumonia [4, 9].

Loss of appetite

or anorexia is a condition of absence of hunger in patients and can occur due to a wide variety of reasons such as age, acute or chronic disease conditions, and associated medications [19].

Treatment for 2days (at least 48 h): Since it is treatment outcome not outcome of the disease it needs time to see the effect of the treatment (antibiotics need time to give response). In the treatment of CAP most signs and symptoms relive within 48 h of starting treatment with antibiotics [4].

Community-Acquired Pneumonia (CAP)

is pneumonia that occurs within 48 h of hospital admission or is present on admission to the hospital [20].

Drug therapy change

Antibiotic drug therapy change during treatment of the patient regardless of recommendations may be due to the suspecting of choosing unnecessary therapy, needing additional therapy or ineffective therapy.

Results

Demographic characteristic of patients

A total of 385 patients (Children) with community acquired pneumonia were included in this study. Out of the 385 patients 355(92.2%) were age < 2 years. Two hundred forty eight patients (64.4%) were lived in urban and 137(35.6%) lived in rural areas. Male patients were accounted for 233(60.5%) with male to female ratio of 1.53:1. One hundred eighty three (47.5%) of patients came to health care facility within 3 days and 23(6%) of children come and received hospital care after one week.

Treatment outcome

Out of the 385 children admitted and hospitalized with CAP 17 (4.4%) pediatric patients died due to community acquired pneumonia, while 368 (95.6%) were cured and discharged. Fourteen of the 17 deaths occurred among children less than 2 years of age but the rest above 5years old Fourteen (3.6%), COR (95% CI), 0.079(0.018–0.348)) of the deaths occurred due to sever pneumonia and the remaining three deaths were due to very sever pneumonia. Eleven of the total deaths, were rural residents COR (95%) 0.284 (0.103–0.786) and 9 of the total deaths were from those admitted after more than 8 days of illness before admitting to the hospital. From the total of 17 deaths 16 deaths were from those with no previous admissions COR (95% CI) (0.43 (0.070–4.080)) whereas 11 of the deaths were from those with co- morbid disease. Nine of the deaths from the 17 deaths were not vaccinated at all and with normal nutritional status whereas 5 were from those with severe acute malnutrition. Five deaths occurred from the 13 patients transfused with blood and 13 deaths from 32 patients and 11 deaths from 24 patients for whom drug therapy change was made and who took more than 5 drugs respectively (Table 1).

Discussion

Pneumonia remains one of the most common cause of respiratory failure and infectious cause of death in children. Childhood pneumonia and associated severe clinical manifestations are a result of the complex interaction between the host, the pathogen, and environmental risk factors. The overall aim of treating pneumonia is to prevent recurrence, complications and death.

The overall treatment outcome of this study revealed that 368(95.6%) of the patients were improved by the treatment they were received and 17(4.4%) were dead while receiving treatment. Based on this study 6(1.6%) of patients were dead from community acquired pneumonia only and case fatality of community acquired pneumonia is (1.6%) which is lower than a study done in Addis Ababa (2.37%) [13] and a multihospital, retrospective cohort study done in Kenya (5%) [21].

From the patients who were dead, 11(2.9%) of patients were dead from those with co-morbid disease, but there is no significant association between death and presence of co-morbid disease (P = 0.344), this is in contrast to another study done in Tanzania (P = 0.000) [22]. Although it may differed by type of co-morbid disease, the type of co-morbid disease is also not significantly associated with the treatment outcome of death (P = 0.141).

In this study ceftriaxone based drug treatment modalities is administered more frequently 190(49.4%) followed by Crystaline penicillin based drug treatment modalities 158(41%). This is the same to a study done in Ethiopia (Nekemt) [2]. But there is no significant association between type of drug treatment modalities and treatment outcome of death (P = 0.132). This shows that all the antibiotic drug treatment modalities such as ceftriaxone based, Crystaline penicillin based and Ampicillin plus gentamycin based drugs have similar effectiveness of improved treatment outcome.

In this study drug therapy change and total number of drugs per prescription were significantly associated with higher rates of death. Univariable and multivariable analysis results shows that drug therapy change from one antibiotic drug to another antibiotic drug before the optimal duration of therapy (at least for 48 h) is one risk factor for death of a child with community acquired pneumonia (COR: 34.125 CI: 10.508-110.817, P = 0.000) and (AOR: 20.308 CI: 3.666-112.501, P = 0.001) [6, 7]. It may be due to the reason that changing of one drug to another drug before having full therapeutic duration can increase bacterial tolerance, resistance and the pathogenicity of the bacteria. So this makes the bacteria resistant for effective regimens and hindered the curability of the disease [23].

On univariable regression analysis using two and less than two drugs is significantly associated with improved treatment outcome. Whereas using more than two drugs including antibiotics and non-antibiotics concomitantly is significantly associated with treatment outcome of death.

on multivariable analysis using more than five drugs with different classes such as antibiotic and non-antibiotic drugs concomitantly per prescription is significantly associated with treatment outcome of death (AOR :0.067, CI (0.015–0.313), P = 0.001)). Based on this study it has 6.7% decreased risk/ Protective effect for childhood mortality due to community acquired pneumonia. This may be due to the reason that having large number of drugs (> 5drugs per patient) may interact to each other pharmacokinetically and increase the therapeutic effect of one to another or taking different classes of drugs simultaneously may result in increased pharmacodynamic mechanism of actions in the treatment of CAP and prevent the patient from complications. This is in contrast to studies that show using multiple drugs can increase toxicity and side effects while decreased effectiveness due to interaction and side effects [24].

Based on results of this study loss of appetite and history of blood transfusion are significantly associated with treatment outcome of death. Patients who have loss of appetite were significantly associated with high rate of poor treatment outcome (death) (AOR 38.641, CI (5.454-273.769), P = 0.000). Loss of appetite which is also called anorexia in medical parlance is common symptom of pneumonia. The exact cause contributing loss of appetite is not known with certainty, but it may be believed to be due to the condition itself or due to treatment with antibiotics which affects the taste of food. Persons suffering from pneumonia losses desire to eat food. As a result patient suffers from loss of weight and weakened immune system which intern decrease disease tolerance and increase vulnerability of the patient for poor treatment outcome.

History of blood transfusion was also significantly associated with high rate of death for patients with pneumonia (AOR 10.514, CI (1.752–63.113), P = 0.01). This is the same to a study done in adults which showed that a lower transfusion trigger (Hb trigger category ≤ 7 g/dL) before transfusion is significantly associated with higher in-hospital mortality in patients hospitalized with pneumonia requiring RBC transfusion. It may be due to transfusion reactions which are the most frequent adverse effects associated with the administration of blood components [25].

Another study done in pediatrics with acute lung injury due to pneumonia, aspiration, sepsis, near drowning, cardiac disease, and others showed patients who received a blood product transfusion had a higher inpatient mortality independent of the severity of illness and fewer ventilator-free days [26].

The possible mechanism is passive transfusion of donor antibodies (to human leukocyte antigen class I or II antigens, granulocytes or monocytes), lipids, or cytokines may modify the host response, as hypothesized in transfusion-related acute lung injury (TRALI). In addition to this, fluid overload, bacterial contamination, and transfusion reactions are potential effects associated with transfusion that may account in part for the adverse outcomes in patients who receive a transfusion.

Limitations of the study

This study was a retrospective study done on patient medical records which depends on patient medical records, which are not designed for research. The other limitation is the reliance on clinical diagnosis. Most of the patients were diagnosed through physicians experience relaying on complete blood count results and signs and symptoms of patients. In this study there is no patient for whom laboratory detection of a microorganism causing the infection is done. The other limitation is the potential for misclassification of the type of pneumonia. This study includes treatment outcomes of patients treated with in 4-years and seven months period, which is a short period to generalized 100%. Therefore undergoing a study which covers longer years of period with large sample size is beneficial for future researchers.

Conclusion and recommendations

Poor treatment outcome (death) was accounted for 4.4% of the pediatric patients admitted with community acquired pneumonia in this study setting. Based on the findings of this study community acquired pneumonia is still a cause of substantial mortality of children under 15years old while there are appropriate and standardized treatment strategies. Antibiotic drug therapy change, taking increased number of drugs, loss of appetite and blood transfusion are the factors that contribute to this poor treatment outcome of CAP among children under 15years old. Early identification of high-risk children for loss of appetite and taking appropriate measures like adequate parenteral feeding and using appetite inducers such as fruits and multivitamins is important. For patients with anemia instead of transfusing with blood using alternative drugs and adequate feeding with iron containing food components is recommended. In treating patients with CAP instead of changing of antibiotics during treatment and prescribing and giving of more drugs it is better to select and prescribe appropriate medications based on reasonable laboratory and chest x-ray findings to reduce such poor treatment outcomes. Physicians should assess their work to identify gaps and took appropriate measures to fill these gaps. Great consideration should be given for pharmacists to work with physicians on the hospital wards in order to detect poly pharmacies and other drug therapy problems and to assess and resolve shortage of essential drugs availability.
