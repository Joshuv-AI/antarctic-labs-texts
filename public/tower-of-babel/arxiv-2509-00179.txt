Playing Markov Games Without Observing Payoffs
Daniel Ablin, Alon Cohen

Abstract
Optimization under uncertainty is a fundamental problem in learning and decision-making, particularly in multi-agent systems. Previously, Feldman et al. 2010 demonstrated the ability to efficiently compete in repeated symmetric two-player matrix games without observing payoffs, as long as the opponent’s actions are observed. In this paper, we introduce and formalize a new class of zero-sum symmetric Markov games, which extends the notion of symmetry from matrix games to the Markovian setting. We show that even without observing payoffs, a player who knows the transition dynamics and observes only the opponent’s sequence of actions can still compete against an adversary who may have complete knowledge of the game. We formalize three distinct notions of symmetry in this setting and show that, under these conditions, the learning problem can be reduced to an instance of online learning, enabling the player to asymptotically match the return of the opponent despite lacking payoff observations. Our algorithms apply to both matrix and Markov games, and run in polynomial time with respect to the size of the game and the number of episodes. Our work broadens the class of games in which robust learning is possible under severe informational disadvantage and deepens the connection between online learning and adversarial game theory.



1
Introduction

In unfamiliar or competitive settings, imitation often emerges as a natural survival tactic.
In rapidly evolving online markets, where advertising tactics can shift daily, newer companies frequently align their campaigns with those of more seasoned competitors.
Directly assessing the success of a marketing effort is challenging, so inexperienced firms may gain by replicating the strategies of those with greater market knowledge.
Lacking the time or budget for extensive research, these newcomers must act decisively to stay competitive against entrenched players. However, imitation carries significant risk: executed well, it can lead to outsize gains with minimal investment; executed poorly, it can result in outcomes worse than random chance.
This problem is even greater if one consider the scenario where company uses a long range advertising campaign where each advertising strongly dependent on previous tactics.
To study this behavior, we frame it as a strategic interaction where the objectives of the competitor may conflict with your own. More specifically a zero-sum symmetric game.

Previously,
Feldman et al. 2010
studied repeated play of finite zero-sum symmetric matrix games.
They considered a scenario where the learner has no information about the game and does not observe any payoff.
The learner is only aware of the available actions and observe the actions played by the adversary.
On the other hand, the adversary may have complete prior knowledge of the payoff matrix.
They demonstrated a simple
copycat strategy
that guarantees, against any opponent’s strategy:

\mathbb{E}[|\sum_{t=1}^{T}u(i_{t},j_{t})|]=O(n\sqrt{T})
,
where
T
is the number of rounds,
u
is the payoff function,
i_{t},j_{t}
are the actions played by the learner and adversary on round
t
, respectively, and
n
is the number of actions.

In our work we ask the following natural question: can the
copycat strategy
of
Feldman et al. 2010
be extended to Markov games, where the current state depends on the history of actions?
In such setting, how can a learner mimic an adversary while ensuring sublinear payoff growth?
Our first challenge is to define what constitutes a zero-sum symmetric Markov game, for which we identify three natural notions of symmetry.
The first is
per-state symmetric games
(SSG), where each state corresponds to a symmetric zero-sum matrix game.
The second,
symmetry with respect to Markov policies
(MSG), captures symmetry between any two Markov policies.
The third,
symmetry with respect to history-dependent policies
(HSG), generalizes this further to symmetry between any pair of history-dependent policies.

We aim to design a learning algorithm that works across all three settings.
Unlike in symmetric matrix games, the learner cannot simply copy the opponent’s actions, and must take into account the effect actions have over state transitions.
Nevertheless, we observe that the original copycat problem can be framed as an instance of Blackwell’s approachability, and as such as a form of Online Convex Optimization (OCO), which we generalize to Markov games.
A key technical challenge in this formulation is the projection step required in OCO algorithms.
In the SSG setting, we show that projection reduces to solving a set of independent projections for each matrix game.
In the MSG setting, we develop a method to construct a set of vectors that span the orthogonal complement to the space of MSG payoff functions, based on a careful analysis of the symmetry structure.
This allows for an efficient projection procedure.
Finally, we observe that the HSG assumption is stronger than it may first appear.
In fact, we prove that under HSG, the problem reduces to a single zero-sum symmetric matrix game, enabling the use of a matrix-game copycat strategy.

Our algorithms are efficient and run in polynomial time.
In fact, the symmetry assumptions circumvent known computational hardness results for learning Markov games against adaptive adversaries (see, e.g.,
Liu et al. 2022
).
To the best of our knowledge, our results are the first to show tractable learning in Markov games without any assumptions limiting the opponent, and without additional information received by the learner.

1.1
Related work

Blackwell Approachability.

Blackwell Approachability is an extension of Von Neumann’s minimax theorem
(
Von Neumann 1928
)
to vector-valued payoffs.
The objective is to ensure, in repeated play, that the average payoff vector converges to a desirable convex target set
K
.
Unlike in the scalar case, fixed (oblivious) strategies may fail to guarantee convergence.
Blackwell’s celebrated
Approachability Theorem

(
Blackwell 1956
)
shows that in the repeated game setting, an adaptive strategy that reacts to the opponent’s play can still guarantee convergence to
K
.

Prior work by
Abernethy et al. 2011
;
Shimkin 2016
show that Blackwell Approachability and Online Convex Optimization, are algorithmically equivalent in the context of repeated games.
Specifically, any algorithm that achieves approachability can be transformed into an online algorithm with low regret, and vice versa.

Symmetric Markov Games.

Several recent papers have studied how to formalize symmetry in Markov decision processes (MDPs) and Markov games.

Zinkevich and Balch 2001
introduce a notion of symmetry in MDPs and show how it can be exploited for more effective learning in single-agent as well as multi-agent systems.
In their formulation, two states are symmetric if they have symmetric actions, and two actions are symmetric if they lead to symmetric outcomes.
They further extend this idea to symmetric MDPs, requiring both the payoff and transition functions to be symmetric.

Zinkevich 2006
broaden this perspective to settings involving multiple agents.

Another line of work formulates symmetry at the level of payoffs in Markov games.
Flesch et al. 2013
analyze a two-player Markov game in which the payoff functions are symmetric: if the players’ actions at a given state are exchanged, then their payoffs are swapped as well. A similar approach is taken by
Yongacoglu et al. 2023
, who consider a multi-player setting and require invariance under arbitrary permutations of the action sets.
Both frameworks are flexible enough to capture zero-sum games.
A key limitation, however, is that they assume transition dynamics are insensitive to the ordering of players’ actions, i.e., swapping the players does not alter the state dynamics, which restricts the generality of the resulting games.

Learning in Markov games.

Markov games pose significant theoretical and algorithmic challenges, particularly in adversarial and partially observable settings.
Prior work identified strong hardness results for core learning objectives in this domain.
For instance,
Bai et al. 2020
prove that computing a best response policy in zero-sum Markov games when facing an adversarial opponent is at least as difficult as the well-known parity with noise problem, which is widely believed to be computationally intractable.

A closely related special case that has been extensively studied is that of MDPs with known transitions but adversarial or unknown rewards. Such an MDP can be interpreted as a Markov game in which the adversary acts as a second player whose “actions” either modify the state payoffs or, equivalently, redirect transitions to payoff-altered versions of the next state. This line of research
(
Even-Dar et al. 2009
;
Zimin and Neu 2013
;
Jin and Luo 2020
)
primarily focuses on scenarios where the payoff function may vary over time.
In a related line of work,
Tian et al. 2021
;
Liu et al. 2022
establish tight lower bounds on regret in the online learning setting, showing that no algorithm can achieve better than
\Omega(\min\{\sqrt{2^{H}T},T\})
regret in episodic zero-sum Markov games with unknown transitions, even when the adversary is limited to using Markov strategies.

Despite these limitations, recent advances have uncovered tractable settings under more cooperative assumptions. For example,
Erez et al. 2023
;
Cai et al. 2024
;
Cui et al. 2023
;
Yang and Ma 2022
;
Jin et al. 2021
design decentralized algorithms for general-sum Markov games that achieve sublinear regret, assuming all players employ the same proposed learning rule.
Their approach requires no communication between agents and guarantees convergence to a (coarse) correlated equilibrium.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Markov games

A two-player zero-sum finite horizon Markov game is defined by the quintuple
(S,A,H,P,u)
, where
H\geq 1
is the time horizon of the game;
S
is a finite set of states partitioned as
S=\bigsqcup_{h=1}^{H}S_{h}
, where
S_{1}=\{s_{1}\}
;
A=\{1,\dots,n\}
is a finite set of actions available to both players;
P(\cdot\mid s_{h},a^{1},a^{2})\in\Delta(S_{h+1})
is the probability distribution over the next state when the players 1 and 2 take actions
a^{1}
and
a^{2}
respectively at state
s_{h}\in S_{h}
.
Finally,
u:S\times A\times A\rightarrow[-1,1]
is the payoff function for player 1.
As the game is zero-sum,
-u
is the payoff function for player 2.

At every time step
h=1,\dots,H
, the players observe the current state
s_{h}
, then choose actions
a^{1}_{h},a^{2}_{h}
respectively. The players may choose their actions independently at random conditioned on the current state.
Then player 1 gains
u(s_{h},a^{1}_{h},a^{2}_{h})
and player 2 gains
-u(s_{h},a^{1}_{h},a^{2}_{h})
, and the game transitions to the next state
s_{h+1}
drawn from
P(\cdot\mid s_{h},a^{1}_{h},a^{2}_{h})
.
The game ends after time step
H
.

A history
z_{h}=(s_{1},a_{1}^{1},a_{1}^{2},\dots,s_{h-1},a_{h-1}^{1},a_{h-1}^{2},s_{h})
is the sequence of states and actions generated by the players up to time
h
. We denote by
Z_{h}
the set of histories up to time
h
.
A (history-dependent) policy for player
i
is a function
\pi^{i}:Z_{h}\mapsto\Delta(A)
that maps each history
z_{h}
to a probability distribution from which player
i
draws their action when observing
z_{h}
.
A policy is Markov if it is a function of only the current state, namely
\pi^{i}:S\mapsto\Delta(A)
.

We also introduce the concept of the
value function
, which captures the expected cumulative payoff from a given state onward and will be fundamental in defining optimality and proving performance guarantees.
For policies
\pi^{1},\pi^{2}
of the two players, time step
h
and state
s\in S_{h}
, the
value function
is defined as:

\displaystyle V^{\pi^{1},\pi^{2}}(s)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}^{\pi^{1},\pi^{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\sum_{h^{\prime}=h}^{H}u(s_{h^{\prime}},a_{h^{\prime}}^{1},a_{h^{\prime}}^{2})~\bigg|~s_{h}=s}\right]}}.

where the expectation is taken over the randomness in the actions taken by the policies and the transition dynamics.
The value function satisfies the Bellman equations (see, e.g.,
Puterman 1990
):

V^{\pi^{1},\pi^{2}}(s)=\mathbb{E}^{\pi^{1},\pi^{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{u(s,a^{1}_{h},a^{2}_{h})+\sum_{s^{\prime}\in S_{h+1}}P(s^{\prime}\mid s,a^{1}_{h},a^{2}_{h})V^{\pi^{1},\pi^{2}}(s^{\prime})~\bigg|~s_{h}=s}\right]}}.

We define the value of the game as
V^{\star}=\max_{\pi^{1}}\min_{\pi^{2}}V^{\pi^{1},\pi^{2}}(s_{1})
.
The value is guaranteed by a Markov safety level policy
\pi^{\star}
for player 1, that is
V^{\pi^{\star},\pi^{2}}(s_{1})\geq V^{\star}
for any strategy
\pi^{2}
for player 2.
Moreover, there is a polynomial-time dynamic programming algorithm that returns such
\pi^{\star}
(
Wal and Wessels 1976
).

For policies
\pi^{1},\pi^{2}
, we define the occupancy measure
k^{\pi^{1},\pi^{2}}\in\mathbb{R}^{|S|\times n\times n}
as:

k^{\pi^{1},\pi^{2}}(s,a^{1},a^{2})=\mathbb{P}^{\pi^{1},\pi^{2}}[s_{h}=s,a^{1}_{h}=a^{1},a^{2}_{h}=a^{2}],\;\;\forall h=1,\dots,H,s\in S_{h},a^{1},a^{2}\in A.

A useful property of occupancy measures is that
V^{\pi^{1},\pi^{2}}(s_{1})=k^{\pi^{1},\pi^{2}}\cdot u
, when we arrange
u
as a matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{|S|\times n\times n}
.

2.2
Online linear optimization

Online linear optimization is a sequential game between a learner and the environment played for
T
rounds.
Before the game begins, the environment privately generates a sequence of linear loss functions
l_{1},\dots,l_{T}:U\mapsto\mathbb{R}
where
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a compact convex set.
At every round
t
, the learner predicts
u_{t}\in U
.
Only then, the environment reveals
l_{t}
and the learner suffers
l_{t}(u_{t})
.
The regret of the learner is defined as

R(T)\coloneqq\sum_{t=1}^{T}l_{t}(u_{t})-\min_{u\in U}\sum_{t=1}^{T}l_{t}(u).

A learning algorithm is said to be consistent if its regret grows sub-linearly with time, i.e.,
R(T)\leq o(T)
.
This ensures that, on average, the algorithm performs asymptotically as well as the best fixed strategy in hindsight.

Online Gradient Descent (OGD).

OGD refers to the online optimization algorithm that starts from some
u_{1}\in U
, then on every round
t
updates: (1)
u_{t+1}^{\prime}=u_{t}-\eta\,l_{t}
where
\eta>0
is the learning rate; (2) sets
u_{t+1}
to be the Euclidean projection of
u_{t+1}^{\prime}
onto
U
.
For a specific choice of
\eta
, OGD achieves regret bounded by
O(GD\sqrt{T})
when
\|l_{t}\|\leq G
for all
t=1,\dots,T
and
\max_{u,u^{\prime}\in U}\|u-u^{\prime}\|\leq D
(see, e.g.,
Hazan 2022
).
Note that OGD operates in time complexity of
O(T(m+P))
, where
P
is the time to compute the projection on
U
.
Thus if
P
is polynomial in the problem parameters, the OGD runs in polynomial time as well.

3
Problem definition and main results

We consider the problem of learning a two-player zero-sum Markov symmetric game by repeated play, where the learner never observes the game’s payoff but rather only the adversary’s actions.
We assume both players know the transition probability function
P
, yet the payoff function
u
is only known to the adversary.

The game is played for
T
episodes.
At the beginning of each episode
t
, both players choose policies
\pi_{t}^{1},\pi_{t}^{2}
, that are then used to play the game in the current episode.
While the opponent can choose any, possibly history-dependent, policy, we restrict the learner to select only Markov policies to facilitate efficient learning.
Thereafter, both players observe the trajectory sampled from the game’s dynamics and the actions of their opponent.
Crucially, only the adversary can calculate the incurred payoff.
The goal of the learner is to guarantee their own payoff approaches the value of the game, which is 0.
Formally, the learner minimizes
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\sum_{t=1}^{T}V^{\pi_{t}^{1},\pi_{t}^{2}}(s_{1})}\right]}}}\right\rvert}}
.
We aim for a bound that is sublinear in
T
, implying the average payoff converges to zero.

In this work we consider three types of symmetry in Markov games.

Assumption 1

(Per-state symmetric game; SSG)
.

A zero-sum Markov game is SSG if every state corresponds to a symmetric matrix game. Formally,
u(s,a^{1},a^{2})=-u(s,a^{2},a^{1})
for all
s\in S
and
a^{1},a^{2}\in A
.

Assumption 2

(Symmetry w.r.t. Markov policies; MSG)
.

A zero-sum Markov game is MSG if for every Markov policies
\pi^{1},\pi^{2}
it holds that

V^{\pi^{1},\pi^{2}}(s_{1})=-V^{\pi^{2},\pi^{1}}(s_{1}).

Assumption 3

(Symmetry w.r.t. history-dependent policies; HSG)
.

A zero-sum Markov game is HSG if for every two policies
\pi^{1},\pi^{2}
, possibly history-dependent, it holds that

V^{\pi^{1},\pi^{2}}(s_{1})=-V^{\pi^{2},\pi^{1}}(s_{1}).

Copycat strategy in MSG

In this section we prove

Our approach to learning under the MSG assumption (

The key difference lies in the virtual player’s strategy, which now leverages an online linear optimization algorithm

\displaystyle U=\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{|S|\times n\times n}~:~

\displaystyle|u(s,a^{1},a^{2})|\leq 1,\;\forall s\in S,a^{1},a^{2}\in A~\text{and}~

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\pi^{1},\pi^{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\sum_{h=1}^{H}u(s_{h},a^{1}_{h},a^{2}_{h})}\right]}}=-\mathbb{E}^{\pi^{2},\pi^{1}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\sum_{h=1}^{H}u(s_{h},a^{2}_{h},a^{1}_{h})}\right]}},\forall\pi^{1},\pi^{2}\in\Pi_{M}\},

The central challenge in the MSG setting is computing the projection step within

Indeed, the set
U
is the intersection of the unit

We first define the main concepts of approachability theory.
A Blackwell instance is a tuple

such that

The set

Definition 2

where

Finally, we state Blackwell’s Approachability Theorem.

Theorem 2
.

For any Blackwell instance

2
