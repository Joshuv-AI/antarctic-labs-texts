Glucose restriction reprograms lipid metabolism and enhances immunotherapy through ZNRF3-Wnt-SCD signaling axis
Authors: Yarui Ma, Qi Zhang, Zhewen Wei, Zhendiao Zhou, Xue Wang, Chungui Xu, Xiu Liu, Shitong Min, Peng Wang, Fangqing Yuan, Yuchen Jiao, Guangwen Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlucose restriction is a hallmark of the tumor microenvironment (TME), yet how tumor cells adapt to this metabolic stress and the impact of metabolic reprogramming on the TME remains incompletely understood.MethodsA genome-wide CRISPR knockout positive screen was performed to identify key mediators of cellular adaptation to glucose restriction. Using biochemistry, molecular biology, metabolomics and confocal immunofluorescent microscopy to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms. Additionally, single-cell RNA sequencing and orthotopic tumor models were used to characterize TME remodeling and assess therapeutic efficacy.ResultsWe identified zinc and ring finger 3 (ZNRF3) as a key mediator of cellular adaptation to glucose restriction through genome-wide CRISPR/Cas9 screening. Multiple tumor cells adaptively survived in glucose-restricted conditions with downregulated ZNRF3. Mechanistically, low glucose suppresses ZNRF3 expression, leading to Wnt pathway activation and subsequent transcriptional repression of stearoyl-CoA desaturase (SCD). This metabolic rewiring enhances antitumor immunity by increasing T-cell infiltration and cytotoxicity. In multiple preclinical models, dietary glucose restriction synergizes with immune checkpoint blockade to suppress tumor growth.ConclusionThese findings establish the ZNRF3-Wnt-SCD axis as a metabolic checkpoint controlling tumor cell fate under glucose restriction and provide a rationale for combining dietary intervention with immunotherapy.Keywords: Immune Checkpoint Inhibitor, Nutrition, Tumor Burden, Immunotherapy

Background

Metabolic reprogramming is a hallmark of cancer, characterized by alterations in glucose utilization and energy production.1 2 The Warburg effect—enhanced glucose uptake and lactate production even in the presence of oxygen—represents a fundamental feature of cancer metabolism.3 4 However, rapid tumor growth often creates regions of glucose deficiency within the tumor microenvironment (TME), necessitating adaptive mechanisms for cell survival.5 In light of these metabolic features, dietary interventions have emerged as promising adjunctive strategies in oncology.6 For example, the ketogenic diet (KD) sensitizes tumors to conventional therapies by depriving tumor cells of their preferred fuel and, critically, alters the epigenetic and immune landscape of tumors, reversing resistance to immune checkpoint blockade.7 8 Dietary restriction reprograms CD8+ T-cell fate within the TME, expanding effector subsets with enhanced metabolic fitness and cytotoxic potential.9 Dietary restriction can also enhance innate antitumor immunity, with fasting shown to metabolically rewire natural killer cells to improve tumor killing,10 and dietary interventions that exploit metabolic vulnerabilities in tumors represent a promising adjunct to standard cancer therapies.11

Multiple pathways have been implicated in cellular adaptation to glucose limitation. AMPK functions as a primary glucose sensor by modulating mTORC1 activity during energy stress.12 13 Recent studies have identified additional mechanisms, including DCAF1-mediated regulation of Rheb-mTORC1 signaling,5 KRAS-MAPK pathway activation,14 and glucose deprivation-induced expression of the long non-coding RNA HOXC-AS3.15 Notably, mTORC1 effectors 4EBP1/2 have been shown to confer cell survival under glucose starvation by promoting translational reprogramming of fatty acid metabolism, linking nutrient sensing directly to lipid homeostasis.16 Despite these advances, the precise mechanisms enabling tumor cell survival under glucose restriction remain incompletely understood.

Genome-wide CRISPR screen has emerged as a powerful tool for systematical identification of cellular stress response mechanisms. A recent study focused on metabolic pathways and identified de novo pyrimidine biosynthesis pathway involved in protecting cancer cells from low glucose condition.17 Several CRISPR screens have revealed novel regulators of copper toxicity and proteotoxic stress, highlighting the utility of this approach for understanding cellular adaptation to metabolic perturbations.18 19 More recently, CRISPR-based functional genomics has been applied to systematically dissect stress-adaptation pathways in cancer, revealing novel survival mechanisms under hypoxia, nutrient deprivation, and oxidative stress.20 Sequential CRISPR screens in pancreatic tumor models have further demonstrated that metabolic fitness genes identified in vitro are highly relevant to in vivo tumor biology.21 Notably, CRISPR screens have also linked cancer metabolic states to immune recognition, identifying pathways that regulate γδ T cell-mediated tumor surveillance.22

Zinc and ring finger 3 (ZNRF3), the cell-surface transmembrane E3 ubiquitin ligase, functions as a negative regulator of Wnt signaling through promoting the turnover of frizzled and low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 6.23 Comprehensive analyses of ZNRF3 and its paralog RNF43 have revealed their versatile roles as membrane-associated tumor suppressors, with loss-of-function mutations identified across multiple cancer types.24 Structural and functional studies have further characterized how ZNRF3 missense mutations found in tumors contribute to cancer progression through loss of Wnt receptor regulation.25 The Wnt/β-catenin pathway, regulated by ZNRF3, is one of the most extensively studied oncogenic signaling cascades, with broad implications for tumor initiation, progression, and therapeutic resistance.26 Wnt/β-catenin signaling drives immune regulation and therapeutic resistance across multiple cancer types.27 ZNRF3 alterations are frequently observed in human cancers, with loss of ZNRF3 predicting metastatic progression in prostate cancer and promoting cellular senescence in adrenocortical carcinoma.28,31 Recent studies have also implicated ZNRF3 in lipid metabolism regulation, suggesting broader roles beyond Wnt pathway control.32 However, how ZNRF3 integrates metabolic signals with tumor cell survival remains unknown.

Using genome-wide CRISPR/Cas9 screening, we identify ZNRF3 as a critical mediator of tumor cell adaptation to glucose restriction. We demonstrate that glucose restriction triggers a signaling cascade involving ZNRF3 downregulation, Wnt pathway activation, and stearoyl-CoA desaturase (SCD)-dependent metabolic reprogramming. This metabolic rewiring not only enables tumor cell survival under glucose restriction but also enhances antitumor immune responses. Our findings establish dietary glucose restriction as a potential strategy to augment immunotherapy efficacy and highlight ZNRF3-Wnt-SCD signaling as a therapeutic target.

Methods

Cell lines and compounds

K210 cells were isolated from the spontaneous pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma of C57BL/6J LSL-Kras (G12D); Trp53 fl/fl; Pdx1-Cre mice. The human U251-MG and PANC-1 cell lines were purchased from the National Infrastructure of Cell Line Resource (Beijing, China). All cell lines were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM), and culture medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and penicillin (100 U/mL)/streptomycin (0.1 mg/mL). Cells were incubated in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 at 37℃. Cell cultures with different glucose concentrations were prepared by mixing glucose-free DMEM and regular DMEM in proportion.

The glucose concentrations used for cells cultured in 15 cm and 6 cm dishes were determined based on cell counting following 48 hours of treatment with increasing glucose concentrations. Specifically, for positive screening experiments, a glucose concentration of 15 mM, corresponding to the IC90, was applied to U251 cells cultured in 15 cm dishes, resulting in approximately 90% inhibition of cell proliferation after 48 hours (online supplemental figure S1A). For assays assessing cellular metabolism, protein expression, or RNA expression, cells were treated with 8 mM glucose for 48 hours, which allowed the majority of cells to remain viable while exhibiting measurable molecular changes. For cell viability and apoptosis assays, cells were treated with 4 mM glucose for 48 hours, a concentration sufficient to induce pronounced cytotoxic and apoptotic responses.

Wnt agonists (S8178) and inhibitors (S6901) were purchased from MedChemExpress (Beijing, China). Drug concentrations used in this study were determined based on preliminary dose-response assays to ensure effective pathway modulation while maintaining >85% cell viability in normal glucose conditions (S8178, 500 nM, S6901, 1 µM).

CRISPR screening study

U251-MG cells were subjected to positive CRISPR/Cas9 screening with the human CRISPR Brunello library (Addgene #73179), containing 76,441 sgRNAs targeting 19,114 human genes (4 sgRNAs/gene) plus 1,000 non-targeting controls.33 The detailed procedures were performed as previously described.34 Specifically, 15 mM was determined as IC90 in 15 cm plate culture and used as the low glucose concentration for CRISPR/Cas9 screening.

Individual sgRNA lentivirus production and transduction

Production of lentiviruses containing a single sgRNA using the lentiCRISPR v2 system. On the day before transfection, HEK293T cells were plated into a 6 cm plate and transfected on reaching approximately 70% confluence. Cells were co-transfected with lentiCRISPR v2 (Addgene, #52961), psPAX2 (Addgene #12260), and pMD2.G (Addgene #12259) plasmids using Neofect transfection reagent (Neo Biotech, TF201201) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The viral supernatant was collected 60 hours post-transfection, centrifuged at 1,000 rpm for 10 min to remove debris, and stored at –80°C. Target cells were infected with the viral supernatant and the culture medium was replaced with fresh medium after 24 hours. 48 hours post-infection, cells were selected with puromycin (Thermo Fisher, A1113803, 2 µg/mL) to enrich successfully transduced populations. Protein lysates were subsequently prepared, and knockout efficiency was evaluated by western blot analysis.

Reconstitution of gene expression

Production of lentiviruses of pCMV-ZNRF3 (human-opt-human)−3×FLAG Neo (Miaoling Plasmid, P41235) using the second-generation lentiviral packaging system (psPAX2 and pMD2.G). Cells were transfected according to the above method. 48 hours post-transfection, cells were selected with 400 µg/mL G418 (Beyotime, ST081) for 14 days to generate a stable polyclonal population. Successful reconstitution of ZNRF3 expression was confirmed by western blot.

Western blot analysis

Cells were lysed with lysis buffer (1 M Tris-HCl (pH 6.8), 80% glycerol, and 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS)). The total protein (20 µg) was separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate‑polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) gel and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane (Millipore). Seal with Tris‑buffered saline with Tween‑20 (TBST) solution containing 5% skim milk powder and incubate overnight with primary antibody at 4°C. Incubate horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for protein visualization and quantification. Antibodies used for measuring protein expression. The antibodies used are listed in online supplemental table S4.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted using an RNA-quick purification kit (ES Science, Shanghai, China), and reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara, Tokyo, Japan), using SYBR Premix Ex Taq (Takara, Tokyo, Japan) for reverse transcription‑quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) of cDNA. The quantification of gene expression was normalized to β-actin or GAPDH using the ΔΔCt method. The sequences of the primers used are shown in online supplemental table S5.

Flow cytometry analysis of apoptosis

Apoptosis was performed by flow cytometry using a full-spectrum flow cytometer (NL-CLC3000, Cytek Biosciences, Fremont, California, USA) and Annexin V-FITC/Annexin V-633 Apoptosis Detection Kit (DOJINDO, Kumamoto, Japan). To summarize, wash cells (1×106) two times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and dye them in darkness at room temperature for 15 min with 5 µL Annexin V-FITC/Annexin V-633 and 5 µL propidium iodide (PI) solution. For flow cytometry analysis on a Beckman Coulter, add 400 µL 1X Annexin V Binding Solution. Furthermore, the data are analyzed using FlowJo V.10.8.1.

Cell viability assay

Cells were plated into 96-well plates, switch to a medium containing different glucose the next day, and continue culturing for 48 hours. Add 100 µL of Cell Counting Kit‑8 (CCK8) diluted at a ratio of 1:10 to each well, incubate at 37℃ for 2 hours, measure the absorbance at 450 nm, and draw a growth curve to evaluate cell survival rate. The remaining cells after testing were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet.

Confocal immunofluorescent microscopy and immunohistochemistry

Cells were plated into a 35 mm confocal dish the day before, the experimental group was given a low glucose medium (8 mM) for 48 hours, washed two times with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, then infiltrated with 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min at room temperature. The cells were blocked with goat serum for 30 min before incubating with primary antibody overnight at 4℃. The following day, wash the cells in PBS and incubate with Alexa Fluor 594 conjugated antibody at room temperature for an hour. Seal the cell nucleus with a 4′,6‑diamidino‑2‑phenylindole (DAPI)-containing sealing agent (ZGSB-BIO, Beijing, China) and stain. Construct images with confocal laser microscopy.

For tumor tissues, standard laboratory protocols were followed to embed paraffin sections and perform immunohistochemistry. Scan and analyze stained sections using Aperio Scan Scope software (Aperio Technologies, Vista, California, USA). The antibodies used are listed in online supplemental table S4.

Preparation and analysis of metabolomics cell samples

Digest the adherent cells into a cell suspension with trypsin, wash once with PBS, add 500 µL of pre-cooled chromatography-grade methanol at −80℃, blow and mix well, and leave overnight at −80℃. Centrifuge the tube at 14,000 g for 20 min at 4–8℃ and transfer the metabolite-containing supernatant to a new 1.5 mL tube on dry ice. Then use Speedvac/lyophilize to dry to pellet using no heat (room temperature). The centrifuged precipitate was lysed with protein lysate and quantified for standardization.

Data analysis

Differential metabolites were identified based on unpaired t-test statistical analysis, using significance thresholds of fold-change >1.5 or <0.75 and p<0.05. Pathway enrichment analysis was conducted using MetaboAnalyst V.4.0 (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/faces/home.xhtml),35 with the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (Homo sapiens) pathway library as the reference database. Enrichment results were visualized by plotting enrichment ratio (x-axis) against metabolite sets (y-axis), with a color gradient indicating p value significance.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation-qPCR assay

Chromatin immunoprecipitation assay was performed using the Chromatin Immunoprecipitation Assay Kit (#17-295, Millipore) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, a total of 1×106 U251-MG cells were used for each chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay. In 50W mode, 30% of the maximum power was set, and a 2 mm ultrasound probe was used. Under ice bath conditions, ultrasound was stopped for 30 s every 20 s, and repeated seven times. This sonication procedure resulted in DNA fragments of approximately 400 bp tested by agarose gel electrophoresis. Add the corresponding ChIP level antibody to the prewashed sample according to the recommended concentration in the antibody manual, and set-up an isotype control. Finally, DNA fragments were recovered using DNA Clean&Concentrator Kit (# D4013, ZYMO). Primers used for quantitative PCR (qPCR) analysis of immunoprecipitated DNA and antibodies for precipitating chromatin DNA are shown in the online supplemental table S5.

Dual-luciferase reporter assays

Cells were seeded in 6-well plates and co-transfected with the pLV3-CMV-TCF4 (human) expression plasmid (Miaoling Plasmid, P69042), the Renilla luciferase control vector (Miaoling Plasmid, P0372), and the SCD promoter luciferase reporter construct (Miaoling Plasmid, P46534). Luciferase activities were determined 24 hours post-transfection using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (#E1910, Promega), with Renilla luciferase serving as the internal control.

Enzyme-linked Immunosorbent assay

ELISA was performed using the Proteintech Human Granzyme B ELISA Kit, according to the manufacturer’s instructions, 500×g of cell supernatant was centrifuged for 5 min and transferred to a new 1.5 mL tube for storage at −20℃.

Animal studies

5–6 weeks male C57BL/6 mice, purchased from Beijing Huafukang Biological Technology (Beijing, China), were used for animal experiments. All animals are housed in individually ventilated cages, depending on the experimental requirements and pathogen containment needs.

To evaluate treatment efficacy, subcutaneous tumor xenograft model (n=8 per group) and orthotopic tumor xenograft model (n=3 per group) were both established in mice. Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane, and 2.5×105 K210 luc cells were suspended in 50 µL PBS and injected into the left flank or the tail of the pancreas. On day 3 post-implantation, subcutaneous tumors had reached a mean volume of approximately 80 mm³ across all animals and the animals were randomly divided into a normal diet group and a glucose-restricted diet group. The control mice were fed daily with the determined average daily food consumption weight, and calorie-restricted mice were fed daily with a food weight corresponding to 40% of the control caloric consumption (online supplemental table S6).36 Mice in the mono-immunotherapy group or combination therapy group received intraperitoneal administration of the immunotherapeutic agents (anti-programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1)/programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) antibodies) at a dose of 200 µg per animal every 3 days. After four rounds of treatment, mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation, and tissue and blood samples were collected. The formula for mouse feed is shown in online supplemental table S6.

All mice were marked with laboratory animal ear tag. Blood glucose was measured from tail vein blood collected approximately 20–24 hours after the previous meal using a Contour glucose meter (Ascensia Diabetes Care). The weight and the tumor size of subcutaneous tumor xenograft models were measured every 3 days. And the tumor volume was calculated with the following formula: (length×width2)/2. For orthotopic tumor xenograft models, mice were intraperitoneally injected with sodium fluorescein for in vivo animal imaging, and the fluorescence intensity was used to evaluate tumor burden on the completion of four treatment cycles.

Single-cell immunome sample preparation and sequencing

Cells were encapsulated in nanoliter-scale Gel Bead-in-Emulsions (GEMs) using the Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 5′ Library and Gel Bead Kit v2 (10x Genomics, Cat: 1000006), followed by reverse transcription (RT) in a thermal cycler (Bio-Rad C1000 Touch). After breaking the GEMs, barcoded cDNA was purified with Dynabeads MyOne SILANE and amplified by PCR. The amplified cDNA was then used for constructing 5’ gene expression libraries. Using 50 ng of cDNA, fragmentation, end repair, size selection with SPRIselect beads, adaptor ligation, and sample index PCR were performed to construct the 5’ gene expression library. All libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (CapitalBio Technology) using a standard configuration for 10x Genomics dual-index libraries.

Single-cell data processing

The FASTQ reads from 10x Genomics were processed using Cell Ranger (V.7.0.0) and the GRCm38 reference genome. To eliminate doublets, we used scDblFinder37 (V.1.13.14), and subsequent analyses were performed with scanpy38 (V.1.4.5). Cells were retained if they expressed between 500 and 6,000 genes and had a mitochondrial RNA content of less than 20%. Given the short lifespan of neutrophils and their tendency toward apoptosis during tissue dissociation, neutrophils were retained if they expressed between 100 and 6,000 genes.39 The filtered expression matrix was total-count normalized to 10,000 reads per cell, log2-transformed, and used to identify the top 3,000 highly variable genes for principal component analysis. The nearest-neighbor distance matrix was calculated based on the top 30 principal components (PCs), followed by dimensionality reduction using uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP). Cells were clustered using the Leiden algorithm, identified cluster-specific marker genes using Wilcoxon test, and main cell types and subtypes were annotated based on the expression of cluster-specific marker genes. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis was performed using the Enrichr implementation in GSEApy.40

Multicolor flow cytometry analysis of tumor-infiltrating T cells

Tumor tissues were excised from mice and mechanically dissociated to generate single-cell suspensions. Cells were stained with a fixable Live/Dead viability dye to exclude dead cells, followed by surface staining with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies against CD45, CD3, CD8, and PD-1. For intracellular staining, cells were subsequently fixed and permeabilized according to the manufacturer’s instructions and stained with an anti-perforin 1 (PRF1) antibody. Tumor-infiltrating T cells were identified as live CD45+CD3+ cells, and CD8+ T cells were further gated for analysis. PD-1 and PRF1 expression levels were used to assess T-cell exhaustion and cytotoxic activation, respectively. Data were acquired by flow cytometry and analyzed using FlowJo V.10.8.1. The reagent kits and antibodies used are listed in online supplemental table S4.

Activated T-cell cytotoxicity assay

Human CD3+ T cells were obtained from Milecell Biological Science & Technology (Shanghai, China) and cultured in ImmunoCult-XF T Cell Expansion Medium. After activation with a CD3/CD28 T Cell Activator and IL-2 (200 IU/mL) for 1 week, activated T cell and tumor cells in a 6-well plate (tumor cells:T cells=1:2). Following 24 hours of co-culture, all cells were collected for flow cytometry analysis. Tumor and immune cells were distinguished using CD45 staining, and Annexin V-FITC/PI double staining was performed to detect tumor cell apoptosis and T-cell killing ability.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism V.8.0 was used for statistical analysis, and the data were displayed as the mean±SEM. Unpaired two-tailed t-test were used to compare two groups, while one-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test were used to compare three or more groups.

Results

CRISPR/Cas9 screening identifies ZNRF3 as a key regulator of glucose restriction response

Cancer cells within the TME compete for limited nutrients to sustain their rapid proliferation, leading to areas of glucose deficiency. To systematically identify mechanisms of glucose restriction adaptation, we performed a genome-wide CRISPR/Cas9 screen in glioblastoma cells, which face unique metabolic challenges given the brain’s high glucose consumption (approximately 20% of total body glucose despite representing only 2% of body mass).41 Using the U251-MG (a human glioblastoma cell line) cultured under glucose restriction, we compared cell populations at baseline (T0) and after 18 days of selection (T18) (figure 1A). Among the 19,114 genes tested, we identified 926 hits significantly affecting cell survival under glucose restriction (p<0.05, comparing T18 vs T0 populations) (figure 1B, online supplemental table S1). To validate our screen, we chose three top-ranked genes (OTOP3, ZNRF3, UBE2D1) based on their dropout scores and reported roles in metabolic regulation (figure 1B). Tumor cells with individual knockout of each gene were enriched under glucose restriction survival selection, validating their function as negative regulators of glucose restriction adaptation (figure 1C–E, online supplemental figure S1B).

ZNRF3 deficiency promotes cellular adaptation to glucose restriction

ZNRF3 emerged as the strongest hit from our screen (figures1CE and 2A). To validate its role across cancer types, we generated ZNRF3-knockout (ZNRF3-KO) in multiple cell lines: PANC-1 (a human PDAC cell line) and K210 cells (the tumor cells derived from the LSL-Kras (G12D); Trp53fl/fl; Pdx1-Cre mouse model) (online supplemental figure S1C), all of which exhibit comparable baseline ZNRF3 expression levels (online supplemental figure S1D). When cultured in low glucose medium (4 mM), wild-type cells exhibited significantly reduced viability, whereas ZNRF3-KO cells maintained a significant survival advantage across all tested lines (figures1C 2B,C). Flow cytometry analysis revealed that ZNRF3 deletion protected cells from glucose restriction-induced apoptosis (figure 2D,E). Notably, reconstitution of ZNRF3 expression in ZNRF3-KO cells restored sensitivity to glucose restriction, confirming the specificity of this phenotype (figure 2D–F, online supplemental figure S1E). The CCLE database indicates that the human pancreatic cancer cell line ASPC1 carries a stop-gained SNV in ZNRF3 (SNV stop gained position: 28883950). Consistent with this genetic alteration, our western blot analysis showed that ZNRF3 protein expression in ASPC1 cells was markedly lower than in PANC-1, which harboring wild-type ZNRF3 (online supplemental figure S1F). Notably, ASPC1 cells displayed significantly higher viability under low-glucose culture conditions compared with PANC-1 cells (online supplemental figure S1G–I). Together, these findings suggest that loss of ZNRF3 confers a selective survival advantage under low-glucose conditions.

ZNRF3 regulates glucose restriction-induced metabolic reprogramming through SCD

To understand how tumor cells adapted to glucose restriction, we performed untargeted metabolomic profiling using liquid chromatograph mass spectrometer (LC-MS/MS) analysis. We compared metabolite profiles of tumor cells (U251-MG) cultured for 48 hours with normal glucose (25 mM) medium and low glucose (8 mM) medium. The analysis identified significant alterations in 254 metabolites (p<0.05, fold change <0.75 or >1.5): 209 downregulated (74 and 135 in negative and positive ion modes) and 45 upregulated (22 and 23 in negative and positive ion modes) (figure 3A, online supplemental tables S2 and S3). Pathway analysis highlighted significant enrichment of unsaturated fatty acid metabolism among the differentially regulated pathways (figure 3A,B).

Targeted metabolomic analysis of fatty acid species revealed significant downregulation of two major monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs)—oleic acid and palmitoleic acid—under glucose restriction or in ZNRF3-KO cells under normal glucose medium (figure 3C,D, online supplemental tables S7 and S8). SCD, an important rate-limiting enzyme for synthesizing MUFA, is encoded by the SCD gene and the homologous gene in mice is Scd1 (figure 3E).42 Consistently, the deletion of SCD in tumor cells was significantly enriched in glucose-restricted conditions according to the screening result (online supplemental table S1). To test whether SCD mediates the metabolic effects of ZNRF3, we generated SCD-knockout (SCD-KO) cells across multiple lines including U251-MG, PANC-1 and K210 (figure 3G). Similar to ZNRF3-KO cells, SCD-KO cells exhibited enhanced survival under glucose restriction (figure 3F, online supplemental figure S2A). To further support this, wild-type and ZNRF3-KO cells were supplemented with exogenous oleic acid or palmitoleic acid and then subjected to low glucose stress. Notably, while ZNRF3-deficient cells exhibited a robust survival advantage under glucose deprivation, addition of either monounsaturated fatty acid completely abolished this phenotype (online supplemental figure S2B–D). On MUFA repletion, ZNRF3-KO cells displayed markedly increased apoptosis, reaching levels comparable to glucose-stressed wild-type cells, as quantified by flow cytometry analysis. In contrast, MUFA supplementation did not substantially alter the response of wild-type cells to low glucose (online supplemental figure S3A). Mechanistically, ZNRF3 knockout led to decreased SCD expression, while SCD-KO did not affect ZNRF3 levels (figure 3G,H), establishing SCD as a downstream effector of ZNRF3 signaling.

Tumor cells survive in low glucose condition through ZNRF3-Wnt-SCD signaling axis

Having established ZNRF3 as a critical regulator of glucose restriction-induced cell death, we investigated the underlying mechanisms. We found that glucose restriction significantly downregulated ZNRF3 expression at both messenger RNA (mRNA) and protein levels across multiple cell lines, potentially conferring a growth advantage under low-glucose stress conditions (figure 4A,B). ZNRF3 is a negative regulator of the Wnt pathway.23 Glucose restriction increased the level of active β catenin and enhanced nuclear translocation, as demonstrated by western blot and confocal microscopy analyses (figure 4B, online supplemental figure S3C). These data suggested that glucose restriction could activate the Wnt pathway. Consistently, qPCR analysis further revealed that glucose-restricted cells exhibited marked upregulation of the canonical Wnt target genes MYC and CCND1, accompanied by a reduction in the negative feedback regulator AXIN2 (online supplemental figure S3B). These results collectively demonstrate that glucose restriction activates the Wnt signaling.

To investigate the function of Wnt activation, we pharmacologically manipulated Wnt signaling using small molecule agonists (S8178) and inhibitors (S6901) prior to glucose restriction. Wnt pathway activation significantly protected cells from glucose restriction-induced death, whereas pathway inhibition sensitized cells to low glucose cultivation (figure 4C, online supplemental figure S3D–E). Together, these findings indicate that ZNRF3 regulates cellular survival under glucose limitation by modulating Wnt signaling.

We next investigated whether ZNRF3 regulates SCD expression through the Wnt pathway, leading to metabolic reprogramming of tumor cells under low glucose conditions. We found that Wnt activation decreased SCD protein levels (figure 4D). ChIP analysis in U251-MG cells revealed direct binding of the Wnt effector TCF4 and β-catenin to the SCD promoter (figure 4E). Importantly, TCF4 knockout (online supplemental figure S3F) abolished the low glucose-induced SCD downregulation in U251-MG, PANC-1 and K210 cell lines, supporting the ZNRF3-Wnt-SCD axis as a key mediator of metabolic adaptation (figure 4F,I). Dual-luciferase reporter assays further demonstrated that TCF4 directly represses SCD transcription via promoter binding (figure 4G). Mechanistically, ChIP analysis revealed that Wnt agonist treatment enhanced TCF4/β-catenin recruitment to the SCD promoter in U251-MG cells (figure 4H). Given that the Groucho/TLE family acts as classical Wnt co-repressors, we tested whether TLE1 contributes to SCD repression. However, TLE1 knockout did not reverse SCD suppression under low glucose condition in all three cell lines (online supplemental figure S3G,H), indicating that this process is independent of TLE-mediated transcriptional inhibition. Collectively, these data establish a signaling cascade wherein glucose restriction downregulates ZNRF3, leading to Wnt pathway activation and TCF4-mediated transcriptional repression of SCD, independent of Groucho/TLE co-repressors, ultimately promoting metabolic adaptation under glucose-limited conditions.

Low glycemic diet reshapes the tumor immune microenvironment

Aberrant metabolism in tumor cells may reshape the tumor immune microenvironment.43 44 Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is characterized by surviving in nutrient and oxygen-deregulated TME with a complex TME. To investigate whether metabolic reprogramming through the ZNRF3-Wnt-SCD axis influences antitumor immunity in vivo, we established an orthotopic PDAC model (C57BL/6) using K210 (the tumor cells derived from the LSL-Kras (G12D); Trp53fl/fl; Pdx1-Cre mouse model) cells. Mice were randomized to receive either a standard diet or calorie-restricted diet (CR, 40% caloric reduction) for 2 weeks, followed by single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) collected from the PDAC tumors (figure 5A). After quality control, we got 9,365 single-cell transcriptomes, including malignant, immune and stromal populations (figure 5B, and online supplemental figure S4A,B).

CR treatment significantly altered the tumor immune landscape, most notably increasing fractions of T cells and B cells (figure 5C). Unsupervised clustering analyses of T cells identified seven distinct states (figure 5D, online supplemental figure S4C). Detailed analysis revealed significant expansions of exhausted cytotoxic T cells (Cd8_Tex) in the CR group that simultaneously exhibit high exhaustion and cytotoxicity signatures (figure 5E, online supplemental figure S4D,E). Also, we observed significant enrichment of T cell-mediated immune response pathways (figure 5F), suggesting that metabolic intervention may enhance the functional capacity of these T cells despite their exhausted phenotype. Furthermore, under CR conditions, Cd4+Th17 cells (Cd4_Th17) were increased (figure 5E), which were known to promote cytotoxic T-cell responses in tumors.45 Consistent with our in vitro findings, malignant cells from CR-treated mice showed reduced Znrf3 and Scd1 expression at mRNA and protein levels, accompanied by increased CD3+, CD4+ and CD8+T cell infiltration in CR-treated tumors (figure 5G,H, online supplemental figure S4F). Multicolor flow cytometry was performed on orthotopic PDAC tumors harvested from mice subjected to either a normal diet or CR diet for 14 days (n=3 per group). In line with the scRNA-seq results, CD8+ T cells from CR tumors displayed increased PD-1 and PRF1 expression (figure 5I, online supplemental figure S4C,G), indicative of enhanced activation accompanied by a pre-exhausted phenotype.

In addition, B cells showed enrichment of antigen presentation pathways, specifically with upregulation of B-cell receptor signaling and major histocompatibility complex (MHC)-II protein binding genes, a key process for presenting antigens to activate CD4+ helper T cells (online supplemental figure S5A,B). In addition, we observed increased expression of B2m, Tap1, Tap2, and Tapbpl in B cells (online supplemental figure S5C), which may enhance MHC class I antigen presentation and thereby directly activate CD8+ T cells.

Caloric restriction diet enhances immunotherapy efficacy

To evaluate the therapeutic implications of our findings, we established an orthotopic PDAC model using luciferase-expressing K210 cells in situ injected into the pancreas of immune-competent mice. The mice were randomized into four treatment groups: (1) normal diet (control), (2) CR (40% reduction), (3) anti-PD-1 antibody (200 µg/mouse, intraperitoneal (i.p.), every three days (q3d)), or (4) CR plus anti-PD-1 combination (n=3). CR alone significantly reduced tumor burden by 2.1-fold (0.31 g vs 0.64 g, CR group vs control group, p<0.001) (figure 6A–C, online supplemental figure S6A) and significantly enhanced anti-PD-1 efficacy. The combination therapy reduced tumor weight by 2.0-fold compared with the anti-PD-1 treatment alone (0.16 g vs 0.32 g, p<0.05) (figure 6A–C, online supplemental figure S6A), and by 4.0-fold compared with control (0.16 g vs 0.64 g, p<0.001) (figure 6B,C). Immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis confirmed that the combination produced the highest intratumoral densities of CD8+, granzyme B+, and B220+cells across all groups (all p<0.001; online supplemental figure S5D). These in vivo immune infiltration patterns closely mirror the cytotoxic immune activation signatures identified in our single-cell analysis, providing orthogonal mechanistic evidence that CR remodels the immunosuppressive PDAC TME to potentiate checkpoint blockade. Importantly, although CR lowered systemic glucose levels, these values remained within the physiological range and were not associated with detectable organ impairment (online supplemental figure S6B–D).

Consistent with the orthotopic findings, subcutaneous K210 tumor model in C57BL/6 mice revealed that CR alone markedly inhibited tumor growth (CR vs control, 0.32 g vs 0.50 g, p<0.001), and its combination with anti-PD-1 therapy produced the strongest antitumor effect (combination vs anti-PD-1 alone, 0.08 g vs 0.23 g, p<0.01; combination vs control alone, 0.08 g vs 0.50 g, p<0.001; figure 6F–H, online supplemental figure S6E). Similar results were obtained using anti-PD-L1 therapy (figure 6A,D–H, online supplemental figure S6F), confirming the robustness of this therapeutic strategy. In addition, CR-fed mice exhibited a significant reduction in body weight compared with control animals (p<0.01; figure 6I), indicative of the systemic metabolic effects associated with CR in this tumor context. To assess whether the observed tumor growth inhibition translates into durable survival benefit, we performed Kaplan-Meier survival analysis across all six treatment groups (figure 6J). The control group exhibited the shortest survival, with all mice succumbing to tumor burden by approximately day 31. CR monotherapy and immune checkpoint blockade monotherapies (anti-PD-1 and anti-PD-L1) each modestly extended survival compared with the untreated control. Strikingly, the CR-based combination groups achieved the longest median survival of approximately 50 days, substantially outperforming their respective monotherapy counterparts and the control group. Survival differences were statistically significant across all pairwise comparisons (p<0.05), confirming that the antitumor effects of CR and immune checkpoint blockade are not only additive at the level of tumor burden but also confer meaningful and durable improvements in overall survival. These results demonstrate that the therapeutic benefits of CR and its synergy with anti-PD-1 treatment are reproducible across anatomically distinct PDAC models. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that CR not only reduces tumor burden but fundamentally reshapes the tumor immune microenvironment to sustain durable antitumor immunity, thereby markedly enhances the efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade in PDAC models.

ZNRF3 deficiency enhances tumor cell susceptibility to immunotherapy

To directly test glucose restriction’s effects on antitumor immunity, we established an in vitro CD3+ T-cell cytotoxicity assay using human T cells prior with CD3/CD28 activator and interleukin-2 treatment (figure 7A).46 Representative flow cytometry plots revealed high CD25 and CD69 expression on T cells used for co-culture, indicating that they were in an activated phenotype (online supplemental figure S6G). Tumor cells (PANC-1) pre-exposed to glucose restriction showed significantly enhanced susceptibility to T cell-mediated killing (figure 7B, online supplemental figure S6H, S6I), accompanied by increased granzyme B release (figure 7C). Previous studies have shown that glucose restriction can lead to downregulation of ZNRF3 and activation of the WNT pathway, we pretreated tumor cells (PANC-1) with WNT pathway agonists for 48 hours and performed T-cell toxicity assays, analysis revealed that activation of the WNT pathway also can enhance the sensitivity of tumor cells to T-cell toxicity (figure 7D, online supplemental figure S6J). Similarly, we performed T-cell cytotoxicity assay using ZNRF3-KO or SCD-KO tumor cells (PANC-1). Flow cytometry analysis revealed that ZNRF3 or SCD-KO enhanced tumor cell sensitivity to T-cell cytotoxicity, accompanied by increased granzyme B release (figure 7E, online supplemental figure S6K,L).

Analysis of clinical datasets revealed that low tumor expression of either ZNRF3 or SCD correlated with improved survival following anti-PD-1 therapy (ZNRF3: 33.5 months vs 17.02 months, low vs high expression cohort; SCD: 27.5 months vs 15.51 months; low vs high expression cohort), comparable to established PD-L1 expression markers (31.27 months vs 17.02 months, high vs low expression cohort) (figure 7F,G, online supplemental figure S6M).47

Discussion

The ability of tumor cells to adapt to nutrient stress is a pivotal driver of tumor progression. However, the mechanisms enabling survival under glucose deprivation remain poorly characterized. By employing systematic CRISPR/Cas9 screening, we identified the ZNRF3-Wnt-SCD signaling axis as a key pathway mediating cellular adaptation to glucose restriction. This axis bridges the gap between metabolic stress-induced lipid metabolism reprogramming and immune modulation, providing novel insights into the interplay between tumor metabolic rewiring and antitumor immunity.

Our screen identified multiple regulators of glucose restriction response, with ZNRF3 emerging as the most significant candidate. Our study reveals that glucose restriction downregulates ZNRF3, the cell-surface transmembrane E3 ubiquitin ligase, activates the Wnt pathway and suppresses SCD expression, which reprograms lipid metabolism.23 48 Our study uncovers a previously unrecognized role of ZNRF3 in metabolic adaptation, including its impact on downstream gene alterations leading to metabolic reprogramming in response to changes in glucose concentration. This finding adds to growing evidence that ZNRF3 functions through the Wnt pathway, consistent with recent studies that highlight its involvement in cellular senescence and lipid metabolism.32

Yusuf et al demonstrated that glucose deprivation activates Wnt/β-catenin signaling in glioblastoma stem cells,49 50 which is consistent with our observation of Wnt activation under glucose restriction. Recent studies have further established that Wnt/β-catenin signaling is a broad driver of metabolic reprogramming across solid tumors, regulating not only glycolysis but also amino acid metabolism,51 52 underscoring the pathway’s central role in coordinating nutrient-stress responses. A key advance of our study lies in the identification of SCD as a downstream effector of ZNRF3-Wnt signaling under glucose restriction. SCD, a rate-limiting enzyme in the synthesis of MUFAs, plays a critical role in lipid metabolic reprogramming, which is important for cancer cell proliferation.42 53 54 Through combined metabolomic profiling and functional studies, we demonstrate that glucose restriction significantly alters fatty acid metabolism through transcriptional regulation of SCD. Consistently, another study reported that calorie-restricted diet reduces tumor SCD activity, leading to an imbalance between unsaturated and saturated fatty acids in mouse model.36 Importantly, our findings further reveal that this metabolic rewiring is driven by TCF4-mediated direct regulation of SCD expression. Although TCF4/β-catenin complexes are classically viewed as transcriptional activators, accumulating evidence supports their ability to function as transcriptional repressors in specific biological contexts. For example, Cadoux et al demonstrated that Wnt/β-catenin signaling can repress NKG2D ligand expression in mouse and human HCC models.55 Consistent with this concept, our findings identify SCD repression as another example of TCF4/β-catenin-mediated transcriptional inhibition. Mechanistically, this repression appears to be independent of canonical Groucho/TLE co-repressors. Although Groucho/TLE factors are known to convert TCF complexes into transcriptional repressors, TLE1 knockout did not rescue SCD suppression under low glucose, indicating that this regulation does not rely on classical TLE-dependent mechanisms. These findings identify a ZNRF3-Wnt-TCF4 signaling axis that represses SCD expression under glucose restriction, linking Wnt signaling to lipid metabolic remodeling and expanding the functional repertoire of β-catenin/TCF complexes beyond transcriptional activation.

scRNA-seq has emerged as a transformative tool for dissecting the cellular complexity of the pancreatic cancer immune microenvironment at single-cell resolution.56,58 Using scRNA-seq, we comprehensively characterized the remodeling of the tumor immune microenvironment under glucose restriction. This remodeling was marked by enhanced T-cell infiltration, indicative of a heightened antitumor immune response. Intriguingly, our scRNA-seq analysis revealed that caloric restriction expanded an Tex population characterized by the simultaneous upregulation of both cytotoxic effector genes (Prf1, Gzmb, Prdm1) and exhaustion markers (Pdcd1, Lag3, Tox). This seemingly paradoxical phenotype—increased exhaustion markers correlating with improved immunotherapy response—can be reconciled by recent advances in our understanding of T-cell exhaustion biology. Importantly, a recent study by Cillo et al demonstrated that dual lymphocyte‑activation gene‑3 (LAG-3)/PD-1 blockade drives CD8+ T cells toward a “terminal exhaustion” state with paradoxically enhanced cytotoxic function, characterized by co-expression of exhaustion and effector programs.59 Our findings suggest that caloric restriction may prime a similar T-cell state, wherein elevated PD-1 expression renders these functionally competent cells highly responsive to anti-PD-1 therapy. This model explains the synergistic efficacy observed when combining dietary intervention with checkpoint blockade: caloric restriction expands a population of PD-1+ effector-competent T cells that are optimally positioned to respond to PD-1 blockade. Flow cytometric validation confirmed that CD8+ T cells from CR-treated tumors exhibited concurrent increases in both PD-1 and PRF1 expression, supporting the coexistence of exhaustion markers and cytotoxic capacity within this population.

Glucose-restricted diets have long been recognized for their potential antitumor effects, often attributed to reductions in blood glucose and insulin levels, or their impact on dysregulated fatty acid and pyrimidine metabolism.36 60 Recent mechanistic studies have substantially advanced our understanding of how dietary restriction modulates antitumor immunity. Caloric restriction also remodels tumor-infiltrating neutrophil lipid metabolism, restricting lipid accumulation and thereby mediating broad antitumor effects across multiple cancer models.58 Dietary restriction further enhances immune checkpoint blockade efficacy by inhibiting the intratumoral mTORC1/B7-H3 axis.61 Pan-cancer analyses have revealed that dietary restriction-associated molecular activities are broadly distributed across cancer types and correlate with improved immune infiltration in the TME.62 These findings provide a strong mechanistic rationale for combining dietary glucose restriction with immune checkpoint blockade, as demonstrated in our study. However, our study identifies a previously uncharacterized mechanism through which glucose restriction exerts its effects: the reprogramming of lipid metabolism via the ZNRF3-Wnt-SCD axis in tumor cells. This metabolic rewiring explains the reason that tumor cells adapt to glucose restriction environments for growth, and also drives profound changes in the tumor immune microenvironment, thereby enhancing antitumor immunity.

Our findings reveal that glucose restriction downregulates ZNRF3, triggering the Wnt pathway and suppressing SCD expression. This reprogramming sensitizes tumor cells to T cell-mediated cytotoxicity, and highlights the therapeutic potential of targeting this axis in combination with immunotherapy. Analysis of public databases further corroborates our findings, showing that patients with ZNRF3-deficient or SCD-deficient tumors are more likely to benefit from anti-PD-1 therapy. Recent studies have substantially expanded our understanding of SCD as a regulator of antitumor immunity: SCD1 inhibition has been shown to synergize with anti-PD-1 antibody by simultaneously modulating β-catenin signaling in tumor cells and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress in T cells.63 Furthermore, SCD1 in CD4+ T cells has been shown to suppress tumor growth through activation of the CXCR3/CXCL11 axis in CD8+ T cells,64 and SCD inhibition triggers lipotoxicity and enhances antitumor immunity.65,67 The balance between saturated, monounsaturated, and polyunsaturated fatty acids profoundly shapes CD8+T cell fitness and antitumor efficacy.68 69 While previous studies have linked SCD activity to immune regulation,70 71 our work uniquely positions the ZNRF3-Wnt-SCD axis as a critical metabolic checkpoint that integrates dietary glucose restriction with immune activation. Notably, the relationship between Wnt signaling and antitumor immunity may involve multiple complementary mechanisms. Sung et al demonstrated that Wnt pathway activation directly enhances T-cell polyfunctionality and persistence, improving adoptive T-cell therapy efficacy.72 In our study, we focused on the tumor cell-intrinsic effects of Wnt activation, showing that Wnt agonist pretreatment of tumor cells—rather than T cells—enhanced their susceptibility to T cell-mediated cytotoxicity. These findings suggest that Wnt signaling may exert dual beneficial effects in cancer immunotherapy: directly augmenting T-cell effector functions while simultaneously rendering tumor cells more vulnerable to immune attack through metabolic reprogramming. Future studies exploring the combined modulation of Wnt signaling in both tumor and immune compartments may reveal additional therapeutic opportunities.

Collectively, our study establishes a novel paradigm for how glucose restriction modulates tumor metabolism and immunity through the ZNRF3-Wnt-SCD axis. The PDAC tumor immune microenvironment is characterized by profound immunosuppression, with complex interactions among tumor cells, cancer-associated fibroblasts, and immune cells that collectively restrict T-cell infiltration and function.73,75 Our findings that glucose restriction reshapes this immunosuppressive landscape through the ZNRF3-Wnt-SCD axis provide a novel mechanistic framework for understanding how metabolic interventions can overcome immune evasion in PDAC. Metabolic regulation of immunity in the TME is increasingly recognized as a critical determinant of immunotherapy response,76 and our study identifies a specific molecular axis that links tumor metabolism to immune susceptibility.

The therapeutic implications of our findings are particularly noteworthy. Our study demonstrates that dietary glucose restriction synergizes with immune checkpoint blockade, resulting in significantly enhanced treatment efficacy in preclinical models. Importantly, the identification of ZNRF3 and SCD expression as potential biomarkers for immunotherapy response provides a compelling rationale for patient stratification. However, several questions remain unanswered, including the optimal timing, dosage and duration of dietary intervention. Additionally, further research is needed to determine whether these findings are applicable to other tumor types beyond pancreatic cancer. In conclusion, our study establishes ZNRF3 as a central regulator of tumor cell adaptation to glucose restriction and reveals a novel and critical link between metabolic stress and antitumor immunity. These insights lay the groundwork for leveraging the ZNRF3-Wnt-SCD axis as a therapeutic target and provide compelling evidence for the integration of dietary interventions with immunotherapy. By demonstrating the synergistic potential of these approaches, our findings offer a framework for innovative cancer treatment strategies.

Limitations of the study

First, all dietary interventions were performed in mouse models, and it remains unclear whether the observed metabolic and immunological effects of caloric restriction can be directly translated to human patients. Second, we only evaluated the combination of caloric restriction with PD-1 or PD-L1 blockade; whether similar synergistic effects occur with other immunotherapies or targeted treatments remains to be determined. Third, the optimal timing, duration, and intensity of CR necessary to achieve maximal therapeutic benefit are still unknown, which may limit the clinical applicability of our findings.
