Phosphorylation of OTUB1 promotes autophagy initiation under starvation
Authors: Liangbo Sun, Meng He, Dong Liu, Jiahong Liu, Meihua Shan, Mingzhen Yang, Yan Zhang, Xufang Dai, Jie Yao, Yanhao Zhang, Haojun Xiong, Fengtian He, Jiqin Lian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Eukaryotes initiate autophagy in response to environmental challenges such as nutrient deprivation. However, the mechanisms governing autophagy initiation remain incompletely understood. Here, we demonstrated that OTUB1 phosphorylation plays a key role in starvation-induced autophagy initiation. Specifically, ERK phosphorylates OTUB1 at S118 during starvation. This phosphorylation enables competitive binding of ANXA2 to OTUB1, disrupting its interaction with TRIM29 and enhancing OTUB1 stability. Stabilized OTUB1 upregulated DEPTOR, thereby inhibiting mTOR and inducing autophagy. Furthermore, in vivo studies reveal that fasting-induced OTUB1-mTOR-autophagy axis counteracted Western diet-mediated mTOR activation by restoring ERK-dependent OTUB1 phosphorylation, ultimately ameliorating MASLD progression. Collectively, this study uncovers a phosphorylation-dependent regulatory mechanism controlling OTUB1’s function in nutrient deficiency-triggered autophagy initiation, providing cellular and molecular evidence supporting the therapeutic potential of fasting in alleviating MASLD.

Introduction

Macroautophagy (hereafter referred to as autophagy) is an intracellular degradation mechanism widely recognized as a process that provides cells with energy for survival under nutrient-deprived conditions. By eliminating unnecessary proteins and damaged organelles, it plays a critical role in maintaining cellular integrity and survival1. Fasting regimens and continuous caloric restriction (CR) are considered among the most effective strategies for extending lifespan and healthspan, serving as classical methods to activate autophagy and are increasingly being adopted as alternative clinical interventions for obesity. Strong evidence supports the notion that autophagy mediates these beneficial effects2,3. Meanwhile, a growing body of research indicates that under physiological conditions, autophagy is regulated by nutrient-sensing factors during feeding/fasting cycles—such as the feeding sensors FXR and SHP, and the fasting sensors TFEB, CREB, and PPARα—which dynamically decrease or increase autophagic flux, respectively, by modulating the transcription of autophagy-related gene networks4–7. Fasting initiates hepatic autophagy through multiple pathways; for instance, glucose starvation largely relies on the induction of autophagy via activation of 5′-adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK). According to current models, AMPK phosphorylates and activates ULK1 (UNC-51-like kinase 1) to induce autophagy, while mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1), a key negative regulator of autophagy, inhibits AMPK function by disrupting the interaction between AMPK and ULK18. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying these models are unlikely to be singular, and additional molecular signaling pathways may exist to supplement and refine them.

The ubiquitin system couples ubiquitin to cellular target proteins through the complex functional interplay of ubiquitin-activating (E1s), ubiquitin-conjugating (E2s), and ubiquitin-ligating (E3s) enzymes9. Proteins in nearly all known signaling pathways are regulated by this system, making it a critical contributor to cellular and tissue homeostasis9. Deubiquitinating enzymes act as negative regulators of ubiquitination, counteracting this process by trimming or removing conjugated ubiquitin chains from substrates9,10. Ubiquitin aldehyde-binding protein 1 with an ovarian tumor (OTU) domain (OTUB1) is one of the most abundantly expressed deubiquitinating enzymes in cells9,11. However, emerging evidence suggests that OTUB1 also exerts regulatory functions through non-canonical mechanisms independent of its intrinsic deubiquitinase catalytic activity12. OTUB1 is a crucial regulator of metabolic homeostasis13 and participates in cellular energy metabolism, although its underlying molecular mechanisms remain incompletely understood. The absence of Otub1 exacerbates hyperinsulinemia and hepatic steatosis induced by either a high-fat diet or a high-fat, high-cholesterol diet14, suggesting that OTUB1 plays a significant role in hepatic metabolism. We found that OTUB1 is sensitive to both fasting in vivo and cellular starvation in vitro. Following a certain degree of nutrient deprivation, extracellular nutrients rapidly upregulate OTUB1 through a phosphorylation-dependent pathway. Conversely, nutrient replenishment or excess downregulates OTUB1. Given that OTUB1 is a key positive regulator of autophagy, these findings help elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying the rapid initiation of autophagy in response to nutrient deficiency.

To date, approved pharmacological treatment options for MASLD/MASH remain limited; therefore, lifestyle-based interventions, including fasting-related dietary regimens, continue to hold substantial clinical significance. A growing body of evidence indicates that intermittent fasting (IF) and other dietary regimens, such as time-restricted feeding (TRF) or fasting-mimicking diets, are gaining prominence as viable interventions to combat obesity and metabolic diseases15,16. Furthermore, studies have shown that mild caloric restriction in humans can improve metabolic health and healthspan17. Additionally, IF and intermittent TRF (iTRF) have also been reported to enhance healthspan and lifespan in Drosophila18,19. However, the molecular mechanisms by which fasting regimens ameliorate MASLD are not yet fully understood. A better understanding of these mechanisms is crucial and will form the foundation for their potential beneficial effects.

In this study, we discovered that nutrient deprivation upregulates OTUB1 through a phosphorylation-dependent pathway, targeting DEPTOR-mTOR to induce autophagy initiation. Mechanistically, starvation rapidly activates ERK, which mediates the phosphorylation of OTUB1 at serine 118. Phosphorylated OTUB1 exhibits enhanced binding to ANXA2 and dissociation from TRIM29, significantly increasing its protein stability. Elevated OTUB1 further promotes ERK activation. The upregulated OTUB1 enhances DEPTOR expression by stabilizing DEPTOR, thereby inhibiting mTOR and ultimately inducing autophagy. In summary, our findings elucidate the dynamic regulation of OTUB1 protein and the molecular mechanism of autophagy initiation following nutrient deprivation, providing cellular and molecular biological evidence supporting the ameliorative effects of fasting diets on MASLD.

Results

Starvation upregulates OTUB1 expression and induces autophagy by enhancing OTUB1 protein stability

Short-term starvation induces changes in key proteins that enable the organism to rapidly respond to external signals20–22. Our previous studies identified OTUB1 as a nutrient-sensitive protein. Following short-term starvation, OTUB1 protein levels were rapidly upregulated (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Fig. 1A, B), a phenomenon particularly evident in immortalized hepatocytes and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cells, whereas no significant changes in OTUB1 were observed in other cell lines (Supplementary Fig. 1C–H). In HCC cells, starvation also induced autophagy (Fig. 1A–C), the cotreatment with chloroquine (CQ) or Baf-A1 resulted in the enhancement of the accumulation of LC3-II and the inhibition of the reduction of SQSTM1 induced by EBSS (Supplementary Fig. 1I–L), this suggests that the autophagy was induced with an unobstructed autophagic flux. Moreover, OTUB1 mRNA levels remained unchanged (Fig. 1D), this suggests that OTUB1 protein levels may be rapidly modulated through post-translational modifications under nutrient deprivation. To determine whether OTUB1 upregulation plays a regulatory role in autophagy induction, we knocked down OTUB1 during starvation, which partially suppressed starvation-induced autophagy (Fig. 1E–G and Supplementary Fig. 2A, B) and also reduced cell survival (Fig. 1H). These results indicate that starvation-induced OTUB1 upregulation contributes to the induction of protective autophagy. Knockdown of OTUB1 inhibited starvation-induced autophagy while maintaining an intact autophagic flux (Supplementary Fig. 2C–F), this indicates that the alteration of OTUB1 under starvation does not affect autophagic flux. Protein stability assays under starvation conditions revealed that short-term starvation significantly enhanced the stability of both endogenous and exogenous OTUB1 (Supplementary Fig. 3A–D). To further investigate which nutrient deficiency was responsible, we mimicked amino acid, serum, and glucose deprivation, respectively. The results showed that glucose deprivation had the most pronounced effect on OTUB1 upregulation (Supplementary Fig. 4A, B), while amino acid deprivation also induced OTUB1 upregulation (Supplementary Fig. 4C, D), and serum deprivation had no such effect (Supplementary Fig. 4E, F). We then increased the glucose concentration in normal DMEM medium (4500 mg/L) to twice its original level (9000 mg/L), and found that this reduced the stability of both endogenous and exogenous OTUB1 (Supplementary Fig. 3E–H). Prolonged treatment with high glucose led to further downregulation of OTUB1 (Supplementary Fig. 4G, H). Conversely, re-supplementation of glucose after glucose starvation reversed the starvation-induced OTUB1 upregulation (Supplementary Fig. 4I, J). These findings demonstrate that nutrients, including glucose, act as signals to rapidly modulate OTUB1 protein stability, and that OTUB1 upregulation in response to nutrient deficiency is a reversible regulatory mechanism. Starvation enhances OTUB1 protein stability by activating ERK signaling

Given that short-term starvation enhances OTUB1 protein stability without significantly altering its transcriptional levels, we hypothesized that the mechanism underlying nutrient deprivation-induced OTUB1 upregulation might involve post-translational modifications (PTMs). We screened several common PTMs and found that the increase in OTUB1 protein levels following starvation at various time points was accompanied by a decrease in its ubiquitination (Supplementary Fig. 5A). Furthermore, when the proteasome inhibitor MG132 was administered concurrently with starvation, the starvation-induced upregulation of OTUB1 was abolished, and the basal level of OTUB1 was significantly elevated (Supplementary Fig. 5B–D). This suggests that starvation may enhance OTUB1 stability by inhibiting its degradation via the proteasomal pathway. Concurrently, we detected no significant changes in the methylation or lactylation of OTUB1 following starvation (Supplementary Fig. 5E–H). We further examined changes in the serine and threonine phosphorylation of OTUB1 and discovered that while serine phosphorylation was significantly increased after starvation, threonine phosphorylation remained unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 6A–C). We also observed a decrease in OTUB1 acetylation post-starvation (Supplementary Fig. 6D, E). However, treatment with the deacetylase inhibitors Trichostatin A (TSA, an inhibitor of HDAC classes I, II, and IV) and/or Nicotinamide (NAM, an inhibitor of SIRT family deacetylases) had no effect on starvation-induced OTUB1 upregulation (Supplementary Fig. 6F–H), indicating that OTUB1 acetylation is not involved in the changes to its protein stability following starvation. To determine whether the starvation-induced upregulation of OTUB1 is dependent on autophagy, we used the autophagy inhibitor CQ and found that it did not alter the starvation-induced increase in OTUB1 (Supplementary Fig. 6I–K). This demonstrates that starvation-mediated OTUB1 upregulation is not regulated by autophagy. To further investigate whether the increased serine phosphorylation of OTUB1 contributes to its enhanced stability, we examined several common phosphorylation-related signaling pathways. We found that starvation rapidly activated JNK, ERK, and AKT, as evidenced by their increased phosphorylation, with the activation of ERK (p-ERK) being the most pronounced (Fig. 2A and Supplementary Fig. 7A–C). Next, we inhibited these pathways using the JNK inhibitor SP600125, the ERK inhibitor U0126, and the AKT inhibitor BEZ235, respectively, prior to starvation. We discovered that inhibiting the ERK pathway significantly suppressed the starvation-mediated upregulation of OTUB1 (Fig. 2B and Supplementary Fig. 7D), whereas inhibition of the JNK and AKT pathways had little to no effect (Fig. 2C, D and Supplementary Fig. 7E, F). Interestingly, when we knocked down OTUB1 prior to starvation, we observed that OTUB1 depletion inhibited the starvation-mediated phosphorylation and activation of the ERK pathway (Fig. 2E and Supplementary Fig. 7G–I). This suggests that ERK pathway activation may enhance OTUB1 protein stability through a phosphorylation-dependent mechanism, while the upregulation of OTUB1, in turn, further activates the ERK pathway, thus forming a positive feedback loop. To confirm this, we assessed OTUB1 protein stability under starvation conditions after pre-treatment with the ERK inhibitor U0126. We found that inhibiting the ERK pathway significantly reduced the stability of both endogenous and exogenous OTUB1 protein during starvation (Fig. 2F–I). Inhibition of ERK did not significantly alter OTUB1 mRNA expression (Supplementary Fig. 7J), but also markedly reduced its protein stability (Supplementary Fig. 7K–M). ERK-mediated S118 phosphorylation of OTUB1 enhances the OTUB1 stability

To identify the phosphorylation site(s) on OTUB1, we isolated ectopically expressed OTUB1 and analyzed its phosphorylation by mass spectrometry. In three independent assays, we identified a phospho-serine-containing peptide (FKAVSpAKSK) corresponding to the region surrounding S118 in human OTUB1 (Fig. 3A). Homology analysis of the protein sequence revealed that S118 is highly conserved across different species (Fig. 3B). To investigate the function of this potential phosphorylation site, we mutated serine 118 to alanine (S118A), a mutation that mimics the dephosphorylated state. We transfected cells with plasmids expressing either wild-type OTUB1 (OTUB1WT) or the OTUB1S118A mutant and subjected them to starvation. We then assessed the serine phosphorylation status of the ectopic OTUB1. The results showed that starvation significantly increased serine phosphorylation of OTUB1WT. In contrast, the OTUB1S118A mutation markedly reduced the basal level of OTUB1 serine phosphorylation and prevented its starvation-induced increase (Fig. 3C and Supplementary Fig. 8A). Next, we examined the impact of this site-specific mutation on OTUB1 protein stability in cells. As shown in Fig. 3D, E, the OTUB1S118A mutation significantly decreased OTUB1 protein stability. Conversely, the OTUB1S118E mutation (S118 to glutamic acid, which mimics the phosphorylated state) enhanced OTUB1 protein stability. Subsequently, we generated a custom antibody specifically targeting phosphorylated OTUB1 at S118 (p-OTUB1S118), using the peptide N-RFKAV(p-S)AK-C as an immunogen. The antibody titer exceeded 1:32,000 (Supplementary Fig. 8B). We validated the antibody’s specificity by Western blot (Supplementary Fig. 8C, D). Using this antibody, we demonstrated that starvation stimulation significantly increased p-OTUB1S118 levels (Fig. 3F and Supplementary Fig. 8E–G). Furthermore, treatment with an ERK inhibitor markedly suppressed p-OTUB1S118, which was accompanied by a decrease in total OTUB1 protein levels (Fig. 3G and Supplementary Fig. 8H). Cisplatin, a known activator of the ERK pathway, significantly enhanced p-OTUB1S118 and concurrently upregulated OTUB1 protein levels (Fig. 3H and Supplementary Fig. 8I). Meanwhile, to investigate the correlation between OTUB1 levels, p-OTUB1, and autophagy, we supplemented starved cells with either amino acids or glucose. The results revealed that changes in OTUB1 levels upon glucose replenishment strongly correlated with autophagic alterations (Supplementary Fig. 9A–C). In contrast, amino acid supplementation induced only marginal changes in OTUB1, which showed a weak correlation with autophagy (Supplementary Fig. 9D–F). This suggests that autophagy induced by glucose deprivation is tightly linked to OTUB1 upregulation and the phosphorylation dynamics at S118. To further confirm the functional role of S118, we transfected cells with OTUB1WT or OTUB1S118A and applied starvation. The results showed that while OTUB1S118A effectively blocked the starvation-induced increase in OTUB1S118 phosphorylation, it also inhibited the starvation-mediated upregulation of OTUB1 and the induction of autophagy (Fig. 3I and Supplementary Fig. 10A). In contrast, the OTUB1S118E mutant further enhanced the level of starvation-induced autophagy (Fig. 3J and Supplementary Fig. 10B). Moreover, the cisplatin-mediated increase in OTUB1S118 phosphorylation was significantly reversed in cells expressing the OTUB1S118A mutant (Fig. 3K and Supplementary Fig. 10C). Finally, we observed that U0126, by inhibiting ERK, reduced the protein levels of OTUB1WT. However, this effect was not observed in cells expressing the phospho-mimetic OTUB1S118E mutant (Fig. 3L and Supplementary Fig. 10D). Furthermore, we performed an in vitro kinase assay to verify the direct phosphorylation of OTUB1 by ERK. The results demonstrated that immunoprecipitated FLAG-ERK mediated the phosphorylation of wild-type OTUB1 at the S118 site, whereas it failed to phosphorylate the OTUB1 S118A mutant. Concurrently, the immunoprecipitated kinase-dead FLAG-ERKK52R mutant significantly impaired the phosphorylation of OTUB1 (Supplementary Fig. 10E). Beyond nutrient deprivation, we subjected cells to various stressors, including ER stress, oxidative stress, and DNA damage. Interestingly, all these stimuli mediated autophagy via the ERK-OTUB1 S118 phosphorylation axis (Supplementary Fig. 10F, G). This indicates that this pathway is not exclusively restricted to nutrient starvation, suggesting a potentially universal role for ERK-mediated OTUB1 phosphorylation in autophagy induction. To further investigate the molecular mechanism underlying starvation-induced ERK activation, we reintroduced serum, scavenged ROS, or inhibited AMPK under starvation. The results revealed that only ROS scavenging significantly attenuated ERK activation, whereas serum repletion or AMPK inhibition had no significant effect (Supplementary Fig. 11A–F). This suggests that the ERK-OTUB1S118 pathway may depend on ROS alterations, acting as an autophagy-inducing effector by amplifying ROS signals. Starvation-mediated upregulation of OTUB1 participates in autophagy induction by targeting the DEPTOR-mTOR

Our initial findings revealed a positive correlation between starvation-induced OTUB1 upregulation and enhanced autophagy, as indicated by the increased LC3-II/GAPDH ratio. While it has been reported that OTUB1 can target DEPTOR12, this interaction had not been validated in the context of starvation. Our results demonstrate that under starvation stimulation, the induction of autophagy coincides with increased OTUB1 and DEPTOR protein levels, accompanied by the inhibition of phosphorylated mTOR (p-mTOR) (Fig. 4A and Supplementary Fig. 12A–C). This indicates the suppression of the mTOR signaling pathway, a consequence of DEPTOR being a key endogenous inhibitor of mTOR. To investigate whether OTUB1 induces autophagy by targeting DEPTOR during starvation, we found that the knockdown of OTUB1 significantly attenuated the starvation-induced upregulation of DEPTOR, the inhibition of p-mTOR, and the induction of autophagy (Fig. 4B and Supplementary Fig. 12D, E). To further explore the impact of OTUB1 S118 phosphorylation on the DEPTOR-mTOR axis, we subjected cells expressing either OTUB1WT or OTUB1S118A to starvation for varying durations. In the OTUB1S118A group, the starvation-mediated upregulation of DEPTOR and downregulation of p-mTOR were both suppressed (Fig. 4C and Supplementary Fig. 12F, G). This further indicates that starvation stimulation requires the phosphorylation of OTUB1 at S118 to enhance its stability, leading to OTUB1 upregulation, which in turn upregulates DEPTOR, inhibits the mTOR signaling pathway, and ultimately induces autophagy. Meanwhile, previous work has emphasized that the Cys91 site in OTUB1 is crucial for its deubiquitinase activity23. Therefore, we generated the OTUB1C91S mutant. OTUB1 can also stabilize proteins via an Asp88-dependent, non-canonical mechanism24. Additionally, based on the work by Zhao et al.12, we generated OTUB1 D88A and C23S mutants. Our data demonstrated that the expression of the OTUB1C91S mutant in OTUB1−/− cells still effectively promoted starvation-mediated DEPTOR upregulation, whereas the D88A mutant failed to support DEPTOR stabilization (Supplementary Fig. 13A, B). This indicates that under starvation conditions, DEPTOR stabilization primarily depends on the non-canonical, Asp88-dependent activity of OTUB1, rather than its canonical Cys91 deubiquitinase activity. This finding is consistent with the model proposed by Zhao et al.12, which posits that OTUB1 stabilizes DEPTOR in a Cys91-independent but Asp88-dependent manner, suggesting that OTUB1 targets DEPTOR for deubiquitination via a deubiquitinase activity-independent, non-canonical mechanism. Under normal nutrient conditions, we overexpressed wild-type OTUB1 or the S118E mutant while concurrently inhibiting ERK. We found that the S118E mutant triggered mTOR inhibition and induced autophagy. Furthermore, S118E mutant significantly reversed the elevated OTUB1 ubiquitination and the suppression of autophagy caused by ERK inhibition (Supplementary Fig. 13C). This suggests that phosphorylation at S118 is essential for maintaining OTUB1 stability and acting as a key downstream mediator of ERK signaling in autophagy regulation. Furthermore, we investigated whether the levels of OTUB1 and autophagy could be modulated by starvation in vivo using a C57BL/6N mouse model. As shown in Fig. 4D–F and Supplementary Fig. 13D–F, analysis of liver tissues from fasted mice revealed that glucose starvation elevated hepatic OTUB1 and DEPTOR levels, inhibited mTOR activity, and enhanced autophagy. Conversely, administration of a high-glucose diet slightly downregulated OTUB1 and DEPTOR in the liver tissue, but no significant changes in mTOR activity or autophagy levels were observed. Starvation-induced OTUB1 phosphorylation promotes OTUB1 binding to ANXA2 and dissociation from TRIM29

To further investigate the molecular mechanisms regulating OTUB1 protein stability, we employed a combination of immunoprecipitation (IP) and mass spectrometry (IP-MS) to screen for potential E3 ubiquitin ligases that interact with OTUB1. As shown in Fig. 5A, TRIM29 was identified as a strong candidate E3 ligase binding to OTUB1 in three independent experiments. Furthermore, co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) assays confirmed a physical interaction between TRIM29 and OTUB1 at both endogenous and exogenous levels (Fig. 5B, C). Subsequently, we found that overexpression of TRIM29 enhanced the ubiquitination of OTUB1 and decreased its protein levels (Fig. 5D). Knockdown or overexpression of TRIM29 effectively regulated OTUB1 protein levels (Fig. 5E and Supplementary Fig. 14A). We then explored whether the interaction between OTUB1 and TRIM29 changes during starvation. As shown in Fig. 5F and Supplementary Fig. 14B, the results indicated that the OTUB1-TRIM29 interaction progressively weakened with the duration of starvation. We next examined whether the phosphorylation of OTUB1 at S118 affects its binding to TRIM29. The results showed that the OTUB1S118A mutation increased binding to TRIM29, whereas the OTUB1S118E mutation decreased this interaction (Fig. 5G and Supplementary Fig. 14C). Further experiments revealed that the OTUB1S118A mutation not only enhanced the binding of OTUB1 to TRIM29 but also reversed the starvation-induced weakening of this interaction. Consequently, the OTUB1S118A mutation reversed the starvation-mediated upregulation of DEPTOR, downregulation of p-mTOR, and induction of autophagy (Fig. 5H and Supplementary Fig. 14D–G). In contrast, the OTUB1S118E mutation further weakened the starvation-induced reduction in OTUB1-TRIM29 binding, while simultaneously enhancing the inhibitory effect of starvation on p-mTOR and further promoting autophagy induction (Fig. 5I and Supplementary Fig. 14H–K). To further explore the molecular mechanisms regulating OTUB1 protein stability, we again used IP-MS to screen for other OTUB1-interacting candidate proteins. As shown in Fig. 5J, Annexin A2 (ANXA2) was identified as the strongest candidate binding partner for OTUB1 across three independent experiments. Moreover, co-IP assays demonstrated a physical interaction between ANXA2 and OTUB1 at both endogenous and exogenous levels (Fig. 5K, L). We found that knockdown or overexpression of OTUB1 did not affect the ubiquitination level or protein abundance of ANXA2 (Fig. 5M), indicating that while ANXA2 interacts strongly with OTUB1, it is not a downstream target of OTUB1. Next, we investigated whether ANXA2 is involved in regulating OTUB1 stability during starvation. The results showed that knockdown of ANXA2 significantly attenuated the starvation-induced upregulation of OTUB1 protein (Fig. 5N and Supplementary Fig. 14L). We further examined whether the OTUB1-ANXA2 interaction changes during starvation. As shown in Fig. 5O and Supplementary Fig. 14M, the results revealed a strong interaction between ANXA2 and OTUB1, which progressively increased with the duration of starvation. We then tested whether the phosphorylation of OTUB1 at S118 affects its binding to ANXA2. The results indicated that the OTUB1S118A mutation reduced binding to ANXA2, whereas the OTUB1S118E mutation slightly increased this interaction (Fig. 5P and Supplementary Fig. 14N). The OTUB1S118A mutation impaired the starvation-induced enhancement of OTUB1-ANXA2 binding (Fig. 5Q and Supplementary Fig. 14O), while the OTUB1S118E mutation maintained a strong interaction between OTUB1 and ANXA2 (Fig. 5R and Supplementary Fig. 14P). To investigate the competitive and direct interactions among ANXA2, TRIM29, and OTUB1, in vitro binding assays using purified proteins demonstrated that, compared to His-tagged wild-type OTUB1, the S118A mutant exhibited significantly reduced binding to ANXA2 but enhanced binding to TRIM29 (Supplementary Fig. 15A, B). This indicates that the S118 site of OTUB1 serves as a critical “phospho-switch” governing the direct competitive binding between ANXA2 and TRIM29. Furthermore, we purified OTUB1 protein from HepG2 cells for Bio-Layer Interferometry (BLI) experiments. BLI analysis revealed that both ANXA2 and TRIM29 interact with OTUB1, albeit with different binding affinities: ANXA2 exhibited a stronger binding affinity for OTUB1 (Kd = 2.64 nM) (Supplementary Fig. 15C), whereas TRIM29 showed a relatively weaker affinity (Kd = 154.1 nM) (Supplementary Fig. 15D). To directly verify this competitive binding, we performed a competitive inhibition assay using purified proteins. The BLI results demonstrated that when ANXA2 was pre-saturated on OTUB1, the subsequent binding of TRIM29 was abolished (Supplementary Fig. 15E, red line). Conversely, in the absence of pre-bound ANXA2, TRIM29 successfully bound to OTUB1 (Supplementary Fig. 15E, blue line). Taken together, our findings reveal the interplay and competition among ANXA2, TRIM29, and OTUB1. Since the OTUB1 used in our BLI experiments was purified from HepG2 cells cultured under normal nutrient conditions, it retains a certain degree of phosphorylation. This basal phosphorylation may primarily account for the significantly higher binding affinity of OTUB1 for ANXA2 compared to TRIM29 in the in vitro BLI assay. The BLI assay measures intrinsic binding affinities in an artificial system where both binding partners are fully accessible. This does not necessarily reflect the predominant OTUB1 binding partner within cells, where binding is also dictated by multiple factors, including the effective local concentration of each protein, subcellular localization, phosphorylation-dependent accessibility of relevant binding interfaces, and nutrient-dependent dynamic changes in partner availability. Under nutrient-rich conditions or when cells take up excess nutrients, the phosphorylation of OTUB1 at S118 decreases, and our cellular IP data indicate that OTUB1 preferentially binds to TRIM29. In this context, ANXA2 may have limited access to OTUB1 due to its subcellular distribution or sequestration within other cellular complexes. During starvation, increased OTUB1 phosphorylation, coupled with starvation-induced ANXA2 redistribution or increased availability, may expose or stabilize the ANXA2 binding interface, thereby favoring the OTUB1-ANXA2 interaction even in the presence of TRIM29. The selective binding of ANXA2 to S118-phosphorylated OTUB1 is crucial for enhancing OTUB1 protein stability. Under starvation conditions, the increased phosphorylation of OTUB1 at S118 favors its association with ANXA2 while attenuating its interaction with TRIM29. TRIM29 leads to ubiquitination of K77, K78, and K151 in OTUB1 and reduces its protein stability

We then assessed the changes in OTUB1 protein stability following overexpression of TRIM29. The results indicated that TRIM29 overexpression reduced OTUB1 protein stability (Fig. 6A, B). Since TRIM29 is a E3 ligase and there are currently no reports on OTUB1 ubiquitination, we attempted to detect potential ubiquitination sites on OTUB1 using mass spectrometry. By isolating ectopically expressed OTUB1 and analyzing its ubiquitination status via mass spectrometry, we identified the aforementioned seven ubiquitinated lysine-containing peptides (IKDLH[K-Ub]; [K-Ub]YSYIR; F[K-Ub]AVSAKSK; FKAVSA[K-Ub]; AVSAKS[K-Ub]; SKEDLVSQGFTEFTIEDFHNTFMDLIEQVE[K-Ub]; GEGGTTNPHIFPEGSEP[K-Ub]) in three separate tests. These peptides were mapped to lysines K77, K78, K115, K120, K122, K151, and K256 on human OTUB1 (Fig. 6C). We mutated all seven lysine residues to arginine and observed that only the K77R, K78R, and K151R mutants showed a reduction of OTUB1 ubiquitination induced by TRIM29, whereas the other mutants did not exhibit significant changes (Fig. 6D). Interestingly, we observed that the OTUB13KR mutant (a triple mutant combining K77, K78, and K151 negative mutations) was more stable following OTUB1 overexpression (Fig. 6E, F). When ERK was inhibited alongside the overexpression of wild-type OTUB1 or the OTUB13KR mutant, we found that compared to wild-type OTUB1, OTUB13KR successfully suppressed mTOR and strongly induced autophagy. Furthermore, the ERK inhibition-induced increase in OTUB1 ubiquitination and mTOR activation were significantly reversed in the OTUB13KR group (Supplementary Fig. 16A). The result (Supplementary Fig. 16B) demonstrates that the increased OTUB1 ubiquitination driven by reduced S118 phosphorylation strictly relies on ubiquitination occurring at the three key lysine residues (K77, K78, and K151). This suggests that ERK-mediated S118 phosphorylation dynamically regulates OTUB1 protein stability by preventing its ubiquitination at these sites, thereby targeting mTOR to control autophagy. Based on the fact that ubiquitin can be conjugated by another ubiquitin through any of its seven Lys residues (K6, K11, K27, K29, K33, K48, and K63) or, alternatively, the methionine (Met/M)1, we then characterized which of these eight linkage types is responsible for TRIM29-mediated OTUB1 ubiquitination by generating a panel of ubiquitin mutants which substitutes arginine (Arg/R) for lysine residues at all sites except the indicated one. The ubiquitination assay revealed that TRIM29-mediated K27-linked and K48-linked ubiquitination of OTUB1 (Fig. 6G). Complementarily, KK27/48RR ubiquitin, in which K27 and K48 were replaced with arginine, was unable to facilitate overexpression of TRIM29-mediated OTUB1 ubiquitination modification (Fig. 6H). Furthermore, in vitro ubiquitination assay provided additional evidence that TRIM29 can directly ubiquitinate OTUB1 (Supplementary Fig. 16C). ERK mediated phosphorylation of OTUB1 is critical for fasting-induced autophagy and amelioration of MASLD in vivo

To further validate the importance of the aforementioned signaling pathway in fasting-induced hepatic autophagy, we subjected C57BL/6N mice to a fasting regimen (food deprivation with ad libitum water) and treated them with either the ERK inhibitor U0126 or a vehicle control, followed by collection of liver tissues for further analysis. Initially, no significant differences were observed in Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining of liver tissues across all groups, regardless of fasting status (Fig. 7A). However, in the normally fed group, inhibition of the ERK signaling pathway by U0126 resulted in markedly lower levels of p-OTUB1S118, higher levels of p-mTOR, and reduced autophagy compared to the vehicle control. We next found that fasting significantly upregulated p-ERK and p-OTUB1S118, downregulated p-mTOR, and induced autophagy in wild-type mice. However, these effects were abolished in the fasted group co-treated with U0126 (Fig. 7A–C and Supplementary Fig. 17A–C). This indicates that activation of the ERK pathway plays a critical role in mediating fasting-induced p-OTUB1S118, which in turn upregulates OTUB1 protein stability, leading to increased DEPTOR expression, mTOR inhibition, and ultimately, the promotion of autophagy in vivo. In mice fed a Western diet (WD), levels of p-ERK, p-OTUB1S118, OTUB1, and DEPTOR were all reduced to some extent, resulting in mTOR hyperactivation and suppression of hepatic autophagy. In contrast, the combination of fasting with the WD reversed the WD-induced reduction in p-ERK, p-OTUB1S118, OTUB1, and DEPTOR. This led to attenuated mTOR hyperactivation, partial restoration of hepatic autophagy, and a significant reversal of WD-induced MASLD progression (Fig. 7D–F and Supplementary Fig. 17D–F). OTUB1 is a critical mediator of the beneficial effects of intermittent fasting on hepatic autophagy and the amelioration of MASLD

We identified OTUB1 as a nutrient-sensitive protein in the liver. Following fasting (food deprivation with ad libitum access to water), hepatic OTUB1 was upregulated, concomitant with the induction of hepatic autophagy (Fig. 7C). Conversely, in mice fed a Western diet (WD), hepatic OTUB1 was downregulated, and autophagy was suppressed (Fig. 7D). Furthermore, co-administration of the autophagy inhibitor CQ with the WD exacerbated the WD-induced MASLD phenotype (Fig. 8A–I). To investigate the therapeutic potential of fasting, we implemented an IF regimen in WD-fed mice, consisting of two 24-h fasts per week (on Mondays and Thursdays), with ad libitum feeding on the remaining days. The results demonstrated that IF partially reversed the WD-induced downregulation of OTUB1 and suppression of autophagy (Fig. 7D), while also attenuating the progression of the MASLD phenotype (Fig. 8J–R). To determine the causal relationship between OTUB1 expression, autophagy and the WD-induced MASLD phenotype, we subjected liver-specific OTUB1 knockout (OTUB1-HKO) mice and their wild-type (WT) littermates to either a WD alone or a WD combined with IF. Consistent with previous reports14, OTUB1-HKO mice were more susceptible to developing MASLD than WT mice. Intriguingly, the beneficial effects of IF on the MASLD phenotype were significantly blunted in OTUB1-HKO mice (Supplementary Fig. 18A–I), and IF failed to effectively reverse the WD-induced suppression of hepatic autophagy in these mice (Supplementary Fig. 18J, K). In contrast, in mice with liver-specific overexpression of OTUB1 (OTUB1-HTG), IF robustly reversed the WD-induced suppression of hepatic autophagy (Supplementary Fig. 18L, M) and markedly improved the MASLD phenotype in OTUB1-HTG mice (Supplementary Fig. 18N–V). Collectively, these findings indicate that the downregulation of OTUB1 is a key event in the pathogenesis of WD-induced MASLD. Moreover, they demonstrate that fasting ameliorates MASLD progression by upregulating OTUB1. Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study provides the evidence delineating the specific molecular mechanisms underlying the dynamic regulation of OTUB1 stability in response to starvation. Upon activation, ERK phosphorylates OTUB1, leading to its rapid upregulation to induce autophagy. By acting as a critical regulator, OTUB1 effectively amplifies the ERK-dependent autophagic signaling. Specifically, we found that OTUB1 protein stability is rapidly enhanced in a starvation environment and plays a crucial role in autophagy initiation, thereby advancing the understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying starvation-induced autophagy (Fig. 9). Under nutrient deprivation, the ERK signaling pathway is rapidly activated, leading to ERK-mediated phosphorylation of OTUB1 at serine 118. Phosphorylated OTUB1 exhibits enhanced binding to ANXA2 and dissociation from TRIM29, which significantly increases OTUB1 protein stability. The elevated OTUB1 further promotes ERK activation and upregulates DEPTOR expression by stabilizing the DEPTOR protein, thereby inhibiting mTOR and ultimately inducing autophagy. In contrast, under nutrient-rich conditions, ERK activity is relatively low. Dephosphorylated OTUB1 dissociates from ANXA2 and binds more strongly to TRIM29, resulting in decreased OTUB1 protein levels. This reduction leads to destabilization and downregulation of DEPTOR, which in turn activates mTOR and suppresses autophagy. Furthermore, our findings extend the previous work by Zhao et al.12, which demonstrated that OTUB1 stabilizes DEPTOR to suppress mTORC1 activity and promote autophagy under amino acid deprivation. Previous reports have documented the phosphorylation of OTUB1. For instance, non-receptor tyrosine kinases (Src and SRMS) mediate the phosphorylation of OTUB1 at Y26, which enhances its interaction with Raptor25. Additionally, casein kinase 2 (CK2) phosphorylates OTUB1 at Ser16 to promote its nuclear accumulation26. Notably, CK2 is likely activated under starvation conditions27. It is therefore plausible that the residual phospho-serine signal observed upon the overexpression of the OTUB1 S118A mutant during starvation may be attributed to the activation of CK2 or other alternative serine kinases. In this study, we identify a previously unreported post-translational modification (PTM)-dependent mechanism by which OTUB1 is upregulated specifically during fasting/starvation. This mechanism induces autophagy, enabling cells to generate catabolized nutrients internally to sustain survival. The upregulation of OTUB1 is thus advantageous for hepatocytes themselves in environments of nutrient scarcity or energy insufficiency. According to a report by Wang et al., OTUB1-deficient mice exhibit increased energy expenditure and reduced age-dependent weight gain13, suggesting that OTUB1 may be a key regulator of energy metabolism. Taken together, these studies collectively indicate that OTUB1 stabilization may be a critical regulator of hepatic lipid homeostasis. The research by Zhang et al. showed that OTUB1 in the liver inhibits MASH progression by targeting TRAF6 for deubiquitination, thereby attenuating TRAF6-mediated ASK1 activation14. During the progression from MASLD to MASH, OTUB1 in the liver is significantly downregulated, and liver-specific OTUB1 knockout significantly aggravates hepatic steatosis induced by high-fat and high-cholesterol diets. Zhong et al. found that elevated TMAO promotes ferroptosis and ultimately aggravates MASLD by inhibiting OTUB1 and reducing the stability of SLC7A11. OTUB1 is downregulated in the liver during MASLD progression, and overexpression of OTUB1 can significantly alleviate MASLD28. However, Ai et al. reached seemingly contradictory conclusions. In their animal models, they established an insulin-resistant mouse model after three months of a high-fat diet (60% HFD, XTHF60). They proposed that OTUB1 is significantly upregulated in MASLD and regulates MASLD progression by targeting PARP1/HMGB1, and that knocking down OTUB1 can significantly improve hepatic steatosis caused by a high-fat diet29. However, they only performed knockdown using AAV-si-OTUB1, and did not perform OTUB1 knockout, let alone a liver-specific OTUB1 knockout experiment. We used hepatocyte-specific OTUB1 inactivated mice, and the mice were fed a Western Diet for 32 weeks. We observed that after liver-specific OTUB1 knockout, there was a more obvious aggravated phenotype of hepatic steatosis and autophagy defects, which is consistent with the report by ref. 14. But regardless of whether OTUB1 acts through downstream autophagy pathways or non-autophagy pathways, OTUB1 is ultimately a protective factor that prevents fatty liver disease. Given that all in vivo studies were conducted in male mice, the generalizability of these findings to female mice remains to be determined, which represents a potential limitation of this study.

However, the dynamic changes in OTUB1 homeostasis itself represent an even more crucial prerequisite. We found that hepatic OTUB1 levels undergo reversible changes in response to both fasting and overfeeding, which in turn dictate the long-term activation or suppression of mTOR and determine the extent of hepatic autophagy. This phenomenon is mirrored at the hepatocellular level: in response to starvation, hepatocytes rapidly and significantly upregulate OTUB1 protein levels via a PTM-dependent pathway. Conversely, under conditions of nutrient overload, OTUB1 is rapidly downregulated, leading to the inhibition of hepatic autophagy. This entire process is reversibly regulated30. OTUB1 is a key factor in hepatic autophagy, and its dynamic regulation is the critical switch that controls autophagic flux-upregulating it during fasting and downregulating it after overfeeding. Changes in hepatic autophagy, in turn, are a major factor in controlling hepatic steatosis. Under nutrient deprivation, the rapid upregulation of OTUB1 plays a vital role in suppressing mTOR activity, which is beneficial for maintaining liver health and preventing the onset of fatty liver. Therefore, this OTUB1-mTOR-autophagy axis may have a profound impact on diet-modulated autophagy. Given that OTUB1 is conserved across many organisms, including yeast, C. elegans, mice, and humans, OTUB1-mediated autophagy induction likely represents a common adaptive mechanism for promoting cell survival and maintaining energy balance during prolonged nutrient scarcity, while its suppression under conditions of overnutrition contributes to metabolic dysregulation.

For millennia, dietary and fasting regimens have been devoutly practiced, and the scientific community is increasingly recognizing their benefits for obesity, metabolic diseases, and even cancer. In our study, we employed an IF 5:2 regimen31 in mice fed a Western diet for four months. At the molecular level, we demonstrate that OTUB1 acts as a key executor of the beneficial hepatic response to fasting. We found that a prolonged fasting period of at least 24 h in mice is required to significantly upregulate OTUB1 through the activation of a post-translational modification (PTM)-dependent pathway. This upregulation of OTUB1 leads to the sustained inhibition of mTOR and the maintenance of hepatic autophagy. Furthermore, it effectively reverses the Western diet-induced downregulation of OTUB1 and activation of mTOR, thereby ameliorating the progression of MASLD caused by overnutrition.

Autophagy defects lead to abnormal hepatic triglyceride (TG) accumulation and fatty liver, yet the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood. In this study, we elucidate a signaling mechanism by which OTUB1 promotes autophagy, specifically the fasting-induced upregulation of OTUB1. Impaired autophagy was observed in OTUB1-deficient mice, and further in vivo experiments demonstrated that OTUB1 knockout (OTUB1−/−) attenuates fasting-triggered hepatic autophagy. This indicates that the fasting-induced upregulation of hepatic OTUB1 is crucial for the induction of liver autophagy in vivo. In the liver, OTUB1’s ability to rapidly control mTOR activation and inhibition via post-translational modifications (PTMs) is a key regulator of hepatic autophagy, making it a compelling factor in strategies of using fasting to counteract the effects of overnutrition. Autophagy-mediated lipid degradation is vital for reducing hepatic TG levels and exerting beneficial effects in obese mice. The hepatic fasting response is mediated by inducing the transcription of a network of genes involved in autophagy-mediated lipolysis and fatty acid β-oxidation, thereby maintaining energy homeostasis. Autophagy is suppressed in the livers of patients with MASLD, and further inhibition of autophagy can exacerbate MASLD progression. Substantial evidence also links autophagy deficiency to the onset and pathogenesis of MASLD. In the current study, we observed that fasting effectively activates ERK, leading to enhanced phosphorylation of OTUB1 at Serine 118 (p-OTUB1S118), upregulation of OTUB1, and subsequent mTOR inhibition, which induces hepatic autophagy. Notably, the combination of fasting with an overnutrition diet was able to rescue and restore the levels of p-ERK, p-OTUB1S118, and OTUB1, thereby restoring hepatic autophagy. However, given that ERK and OTUB1 regulate a vast array of downstream targets, it must be acknowledged that the phenotypic effects we observed might not be exclusively attributed to the ERK-OTUB1-DEPTOR-mTOR signaling axis. Alternative mechanisms driven by other ERK or OTUB1 downstream targets may also be at play. For instance, the ERK signaling pathway modulates energy metabolism and autophagy through numerous targets. These include directly phosphorylating ULK1 to alter its activity32 and regulating TFEB phosphorylation to dictate its subcellular localization, thereby orchestrating autophagy levels across various tissues33. Concurrently, research on OTUB1 in the context of autophagy regulation has predominantly been restricted to reports involving DEPTOR. Nevertheless, OTUB1 is capable of mediating the deubiquitination of a broad spectrum of alternative substrates. A notable example is its regulation of ferroptosis by targeting GPX434. Considering the intricate crosstalk between ferroptosis and autophagy, this pathway could indirectly modulate autophagic flux. Therefore, while the activation of the ERK-OTUB1-DEPTOR-mTOR axis during fasting or IF regimens plays a pivotal role in initiating hepatic autophagy in our in vivo and in vitro models, it is highly plausible that complementary parallel pathways co-contribute to the ultimate biological outcomes.

However, to date, the dynamic regulatory mechanism of the OTUB1 protein has not been reported. We decipher the molecular mechanism governing changes in OTUB1 protein stability, a mechanism that plays a pivotal role in response to alterations in the nutritional environment. On one hand, this mechanism facilitates the triggering of autophagy during fasting/starvation; on the other, OTUB1 has numerous downstream target proteins. To date, OTUB1 has been shown to catalyze the deubiquitination of multiple targets, including p100, ERα, UBE2E1, Snail, DEPTOR, YB-1, SMAD2/3, c-IAP1, SOCS1, UBC13, PD-L1, Cyclin E1, MSH2, SLC7A11, TRAF3, TRAF6, and Nur77, resulting in either positive or negative regulation of their functions35. In the context of normal physiology, OTUB1 is largely considered to play a protective role. However, the majority of reports on OTUB1 have focused on its role in cancer, where it is widely believed to promote tumor proliferation, metastasis, and immunosuppression by targeting a multitude of these proteins34,36–38. Consequently, targeting OTUB1 may inhibit cancer progression. This positions OTUB1 as a viable and potential therapeutic target for treating various cancers and immune-related diseases.

Annexin A2 (ANXA2) is an evolutionarily conserved, calcium-dependent protein reported to participate in the initiation of autophagy. It promotes phagophore assembly via actin polymerization under physiological conditions or facilitates the trafficking of ATG9A to phagophores during starvation39,40. Other studies have shown that ANXA2 promotes TFEB activation41 or upregulates HSF1 phosphorylation, leading to the transcriptional activation of ATG7 and subsequent promotion of autophagy42. ANXA2 sustains autophagy levels in various tumors, mediating tumor progression and chemoresistance, and is associated with poor prognosis and metastatic growth in multiple cancers. In our experiments, we identified a strong interaction between ANXA2 and OTUB1. However, experimental validation confirmed that OTUB1 is not a deubiquitinase for ANXA2 and does not mediate its ubiquitination. According to a report by Wang et al.43, ANXA2 stabilizes nuclear MYC by binding to it and mediating its deubiquitination. Our experiments demonstrate that ANXA2 is indeed an upstream regulator of OTUB1. By binding to phosphorylated OTUB1, ANXA2 promotes the dissociation of TRIM29 from OTUB1, thereby maintaining OTUB1 in a deubiquitinated state, regulating its protein stability, and ultimately inducing autophagy. Our findings thus refine and supplement the molecular mechanism by which ANXA2 regulates autophagy.

Hua et al. found that TRIM29 is overexpressed in high glucose (HG)-treated mouse podocytes, and that TRIM29 silencing significantly attenuates HG-induced podocyte injury. HG treatment promotes podocyte pyroptosis by activating the NF-κB/NLRP3 inflammasome pathway via TRIM2944. Our research reveals that glucose deprivation enhances OTUB1 phosphorylation via the ERK pathway, leading to the dissociation of OTUB1 from TRIM29. Concurrently, other studies have shown that high glucose conditions reduce the osteogenic differentiation potential of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), accompanied by decreased ANXA2 expression. The addition of recombinant ANXA2 can restore glucose-impaired osteogenic differentiation of MSCs and ameliorate glucose-induced cellular senescence45. Furthermore, high glucose induces ANXA2 upregulation in HK-2 cells, and its knockdown alleviates high glucose-induced apoptosis and inflammation46. These reports suggest that ANXA2 is involved in glucose-stimulated signaling pathways. Our experiments corroborate this, showing that under glucose starvation, elevated OTUB1 phosphorylation enhances its binding to ANXA2 while reducing its interaction with TRIM29. This shift promotes OTUB1 deubiquitination and stability, ultimately mediating a protective autophagic response. TRIM29 mediates the ubiquitination of OTUB1 via both K27- and K48-linked polyubiquitin chains. Given that K27-linked modifications are rarely associated with substrate degradation and have been closely implicated in autophagy47, we speculate that the K27-linked ubiquitination of OTUB1 may confer non-degradative functions that specifically modulate its autophagic activity. The precise biological significance of this modification warrants further in-depth investigation. The OTUB1-TRIM29 and OTUB1-ANXA2 complexes undergo reversible changes in response to alterations in extracellular glucose levels, a regulatory mode controlled by the degree of ERK-mediated phosphorylation of OTUB1 at serine 118.

In our model, total nutrient deprivation (including glucose deprivation) significantly upregulates OTUB1 protein levels. This promotes the stabilization of DEPTOR, an inhibitor of mTOR, and accelerates the initiation of autophagy. Our data indicate that nutrients, including glucose, act as external signals that modulate OTUB1 phosphorylation via the ERK signaling pathway. Both ANXA2 and TRIM29 interact strongly with OTUB1, but elevated OTUB1 phosphorylation enhances its binding to ANXA2 while reducing its association with TRIM29. Therefore, we propose that ANXA2 and TRIM29 compete for binding to OTUB1, and this competition is determined by the phosphorylation status of OTUB1.

In summary, this study reveals a phosphorylation-dependent regulatory mechanism that controls the role of OTUB1 in autophagy initiation under nutrient deprivation. It also provides cellular and molecular evidence supporting the therapeutic potential of fasting in alleviating MASLD progression, and may offer clinical opportunities for treating diseases associated with dysregulation of OTUB1 and autophagy.

Methods

All animal experiments were approved by the Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of the Army Medical University. All the animal studies strictly adhered to the relevant ethical regulations of the corresponding ethics committee. Sex/gender was not considered in the study design and analysis because this study was not designed to detect sex difference.

Cell culture and reagents

HepG2 and L02 cell lines were acquired from the American Type Culture Collection, and cultured in Dulbecco’s modified DMEM medium (GE Healthcare Life Sciences, Buckinghamshire, UK) supplemented with 10% FBS (GE Healthcare Life Sciences), streptomycin (100 μg/mL) and penicillin (100 U/mL). Cells were rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and grown for 0.5 to 3 h in EBSS (Sigma-Aldrich) to simulate starvation. After receiving these cell lines, they were authenticated by STR analysis and used for experiments within 6 months after receipt. Cells were separately incubated with small molecule inhibitors purchased from Selleck (Houston, USA), including 20 μM CQ, 10 μM MG132, 0.5 μM TSA, 100 μM NAM, 10 μM U0126, 10 μM BEZ235, 1 μM SP600125, 10 μM Cisplatin and 10 μM CHX as indicated. Transient transfection of HepG2 and L02 cells with expression vectors was performed using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, USA) as recommended by the supplier.

OTUB1-knockout cells

Western blotting and immunoprecipitation

Whole-cell extracts were prepared by direct lysis of cells using cell lysis buffer (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, USA, #87787). The lysates were boiled in 6× SDS loading buffer for 10 min and separated by SDS-PAGE. A total of 25 μg of protein was analyzed per sample. The proteins were then electrotransferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Bio-Rad). The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk at room temperature for 1 h and then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After three washes with PBST (phosphate-buffered saline, 0.1% Tween 20), the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. Western blots were detected and imaged using the ChemiDoc Touch Imaging System (Bio-Rad). For IPn, the Pierce Crosslink Magnetic IP/Co-IP Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific) was used. Cells were collected using a cell scraper and centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 10 min. For each sample, 1.5 mg of total protein in 500 μl of cell lysis buffer (pH 7.4, 25 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1% NP-40, 5% glycerol) was immunoprecipitated using 5 μg of an antibody against the protein of interest. Briefly, the antibody was bound to protein A/G magnetic beads and crosslinked. Next, 500 μl of diluted lysate was added to the tube containing the crosslinked magnetic beads and incubated overnight at 4 °C on a rotator or mixer. The final eluate was neutralized, boiled in non-reducing electrophoresis loading buffer for denaturation, and subjected to immunoblotting. The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBS-T and probed for the protein of interest. Protein band density was analyzed using Image Lab software. Ratios relative to the control were calculated based on three independent experiments.

Mass spectrometry

Regarding the identification of OTUB1 interacting proteins, HepG2 cells were transfected with the OTUB1 plasmid for 36 h, followed by treatment with control medium or high-glucose medium (supplemented with 9000 mg/L-glucose) for an additional 3 h. Cells were then lysed and subjected to Co-IP using an anti-OTUB1 antibody to pull down OTUB1 and its interacting proteins. The immunoprecipitates were boiled in 5× SDS-PAGE loading buffer, separated by SDS-PAGE, and stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue (Proteintech). Gel bands displaying differences between the control and experimental groups were excised and subjected to LC-MS/MS analysis at Applied Protein Technology. For liquid chromatography (LC), peptides were separated using an EASY-nLC system (Thermo Scientific). The eluted peptides were analyzed using a Q Exactive mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) operated in positive ion mode. Full MS scans (m/z 300–1800) were acquired at a resolution of 70,000 at m/z 100. Raw MS data were processed using Proteome Discoverer Daemon (version 2.5, Thermo Scientific). The database search parameters included searching against the UniProt human protein database (version 2023-01), allowing a maximum of two missed cleavages, with carbamidomethylation on cysteine as a fixed modification and oxidation on methionine as a variable modification. Regarding the identification of OTUB1 post-translational modification sites, for the identification of phosphorylation and ubiquitination sites on OTUB1, HepG2 cells were transfected with the OTUB1 expression plasmid for 48 h. Cells were then lysed and subjected to Co-IP using an anti-OTUB1 antibody to immunoprecipitate OTUB1. The immunoprecipitates were subsequently denatured and separated by SDS-PAGE. Following Coomassie Brilliant Blue staining, the target protein bands were excised and sent to Applied Protein Technology for LC-MS/MS analysis. The Uniprot_human database was searched using Mascot 2.2 software to identify protein modifications.

Histological and immunohistochemical staining

Liver tissues were fixed in 4% PFA, paraffin-embedded, sectioned, and stained with H&E. Immunohistochemistry for OTUB1, p-OTUB1 S118, DEPTOR, p-mTOR, LC3-Ⅱor p-ERK was performed on paraffin sections using respective primary antibodies and an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody. DAB staining (Abcam, ab64238) visualized the signals as per manufacturer’s instructions, with brown coloration indicating positive staining. In addition, liver tissues were frozen on dry ice at optimal cutting temperature and sectioned. These frozen liver sections were stained with Oil Red O to observe lipid droplet accumulation. Images were obtained using an optical microscope.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Cells were lysed with TRIzol reagent (ComWin Biotechnology, Beijing, China, #CW0580S), and total RNA was extracted. First-strand cDNA synthesis was performed using M-MLV transcriptase (Invitrogen, CA, USA, #C28025-011). mRNA levels were subsequently quantified by qPCR on a LightCycle® 96 (Roche) using SYBR Green Master Mix (TAKARA, Tokyo, Japan, #RR091A) in a 20-μL reaction volume, following the manufacturer’s instructions. The primer sequences were as follows: OTUB1 forward: 5′-TCGGTCCTATACAAGGAGTATGC-3′, reverse: 5′-GGTCTTGCGGATGTACGAGT-3′; GAPDH forward: 5′-ACGGATTTGGTCGTATTGGG-3′, reverse: 5′-CGCTCCTGGAAGATGGTGAT-3′.

Cell viability assay

Cells were seeded at ~2000 cells per well in 96-well plates and cultured overnight before being treated under the indicated conditions. Subsequently, cells were incubated in the dark for 1–3 h with CCK-8 reagent (Dojindo Molecular Technologies, MD, USA, #CK04). Absorbance at 450 nm was then measured using a microplate reader.

GFP-LC3 analysis

Cells were seeded in 12-well plates and co-transfected with GFP-LC3 plasmid along with other plasmids or siRNAs using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) for 24 h. After treatment with the EBSS for indicated times, cells were fixed with 4% formaldehyde for 10 min. For each of five randomly selected fields, autophagic cells (defined as those containing ≥5 GFP-LC3 puncta) were counted among 50 cells under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus IX81, Tokyo, Japan). Representative images were captured using a laser confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss AG, Oberkochen, Germany).

Fluorescent assay of autophagosomes

Autophagy in live cells was analyzed using the Cyto-ID autophagy detection kit (Enzo, NY, USA), following the manufacturer’s protocol. The kit employs specific probes to stain autophagosomes. Cells were harvested, resuspended in 500 µL of freshly diluted Cyto-ID green detection reagent, and incubated at room temperature in the dark for 30 min. Fluorescence intensity was then measured by flow cytometry (Beckman, CA, USA).

In vitro kinase assay

HepG2 cells were transiently transfected with plasmids encoding FLAG-ERK-WT or FLAG-ERK-K52R mutant using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Following a 48 h incubation, cells were harvested and disrupted in lysis buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 0.5% Nonidet P-40, 250 mM NaCl, 3 mM EDTA, 3 mM EGTA) supplemented with 2 mM DTT, 0.5 mM PMSF, 20 mM β-glycerolphosphate, 1 mM sodium orthovanadate, and 1 μg/mL leupeptin. Endogenous FLAG-ERK complexes were captured overnight at 4 °C with constant rotation using Sepharose-conjugated anti-FLAG antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, #3400). The precipitated FLAG-ERK proteins were re-suspended in 40 μL of 1× kinase buffer (Cell Signaling, #9802) supplemented with 200 μM ATP (Cell Signaling, #9804) and purified His-OTUB1-WT or –S118A protein. The kinase reactions were allowed to proceed for 30 min at 30 °C before being halted by the addition of 20 μL of 3× SDS loading buffer. Samples were subsequently denatured at 100 °C for 10 min and subjected to immunoblotting (IB) analysis following SDS-PAGE.

In vivo ubiquitination assays

To evaluate ubiquitination levels, HepG2 and L02 cells cultured in 10 cm plates were co-transfected with His-Ub, FLAG-TRIM29 and HA-OTUB1 constructs. After 48 h of transfection, cellular proteins were extracted on ice for 1 h using a highly denaturing lysis buffer (buffer A: 6 M guanidinium-HCl, 0.1 M Na2HPO4/NaH2PO4, 10 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 5 mM imidazole, and 10 mM β-mercaptoethanol). The resulting lysates were incubated overnight at 4 °C with 50 μL of Ni-NTA agarose beads (Qiagen, Valencia, CA). To eliminate non-specific binding, the captured beads underwent a stringent, sequential washing regimen: initially with buffer A, followed by buffer B (8 mM urea, 0.1 M Na2HPO4/NaH2PO4, 10 mM Tris/HCl pH 8.0, 10 mM β-mercaptoethanol), and twice with buffer C (8 mM urea, 0.1 M Na2HPO4/NaH2PO4, 10 mM Tris/HCl pH 6.3, 10 mM β-mercaptoethanol) containing either 0.2 or 0.1% Triton ×-100. Finally, His-tagged ubiquitinated conjugates were eluted using buffer D (comprising 200 mM imidazole, 0.15 M Tris-HCl pH 6.7, 30% glycerol, 0.72 M β-mercaptoethanol, and 5% SDS) and subjected to immunoblotting analysis.

In vitro ubiquitination assay

HepG2 cells transfected with SBP-FLAG-TRIM29 or SBP-His-OTUB1 were collected 36 h after transfection. Cells were subjected to sonication in RIPA-SDS buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.125% SDS, 0.125% sodium deoxycholate, 1% Triton ×-100, supplemented with protease/phosphatase inhibitors) and centrifuged at 9660 × g for 20 min. The cleared supernatants were incubated with Streptavidin beads (Beyotime, P2159) overnight. After four stringent washes, the bound proteins were gently eluted using 50 mM biotin for 2 h at 4 °C and visualized via SDS-PAGE. The enzymatic reaction was assembled in a mixture containing 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM DTT, 1 mM ATP, 50 μM ubiquitin (UBbiotech, UB-100H), and the enzyme cascade: 100 nM UBE1 (UBbiotech, UBE-024), 200 nM UBE2D1 (R&D, E2-616-100), and 200 nM UBE2D3 (Proteintech, Ag2278). Purified TRIM29 and OTUB1 (200 nM each) were added to initiate the reaction. After incubating at 37 °C for 2 h, the mixtures were quenched with SDS sample buffer, boiled for 7 min, and processed for western blotting.

Pull-down assay

SBP-His-OTUB1 (WT/S118A), SBP-FLAG-TRIM29, and GST-ANXA2 were individually expressed in HepG2 cells. For affinity purification under denaturing conditions, cells were lysed in RIPA-SDS buffer, followed by sonication and centrifugation at 9660 × g for 30 min. The resulting supernatants were separately incubated with streptavidin agarose or glutathione agarose. Bound proteins were eluted using 50 mM biotin or reduced glutathione, respectively, and subsequently verified by SDS-PAGE and Coomassie blue staining. Alternatively, for native complex purification, cell lysates were incubated with the respective agarose beads in binding buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM DTT, 1.5 mM MgCl2, 0.2 mM EDTA, and 10% glycerol) overnight at 4 °C. To remove non-specific binders, the beads were subjected to a stringent wash series: once with 1 mL of DPBS containing 0.1% NP-40, twice with 1 mL of buffer C (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 420 mM NaCl, 1.5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EDTA, 10% glycerol, and 0.1% NP-40), once with 1 mL of BC500 (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, 1 mM EDTA, 10% glycerol, and 0.1% NP-40), and finally twice with 1 mL of BC100 (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 100 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, 1 mM EDTA, 10% glycerol, and 0.1% NP-40). The target proteins were then eluted using the corresponding competitive ligands in TBS and analyzed by SDS-PAGE and immunoblotting.

BLI analysis

The binding affinities and kinetic parameters governing the interactions between OTUB1 purified from HepG2 cells and its partners (ANXA2 or TRIM29) were evaluated via BLI on an Octet RED96e instrument (Sartorius) at 37 °C. Biotinylated OTUB1 was captured on streptavidin (SA) biosensors and baseline-stabilized in PBST. To establish the binding profiles, the OTUB1-coupled sensors were exposed to a concentration gradient of recombinant ANXA2 (0.5–8 nM) or TRIM29 (125–2000 nM) to record real-time association. This was immediately followed by immersion in kinetics buffer to monitor complex dissociation. The association rate, dissociation rate, and equilibrium dissociation constants were extracted by globally fitting the reference-subtracted sensorgrams to a 1:1 binding model within the Octet Analysis Studio Software.

Mice, diets, fasting regimens, and treatments

All mice used in this study were on a C57BL/6J background. Unless otherwise specified, mice were housed in specific pathogen-free barrier conditions within individually ventilated cages under a 12-h light/dark cycle at a room temperature of 21–23 °C and humidity (30–70%), with ad libitum access to food and water. All experiments used male mice (8 weeks old) to minimize sex-related variability in MASH progression, as pilot studies indicated more severe liver pathology in males compared to females under Western Diet feeding. To establish the MASLD model and evaluate MASH progression, 8-week-old male mice were fed either a standard chow diet (KLIBA NAFAG, Switzerland), a Western Diet (WD; Research Diets, D16022301i: 40% kcal from fat, 20% kcal from fructose, 2% cholesterol), or a choline-deficient high-fat diet (CD-HFD; Research Diets D05010402i: 45% kcal fat, 0% choline) to assess the development of NASH. Mice fed a normal chow diet served as controls. For MASH prevention experiments, 8-week-old male C57BL/6J mice were fed either a chow diet or MASH-inducing diets (WD or CD-HFD) for 32 weeks, unless otherwise specified. During the experimental period, control mice had ad libitum access to food. In contrast, mice subjected to IF were deprived of food during the fasting periods according to the IF protocol. The IF (5:2) regimen specifically involved fasting for 2 days per week (food deprivation but ad libitum access to water). Mice undergoing the IF (5:2) regimen had ad libitum access to food on all non-fasting days. Notably, all mice were sacrificed in the fed state. Thus, fasted mice were euthanized 48 h (2 days) after the completion of the final fasting cycle. This ensured that fasted mice were also in a fed state at the time of sacrifice, consistent with the ad libitum (A/L) controls. All animal experiments were performed in accordance with the guidelines of the Laboratory Animal Center of the Army Medical University and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Army Medical University (No. AMUWEC20224701), and were conducted according to the standards outlined in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals prepared by the National Academy of Sciences and published by the National Institutes of Health. For the generation of genetically modified mice, OTUB1-Flox mice were generated using the CRISPR-Cas9 gene-editing system. These mice were then crossed with Albumin-Cre mice to generate hepatocyte-specific OTUB1 knockout mice (OTUB1-HKO). Hepatocyte-specific OTUB1 transgenic mice (OTUB1-HTG) were generated by microinjecting the Alb-OTUB1 construct into fertilized mouse embryos (C57BL/6J background). The primer sequences used for construction and identification were generated using the method described in a previous publication14. OTUB1-HKO, OTUB1-HTG, and wild-type C57BL/6J mice were all male and obtained from Cyagen Biosciences Inc., Guangzhou, China.

Measurement of blood/serum parameters

Blood was collected in serum separator gel tubes, and serum was isolated for analysis. Serum Alanine Aminotransferase (ALT) levels were measured using a DRI-CHEM 500i analyzer (Fujifilm, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Blood glucose levels were measured using a handheld glucometer (Accu-Chek Aviva, Roche).

Measurement of hepatic triglycerides

To extract hepatic triglycerides, approximately 25 mg of snap-frozen liver tissue was homogenized in 0.5 mL of 5% NP-40 solution using a QIAGEN TissueLyser II. The homogenate was then slowly heated to 100 °C twice. After centrifugation, the supernatant was collected, and hepatic triglyceride content was measured using a DRI-CHEM 500i analyzer (Fujifilm, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Body-mass composition and metabolic phenotyping

Whole-body composition was non-invasively determined using an EchoMRI™ Whole Body Composition Analyzer (Echo Medical Systems, Houston, TX). The primary accumulation setting was 1T4. Mouse body weight was measured immediately prior to EchoMRI analysis to calculate the percentage of fat mass and lean mass. For comprehensive metabolic phenotyping, mice were subjected to indirect calorimetry using a PhenoMaster system (TSE Systems). Briefly, mice were singly housed during the analysis. Following a 2–3 day acclimatization period, food intake, water intake, oxygen (O2) consumption, carbon dioxide (CO2) production, respiratory exchange ratio, and total locomotor activity were continuously monitored for at least one week, with data recorded as 5-min averages per hour. Subsequently, Analysis of Covariance was performed using SigmaPlot (version 14.0) to statistically correct for the potential confounding influence of body weight on the measured metabolic parameters.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as means ± SD from at least three independent experiments. All graphs were made on GraphPad Prism 9.0 software (GraphPad Software, CA, USA). Statistical comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s or Dunnett’s test. Pearson Chi-squared test were used to assess the correlation between LC3-Ⅱ expression and p-OTUB1(S118) expression. Comparisons between two groups were made using Two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test. And P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For in vivo studies, sample sizes were determined based on statistical power and experimental feasibility, with randomization of animals and samples. Investigators were not blinded to group allocation during experiments or outcome assessment.
