Cut loci of Berger type Lorentzian structures
A. V. Podobryaev

Abstract
Consider the deformation of the standard Lorentzian metric on the anti de-Sitter space along the fibers of the Hopf fibration. We study the universal covering of this Lorentzian manifold to exclude a priori presence of time-like cycles. We describe the sets attainable by admissible curves and study the question of the existence of the longest arcs. Next, we investigate Lorentzian geodesics for optimality: we find the cut time and the cut locus. As a geometric application we compute the injectivity radius of the corresponding Lorentzian manifold. Keywords : Lorentzian geometry, Berger metric, Hopf fibration, cut locus, attainable set, geometric control theory. AMS subject classification : 53C50, 53C30, 49J15.



Introduction

Let us define the Lorentzian manifold we deal with. Consider the surface

\mathbb{H}^{1,n}=\{(z,w_{1},\dots,w_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{n+1}\,|\,-|z|^{2}+|w|^{2}=-1\},

where
w=(w_{1},\dots,w_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
|w|^{2}=|w_{1}|^{2}+\dots+|w_{n}|^{2}
.
This manifold equipped with the restriction from
\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
to
\mathbb{H}^{1,n}
of the quadratic form
-|z|^{2}+|w|^{2}
of the signature
(2,2n)

is called
the anti de-Sitter space
and is denoted by
\mathrm{AdS}_{2n+1}
. Notice, that the restricted quadratic form has the signature
(1,2n)
.

Next, consider the diagonal action of the circle, i.e., the Lie group
\mathrm{U}_{1}
,

e^{i\varphi}\cdot(z,w)=(e^{i\varphi}z,e^{i\varphi}w_{1},\dots,e^{i\varphi}w_{n}),\qquad(z,w)\in\mathbb{H}^{1,n},\qquad e^{i\varphi}\in\mathrm{U}_{1}.

We get
the Hopf fibration

\mathbb{H}^{1,n}\rightarrow\mathbb{H}^{1,n}/\mathrm{U}_{1}\subset\mathbb{C}P^{n}
.
In this paper, we consider Lorentzian quadratic forms on the manifold
\mathbb{H}^{1,n}
, i.e., nondegenerate quadratic forms with signature
(1,2n)
, that are deformations of the anti de-Sitter Lorentzian form along the fibers of the Hopf fibration.
More precisely, fix the point
o=(1,0)\in\mathbb{H}^{1,n}
, consider the tangent space
T_{o}\mathbb{H}^{1,n}=\{(ia,w)\,|\,a\in\mathbb{R},\,w\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\}
and the quadratic form

Q_{o}(a,w)=-I_{1}a^{2}+I_{2}|w|^{2}
on this space, where
I_{1},I_{2}>0
are fixed. Next, we define the quadratic form
Q
on the whole space
\mathbb{H}^{1,n}
by the shifts of the form
Q_{o}
via the transitive action of the Lie group
\mathrm{U}_{1,n}
on the manifold
\mathbb{H}^{1,n}
. Note, that this definition is correct, since the form
Q_{o}
is invariant under the stabilizer of the point
o
that is equal to

\left\{\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}1&0\\
0&A\\
\end{array}\right)\,\Bigm|\,A\in\mathrm{U}_{n}\right\}\simeq\mathrm{U}_{n}.

Thus, we consider invariant Lorentzian quadratic forms on the homogeneous space
\mathrm{U}_{1,n}/\mathrm{U}_{n}
. We call them
Berger type Lorentzian structures
by analogue with well known
Berger spheres

\mathrm{U}_{n+1}/\mathrm{U}_{n}\simeq S^{2n+1}
with Riemannian metrics deformed along the fibers
S^{1}
of the Hopf fibration
S^{2n+1}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}P^{n}

given by the
\mathrm{U}_{1}
-action on the unit sphere
S^{2n+1}\subset\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
. Cut loci for Berger spheres were found by C. Rakotoniaina
[
1
]
.

The considered Lorentzian structures are more complex than anti de-Sitter one. The classical anti de-Sitter space appears as a particular case when
I_{1}=I_{2}
and the corresponding homogeneous space
\mathrm{AdS}_{2n+1}=\mathrm{SO}_{2,2n}/\mathrm{SO}_{1,2n}
has a richer symmetry group.
For a general Berger type Lorentzian structure the sectional curvature is not constant in contrast with the anti de-Sitter space.
Recently the interest in such spaces is rising
[
2
]
.

Having a Lorentzian quadratic form
Q
on a manifold
M
one can define admissible curves and their lengths. A curve
\gamma:[0,t_{1}]\rightarrow M
is called
admissible
if almost all of its tangent vectors are
light-like
or
time-like
with respect to the Lorentzian quadratic form
Q
, i.e.,

Q_{\gamma(t)}(\dot{\gamma}(t))=0
or
Q_{\gamma(t)}(\dot{\gamma}(t))<0
for almost all
t\in[0,t_{1}]
and any velocity vector
\dot{\gamma}(t)
must be inside or on the border of the same component of the light-cone as the fixed time-like vector field (called
a time direction
).

The length of the curve

\gamma
is defined as

\int\limits_{0}^{t_{1}}{\sqrt{|Q_{\gamma(t)}(\dot{\gamma}(t))|}\,dt}
.

The Lorentzian distance
from the point
q_{0}\in M
to the point
q_{1}\in M
is the supremum of Lorentzian lengths of all admissible curves from the point
q_{0}
to the point
q_{1}
. If this supremum does not exist, then we say that the Lorentzian distance equals
+\infty
. If the curve where this supremum is achieved exists, then it is called
the longest arc
.

It is easy to see that the existence of an admissible cycle implies the non-existence of the longest arc. Thus, it is natural to consider simply connected Lorentzian manifolds. So, we investigate the universal covering of the homogeneous space
\mathbb{H}^{1,n}=\mathrm{U}_{1,n}/\mathrm{U}_{n}
which we denote by
M=\widetilde{\mathbb{H}}^{1,n}
.
The Lie group
\mathrm{U}_{1,n}
is homotopic to its maximal compact subgroup
\mathrm{U}_{1}\times\mathrm{U}_{n}
. Hence, for fundamental groups we have

\pi_{1}(\mathrm{U}_{n})=\mathbb{Z}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}=\pi_{1}(\mathrm{U}_{1}\times\mathrm{U}_{n})=\pi_{1}(\mathrm{U}_{1,n})
, this is the embedding to the second component.
It follows, that the universal covering
\widetilde{\mathrm{U}}_{1,n}
acts transitively on the space
M=\widetilde{\mathrm{U}}_{1,n}/\widetilde{\mathrm{U}}_{n}
and
the Lorentzian structure is invariant under this action.
Notice, that the universal covering of the anti de-Sitter space
\mathrm{AdS}_{2n+1}
is also often called the anti de-Sitter space.

Our goal is to find the longest arcs.
We use the following coordinates on the manifold
M
and the projection map:

\Pi:M=\{(c,w)\,|\,c\in\mathbb{R},w\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\}\rightarrow\mathbb{H}^{1,n}=\mathrm{U}_{1,n}/\mathrm{U}_{n},\qquad\Pi(c,w)=\left(\sqrt{1+|w|^{2}}e^{ic},w\right).

Since our structure is
\widetilde{\mathrm{U}}_{1,n}
-invariant, it is sufficient to consider the fixed point
\tilde{o}=(0,0)
of the manifold
M
as a start point for admissible curves.

The basic case is the three dimensional case
\widetilde{\mathbb{H}}^{1,1}
. Any case of bigger dimension
\widetilde{\mathbb{H}}^{1,n}
can be reduced to the three dimensional case with the help of the
\mathrm{U}_{n}
-symmetry.

We use methods of geometric control theory
[
3
]
. We find extremal trajectories called
geodesics
with the help of Pontryagin maximum principle. It turns out that geodesics are orbits of one-parametric subgroups of the group of isometries. Then we find the attainable set
\mathcal{A}
and study the question of the existence of the longest arcs on the attainable set. This depends on the ratio of the eigenvalues
-I_{1}
and
I_{2}
.
We refer to the case
I_{1}>I_{2}
as
the oblate case
, the case
I_{1}=I_{2}
as
the symmetric case
, and the case
I_{1}<I_{2}
as
the prolate case
.

Theorem A

We have the following description of the attainable set
\mathcal{A}
.

(1)
In the oblate case we can attain any point of the manifold
M
, i.e.,
\mathcal{A}=M
.
The longest arcs do not exist.

(2)
In the symmetric case

\mathcal{A}=\{(c,w)\in M\,|\,\arctan{|w|}\leqslant c\}.

The longest arcs exist only for the terminal points located in the set

\mathcal{A}_{exist}=\{(c,w)\in M\,|\,\arctan{|w|}\leqslant c<\pi-\arctan{|w|}\}\cup\{(\pi,0)\}.

(3)
In the prolate case

\mathcal{A}=\Biggl\{(c,w)\in M\,\Bigm|\,c\geqslant\atan{\Biggl(\frac{\tan{(\tau\sqrt{\eta})}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\eta}}\tanh{\tau}}{1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\eta}}\tanh{\tau}\tan{(\tau\sqrt{\eta})}}\Biggr)},\ \text{where}\ \tau=\arcsinh{\Bigl(|w|\frac{\sqrt{\eta}}{\sqrt{\eta+1}}\Bigr)}\Biggr\},

where
\eta=\frac{I_{2}}{I_{1}}-1
.
Any point of the set
\mathcal{A}
can be reached from the point
\tilde{o}
by the longest arc.

We prove item (1) of this theorem by constructing a sequence of constant controls to reach an arbitrary point.

In the symmetric case (2) the set
\mathcal{A}_{extr}
attainable by geodesics is well known.
It can be found in textbooks on the Lie theory, see a description of the Lie exponential map
\exp:\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\rightarrow\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, for example
[
4
]
.
Then, one can prove that any point of the set
\mathcal{A}\supset\mathcal{A}_{extr}
is attainable by admissible trajectories. Moreover, admissible trajectories can not leave the set
\mathcal{A}
,
since admissible velocities on its boundary are directed to its interior.
The attainable set in this case was described in paper
[
5
]
studying the sub-Lorentzian structure on the manifold
\widetilde{\mathbb{H}}^{1,1}
.
However, we put here this fact and its proof to illustrate the general situation.
But it seems to us that the geodesics optimality is not sufficiently investigated in this case.
Therefore, we explicitly indicate the set
\mathcal{A}_{exist}
where the longest arcs exist.
It remains to mention a similar study of two-dimensional anti de-Sitter space
[
6
]
which is an axial section of our symmetric case.

Next, we prove item (3) using the sufficient condition for the existence of the longest arcs on the attainable set developed in paper
[
7
]
.
It turns out that the longest arcs exist on the attainable set in this case.
Hence, thanks to the Pontryagin maximum principle the set
\mathcal{A}_{extr}
attainable by geodesics coincide with the whole attainable set
\mathcal{A}
.
Moreover, the set
\mathcal{A}_{extr}
has a simple description, since we know the equations of geodesics.

For the symmetric and the prolate cases we find
the cut locus
and
the cut time
, i.e., the set of points and the time where geodesics lose their optimality.
Since in the oblate case the longest arcs do not exist, then the cut locus question is senseless in this case.

Let us use the following conventions. Let us denote by
\mathfrak{g}
and
\mathfrak{h}
the Lie algebras of the Lie groups
\mathrm{U}_{1,n}
and
\mathrm{U}_{n}
, respectively.
Since the stabilizer
\mathrm{U}_{n}
is compact we have a reductive decomposition of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{m}\oplus\mathfrak{h}
,
where
\mathfrak{m}
is
(\Ad{\mathrm{U}_{n}})
-invariant and the subspaces
\mathfrak{m}
and
\mathfrak{h}
are orthogonal with respect to the Killing form
\Kil
on the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
,
see
[
8
, Prop. 1]
. Moreover, the subspace
\mathfrak{m}
can be identified with the tangent space
T_{\tilde{o}}M
.

Any geodesic is a projection of a trajectory of the Hamiltonian vector field on the cotangent bundle
T^{*}M
.
The initial data of such a trajectory is a covector from the space
T^{*}_{\tilde{o}}M\simeq\mathfrak{m}^{*}
which is canonically isomorphic to the annihilator
\mathfrak{h}^{\circ}\subset\mathfrak{g}^{*}
.
Thus, the cut time is a function
t_{\mathrm{cut}}:\mathfrak{h}^{\circ}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}\cup\{+\infty\}
.
Since the Killing form delivers the isomorphism
\mathfrak{g}\simeq\mathfrak{g}^{*}
,
we can consider it as a non-degenerate quadratic form on the space
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
which we will also denote by
\Kil
.

Theorem B

The cut locus and the cut time have the following description, where
\Kil
is the Killing form and
|h|=\sqrt{|\Kil(h)|}
for
h\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
.

(1)
For the symmetric case
I_{1}=I_{2}

\mathrm{Cut}=\{(\pi,0)\},\qquad t_{\mathrm{cut}}(h)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\frac{2\pi I_{2}}{|h|},&\text{if}&\Kil{h}<0,\\
+\infty,&\text{if}&\Kil{h}=0,\\
\end{array}\right.\qquad h\in\mathfrak{h}^{\circ},

there are no geodesics with initial covectors
h
such that
\Kil{h}>0
.

(2)
For the prolate case
I_{1}<I_{2}

\mathrm{Cut}=\left\{(c,0)\,\Bigm|\,c\geqslant\frac{\pi I_{2}}{I_{1}}\right\},\qquad t_{\mathrm{cut}}(h)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\frac{2\pi I_{2}}{|h|},&\text{if}&\Kil{h}<0,\\
+\infty,&\text{if}&\Kil{h}\geqslant 0,\\
\end{array}\right.\qquad h\in\mathfrak{h}^{\circ}.

We use the symmetry method developed in
[
11
]
to prove this theorem.
The main idea is to study the diffeomorphic properties of the exponential map for the corresponding optimal control problem and to find maximal domains in its pre-image and image diffeomorphic to each other.

This result allows us to compute the injectivity radius of our Lorentzian metric.
Recall that the injectivity radius is the supremum of the ball radii in
\mathfrak{m}^{*}
(with respect to some reference Riemannian metric
g_{R}
) such that the exponential map restricted to these balls is a diffeomorphism.

Theorem C

The injectivity radius is equal to

R_{inj}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}0,&\text{if}&I_{1}>I_{2},\\
2\pi\frac{I_{2}}{\sqrt{|\lambda|}},&\text{if}&I_{1}\leqslant I_{2},\\
\end{array}\right.

where
\lambda
is the negative eigenvalue of the Killing form with respect to the Riemannian metric
g_{R}
.

1
Optimal control problem in the three dimensional case

In this section, we discuss the place of the considered Lorentzian structure among possible invariant Lorentzian structures on the manifold
\widetilde{\mathbb{H}}^{1,1}
. After that we state the optimal control problem for the Lorentzian longest arcs.

First, note that the homogeneous space

\mathbb{H}^{1,1}=\mathrm{U}_{1,1}/\mathrm{U}_{1}=\mathrm{U}_{1,1}/\{\diag{(1,e^{i\varphi})}\,|\,\varphi\in\mathbb{R}\}

is equivalent to the homogeneous space

(\mathrm{SU}_{1,1}\times\mathrm{U}_{1})/\mathrm{U}_{1}=\left\{\left(A,\diag{(e^{i\varphi},e^{-i\varphi})}\right)\,|\,A\in\mathrm{SU}_{1,1},\,\varphi\in\mathbb{R}\right\}/\left\{\left(\diag{(e^{-i\varphi},e^{i\varphi})},\diag{(e^{i\varphi},e^{-i\varphi})}\right)\,|\,\varphi\in\mathbb{R}\right\},

where the stabilizer
\mathrm{U}_{1}
is embedded in the direct product
\mathrm{SU}_{1,1}\times\mathrm{U}_{1}
in the anti-diagonal way.
Indeed, there is the two-leaves covering

\Psi:\mathrm{SU}_{1,1}\times\mathrm{U}_{1}\rightarrow\mathrm{U}_{1,1},\qquad\Psi\left(A,\diag{(e^{i\varphi},e^{-i\varphi})}\right)=A\cdot\diag{(e^{i\varphi},e^{i\varphi})},

\Psi\left(\diag{(e^{-i\varphi},e^{i\varphi})},\diag{(e^{i\varphi},e^{-i\varphi})}\right)=\diag{(1,e^{2i\varphi})},

so the anti-diagonal stabilizer
\mathrm{U}_{1}
covers the stabilizer
\mathrm{U}_{1}=\{\diag{(1,e^{i\varphi})}\,|\,\varphi\in\mathbb{R}\}
twice.
Notice, that the homogeneous space
(\mathrm{SU}_{1,1}\times\mathrm{U}_{1})/\mathrm{U}_{1}
is the famous Selberg’s example of a weakly symmetric homogeneous space
[
12
]
.

In other words, we study a left-invariant Lorentzian structure on the universal covering of the Lie group
\mathrm{SU}_{1,1}\simeq\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
that is also
\mathrm{U}_{1}
-invariant, i.e., has the circle symmetry.
We denote this group by
G=\widetilde{\mathrm{SU}}_{1,1}
. Let us look at a general left-invariant Lorentzian structure on the Lie group
G
.

A left-invariant Lorentzian structure on a three dimensional Lie group
G
is defined by a nondegenerate quadratic form
Q
of the signature
(1,2)
on the corresponding Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
.
What are the parameters of this quadratic form? It is natural to diagonalize the quadratic form with respect to the Killing form
\Kil
.
But since the Killing form is also indefinite in our case, then it is not always possible. The famous Finsler’s lemma gives the sufficient condition.

Lemma 1
(
[
13
]
).

Let
R
and
Q
be quadratic forms on a finite dimensional vector space
V
. Assume that for any nonzero
v\in V
such that
Q(v)=0
it follows that
R(v)>0
.
Then there exists a number
\lambda
such that
R+\lambda Q
is positive definite.

If we assume that the quadratic form
Q
satisfies Finsler’s condition of Lemma
1
with respect to the Killing form
\Kil
or vice versa, then the following proposition implies that such a quadratic form is defined by three parameters.

Proposition 1
.

Let
Q
be a quadratic form on
\mathfrak{g}
with signature
(1,2)
.
Assume that the Killing form
\Kil(v)
has the same sign for any
v\in\mathfrak{g}
such that
Q(v)=0
.
Then there is a basis
e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}
of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
which is isomorphic to
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
such that

[e_{1},e_{2}]=e_{3},\qquad[e_{1},e_{3}]=-e_{2},\qquad[e_{2},e_{3}]=-e_{1}

(1)

and the form
Q
has the matrix
\diag{(-I_{1},I_{2},I_{3})}
in this basis, where
I_{1},I_{2},I_{3}>0
.

Proof.

There exists a number
\lambda
such that the quadratic form
S=\pm\Kil+\lambda Q
is positive definite due to Finsler’s Lemma
1
.
This implies that there exists a basis
f_{1},f_{2},f_{3}
of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
such that the quadratic forms
S
and
Q
have the matrixes

\diag{(1,1,1)}
and
\diag{(a,b,c)}
in this basis, respectively.
Hence, the matrix of the form
\pm\Kil
is equal to
\diag{(1-\lambda a,1-\lambda b,1-\lambda c)}
.
Since the Killing form has signature
(1,2)
, we may assume that
1-\lambda a<0
,
1-\lambda b>0
,
1-\lambda c>0
without loss of generality.
Then put
e_{1}=\frac{f_{1}\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\lambda a-1}}
,
e_{2}=\frac{f_{2}\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{1-\lambda b}}
,
e_{3}=\frac{f_{3}\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{1-\lambda c}}
.
In the new basis
e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}
the Killing form reads as
\diag{(-2,2,2)}
, while the form
Q
is still diagonal.
Due to Finsler’s condition the signs of its diagonal elements coincide with the signs of the elements of the Killing form.
So, the
Q
-matrix has the form
\diag{(-I_{1},I_{2},I_{3})}
for some
I_{1},I_{2},I_{3}>0
.
It remains to note that in the following basis of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
:

\bar{e}_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}i&0\\
0&-i\\
\end{array}\right),\qquad\bar{e}_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}0&1\\
1&0\\
\end{array}\right),\qquad\bar{e}_{3}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}0&i\\
-i&0\\
\end{array}\right),

the Killing form has the matrix
\diag{(-2,2,2)}
.
Moreover, the commutator relations of these elements coincide with (
1
).
So, the linear transformation from the basis
\bar{e}_{1},\bar{e}_{2},\bar{e}_{3}
to the basis
e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}
belongs to the Lie group
\mathrm{SO}_{1,2}
.
Whence, this transformation is a result of the adjoint action, in particular, it is an automorphism of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
.
It follows that the elements
e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}
have the same commutators.
∎

The left-shifts of the form
Q
give a left-invariant Lorentzian structure on the group
G
. We consider the corresponding Lorentzian problem as an optimal control problem where the goal is to find an admissible curve connecting the two given points that maximize the Lorentzian length computed with the help of the quadratic form Q.
We will call a curve
g:[0,t_{1}]\rightarrow G

admissible
if for almost all
t\in[0,t_{1}]
for its tangent vector we have
Q(L_{g(t)*}^{-1}\dot{g}(t)))\leqslant 0
,
where
L_{g}
is a left-shift by an element
g\in G
. In other words, admissible velocities are
not spacelike
.
Furthermore, an admissible curve must be future directed, i.e.,
the vector
\dot{g}(t)
and the vector of time direction
L_{g(t)*}e_{1}
are located in the same component of the non-spacelike cone.
Of course, due to left-invariance of the problem we may assume that the starting point coincides with the identity element
\mathrm{id}\in G
.
So, the problem reads as

\begin{array}[]{ll}g\in\Lip{([0,t_{1}],G)},&u\in L^{\infty}([0,t_{1}],U),\\
g(0)=\mathrm{id},\qquad g(t_{1})=g_{1},&\dot{g}(t)=L_{g(t)*}(u_{1}(t)e_{1}+u_{2}(t)e_{2}+u_{3}(t)e_{3}),\\
\int\limits_{0}^{t_{1}}{\sqrt{I_{1}u_{1}^{2}(t)-I_{2}u_{2}^{2}(t)-I_{3}u_{3}^{2}(t)}\,dt}\rightarrow\max,&\\
\end{array}\underline{}

(2)

where the terminal time
t_{1}
is free and
U=\{u=(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\,|\,I_{1}u_{1}^{2}\geqslant I_{2}u_{2}^{2}+I_{3}u_{3}^{2},\ u_{1}>0\}
is the control set.

The Lorentzian manifold in the symmetric case
I_{1}=I_{2}=I_{3}
is the universal covering of the well known anti-de Sitter space that is a homogeneous space

\mathrm{AdS}_{3}=\mathrm{SO}_{2,2}/\mathrm{SO}_{1,2}=\mathrm{SU}_{1,1}\times\mathrm{SU}_{1,1}/\mathrm{SU}_{1,1}.

The second equality here is due to the isomorphism
\mathrm{SO}^{+}_{2,2}\simeq\mathrm{SU}_{1,1}\times\mathrm{SU}_{1,1}
, see for example survey
[
14
, Sec. 3.1]
.
The corresponding Lorentzian quadratic form is just the Killing form. Indeed, the two copies of
\mathrm{SU}_{1,1}
act via left- and right-shifts, the stabilizer is the diagonal subgroup of the direct product and the Killing form is bi-invariant.
The anti de-Sitter space
\mathrm{AdS}_{3}
is a three dimensional Lorentzian manifold of constant negative sectional curvature.

In this paper, we consider a more complicate case that still has rotation symmetry, i.e., the axisymmetric case
I_{2}=I_{3}
.
The next proposition characterises the sectional curvature in our case, in particular, it is not constant.

Let us introduce a parameter
\eta=\frac{I_{2}}{I_{1}}-1

Below we denote the bilinear form associated with the quadratic form

Proposition 2
.

Assume that
I_{2}=I_{3}

Then the sectional curvature as a function of a two-dimensional plane

reads as

K(P_{w})=-\frac{1}{4I_{1}(\eta+1)^{2}}\left(1-\frac{4\eta Q(w,e_{1})^{2}}{I_{1}Q(w,w)}\right).

Proof.

Since the quadratic form
Q
is axisymmetric, then without loss of generality we may assume that

Hence, the plane

is spanned by two vectors:

So, we can compute
K(P_{w})=\frac{Q(R(u,v)u,v)}{Q(u,u)Q(v,v)-Q(u,v)^{2}}

R(u,v)u=\nabla_{u}\nabla_{v}u-\nabla_{v}\nabla_{u}u-\nabla_{[u,v]}u,

the Koszul formula that in the left-invariant case reads as

Q(\nabla_{e_{i}}e_{j},e_{k})={{1}\over{2}}\Bigl(Q([e_{i},e_{j}],e_{k})-Q([e_{j},e_{k}],e_{i})+Q([e_{k},e_{i}],e_{j})\Bigr)

Lorentzian geodesics

In this section, we write the Hamiltonian system for geodesics, it is convenient to do it in the general case.
Then we derive the parametric equations for geodesics in the axisymmetric case.

Introduce the following family of functions on the cotangent bundle

Theorem 1

3

\dot{\lambda}(t)=\vec{H}^{\nu}_{\hat{u}(t)}(\lambda(t))

(2)

H^{\nu}_{\hat{u}(t)}(\lambda(t))=\max\limits_{u\in U}{H^{\nu}_{u}(\lambda(t))}

(3)

Recall that a curve

Definition 1

A light-like geodesic

Instead of Lorentzian length one can consider the cost functional that quadratically depends on a control.
Such functional is much more practical since it does not contain a square root.
It turns out that extremal trajectories for the quadratic cost functional geometrically coincide with Lorentzian geodesics.

Proposition 5

A geodesic with an initial covector

Let us check that

As usual we denote by

\dot{g}(t)=L_{\exp{\frac{t}{I_{2}}\left(-h_{1}e_{1}+h_{2}e_{2}+h_{3}e_{3}\right)}*}R_{\exp{(-tZ)}*}\frac{1}{I_{2}}\bigl(-h_{1}e_{1}+h_{2}e_{2}+h_{3}e_{3}\bigr)+L_{\exp{\frac{t}{I_{2}}\left(-h_{1}e_{1}+h_{2}e_{2}+h_{3}e_{3}\right)}*}L_{\exp{(-tZ)}*}(-Z)=

Remark 2

The parametrization of geodesics from Proposition

This Proposition

Let us mention these general considerations in addition to the explicit proof of this Proposition.
Note that the group
\mathbb{R}
acts on

by rotations in the plane
\sspan{\{e_{2},e_{3}\}}

[

This means that the isometry group of the Lorentzian structure on the group

The corresponding isotropy subgroup is

Since the vertical part of the Hamiltonian vector field is tangent to the orbits of the isotropy group

then the geodesic corresponding to the initial covector

We have the following elements of the Lie algebra

(see Theorem 1 in

Let

(

Lorentzian manifold, and

be any fixed point and

\forall\,x\in M

Lemma 12
.

Assume that
\eta>0

Proof.

Let us apply Theorem
2

\frac{|v|_{R}}{\tau_{(c,w)}(v)}\leqslant 1+\dist_{R}{((0,0),(c,w))}

From Lemma
2
we know the admissible velocities

at a point
(c,w)\in G
.
Note that
|\omega|\leqslant\frac{\xi}{\sqrt{\eta+1}}

We have

\tau_{(c,w)}(v)=\frac{\xi\sqrt{1+|w|^{2}}+\Image{w\bar{\omega}e^{-ic}}}{\sqrt{1+|w|^{2}}}.

Hence, we can estimate

Moreover,

\leqslant\frac{\sqrt{1+|w|^{2}}}{\sqrt{\eta+1}}+|w|\leqslant\frac{1+|w|}{\sqrt{\eta+1}}+|w|,

4
Optimality of geodesics

In this section, we recall some basic definitions and the scheme for investigation of geodesics for optimality.

Any extremal is a trajectory of the Hamiltonian vector field
\vec{H}
on the cotangent bundle
T^{*}G
. Hence, an extremal is defined by its initial point, i.e., a point (called
an initial covector
) in the fiber
T^{*}_{\mathrm{id}}G\backsimeq\mathfrak{g}^{*}
of the cotangent bundle. This gives rise to the following definition.

Definition 2
.

The exponential map
is the map

\Exp:\mathfrak{g}^{*}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow G,\qquad\Exp(h,t)=\pi\circ e^{t\vec{H}}h,\qquad(h,t)\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}\times\mathbb{R}_{+},

where
e^{t\vec{H}}
is the flow of the Hamiltonian vector field
\vec{H}
and
\pi:T^{*}G\rightarrow G
is the canonical projection.

So, a geodesic can be written as
\Gamma_{h,t_{1}}=\{\Exp(h,t)\,|\,t\in[0,t_{1}]\}
for some
h\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
.

Definition 3
.

A time
t_{\mathrm{cut}}(h)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\cup\{+\infty\}
is called
the cut time for a geodesic with an initial covector

h
,
if
\Gamma_{h,t_{1}}
is optimal for
t_{1}\leqslant t_{\mathrm{cut}}(h)
and is not optimal for
t_{1}>t_{\mathrm{cut}}(h)
.
The corresponding point
\Exp(h,t_{\mathrm{cut}}(h))
is called
a cut point
.
The set of cut points for all geodesics starting at the identity point is called
the cut locus
and denoted by
\mathrm{Cut}
.

Our goal is to describe the cut locus and to find the cut time.
There are two reasons for a geodesic to lose optimality. The first one is existence of Maxwell points.

Definition 4
.

A time
t_{\mathrm{max}}(h)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\cup\{+\infty\}
is called
a Maxwell time
if there exist two different geodesics with initial covectors
h
and
\hat{h}
meeting one another at the time
t_{\mathrm{max}}(h)=t_{\mathrm{max}}(\hat{h})
with the same Lorentzian lengths. The corresponding point
\Exp(h,t_{\mathrm{max}}(h))=\Exp(\hat{h},t_{\mathrm{max}}(\hat{h}))
is called
a Maxwell point
.

It is well known, that after a Maxwell point an extremal trajectory can not be optimal, see for example
[
11
, Prop. 2.1]
.
That is why we are interested in the first Maxwell time.

The second reason is existence of conjugate points.

Definition 5
.

A time
t
is called
a conjugate time along a geodesic with initial covector

h\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
, if the point
(h,t)
is a critical point of the exponential map.
The corresponding point
\Exp(h,t)
is called
a conjugate point
.

After a conjugate point a geodesic loses local optimality. That is why we are interested in the first conjugate time
t_{\mathrm{conj}}(h)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\cup\{+\infty\}
and
the set
\mathrm{Conj}
of the first conjugate points along all geodesics starting from the identity point (
the first caustic
).

Usually Maxwell points appear for symmetric extremal trajectories. Let us give the formal definition.

Definition 6
.

A pair of diffeomorphisms

s:\mathfrak{g}^{*}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow\mathfrak{g}^{*}\times\mathbb{R}_{+},\qquad S:G\rightarrow G,\qquad\text{such that}\qquad\Exp\circ s=S\circ\Exp

is called
a symmetry of the exponential map
.

Proposition 10
.

The group of symmetries of the exponential map for problem
(
2
)
contains the group
\mathrm{SO}_{2}
that
acts by rotations around the axis
h_{1}
in
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
and keeps the time in the pre-image of the exponential map and
acts by rotations around the axis
c
in the image of the exponential map.

Proof.

The dual map for any such a rotation is an automorphism of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
. Thus, it induces a symmetry of the exponential map, see
[
17
, Th. 1]
.
∎

We will see below in Section
3
that these symmetries are enough to obtain the cut locus. More precisely, following
[
11
]
we prove that the exponential map restricted to the open set bounded by the first Maxwell time corresponding to these symmetries is a diffeomorphism on to the open set
\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{M}
,
where
\mathcal{M}
is the set of corresponding first Maxwell points. Hence, for any point of the set
\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{M}
there is unique geodesic coming to this point.
If we know that the optimal solutions on the attainable set
\mathcal{A}
exist, then this unique geodesic is optimal.
This will imply that
\mathcal{M}
is the cut locus.

The first conjugate time

Note that formulae (

c(\tau,h)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\cos{\tau},&\text{if}&\Kil{h}<0,\\
\cosh{\tau},&\text{if}&\Kil{h}>0,\\
\end{array}\right.\qquad s(\tau,h)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\sin{\tau},&\text{if}&\Kil{h}<0,\\

Note that the following equalities are satisfied:

c^{2}(\tau,h)-\ttt{(h)}s^{2}(\tau,h)=1,\qquad c^{\prime}(\tau,h)=\ttt{(h)}s(\tau,h),\qquad s^{\prime}(\tau,h)=c(\tau,h).

Theorem 3
.

The first conjugate time has the following properties.

(1)
If
\eta<0

\end{array}\right.

Proof.

A point
(h,t)\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}

\Pi\circ\Exp:\mathfrak{g}^{*}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow\mathrm{SU}_{1,1}

\mathrm{SU}_{1,1}

4

6

Moreover, the Jacobi matrix of the map

\frac{\partial{(q_{0},q_{1},q_{2},q_{3})}}{\partial{(\tau,\bar{h}_{1},\bar{h}_{2},\bar{h}_{3})}}\cdot\frac{\partial{(\tau,\bar{h}_{1},\bar{h}_{2},\bar{h}_{3})}}{\partial{(t,h_{1},h_{2},h_{3})}},

where the second multiplier is non degenerate. So, we need to find zeros of the Jacobian

Let us compute the partial derivatives:

\begin{array}[]{lll}\partial q^{e}_{0}/\partial\tau&=&(\ttt{(h)}-\eta\bar{h}_{1}^{2})s(\tau,h)\cos{(\tau\eta\bar{h}_{1})}-\bar{h}_{1}(1+\eta)c(\tau,h)\sin{(\tau\eta\bar{h}_{1})},\\
\partial q^{e}_{0}/\partial\bar{h}_{1}&=&-\tau\eta c(\tau,h)\sin{(\tau\eta\bar{h}_{1})}-s(\tau,h)\sin{(\tau\eta\bar{h}_{1})}-\tau\eta\bar{h}_{1}s(\tau,h)\cos{(\tau\eta\bar{h}_{1})},\\
\partial q^{e}_{1}/\partial\tau&=&(-\ttt{(h)}+\eta\bar{h}_{1}^{2})s(\tau,h)\sin{(\tau\eta\bar{h}_{1})}-\bar{h}_{1}(1+\eta)c(\tau,h)\cos{(\tau\eta\bar{h}_{1})},\\
\partial q^{e}_{1}/\partial\bar{h}_{1}&=&-\tau\eta c(\tau,h)\cos{(\tau\eta\bar{h}_{1})}-s(\tau,h)\cos{(\tau\eta\bar{h}_{1})}+\tau\eta\bar{h}_{1}s(\tau,h)\sin{(\tau\eta\bar{h}_{1})}.\\
\end{array}

It is easy to see that

J=s^{2}(\tau,h)\left(\frac{\partial q^{e}_{0}}{\partial\tau}\frac{\partial q^{e}_{1}}{\partial\bar{h}_{1}}-\frac{\partial q^{e}_{0}}{\partial\bar{h}_{1}}\frac{\partial q^{e}_{1}}{\partial\tau}\right)=s^{3}(\tau,h)\Bigl(-\tau\eta(\ttt{(h)}+\bar{h}_{1}^{2})c(\tau,h)-(\ttt{(h)}-\eta\bar{h}_{1}^{2})s(\tau,h)\Bigr).

Let us estimate the first positive root of the expression in the brackets. Assume that
s(\tau,h)\neq 0

, since otherwise
s(\tau,h)=0
.
Dividing the expression in the brackets by

we get that the first positive root of

\tau\eta\frac{\ttt{(h)}+\bar{h}_{1}^{2}}{\ttt{(h)}-\eta\bar{h}_{1}^{2}}=-\frac{s(\tau,h)}{c(\tau,h)}.

(15)

If

Consider now the case (c). If there is a subsequence such that

\end{array}\right.\qquad t_{\mathrm{cut}}(h)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\frac{2\pi I_{2}}{|h|},&\text{if}&\Kil{h}<0,\\

\end{array}\right.

Proof.

It follows from Propositions
12
–
13
that there is unique geodesic coming to an arbitrary point of the set

\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{M}
. From Proposition
14
and Proposition
9
(2) we know that the longest arcs exist.
This implies that the set
\mathrm{Cut}=\mathcal{M}
is in fact the cut locus.
Hence, we proved Theorem
B
in the three dimensional case.
∎

8
The injectivity radius

In this section, we compute the injectivity radius of our Lorentzian metric using the previous result on the cut time.

Let us recall the notion of the injectivity radius for Lorentzian manifolds. We follow
[
24
, Def. 2.1]
.
Recall that we denote our Lorentzian quadratic form by
Q
. The Killing form
\Kil
defines an isomorphism
\mathfrak{g}\simeq\mathfrak{g}^{*}
.
Thus, we can consider (with the help of this isomorphism) the quadratic forms
Q
and
\Kil
as quadratic forms on the space
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
.
Fix some covector
p\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
such that
H(p)=-\frac{1}{2}
and
p_{1}<0
.
We will call this covector
an observer momentum
, the corresponding velocity
(-p_{1}I_{1},p_{2}I_{2},p_{3}I_{2})
is the velocity of a time-like observer.
Consider the orthogonal complement to
p
with respect to the bilinear form associated with
Q
and denote it by
W=(\sspan{\{p\}})^{\perp_{Q}}
.
Note that the subspace
W
is space-like.
Define a positive definite quadratic form
g_{R,p}
as follows:

g_{R,p}(h)=-Q(h_{p})+Q(h_{W}),\qquad\text{where}\qquad h=h_{p}+h_{W}\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}

is a decomposition of an element
h
with respect to the direct sum
\mathfrak{g}^{*}=\sspan{\{p\}}\oplus W
.

Definition 7
.

The supremum of radii
r
such that the restriction of the Lorentzian exponential map
\Exp
to the Riemannian ball
B_{r}^{p}=\{h\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}\,|\,g_{R,p}(h)<r^{2}\}
is a diffeomorphism is called
the injectivity radius
R_{inj}(p)
with respect to an observer momentum
p
.

In other words,
R_{inj}(p)=\inf{\{t_{\mathrm{cut}}(h)\,|\,h\in B_{1}^{p}\}}
.

Theorem
(
C
.)

The injectivity radius with respect to an observer momentum
p
is equal to

R_{inj}(p)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}0,&\text{if}&\eta<0,\\
2\pi\frac{I_{2}}{\sqrt{|\lambda|}},&\text{if}&\eta\geqslant 0,\\
\end{array}\right.

where
\lambda
is the negative eigenvalue of the Killing form with respect to the Euclidian structure
g_{R,p}
.

Proof.

First, consider the case
\eta<0
. It follows from Remark
3
that
R_{inj}(p)=0
.

Second, consider the case
\eta\geqslant 0
.
It follows from Theorem
B
that

R_{inj}(p)=2\pi I_{2}\Bigm/\sup{\left\{|h|\,|\,h\in B_{1}^{p},\,H(h)=-\frac{1}{2},\,h_{1}<0,\,\Kil{h}<0\right\}}.

(16)

Let us consider an orthonormal basis with respect to the positive definite bilinear form associated with
g_{R,p}
that is orthogonal with respect to the Killing form
\Kil
.
The supremum in formula (
16
) is taken over an open set that is time-like with respect to the Killing form
\Kil
.
Hence, this supremum is achieved on the element
x
of this common basis such that the corresponding eigenvalue
\lambda=\Kil{x}
of the form
\Kil
is negative.
So, this supremum equals
\sqrt{|\lambda|}
.
Thus, the proof of Theorem
C
in the three dimensional case is complete.
∎

Example 1
.

The simplest case is
p=(-\sqrt{I_{1}},0,0)
, the corresponding velocity of the observer is directed along
c
-axis. We have already the required common diagonal forms for
g_{R,p}
and
\Kil
. So, the supremum from the proof of Theorem
C
is achieved on the vector
x=p
.
Whence,
\lambda=\Kil{p}=-I_{1}
.
It follows that
R_{inj}(p)=2\pi\frac{I_{2}}{\sqrt{I_{1}}}
.

9
General case

In this section, we consider the general case of the Lorentzian homogeneous space
\mathbb{H}^{1,n}=\mathrm{U}_{1,n}/\mathrm{U}_{n}
and its universal covering
M
and reduce the corresponding problem to the three dimensional case
\mathbb{H}^{1,1}=\mathrm{U}_{1,1}/\mathrm{U}_{1}
.

Lemma 13
.

The submanifold
\mathbb{H}^{1,1}=\{(z,w)\in\mathbb{H}^{1,n}\,|\,w_{2}=\dots=w_{n}=0\}\subset\mathbb{H}^{1,n}
is totally geodesic, i.e.,
the geodesics of the submanifold
\mathbb{H}^{1,1}
are also geodesics of the manifold
\mathbb{H}^{1,n}
.

Proof.

The submanifold
\mathbb{H}^{1,n-1}=\{(z,w)\in\mathbb{H}^{1,n}\,|\,w_{n}=0\}\subset\mathbb{H}^{1,n}
is totally geodesic, since
it is the set of the fixed points of the isometry
\diag{(1,\dots,1,e^{i\varphi})}\in\mathrm{U}_{1,n}
where
\varphi\notin 2\pi\mathbb{Z}
.
It remains to apply this fact many times.
∎

Lemma 14
.

(1)
Any geodesic of the manifold
\mathbb{H}^{1,n}
starting from the point
o
has the form
Ag(t)
for some
A\in\mathrm{U}_{n}
,
where
g(\cdot)
is a geodesic of the manifold
\mathbb{H}^{1,1}
starting from the point
o
.

(2)
The same is true for the submanifold
\widetilde{\mathbb{H}}^{1,1}\subset\widetilde{\mathbb{H}}^{1,n}
and
\widetilde{\mathrm{U}}_{n}
-action.

Proof.

(1) The stabilizer
\mathrm{U}_{n}
acts by isometries. Thus, it transforms geodesics to geodesics.
Moreover, it acts transitively on spheres in the subspace

\{(0,w)\,|\,w\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\}\subset\{(ia,w)\,|\,a\in\mathbb{R},\,w\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\}=T^{*}_{o}\mathbb{H}^{1,n}

of the initial covectors space.
Hence, choosing a suitable
A^{-1}
we can move a geodesic initial covector to the subspace

T^{*}_{o}\mathbb{H}^{1,1}=\{(ia,w)\,|\,w_{2}=\dots=w_{n}=0\}\subset T^{*}_{o}\mathbb{H}^{1,n}
.
The corresponding geodesic is a geodesic of the submanifold
\mathbb{H}^{1,1}
due to Lemma
13
.
The same arguments prove item (2).
∎

Lemma 15
.

The set attainable by geodesics for the general case has the same description as in the three dimensional case.

Proof.

Immediately follows from Lemma
14
,
Indeed, the conditions for the set attainable by geodesics in Theorem
A
include only
c
and
|w|
values.
But both of them are invariant under the
\widetilde{\mathrm{U}}_{n}
-action.
∎

Proposition 15
.

The attainable set for the general case has the same description as in the three dimensional case.

Proof.

Obviously, the
\widetilde{\mathrm{U}}_{n}
-orbit of the three dimensional attainable set is attainable in the general case.

In the oblate case the three dimensional attainable set coincides with the whole space
\widetilde{\mathbb{H}}^{1,1}
.
Thus, the attainable set in the general case coincides with the manifold
M=\widetilde{\mathbb{H}}^{1,n}
.

Let us denote by
C^{1,n}_{(c,w)}
the cone of the admissible velocities at a point
(c,w)\in M
in the general case
By the same argument as in Lemma
14
we may assume that
(c,w)\in\widetilde{\mathbb{H}}^{1,1}
without loss of generality.
Let
C^{1,1}_{(c,w)}
be the admissible velocities cone for the three dimensional problem on the manifold
\widetilde{\mathbb{H}}^{1,1}
.

Consider the symmetric case. Let us prove that the light-like geodesics sweep the boundary of the attainable set.
We should show that the cone
C^{1,n}_{(c,w)}
at any point
(c,w)
of this surface is directed inside this set
like in the three dimensional case, see Section
3.2
.
In the symmetric case, this is equivalent to the fact that the surface normal vector is located in the cone
C^{1,n}_{(c,w)}
.
Due to
\mathrm{U}_{n}
-symmetry the normal vector to this surface is the same as in Lemma
8
.
Next, Lemma
9
shows that this normal vector is located in the cone
C^{1,1}_{(c,w)}\subset C^{1,n}_{(c,w)}
.

In the prolate case we can prove the existence of the longest arcs on the attainable set using Theorem
2

the same way as in the three dimensional case, see Lemma
12
.
Namely, in fact this lemma gives the upper bound for the length of tangent vector such that their ends lie on the affine section
\tau_{(c,w)}=1

of the cone
C^{1,1}_{(c,w)}
.
Let us achieve the similar bound in the general case.
Remember that we assume without loss of generality that
(c,w)\in\widetilde{\mathbb{H}}^{1,1}
.
It follows that the cone
C^{1,n}_{(c,w)}
is symmetric with respect to the three dimensional subspace
\widetilde{\mathbb{H}}^{1,1}\subset\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}
and

C^{1,n}_{(c,w)}=\left\{\left(\xi+\frac{\Image{w^{T}\bar{\omega}e^{-ic}}}{\sqrt{1+|w|^{2}}},\ A\omega-iw\xi\right)\,\Bigm|\,\xi>0,\,\omega\in\mathbb{C}^{n},\,|\omega|\leqslant\frac{\xi}{\sqrt{\eta+1}}\right\},

where
A=\diag{(\sqrt{1+|w|^{2}}e^{ic},1,\dots,1)}
is a diagonal matrix with
n-1
unit elements.
So, for the bound (
14
) as in the proof of Lemma
12
we obtain

\frac{|v|_{R}}{\tau_{(c,w)}(v)}<\frac{\sqrt{C_{2}^{2}\xi^{2}+|A\omega-iw\xi|^{2}}}{C_{1}\xi}=\frac{1}{C_{1}}\sqrt{C_{2}^{2}+\left|A\frac{\omega}{\xi}-iw\right|^{2}}.

We have

\left|A\frac{\omega}{\xi}-iw\right|\leqslant\left|A\frac{\omega}{\xi}\right|+|iw|\leqslant\frac{|\omega|}{\xi}\sqrt{n+|w|^{2}}+|w|\leqslant\frac{\sqrt{n+|w|^{2}}}{\sqrt{\eta+1}}+|w|\leqslant\frac{n+|w|^{2}}{\sqrt{\eta+1}}+|w|,

where the first inequality is the triangle inequality and the second one is the Cauchy-Bunyakovsky-Schwarz inequality.
Following the rest part of the proof of Lemma
12
we get the following bound:

\frac{|v|_{R}}{\tau_{(c,w)}(v)}<\frac{C_{2}}{C_{1}}\left(1+\frac{1}{C_{2}}\left(\frac{n+|w|}{\sqrt{\eta+1}}+|w|\right)\right)=A+B|w|,

for some constants
A,B>0
.

Thus, the longest arcs exist on the attainable set.
It remains to repeat the argument from the proof of Proposition
9
(1), i.e., due to the existence of the longest arcs the attainable set has the boundary and this boundary consists of the light-like geodesics which have the same description as in the three dimensional case by Lemma
15
.
∎

Thus, due to Lemma
15
and Proposition
15
Theorem
A
is also true for the general case.

Let us now discuss the cut locus in the general case.
We need to show that if
(c,w)\in\widetilde{\mathbb{H}}^{1,n}
is an intersection point of two geodesics,
then the values
c
and
|w|
depend only on the Killing norm of the initial covectors of these geodesics (see the statement of Theorem
B
).

Lemma 16
.

Assume that two geodesics
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
of the space
\mathbb{H}^{1,n}
intersect at a point
(z,w)\in\mathbb{H}^{1,n}
.
Then there exist
g_{1},g_{2}\in\mathrm{U}_{n}
such that the curves
g_{1}\cdot\gamma_{1}
and
g_{2}\cdot\gamma_{2}
are geodesics of the space
\mathbb{H}^{1,1}
and
intersect at a point
(z,w_{1})
where
|w_{1}|=|w|
.

Proof.

It follows from Lemma
14
that there exist
g_{1},g_{2}^{\prime}\in\mathrm{U}_{n}
such that
the curves
g_{1}\cdot\gamma_{1}
and
g_{2}^{\prime}\cdot\gamma_{2}
are geodesics of the space
\mathbb{H}^{1,1}
.
Since
\mathrm{U}_{n}
-action keeps
z
-coordinate we get
g_{1}(z,w)=(z,w_{1})\in\mathbb{H}^{1,1}
and
g_{2}^{\prime}(z,w)=(z,w_{2})\in\mathbb{H}^{1,1}
. Moreover,
|w_{1}|=|w_{2}|=|w|
.
Hence, there exists an element
s\in\mathrm{U}_{1}
keeping
\mathbb{H}^{1,1}
such that
s\cdot(z,w_{2})=(z,w_{1})
.
So, we can put
g_{2}=sg_{2}^{\prime}
.
∎

Since the
\mathrm{U}_{n}
-action keeps the Killing norm we get from Lemma
16
that the statement of Theorem
B
is also true for the general case.

Finally, Theorem
C
in the general case follows from the fact that the Killing form
\Kil
, the Lorentzian form
Q
and the Riemannian form
g_{R,p}

(see the corresponding definition at the beginning of Section
8
) are
\mathrm{U}_{n}
-invariant.
