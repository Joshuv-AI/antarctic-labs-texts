Comparative evaluation of the Manchester Triage System and emergency severity index in predicting critical events in the emergency department
Authors: Anna Ingielewicz, Marzena Szarafińska, Petra M Grešner, Piotr Rychlik, Maciej Zając, Szymon Bielicki, Tomasz Stolarewicz, Mariusz Siemiński
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGiven the increasing number of patients presenting to Emergency Departments (EDs), the use of effective and reliable triage tools is essential. Such systems enable rapid assessment of the urgency of medical intervention, which contributes to improved workflow, optimized resource allocation, and potentially better clinical outcomes. This study presents a direct comparison of two of the most widely used triage systems globally: the Manchester Triage System (MTS) and the Emergency Severity Index (ESI), evaluating their classification agreement and predictive value for critical events in the ED population.MethodsThis retrospective study included 1,072 patients who were concurrently assessed using both systems during a transitional six-month period in which both MTS and ESI were applied in parallel at the study hospital. The correlation between triage categories assigned by each system was analyzed, as well as their association with predefined critical events.ResultsA moderate level of classification agreement was observed between the two systems (Cohen’s kappa = 0.51; Spearman’s rho = 0.49). ESI assigned over 80% of patients to priority level 3, whereas MTS distributed patients more evenly between levels 3 and 4. Both systems demonstrated a statistically significant association between higher acuity levels and an increased risk of critical events—lower category numbers (i.e., higher priority) corresponded with a greater likelihood of severe complications.ConclusionsThe results confirm the effectiveness of both triage systems in assessing patients’ clinical condition while highlighting important differences in their classification structures. These findings may inform the choice of triage system in clinical practice and underscore the need for further research on optimization and potential integration with artificial intelligence-based decision support tools. Keywords: Manchester Triage System, Emergency Severity Index, Clinical outcome, Triage accuracy, Risk stratification, Triage agreement

Introduction

In the context of a continuously growing number of patients presenting to Emergency Departments (EDs), the demand for effective, rapid, and reliable triage tools remains high [1]. Medical triage contributes significantly to identifying patients requiring urgent intervention [2], not only improving prognoses but also enhancing departmental organization and optimizing the use of limited resources, including medical staff, diagnostic testing, and hospital beds.

Despite the wide range of triage systems used worldwide, there is still no clear consensus on the superiority of any one system [3]. Evidence suggests that the most commonly used in-hospital triage systems offer comparable performance in terms of mortality, ICU admission rates, and hospitalization needs [4, 5]. However, individual system effectiveness may vary depending on clinical context, local resources, and staff experience [6, 7].

The Manchester Triage System (MTS) was developed in the UK in the 1990s [8]. It is based on 52 complaint-specific flowcharts, each corresponding to a chief complaint (e.g., shortness of breath, headache). Each chart includes five urgency levels, color-coded according to the maximum recommended waiting time for physician consultation: red – immediate, orange – 10 min, yellow – 60 min, green – 120 min, and blue – 240 min. Assignment is based on the presence or absence of specific descriptors such as signs of shock or oxygen saturation. MTS is relatively user-friendly, even for less experienced staff, although selecting the appropriate chart and analyzing descriptors can be time-consuming.

The Emergency Severity Index (ESI), developed in the US in the late 20th century [9], uses a single algorithm regardless of the presenting complaint. Classification occurs in four steps: A – identification of life-threatening conditions, B – assessment of high-risk situations, C – estimation of resource needs, and D – evaluation of vital signs (only for patients initially classified as level 3, to determine if escalation to level 2 is needed). ESI assigns patients to one of five urgency levels (1 = most urgent, 5 = least urgent), but only levels 1 and 2 have defined maximum waiting times (immediate and up to 10 min, respectively). While ESI allows for rapid triage, it is considered to require more clinical experience compared to MTS.

The aim of this study was to directly compare the two most commonly used triage systems—MTS and ESI—in an ED setting. One system served as the routine method in the studied department, while the other functioned as an additional assessment tool. The central question was whether the systems are equivalent in terms of effectiveness or whether one demonstrates advantages in specific aspects of clinical practice. To address this, a retrospective analysis was conducted involving 1,072 randomly selected patients triaged concurrently using both systems over a six-month period. Hospitalization course and clinical outcomes were subsequently compared between the groups. The study received approval from the Bioethics Committee of the Medical University of Gdańsk (approval no. NKBBN/143/2021).

Methods

The study was conducted in the emergency department of a specialist hospital located in northern Poland, serving a catchment area of approximately 240,000 residents. The ED admits around 40,000 patients annually and is staffed by four physicians and eleven nurses per 12-hour shift. It provides care for all acute medical and traumatic conditions across all age groups.

For many years, MTS was the standard triage system used in this ED. In July 2021, following nationwide organizational changes, ESI was implemented as the primary triage tool. During the six-month transition period from January 1 to June 30, 2021, both MTS and ESI were applied concurrently.

This retrospective observational study was based on the analysis of electronic medical records documented in the Clininet system. The data collection process is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Diagram illustrating the data collection process

All patients with dual triage documentation—regardless of age or mode of arrival—were included in the study. Patients were excluded if they were admitted via fast-track pathways for myocardial infarction (i.e., transported directly from the ambulance to the cardiac catheterization lab) or stroke (i.e., transported directly to the stroke unit), as well as those with incomplete medical records.

Demographic data, mode of arrival to the emergency department, and triage levels were collected for all patients. Additionally, the occurrence of critical events/diagnoses during hospital stay was assessed for each individual. Critical events were pre-defined as: (1) cardiac arrest in the ED (need for resuscitation and/or defibrillation) (2), ED death (3), in-hospital death (4), ICU admission directly from the ED or ward (5), surgery (6), PCI – percutaneous coronary intervention (7), thrombolysis (8), blood transfusion (9), vasopressor administration in the ED (continuous infusion) (10), initiation of invasive mechanical ventilation in the ED, and severe diagnoses in the ED: shock, sepsis, acute coronary syndrome, pulmonary edema, pulmonary embolism, stroke, multiple trauma. All statistical analyses were performed using R software [R Core Team (2024) using the following packages: irr (κ), stats/Hmisc (correlations, CIs by Fisher’s z), boot (bootstrap CIs), ggplot2 (visualizations). R: A language and environment for statistical computing. R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria. https://www.R-project.org/]. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. As the study was opportunistic (during a system change), no a priori sample size calculation was feasible. A post hoc precision analysis indicated that with n = 1,072 the minimal detectable correlation (α = 0.05; power 80%) was approximately r = 0.085. For the 5 × 5 χ² test, power was very high even for small effect sizes.

Nominal data—such as individual triage priorities and the frequency of major medical events during hospitalization—were presented as absolute numbers and percentages. For analytical purposes, MTS color categories were numerically coded as follows: MTS red = 1, orange = 2, yellow = 3, green = 4, and blue = 5.

Triage results from the MTS and ESI systems were compared to identify levels of agreement or discrepancy. Priority distributions were visualized using histograms, and inter-rater agreement was assessed using Cohen’s kappa statistic. Correlation analysis was conducted using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (rho) and the coefficient of determination (R²), indicating the proportion of variance in one system explained by the other. Chi-square tests were used to assess significant differences between the triage distributions, with significance determined via Monte Carlo simulation (100,000 iterations).

The predictive value of each triage system for serious in-hospital medical events was also assessed. Logistic regression models were applied to calculate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), evaluating the association between triage priority and event occurrence.

Results

During the study period, 1,072 patients met the inclusion criteria, accounting for 7.2% (n = 14,823) of all ED admissions. The study group consisted of 53% male patients (n = 569) and 47% female patients (n = 502), with a median age of 62 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 44–73). Among these, 66.3% (n = 711) were discharged home, 33.6% (n = 360) were hospitalized, 1.8% (n = 20) died in the ED, 2.1% (n = 23) died during hospitalization, and 1.1% (n = 12) required admission to the intensive care unit (ICU). Agreement between the systems was moderate. The overall Cohen’s κ was 0.51 (95% CI 0.47–0.55; p < 0.0001), and Spearman’s rank correlation was ρ = 0.49 (95% CI 0.44–0.53; p < 0.0001; Fig. 4), explaining approximately 24% of the observed variability (R² = 0.24). The distribution of patients across categories differed significantly (χ² test, p < 0.00001; Table 3).

Correlation between ESI and MTS results

Notably, the intermediate categories—particularly level 3—carry the highest risk of misclassification. Prior studies have indicated that ESI may more often underestimate severity in this group, which may partly explain the high proportion of patients concentrated in ESI-3.

The results suggest a statistically significant direct association between the MTS-assigned triage level and the occurrence of several critical events, including death in the ED, cardiac arrest in the ED, hospitalization, shock, pulmonary edema, stroke, ICU admission from the ED, the need for vasopressor infusion, and mechanical ventilation in the ED. Notably, patients assigned to the most urgent MTS categories (levels 1 and 2) had the highest risk of critical events, while those classified into less urgent categories (levels 4 and 5) had the lowest risk. In other words, the probability of experiencing a critical event decreased consistently as the triage level number increased. This trend is consistent across events, with the exception of MTS level 5, where the results were not statistically significant, likely due to the very small number of patients assigned to this category (Fig. 5).

The conclusion based on the ESI system results closely mirrors the findings for MTS: there is a statistically significant direct association between the ESI-assigned triage level and the occurrence of critical events. For example, in our cohort, over 60% of patients classified as ESI level 1 experienced at least one critical event, compared with around 35% in level 2, about 10% in level 3, and fewer than 2% in levels 4–5. Logistic regression confirmed this gradient, with odds ratios decreasing steadily from the highest- to the lowest-priority categories.

It is also worth noting that as the ESI level increases (i.e., indicating lower clinical urgency), the likelihood of each of these critical events consistently decreases. In other words, the higher the ESI level, the lower the risk of critical complications. This trend is only disrupted at ESI level 5, where results were not statistically significant—likely due to the very small number of cases assigned to this category (Fig. 6).

Discussion

An effective triage system should ensure that patients in the most critical condition receive appropriate medical care more promptly, which can be assessed by analyzing their subsequent clinical outcomes. This study compared the population of emergency department (ED) patients assessed at admission using two widely used triage systems—MTS and ESI. The analysis focused on the occurrence of predefined serious diagnoses, the necessity of invasive procedures, and critical events, linking these data to the originally assigned urgency categories.

To the authors’ knowledge, this study is one of the few that directly compares two triage systems without referencing an expert gold standard. The existing literature contains many analyses evaluating triage effectiveness by comparing inter- and intra-observer agreement [10, 11]; however, some researchers question the reliability of this methodology, emphasizing the superiority of hard clinical data [12].

It is noteworthy that most previous analyses have been based on simulated cases [13], which may not fully reflect the complexity and dynamics of a real ED environment. This study included a representative sample of patients from a full-profile ED, with men constituting 53% of the population—consistent with proportions reported in other analyses concerning MTS and ESI.

Compared to earlier publications, significant differences were observed in the distribution of patients across urgency categories. Less than 2% of patients were assigned the highest priority—this rate is lower than in other studies, such as those focusing on trauma patients, where the corresponding rates were 0.4% (MTS) and 1.8% (ESI) [14]. The proportion of patients assigned to the second priority was also relatively low—5.3% for MTS and 3.5% for ESI—compared to previous results ranging from 15.4% to 16.4% (MTS) and 11.1% to 15.9% (ESI) [15]. These discrepancies may be explained by local organizational conditions and the fact that the study was conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic, which may have influenced clinical decisions and triage organization.

The distribution of patients in intermediate categories revealed striking differences between the systems. In ESI, nearly 80% of patients were classified as category 3, creating a single large group, whereas in MTS patients were more evenly spread (42.3% in category 3 and 49.5% in category 4). This discrepancy likely reflects differences in the underlying algorithms and urgency assessment criteria. Importantly, category 3 is well known to carry the highest risk of misclassification, with prior studies reporting a tendency of ESI to underestimate severity in this group [16–18]. Such concentration produces a triage bottleneck, necessitating secondary stratification (e.g. short re-triage, specific protocols for high-risk ESI-3 patients, or referral to rapid diagnostic pathways). By contrast, the more even distribution in MTS facilitates queue management and resource allocation, although at the expense of somewhat longer application time. Overall, the choice between systems should consider the local patient mix, staff experience, and the acceptable trade-off between speed and granularity.

The use of advanced tools such as machine learning models may reduce these shortcomings. Studies have shown that AI-based algorithms better predict ICU admissions and in-hospital mortality, especially for patients assessed as less urgent [19].

This study demonstrated a consistent association between higher priority (lower category number) and increased risk of adverse clinical events such as death or ICU admission—consistent with findings from other authors [19–22]. For both MTS and ESI systems, a monotonic decrease in risk with lower priorities was observed (OR 9 and 7, respectively), confirming their accuracy in assessing patient severity.

The relationship between triage category and length of stay in the ED and hospitalization time was also statistically significant (p < 0.05), with lower priority associated with shorter treatment duration and reduced complication risk. An interesting observation was that despite being assigned the highest priority, these patients did not achieve better clinical outcomes—likely reflecting the severity of their condition rather than the effectiveness of interventions.

The findings confirm the usefulness of triage scales not only as tools for organizing the order of care delivery but also as clinical prognosticators. They also highlight the need for further research to improve triage systems, considering additional factors such as age, comorbidities, and technological support.

Limitations

Despite valuable observations, the study has several significant limitations. First, the single-center design may limit the generalizability of the results. Only ~ 7.2% of ED visits were included (those with both MTS and ESI), which may bias towards more complex cases. Second, the 6-month transition period may have introduced learning effects and temporal variation. The study coincided with the peak of the COVID-19 pandemic, which may have influenced admission thresholds and organizational factors.

A third limitation is the lack of an objective reference standard—predefined critical events were used instead of expert opinion. Additionally, triage was performed by a single paramedic, s this was the organizational model in our ED. Although this ensured uniformity in applying both systems, it may have introduced observer-related bias and limits the generalizability of the findings. The analysis did not account for re-triage due to the absence of such a procedure at the center. Furthermore, although the sample size was statistically significant, the absolute number of cases was limited mainly due to technical and organizational reasons. The lack of integration of electronic documentation systems forced manual data collection, limiting the number of analyzed cases.

Conclusions

The study demonstrated that both MTS and ESI effectively differentiate patients based on the risk of critical events. The clear association between assigned priority and clinical condition confirms their utility not only as organizational tools but also as prognostic indicators. Both systems showed a monotonic decrease in the risk of adverse events with lower priority levels. At the same time, the ESI system concentrated a larger proportion of patients in category 3, whereas MTS provided a more balanced distribution of classifications, which may have practical significance for ED workflow logistics. These differences may stem from differing classification algorithms and resource need assessments.

The results highlight the need for further multicenter research incorporating objective reference standards and the potential use of AI-based tools to increase triage decision accuracy and consistency. In the future, integrating decision support systems could be a key factor in improving the quality of prehospital and hospital care.
