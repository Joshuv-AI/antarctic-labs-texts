Oncogenic mutations in non-small cell lung cancer: from molecular mechanisms to clinical practice
Authors: Chaozhuo Li, Xiaoyan Yin, Shujie Zhou, Junxu Wen, Jinming Yu, Xiangjiao Meng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Targeted therapy has revolutionized the treatment of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients harboring oncogenic alterations, and next-generation tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) significantly increase blood-brain barrier (BBB) penetration, leading to improved disease control and survival benefits. However, several issues remain to be addressed, including the selection of more appropriate and individualized agents, the emergence of TKI resistance, and the challenging management of central nervous system (CNS) progression. In this review, we comprehensively summarize the efficacy and safety of TKIs and targeted specific antibodies in advanced or metastatic patients with common or rare mutations, providing guidance in selecting the optimal agent. We further explore the mechanisms underlying drug resistance and regimens aimed at overcoming resistance. In nonmetastatic resectable or unresectable NSCLC, we discuss the therapeutic potential of targeted agents and current issues requiring further research. In addition, we distinguish current and potential therapeutic strategies for patients with baseline brain metastasis (BM) and those without baseline BM who subsequently develop brain progression, leading to more effective and personalized treatment. Finally, we provide perspectives on challenges and future directions in the identification and treatment of oncogenic alterations, particularly in the context of co-mutations and the heterogeneous nature of metastases, and emphasize the clinical importance of artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted genetic testing and biomarker development.

Introduction

Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer morbidity and mortality worldwide, and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for 85% of lung cancer cases.1 Approximately 60% of patients with nonsquamous NSCLC harbor actionable oncogenic mutations.2–4 Mutation profiles differ between Asian and Western populations, with Asian patients exhibiting a higher prevalence of EGFR mutations and lower rates of KRAS G12C mutations.3,4 In recent years, advances in target discovery and detection technologies have further increased this proportion, presenting significant challenges for the clinical management of NSCLC patients harboring oncogenic mutations.

The discovery of activating oncogenes and the development of targeted therapies have transformed the treatment landscape for patients with NSCLC. The promising novel cancer therapies, tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), represent a breakthrough in the treatment of NSCLC with targetable oncogenic alterations, binding selectively to mutant gene sites, rarely affecting normal tissue surrounding the tumor, overcoming the limitations of chemotherapy and radiotherapy, and significantly reducing the metastatic burden in patients with advanced NSCLC.5,6 Advances in adjuvant and neoadjuvant targeted therapy have also improved the clinical outcomes of patients with resectable NSCLC.7–12 Currently, the utility of targeted drugs in the treatment of unresectable locally advanced NSCLC has also garnered increasing attention.13,14 Thus, targeted therapies have become the most promising first-line treatment option for patients with oncogene-activating mutations diagnosed at early or late stages.

Nevertheless, several challenges remain unresolved. First, the role of certain oncogenic mutations, such as EGFR or ALK mutations, in promoting tumor metastasis in NSCLC remains incompletely understood. Second, drug resistance poses a major challenge in treating NSCLC patients harboring oncogenic mutations, and no consensus has yet been reached regarding the optimal strategy for addressing TKI resistance. Moreover, systemic treatment failure with isolated intracranial progression represents a unique clinical scenario that requires careful differentiation to guide appropriate subsequent treatment strategies.

In this review, we summarize the available systemic and intracranial efficacy data on classical and novel targeted agents for common or rare mutations, explore the mechanisms underlying oncogenic mutation-driven metastasis and resistance, and discuss strategies to overcome TKI resistance in NSCLC patients harboring oncogenic mutations. We also focus on the utility of targeted agents in early-stage and locally advanced resectable and unresectable NSCLC. Additionally, further progression of central nervous system (CNS) metastases under TKI therapy has become a research priority, and this topic is specifically addressed in this review.

Targeted treatment for patients with advanced or metastatic NSCLC and oncogenic alterations

Multiple oncogenic mutations, such as EGFR, ALK, ROS1, KRAS, and BRAF mutations, have been identified in NSCLC.4 Targeted agents aimed at specific oncogenes have become increasingly potent for the treatment of NSCLC patients. Currently, more than thirty targeted agents have been approved by the FDA for marketing (Fig. 1). One of the most distinguishing features of this population is a higher frequency of baseline and cumulative incidence of brain metastasis (BM). This makes the selection of targeted agents particularly critical. The high BBB penetration of drugs, as well as the ability to induce rapid and durable remission of brain lesions, should also be considered in the management of patients. EGFR mutations

Epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) is activated by binding to ligands followed by dimerization and phosphorylation of EGFR, which activates a variety of downstream signaling pathways, such as the RAS/ERK signaling pathway, the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, and the STAT3 signaling pathway.15 EGFR mutations appear in 10–44% of lung adenocarcinomas and are associated with strong proliferation ability, insensitivity to chemotherapy, and poor prognosis.16,17 In patients with stage IV or recurrent metastatic EGFR-mutant NSCLC, the rates of BM at diagnosis are 24.4–31%.18–20 EGFR TKIs, which bind to the ATP-binding pocket of EGFR, can penetrate BBB, promote tumor cell apoptosis, impede tumor proliferation, and prevent metastasis by inhibiting EGFR phosphorylation and downstream signaling.21 Currently, various EGFR-targeting antibodies and antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) are emerging for the treatment of EGFR-mutant NSCLC and exhibit both extracranial and intracranial antitumor efficacy.

EGFR-sensitizing mutations

EGFR exon 19 deletion (19del) and exon 21 L858R (21L858R) mutations are sensitizing mutations, as tumors harboring these mutations are sensitive to most EGFR TKIs, especially third-generation EGFR TKIs. First- and second-generation EGFR TKIs

First-generation TKIs, including erlotinib and gefitinib, were the first to receive FDA approval for the treatment of patients with advanced NSCLC and EGFR 19del or 21L858R mutations. Although these TKIs exhibit superior clinical efficacy compared with traditional chemotherapy, drug resistance frequently occurs during treatment. Furthermore, afatinib, dacomitinib and icotinib are noninferior to gefitinib or chemotherapy in terms of median progression-free survival (mPFS).17,22,23 To overcome resistance and improve BBB permeability, third-generation EGFR TKIs have been developed to target 19del, 21L858R, or T790M mutations.

Third-generation EGFR TKIs

For NSCLC patients with acquired EGFR T790M mutation following prior EGFR TKIs, osimertinib has demonstrated favorable clinical efficacy and is also effective in patients with primary T790M mutation.24–27 In the FLAURA study, osimertinib exhibited superior overall response rate (ORR; 80% vs 76%) and CNS ORR (91% vs 68%) to first-generation EGFR TKIs and significantly reduced the rate of CNS progression (6% vs 15%) as first-line treatment for patients with EGFR 19del or 21L858R mutations.28–30 Rash and diarrhea were the most commonly reported adverse events (AEs) during osimertinib treatment. In terms of emotion, socialization and cognition, osimertinib also shows advantages over previous-generation drugs.31

Aumolertinib (almonertinib, HS-10296) shows antitumor activity against both the EGFR 19del/21L858R mutation and the T790M mutation. The AENEAS and APOLLO trials revealed great clinical efficacy and favorable safety profiles in both first-line treatment and subsequent treatment following EGFR TKI progression. Aumolertinib was associated with survival benefits in all subgroup analyses, including age, sex, and CNS metastases.32–34 Aumolertinib was associated with elevated blood creatine phosphokinase (CPK) levels and increased alanine transaminase (ALT) and aspartate transaminase (AST).

Lazertinib (YH25448), a potent, highly CNS-penetrant, third-generation EGFR TKI, is a substrate of MDR1/P-glycoprotein (P-gp), unlike osimertinib, which is a substrate of both BCRP and MDR1, suggesting that lazertinib may exert better intracranial tumor control than osimertinib.35 In the LAZER301 study, lazertinib significantly improved the median duration of response (mDOR; 19.4 months vs 8.3 months) and mPFS (20.6 months vs 9.7 months) compared with gefitinib, while the ORR showed no significant difference.36 In patients with baseline BM, lazertinib produced a promising CNS ORR of 94.4% and disease control rate (DCR) of 94.4%.37 Lazertinib also showed durable efficacy in EGFR T790M-positive patients after previous EGFR TKI treatment.38 According to several studies, lazertinib also appears to be superior to osimertinib in some safety aspects, including fewer skin toxicities, less hyperglycemia and reduced cardiac dysfunction.35,39

Furmonertinib, a highly selective and potent third-generation EGFR TKI, exhibits CNS activity in both first-line and later-line settings.40 In the FURLONG study, the mPFS with furmonertinib in treatment-naïve patients was 20.8 months, which was significantly longer than that of gefitinib.41,42 Although the median overall survival (OS) was not reached, the hazard ratio (HR) for OS was 0.94, indicating the potential for survival benefits. Whether the PFS benefit could translate into an OS benefit requires follow-up data.

AZD3759 (zorifertinib) strongly inhibits EGFR phosphorylation and controls BM and leptomeningeal metastasis (LM).43,44 A study of CTONG1702-arm 8 supported low-dose AZD3759 treatment (200 mg) and indicated that higher doses (300 mg) were unlikely to provide additional therapeutic benefits.45 Notably, EGFR T790M was the main resistance mechanism after AZD3759 treatment, and later-line osimertinib could provide survival benefits.45 The sequential use of AZD3759 and third-generation EGFR TKIs can be considered a new treatment option for TKI-naïve EGFR-mutant NSCLC.

Befotertinib had a similar systemic ORR and DCR to icotinib in NSCLC patients with EGFR-sensitizing mutations as the first-line therapy in the IBIO-103 study. However, befotertinib was associated with a higher intracranial ORR and mPFS, indicating that it can be used as a first-line treatment option, especially in patients with BM.46 The phase II IBIO-102 study demonstrated that NSCLC patients with the EGFR T790M mutation who progressed on EGFR TKIs also benefited from befotertinib.47,48

Limertinib (ASK120067), a novel third-generation EGFR TKI, exhibits potent BBB penetration ability comparable to that of other third-generation EGFR TKIs. In patients with confirmed primary EGFR T790M mutation or acquired EGFR T790M mutation after prior EGFR TKI treatment, the ORR and DCR were 68.8% and 92.4%, respectively, and mPFS was 11 months.49

Rezivertinib (BPI-7711), a novel and irreversible third-generation EGFR TKI, has shown favorable efficacy and tolerability in patients with NSCLC harboring the EGFR T790M mutation.50 In the phase III REZOR study, patients with EGFR 19del or 21L858R mutations treated with first-line rezivertinib achieved a significantly longer mPFS versus those in the gefitinib group (19.3 months vs 9.6 months, HR = 0.48, P < 0.0001).51

Rilertinib, another novel and selective oral third-generation EGFR TKI, was associated with a significantly longer mPFS than gefitinib (19.3 months vs 9.8 months, HR = 0.46, P < 0.0001), although the ORR was similar between the two groups (72.8% vs 78.3%).52

Fourth-generation EGFR TKIs

The abovementioned third-generation EGFR TKIs significantly prolonged survival compared with first- and second-generation EGFR TKIs in EGFR-mutant patients. However, resistance to third-generation EGFR TKIs is still a severe clinical problem. The secondary EGFR C797S mutation has been identified as the dominant resistance mechanism, and complex mutation combinations (e.g., EGFR C797S/T790M/19del/21L858R) pose a significant challenge in overcoming third-generation EGFR TKI resistance.53,54 Fourth-generation EGFR TKIs are currently undergoing preclinical and clinical development to further improve their inhibitory activities against EGFR-mutant cells harboring C797S, T790M, and common sensitizing mutations.

The phase I/II SYMPHONY study aimed to investigate the antitumor activity of BLU-945 as monotherapy and in combination with osimertinib.55 A clinical trial reported that patients with the EGFR 19del/T790M/C797S mutation could achieve tumor shrinkage after BBT-176 treatment.56 However, these two agents have been discontinued due to unsatisfactory clinical data. Other phase I/II studies (NCT05394831, NCT05552781, NCT05555212, NCT05393466, NCT05153408, CTR20221031, NCT05662670, NCT05256290, and NCT06631989) are ongoing to evaluate the safety and efficacy of JIN-A02, H002, QLH11811, BPI-361175, BLU-701, DAJH-1050766, WJ13404, BDTX-1535, and WSD0922-FU in patients with advanced NSCLC harboring the EGFR C797S mutation. The efficacy of EAI045, JBJ-04-125-02, JBJ-09-063, BI-4020, LS-106, CH7233163, and TQB3804 has not been sufficiently explored in clinical trials.

Overall, third-generation EGFR TKIs exhibit superior systemic and CNS activities in patients with NSCLC harboring classical sensitizing EGFR mutations (19del or 21L858R) or T790M mutations. The development of fourth-generation EGFR TKIs has generated much excitement and hope among NSCLC patients who are resistant to third-generation EGFR TKIs, although the antimetastatic effects against BM require further elucidation. Currently, combination regimens, such as EGFR TKIs combined with chemotherapy or antibodies, afford robust therapeutic options. This represents a potent treatment strategy, which we discuss in the following sections. Furthermore, responses to EGFR TKIs may be heterogeneous over time due to acquired resistance. The search for reliable predictive biomarkers associated with the response to TKIs is therefore essential to improve clinical outcomes and help detect the emergence of drug resistance mutations.

The results of the WU-KONG6 study investigating the efficacy of sunvozertinib for pretreated patients were recently reported, with a confirmed ORR of 61%.58 In the FAVOUR study, furmonertinib at a daily dose of 240 mg showed promising efficacy, with an ORR of 69.0% in treatment-naïve patients and 50.0% in pretreated patients.59 The phase III FURVENT trial is currently ongoing to compare furmonertinib and chemotherapy as first-line therapies. Zipalertinib (CLN-081, TAS6417) demonstrated preliminary efficacy after prior chemotherapy with or without prior amivantamab in the REZILIENT1 study, and the REZILIENT3 trial is now underway investigating zipalertinib plus chemotherapy as first-line treatment.60 Mobocertinib received accelerated approval for patients with EGFR ex20ins-mutant NSCLC who progressed on or after platinum-based chemotherapy in 2021.57 However, mobocertinib did not exhibit encouraging systemic efficacy compared with chemotherapy in the EXCLAIM-2 study in treatment-naïve patients.61 Owing to its limited efficacy, the use of mobocertinib has been withdrawn. Poziotinib, luminespib and tarloxotinib were also explored in patients with EGFR ex20ins mutations, although the latter two agents showed limited activity and failed to demonstrate benefits in clinical trials, potentially due to small sample sizes in these cohorts.62–64

Other rare EGFR mutations

The biological characteristics and therapeutic responses of other rare mutations within exons 18–21 of the EGFR gene, such as G719X, L861Q, and S768I, are currently being investigated. Currently, the EGFR TKIs afatinib and osimertinib have induced clinical remission in patients with these rare mutations (Table 2).

Afatinib and osimertinib for rare mutations

The LUX-lung 2, 3, and 6 trials, which evaluated the efficacy of afatinib in EGFR-mutant NSCLC, included patients with uncommon mutations. In these trials, patients with point mutations or duplications in exons 18–21 presented an ORR of 71.1% and mPFS of 10.7 months.65 The phase III ACHILLES/TORG1834 study revealed increased efficacy of afatinib over chemotherapy against rare EGFR mutations, especially compound mutations.66 The KCSG-LU15-09 trial revealed favorable activity of osimertinib in patients with uncommon EGFR mutations, such as G719X, L861Q, and S768I. The ORR was 50%, mPFS was 8.2 months, and mDOR was 11.2 months.67 Notably, a recent comprehensive analysis highlighted the use of afatinib for G719X, S768I, E709X, and L747X mutations and for uncommon compound mutations. For other uncommon mutations, such as L861Q, the use of a third-generation TKI, such as osimertinib, can also be considered on the basis of its favorable efficacy and toxicity profile.68 Aumolertinib induced ORRs of 12.5% and 47.1% in patients with EGFR ex20ins mutation and other rare mutations, such as G719X, L861Q, and S768I. The corresponding mPFS was 6.5 months and 11.8 months, and the mOS was 20.6 months and 29.4 months, respectively.69 A phase III study (NCT04951648) is ongoing to evaluate the efficacy and safety of aumolertinib compared with chemotherapy as first-line treatment in patients harboring uncommon EGFR mutations, including L861Q, G719X or S768I.

Treatment for PACC mutations

Notably, mutations on the interior surface of the ATP-binding pocket or C-terminal end of the αC-helix are categorized as P-loop and αC-helix compressing (PACC) mutations, including G719X, S768I, E709X, L747X, L792X, and T854I, which is currently a hotspot in the treatment of uncommon mutations.68,70,71 When PACC mutations are targeted, first-, second-, and third-generation TKIs yield ORRs of 37.2%, 59.6% and 46.3%, respectively.68 The FURTHER study is the first to evaluate the clinical efficacy and safety of furmonertinib as a first-line treatment for patients with EGFR PACC mutations. The confirmed ORR was 63.6%, and the DCR was 100% in the 240 mg dose group.72

Target-specific antibodies

Some target-specific antibodies enhance antitumor activity by blocking EGFR and other targets in NSCLC patients harboring EGFR-sensitizing or ex20ins mutations (Table 3). Amivantamab, an EGFR-MET bispecific antibody, can block the binding of receptors (EGFR/MET) to their ligands (EGF/HGF), induce the degradation of both receptors, and interact with immune cells to exert biological effects, such as antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC) and antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP).73 In the MARIPOSA study, the combination of amivantamab and lazertinib has emerged as an effective first-line therapeutic option for patients with EGFR 19del or 21L858R mutations.74 Additionally, first-line amivantamab combined with chemotherapy was associated with a superior ORR (73% vs 47%) and mPFS (11.4 months vs 6.7 months) than chemotherapy alone in patients with EGFR ex20ins mutations in the PAPILLON study.75 The EGFR/MET bispecific antibody MCLA-129 Combined therapeutic strategies with EGFR TKIs

EGFR TKI + chemotherapy

The FLAURA2 study is the first to report that osimertinib plus chemotherapy has greater efficacy than osimertinib alone in patients with 19del or 21L858R mutations.78,79 Although the combination regimen improved the systemic and CNS ORR as well as mPFS, the incidence of grade ≥3 AEs increased from 27% to 64% with the addition of chemotherapy, which indicates that the combination of osimertinib and chemotherapy is not appropriate for all patients. Despite this, the FLAURA2 study provides a new first-line treatment option for patients with advanced or metastatic EGFR-mutant NSCLC. The FOREFRONT study also aims to evaluate the combination of osimertinib and chemotherapy in first-line treatment. However, the TAKUMI trial revealed that the addition of chemotherapy to osimertinib as a second-line treatment did not improve the ORR or mPFS compared with osimertinib alone in patients with the EGFR T790M mutation after prior EGFR TKIs.80

Aumolertinib plus chemotherapy also significantly improved mPFS compared with aumolertinib alone (28.9 months vs 18.9 months, HR = 0.47, P < 0.0001) as first-line treatment in advanced EGFR-mutant NSCLC patients, although the incidence of grade ≥3 AEs was higher in the combination group than in the monotherapy group (75.7% vs 23.7%).81

EGFR TKI + anti-EGFR antibody

Amivantamab demonstrated more potent activity in combination with lazertinib than osimertinib in treatment-naïve EGFR-mutant (19del or 21L858R) NSCLC patients in the MARIPOSA study. The combination group and the osimertinib group achieved ORRs of 86% and 85%, and mPFS of 23.7 and 16.6 months, respectively.74 Venous thromboembolic (VTE) AEs were commonly reported with amivantamab plus lazertinib, occurring at a rate of 37%, and the majority of these AEs could be effectively controlled with anticoagulation therapy.74 In the MARIPOSA-2 study, amivantamab-chemotherapy and amivantamab-lazertinib-chemotherapy provided greater clinical benefits than chemotherapy alone for patients who progressed on osimertinib.82 Amivantamab also demonstrated promising antitumor activity in the CHRYSALIS study when it was combined with lazertinib for patients who experienced osimertinib relapse and had no prior chemotherapy, offering a novel therapeutic option after resistance to third-generation EGFR TKIs.83

MCLA-129 combined with osimertinib exhibited promising clinical efficacy as a first-line or subsequent treatment after progression on osimertinib, with systemic ORRs of 80% and 50%, respectively.77 The combination of osimertinib (160 mg) and JMT101, an anti-EGFR monoclonal antibody (mAb), yielded a confirmed ORR of 36.4%, DCR of 95.0%, and mPFS of 8.2 months in patients with EGFR ex20ins mutations.84

EGFR TKI + immunotherapy

Evidence regarding the efficacy of PD-1 or PD-L1 inhibitors in EGFR-mutant NSCLC is scarce and sometimes conflicting. The phase I CheckMate 012 study evaluated nivolumab combined with erlotinib in TKI-naïve and TKI-pretreated patients. Although both populations benefited from erlotinib plus nivolumab, toxicity was a critical issue.85 The TATTON trial aimed to evaluate osimertinib combined with durvalumab in EGFR-mutant NSCLC. Owing to the high incidence of interstitial lung disease, this combination was not considered feasible.86 The combination of TKIs and immunotherapy remains limited, mainly due to side effects, cost and potential risks. Further clinical trials are needed to evaluate the feasibility of the addition of PD-1 or PD-L1 inhibitors to targeted treatment for EGFR-mutant patients, and the dual toxic effects demand special attention.

In summary, the combination of specific antibodies with TKIs or chemotherapy appears to be a more efficient treatment modality than the use of specific antibodies or TKIs alone. Owing to its outstanding systemic efficacy, the combination regimen of amivantamab and chemotherapy has received FDA approval as a first-line treatment for patients with EGFR ex20ins mutations. Nevertheless, AEs induced by the combination treatment should be closely monitored despite the higher efficacy. To improve safety and convenience, the subcutaneous injection of amivantamab is currently under evaluation. In the PALOMA-3 study, subcutaneous amivantamab combined with lazertinib not only exhibited great efficacy but also had lower resource utilization and higher patient satisfaction than intravenous amivantamab.87

First-generation ALK TKIs

Crizotinib is the first FDA-approved ALK TKI for the treatment of ALK-positive NSCLC patients with local or distant metastasis, and has demonstrated superior efficacy to chemotherapy. However, the CNS activity of crizotinib in ALK-positive patients is limited, likely due to its poor BBB permeability.90

Second-generation ALK TKIs

The second-generation ALK TKIs, including ceritinib, alectinib, brigatinib, and ensartinib, have demonstrated robust antitumor activity in crizotinib-pretreated patients.91–94 The ASCEND-4, ALEX, ALTA-1L, and eXalt3 studies assessed the systemic and intracranial response of ALK TKI-naïve patients to ceritinib, alectinib, brigatinib, and ensartinib. The ORRs were 72.5%, 82.9%, 74.5%, and 74.1%, and the intracranial ORRs were 72.7%, 81.0%, 77.8%, and 63.6%, respectively.89,95–98 Each ALK TKI is associated with unique side effect profiles, such as the most common AEs in ceritinib (diarrhea, nausea, and vomiting), alectinib (anemia and elevated liver enzymes), brigatinib (gastrointestinal events and increased blood creatine phosphokinase), and ensartinib (rash). These adverse drug effects could influence decision-making regarding the determination of treatment, and thus warrant special focus.

Iruplinalkib (WX-0593) is a novel and highly selective ALK/ROS1 TKI. In the phase III INSPIRE study, it demonstrated promising efficacy in ALK TKI-naïve patients, with systemic and CNS ORR of 93.0% and 90.9%, respectively.99 According to the updated analysis, iruplinalkib significantly improved the mPFS compared with crizotinib (36.8 months vs 14.6 months, HR = 0.31, P < 0.0001).100 Iruplinalkib is also an effective subsequent treatment option that has favorable efficacy and manageable safety profiles in the INTELLECT study.101 The mOS reached 41.8 months after iruplinalkib treatment in ALK-positive crizotinib-resistant patients.102 Hypertension and hepatic function abnormalities are the most common grade 3/4 treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) after iruplinalkib treatment and require close monitoring.99

In the TQ-B3139-III-01 and REMARK studies, envonalkib and foritinib (SAF-189s) also showed superior ORR and mPFS to crizotinib in ALK TKI-naïve patients, suggesting their promise as first-line treatment choices.103,104 PLB1003 and conteltinib (CT-707) were evaluated in phase I clinical trials showing preliminary efficacy, with the latter exhibiting lower systemic and CNS activity than the previous second-generation TKIs like alectinib and brigatinib.105,106

Third-generation ALK TKIs

The third-generation ALK TKI lorlatinib (PF-06463922) enhances BBB permeability through the low-efflux pump P-gp and shows high intracranial efficacy.107 Lorlatinib induced an ORR of 47.0% and a CNS ORR of 63.0% in patients with ALK-positive NSCLC after progression on prior ALK TKI therapy.108 The ORR was 81%, and the CNS ORR was 92% in treatment-naïve patients in the phase III CROWN study. In patients with measurable BM at baseline, 58% achieved intracranial CR, which was significantly greater than the results of ceritinib, alectinib, and brigatinib obtained in ASCEND-4, ALEX, and ALTA-1L, which had 9.1%, 38.1%, and 27.8% intracranial CR rates, respectively.95–97,109 After 5 years of follow-up, the mPFS of patients receiving lorlatinib has still not been reached, making it the only ALK TKI with mPFS exceeding 60 months.

However, the safety of lorlatinib is an important issue that cannot be overlooked. Grade 3/4 AEs occurred more frequently in patients after lorlatinib treatment than in those after crizotinib treatment, largely due to hypertriglyceridemia, hypercholesterolemia, and weight gain. Therefore, antitumor treatment and dyslipidemia management (early screening and combination with lipid-lowering drugs) should be performed in parallel. Notably, lorlatinib treatment induced ALK inhibition accompanied by tropomyosin receptor kinase (TRK)-related AEs, which were caused by TRK inhibition. TRKB (a type of TRK receptor) inhibition may cause weight gain via the TRKB pathway, which regulates appetite centers, and has been reported to be associated with 23% of grade 3/4 AEs.109,110 Since TRK receptors are critical for neuronal differentiation and homeostasis, the inhibition of TRK by ALK TKIs such as lorlatinib is neurological. Although lorlatinib has potent anti-brain tumor activity, CNS AEs, such as cognitive, mood, speech, and psychotic effects, often arise after treatment with lorlatinib, which also contributes to the inhibition of TRKB in the CNS.109,111 In the CROWN study, all-cause CNS AEs occurred in 42% of patients receiving lorlatinib, with a particularly high incidence (67%) observed in those who had prior brain radiotherapy. However, the high incidence of AEs does not mean that they are unmanageable.109 Assessing psychological conditions and monitoring mood and cognition are important before, during, and after lorlatinib treatment, especially in patients who receive brain radiotherapy or who have a prior history of psychiatric illness. For patients with psychotic problems, adjusting the treatment dose in a timely manner is essential. Overall, lorlatinib is one of the first-line treatment options for ALK-positive patients, with better control of the occurrence and progression of BM, and CNS toxicity should be managed by monitoring and individualized dose adjustment.

Fourth-generation ALK TKIs

TPX-0131 and NVL-655 are new fourth-generation ALK TKIs, that show high abilities to penetrate the BBB and combat resistance mutations in prior generations of drugs, such as G1202R and L1196M.112,113 The phase I/II ALKOVE-1 trial of NVL-655 is changing the treatment landscape of patients who are resistant to third-generation ALK TKIs. NVL-655 is designed to be ALK-selective and TRK-sparing, as it inhibits ALK over TRK with 22-fold to >874-fold selectivity.111 NVL-655 exhibited efficacy in the ALKOVE-1 trial, and the preliminary overall safety profile revealed the avoidance of TRK-related neurologic AEs.114 However, phase I/II FORGE-1 trials of TPX-0131 have been discontinued because of unfavorable changes in risks and benefits.

Currently, owing to their superior systemic antitumor activity and brain-penetrating ability, the second- and third-generation ALK TKIs alectinib, brigatinib, and lorlatinib are preferred over sequential treatment with crizotinib. The treatment order also merits increased attention when choosing first-line TKIs, and treatment should be individualized. To solve drug tolerance, several potent new-generation TKIs are currently in the drug development pipeline, but novel TKIs should be used cautiously, and comparisons with other TKIs in terms of sensitivity to some resistance mutations are needed. Moreover, the toxic effects of these TKIs need great consideration when selecting targeted agents on the basis of individual patient conditions and disease progression.

First- and second-generation ROS1 TKIs

Crizotinib is the first TKI approved by the FDA for the treatment of advanced or metastatic ROS1-positive NSCLC and has favorable clinical efficacy.116 The ROS1/NTRK inhibitor entrectinib induced a durable response and provided clinical benefits for patients in the integrated analysis of the ALKA-372-001, STARTRK-1, and STARTRK-2 trials.117,118 Moreover, entrectinib was tolerated, with 42.9% of patients developing grade 3/4 TRAEs (grade 3: 39.3%; grade 4: 3.6%), and the most common grade 3/4 TRAEs were weight increases related to potent TRK inhibition. Early-onset neurologic AEs were observed but resolved in many patients within approximately 4 months.118 The BFAST trial revealed favorable efficacy of entrectinib, with an ORR of 81.5% and mPFS of 12.9 months, and the safety profile was generally consistent with previous reports.119

ALK/ROS1 dual inhibitors, such as SAF-189s, brigatinib, and ceritinib, exhibit systemic antitumor efficacy with CNS activity.120–122 Notably, hyperglycemia is the most common grade ≥3 AE related to foritinib, which might contribute to the binding of foritinib to the insulin receptor and subsequent signaling inhibition. However, patients who progressed on prior ROS1 inhibitors had poorer responses to the multitargeting inhibitor cabozantinib than to other inhibitors.123

New-generation ROS1 TKIs

The ALK/ROS1 inhibitor lorlatinib is effective in both TKI-naïve patients and crizotinib-pretreated patients.124–126 The most common grade ≥3 AEs reported in ROS1 patients after lorlatinib treatment were similar to those reported in ALK patients.

Taletrectinib is a next-generation, highly selective ROS1 TKI with CNS activity. Data from TRUST-I and TRUST-II revealed that in TKI-naïve patients, the ORR was 88.8%, and the mPFS was 45.6 months. In patients pretreated with ROS1 TKIs, the ORR was 55.8%, and the mPFS was 9.7 months. The most frequent TRAEs were increased AST (70%), increased ALT (67%), and diarrhea (61%).127 Notably, taletrectinib has potent brain-penetrating ability and anti-BM effects, and among patients with measurable baseline BM, the intracranial ORR was 76.5% in TKI-naïve patients and 65.6% in those who were TKI-pretreated.

Repotrectinib effectively targets ROS1 and TRK. In the TRIDENT-1 study, ROS1 TKI-naïve patients had an ORR of 79%, mDOR of 34.1 months and mPFS of 35.7 months, with a favorable safety profile. In patients with measurable BM at baseline, the intracranial ORR was 89%. In patients who had received one previous ROS1 TKI and no chemotherapy, the mPFS was 9.0 months, and the mOS reached 25.1 months.128 Like entrectinib, repotrectinib inhibits TRK receptors, thus leading to nervous system disorders such as dizziness and ataxia.

The novel ROS1 inhibitor NVL-520 has powerful antitumor activities, such as acting against multiple ROS1 mutations, including ROS1 G2032R, exerting potent brain-penetrating ability, and preventing TRK-related neurotoxicity.129 The ARROS-1 study revealed that NVL-520 induced an ORR of 48% in TKI-pretreated patients and exhibited potent activity in those with the ROS1 G2032R resistance mutation, with an ORR of 78%.130 Unecritinib (TQ-B3101) is a novel derivative of crizotinib and ROS1/ALK/c-MET TKI. The updated data of a phase II trial at the 2024 WCLC showed that the ORR was 81.1%, and the mPFS was 17.3 months.131 The promising data concerning the efficacy of novel TKIs provide new hope for novel therapeutic approaches in ROS1-positive patients.

Notably, because ROS1 shares high structural homology with ALK, some ALK inhibitors, such as crizotinib, ceritinib, and lorlatinib, can act on these two targets at the same time. However, these inhibitors are typically designed for specific kinases and active sites. Owing to structural differences, not all ALK inhibitors are effective against ROS1 fusion mutations. While some inhibitors can target both ALK and ROS1, their activities vary across these targets.

MET mutations

MET exon 14 skipping mutations

The binding of hepatocyte growth factor (HGF) to MET leads to MET phosphorylation and activates several downstream signaling pathways. The MET exon 14 skipping (ex14 skipping) mutations lead to the deletion of the binding sites for the tyrosine Y1003 residue and c-Cbl, impair c-Cbl-mediated degradation of MET and sustain MET activation.132 In NSCLC, MET ex14 skipping mutations occur in approximately 3–4% of patients.133 Approximately 35% of patients with MET ex14-mutant NSCLC have BMs at the time of metastatic disease diagnosis.134 TKIs and antibodies targeting MET ex14 skipping mutations are under development and have shown promising results (Tables 3 and 6). MET TKIs

Capmatinib showed clinical effectiveness in the GEOMETRY mono-1 trial. The mPFS (12.5 months vs 5.5 months) and mOS (21.4 months vs 16.8 months) were longer in treatment-naïve patients than in pretreated patients.135 The VISION trial analyzed the efficacy of tepotinib and revealed superior mPFS and mOS in pretreated patients than in control patients in the GEOMETRY trial.136 Peripheral edema was the most common TRAE in patients treated with capmatinib and tepotinib.

The KUNPENG trial reported an ORR of 77.1% for first-line vebreltinib (bozitinib, PLB1001, APL-101, and CBT-101), and 70.6% in later-line settings.137 Similarly, in the GLORY trial, gumarontinib (glumetinib, SCC244) achieved an ORR of 71% in the first-line treatment and 60% in later-line treatment.138 In a long-term analysis of savolitinib, there was no difference in mPFS between treatment-naïve and pretreated patients (both 6.9 months) with similar systemic ORRs of 54.2% and 52.6% respectively.139 Ensartinib can inhibit c-MET and exert potent efficacy against MET ex14 skipping mutations, with an ORR of 53.3%.140 The multikinase inhibitor crizotinib was assessed in an expansion cohort of an open-label phase I study in patients with MET ex14 alterations.141 Owing to its lower activity than other MET TKIs, crizotinib may not be the best treatment option for MET ex14 skipping mutations.

Bispecific antibodies

The novel EGFR/MET bispecific antibodies MCLA-129 and CKD-702 are effective in MET TKI-naïve and -resistant patients, with ORRs of 43.5% and 50%, respectively.76,142 Patients who were MET TKI-naïve had responses to REGN5093 and Sym015 treatment but not those who were resistant to MET TKIs.143,144 In addition, Sym015 showed a favorable safety profile, with 13.3% of patients experiencing grade ≥3 TRAEs.

MET amplification

MET copy number gains include polysomy and amplification. MET amplification indicates regional or focal gains in copy number in one specific arm of chromosome 7 without alterations in other regions.145 Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) is the standard method for detecting MET amplification. Currently, MET amplification in FISH is defined by the MET GCN or MET/CEP7 ratio, although the specific threshold value varies among studies.146 The former could not distinguish polysomy and amplification well, but the latter could. NGS is also used for MET amplification, with the advantages of identifying other genomic alterations and differentiating broad chromosomal gains from focal amplification. The cutoffs for determining MET amplification vary on the basis of the NGS platform used.147

Crizotinib, tepotinib, and capmatinib exhibited activity in patients with primary MET amplification (Table 6). Stratified by the MET/CEP7 ratio, patients received crizotinib and achieved ORRs of 38.1%, 14.3%, and 33.3% in the high (ratio ≥4.0), median (ratio >2.2–<4.0), and low MET amplification (ratio ≥1.8–≤2.2) groups, respectively.148 In the GEOMETRY mono-1 trial, capmatinib induced ORRs of 29%, 12%, 9%, and 7% in patients with MET amplification according to MET amplification levels (GCNs ≥10, 6 to 9, 4 or 5, and <4, respectively).133 Cohort B of the VISION trial evaluated tepotinib in patients with NSCLC and high-level MET amplification and reported favorable clinical efficacy, particularly for first-line treatment.149 However, MET amplification can co-occur with other oncogenic mutations, which may influence treatment choice.

MET overexpression

MET amplification or copy number gains are not equal to MET protein overexpression, although MET overexpression may co-occur with MET amplification.145,150 This may be related to lower levels of MET amplification, which do not result in substantial protein expression, or to MET protein expression, which is modulated at the posttranscriptional/translational level.147

A pooled analysis revealed that gumarontinib achieved ORRs of 41.7% and 35.0% in treatment-naïve and pretreated patients with MET overexpression, respectively (Table 6). Among them, gumarontinib led to a higher ORR in patients with concurrent MET overexpression and amplification than in those with MET overexpression but no amplification.151 The bispecific MET antibody REGN5093 also inhibited MET amplification or protein overexpression in the first-line treatment setting.143 Telisotuzumab vedotin (Teliso-V), a MET-targeting ADC drug, led to durable responses in EGFR wild-type patients with MET overexpression, especially those with high MET expression (Table 3).152 Currently, studies on the treatment of MET overexpression are limited whether in the first-line setting or in the later-line setting.

KRAS G12C mutation

KRAS is one of the most frequently altered genes in human cancers. Mutations in KRAS make it insensitive to GAP inactivation and allow it to remain in the active GTP-bound state, driving the activation of downstream signaling pathways and promoting the survival and migration of cancer cells.153 The most common KRAS G12C mutation occurs in approximately 12–14% of NSCLCs.154 Patients with recurrent or metastatic NSCLC and KRAS G12C mutation have a high frequency of BM (28%) at diagnosis.155 Thus, evaluating the brain penetrability of targeted agents against KRAS mutations is crucial (Table 6).

Sotorasib

In the CodeBreaK 100 trial, sotorasib provided long-term efficacy, with an ORR of 41%, DCR of 84%, mPFS of 6.3 months, and mOS of 12.5 months. The intracranial DCR of patients with measurable BM was 88%, which was similar to the systemic DCR.156 The CodeBreaK 200 trial further demonstrated that sotorasib significantly prolonged mPFS compared with chemotherapy in later-line treatment.157 Compared with docetaxel, sotorasib reduced the occurrence rate of grade ≥3 TRAEs and improved cough symptoms; however, hepatotoxicity events need to be considered. Notably, the study indicated that a longer duration between previous immunotherapy and the use of sotorasib could reduce the incidence of grade ≥3 TRAEs and hepatotoxicity events.

Adagrasib

In the 2-year follow-up of the KRYSTAL-1 trial, patients achieved an ORR of 43.0%, mPFS of 6.9 months, and mOS of 14.1 months after adagrasib treatment.158 Moreover, adagrasib has antitumor effects on patients with co-occurring mutations, including KEAP1, STK11, and TP53. The phase III trial KRYSTAL-12 further evaluated the clinical efficacy and safety of adagrasib compared with those of chemotherapy.159

Other KRAS G12C TKIs

Fulzerasib (IBI351, GFH925), glecirasib (JAB-21822), garsorasib (D-1553), sosimerasib (HBI-2438), and olomorasib (LY3537982) showed promising clinical efficacy in patients who failed standard therapy in phase I/II trials, exhibiting ORRs of 49.1%, 47.9%, 50%, 52.4%, and 39% in these groups.160–164 Liver function impairment is the main side effect of KRAS G12C TKIs. A phase III trial is ongoing comparing garsorasib with docetaxel in patients with prior standard therapy failure (NCT06300177). Among patients who were administered divarasib (GDC-6036), the confirmed ORR was 53.4%, and the mPFS was 13.1 months.154 The efficacy of opnurasib (JDQ443) is now being evaluated and compared with that of chemotherapy in the phase III KontRASt-02 trial and is also being investigated as a first-line treatment in patients with the KRAS G12C mutation, including the STK11 co-mutation (KontRASt-06).

KRAS G12C TKI + chemotherapy

Sotorasib plus platinum-doublet chemotherapy conferred potent and durable responses and exhibited a manageable safety profile in the first-line and second-line settings in the CodeBreaK 101 trial.165 The SCARLET trial evaluated sotorasib plus platinum-doublet chemotherapy in chemotherapy-naïve patients. In patients with PD-L1 expression ≥1% and <1%, the ORRs were 82.3% and 100%; the mPFS was 7.6 and 9.7 months, respectively.166

KRAS G12C TKI + immunotherapy

RAS signaling drives immune evasion, and KRAS inhibition can reverse immune evasion and promote remodeling of the TIME, which may increase sensitivity to antitumor therapies that promote immune responses.167 Thus, the modalities of KRAS inhibitors and immunotherapy may be reasonable. The CodeBreaK 100/101 trial evaluated the efficacy and safety of sotorasib combined with anti-PD-(L)1 immunotherapy, including pembrolizumab and atezolizumab, in KRAS G12C inhibitor-naïve patients.168 In addition to worse clinical outcomes, the combination of sotorasib and immunotherapy also led to a higher incidence of grade 3/4 TRAEs (especially liver function abnormalities) than monotherapy. Dose reduction and lead-in administration of sotorasib could help lower the incidence of grade 3/4 TRAEs.

KRYSTAL-7 investigated the efficacy of adagrasib combined with pembrolizumab as first-line therapy in patients with KRAS G12C-mutant NSCLC. Efficacy was more prominent in patients with high PD-L1 expression.169 The LOXO-RAS-20001 study explored olomorasib combined with pembrolizumab, and the ORR was 70% in the first-line metastatic setting.170 When KRAS G12C TKIs are combined with PD-1 or PD-L1 inhibitors, safety risks may increase. The combination of olomorasib and pembrolizumab was tolerable in patients in this trial. Currently, the phase III SUNRAY-01 trial is ongoing to evaluate the combination regimen of olomorasib and chemoimmunotherapy as a first-line treatment.

KRAS G12C TKI + anti-EGFR inhibitor

EGFR activation is one of the main mechanisms of resistance to KRAS inhibition.171 Cetuximab is a monoclonal antibody that targets the extracellular domain of EGFR.172 The KROCUS study investigated the combination of fulzerasib and cetuximab in the first-line setting, and patients achieved an ORR of 80% and DCR of 100%.171 The difference from the abovementioned combination regimens is that rash is the most common TRAE associated with fulzerasib plus cetuximab, whereas elevated liver enzymes are rare. Owing to its increased clinical efficacy and favorable safety, the combination of fulzerasib and cetuximab is expected to become the first-line treatment for patients with NSCLC and KRAS G12C mutation.

In addition to chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and anti-EGFR inhibitors, other KRAS upstream receptor inhibitors and their downstream pathway (PI3K/AKT/mTOR, RAF/MEK/ERK pathways) inhibitors have been developed and shown to have preliminary efficacy in KRAS G12C-mutant NSCLC. Patients with KRAS G12C-mutant NSCLC are generally managed with systemic therapies, such as chemotherapy and immunotherapy, as first-line treatments. Emerging targeted agents are changing the treatment landscape in first-line or later-line settings. However, the efficacy of TKI monotherapy is less than satisfactory. The combination regimens of KRAS G12C inhibitors with chemotherapy, immunotherapy, or upstream and downstream pathway inhibitors are being evaluated further and have demonstrated increased efficacy in first-line and later-line settings with favorable safety. However, whether combination regimens are more effective than KRAS G12C monotherapy still needs to be demonstrated in phase III trials.

BRAF V600E mutation

BRAF mutations have been reported in 1–5% of NSCLCs, and the BRAF V600E mutation accounts for approximately 50%, constitutively activating BRAF kinase activity and downstream ERK signaling.173,174 The combination of a BRAF inhibitor and a MEK inhibitor leading to the inhibition of the BRAF/MEK/ERK pathway has been shown to be effective in the treatment of patients with advanced NSCLC and the BRAF V600E mutation (Table 6).

BRAF inhibitor plus MEK inhibitor

The combination regimen of the BRAF inhibitor dabrafenib and the MEK inhibitor trametinib is highly effective in the first-line setting or following chemotherapy.175,176 The ORRs were 64% and 63%, and the mPFS was 10.9 months and 9.7 months, respectively. Notably, pyrexia and cutaneous effects, such as squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) of the skin, are common severe AEs (SAEs) associated with dabrafenib and trametinib treatment. Close follow-up, timely dosage adjustment, and effective symptomatic treatment are necessary. In the PHAROS trial, another combination regimen of encorafenib and binimetinib achieved ORRs of 75% and 46%, respectively, in treatment-naïve and pretreated patients.177 Compared with dabrafenib and trametinib, encorafenib and binimetinib cause less pyrexia.

Other RAF inhibitors

Current studies have demonstrated that patients with the BRAF V600E mutation achieve durable responses to vemurafenib, but its therapeutic effect is inferior to that of the two combination regimens.178,179 The first-in-human phase I study included advanced NSCLC patients harboring KRAS or BRAF mutations, but the efficacy of naporafenib (LXH254) in NSCLC and specific mutation types remains to be further studied.180

In general, the combination regimens of dabrafenib plus trametinib and encorafenib plus binimetinib exhibit great clinical efficacy in patients with BRAF V600E-positive NSCLC regardless of prior treatment. The two combination regimens can cross the BBB, induce an intracranial response, and relieve nervous system symptoms.181 Studies on the CNS penetrability of these targeted agents remain limited. Further clinical studies and an expanded BM population are needed to prove the intracranial effectiveness of targeted agents for BRAF V600E-positive NSCLC.

RET fusions

RET is a transmembrane receptor tyrosine kinase, and the gene fusions of RET lead to the constitutive activation of the RET tyrosine kinase domain and the activation of downstream pathways.182,183 RET fusions are identified in approximately 1–2% of NSCLC patients.184 The frequency of BM at the time of diagnosis in stage IV patients with RET rearrangements is 25%.185 RET inhibitors, including pralsetinib, selpercatinib, KL590586, SY-5007, and HS-10365, which have significant clinical activity and limited toxicity as well as the ability to induce an intracranial response, have been developed. Vepafestinib, TY-1091 and LOXO-260 are being investigated in phase I/II trials (Table 6).

Pralsetinib

Pralsetinib (BLU-667) demonstrated great clinical benefits for RET-positive NSCLC patients in first-line and later-line settings in the ARROW trial.186 The ORR was 72% and the mPFS was 13.0 months for treatment-naïve patients; the ORR was 59% and the mPFS was 16.5 months for patients with prior platinum-based chemotherapy. In addition to hematological toxicity, pulmonary-related AEs deserve special attention. Grade 3/4 treatment-related pneumonia was reported in 6% of the treatment-naïve population and 2% of the pretreated population. The phase III AcceleRET Lung study aims to further evaluate the efficacy and safety of pralsetinib as a first-line treatment. Unfortunately, pralsetinib was granted marketing authorization in the EU in 2021 for the treatment of NSCLC but was withdrawn in 2024.

Selpercatinib

In the LIBRETTO-001 trial, the ORR was 83% and 62% in treatment-naïve and pretreated patients after selpercatinib treatment, respectively, and the mPFS was 22.0 months and 26.2 months, respectively.187 The phase III LIBRETTO-431 trial further demonstrated that patients who received first-line selpercatinib had a significantly prolonged mPFS of 13.6 months compared with those who received platinum-based chemotherapy and pembrolizumab.188 In analyses of intracranial efficacy, 22 patients with measurable intracranial disease had an intracranial ORR of 82% (23% CR), indicating the high CNS activity of selpercatinib.187,189 A recent report also demonstrates that selpercatinib may have superior intracranial activity to pralsetinib and can be used as a new strategy to combat pralsetinib resistance.190

Other RET TKIs

SY-5007 showed preliminary antitumor efficacy in patients with advanced NSCLC and RET mutations, with ORRs of 71.4% and 58.3% in treatment-naïve and pretreated cohorts, respectively.184 The KL400-I/II-01 study demonstrated that the clinical activity of KL590586 was similar to that of SY-5007.191 Patients who received KL590586 were prone to developing grade ≥3 TRAEs, such as increased ALP/GGT and ileus, and were at a lower risk of developing hypertension than those who received SY-5007. HS-10365 showed promising efficacy, with ORRs of 83.3% and 66.7% in treatment-naïve and pretreated patients, respectively, in the primary analysis.192 Furthermore, other novel RET TKIs, such as vepafestinib, TY-1091, and LOXO-260, are being explored and show great antitumor activity against RET alterations, including resistance mutations. Further analyses are expected to illustrate the efficacy of novel TKIs in NSCLC patients with RET fusion mutations.

Several multitargeted kinase inhibitors, such as cabozantinib and alectinib, exert less therapeutic effects than selective and highly potent RET TKIs.193,194 These novel effective therapeutic agents with high RET inhibitory activity and high degree of brain penetration have great curative effects on these patients. For acquired RET fusion after resistance to other TKIs, the combination of an RET TKI and another TKI may serve as a treatment option.

HER2 mutations

Human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2; also known as ERBB2), a member of the EGF family, comprises an extracellular ligand-binding domain, a transmembrane domain, and an intracellular kinase domain. HER2 mutations occur in approximately 2–4% of NSCLC patients, most of which are ex20ins mutations.195 Approximately 19% of metastatic NSCLC patients with HER2 mutations have baseline BM at diagnosis, and this population has a greater risk of developing BM during treatment than those with KRAS and EGFR mutations.19 Currently, the treatments for HER2-mutant NSCLC include HER TKIs, HER2-targeted monoclonal antibodies and ADCs (Tables 3 and 6). TKIs

Pan-HER TKIs

Poziotinib is an oral, irreversible pan-HER TKI against HER1 (EGFR), HER2, and HER4. The systemic antitumor effects of poziotinib in patients with HER2 ex20ins mutations who progressed on systemic therapies were proven in the ZENITH20-2 trial.201 The treatment of BM is a clinical challenge for patients with HER2-mutant NSCLC. In this trial, 14 patients with BM had no intracranial progression after poziotinib treatment, and one had complete resolution of CNS lesions.201 The CNS activity of poziotinib was also demonstrated in treatment-naïve patients in ZENITH20 Cohort 4.202

Additional phase II studies have shown limited systemic activity of other pan-HER inhibitors, dacomitinib, pyrotinib, and afatinib, in HER2-mutant NSCLC patients, with ORRs of 12%, 30%, and 7.7%, respectively.203–205 A phase I/II FURTHER study is ongoing to evaluate the efficacy of furmonertinib in patients with HER2 ex20ins mutations. Diarrhea, skin rash, vomiting, and elevated liver injury markers are common after treatment with these TKIs.

The novel HER2 TKIs

BAY 2927088 and zongertinib (BI 1810631) are novel small-molecule TKIs that lead to a significantly greater response than other pan-HER TKIs in patients with NSCLC harboring HER2 mutations. In the SOHO-01 trial, treatment-naïve and pretreated patients with HER2 mutations achieved ORRs of 59.0% and 59.3%, respectively, after treatment with BAY 2927088.206 Zongertinib specifically binds to both wild-type and mutant HER2 receptors and spares EGFR.207 After zongertinib treatment at the 120 mg dose, the ORR reached 71% in the Beamion LUNG-1 study.208 A phase III Beamion LUNG-2 trial is ongoing to compare zongertinib with chemotherapy and immunotherapy.

Monoclonal antibodies and ADCs

The combination of macromolecular trastuzumab, pertuzumab, and docetaxel had a systemic ORR of 29% and DCR of 87% in patients with HER2 mutations.209 The most common grade ≥ 3 TRAEs were neutropenia, diarrhea, and anemia. The phase II DESTINY-Lung02 trial evaluated the efficacy and safety of trastuzumab deruxtecan (T-DXd) at two dose levels (5.4 mg/kg and 6.4 mg/kg) in previously treated HER2-mutant metastatic NSCLC patients and reported clinically meaningful outcomes, with ORRs of 50.0% and 56.0%, mDOR of 12.6 and 12.2 months, and mPFS of 10.0 and 12.9 months for the 5.4 mg/kg and 6.4 mg/kg dose groups, respectively.210 The safety profile was consistent with previous observations, and the lower dose demonstrated a more favorable safety profile. On the basis of these clinically significant results, T-DXd has been approved in more than 40 countries and regions, including the United States, the European Union, and China, becoming the first globally approved targeted therapy specifically indicated for HER2-mutant advanced NSCLC patients. In addition, iza-bren (BL-B01D1) and ado-trastuzumab emtansine (T-DM1) also showed favorable systemic efficacy in patients with HER2 mutations.211,212 In addition, SHR-A1811 has been explored in patients with HER2-mutant solid tumors, and its efficacy in NSCLC needs to be further investigated.213

Overall, patients with HER2-mutant NSCLC are commonly treated with systemic first-line therapies, and HER2-targeted agents have shown efficacy in subsequent treatment, conferring great remission rates and survival benefits. However, intracranial data concerning these agents are still limited. Therapeutic agents for HER2-positive NSCLC patients with BM remain an active area of investigation.

NTRK fusions

The incidence of NTRK fusions in NSCLC is 0.1–1% lower than that of other oncogene driver alterations.214,215 The fusion protein produced by the kinase domain of NTRK and the 5’ region of a partner gene leads to constitutive activation of downstream pathways, such as the PI3K, MAPK, and phospholipase C (PLC) pathways. NTRK inhibitors can block tyrosine kinases and inhibit downstream signaling. At present, larotrectinib, entrectinib, and repotrectinib have been approved for clinical use in NTRK fusion-mutant NSCLC (Table 6). To combat TRK-resistant solvent front mutations, several next-generation NTRK inhibitors, such as selitrectinib (BAY 2731954) and zurletrectinib, have been investigated in NTRK fusion-positive advanced solid tumors.

Larotrectinib

Subsequent analysis of some phase I/II trials revealed that larotrectinib was active in NTRK fusion-positive NSCLC.216–218 Treatment-naïve or pretreated patients had an ORR of 66%, mPFS of 22.0 months, and mOS of 39.3 months after treatment with larotrectinib.219 Larotrectinib induces a robust intracranial response not only in brain metastatic tumors but also in CNS primary tumors in NTRK fusion-positive patients.220,221 These findings indicate that larotrectinib has excellent CNS exposure and is an effective treatment option for patients with NTRK fusion-positive NSCLC and brain metastases. An expanding population is needed to explore the CNS activity of larotrectinib in NSCLC.

Entrectinib

A comprehensive analysis of three phase I/II trials (ALKA-372-001, STARTRK-1, and STARTRK-2) revealed that entrectinib induced a significant response in NSCLC patients with NTRK fusion, with an ORR of 64.5% and mPFS of 20.8 months.222 Entrectinib can penetrate the BBB and reach effective exposure concentrations in the CNS, and the intracranial data from these analyses support the preclinical data.223 Despite high intracranial activity, nervous system disorders caused by entrectinib treatment require attention, which can often affect patients’ treatment.224

Repotrectinib

NTRK mutations in the solvent front, the gatekeeper residue, and the xDFG motif lead to resistance to NTRK TKIs.225 The second-generation ROS1/TRK inhibitor repotrectinib showed great systemic efficacy in the phase I/II TRIDENT-1 trial, which included those with solvent front mutations, and the ORR was 79% in treatment-naïve patients and 38% in pretreated patients.226

Other TRK inhibitors

Some novel agents, such as zurletrectinib and selitrectinib, are currently being evaluated. A preclinical study demonstrated the superior brain-penetrating ability of zurletrectinib compared with that of repotrectinib and selitrectinib, but whether zurletrectinib confers superior efficacy in NTRK fusion-positive NSCLC patients with BM requires further clinical investigation.227

Overall, the FDA-approved agents entrectinib, larotrectinib and repotrectinib have shown great clinical activity in patients with NTRK fusion-positive NSCLC. Next-generation NTRK TKIs are under investigation, providing more selective options for patients receiving first-line or subsequent treatment after progression. Because NTRK fusion mutations occur in multiple malignancies, most clinical trials have been conducted in several solid tumors. Further analyses are needed to clarify the systemic and CNS efficacy of next-generation NTRK TKIs in NSCLC.

NGR1 fusions

Neuregulin 1 (NGR1) is a member of the epidermal growth factor (EGF) family. NGR1 fusions can activate HER2/HER3 signaling and promote the activation of additional downstream signaling pathways, further leading to oncogenesis.228 NGR1 fusions are relatively rare, with an incidence of 0.3% in NSCLC.229

Some studies have reported that patients with NSCLC harboring NGR1 fusions could benefit from afatinib, which could inhibit EGFR/HER2/HER4 and indirectly target HER3.230,231 GSK2849330 and zenocutuzumab (MCLA-128; Zeno), which are novel humanized antibodies that block HER3/NRG1 signaling in cancer cells, also demonstrated activity in NGR1 fusion-positive NSCLC patients (Table 3).232,233

In conclusion, investigations of oncogene-activating mutations and the development of targeted therapy have reformed the traditional treatment modality for patients with advanced or metastatic NSCLC in recent years, resulting in great clinical benefits. Currently, the use of TKIs combined with other targeted agents, chemotherapy or immunotherapy as first-line treatments is under active investigation, with the expectation of improving clinical benefit responses and prolonging survival.

Mechanisms of metastasis promoted by oncogenic mutations

Clinical evidence shows that EGFR-mutant patients have a significantly higher incidence of distant metastases to the brain and bone than EGFR wild-type patients.234,235 ALK-positive tumors also have a greater frequency of distant brain and nodal metastases than EGFR/ALK wild-type tumors, indicating that some oncogenic mutations confer a greater propensity for metastasis to specific organs than wild-type tumors.236,237 Next, we discuss the reported mechanisms of metastasis promoted by EGFR or other oncogenic mutations.

EGFR mutations can activate multiple signaling pathways related to tumor cell proliferation, invasion, and survival, such as the PI3K/AKT, MAPK/ERK, and JAK3/STAT3 pathways, and therefore enhance tumor metastasis.238 For example, the EGFR 21L858R mutation increases lung adenocarcinoma cell invasion ability through ERK activation of the CXCL12-CXCR4 axis, which is related to malignant pleural effusion formation.239 Activation of these pathways elicits a cascade of downstream responses. Matrix-degrading enzymes, such as matrix metalloproteinases, which can promote the degradation of the extracellular matrix (ECM) and the basement membrane, can act as downstream targets in the EGFR pathway, thereby promoting lung cancer cell migration and invasion.240,241 Tumor invasion and metastasis are closely associated with tumor angiogenesis, which is highly correlated with EGFR mutations via the upregulation of the vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) pathway.242 Therefore, antiangiogenic agents combined with TKIs may serve as effective tumor-targeting strategies. Some studies have reported that EGFR, ALK, or KRAS mutations can induce the expression of immune checkpoint molecules such as PD-L1 in NSCLC, promoting tumor cell escape from T-cell autoimmunity and leading to tumor progression.243–245 The expression of PD-L1 in EGFR-mutant NSCLC patients, compared with EGFR wild-type patients, is a matter of debate. Pooled analysis of studies from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) revealed that patients with EGFR mutations had lower PD-L1 expression in tumor tissue.246 Therefore, the upregulation of PD-L1 expression mediated by EGFR signaling may serve as one of the immune escape mechanisms for some patients. The upregulation of transcription factor expression is involved in tumor progression during lung cancer development and is also associated with the acquisition of mutations encoding many cell cycle-related proteins. Cell cycle dysregulation leads to abnormal proliferation of tumor cells and promotes tumor angiogenesis.247,248 TGF-β-induced factor homeobox 2 (TGIF2) affects the EGFR-RAS-ERK signaling pathway to increase the stemness of lung adenocarcinoma cells, thereby promoting tumor progression and metastasis.249

Moreover, EGFR mutations modulate the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) through growth factors, chemokines, and cytokines, including changes in the composition and function of immune cells, fibroblasts, and vascularization, leading to immune escape and tumor metastasis. Studies have shown that, compared with EGFR-wild type NSCLC, EGFR-mutant NSCLC is characterized by reduced proinflammatory immune cells and increased inhibitory immune cells, such as fewer CD8+ T cells and CD8+ TRM cells, more Treg cells, and M2 polarization.250–252 Moreover, the proportions of several types of cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) are altered in EGFR-mutant NSCLC.252 For example, the number of myofibroblastic CAFs (myCAFs) involved in fibrotic remodeling of the ECM is significantly reduced, which may result in an imbalance in ECM remodeling and increased invasion of tumor cells.252 EGFR mutations in NSCLC also impair macrophage phagocytosis, facilitating tumor escape through the upregulation of CD47 via the AKT and ERK pathways.253 On the basis of these studies, we illustrate how EGFR mutations promote lung cancer metastases (Fig. 5).Fig. 5Mechanisms of metastasis promoted by EGFR mutations. KRAS-mutant BM has a relatively immune-activated TIME with the highest infiltration of CD8+ T cells and CTLs and the lowest infiltration of M2 macrophages, which could explain the relatively high response rate of KRAS-positive tumors to immunotherapy.261

EGFR and MET signaling have been implicated in the pathogenesis of other metastases, such as bone metastasis and pleural metastasis, in NSCLC. The EGFR TKI erlotinib inhibits osteolytic invasion in bone metastasis by inhibiting osteoclast activation via suppression of tumor growth, osteolytic factor production, osteoblast/stromal cell proliferation and osteoclast differentiation.262 In KRAS G12D-driven NSCLC, downregulation of PGC1α promotes epithelial‒mesenchymal transition (EMT) and bone metastasis.263

Some oncogenic mutations, such as EGFR, ALK, or KRAS mutations, promote tumor metastasis not only through intrinsic aberrant signaling pathways but also by inducing changes in the TIME, thus leading to immune escape. Targeted agents and combination therapies have been proven effective by blocking active signaling pathways and remodeling the TIME. However, resistance to oncogenic mutations is usually inevitable in the clinic. Elucidating the complex molecular mechanism underlying drug resistance is important for the treatment of patients with oncogenic mutations.

Mechanisms of resistance to TKIs

Targeted therapy resistance can be classified into two types: primary resistance and secondary resistance. The development of primary or secondary resistance following targeted therapy is an inevitable clinical challenge, which invariably leads to disease progression in patients. Thus, overcoming drug resistance remains a major hurdle for targeted therapy in NSCLC, and an in-depth elucidation of the underlying resistance mechanisms may provide novel insights and therapeutic strategies for NSCLC.

Primary resistance

Exon 20 insertions in EGFR and HER2 induce a notable shift of the α-C helix into the drug-binding pocket due to insertions at the C-terminal end of the α-C helix, along with a shift of the phosphate-binding loop (P-loop) into the drug-binding pocket (Fig. 6).264 The alteration of the drug-binding pockets of exon 20 insertions results in the ineffective binding of conventional EGFR/HER2 TKIs, which renders EGFR and HER2 ex20ins mutations insensitive to conventional TKIs, leading to intrinsic resistance.264 Despite this, EGFR ex20ins mutations and HER2 ex20ins mutations are the main targets of treatment, and several small-molecule, highly selective agents for exon 20 insertions, such as mobocertinib, poziotinib, sunvozertinib, furmonertinib, and zipalertinib, which are mentioned above, are being developed with great clinical activity. EGFR-dependent resistance mechanisms include EGFR amplification and secondary mutations in the kinase domain. The mechanism of bypass pathway activation refers to the activation, hyperactivation, amplification or fusion of other oncogenic mutations and is a critical factor in secondary resistance in addition to primary resistance. The transformation to SCLC, SCC, SC, and EMT phenotypes also drives secondary resistance. The co-occurrence of rare EGFR mutations with sensitizing mutations may mediate primary resistance, such as the co-occurrence of a drug-sensitive G719C mutation and an E709A mutation.266 Concurrent bypass signaling activation, such as BRAF, HER2, and KRAS mutations, has been reported to cause primary resistance.265 The deletion polymorphism in the gene encoding BCL2L11 (BIM) also results in intrinsic EGFR TKI resistance.267 Abnormal cell cycle regulation, such as CCND1, CCND2, CCNE1, CDK4, and CDK6 amplification, as well as CDKN2A mutation, is involved in EGFR TKI resistance and acquired resistance.268,269 Moreover, the tumor suppressor p53 regulates cycle arrest, apoptosis and DNA repair, and the loss of p53 is associated with both primary and acquired resistance to EGFR TKIs.265

Primary resistance to ROS1 TKIs is uncommon and rarely reported. The ROS1 L1951R (analogous to ALK L1196M), S1986Y/F, and L2086F (analogous to ALK L1256F) mutations also hinder the binding of some ROS1 TKIs (Fig. 7).275 Additionally, ROS1/EGFR, ROS1/KRAS, ROS1/PIK3CA, ROS1/BRAF, and ROS1/TP53 co-mutations were also detected in patients with ROS1-positive NSCLC, which may confer intrinsic resistance to ROS1 TKIs.276

Primary resistance occurs frequently in NSCLC patients with MET ex14 skipping mutations. PTEN loss and PIK3CA mutation associated with sustained activation of the PI3K pathway may confer primary resistance to MET TKIs.277 Co-occurring RAS-MEK pathway gene alterations contribute to resistance, and KRAS mutations mediate primary and secondary resistance to MET TKIs in MET ex14 NSCLC.278 KRAS overexpression or NF1 downregulation hyperactivates the RAS-MAPK signaling pathway, thereby promoting MET TKI resistance.279

Secondary resistance

Acquired TKI resistance mechanisms can be broadly classified into two categories: target-site mechanisms, including secondary resistance mutations in the kinase domain, and nontarget-site mechanisms, such as bypass pathway activation, downstream signaling pathway activation, and histological phenotype transition.

EGFR

EGFR amplification and resistance mutations in the kinase domain

EGFR gene amplification is reported as one of the EGFR-dependent resistance mechanisms.280 For NSCLC with EGFR-sensitizing mutations, the development of T790M and C797S mutations is the main resistance mechanism associated with third-generation EGFR TKIs.281,282 Other uncommon mutations (C797G, L747P/S, G724S, G719C/S/A, L718Q/V, L769X, S768I, G796S/R/D, G834L, L792F/H/R/V, A750P, and SV768IL) have also been found in EGFR TKI-treated NSCLC, which reduces the sensitivity of EGFR-sensitizing mutations to these agents.283–287

Bypass signaling pathway activation

Bypass signaling pathway activation is a significant acquired resistance mechanism to EGFR TKIs, including fusions in ALK, RET, TRK, BRAF, FGFR, MET, and ROS1; activation of IGF1R, HER2, FGFR and PIK3CA; hyperactivation of AXL and MET; and amplification of MET, HER2, FGFR, KRAS, EGFR, and YES1.288–302

Activation of downstream pathways

Other resistance mechanisms to EGFR TKIs involve the activation of pathways downstream of EGFR, including the PI3K-AKT, MEK-ERK, and JAK-STAT3 pathways.282 A recent study revealed that STING-dependent TBK1-IRF3-STAT1 signaling was activated in MET-amplified and EGFR TKI-resistant cells.303

Phenotype transformation

A common drug resistance mechanism for EGFR TKIs also involves phenotypic transformation, including the small cell lung cancer (SCLC), SCC, and sarcomatoid carcinoma (SC) phenotypes.280,304 In a study of 37 patients with drug-resistant EGFR-mutant NSCLC, 5 (14%) underwent transformation to SCLC and were sensitive to standard SCLC treatment.288

EMT is a crucial process in cancer cell metastasis and EGFR TKI resistance (Fig. 6). Hedgehog (Hh) signaling functions in the EMT of NSCLC cells resistant to EGFR TKIs.305 LMNA reduces acquired EGFR TKI resistance by inhibiting EMT via the FGFR/MAPK/c-fos signaling pathway.306 The Hippo pathway is also associated with the EMT-mediated T790M-induced resistance in response to EGFR TKIs.307

ALK

ALK amplification and resistance mutations in the kinase domain

ALK amplification and resistance mutations in the kinase domain are both ALK-dependent resistance mechanisms.308 The common ALK on-target resistance mutations to crizotinib are L1196M, G1269A, C1156Y, G1202R, I1171T/N/S, E1210K, S1206C/Y, L1152P/R, V1180L, L1151T, F1174C, and 1151Tins.280,309,310 ALK resistance mutations constitute the main mechanism of resistance to second-generation ALK inhibitors, such as ceritinib, alectinib, and brigatinib. The incidence of these resistance mutations is higher in patients who developed resistance to second-generation ALK TKIs (56%) than in those whose disease progressed following crizotinib (20%), including G1202R, F1174C/L, I1151Tins, L1152P/R, C1156Y/T, I1171N/T/S, V1180L, G1202del, L1196M, E1210K, S1206C/Y, and D1203N.280,309 The resistance mutations of lorlatinib include L1198F, G2023R, D1203N, G1123D, and L1256F.311,312 Some compound resistance mutations have been discovered, such as L1196M/D1203N, F1174L/G1202R, C1156Y/G1269A, G1202R/S1206Y, C1156Y/L1198F, L1196M/G1202R, and I1171N/D1203N.311,313,314

Bypass and downstream signaling pathway activation

In clinical specimen biopsies, activation of FGFR2, PIK3CA, MET, EGFR, KRAS, NARS, HER, BRAF, SRC, YAP and IGF-1R; fusion of RET and NRG1; amplification of MET and KIT; MET hyperactivation; and mutations in NF1 and NF2 are identified as bypass activation mechanisms.309,315–319 In addition to the PI3K-AKT, MEK-ERK, and JAK-STAT3 downstream pathways, SRC pathway signaling also functions in acquired resistance to ALK TKIs.280,320,321

Phenotype transformation

Histological transformation occurs in patients from ALK-positive adenocarcinoma to SCLC or SCC after acquired resistance to ALK TKIs.322–324 Transformation to SC or neuroendocrine carcinoma (NEC) has also been reported in patients with ALK TKI resistance.325 It has been reported that the ALK G1202R mutation induces the EMT phenotype by activating the STAT3/Slug signaling pathway, resulting in resistance to ceritinib (Fig. 7).326 Resistance to crizotinib is induced by the ALK G1202R and L1196M mutations, which promote the EMT process and metastasis via MDM2/MEK/ERK signaling.327 The AMPK/mTOR/S6K signaling pathway also contributes to EMT promotion and crizotinib resistance in ALK L1196M- and G1202R-mutant NSCLC.328

ROS1

Secondary mutations in the ROS1 kinase domain impart resistance to ROS1 TKIs. G2032R is the most common solvent front mutation identified in tumors with acquired resistance to ROS1 TKIs.329 Other common secondary on-target resistance mutations to crizotinib include G2032R, D2033N, L2026M, L1951R, S1986F/Y, and L2155S; resistance mutations to entrectinib include F2004C/I and G2023R; and resistance mutations to lorlatinib include G2032K/R, S1986F, L2000V, and L2086F.310,330–332

Another major resistance mechanism involves bypass signaling pathway activation via activation of KRAS, NRAS, EGFR, HER2, BRAF V600E, MET, KIT, and PIK3CA; amplification of KRAS and MET; and loss-of-function mutations in NF1, rendering cells resistant to ROS1 inhibition.333–338 SCLC transformation is another mechanism of acquired resistance to ROS1 TKIs (Fig. 7).339

BRAF V600E

BRAF functions in cell proliferation and differentiation through the RAS/ERK signaling pathway, which is linked to mechanisms of resistance. Switching to dimerized p61VE from full-length BRAF V600E drives resistance to BRAF inhibitor treatment through maintenance of the MAPK pathway.340 The PI3K-AKT-mTOR pathway and the RAS-RAF-MEK pathway act in the resistance of BRAF V600E-positive NSCLC.341 The bypass activation mutation is one of the main causes of resistance. KRAS resistance mutations in signaling components directly upstream of BRAF lead to activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, thereby promoting resistance.342,343 NRAS mutation can also lead to reactivation of the pathway and induce resistance to BRAF V600E.344 MEK1 K57N, PTEN N329fs, NRAS Q61R, and KRAS Q61R were related to BRAF/MEK inhibitor resistance in BRAF V600E-mutant NSCLC.341 Upregulation of FGF1 and subsequent autocrine activation of FGFR sustain ERK activation, which is related to resistance in BRAF V600E-driven tumors, including lung cancer.345 EGFR signaling activation drives resistance in BRAF V600E-mutant NSCLC, which is characterized by hyperphosphorylated protein kinase AKT.340

MET ex14 skipping

MET TKD mutations, including L1195V, D1228A/V/N/H/Y/E, Y1230C/H/N/S, L1195V, F1200, G1163E/R, V1155, Y1159, M1211, G1090A/S, V1092I/L, D1133V, H1094Y, and double mutants D1228 and M1229, are common resistance mechanisms of anti-MET treatment in MET ex14-mutant NSCLC patients.346–349 Off-target genomic alterations identified included KRAS activation and amplification, along with amplifications of EGFR, HER3, and BRAF.278,349,350

RET

The RET G810 residue in the kinase solvent front, the V804 gatekeeper mutation, and S904F in the activation loop were identified as acquired resistance to RET TKIs.280,351–353 Acquired MET or KRAS amplification is a RET-independent resistance mechanism of drugs.351 NRAS mutation and upregulation of EGFR and AXL signaling restore signaling through the RAS/MAPK pathway, resulting in RET resistance.354 Acquired HER2 amplification is also reported as a resistance mechanism of RET inhibition in NSCLC.355 The transformation to SCLC mediates resistance to RET TKIs in RET-rearranged lung adenocarcinoma.356,357

KRAS G12C

Acquired KRAS resistance alterations to KRAS G12C inhibitors include G12D/R/V/W, G13D, A59S, Q61H, R68S, H95D/Q/R, Y96C, and amplification of the KRAS G12C allele.358,359 The PI3K-AKT and RAF-ERK pathways play major roles in resistance to KRAS G12C inhibitors in NSCLC. MET amplification maintains the GTP-bound active form of RAS, subsequently activating the RAS-ERK pathway and mediating resistance to KRAS G12C inhibitors as well as AKT activation in a non-RAS-dependent manner.360 EMT induces intrinsic and acquired resistance to KRAS G12C inhibition by maintaining PI3K activation regulated by the IGFR-IRS1 pathway.361 EGFR, HER2, and FGFR signaling may promote the adaptive feedback of KRAS G12C inhibition, and interrupting this feedback loop may help overcome adaptive resistance. Feedback reactivation is also driven by receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK)-mediated activation of wild-type NRAS and HRAS.362 Other reported acquired bypass mechanisms of resistance include activating mutations of BRAF, MAP2K1, RET, MEK, and PIK3CA; fusions of ALK, RET, BRAF, RAF1, and FGFR3; and loss-of-function mutations in NF1 and PTEN.358,359 Transformation to SCC is another resistance mechanism to KRAS G12C inhibition.358

However, studies on the mechanisms of secondary resistance to NTRK or HER2 inhibitors have focused mainly on multiple solid tumors, such as melanoma, breast cancer, and gastric cancer, and have included secondary mutations in the TRK kinase domain, bypass pathway activation, and downstream signaling activation.196,363 The understanding of NTRK and HER2 inhibition resistance mechanisms in NSCLC is still insufficient.

Strategies to overcome TKI resistance

To overcome primary and secondary TKI resistance, subsequent treatment options could be adopted depending on resistance mechanisms.

Targeting primary resistance

As suggested by Jaime L Schneider et al., different types of ALK compound mutations need to be managed with distinct ALK TKIs with varied sensitivities, and a single novel ALK TKI is insufficient to overcome all on-target resistance.364 Patients with NSCLC and oncogenic mutations harboring other co-mutations, such as MET amplification, PIK3CA mutation, and TP53 mutation, will be discussed next in subsequent sections on treatment options for patients with co-mutations.

Dysregulation of the cell cycle has been reported to participate in primary resistance to targeted agents. The combination of the CDK4/6 inhibitor palbociclib and osimertinib may be considered an effective first-line treatment option in patients with a common EGFR mutation and CDK4 amplification instead of awaiting disease progression on osimertinib alone.365 It has been demonstrated in vitro that cyclin D1 overexpression and p16 deletion confer intrinsic resistance to osimertinib, which correlates with elevated Rb protein phosphorylation. Blocking the phosphorylation of Rb with the CDK4/6 inhibitor abemaciclib increased sensitivity to osimertinib and reversed the intrinsic resistant phenotype.269

Targeting acquired resistance

Exploring resistance mechanisms

Tumor cells may undergo genetic mutations and other epigenetic changes, as stated above, which may lead to resistance to the original treatment regimen. The acquired resistance mechanisms vary among different patients. Thus, tissue rebiopsy, the gold standard for molecular diagnosis in clinic, is necessary to explore resistance mechanisms and achieve individualized treatment upon systemic progression. Understanding resistance mechanisms is a critical step in guiding further treatment. However, there are several significant limitations related to tissue biopsy. For example, it is not feasible for patients who experience multiple tumor progression events, and a single biopsy sample is not enough to represent all the resistance mechanisms of different sites due to selection bias, which has driven the development of liquid biopsy.

Liquid biopsy testing for plasma mutations via ctDNA analyses has been developed as a complementary assay to tissue biopsy, with the advantages of its noninvasiveness, capacity to address tumor heterogeneity, and ability to continuously monitor emerging resistance mechanisms and treatment responses. However, its sensitivity for detecting early-stage disease and diagnosing BM is limited.366 Plasma mutations can be detected by multiple highly sensitive genotyping methods, including next-generation sequencing (NGS), droplet digital polymerase chain reaction (ddPCR), and bead, emulsion, amplification, and magnetics (BEAMing) assays.367 For patients with negative plasma mutation results, the feasibility of tissue biopsy could be reassessed.

Overall, local treatment combined with the original TKI could be used to control oligoprogression or CNS progression in accordance with NCCN guidelines.368 With the widespread progression of disease, rebiopsy or plasma testing is recommended to identify resistance mechanisms. At the time of progression, biopsy is crucial not only for detecting resistance mutations but also for identifying any histological transformation. On the basis of the distinct mechanisms of drug resistance, targeted strategies to overcome resistance are outlined below. Although we highlight the importance of rebiopsy and the development of resistance mechanism-based therapeutic approaches, some resistant samples from patients with undetectable resistance mutations still exist. These patients may benefit from the addition of chemotherapy, antiangiogenic therapy, and immunotherapy.

Targeting kinase domain mutations

Some effective TKIs have been developed to target mutations at specific sites. For example, third-generation EGFR TKIs (osimertinib, almonertinib, and furmonertinib) were primarily developed to target the secondary resistance mutation T790M, and fourth-generation TKIs (BLU-945 and BBT-176) mainly target T790M/C797S dual mutations caused by third-generation TKI resistance.55,56 For other rare resistance mutations after failure of EGFR TKIs, afatinib and osimertinib could be considered.65–68 In patients with ALK-positive NSCLC, G1202R is the most common resistance mutation after treatment with second-generation ALK TKIs, and this induced resistance can be overcome by the third-generation ALK inhibitor lorlatinib.309 G1202R compound mutations to the next-generation ALK inhibitor, such as L1196M/G1202R and F1174L/G1202R, are common after subsequent lorlatinib treatment, novel ALK inhibitors like NVL-655 and TPX-0131 are designed to overcome a variety of ALK resistance mutations, including G1202R compound mutations.111,112,313 For target-site resistance mechanisms, the combination of first- and third-generation EGFR TKIs could also enhance the inhibitory effect on both acquired C797S and T790M mutations.369

Targeting bypass signaling activation

Growing evidence indicates that for NSCLC patients with other targetable bypass molecular resistance mechanisms after third-generation EGFR TKI therapy, combining EGFR TKIs with agents targeting resistance mutations offers significant clinical benefits.

Targeting EGFR and MET amplification

MET amplification is one of the most common bypass mechanisms of acquired resistance in EGFR-mutant patients. Dual targeting of EGFR and MET has demonstrated antitumor activity. In the INSIGHT trial, tepotinib plus gefitinib provided superior clinical benefit compared with chemotherapy in patients with MET overexpression or amplification who had progressed on prior EGFR TKI treatment.370 Recently, the INSIGHT 2 study highlighted that patients could derive great symptomatic and survival benefits from continuing osimertinib combined with tepotinib, with an ORR of 50%, mPFS of 5.6 months, and mOS of 17.8 months.371 Other combination regimens, such as osimertinib with savolitinib and gumarontinib, have also been explored in EGFR TKI-resistant patients with MET amplification, demonstrating the promising efficacy of MET TKI combination therapy.372,373

The SAVANNAH study aimed to identify individuals most likely to benefit from MET-targeting therapy in EGFR TKI-resistant patients with MET overexpression and/or amplification.374 The outcomes were more favorable in patients with MET IHC90+ and/or FISH10+ status, with an ORR of 49% and mPFS of 7.1 months after treatment with savolitinib plus osimertinib. On the basis of the SAVANNAH findings, the phase III SAFFRON trial (NCT05261399) is currently ongoing, with results yet to be announced.

Targeting EGFR and MET overexpression

In addition to MET amplification, the overexpression of c-MET is also a common mechanism leading to third-generation EGFR TKI resistance. In the I/Ib multicenter study (NCT02099058), Teliso-V combined with osimertinib demonstrated an ORR of 58% in c-MET overexpressing EGFR-mutant NSCLC patients who had progressed on prior osimertinib treatment.375 Another Ib study evaluated Teliso-V combined with erlotinib in advanced c-MET overexpressing NSCLC.376 Notably, Teliso-V treatment can cause neurological adverse effects, such as peripheral sensory neuropathy and peripheral neuropathy.

Targeting EGFR and other resistance mutations

HER2 alteration is another related resistance mechanism of EGFR TKI resistance. The coadministration of osimertinib and T-DM1 represents a promising therapeutic strategy for overcoming this resistance.377 Combining EGFR TKIs with BRAF V600E-specific inhibitors effectively suppresses bypass signaling pathways mediating acquired resistance driven by BRAF V600E mutations.378–380 The combination of osimertinib and sotorasib has shown favorable efficacy and tolerability in patients with resistance to first-line osimertinib caused by acquired KRAS G12C mutation.381 However, existing evidence is largely based on preclinical and retrospective studies, with limited prospective clinical trial data available. Overall, for patients with other targetable alterations emerging after resistance to third-generation EGFR TKIs, a dual-targeted combination strategy can be considered.

Targeting histological phenotype transition

Clinical evidence has shown that chemotherapy treatment for the SCLC phenotype seems feasible in patients who have EGFR-mutant lung adenocarcinoma and SCLC transformation in their drug-resistant tumor biopsies.288 A patient with ALK-mutant adenocarcinoma underwent histological transformation into SC after ALK TKI resistance, and could benefit from an ALK TKI regimen switch combined with chemotherapy, suggesting a promising option for patients with SC transformation.382 However, treatment for other types of phenotypic transformation during TKI resistance remains poorly understood. Further research is needed to refine the treatment guidelines for this population.

EMT is a common mechanism of acquired resistance to EGFR TKIs. Snail, a key transcription factor related to EMT, functions in osimertinib resistance, which can be reversed by downregulating the expression of Snail through the combination of osimertinib and the CDK4/6 inhibitor palbociclib.383 IL-6 is also a potent, actionable target to overcome EMT-driven EGFR TKI resistance. Metformin has been reported to block IL-6 expression and EMT in EGFR TKI-resistant cells. The further application of the combination of EGFR TKIs and IL-6-targeting drugs or metformin is highly anticipated.384

General strategies for acquired resistance

Combining TKIs or antibodies and chemotherapy

The superior efficacy of osimertinib or amivantamab combined with chemotherapy as a first-line treatment has been demonstrated, establishing a foundation for the era of combined targeted therapies and chemotherapy. However, the use of targeted agents combined with chemotherapy after the development of TKI resistance remains controversial.

EGFR TKI + chemotherapy

The phase III IMPRESS trial demonstrated that continuing first-generation EGFR TKIs in combination with standard chemotherapy after disease progression negatively impacted OS (HR = 1.44, P = 0.016), highlighting the need to discontinue first-generation EGFR TKIs upon radiological progression.385 Nonetheless, patients continuing osimertinib in combination with chemotherapy after progression had superior mPFS than those who discontinued osimertinib.386 Ongoing trials, such as the phase III COMPEL study evaluating chemotherapy with or without osimertinib following non-CNS progression to first-line osimertinib and the phase I/II HARMONY trial investigating the fourth-generation TKI BLU-701 monotherapy or in combination with osimertinib or platinum-based chemotherapy, are expected to further elucidate the efficacy of these combination strategies.

Amivantamab + chemotherapy

Compared with chemotherapy alone, the MARIPOSA2 trial demonstrated significant improvements in ORR and mPFS with the addition of amivantamab to chemotherapy.82 However, amivantamab may cause considerable toxicity, which could be mitigated by an osimertinib-based treatment strategy.

Combining TKIs and antiangiogenic agents

Antiangiogenic drugs inhibit tumor angiogenesis by targeting key pathways and downregulating active vascular factors. Combining antiangiogenic agents with TKIs in oncogene-driven NSCLC exerts a synergistic effect, where VEGF inhibition enhances the efficacy of TKIs.387

Bevacizumab

Bevacizumab is a VEGF-targeted monoclonal antibody that inhibits tumor angiogenesis, improves the delivery of TKIs through vascular normalization, and alleviates immunosuppression.388 Combining bevacizumab with TKIs, especially first- and second-generation TKIs, is an effective first-line or later-line treatment approach that provides clinical benefits in advanced NSCLC patients with EGFR, ALK, ROS1, or MET mutations.389–392 However, controversial reports also exist concerning the impact of the addition of bevacizumab on survival outcomes.388 Additionally, current evidence suggests that the addition of bevacizumab may not enhance the efficacy of osimertinib as a first-line or later-line treatment,393 but the vascular normalization effect could improve drug delivery to the central nervous system, thereby enhancing CNS disease control.394

Anlotinib

Anlotinib is an oral small-molecule multitarget antiangiogenic agent that inhibits both tumor cells and angiogenesis. Concurrent use of anlotinib and TKIs, such as gefitinib, osimertinib, and crizotinib, has been proven to restore sensitivity in TKI-resistant NSCLC cells, overcome acquired resistance, and enhance therapeutic efficacy.395–397 Owing to its favorable efficacy and manageable toxicity, dual therapy with anlotinib and TKI offers a potential strategy for overcoming TKI resistance.

The penetration of anlotinib across human brain microvascular endothelial cells was comparable to that of afatinib and gefitinib, indicating its efficacy in NSCLC patients with BM, and the CNS activity of anlotinib has been confirmed in clinical trials.398–400 However, comparisons of extracranial and intracranial control between anlotinib plus TKI and anlotinib monotherapy in TKI-resistant patients in large randomized trials are lacking.

Apatinib

Apatinib (a small-molecule TKI that inhibits VEGFR, PDGFR, c-Kit, RET, and SRC) has been shown to improve the antitumor efficacy of EGFR TKIs in NSCLC patients with acquired resistance.401,402 In a retrospective analysis of 39 EGFR-mutant NSCLC patients with osimertinib resistance, osimertinib combined with apatinib achieved an ORR of 12.8% and mPFS of 4 months, with 36% of patients remaining on treatment for more than 6 months, indicating that this combination is also a viable treatment option for patients with osimertinib resistance.402

Immunotherapy + antiangiogenic agent + chemotherapy

In recent years, immunotherapy has transformed the treatment landscape of patients with metastatic NSCLC, including those with oncogenic mutations. Increasing evidence confirms that immunotherapy combinations exert a synergistic effect, enhancing anticancer activity within the TIME of oncogene-driven NSCLC.

EGFR mutations

The IMPOWER150 study demonstrated a significantly longer mOS with the atezolizumab-bevacizumab-chemotherapy (ABCP) regimen than with bevacizumab-chemotherapy (BCP) alone, albeit with increased toxicity.403 Phase II and III studies, such as GFPC 06-2018,404 NEJ043,405 and ATTLAS406 also demonstrated favorable outcomes of atezolizumab combined with bevacizumab and chemotherapy in EGFR TKI-resistant patients.

The combination regimen of sintilimab plus IBI305 (a bevacizumab analog) and chemotherapy also significantly improved mPFS compared with chemotherapy alone in the ORIENT-31 study.407 The phase III HARMONi-A study reported the efficacy of the novel PD-1/VEGF bispecific antibody ivonescimab (AK112) in combination with chemotherapy.408 For patients with baseline BM, the combination yielded even greater PFS benefits (HR = 0.40). The treatment was well tolerated, with most AEs being hematologic toxicities and fewer immune-related AEs compared with other four-drug combination regimen studies, highlighting its potential as a promising treatment option for EGFR TKI-resistant patients.

ALK rearrangements

In contrast, patients with ALK fusion-positive NSCLC derive limited benefit from ICIs, either as monotherapies or in combination with chemotherapy. Retrospective studies have shown that ALK-positive patients treated with ICIs achieve an ORR of only 0–3.6% and mPFS of approximately 2 months following ICI initiation.409–411 In a phase II study evaluating pembrolizumab combined with chemotherapy in EGFR TKI- or ALK TKI-resistant NSCLC patients, the ORR for ALK-positive patients was 29%; however, both the mPFS and mOS were only 2.9 months, indicating that further optimization of treatment strategies is warranted.412

Other mutations

The IMAD2 (GFPC 01-2018) study evaluated the efficacy of first-line or later-line ICIs in patients with NSCLC and rare mutations, such as BRAF V600, BRAF non-V600, HER2, MET mutations, and RET rearrangements. The efficacy of ICIs in these patients was comparable to that reported in pretreated unselected NSCLC patients in randomized controlled trials or observational studies.413 In patients with BRAF-mutant NSCLC, the combination of ICIs and chemotherapy appears to be a more effective treatment option than ICI monotherapy.414

Overall, no consensus has been reached on immunotherapy in oncogene-driven NSCLC patients with drug resistance because of the limited clinical evidence. For patients with extensive disease progression following TKI treatment and unknown resistance mechanisms, immunotherapy could be a potential therapeutic option. Given the high toxicity of the combination of ICIs, chemotherapy, and antiangiogenic therapy, individualized treatment strategies should be considered based on patients’ conditions and preferences. More prospective trials are needed to provide clinical data on ICIs in advanced oncogene-driven NSCLC patients, particularly those harboring rare mutations. As mentioned previously, different oncogenic mutations have different TIME features, which may affect the immunotherapy response. Recently, Zhang et al. investigated TIME heterogeneity in response variability in NSCLC patients receiving neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy.415 Although patients with EGFR or ALK mutations were excluded from this study, the heterogeneity of the immune context and immune cells in the TIME may underlie TKI resistance in patients with oncogenic mutations and influence subsequent anti-PD-1 treatment, which needs to be further explored. This study provides a new idea on the basis of heterogeneous TIME features for immunotherapy in patients with different oncogenic mutations.

ADCs

With advanced linker-payload system, ADCs can target tumor cells more precisely and have shown promising antitumor efficacy. In addition to their role in first-line therapy, ADCs represent a novel therapeutic option for patients with TKI resistance.416

TROP2 ADCs

Sacituzumab tirumotecan (Sac-TMT). Sac-TMT is a novel TROP2 ADC that has shown encouraging antitumor activity in patients with EGFR mutations. In the OptiTROP-Lung03 study, Sac-TMT showed promising efficacy in patients with pretreated advanced EGFR-mutant NSCLC (Table 3). Compared with docetaxel, Sac-TMT significantly improved the ORR (45.1% vs 15.6%), mPFS (6.9 months vs 2.8 months, HR = 0.30) and mOS (not reached for both groups, HR = 0.49). In addition, Sac-TMT decreased the rate of grade ≥3 TRAEs (56.0% vs 71.7%).417 These results suggest that Sac-TMT could emerge as a new standard of care for patients resistant to TKIs.

Datopotamab deruxtecan (Dato-DXd)

Dato-DXd, a TROP2 ADC, secured the FDA breakthrough therapy designation for pretreated EGFR-mutant advanced NSCLC based on findings from the TROPION-Lung05 and TROPION-Lung01 trials. In the phase II ORCHARD trial, Dato-DXd combined with osimertinib showed promising efficacy and manageable safety in patients with EGFR-mutant advanced NSCLC who progressed on first-line osimertinib. In patients receiving Dato-DXd (6 mg/kg) plus osimertinib, the ORR was 36%, and the mPFS was 11.7 months.418 The phase III TROPION-Lung15 trial was initiated to assess the efficacy of Dato-DXd, either alone or combined with osimertinib, versus platinum-based doublet chemotherapy in locally advanced or metastatic EGFR-mutant NSCLC patients who progressed on prior osimertinib therapy.419

HER3 ADCs

Patritumab deruxtecan (HER3-DXd). HER3-DXd is a novel ADC consisting of a fully human mAb to HER3. According to the results of the phase III HERTHENA-Lung02 trial, HER3-DXd significantly improved the mPFS of patients resistant to third-generation EGFR TKIs (5.8 months vs 5.4 months, HR = 0.77) compared with platinum-based chemotherapy. In patients receiving HER3-DXd, the ORR was 35.2%, and the mDOR was 5.7 months. In addition, the intracranial mPFS was 5.4 months in patients with baseline BM.420

DB-1310

DB-1310 is a novel ADC comprising a humanized anti-HER3 IgG1 monoclonal antibody, a cleavable peptide linker, and a DNA topoisomerase I inhibitor. In EGFR-mutant NSCLC patients who had failed standard therapy, the ORR was 35.7%, the DCR was 90.5%, and the mPFS was 7.0 months.421

EGFR×HER3 ADCs

: BL-B01D1. BL-B01D1, a first-in-class EGFR-HER3 bispecific ADC, has shown clinical efficacy in patients with EGFR-mutant NSCLC. The ORR was 66.7% in patients with EGFR ex20ins or nonclassical mutations.211,422 In addition to EGFR mutations, BL-B01D1 also exhibits activity against other oncogenic mutations (ALK, ROS1, RET, KRAS, BRAF, MET).211

EGFR×MET ADCs:

Amivantamab. Patients with EGFR ex20ins mutations after progression on platinum-based chemotherapy achieved an ORR of 40% and mPFS of 8.3 months with amivantamab treatment.423 In the MARIPOSA-2 study, amivantamab-chemotherapy and amivantamab-lazertinib-chemotherapy conferred greater clinical benefits than chemotherapy alone in patients who progressed on osimertinib.82

EMB-01

EMB-01, an EGFR/MET bispecific antibody, exhibits relatively low antitumor activity, with an ORR of only 5% among 38 response-evaluable NSCLC patients, including those with acquired EGFR TKI resistance and EGFR exon 20ins mutations.424

Adjuvant EGFR TKIs

Gefitinib

The ADJUVANT-CTONG1104 study was the first to compare OS between adjuvant gefitinib and standard chemotherapy and indicated that adjuvant gefitinib could confer long-term benefits and represent an alternative adjuvant treatment option to chemotherapy.8,429 Compared with chemotherapy, adjuvant gefitinib also significantly prolonged DFS (30.8 months vs 19.8 months, HR = 0.56, P = 0.001). However, statistically significant differences in DFS were not observed in the IMPACT study.430 Moreover, gefitinib had better tolerability and less toxicity than standard chemotherapy in the ADJUVANT and IMPACT studies.429,430

Icotinib

The CORIN (GASTO1003) and EVIDENCE trials demonstrated that adjuvant icotinib significantly improved DFS.431,432 Moreover, adjuvant icotinib showed a better safety profile than chemotherapy, with significantly fewer reported grade ≥3 TRAEs (4% vs 60%).431 The ICOMPARE study investigated the duration of adjuvant icotinib treatment, and 2-year adjuvant icotinib treatment provided better survival benefits with a similar safety profile compared with 1-year treatment.433 The ICTAN (GASTO1002) trial showed that both 12-month and 6-month adjuvant icotinib provided better DFS and OS benefits than observation. However, no significant difference in survival outcomes was observed between the 12-month and 6-month treatment durations.434

Osimertinib

Adjuvant osimertinib is another treatment option for resectable NSCLC. In patients with stage IB-IIIA NSCLC, the median DFS was 65.8 months in the osimertinib group, which was significantly longer than 28.1 months in the placebo group. Patients treated with osimertinib had lower local/regional and distant recurrence rates than those treated with placebo. However, the toxic effect of osimertinib was greater than that of placebo, and cardiac AEs occurred more commonly in the osimertinib group.7,435 The phase III ADAURA2 trial is ongoing to assess the clinical benefits of the adjuvant osimertinib in stage IA2-IA3 EGFR-mutant NSCLC patients.

Aumolertinib

In the ARTS trial, adjuvant aumolertinib improved the DFS of patients with stage II-IIIB EGFR-mutant NSCLC after complete resection compared with placebo (NR vs 19.4 months, HR = 0.17, P < 0.0001). The 2-year DFS rate was 88.2% in the aumolertinib arm and 40.6% in the placebo arm.436 The APPOINT study explored aumolertinib as an adjuvant therapy in stage IA EGFR-mutant NSCLC patients with high-risk factors. The 1-year DFS rates were 100% and 87%, with corresponding 1-year risks of tumor recurrence of 0% and 12.8% in the two arms, respectively.437

Other EGFR TKIs

Adjuvant afatinib was explored in a phase II study, with median recurrence-free survival (RFS) of 42.8 months and 58.6 months in the 3-month and 2-year treatment groups, respectively.438 Some studies are ongoing to evaluate the efficacy and safety of befotertinib, SH-1028, and furmonertinib as adjuvant therapy in patients with resectable stage I-IIIB EGFR-mutant NSCLC (NCT06041776, NCT06080776, NCT05165355, and NCT04853342).

Neoadjuvant EGFR TKIs

Erlotinib

The EMERGING-CTONG 1103 study revealed an improved ORR (54.1% vs 34.3%) and mPFS (21.5 months vs 11.4 months), and less frequent grade ≥3 treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) (13.5% vs 29.4%) with neoadjuvant erlotinib than with chemotherapy (Table 7).9,439 Erlotinib is an effective neoadjuvant treatment option for resectable EGFR-mutant NSCLC with better response rates and superior OS than chemotherapy for patients with wild-type EGFR.440

Gefitinib

Gefitinib exhibited an acceptable safety profile with no reported grade 3 or 4 AEs and showed favorable efficacy in stage II-IIIA operable NSCLC, with a reported ORR of 54.5%, similar to that of erlotinib (54.1%) in the EMERGING-CTONG 1103 study and an MPR rate of 24.2%, which was higher than that of erlotinib (9.7%).441

Afatinib

The ASCENT trial reported an ORR of 63% with induction afatinib in patients with stage III EGFR-mutant NSCLC.442 In another trial conducted in stage III patients, the ORR, DCR, MPR, pCR, pathological downstaging, and R0 resection rates were 70.2%, 93.6%, 9.1%, 3.0%, 57.6%, and 87.9%, respectively.443 The phase II Neoafa trial (NCT04470076) is currently evaluating neoadjuvant afatinib in combination with chemotherapy in patients with stage IIA-IIIB EGFR-mutant NSCLC.

Osimertinib

In the NEOS trial, the ORR was 71.1% in stage IIA-IIIB EGFR-positive NSCLC patients who completed the 6-week neoadjuvant osimertinib treatment, which was higher than the abovementioned ORRs of gefitinib and erlotinib. Neoadjuvant osimertinib also increased the rate of curative resection, with 93.8% R0 resection rate in patients who underwent surgery.444 The MPR rate was 16.7%, and the pCR rate was 0% in patients who underwent surgical resection after neoadjuvant osimertinib treatment in another phase II study.445 The phase III NeoADAURA trial further demonstrated the therapeutic benefits of neoadjuvant osimertinib with or without chemotherapy.446 The combination of osimertinib and chemotherapy as neoadjuvant therapy is also being investigated in patients with resectable stage III EGFR-mutant NSCLC (NCT05011487).

Other EGFR TKIs

Currently, numerous studies on other neoadjuvant EGFR TKIs have been conducted. Neoadjuvant icotinib, almonertinib, furmonertinib, and lazertinib are being evaluated in patients with EGFR-mutant NSCLC (NCT03749213, NCT04685070, NCT04455594, NCT05987826, and NCT05469022). The clinical feasibility of TKIs combined with chemotherapy, bevacizumab, or anlotinib is also being explored in trials (NCT05132985, FORESEE, NeoLazer, NCT05503667, and NCT06585644).

Adjuvant and neoadjuvant ALK TKIs

Alectinib

A phase III ALINA study identified alectinib as a novel type of adjuvant treatment modality for patients with stage IB-IIIA ALK-positive NSCLC. In the intention-to-treat (ITT) population, the 2-year DFS rate was 93.6% in the alectinib group and 63.7% in the chemotherapy group. Adjuvant alectinib can reduce recurrence, especially in the brain, and the HR for CNS disease recurrence or death is 0.22, suggesting that alectinib is a preferable option.10 Moreover, the incidence of grade ≥3 AEs related to alectinib treatment was similar to that of chemotherapy.

In the NAUTIKA1 study, alectinib showed preliminary efficacy in the neoadjuvant setting, with an MPR rate of 71.4% and a pCR rate of 28.6%.447 The MPR rate was 46%, and the pCR rate was 12% in the ALNEO trial.448 Compared with crizotinib, induction alectinib also had a superior pathological response, with both MPR (65% vs 46%) and pCR (35% vs 15%) rates.449

Crizotinib

Crizotinib may be a feasible treatment option in the neoadjuvant setting for patients with pathologically confirmed N2 ALK-positive NSCLC. Ten out of eleven patients (91.0%) achieved R0 resection, and two patients achieved a pCR with neoadjuvant crizotinib.450 Another phase III ALCHEMIST trial is ongoing to further evaluate neoadjuvant crizotinib in patients with resectable IB-IIIA ALK-positive NSCLC.

Ceritinib

The SAKULA trial enrolled resectable locally advanced ALK-positive NSCLC patients who received three cycles of ceritinib as induction therapy, and the R0 resection rate was 83% in patients who underwent surgical resection. The MPR rate was 57%, and the pCR rate was 29%.11 Despite the small sample size, ceritinib showed favorable neoadjuvant efficacy.

Other ALK TKIs

Induction lorlatinib was demonstrated to be clinically feasible and tolerable in patients with stage III ALK-positive NSCLC in the LORIN trial.451 There are also clinical trials (NCT05361564 and NCT05380024) investigating the clinical feasibility of brigatinib and ensartinib as neoadjuvant therapies. Other phase II and III clinical trials (NCT05186506, NCT05241028, and NCT05341583) are evaluating the efficacy and safety of ensartinib in the adjuvant setting for ALK-positive NSCLC patients following surgical resection.

Notably, ALK TKIs significantly increase the rate of histologically complete resection and achieve a higher pCR in patients with resectable ALK-positive NSCLC. However, few patients achieve pCR after neoadjuvant EGFR TKI treatment, which may be related to the different biological characteristics of tumors and the mechanisms of action of targeted agents compared with those of ALK.

Other adjuvant and neoadjuvant TKIs

For NSCLC patients carrying other rare mutations, such as ROS1, RET, MET ex14 skipping or MET amplification, BRAF V600, KRAS G12C, and NTRK mutations, new trials have explored the efficacy and safety of adjuvant or neoadjuvant targeted therapies, including iruplinalkib, selpercatinib, vebreltinib, capmatinib, dabrafenib plus trametinib, adagrasib, sotorasib, divarasib, entrectinib, vemurafenib plus cobimetinib, and pralsetinib (Neo-INFINITY,452 LIBRETTO-432, NCT06644313, NCT06054191, Neo-KAN, NCT05118854, NAUTIKA1). The results are eagerly awaited.

Neoadjuvant immunotherapy

A multicenter pooled analysis revealed the following outcomes in 39 patients with oncogene-driven NSCLC (including EGFR, KRAS, RET, ALK, ROS1, BRAF, and HER2 mutations) who received neoadjuvant immunotherapy without discontinuation due to severe toxicity and proceeded to surgery: an ORR of 62.5%, an MPR rate of 37.5%, a pCR rate of 12.5%, and an R0 resection rate of 97.4%. The 2-year DFS rates were 72.8%, 61.9%, and 87.3% for all oncogenic mutations, EGFR mutations and KRAS mutations, respectively.453 Neoadjuvant immunotherapy can also result in significant pathological remission and confer a strong survival benefit in oncogene-driven NSCLC patients. In addition, patients with KRAS mutations seem to benefit more from neoadjuvant immunotherapy.

To investigate the efficacy of different neoadjuvant treatment modalities in EGFR-mutant NSCLC, data from multicenter cohorts were compared with those from the EGFR cohort of the CTONG1103 study.453 The PD-1/PD-L1 blockade plus platinum-based chemotherapy (IO + CT) group had higher ORR and MPR rates than the gemcitabine/cisplatin (GC) or erlotinib groups, and pCR was induced only in the IO + CT group at a rate of 10.5%. Notably, patients who had EGFR 19del or 21L858R mutations achieved MPR, whereas patients with rare EGFR mutations did not achieve MPR. Thus, neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy holds substantial promise for patients with EGFR-mutant NSCLC, especially those with sensitizing mutations.453

Overall, therapeutic agents targeting oncogenic mutations yield remarkable survival benefits in both neoadjuvant and adjuvant settings. However, two issues need to be addressed. First, the optimal duration of adjuvant or neoadjuvant treatment requires clarification. The ICOMPARE and ICTAN studies have focused on the duration of adjuvant icotinib, whereas other investigations have explored adjuvant afatinib.433,434,438 The results indicate that 2 years of treatment may be more effective than 3 months or 1 year, but 1-year treatment has no additional benefit over 6 months. Additional studies are needed to validate these results, and such investigations also need to be conducted for other TKIs. Validation in the neoadjuvant setting is also needed, as there is still no consensus on the optimal duration of neoadjuvant targeted therapy. Second, the combination of TKIs with chemotherapy or antiangiogenic agents is emerging in adjuvant and neoadjuvant settings. It is necessary to clarify whether the combination regimen is essential and more effective than TKI monotherapy. Further consideration is needed regarding both drug efficacy and safety profile. Moreover, neoadjuvant immunotherapy alone or combined with chemotherapy also exhibits promising efficacy in oncogene-driven NSCLC. How to select the population more suitable for neoadjuvant immunotherapy rather than for neoadjuvant chemotherapy or targeted therapy needs to be considered.

Treatment for patients with unresectable stage III NSCLC harboring oncogenic alterations

Induction of TKI + chemoradiotherapy

Gefitinib

The phase II LOGIK0902/OLCSG0905 study demonstrated the efficacy of gefitinib and subsequent concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT) in patients with unresectable EGFR-mutant stage III NSCLC, with an ORR of 85.0%. The 2-year OS and PFS rates were 90.0% and 36.9%, respectively. OS data for induction gefitinib were favorable compared with standard platinum-based CRT in the EGFR mutation-unselected population in other studies, indicating that induction EGFR TKI before CCRT helps achieve tumor remission and prolong survival.14 The overall safety profiles following gefitinib and CCRT were manageable, hepatic toxicity and hematological toxicity were predominant during the induction phase and CRT phase, respectively.

Afatinib

In the ASCENT trial, the ORR with induction afatinib was 63.0%. Two of the nine patients who were previously unresectable achieved resectable disease.442 However, further studies are needed to verify which treatment, surgery or continued definitive CCRT, confers a better prognosis in patients who experience this conversion. A new phase II trial (NCT06709859) aims to explore the efficacy and safety of afatinib plus chemotherapy as conversion treatment, and its primary endpoint is the rate of conversion to resectable disease.

Erlotinib

Patients with stage III EGFR-mutant NSCLC were randomized to receive three cycles of erlotinib first and then received either CCRT with erlotinib followed by erlotinib or CCRT with irinotecan-cisplatin (IP) alone. The ORR was 71.4% and 80.0%, and the mOS was 39.3 months and 31.2 months for the two arms, respectively.454 The study demonstrated that the both combination regimens were effective and safe, although it was closed early due to slow accrual.

Other TKIs

The APPROACH study aims to evaluate the efficacy and safety of induction almonertinib and to investigate almonertinib maintenance after almonertinib induction and local therapy. The ongoing umbrella NUMER trial explores induction TKIs (including sunvozertinib, crizotinib, pralsetinib, larotrectinib, savolitinib, pyrotinib, dabrafenib+trametinib, glecirasib, and ensartinib) followed by surgery in patients with unresectable stage III NSCLC and rare oncogenic mutations.

TKIs in combination with radiotherapy/chemoradiotherapy

TKI + radiotherapy

In the WJOG6911L trial, patients who received gefitinib plus concurrent thoracic radiotherapy achieved an ORR of 81.5%, mPFS of 18.6 months, and mOS of 61.1 months.13 Among the AEs that occurred during treatment, pneumonitis was one of the most common events and led to drug discontinuation. The RECEL and ADVANCE studies demonstrated that erlotinib plus radiotherapy and aumolertinib plus radiotherapy provided better PFS benefits than CCRT, with a favorable tolerability.455,456 There are also other trials evaluating the efficacy and safety of almonertinib and icotinib in combination with concurrent radiotherapy in patients with unresectable stage III EGFR-mutant NSCLC (NCT04636593, NCT02883543, and NCT02407366).

TKI + chemoradiotherapy

A phase II trial added erlotinib to CCRT in patients with inoperable stage III NSCLC and reported promising OS and acceptable toxicity. Among the 4 patients with EGFR mutations, the mOS was 41.1 months, and the 1-, 2-, and 5-year OS rates were 100%, 100% and 50%, respectively.457 However, the primary endpoint (improvement in time to progression to 25 months) was not met in this study.

Concurrent EGFR TKIs and radiotherapy confer a greater survival benefit than CCRT, but the addition of TKIs to CCRT still needs to be considered. Phase II trials are ongoing to evaluate the efficacy and safety of almonertinib plus CCRT in unresectable stage III EGFR-mutant NSCLC patients (NCT04952168) and iruplinalkib plus CCRT in unresectable locally advanced NSCLC patients with ALK or ROS1 mutations (NCT05351320).

Chemoradiotherapy + TKI maintenance

The phase III LAURA study revealed that osimertinib significantly improved mPFS compared with placebo (39.1 months vs 5.6 months) in unresectable stage III EGFR-mutant NSCLC patients without progression after definitive CRT.458 The preliminary results of the PLATINUM study revealed that consolidative lazertinib exhibited promising clinical efficacy and tolerability in unresectable stage III EGFR-mutant NSCLC patients following CCRT.459 In the phase III POLESTAR trial (NCT04951635), interim results showed that maintenance aumolertinib after chemoradiotherapy significantly extended mPFS compared to placebo (30.4 months vs 3.8 months), positioning it as a promising new maintenance strategy.460 The ongoing NEOLA study is assessing the efficacy and safety of osimertinib as induction therapy before CRT and as maintenance therapy.

Chemoradiotherapy + immune maintenance

The KCSG LU-22-18 study evaluated maintenance durvalumab after definitive CCRT in patients with phase III unresectable EGFR- or ALK-mutant NSCLC compared with patients with EGFR- or ALK-wild type NSCLC, and there was no significant difference in the mPFS. Notably, patients with higher PD-L1 expression had a significantly longer mPFS, with mPFS values of 13.6, 18.7, and 24.7 months for TPSs of 0, 1–49, and ≥50, respectively.461 The study demonstrated that immune maintenance following CCRT conferred patient survival benefits regardless of the EGFR or ALK status, and the biomarker PD-L1 could be used to screen for the population that would benefit from the immune maintenance modality.

Another retrospective study revealed that KRAS-mutant patients were more likely to experience rapid disease progression after CRT and had worse mPFS than KRAS-wild type patients; however, ICI consolidation could contribute to a significant improvement in PFS and could be a promising treatment option following CRT.462 Patients harboring KRAS mutations are more likely to benefit from durvalumab consolidation after CRT than those harboring EGFR, ALK, or BRAF mutations.463 However, current studies are limited by their retrospective nature, and large-scale prospective studies are warranted in the future.

Overall, targeted therapy, including induction, concurrent combination, and maintenance therapy in conjunction with CRT, has gradually come to play a critical role in the treatment of stage III unresectable oncogene-driven NSCLC, breaking the PACIFIC treatment paradigm for stage III unresectable NSCLC. Currently, the CRT and ICI consolidation modality is being investigated in stage III unresectable oncogene-driven NSCLC as a continuation of the PACIFIC paradigm and shows great survival benefits, indicating that the PACIFIC paradigm is also effectively feasible in oncogene-driven NSCLC. However, which treatment pattern is more effective and safer for these patients: CRT followed by TKI maintenance or CRT followed by ICI maintenance? This question requires further validation in large-scale clinical trials. Moreover, the identification and selection of immune-related biomarkers are important in predicting the response to ICI consolidation, thereby helping in the selection of patients who are likely to benefit from consolidation therapy.

CNS progression during TKI treatment

Although TKIs such as osimertinib and lorlatinib exhibit potent extracranial and intracranial efficacy in oncogene-driven NSCLC, nearly all NSCLC patients eventually develop TKI resistance accompanied by limited or extensive CNS and systemic progression. The treatment for CNS progression after effective TKIs is still a research hotspot involving the continued use of the original TKIs plus radiotherapy or regimen switching targeting the CNS.

Synergistic effects of TKI and radiotherapy

Upfront targeted agents with high BBB penetrability are critical for oncogene-driven NSCLC patients with CNS metastases, especially those with asymptomatic CNS metastases. Radiotherapy, such as stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) or whole-brain radiotherapy (WBRT), is also the cornerstone of treatment for NSCLC patients with CNS metastases, as it provides effective local control. Targeted agents in combination with SRS or WBRT help improve patient prognosis and intracranial control.464,465 However, the effectiveness of radiotherapy in combination with next-generation TKIs has been demonstrated along with increased cytotoxicity. For example, an increased risk of CNS necrosis has been reported, and acute and late chest radiation toxicity, such as lung, esophageal, cardiac, and skin toxicity, has also increased.466,467

Omission of radiotherapy

A question emerges as to whether additional SRS or WBRT can be omitted to avoid radiotherapy-related side effects, such as neurocognitive deficits, in patients with great intracranial remission. Patients with EGFR- and ALK-positive NSCLC were divided into two groups (CNS-penetrant TKI monotherapy vs TKI plus intracranial radiotherapy) for intracranial progression. The study did not show substantial differences in time to progression, time to intracranial progression, or time to treatment failure and concluded that the use of next-generation CNS-penetrant TKIs could delay brain radiotherapy.468 The ASCO-SNO-ASTRO guidelines indicate that patients with symptomatic BM should receive local radiotherapy or surgery despite the targeted therapy used. For patients with asymptomatic BM, local therapy may be delayed only if high-BBB-penetrating targeted agents are used, such as osimertinib for EGFR-positive NSCLC patients, and alectinib or brigatinib for ALK-positive NSCLC patients.469 Notably, a recent large multi-institutional retrospective study reported that upfront SRS in combination with osimertinib or alectinib improved the time to CNS progression and local CNS control in TKI-naïve patients with EGFR- or ALK-positive NSCLC, especially for those with BM ≥ 1 cm, and this finding demonstrated the importance of upfront SRS despite treatment with high brain-penetrating TKIs and challenged the guidelines.470 Further validation with prospective multicenter studies is needed to verify the results.

Response to radiotherapy

The response to radiotherapy varies by tumor molecular status; tumors with EGFR or ALK mutations are more sensitive to radiotherapy than those with KRAS mutations, and patients with EGFR mutations, in particular, derive greater benefit from radiotherapy.471,472 MET mutations are associated with radioresistance in NSCLC, so radiotherapy alone is not a favorable choice for NSCLC patients with BM, and the addition of a MET inhibitor may help enhance radiosensitivity.473

Pulsed dosing

Owing to the limited cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) concentration, disease progression in the CNS may occur under standard-dose TKIs. To address this issue, high-dose pulsed dosing was investigated to improve CNS permeability and increase the TKI concentration in the CSF, which may reach the therapeutic dose to combat CNS metastases more effectively even in drugs with poor BBB permeability. Pulsatile erlotinib was reported to induce great intracranial remission in patients with treated or untreated stable BM.474 Similarly, patients who received increasing doses of osimertinib from 80 mg to 160 mg after intracranial progression achieved an intracranial ORR of 54% and DCR of 72%. Notably, patients with isolated intracranial progression had an excellent intracranial ORR of 83%, indicating the optimal population for dose escalation.475 Given its tolerable safety profile, the high-dose schedule of EGFR TKIs is considered feasible. Further elucidation of the most appropriate dose, as well as the safety, effectiveness, and pharmacokinetics for each dose group is needed.

Overall, when oncogene-driven NSCLC patients without BM develop localized intracranial progression after TKI treatment, SRS and continued TKI treatment could be considered. Continued TKI treatment combined with local radiotherapy could improve survival outcomes and disease control. Notably, the rate of resistance mutation detection significantly increases at the time of radiological progression compared with that at baseline. Thus, it is essential to detect mutations to switch to next-generation potent TKIs, which are also effective treatments. In addition, TKI dose doubling, as we have stated previously, is also a valid targeting approach in this population. For patients with progression on LM, intracranial molecular testing is difficult to perform when the disease progresses. In patients with LM-only progression, the addition of other therapies (chemotherapy/antiangiogenic therapy/radiotherapy) to the original osimertinib regimen and dose escalation of osimertinib were superior to regimen switching, whereas no such benefit was seen in those with systemic progression.476 When further intracranial lesion progression occurs, palliative systemic therapy is generally administered.

Challenge and future directions

Treatment options for patients with co-mutations

The incidence of NSCLC patients harboring co-occurring oncogenic mutations is approximately 1.5%, most of whom harbor EGFR mutations.477 Studies have shown that co-occurring mutations, such as EGFR/MET/HER2 amplification, ALK rearrangement, PIK3CA amplification, CDK4/6 mutation, and TP53 mutation, cooperate to promote tumor metastasis, worsen the prognosis, and influence the response to TKI treatment in patients with NSCLC.477–479

Co-occurring mutations result in tumor heterogeneity; however, studies on the biological and clinical characteristics of specific co-mutations are insufficient. One study reported that KRAS-mutant NSCLC can be classified based on co-mutations. Co-occurring mutation-associated tumors exhibit different tumor characteristics, such as lower expression levels of immune markers of KL tumors (STK11/LKB1 co-mutation) and higher expression levels of immune checkpoint effector molecules with improved RFS in KP tumors (TP53 co-mutation). This study suggests that further classification of NSCLC based on oncogenic co-mutations could help better identify biological features and develop more tailored treatments for patients.480

These combined mutations may play crucial roles in TKI resistance. For KRAS-mutant NSCLC patients with STK11, KEAP1, or TP53 co-mutations, immunotherapy combined with an antiangiogenic agent and chemotherapy could be effective.481 Notably, primary genetic abnormalities and secondary resistance mutations have distinct impacts on treatment decisions. More targeted large randomized clinical trials still need to be conducted in NSCLC patients with primary and secondary oncogenic co-mutations to achieve individualized and effective treatment strategies.

The heterogeneous nature of central nervous system metastases and treatment optimization challenges

The baseline characteristics of the enrolled patients with BM varied across different studies, which may affect treatment efficacy and survival. Kotecha et al. reported that patients with EGFR + , HER2 + , and BRAF V600+ mutations exhibited survival differences according to the number of lesions or intracranial disease volume. Additionally, multiple metastases and oligometastases tend to have different responses.482 Subgroup analyses of intracranial efficacy and survival on the basis of the number or volume of BMs in patients with oncogene-driven NSCLC and BM have rarely been performed, and the addition of these analyses may improve the treatment of the target population.

Moreover, in some trials, systematic baseline craniocerebral examination is not mandatory for asymptomatic patients, which may lead to an underestimation of baseline BM and thereby resulting in a higher incidence of BM detected during treatment. Most current studies exclude patients with symptomatic or unstable BM, resulting in limited intracranial data for these patients. Although some trials enroll both asymptomatic and symptomatic patients, the clinical benefits are often analyzed in the overall population and compared with those of the asymptomatic BM population from other trials, which fails to reflect real-world scenarios accurately. In fact, symptomatic status and palliative treatments may influence intracranial efficacy and survival. Therefore, conclusions regarding differential intracranial efficacy between these populations and the suitability of these drugs as first-line therapies for patients with oncogene-driven NSCLC and symptomatic CNS metastases should be drawn with caution.

Isolated CNS progression is a distinct disease progression from oligoprogression or widespread systemic progression, and different treatment approaches are adopted for each. Currently, few prospective studies have evaluated intracranial efficacy in patients with oncogene-driven NSCLC experiencing isolated CNS progression. Such progression is presumed to reflect the limited bioavailability of the drug in the CNS rather than acquired resistance to the therapy.483 A phase II trial demonstrated that isolated CNS progression during crizotinib may remain ROS1-dependent and could be more sensitive to lorlatinib than multisite systemic progression.483 Further detailed studies are needed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms and develop more tailored targeted therapies for these patients.

Challenges and innovations in genomic testing for oncogenic mutations

The detection of EGFR, BRAF, and KRAS hotspot mutations is typically performed via allele-specific PCR, which does not require sophisticated infrastructure or extensive laboratory resources. For more complex genetic alterations, such as ALK, ROS1, RET, NTRK1/2/3 rearrangements, and MET exon 14 skipping mutations, NGS is often necessary.484 Current NGS technologies largely meet the needs of genetic testing in NSCLC,485 but lack advantages in terms of cost and turnaround time and may suffer from insufficient sensitivity.486

Artificial intelligence (AI) has significantly improved the precision and efficiency of genetic testing for oncogenic mutations in NSCLC through deep learning algorithms and high-throughput data integration, demonstrating particular strengths in detecting complex co-mutations.487,488 Future directions include improving detection sensitivity and specificity through technological innovation, fostering multidisciplinary collaboration for real-time dynamic monitoring of resistance mutations, and optimizing personalized treatment strategies for patients.

The new era of biomarker-guided adaptive treatment

With the advancement of precision medicine and the increasing demand to balance efficacy and quality of life as patients’ survival periods extend, traditional standard treatments are gradually unable to meet clinical needs, giving rise to the concept of adaptive personalized treatment. Adaptive treatment utilizes biomarkers to precisely select eligible patients and implements escalation or de-escalation of treatment on the basis of standard therapies to achieve superior efficacy, improved quality of life, and improved cost-effectiveness. However, premature or excessive combination therapy may increase the incidence of toxic adverse effects. Therefore, accurately identifying patients for whom standard treatments are insufficient and providing them with cost-effective therapy remain an important challenge.

Identifying patients who are likely to benefit from escalation or de-escalation therapy requires effective clinical features and reliable biomarkers. Recent studies have demonstrated that ctDNA minimal residual disease (MRD) can effectively reflect tumor burden and detect resistant mutations months before clinical progression, with the FDA supporting escalation therapy for positive MRD patients and de-escalation therapy for negative MRD patients.489–492 However, evidence supporting the application of MRD-driven escalation therapy in routine clinical practice is still limited, and the definition and clinical application of escalation therapy are still under investigation. A multicenter study (NCT06780839) aims to compare the use of ensartinib with or without combined chemotherapy in the postsurgical treatment of ALK-positive NSCLC under the guidance of ctDNA-MRD monitoring.

For patients who can achieve long-term tumor control with single-target therapy and local treatment, exploring TKI de-escalation treatment strategies through ctDNA-MRD monitoring is worth investigating. A study by Wu et al. demonstrated that an initiative “drug holiday” strategy (treatment break) following local treatment was an effective adaptive de-escalation approach for patients with advanced oligometastatic disease.493 In advanced EGFR/ALK/ROS1-positive NSCLC patients who achieved radiologically negative disease and undetectable ctDNA after targeted therapy and local treatment, the mPFS was 18.4 months, and 75% of patients benefited from this de-escalation strategy.493 This study is the first to demonstrate the feasibility of ctDNA-guided TKI de-escalation therapy. Dynamic treatment adjustment not only reduces drug toxicity and alleviates patient burden but also delays the emergence of drug resistance while preserving therapeutic efficacy. Additionally, another study provides preliminary support for the application of ctDNA-MRD monitoring in adjuvant EGFR TKI de-escalation strategies.494 Ongoing prospective clinical trials, such as CTONG 2105, CTONG 2101, and ECTOP-1022, are further exploring MRD-guided adaptive treatment strategies for patients with EGFR mutations. Notably, another “drug holiday” strategy for overcoming EGFR TKI resistance, which involves TKI discontinuation, chemotherapy administration, and subsequent original TKI reinitiation (e.g., erlotinib or gefitinib), has been explored in several small-scale studies.495–497 In patients with advanced NSCLC harboring EGFR-sensitizing mutations and secondary T790M mutations, osimertinib retreatment has shown moderate efficacy and manageable toxicity, with an ORR and DCR of 33% and 73%, respectively.498 However, this resistance management strategy lacks support from large-scale clinical studies and carries the risk of tumor progression during this period; therefore, it has not been established as a standard approach for overcoming resistance in clinical practice. Compared with the “drug holiday” strategy proposed by Wu’s team, the two approaches differ fundamentally in their timing of application and selection criteria.

Currently, MRD testing has significant clinical potential in adaptive treatment; however, additional evidence from clinical studies is needed to address key issues. Determining the optimal timing and frequency of MRD monitoring and selecting appropriate testing methods to ensure high specificity (positive predictive value) for escalation treatment and high sensitivity (negative predictive value) for de-escalation treatment are critical. Therefore, establishing standardized ctDNA testing protocols and clinical guidelines to ensure the reliability and consistency of results across different platforms and institutions is essential. Current ctDNA-MRD technologies still have limitations in monitoring brain metastases, and developing innovative testing technologies to overcome the BBB penetration and create cost-effective solutions is vital for expanding their clinical applicability.

Current dilemmas of new drugs

The development of fourth-generation EGFR TKIs, optimized for C797S mutation resistance, has clinical potential, providing a new targeted therapy option for patients who are either intolerant to chemotherapy or resistant to prior treatments. The primary challenge for fourth-generation EGFR TKIs lies in the complex resistance mechanisms that emerge after osimertinib resistance, including intrinsic resistance (e.g., L747P and L718Q) and compound mutations (e.g., MET amplification, HER2 mutation). Furthermore, tumor cells may continuously acquire new mutations, with resistant clones replacing sensitive clones, reducing the effectiveness of single-target therapies.

Several fourth-generation TKIs have demonstrated significant toxicity in clinical studies, with notable adverse effects on the liver and heart. The long-term safety and tolerability of these agents also remain a challenge. Therefore, despite breakthroughs in overcoming C797S mutation resistance, fourth-generation drugs still face issues related to resistance, drug safety, and narrow therapeutic windows. Current clinical data on these drugs are incomplete, especially regarding their efficacy in the context of multiple mutations.

Conclusions

In summary, alterations in the TIME and sustained activation of receptor tyrosine kinase signaling pathways may facilitate metastasis in patients with NSCLC. The development of highly selective, brain-penetrant TKIs can significantly reduce the size of both primary tumors and metastases, convert unresectable tumors into resectable ones, and provide substantial survival benefits for patients with oncogene-driven NSCLC. However, drug resistance often emerges following initial treatment. Strategies targeting resistance mechanisms, such as next-generation TKIs, dual-targeting regimens, or combinations of targeted therapy, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and antiangiogenic therapy, have been proven to be effective. The development of more efficient methods for detecting secondary resistance mutations and the optimization of drug administration regimens remain major issues in clinical practice, particularly for patients harboring rare oncogenic mutations.
