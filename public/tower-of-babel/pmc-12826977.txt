Oncolytic HSV-1 expressing GM-CSF and IL-12 enhances anti-tumor efficacy in immunocompetent murine melanoma model
Authors: Han Xiao, Qiran Yin, Jia Liu, Hengrui Hu, Jiang Li, Manli Wang, Zhihong Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOncolytic virus therapy is a promising strategy for cancer treatment. Herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1) has been successfully used in oncolytic virotherapy. In the present research, we applied an HSV-1 synthetic genomics platform to construct two oncolytic viruses, oHSV-1.1 and oHSV-1.2. oHSV-1.1 had the virulence gene ICP34.5 and ICP47 deleted for attenuation, and oHSV-1.2 was additionally armed with murine granulocyte macrophage-colony stimulating factor (GM-CSF) and interleukin-12 (IL-12). The oncolytic viruses were evaluated in vitro and in an immunocompetent murine melanoma model. The animal experiments confirmed that both oncolytic viruses displayed antitumor efficacy, including inhibiting tumor growth and prolonging overall survival. Compared with oHSV-1.1, oHSV-1.2 demonstrated superior tumor growth suppression and enhanced antitumor efficacies, as evidenced by increased tumor cell apoptosis, cytotoxic T cells and macrophages infiltration, IFN-γ production, and upregulation of inflammatory-related gene expression. Our research highlights the potential of oncolytic HSV-1 expressing both GM-CSF and IL-12 for melanoma therapy, and provides a promising strategy for further development of oncolytic virotherapy.Keywords: Herpes simplex virus type I (HSV-1), Oncolytic virus, Synthetic genomics, Melanoma, GM-CSF, IL-12

Introduction

Oncolytic virotherapy has become a promising strategy in cancer immunotherapy (Peters et al., 2019; Volovat et al., 2024). Oncolytic viruses are a series of genetically engineered virus that can selectively replicate in tumor cells, cause cell death and stimulate antitumor immune responses (Lawler et al., 2017; Ma et al., 2023). Oncolytic viruses facilitate their oncolysis through two major mechanisms: direct killing of tumor cells and induction of host antitumor immunity (Kaufman et al., 2015). The direct killing of oncolytic virus mainly relies on viral replication in tumor cells. And during the tumor cell lysis, the released tumor associated antigens are recognized by antigen presenting cells, thereby activate systemic antitumor immunity (Schietinger et al., 2010). Since the oncolytic virotherapy can reverse the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, administration of oncolytic virus could potentially promote the efficacy of other cancer immunotherapy such as immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy, which makes oncolytic virotherapy more efficient (Harrington et al., 2019; Shen et al., 2025). Over the past decades, the industry of oncolytic virus has been rapidly developed. Currently, several oncolytic virus products have been approved and various oncolytic virus projects are under clinical trial stage worldwide (Li and Cheng, 2024).

Among the various viral backbones, herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1) is one of the most commonly-used backbone for oncolytic virus development (Zhou and Shen, 2025). HSV-1 is a large DNA virus that causes prevalent infections in humans worldwide (Whitley and Roizman, 2001). It has been widely studied, which provides theoretical support for the application of HSV-1-based oncolytic virus (Koch et al., 2020). The advantages of using HSV-1 as oncolytic virus include its large genome capacity, low mutation risk, and broad tissue tropism for infection (Tang et al., 2023). HSV-1-based oncolytic viruses have been extensively investigated in preclinical studies of solid tumor treatments (Aldrak et al., 2021; Hong et al., 2022). Currently, two HSV-1 oncolytic viruses, T-VEC (Talimogene laherparepvec) and G47Δ (teserpaturev), have been approved for the treatment of melanoma and glioblastoma, respectively (Ferrucci et al., 2021; Frampton, 2022). Despite the considerable progress, current HSV-1 oncolytic viruses still show some drawbacks, particularly their inadequate stimulation of antitumor immunity (Chowaniec et al., 2024). Therefore, further investigation is needed to optimize the modification for oncolytic HSV-1.

The oncolytic modification of viral genome can be typically classified into two aspects: virulence attenuation and antitumor immune activation. The attenuation modification involves restraining viral replication and inhibiting viral immune evasion (Tian et al., 2022). For HSV-1 genome, the general strategy of attenuation modification is deleting viral genes related with replication and immune evasion, such as ICP34.5, ICP47, and ICP6 (Kanai et al., 2012; Ferrucci et al., 2021; Ni et al., 2022). Apart from those mentioned above, the deletion of other viral genes including ICP0, UL23 (thymidine kinase), UL20, and UL53 (gK), have also been reported (Uche et al., 2021; Omole et al., 2022; Nabi, R. et al., 2023; Nabi, Rafiq et al., 2025). Among the various attenuation modifications, the deletion of ICP34.5 and ICP47 is most prevalent approach as it reduce the neurovirulence and prevents the inhibition of antigen presentation (Chou and Roizman, 1994; York et al., 1994). To enhance the stimulation of antitumor immune responses induced by oncolytic HSV-1, various genes expressing pro-inflammatory cytokines, co-stimulatory molecules, or immune checkpoint inhibitors have been inserted into the HSV-1 genome. For example, granulocyte macrophage-colony stimulating factor (GM-CSF) is one of the cytokines that has been extensively applied in oncolytic modification (Raman et al., 2019). As a multifunctional cytokine, GM-CSF plays important roles in proliferation and differentiation of neutrophils, macrophages, and dendritic cells (Achuthan et al., 2021). In the approved oncolytic T-VEC, the expressing cassette of GM-CSF has been inserted into the HSV-1 viral genome. Apart from GM-CSF, cytokines from the family of interleukin (IL) are also been widely reported (Nguyen et al., 2020; Mullins-Dansereau et al., 2025; Shen et al., 2025). IL-12 is one of the most commonly used cytokines among the interleukins for oncolytic modification. The main functions of IL-12 include promoting the proliferation and differentiation of T helper cells and NK cells (Kim et al., 2021). Oncolytic HSV-1 expressing IL-12 have been proved to be effective in preclinical studies, and relevant clinical trials have been conducted (Cheema et al., 2013; Haines et al., 2021). Since GM-CSF and IL-12 stimulate the myeloid cell-associated immune response and lymphoid cell-associated immune response, respectively, the combination of GM-CSF and IL-12 has demonstrated synergistic oncolytic efficacy in viral and non-viral oncolytic studies (Kim et al., 2021; Kang et al., 2024).

Here, we report the design, construction and evaluation of two oncolytic viruses, oHSV-1.1 and oHSV-1.2, based on our previously established synthetic platform using transformation-associated recombination (TAR) (Xiao et al., 2024). In comparison with the parental virus F-Syn (Xiao et al., 2024), oHSV-1.1 had the viral gene ICP34.5 and ICP47 deleted to attenuate virulence, and oHSV-1.2 was further armed with murine GM-CSF and IL-12 to enhance the activation of antitumor immune response. The replication dynamics and virus associated cytotoxicity of oHSV-1.1 and oHSV-1.2 were evaluated in murine melanoma cells. Additionally, we assessed the oncolytic efficacy of the two viruses in immunocompetent murine melanoma model, and investigated the activation of antitumor immune responses triggered by the oHSVs.

Result

Construction and characterization of oHSV-1.1 and oHSV-1.2

The synthetic oncolytic viruses oHSV-1.1 and oHSV-1.2 were designed based on F-Syn genome (Xiao et al., 2024). For oHSV-1.1, coding sequences of viral gene ICP34.5 and ICP47 were deleted to attenuate the virulence. For oHSV-1.2, apart from the above deletions, the expressing cassette of murine granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (mGM-CSF) was inserted between the open reading frame of UL22 and UL23, and tandem expressing cassette of mIL-12β and mIL-12α was inserted between the open reading frame of US7 and US8. Both oHSV-1.1 and oHSV-1.2 retained the mGFP-2A-eGFP cassette in F-Syn (Fig. 1A).

oHSV-1.1 and oHSV-1.2 were constructed and rescued using TAR cloning in yeast followed by co-transfection in Vero cells (Fig. 1B). Vero cells were individually infected with the transfection supernatants of oHSV-1.1 or oHSV-1.2. Fluorescence and cytopathic effects were observed in both samples, indicating that oHSV-1.1 and oHSV-1.2 were successfully rescued (Fig. 1C). To compare the replication dynamics of oHSVs and parental virus F-Syn, growth curve assays were performed at high infection multiplicity (MOI = 3) and low infection multiplicity (MOI = 0.001) in Vero cells. Both oHSV-1.1 and oHSV-1.2 exhibited attenuated replication dynamics compared with F-Syn in different infection multiplicity; no statistical significance was detected between the replication of oHSV-1.1 and oHSV-1.2 (Fig. 1D).

For oHSV-1.2, we further detected the expressing of mGM-CSF and mIL-12 in infectious supernatants using ELISA assay. The results showed that significant levels of mGM-CSF and mIL-12 were secreted into supernatants of Vero cells infected by oHSV-1.2, compared with those infected with F-Syn and oHSV-1.1 (Fig. 1E). The result indicates that oHSV-1.2 infection can successfully induce the production of mGM-CSF and mIL-12.

To obtain the concrete sequences of viral genome, genomic DNA of oHSV-1.1 and oHSV-1.2 were extracted from infectious supernatants and processed for genome sequencing. The results showed that the genome sequences were as designed, although some point mutations were identified. Four point mutations occurred in oHSV-1.1 genome, resulting in three amino acid changes in UL39, UL41, and US8 protein (Table 1). For oHSV-1.2, apart from the aforementioned four point mutations, two additional mutations occurred, includes one amino acid changes in UL9 protein (Table 1). Genomic sequences oHSV-1.1 and oHSV-1.2 have been submitted to GenBank under accession number PV844764 and PV844765, respectively.

In vitro oncolytic assay of oHSV-1.1 and oHSV-1.2 in murine melanoma cells

To evaluate the replication dynamics and virus associated cytotoxicity of oHSV-1.1 and oHSV-1.2 in murine melanoma B16-nectin1 cells, we first performed infection and growth curve assay. Observation of fluorescence revealed that F-Syn could exert competent infection in B16-nectin1 cells in both high (MOI = 3) and low MOI (MOI = 0.1). However, in comparison with F-Syn, oHSV-1.1 and oHSV-1.2 manifested a significantly weaker infection (Fig. 2A). Growth curve analysis confirmed that in B16-nectin1 cells, oHSV-1.1 and oHSV-1.2 had a significant reduction of replication at both high and low MOI compared with parental virus F-Syn (Fig. 2B). Cytotoxicity assay in B16-nectin1 cells showed that virus-associated cytotoxicity of oHSV-1.1 and oHSV-1.2 had no significant difference at MOIs of 1 and 10, and both were lower than that of F-Syn (Fig. 2C). Overall, the results suggest that the in vitro infection of oHSV-1.1 and oHSV-1.2 in B16-nectin1 cells is limited. Nevertheless, we could still detect significant mGM-CSF and mIL-12 production in the supernatant of oHSV-1.2 infected B16-nectin1 cells (Fig. 2D).

oHSVs administration inhibits tumor growth in immunocompetent murine melanoma model

To investigate the oncolysis effects of oHSV-1.1 and oHSV-1.2 in vivo, we used an immunocompetent murine melanoma model. B16-nectin1 cells were subcutaneously injected into the flank of C57BL/6 mice for tumor implantation. When tumor volume reached about 100 mm3, mice were intratumorally administrated with 1 × PBS (control), oHSV-1.1 or oHSV-1.2 for five consecutive doses, respectively (Fig. 3A). Mice from the control group exhibited a rapid tumor growth, and their tumor volume all exceeded 1500 mm3 with 18 days post implantation. For oHSV-1.1 and oHSV-1.2 treated group, tumor exhibited a slow rate of growth compared with the control group (Fig. 3B and C). Analysis of survival curve revealed that oHSV-1.1 and oHSV-1.2 treated group significantly prolonged the median survival time of mice compared with the control group (Fig. 3D). oHSV-1.2 treated group displayed longer survival time compared with oHSV-1.1 treated group, albeit no statistical significance was determined (P = 0.0607). Statistics of overall body weight showed that mice from all groups kept steady weight during the treatments, indicating that the oncolytic treatment do not trigger obvious adverse effect (Fig. 3E).

Oncolytic effects triggered by oHSVs in tumor tissues

To further evaluate the oncolytic effects induced by oHSV-1.1 and oHSV-1.2 in immunocompetent murine melanoma model, we performed an independent experiment. Mice were individually administrated with same dose of PBS, oHSV-1.1 or oHSV-1.2 after tumor implantation. All mice were euthanized at 2 days post final dose of treatments and samples were harvested for further evaluation (Fig. 4A). Tumors were observed and weighted, and the result showed that oHSV-1.2 treatment significantly reduced the tumor growth compared with control group and oHSV-1.1 group (Fig. 4B and C), which is consistent with our previous experiment (Fig. 3B and C). oHSV-1.1 treatment also reduced the overall tumor growth compared with the control group, albeit no statistical significance was determined in this experiment (Fig. 4B and C).

To investigate the in situ oncolytic effects triggered by oHSVs, tumor samples harvested at 2 days post final dose of treatments were subjected to histological analysis. Tumor tissues were first analyzed by hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining. In the control group, high density distribution of capillary was observed across the tumor tissue, and melanoma cells clustered around the capillary with high cellularity and nuclear polymorphism. In oHSV-1.1 treated group, necrotic area and nuclear debris of tumor cells were observed, indicating lesions occur in the tumor tissue. In the oHSV-1.2 treated group, few viable tumor cells were observed, and severe lesions associated with ischemia were distributed in most areas of the tumor tissue (Fig. 4D).

TUNEL staining was performed to investigate whether apoptosis is associated with the melanoma cell death. The result showed that oHSV-1.2 treated tumor exhibited obviously more signals of apoptosis compared with control group and oHSV-1.1 treated group (Fig. 4E). This result suggests that the higher level of apoptosis induced by oHSV-1.2 treatment may contribute to the significant damages in the tumor tissue.

Additionally, tumor samples were subjected to ELISA assay to detect the in situ production of mGM-CSF and mIL-12 post oHSV-1.2 treatment. Expression of mGM-CSF varied among the individuals in oHSV-1.2 group, whereas oHSV-1.2 treated tumors displayed significantly higher expression of mIL-12 compared with tumors from control group and oHSV-1.1 group (Fig. 4F and G). This result suggests that oHSV-1.2 treatment could induce detectable in situ production of mIL-12 in tumor.

oHSV-1.2 triggered significantly higher antitumor immune responses

Immunofluorescence staining of CD8 revealed that in the oHSVs treated tumors, CD8+ cells were observed with stronger positive signals in the oHSV-1.1 and oHSV-1.2 treated samples; while hardly any CD8+ cells were observed in the control group (Fig. 5A). Compared with oHSV-1.1 group, oHSV-1.2 group showed significantly stronger CD8+ fluorescence intensity (Fig. 5B). Immunofluorescence staining of CD68 showed that basal level of macrophages infiltration was observed in control tumors and oHSV-1.1 treated tumors, and significant enhancement of macrophages infiltration was detected in oHSV-1.2 treated tumors (Fig. 5C and D). Overall, these results indicate that oHSVs treatments, especially oHSV-1.2 treatment, could enhance the infiltration of cytotoxic T cells and macrophages in tumor tissue.

To detect specific antitumor immunity against murine melanoma cells, splenocytes from each mouse were incubated with B16-nectin1 cells, and expression of IFN-γ was measured using ELISPOT assay. The result showed that for control group and oHSV-1.1 group, little specific immune response was detected post B16-nectin1 incubation, whereas splenocytes from oHSV-1.2 treated group induced a significantly higher level of IFN-γ production, suggesting that oHSV-1.2 treatment could efficiently activates specific antitumoral immune responses against tumor antigens (Fig. 6A and B).

Systemic antitumoral immune responses were further evaluated by quantifying mRNA level of inflammatory genes in tumor, spleen, and tumor draining lymph node (TDLN) using qRT-PCR. The results showed that significantly higher transcription level of IFN-γ was occurred in tumor post oHSV-1.2 treatment. Albeit no statistical significance was determined in comparison with the control group, slight increase of IFN-γ transcription was detected in spleen samples from oHSV-1.2 groups. No significance of IFN-γ transcription was observed among the TDLN samples (Fig. 6C). For granzyme B (GZMB), significantly higher transcription level was detected in oHSV-1.2 treated tumor, and both oHSV-1.1 and oHSV-1.2 treatments enhanced the transcription of GZMB in spleen and TDLN (Fig. 6D). Overall, these results demonstrated that oHSV-1.2 treatment triggered systemic antitumor immune responses in immunocompetent murine melanoma model.

Discussion

Oncolytic virus exerts the oncolysis effect via two major mechanisms: direct cell killing and stimulation of antitumor immunity. Viral replication leads to the lysis of tumor cells and further release of tumor associated antigens in tumor microenvironment, which could convert immunogenic “cold tumor” into “hot tumor” (Schietinger et al., 2010; Zamarin et al., 2014). To enhance the immune activation from oncolytic virotherapy, a general strategy is to express pro-inflammatory molecules by oncolytic viruses. In this study, we constructed oHSV-1.2, an oncolytic HSV-1 expressing murine GM-CSF and IL-12 to promote the stimulation of antitumor immunity. As a multifunctional cytokine, GM-CSF facilitates macrophages proliferation and antigen recognition, and strengthens antigen presentation (Helft et al., 2015; Achuthan et al., 2021). IL-12 facilitates the differentiation of T cells, which can promote the expression of IFN-γ, leads to the activation of NK cells and cytotoxic T cells (Kodama et al., 1999). In previous research, oncolytic HSV-1 expressing solely GM-CSF or IL-12 have been approved or step into clinical trials (Ferrucci et al., 2021; Shen et al., 2025). Oncolytic HSV-1 expressing both GM-CSF and IL-12 have been reported, but in a ICP6 deleted backbone of HSV-1 KOS strain (Kim et al., 2021). Among the various HSV-1 strains, HSV-1 F strain have been widely studied in oncolytic research (Todo et al., 2001; Fukuhara et al., 2005). Hence, in this study, we constructed oHSV-1.2 in which mGM-CSF and mIL-12 were inserted in HSV-1 F strain backbone deleting ICP34.5 and ICP47, and evaluated its oncolytic efficacy along with its control oncolytic virus oHSV-1.1 (without expressing mGM-CSF and mIL-12).

Oncolysis assay of oHSV-1.1 and oHSV-1.2 in immunocompetent murine melanoma model showed that the oHSVs treatment significantly prolonged the survival time, although neither oHSV-1.1 nor oHSV-1.2 could induce complete tumor regression (Fig. 3D). Notably, in oHSV-1.1 treated group, tumor growth in half of mice had been significantly inhibited. By checking the tumor volume data, we found that the oncolysis was more efficient in the individuals with smaller initial tumor volume in oHSV-1.1 treated group. These data suggest the dose or frequency of the oncolytic treatment we used was inadequate. Compared with oHSV-1.1, oHSV-1.2 treatment significantly inhibited the tumor growth (Fig. 4B and C). In tumor tissue, tumor lysis and apoptosis of tumor cells were more evident in oHSV-1.2 treated group (Fig. 4D and E). Moreover, in oHSV-1.2 treated mice, more extensive infiltration of cytotoxic T cells and macrophages was observed in the tumor tissue; splenocytes exerted stronger specific antitumor immune response after incubation with tumor associated antigens; and the elevated expression of inflammatory genes were observed in multiple sites including tumor, spleen and TDLN (Fig. 5, Fig. 6). Overall, these results demonstrated that oHSV-1.2 boosted stronger systemic antitumor immunity in comparison to oHSV-1.1, attributed to the synergistic effects of GM-CSF and IL-12 (Fig. 7).

Schematic diagram of the synergistic oncolytic effects induced by oncolytic HSV-1 expressing GM-CSF and IL-12. A In oHSV-1.1 treated tumor, modest level of apoptosis was induced, and limited antitumor immune responses in spleen and TDLN were detected. B In oHSV-1.2 treated tumor, GM-CSF and IL-12 were expressed by the oncolytic virus, which facilitate the immune responses and tumor infiltration of macrophages and cytotoxic T cells, respectively. Robust apoptosis was induced in the tumor, and activated immune responses including upregulated IFN-γ and GZMB production were detected in spleen and TDLN. Altogether, these resulted in improved oncolytic efficacy in the oHSV-1.2 treated animals.

We noticed that compared with the parental virus F-Syn, the replication and cytotoxicity of oHSV-1.1 and oHSV-1.2 were significantly attenuated (Fig. 2B). These results are likely caused by the deletion of virulence related genes ICP34.5 as reported before (Perng et al., 1995). In addition, several mutations have been found in oHSV-1.1 and oHSV-1.2 (Table 1), which may also contribute to the reduced viral replication and cytotoxicity. UL9, UL39, UL41, and US8 encodes for origin binding protein, large subunit of ribonucleotide reductase, tegument protein for host mRNA degradation, and glycoprotein E, respectively (Jones et al., 1995; Marintcheva and Weller, 2001; Polcicova et al., 2005; Hu et al., 2020). The impacts of these mutations need to be further investigated.

Virulence attenuation is necessary for the security of oncolytic virotherapy, whereas it also limits the direct oncolysis induced by viral infection. To overcome this effect, one strategy is to regulate virulence related genes in a tumor specific way, so that oncolytic virus could retain competent replication in tumor without affecting security (Chiocca et al., 2020). Oncolytic HSV-1 projects modified with this strategy have been investigated in phase I clinical trial for glioblastoma therapy (Ling et al., 2023). In future, we plan to apply tumor specific promoter to drive ICP34.5 to improve the direct oncolysis efficacy of our oHSVs. For oHSV-1.2, the detected concentration of mIL-12 was much higher than that of mGM-CSF (Fig. 1, Fig. 2D), indicating that the transcriptional activity of EF1α promoter is stronger than that of SV40 promoter, at least in Vero cells and B16-nectin1 cells. Further optimization of promoter might potentially increase the production of mGM-CSF.

The size and complexity of HSV-1 genome present significant challenge for systematically genetic modifications for optimizing its oncolytic properties through the viral genome for oncolysis using conventional approaches (Perng et al., 1994). Combining TAR cloning in yeast with in vitro CRISPR/Cas9 editing, HSV-1 genome can be efficiently modified in multiple sites simultaneously, facilitate the systematic design and construction of oncolytic viruses (Xiao et al., 2024). The successful construction and rescue of oHSV-1.1 and oHSV-1.2 further demonstrated the advantage of the platform. In the future, we plan to further modify the oncolytic HSV-1 by improving its direct tumor killing ability and by inserting different antitumor exogenous genes. The constructed oHSVs will also be tested in different models, not limited to the murine melanoma model, to facilitate the development of oncolytic virotherapy.

Conclusions

In summary, we designed and constructed two oncolytic viruses, oHSV-1.1 and oHSV-1.2 using synthetic genomics. oHSV-1.2 was armed with murine GM-CSF and IL-12 to activate systemic antitumor immune responses. Both oHSV-1.1 and oHSV-1.2 were evaluated in immunocompetent murine melanoma model, and oHSV-1.2 treatment exhibited more optimized oncolytic effects. Our research highlights the potential of oncolytic HSV-1 armed with GM-CSF and IL-12 for melanoma therapy.

Materials and methods

Cell culture, virus, strain and plasmid

Vero cell (ATCC no: CCL-81) was purchased from ATCC and maintained in our laboratory. Murine melanoma cell line B16-nectin1 was kindly provided by Prof. Hongkai Zhang of Nankai University. B16-nectin1 cells were constructed by stably expressing nectin-1, a human receptor for HSV-1 infection, in murine melanoma cell line B16 (Linehan et al., 2004). Vero cells were cultured in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco). B16-nectin1 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS. All cells were cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO2. The synthetic HSV-1 F-Syn and TAR cloning vector pTZ were constructed in our previous research (Xiao et al., 2024). F-Syn was propagated and titrated on Vero cells. S. cerevisiae strain VL6-48, E. coli strain EPI300, and the TAR cloning vector pGF were gifts from Prof. Gengfu Xiao of the Wuhan Institute of Virology, Chinese Academy of Sciences, and maintained in our laboratory (Hou et al., 2016).

Construction of oncolytic virus oHSV-1.1 and oHSV-1.2

Both oHSV-1.1 and oHSV-1.2 were constructed using synthetic genomics platform. Briefly, to constructed oHSV-1.1, the coding sequences of viral gene ICP34.5 and ICP47 were deleted from genomic fragments by in vitro CRISPR/Cas9 editing combined with TAR cloning in VL6-48 as reported (Xiao et al., 2024). To construct oHSV-1.2, the coding sequence of mGM-CSF and mIL-12β were obtained by PCR amplification using the cDNAs of murine tumor cell line as template, and the coding sequence of mIL-12α (based on the GenBank accession number BC116851) was obtained by DNA synthesis. The cassette expressing murine GM-CSF regulated by SV40 promoter (pSV40) and terminated with polyA tail was inserted into the downstream of EGFP cassette, and tandem cassette expressing murine IL-12β subunit and IL-12α subunit regulated by EF1α promoter was inserted between US7 and US8 (Fig. 1A). The insertions of expressing cassettes were accomplished by TAR cloning and the corresponding intermediated fragments were constructed using previously reported method (Xiao et al., 2023, 2024). Oncolytic viruses oHSV-1.1 and oHSV-1.2 were rescued by co-transfection in Vero cells with the corresponding modified intermediate fragments (Fig. 1B).

Genome sequencing of oHSV-1.1 and oHSV-1.2

Genomic DNA of oHSV-1.1 and oHSV-1.2 were extracted from infectious supernatants and was processed for Illumina sequencing following the standard protocol of the Sequencing Platform of Huazhong Agricultural University (Wuhan, China). The reads generated by Illumina sequencing were assembled into the SAM format using Bowtie2 (version 2.5.2) with default settings based on the complete genome sequence of F-Syn (GenBank accession number: PP803678) as the reference (Langmead and Salzberg, 2012). The SAM files were further analyzed using Tablet (version 1.21.02.08) (Milne et al., 2012).

Infection assay and growth curve assay

For growth curve assay, B16-nectin1 cells (7.5 × 105 in 6-well plates) were individually infected with F-Syn, oHSV-1.1, or oHSV-1.2 at indicated multiplicity of infection (MOI). The fluorescence of infected cells was observed by fluorescent microscopy, and supernatants of infection were harvested at indicated time points. Viral titers were measured by plaque assay on Vero cells. For each growth curve assay, three independent biological replicates were performed. Growth curves were plotted using GraphPad Prism 8.3.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., USA). Statistical differences between F-Syn, oHSV-1.1, and oHSV-1.2 were analyzed using two-way ANOVA (Grosche et al., 2019).

Cytotoxicity assay

The cytotoxicity of viruses was detected using Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) Reagent (MedChemExpress, USA). Briefly, B16-nectin1 cells (1 × 104 in 96-well plates) were individually infected with F-Syn, oHSV-1.1 and oHSV-1.2 at an MOI of 1 and 10 (PFU/cell). Infected and mock cells were incubated with CCK-8 solution at indicated time phases. The absorbance of each sample at 450 nm was measured by microplate reader (Synergy H1, BioTek, USA). The cytotoxicity of virus was defined as the ratio between the absorbance of infected cells and that of the mock cells. All assays were performed with three independent biological repeats, and cytotoxicity curves were plotted using GraphPad Prism 8.3.0. Statistical differences of the cytotoxicity between F-Syn, oHSV-1.1, and oHSV-1.2 were analyzed by two-way ANOVA.

ELISA assay

To detect the expression of mGM-CSF and mIL-12 in infectious supernatants, Vero cells and B16-nectin1 cells (2 × 105 in 12-well plates) were infected with F-Syn, oHSV-1.1 and oHSV-1.2 at an MOI of 3 (PFU/cell), and infectious supernatants were harvested at 48 h post infection. Concentration of mGM-CSF was measured using Mouse GM-CSF ELISA Kit (Solarbio, China), and concentration of mIL-12 was measured using Mouse IL-12 p70 ELISA Kit (Beyotime, China). To detect the expression of mGM-CSF and mIL-12 in tumor tissue, tumor tissues were harvested from different treatment groups of the animal experiment. Tumor tissues (0.1 g) were grinded and resuspended in 500 μL 1 × phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and concentration of mGM-CSF and mIL-12 were measured using the aforementioned ELISA kits. Statistical differences of the protein concentration between different groups were analyzed by one-way ANOVA.

Animal experiments

Six-week female C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Charles River (Beijing, China). For tumor implantation, B16-nectin1 cells (2 × 105) were suspended in 75 μL of 1 × PBS in ice bath, and mixed with 25 μL Matrigel (356234, Corning, USA). About 100 μL of mixture was injected subcutaneously into the right flank of each C57BL/6 mouse. Mice were observed every other day, and diameter of tumor was measured by caliper. Tumor volume (V) was calculated by the formula of V = 0.5 × (L × W2), which L and W denotes the longest and narrowest diameter of tumor (Faustino-Rocha et al., 2013). When tumor volume reached about 100 mm3, mice were randomly divided into three groups and intratumorally injected with 1 × PBS, 5 × 106 PFU of oHSV-1.1 or oHSV-1.2, respectively. Each mouse received five doses of injection for five consecutive days. Tumor volumes were measured every other day, and mice were euthanized when tumor volume exceed 1500 mm3. Tumor volume curve and Kaplan-Meier survival curve were plotted by GraphPad Prism 8.3.0, and the statistical differences of survival curve between different treatment groups was analyzed by Log-Rank test (Dudley et al., 2016). To investigate the details of the oncolytic effects of oHSV-1.1 and oHSV-1.2, an independent animal experiment was conducted as aforementioned, and mice from each group were euthanized on 2 days post the final dose of treatment, then tumor, spleen, and tumor draining lymph node (TDLN) for each mouse were harvested for further detection. The breed of mice and tumor implantation were performed under specific pathogen-free condition, and oncolysis experiments were performed in animal bio-safety level 2 laboratory at animal center of Wuhan Institute of Virology. All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Ethics Committee of Wuhan Institute of Virology, Chinese Academy of Science (WIVA01202403).

Hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining

Tumor samples were fixed by 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde and then dehydrated. Dehydrated tumors were embedded in paraffin, and cut into 3 μm slides for further processing. For H&E staining, embedded tumor slides were deparaffinized and stained by hematoxylin and eosin. The stained tumor slides were observed and scanned using Pannoramic SCAN II system (3DHISTECH, Hungary). The images of scanning were processed by CaseViewer software (version 2.4).

TUNEL staining

For TUNEL staining assay, deparaffinized tumor slides were processed using Fluorescein Tunel Cell Apoptosis Detection Kit (Servicebio, China). Briefly, deparaffinized tumor slides were treated by proteinase K, equilibrated and then labeled with recombinant TdT enzyme and FITC-dUTP. Labeled slides were stained using Hoechst 33258 (Beyotime) to mark the cell nucleus. Stained slides were observed and scanned.

Immunofluorescence staining

For immunofluorescence staining assay, deparaffinized tumor slides were first repaired in citric acid repairing solution (Servicebio) by microwave heating for 20 min, and then treated with 0.1% (w/v) Sudan Black B (Sigma-Aldrich, Germany) for 30 min. The treated slides were blocked by 5% BSA solutions for 2 h at 25 °C, and then respectively incubated with anti-CD8 antibody (98941, Cell Signaling Technology, USA) or anti-CD68 antibody (97778, Cell Signaling Technology) overnight at 4 °C. The incubated slides were washed by 1 × PBS and incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (Abcam, UK) for 2 h at 25 °C. The slides were finally stained with Hoechst 33258 and scanned. To statistically analyze the positive fluorescence intensity, six random fields of section form each group were picked, and fluorescence intensity of CD8 or CD68 were measured using imageJ software (version 1.53t). Statistical significance of CD8 or CD68 fluorescence intensity between different groups was determined using Student t-test.

ELISPOT assay

For ELISPOT assay, spleen samples harvested from each group of mice were grinded and resuspended in 10% RPMI 1640 medium. 1 × 105 splenocytes were incubated with 1 × 105 B16-nectin1 cells for 18 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2. After incubation, IFN-γ was detected by Mouse IFN-γ Precoated ELISPOT Kit (Dakewe, China). IFN-γ specific spots were captured and counted using imageJ software. Statistical differences of IFN-γ specific spots number between different groups were analyzed by one-way ANOVA.

Quantitative real-time PCR assay

Tumor, spleen, and TDLN were harvested from each group of mice, and total RNA was extracted using RNAiso Plus reagent (Takara, Japan). RNA (1 μg) was reverse transcribed into cDNA using HiScript III RT SuperMix with gDNA wiper (Vazyme, China). cDNA (1 μL) for each sample was used for quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) using Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme) to detect the transcription level of IFN-γ and granzyme B (GZMB). Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was set as control gene, and relative gene transcription normalized to GAPDH was calculated by the 2−ΔΔCT method. Statistical differences of the target gene transcription between samples from different group were analyzed by one-way ANOVA. Primers sequences for quantitative real-time PCR listed on Supplementary Table S1.
