Joint passive beamforming and deployment design for active intelligent reflecting surface-assisted energy harvesting-cognitive radio sensor networks
Authors: Jihong Wang, Yanan Zhu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo prolong the network lifetime of energy harvesting-cognitive radio sensor networks (EH-CRSNs), this paper integrates active intelligent reflecting surface (IRS) to support both downlink EH and uplink data transmissions and formulates a constrained non-convex optimization problem that maximizes the net energy gain. To solve this problem, a joint passive beamforming and IRS deployment mechanism is proposed to determine the optimal deployment location of the active IRS and optimally configure the IRS reflection coefficient matrices. Specifically, the net energy gain maximization problem at a specified location is divided into two sub-problems according to its characteristics: maximizing the cumulative energy harvested by all CRSNs nodes in the downlink and minimizing the energy consumption of cluster heads in uplink data transmissions, with each sub-problem being solved independently. Furthermore, this paper explores how factors such as the number of active reflecting elements and the amplification power budget influence the optimal deployment location of the active IRS. The findings from this investigation can guide the design of active IRS-assisted EH-CRSNs under specified parameters. Simulation results confirm the aforementioned conclusions and highlight the benefits of the proposed joint mechanism in enhancing net energy gain over various benchmark mechanisms.Subject terms: Energy harvesting, Information technology

Introduction

Cognitive radio sensor networks (CRSNs) are an emerging network paradigm that combines cognitive radio (CR) technology with wireless sensor networks (WSNs)1. By incorporating CR technology, nodes can communicate by opportunistically accessing idle licensed spectrum bands without disrupting the communications of primary users, thus improving spectral efficiency and mitigating the spectrum shortage challenges in WSNs2,3. CRSNs nodes are powered by batteries that have limited capacity and are not easily replaceable, which constrains the network lifespan of CRSNs. Additionally, the execution of CR functions by CRSNs nodes exacerbates their energy consumption issues4. Energy harvesting (EH) is a viable solution to the limited energy issues in CRSNs nodes, enabling them to harvest energy from environmental natural sources (especially renewable energy sources such as solar and wind energy)5 or radio frequency (RF) sources. This energy is then converted into usable electrical power through energy conversion circuits6, decreasing dependence on conventional batteries and prolonging the network lifespan7, which contributes to the sustainable development of the environment8. CRSNs nodes generally utilize RF EH technology to recharge their batteries due to the stable and predictable energy supply from RF sources9.

Existing EH-CRSNs nodes collect energy and transmit data through direct links with the sink, but the substantial path loss significantly diminishes the energy collected by nodes located at greater distances from the sink and increases their energy expenditure for uplink data transmissions. This imbalance in EH and energy consumption adversely impacts the overall network lifetime. To address these challenges, scholars have introduced the innovative communication technology of intelligent reflecting surface (IRS), which smartly reshapes the wireless communication environment to boost the efficiency of both wireless energy transfer (WET) and wireless information transmission (WIT)10. Typically, IRS is classified into two types—passive IRS and active IRS. A passive IRS is a planar surface composed of numerous passive reflecting elements (REs)11, each capable of reflecting incident RF signals with a controllable phase shift. This setup allows the signals from the direct link and the IRS-enhanced reflection link to coherently combine at the receiver, thereby strengthening the received signal intensity12,13. In the past few years, the deployment of passive IRS in simultaneous wireless information and power transfer (SWIPT) systems and wireless powered communication networks (WPCNs) has been explored to enhance both EH and data transmission efficiencies. In IRS-assisted SWIPT systems14–17, an IRS is deployed to aid a multi-antenna base station (BS) in simultaneously serving multiple information decoding receivers while ensuring minimal reception power at multiple EH receivers. Specifically, reference14 explores the problem of maximizing the weighted sum of the received power at EH receivers, which facilitates the trade-off between rate and energy performance in SWIPT system. In reference15, the active and passive beamforming matrices at the BS and the IRS are jointly designed with the objective of minimizing the transmit power of the BS, which highlights the potential advantages of IRS in enhancing the energy efficiency and quality of service in wireless networks. Reference16 takes into account the discrete characteristics of IRS phase shifts and investigates the problem of maximizing the minimum signal to interference plus noise ratio (SINR) for all information decoding receivers. By jointly optimizing the active and passive beamforming at the BS and IRS, it effectively enhances the SINR performance of SWIPT systems. Reference17 explores the application of IRS-assisted multi-input multi-output CR systems in secure SWIPT scenarios. It effectively enhances the secrecy rate for secondary users while ensuring that the energy demands of energy receivers are met and there is no interference with primary users. Studies in references18–21 are centered on IRS-assisted WPCNs. Specifically, reference18 significantly boosts the utility of power stations and transmitters by jointly optimizing the IRS reflection phase shift matrices during both the WET and WIT phases. Reference19 examines the influence of IRS phase errors on the total transmission time in full-duplex WPCNs, confirming the effectiveness of IRS in minimizing the total completion time for downlink EH and uplink data transmissions for all wireless devices. In references20,21, non-linear EH models are applied to improve the performance of actual systems. Reference20 explores the problem of minimizing the transmission energy expenditure at hybrid wireless access point (AP) under 3 IRS beamforming strategies, achieving an effective trade-off between system performance, signaling overhead, and implementation complexity. Reference21 considers the actual limited self-interference at a hybrid AP and explores the design of joint passive beamforming and resource allocation in full-duplex WPCNs, successfully boosting the system’s overall weighted throughput. Nevertheless, these studies optimize the performance of IRS-assisted wireless communication systems assuming static IRS deployment locations. In practice, the deployment location of the IRS significantly affects system performance. Reference22 investigates achievable user rates in IRS-assisted single-input single-output (SISO) systems under varying IRS deployment locations. The conclusion was reached that deploying IRS close to users or APs can substantially improve data transmission performance. Reference23 explores the optimal deployment strategies for single and multiple IRSs in wireless relay systems. Simulation results indicate that deploying a single IRS close to the relay enhances system capacity, whereas deploying multiple IRSs can offer greater capacity expansion potential when a sufficient number of REs are used. Aimed at minimizing the total transmit power of the system, reference24 explores the problem of IRS beamforming and deployment. The findings indicate that a distributed IRS deployment strategy results in lower system power consumption compared to a centralized IRS deployment approach. Reference25 explores the optimization problem of IRS deployment location by considering various multiple access schemes with the objective of maximizing the weighted sum rate of all users. The findings reveal that the optimal deployment strategy for the IRS is highly dependent on the choice of multiple access schemes.

Although passive IRS can attain square-law array gains in terms of user signal to noise ratio (SNR)26, its effectiveness is limited by substantial path loss. Specifically, passive IRS only reflects signals, introducing controlled phase shifts without amplifying the signal. The path loss involved in the cascaded reflection link from the transmitter through a passive IRS to the receiver is calculated by multiplying the path losses of the two sub-links—transmitter to passive IRS and passive IRS to receiver. The multiplicative fading effect substantially limits its capability to improve system performance. To address the issue of multiplicative fading loss caused by passive IRS, the concept of active IRS has been introduced27. Active IRS is equipped with REs that have low-cost active loads, which enables the amplification of signals that have decayed during the initial hop back to normal levels. The signal reflected by the active IRS is influenced solely by the path loss of the sub-link from the IRS to the receiver28. Consequently, the active IRS can substantially boost the received signal power or attain comparable performance at reduced power levels, overcoming the multiplicative fading loss challenges encountered by passive IRS. Additionally, unlike traditional amplify-and-forward relays, active IRS does not require any RF chain29. Therefore, scholars have incorporated active IRS into wireless communication systems to aid data transmission and realize enhanced performance gains. Existing research on active IRS-assisted wireless communication systems30–33 has demonstrated that under the same total power budget, active IRS-assisted systems have a distinct advantage in enhancing performance compared to the baseline mechanisms of passive IRS-assisted wireless communication systems. The superiority of the active IRS is demonstrated at http://u.cxpic.cn/html/0710/.

Reference34 investigates the throughput maximization problem in active IRS-assisted energy-limited Internet of Things (IoT) systems under 2 different multiple access schemes. It confirms the practical advantages of active IRS over passive IRS in terms of extending coverage, supporting multiple energy-constrained devices, and reducing the number of required REs. Reference35 aims to minimize the transmit power of BS under constraints of active IRS amplification power budget and user quality of service requirements, effectively conserving power resources. Reference36 verifies that the introduction of active IRS can significantly improve the performance of millimeter-wave wireless communication systems by optimizing the data transmission rate of the network. References37,38 explore the deployment location problem of double active IRSs in assisting downlink communication from a multi-antenna BS to single-antenna users. Specifically, reference37 enhances the achievable rates for users by jointly optimizing the reflection coefficient matrices of the 2 active IRSs, distribution of REs, allocation of amplification power budget, deployment locations, and beamforming vector at the BS; reference38 maximizes the achievable rate of each user under a fixed amplification power budget for each active IRS using a time division multiple access scheme, which results in enhanced system capacity and rate performance. The aforementioned research primarily considers the data transmission phase of wireless communication systems assisted by active IRS, but does not address the issue of energy limitations in battery-powered devices, which restricts the system lifespan. To this end, references39–41 deploy active IRS in WPCNs and SWIPT systems to simultaneously assist both EH and data transmission. Specifically, reference39 studies the optimal deployment location of active IRS with the goal of maximizing achievable rates and validates the significant gains in rate and energy efficiency with active IRS. Reference40 maximizes the weighted sum of throughput for all devices under the constraint of active IRS amplification power budget, demonstrating that deploying active IRS in WPCNs achieves higher throughput with lower energy consumption. Reference41 investigates the maximization problem of both the weighted sum of received power and the weighted sum rate in active IRS-assisted SWIPT systems by considering 2 different design criteria. The study demonstrates that, compared to passive IRS, active IRS offers greater advantages in enhancing both the EH efficiency and data transmission rates of the system. The issues with existing research on IRS-assisted wireless communication systems lie in:

The research outcomes from studies on active IRS-assisted WPCNs and active IRS-assisted SWIPT systems are not applicable to active IRS-assisted EH-CRSNs. Specifically, for research on active IRS-assisted WPCNs, uplink data transmission is constrained by downlink EH, which implies an energy causality constraint. However, in active IRS-assisted EH-CRSNs, the energy collected in the downlink merely supplements the node’s battery energy without imposing causality constraints. For research on active IRS-assisted SWIPT systems, the BS transmits both information and energy via the downlink, with the key challenge being the trade-off between EH and information transmission. In active IRS-assisted EH-CRSNs, downlink EH is used to replenish the battery energy of nodes, and uplink data transmission is employed to send the collected data to the sink. This involves a coordinated optimization of both uplink and downlink to achieve the goal of prolonging the network lifetime.

System model and problem formulation

This paper focuses on the active IRS-assisted EH-CRSNs as depicted in Fig. 1. The system consists of a sink equipped with a single antenna, an active IRS containing M REs and N energy-constrained, single-antenna, homogeneous CRSNs nodes. These CRSNs nodes are randomly and uniformly distributed within a circular monitoring area of radius A, and they are partitioned into K clusters according to CogLEACH protocol42. In this paper, a three-dimensional Cartesian coordinate system is established with the sink as the origin. Accordingly, the position of the sink is represented as (0,0,0), while CRSNs node n (n∈{1,…,N}) is located at (xn,yn,0). The central coordinates of the active IRS are noted as (0,yIRS,dv), meaning that the active IRS is aligned parallel to the x-y plane and is at a vertical distance dv from this plane25. The IRS can move along the direction parallel to the y-axis. The system operation is divided into 2 phases: the downlink EH phase and the uplink data transmission phase. During the downlink EH phase, the sink radiates RF signal to CRSNs nodes at a constant transmit power PS. CRSNs nodes receive the RF signals from the sink through the combination of direct and cascaded reflection links. The received RF energy is converted into electrical energy through EH circuits with a conversion efficiency of η(η∈(0,1]), and it serves as a supplement to the node battery energy. In the uplink data transmission phase, member nodes within cluster k(k∈{1,…,K}) perceive the environment and send the collected data to CH k. CH k receives and aggregates data from all members and transmits the data towards the sink via both the direct link and the IRS-assisted cascaded reflection link with a transmit power pk. The active IRS has a total power budget designated as PIRS. For each RE, the power used by its switching and control circuits and the DC biasing power consumption are PC and PDC, respectively. Therefore, the maximum amplification power available from the active IRS is calculated as Pout=PIRS–M(PC+PDC). By judiciously configuring the diagonal reflection coefficient matrix Φ = diag(ϕ1,…,ϕm,…,ϕM)∈ℂM×M, the coherent superposition of reflected and direct signals at the receiver can enhance the strength of the received signal. Here, represents the reflection coefficient of the mth (m∈{1,…,M}) RE of the active IRS, am and θm represent the reflection amplitude and phase shift introduced by the mth RE, respectively, and am≥1. This paper assumes that θm can continuously vary within the interval [0,2π). Specifically, Φ0 = diag(ϕ0,1,…,ϕ0,m,…,ϕ0,M)∈ℂM×M represents the reflection coefficient matrix of the active IRS during the downlink EH phase, while Φk = diag(ϕk,1,…,ϕk,m,…,ϕk,M)∈ℂM×M represents the reflection coefficient matrix of the active IRS when it serves CH k during the uplink data transmission phase. The equivalent baseband channels from the active IRS to the sink, from CRSNs node n to the active IRS and from CRSNs node n to the sink are denoted by g∈ℂ1×M, hr,n∈ℂM×1 and hd,n∈ℂ1 × 1, ∀n∈{1,…,N}, respectively. The assumption is made that the instantaneous channel state information (CSI) of each channel involved is entirely known, and the CSI estimates can be acquired using compressed sensing techniques that exploit the sparce structure of the IRS cascaded channel or by deploying active sensors at the IRS43. Investigation of specific channel estimation methods is beyond the scope of this paper. In this paper, the primary goal of the joint mechanism is to maximize the net energy gain Ebenefit of active IRS-assisted EH-CRSNs by adjusting the deployment location of the active IRS and configuring its reflection coefficient matrices, adhering to the active IRS amplification power budgets for uplink and downlink. The optimal location for deploying the active IRS is identified as the coordinates where Ebenefit reaches its peak. As demonstrated by Eqs. (6a), (6b), (6c), the amplification power budget Pout of the active IRS, the RF signal power PS emitted by the sink, the number of active REs M and the number of clusters K formed in CRSNs are crucial in determining the configuration of the reflection coefficient matrices of the active IRS, which ultimately affects the system’s net energy gain and the optimal deployment location of the active IRS. Therefore, it is essential to study the effects of these factors on the optimal deployment location of the active IRS to provide guidance for the design of active IRS-assisted EH-CRSNs under given system parameters.

Verification of the effectiveness of the proposed mechanism

To substantiate the effectiveness of the proposed joint passive beamforming and IRS deployment mechanism for active IRS-assisted EH-CRSNs, we have conducted comparisons with the following baseline mechanisms: (1) Joint passive beamforming and IRS deployment mechanism for passive IRS-assisted EH-CRSNs (abbreviated as PIRS): Uplink data transmission and downlink EH utilize optimally configured reflection phase shifts, with maximum reflection amplitudes of 1. (2) Uplink passive beamforming and IRS deployment mechanism for active IRS-assisted EH-CRSNs (abbreviated as AUplink): This uses the optimal reflection coefficient matrices for uplink data transmission and a constraint-satisfying random reflection coefficient matrix for downlink EH. (3) Downlink passive beamforming and IRS deployment mechanism for active IRS-assisted EH-CRSNs (abbreviated as ADownlink): The downlink EH uses the optimal reflection coefficient matrix, while the uplink data transmissions use random reflection coefficient matrices that meet constraints. (4) Random reflection matrix configuration mechanism for active IRS-assisted EH-CRSNs (abbreviated as ARandom): Both uplink data transmissions and downlink EH utilize random reflection coefficient matrix configurations that satisfy constraints. (5) Unified passive beamforming and IRS deployment mechanism for uplink and downlink in active IRS-assisted EH-CRSNs (abbreviated as AUnified): Both the uplink data transmissions and downlink EH use the optimal reflection coefficient matrix configured for downlink EH. For fairness, the passive IRS is configured with a total power budget equivalent to PIRS= 9.4dBm for 12 active REs, resulting in the passive IRS having 87 REs. Under these conditions, the optimal deployment location for the passive IRS is at yIRS= 0. The comparative results of their net energy gains are shown in Fig. 2 below.

Comparison results of net energy gains against baseline mechanisms: dv=5 m, M = 12, K = 20, PS=40dBm, Pout=8dBm.

As illustrated in Fig. 2, the joint passive beamforming and IRS deployment mechanism proposed for active IRS-assisted EH-CRSNs in this paper outperforms various baseline mechanisms by optimally configuring the reflection coefficient matrices of the active IRS for both uplink and downlink. This mechanism not only enhances EH effectiveness across all CRSNs nodes but also lowers the energy requirements of CHs for uplink data transmissions, which significantly boosts the system’s net energy gains. Additionally, the optimal IRS deployment locations for the proposed joint mechanism, AUplink and PIRS are all near the sink, whereas for other mechanisms, the optimal IRS deployment locations are closer to CRSNs nodes. An analysis of the underlying reasons is provided below: (1) The AUplink mechanism focuses on optimizing the active IRS reflection coefficient matrix for each time slot specifically to reduce the energy used by CHs in uplink data transmissions, but it does not account for the joint optimization of the downlink EH performance of CRSNs nodes. As a result, the net energy gain of the system is inferior to that of the joint mechanism introduced in this paper. The ADownlink mechanism optimizes the active IRS reflection coefficient matrix to maximize the total energy harvested by CRSNs nodes in the downlink, without accounting for the joint optimization of energy expenditure in uplink data transmissions. Given that the energy used for uplink data transmission is a dominant factor in the net energy gain of EH-CRSNs, the net energy gain under the ADownlink mechanism is lower than that achieved by the AUplink mechanism. In the ARandom mechanism, both uplink and downlink utilize randomly configured reflection coefficient matrices, resulting in a net energy gain that is inferior to the aforementioned mechanisms. In the AUnified mechanism, the sink emits RF signals at a higher power during the downlink EH phase, which leads to minimal increases in the amplitude amplification for downlink. When this limited amplification is used for the uplink data transmissions of CHs at lower power levels, it does not effectively reduce the energy used for data transmissions, leading to the lowest net energy gain. The simulation results demonstrate that under the same total power budget, the performance gains from an active IRS are not guaranteed but require a combination of passive beamforming and IRS deployment optimization to achieve a higher net energy gain compared to the baseline passive IRS mechanism. Additionally, under the same total power budget, the net energy gain of the proposed joint mechanism is significantly higher than that of the PIRS. The main reason for this is that the passive IRS only passively reflects signals and cannot amplify incident signals, with each passive RE always introducing a reflection amplitude of 1 for the incident signal. Consequently, the passive IRS is prone to “multiplicative fading” effect, where the path loss of the CRSNs node-passive IRS-sink link (during the uplink data transmission phase) is the product of the path losses of the CRSNs node-passive IRS and passive IRS-sink sub-links, leading to longer transmission time and higher energy consumption for CRSNs nodes to complete uplink data transmission at the same transmit power. Conversely, the active IRS can amplify incident signals, boosting the signal amplitude to normal levels after attenuation in the CRSNs node-active IRS sub-link, thus effectively mitigating the “multiplicative fading” effect and reducing the energy consumption of CRSNs nodes. To validate the above analysis, we record the reflection amplitudes introduced to the incident signal by each active and passive RE during the uplink data transmission and downlink EH phases when the IRS is positioned directly above the sink. Given that CHs conduct uplink data transmission in a time-division manner and their locations vary, the reflection amplitudes of the active REs may differ when serving different CHs. We randomly select 3 CHs out of 20, with locations at CH1(‒7.3,‒8.9,0), CH2(‒33.2,‒35.1,0), and CH3(20.8,‒14.2,0). The detailed simulation results are illustrated in Figs. 3, 4.

When identifying the optimal deployment location for the active IRS, apart from its movement parallel to the y-axis, how variations in the Euclidean distance dv between the active IRS center and the sink affect the net energy gain of active IRS-assisted EH-CRSNs is explored. The specific results are shown in Fig. 5.

Net energy gain of active IRS-assisted EH-CRSNs versus dv.

As shown in Fig. 5, the optimal deployment location for the active IRS does not depend on dv and is always positioned near the sink. With the active IRS’s vertical coordinate yIRS fixed, the system’s net energy gain Ebenefit decreases as dv increases. Furthermore, the Ebenefit-yIRS curves exhibit approximate symmetry around the center of the network. The underlying causes are explained as follows: (1) Given the active IRS’s capability to amplify reflected signals, it can restore significantly attenuated signals over the link between the transmitter and IRS to normal levels. When the active IRS is placed closer to the receiver, the propagation loss of the amplified reflected signal is reduced. Therefore, deploying the active IRS near the receiver enhances overall system performance. Specifically, the active IRS should be nearer to CRSNs nodes in the downlink EH phase and closer to the sink in the uplink data transmission phase. Therefore, when jointly optimizing for both uplink and downlink, the IRS deployment is predominantly oriented towards the uplink, with the ideal positioning always directly above the sink. (2) When yIRS remains constant, the large-scale fading in the cascaded reflection channel worsens as dv increases, leading to greater attenuation of the signals reflected by the active IRS. This will result in reduced energy harvested by CRSNs nodes in the downlink and increased energy expenditure for CHs during uplink data transmissions. As a result, the overall net energy gain of the system decreases. (3) The impact of the IRS deployment location on Ebenefit is related to the spatial distribution of CRSNs nodes. All CRSNs nodes are randomly and uniformly distributed in a circular monitoring area centered at the sink, with their Euclidean distances to the IRS being nearly symmetric around the network center. Therefore, as the IRS moves from the extreme left to the extreme right of the network, the Ebenefit curve exhibits a fundamentally symmetrical trend with respect to the network center.

The impact of key factors on the optimal IRS deployment location

Figure 6 illustrates how the optimal deployment location of the IRS varies with the quantity of active REs M. As depicted, the optimal deployment location of the active IRS tends to approach closer to CRSNs nodes with an increasing number of active REs M; With a constant IRS amplification power budget and positioning, the net energy gain Ebenefit steadily rises as M becomes larger. An analysis of the underlying reasons is presented below: (1) The number of REs M in the active IRS simultaneously influences the system’s downlink EH and uplink data transmissions. Under a fixed active IRS amplification power budget, an increase in M amplifies the influence on the optimal IRS deployment for both EH and data transmission phases. This leads to a gradual reduction in the energy consumption of CHs’ uplink data transmissions, while the energy collected by all CRSNs nodes through downlink gradually increases. However, the amplification effect of the active IRS is more evident for signals with lower transmit power, causing a greater impact of M on the energy consumption of uplink data transmissions than on downlink EH. Consequently, the IRS deployment location gradually shifts to favor the downlink, i.e., the optimal location of the active IRS progressively approaches the CRSNs nodes. (2) With the deployment location and amplification power budget of the active IRS remaining constant, the increased number of REs M strengthens the capability of the active IRS to amplify incident signals. This amplification improves the EH and data transmission efficiency of all CRSNs nodes, leading to a gradual increase in Ebenefit for active IRS-assisted EH-CRSNs.

The simulation results under LoS channel conditions indicate the maximum achievable Ebenefit for active IRS-assisted EH-CRSNs. From the figure, it is evident that with an increase in Pout for the active IRS, its optimal deployment location shifts nearer to the CRSNs nodes under the same channel conditions, resulting in a higher Ebenefit for EH-CRSNs at the same IRS location. The optimal deployment location of the active IRS is even closer to the CRSNs nodes under LoS channels than under Rician channels. The underlying reasons are detailed as follows: (1) The amplification power budget Pout of the active IRS affects both the downlink EH and uplink data transmissions in CRSNs. According to reference27, the active IRS is less impacted by the amplification power budget constraint when the transmit signal power is low. As the transmit power increases, this constraint starts to limit the amplification gain of the active IRS. Under identical channel conditions, the constraint in Eq. (7b) has a limited impact on the active IRS during the uplink data transmission phase due to the lower transmit power of CHs. However, during the downlink EH phase, the higher transmit power of the sink makes the amplification power budget constraint in Eq. (8b) more prominent, resulting in a reduced amplification gain by the active IRS. As Pout decreases, the signal amplification by the active IRS is diminished in both the uplink and downlink phases, with the performance enhancement in the downlink EH phase becoming less noticeable. Consequently, the energy consumption of CHs in uplink data transmissions increases, while the energy collected by all nodes in the downlink decreases, and the optimal deployment location of the active IRS moves closer to the sink. Additionally, under the same transmit power, a higher Pout for the active IRS provides more amplification gain, leading to a higher Ebenefit for EH-CRSNs at a given deployment location of the active IRS. (2) During the downlink EH phase, the direct link plays a dominant role, with nodes collecting more energy through the direct link with the sink than through the cascaded reflection link. Due to the lower path loss exponent in LoS channels compared to Rician channels, signal transmission is more effective in LoS channels. Compared to Rician channels, the impact of the active IRS deployment location is more pronounced during the downlink EH phase in LoS channels. Consequently, with an increase in Pout, the optimal deployment of the active IRS tends to be nearer to CRSNs nodes.

This paper also explores how the optimal deployment location of the active IRS varies with the number of clusters K formed in CRSNs, as depicted in Fig. 8. As illustrated, with the increase in K, the optimal deployment location of the active IRS progressively moves closer to directly above the sink. At the same IRS location, a higher number of CHs leads to a lower Ebenefit for the EH-CRSNs. The specific reasons are as follows: (1) The number of clusters K only influences the energy consumed during the uplink data transmissions by CHs and does not affect the downlink EH of CRSNs nodes. As K rises, the energy needed for uplink data transmissions increases. Thus, the IRS deployment should increasingly focus on the uplink, leading to an optimal location closer to above the sink. (2) With a fixed location of the active IRS, a higher K leads to increased energy usage for uplink data transmissions from CHs to the sink, while the cumulative energy collected in the downlink by all CRSNs nodes remains constant. As a result, the Ebenefit for the EH-CRSNs diminishes.

Figure 9 shows the trend of the optimal deployment location of the active IRS in relation to changes in the transmit power PS of the sink. As can be observed from Fig. 9, the variation in PS has a negligible impact on the optimal deployment location of the IRS, which remains consistently near the sink. When the active IRS is stationed at the same location, a higher PS results in an increased Ebenefit for the EH-CRSNs. The detailed reasons are as follows: (1) The sink’s transmit power PS primarily influences the energy collected by CRSNs nodes in the downlink, without affecting the energy consumption of CHs in uplink data transmissions. As PS increases, the energy harvested in the downlink by all CRSNs nodes also increases, implying that the active IRS should gradually be placed closer to CRSNs nodes. Nonetheless, due to the substantial energy consumption in uplink data transmissions by CHs, the energy collected by all CRSNs nodes cannot compensate for the energy spent in data transmissions, leading to a limited impact of PS on the IRS’s optimal deployment location. Therefore, the active IRS tends to be deployed near the sink when considering both uplink and downlink phases. (2) With the active IRS at a constant location, an increase in PS results in greater total downlink energy harvested by all CRSNs nodes, while the uplink data transmission energy consumption by CHs remains the same, thereby enhancing the Ebenefit for EH-CRSNs.

Conclusions

This paper incorporates active IRS into EH-CRSNs to facilitate downlink EH and uplink data transmission. The research focuses on determining the joint passive beamforming and optimal deployment location for active IRS by simultaneously considering uplink and downlink, and by formulating and solving a constrained non-convex optimization problem to maximize the net energy gain of the system. The optimization problem constructed indicates that the optimal deployment location of the active IRS, when jointly considering uplink and downlink, depends on various factors such as the number of active REs, the amplification power budget of the active IRS, the number of clusters formed in CRSNs, and the transmit power of the sink. Simulation results indicate that the transmit power of the sink has a negligible influence on the optimal deployment location of the active IRS, and deploying the active IRS directly above the sink yields the highest net energy gain for the EH-CRSNs, relative to other possible locations. The growing number of clusters increases the energy used in data transmission, which in turn prompts the IRS’s optimal deployment location to progressively move towards the sink. Increases in the quantity of active REs and the amplification power budget concurrently amplify EH and diminish energy consumption in data transmission, leading to a shift in the IRS’s optimal deployment towards CRSNs nodes. Additionally, when the total power budget remains constant, the joint passive beamforming and IRS deployment mechanism proposed for active IRS-assisted EH-CRSNs exhibits at most a 29.33% enhancement in net energy gain compared to various baseline mechanisms. Our research focuses on maximizing the system’s net energy gain without considering the equilibrium of residual energy among CRSNs nodes, which could constrain the potential to extend the network lifespan. In future work, we aim to develop an optimization problem focused on maximizing the balance of residual energy among EH-CRSNs nodes facilitated by active IRS and address this problem by designing an improved joint passive beamforming and IRS deployment mechanism. We plan to further establish a hardware platform to validate the superiority of the proposed joint mechanism over PIRS through experimental measurements. Additionally, we aim to develop a dedicated clustering protocol for active IRS-assisted EH-CRSNs to further reduce the energy consumption of uplink data transmission and effectively extend the network lifespan.
