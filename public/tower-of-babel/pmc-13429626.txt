Engineering a visible-light-driven heterostructure: synergistic catalysis of bimetallic MIL-88B(Fe/Co)@TiO2 for efficient BPA mineralization
Authors: Zinat Gordi, Hamid Rashidiyanfar, Leila Hokmabady
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe pervasive environmental presence of endocrine-disrupting bisphenol A (BPA) necessitates advanced remediation technologies. This study presents the rational design and synthesis of a novel bimetallic MIL-88B(Fe/Co)@TiO2 heterostructure as a highly efficient visible-light-driven photocatalyst for BPA degradation. Comprehensive characterization via XRD, SEM-EDX, FTIR, and BET confirmed the successful integration of TiO2 nanoparticles with the bimetallic MOF, forming a composite with a narrowed bandgap (2.13 eV) and enhanced visible-light absorption. Response Surface Methodology (RSM) based on a Central Composite Design (CCD) optimized key operational parameters, predicting and achieving a remarkable 95.3% degradation under optimal conditions (pH 4.4, catalyst dose 43 mg, [BPA] 78 mg L− 1, [H2O2] 3.20%). Kinetic studies revealed rapid degradation following a pseudo-first-order model with a rate constant of 0.050 min− 1 and a half-life of 13.86 min. Scavenger experiments elucidated a synergistic mechanism where hydroxyl radicals (·OH), generated via a combined photocatalytic and Fenton-like pathway, played the dominant role. The catalyst demonstrated satisfactory stability over four consecutive cycles. Introduction

Bisphenols, a class of synthetic organic compounds characterized by a fundamental structure of two hydroxyphenyl groups, have emerged as ubiquitous environmental contaminants1. Bisphenol A (BPA), the most prominent member of this family, serves as a primary monomer in the production of polycarbonate plastics and epoxy resins, found in a vast array of products ranging from food containers and dental sealants to thermal papers2. The extensive utilization of bisphenols has resulted in their inevitable leaching into aquatic and terrestrial ecosystems, where they are now detected in water, soil, and even atmospheric samples3,4. These compounds are recognized as endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs), capable of interfering with hormonal systems in both humans and wildlife, leading to potential adverse effects including reproductive disorders, developmental abnormalities, metabolic diseases, and an increased risk of certain cancers4,5. Consequently, the efficient and complete removal of bisphenols from the environment represents a critical and pressing public health and ecological priority.

Traditional water treatment methods, including adsorption, coagulation, and biological degradation, often prove insufficient for achieving complete mineralization of persistent organic pollutants like bisphenols6,7. These approaches may merely transfer contaminants from one phase to another or generate secondary toxic byproducts8. In this context, advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) have emerged as a powerful alternative. Among these, photocatalytic degradation stands out as a highly promising technology9. This method employs a semiconductor photocatalyst, which, upon irradiation with light of sufficient energy, generates electron-hole pairs10. These charge carriers subsequently drive redox reactions that produce highly reactive oxygen species (ROS), including hydroxyl radicals (·OH) and superoxide anions (·O2–), which can non-selectively oxidize and mineralize complex organic pollutants into harmless end products like CO2 and H2O11,12. Beyond its strong oxidative power, photocatalysis offers distinct practical benefits: operation under ambient conditions, direct utilization of solar energy, complete mineralization of persistent pollutants, and the complete absence of secondary waste streams13.

Titanium dioxide (TiO2) nanoparticles have been extensively investigated as the benchmark photocatalyst due to their strong oxidative power, high photochemical stability, non-toxicity, and cost-effectiveness14,15. However, the practical application of pure TiO2 is constrained by two significant limitations: its wide bandgap, which restricts light absorption primarily to the ultraviolet (UV) region (accounting for only ~ 4% of solar energy), and the rapid recombination of photogenerated electron-hole pairs, which drastically reduces its quantum efficiency16,17.

To overcome these challenges, metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) have recently attracted immense attention as a promising class of photocatalytic materials18,19. MOFs are crystalline porous materials composed of metal ions or clusters coordinated with organic linkers20–27. Their extraordinary properties, such as an exceptionally high surface area, tunable porosity, and structurally designable designable, make them highly attractive for photocatalysis24,26,28–31. The organic linkers can act as antennas for visible-light absorption, while the metal clusters can serve as catalytic sites32,33. Furthermore, their modular nature allows for precise pre- and post-synthetic modifications to tailor their electronic properties and bandgap for specific photocatalytic reactions34,35.

The integration of TiO2 nanoparticles with MOFs presents a compelling strategy to create synergistic heterostructures that combine the strengths of both materials36,37. In such composites, the MOF matrix can enhance the adsorption capacity for target pollutants, concentrating them near the active sites of TiO238. More importantly, a synergistic electronic interaction can be established at the TiO2/MOF interface. A properly aligned MOF can act as a sensitizer, extending the light absorption of the composite into the visible region39. Simultaneously, the MOF can serve as an efficient electron acceptor or donor, facilitating the spatial separation of photogenerated charge carriers from TiO2, thereby significantly suppressing their recombination and enhancing overall photocatalytic efficiency40,41.

While TiO2-based photocatalysts and MOF@TiO2 heterojunctions have been widely reported, including NH2-MIL-88B(Fe)/TiO2 electrospun membranes for tetracycline degradation and oil-water separation30, defective TiO2/MIL-88B(Fe) composites prepared by electron-beam irradiation31, monolithic MOF/TiO2 crystal glass composites for micropollutant removal42, and Ti-based MOF/TiO2 systems for dye degradation43, the specific combination of bimetallic MIL-88B(Fe/Co) with anatase TiO2 remains unexplored. The MIL-88B family—with its distinctive flexible framework, breathing effect, and tunable bimetallic nodes—has not yet been integrated with TiO2 for visible-light photocatalysis. Furthermore, the progressive band gap engineering achieved by sequential cobalt doping and TiO2 coupling in a MIL-88B(Fe/Co)@TiO2 system has never been demonstrated. Therefore, this study aims to bridge these gaps by strategically developing a bimetallic MOF-TiO2 heterostructure. We hypothesize that coupling TiO2 with a bimetallic MIL-88B(Fe/Co) framework will yield a synergistic composite that combines the stability of TiO2 with the superior visible-light absorption and Fenton-like activity of the bimetallic MOF, thereby achieving highly efficient photocatalytic degradation of BPA. To test this hypothesis, we systematically (i) synthesized and characterized the bimetallic MIL-88B(Fe/Co)@TiO2 composite; (ii) employed a statistically rigorous response surface methodology based on central composite design (RSM-CCD) to optimize the degradation process; (iii) investigated the degradation kinetics and reusability of the optimal catalyst; and (iv) conducted a mechanistic study to identify the primary reactive oxygen species and elucidate the synergistic degradation pathway. This work thus presents the first example of a MIL-88B(Fe/Co)@TiO2 heterojunction and provides a comprehensive framework for designing advanced heterostructured photocatalysts for effective environmental remediation.

Experimental

Chemicals and instruments

All chemical reagents, including titanium tetra-isopropoxide, isopropanol, diethylenetriamine, terephthalic acid, iron(III) chloride hexahydrate (FeCl3·6H2O), cobalt(III) nitrate hexahydrate (Co(NO3)3·6H2O), and N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF), were of analytical grade and procured from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). The morphological, structural, and textural properties of the synthesized composites were characterized using field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM; Mira 3 Tescan, Czech Republic), X-ray diffraction (XRD; Bruker D8 ADVANCE, Germany), Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR; Bruker, Germany), and N2 physisorption analysis (BET; BELSORP Mini II, Japan). The concentration of BPA in aqueous solutions was quantified by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC; Knauer, Germany) equipped with a K-2600 UV detector set at 215 nm and a Eurospher 100-5 C18 column (4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm). The mobile phase consisted of acetonitrile and deionized water (60:40, v/v) with a flow rate of 1.0 mL min− 1. Under these conditions, the retention time for BPA was 4.74 minutes44. The pH of all solutions was measured and adjusted using a Metrohm 780 pH meter (Switzerland).

Photocatalyst preparation

TiO2 NP preparation

TiO2 microspheres were prepared via a solvothermal procedure by first preparing a homogeneous mixture of 0.03 mL of diethylenetriamine and 42.0 mL of isopropanol under constant stirring at 400 rpm for 20 min. To this mixture, titanium tetra-isopropoxide (1.5 mL) was introduced dropwise under continuous stirring. The mixture was transferred into a Teflon-lined autoclave and subjected to a solvothermal treatment at 200 °C for 24 h. The resulting solid product was collected, washed sequentially with distilled water and absolute ethanol to remove impurities, followed by drying at 80 °C for 8 h. Finally, the material was calcined at 400 °C for 2 h in air to crystallize the TiO2 microspheres and remove any residual organic compounds24.

MIL-88B (Fe)@TiO2 composite

The MIL-88B(Fe)@TiO2 composite was synthesized via a modified solvothermal method based on previously reported procedures. In a typical synthesis, 2 mmol (0.362 g) of terephthalic acid was dissolved in 7.5 mL of DMF. Concurrently, a separate suspension was prepared by dissolving 0.359 g of iron(III) chloride hexahydrate (FeCl3·6H2O) and dispersing 0.50 g of TiO2 mesoporous in 7.5 mL of DMF under ultrasonication. The two solutions were then combined, and the resultant mixture was stirred vigorously for 60 min at 25 °C to ensure homogeneity. The final reaction mixture was transferred into a Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave and subjected to a solvothermal reaction at 120 °C for 24 h. The resulting solid product was collected, washed successively with DMF and ethanol to remove unreacted precursors and solvent residues, and subsequently dried at 100 °C for 12 h to yield the final MIL-88B(Fe)@TiO2 composite material.

Bimetallic Fe/Co MIL-88B@ TiO2 composite

The bimetallic MIL-88B(Fe/Co)@TiO2 composite was synthesized following an analogous solvothermal procedure to that of MIL-88B(Fe)@TiO2, with a modification in the metal precursor composition. Specifically, a mixture of FeCl3·6H2O (0.359 g) and Co(NO3)3·6H2O (0.192 g) was used together with the 0.5 g of TiO2 during the preparation of the initial metal precursor suspension. All subsequent steps, including dissolution, mixing, solvothermal treatment, washing, and drying, were performed identically to yield the final bimetallic composite45.

BPA degradation procedure

The photodegradation of BPA was evaluated using an initial contaminant concentration of 78 mg L–1 in a 100 mL aqueous solution, the pH of which was pre-adjusted to 4.4 using 0.1 mol L⁻¹ hydrochloric acid. A mass of 43 mg of the bimetallic MIL-53(Fe/Co)@TiO2 photocatalyst was dispersed into the solution, and the mixture was stirred at 400 rpm for 100 min in the dark to establish adsorption-desorption equilibrium. This duration was confirmed by preliminary adsorption tests to be sufficient for reaching equilibrium, in agreement with previous reports on MIL-88B(Fe)-based photocatalysts. Subsequently, hydrogen peroxide was introduced into the system at a concentration of 3.2% (v/v) to initiate a photo-Fenton-like reaction. The suspension was then irradiated with a commercial 30 W visible LED lamp (Generic, cool white, 6000 K, 3000 lm, E27 base) emitting in the 400–700 nm range with no significant UV output, as confirmed by the manufacturer’s specifications. The lamp was positioned at a fixed distance of 10 cm above the reactor, providing an illumination intensity of approximately 85 mW/cm² as measured by a digital lux meter. Stirring was maintained at 400 rpm for a further 90 min. Upon completion of the reaction, the photocatalyst was separated from the aqueous phase by centrifugation at 7000 rpm for 12 min, and the residual BPA concentration in the supernatant was quantified via high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) to determine the photocatalytic removal efficiency. Results and discussion

Catalyst characterization

The morphology and elemental composition of the synthesized materials were investigated using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX). Figure 1a displays the characteristic morphology of the pure bimetallic MIL-88B(Fe/Co), revealing well-defined, rod-like crystals with smooth surfaces and a relatively uniform size distribution, consistent with the typical hexagonal structure of the MIL-88B family. Figure 1b presents the SEM image of the bimetallic MIL-88B(Fe/Co)@TiO2 composite, showcasing a pronouced morphological transformation. The once-smooth surface of the MOF rods is now densely decorated with a layer of granular TiO2 nanoparticles. This intimate association, where the TiO2 particles are firmly anchored onto and around the MOF micro-rods, confirms the successful formation of a heterostructured composite and is crucial for facilitating synergistic charge transfer between the two components. The EDX spectrum in Fig. 1c, acquired from the composite material, provides conclusive qualitative and quantitative evidence of its chemical composition. The spectrum confirms the presence of all expected elements, with quantitative weight% analysis revealing 28.23% Iron (Fe), 25.19% Titanium (Ti), 14.46% Carbon (C), and 32.12% Oxygen (O). The substantial and comparable proportions of Fe and Ti quantitatively verify the successful integration of the TiO2 nanoparticles with the bimetallic MOF framework, while the presence of Carbon confirms the integrity of the organic linker within the composite structure. This elemental profile conclusively demonstrates the successful synthesis of the bimetallic MIL-88B(Fe/Co)@TiO2 composite.

Fourier-Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was employed to confirm the chemical structure of the synthesized materials and the successful formation of the bimetallic MIL-88B(Fe/Co)@TiO2 composite (Fig. 2a and b). The spectrum of the pristine bimetallic MIL-88B(Fe/Co) exhibits the characteristic vibrations of the framework (Fig. 2a). The broad band centered at 3425 cm–1 is attributed to the O-H stretching vibration from water molecules adsorbed within the porous structure. The sharp, distinct peaks at 1581 cm–1 and 1419 cm–1 are assigned to the asymmetric and symmetric stretching vibrations of the carboxylate (-COO–) groups from the organic linker, respectively, confirming the coordination between the linker and the metal clusters. The peak at 1072 cm–1 is associated with C-H stretching, while the low-frequency bands at 632 cm–1 and 594 cm–1 correspond to the metal-oxygen (Fe/Co-O) stretching vibrations, providing direct evidence of the MOF’s inorganic building blocks. In the spectrum of the bimetallic MIL-88B(Fe/Co)@TiO2 composite (Fig. 2b), the characteristic peaks of the MOF are preserved but exhibit noticeable shifts (e.g., the carboxylate stretches shift to 1589 cm–1 and 1388 cm–1) and a changes in relative intensity, indicating a perturbation of the MOF’s electronic environment resulting from interaction with TiO2. Notably, the composite spectrum reveals a new, prominent absorption band at 532 cm–1, which falls within the fingerprint region characteristic of Ti-O-Ti stretching vibration of the anatase phase of TiO2. The simultaneous presence of the shifted MOF signatures and the distinct TiO2 band provides compelling evidence for the successful integration of TiO2 nanoparticles with the bimetallic MIL-88B(Fe/Co) framework, confirming the formation of an integrated composite material rather than a simple physical mixture.

The X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern of the synthesized bimetallic MIL-88B(Fe/Co)@TiO2 composite is presented in Fig. 2c, confirming the successful coexistence of both crystalline phases. The pattern reveals the characteristic diffractions of the bimetallic MIL-88B(Fe/Co) structure, with prominent peaks observed at 2θ values of 9.28°, 11.08°, 12.05°, 19.63°, and 22.25°, which can be indexed to the (100), (101), (102), (202), and (211) crystallographic planes, respectively46. This confirms the preservation of the highly crystalline MOF framework after the incorporation of cobalt and integration with TiO2. Simultaneously, the pattern distinctly shows the fingerprint of the anatase phase of TiO2, evidenced by the principal diffraction peaks at 25.49°, 37.73°, and 48.09°, corresponding to the (101), (004), and (200) planes (JCPDS card no. 21-1272)37. The presence of these sharp, well-defined peaks from both components without the appearance of additional or amorphous phases indicates the high crystallinity of the individual materials and the successful formation of a composite where both the MIL-88B(Fe/Co) and anatase TiO2 structures remain intact.

The textural properties of the synthesized materials were characterized using N2 physisorption analysis. All samples exhibit Type IV isotherms with distinct H3-type hysteresis loops according to IUPAC classification, confirming the presence of a well-developed mesoporous structure. The pristine bimetallic MIL-88B(Fe/Co) exhibited a BET surface area of 25.871 m²g⁻¹, which is consistent with the typical porous structure of MIL-88B-type materials. The material demonstrated a total pore volume of 0.1894 cm³g⁻¹ and a mean pore diameter of 21.322 nm, indicating a predominantly mesoporous structure. The high C value of 46.286 suggests strong interactions between the MOF framework and nitrogen molecules, reflecting a highly polar adsorbent surface. For the bimetallic MIL-88B(Fe/Co)@TiO2 composite, significant changes in textural parameters were observed due to the successful incorporation of TiO2 nanoparticles. The BET surface area decreased to 21.391 m2g–1, while the total pore volume was reduced to 0.1452 cm3g–1, confirming that TiO2 nanoparticles effectively infiltrated and partially occupied the porous network of the MOF (Fig. S1a). Concurrently, the mean pore diameter increased to 25.692 nm, indicating that the smaller micropores were preferentially blocked, leaving larger mesopores intact. This clear evolution in textural properties, characterized by reduced surface area and altered porosity, provides compelling evidence for the successful formation of an integrated composite structure, further corroborating the findings from XRD, SEM, and FTIR analyses.

The optical properties and electronic band structures of the synthesized photocatalysts were investigated using Diffuse Reflectance Spectroscopy (DRS), and the corresponding band gap energies were determined from the Tauc plots. The obtained results form a highly consistent and logical dataset that strongly supports the intended design of the composite materials. The measured band gap for the pristine TiO2 is 3.18 eV, a characteristic value for the anatase phase, confirming that its photoactivity is primarily confined to the ultraviolet region. In contrast, the MIL-88B(Fe) exhibits a substantially narrower band gap of 2.49 eV, which arises from metal-to-ligand charge transfer within the framework, enabling visible-light absorption. A crucial finding is the further reduction of the band gap to 2.27 eV for the bimetallic MIL-88B(Fe/Co). This decrease is rationally attributed to the synergistic electronic interaction between iron and cobalt cations, where the introduction of cobalt creates new mid-gap energy levels, effectively enhancing the material’s visible-light harvesting capability. The most compelling evidence for successful composite integration comes from the heterojunctions with TiO2. For the MIL-88B(Fe)@TiO2 composite, the band gap is measured at 2.32 eV. This value does not represent a simple average but indicates a complex electronic interaction at the interface. Significantly, the band gap of the bimetallic MIL-88B(Fe/Co)@TiO2 composite is the narrowest of the series at 2.13 eV (Fig. S1b). This confirms that the beneficial electronic effects of the Fe/Co bimetallic system are preserved and enhanced upon coupling with TiO2. Therefore, the progressive narrowing of the band gap from TiO2 (3.18 eV) to the bimetallic MIL-88B(Fe/Co)@TiO2 composite (2.13 eV), as definitively measured by DRS, provides clear evidence of successful band gap engineering. This trend demonstrates that the composite strategy successfully merges the narrow band gap of the bimetallic MOF with TiO2, resulting in a hybrid material with superior visible-light absorption, which directly correlates with the enhanced photocatalytic performance observed.

Evaluation of photocatalyst performance and selection of the optimal composite

The selection of an appropriate photocatalyst is paramount, as its electronic structure, particularly its band gap energy (Eg), dictates fundamental processes such as light absorption range, the efficiency of charge carrier generation, and the resulting redox potential for reactive species generation. To this end, the photocatalytic degradation of BPA was evaluated under visible light irradiation using a series of catalysts, with the process carried out under consistent conditions: an initial BPA concentration of 80 mg L–1, a hydrogen peroxide concentration of 2% (v/v), a catalyst loading of 40 mg, and a stirring speed of 400 rpm for a reaction time of 80 min.

The degradation percentages (R%), presented in Table 1, reveal a clear performance trend that is rationalized by the band structures of the materials. The negligible activity without a catalyst (8.3 ± 2.9%) and the low efficiency of pure TiO2 (14.6 ± 3.2%) are direct consequences of their inability to effectively utilize visible light, with TiO2’s wide band gap (3.18 eV) confining its activity primarily to the UV region. In contrast, MIL-88B(Fe) demonstrated significantly higher activity (37.2 ± 4.7%), which can be attributed to its narrower band gap (2.49 eV), enabling superior visible light absorption. The incorporation of cobalt to form the bimetallic MIL-88B(Fe/Co) further enhanced the degradation rate to 51.7 ± 4.3%. This improvement is explained by the introduction of new electronic states from cobalt, leading to a further reduction in band gap (2.27 eV) and more efficient electron transfer between the Fe3+/Fe2+ and Co3+/Co2+ redox couples.

The most significant performance leap was observed in the TiO2-based composites. The MIL-88B(Fe)@TiO2 heterostructure achieved 63.5 ± 3.7% degradation. While the composite’s effective light absorption is governed by the narrow band gap of the MIL-88B(Fe) component (2.32 eV), the key to its enhanced activity is the formation of a heterojunction. The predicted Type-II or S-scheme band alignment facilitates the spatial separation of photogenerated electrons and holes across the two materials, drastically reducing recombination. The ultimate performance was exhibited by the bimetallic MIL-88B(Fe/Co)@TiO2 composite, which degraded 83.7 ± 2.5% of BPA. This superior activity is a consequence of a synergistic effect: the narrowest predicted band gap among the catalysts (2.13 eV) ensures the strongest visible light absorption, while the intimate interface with TiO2 provides an optimal pathway for charge separation, collectively maximizing the production of hydroxyl and superoxide radicals. Therefore, based on its superior degradation efficiency, which is mechanistically explained by its favorable band gap and synergistic heterojunction architecture, the bimetallic MIL-88B(Fe/Co)@TiO2 composite is unequivocally selected as the optimal photocatalyst for subsequent investigations.

Optimization of BPA degradation

The optimization of the photocatalytic degradation process for BPA using the bimetallic MIL-88B(Fe/Co)@TiO2 composite was conducted employing a Central Composite Design (CCD) within the framework of Response Surface Methodology (RSM). This approach was selected to overcome the significant limitations inherent in traditional one-factor-at-a-time (OFAT) optimization, which fails to account for critical interaction effects between operational parameters and can lead to an incorrect identification of optimal conditions. The CCD provides a statistically sound and highly efficient experimental framework that enables the comprehensive exploration of the multi-factor space—including pH, catalyst dose, BPA concentration, and hydrogen peroxide percentage—with a minimized number of experimental runs. A key advantage of this design is its capacity to quantify both the individual and interactive influences of these factors on the degradation efficiency, as well as to accurately model the nonlinear, quadratic responses that are characteristic of catalytic systems, where performance reaches a maximum before declining. The resultant data is used to construct a robust second-order polynomial regression model, which not only facilitates the precise prediction of performance under any given set of conditions within the studied range but also allows for the rigorous statistical identification of the true global optimum, thereby providing a deeper mechanistic understanding of the system while ensuring resource-efficient experimentation. The adequacy and significance of the developed response surface quadratic model were rigorously evaluated using Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) at a 95% confidence level (Table 3). The model’s exceptionally high F-value of 27.43 and a corresponding p-value of less than 0.0001 demonstrate that the model is highly significant, implying there is only a minuscule chance (less than 0.01%) that an F-value this large could occur due to noise. This confirms that the regression model possesses a very strong ability to explain the variation in the data and reliably predicts the BPA degradation efficiency within the defined experimental domain. All four linear main factors—catalyst dose (A), pH (B), BPA concentration (C), and H2O2 percentage (D)—were statistically significant, with p-values below 0.05. Among these, pH (B) exhibited the most profound individual effect on the degradation efficiency, as evidenced by its highest F-value (74.98), indicating it is the most influential single parameter in the process. Furthermore, several interaction and quadratic terms were significant. The significant interaction between catalyst dose and pH (AB) suggests that the effect of one factor is dependent on the level of the other. The significant interaction between BPA concentration and H2O2 percentage (CD) indicates a synergistic relationship between the pollutant load and the oxidant amount. The significant quadratic terms for catalyst dose (A2) and BPA concentration (C2) confirm the presence of curvature in the response surface, meaning that the degradation efficiency reaches a maximum value at optimal levels of these factors and decreases thereafter. The “Lack of Fit” test, which compares the residual error to the pure error, is not significant (p-value = 0.3302). This is a desirable outcome, as it indicates that the model is well-fitted to the experimental data and that the unexplained variation is not substantially greater than the inherent noise in the replication (pure error). The non-significant lack of fit further validates the suitability of the quadratic model for accurate prediction and optimization. Besides, the fit statistics presented in Table S1 confirm the high predictive capability and robustness of the developed model. The high coefficient of determination (R2 = 0.9624) indicates that the model explains 96.24% of the total variation in the BPA degradation response. The adjusted R² (0.9273), which penalizes the inclusion of non-significant terms, is in reasonable agreement with the R2, further validating the model’s significance. The predicted R2 of 0.8593 is in acceptable agreement with the adjusted R2, indicating good predictive power for new observations. A low coefficient of variation (C.V. % = 2.78) demonstrates high precision and reliability of the experimental data. Most notably, the high “Adeq Precision” value of 20.426, which measures the signal-to-noise ratio, confirms an adequate signal, suggesting this model can be reliably used to navigate the design space defined by the studied factors.

The plot reveals a pronounced interaction, where the effect of catalyst dose is highly dependent on the pH of the solution. Simultaneously, a higher catalyst dose provides more active sites for both photocatalysis and this radical generation process. Therefore, the optimal condition exists at a balanced ratio where the H2O2 concentration is sufficient to degrade the specific BPA load effectively without inducing significant scavenging side reactions. Mechanistic investigation via reactive species trapping

The identification of the primary reactive oxygen species involved in the photocatalytic process is fundamental to elucidating the underlying degradation mechanism and enables the rational design of more efficient catalysts47–49. To this end, a scavenger study was performed by introducing specific compounds that selectively quench particular reactive species: isopropanol for hydroxyl radicals (·OH), benzoquinone for superoxide anion radicals (O2·–), and ammonium oxalate for photogenerated holes (h⁺). The results of this investigation, summarized in Figure S2, provide definitive insight into the operative degradation pathways. The control experiment conducted without any scavenger yielded a high degradation efficiency of 95.1%, establishing a performance baseline. The introduction of isopropanol caused the most profound inhibition, drastically reducing the efficiency to 24.8%, which unequivocally identifies the hydroxyl radical as the predominant oxidative species responsible for BPA degradation. This exceptional ·OH activity is attributed to the synergistic combination of the photocatalyst’s strong oxidation power and a highly efficient Fenton-like reaction catalyzed by the Fe/Co sites within the bimetallic MOF structure. A significant, though less severe, inhibition was observed with benzoquinone, which lowered the efficiency to 52.6%, underscoring the vital secondary role of superoxide anion radicals in consuming photogenerated electrons and contributing to the oxidative chain reaction. Furthermore, the addition of ammonium oxalate, which scavenges photogenerated holes, resulted in a reduction to 63.9%, confirming that direct oxidation by holes is a consequential but less dominant pathway compared to the radical-driven processes. Collectively, these results validate a multi-pathway degradation mechanism where upon visible light irradiation, the bimetallic MIL-88B(Fe/Co)@TiO2 composite facilitates efficient charge separation at its heterojunction interface. The electrons subsequently reduce oxygen to O2·–, while the holes both directly oxidize BPA and, critically, drive a Fenton-like process that generates a prolific flux of ·OH radicals, the latter being the primary agent for the rapid and complete mineralization of BPA.

The collective experimental evidence allows for the proposal of a coherent degradation mechanism for BPA utilizing the bimetallic MIL-88B(Fe/Co)@TiO2 composite under visible light. The process initiates with the photoexcitation of both components; the MIL-88B(Fe/Co) component, with its narrow bandgap, acts as the primary harvester of visible photons, while the TiO2 component can also be activated by higher-energy photons. The intimate contact at the heterojunction interface then drives the spatial separation of photogenerated charge carriers, with electrons (e⁻) migrating from the conduction band of the MOF to that of TiO2, while the holes (h+) remain in the valence band of the MOF. This effective charge separation is the cornerstone of the high activity observed. The subsequent pathways involve these separated charges in generating reactive oxygen species. The electrons accumulated in the conduction band of TiO2 are captured by dissolved oxygen to yield superoxide anion radicals (O2·–), the quenching of which was shown to significantly impede degradation. Concurrently, the powerful holes in the MOF’s valence band drive two critical processes: the direct oxidation of adsorbed BPA molecules and, most importantly, the activation of H2O2 via the Fe/Co bimetallic sites in a Fenton-like reaction, leading to the prolific generation of hydroxyl radicals (·OH). The scavenger study conclusively identified this ·OH pathway as the dominant route for oxidation. Thus, the synergy between the bimetallic MOF—serving as a potent photosensitizer and Fenton-like catalyst—and TiO2—acting as an excellent electron acceptor—creates a multi-pronged oxidative attack involving h+, O2·–, and predominantly ·OH, which collaboratively mineralize BPA into harmless end products.

Based on the extensive literature on BPA photodegradation in ·OH-dominated advanced oxidation processes and the results of our scavenger studies, a well-established degradation pathway can be rationally inferred for our MIL-88B(Fe/Co)@TiO2 system (Scheme 1). The degradation initiates with hydroxyl radical (·OH) attack on the BPA molecule, targeting the electron-rich isopropylidene bridge and the aromatic rings. This primary attack generates two key intermediates: 4-(2-(4-hydroxyphenyl)propan-2-yl)benzene-1,2-diol (a dihydroxylated BPA derivative) and 4-isopropylphenol.

Upon further ·OH attack, the dihydroxylated intermediate undergoes cleavage to produce 4-isopropylphenol and 1,2,4-benzenetriol (hydroxyhydroquinone). The 1,2,4-benzenetriol is subsequently mineralized to CO2 and H2O through continued ·OH-mediated oxidation and ring-opening reactions.

Simultaneously, the accumulated 4-isopropylphenol undergoes sequential ·OH attacks to generate a mixture of lower molecular weight phenolic compounds, including phenol, hydroquinone, benzoquinone, and catechol (1,2-dihydroxybenzene). These intermediates exist in dynamic redox equilibrium under photocatalytic conditions. Continued ·OH attack on these aromatic compounds leads to ring opening, producing short-chain carboxylic acids (e.g., maleic acid, succinic acid, oxalic acid) and aldehydes, which are ultimately mineralized to CO2 and H2O50–53.

This predicted pathway is fully consistent with: (i) our scavenger studies identifying ·OH as the predominant reactive species; (ii) the enhanced Fenton-like activity of the bimetallic Fe/Co nodes facilitating continuous ·OH generation; and (iii) the high TOC removal efficiency (76.4%) confirming extensive mineralization. The proposed degradation route therefore aligns rigorously with both our experimental evidence and the well-documented mechanistic literature for analogous TiO2-based and MOF-based photocatalytic systems.

Proposed S-scheme charge transfer mechanism

Based on the calculated band alignment (Table S2) and the results of radical scavenging experiments, we propose that the MIL-88B(Fe/Co)@TiO2 heterojunction operates via an S-scheme (Step-scheme) charge transfer mechanism, as illustrated in Scheme 2. Upon formation of the heterojunction, Fermi level equilibration drives electron transfer from TiO2 (which initially possesses a higher Fermi level due to its more negative conduction band) to the MIL-88B(Fe/Co) framework. This creates an internal electric field directed from TiO2 toward the MOF at the interface, accompanied by upward band bending in TiO2 and downward band bending in the MOF.

Under visible light irradiation, only the MIL-88B(Fe/Co) component (Eg = 2.27 eV) is photoexcited, generating electrons in its conduction band (+ 0.35 eV) and holes in its valence band (+ 2.62 eV). TiO2 (Eg = 3.18 eV) cannot be directly excited by visible light; however, its role in the heterojunction is critical for charge separation. Driven by the internal electric field, the photogenerated electrons in the MOF’s conduction band (+ 0.35 eV, weak reducing power) migrate toward the interface and recombine with holes accumulated in TiO2’s valence band (+ 2.90 eV). This recombination selectively eliminates the weakly reductive electrons (MOF CB) and the weakly oxidative holes (TiO2 VB), while preserving the strongly reductive electrons in TiO2’s conduction band (− 0.28 eV) and the strongly oxidative holes in the MOF’s valence band (+ 2.62 eV). The intimate interfacial contact, evidenced by the reduced band gap of the composite (2.13 eV), facilitates this efficient charge transfer. The catalyst reusability and metal leaching

The assessment of catalyst reusability and long-term stability is of paramount importance for evaluating its practical applicability and economic viability, as a robust catalyst must maintain its activity over multiple treatment cycles without requiring frequent replacement. To investigate this, the bimetallic MIL-88B(Fe/Co)@TiO2 composite was subjected to five consecutive cycles of BPA degradation under the previously determined optimum conditions, with the catalyst being recovered, washed, and dried after each run. The results, detailed in Table S3, demonstrate a high initial performance of 95.3% degradation in the first cycle, confirming the catalyst’s potency. The efficiency remained excellent in the second cycle (93.8%) and good in the third (90.1%), indicating reasonable structural stability over initial uses. However, a more pronounced decline was observed in the fourth (84.6%) and fifth (76.5%) cycles. This gradual deactivation is attributed primarily to the cumulative effect of two factors: first, the slow but progressive leaching of Fe and Co ions from the MOF structure under the acidic operational pH, which diminishes the highly active Fenton-like sites crucial for hydroxyl radical generation; and second, the fouling of the catalyst’s active sites and pores through the strong adsorption of stable, intermediate degradation products that are not fully removed by standard washing. In conclusion, while the bimetallic MIL-88B(Fe/Co)@TiO2 composite demonstrates satisfactory stability for up to three cycles with over 90% efficiency, its performance shows significant decline thereafter under these acidic conditions, highlighting that while the catalyst is reusable, its long-term structural integrity against metal leaching and fouling remains a key area for future improvement to enhance its practical durability.

The stability of the MIL‑88B(Fe/Co)@TiO2 composite was assessed by quantifying the leached metal ions after the photocatalytic degradation of BPA. Following the BPA phodegradation under the optimum conditions, the catalyst was removed by centrifugation (7000 rpm, 12 min) followed by filtration through a 0.22 μm syringe filter. The concentrations of Fe, Co, and Ti ions in the supernatant were measured using a flame atomic absorption spectrometer (Hitachi Z – 2000, Japan). All measurements were performed in triplicate, and the mean values are reported. The absolute leached mass (mg) was calculated as C×V, where C is the measured concentration (mg L− 1) and V is the solution volume (0.1 L). The leached amount per gram of catalyst (mg g− 1) was then obtained by dividing the absolute leached mass by the catalyst mass (0.043 g). The concentrations of leached ions were exceptionally low: 0.11 ± 0.005 mg L− 1 for Fe, 0.09 ± 0.003 mg L− 1 for Co, and 0.04 ± 0.002 mg L− 1 for Ti. These values correspond to absolute leached masses of only 0.011 mg (Fe), 0.009 mg (Co), and 0.004 mg (Ti) from an initial catalyst loading of 43 mg, i.e., less than 0.6 mg of metal per gram of catalyst. The negligible leaching demonstrates that the bimetallic MOF framework and its heterojunction with TiO2 remain structurally intact during photocatalysis, and that the observed activity is genuinely heterogeneous.

Performance in real water matrices

To evaluate the practical applicability of the MIL-88B(Fe/Co)@TiO2 composite, real water samples were collected from two different sources in Iran: river water from Mayan River (Torqebah-Shandiz, Iran) and well water from the outskirts of Mashhad, Iran. The samples were filtered through 0.45 μm membrane filters to remove suspended particles. The native BPA concentration in both samples was determined by HPLC and found to be below the detection limit. Therefore, the samples were spiked with a standard BPA solution to achieve a final concentration of 76.4 mg/L, matching the optimized concentration used in synthetic solution studies. Photocatalytic degradation experiments were then performed under the same optimized conditions (43 mg catalyst, pH 4.4, 3.2% H2O2, 90 min visible light irradiation). All experiments were conducted in triplicate. The results indicated that the composite achieved degradation efficiencies of 90.8 ± 2.8% in river water and 91.6 ± 3.1% in well water after 90 min of visible light irradiation. These values are only slightly lower than the 95.1% efficiency obtained in deionized water, demonstrating minimal matrix interference and excellent catalyst robustness. The small reduction in performance is attributed to the presence of naturally occurring dissolved ions, organic matter, and other competing species in real water matrices, which can scavenge reactive radicals or compete for adsorption sites.

While catalysts like Zn3In2S6 achieve a comparable efficiency (95.8%), they require significantly longer irradiation times (200 min) and UV light, whereas our composite attains a 95.3% degradation in just 90 min under visible light. Conclusion

This study successfully demonstrated the fabrication of a novel bimetallic MIL-88B(Fe/Co)@TiO2 heterostructure with exceptional photocatalytic activity for the degradation of BPA under visible light. The intimate interface between the MOF and TiO2 facilitated efficient charge separation, while the Fe/Co bimetallic system concurrently enabled enhanced visible-light harvesting and a potent Fenton-like reaction, leading to a synergistic effect that surpassed the performance of its individual components. The systematic optimization via RSM-CCD provided a robust model for predicting and maximizing degradation efficiency, ultimately achieving near-complete BPA removal. Furthermore, the kinetic and mechanistic investigations confirmed a rapid degradation process predominantly driven by hydroxyl radicals, with the catalyst maintaining significant activity over multiple cycles. The insights gained from this work, particularly the successful integration of bimetallic MOF chemistry with semiconductor photocatalysis, offer a valuable and generalizable strategy for developing high-performance, solar-driven catalysts for the removal of recalcitrant organic pollutants from water.

Current limitations and future roadmap

Despite the promising photocatalytic performance and excellent stability demonstrated by the MIL‑88B(Fe/Co)@TiO2 heterojunction, several limitations remain that warrant future investigation. To date, the composite has been evaluated only at the laboratory scale using simulated wastewater and model pollutants; its performance in real water matrices such as surface water or industrial effluents, and under continuous‑flow conditions, has not yet been assessed. Moreover, while the material exhibits good reusability over initial cycles, long‑term operational stability and regenerability over extended timeframes (> 10 cycles) require further validation to establish commercial viability. Looking forward, several strategic directions are proposed to advance this class of materials. Rational design of bimetallic nodes through systematic variation of the Fe/Co ratio or introduction of other transition metals (e.g., Ni, Cu, Mn) could further tune the band structure and redox potential, with machine‑learning‑assisted high‑throughput screening offering an accelerated pathway to discover optimal compositions. Concurrently, scale‑up and real‑world application studies should focus on immobilising the composite onto suitable supports such as membranes or monolithic reactors and evaluating performance in continuous‑flow photoreactors using genuine water samples, complemented by techno‑economic and life‑cycle assessments. Finally, sustainability considerations call for the development of greener synthesis routes—including solvent‑free or water‑based methods and the use of non‑toxic metal precursors—as well as integration into circular economy frameworks that enable recovery and reuse of leached metals.
