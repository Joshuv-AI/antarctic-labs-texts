Abstract
This paper investigates the identification power gained by combining experimental data, in which treatment is randomized, with observational data, in which treatment is self-selected, for distributional treatment effect (DTE) parameters. While experimental data identify average treatment effects, many DTE parameters, such as the distribution of individual treatment effects, are only partially identified. We examine whether, and how, combining the two data sources tightens the identified set for these parameters. For broad classes of DTE parameters, we derive sharp bounds under the combined data and clarify the mechanism through which the data combination tightens the identified set relative to using experimental data alone. Our analysis highlights that self-selection in the observational data is a key source of identification power. We also characterize necessary and sufficient conditions under which the combined data shrink the identified set, showing that such shrinkage generally occurs unless selection-on-observables holds in the observational data. We also propose a linear programming approach to compute the sharp bounds, which can accommodate additional structural restrictions such as mutual stochastic monotonicity of potential outcomes and the generalized Roy model. An empirical application using data on negative campaign advertisements in a U.S. presidential election illustrates the practical relevance of the approach. Keywords: Copula; Data combination; Heterogeneous treatment effect; Partial identification; Self-selection.

Introduction

In evaluating public policies and medical interventions, researchers often have access to both experimental and observational data. Experimental studies—such as randomized controlled trials (RCTs)—are widely regarded as the gold standard for causal inference, whereas observational data are far more prevalent in practice and often contain rich behavioral variation. This paper investigates the identification gains that arise from combining these two data sources, with a focus on distributional treatment effects.

We consider a general setting in which treatment receipt and outcomes are observed from two data sources: experimental data, where treatment is randomly assigned, and observational data, where treatment is self-selected. This structure arises naturally in various policy and medical contexts. In development policy, for example, the efficacy of public health interventions such as mosquito nets is evaluated both through field experiments (where nets are randomly assigned) and through observational data (where nets are self-purchased). In education, randomized class-size assignment (e.g., Project STAR) coexists with parental or administrative decisions about classroom placements. In clinical medicine, drugs may be randomly assigned in clinical trials but, once approved, are prescribed based on physician judgment.

Experimental data are valuable because the random assignment of treatment enables identification of key causal parameters, such as the average treatment effect (ATE). However, beyond the ATE, distributional treatment effect (DTE) parameters are often crucial to policy and medical evaluation, as well as to understanding treatment effect heterogeneity across individuals. Letting
Y_{1}
and
Y_{0}
denote potential outcomes under treatment and no-treatment, respectively, examples of such parameters include:
(i) the fraction of individuals who benefit from treatment,
\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}>Y_{0})
;
(ii) the distribution function of individual treatment effects,
F_{Y_{1}-Y_{0}}(\delta)\equiv\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}-Y_{0}\leq\delta)
;
(iii) the ATE for disadvantaged individuals,
\mathbb{E}[Y_{1}-Y_{0}\mid Y_{0}\leq c]
, where the subpopulation with
Y_{0}\leq c
represents individuals whose baseline outcomes fall below a threshold level
c
(e.g., a poverty line); and (iv) the correlation between
Y_{1}
and
Y_{0}
.

Although these parameters are highly relevant for policy and medical evaluation, they are generally not point-identified from experimental data alone, as identification generally requires knowledge of the joint distribution of the two potential outcomes. Experimental data identify only the marginal distributions of
Y_{1}
and
Y_{0}
, and thus yield partial identification (e.g.,
Heckman et al. 1997
;
Fan and Park 2010
). However, the resulting identified sets are often wide and, consequently, not very informative.

This paper explores the extent to which combining experimental and observational data can improve identification for such distributional parameters. Specifically, we address the following questions:
(i) Does combining the two data sources shrink the identified set for DTE parameters?;
(ii) If so, what is the mechanism behind such shrinkage, and when does it occur?;
(iii) How can we compute the identified set under such a data combination?

To investigate these questions, we build on a framework that incorporates both experimental and observational data. In this setting, researchers observe individuals’ outcomes
Y
, treatment status
D\in\{0,1\}
, and a data source indicator
G\in\{\mathrm{exp},\mathrm{obs}\}
, denoting whether the observation comes from an experimental study (
G=\mathrm{exp}
) or an observational study (
G=\mathrm{obs}
). A key element of the framework is a latent self-selection variable
S\in\{0,1\}
, which represents the treatment choice an individual would make under self-selection. This variable is observed in the observational data (where
S=D
) but unobserved in the experimental data. We compare the identified sets under each data setting using a copula-based approach. Under standard assumptions—random treatment assignment in the experimental data and external validity across data sources— this framework enables a systematic comparison of what can be learned from experimental data alone versus from the combination of both data sources.

Our central theoretical results show that the identified set under the combined data is generally smaller than that under the experimental data alone, except in special cases where the self-selection variable
S
is independent of the potential outcomes. The key intuition is as follows. While experimental data identify only the marginal distributions
F_{Y_{1}}
and
F_{Y_{0}}
of the potential outcomes, combining the two data sources enables identification of the joint distributions
F_{Y_{1}S}
and
F_{Y_{0}S}
of
(Y_{1},S)
and
(Y_{0},S)
, respectively. We also formally show that the distribution pairs
(F_{Y_{1}},F_{Y_{0}})
and
(F_{Y_{1}S},F_{Y_{0}S})
fully characterize the identified sets under the experimental and combined data, respectively.
Because the self-selection variable
S
encodes information about the dependence structure between the potential outcomes, knowledge of
(F_{Y_{1}S},F_{Y_{0}S})
can tighten the set of feasible joint distributions of
(Y_{1},Y_{0})
.

To quantify this identification gain and derive analytical expressions for the sharp bounds, we apply copula bound analysis by building on and extending
Fan et al. 2017
, who study the identifying power of covariates, to settings with a self-selection variable
S
.
For broad classes of DTE parameters, represented by super-modular functions or
\varphi
-indicator functions (defined later), we derive explicit sharp bounds, which facilitate computing the identified set under data combination, and present necessary and sufficient conditions under which the data combination yields tighter identified sets. We show that combining the data sources improves identification when the dependence structure between the potential outcomes varies across self-selection types.

To further tighten the identified sets, we also consider incorporating additional structural restrictions that are plausible in many empirical contexts. In particular, we consider two such restrictions: mutual stochastic monotonicity of the potential outcomes, as studied by
Frandsen and Lefgren 2021
, and a generalized Roy model condition. Though not required for our main results, these restrictions can substantially narrow the identified sets when imposed. To incorporate them under data combination, we develop a computational approach based on linear programming that efficiently computes sharp bounds for a broad class of DTE parameters.

Our analysis also applies to data from Doubly Randomized Preference Trials (DRPTs)
(
Rücker 1989
)
, a unique yet recently prevalent design. In a DRPT, individuals are randomly assigned to one of three groups: a treatment group, a control (no-treatment) group, or a self-selection group, in which individuals choose between treatment and no-treatment. This design has been employed in a range of studies across the social sciences
(
Gaines and Kuklinski 2011
;
Arceneaux et al. 2012
;
De Benedictis-Kessner et al. 2019
;
Ida et al. 2024
)
and medical sciences
(
King et al. 2005
;
Howard and Thornicroft 2006
)
.

Related Literature

This paper relates to and contributes to two strands of literature: (i) the combination of random-assignment and self-selection data sources for causal inference, and (ii) the partial or point identification of distributional treatment effects.

The first strand of the literature explores the potential benefits of combining data from random-assignment and self-selection sources. This literature typically pursues two objectives: (1) improving the precision of estimates when experimental data suffice for identification, and (2) identifying causal effects that are not identifiable from experimental data alone. Regarding the first objective,
Rosenman et al. 2023
,
Yang et al. 2023
,
Gui 2024
, and
Lin et al. 2025
propose novel estimation methods that improve the efficiency of treatment effect estimation through combining experimental and observational data. For the second objective,
Long et al. 2008
show that the ATE for each self-selection group,
\mathbb{E}[Y_{1}-Y_{0}|S=s]
for
s=0,1
, can be identified using data from a DRPT.
Knox et al. 2019
extend this to multiple treatment settings and provide partial identification results. In a different context where experimental data contain secondary outcomes and observational data contain primary outcomes,
Athey et al. 2025
show the identification of the ATE for the primary outcomes. Our setting differs from theirs, as we consider two data sources sharing the same outcome type.

Our contribution to this strand of literature is to uncover a novel advantage of data combination: it can shrink the identified set for DTE parameters. In contrast to prior works focused on point identification of new parameters or improvements in estimation efficiency, we show that combining data improves informativeness by tightening bounds in partial identification settings.

The second strand of the literature investigates the partial or point identification of distributional treatment effects under various assumptions and structural restrictions.

Setup

2.1
Combined Experimental and Observational Data

We suppose that the observed data consist of the quadruple
(Y,D,G,X)
, where
Y
is the observed outcome;
D\in\{0,1\}
is a binary treatment indicator;
G\in\{\mathrm{exp},\mathrm{obs}\}
indicates the data source; and
X
denotes a vector of covariates (pre-treatment variables). Specifically,
G
indicates whether each observation originates from an experimental study (
G=\mathrm{exp}
) or an observational study (
G=\mathrm{obs}
). The treatment indicator
D
equals 1 if the individual receives the treatment and 0 otherwise. In the experimental data,
D
is randomly assigned, whereas in the observational data, it is determined by self-selection. Let
Y_{1}
and
Y_{0}
be continuous random variables representing the potential outcomes under treatment and no-treatment, respectively. The observed outcome is defined as
Y\equiv DY_{1}+(1-D)Y_{0}
. We denote the support of
X
by
\mathcal{X}
.

We introduce a latent self-selection variable
S\in\{0,1\}
that represents the treatment choice an individual would make if he or she self-selects. In the observational data (
G=\mathrm{obs}
), we suppose that
S
coincides with the observed treatment, i.e.,
S=D
. In the experimental data (
G=\mathrm{exp}
),
S
is unobserved. The variable
S
can also be interpreted as a latent preference for treatment versus no-treatment.

Let
F^{\ast}
denote the true cumulative distribution function (CDF) of all defined variables
(Y_{1},Y_{0},Y,D,S,G,X)
, including both observed and unobserved ones.
For any generic CDF
F
, we denote probability and expectation under
F
by
\mathbb{P}_{F}(\cdot)
and
\mathbb{E}_{F}[\cdot]
, respectively. Specifically, for the true CDF
F^{\ast}
, we use the shorthand
\mathbb{P}(\cdot)
and
\mathbb{E}[\cdot]
in place of
\mathbb{P}_{F^{\ast}}(\cdot)
and
\mathbb{E}_{F^{\ast}}[\cdot]
.

Throughout the paper, we suppose that the true CDF
F^{\ast}
satisfies the following assumptions.

Assumption 2.1

(Potential Outcomes)
.

Y=DY_{1}+(1-D)Y_{0}
a.s.

Assumption 2.2

(Self-selection)
.

\mathbb{P}(S=D\mid G=\mathrm{obs},X)=1
a.s.

Assumption 2.3

(Random Assignment)
.

(Y_{1},Y_{0})\perp\!\!\!\perp D\mid X,G=\mathrm{exp}
.

Assumption 2.4

(External Validity)
.

(Y_{1},Y_{0},S)\perp\!\!\!\perp G|X
.

Assumption 2.5

(Overlap)
.

\mathbb{P}(D=d,G=g|X)>0
a.s. for all
d\in\{0,1\}
and
g\in\{\mathrm{exp},\mathrm{obs}\}
.

Assumption
2.2
states that self-selection corresponds to the received treatment in the observational data, which is trivially satisfied by the definition of
S
. Assumption
2.3
requires that treatment is randomly assigned in the experimental study, possibly conditional on the pre-treatment variables
X
.
Assumption
2.4
concerns the external validity of the data sources, requiring that systematic difference between the populations in the experimental and observational studies are captured by the pre-treatment variables
X
.
This assumption is automatically satisfied under a DRPT design, where the data source indicator
G
is randomly assigned; specifically, individuals are randomly assigned to the treatment, no-treatment, and self-selection groups. Assumption
2.5
imposes an overlap condition on both the treatment status and data source.
3
3

3

This overlap condition can be relaxed at the cost of obtaining a wider identified set from the combined data. For instance, if a covariate value
x
does not satisfy
\mathbb{P}(D=d,G=g\mid X=x)>0
for some
d
or
g
, then experimental and observational data cannot be combined for that value of
x
. However, one may still use either the experimental or the observational data alone to construct an identified set conditional on
x
.

2.2
Distributional Treatment Effect Parameters

Following
Fan et al. 2017
, we consider a parameter of interest
\theta_{o}\in\mathbb{R}
that has the following form:

\displaystyle\theta_{o}\equiv\mathbb{E}\left[\psi(Y_{1},Y_{0})\right],

where
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
is a specified function. With various specification of
\psi
, this formulation encompasses a wide range of DTE parameters, as illustrated below:

Example 2.1

(Fraction of Positive Treatment Effects)
.

When
\psi(y_{1},y_{0})=\mathbf{1}\{y_{1}>y_{0}\}
, we have
\theta_{o}=\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}>Y_{0})
, the fraction of individuals who benefit from the treatment. This parameter captures the distributional impact of the treatment. Importantly, even when the ATE
\mathbb{E}[Y_{1}-Y_{0}]
is positive,
\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}>Y_{0})
may be small, indicating that few individuals experience large gains while many incur losses. Focusing on
\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}>Y_{0})
is thus essential to avoid interventions that are unfavorable to the majority.

Example 2.2

(Distribution Function of Treatment Effects)
.

More generally, when
\psi(y_{1},y_{0})=\mathbf{1}\{y_{1}-y_{0}<\delta\}
for a fixed
\delta
, the parameter
\theta_{o}
corresponds to the distribution function of the individual treatment effect:
F_{Y_{1}-Y_{0}}^{\ast}(\delta)\equiv\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}-Y_{0}\leq\delta)
.

Example 2.3

(ATE for the Disadvantaged)
.

We can also consider the ATE for disadvantaged individuals, defined as
\mathbb{E}[Y_{1}-Y_{0}\mid Y_{0}\leq c]
for a fixed threshold
c
. This can be expressed by the function
\psi(y_{1},y_{0})=(y_{1}-y_{0})\cdot\mathbf{1}\{y_{0}\leq c\}/\mathbb{P}(Y_{0}\leq c)
. The subpopulation with
Y_{0}\leq c
represents individuals whose baseline outcomes fall below the disadvantage threshold
c
(e.g., a poverty line). The nuisance parameter
\mathbb{P}(Y_{0}\leq c)
appearing in
\psi(y_{1},y_{0})
is point-identified from experimental data under the maintained assumptions as
\mathbb{P}(Y_{0}\leq c)=\mathbb{P}(Y\leq c\mid D=0,G=\textit{exp})
.

Example 2.4

(Probability of Upward Mobility)
.

When
\psi
is specified as
\psi(y_{1},y_{0})=\mathbf{1}\{y_{1}>c,y_{0}\leq c\}/\mathbb{P}(Y_{0}\leq c)
for a fixed threshold
c
, the target parameter
\theta_{o}
becomes
\theta_{o}=\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}>c\mid Y_{0}\leq c)
, which represents the probability of upward mobility among individuals initially below
c
. This parameter captures the likelihood that individuals whose baseline outcomes fall below the threshold—such as a poverty line—will exceed it under treatment. Unlike the ATE, this measure focuses specifically on distributional gains among the disadvantaged.

Example 2.5

(Correlation Between the Two Potential Outcomes)
.

When
\psi(y_{1},y_{0})=(y_{1}-\mathbb{E}[Y_{1}])(y_{0}-\mathbb{E}[Y_{0}])/\sqrt{\text{Var}(Y_{1})\text{Var}(Y_{0})}
, the target parameter
\theta_{o}
corresponds to
\text{Cor}(Y_{1},Y_{0})
, the correlation between the two potential outcomes. This parameter reflects the degree of similarity in individual-level responses to the two interventions, offering insight into treatment effect heterogeneity. The nuisance components
\mathbb{E}[Y_{d}]
and
\text{Var}(Y_{d})
for each
d\in\{0,1\}
are point-identified from experimental data under the maintained assumptions.

Note that none of the DTE parameters introduced above can be point-identified, even with experimental data. This is because their identification generally requires knowledge of the joint distribution of the two potential outcomes,
F_{Y_{1}Y_{0}}^{\ast}
, whereas the experimental data only identify the marginal distributions,
(F_{Y_{1}}^{\ast},F_{Y_{0}}^{\ast})
.

We can also consider DTE parameters for various subpopulations: the parameter for each self-section group,
\theta_{o,s}\equiv\mathbb{E}[\psi(Y_{1},Y_{0})\mid S=s]
; the parameter conditional on covariates,
\theta_{o,x}\equiv\mathbb{E}[\psi(Y_{1},Y_{0})\mid X=x]
; and the parameter for each data source population,
\theta_{o,g}\equiv\mathbb{E}[\psi(Y_{1},Y_{0})\mid G=g]
for
g\in\{\mathrm{exp},\mathrm{obs}\}
.
In particular,
\theta_{o,s}
captures the treatment effect for individuals who self-select into treatment (
s=1
) or no-treatment (
s=0
), and is relevant in many empirical applications.

These subpopulation parameters can be incorporated into our framework with slight modifications. For example, given a fixed
\psi
, the parameter
\theta_{o,g}
for each data source population can be expressed as
\theta_{o,g}=\mathbb{E}\left[\psi(Y_{1},Y_{0})\cdot\mathbf{1}\{G=g\}/\mathbb{P}(G=g)\right]
, where
\mathbb{P}(G=g)
is point-identified from the observed data. By defining
\tilde{\psi}(y_{1},y_{0})=\psi(y_{1},y_{0})\cdot\mathbf{1}\{G=g\}/\mathbb{P}(G=g)
, the parameter
\theta_{o,g}
takes the form
\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{\psi}(Y_{1},Y_{0})\right]
, and can therefore be treated analogously to
\theta_{o}
in our analysis. As for
\theta_{o,s}
, Lemma
C.1
in the supplementary appendix shows that
\theta_{o,s}=\mathbb{E}[\psi(Y_{1},Y_{0})|D=s,G=\mathrm{obs}]
, which can be equivalently written as
\theta_{o,s}=\mathbb{E}[\psi(Y_{1},Y_{0})\cdot\mathbf{1}\{D=s,G=\mathrm{obs}\}/\mathbb{P}(D=s,G=\mathrm{obs})]
. Hence,
\theta_{o,s}
can likewise be handled in the same manner as
\theta_{o}
.

In what follows, we examine the partial identification of
\theta_{o}
using experimental data alone and using both experimental and observational data.

2.3
Identified Sets for DTE parameters under Experimental and Combined Data

We begin by formally defining the identified sets for the DTE parameter
\theta_{o}
under two data scenarios: experimental data only and the combined experimental and observational data. We first consider the case based solely on experimental data.

Let
\mathcal{F}^{\dagger}
denote the class of CDFs for all defined variables
(Y_{1},Y_{0},Y,D,S,G,X)
that satisfy Assumptions
2.1
–
2.5
.
4
4

4

Formally,
\mathcal{F}^{\dagger}
is the set of all CDFs
F
of
(Y_{1},Y_{0},Y,D,S,G,X)
that satisfy Assumptions
2.1
–
2.5
with
F^{\ast}
replaced by
F
.

We define the identified set for the joint CDF
F^{\ast}
under the experimental data as

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{exp}}^{\ast}\equiv\left\{F\in\mathcal{F}^{\dagger}:F_{YDX|G}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot|\mathrm{exp})=F_{YDX|G}^{\ast}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot|\mathrm{exp}),\ F_{X}(\cdot)=F_{X}^{\ast}(\cdot)\right\},

where
F_{YDX|G}^{\ast}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot|\mathrm{exp})
is the distribution of the observables
(Y,D,X)
in the experimental data, and
F_{X}^{\ast}
is the marginal distribution of covariates in the overall population.
The set
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{exp}}^{\ast}
comprises all CDFs that satisfy the maintained assumptions and are consistent with the distribution of the observed experimental data and the marginal distribution of covariates in the population.

We assume that
F_{X}^{\ast}
is known, as it is required to adjust for potential imbalances in the covariate distribution between the experimental and observational data and to facilitate meaningful comparisons between the identified sets. In practice, such covariate information is often available from external sources, such as demographic data for the overall population.
There are also some cases where knowledge of
F_{X}^{\ast}
is not required—for example, when the covariate distribution is balanced between the data sources (i.e.,
F_{X|G}^{\ast}(\cdot\mid\mathrm{exp})=F_{X|G}^{\ast}(\cdot\mid\mathrm{obs})
), or when the parameter of interest is the covariate-conditional effect
\theta_{o,x}
or the effect
\theta_{o,\mathrm{exp}}
defined for the experimental population. In such cases,
F_{X}^{\ast}
is either identified from the experimental data or irrelevant to the target parameter.

Given the identified set of CDFs
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{exp}}^{\ast}
, the identified set for
\theta_{o}
under the experimental data is defined as

\displaystyle\Theta_{I}\equiv\left\{\mathbb{E}_{F}[\psi(Y_{1},Y_{0})]:F\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{exp}}^{\ast}\right\}.

(1)

This set comprises all parameter values that are attainable under some distribution in
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{exp}}^{\ast}
. Any parameter value outside
\Theta_{I}
is incompatible with either the experimental data or the maintained assumptions.

We next consider the case where both experimental and observational data are available. As before, we begin by defining the identified set for the joint CDF
F^{\ast}
under the combined data, in analogy to
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{exp}}^{\ast}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\ast}\equiv\left\{F\in\mathcal{F}^{\dagger}:F_{YDGX}=F_{YDGX}^{\ast}\right\},

where
F_{YDGX}^{\ast}
is the joint distribution of the observables
(Y,D,G,X)
in the combined data. This set consists of all CDFs that satisfy the maintained assumptions and are consistent with the observed distribution
F_{YDGX}^{\ast}
.
Note that
\mathcal{F}^{\ast}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{exp}}^{\ast}
, since the condition
F_{YDGX}=F_{YDGX}^{\ast}
implies both
F_{YDX|G}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot\mid\mathrm{exp})=F_{YDX|G}^{\ast}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot\mid\mathrm{exp})
and
F_{X}=F_{X}^{\ast}
.

Given the identified set of CDFs
\mathcal{F}^{\ast}
, the identified set for
\theta_{o}
under the combined data is defined as

\displaystyle\Theta_{IC}\equiv\left\{\mathbb{E}_{F}[\psi(Y_{1},Y_{0})]:F\in\mathcal{F}^{\ast}\right\}.

(2)

This set comprises all parameter values that are attainable under some CDF in
\mathcal{F}^{\ast}
. Any value outside
\Theta_{IC}
contradicts either the maintained assumptions or the combined data.

Since
\mathcal{F}^{\ast}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{exp}}^{\ast}
, we have
\Theta_{IC}\subseteq\Theta_{I}
; that is, the identified set under the combined data is no larger than that under experimental data alone. Our primary interest, however, lies in whether the strict inclusion
\Theta_{IC}\subset\Theta_{I}
holds—that is, whether combining experimental and observational data strictly tightens the identified set for
\theta_{o}
.
This is a nontrivial question, as a strict inclusion
\mathcal{F}^{\ast}\subset\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{exp}}^{\ast}
at the level of CDFs does not necessarily imply a strict inclusion
\Theta_{IC}\subset\Theta_{I}
at the level of the target parameter. We examine this question in the following sections.

3
Characterization of the Identified Sets

To investigate whether the strict inclusion
\Theta_{IC}\subset\Theta_{I}
holds, the definitions of
\Theta_{I}
and
\Theta_{IC}
given in equations (
1
) and (
2
) are too abstract to draw any meaningful insight. We therefore seek to characterize these identified sets in a more interpretable form. To this end, we make use of the concept of bivariate copulas
(
Sklar 1959

\Theta_{I}

(F_{Y_{1}|X}^{\ast},F_{Y_{0}|X}^{\ast})
, as
F_{Y_{d}|X}^{\ast}(\cdot|x)=F_{Y|DGX}^{\ast}(\cdot|d,\mathrm{exp},x)
for
d=0,1
.

4

In this section, we explicitly characterize the sharp bounds for the DTE parameter
\theta_{o}

Following

5
Additional Restrictions and Computational Approach

As in the philosophy of partial identification analysis
(
Manski 2003
)
, additional model restrictions can help further narrow the identified set for
\theta_{o}
when they are plausible. However, such restrictions may also complicate the computation of the identified set, particularly by making it difficult to derive analytical expressions for the sharp bounds, as was done in the previous section. In this section, we introduce two additional restrictions that are plausible in many empirical contexts and present a computational approach to obtain the identified set under these restrictions.

5.1
Additional Restrictions

The first restriction we consider is mutual stochastic monotonicity of the potential outcomes, as studied by
Frandsen and Lefgren 2021
. We say that the potential outcomes
Y_{1}
and
Y_{0}
are
mutually stochastically increasing
if they satisfy the following condition.

Assumption 5.1

(Mutually Stochastically Increasing)
.

The conditional distributions
\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}\leq t\mid Y_{0}=y,S=s,X=x)
and
\mathbb{P}(Y_{0}\leq t\mid Y_{1}=y,S=s,X=x)
are each non-increasing in
y
almost everywhere, for almost all
(s,x)\in{0,1}\times\mathcal{X}
.
10
10

10

Conditioning on
S
and
X
may render this assumption restrictive. Alternatively, one may impose the same condition without conditioning on
S
and
X
, resulting in a weaker assumption with less identifying power.

This assumption implies that individuals with higher potential outcomes under one treatment state are more likely to have higher potential outcomes under the other state. Such an assumption is plausible in many empirical contexts—for example, in a small-class-size program, students who perform well academically in either small or regular-sized classes are also likely to perform well in the alternative class size.
Frandsen and Lefgren 2021
demonstrate that this assumption substantially tightens the identified set for the distribution of treatment effects.
Chetty et al. 2017
also employ a similar restriction to sharpen bounds on DTE parameters in their empirical study of income mobility.

The second restriction concerns the self-selection mechanism for treatment.

Assumption 5.2

(Generalized Roy Model Selection)
.

The inequality
\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}-Y_{0}>c\mid S=1,X=x)\geq\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}-Y_{0}>c\mid S=0,X=x)
holds for all

Y_{1}-Y_{0}
and for almost all

.

11

11

One may also impose this assumption without conditioning on
X

This assumption implies that individuals who self-select into treatment are more likely to experience higher treatment effects than those who self-select into no-treatment.

12

It may be more natural to formulate this assumption solely based on observational data by replacing the inequality

We refer to this as the generalized Roy model selection assumption, as it is implied by the selection behavior in the generalized Roy model. This assumption pertains specifically to observational data, as it restricts the self-selection mechanism. Hence, a further benefit of incorporating observational data is the ability to impose such restrictions on the self-selection mechanism to improve identification.

6
Empirical Illustration

We illustrate the identification result using the DRPT data from
Gaines and Kuklinski 2011
, conducted in Illinois as part of the Cooperative Campaign Analysis Project 2008. The study evaluated the impact of negative campaign advertisements on candidate evaluations during the 2008 U.S. presidential election.

The sample consists of 483 adult residents of Illinois, randomly assigned to three groups: treatment (118 residents), no-treatment (129 residents), and self-selection (236 residents). Individuals in the treatment group were exposed to negative campaign e-flyers about John McCain and Barack Obama, while those in the no-treatment group were not. Participants in the self-selection group chose whether to view the material, with 90 of 236 opting in. The outcome variable
Y
is each respondent’s feeling thermometer rating toward each candidate, ranging from 0 (unfavorable) to 100 (favorable), with 50 representing a neutral stance. The dataset includes a single covariate
X
indicating each respondent’s partisanship: Republican, Democrat, or Independent.

Let
Y_{d,\text{McCain}}
and
Y_{d,\text{Obama}}
denote the potential feeling thermometer ratings toward John McCain and Barack Obama, respectively, under treatment status
d\in\{0,1\}
, where
d=1
indicates exposure to the negative campaign flyer. Our parameter of interest is
\mathbb{P}(Y_{1,j}<Y_{0,j})
, the proportion of individuals whose evaluation of candidate
j\in\{\text{McCain},\text{Obama}\}
is negatively affected by the negative campaign material. A notable feature of this parameter is that it remains well-defined even if the outcome
Y_{d,j}
is ordinal rather than cardinal. This is particularly relevant in our application, as the feeling thermometer rating reflects individuals’ subjective assessments and lacks strong cardinal meaning (see, e.g.,
Wilcox et al. 1989
). Many other common causal parameters such as the ATE,
\mathbb{E}[Y_{1,j}-Y_{0,j}]
, are less meaningful in this context.

7
Conclusion

This paper examines how combining experimental data with observational data improves the identification of DTE parameters.
We show that the identified set under the combined data is fully characterized by the joint distribution of each potential outcome, the self-selection variable, and covariates, with the self-selection variable serving as a key source of the identification gain.
For a broad class of DTR parameters—represented by super/sub-modular functions and
\varphi
-indicator functions—we derive analytical expressions for the sharp bounds under the combined data. We further characterize necessary and sufficient conditions under which the combined data strictly shrink the identified set, showing that such shrinkage generally occurs unless selection-on-observables holds in the observational data.
We also propose a computational approach based on linear programming to compute the sharp bounds, which can incorporate additional structural assumptions such as mutual stochastic monotonicity of the potential outcomes and the generalized Roy model.
An empirical application using DRPT data on negative campaign advertisements in a U.S. presidential election illustrates the practical relevance of combining random-assignment and self-selection data sources, and highlights a novel advantage of DRPT designs in improving the informativeness of distributional treatment effects analyses.
