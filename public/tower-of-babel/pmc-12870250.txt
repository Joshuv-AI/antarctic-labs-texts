Rg1-preconditioned adipose-derived mesenchymal stromal cells alleviate colitis via exosome-mediated Inhibition of macrophage glycolysis through RAS signaling
Authors: Yuan Fang, Yanni Chen, Xinlang Yu, Jiakang Zhang, Weina Zhu, Chenghong Mou, Dawei Wang, Ao Chen, Rui Zhang, Qizhi Liu, Taosheng Li, Bin Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AimsThe therapeutic potential of adipose-derived mesenchymal stromal cells (ADSCs) in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is well established, yet the mechanisms underlying their immunoregulatory effects remain unclear. Ginsenoside Rg1 has been shown to enhance the immunomodulatory properties of ADSCs. Given the key role of macrophages in IBD pathogenesis, this study aimed to explore whether mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs), particularly following Rg1 pretreatment, alleviate colitis by modulating macrophage immunometabolism.Main methodsIn vitro experiments were performed in RAW264.7 macrophages and NCM460 cell. In vivo experiments were performed in C57BL/6 mice.Key findingsRg1 pretreatment upregulated stemness-related genes and downregulated immunogenic markers in ADSCs. In vivo, Rg1-preconditioned ADSCs significantly alleviated DSS-induced colitis and inhibited M1 macrophage polarization by reducing glycolytic activity. Mechanistically, ADSC-derived exosomes delivered miRNAs that suppressed glycolysis and RAS signaling in macrophages, thereby limiting pro-inflammatory polarization and ameliorating colitis. This miRNA is presumed to be miR-574-3p.SignificanceMSCs alleviate experimental colitis by modulating macrophage immunometabolism, and Rg1 pretreatment further enhances this therapeutic potential. This effect may involve exosomal miRNAs that inhibit macrophage glycolysis by suppressing the RAS pathway, one of which is predicted to be miR-574-3p. These results suggest a possible metabolic mechanism underlying MSC-based immunotherapy for ulcerative colitis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-025-04822-4.

Introduce

Inflammatory bowel diseases (IBD), mainly Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC), involve chronic idiopathic gastrointestinal inflammation and have become a rising global health concern over the past decade [1]. Evidence points to immune dysregulation as a key mechanism underlying IBD [2]. Pharmacological interventions for ulcerative colitis primarily target immune dysregulation through agents such as 5-ASA (5-aminosalicylic acid), corticosteroids, immunomodulators, biologics, and calcineurin inhibitors, yet concerns persist regarding their long-term effectiveness and safety [3, 4].

Macrophages adapt to their microenvironment by adopting either pro-inflammatory or anti-inflammatory phenotypes, reflecting their high degree of functional plasticity [5]. To cope with environmental shifts, immune cells dynamically adjust metabolic processes like glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation [6]. In addition to meeting energy demands, metabolic reprogramming orchestrates M1/M2 polarization in macrophages, thereby maintaining their specialized functions [7]. Immune-driven M1 macrophage polarization contributes significantly to the pathogenesis of colitis by amplifying inflammatory injury [8]. During polarization, M1 macrophages primarily depend on glycolysis for rapid ATP production, driven by TCA (tricarboxylic acid) cycle disruption and succinate-mediated stabilization of HIF-1α (hypoxia-inducible factor-1α), which enhances glycolytic gene expression [9]. Impaired resolution of intestinal inflammation in IBD is strongly associated with macrophage activity, highlighting them as promising targets for therapeutic intervention [10].

Mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) are multipotent cells with self-renewal capacity that can be isolated from various tissues, including bone marrow, adipose tissue, umbilical cord, placenta, and dental pulp [11]. Adipose-derived mesenchymal stromal cells (ADSCs), which are readily accessible and abundant, excel in immunomodulation, making them a promising candidate for immune-related therapies [12, 13]. Studies have shown that ADSC clusters can significantly inhibit the infiltration of pro-inflammatory macrophages [14]. MSCs are thought to contribute to therapy via paracrine signaling, primarily through exosome secretion that facilitates crosstalk with macrophages [15]. Exosomes are nanosized extracellular vesicles (50–100 nm) that mediate communication between cells by transferring molecular signals [16]. Ginsenoside Rg1 (Rg1), extracted from ginseng roots or stems, exhibits multiple pharmacological activities, including anti-aging, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant effects [17]. Rg1 promotes MSC proliferation via the PI3K/AKT pathway, reduces senescence by inhibiting p16^INK4a and p53/p21^CIP1, and enhances their paracrine effects [18]. Evidence suggests that MSCs pretreated with Rg1 facilitate M2 polarization of macrophages [19], alleviate local inflammation [20], and reduce apoptotic activity [21].

MSCs hold strong therapeutic potential in IBD and regenerative medicine due to their immune and tissue-repair capacities [22]. Both preclinical and clinical studies have highlighted the therapeutic potential of MSCs. Although further large-scale studies are needed, current clinical evidence supports the safety and therapeutic potential of MSCs in UC [23, 24]. In parallel, emerging preclinical data suggest that MSCs may alleviate UC by activating macrophage SIRT1–FXR signaling and suppressing Th1/Th17-mediated immune responses [25–27].

Emerging evidence has linked the pathogenesis of IBD to macrophage dysfunction and metabolic dysregulation, highlighting MSCs as a promising therapeutic strategy. Pretreatment with Rg1 has been shown to enhance the reparative properties of MSCs. In this study, we further demonstrated that MSCs promote tissue repair by releasing exosomes, which are internalized by pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages. This exosomal uptake inhibits glycolytic activity in M1 macrophages through activation of the RAS–RAF–MEK–ERK signaling pathway, thereby contributing to the restoration of intestinal epithelial integrity under inflammatory conditions.

Methods

Animal experiments

Male C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old) were obtained from Charles River (Beijing, China) and housed in the Animal Center of Southeast University (Jiangbei Campus). After a 7-day acclimation period, colitis was induced by administering 3% dextran sulfate sodium (DSS; 60316ES60, YEASEN, Shanghai, China) in drinking water for 7 days, followed by 3 days of regular water. On days 3 and 6, the ADSC group received intraperitoneal (i.p.) injections of 1 × 10⁶ ADSCs suspended in 200 µL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS); the Rg1 group received i.p. injections of 1 × 10⁶ Rg1-pretreated ADSCs in 200 µL PBS; the GW4869 group received i.p. injections of 1 × 10⁶ ADSCs pretreated with 20 µM GW4869 (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 24 h in 200 µL PBS [28, 29]; and the EXO group received 100 µg of ADSC-derived exosomes suspended in 200 µL PBS via tail vein injection. Body weight and stool consistency were recorded daily as previously described [30]. Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (R510-22-10, RWD Life Science, China) for blood collection and subsequently euthanized by cervical dislocation, in accordance with institutional ethical guidelines. All procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Southeast University (Approval No. 20240402008).

Cell culture

ADSCs (catalog HUXMD-01001) were sourced from OriCell (Shanghai, China). The murine macrophage cell line RAW264.7 (CL-0190) and the human normal colonic epithelial cell line NCM460 (ZQ1079) were obtained from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China) and Shanghai Zhong Qiao Xin Zhou Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), respectively. Cells were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Biochannel, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Biochannel, China) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (P/S; Biochannel, China), and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. Once reaching approximately 80% confluence, cells were passaged, and those at passage 5 (P5) were used for all experiments. Rg1 (B21057, Shanghai Yuanye, China) was initially dissolved in 50 µL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and then diluted with 10.986 mL of DMEM to prepare a 1 mM stock solution. For treatment, the stock solution was further diluted with culture medium to a final concentration of 30 µM.

For macrophage polarization, RAW264.7 cells were treated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS, L2880, Sigma, USA) and interferon-gamma (IFN-γ; HY-P7071, MedChemExpress, USA) dissolved in sterile ddH₂O to induce the M1 phenotype, or with interleukin-4 (IL-4; HEK293-derived, MedChemExpress, USA) dissolved in sterile ddH₂O to induce M2 polarization. NCM460 cells were stimulated with LPS dissolved in sterile ddH₂O, and ADSCs were exposed to IFN-γ dissolved in sterile ddH₂O to induce a pro-inflammatory phenotype.

Indirect co-culture experiments were performed using Transwell inserts (6-well plates, 14111, LABSELECT, China), with ADSCs or Rg1-pretreated ADSCs seeded in the upper chamber at ~ 80% confluence and RAW264.7 or NCM460 cells in the lower chamber, depending on the experiment. RNA and proteins were extracted exclusively from cells in the lower chamber. Experimental groups were as follows: CON, untreated RAW264.7 or NCM460; MOD, LPS-treated RAW264.7 or NCM460; ADSCs, LPS-treated RAW264.7 or NCM460 co-cultured with ADSCs; Rg1, LPS-treated RAW264.7 or NCM460 co-cultured with Rg1-pretreated ADSCs; and GW4869, ADSCs pretreated with 20 µM GW4869 for 24 h prior to co-culture. All co-culture conditions and grouping strategies were consistent with those described in the Results section.

Flow cytometry analysis

ADSCs and Rg1-pretreated ADSCs were harvested using 0.25% Trypsin-EDTA with phenol red (BC-CE-005, Biochannel, China). For immunogenicity assessment, ADSCs were pretreated with IFN-γ [28] (20 ng/mL, HYP7025, MedChemExpress, USA) dissolved in sterile ddH₂O for 72 h before staining with HLA-G (335909), HLA-DR (327005), and HLA-DQ (318105). CD73 (344003) and CD59 (989302) were directly stained without pretreatment (all BioLegend, USA). Flow cytometry was performed on a Beckman Coulter FC500. For macrophage polarization, splenic single-cell suspensions were stained with F4/80-PE-EF610 (61-4801-82), CD11b-FITC (11-0112-82), and CD86-PE (12-0862-82) to identify M1 macrophages, and with CD206-APC (17-2061-82) after fixation (all BioLegend, USA) and permeabilization (FMS-FP0100, Fumaisi Bio, China) to assess M2 macrophages. Fc receptors were blocked using FcR Blocking Reagent (130-092-575, Miltenyi, Germany). RAW264.7 cells were stained with CD86-PE for M1 and CD206-APC for M2 identification following the same protocol. For Th17 cell analysis, splenic lymphocytes were resuspended in 1 mL of complete RPMI-1640 medium (Servicebio, G4535, China) and restimulated with 2 µL of Cell Stimulation + Transport Inhibitor Cocktail (00-4975-93, eBioscience, USA) for 6 h at 37 °C. Cells were then stained with Fixable Viability Dye eFluor™ 780 (65-0865-14, eBioscience, USA), CD4-FITC (11-0041-82, eBioscience, USA), and intracellular IL-17 A-APC (17-7177-81, eBioscience, USA) using the same fixation/permeabilization kit. Data were acquired on a BD FACS Canto II and analyzed using FlowJo V10.

Cell cycle assay

Cell cycle analysis was performed using the Cell Cycle Staining Kit (E-CK-A352, Elabscience, China). Cells were fixed in 70% ethanol at 4 °C overnight, washed with PBS, and stained with PI/RNase A solution for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. Cell cycle distribution was analyzed by Beckman Coulter FC500 flow cytometry.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) kit (C0037, Beyotime Biotechnology, China). ADSCs, NCM460, and RAW264.7 cells were seeded into 96-well plates and exposed to varying concentrations of LPS (L2880, Sigma, USA) or ginsenoside Rg1 for 24 h. After treatment, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 1–2 h. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured to evaluate cell viability.

Targeted metabolomic analysis

Cell samples were mixed with 200 µL of extraction solvent (methanol, acetonitrile, and water in a 2:2:1 ratio, v/v/v) pre-chilled at − 20 °C, followed by addition of 2 µL isotopic internal standard mixture. The mixture was sonicated on ice at 4 °C for 5 min, then incubated at − 80 °C for 30 min to precipitate proteins. After centrifugation at 12,000 × g and 4 °C for 15 min, 200 µL of the clear supernatant was collected into a 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube and centrifuged again under identical conditions. The final supernatant (200 µL) was transferred to glass autosampler vials with inserts for LC–MS analysis. Chromatographic separation and mass detection were performed using an Agilent 1290 liquid chromatography system coupled to an Agilent 6545 Q-TOF mass spectrometer. Raw mass spectrometry data were converted to MzXML format and processed using XCMS software for metabolite identification and data analysis. The data supporting the findings of this study have been deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under the BioProject accession number PRJNA1291727.

High-Throughput sequencing analysis

For RNA sequencing, libraries were constructed and sequenced using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Bioinformatics analyses—including pathway enrichment, volcano plot generation, and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA)—were conducted in R (https://www.r-project.org) using tools from the OmicStudio platform (http://www.omicstudio.cn/tool). For microRNAs (miRNAs) sequencing, total RNA was isolated from exosomes using the Total Exosome RNA and Protein Isolation Kit (Invitrogen, USA). The concentration and integrity of small RNAs were evaluated by LC Bio Technology Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China), which also performed small RNA library preparation and sequencing.

UC target identification and enrichment analysis

UC-related targets were obtained from GeneCards, the Therapeutic Target Database (TTD), Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM), and PharmGKB using the keyword “ulcerative colitis.” GeneCards entries with a relevance score ≥ 3.1982989 (top 25%) were included. Targets were limited to Homo sapiens, standardized via the UniProt database, and duplicates were removed. Gene expression datasets GSE87466 and GSE206285 were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the “limma” package in R. UC-related hub genes were defined as the intersection of DEGs and disease targets. A Venn diagram was generated with the “VennDiagram” package. KEGG pathway enrichment was conducted using “clusterProfiler” and visualized with “ggplot2.”

Characterization of ADSCs

ADSCs at passages 3–5 were digested with 0.25% trypsin (BC-CE-002, Biochannel; free of EDTA and phenol red), centrifuged, and resuspended in cold PBS containing 0.1% BSA (BSA; A8020, Solarbio, China). After two washes (450 × g, 5 min), 1 × 10⁶ cells were stained with fluorophore-conjugated antibodies—CD73-APC (E-AB-F1242E, Elabscience, Wuhan, China), CD90-PE (E-AB-F1167D, Elabscience, Wuhan, China), CD105-FITC (323203, BioLegend, USA), CD34-PE (E-AB-F1143D, Elabscience, Wuhan, China), and CD59-APC (304712, BioLegend, USA)—in 100 µL buffer for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark. Cells were then centrifuged (450 × g, 10 min), washed, and analyzed on a Beckman Coulter FC500 flow cytometer. Data were processed using FlowJo V10. To assess differentiation potential, passage 2 ADSCs (2 × 10⁴ cells/well) were cultured in 6-well plates and induced with commercial kits (HUXMD-90021, HUXMD-90041, HUXMD-90031, OriCell, China) for osteogenic, adipogenic, and chondrogenic differentiation. Media were changed every 2–3 days. After 21 days, cells were stained with Oil Red O, Alcian Blue, or Alizarin Red, and observed microscopically (Leica, Germany).

Extraction and identification of exosome

Third-passage ADSCs were cultured in DMEM with 10% exosome-depleted FBS (Life-iLab, China) until ~ 80% confluence. After 24 h incubation, the supernatant was collected and centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min, 2000 × g for 10 min, and 10,000 × g for 30 min. Exosomes were isolated by ultracentrifugation at 100,000 × g for 70 min, repeated twice (Beckman XPN-100, USA), and resuspended in PBS. Exosome morphology was observed by TEM (Hitachi, Japan), and particle size was analyzed using ZetaView (Particle Metrix, Germany). CD63 (sc-365604, Santa Cruz, USA), TSG101 (sc-101254, Santa Cruz, USA), and Calnexin (sc- 46669, Santa Cruz, USA) expression was detected by western blotting.

Evaluation of exosome uptake

Exosomes were labeled with PKH26 (C3635S, Beyotime, China) and incubated with RAW264.7 cells for 24 h. Simultaneously, the cytoskeleton was stained with phalloidin (C2207S, Beyotime, China). After fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde and nuclear counterstaining with DAPI (P0131, Beyotime, China), samples were examined under a fluorescence microscope (OlympusBX43, Japan).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Cell culture supernatants and mouse serum from each group were collected. The levels of nitric oxide (NO; Solarbio LIFE SCIENCES, BC1475), TGF-β1, and IL-6 were quantified using ELISA kits (MULTISCIENCES, EK981 and EK206) according to the manufacturers’ instructions.

Analysis of cellular metabolites

The cell culture supernatants were collected, and the levels of pyruvate kinase (S0303S), pyruvate (S0299S), and L-Lactate(S0208S) were measured using corresponding commercial assay kits (Beyotime, China), following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Scratch assay

Cells were seeded in 12-well plates containing DMEM with 10% FBS and cultured at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator until a confluent monolayer formed. A straight scratch was made using a sterile 200 µL pipette tip held vertically. After removing debris by two PBS washes, serum-free medium was added to minimize proliferation. Plates were then incubated under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂), and wound closure was monitored over 24 h. Images were captured at 0 and 24 h to assess cell migration.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

Colonic tissues were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin (Jinyibai Biotech, China) for 24 h. Samples were then dehydrated through a graded ethanol series and embedded in paraffin. Sections were cut at a thickness of 5 μm. For staining, hematoxylin (Jinyibai Biotech, China) was used to label nuclei, followed by eosin (Jinyibai Biotech, China) for cytoplasm and extracellular matrix staining. Morphological evaluation and pathological assessment were performed using a digital pathology scanner (Leica Aperio Versa, Germany). The severity of colitis was histopathologically assessed in a blinded manner by two independent researchers using the Disease Activity Index (DAI) scoring system. Three parameters were evaluated: inflammatory cell infiltration (0–3: none, mild, moderate, severe), crypt architecture damage (0–3: normal, basal one-third damaged, basal two-thirds damaged, entire crypt lost), and mucosal ulceration/erosion (0–3: none, mild, moderate, severe). Scores for each parameter were summed to obtain a total histological score ranging from 0 to 9.

Alcian blue–periodic acid–schiff (AB–PAS) staining

To assess goblet cells and mucin content in colonic tissue, sections were stained using the AB-PAS Staining Kit (G1285, Solarbio, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Stained slides were scanned using a digital pathology scanner (Leica Aperio Versa, Germany), and the mucin-positive area was quantitatively analyzed using Image-Pro Plus software (Media Cybernetics, USA). All procedures were performed by Jinyibai Biotech Co., Ltd. (China).

Immunofluorescence staining

Paraffin-embedded tissue sections and cell-climbing slides were subjected to antigen retrieval and blocking. Samples were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1, 1:200, 28L12K31), intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1, 1:200, 33M53E27) (both from Epizyme, China), and iNOS (22226-1-AP, Proteintech, China, 1:300), Arginase-1 (16001-1-AP, Proteintech, China, 1:300), LGR5 (30007-1-AP, Proteintech, China, 1:300), F4/80 (28463-1-AP, Proteintech, China, 1:300), and GLUT1 (21829-1-AP, Proteintech, China, 1:300). After PBS washing, DyLight 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:400, ab96899, Abcam, UK) and Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated donkey anti-mouse IgG (1:1000, R37115, Invitrogen, USA) were applied for 1 h at room temperature. Slides were mounted and imaged using a BX43 fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was isolated using the FastPure Cell/Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit V2 (Vazyme, China), and reverse transcription was carried out with the HiScript III RT SuperMix (+ gDNA wiper) (Vazyme, China). Primers (see Table 1) were designed and synthesized by GENEray Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). qPCR was performed on an Applied Biosystems 7500 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, China). Gene expression levels were normalized to GAPDH and analyzed using the 2^−ΔΔCt method.

Western blot

Total proteins were isolated from RAW264.7 cells, NCM460 cells, and mouse colonic tissue using RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. After centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, protein concentrations in the supernatants were determined using a BCA assay kit (ZJ101, Epizyme Biotech, China). Protein extracts were adjusted to 2 µg/µL and denatured by heating at 100 °C for 10 min. Equal amounts of protein (20 µg per lane) were loaded onto SDS-PAGE gels, separated electrophoretically, and transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked for 1 h and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies targeting RAS (Abcam, EPR3255), RAF (Proteintech, 84994-4-RR), MEK (Proteintech, 11049-1-AP), phosphorylated MEK (Abcam, EPR3338), ERK (Proteintech, 66192-1-Ig), phosphorylated ERK (Proteintech, 28733-1-AP), HIF-1α (Abcam, EPR16897), and β-actin (Proteintech, 66009-1-Ig). After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, and protein signals were visualized and quantified using the ChemiDoc XRS + system (Bio-Rad, USA).

Statistical analysis

Statistical data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism. Two-tailed Student’s t-test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to determine statistical significance, with a P value less than 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Rg1 pretreatment modulates the immunophenotype of ADSCs

The immunomodulatory function of MSC is closely associated with their therapeutic efficacy [31]. Rg1 was used to pretreat ADSCs with the aim of improving the therapeutic efficacy of MSC-based interventions. Surface marker and differentiation assays confirmed that ADSCs met MSC criteria and maintained trilineage potential (Figure S1A-H). Rg1 pretreatment preserved the spindle-shaped MSC morphology of ADSCs (Fig. 1A). Cell proliferation analysis using the CCK-8 assay identified 30 µM as the optimal concentration of Rg1, which significantly promoted ADSC proliferation compared with untreated cells (Fig. 1B). Rg1 treatment upregulated stemness-related genes, with Nanog and OCT4 showing significant increases and Sox2 exhibiting an upward trend (Fig. 1C). Flow cytometry demonstrated a significant accumulation of ADSCs in the G2 phase after Rg1 treatment (Fig. 1D, E).

Rg1 pretreatment enhanced the immunomodulatory properties of ADSCs. Given that VCAM-1 and ICAM-1 are key adhesion molecules involved in immune cell recognition and recruitment [32], immunofluorescence staining confirmed that Rg1 treatment significantly downregulated their expression in ADSCs (Fig. 1F–I). Upon IFN-γ challenge, Rg1-pretreated ADSCs displayed a reduced immunogenic phenotype, with downregulation of HLA-DQ and HLA-DR and upregulation of immunosuppressive markers such as HLA-G, CD59, and CD73 (Fig. 1J, K). These results suggest that Rg1 reinforces the low-immunogenic and immune-privileged phenotype of ADSCs.

Rg1 pretreatment of ADSCs suppresses macrophage glycolysis and promotes mucosal repair via the RAS signaling pathway

To assess the impact of Rg1-pretreated ADSCs on macrophage immunometabolism and mucosal healing, an in vitro co-culture system was established using LPS-stimulated RAW 264.7 macrophages and human colonic epithelial NCM460 cells in the presence of ADSCs (Figure S2B). NO (Fig. 2A) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) (Figure S2C) levels in the supernatants were measured to optimize the LPS concentration. RT-qPCR analysis demonstrated dynamic changes in glycolytic gene expression after LPS stimulation, with significant upregulation of GLUT1, LDHA, and PKM2 at 24 h, in agreement with previous studies [33]. Compared to untreated ADSCs, Rg1-pretreated ADSCs exhibited enhanced regulatory effects on these genes (Fig. 2B–D). Glycolytic metabolites such as lactate, pyruvate, and pyruvate kinase were markedly decreased in macrophage supernatants following co-culture with ADSCs, with a more pronounced reduction observed when ADSCs were pretreated with Rg1 (Fig. 2G, J, M). Flow cytometry revealed that ADSCs significantly suppressed macrophage CD86 expression, with greater inhibition after Rg1 pretreatment (Fig. 2N, O). Similarly, immunofluorescence staining indicated that Rg1-pretreated ADSCs more effectively suppressed M1 and promoted M2 polarization in RAW 264.7 cells (Fig. 2P–S).

Targeted energy metabolism profiling revealed significant upregulation of glycolytic metabolites in the model group (Fig. 3A, B), with KEGG pathway enrichment analysis implicating the RAS signaling pathway (Fig. 3H). Quantitative analysis showed that, compared with the model group, ADSC treatment significantly increased ADP (Fig. 3C) and fructose-6-phosphate (F6P) (Fig. 3E), while markedly decreasing L-Lactate (Fig. 3G), glucose-6-phosphate (G6P) (Fig. 3F), and ATP (Fig. 3D), indicating suppressed glycolytic flux. Western blot analysis demonstrated that LPS-induced phosphorylation activated the RAS pathway, whereas ADSC co-culture substantially reduced RAS, RAF, p-MEK, p-ERK, and HIF-1α levels, with Rg1 pretreatment further enhancing this inhibitory effect on HIF-1α (Fig. 3I–O). Co-culture of Rg1-pretreated ADSCs with NCM460 cells accelerated wound closure in scratch assays (Fig. 2E, F) and increased expression of tight junction proteins Occludin and zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1) (Fig. 2H, I, K, L). Our findings support that activation of the RAS pathway may drive increased macrophage glycolysis. Compared to untreated ADSCs, Rg1-pretreated ADSCs more effectively inhibited glycolysis, promoted M2 polarization, and facilitated mucosal healing.

Treatment with Rg1-preconditioned ADSCs enhanced mucosal regeneration in mice with DSS-induced colitis

To assess the therapeutic efficacy of Rg1-pretreated ADSCs in colitis, the cells were intraperitoneally administered on days 3 and 6 after DSS induction (Fig. 4A). Rg1-pretreated ADSCs significantly enhanced recovery from DSS-induced colitis relative to untreated ADSCs, as supported by shorter colons, lower DAI scores, and milder body weight loss (Fig. 4B–F). H&E staining showed no evidence of tumor formation in major organs, indicating good biosafety of ADSC-based treatment (Figure S2D). Intestinal stem cells (ISCs) are essential for epithelial homeostasis and mucosal regeneration via constant self-renewal and multilineage differentiation [34]. Following ADSC administration, LGR5 expression in colonic crypts was markedly elevated [35], consistent with ISC activation (Fig. 4G, I). Rg1-pretreated ADSCs further enhanced the expression of proliferation- and regeneration-related genes (Pcna, Mcm5, Mcm6, Ly6a, Lama3) in colonic epithelial cells (Fig. 4L–P). AB-PAS staining revealed increased goblet cell numbers and mucin secretion in the colonic mucosa following ADSC transplantation (Fig. 4H, J, K). Taken together, these findings highlight the superior therapeutic efficacy of Rg1-preconditioned ADSCs in promoting mucosal regeneration in DSS-induced colitis.

Treatment with Rg1-preconditioned ADSCs modulate macrophage metabolism and polarization to mitigate immune imbalance and epithelial damage in experimental colitis

Evidence suggests that increased glycolytic activity in macrophages facilitates their polarization toward the M1 phenotype and contributes to exacerbated local inflammation [36]. Flow cytometry of splenic immune cells showed that ADSC treatment reduced M1 macrophage and Th17 cell infiltration while increasing M2 macrophages, with more pronounced effects observed in the Rg1-pretreated ADSC group (Fig. 5A–D, H, I). Macrophage infiltration in the colonic submucosa was further examined, revealing a decrease in M1 macrophage infiltration following ADSC treatment (Fig. 5E, F). Compared with untreated ADSCs, Rg1-pretreated ADSCs significantly reduced serum IL-6 and more substantially increased Transforming Growth Factor Beta 1 (TGF-β1) levels (Fig. 5G, M). DSS-induced colitis mice exhibited elevated serum levels of glycolysis-related metabolites—L-Lactate, pyruvate, and pyruvate kinase—which were substantially decreased by Rg1-pretreated ADSC treatment (Fig. 5J–L). Correspondingly, immunofluorescence and western blot analyses demonstrated that the upregulation of glycolytic enzymes LDHA, PKM2, and GLUT1 in inflamed colonic mucosa was significantly inhibited by ADSCs, with the greatest suppression observed after Rg1 pretreatment (Fig. 6L–P). As expected, the suppression of glycolysis was accompanied by a restoration of epithelial barrier markers, including Occludin and ZO-1, whose expression was significantly restored in the Rg1 group (Fig. 5N–Q). Collectively, these results indicate that Rg1-preconditioned ADSCs more effectively modulate macrophage polarization via glycolysis suppression, thereby alleviating mucosal injury in UC.

Rg1-pretreated ADSC downregulates RAS signaling to ameliorate inflammation and barrier dysfunction in experimental colitis

To explore the mechanisms underlying the therapeutic effects of ADSCs in UC, we performed multi-database bioinformatic analyses to identify key molecular targets associated with disease pathogenesis (Figure S2A). KEGG analysis revealed that these targets were primarily enriched in the RAS, MAPK, and HIF-1α signaling pathways (Fig. 6A). Consistently, GSEA of colonic tissues from UC patients showed significant upregulation of the RAS, MAPK, and HIF-1α pathways (Fig. 6B–D). Phosphorylation-driven activation of the RAS signaling cascade is crucial for modulating inflammation, fibrosis, and epithelial barrier function, thereby shaping immune homeostasis in IBD [37]. Protein expression analysis revealed that phosphorylation of key components in the RAS pathway, including RAF, MEK, ERK, and HIF-1α, was suppressed following ADSC therapy (Fig. 6E–J), and this inhibitory effect was more pronounced in the Rg1-pretreated ADSC group. The three RAS isoforms—H-, N-, and K-RAS—collectively reflect the activation of the RAS signaling pathway [38, 39]. Although different isoforms could theoretically produce multiple bands in immunoblots, our antibody reference images and subsequent experiments showed no such bands, suggesting that the double bands in Fig. 6E are likely due to protein degradation. Overall, RAS upregulation still supports pathway activation. RAS RT-qPCR results validated reduced mRNA expression of relevant pathway genes (Figure S2E–H). In colonic tissues, glycolysis-related proteins LDHA and PKM2 were markedly downregulated in the Rg1 group (Fig. 6K–N). Consistently, immunofluorescence staining showed lower GLUT1 expression in the submucosa of the Rg1-treated group (Fig. 6O, P). These findings highlight the pivotal role of RAS signaling in ulcerative colitis and suggest that ADSCs exert immunomodulatory effects partly through RAS/MAPK/HIF-1α suppression, which is further enhanced by Rg1 pretreatment.

ADSC therapy relies on exosome-mediated mechanisms to exert therapeutic effects

Exosomes are functional nanoscale vesicles that mediate the therapeutic effects of MSCs [40]. To determine whether exosomes mediate the therapeutic effects of ADSCs, we first characterized ADSC-derived exosomes in vitro. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) revealed that the exosomes exhibited a typical round, hollow morphology and ranged in size from 40 to 150 nm (Fig. 7A, C). Western blotting confirmed the enrichment of CD63 and TSG101, while Calnexin was not detected (Fig. 7B). To track exosome uptake, ADSC-exosomes were labeled with the fluorescent dye PKH67 and incubated with RAW264.7 macrophages. Fluorescence microscopy revealed efficient internalization of ADSC-exosomes by macrophages (Fig. 7J). ADSCs were pretreated with the exosome inhibitor GW4869 and co-cultured with RAW264.7 and NCM460 cells in a transwell system to assess the contribution of exosomes to therapeutic efficacy. Blocking exosome secretion with GW4869 attenuated the immunomodulatory capacity of ADSCs, leading to M1-skewed polarization in RAW macrophages and increased glycolytic activity (Fig. 7D–H, M, Q, R). Consistently, inhibition of exosome release upregulated the expression of glycolytic enzymes and transporters, including LDHA, PKM2, and GLUT1 (Figs. 7S–U and 8E–H). In LPS-stimulated NCM460 cells, exosome blockade abrogated the protective effects of ADSCs, as indicated by disrupted tight junction protein expression (Fig. 7I, L, K, N) and elevated Tumor Necrosis Factor-alpha (TNF-α) levels (Fig. 7O, P). These results confirm that exosome-mediated signaling is indispensable for ADSC-induced immunoregulation and epithelial protection.

ADSC-derived exosomal MiRNA inhibits RAS signaling activation by directly targeting RASSF1

Exosomes transfer miRNAs to recipient cells and regulate gene expression [41]. ADSC-treated RAW264.7 cells showed increased expression of several miRNAs compared with LPS-stimulated controls, among which miR-574-3p was slightly upregulated and also enriched in ADSC-derived exosomes (Fig. 8A). Pathway and target prediction analyses suggested that miR-574-3p may inhibit the RAS signaling pathway by binding to the 3′UTR of RASSF1, which encodes a Ras-association (RA) domain–containing protein that serves as a critical regulator of RAS signaling activity (Fig. 8B, C) [42]. Functionally, inhibition of exosome release restored RAS activity in ADSC-treated cells, confirming the requirement of exosomes for effective pathway suppression (Fig. 8D, I–M). Together, these findings demonstrate that miR-574-3p in ADSC-exosomes suppresses RAS signaling by directly targeting the 3′ UTR of RASSF1 mRNA.

Discussion

While MSCs have shown therapeutic promise in IBD, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. In this study, we found that Rg1 pretreatment enhanced the ability of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) to alleviate intestinal inflammation by suppressing glycolysis in M1 macrophages through inhibition of the RAS–RAF–MEK–ERK signaling pathway. Furthermore, this regulatory effect of MSCs may be associated with exosome-mediated miRNA communication.

Despite their promise, MSC therapies face challenges such as senescence during culture and immune rejection risks in allogeneic transplantation [43, 44]. Among ginsenosides, Rb1 and Rg1 exhibit superior anti-inflammatory effects [45], and Rg1 has been reported to enhance MSC proliferation, differentiation, and survival, thereby potentially overcoming these limitations [46, 47]. We pretreated ADSCs with Rg1 to enhance their viability and regenerative ability after intraperitoneal injection and to reduce immunogenicity. The optimal Rg1 pretreatment concentration was identified based on ADSC proliferation. At this dose, Rg1 upregulated stemness-related genes and increased G1 phase arrest, thereby supporting the maintenance of self-renewal and multipotency. ADSCs exhibit strong immunoregulatory capabilities in response to inflammatory stimuli, making them valuable candidates for treating tissue damage and persistent inflammatory disorders [48]. Rg1 also reduced immunogenic markers on MSCs and impaired their ability to recruit immune cells, suggesting a role in enhancing transplantation compatibility.

In UC, macrophages are heavily recruited to the gut microenvironment, and their persistent overactivation has been closely linked to disease development [49]. These activated macrophages promote Th17 cell differentiation through cytokine signaling, while Th17-derived cytokines further reinforce M1 polarization, forming a pro-inflammatory loop that exacerbates disease progression [50]. The main approach in IBD immunotherapy involves suppressing M1 macrophages and enhancing M2 macrophage populations to decrease the harmful effects of M1-secreted inflammatory mediators in the intestinal tract [51]. Growing evidence supports that MSC-mediated tissue regeneration is mainly driven by their immunoregulatory properties. Programmed immunomodulation via M1/M2 macrophage polarization effectively promotes bone marrow mesenchymal stromal cell (BMSC)-mediated craniofacial bone regeneration [52]. A similar approach using 3D-Cad/IL6-MSCs has shown improved outcomes in systemic sclerosis by enhancing MSC immunoregulatory and antifibrotic capacities [53]. Beyond immunomodulation, Cu²⁺-induced metabolic reprogramming enhances BMSC osteogenesis by reshaping the local metabolic niche, offering a new strategy for alveolar bone repair [54]. In this study, co-culture analysis demonstrated a marked decrease in the M1/M2 macrophage ratio, consistent with our previous observations [55]. In DSS-induced colitis mice, M1-polarized macrophages accumulated in the spleen and submucosa, promoting Th17 differentiation, whereas MSC administration reversed this inflammatory phenotype and promoted mucosal regeneration. Collectively, these findings are consistent with prior studies and extend our understanding by revealing that Rg1-pretreated MSCs further potentiate this immunoregulatory effect.

Distinct metabolic programs govern macrophage fate, with glycolysis serving as the dominant pathway during M1 polarization [56]. The pro-inflammatory function of M1 macrophages is largely sustained by glycolytic metabolism, which facilitates their activation and proinflammatory output [57]. Consistently, TE-11 has been shown to ameliorate CD-like colitis by inhibiting M1 polarization and its associated metabolic reprogramming [58]. Likewise, a chitosan/gelatin-based patch loaded with 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG) locally suppresses macrophage glycolysis and pro-inflammatory activation, thereby enhancing MSC survival and cardiac repair while minimizing systemic toxicity [59].

These findings were further supported by our metabolic profiling, which showed decreased glycolytic intermediates following MSC treatment, highlighting immunometabolic reprogramming as a key therapeutic mechanism. Notably, this effect was further enhanced by Rg1 pretreatment.

The RAS/ERK signaling cascade has emerged as a key modulator of macrophage metabolic reprogramming, with strong implications for glycolytic control in inflammatory contexts [60–62]. Upon activation, RAS triggers a phosphorylation cascade that sequentially activates downstream signaling proteins, initiating complex intracellular responses [63]. Ras and MAPK pathway proteins were found to be upregulated in UC tissues, as shown by RNA-seq and bioinformatic analyses, confirming our earlier predictions [64]. Local hypoxia in IBD activates the RAS/ERK signaling cascade via stabilization of HIF-1α, while RAS-mediated signaling further enhances HIF-1α expression, forming a self-reinforcing pro-inflammatory loop [65–67]. Through the regulation of glycolytic enzymes including GLUT1, PKM2, and LDHA, HIF-1α enhances glycolytic flux; particularly, upregulation of LDHA leads to lactate buildup and drives pro-inflammatory macrophage polarization [68, 69]. Although MSCs have shown therapeutic potential in UC, their role in regulating macrophage glycolysis to relieve mucosal inflammation has not yet been clearly defined. Therefore, we further experimentally confirmed that MSCs suppress M1 macrophage glycolysis and promote M2 polarization through the RAS/ERK signaling pathway, thereby alleviating mucosal inflammation in UC.

Growing evidence suggests that stem cells exert their therapeutic effects mainly through paracrine mechanisms rather than direct proliferation [70–72]. Exosomes are key mediators in this process, transferring bioactive molecules from donor to recipient cells to regulate cellular functions [73]. Among these, MSC-EXOs have been shown to alleviate intestinal inflammation and epithelial injury in IBD models by upregulating O-GlcNAcylation, thereby reversing epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) [40]. In our study, treatment with MSC-derived exosomes led to downregulation of the RAS signaling pathway. The antagonistic effect observed after exosome inhibition suggests that exosomes represent a central mechanism underlying the therapeutic efficacy of MSCs in UC.

Microscopic observations further confirmed that M1 macrophages had taken up the exosomes. miRNAs are non-coding RNAs approximately 22 nucleotides in length that regulate gene expression post-transcriptionally by binding to target mRNAs [74]. Through the delivery of regulatory miRNAs, MSC-EXOs can attenuate the progression of diabetic kidney disease (DKD) and reduce cellular apoptosis [75]. Exosomal miR-16-5p derived from human umbilical cord MSCs alleviates inflammation by concurrently inhibiting M1 macrophage polarization and Th1 cell differentiation [76]. Similarly, human umbilical cord Mesenchymal Stromal Cell (hucMSC)-derived exosomal miR-129-5p alleviates IBD by suppressing ACSL4-mediated ferroptosis in intestinal epithelial cells [77]. miRNA sequencing identified a subset of microRNAs that are highly expressed in both ADSC-derived exosomes and macrophages. Subsequent target prediction analyses suggested that these miRNAs may regulate RASSF1, an important downstream modulator of the RAS signaling pathway [78]. Therefore, targeting immune dysfunction in IBD patients with miR-574-3p–rich ADSC exosomes could offer a promising therapeutic approach.

This study reveals a potential mechanism by which MSCs regulate macrophage polarization via exosome-driven metabolic pathways, with improved outcomes following Rg1 pretreatment. However, the exclusive use of the DSS colitis model limits the generalizability of these findings. Future studies utilizing more complex models—such as transgenic or macrophage-depleted mice—are warranted. Moreover, although miR-574-3p was bioinformatically predicted to target RASSF1, a key downstream regulator of RAS signaling, experimental validation and clinical relevance remain to be established (Fig. 10), representing an important direction for future research.

Conclusion

This study elucidates the immunoregulatory mechanism by which MSCs alleviate ulcerative colitis (UC) via suppression of macrophage glycolysis. Rg1 preconditioning potentiated this therapeutic effect, which may be mediated by exosomal transfer of miRNAs, possibly miR-574-3p, acting on the RAS signaling pathway. These insights offer a promising strategy for targeted immunotherapy in UC.
