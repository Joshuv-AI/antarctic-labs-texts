Risk factors for frailty in older adults with cancer: a systematic review
Authors: Feng Shubing, Wang Pengpeng, Huang Hongxia, Lin Zhenhong, Liu Yuanhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFrailty and cancer interact bidirectionally and synergistically, exacerbating multiple adverse clinical outcomes in elderly patients. Accelerated global population aging has led to a continuous rise in the incidence of cancer among older adults, who inherently represent a high-risk population for frailty. Antitumor therapies further elevate the risk of frailty and impair long-term prognosis. Nevertheless, existing systematic reviews lack stratified integration of cross-sectional correlational evidence and longitudinal predictive evidence of frailty risk factors in older cancer survivors, and comprehensive categorized summaries of relevant variables remain scarce.MethodsWe electronically searched PubMed, Embase and Web of Science from database inception to July 2025 using a combination of subject headings and free-text terms. Reference tracing of included articles was performed to supplement potential eligible studies. Three researchers independently completed literature screening per predefined eligibility criteria, while two investigators extracted data and conducted mutual cross-verification.ResultsA total of 30 studies published between 2014 and 2025 were incorporated, adopting multiple validated frailty assessment scales. Among them, 26 cross-sectional studies only identified coexistent correlational factors of frailty, and 4 longitudinal cohort studies screened independent predictors for the onset and progression of frailty. The influencing factors were divided into six categories: disease-related, physiological, psychological, lifestyle, sociodemographic and treatment-related factors.ConclusionFrailty in older adults with cancer is affected by multi-dimensional, complex determinants. Targeted screening and intervention strategies can be formulated upon identification of these factors to reduce frailty incidence and improve clinical prognosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12877-026-08260-6.

Introduction

The World Health Organization defines people aged 60 years and above as older adults [1]. Epidemiological projections indicate that approximately 14 million new cancer cases will occur in older populations globally by 2035, accounting for nearly 60% of all new malignant tumor diagnoses, imposing substantial burdens on geriatric comprehensive healthcare systems [2]. Frailty is a characteristic geriatric syndrome defined by Fried’s phenotype criteria: older adults presenting with three or more of the following five manifestations (unintentional weight loss, fatigue, reduced grip strength, slowed gait speed and low physical activity) are classified as frail [3]. Physiological degenerative aging continuously impairs organic reserve capacity, leading to markedly elevated frailty risk among the elderly [4].

Cancer and frailty share bidirectional, intertwined pathophysiological mechanisms that form a vicious cycle and worsen clinical adverse events. Current research subdivides frailty into physical, cognitive and psychosocial subtypes, which may exist independently or simultaneously in older populations [5]. Malignant tumors chronically deplete bodily energy reserves, while surgical, chemotherapeutic, radiotherapeutic, targeted and immunotherapeutic treatments induce severe physiological stress that further exhausts the multi-system compensatory capacity of aging bodies, ultimately increasing frailty susceptibility. Conversely, frailty distorts the full course of oncological management: frail older patients demonstrate poorer tolerance to antitumor therapy and attenuated therapeutic responses, accompanied by higher risks of treatment-related adverse events and long-term unfavorable prognoses [6].

Numerous primary studies have separately analyzed various correlates of frailty among older cancer patients, yet existing reviews fail to stratify cross-sectional coexistence data and longitudinal frailty predictive data, lacking systematic categorized integration of relevant variables. Given the vicious bidirectional interplay between cancer and frailty, the present systematic review stratified coexistent correlational factors (from cross-sectional studies) and independent frailty predictors (from cohort studies), to provide evidence-based support for early high-risk screening and individualized intervention development in elderly oncology populations.

Methods

Search strategy

Electronic retrievals were conducted across PubMed, Embase and Web of Science from database inception to July 20, 2025. This systematic review was performed following a pre-registered protocol and reported in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 statement (Registration ID: CRD420261381642). Search terms integrated controlled vocabulary and free-text keywords covering aging, cancer and frailty determinants; full detailed search strategies for each database are presented in Supplementary Table S1.

Eligibility criteria

All retrieved bibliographic records were imported into Zotero for duplicate removal and document management. To guarantee consistency and methodological rigor during screening, a dual independent review rule was implemented: two researchers separately conducted title/abstract screening and full-text evaluation. Multiple investigators divided the workload for preliminary abstract screening to cover all potentially eligible records. When disagreements emerged in either screening stage, discussions involving the senior corresponding author were held to reach a unified decision.

We defined clear inclusion and exclusion criteria across five dimensions: study population, study design, research setting, frailty assessment tools, and outcome indicators.

Inclusion criteria

Study population

Participants aged ≥ 60 years consistent with the WHO definition of older adults, with histopathologically confirmed malignancies meeting the WHO International Classification of Diseases for Oncology (ICD-O) diagnostic criteria. Both solid and hematological tumors were eligible, with no restrictions on TNM stage (early, locally advanced, metastatic disease). Participants receiving surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted therapy, immunotherapy, rehabilitation follow-up or palliative care, as well as cancer survivors were all included. Outpatient, inpatient and community-dwelling older adults were permitted; studies enrolling patients with ≥ 2 distinct primary malignancies were excluded.

Study design

Cross-sectional observational studies analyzing coexistent frailty correlates, and original longitudinal cohort studies identifying independent predictors of frailty onset and progression were eligible. Systematic reviews, narrative reviews, expert commentaries, case reports, conference abstracts, editorial notes, animal and in vitro laboratory experiments were excluded.

Clinical setting

Studies conducted in hospital inpatient wards, outpatient clinics and community follow-up cohorts of older cancer patients were acceptable.

Frailty assessment

Only manuscripts adopting fully validated standardized frailty scales were retained, including Fried Frailty Phenotype (FP), Groningen Frailty Indicator (GFI), Tilburg Frailty Indicator (TFI), Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS), FRAIL Scale, Edmonton Frail Scale (EFS), Frailty Index (FI), VES-13, Multidimensional Fatigue Inventory (MFI). Manuscripts relying solely on verbal descriptive definitions without quantitative frailty cut-off values were excluded.

Outcome indicators

Articles reporting complete correlation and multivariable regression data with extractable frailty association effect sizes were included. Manuscripts lacking retrievable quantitative data for frailty risk factors were eliminated.

Additional exclusion criteria

Duplicate publications, manuscripts with incomplete core statistical data, and records for which full-text could not be obtained after repeated correspondence with corresponding authors were excluded.

Literature screening and data extraction

The primary outcomes of this review quantified the magnitude of associations between candidate risk factors and frailty in older cancer patients, measured via adjusted odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI), correlation coefficients (r) and P-values. Narrative qualitative synthesis was performed on all aggregated study data. We systematically extracted baseline participant characteristics, frailty assessment instruments, frailty-related risk factors and quantitative effect sizes alongside core study conclusions. Risk factors with analogous connotations across different manuscripts were uniformly categorized into physiological, psychosocial, antitumor therapeutic and lifestyle subgroups. We summarized the volume of evidence supporting each determinant and synthesized the consistency of association magnitudes across eligible studies.

All citations were exported to Microsoft Excel for duplicate identification via titles and DOIs. Two researchers independently completed title/abstract screening and full-text eligibility evaluation to guarantee screening consistency and rigor. Any disagreements were first resolved via group discussion; if consensus could not be achieved, a third investigator was invited for joint adjudication. Complete exclusion rationales were documented for every record at both screening phases, with the full PRISMA screening workflow illustrated in Fig. 1.

Flowchart of literature screening

Upon completion of literature screening, two researchers independently performed standardized data extraction and cross-validated all entries to ensure data accuracy. Discrepancies arising during extraction were resolved by revisiting original manuscripts and team negotiation; unresolved disagreements were adjudicated by a third reviewer until consensus was reached. A standardized extraction template was built in Microsoft Excel, covering first author, publication year, population demographics, sample size, frailty assessment scale, candidate risk factors, adjusted OR (95%CI), correlation coefficient r and P-value. We pre-specified a protocol to contact corresponding authors to supplement raw data if key metrics were missing. After full-text inspection of all 30 included studies, all manuscripts fully reported required indicators with no missing core data, so no data request emails were dispatched to authors and no supplementary data replies were received. This review only included formally published journal articles in Chinese and English; grey literature including dissertations, conference abstracts, conference proceedings and unpublished datasets was excluded.

Risk of bias appraisal of included studies

The Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) 2020 Critical Appraisal Tools for evidence synthesis were adopted to conduct risk of bias assessment for all eligible manuscripts [7]. Separate dedicated checklists were matched per study design: the JBI 2020 Checklist for Analytical Cross-Sectional Studies for cross-sectional research, and the JBI 2020 Checklist for Cohort Studies for longitudinal research.

Cross-sectional manuscripts were evaluated across five dimensions: clear definition of study samples, detailed reporting of research settings, validity and reliability of exposure/outcome measurements, identification and control of confounding variables, and appropriateness of statistical models. Cohort studies were assessed based on baseline group comparability, uniform exposure measurement, validity of exposure and frailty endpoints, identification of confounders, confounder adjustment strategies, confirmation of frailty-free baseline enrollment, reproducibility of frailty testing, adequate follow-up duration, completeness and reporting of loss to follow-up, and statistical corrections for incomplete follow-up data.

Two researchers with specialized JBI training independently scored all manuscripts. Disagreements over appraisal results were first resolved by cross-referencing official checklist definitions and group discussion; if consensus could not be reached, a third investigator provided final arbitration. Each checklist item was rated as Yes, No, Unclear, or Not Applicable. Overall methodological quality was stratified by the proportion of “Yes” responses: ≥70% = high quality, 50%–69% = moderate quality, < 50% = low quality. Itemized JBI appraisal scores and overall quality grades for all included studies are summarized in Supplementary Tables S2 and S3.

Rules for data and effect size processing

Definition of eligible effect sizes

Three distinct types of statistical effect sizes were extracted in this review: adjusted odds ratios (OR), multivariate linear regression coefficients (β), and Pearson correlation coefficients (r). These metrics adopt incompatible statistical frameworks and measurement units and cannot be pooled to calculate a combined overall effect. In addition, nine divergent standardized frailty scales (FP, TFI, GFI, FRAIL, CFS, EFS, FI, VES-13, MFI) were utilized across included manuscripts, with inconsistent diagnostic cut-offs and scoring algorithms. Study populations also exhibited wide variability in cancer subtype and age stratification, alongside divergent antitumor treatment regimens, resulting in substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity that violates the homogeneity assumption required for meta-analysis pooling. Therefore, only stratified narrative qualitative synthesis was performed, without quantitative meta-analysis.

Priority rule for extracting adjusted effect estimates

A strict extraction hierarchy was followed: effect estimates derived from multivariable models controlling for multiple confounders were extracted as primary data. Crude unadjusted values were only retained if no multivariable regression outputs were available, with explicit annotations added in study characteristic tables.

Selection rule for multiple regression models within a single manuscript

If one article reported multiple regression specifications, the definitive primary analysis model confirmed by original authors with the most comprehensive covariate adjustment and robustness verification was prioritized. Exploratory subgroup models and univariate crude regression outputs were excluded, with a complete list of adjusted confounders annotated in supplementary tables for each selected primary model.

Selection rule for multiple follow-up time points in cohort research

For longitudinal cohort manuscripts with serial follow-up measurements, only analytical outputs corresponding to baseline frailty assessment were extracted. Long-term repeated follow-up data were described narratively only and excluded from core risk factor aggregation to eliminate temporal confounding bias that distorts conclusion stability.

Results

Literature search outcomes

Initial database searches retrieved a total of 2,853 unique citations. After duplicate removal via Excel (n = 1,076 excluded), 1,777 abstracts underwent preliminary screening, among which 1,729 irrelevant records were eliminated, leaving 48 full-text manuscripts for eligibility evaluation. Reference tracing of all retrieved articles was conducted, with zero additional eligible studies identified; grey literature was not searched. During full-text assessment, three articles were excluded due to inaccessible full manuscripts, and 15 manuscripts failed to meet inclusion criteria (2 with disqualified statistical frameworks, 13 inconsistent with population/design eligibility rules). Ultimately, 30 original studies published from 2014 to 2025 were incorporated, enrolling a combined total of 10,399 older cancer patients. The full PRISMA screening flow is illustrated in Fig. 1. Final included studies n = 30.

Baseline characteristics of included studies

Extracted effect sizes covered adjusted odds ratios (OR), regression coefficients β and correlation coefficients r. Data extraction prioritized multivariable-adjusted effect estimates; crude unadjusted values were only collected when no multivariable models were available. For manuscripts presenting multiple regression specifications, the author-verified fully adjusted primary model was extracted. For longitudinal cohorts with serial follow-ups, only baseline frailty assessment data were aggregated. Given the incompatible statistical units of the three distinct effect metrics, meta-analysis was not performed, and only narrative qualitative synthesis was implemented. The 30 eligible articles were published from 2014 to 2025 and adopted nine validated frailty assessment instruments: Fried Frailty Phenotype (FP), Tilburg Frailty Indicator (TFI), Groningen Frailty Indicator (GFI), FRAIL Scale, Multidimensional Fatigue Inventory (MFI), Edmonton Frail Scale (EFS), Frailty Index (FI), Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS) and VES-13.Despite the absence of a universal gold-standard frailty measurement tool in clinical practice [8], FP, GFI, TFI, CFS and VES-13 demonstrated robust reliability and validity for geriatric frailty screening across the included publications.

Stratified synthesis of all 30 manuscripts differentiated evidence from cross-sectional versus longitudinal designs: cross-sectional studies identified coexistent frailty correlates, while cohort research extracted independent predictors of frailty onset and progression. Determinants were categorized into six domains: sociodemographic, physiological, psychological, lifestyle, oncological and treatment-related factors.

Methodological quality assessment results

JBI 2020 risk of bias appraisal checklists were applied to all eligible manuscripts. Among the 30 included studies, 26 were cross-sectional and 4 longitudinal cohorts. Within cross-sectional articles, 18 were graded high quality and 8 moderate quality, with total scores ranging from 5 to 8. The predominant methodological limitation of cross-sectional research was incomplete identification and adjustment for confounding variables. All four cohort studies achieved high quality (score = 9/11), with the most common flaw being insufficient reporting of loss-to-follow-up and lack of statistical corrections for incomplete follow-up datasets.

Overall methodological quality ranged from moderate to high; longitudinal cohorts exhibited superior methodological rigor relative to cross-sectional studies (Tables 1, 2, 3 and 4).

Frailty-associated determinants

Six cross-sectional studies only conducted bivariate correlation analyses to identify frailty-related factors (Table 5). Through integrated evidence synthesis, these correlational factors were grouped into five major domains: sociodemographic and social support factors, physiological and clinical indicators, nutritional status indicators, psychological factors, and inflammatory biomarkers [9–14].Sociodemographic and social support-related correlates included advanced age, widowed or single marital status, solitary residence, low educational attainment, inadequate social participation and weak family cohesion.Clinical correlates consisted of elevated comorbidity burden, postoperative complications, reduced grip strength, fatigue, limited physical activity and poor self-rated health. Nutritional correlates covered malnutrition and elevated nutritional risk screening scores. Psychological factors including depression, anxiety, perceived stress and chronic psychological distress showed consistent correlations with frailty. In addition, inflammatory biomarkers such as the neutrophil-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) were also associated with frailty in partial publications.

Characteristics of cross-sectional correlation analysis studies

Twenty-four manuscripts implemented multivariable regression to screen independent frailty risk factors (Table 6). Advanced age represented the most frequently replicated independent predictor across all publications [15–27]. Additional sociodemographic independent factors included female sex, low educational attainment, unemployment and low household income [24, 27–32]. Oncological and treatment-related predictors encompassed higher tumor stage, prolonged disease duration, chemotherapy/endocrine treatment exposure and polypharmacy [16, 18, 26, 27, 33]. Lifestyle and physical function correlates included impaired activities of daily living, reduced grip strength, limited physical activity and delayed postoperative ambulation [16, 18, 22, 24, 31–36]. Nutritional risk factors comprised malnutrition, anemia, low albumin and abnormal BMI [20, 22, 24, 25, 27, 32, 36–38]. Psychological and social predictors included depression, anxiety, loneliness, inadequate family support and low health literacy [21, 23, 26–28, 34–36, 39]. Biomarkers such as D-dimer [38], hemoglobin, leukocyte and serum sodium levels [16, 36] also demonstrated independent associations with frailty in select cohorts.

Discussion

This systematic review synthesized data from 30 studies published 2014–2025, categorizing frailty determinants into six mutually interactive domains: oncological, physiological, psychological, lifestyle and sociodemographic, treatment-related factors. Systematic classification of these determinants provides structured clinical guidance to reduce frailty incidence and improve geriatric oncology outcomes (Table 7).

Oncological-related factors

This review identified multiple risk factors for frailty among older cancer patients, including AJCC tumor stage, comorbidity index, number of chronic comorbidities, cancer subtype, chronic pain, history of hepatic surgery, and number of regular medications taken.

Cancer subtype is associated with differential frailty prevalence. Distinct malignancies present unique clinical manifestations, disease trajectories and complication profiles, which directly alter patients’ physiological reserve and stress tolerance, resulting in subtype-specific frailty risks. For instance, multiple studies [16, 36] reported that patients with gastric, colorectal and liver cancers exhibit higher frailty prevalence compared with those suffering from other tumor types. This discrepancy may be attributed to tumor-specific metabolic disturbances and suppressed appetite, which trigger malnutrition and progressive loss of physiological reserve, accelerating frailty onset and progression.

Tumor stage and disease duration exert prominent impacts on frailty development. As disease advances, patients face heavier tumor burden, aggravated organ dysfunction, chronic pain and treatment-related toxicities, which collectively diminish bodily tolerance to physiological stress. As documented by Zheng et al. [27], patients with stage III and IV cancers have higher frailty rates than those diagnosed at stage I and II, demonstrating a close correlation between advanced malignancy and frailty.

High comorbidity burden and polypharmacy also constitute critical frailty risk factors for older cancer patients. Previous evidence indicated that an increased number of chronic illnesses impairs systemic function and activates chronic inflammation to deplete physiological reserves, while polypharmacy elevates frailty risk via drug-drug interactions, adverse effects and poor treatment adherence [18, 21, 23, 27]. Given the prevalent clinical phenotype of multimorbidity and polypharmacy in elderly oncology populations, comprehensive geriatric assessment and standardized medication management should be prioritized in clinical practice.

Notably, perioperative parameters correlate with frailty risk. Several studies found that delayed first ambulation postoperatively (≥ 48 h) is linked to elevated frailty susceptibility [33]. The underlying mechanism may involve elevated risks of postoperative pulmonary infection, insulin resistance, muscle loss and delayed functional recovery following prolonged bed rest, which collectively facilitate frailty development. Nevertheless, this association is only supported by cross-sectional evidence to date; longitudinal research is required to validate causal relationships.

Physiological factors

The development of frailty is closely associated with numerous physiological factors. The physiological factors summarized in this review primarily include nutritional status, laboratory test indicators, sleep quality, activities of daily living (ADL), self-rated health status, heart rate variability, immediate memory, calf circumference, grip strength, metabolic equivalents (METs), and body mass index (BMI).

Nutritional status, ADL capacity, and self-rated health are significantly correlated with frailty. For example, a statistical analysis by Zhang, Yu et al. [9] on 265 elderly hospitalized patients with gastrointestinal tumors revealed that patients with poor nutritional status had a 2.5-fold higher incidence of frailty compared to those with good nutritional status. This issue often arises due to limitations in nutrient intake and digestive absorption among gastrointestinal cancer patients, leading to reduced energy intake that fails to meet the high-energy demands required for combating tumors, thereby depleting the body’s energy reserves and accelerating the onset and progression of frailty. For instance, studies have shown that each one-point increase in instrumental activities of daily living reduces the risk of frailty by 61% and the risk of pre-frailty by 69% [32]. A possible explanation is that reduced physical activity due to impaired ADL leads to muscle weakness, diminished neuromuscular coordination, and decreased cardiorespiratory endurance, further exacerbating frailty [40].

Research has confirmed that blood biochemical markers such as hemoglobin, white blood cell count, and neutrophil count are closely related to frailty in patients [16]. Lower hemoglobin levels are strongly associated with frailty; the underlying mechanism may involve tissue hypoxia caused by anemia, resulting in reduced muscular endurance and limited physical function, thus promoting the development of frailty [41]. Elevated white blood cell and neutrophil counts typically indicate chronic inflammation or a state of stress. Previous studies have found that elevated inflammatory markers are linked to frailty progression, and neutrophil count shows a positive correlation with frailty scores, particularly with muscle strength decline and reduced physical activity [42] Therefore, laboratory parameters such as hemoglobin, white blood cells, and neutrophils may serve as auxiliary indicators for identifying frailty risk in elderly cancer patients.

Existing research has demonstrated that reduced calf circumference and grip strength are significantly associated with frailty [16]. Calf circumference and grip strength are important, simple clinical indicators for assessing frailty. Calf circumference indirectly reflects lower limb muscle mass and nutritional reserves; its reduction indicates muscle loss and diminished functional capacity. Grip strength, as a key surrogate measure of overall muscle power, effectively reflects the body’s muscular function. These findings suggest that muscle weakness may be one of the critical physiological foundations underlying the development and progression of frailty.

Psychological factors

Psychological factors constitute critical determinants of frailty in older adults with cancer. This systematic review demonstrated that anxiety, depression, elevated perceived stress, negative emotions and psychological distress are all significantly correlated with frailty, indicating mental health exerts a vital role in the onset and progression of frailty.

Anxiety and depression represent the most consistently reported psychological correlates of frailty. Multiple studies verified that depression, anxiety and psychological distress are significantly associated with elevated frailty risk among elderly cancer patients [11, 29, 30, 38, 39, 43]. The underlying mechanisms involve multiple pathways. First, chronic psychological stress and depressive states disrupt hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis homeostasis, suppressing immune function and accelerating tumor progression, which further fuels frailty development [44]. Second, negative emotions and stress stimulate the secretion of interleukin-6 (IL-6) [45] and other pro-inflammatory cytokines, and IL-6 drives physical dysfunction and frailty onse [12, 46]. In addition, Ding et al. [38] identified anxiety and avoidant attachment as independent frailty risk factors. Patients with avoidant attachment tend to maintain emotional distance, avoid intimate interactions, suppress negative feelings and refrain from seeking help, resulting in social isolation, heightened psychological distress, reduced daily activity, aggravated anxiety and depression, and ultimately accelerated cognitive and physical frailty [47].

Lifestyle-related factors

Lifestyle indicators including living alone, alcohol consumption, exercise habits and activities of daily living (ADL) scores were identified as risk factors for frailty in older cancer patients.

Numerous studies have identified solitary living as an independent frailty risk factor, whose adverse effects are mediated by insufficient social support, loneliness and altered daily routines. Solitary individuals are more prone to social isolation and loneliness [48], and loneliness is positively correlated with frailty incidence and progression [49]. Nevertheless, living alone does not equate to social isolation; its impact is modulated by the quality of social networks and family support.

Patients with high health literacy demonstrate superior self-management capacity, and are more likely to adopt balanced diets and regular exercise, as well as proactive disease monitoring, enabling timely identification and intervention of malnutrition, pain and psychological distress to reduce frailty risk. Physical activity is essential for metabolic homeostasis and muscle maintenance, and low physical activity constitutes a major frailty risk factor [50]. Impaired activities of daily living serve as a powerful predictor of frailty, reflecting depleted physiological reserve that requires early intervention to slow frailty progression [51].

Sociodemographic factors

Sociodemographic characteristics act as distal social determinants of frailty onset and progression, encompassing age, gender, monthly household income, social support, educational attainment, marital status and family cohesion.

These factors do not function in isolation, but collectively reflect patients’ socioeconomic status, availability of social support and accessibility to healthcare resources. Advanced age is the most powerful, non-modifiable frailty risk factor. Progressive degenerative changes in organs and tissues reduce physiological reserve and immune competence with age, raising frailty susceptibility [52]. Women exhibit higher frailty prevalence than men, which may be attributed to lower lean muscle mass and menopausal endocrine shifts [53]. Low household income, limited educational attainment and unemployment restrict access to nutritional, rehabilitative and medical care, while diminishing health literacy and self-management skills. Moreover, social support and family cohesion modulate frailty risk via emotional comfort, daily care, treatment companionship and health behavioral patterns. Collectively, older cancer patients with disadvantaged sociodemographic profiles should be prioritized for frailty screening and early intervention, and targeted comprehensive management plans should be formulated in clinical practice.

Treatment-related factors

Treatment-related variables are key contributors to frailty among older adults with cancer. This review confirmed postoperative complications, radiotherapy adverse reactions and chemotherapy-induced taste disturbances are significant frailty risk factors.

Overall, antitumor therapies promote frailty through multiple overlapping pathways including inflammatory stress [54], nutritional and metabolic dysregulation [55], restricted physical activit [53], neurocognitive impairment [53], and synergistic interactions with pre-existing chronic illnesses.

Patients undergoing surgery commonly develop postoperative infections, hemorrhage and other complications that deplete physical energy reserves, triggering or exacerbating frailty. If patients already present pre-operative frailty risk, their tolerance to surgical stress declines, leading to higher postoperative complication rates and forming a self-perpetuating vicious cycle [56].

Radiotherapy frequently induces oral mucositis and dysphagia, resulting in suppressed appetite and reduced food intake [57]. Chemotherapy causes taste distortion, gustatory hallucinations and dysgeusia, which also impair dietary intake, precipitate malnutrition and sarcopenia, and accelerate frailty development. Furthermore, certain treatments exert neurotoxic effects that impair attention and memory, and raise the risk of delirium, anxiety and depression; these psychological and cognitive deficits are independently linked to frailty [54]. Therefore, clinical staff should implement comprehensive monitoring and management for elderly patients receiving antitumor therapy, with focused assessment of nutritional status, physical function, mental health and cognitive function to identify high-risk patients and deliver targeted interventions at an early stage.

Strengths and limitations

This is the first systematic review to separately stratify and synthesize coexistent cross-sectional correlates and longitudinal independent predictors of frailty among older cancer patients, filling the evidence gap of layered classification in this research field and providing evidence-based support for clinical stratified frailty risk screening and individualized intervention design. This review categorized frailty-related variables into six systematic domains (sociodemographic, physiological, psychological, oncological, lifestyle and antitumor treatment factors), and clearly elaborated complex interactive pathways among these variables to facilitate structured clinical interpretation of multi-factorial frailty triggers. Nine validated standardized frailty assessment tools were incorporated in the analysis, including Fried Frailty Phenotype (FP), Tilburg Frailty Indicator (TFI) and Groningen Frailty Indicator (GFI). Evidence from different scales was synthesized across all tumor types and treatment phases to achieve comprehensive coverage. Furthermore, this review strictly distinguished two types of research evidence: cross-sectional studies only summarized concurrent frailty correlates, while longitudinal cohort studies identified independent predictors of frailty onset and progression. Both univariate correlation and multivariable adjusted regression results were integrated to achieve multi-layered evidence collation and improve the comprehensiveness and clinical applicability of conclusions.

Three major limitations exist in the present study. First, only published observational research was synthesized, without basic mechanistic experiments; only correlational associations can be described, and causal pathways between individual risk factors and frailty cannot be clarified. Second, meta-analysis was not performed; only stratified narrative qualitative synthesis was adopted. The core reason for omitting quantitative pooling lies in severe clinical heterogeneity: the nine included frailty scales adopt inconsistent diagnostic cut-offs and scoring algorithms, and the manuscripts report three incompatible effect metrics (adjusted OR, regression β, correlation r) with distinct measurement units, violating the statistical and clinical homogeneity prerequisites for meta-analysis, so pooled effect sizes and heterogeneity tests cannot be calculated. Third, all malignant tumor types were pooled for overall analysis without subgroup stratification by lung, gastrointestinal, breast and other cancer subtypes; frailty risk profiles vary significantly across tumor categories, limiting the precision of conclusions when applied to single cancer populations.

Conclusion

This systematic review integrated comprehensive evidence on frailty risk factors in older adults with cancer. The results demonstrated that frailty is jointly shaped by multi-dimensional determinants including oncological, physiological, psychological, sociodemographic, lifestyle and treatment-related factors, highlighting the multi-factorial, heterogeneous nature of frailty in elderly oncology populations.

Frailty is not merely a byproduct of tumor progression and physiological aging, but a cumulative adverse outcome of long-term psychological, sociodemographic and therapeutic exposures. Clinicians should establish multi-dimensional frailty risk assessment frameworks that integrate tumor characteristics, physical function, mental health and social support to conduct early screening and precise individualized interventions, thereby reducing frailty incidence and optimizing patient prognosis.

Future high-quality longitudinal cohort and mechanistic research is warranted to further clarify causal relationships and action pathways between frailty risk factors, and develop validated frailty prediction models and targeted intervention strategies for older cancer patients.
