The role of gut microbiota in neuropathic pain: insights into immune mechanisms
Authors: Zexiong Gong, Jipeng Zou, Yuxiang Fang, Jie Li, Yan Luo, Qingsheng Xue, Buwei Yu, Bo Hua, Zhiheng Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeuropathic pain (NP), characterized by its complex pathophysiological mechanisms, has long posed a formidable therapeutic challenge. The burden of NP is further exacerbated by the increasing prevalence of chronic diseases. Emerging evidence highlights the pivotal role of gut microbiota in modulating immune responses, offering novel insights into NP pathogenesis. This review explores recent advancements in understanding how gut microbiota-derived metabolites - including short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), bile acids, and tryptophan derivatives - regulate immune processes that influence neuroinflammation and nociceptive signaling. We focus on key immune mediators, including macrophages, microglia, T cells, and astrocytes, elucidating their involvement in microbiota-driven immune regulation via pathways such as TLR4/NF-κB signaling, histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibition, and aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) activation. Additionally, we examine emerging evidence of sex-specific immune mechanisms in NP. Despite promising preclinical findings on microbiota-targeted therapies, such as probiotics and fecal microbiota transplantation, translational challenges, such as microbiota heterogeneity and sex-specific responses, necessitate further investigation. This review aims to bridge microbiology, neuroimmunology, and pain research, offering a multidimensional perspective and actionable insights for the future management of NP.Keywords: Gut microbiota, Neuropathic pain, Macrophages, Microglia, T cells

Introduction

Neuropathic pain (NP) is a major public health concern, with its prevalence increasing due to factors such as population aging, metabolic disorders, and central nervous system (CNS) diseases [1]. The International Association for the Study of Pain (IASP) classifies chronic pain into nociceptive, nociplastic, and neuropathic categories [2]. Nociceptive pain results from ongoing inflammation and tissue injury, whereas nociplastic pain represents a newer category characterized by unclear mechanisms and may occur alone or as part of mixed-type pain (see review [3] for details). NP is defined as a complex condition caused by neurological lesions or systemic diseases and involves multiple mechanisms, including neuronal injury, neuroinflammation, ion channel dysfunction, glial activation, and epigenetic regulation [4, 5]. Inflammatory mechanisms constitute a core biological response to injury, mainly through immune cell recruitment and mediator release [6]. Inflammation is the well-established driver of nearly all nociceptive pain. Notably, numerous etiologies of NP (such as traumatic neuropathy and spinal cord injury) are linked to excessive neuroinflammation [7]. Neuroinflammation refers specifically to localized inflammation within the nervous system. It is more potent than systemic inflammation in initiating and sustaining pain and is clinically more difficult to detect. It affects damaged and degenerating neurons as well as adjacent, undamaged afferent fibers [7]. Neuroinflammation acts through mediator release that activates multiple G protein-coupled receptors, ionotropic receptors, and tyrosine kinase receptors on immune cells [8], or directly sensitizes nociceptors through transient receptor potential vanilloid (TRPV) channels [8, 9]. A defining feature distinguishing neuroinflammation in NP from inflammatory mechanisms in nociceptive pain is the activation of peripheral glial cells. This review focuses on neuroinflammation-induced activation of microglia and astrocytes [10, 11]. Pain research increasingly acknowledges that pain mechanisms vary across diseases, especially in mixed-type pain conditions such as cancer pain and spinal pain [12]. Consequently, reducing systemic inflammation can partially alleviate NP, explaining why non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), though primarily effective for nociceptive pain, also provide modest benefits in NP.

From an anatomical perspective, NP can be broadly divided into central neuropathic pain (CNP) and peripheral neuropathic pain (PNP). CNP results from disorders of the central nervous system, such as post-stroke central pain and Parkinson’s disease-related pain [13]. In contrast, PNP arises from damage to peripheral nerves, as seen in sciatica caused by compressive injuries [14] and diabetic peripheral neuropathic pain (DPNP), which is driven by neuroinflammation [15]. Persistent NP induces maladaptive CNS plasticity, often progressing to refractory chronic pain in clinical settings. This chronic condition imposes substantial disease burden and serves as a key driver of opioid analgesic misuse [16]. Current pharmacological management primarily relies on anticonvulsants, antidepressants, and opioids, which act by silencing pain pathways. However, these approaches fail to address the underlying pathophysiology and are associated with significant adverse effects. Despite expanding research efforts, breakthrough therapies remain elusive. The complexity of the etiological mechanisms of NP, the absence of definitive biomarkers, and the lack of reliable prognostic monitoring tools continue to pose major challenges in neurological medicine [5]. These issues underscore the need for deeper investigation into NP pathogenesis, the identification of suitable biomarkers, the refinement of therapeutic strategies, and enhanced prognostic monitoring.

The immune system plays a central role in NP, involving both intrinsic immune cells within the CNS [17]—such as astrocytes [18] and microglia [19]—and interactions between neurons and peripheral immune cells, including T cells and macrophages [20, 21]. Notably, specific immune cell subtypes may mitigate neuroinflammation by releasing anti-inflammatory cytokines, potentially alleviating NP—a phenomenon that will be explored in subsequent sections. The neuroimmune axis in pain regulation is highly sensitive to both intrinsic and extrinsic environmental factors, with gut microbiota emerging as a key modulator. This review focuses on elucidating microbiota-regulated immune mechanisms underlying NP.

The gut microbiota consists primarily of bacterial populations, but also encompasses fungi, viruses, and helminths present within the gut. However, compared to bacteria, these other microbial pathogen communities remain less well characterized, and they will not be discussed in detail in this review (for comprehensive discussions, readers are referred to review [22]). The symbiotic relationship between the gut microbiota and the host begins at birth, with evidence suggesting that maternal intestinal microbiota influences fetal mice thalamic-cortical axon development [23]. Maternal dietary patterns during pregnancy [24], mode of delivery [25], and even the delivery environment [26] all influence the early gut microbiota composition of neonates. While maintaining a dynamic equilibrium, gut microbial composition exhibits significant interindividual variability that is influenced by factors such as geography, sex, age, antibiotic exposure, dietary patterns, as well as physical and mental health status [27, 28]. Gut microbiota metabolites interact with the host via humoral, endocrine, and neuroimmune pathways, thereby affecting various physiological and pathological processes. The significance of gut microbiota has been extensively documented in autoimmune diseases [29], CNS disorders [30], and metabolic conditions [31]. We examined the intricate crosstalk between immune mechanisms and gut microbiota in the context of NP alleviation [32]. Recent epidemiological investigations suggest that gut microbiota may influence NP via both direct and immune-mediated mechanisms [33, 34].

This review synthesizes the latest evidence on cellular immune mechanisms underlying NP, with particular emphasis on how gut microbiota and their metabolites modulate these immunological pathways. These mechanisms include direct modulation of the gut immune microenvironment and indirect effects mediated by microbial products that cross the intestinal barrier, enter the systemic circulation, and reach the nervous system. Ultimately, these factors can directly influence the nervous system, modulating the release of neuroinflammatory and chemotactic mediators during nociceptive signaling through interactions with immune cells and neuroglia (to be further discussed in the following sections). Moreover, this review delineates sex-specific immune responses and gut microbiota alterations contributing to NP disparities. We conclude by highlighting emerging opportunities and challenges in the field, proposing that advances in microbiological and immunological research may yield novel therapeutic paradigms for NP.

Methods of review

The search strategy was conducted using PubMed from inception through 2025 with the following search terms: “gut microbiota”, “intestinal microbiome”, “gut bacteria”, “immune mechanism”, “neuropathic pain”, “chronic pain”, “pain signaling”, “NP”, “neuroinflammation”, “lipid”, “polyunsaturated fatty acids”, “PUFAs”, “butyrate”, “bile”, “tryptophan metabolism”, “macrophages”, “microglia”, “natural killer cells”, “NK cells”, “dendritic cells”, “DCs”, “MCs”, “Mast cells”, “T lymphocytes”, “Regulatory T cells”, “Treg”, “B lymphocytes”, “astrocytes”, “aryl hydrocarbon receptor”, “AhR”, “lipopolysaccharides”, “LPS”, “long-chain fatty acids”, “short-chain fatty acids”, “SCFAs”, “LCFAs”, “tryptophan metabolism”, “kynurenine”, “transient receptor potential vanilloid”, “TRPV”, “toll-like receptor”, “TLR”. We included clinical trials, laboratory studies, narrative reviews, metaanalyses, systematic reviews, and editorials published in English. Conference abstracts were excluded. All titles were screened, and we excluded papers where the content was not relevant to the topic.

Gut microbiota

The gut microbiota communicates with immune cells through lipid molecules, gut-derived secondary bile acids, tryptophan and its derivatives, and neurotransmitters, ultimately influencing NP. This review focuses solely on lipid molecules associated with neuroinflammation, as the types and functions of gut microbiota-derived lipids are complex and have been extensively reviewed elsewhere [35, 36]. From a developmental perspective, the impact of gut microbiota on neuroinflammation and pain regulation begins prenatally. Neuroinflammation induced by maternal ischemia and hypoxia during the perinatal period is influenced by the gut microbiota, ultimately altering the mouse pups susceptibility to cerebral ischemia and hypoxia [37]. Tracing further back, prenatal exposure to opioids significantly disrupts neonatal gut microbial composition, heightening pain sensitivity—a condition that can be ameliorated through maternal probiotic supplementation [38]. During the critical period of neonatal neurodevelopment, the gut microbiota plays a pivotal role. Recent studies have shown that the absence of gut microbiota in germ-free (GF) neonatal mice directly disrupts the trafficking of TRPV 1 to the cell membrane and negatively influences other pain-associated ion channels, including calcium and potassium channels, as well as G protein-coupled receptors (GPR) involved in itch and nociceptive pain [39]. As a result, overall nociceptor sensitivity is markedly reduced. Encouragingly, microbial recolonization can reverse these adverse effects and restore pain perception [40]. Recolonized gut microbiota maintains normal visceral pain perception partly through Toll-like receptor upregulation and spinal cytokine expression [41], and preserves somatic pain perception by regulating mast cell-derived nerve growth factor (NGF) [39]. These findings emphasize the importance of early-life gut microbiota colonization in shaping nociceptor sensitivity across the lifespan. In addition, experiments in neonatal rats have shown that transient changes in early-colonizing gut microbiota lead to increased visceral pain sensitivity. Importantly, these alterations in nociceptive pathways are long-lasting and resistant to antibiotic treatment [42]. This evidence highlights the profound and enduring influence of gut microbiota on the development of nociceptive systems during the neonatal period, offering new insights into mechanisms of chronic pain susceptibility and potential therapeutic approaches. Furthermore, gut microbiota-based therapies [38, 43–46] offer potential strategies for modulating gut microbial communities, thereby influencing immune responses and neuroinflammation. These approaches hold promise in the treatment of NP. The following sections will explore the intricate crosstalk between gut microbiota and immune mechanisms in the context of NP and neuroinflammation.

Lipid

Lipid mediators derived from gut microbiota involved in NP can be categorized into structural lipid components and bioactive metabolites (Fig. 1). Microbial structural lipids exhibit distinct molecular patterns compared to their eukaryotic counterparts, triggering host innate immune recognition through pattern recognition receptors – particularly Toll-like receptor (TLR2/4) – which play a role in NP pathogenesis [36, 47, 48]. The TLR family recognizes conserved molecular patterns, such as lipopolysaccharides (LPS) from Gram-negative bacteria, triggering immune responses that promote the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines and induce oxidative stress [49, 50]. Among these receptors, TLR4 is highly expressed on the surface and within endosomes of macrophages, dendritic cells (DCs), astrocytes, and microglia. In NP, LPS activates TLR4 on microglia, promoting their shift towards a pro-inflammatory phenotype and facilitating the release of neuroinflammatory factors, contributing to neuroinflammation [51, 52] and the transition to chronic pain [53] (Fig. 2). Additionally, LPS directly acts on nociceptor-expressed TLR4 in mice, thereby inducing pain responses [54, 55].

Mechanisms underlying the role of gut microbial metabolites in neuropathic pain. Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), particularly butyrate, alleviate neuropathic pain by shifting immune cells toward an anti-inflammatory state through activation of aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) and G-protein-coupled receptors (GPRs), while also suppressing neuroinflammation via inhibition of histone deacetylases (HDACs). In contrast, long-chain fatty acids (LCFAs) and polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) tend to exacerbate neuropathic pain. Secondary bile acids, modified by gut microbiota, play a complex role in pain modulation, primarily exerting analgesic effects. These effects are mediated through two key mechanisms: (1) direct actions on immune cells, TGR5 signaling, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, influencing immune cell differentiation and neuroinflammation; and (2) suppression of TLR4 signaling and NLRP3 inflammasome activation, thereby reducing neuroinflammatory responses. Gut microbiota-derived lipid metabolites exhibit greater structural diversity, making this a rapidly expanding area of therapeutic research. Among these metabolites, short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) are among the most representative. The gut microbiota ferments indigestible dietary fibers into monosaccharides, which are further metabolized into SCFAs such as acetate, propionate, and butyrate (as reviewed in [56, 57]). SCFAs are primarily absorbed by colonocytes through monocarboxylate transporters, traverse the intestinal epithelium to reach the intestinal lamina propria and circulatory system, where they function as immunomodulatory compounds involved in the neuroinflammatory responses of immune cells in the peripheral nervous system (PNS) and CNS. Their mechanisms of action in NP include, but are not limited to: 1) Direct modulation of neuronal excitability through GPR41/43/109a [56, 58]; 2) Epigenetic modifications and gene expression by inhibiting histone deacetylases (HDAC) [59], thereby modulating the levels of neuroinflammatory factors [60]. Butyrate emerges as a particularly potent modulator, demonstrating dual therapeutic actions. Immunologically, it regulates macrophage and T-cell differentiation [61, 62] and modulates the release of neuroinflammatory factors [60, 63–65] through immune mechanisms. Neurologically, it activates vagal signaling through GPR41-dependent mechanisms [58]. Evidence of SCFAs influencing neurons via the gut-brain axis can be traced back to the prenatal stage, with offspring from GF dams exhibiting congenital neurological defects, diminished cognitive function, and impaired social skills—conditions that could be ameliorated by supplementary propionate [66].

Long-chain fatty acids (LCFAs) and polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), although diet-dependent, also undergo microbial biotransformation into bioactive species. LCFAs derived from Lactobacillus species act as peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR) agonists, modulating macrophage infiltration patterns in NP models [67, 68]. Recent studies have further demonstrated a significant reduction in plasma levels of LCFAs in mice following fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) from fibromyalgia (FM) patients [46]. Meanwhile, In mice model, microbial-modified PUFAs engage TRPV channels-particularly TRPV4-in peripheral nociceptors, transducing chemical and mechanical stimuli into neuronal depolarization, ultimately initiating pain responses [69]. The regulation of dietary PUFAs by gut microbiota remains underexplored, yet there is potential for microbial transformation of PUFAs into biologically active metabolites that influence human health.

Secondary bile acids

Most bile acids in the human body enter the enterohepatic circulation, where they are transported back to the liver. A small portion is reabsorbed in the ileum and undergoes further modifications in the colon, typically conjugation with taurine or glycine. These modifications lead to the formation of at least 97 known secondary bile acid derivatives [70], including lithocholic acid (LCA), deoxycholic acid (DCA), hyodeoxycholic acid (HDCA), and ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA), which modulate metabolic processes in the organism [71–73]. In NP, secondary bile acids derived from gut microbiota influence the immune system through multiple mechanisms (Fig. 1): 1) Microbial secondary bile acids serve as endogenous inhibitors of TLR4 [71], engaging immune cells such as macrophages and T cells to reduce neuroinflammation in septic mice [71] and alleviate NP [74]. 2) Takeda G protein-coupled receptor (TGR) −5, a G-protein coupled receptor widely expressed in the nervous system [75], can be activated by bile acids, which then alleviates NP through TGR-5-dependent pathways [76]. Additionally, In mice model, TGR-5 influences TRPV1 expression, modulating nociceptive signaling and exerting analgesic effects [77]. 3) Bile acids regulate TRPV1 activity in dorsal root ganglia (DRG) neurons, which function as sensory neurons detecting mechanical, dietary, and noxious stimuli. TRPV1, a cation channel highly expressed in DRG neurons [78], plays a critical role in nociception by detecting harmful stimuli and relaying signals to the CNS. TRPV1 can be activated by various inflammatory mediators, exacerbating neuroinflammation [79]. In DPNP, secondary bile acid metabolism disorders may dysregulate TGR5-mediated cAMP-CREB signaling, leading to abnormal TRPV1 expression and transcription, thereby promoting regional pain development [80]. In a FM mouse model, oral administration of UDCA significantly attenuated mechanical hypersensitivity and alleviated specific pain phenotypes [46]. Therefore, supplementation with exogenous bile acids or bile acid receptor agonists may provide a promising approach to alleviating NP [81].

Tryptophan metabolism

Tryptophan metabolism plays a crucial role in regulating immune responses and inflammation, with the gut microbiota serving as a key modulator of this process. Tryptophan metabolites influence neuroinflammation by modulating the indole pathway [82], the kynurenine (Kyn) pathway [83] (Fig. 1), and the synthesis of 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT) [84]. Among these, Kyn is recognized as a neuroprotective endogenous antagonist of N-Methyl-D-Aspartate receptors (NMDA) with analgesic properties [85], and has recently been identified as an endogenous agonist of GPR-35 [86]. Significant improvements in NP have been observed when the Kyn pathway—activated by indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase—is either pharmacologically or genetically ablated [83]. Metabolic pathway analysis in FM research revealed that female FM patients receiving FMT exhibited significantly elevated fecal concentrations of tryptamine, tyramine, aspartate, and proline, alongside reduced plasma levels of lysine and Kyn [46]. These findings are highly consistent with preclinical studies. Tryptophan metabolism exerts its immunomodulatory effects primarily through the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR), a cytoplasmic transcription factor activated by tryptophan metabolites derived from mammalian and bacterial sources (AHR can also be activated by exogenous ligands from food) [87]. Indole derivatives, Kyn, and 5-HT, produced through tryptophan metabolism in the gut, facilitate communication between immune cells and the CNS via AhR-dependent mechanisms [88]. AhR expression in mouse intestine was predominantly localized to the outer muscular layer of the colon, particularly in microbially enriched segments, with minimal detection in duodenum and jejunum. Microbiota-induced neuronal AhR activation forms a responsive microbiota-AhR-neural axis that mediates neuron-specific effector mechanisms [89]. In an autoimmune multiple sclerosis mouse model, tryptophan metabolites regulate CNS immune responses by targeting AhR on astrocytes and microglia [90]. The mechanism by which gut microbe-derived tryptophan influences AhR is a key focus in understanding neuroinflammation and holds promise as a novel therapeutic target for NP and other neurorelated disorders. A tryptophan-rich diet is considered a promising non-invasive approach for alleviating neuroinflammatory diseases.

Innate immune system

Macrophages

Macrophages are a highly adaptable class of immune cells [91] that play a crucial coordinating role in NP models [92–94]. They detect neurogenic inflammation [92, 95] and are recruited from blood vessels to damaged nerves [96], where they become activated and contribute to the inflammatory response. The accumulation of macrophages and their secretion of pro-inflammatory factors are significant contributors to the development of NP [97, 98]. In response to neurogenic inflammation, macrophages proliferate, and their cell surface markers and functions become activated [92, 95]. They polarize into two distinct phenotypes with the potential for interconversion [94, 99]: M1-like macrophages and M2-like macrophages. M1-like macrophages express pro-inflammatory markers such as major histocompatibility complex class II (MHC-II), nitric oxide synthase 2, interleukin (IL) −1 beta, IL-6, tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), and Chemokine C-C ligand 5 (CCL) − 5 [100]. These cells sensitize nociceptors through direct interactions and part of the mechanism to is induce pain hypersensitivity via TRPV1 [9]. Furthermore, M1-like macrophages exacerbate pain by activating sensory neurons through the release of additional pro-inflammatory mediators [21]. In contrast, M2-like macrophages express anti-inflammatory markers, including cluster of differentiation (CD) 206, arginase 1, CD163, PPAR-γ, and IL-10, which contribute to the resolution of neurogenic inflammation [94, 99, 101]. These mechanisms establish a strong link between macrophages, neurogenic inflammation, and NP (Fig. 3). Additionally, nerve-associated macrophages have emerged as a key focus of research in recent years. Recent investigations have elucidated that CCR2+ expressing macrophages exert a distinctive protective role in the peripheral nerves of prediabetic obese mice. These macrophages facilitate neuroprotection through the expression of neuroprotective factors, such as Galectin-3 (Gal-3), thereby promoting axonal regeneration and eliminating injury-associated molecules, thus contributing to neural preservation [102]. These macrophages, which exhibit specific tissue preferences and originate from late embryonic precursors, differ from both bone marrow-derived tissue macrophages and early embryonic-derived microglia [103]. In models of PNI, nerve-associated macrophages play an active role in myelin formation and nerve regeneration, responding more rapidly to neurogenic inflammation than bone marrow-derived macrophages [104].

Immune mechanisms of neuroinflammation mediated by the gut microbiota. The gut microbiota exerts bidirectional regulatory effects on neuroinflammation through lipid molecules, microbial-derived secondary bile acids, and tryptophan derivatives. Through multiple mechanisms including macrophage polarization modulation, astrocyte functionality alteration, NK cell population regulation, T cell differentiation control, and microglial activation, gut microbiota can either promote the progression or facilitate the regression of neuroinflammatory processes, ultimately influencing the pathogenesis of various neurological disorders, including neuropathic pain

Ingesting dietary fiber can enhance the abundance of bacteria in the colon that produce SCFAs, commonly including the Akkermansia, Bifidobacterium, and Lactobacillus genera species [105]. Accumulating evidence indicates that microbiota-derived SCFAs alleviate NP by modulating macrophage polarization and suppressing neuroinflammatory mediators through several mechanisms (Table 1): 1) HDAC inhibition: Butyrate-mediated HDAC suppression reduces LPS-induced IL-6 and IL-12 release from macrophages, thereby decreasing neuronal hyperexcitability and neuroinflammation [106]. 2) GPCR activation: SCFAs activate GPCR signaling, inducing IL-10 production in macrophages and DCs, contributing to neuroimmune homeostasis (mice) [107]. 3) Microbial modulation: In chronic constriction injury (CCI) models, Lactobacillus plantarum restores Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes balance to enhance SCFA production. In rat model of peripheral neuropathy, this intervention preserves myelin integrity, suppresses ectopic discharges, and rebalances macrophage phenotypes by reducing M1-associated cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) while promoting M2 differentiation (IL-10, IL-4, IL-13) [108]. Similarly, Lactobacillus plantarum-derived exosomes increase M2 differentiation in human skin organoid culture models, enhancing IL-10 expression and exerting an anti-inflammatory effect [109]. 4) Metabolic reprogramming: in NP associated with obesity, FMT from lean mice increased butyrate secretion in obese mice, altering gene expression in peripheral macrophages and increasing the levels of M2 macrophages, thereby alleviating NP [120]. Butyrate also shifts macrophage metabolism by activating oxidative phosphorylation and lipid metabolism pathways [61], suggesting a potential avenue for macrophage-targeting NP therapies.

While SCFAs are predominantly associated with reducing neuroinflammation and pain, they can also contribute to inflammation under some conditions. When the intestinal epithelial barrier is compromised, the presence of TLR ligands and other pro-inflammatory factors allows butyrate and propionate to activate the NLRP3 inflammasome in macrophages, triggering inflammatory responses and promoting the release of IL-1β [63]. Given the strong association between IL-1β and neuroinflammatory, we propose that during intestinal epithelial injury, SCFAs may promote neuroinflammatory through the NLRP3 inflammasome in macrophages. Apart from SCFAs, LCFAs from gut microbiota lipid metabolites regulate macrophage infiltration and the production of anti-inflammatory molecules at sites of nerve injury through PPAR signaling in peripheral macrophages during the early stages of NP progression [67, 121], thereby reducing tactile allodynia. PPAR agonism promotes M2 polarization, suggesting its potential as a macrophage-targeted therapeutic strategy [122].

The gut microbiota improves NP by modulating macrophage status and function through bile acid metabolism (Table 2): Secondary bile acids, such as DCA and LCA, inhibit NLRP3 in macrophages, reducing the release of inflammatory factors [123]. Targeted inhibition of the NLRP3 inflammasome in macrophages has been demonstrated to effectively alleviate pain in nerve injury mouse models [136] and CCI rat models [137]. Exogenous supplementation of DCA or LCA to inhibit macrophage NLRP3 activity may hold promise as a potential strategy for alleviating NP in the future. (Fig. 2). This also suggests a strong correlation between HDCA and diseases characterized by excessive TLR4 activation, with potential therapeutic implications for autoimmune diseases, and drug addiction. Additionally, LCA binds to the pregnane X receptor in macrophage nuclei [138], downregulating the mRNA expression of inflammatory factors, including IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-8 [124], thereby reducing neuroinflammation. Similar to SCFAs, not all microbiota-derived secondary bile acids contribute to the improvement of NP. Some secondary bile acids enhance immune responses and neuroinflammation by inducing M1 macrophage differentiation, a process linked to M2 macrophage regulation via the surface receptor TGR-5 on macrophages [125]. Activation of TGR-5 promotes the polarization of macrophages from the M1 to the M2 phenotype [126]. However, other macrophage surface receptors mediate M1 macrophage differentiation in response to bile acids. For instance, chenodeoxycholic acid induces the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and potassium ion efflux in macrophages, leading to NLRP3 activation and the release of IL-1β [127]. Similarly, certain bile acids, in conjunction with adenosine triphosphate (ATP), induce calcium ion influx, further activating NLRP3 [123], exacerbating the progression of neuropathic inflammation.

Additionally, the structural sulfolipid produced by Chryseobacterium species enhances macrophage-mediated proinflammatory cytokine production, increasing the levels of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α [139]. In oxaliplatin-induced chemotherapy-associated pain models, gut microbiota exacerbates NP by amplifying post-chemotherapy inflammation via the LPS/TLR4 axis. In GF mice model, This pathological process was attenuated by antibiotic intervention, which significantly reduced macrophage infiltration in the DRG and lowered IL-6 and TNF-α levels [140]. While current understanding suggests that LPS exacerbates NP through macrophage-derived neuroinflammatory mediators [49, 50], emerging evidence highlights dietary modulation as a potential strategy for counteracting these proinflammatory effects. Notably, mice fed a long-term soy isoflavone-enriched diet exhibited alterations in LPS biosynthesis pathways. Fecal LPS extracts from isoflavone-supplemented mice induced increased IL-10 levels while suppressing TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β production in bone marrow-derived macrophages [141], indicating diet-specific modulation of LPS biosynthesis may favor anti-inflammatory polarization and systemic immune homeostasis. This metabolic reprogramming could mitigate inflammatory disease progression. Parallel findings suggest that genistein, a phytoestrogen, promotes M1-to-M2 macrophage transition while enhancing IL-10 expression, further underscoring the therapeutic potential of phytochemical-mediated immunomodulation [142].

Targeting macrophage phenotype modulation represents a promising avenue for the treatment of NP. M2 macrophages exhibit pronounced anti-inflammatory properties and release elevated levels of anti-inflammatory cytokines. Additionally, they secrete higher levels of endogenous opioid peptides, such as methionine-enkephalin (Met-ENK), dynorphins (DYNs), and β-endorphins (β-END) compared to other macrophage subtypes [143]. The endogenous opioid system plays a critical role in PNP, and recent studies indicate that M2 macrophages can attenuate mechanical hypersensitivity by activating peripheral opioid receptors through IL-4, ultimately alleviating NP [94, 99]. Peripheral opioid receptor activation provides effective analgesia without the typical adverse effects associated with centrally-acting medications [144], offering a strategy to reduce opioid misuse. Modulating M2 macrophages through non-invasive, personalized interventions, such as SCFAs, FMT, or a diet rich in phytoestrogens, represents a promising therapeutic approach toward enhancing endogenous opioid peptide secretion and alleviating NP.

Microglia

Microglia, the resident macrophages of the CNS, primarily originate from progenitor cells in the extra-embryonic yolk sac and persist throughout the CNS [145–147]. Recent studies have identified a population of PNS microglia in the PNS that shares identical transcriptomic, epigenomic, proteomic, developmental origin, and functional characteristics with CNS microglia [148]. Previously considered passive observers, with the advent of single-cell analysis techniques, it has become evident that microglia are not static entities but instead exist in diverse functional states, responding dynamically to physiological and pathological conditions [149–153]. This adaptability is also reflected in NP [154, 155]. In models of neural injury, primary sensory neurons activate microglia in the spinal cord dorsal horn through the release of ATP, colony-stimulating factor 1 (CSF-1), neuroregulatory protein-1, and chemokines [19]. Once activated, microglia secrete inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α, IL-1β, brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), and IL-6, which exacerbate neuroinflammation, amplify NP-related signaling pathways [21, 156–158], and contribute to synaptic plasticity changes that drive hyperalgesia [21]. Similarly, in the FM mouse model, microglia in the dorsal spinal cord of the lumbar spine participate in the occurrence of FM through morphological changes (reduction in branch number and length) [46]. Following nerve injury, microglia swiftly polarize into the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype [95, 159]. In contrast, during the pain resolution phase, microglia polarize into the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype [160] (Fig. 3). The activation and polarization of microglia are influenced by factors such as neurotransmitters, hormones, and nutrients within the internal environment, as well as by pathogens and microbial communities in the external environment [150].

The gut microbiota serves as a critical environmental factor governing microglial ontogeny, functional maturation, and activation [161, 162]. Pathological depletion of microbial communities through antibiotic treatment induces microglial hyperactivation, exacerbating disease progression [163, 164]. In CIPN mice models, paclitaxel administration induces gut dysbiosis, significantly depleting Akkermansia muciniphila, a keystone species essential for intestinal barrier integrity. Intestinal barrier dysfunction is associated with exposure to pathogenic microorganisms, including Porphyromonadaceae, driving spinal microglial proliferation via gut-immune-brain axis activation and ultimately contributing to NP [165]. Probiotic supplementation has been shown to counteract this process by suppressing proinflammatory and neurotoxic pathways in Parkinsonian mice models while promoting M2-polarized microglial phenotypes that mitigate neuroinflammation [166]. Furthermore, sodium oligomannate administration has demonstrated microbiota-modulating effects, selectively suppressing Desulfovibrio spp. while enriching Bifidobacterium pseudolongum. This compositional shift in the microbiota coordinates microglia-mediated reductions in peripheral and cortical proinflammatory mediators (IL-6, IL-22, and CCL5), facilitating sex-dependent resolution of neuroinflammation [167].

The systemic dissemination of gut microbiota-derived SCFAs through intestinal immune niches establishes these metabolites as key modulators of microglial homeostasis [168]. Several key mechanisms have been identified. First, developmental regulation studies show that GF animals exhibit impaired microglial maturation and functional deficits, with SCFA deprivation inducing cell intrinsic defects in microglial surveillance capacity [161]. SCFAs also orchestrate systemic immune remodeling that indirectly modulates microglial polarization through cytokine milieu alterations. Second, SCFAs serve as endogenous agonists for AhR in the CNS, regulating microglial transcriptional programs through nuclear receptor activation [169]. Third, in Alzheimer’s disease mice models, acetate promotes M1-like microglial polarization, enhancing phagocytic clearance during neurodegeneration [116]. Targeting acetate depletion may thus hold promise for mitigating neuroinflammatory cascades in both neurodegenerative and NP conditions. Fourth, butyrate can inhibit neuroinflammation induced by microglia through the GPR109A/PPAR-γ/TLR-4/NF-κB signaling pathway, ameliorating the imbalance of M1/M2 microglia, promoting the secretion of IL-10, and mitigating neural damage in mice [64]. This mechanism, validated in alcoholic CNS injury models, warrants investigation in the context of NP. Fifth, propionate and butyrate supplementation suppresses neuroinflammation in GF rats through HDAC1 inhibition and CNS IL-6 downregulation, accompanied by microglial morphological normalization [110]. While existing evidence predominantly delineates SCFA-mediated neuroinflammatory regulation and neuroprotective mechanisms, their direct analgesic efficacy in NP remains to be empirically validated.

Contrasting with SCFA effects, microbiota-derived PUFAs exacerbate NP through spinal TRPV4 activation [69]. In sciatic nerve injury (SNI) models, TRPV4 mediates microglial proliferation and activation. Activated microglia release lipocalin-2 to enhance neuronal structural and functional plasticity [170]. This PUFA-TRPV4-microglia-neuron axis plays a key role in the transition from acute to chronic pain. Mechanistically, PUFAs serve as endogenous TRPV4 agonists, establishing this signaling cascade as a novel therapeutic target for chronic NP intervention.

Microbially modified secondary bile acids also influence the role of microglia in NP and neuroinflammation (Table 2). In SNI mice models, bile acid levels significantly decrease, and microglial activation within the DRG increases. Supplementation with exogenous bile acids alleviates NP by upregulating TGR5 to regulate microglia [76]. Additionally, in patients with multiple sclerosis, tauroursodeoxycholic acid and UDCA reduce microglia and astrocyte polarization toward a pro-inflammatory phenotype in a dose-dependent manner, thereby ameliorating neuroinflammation and exerting neuroprotective effects [128]. Recent studies have highlighted the importance of the farnesoid X receptor (FXR) in pain signaling [171]. In the SNL mouse model, gut and spinal cord levels of FXR were significantly reduced, accompanied by a marked decrease in total bile acids, particularly HDCA [81]. Further research has confirmed that HDCA exerts analgesic effects by activating FXR, thereby regulating the PPAR-γ, MMP2, and MMP9 signaling pathways. Administration of exogenous HDCA alleviated SNL-induced mechanical and thermal hyperalgesia [81]. Activation of FXR can alter the gut microenvironment by promoting mucus secretion and increasing the expression of antimicrobial peptides, thereby influencing gut microbiota composition [172]. FXR activation and gut microbiota may hold significant potential in maintaining gut health and modulating inflammation, which could ultimately contribute to NP management.

In studies on NP and microglia, LPS is frequently used to induce the polarization of microglia towards a pro-inflammatory phenotype. LPS promotes this polarization by activating various signaling pathways, including the cGAS-STING pathway (rats) [173], TAK1/p38/JNK1/2 pathway (rats) [174], and MAPK/NF-κB pathway (mice) [175]. LPS is widely recognized for its detrimental role in NP by regulating the transformation of microglia into a pro-inflammatory phenotype.

Observational evidence further explores the link between gut microbiota and microglia in neuroinflammation. Healthy neuroinflammation reduces excessive immune activation, inhibits the increase in neural myelin debris, and promotes the phagocytic clearance of harmful substances, ultimately benefiting neural healing and myelin regeneration. Conversely, unresolved chronic neuroinflammation impairs neural function and contributes to NP [176]. Strong evidence indicates that persistent neuroinflammation in both the central and PNS underlies NP [96]. We posit that the gut microbiota-microglia axis is involved in neuroinflammation-related NP, though further NP models or clinical research evidence are needed for validation. Advances in microglial labeling techniques and brain functional imaging are expected to yield new insights into the gut microbiota-microglia axis. Future research should further confirm that this axis alters detrimental feedforward loops and modulates peripheral-central immune crosstalk by regulating neuroinflammation in NP.

Natural killer cells

Natural killer (NK) cells, though a minority population in the immune system, are primarily recognized for their role in tumor immunity and viral defense. Their main function involves inducing apoptosis in target cells by releasing cytotoxic granules containing perforin [177]. However, recent evidence suggests that NK cells also play a critical, yet less widely recognized, role in NP modulation. A study on postherpetic neuralgia, albeit with a small sample size, revealed an inverse correlation between NK cell counts and patients’ sensitivity to pain [178]. Similarly, clinical observations indicate that individuals with FM exhibit significantly lower NK cell counts compared to healthy controls [46, 179]. Furthermore, RNA sequencing data from patients with low back pain demonstrated a marked increase in endogenous NK cells following pain relief, suggesting a potential link between NK cell activity and the alleviation of pain [180]. Collectively, these findings suggest that maintaining adequate NK cell levels may have therapeutic implications in the management of NP. Preclinical models provide further evidence supporting this hypothesis. In mouse models of traumatic nerve injury, NK cells mitigate NP by pruning aberrant connections formed at damaged nerve endings [181]. Additionally, cellular senescence and fibrosis, key drivers of NP, are associated with the upregulation of senescence-associated genes in the sciatic nerve following CCI [182]. NK cells may suppress NP progression by selectively targeting and eliminating senescent cells [183]. Beyond their direct effects, NK cells also influence NP indirectly by modulating the activity of other immune and glial cells, including DCs [184], neutrophils [185], CD4+ T cells and astrocytes [111].

Emerging evidence suggests that gut microbiota may regulate neuroimmune interactions in the CNS through NK cell mobilization. Experimental models demonstrate that, compared to GF mice, wild-type mice permit gut microbiota-mediated NK cells to migrate to the meninges and produce IFNγ, which binds to receptors on astrocyte terminals. This interaction triggers astrocyte-mediated T lymphocyte apoptosis, establishing an immunosuppressive CNS microenvironment in murine systems [111]. This newly identified gut-brain-immune axis highlights the capacity of the gut microbiota to remotely modulate neuroinflammation via NK cell orchestration, potentially opening new therapeutic avenues for CNP and other CNS disorders. Additionally, butyrate exhibits pronounced anti-inflammatory effects on human peripheral NK cells. Under butyrate exposure, NK cells demonstrate reduced activation receptor expression and diminished proinflammatory cytokine output, including IFNγ, TNF-α, and IL-22. Mechanistic studies attribute this functional suppression to downregulation of mTORC1 activity and c-Myc mRNA expression [65]. The conserved anti-inflammatory effects of butyrate across immune cell populations align with its broader immunomodulatory properties, further supporting its potential role in NP management.

Dendritic cells

DCs, specialized antigen-presenting cells bridging innate and adaptive immunity, maintain immunological homeostasis through their functional plasticity. Under physiological conditions, DCs exist in a quiescent state [186]. In the mice SNI model, nociceptive stimuli trigger the recruitment of immunogenic DC subsets to dorsal root lesion (DRL) [83], where they activate naïve T cells and initiate adaptive immune responses [187], thereby contributing to NP pathogenesis. Interestingly, aging-associated DC populations exhibit immunosuppressive properties, enhancing the production of anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-10 and TGF-β and promoting regulatory T cells (Treg) differentiation [188], suggesting a context-dependent regulatory role in pain modulation. Given that DCs regulate the balance between T-cell activation and tolerance, we propose that DCs have a unique role in NP. Nociceptors, the specialized peripheral sensory neurons that detect noxious stimuli [189], form functional neuroimmune units with myeloid DCs. Recent in vitro co-culture models have demonstrated that these nociceptor-DC complexes facilitate rapid pain signaling and coordinate innate-adaptive immune crosstalk, serving as an integrated early warning system for tissue damage [189].

In spinal nerve transection mice models, marked enrichment of Ruminococcaceae, Rikenellaceae, Oscillospira, and Parabacteroides has been observed [45]. Ruminococcus, a rhamnopolysaccharide-producing Firmicutes species, has been shown to potently induce TNF-α expression in DCs [190]. Given the known role of DC accumulation in the spinal DRL during NP progression [83], we hypothesize that post-neural injury proliferation of Ruminococcus may promote DC recruitment to dorsal root regions and subsequent TNF-α production, potentially constituting a novel mechanism in neuropathic pathogenesis.

Secondary bile acids modulate DC activity through anti-inflammatory mechanisms. DCA suppresses NF-κB-mediated proinflammatory cytokine release via TGR5 signaling in autoimmune uveitis models [129], while isoDCA enhances Forkhead box p-3 (FOXP3) expression, conferring DCs with Treg-promoting capacity [130]. Similarly, butyrate and propionate have been shown to suppress inflammation by reducing the release of pro-inflammatory factors and inhibiting LPS-induced IL-6 and IL-12 expression in DCs [113]. However, the effects of SCFAs on DCs are context-dependent - propionate has been reported to promote DC maturation and inflammatory progression via ACSL4-mediated ferroptosis in lymphoma models [191], highlighting the need for NP-specific investigations into SCFA-DC interactions.

Although current evidence suggests that gut microbiota metabolites regulate neuroinflammation through DC modulation, direct experimental validation in NP models remains absent. Our efforts are now focused on elucidating microbiota-DC-immune cell interactions in NP pathogenesis. Two key DC-mediated regulatory axes have emerged: 1) DCs-T cell axis: Gut dysbiosis activates NF-κB signaling in DCs, driving proinflammatory CD4+ T cell differentiation. Probiotic interventions, such as Lactobacillus plantarum restoration of tolerogenic DC function, promote Treg polarization and inflammation resolution [188]. Structural phospholipids from L. johnsonii N6.2 similarly induce Treg differentiation via bone marrow-derived DC modulation [192]. Additionally, secondary bile acids isoDCA and ω-muricholic acid enhance FOXP3 expression in naïve CD4+ T cells through DC-mediated mechanisms [130], a particularly relevant finding given the established neuroprotective effects of Tregs [193, 194]. 2) DC-astrocyte axis via Kyn pathway: IDO1+DC accumulation in dorsal root leptomeninges elevates spinal Kyn levels. Astrocytic metabolism through kynurenine-3-monooxygenase converts Kyn into 3-hydroxykynurenine, ultimately inhibiting NMDA receptors via classical Kyn pathway signaling to maintain NP [83]. This reveals a novel gut microbiota-regulated circuit connecting DCs, astroglial metabolism, and neuronal sensitization.

These findings establish gut microbiota-DC-immune axis modulation as a critical frontier in NP research. Future studies should prioritize: 1) Functional validation of DC-mediated mechanisms in NP models; 2) Elucidation of how DCs regulate other immune cells in immune homeostasis; 3) Temporal analysis of microbiota-DC crosstalk during pain chronification. Addressing these gaps may reveal novel therapeutic targets for this refractory condition.

Mast cells

Mast cells (MCs) originate from hematopoietic stem cells in the bone marrow or yolk sac and are predominantly located in tissues that interface with the external environment, such as the skin, gastrointestinal tract, and respiratory tract. As sentinel cells of the immune system, MCs act as a critical bridge between the immune and nervous systems during neuroinflammatory responses [195]. They are strategically positioned near vascular structures, peripheral nerve endings, and nociceptive neurons, and their cytoplasmic granules store a variety of inflammatory mediators, including histamine, proteases, and TNF-α [21, 196]. Through degranulation, MCs release these mediators and actively contribute to neurogenic inflammation and NP processes [197–199]. Studies using GF mouse models have demonstrated that gut microbiota modulate adult nociceptor thresholds by promoting NGF production in MCs [39]. In addition, MCs sustain neuroinflammation by recruiting neutrophils, macrophages, and T cells, which in turn contribute to secondary hyperalgesia [196]. MCs can also activate protease-activated receptor 2 (PAR2) on microglia through tryptase, leading to the release of TNF-α, IL-6, and ROS [200]. Increased IL-6 further induces MCs to release IL-13 and modulates TLR2/TLR4 expression [201] Elevated TNF-α upregulates PAR2 expression on MCs, thereby enhancing PAR2-mediated MC activation [202]. The involvement of MCs in neuropathic pain has been demonstrated in chemotherapy-induced models, including those triggered by vincristine [203], oxaliplatin [204], and paclitaxel [205]. Recent evidence from a DPNP mouse model reveals that sustained hyperglycemia induces endoplasmic reticulum stress and exacerbates mitochondrial oxidative stress, impairing MCs mitochondrial function. This dysfunction leads to MCs degranulation and the release of inflammatory factors such as histamine and tryptase into peripheral nerve endings, contributing to the development of diabetic neuropathy [206]. In a rat sciatica model, targeted reduction of PGE2 synthesis through nanomedicine approaches has been shown to specifically decrease MCs activation, effectively alleviating pain [207]. MCs are also present in the dura mater associated with meningeal nociceptors [208]. In preclinical migraine studies, MC degranulation has been shown to prolong the excitation state of meningeal nociceptors and activate the trigeminal pain pathway [209, 210]. These findings highlight the multifaceted role of MCs in neuroinflammatory and neuropathic pain conditions and underscore their potential as therapeutic targets.

When intestinal MCs are exposed to LPS, they undergo degranulation and release substantial amounts of TNF-α [211]. This upregulates the expression of TRPV1 and leads to the sensitization of nociceptive neurons [212] In neuroinflammatory cellular models, it has also been shown that LPS can induce MCs to increase TRPV1 expression [213]. Furthermore, LPS, acting in synergy with trypsin, stimulates MCs to secrete prostaglandin E2 (PGE2). This process downregulates the serotonin reuptake transporter (SERT) in the mucosa, resulting in elevated mucosal 5-HT levels and the development of abdominal pain. Such pain can be effectively relieved by restoring the LPS inhibitor produced by Rhodobacter sphaeroides [214]. SCFAs, particularly butyrate and propionate produced by the gut microbiota, are also closely linked to mast cell activation [215]. These SCFAs attenuate HDAC activity in both murine and human MCs, thereby reducing mast cell degranulation through epigenetic modifications [215]. In vitro studies using human and murine mast cell cultures have shown that propionate and butyrate inhibit both IgE- and non-IgE-mediated MCs degranulation in a concentration-dependent manner, a mechanism also involving HDAC activity [216]. However, direct evidence connecting MCs and gut microbiota in classical NP models remains limited, and robust data in this field are still lacking. Based on the current evidence, we propose that gut microbiota-derived LPS and SCFAs may influence mast cell activation and degranulation, providing a novel therapeutic avenue for NP associated with mast cell activation.

Finally, it is noteworthy that we have also focused on palmitoylethanolamide (PEA), an endogenous lipid primarily derived from egg yolk, soybean, and peanut oil, which is also present in mammalian tissues [217]. PEA can directly activate PPARα on the surface of microglia [218] or indirectly activate TRPV1 through PPARα [219], both pathways being closely associated with NP. Negative regulation of MC activation represents one of the key cellular mechanisms underlying the protective effects of PEA in vivo [220], including its capacity to counteract NP [221]. Additionally, PEA modulates tryptophan metabolism in the colon, reshaping serotonin (5-HT) turnover, reducing kynurenine levels, and increasing the abundance of butyrate-producing bacteria such as Bifidobacterium, Oscillospiraceae, and Turicibacter sanguinis [222]. We therefore propose that PEA represents an innovative molecule capable of modulating the analgesic activity of immune cells, including MCs, while simultaneously alleviating NP through both direct and indirect regulation of gut microbiota metabolism.

Adaptive immune system

B cells

The adaptive immune system exhibits intricate crosstalk with gut microbiota in NP pathogenesis. Dysregulated immune responses alter immune cell subtype composition and activation, ultimately influencing pain progression. While B lymphocytes have been implicated in NP, in the rat CCI model, female subjects exhibited marked endoneurial B cell infiltration [223]. Parallel observations in murine discogenic pain models demonstrated substantial B cell accumulation in both intervertebral discs and cerebrospinal fluid [224], collectively indicating a strong positive correlation between B cell proliferation and NP development across preclinical models. RNA sequencing analysis of juvenile mouse DRG in the CCI model revealed significant enrichment of B cell transcriptional signatures [225]. Mechanistic studies established that B cells contribute to mechanical allodynia through Fcγ receptor-mediated pathways. Strikingly, single-dose administration of anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody to deplete B cells completely prevented pain hypersensitivity development in mice [225]. This therapeutic strategy demonstrates translational potential, as evidenced by recent clinical findings where CD20+ B cell depletion effectively ameliorated both autoimmune manifestations and NP in paraneoplastic neurological syndrome patients [226].

These findings collectively support B cell-targeted therapies - particularly monoclonal antibody and Fcγ receptor blockade strategies - as promising investigational approaches for NP management. However, a critical knowledge gap remains regarding potential B cell-microbiome interactions in NP pathogenesis. Elucidation of this gut-immune-neural axis represents one of the most compelling yet challenging frontiers in pain research.

T lymphocytes

T cells are a specific group of immune cells originating from the bone marrow, which become activated and exert their effects in peripheral immune tissues [227]. T cells are categorized based on their receptors into CD4+ T cells and CD8+ T cells. The role of the T cell immune response in NP development and modulation is complex, involving dynamic interactions among various subtypes (Fig. 3). In mouse nociceptive pain models, depletion of CD4+ T cells results in prolonged pain hypersensitivity [228, 229]. Additionally, low doses of CCL4 stimulate mouse CD4+ T cells to release endogenous opioid peptides (Met-ENK), which activate peripheral δ-opioid receptors and result in systemic antinociception [230]. In the SNI model, Tregs infiltrate the injured nerve, DRG, and spinal cord, modulating the systemic cytokine profile. This modulation is partly achieved through upregulating IL-10 and downregulating RANTES, IL-5, and IL-2, ultimately relieving mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia [193, 231]. Tregs, a specialized subset of CD4+ T cells, are characterized by high expression of the transcription factor FOXP3 and the IL-2 receptor complex CD25 [232]. Depletion of Treg through CD25 antibody treatment [193] or FOXP3-DTR mice [231] leads to increased mechanical hypersensitivity following SNI. Similarly, previous studies have shown that miR-124a and miR-155, by targeting sirtuin −1, enhance FOXP3 expression, promoting the differentiation of CD4+ T cells into Treg and ultimately alleviating NP [233]. Additionally, activation of CD4+ T cells expressing adenosine A3 receptor results in IL-10 release, which reduces neuronal excitability and reverses NP [234]. These findings highlight the role of CD4+ T cells, particularly Tregs, in alleviating NP. However, in models of PNI, infiltrating CD4+ T cells have also been shown to promote and sustain NP [194], through the specific CD4+ T cell subtype responsible remains unidentified. The role of CD8+ T cells in NP is also of interest. In models of PNI by cisplatin, CD8+ T cells cultivated with cisplatin effectively mitigate the onset of chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy (CIPN), a mechanism involving IL-10, which promotes the maturation and functionality of memory CD8+ T cells [235]. Activation of the P2×7 purinergic receptor (P2RX7) promotes CD8+ T cell proliferation and activation, exacerbating NP. In vivo inhibition of P2RX7 reduces CD8+ T cell production and alleviates NP [20]. These findings suggest that CD8+ T cells exhibit strikingly divergent behaviors depending on the mediators involved, warranting further investigation into their mechanisms in NP.

Emerging evidence highlights a gut microbiota-dependent enrichment pattern of both CD4+ and CD8+ T cell populations [236]. Dysbiosis-induced aberrant T cell responses have been documented in autoimmune [237] and neurological disorders [238]. Notably, microbial modulation predominantly impacts CD4+ T cells, where microbial-derived SCFAs and secondary bile acids orchestrate T cell differentiation through surface receptor interactions, dynamically regulating Treg/Th17 cell balance [239, 240] and ultimately determining NP progression [193, 241]. In the CCI model, Treg depletion exacerbates NP by promoting spinal infiltration of Th1 and Th17 cells, whereas microbiota restoration alleviates pain by normalizing mice Treg levels and suppressing pro-inflammatory Th17 expansion [241].

The immunomodulatory mechanisms of SCFAs on CD4+ T cells involve (Table 1): 1) In mice model, Butyrate-induced Treg differentiation via enhanced histone acetylation at promoter regions and conservation of the FOXP3 locus in vitro and in vivo [62]; 2) Propionate-mediated FOXP3 and IL-10 upregulation through GPCR signaling correlates with enhanced mice Treg functionality [115]. Intriguingly, SCFAs selectively modulate gut-resident Tregs without affecting thymic or splenic populations. 3) GF mice exhibit colonic Treg developmental deficits, which can be reversed through microbial recolonization [239, 242]. This process is mediated by valeric acid, which regulates iron absorption, a key factor in mice Treg development [117]. 4) Faecalibacterium prausnitzii-derived valerate reprograms CD4+ T cell metabolism, promoting glucose oxidation for IL-10 production, while suppressing mice Th17 differentiation through HDAC inhibition [118]. 5) Butyrate facilitates the production of IL-10 in differentiated mice Th1 cells via GPCR activation [112]. Alternatively, it stimulates IL-22 production in CD4+ T cells through GPR-41 activation and HDAC inhibition [60]. 6) Butyrate also upregulates IL-22 through AhR and hypoxia-inducible factor 1α activation [60]. Notably, both IL-10 and IL-22 exert potent neuroprotective effects, with IL-10 playing a particularly critical role in Treg-mediated NP suppression [234, 243].

CD4+ T cells are further modulated by secondary bile acids derived from gut microbiota (Table 2): 1) 3-Oxo-lithocholic acid (3-oxoLCA) inhibits Th17 polarization by antagonizing retinoic acid receptor-related orphan receptor-γt (RORγt), the master transcription factor governing Th17 differentiation [131, 132]. Lithocholic acid 3-sulfate similarly suppresses Th17 differentiation via RORγt targeting [133]; 2) Ruminococcus gnavus-derived isolithocholic acid enhances mitochondrial ROS generation, promoting FOXP3+ Treg development [132, 134]; 3) A recently identified gut bacterium capable of synthesizing the bile acid metabolite isoalloLCA in the feces of patients with inflammatory bowel disease facilitates chromatin remodeling at the FOXP3 promoter, thereby promoting Treg differentiation [135]; 4) In vivo experiments demonstrated that genetically engineered Bacteroides enhance FOXP3 expression through the intermediary action of isoDCA on DCs, thereby increasing Treg populations. RNA sequencing analysis revealed that isoDCA treatment significantly reduces TNF-α and IL-6 production in response to TLR agonists [130] (Fig. 2); 5) Within DPNP, bile acids ameliorate pain by attenuating TRPV1 activation [15]. Sustained activation leads to a reduction in RORγ+ Tregs and disrupts their niche [244]. Bile acids, however, restore RORγ+ Treg cells to their baseline levels [15, 244]. Collectively, microbial-modified bile acids promote pro-inflammatory T cell polarization and gut immune tolerance, exerting beneficial effects on NP. Moreover, Clostridium ramosum, a potent inducer of RORγ+ Tregs [245], augments Treg populations within the intestinal milieu by decreasing neuronal cell body density and reducing nerve fiber caliber in the myenteric plexus, thereby attenuating the inhibitory influence of enteric neurons on Tregs [246]. This microbiota-neuro-immune axis provides mechanistic insights into how localized gut effects translate into systemic neuromodulation.

The gut microbiota influences NP by regulating T cell differentiation and inflammatory cytokine levels, particularly enhancing Treg activity and IL-10 production through microbial metabolites. Tregs play an important role in alleviating NP, with IL-10 serving as a key mediator of their beneficial effects [243]. Enhancing the function and activity of Tregs via microbial therapy represents a promising endogenous strategy to suppress inflammation and mitigate the severity of NP. Furthermore, the development of IL-10 agonists presents a potential therapeutic approach to reduce PNP.

Astrocytes

Astrocytes, the most abundant glial cell type in the CNS, are not classical immune cells but play a crucial role in neuroinflammation and the regulation of neuroinflammatory diseases within the gut-brain axis [247–250]. Originating from neural progenitor cells, astrocytes migrate along radial glial cells to populate the CNS, where they provide structural and metabolic support to neurons, and maintain CNS homeostasis by regulating extracellular ion composition and water balance [251–253]. In the context of NP, astrocytes actively contribute to nociceptive signal transmission within the spinal dorsal horn (SDH) [254–256]. Following nerve injury, reactive astrocytes proliferate and undergo significant morphological changes, including hypertrophy of processes and cell bodies, which are key mechanisms in sustaining NP [18]. These reactive astrocytes acquire a pro-inflammatory phenotype, characterized by the secretion of inflammatory mediators and chemokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, CCL7, and matrix metalloproteinase 2, thereby exacerbating neuroinflammation [257, 258] (Fig. 3). Extensive evidence highlights astrocytes as key contributors to both the development and maintenance of NP [254–256, 259, 260]. Several mechanisms have been implicated in mediating this process. 1) Aquaporin-4 (AQP4), precisely localized at the endfeet of astrocytes, plays a critical role in their activation [261]. Recent studies demonstrate that activation of the NFAT5–AQP4 signaling pathway in the CCI model induces astrocyte swelling in the SDH, which not only initiates but also perpetuates NP. Targeting this signaling pathway presents a promising therapeutic strategy for alleviating NP [251]. Similarly, in the CCI model, intrathecal injection of phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) has been demonstrated to inhibit astrocyte activation, preventing NP onset [262]. 2) Ion channel dysregulation is another critical factor in NP. Elevated extracellular K+ levels enhance neuronal excitability, a major driver of hyperalgesia [263]. Astrocytes that overexpress the inward rectifier potassium channel Kir4.1 can enhance neuronal excitability, thereby exacerbating hyperalgesia [252]. 3) In a juvenile rat model, transient optogenetic stimulation of astrocytes has been shown to induce mechanical allodynia within a short period [264]. 4) Astrocyte activation enhances NMDA receptor function on spinal cord neurons by increasing the secretion of D-serine, thereby promoting central sensitization [265].

The interaction between gut microbiota and astrocytes plays a pivotal role in NP regulation, especially through tryptophan metabolism: 1) Gut microbiota-derived tryptophan metabolites, including indole, indoxyl-3-sulfate, indole-3-propionic acid, and indole-3-aldehyde, synthesized by tryptophanase, have been shown to regulate astrocyte activity [266]. By engaging AhR on microglia, these metabolites upregulate transforming growth factor TGF-α, leading to suppressed astrocyte-mediated pro-inflammatory cascades [90]. This pathway presents a potential therapeutic target for CNP. Similarly, supplementation with Lactobacillus or SCFAs mimics this mechanism by enhancing AhR ligand levels, thereby attenuating astrocyte hyperactivity [119]. 2) Kynurenine-3-monooxygenase, an enzyme expressed by brain astrocytes, converts Kyn into 3-hydroxykynurenine, a process that exacerbates neuronal injury and sustains NP [83].

LPS is a potent activator of astrocytes, contributing to the pathogenesis of NP. In mouse CIPN models, irinotecan administration compromises intestinal barrier integrity, allowing LPS translocation, inducing spinal astrocyte proliferation and exacerbating NP [267]. In paclitaxel-induced peripheral neuropathy, LPS engages TLR4 signaling on astrocytes, leading to the activation of the NF-κB and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathways, culminating in the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines [268]. FMT (Rat) targeting astrocytes and the TLR4/MAPK axis has demonstrated therapeutic potential in the treatment of pain [269] (Fig. 2). Furthermore, impairment of astrocyte autophagy is linked to NP. Activation by LPS of MAPK signaling and the NF-κB pathways suppresses autophagy in astrocytes, exacerbating NP [260]. This raises the possibility that FMT may mitigate NP by restoring astrocyte autophagy, making it another promising target of research inquiry.

Astrocyte activation is a key mechanism of NP maintenance, with chronically activated astrocytes exhibiting remarkable stability in persistent pain states. Given this, targeting specific tryptophan metabolic pathways within gut microbiota or modulating the gut microenvironmental to reduce LPS exposure could serve as an immunological strategy for chronic pain management. Although the full spectrum of astrocyte activation mechanisms in NP remains incompletely understood, identifying key astrocyte activation checkpoints in chronic pain conditions offers a promising avenue for future therapeutic innovations.

Sex differences in neuropathic pain

Epidemiological studies have increasingly highlighted pronounced sex differences in NP prevalence, with women demonstrating significantly higher susceptibility than men across multiple clinical cohorts [270, 271]. This disparity is particularly pronounced in chronic NP populations, where female patients are disproportionately overrepresented [270]. Despite these observations, mechanistic investigations into sex differences have been historically neglected in biomedical research. A key contributor to this was the predominant reliance on male-only animal models in neuroscience, a trend that increased between 2010 and 2014 [270]. This sex bias, likely stemming from methodological inertia and concerns over data variability introduced by sex hormone fluctuations in female subjects, necessitates urgent correction given its translational implications for sex-specific therapeutic efficacy in pain management. While not all NP mechanisms exhibit sexual dimorphism [272], accumulating evidence suggests that distinct pathophysiological pathways operate in females, with spinal neuroimmune interactions emerging as key modulators of these differences. This section focuses on these immune-mediated sex differences, although sex-specific pain mechanisms extend beyond this specific domain.

Current mechanistic frameworks, predominantly derived from male rodent models, highlight TLR4 as a central regulator of NP pathogenesis in males. Gut microbiota-derived lipid metabolites and bile acid derivatives modulate TLR4 signaling, coordinating immune responses involving macrophages, microglia, and astrocytes, to regulate pain phenotypes in male models [71, 268, 269]. Although no significant sex differences in TLR4 expression levels have been reported [273], functional studies suggest that this signaling pathway is largely inactive in female mice [274]. Experimental findings further demonstrate that TLR4-mediated modulation of pain behaviors in male mice is partially testosterone-dependent and particularly reliant on free dihydrotestosterone [275]. Pharmacological inhibition of microglial activity effectively reverses NP in male rodents but shows limited efficacy in females [275–277]. This divergence is linked to male-specific upregulation of spinal microglial P2×4 receptors [278] and caspase-6 [279] activity post-neuronal injury. Beyond TLR4 signaling, the TLR9 pathway in CIPN models also exhibits distinct sexual dimorphism through macrophage-mediated mechanisms. Genetic ablation of TLR9 attenuates mechanical allodynia in male mice by suppressing TNF and CXCL1 upregulation in both macrophage cultures and DRG tissues [280]. In contrast, female NP pathophysiology is driven by an alternative, TLR9-independent mechanism involving macrophage-TRPV-1 signaling [281].

Unlike the microglia-dependent mechanisms observed in males, NP pathophysiological mechanisms in females involve increased CD4+ T cell infiltration into the SDH [282], with significantly greater T cell accumulation in female DRG compared to males [283]. Tregs demonstrate enhanced pain-attenuating effects in females through immunosuppressive mechanisms [284]. Additionally, IL-16 represents a female-specific mechanism that promotes astrocyte activation in the SDH through CD4+ T cell-mediated pathways, sustaining NP in female mice following spinal nerve ligation. Notably, this pathway operates independently of microglial activation [194]. These findings suggest that IL-16-associated immune signaling pathways hold promise as a target of NP treatment in women. Macrophages in female mice also provide higher levels of IL-23 and IL-17A, while TRPV1 sensory neurons in female mice demonstrate greater sensitivity compared to males [281]. The crosstalk between macrophages and TRPV1 sensory neurons contributes to the sex-specific generation of mechanical pain, potentially explaining the greater pain susceptibility that is observed in women.

Gut microbiota modulation and sex-specific immune responses must be considered in the development of NP therapies. SCFAs exhibit sexually dimorphic effects: butyrate enhances BDNF expression in female astrocytes via HDAC inhibition, potentially exacerbating nociceptive sensitivity, whereas, in males, acetate upregulates AhR in astrocytes, ameliorating pain [114]. The butyrate pathway appears to predispose females to increased sensitivity to NP, whereas AhR upregulation in males suppresses pain. Consequently, therapeutic supplementation with SCFAs for NP requires a careful evaluation of sex as a key factor. Despite these differences, certain microbiome interventions, such as antibiotic-induced gut microbiota depletion, show comparable efficacy in preventing NP across both sexes [45].

The longstanding male bias in NP research represents a small part of a broader issue. Future research must prioritize sex-stratified study designs and relevant analyses to fully elucidate sex-specific mechanisms and develop more targeted, effective, and safe therapeutic interventions.

Future directions and challenges

In clinical practice, there remains a lack of effective methodologies to guide physicians in tailoring treatments to individual patients, prompting an increasing focus on personalized therapeutic approaches for NP. Integrating gut microbiota and immune mechanisms into the diagnosis and treatment of NP holds promise, but significant challenges must be addressed to translate these findings to the clinic.

Biomarker

Identifying predictive and prognostic biomarkers is critical for establishing baseline criteria in clinical trials and improving outcome assessment. This is particularly important for patients with NP with comorbid conditions, such as DPNP, where diagnosis can prove challenging. TRPV1 has been recognized as a key ion channel in the sensory system for pain and neurogenic inflammation signaling, with potential as a biomarker for CNP [285]. In CIPN models, serum neurofilament light chain, a neuronal structural protein, correlates with chemotherapy-induced neuronal damage, suggesting its potential as a prognostic indicator [286]. Additionally, epoxy fatty acids derived from intestinal PUFAs, even at low concentrations, can stimulate neurons by modulating ion channels. The lipid diols formed from different molecular structures of epoxy fatty acids have been found to exert either pain-alleviating or pain-promoting effects, positioning PUFAs as a potential biomarker for NP [287]. A recent case report leveraged invasive intracranial electrodes to obtain dynamic, continuous neurophysiological data, offering real-time insights into refractory NP [288]; However, due to the invasive nature of this procedure, its clinical applicability is limited. Despite promising biomarker candidates, none have been incorporated into clinical guidelines or achieved expert consensus, underscoring the need for further validation and refinement in future research.

Comorbidity

In the realm of clinical diagnostics and treatment, NP frequently manifests as a comorbidity of numerous underlying conditions [30], with a notably high prevalence [5]. In many cases, the burden of NP surpasses that of the primary disease, underscoring the need for a deeper understanding of its shared pathological mechanisms and potential therapeutic targets. For instance, individuals with diabetes frequently exhibit gut microbiota dysbiosis, and probiotic supplementation has been shown to restore intestinal balance while improving glycemic control [31]. This effect is mediated through the upregulation of TGR5 expression by gut microbiota, which enhances glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) secretion and increases energy expenditure, ultimately improving glucose homeostasis [289]. Furthermore, probiotic therapy significantly reverses neural fiber damage, restores neural excitability, and ameliorates DPNP by inhibiting the activation of the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling pathway in the sciatic nerve [290]. Additionally, exogenous glycoursodeoxycholic acid [291] has been found to enhance pancreatic beta cell function, while gut microbiota-derived secondary bile acids contribute to the alleviation of DPNP by modulating TRPV1 [15]. Additionally, secondary bile acids can alleviate other forms of NP by modulating TGR5 on microglial cell membranes [76]. These findings suggest that gut microbiota-targeted interventions may offer a promising approach for the prevention and treatment of diabetes-related NP [291]. Future endeavors may consider exploring the application of gut microbiota in the treatment mechanisms of diabetes and PNP, identifying specific probiotics suitable for diabetic patients or developing targeted pharmaceuticals.

When examining the interplay between gut microbiota and T-cell immunity, studies have shown that T-cells promote neural recovery in mouse stroke models [292]. Additionally, in mouse models of depression, T-cell deficiencies have been linked to prolonged depressive-like behaviors [293]. These findings indicate that T-cell immunity may help regulate recovery from post-stroke headaches and treatment-resistant NP in depression. This raises the possibility that modulation of T-cell differentiation and immune tolerance through gut microbiota-targeted therapies may serve as a potential therapeutic strategy. Additionally, disruptions in the Kyn metabolic pathway, which influence NP through the activation of DCs and astrocytes, have been linked to depression, Parkinson’s disease, and schizophrenia [294–296]. Elevated Kyn levels in the CNS contribute to neuronal dysfunction and damage, suggesting that targeting of this pathway, through immunotherapies or gut microbiota interventions, could offer a promising therapeutic avenue.

Investigating the shared mechanisms between NP and its comorbidities through the lens of gut microbiota and immune interactions may provide critical insights into shared pathological processes and related therapeutic directions. This approach could support earlier identification of patients at risk for multiple conditions, inform combination treatment strategies, and guide the development of novel therapeutic targets.

Translational

Despite significant advances in understanding the role of gut microbiota in NP, translating these findings into workable clinical applications is challenging. In CIPN mouse models, reductions in Akkermansia muciniphila levels have been observed, and supplementation with this bacterium as part of a probiotic formulation was found to prevent paclitaxel-induced neurotoxicity [297]. Similarly, FMT therapy has shown promise in alleviating PNP by modulating the TLR4 pathway [229]. In the mouse SCI model, Firmicutes, Blautia, Anaerotruncus, Christensenellaceae, and Butyricimonas were shown to be potentially beneficial components of FMT, significantly increasing fecal propionate and butyrate levels, thereby ameliorating neural damage [43]. The efficacy of gut microbiota-related therapies has been demonstrated across multiple classic pain models; however, translating these findings from laboratory research to the clinic has several challenges.

We must consider the following key factors: 1) The heterogeneity of the human microbiome across different geographic regions [298]. Do microbial formulations or FMT therapies used in treatment regimens share conserved genes or functional pathways? Establishing the universal applicability of gut microbiota therapies is essential for enabling the industrial production of targeted probiotic formulations. 2) Most preclinical data indicate that NP is strongly associated with elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α and IL-6, with inhibitors effectively reducing pain hypersensitivity [299, 300]. However, no pharmacological agents are currently available that specifically target NP through immunomodulation. For microbiota-based immunotherapeutic strategies to become a viable approach, we must integrate immune mechanisms regulated by gut microbiota and reassess how the gut immune environment influences the efficacy of inhibitors and agonists, ensuring that these agents can achieve their intended therapeutic effects. 3) We must also consider the impact of microbial therapies on existing NP treatments, particularly their interaction with opioid analgesics. Opioid use has been shown to disrupt gut motility and compromise intestinal epithelial integrity, contributing to gut microbiota dysbiosis [301–303]. Probiotic formulations or FMT therapies can mitigate opioid resistance and dependence [301], while also improving mental health issues associated with chronic pain [304]. Current evidence indicates that microbial therapies may serve as a complementary approach in opioid-based treatment of NP [303]. 4) The clinical effectiveness of gut metabolite therapies and dietary interventions. Preclinical studies suggest that SCFAs significantly alleviate NP, with butyrate acting via various immune-mediated mechanisms. However, SCFAs are readily absorbed by intestinal epithelial cells, and previous studies on ulcerative colitis have shown that SCFA enemas provide more effective outcomes than other routes of administration [305]. Determining the optimal delivery method and dosage control is a critical step that must be addressed before microbiota-based therapies can be successfully implemented in clinical practice. Recent findings from a large-scale population study revealed that increasing daily intake of caffeine and caffeic acid can result in an over 8-fold increase in the abundance of butyrate-producing bacteria, specifically Lactobacillus asaccharolyticus and Akkermansia muciniphila, thereby significantly reducing the risk of neuroinflammation-associated disorders [306].

Conclusions

In our review of the immunological mechanisms of gut microbiota in NP, we have comprehensively explored this broad topic. However, certain limitations and constraints are inevitably present. Firstly, we have not included all immune cell types, such as neutrophils and B cells, due to insufficient evidence to support their discussion in the context of NP. Nonetheless, their importance cannot be overlooked, and future studies may shed light on their potential roles. Secondly, although our focus was initially on NP, the complexity and multifactorial nature of pain led us to discuss other pain types and even non-pain conditions. This expansion has somewhat reduced the strength of the evidence, leaving us to rely on assumptions and inferences. Therefore, future research should employ more specific NP models to validate these hypotheses. Lastly, while DCs and MC play crucial roles in NP, the evidence linking them to gut microbiota remains insufficient, limiting our ability to thoroughly explore this mechanism. In conclusion, while our review provides valuable insights based on current evidence, further research is needed to enhance our understanding of this intricate field.

The management of NP requires the development of more effective therapeutic strategies that provide substantial pain relief while minimizing adverse effects and abuse potential. Growing evidence supports the role of gut microbiota in regulating NP through immune-related mechanisms, highlighting its potential therapeutic target. Metabolites derived from gut microbiota, such as SCFAs and secondary bile acids, modulate neuroinflammation and nociceptive processing by regulating macrophage polarization, microglial activation, T-cell differentiation, and astrocyte activation. Furthermore, gut microbiota contributes to immune regulation in NP via interactions with NK cells and DCs. These discoveries present promising opportunities, including probiotics, dietary interventions, and metabolite supplementation, aimed at restoring immune homeostasis and alleviating NP. Notably, immune cell involvement in sex-based differences in pain underscores the need for sex-specific treatment strategies. Despite these promising findings, several challenges must be addressed before these interventions can be implemented in clinical practice. These include addressing microbiome variability, determining optimal administration methods, and managing comorbidities. Future research should focus on identifying reliable biomarkers, validating mechanistic findings in human cohorts, and integrating microbiota-based therapies with existing NP treatments to maximize their clinical potential.

It is anticipated that future studies will lead to the development of gut microbiota-based diagnostic and therapeutic strategies for NP, rooted in immune mechanisms, or alternatively, safe and effective immunotherapies informed by gut microbial interactions. Advancements in both immunological and microbiological research are essential to properly elucidate the contributions of immune mechanisms and the gut microbiota to human health. Moreover, many microbial molecules and immune mechanisms remain unexplored—hidden dialogues yet to be uncovered—that may also play significant roles in the modulation of NP.
