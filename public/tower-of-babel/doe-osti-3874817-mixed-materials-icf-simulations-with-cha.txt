3.4 Source Material Assignment
To ensure newly created particles are correctly assigned to their birth material, we properly initial-
ized the material tracking array for source particles. We established a new file containing routines
that initialize material tracking for particles generated by various source models.
 When the code generates new particles from a source, whether it’s a user saying "put 1000 alpha
particles at this radius with this energy" or a thermonuclear burn calculation determining that a
fusion reaction just occurred at a specific location, the particles are first created with their basic
properties. But, their material handles are initially undefined. The source material assignment
routine processes these newly created particles before they begin transport. For each new particle,
the routine looks up the particle’s initial side index, then broadcasts this side to all materials using
the same broadcast routine described in the previous subsection. The material that owns that side
responds with its handle, and this handle is stored in the particle’s material tracking array entry.

3.5 Integrated Raytracing with Material Switching
With all the material tracking software development, the last thing to do was integrate material
tracking into the primary particle transport loop, enabling dynamic material switching during trans-
port. We modified the core raytracing routine to include material switching logic throughout the
particle substep calculation. This is where all the previous components come together to create
fully coupled mixed-material transport.
 The raytracing loop processes each particle through global timesteps via subcycling, which is how
each particle takes many small substeps to traverse its assigned distance for the current timestep.
For each substep, the algorithm must determine how long that substep should be computing several
competing physical timescales and choosing the shortest one. The original code computed four com-
peting timescales. We added a fifth competing timescale by calling the stochastic interface sampling
routine. This mixing timestep asks "how long until this particle crosses a material interface?" using
the CLS algorithm.
 After computing all five timescales, the algorithm selects the minimum. This determines what
physical process limits the substep. The particle then advances. Its position updates based on its
velocity and the substep time. Its energy decreases based on the stopping power and the substep
time. Finally, its velocity updates to reflect the energy loss.
 After advancing the particle, the algorithm checks which timescale was limiting. We added
checks if the mixing timestep was smallest. This means the particle has crossed a material in-
terface before any other event occurred. When this happens, the algorithm sets a flag indicating
a material switch is occurring, then broadcasts the particle’s current side index to all materials
using the broadcast routine we developed to query which material owns a particular mesh side.
When the material ownership query returns, the algorithm compares the returned material handle
to the particle’s current material tracking array value. If they’re different, a material switch has
occurred. The algorithm updates the particle’s material tracking array to the new material handle.
When a material switch occurs, several material-dependent physics quantities must be recalculated
immediately, before the next substep begins.

3.6 ICF Toy Model Development
We developed 1D and 2D toy models of the double-shell ICF capsules for future validation and
testing of our implementation. The 1D results are omitted in this report for brevity, however. The
test problems consisted of simplified multi-layer capsule geometries designed to capture the essential
physics of double-shell ICF capsules.

3.6.1 Without Coupled Material Mixing and Transport Implementation
To investigate the impact of material mixing on ICF capsule performance, we employed a La-
grangian shock-hydrodynamics code. The simulations were conducted in two-dimensions (2D) with
axisymmetric geometry to capture the transient hydrodynamic behavior and establish baseline per-
formance characteristics before enabling material mixing mechanisms. The total simulation time
extends to 10 ns with adaptive timestepping starting from an initial timestep 10 ps.
 We developed a simplified toy model of a double-shell ICF capsule with the following layer
structure from inside to outside: DT fuel → Tungsten → Glow Discharge Polymer (GDP)
→ Foam → Aluminum. The detailed geometric and material properties of each layer are sum-
marized in Table 3-1. The configuration consists of central DT fuel core (50:50 deuterium-tritium
atomic fraction, mf uel = 180 ng) surrounded by a tungsten pusher, GDP tamper, low-density foam
cushion, and outer aluminum ablator shell. In this configuration, the beryllium layer used in [2] was
eliminated to focus specifically on tungsten-DT fuel mixing in a more simplified manner. This con-
figuration represents a typical double-shell target design where tungsten serves as a high-Z pusher
providing inertial confinement, GDP acts as a tamper layer, foam provides a cushion for shock
smoothing, and aluminum forms the outer ablator shell.

impact of material mixing mechanisms and implementing a simplified model rather than achieving
exact predictive accuracy for a specific target design.
 To enable hydrodynamic mixing between high-Z pusher and low-Z fuel, we implemented a spe-
cialized "mixed gas" material region that spatially overlays both the DT fuel core (r = 0 to
214 µm) and the tungsten pusher layer (r = 214 µm to 262 µm). This mixed gas region is con-
figured as a two-phase material system in the code’s material database, containing: (1) a DT gas
phase with properties inherited from the fuel material definition, and (2) a tungsten phase with
properties inherited from the pusher material definition. In the baseline simulation presented here,
although the mixed gas infrastructure is fully defined in the input deck (noregion = 1), it occupies
zero physical volume and remains inactive, these two phases exist as distinct materials in the sim-
ulation. This configuration establishes the necessary computational framework for the multiphase
BHR3 mixing model while providing an unmixed reference case. The physical expectation is that
under extreme compression, strong density gradients, and hydrodynamic instabilities (particularly
Rayleigh-Taylor instabilities at the pusher-fuel interface), these initially distinct material layers
(DT and tungsten pusher) would undergo turbulent interpenetration and atomic-scale diffusion.
When mixing is enabled in subsequent simulations, the BHR3 model will activate at t = 1 ns and
dynamically compute volume fractions of each phase throughout the mixed gas region. This two-
phase framework enables comparison between mixed and unmixed implosion dynamics to assess the
impact of high-Z material mixing on capsule performance.
 The simulation was initialized with uniform temperatures of 20K across all material layers.
The energy deposition mimics the indirect-drive approach used in ICF experiments at facilities like
the National Ignition Facility (NIF). In this setup, energy is deposited from the outer layers and
propagates inward through the capsule layers toward the DT core region. A total drive energy of

a series of convergent shock waves. This outside-in energy deposition drives the compression of
inner layers, heating and compressing the DT fuel core toward fusion conditions. The hohlraum-
like energy drive ensures that our toy model captures the radiation-driven implosion dynamics
and X-ray effects observed in NIF ICF capsule experiments. The simulation employs Arbitrary
Lagrangian-Eulerian (ALE) mesh optimization with volume relaxation to maintain mesh quality

 The computational mesh employs a mesh that transitions from a Cartesian grid to a radial
grid inside the DT region. This specifically has a 2D axisymmetric geometry with three radial
zones per layer, yielding 18 zones total spanning the 1.11 mm capsule radius. Geometric functions
define sphere boundaries at each layer interface, and Boolean operations construct material regions.
Critically, a mixed gas region was defined, overlapping spatially with the pusher layer to enable
multi-phase physics where mixing generates interpenetration of tungsten and DT fuel.
 The fuel material is configured as a gas-phase substance using finite-volume polynomial ele-
ments with Barton artificial viscosity for shock capturing. Its EOS is specified through SESAME
table 5267 (deuterium) as there is no EOS specified for deuterium-tritium. This provides simplified
thermodynamically-consistent pressure and energy data closely related for deuterium-tritium mix-
tures across the liquid-gas-plasma phase space encountered during compression and heating. The
fuel composition is exactly 50-50 atomic fraction DT, specified via ZAID identifiers, optimizing fu-

thermonuclear burn module is configured with NDI nuclear data library and explicitly enables the

Beryllium used a pure Be-9 isotopic composition while tungsten used a W-184 composition. Both
materials employ stress-temperature-rho-energy initialization at 20 K and include non-equilibrium
temperature capability and grey radiation coupling similar to the fuel region.
 The mixed gas material represents the core innovation enabling our coupled mixing-transport
capability. Defined as a mixed gas, it initially occupies zero mesh volume but activates wherever
the BHR3 turbulent mixing model generates non-trivial volume fractions of multiple phases. More
specifically, when the mixed gas activates, the two DT and tungsten materials are both deleted
from the simulation and replaced by a single mixed gas material that models them and allows them
to diffuse into each other. Additionally, it uses a coupled pressure-temperature equilibrium model,
enforcing mechanical and thermal equilibrium between phases within each mixed zone while allowing
spatial variations in composition. The model activates at a start time of 1 nanosecond, shortly
before peak compression and remains active until a stop time of 100 seconds. Within the mixed
gas, two distinct phases coexist: a gas-phase fuel component and a solid-phase pusher component.
The fuel phase inherits the SESAME EOS 5267, 50-50 DT isotopic composition, non-equilibrium
temperatures, grey radiation, and fusion reaction capabilities from the pure fuel material definition.
The pusher phase inherits the gamma-law EOS and W-184 composition as before. Both phases
are initialized with placeholder density and temperature values that are overridden once the BHR3
model computes spatially-varying volume fractions throughout the mixed region. The important
physics linkage occurs when fusion-born alpha particles are created in the mixed gas material via
the SINGE module. These particles carry the material tracking array handles pointing to the mixed
gas material, and our CLS routine uses a placeholder chord length, stochastically switching particles
between fuel-phase and pusher-phase properties at exponentially distributed intervals along their
trajectories.
 The GDP tamper and CH foam cushion both use the SESAME EOS 7834 with SESAME opacity
table 17834. Both materials have identical isotopic composition with 50% carbon-12 and 50%
hydrogen-1 by atom fraction, but have vastly different densities. The tamper’s density represents
solid polymer while the foam’s density is a highly porous open-cell structure to differentiate the two
regions. The aluminum ablator employs SESAME EOS 3720 and SESAME opacity 13719, with
pure Al-27 composition and solid density. Its initialization is unique, where instead of specifying
temperature, the ablator is initialized with specific internal energy computed as the the drive divided
by the mass. The drive in this case is 1.25 MW, representing the peak laser power. This energy is
deposited according to a time-dependent energy rate table that ramps linearly from zero at time =
0 to a peak rate of two times the drive divided by 1e-12 of wattage at a time of 1 picosecond. Then
it immediately drops to zero, creating a triangular pulse that delivers approximately 2.5 MJ total
energy over 1 picosecond. This rapid volumetric heating drives ablative pressure that launches the
implosion. The global radiation transport system uses grey diffusion with flux limiter multiplier set
to zero, enabling full diffusion-speed propagation in thick regions. Kramer opacity fits were also

4 Conclusion and Future Work
This work represents a significant advancement in multiphysics ICF simulation capability by suc-
cessfully coupling charged particle transport with material mixing. We developed and validated a
stochastic interface sampling algorithm based on the Levermore-Pomraning Chord Length Sampling
model, implementing universal material tracking, dynamic material switching logic, and integration
with existing physics calculations. The software can now be used to explore and understand the
physical phenomena occurring during material mixing between the fuel and high-Z shell materials.
Furthermore, we developed working toy models based on double-shell ICF capsule designs, which
lay the foundation for future validation and simulations of ICF experiments.

4.1 Near-Term Development Priorities
While the broadcast query architecture between the CLS subroutine and BHR3 codes is imple-
mented, we are currently encountering MPI communication errors when running simulations with
BHR3 active. Resolving these parallel computing issues is a high priority, as they prevent validation
of whether the turbulent length scale field correctly propagates to particle transport calculations.
Once stable, we must verify that the chord length varies spatially across zones as expected, the tur-
bulent lengths evolve during instability growth, and the coupling remains numerically stable across
the full parameter space of the double shell implosions.
 Additionally, our 2D axisymmetric toy model provided essential validation but will most likely
not capture all observed results in experiments. Extending the capability to 3D meshes requires
verifying that material handles and chord length sampling remain consistent across zones, meshes,
and boundaries. We should target near-term achievable 3D simulations first using simplified geome-
tries, progressively increasing geometric fidelity and resolution as we gain confidence in scalability
and physics accuracy.
 LANL and LLNL have also accumulated extensive experimental data on capsules where mixing
is suspected but not definitively measured. We should identify benchmark shots where diagnostic
data is sufficiently rich to constrain simulation inputs and enable quantitative comparison. Running
our coupled mix-transport capability on these shot geometries with realistic drive histories will test
whether predicted degraded mechanisms, such as reduced fuel heating and cooled hotspot, match
observed signatures. Discrepancies will guide model refinement (e.g., if simulations under-predict
mix losses), potentially resulting in the addition of physics like species separation or shock-driven
atomic mixing.

4.2 Long-Term Vision and Impact
Understanding mixing is only valuable if it informs design changes that preserve ignition conditions.
We should conduct systematic parameter studies exploring: (1) optimized layer thickness ratios to
minimize Rayleigh-Taylor growth rates while maintaining sufficient convergence; (2) engineered
density gradients; (3) modified drive pulse shapes for enhanced stability; and (4) alternative pusher
materials that reduce mix-induced transport losses even if some mixing occurs. Each strategy
require simulation runs spanning parameter space to map performance surfaces. This would be
computationally expensive but feasible with current resources.
 We could also look into coupled atomic physics in mixed regions. Our current implementation
assumes instantaneous atomic equilibrium after material switches. When a particle enters tung-

sten, we immediately compute ionization states and electron density. In reality, atomic relaxation
timescales may be comparable to or longer than mixing timescales in rapidly evolving interfaces.
This can lead to transient non-equilibrium stopping power where tungsten atoms in DT fuel may
remain partially ionized for finite time, or vice versa. Implementing non-equilibrium atomic ki-
netics coupled to material switching may refine stopping power predictions, particularly in high-Z
materials where ionization state strongly affects stopping power. This likely requires coupling to ex-
isting LANL atomic physics capabilities through database exchange or from developing new atomic
solvers.
 Overall, LANL’s double shell program represents one of several alternative pathways to ignition
beyond NIF’s baseline design. But it’s one of many concepts, and it’s possible that applying our
coupled mixing-transport capability across the diverse concepts to identify which designs are most
robust to mixing-induced degradation. This would potentially redirect programmatic focus toward
inherently stable configurations. For now, our coupled mixing-transport capability establishes the
foundational work for a new generation of ICF simulations that accurately predict the complex,
multi-scale interplay between hydrodynamic instabilities, material mixing, and charged particle
transport. Ultimately, we have enabled more confident performance predictions and more effective
experimental campaign planning as LANL pursues alternative pathways to achieving robust, high-
yield fusion ignition that can transition to practical fusion energy applications.
