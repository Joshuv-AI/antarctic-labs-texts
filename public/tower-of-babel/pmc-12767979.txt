A comparison of upper versus lower extremity rhabdomyosarcoma survival: A SEER database analysis
Authors: Connor J. Tupper, Laura M. Cogua, Harvey Y. Wang, Peter T. Silberstein
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRhabdomyosarcoma (RMS) of the extremities has a particularly poor prognosis compared to other primary sites due to an increased rate of alveolar histology, higher rate of metastasis, and the extent of regional lymph node involvement. To date there are few assessments comparing upper extremity (UE) to lower extremity (LE) RMS of the extremities using population-based registry, so we sought to compare survival between UE and LE RMS.Materials and methodsUsing the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database, cases of RMS of the UE and LE diagnosed between 2000–2020 were collected. Descriptive statistics and chi-square analyses were completed for one-and five-year survival. Log-rank and Cox regression analyses were completed to compare UE versus LE survival.ResultsA total of 641 cases were included, of which 221 (34.5%) were UE and 420 (65.5%) were LE. On log-rank tests, UE survival was longer than LE survival (p = 0.021). The one-year survival rate was greater for the UE (88.7%) compared to the LE (81.4%) (p = 0.020) but similar at five-years. Cox regression analysis showed no difference in survival between UE and LE primary site (hazard ratio = 1.172, p = 0.322).ConclusionsIn comparing UE and LE RMS survival, UE survival was greater at one-year, but not on adjusted analyses. These findings contribute to the few prior assessments of outcomes between UE and LE RMS, though direct comparisons between UE and LE should be included in future prospective studies.

Introduction

Rhabdomyosarcoma (RMS) is a rare malignant soft tissue sarcoma, presenting on histology as a mass of undifferentiated rhabdomyocytes [1]. RMS is the most common soft tissue sarcoma in children and adolescents, accounting for over half of such cases [2]. The incidence of RMS is around 4.3–4.4 cases per one million in the pediatric population and around 400–500 new cases annually [3, 4]. RMS occurs most commonly in the trunk, head/neck, genitourinary system, and extremities [5]. Classification by histology includes several subtypes such as embryonal, alveolar, and pleomorphic, with embryonal and alveolar being the most prevalent in pediatric populations [6].

Genetic mutations and chromosomal abnormalities have been linked to the development of RMS, particularly translocations between the PAX3/FOXO1 and PAX7/FOXO1 genes [6–9]. The fusion of these genes drives the malignant phenotype of RMS and their presence creates a biologically, and clinically, more aggressive tumor than fusion-negative RMS [6, 7, 9, 10]. Aside from metastatic disease, fusion status is the second most important prognostic factor in RMS, followed by histologic type [7, 8]. Evaluation of fusion status is crucial for risk stratification and treatment planning [6, 8, 11, 12].

RMS of the extremities has a particularly poor prognosis relative to other primary sites [13]. This discrepancy has been attributed to an increased rate of alveolar histology, higher rate of metastasis, and the extent of regional lymph node involvement [11, 14, 15]. While these have been established with previous research, to date there have been few assessments of RMS of the extremities using population-based registry aside from three analyses of pediatric, surgical, and metastatic cases, respectively [16–18]. The primary goal of this project is to compare cancer-specific survival (CSS) between upper extremity (UE) and lower extremity (LE) RMS using the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database. The secondary goals were to determine variables associated with survival differences over time and at one- and five years after diagnosis.

Materials and methods

A retrospective cohort study of the SEER database was performed. The SEER database is a United States national registry comprised of data from smaller regional or state registries that, when combined, account for around 47% of cancer diagnoses in the United States annually [19].

Cases of RMS of the UE and LE diagnosed between 2000–2020 were collected. Survival duration, as well as covariables of gender, age, race, stage, primary site, tumor size, surgery status, radiation therapy status, chemotherapy status, median household income, and population size, were collected. Survival status was determined to be cancer-specific per the SEER database, so all subsequent survival comparisons were deemed cancer-specific. Survival was collected for overall duration, and at one and five years after diagnosis. Only cases with completed follow-up at these respective time points were included in these calculations.

Descriptive statistics were performed for the cohort overall and for each variable based on the one-and five-year survival status. Log-rank tests were completed to assess UE vs LE survival over time. Chi-square analyses were performed to compare the rates of each variable based on survival status at one- and five years. Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were performed to compare survival between UE and LE RMS and to determine the influence of any covariables on survival. All comparisons were made with alpha = 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Version 29 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

A total of 641 cases were included, of which 221 (34.5%) were UE and 420 (65.5%) were LE. On log-rank tests, UE survival was longer than LE survival (p = 0.021, Figure 1). The one-year survival rate was higher for the UE (88.7%) compared to the LE (81.4%) (p = 0.020). The five-year survival rate was similar between the UE (52.3%) and the LE (48.3%) (p = 0.402). Significant differences in one-year survival rates were present for age, stage, tumor size, surgery status, radiation therapy status, chemotherapy status, and median household income (p’s < 0.05). Significant differences in five-year survival rates were also present for gender, age, stage, tumor size, surgery status, radiation therapy status, and median household income. Complete descriptive and chi-square results are displayed in Table 1.

Cox regression analysis showed no difference in survival between UE and LE primary site [hazard ratio (HR) = 1.172, p = 0.322). Ages 10–29 years old (HR = 2.867, p < 0.001) and 30+ years old (HR = 3.232, p < 0.001) were both associated with worse survival. Staging categorized as regional (HR = 1.688, p = 0.012) and distant (HR = 4.390, p < 0.001) were both associated with shorter survival. Tumor size >10.0cm was associated with worse survival (HR = 2.112, p < 0.001). Living in a non-metropolitan area was associated with worse survival (HR = 1.628, p = 0.049). Surgery (HR = 0.546, p < 0.001), radiation therapy (HR = 0.513, p < 0.001), and chemotherapy (HR = 0.520, p < 0.001) were all associated with longer survival. No differences were found based on gender, race, or median household income. Complete Cox regression results are displayed in Table 2.

Discussion

Prior studies have assessed extremity RMS, specifically in the pediatric population [16], only cases with metastases at diagnosis [18], or only surgical cases with older patient cohorts and without chemotherapy status [17]. Adding to this literature, the present study is the largest assessment of RMS of the extremities to date, with a specific focus on comparing CSS between UE and LE primary sites. Out of 641 included cases, UE RMS survival was longer on log-rank and had a higher rate of survival at one-year compared to the LE. However, survival was not different from LE at five years or on Cox regression. These results indicate that there may be an initial survival advantage for UE RMS, but this is not sustained over a longer period nor after adjusting for other variables.

There are several investigations showing the overall prognosis of extremity RMS is poor, as displayed by Oberlin et al. and the subsequent Oberlin criteria for RMS [13]. Our results reflect those of prior analyses which showed similar survival between UE and LE primary sites [16–18]. A 21-year assessment of cases at Memorial Sloan Kettering also yielded no differences in RMS survival between UE and LE [11]. Another recent study by Ren et al., which focused on surgical RMS cases only, also found no differences in survival between UE and LE [17]. Though extremity RMS has a poor prognosis, investigations specifically addressing differences between UE and LE have seldom been performed. Inclusion of subanalyses of extremity RMS that compare outcomes between UE and LE will clarify if prognostic differences are present between the primary sites.

Comparisons of survival between UE and LE have been more commonly performed with soft tissue sarcomas (STS) with similar results to ours. A recent National Cancer Database analysis of STS showed no survival differences at two, five, and ten years on multivariate Cox regression [20]. A SEER analysis of STS, which included over 15,000 patients, also did not find an association between UE vs LE and survival [21]. An older single-center retrospective series also concludes that there is no difference is outcomes between UE and LE STS [22]. Direct comparisons between UE and LE RMS, similar to these for STS, should be a focus in future studies and trials.

Aside from extremity primary site, other factors like age, bone and marrow involvement, and multi-site metastases are also poor prognostic factors for RMS [13]. Similar to the Oberlin et al. findings of age 10+ years being a risk factor for worse prognosis, our results also suggest this age group is associated with worse survival. Compared to < 10 years old, the adjusted results indicate greatly increased risk of mortality with increasing age. This may be result of PAX fusion occurring at higher rates in patients 10+ years old [9, 23]. These findings are well supported by prior literature and further emphasize the importance of improving treatments for this high-risk population [13, 18, 24, 25].

Bone and bone marrow involvement, as well as multi-site metastases, are two other Oberlin et al. risk factors associated with worse survival for RMS. While our investigation did not specifically assess for sites of metastasis or the number of metastatic sites, both regional (HR = 1.688) and distant (HR = 4.390) staging were associated with worse survival on Cox regression. A component that may be associated with spread is tumor size, which is positively correlated with the risk of metastasis and poor prognosis likely because of higher rate of PAX fusion with larger tumors [9, 23]. One prior study by Oberlin et al. on pediatric RMS of the extremities found no association between tumor size and overall survival for tumor sizes < 5 cm and > 5 cm [26]. To investigate whether larger tumor size was associated with survival, we increased the cutoff between groups to 10.0 cm width and found that tumors > 10.0 cm were associated with worse survival (HR = 2.112). For high-risk tumors, aggressive treatment intervention is necessary and involves surgery, radiation, and/or chemotherapy. Our results displayed survival improvements with all three modalities, supporting aggressive treatment of extremity RMS. Particularly for cases with high-risk characteristics, multi-agent chemotherapy is the mainstay of treatment [27]. Treatment advancements including chemotherapy regimens, monoclonal antibodies, and targeted therapies are also being evaluated in clinical trials and will hopefully improve the prognosis of future extremity RMS cases.

There are several limitations to note in this investigation. First, the retrospective design of the study brings about a chance of bias. The overall sample size of the study, though at least 66% larger than all prior extremity RMS investigations [16–18], is still small with 641 patients. Additionally, the inclusion of a “no or unknown” treatment group is a limitation as this is how the data is presented in the SEER database, so comparisons between treatment and nontreatment groups may be skewed. With a small sample size, this may influence some results that near significance, though could also strengthen the confidence in the results that were significant. Unfortunately, the reasoning behind not undergoing treatment, whether related to patient wishes versus clinical risk, is not recorded in SEER. Further, utilization of data from SEER limits inclusion of variables that may be of clinical importance such as exact chemotherapy regimen and dosage used, patient comorbidities, insurance status, and more. Though not included in the present study, genetic data such as translocations PAX3/FOXO1 and PAX7/FOXO1 status should be included whenever possible for RMS studies. Finally, data on tumor recurrence was not included in this study. As the rate of recurrence is 1/3 for localized disease and 2/3 for metastatic disease, this is a critical prognostic factor that should continue to be evaluated in future studies [9]. Though the SEER database does not currently monitor recurrence well, it is an area of improvement being worked on actively that will provide great insight for treatment outcomes and progression-free survival in future research [28].

Conclusions

Extremity RMS is a known risk factor for poor prognosis, but investigations between UE and LE RMS outcomes are limited. In this direct comparison of survival between UE and LE RMS, UE RMS survival was greater on unadjusted, but not adjusted, analyses. These findings contribute to the few prior assessments of outcomes between UE and LE RMS, though subanalyses in future studies will help further delineate whether the prognosis between primary site differs.
