Oxysterol-binding protein-related protein 6 regulates neuronal morphology and migration of cerebellar granule cells during cerebellar development in vivo
Authors: Shinya Mochizuki, Wataru Nishimura, Masanori Takahashi, Reiji Yamazaki, Ruyun Zhou, Harukata Miki, Yasuko Noda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOxysterol-binding protein (OSBP)-related protein (ORP) 6, a member of subfamily III of the ORP family, localizes at endoplasmic reticulum-plasma membrane contact sites and is involved in regulating the turnover of the lipid composition of the plasma membrane through the counter-transport of phosphatidylinositol-4-phosphate and phosphatidylserine in neurons. However, the physiological functions of ORP6-mediated lipid counter-transport remain poorly understood. In the present study, we investigated the developmental expression of ORP6 protein in the mouse cerebellum and the role of ORP6 in neuronal differentiation and migration using Neuro-2A cells and primary cultured cerebellar granule cells (CGCs). The expression of the ORP6 protein increased from P7 to P21, coinciding with a key period of granule cell progenitor proliferation and immature CGC migration. Both ORP6 RNAi knockdown and overexpression of two different types of dominant-negative ORP6 constructs significantly increased the number and length of neurites in retinoic acid-induced differentiated Neuro-2A cells. Similarly, ORP6 knockdown in primary cultured CGCs increased neurite number and length and disrupted their migration in vitro. Furthermore, overexpression of a dominant-negative form of ORP6 in immature CGCs using in vivo electroporation impaired their migration into the granule layer. Taken together, these results suggest the possible involvement of lipid counter-transport by ORP6 in the regulation of neuronal morphology and migration of CGCs.Keywords: Cerebellar granule cells, Membrane contact site, Oxysterol-binding protein, ORP6, Migration

Introduction

Membrane contact sites (MCS)s formed between different membrane organelles within cells are key regions for non-vesicular lipid transport and intracellular signaling [1]. Lipid transfer proteins localized at these sites, such as oxysterol-binding protein (OSBP)-related proteins (ORPs), regulate lipid gradients, phosphoinositide turnover, and membrane identity through the counter-transport of specific lipid species [2]. ORP6 is a member of this family and was first identified as being involved in cholesterol transport between the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and endosomes in macrophages [3]. We previously reported that ORP6 interacts with itself through the intermediate region of ORP6 in primary cultured cerebellar granule cells (CGCs) and that overexpression of the intermediate region of ORP6 suppressed the original ER-plasma membrane (PM) contact localization of ORP6 and changed the localization of phosphatidylinositol-4-phosphate (PI4P) in the same manner as ORP6 RNAi [4]. Furthermore, ORP6 knockdown or overexpression of ORP6 intermediate region or a deletion mutant lacking the ORD (OSBP-related ligand binding domain) of ORP6 changed the localization of phosphatidylserine (PS); thus, these two ORP6 mutants function as dominant-negative forms. Overexpression of these constructs also disrupted the transport of PI4P and PS at the ER-PM contact sites in primary cultured CGCs [5], thereby predicting a potential role of lipid exchange by ORP6 in neuronal function. However, the physiological functions of lipid counter-transport by ORP6 remain poorly understood.

The developing cerebellum provides a suitable model system for elucidating molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying dynamic morphological changes and high motility of neurons due to the dynamic developmental processes [6]. Cerebellar granule cell progenitors (GCPs) at the external granular layer (EGL) proliferate for 15 days during the neonatal period in mice, peaking at approximately postnatal day (P) 6 [7]. After cell cycle exit, immature granule cells subsequently migrate inward radially along the fibers of Bergmann glia to establish the internal granular layer (IGL), while extending their axons in the molecular layer (ML) by approximately P21 [8,9]. These events are tightly coordinated by the complex interplay between signaling pathways and cytoskeletal remodeling [[10], [11], [12]]. Orp6 (OSBPL6) transcripts are detected in databases using cDNA libraries derived from various brain regions, and our previous study demonstrated that Orp6 mRNA is highly expressed in mouse CGCs [4,13].

In the present study, we first characterized the developmental expression of ORP6 in the mouse cerebellum, which coincided with postnatal development of the cerebellar cortical layer. To elucidate the function of ORP6 in the developing nervous system, we investigated its role in neuronal differentiation and migration using Neuro-2A cells and primary cultured CGCs in vitro, and the mouse cerebellum in vivo. We found that inhibition of ORP6 promoted extra neurite formation and impaired migration of Neuro-2A cells and CGCs. Together, our findings suggest that ORP6 regulates the neuronal morphology and migration of CGCs during cerebellar development in vivo, possibly through lipid counter-transport.

Materials and methods

Animals

Animal experiments were carried out in accordance with the Animal Care and Use Committee of Jichi Medical University (No. 24004-01) and the ARRIVE guidelines and were also approved by the Recombinant DNA Experiments Committee of Jichi Medical University (Approval No. 24-30) [14]. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the 3R principles (Replacement, Reduction, and Refinement), with careful consideration given to the use of alternative methods, reduction in the number of animals used, and particularly, implementation of procedures to minimize pain and distress. Postnatal and adult ICR mice were purchased from Japan SLC, Inc. (Shizuoka, Japan). The mice used included: postnatal day (P) 1 females weighing approximately 1-2 g, P7 females weighing approximately 4-5 g, P14 females weighing approximately 7 g, P21 females weighing approximately 10 g, P28 females weighing approximately 20 g, and 12-week-old females weighing approximately 35 g. A total of 50 mice were used in this study. For the analysis of ORP6 protein expression, 18 mice were used, with three mice at each age (P1, P7, P14, P21, P28, and 12 weeks). For in vivo electroporation experiments, eight P7 mice were used (n = 4 control, n = 4 ORP6 int). For the in vitro CGC migration assay, 15 P7 mice were used, equally divided between the control and RNAi groups. Finally, for the analysis of neurite morphology in ORP6 knockdown CGCs, nine P7 mice were used, equally divided between the control and RNAi groups. Exclusion criteria were not predetermined. No randomization was performed to allocate subjects to the study.

Expression constructs

ORP6 and two dominant-negative ORP6 expression constructs were described previously [4]. In brief, the full-length ORF, the ORP6 intermediate region (ORP6 int; amino acids 184–594), and a truncated form lacking OSBP-related ligand binding domain (ORD; ORP6 ΔORD) were cloned into the multiple cloning site of pEGFP-C1 (Clontech, Mountain View, CA), resulting in the formation of N-terminal EGFP-fused proteins [4]. pCAGGS-AcGFP-C-ORP6 int was constructed by inserting the ORP6 int into the multi-cloning site of pCAGGS-AcGFP (RRID: Addgene_203325, Addgene, Watertown, MA) [15], and was confirmed by sequencing.

Cell culture and retinoic acid (RA) treatment

Neuro-2A cells were cultured in DMEM (D5796, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (10437-028, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Bothell, WA) at 37 °C under 5% CO2. For cell differentiation, Neuro-2A cells cultured in DMEM supplemented with 2% FBS were treated with RA (302-79-4, Sigma) (final concentration, 20 μM RA, 0.02% DMSO) or DMSO (134-45, Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan) (final concentration, 0.02% DMSO) as a control. Neuro-2A cells were harvested 24 or 48 h after RA treatment, and Western blot analysis or immunostaining was performed. Experiments were performed with Neuro-2A cells between passages 100 and 200. Despite the relatively high passage number, Neuro-2A cells showed consistent morphology and differentiation responsiveness across experiments. Neuro-2A cells are not listed as a commonly misidentified cell line in the ICLAC Register of Misidentified Cell Lines. For the primary culture of CGCs, P7 mice were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane (240KKT, VIATRIS, Canonsburg, PA) and then decapitated. The cerebellum was excised and treated with trypsin-EDTA (25300062, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 7 min at room temperature (RT). Dissociated cells were rinsed with HBSS (084-08345, Wako Pure Chemical Industries, Osaka, Japan) three times and then plated into 4-compartment glass-bottom culture dishes (627870, Greiner Bio-one, Kremsmünster, Austria) or PhenoPlate 96-well microplates (6055300, Revvity, Waltham, MA) at a concentration of 4 × 104/mL cells or 2 × 104/mL cells per well, respectively [16]. Two primary cultures of CGCs were prepared for ORP6 knockdown experiments (control or ORP6 RNAi). Cells were maintained in 0.5 mL or 0.1 mL Neurobasal Medium (21103049, Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with B27 supplement (17504044, Thermo Fisher Scientific).

SDS-PAGE and Western blot analysis

Mice were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane before tissue collection. Euthanasia was carried out via cervical dislocation or decapitation. Cerebella dissected from postnatal and adult mice or Neuro-2A cells were homogenized in RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5% deoxycholate, and 1% NP-40) containing Complete, EDTA-free protease inhibitor (11836170001, Roche, Basel, Switzerland). Protein concentrations were measured using the BCA kit (T9300A, Takara Bio, Shiga, Japan). Cerebellar tissue and cells were lysed and denatured in SDS-containing buffer and heated at 100 °C for 7 min. One hundred μg of tissue protein was loaded per lane and analyzed by SDS-PAGE using a 10% polyacrylamide gel, followed by transfer to PVDF membranes and blocking with Tris-buffered saline (TBS) containing 5% skim milk. Membranes were incubated with primary antibodies at 37 °C for 2 h, followed by incubation with the secondary antibodies at 37 °C for 1 h. Antibodies used are described in Table 1, Table 2. Membranes were washed with TBS with Tween20 (TBS-T) four times at RT. The signals were detected using an ImageQuant™ 800 imager (GE Healthcare, CHI, USA). The bands were quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ-win64 software.

Image acquisition and analysis of Neuro-2A and CGCs transfected with ORP6 stealth siRNA or dominant-negative ORP6 constructs

Neuro-2A cells and primary cultured CGCs at day in vitro (DIV) 1 were transfected with control stealth siRNA or ORP6 stealth siRNA, as well as with expression constructs for ORP6 or two dominant-negative ORP6 constructs using Lipofectamine LTX Plus Reagent (15338100, Thermo Fisher Scientific), according to the manufacturer's instructions. A predesigned Stealth RNAi (MSS23533, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to knockdown ORP6. Stealth RNAi siRNA Negative Control (12935300, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used as a control. RNAi targeting ORP6 decreased the expression of ORP6 to 12.0 ± 4.8% and 9.9 ± 8.8% at 24 and 48 h after transfection, respectively, compared to control RNAi [4]. At 24 or 48 h after transfection with siRNA or expression constructs, cells were subjected to immunostaining and then examined using the Operetta CLS high-content analysis system (RRID: SCR_018810, Revvity) with a 10× objective. Images from at least 16 fields per well were acquired for each experiment. For quantitative analysis, neurites were defined as follows: the outline of the cell body was detected based on the Hoechst-stained nucleus, and neurites were identified using β-tubulin isotype Ⅲ staining. The neurite root was defined as the point pixel on the soma perimeter where a continuous projection first emerged. The neurite endpoint was defined as the most distal β-tubulin isotype III-positive pixel along the segmented neurite. Total neurite length was defined as the summed length of all neurite segments from their root to endpoint extending from a single cell. The neurite terminal corresponded to the number of endpoints of individual neurites. To evaluate cell migration ability in vitro, control stealth siRNA or ORP6 stealth siRNA-transfected cells were imaged every 10 min for 5 h using the Operetta cell-tracking system, and nuclei labeled with Hoechst were automatically tracked over time and then analyzed for total migration distance (equal to accumulated distance). The number of neurite roots, neurite terminals, total neurite length, cell number, and accumulated distance were automatically analyzed using PerkinElmer Harmony 4.9 Image Analysis Software.

In vivo electroporation

In vivo electroporation for the mouse cerebellum was performed essentially according to protocols described previously [17,18]. pCAGGS-AcGFP-C or pCAGGS-AcGFP-C-ORP6 int were diluted to 1 μg/μL in MilliQ water, and Fast Green (final concentration of 0.05%, 15939-54, Nacalai Tesque) was added to the DNA solution for visualization. To reduce animal pain during experiments, P7 mice were anesthetized with vaporized 1–2% isoflurane delivered via a calibrated vaporizer (NARCOBIT-E, Natsume, Tokyo, Japan), and a small incision was made on the skull. Then, the DNA solution was injected onto the cerebellar surface using fine needles made by pulling glass capillaries (GD-1, NARISHIGE, Tokyo, Japan). Following injection, electric square pulses (80 V, 50 ms duration, 150 ms interval, and seven times) were delivered using a tweezer-type electrode with a 5-mm-diameter (LF650, BEX, Tokyo, Japan) and an electroporator (CUY21, BEX). After electroporation, mice recovered spontaneously. Because anesthesia can reduce body temperature, mice were warmed on a heating pad before being returned to their home cage. Expression of exogenous genes may impair motor function and potentially lead to a moribund state. Signs of distress include abnormal posture or persistent recumbency. If a rapid body weight loss exceeding 20% within a few days or a moribund condition is observed, the animal is considered to have reached a humane endpoint and is euthanized under 3% isoflurane anesthesia. The brain was fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA)/PBS for 2 days after electroporation, and immunostaining was performed.

Immunostaining

Neuro-2A cells and primary cultured CGCs were fixed with 4% PFA/PBS at RT for 30 min. The cerebellum was embedded in O.C.T compound (4583, Sakura Finetek, Tokyo, Japan) after cryoprotection using 30% sucrose/PBS, and sagittally sectioned (14-μm thick) using a cryostat (Leica CM3050S or CM1860, Nussloch, Germany). Neuro-2A cells, primary cultured CGCs, and cerebellar sections were incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubation with secondary antibodies at 37 °C for 1 h, as described in Table 1, Table 2 Cells and cerebellar sections were then washed four times with PBS and incubated with Hoechst stain (346-07951, DOJINDO, Kumamoto, Japan) in PBS at RT for 10 min. After washing with PBS, the cerebellar sections were mounted with CC/Mount (K002, Diagnostic Biosystems, Pleasanton, CA, USA). Images of Neuro-2A cells and primary cultured CGCs were acquired using the Operetta CLS high-content analysis system, while images of cerebellar sections were acquired using a confocal microscope (FV1000, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Microsoft Excel version 2510. Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Normality was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. When normally distributed, differences between two groups were assessed using a two-tailed unpaired Welch's t-test, which was applied to the analysis of ORP6 protein expression, ORP6 RNAi and pCAGGS-Ac-C-ORP6 int experiments (Fig. 1B and 2G-I, P-R, 4G-I, 5C, and 6H, I). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Data from dominant-negative experiments that did not meet normality criteria were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test with a post-hoc Steel test (Fig. 3R–T) to assess statistical differences among three or more independent groups. P-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Full statistical parameters (t values, H values, degrees of freedom (df), and p values) are summarized in Table 3. The sample size was selected based on prior research to ensure adequate statistical power with minimizing animal use. In the migration analysis in vivo, we quantified 163 GFP-positive cells in the control group and 162 cells in the dominant-negative ORP6 group. These cells were obtained from four independent brains per condition. This sample size is consistent with or comparable to that in previously published studies employing similar approaches in developing mouse cerebellum, including one study (n = 2–3 mice per condition) [18] and another study (n > 30 cells per condition, collected from at least four animals per condition) [19]. No blinding was performed in this study. Outlier tests were not performed. No apparent outliers were observed, and all data was included in the analysis.

Results

Transient increase in ORP6 expression during cerebellar development

Previously, we reported that ORP6 is expressed in neurons. To further clarify the biological role of ORP6, we examined the developmental expression of ORP6 protein in the cerebellum successively from neonates to 12-week-old postnatal adult mice. Quantitative analysis revealed that the expression of ORP6 protein increased from P7, peaked at P21, and decreased thereafter (Fig. 1A, Fig. S1A–C), coinciding with the key period of GCP proliferation and migration. GCPs begin proliferating at E16.5, with peak activity around P6, and thereafter, postmitotic granule cells in the EGL migrate inward, extending two axons as they pass through the ML and the Purkinje cell layer into the deeper area of IGL until P21 [[7], [8], [9]].

To investigate whether ORP6 is commonly induced in the early phases of neuronal development, we first examined ORP6 protein expression in Neuro-2A cells after RA treatment [20,21]. ORP6 protein expression was significantly increased 48 h after treatment with RA (Fig. 1B, DMSO: 19.41 ± 10.29%, RA: 100%, Fig. S1D–F). These findings suggest that ORP6 may play a transient but potential role in early stage of postnatal cerebellar development.

ORP6 RNAi and overexpression of mutant ORP6 increased the number of neurites and total neurite length of Neuro-2A cells

To determine whether ORP6 affects neuronal morphology during neuronal differentiation, we examined the number of neurites, total neurite length, and cell number after RA treatment of Neuro-2A cells transfected with RNAi-targeting ORP6, using the Operetta CLS high-content analysis system and PerkinElmer Harmony 4.9 Image Analysis Software (Fig. S2). In a previous study, we have reported that this ORP6 RNAi construct decreased the expression of ORP6 protein to 12.0 ± 4.8% and 9.9 ± 8.8% at 24 and 48 h after transfection, respectively, compared to control oligonucleotides in Neuro 2A cells [4]. Therefore, we used the same ORP6 RNAi construct for the experiments in this study. After transfecting the cells with ORP6 RNAi, the number of neurite roots, neurite terminals, and the total neurite length were significantly increased compared to the cells transfected with control RNA 24 h after RA treatment (Fig. 2A–F). Statistical analysis showed significant increases in the number of neurite roots (ORP6 RNAi: 0.18 ± 0.01 vs. control: 0.12 ± 0.01), neurite terminals (ORP6 RNAi: 0.19 ± 0.01 vs. control: 0.12 ± 0.01), and total neurite length (ORP6 RNAi: 6.29 ± 0.52 μm vs. control: 3.93 ± 0.25 μm), as shown in Fig. 2H and I. The same results were also obtained with cells 48 h after RA treatment (Fig. 2J–O). Statistical results of the number of neurite roots (ORP6 RNAi: 0.41 ± 0.02 vs. control: 0.3 ± 0.03), number of neurite terminal (ORP6 RNAi: 0.45 ± 0.03 vs. control: 0.33 ± 0.03), and total neurite length (ORP6 RNAi: 17.78 ± 1.56 μm vs. control: 11.86 ± 1.47 μm) are shown in Fig. 2Q and R. Because total cell density was not significantly different between the two groups, the differences were attributed to changes in cell morphology. Cell number after 24 h treatment with RA (ORP6 RNAi: 5114 ± 769.48 cells vs. control: 6274.25 ± 1422.71 cells) and cell number after 48 h (ORP6 RNAi: 3271.8 ± 649.91 cells vs. control: 3248.2 ± 772.83 cells) are shown in Fig. 2G and P.

We also investigated changes in cell morphology in cells transfected with two dominant-negative ORP6 constructs (Fig. 3A). In this experiment, we used truncated forms of ORP6 lacking the specific lipid binding domain known as ORD or the intermediate region of ORP6 that mediates ORP6 self-binding and inhibits its localization to MCS, both of which have been previously shown to alter the localization of the phosphatidylserine marker in neurons, similar to ORP6 RNAi, as reported previously [5]. We examined the number of neurite terminals and the total neurite length of Neuro-2A cells transfected with GFP (Fig. 3B–E), GFP-tagged full-length (GFP-ORP6, Fig. 3F–I), an intermediate region (GFP-ORP6 intermediate region, Fig. 3J–M), and a truncated form lacking the ORD (GFP-ORP6ΔORD, Fig. 3N–Q) of ORP6. In cells expressing GFP-ORP6 intermediate region or GFP-ORP6 ΔORD, the number of neurite terminals and total neurite length were significantly increased compared with cells transfected with GFP (Fig. 3S, number of neurite terminals: GFP (0.12 ± 0.004), GFP-ORP6 int (0.13 ± 0.001), ORP6 ΔORD (0.14 ± 0.01), and Fig. 3T, total neurite length: GFP (3.67 ± 0.15 μm), GFP-ORP6 int (4.21 ± 0.06 μm), GFP-ORP6 ΔORD (4.53 ± 0.17 μm)). In contrast, in cells expressing GFP-full-length ORP6, the number of neurites and total neurite length were not significantly different (Fig. 3S, neurite terminals: GFP (0.12 ± 0.004), GFP-ORP6 (0.11 ± 0.01), and Fig. 3T, total neurite length: GFP (3.67 ± 0.15 μm), GFP-ORP6 (3.55 ± 0.3 μm)). Consistent with these findings, total cell number was not significantly different among the four groups 24 h after RA treatment (Fig. 3R, GFP: 6227 ± 576.99 cells, GFP-ORP6: 5193 ± 627.07 cells, GFP-ORP6 int: 6036.25 ± 1142.69 cells, GFP-ORP6 ΔORD: 5313.25 ± 198.7 cells). These results suggest that ORP6 may function to suppress extra neurite outgrowth in Neuro-2A cells.

ORP6 RNAi increased the number of neurites, total neurite length, and decreased cell motility of primary cultured CGCs

Next, to examine whether ORP6 affects the morphology in neurons, we transfected primary cultured CGCs with control or ORP6 RNAi at DIV1 and examined the cell number, neurite number, and total neurite length at DIV2. Total cell number was not significantly different between cells transfected with control (Fig. 4A–C) or with ORP6 RNAi (Fig. 4D–F; statistics shown in Fig. 4G; ORP6 RNAi: 4609.17 ± 239.79 cells, control: 4287 ± 142.99 cells). When cells were transfected with ORP6 RNAi, the number of neurite roots, neurite terminals (Fig. 4H, ORP6 RNAi: 1.16 ± 0.02 neurite root, control: 0.94 ± 0.02 neurite root, ORP6 RNAi: 2.47 ± 0.04 neurite terminal, control: 1.66 ± 0.07 neurite terminal), as well as total neurite length (Fig. 4I, ORP6 RNAi: 99.58 ± 7.11 μm, control: 78.79 ± 3.44 μm) were significantly increased compared with cells transfected with control. These results suggest that ORP6 may play an inhibitory role in the extra neurite outgrowth of CGCs during neonatal development.

Next, to determine whether ORP6 knockdown also affects neuronal motility in vitro, we transfected primary cultured CGCs with control RNA or ORP6 RNAi and examined the accumulated distance of CGC migration using the Operetta CLS high-content analysis cell tracking system (Fig. S3). When cells were transfected with ORP6 RNAi, the accumulated distance of migration was significantly decreased compared to that of cells transfected with control (Fig. 5A–C, ORP6 RNAi: 14.43 ± 0.49 μm, control: 16.84 ± 0.69 μm, Fig. S3 movies), suggesting a disruption in CGC motility. Collectively, these findings indicate that ORP6 is involved in regulating CGC morphology as well as motility.

Suppression of ORP6 impairs cell motility and migration of CGCs in vivo

Finally, to inhibit the function of ORP6 in vivo, we used an intermediate (int.) region of ORP6 as a dominant-negative construct. To determine whether ORP6 contributes to cerebellar development in vivo, we performed electroporation of the cerebellum in P7 mice with pCAGGS-Ac-GFP-C-ORP6 intermediate region constructs; subsequently, we followed the behavior of transfected granule cells using NeuroD1 (Fig. S4) and calbindin immunostaining at P9 (Fig. 6A–C, F). We also electroporated the cerebellum with pCAGGS-AcGFP-C as a control. At this developmental stage, CGCs are migrating inward from the EGL and forming the IGL while extending axons in the frontal plane [7,8]. Cells expressing GFP or GFP-ORP6 intermediate region were positive for NeuroD1, a marker of both GCPs and immature CGCs (Fig. S4). While more than one-third of GFP-expressing CGCs have already localized in the IGL at this stage (Fig. 6B–D, H), CGCs expressing the GFP-ORP6 intermediate region failed to reach the IGL and primarily remained in the ML (Fig. 6E–G, I). Statistical analysis showed that the population of transfected cell in control group was significantly higher in IGL (pCAGGS-AcGFP-C: 39.23 ± 9.51%, pCAGGS-AcGFP-C-ORP6 int: 6.45 ± 1.52%) and significantly lower in ML than in the dominant-negative construct of ORP6 (pCAGGS-AcGFP-C: 8.08 ± 1.9%, pCAGGS-AcGFP-C-ORP6 int: 31.02 ± 6.25%) (Fig. 6H and I). Collectively, these findings indicate that ORP6 is involved in regulating CGC morphology and inducing migration in the neonatal cerebellum.

Discussion

We previously reported that ORP6 localizes to ER-PM contact sites and is involved in the turnover of PI4P and PS [5]; however, we did not identify the physiological function of ORP6. In this study, we found that ORP6 protein expression increased during cerebellar development, particularly during the differentiation and migration of CGCs [8,9]. ORP6 knockdown increased the number of neurites in vitro and reduced CGC migration in vivo, suggesting that neurite overextension caused by loss of ORP6 may be involved in the inhibition of CGC motility.

ORP6 possesses a pleckstrin homology (PH) domain that targets specific organelle membranes in the N-terminal region, an ORD that mediates lipid transfer in the C-terminal region, two phenylalanines in an acidic tract (FFAT) motif that binds to VAP in the ER, and an anticipated dimerization domain in the intermediate region [22]. Among ORP family members, OSBP shares this domain organization with ORP6 [22] and has been reported to suppress neurite outgrowth [23], whereas ORP2, another subfamily member that consists mainly of a ligand-binding domain and lacks an N-terminal region including a PH domain, promotes neurite extension [24,25]. In the present study, ORP6 RNAi increased the number of neurites and neurite extensions in differentiated Neuro-2A cells (Fig. 2) and primary cultured CGCs (Fig. 4). These findings support the notion that ORP6 functions as a negative regulator of neurite outgrowth, similar to OSBP, and suggest a potential involvement of the PH domain in this regulation.

To clarify the relationship between lipid turnover and neurite extension or neuronal differentiation, we transfected Neuro-2A cells with two dominant-negative constructs that have both been shown to alter the localization of two specific lipids in the MCS, PI4P and PS, in our previous study [5]. ORP6ΔORD, which lacks a C-terminal region containing the lipid transfer domain, inhibited the localization of endogenous ORP6 at MCS upon overexpression. An ORP6 intermediate region containing an anticipated dimerization domain would pull endogenous ORP6 away from MCS through direct binding [5]. Regarding expression levels, both constructs are expressed under strong promoters (pEGFP-C1: CMV promoter; pCAGGS-AcGFP: CAG promoter) and may exceed endogenous ORP6 levels. In the present study, overexpression of these two mutants increased the number and length of neurites in Neuro-2A cells (Fig. 3). ORP6 knockdown also increased the number and length of neurites in Neuro-2A cells and primary cultured CGCs. These findings speculate that ORP6-mediated lipid turnover may be involved in neurite growth, but remains to be seen. To directly address the relationship between ORP6-mediated lipid transport and neuronal morphological changes, subcellular lipid analysis using a single-cell intracellular sampling system (Single Cellome™ System SS2000) would be helpful. Future studies will be required to directly detect changes in lipid transport in cells that cause these phenotypes.

To explain how altered lipid homeostasis might drive neurite outgrowth and migration, accumulating evidence indicates that membrane lipid composition can influence cytoskeletal regulators or signaling pathways. For example, PS, a candidate transport lipid for ORP6, together with phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate (PI(4,5)P2) binds to and promotes the activation of phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) [26,27]. Loss of PTEN enhances phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K) signaling, which increases the level of stable microtubules and induces excessive neurite outgrowth [28]. These findings suggest that intracellular PS and PI(4,5)P2 homeostasis critically regulates neuronal morphology through the PTEN and PI3K signaling pathways. Therefore, lipid transport proteins such as ORP6 may indirectly modulate cytoskeletal dynamics and neurite development by controlling membrane lipid composition.

In our electroporation experiments, overexpression of the dominant-negative form of ORP6 increased the percentage of CGCs distributed in the ML and decreased that in the IGL (Fig. 6). This phenotype coincided with that observed in primary cultured CGCs transfected with ORP6 RNAi, which showed a decreased accumulated migration distance in vitro (Fig. 5). The consistent results obtained using independent RNAi and dominant-negative approaches strongly support the specificity of ORP6-dependent phenotypes. However, to further demonstrate specificity using in vivo loss-of-function studies, rescue experiments involving co-transfection of ORP6 RNAi and an RNAi-resistant ORP6 construct would strengthen the conclusions.

Since abnormal elongation of neurites [29] and neurite tension [30] have previously been reported to physically affect neuronal migration, the excessive neurite outgrowth observed in cells with reduced ORP6 expression presumes that it may be involved in migration defects. Alternatively, the increased number and length of neurites may reflect changes in the differentiation state or cell polarity. Future studies using imaging analyses with differentiation markers, as well as live-cell imaging to simultaneously monitor neurite morphology and migration within the same cells, will be necessary to clarify the causal relationship between these processes.

In the developing cerebellum, CGCs account for most cerebellar neurons [7,31], with their progenitors derived from the upper rhombic lip to form the EGL. GCPs proliferate for approximately 15 days, peaking around P6 in the outer EGL [7], and differentiate into NeuroD1-positive GCPs under the control of Notch1 signaling [32]. Notch1 signaling also supports granule cell migration [33,34]. Notch1-positive CGCs migrate from the inner EGL to the interior of the Purkinje cell layer through the ML, using Bergmann glial processes as a scaffold [35]. During this migration process, PTEN deletion in Bergmann glia has also been reported to impair CGC migration [36]. Additionally, suppression of Notch1 increases neurite length [37]. Loss of PTEN in neurons induces an increase in stable microtubules and enhances neurite outgrowth [28], which resembles the morphology of ORP6 knockdown neurons. These observations imply that Notch1 and PTEN could be candidates for ORP6 regulators and may contribute to enhanced cellular migration while inhibiting neurite outgrowth. Future research should elucidate the mechanistic link between ORP6 and key regulators of cell migration and neurite formation, such as Notch1 and PTEN.

A limitation of this study is that the experiments were not performed in a blinded or randomized manner, which may introduce potential bias. However, quantification of the in vitro experiments was conducted using automated image analysis. Moreover, both in vitro and in vivo analyses were conducted using predefined criteria, and consistent phenotypes were observed across independent experiments and biological replicates. Future studies incorporating randomized and blinded designs will further strengthen the robustness of these findings.

In conclusion, we revealed that ORP6, identified as a molecule localizing at ER–PM contact sites and involved in the counter-transport of PI4P and PS, is transiently expressed in CGCs and possibly regulated migration while suppressing extra neurite growth during cerebellar development in vivo.
