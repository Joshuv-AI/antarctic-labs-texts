Identification of HSPG2 as a bladder pro-tumor protein through NID1/AKT signaling
Authors: Cong Li, Pengwei Luo, Fengzhu Guo, Xu Jia, Min Shen, Ting Zhang, Shusen Wang, Ting Du
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
PurposeHeparan sulfate proteoglycans (HSPGs) are complex molecules found on the cell membrane and within the extracellular matrix, increasingly recognized for their role in tumor progression. This study aimed to investigate the involvement of Heparan sulfate proteoglycan 2 (HSPG2) in the progression of bladder cancer.MethodsWe identified HSPG2 as a promoter of bladder tumor progression using single-cell RNA sequencing and transcriptome analysis of sequencing data from seven patient samples obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (GSE135337). Transcript profiles of 28 normal tissues and 407 bladder tumor tissues were analyzed for HSPG2 expression and survival outcomes using the Sanger tools and cBioPortal databases. HSPG2-overexpressing T24 and Biu-87 cell lines were generated, and cell proliferation and migration were assessed using CCK-8 and Transwell assays. Western blotting and immunostaining were performed to evaluate the activation of Nidogen-1 (NID1)/protein kinase B (AKT) signaling. Mouse models with patient-derived tumor organoids (HSPG2high and HSPG2low) were established to assess anticancer effects.ResultsOur results demonstrated a marked upregulation of HSPG2 in malignant bladder tumors, which correlated significantly with poor patient prognosis. HSPG2 overexpression consistently enhanced bladder tumor cell proliferation and conferred chemotherapy resistance, as shown in both in vitro and in vivo experiments. Mechanistically, HSPG2 upregulated NID1 expression, leading to the activation of the AKT pro-survival signaling pathway and promoting sustained tumor growth in bladder cancer.ConclusionThis study highlights the critical pro-tumor role of HSPG2/NID1/AKT signaling in bladder cancer and suggests its potential as a therapeutic target in clinical treatment.

Introduction

Bladder cancer is a prevalent malignancy arising on the mucous membrane of the bladder, representing the most common neoplasm within the urinary system [1]. Currently, primary therapeutic modalities for bladder cancer encompass surgical intervention, chemotherapy, and radiation therapy. Localized treatment options, including drug infusion and other methodologies, find application in specific bladder cancer subtypes, such as non-muscle-invasive and early-stage cases [2]. Despite achieving some efficacy in conventional interventions, patients with bladder cancer persistently face challenges, notably elevated risks of tumor recurrence and drug resistance [3]. Consequently, the central focus of bladder cancer research resides in elucidating the mechanisms governing its development and pinpointing novel targets for therapeutic intervention.

The occurrence and development of tumors are intricately regulated by multiple signaling pathways and cytokines [4]. Recent research highlights the pivotal role of enzymatic degradation of heparan sulfate proteoglycans on the tumor cell surface in tumor progression [5]. HSPG, a complex polysaccharide molecule comprising monosaccharide units such as glucosamine and glucuronic acid, is widely distributed in the extracellular matrix and on the cell membrane surface, actively participating in various cell signal transductions [6]. Within tumors, HSPG is not confined to cancer cells and tumor stroma but significantly contributes to processes like tumor invasion, metastasis, and angiogenesis. Its regulatory influence extends to the proliferation, differentiation, and migration capabilities of tumor cells through interactions with growth factors, cytokines, extracellular matrix components, and their receptors [7]. Moreover, HSPG plays a crucial role in mediating interactions between tumor cells and the tumor microenvironment, fostering angiogenesis and immune escape in tumors. Additionally, some studies indicate that HSPG is implicated in the development of resistance to radiotherapy and chemotherapy in tumors [8]. Consequently, modulating HSPG function holds potential to heighten tumor sensitivity to treatment. Nonetheless, the association between HSPG and bladder cancer remains poorly understood.

NID1, also known as Notch Inhibitor Delta-1, plays a significant role in tumor development and progression [9]. The aberrant expression and dysfunction of the NID1 gene closely correlate with the onset, advancement, and metastasis of various tumors, including breast cancer, lung cancer, and colorectal cancer [10]. Furthermore, irregular NID1 expression may be linked to factors such as gene mutation and genomic instability, which further expedite tumor formation [11]. Tumor cells typically exhibit elevated levels of NID1 expression compared to normal tissues, potentially fostering increased proliferation and heightened susceptibility to metastasis. Research has also underscored that NID1 influences tumor cell invasion and metastasis by modulating cell adhesion and restructuring the cytoskeleton [12]. Mechanistically, studies suggest that NID1 inhibits the Notch signaling pathway, thereby impacting cell proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis, ultimately facilitating tumorigenesis. Additionally, NID1 can influence the progression of lung cancer through its impact on the AKT growth pathway [13]. Nonetheless, the relationship between NID and HSPG signaling remains uncertain, and the role of NID1 in the development of bladder cancer has not been conclusively determined.

In this investigation, we present compelling evidence demonstrating the upregulation of HSPG2 in malignant bladder tumor tissues, establishing a significant correlation with the unfavorable prognosis of patients with bladder cancer. The overexpression of HSPG2 is implicated in the promotion of bladder cancer cell proliferation and chemoresistance. Moreover, our study elucidates the underlying mechanism driving HSPG2-induced tumor progression, emphasizing its pro-tumor role through the regulation of the NID1/AKT signaling pathway. The findings of this research propose that HSPG2 holds potential as a novel therapeutic target and diagnostic indicator for bladder cancer in clinical settings.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and reagents

Human bladder cancer cell lines T24 and Biu-87 were procured from the American Type Culture Collection and cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute complete 1640 culture medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum. The cells were maintained in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37 °C. The AKT inhibitor MK-2206 HCl was obtained from Antgene company (China), while MMC and HCPT were acquired from Sigma (USA). T24 and Biu-87 cells overexpressing HSPG2, as well as their corresponding vector controls, were obtained from Edgene Company (China). For HSPG2 overexpression, the lentiviral vector plv-EF1a-HSPG2-IRES-puro was constructed. The control vector, plv-EF1a-IRES-puro, was transfected into Biu-87 and T24 cells to establish the corresponding vector control cell lines. Cloning was performed using the EcoRI and BamHI restriction sites.

Cell proliferation and colony formation

In assessing cell proliferation, flow cytometry (BD Biosciences, USA) was employed to enumerate cells, which were subsequently seeded into 24-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well. After 48 h, cells were harvested, recounted using flow cytometry, and the cell proliferation rate was determined.

For the colony formation assay, either vector or HSPG2-overexpressing Biu-87 and T24 cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 500 cells/well. Following a 14-day incubation period, colonies were fixed in methanol and stained with crystal violet. All experiments were conducted in triplicate independently.

Transwell assay

Cellular migration was assessed using the Transwell assay (Corning, USA). Biu-87 and T24 cells, either carrying the vector or overexpressing HSPG2, were suspended in 500 μl of culture medium and placed in the upper chamber at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well. The lower chamber received 1 ml of culture medium containing 15% fetal calf serum. After a 24-h incubation period, cells that migrated through the membrane to the underside were fixed and stained with crystal violet.

Cell apoptosis

Vector or HSPG2 overexpressed Biu-87 and T24 were treated with MMC (0.5 μg/ml) or HCPT (1 μg/ml) for 48 cells. Then cell apoptosis was determined by flow cytometry (BD Biosciences, USA) using the Annexin V/PI apoptosis assay kit (BD Biosciences, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Clinical specimens

Transcript profiles of 28 normal tissues (adjacent non-cancerous tissues) and 407 bladder tumor tissues were obtained from the Sanger tools and cBioPortal databases. Clinical specimens from 24 patients with bladder cancer were collected, and these were further categorized into recurrent and non-recurrent groups based on follow-up visits at The First Affiliated Hospital of Chengdu Medical College. Informed consent was obtained from both patients and healthy volunteers. The experimental protocols in our study were approved by the Institutional Ethics Review Board of The First Affiliated Hospital of Chengdu Medical College (2024CYFYIRB-BA-Mar21).

scRNA-seq analysis

This study utilized single-cell sequencing data sourced from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (GSE135337). Following the retrieval of the matrix, we performed quality controls and downstream analysis using the Seurat package (version 4.3.0) in R (version 4.1.3, The R Foundation). In summary, the filtered gene expression matrix underwent normalization using the LogNormalize method, and subsequently, the top 2000 highly variable features were selected.

Clustering and cell type annotation

Dimension reduction involved principal-component analysis (PCA) utilizing the RunPCA function, and the optimal number of principal components (PCs) was determined through the ElbowPlot function. Subsequently, the identified PCs were employed in cell clustering via modularity optimization with the kNN graph algorithm. Visualization of cell clusters was achieved using the UMAP algorithm. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) within each cluster were identified through the Wilcoxon test. Only genes exhibiting a difference in expression with an adjusted P value < 0.05 and a logFC > 0.25 were considered marker genes. Cell types were annotated based on the expression of known marker genes: EPCAM, KRT18, KRT19, KRT8, and UPK1B for epithelial/tumor cells; CD2, CD3D, and CD3E for T cells; CD68 and LYZ for macrophages; DCN, COL3A1, COL1A1, and COL1A2 for fibroblasts; PECAM1, CDH5, and VWF for endothelial cells.

scRNA-seq based CNV detection and cell trajectory analysis

To identify malignant cells, we examined copy number variations (CNVs) within each subcluster of the epithelial cell cluster using infercnvpy (https://github.com/icbi-lab/infercnvpy) on scRNA-seq data. Macrophages were included as a control reference, and infercnvpy analysis was performed with default parameters.

For cell trajectory analysis, single-cell pseudotime trajectories were constructed using the Monocle2 package (v2.22.0) following the provided documentation (http://cole-trapnell-lab.github.io/monocle-release/). UMI counts were modeled under a negative binomial distribution, and ordering genes were identified based on high dispersion across cells (mean_expression ≥ 0.01; dispersion_empirical ≥ 1). The discriminative dimensionality reduction with trees (DDRTree) method was employed to reduce data to two dimensions. Gene sets obtained from the destiny analysis were clustered and visualized using the plot_genes_in_pseudotime function.

Western blotting

Bladder tumor cells were lysed using RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher, USA) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher, USA). The proteins were subjected to blots according to standard protocols. Primary antibodies were listed: anti-HSPG2 primary antibody (ab2501, Abcam, UK), anti-NID1 primary antibody (ab254325, Abcam, UK), anti-AKT primary antibody (Ab8805, Abcam, UK), anti-phosphorylated AKT primary antibody (ab38449, Abcam, UK), anti-PI3K primary antibody (ab139107, Abcam, UK), anti-phosphorylated PI3K primary antibody (ab278545, Abcam, UK), and anti-β-actin primary antibody (ab8226, Abcam, UK).

Immunostaining

Paraffin sections were processed by deparaffinization, rehydration and antigen retrieval. Samples were blocked by 5% bovine serum albumin and then incubated with the following primary antibodies: anti-HSPG2 primary antibody (ab2501, Abcam, UK), anti-NID1 primary antibody (ab254325, Abcam, UK) and anti-phosphorylated AKT primary antibody (ab38449, Abcam, UK) at 4ºC overnight. Then, sections were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Abcam, UK) at room temperature. Protein expression was quantified by Image Pro Plus 5.0 software.

RNA interference

NID1 siRNAs were chemically synthesized by RUIBIO company (China). SiRNA transfection of T24 and Biu-87 cells was conducted using Lipo8000™ Transfection Reagent (Biyuntian, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The sense sequences of the siRNA were listed: siRNA #1 5ʹ-CAACGGAGCUUAUAACAUAUU-3ʹ and 5ʹ-GGAAAUACCAUGAGGAAGAUU-3ʹ.

Animal protocols

Primary bladder tumor tissues were minced into 1–2 mm3 pieces, and digested in a mixture of culture medium containing type IV collagenase (Sigma, USA) and DNase I (Sigma, USA) for 2 h. The remaining cells were digested to single cell by trypsin (Sigma, USA) and seeded in Matrigel (Coring, USA) in 96 well plate (one colony per well). After 20 days, colonies derived from single cells were harvested and expanded. HSPG2-high and HSPG2-low subpopulations were determined by western blotting. Subsequently, HSPG2-high and HSPG2-low subpopulations isolated from bladder tumors (105 cells per mouse) were subcutaneously inoculated into NOD-SCID mice (n = 5 in each group, Huafukang, China). Tumor volume and survival were recorded daily. The calculation formula of tumor volume: tumor volume = length × width 2/2. All animal experiments were performed according to the guidelines of the Institute Ethics Committee of Chengdu Medical College.

Statistical analysis

Data was presented as mean ± SD and analyzed using Graphpad 6.0 software. The differences between two groups were compared using an independent T test. Comparisons among multiple groups were analyzed using one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. The Kaplan–Meier estimator was conducted to evaluate the overall survival. Each experiment was performed for at least three independent times. P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Identification of HSPG2 as a potential tumor driver in bladder cancer

To investigate the function of specific tumor subpopulations in bladder cancer, scRNA-seq analysis was conducted using 7 bladder tumor tissues downloaded from the GEO database. After performing quality control measures and eliminating batch effects, a total of 33,171 single cells were classified into 11 major clusters. Cell types were identified using cluster-specific genes and classic markers described in previous studies: epithelial cells/tumor cells, endothelial cells, fibroblasts cells, T cells, and macrophage cells. Among these, 32,037 epithelial cells/tumor cells were identified and further subdivided into 5 subclusters. Notably, these subclusters were consistently observed in all patients from our scRNA-seq cohorts (Fig. 1A and B). The expression levels of GPX3, FABP4, S100A7, PLCG2, and MGP were found to be substantially elevated in 5 subclusters, respectively (Fig. 1C). In order to uncover the origin of bladder cancer at the single-cell level, trajectory analysis was performed to delineate the transition of normal epithelial cells to cancer cells (Fig. 1D). For the cancer cell subtypes, it was observed that both Epi-C3 and Epi-C4 displayed higher CNV scores compared to Epi-C0, Epi-C1, and Epi-C2 (Fig. 1E and F). These findings suggest that Epi-C3 and Epi-C4 may represent malignant tumor types in bladder cancer. To elucidate the specific molecular mechanisms underlying the development of malignant tumor cells, differentially expressed genes between Epi-C3–C4 and Epi-C0–C1 were analyzed. Additionally, differentially expressed genes between 407 bladder tumor tissues and 28 normal tissues were assessed using the TCGA database. Ultimately, a set of 23 genes was identified, and these genes were further investigated through survival analysis and expression analysis in high/low grade bladder tumor tissues. Interestingly, the HSPG2 gene was found to be closely associated with tumor progression in bladder cancer (Fig. 1G). Subsequently, the scRNA-seq data for the 7 bladder tumor tissues were categorized into high/low grade groups based on the clinical stages of the patients, and the expression of HSPG2 was examined. As a result, the proportion of HSPG2 positive tumor cells was found to be upregulated in high-grade bladder tumor tissues (Fig. 1H). HSPG2 promoted bladder tumor progression in vitro and in vivo

To investigate the role of HSPG2 during bladder tumor progression, we used the TCGA database to measure the transcriptome expression of HSPG2 in 407 bladder tumor tissues in comparison with 28 normal tissues. As shown in Fig. 2A, HSPG2 expression was decreased in bladder tumor tissues compared to normal tissues (Fig. 2A). However, HSPG2 expression was progressively upregulated along with clinical stages in patients with bladder cancer (Fig. 2B). Thus, we sought to explore the influence of HSPG2 on overall survival by dividing patients into high and low-HSPG2 expression groups. Patients with high levels of HSPG2 exhibited a shortened survival time compared to the low-HSPG2 group (Fig. 2C), indicating a potential pro-tumor role of HSPG2 in bladder cancer. To validate these results, we included 24 clinical specimens in our study and divided them into recurrent and non-recurrent groups according to follow-up visits (Fig. 2D). Consistently, immunostaining confirmed that HSPG2 was upregulated in tumor tissues from recurrent patients with bladder cancer, when compared to the non-recurrent group (Fig. 2E). These results suggested that increased HSPG2 expression was closely correlated with the poor prognosis of bladder cancer. Subsequently, we chose two bladder cancer cell lines, T24 and Biu-87, and overexpressed HSPG2 (Fig. 2F). Cell proliferation and colony formation assays were performed to assess the influence of HSPG2 on tumorigenic potential. The results showed that the overexpression of HSPG2 significantly enhanced cell proliferation (Fig. 2G) and tumorigenic potential (Fig. 2H) in T24 and Biu-87 cells. However, the Transwell assay revealed that HSPG2 had limited influence on cell migration (Fig. 2I). To evaluate if HSPG2 played a role in chemoresistance regulation, we treated vector or HSPG2 overexpressed cells with chemotherapeutic agents, HCPT and MMC, for 48 h. The results showed that HSPG2 overexpression significantly reduced the sensitivity of T24/Biu-87 cells to HCPT and MMC (Fig. 2K and L). AKT signaling was activated in HSPG2-high bladder tumor tissues

We next endeavored to elucidate the molecular pathways influenced by HSPG2 in modulating bladder tumor progression. Utilizing the TCGA database, we compared mRNA expression profiles among 296 patients with bladder cancer, categorized into HSPG2-high (n = 148) and HSPG2-low (n = 148) groups. This analysis identified 6081 differentially expressed genes, comprising 4063 upregulated and 2017 downregulated genes (refer to Fig. 3A). Subsequent KEGG enrichment analysis identified key signaling pathways associated with HSPG2 (refer to Fig. 3B). Notably, the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, recognized for its involvement in regulating tumor stemness and fostering sustained tumor growth and chemoresistance across various tumor types [14], was among the identified pathways. To assess the role of AKT signaling in HSPG2-induced tumor progression, we scrutinized PI3K and AKT protein levels in T24/Biu-87 cells overexpressing HSPG2, compared to vector-only cells. As hypothesized, an activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway was observed in HSPG2-overexpressed T24 and Biu-87 cells (refer to Fig. 3C). Subsequently, to validate these findings, HSPG2-overexpressed cells were treated with the AKT signaling inhibitor MK-2206 2HCl, and their tumorigenic potential was assessed. Indeed, inhibition of AKT signaling suppressed the proliferative characteristics and colony formation induced by HSPG2 (refer to Fig. 3D and E). These results were corroborated in the chemosensitivity assay (refer to Fig. 3F and G). Further substantiating our findings, we conducted an immunostaining assay for phosphorylated AKT in clinical specimens from non-recurrent and recurrent patients. Consistently, recurrent patients exhibited elevated expression of AKT in tumor tissues compared to the non-recurrent group (refer to Fig. 3H). HSPG2 upregulated AKT signaling through NID1

To delve deeper into the mechanism by which HSPG2 mediates AKT signaling activation, we conducted an analysis of HSPG2 protein–protein interaction networks using the Genemania database (Fig. 4A). Significantly, our investigation revealed a potential interaction between HSPG2 and NID1, a sulfated glycoprotein widely distributed in basement membranes, which has previously been associated with PI3K/AKT signaling activation in salivary gland adenoid cystic carcinoma [15]. Consequently, we examined the protein level of NID1 in T24/Biu-87 cells overexpressing HSPG2, observing an upregulation of NID1 in the HSPG2 overexpressed group (Fig. 4B). Further, upon silencing NID1 in HSPG2 overexpressed T24/Biu-87 cells (Fig. 4C), we observed a suppression of AKT upregulation (Fig. 4D), suggesting that HSPG2 upregulates NID1 to mediate AKT signaling activation. To validate the role of NID1 in HSPG2-induced tumor progression, we silenced NID1 in HSPG2 overexpressed Biu-87/T24 cells and reassessed tumorigenic potential. Subsequently, we observed inhibited cell proliferation and colony formation in NID1 silenced cells, with limited impact on vector T24 and Biu-87 cells (Fig. 4E and F). Consistent results were obtained in the chemosensitivity assay (Fig. 4G and H), indicating that HSPG2 induces bladder tumor progression via NID1. To assess the overall survival impact of NID1 in bladder cancer patients, we analyzed the TCGA database and found a significant decrease in overall survival among patients with high NID1 levels compared to the low NID1 group (Fig. 4I). Additionally, a high correlation was observed between HSPG2 and NID1 expression in patients (Fig. 4J), with elevated NID1 expression in clinical specimens from recurrent bladder cancer patients (Fig. 4K). HSPG2/NID1/AKT axis governed bladder tumor growth in vivo

Motivated by our in vitro findings, we aimed to investigate the impact of HSPG2 on bladder tumor progression in an in vivo setting. To achieve this, primary bladder tumor tissues obtained from patients were enzymatically digested into single cells and cultured in 3D Matrigel. Subsequently, the protein levels of HSPG2 were assessed in tumor cells from distinct colonies. The identified HSPG2-high and HSPG2-low expressing colonies were then subcutaneously inoculated into NOD-SCID mice (Fig. 5A and B). Consistent with heightened HSPG2 expression, tumor-bearing mice in the HSPG2-high group exhibited elevated levels of NID1 and phosphorylated AKT compared to the HSPG2-low group (Fig. 4C and D). Moreover, colonies expressing high levels of HSPG2 formed tumors that grew more rapidly than those in the HSPG2-low group (Fig. 4E). Discussion

Our study offers mechanistic insights into the role of HSPG2 as an oncogene in the regulation of bladder tumor progression. HSPGs, a class of cell surface and extracellular matrix molecules, exert a crucial influence on tumor growth, invasion, and metastasis by interacting with growth factors such as VEGF and FGF [16]. This interaction promotes signaling, fostering the formation of new blood vessels that supply nutrients and oxygen to the growing tumor. Targeting HSPGs emerges as a potential strategy to inhibit tumor growth, angiogenesis, invasion, and metastasis by interacting with various cell adhesion molecules and proteases [17]. Alterations in the expression levels of HSPGs are observed in various cancers, including lung and breast cancer, and are associated with tumor progression and patient prognosis [18, 19]. Our study underscores the pivotal role of HSPG2 in bladder cancer progression, highlighting the need for further research to fully explore therapeutic strategies targeting the HSPG2 signaling pathway in cancer development and progression. While the findings in this paper suggest that targeting HSPGs holds promise for bladder cancer treatment, additional investigations are warranted.

To explore the impact of HSPG2 on bladder cancer cell behavior, we conducted a protein-to-protein interaction analysis, revealing a potential direct interaction with NID1, also known as Nidogen-1. NID1, a basement membrane protein, plays a critical role in cell adhesion, migration, and signaling, garnering significant interest in cancer research. Previous studies implicated NID1 in tumor growth and progression in ovarian cancer [20], with upregulated expression in various cancer types, including breast, lung, and colorectal cancer, leading to increased tumor aggressiveness and poor patient prognosis [10, 21]. Furthermore, NID1 has been linked to promoting cancer cell proliferation and survival, suggesting a direct role in driving tumor growth [22]. Consistent with these findings, our results indicate that HSPG2 may upregulate NID1, stimulating bladder cancer cell proliferation and inducing chemoresistance. Our study provides evidence of the association between HSPG2 and NID1, shedding light on NID1's role in bladder cancer progression and drug resistance. These findings have substantial implications for cancer diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment. The upregulation of NID1 in cancer tissues suggests its potential as a biomarker for cancer diagnosis, aiding in early detection and treatment decisions. Furthermore, the association of NID1 expression with poor patient prognosis suggests its potential as a prognostic marker, identifying patients at higher risk of disease progression and poor outcomes in bladder cancer.

The NID1 gene plays a pivotal role in cancer development, participating in multiple signaling pathways that regulate tumor growth, invasion, and metastasis [23]. Notably, one of the prominent signaling pathways associated with NID1 in tumors is the TGF-β signaling pathway, exhibiting a dual role in cancer by acting as a tumor suppressor in early tumorigenesis but promoting progression in advanced stages. Existing reports indicate that NID1 enhances TGF-β signaling [24], leading to the epithelial-mesenchymal transition process in cancer cells. Moreover, NID1 has been linked to the activation of the Akt/mTOR pathway, associated with increased cell proliferation and survival in various cancers, including lung, gastric, and breast cancers [25]. Our study substantiates that NID1 overexpression activates the AKT pathway, resulting in heightened tumor cell growth and drug resistance in bladder cancer. Additionally, our research delineates the role of HSPG2/NID1/AKT signaling in modulating bladder cancer progression and elucidates the underlying mechanism of HSPG2-induced bladder tumor progression. These findings suggest that targeting NID1 may be a viable strategy to inhibit the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway and suppress tumor progression.

Building upon these results, our study demonstrated that: (1) Elevated expression of HSPG2 correlated with poor prognosis, potentially serving as a prognostic indicator for bladder cancer patients. (2) HSPG2 directly facilitated bladder cancer cell proliferation and contributed to the development of chemoresistance. (3) The pro-tumor effects of HSPG2 were reliant on the AKT signaling pathway, induced by the NID1 protein. (4) Overexpression of HSPG2 resulted in heightened tumor growth and chemoresistance in tumor-bearing mice, presenting a potential target for managing bladder cancer. However, our study has several limitations. Compared to tumor tissues, HSPG2 expression levels are relatively higher in normal tissues. However, the specific mechanisms underlying this phenomenon remain unclear. We hypothesize that mutations in the HSPG2 gene may influence the HSPG2 signaling pathway. Additionally, the high expression of HSPG2 in endothelial cells may also contribute to this observation. Further research is needed to investigate these possibilities. Additionally the number of clinical samples analyzed was limited, which restricts the strength of the evidence for the correlation between HSPG2 expression and clinical characteristics. Moreover, the role of HSPG2 in regulating tumor stemness remains unclear. Future studies are needed to explore the potential applications of HSPG2 in clinical diagnosis, treatment, and targeted therapy.

In summary, our study underscores the pro-tumor role of HSPG2 in bladder cancer through NID1/AKT signaling. These findings suggest that HSPG2 might be leveraged as a diagnostic indicator and therapeutic target for bladder cancer.
