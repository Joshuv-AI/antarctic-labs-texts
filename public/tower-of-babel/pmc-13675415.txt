Psychometric Validation of the Kannada Translated Version of the Adult Adolescent Sensory Profile
Authors: Shalini Quadros, Ravindra N. Munoli, Podila Satya Venkata Narasimha Sharma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Sensory modulation assessment is an integral part of occupational therapy practice; however, validated assessment tools in Indian regional languages are absent. One of the most widely recommended tools for measuring sensory modulation is the Adult Adolescent Sensory Profile (AASP). It is available only in English and a few other languages. This study aims to translate the AASP into Kannada and evaluate its content and construct validity, internal consistency, and test–retest reliability.Methods:We followed the International Test Commission (2014) guidelines for translation. Three subject experts assessed content validity. We examined internal consistency across the four quadrants. We assessed construct validity via confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) in a stratified sample of 150 healthy participants. We evaluated test–retest reliability in 25 adults over a 3-week interval between administrations.Results:The scale-level content validity index was 0.82, and S-CVI/Avg was 0.94. Cronbach’s alpha for sensation seeking was 0.72, and 0.93 for sensation avoiding. Pearson’s r in test–retest ranged from 0.58 to 0.69. The CFA fit indices (CFI = 0.321, TLI = 0.295, RMSEA = 0.07) suggest that the four-factor structure requires cultural adaptation. The sensory sensitivity-sensation avoiding factor covariance (r = 0.955) indicates limited discriminant validity between the sensory sensitivity-sensation avoiding quadrants.Conclusion:The Kannada AASP demonstrates adequate content validity and internal consistency. The original four-factor form does not fully replicate in the Kannada version, suggesting the need for further studies with a larger sample size. The translated tool offers a promising foundation for improving research in sensory modulation in the Indian population.

Sensory modulation refers to the brain’s ability to regulate the sensory stimulations received from the environment. It is a neuro-behavioral function that helps in processing sensory information from the body and environment to respond appropriately and maintain alertness. 1 It is a neuro-behavioral function that influences occupational participation across all areas of daily life and is therefore a domain of concern in occupational therapy assessment. 2

Dunn’s Model of Sensory Processing breaks down the complex ways in which individuals process sensory information into more understandable patterns. It describes neurological thresholds along a continuum from low to high and behavioral self-regulation responses from passive to active. Dunn’s model provides valuable insights into the complex world of sensory experiences. 3 The intersection of these continua yields four sensory processing quadrants: low registration, sensation seeking, sensory sensitivity, and sensation avoiding. This model provides a theoretically supported framework for understanding differences in sensory experiences.

Multiple instruments exist to evaluate sensory modulation in adults, including the Sensory Responsiveness Questionnaire 4 and the Adult Sensory Processing Scale. 5 The Adult Adolescent Sensory Profile 6 is the most commonly used, extensively validated, and widely recommended assessment tool for use in clinical and research settings for populations with mental health conditions, including schizophrenia. 7

The AASP is a 60-item, self-report questionnaire with domains of low registration, sensation seeking, sensory sensitivity, and sensation avoiding. It applies to adolescents (11–17 years) and adults (18 years and above). Given the self-administered nature of the tool, people whose primary language is Kannada and who come from a different cultural background may find the English terms in the AASP difficult to understand. In this scenario, translation and measurement of the translated versions of the scales become necessary. 8

The original English version has acceptable internal consistency 6 (Cronbach’s α = 0.60–0.78) and has been translated and used in populations speaking languages such as Arabic, 9 Hebrew, 10 Spanish, 11 Chinese, 12 and Turkish. 13 Translated versions have consistently shown differences in cut-off scores and normative values, underscoring the importance of independent psychometric validation in each new linguistic context.

The present study, therefore, aimed to translate the AASP into Kannada and evaluate its psychometric properties, specifically: (a) translation fidelity via content validity assessment, (b) internal consistency, (c) test–retest reliability, and (d) construct validity using confirmatory factor analysis (CFA).

Rationale for the study India is a diverse nation with more than 22 languages. Kannada is a Dravidian language spoken by around 44 million people in Karnataka, a Southern Indian state. Although sensory modulation is a universal factor influencing occupational participation, no validated Kannada version of any widely used sensory modulation assessment tool existed to assess sensory modulation issues in people whose primary language is Kannada. Therefore, this study was necessary not only to bridge the linguistic barrier but also to support culturally appropriate practice.

Methods

The institutional ethics committee approved the study, which is part of a larger project funded by the Department of Science and Technology, Ministry of Science and Technology, Government of India. We used International Test Commission guidelines, 14 to translate the assessment tool. We conducted expert validation to establish content validity. We collected cross-sectional data using the translated tool to assess construct validity and test–retest reliability. A trained research assistant collected data from study participants face-to-face between February 2024 and May 2024. All participants provided written informed consent; adolescent participants provided assent with parental consent.

Translation Procedure

Translation followed ITC guidelines. Four bilingual individuals conducted forward translation of the original English AASP into Kannada: two professional translators (D1 and D2) and two bilingual occupational therapists with expertise in sensory processing (D3 and D4). The principal investigator (a bilingual occupational therapist) produced a synthesized forward-translated draft by reconciling the four versions.

Four independent bilingual individuals then back-translated the Kannada draft into English: two professional translators (B1 and B2) and two bilingual occupational therapists (B3 and B4). A language expert reviewed the back-translated English version and the original English AASP, and we revised the Kannada version accordingly.

We conducted cognitive debriefing by administering the draft Kannada AASP to 10 healthy, community-dwelling individuals to assess comprehensibility. Necessary wording revisions were incorporated, yielding the final Kannada AASP version used in subsequent psychometric testing.

The principal investigator conducted the forward synthesis rather than an independent committee because bilingual occupational therapists were in short supply in the region. Additionally, all eight translators were from the same regional dialect area (coastal Karnataka), meaning dialectical variation across Kannada-speaking regions was not systematically addressed.

We conducted the psychometric instrument validation from February 2024 to May 2024 in Udupi district, Karnataka. We recruited a community-based subsample of 150 healthy participants from Udupi district, Karnataka, using purposive sampling from a larger study of 438 participants with broader objectives and not designed exclusively to validate the AASP. All people aged 11 years and above who were able to read and write were approached for the study, and they were further divided into three age groups: 11–17 years (n = 50), 18–64 years (n = 50), and 65 years and above (n = 50). People with any self-reported long-term comorbid medical conditions (chronic respiratory disease, cancer, diabetes, and neurodegenerative conditions), diagnosed mental health conditions, or current use of medications acting on the central nervous system (all as per their report and available treatment documents) were excluded from the study.

Tools

We collected socio-demographic and clinical data using a semi-structured tool developed for the study. We assessed sensory modulation using the Kannada-translated AASP.

Statistical Analysis

We used Jamovi (version 2.3.28.0) to analyze the data. For content validity, three experts – one psychiatric social worker, one occupational therapist, and one language expert were approached, and written informed consent was obtained to validate the Kannada version of the AASP (see Supplementary Table S1). Next, we prepared a criterion rating scale to assess agreement with the original English version on a scale of 1 (no agreement at all) to 5 (excellent agreement). Each expert then independently rated each of the 60 items on the agreeability scale. Later, during the calculation of content validity indices (CVI), these were converted into a dichotomous/binary scale: ratings of 1 and 2 were equated as “not relevant” and scored as 0, whereas ratings of 3, 4, and 5 were equated as “relevant” and coded as 1. We calculated item-level CVI (I-CVI) and scale-level indices (S-CVI/Avg and S-CVI/UA) following the method of Lynn, 15 as adapted by Polit and Beck. 16 An I-CVI ≥0.80 was considered acceptable. Items with I-CVI <0.80 were noted for discussion of potential future revision (see Supplementary Table S2 for full item-level CVI data).

We computed Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for each of the four AASP quadrants to assess internal consistency. Values ≥0.90 were considered excellent, 0.80–0.89 good, 0.70–0.79 acceptable, and <0.70 questionable. 17

We examined construct validity using CFA specifying the original four-factor AASP structure. We preferred CFA over exploratory factor analysis (EFA) to test whether the original scale’s factor structure was supported in the Kannada-speaking population. Before CFA, we assessed the appropriateness of factor analysis using Bartlett’s test of sphericity and the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy. Model fit was evaluated using the Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI), Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR), and Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA). Conventional acceptable fit thresholds were 17 : CFI and TLI ≥0.90, SRMR ≤0.08, RMSEA ≤0.06.

To assess test–retest reliability, a subsample of 25 adult participants (18–64 years) completed the Kannada AASP on two occasions 3 weeks apart to evaluate temporal stability. We calculated Pearson’s correlation coefficients for each quadrant. We considered values >0.70 adequate for clinical instruments.

Results

Three experts validated the translated tool, and we administered it to 150 participants to assess construct validity. We re-administered the tool to a subsample of 25 participants to assess test–retest reliability.

Content Validity

Three experts reviewed the translated items. Based on the expert review, 49 of the 60 items achieved an I-CVI of 1.00 (unanimous agreement across all three experts). Eleven items (Items 3, 6, 8, 10, 15, 18, 27, 28, 34, 43, and 45) received an I-CVI of 0.67 (below the 0.80 threshold), and two of three experts rated each as relevant. Several of these items pertain to touch processing (Items 27 and 34) and olfactory stimuli (Items 3 and 6), which may reflect cultural differences in how sensory experiences are framed. Item 28, concerning responses to haircuts, received lower expert ratings, partly attributable to cultural norms around hair-cutting for women in Karnataka. The S-CVI/Avg was 0.94, and the S-CVI/UA was 0.82, both meeting acceptable thresholds. Full item-level CVI data are presented in Supplementary Table S2. We made no changes to item content at this stage to preserve fidelity to the original English version.

The quadrants’ low registration (0.90) and sensation avoiding (0.93) showed excellent Cronbach’s values, followed by sensory sensitivity (0.86) with a good Cronbach’s value, and the sensation seeking quadrant showed an acceptable (0.72) Cronbach’s value in the Kannada version of AASP, owing to a higher Cronbach’s value indicating greater internal consistency. The sensation seeking quadrant showed a reduction from the original English value (α = 0.79). Test–retest correlations ranged from r = 0.582–0.688, indicating moderate temporal stability below the r ≥ 0.70 clinical threshold.

We also examined test–retest reliability using two-way random-effects, absolute-agreement, single-measure ICCs (2,1). Moderate reliability was observed for low registration, ICC = 0.556, 95% CI [0.267, 0.750]; sensation seeking, ICC = 0.538, 95% CI [0.090, 0.770]; sensory sensitivity, ICC = 0.562, 95% CI [0.294, 0.750]; and sensation avoiding, ICC = 0.660, 95% CI [0.428, 0.812].

Internal consistency was evaluated separately for each item at test and retest using Cronbach’s alpha (α) and McDonald’s omega (ω). At Test 1, low registration (α = 0.738, ω = 0.763) and sensation avoiding (α = 0.703, ω = 0.741) demonstrated acceptable internal consistency; sensation seeking demonstrated low internal consistency (α = 0.420, ω = 0.482), while sensory sensitivity produced lower reliability estimates (α = 0.568, ω = 0.637). At Test 2, reliability estimates were α = 0.631 and ω = 0.654 for low registration, α = 0.485 and ω = 0.511 for sensation seeking, α = 0.570 and ω = 0.625 for sensory sensitivity, and α = 0.624 and ω = 0.634 for sensation avoiding.

Construct Validity

We conducted a CFA to examine the proposed four-factor structure of the Kannada AASP in 150 participants. We specified the 60 ordinal items as indicators of low registration, sensation seeking, sensory sensitivity, and sensation avoiding. We estimated the model using diagonally weighted least squares with robust standard errors.

The four-factor model converged; however, the mean-adjusted scaled Chi-square test was statistically significant, χ² (1704) = 2254, p < .001. The comparative fit indices were below conventional thresholds (CFI = 0.627; TLI = 0.612), and the SRMR was elevated (SRMR = 0.122). The scaled RMSEA was 0.047, 95% CI [0.041, 0.052], with p = .865 for the close-fit test. Although the scaled RMSEA suggested a close approximate fit, the low CFI and TLI and elevated SRMR indicated that the proposed four-factor structure did not adequately reproduce the observed relationships among the items.

Standardized factor loadings for low registration ranged from 0.218 to 0.553, and all freely estimated loadings were statistically significant. For sensation seeking, standardized loadings ranged from −0.402 to 0.684. Item 8 in sensation seeking loaded negatively on the factor (β = −0.402, p = .013), while item 6 in sensation seeking (β = 0.202, p = .054), item 11 in sensation seeking (β = 0.033, p = .736), and item 12 in sensation seeking (β = 0.048, p = .626) did not load significantly. Sensory Sensitivity loadings ranged from −0.229 to 0.554. Item 8 in sensory sensitivity had a negative loading (β = −0.229, p = .216), and none of the freely estimated sensory sensitivity loadings reached statistical significance. Standardized loadings for sensation avoiding ranged from 0.137 to 0.607; item 12 in sensation avoiding was the only freely estimated indicator with a nonsignificant loading (β = 0.137, p = .174). Supplementary Table S3 presents complete item-level estimates, and Supplementary Figure S1 shows the standardized path diagram.

The estimated latent-factor correlations were 0.392 between low registration and sensation seeking, 0.792 between low registration and sensory sensitivity, 0.594 between Low Registration and Sensation Avoiding, 0.559 between Sensation Seeking and Sensory Sensitivity, 0.384 between sensation seeking and sensation avoiding, and 0.972 between sensory sensitivity and sensation avoiding. The very high correlation between sensory sensitivity and sensation avoiding indicated poor discriminant validity between these constructs. In addition, the latent covariance matrix was not positive definite, requiring cautious interpretation of the factor correlations and model parameters. Collectively, the findings provided limited support for the proposed four-factor structure in the Kannada-speaking sample.

Discussion

This study reports the first translation and psychometric evaluation of the AASP in Kannada, contributing to a growing body of cross-cultural adaptations of the AASP. The Kannada-translated tool demonstrated adequate content validity and internal consistency. However, the CFA results indicate that the original four-factor form does not fully replicate in the current sample.

Translation and Content Validity

The translation process was made rigorous and systematic by (a) the use of ITC (2014) guidelines and (b) the incorporation of eight (four for forward translation and four for back translation) bilingual translators. Occupational therapists were part of the translation team. Their familiarity with sensory processing concepts enhanced the translation of domain-specific items. This approach aligns with best practices for instrument translation in the health sector. 18

We acknowledge two limitations of the translation process. The principal investigator reviewed the synthesized forward and back translations instead of an independent expert committee, so consolidation bias may have occurred and cannot be ruled out. Also, ideally, including translators whose first language is English for the back translation would have reduced potential bias. This was unavoidable because bilingual occupational therapists were scarce in the region. All the translators were from coastal Karnataka and spoke the same dialect of Kannada. Kannada has nearly 20 dialects. Therefore, the translated version may not be equally comprehensible to people who use other Kannada dialects. Future work can address this by including translators from different dialectal backgrounds.

In the present study, the S-CVI/UA was 0.82. This indicates that the translated tool’s overall content validity is acceptable. The 11 items with an I-CVI below 0.80 (rated relevant by only two of three experts) predominantly relate to touch processing and olfactory stimuli. Items 27 and 28 are discussed further in the context of factor analysis findings below. In future revisions, these items should be prioritized for linguistic refinement by a larger panel of experts.

Internal Consistency

The Kannada version of AASP showed internal consistency comparable to or higher than the original English version. The sensation avoiding and low registration quadrants showed improvement (α = 0.93 vs. 0.66 in the original English version and α = 0.90 vs. 0.82, respectively), suggesting strong item homogeneity in the Kannada-speaking population in the current study. The sensation seeking quadrant showed lower internal consistency than the original English version (α = 0.72 vs. 0.79). This may reflect cultural differences in the Udupi region, including a collectivistic pattern in social norms. Similar observations were noted in the Turkish adaptation 12 of the scale, where participants focused on others’ expectations rather than their own sensory preferences.

Construct Validity and Factor Structure

The ordinal four-factor CFA produced mixed model-fit results. The scaled RMSEA of 0.047, 95% CI [0.041, 0.052], suggested a close approximate fit. In contrast, the CFI of 0.627 and TLI of 0.612 were substantially below conventional thresholds, while the robust SRMR of 0.122 was elevated. The significant scaled Chi-square test also indicated a discrepancy between the proposed model and the observed data. Taken together, these findings suggest that the apparently favorable RMSEA should not be interpreted in isolation. The comparative and residual-based fit indices provided limited support for the proposed four-factor model.

The factor-loading pattern further clarified the sources of model misfit. Low Registration demonstrated the most consistent measurement pattern, with all freely estimated loadings reaching statistical significance. Sensation Seeking included a significant negative loading and several weak or nonsignificant loadings. The sensory sensitivity factor was particularly unstable: one item loaded negatively, and none of its freely estimated loadings was statistically significant. Sensation Avoiding performed better, although one indicator had a weak, nonsignificant loading. These results indicate that the four quadrants were not equally well represented by their assigned items in this sample.

A further concern was the very high standardized correlation between sensory sensitivity and sensation avoiding (r = 0.972). This finding suggests inadequate discriminant validity and indicates that these two factors were not empirically distinguishable in the present sample. The non-positive-definite latent covariance matrix reinforces this concern and indicates that the factor correlations and related parameter estimates should be interpreted cautiously. The high correlation may reflect substantial conceptual overlap between the two constructs, sample-specific response patterns, or differences in how participants understood the translated items. It should not, however, be interpreted as definitive evidence that the two quadrants are universally equivalent.

Several cultural and linguistic factors may have contributed to the observed findings. Although translated items may preserve their literal meaning, respondents may interpret the frequency, relevance, or social acceptability of sensory behaviors differently across cultural and language contexts. Items describing sensory sensitivity and active avoidance may also be difficult to distinguish if respondents perceive both as manifestations of the same underlying discomfort. Cognitive interviews with Kannada-speaking respondents could help determine whether particular items are ambiguous, culturally unfamiliar, or interpreted differently from their intended meaning.

The findings have practical implications. The Kannada AASP may be useful for describing sensory-processing patterns at the group level, particularly for Low Registration and Sensation Avoiding, but individual quadrant scores should be interpreted cautiously. Sensation Seeking and Sensory Sensitivity scores require caution because of their lower internal consistency and problematic item-level performance. Until additional validation evidence is available, clinical or research decisions should not rely exclusively on a single AASP quadrant score. Interpret scores alongside clinical observations, interviews, and other relevant assessments.

This study has several limitations. The CFA included 60 ordinal indicators but only 150 participants, which may have reduced the stability and precision of the parameter estimates. The analysis also produced a non-positive-definite latent covariance matrix. The findings were obtained from a single Kannada-speaking sample and may not generalize to other populations or clinical groups. In addition, item modifications were not tested in an independent sample; therefore, data-driven item deletion or model specification could have capitalized on sample-specific characteristics.

Future research should examine the Kannada AASP in a larger and more diverse sample. Exploratory analyses, cognitive interviewing, and examination of item response distributions may help clarify why certain items performed poorly. Researchers should then evaluate any revised structure using CFA in an independent sample. Researchers may also consider alternative, theoretically justified models, including those that account for the substantial overlap between sensory sensitivity and sensation avoiding. Cross-validation will be essential before recommending changes to the established quadrant structure or scoring system.

This finding is not unique: cross-cultural adaptations of the AASP have repeatedly demonstrated normative and structural differences from the original. In the Arabic translated version, different cut-off scores were observed, particularly for sensory sensitivity and sensation avoiding. 8 In the Hebrew translated version, factor relationships were modified. 9 In the Chinese translated version, item-level incompatibilities were noted. 11 The present study findings depict a similar pattern. Further, the present translation suggests that the AASP’s four-factor form may require culture-specific adjustment for the Kannada-speaking population.

A sample size of n = 150 is also likely underpowered for a 60-item, four-factor CFA (requiring an estimated 400–800 participants for stable parameter estimates). The unstable model fit observed here may partly reflect sampling variance. Future studies should target substantially larger samples.

Test–retest Reliability

The moderate test–retest correlations (r = 0.58–0.69) suggest this is acceptable. However, this is not ideal temporal stability. These values fall below the r ≥ 0.70 threshold, which is a recommended threshold for clinical assessment tools. 19 The sample size for the test–retest was small (n = 25) in the present study; further, the test–retest study was limited to adult participants. This leaves a lacuna in establishing reliability in adolescents and the elderly. Future studies may obtain test–retest data across all three target age groups.

In conclusion, the Kannada AASP demonstrated moderate test–retest reliability but inconsistent internal consistency across quadrants. The four-factor CFA provided limited support for the proposed structure, with problematic loadings and insufficient discriminant validity between sensory sensitivity and sensation avoiding. The results support cautious use of the current version while highlighting the need for further cultural, linguistic, and psychometric refinement.

Cultural Observations

Qualitative observations during data collection highlighted the cultural dimensions of sensory experience in Karnataka state. Here, the participants gave responses that depended on the relationship of the person involved (e.g., response to being touched would depend on who was touching them; response to auditory stimuli would depend on social context and expectations of deference). This indicated a collectivistic cultural orientation toward sensory response (based on relationship). This differs from the original AASP. The original AASP assumed an individualistic framing of sensory preferences. This contextual interpretation was especially evident in items requiring auditory (Items 50, 53, and 56–59) and tactile (Items 27, 30, 35, 38, and 39) responses. The occupational therapist clarified item intent during tool administration. This was useful because self-administration without professional support would have produced unreliable responses, especially in this cultural context.

Dietary habits have also influenced responses. Item 5 (eating familiar foods) was difficult for participants with limited autonomy over food choices. Many participants ate whatever was available rather than based on personal sensory preferences. Items like this in the tool will require local and cultural adaptation. Only then will the items reflect the lived realities of the target population.

Limitations

The principal limitations are highlighted in the sections above. The main limitations include (a) inadequate small sample size: the sample was inadequate for stable CFA estimation; (b) the study had 70% females: this gender imbalance will have an effect on certain item-level responses, such as for item 28; (c) the test–retest reliability was only for adults; (d) restriction to only one region of Karnataka: may not be generalizable to other regions of the state; (e) reliability was estimated using Pearson’s r instead of ICC; and (f) dialect: single-dialect group did the translation. The expert panel for content validity consisted of only three raters, the minimum recommended number, which reduces the statistical precision of I-CVI estimates.

Future Directions

Future research should (a) replicate the CFA with a substantially larger, geographically and gender-balanced sample; (b) conduct EFA to identify a culture-specific factor structure; (c) evaluate the tool’s psychometric properties in clinical populations with schizophrenia and other mental health conditions; (d) assess measurement invariance across age groups and gender; (e) obtain ICC-based test–retest reliability across all three age groups; and (f) review and culturally adapt items with negative or nonsignificant factor loadings in collaboration with the Kannada-speaking community.

Conclusions

This study presents the first Kannada translation and psychometric evaluation of the Adult Adolescent Sensory Profile. The translated tool demonstrates acceptable content validity (S-CVI/UA = 0.82) and adequate internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.72–0.93). However, the four-factor structure of the original AASP did not replicate adequately in this sample, as evidenced by poor CFA fit indices and near-unity covariances between sensory sensitivity and sensation avoiding. These findings underscore the need for culture-specific validation and potential structural adaptation of the AASP for the Kannada-speaking population. Nonetheless, the Kannada AASP represents a meaningful first step toward equitable sensory modulation assessment for the 44 million Kannada speakers in India and provides a validated foundation for future clinical and research use in occupational therapy practice.
