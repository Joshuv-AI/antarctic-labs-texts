Nasal obstruction during development leads to defective synapse elimination, hypersynchrony, and impaired cerebellar function
Authors: Moe Tanigawa, Mengke Liu, Mariko Sekiguchi, Kyosuke Goda, Chiho Kato, Takashi Ono, Naofumi Uesaka
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Nasal respiratory disorders are linked to craniofacial anomalies and systemic dysfunctions. However, the implications of nasal respiratory disorders on brain development and their subsequent impact on brain functionalization remain largely unknown. Here, we describe that nasal obstruction from postnatal developmental stages in mice precipitates deficits in cerebellum-associated behaviors and compromised refinement and maturation of neural circuits in the cerebellum. We show that mice with nasal obstruction during developmental phases exhibit marked impairments in motor function and exhibit increased immobility time in forced swimming test. Additionally, we identified critical periods during which nasal respiration is essential for optimizing motor function and preserving mental health. Our study also reveals that nasal obstruction in mice disrupts the typical developmental process of synapse elimination in the cerebellum and hinders the normal transition of activity patterns in cerebellar Purkinje cell populations during development. Through comparing activity patterns in mouse models subjected to nasal obstruction at various stages, we suggest that the maturation of specific activity pattern among Purkinje cell populations is fundamental to the functional integrity of the cerebellum. Our findings highlight the indispensable role of adequate nasal respiration during development for the establishment and functional integrity of neural circuits, thereby significantly affecting brain function.

Introduction

Nasal respiratory disorders have been implicated in the perturbation of structural malformations and systemic physiological functions, suggesting a complex interplay between nasal respiration and various biological processes1,2. During the developmental window spanning prepuberty to adolescence, nasal obstruction has been causatively linked with a spectrum of dental and craniofacial malformations, including anterior open bite, maxillary protrusion, and transverse dental crossbite3. Beyond its role in physical structure, recent studies have revealed that nasal respiration contributes significantly to neural activity, affecting domains as crucial as learning and memory4. Studies involving human subjects have elucidated that disruption in nasal airflow can impair cortical rhythms, thereby diminishing memory performance and affecting the consolidation of episodic events5,6. This disruption highlights the neural impact of nasal respiratory disorders, extending beyond physical malformations to cognitive functions. In animal models, nasal respiratory deficits characterized by nasal obstruction have been shown to induce dysregulation of hippocampal molecular signaling as well as marked impairments in memory and learning capabilities, providing a mechanistic insight into the cognitive deficits observed with nasal respiratory disorders7,8. Moreover, while nasal respiratory disorders may not directly cause cognitive deficits, they may indirectly lead to these issues through mechanisms such as reduced sleep quality, insufficient oxygen supply, and decreased quality of life. Despite these significant advances in understanding the broad impacts of nasal respiratory disorders, the specific implications of developmental nasal obstruction on brain development and functionalization remain largely unexplored. This gap in knowledge underscores the need for further research to delineate the mechanisms through which nasal respiration influences neural circuit formation and maturation, and how disruptions in this process might contribute to long-term deficits in brain function.

In this study, we utilized a mouse model of nasal respiratory disorders, induced through unilateral nasal closure7–10 to investigate the impact of nasal respiration during critical developmental stages on the refinement of neural circuits and the activity patterns of neural ensembles, as well as on overall brain functionality. Our comprehensive analysis demonstrates that nasal obstruction during key developmental phases significantly impairs motor coordination in rotarod test and elicits increased immobility time in the forced swimming test interpreted often as depressive-like behaviors, while it does not markedly influence social interaction or general activity levels. Importantly, our findings highlight specific developmental windows during which nasal respiration plays a crucial role in the development of motor function and likely maintenance of mental health. To examine the link between these impaired behaviors and neural circuit formation, we focused on the cerebellum because the cerebellum plays a crucial role in sensory-motor functions, including chewing, postural control, tone, movement, the coordination of balance, eye movements, and reflexes11,12. Researches have also highlighted the potential role of the cerebellum in depressive-like behaviors, extending beyond its traditional association with motor control13. We found that nasal obstruction during the critical period disrupts the normal process of synapse elimination and alters the standard progression of neural activity patterns among Purkinje cells (PCs), essential processes that typically occur within pivotal developmental stages. These findings emphasize the vital importance of uninterrupted nasal respiration during development for the support of motor coordination and mental health, likely through facilitating the proper maturation of cerebellar neural circuits.

Results

Nasal obstruction from postnatal development results in increased immobility and impaired motor function in adult mice

To examine effects of nasal respiration obstruction on brain development and functionalization, we focused on the cerebellum. The cerebellum plays a crucial role in sensory-motor functions, including chewing, postural control, tone, movement, the coordination of balance, eye movements, and reflexes11,12. Additionally, the cerebellum is involved in cognitive, social, and emotional coordination14,15. To elucidate the roles of developmental nasal respiration in these brain functions, we utilized the nasal obstruction mouse model with right-sided nasal closure by selective cauterization of the right external nostril at postnatal day 3 (P3_UNO) (Supplementary Fig. 1A). These mice have been shown to have a notable reduction in blood oxygen saturation level8,16. There was no significant difference in the body mass between the control and P3_UNO mice at P24 (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Moreover, we investigated whether nasal obstruction during the growth period affects the gross morphology of the cerebral cortex and cerebellum. Our analysis revealed no statistically significant differences between control mice and P3_UNO mice at P20–24 in two key aspects: the thickness of the cerebral cortex and the total area of the cerebellum (Supplementary Fig. 1C–F). These findings suggest that nasal obstruction during this developmental period does not substantially alter these specific morphological features of the brain.

We implemented a suite of behavioral assays to assess anxiety, locomotor activity, sociality, and potential depressive-like behaviors in mice at adult stage (Supplementary Fig 1G). To evaluate locomotor activity and anxiety levels, open field tests were conducted. Analysis revealed no significant difference in the distance traveled between P3_UNO and control mice, suggesting comparable levels of locomotor activity (Fig. 1A, B). Additionally, the proportion of time spent in the central zone of the open field was similar between the two groups (Fig. 1A, B), suggesting no substantial difference in anxiety-related behaviors. We assessed a mouse sociality through a sociality test, wherein we measured the duration spent by the mice in proximity to a novel mouse and an inanimate mouse doll (Fig. 1C). The sociality index, calculated as the ratio of time spent near the novel mouse to that spent near the mouse doll, showed no significant difference between P3_UNO and control mice (Fig. 1D). The finding suggests that nasal respiration impairment does not notably influence social behaviors. To determine the effects of nasal obstruction on motor performance, we performed a rotarod test with increased rotation speed. When we analyzed the latency to fall over 3 consecutive days, P3_UNO mice showed significantly shorter latency to fall from the rod than control mice from days 1 to 3 (Fig. 1E). To assess potential motor learning deficits resulting from nasal obstruction, we compared fall latencies between consecutive testing days (Days 1–2 and Days 2–3). We calculated the ratio of each day’s performance to the previous day’s performance. No significant differences in these ratios were observed between control and P3_UNO mice (Fig. 1F). To examine a behavior representing depressive-like state, we conducted a forced swimming test, recognized as an assay by which we may interpret behavior patterns as depressive-like states. The P3_UNO mice exhibited prolonged periods of immobility and reduced swimming durations compared to control mice (Fig. 1G). Given that increased immobility in the forced swimming test can be interpreted as a depressive-like behavior, these findings suggest that the P3_UNO mice may exhibit depressive-like states attributed to the nasal obstruction from early postnatal development. Unilateral nasal obstruction is considered to constrain respiratory efficacy, with putative repercussions for cardiopulmonary capability, potentially culminating in the impaired motor performance in the rotarod test and immobility during the forced swimming test. To discern whether acute nasal obstruction initiated from adulthood influences motor performance and immobility in forced swimming test, unilateral nasal obstruction was induced in P45 mice (Adult_acute_UNO, Supplementary Fig. 1G). Subsequent behavioral evaluations, including the rotarod and forced swimming tests, were conducted at P48. We found no significant difference in the latency to fall from the rotarod over a 3-day testing period between control and Adult_acute_UNO mice (Fig. 2A). Moreover, there were no significant differences in the ratio of the latency to fall of a day to the previous day’s performance between control and the UNO mice (Fig. 2B). In the forced swimming test, the durations of immobility and swimming for Adult_acute_UNO mice were not markedly different from those observed in control mice (Fig. 2G). Considering the possibility that nasal obstruction may not influence the developmental trajectory of mice, we examined a critical period of the effects of nasal respiration on cerebellar function. We generated two types of mouse models of chronic nasal obstruction: one with chronic nasal obstruction from the adult stage (Adult_chronic_UNO), wherein the obstruction was instituted during P52–54, and another with chronic nasal obstruction from 3 weeks of age (3W_UNO) (Supplementary Fig 1G). These two types of mice were subjected to a suite of behavioral assessments after 2 months. For Adult_chronic_UNO mice, there were no significant differences in the latency to fall from the rod during the 3-day testing period compared to control mice (Fig. 2C). There were no significant differences in the ratio of the latency to fall of a day to the previous day’s performance between control and the Adult_chronic_UNO mice (Fig. 2D). The forced swimming test did not exhibit significant differences in the durations of immobility and swimming between control and Adult_chronic_UNO mice (Fig. 2H). In contrast, 3W_UNO mice had a shorter latency to fall than control mice during the 3-day test period (Fig. 2E), indicating the impaired motor function. Moreover, the ratio of the latency to fall of Day 3 to that of Day 2 was decreased in 3W_UNO mice compared with the control, suggesting the impaired motor learning (Fig. 2F). However, there was no significant difference in the forced swimming test between control and 3W_UNO mice (Fig. 2I). These aggregated findings suggest developmental critical periods during which cerebellar functionalization is susceptible to modulation by the nasal respiration (postnatal day 3 to postnatal week 3 for immobility behavior in forced swimming test, postnatal weeks 3–7 for motor function).

Nasal obstruction from postnatal day 3 impaired synapse elimination in the PCs of the mouse cerebellum

These results suggest that the deficit of a developmental process by nasal obstruction leads to impaired motor function and immobility phenotype interpreted as depressive-like behavior. The cerebellum emerged as a primary region of interest for several compelling reasons. First, it plays a crucial role in motor function, which aligns with the observed motor abnormalities. Second, recent studies have implicated the cerebellum in the depressive behaviors13. Additionally, the cerebellum’s known involvement in respiratory control suggests it might be particularly susceptible to the effects of nasal obstruction. Furthermore, the cerebellum undergoes significant development and refinement during the postnatal period, making it potentially vulnerable to environmental perturbations such as impaired nasal breathing during critical developmental stages. Thus, we decided to investigate the neural circuit development in the cerebellum to elucidate the underlying neural mechanisms of these observed behaviors.

During the 1st–3rd postnatal week when a developmental process can affect the immobility phenotype in forced swimming test, climbing fiber (CF) to PC synapses in the cerebellum undergo selective strengthening of a single CF and elimination of surplus CFs, ultimately leading to the mono-innervation of PCs by a dominant CF17,18. This process, termed synapse elimination, is thought to be crucial for brain functional integrity19–21. Yet, the influence of nasal obstruction on these critical developmental processes remains unclear.

To assess the repercussions of nasal obstruction on the synapse elimination, we evaluated the elimination of CF synapses from PC somata at P21–24 when CF synapse elimination is typically finalized and at the mature adult stage. We found that the number of VGluT2 puncta on PC somata in P3_UNO mice at P21–24 was larger than that in control mice (Fig. 3A, C). In adult P3_UNO mice, the number of VGluT2 puncta on PC somata remained larger than that in control mice (Fig. 3B, D). We meticulously examined CF synapse elimination through comprehensive electrophysiological analysis. Utilizing whole-cell recording from visually identified PCs in mouse acute cerebellar slices, we stimulated CFs in the granule cell layer. This stimulation elicited a clearly discernible excitatory postsynaptic current (EPSC) in an all-or-none fashion in PCs. To differentiate CF-EPSCs from parallel fiber (PF)-mediated EPSCs, we employed pairs of stimuli, a method that has been validated in previous research22–24. In cases of multiple innervations, we observed more than one discrete step of CF-mediated EPSC, which became apparent either by gradually increasing stimulus intensity or by systematically moving the stimulating electrode. The number of CFs innervating a given PC was estimated by counting these discrete CF-EPSC steps. In control mice, the majority of PCs (>93%) exhibited large EPSCs in an all-or-none manner, indicative of single CF innervation (Fig. 3E). However, in P3_UNO mice, a significant deviation was observed: 60% of PCs had single CF-EPSC steps, while 40% exhibited two CF-EPSC steps (Fig. 3E). The frequency distribution histogram of PCs, in terms of the number of CF-EPSC steps, showed a significant difference between control and P3_UNO mice (Fig. 3F). These data suggest a persistent developmental deficiency in the elimination of somatic CF synapses in P3_UNO mice. The amplitude of total CF-EPSCs and the paired-pulse ratio of CF-EPSCs did not differ significantly between control and P3_UNO mice (Fig. 3G, H), indicating that the total CF inputs into PCs and the characteristics of CF presynapses remained unaltered in P3_UNO mice compared to controls. We further examined the amplitude of CF inputs, categorizing them into three types: dominant CFs (single CFs in mono-innervation and strongest CFs in multiple-innervation cases), strongest CFs, and weaker CFs. Our analysis showed no significant differences in the amplitudes of any CF type between control and P3_UNO mice (Supplementary Fig. 2). The relative height of the VGluT2-labeled CF terminus to the thickness of the molecular layer was 73.81 ± 0.88 (mean ± SEM) % for control mice, but 65.05 ± 1.05 (mean ± SEM) % for P3_UNO mice during P21–24 (Fig. 4A, C). There was a significant difference of the relative CF height between control and P3_UNO mice during P21–24. Since we used mice with unilateral obstruction of the right nose, we next investigated the potential bilateral effects on cerebellar synapse elimination. We examined whether right-side nasal obstruction impaired CF synapse elimination in both cerebellar hemispheres. Quantification of VGluT2 puncta on PC somata revealed a significantly higher number in P3_UNO mice compared to control mice at P20 in both left and right cerebellar hemispheres (Supplementary Fig. 3A–D). Additionally, the relative height of VGluT2-labeled CF terminals with respect to the molecular layer thickness was significantly different between control and P3_UNO mice in both hemispheres (Supplementary Fig. 3E–H). These findings suggest that unilateral nasal obstruction affects CF synapse elimination bilaterally in the developing cerebellum (Supplementary Fig. 3E–H).

Past studies have demonstrated that glial cells are instrumental in the regulation of synapse elimination in the cerebellum and other nervous system25–29. Moreover, parallel fibers (PFs) onto PCs are shown to affect CF synapse elimination17,23. To determine whether nasal occlusion from the early development affects glial cell and PFs during development within the mouse cerebellum, we analyzed the density of oligodendrocytes, microglia, and astrocytes (Bergmann glia) and PF inputs onto PCs in the developing cerebellum. The number of oligodendrocytes in the cerebellum at P14 was not different between control and P3_UNO mice as depicted in Supplementary Fig. 4A, B. The density of microglia in P3_UNO mice was also not significantly different compared with control mice (Supplementary Fig. 4C, D). The density of Bergmann glia, astrocytes specific to the cerebellum, did not exhibit significant difference between these two groups (Supplementary Fig. 4E, F). Thus, these data likely support that the influence of unilateral nasal occlusion on synaptic elimination is independent of glial cell-mediated mechanisms, though the possibility that functional changes in glial cells may affect synapse elimination cannot be entirely ruled out. We also found no significant difference of PF inputs onto PCs between control and P3_UNO (Supplementary Fig. 5), suggesting that impaired synapse elimination is not mediated by an abnormality of PF inputs.

Nasal obstruction from P3 leads to abnormal activity pattern of PC ensembles

During early cerebellar development, PCs exhibit a phase of synchronized activity, which gradually transitions to a decorrelated state as development progresses. It has been suggested that the shift from synchronization to desynchronization is associated with the process of CF synapse elimination20. We probed the potential role of developmental nasal respiration in modulating the PC activity in the cerebellum. We utilized in vivo calcium imaging to observe spontaneous PC population activity in the developmental window of P13–15. We injected adeno-associated viruses (AAVs) with the gene encoding jGCaMP7f under the control of the L76 promoter30 into the mouse cerebellum at P6-8. Post-AAV injection, we performed cranial window surgeries and in vivo calcium imaging during P13–15 and at adult stage. Calcium transients from designated regions of interest (ROIs), each representing single PC or a few PCs, were extracted (Fig. 5A and Supplementary Fig. 6A). We quantitatively evaluated the synchronicity of PC activity by computing pairwise correlation coefficients of jGCaMP7f fluorescent intensity alterations among PCs (Fig. 5B and Supplementary Fig. 6B, C). At P13–15, we observed a marked elevation in cross-correlation in P3_UNO mice compared to controls (Fig. 5C). The mean cross-correlations in control mice for distances of 0–160 µm and 160–320 µm were 0.70 ± 0.023 (mean ± SEM) and 0.51 ± 0.03 (mean ± SEM), respectively, contrasting with 0.86 ± 0.01 (mean ± SEM) and 0.80 ± 0.015 (mean ± SEM) in P3_UNO mice, signifying a notable difference between these two groups (Fig. 5D). Previous research has established a correlation between individual calcium transients of PCs and the occurrence of complex spikes within these cells20. To discern potential variations in the complex spike activity of PCs between control and P3_UNO groups, we conducted an analysis of the calcium transient kinetics in PCs (Supplementary Fig. 7). Our findings revealed a notable diminution in the frequency of calcium transients in individual PCs in P3_UNO mice compared to controls (Fig. 5E). However, the temporal width of these transients showed no significant difference between P3_UNO and control mice (Fig. 5F). The amplitude of calcium transients was markedly elevated in P3_UNO mice during P13–15 (Fig. 5G). No significant differences between the two groups were detected in the area of individual calcium transients (Fig. 5H). Given that calcium transients in PCs are primarily triggered by complex spikes resulting from CF inputs, we further investigated the characteristics of complex spikes in both groups. Despite the observed differences in calcium transient amplitudes, our analysis revealed no significant disparities in complex spike properties between control and P3_UNO mice at P15–16 (Supplementary Fig. 8). This finding suggests that the altered amplitude of calcium transients in P3_UNO mice is not attributed to changes in complex spike characteristics, but rather to a densification of CF inputs, which is consistent with a decrease in calcium transient frequency.

PCs in the cerebellum, the sole output neurons in the cerebellar cortex, play a pivotal role in cerebellar function, suggesting that activity pattern of PC ensembles represents cerebellar function. Extending calcium imaging analysis to the adult phase (P59–64), we found that cross-correlation among PCs in P3_UNO mice remained significantly elevated compared with that in control mice (Fig. 5I–K and Supplementary Fig. 6D, E). In control mice, mean cross-correlations at distances of 0–160 µm and 160–320 µm were 0.65 ± 0.02 (mean ± SEM) and 0.48 ± 0.022 (mean ± SEM), respectively, while P3_UNO mice displayed values of 0.74 ± 0.02 (mean ± SEM) and 0.60 ± 0.027 (Fig. 5L). A notable diminution in the frequency of calcium transients in individual PCs in P3_UNO mice compared to controls (Fig. 5M), although the differences were not observed for width, amplitude, and area of these transients in the adult stage (Fig. 5N–P). The persistent higher synchronicity and altered kinetics in P3_UNO mice suggest an enduring disruption in the maturation of PC population activity, underscoring the pivotal role of nasal respiration in the normal developmental trajectory of PC activity within the cerebellum.

These data suggest that the frequency of complex spikes in individual PCs is reduced by UNO across both developmental and adult stages. Additionally, the larger amplitudes in P3_UNO suggest a concurrent occurrence of a larger number of complex spikes in mice during postnatal development, as compared to typically developing counterparts. This altered pattern of complex spike activity in PCs due to UNO underscores the intricate relationship between nasal respiration and cerebellar PC activity across various stages of development and maturation.

Nasal respiration affects cerebellar function in specific critical periods

To elucidate the relationship between PC activity patterns and behavioral phenotypes, we conducted calcium imaging experiments on two mouse models: Adult_acute_UNO and 3W_UNO. Our analysis revealed a reduction in the cross-correlation among proximal PC populations in Adult_acute_UNO mice, while distal PC populations remained unaffected (Fig. 6A–D and Supplementary Fig. 6F, G). We observed a significant increase in the frequency of calcium transients in Adult_acute_UNO mice compared to control mice (Fig. 6E), alongside a notable reduction in the amplitude of these transients (Fig. 6O). However, the width and area of calcium transients did not significantly differ between Adult_acute_UNO and control mice (Fig. 6F–H). In contrast, for 3W_UNO mice, the cross-correlation between PCs decreased in both proximal and distal PC populations when compared to control mice (Fig. 6I–L and Supplementary Fig. 6H, I), with no significant differences observed in the frequency, width, and area of calcium transients (Fig. 6M, N, P and Supplementary Fig. 7). The amplitude of calcium transients was significantly reduced in 3W_UNO mice (Fig. 6O). These findings suggest a shared alteration in PC activity patterns between P3_UNO and 3W_UNO mice, both of which demonstrated impaired motor function. This alteration pertains to the synchronicity of activity among PCs, including both proximal and distal populations, albeit in opposing directions for P3_UNO (increase) and 3W_UNO (decrease), suggesting that proper activity synchrony among PCs in broad cerebellar region is necessary for motor function. As a specific change observed in P3_UNO mice exhibiting the immobility phenotype in forced swimming test, there was a decrease in the frequency of PC activity, accompanied by an increase in both the amplitude and synchrony of PC activity. Discussion

This study illuminates the profound effects of nasal obstruction during developmental stages on neural circuit formation, the activity patterns of neural ensembles, and behavior, with a focus on cerebellar dynamics. Taken together, these findings suggest the necessity of maintaining neural activity pattern dependent on nasal respiration during specific developmental periods for the optimal structuring and functionalization of cerebellar circuitries.

Our findings in the mouse model of nasal obstruction have potential clinical implications. Chronic nasal obstruction in humans, often associated with conditions such as allergic rhinitis, adenoid hypertrophy, or congenital nasal deformities, has been linked to various behavioral and emotional phenotypes. For instance, children with sleep-disordered breathing, which can result from nasal obstruction, show higher rates of depression, anxiety, and attention problems compared to their healthy peers31. Similarly, adults with obstructive sleep apnea often present with mood disorders and cognitive impairments32. These clinical observations align with our findings and the cerebellum’s known involvement in cognitive and emotional processing15. Our findings underscore the importance of considering the broader neurological implications of nasal respiratory disorders in clinical practice and highlight the need for a multidisciplinary approach in managing these conditions.

The question arises whether chronic mild hypoxia would produce a similar phenotype to what we observed with nasal obstruction. Chronic mild hypoxia has been shown to affect brain development and working memory33. Nasal obstruction induced similar phenotypes8. Nasal obstruction not only potentially induces mild hypoxia but also alters breathing patterns, affects sensory inputs, and may induce stress responses. While hypoxia may play a role in our observed phenotype, the full spectrum of effects seen with nasal obstruction likely involves additional mechanisms beyond those induced by hypoxia alone.

Our study’s interpretation of behavioral changes in mice with chronic nasal obstruction requires careful consideration. The forced swim test showed decreased swimming time, often associated with depressive-like behavior. However, labeling these changes as a clear “depressive-like state” may be an overstatement. Several factors could contribute to the observed behaviors: chronic nasal obstruction might impair motor abilities through compromised airflow and reduced oxygen delivery to muscles34, without necessarily indicating depression. Acute nasal obstruction did not immediately impair behaviors, ruling out direct respiratory effects. Therefore, we propose that the behavioral changes observed, particularly in the forced swim test, likely result from a complex interplay of potential mood alterations, physical discomfort, and motor impairments related to chronic nasal obstruction. While these changes share some features with depressive-like behaviors, our data do not provide conclusive evidence for a full depressive-like state. Future studies employing a wider range of behavioral tests, physiological measurements, and longer-term observations may further elucidate the nature of these behavioral changes and their relationship to depressive states.

From an anatomical perspective, the cerebellar cortex, intricately folded into discrete lobes, comprises the anterior cerebellar lobes (I–V), the posterior cerebellar lobes (VI–IX), and the cohesive cerebellar lobes (X)35. Each cerebellar region is associated with distinct functional domains, encompassing sensory-motor, cognitive, emotional, and social behaviors36–40. The anterior cerebellum, known for its preferential interaction with the motor and somatosensory cortices, plays a pivotal role in sensory-motor functions. Thus, the motor function impairments observed in our study may be directly linked to disruptions in the circuitry of the anterior cerebellar lobes. Further anatomical subdivision reveals a central mediastinum, a middle subdiastinum, and a lateral hemisphere within the anterior and posterior lobes35. Notably, the mediastinum and the accessory mediastinum are connected to the limbic system35, a crucial network activated during emotional tasks41–43. This intricate connection between the cerebellum and the limbic system could elucidate the depressive-like behavior observed in our nasal obstruction model.

Nasal obstruction can have effects on various behavioral deficits6,44–48. However, it was unclear how long nasal obstruction or nasal respiratory disorders would impair these brain functions. It was also unclear at what period in life they would affect brain functions. The immobility phenotype in forced swimming test interpreted as depressive-like state and impaired motor function in P3_UNO mice were not attributed to the direct effects of transient nasal closure but rather to nasal obstruction beginning at the developmental stage. This raises a compelling hypothesis: long-term nasal obstruction may influence mental and motor function indirectly, primarily through alterations in cerebellar circuitry, rather than through immediate respiratory distress or other systemic effects. This hypothesis is supported by the cerebellum’s role in cognitive and emotional processing and the potential for prolonged nasal obstruction during critical developmental periods to lead to profound changes in cerebellar activity and neural connections. These alterations might accumulate over time, leading to the observed behavioral changes49. The impact of nasal obstruction on cerebellar circuits underscores the importance of considering long-term developmental factors in the etiology of nasal aspiratory disorders and opens new avenues for therapeutic interventions targeting these altered neural pathways.

The results of our experiment demonstrate that nasal obstruction from the developmental period in mice resulted in sustained high synchronization of PC activity, and deficient motor behavior and increased immobility behavior, but nasal obstruction from later developmental period (3 weeks old) led to low synchronization and deficient motor behavior. These findings raise important questions about whether high or low synchronization is more beneficial for brain function. High synchronization of PCs is crucial for precise and coordinated motor activities, as it ensures that movements are smooth and well-timed50. However, previous studies suggest that CF-induced calcium transients are highly synchronized between PCs in immature mice and then desynchronize with development20 and prolonged high synchronization can also be detrimental, potentially leading to rigid and less adaptable motor behaviors and contributing to affective disorders such as depression51,52. The sustained high synchronization observed in nasal obstruction mice may thus hinder these adaptive processes, leading to both motor deficits and increased immobility in forced swimming test observed in our study. Desynchronization, on the other hand, allows for more flexible and adaptive motor responses, which are essential during the learning of new motor skills50. This concept is consistent with our findings that nasal obstruction from 3 weeks resulted in low synchronization and motor learning deficit. These suggest that a balanced state of synchronization and desynchronization is necessary for optimal motor function/learning in rotarod test and mobility states in forced swimming test, highlighting the importance of dynamic regulation of PC activity in the cerebellum13.

CFs are critical for generating complex spikes in PCs, which are characterized by significant calcium influx53,54. We observed the decrease of calcium transient frequency in PCs of P3_UNO mice. Several mechanisms could account for the decreased calcium transients observed. A reduction in calcium transients likely reflects a decrease in the frequency or efficacy of CF input. There may be a direct reduction in the firing rate of CFs, which typically fire at a low frequency but with high synchronicity55; any disruption to this pattern could diminish calcium transients in PCs56. Additionally, synaptic plasticity at the CF–PC synapse, such as long-term depression (LTD) or long-term potentiation (LTP), could alter synaptic strength and modulate calcium influx57. Lastly, intrinsic changes within PCs, such as alterations in calcium channel expression or function and changes in intracellular calcium buffering, could reduce calcium signals independently of CF activity56.

This study reveals for the first time that aberrant biological functions or atypical sensory inputs, such as nasal obstruction, affect the trajectory of synapse elimination in the cerebellum. We propose several interconnected mechanisms underlying this observation: (1) altered neuronal activity patterns due to disrupted breathing, affecting activity-dependent synapse elimination; (2) oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction resulting from intermittent hypoxia, potentially disrupting synaptic plasticity; (3) neuroinflammation triggered by nasal obstruction, influencing microglial-mediated synaptic pruning; (4) epigenetic modifications induced by developmental stress, altering expression of genes crucial for synapse elimination; and (5) broader disruption of cerebellar microcircuits, indirectly impacting CF–PC synapse elimination. The dysregulation of neurotrophic factors, such as brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), by nasal obstruction7,58,59 could also contribute to the observed impairment in synapse elimination. BDNF–TrkB signaling, known to regulate synapse elimination of CF–PC projections58–60 may be a key mediator of physiological changes by nasal aspiratory disorders.

Furthermore, nasal obstruction during developmental stages significantly impacts the desynchronization process in developing mouse PCs, with effects persisting into adulthood. This disruption in the typical synchronicity and rhythmic firing patterns of PCs could be crucial for cerebellar circuit formation and functionalization. Previous research, including studies by Hashimoto et al.61 and Lorenzetto et al.62, has highlighted the role of PC activity during development in regulating CF synapse elimination. Additionally, research demonstrates that in mice deficient in GluD2 and P/Q-type calcium channels, there is an abnormal synchronization of spontaneous CF responses within PC dendritic populations20,63. These genetic alterations impair CF synapse elimination61,64, suggesting a potential link between excess CF synapses and abnormal synchronization in PC ensembles.

Our findings suggest that nasal obstruction may influence the neural activity patterns in the inferior olive whose neurons send CFs because calcium transients of PCs are mainly elicited by the activation of CFs. The inferior olive is known for its subthreshold oscillations (STOs) and synchronous neuronal activity, which can be facilitated by gap junctions65. These STOs could be crucial for motor task and learning66. Nasal obstruction might alter sensory inputs to the brain, which could, in turn, affect the input pathways to the inferior olive which modulate STOs within the inferior olive, impacting the network dynamics and potentially leading to alterations in motor behavior and coordination. The synchronous activity facilitated by gap junctions in the inferior olive is vital for these processes67,68. Thus, the observed abnormal synchronicity in mice with nasal obstruction may stem from the disrupted inferior olive development and/or functionality.

This study utilized diverse mouse models with chronic nasal obstruction initiated at varying developmental stages, uncovering differences in cerebellar function. Adult_chronic_UNO mice exhibited no significant behavioral differences compared to control mice, suggesting that the effect of nasal respiration on cerebellar function is less important after adulthood. Conversely, nasal respiration from postnatal day 3 is crucial for PC activity, synapse elimination, and the development and maintenance of mental and motor functions. Given that long-term synaptic depression (LTD) is enhanced in depressed patients69 and normal LTD is involved in optimal CF–PC synapse elimination70, it is hypothesized that disrupted neural circuitry formation, characterized by abnormal LTD and impaired synapse elimination due to nasal obstruction in this phase, impacts PC activity and depressive-like behavior. Furthermore, our observations indicate that nasal respiration from 3 weeks to 2 months of age influences PC activity patterns distinctively compared to respiration from postnatal day 3, significantly affecting motor functions without altering depressive-like behaviors. These findings suggest that nasal respiration plays a pivotal role in optimizing motor function and mental health by modulating PC activity patterns in a stage-dependent manner.

In conclusion, this study highlights the intricate relationship between nasal respiration during critical developmental periods and the formation and function of neural circuits in the cerebellum. It underscores the importance of proper nasal respiration during development for not only cognitive development but also for the maintenance of motor functions and mobility states in forced swimming test which may represent mental states. These findings open avenues for further research into the mechanisms linking respiratory patterns with neural development and behavior, and they underscore the potential long-term impacts of developmental nasal obstructions on neurological health.

Methods

Experimental model

Male ICR mice were used in the present study. All experiments were approved by the experimental animal ethics committee of Tokyo Medical and Dental University (approval number: [A2024-067C2]) and conducted in accordance with its guidelines, as well as the ethical guidelines of The Japan Neuroscience Society.

Mice, aged 3 postnatal days (P3), underwent unilateral nasal congestion treatment. Mouse pups at P3 were subjected to cryoanesthesia for 5 min at −18 °C. Pups in the unilateral nasal obstruction (UNO) group experienced right-sided nasal obstruction via cauterization of the right external nostril, a standard method for inducing nasal obstruction in neonates71. Specifically, the surrounding tissue of the right nostril was cauterized using a 1 mm surgical tool, avoiding damage to the olfactory mucosa9. This unilateral treatment was chosen based on evidence suggesting mortality in mice with bilateral nasal congestion72. After the procedure, pups were maintained at 37 °C for 30 min before returning to their cages. Control group pups experienced the same cryoanesthesia duration without nasal treatment. The Adult_acute_UNO mice, Adult_chronic_UNO mice, and 3W_UNO mice underwent nasal congestion treatment at P45, P52–54, and P23–25, respectively, using a similar protocol, but instead of cryoanesthesia, they were anesthetized with isoflurane before the nasal obstruction procedure. Afterward, the Adult_acute_UNO mice, Adult_chronic_UNO, and 3W_UNO mice were allowed to naturally awaken for ~2–3 min before being returned to their cages.

Immunohistochemistry

Mice at P21–24 and P56 of age were deeply anesthetized intraperitoneally with a combination anesthetic of medetomidine/midazolam/butorphanol (0.3/4/5) (100 μg/g of body weight, intraperitoneal injection), perfused with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in 0.1 M phosphate buffer. Sagittal sections (150 μm in thickness) were prepared. The sections were washed three times in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 5 min each and incubated in 1.0% Triton X-100/PBS and bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h at room temperature. Following another PBS wash, the slices were incubated in 1% Triton X-100 in PBS with primary antibodies: a goat monoclonal antibody against Car8 (1 µg/1 ml)(diluted 1:300, #MSFR100500, Nittobo) to visualize PCs; an antibody against guinea pig vesicular glutamate transporter VGluT2 (1 µg/1 ml) (1:300, #MSFR106290, Nittobo) for overnight or for 3 days at 4 °C on shaker. Then we washed the slices with PBS and added secondary antibodies: (an anti-Goat Alexa Fluor 488 antibody; 1:300; or an anti-guinea pig Alexa Fluor 647 antibody; 1:300) in PBS for overnight at 4 °C on shaker. After staining, the slices were put on slide glasses with a mounting medium for fluorescence (Fluorescence Mounting Medium #S3023, Dako), and cover glasses. The sections were examined under a fluorescence microscope (VZ-X800, KEYENCE). For morphological analysis of CFs, we used anti-vesicular glutamate transporter 2 (VGluT2) (guinea pig, 1:300, Nittobo) as primary antibodies, and anti-guinea pig Alexa Fluor 488 (1:300) as secondary antibodies. We quantified VGluT2 signals on the PC soma and dendrite in lobules 3–6 of the cerebellum by using Fiji/ImageJ (JAVA-based processing program, USA).

For ASPA, Iba1, and s100β, mice at P14 of age were deeply anesthetized intraperitoneally with a combination anesthetic of medetomidine/midazolam/butorphanol (0.3/4/5) (100 μg/g of body weight, intraperitoneal injection), perfused with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in 0.1 M phosphate buffer. Sagittal sections (150 μm in thickness) were prepared. The sections were washed three times in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 5 min each and incubated in 1.0% Triton X-100/PBS and bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h at room temperature. Following another PBS wash, the slices were incubated in 1% Triton X-100 in PBS with primary antibodies: a rabbit monoclonal antibody against ASPA (1 µg/1 ml) (diluted 1:1200, #3519817, EMD Millipore Corp) to visualize oligodendrocyte, rabbit antibody (1 µg/1 ml) against Iba1 (1:1000, #019-19741, FUJIFILM) to visualize microglia, and a guinea pig antibody (1 μg/1 ml) against s100β (1:200, #MSFR105350, Nittobo) was used to visualize. These were then mixed on a shaker at 4 °C for 3 days. Then we washed the slices with PBS and added secondary antibodies: (an anti-Rabbit Alexa Fluor 647 antibody; 1:300, an anti-chicken Alexa Fluor 488 antibody; 1:300, an anti-Rabbit cy3 antibody; 1:300, or an anti-guinea pig Alexa Fluor 488 antibody; 1:300) in PBS for overnight at 4 °C on shaker. After staining, the slices were put on slide glasses with a mounting medium for fluorescence (Fluorescence Mounting Medium #S3023, Dako), and cover glasses. The sections were examined under a fluorescence microscope (VZ-X800, KEYENCE). For glial cell analysis, Fiji/ImageJ (JAVA-based processing program, USA) was used to quantify the density per area of oligodendrocytes, microglia, and astrocytes, respectively.

AAV packaging

The jGCaMP7f gene sequence was amplified by PCR from pGP-AAV-syn-jGCaMP7f-WPRE (a gift from Douglas Kim & GENIE Project, Addgene plasmid # 104488) and inserted into sites of GFP in pAAV/L7-6-GFP-WPRE (a gift from Hirokazu Hirai, Addgene plasmid # 126462) (pAAV-L7-6-jGCaMP7f). The AAVs were produced using standard production methods. HEK293 cells were transfected with by Polyethylenimine. PHPeB73 was used. Virus was collected after 120 h from both cell lysates and media. Purification Kit (Takara) was used for viral particle purification. All batches produced were in the range 1010–1011 viral genomes/milliliter.

AAV injection in vivo

Mice at P7–8, P52–58, or P59–61 underwent surgical procedures for AAV injection. Mice in the P7–8 group were anesthetized using isoflurane (4–5% for induction and 1–2.5% for maintenance), while those at P52–58 and P59–61 were deeply anesthetized with a combination anesthetic of medetomidine/midazolam/butorphanol (0.3/4/5) (100 μg/g of body weight, intraperitoneal injection). We exposed skull and made a hole in the bone using a drill (Micromotor LM-II, GC) so as not to damage the cerebellum. Then we performed a tourniquet until the effusion and blood stopped. A 33-gauge Hamilton syringe, loaded with pAAV-L7-6-jGCaMP7f, was placed in an Ultra MicroPump III (WPI). The AAV solution was injected into the cerebellum at rates specific to each age group, controlled by a Micro4 Microsyringe Pump (WPI). We injected 1000 nl of the AAV solution into the cerebellar hole at a rate of 25 nl/min for P7–8 mice, and 1500 nl of the AAV solution at 50 nl/min for P52–58 and 1300 nl of the AAV solution at 100 nl/min for P59–61. The syringe remained in place for an additional 5–10 min post-injection. Finally, the scalp of P7 mice was sutured (nylon No. 8-0, Natsume) and P52–58, P59–61 mice had their scalps glued together (ethyl cyanoacrylate, Aron Alpha A Sankyo, Toagosei). After completion of the surgery, P7 mice were maintained at 37 °C at least for 30 min before returning to their cages. P52–58 or P59–61 mice were maintained at 37 °C at least for 30 min, injected by the atipamezole (0.3 mg/kg, anti-sedan) (100 μg/g of body weight, intraperitoneal injection), and returned to the home cage. We commenced a minimum of 7 days post-surgery to ensure complete recovery and expression of jGCaMP7f expression.

In vivo cranial window surgery for calcium imaging

Control and UNO mice expressing jGCaMP7f at P13–15, P59–64, and P66–68 were anesthetized either with isoflurane (3–4% for induction and 1–2.5% for maintenance) or with a combination anesthetic of medetomidine/midazolam/butorphanol (0.3/4/5) (100 μg/g of body weight, intraperitoneal injection). A 3 mm cranial window was created using a derma punch (3 mm diameter, 213001610, Maruho). After hemostasis was applied to prevent damage to the cerebellum, a coverslip (#CS01078 3 mm diameter, Matsunami) was placed over the craniotomy to prevent the surface from drying out. A metal plate with a hole (4 mm diameter) was affixed using dental adhesive acrylic resin (Superbond, Sun Medical). Once the bond hardened, the head of the mouse was immobilized by attaching the head plate to a custom-made stage. To reverse anesthesia, we then administered atipamezole (0.3 mg/kg, anti-sedan) (100 μg/g of body weight, intraperitoneal injection) for anesthesia reversal. After a 10–15 min waiting period, mice were observed awakening from anesthesia. Imaging was performed using an Olympus MVX10 fluorescence microscope, and data were saved as TIFF files via Andor SOLIS software (Oxford Instruments). We performed calcium imaging from the dendrites of the cerebellar PCs expressing jGCaMP7f in the mice of the awake state. Imaging was performed on PCs located in lobules 5–7.

Calcium imaging data analysis

We resized raw calcium imaging videos (TIFF format) to a resolution of 512 × 512 pixels with a 8-bit depth using ImageJ (JAVA-based processing program, USA). We cropped out the unwanted areas. Regions of interest (ROIs) corresponding to the active PC dendrites were detected using the Suite2p software (Marius Pachitariu, GitHub). This software executed a two-dimensional phase-correlation-based non-rigid image registration in the XY plane, extracted the ROIs considered as PC dendrites, and calculated the fluorescence intensity changes in each ROI. ROIs were extracted using the parameters shown in the Supplementary Table. We exported these data as MAT files. The MAT files were imported into MATLAB R2023b (MathWorks, USA) for further analysis using custom-made MATLAB routines. The fluorescence intensity (F) was converted to ΔF/F0 wave, expressed as ΔF/F0 = (F − F0)/(F0), where F0 is the baseline fluorescence in the absence of calcium transients. F0 was defined as follows. First, we calculated a threshold as mean + 1 SD of the F wave of each ROI, and then the F wave below the threshold was averaged to obtain F0. We excluded ROIs whose area was smaller than 20 pixels and in which the mean baseline intensity in the last 50 frames (5 s) changed more than twice compared with that in the first 50 frames.

Parameters such as amplitude, frequency, half-width, and area of each calcium transient were quantified by the custom-made MATLAB routines. Amplitude is defined as the difference between the peak value of the calcium signal and the baseline value, typically expressed as relative fluorescence intensity (ΔF/F0). Frequency is defined as the number of calcium spikes within a specified time period. The width of a calcium transient is defined as the duration during which the signal remains above the value typically measured at half of the maximum amplitude (half-maximum width). The area of a calcium transient is calculated as the integral of the calcium signal over time, representing the total calcium signal during the event and is often determined as the area under the curve (AUC) of the calcium signal. The correlation coefficient among all ROI pairs was calculated as previously described74. In calcium imaging, we used Pearson’s correlation coefficient to measure the relationship between calcium signals from different ROIs or the same ROI. The resulting correlation matrix captured relationships among all ROI pairs in the dataset.

Electrophysiology

Control and UNO mice were anesthetized with carbon dioxide and sacrificed by decapitation. The ages of mice were >P21 for climbing fiber-mediated EPSCs (CF-EPSCs). Parasagittal cerebellar slices (with 250 μm thickness) were prepared from the left region of the cerebellum, ipsilateral to the obstructed naris, as described previously75. The slices were then bathed in a reservoir chamber in an external solution composed of 125 mM NaCl, 2.5 mM KCl, 2 mM CaCl2, 1 mM MgSO4, 1.25 mM NaH2PO4, 26 mM NaHCO3, and 20 mM glucose, which was bubbled with 95% O2 and 5% CO2 for further 30 min before being transferred to the recording chamber on the stage of an Olympus BX51WI microscope. The chamber was perfused continuously with oxygenated external solution. Picrotoxin (Tocris; 0.1 mM) was added to the bath solution to block GABAA receptor-mediated inhibitory synaptic transmission. The temperature of the chamber in all experiments was held at 32 °C. Patch pipettes were filled with an intracellular solution composed of 60 mM CsCl, 10 mM Cs d-gluconate, 20 mM TEA-Cl, 20 mM BAPTA, 4 mM MgCl2, 4 mM ATP, and 30 mM HEPES (pH 7.3, adjusted with CsOH) for recording CF-EPSCs and PF-EPSCs or 130 K d-gluconate, 10 KCl, 10 NaCl, 10 HEPES, 0.5 EGTA, 4 Mg-ATP, and 0.4 Na-GTP (pH 7.3, adjusted with KOH) for recording complex spikes. The resistance of patch pipette was usually between 1.5 and 2.5 MΩ and the pipette access resistance was compensated by 70%. CF-EPSCs were elicited at a holding potential of −20 mV or −80 mV by electrical pulse stimuli (duration, 0.1 ms) at 0.2 Hz applied to the granule cell layer 20–100 μm from the targeted PC soma. To search all the CFs innervating the PC under recording, stimulation was applied at up to 10 different locations and the stimulus current intensity was gradually increased from 0 to 30 μA at each location. Holding potential was −80 mV for PF-EPSCs. PFs were stimulated in the molecular layer at the position where a maximum response was elicited with the stimulus current of 10 µA. Then, the stimulus intensity was decreased gradually from 10 to 1 µA to obtain input–output relations. Recording was performed on PCs located in lobules 5–8.

Behavioral tests

Behavioral assays were performed with age-matched adult male mice (8–10 weeks). The acute nasal congestion mice underwent nasal congestion at P45 and behavioral experiments were conducted at P48. The chronic nasal obstruction mice underwent nasal blockage treatment at P52–54, and behavioral experiments were performed at P108–110. Mice that were nasally closed from post-weaning were nasally closed at P23–25 and behavioral experiments were conducted at P55–57. All behavioral tests were performed during the dark phase to account for the nocturnal activity patterns of the mice. Prior to all experiments, mice were acclimatized to the behavioral testing areas for at least 30 min. Prior to each testing session, the chamber was sterilized with 70% ethanol to eliminate any olfactory cues from previous subjects. A waiting period of 3–5 min was observed to ensure complete evaporation of the ethanol before initiating the behavioral tests.

Open field test

Mice were placed in an open-field apparatus (50 × 50 × 50 cm). Behavioral activities were recorded for 15 min using a video camera (HC-VX992M, Panasonic). Previous research indicates that mice tend to avoid open spaces and exhibit wall-following behavior as an indicator of anxiety76. The tendency towards anxiety was quantified by evaluating the time spent by the mice in the open central area of the apparatus during the 15-min test period. Simultaneously, the total distance traveled by the mice was measured during the 15-min period. Anxiety-like behaviors and activity levels were extracted from the 15-min video recordings using the MouBeAT plugin77 of Image J software, a JAVA-based image processing program. Subsequent calculations and comparisons between control and P3UNO mice were performed using Microsoft Excel and GraphPad Prism software.

Sociality test

Sociability in mice was assessed by evaluating their preference for a cage containing a novel mouse over a cage containing a novel doll mouse. An open-field apparatus (50 × 50 × 50 cm) was used for the sociality test. In the pretest, two empty cages (8 cm × 8 cm, height 19 cm) were centrally positioned within the open-field apparatus, allowing for free movement around their perimeters. Test mice were acclimated to the environment and recorded for 10 min using the Panasonic video camera. In the subsequent session, a mature male mouse of the ICR strain was introduced into one cage, while the doll mouse was placed in the other. The time taken for the test mice to approach the perimeters of both cages was quantified using the MouBeAT plugin of Image J. Subsequent analyses were conducted using Microsoft Excel. A social index was calculated as the ratio of time spent approaching the novel mouse to the time spent approaching the doll mouse, serving as a measure for social preference.

Rotarod test

We utilized a standard accelerating Rotarod apparatus (Model 76-0770, Panlab/Harvard Apparatus) capable of testing up to five mice simultaneously. Prior to the test, mice were acclimated to the apparatus by being placed on the rods for a 15-min period. Subsequently, four mice were allocated to each rod, which was programmed to accelerate from 4 to 40 rpm over a span of 300 s. In accordance with standard Rotarod procedures, a mouse that fell from the rod landed on a platform connected to a timer, thereby recording the time-to-fall or the raw Rotarod score. Data were captured and subjected to analysis using Microsoft Excel [4]. Trials were conducted three times daily for 3 consecutive days, and the average time-to-fall was calculated to assess motor coordination and learning capabilities.

Forced swim test

Mice were placed in a transparent plastic cylinder (30.5 cm height, 20 cm diameter) filled to a depth of 15 cm with water maintained at 25 ± 0.5 °C. The animals were compelled to swim for a total duration of 6 min, during which their behaviors were captured using the Panasonic video camera. The initial 2 min served as an acclimatization period, followed by a 4-min main test phase. We evaluated swimming time and immobility time to examine the effect of nasal closure on depressive-like behavior. Behavioral episodes were categorized into three types: immobility, characterized by minimal movement and nose placement on the water surface; swimming, involving paddling motions with front or hind legs; and struggling, marked by rapid surface-breaking movements. These behaviors were visually assessed at 5-s intervals.

Statistics and reproducibility

All data are presented as mean ± SD. Statistical significance was assessed by Mann–Whitney U test, Kruskal–Wallis test, one-way ANOVA, and two-way ANOVA. Sample sizes are shown in the figure legends. Statistical analysis was conducted with GraphPad Prism program. Differences between groups were judged to be significant when p values were smaller than 0.05. *, **, ***, ****, and ns represents p < 0.05, p < 0.01, p < 0.001, p < 0.0001, and not significant, respectively.
