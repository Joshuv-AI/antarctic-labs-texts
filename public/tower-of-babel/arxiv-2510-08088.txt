Quantum Algorithm for Low Energy Effective Hamiltonian and Quasi-Degenerate Eigenvalue Problem
Chun-Tse Li, Tzen Ong, Chih-Yun Lin, Yu-Cheng Chen, Hsin Lin, Min-Hsiu Hsieh

Abstract
Quasi-degenerate eigenvalue problems are central to quantum chemistry and condensed-matter physics, where low-energy spectra often form manifolds of nearly degenerate states that determine physical properties. Standard quantum algorithms—such as phase estimation and QSVT-based eigenvalue filtering—work well when a unique ground state is separated by a moderate spectral gap, but in the quasi-degenerate regime they require resolution finer than the intra-manifold splitting; otherwise, they return an uncontrolled superposition within the low-energy span and fail to detect or resolve degeneracies. In this work, we propose a quantum algorithm that directly diagonalizes such quasi-degenerate manifolds by solving an effective-Hamiltonian eigenproblem in a low-dimensional reference subspace. This reduced problem is exactly equivalent to the full eigenproblem, and its solutions are lifted to the full Hilbert space via a block-encoded wave operator. Our analysis provides provable bounds on eigenvalue accuracy and subspace fidelity, together with total query complexity, demonstrating that quasi-degenerate eigenvalue problems can be solved efficiently without assuming any intra-manifold splitting. We benchmark the algorithm on several systems (the Fermi–Hubbard model, LiH, and the transition-metal complex [Ru(bpy) 3 ] 2+ ), demonstrating robust performance and reliable resolution of (quasi-)degeneracies.



1
Introduction

Solving eigenvalue problems and preparing eigenstates are one of the most fundamental tasks across theoretical quantum many body physics, quantum information and quantum chemistry. Most existing efforts on quantum algorithms focus on preparing a single ground state, i.e., the lowest-energy state of the system. These methods, such as phase estimation and QSVT-based eigenvalue filtering
[
GTC19
,
PW09
,
LT20
,
GSLW19
,
MRTC21
,
KCHH25a
,
KCHH25b
]
, work well when the ground state is well separated from all other states by a large
external
energy gap
\Delta_{\mathrm{ext}}
. In many realistic situations, however, the lowest energy levels are not well isolated from the rest of the spectrum but instead bunch together tightly, forming a
quasi-degenerate manifold
. The small spacing between levels inside this manifold is termed the
intra-manifold
splitting
\Delta_{\mathrm{intra}}
. To pick out the exact ground state within such a manifold, previously proposed algorithms would need an energy resolution finer than
\Delta_{\mathrm{intra}}
, which may be exponentially small. With coarser resolution, these methods only produce an arbitrary superposition of states within the manifold: they identify the general low-energy space, but fail to determine whether degeneracy is present, much less solve for and resolve the individual eigenstates.

This limitation has important consequences. For example, in condensed matter physics, locating a quantum phase transition often requires tracking how the lowest energy levels evolve as system parameters change
[
CS05
,
SK11
]
. At a transition point, different symmetry sectors may cross or nearly cross, and the eigenvalue problem becomes quasi-degenerate. Existing approaches based on phase estimation or filtering cannot reliably identify such crossings, since they only return an arbitrary state in the low-energy span without revealing whether multiple levels are nearly degenerate
[
GTC19
,
PW09
,
LT20
,
GSLW19
,
MRTC21
,
RCL+24
]
. Unless one resolves energies finer than the intra-manifold splitting
\Delta_{\mathrm{intra}}
, they return an arbitrary superposition in the low-energy span and provide no canonical basis with which to label the levels. Beyond quantum criticality, low-energy physics often occurs in clustered bands or impurity subspaces near the Fermi level, where downfolding/embedding and Wannier-based projections are standard practice
[
MMY+12
,
SMV01
,
GKKR96
]
; effective models (Heisenberg,
t
–
J
, Kugel–Khomskii) explicitly depend on matrix elements and splittings inside that quasi-degenerate manifold
[
And50
,
ZR88
,
KK82
]
, and in topologically ordered phases the ground-state degeneracy on nontrivial manifolds must be identified together with a canonical basis
[
Kit03
,
CV13
]
.

In quantum chemistry, static (near-degeneracy) correlation in open-shell molecules and catalytic complexes places several electronic configurations within chemical accuracy, shaping structure, magnetism, and reaction barriers; this necessitates multireference, subspace-based treatments rather than single-reference methods
[
S+12
,
HJO00
,
AB70
]
. Quasi-degeneracy also arises along nonadiabatic seams: conical intersections and avoided crossings couple multiple adiabatic surfaces so that dynamics unfolds within a shared subspace; correctly describing these regions requires diagonalizing the coupled states to obtain a controlled basis for forces, transition amplitudes, and nonadiabatic couplings
[
Yar96
,
SS18
,
DYK11
]
. Across both domains, the relevant object is not merely a low-energy state but the quasi-degenerate manifold and its internal structure (canonical basis and splittings), motivating algorithms that diagonalize the manifold rather than simply preparing an element of it.

On the other hand, classical quantum chemistry and condensed-matter physics have developed a rich toolkit for low-energy problems with quasi-degeneracies
[
Fes62
,
Löw62
,
SW66
,
BDL11
]
. At the heart of these approaches lies the effective-Hamiltonian (downfolding) formalism. The idea is to map the full-space eigenproblem onto a much smaller model subspace, yielding an algebraically simpler problem in which interaction effects appear as renormalized (dressed) parameters. When constructed consistently, the effective Hamiltonian reproduces—exactly or, in some scenarios, approximately—the portion of the spectrum supported on the target manifold of the original Hamiltonian, enabling reliable detection of quasi-degeneracies and characterization of low-energy properties. However,
exact
downfolding on a classical computer typically requires manipulating objects that grow exponentially with system size; in practice one therefore resorts to perturbative or low-order series truncations, which can break down in strongly correlated regimes and limit the applicability of effective-Hamiltonian methods to the most challenging systems.

In this work, building on the effective-Hamiltonian philosophy, we address quasi-degenerate eigenvalue problems
without
requiring energy resolution finer than
\Delta_{\mathrm{intra}}
by formulating the task directly at the
subspace
level. We choose a
d
-dimensional low-energy reference subspace
\mathcal{H}_{d}\subset\mathcal{H}
(with
d\ll N
) and denote by
P
the projector onto it (the “
P
-space”). We then solve the effective-Hamiltonian eigenproblem within
\mathcal{H}_{d}
, in the sense of the Feshbach projection framework
[
Fes62
,
Löw62
]
: the reduced problem reproduces exactly those eigenvalues of
H
whose eigenstates have support on
\mathcal{H}_{d}
, and the corresponding model-space eigenvectors are
lifted
to full-space eigenstates using a block-encoded wave operator
\widetilde{\Omega}(\lambda)
. This subspace-based approach naturally diagonalizes quasi-degenerate manifolds, directly detects (quasi-)degeneracies, and is broadly applicable in condensed-matter and quantum-chemistry settings where such manifolds are ubiquitous.

1.1
Main Results Overview

We present, to our knowledge, the first fault-tolerant quantum algorithm that directly addresses (quasi-)degenerate eigenvalue problems through the effective-Hamiltonian formalism. This work is also the first to provide provable guarantees and explicit error bounds in the quasi-degenerate regime. Our method operates at the subspace level and is capable of detecting degeneracies and producing a well-defined basis of low-energy states. We show how the effective Hamiltonian
H_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)
and the associated wave operator
\Omega(\lambda)
can be realized on a quantum computer, assuming access to a block-encoding of the Hamiltonian
H
and a
P
-controlled NOT gate (where
P
is the projector onto the reference subspace). This enables us to estimate the matrix elements of the effective Hamiltonian on a quantum computer and to systematically lift reduced-space solutions
|\hat{\phi}_{k}\rangle
to full Hilbert-space eigenstates
|\widetilde{\Psi}_{k}\rangle
. We further provide a modular error analysis that expresses eigenvalue accuracy and subspace fidelity entirely in terms of three tunable QSVT approximation error, matrix estimation error and root-finding tolerance. Our main results are summarized by the following informal theorems.

Theorem 1

(Informal – Eigenvalue estimation)
.

Given a block-encoding of the Hamiltonian
H
and a chosen
d
-dimensional reference subspace with projector
P
(and its orthogonal complement projector
Q=I-P
),
fix error tolerances in each subroutine to be
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
, and assume the target eigenvalues
\Lambda=\{\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{m}\}
are separated from the spectrum of the Hamiltonian projected to the complement space
QHQ
by at least a gap
g
.
For the target eigenstate
|\Psi_{k}\rangle
of
H
, let
\gamma=\sqrt{\langle\Psi_{k}|P|\Psi_{k}\rangle}
denote its overlap with the reference subspace.
Then the eigenvalue estimation algorithm returns an approximated eigenvalue
\hat{\lambda}_{k}
with total number of queries

\displaystyle C=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{d^{3}}{g^{2}\,\varepsilon}\,\log\frac{1}{g\,\varepsilon}\,\log\frac{1}{\gamma\,\varepsilon}\right),

(1)

to the block-encoding of
H
, and the returned eigenvalue satisfies

|\hat{\lambda}_{k}-\lambda_{k}|\leq\mathcal{O}\big(\gamma^{2}\varepsilon\big)

for the true eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}\in\Lambda
.

Theorem 2

(Informal – Eigenstate and subspace preparation)
.

Given an approximated eigenvalue
\hat{\lambda}_{k}\approx\lambda_{k}
returned by our algorithm with error tolerances
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
in each subroutine,
let
\gamma_{k}=\sqrt{\langle\Psi_{k}|P|\Psi_{k}\rangle}
denote the overlap between the eigenstate
|\Psi_{k}\rangle
and the reference subspace.

(i) Non-degenerate case.
If
\lambda_{k}
is isolated from the rest of
\text{spec}(H)
by a gap
\Delta>0
, then for any reduced-space eigenvector the lifted normalized state
|\widetilde{\Psi}_{k}\rangle\propto\widetilde{\Omega}(\lambda)\,|\hat{\phi}_{k}\rangle
satisfies

1-F\big(\widetilde{\Psi}_{k},\Psi_{k}\big)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\gamma^{2}\,\varepsilon^{2}}{\Delta^{2}}\right).

(ii) Quasi-degenerate manifold.
If the target eigenspace has dimension
m
and is separated from the rest of
\text{spec}(H)
by an external gap
\Delta>0
, then writing

\gamma_{\max}=\max_{k}\gamma_{k}
, we obtain the bound for the subspace fidelities:

1-F_{\min}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\gamma_{\max}^{2}\,\varepsilon^{2}}{\Delta^{2}}\right).

These results highlight three central features of our guarantees.
First, the eigenvalue error scales linearly with the target tolerance
\varepsilon
, whereas the state infidelity improves quadratically, scaling as
\varepsilon^{2}
; the overall query complexity is
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(1/\varepsilon)
up to logarithmic factors. This reflects that eigenvalue accuracy depends directly on the precision of the effective-Hamiltonian entries, while state preparation enjoys second-order robustness: small amplitude perturbations induce only quadratic changes in fidelity.
Second, the bounds exhibit a favorable quadratic dependence on the overlap
\gamma=\sqrt{\langle\Psi_{k}|P|\Psi_{k}\rangle}
. At first glance this may seem counterintuitive—one might expect poor overlap to degrade performance more sharply. The explanation is that the wave operator maps the reduced-space eigenvector
|\hat{\phi}_{k}\rangle
to the full eigenstate
|\widetilde{\Psi}_{k}\rangle
with an overall normalization
>1
; upon renormalization this suppresses the effective error within
P
by the same factor, yielding the observed
\gamma^{2}
scaling for both energy and fidelity.
Third, the complexity depends on a spectral-separation parameter
g>0
: the minimal external distance between the target energies and levels outside the model space
P
. Intuitively,
g
measures proximity to “intruder states”—nearby out-of-space levels that increase the condition number of the matrix we want to inverse in our subroutine and hence require higher-degree QSVT polynomials to accurately approximate the matrix inverse, producing the characteristic
1/g^{2}
dependence. This is the same obstruction long recognized in multireference/effective-Hamiltonian formalisms, and classical quantum-chemistry techniques can be used to diagnose such intruders. In fact, there are subtle relationships between
\gamma
and
g
. Loosely speaking, smaller overlap
\gamma
can imply smaller separation
g
, while large
g
often implies larger
\gamma
; however, the converses need not hold, and there is no nontrivial lower bound on
g
in terms of
\gamma
. We will elaborate on these relationships in the later section. From Theorem
1
, as long as the spectral separation
g
is
\mathcal{O}(1)
(i.e., the instance is intruder-free), our algorithm achieves a nearly optimal parameter dependence.

Taken together, these scalings indicate that the algorithm is most advantageous when
(i) moderate accuracy
\varepsilon
suffices while the intra-manifold gap is extremely small (
\Delta_{\mathrm{intra}}\ll\varepsilon\ll\Delta_{\mathrm{ext}}
);
(ii) the target states have moderately large overlap with the chosen reference space (so that the spectral separation
g
is not driven to
0
when
\gamma\ll 1
);
and (iii) there is a nonvanishing external separation from out-of-space levels (i.e.,
g
is bounded away from zero).
In such regimes, the method avoids resolving exponentially small intra-manifold splittings yet still certifies both eigenvalues and eigenstates, with complexity
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(1/\varepsilon)
in precision and polynomial in
d
, together with appropriate powers of
1/g
and
1/\gamma
—in contrast to previous approaches that hinge on resolving the intra-manifold gap in order to prepare a definite ground state.

1.2
High-level Algorithm Overview

Our algorithm is built on the
P/Q
projection formalism and consists of two main components:

1.

Eigenvalue estimation
— solving the nonlinear reduced eigenproblem to find approximated roots of the fixed-point (self-consistency) condition
\xi_{i}(\lambda)=\lambda
.

2.

Eigenstate preparation
— lifting the corresponding
P
-space eigenvectors to physical eigenstates of
H
via the wave-operator.

The workflow is summarized in Figure
1
. The first stage supplies the relevant spectral parameter for the second stage, which then enable state preparation with fidelity guarantees.

The core idea of our algorithm is to reformulate the eigenvalue problem in terms of an effective Hamiltonian acting on a much smaller reference subspace. Instead of solving the full
N
-dimensional problem (with
n
-qubit Hamiltonian
N=2^{n}
), we identify a
d
-dimensional subspace (
d\ll N
) spanned by physically motivated states expected to overlap strongly with the low-energy spectrum of
H
. Let
P
be the projector onto this model space and
Q=I-P
its orthogonal complement. In the resulting
P/Q
block decomposition,

(H-\lambda I)|\Psi\rangle=\begin{bmatrix}H_{11}-\lambda I&H_{12}\\
H_{21}&H_{22}-\lambda I\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Psi_{1}\\
\Psi_{2}\end{bmatrix}=0.

Eliminating the off-diagonal block by performing block Gaussian elimination (the Schur-complement step) yields the effective Hamiltonian

H_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)=H_{11}-H_{12}(H_{22}-\lambda I)^{-1}H_{21}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{d}).

Thus the eigenvalue problem for
H
is equivalent to the nonlinear reduced eigenproblem

\big(H_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)-\lambda I\big)\,|\phi(\lambda)\rangle=0,\qquad|\phi(\lambda)\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{d},

posed entirely in the reference subspace. Because
H_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)
depends on
\lambda
, this must be solved
self-consistently
. One can view this as diagonalizing
H_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)
for each
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
to obtain

H_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)\,|\phi_{i}(\lambda)\rangle=\xi_{i}(\lambda)\,|\phi_{i}(\lambda)\rangle,

where the
d
“eigenvalues” of effective Hamiltonian
\{\xi_{i}(\lambda)\}_{i=1}^{d}
forms continuous functions of
\lambda
. To avoid confusion with the true eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N}
of
H
, we will refer to
\xi_{i}(\lambda)
as
eigenbranches
in the later discussion. Self-consistency then amounts to finding intersections of the form
\xi_{i}(\lambda)=\lambda
.

Each solution
|\phi_{i}(\lambda_{k})\rangle
determines a genuine eigenpair
(\lambda_{k},|\Psi_{k}\rangle)
of
H
. The corresponding full-space eigenstate is obtained by “lifting” via the wave operator
\Omega(\lambda)
,

|\Psi_{k}\rangle\propto\Omega(\lambda_{k})\,|\phi_{i}(\lambda_{k})\rangle.

Classically, the main obstacle is the resolvent
(H_{22}-\lambda I)^{-1}
: its dimension is exponential in
n
, it must be recomputed as
\lambda
varies, and it is typically handled by perturbative expansion or series truncations in the classical methods. On a quantum computer we overcome this using two primitives:

1.

Block-encoding
, to provide coherent access to large matrices by embedding them in unitaries;

2.

Quantum singular value transformation (QSVT)
, to implement polynomial transformations of block-encoded matrices.

Choosing a polynomial
f(x)\approx 1/x
on the spectrum of
H_{22}-\lambda I
, QSVT yields a block-encoding of the dressed Hamiltonian (self-energy) term

\widetilde{H}_{D}(\lambda)=-H_{12}f\big(H_{22}-\lambda I\big)H_{21},

which approximates
-H_{12}(H_{22}-\lambda I)^{-1}H_{21}
whenever
\lambda
is away from those poles
\lambda=\chi\in\mathrm{spec}(H_{22})
. With
\widetilde{H}_{D}(\lambda)
available as a block-encoding, we proceed with the following workflow:

1.

Construct an adjustable block-encoding of
H_{22}-\lambda I
, where changing
\lambda
only requires retuning an ancilla rotation.

2.

Apply QSVT with polynomial
f(x)\approx 1/x
to approximate the resolvent
(H_{22}-\lambda I)^{-1}
up to precision
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{qsvt}}
.

3.

Estimate the
d\times d
matrix elements of
\widetilde{H}_{D}(\lambda)
via generalized Hadamard tests to precision
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{est}}
, obtaining
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)
.

4.

Diagonalize the resulting matrix
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)
classically to obtain approximate eigenbranches
\hat{\xi}_{i}(\lambda)
.

5.

Run a noise-aware bisection on
\hat{\mu}_{i}(\lambda)=\hat{\xi}_{i}(\lambda)-\lambda
until the residual condition
|\hat{\xi}_{i}(\lambda)-\lambda|\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{root}}
is satisfied, certifying
\hat{\lambda}_{k}\approx\lambda_{k}
.

The outputs of this stage are: (i) eigenvalue estimates
\hat{\lambda}_{k}\approx\lambda_{k}
, (ii) reduced-space eigenvectors
\{\hat{\phi}_{k}\}
with small residuals
\big(H_{\mathrm{eff}}(\hat{\lambda}_{k})-\hat{\lambda}_{k}I\big)\hat{\phi}_{k}\approx 0
. Identifying quasi-degenerate spectra naturally arises in (ii): when several eigenpairs
(\hat{\lambda}_{k},\hat{\phi}_{k})
yield residuals within error tolerance, they collectively span the quasi-degenerate subspace. With eigenvalues in hand, we perform eigenstate preparation. A
\lambda
-tunable, block-encoded wave-operator circuit applied to a reduced eigenvector
\ket{\hat{\phi}_{k}}
yields an approximation
\ket{\widetilde{\Psi}_{k}}
of the full-space eigenstate. The fidelity of this preparation can be lower bounded in terms of the errors
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{qsvt}},\varepsilon_{\mathrm{est}},\varepsilon_{\mathrm{root}}
in different subroutine, together with the external gap
\Delta
protecting the manifold.

1.3
Related Works

Effective-Hamiltonian formalism.

Classical effective-Hamiltonian formalisms can be roughly divided into two categories: (i)
energy-dependent
approaches, where the effective Hamiltonian is obtained by conjugating the Hamiltonian with an energy-dependent wave operator,

H_{\text{eff}}(\lambda)-\lambda I=\Omega^{\dagger}(\lambda)(H-\lambda I)\Omega(\lambda)
,
first proposed by Feshbach
[
Fes62
]
for nuclear resonances and later shown to be formally equivalent by Löwdin
[
Löw62
]
in quantum chemistry. The basic idea is to recast the problem as a nonlinear eigenvalue equation on a reference subspace; quasi-degenerate manifolds can then be detected by checking whether several eigenbranches satisfy the self-consistency condition within a chosen tolerance. (ii)
Energy-independent
similarity transformations, including the Schrieffer–Wolff (SW)
[
SW66
,
BDL11
]
and coupled-cluster (CC)
[
Číž66
,
BM07
,
HJO00
]
methods, define an effective Hamiltonian via an invertible wave operator
\Omega
such that
H_{\text{eff}}=\Omega^{-1}H\Omega
. CC sets
\Omega=e^{T}
with
T=T_{1}+T_{2}+\cdots
the excitation operator. In practice one truncates the series (e.g., CCSD, including up to double excitations), leading to a non-Hermitian
H_{\mathrm{eff}}
and
O(N^{6})
scaling (worsening at higher ranks), whereas SW takes
\Omega=e^{S}
with
S^{\dagger}=-S
an anti-Hermitian operator. Typically
S
cannot be obtained exactly; instead one uses a perturbative expansion
S=S_{1}+S_{2}+\cdots
chosen so that the off-diagonal block
QH_{\text{eff}}P
is eliminated order by order, yielding a Hermitian
H_{\mathrm{eff}}
but rapidly proliferating higher-order terms. Both lines face well-known limitations in strongly correlated regimes—either missing correlation from higher excitations or incurring prohibitive cost to evaluate high-order terms
[
Blo58
,
Fes62
,
SW66
,
BDL11
,
HJO00
]
. We therefore adopt the Feshbach viewpoint, which dovetails with our subspace-level, self-consistent formulation.

Fault tolerant quantum algorithms on eigenstate preparation.

On quantum hardware, several rigorous algorithms for ground-state or low-energy eigenvalue problems have been developed. Canonical examples include quantum phase estimation (QPE)
[
PW09
,
GTC19
]
, polynomial filters based on qubitization and QSVT
[
LT20
,
GSLW19
,
MRTC21
]
, and dissipative preparation schemes that engineer Lindbladian dynamics to mix into the ground state
[
DCL24
,
ZDH+25
]
. These approaches achieve near-optimal scaling when the ground state is unique and separated by a spectral gap. However, as the intra-manifold splitting shrinks, their resolution does not extend below the quasi-degeneracy scale: they project only into the low-energy span, without diagnosing degeneracies or providing a canonical basis within the manifold. Even block-Krylov and block-Lanczos methods built from block encodings
[
KKSA23
,
B+24
]
primarily target extremal eigenvalues or a handful of excitations, rather than certifying an entire quasi-degenerate manifold.

NISQ algorithm for eigenstate preparation.

On noisy intermediate-scale devices, a wide range of variational approaches has been explored. These include variational quantum deflation (VQD)
[
HWB19
]
, subspace-search VQE (SSVQE)
[
NMF19
]
, equation-of-motion and quantum subspace expansion (EOM-/QSE-based) methods
[
MKSCdJ17
,
MST+20
]
, Krylov/Lanczos and filter-diagonalization variants
[
SHE20
,
MRWC19
,
HCL+19
]
, and folded-spectrum formulations
[
PHMM19
,
MMN+20
,
RCL+24
]
. These methods often recover multiple low-lying states in practice, but rely on nonconvex optimization, heuristic penalty terms, or ansatz expressibility. As a result, they lack uniform performance guarantees, do not certify degeneracy, and do not construct a canonical basis of the quasi-degenerate manifold.

Preliminaries

This section provides the theoretical foundation for our projection-based approach to the eigenvalue problem. Section
2.1
fixes notation, reviews the
P/Q
projection framework, and presents the effective-Hamiltonian (spectral Schur complement) formalism together with its matrix-level equivalence to the full problem. Section
2.2
then explains how to implement the required matrix operations on a quantum computer using block-encodings, including explicit circuit constructions for the effective Hamiltonian and the associated wave operator.

Throughout this paper, we denote the full
n
-qubit Hilbert space as
\mathcal{H}
and we use the notation
N=2^{n}=\dim\mathcal{H}
for the Hilbert space dimension, and we fix a
d
-dimensional model subspace
\mathcal{H}_{d}\subset\mathcal{H}
(“
P
-space”) together with its orthogonal complement
\mathcal{H}_{d}^{\perp}
(“
Q
-space”). For Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}
, let
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2})
denote the bounded linear maps
\mathcal{H}_{1}\to\mathcal{H}_{2}
, and write
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})\coloneqq\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{H})
. The symbol
\|\cdot\|
denotes the Euclidean norm for vectors in
\mathcal{H}
and the operator (spectral) norm for elements of
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
. We write
\mathrm{spec}(\cdot)
for the spectrum. For the notation convention, given
A\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
, the symbol
\widetilde{A}
denotes a circuit-level implementation of
A
on a quantum device, while
\hat{A}
denotes a measured/estimated quantity (operator or scalar) derived from the estimation subroutine.

2.1
Spectral Schur complement and effective Hamiltonian

Consider a Hamiltonian that can be decomposed into two parts
H=H_{0}+V
acting on
\mathcal{H}
, where
V
need not be perturbative. Assume
H_{0}
is analytically solvable:
H_{0}|\varphi_{i}\rangle=\lambda_{i}|\varphi_{i}\rangle
with an orthonormal eigenbasis
\{|\varphi_{i}\rangle\}_{i=1}^{d}
. Define the orthogonal projectors

\displaystyle P=\sum_{i=1}^{d}|\varphi_{i}\rangle\langle\varphi_{i}|\,,\qquad Q=I-P=\sum_{i=d+1}^{N}|\varphi_{i}\rangle\langle\varphi_{i}|,

(2)

so
[P,H_{0}]=[Q,H_{0}]=0
. Every
H\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
and
|\Psi\rangle\in\mathcal{H}
then admit the block decompositions

\displaystyle H=PHP+PHQ+QHP+QHQ,\qquad|\Psi\rangle=P|\Psi\rangle+Q|\Psi\rangle,

(3)

or, with respect to
\mathcal{H}_{d}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{d}^{\perp}
,

\displaystyle H=\begin{bmatrix}H_{11}&H_{12}\\[2.0pt]
H_{21}&H_{22}\end{bmatrix},\qquad|\Psi\rangle=\begin{bmatrix}\Psi_{1}\\
\Psi_{2}\end{bmatrix},

(4)

where
H_{11}=PHP|_{\mathcal{H}_{d}}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{d})
,
H_{22}=QHQ|_{\mathcal{H}_{d}^{\perp}}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{d}^{\perp})
, and
H_{12}=PHQ|_{\mathcal{H}_{d}^{\perp}}^{\,\mathcal{H}_{d}}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{d}^{\perp},\mathcal{H}_{d})
(for Hermitian
H
,
H_{21}=H_{12}^{\dagger}
). Since
PH_{0}Q=0
, the off-diagonal couplings arise entirely from
V
:

\displaystyle H_{12}=PVQ\big|_{\mathcal{H}_{d}^{\perp}}^{\,\mathcal{H}_{d}},\qquad H_{21}=QVP\big|_{\mathcal{H}_{d}}^{\,\mathcal{H}_{d}^{\perp}}.

(5)

In this block (projector) representation, the eigenvalue equation
(H-\lambda I)|\Psi\rangle=0
reads

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}H_{11}-\lambda I_{\mathcal{H}_{d}}&H_{12}\\
H_{21}&H_{22}-\lambda I_{\mathcal{H}_{d}^{\perp}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Psi_{1}\\
\Psi_{2}\end{bmatrix}=0.

(6)

When
\lambda\notin\mathrm{spec}(H_{22})
, the lower block is invertible and

\displaystyle\Psi_{2}=-\,(H_{22}-\lambda I_{\mathcal{H}_{d}^{\perp}})^{-1}H_{21}\,\Psi_{1}.

(7)

Substituting into the upper block gives the
spectral Schur complement
on
\mathcal{H}_{d}
,

\displaystyle S(\lambda)\,\Psi_{1}=\Bigl(H_{11}-\lambda I_{\mathcal{H}_{d}}-H_{12}(H_{22}-\lambda I_{\mathcal{H}_{d}^{\perp}})^{-1}H_{21}\Bigr)\,\Psi_{1}=0.

(8)

The above equation can be viewed as the block-diagonalization via the (invertible, block-unitriangular)
wave operator

\displaystyle\Omega(\lambda)=\begin{bmatrix}I_{\mathcal{H}_{d}}&0\\
-\bigl(H_{22}-\lambda I_{\mathcal{H}_{d}^{\perp}}\bigr)^{-1}H_{21}&I_{\mathcal{H}_{d}^{\perp}}\end{bmatrix}

(9)

where conjugation by the wave operator leads to block Gaussian elimination of the off-diagonal blocks

\displaystyle\Omega^{\dagger}(\lambda)\,(H-\lambda I)\,\Omega(\lambda)=\begin{bmatrix}S(\lambda)&0\\
0&H_{22}-\lambda I_{\mathcal{H}_{d}^{\perp}}\end{bmatrix}.

(10)

Because
\Omega(\lambda)
is unitriangular,
\det\Omega(\lambda)=\det\Omega^{\dagger}(\lambda)=1
, and hence

\displaystyle\det\begin{bmatrix}S(\lambda)&0\\
0&H_{22}-\lambda I_{\mathcal{H}_{d}^{\perp}}\end{bmatrix}=\det(H-\lambda I)\quad\Rightarrow\quad\det S(\lambda)=\frac{\det(H-\lambda I)}{\det(H_{22}-\lambda I_{\mathcal{H}_{d}^{\perp}})}=\frac{p_{H}(\lambda)}{p_{H_{22}}(\lambda)}.

(11)

Thus
\det S(\lambda)
is a rational function whose zeros coincide with those of
p_{H}(\lambda)
except at points where
p_{H}(\lambda)
and
p_{H_{22}}(\lambda)
vanish simultaneously. In particular, for
\lambda\notin\mathrm{spec}(H_{22})
,
S(\lambda)
is singular if and only if
\lambda\in\mathrm{spec}(H)
.

Having established that the roots of
\det S(\lambda)
and
\det(H-\lambda I)
coincide we can further show that the eigenstate we obtained from the reduced problem
S(\lambda)|\phi\rangle=0
can be mapped to the eigenstate of the full problem
(H-\lambda I)|\Psi\rangle=0
. Equivalence with the full problem is transparent in block form:

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}S(\lambda)&0\\
0&H_{22}-\lambda I\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\phi(\lambda)\\
0\end{bmatrix}=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad(H-\lambda I)\Omega(\lambda)|\phi\rangle=0,

(12)

where the right hand side is obtained by multiplying
(\Omega^{\dagger})^{-1}
on the left hand side. That is, given any nonzero
\phi(\lambda)\in\mathcal{H}_{d}
with
S(\lambda)\phi(\lambda)=0
, the lifted vector

\displaystyle|\Psi\rangle=\Omega(\lambda)\,|\phi(\lambda)\rangle=\begin{bmatrix}\phi(\lambda)\\[2.0pt]
-\,(H_{22}-\lambda I_{\mathcal{H}_{d}^{\perp}})^{-1}H_{21}\,\phi(\lambda)\end{bmatrix}

(13)

satisfies
(H-\lambda I)|\Psi\rangle=0
. Hence we obtain a bijection

\displaystyle\mathrm{null}\bigl(S(\lambda)\bigr)\xleftrightarrow[\ P\ ]{\ \Omega(\lambda)\ }\mathrm{null}(H-\lambda I),\qquad\lambda\notin\mathrm{spec}(H_{22}),

(14)

with inverses
P|\Psi\rangle=\phi(\lambda)
and
\Omega(\lambda)|\phi(\lambda)\rangle=|\Psi\rangle
. We summarize the results in the following theorem:

Theorem 3

(Spectral Schur complement)
.

Let
P,Q
be orthogonal projectors with
P+Q=I
and
\mathrm{Tr}\,P=d
. Define

\displaystyle S(\lambda)=H_{11}-\lambda I_{\mathcal{H}_{d}}-H_{12}\bigl(H_{22}-\lambda I_{\mathcal{H}_{d}^{\perp}}\bigr)^{-1}H_{21}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{d})

(15)

If
\lambda\notin\mathrm{spec}(H_{22})
, then:

1.

\lambda\in\mathrm{spec}(H)
if and only if
S(\lambda)
is singular.

2.

For any eigenpair
H|\Psi\rangle=\lambda|\Psi\rangle
one has
|\phi(\lambda)\rangle\coloneqq P|\Psi\rangle\in\mathrm{null}(S(\lambda))
and
|\Psi\rangle=\Omega(\lambda)|\phi(\lambda)\rangle
; conversely, any nonzero
|\phi(\lambda)\rangle\in\mathrm{null}(S(\lambda))
lifts to
|\Psi\rangle=\Omega(\lambda)|\phi(\lambda)\rangle
with
(H-\lambda I)|\Psi\rangle=0
.

3.

\dim\mathrm{null}(H-\lambda I)=\dim\mathrm{null}\bigl(S(\lambda)\bigr)
.

Remark 4
.

If
\lambda\in\mathrm{spec}(H_{22})
while
\lambda\notin\mathrm{spec}(H)
, the operator
H_{22}-\lambda I_{\mathcal{H}_{d}^{\perp}}
is not invertible and then the operator pair
(S,\Omega)
are ill-defined. A standard remedy replaces the inverse by the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse
(H_{22}-\lambda I_{\mathcal{H}_{d}^{\perp}})^{+}
, which projects out singular directions. In later sections, our polynomial implementations on a quantum computer approximate this pseudoinverse consistently.

A completely equivalent viewpoint—more familiar to physicists and quantum chemists—uses the energy-dependent effective Hamiltonian on
\mathcal{H}_{d}
,

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)=H_{11}-H_{12}\bigl(H_{22}-\lambda I_{\mathcal{H}_{d}^{\perp}}\bigr)^{-1}H_{21}=S(\lambda)+\lambda I_{\mathcal{H}_{d}}.

(16)

Here
H_{11}=PHP=PH_{0}P+PVP
; in the basis
\{|\varphi_{i}\rangle\}_{i=1}^{d}
spanning
\mathcal{H}_{d}
, the block
PH_{0}P
is diagonal while
PVP
is not in general. The second term
-\,H_{12}(H_{22}-\lambda I_{\mathcal{H}_{d}^{\perp}})^{-1}H_{21}
is the
dressed
(self-energy) contribution from virtual excursions into
Q
-space. Solving
S(\lambda)\,\phi(\lambda)=0
is equivalent to the self-consistent eigenproblem

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)\,\phi(\lambda)=\lambda\,\phi(\lambda),

(17)

which resembles a standard eigenvalue problem, except that the effective Hamiltonian depends on the spectral parameter
\lambda
. Thus the effective-Hamiltonian formalism reduces the original
N
-dimensional eigenproblem
H|\Psi\rangle=\lambda|\Psi\rangle
to a
d
-dimensional
nonlinear
eigenproblem on
\mathcal{H}_{d}
.

For real
\lambda\notin\mathrm{spec}(H_{22})
, both
S(\lambda)
and
H_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)
are Hermitian and admit orthonormal eigenbases

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}S(\lambda)\,\phi_{i}(\lambda)=\mu_{i}(\lambda)\,\phi_{i}(\lambda),\\[5.69046pt]
H_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)\,\phi_{i}(\lambda)=\xi_{i}(\lambda)\,\phi_{i}(\lambda),\end{array}\right.\qquad 1\leq i\leq d,

ordered so that
\mu_{1}(\lambda)\leq\cdots\leq\mu_{d}(\lambda)
and
\xi_{1}(\lambda)\leq\cdots\leq\xi_{d}(\lambda)
, with the identity

\displaystyle\mu_{i}(\lambda)=\xi_{i}(\lambda)-\lambda.

(20)

Thus the nonlinear problem can be approached either by root finding in one dimension (seek
\mu_{i}(\lambda)=0
) or by a fixed-point (self-consistency) condition (
\xi_{i}(\lambda)=\lambda
). Since the two formulations are equivalent, we will take the effective-Hamiltonian viewpoint in later sections. Here, we refer to the functions
\mu_{i}(\lambda)
and
\xi_{i}(\lambda)
as “eigenbranches” to avoid confusion with the true eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}
that satisfies
\xi_{i}(\lambda_{k})=\lambda_{k}
.

We now discuss the properties of the eigenbranches; in particular, their derivatives. Assume that
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathrm{spec}(H_{22})
, so
H_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)
is Hermitian and differentiable on any interval that avoids the poles of
H_{22}
. The first claim is monotonicity. Differentiating the resolvent yields
\tfrac{d}{d\lambda}(H_{22}-\lambda I)^{-1}=(H_{22}-\lambda I)^{-2}
and hence

\displaystyle\frac{dH_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)}{d\lambda}=-\,H_{12}\,(H_{22}-\lambda I)^{-2}\,H_{21}.

(21)

Because
(H_{22}-\lambda I)^{-2}\succ 0
and
H_{12}(\cdot)H_{21}\succeq 0
on
\mathcal{H}_{d}
, one has

\displaystyle\langle\phi|\,\tfrac{dH_{\mathrm{eff}}}{d\lambda}\,|\phi\rangle=-\,\bigl\|(H_{22}-\lambda I)^{-1}H_{21}\phi\bigr\|^{2}\leq 0\qquad\text{for all }\phi\in\mathcal{H}_{d},

(22)

so
\tfrac{dH_{\mathrm{eff}}}{d\lambda}\preceq 0
. By the Hellmann–Feynman theorem, any normalized eigenpair
(\xi_{i}(\lambda),\phi_{i}(\lambda))
satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{d\xi_{i}(\lambda)}{d\lambda}=\Big\langle\phi_{i}(\lambda)\Big|\frac{dH_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)}{d\lambda}\Big|\phi_{i}(\lambda)\Big\rangle=-\,\bigl\|(H_{22}-\lambda I)^{-1}H_{21}\,\phi_{i}(\lambda)\bigr\|^{2}\leq 0,

(23)

with equality only if
H_{21}\phi_{i}(\lambda)=0
. Thus each eigenbranch is monotonically decreasing on every pole-free interval.

The slope admits a natural interpretation in terms of the
Q
-space weight of the reconstructed full-space state. Using the wave-operator in Eq. (
13
), the unnormalized eigenvector in
\mathcal{H}
associated with
\phi_{i}(\lambda)
is

\displaystyle|\Psi\rangle=\begin{bmatrix}|\phi_{i}(\lambda)\rangle\\[2.0pt]
-\,(H_{22}-\lambda I)^{-1}H_{21}\,|\phi_{i}(\lambda)\rangle\end{bmatrix}.

(24)

With
\|\phi_{i}(\lambda)\|=1
, its squared norm is

\displaystyle\|\,|\Psi\rangle\|^{2}=1+\bigl\|(H_{22}-\lambda I)^{-1}H_{21}\,\phi_{i}(\lambda)\bigr\|^{2}=1-\frac{d\xi_{i}(\lambda)}{d\lambda}.

(25)

Hence, for the normalized state
|\Psi^{\prime}\rangle=|\Psi\rangle/\|\,|\Psi\rangle\|
, the overlap between the eigenstate in
P
space reads

\displaystyle\gamma=\bigl|\langle\phi_{i}(\lambda)\,|\,\Psi^{\prime}\rangle\bigr|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\,1-\tfrac{d\xi_{i}(\lambda)}{d\lambda}\,}}<1,

(26)

In fact, this quantity can be viewed as the overlap between
|\Psi\rangle
and the subspace
\mathcal{H}_{d}
. Since
\{|\phi_{i}(\lambda)\rangle\}_{i=1}^{d}
forms the basis of
\mathcal{H}_{d}
, therefore we can write
P=\sum_{j=1}^{d}|\varphi_{j}\rangle\langle\varphi_{j}|=\sum_{j=1}^{d}|\phi_{j}(\lambda)\rangle\langle\phi_{j}(\lambda)|
, then

\displaystyle\sqrt{\langle\Psi^{\prime}|P|\Psi^{\prime}\rangle}=\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{d}\bigl|\langle\phi_{j}(\lambda)|\Psi^{\prime}\rangle\bigr|^{2}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\,1-\tfrac{d\xi_{i}(\lambda)}{d\lambda}\,}}=\gamma.

(27)

Therefore the magnitude of the negative slope,
-\tfrac{d\xi_{i}}{d\lambda}
, equals the squared
Q
-space norm of the reconstructed eigenstate and directly controls the normalization overhead. Large
Q
-weight (strong
P
–
Q
mixing) produces a steeper branch and smaller
P
-overlap; conversely, selecting a model space with large overlap to the target eigenstate flattens the branch and eases state preparation.

Two immediate consequences are useful downstream. First, between successive poles of
H_{22}
, monotonicity implies each branch can cross the line
\xi(\lambda)=\lambda
at most once; this justifies one-dimensional root-finding on fixed intervals. Second, in the decoupled case
H_{21}\phi_{i}(\lambda)=0
, the branch is locally flat and the reconstructed state already lies entirely in
P
. Finally, for a degenerate subspace one obtains the projector analogue

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\lambda}\,\mathrm{Tr}\big[P_{\mathrm{cl}}(\lambda)\,H_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)\big]=-\,\bigl\|(H_{22}-\lambda I)^{-1}H_{21}P_{\mathrm{cl}}(\lambda)\bigr\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\leq 0,

(28)

which shows that all eigenvalues within the subspace are nonincreasing on pole-free intervals, with the Frobenius norm quantifying the aggregated
Q
-space weight.

3
Main result

We now present the overall framework. The workflow has two main stages: (i) in Section
3.1
we discuss
eigenvalue estimation
, which reduces to a one-dimensional fixed-point/root-finding problem for the energy-dependent effective Hamiltonian
H_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)
. A key idea is that the dressed Hamiltonian (self-energy) part of the effective Hamiltonian can be constructed on the quantum computer via block-encodings and subsequently read out through the matrix-element estimation subroutine; and (ii) in Section
3.2
we address
eigenstate preparation
, which applies a block-encoded wave operator to the
P
-space eigenvector to obtain high-fidelity physical states. We derive overall lower bounds on the fidelity for both the non-degenerate and quasi-degenerate cases. Moreover, for a quasi-degenerate manifold, a Löwdin orthonormalization can be performed to prepare an orthonormal basis within that manifold.

3.1
Algorithm for eigenvalue estimation

The objective is to find
(\lambda,|\phi_{i}(\lambda)\rangle)
such that

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)\,|\phi_{i}(\lambda)\rangle=\xi_{i}(\lambda)\,|\phi_{i}(\lambda)\rangle,\qquad\text{with}\quad\xi_{i}(\lambda)=\lambda.

(55)

Equivalently, we seek a root of
\mu_{i}(\lambda)\coloneqq\xi_{i}(\lambda)-\lambda
.
The high-level procedure is as follows:

1.

Initialize.
Choose a starting energy (or a bracket) for
\lambda
; for example, a lower bound for the ground state or an interval that avoids poles of
(H_{22}-\lambda I)^{-1}
.

2.

Build the oracle.
Using QSVT, construct a tunable block-encoding of the dressed term

\widetilde{H}_{D}(\lambda)=-H_{12}\,f(H_{22}-\lambda I)\,H_{21}

at the desired precision, and form the effective Hamiltonian

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)=H_{11}+\hat{H}_{D}(\lambda)
by estimating matrix elements of
\widetilde{H}_{D}(\lambda)
on the quantum computer (via generalized Hadamard tests and quantum amplitude estimation).

3.

Diagonalize.
Classically diagonalize the
d\times d
matrix
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)
to obtain noisy eigenpairs
\bigl(\hat{\xi}_{j}(\lambda),|\hat{\phi}_{j}(\lambda)\rangle\bigr)_{j=1}^{d}
; select/track the branch
i
(or cluster) by maximum overlap with the previous iterate.

4.

Update
\lambda
.
Perform a fixed-point/root-finding step on
\hat{\mu}_{i}(\lambda)\coloneqq\hat{\xi}_{i}(\lambda)-\lambda
and update the spectral parameter
\lambda
for the next iteration. Repeat Steps 2–4.

5.

Stop.
Terminate when the stopping criterion is satisfied, e.g.,
|\hat{\xi}_{i}(\lambda)-\lambda|\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{root}}
or the bracket length is
\leq\tau_{\lambda}
. Output the energy estimate
\hat{\lambda}
and the associated
P
-space eigenvector
|\hat{\phi}_{i}(\hat{\lambda})\rangle
.

Having established how to evaluate
H_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)
, the next step is to solve the fixed-point equation
\xi(\lambda)=\lambda
for some eigenbranch
\xi(\lambda)
. Because measurements are probabilistic, we only access a noisy effective Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)
whose diagonalization yields approximate eigenpairs
(\hat{\xi}_{i}(\lambda),|\hat{\phi}_{i}\rangle)
. We therefore treat
\hat{\xi}(\lambda)
as a noisy black-box oracle and count queries to it. The surrogate root-finding problem is then
\hat{\xi}(\lambda)=\lambda
, solved only up to a prescribed tolerance, and we certify how close the returned
\lambda
is to a true solution.

We terminate the surrogate search once

\displaystyle|\hat{\xi}(\lambda)-\lambda|\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{root}}.

(64)

From the construction of
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)
(QSVT polynomial plus amplitude-estimation noise), the oracle error admits the uniform bound

\displaystyle\big|\hat{\xi}(\lambda)-\xi(\lambda)\big|\leq\big\|\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)-H_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)\big\|\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{est}}+\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{qsvt}}\eqqcolon\varepsilon_{\mathrm{approx}}.

(65)

Let
\lambda_{k}
be a true solution of
\xi(\lambda)=\lambda
in an interval free of poles of
H_{22}
, where
\xi
is differentiable and strictly decreasing. To relate a returned
\lambda
to
\lambda_{k}
, decompose

\displaystyle\lambda-\lambda_{k}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{(\lambda-\hat{\xi}(\lambda))}_{\text{surrogate residual}}+\underbrace{(\hat{\xi}(\lambda)-\xi(\lambda))}_{\text{oracle error}}+\underbrace{(\xi(\lambda)-\xi(\lambda_{k}))}_{\text{branch variation}},

(66)

using
\xi(\lambda_{k})=\lambda_{k}
.
By the mean value theorem there exists
c\in[\lambda,\lambda_{k}]
such that

\displaystyle\xi(\lambda)-\xi(\lambda_{k})=\xi^{\prime}(c)\,(\lambda-\lambda_{k}).

(67)

Substituting into Eq. (
66
) yields the exact relation

\displaystyle\bigl(1-\xi^{\prime}(c)\bigr)(\lambda-\lambda_{k})=(\lambda-\hat{\xi}(\lambda))+(\hat{\xi}(\lambda)-\xi(\lambda)),\qquad c\in[\lambda,\lambda_{k}].

(68)

Taking absolute values and inserting the stopping criterion Eq. (
64
) and oracle bound gives

\displaystyle|\lambda-\lambda_{k}|\leq\frac{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{root}}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{approx}}}{\,1-\xi^{\prime}(c)\,},\qquad c\in[\lambda,\lambda_{k}].

(69)

The denominator has direct physical meaning. From Section
2.1
,

\displaystyle\xi^{\prime}(\lambda)=-\bigl\|(H_{22}-\lambda I)^{-1}H_{21}\phi(\lambda)\bigr\|^{2}\leq 0,

(70)

and the reconstructed (unnormalized) eigenstate satisfies

\|\,|\Psi(\lambda)\rangle\|^{2}=1-\xi^{\prime}(\lambda)
. Thus for the normalized state
|\Psi^{\prime}(\lambda)\rangle
,

\displaystyle\big|\langle\phi(\lambda)|\Psi^{\prime}(\lambda)\rangle\big|^{2}=\frac{1}{1-\xi^{\prime}(\lambda)}.

(71)

Evaluating at
c
in Eq. (
69
) gives the overlap dependent upper bound

\displaystyle|\lambda-\lambda_{k}|\leq\big|\langle\phi(c)|\Psi(c)\rangle\big|^{2}\,\big(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{root}}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{approx}}\big),\qquad c\in[\lambda,\lambda_{k}].

(72)

When
\lambda
is close to
\lambda_{k}
, continuity implies

\langle\phi(c)|\Psi(c)\rangle\approx\langle\phi(\lambda_{k})|\Psi(\lambda_{k})\rangle
, so for simplicity we assume
|\langle\phi(c)|\Psi(c)\rangle|=\gamma
, where
\gamma
represents the
P
-space overlap of the target eigenstate. This connects Eq. (
72
) to fidelity bounds discussed later.
Dropping the overlap factor yields a universal bound since
\gamma\leq 1
:

\displaystyle|\lambda-\lambda_{k}|\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{root}}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{approx}}.

(73)

|\hat{\xi}(\lambda)-\xi(\lambda)|\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{approx}}\quad\text{with}\quad\varepsilon_{\mathrm{approx}}=\varepsilon_{\mathrm{est}}+\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{qsvt}},

then the returned
\lambda
satisfies

\displaystyle|\lambda-\lambda_{k}|

\displaystyle\leq\gamma^{2}\,\big(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{root}}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{approx}}\big),

(74)

where
\gamma
is the overlap between the true eigenstate
|\Psi_{k}\rangle
and
P
-space.

3.1.3
Noise-aware root finding by bisection

We now present a certified bisection routine for solving the noisy fixed-point equation

\tilde{\xi}_{i}(\lambda)\approx\lambda
for a chosen eigenbranch using only the sign
of a surrogate residual. Define

\hat{\mu}(\lambda)\coloneqq\hat{\xi}_{i}(\lambda)-\lambda,

(75)

where
\hat{\xi}_{i}(\lambda)
is the noisy eigenbranch obtained by diagonalizing the
estimated effective Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)
.
Throughout this subsection we assume a uniform per-evaluation guarantee

\big\lvert\hat{\xi}_{i}(\lambda)-\xi_{i}(\lambda)\big\rvert\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{approx}}\qquad\text{with probability at least }1-\theta_{0},

(76)

on any interval that is free of poles of
H_{22}
. We track the intended branch by maximal
overlap of eigenvectors across successive values of
\lambda
(or, for a cluster, by subspace
overlap), so the label
i
remains consistent along the path.

The idea of performing bisection for root-finding comes from the eigenvalue bracketing principle
[
Löw65
]
.
On any pole-free interval
\mathcal{I}=[a,b]
, the exact eigenbranch
\xi_{i}(\lambda)
is differentiable
and strictly decreasing, i.e.,
\xi_{i}^{\prime}(\lambda)<0
on
\mathcal{I}
.
Then the bracketing theorem says that for any
\lambda\in\mathcal{I}
with
\xi_{i}(\lambda)\in\mathcal{I}
,
there exists at least one eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}
that satisfies
\xi_{i}(\lambda_{k})=\lambda_{k}
between

\lambda
and
\xi_{i}(\lambda)
; that is, these two numbers “bracket” an eigenvalue.
Since the eigenbranch is monotonically decreasing on
\mathcal{I}
, by referring to the sign of

\mu_{i}(\lambda)=\xi_{i}(\lambda)-\lambda
, we can determine whether we are on the left side

(\mu_{i}(\lambda)>0)
or the right side
(\mu_{i}(\lambda)<0)
of the true eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}
.

Under Eq. (
76
), the sign of
\hat{\mu}(\lambda)
is
reliable whenever
|\mu(\lambda)|>\varepsilon_{\mathrm{approx}}
. In that regime,

\operatorname{sign}\hat{\mu}(\lambda)=\operatorname{sign}\mu(\lambda)
with probability at least
1-\theta_{0}
.
This observation lets us run a standard bisection that only consults
\operatorname{sign}\hat{\mu}
at midpoints, and
we stop when
|\hat{\mu}(\lambda)|\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{root}}
. We summarize the basic procedure to the noisy bisection subroutine:

1.

Initialization.

We start from a certified lower bound
\lambda_{L}\leq\lambda_{k}
for the target root
\lambda_{k}

on a pole-free interval and evaluate
\hat{\xi}(\lambda_{L})
.
Since
\xi_{i}(\lambda)
is strictly decreasing between poles, the upper endpoint

\displaystyle\lambda_{U}\coloneqq\hat{\xi}(\lambda_{L}),\qquad|\lambda_{U}-\lambda_{L}|>\varepsilon_{\mathrm{root}},

(77)

forms, with probability at least
1-\theta_{0}
, a valid bracket
[\lambda_{L},\lambda_{U}]

that contains
\lambda_{k}
.
The initial bracket length is
L_{0}=\lambda_{U}-\lambda_{L}
. If the initial
|\lambda_{U}-\lambda_{L}|\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{root}}
, then directly return
\lambda_{L}
.

2.

Bisection with certified sign tests.

Given a current bracket
[\lambda_{L},\lambda_{U}]
, we compute

\lambda=\tfrac{1}{2}(\lambda_{L}+\lambda_{U})

and evaluate
\hat{\mu}(\lambda)
.
The noise-aware sign rule is

\displaystyle\text{if }\hat{\mu}(\lambda)>\varepsilon_{\mathrm{approx}}\ \Rightarrow\ \lambda_{L}\leftarrow\lambda,\qquad\text{if }\hat{\mu}(\lambda)<-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{approx}}\ \Rightarrow\ \lambda_{U}\leftarrow\lambda,

(78)

and otherwise we treat the midpoint as ambiguous at the current precision.
Each non-ambiguous update halves the bracket length:
L_{t+1}\leq L_{t}/2
.

3.

Stopping criterion.

The search terminates once

\displaystyle\big|\hat{\xi}_{i}(\lambda)-\lambda\big|<\varepsilon_{\mathrm{root}},

(79)

with
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{root}}=\varepsilon_{\mathrm{approx}}
.
This choice is natural: requiring
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{root}}\ll\varepsilon_{\mathrm{approx}}
cannot
improve the certified outcome, while
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{root}}\gg\varepsilon_{\mathrm{approx}}

stops prematurely.
As a fallback, one may also stop by a bracket certificate when

\lambda_{U}-\lambda_{L}\leq\tau_{\lambda}
, returning the midpoint.

At termination, in the worst case the surrogate residual satisfies

\lvert\xi_{i}(\lambda)-\lambda\rvert\leq 2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{approx}}
.
Therefore, we define the worst-case convergence interval length
\tau_{\lambda}
by

\displaystyle\frac{\tau_{\lambda}}{2}\,\bigl(1-\xi_{i}^{\prime}(\lambda_{k})\bigr)=2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{approx}}\implies\tau_{\lambda}=4\,\gamma^{2}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{approx}},

(80)

where we use the identity
\gamma^{2}=\frac{1}{1-\xi_{i}^{\prime}(\lambda_{k})}
.
The quantity
\tau_{\lambda}
can be interpreted as the certified horizontal tolerance
corresponding to the slope of the eigenbranch at
\lambda_{k}
.

Let
T=\lceil\log_{2}(L_{0}/\tau_{\lambda})\rceil
be the number of non-ambiguous steps
needed to shrink the bracket to length
\tau_{\lambda}

(the residual stop may occur earlier).
Each step requires evaluating
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)
with operator-norm accuracy

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{approx}}
, which, by Lemma
10
,
can be done with query cost
C_{\xi}(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{approx}})
.
Thus the total query complexity is

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\bigl(T\,C_{\xi}(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{approx}})\bigr).

(81)

Under the uniform guarantee (
76
),
the probability that all certified decisions fail is at most
\mathcal{O}(1-(1-\theta_{0})^{T})=\mathcal{O}(T\theta_{0})
. Therefore, in order to have success probability for at least
1-\theta
, the parameter for
\theta_{0}
is chosen to be
\theta/T
. We summarize the query complexity in the following Lemma:

Lemma 14

(Noise-aware bisection with residual stopping)
.

Fix a pole-free interval on which
\xi_{i}(\lambda)
is strictly decreasing,
and suppose the surrogate oracle satisfies

|\hat{\xi}_{i}(\lambda)-\xi_{i}(\lambda)|\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{approx}}

with probability at least
\theta_{0}
for every query.
Let
\lambda_{k}
be the target root with overlap

\gamma^{2}=\big|\langle\phi(\lambda_{k})\,|\,\Psi(\lambda_{k})\rangle\big|^{2}
.
Initialize with a certified lower bound
\lambda_{L}\leq\lambda_{k}
and upper endpoint

\lambda_{U}=\hat{\xi}(\lambda_{L})+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{approx}}
,
with initial bracket length
L_{0}=\lambda_{U}-\lambda_{L}
.
Run bisection using the certified sign rule Eq. (
78
) and stop when

|\hat{\xi}_{i}(\lambda)-\lambda|<\varepsilon_{\mathrm{root}}
,
with
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{root}}=\varepsilon_{\mathrm{approx}}
.

Then the number of oracle evaluations is

T=\mathcal{O}\Big(\log\tfrac{L_{0}}{\gamma^{2}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{approx}}}\Big),

the total query complexity is

\mathcal{O}\Big(T\cdot C_{\xi}(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{approx}})\Big),

and the success probability is at least 1 -
\mathcal{O}(T\theta_{0})
.

Now, we can summarize the total query complexity for the whole eigenvalue estimation algorithm:

Theorem 15

(Eigenvalue estimation)
.

Assume an
(\alpha,a,0)
block-encoding of the Hamiltonian
H
and a
(\widetilde{\alpha},\widetilde{a},0)

block-encoding of the coupling
V
. Let
P
be a
d
-dimensional reference projector with efficiently
preparable orthonormal basis
\{\,|\varphi_{i}\rangle\,\}_{i=1}^{d}
, and let
Q=I-P
. Let
\Lambda=\{\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{M}\}

be the target eigenvalues, and suppose each
\lambda\in\Lambda
is separated from
\mathrm{spec}(H_{22})

by at least
g>0
, i.e.
\mathrm{dist}(\lambda,\mathrm{spec}(H_{22}))\geq g
. For a target eigenpair

(\lambda_{k},|\Psi_{k}\rangle)
define the model-space overlap

\gamma=\sqrt{\langle\Psi_{k}|P|\Psi_{k}\rangle}
.

Run the algorithm with per-call additive eigenbranch error

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{approx}}=\Theta(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{root}}+\tilde{\alpha}^{2}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{qsvt}})
,
QSVT polynomial accuracy
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{qsvt}}
, and total failure probability
\theta\in(0,1)
.
Then, with probability at least
1-\theta
, the algorithm outputs
\hat{\lambda}_{k}
such that

\bigl|\hat{\lambda}_{k}-\lambda_{k}\bigr|\leq\gamma^{2}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{approx}},

and the total number of queries to the block-encoding of
H
is

C=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{\alpha\,\widetilde{\alpha}^{\,2}\,d^{3}}{g^{2}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{est}}}\right),

where
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}
hides polylogarithmic factors

\log\bigl(\tfrac{1}{g\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{qsvt}}}\bigr)
,

\log\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\gamma\varepsilon_{\mathrm{approx}}}\bigr)
,
and
\log\bigl(\tfrac{d}{\theta}\log(\tfrac{1}{\gamma\varepsilon_{\mathrm{approx}}})\bigr)
.

3.2
Eigenstate preparation

Having obtained approximate eigenvalues
\lambda\approx\lambda_{k}
and a block-encoding of the approximate wave operator
\widetilde{\Omega}(\lambda)
(Lemma
9
), we now turn to preparing the corresponding eigenstates of
H
. The basic idea is to lift the
P
-space eigenvectors
|\phi(\lambda)\rangle
of the effective Hamiltonian into the full Hilbert space by applying
\widetilde{\Omega}(\lambda)
. In the ideal case, the exact wave operator
\Omega(\lambda)
maps
|\phi(\lambda_{k})\rangle
to a true eigenstate
|\Psi_{k}\rangle
; in practice, approximation errors from the QSVT polynomial, the root-finding tolerance, and inexact
P
-space eigenstates must be accounted for.

To analyze this process uniformly for both non-degenerate and quasi-degenerate spectra, in Section
3.2.1
we introduce the rotated eigenproblem

H_{\mathrm{rot}}=\widetilde{\Omega}^{\dagger}(\lambda)\,(H-\lambda I)\,\widetilde{\Omega}(\lambda),

defined using the approximate wave operator
\widetilde{\Omega}(\lambda)
. From the previous discussion, if the wave operator is exact, then
H_{\mathrm{rot}}
is block diagonal. However, because
\widetilde{\Omega}(\lambda)
deviates slightly from the true wave operator, we can expect that residual off-diagonal terms appear. The size of these residual terms determines how close the lifted state (or subspace) is to an exact eigenspace of
H
. In Section
3.2.2
we first treat the non-degenerate case, where a single eigenstate is isolated by a spectral gap, and then extend the argument to a degenerate or quasi-degenerate manifold in Section
3.2.3
. Finally, we describe how Löwdin orthonormalization restores an orthonormal basis of prepared states when multiple eigenvalues are involved.

3.2.1
Rotated eigenproblem with approximate wave operator

In the ideal setting, the exact wave operator
\Omega(\lambda)
maps a
P
-space eigenvector

|\phi(\lambda_{k})\rangle
to the full eigenstate
|\Psi_{k}\rangle
of
H
.
Since we work with an approximate block-encoding
\widetilde{\Omega}(\lambda)
, it is convenient to analyze the
rotated eigenproblem

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Omega}^{\dagger}(\lambda)\,(H-\lambda I)\,\widetilde{\Omega}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}I&-H_{12}f(H_{22}-\lambda I)\\
0&I\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}H_{11}-\lambda I&H_{12}\\
H_{21}&H_{22}-\lambda I\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}I&0\\
-f(H_{22}-\lambda I)H_{21}&I\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)-\lambda I+R_{11}(\lambda)&R_{12}(\lambda)\\
R_{21}(\lambda)&H_{22}-\lambda I\end{bmatrix}.

(82)

This conjugation is analogous to Schur-complement elimination, except that
f
replaces the exact inverse.
We define the residual blocks as

\displaystyle R_{12}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=R_{21}^{\dagger}(\lambda)=H_{12}\Big(I-f(H_{22}-\lambda I)\,(H_{22}-\lambda I)\Big),

(83)

\displaystyle R_{11}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=-\,R_{12}(\lambda)\,f(H_{22}-\lambda I)\,H_{21}.

(84)

Thus the rotated operator is no longer exactly block diagonal: the
P
-space block acquires an additive residual
R_{11}(\lambda)
, and the off-diagonal couplings are
R_{12}(\lambda)
and
R_{21}(\lambda)
.

These terms vanish whenever
f
acts as the exact inverse on the spectrum of
H_{22}-\lambda I
, and they are small whenever the QSVT approximation error is small. Indeed,

\displaystyle\|R_{12}(\lambda)\|

\displaystyle\leq\|H_{12}\|\cdot\bigl\|I-f(H_{22}-\lambda I)\,(H_{22}-\lambda I)\bigr\|

(85)

\displaystyle=\|H_{12}\|\cdot\Big\|(H_{22}-\lambda I)\,\big((H_{22}-\lambda I)^{-1}-f(H_{22}-\lambda I)\big)\Big\|

(86)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{O}(\alpha\,\widetilde{\alpha}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{qsvt}}),

(87)

so if
f
is an
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{qsvt}}
-approximation to the pseudo-inverse on the relevant spectral window, then

\|R_{12}(\lambda)\|=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{qsvt}})
and
\|R_{11}(\lambda)\|=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{qsvt}})
. In fact, as shown later, the diagonal residual
R_{11}
does not appear in the fidelity bound.

Consequently, the rotated problem deviates from the exact block-diagonal form only by
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{qsvt}})
terms, and the lifted states produced by
\widetilde{\Omega}(\lambda)
remain close to true eigenstates provided the spectral separation exceeds these residuals.

3.2.2
Error in non-degenerate eigenstate preparation

Building on the rotated eigenproblem introduced above, we now analyze the fidelity loss when the target eigenvalue is non-degenerate. In the error-free setting, the normalized eigenstate of the full Hamiltonian is

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi_{k}}=\frac{\Omega(\lambda_{k})\ket{\phi(\lambda_{k})}}{\|\Omega(\lambda_{k})\ket{\phi(\lambda_{k})}\|},

(88)

where
\Omega(\lambda)=I-(H_{22}-\lambda I)^{-1}H_{21}
is the exact wave operator and
\ket{\phi}
is a
P
-space eigenvector of
H_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)
. In practice, however, we only have access to an approximate eigenvector
\ket{\hat{\phi}}
returned by the root-finding procedure, an eigenvalue estimate
\hat{\xi}(\lambda)
with
|\hat{\xi}(\lambda)-\lambda|\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{root}}
, and the approximate wave operator
\widetilde{\Omega}(\lambda)=I-f(H_{22}-\lambda I)H_{21}
based on the QSVT polynomial
f
.

The inaccuracy of the
P
-space eigenpair leads to a residual

\displaystyle r_{P}(\lambda)\coloneqq(\widetilde{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)-\lambda I)\ket{\hat{\phi}},

(89)

which can be decomposed as

\displaystyle r_{P}(\lambda)=\bigl(\widetilde{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)-\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)\bigr)\ket{\hat{\phi}}+\bigl(\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)-\lambda I\bigr)\ket{\hat{\phi}}.

(90)

The norm of this residual satisfies

\displaystyle\|r_{P}(\lambda)\|\leq\|\widetilde{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)-\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)\|+|\hat{\xi}(\lambda)-\lambda|\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{est}}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{root}},

(91)

where
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{est}}
is the operator-norm error in estimating
\widetilde{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)
.

To lift
\ket{\hat{\phi}}
into the full space, we apply the rotated operator derived earlier. The cancellation in the off-diagonal block shows that

\displaystyle(H-\lambda I)\,\widetilde{\Omega}(\lambda)\ket{\hat{\phi}}

\displaystyle=(\widetilde{\Omega}^{\dagger})^{-1}\,\widetilde{\Omega}^{\dagger}(H-\lambda I)\widetilde{\Omega}\,\ket{\hat{\phi}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}I&H_{12}f(H_{22}-\lambda I)\\
0&I\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)-\lambda I+R_{11}(\lambda)&R_{12}(\lambda)\\
R_{21}(\lambda)&H_{22}-\lambda I\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\hat{\phi}\\
0\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}I&H_{12}f(H_{22}-\lambda I)\\
0&I\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}r_{P}(\lambda)+R_{11}(\lambda)\hat{\phi}\\
R_{21}(\lambda)\hat{\phi}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}r_{P}(\lambda)+R_{11}(\lambda)\hat{\phi}+H_{12}f(H_{22}-\lambda I)R_{21}(\lambda)\hat{\phi}\\
R_{21}(\lambda)\hat{\phi}\end{bmatrix}.

It is easy to show that

\displaystyle R_{11}(\lambda)+H_{12}f(H_{22}-\lambda I)R_{21}(\lambda)=0,

(92)

using the commutativity of
(H_{22}-\lambda I)
and
f(H_{22}-\lambda I)
, since they share the same eigenbasis.

Define the normalized lifted state

\displaystyle\ket{\widetilde{\Psi}_{k}}=\frac{\widetilde{\Omega}(\hat{\lambda}_{k})\ket{\hat{\phi}(\hat{\lambda}_{k})}}{\|\widetilde{\Omega}(\hat{\lambda}_{k})\ket{\hat{\phi}(\hat{\lambda}_{k})}\|},\qquad\widetilde{\gamma}=\|\widetilde{\Omega}(\hat{\lambda}_{k})\ket{\hat{\phi}(\hat{\lambda}_{k})}\|^{-1}.

(93)

For simplicity, we assume that
\widetilde{\gamma}=\gamma
in the small-error regime. Therefore, the residual vector of this state satisfies

\displaystyle r=(H-\lambda I)\ket{\widetilde{\Psi}}=\gamma\begin{bmatrix}r_{P}(\lambda)\\
R_{21}(\lambda)\ket{\hat{\phi}}\end{bmatrix},

(94)

with norm bounded by

\displaystyle\|r\|=\gamma\sqrt{\|r_{P}(\lambda)\|^{2}+\|R_{21}(\lambda)\hat{\phi}\|^{2}}\leq\gamma\bigl(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{est}}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{root}}+\|R_{21}(\lambda)\|\bigr).

(95)

Expanding
\ket{\widetilde{\Psi}}=\sum_{i}c_{i}\ket{\Psi_{i}}
in the eigenbasis of
H
with eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{i}\}
, let
k=\arg\min_{i}|\lambda_{i}-\lambda|
and define the spectral gap
\Delta=\min_{j\neq k}|\lambda_{j}-\lambda|
. The residual then satisfies

\displaystyle\|r\|^{2}=\sum_{i}|c_{i}|^{2}(\lambda_{i}-\lambda)^{2}\geq\Delta^{2}\sum_{i\neq k}|c_{i}|^{2}.

(96)

Writing
\cos\theta=|\braket{\Psi_{k}|\widetilde{\Psi}}|
and
\sin\theta=\sqrt{1-|c_{k}|^{2}}
gives

\displaystyle\sin\theta\leq\frac{\|r\|}{\Delta},\qquad F=|\langle\Psi_{k}|\widetilde{\Psi}\rangle|\geq\sqrt{1-\frac{\|r\|^{2}}{\Delta^{2}}}.

(97)

Substituting the residual bound yields

\displaystyle F\geq\sqrt{\,1-\frac{\gamma^{2}\bigl(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{est}}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{root}}+\|R_{21}(\lambda)\|\bigr)^{2}}{\Delta^{2}}\,}=1-\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\gamma^{2}\bigl(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{est}}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{root}}+\alpha\widetilde{\alpha}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{qsvt}}\bigr)^{2}}{\Delta^{2}}\right).

(98)

This establishes that the fidelity of the prepared eigenstate depends on three contributions: the
P
-space approximation errors
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{est}}
and
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{root}}
, the rotated off-diagonal error
\|R_{21}(\lambda)\|
, and the conditioning parameters
\gamma
and
\Delta
. High fidelity is therefore guaranteed whenever the spectral gap exceeds the effective residual error.

3.2.3
Error in the quasi-degenerate subspace

In contrast to the non-degenerate case, when several eigenbranches satisfy the root-finding criterion simultaneously,

\bigl|\xi_{i}(\lambda)-\lambda\bigr|\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{root}},\quad\forall\,i\in\mathcal{S},|\mathcal{S}|=m,

the target object is an
m
-dimensional (quasi-)degenerate subspace rather than a single state.
The index set
\mathcal{S}
labels the
P
-space eigenvectors and corresponds to
m
eigenstates in the full Hilbert space, which we index by
k=1,\ldots,m
.
For clarity, we suppress the
P
-space index
i\in\mathcal{S}
and use only the label
k
in what follows.

We now define a notion of fidelity at the subspace level. Let
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
be two
m
-dimensional subspaces with basis matrices

\displaystyle A=\bigl[\,\mathbf{a}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{m}\,\bigr]\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times m},\qquad B=\bigl[\,\mathbf{b}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{b}_{m}\,\bigr]\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times m}.

(99)

If
A
and
B
are not orthonormal, they can be replaced by orthonormal representatives

\displaystyle\widetilde{A}=A\,(A^{\dagger}A)^{-1/2},\qquad\widetilde{B}=B\,(B^{\dagger}B)^{-1/2},

(100)

which span the same subspaces. Below we assume, without loss of generality, that
A^{\dagger}A=B^{\dagger}B=I_{m}
.

To measure the discrepancy between two subspaces, we use the principal angles
0\leq\theta_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\theta_{m}\leq\pi/2
, defined by the singular values of the overlap matrix

\displaystyle A^{\dagger}B=U\,\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{m})\,V^{\dagger},\qquad\sigma_{i}=\cos\theta_{i}\in[0,1],

(101)

with
U,V\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
unitary matrices. Let
A_{\perp}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times(N-m)}
denote an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{A}^{\perp}
, so that
AA^{\dagger}+A_{\perp}A_{\perp}^{\dagger}=I
. From
B^{\dagger}AA^{\dagger}B+B^{\dagger}A_{\perp}A_{\perp}^{\dagger}B=B^{\dagger}B=I_{m}
one obtains

\displaystyle B^{\dagger}A_{\perp}A_{\perp}^{\dagger}B=I_{m}-V\,\mathrm{diag}(\cos^{2}\theta_{1},\ldots,\cos^{2}\theta_{m})\,V^{\dagger},

(102)

and therefore the overlap matrix
A_{\perp}^{\dagger}B
has the corresponding decomposition

\displaystyle A_{\perp}^{\dagger}B=\bar{U}\,\mathrm{diag}(\sin\theta_{1},\dots,\sin\theta_{m})\,V^{\dagger},

(103)

for some semi-unitary
\bar{U}\in\mathbb{C}^{(N-m)\times m}
with
\bar{U}^{\dagger}\bar{U}=I_{m}
. Hence the singular values of
A_{\perp}^{\dagger}B
are precisely
\{\sin\theta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
. It follows that

\displaystyle\|P_{\mathcal{A}}-P_{\mathcal{B}}\|=\sin\theta_{\max},\qquad\|P_{\mathcal{A}}-P_{\mathcal{B}}\|_{F}^{2}=2\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sin^{2}\theta_{k},

(104)

where
P_{\mathcal{A}}=AA^{\dagger}
and
P_{\mathcal{B}}=BB^{\dagger}
.

From the principal angles one can define two natural fidelities. The worst-case fidelity is

\displaystyle F_{\min}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|x\|=1\\
x\in\mathcal{A}\end{subarray}}\|P_{\mathcal{B}}x\|^{2}=\cos^{2}\theta_{\max}=1-\|P_{\mathcal{A}}-P_{\mathcal{B}}\|^{2},

(105)

and the average fidelity is

\displaystyle F_{\mathrm{avg}}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})=\frac{1}{m}\,\mathrm{Tr}\bigl(A^{\dagger}B\,B^{\dagger}A\bigr)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\cos^{2}\theta_{k}=1-\frac{1}{2m}\,\|P_{\mathcal{A}}-P_{\mathcal{B}}\|_{F}^{2}.

(106)

Now suppose a set of
m
eigenbranches pass the root-finding criterion
|\hat{\xi}_{i}(\lambda)-\lambda|\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{root}}
. Lifting each branch to the full space gives vectors
|\widetilde{\Psi}_{k}\rangle
with residuals

\displaystyle(H-\lambda I)\,|\widetilde{\Psi}_{k}\rangle=r_{k},\qquad\|r_{k}\|\leq\gamma_{k}\bigl(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{est}}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{root}}+\|R_{21}(\lambda)\|\bigr),\qquad 1\leq k\leq m,

(107)

where
\gamma_{k}=\sqrt{\langle\Psi_{k}|P|\Psi_{k}\rangle}
. Collecting the columns into

\displaystyle\widetilde{B}=\bigl[\,|\widetilde{\Psi}_{1}\rangle,\dots,|\widetilde{\Psi}_{m}\rangle\,\bigr]\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times m},\qquad R=\bigl[\,r_{1},\dots,r_{m}\,\bigr]\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times m},

(108)

we have the residual relation
H\widetilde{B}-\widetilde{B}\widetilde{\Lambda}=R
with
\widetilde{\Lambda}=\lambda I_{m}
. In general, the states lifted from
P
-space to the full Hilbert space by the wave operator are not necessarily orthogonal. Therefore,
\widetilde{B}
need not have orthonormal columns; define the Gram matrix
G=\widetilde{B}^{\dagger}\widetilde{B}\succ 0
and set

\displaystyle B=\widetilde{B}\,G^{-1/2},\qquad E=R\,G^{-1/2},

(109)

to obtain the orthonormal residual equation

\displaystyle HB-B\widetilde{\Lambda}=E,\qquad B^{\dagger}B=I_{m}.

(110)

Let
A
be an orthonormal eigenbasis for the exact quasi-degenerate subspace satisfying
HA=A\Lambda
, with
\Lambda=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
collecting the exact eigenvalues of
H
. Also define an orthonormal eigenbasis matrix
A_{\perp}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times(N-m)}
such that
HA_{\perp}=A_{\perp}\Lambda_{\perp}
with
\Lambda_{\perp}\in\mathbb{R}^{(N-m)\times(N-m)}
.
Right-multiplying Eq. (
110
) by
A^{\dagger}_{\perp}
gives

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\perp}\,A_{\perp}^{\dagger}B-A^{\dagger}_{\perp}B\,\widetilde{\Lambda}=A_{\perp}^{\dagger}E\implies(\Lambda_{\perp}-\lambda I_{N-m})\,A_{\perp}^{\dagger}B=A^{\dagger}_{\perp}E,

(111)

where we commute
\widetilde{\Lambda}
from right to left since it is proportional to the identity. This relation is governed by the external spectral gap

\displaystyle\Delta=\min_{\lambda^{\prime}\in\Lambda_{\perp}}|\lambda^{\prime}-\lambda|>0,

(112)

so that
(\Lambda_{\perp}-\lambda I_{N-m})
is invertible:

A^{\dagger}_{\perp}B=(\Lambda_{\perp}-\lambda I_{N-m})^{-1}A^{\dagger}_{\perp}E.

This implies the bounds

\displaystyle\|A_{\perp}^{\dagger}B\|\leq\frac{\|E\|}{\Delta},\qquad\|A_{\perp}^{\dagger}B\|_{F}\leq\frac{\|E\|_{F}}{\Delta},

(113)

where we use that
\|\cdot\|
and
\|\cdot\|_{F}
are isometry invariant, and
\|(\Lambda_{\perp}-\lambda I_{N-m})^{-1}\|\leq 1/\Delta
. Since the singular values of
A_{\perp}^{\dagger}B
equal
\{\sin\theta_{i}\}

5
Discussion

In the previous section, we discussed how the query complexity for the block-encoding
U_{H}
depends on the spectral separation
g
and the subspace overlap
\gamma
.
These two parameters are related via

\displaystyle\gamma=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\,1+\langle\phi_{k}|\,H_{12}(H_{22}-\lambda_{k})^{-2}H_{21}\,|\phi_{k}\rangle\,}}\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\,1+\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}/g^{2}\,}}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad g\leq\frac{\widetilde{\alpha}\,\gamma}{\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}}}.

(128)

Hence, large spectral separation
g
implies large overlap
\gamma
, and small overlap
\gamma
implies small spectral separation
g
.
However, the converse need not hold: one can have small
g
while still maintaining a large
\gamma
.
To the best of our knowledge there is no nontrivial lower bound for
g
in terms of other parameters, and consequently no simple upper bound for the factor
\mathcal{O}(1/g^{2})
in the query complexity in terms of
\gamma
as
g\to 0
.

The
intruder state problem
arises when an eigenvalue
\chi\in\mathrm{spec}(H_{22})
approaches the target energy
\lambda_{k}
, so that the external separation

g:=\mathrm{dist}(\lambda_{k},\mathrm{spec}(H_{22}))
becomes small. This is a long-standing issue in multi-reference and subspace methods. In our setting, when
g
is small the QSVT pseudo-inverse for
(H_{22}-\lambda I)^{-1}
requires degree

m=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\alpha/g)
in order to exclude the target eigenvalue from the window where the QSVT approximation is unreliable, so the query complexity increases as
g\to 0
.
Moreover, since the normalization of the polynomial approximation scales as
\mathcal{O}(g)
, an additional
\mathcal{O}(1/g)
queries are needed to achieve a target precision in the matrix-element estimation subroutine.

This small-
g
mechanism is not specific to our quantum approach; it is a structural feature of any
P/Q
partitioning that downfolds onto a model space
P
:

H_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)=H_{11}+H_{12}\,(H_{22}-\lambda I)^{-1}H_{21},\qquad g:=\mathrm{dist}\bigl(\lambda,\mathrm{spec}(H_{22})\bigr).

(129)

Since
\|(H_{22}-\lambda I)^{-1}\|=1/g
, the dressing term can scale as
\|H_{21}\|^{2}/g
, and the
P
-space eigenvectors rotate rapidly as
g\to 0
(Davis–Kahan–type bounds imply subspace angles that scale like
1/g
). This is precisely the “intruder” phenomenon. Below are representative classical methods that suffer from intruder states:

•

Multi-reference perturbation theory (e.g., CASPT2, MRMP).

First-order amplitudes scale as

t_{\mu}\sim\langle\Phi_{\mu}|V|\Psi_{0}\rangle/\Delta E_{\mu}
,
where
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
is the reference in
P
,
|\Phi_{\mu}\rangle
is a
Q
-space configuration,

\Delta E_{\mu}:=E_{0}^{(0)}-E_{\mu}^{(0)}
is the zeroth-order energy denominator (up to sign convention),
and
V=H-H_{0}
is the perturbation.

•

NEVPT2 / Dyall partitionings.

These modify denominators to enforce
\Delta E_{\mu}>0
by construction; they are widely used because they are (practically) intruder-free, underscoring that the issue is generic without safeguards.

•

MRCI (internally contracted or uncontracted).

The truncated secular problem remains sensitive to near-degenerate
Q
configurations; Ritz vectors/values can change abruptly with small geometry shifts when
g
is small.

•

MRCC (FSMRCC, HS-MRCC, JM-MRCC).

Even without explicit
\lambda
-dependence, projected amplitude equations and their Jacobians become nearly singular as
g\to 0
; Fock-space MRCC often uses intermediate-Hamiltonian shifts to decouple dangerous
Q
roots.

•

Subspace diagonalization / Rayleigh–Ritz (Davidson/Lanczos on active spaces).

Condition numbers of the projected problem grow like
1/g
; state tracking and variational monotonicity degrade unless one enlarges the model space.

•

EOM/LR frameworks (EOM-CC, ADC).

Truncated manifolds effectively sample resolvents
(\omega-\bar{H})^{-1}
(with
\bar{H}=e^{-T}He^{T}
and
\omega
an excitation energy); proximity of external poles yields unstable roots and strong state mixing—an intruder analogue.

A standard strategy to mitigate intruder states is the
intermediate Hamiltonian
technique
[
MDD85
,
EDM87
]
.
The idea is to introduce a “buffer” model space within
P
-space so that eigenstates with energies below this buffer are protected and remain well separated from problematic
Q
-space poles.
Concretely, we split
P=P_{\mathrm{t}}\oplus P_{\mathrm{b}}
(targets
\oplus
buffer) and construct a wave-operator–like object
R(\lambda)
such that the eigenvalues in the target space
P_{\mathrm{t}}
coincide with those of
H
, while the buffer subspace
P_{\mathrm{b}}
is allowed to mix strongly with
Q
-space eigenstates and therefore need not reproduce the exact spectrum.
This enlarges the spectral separation of the
P_{\mathrm{t}}
eigenbranches from
\mathrm{spec}(H_{22})
that controls resolvent norms, thereby reducing the QSVT degree and improving the query complexity matrix element estimation subroutine.
A careful design of the buffer subspace
P_{\mathrm{b}}
(which states to lift) and of
R(\lambda)
is important in practice; however, a full, end-to-end implementation of the intermediate-Hamiltonian variant within our framework is beyond the present scope and is left for future work.

6
Conclusion

We presented a fault-tolerant quantum algorithm that
directly
solves quasi-degenerate eigenvalue problems by working at the subspace level via the Feshbach effective-Hamiltonian formalism. Rather than demanding energy resolution finer than the intra-manifold splitting
\Delta_{\mathrm{intra}}
, the method reduces the full problem to a nonlinear eigenproblem on a chosen reference subspace and then systematically lifts reduced-space solutions to full eigenstates using a wave operator. In this formulation, the effective Hamiltonian
H_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)
exactly reproduces the target part of the spectrum and is accompanied by a wave operator
\Omega(\lambda)
that maps
P
-space eigenvectors to physical eigenstates, making degeneracy detection and manifold-level diagonalization native to the algorithmic workflow.

On a quantum computer, we realize the resolvent
(H_{22}-\lambda I)^{-1}
by combining block-encodings and quantum singular value transformation (QSVT). In particular, we block-encode the blocks
H_{22}-\lambda I
,
H_{12}
, and compose them to obtain a QSVT polynomial
f(H_{22}-\lambda I)\approx(H_{22}-\lambda I)^{-1}
outside a small exclusion window, thereby implementing the dress Hamiltonian (self-energy) term and
H_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)
. This block-encoding pipeline uses an
n
-qubit
P
-controlled NOT (constructed in the Appendix
A
) to pick out the
P/Q
blocks coherently.

We then estimate matrix elements of the block-encoded
H_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)
, diagonalize the resulting
d\times d
reduced matrix to track eigenbranches
\xi_{i}(\lambda)
, and run a noise-aware root-finding routine on
\hat{\mu}_{i}(\lambda)=\hat{\xi}_{i}(\lambda)-\lambda
. A rotated-eigenproblem analysis shows that when the QSVT pseudo-inverse is
\varepsilon
-accurate on the relevant spectral window, the off-diagonal residuals and the
P
-block perturbation are

remain close to exact eigenstates whenever the external spectral separation dominates these residuals. This yields certified bounds for eigenvalue accuracy and subspace fidelity that are robust to quasi-degeneracy.

Numerically, we validated the full workflow on three benchmarks designed to stress regimes where gap-dependent or single reference state methods are unable to detect the (quasi-)degeneracy: a

Conceptual and technical contributions.

(i) A nonperturbative, energy-dependent downfolding procedure realized via block-encodings and QSVT, avoiding classical series truncations and ill-conditioned linear solves;

Scope and implications.

Because the algorithm operates on manifolds rather than single states, it

In sum, we establish a rigorous, nonperturbative route to eigenvalue estimation and eigenstate preparation in the quasi-degenerate regime. By realizing the effective-Hamiltonian and wave-operator formalism within the block-encoding/QSVT toolkit, our method provides a scalable, subspace-level algorithm that resolves clustered spectra, prepares multiple eigenstates with guarantees, and aligns with applications in condensed matter and quantum chemistry that require subspace methods and explicit degeneracy detection.

Appendix B
Continuity of Eigenbranches across the Pole

A particularly interesting property of the eigenbranches is that when
\lambda
crosses an eigenvalue of
H_{22}
—where the effective Hamiltonian
H_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)
becomes ill-defined—some eigenbranches can nevertheless persist and remain differentiable functions of
\lambda
. The ill-defined nature of
H_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)
arises because, as
\lambda
approaches an eigenvalue of
H_{22}
, some of the eigenvalues
\xi_{i}(\lambda)
diverge to infinity while the remaining ones stay finite. For convenience, we introduce the notations
\xi_{i}^{\uparrow}(\lambda)
and
\xi_{i}^{\downarrow}(\lambda)
to denote the eigenvalues ordered increasingly and decreasingly, respectively, so that
\xi_{1}^{\uparrow}(\lambda)\leq\cdots\leq\xi_{d}^{\uparrow}(\lambda)
and
\xi_{1}^{\downarrow}(\lambda)\geq\cdots\geq\xi_{d}^{\downarrow}(\lambda)
.

This behavior is illustrated in Figure
6
. As
\lambda
approaches the pole associated with
\xi^{\uparrow}_{1}(\lambda)
from the left, we observe
\xi^{\uparrow}_{1}(\lambda)\to-\infty
while
\xi^{\uparrow}_{2}(\lambda),\ldots,\xi^{\uparrow}_{d}(\lambda)
remain finite. Conversely, when
\lambda
approaches the pole associated with
\xi^{\uparrow}_{d}(\lambda)
from the right, we have
\xi^{\uparrow}_{d}(\lambda)\to\infty
while
\xi^{\uparrow}_{1}(\lambda),\ldots,\xi^{\uparrow}_{d-1}(\lambda)
remain finite. This phenomenon is formalized in
[
LKS16
]
, Theorem 4.1: by considering an alternative spectral Schur complement that projects out the eigenspace corresponding to the diverging (infinite) branch, one obtains eigenbranches that are continuous and differentiable at
\lambda\in\mathrm{spec}(H_{22})
.

Here we slightly generalize this result by allowing eigenvalues of
H_{22}
to be degenerate rather than simple. Let the distinct spectrum of
H_{22}
be

\mathrm{spec}(H_{22})=\{\chi_{1},\ldots,\chi_{M}\},\qquad M\leq\dim(\mathcal{H}_{d}^{\perp}),

and for each
\chi_{j}
let
\{\,|\psi_{j,1}\rangle,\ldots,|\psi_{j,\widetilde{d}_{j}}\rangle\,\}
be an orthonormal eigenbasis of its eigenspace, where
\widetilde{d}_{j}=\dim\bigl(\mathrm{null}(H_{22}-\chi_{j}I)\bigr)
is the degeneracy of
\chi_{j}
. With this notation,
H_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)
can be written as

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)=H_{11}-\sum_{j=1}^{M}\sum_{k=1}^{\widetilde{d}_{j}}\frac{1}{\chi_{j}-\lambda}|\widetilde{\psi}_{j,k}\rangle\langle\widetilde{\psi}_{j,k}|,

(130)

where
|\widetilde{\psi}_{j,k}\rangle=H_{12}|\psi_{j,k}\rangle
, and
\{|\widetilde{\psi}_{j,k}\rangle\}_{k=1}^{\widetilde{d}_{j}}
are not necessarily linearly independent and therefore
\sum_{k=1}^{\widetilde{d}_{j}}|\widetilde{\psi}_{j,k}\rangle\langle\widetilde{\psi}_{j,k}|
might have rank less than
\widetilde{d}_{j}
. We can diagonalize the positive operator

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\widetilde{d}_{j}}|\widetilde{\psi}_{j,k}\rangle\langle\widetilde{\psi}_{j,k}|=\sum_{k=1}^{d_{j}}\sigma_{j,k}|\phi_{j,k}\rangle\langle\phi_{j,k}|,

(131)

with
d_{j}\leq\widetilde{d}_{j}
and
\sigma_{j,k}\geq 0
for all
k=1,...,d_{j}
. Define the eigenspace
\mathcal{E}_{j}=\text{span}\{|\phi_{j,1}\rangle,...,|\phi_{j,d_{j}}\rangle\}
and its orthogonal complement
\mathcal{E}_{j}^{\perp}=\mathcal{H}_{d}-\mathcal{E}_{j}
and the operator

\displaystyle H_{{\mathrm{eff}},j^{\prime}}(\lambda)=H_{11}-\sum_{j=1,j\neq j^{\prime}}^{M}\sum_{k=1}^{d_{j}}\frac{\sigma_{j,k}}{\chi_{j}-\lambda}|\phi_{j,k}\rangle\langle\phi_{j,k}|,\quad\quad\overline{H}_{\mathrm{eff},j^{\prime}}(\lambda)=Q_{j^{\prime}}H_{\mathrm{eff},j^{\prime}}(\lambda)Q_{j^{\prime}}\big|_{\mathcal{E}_{j^{\prime}}^{\perp}}

(132)

where
Q_{j^{\prime}}=\sum_{k}|\phi_{j^{\prime}k}\rangle\langle\phi_{j^{\prime}k}|
is the orthogonal projector onto the subspace
\mathcal{E}_{j^{\prime}}^{\perp}
. We denote the eigenvalues of
\overline{H}_{\mathrm{eff},j^{\prime}}(\lambda)
by
\nu_{i}(\lambda)
. The following theorem characterize the limit of eigenvalue as
\lambda\to\chi_{j^{\prime}}^{-}
and
\lambda\to\chi_{j^{\prime}}^{+}

Theorem 18
.

Let
\chi_{j^{\prime}}
be an eigenvalue of
H_{22}
with degeneracy
d_{j^{\prime}}
. As
\lambda
approaches
\chi_{j^{\prime}}
, the eigenbranches
\xi_{i}(\lambda)
of the effective Hamiltonian satisfy the following limits:

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\to\chi_{j^{\prime}}^{-}}\xi_{i}^{\uparrow}(\lambda)=\begin{cases}-\infty&\text{for }i\leq d_{j^{\prime}},\\
\nu_{i-d_{j^{\prime}}}^{\uparrow}(\lambda)&\text{for }i>d_{j^{\prime}},\end{cases}\quad\quad\lim_{\lambda\to\chi_{j^{\prime}}^{+}}\xi^{\uparrow}_{i}(\lambda)=\begin{cases}+\infty&\text{for }i>d-d_{j^{\prime}},\\
\nu^{\uparrow}_{i}(\lambda)&\text{for }i\leq d-d_{j^{\prime}}.\end{cases}

(133)

Proof.

We first consider the left limit
\lambda\to\chi_{j^{\prime}}^{-}
. For the case
i=1
, the associated eigenvalue
\xi^{\uparrow}_{1}(\lambda)
can be characterized variationally as

\displaystyle\xi^{\uparrow}_{1}(\lambda)=\min_{|\psi\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{d}}\langle\psi|H_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)|\psi\rangle=\min_{|\psi\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{d}}\left(\langle\psi|H_{\mathrm{eff},j^{\prime}}(\lambda)|\psi\rangle-\sum_{k=1}^{d_{j^{\prime}}}\frac{\sigma_{j^{\prime}k}}{\chi_{j^{\prime}}-\lambda}\left|\langle\psi|\phi_{j^{\prime}k}\rangle\right|^{2}\right),

(134)

where
H_{\mathrm{eff},j^{\prime}}(\lambda)
denotes the part of
H_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)
regular at
\lambda=\chi_{j^{\prime}}
and
\{|\phi_{j^{\prime}k}\rangle\}_{k=1}^{d_{j^{\prime}}}
are the eigenvectors of singular eigenspace
\mathcal{E}_{j^{\prime}}
. As
\lambda\to\chi_{j^{\prime}}^{-}
, the term
\frac{1}{\chi_{j^{\prime}}-\lambda}\to+\infty
, causing the second term to dominate, while
\langle\psi|H_{\mathrm{eff},j^{\prime}}(\lambda)|\psi\rangle
remains finite for all
|\psi\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{d}
. Therefore,
\xi^{\uparrow}_{1}(\lambda)\to-\infty
in this limit. The corresponding eigenvector
|\phi^{\uparrow}_{1}(\lambda)\rangle
converges to one of the
|\phi_{j^{\prime}k}\rangle
associated with the largest
\sigma_{j^{\prime}k}
. In fact, for
\lambda
sufficiently close to
\chi_{j^{\prime}}
from the left (not necessarily taking the limit), we have
|\phi^{\uparrow}_{1}(\lambda)\rangle=|\phi_{j^{\prime}k}\rangle
.

For
2\leq i\leq d_{j^{\prime}}
, the eigenvalues of
S(\lambda)
can be characterized using the min-max principle:

\displaystyle\xi^{\uparrow}_{i}(\lambda)=\min_{\dim(\mathcal{U}_{i})=i}\max_{|\psi\rangle\in\mathcal{U}_{i}}\langle\psi|H_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)|\psi\rangle=\min_{\dim(\mathcal{U}_{i})=i}\max_{|\psi\rangle\in\mathcal{U}_{i}}\left(\langle\psi|H_{\mathrm{eff},j^{\prime}}(\lambda)|\psi\rangle-\sum_{k=1}^{d_{j^{\prime}}}\frac{\sigma_{j^{\prime}k}}{\chi_{j^{\prime}}-\lambda}\left|\langle\psi|\phi_{j^{\prime}k}\rangle\right|^{2}\right),

(135)

and by the same reasoning, we conclude that
\xi_{i}(\lambda)\to-\infty
as
\lambda\to\chi_{j^{\prime}}^{-}
. The eigenvector
|\phi^{\uparrow}_{i}(\lambda)\rangle=|\phi_{j^{\prime}k}\rangle
corresponding to the
i
-th largest
\sigma_{j^{\prime}k}
when
\lambda
is sufficiently close to
\chi_{j^{\prime}}
from the left (not necessarily taking the limit).

For
i>d_{j^{\prime}}
, we again apply the min-max theorem:

\displaystyle\xi^{\uparrow}_{i}(\lambda)=\min_{\dim(\mathcal{U}_{i})=i}\max_{|\psi\rangle\in\mathcal{U}_{i}}\langle\psi|H_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)|\psi\rangle.

(136)

Since
\dim(\mathcal{U}_{i})>d_{j^{\prime}}
and the orthogonal complement of the singular subspace,
\mathcal{E}_{j^{\prime}}^{\perp}
, has dimension
\dim(\mathcal{H}_{d})-d_{j^{\prime}}
, the intersection
\mathcal{U}_{i}\cap\mathcal{E}_{j^{\prime}}^{\perp}
is non-trivial. Thus, we have the bound:

\displaystyle\max_{|\psi\rangle\in\mathcal{U}_{i}}\langle\psi|H_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)|\psi\rangle\geq\max_{|\psi\rangle\in\mathcal{U}_{i}\cap\mathcal{E}_{j^{\prime}}^{\perp}}\langle\psi|H_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)|\psi\rangle=\max_{|\psi\rangle\in\mathcal{U}_{i}\cap\mathcal{E}_{j^{\prime}}^{\perp}}\langle\psi|H_{\mathrm{eff},j^{\prime}}(\lambda)|\psi\rangle\geq B,

(137)

where the last inequality follows from the fact that
H_{\mathrm{eff},j^{\prime}}(\lambda)
is regular and bounded below by some constant
B
. Consequently,
\xi^{\uparrow}_{i}(\lambda)\geq B
, i.e., the eigenvalue remains finite as
\lambda\to\chi_{j^{\prime}}^{-}
.

Now consider the eigenvalue equation:

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)|\phi^{\uparrow}_{i}(\lambda)\rangle=\xi^{\uparrow}_{i}(\lambda)|\phi^{\uparrow}_{i}(\lambda)\rangle=H_{\mathrm{eff},j^{\prime}}(\lambda)|\phi^{\uparrow}_{i}(\lambda)\rangle-\sum_{k=1}^{d_{j^{\prime}}}\frac{\sigma_{j^{\prime}k}}{\chi_{j^{\prime}}-\lambda}|\phi_{j^{\prime}k}\rangle\langle\phi_{j^{\prime}k}|\phi^{\uparrow}_{i}(\lambda)\rangle.

(138)

Rearranging this expression, we obtain:

\displaystyle\left(\xi^{\uparrow}_{i}(\lambda)-H_{\mathrm{eff},j^{\prime}}(\lambda)\right)|\phi^{\uparrow}_{i}(\lambda)\rangle=-\sum_{k=1}^{d_{j^{\prime}}}\frac{\sigma_{j^{\prime}k}}{\chi_{j^{\prime}}-\lambda}|\phi_{j^{\prime}k}\rangle\langle\phi_{j^{\prime}k}|\phi^{\uparrow}_{i}(\lambda)\rangle.

(139)

Since both
\xi^{\uparrow}_{i}(\lambda)
and
H_{\mathrm{eff},j^{\prime}}(\lambda)
are bounded as
\lambda\to\chi_{j^{\prime}}^{-}
, the right-hand side must also remain bounded. To evaluate its limit, we recall from the earlier analysis that when
\lambda
approaches sufficiently close to
\chi_{j^{\prime}}
from the left, the eigenvectors
|\phi^{\uparrow}_{i}(\lambda)\rangle=|\phi_{j^{\prime},k}\rangle
for
i=1,\dots,d_{j^{\prime}}
. As a result, for
i>d_{j^{\prime}}
and sufficiently small
|\chi_{j^{\prime}}-\lambda|
with
\lambda<\chi_{j^{\prime}}
, the overlap
\langle\phi_{j^{\prime}k}|\phi^{\uparrow}_{i}(\lambda)\rangle=0
, and we have:

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\to\chi_{j^{\prime}}^{-}}\frac{\langle\phi_{j^{\prime}k}|\phi^{\uparrow}_{i}(\lambda)\rangle}{\chi_{j^{\prime}}-\lambda}=0,

(140)

for all
d_{j^{\prime}}<i\leq\dim(\mathcal{H}_{d})
. Therefore, in the limit
\lambda\to\chi_{j^{\prime}}^{-}
, the eigenvalue equation converges to

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{eff},j^{\prime}}(\lambda)|\phi^{\uparrow}_{i}(\lambda)\rangle=\xi^{\uparrow}_{i}(\lambda)|\phi^{\uparrow}_{i}(\lambda)\rangle,\quad\quad|\phi^{\uparrow}_{i}(\lambda)\rangle\in\mathcal{E}_{j^{\prime}}^{\perp}

(141)

which is indeed the eigenvalue equation for
H_{\mathrm{eff},j^{\prime}}(\lambda)
restricted in the subspace
\mathcal{E}_{j^{\prime}}^{\perp}
. This confirms that the corresponding eigenbranches
\xi^{\uparrow}_{i}(\lambda)
for
i>d_{j^{\prime}}
remain finite and smoothly connected through the pole and have the corresponding limit
\xi^{\uparrow}_{i}(\lambda)\to\nu^{\uparrow}_{i-d_{j^{\prime}}}(\lambda)
.

The proof for the right-hand limit
\lambda\to\chi_{j^{\prime}}^{+}
is completely analogous to the left-hand case. In this case, it is convenient to consider the eigenvalues of
H_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)
arranged in
decreasing
order:

\displaystyle\xi^{\downarrow}_{1}(\lambda)\geq\xi^{\downarrow}_{2}(\lambda)\geq\cdots\geq\xi^{\downarrow}_{d}(\lambda),

(142)

which can be characterized using the max-min principle:

\displaystyle\xi^{\downarrow}_{i}(\lambda)=\max_{\dim(\mathcal{U}_{i})=i}\min_{|\psi\rangle\in\mathcal{U}_{i}}\langle\psi|H_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)|\psi\rangle.

(143)

Following the same reasoning as in the left-limit case, we find:

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\to\chi_{j^{\prime}}^{+}}\xi^{\downarrow}_{i}(\lambda)=\begin{cases}+\infty&\text{for }i=1,\dots,d_{j^{\prime}},\\
\nu^{\downarrow}_{i-d_{j^{\prime}}}(\lambda)&\text{for }i>d_{j^{\prime}}.\end{cases}

(144)

By returning to the original ordering
\xi^{\uparrow}_{1}(\lambda)\leq\cdots\leq\xi^{\uparrow}_{d}(\lambda)
and relabeling the indices appropriately, we obtain the result stated in the theorem.
∎
