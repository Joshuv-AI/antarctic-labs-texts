Abstract
We investigate deterministic identification over arbitrary memoryless channels under the constraint that the error probabilities of first and second kind are exponentially small in the block length 𝐧 \mathbf{n} , controlled by reliability exponents 𝐄 𝟏 , 𝐄 𝟐 ≥ 𝟎 \mathbf{E_{1},E_{2}\geq 0} . In contrast to the regime of slowly vanishing errors, where the identifiable message length scales linearithmically as 𝚯 ⁡ ( 𝐧 ​ log ⁡ 𝐧 ) \mathbf{\Theta(n\log n)} , here we find that for positive exponents linear scaling is restored, now with a rate that is a function of the reliability exponents. We give upper and lower bounds on the ensuing rate-reliability function in terms of (the logarithm of) the packing and covering numbers of the channel output set, which for small error exponents 𝐄 𝟏 , 𝐄 𝟐 > 𝟎 \mathbf{E_{1},E_{2}>0} can be expanded in leading order as the product of the Minkowski dimension of a certain parametrisation the channel output set and log ⁡ min ⁡ { 𝐄 𝟏 , 𝐄 𝟐 } \mathbf{\log\min\{E_{1},E_{2}\}} . These allow us to recover the previously observed slightly superlinear identification rates, and offer a different perspective for understanding them in more traditional information theory terms. We also show that even if only one of the two errors is required to be exponentially small, the linearithmic scaling is lost. We further illustrate our results with a discussion of the case of dimension zero, and extend them to classical-quantum channels and quantum channels with tensor product input restriction.



I
Introduction

I-A

Background and previous work

In Shannon’s fundamental model of communication
[
2
]
, the receiver aims to faithfully recover an original message sent through
n
uses of a memoryless noisy channel
W:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
, given by a probability transition kernel between measurable spaces
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
. The maximum number
M
of transmittable messages scales exponentially with
n
, and thus we define the
(linear-scale) rate
of a transmission code as
R=\frac{1}{n}\log M
, with
\log
the binary logarithm. The maximum rate for asymptotically faithful transmission – that is, with arbitrarily small probability of error for sufficiently long code words – is known as the
capacity

C
of the channel
[
2
,
3
]
.

This model contrasts with the
identification
task in which instead of recovering the initial message, the receiver is only interested in knowing whether it is equal to one particular message of their choice or not.
Jaja demonstrated in
[
4
]
that this task involves lower communication complexity than Shannon’s original transmission scenario. Building on this result, Ahlswede and Dueck
[
5
]
fully characterized the identification problem and analyzed its performance over general discrete memoryless channels (DMCs) using a randomized encoder.
They showed that identification codes can achieve doubly exponential growth of the number
N
of messages as a function of the block length
n
, i.e.
\log N\sim 2^{nR}
. In other words, we can identify exponentially more messages than we can transmit.
In general, randomized identification codes
can achieve this exponential scaling
[
6
,
7
]
.
As these results rely crucially on randomized encodings, it is natural to ask what happens if we impose a deterministic approach.

Definition 1
.

An
(n,N,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})

deterministic identification (DI) code
is a family
\{(u_{j},\mathcal{E}_{j}):j\in[N]\}
consisting of code words
u_{j}\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
and subsets
\mathcal{E}_{j}\subset\mathcal{Y}^{n}
, such that

\forall j\neq k\in[N],\quad W_{u_{j}}(\mathcal{E}_{j})\geq 1-\lambda_{1},\quad W_{u_{j}}(\mathcal{E}_{k})\leq\lambda_{2},

where we use the notation
W_{u_{j}}:=W^{n}(\cdot|u_{j})
for the conditional probability distributions at the output.

It was initially observed that DI leads to much poorer results in terms of code size scaling in the block length
[
5
,
8
]
than the unrestricted randomized identification. Indeed, DI over discrete memoryless channels can only lead to linear scaling of the message length,
\log N\sim Rn

[
9
]
as in Shannon’s communication paradigm (albeit with a higher rate
R
).
Despite this poorer performance, interest in deterministic codes has recently been renewed, as they have proven to be easier to implement and simulate
[
10
]
, to explicitly construct
[
11
]
, and offer reliable single-block performance
[
5
]
; see in particular
[
12
]
for identification codes.
Also, surprisingly, certain channels with continuous input alphabets have DI codes governed by a slightly superlinear scaling in the block length:
\log N\sim Rn\log n
(commonly referred to as
linearithmic
).
This was first observed for Gaussian channels
[
9
]
, Gaussian channels with both fast and slow fading
[
13
,
14
]
, and Poisson channels
[
15
,
16
]
. In our recent work
[
17
,
18
]
we have generalized all these particular DI results, proving that the linearithmic scaling observed in these special cases is actually a feature exhibited by general channels with discrete output. For the rest of the paper we will in fact assume that
\mathcal{Y}
is finite.

It is important to emphasize the fundamental implications of different scalings beyond the value of the rate expressions. When a code achieves superlinear scaling – for example linearithmic
\log N(n)\sim Rn\log n
–, the number of identifiable messages grows
asymptotically faster
than in the linear regime
\log N(n)\sim R^{\prime}n
, regardless of how large the linear rate constant might be. That is, even if a linear code achieves a higher rate
R^{\prime}>R
, the superlinear code will eventually yield
infinitely more
identifiable messages as
n\to\infty
.
This distinction justifies a careful attention to the growth regime. Specifically when calculating the capacity of a channel, it is crucial to define it in the correct scaling. Indeed, if we have a linearithmic code and calculate the maximum linear (or exponential) rate, we will trivially get
\infty
(or 0). A meaningful optimum rate value can only be calculated in this example through a suitably defined linearithmic capacity.

In
[
17
]
slightly superlinear (in fact, linearithmic) DI capacities
\dot{C}_{\text{DI}}
of general channels were calculated in the pessimistic and optimistic regimes
(for further insight on these regimes see
[
19
]
or the discussion in
[
17
, Sect. 7.1]
,
the formal definition of the capacities can be found in Subsection
IV-A
), and it was shown that they can be respectively bounded in terms of the lower and upper covering dimension
\underline{d}_{M}
and
\overline{d}_{M}
[an introduction and the definitions of these concepts can be found in Section
II
below, see Equations (
1
) and (
2
)] of a certain algebraic transformation of the set of output probability distributions of the channel:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\underline{d}_{M}

\displaystyle\leq\dot{C}_{\text{DI}}(W)\,\,\leq\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n\log n}\log N\,\leq\frac{1}{2}\underline{d}_{M},

(1)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\overline{d}_{M}

\displaystyle\leq\dot{C}_{\text{DI}}^{\text{opt}}(W)\leq\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n\log n}\log N\leq\frac{1}{2}\overline{d}_{M}.

(2)

When
d=0
, like in the case of the discrete memoryless channel, a more general and abstract theorem is provided to meaningfully bound the size of the code at the correct (slower, linear for DMCs) scaling
[
17
, Thm. 5.11]
.

I-B

Motivation and preliminary problem setting

While the DI capacity is defined as the maximum achievable (superlinear) rate for reliable communication – where the error probability can be made arbitrarily small with sufficiently large
n
–, it does a priori not provide information on how quickly the errors vanish.
In contrast, it is well-known in the communication setting
[
2
,
20
]
that at (linear scale) rates below capacity,
0\leq R<C
, the error probability decreases exponentially with the block length:
\lambda\sim 2^{-En}
.
In the present paper we initiate the study of the performance of DI codes when
\lambda_{1}
and/or
\lambda_{2}
are exponentially small, in an attempt to better understand the nature of the superlinear rates
.

Anticipating our subsequent results, we associate to every
(n,N,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
-DI code (recall Definition
1
) the triple of linear-scale message and error rates
(R(n),E_{1}(n),E_{2}(n))
, where:

R(n):=\frac{1}{n}\log N,\,\,E_{1}(n):=\frac{1}{n}\log\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}},\,\,E_{2}(n):=\frac{1}{n}\log\frac{1}{\lambda_{2}}.

The main object of study in this work is the tradeoff between the rate functions
R(n)
and the error exponents
E_{1,2}(n)
(with the notation
E_{1,2}
meaning that the same equation holds for both
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
), first in the finite block length regime, and then on the asymptotic limit
n\rightarrow\infty
. The error exponent, can of course be a function of
n
, under the only condition that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}nE_{1,2}(n)=\infty
, as we need the errors to vanish in the asymptotic limit. In other words,
E_{1,2}(n)\geq\omega(1/n)
.

We call
(R,E_{1},E_{2})
an
(asymptotically) achievable triple
if there exist DI codes with
(R(n),E_{1}(n),E_{2}(n))
converging to
(R,E_{1},E_{2})
for
n\rightarrow\infty
. The set of all achievable triples will be referred to as the
achievable region

\mathcal{R}(W):=\{(R,E_{1},E_{2})\text{ achievable}\}
. The rate-reliability function is then

R(E_{1},E_{2}):=\sup_{E_{1},E_{2}}R\quad\text{s.t.}\quad(R,E_{1},E_{2})\in\mathcal{R}(W).

Similarly, we can define an
optimistic achievable region

\overline{\mathcal{R}}(W)\supseteq\mathcal{R}(W)
by allowing the convergence to happen only for a subsequence
n_{k}\rightarrow\infty
of block lengths. That is to say,
\overline{\mathcal{R}}(W)
is the set of all triples
(R,E_{1},E_{2})
such that there exist deterministic identification codes of block length
n_{k}\rightarrow\infty
with
(R{(n_{k})},E_{1}{(n_{k})},E_{2}{(n_{k})})\rightarrow(R,E_{1},E_{2})
. The optimistic rate-reliability function is thus

\overline{R}(E_{1},E_{2}):=\sup_{E_{1},E_{2}}R\quad\text{s.t.}\quad(R,E_{1},E_{2})\in\overline{\mathcal{R}}(W).

Note that both
\mathcal{R}(W)
and
\overline{\mathcal{R}}(W)
are so-called
corners
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
: they are both actually contained in the positive orthant
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{3}
, contain the origin, and with every point
(R,E_{1},E_{2})
contain the entire box
[0;R]\times[0;E_{1}]\times[0;E_{2}]
.

While in our previous work
[
17
]
we had no restrictions on the identification errors other than them being sufficiently small as
n\rightarrow\infty
, here we study in depth the performance of codes with different error settings. We start with the case where both errors vanish exponentially, for which we provide lower and upper bounds on the trade-off between rate and error exponents in the finite block length regime. The study of these bounds in the asymptotic limit allow us to show not only that codes with exponentially vanishing errors can only have linear scaling, but also to find the error conditions under which the main superlinear results in
[
17
]
can be recovered. We furthermore study the cases where only one of the two errors is constrained to vanish exponentially, showing that linearithmic scaling is also lost. Despite the current analysis being much more complete both in terms of error settings and non-asymptotic regimes, we can rely on many ideas and tools from our previous work
[
17
]
, as it was written with an eye to finite block length.

Fig. 1:
Schematic of the rate-reliability function for transmission and
DI. The rate-reliability function for transmission (blue line) is a monotonically non-increasing function with different regions depending on the optimum coding strategies (see the tendency change from linear to quadratic behaviour marked by the grey dotted line). For error exponents going to zero (very slow error decrease with the block length
n
), the linear rate converges to the transmission capacity
C(W)
; no higher rates are possible. Here, in Sections
III
and
IV
, we provide bounds on the rate-reliability function for DI (red line) that show how the rate goes to infinity logarithmically in the error exponents
E_{1}=E_{2}=E\approx 0
, corresponding to the linearithmic code length in that regime. Note that the DI curve must dominate the transmission curve everywhere, since an
(n,N,\lambda)
-transmission code is automatically an
(n,N,\lambda,\lambda)
-DI code – indeed, if we can decode a message, we can identify it.

I-C

Outline

After giving the necessary technical tools in Section
II
, we present upper and lower bounds on the non-asymptotic rates as functions of the error exponents in Section
III
.
We remark already here that while these bounds are stated for general exponents, they are most suitable for exponents below a certain threshold. A full account of this fact and alternative analyses for the regime of large error exponents are given in the subsequent sections.

In Section
IV
, we discuss the bounds from three different angles: in
IV-A
we show that for all (sufficiently small)
E_{1},E_{2}>0
, the rate-reliability functions
R(E_{1},E_{2})
and
\overline{R}(E_{1},E_{2})
are bounded away from
0
and
\infty
; and by letting
E_{1}(n)
and
E_{2}(n)
vanish slowly, we recover the main linearithmic capacity results from
[
17
]
(see the schematic Figure
1
). In Subsection
IV-B
we show how to deal with channels characterised by dimension zero, where the previous bounds seem not to give the full picture of what is happening.
Finally, in
IV-C
we discuss the regime of large error exponents (quickly vanishing errors) and why the bounds in
III
are not suitable there.

Section
V
studies the case of asymmetric error exponents, that is, when we constrain one of the errors to vanish exponentially while the other is just bounded. While the results are used to broaden the previous discussion, we need a completely different approach, hence the separate section.

Finally, in Section
VI
, we generalize the main bounds in Section
III
to the quantum setting for classical-quantum channels, and general quantum channels under the restriction that only product states are used on the input. We close with a brief discussion and open problems in Section
VII
.

II
Preliminaries

As we have already mentioned, a number of technical ideas in this work rely on the methods developed in
[
17
]
. These are mainly based on metric studies of the output probability sets. The essential distance measures needed are defined next: the
total variation distance
is a statistical distance measure which coincides with half the
L^{1}
distance between the probability mass functions. Let
P
and
Q
be two probability distributions defined on a finite or countably infinite measurable space
\mathcal{L}
, then the total variation distance is defined as

\frac{1}{2}\|P-Q\|_{1}:=\sup_{L\in\mathcal{L}}|P(L)-Q(L)|=\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}}\frac{1}{2}|P(\ell)-Q(\ell)|.

(3)

The
Bhattacharyya coefficient
(in quantum information called
fidelity
) is given by
F(P,Q)=\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}}\sqrt{P(\ell)Q(\ell)}
, and it is related to the total variation distance by the following bounds:

1-F(P,Q)\leq\frac{1}{2}\|P-Q\|_{1}\leq\sqrt{1-F(P,Q)^{2}}.

(4)

Finally, following
[
17
, Sect. 3]
we use a metric on a modified output set that comes out naturally for product distributions, and will allow us to work in a Euclidean space (enabling the use of the Minkowski dimensions which already appeared in Subsection
I-A
above, and are defined below). For a probability distribution
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{L})
, define the unit vector
\sqrt{P}:=(\sqrt{P(\ell)}:\ell\in\mathcal{L})\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{L}}
. The image of
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{L})
under the square root map is the non-negative orthant sector of the unit hypersphere in
\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{L}|}
, which we shall denote
S_{+}(|\mathcal{L}|,1)
.
As proven in
[
17
]
, these objects obey the relation

1-F(P,Q)^{2}\leq\left|\sqrt{P}-\sqrt{Q}\right|_{2}^{2}\leq 2\left(1-F(P,Q)^{2}\right).

(5)

For a channel
W:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
, we denote the output probability set
\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}:=W(\mathcal{X})\subset\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})
, with
W_{x}
the output distribution conditioned on an input
x
as described in Definition
1
. Hence, we have

\sqrt{\!\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}}=\left\{\sqrt{W_{x}}:x\in\mathcal{X}\right\}\subset S_{+}(|\mathcal{Y}|,1).

For the code construction, the
(entropy) conditional typical set
in the output is used as the identification test
\mathcal{E}_{j}:=\mathcal{T}_{u_{j}}^{\delta}\subset\mathcal{Y}^{n}
of the code word
u_{j}
. For each
x^{n}\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
it is defined as

\mathcal{T}_{x^{n}}^{\delta}\!:=\left\{y^{n}\in\mathcal{Y}^{n}\!:\left|\log W_{x^{n}}(y^{n})+H(W_{x^{n}})\right|\leq\delta\sqrt{n}\right\}.

(6)

The nice properties exhibited by typical sets (they collect almost all the probability, which is almost evenly distributed among their elements, cf.
[
21
, Lemmas I.11 & I.12]
) allow us to bound the errors of first and second kinds (when choosing
\mathcal{T}_{u_{j}}^{\delta}
as decision rule) through the following lemmas, which will be instrumental in the next section.

Lemma 2

(
[
17
, Lemma 2.1]
)
.

For an arbitrary channel
W:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
, arbitrary block length
n
,
0<\delta\leq\sqrt{n}\log|\mathcal{Y}|
, and for any
x^{n}\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
, we have

W_{x^{n}}(\mathcal{T}_{x^{n}}^{\delta})\geq 1-2\exp\left(-\delta^{2}/36K(|\mathcal{Y}|)\right),

where
K(d)=(\log\max\{d,3\})^{2}
.

Lemma 3

(
[
17
, Lemma 3.1]
)
.

Let
x^{n},{x^{\prime}}^{n}\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
be two input sequences such that the corresponding output probability distributions satisfy

1-\frac{1}{2}\left\|W_{x^{n}}-W_{{x^{\prime}}^{n}}\right\|_{1}\leq\epsilon
. Then,

\begin{split}W_{{x^{\prime}}^{n}}(\mathcal{T}_{x^{n}}^{\delta})&\leq 2\exp\left(-\delta^{2}/36K(|\mathcal{Y}|)\right)\\
&\phantom{\leq.}+\epsilon\left(1+2^{2\delta\sqrt{n}}2^{H(W_{x^{n}})-H(W_{{x^{\prime}}^{n}})}\right).\end{split}

While in classical channel coding over discrete memoryless channels it is customary to rely on the method of types, such techniques are not applicable in our more general setting, which includes continuous and quantum channels where empirical distributions (types) are not well defined.
Our employ of the entropy conditional typical sets, which could potentially result in looser bounds in the discrete case where types are available, has the advantadge of naturally extending to the general case. Furthermore, we remark that while the original proof of deterministic identification over DMCs
[
9
]
, referenced in the introduction
I-A
, does indeed use the method of types, in this work we show that our bounds can recover the same capacity result (see Example
12
), validating in some sense our typicality-based approach even in discrete classical cases.

For both the coding and converse part, we will need the packing and covering of general sets in arbitrary dimensions. For a non-empty bounded subset
F
, the
covering
problem consists in finding the minimum number
\Gamma_{\delta}(F)
of closed balls of radius
\delta
centered at points in
F
such that their union contains
F
; in contrast, the
packing
problem consists in finding the maximum number
\Pi_{\delta}(F)
of pairwise disjoint open balls of radius
\delta
centered at points in
F
.
The packing and covering numbers are not equal in general, but they exhibit the same asymptotic behaviour in the exponent as
\delta\to 0

[
22
]
. These are fundamental objects in geometry as they can be used to define the
Minkowski dimension
(a.k.a covering, Kolmogorov, or entropy dimension) of a subset in Euclidean space as

d_{M}(F)=\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\frac{\log\Gamma_{\delta}(F)}{-\log\delta}=\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\frac{\log\Pi_{\delta}(F)}{-\log\delta}.

(7)

While for smooth manifolds the topological and Minkowski dimensions coincide, the latter is especially relevant in theory of fractals, as it captures the geometric complexity of the set beyond the scope of the classical topological dimension, see the excellent textbook
[
22
]
, as well as
[
23
,
24
]
for further insight. For even more general and irregular sets, it is quite common that the above limit [Eq. (
7
)] does not exist. In that case, we define the
upper
and
lower Minkowski dimensions
through the limit superior and limit inferior, respectively:

\displaystyle\overline{d}_{M}(F)

\displaystyle:=\limsup_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\frac{\log\Gamma_{\delta}(F)}{-\log\delta}=\limsup_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\frac{\log\Pi_{\delta}(F)}{-\log\delta},

(8)

\displaystyle\underline{d}_{M}(F)

\displaystyle:=\liminf_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\frac{\log\Gamma_{\delta}(F)}{-\log\delta}=\liminf_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\frac{\log\Pi_{\delta}(F)}{-\log\delta}.

(9)

The dimensions of the image of output probability set
\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}
and its square root
\sqrt{\!\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}}
are subject to the inequalities

d_{M}(\widetilde{\mathcal{X}})\leq d_{M}\!\left(\!\sqrt{\!\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}}\right)\leq 2d_{M}(\widetilde{\mathcal{X}})
.
This is because
\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}
is a Lipschitz-continuous image of
\sqrt{\!\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}}
; and conversely,
\sqrt{\!\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}}
is a
\frac{1}{2}
-Hölder-continuous image of
\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}
, cf.
[
23
,
24
]
.

III
Bounding the rate-reliability function

We divide this section into three parts. In
III-A
, we present a lower bound on the rate-reliability function for deterministic identification, expressed in terms of the packing number of a transformed output probability set, where the radius is determined by the error exponents. This is followed by an analysis of the bound in the regime of small error exponents. Similarly, Subsection
III-B
provides the upper bound on the rate-reliability function using a covering number, along with its analysis for small error exponents. Finally, in
III-C
we analyse the rate bounds when the error exponents are restricted to certain suitable subsets in the vicinity of
0
, raising our lower bound and decreasing our upper bound in the regime of small errors, and illuminating the separate roles of the lower and upper Minkowski dimensions as the objects of merit in pessimistic and optimistic approaches respectively, beyond the capacity value (in the asymptotic limit), for which this separation was first observed in
[
17
]
.

III-A

Lower bound on the rate-reliability function (coding)

We present our lower bound in Theorem
4
, and identify the regime where it is more tight in Remark
5
. In Corollary
6
, we analyse further the regime of small error exponents. Finally,
we include an intuitive discussion on the meaning of the bounds.
From now on we will use the shorthand
c=1/36K(|\mathcal{Y}|)
.

Theorem 4
.

For any
0<t<1
, and error exponent function
E(n)>0
, there exists a DI code on block length
n
with error exponents
E_{1}(n)\geq E(n)-\frac{1}{n}
and
E_{2}(n)\geq E(n)-\frac{3}{n}
, and its rate lower-bounded

\begin{split}R(n)&\geq(1-t)\log\left[\Pi_{\sqrt[4]{\frac{6E(n)}{ct^{2}}}}\left(\!\sqrt{\!\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}}\right)\right]\\
&\phantom{\geq.}-H(t,1-t)-O\left(\frac{\log n}{n}\right),\end{split}

(10)

with
H(t,1-t)
the binary entropy of the distribution
\{t,1-t\}
.

Proof.

Inspired by the code construction in
[
17
]
, we use the typical sets of input strings as decoding elements, for which we know that Lemmas
2
and
3
give us upper bounds on the errors of first and second kind. We want to impose exponentially decreasing errors, construct a code, and then analyze the resulting rate. Looking at Lemma
2
it is immediate to see that for the error of first kind, the exponential decrease follows by letting
\delta:=\tau\sqrt{n}
, so that

\lambda_{1}\leq 2\exp(-c\tau^{2}n).

(11)

To bound the error of second kind, additional effort is required. We want to use Lemma
3
, which requires a minimum separation
1-\frac{1}{2}\|W_{u_{j}}-W_{u_{k}}\|_{1}\leq\epsilon
between any two different code words
u_{j}\neq u_{k}\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
. That is, the output probability distributions generated by code words need to form a packing with pairwise total variation distance being almost exponentially close to
1
.
Using the left inequality in Equation (
4
) first, and the multiplicativity of the fidelity under tensor products after, we have

1-\frac{1}{2}\|W_{x^{n}}-W_{{x^{\prime}}^{n}}\|_{1}\leq F\left(W_{x^{n}},W_{{x^{\prime}}^{n}}\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}F\left(W_{x_{i}},W_{x_{i}^{\prime}}\right).

Following
[
17
]
, we study the negative logarithm of the expression above. Letting
u_{j}=x^{n}=x_{1}\dots x_{n}
and
u_{k}=x^{\prime n}=x^{\prime}_{1}\dots x^{\prime}_{n}
, we have

\begin{split}-\ln\left(\!1-\frac{1}{2}\|W_{x^{n}}-W_{{x^{\prime}}^{n}}\|_{1}\!\right)\!&\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}-\ln F\left(W_{x_{i}},W_{x_{i}^{\prime}}\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}-\ln F\left(W_{x_{i}},W_{x_{i}^{\prime}}\right)^{2}\\
&\geq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}1-F\left(W_{x_{i}},W_{x_{i}^{\prime}}\right)^{2}\\
&\geq\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\sqrt{W_{x_{i}}}-\sqrt{W_{x_{i}^{\prime}}}\right|_{2}^{2}.\\
\end{split}

(12)

Where the second line follows from basic logarithm properties, the third from the leading order of the logarithm when the fidelity is close to 1, and the last line from Equation (
5
).
Notice the important simplification that this achieves: before, we had to construct and analyze a packing of full sequences of length
n
in total variation distance, and now we just need a letter-wise Euclidean packing to ensure a distance between output probability distributions exponentially close to 1. Let
\mathcal{X}_{0}
be a maximum-size Euclidean packing in
\sqrt{\!\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}}
of radius
\beta
and
\Pi_{\beta}:=\Pi_{\beta}\left(\!\sqrt{\!\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}}\right)=|\mathcal{X}_{0}|
the corresponding packing number.

Next we choose a maximum-size code
\mathcal{C}_{t}\subset\mathcal{X}_{0}^{n}
such that the letters are elements of the packing and there is a minimum Hamming distance
d_{H}(x^{n},x^{\prime n})>tn
between code words
x^{n}\neq x^{\prime n}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}
. Then, in the last sum in Equation (
12
) at least
tn
terms are
\geq 4\beta^{2}
(and could be
0
in the others), therefore

\displaystyle-\ln\left(1-\frac{1}{2}\|W_{x^{n}}-W_{{x^{\prime}}^{n}}\|_{1}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\sqrt{W_{x_{i}}}-\sqrt{W_{x_{i}^{\prime}}}\right|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq tn\beta^{2}.

Thus we can choose
\epsilon=\exp(-t\beta^{2}n)
. Using simple combinatorics, it is evident that the Hamming ball around any point in
\mathcal{X}_{0}^{n}
with radius
tn
contains at most
\leq\binom{n}{tn}\Pi_{\beta}^{tn}
elements. Therefore, any maximal code with a Hamming distance of
tn
must have at least the following number of code words, which is the ratio of the total number of elements to the size of the Hamming ball (otherwise, the code could be extended):

|\mathcal{C}_{t}|\geq\Pi_{\beta}^{n(1-t)}2^{-nH(t,1-t)}.

The Gilbert-Varshamov bound
[
25
,
26
]
provides a similar (asymptotically equivalent) result, by constructing a linear code over the prime field
\mathbb{F}_{p}
, after reducing
\mathcal{X}_{0}
to the nearest smaller prime cardinality
p\geq\frac{1}{2}|\mathcal{X}_{0}|
(Bertrand’s postulate).
It only remains to bound the entropy difference
H(W_{x^{n}})-H(W_{x^{\prime n}})
to apply Lemma
3
. As these entropies are in the interval
[0;n\log|\mathcal{Y}|]
, we can just partition the code into
S=\lceil n\log|\mathcal{Y}|\rceil
parts
\mathcal{C}_{t}^{(s)}
(
s=1,\ldots,S
) in such a way that all
j\in\mathcal{C}_{t}^{(s)}
have
H(W_{u_{j}})\in[s-1;s]
, meaning that any two
j,k\in\mathcal{C}_{s}
satisfy
|H(W_{u_{j}})-H(W_{u_{k}})|\leq 1
. We can now define
\mathcal{C}
as the largest
\mathcal{C}_{t}^{(s)}
, which by the pigeonhole principle has
|\mathcal{C}|\geq|\mathcal{C}_{t}|/\lceil n\log|\mathcal{Y}|\rceil
elements.
We thus get the rate of the code as

\begin{split}R(n)&=\frac{1}{n}{\log|\mathcal{C}|}\geq\frac{1}{n}{\log|\mathcal{C}_{t}|}-\frac{1}{n}{\log\left\lceil n\log|\mathcal{Y}|\right\rceil}\\
&\geq(1-t)\log\Pi_{\beta}-H(t,1-t)-O\left(\frac{\log n}{n}\right).\end{split}

(13)

To this code, we finally apply Lemma
3
to bound the error of second kind, and obtain

\begin{split}\lambda_{2}&\leq 2\exp(-c\tau^{2}n)+3\exp(2\tau n-t\beta^{2}n)\\
&\leq 5\exp(-c\tau^{2}n),\end{split}

where the last inequality is true if
-c\tau^{2}\geq 2\tau-t\beta^{2}
. Solving the quadratic constraint we obtain

\tau\leq\frac{-2+\sqrt{4+4ct\beta^{2}}}{2c}=\frac{\sqrt{1+ct\beta^{2}}-1}{c}.

We may assume w.l.o.g.
ct\beta^{2}\leq 1
(see the definition of
c
, the range of
t
and the meaningful range of
\beta
; otherwise our rate lower bound is trivial), hence by the concavity of the square root the above condition will hold for

\tau=\left(\sqrt{2}-1\right)t\beta^{2}
.
Notice that the two error exponents in the present construction are
E_{1}(n)\geq c\tau^{2}-\frac{1}{n}
and
E_{2}(n)\geq c\tau^{2}-\frac{3}{n}
.
Then, they have the form claimed in the theorem if we choose
E(n)=\frac{1}{6}ct^{2}\beta^{4}
. To conclude, we solve for
\beta
and substitute the solution
\beta=\sqrt[4]{\frac{6E(n)}{ct^{2}}}
into Equation (
13
).
∎

Remark 5
.

While the lower bound in Theorem
4
is well suited for small error exponents
, it becomes trivial for bigger exponents. Clearly, for
E(n)\geq 2ct^{2}/3
the packing radius
\beta\geq\sqrt{2}
is at least the maximum possible distance between any two elements in
\sqrt{\!\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}}
, so the packing number can only be 1. This results on a non-positive (trivial) lower bound on the rate in (
10
).
The reason behind this behaviour and further remarks on the regime of large error exponents can be found in Section
IV-C
.
However, for the main part of the discussion, where the small exponent regime is the relevant one as only there we will find super-linearity, this will not be a problem.

Corollary 6
.

Let the constants
\eta>0
and
0<t<1
, and small enough
E(n)>0
be given. Then, for all sufficiently large
n
, there exists a DI code with
E_{1}(n),\,E_{2}(n)\geq E(n)-\frac{3}{n}
and the following lower bound on the rate in terms of the lower Minkowski dimension:

\begin{split}R(n)&\geq\frac{1-t}{4}\left[\underline{d}_{M}\left(\!\sqrt{\!\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}}\right)-\eta\right]\log\frac{ct^{2}}{6E(n)}\\
&\phantom{\geq.}-H(t,1-t)-O\!\left(\frac{\log n}{n}\right).\end{split}

(14)

Proof.

By definition of the lower Minkowski dimension [Equation (
9
)], for any
\eta>0
there is an
E_{0}>0
such that for all
E(n)\leq E_{0}
we have

\log\left[\Pi_{\sqrt[4]{\frac{6E(n)}{ct^{2}}}}\left(\!\sqrt{\!\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}}\right)\right]\geq(\underline{d}_{M}-\eta)\log\sqrt[4]{\frac{ct^{2}}{6E(n)}}.

The proof is completed by direct substitution of the above expression into Equation (
10
) and taking the fourth square root out of the logarithm.
∎

While the bounds presented in this subsection might look a bit abstract, an important conclusion can be extracted easily. While in transmission the rate-reliability functions converge to the capacity value when the error exponent goes to 0, in deterministic identification it diverges! This behaviour was already hinted in the schematic Figure
1
, and it is clear looking at the bound in Corollary
6
, where the term
\sim\log(1/E(n))\rightarrow\infty
when
E(n)\rightarrow 0
. Maybe a bit hidden, we of course have the same effect in Theorem
4
, because the packing number of radius
\delta\propto\sqrt[4]{E(n)}\rightarrow 0
diverges to infinity.
So, we have a lower bound that diverges for arbitrary small error exponents, meaning that we can construct a code that achieves infinite linear-scale rate. The correct way to interpret this result is that the rate is not defined in the correct scaling, a faster one is needed. Indeed, we will see in Section
IV-A
that, in the regime of small error exponents, the rates scaling is linearithmic, and hence, if we define
R(n)=\frac{1}{n\log n}\log N
, the modified reliability function will converge to the (non-trivial) linearithmic capacity lower bound in
[
17
]
when
E(n)\rightarrow 0
.

III-B

Upper bound on the rate-reliability function (converse)

Following the structure of the previous section, we start presenting our general upper bound in Theorem
7
, and analyse the regime of small error exponents in Corollary
8
.

Theorem 7
.

For any DI code of block length
n
with positive error exponents,
E_{1}(n),\,E_{2}(n)\geq E(n)>0
, the rate is upper-bounded as follows:

R(n)\leq\log\left[\Gamma_{\frac{1}{2}{\sqrt{1-e^{-E(n)/2}}}}\left(\!\sqrt{\!\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}}\right)\right].

(15)

Proof.

We start by noticing that, given two different codewords
u_{j}\neq u_{k}
of any DI code, their output distributions have total variation distance bounded. Indeed, we know that there exist a subset
\mathcal{E}_{j}
, the identification test, for which the probability to accept
u_{j}
is
1-\lambda_{1}
, and
\lambda_{2}
for any other
u_{k\neq j}
. Therefore, by definition of the total variation distance as a supremum over all possible output subsets [see Equation (
3
)], it is clear that

\frac{1}{2}\|W_{u_{j}}-W_{u_{k}}\|_{1}\geq|W_{u_{j}}(\mathcal{E}_{j})-W_{u_{k}}(\mathcal{E}_{j})|\geq 1-\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}.

Thus,

1-\frac{1}{2}\|W_{u_{j}}-W_{u_{k}}\|_{1}\leq 2e^{-E(n)n}.

Using the relations between fidelity and total variation distance in Equation (
4
), we get

F(W_{u_{j}},W_{u_{k}})^{2}\leq 1-\left(1-2e^{-E(n)n}\right)^{2}<4e^{-E(n)n}.

(16)

This is the maximum possible fidelity between the output distributions of any two code words of a good DI code. Now, our strategy consists in creating a covering such that elements in the same ball have a fidelity higher than the limit stipulated by Equation (
16
). In other words, we will ensure that elements in the same ball cannot be well-distinguishable code words, so that only one code word can be on each ball. Upper-bounding the number of code words (and therefore the rate) is then reduced to upper-bounding the cardinality of the covering.

We start with an Euclidean
\frac{\delta}{2}
-covering
\mathcal{X}_{0}
of the square root output probability set
\sqrt{\!\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}}
which has
|\mathcal{X}_{0}|=\Gamma_{\delta/2}\!\left(\!\sqrt{\!\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}}\right)=:\Gamma_{\delta/2}
elements, meaning that for every
x\in\mathcal{X}
there is a
\xi\in\mathcal{X}_{0}
such that

\left|\sqrt{W_{x}}-\sqrt{W_{\xi}}\right|_{2}\leq\frac{\delta}{2}.

Hence, our input set
\mathcal{X}
is partitioned according to the
\frac{\delta}{2}
-balls around each
\sqrt{W_{\xi}}
with
\xi\in\mathcal{X}_{0}
. That is, to each ball
\ell
corresponds a set of the input sequences
\mathcal{X}_{\ell}
that generate only probability distributions in that ball. Meaning that
for any two
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}_{\ell}
we have
|\sqrt{W_{x}}-\sqrt{W_{x^{\prime}}}|_{2}\leq\delta
.
We know from Equation (
5
) that
1-F(W_{x},W_{x^{\prime}})^{2}\leq|\sqrt{W_{x}}-\sqrt{W_{x^{\prime}}}|_{2}^{2}
. Then,

F(W_{x},W_{x^{\prime}})^{2}\geq 1-\delta^{2}.

(17)

The whole input space is partitioned as
\mathcal{X}=\dot{\bigcup}_{\ell=1}^{|\mathcal{X}_{0}|}\mathcal{X}_{\ell}
.
In block length
n
, this gives rise to the partition
\mathcal{X}^{n}=\dot{\bigcup}_{\ell^{n}}\mathcal{X}_{\ell^{n}}
, where
\mathcal{X}_{\ell^{n}}:=\mathcal{X}_{\ell_{1}}\times\dots\times\mathcal{X}_{\ell_{n}}
and
\ell^{n}\in[|\mathcal{X}_{0}|]^{n}
is the sequence that characterizes the ball for each letter. By virtue of the multiplicativity of the fidelity under tensor products and Equation (
17
), we have for all
x^{n},x^{\prime n}\in\mathcal{X}_{\ell^{n}}
,

F(W_{x^{n}},W_{x^{\prime n}})^{2}\geq(1-\delta^{2})^{n}.

If we now choose
1-\delta^{2}=e^{-E(n)/2}
, we find that as long as
e^{-E(n)n/2}\geq 4e^{-E(n)n}
(i.e.
E(n)n/2\geq\ln 4
) the fidelity between elements of the same
\mathcal{X}_{\ell^{n}}
is bigger than the maximum possible fidelity that the DI code allows. In other words, there cannot be two code words in the same
\mathcal{X}_{\ell^{n}}
. So we can upper bound the number of elements in our code by counting the cardinality of the index
[|\mathcal{X}_{0}|]^{n}
:

N\leq|\mathcal{X}_{0}|^{n}=\left(\Gamma_{\delta/2}\right)^{n}
with the chosen value
\delta=\sqrt{1-e^{-E(n)/2}}
. We get

R(n)=\frac{1}{n}\log N\leq\log\left[\Gamma_{\frac{1}{2}{\sqrt{1-e^{-E(n)/2}}}}\left(\!\sqrt{\!\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}}\right)\right],

concluding the proof.
∎

Corollary 8
.

Let
\eta>0
and a small enough
E(n)>0
be given. Then, for all sufficiently large
n
, the rate of any DI code with error exponents
E_{1}(n),\,E_{2}(n)\geq E(n)
can be upper-bounded in terms of the upper Minkowski dimension
\overline{d}_{M}
as

R(n)\leq\frac{1}{2}\left[\overline{d}_{M}\left(\!\sqrt{\!\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}}\right)+\eta\right]\log\frac{8}{E(n)}+O\left(E(n)\right).

(18)

Proof.

By the definition of the upper Minkowski dimension, for all
\eta>0
there exists an
E_{0}>0
such that for all
E(n)\leq E_{0}
it holds

\log\left[\Gamma_{\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{1-e^{-E(n)/2}}}\left(\!\sqrt{\!\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}}\right)\right]\!\leq\!\left(\overline{d}_{M}+\eta\right)\log\frac{2}{\sqrt{1-e^{-E(n)/2}}}.

(19)

For
E(n)
small (close to zero) we can expand the log as follows,

\log\frac{2}{\sqrt{1-e^{-E(n)/2}}}\leq\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{8}{E(n)}+O\left(E(n)\right).

(20)

The proof is completed by substituting the above expressions into Equation (
15
).
∎

III-C

Improved bounds for subsets of error exponents

In Corollaries
6
and
8
we have lower- and upper-bounded the rate-reliability function in terms of the lower and upper Minkowski dimensions, respectively. These bounds are universal in the sense that they hold for all error exponents
E
smaller than some threshold
E_{0}=E_{0}(\eta)
that decreases with the arbitrary parameter
\eta>0
.
We might however be interested only in certain (infinitely many) sufficiently small values of
E
. To capture this, we consider subsets
\mathcal{E}\subset(0;+\infty)
that have
0
as an accumulation point (meaning that
\mathcal{E}
contains sequences converging to
0
).

Let us start with a subset
\mathcal{E}_{g}
such that

\lim_{\mathcal{E}_{g}\ni E\rightarrow 0}\frac{\log\left[\Pi_{\sqrt[4]{6E/ct^{2}}}\left(\!\sqrt{\!\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}}\right)\right]}{-\log\sqrt[4]{6E/ct^{2}}}=\overline{d}_{M}\!\left(\!\sqrt{\!\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}}\right).

This is a “good” subset of error exponents, in the sense that it implies the convergence of
(\log\Pi_{\delta})/(-\log\delta)
to the best possible value (the limsup) as
\delta\rightarrow 0
. Furthermore, due to the properties of
\mathcal{E}_{g}
, given any
\eta>0
and its threshold
E_{0}>0
, there exist values
\mathcal{E}_{g}\ni E\leq E_{0}
, and for those we have

\log\left[\Pi_{\sqrt[4]{\frac{6E}{ct^{2}}}}\left(\!\sqrt{\!\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}}\right)\right]\geq\frac{1}{4}(\overline{d}_{M}-\eta)\log\frac{ct^{2}}{6E}.

(21)

Substituting the above expression into Equation (
10
), we obtain an improved achievability bound in Corollary
6
, but now with the upper Minkowski dimension and under the added condition that the error exponents
E(n)\in\mathcal{E}_{g}
.

We could similarly choose a “bad” subset
\mathcal{E}_{b}\subset(0;+\infty)
such that the covering numbers converge to the lower Minkowski dimension:

\lim_{\mathcal{E}_{b}\ni E\rightarrow 0}\frac{\log\left[\Gamma_{\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{1-e^{-E/2}}}\left(\!\sqrt{\!\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}}\right)\right]}{-\log\left(\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{1-e^{-E/2}}\right)}=\underline{d}_{M}\!\left(\!\sqrt{\!\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}}\right).

As before, for all
\eta>0
and its associated threshold
E_{0}
, there exist values
\mathcal{E}_{b}\ni E\leq E_{0}
, for all of which

\log\left[\Gamma_{\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{1-e^{-E/2}}}\left(\!\sqrt{\!\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}}\right)\right]\leq\frac{1}{2}(\underline{d}_{M}+\eta)\log\frac{8}{E}+O(E),

(22)

where we have followed the last steps in the proof of Corollary
8
, using again the expansion in Equation (
20
). Plugging the above expression into Equation (
15
), we obtain an improved upper bound on the rate-reliability function in Corollary
8
, but now with the lower Minkowski dimension and under the added condition that the error exponents
E(n)\in\mathcal{E}_{b}
.
These improved bounds for good and bad subsets of error exponents will be realted to the optimistic and pessimistic capacity approaches respectively in the next section, specifically in
IV-A
.

IV
Discussion: constant vs vanishing exponents, dimension zero, and large exponent regime

In this section, we demonstrate how the new reliability framework developed in this study can yield the established results in
[
17
]
.
We begin by deriving bounds for the rate-reliability functions in the asymptotic regime (
n\rightarrow\infty
), showing how these bounds allow us to recover the main established capacity results under some particular error conditions. Next, we consider the special case where the Minkowski dimension of the output probability set is zero—a scenario where Corollaries
6
and
8
do not fully capture the underlying behaviour meaningfully. We illustrate an approach to handle such cases with a couple of straightforward examples. Finally, in
IV-C
, we discuss the regime of large error exponents which, as we have already hinted in
5
, is not bounded meaningfully through the presented Theorems.

IV-A

From linear to linearithmic DI rates

The results from the preceding section tell us that the superlinear rates which, as discussed in the introduction, are a defining characteristic of general DI codes, are lost when imposing exponentially vanishing errors. Indeed, in the asymptotic limit
n\rightarrow\infty
, let
E=\min\{E_{1},E_{2},E_{0}(\eta)\}>0
, then Corollary
8
shows that for every
\eta>0
:

\overline{R}(E_{1},E_{2})\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\overline{d}_{M}+\eta\right)\log\frac{8}{E}+O\left(E\right)<\infty.

(23)

This is because we can take the limit in Equation (
18
) for every parameter
\eta>0
, as long as
E
is below the cutoff exponent
E_{0}(\eta)
.

Conversely, and similarly, for every
\eta>0
and
0<t<1
, Corollary
6
implies that in the asymptotic limit and for all
E_{1},\,E_{2}\leq E
:

R(E_{1},E_{2})\geq\frac{1}{4}(1\!-\!t)(\underline{d}_{M}-\eta)\log\frac{ct^{2}}{6E}-H(t,1\!-\!t),

(24)

which is positive for sufficiently small
E>0
. Indeed, Equations (
23
) and (
24
) tell us that the linear-scale rate for DI is a bounded positive number under exponentially vanishing errors (the condition under which the previous Section
III
is developed) with
E>0
.

At the same time, however, we notice that the DI rates increase when we lower
E
. Actually, both Equations (
23
) and (
24
) diverge to infinity as
E\rightarrow 0
! Meaning that the rate scaling is faster than linear in this regime. As a matter of fact, we show next that the general linearithmic scaling of DI codes featured in
[
17
]
does reappear when we consider (slowly) vanishing error exponents.

Let
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}E_{1}(n)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}E_{2}(n)=0
.
Evidently, we still need small errors
\lambda_{1},\,\lambda_{2}\ll 1
as
n\rightarrow\infty
to have a good code, so the
nE_{1,2}(n)
must remain bounded away from
0
; more precisely, we need
E_{1}(n),\,E_{2}(n)\geq\omega(1/n)
for the coding part and weak converse, and
E_{1}(n),\,E_{2}(n)\geq\Omega(1/n)
for the strong converse. By direct substitution of these two “settings” into the results above we can recover the results from
[
17
]
. Before proving it, we point out that those results are bounds on the linearithmic capacity (the maximum asymptotic rate), which we can recover as follows in the present notation:

\dot{C}_{\text{DI}}(W)=\sup_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{E_i(n)\rightarrow 0,}{nE_i(n)\rightarrow\infty}}\left(\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{\log n}\max_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\text{errors }\lambda_i\text{ s.t.}}{\log\lambda_i \leq-nE_i(n)}}R(n)\right),

where the inner maximum is over all DI codes with errors of first and second kind bounded as
\lambda_{1}\leq 2^{-nE_{1}(n)}
and
\lambda_{2}\leq 2^{-nE_{2}(n)}
, and the outer supremum is over functional dependencies of the exponents on
n
going to
0
sufficiently slowly:
\omega(1/n)\leq E_{1,2}(n)\leq o(1)
.

Theorem 9

(Cf.
[
17
, Thm. 5.7]
)
.

The linearithmic DI capacity
\dot{C}_{\text{DI}}(W)
for the channel
W:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
is bounded in terms of the lower Minkowski dimension of the set
\sqrt{\!\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}}
as follows (for slowly vanishing error exponents
\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}E_{i}(n)=0
):

\frac{1}{4}\underline{d}_{M}\!\left(\!\sqrt{\!\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}}\right)\leq\dot{C}_{\text{DI}}(W)\leq\frac{1}{2}\underline{d}_{M}\!\left(\!\sqrt{\!\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}}\right).

Proof.

We start with the coding part taking
E_{1}(n)=E_{2}(n)=\frac{\log n}{n}
in Equation (
14
), from Corollary
6
:

\begin{split}R(n)&\geq\frac{1-t}{4}\left[\underline{d}_{M}\left(\!\sqrt{\!\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}}\right)-\eta\right]\log\frac{ct^{2}n}{6\log n}\\
&\phantom{\geq.}-H(t,1\!-\!t)-O\!\left(\frac{\log n}{n}\right).\end{split}

We can maximize this rate choosing the parameter
t=t(n)>0
going to zero slowly enough for increasing
n
, such that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log(t^{2}n/\log n)}{\log n}=1
(we can for example choose
t=1/\log n
), and
\eta=\eta(n)>0
also going to zero as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Then, when taking the limit of large
n
we find

\dot{C}_{\text{DI}}(W)\geq\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{\log n}R(n)\geq\frac{1}{4}\underline{d}_{M}\left(\!\sqrt{\!\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}}\right),

completing the direct part.

Similarly, for the upper bound we need to take
\mathcal{E}_{b}\ni E_{1}(n)=E_{2}(n)\geq\frac{C}{n}
in Equation (
22
), the (pessimistic) upper bound for bad sequences of errors. Choosing also
\eta=\eta(n)
going slowly enough towards
0
for increasing
n
, such that the error exponents, which are upper bounded by the threshold
E_{0}\!\left(\eta(n)\right)
, can also go to
0
slowly, we find

\begin{split}\dot{C}_{\text{DI}}(W)&\leq\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{2\log n}\underline{d}_{M}\left(\!\sqrt{\!\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}}\right)\log\frac{8n}{C}+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\underline{d}_{M}\left(\!\sqrt{\!\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}}\right),\end{split}

and we are done.
∎

The capacity
\dot{C}_{\text{DI}}
has to be approach by all sufficiently large values of
n
, i.e. we take the lowest convergence value of the limit (the inferior limit), this is why it is commonly referred to as a
pessimistic capacity
. It might be possible to find a subsequence of block lengths
n_{k}\rightarrow\infty
for which the rate converges to a higher number. This leads to the definition of the
optimistic capacity
, which provides information about the best achievable rates (though only for specific block lengths) through the superior limit:

\dot{C}_{\text{DI}}^{\text{opt}}(W)=\sup_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{E_i(n)\rightarrow 0,}{nE_i(n)\rightarrow\infty}}\left(\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{\log n}\max_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\text{errors }\lambda_i\text{ s.t.}}{\log\lambda_i \leq-nE_i(n)}}R(n)\right),

with the maximum inside and the supremum outside ranging over the same objects as in the formula for the pessimistic capacity.

We refer the reader to
[
17
, Sect. 7.1]
for an extensive discussion on the origins, comparison, and interpretation of the optimistic and pessimistic capacities, or else
[
19
]
which inspired it.
We can recover the general superexponential optimistic capacity bounds in
[
17
]
with similar methods as in the previous theorem.

Theorem 10

(Cf.
[
17
, Thm. 5.9]
)
.

The optimistic linearithmic DI capacity
\dot{C}_{\text{DI}}^{\text{opt}}(W)
of a channel
W:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
is bounded in terms of the upper Minkowski dimension of
\sqrt{\!\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}}
as follows (for slowly vanishing error exponents
\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}E_{i}(n)=0
):

\frac{1}{4}\overline{d}_{M}\!\left(\!\sqrt{\!\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}}\right)\leq\dot{C}_{\text{DI}}^{\text{opt}}(W)\leq\frac{1}{2}\overline{d}_{M}\!\left(\!\sqrt{\!\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}}\right).

Proof.

We follow the same steps as in the previous proof. For the direct part we need to take
\mathcal{E}_{g}\ni E_{1}(n),\,E_{2}(n)\geq\frac{C}{n}
in the (optimistic) lower bound for good sequences of error exponents [Equation (
21
)], and similarly choose
t=t(n)
and
\eta=\eta(n)
going to
0
simultaneously and sufficiently slowly as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Then,

\begin{split}\dot{C}_{\text{DI}}^{\text{opt}}(W)&\geq\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{4\log n}\left[\overline{d}_{M}\!\left(\!\sqrt{\!\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}}\right)-\eta(n)\right]\log\frac{ct^{2}n}{6C}\\
&=\frac{1}{4}\overline{d}_{M}\!\left(\!\sqrt{\!\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}}\right).\end{split}

For the converse can we take
E_{1}(n)=E_{2}(n)=\frac{\log n}{n}
in Equation (
18
) and
\eta=\eta(n)
going to zero slowly enough, and find

\begin{split}\dot{C}_{\text{DI}}^{\text{opt}}(W)&\leq\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{2\log n}\left[\overline{d}_{M}\!\left(\!\sqrt{\!\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}}\right)+\eta(n)\right]\log\frac{8n}{\log n}\\
&\phantom{\leq.}+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\overline{d}_{M}\!\left(\!\sqrt{\!\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}}\right),\end{split}

completing the proof.
∎

In conclusion, while for exponentially fast vanishing errors the superlinear nature of DI is lost, when imposing a very slow decrease of the errors in block length we can recover the linearithmic capacity bounds in
[

]
from the rate-reliability bounds derived in Section

. This shows that, despite the slightly superlinear nature of the optimum rates being a general feature of DI, it is also very sensitive to the particular error regimes.

IV-B

Zero-dimensional output probability sets

In Corollaries
6
and
8
, and in Section
III-C
, we have demonstrated how to bound the rate
R(n)
of a code with small error exponents using the lower and upper Minkowski dimensions featured in the main pessimistic and optimistic capacity results of
[
17
]
.
This could cause some confusion on how to approach channels for which the output probability set (and, equivalently, its square-root) have Minkowski dimension
d_{M}\!\left(\!\sqrt{\!\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}}\right)=0
, particularly in the regime of small error exponents, where the aforementioned corollaries apply. Indeed, in such a case, the lower bound given in Corollary
6
becomes trivial, and the upper bound only tells us that
R(n)\leq o\left(\log\frac{1}{E(n)}\right)
for small
E(n)\leq E_{1}(n),\,E_{2}(n)
.
However, we can obtain much more meaningful information if we go back to Theorems
4
and
7
(for the lower and upper bounds respectively) and impose the regime of small errors only at an appropriate point.

Let us demonstrate this through two examples. To state the first, we recall the definition of the
Bernoulli channel

B:[0;1]\rightarrow\{0,1\}
on input
x\in[0;1]
(a real number), which outputs a binary variable distributed according to the Bernoulli distribution
B_{x}
with parameter
x
:

B(y|x)=B_{x}(y)=xy+(1-x)(1-y)=\begin{cases}x&\text{for }y=1,\\
1-x&\text{for }y=0.\end{cases}

Example 11

(Cf.
[
17
, Subsect. 5.4]
)
.

For a real number
a>1
, let
\mathcal{X}_{a}:=\{0\}\cup\{a^{-k}:0\leq k\in\mathds{N}_{0}\}
be a set of numbers in the interval
[0,1]
.
Then, the maximum rate
R(n)
of a DI code for
B|_{\mathcal{X}_{a}}
, the Bernoulli channel restricted to inputs from
\mathcal{X}_{a}
, for small and equal error exponents
E(n):=E_{1}(n)=E_{2}(n)>0
, is bounded as follows:

\log\log\frac{1}{E(n)}-O(1)\leq\max R(n)\leq\log\log\frac{1}{E(n)}+O(1).

The capacity of this channel in the suitably defined
n\log\log n
scale is thus
\mathring{C}_{\text{DI}}(\mathcal{B}|_{\mathcal{X}_{a}})=1
.

Proof.

As the set of all probability distributions with binary output
\mathcal{P}(\{0,1\})
(with total variation distance) is isometric to the interval
[0;1]
(with the usual metric on real numbers), the covering (or packing) of the output probability set of a channel with binary output, like
B|_{\mathcal{X}_{a}}
, corresponds to the covering (or packing) of its input, which is
\mathcal{X}_{a}
.
It is not hard to see that

\log_{a}\frac{a-1}{3\epsilon}\leq\Gamma_{\epsilon}(\mathcal{X}_{a})\leq\log_{a}\frac{a^{2}}{2\epsilon}.

(25)

Indeed, the upper bound results from placing an interval of length
2\epsilon
at
[0;2\epsilon]
, which covers all
k
such that
a^{-k}\leq 2\epsilon
, plus a separate interval for each smaller
k
. The lower bound comes from the realization that if the gap between two consecutive points in
\mathcal{X}_{a}
is bigger than
2\epsilon
, then they cannot be covered by the same interval. Say,
2\epsilon<3\epsilon\leq a^{1-k}-a^{-k}=(a-1)a^{-k}
, which is true for
k\leq\log\frac{a-1}{3\epsilon}
. This means that each such
a^{-k}
needs its own interval.

To prove the lower bound, we start from Theorem
4
, we use the covering/packing relation
\Pi_{\epsilon}(F)\geq\Gamma_{2\epsilon}(F)
, and apply the lower bound in Equation (
25
), noting that in this example
\sqrt{\mathcal{X}_{a}}=\mathcal{X}_{\sqrt{a}}
.
After some basic algebra we get:

\begin{split}R(n)&\geq(1-t)\log\log_{a}\frac{t\sqrt{c}(\sqrt{a}-1)^{2}}{36\sqrt{6E(n)}}\\
&\phantom{\geq.}-H(t,1-t)-O\left(\frac{\log n}{n}\right).\end{split}

To optimise the right-hand side, choose
t=\sqrt[4]{E(n)}
such that

\begin{split}R(n)&\geq\left(1-\sqrt[4]{E(n)}\right)\log\log_{a}\frac{O(1)}{\sqrt[4]{E(n)}}\\
&\phantom{===}-H\left(\sqrt[4]{E(n)},1-\sqrt[4]{E(n)}\right)-O\left(\frac{\log n}{n}\right)\\
&=\log\log\frac{1}{E(n)}-O(1).\end{split}

For the upper bound we start from Theorem
7
and use the right-hand inequality in Equation (
25
) and that
\sqrt{\mathcal{X}_{a}}=\mathcal{X}_{\sqrt{a}}
:

\begin{split}R(n)&\leq\log\left[\Gamma_{\frac{1}{2}{\sqrt{1-e^{-E(n)/2}}}}\left(\!\sqrt{\!\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}}\right)\right]\\
&\leq\log\log_{\sqrt{a}}\left(\frac{a}{\sqrt{1-e^{-E(n)/2}}}\right).\end{split}

For small error exponents
E(n)>0
we can expand the expression above as follows:

\begin{split}R(n)&\leq\log\log_{a}\frac{2a^{2}}{E(n)}+O\left(\frac{E(n)}{-\log E(n)}\right)\\
&=\log\log\frac{1}{E(n)}+O(1).\end{split}

Considering the bounds above and the arguments in Section
IV-A
, where we took
E(n)=\omega(1/n)
for the lower bound and
E(n)=\Omega(1/n)
for the upper, we conclude that the DI capacity in this case ought be defined at
n\log\log n
scale (cf.
[
17
, Subsect. 5.4]
), and that it evaluates to

\mathring{C}_{\text{DI}}(\mathcal{B}|_{\mathcal{X}_{a}})\!:=\!\!\!\!\sup_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{E_i(n)\rightarrow 0,}{nE_i(n)\rightarrow\infty}}\!\!\!\!\left(\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{\log\log n}\!\!\!\!\!\!\max_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\text{errors }\lambda_i\text{ s.t.}}{\phantom{==}\log\lambda_i \leq-nE_i(n)}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!R(n)\right)\!=\!1,

completing the proof.
∎

Example 12

(Cf.
[
9
]
)
.

Given small enough
E(n)>0
, for all sufficiently large
n
there exists a DI code with
E_{1}(n),\,E_{2}(n)\geq E(n)-\frac{3}{n}
for a DMC
W:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
with
|W(\mathcal{X})|
pairwise different output probability distributions (equivalently:
N_{\text{row}}=|W(\mathcal{X})|
distinct rows of the channel stochastic matrix) and rate lower-bounded

R(n)\!\geq\!\log|W(\mathcal{X})|-O\left(-\sqrt{E(n)}\log E(n)\right)-O\left(\frac{\log n}{n}\right).

(26)

Conversely, for any DI code with
E_{1}(n),\,E_{2}(n)\geq E(n)>0
and sufficiently large
n
,

R(n)\leq\log|W(\mathcal{X})|-O\Bigl(-E(n)\log E(n)\Bigr).

(27)

The linear capacity results for the DMC from
[
8
,
9
]
are recovered from these two bounds in the limit of vanishing exponents:
C_{\text{DI}}(W)=\log|W(\mathcal{X})|
.

Proof.

For the direct part [Equation (
26
)] we can reuse the proof of Theorem
4
with slight modifications. There, we started by defining a packing on
\sqrt{\!\mathcal{X}}
of radius
\beta
, and worked after with the discrete set of
|\mathcal{X}_{0}|
packing elements. Since now we already have a finite set of output probability distributions
\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}=W(\mathcal{X})
, we let

2\beta:=\min_{x\neq x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}\left|\sqrt{W_{x}}-\sqrt{W_{x^{\prime}}}\right|_{2},

the minimum Euclidean distance between square roots of output probability distributions, and the rest of the proof is recycled. We will create a maximum-size code in block length
n
with minimum Hamming distance between different code words
d_{H}(x^{n},x^{\prime n})\geq tn
for
0<t<1
, and partition the code into regions of similar entropy. Following the arguments, we can lower-bound the rate as [cf. Equation (
13
)]:

R(n)\geq(1-t)\log|W(\mathcal{X})|-H(t,1-t)-O\left(\frac{\log n}{n}\right),

and the error exponents are as claimed in the theorem by requiring
E(n)=\frac{1}{6}ct^{2}\beta^{4}
. Solving for
t
and substituting in the equation above we get that, for small error exponents:

R(n)\geq\log|W(\mathcal{X})|-O\left(-\sqrt{E(n)}\log E(n)\right)-O\left(\frac{\log n}{n}\right).

For the converse [Equation (
27
)] we start by noticing that Equation (
16
) translates to a minimum Hamming distance between code words.
Namely, letting
\frac{1}{\alpha}:=\displaystyle\max_{x\neq x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}F(W_{x},W_{x^{\prime}})<1
be the maximum fidelity between different output probability distributions, we have for two distinct code words
u_{j}=x^{n}
and
u_{k}={x^{\prime}}^{n}
:

\frac{1}{\alpha^{d_{H}(u_{j},u_{k})}}\leq 4e^{-nE(n)},

which implies

d_{H}(u_{j},u_{k})\geq nE(n)\log_{\alpha}e-\log_{\alpha}4.

With the sphere packing (aka Hamming) bound and in the regime of small error exponents we obtain

R(n)\leq\log|W(\mathcal{X})|-O\left(-E(n)\log E(n)\right).

The only thing left to show is that in the asymptotic limit, the rate bounds above yield the linear capacity results from
[
9
]
. Repeating the argument in Section
IV-A
we take
E(n)\geq\Omega(1/n)
for the upper bound and
E(n)\geq\omega(1/n)
for the lower, which translates into:

\begin{split}\max R(n)&\geq\log|W(\mathcal{X})|-O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\log n\right)\\
\max R(n)&\leq\log|W(\mathcal{X})|-O\left(\frac{1}{n}\log n\right).\end{split}

As both the
O(\,\cdot\,)
terms go to zero in the asymptotic limit, the linear capacity is given by

C_{\text{DI}}(W)=\!\!\!\sup_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{E_i(n)\rightarrow 0,}{nE_i(n)\rightarrow\infty}}\!\left(\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\max_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\text{errors }\lambda_i\text{ s.t.}}{\phantom{==}\log\lambda_i \leq-nE_i(n)}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!R(n)\right)=\log|W(\mathcal{X})|,

and the proof is completed.
∎

Example
12
can be generalized to DMCs with an input power constraint. Given a cost function
\phi:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
(power per symbol), an input power constraint
A
is an upper bound on the average power that admissible code words have to obey:

\phi^{n}(x^{n})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\phi(x_{t})\leq A
.
From
[
9
]
we know that the DI capacity of the DMC
W
under the power constraint
A
is

C_{\text{DI}}(W;\phi,A)=\max_{p_{X}\,:\,\mathbb{E}\phi(X)\leq A}H(X),

after purging the channel of duplicate input symbols: if
W_{x}=W_{x^{\prime}}
, and say
\phi(x^{\prime})\geq\phi(x)
, remove the input with the larger cost (
x^{\prime}
) from
\mathcal{X}
and keep the other one (
x
); in case of a tie decide arbitrarily.
Example
12
above is recovered when the power constraint is irrelevant (if
A\geq\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\phi(x)
), in which case the capacity becomes
\max_{p_{X}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})}H(X)=\log|\mathcal{X}|=\log|W(\mathcal{X})|
. With the power constraint active, and for positive error exponents, we obtain lower and upper bounds on
R(n)
as given in Equations (
26
) and (
27
), with
\log|W(\mathcal{X})|
replaced by
\max_{p_{X}\,:\,\mathbb{E}\phi(X)\leq A}H(X)
.

Despite not having a closed formula for the general case with Minkowski dimension zero, we show how to tackle such cases through the manipulation of Theorems
4
and
7
. It is possible that calculating the needed covering and packing numbers is difficult in some cases, but regardless, it is clear that we will find the rate-reliability function bounded in terms of some (sublinearly growing) function of
\log\frac{1}{E}
.

IV-C

Large error exponent regime

The bounds in Section
III
have permitted an extensive analysis of the trade-off between rate and reliability in the regime of small error exponents. But, as already mentioned in Remark
5
, our lower bound on the rate [Equation (
10
)] does not behave well for bigger error exponents. In this section we will explain the reason why this happens and show that a bypass to the problem is not at all trivial. After that, we include a discussion on what do we know about the rate-reliability functions in the regime of large error exponents.

Let us start by understanding when and why our analysis is limited to the regime of small error exponents. We have to go back to the proof of Theorem
4
where, in the second inequality of Equation (
12
), we use the bound
-\ln F^{2}\geq 1-F^{2}
, with
F
the fidelity between two output distributions. Notice that this bound is only a good approximation when the letterwise fidelity is close to 1. That is, when the probability distributions are similar, which can be related to a finer packing, so smaller error exponents. Conversely, large error exponents are related to wider packings, which result on fidelity values getting closer to
0
, making the bound
-\ln F^{2}\geq 1-F^{2}
grossly inaccurate. So, when applying this bound, we limit our analysis to small error exponents.

The reader might wonder now why it is at all necessary to apply such a problematic bound. Well, in order to apply our proof idea, we need a metric so that a packing can be defined, but the negative logarithm of the fidelity [second line in Equation (
10
)] is not a well-defined distance, as it does not satisfy the triangle inequality. This is why we use the bound
-\ln F^{2}\geq 1-F^{2}
together with the lower bound in Equation (
5
), conveniently obtaining an Euclidean metric. As we have already discussed, this step is a good approximation for the small exponent regime, which provides the framework for the superlinearity study and allows the recovery of the capacity results from
[
17
]
(which were the initial objectives of this work). However, it is increasingly inexact for bigger error exponents making the bound inadequate in the regime of large error exponents.

The lack of a lower bound able to well-approximate
-\ln F^{2}
in the range of small fidelities (for which
-\ln F^{2}
grows to infinity) and from which a genuine distance can be extracted, suggests that our proof will probably not be trivially generalised to the large error exponent regime. Different ideas are needed. However, we can make some comments on how the rate-reliability function behaves in the large exponent regime, as follows.

To start, we can upper-bound the rate for DI over any channel
W
meaningfully for arbitrary error exponents starting from a transmission code for
W
. It is clear that any transmission code without randomness on the encoder and error probability
\lambda=2^{-nE_{\text{T}}}
is also a DI code with
2^{-nE_{\text{DI}}}=\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}=\lambda
. This is because to identify any message
m
, we could use the transmission code to read the decoded message
\hat{m}
, and decide whether they are the same
m\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{=}}\hat{m}
, hence the DI errors and rate would then be equal to the error and the rate of the transmission code used. Thus, the rate-reliability function for transmission is a lower bound on the rate-reliability function for DI:
E_{\text{DI}}(R)\geq E_{\text{T}}(R)
. Furthermore, by using a sequential decoding of the
N=2^{nR}
transmission messages using an
(n,N,\lambda,\lambda)
-DI code (decoding a message by trying to identify each possible output), we can bound

2^{-nE_{\text{T}}}=\lambda\leq 2^{nR}2^{-nE_{\text{DI}}}=2^{-n(E_{\text{DI}}-R)},

which directly implies the following upper bound on the rate-reliability function for DI:
E_{\text{DI}}(R)\leq E_{\text{T}}(R)+R
.

V
Asymmetric error regimes

All our previous results study the performance of DI codes with errors vanishing exponentially fast:
\lambda_{1}=2^{-nE_{1}}
and
\lambda_{2}=2^{-nE_{2}}
. The error exponents
E_{1},E_{2}>0
are taken to be larger than some threshold
E_{1},E_{2}>E
, and this
E
is actually the defining value of the different bounds. Therefore, the codes analysed can only produce error-symmetric asymptotic achievable triples
(R,E,E)
.

This begs the natural question: what happens to the rate when the errors are asymmetric? Can we achieve superlinear codes when only one error vanishes slowly? Here we analyse the following maximally asymmetric settings:

1.

Stein regime:

\lambda_{1}<1
and
\lambda_{2}=2^{-nE_{2}}
,

2.

Sanov regime:

\lambda_{1}=2^{-nE_{1}}
and
\lambda_{2}<1
.

These two regimes mirror two well-known classical asymmetric hypothesis testing settings. In that context, the goal is to distinguish between two probability distributions (hypotheses)
P
and
Q
, defined on the same sample space
\mathcal{Y}
, using
n
independent realizations of one of them. Two errors may occur: accepting
P
(the null hypothesis) when the samples were produced by
Q
, and the converse case where
Q
is accepted (the alternative hypothesis) while the samples come from
P

[
27
]
.

Remark 13
.

In the settings described above, we refer to the two types of errors using the parameters
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
, defined explicitly in the identification context. While it may be tempting to describe these errors as “false positives” or “false negatives,” such terminology can lead to confusion, as their interpretation is not consistent across domains.

For example, in classical hypothesis testing, the null hypothesis often models a healthy patient, and the alternative an illness. A “positive” result means accepting the alternative, so a type I error is interpreted as a false positive. In identification, on the other hand, a “positive” outcome naturally refers to accepting the message under test. In this case, the first type of error (failing to identify the transmitted message) becomes a false negative, and the second (accepting an incorrect message) becomes a false positive. This reversal illustrates how informal labels, which are widely used in the literature, can obscure rather than clarify.

In the Chernoff-Stein Lemma setting, the probability
\alpha_{n}
of accepting the alternative hypothesis when the null is true is required to be upper bounded for all
n
by
\epsilon\in(0,1)
; then, the probability
\beta_{n}
of accepting the null hypothesis when the alternative is true decays exponentially with
n
, and the best achievable exponent is given by the Kullback-Leibler divergence
D(P\|Q):=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}P(y)\log[P(y)/Q(y)]
of the two distributions. Indeed,

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}-\frac{1}{n}\log\beta_{n}=D(P\|Q).

In the finite block length regime, a more refined quantity is used to characterize the tradeoff between the errors: the hypothesis testing relative entropy

D_{h}^{\epsilon}(P\|Q):=-\log\inf_{\mathcal{S}\subset\mathcal{Y}\atop P(\mathcal{S}^{c})\leq\epsilon}Q(\mathcal{S}),

where
\mathcal{S}
is a decision region. This object converges to the KL divergence for increasing
n
:

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}D_{h}^{\epsilon}(P^{\otimes n}\|Q^{\otimes n})=D(P\|Q)
for any
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, connecting with the optimum exponent in the asymptotic regime.

Clearly, if we take a good DI code in the Stein regime, the identification test
\mathcal{E}_{j}
satisfies
W_{u_{j}}(\mathcal{E}_{j}^{c})\leq\lambda_{1}
and
W_{u_{k}}(\mathcal{E}_{j})\leq\lambda_{2}
(for
k\neq j
). Hence we get immediately, by definition,

D_{h}^{\lambda_{1}}(W_{u_{j}}\|W_{u_{k}})\geq-\log\lambda_{2}=nE_{2}.

Similarly, by taking
\mathcal{E}_{k}
as the test, we get for a good DI code in the Sanov regime that
D_{h}^{\lambda_{2}}(W_{u_{j}}\|W_{u_{k}})\geq nE_{1}
. In other words, all good DI codes in the Stein (or Sanov) regime have output distributions with a minimum pairwise hypothesis testing relative entropy linear in
n
. We will use this property to prove converses for general channels in the Stein and Sanov error settings.

We are motivated to transform the entropies above into Rényi relative entropies, which are generally easier to handle, and above all additive. Given a parameter
\alpha\in(0,\infty)\setminus\{1\}
they are defined as

D_{\alpha}(P\|Q)=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}P(y)^{\alpha}Q(y)^{1-\alpha},

(28)

and connected to the hypothesis testing relative entropies, for both
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
and
\alpha>1
to be fixed later, through the following bound
[
28
]
:

D_{h}^{\epsilon}(W_{u_{j}}\|W_{u_{k}})\!\leq\!D_{\alpha}(W_{u_{j}}\|W_{u_{k}})+\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\log\left(\!\frac{1}{1-\epsilon}\!\right).

As the Rényi relative entropies are additive under product distributions, we will be able to transform terms referring to code words into sums of letter-wise objects. Indeed, given two codewords
u_{j}=x_{1}\dots x_{n}\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
and
u_{k}=x^{\prime}_{1}\dots x^{\prime}_{n}\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
,

D_{\alpha}(W_{u_{j}}\|W_{u_{k}})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}D_{\alpha}(W_{x_{i}}\|W_{x^{\prime}_{i}}).

For our converse proof, we want to use the linear lower bounds of
D_{h}^{\lambda_{1,2}}
described above, which in turn lower bound the sum of letter-wise Rényi entropies as follows:

\sum_{i=1}^{n}D_{\alpha}(W_{x_{i}}\|W_{x^{\prime}_{i}})\geq nE_{1,2}-\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\log\left(\frac{1}{1-\lambda_{2,1}}\right).

(29)

Next, we will create a partition of the code such that, any two different distributions in the subgroup violate the inequality above, meaning that each subset can only contain a single codeword. The task is therefore reduced to counting the number of subsets in the partition.

However, looking at Equation (
28
), we see that the sum can diverge if there are small values of
W_{x^{\prime}_{i}}(y)
(as this term has a negative exponent in the regime
\alpha>1
). In this case, the lower bound we want to use becomes trivial. We overcome this issue by
defining the following partition:
let
\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}_{\ell}=W(\mathcal{X}_{\ell})
be subsets of the output probability space
W(\mathcal{X})=:\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}=\dot{\bigcup}_{\ell=1}^{L}\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}_{\ell}
such that for all
\ell\in[L]
,
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}_{\ell}
, and
y\in\mathcal{Y}
:

\frac{1}{1+\delta}W_{x^{\prime}}(y)\leq W_{x}(y)\leq(1+\delta)W_{x^{\prime}}(y),

(30)

with
\delta>0
. With this, if for some
\ell\in[L]
we have a problematic
W_{x^{\prime}}(y)=0
, then all the other
x\in\mathcal{X}_{\ell}
are also
W_{x}(y)=0
, so the sum in Equation (
28
) will no longer diverge for distributions in the same partition (it will just be 0). In general, for all
x_{i},x_{i}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}_{\ell}

\begin{split}D_{\alpha}(W_{x_{i}}\|W_{x^{\prime}_{i}})&=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}W_{x^{\prime}_{i}}(y)\left(\frac{W_{x_{i}}(y)}{W_{x^{\prime}_{i}}(y)}\right)^{\alpha}\\
&\leq\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}W_{x^{\prime}_{i}}(y)\left(1+\delta\right)^{\alpha}\\
&=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}\log(1+\delta)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle!}}{{\leq}}\frac{E_{1,2}}{2},\end{split}

where the first inequality follows from the upper bound in Equation (
30
), and the last can always be true by defining the parameter
\delta>0
suitably. In block length
n
, we will have for any two sequences
x^{n},x^{\prime n}\in\mathcal{X}_{\ell^{n}}
, where
\ell^{n}=\ell_{1}\dots\ell_{n}
and
\mathcal{X}_{\ell^{n}}=\mathcal{X}_{\ell_{1}}\times\dots\times\mathcal{X}_{\ell_{n}}
,

\sum_{i=1}^{n}D_{\alpha}(W_{x_{i}}\|W_{x^{\prime}_{i}})\leq n\frac{E_{1,2}}{2}.

(31)

So, any two sequences
x^{n},x^{\prime n}\in\mathcal{X}_{\ell^{n}}
in the same partition have maximum Rényi entropy sum given by Equation (
31
), and we have seen that any two codewords need to have this sum lower bounded by
nE_{1,2}-C
, with
C
the constant last term in Equation (
29
). Therefore, for any big enough
n
(specifically for any
n>n_{0}
with
n_{0}:=-2\alpha\log(1-\lambda_{1,2})/[E_{2,1}(\alpha-1)]
) two sequences in the same partition can not be two different valid codewords. So, necessarily,
N\leq L^{n}
. It only remains to upper-bound the size of such a partition.

Before presenting the general case, let us start with the particular family of channels which have all the output probabilities bounded away from
0
by some positive constant
\omega>0
. That is, for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
y\in\mathcal{Y}
,
W(x|y)\geq\omega
. Notice that the partition defined above, is very natural for these channels, as the problematic region (when we have probabilities close to zero) simply does not appear.

Theorem 14
.

Given a channel
W
and a constant
\omega>0
such that for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
y\in\mathcal{Y}

W(x|y)\geq\omega
, any DI code
\mathcal{C}
for
W
in the Stein or Sanov regime can have at most a linear scaling rate
R(n)\leq O(1)
.

Proof.

As we have seen, we just need to upper bound the size of the partition. Given some
y\in\mathcal{Y}
and
x\in\mathcal{X}_{\ell}
, notice that the closest probability of
y
for an
x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}_{k\neq\ell}
is at most
W_{x}(y)/(1+\delta)
by virtue of Equation (
30
), and the closest
x^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{X}_{j\neq k,\ell}
is
W_{x}(y)/(1+\delta)^{2}
. We will find valid
x
values in
L
different partitions until
W_{x}(y)/(1+\delta)^{L}<\omega
, as the channel does not have probabilities that small. Then, we can upper bound the number of allowed partitions
L
with

\omega\leq\frac{1}{(1+\delta)^{L}}\,\implies\,L\leq-\frac{\log\omega}{\log(1+\delta)}:=A(\omega,\delta).

(32)

Where we have used that, for any pair
x,y
, the probability
W_{x}(y)\leq 1
in the expression on the left, and simple algebra to find the expression on the right, where we have also defined the positive constant
A(\omega,\delta)>0
. The code size is then bounded as claimed:
|\mathcal{C}|=N\leq L^{n}\leq 2^{n\log A}
, and the rate thus
R(n)=\frac{1}{n}\log N\leq\log A=O(1)
.
∎

This result shows that for channels with all possible output probabilities uniformly bounded away from
0
, the code length scaling in the Stein or Sanov error regimes (that is, when we only force one error type to go exponentially fast to zero) can only be linear. Next, we study the general case, where we find that the superlinear scaling of order
n\log n
(linearithmic) is excluded both for the Stein and Sanov error regimes.

Theorem 15
.

Any code
\mathcal{C}
in the Stein or Sanov regime for DI over an unrestricted channel
W
can have at most a rate scaling as
R(n)\leq O(\log\log n)
.

Proof.

To be able to use the proof idea of Theorem
14
, we have to somehow avoid the very small probability values
W_{x}(y)
. To this end, we
start by defining a new channel
V
approximating
W
such that for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
y\in\mathcal{Y}
,

V_{x}(y)=\left\{\frac{1}{K}W_{x}(y)\quad\text{if}\quad W_{x}(y)\geq\frac{\delta}{n|\mathcal{Y}|},\atop 0\hskip 42.67912pt\text{if}\quad W_{x}(y)<\frac{\delta}{n|\mathcal{Y}|},\right.

(33)

with
\displaystyle K=\!\!\!\!\sum_{y:W_{x}(y)\geq\frac{\delta}{n|\mathcal{Y}|}}\!\!W_{x}(y)\geq 1-\frac{\delta}{n}
a normalization constant. Here,
0<\delta<1
is a parameter to be fixed later.

This transformed channel will have different errors
\lambda_{1}^{\prime}
and
\lambda_{2}^{\prime}
which we need to control in the Stein or Sanov regime. In fact, notice that the particular condition in Equation (
33
) has been chosen such that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|W_{x^{n}}-V_{x^{n}}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|W_{x_{i}}-V_{x_{i}}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}|W_{x_{i}}(y)-V_{x_{i}}(y)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\frac{\delta}{n|\mathcal{Y}|}=\frac{\delta}{2},

where the first inequality follows from the subadditivity of the total variation distance under tensor products (triangle inequality), and the last from the bound we have chosen for our modified channel in Equation (
33
). Now, letting
u_{j}\neq u_{k}\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
be two different words from a good DI code for
W
, and
\mathcal{E}_{j}\subset\mathcal{Y}^{n}
the decision region for the word
u_{j}
, we get:

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\sup_{j\in[N]}V_{u_{j}}(\mathcal{E}_{j}^{c})\leq\sup_{j\in[N]}W_{u_{j}}(\mathcal{E}_{j}^{c})+\frac{\delta}{2}\leq\lambda_{1}+\frac{\delta}{2},

(34)

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\!\!\!\sup_{j\neq k\in[N]}\!\!\!V_{u_{j}}(\mathcal{E}_{k})\leq\!\!\!\sup_{j\neq k\in[N]}\!\!\!W_{u_{j}}(\mathcal{E}_{k})+\frac{\delta}{2}\leq\lambda_{2}+\frac{\delta}{2}.

(35)

When
\lambda_{1,2}<1
, the errors of the transformed channel
V
maintain the bounded nature
\lambda^{\prime}_{1,2}<\lambda_{1,2}+\frac{\delta}{2}<1
. However, the exponential bound
\lambda_{1,2}<2^{-nE_{1,2}}
is lost, and we have to be more careful in this regime. For this, observe the following letter-wise domination for all
x,y
:
V_{x}(y)\leq\frac{1}{K}W(x(y))
. Then,

V_{x^{n}}(y^{n})\leq\left(\frac{1}{1-\frac{\delta}{n}}\right)^{n}W_{x^{n}}(y^{n})\leq e^{2\delta}W_{x^{n}}(y^{n}),

where we have used
\delta<1
.
For any subset
\mathcal{E}\subset\mathcal{Y}^{n}
we thus find
V_{x^{n}}(\mathcal{E})\leq e^{2\delta}W_{x^{n}}(\mathcal{E})
, so

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\sup_{j\in[N]}V_{u_{j}}(\mathcal{E}_{j}^{c})\leq\sup_{j\in[N]}e^{2\delta}W_{u_{j}}(\mathcal{E}_{j}^{c})\leq e^{2\delta}\lambda_{1},

(36)

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\!\!\!\sup_{j\neq k\in[N]}\!\!\!V_{u_{j}}(\mathcal{E}_{k})\leq\!\!\!\sup_{j\neq k\in[N]}\!\!\!e^{2\delta}W_{u_{j}}(\mathcal{E}_{k})\leq e^{2\delta}\lambda_{2}.

(37)

Therefore, any DI code for
V
in the Stein error regime is a code for
W
which, by Equations (
36
) and (
35
), is also in the Stein regime. Taking Equations (
34
) and (
37
), we can argue the same for the Sanov regime.

It only remains to upper-bound the size of a code for the transformed channel
V
. Notice that, if we restrict the input to letters with output probability distributions having the same support
\mathcal{Y}_{s}\subset\mathcal{Y}
(with
s\in[S]
and
|S|\leq 2^{|\mathcal{Y}|}
), the problem effectively becomes the one in Theorem
14
, so we can reuse the partition strategy with the only difference that now the parameter
\omega
that bounds all probabilities is a function of
n
, indeed we have imposed
\omega(n)=\frac{\delta}{n|\mathcal{Y}|}
. Directly form Equation (
32
) we extract:

L\leq-\frac{\log\omega(n)}{\log(1+\delta)}=\frac{\log n-\log(\delta/|\mathcal{Y}|)}{\log(1+\delta)}=O(\log n).

We can finally bound the size of the code by
L^{n}
times the total number of supports in
\mathcal{Y}^{n}
, which is bounded by
|S|^{n}\leq 2^{n|\mathcal{Y}|}
:

N\leq L^{n}2^{n|\mathcal{Y}|}=2^{n(|\mathcal{Y}|+\log L)}\leq 2^{O(n\log\log(n))},

and therefore the rate
R(n)=\frac{1}{n}\log N\leq O(\log\log n)
, completing the proof.
∎

Remark 16
.

The last steps in the proof above may represent a huge overcount. Indeed, it is highly improbable that different codes constructed for each support can produce a reliable general code for the overall channel without losing any word (just a sequence is received, so there is no way of knowing the support of the probability distribution that produced it). However, notice that this does not affect the size of the code at leading order, as
L
scales with
n
logarithmically faster than the number of supports
|S|
.

Furthermore, however, we do not know if the bound
R\leq O(\log\log n)
is tight. The double logarithmic scaling could be an artifact of the proof. We have no code constructions for the Stein or Sanov error settings that achieve such a scaling. So it is possible that, in the general case,
R\leq O(1)
as in Theorem
14
. We leave this problem open for future investigation.

The results in this section show that having one of the two errors vanishing slowly is not enough to recover superlinearity, we actually need both of them to be slow. This shows, once more and in an even more dramatic manner, the fragility of the superlinear behaviour which, while being a defining property of DI codes, appears only in a very specific (and extreme) error setting.

Let us start studying the reliability functions for DI over cq-channels
W:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}(B)

x\in\mathcal{X}
) as input and a quantum state
W_{x}\in\mathcal{S}(B)
as output. The
n

W^{n}:\mathcal{X}^{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}(B^{n})
maps input code words
x^{n}\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
to
W_{x^{n}}=W_{x_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes W_{x_{n}}

For the direct part, we just need to define an informationally complete POVM (positive operator-valued measure) a collection of positive semidefinite operators
T=(T_{y}:y\in\mathcal{Y})
, such that
\sum_{y}T_{y}=\mathds{1}
. Then, we can form the concatenated channel
\overline{W}=\mathcal{T}\circ W
of the cq-channel
W
followed by the quantum-classical (qc)-channel
\mathcal{T}(\sigma)=\sum_{y}(\Tr\sigma T_{y})\ket{y}\bra{y}
, which effectively outputs the probability of each outcome
y
when the POVM
T
is used as measurement. Now, since that the channel
\overline{W}
can be identified with the classical channel
\overline{W}(y|x)=\Tr W_{x}T_{y}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{X}}:=\overline{W}(\mathcal{X})\subset\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})
its image of output distributions we can reduce the problem directly to the setup of Theorem
4
to get the following:

Theorem 17
.

For any
0<t<1
, and
E(n)>0
, there exists a simultaneous DI code over the cq-channel
W:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}(B)
(and its associated
\overline{W}
as above) on block length
n
with error exponents
E_{1}(n)\geq E(n)-\frac{1}{n}
and
E_{2}(n)\geq E(n)-\frac{3}{n}
, and its rate lower-bounded

\begin{split}R(n)&\geq(1-t)\log\left[\Pi_{\sqrt[4]{\frac{6E(n)}{ct^{2}}}}\left(\!\sqrt{\!\widehat{\mathcal{X}}}\right)\right]\\
&\phantom{\geq.}-H(t,1-t)-O\left(\frac{\log n}{n}\right).\qed\end{split}

With the same arguments, we can also extend Corollary
6
and the improved lower bound in Equation (
21
) to cq-channels obtaining similar results, albeit changing the lower (for the corollary) or upper (for the improvement) Minkowski dimensions of
\sqrt{\!\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}}
to
d_{M}\!\left(\!\sqrt{\!\widehat{\mathcal{X}}}\right)
. Notice also that these are always rates for
simultaneous
deterministic identification because we measure the output quantum states with the fixed POVM

. See
[

for a non-simultaneous code construction which can achieve higher rates.

Finally, we can also extend the results to general quantum channels
\mathcal{N}:A\rightarrow B
with an input restriction to a subset
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathcal{S}(A)
which can be effectively reduced to a cq-channel
W:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}(B)
,
W_{x}=\mathcal{N}(x)
. The resulting code is defined as a quantum DI
\mathcal{X}
-code (see
[

, Sect. 6]
) and the corresponding lower bound on the simultaneous rate-reliability function is given, thanks to the reduction from quantum to classical-quantum channels, by Theorem

For the converse part, we can also follow closely the proof of Theorem
7
. We have to be careful though, as in the quantum setting, we can no longer use the total variation and Euclidean distances to calculate the separation between outputs. We use instead their well-known generalization to quantum states: the
trace
and the
Hilbert-Schmidt distances
, based on the Schatten-
1
and -
2
norms, respectively. Given two density matrices
\rho
and
\sigma
, these distances are related as follows
[
17

\frac{1}{2}\|\rho-\sigma\|_{1}\leq\left\|\sqrt{\rho}-\sqrt{\sigma}\right\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{2}\sqrt{\|\rho-\sigma\|_{1}},

and the Fuchs-van-de-Graaf inequalities in Equation (

[

the notation of the classical stochastic distances or fidelities between probability distributions is equivalent to the corresponding ones in the quantum setting, and we can actually follow the converse proof step by step. We fix a maximum possible fidelity between output states corresponding to code words from a good DI code, create a covering of
\sqrt{\!\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}}:=\left\{\sqrt{\rho}:\rho\in\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}\right\}\subset\mathcal{S}(B)
such that any two states in the same ball have a fidelity higher than the initial requirement, and finally count the cardinality of such a covering. We obtain:

Theorem 18

For any DI code over the cq-channel
W:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}(B)
on block length
n
with positive error exponents,
E_{1}(n),\,E_{2}(n)\geq E(n)>0

R(n)\leq\log\left[\Gamma_{\frac{1}{2}{\sqrt{1-e^{-E(n)/2}}}}\left(\!\sqrt{\!\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}}\right)\right].\qed

Corollary
8
and the improved bound in Equation (
22
) are similarly extended to the quantum setting using again the reduction from general quantum channels with an input state restriction
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathcal{S}(A)
to cq-channels.

The main objective of this (rather schematic) section is to show that all the results in the classical part of this work extend to the quantum setting of cq-channels and fully quantum channels restricted to product states in a similar way as the classical capacity results from
[

extended to the same quantum setting in
[

VII
Conclusions

We have initiated the study of the rate-reliability tradeoff between the message length and the exponents of the two error probabilities occurring in DI. The main finding is that, unlike in the communication problem, imposing exponentially (in the block length
n
) small errors of first and second kind makes the slightly superlinear scaling of the message length disappear, and replaces it with a “regular” linear-scale rate. Even in the case where only one of the two errors vanishes exponentially, superlinearity is lost. We do not have exact formulas for the rate-reliability function, whose derivation remains as the main open question for further research. But our results are strong enough to show that for sufficiently small error exponents a universal behaviour manifests itself: the rate is essentially proportional to the Minkowski dimension of the set
\sqrt{\!\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}}
(up to a factor between
\frac{1}{4}
and
\frac{1}{2}
) multiplied by the logarithm of the inverse exponent.
While our analysis is rooted in typicality arguments, which are more naturally extendable to continuous and quantum settings, we also demonstrate that it recovers known results traditionally derived using the method of types (see Example
12
).

Technically, we derive upper and lower bounds on the DI rate for fixed reliability exponents and at finite block length. While the proofs are mainly inspired by those in
[
17
]
, the analysis in the non-asymptotic regime allows us to separate the information theoretic from the geometric aspects of the problem, resulting in the bounding of the DI rate in terms of error-exponent-dependent packings (for the lower bound, Theorem
4
) and coverings (for the upper bound, Theorem
7
). The geometric inspection of the bounds in the regime of small errors yields the appearance of the lower and upper Minkowski dimensions featuring in the main results of
[
17
]
. There, these two aspects appeared intertwined, as the covering and packing radii were made to directly depend on the block length (which is treated asymptotically), perhaps obscuring the main ideas behind the proof.
The finite block length approach started in this paper, brings this still very abstract but exciting theory closer to potential implementation.

We show how to approach the special case of channels with a vanishing Minkowski dimension of the output probability set through a couple of examples, and extend the reliability results to classical-quantum channels and quantum channels under some input restriction.
We also approach the case of big error exponents for which our lower bound becomes trivial, and comment on some characteristics that the reliability curve must have in that regime, leaving a more thorough study for future work.
These sections complete our first reliability study of deterministic identification making it a parallel to the capacity study
[
17
]
, in the sense that every capacity result there can now be obtained through the reliability results in this paper, in particular error regimes.

The present results explain somewhat the origin of the surprising superlinear performance characteristic of deterministic identification: here we see that it has its roots in a sufficiently slow convergence of both errors to zero. By decoupling the error exponents from the block length, we discover that they determine the geometric scale at which the packing and covering properties of
\sqrt{\!\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}}
determine the (linear) DI rate. Thus, the Minkowski dimension emerges in the regime of small error exponents.

As already mentioned, probably, the biggest open problem is closing the gap between our upper and lower bounds on the rate-reliability functions and capacity results. Indeed, our bounds could be taken to suggest that the linearithmic capacity of DI over general channel is
\dot{C}_{\text{DI}}(W)=\alpha\underline{d}_{M}\left(\!\sqrt{\!\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}}\right)
, with
\alpha\in[\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{2}]
a constant which we still need to fix. Given the apparent tightness of our upper bounds, and the uncountable different ways to construct codes, we are prone to believe that we can find code constructions that over-perform the packing ideas in this paper and in
[
17
]
. The results here, give valuable information to the design of the codes. For starters, it is evident now that we need the errors to vanish very slowly (otherwise superlinearity is lost).
