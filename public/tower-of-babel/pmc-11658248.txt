Redox homeostasis of one-carbon metabolism-dependent reprogramming is critical for RCC progression under exogenous serine/glycine-deprived conditions
Authors: Huijuan Wang, Mengzhen Fan, Sichang Liu, Mengjiao Qu, Xin Hou, Junqing Hou, Yanxin Xu, Xiaodi Shang, Chen Liu, Mingxia He, Jianzheng Gao, Jingying Chen, Xia Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSerine/glycine are critical for the growth and survival of cancer cells. Some cancer cells are more dependent on exogenous serine/glycine than endogenously synthesized serine/glycine. However, the function and underlying mechanisms of exogenous serine/glycine in renal cell carcinoma (RCC) remain unclear.MethodsWe conducted a comprehensive assessment of RCC progression under conditions of exogenous serine/glycine deprivation and explored the underlying mechanism via immunofluorescence, autophagic flux analysis, extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) and oxygen consumption rate (OCR) measurements.ResultsThe expression of the serine synthesis pathway enzymes was decreased in RCC specimens, the de novo serine synthesis pathway (SSP) was reduced in RCC. And the levels of endogenously synthesized serine/glycine were little. Yet, the exogenous serine/glycine deprivation significantly inhibited the growth of RCC cells both in vitro and in vivo, indicating that exogenous serine/glycine were important for RCC progression. Mechanistically, the deprivation of exogenous serine/glycine disrupted one-carbon metabolism and increased the ratio of NAD(P)+/NAD(P)H, resulting in the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and oxidative stress, which induced autophagic flux and enhanced lysosome membrane permeabilization (LMP), leading to the release of lysosomal cathepsins into the cytoplasm, which ultimately triggered lysosomal dependent cell death (LDCD) and inhibited the progression of RCC.ConclusionsOur results indicate that exogenous serine/glycine are critical for RCC progression by maintaining one-carbon metabolism-dependent redox homeostasis, which provides new insights for the development of dietary serine/glycine starvation-based therapeutic approaches for RCC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-024-13304-4.Keywords: Serine/glycine, Renal cell carcinoma, ROS, Lysosome membrane permeabilization, Lysosomal dependent cell death

Background

Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) is one of the most common human tumors worldwide and has an incidence approaching 400,000 cases per year [1]. RCC arises from renal tubular epithelial cells and accounts for more than 90% of all types of kidney cancer cases [2]. The incidence and mortality of kidney cancer are 434,419 and 155,702 worldwide, respectively, according to Global Cancer Statistics 2022, which approximately accounted for 2.2% and 1.6% of all cancers, respectively [3]. In recent decades, despite great progress in surgical resection, the multimodal adjuvant treatment strategies, including chemoradiotherapy, immunotherapy, and targeted drug therapy, is still unsatisfactory, particularly when metastasis occurs [4]. None of the studies showed any results indicating the effectiveness and benefits of IL-2 and INFα in metastatic RCC as adjuvant treatments [4, 5]. Targeted drug therapy has become a promising strategy for improving overall survival, however, the common side effects of targeted drugs in metastatic RCC limit their use [2]. Therefore, it is important to elucidate the molecular mechanisms of RCC neoplasia and develop new therapeutic targets and new treatment strategies.

Recent evidence suggests that RCC may be classified as a metabolic disease. Metabolic reprogramming is a key feature of RCC [6]. Metabolic reprogramming provides cancer cells with a competitive advantage in obtaining survival resources [7]. However, in addition to these advantages, modulating the metabolic pathways can limit their metabolic advantages and hinder the development of cancer cells [8], indicating that metabolic alterations may serve as effective targets for novel therapeutic interventions or biomarkers for monitoring tumor progression and prognosis. Besides the well-established “Warburg effect”, the metabolic events, such as TCA cycle, metabolism of glutamine, ATP production and redox balance, are remodel or reprogram to fulfill the energy and anabolic needs of RCC [9]. Glycolysis cannot meet the needs of cancer cells for carbon source in most cases, so other amino acids will be needed. Serine, a key nonessential amino acid, can be taken up from the extracellular microenvironment or synthesized de novo by the SSP [10]. Serine is converted into glycine, which can provide a carbon atom to enter the one-carbon cycle and contribute to many important physiological processes, including the synthesis of proteins, nucleotides and lipids, maintaining redox homeostasis, and providing substrates for methylation [11]. A large number of studies have shown that the exogenous intake of serine/glycine in tumor cells increases and that the endogenous synthesis pathway is activated, reflecting the high dependence of tumor growth on serine/glycine [12, 13]. Moreover, some studies have shown that reducing dietary serine content can slow tumor growth [14–16], and feeding mice with diets free of serine and glycine can reduce serine/glycine levels in the cell cycle and inhibit tumor growth [17]. These studies demonstrate the feasibility of dietary serine deficiency therapy. However, the role and mechanism of exogenous serine/glycine in the development of RCC are still unclear.

Therefore, in the present study, we focused on the role of exogenous serine/glycine in the progression of RCC and the potential molecular mechanisms involved. Deprivation of exogenous serine/glycine significantly inhibited the proliferation and migration of tumor cells, indicating that the development of RCC depended on the supply of exogenous serine/glycine. Furthermore, exogenous serine/glycine deprivation induced lysosomal-dependent cell death (LDCD) via excessive autophagic flux. LDCD is a lysosome membrane permeabilization (LMP)-dependent cell death pathway, which releases cathepsins into the cytoplasm to kill cells. LMP is associated with one-carbon metabolism-dependent redox stress after serine/glycine deprivation. Taken together, our results confirme that exogenous serine/glycine are critical for RCC progression via metabolic reprogramming.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and reagents

The cell culture reagents used were purchased from Gibco (USA), and chemicals used were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). RCC cells were purchased from Procell Life Science & Technology Co.,Ltd. (Wuhan, China). The F3-F10 passage cell lines after purchase were used in the study. The cells were cultured in MEM (A498, ACHN), RPMI-1640 (786-O) or McCoy’s 5 A (Caki-2) medium supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS). The cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator. For the serine and glycine deficiency experiments, the cells were cultured in complete medium (S + G+) consisting of MEM (Gibco, 21090) supplemented with 10% dialyzed FBS (Gibco, 30067334), 1 × MEM vitamin solution (Gibco, 11090), L-glutamine 2 mM (Sigma, G7513), and additional D-glucose to 25 mM (Sigma, G8769); serine and glycine deficiency (S-G-) medium corresponded to the above described medium formulation without serine and glycine; and formate supplementation (S-G + F+) medium corresponded to the above described medium formulation without serine and supplemented with formate 1 mM (Sigma, 247596).

In vivo experiments

All the animal experiments were approved by the Henan University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. All procedures were subject to the guide of the NIH for the care and use of laboratory animals. Six-week-old male BALB/c nude mice were obtained from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (China). All the animals (5 per cage) had free access to food and water, and were maintained in controlled pathogen-free IVC cages at 22 ± 2°C, with a relative humidity of 60 ± 5%, and a 12/12-h day/night cycle. To test the effects of exogenous serine/glycine on RCC cell proliferation in vivo, the nude mice were fed special diets for at least one week in advance, and 3 × 106 cells were randomly injected subcutaneously into mice (n = 5/group). They were then randomly assigned to experimental groups. The control diet contained all the essential amino acids (AAs), including serine and glycine, as part of the AA mixture. The -SG diet was the same as the control diet but was deficient in serine and glycine, which were compensated by proportionally increased levels of the other AAs to achieve the same total AA content. The -S diet was only deficient in serine, as described above. The tumor volumes were measured using digital calipers every 3 days and calculated using the following formula: tumor volume = (length × width2)/2. The animals were euthanized using carbon dioxide when the tumors reached the clinical endpoint of predetermined size or ulceration. The animals were placed in a carbon dioxide anesthesia chamber with carbon dioxide gas at a flow rate of 30–70% volume displacement per minute and increasing concentration to 100%, rendering them unconscious and ultimately leading to death. The tumors, plasma and organs were collected.

Serine and glycine assay

The plasma serine and glycine contents were measured using a serine assay kit (Abcam, ab241027, USA) and a glycine assay kit (Abcam, ab211100, USA), respectively, according to the manufacturers’ instructions.

Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining

The heart, liver, spleen, lung and kidney were collected from the mice and subjected to HE staining to evaluate the damage to these organs in special diets. Briefly, the tissues were fixed with 10% formaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, cut into 4 ~ 6 μm sections and stained with HE solution. Then, the sections were sealed with neutral gum and dried under a microscope.

Cell proliferation assay

To measure the effects of the exogenous serine/glycine on cell proliferation, cells (800 ~ 2000 cells/well depending on the cell line) were seeded in 96-well plates in their regular media on day 1 after the indicated treatments. Next day, the cells were respectively transferred to S + G+, S-G- or S-G + F + medium after being washed with PBS. In accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions, the cells were incubated with 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent in the dark for 2 ~ 4 h, and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a spectrophotometer (Beckman, USA).

Colony formation assay

The cells were seeded in 6-well plates at low density with regular media (1000 cells/well). Next day, the cells were respectively transferred to S + G+, S-G- or S-G + F + medium after being washed with PBS, and continuously cultured for 14 days. The cell colonies were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, and 1 ml of 0.1% crystal violet was added (Sigma, USA) to each well for 30 min at room temperature for visualization.

Cell migration assays

Cell migration assays were performed using transwell chambers (Millipore, USA). First, cells (4 ~ 8 × 104 cells/well depending on the cell line) were seeded into the upper chamber with serum-free medium, and experimental media (S + G+, S-G- or S-G + F+) supplemented with serum were placed in the lower chamber. After 24 h, the nonmigrating cells were discarded, and the cells on the lower membrane surface were stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution (Sigma, USA) and observed using a microscope.

Wound healing assay

A total of 2 × 105 RCC cells in regular media were seeded into 6-well plates. The cells were allowed to grow to 100% confluency and respectively transferred to S + G+, S-G- or S-G + F + medium after being washed with PBS. A scratch was made using a P200 pipette tip. Images were collected at 0, 12, and 24 h under an inverted microscope. Cell migration was analyzed using NIH ImageJ software.

Protein preparation and Western blot analysis

The cells were seeded in 6-well plates in regular media. Next day, the cells were respectively transferred to S + G+, S-G- or S-G + F + medium after being washed with PBS, and continuously cultured for 48 ~ 72 h. The cells were subsequently lysed in RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) supplemented with complete protease inhibitors (Roche). The lysates were separated by SDS‒PAGE, and the proteins were subsequently transferred to a PVDF membrane (Millipore, USA). The membranes were incubated overnight with primary antibodies. Next day, the membranes were incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies at room temperature for 2 h. Enhanced chemiluminescence (Thermo Fisher, USA) was used to visualize the target bands.

Primary antibodies: PSAT1 (20180-1-AP), SHMT2 (11099-1-AP), LAMP1 (21997-1-AP), LAMP2 (66301-1-AP), P62 (18420-1-AP), GAPDH (60004-1-lg) were purchased from Proteintech (China). PHGDH (13428), PSPH (58258), LC3 (12741), Cathepsin D (69854) were purchased from CST (USA).

Immunocytochemistry and confocal imaging

The cells were seeded in confocal dishes in regular media. Next day, the cells were transferred to S + G+, S-G- or S-G + F + medium after being washed with PBS, and continuously cultured for 24 ~ 48 h. Then, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min at room temperature, and blocked with 5% BSA in PBS for 1 h. Between each step, the cells were washed with PBS. To detect the autophagy‒lysosome pathway, the cells were double immunostained with antibodies against LC3 and LAMP1 for 1 h at room temperature, followed by incubation with the corresponding secondary antibodies. The cell nuclei were stained with DAPI. The cells were imaged by laser confocal scanning microscopy. To measure LMP, antibodies against galectin-1 and cathepsin D were used for immunostaining. The immunocytochemistry protocol was the same as that described above.

The mRFP-GFP-LC3 system

To monitor autophagic flux, cells infected with mRFP-GFP-LC3 adenovirus (Hanbio Inc. China) for 24 h were respectively transferred to S + G+, S-G- or S-G + F + medium for 48 h, and then counterstained with DAPI. Fluorescence images were taken by laser confocal scanning microscopy. The mRFP-GFP-LC3 adenovirus were used to trace the formation and degradation of autophagosomes. Because the green fluorescent protein (GFP) is sensitive to acid, GFP can be degraded by lysosomal acids where monomeric Red Fluorescent Protein (mRFP) is resistant. Weak GFP signals could indicate the fusion of autophagosomes and lysosomes to form autolysosomes. When autophagosomes fused with lysosomes, GFP fluorescence is inhibited, and only mRFP can be detected. The yellow spots (red/green merged) represent autophagosomes, and the red spots represent autolysosomes.

Transmission electron microscopy

After being cultured in different media for 48 h, the cells were fixed with TEM fixative, and pre-embedded in 1% agarose solution. After being embedded in pure EMB, the cells were cut into thin Sections. (60 ~ 80 nm). After the samples were stained with 0.2% lead citrate and 1% uranyl acetate, the images were detected by TEM (HITACHI).

ROS measurement by flow cytometry

To test cellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, the cells were cultured overnight in 6-well plates and then cultured with different media for 48 h. The cells were incubated with 10 µM 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescein-diacetate (DCFH-DA, Beyotime) working solution at 37 °C for 30 min. After washing, the labeled cells were harvested by trypsinization and resuspended in PBS. The intracellular ROS levels were measured by flow cytometry.

Seahorse metabolic flux analysis

The extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) and oxygen consumption rate (OCR) tests were performed using a Seahorse XFe24 Analyzer (Seahorse Bioscience) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were seeded in XFe24 cell culture microplates and transferred to different media and incubated for 48 h prior to analysis. For the determination of the ECAR, 10 mM glucose, 1 µM oligomycin, and 100 mM 2-deoxyglucose (DG) were sequentially added. All ECAR results were normalized to the protein contents in each well. For the determination of the OCR, 1 µM oligomycin, 1 µM FCCP, 1 µM rotenone, and 1 µM antimycin were injected into each well sequentially. All the data were viewed and analyzed using Seahorse Wave software for XF analyzers. Glycolytic activity, glycolytic capacity, and glycolytic reserve were calculated based on ECAR data obtained from the glycolysis stress tests. Basal respiration, maximal respiration, and estimated ATP production were calculated on the basis of OCR data obtained from the mitochondrial stress tests.

Metabolomics analysis

The cells were seeded into 10 mm dishes with their regular media, transferred to S + G + or S-G- medium after being washed with PBS, and continuously cultured for 48 h. To extract metabolites, 1 mL of chilled methanol: acetonitrile: water (2:2:1, v/v/v) was added to each sample (1 × 107 cells). The protocol was previously described. Briefly, the mixture was vortexed for 1 min, ultrasonicated twice at 4 °C for 30 min, incubated at − 20 °C for 60 min, and then centrifuged at 4 °C at 14,000 rcf for 20 min. Finally, the supernatant liquid phase of each sample was transferred into a new tube for LC‒MS/MS analysis. The samples were analyzed using an AB5500 QqQ mass spectrometer (AB Sciex, USA) coupled with a Waters I-class HPLC system (Waters, Ireland). Data acquisition and analysis were performed by Shanghai Applied Protein Technology Co., Ltd.

LDH release cytotoxicity assay

The assay was performed using a lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) assay kit (Thermo, USA) according to the manufacturers’ instructions. The cells were seeded into 96-well plates with regular media, transferred to S + G + or S-G- medium after being washed with PBS, and continuously cultured for 48 h. Post-treatments, the appropriate culture medium was used to measure free LDH levels. Lysis solution was then added to the cells to release all LDH, and the appropriate sample was subsequently used to measure total intracellular LDH levels. Compared with total intracellular LDH, LDH release was calculated according to the manufacturers’ instructions.

Glucose uptake assay

Glucose uptake was measured by a Glucose Uptake-Glo assay (Promega, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were cultured with different media for 48 h, and then preincubated with 2-DG. Then, stop buffer, neutralization buffer and 2-deoxyglucose-6-phosphate detection reagent (G6PDH) were added to the samples in sequence. After incubation for 60 min, luminescence was recorded using a Varioskan Flash reader (Thermo, USA).

Determination of intracellular NAD(P)+/NAD(P)H ratios

The concentrations of the pyridine nucleotides NAD(P)H and NAD(P) + were determined with an EnzyChrom™ NAD+/NADH assay kit (BioAssay Systems, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were cultured with different media for 48 h, harvested and washed twice with cold PBS. The samples were resuspended either in acid extraction buffer or in base extraction buffer (both parts of the kit) to extract oxidized pyridine nucleotides or reduced pyridine nucleotides, respectively. The concentrations of NAD(P) + and NAD(P)H in each extract were then quantified.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed with GraphPad Prism software. The results were presented as the means ± SDs or means ± SEMs. Immunofluorescence was quantified using NIH ImageJ. Significant p values were calculated using two-tailed Student’s t test between two groups and one-way ANOVA among multiple groups. Each experiment was performed in triplicate or at least two independent experiments. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Exogenous serine/glycine deprivation inhibited the growth of RCC cells both in vitro and in vivo

As a nonessential amino acid, serine can be synthesized de novo by SSP or taken up from the extracellular environment. Bioinformatics analysis of TCGA (The Cancer Genome Atlas) database revealed that the expression of the key enzymes of SSP did not increase but rather decreased (Fig. S1) in RCC specimens, suggesting that endogenous synthetic serine might not play an important role in the progression of RCC. Some cancer cells are more dependent on exogenous serine than endogenously synthesized serine, thus, we explored the function of exogenous serine in RCC in this study.

Initially, a CCK-8 assay showed that both S-G + and S-G- significantly inhibited RCC cell proliferation. Compared with S + G+, S-G- had a more pronounced effect (Fig. 1A and S2). Compared with S-G+, S + G- had little effect on cell proliferation (Fig. S2), highlighting the superior importance of serine over glycine for RCC cell proliferation. Formate supplementation can replenish a single carbon atom into the one-carbon cycle after exogenous serine/glycine deprivation. We found that the addition of formate rescued the cell proliferation inhibition caused by serine deficiency (Fig. 1A and B, Fig. S3 and S4), suggesting that serine likely supports RCC cell growth through metabolic pathways. Transwell and wound healing experiments also revealed that serine/glycine deprivation inhibited RCC cell migration (Fig. 1C and D).

To further explore the effects of exogenous serine/glycine in vivo, xenograft models with Caki-2 cells were generated after one week of feeding with -S, -SG or control diets. Analysis of the serum amino acid levels at the end of the study showed a decrease in circulating serine levels due to the -S and -SG diets (Fig. 2A) (versus 174.18 µM in the control mice, and 137.12 µM and 130.89 µM in the -SG and -S diets, respectively), whereas the circulating glycine levels decreased only on the -SG diets (Fig. 2B) (versus 188.75 µM in the control mice, and 56.57 µM and 170.5 µM, respectively, in the -SG and -S diets). The tumor volume and weight significantly decreased in the -SG and -S groups compared with those in the control group (Fig. 2D-F), indicating that the proliferation of RCC cells was more dependent on serine than glycine. The -SG diet slightly reduced the body weight (Fig. 2C). HE staining revealed no significant changes in the structure of various organs (Fig. 2G), suggesting the feasibility of this dietary intervention.

In summary, exogenous serine/glycine deprivation inhibits the proliferation and migration of RCC cells.

Exogenous serine/glycine deprivation in RCC cells induced the autophagy‒lysosome pathway and affected autophagic flux

When cells are under stress or nutrient deficiency, they form autophagosomes to encapsulate damaged organelles and cytoplasmic proteins, transporting them to lysosomes for degradation to maintain cellular homeostasis. LC3 is a hallmark protein of autophagy. Upon autophagy activation, the cytosolic form of LC3I is converted to LC3II and recruited to autophagosome membranes. The western blot results revealed a significant increase in the LC3II/I ratio in S-G- cells, accompanied by a decrease in the level of the autophagic substrate P62 (Fig. 3A). The immunofluorescence results also showed a noticeable increase in the number of LC3 puncta after extrinsic serine/glycine deprivation (Fig. 3B, Fig. S5A). The expression of lysosome-associated membrane proteins (LAMPs) was indeed significantly increased after serine/glycine deficiency (Fig. 3A), and the colocalization of LC3 and LAMP1 was also increased (Fig. 3C, Fig. S5B). In addition, the transmission electron microscopy (TEM) showed similar results, which indicated that the autophagy‒lysosome pathway was activated under serine/glycine deprivation (Fig. 3D).

For a more intuitive observation of autophagic changes, we used mRFP-GFP-LC3 adenoviruses to monitor autophagic flux and employed autophagy-lysosomal inhibitors CQ and 3-MA, respectively. CQ was used to block autolysosome formation and 3-MA was specifically used to inhibit autophagy by preventing acidic vacuoles formation. As autophagic flux increases, both yellow (mRFP and GFP) and red (mRFP only) puncta will increase. We found that the number of red puncta exceeded that of yellow puncta after serine/glycine deprivation due to the increased autophagic flux. As autophagy-lysosomal inhibitor, CQ treatment increased the number of yellow puncta, indicating that the fusion of autophagosome with lysosomes was blocked. 3-MA treatment reduced both red and yellow puncta, indicating that autophagosome formation was inhibited. (Fig. 3E).

In summary, these results indicate that RCC cells induce the autophagy‒lysosome pathway and over-activate autophagic flux after exogenous serine/glycine deprivation.

Exogenous serine/glycine deprivation induced LMP in RCC cells

Autophagy plays a dual role in cellular fate. Generally, autophagy has a positive effect on protection against cell death, but disruption of autophagy mechanisms or excessive autophagic flux may aid in cell death. Next, we wanted to explore the underlying mechanism of the excessive autophagy‒lysosome pathway on RCC progression inhibition under serine/glycine deprivation. Lysosomes act as cell recycling centers, receiving goods primarily through autophagy. The most common cell death pathway in which lysosomes are involved is known as LDCD, and its main feature is LMP. Galectin 1 present in the cytoplasm will translocate to lysosomes when the permeability of lysosomes changes, and galectin puncta assay have been proven to be a sensitive method to detect LMP. Additionally, lysosomal proteases (cathepsins) are released into the cytoplasm when LMP occurs.

We observed an increase in punctate fluorescence of galectin 1 and its colocalization with LAMP1 (Fig. 4A, Fig. S6A), along with a significant increase in the diffuse fluorescence intensity of cathepsin D after serine/glycine deprivation (Fig. 4B, Fig. S6B). These immunofluorescence results suggest an increase in LMP after exogenous serine/glycine deprivation.

To verify the immunofluorescence results, we employed membrane protein extraction kits for membrane fractionation and performed western blot experiments to detect the expression of cytoplasmic cathepsin D, which showed an increase expression of cathepsin D protein in the cytoplasm after serine/glycine deficiency (Fig. 4C), which was consistent with the immunofluorescence results.

We subsequently performed LDH assays to further investigate the toxicity of leaking on RCC cells. As shown in Fig. 4D, extrinsic serine/glycine deprivation resulted in an elevated level of LDH in RCC cells compared with that in the control group.

These findings suggest that excessive autophagy under exogenous serine/glycine deprivation may increase LMP, resulting in LDCD, which ultimately inhibits the growth of RCC cells.

Exogenous serine/glycine deprivation led to metabolic disturbance in RCC cells

Most cells can activate the SSP after exogenous serine/glycine deficiency. The upregulated protein expression of key enzymes (PHGDH, PSPH, PSAT1, and SHMT2) in the SSP after exogenous serine/glycine deprivation was shown in Fig. 5A, indicating the ability of RCC cells to activate serine biosynthesis pathway under conditions of serine/glycine limitation. However, the upregulation of these enzymes cannot sustain the normal growth of RCC cells under exogenous serine/glycine deprivation, which may be due to reduced glucose uptake (Fig. 5B). Moreover, the Seahorse glycolysis stress test demonstrated that the extracellular acidification rate (ECAR), glycolysis, glycolytic capacity and glycolytic reserve were all significantly decreased after exogenous serine/glycine deprivation (Fig. 5C, Fig. S7A). Therefore, increased glucose did not prevent the inhibition of cell proliferation by serine/glycine deficiency (Fig. 5D), due to the reduced intake of glucose (Fig. 5B), which cannot support the rapid progress of glycolysis, resulting in inhibiting the growth of RCC cells. The tumor suppressor gene VHL (von Hippel-Lindau) is an essential gene involved in regulating metabolic reprogramming. The VHL/HIF axis is the most frequently activated pathway in RCC. After exogenous serine/glycine deprivation, the inactivation of VHL/HIF axis was shown in Fig. 5E in our study. The decreased HIF1α level cannot drive the expression of several enzymes involved with glucose uptake and glycolysis, which led to RCC cell metabolic reprogramming under the deprivation of exogenous serine/glycine. The Seahorse mitochondrial stress test results revealed that the oxygen consumption (OCR) rate, the basal and maximal respiration were markedly reduced in the S-G- cells (Fig. 5F, Fig. S7B). In addition, ATP production was also decreased (Fig. 5G, Fig. S7B), which cannot meet the needs of RCC cell growth. The serine/glycine starvation and the drop of ATP activated AMPK, which inhibited mTORC1 pathway and thereby activated autophagy (Fig. S8, Fig. 3A and B).

Serine/glycine is an important one-carbon donor for one-carbon metabolism, which is crucial for sustaining cancer cell proliferation and survival. A total of 160 distinguishing metabolites were identified between S + G + and S-G- cells via untargeted LC/MS analysis, of which 18 distinguishing metabolites showed significant changes and were associated with one-carbon metabolism (Fig. 5H). In addition to some nucleotides, some metabolites related to redox homeostasis were also altered (glutathione, NAD+, etc.). One of the outputs of one-carbon metabolism is involved in maintaining the cellular redox balance. Flow cytometry analysis revealed an increase in intracellular ROS levels in S-G- cells (Fig. 5I, Fig. S9), which might be due mainly to an increase in the NAD(P)+/NAD(P)H ratio (Fig. 5J-K), which are important products of one-carbon metabolism. Collectively, these results suggest that exogenous serine/glycine deprivation leads to metabolic disturbance, disrupts cellular redox homeostasis and results in oxidative stress.

Inhibition of oxidative stress reduced serine/glycine deprivation-induced LMP in RCC cells

Previously, we reported that the serine/glycine deprivation disrupted cellular metabolism and led to the production of ROS. We next explored the effects of ROS on the progression of RCC cells. N-acetylcysteine (NAC), a widely used antioxidant, significantly reduced the intracellular ROS levels in S-G- cells (Fig. S10). The western blot results revealed that the autophagy‒lysosome pathway was clearly inhibited in NAC-pretreated cells (Fig. 6A). Immunofluorescence experiments revealed strong galectin 1 puncta and colocalization with LAMP1 in S-G- cells, while the increased colocalization of galectin 1 with LAMP1 in S-G- cells disappeared significantly after pretreatment with NAC (Fig. 6B, Fig. S11A). Similarly, NAC pretreatment also reduced the irregular cathepsin D-positive cytoplasmic aggregation in S-G- cells (Fig. 6C, Fig. S11B). Moreover, the levels of LDH in S-G- cells also decreased after pretreatment with NAC (Fig. 6D). These results indicate that excessive autophagy‒lysosome pathway activity induced by serine/glycine deprivation increases LMP, whereas inhibition of ROS reduces LMP.

Discussion

An increasing number of recent studies have highlighted the importance of serine/glycine in fueling one-carbon metabolism, which supports nucleotide synthesis and redox balance [13, 18, 19], which are crucial for cancer cell survival. Serine can be synthesized de novo by SSP or taken up from the exogenous environment. However, the function of SSP has rarely been reported in RCC. Only one study proposed PHGDH, a key enzyme of SSP, as a candidate therapeutic target to eradicate advanced or metastatic ccRCC resistant to HIF2α antagonists [20]. We found that the expression of SSP enzymes was decreased in RCC specimens, which indicated that endogenously synthesized serine/glycine were little in RCC development. However, the contribution of exogenous serine/glycine to RCC was unclear. In this study, we revealed that exogenous serine/glycine deprivation inhibited the growth of RCC cells both in vitro and in vivo. Serine was more important than glycine for RCC progression. Formate supplementation reversed this phenomenon, which suggested that the inhibition of RCC progression caused by serine/glycine deprivation might be related to serine/glycine-fueled one-carbon metabolism. The mechanisms underlying the suppressive effects of exogenous serine/glycine deprivation on RCC progression were related to LDCD caused by excessive autophagy under stress and nutrient starvation conditions.

Autophagy is a tightly coordinated process, that can enrich the contents of the double-membrane vesicles of autophagosomes and eventually fuse with lysosomes for conversion and utilization [21]. Autophagy plays dual roles in various diseases. In general, autophagy acts a protective role by maintaining cell homeostasis and ensuring cell survival during stress and nutrient deprivation [22]. Disruption of autophagy or excessive autophagic flux will promote cell death under certain conditions [22, 23], including neurodegenerative diseases, cardiomyopathy, infectious diseases, and cancers et al. [24]. In the present study, exogenous serine/glycine deprivation inhibited RCC progression, and induced the autophagy‒lysosome pathway and enhanced autophagic flux, indicating that autophagy played a destructive role in RCC under exogenous serine/glycine deprivation. Autophagy is regarded as lysosomal-dependent degradation process. When the afflux of degradation substances exceeds the lysosomal purifying capacity, the lysosomal membrane will be perturbated, resulting in LMP [25, 26]. Various lysosomal contents, including cathepsins, are released into the cytoplasm, leading to LDCD [27, 28]. Cathepsins are key players in LDCD. The main feature of this form of regulated cell death is LMP. The galectin puncta assay is considered as a sensitive method for detecting LMP [29]. Strong galectin punctate fluorescence and its colocalization with LAMP1 were detected, resulting in the translocation of the cathepsins to the cytoplasm in our study. Thus, we considered that RCC promoted LDCD under exogenous serine/glycine deprivation. LDCD in RCC has been reported in previous studies [30, 31]. Disruption of lysosomal function caused selective cytotoxicity and significantly inhibited the proliferation of VHL-inactivated ccRCC cells [32]. The apoptosis of ccRCC cells induced by SHMT2 knockdown was related to LMP [33]. However, the mechanism by which exogenous serine/glycine deficiency induces LMP in RCC needs to be further explored.

Reprogramming energy metabolism has been defined as one of ten cancer hallmarks [34]. The Agilent Seahorse XFe24 Analyzer results revealed that glycolysis and mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation were markedly reduced under exogenous serine/glycine deprivation, indicating that RCC cells underwent overall metabolic reprogramming, especially one-carbon metabolism. Serine/glycine is the major one-carbon donor for one-carbon metabolism [35], which is crucial for the growth and survival of proliferating cells through the biosynthesis of lipids, nucleotides and proteins, the maintenance of redox balance, and provision of substrate for methylation reactions. Untargated metabolomics showed that the contents of metabolites related to redox status, such as homocysteic acid, cysteine, glutathione, and NAD+, changed after serine/glycine deprivation. The ratio of NAD(P)+/NAD(P)H produced during serine/glycine metabolism is crucial for maintaining the cellular redox status [36]. Cellular metabolic disorders can affect the expression of glutathione and the ratio of NAD(P)+/NAD(P)H, leading to cellular oxidative stress and the production of ROS [37]. Our results also showed that an increased ratio of NAD(P)+/NAD(P)H after exogenous serine/glycine deficiency, resulting in excessive ROS production. Maintaining redox balance is crucial for cell proliferation, death, and energy metabolism. How did the redox imbalance caused by serine/glycine deprivation affect the progression of RCC in our study? ROS are the most important factor triggering LMP [38, 39], which is the main feature of LDCD. Unlike other organelles, such as mitochondria, lysosomes lack common antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase, catalase, or glutathione peroxidase. Therefore, when ROS levels are high, they can easily penetrate and damage the lysosome membrane [40, 41]. Our results also showed that LMP increased after serine/glycine deprivation, whereas, the addition of the antioxidant NAC affected the occurrence of autophagy, reduced LMP, and alleviated cytotoxicity.

In conclusion, we demonstrate that exogenous serine/glycine play a crucial role in the progression of RCC. Exogenous serine/glycine deprivation disrupt one-carbon metabolism, and increase the ratio of NAD(P)+/NAD(P)H, resulting in excessive production of ROS and oxidative stress. The accumulation of ROS induces a significant increase in autophagic flux, which exceeds the purifying capacity of lysosomes. Consequently, LMP is enhanced, leading to the release of lysosomal cathepsins into the cytosol, which ultimately triggers LDCD and inhibits the progression of RCC (Fig. 6E). Our work explores the role and mechanism of exogenous serine/glycine in RCC, provides new insights for the treatment of RCC and theoretical support for the development of dietary deficiency-based therapeutic approaches for RCC.
