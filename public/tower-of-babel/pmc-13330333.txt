An intelligent nano-engineered PLGA@IGF-1 hydrogel for programmed immune modulation and tendon regeneration via IGF-1R/AKT signaling
Authors: Xiaojun Yan, Yulin Zhang, Wen Zeng, Yang Li, Chenhui Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTendon repair remains a major clinical challenge, largely because current strategies often fail to simultaneously achieve early inflammatory control and subsequent matrix remodeling. To address this problem, we developed a dynamically responsive nano-hydrogel, CAPP@IGF-1, composed of 3-carboxyphenylboronic acid-modified chitosan, polyvinyl alcohol, and IGF-1-loaded PLGA microspheres, which were integrated through phenylborate ester crosslinking. The hydrogel exhibited favorable self-healing behavior together with enhanced mechanical properties, with an elastic modulus of 4.5 MPa and a toughness of 15.3 MJ/m³, enabling it to maintain local structural stability under the complex dynamic conditions of tendon repair. Under the acidic inflammatory microenvironment, CAPP@IGF-1 enabled controlled local release of IGF-1 and activated the IGF-1R/AKT signaling pathway, thereby markedly promoting macrophage reprogramming from the pro-inflammatory M1 state to the pro-reparative M2 state. This was accompanied by suppression of IL-6, IL-2, and TNF-α and upregulation of IL-10, leading to the establishment of a local immune microenvironment favorable for repair. On this basis, the hydrogel further promoted tendon stem/progenitor cell migration, proliferation, extracellular matrix remodeling, and more ordered collagen deposition. In a rabbit tendon injury model, CAPP@IGF-1 notably reduced peritendinous adhesion, and enhanced the mechanical recovery of repaired tendons. Collectively, CAPP@IGF-1 achieves stage-specific intervention during tendon healing through the combined effects of a dynamically crosslinked network, enhanced mechanical properties, and intelligent IGF-1 release. Introduction

Tendon injury remains a major clinical challenge, particularly in athletes and elderly individuals [1]. As highly specialized connective tissues linking muscle to bone, tendons are composed of densely packed and hierarchically aligned collagen fibers, a structural organization that provides remarkable mechanical strength and gliding function [2]. However, once disrupted, this highly ordered architecture is difficult to re-establish during healing [3, 4]. Together with the intrinsically limited regenerative capacity and poor vascularity of tendon tissue, tendon injury is therefore frequently associated with slow healing, disorganized extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition, scar formation, and unsatisfactory repair quality [5]. Beyond these structural constraints, increasing evidence indicates that tendon healing is also critically dependent on the local immune microenvironment, especially the regulation of inflammation during the early phase after injury. In this process, macrophages play a pivotal role [6, 7]. Early M1 macrophage activation is required for debris clearance and host defense, but excessive or persistent pro-inflammatory signaling can impair tendon stem/progenitor cell (TSPCs) function, disrupt collagen reorganization, and promote fibrotic adhesion, thereby compromising subsequent tendon remodeling [8]. Successful tendon repair therefore requires not only restoration of tissue continuity, but also temporally appropriate modulation of inflammation and the establishment of a local microenvironment favorable for ordered matrix regeneration. Current clinical strategies, including direct suturing, tendon grafting, and implantation of artificial materials, mainly focus on restoring structural continuity or providing temporary physical support [9, 10]. However, these approaches often fail to effectively address the core issue that largely determines tendon healing outcomes, particularly persistent inflammation, peritendinous adhesion, and disordered collagen remodeling. To overcome these limitations, increasing attention has been directed toward growth factor-based strategies [11, 12]. Given the critical roles of growth factors in macrophage regulation, stem cell recruitment, and ECM remodeling, localized delivery systems have become particularly attractive. Among these growth factors, insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) has emerged as a promising candidate because of its roles in early inflammatory regulation and in promoting collagen fiber differentiation and maturation [12, 13]. Nevertheless, despite its therapeutic promise in tendon repair, the direct use of IGF-1 remains limited by rapid in vivo clearance, insufficient local retention, and the difficulty of achieving a release profile that matches the sequential phases of tendon healing [14, 15]. Accordingly, how to construct a biomaterial system based on IGF-1 that can actively regulate the local repair microenvironment in response to the combined demands of localized delivery, inflammatory control, and organized tissue remodeling has become a critical scientific challenge for achieving high-quality tendon regeneration.

In recent years, hydrogels have emerged as highly attractive materials for tendon repair because of their biocompatibility, ECM mimicking characteristics, and potential as localized delivery platforms for bioactive factors [16–18]. However, conventional hydrogels still exhibit several important limitations in tendon repair applications. First, many systems lack the toughness, interfacial adhesion, and structural stability required to withstand the complex dynamic mechanical environment associated with tendon healing, making them prone to deformation, displacement, or structural failure after implantation [19]. Second, the release behavior of loaded bioactive factors is often difficult to control precisely, resulting in burst release, insufficient sustained delivery, or poor temporal matching with the biological requirements of different healing stages [20]. These shortcomings are particularly critical in tendon regeneration. Among candidate biomaterials, chitosan (CS) is particularly anticipated because of its favorable biocompatibility and intrinsic antibacterial activity, yet unmodified CS hydrogels still suffer from limited mechanical performance and insufficient stability in moist or high-stress environments [21]. Dynamic crosslinking strategies based on boronate (BA) interactions offer an effective route to overcome these drawbacks by enhancing network adaptability while imparting self-healing capability and greater structural resilience under complex biological conditions [22]. Meanwhile, PLGA microspheres provide important support for the construction of localized delivery systems owing to their tunable degradation behavior and load capacity [23, 24]. Although previous studies have explored hydrogel scaffolds or growth factor delivery systems for tendon repair [16, 18], an effective integrated strategy that can simultaneously coordinate inflammatory regulation, adhesion suppression, and ordered matrix reconstruction within a single platform remains lacking.

To address these limitations, we developed a dynamically responsive nano-hydrogel, CAPP@IGF-1, by integrating 3-carboxyphenylboronic acid-modified chitosan (CS-BA), polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), and IGF-1-loaded PLGA microspheres within a reversibly crosslinked phenylborate ester network. This design confers self-healing behavior, improved toughness and enhanced tensile strength, allowing the hydrogel to better preserve structural continuity in the mechanically environment of tendon repair. The incorporation of PLGA microspheres further enables more controlled local delivery of IGF-1, such that release is better aligned with the acidic inflammatory milieu at the injury site. Through the IGF-1R–AKT signaling axis, this system regulates macrophage polarization, restrains excessive inflammation and reduces peritendinous adhesion, while supporting TSPCs migration, ECM remodeling and ordered collagen reconstruction (Fig. 1). In this way, CAPP@IGF-1 provides a materials strategy that connects early inflammatory control with subsequent tissue rebuilding and offers translational potential for tendon repair and other regenerative settings marked by inflammatory imbalance and defective matrix restoration.

Results and discussion

Synthesis, and characterization of the CAPP@IGF-1 hydrogel

To address the complex challenges of tendon repair, a hydrogel system based on 3-carboxyphenylboric acid-modified chitosan (CS-BA) and PVA were designed and fabricated. Additionally, IGF-1-loaded PLGA microspheres (PLGA@IGF-1) were prepared using an emulsion method to achieve controlled drug release. The multifunctional hydrogel network was constructed through phenylborate ester bonds formed by crosslinking CS-BA with PVA. The synthetic pathway of CS-BA is shown in Fig. 2A (Ⅰ). Through the activation of EDC and NHS, the carboxyl group of 3-carboxyphenylboronic acid (BA) was coupled with the amino group on the chitosan (CS) chain, successfully grafting boronic acid groups onto the CS molecular backbone. Subsequently, the modified CS-BA was structurally characterized using FT-IR and ¹H NMR, with the results presented in Fig. 2B and C. FT-IR analysis revealed significant spectral changes between CS and CS-BA. In the region of 3500–3200 cm−¹, both samples exhibited the characteristic broad absorption bands of -OH and -NH groups; however, the intensity and shape of this band in CS-BA were noticeably altered, indicating partial involvement of amino groups in the coupling reaction. Additionally, in the region of 1600–1500 cm−¹, distinct absorption peaks corresponding to the benzene ring were observed, confirming the grafting of phenylboronic acid groups. Furthermore, characteristic absorption peaks associated with B-O and C-B bonds, corresponding to boronic acid groups, were observed in the region of 1200–1000 cm−¹. These spectral changes collectively confirm the successful grafting of boronic acid groups onto the CS molecular chains. Similarly, in the ¹H NMR spectrum, new characteristic peaks corresponding to benzene ring protons appeared in the region of 7.5–8.0 ppm in CS-BA, confirming the successful grafting of phenylboronic acid groups onto the CS molecular chains. In the region of 3.0–4.5 ppm, both samples exhibited characteristic signals of the chitosan sugar ring; however, CS-BA showed changes in signal intensity and chemical shifts in this region, indicating the involvement of amino groups in the coupling reaction. These spectral changes further confirm the successful conjugation of boronic acid groups to the CS molecules. Subsequently, CS-BA and PVA were successfully crosslinked through boronic ester bonds to construct a CS-BA/PVA (CAP) hydrogel network (Fig. 2A Ⅱ). FT-IR analysis revealed a distinct characteristic absorption peak at 1340 cm−¹, corresponding to B-O bonds, which was attributed to the interaction between the boronic acid groups in CS-BA and the cis-diol groups in PVA chains. This result confirmed the successful chemical crosslinking between CS-BA and PVA (Fig. 2D). SEM results revealed that the CAP hydrogel exhibited a uniform porous structure with a pore size of approximately 10–25 μm (Fig. 2E), which not only accommodates PLGA@IGF-1 microsphere incorporation, but also matches the structural scale commonly adopted in tendon-repair hydrogels [25, 26], thereby favoring cell adhesion, migration, and matrix remodeling.

To achieve optimal PLGA@IGF-1 microspheres (Fig. 2F), the concentration of IGF-1 was first evaluated. The results demonstrate that an IGF-1 concentration of 20 ng/mL markedly promoted fibroblast proliferation (Fig. S1). Meanwhile, the optimal encapsulation efficiency (EE) and loading degree (LD) of IGF-1 in PLGA microspheres were 83.76 ± 1.97% and 2.09 ± 0.13 × 10⁻⁵%, respectively (Table S2). The SEM analysis revealed that the PLGA@IGF-1 microspheres exhibited a well-defined spherical morphology, and a particle size ranging from 100 to 120 nm (Figs. 2G and H). Following tendon injury, the local inflammatory microenvironment typically undergoes remarkably changes, with one of the most prominent features being a shift in tissue pH from neutral to acidic. This alteration places higher demands on the repair capabilities of microspheres [27]. To evaluate the responsiveness and repair potential of the PLGA@IGF-1 microspheres under acidic pH conditions simulating the inflammatory microenvironment of tendon injury, we conducted an in vitro release experiment. The results demonstrated that PLGA@IGF-1 exhibited a sustained and pH-responsive release profile (Fig. 2I). Under acidic conditions (pH 5.5), IGF-1 release was markedly accelerated, showing an initial rapid-release phase within the first 7 days and reaching over 85% cumulative release by day 14. In comparison, the release rate was lower at pH 6.8 and 7.4, with cumulative release of approximately 48% and 21%, respectively, on day 14. This phenomenon is primarily attributed to the accelerated degradation of PLGA microspheres in acidic conditions, leading to the rapid release of encapsulated IGF-1. This characteristic demonstrates the excellent adaptability and targeted release performance of the PLGA@IGF-1 microspheres in inflammatory microenvironments, providing effective support for tendon repair. Furthermore, CCK-8 assay results demonstrated that PLGA@IGF-1 microspheres notably enhanced the viability of fibroblast at 48 h, indicating excellent cytocompatibility and pro-proliferative effect (Fig. 2J). This observation was corroborated by AO/EB staining, which confirmed the high viability of cells in the presence of the microspheres (Fig. 2K). Notably, both fresh IGF-1 and IGF-1 released at different time points remarkably increased TSPCs viability. The Day 7 release group showed a proliferative effect comparable to that of fresh IGF-1, while the Day 14 release group showed a slight decrease, but no significant difference compared with the fresh IGF-1 group, indicating that the released IGF-1 retained favorable biological activity (Fig. S2). Additionally, Fig. 1L illustrate the preparation process of the CAPP@IGF-1 hydrogel, which was successfully fabricated by mixing the CS-BA solution with the PVA/PLGA@IGF-1 solution.

The self-healing, rheological, mechanical properties and adhesive properties of the multifunctional CAPP@IGF-1 hydrogel

Tendon injuries are often associated with severe structural damage and a marked decline in mechanical properties. The high mechanical loads and complex biomechanical environment of tendons place stringent demands on the mechanical properties and biological adaptability of repair materials [4, 28]. Therefore, an ideal repair material must possess self-healing capability, enhanced mechanical properties, and excellent tissue adhesion properties to meet the demands of complex microenvironments. In this study, a multifunctional CAPP@IGF-1 hydrogel was designed and fabricated based on a dynamic phenylborate ester bonds crosslinking system. The hydrogel was constructed through the dynamic crosslinking of CS-BA with PVA and the incorporation of PLGA microspheres loaded with IGF-1, endowing the material with exceptional mechanical properties and biological functionality. Figure 3A illustrates the network structure of the CAPP@IGF-1 hydrogel, in which phenylborate ester bonds serve as dynamic covalent linkages capable of breaking and reforming under external forces, enabling rapid self-healing. This dynamic crosslinking mechanism significantly enhances the hydrogel’s remodeling capacity and self-repairing performance. Furthermore, Fig. 3B provides additional evidence of the hydrogel’s self-healing properties, demonstrating that separated hydrogel fragments can rapidly restore their integrity upon simple contact. Figures 3C and D demonstrate the rheological properties of the CAPP@IGF-1 hydrogel at 25 °C. When the strain was below 200%, the storage modulus (G’) was remarkably higher than the loss modulus (G’’), indicating an elasticity-dominated network with strong energy storage capacity. At a strain of 200%, partial network disruption occurred; however, upon returning to 0.1% strain, the structure rapidly recovered to its initial state. These results highlight the outstanding high-strain tolerance and rapid self-healing ability of the CAPP@IGF-1 hydrogel.

The mechanical properties of the CAPP@IGF-1 hydrogel were systematically evaluated by tensile testing. As shown in Figs. 3F and G, the CAPP@IGF-1 hydrogel exhibited notably higher elastic modulus and toughness than the CAP hydrogel, with the elastic modulus increasing from 2.3 MPa to 4.5 MPa and the toughness increasing from 6.2 MJ/m³ to 15.3 MJ/m³. These results indicate that the incorporation of PLGA@IGF-1 microspheres and the resulting reinforcement of the network structure markedly enhanced the stiffness and energy dissipation capacity of the hydrogel. Consistently, the stress–strain curves shown in Fig. 3H further demonstrated that the CAPP@IGF-1 hydrogel possessed favorable tensile deformability. Notably, the hydrogel developed in this study exhibited optimal combination between tensile strength and toughness [18, 29, 30], highlighting its potential in tendon repair related (Fig. 3I).

The adhesive properties represent a critical characteristic of tendon repair materials, playing a vital role in maintaining material stability, promoting tissue integration, and supporting ECM reconstruction [31, 32]. Therefore, we conducted a comprehensive evaluation of the adhesive properties of the CAPP@IGF-1 hydrogel. Figure 3E shows that the CAPP@IGF-1 hydrogel remains firmly adhered to the tissue surface even under large-angle bending at the joint, with no obvious sliding or detachment observed. Furthermore, lap shear tests on simulated biological tissues (porcine skin) demonstrated that the adhesive strength of the CAPP@IGF-1 hydrogel was noticeably greater than that of the CAP hydrogel lacking PLGA@IGF-1 loading (Figs. 3J and K). This enhancement in performance can be attributed to the introduction of PLGA@IGF-1, which likely increased the molecular interactions between the hydrogel and the tissue surface, such as electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and van der Waals forces. These interactions significantly improved the adhesive performance and interfacial stability of the hydrogel. Using crysectioning techniques, thin tendon samples were prepared to evaluate the adhesive performance of the CAPP@IGF-1 hydrogel on tendon tissues (Fig. 3L). The results indicated that the CAPP@IGF-1 hydrogel rapidly formed a stable adhesive interface with the tendon surface, with adhesion energy gradually increasing over time (Fig. 3M). After approximately 60 min, the adhesion energy stabilized at a maximum value of nearly 560 J/m², demonstrating outstanding adhesive strength and time-dependent behavior. Furthermore, under simulated blood conditions, the CAPP@IGF-1 hydrogel exhibited excellent adhesive performance, with adhesion energy excellently enhanced (Fig. 3N). This finding suggests that the hydrogel can rapidly and securely adhere to tendon surfaces even in blood-rich environments. This property is of critical importance in complex clinical settings. In summary, the multifunctional CAPP@IGF-1 hydrogel, designed with dynamic phenylborate ester bonds crosslinking and PLGA@IGF-1 loading, demonstrates excellent dynamic adaptability, self-healing capability, enhanced mechanical properties and efficient adhesive performance, effectively meeting the critical requirements of tendon repair.

The antibacterial properties, and biocompatibility of the multifunctional CAPP@IGF-1 hydrogel

Postoperative infection remains one of the primary challenges affecting the therapeutic outcomes of tendon repair [33, 34]. Due to the low blood supply and high mechanical load characteristics of tendon tissue, the repair site is highly susceptible to pathogen invasion, particularly by S. aureus and E. coli [35], which can lead to local inflammation, tissue necrosis, and even repair failure. CS, as promising functional biomaterial, has garnered important attention for its excellent biocompatibility and broad-spectrum antimicrobial properties [21, 36]. Therefore, this study developed a CAPP@IGF-1 hydrogel, incorporating CS as a key component of the dynamic phenylborate ester bonds crosslinked network. CS not only contributed to the hydrogel’s structural network but also remarkably enhanced its antibacterial performance. The results demonstrated that the CAPP@IGF-1 hydrogel exhibited excellent antibacterial activity against both S. aureus and E. coli (Figs. 4A and B). Compared to the control group, the CAPP@IGF-1 hydrogel markedly inhibited bacterial growth, as evidenced by an obvious reduction in colony-forming units on agar plates. Statistical analysis of bacterial viability (Figs. 4C and D) showed that the hydrogel achieved bactericidal rates exceeding 95% for both S. aureus and E. coli, confirming its broad-spectrum and highly effective antimicrobial performance. This antibacterial capability provides additional protection during tendon repair by effectively reducing the risk of postoperative infection while creating a more favorable microenvironment for tissue regeneration.

Furthermore, hydrogels with good biocompatibility are widely regarded as safe and effective materials for tissue repair and wound dressing applications [37]. In this study, the biocompatibility of the CAPP@IGF-1 hydrogel was systematically evaluated to ensure its safety for tendon repair applications. Hemolysis assay results demonstrated that the hemolysis rate of the CAPP@IGF-1 hydrogel was comparable to that of the negative control (PBS) and lower than that of the positive control (distilled water) (Fig. 4E), indicating that the hydrogel is non-hemolytic and does not cause blood-related damage. In addition, CCK8 assays showed that the cell survival rate in the CAPP@IGF-1 hydrogel-treated group remained consistently high at both 24 and 48 h, distinctly exceeding that of the control group, with a particularly pronounced difference at 48 h (p < 0.001) (Fig. 4F). AO/EB staining further confirmed the biosafety of the CAPP@IGF-1 hydrogel (Figs. 4G and S2). In summary, the CAPP@IGF-1 hydrogel effectively maintains cell viability, demonstrating excellent biocompatibility and support for cell proliferation, making it a promising candidate for tendon repair.

CAPP@IGF-1 hydrogel-mediated macrophage reprogramming and cytokine regulation

To investigate the immunomodulatory role of the CAPP@IGF-1 hydrogel during the early phase of tendon repair, we examined its effects on macrophage polarization and inflammatory cytokine expression, as these events are critical determinants of the initial healing microenvironment [6, 7]. RAW264.7 cells were first stimulated with LPS to induce a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype, followed by treatment with CAPP@IGF-1. Flow cytometry analysis showed that, compared with the RAW264.7 + LPS group, CAPP@IGF-1 markedly increased the expression of the anti-inflammatory M2 macrophage marker CD206 to 69.52% (Fig. 5A), while reducing the expression of the pro-inflammatory M1 marker CD86 to 29.6% (Fig. 5B). These results indicate that CAPP@IGF-1 effectively promotes the transition of macrophages from a pro-inflammatory to an anti-inflammatory phenotype, a process that is particularly important during the early inflammatory stage of tendon healing. To further clarify the associated cytokine changes, we analyzed the expression levels of inflammatory mediators in different groups. Compared with the RAW264.7 + LPS group, CAPP@IGF-1 significantly reduced the proportions of cells expressing the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-2, from 20.6%, 30.3%, and 75.6% to 9.59%, 18.0%, and 1.91%, respectively (Figs. 5C–E). In contrast, the proportion of cells positive for the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 increased markedly from 11.7% to 49.9% (Fig. 5F). Together, these findings demonstrate that CAPP@IGF-1 reshapes the early inflammatory microenvironment by suppressing excessive pro-inflammatory signaling while enhancing anti-inflammatory responses. Such early-stage immunomodulation is expected to alleviate inflammatory overactivation, reduce the risk of subsequent fibrotic adhesion, and create favorable conditions for the later phases of tendon regeneration and matrix remodeling.

CAPP@IGF-1 hydrogel promotes TSPCs migration, adhesion, and ECM remodeling

Because tendon healing is a sequential process, early improvement of the inflammatory microenvironment is influencing the subsequent reparative phase, particularly the functional state of TSPCs and matrix reconstruction [38]. We therefore further evaluated the regulatory effects of CAPP@IGF-1 on TSPCs migration, cytoskeletal remodeling, and ECM synthesis. Scratch assay results showed that CAPP@IGF-1 greatly promoted TSPCs migration. Specifically, the wound closure rates in the CAPP@IGF-1 group reached 49.31 ± 4.30% at 24 h and 61.67 ± 8.21% at 48 h, both of which were higher than those in the CAP and control groups (Figs. 5G–I). These results indicate that hydrogel enhances the migratory capacity of TSPCs, thereby facilitating rapid cellular recruitment to the repair site. Subsequently, phalloidin staining was performed to further analyze the effects of CAPP@IGF-1 on cell morphology and cytoskeletal remodeling. Compared with the control group, cells treated with CAPP@IGF-1 exhibited more pronounced F-actin formation and polymerization, as evidenced by clearer filamentous structures, denser distribution, and a more ordered cytoskeletal arrangement (Fig. 5J). Given that cytoskeletal organization is closely associated with cell adhesion and migration, these findings suggest that CAPP@IGF-1 promotes a cellular state that is more favorable for subsequent reparative activities. In addition, ECM-related protein expression was examined to assess the effect of the hydrogel on matrix reconstruction. Western blot analysis showed that, compared with the CAP and control groups, the CAPP@IGF-1 group upregulated the expression of collagen I (Col-I) and fibronectin 1 (FN1) (Figs. 5K–M). As the major structural component of tendon ECM, Col-I is essential for tendon matrix reconstruction, whereas FN1 plays an important role in cell–matrix interaction and matrix assembly [39, 40]. The simultaneous upregulation of these two proteins therefore indicates that CAPP@IGF-1 contributes to the establishment of a matrix environment that is more conducive to ordered tissue reconstruction. Taken together, these findings demonstrate that, following the improvement of the early inflammatory microenvironment, CAPP@IGF-1 further promotes TSPCs migration, cytoskeletal remodeling, and ECM synthesis during the subsequent reparative phase. This stage-continuous regulatory effect provides important support for the ability of the hydrogel to enhance tendon repair in vivo.

In vivo evaluation of the tendon repair potential of the CAPP@IGF-1 hydrogel

Based on the above findings, a rabbit standard tendon transection model was established (Fig. 6A) to systematically evaluate the repair-promoting effects of the CAPP@IGF-1 hydrogel. This model has been widely used as a classical disease model in tendon repair studies [16, 41, 42]. Four groups were included in this study: Sham, Control, CAP, and CAPP@IGF-1. Among them, the Control group served as the primary disease-model control for assessing treatment effects after tendon injury, whereas the Sham group served as a reference group representing the normal tissue condition and providing a baseline for comparison with the repaired tendons. After administration of the corresponding treatments, the repair sites were continuously monitored and recorded. At 8 weeks post-surgery, the CAPP@IGF-1 group showed markedly improved gross repair outcomes compared with the Control group. Specifically, the repaired tendon in the CAPP@IGF-1 group exhibited a smoother surface, tighter tissue integration, and no obvious peritendinous adhesion (Fig. 6B). In contrast, the Control and CAP groups displayed varying degrees of adhesion and tissue defects at the repair site, indicating less satisfactory healing quality than that observed in the CAPP@IGF-1 group. These observations were further supported by quantitative analyses. Compared with the Control group, the CAPP@IGF-1 group showed a reduced tendon injury area (Fig. 6E), indicating more effective closure and structural restoration of the injured region. In addition, histological evaluation demonstrated that the adhesion score in the CAPP@IGF-1 group was remarkably lower than that in the Control group (Fig. 6F), further confirming the pronounced anti-peritendinous adhesion effect of the hydrogel. H&E and Masson staining showed that the CAPP@IGF-1 group exhibited reduced inflammatory cell infiltration and more abundant, better aligned collagen fibers in the repair region, with a tissue architecture more closely resembling that of normal tendon (Figs. 6C and D) (indicated by the red arrow). In contrast, the Control group showed more prominent inflammatory features and disorganized fiber arrangement, whereas the CAP group showed an intermediate degree of improvement. These findings indicate that CAPP@IGF-1 effectively improves tendon healing by attenuating local inflammation and promoting more ordered collagen reconstruction. We further evaluated the effect of CAPP@IGF-1 on the recovery of mechanical properties in repaired tendon. The results showed that the tensile stress and maximum load of the CAPP@IGF-1 group were markedly higher than those of the Control and CAP groups (Figs. 6G and H), indicating improved functional restoration of the repaired tissue. Although the mechanical properties remained lower than those of normal tendon, this enhancement suggests that the hydrogel provides a more favorable structural environment for tendon regeneration.

In vivo regulation of the inflammatory microenvironment and collagen reconstruction by the CAPP@IGF-1 hydrogel

To clarify the reparative effects of CAPP@IGF-1 during the later stage of tendon healing, we next examined cell recruitment, proliferative activity, ECM remodeling, and local inflammatory status in the repaired tissue. CXCL12, a key chemokine involved in TSPCs homing [43], was first evaluated by immunofluorescence staining. The results showed that the number of CXCL12-positive cells was markedly increased in the CAPP@IGF-1 group compared with the control group (Figs. 7A and E), indicating an enhanced capacity for cell recruitment within the repair region. In parallel, Ki67 staining revealed stronger fluorescence signals in the CAPP@IGF-1 group (Figs. 7B and F), suggesting elevated proliferative activity of reparative cells. We further assessed ECM remodeling by examining the expression of Col-I and Col-III, which are closely associated with tendon maturation and scar formation, respectively [39, 44]. Immunofluorescence analysis showed that Col-I expression was distinctly increased in the CAPP@IGF-1 group (Figs. 7C and G), whereas Col-III expression was reduced compared with the Control group (Figs. 7D and H). These results suggest that CAPP@IGF-1 promotes a matrix remodeling pattern more favorable for tendon repair, characterized by enhanced deposition of mature tendon-associated collagen (Col-I) and reduced accumulation of matrix components associated with scar-like healing and adhesion formation (Col-Ⅲ).

To further determine whether these changes were accompanied by sustained improvement of the local inflammatory status, macrophage polarization and the expression of key inflammatory cytokines in the repaired tissue were assessed by IHC staining and RT-qPCR. The results showed that CAPP@IGF-1 markedly increased CD206 expression while reducing CD86 expression in the repaired tissue (Fig. S3), indicating that this system maintained an immune microenvironment more favorable for tissue repair in vivo. This observation is consistent with the macrophage reprogramming effect observed in our in vitro experiments, further supporting the persistent immunomodulatory activity of CAPP@IGF-1. Meanwhile, RT-qPCR analysis demonstrated that CAPP@IGF-1 decreased the mRNA levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-2, IL-6, and TNF-α, while increasing the expression of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 (Fig. 7I). These in vivo changes in inflammatory mediators were also in line with the cytokine regulation trend observed in vitro, indicating good consistency and continuity in the ability of CAPP@IGF-1 to regulate the local inflammatory microenvironment. Collectively, these findings indicate that CAPP@IGF-1 maintains a favorable low-inflammatory repair microenvironment throughout tendon healing, thereby continuously supporting cell recruitment, promoting cell proliferation, and facilitating ordered ECM reconstruction.

Transcriptomic analysis into CAPP@IGF-1-mediated inflammatory regulation and ECM remodeling

To delineate the molecular programs underlying the pro-reparative effects of CAPP@IGF-1, we performed transcriptomic analysis on repaired tendon tissues. Principal component analysis (PCA) showed clear segregation between the Control and CAPP@IGF-1 groups, with good intra-group consistency, indicating a substantial shift in the global transcriptional landscape after CAPP@IGF-1 treatment (Fig. 8A). This divergence was further supported by differential expression analysis. The results showed that, compared to the Control group, the CAPP@IGF-1 group had 537 genes upregulated and 397 genes downregulated (p < 0.01, fold change ≥ 2) (Figs. 8B–C). Functional enrichment analyses revealed that these transcriptional changes were predominantly associated with immune system processes, cell proliferation, ECM organization, and cell adhesion (Fig. 8D), suggesting that CAPP@IGF-1 regulates both the inflammatory niche and the structural rebuilding program during tendon healing. Heatmap visualization demonstrated that CAPP@IGF-1 treatment was accompanied by upregulation of genes related to reparative activity and matrix reconstruction, including AKT, IGF-1R, Ki67, CXCL12, and Collagen I, together with downregulation of inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α, IL-2, IL-6, and CD86 (Fig. 8E). Consistently, KEGG enrichment analysis showed that the IGF-1R–AKT pathway, together with chemokine signaling, VEGF signaling, cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction, and JAK–STAT signaling, represented the major pathways enriched following CAPP@IGF-1 intervention (Fig. 8F). These pathways are respectively associated with cell recruitment, support of the reparative microenvironment, and the remodeling of inflammatory and immune responses [45, 46], indicating that CAPP@IGF-1 induces a multilayered repair-associated transcriptional reprogramming. Notably, GSEA further confirmed significant enrichment of the IGF-1R–AKT signaling axis (Fig. 8G), suggesting that this pathway serves as a central function in coordinating inflammatory regulation and tissue reconstruction.

In vivo validation of the IGF-1R/AKT signaling mechanism and biosafety evaluation

To further elucidate the molecular basis underlying the functional recovery and structural improvement promoted by CAPP@IGF-1, we systematically examined the activation status of the IGF-1R/AKT/mTOR/S6K1 signaling axis in repaired tendon tissue. RT-qPCR analysis showed that, compared with the Control and CAP groups, the CAPP@IGF-1 group exhibited notably increased expression of IGF-1R, AKT, mTOR, and S6K1 (Figs. 9A–D), indicating that this pathway was markedly activated during tendon repair. Western blot analysis and its quantitative analysis further corroborated these findings. As shown in Fig. 9E, CAPP@IGF-1 treatment increased the protein levels of IGF-1R, as well as the phosphorylation of AKT and mTOR, together with enhanced S6K1 expression (Figs. 9F–I). These results collectively demonstrate that CAPP@IGF-1 effectively activates the IGF-1R/AKT downstream signaling cascade in vivo. As summarized in Fig. 9J, the pH-responsive release of IGF-1 from PLGA microspheres activates the IGF-1R/AKT pathway, suppresses excessive inflammation, and promotes TSPCs proliferation together with ECM remodeling, thereby facilitating tendon regeneration. In this context, the in vivo signaling data provide important mechanistic support for the coordinated effects of CAPP@IGF-1 on inflammatory regulation and subsequent tissue reconstruction.

Because biosafety is essential for translational applications, we finally evaluated the systemic safety of hydrogel treatment. H&E of major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney, revealed no obvious pathological abnormalities or tissue injury in any treatment group (Fig. 9K), indicating that local administration of CAPP@IGF-1 did not induce detectable systemic toxicity. These findings support in vivo safety of the CAPP@IGF-1 hydrogel.

This study has several limitations. First, although we systematically demonstrated the beneficial effects of CAPP@IGF-1 on inflammatory regulation and tendon repair, the mechanistic analysis was primarily focused on the IGF-1R/AKT signaling axis, and other relevant pathways and their interactions remain to be further elucidated. Second, no clinically used material was included as positive control, and therefore the relative advantage of this strategy over existing clinical adjuncts remains to be established. Future studies with larger sample sizes and more clinically relevant settings will be required to further evaluate its therapeutic efficacy, safety, and translational potential.

Conclusion

In summary, we developed a dynamically responsive nano-hydrogel platform, CAPP@IGF-1, that resolves a central obstacle in tendon repair: the lack of effective coordination between early inflammatory control and subsequent tissue regeneration. By integrating a self-healing phenylborate ester network that exhibits enhanced mechanical properties with pH-responsive PLGA-mediated IGF-1 delivery, this system establishes a stepwise repair program that first reshapes the local inflammatory milieu and then promotes tendon-specific matrix formation. Mechanistically, CAPP@IGF-1 activates the IGF-1R/AKT signaling axis, drives macrophage reprogramming toward a pro-reparative state, and subsequently enhances TSPCs migration, proliferation, and ECM remodeling, while reducing peritendinous adhesion and improving the overall quality of tendon healing. This work advances tendon biomaterial design from passive structural supplementation toward active microenvironment regulation and provides a promising strategy for clinically relevant tendon regeneration.

Experimental section

Animal ethics statement

All animal experiments involved in this study were conducted in accordance with the standards of the Animal Ethics Committee of Northwest University (NWU-AWC-20220902 L).

Animal

Adult male New Zealand white rabbits were obtained from Shaanxi Dilepu Biological Resources Development Co., Ltd.

Cell lines

Murine fibroblast cell line (L929: SCSP-5039), and murine monocyte/macrophage leukemia cell line (RAW 264.7: TCM13) were purchased from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. Tendon stem/progenitor cells (TSPCs: CP-M176) were obtained from Wuhan Pricella Biotechnology Co., Ltd. L929 and RAW 264.7 cells were cultured in DMEM medium with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. TSPCs were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. All cell lines used in this study were tested and confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination.

Experimental reagents

Chitosan (CS) (MW ~100000 Da), 3-carboxyphenylboric acid (BA), Polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) (99.0% alcoholysis), 1-(3-Dimethylaminopropyl)−3-ethylcarbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC), N-hydroxy succinimide (NHS), and Poly (lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) were purchased from Aladdin Bio-Chem Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1) was sourced from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, Missouri, USA). All reagents mentioned above were used as received, without additional purification. Details of other reagents are provided in the Supporting Information.

Preparation of CAPP@IGF-1 hydrogels

The synthesis of 3-carboxyphenylboronic acid-modified chitosan (CS-BA) was performed as follows: CS, BA, EDC, and NHS were combined in a molar ratio of 1:2:2.5:2.5. Specifically, 5 g of CS was dissolved in 2 L of 0.3% acetic acid, while 3-carboxyphenylboronic acid, EDC, and NHS were dissolved in 150 mL of methanol and then added to the CS solution. The mixture was stirred continuously for 24 h and then dialyzed against ultrapure water for 3 d, with water replaced every 6 h. Finally, purified CS-BA was obtained by freeze-drying.

The synthesis of PLGA loading microspheres (PLGA@IGF-1) was performed as follows: 80 mg of PLGA was thoroughly dissolved in 4 mL of dichloromethane, and the solution was stirred at 1000 rpm for 30 min at 25 °C to form a homogeneous oil phase. A 1 mL IGF-1 solution at a concentration of 20 ng/mL was then slowly added as the internal aqueous phase, and stirring continued until a uniform milky emulsion was formed. Subsequently, 100 mL of ultrapure water was added, and the mixture was stirred at 400 rpm for 6 h. After washing the mixture repeatedly (5–7 times), the organic solvent was removed using a rotary evaporator. Finally, the purified PLGA@IGF-1 microspheres were freeze-dried and stored at 4 °C.

The CAP and CAPP@IGF-1 hydrogels was prepared as follows: A 5% (wt%) CS-BA solution and a 20% (wt%) PVA solution were prepared using deionized water. To form the CAP hydrogel, 0.4 mL of the CS-BA solution and 0.75 mL of the PVA solution were thoroughly mixed. For the preparation of the CAPP@IGF-1 hydrogel, CS-BA and PVA solutions were combined following the same procedure as for CAP hydrogel preparation, with the addition of 80 mg of PLGA@IGF-1 to the CS-BA and PVA mixture, resulting in the final CAPP@IGF-1 hydrogel. Based on the encapsulation efficiency of IGF-1 in the PLGA microspheres, each hydrogel contained 16.75 ng of IGF-1.

All precursor solutions were sterilized by filtration through a 0.22 μm membrane and mixed under aseptic conditions to form the hydrogel. At the same time, the hydrogels were further sterilized by UV irradiation before use.

Characterization of CAPP@IGF-1 hydrogels

The chemical structures of CS and CS-BA were characterized using 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR). Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) was employed to analyze the structural features of CS, CS-BA, PVA, and CAP. Furthermore, particle size distribution, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) were examined. The details are provided in Supporting Information.

Release of IGF-1

The release of IGF-1 from the PLGA@IGF-1 microspheres was quantified under pH = 5.5, 6.8, and 7.4 using an ELISA kit (Mlbio, China). The details are provided in Supporting Information.

Rheological tests

The rheological properties of the CAPP@IGF-1 hydrogel were evaluated using a rheometer (Anton Paar, MCR302). The rheological tests included strain amplitude sweep measurements and alternating step-strain tests, where the hydrogel’s storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) were monitored under alternating step-strain conditions, switching between small strain (0.5%) and large strain (200%).

Mechanical performance tests

Uniaxial tensile tests were performed using a mechanical tester (Instron 5565, Instruments, Norwood, USA) to evaluate the mechanical properties of the samples. The details are provided in Supporting Information.

Adhesion energy strength tests

The adhesive properties of the CAPP@IGF-1 hydrogel were evaluated through lap shear tests conducted at 25 °C using a tensile testing machine. The details are provided in Supporting Information.

The adhesive toughness of the CAPP@IGF-1 hydrogel was evaluated using a 180° peel test performed on a universal testing machine. The details are provided in Supporting Information.

Antibacterial test

The antibacterial activity of the CAPP@IGF-1 hydrogel against S. aureus and E. coli was evaluated using the plate count method. The details are provided in Supporting Information.

Cytology experiment

Macrophage polarization: Murine monocyte/macrophage leukemia cells (RAW 264.7) were used as the experimental model. The experiment was divided into three groups: the Control group (RAW 264.7), the LPS group (RAW 264.7 + LPS), and the LPS + CAPP@IGF-1 hydrogel group (CAPP@IGF-1). After co-culture for 48 h, flow cytometry was performed to analyze the expression levels of CD206, CD86, IL-6, IL-2, TNF-α, and IL-10 in each group. The details are provided in Supporting Information.

In vitro cytotoxicity: The cytotoxicity of IGF-1, PLGA@IGF-1 microspheres, and CAPP@IGF-1 hydrogels on L929 cells was evaluated using the CCK-8 assay. Furthermore, the cytotoxicity of PLGA@IGF-1 microspheres and CAPP@IGF-1 hydrogels on L929 cells was determined by live-dead cell staining (AO/EB). The details are provided in Supporting Information.

Cell migration: The scratch assay was performed to evaluate the effect of the CAPP@IGF-1 hydrogel on TSPCs migration. The details are provided in Supporting Information.

Phalloidin staining: To assess the impact of the CAPP@IGF-1 hydrogel on the structure of actin filaments in the cytoskeleton of TSPCs, F-actin and cell nuclei were stained with phalloidin and DAPI, respectively. The details are provided in Supporting Information.

Intracellular protein expression assay: Western blot analysis was performed to determine the protein expression levels of Col-Ⅰ and FN1 in TSPCs. Detailed procedures are provided in the Supporting Information.

Animal experiment

A total of 40 New Zealand white rabbits (male, 3 months old, body weight 2.0–2.3 kg) were used in this study. All animals were allowed to acclimate for one week before the experiment and were housed under standard environmental conditions (temperature 22–25 °C, relative humidity 50–55%, and a 12 h light/dark cycle) with free access to food and water.

After the acclimation period, the rabbits were randomly divided into four groups (n = 10 per group): the Sham group, Control group, CAP group, and CAPP@IGF-1 group. During surgery, the rabbits were anesthetized via intravenous injection of sodium pentobarbital. The right hind limb was shaved and disinfected with iodophor solution. Under sterile conditions, a longitudinal incision parallel to the Achilles tendon was made to fully expose the tendon. Except for the Sham group, all animals underwent a semi-transection transection injury of the right Achilles tendon to simulate partial tendon rupture. Specifically, approximately half of the tendon thickness at the mid-portion of the Achilles tendon was transected to establish a standardized tendon injury model. Subsequently, different treatments were applied according to the experimental design (Table 1): (I) Control group: the injured tendon was repaired by routine suturing without additional treatment, (II) CAP group: 0.1 mL CAP hydrogel was applied around the tendon suture site before closure, (III) CAPP@IGF-1 group: 0.1 mL CAPP@IGF-1 hydrogel was applied around the tendon suture site before closure. All surgical incisions were repaired using a modified Kessler suture technique. During the recovery period, the animals were allowed free activity, and the wound healing and overall health status were monitored weekly.

Experimental grouping and treatment design in the rabbit Achilles tendon injury model

At 8 weeks postoperatively, the rabbits were deeply anesthetized and euthanized by an overdose of sodium pentobarbital. The surgical area of the right hind limb was then shaved and disinfected. A posterior longitudinal incision along the Achilles tendon was made to re-expose the repaired tendon, and the surrounding soft tissues were carefully dissected to avoid additional mechanical damage. The entire Achilles tendon segment containing the injury was subsequently harvested, together with approximately 2 cm of tendon tissue both proximal and distal to the injury site. The harvested tendon samples were gently rinsed with sterile saline to remove residual blood. The collected tissues were then processed according to the requirements of subsequent analyses. Some samples (n = 3) were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for histological evaluation; some samples (n = 3) were stored at −80 °C for transcriptomic and molecular biological analyses; and the remaining samples (n = 4) were used for biomechanical testing.

Histological, immunofluorescence, and immunohistochemistry staining: rabbit tendon tissues were embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. H&E staining, Masson staining, and immunofluorescence analysis targeting CXCL12, Ki67, Col-Ⅰ, and Col-Ⅲ, and immunohistochemistry analysis CD86, and CD206 were performed to evaluate tissue morphology and protein expression. Cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software.

Adhesion assessment: The adhesion at the repair site was evaluated using a standardized histological adhesion scoring system. The details are provided in Supporting Information.

Biomechanical properties assessment: The tensile strength and maximum load of the tendon tissue were evaluated using a mechanical testing system. The details are provided in Supporting Information.

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR analysis: Total RNA was extracted from rabbit tendon tissues following standard protocols, and the mRNA expression levels of IL-6, IL-2, TNF-α, IL-10, IGF-1R, AKT, mTOR, and S6K1 were quantified using RT-qPCR. The details are provided in Supporting Information.

Tendon tissue protein expression assay: Western blot analysis was performed to determine the protein expression levels of IGF-1R, AKT, p-AKT, mTOR, p-mTOR, and S6K1 in tendon tissues. Detailed procedures are provided in the Supporting Information.

Bioinformatics analysis

RNA sequencing was conducted with the assistance of BGI Genomics Co., Ltd. Total RNA was extracted from tendon samples, and RNA quality was assessed prior to library construction. Poly(A)+ mRNA was enriched using oligo(dT)-attached magnetic beads and then fragmented into short sequences. First-strand cDNA was synthesized using the fragmented mRNA as template, followed by second-strand cDNA synthesis. After end repair, adapter ligation, and PCR amplification, the cDNA libraries were constructed and subjected to sequencing on a high-throughput sequencing platform. Raw sequencing reads were processed by quality control to remove low-quality reads and adaptor sequences, generating clean reads for downstream analysis. The filtered reads were aligned to the reference genome, and gene expression levels were quantified. DEGs between the Control and CAPP@IGF-1 groups were identified based on standard statistical criteria. To clarify the biological significance of these transcriptional changes, GO enrichment and KEGG pathway analyses were performed. In addition, GSEA was conducted to further identify signaling pathways associated with the CAPP@IGF-1 response. Heatmap visualization was used to display representative genes related to inflammatory regulation, cell recruitment, proliferation, and ECM remodeling.

Statistical data

Visualization was performed using Origin 2021 software, while statistical analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 27. All results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
