A MOOC-based and high-fidelity surgical simulation-integrated clinical internship mode in optometry teaching for ophthalmology graduate students
Authors: Yang Kang, Kanwen Wang, Jing Cui, Zidan Guo, Chenggong Jiang, Xue Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClinical medicine postgraduates are expected to attain competencies equivalent to senior resident physicians. However, ophthalmology graduate students often struggle to integrate optometry theory with clinical practice during rotations. To address this gap, we developed and evaluated a blended clinical internship model combining Massive Open Online Courses (MOOC) and digital twin-supported high-fidelity surgical simulation (HFS) technology.MethodsA randomized controlled trial was conducted with 54 ophthalmology graduate students and 16 trained teachers. Students were randomly assigned to an experimental group (n = 27) receiving the MOOC-HFS blended internship and a control group (n = 27) receiving traditional teaching. Outcomes included an initial assessment after the MOOC phase (henceforth referred to as Test 1), a post-class assessment (Test 2), and a final examination (Test 3) as well as scores on the Course Experience Questionnaire (CEQ), Revised Two-Factor Study Process Questionnaire (R-SPQ-2 F), and a teacher satisfaction questionnaire. Data were analyzed using t-tests and non-parametric tests.ResultsThe experimental group achieved significantly higher scores in all assessments (P < 0.05), with greater improvements in practical skill–focused Refractive Classroom than memory-based Amblyopia Classroom. They also demonstrated superior CEQ scores, deeper learning approaches in R-SPQ-2 F, and higher teacher satisfaction (all P < 0.001).ConclusionsThe MOOC-HFS blended model, structured as “theory → simulation → practice,” effectively synergizes MOOC flexibility with HFS realism, significantly enhancing academic performance, deep learning, course experience, and teaching satisfaction. This model redefines teacher roles and establishes a competency-based framework, providing an evidence-based reference for optometry clinical education optimization. Further multi-center and long-term studies are needed to confirm generalizability.Keywords: MOOC-HFS blended learning model, Optometry teaching, Clinical internship, Online learning resources, Self-directed learning ability

Background

Within the master’s program for clinical medicine graduate students, the curriculum is integrated with the standardized residency training (SRT) requirements. Upon graduation, students are expected to possess foundational clinical research aptitude and diagnostic and therapeutic competencies equivalent to those of a senior resident physician [1]. Optometry is a distinct subspecialty characterized by complex theoretical foundations that differ substantially from general ophthalmology. This separation, coupled with limited clinical exposure opportunities, creates significant learning barriers. Consequently, when ophthalmology graduate students undertake optometry rotations, deficits such as inadequate mastery of core concepts and difficulty integrating theory with practice frequently emerge. These challenges represent a growing educational concern.

The application of MOOC into medical education has improved students’ comprehension and demonstrated favorable outcomes [2]. However, MOOCs alone remain inadequate for disciplines requiring hands-on skills or credentialing [3–5].HFS which enables repeated practice and enhances technical proficiency without patient risk [6], and has been successfully applied in several medical specialties such as medical imaging training [7]. Nevertheless, its value in ophthalmic and optometry education remains underinvestigated [8]. However, there is a significant knowledge gap in its implementation in ophthalmology and optometry education [8]in this novel pursuit. Blended learning combines traditional classroom instruction with structured online activities in a flipped classroom model, providing equitable access to high-quality instructional resources [9, 10] while maintaining classroom framework [11]. MOOC and HFS are complementary and mutually reinforcing: MOOCs support standardized, flexible, and autonomous theoretical learning; HFS provides safe, realistic, and repeatable hands-on skill training. Their integration is theoretically supported by flipped classroom and deliberate practice frameworks. Over the past five years, while higher education has increasingly adopted MOOC-enhanced flipped classrooms [12], challenges including insufficient personalized learning design for specific disciplines [13] and inadequate development of practical skills have gradually emerged [14].

Given the inherent complexity of optometric science and variable clinical proficiency among trainees, we implemented a blended pedagogical framework integrating online and offline modalities. The online component centers on a MOOC-based digital learning ecosystem, leveraging information technology to deliver curated content through dynamic visualization of abstract theoretical concepts. This platform employed specialized software applications and multimedia production techniques to establish a structured virtual learning environment. During offline sessions, instructors synthesize knowledge, refine clinical teaching, and facilitate high-fidelity procedural practice using high-fidelity surgical simulation technology for refractive surgery training. The MOOC-HFS blended learning model addresses insufficient hands-on training of online-only teaching, and closely integrates abstract basic knowledge with clinical practice, to improve teaching outcomes and the student learning experience.

This study aims to evaluate a MOOC-HFS blended learning model. By comparing it with the traditional teaching model in terms of academic performance, learning environment perceptions, learning approaches, and teacher experience, we assess whether this new model can remedy the insufficient hands-on training of the traditional single-mode teaching and strengthen the integration of abstract basic knowledge with clinical practice, to improve teaching outcomes and provide evidence for optometry education.

Methods

Study design and sample

This study used a randomized controlled design, a standard experimental approach for educational research. Participant included 54 2023 cohort Ophthalmology graduate students and 16 qualified, standardized optometry teachers from the Ophthalmology Teaching and Research Section, The First Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University. Clinical teaching and internships were conducted from January to June 2024.

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before inclusion in the study, which was previously approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University. Outcome evaluators were blinded to group assignment to minimize assessment bias. Participants and intervention implementers were not blinded due to the nature of the different teaching intervention. After detailed explanations were provided to the students and trained teachers, the students were randomly divided into an experimental (n = 27) and control (n = 27) groups using a computer generated random number list. Baseline characteristics, including age, gender, and previous academic year GPA, were balanced between the two groups (all P > 0.05), confirming the success of randomization. Therefore, any initial difference in domain-specific knowledge can be attributed to the first phase of the intervention.Teachers participated in both groups. To minimize contamination bias, teachers were instructed to strictly adhere to the respective teaching protocols for each group. Specifically, for the control group, teachers followed the traditional didactic curriculum without incorporating any online resources or simulation demonstrations. For the experimental group, teachers implemented the full MOOC-HFS blended protocol as described. Teachers were also reminded to avoid discussing the experimental teaching methods or materials with the control group. The experimental group received a MOOC-HFS technology blended clinical internship, while the control group received a standard clinical internship. Teaching process design

Optometry teaching content was divided into two modules:

Refractive Classroom: clinical and surgical skills (optometry, corneal refractive surgery).

Refractive Classroom: clinical and surgical skills (optometry, corneal refractive surgery).

Amblyopia Classroom: theoretical and memory-based learning (optometry basics, strabismus, amblyopia).

Amblyopia Classroom: theoretical and memory-based learning (optometry basics, strabismus, amblyopia).

Experimental group

Prior to the clinical internship, the experimental group completed a 6-day structured MOOC-HFS curriculum (Table 1).

The 6-day structured MOOC-HFS technology curriculum in experimental group

On Days 1–2, students used a dedicated online platform for self-study of instructional videos covering concepts, principles, indications, contraindications, postoperative management, and complication management relevant to subsequent internship procedures.

On Day 3, an initial knowledge assessment (Test 1) was administered via the platform. This test was designed to evaluate students’ mastery of the content learned during the first two days of MOOC self-study, serving as the first measure of learning effectiveness of the intervention rather than a baseline of prior knowledge. Responses were collected and analyzed automatically. Results were provided in real time to instructors and students.

On Day 5, students reviewed HFS procedural demonstrations on the platform.

On Day 6, instructors summarized preview content, clarified key concepts and procedural challenges, and provided individualized guidance. Students practiced repeatedly using HFS until proficiency was achieved. A post-class test was administered immediately after review.

Control group

The control group received a 6-day face-to-face traditional didactic curriculum with teacher demonstrations and explanations. Only textbook materials were used; no online resources or simulation training were provided. A pre-class test was administered on Day 3 and a post-class test on Day 6.

The application of HFS technology in experimental group

The HFS system was a high-fidelity ophthalmic surgical simulator (Eyesi Ophthalmology, VRmagic GmbH, Germany), integrating virtual reality display, a microscopic surgical interface, and a force-feedback tactile system [15]. The HFS system is a high-fidelity virtual reality–based surgical simulator, and does not involve patient-specific digital twin technology based on image fitting or clinical data fusion. For refractive surgery training, the system simulates the complete procedure of femtosecond laser-assisted laser in situ keratomileusis (FS-LASIK) from a first-person surgeon’s view, including the microscopic operating field. Students completed progressive tasks and practiced repeatedly at key stages. The built-in software automatically generated objective performance reports for each procedure [16] and provided guidance.Standardized simulator-based assessments were performed before and after training to quantify skill improvement [17].

Outcome measures

Teaching effectiveness was evaluated using objective academic performance and subjective perceptions of the learning environment and process among students and teachers.After providing informed consent, all students completed the same questionnaires and scales. Collected data included demographics, examination scores, learning outcomes, and student experiences. The demographic questionnaire included age, gender, and previous academic year grade point average (GPA, maximum 5.0). One research assistant per class assisted with questionnaire distribution.

Academic performance

Assessment included three components: pre-class test, post-class test (100 points each, equal weight), and final examination (100 points). The pre-class test and post-class test were administered on Day 3 and Day 6, respectively. The final exam was given one week after course completion.All tests were administered at the same time for both groups and covered theoretical knowledge, patient consultation, eye examination, medical record writing, diagnosis and differential diagnosis, disease identification, treatment planning, and standardized clinical skills.

Examination questions were selected from the ophthalmology question bank according to the teaching syllabus, reviewed by two lecturers, and approved by the teaching director.The content validity index of the final exam was 0.9. The mean difficulty index and discrimination index were 0.66 and 0.25, respectively. Construct validity, reliability coefficient, and item reliability were 0.17, 0.81, and 0.78, respectively.

Student and teacher perceptions

The Course Experience Questionnaire (CEQ), Revised Two-Factor Study Process Questionnaire (R-SPQ-2 F), and a teacher-developed teaching experience questionnaire were used to assess subjective experiences. All participants completed the questionnaires at the end of the internship, with a 100% response rate.

The CEQ [18] was used to evaluate students’ perceptions of course quality. It includes five subscales: Good Teaching, Clear Goals and Standards, Appropriate Workload, Appropriate Assessment, and Generic Skills Development. Cronbach’s α for each subscale ranged from 0.79 to 0.87, indicating acceptable reliability.

Evaluation methods for students’ internal learning processes

The R-SPQ-2 F [19] was used to assess learning approaches (Deep Approach and Surface Approach), each with motive and strategy subscales. It includes 20 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale. Higher scores indicate a stronger tendency toward that learning approach. Cronbach’s α was 0.86 for the Deep Approach and 0.79 for the Surface Approach.

Evaluation methods for teachers’ comfort and satisfaction with teaching experience

A 5-item teacher experience questionnaire was used, with responses on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree). Domains included satisfaction with resources, pedagogical autonomy, burnout, workload, and student interaction (see Table 5). Cronbach’s α was 0.91.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics 25.0. Baseline characteristics are presented as mean ± standard deviation or number and percentage. Between-group balance at baseline was tested using the chi-square test and independent t-test.Student examination scores and questionnaire data were compared between groups. Independent t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests were used as appropriate. Effect sizes (Cohen’s d for parametric data, r for nonparametric data) were calculated for significant differences. A repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted to examine the effects of Time, Group, and the Time × Group interaction on examination scores. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Fifty-four ophthalmology graduate students were included, 27 in each group. The experimental group included 17 (62.96%) female and 10 (37.04%) male students. The control group included 16 (59.26%) female and 11 (40.74%) male students. Mean age was 22.50 years in the experimental group and 22.30 years in the control group.There were no significant between-group differences in age, gender, or previous GPA (all P > 0.05). On the initial assessment (Test 1), the experimental group scored significantly higher than the control group for both modules (Refractive Classroom: t = 4.10, P < 0.001; Amblyopia Classroom: t = 3.20, P = 0.002). Differences were more pronounced in the Refractive Classroom (Fig. 2A).

In post-class tests, significant differences were observed for the Refractive Classroom (t = 5.74, P < 0.001) and Amblyopia Classroom (t = 2.67, P = 0.01). The experimental group performed better in both modules, with larger effects in the Refractive Classroom (Fig. 2B).

In the final exam, the experimental group scored significantly higher in the Refractive Classroom (t = 3.64, P < 0.001) and Amblyopia Classroom (t = 2.33, P = 0.02), with a larger difference in the Refractive Classroom (Fig. 2C).

A repeated-measures ANOVA revealed a significant Time × Group interaction (F(2, 104) = 7.11, P = 0.001, partial η² = 0.12). Post-hoc comparisons showed that while both groups improved from Test 1 to Test 2, the control group’s scores declined at the final examination (85.56) to near their Test 1 level (84.74), whereas the experimental group maintained higher scores (94.82).

Discussion

This study evaluates a MOOC-HFS blended clinical internship model for optometry teaching among ophthalmology graduate students. The model allows students to learn theoretical knowledge in advance via online resources, then consolidate and apply that knowledge in hands-on clinical practice.Improvements in academic performance, deep learning approaches (R-SPQ-2 F), course experience (CEQ), and teacher satisfaction suggest that this model enhances self-regulated learning, deep engagement, and clinical reasoning. These effects led to better academic performance and more positive experiences for both students and teachers. This study provides empirical evidence supporting the value of this blended model for optometry internship education.

MOOC learning supports autonomous learning

The blended learning group achieved significantly higher scores on the initial assessment (Test 1). This early advantage suggests that the MOOC-based self-learning phase was effective in enhancing knowledge acquisition, even before the classroom session. Subsequent post-class (Test 2) and final exam (Test 3) scores were also significantly higher in the experimental group, further supporting the overall efficacy of the blended model. These findings indicate that MOOC-based learning improves autonomous learning and academic performance, consistent with previous studies on self-regulated learning [20].The MOOC environment helps students develop self-regulated learning: they independently plan progress, monitor understanding, and use repeated video access and immediate test feedback. This study confirms that MOOCs act not only as knowledge resources but also foster metacognitive abilities, including preview strategies and self-monitoring, which may transfer to other learning contexts.

HFS improves performance in practical courses

Performance differences were more pronounced in operation-oriented courses than in memory-oriented courses, which can be attributed to HFS. As noted by Xie et al. [21], HFS allows repeated, exploratory skills training in a safe, low-cost environment using high-fidelity interactive virtual models. HFS provides realistic, real-time feedback for each procedural step. This immersive experience enhances procedural mastery and immediate knowledge application, which likely contributed to the observed improvements in academic performance [22, 23]. The significant Time × Group interaction (P = 0.001) further supports the blended model’s effectiveness, suggesting better knowledge retention in the experimental group.

Analysis of the complementary integration trend of MOOC and HFS technology

MOOC and HFS are not isolated educational tools, but strongly complementary.Theoretically, this integrated model aligns with flipped classroom and blended learning theories: MOOCs support pre-learning and standardization of theoretical knowledge; HFS supports hands-on practice and clinical reasoning.Practically, in optometry training: MOOCs improve the consistency and flexibility of theoretical teaching; HFS provides a low-risk, repeatable environment for skill training.This combination creates a “theory → simulation → practice” learning pathway that effectively bridges knowledge and practice. As reported by Li [24], this integrated model creates a reinforcing closed loop that promotes deep integration of knowledge and skills, representing a promising direction for skills-oriented medical education.

Comprehensive effects of the MOOC-HFS blended model

CEQ, R-SPQ-2 F, and teacher questionnaire data confirm that the MOOC-HFS model significantly improves teaching efficiency, deep learning, and satisfaction for both students and teachers.Higher scores in Good Teaching and Generic Skills (CEQ) show that students perceived higher-quality teaching and reported greater development of generic skills rather than passively receiving knowledge [25]. In the R-SPQ-2 F, Deep Motivation and Deep Strategy improved significantly, indicating a shift toward deep learning. As Park [26] suggested, the safe, feedback-rich HFS environment may encourage exploration and more engaged learning approaches. In the blended setting, teachers focus on learning design and cognitive guidance, supporting student growth more effectively and improving professional satisfaction.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it is a single-center design, which may limit generalizability. Second, the same teachers delivered instruction to both groups, raising the possibility of contamination bias. Third, teacher satisfaction results should be interpreted with caution due to the non-blinded design and small number of teachers (n = 16). Fourth, outcomes were mainly academic performance and self-reported questionnaires rather than direct assessments of clinical competence. Fifth, the large effect sizes (Cohen’s d up to 2.99) should be interpreted with caution due to the small sample size and self-reported data. Sixth, while we supplemented our analysis with repeated-measures ANOVA, future studies should consider more advanced methods such as mixed-effects modeling. Future multi-center studies with larger samples and objective competency assessments are needed to confirm generalizability.

Conclusions

This study confirms that a MOOC-HFS blended clinical internship model in optometry teaching is associated with improved academic performance, more favorable learning experiences (CEQ), deeper learning approaches (R-SPQ-2 F), and higher teaching satisfaction among students and teachers.This model integrates traditional and online teaching, redefines the role of instructors, and provides a practical reference for local teaching practice and curriculum optimization in optometry education.Further multi-center and long-term studies are needed before extending this model to other disciplines or institutions.
