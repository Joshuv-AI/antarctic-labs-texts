Microstructural analysis does not support altered interhemispheric wiring of the human anterior commissure in corpus callosum dysgenesis
Authors: Timothy J Edwards, Ryan J Dean, Gail A Robinson, Jacquelyn Knight, Simone A Mandelstam, Linda J Richards
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Highlights•The anterior commissure of those with corpus callosum dysgenesis was investigated.•Cross-sectional volume, axon density, and connectivity were broadly neurotypical.•Cognitive scores were not dependent on the neuroanatomy of the commissure.•The commissure is unlikely the sole communicative substrate in all CCD individuals.Keywords: Corpus callosum dysgenesis, Agenesis of the corpus callosum, Anterior commissure, Diffusion magnetic resonance imaging

Introduction

Of the estimated 86 billion neurons that comprise the adult human brain (Azevedo, 2009), approximately 190 million contribute their axons to form the corpus callosum, its largest interhemispheric connection (Tomasch, 1954). This commissure is the primary substrate for bilateral neocortical communication and provides the means to coordinate a wide range of excitatory and inhibitory events across the cerebral hemispheres (van der Knaap and van der Ham, 2011). However, ∼1:4000 individuals are born with corpus callosum dysgenesis (CCD) (Hetts et al., 2006, Glass et al., 2008), a continuum of neurodevelopmental disorders, typically genetic in origin, which result in various malformations of the corpus callosum (Fenlon and Richards, 2015, Paul, 2007); in cases where all callosal axons fail to cross the brain midline, this is termed agenesis of the corpus callosum (ACC).

Intriguingly, individuals with CCD in isolation (i.e., callosal dysgenesis is not associated with a wider syndrome) generally have a favorable prognosis, despite their reduced interhemispheric connectivity (Mangione, 2011). Furthermore, while individuals with CCD exhibit a unique neuropsychological presentation, they retain the ability to integrate information across both brain hemispheres (Paul, 2007, Brown and Paul, 2019, Hearne, 2018, Saul and Sperry, 1968, Sperry, 1982). This retained capacity for interhemispheric information transfer in CCD has been widely attributed to in-utero neuroplasticity − the reorganization of neocortical connections to functionally compensate for the absence of the corpus callosum. It has been hypothesized that in the absence of the corpus callosum alternative commissural substrates can be remodeled to facilitate continued communication between the cerebral hemispheres (Barr and Corballis, 2002, Tovar-Moll, 2014). The anterior commissure is a promising candidate for this substrate, as it predates the corpus callosum evolutionarily and serves as the primary substrate for interhemispheric communication in mammalian species that do not possess a corpus callosum (i.e., marsupials and monotremes) (Suárez et al., 2014). Furthermore, multiple studies have reported an enlarged anterior commissure in individuals with CCD (Barr and Corballis, 2002, Fischer et al., 1992, Siffredi, 2019), and a previous investigation of anterior commissural connectivity in CCD using diffusion magnetic resonance imaging (dMRI) tractography identified an individual in whom this structure homotopically connects the posterior parietal cortices (Tovar-Moll, 2014). However, it is yet to be demonstrated conclusively that the neuroanatomy of the anterior commissure correlates with preserved interhemispheric communication in individuals with CCD.

The purpose of the present study was to characterize the structure of the anterior commissure across a diverse cohort of adults with CCD using high-field MRI and to determine whether this commissure may have been modified to facilitate interhemispheric communication in the complete or partial absence of the corpus callosum.

Methods and materials

Human participants

The University of Queensland Human Research Ethics Committee approved all procedures described hereafter, and all participants involved in the study provided their written, informed consent to all research activities in which they were involved. Sixteen individuals with CCD (six with ACC) were recruited from October 2014 to January 2019, as part of an ongoing human imaging study, aged 20 – 72 years (mean age of 40.2 years, standard deviation (SD) of 16.8 years, six males); the genetic cause of CCD was not established in any of the individuals prior to their participation in this study. Each CCD individual had a sex and age-matched neurotypical control for comparison (mean age = 40.3 years, SD = 15.9 years, six males). Ten of the sixteen controls were unrelated to any individuals with CCD; of the remaining six controls, one was a sibling of an individual with CCD, three were mothers of individuals with CCD, one was an aunt of an individual with CCD, and one was a grandmother of an individual with CCD. All participant MRI scans were reviewed by a qualified and experienced neuroradiologist (SM) to identify any additional malformations in the CCD cohort and to ensure the control brains presented normally; the salient neuroradiological findings for the individuals with CCD are presented in Table 1.

Neuropsychological testing

A subset of eleven CCD individuals (three with ACC) and their sex and age-matched controls undertook a neuropsychological evaluation. This evaluation included three subtests from the Test for Everyday Attention (Robertson et al., 1994); Telephone Search (TelSearch), Telephone Search while Counting (TelCount), and Elevator Counting with Distraction (ElDistract). The different aspects of attention evaluated by these subtests (see Supplementary material: Test of Everyday Attention for more information) fall under the domain of the executive functional brain network, and have been reported as being highly dependent on the interconnectivity of the two brain hemispheres (Prado et al., 2011); consequently, these tests were employed as proxies for participant bilateral information integration. To provide a contrast to these tests of attention, an Oral Graded Difficult Spelling Test (OGDST) and Oral Graded Difficult Arithmetic Test (OGDAT), which involve spelling aloud single words or solving addition/subtraction problems, were also administered to these participants. These two auditory-verbal tasks tend to invoke more lateralized brain responses (Baxter and Warrington, 1994, Arsalidou and Taylor, 2011, Semenza and Benavides-Varela, 2017, Jackson and Warrington, 1986), and participant performance on these tests should consequently be less dependent on the capacity of an individual to transfer information between their cortical hemispheres. Finally, the Wechsler Abbreviated Scale of Intelligence (Wechsler, 1999) was conducted, to assess participants’ full-scale intelligence quotients (FSIQ), along with the Symbol Digit Modalities Test (SDMT), to assess participant visuomotor processing speed (Smith, 1982).

Magnetic resonance image acquisition

Magnetic resonance (MR) images for all subjects were acquired at the Centre for Advanced Imaging (The University of Queensland) using a 7 Tesla Siemens Magnetom whole-body MR scanner. The dMRI was conducted using a stimulated-echo diffusion echo planar imaging (EPI) pulse sequence (Merboldt et al., 1991) using the following parameters: image matrix (MTX) 142 × 142 × 70, field of view (FOV) 220 × 220 × 126 mm (0.3 mm axial slice gap), voxel size 1.55 × 1.55 × 1.55 mm, echo time (TE) = 54.4 ms, and repetition time (TR) = 12000 ms, two diffusion shells (32 directions at b = 700 s mm−2, 64 directions at b = 2000 s mm−2; Δ = 110 ms, δ = 9.6 ms). Four b = 0 s mm−2 volumes were also acquired, including one reverse phase-encoded volume for EPI distortion correction. High-resolution 3D T1-weighted MR images, using the MP2RAGE (Marques et al., 2010) pulse sequence, were also acquired for each participant to provide an anatomical reference: MTX 256 × 300 × 320, FOV 192 × 225 × 240 mm, voxel size 0.75 × 0.75 × 0.75 mm, TR = 4300 ms, TE = 3.44 ms. All acquired images were subjected to a series of stringent quality control checks (see Supplementary material: Data quality control checks).

MRI data pre-processing

All diffusion MR images were pre-processed using MRtrix3 (Tournier et al., 2012) to correct for participant motion, bias-field inhomogeneities, and eddy currents. The diffusion MR images were used to calculate orientationally invariant estimates of intravoxel volume fraction (ICVF, vin) and isotropic volume fraction (viso) using the Accelerated Microstructure Imaging via Convex Optimization (Daducci, 2015) implementation of Neurite Orientation Dispersion and Density Imaging (NODDI) (Zhang et al., 2012). Fractional anisotropy (FA) and radial diffusivity (RD) were also generated using the weighted least-squares diffusion tensor estimation method implemented by MRtrix3 (Tournier et al., 2012). Additionally, multi-shell multi-tissue constrained spherical deconvolution (maximum degree (lmax) = 8) was employed to convert the dMRI datasets into fiber orientation density function (fODF) maps (Jeurissen et al., 2014); these maps were then used to generate diffusion tractographs of the anterior commissure (see below).

Neuroanatomical and microstructural measures of connectivity

To test whether the overall size of the anterior commissure differed between the CCD and control groups, its cross-sectional volume (CSV) in the midsagittal plane of the T1-weighted MR images was determined for all participants; the CSV of the anterior commissure was defined as its cross-sectional area in the MR image, multiplied by the thickness of a single voxel. CSV values were also normalized by total brain volume (CSV’) to account for differences in brain size across the participant cohort. To evaluate the microstructural properties of the anterior commissures (e.g. the type and/or packing density of the axons that constitute the commissure), a ∼10 mm section of each participant’s anterior commissure (centered close to the brain midline) was reconstructed via probabilistic spherical deconvolution tractography (iFOD2, 2000 streamlines, step size = 0.25 mm, maximum angle Δ = 22.5° per step, termination threshold = 0.1). The generated streamlines were then mapped to the participant’s diffusion MR images to create a track density map (Calamante et al., 2010) that would be used to define the region of interest (ROI) for the microstructural analysis. Voxels were excluded from an anterior commissure ROI if (i.) the voxel had a streamline count <20 and/or (ii.) the voxel had a viso > 0.1, to prevent the surrounding cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) from biasing the results through partial-volume effects.

A series of dMRI tractographs were generated for all CCD participants and their matched controls to test the hypothesis previously proposed by Tovar-Moll et al. in 2014, specifically that there exists a defined connection, unique to CCD individuals, that links the bilateral posterior parietal cortices via the anterior commissure. Streamlines were generated from bilateral seed ROIs parasagittal to the anterior commissure (see Supplementary material: Diffusion tractography parameters). Anterior commissure tractographs were generated using the same tracking parameters, with a reduced total streamline count (5,000 streamlines), utilizing ipsilateral inclusion and exclusion ROIs to remove extraneous streamlines.

Tract-based spatial statistics

To complement the analyses of the anterior commissure, the microstructural analyses outlined above were extended to include all brain white matter. To conduct voxel-wise comparisons, the diffusion metric maps of interest (i.e., FA, RD, and ICVF) were co-registered using the warp maps that were generated during the creation of a fODF population template that was based on the diffusion imaging data of all the participants that took part in the study (MRtrix3). We utilized tract-based spatial statistics (TBSS) in FSL (v6.0.5) to improve further the robustness of these comparisons to minor misalignments in participant co-registration and spatial smoothing (Smith, 2006). In brief, the co-registered FA maps were averaged to generate an image that depicts the tracts that are commonly shared amongst the participants. These tracts were then thinned via a multiple-step process, referred to as skeletonization, to create a framework of thin sheets and lines that would represent the white matter. Values from the diffusion metric maps, i.e., FA, RD, and ICVF, were then projected onto the closest adjoining voxel of this white matter skeleton.

2D-based threshold-free cluster-enhanced voxel-based analyses (utilizing 20,000 permutations) were then conducted on the resultant skeletonized diffusion metric maps to determine whether the two groups exhibited significant differences in their white matter microstructural features; participant age and sex were included in the analyses as nuisance regressors. Affected tracts were identified via referencing the Johns Hopkins University ICBM atlases of brain white matter (FSL v6.0.5), which had been co-registered with the population template beforehand.

Statistical analyses

Group-wise comparisons of ROI averaged neuroanatomical metrics and neuropsychological scores were conducted using a two-tailed Welch’s t-test (β = 0.2 and α = 0.05) using R; each data set was first tested to determine whether the distributions were Gaussian (Shapiro-Wilk test, p = 0.05). Results

Phenotyping of the CCD and neurotypical cohorts

In keeping with previous observations (Paul, 2007), CCD individuals demonstrated considerable variability in the gross anatomy of the corpus callosum and surrounding structures. Depictions of the neuroanatomy of each CCD participant are provided in Fig. 1 and Table 1; all CCD and control participants had an intact anterior commissure (as determined via T1-weighted MR imaging and diffusion tractography). No atypical neuroanatomy was noted amongst the control cohort. The IQ scores for the CCD individuals tested were likewise heterogeneous, with individuals’ scores ranging from well above the general population average to scores that would suggest intellectual impairment (i.e., superior to extremely low); of these IQ scores, only performance IQ was significantly lower than the neurotypical controls (percentage change (PC) = −14 %, p = 0.05). All neurotypical participants received IQ scores that were of, or above, the general population average (Table 2). Group-wise comparisons determined that the CCD cohort performed poorer than the neurotypical controls on tasks of attention switching (ElDistract, –22 %, p = 0.05), visuomotor processing speed (SDMT, PC = −46 %, p < 0.01), and visual selectivity (TelSearch, PC = +42 %, p < 0.01.

White matter microstructure in corpus callosum dysgenesis does not support altered interhemispheric wiring of the anterior commissure across the CCD cohort

The results of the analysis of anterior commissure neuroanatomy are summarised in Fig. 2. Overall, the analysis of the midsagittal anterior commissure CSV’ failed to detect a significant group-wise difference between the CCD and control groups (PC = 2 %, p = 0.91), although CSV’ was found to be considerably more variable in the CCD group (F test, p < 0.01); these findings held when CSV was not normalized (data not shown). While no group-wise increase in the average size of the anterior commissure was detected in CCD individuals, the microstructural organization of this commissure may have been modified instead. A collection of metrics, derived from the acquired dMRI data using NODDI, were analyzed to test this possibility. NODDI metrics offer more specific markers of microstructure alteration than traditional diffusion-tensor derived values, such as FA and MD, and are also more robust to the effect of crossing, diverging, or curving axonal projections. Of particular interest is their capacity to capture potential differences in axonal packing density between the two groups. The results of these analyses found that the CCD group exhibited a non-significant reduction in vin (PC = −9%, p = 0.21) and an increase in RD (PC = +2%, p = 0.68) when compared to the control group. These findings are consistent with a negligible/minor increase in extra-axonal spacing within the anterior commissure of CCD participants. While a non-significant increase in FA was observed across the CCD group (PC = 14 %, p = 0.11), this negligible/minor increase in FA was attributed to the CCD group exhibiting larger axial diffusivities (PC = 11 %, p < 0.01) and lower OD (PC = −19 %, p = 0.04), indicating that the axonal fibers of the anterior commissure in the CCD group had a tighter alignment and/or had a shallower curve in their projections.

Whole brain white matter neurite density was overall reduced across the CCD cohort

Significant concurrent decreases in ICVF and FA, and increases in RD, were detected in the anterior commissure and other white matter tracts in the CCD group. These other affected tracts included the dorsal projections of the corpus callosum, corticospinal tracts, anterior corona radiata, superior longitudinal fasciculi, and posterior thalamic and optic radiations (Fig. 4). While the posterior cingulum bundles also exhibited significant decreases in ICVF and FA, and increases in RD, this was primarily driven by the intersection of the cohort-averaged tracts with the enlarged posterior lateral ventricles of the CCD participants; the boundaries of these structures could not be adequately co-registered with their neurotypical counterparts due to their size.

Conversely, CCD white matter tracts that demonstrated significant increases in FA included the Probst bundles and the superior corona radiata (Fig. 4). The Probst bundles are a pair of longitudinal tracts, located ventrally to the cingulum bundles, comprised of the callosal axons that failed to cross the brain midline (Probst, 1901); these white matter structures are found exclusively in individuals with CCD. The associated significant decrease in superior corona radiata ICVF indicates that the increase in FA across the superior corona radiata was due to the reduced number of callosal axons that typically project perpendicularly through to this region.

Cognitive performance in CCD participants was not dependent on the neuroanatomy of the anterior commissure

The visual selective attention, divided attention, and attention-switching tasks were chosen for this study as they each test a different aspect of attention in addition to requiring ongoing communication between the cerebral hemispheres. Consequently, these scores were utilized as proxies for bilateral information integration across the telencephalic midline. The spelling and arithmetic tasks, on the other hand, primarily invoke activity in a single hemisphere for each operation performed and are therefore less dependent on bilateral information integration. The linear regression of these scores would thus provide a negative control for the analysis of attention paradigm data. The results of these multiple linear regressions are presented in Table 3; in summary, visual selective attention scores (TelSearch) showed a significant correlation between the ICVF of the anterior commissure and participant performance (β = -0.37 (95 % confidence interval (CI0.95) = -0.63 to −0.10), p = 0.01). However, the group × ICVF interaction term was not significant (β = -0.05 (CI0.95 = -0.29 to 0.20), p = 0.64). Participant divided attention (TelCount) and attention switching (ElDistract) scores, on the other hand, did not exhibit any notable relationship with the anterior commissure neuroanatomy for either group. Similarly, variances in participant spelling (OGDST) and arithmetic (OGDAT) scores did not exhibit any notable relationship with the neuroanatomical features of the anterior commissure of the participants.

Discussion

It has been proposed that the anterior commissure is the primary substrate through which interhemispheric connectivity is maintained in CCD (Barr and Corballis, 2002, Tovar-Moll, 2014). However, callosotomised individuals (those who have had their corpus callosum surgically severed) likewise often possess an intact anterior commissure, and yet bilateral information integration in these cases is not preserved (Sperry, 1982, Roland, 2017). One possible explanation is that the anterior commissure is neuroplastically restructured in utero to facilitate the transmission of low-level information (Tovar-Moll, 2014, Siffredi, 2019). The purpose of the present study was to test this hypothesis; it also includes (to the authors’ knowledge) the first 7 T MR images of a cohort of CCD individuals with a multi-shell diffusion MRI sequence. The high signal strength afforded by this spectrometer permitted the acquisition of high-resolution MR images for the analysis of the anterior commissure, while the use of the multi-shell diffusion MRI sequence allowed for the use of advanced multi-compartment axonal modeling. This permitted a systematic investigation of anterior commissure microstructure in CCD.

The overall size of the anterior commissure in the CCD cohort was the first neuroanatomical feature to be assessed. The anterior commissure of a neurotypical adult consists of approximately 3.5 million axons, compared to the approximately 190 million axons supported by the corpus callosum. It is therefore not unreasonable to anticipate that the size of the anterior commissure would be substantially increased in the CCD population, a prediction supported by commonly cited reports of enlarged anterior commissures in CCD (Barr and Corballis, 2002, Fischer et al., 1992, Siffredi, 2019). However, no significant group-wise increase in the anterior commissural size was found in the CCD cohort in the present study. This apparent contradiction with the findings of previous studies was due to the significantly wider variance of the CCD anterior commissure CSV’, which reduced the statistical power of the analyses. However, this result does demonstrate that there are individuals within the CCD population that have a notably attenuated anterior commissure, which would suggest a reduced capacity for this structure to support interhemispheric connectivity. This finding also matches other previous reports of the anterior commissures being either reduced or unchanged in size in mouse models that exhibit callosal dysgenesis (Schmidt and Caparelli-Dáquer, 1989, Livy, 1997).

While no group-wise increase in the average size of the CCD anterior commissure was detected, the microstructural organization of this commissure may be modified instead. For example, a relative increase in the axonal density of the CCD anterior commissures would be indicative of an increase in the total number of axons per unit volume, which would likewise increase the theoretical number of different cognitive processes that can be simultaneously supported by this substrate. However, the group-wise analyses of ICVF found a non-significant decrease in CCD mean axonal density, corroborated by a likewise non-significant increase in mean RD. Combined with the measures of the anterior commissure CSV’, these findings were unexpected; for the anterior commissure to act as the primary substrate for facilitating bilateral information transfer across the CCD population, a more consistent and substantial bolstering of this structure was anticipated.

The possibility must then be considered that it is not necessary for the anterior commissure microstructure to be remodeled to mediate hemispheric integration in CCD; for example, a change in the connectivity profile of the anterior commissure may occur that increases the expanse of neocortex that this commissure directly connects. Tovar-Moll and colleagues have previously provided evidence of this (Tovar-Moll, 2014), demonstrating that they were able to generate tractograph streamlines that connected homotopic regions of the posterior parietal cortices via the anterior commissure in one individual with CCD. This homotopic connection was not detected amongst the individuals in their control group, nor has such a connection been described in previous dissection studies (Livy, 1997, Jouandet and Hartenstein, 1983). We consequently attempted to replicate the finding of Tovar Moll et al. in our own participant cohort.

When utilizing a comparable tractography protocol to that implemented by Tovar Moll et al., 2014, streamlines could be generated that reached the homotopic parietal or occipital cortical regions via the anterior commissure in the majority of CCD participants; however, these same ectopic streamlines were also generated in a comparable number of control participants. The reason this aberrant connection could be generated in such a sizable number of control participants was likely due to the proximity of the anterior commissure to posteriorly projecting white matter tracts, resulting in the generation of spurious connections in the participant tractography. Thus, while our findings cannot preclude the possibility that CCD individuals may possess anterior commissural connectivity profiles that are distinctly different from the neurotypical population, the generation of tracts that are likely artifactual in the control participants means this experiment was unable to provide evidence for or against this theory.

We consequently turned to the results of the whole-brain white matter FA, RD, and ICVF via tract-based spatial statistical (TBSS) analyses to determine whether we could identify microstructural modifications in the neural tracts projecting from the anterior commissure which may indicate neuroplastic changes relating to their connectivity profiles. However, the results of these analyses signify significant decreases in CCD axon densities across multiple white matter tracts, which included the anterior commissure. Again, we were unable to detect any significant change that would suggest that axonal pathways projecting through the anterior commissure formed new or denser connections to bolster interhemispheric integration across the CCD cohort.

Having failed to detect notable group-wise differences in either the neuroanatomical structure or connectivity patterns of the anterior commissure, we sought to determine whether within-group variations in the CCD anterior commissure neuroanatomy correlate with participant performance in cognitive tests of bilateral information integration. In this way, we would test whether individuals with reduced callosal connectivity were more dependent on the anterior commissure for interhemispheric integration than their neurotypical counterparts. To investigate this possibility, we tested whether participant performance on a collection of attentional tasks (i.e., the TelSearch, TelCount, and ElDistract subtests) correlated with either anterior commissure size or axon density. If the interaction terms between group membership and the anterior commissure neuroanatomical features were significant, this would suggest that the performance of the CCD participants on these tasks was dependent on these features of this substrate. Participant performance on highly lateralized cognitive tasks (i.e., the spelling and arithmetic tests) provided a negative control, as the performance of either group on these tasks should exhibit little relation to anterior commissure neuroanatomy. The primary finding of this experiment was that participant performance on the attentional tasks did not correlate with the neuroanatomy of anterior commissure on two of the three attentional tasks (i.e., the TelCount and ElDistract subtests). On the TelSearch subset participant performance did correlate with vin (β = -0.24 (CI −0.49 – 0.02), p = 0.07), however, the Group × vin term did not contribute to the model (β = -0.09 (CI −0.34 – 0.15), p = 0.42). This would imply that bilateral information integration in the CCD cohort was no more dependent on the anterior commissure than it was within the neurotypical individuals.

Based on the results reported above it does not seem plausible that the anterior commissure acts as the primary substrate for compensatory interhemispheric communication in this CCD cohort. Nevertheless, this study has some limitations, chiefly the modest size of the cohort. Due to the rarity of this neurodevelopmental condition, only 16 individuals with CCD were recruited for the current study. With a two-tailed significance threshold of 0.05, the study had a power of 80 % in detecting a very large effect size of 1.02. Consequentially, there would be a substantial risk of one or more type II errors occurring in accepting the null hypothesis, and the findings of this study must be interpreted with caution. We can state, however, that there are individuals with CCD who possess far smaller and/or less axon-dense anterior commissures than is neurotypical; this diminishes the likelihood that the anterior commissure is able to act as an alternative substrate for interhemispheric communication for these specific individuals.

We also acknowledge that because our cohort consisted solely of adults, our findings may not generalize to children and adolescents with CCD. We raise this point due to a recently published study of anterior commissure neuroanatomy in a pediatric CCD cohort (aged 8–17, n = 21) found that this white matter tract had a larger cross-section when compared with neurotypical children (Siffredi, 2019) and that differences in the neuroanatomical properties of the anterior commissure (e.g., increased FA and anterior commissure size) were found to correlate with better performance on certain attentional tasks. Intriguingly, the correlation between the microstructure of the anterior commissure and performance varied with age. This previous finding could indicate a transient overgrowth (or overdevelopment) of the anterior commissure during the early childhood of CCD individuals, to provide better baseline support for bilateral information integration between the brain hemispheres during their formative years, with neurotypically developing individuals achieving similar structural configurations only as they approach adulthood.

Finally, we acknowledge that while the Test of Everyday Attention is useful as a proxy for interhemispheric integration this study may have benefited from the inclusion of functional radiography. This would have provided a means of studying interhemispheric communication directly. It has been previously established that homologous interhemispheric functional connectivity and cortical arealization are broadly preserved in CCD (Tyszka et al., 2011, Owen, 2013), but it is not clear what physical substrates support their synchronous activity. One possible method of investigating the physical basis of interhemispheric functional connectivity, to be explored in future works, would be to use diffusion tractography to determine in which cortical regions the anterior commissure terminates. If the anterior commissure is indeed a primary communicative substrate in CCD, we anticipate that a regression-based analysis would demonstrate a substantive relationship between the neuroanatomical features of the anterior commissure and the degree of functional connectivity between the terminal homologous interhemispheric regions. We also hypothesize that the cortical regions innervated by the anterior commissure would behave as functional connectome hubs that coordinate functional activity across their respective hemispheres; these hubs would demonstrate an increase in their functional connectome participant coefficients (i.e., they participated in a greater number of functional subnetworks), reduced clustering coefficients (i.e., nodal neighbors are more likely to be part of different subnetworks rather than the same subnetwork), increased betweenness centrality (i.e., they are used to mediate communication between non-neighboring nodes) and/or an increase in efficiency and degree centrality (i.e., they are readily accessible to others nodes across the network).

While the results of the current study do not preclude the possibility that the presence of the anterior commissure may assist bilateral information integration in some adults with CCD, they do suggest that it is unlikely that this structure acts as an alternative compensatory pathway for corpus callosum for specific individuals with this condition. This consequently implies that other mechanisms are likely involved in maintaining interhemispheric connectivity. For example, it has been previously noted that this connectivity is consistent with an external subcortical driver of synchronous functional activity (Tyszka et al., 2011, Owen, 2013). Indeed, Uddin and colleagues (Uddin, 2008) have previously demonstrated that an individual who had undergone a complete forebrain commissurotomy still possessed residual bilateral functional connections, which they attributed to subcortical coordination. However, there has not yet been any direct evidence of subcortical structures driving bilateral functional connectivity in individuals with CCD. Consequently, further research will be necessary if the mechanism of interhemispheric communication in the absence of the corpus callosum is to be elucidated.
