The Many-Body Ground State Manifold of Flat Band Interacting Hamiltonian for Magic Angle Twisted Bilayer Graphene
Kevin D. Stubbs, Michael Ragone, Allan H. MacDonald, Lin Lin

Abstract
. At a magic relative twist angle, magic angle twisted bilayer graphene (MATBG) has an octet of flat bands that can host strong correlation physics when partially filled. A key theoretical discovery in MATBG is the existence of ferromagnetic Slater determinants as exact ground states of the corresponding flat band interacting (FBI) Hamiltonian. The FBI Hamiltonian describes the behavior of electrons that interact with each other in a high-dimensional space, and is constructed from the band structure of the non-interacting Bistritzer–MacDonald model at the chiral limit. A key property of the FBI Hamiltonian for MATBG is that it is frustration free and can be written as a sum of non-commuting terms. In this work, we provide a complete characterization of the ground state manifold of the FBI Hamiltonian, proving that it is precisely the linear span of such ferromagnetic Slater determinants.

One of the most remarkable features of the FBI Hamiltonian for MATBG is that the Hamiltonian includes strong long range electron-electron interactions, yet there exist ferromagnetic type Slater determinant states (also called Hartree–Fock states), which are exact ground states of the Hamiltonian

This result is surprising: when electron-electron interactions are strong one would typically expect the ground states are far from being a Slater determinant, such as in the case of Hubbard models.

The FBI Hamiltonian with 8 flat bands (also called the spinful, valleyful model) is relatively complex to study.
Within the 8 flat bands, there are two 4 dimensional subspaces related to each other by the
C_{2z}\mathcal{T}
symmetry, which is a combination of
180^{\circ}

The FBI Hamiltonian commutes with rotations within each of these 4 dimensional subspaces so this model has a large

As a consequence of this symmetry, given any Slater determinant ground state, applying a
\mathsf{U}(4)\times\mathsf{U}(4)
transformation yields another Slater determinant ground state.
Furthermore, any linear combination of these Slater determinant ground states is also a ground state.

To facilitate analysis, simplified models have also been considered in the literature:

(1)

A spinless, valleyless model (also called the single spin, single valley model) that involves only the chiral flavor.

(2)

A spinless, valleyful model (also called the single spin, valleyful model) that includes both chiral and valley flavors.

To the best of our knowledge, it remains an open question whether the ground state manifold of the FBI Hamiltonian is fully spanned by linear combinations of Slater determinant ground states, both in the full model and in the simplified models above.

Contribution

The main result of this paper is to provide an affirmative answer to the question above, which was conjectured in

Theorem 1

(Main Result, informal version of

A many-body state is a ground state of the FBI Hamiltonian for MATBG if and only if it can be written as a linear combination of Slater determinant states, each of which is a ground state of the FBI Hamiltonian.

Our results consist of three main components:

The dimension of the ground state manifold differs in the three settings and we provide an explicit dimension count in all these cases.

It is known that the Slater determinant ground states are

\mathbf{k}
in the Brillouin zone. Consequently, any linear combination of these Slater determinant states must also be uniformly half-filled. Therefore our first step is to establish that any state in the ground state manifold must be uniformly half-filled in all three settings. A key insight is that any ground state must vanish under the application of chiral transfer operators; this imposes strong constraints for a many-body state to be a ground state of the FBI Hamiltonian for MATBG (
Proposition

4

4

Following this result, the characterization of the ground state manifold is straightforward in the spinless, valleyless case since there are only two possible Slater determinant ground states. However, the complexity increases significantly when a continuous “hidden symmetry” is present:
\mathsf{U}(2)\times\mathsf{U}(2)
in the spinless, valleyful case and
\mathsf{U}(4)\times\mathsf{U}(4)

\mathsf{U}(2)\times\mathsf{U}(2)

\mathsf{U}(4)\times\mathsf{U}(4)

The dimension of the ground state manifold then follows directly from the hook length formula

1.2.
Discussion

Our results significantly extend the previous findings in
[
9
,
36
]
, which are the only rigorous results on interacting electrons in MATBG to our knowledge, and focus solely on determining whether non-ferromagnetic Slater determinant ground states can exist. Notably, the techniques employed in
[
9
,
36
]
differ entirely from this work. The proof in
[
9
]
assumes the absence of translation symmetry breaking and relies on an algebraic condition that must be satisfied by all nontrivial orthogonal projectors. While this condition can be computationally challenging to verify in general, it has potential applicability beyond MATBG. In contrast,
[
36
]
introduces a more computationally tractable condition specifically for MATBG, leveraging a set of Sylvester equations to explicitly rule out solutions that break translation symmetry. Neither of these approaches is applicable in this work for characterizing the many-body ground state manifold.
It is also worth noting that our theoretical results align with previous numerical studies, which have shown that the ground states of MATBG are well described by Slater determinant states well beyond this idealized setting
[
17
,
35
,
15
,
23
,
12
]
.

In the spinless, valleyless case, where the only degree of freedom is a chirality pseudospin, we prove that the system is essentially an Ising ferromagnet along the chiral axis. Although one might expect, on heuristic grounds, that an interaction term with chirality-dependent form factors would select the fully polarized states, it is highly nontrivial to rule out the existence of any other degenerate many-body states. When valley and spin degrees of freedom are included, the situation becomes even richer. We prove that these degrees of freedom do not introduce any additional “magnetic” type anisotropy beyond that already present in the chirality sector. The study of the many-body magnetic anisotropy beyond the chiral model is an interesting direction for future research.

Our proof of
Proposition

4
encounters subtle difficulties at the thermodynamic limit. Specifically, to prove
Proposition

4
, we show that a certain linear transformation is invertible. However, with our current techniques, this argument breaks down in the thermodynamic limit even in the spinless, valleyless model, suggesting the possibility of new emergent behavior that is not captured by the present theory. We note that the vanishing neutral excitation gap is not solely due to the presence of Goldstone modes, which correspond to zero-energy excitations arising from continuous symmetry
[
10
]
.

Another important open question is understanding the energy gap between the ground state manifold and the remaining states, both in neutral excitations and in few-particle excitations. Recent studies have suggested the existence of a family of skyrmionic states, whose pairing could give rise to superconducting behavior
[
15
,
23
]
. While neutral excitations are crucial for assessing the stability of Slater determinant ground states, few-particle excitations can provide deeper insight into the superconductivity observed away from integer fillings.

2.1.
Chiral, Valley, and Spin Degrees of Freedom

The Hamiltonian in
Eq.

1

(5)

\begin{bmatrix}I_{2\times 2}&\\
&-I_{2\times 2}\end{bmatrix}H(\alpha)\begin{bmatrix}I_{2\times 2}&\\
&-I_{2\times 2}\end{bmatrix}=-H(\alpha).

Thus we can choose the eigenstates corresponding to zero eigenvalues of

\diag{(1,1,-1,-1)}

In particular, the eigenfunctions of

can be written as
\begin{bmatrix}w(\mathbf{r})&0\end{bmatrix}^{\top}

\begin{bmatrix}0&v(\mathbf{r})\end{bmatrix}^{\top}

This partitions the zero energy subspace of

into two classes referred to as the two “chiral” sectors of MATBG.
By

1

H(\alpha)
has two flat bands so we can subdivide this two dimensional subspace into two 1 dimensional subspaces using chiral symmetry.
Furthermore,
H(\alpha)
commutes with an additional symmetry
C_{2z}\mathcal{T}
, which a combination of
180^{\circ}
in-plane rotation and spinless time reversal symmetry. Algebraically, the
C_{2z}\mathcal{T}
symmetry acts on

(6)

C_{2z}\mathcal{T}\begin{bmatrix}w(\mathbf{r})\\[2.15277pt]
v(\mathbf{r})\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\overline{v(-\mathbf{r})}\\[2.15277pt]
\overline{w(-\mathbf{r})}\end{bmatrix}.

In particular, the
C_{2z}\mathcal{T}
symmetry maps the two chiral sectors onto each another.

A physically complete description of MATBG has two additional degrees of freedom which are not included in the definition of
H(\alpha)
: valley and spin.
To understand the valley degree of freedom, recall that
Eq.

1
is derived by performing a low energy expansion near one of the Dirac points of the monolayer graphene and setting some of the hopping coefficients to zero

37
]
.
However, monolayer graphene has two Dirac points so we could have equally well performed an expansion about the other Dirac point.
We refer to the expansion about the first Dirac point as the first valley and the second Dirac point as the second valley.
Since the two valleys are related by time reversal symmetry, the corresponding Hamiltonian which expands about both valleys takes the form:

(7)

H^{(v)}(\alpha)=\begin{bmatrix}H(\alpha)&\\
&\overline{H(\alpha)}\end{bmatrix}.

Note that the spectral properties of
H^{(v)}(\alpha)
are completely determined by
H(\alpha)
and that under
Assumption

1
,
H^{(v)}(\alpha)
has four flat bands which are further subdivided into two 2 dimensional chiral subspaces related by
C_{2z}\mathcal{T}
.

To understand the spin degree of freedom, we recall that electrons are spin-1/2 particles and have two spin states
\uparrow
or
\downarrow
.
Experiments have shown that spin-orbit coupling in graphene is extremely small so we may neglect the dependence on spin
[
20
,
2
]
.
Hence, the non-interacting Hamiltonian including both valley and spin takes the form:

(8)

H^{(v,s)}(\alpha)=\begin{bmatrix}H(\alpha)&\\
&\overline{H(\alpha)}\end{bmatrix}\otimes I_{2\times 2}

where the additional tensor product with identity is due to the inclusion of spin.
Similar to
H^{(v)}(\alpha)
, the spectral properties
H^{(v,s)}(\alpha)
are completely determined by
H(\alpha)
and under
Assumption

1
,
H^{(v,s)}(\alpha)
has eight flat bands which are further subdivided into two 4 dimensional chiral subspaces which are related by
C_{2z}\mathcal{T}
.

In the interacting model, valley and spin degrees of freedom significantly enhance the ground state degeneracy.
In the following sections, we will analyze three important cases: the single valley case, the valleyful case, and the valleyful and spinful case.
These cases correspond to
H(\alpha)
,
H^{(v)}(\alpha)
,

respectively.

Flat band interacting model

The flat band interacting model (FBI) Hamiltonian models the chiral MATBG with electron-electron interactions, which can be defined using second quantization in momentum space.

3.1.
Form Factor of the Spinless, Valleyless Model

While the FBI Hamiltonian is defined with an orthogonal basis
\{u_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r}):n\in\mathcal{N},\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}\}
, the choice of this orthogonal basis is arbitrary so we could also define this FBI Hamiltonian using a different orthogonal basis.
This observation amounts to rotating each creation and annihilation operator by a family of unitaries
\{U(\mathbf{k})\}_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}\subset\mathsf{U}(\#|\mathcal{N}|)
as follows:

(16)

\hat{f}_{m\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\mapsto\sum\hat{f}_{n\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}[U(\mathbf{k})]_{nm}\qquad\hat{f}_{m\mathbf{k}}\mapsto\sum\hat{f}_{n\mathbf{k}}[\overline{U(\mathbf{k})}]_{nm}.

Under this basis change the form factor transforms as
\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\mapsto U(\mathbf{k})\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})U(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q})
and one can verify that
\hat{H}_{FBI}
remains unchanged.
In the physics context, this freedom in the basis choice is known as a “gauge freedom” and a choice of rotation is known as a “choice of gauge”.

As proven in
[

and all
\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
the form factor can be written as:

(17)

\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})=\begin{bmatrix}a_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})&\\
&\overline{a_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})}\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{where}\quad a_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\in\mathbb{C}.

Additionally, in this choice of gauge for all
\mathbf{G}\in\Gamma^{*}

(18)

\overline{a_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{G})}=a_{-\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{G}).

Under the choice of gauge from
Proposition

1
, we label the two flat bands as
\mathcal{N}=\{+,-\}
which we refer to the
+
band and the
-
band.
In this choice of gauge,
\widehat{\rho}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})
(
Eq.

14
) can be written in a particularly simple form:

(19)

\widehat{\rho}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}a_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\left(\hat{f}_{+\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\hat{f}_{+(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q})}-\frac{1}{2}\delta_{\mathbf{q}\in\Gamma^{*}}\right)+\overline{a_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})}\left(\hat{f}_{-\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\hat{f}_{-(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q})}-\frac{1}{2}\delta_{\mathbf{q}\in\Gamma^{*}}\right).

The choice of gauge in
Proposition

1
is also referred to as the sublattice gauge in the literature because the two Bloch eigenfunctions
u_{+\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})
and
u_{-\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})
are supported on the A and B sublattices respectively.

3.2.
Form Factor for with Valley and Spin Degrees of Freedom

Following the setup
[
12
]
, the form factor also can be written in diagonal form when we include the valley and spin degrees of freedom.
In particular, since the two valleys are related by time reversal symmetry, when we include the valley degree of freedom the form factor takes the form

(20)

\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}^{(v)}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})=\begin{bmatrix}a_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})&&&\\
&\overline{a_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})}&&\\
&&\overline{a_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})}\\
&&&a_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\\
\end{bmatrix}

where the first term on the diagonal corresponds to the
+
band in the
K
valley, the second term corresponds to the
-
band in the
K
valley, the third
(+,K^{\prime})
, and the fourth
(-,K^{\prime})
.

Identifying
K=+1
and
K^{\prime}=-1
, we can generate all many body states applying the following creation operators on the vacuum state:

(21)

\Big\{\hat{f}_{(m,\tau),\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}:m\in\{+,-\},~\tau\in\{+,-\},~\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}\Big\}.

When we include both the valley and spin degrees of freedom, the form factor can be written

(22)

\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}^{(v,s)}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})=\begin{bmatrix}a_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})&&&\\
&\overline{a_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})}&&\\
&&\overline{a_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})}\\
&&&a_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\\
\end{bmatrix}\otimes I_{2\times 2}.

The corresponding set of generating creation operators is given by

(23)

\Big\{\hat{f}_{(m,\tau,s),\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}:m\in\{+,-\},~\tau\in\{+,-\},~s\in\{\uparrow,\downarrow\},~\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}\Big\}

where
m
indexes the bands,
\tau
indexes the valley, and
s
indexes spin.

In all cases, the form factor
\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})
is diagonal whose non-zero entries are either
a_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})
or
\overline{a_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})}
.
We refer to the terms multiplied by
a_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})
as the “
+
chiral sector” and the terms multiplied by
\overline{a_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})}
as the “
-
chiral sector”.
Note that when including the valley degree of freedom, the creation operators for the
+
chiral sector have
m\tau=+1
and the creation operators for the
-
chiral sector have
m\tau=-1
.

To unify the FBI model in three cases, we define the
chiral transfer operator

\hat{C}_{\pm,\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}
for any
\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\in\Omega^{*}
as follows:

(24)

\begin{split}\hat{C}_{\pm,\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}&=\hat{f}_{\pm,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\hat{f}_{\pm,\mathbf{k}^{\prime}},\\[4.30554pt]
\hat{C}_{\pm,\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{(v)}&=\sum_{m\tau=\pm}\hat{f}_{(m,\tau),\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\hat{f}_{(m,\tau),\mathbf{k}^{\prime}},\\
\hat{C}_{\pm,\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{(v,s)}&=\sum_{s\in\{\uparrow,\downarrow\}}\sum_{m\tau=\pm}\hat{f}_{(m,\tau,s),\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\hat{f}_{(m,\tau,s),\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}.\end{split}

The special case
\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{k}^{\prime}
is particularly important so we define the special notation
\hat{n}_{\pm,\mathbf{k}}:=\hat{C}_{\pm,\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}}
which we refer to as the
chiral number operators
.
The operators
\hat{n}_{\pm,\mathbf{k}}^{(v)}
and
\hat{n}_{\pm,\mathbf{k}}^{(v,s)}
are defined analogously.

For reasons which will be made clear in
Corollary

1
, we define the integer
N_{occ}
where
N_{occ}=1
for the spinless, valleyless model,
N_{occ}=2
for the spinless, valleyful model, and
N_{occ}=4
for the full model.
With these definitions and suppressing the superscripts on the chiral transfer operators,
\widehat{\rho}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})
in all cases simplifies to

(25)

\widehat{\rho}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}a_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\left(\hat{C}_{+,\mathbf{k},(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime})}-\frac{1}{2}N_{occ}\,\delta_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\in\Gamma^{*}}\right)+\overline{a_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})}\left(\hat{C}_{-,\mathbf{k},(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime})}-\frac{1}{2}N_{occ}\,\delta_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\in\Gamma^{*}}\right).

This unified form of
\widehat{\rho}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})
allows us to analyze the ground states of
\hat{H}_{FBI}
in all three cases simultaneously.

In summary, the FBI Hamiltonian for the chiral MATBG in
Eq.

13
with
\widehat{\rho}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})
defined in
Eq.

25
is called

(1)

Spinless, valleyless if
\hat{C}_{\pm,\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}=\hat{f}_{\pm,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\hat{f}_{\pm,\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}
and
N_{occ}=1
;

(2)

Spinless, valleyful if
\hat{C}_{\pm,\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}=\sum_{m\tau=\pm}\hat{f}_{(m,\tau),\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\hat{f}_{(m,\tau),\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}
and
N_{occ}=2
;

(3)

Spinful, valleyful if
\hat{C}_{\pm,\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}=\sum_{s\in\{\uparrow,\downarrow\}}\sum_{m\tau=\pm}\hat{f}_{(m,\tau,s),\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\hat{f}_{(m,\tau,s),\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}
and
N_{occ}=4
.

4.
Properties of the Many-Body Ground States

Before proving the main result in
Sections

5
and
6
, let us discuss a few properties of the many-body ground states of
\hat{H}_{FBI}
shared in all of the three cases.
The Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{FBI}
(
13
) has the following three properties:

(1)

\hat{H}_{FBI}
can be written as a sum of terms
\hat{H}_{FBI}=\sum_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}H({\mathbf{q}}^{\prime})
. The terms
\{H({\mathbf{q}}^{\prime})\}
do not commute with each other and cannot be simultaneously diagonalized.

(2)

Each term
H({\mathbf{q}}^{\prime})
is a positive semidefinite operator. Therefore
\hat{H}_{FBI}
is also positive semidefinite.

(3)

There exist many-body states,
\ket{\Phi}
, such that
H({\mathbf{q}}^{\prime})\ket{\Phi}=0
for all
{\mathbf{q}}^{\prime}
. This implies
\hat{H}_{FBI}\ket{\Phi}=0
.

A Hamiltonian that satisfies properties (2) and (3) is called
frustration-free
. Combined with (1), this means that
\hat{H}_{FBI}
is a non-commuting, frustration-free Hamiltonian.

The structure of the FBI Hamiltonian (
13
) further implies that
\ket{\Phi}
is a ground state if and only if
\widehat{\rho}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\ket{\Phi}=0
for all
\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}+\Gamma^{*}
since

(26)

\braket{\Phi|\hat{H}_{FBI}|\Phi}=\sum_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}\hat{V}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\|\widehat{\rho}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\ket{\Phi}\|^{2}

and
V(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})>0
for all
\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Frustration-freeness plays a central role in the characterization of the ground states.

As a first consequence of frustration-freeness, we recall the per-
\mathbf{k}
number operator,
\hat{N}_{\mathbf{k}}
, and the total number operator,
\hat{N}
, which can be defined in terms of the chiral number operators,
\hat{n}_{\pm,\mathbf{k}}
as follows

(27)

\hat{N}_{\mathbf{k}}=\hat{n}_{+,\mathbf{k}}+\hat{n}_{-,\mathbf{k}},\quad\hat{N}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}\hat{N}_{\mathbf{k}}.

For
\mathbf{q}^{\prime}=\mathbf{0}
, it’s easily verified that

(28)

\widehat{\rho}(\mathbf{0})=\left(\hat{N}-N_{occ}N_{\mathbf{k}}\right).

Therefore, the frustration-freeness of
\hat{H}_{FBI}
immediately implies the following proposition:

Proposition 2
.

A many-body state
\ket{\Phi}
is a ground state of
\hat{H}_{FBI}
only if
\hat{N}\ket{\Phi}=N_{occ}N_{\mathbf{k}}\ket{\Phi}
.

While
Proposition

2
holds for all frustration-free FBI Hamiltonians, the specific properties of form factors for chiral MATBG allows us to prove something significantly stronger:

Proposition 3
.

If
\ket{\Phi}
is a many-body ground state of
\hat{H}_{FBI}
then
\ket{\Phi}
may be decomposed as

(29)

\ket{\Phi}=\sum_{\ell}c_{\ell}\ket{\Phi^{(\ell)}},

where the states
\ket{\Phi^{(\ell)}}
are joint eigenfunctions of
\{\hat{n}_{\pm,\mathbf{k}}:\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}\}
satisfying the following for all
\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}

(30)

\begin{split}\hat{n}_{+,\mathbf{k}}\ket{\Phi^{(\ell)}}&=\lambda_{+}^{(\ell)}\ket{\Phi^{(\ell)}}\\[2.15277pt]
\hat{n}_{-,\mathbf{k}}\ket{\Phi^{(\ell)}}&=\lambda_{-}^{(\ell)}\ket{\Phi^{(\ell)}}.\end{split}

The important content of
Proposition

3
is not only that the ground state can be decomposed as joint eigenfunctions of
\hat{n}_{\pm,\mathbf{k}}
, but that the eigenvalues
\lambda_{\pm}^{(\ell)}
are
independent
of
\mathbf{k}
.
An immediate corollary of this result is that any many-body ground state
\ket{\Phi}
is uniformly filled:

Corollary 1
.

If
\ket{\Phi}
is a many-body ground state of
\hat{H}_{FBI}
then
\hat{N}_{\mathbf{k}}\ket{\Phi}=N_{occ}\ket{\Phi}
for all
\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}
.
Hence
N_{occ}
is the number of electrons which are occupied at each momentum
\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\ket{\Phi}
is a ground state and has a decomposition as in
Eq.

29
.
We may assume without loss of generality that this decomposition is chosen so that each term is mutually orthogonal (i.e.
\braket{\Phi^{(\ell)},\Phi^{(\ell^{\prime})}}=\delta_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}
).
For each
\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}
, we have that

(31)

\hat{N}_{\mathbf{k}}\ket{\Phi^{(\ell)}}=\left(\hat{n}_{+,\mathbf{k}}+\hat{n}_{-,\mathbf{k}}\right)\ket{\Phi^{(\ell)}}=(\lambda_{+}^{(\ell)}+\lambda_{-}^{(\ell)})\ket{\Phi^{(\ell)}}.

Therefore

(32)

\hat{N}\ket{\Phi}=\left(\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\hat{N}_{\mathbf{k}}\right)\left(\sum_{\ell}c_{\ell}\ket{\Phi^{(\ell)}}\right)=\sum_{\ell}c_{\ell}\left(\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}(\lambda_{+}^{(\ell)}+\lambda_{-}^{(\ell)})\ket{\Phi^{(\ell)}}\right).

Since
\ket{\Phi^{(\ell)}}
are orthogonal and
\hat{N}\ket{\Phi}=N_{occ}N_{\mathbf{k}}\ket{\Phi}
, it follows that
\lambda_{+}^{(\ell)}+\lambda_{-}^{(\ell)}=N_{occ}
for all
\ell
proving the result.
∎

The decomposition in
Proposition

3
is a key part of the characterization of the many-body ground states.
Since the states
\ket{\Phi^{(\ell)}}
can be chosen to have different
\hat{n}_{\pm,\mathbf{k}}
eigenvalues, we can divide the analysis into studying the different eigenspaces of
\hat{n}_{\pm,\mathbf{k}}
independently.

The key observation which simplifies analyzing these eigenspaces is that both
\hat{C}_{\pm,\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}
commute with a
\mathsf{U}(N_{occ})

action within the
+
chiral sector and a
\mathsf{U}(N_{occ})
action within the
-
chiral sector.
Explicitly, in the valleyful case, for any
U\in\mathsf{U}(N_{occ})
if we rotate the creation operators by
U
, that is we define

(33)

\hat{g}_{(m,\tau),\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}=\sum_{m^{\prime}\tau^{\prime}=+}\hat{f}_{(m^{\prime},\tau^{\prime}),\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}[U]_{(m^{\prime},\tau^{\prime}),(m,\tau)}

then we have

(34)

\begin{split}\hat{C}_{+,\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}&=\sum_{m\tau=+}\hat{f}_{(m,\tau),\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\hat{f}_{(m,\tau),\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\\
&=\sum_{m\tau=+}\left(\sum_{m^{\prime}\tau^{\prime}=+}\hat{g}_{(m^{\prime},\tau^{\prime}),\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}[\overline{U}]_{(m^{\prime},\tau^{\prime}),(m,\tau)}\right)\left(\sum_{m^{\prime\prime}\tau^{\prime\prime}=+}\hat{g}_{(m^{\prime\prime},\tau^{\prime\prime}),\mathbf{k}}[U]_{(m^{\prime\prime},\tau^{\prime\prime}),(m,\tau)}\right)\\
&=\sum_{m\tau=+}\hat{g}_{(m,\tau),\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\hat{g}_{(m,\tau),\mathbf{k}}\end{split}

where in the last line we have used that when
U
is a unitary
\sum_{\alpha}[U]_{\beta\alpha}[\overline{U}]_{\gamma\alpha}=\delta_{\beta\gamma}
.
Similar calculations show that in all cases the chiral transfer operators,
\hat{C}_{\pm,\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}
, commute with basis transformation within each chiral sector from
\mathsf{U}(N_{occ})\times\mathsf{U}(N_{occ})
.

With this observation, one can easily check that
\widehat{\rho}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})
also commutes with this
\mathsf{U}(N_{occ})\times\mathsf{U}(N_{occ})
action and therefore the ground state manifold is an irreducible representation of the group
\mathsf{U}(N_{occ})\times\mathsf{U}(N_{occ})
.
This
\mathsf{U}(N_{occ})\times\mathsf{U}(N_{occ})
freedom in the ground state is known as the “hidden symmetry” of chiral MATBG in the physics literature
[
12
]
.
Once we restrict to a specific eigenspace
\lambda_{+}
, we can use the representation theory of
\mathsf{U}(N_{occ})\times\mathsf{U}(N_{occ})
to show that that all ground states lie in the irreducible representation generated by the Hartree-Fock ground states proving
Theorem

1
.

As a first step, we will derive a necessary and sufficient condition for a many-body state
\ket{\Phi}
to be a ground state in
Section

5
.
This condition will almost immediately imply
Proposition

3
.
Using this condition, we will then study the representation theory of the ground state manifold in
Section

6
.

5.
An Equivalent Characterization of the Ground States

The main result of this section is to prove the following equivalent condition for a state
\ket{\Phi}
to be a ground state:

Proposition 4
.

For the spinless, valleyless FBI model of MATBG, a many-body state
\ket{\Phi}
satisfies
\hat{H}_{FBI}\ket{\Phi}=0
if and only if
\ket{\Phi}
is half filled and for all
\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}

(35)

\hat{C}_{\pm,\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\ket{\Phi}=0\quad\text{whenever}\quad\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\not\in\Gamma^{*}.

where
\hat{C}_{\pm,\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}
is as defined in
Eq.

24
.
The same equivalence is true for valleyful MATBG and valleyful and spinful MATBG if we replace the
\hat{C}
operators with
\hat{C}^{(v)}
and
\hat{C}^{(v,s)}
respectively.

To simplify the notation in our proof, we will henceforth suppress the superscripts on the
\hat{C}
operators; the arguments we use to prove
Proposition

4
will be independent of which case we consider.
The requirement that every ground state
\ket{\Phi}
must lie in the kernel of
\hat{C}_{\pm,\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}
for all pairs of momenta implies that the ground state must be composed to states which uniformly fill each chiral sector:

Lemma 1
.

Suppose that
\ket{\Phi}
is a many-body state which satisfies
Eq.

35
.
Then for all
\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}

(36)

\Big(\hat{n}_{\pm,\mathbf{k}}-\hat{n}_{\pm,\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\Big)\ket{\Phi}=0.

Proof.

The conclusion of the lemma is trivial if
\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\in\Gamma^{*}
so we only need to consider
\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\not\in\Gamma^{*}
.
By the CAR for all
\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}

(37)

[\hat{C}_{\pm,\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}},\hat{C}_{\pm,\mathbf{k}^{\prime},\mathbf{k}}]=\hat{C}_{\pm,\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}}-\hat{C}_{\pm,\mathbf{k}^{\prime},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}=\hat{n}_{\pm,\mathbf{k}}-\hat{n}_{\pm,\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}.

But for any
\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\not\in\Gamma^{*}

(38)

[\hat{C}_{\pm,\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}},\hat{C}_{\pm,\mathbf{k}^{\prime},\mathbf{k}}]\ket{\Phi}=\bigg(\hat{C}_{\pm,\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\hat{C}_{\pm,\mathbf{k}^{\prime},\mathbf{k}}-\hat{C}_{\pm,\mathbf{k}^{\prime},\mathbf{k}}\hat{C}_{\pm,\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\bigg)\ket{\Phi}=0

where the last equality is due to
Eq.

35
which proves the lemma.
∎

This lemma implies
Proposition

3
since the set of operators
\{\hat{n}_{\chi,\mathbf{k}}:\chi\in\{+,-\},~\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}\}
are self-adjoint and commuting. So any many-body state may be orthogonally decomposed into a sum of joint eigenvectors.

We begin by showing that
Eq.

35
is sufficient for
\ket{\Phi}
to be a ground state in
Section

5.1
.
To prove
Eq.

35
is also necessary for
\ket{\Phi}
to be a ground state, we split the analysis into two steps: considering
\widehat{\rho}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})
where
\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\in\Gamma^{*}
, which we refer to as the “on lattice terms”, and
\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\not\in\Gamma^{*}
, which we refer to as the “off lattice terms”.
Due to frustration-freeness, every ground state must satisfy
\widehat{\rho}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\ket{\Phi}=0
.
Using the on lattice terms, we will show that every ground state must be a joint eigenvector of a family of self-adjoint commuting operators with a fixed eigenvalue.
Using this joint eigenvector property combined with the frustration-freeness of the off lattice terms, will then prove
Proposition

4
.
We consider the on lattice terms in
Section

5.2
and the off lattice terms in
Section

5.3
.

5.1.
Proof that
Equation

35
is Sufficient

Suppose that
\ket{\Phi}
is a many-body state which is half-filled and satisfies
Eqs.

36
and
35
.
When
\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\not\in\Gamma^{*}
, the operator
\widehat{\rho}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})
applied to
\ket{\Phi}
evaluates to

(39)

\widehat{\rho}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\ket{\Phi}=\left(\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}a_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\hat{C}_{+,\mathbf{k},(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime})}+\overline{a_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})}\hat{C}_{-,\mathbf{k},(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime})}\right)\ket{\Phi}=0

where the final equality is because
\mathbf{k}-(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime})=-\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\not\in\Gamma^{*}
and
Eq.

35
.
Therefore, to prove
\ket{\Phi}
is a ground state we only need to show that
\widehat{\rho}(\mathbf{G})\ket{\Phi}=0
for all
\mathbf{G}\in\Gamma^{*}
.

As noted above, the family of operators
\{\hat{n}_{c,\mathbf{k}}:c\in\{+,-\},\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}\}
are all self-adjoint and mutually commuting, we may decompose the state
\ket{\Phi}
into a sum of joint eigenvectors of this family as follows:

(40)

\begin{split}\ket{\Phi}&=\sum_{\ell}c_{\ell}\ket{\Psi^{(\ell)}},\quad\text{where}\\
&\hat{n}_{\pm,\mathbf{k}}\ket{\Psi^{(\ell)}}=\lambda_{\pm}^{(\ell)}\ket{\Psi^{(\ell)}}.\end{split}

Following the reasoning in the proof of
Corollary

1
, for all
\ell
,
\lambda_{+}^{(\ell)}+\lambda_{-}^{(\ell)}=N_{occ}
.

For each
\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\in\Gamma^{*}
, we may apply
\widehat{\rho}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})
to each
\ket{\Psi^{(\ell)}}
and we have that

(41)

\begin{split}\widehat{\rho}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\ket{\Psi^{(\ell)}}&=\left[\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}a_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\left(\hat{n}_{+,\mathbf{k}}-\frac{1}{2}N_{occ}\right)+\overline{a_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})}\left(\hat{n}_{-,\mathbf{k}}-\frac{1}{2}N_{occ}\right)\right]\ket{\Psi^{(\ell)}}\\[4.30554pt]
&=\left[\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}a_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\left(\lambda_{+}^{(\ell)}-\frac{1}{2}N_{occ}\right)+\overline{a_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})}\left(\lambda_{-}^{(\ell)}-\frac{1}{2}N_{occ}\right)\right]\ket{\Psi^{(\ell)}}\\
&=\left[\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}\operatorname{Re}{(a_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime}))}\left(\lambda_{+}^{(\ell)}+\lambda_{-}^{(\ell)}-N_{occ}\right)+\operatorname{Im}{(a_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime}))}\left(\lambda_{+}^{(\ell)}-\lambda_{-}^{(\ell)}\right)\right]\ket{\Psi^{(\ell)}}.\end{split}

The first term vanishes since
\lambda_{+}^{(\ell)}+\lambda_{-}^{(\ell)}=N_{occ}
and so we conclude that

(42)

\widehat{\rho}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\ket{\Psi^{(\ell)}}=\left(\lambda_{+}^{(\ell)}-\lambda_{-}^{(\ell)}\right)\left(\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}\operatorname{Im}{(a_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime}))}\right)\ket{\Psi^{(\ell)}}.

To show that the above expression vanishes, we recall that due to the choice of gauge
a_{-\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{G})=\overline{a_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{G})}
for all
\mathbf{G}\in\Gamma^{*}
(
Eq.

18
).
Since the grid
\mathcal{K}
(
Eq.

12
) is closed under inversion,
\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}\operatorname{Im}{(a_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime}))}=0
for all
\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\in\Gamma^{*}
.
Hence, for all
\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\in\Gamma^{*}
,
\widehat{\rho}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\ket{\Phi}=0
and so
\ket{\Phi}
is a ground state.

From the above calculation, we have shown that any half-filled many-body state
\ket{\Phi}
which satisfies
Eq.

36
is a ground state.
In the next section, we begin our proof that these conditions are also necessary.

5.2.
On Lattice Terms,
\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\in\Gamma^{*}

For
\mathbf{q}^{\prime}=\mathbf{G}\in\Gamma^{*}
, we can use a property of the gauge choice (
Eq.

18
) to simplify
Eq.

25
.
In particular, we have

(43)

\begin{split}\widehat{\rho}(\mathbf{G})&=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}a_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{G})\left(\hat{n}_{+,\mathbf{k}}-\frac{1}{2}N_{occ}\right)+\overline{a_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{G})}\left(\hat{n}_{-,\mathbf{k}}-\frac{1}{2}N_{occ}\right)\\
&=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}a_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{G})\left(\hat{n}_{+,\mathbf{k}}-\frac{1}{2}N_{occ}\right)+a_{-\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{G})\left(\hat{n}_{-,\mathbf{k}}-\frac{1}{2}N_{occ}\right)\\
&=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}a_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{G})\left(\hat{n}_{+,\mathbf{k}}+\hat{n}_{-,(-\mathbf{k})}-N_{occ}\right).\end{split}

where to get the third line, we have performed the change of variables
\mathbf{k}\mapsto-\mathbf{k}
.

For a state
\ket{\Phi}
to be annihilated by
\widehat{\rho}(\mathbf{G})
, the following condition is sufficient:

(44)

\left(\hat{n}_{+,\mathbf{k}}+\hat{n}_{-,(-\mathbf{k})}-N_{occ}\right)\ket{\Phi}=0\qquad\forall\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}.

The main goal in this section is to show that
Eq.

44
is in fact
necessary
to be a ground state.

Proposition 5
.

A many-body state satisfies
\widehat{\rho}(\mathbf{G})\ket{\Phi}=0
for all
\mathbf{G}\in\Gamma^{*}
if and only if it is a joint eigenvector of the family of operators
\{\hat{n}_{+,\mathbf{k}}+\hat{n}_{-,(-\mathbf{k})}:\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}\}
with eigenvalue
N_{occ}
.

Before proving
Proposition

5
, we remark that the operators
\hat{n}_{+,\mathbf{k}}+\hat{n}_{-,(-\mathbf{k}),(-\mathbf{k})}
will appear many times during our proof so we define the shorthand

(45)

\hat{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{k}}:=\hat{n}_{+,\mathbf{k}}+\hat{n}_{-,(-\mathbf{k})}.

Proof of
Proposition

5
.

Any
\ket{\Phi}
satisfying
Eq.

44
implies
\widehat{\rho}(\mathbf{G})\ket{\Phi}=0
for all
\mathbf{G}\in\Gamma^{*}
. So we only need to prove the converse.
If
\ket{\Phi}
is a ground state then for all
\mathbf{G}\in\Gamma^{*}
:

(46)

\widehat{\rho}(\mathbf{G})\ket{\Phi}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}a_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{G})\left(\hat{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{k}}-N_{occ}\right)\ket{\Phi}=0.

The key observation is the following: Suppose we fix some complete basis for all many-body states,
\{\ket{\Psi^{(\nu)}}\}_{\nu}
, where
\nu
runs over an arbitrary indexing set.
If we take the inner product with
\ket{\Psi^{(\nu)}}
on both sides of
Eq.

46
then it must be that for all
\nu
:

(47)

\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}a_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{G})\braket{\Psi^{(\nu)},\left(\hat{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{k}}-N_{occ}\right)\Phi}=0.

However, the sum over
\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}
can now be viewed as an inner product between two vectors whose entries are indexed by
\mathbf{k}
.
Since
Eq.

47
must be true for all
\mathbf{G}\in\Gamma^{*}
, if we can show the infinite matrix

(48)

\begin{bmatrix}a_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}(\mathbf{G}_{1})&a_{\mathbf{k}_{2}}(\mathbf{G}_{1})&\cdots&a_{\mathbf{k}_{N_{\mathbf{k}}}}(\mathbf{G}_{1})\\
a_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}(\mathbf{G}_{2})&a_{\mathbf{k}_{2}}(\mathbf{G}_{2})&\cdots&a_{\mathbf{k}_{N_{\mathbf{k}}}}(\mathbf{G}_{2})\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\end{bmatrix}

is full rank, then we can conclude that for all
\nu

(49)

\braket{\Psi^{(\nu)},\left(\hat{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{k}}-N_{occ}\right)\Phi}=0\qquad\forall\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}

which implies
Proposition

5
since
\{\ket{\Psi^{(\nu)}}\}_{\nu}
forms a complete basis set.
We defer the proof of the following technical lemma, which implies
Proposition

5
, to
Appendix

A
:

Lemma 2
.

Let
a_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{G})
be the entries of the form factor as in
Proposition

1
.
For any finite set
\mathcal{K}\subseteq\Omega^{*}
and any
\{c_{\mathbf{k}}\}_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}\subseteq\mathbb{C}

(50)

\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}a_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{G})c_{\mathbf{k}}=0\quad\forall\mathbf{G}\in\Gamma^{*}\quad\text{if and only if}\quad c_{\mathbf{k}}=0\quad\forall\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}.

∎

5.3.
Off Lattice Terms,
\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\not\in\Gamma^{*}

The main goal in this section is to prove the following proposition which combined with
Proposition

2
immediately implies the equivalent characterization (
Proposition

4
:

Proposition 6
.

Let
\ket{\Phi}
be any state which is a joint eigenstate of
\{\hat{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{k}}\}_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}
and satisfies
\widehat{\rho}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\ket{\Phi}=0
for all
\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\in(\mathcal{K}+\Gamma^{*})\setminus\Gamma^{*}
.
For all
\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}
with
\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\not\in\Gamma^{*}
,
\ket{\Phi}
also satisfies

(51)

\hat{C}_{\pm,\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\ket{\Phi}=0.

Towards proving
Proposition

6
, let us recall that in the previous section we have shown that every ground state
\ket{\Phi}
must be a joint eigenvector of the family
\{\hat{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{k}}\}_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}
which are all self-adjoint and mutually commuting.
To make use of this property, let us recall the following basic fact from linear algebra:

Lemma 3
.

Suppose that
v
is a joint eigenvector
of two matrices
A
and
B
, then
[A,B]v=0
.

As a consequence of this lemma, if
\ket{\Phi}
is a ground state (and so
\widehat{\rho}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\ket{\Phi}=0
) then it must be that for all
\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}

(52)

\Big[\hat{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{k}},~\widehat{\rho}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\Big]\ket{\Phi}=0.

Since
\hat{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{k}}
only involves two momenta
\mathbf{k}
and
-\mathbf{k}
, for any
\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\in(\mathcal{K}+\Gamma^{*})\setminus\Gamma^{*}
one can check that the commutator
[\hat{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{k}},\widehat{\rho}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})]
only involves
4
terms.
We can further reduce this to
2
terms by considering the double commutator
[\hat{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime}},[\hat{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{k}},\widehat{\rho}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})]]
.
In particular, a straightforward calculation (see
Appendix

B
) shows that

(53)

\Bigg[\hat{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime}},~\Big[\hat{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{k}},~\widehat{\rho}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\Big]\Bigg]=a_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\hat{C}_{+,\mathbf{k},(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime})}-\overline{a_{-\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})}\hat{C}_{-,(-\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}^{\prime}),(-\mathbf{k})}

and due to
Lemma

3
every ground state must be annihilated by the above operator.

It turns out we can further simplify the above condition, by noticing that the outcome of applying these two chiral transfer operators (
\hat{C}_{+,\mathbf{k},(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime})}
and
\hat{C}_{-,(-\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}^{\prime}),(-\mathbf{k})}
) must be orthogonal and so any ground state must be annihilated by these two terms separately.

Lemma 4
.

If
\ket{\Phi}
is a many-body ground state which is a joint eigenvector of
\{\hat{N}_{\mathbf{k}}\}_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}
and
\widehat{\rho}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\ket{\Phi}=0
for all
\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\in(\mathcal{K}+\Gamma^{*})\setminus\Gamma^{*}
, then for all
\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}
and all
\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\in(\mathcal{K}+\Gamma^{*})\setminus\Gamma^{*}

(54)

\begin{split}a_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\hat{C}_{+,\mathbf{k},(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime})}\ket{\Phi}&=0\\[4.30554pt]
\overline{a_{-\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})}\hat{C}_{-,(-\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}^{\prime}),(-\mathbf{k})}\ket{\Phi}&=0\end{split}

Note that
Lemma

4
does not yet imply
Proposition

4
because
a_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})
or
a_{-\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})
can be zero (and hence
Eq.

54
may be vacuously true).

Proof.

Using the CAR we can verify that

(55)

\begin{split}[\hat{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{k}},\,\hat{C}_{+,\mathbf{k},(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime})}]&=\hat{C}_{+,\mathbf{k},(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime})}\\
[\hat{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{k}},\,\hat{C}_{-,(-\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}^{\prime}),(-\mathbf{k})}]&=-\hat{C}_{-,(-\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}^{\prime}),(-\mathbf{k})}.\end{split}

Since
\ket{\Phi}
is an eigenvector of
\hat{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{k}}
with eigenvalue
N_{occ}
, we have that

(56)

\begin{split}\hat{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{k}}\hat{C}_{+,\mathbf{k},(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime})}\ket{\Phi}&=\hat{C}_{+,\mathbf{k},(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime})}\hat{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{k}}\ket{\Phi}+\Big[\hat{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{k}},\hat{C}_{+,\mathbf{k},(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime})}\Big]\ket{\Phi}\\
&=(N_{occ}+1)\hat{C}_{+,\mathbf{k},(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime})}\ket{\Phi}.\end{split}

So
\hat{C}_{+,\mathbf{k},(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime})}\ket{\Phi}
is an eigenvector of
\hat{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{k}}
with eigenvalue
(N_{occ}+1)
.

A similar calculation shows that

(57)

\hat{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{k}}\hat{C}_{-,(-\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}^{\prime}),(-\mathbf{k})}\ket{\Phi}=(N_{occ}-1)\hat{C}_{-,(-\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}^{\prime}),(-\mathbf{k})}\ket{\Phi}.

But
\hat{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{k}}
is a self-adjoint operator so it must be that
\hat{C}_{+,\mathbf{k},(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime})}\ket{\Phi}
and
\hat{C}_{-,(-\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}^{\prime}),(-\mathbf{k})}\ket{\Phi}
are orthogonal.
Since these two states are orthogonal,
Eq.

53
implies

(58)

\begin{split}&a_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\hat{C}_{+,\mathbf{k},(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime})}\ket{\Phi}=0,\\[4.30554pt]
&\overline{a_{-\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})}\hat{C}_{-,(-\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}^{\prime}),(-\mathbf{k})}\ket{\Phi}=0\end{split}

which proves the statement.
∎

While generally the value of
a_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})
can be difficult to determine, for any
\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}
, we have that
a_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{0})=1
since by definition

(59)

a_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{0})=\int_{\Omega}\|u_{1,\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})\|^{2}\,\textrm{d}{\mathbf{r}}=1.

Using the Lipschitz property of the map
\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\to|a_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})|^{2}
, we have the following proposition

Proposition 7

(
[
9
, Lemma 4.1]
)
.

There exists a constant
q_{c}>0
so that
|a_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})|>0
for all
\mathbf{k}\in\Omega^{*}
,
|\mathbf{q}^{\prime}|<q_{c}
.

To make use of this fact, we make the following assumption on the grid
\mathcal{K}
.

Assumption 2
.

We assume that
n_{\mathbf{k}_{x}}
and
n_{\mathbf{k}_{y}}
are chosen so that the spacing between two neighboring points in
\mathcal{K}
are separated by a distance less than
q_{c}
.

We now have all the tools needed to prove
Proposition

6
.

Proof of
Proposition

6
.

To prove this proposition, we make use of the following identity

(60)

\Big[\hat{C}_{\pm,\mathbf{k},(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}_{1})},~\hat{C}_{\pm,(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}_{1}),(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}_{1}+\mathbf{q}_{2})}\Big]=\hat{C}_{\pm,\mathbf{k},(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}_{1}+\mathbf{q}_{2})}-\delta_{\mathbf{q}_{1}+\mathbf{q}_{2}\in\Gamma^{*}}\hat{C}_{\pm,(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}_{1}),(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}_{1})},

Therefore, for any
\mathbf{q}_{1}+\mathbf{q}_{2}\not\in\Gamma^{*}
we have the following implication:

(61)

\begin{array}[]{r}\hat{C}_{\pm,\mathbf{k},(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}_{1})}\ket{\Phi}=0\\[4.30554pt]
\hat{C}_{\pm,(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}_{1}),(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}_{1}+\mathbf{q}_{2})}\ket{\Phi}=0\end{array}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\hat{C}_{\pm,\mathbf{k},(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}_{1}+\mathbf{q}_{2})}\ket{\Phi}=0.

As for the proof, we begin by fixing an arbitrary
\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}
and let
\mathbf{e}_{1}=\frac{\mathbf{b}_{1}}{n_{k_{x}}}
,
\mathbf{e}_{2}=\frac{\mathbf{b}_{2}}{n_{k_{y}}}
where we recall
\mathbf{b}_{1}
,
\mathbf{b}_{2}
are generating vectors for
\Gamma^{*}
.
We will show that for any
\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}
where
\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\neq\mathbf{k}
that
\hat{C}_{\pm,\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\ket{\Phi}=0
.

Because two neighboring points in
\mathcal{K}
differ by
\pm\mathbf{e}_{1}
or
\pm\mathbf{e}_{2}
(modulo the lattice)
Assumption

2
and
Eq.

61
gives us the implication

(62)

\begin{split}\hat{C}_{\pm,\mathbf{k},(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{e}_{1})}\ket{\Phi}&=0\\
\hat{C}_{\pm,(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{e}_{1}),(\mathbf{k}+2\mathbf{e}_{1})}\ket{\Phi}&=0\end{split}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\hat{C}_{\pm,\mathbf{k},(\mathbf{k}+2\mathbf{e}_{1})}\ket{\Phi}=0.

so long as
2\mathbf{e}_{1}\not\in\Gamma^{*}
.

Once we know that every ground state must satisfy
\hat{C}_{\pm,\mathbf{k},(\mathbf{k}+2\mathbf{e}_{1})}\ket{\Phi}=0
, we also have the implication

(63)

\begin{split}\hat{C}_{\pm,\mathbf{k},(\mathbf{k}+2\mathbf{e}_{1})}\ket{\Phi}&=0\\
\hat{C}_{\pm,(\mathbf{k}+2\mathbf{e}_{1}),(\mathbf{k}+2\mathbf{e}_{1}+\mathbf{e}_{2})}\ket{\Phi}&=0\end{split}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\hat{C}_{\pm,\mathbf{k},(\mathbf{k}+2\mathbf{e}_{1}+\mathbf{e}_{2})}\ket{\Phi}=0.

so long as

.
Since any

may be written as

for some

, we can repeat this argument to conclude that
\hat{C}_{\pm,\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\ket{\Phi}=0
so long as

.
∎

6.
Ground State Manifold of the Spinless, Valleyless Model and Occupation Vector Representations

From the arguments in the previous section we have proven
Proposition

4
which fully characterizes all ground states of MATBG.
It is worth observing that this characterization only depends on the
\hat{C}
operators and hence is
independent
of the underlying material details.
As outlined in
Section

4
,
Lemma

1
implies that we can decompose any many-body ground state

(64)

\begin{split}\ket{\Phi}&=\sum_{\lambda_{+}=0}^{N_{occ}}c_{\lambda_{+}}\ket{\Phi_{\lambda_{+}}},~\text{where}\\
\hat{n}_{+,\mathbf{k}}&\ket{\Phi_{\lambda_{+}}}=\lambda_{+}\ket{\Phi_{\lambda_{+}}}\\
\hat{n}_{-,\mathbf{k}}&\ket{\Phi_{\lambda_{+}}}=(N_{occ}-\lambda_{+})\ket{\Phi_{\lambda_{+}}}\end{split}

Here, the sum over
\lambda_{+}
runs over
\{0,\cdots,N_{occ}\}
since these are all eigenvalues of
\hat{n}_{+,\mathbf{k}}
.
For the single valley case,
Eq.

64
is enough to completely fix the ground state

Theorem 3
.

For the spinless, valleyless model of MATBG, a many-body state
\ket{\Phi}
is a ground state if and only if it can be written as

(65)

\ket{\Phi}=\alpha\ket{\tilde{\Phi}_{1}}+\beta\ket{\tilde{\Phi}_{0}}\qquad\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}

where

(66)

\ket{\tilde{\Phi}_{1}}=\left(\prod_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}\hat{f}_{+,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\right)\ket{\mathrm{vac}}\qquad\ket{\tilde{\Phi}_{0}}=\left(\prod_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}\hat{f}_{-,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\right)\ket{\mathrm{vac}}

Proof.

For the single valley model,
N_{occ}=1
so the sum in
Eq.

64
only has two terms,
\ket{\Phi_{1}}
and
\ket{\Phi_{0}}
, which must satisfy

(67)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\hat{n}_{+,\mathbf{k}}\ket{\Phi_{1}}&=\ket{\Phi_{1}}\\
\hat{n}_{-,\mathbf{k}}\ket{\Phi_{1}}&=0\end{array}\right.\qquad\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\hat{n}_{+,\mathbf{k}}\ket{\Phi_{0}}&=0\\
\hat{n}_{-,\mathbf{k}}\ket{\Phi_{0}}&=\ket{\Phi_{0}}\end{array}\right.

The only state which is a
+1
eigenstate of
\hat{n}_{+,\mathbf{k}}
for all
\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}
is
\left(\prod_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}\hat{f}_{+,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\right)\ket{\mathrm{vac}}
and the only state
+1
eigenstate of
\hat{n}_{-,\mathbf{k}}
for all
\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}
is
\left(\prod_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}\hat{f}_{-,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\right)\ket{\mathrm{vac}}
which proves the claim.
∎

The argument used in the proof of
Theorem

3
generally fixes the form of
\ket{\Phi_{\lambda_{+}}}
when
\lambda_{+}=0
and
\lambda_{+}=N_{occ}
since there is only one state which has
\lambda_{+}=0

\mathbf{k}
and
\lambda_{+}=N_{occ}
for all
\mathbf{k}

Since in the single valley case, the only possible values of
\lambda_{+}
are

N_{occ}
so this completely fixes the ground state.

To handle the other cases (i.e.
\lambda_{+}\not\in\{0,N_{occ}\}

To make a formal argument, we introduce a bijection between the states
\ket{\Phi_{\lambda_{+}}}
from the decomposition in

we refer to this mapping as the

6.1.
Occupation Vector Representation of a Many-Body State

Throughout this work, we have described both the many-body operators and many-body states using the language of second quantization (i.e. in terms of the creation and annihilation operators).
Second quantization is an attractive language for studying many particle systems, because it expresses many-body operators in a way which is agnostic to the number of particles in the system.
Because of the decomposition in
Eq.

64
however, once we know
\lambda_{+}
, we know precisely how many electrons are in each chiral sector at each
\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}

Let us begin by considering the valleyful model with
\lambda_{+}=1
and

Every state from this space may be written as a linear combination of states of the form

7.
Tools from Representation Theory

Due to the hidden symmetry, the ground states of the valleyful models (both spinless and spinful) exhibit significantly greater complexity than those of the spinless, valleyless model. Tools from representation theory play an important role in analyzing these states. At a high level, we examine the action of the
\mathsf{U}(N_{occ})
group on some tensor product space
(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes N}
. By the Schur–Weyl duality, this space decomposes into irreducible representations (irreps) indexed by Young diagrams. We will argue that most of these irreps do not contribute to the ground state manifold.
The key results supporting this argument are the Theorem of the Highest Weight (
Section

7.1
), which enables us to identify generating vectors for the irreps, and the Littlewood–Richardson rules (
Section

7.2
), which determine the irreps relevant to the ground state manifold. To illustrate these methods, we analyze a simple example of a valleyful model with two sites in
Section

7.3
.

We first state without proof a few important results from the representation theory of
\mathfrak{su}(d)

7.1.
Theorem of Highest Weight

We begin by stating a special case of the Theorem of Highest Weight. Here, we are working with tensor powers
(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes N}
of the defining representation of the Lie group
\mathsf{SU}(d)
, which are given by the map
U\mapsto U^{\otimes N}
. This then induces a representation of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{su}(d)
on
(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes N}
given by the map

X\mapsto X\otimes(\mathds{1}_{d\times d})^{\otimes N-1}+(\mathds{1}_{d\times d})\otimes X\otimes(\mathds{1}_{d\times d})^{\otimes N-2}+\dots+(\mathds{1}_{d\times d})^{\otimes N-1}\otimes X.

Theorem 4

(Theorem of Highest Weight for
\mathfrak{su}(d)
and
(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes N}
)
.

For any
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, consider the tensor representation
(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes N}
of
\mathfrak{su}(d)
and let
V
be an irreducible subrepresentation.
There exists a highest weight vector
v\in(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes N}
such that

(74)

V=\gen_{\mathfrak{su}(d)}\{v\}.

Remark 1
.

Since
\mathsf{SU}(d)
is simply connected, the representations of
\mathsf{SU}(d)
and of
\mathfrak{su}(d)
are in one-to-one correspondence
[
19
, Theorem 5.6]
. Further, any irreducible representation of
\mathsf{U}(d)
remains irreducible upon restricting to the subgroup
\mathsf{SU}(d)
.

This is a special instance of the more general definition of a highest weight vector, which may be found in
Appendix

C
). For our purposes we equivalently characterize highest weight vectors in terms of the operators
H_{j}
and
E_{j}
defined as follows:

(75)

\begin{array}[]{lll}H_{j}:=\ket{j}\bra{j}&&j\in\{1,\cdots,d\}\\[4.30554pt]
E_{j}:=\ket{j}\bra{j+1}&&j\in\{1,\cdots,d-1\}\end{array}

Highest weight vectors may then be identified using the following proposition.

Proposition 8
.

A vector
v\in(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes N}
is a
highest weight vector
for
\mathfrak{su}(d)
if it lies in the joint kernel of the family of operators

(76)

\sum_{\ell=0}^{N-1}\left(\mathds{1}_{d\times d}\right)^{\otimes\ell}\otimes E_{j}\otimes\left(\mathds{1}_{d\times d}\right)^{\otimes(N-\ell-1)}\qquad\forall j\in\{1,\cdots,d-1\}.

Additionally,
v
is a joint eigenvector of the family of operators

(77)

\sum_{\ell=0}^{N-1}\left(\mathds{1}_{d\times d}\right)^{\otimes\ell}\otimes H_{j}\otimes\left(\mathds{1}_{d\times d}\right)^{\otimes(N-\ell-1)}\qquad\forall j\in\{1,\cdots,d\}.

The tuple of eigenvalues
(\mu_{j})_{j=1}^{d}
corresponding to this family is called the
weight
of
v
.

We note that the weights
(\mu_{j})_{j}
of
v
allow us to distinguish highest weight vectors from different irreps:

Lemma 5
.

Suppose that
v
and
v^{\prime}
are highest weight vectors with weights
(\mu_{j})_{j}
and
(\mu_{j}^{\prime})_{j}
respectively.
If there exists a
j_{*}
so that
\mu_{j_{*}}\neq\mu_{j_{*}}^{\prime}
, then
v\perp v^{\prime}
.
Additionally, the irreps generated by
v
and
v^{\prime}
are orthogonal.

Proof.

The orthogonality of
v
and
v^{\prime}
is immediate since
\{H_{j}\}_{j}
is a family of self-adjoint commuting operators and hence eigenvectors with different eigenvalues are orthogonal.
Since the representation
U^{\otimes N}
is unitary, the irreps generated by
v
and
v^{\prime}
are necessarily orthogonal
[
19
, Proposition 4.27]
.
∎

The Theorem of Highest Weight also holds when we consider
V\subseteq(\mathbb{C}^{d}\wedge\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes N}
instead of
(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes N}
with some minor modifications:

Proposition 9
.

Theorem

4
holds when
\mathbb{C}^{d}
is replaced with
\mathbb{C}^{d}\wedge\mathbb{C}^{d}
.

Proposition

8
also holds when
\mathbb{C}^{d}
is replaced with
\mathbb{C}^{d}\wedge\mathbb{C}^{d}
if the operators
H_{j}
are
E_{j}
are replaced with

(78)

\begin{array}[]{lll}H_{j}:=\ket{j}\bra{j}\otimes\mathds{1}_{d\times d}+\mathds{1}_{d\times d}\otimes\ket{j}\bra{j},&&\forall j\in\{1,\cdots,d\}.\\[4.30554pt]
E_{j}:=\ket{j}\bra{j+1}\otimes\mathds{1}_{d\times d}+\mathds{1}_{d\times d}\otimes\ket{j}\bra{j+1},&&\forall j\in\{1,\cdots,d\}.\end{array}

Now that we can characterize generating vectors for different irreps, we can turn our attention to understanding which irreps are relevant for the ground state manifold.

\begin{split}\mathbb{S}_{(2,2)}(\mathbb{C}^{4})&=\gen_{\mathsf{U}(4)}\big\{\ket{1^{+}\wedge 2^{+}}^{\otimes 2}\big\}\\

Appendix C
Additional background materials on representation theory

The following is based on
[
18
, Chapter 15]
, and another careful presentation with thoroughly worked examples may be found in
[
19
, Chapter 6]
. For a quantum spin systems perspective on these constructions see
[
32
, Chapter 3]
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a semisimple Lie algebra and let
V
be a finite dimensional complex vector space. A representation
(\pi,V)
is a Lie algebra homomorphism
\pi:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{gl}(V)
, where
\mathfrak{gl}(V)
denotes the Lie algebra of linear operators acting on
V
equipped with the usual matrix commutator
[X,Y]=XY-YX
.
Throughout the following one should have in mind
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{su}(d)
, the
d\times d
skew-adjoint complex matrices.
Of particular importance is the adjoint representation, the Lie algebra homomorphism
\text{ad}:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{gl}(\mathfrak{g})
given for each
Y\in\mathfrak{g}
by

\text{ad}_{Y}(X)=[Y,X],\qquad\text{for all }X\in\mathfrak{g}.

Since we only care about complex representations, we may freely pass between the real Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
and its complexification
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{C}}
. A standard fact
[
19
, Proposition 3.39]
ensures that every real Lie algebra representation of
\mathfrak{g}
on
V
extends uniquely to a complex Lie algebra representation of
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{C}}
on
V
. The complexification of
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{su}(d)
is
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{sl}(d,\mathbb{C})
, the
d\times d
traceless complex matrices.

Definition 5
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a complex semisimple Lie algebra. A
Cartan subalgebra

\mathfrak{h}
is a maximal abelian subalgebra in
\mathfrak{g}
such that for every
H\in\mathfrak{h}
, the adjoint representative
\text{ad}_{H}:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}
is diagonalizable.

To find a Cartan subalgebra of
\mathfrak{sl}(d,\mathbb{C})
, start with the standard basis of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
, denoted
\{e_{j}\}_{j=1}^{d}
with bra-ket notation
\ket{j}:=e_{j}
. Define
H_{j}:=\ket{j}\bra{j}
to be the rank-1 projectors onto these basis vectors. Then a Cartan subalgebra is given by the following subspace of diagonal traceless matrices:

(166)

\mathfrak{h}:=\left\{a_{1}H_{1}+\dots+a_{d}H_{d}:\sum_{j=1}^{d}a_{j}=0\right\}\subseteq\mathfrak{sl}(d,\mathbb{C}).

We will need to work on the dual space
\mathfrak{h}^{*}
. There are several natural choices for a basis of
\mathfrak{h}^{*}
. We will simply take the dual basis: define for
i=1,\dots,d
linear functionals
L_{i}:\mathfrak{h}\to\mathbb{C}
by
L_{i}(H_{j})=\delta_{ij}
and note that

(167)

\mathfrak{h}^{*}=\text{span}(L_{1},\dots,L_{d})/\{L_{1}+\dots+L_{d}=0\}.

We will often write
L_{i}
to denote the equivalence class in
\mathfrak{h}^{*}
containing
L_{i}
. Observe that
L_{i}(H)
extracts the
i^{th}
diagonal entry of
H\in\mathfrak{h}
.

Definition 6
.

A linear functional
L\in\mathfrak{h}^{*}
is called a
weight
of
(\pi,V)
if there exists a nonzero vector
v\in V
such that

\pi(H)v=L(H)v\qquad\text{for all }H\in\mathfrak{h}.

The
weight space
corresponding to
L
is the set
\{v\in V:\pi(H)v=L(H)v\text{ for all }H\in\mathfrak{h}\}
, and the
multiplicity
of
L
is the dimension of the corresponding weight space.

Weights are a tool to track the eigenvalues of the simultaneously diagonalized representatives of
\mathfrak{h}
. Indeed, in the setting of
Proposition

8
, we are working on the tensor representation
(\pi,(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes N})
and have distinguished a basis for
\mathfrak{h}
. Then, we have that for any fixed
\ket{i_{1}}\ket{i_{2}}\dots\ket{i_{N}}\in(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes N}
,

\pi(H_{j})\ket{i_{1}}\ket{i_{2}}\dots\ket{i_{N}}=(L_{i_{1}}(H_{j})+L_{i_{2}}(H_{j})+\dots+L_{i_{N}}(H_{j}))\ket{i_{1}}\ket{i_{2}}\dots\ket{i_{N}},

so
\ket{i_{1}}\ket{i_{2}}\dots\ket{i_{N}}
is a vector of weight
L_{i_{1}}+L_{i_{2}}+\dots+L_{i_{N}}
. In particular, if we define

\mu_{j}:=(L_{i_{1}}+L_{i_{2}}+\dots+L_{i_{N}})(H_{j}),

then the tuple of eigenvalues
(\mu_{j})_{j=1}^{d}
is the same data as the weight
L_{i_{1}}+L_{i_{2}}+\dots+L_{i_{N}}
. In general one uses weights because, unlike eigenvalues, they do not depend on the choice of basis, but in our context we may simply fix a basis at the outset.

The following definition generalizes the notion of raising and lowering operators, allowing us to “hop” between simultaneous eigenvectors of the image of the Cartan subalgebra
\pi(\mathfrak{h})
in the representation.

Definition 7
.

A nonzero linear functional
\alpha\in\mathfrak{h}^{*}
is a
root
if there exists a nonzero
X\in\mathfrak{g}
with

[H,X]=\alpha(H)X\qquad\text{for all }H\in\mathfrak{h}.

In other words,
\alpha
is a nonzero weight of the adjoint representation
\text{ad}:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{gl}(\mathfrak{g})
. We call the weight space corresponding to a root
\alpha
the
root space
of
\alpha
.

The finite set of roots, which thought of as vectors in
\mathfrak{h}^{*}
with a suitable inner product, is called a root system. The structure of a semisimple Lie algebra is characterized by its root system, which then dictates the structure of representations, thanks to the following computation: if
(\pi,V)
is a representation,
\alpha
is a root with
X_{\alpha}
in the associated root space, and
v\in V
a weight vector of weight
\beta
, then

\displaystyle\pi(H)\pi(X_{\alpha})v

\displaystyle=\pi(X_{\alpha})\pi(H)v+[\pi(H),\pi(X_{\alpha})]v

\displaystyle=\pi(X_{\alpha})\beta(H)v+\alpha(H)\pi(X_{\alpha})v

\displaystyle=(\beta(H)+\alpha(H))\pi(X_{\alpha})v.

In other words, given a simultaneous eigenvector
v
of the image of the Cartan subalgebra
\pi(\mathfrak{h})
, then
\pi(X_{\alpha})v
is also a simultaneous eigenvector of
\pi(\mathfrak{h})
. In this sense we may think of roots (or more precisely root vectors) as generalizing the spin “raising and lowering” operators from the
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{C})
case.

We may obtain a compressed description of a root system by making a few choices. Roots always come in such positive and negative pairs, so by choosing a hyperplane in
\mathfrak{h}^{*}
we may distinguish a set of positive roots. This is akin to paying attention only to the raising operator for
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{C})
. Further, we may restrict to a set of simple roots, which is a minimal set of roots which generate the rest of the root system by integer linear combinations. The resultant set of positive simple roots
\Delta
stores a complete description of
\mathfrak{g}
and will allow us to state the theorem of highest weight.
For
\mathfrak{sl}(d,\mathbb{C})
, such a set is given by

(168)

\Delta:=\{L_{1}-L_{2},L_{2}-L_{3},\dots,L_{d-1}-L_{d}\}.

Given a root
\alpha
, it is not too hard to find an explicit matrix
X_{\alpha}\in\mathfrak{g}
in the associated root space. In our specific case, the root
L_{i}-L_{j}
,
1\leq i<j\leq d
, has the matrix
E_{ij}:=\ket{i}\bra{j}
in its root space, since

(169)

\begin{split}[a_{1}H_{1}+\dots+a_{d}H_{d},E_{ij}]&=(a_{i}-a_{j})E_{ij}\\
&=(L_{i}-L_{j})(a_{1}H_{1}+\dots+a_{d}H_{d})E_{ij}.\end{split}

We now arrive at a key definition, which generalizes the notion of “highest spin” of an irreducible representation of
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{C})
.

Definition 8
.

Let
\Delta
be a set of positive simple roots for a semisimple Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
, and let
(\pi,V)
be a representation of
\mathfrak{g}
. A
highest weight vector

v\in V
is a vector annihilated by all of the positive simple root vectors
X_{\alpha}
, i.e.

\pi(X_{\alpha})v=0\qquad\text{for all }\alpha\in\Delta.

The
highest weight
of a representation is the weight of its highest weight vector.

We finally arrive at a simplified statement of the theorem of highest weight.

Theorem 9
.

(Theorem of highest weight)

(1)

Every irreducible representation of
\mathfrak{g}
has a highest weight whose weight space is one dimensional.

(2)

Two irreducible representations of
\mathfrak{g}
are isomorphic if and only if they have the same highest weight.

(3)

If
v
is a highest weight vector of an irreducible representation
(\pi,V)
, then
V
is cyclically generated by
v
, i.e.

V=\{\pi(X)v:X\in\mathfrak{g}\}.

As a special case, we obtain the theorem which appeared in the main text. We mentioned already that the (finite-dimensional) complex representations of
\mathfrak{su}(d)
and its complexification
\mathfrak{sl}(d,\mathbb{C})
are in one-to-one correspondence. We then leverage that since
\mathfrak{su}(d)
is the Lie algebra of the simply connected Lie group
\mathsf{SU}(d)
, their representations too are in one-to-one correspondence
[
19
, Theorem 5.6]
. Of particular note is that a representation of
\mathsf{SU}(d)
is irreducible if and only if the representation it induces on
\mathfrak{sl}(d,\mathbb{C})
is irreducible.

Theorem 10
.

(Theorem of highest weight for
\mathsf{SU}(d)
)
Every irreducible representation
(\pi,V)
of
\mathfrak{sl}(d,\mathbb{C})
has a unique 1 dimensional subspace spanned by
v\in V
such that

v\in\bigcap_{i=1}^{d-1}\ker(\pi(E_{i,i+1})),

where
E_{i,i+1}=\ket{i}\bra{i+1}\in\mathfrak{sl}(d,\mathbb{C})
.

Suppose
v
has weight
\lambda\in\mathfrak{h}^{*}
. If
(\tilde{\pi},\tilde{V})
is another irreducible representation with highest weight
\nu\in\mathfrak{h}^{*}
, then
V\cong\tilde{V}
as representations of
\mathfrak{g}
.

C.1.
The 2-site Tensor Case for
\mathsf{SU}(2)

As an example, we decompose the two-site tensor representation
\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2}
into irreps of
\mathsf{SU}(2)
.

First, consider the defining representation
(\Pi,\mathbb{C}^{2})
of
\mathsf{SU}(2)
, which is given by the map
\Pi(U)=U
for all
U\in\mathsf{SU}(2)
. This descends to the defining representation of the Lie algebra

(\pi,\mathbb{C}^{2})
of

\pi(X)=X

\pi(E_{1,2})\ket{1}=E_{1,2}\ket{1}=0.

In fact, the highest weight space is 1-dimensional since

\pi(H_{1}-H_{2})\ket{1}=(H_{1}-H_{2})\ket{1}=\ket{1}=L_{1}(H_{1}-H_{2})\ket{1}.

C.2.
The 2-site Tensor Case for
\mathsf{SU}(4)

The treatment for
\mathsf{SU}(4)
is similar to that of
\mathsf{SU}(2)
, but now the Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{h}
of
\mathfrak{sl}(4,\mathbb{C})
defined by
Eq.

166
is 3-dimensional.

First, the defining representation
(\pi,\mathbb{C}^{4})
given by
\pi(X)=X
has the highest weight vector
\ket{1}
:

\ket{1}\in\ker\pi(E_{1,2})\cap\ker\pi(E_{2,3})\cap\ker\pi(E_{3,4}).

Evidently this is the only vector (up to rescaling) of highest weight. Thus the defining representation is an irrep of highest weight
L_{1}
since

\pi(H_{i}-H_{i+1})\ket{1}=L_{1}(H_{i}-H_{i+1})\ket{1},\qquad i=1,2,3.

Let us denote this irrep by
\mathbb{S}_{(1)}(\mathbb{C}^{4})
, or diagrammatically by the box

\mathbb{S}_{(1)}(\mathbb{C}^{4})=\hbox{$\vbox{\hbox{\vtop{\halign{&\opttoksa@YT={\font@YT}\getcolor@YT{\save@YT{\opttoksb@YT}}\nil@YT\getcolor@YT{\startbox@@YT\the\opttoksa@YT\the\opttoksb@YT}#\endbox@YT\cr\lower 0.39993pt\vbox{\kern 0.19997pt\hbox{\kern 0.39993pt\vbox to10.39995pt{\vss\hbox to10.00002pt{\hss$$\hss}\vss}\kern-10.39995pt\vrule height=10.39995pt,width=0.39993pt\kern 10.00002pt\vrule height=10.39995pt,width=0.39993pt}\kern-0.19997pt\kern-10.39995pt\hrule width=10.79988pt,height=0.39993pt\kern 10.00002pt\hrule width=10.79988pt,height=0.39993pt}\cr}}\kern 345.4pt}}$}.

On to the 2-site tensor representation
\pi:{\mathfrak{sl}}(4,\mathbb{C})\to\mathfrak{gl}(\mathbb{C}^{4}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{4})
, given by
\pi(X)=X\otimes\mathds{1}+\mathds{1}\otimes X
. We once again compute that the space of highest weight vectors is two-dimensional:

\text{span}\{\ket{1}\ket{1},\ket{1}\ket{2}-\ket{2}\ket{1}\}=\ker\pi(E_{1,2})\cap\ker\pi(E_{2,3})\cap\ker\pi(E_{3,4}).

The weights of these vectors are

(174)

\begin{split}\pi(H_{1}-H_{2})\ket{1}\ket{1}&=2L_{1}(H_{1}-H_{2})\ket{1}\ket{1}\\
\pi(H_{1}-H_{2})\Big(\ket{1}\ket{2}-\ket{2}\ket{1}\Big)&=(L_{1}+L_{2})(H_{1}-H_{2})\Big(\ket{1}\ket{2}-\ket{2}\ket{1}\Big)=0.\end{split}

So,
\ket{1}\ket{1}
is a vector of highest weight
2L_{1}
, while
\ket{1}\ket{2}-\ket{1}\ket{2}
is a vector of highest weight
L_{1}+L_{2}
. Hence, they both cyclically generate irreps, which we will notationally call

(175)

\mathbb{S}_{(2)}(\mathbb{C}^{4}):=\gen_{{\mathfrak{sl}}(4,\mathbb{C})}\{\ket{1}\ket{1}\},\qquad\mathbb{S}_{(1^{2})}(\mathbb{C}^{4}):=\gen_{{\mathfrak{sl}}(4,\mathbb{C})}\{\ket{1}\ket{2}-\ket{2}\ket{1}\},

again yielding the decomposition
\mathbb{C}^{4}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{4}\cong\mathbb{S}_{(2)}(\mathbb{C}^{4})\oplus\mathbb{S}_{(1^{1})}(\mathbb{C}^{4})
with an identical diagrammatic representation to
Eq.

173
. In this case, by counting symmetric and antisymmetric vectors. we obtain that
\dim(\mathbb{S}_{(2)}(\mathbb{C}^{4}))=4(4+1)/2=10
and
\dim(\mathbb{S}_{(1^{2})}(\mathbb{C}^{4}))=4(4-1)/2=6
.
