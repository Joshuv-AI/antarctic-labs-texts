1.
Introduction

Suppose that
A=\{A_{n}\}\in C_{c}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
, space of finitely supported functions on
\mathbb{N}_{0}
with the assumption that
A_{0}=0
. Then the renowned discrete Hardy’s inequality (
[
6
]
, Theorem 326) in one dimension states that

(1.1)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|A_{n}-A_{n-1}|^{2}\geq\frac{1}{4}\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{|A_{n}|^{2}}{n^{2}},

where the associated constant term is sharp. The inequality (
1.1
) has a rich history and wider applications. Due to its enormous applications in multiple branches of mathematics such as differential equations, graph theory, and spectral theory, the inequality (
1.1
) has been extended in various ways. One of the such valuable extensions is given by Hardy himself (
[
7
]
,
[
8
]
), which says that for a strictly positive sequence
\{q_{n}\}
of real numbers if
\{\sqrt{q_{n}}a_{n}\}\in\ell_{2}
holds then

(1.2)

\displaystyle{}\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}q_{n}Q_{n}^{-2}|A_{n}|^{2}

\displaystyle<4\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}q_{n}|a_{n}|^{2},

unless all
a_{n}
is null, and where
A_{n}=q_{1}a_{1}+q_{2}a_{2}+\ldots+q_{n}a_{n}
,
Q_{n}=q_{1}+q_{2}+\ldots+q_{n}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Also the constant is best possible. Another important extensions is given by Hardy and Littlewood
[
9
]
as below:

(1.3)

\displaystyle{}\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{-c}(\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k})^{2}\leq K(c)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{-c}(na_{n})^{2},

where
p>1
,
c\neq 1
and
K=K(c)
is a constant depending on
c
such that RHS of inequality (
1.3
) is convergent. Motivated by the inequalities (
1.2
), and (
1.3
), Copson
[
2
]
(see also
[
1
]
) established a sharp inequality as below:

(1.4)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{Q_{n}^{\alpha}|A_{n}-A_{n-1}|^{2}}{q_{n}}\geq\frac{(\alpha-1)^{2}}{4}\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{q_{n}}{Q_{n}^{2-\alpha}}|A_{n}|^{2},

where
\alpha\in[0,1)
with
\alpha=2-c
for
1<c\leq 2
. The inequality (
1.4
) is known as Copson inequality.

The recent surprising discovery of Keller, Pinchover and Pogorzelski
[
12
]
(see also
[
13
]
) suggests that the inequality (
1.1
) is not optimal in the following sense

(1.5)

\displaystyle{}\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|A_{n}-A_{n-1}|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}w_{n}^{KPP}|A_{n}|^{2}>\frac{1}{4}\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{|A_{n}|^{2}}{n^{2}},

where
w_{n}^{KPP}=2-\sqrt{\frac{n-1}{n}}-\sqrt{\frac{n+1}{n}}>\frac{1}{4n^{2}}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
. This notable contribution has stimulated the interest of research in the direction of improvement of various discrete Hardy-type inequalities in one dimension by several mathematicians and researchers (see e.g.
[
5
]
,
[
10
]
,
[
11
]
). A short proof of (
1.5
) with optimality was presented by Krejčiřík and Štampach
[
15
]
. Later on, Krejčiřík, Laptev and Štampach (see
[
16
]
, Theorem 10) have jointly established an extended version of improved discrete Hardy inequality (
1.5
), and proved that

(1.6)

\displaystyle{}\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|A_{n}-A_{n-1}|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}w_{n}(\mu)|A_{n}|^{2},

where
w_{n}(\mu)=2-\frac{\mu_{n-1}}{\mu_{n}}-\frac{\mu_{n+1}}{\mu_{n}}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and
\{\mu_{n}\}
is a strictly positive sequence of real numbers such that
\mu_{0}=0
. Unifying both the improved discrete Hardy inequalities (
1.5
,
1.6
), we
[
3
]
have obtained an extended improved discrete Hardy’s inequality in one dimension as follows

(1.7)

\displaystyle{}\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{|A_{n}-A_{n-1}|^{2}}{\lambda_{n}}\geq\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}w_{n}(\lambda,\mu)|A_{n}|^{2},

where
\lambda=\{\lambda_{n}\}
is a real sequence such that
\lambda_{n}>0
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
w_{n}(\lambda,\mu)
has the following expression

w_{n}(\lambda,\mu)=\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}}+\frac{1}{\lambda_{n+1}}-\frac{\mu_{n-1}}{\lambda_{n}\mu_{n}}-\frac{\mu_{n+1}}{\lambda_{n+1}\mu_{n}}
.
It is pertinent to mentioned here that our inequality (
1.7
) not only gives the above inequalities (
1.5
,
1.6
) but also the improved discrete Hardy inequality with power weights, which is established recently by Gupta
[
5
]
. He proved that for
\alpha\in\{0\}\cup[1/3,1)
and
\beta=\frac{1-\alpha}{2}

(1.8)

\displaystyle{}\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|A_{n}-A_{n-1}|^{2}n^{\alpha}

\displaystyle\geq\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}w_{n}(\alpha,\beta)|A_{n}|^{2}>\frac{(\alpha-1)^{2}}{4}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{|A_{n}|^{2}}{n^{2}}{n}^{\alpha},~~~\mbox{holds}

where
w_{1}(\alpha,\beta):=1+2^{\alpha}-2^{\alpha+\beta}
, and
w_{n}(\alpha,\beta)=n^{\alpha}\Big[1+\Big(1+\frac{1}{n}\Big)^{\alpha}-\Big(1-\frac{1}{n}\Big)^{\beta}-\Big(1+\frac{1}{n}\Big)^{\alpha+\beta}\Big]
for
n\geq 2
. We note that the inequality (
1.8
) is indeed a Copson’s inequality (
1.4
) for
q_{n}=1
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
. That means the Copson’s inequality for
q_{n}=1
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
(i.e. Hardy’s inequality with power weights) achieved an improvement when
\alpha\in\{0\}\cup[1/3,1)
. This leads to an important question given below, and which will be our first investigation in this paper:

Q(a)
Is it possible to extend the interval of
\alpha
for which improvement of (
1.8
) still possible?

Many authors studying improvement of discrete Hardy inequality in different contexts such as in graph setting (see
[
13
]
,
[
14
]
), in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
(see
[
11
]
,
[
13
]
) etc. But there is no such study has been found on the improvement of the Copson inequality (
1.4
). Although, we have considered the improvement of the Copson inequality (
1.4
) for
q_{n}=n
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
in
[
3
]
, and have shown that it admits an improvement only when
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}
(or
c=\frac{3}{2}
). Again in our another work
[
4
]
, we were able to achieve an improvement of the Copson inequality (
1.4
) for
q_{n}=n^{2}
, and
q_{n}=n^{3}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
and when
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}
only. With these investigations following questions may be asked.

Q(b)
Can we improve the Copson inequality (
1.4
) for an arbitrary sequence
\{q_{n}\}
?

If we get a favourable answer to this question
Q(b)
then

Q(c)
What are the possible values of
\alpha
for which (
1.4
) gets an improvement?

Therefore the main objective of this paper is to supply the possible answers of the above proposed queries
(Q(a)
to
Q(c))
. To reach our objectives first we give an affirmative answer to the question
Q(a)
. That is we prove that the range of
\alpha
from
\{0\}\cup[1/3,1)
can be extended to a larger one
[0,1)
for which the improvement of (
1.8
) is still possible. Consequently, we strengthen the result of Gupta
[
5
]
. When providing an answer to
Q(b)
, we have seen that for any decreasing sequence
\{q_{n}\}
, the improvement of (
1.4
) is attainable for
\alpha\in[1/3,1)
. But we couldn’t manage to answer this question for an arbitrary increasing sequence
\{q_{n}\}
. Rather, we have shown that there exists improvements of (
1.4
) for some strictly increasing sequences
\{q_{n}\}
such as
q_{n}=n
, and
q_{n}=n^{3}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
. We prove that the Copson’s inequality (
1.4
) for
q_{n}=n
achieves an improvement when
\alpha\in[17/50,1)
, whereas for
q_{n}=n^{3}
, Copson’s inequality (
1.4
) gets an improvement when
\alpha\in[0,1/2]
.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we establish an improved Hardy’s inequality with power weights

for
\alpha\in[0,1)
. Section 3 deals with the improvement of the Copson inequality (
1.4
) for an arbitrary sequence
\{q_{n}\}
. In fact, in first subsection we have shown an improved Copson inequality (
1.4
) for an arbitrary decreasing sequence
\{q_{n}\}

1.4
) for some increasing sequences
\{q_{n}\}
.

2.
Improved Hardy’s inequality with power weights
n^{\alpha}
for
\alpha\in[0,1)

We have seen that the Copson inequality (
1.4
) in case of
q_{n}=1
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
is equivalent to the inequality (
1.8
) established by Gupta
[
5
]
. More precisely, he has obtained an improvement of the inequality (
1.8
) for
\alpha\in\{0\}\cup[\frac{1}{3},1)
. In this section, we strengthen the result of Gupta
[
5
]
and prove that the improvement of (
1.8
) is possible for all
\alpha\in[0,1)
. In fact we have the following result. Let us begin with the following lemma.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\alpha\in[0,1)
. Then we have

\displaystyle 1+2^{\alpha}-2^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}}

\displaystyle>\frac{1}{4}(\alpha-1)^{2}.

Proof.

Let us consider a function
f(\alpha)
defined as below:

\displaystyle f(\alpha)

\displaystyle=1+2^{\alpha}-2^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}}-\frac{1}{4}(\alpha-1)^{2}.

Then we have the following derivative:

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{\log 2}{2}(2^{\alpha+1}-2^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}})+\frac{(1-\alpha)}{2}\Big)>0

\displaystyle\Big[\mbox{since},~~2^{\alpha+1}>2^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}~~\mbox{for~all}~~\alpha\in[0,1)\Big]

Hence
f^{\prime}(\alpha)
is increasing in
[0,1)
. Since
f(0)>0
, so we conclude that
f(\alpha)>0
for
\alpha\in[0,1)
. Hence the proof.
∎

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
\{A_{n}\}\in C_{c}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
be such that
A_{0}=0
and
\alpha\in[0,1)
. Then we have the following improvement

(2.1)

\displaystyle{}\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|A_{n}-A_{n-1}|^{2}n^{\alpha}

\displaystyle\geq\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}w_{n}(\alpha)|A_{n}|^{2}>\frac{(\alpha-1)^{2}}{4}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{|A_{n}|^{2}}{n^{2}}{n}^{\alpha},

where
w_{1}(\alpha):=1+2^{\alpha}-2^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}
, and for
n\geq 2

\displaystyle w_{n}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=n^{\alpha}\Big[1+\Big(1+\frac{1}{n}\Big)^{\alpha}-\Big(1-\frac{1}{n}\Big)^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}-\Big(1+\frac{1}{n}\Big)^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}\Big].

Proof.

The LHS of the inequality
(\ref{GUPI01})
immediately follows from (
1.7
) by choosing
\lambda_{n}=\frac{1}{n^{\alpha}}
and
\mu_{n}=n^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}
. Throughout the proof we consider only
\alpha\in(0,1)
as for
\alpha=0
improvement is already achieved in inequality (
1.5
). For
n=1
, the improvement is easily followed from Lemma
2.1
. In fact we have

\displaystyle w_{1}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=1+2^{\alpha}-2^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}>\frac{1}{4}(\alpha-1)^{2}.

For
n\geq 2
, we define a real valued continuous function
R(x)
on
x\in[0,\frac{1}{2}]
as below

\displaystyle R(x)=

\displaystyle 1+(1+x)^{\alpha}-(1-x)^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}-(1+x)^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}.

Then we have

\displaystyle R^{\prime}(x)=

\displaystyle\alpha(1+x)^{\alpha-1}+\frac{1-\alpha}{2}(1-x)^{\frac{-1-\alpha}{2}}-\frac{1+\alpha}{2}(1+x)^{\frac{\alpha-1}{2}},

\displaystyle R^{\prime\prime}(x)=

\displaystyle\alpha(\alpha-1)(1+x)^{\alpha-2}+\big(\frac{1-\alpha^{2}}{4}\big)\Big[(1-x)^{\frac{-3-\alpha}{2}}+(1+x)^{\frac{\alpha-3}{2}}\Big],

\displaystyle R^{\prime\prime\prime}(x)=

\displaystyle\alpha(1-\alpha)(2-\alpha)(1+x)^{\alpha-3}+\big(\frac{1-\alpha^{2}}{4}\big)\Big[\frac{3+\alpha}{2}(1-x)^{\frac{-5-\alpha}{2}}-\frac{3-\alpha}{2}(1+x)^{\frac{\alpha-5}{2}}\Big],

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle I+\big(\frac{1-\alpha^{2}}{4}\big)f(x),

where

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=\alpha(1-\alpha)(2-\alpha)(1+x)^{\alpha-3},

\displaystyle f(x)=

\displaystyle\frac{3+\alpha}{2}(1-x)^{\frac{-5-\alpha}{2}}-\frac{3-\alpha}{2}(1+x)^{\frac{\alpha-5}{2}},~~~\mbox{and hence}

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(x)=

\displaystyle\frac{3+\alpha}{2}\frac{5+\alpha}{2}(1-x)^{\frac{-7-\alpha}{2}}+\frac{3-\alpha}{2}\frac{5-\alpha}{2}(1+x)^{\frac{\alpha-7}{2}}.

Clearly
f^{\prime}(x)>0
for
x\in[0,\frac{1}{2}]
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
. This says that
f(x)
is increasing. Since
f(0)=0
, so we have
f(x)\geq 0
for
x\in[0,\frac{1}{2}]
.
Also since
I>0
for
\alpha\in(0,1)
therefore we asserts that
R^{\prime\prime\prime}(x)>0
for
x\in[0,\frac{1}{2}]
.

Now denote
S(x)=R(x)-\frac{(\alpha-1)^{2}}{4}x^{2}
. Then for
x\in[0,\frac{1}{2}]
, we get

\displaystyle S^{\prime}(x)=

\displaystyle R^{\prime}(x)-\frac{(\alpha-1)^{2}}{2}x,

\displaystyle S^{\prime\prime}(x)=

\displaystyle R^{\prime\prime}(x)-\frac{(\alpha-1)^{2}}{2},

\displaystyle S^{\prime\prime\prime}(x)=

\displaystyle R^{\prime\prime\prime}(x).

Since
R^{\prime\prime\prime}(x)>0
so we have
S^{\prime\prime\prime}(x)>0
in
[0,\frac{1}{2}]
. Note that
S^{\prime\prime}(0)=S^{\prime}(0)=S(0)=0
. This tells that
S(x)>0
for
x\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
, consequently we have
R(x)>\frac{(\alpha-1)^{2}}{4}x^{2}
for
x\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
. We choose
x=\frac{1}{n}
for
n\geq 2
, then we get
R(\frac{1}{n})>\frac{(\alpha-1)^{2}}{4n^{2}}
. Since
w_{n}(\alpha)=n^{\alpha}R(\frac{1}{n})
then we have for
n\geq 2
and
\alpha\in(0,1)

\displaystyle w_{n}(\alpha)>\frac{(\alpha-1)^{2}}{4}{n}^{\alpha-2}.

This completes proof of the theorem.
∎

3.
Improvement of general Copson’s inequality

In this section, we study the improvement of the Copson’s inequality (
1.4
) in two cases. First we consider the inequality (
1.4
) for decreasing sequence
\{q_{n}\}
, and second for some increasing sequences
\{q_{n}\}
, especially
q_{n}=n
and
q_{n}=n^{3}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
. At this point, we indicate that for arbitrary increasing sequence
\{q_{n}\}
, we were not able to conclude the improvement of (
1.4
). Although in our previous works (
[
3
]
,
[
4
]
), we were only able to prove that the inequality (
1.4
) admits improvement in a special case of
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}

(c=\frac{3}{2})
, and for some increasing sequences such as
q_{n}=n,n^{2},n^{3}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Let us start with an arbitrary positive decreasing sequence
\{q_{n}\}
.

3.1.
For decreasing sequence
\{q_{n}\}

Let
\alpha\in[0,1)
, and
\{\mu_{n}\}
be a strictly positive sequence of real numbers. Then we define a weight sequence
w_{n}(q,Q)
as below:

\displaystyle w_{n}(q,Q)

\displaystyle=\frac{Q_{n}^{\alpha}}{q_{n}}+\frac{Q_{n+1}^{\alpha}}{q_{n+1}}-\frac{\mu_{n-1}Q_{n}^{\alpha}}{q_{n}\mu_{n}}-\frac{\mu_{n+1}Q_{n+1}^{\alpha}}{q_{n+1}\mu_{n}}.

Now with the choice of
\mu_{n}=Q_{n}^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}
, weight sequence
w_{n}(q,Q)
can be rewritten as below:

\displaystyle w_{n}(q,Q)

\displaystyle=\frac{Q_{n}^{\alpha}}{q_{n}}T_{n},

where of course

\displaystyle T_{n}

\displaystyle=1+\frac{q_{n}}{q_{n+1}}\big(\frac{Q_{n+1}}{Q_{n}}\big)^{\alpha}-\big(\frac{Q_{n-1}}{Q_{n}}\big)^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}-\frac{q_{n}}{q_{n+1}}\big(\frac{Q_{n+1}}{Q_{n}}\big)^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}

\displaystyle=1+\frac{q_{n}}{q_{n+1}}\Big(1+\frac{q_{n+1}}{Q_{n}}\Big)^{\alpha}-\Big(1-\frac{q_{n}}{Q_{n}}\Big)^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}-\frac{q_{n}}{q_{n+1}}\Big(1+\frac{q_{n+1}}{Q_{n}}\Big)^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}.

Expanding
T_{n}
as an infinite series, we have

\displaystyle T_{n}

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\Big[\binom{\alpha}{k}\frac{q_{n}q_{n+1}^{k-1}}{Q_{n}^{k}}-\binom{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}{k}\frac{q_{n}q_{n+1}^{k-1}}{Q_{n}^{k}}-(-1)^{k}\binom{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}{k}\frac{q_{n}^{k}}{Q_{n}^{k}}\Big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\alpha^{2}}{8}\frac{q_{n}^{2}}{Q_{n}^{2}}-\frac{(1-\alpha)(3\alpha-1)}{8}\frac{q_{n}q_{n+1}}{Q_{n}^{2}}+\displaystyle\sum_{k=3}^{\infty}C_{k}(q,\alpha),

where

(3.1)

\displaystyle{}C_{k}(q,\alpha)

\displaystyle=\Big[\binom{\alpha}{k}\frac{q_{n}q_{n+1}^{k-1}}{Q_{n}^{k}}-\binom{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}{k}\frac{q_{n}q_{n+1}^{k-1}}{Q_{n}^{k}}-(-1)^{k}\binom{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}{k}\frac{q_{n}^{k}}{Q_{n}^{k}}\Big].

Notice that
(1-\alpha)(3\alpha-1)\geq 0
for
\alpha\in[\frac{1}{3},1)
. Hence if
\alpha\in[\frac{1}{3},1)
then decreasing-ness of
\{q_{n}\}
implies that

(3.2)

\displaystyle{}T_{n}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{(\alpha-1)^{2}}{4}\frac{q_{n}^{2}}{Q_{n}^{2}}+\displaystyle\sum_{k=3}^{\infty}C_{k}(q,\alpha).

Now our aim is to show that
C_{k}(q,\alpha)
is positive for
k\geq 3
. With this aim, we first prove the following lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\alpha\in[\frac{1}{3},1)
and
\{q_{n}\}
be decreasing sequence of positive real numbers. Then
C_{k}(q,\alpha)
is strictly positive for
k\geq 3
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

Denote
\gamma_{1}=\frac{1-\alpha}{2}
and
\gamma_{2}=\frac{1+\alpha}{2}
for
\alpha\in[\frac{1}{3},1)
. Then one can re-write
C_{k}(q,\alpha)
as below:

\displaystyle C_{k}(q,\alpha)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k-1}\frac{\alpha}{k!}\frac{q_{n}q_{n+1}^{k-1}}{Q_{n}^{k}}\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}(i-\alpha)+\frac{\gamma_{1}}{k!}\frac{q_{n}^{k}}{Q_{n}^{k}}\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}(i-\gamma_{1})+(-1)^{k}\frac{\gamma_{2}}{k!}\frac{q_{n}q_{n+1}^{k-1}}{Q_{n}^{k}}\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}(i-\gamma_{2}).

We now divide
k\geq 3
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
in two cases, one is odd natural number
k
, and another is even natural number
k
.

Case I:
k
is odd.

Note that when
k
is odd then

\displaystyle C_{k}(q,\alpha)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{k!}\frac{q_{n}q_{n+1}^{k-1}}{Q_{n}^{k}}\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}(i-\alpha)+\frac{\gamma_{1}}{k!}\frac{q_{n}^{k}}{Q_{n}^{k}}\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}(i-\gamma_{1})-\frac{\gamma_{2}}{k!}\frac{q_{n}q_{n+1}^{k-1}}{Q_{n}^{k}}\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}(i-\gamma_{2})

\displaystyle=I_{1}+I_{2}-I_{3},

where

I_{1}=\frac{\alpha}{k!}\frac{q_{n}q_{n+1}^{k-1}}{Q_{n}^{k}}\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}(i-\alpha)
,

I_{2}=\frac{\gamma_{1}}{k!}\frac{q_{n}^{k}}{Q_{n}^{k}}\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}(i-\gamma_{1})
, and

I_{3}=\frac{\gamma_{2}}{k!}\frac{q_{n}q_{n+1}^{k-1}}{Q_{n}^{k}}\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}(i-\gamma_{2}).

Now for odd
k\geq 3
, and
\alpha\in[\frac{1}{3},1)
, it is immediate that
I_{1}>0
. But the following difference gives

\displaystyle I_{2}-I_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma_{2}}{k!}\frac{q_{n}^{k}}{Q_{n}^{k}}\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}(i-\gamma_{2})\Big[\frac{\gamma_{1}}{\gamma_{2}}\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}\big(\frac{i-\gamma_{1}}{i-\gamma_{2}}\big)-\frac{q_{n+1}^{k-1}}{q_{n}^{k-1}}\Big]

\lambda_{n}=\frac{n^{3}}{\hat{S}_{n}^{\alpha}}
, and
\mu_{n}=\hat{S}_{n}^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}
, where
\hat{S}_{n}=\big(\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\big)^{2}
. The RHS of inequality (
3.13
) is a consequence of Lemma
3.8
, which is proved below. This completes proof of the theorem.
∎

Lemma 3.8
.

If
\alpha\in[0,\frac{1}{2}]
then for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\displaystyle\hat{w}_{n}(n^{3},S)>\frac{(\alpha-1)^{2}}{4}\frac{n^{3}}{\hat{S}_{n}^{2-\alpha}}.

Proof.

Note that one can write
\hat{w}_{n}(n^{3},S)=\frac{\hat{S}_{n}^{\alpha}}{n^{3}}V(n)
, where

\displaystyle V(n)

\displaystyle=1+\frac{1}{(1+\frac{1}{n})^{3}}\Big(1+\frac{2}{n}\Big)^{\alpha}-\Big(\frac{1-\frac{1}{n}}{1+\frac{1}{n}}\Big)^{1-\alpha}-\frac{1}{(1+\frac{1}{n})^{3}}\Big(1+\frac{2}{n}\Big)^{1+\alpha}.

To establish the desired inequality it is enough to prove that for each
n\in\mathbb{N}

(3.14)

\displaystyle V(n)>\frac{(\alpha-1)^{2}}{4}\frac{n^{6}}{\hat{S}_{n}^{2}}=\frac{4(\alpha-1)^{2}n^{2}}{(n+1)^{4}}~~~~~\mbox{holds}.

In case of
n=1
, we denote the difference in inequality (
3.14
) by
T(\alpha)
, where

(3.15)

\displaystyle T(\alpha)

\displaystyle=1+\frac{3^{2\alpha}-3^{\alpha+1}}{8}-\frac{(\alpha-1)^{2}}{4}.

Now differentiation of
T(\alpha)
gives

\displaystyle T^{\prime}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\alpha}{2}+\frac{(3^{2\alpha}2-3^{\alpha+1})\log 3}{8},

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1-\alpha}{2}-\frac{3^{\alpha}}{8}\log 3~~\Big[\mbox{since},~~3^{2\alpha}\geq 3^{\alpha}\Big]

\displaystyle\geq\Big(\frac{1}{4}-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{8}\log 3\Big)>0.

That is we see that
T^{\prime}(\alpha)>0
for any
\alpha\in[0,\frac{1}{2}]
. Again we have
T(0)>0
, therefore
T(\alpha)>0
for any
\alpha\in[0,\frac{1}{2}]
. This proves the inequality (
3.14
).

Let us now choose
n\geq 2
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
. We define a real-valued function
V(x)
on
[0,1/2]
as below

\displaystyle V(x)

\displaystyle=1+\frac{1}{(1+x)^{3}}\Big(1+2x\Big)^{2\alpha}-\Big(\frac{1-x}{1+x}\Big)^{1-\alpha}-\frac{1}{(1+x)^{3}}\Big(1+2x\Big)^{1+\alpha}-\frac{4(\alpha-1)^{2}x^{2}}{(x+1)^{4}}

\displaystyle=\frac{U(x)}{(x+1)^{4}},~~~\mbox{where}

U(x)=(x+1)^{4}+(1+x)(1+2x)^{2\alpha}-(1-x)^{1-\alpha}(1+x)^{3+\alpha}-(x+1)(1+2x)^{1+\alpha}-4(1-\alpha)^{2}x^{2}
. In case of
\alpha=0
, we have
U(x)=(x+1)^{4}+(1+x)-(1-x)(1+x)^{3}-(x+1)(1+2x)-4x^{2}=2x^{4}+6x^{3}>0
. Suppose now that
\alpha\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
. Then repeated differentiation of
U(x)
gives

\displaystyle U^{\prime}(x)=4(x+1)^{3}+4\alpha(x+1)(1+2x)^{2\alpha-1}+(1+2x)^{2\alpha}+(1-\alpha)(1+x)^{3+\alpha}(1-x)^{-\alpha}

\displaystyle-(3+\alpha)(1+x)^{2+\alpha}(1-x)^{1-\alpha}-(1+2x)^{1+\alpha}-2(x+1)(1+\alpha)(1+2x)^{\alpha}-8x(\alpha-1)^{2},

\displaystyle U^{\prime\prime}(x)=12(x+1)^{2}+\alpha(1-\alpha)(1+x)^{\alpha+3}(1-x)^{-\alpha-1}+2(1-\alpha)(\alpha+3)(x+1)^{\alpha+2}(1-x)^{-\alpha}

\displaystyle+8\alpha(2\alpha-1)(x+1)(2x+1)^{2\alpha-2}+8\alpha(1+2x)^{2\alpha-1}-(\alpha+3)(\alpha+2)(x+1)^{\alpha+1}(1-x)^{1-\alpha}

\displaystyle-4\alpha(\alpha+1)(x+1)(2x+1)^{\alpha-1}-4(\alpha+1)(2x+1)^{\alpha}-8(\alpha-1)^{2},

and
U^{\prime\prime\prime}(x)=R_{1}+R_{2}+R_{3}+R_{4}
, where

\displaystyle R_{1}

\displaystyle=24(x+1)-12\alpha(\alpha+1)(1+2x)^{\alpha-1},

\displaystyle R_{2}

\displaystyle=3(1-\alpha)(\alpha+2)(\alpha+3)(1+x)^{\alpha+1}(1-x)^{-\alpha}-(\alpha+1)(\alpha+2)(\alpha+3)(1+x)^{\alpha}(1-x)^{1-\alpha},

\displaystyle R_{3}

\displaystyle=32\alpha(1-2\alpha)(1-\alpha)(1+x)(1+2x)^{2\alpha-3}-8\alpha(1-2\alpha)(1+2x)^{2\alpha-2},~~~\mbox{and}

\displaystyle R_{4}

\displaystyle=\alpha(1-\alpha^{2})(1-x)^{-\alpha-2}(1+x)^{\alpha+3}+3\alpha(1-\alpha)(\alpha+3)(1+x)^{2+\alpha}(1-x)^{-1-\alpha}

\displaystyle+8\alpha(1-\alpha^{2})(1+x)(2x+1)^{\alpha-2}-16\alpha(1-2\alpha)(1+2x)^{2\alpha-2}.

One can immediately sees that
R_{1}>0
for any
\alpha\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
. Note that
R_{2}
can be simplified as below

\displaystyle R_{2}=

\displaystyle(\alpha+1)(\alpha+2)(\alpha+3)(1+x)^{\alpha}(1-x)^{1-\alpha}\Big(\frac{3(1-\alpha)}{1+\alpha}\frac{(1+x)}{(1-x)}-1\Big).

Since
\frac{3(1-\alpha)}{1+\alpha}\geq 1
for
\alpha\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
so we have
R_{2}\geq 0
for any
\alpha\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
. Again for
\alpha\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
, we have
R_{3}=8\alpha(1-2\alpha)(1+2x)^{2\alpha-3}\Big((3-4\alpha)+2x(1-2\alpha)\Big)\geq 0
. Since
4(2x+1)^{\alpha-2}>1
,
(1-x)^{-\alpha-2}(1+x)^{\alpha+3}\geq 1
and
(1+x)^{2+\alpha}(1-x)^{-1-\alpha}\geq 1
in
x\in[0,\frac{1}{2}]
, so we have

\displaystyle R_{4}

\displaystyle>\frac{1}{4}\Big(4\alpha(1-\alpha^{2})+12\alpha(1-\alpha)(\alpha+3)+8\alpha(1-\alpha^{2})-64\alpha(1-2\alpha)\Big)=\frac{S(\alpha)}{4},

where we denote
S(\alpha)
by the following function

\displaystyle S(\alpha)

\displaystyle=-24\alpha^{3}+104\alpha^{2}-16\alpha=24\alpha\Big(\alpha-\frac{13-\sqrt{145}}{6}\Big)\Big(\frac{13+\sqrt{145}}{6}-\alpha\Big).

Clearly
S(\alpha)>0
for any
\alpha\in(\frac{13-\sqrt{1}45}{6},\frac{1}{2}]
. Hence
R_{4}>0
for any
\alpha\in(\frac{13-\sqrt{1}45}{6},\frac{1}{2}]
. Again we note that
R_{4}
is dominated by the function
R(x)
, where

\displaystyle R(x)

\displaystyle=\alpha(1-\alpha^{2})+3\alpha(1-\alpha)(\alpha+3)+8\alpha(1-\alpha^{2})(2x+1)^{\alpha-2}-16\alpha(1-2\alpha)(1+2x)^{2\alpha-2}.

Differentiating
R(x)
, we get

\displaystyle R^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=64\alpha(1-\alpha)(1-2\alpha)(1+2x)^{2\alpha-3}-16\alpha(1-\alpha^{2})(2-\alpha)(2x+1)^{\alpha-3}

\displaystyle=16\alpha(1-\alpha^{2})(2-\alpha)(2x+1)^{\alpha-3}\Big(\frac{4(1-2\alpha)}{(1+\alpha)(2-\alpha)}(1+2x)^{\alpha}-1\Big).

Since
4(1-2\alpha)-(1+\alpha)(2-\alpha)=\alpha^{2}-9\alpha+2=\Big(\alpha-\frac{(9-\sqrt{73})}{2}\Big)\Big(\alpha-\frac{(9+\sqrt{73})}{2}\Big)
so one gets
\frac{4(1-2\alpha)}{(1+\alpha)(2-\alpha)}\geq 1
for any
\alpha\in(0,\frac{(9-\sqrt{73})}{2}]
, and hence
R^{\prime}(x)\geq 0
for
x\in[0,\frac{1}{2}]
and
\alpha\in(0,\frac{(9-\sqrt{73})}{2}]
. As
R(0)>0
so
R(x)\geq 0
for
x\in[0,\frac{1}{2}]
and
\alpha\in(0,\frac{(9-\sqrt{73})}{2}]
. Hence
R_{4}\geq 0
for any
\alpha\in(0,\frac{(9-\sqrt{73})}{2}]
. Finally we get
R_{4}\geq 0
for any
\alpha\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
and
x\in[0,\frac{1}{2}]
. This proves that
U^{\prime\prime\prime}(x)>0

\forall

\alpha\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
.
Therefore
U^{\prime\prime}(x)
is increasing on
(0,\frac{1}{2}]
. Also since
U^{\prime\prime}(0)=U^{\prime}(0)=U(0)=0
, therefore we get
U(x)>0
in
\alpha\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
, and consequently we have
V(x)>0
for
x\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
and
\alpha\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
. Therefore
V(x)>0
for
x\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
and
\alpha\in[0,\frac{1}{2}]
. This proves the Lemma.
∎
