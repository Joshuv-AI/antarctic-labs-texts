A reactive oxygen species-related signature predicts the prognosis and immunosuppressive microenvironment in gliomas
Authors: Xia Shan, Ruoyu Huang, Kuanyu Wang, Pei Yang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjective:Intracellular redox homeostasis is crucial for a series of physiological processes. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) play important roles in redox processes. ROS can maintain cell reproduction and survival at moderate levels while promoting the initiation and progression of tumors at high levels.Methods:Based on a comprehensive analysis of ROS-related gene expression profiles, we established a gene signature associated with ROS to explore its influence on prognosis and immune microenvironment in gliomas.Results:The ROS-related gene expression profile dichotomized patients into two groups with different clinicopathological features and prognoses. A 19-gene ROS-related signature was used to robustly predict prognosis in both training and validation datasets. Functional analysis indicated an association between ROS levels and the immune microenvironment. The expression of immune checkpoints and M2-type markers was upregulated in the high-risk group, which suggested the immunosuppressive function of ROS.Conclusion:ROS-related signature is an independent prognostic factor in gliomas and could potentially exert immunosuppressive effects on the tumor microenvironment.

Introduction

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) are chemically reactive species containing oxygen formed by the incomplete one-electron reduction of oxygen, which includes hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), superoxide anion (O2−), and hydroxyl radical (OH−) [1]. The mitochondrial respiratory chain, nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) oxidase, and peroxisomes are involved in endogenous ROS production. Maintaining redox homeostasis with low basal ROS levels is essential for normal cell proliferation and survival. Cancer, ionizing radiation, heavy metals, and cigarette smoking cause abnormal exogenous elevation of ROS levels, which may trigger cell death and metabolic remodulation [2,3]. Gliomas are one of the most common primary malignant tumors of the central nervous system. ROS are an important component of tumor microenvironment (TME), and their functions have been extensively studied in gliomas. The invasive front of glioblastoma (GBM) produces high level ROS leading to highly aggressive feature [4]. In addition to promoting the occurrence and development of gliomas, ROS can also affect the immune microenvironment and efficacy of adjuvant treatment, which was multidimensional and concentration dependent. The effect could be either stimulatory or suppressive [5]. Radiotherapy have been previously reported to increase the ROS induction, and further activate effector T cell via NFAT/IL-2 signaling [6]. On the contrary, the overloaded ROS may exert the immunosuppressive function mediated by Treg cells [7]. Therefore, we attempt to establish a ROS-related gene signature that can predict the prognosis of gliomas and provide a comprehensive understanding of its role in gliomas.

Materials and methods

Datasets

Three hundred and ninety-three patients with whole grade gliomas were enrolled in the training cohort. Clinicopathological features and corresponding RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data were downloaded from the Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas (CGGA) website (http://www.cgga.org.cn). RNA-seq data from 580 samples from the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database were used for validation. Considered the importance in glioma subtyping and prognosis, isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH), 1p/19q and O6-methylguanine-DNA methyltransferase promoter (MGMTp) were selected as grouping indicator [8]. The approaches of sample collection and processing and biomarker detection have been previously described, including IDH, 1p/19q and MGMTp [9,10]. The details of patients’ clinicopathological information from the two datasets are presented in Table 1 and Table S1. This study was approved by Beijing Tiantan Hospital, and written informed consent was obtained from all patients. Consensus clustering

The ROS-related gene set was obtained from the Molecular Signature Database v7.2 (MSigDB) (http://www.broad.mit.edu/gsea/msigdb/). After removing the duplicates, a gene set containing 576 ROS-related genes was obtained. The most variable genes were used for clustering as measured by the median absolute deviation. The R package ‘ConsensusClusterPlus’ was used in R v4.0.3, to perform consensus clustering (https://cran.r-project.org/bin/windows/base/).

Construction of gene signature

Prognostic gene signatures were screened using the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO). The risk score for each patient was calculated using a linear combination of signature gene expression (expr) weighted by regression coefficients (coef): risk score = (exprgene1 × coefgene1) + (exprgene2 × coefgene2) + … + (exprgenen × coefgenen). The same formula and regression coefficients were used to generate the risk score for the validation cohort.

Bioinformatics analysis

Gene oncology (GO) analysis was applied for functional annotation of genes positively correlated with the risk score (DAVID website). http://david.abcc.ncifcrf.gov/home.jsp). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed to identify the signaling pathways involved. Gene set variation analysis (GSVA) was used to determine the correlation between the 19-gene signature and immune state. The informative genes of the immune cells were acquired from an open-access study with open access [11].

Statistical analysis

R language v4.0.3, SPSS v20, and GraphPad Prism v6.01 were applied for statistical analyses and graphic generation. Chi-square test was performed to detect differences in the clinicopathological characteristics between the groups. Kaplan-Meier (K-M) curve analysis was conducted using log-rank test to evaluate the prognostic differences between the stratified patients. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from surgery to death. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed to identify the independent prognostic factors. P value less than 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Expression status of ROS-related genes in patients with glioma

A total of 393 patients with whole grade gliomas were enrolled as the training set in this study. To profile the expression status of the 576 ROS-related genes, we first performed consensus clustering using RNA-seq data. Cumulative distribution function and consensus matrices were applied to evaluate the optimal number of subgroups. The results demonstrated that patients could be distinctly divided into two subgroups (Figure 1(A–C)). The heatmap showed that the expression status of ROS-related genes varied between clusters (Figure 1(D)). Based on clinicopathological features, we identified that older age, higher grade of tumor, IDH wildtype, and 1p/19q non-codeletion had a higher proportion in cluster 2 (Table 2). Furthermore, survival analysis revealed that patients in cluster 2 had worse prognosis than those in cluster 1 (P < 0.001, Figure 1(I)). These results were verified in the TCGA cohort. Two subgroups were established via consensus clustering, and the OS and clinicopathological features were markedly different between the two clusters (Figure 1(E,F–H,J); Table S2). Identification of a ROS-related prognostic signature in glioma

Considering the prognostic value of ROS-related gene expression, we constructed a gene signature to predict the prognosis of patients with glioma. We used the LASSO Cox regression algorithm to identify the genes with the best prognostic value (Figure 2(A,B)). A 19-gene signature was established and the risk score for each patient was calculated using the formula described above. The same regression coefficient was applied to calculate the risk scores of patients in TCGA dataset for validation. The heatmap shows an overview of the correlation between the 19-gene signature expression and clinicopathological characteristics (Figure 2(C,D)). MGMTp methylation status showed no difference between the low- and high-risk groups (Figure 3(A–H)). Similar results were observed in TCGA dataset, except for sex and MGMTp (Figure 3(I–N); Table S5). Prognostic value of the 19-gene ROS-related signature

To further assess the prognostic significance of the 19-gene signature, we performed K-M survival analysis based on the risk score. The K-M curve showed that patients with low-risk scores had prolonged OS (P < 0.001, Figure 4(A)). The survival difference between the low- and high-risk groups still existed after stratification according to the IDH status (Figure 4(B,C)). In TCGA dataset, patients with low-risk scores had a better prognosis, and this tendency was retained in the IDH mutation group (Figure 4(D,E)). However, in the IDH wild-type group, the survival difference was eliminated, probably because only a few wild-type IDH patients had a low-risk score (Figure 4(F)). After evaluating its influence on survival, we focused on therapeutic effects based on different risk scores. The results showed that, in higher-grade gliomas (grades 3 and 4), patients with high-risk scores were resistant to chemotherapy (Figure 4(G,H)). However, we failed to detect chemoresistance in whole-grade gliomas (Figure S1A, S1B). Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses revealed that the 19-gene based risk score was an independent prognostic factor in both TCGA cohorts (Table 3). Functional annotations of ROS-related signature based on bioinformatics analysis

The results of the GO analysis showed that the positively expressed genes related to the 19-gene signature were functionally involved in the transcriptome process, immune and inflammatory responses, angiogenesis, and cell chemotaxis (Figure 5(A)). Furthermore, the GSEA results demonstrated the activation of certain signaling pathways in the high-risk score group, including the JAK-STAT and TOLL-like receptor signaling pathways (Figure 5(B)). Considering the influence of ROS on the TME, especially the immune microenvironment, the correlation between the risk score and immune cell infiltration is shown in the heatmap. Many immune cells showed an obvious correlation with the risk score, of which activated dendritic cells (DCs), T cells, macrophages, and Th2 cells were enriched in the high-risk score group in both datasets, while CD8+ T cells and CD56 + natural killer (NK) cells were negatively correlated with the risk score (Figure 5(E,F)). We also analyzed the expression of multiple immune checkpoint genes, of which PDIA5 was reported to be a marker of macrophage exhaustion [12]. Red circle indicates a positive correlation with the risk score. The graph demonstrates that PDIA5, CD276, CD274, and IDO1 were upregulated with an increase in the risk score (Figure 5(G,H)). Considering the significance of PDIA5 in macrophages, we analyzed the expression of M1-type and M2-type markers in macrophages. Despite the dynamic process of macrophage polarization, many M2-type markers were upregulated in the high-risk score group (Figure 5(I,J)).

Discussion

High concentrations of ROS have damaging effects, but at lower concentrations, they can act as signaling molecules [3]. These antioxidant defense systems are composed of a series of enzymes, such as superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), glutathione peroxidase (GPX), and glutathione reductase (GSR), as well as non-enzymes, such as glutathione (GSH), α-lipoic acid, and coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10) [13,14]. Elevated intracellular levels of ROS have been recognized as a hallmark of most cancers, where they contribute to many aspects of tumor development and progression, including cell proliferation, genomic instability, apoptotic resistance, cell adhesion and motility, and a metabolic shift to glycolysis [13]. However, constant exposure to high concentrations of ROS leads to cell death. Cancer cells and immunosuppressive cells, including myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and Treg cells, could release high levels of ROS and cytokines, aggravating the suppression of T cell activation [15]. Redox modulation has been considered a promising target for cancer treatment, and the treatment via intervening ROS is efficient in breast cancer, GBM, lung cancer, etc [4,16,17]. However, the concentration-dependent feature makes ROS a double-edged sword, especially in immune modulation. ROS are essential in T-cell activation and hypo-responsiveness. On the other hand, ROS can stimulate both cancer and immune cells to discharge inflammatory cytokines devoting to establish a therapy-resistant and immunosuppressive microenvironment [17]. The administration of antioxidants also faces the same problem that the antioxidants could alleviate the injury of immune cells, but reduced oxidative stress in tumor cells could promote survival and treatment resistance [5]. The understanding of the ROS threshold in cancer cells is still unclear. The concentration and type of ROS, the activity of intracellular antioxidant enzymes, and the type and physiology state of tumor cells are all factors influencing the ROS measurement [13]. In gliomas, tumor cells initiate their own antioxidant defense systems in response to oxidative stress when ROS levels are elevated.

In the present study, we identified a 19-gene signature related to ROS signaling. The prognostic value of the ROS-related signature was fully validated by K-M survival analyses and Cox regression analyses in both CGGA and TCGA datasets. The 19-gene ROS-related signature was an independent prognostic factor in the CGGA and TCGA cohorts, and patients with high-risk scores were prone to gain a more malignant tumor phenotype and impaired survival. Among the gene signature, some genes were identified to be correlated with ROS regulation and had been widely investigated. ANXA1 played a protective role in DNA damage and could exert chemotherapy resistance role by stabilizing ROS in breast carcinoma and colon cancer [18,19]. The effects of FOXM1 regulating ROS production and apoptosis have also been recognized in tumor cells. The inhibition of FOXM1 promoted apoptosis and autophagy via activating the generation of ROS, and mitigated chemoresistance [20]. The absence of FOXM1 made cells more susceptible to oxidative stress, which suggested the potential therapeutic target [21]. Similarly, the overexpression of BRCA1 attenuated ROS production and promoted DNA double strand break repair through non-homologous end joining pathway [22]. The MAP3K5 was crucial in cellular reactions to oxidative stress. Excessive production of ROS was a highly effective stimulator of MAP3K5, and further promoted the sustained activation of downstream P38 and JNK signaling cascades which were associated with damaging inflammatory responses and cell death [23,24]. Together, we had reasons to believe that the expression of the 19-gene reflected the high antioxidant capacity of gliomas to protect tumor cells from apoptosis induced by excess of ROS.

In addition to its influence on survival, ROS can also exert their effects by regulating certain signaling pathways. The physiological activation of signaling pathways by ROS at moderate concentrations involves mitogen-activated protein kinase/extracellular signal-regulated protein kinase 1/2 (MAPK/ERK1/2), c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK), and phosphoinositide-3-kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K/Akt), which subsequently activate nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB), matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF). The intracellular level of ROS exceeding the toxic threshold activates cell death signaling [14,25]. The bioinformatic analyses in our study suggested that the high-risk score was positively related to the transcriptome process, immune and inflammatory response, angiogenesis, etc. Several signaling pathways were upregulated in the high-risk score group, including JAK-STAT and TOLL-like receptor signaling pathways. Toll-like receptors (TLR) play an important role in innate and adaptive immune responses in normal cells, and TLR4 has been identified as a key regulator of autophagy and oxidative stress [26,27]. The JAK-STAT pathway is also functionally involved in the production of a variety of inflammatory cytokines, as well as growth-controlling factors [14].

As important regulatory molecules, ROS have a significant impact on TME, especially on the immune TME [13]. We identified that some immune cells were positively correlated with the risk score, such as activated DCs, T cells, and macrophages. CD8 + and CD56+ NK cells were negatively correlated with the risk score. Moreover, multiple immune checkpoints were upregulated in the high-risk score group, suggesting an immunosuppressive microenvironment, of which PDIA5 was significantly upregulated with an increase in the risk score in both datasets. As the most infiltrated immune cells, macrophages take up more than one third of the total tumor mass and being a compacted relationship with the prognosis of patients [28]. Accord with previous studies, the TME of glioma was inclined to induce the differentiation of macrophages towards the M2 type promoting the tumor progression as well as aggravating immunosuppression. Additionally, the M2-produced ROS could excite Tregs as well as suppress T cells dampening antitumor responses [29,30].

Currently, emerging therapeutic strategies targeting ROS are burgeoning. ROS-based nanomedicine is an alternative strategy for the treatment of glioma. Nano sensitizers can catalyze the production of ROS when combined with photodynamic therapy, photothermal therapy, chemodynamic therapy, sonodynamic therapy, and radiation therapy [31]. There are also several ROS-inducing agents targeting the pathways generating ROS show potential antitumor effects [32]. Therefore, treatment targeting ROS or their combination with other strategies is promising in the future.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we established a 19-gene ROS-related signature that can predict the prognosis of glioma patients. In addition to its prognostic value, high risk indicates a more immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. Thus, the therapeutic value of targeting ROS needs to be explored further.
