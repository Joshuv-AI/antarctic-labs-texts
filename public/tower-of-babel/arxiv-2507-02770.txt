Abstract
. GPU-CC ( GPU-CC ) was introduced as part of the NVIDIA Hopper Architecture, extending the trust boundary beyond traditional CPU-based confidential computing. This innovation enables GPUs to securely process AI workloads, providing a robust and efficient solution for handling sensitive data. For end users, transitioning to GPU-CC mode is seamless, requiring no modifications to existing AI applications. However, this ease of adoption contrasts sharply with the complexity of the underlying proprietary systems. The lack of transparency presents significant challenges for security researchers seeking a deeper understanding of GPU-CC ’s architecture and operational mechanisms. The challenges of analyzing the NVIDIA GPU-CC system arise from a scarcity of detailed specifications, the proprietary nature of the ecosystem, and the complexity of product design. In this paper, we aim to demystify the implementation of NVIDIA GPU-CC system by piecing together the fragmented and incomplete information disclosed from various sources. Our investigation begins with a high-level discussion of the threat model and security principles before delving into the low-level details of each system component. We instrument the GPU kernel module — the only open-source component of the system — and conduct a series of experiments to identify the security weaknesses and potential exploits. For certain components that are out of reach through experiments, we propose well-reasoned speculations about their inner working mechanisms. We have responsibly reported all security findings presented in this paper to the NVIDIA PSIRT Team.



1.
Introduction

Confidential computing enables secure outsourcing of computations involving sensitive data to
TEE
on public cloud infrastructures. Its goal is to ensure the confidentiality and integrity of data while in use. Initial efforts in this field focused on
CPU-CC
(
CPU-CC
), which protects computations within the CPU package and encrypts data residing in system memory. However, as computing workloads have evolved — particularly with the explosive growth of
LLM
— more computation and data are being offloaded to
GPU
to take advantage of their massive parallelism.

To meet this shift, NVIDIA recently introduced support for
GPU-CC

(
Dhanuskodi et al. 2023
)
in its Hopper architecture (as well as in the forthcoming Blackwell architecture). Software support for
GPU-CC
is being gradually integrated into the CUDA and NVIDIA kernel drivers.
GPU-CC
extends the trust boundary from CPUs to
GPU
, encapsulating the
AI
(
AI
) computation pipeline within a unified protected domain leveraging both CPU-CC and GPU-CC technologies.
GPU-CC
significantly broadens the landscape of
AI
applications that require both GPU acceleration and secure execution environments. It establishes a clear separation of roles and privileges among model owners, data owners, and compute providers, while protecting the confidentiality and integrity of computation and data within the trusted domains of both CPUs and
GPU
. Furthermore,
GPU-CC
can also be leveraged in secure collaborative learning
(
Mo et al. 2022
;
Guo et al. 2024
;
Quoc and Fetzer 2021
;
Eichner et al. 2024
;
Gu et al. 2019
;
Cheng et al. 2024a
)
, allowing multiple mutually distrusting parties to jointly perform
AI
computations.

For end users, enabling
GPU-CC
is designed to be seamless —
AI
applications can run without modification. However, enabling GPU-CC demands substantial changes to the underlying system stack. This includes modifications to the CUDA Runtime and user-mode driver, NVIDIA UVM and kernel-mode drivers, and support from a suite of dedicated hardware engines on the GPU. For security researchers and performance engineers, understanding the inner workings of GPU-CC is essential. Yet, analyzing this system poses significant challenges due to the lack of public specifications, the proprietary nature of NVIDIA’s ecosystem, and the architectural complexity involved.

In this paper, we demystify the operations of NVIDIA Hopper GPUs in
GPU-CC
mode. We begin with a high-level overview of the threat model and the security principles guiding its design. We then in-depth examine the key architectural components, the bootstrap process, data protection mechanisms, and the attestation flow that collectively enable
GPU-CC
. Our approach synthesizes fragmented information from diverse sources to reconstruct a coherent and comprehensive understanding of the system. This understanding is grounded in experimental evidence and systematic analysis. For each component, we detail the experimental methodologies employed and highlight the resulting security insights. In cases where direct information is unavailable, we provide well-reasoned speculations to fill in the gaps. Ultimately, this work aims to shed light on the current state of
GPU-CC
and to lay the foundation for future research on this emerging technology within the security community. In summary, this paper makes the following key contributions:

Examination of Threat Model and Security Principles.
We provide a systematic examination of the threat model addressed by NVIDIA
GPU-CC
, outlining the types of attacks it aims to prevent, the components in the root of trust and the categories of information that may remain exposed. We also present the core security principles that underpin the
GPU-CC
system design.

Exploration of System Architecture.
We have conducted an in-depth exploration of the NVIDIA
GPU-CC
architecture, focusing on key components and their roles. Our analysis expands on technical details that are sparsely documented or only briefly mentioned in NVIDIA’s official materials
(
Dhanuskodi et al. 2023
;
Rogers et al. 2023a
;
Rogers et al. 2023b
;
Rogers et al. 2025
)
. This understanding is derived from auditing and instrumenting CUDA stacks, GPU kernel modules, as well as inter-component communications.

Security Analysis of Data Protection Mechanisms.
We have performed dedicated security experiments to evaluate the robustness of NVIDIA
GPU-CC
’s bootstrap and data protection mechanisms. For each mechanism, we assess potential information leaks through untrusted interfaces, discuss risks of exploitation, and suggest possible mitigation. All security findings presented in this paper have been responsibly disclosed to the NVIDIA PSIRT Team.

2.
Background and Related Work

CPU-CC
Technologies.
NVIDIA
GPU-CC
cannot operate independently. It must work in conjunction with
VM
(
VM
) based
CPU-CC
, such as Intel
TDX
(
TDX
)
(
Intel 2023b
;
Cheng et al. 2024b
)
and AMD
SEV
(
SEV
)
(
Kaplan et al. 2016
;
Kaplan 2017
;
AMD 2020
)
.
CPU-CC
protects the confidentiality and integrity of computations and data within
CVM
by enforcing cryptographic isolation from the host environment — including the hypervisor, system administrators, and I/O devices — which are considered untrusted or potentially compromised. This protection relies on security features such as trusted
VM
management and runtime memory encryption. Each
CVM
is assigned a unique ephemeral key that is used to encrypt the memory. The
AES
(
AES
) engine in the on-die memory controller handles the encryption and decryption of data transferred between the CPU and the
CVM
’s private memory.
CPU-CC
also supports remote attestation to verify the authenticity and integrity of the trusted platform.

I/O Protection in
CPU-CC
.
The trust boundary of
CPU-CC
is limited to the CPU and its designated private memory regions. When data leaves this boundary, e.g., during I/O operations, it must pass through untrusted components such as the hypervisor and I/O devices. I/O devices commonly use
DMA
(
DMA
) to read/write memory without CPU involvement. Since these devices are untrusted, they are not permitted direct access to the
CVM
’s private memory, which is encrypted with the
CVM
’s key. The general practice is to employ software-based encryption mechanisms, e.g.,
TLS
(
TLS
) is used for network traffic and
LUKS
(
LUKS
) is used for disk encryption. In this model, I/O-bound data is first encrypted within the
CVM
’s private memory using
TLS
or
LUKS
, then transferred to a staging buffer. This buffer, which is outside the CVM’s encrypted memory, is accessible via
DMA
by I/O devices. Therefore, the encrypted I/O data can be securely transmitted to the devices.

Research on
GPU-CC
.
However,
GPU
is not merely a simple I/O device. Originally designed for graphic rendering, it has evolved into a general-purpose compute device composed of numerous
SM
and equipped with high-bandwidth device memory. Before execution, code and data must be loaded into GPU memory in plaintext. Unfortunately, the PCIe interconnect between the CPU and GPU is generally considered untrusted and is vulnerable to snooping and tampering attacks. Extending the trust boundary of confidential computing from the CPU to the GPU requires establishing end-to-end secure channels that bridge this untrusted interconnect.

Wang and Oswald
(
Wang and Oswald 2024
)
provide a comprehensive survey of recent advances in confidential computing for CPU-GPU systems. Research efforts to support
GPU-CC
began with systems like Graviton
(
Volos et al. 2018
)
and HIX
(
Jang et al. 2019
)
. Graviton proposes modifications to the GPU’s command processor, while HIX introduces changes to the I/O interconnect. However, due to the proprietary nature of NVIDIA GPUs, Graviton’s approach could only be evaluated via emulation, and HIX lacks protection against hardware-based attacks on PCIe and GPU memory. To avoid hardware modifications, researchers have also explored software-based
GPU-CC
approaches. For instance, SAGE
(
Ivanov et al. 2023
)
uses Intel
SGX
(
SGX
) as a local verifier to establish a dynamic root of trust on the GPU, while Honeycomb
(
Mai et al. 2023
)
performs static analysis to validate GPU applications at load time. Confidential computing has also been explored for other accelerators such as IPUs
(
Vaswani et al. 2023
)
and NPUs
(
Lee et al. 2022
)
, where there is generally more flexibility in customizing the hardware. In addition to x86 systems,
GPU-CC
have also been studied in the context of Arm systems
(
Wang et al. 2024
;
Jiang et al. 2022
;
Deng et al. 2022
)
.

One of the greatest challenges in designing
GPU-CC
stems from the immutability of commodity GPUs. While modifying hardware and firmware is the most direct path to enabling secure execution on GPU, it is often impractical. However, GPU vendors like NVIDIA possess the flexibility to add confidential computing functionalities directly into their hardware/firmware. NVIDIA introduced the first commercial
GPU-CC
solution
(
Dhanuskodi et al. 2023
)
as part of its Hopper architecture. Recent research has started to focus on improving the performance of NVIDIA GPU-CC
(
Mohan et al. 2024
;
Tan et al. 2024
;
Tan and Mi 2024
)
. However, a significant gap remains between using the GPU-CC system and understanding its inner workings, largely due to the closed nature of NVIDIA’s ecosystem. This lack of transparency makes it difficult to evaluate whether NVIDIA GPU-CC meets the security requirements of confidential computing or if its implementation can withstand various attack vectors. This paper aims to bridge this gap by presenting the first in-depth examination of the NVIDIA
GPU-CC
architecture, along with a comprehensive security analysis of the system. Our study focuses on the GPU-CC of NVIDIA H100 GPU. At the time of writing, multi-GPU support for GPU-CC is disabled in the current Hopper release, and Trusted I/O is planned for inclusion in the upcoming Blackwell architecture. We discuss both topics in Section
C
of the Appendix.

3.
Threat Model

NVIDIA
GPU-CC

inherits
and
extends
the threat model from VM-based CPU-CC technologies, such as Intel TDX and AMD SEV-SNP.

CPU-CC
’s Threat Model.

The
CPU-CC
threat model is relevant in multi-tenant cloud environments. Adversaries can be external attackers exploiting vulnerabilities to infiltrate the cloud management system or internal attackers like malicious cloud administrators. These adversaries are assumed to have physical or remote access to a host machine, potentially gaining control over the boot firmware,
SMM
(
SMM
), host
OS
(
OS
), hypervisor, and peripheral devices. They can also access host system memory.

The adversaries’ objective is to compromise the confidentiality and integrity of the code and data within a
VM
owned by a cloud tenant. Compromising system availability is not considered in this threat model, as adversaries can control compute resources and launch
DoS
(
DoS
) attacks from the host.

Extension in NVIDIA GPU-CC’s Threat Model.

The assumption is that discrete NVIDIA GPUs are attached to the host system. Adversaries have either physical or remote access to the GPUs, allowing them to query or modify GPU settings—including GPU-CC configurations—via in-band tools (e.g., nvtrust
(
NVIDIA 2025a
)
) or out-of-band interfaces such as the
BMC
(
BMC
). They may also flash the VBIOS of GPU and update the GPU firmware. In addition, adversaries can virtually detach the GPU from one VM and/or reassign it to another VM. Physically removing the GPU from the PCIe bus is also possible, although such actions are generally considered
DoS
attacks.

The interconnect between the CPU and GPU (e.g., PCIe) is considered untrusted, permitting adversaries to interpose on the bus and capture traffic. However, the threat model excludes advanced physical attacks such as decapsulating the GPU package and probing the internal interconnect between GPU cores and on-package
HBM
(
HBM
), as such attacks are considered highly impractical in real-world scenarios. Consequently, unlike CPU-CC, GPU-CC relies solely on access control mechanisms, such as firewall, to protect on-package GPU memory. It does not employ runtime memory encryption to guard against this decapsulation attack.

The adversaries’ objective is to compromise the confidentiality and integrity of the data transferred between
CVM
to the GPUs. This data includes
RPC
(
RPC
) between
CVM
and
GSP
(
GSP
), memory transfer between
CVM
and
GSP
,
UVM
(
UVM
) operations, memory scrubbing operations, delivery of memory faults, CUDA operations, etc.

4.
Security Principles

Figure 1
.
Secure Data Exchange between CVM and GPU

This section discusses the security principles of NVIDIA GPU-CC to address the attacks mentioned in the threat model.

Secure Boot.

Based on the threat model, adversaries are assumed to have access to the GPU and may attempt to flash the VBIOS or update the
GSP
firmware with malicious code. To mitigate such threats, NVIDIA GPU-CC implements a secure boot mechanism that ensures that all firmware components are authentic, untampered with, and cryptographically signed by a trusted authority (e.g., NVIDIA). The hardware engines involved in GPU-CC are described in detail in Section
5
, and their secure boot process is explained in Section
6.1
.

Device Attestation.

While secure boot ensures that the loaded firmware is authentic and signed, it cannot guarantee that the firmware is free of vulnerabilities or has not been revoked. Furthermore, adversaries with access to the host can freely modify GPU-CC settings. To address these limitations, device attestation is employed to verify that the
GPU-CC
system is operating in a secure state, e.g., with up-to-date firmware and drivers, and correctly configured security settings.

GPU-CC
supports the generation of a signed attestation report that includes the device identity, VBIOS and firmware measurements, and current security settings. An attestation agent within the
CVM
can verify this report to ensure that the GPU complies with the expected security policy before enabling
GPU-CC
functionalities. We detail NVIDIA
GPU-CC
attestation in Section
8
.

Access Control.

The
OS
and PCIe devices communicate via
BAR
, which allow the

OS
to access a device’s control registers and memory. In NVIDIA GPUs, BAR0 is mapped to the control registers, while BAR2 (or BAR1, depending on the model) is mapped to GPU memory. When a GPU is passed through to a VM, these BAR regions are also accessible by the VFIO-PCIe driver on the host, which is no longer trusted under the confidential computing’s threat model. To prevent malicious access or manipulation of the GPU from the untrusted host,
GPU-CC
implements two key access control mechanisms:

BAR0 Decoupler.
Only a minimal subset of control registers is exposed through BAR0, while most registers are blocked. We analyze the effectiveness of this mechanism in Section
6.2
.

Restricted Access to the GPU memory.
A substantial portion of the GPU’s memory, e.g., 90%, can be designated as the
CPR
(
CPR
). Direct ingress and egress to the
CPR
are blocked. All data exchange between the CVM and GPU must pass through controlled interfaces. This mechanism relies on
SPDM
(
SPDM
) based key negotiation and uses staging buffers as intermediaries for data transfer. As illustrated in Figure
1
, the
CVM
and
GPU
negotiate encryption keys using the SPDM protocol. The staging buffer is a shared, unencrypted memory region accessible by both the
CVM
and
GPU
. Any data transferred between protected memory regions (i.e.,
CVM
’s private memory or GPU’s
CPR
) must first be encrypted with the negotiated key before being placed in the staging buffer. Upon retrieval, the data is decrypted using the same key. Although the staging buffer is accessible from the host, any data observed by an adversary should remain encrypted and unintelligible. We discuss the
SPDM
key negotiation process in Section
6.3
and analyze all data protection mechanisms and their limitations in Section
7
.

Data Scrubbing.

Since decapsulation attacks are considered highly impractical and thus out of scope, GPU-CC does not implement runtime memory encryption. However, adversaries are assumed to be capable of detaching a GPU from one VM and reassigning it to another. In such cases, residual data in the GPU may reveal information from a prior execution. Moreover, sensitive material, such as cryptographic keys used to protect data in transit, may still reside in GPU memory. To mitigate this risk, it is essential to scrub all memory and reset the device before reuse. This memory scrubbing process is managed by the
SEC2
(
SEC2
) engine, whose mechanism we describe in detail in Section
7.4
.

Multiple architectural engines have been designed, customized, or specialized to support GPU-CC. These include the
FSP
(
FSP
),
GSP
(
GSP
),
SEC2
(
SEC2
), and
CE
(
CE
). As shown in Figure
2
, these engines interact directly with NVIDIA software components, such as the NVIDIA kernel-mode driver,
UVM
driver, and CUDA user-mode driver. In the following, we outline the key functionalities of each engine and their roles in enabling GPU-CC.

5.1.
FSP
(
FSP
)

FSP
is a RISC-V micro-controller that undergoes a secure boot early in the chain-of-trust. Public information about
FSP
is limited. We only know that once the
FSP
is securely initialized, the NVIDIA kernel-mode driver transmits the
GSP
’s
FMC
(
FMC
), and GSP’s
RM
(
RM
) microcode/firmware as binary blobs over a communication channel with the
FSP
. These blobs are digitally signed using NVIDIA’s trusted private key to ensure integrity and authenticity. The
FSP
verifies the signatures before initializing and launching GSP-RM on the
GSP
. A detailed overview of the chain-of-trust bootstrap process can be found in Section
6.1
.

5.2.
GSP
(
GSP
)

The
GSP
offloads
GPU
initialization and resource management tasks from the kernel-mode driver to the
GPU
. The
GSP
engine is a RISC-V micro-controller equipped with
AES
hardware for encryption and decryption. It communicates
exclusively
with the NVIDIA kernel-mode driver.

When
GPU-CC
is enabled, a
SPDM
session is established before launching the GSP-RM. The
GSP
hosts an
SPDM
Responder
, and during the handshake process, the NVIDIA kernel-mode driver negotiates a shared master key with the
SPDM
Responder
. This key serves as the foundation for deriving all shared keys that secure communications between the
CVM
and the
GPU
. Further details on
SPDM
session establishment can be found in Section
6.3
.

Once the GSP-RM is launched, it communicates with the NVIDIA kernel-mode driver via the RMAPI
RPC
channel and transfers memory through the CPU-GSP
DMA
channel. Separate shared keys, derived from the master key, protect data transmitted through the bi-directional
RPC
and
DMA
channels. Additionally, secure software running within the
GSP
may sanitize all inputs and outputs to prevent unauthorized access or tampering by the hypervisor or host. More details on
RPC
communication and secure memory transfers between the CPU and
GSP
are provided in Sections
7.1
and
7.2
.

5.3.
SEC2
(
SEC2
)

The
SEC2
is a RISC-V micro-controller specifically designed for GPU-CC.
SEC2
can only decrypt and verify data, but cannot encrypt data. Unlike
GSP
,
SEC2
is accessible to both kernel-mode clients (e.g., NVIDIA kernel-mode driver and
UVM
driver) and user-mode clients (e.g., CUDA user-mode driver). Based on the function descriptions in NVIDIA’s patents
(
Rogers et al. 2023a
;
Rogers et al. 2023b
;
Rogers et al. 2025
)
,
SEC2
provides multiple security functionalities:

Setup of
CPR
and GPU-CC Mode.

According to the patents
(
Rogers et al. 2023a
;
Rogers et al. 2025
)
of NVIDIA,
SEC2
is responsible for creating the
CPR
and transitioning the GPU into
GPU-CC
mode. The
GPU-CC
mode setting can be stored in an EEPROM attached to the GPU but takes effect only after the next GPU reset. However, the description in the presentation
(
Antoine Delignat-Lavaud 2023
)
differs slightly regarding which engine sets up
GPU-CC
mode. It indicates that this operation is controlled by
FSP
, with
SEC2
not appearing in the diagram. Since the GPU-side implementation is not open, the exact details remain unclear. Nonetheless, both
FSP
and
SEC2
fall within the trusted boundary, meaning this difference in handling the operation does not introduce security implications.

Device Attestation.

According to NVIDIA’s patents
(
Rogers et al. 2023a
;
Rogers et al. 2023b
;
Rogers et al. 2025
)
,
SEC2
is responsible for generating attestation report to prove the authenticity and trustworthiness of the GPU. The device identity key is burned into the key fuse during silicon manufacturing, which is unique and immutable. The device identity key is exclusively accessible by
SEC2
, preventing access from any other entity. The attestation report is signed using an attestation key derived from a hierarchy of cryptographic identities embedded in the silicon. However, presentation
(
Antoine Delignat-Lavaud 2023
)
suggests that
FSP
is responsible for generating the attestation report instead. Although exact details also remain unclear, this difference does not impact the process from the user’s perspective. The detailed process of attestation can be found in Section
8
.

Secure Data Exchange.

SEC2
engine has shared keys with the
CVM
to protect the data in transit. The
CVM
may either sign or encrypt the data using these shared keys before placing it in an unprotected staging buffer. The
SEC2
engine then retrieves the data: if it is signed,
SEC2
verifies its integrity to detect any tampering during transit; if it is encrypted,
SEC2
decrypts the data and stores it in the GPU’s
CPR
.

Memory Scrubbing.

Sensitive data within the
GPU
’s
CPR
must be erased when no longer needed. The NVIDIA kernel-mode driver can establish an SWL Scrubber Channel associated with the
SEC2
engine. When physical memory pages are freed and require scrubbing, commands submitted to the SWL scrubber channel are signed to prevent tampering.
SEC2
can trigger a soft reset of the GPU, ensuring that before the GPU memory becomes visible on the system bus, all shared keys are deleted, and memory is wiped. More details on memory scrubbing can be found in Section
7.4
.

Secure Workload Submission.

SEC2
can be used for bootstrapping secure workload submission. The CUDA user-mode driver and
UVM
driver can allocate secure channels bound to the
SEC2
engine, ensuring command integrity. Commands in this channel can be signed using a shared HMAC key.
SEC2
verifies the integrity of these commands before execution. More details are provided in Section
7.3
.

5.4.
CE
(
CE
)

The
CE
manages memory transfers for GPUs and includes
AES
hardware for encryption and decryption. It utilizes one or more
LCE
and
PCE
, which are implemented as hardware blocks in the GPU.
PCE
handle data movement, while
LCE
manage the control logic for
PCE
.

LCE
are accessible to both kernel-mode clients (e.g., NVIDIA kernel-mode driver and
UVM
driver) and user-mode clients (e.g., CUDA user-mode driver). Clients can allocate secure channels to schedule work with
LCE
, with encryption keys obtained from the NVIDIA kernel-mode driver. Each
LCE
negotiates four keys with the kernel-mode driver in
CVM
: two keys are for user mode usage and two for kernel mode usage. For each mode, one key protects host-to-device (h2d) data transfers, while the other protects device-to-host (d2h) transfers. All channels that are bond to the same
LCE
share the same key.

Data Movement Protection.

CE
ensures that data copied from
CPR
to non-
CPR
memory is encrypted, while data transferred from non-
CPR
to
CPR
is decrypted and integrity-checked. For example, it can retrieve encrypted GPU pushbuffers or CUDA kernels from unprotected memory, decrypting them into
CPR
before executing them. Conversely,
CE
can encrypt and sign data in
CPR
before moving it to unprotected memory, such as when the GPU sends encrypted and signed synchronization signals (e.g., semaphores) to notify command completion. Clients can then decrypt and verify these signals to proceed. Additionally, it may also support plaintext transfers between
CPR
-to-
CPR
and non-
CPR
-to-non-
CPR
memory. We discuss some specific
UVM
cases of using
CE
in Section
7.3
.

Memory Access Control.

CE
, in conjunction with the GPU’s
MMU
(
MMU
), enforces access control by restricting compute engines to
CPR
memory. Once a compute engine accesses
CPR
, it is prevented from accessing any other unprotected memory regions. Any attempt to access memory outside
CPR
is blocked, triggering a fault. Notably, fault delivery occurs across an untrusted interface, necessitating additional protection. More details on fault delivery protection can be found in Section
7.5
.

Replay Attack Prevention.

AES’s
IV
are used to prevent replay attacks. While channels on the same
CE
share the same h2d and d2h keys, each channel independently maintains its own pair of
IV
for h2d and d2h and increments them after each encryption or decryption. As a result, attempts to replay the old ciphertext fail due to authentication tag mismatches.

6.
GPU-CC Bootstrap

This section explains the
GPU-CC
initialization process, covering:
(1) The secure boot of architectural engines required for
GPU-CC
.
(2) Firewall configuration to block regular access to GPU registers and
CPR
via the
BAR
.
(3)
SPDM
session establishment to negotiate cryptographic keys for securing communication between a
CVM
and a GPU.

6.1.
Secure Boot of Architectural Engines

According to the information shared in the NVIDIA developer forum
(
NVIDIA 2024
)
, the chain of trust follows this sequence: CEC EROT (if present)
\rightarrow

FSP

\rightarrow

GSP

\rightarrow

SEC2
. Here, CEC EROT refers to Microchip’s
CEC1736

(
MICROCHIP [n. d.]
)
, a real-time
RoT
(
RoT
) controller, where EROT stands for “External Root of Trust.” The
CEC1736
plays a critical role as a hardware
RoT
, anchoring the secure boot process and establishing the system’s chain-of-trust. Both CEC EROT and
FSP
’s BootROM authenticate
FSP
firmware. This dual-layer verification ensures both external and internal integrity check before
FSP
is fully booted.

Once the
FSP
has securely booted, the NVIDIA kernel-mode driver sends a command to the
FSP
via the
MCTP
(
MCTP
) to initiate the
GSP
boot process. This command includes the system memory address of the
GSP
-
FMC
image along with its authentication fields — such as the hash, public key, and signature — enabling the
FSP
to verify the authenticity and integrity of the microcode before launching the GSP-RM. Additionally, the command contains boot arguments for
GSP
-
FMC
, allowing it to fetch, authenticate, and load the GSP-RM firmware. Notably,
GSP
-
FMC
is distributed as a pre-generated binary archive within the NVIDIA kernel-mode driver’s source code, while GSP-RM is provided as a binary file installed by the driver in the system directory. This approach provides the flexibility to update the
GSP
firmware at runtime.

The
GSP
is responsible for verifying and booting the
SEC2
engine, though the detailed steps remain unclear, as the process is concealed within the
GPU
. The method for provisioning and updating
SEC2
firmware is also unknown. We speculate that the launch of
SEC2
likely follows a similar secure boot and authenticated code loading approach. The
SEC2
firmware image should be cryptographically signed by NVIDIA, with the
GSP
verifying the signature using an embedded public key — potentially stored in the hardware or
OTP
(
OTP
) memory. Once all checks are passed, the
GSP
can proceed to load and initialize the
SEC2
engine.

6.2.
Firewall

In non-GPU-CC mode, system administrators on the host can freely access and modify the GPU’s control and status registers, as well as its device memory, through
BAR
. These registers configure various GPU settings, while the device memory may contain sensitive user data provisioned within the
CVM
. Since the host — along with its administrators and software stack — is no longer considered trusted in the confidential computing’s threat model, BAR-based PCIe access substantially enlarges the attack surface.

To mitigate this, NVIDIA’s paper
(
Dhanuskodi et al. 2023
)
states that enabling
GPU-CC
mode activates a PCIe firewall mechanism, which blocks unauthorized access to the majority of control registers and the
CPR
of the GPU’s memory. However, a limited subset of registers remains accessible to support essential GPU management operations.

These registers reside in the address space mapped by BAR0. A critical concern is determining which registers remain accessible after
GPU-CC
is enabled and whether their exposure poses any potential security risks. Unfortunately, the specifications for NVIDIA GPU control registers are proprietary, and no public documentation is available detailing the functionality of each register. Only a limited number of register fields have been referenced through the
GPU kernel modules
and the
nvtrust
utility.

Although the precise semantics of individual registers remain unclear, we attempt to quantitatively assess the difference in register accessibility before and after enabling
GPU-CC
mode. To this end, we developed a scanning program that simulates an adversary probing the entire BAR0 space on two H100 GPUs — one operating in regular mode and the other in GPU-CC mode. The program reads from BAR0 starting at offset 0x0 and advances in 4-byte strides. Given that BAR0 spans 16 MB on H100, this results in a total of 0x400000 read operations.

We categorize the returned values into three types: (1)
Values
: non-zero numerical returns, (2)
Zeros
: reads that return 0x0, and (3)
Errors
: reads returning error codes in the form of
0xbadxxxxx
. As shown in Figure
3
, in non-GPU-CC mode, 7.94% of the fields return values, and 80.25% return errors. In contrast, in GPU-CC mode, 99.78% of the fields return zero, with only 0.02% (1,042 fields) returning non-zero values and 0.19% returning errors. This significant zeroing effect is attributed to the activation of the firewall’s
BAR0 Decoupler
, which hides most control registers.

From an adversarial perspective, the decoupler obfuscates register visibility by returning zeros, making it difficult to determine whether a field is simply unmapped or protected. However, the small fraction (0.02%) of accessible fields raises security concerns. We advocate for transparency from NVIDIA regarding the functionality of these exposed registers and their necessity for GPU management.

Beyond register access, the PCIe firewall also blocks host access to the
CPR
of GPU memory. This restriction fundamentally alters how data is exchanged between the
CVM
and GPU. We examine the implications and mechanisms of this enforcement in detail in Section
7
.

This section explores the mechanisms for securing data transmitted across the untrusted interface. For each mechanism, we examine the following aspects: the
methodology

observations

security insights

7.1.
CPU-GSP RPC

Methodology.

The NVIDIA kernel-mode driver communicates with the GSP-RM via the physical RMAPI
RPC
interface over a bi-directional channel. The driver sends commands to the
GSP
for offloading specific tasks, while the
GSP
responds with execution status and
GPU
events to notify the driver. Since the
RPC
occurs over an untrusted medium that attackers on the host can examine and interpose, all data transmitted through the
RPC
channel must be encrypted.

We intercepted all physical RMAPI invocations from the NVIDIA kernel-mode driver and captured their corresponding responses from the
GSP
. Each command type follows a specific encoding, automatically generated by NVIDIA’s
FINN
tool. To better understand the semantics of each RPC invocation, we extracted the encodings of 1,588 RMAPI commands from NVIDIA’s
SDK
(
SDK
) and replaced them in our captured log.

Observations.

The RPC infrastructure is initialized during the GSP engine construction phase. A shared memory region is allocated in unprotected system memory for communication, consisting of a physical address table, a command queue, and a status queue. The physical address table, which holds the physical addresses of all pages in the shared region, informs the GPU of the physical memory layout. The command queue stores commands and their parameters sent from the driver to the
GSP
, while the status queue contains status information returned from the
GSP
to the driver. The locations of these structures are communicated to the
GSP
during initialization.

To send a command, the driver first sets up the command header with a sequence number and element count. The header also includes an
AAD
(
AAD
) buffer, an authentication tag, and a checksum, but it remains in plaintext. The driver then encrypts the command payload in private memory using the
cpu_gsp_locked_rpc
key and computes a checksum over both the header and the encrypted payload, recording the value in the header. The header and encrypted payload are then copied into the command queue in the staging buffer. The
GSP
retrieves the command, verifies the checksum and the integrity of the encrypted payload, decrypts it using the same key, and executes the command.

To send a status update or notify the driver of an event, the
GSP
encrypts the status from its
CPR
using the
gsp_cpu_locked_rpc
key and copies the status header along with the encrypted payload into the status queue in the staging buffer. The driver retrieves the data from the status queue, copies it to private memory, and verifies the checksum and the integrity of the encrypted payload. Then it decrypts the status payload and processes it accordingly.

Security Insights.
It is important to note that only the command and status
RPC
payloads are encrypted in the unprotected staging buffers, while the physical address table, queue headers, and queue element headers remain in plaintext and are exposed to potential attackers on the host. Figure
4
illustrates these data structures by dumping memory from their corresponding physical addresses in the hypervisor.

The physical address table consists of 129 entries (Figure
4
shows the first four). The first entry stores the table’s own physical address, followed by one entry for the TX queue’s header, 63 entries for TX queue elements, one entry for the RX queue’s header, and 63 entries for RX queue elements. If an attacker gains access to the physical address table, they can easily locate all elements in the TX/RX queues. For example, the second entry (
0x000000016921f000
) points to the TX/RX queue’s header, where the
readPtr
and
writePtr
fields indicate the next elements to read and write. These fields can then be used as indices in the address table to locate active elements in queues.

For instance, the third entry (
0x0000000169297000
) points to a specific queue element. Each element contains a header with fields such as
authTagBuffer
,
aadBuffer
,
checkSum
,
seqNum
, and
elemCount
, followed by the encrypted RPC payload. The plaintext header can also reveal significant information about the currently active element. For example, if the
elemCount
has a value greater than one, it can be used to infer the
RPC
type with high accuracy

A key challenge is locating the physical address table within the large physical memory space of a
CVM
. The vulnerability arises because the first entry in the address table stores its own physical address (e.g.,
0x000000016921e000
in Figure
4
). Given that the address table is page-aligned, an attacker can perform a memory scan in 4096-byte strides (the typical page size) across the entire physical memory. If a 64-bit physical address found during the scan
equals
the value stored at that location, the attacker can uniquely identify the starting address of the physical address table, potentially exposing critical memory mappings.

To prevent the leakage of RPC execution status, we recommend encrypting not only the RPC payload but also the metadata of the TX/RX queues mentioned above.

7.2.
CPU-GSP Memory Transfer

Methodology.

GPU typically exposes a dedicated window via the PCIe
BAR
into its on-package memory.
BAR
2 is a large aperture, providing the host with a virtual address space for accessing the GPU’s memory. However, once GPU-CC mode is enabled, the
BAR
2 access to the
CPR
is blocked. The read and write to the data within
CPR
should use alternative routing. For the memory transfers between
CVM
and
GSP
, the driver uses the CPU-GSP
DMA
. As
DMA
cannot access the private memory in the
CVM
directly, the driver needs to allocate and map a staging buffer in the unprotected memory, which is not protected by the
CVM
’s key.

In our experiment, we intercepted all memory transfers between the
CVM
and
GSP
and recorded their execution time throughout the lifecycle of a
CVM
. Our goal is to investigate how the source or destination aperture and the size of memory transfers impact execution time and whether the potential presence of timing channels can be exploited.

Observations.

To read data from the GPU’s
CPR
, the driver sends a memory transfer
RPC
command with parameters specifying the size of memory size, the source in the GPU’s
CPR
and the destination in a staging buffer located in unprotected system memory. The protection for transmitting this RPC command has been discussed in Section
7.1
. Upon receiving the command, the
GSP
encrypts the source data in the CPR using the
gsp_cpu_dma
key and copies it via
DMA
into the unprotected staging buffer. The driver then transfers the data to private memory and decrypts it using the same key.

To write data to the GPU’s
CPR
, the driver first encrypts the data in the
CVM
’s private memory using the
cpu_gsp_dma
key and copies it to the unprotected staging buffer. The driver then sends the memory transfer command via RPC again, specifying the memory size, the source in the staging buffer, and the destination in the
CPR
. Upon receiving the command, the
GSP
copies the data via
DMA
from the unprotected staging buffer, decrypts it using the same key, and writes it to the target address in the
CPR
.

Security Insights.

We recorded the execution time for 4,394 memory transfers between the CPU and GSP throughout an entire
CVM
lifecycle, consisting of 453 read operations and 3,941 write operations. Our goal is to analyze whether the transfer size impacts execution time. Figure
5
presents histograms of both read and write operations, with the x-axis representing execution time and the y-axis showing the count of memory transfers within each time range. Different colors indicate varying memory transfer sizes, ranging from 8 bytes to 4,096 bytes.

Our observations reveal that both read and write operations exhibit a bimodal distribution of execution times. For small transfer sizes (8 to 256 bytes), the execution time is largely dominated by the inherent cost of the
RPC
itself, making the effect of memory size relatively insignificant. However, for larger transfers (4,096 bytes, represented by blue bars), a noticeable shift occurs, with execution times increasing significantly compared to smaller transfers.

To mitigate potential timing side-channel attacks — where an attacker monitoring the RPC channel in untrusted memory could infer sensitive information from execution time variations — we recommend implementing constant-time execution for
RPC
invocations or introducing statistical noise to obfuscate timing patterns.

7.3.
UVM
(
UVM
)

Methodology.

NVIDIA’s
UVM
system provides a unified address space across system memory and GPU memory, enabling seamless memory access by managing memory migrations, page faults, and coherence automatically.
UVM
relies on pushbuffers to send GPU methods for execution on various memory operations.

In traditional non-CC mode,
UVM
utilizes
CE
to facilitate memory operations. A pushbuffer is allocated in system memory and mapped for both CPU and GPU access. The CPU populates the pushbuffer with GPU method streams and writes a
GPFIFO
entry containing the pushbuffer’s address and length. It then updates the
GPPUT
pointer and rings the doorbell to notify the
CE
. The
CE
subsequently fetches the methods from the
GPFIFO
and executes them. Upon completion, it signals the CPU to clean up the used
GPFIFO
entries.

In GPU-CC mode, GPU cannot access the private memory
of the
CVM
and
BAR
2 access to the GPU’s
CPR
is also disabled. Consequently, key components essential for
UVM
operations — such as the pushbuffers,
GPFIFO
entries,
GPPUT
pointer, and synchronization semaphores — can no longer be directly accessed or modified by either the CPU or GPU, as shared memory is untrusted. To mitigate the risk of compromise or leakage, these components require additional security measures while residing in unprotected staging buffer.

The implementation of
UVM
in GPU-CC is significantly more complex, involving multiple phases and recursive interactions. To better understand its execution, we instrumented both the NVIDIA kernel-mode driver and the
UVM
driver, restructuring the control flow into a clear temporal sequence. Our primary focus is analyzing the interactions between different engines, such as
SEC2
and
CE
, and examining how their derived keys protect specific target components within this new memory management model.

Observations.

Three specialized channels have been introduced in UVM for GPU-CC: the
SEC2
channel, the
WLC
(
WLC
), and the
LCIC
(
LCIC
).

SEC2
Channel
: The
SEC2
channel is the first to be created, serving as the foundation for bootstrapping secure workload submission. Its primary role is to verify and set up the
WLC
and
LCIC
channels. There is only one
SEC2
channel, which is associated with the
SEC2
engine and uses two cryptographic keys:
cpu_sec2_hmac_kernel
and
cpu_sec2_data_kernel
, which are used for signing methods and encrypting payloads respectively.

WLC
Channel
:
WLC
channels are responsible for indirectly launching
UVM
workloads. They process encrypted
UVM

CE
pushes submitted by the
UVM
driver, verify their integrity, and dispatch them to selected
CE
for execution. The
SEC2
channel initializes 16
WLC
channels, corresponding to the maximum number of concurrent pushes. Each
WLC
channel is associated with a
LCE
with two keys:
lce{x}_h2d_kernel
for host-to-device encryption and
lce{x}_d2h_kernel
for device-to-host encryption, where
{x}
denotes the index of the associated
LCE
.

LCIC
Channel
: Similar to
WLC
channels,
LCIC
channels are also initialized by the
SEC2
channel. Each
LCIC
channel is paired with a corresponding
WLC
channel, sharing the same
LCE
association, resulting in a total of 16
LCIC
channels. The
LCIC
channel is designed to track the execution progress of its paired
WLC
channel.

The workflow can be divided into two phases:

Phase I
: Setup of WLC/LCIC via SEC2.
The
SEC2
channel acts as the anchor of trust for bootstrapping
WLC
and
LCIC
channels. SEC2’s
GPPUT
,
GPFIFO
entries, and pushbuffers are allocated in an unprotected staging buffer and remain unencrypted. However, the methods in the
SEC2
pushbuffers are signed with the
cpu_sec2_hmac_kernel
key. Thus, the
SEC2
engine can verify the integrity of the methods to detect any tampering.

With GPU-CC enabled, the
GPPUT
,
GPFIFO
, and pushbuffers of WLC/LCIC channels must be placed in GPU’s
CPR
to protect them from unauthorized access. The UVM driver populates an
SEC2
push
to set up these data structures for WLC/LCIC channels. This process repeats for all 16
WLC
and
LCIC
channels. At this stage, the
WLC
/
LCIC
channels are launched but not yet operational. The next step is to set up their schedules.

The WLC schedule consists of two alternating pushes: (1)
decrypt_push
— Responsible for decrypting the next
run_push
and placing it into CPR. The
decrypt_push
is static and remains unchanged. (2)
run_push
— Executes GPU methods to launch a
UVM

CE
push that contains the payloads for the UVM operations, and advances the LCIC channel’s
GPPUT
by one step for synchronization. The
run_push
is mutable, as its parameters must be dynamically updated to accommodate different
UVM

CE
pushes.

The
LCIC
schedule is entirely static and simply advances the
WLC

GPPUT
by two steps ahead, ensuring that the
WLC
channel always starts from the
decrypt_push
in the next cycle.

Finally, the static parts (marked blue in Figure
6
) of the WLC/LCIC schedules are uploaded and their

entries are updated by the
SEC2
push
. Once this is complete, the WLC/LCIC channels are fully operational and ready for accepting UVM CE pushes.

Phase II
: Indirect Launch of UVM CE Pushes via WLC.

Once the WLC/LCIC channels are fully initialized, they can be selected at runtime to indirectly launch a
UVM CE push
. A
UVM CE push
’s pushbuffer contains methods for executing a specific
UVM
task, e.g., writing
PTE
for an address range.

To execute a
UVM CE push
, the
UVM
driver selects a
WLC
channel and encrypts the scheduled
UVM CE push
’s pushbuffer using the
lce{x}_h2d_kernel
key of that

channel. The encrypted pushbuffer is placed in the unprotected staging buffer. A corresponding
run_push
for the
WLC
channel is generated based on the parameters of the
UVM

CE
push
, encrypted, and also stored in the staging buffer. The
decrypt_push
, preloaded during Phase I, executes first to decrypt the

into
CPR
. Once decrypted, the
run_push
begins execution by decrypting the
UVM

CE
push
’s pushbuffer, setting up the
GPFIFO
and
GPPUT
within
CPR
, and triggering it to run on another
CE
. The
UVM

CE
push
uses the keys,
lce{y}_h2d_kernel
and
lce{y}_d2h_kernel
, tied to the newly attached
CE
. Finally, the
run_push
signals the
LCIC
channel to advance the
WLC

, preparing it for the next execution cycle.

Throughout this process,
WLC
,
LCIC
, and
UVM

CE
update their respective tracking semaphores, which are then encrypted and stored in the unprotected staging buffer. The
UVM
driver continuously monitors these encrypted semaphores to track execution progress. The decryption of tracking semaphores is handled by different keys:
lce{x}_d2h_kernel
for the
WLC
/
LCIC

and
lce{y}_d2h_kernel
for the
UVM

CE

.

Security Insights.

In
UVM
, the system relies on the signed push of
SEC2
channel to bootstrap WLC/LCIC channels. The
SEC2
push
is not encrypted since it only consists of predefined methods without embedded secrets. However, the
GPFIFO
,
GPPUT
, and semaphores of the
SEC2
channel reside in an unprotected staging buffer and are neither signed nor encrypted. Adversaries might exploit this by manipulating the
GPFIFO
and
GPPUT
to redirect the execution to a crafted pushbuffer. However, since the methods themselves are signed, the SEC2 engine can still detect and prevent tampered methods from being executed. As a result, such an attack would fall under
DoS
, which is generally considered out of scope.

The critical data structures for
WLC
,
LCIC
, and
CE
command queues — such as
GPFIFO
and
GPPUT
— are securely placed in

. The pushbuffers, including WLC’s

and
UVM

, are encrypted while in the unprotected staging buffer before being transferred into

. Unlike the
SEC2

, these buffers are mutable and contain sensitive information, including memory addresses and

7.4.
Memory Scrubbing

Methodology.

Memory scrubbing is used for deliberate overwriting of memory contents to clear out sensitive information. This prevents the recovery of confidential data, which is particularly important in secure environments where old data could otherwise be exploited by unauthorized users. Typically, the NVIDIA kernel-mode driver establishes a scrubber channel with the
CE
, where it submits GPU methods for memory scrubbing, allowing the
CE
to execute the task. However, when GPU-CC is enabled, a dedicated and secure channel is required to submit memory scrubbing methods to prevent tampering through the untrusted interface. Instead of using
CE
,
SEC2
is used for memory scrubbing when GPU-CC is enabled, as it supports
signed push
.

In our experiment, we intercepted the signing function invocations in the
SEC2
utility responsible for signing methods in the SWL scrubber channel. We then backtracked the call trace to identify the origin of memory scrubbing requests within the GPU’s memory manager. This approach provided a deeper understanding into the mechanism underlying GPU’s memory scrubbing.

Observations.

The NVIDIA kernel-mode driver establishes an SWL scrubber channel linked to the
SEC2
engine to handle memory scrubbing tasks securely. When the GPU’s memory manager needs to free certain memory pages, it populates a pushbuffer with a sequence of methods for a secure memory set operation using
SEC2
. These method streams are then submitted to the SWL scrubber channel.

To ensure integrity, the method streams in the pushbuffer are signed using the
cpu_sec2_hmac_scrubber
key, and their HMAC digests are stored in the authentication tag buffers. Two types of authentication tags are generated — one for scrub methods and the other for semaphore methods. The GPU virtual addresses of these authentication tag buffers are passed as parameters within the methods. Semaphores play a crucial role in tracking authentication tag usage and signaling the status of method execution. They are also configured within the commands to trigger specific notifications for the driver.

Once all methods are added to the pushbuffer, it is linked to the
GPFIFO
queue of the SWL scrubber channel, and a doorbell signal is sent to initiate execution on
SEC2
engine. On the GPU side, the
SEC2
engine retrieves the method streams from the SWL scrubber channel and verifies their integrity by validating the HMAC digests against the
cpu_sec2_hmac_scrubber
key. This ensures that the method streams remain untampered with as they traverse the untrusted interface.

Security Insights.

In earlier versions of the NVIDIA kernel-mode driver,
SEC2
memory scrubbing reused the
cpu_sec2_hmac_kernel
key for signing method streams. However, in the latest driver version, dedicated scrubber keys —
cpu_sec2_hmac_scrubber
and
cpu_sec2_data_scrubber
— have been introduced specifically for scrubbing tasks. The
cpu_sec2_hmac_scrubber
key is now used for signing methods, while the
cpu_sec2_data_scrubber
key exists but remains unused. Given that the pushbuffer and the authentication tag buffers in the SWL scrubber channel are currently allocated in the unprotected staging buffer and lack encryption, we speculate that
cpu_sec2_data_scrubber
may eventually be used to protect these structures.

Additionally, the
SEC2
engine only supports decryption and does not provide encryption capabilities. Notably, while there are
cpu_sec2_*
keys for securing data sent to
SEC2
, there are no corresponding
sec2_cpu_*
keys for protecting data from
SEC2
back to the driver. This means that the semaphores used to notify the driver remain unprotected. The rationale behind
SEC2
’s lack of encryption support is unclear and needs further clarification from NVIDIA. This could present a security risk, as attackers on the host could observe the unencrypted pushbuffers and authentication tag buffers — though they cannot tamper with commands due to integrity verification — and manipulate the unprotected semaphores.

7.5.
GPU Memory Faults

Methodology.

In
GPU-CC
mode, handling GPU memory faults presents unique challenges due to the stricter memory access control. Typically, memory faults occur when a program attempts to access out-of-bound memory, dereference null pointers, or write to freed memory. In a non-CC environment, both the GPU and NVIDIA drivers have access to a shared hardware buffer, allowing the GPU to log fault packets directly into the buffer, which the driver can then retrieve and handle. However, in
GPU-CC
mode, this hardware buffer is placed within the GPU’s
CPR
, making it inaccessible to the CPU. As a result, a new mechanism is required to securely transfer fault packets through an untrusted interface.

To analyze this memory fault workflow in
GPU-CC
mode, we developed a CUDA program designed to deliberately trigger memory faults. By instrumenting the NVIDIA kernel-mode driver and
UVM
driver, we intercepted RPC invocations used for setting up the staging buffers to deliver the fault packets and monitored the use of cryptographic keys related to memory faults.

Observations.

To meet memory access control requirements in GPU-CC, the
UVM
driver requests the NVIDIA kernel-mode driver to allocate two shadow buffers — one for replayable memory faults and another for non-replayable memory faults. These shadow buffers reside in unprotected staging buffers, enabling the
GSP
to write directly to them. The NVIDIA kernel-mode driver registers these shadow buffers with the
GSP
through the GSP-RM RPC, informing the
GSP
of their structures. The
GSP
then encrypts the fault packets using the
gsp_cpu_replayable_fault
or
gsp_cpu_non_replayable_fault
key before copying them into the shadow buffers. Once the fault packets are stored, the
GSP
sends a
MMU_FAULT_QUEUED
event via the RPC return path, notifying the driver. The UVM driver monitors this event and schedules an
ISR
(
ISR
) to retrieve the encrypted fault packets from the shadow buffers. Each fault packet includes an
authentication tag
and a
valid
field (used as
AAD
). The
UVM
driver uses the corresponding key to verify and decrypt incoming packets, then parses them to handle memory faults effectively while preserving data confidentiality and integrity.

Security Insights.

According to the comment
(
NVIDIA 2025c
)
in the
UVM
driver’s source code, replayable faults originate from the
GE
(
GE
), while non-replayable faults come from other engines. In non-CC mode, these two types of fault follow different handling paths. The
UVM
driver exclusively manages replayable faults and owns the hardware buffer that stores them. In contrast, handling non-replayable faults requires interaction with the NVIDIA kernel-mode driver via a specific
shadow buffer
since only the kernel-mode driver can access the hardware buffer for non-replayable faults.

However, in
GPU-CC
mode, where the GSP-RM owns the hardware buffer and the CPU lacks direct access, both replayable and non-replayable faults rely on shadow buffers to store encrypted fault packets. Therefore, their handling paths become similar in GPU-CC mode. Additionally, the GSP-RM need to update the shadow buffer’s
PUT
pointer whenever a new fault packet is placed. NVIDIA repurposes the access counter registers mapped in BAR0 to expose this pointer to the CPU. This means that attackers can also observe this value on the host. However, given that memory faults occur infrequently, the risk of exposing the
PUT
pointer remains low.

7.6.
CUDA

Methodology.

Since the CUDA user-mode driver is not open source, its execution flow under GPU-CC remains opaque. To analyze its behavior, we intercepted its interactions with external components that we could instrument. Our approach involved developing a sample CUDA application that performed key operations such as GPU memory allocation, host-to-device data transfers, and CUDA kernel launches. However, instead of using the CUDA Runtime APIs, we directly invoked the low-level CUDA Driver APIs to gain finer control over execution. For instance, we compiled the CUDA kernel into
PTX
(
PTX
) code and launched it via
cuLaunchKernel
.

To further dissect communication flows, we instrumented the NVIDIA kernel-mode driver and
UVM
driver, capturing their interactions with the CUDA user-mode driver. Additionally, drawing insights from PipeLLM
(
Tan et al. 2024
)
, which identified OpenSSL APIs as being used for encryption, decryption, and MAC verification by CUDA, we also instrumented relevant OpenSSL functions. By preloading a custom version of the OpenSSL library, we intercepted all cryptographic operations invoked by the CUDA user-mode driver, allowing us to observe how security mechanisms are enforced under GPU-CC.

Observations.

By intercepting OpenSSL functions, we are able to monitor the keys used by the CUDA user-mode driver and map them to those derived from the master secret negotiated during the SPDM session. Our analysis reveals that the CUDA user-mode driver retrieves two categories of keys from the NVIDIA kernel-mode driver through the
GetKMB
API: (1)
cpu_sec2_data_user
/
cpu_sec2_hmac_user
keys — These are associated with the
SEC2
engine and are likely used for encrypted data transfer and integrity verification with
SEC2
. (2)
lce{x}_h2d_user
/
lce{x}_d2h_user
keys — These are tied to specific
LCE
and facilitate encrypted data transfers between CPU and GPU’s
CE
. It is worth noting that, since CUDA operates in user space, all these keys are post-fixed with
_user
, indicating their privilege level.

Security Insights.

The CUDA user-mode driver has limited interactions with the NVIDIA kernel-mode driver, primarily occurring during: CUDA API/context initialization, kernel channel creation and key retrieval, and memory cleanup. However, the most security-critical operations related to launching CUDA kernel remain self-contained within the user-mode driver, making them less observable. Given the limited information available, we can propose the following reasonable speculations regarding CUDA kernel execution under GPU-CC:

(1) Protecting the command queue’s data structures is essential. The
GPFIFO
,
GPPUT
, pushbuffers, and semaphores should reside in the GPU’s
CPR
or be encrypted when stored in unprotected staging buffers. A mechanism similar to bootstrap secure work submission using
SEC2
, as seen in
UVM
, could be employed. The
cpu_sec2_hmac_user
key could be used to sign
SEC2 pushes
, while the
cpu_sec2_data_user
key could encrypt data passed to the SEC2 engine. Work submission may be performed indirectly through
SEC2
or other secure channels, e.g.,
WLC
, which itself is securely bootstrapped by
SEC2
.

(2) Protecting the code/data transfer between CUDA applications and the GPU is necessary. This includes both the compiled CUDA kernel and the data referenced by the kernel during execution. We speculate that
CE
should play a crucial role in maintaining confidentiality, with the
lce{x}_h2d_user
key encrypting data copied from the
CVM
to the GPU, and the
lce{x}_d2h_user
key protecting data transferred back to the
CVM
.

(3) Protecting the meta-data structures associated with CUDA kernel is also critical. The
QMD
(
QMD
), which contains information such as kernel function addresses, kernel arguments, and execution configurations, must be secured. Since the
QMD
address is passed as a parameter in the GPU method within the command queue, it should be encrypted in the unprotected staging buffer and transferred into the
CPR
before CUDA kernel execution.

Device Attestation

GPU’s Device and RIM File Certificate Chains

Device attestation is a process that is used to establish the trustworthiness of a hardware component by verifying its authenticity and integrity. In the context of
GPU-CC
, attestation enables users to confirm that the GPU is manufactured by a trusted vendor and that its confidential computing features are correctly enabled. A successful attestation provides an assurance to users and third parties that the GPU can securely execute code and process sensitive data. NVIDIA’s
GPU-CC
attestation follows the
RATS
(
RATS
) specification
(
Birkholz et al. 2023
;
Overby 2023
;
Nertney 2025
)
. Below, we describe the concrete steps involved in attesting a confidential GPU.

Device Certificate Chain Retrieval/Validation.
The process begins with each NVIDIA GPU being provisioned with a unique cryptographic identity: a hardware-fused device identity key and a corresponding
Device Identity Certificate
. This certificate, anchored to NVIDIA’s Root
CA
(
CA
), forms the basis of a certificate chain used to establish trust. When a verifier running inside a
CVM
initiates attestation, it retrieves the device certificate chain. As illustrated in the left part of Figure
7
, the chain comprises five certificates: the
Attestation Certificate
and
Device Identity Certificate
are retrieved from the GPU, while the remaining
Provisioner Certificate
,
Model Certificate
, and
Root Certificate
are obtained from the NVIDIA kernel-mode driver.

The
Device Identity Certificate
corresponds to the immutable
GSP

BROM
(
BROM
) and is signed by the NVIDIA Provisioner
CA
. The
Device Identity Certificate
uniquely identifies the device as an authentic NVIDIA GPU. The immutable
GSP
-
BROM
starts execution and ensures that the system begins in a secure state.
The
GSP
-
FMC
is loaded and authenticated.
Specific hardware security fuses (configured at manufacturing) are measured. These measurements are used to derive the attestation key pair. The private attestation key is used to sign attestation reports. The
Attestation Certificate
is signed by the device identity’s private key, establishing a chain-of-trust. It contains the public attestation key required to verify the attestation report. The root of the certificate chain is the
Root Certificate
, a self-signed certificate that serves as the trust anchor.

The verifier first replaces the
Root Certificate
with a local one to prevent a compromised driver from undermining the certificate chain. It then verifies the chain in reverse order, starting with the
Device Identity Certificate
and proceeding up to the
Root Certificate
. During this process, the verifier sends multiple requests to the NVIDIA
OCSP
(
OCSP
) service
(
NVIDIA 2025b
)
to check the revocation status of each certificate. If the chain passes both the signature and revocation checks, the verifier proceeds to request the attestation report.

Attestation Report Retrieval/Validation.
The verifier then retrieves a signed attestation report, which contains measurement data and an opaque metadata block that identifies the GPU’s firmware and driver. The measurement data consists of 64 structured records, each containing a measurement specification, a size field, and a cryptographic hash of a measured component (e.g., firmware, configuration). The opaque data block provides information to identify the GPU driver and firmware version. The verifier validates the attestation report’s signature using the
Attestation Certificate
.

Measurements Retrieval/Validation.
The verifier derives the driver and VBIOS IDs from the opaque block and retrieves the corresponding
RIM
(
RIM
) files from the NVIDIA
RIM
service
(
NVIDIA 2025d
)
. The verifier checks the status of the
RIM
files by validating their schemas and endorsements. Each RIM file includes a certificate chain. The verifier appends a local
Root RIM Certificate
to complete the chain (the left portion of Figure
7
) and verify it. Once the chain is validated, the leaf certificate, i.e., the
RIM File Certificate
, is used to verify the signature on the RIM file. To validate the measurements, the verifier compiles a list of golden measurements from the
RIM
files. It compares each measurement in the attestation report with the allowed values from the golden list. If a mismatch is found, the verifier concludes that the GPU is not in an expected state.

If all checks succeed, including certificate validation, signature verification, and measurement comparison, the verifier reports the attestation result and the user can choose to transition the GPU into the
READY
state.

9.
Conclusion

The NVIDIA GPU-CC system is designed to provide an isolated and secure execution pipeline for emerging AI applications that handle sensitive data. However, the lack of public specifications, the proprietary nature of its hardware and software ecosystem, and the complexity of its design present significant challenges in evaluating whether its implementation satisfies the security requirements of confidential computing. This paper takes the first step toward demystifying the inner workings of GPU-CC and establishing a foundation for future security research. We describe the security principles and architectural components of
GPU-CC
and present a series of in-depth security experiments that assess potential information leaks and explore possible avenues of exploitation. All security findings have been responsibly disclosed to NVIDIA to help strengthen the security of GPU-CC.

Appendix C
Future Directions

Here, we discuss the security features expected in upcoming GPU-CC releases, along with the architectural and software changes required to support them.

C.1.
Multi-GPU Support

As AI models continue to grow in size, a single GPU’s memory often cannot accommodate the full model or dataset, necessitating multi-GPU support for both inference and training tasks. On our NVIDIA H100 SXM5 platform, each H100 GPU is connected to all four NVSwitches via 4th-generation NVLinks, enabling high-bandwidth communication between GPUs.

In GPU-CC’s threat model, adversaries capable of intercepting traffic at the NVSwitches or along the NVLinks between GPUs and NVSwitches may be able to observe or tamper with inter-GPU data transfers. While NVIDIA’s paper
(
Dhanuskodi et al. 2023
)
describes the multi-GPU support, at the time of writing, current software releases only support passing through a single confidential GPU to a single
CVM
. We speculate that enabling multi-GPU passthrough with GPU-CC will require updates to the VBIOS, GPU firmware, and NVIDIA drivers. According to the latest NVIDIA OC3 presentation
(
Nertney 2025
)
, even if multi-GPU support is enabled on Hopper, data transmitted over NVLink remains unencrypted. NVLink encryption is expected to be introduced with the upcoming Blackwell architecture.

Supporting GPU-CC in a multi-GPU setting introduces new requirements for securely transferring data across GPUs. Specifically, peer-to-peer keys must be established to protect memory transfers between each pair of GPUs. A GPU cannot directly access another GPU’s CPR, as the interface between them is considered untrusted. Although CPR occupies the majority of GPU memory, a small unprotected portion remains available and can serve as a staging buffer, which is accessible by other GPUs. To transfer data securely, the sending GPU must first encrypt the data and write it to the staging buffer. The receiving GPU can then copy the encrypted data from the buffer and decrypt it into its own
CPR
.

C.2.
Trusted I/O Support

One limitation of the current GPU-CC architecture is that the GPU cannot directly access the private memory of a
CVM
. Instead, data must be transferred through a staging buffer, introducing additional software-based encryption and decryption overhead. This not only complicates software development — particularly when determining which data objects to protect — but also increases the risk of leaving some data unprotected during transmission.

A promising direction to address this limitation is the development of Trusted I/O for confidential computing. Trusted I/O requires architectural support from both CPU-CC and GPU-CC. On the CPU side, technologies like Intel TDX Connect
(
Intel 2023a
)
and AMD SEV-TIO
(
AMD 2023
)
aim to enable this capability. On the GPU side, NVIDIA’s upcoming Blackwell architecture is expected to introduce Trusted I/O support as well.

Intel and AMD’s Trusted I/O technologies rely on a combination of standardized protocols and trusted components to ensure secure device integration and communication within confidential computing environments. Protocols such as
TDISP
(
TDISP
),
SPDM
, and
IDE
(
IDE
) are central to this infrastructure.
TDISP
governs the device interface lifecycle, while
SPDM
is used for device authentication and establishing secure communication channels.
IDE
provides encryption for PCIe traffic, securing both control and data paths.

Key trusted components include the
TSM
(
TSM
) and the
DSM
(
DSM
). The
TSM
is responsible for defining and enforcing security policies and managing secure communication with devices. The
DSM
, a trusted logical component within the device, works in conjunction with the
TSM
to establish secure channels.

Device attestation is supported through
SPDM
and the
RIM
, which validate device identity and firmware integrity. When a device is assigned to a
CVM
, the host initiates attestation by communicating with the device’s
DSM
using
SPDM
. The
TSM
verifies the identity and measurements of the device, checks them against security policies, and passes the evidence to the
CVM
for final evaluation. If the device is verified, it is admitted into the trusted environment.

Once a device is trusted, it can perform
DMA
to private guest memory. In Intel TDX Connect, this access requires the device to be included in the
CVM
’s
TCB
(
TCB
). The TDX module manages translation of device transactions to private memory, with the IOMMU enforcing these mappings and ensuring access is restricted to assigned devices. Secure PCIe transactions use IDE
TLP
marked with a T-bit to signal trusted communication. In AMD SEV-TIO,
DMA
access is permitted after the guest establishes trust in the device. The IOMMU maintains a
SDT
(
SDT
) that tracks the security attributes of devices and enforces access control accordingly.

Overall, both platforms ensure a secure and authenticated path for data and control between hosts, devices, and guest
CVM
, building trust through layered verification and secure protocol enforcement.
