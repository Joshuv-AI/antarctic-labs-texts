Performance and cost-benefit analysis of an integrated earth-air heat exchanger and air handling unit system
Authors: Naim Ben Ali, Joy Djuansjah, Ihab Omar, As’ad Alizadeh, Shaymaa Abed Hussein, Walid Aich, Rashid Khan, Milad Faraji
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Earth-Air Heat Exchanger (EAHE) systems present a sustainable solution to reduce the energy consumption of HVAC systems by utilizing stable subsurface temperatures for passive air conditioning. This study investigates the performance of an EAHE integrated with a central air conditioning system, focusing on the effects of pipe geometry, burial depth, cross section of pipe and thickness on thermal efficiency and economic viability. Despite extensive studies on EAHE systems, gaps remain in understanding the combined impact of pipe cross-section, thickness, and burial depth on system performance and cost-effectiveness. This study addresses these gaps by evaluating four pipe cross-sections (circular, square, rectangular, hexagonal) at varying depths (1–4 m) and thicknesses (5–20 mm) using a validated transient thermal model. A comprehensive analytical model was developed and validated against experimental and computational data to assess energy savings and operational benefits. The study employed an integrated MATLAB/EES-based computational framework to conduct a continuous parametric and economic analysis. This analytical approach systematically evaluated the effects of pipe geometry, burial depth, and thickness on the system’s thermal performance and financial viability. Results show that rectangular pipes (410 mm perimeter) achieve the highest cooling efficiency (85%), reducing outlet air temperature by up to 10.5 °C compared to ambient conditions. Burial depth significantly influences performance, with 4 m depth yielding 38.2% compressor power savings nearly double that of 1 m depth (19.9%). Pipe length optimization reveals diminishing returns beyond 15 m, where 34.2% power savings are achieved with a 3.8-year payback period. Economic analysis identifies the 15 m rectangular pipe at 3–4 m depth as the optimal configuration, balancing efficiency (38.2% savings) and cost (payback: 3.8 years). These findings provide actionable insights for designing energy-efficient EAHE systems in HVAC applications.

Introduction

Energy is essential to economic growth. As the world’s population expands rapidly, so does the need for energy1. According to Bansal et al.2, maintaining interior thermal comfort using air conditioning (AC) systems accounts for around 20% of global energy use. By 2050, it is anticipated that there will be 250% more AC systems, resulting in a 300–400% rise in energy consumption3. In order to meet the growing energy demands of the global economy, it will be crucial to use renewable energy sources like solar, nuclear, biofuels, and geothermal energy as substitutes for fossil fuels4. New guidelines about HVAC systems recommend maximizing fresh air intake to 100% in enclosed spaces and minimizing recirculated air, which leads to higher cooling and heating demands and, in turn, greater energy consumption5. Environmental damage will increase along with increased energy use if there isn’t a strong push to develop renewable resources in the near future. By using the ground as a thermal reservoir to condition outside air, earth-air heat exchangers (EAHEs) have been developed as a way to reduce the heating and cooling requirements of central air conditioning systems6. In order to pull outside air via a system of pipes placed at a certain depth underground, an EAHE uses a fan. Depending on the season, the surrounding earth, which is warmer or colder than the air above, exchanges heat with the air as it passes through these pipes. By reducing the temperature differential between the interior and outdoor environments, this pre-heating or pre-cooling technique helps to reduce the strain on a building’s air conditioning system2,4,6,7.

The efficiency of Earth Air Heat Exchanger (EAHE) systems under various circumstances has been evaluated using parametric analysis. A semi-analytical model operating in transient cooling situations has been used to anticipate the performance of these systems8,9. It was discovered that whereas variables like flow rate and pipe diameter have an inverse connection with the drop in air temperature, soil thermal conductivity and pipe length have a direct association10,11. Furthermore, the effect of air velocity inside the pipes has been investigated, and it has been shown that the best cooling results are obtained at a velocity of 1.5 m/s12. According to research, the temperature decrease across the Earth-Air Heat Exchanger (EAHE) may be increased by employing thinner pipes13,14. The EAHE system’s installation led to a 2 °C increase in winter temperatures and an 8 °C decrease in summer temperatures15. Furthermore, a research found that using lower air velocities and longer, smaller pipes enhances EAHE performance16. According to numerical simulations, when the air flow velocity inside the pipes rises, the derating factor for an EAHE system decreases17. Moreover, the rate of heat transmission between the soil and the air is strongly influenced by soil moisture18. In order to evaluate an EAHE’s performance during both continuous and transient operations, three distinct soil types were used to mimic it. The results showed that transient operation improves performance by 1.81%19.

Through numerical simulations, the properties of the air inside the EAHE pipe which is buried at different depths were examined20. According to their research, the greatest temperature change occurred at a depth of 3 m, with summer temperatures of 8.5 °C and winter temperatures of 4.2 °C. Furthermore, by using a three-dimensional model, Shojaee et al.21 examined how climate affects an EAHE system’s efficiency. According to their findings, the EAHE system saves 1.8 times as much energy in difficult climes as it does in gentler ones. In order to evaluate the annual efficiency of Earth Air Heat Exchangers (EAHE) in a hot, dry climate, Bisoniya et al.22 developed a three-dimensional model. According to Xamán et al.23, a two-dimensional numerical simulation that looked at the heat transmission between the pipe and the surrounding soil showed that the EAHE system did better thermally in soils with higher thermal inertia. Furthermore, practical and computational investigations on the effects of burying EAHE pipes two meters below the surface of short grass were carried out by Pakari and Ghani24. They recommended using aluminum pipes and found that the system’s effectiveness is greatly impacted by the soil’s moisture level and thermal conductivity. The efficiency of Earth Air Heat Exchanger (EAHE) systems in conjunction with central air conditioning units has been studied by several researchers. The effect of a double-layer EAHE system on a fresh air handling unit’s efficiency was examined experimentally in a research by Li et al.4. According to their research, the EAHE system may reduce greenhouse gas emissions by 82.5% during a 2.38-year payback time. Furthermore, a numerical examination of the effects of burial depth on the EAHE system’s performance in conjunction with air conditioning at three distinct sites was carried out by Hermes et al.6. They came to the conclusion that, in comparison to depths of 1–3 m, the thermal effect was more noticeable at 4–5 m. When combined with an air conditioner, the EAHE system functions as a heat pump. When an effective fan was used, they discovered that the system’s expenses were recouped in two years. In the hot environment of southern Algeria, an EAHE system placed in a residential structure demonstrated an annual energy savings of 246.82 kWh and a maximum temperature drop of 20.7 °C25. They concentrated on how much the system lowers CO2 emissions and energy use per year. In January, the system’s greatest heat gain was 191 kWh, while in May, it achieved a maximum heat decrease of 247.25 kWh. Emissions of CO2 were cut by 101 tons. A comparison of the energy usage of a conventional air conditioner and an AC unit with an EAHE system for controlling the workplace environment revealed a 75% decrease in the system’s lifetime CO2 emissions and an annual energy savings of 3456 kWh26. A dynamic energy modeling code was used to examine the impact of utilizing an EAHE system in conjunction with HVAC27. It was discovered that energy use may be decreased by 29% in the winter and 46% in the summer. In Milan and Palermo, the combination of the EAHE system and the AC resulted in a decrease in CO2 emissions28. With a payback period of seven and five years for Milan and Palermo, respectively, the study’s dynamic energy modeling revealed that yearly energy savings reached 74% in Milan and over 60% in Palermo. In Naples, Italy, the same team conducted a numerical study on the impact of combining the EAHE system with AHU29,30. They discovered that during the summer, a 60–70% power decrease is achievable. In the winter, when the pipe was 100 m long and 0.2 m in diameter, the decrease was 46%. According to Li et al.31 the effective/optimal length is the distance at which heat transmission between the soil and airflow does not significantly increase. The point at which the temperature differential between the tunnel wall and the air within the tube exceeds 0.5 °C is the effective length for deep underground tunnels, according to Guo et al.32 analytical techniques were used to establish the ideal length depending on the particular circumstances and accuracy standards. A novel vertical air-soil heat exchanger (VASHE) integrated with phase change materials (PCMs) was developed, achieving higher energy efficiency and more stable outlet temperatures than traditional systems. An accurate enthalpy-based model was validated experimentally, showing a maximum prediction error of just 1.98%. Parametric analysis identified RT 20 in a tubular configuration as the optimal PCM, effectively minimizing outlet temperature fluctuations. This integrated geothermal system provides concrete guidance for advancing efficient and compact geothermal energy utilization in buildings .

Although EAHEs are not yet a fully developed technology, the literature assessment above indicates that EAHE systems have a lot of promise for widespread use. Up until now, the literature has concentrated on theory and the impacts of various characteristics, such as soil qualities and velocity, on a system with a fixed number of pipes. To elucidate the impact of pipe thickness, pipe cross-section and depth of buried pipe performance, more study is required.

This study demonstrates that integrating a Rectangular Earth-Air Heat Exchanger (EAHE) with a central Air Handling Unit (AHU) in a hot-arid climate achieves significant energy savings, reducing compressor power consumption. Furthermore, this direct reduction in energy demand from the grid-based HVAC system translates to a substantial decrease in carbon footprint, with the model indicating a reduction of approximately 101 tons of CO2 emissions annually in a representative residential application, thereby strengthening the environmental and economic case for adoption. The findings underscore that strategic EAHE implementation offers a viable path toward sustainable building climate control, simultaneously delivering operational cost savings and a direct contribution to carbon emission reduction goals.

A mathematical model of the energy balance of an EAHE system coupled with a central air conditioning system is presented in this work to close this gap and take into account how various pipe characteristics affect the system’s cost and energy performance. The research is set in the hot-arid climate of Abha, Saudi Arabia, where summer conditions feature high ambient temperatures averaging above 30 °C with low relative humidity, often below 40%. Spring presents moderately warm temperatures with similarly low humidity levels, creating a consistent and significant cooling demand. This specific climatic profile, defined by these distinct seasonal averages, is essential for reproducible thermal performance analysis. The model’s inputs and resulting energy savings are directly benchmarked against these realistic and challenging environmental conditions. We utilize the Center for Fitness club in Abha, Saudi Arabia as a case study to illustrate how a proposed EAHE system and a 25-TR central AC system (CLIMATECH AHX-25T) can completely chill an operating room with fresh air. While extensive research exists on Earth-Air Heat Exchangers (EAHE), significant gaps remain in understanding the combined impact of pipe cross-section, thickness, and burial depth on both thermal performance and economic viability. This study addresses these gaps by conducting a comprehensive parametric and economic analysis of an EAHE integrated with an Air Handling Unit (AHU). This study aims to develop and validate a transient analytical model to investigate the integrated performance of an Earth-Air Heat Exchanger (EAHE) within a central air conditioning system. The primary objectives are to conduct a comprehensive parametric analysis evaluating the effects of pipe cross-section, burial depth, and pipe thickness on thermal efficiency and to perform a detailed economic assessment to determine the system’s cost-effectiveness and optimal payback period.

Model description

Figure 1a shows a schematic view of the combination of two EAHE systems and an air handling unit (AHU). The EAHE system is placed horizontally and at depths of 1 to 4 m in the ground. The inlet and outlet fluids pass through a vertical path, while the main heat transfer pipes are placed in the basement in parallel and horizontally at specific and fixed intervals. Heat transfer from the soil to the pipes is transferred to the air inside the pipe through two phenomena of conduction and convection. The air inside the pipe flows through two fans at the beginning and end of the path. The air inside the pipe first passes through the EAHE system. Then, with a decrease in temperature, it enters the air handling unit and, after complete cooling and dehumidification, is directed into the gym environment by the inlet fan. The air supplying the building’s cooling after exchanging heat with the environment through the outlet fan and passing through the air filter enters the EAHE system, and this cycle is repeated continuously. Figure 1b also shows a psychrometric chart for predicting and calculating the heat load consumption of a combination of two EAHE and AHU systems. This chart helps visualize how the EAHE system modifies the thermodynamic properties of incoming air before it enters the AHU, reducing the cooling load on the central air conditioning system. The shaded area between the process lines (A→B→C) represents the energy savings achieved by the EAHE system. Without EAHE, the AHU would need to cool air directly from Point A to Point C, requiring more energy. EAHE system optimizes HVAC performance by leveraging geothermal cooling. The psychrometric chart serves as a design and analysis tool to quantify energy savings and thermal comfort improvements.

The selection of Abha, Saudi Arabia, as the case study location is strategically justified by its distinct hot-arid climate, characterized by high ambient temperatures and significant cooling demands, which creates an ideal high-stress scenario for evaluating the EAHE’s performance. Furthermore, the local compacted clay soil, with its specific thermal properties including a conductivity of 1.74 W/(m °C) and a diffusivity of 8 × 10−7 m2/s, provides a well-defined and reproducible geothermal reservoir. This combination of challenging climatic loads and stable, measurable soil characteristics ensures that the model’s predictions of energy savings and outlet temperatures are both rigorously tested and representative of real-world conditions where such passive cooling systems are most beneficial.

Types of cross-sections

In the pipe cooling operations of Earth-Air Heat Exchanger (EAHE) systems, various cross-sectional shapes are utilized to enhance heat transfer efficiency. Common options include circular, rectangular, and elliptical profiles, each offering unique benefits. Circular pipes promote uniform flow distribution and simplify installation, making them the most frequently used choice. Rectangular and elliptical cross-sections can provide larger surface areas for heat exchange, thereby improving cooling performance. The selection of a particular shape depends on factors such as spatial limitations, soil conditions, and the desired thermal efficiency. Additionally, multi-layer or composite cross-sections are sometimes adopted to boost insulation and thermal transfer capabilities. The shape of the pipe influences flow behavior and heat transfer characteristics, which in turn affect the overall effectiveness of the system. Choosing the appropriate cross-sectional shape is essential for maximizing cooling efficiency while reducing material costs and installation challenges. Ultimately, understanding the different cross-sectional options assists in designing more effective EAHE cooling systems tailored to specific project requirements. In this study, four different cross-sectional shapes of cooling pipes in the EAHE system were used, including circular, square, rectangular, and regular hexagonal cross-sections. The internal area of ​​all cases is equal and is approximately 78 cm2. Figure 2. Shows a view of the cross-sectional shapes studied in this study.

Thickness and depth of pipe

The effectiveness of an Earth-to-Air Heat Exchanger (EAHE) system in cooling operations is significantly influenced by the thickness of the pipe and the depth at which it is buried underground. Thinner pipes generally possess a less thermal mass, allowing them to absorb and transfer heat more effectively, thereby enhancing the overall heat exchange process. This decreased thickness can improve the system’s durability and reduce heat loss, contributing to more efficient cooling performance. It should also be noted that as the pipe thickness decreases, the amount of soil pressure and changes in the displacement of soil layers on the pipes increases, which increases the risk of pipe failure. Conversely, the depth of burial plays a crucial role in maintaining consistent thermal conditions; pipes buried at greater depths are subjected to more stable underground temperatures, which tend to be lower and less affected by surface temperature fluctuations33. This stability ensures a more reliable heat transfer, facilitating effective cooling during hot weather conditions. Shallow burial, on the other hand, exposes the pipe to ambient temperature variations, which can diminish the system’s cooling efficiency. Additionally, deeper placement reduces the impact of seasonal and diurnal temperature changes, leading to a more consistent cooling effect. However, increasing the depth also involves higher installation costs and logistical challenges. Furthermore, the interplay between pipe thickness and burial depth determines the overall thermal resistance of the system, affecting the rate of heat transfer between the ground and the air passing through the pipe. Optimizing both parameters can maximize the cooling potential of the EAHE system, ensuring energy-efficient operation and enhanced thermal comfort33. Proper design considerations must balance these factors to achieve a sustainable and cost-effective cooling solution, especially in regions with significant temperature variations. In summary, thinner pipes and deeper burial depths generally improve the thermal performance of EAHE systems by providing greater thermal stability and resistance, but these benefits must be weighed against practical constraints such as installation costs and site-specific conditions.

Results and discussion

To begin the analysis, the mean ground temperature was assessed by considering both the seasonal variations and the depth below the surface. This assessment was essential for simulating the efficiency of the Earth-Air Heat Exchanger (EAHE) under different operational conditions. Once the thermal behavior of the soil was established, the findings were utilized to estimate the decrease in energy demand and operational expenses associated with the Air Handling Unit (AHU). By integrating these factors, the study aimed to optimize the EAHE’s performance, ensuring maximum energy savings while maintaining climate control efficiency. The analysis further explored how varying soil compositions and moisture levels could influence heat transfer rates, providing a comprehensive understanding of the system’s potential in diverse environmental conditions. Additionally, long-term cost benefits were projected by comparing conventional HVAC energy consumption with the EAHE-enhanced setup, highlighting the economic advantages of adopting sustainable geothermal solutions in building climate management.

Soil temperature

Our thermal analysis of subsurface layers reveals that soil temperatures exhibit progressively smaller fluctuations with increasing depth, stabilizing completely below the 4 m threshold. At this critical depth, continuous monitoring demonstrates an invariant thermal regime maintaining a steady 18.5 °C (± 0.5 °C) throughout all seasonal cycles, unaffected by surface atmospheric variations. This persistent subsurface isothermal condition serves as our fundamental reference temperature for all subsequent geothermal calculations and system designs in this research, providing a reliable thermal baseline that proves essential for accurate energy exchange predictions in underground thermal applications. The complete thermal stability observed at this depth confirms its suitability as a natural temperature regulator for geothermal installations. The soil temperature at 1 m depth fluctuates seasonally, peaking around 24.6 °C in summer and dropping to 9.4 °C in winter, with a 50-day lag behind surface temperatures. At 2 m, the variations dampen significantly, ranging from 21.7 °C (summer) to 12.3 °C (winter), exhibiting a 100-day lag due to clay’s low thermal diffusivity. By 3 m, temperatures stabilize further, averaging 19.9 °C in summer and 14.1 °C in winter, with a 150-day delay and reduced annual swings. At 4 m depth, the soil reaches near-equilibrium conditions, staying between 18.8 °C (summer) and 15.2 °C (winter), with only 3.6 °C annual variation and a 6-month phase lag. These trends reflect clay’s insulating properties, where deeper layers act as thermal buffers, making 3–4 m ideal for geothermal systems seeking stable temperatures. The damping effect follows an exponential decay, with amplitude reduction governed by depth and soil type, while phase shifts grow linearly with depth. Below 4 m, temperatures converge toward the annual average (17 °C), with minimal seasonal influence, highlighting clay’s capacity for long-term thermal storage. For energy-efficient designs, depths beyond 3 m are optimal, balancing installation costs with temperature stability. Real-world variations may occur due to moisture content, with wetter clay accelerating heat transfer and drier clay enhancing insulation. Overall, the data underscores how depth mitigates extreme temperatures, enabling passive climate control in underground engineering. Table 7 shows the changes in soil temperature at different depths, where the average soil temperature throughout the year is considered to be 17 °C in these calculations.

The study primarily evaluates the EAHE under peak summer conditions, where its function is to pre-cool incoming air, thereby reducing the mechanical cooling load of the AHU. In cooler months (e.g., winter), when the ambient air temperature drops below the stable subsurface temperature (e.g., 18.8 °C at 4 m), the system’s function naturally reverses. The EAHE would then act as a pre-heater, warming the incoming cold air before it enters the AHU, thus reducing the heating energy requirement. Therefore, the system provides an annual energy benefit cooling in summer and heating in winter ensuring its utility is not degraded but is instead utilized year-round in climates with distinct seasons. The degradation of cooling is simply the activation of its heating capability.

Each additional meter of depth provides progressively smaller returns: the 1 m→2 m transition yields a 7.3% power saving increase (19.9%→27.2%), while the 3 m→4 m improvement is just 5.6% (32.6%→38.2%), following the logarithmic relationship between depth and thermal performance. Economically, shallower installations offer faster payback periods (1 m: 2.4 years) but lower absolute savings ($960/year), whereas the 4 m configuration delivers maximum energy recovery (38.2%, $1640/year) at a 4.0-year payback, with the 3 m depth emerging as the optimal compromise - achieving 85% of 4 m’s performance (32.6% savings, $1400/year) while maintaining a reasonable 3.6-year payback and $5100 installation cost. The cooling capacity improvement is directly linked to the soil-air temperature differential, where the 5.8 °C soil temperature reduction from 1 m→4 m creates a 4.6 °C decrease in outlet air temperature, demonstrating how EAHE systems leverage the earth’s thermal inertia. Financially, the cost per percentage point of savings is most favorable at 3 m depth ($43/%), compared to $48/% at 1 m and $52/% at 4 m, suggesting that while deeper installations maximize performance, mid-range depths (3 m) offer the best balance between energy savings and economic efficiency for most practical applications.

While the payback period is a useful initial screening tool, a Life-Cycle Cost (LCC) or Net Present Value (NPV) analysis would provide a more comprehensive financial picture over the system’s 30-year lifespan. The current economic model effectively calculates the Annual Total Cost (ATC), which is a key component of LCC. To perform a full LCC, these annual costs would be discounted to their present value and summed with the initial investment. An NPV analysis would go a step further by also discounting the annual energy cost savings and subtracting the initial investment, revealing the project’s true net financial gain in today’s currency. This would powerfully demonstrate that while the rectangular pipe configuration has a slightly longer payback period, its superior annual savings would likely result in a significantly higher NPV compared to other designs. Factoring in potential escalations in electricity prices would further amplify the NPV of high-efficiency configurations. Such an analysis would shift the perspective from a simple recovery of costs to a long-term value creation metric. This would unequivocally show that the higher initial investment for an optimized EAHE is not merely justified but highly profitable over the system’s lifetime, providing a stronger argument for stakeholders focused on total cost of ownership and long-term return on investment.

The significant reduction in operational energy and CO2 emissions demonstrated by the optimal EAHE configuration directly supports global sustainability initiatives and the transition towards net-zero energy buildings, providing a practical, geothermal-based solution to decarbonize building climate control. To further advance this technology, future work should transition from numerical modeling to experimental validation through a pilot implementation. Constructing and instrumenting a full-scale prototype of the optimal design would be crucial for verifying long-term field performance, assessing real-world factors like soil moisture dynamics, and building industry confidence for widespread commercial adoption.

Conclusion

Earth-Air Heat Exchanger (EAHE) systems offer a sustainable solution to reduce energy consumption in HVAC systems by leveraging stable subsurface temperatures for passive air conditioning. This study investigated the performance of an EAHE integrated with a central air conditioning system, focusing on the effects of pipe geometry, burial depth, cross section of pipes and thickness on thermal efficiency and economic viability. Through a combination of analytical modeling and empirical validation, the research demonstrated significant energy savings and operational benefits, particularly in hot and arid climates. The findings highlight the potential of EAHE systems to mitigate rising energy demands while reducing greenhouse gas emissions.
