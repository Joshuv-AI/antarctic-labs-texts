Health-related quality of life in patients with psoriasis in Asian countries: a systematic review and meta-analysis of EQ-5D utility scores
Authors: Harshavardhan K, Priyankaa V, Hariharan Murugadoss, Lavanya Ayyasamy, Madhumitha Haridoss
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Health utility estimates are important for cost-utility analyses, treatment prioritization, and health technology assessment in psoriasis. Region-specific estimates may be particularly valuable for informing these decisions in Asian settings. This systematic review and meta-analysis synthesized EQ-5D utility scores reported in Asian psoriasis populations and explored variation by country of study, psoriasis type, EQ-5D version, value set, and disease severity. A systematic search of PubMed, EMBASE, Scopus, the Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar from inception to July 2025 identified studies reporting EQ-5D index or EQ-VAS scores in Asian adults with psoriasis. Cross-sectional, cohort, case-control, and randomized controlled studies were included. Two reviewers independently screened studies and extracted data. Risk of bias was assessed using AXIS, NOS, and RoB-2. Random-effects meta-analyses, subgroup analyses, sensitivity analyses, and domain-wise proportion analyses were conducted. Fourteen studies comprising 2,322 patients with psoriasis were included. The pooled EQ-5D utility index was 0.84 (95% CI: 0.78–0.89; I² = 95.00%) across 10 studies with 1,453 participants. Subgroup analyses suggested variation by country of study, psoriasis type, EQ-5D version, and value set. The pooled EQ-5D VAS score was 70.73 (95% CI: 67.01–74.46; I² = 87.10%) from eight studies with 1,227 participants. In moderate-to-severe psoriasis, the pooled EQ-5D utility estimate was 0.80 (95% CI: -0.14 to 1.75) and the pooled EQ-VAS score was 63.08 (95% CI: 38.71–87.45). Domain-wise analysis indicated that pain/discomfort was the most commonly reported problem domain (22%), followed by mobility (20%), whereas self-care was least frequently reported. Funnel plot asymmetry and Egger’s test suggested possible small-study effects for EQ-5D utility scores, although trim-and-fill adjustment did not materially change the pooled estimate. Interpretation of these findings should take into account substantial unexplained heterogeneity, variation in EQ-5D versions and value sets, limited severity-stratified data, and uneven geographic coverage across Asia. The pooled estimates may provide preliminary, context-informed utility values for Asian psoriasis populations and could be useful as indicative inputs for economic evaluations and health technology assessments where country-specific data are limited.Registration: This review was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251062372).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-48639-4.

Introduction

Psoriasis is a chronic, immune-mediated inflammatory skin condition that affects 2–3% of the global population1. Its impact extends beyond visible lesions, contributing to reduced productivity, increased healthcare use, and significant psychosocial burden2. The disease may progress to psoriatic arthritis and is frequently accompanied by systemic comorbidities such as cardiometabolic disease and mental health disorders3. Together, these factors create a considerable burden for patients and health systems, particularly in regions with large affected populations such as Asia4.

Clinically, psoriasis presents as red, scaly plaques that can be itchy, painful, and emotionally distressing. These symptoms affect multiple aspects of patients’ health-related quality of life (HRQoL), including physical comfort, emotional well-being, social participation, and daily functioning5.

For many years, psoriasis was managed mainly with topical agents, phototherapy, and conventional systemic drugs. The introduction of biologic and targeted therapies has substantially improved outcomes for patients with moderate-to-severe disease3. However, these treatments come at significantly higher costs, placing pressure on healthcare budgets especially in resource-limited settings6. As treatment options expand and costs rise, health technology assessments (HTAs) and economic evaluations, particularly cost-utility analyses, have become essential for guiding reimbursement and ensuring efficient use of healthcare resources. This need is especially relevant in Asia, where large affected populations and constrained health budgets heighten the importance of robust economic evidence.

The EQ-5D instrument is one of the most widely used tools for measuring HRQoL, and its utility estimates are central to cost-utility analyses and the calculation of quality-adjusted life years (QALYs)7,8. Although several Asian studies have reported EQ-5D scores in psoriasis populations, the evidence remains fragmented and varies in methodology, severity reporting, and valuation sets. Despite growing interest in economic evaluation and HTA across Asia, no comprehensive synthesis of EQ-5D utility values for psoriasis has been conducted. Because context-specific utility estimates are essential for representing local disease patterns and patient experiences9, reliance on values generated in other regions may not be accurate for Cost Utility Analysis (CUA) conducted at Asian context, underscoring the need for region-specific data synthesis10.

This systematic review and meta-analysis address this gap by pooling EQ-5D utility values for psoriasis across Asian countries, including disease severity and geographic context specific estimates. The goal is to provide preliminary, region-specific utility estimates to inform HTAs, cost-utility analyses, and policy decisions related to psoriasis management in Asia.

Methodology

Study design and protocol registration

This SRMA was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines. The study protocol was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; Registration ID: CRD420251062372). The protocol was registered on 28 May 2025. The PRISMA checklist was also completed to ensure comprehensive and transparent reporting of the review process (Annexure 1). The review aimed to synthesize HRQoL utility values measured using the EQ-5D instrument among adult psoriasis patients across Asian countries.

Search strategy

A comprehensive electronic literature search was carried out across PubMed, EMBASE, Scopus, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), and Google Scholar databases up to July 2025, with no restrictions on language or publication date. The search strategy was developed using the Population, Exposure, and Outcome (PEO) framework. The Population (P) included adults aged 18 years; the exposure (E) was diagnosis of psoriasis or its related complications; and the Outcome (O) was HRQoL assessed using EQ-5D. Both Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms such as “psoriasis,” “EQ-5D,” “EuroQol,” “utility,” and “health-related quality of life” were combined using Boolean operators (AND, OR) to enhance search sensitivity. Reference lists of all eligible studies and relevant reviews were hand-searched to identify additional records. The detailed database-specific search strings are provided in (Supplementary Tables 1–5).

Eligibility criteria

Studies were included if they met the following criteria: adults aged ≥ 18 years diagnosed with psoriasis; reported HRQoL data using the EQ-5D-3 L/5L or VAS instruments; Study designs: randomized controlled trials, quasi-randomized trials, cohort studies, case-control studies, and cross-sectional studies. From the included RCTs, only baseline utility value of psoriasis patients was considered pooling.

Exclusion criteria were: Studies not focused on psoriasis or mixed dermatological populations without extractable psoriasis data; Case reports, case series, reviews, commentaries, editorials, letters, and conference abstracts; Studies lacking quantitative EQ-5D data.

Screening and reviewing of studies

All retrieved records were imported into the Rayyan11 web-based platform for systematic reviews, where duplicates were removed. Two reviewers (HK and VP) independently screened titles and abstracts for eligibility, followed by full-text assessment based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion or by consulting a third reviewer (HM). The overall study selection process was summarized in a PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1). Screening was conducted independently and in duplicate using Rayyan. Reviewers were blinded to each other’s decisions during the title/abstract screening phase. Statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.5.1) using the ‘meta’ and ‘metafor’ packages. No deviations occurred from the registered PROSPERO protocol. Supplementary Table 6 presents the list of excluded studies and the corresponding reasons for their exclusion.

Data extraction

Data extraction was performed independently by two reviewers (HK, VP) using a standardized Data Extraction Form (DEF) developed in Microsoft Excel 2021 and pilot-tested on five randomly selected studies for consistency. Extracted information included study identifiers (author, year, country, journal), study design and characteristics (sample size, duration, setting), participant demographics mean age, sex, psoriasis types, Severity of psoriasis, comorbidities, disease duration), and outcome measures (EQ-5D version, mean utility or VAS scores, and measures of variability such as SD, SE, or IQR, domains of EQ-5D). When multiple publications used overlapping populations, the most complete dataset was retained. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion among reviewers.

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias was assessed using validated tools appropriate to the respective study designs. The Appraisal Tool for Cross-Sectional Studies (AXIS) was used for cross-sectional studies, the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) for cohort studies, and the Cochrane Risk-of-Bias Tool (RoB-2) for Randomized Controlled Trials (RCTs). The AXIS tool consists of 20 items evaluating clarity of study aims, appropriateness of study design, definition of the target population, validity and reliability of outcome measures, adequacy of data reporting, and ethical considerations, with responses categorized as “Yes,” “No,” or “Do not know.” The single cohort study was appraised using the NOS, which assesses three key domains: selection of study participants, comparability between study groups, and adequacy of outcome or exposure assessment. Although a score of ≥ 7 is commonly interpreted as high quality, this threshold is not considered an absolute standard. The RCT was evaluated using RoB-2, which examines five domains of bias including the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of outcomes, and selection of the reported results. Two authors (HK and VP) independently performed the quality assessment for all included studies, and any disagreements were resolved through consensus or arbitration by a third reviewer (HM). Overall, the methodological quality of the included studies ranged from moderate to high, reflecting acceptable internal validity. The detailed domain-specific risk-of-bias evaluations are presented in Supplementary Fig. 1–3.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

For the main outcomes of this review including EQ-5D index and EQ-5D VAS scores, pooled mean values were estimated. For categorical outcomes, including patients having problems in five domains of EQ-5D such as mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression, pooled proportions were estimated in meta-analysis. For inclusion in the quantitative meta-analysis, studies were required to report both mean/median and a measure of variance (SD, SE, or convertible IQR).

Studies reporting only mean values without any variance measure were excluded from pooling but included in the descriptive summary. No imputation of missing SDs was performed. Subgroup analyses were conducted by country of study, psoriasis type (cutaneous and psoriatic arthritis), EQ-5D instrument version, and value set. In the psoriasis-type subgroup analysis, plaque psoriasis, psoriasis vulgaris, and unspecified psoriasis were classified under the cutaneous psoriasis subgroup. Separate analyses were conducted including studies that had reported severity-specific EQ-5D utility scores. Some studies reported separate EQ-5D values for moderate and severe disease, while others provided only combined estimates for moderate-severe. To enable consistency, EQ-5D scores for patients with moderate and with severe psoriasis were merged into a single moderate-to-severe group before pooling.

EQ-5D index values range from < 0 (states worse than death) to 1 (perfect health)12,13 and EQ-5D VAS ranges from 0, representing the worst imaginable health state, to 100, representing the best imaginable health state. The pooled effect sizes were reported along with their 95% confidence intervals (CI) and 95% prediction intervals. Random-effects models were fitted using the inverse-variance method, with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) used to estimate between-study variance (tau²) and Hartung–Knapp adjustment applied to derive confidence intervals. This method was applied to account for potential geographical, clinical, and methodological heterogeneity among the included studies. Statistical heterogeneity was evaluated using the Chi-square (Q) test and the I² statistic. Following Higgins and Thompson (2002), I² values were interpreted as: 0–40% = might not be important; 30–60% = moderate heterogeneity; 50–90% = substantial heterogeneity; and 75–100% = considerable heterogeneity. A Cochrane Q-test p-value < 0.10 was used as an additional indicator of heterogeneity14. The pooled estimates were graphically represented using forest plots. Sensitivity analyses (Leave-one-out sensitivity analyses) were performed to assess the robustness of the pooled results and to examine the influence of individual studies on the overall estimates. Additionally, exploratory univariable meta-regression analyses were conducted only for EQ-5D utility values to explore potential moderators contributing to between-study variability. The potential covariates considered for conducting meta-regression included age and disease duration. Given the limited number of included studies, these meta-regression analyses were interpreted cautiously and considered exploratory. Publication bias was evaluated both visually using a funnel plot and statistically using Egger’s regression test. Trim-and-fill analysis was additionally performed for the EQ-5D utility meta-analysis to assess the potential impact of funnel-plot asymmetry. Funnel plot asymmetry and a p-value < 0.10 in Egger’s test15 were considered indicative of publication bias.

As this study synthesized secondary data from previously published literature, no new ethical approval or informed consent was required. However, all included studies were verified to have obtained appropriate institutional ethics approval and participant consent as reported in their original publications.

Certainty of evidence assessment

The certainty of evidence for the main pooled outcomes was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework. Certainty was evaluated across the domains of risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias, and was categorized as high, moderate, low, or very low. GRADE was applied to the pooled EQ-5D utility index and EQ-5D VAS outcomes in adults with psoriasis from Asian study settings. Because the evidence base consisted predominantly of observational studies, mainly cross-sectional designs, with only one cohort study and baseline data from one randomized trial, particular attention was given to methodological limitations, substantial between-study heterogeneity, variability in EQ-5D instrument versions and national value sets, and the limited number of studies within several subgroup analyses. Indirectness was assessed in relation to the review population, psoriasis subtype, geographic setting, and outcome measurement approach. Publication bias was interpreted cautiously, as funnel plot and Egger’s test assessments were feasible only for selected pooled analyses and are limited when the number of included studies is small.

Results

After a comprehensive search of multiple databases, a total of 2433 studies were identified, including (1233) from PubMed, (531) from Scopus, (647) from Embase15, from the Cochrane Library, and7 from Google Scholar. After removing 520 duplicates, 1,913 studies remained for title and abstract screening. Of these, 76 studies were considered potentially relevant and were included for full-text assessment. Following the application of inclusion and exclusion criteria, 62 studies were excluded as they did not meet the eligibility criteria (Supplementary Table 6). Ultimately, 14 studies were included in the systematic review and meta-analysis (Fig. 1).

Risk of bias

The quality of the included studies was assessed using tools appropriate to study design and is presented in Supplementary Figs. 1–3. We appraised the methodological quality of 12 cross-sectional studies using the AXIS tool. Overall, these studies generally had clearly stated aims, appropriate study designs, defined target populations, and suitable outcome measures, suggesting acceptable methodological quality in several core domains. However, common limitations included lack of sample size justification, limited assessment and reporting of non-response, and incomplete reporting of funding sources or conflicts of interest. These weaknesses may introduce selection and reporting bias and should be considered when interpreting the pooled estimates.

The single cohort study was assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale and achieved a score of 6/9, indicating a moderate risk of bias. It demonstrated adequate representativeness of the cohort, appropriate ascertainment of exposure, and satisfactory outcome assessment. However, limitations were noted in the selection of the non-exposed cohort, comparability between groups, and the reporting of follow-up duration and adequacy, which may have increased the risk of residual confounding and attrition bias.

The single randomized controlled trial was assessed using RoB-2. All five domains randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and selection of the reported result were judged to be at low risk of bias, indicating good methodological rigor for this study.

In summary, the overall methodological quality of the included studies ranged from moderate to high, but the predominance of cross-sectional designs and recurring limitations in reporting transparency and non-response assessment should be acknowledged. These factors may affect the precision and validity of study-level utility estimates and should be considered when interpreting the overall findings of this review.

Meta-analysis of EQ-5D utility scores and VAS

Ten studies comprising 1,453 participants were included in the revised meta-analysis of EQ-5D utility index scores. The pooled mean EQ-5D score was 0.84 (95% CI: 0.78–0.89), with considerable between-study heterogeneity (I² = 95.00%; tau² = 0.01; Q = 179.92, df = 9, p < 0.001). The corresponding 95% prediction interval ranged from 0.65 to 1.02, indicating substantial variation in utility estimates across comparable study settings (Fig. 2).

Eight studies comprising 1,227 participants were included in the revised meta-analysis of EQ-5D VAS scores. The pooled mean EQ-5D VAS score was 70.73 (95% CI: 67.01–74.46), with substantial between-study heterogeneity (I² = 87.10%; tau² = 17.12; Q = 54.08, df = 7, p < 0.0001). The corresponding 95% prediction interval ranged from 60.26 to 81.20, indicating considerable variation in self-rated health status across study settings (Fig. 3).

Fig. 3Forest plot depicting the pooled EQ-5D VAS scores.

Forest plot depicting the pooled EQ-5D VAS scores.

Subgroup analysis by country of study showed significant between-group differences (Q = 38.20, df = 4, p < 0.001). The pooled EQ-5D utility estimate was 0.74 (95% CI: 0.69–0.79) for Singapore, 0.80 (95% CI: 0.51–1.09) for Japan, 0.90 (95% CI: 0.83–0.96) for China, 0.84 (95% CI: 0.67–1.01) for Thailand, and 0.88 (95% CI: 0.85–0.91) for the Korean subgroup. Heterogeneity remained particularly high within the Japan subgroup (I² = 96.00%) and the Thailand subgroup (I² = 93.90%), whereas the China subgroup showed lower inconsistency (I² = 33.70%). These findings suggest that study setting may explain part of the between-study variability, although substantial residual heterogeneity remained (Supplementary Figure: 4).

Subgroup analysis by country of study showed significant between-group differences (Q = 24.96, df = 5, p = 0.0001). The pooled EQ-5D VAS estimate was 67.25 (95% CI: 63.30–71.20) for Singapore, 72.79 (95% CI: 71.15–74.43) for China, 65.68 (95% CI: 62.72–68.64) for Taiwan, 74.27 (95% CI: 62.20–86.35) for Thailand, 67.32 (95% CI: 63.40–71.24) for the Korean subgroup, and 69.50 (95% CI: 66.45–72.55) for India. Among these, only the Thailand subgroup included more than one study and showed substantial heterogeneity (I² = 87.50%), whereas the remaining country-specific estimates were each derived from a single study (Supplementary Figure: 5).

Subgroup analysis by EQ-5D instrument version showed a statistically significant between-group difference for the EQ-5D utility index (Q = 7.65, df = 2, p = 0.0218). However, this analysis should be interpreted as exploratory rather than confirmatory, because the evidence base was heavily dominated by EQ-5D-5 L studies: 8 studies used EQ-5D-5 L, whereas only 1 study used EQ-5D-3 L and 1 study did not report the instrument version. Accordingly, the overall pooled estimate was necessarily derived by combining studies across instrument versions, and should therefore be interpreted with caution given the known measurement and valuation differences between EQ-5D-3 L and EQ-5D-5 L. We did not rely on formal version-stratified pooling as a standalone basis for inference because the 3 L and non-reported categories were represented by only single studies; instead, subgroup analysis was undertaken to examine the possible direction and magnitude of variation by instrument version. Within this exploratory framework, studies using EQ-5D-5 L yielded a pooled mean utility estimate of 0.83 (95% CI: 0.75–0.90; I² = 95.3%), the single study with non-reported instrument version yielded 0.83 (95% CI: 0.81–0.85), and the single EQ-5D-3 L study yielded 0.88 (95% CI: 0.85–0.91). These findings suggest that instrument version may have contributed to between-study variability, but the sparse representation of non-5 L studies precludes any firm inference regarding systematic differences between versions (Supplementary Figure: 6).

Additional subgroup analysis by value set also showed significant between-group differences (Q = 38.78, df = 4, p < 0.001). The pooled mean EQ-5D utility estimate was 0.74 (95% CI: 0.69–0.79) for the United Kingdom value set, 0.79 (95% CI: 0.52–1.07) for studies in which the value set was not reported, 0.90 (95% CI: 0.83–0.96) for the China value set, 0.84 (95% CI: 0.67–1.01) for the Thailand value set, and 0.90 (95% CI: 0.86–0.94) for the Japan value set. Marked heterogeneity persisted in the non-reported subgroup (I² = 95.80%) and the Thailand value-set subgroup (I² = 93.90%), while the China value-set subgroup showed lower heterogeneity (I² = 33.70%) (Supplementary Figure: 7).

Subgroup analysis by value set did not show statistically significant between-group differences (Q = 4.38, df = 3, p = 0.2229). The pooled mean EQ-5D VAS estimate was 67.25 (95% CI: 63.30–71.20) for the United Kingdom value set, 69.35 (95% CI: 24.20–114.49) for the China value set, 74.27 (95% CI: 62.20–86.35) for the Thailand value set, and 68.68 (95% CI: 55.25–82.11) for studies in which the value set was not reported. Heterogeneity was substantial within the China subgroup (I² = 94.10%) and the Thailand subgroup (I² = 87.50%), whereas the non-reported subgroup showed no observed heterogeneity (I² = 0.00%). These findings suggest that valuation method may have contributed less to variability in VAS scores than to variability in EQ-5D utility index estimates (Supplementary Figure: 8).

Subgroup analysis by psoriasis type showed statistically significant between-group differences (Q = 4.50, df = 1, p = 0.03). Studies involving psoriatic arthritis yielded a pooled mean EQ-5D utility estimate of 0.79 (95% CI: 0.62–0.97), whereas studies involving cutaneous psoriasis yielded a higher pooled mean of 0.88 (95% CI: 0.85–0.91). Heterogeneity remained substantial in both subgroups (I² = 92.60% for psoriatic arthritis and 89.60% for cutaneous psoriasis), indicating considerable variability within each category. These findings suggest that HRQoL impairment may be greater in psoriatic arthritis than in cutaneous psoriasis alone, although the high residual heterogeneity warrants cautious interpretation (Supplementary Figure: 9).

Subgroup analysis by psoriasis type did not show statistically significant between-group differences for EQ-5D VAS scores (Q = 0.02, df = 1, p = 0.90). The pooled mean EQ-5D VAS score was 71.02 (95% CI: 57.74–84.30) for psoriatic arthritis and 70.55 (95% CI: 64.95–76.16) for cutaneous psoriasis. Heterogeneity remained substantial in both subgroups (I² = 88.20% for psoriatic arthritis and 89.20% for cutaneous psoriasis). Although the pooled VAS scores were broadly similar between groups, the high within-subgroup heterogeneity suggests that these estimates should be interpreted cautiously (Supplementary Figure: 10).

Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis showed that omission of individual studies did not materially alter the pooled EQ-5D estimate, which ranged from 0.83 to 0.85 across iterations. The pooled mean was lowest after omitting Zhao et al., 2017 (0.83, 95% CI: 0.77–0.89) and highest after omitting Yamamoto et al., 2020 (0.85, 95% CI: 0.81–0.90). Heterogeneity remained high throughout (I² range: 93.10%–95.60%), suggesting that the overall finding was not driven by any single study. The corresponding prediction intervals remained broad, ranging from 0.63 to 1.03 to 0.72–0.99 across the leave-one-out analyses (Supplementary Figure: 11).

Visual inspection of the funnel plot suggested asymmetry, and Egger’s regression test supported the possibility of small-study effects (t = -2.81, df = 8, p = 0.02; bias estimate = -5.94, SE = 2.11). This finding should nevertheless be interpreted cautiously in view of the limited number of studies and the high underlying heterogeneity (Supplementary Figure: 12).

In the trim-and-fill analysis, 2 potentially missing studies were imputed. After adjustment, the pooled mean EQ-5D utility score was 0.87 (95% CI: 0.80–0.94), with a 95% prediction interval of 0.62 to 1.13. Heterogeneity remained very high (I² = 96.10%; tau² = 0.01; Q = 279.53, df = 11, p < 0.001), but the adjusted estimate remained broadly similar to the primary analysis, suggesting that the overall result was not materially altered after accounting for possible publication bias (Supplementary Figure: 13).

Exploratory meta-regression analyses were conducted to assess whether mean age and disease duration explained between-study heterogeneity. Mean age was not significantly associated with EQ-5D utility values (QM = 0.72, p = 0.40), with a regression coefficient of -0.00 (95% CI: -0.01 to 0.00). Disease duration likewise showed no significant association (QM = 0.02, p = 0.88), with a regression coefficient of 0.00 (95% CI: -0.01 to 0.01). The corresponding bubble plots did not show a strong visual trend, and substantial residual heterogeneity remained in both models (Supplementary Figures: 14–15).

Severity-based separate analysis

A separate analysis of studies reporting moderate-to-severe psoriasis was conducted for both EQ-5D utility index and EQ-5D VAS scores. For the EQ-5D utility index, the pooled mean was 0.80 (95% CI: -0.14 to 1.75), with substantial heterogeneity (I² = 86.20%; tau² = 0.01; Q = 7.24, df = 1, p = 0.0071). The corresponding 95% prediction interval ranged from − 0.76 to 2.37, indicating marked uncertainty around the pooled estimate. This analysis included 2 studies with a total of 156 participants (Supplementary Figure: 16).

For EQ-5D VAS, the pooled mean in moderate-to-severe psoriasis was 63.08 (95% CI: 38.71 to 87.45). Between-study heterogeneity was low (I² = 1.70%; tau² = 0.18; Q = 1.02, df = 1, p = 0.3131), and the 95% prediction interval ranged from 38.12 to 88.04. This analysis was based on 2 studies comprising 98 participants (Supplementary Figure: 17). Given the very small number of studies contributing to these severity-specific analyses, both estimates should be interpreted cautiously.

Domain-wise EQ-5D analysis

Four studies comprising 550 participants contributed to the domain-level meta-proportion analyses. Across the five EQ-5D domains, the pooled proportion of patients reporting problems was highest for pain/discomfort, followed by mobility, anxiety/depression, usual activities, and self-care. Heterogeneity was substantial in all domain-specific analyses, and the corresponding prediction intervals were wide, suggesting marked variability across studies.

For the mobility domain, the pooled proportion was 0.20 (95% CI: 0.09–0.41), based on 118 events. Between-study heterogeneity was substantial (I² = 85.70%; tau² = 0.33; Q = 20.96, df = 3, p = 0.0001), and the 95% prediction interval ranged from 0.03 to 0.67. Individual study estimates ranged from 0.10 to 0.33 (Supplementary Figure: 18).

For the self-care domain, the pooled proportion was 0.09 (95% CI: 0.02–0.29), based on 68 events. Heterogeneity remained substantial (I² = 88.60%; tau² = 0.67; Q = 26.39, df = 3, p < 0.0001), and the 95% prediction interval ranged from 0.01 to 0.66. Reported study-level estimates ranged from 0.04 to 0.22 (Supplementary Figure: 19).

For the usual activities domain, the pooled proportion was 0.14 (95% CI: 0.04–0.38), based on 90 events. Heterogeneity was again substantial (I² = 87.60%; tau² = 0.62; Q = 24.20, df = 3, p < 0.0001), with a 95% prediction interval of 0.01 to 0.73. Individual study estimates ranged from 0.06 to 0.26 (Supplementary Figure: 20).

For the pain/discomfort domain, the pooled proportion was 0.22 (95% CI: 0.09–0.45), based on 125 events, representing the highest pooled burden among the five domains. Between-study heterogeneity was substantial (I² = 91.30%; tau² = 0.39; Q = 34.35, df = 3, p < 0.0001), and the 95% prediction interval ranged from 0.03 to 0.73. Study-level proportions ranged from 0.14 to 0.42 (Supplementary Figure: 21).

For the anxiety/depression domain, the pooled proportion was 0.15 (95% CI: 0.06–0.32), based on 86 events. Heterogeneity remained substantial (I² = 86.40%; tau² = 0.32; Q = 22.08, df = 3, p < 0.0001), and the 95% prediction interval ranged from 0.02 to 0.59. Individual study estimates ranged from 0.10 to 0.29 (Supplementary Figure: 22).

Given that each domain-specific analysis was based on only 4 studies, these estimates should be interpreted cautiously. Even so, the pattern suggests that pain/discomfort may represent the most frequently affected EQ-5D domain in this population, whereas self-care appears to be the least commonly affected.

Certainty of evidence

Using the GRADE framework, the certainty of evidence was rated as very low for all pooled EQ-5D outcomes (Supplementary Table 7). The EQ-5D utility index (10 studies; n = 1,453) was downgraded for serious risk of bias, very serious inconsistency (I² = 95.0%), serious indirectness, and suspected publication bias. The EQ-VAS outcome (8 studies; n = 1,227) was also judged very low certainty because of serious risk of bias, very serious inconsistency (I² = 87.1%), serious indirectness, and serious imprecision. Likewise, the certainty for EQ-5D domain-specific problem estimates (4 studies; n = 550) was very low, primarily due to serious risk of bias, very serious inconsistency (I² = 85.7%–91.3%), serious indirectness, and serious imprecision. These findings suggest that the pooled estimates should be interpreted with caution.

Discussion

In this systematic review and meta-analysis of 14 studies conducted in Asian psoriasis populations, the pooled EQ-5D utility estimate of 0.84 and pooled EQ-5D VAS score of 70.73 suggest a meaningful reduction in health-related quality of life. However, the very wide 95% prediction interval (0.58–0.97) reflects the substantial between-study variability and indicates that this pooled figure should not be treated as a universal estimate applicable to any individual country or setting, with greater reductions observed among those with psoriatic arthritis. Domain-specific analysis indicated that pain and discomfort were the most commonly influenced dimensions, followed by mobility and psychological well-being. While meta-regression did not reveal a significant impact of age or disease duration on EQ-5D values, these analyses were substantially underpowered (11 studies, two covariates) and the non-significant findings should be interpreted as inconclusive rather than as evidence of no association. Overall, these findings underscore the considerable burden of psoriasis on multiple aspects of patients’ daily functioning and perceived health status in Asian settings.

Despite subgroup analyses and meta-regression, heterogeneity was very high (I² > 90%) and remained largely unexplained, likely reflecting differences in study design, population characteristics, disease severity, comorbidity burden, EQ-5D versions used (3 L vs. 5 L), healthcare access, and cultural variation in HRQoL reporting across Asian countries. Most included studies were cross-sectional and of moderate methodological quality. The mixed use of EQ-5D-3 L and EQ-5D-5 L may have further contributed to inconsistency, as the 5 L version generally produces higher and more precise utility estimates, whereas the 3 L version can overestimate health problems30. Additional sources of variability include differences in tariff sets applied and variation in the proportion of patients with different comorbidities. To reduce heterogeneity in future research, studies should standardize severity reporting, consistently account for comorbidities, and clearly specify the EQ-5D version used. A risk-of-bias stratified meta-analysis was not conducted because almost all included studies were cross-sectional and fell within a similar moderate quality range. With only one cohort study and one RCT, subgrouping studies by risk-of-bias would be statistically uninformative and unlikely to explain heterogeneity. Therefore, results were interpreted alongside a narrative assessment of methodological quality.

Our findings are consistent with previous global systematic reviews and meta-analyses indicating that psoriasis has a moderate yet significant effect on HRQoL31. In line with global trends, we noted a significant decrease in EQ-5D utility scores among Asian patients with psoriasis, highlighting the considerable burden of the disease in this region. When categorized by psoriasis type, psoriatic arthritis demonstrated the most significant reduction in HRQoL than cutaneous psoriasis alone, showing lower utility scores in comparison to plaque psoriasis. This pattern aligns with existing evidence showing that around 20% of individuals with psoriasis subsequently develop psoriatic arthritis32,33, and that joint involvement significantly deteriorates daily functioning and overall well-being. Domain-level analysis indicated that pain/discomfort was the most impacted domain, while self-care was the least affected, reflecting the trends identified by Yang et al. and underscoring the pivotal role of pain in contributing to HRQoL decline. Moreover, our aggregated EQ-5D estimates aligned with the range reported by Møller et al. (2015), which indicated utility scores from 0.52 ± 0.39 to 0.90 ± 0.10 in psoriatic populations. Collectively, these findings indicate that the HRQoL burden faced by Asian patients with psoriasis is similar to that seen worldwide and aligns with the range commonly reported for other chronic conditions such as cardiovascular diseases, metabolic dysfunction, End stage renal failure and even cancer34.

This review has several strengths being the first systematic synthesis of EQ-5D utility values specifically in Asian patients with psoriasis, filling an important evidence gap. We followed rigorous methods, including PROSPERO registration, adherence to PRISMA 2020 guidelines, and extensive database searches. We also explored heterogeneity through subgroup analyses, sensitivity analyses, and meta-regression. Finally, we provide preliminary country-level utility estimates for major Asian countries that can serve as indicative benchmarks in the absence of country-specific primary data, acknowledging that the limited number of studies per country constrains their precision.

Despite strengths, several limitations should also be noted. First, the most significant limitation of this analysis is the substantial and largely unexplained heterogeneity across studies. The pooled results should therefore be interpreted as average estimates across diverse settings. Second, inconsistent and often incomplete reporting of psoriasis severity across the included studies limited our ability to conduct robust subgroup analyses. Only two studies provided severity-stratified data, which restricted the depth of comparison possible. Consequently, our estimates were derived using the limited available information, and the findings must be evaluated with this limitation addressed. Third, geographical coverage was uneven. Most studies were from East Asia, while very few were from Central, West, and South Asian countries, limiting generalizability. Fourth, several subgroup analyses were based on a small number of studies, and some subgroup estimates relied on single-study contributions, which limits stability and generalizability. Finally, possible small-study effects were suggested by funnel-plot asymmetry and Egger’s test for EQ-5D utility values, although trim-and-fill adjustment did not materially change the pooled estimate.

These findings have important clinical and policy implications, but they should be applied cautiously. The substantial HRQoL impairment, especially in moderate-to-severe disease, supports the need for timely and appropriate treatment, including escalation when necessary. Clinicians can use these utility values in shared decision-making to help patients understand the expected benefits of different therapies. Routine use of EQ-5D in clinical practice may help identify patients with high burden who need treatment intensification, rehabilitation, pain management, or psychosocial support. Pain/discomfort and mobility issues were the most common problems, highlighting the need for early assessment and targeted management. Anxiety and depression were also notable, reinforcing the value of incorporating mental health screening and support within dermatology care. The pooled utility estimates generated in this review may serve as provisional benchmarks for economic evaluation and health technology assessment in Asian settings where country-specific utility data are unavailable. In particular, these estimates may support health policy and resource-allocation decisions, including cost-utility analyses of psoriasis treatments such as biologics, when local utility inputs are lacking.

Future research should prioritize underrepresented regions within Asia, improve standardization of severity and subtype reporting, and ensure clearer documentation of EQ-5D version, value set, and variance measures. Routine inclusion of EQ-5D in psoriasis studies would improve comparability. Standardized disease severity reporting and consistent use of EQ-5D versions would help reduce heterogeneity. Development of country-specific mapping algorithms to convert disease-specific measures such as DLQI or PASI into EQ-5D values is also needed. Previous mapping has been carried out in China24 and Germany35. Similar region-specific algorithms would support local HRQoL estimation and economic evaluations.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis shows that psoriasis is associated with meaningful HRQoL reductions in Asian populations, with especially pronounced effects in those with psoriatic arthritis. The pooled EQ-5D estimates generated in this review may serve as provisional utility benchmarks for economic evaluations in Asian settings, while acknowledging that substantial heterogeneity and limited country-specific data constrain their precision and generalizability. Larger, prospective, and geographically broader studies are needed to provide more definitive region-specific estimates.
