Transcatheter ASD Closure in a Bidirectional Cardiopulmonary Diagnostic Blind Spot
Authors: Amartya Chakraborti, Anil Kumar Singhi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDyspnea in adults with secundum atrial septal defect (ASD) is often attributed to shunt physiology, whereas coexisting airway disease may remain unrecognized. Conversely, clinically significant ASD may be overlooked in patients presenting with predominant respiratory symptoms, creating a bidirectional cardiopulmonary diagnostic blind spot.Case SummaryWe retrospectively identified 8 adults (median age 55.5 years; 5 women) with secundum ASD (15-30 mm) and clinically significant airway disease through bidirectional cardiology-pulmonology referral (6 cardiology-to-pulmonology; 2 pulmonology-to-cardiology). Airway physiology was obstructive in 6, restrictive in 1, and mixed in 1, with bronchodilator reversibility in 5. Integrated cardiopulmonary evaluation and respiratory optimization improved symptom attribution and guided individualized management. Seven patients underwent transcatheter ASD closure (median device size, 38 mm), including fenestrated closure in 2 after balloon occlusion testing identified impaired left ventricular compliance; 1 deferred closure after symptomatic improvement with respiratory therapy alone. No procedural complications occurred, and 1 noncardiac death occurred 5 years after intervention.Take-Home MessagesDyspnea in adults with ASD may reflect combined cardiac and airway disease. Disproportionate symptoms should prompt integrated cardiopulmonary evaluation. Accurate symptom attribution underpins respiratory optimization and individualized ASD closure.

The goal of integrated cardiopulmonary evaluation is not simply to detect coexisting disease, but to identify the dominant driver of symptoms and individualize management.

Adults with congenital heart disease demonstrate an increasing burden of pulmonary disease that may independently influence symptoms and functional capacity.1 Secundum atrial septal defect (ASD), frequently encountered in adulthood, is commonly associated with exertional dyspnea; however, symptom severity is not always explained by shunt magnitude alone. Pulmonary abnormalities in ASD include ventilatory impairment, gas exchange abnormalities, and airway hyperresponsiveness.2,3 Population-level data further suggest an increased risk of chronic airway disease in adults with congenital heart disease independent of conventional risk factors.4

Conversely, in patients with established airway disease, persistent dyspnea is often attributed to inadequate respiratory control, and underlying cardiac pathology such as significant ASD may remain undetected. As a result, symptom attribution may remain confined within specialty boundaries, potentially overlooking clinically relevant dual pathology.

This study describes adults identified through bidirectional cardiology-pulmonology referral and examines how integrated cardiopulmonary evaluation influenced symptom attribution, patient selection, and transcatheter ASD closure strategy.

Patient Identification and Clinical Trigger

Between January 2020 and February 2026, we retrospectively identified 8 adults with secundum ASD and coexisting clinically significant airway disease through a bidirectional cardiopulmonary referral pathway. Case identification was not based on systematic screening. Instead, cases were retrospectively recognized during routine clinical care because they demonstrated a clinically distinctive pattern of disproportionate symptoms that prompted bidirectional cardiology-pulmonology referrals. Airway or cardiac involvement emerged when symptom burden appeared disproportionate to the known primary diagnosis and could not be adequately explained by a single-system disorder. Accordingly, this cohort should be considered descriptive and hypothesis-generating rather than representative of the broader ASD population.

In the cardiology-to-pulmonology pathway, 6 patients with known ASD were referred for pulmonary evaluation because wheeze or dyspnea appeared disproportionate to the hemodynamic severity of the shunt lesion. In the pulmonology-to-cardiology pathway, 2 patients with established airway disease and suboptimal symptomatic response to respiratory therapy underwent cardiac evaluation, leading to recognition of previously undiagnosed secundum ASD. In both referral directions, the common clinical trigger was symptoms that could not be adequately explained by a single-system diagnosis.

Pulmonary Evaluation

All patients underwent pulmonology evaluation with spirometry-based assessment of airway disease. Spirometric patterns were categorized as obstructive, restrictive, or mixed obstructive-restrictive physiology, and bronchodilator (BD) responsiveness was documented where present. Classification was based on specialist pulmonology interpretation of spirometry in conjunction with clinical assessment and thoracic imaging when clinically indicated. High-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) of the chest was performed selectively to further characterize airway and parenchymal abnormalities when clinically indicated.

Cardiac Imaging and Hemodynamic Assessment

All patients underwent transthoracic and transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) to define ASD anatomy, shunt characteristics, right heart remodeling, and suitability for transcatheter closure. Invasive hemodynamic assessment was performed in all patients undergoing device closure. Mean pulmonary artery pressure (mPAP) and left ventricular end-diastolic pressure (LVEDP) were recorded as part of integrated cardiopulmonary and procedural assessment. Pulmonary hypertension was defined as mPAP >20 mm Hg, and elevated LVEDP (≥15 mm Hg) as impaired left ventricular compliance relevant to procedural planning.

In selected patients with suspected impaired left ventricular compliance, balloon occlusion testing was performed during catheterization. An increase in LVEDP >5 mm Hg during temporary ASD occlusion was considered clinically significant and was used to guide modification of closure strategy, including consideration of fenestrated device closure.

Integrated Cardiopulmonary Management

Management decisions were based on integrated interpretation of pulmonary and cardiac findings rather than anatomical assessment alone. Targeted respiratory therapy was instituted in all patients before final ASD closure decisions to optimize clinical status and clarify the relative contribution of airway disease to symptom burden. Conversely, in patients initially presenting through the pulmonology pathway, recognition of significant ASD prompted subsequent cardiac-directed management, including device closure where appropriate.

Transcatheter ASD Closure Strategy

Transcatheter ASD closure was performed under general anesthesia using fluoroscopic and transesophageal echocardiographic guidance. Device sizing was based on maximal thick-to-thick ASD dimensions. In patients with elevated LVEDP or an unfavorable hemodynamic response during balloon occlusion testing, fenestrated Lifetech septal occluders (Lifetech Scientific) were used to permit controlled residual interatrial shunting. In both patients requiring fenestrated closure, custom-manufactured Lifetech fenestrated atrial septal occluders with an 8-mm fenestration were used. The fenestration size was based on the manufacturer's established design for occluders ≥32 mm and has been previously described. Standard closure was performed using Lifetech or Cocoon septal occluder devices (SMT Ltd) in the remaining patients.

Data Presentation

Continuous variables are presented descriptively as median (IQR; range), and categorical variables as number (percentage). Given the small sample size, analyses were considered descriptive and exploratory.

Respiratory and hemodynamic profile

Airway disease patterns were heterogeneous, including obstructive physiology in 6 patients, restrictive physiology in 1 patient, and mixed obstructive–restrictive physiology in 1 patient. BD responsiveness was observed in 5 patients despite limited conventional exposure risk factors, with smoking history present in only 1 patient and occupational exposure in 1 patient. Median ASD size was 27 mm, median thick-to-thick ASD dimension was 32 mm, and median device size was 38 mm. Median mPAP and LVEDP were 26 mm Hg and 15 mm Hg, respectively. Balloon occlusion testing was selectively performed in patients with suspected impaired left ventricular compliance before closure.

Impact on integrated cardiopulmonary management

Integrated cardiopulmonary evaluation influenced management strategy in all patients. Targeted respiratory therapy was instituted before final ASD closure decisions to optimize clinical status and reassess symptom attribution. Transcatheter ASD closure was performed in 7 patients. In 2 patients (P3 and P8), balloon occlusion testing demonstrated a clinically significant rise in LVEDP (>5 mm Hg), prompting fenestrated device closure to permit controlled residual interatrial shunting. In 1 patient (P7), respiratory optimization resulted in sufficient symptomatic improvement that the patient declined catheterization and device closure at 68 years of age despite the presence of a moderate-sized ASD.

Follow-up

All patients undergoing transcatheter ASD closure tolerated the procedure without immediate complications, and no intervention-related adverse cardiac events were recorded. The overall bidirectional diagnostic and management pathway identified in this cohort is summarized in the Visual Summary .

At a median follow-up of 10 months (range, 3-60 months), clinical improvement was observed in most patients, with the majority reporting NYHA functional class I status. Oxygen saturation ranged from 96% to 100% at the latest follow-up assessment. No residual shunt was detected in any of the 5 patients who underwent standard ASD device closure. In both patients treated with fenestrated devices, persistent left-to-right shunting across the fenestration was documented at the most recent follow-up evaluation. One patient died 5 years after intervention because of a noncardiac cause. No late device-related complications were recorded during the available follow-up period.

Discussion

The principal finding of this study lies not in a single physiological or procedural parameter, but in the recognition of a reproducible clinical pattern: disproportionate dyspnea in adults with secundum ASD may reflect combined cardiac and airway pathology, and diagnostic misattribution may occur bidirectionally. In this cohort, airway disease remained unrecognized in patients with established ASD until symptoms appeared disproportionate to shunt severity, whereas in the reverse direction, hemodynamically significant ASD remained undetected in patients carrying a respiratory diagnosis until treatment response proved inadequate. Importantly, these observations did not emerge through systematic screening, but through routine clinical care when single-system explanations failed. Once recognized, integrated cardiopulmonary evaluation influenced management in every patient, highlighting the importance of accurate symptom attribution in procedural decision-making.

This observation reflects a broader limitation of clinical reasoning, where diagnostic parsimony favors a single explanatory process. In patients with ASD, dyspnea is often attributed predominantly to left-to-right shunting and its hemodynamic consequences, whereas in patients with airway disease, persistent symptoms are commonly interpreted as incomplete respiratory control or disease progression. The cardiology-to-pulmonology referrals in this study represent cases in which airway disease contributed substantially to symptom burden but was initially unrecognized, whereas the pulmonology-to-cardiology pathway illustrates the converse scenario, where an established respiratory diagnosis obscured the presence of a significant hemodynamic lesion. Previous studies have described pulmonary manifestations secondary to ASD or persistence of respiratory symptoms after closure but have not focused on the simultaneous coexistence of both conditions and their reciprocal masking.2,5 The bidirectional referral pattern observed here therefore represents a clinically relevant diagnostic blind spot rather than a theoretical construct.

The mechanistic interface between ASD and airway disease provides a framework for understanding this overlap. Pulmonary overcirculation in ASD may produce wheeze and bronchial hyperresponsiveness through mechanisms analogous to cardiac asthma, resulting in respiratory symptoms and spirometric abnormalities that mimic obstructive airway disease.6 Some studies have shown improvement in asthma-like symptoms following ASD closure, particularly in patients without established airway pathology.7 However, airway dysfunction may also persist despite successful closure and hemodynamic normalization, suggesting that intrinsic airway disease may coexist independently rather than representing hemodynamic mimicry alone.3,7

The cohort described here supports such a dual-pathology model. Obstructive physiology predominated, with BD responsiveness observed frequently despite limited conventional exposure risk factors. Epidemiological studies suggest that adults with noncomplex congenital heart disease may have increased susceptibility to airway disease independent of smoking and other traditional risk factors.4 Restrictive physiology and diffusion abnormalities in ASD have been linked to pulmonary hemodynamic burden and may improve after closure, whereas obstructive abnormalities appear less reversible.2,8 Persistent spirometric impairment after closure has also been described, suggesting that chronic exposure to altered pulmonary blood flow may contribute to structural airway and parenchymal remodeling.9,10 The presence of mosaic attenuation and bronchiectatic changes in selected patients in this cohort provides anatomical support for established airway pathology beyond shunt physiology alone.

The converse failure to identify ASD in patients with airway disease reflects a different but equally important mechanism—diagnostic closure driven by clinical plausibility. In patients presenting with dyspnea and spirometric evidence of airway disease, the diagnostic framework is often considered complete, and further cardiovascular evaluation may not be pursued. The 2 patients referred from pulmonology to cardiology in this study exemplify this process: both had appropriate respiratory diagnoses, yet a hemodynamically significant ASD remained undetected until symptoms persisted despite therapy. These findings suggest that ASD should be considered in selected patients with atypical or treatment-resistant airway disease, particularly when symptom burden appears disproportionate or conventional risk factors are limited.

The most clinically consequential finding of this study was the impact of integrated cardiopulmonary evaluation on management strategy, shifting decision-making from an anatomy-driven to a physiology-informed approach. Although all patients had defects conventionally considered suitable for closure, integrated assessment altered procedural strategy in several patients. In 2 patients, balloon occlusion testing demonstrated a clinically significant rise in LVEDP, prompting fenestrated device closure to permit controlled residual interatrial shunting. This approach aligns with contemporary practice emphasizing individualized hemodynamic assessment in patients with impaired ventricular compliance or borderline closure tolerance.11 Coexisting airway disease may further amplify cardiopulmonary stress through hypoxic vasoconstriction, altered intrathoracic pressure, and pulmonary vascular remodeling, reinforcing the importance of individualized procedural planning.10,12

Contemporary ASD closure practice emphasizes not only anatomical suitability but also careful assessment of shunt significance, right-heart volume overload, pulmonary vascular status, and left ventricular compliance, particularly in older adults and in those with coexisting cardiopulmonary disease. Current adult congenital heart disease guidance supports individualized decision-making based on integrated clinical, imaging, and hemodynamic assessment rather than anatomy alone.13 In patients with pulmonary hypertension or concern regarding elevated pulmonary vascular resistance, invasive hemodynamic evaluation remains important for determining closure candidacy and procedural strategy. In selected patients with impaired left ventricular compliance, balloon occlusion testing may help identify those at risk of adverse hemodynamic consequences following complete defect closure, and fenestrated device closure may provide a useful alternative strategy. The management decisions in our cohort were consistent with these contemporary principles and further illustrate how integrated cardiopulmonary assessment can refine procedural planning in patients presenting with disproportionate dyspnea.

Equally instructive was the patient in whom closure was deferred after respiratory optimization because symptoms improved sufficiently for the patient to decline catheterization and intervention. Despite the presence of a moderate-sized ASD, respiratory evaluation clarified that airway disease represented the dominant contributor to functional limitation. This observation does not imply that ASD closure should be withheld in the presence of airway disease; rather, it highlights the importance of establishing the relative contribution of coexisting pathology before intervention and of setting realistic expectations regarding symptom improvement.

Preprocedural pulmonary optimization served both therapeutic and diagnostic functions within the integrated assessment strategy. Clinical improvement following BD or anti-inflammatory therapy helped clarify symptom attribution and refine expectations regarding postclosure outcomes. This distinction is relevant because respiratory symptoms and inhalant use may persist despite successful ASD closure, consistent with the persistence of intrinsic airway disease after correction of the hemodynamic lesion.9

An important practical lesson from this series relates to the recognition of disproportionate dyspnea. In patients with known ASD, coexisting airway disease should be considered when symptom severity appears excessive relative to the apparent hemodynamic burden of the defect, particularly when symptoms persist despite appropriate cardiac therapy or are accompanied by wheeze, BD responsiveness, or airway abnormalities on thoracic imaging. Conversely, in patients with established airway disease, persistent symptoms despite appropriate respiratory management, especially when accompanied by echocardiographic evidence of right-heart volume overload or unexplained cardiovascular findings, should prompt consideration of an underlying hemodynamically significant ASD. Although these observations are based on clinical experience rather than predefined diagnostic criteria, they may help identify patients who would benefit from integrated cardiopulmonary evaluation.

From a clinical perspective, these findings suggest that disproportionate dyspnea in ASD should prompt evaluation beyond hemodynamics alone, including pulmonology consultation, spirometry, and selective advanced pulmonary imaging. Conversely, in patients with airway disease who demonstrate inadequate treatment response, particularly in the absence of strong conventional risk factors, underlying hemodynamic lesions such as ASD should be considered. Integrated cardiopulmonary evaluation is therefore not an adjunctive exercise, but a clinically important extension of assessment in selected patients with unexplained or disproportionate symptoms.

Study Limitations

This retrospective single-center case series is limited by its small sample size, retrospective design, and lack of uniform longitudinal pulmonary imaging and spirometric follow-up. Detailed pulmonary function datasets were not uniformly available for all patients, although specialist pulmonology assessment was available throughout the cohort. Cases were retrospectively identified based on recognition of a distinctive bidirectional cardiopulmonary diagnostic pattern during routine clinical care rather than through systematic screening; consequently, referral and selection bias are inherent limitations of the present series. Despite these limitations, the consistent bidirectional referral pattern and its influence on symptom attribution, integrated cardiopulmonary evaluation, and procedural decision-making support the clinical relevance of these observations.

Conclusions

In adults with secundum ASD, disproportionate dyspnea may reflect combined cardiac and airway pathology rather than a single-system disorder. Airway disease may remain unrecognized in ASD until symptoms appear disproportionate to hemodynamic severity, whereas ASD may remain undetected in patients with established respiratory diagnoses and inadequate treatment response. Integrated cardiopulmonary evaluation may improve symptom attribution, guide patient selection and closure strategy, and influence decisions regarding fenestration or deferral of intervention. These findings support a shift from anatomy-driven to physiology-informed assessment in selected patients with ASD and unexplained or disproportionate dyspnea.
