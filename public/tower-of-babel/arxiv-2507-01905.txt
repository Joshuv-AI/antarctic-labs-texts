Improved energies and local energies with weighted variational Monte Carlo
Huan Zhang, Robert J. Webber, Michael Lindsey, Timothy C. Berkelbach, Jonathan Weare

Abstract
Neural network parametrizations have increasingly been used to represent the ground and excited states in variational Monte Carlo (VMC) with promising results. However, traditional VMC methods only optimize the wave function in regions of peak probability. The wave function is uncontrolled in the tails of the probability distribution, which can limit the accuracy of the trained wavefunction approximation. To improve the approximation accuracy in the probability tails, this paper interprets VMC as a gradient flow in the space of wave functions, followed by a projection step. From this perspective, arbitrary probability distributions can be used in the projection step, allowing the user to prioritize accuracy in different regions of state space. Motivated by this theoretical perspective, the paper tests a new weighted VMC method on the antiferromagnetic Heisenberg model for a periodic spin chain. Compared to traditional VMC, weighted VMC reduces the error in the ground state energy by a factor of 2 2 and it reduces the errors in the local energies away from the mode by large factors of 10 2 10^{2} – 10 4 10^{4} .

Introduction

The introduction of neural network wave function parametrizations
carleo2017solving
;
luo2019backflow
;
pfau2020ab
;
hermann2020deep
has sparked renewed interest in the variational Monte Carlo (VMC)
(
gubernatis2016quantum
;
becca2017quantum
;
PhysRevLett.121.167204
;
nomura2021helping
;
pathak2021excited
;
entwistle2023electronic
;
filippi2009absorption
;
zimmerman2009excited
;
zhao2016efficient
)
approach to approximating quantum many-body ground and excited states. In its simplest version, VMC approximates the ground state of a given system by minimizing an energy functional
E(\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
over a parametrized class of wave functions
\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
.
The approach uses Monte Carlo sampling
to estimate the parameter gradient
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}E(\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
needed for energy minimization.

Neural quantum states have shown tremendous promise within VMC, but they also lead to a problem of generalization error
westerhout2020generalization
.
The combination of a nonlinear wave function representation with Monte Carlo approximation can lead to low accuracy wave function estimates away from regions of high probability, and it can even lead to unstable optimization
webber2021rayleigh
;
Zhang2023understanding
.

This paper focuses on computing the ground state of
the antiferromagnetic Heisenberg XXX model, which has proved computationally challenging for VMC
park2020geometry
;
Zhang2023understanding
even though the ground state can be derived analytically using the Bethe ansatz
Karbach1998Bethe
.
Our previous work
Zhang2023understanding
documents the generalization error in the estimated ground state for the XXX model.
This error is concentrated in the region of ferromagnetic configurations far away from the mode of the wave function.
To help reduce this error, we proposed
Zhang2023understanding
adding a term to the VMC objective function that prevents the wave function from growing in the ferromagnetic region.
However, this approach does not ensure relative accuracy in the approximation of the ground state in the region of low
\lvert\psi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\rvert^{2}
.

The current paper presents a new approach to improve the wave function accuracy,
based on understanding VMC optimization as a gradient flow followed by a projection step
stokes2020quantum
;
neklyudov2023wasserstein
.
In this theoretical framework, the wave function is first optimized using a gradient flow that reduces the energy. Then the evolved wave function is projected onto the space of parameterized wave functions.
The evolve-and-project approach is at the core of time-dependent variational principle commonly used to optimize wave function approximations
diract1930note
;
PhysRevB.94.165116
.

The projection step depends on the choice of distance function,
since it identifies the element within the parametric class with the
smallest distance
to the evolved wave function.
This paper introduces the idea of a weighted distance that enhances the projection accuracy in low-probability regions.
The weighted distance leads to a modified formula for the VMC parameter updates, which can be evaluated using Monte Carlo samples drawn from a weighted wave function density.
Conversely, the user is free to generate samples from any convenient distribution, and the parameter update calculated with the samples corresponds to a projection step with an associated set of weights.
This new “weighted VMC” approach does not require prior knowledge of the problem and should be widely applicable.

Our numerical experiments indicate the success of weighted VMC applied to the Heisenberg XXX model.
The experiments identify several sampling methods leading to improved energies and local energies throughout the configuration space.
Mixed tempering and well-tempered metadynamics
metadynamics2002
;
welltemperedmetadynamics2008
are broadly applicable methods that yield a factor of
2
energy improvement compared to traditional VMC.
Additionally, uniform CV sampling is based on identifying a physically important collective variable.
It yields competitive energy estimates, and it improves the local energies in targeted regions by factors up to
10^{4}
.

II
Background

This section first introduces the XXX model and the RBM wave function ansatz.
Then, it describes the standard VMC optimization using stochastic reconfiguration.

II.1
Model and ansatz

This paper focuses on the antiferromagnetic Heisenberg XXX model for
N
spin-
1/\penalty 2
particles in a one-dimensional periodic chain.
After a stoquastic transformation
bishop2000marshall
, the Hamiltonian for the XXX model is

\displaystyle\hat{H}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigl(-\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{x}\hat{\sigma}_{i+1}^{x}+\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{y}\hat{\sigma}_{i+1}^{y}+\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{z}\hat{\sigma}_{i+1}^{z}\bigr),

(1)

where
\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{x}
,
\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{y}
, and
\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{z}
are the Pauli operators for the
i
th spin. Here and throughout, periodic boundary conditions are implied via the identification
\hat{\sigma}_{N+i}=\hat{\sigma}_{i}
.
The Hamiltonian admits a unique, nonnegative-valued ground state wave function due to the Perron-Frobenius theorem.

The Hamiltonian for the XXX model commutes with the translation operator, so each ground and excited state
\psi
must satisfy a symmetry relationship

\psi(\mathcal{T}_{j}\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})={\rm e}^{\mathsf{i}2\pi kj/N}\psi({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}),

where
T_{j}
is the translation operator,
(T_{j}\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})_{i}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{i-j}
, and
k\in\{0,1,\ldots,N-1\}
is the symmetry class.
Hereafter we focus on the
k=0
subspace, which contains the ground state due to the Perron-Frobenius theorem.

We define the variational wave function using a basis of simultaneous eigenstates of the operators
\{\hat{\sigma}_{i}\}=\{\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{z}\}
, where we drop the
z
indicator for notational simplicity.
Using this basis, our wave function ansatz
carleo2017solving
can be written as

\psi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})=\\
\sum_{(h_{j})_{1\leq j\leq N}}\exp\Biggl(\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}\sigma_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{\alpha N}b_{j}h_{j}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{\alpha N}W_{ji}h_{j}\sigma_{i}\Biggr),

where
\sigma_{i}\in\{-1,+1\}
are spin variables,

h_{j}\in\{-1,+1\}
are an additional set of
\alpha N
hidden spin variables,
and
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},\boldsymbol{W}\}
are variational parameters.
This ansatz is an example of a restricted Boltzmann machine (RBM)
tieleman2008training
, which is a one-hidden-layer neural network that has been widely used in VMC in recent years
carleo2017solving
;
Zhang2023understanding
;
webber2021rayleigh
.

Following
carleo2017solving
;
webber2021rayleigh
, we modify the RBM ansatz by summing over the hidden spins
h_{k}
and enforcing translation symmetry:

\psi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})=\prod_{k=1}^{\alpha}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\cosh\biggl(b_{k}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}W_{ki}\sigma_{i+j}\biggr).

(2)

This reduces the set of variational parameters to

{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\{\boldsymbol{b},\boldsymbol{W}\}
.
Throughout the numerical experiments, we use the width parameter
\alpha=50
, but see Appendix
A
for further discussion.

II.2
Variational Monte Carlo

In this section, we adopt the standard
L^{2}
inner product and norm:

\langle\psi,\phi\rangle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\overline{\psi({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})}\phi({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}),\quad\|\psi\|^{2}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}|\psi({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})|^{2}.

Variational Monte Carlo (VMC) approximates the ground state by minimizing the Rayleigh quotient

E(\psi)\coloneqq\frac{\langle\psi,H\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi,\psi\rangle}

(3)

within a parametrized manifold
\psi=\psi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
.
Stochastic reconfiguration (SR)
becca2017quantum
is a popular method to optimize traditional
Sorella2001GenLanczos
;
sorella2007weak
and more recent
carleo2017solving
;
pfau2020ab
wave functions within VMC.
SR is motivated by considering a small parameter update
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\leftarrow{\boldsymbol{\theta}}+\boldsymbol{\delta}
.
The parameter update changes the energy in a predictable way, according to the following linearized equation:

E_{\rm linear}(\psi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}+\boldsymbol{\delta}})=E(\psi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})+\boldsymbol{\delta}^{*}{\boldsymbol{g}}+{\boldsymbol{g}}^{*}\boldsymbol{\delta}.

(4)

Here,
\boldsymbol{g}
is the Wirtinger gradient
Wirtinger1927
of the energy, defined by

\boldsymbol{g}=\frac{\langle\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},[H-E(\psi)]\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\rangle}{\langle\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\rangle}.

(5)

Each step of SR minimizes the linearized energy (
4
) subject to quadratic penalty terms.
The first quadratic penalty is based on the square angle between wave functions
webber2021rayleigh

\boldsymbol{\delta}^{*}\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{\delta}\approx(\angle(\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\psi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}+\boldsymbol{\delta}}))^{2}=\biggl(\arccos\frac{\left|\langle\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\psi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}+\boldsymbol{\delta}}\rangle\right|}{\|\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|\|\psi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}+\boldsymbol{\delta}}\|}\biggr)^{2},

where
\boldsymbol{S}
is the overlap matrix defined by

\boldsymbol{S}=\frac{\langle\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}\rangle}{\langle\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\rangle}-\frac{\langle\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\rangle}{\langle\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\rangle}\frac{\langle\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}\rangle}{\langle\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\rangle}.

(6)

The second quadratic penalty is based on the square magnitude of the update,
\eta\lVert\boldsymbol{\delta}\rVert^{2}
.
In summary, SR solves the optimization problem

\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\biggl[\frac{1}{\epsilon}\boldsymbol{\delta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{S}+\eta\boldsymbol{I})\boldsymbol{\delta}+\boldsymbol{\delta}^{*}{\boldsymbol{g}}+{\boldsymbol{g}}^{*}\boldsymbol{\delta}\biggr],\quad\text{for }\eta\geq 0.

The resulting parameter update is the solution to the positive definite linear system

(\boldsymbol{S}+\eta\boldsymbol{I})\boldsymbol{\delta}=\epsilon\boldsymbol{g}.

(7)

In practice, the exact SR update (
7
) is hard to calculate, so a Monte Carlo approximation is used.
Suppose one can sample from the wave function density

\rho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})=\frac{|\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})|^{2}}{\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{\prime}}|\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{\prime})|^{2}}

using a population of Markov chain Monte Carlo walkers (see Sec.
IV
).
Then the energy (
3
) can be reformulated and estimated as an expectation

E(\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}E_{\mathrm{loc}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})\rho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})=\mathbb{E}_{\rho}\left[E_{\mathrm{loc}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})\right],

(8)

where
E_{\mathrm{loc}}
denotes the “local energy”, defined by

E_{\mathrm{loc}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})=\frac{(H\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})}{\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})}.

The gradient vector
\boldsymbol{g}
and overlap matrix
\boldsymbol{S}
can be reformulated and estimated as covariances with respect to the wave function density
\rho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})
.
The resulting SR algorithm is presented as Algorithm
1
.

Algorithm 1
(Stochastic reconfiguration)

Choose the parameter update
\boldsymbol{\delta}
to solve

(\boldsymbol{S}+\eta\mathbf{I})\boldsymbol{\delta}=-\epsilon\boldsymbol{g}.

(9)

Here,
\eta\geq 0
is a nonnegative parameter that makes
\boldsymbol{S}+\eta\boldsymbol{I}
positive definite.
The energy
E(\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
, gradient vector
\boldsymbol{g}
, and overlap matrix
\boldsymbol{S}
are defined by

\displaystyle E(\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\mathbb{E}_{\rho}\left[E_{\mathrm{loc}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})\right],

(10a)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{g}=\mathrm{Cov}_{\rho}\left[\frac{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\psi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})}{\psi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})},E_{\mathrm{loc}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})\right],

(10b)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{S}=\mathrm{Cov}_{\rho}\left[\frac{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\psi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})}{\psi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})},\frac{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\psi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})}{\psi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})}\right],

(10c)

where
\mathbb{E}_{\rho}
and
\mathrm{Cov}_{\rho}
indicate the expectation value and covariance with respect to the wave function density
\rho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})
.

III
Weighted variational Monte Carlo

This section derives stochastic reconfiguration from an alternative perspective of gradient flows, building on the approach in
neklyudov2023wasserstein
.
Then it proposes a modified gradient flow method to improve generalization error in VMC.

III.1
Gradient flow perspective

The energy functional
E(\psi)
has a Wirtinger gradient
Wirtinger1927
that is a
n
-dimensional vector

\nabla_{\psi}E(\psi)=\frac{H\psi-E(\psi)\psi}{\langle\psi,\psi\rangle}.

Therefore, a method to find the exact ground state is based on running the continuous-time gradient flow

\partial_{t}\psi_{t}=-\nabla_{\psi_{t}}E(\psi_{t})=\frac{E(\psi_{t})\psi_{t}-H\psi_{t}}{\langle\psi_{t},\psi_{t}\rangle}.

(11)

Yet, this continuous-time evolution cannot be implemented exactly when the wave function lies on a parametrized manifold
\psi_{t}=\psi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
.
The gradient flow pushes
\psi_{t+\epsilon}
off the manifold, so that no parameter vector
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t+\epsilon}
exactly corresponds to
\psi_{t+\epsilon}
defined by Eq. (
11
).

To apply gradient flow within a parametrized manifold, it is necessary to combine evolution with projection.
We first evolve the wave function from
\psi
to
\psi_{t+\epsilon}
.
We then identify a parameter vector
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t+\epsilon}
that projects
\psi_{t+\epsilon}
onto the manifold:

{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t+\epsilon}=\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}d(\psi_{t+\epsilon},\psi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}).

(12)

The projection step can be carried out in any metric
d(\cdot,\cdot)
, and one natural choice is the Fubini-Study metric
stokes2020quantum

d(\phi,\psi)=\angle(\phi,\psi)=\arccos\frac{\left|\langle\phi,\psi\rangle\right|}{\|\phi\|\|\psi\|},

which measures the angle between two unnormalized wave functions.
In the limit of small time steps
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
, the evolve-and-project method leads to a parameter update

{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t+\epsilon}={\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}-\frac{\epsilon}{\lVert\psi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\rVert^{2}}\boldsymbol{S}^{-1}\boldsymbol{g}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2}).

This update corresponds to the SR method (
9
) with
\eta=0

and a modified step size

\epsilon/\lVert\psi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\rVert^{2}
.

III.2
Weighted variational Monte Carlo

We now exploit the flexibility afforded by the evolve-and-project method.
Given any positive weight function
\mu({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})
, we can
introduce a weighted Fubini-Study metric

d_{\mu}(\phi,\psi)=\arccos\frac{|\langle\phi,\psi\rangle_{\mu}|}{\|\phi\|_{\mu}\|\psi\|_{\mu}},

with an inner product and norm defined by

\displaystyle\langle\phi,\psi\rangle_{\mu}

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}\mu({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})\overline{\phi({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})}\psi({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}),

\displaystyle\|\psi\|^{2}_{\mu}

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}\mu({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})|\psi({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})|^{2}.

Like the standard Fubini-Study metric,
d_{\mu}
is nonnegative and
d_{\mu}(\phi,\psi)=0
only when
\phi=c\psi
where
c
is a complex number.
However, the weight function
\mu({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})
is a new feature in this approach.

The change in metric leads to a change in the projection step.
Now, as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
, the evolve-and-project method leads to

{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t+\epsilon}={\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}-\frac{\epsilon}{\|\psi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\|_{\mu}^{2}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{-1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{g}}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2}).

Here, the gradient vector
\boldsymbol{g}
in (
5
) and overlap matrix
\boldsymbol{S}
in (
6
) are modified to become:

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{g}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\langle\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},(H-E(\psi))\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\rangle_{\mu}}{\langle\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\rangle_{\mu}},

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{S}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\langle\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}\rangle_{\mu}}{\langle\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\rangle_{\mu}}-\frac{\langle\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\rangle_{\mu}}{\langle\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\rangle_{\mu}}\frac{\langle\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}\rangle_{\mu}}{\langle\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\rangle_{\mu}}.

The major difference is that the
L^{2}
inner products have been replaced by
\mu
-weighted inner products.
This evolve-and-project approach can be understood as an example of the time-dependent variational principle
diract1930note
;
PhysRevB.94.165116
;
Bruna_2024
;
zhang2024sequentialintime
.

In weighted VMC, the parameter update
\tilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{-1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{g}}
is hard to calculate exactly.
Instead,
\tilde{\boldsymbol{g}}
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{S}}
can be estimated using covariances with respect to a weighted wave function density

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})=\frac{\mu({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})|\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})|^{2}}{\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{\prime}}\mu({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{\prime})|\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{\prime})|^{2}}.

(13)

The new “weighted VMC” method based on (
13
) is presented as Algorithm
2
.
As usual, the practical algorithm includes a small factor
\eta\mathbf{I}
, which helps stabilize the updates.

Algorithm 2
(Weighted VMC)

Choose the parameter update
\boldsymbol{\delta}
to solve

(\tilde{\boldsymbol{S}}+\eta\mathbf{I})\boldsymbol{\delta}=-\epsilon\tilde{\boldsymbol{g}}.

(14)

Here,
\eta\geq 0
is a nonnegative parameter that makes
\tilde{\boldsymbol{S}}+\eta\boldsymbol{I}
positive definite.
The energy
E(\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
, gradient vector
\tilde{\boldsymbol{g}}
, and overlap matrix
\tilde{\boldsymbol{S}}
are defined by

\displaystyle E(\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\mathbb{E}_{\rho}\left[E_{\mathrm{loc}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})\right],

(15a)

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{g}}=\mathrm{Cov}_{\tilde{\rho}}\left[\frac{\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\psi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})}{\psi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})},E_{\mathrm{loc}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})\right],

(15b)

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{S}}=\mathrm{Cov}_{\tilde{\rho}}\left[\frac{\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\psi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})}{\psi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})},\frac{\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\psi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})}{\psi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})}\right]\,,

(15c)

where
\mathbb{E}_{\rho}
indicates the expectation value with respect to the wave function density
\rho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})
and
\mathrm{Cov}_{\tilde{\rho}}
indicates the covariance with respect to the weighted wave function density
{\tilde{\rho}}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})
.

Weighted VMC uses a sequence of sampling distributions that are defined iteratively.
At each optimization step, we generate samples from a weighted distribution
\tilde{\rho}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})
to optimize the parameter vector
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
.
Then, after all the optimization steps are complete, we generate samples from
\rho_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})
to evaluate the energy
E(\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
.
This approach has the same computational cost as standard VMC (Alg.
1
).

What opportunities does weighted VMC provide us?
In standard VMC, the projection step hardly emphasizes the regions of small wave function density
\rho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})
.
This lack of emphasis is reflected in Eq (
10
), where samples from
\rho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
are used to compute the covariances: these samples overwhelmingly come from the high-probability regions.
In contrast, weighted VMC controls the relative importance of different regions using a weighted sampling distribution
\tilde{\rho}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})
.
Increasing the relative density of
\tilde{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})
at a location
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
results in greater agreement between
\psi_{t+\epsilon}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})
and the parametrized update
\psi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}+\boldsymbol{\delta}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})
, which can lead to higher quality at the selected location.
By adjusting the sampling distribution, we can selectively refine the wave function where refinement is needed the most.

IV
Weighted distributions

The key to weighted VMC is the freedom to choose a weighted distribution that emphasizes important regions of configuration space.
This section describes several distributions that can be used for sampling from
the XXX Heisenberg model.
The section first describes the standard Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) approach to sample from the unweighted wave function density.
Then it proposes three modifications to the sampling approach that emphasize low-probability regions.

IV.1
Markov chain Monte Carlo

Here we explain how to sample from the wave function density
\rho_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})
using MCMC.
All the sampling methods for weighted distributions
\tilde{\rho}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})
are modified from this basic MCMC sampling scheme.

Our approach uses a population of
M=100
independent MCMC walkers, which are labeled
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{t}^{m}
for
m=1,\ldots,M
and
t=0,1,\ldots
.
We evolve each walker by proposing a new configuration
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{t+1}^{m}
where a randomly chosen positive spin
({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{t}^{m})_{i}=+1
and a randomly chosen negative spin
({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{t}^{m})_{j}=-1
are simultaneously flipped,

({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{t+1}^{m})_{\ell}=\begin{cases}-({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{t}^{m})_{\ell},&\ell\in\{i,j\},\\
({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{t}^{m})_{\ell},&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

We accept the “swap move” with probability

\min\biggl\{1,\frac{|\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{t+1}^{m})|^{2}}{|\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{t}^{m})|^{2}}\biggr\}.

Otherwise we reject and set
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{t+1}^{m}={\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{t}^{m}
instead.
We carry out 2000 Metropolis steps in this way, and we subsample the resulting
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
values once per 100 steps, reducing the computation and storage costs to
20
samples per walker.
This MCMC sampling scheme preserves the total magnetization,
\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{t}^{m})_{\ell}=0
, which is appropriate since the exact ground state of the XXX model (
1
) is supported on states of total magnetization zero whenever there is a bipartite lattice
bishop2000marshall
.

IV.2
Mixed tempering

The first version of weighted VMC uses a “mixed tempering” (MT) distribution, where one half of the MCMC walkers are sampled from
\rho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})
and the other half are sampled from a tempered distribution

\frac{\rho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})^{\beta}}{\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}\rho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})^{\beta}},

determined by an “inverse temperature” parameter
\beta\in[0,1]
.
Therefore, the MT distribution can be written as

\tilde{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})=\frac{\rho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})}{2}+\frac{\rho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})^{\beta}}{2\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}\rho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})^{\beta}}.

(16)

Fig.
1
visualizes the MT distribution with
\beta=1.0
,
0.8
, and
0.5
along two choices of “collective variables” (CVs).
The first CV is the nearest-neighbor spin correlation function

\displaystyle s(\boldsymbol{\sigma})\equiv\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{i+1}.

(17)

The second CV is the negative logarithm of the unnormalized wave function density

\displaystyle\xi({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})\equiv-\log|\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})|^{2}\,.

(18)

When
\beta=1
in Eq. (
16
), the MT distribution reduces to the wave function density
\rho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})
.
However, as
\beta\to 0
, the second group of walkers spreads more uniformly over the state space
\{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\}
.
This spreading out is reflected in the histograms in Fig.
1
.

s({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})
defined in (

) (left) and
\xi({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})

) (right), when

\beta=1.0
,

, or

IV.3
Well-tempered metadynamics

Another version of weighted VMC is based on well-tempered metadynamics (WTMD)
metadynamics2002
;
welltemperedmetadynamics2008
.
WTMD is a Monte Carlo method that estimates the marginal distribution of a high-dimensional probability distribution along a user-specified CV.
WTMD drives the walkers to explore regions where the estimated marginal density is small, and it uses reweighting to recover statistics.

In this study, we apply a sampling method inspired by WTMD where the CV is the negative logarithm of the unnormalized wave function density,
\xi({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})=-\log|\psi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})|^{2}
as in (
18
).
Our specific sampling procedure follows:

1.

Initialization.
Set
w
and
\Delta T
parameters for the marginal density estimation.
Set the lower bound
L_{\xi}
and bin interval
\Delta\xi
for the histogram of
\xi
.
Set the initial histogram values
V=(0,\ldots,0)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, and choose an initial configuration of walkers
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{0}^{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{0}^{M}
.

2.

Sampling.
For each
t=0,\ldots,T
and
m=1,\ldots,M
, perform the following steps.

(a)

Propose an evolution from
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{t}^{m}
to
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{t+1}^{m}
using a random swap move.
Accept
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{t+1}^{m}
with probability

\displaystyle\min\biggl\{1,\frac{{\rm e}^{V({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{t}^{m})}\,|\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{t+1}^{m})|^{2}}{{\rm e}^{V({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{t+1}^{m})}\,|\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{t}^{m})|^{2}}\biggr\}.

(19)

Otherwise reject and set
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{t+1}^{m}={\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{t}^{m}
.

(b)

Compute a bin index
i\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

\displaystyle\xi({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{t+1}^{m})\in(L_{\xi}+(i-1)\Delta\xi,L_{\xi}+i\Delta\xi].

Round up to
i=0
if
i
is negative.
Round down to
i=n+1
if
i
exceeds
n+1
.
Then update the associated histogram value

\displaystyle V(i)=V(i)+w\,{\rm e}^{-V(i)/\Delta T}.

In the WTMD sampling procedure, the walkers are driven to explore new regions because of the term
{\rm e}^{V({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{t}^{m})-V({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{t+1}^{m})}
in the acceptance probability (
19
).
After
T
steps, the walkers are done exploring and we define the sampling distribution as the average of delta functions on the walkers’ locations

\tilde{\rho}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})=\frac{1}{MT}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\delta_{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{t}^{m}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}).

We start fresh by re-initializing the marginal density approximation and the sampling distribution after updating the VMC parameter weights.

IV.4
Uniform CV sampling

The MT and WTMD sampling distributions can be applied to any quantum system, since they only depend on the unnormalized wave function density
|\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})|^{2}
.
However, a different approach leverages prior knowledge of the physical system in the form of a well-chosen CV.
For example, the ground state of the XXX model is primarily supported on configurations in the antiferromagnetic region where

s({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{i+1}<0.

In contrast, the wave function density puts little mass on the ferromagnetic region,
\{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\,|\,s({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})>0\}
.
We can help address this imbalance
by applying weighted VMC with a target distribution

\tilde{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})=0.05\,p({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})+0.95\,\rho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}),

(20)

where
p({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})
has a nearly uniform marginal distribution along the CV
s({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})
.
In practice, we can pre-generate samples from
p({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})
and randomly drawn from the pool in each iteration of weighted VMC.
See Appendix
B
for details.

This section describes numerical experiments comparing traditional and weighted VMC methods (Algs.
1
and
2
).
We tested both VMC approaches on the XXX Heisenberg model for a periodic chain of
N=100
spins.
We evaluated performance based on two metrics: energy error and local energy error.

The energy error is defined as

[E(\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})-E_{0}]/N.

(21)

Here,
E(\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
is the energy associated with the VMC wave function
\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, and
E_{0}
is the exact ground state energy.
Throughout this section, energies are normalized by the number of spins
N
.

The local energy error is defined as

|E_{\rm loc}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})-E_{0}|/N.

(22)

Here,
E_{\mathrm{loc}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})=(H\psi)({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})/\psi({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})
is the “local energy”, which satisfies
E_{\mathrm{loc}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})=E_{0}
when
\psi
is the exact ground state.
Therefore, the local energy error quantifies local inaccuracies in the VMC wave function approximation.

The energy and local energy errors were numerically evaluated as follows:

•

We calculated the ground state energy
E_{0}
exactly, using the Bethe ansatz
Karbach1998Bethe

•

To evaluate the energy
E(\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
via Eq.
8

after

\rho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
.
We subsampled every 100 steps and removed the first half of the time series to account for initialization bias.

•

Last, we evaluated the local energy error for traditional and weighted VMC using a diverse set of samples described in Appendix

VI
Conclusion

While neural networks are highly expressive models for quantum states, they lead to a well-known problem of generalization error.
The learned wave functions can be inaccurate in low-probability regions, which makes them risky to use for downstream applications.

In this study, we proposed a weighted VMC method that improves the accuracy of neural network approximations in low-probability regions.
The core idea is to decouple the sampling distribution
\tilde{\rho}_{\theta}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})
from the wave function density
\rho_{\theta}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})
.

We found the weighted VMC approach has several advantages over traditional VMC:

1.

Improved energy estimation
.
Weighted VMC improves the accuracy of the estimated energies and local energies without additional computational cost.

2.

Flexible sampling
. Weighted VMC can use an arbitrary sampling distribution targeting regions where wave function refinement is needed.

3.

Flexible implementation
. The method supports advanced sampling techniques such as tempering, metadynamics, and importance sampling, as well as problem-specific modifications, without any reweighting. It is broadly applicable to any quantum system with or without detailed prior knowledge of the system.

4.

Extensions
. Our general approach can be extended to other optimization problems consisting of gradient flow and projection steps. The metric used in the projection step can be designed to improve accuracy in specific regions.

While our analysis focused on the energy and the local energy accuracy in low-probability regions, we anticipate that weighted VMC will also positively impact other observables beyond energy, such as spin correlation functions.
