Numerical Study of Turbulent Wind Flow and Hydrocarbon Dispersion in Crude Oil Tank Farms
Authors: Zhi Zhang, Yujie Lin, Qi Cao, Kang Yu, Yi Liu, Anfeng Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
As a critical link connecting upstream and downstream sectors in the petrochemical industry, crude oil storage tank farms serve as vital hubs for production, transfer, and import/export operations, where safety management presents unique complexities. However, current safety regulations have failed to keep pace with the rapid development of the petrochemical sector, leading to frequent oil-gas leakage incidents. This study investigates the stress states of hydrocarbon components (both near-leakage sources and far-field) and their mixing characteristics with air under complex turbulent flow conditions. We establish a numerical model and solution algorithm capable of describing cross-scale oil-gas dispersion and cloud evolution dynamics. The model is applicable to (1)­High-speed regions near leakage sources (<300 m/s, without shockwave structures); (2)­Far-field environments where atmospheric turbulence dominates hydrogen dispersion concentration fields. Through numerical simulations studies conducted at an actual crude oil, we systematically analyze the “leakage-mixing, cloud formation, dispersion-migration, and flow-dilution” processes under various scenarios. The work quantifies the spreading range and concentration distribution of hydrocarbon clouds at different stages, providing theoretical guidance for quantitative risk assessment of oil-gas leaks in storage areas and Emergency response design.

Compounding these risks, many tank farms still fail to meet the “single tank-single dike” safety standard, with inadequate spacing between storage units being particularly prevalent. This layout deficiency creates significant hazards during maintenance activities such as hot work on individual tanks, as adjacent units remain in normal operation. In such scenarios, any vapor leakage can rapidly form extensive flammable clouds, substantially increasing explosion risks. This underscores the critical importance of analyzing vapor dispersion patterns in crude oil tank farms to accurately determine flammable cloud propagation zones and potential blast overpressure ranges - knowledge that is fundamental to enhancing facility safety management and emergency response planning.

To address these challenges, researchers have conducted extensive studies on hydrocarbon dispersion processes. The leakage and diffusion of oil-gas mixtures involve complex fluid interactions between hydrocarbons and air. Moreover, the presence of storage tanks, surrounding structures, and intricate piping networks near leakage sources can readily induce vortex formation, making accurate characterization of turbulent flow structures fundamental to understanding dispersion dynamics and mixing behavior. Currently, no universal turbulence model exists that can fully describe turbulent flow evolution across all Reynolds number regimes, as each model type has its specific limitations and applicable ranges. Bermard-Michel et al. conducted comparative numerical simulations and experimental measurements of helium dispersion in a confined space with dual vents. Their findings demonstrated that Large Eddy Simulation (LES) yielded results qualitatively consistent with PIV measurements, though LES tended to be more conservative than RANS methods, often overpredicting helium concentrations. Similarly, Molkov et al. performed numerical simulations and experimental validation for subsonic helium release and dispersion in a single-vented enclosure. Their results consistently showed that the LES model outperformed other approaches in accurately reproducing helium concentration distributions. These studies collectively highlight LES as a particularly suitable approach for simulating gas dispersion scenarios involving complex turbulent flows, though its computational demands remain a consideration for practical applications.

The dispersion patterns of hydrocarbon leaks are significantly influenced by complex environmental and operational factors, including atmospheric flow characteristics, leakage flow rates, ambient temperature/humidity, and obstacle geometries. Luo et al. conducted simulations using FLACS software to investigate the explosion processes of leaked gas clouds in LPG tank farms, specifically analyzing how varying leakage rates, wind speeds, and directions affect gas cloud dispersion, flame propagation, and the evolution of explosion overpressure and temperature fields. Through scaled experiments on refined oil storage tanks, Leite et al. examined how tank dimensions influence key parameters such as vapor cloud coverage, concentration distribution, as well as combustion characteristics including burning rate, flame speed, heat release rate, and near-field temperature profiles during fire incidents. Marsegan et al. performed numerical simulations using PHAST software to study the free dispersion of LNG following accidental pipeline ruptures in turbulent atmospheric conditions, with particular focus on how water vapor and containment berms alter gas dispersion patterns. Their findings provide valuable guidance for optimizing protective measures during hazardous gas leaks. Zhang et al. combined numerical simulations with wind tunnel experiments to analyze how floating deck position variations affect local turbulent wind fields around storage tanks. Their work highlights that during hydrocarbon leaks caused by seal failures or breathing effects in floating roof tanks, local wind turbulence can significantly impede gas exchange between the tank interior and exterior, thereby hindering the dilution process of vapor concentrations.

Current CFD research on hydrocarbon dispersion primarily focuses on applying existing models (or empirical models) to analyze leakage behavior in simplified geometries (e.g., regularly shaped tanks or enclosed cylindrical cavities). These studies typically employ classical models such as RANS or LES to approximate real-world leakage processes and derive corresponding vapor concentration distributions and flow field characteristics. However, given the complex flow dynamics introduced by hydrocarbon components, several critical research gaps remain:(1)Component-Flow Field Coupling: The continuous modification of air properties by intruding hydrocarbons creates bidirectional feedback between mixture transport and flow evolution - a mechanism requiring precise characterization for accurate concentration field prediction.(2)Density-Driven Transport: The substantial density/viscosity disparities between hydrocarbon components generate strong concentration-gradient-driven flows, necessitating enhanced CFD models incorporating buoyancy effects for multicomponent systems.

Aiming at the limitations of traditional CFD models in simulating oil and gas diffusion, particularly in representing the two key mechanisms of “component-flow field bidirectional feedback” and “multicomponent buoyancy-driven flow,” this paper establishes a numerical model and solution algorithm to describe the cross-scale domain oil and gas leakage diffusion and cloud evolution. The model accounts for the stress state of oil and gas components near the leakage source and in the far field, as well as the mixing characteristics of oil-gas-air under complex turbulent flow conditions. It is applicable for the accurate prediction of oil and gas diffusion concentration fields in both the high-speed region near the leakage source (<300 m/s, where no shock wave structure is formed) and the far-field region dominated by environmental wind turbulence. Furthermore, by considering different leakage scenarios such as tank valve breathing effects and failures, the influence of key factors such as liquid level height and Reynolds number of the wind field on oil and gas diffusion is analyzed. This provides theoretical guidance for the quantitative assessment of oil and gas leakage risks in crude oil tank areas and the design of engineering protection measures.

Hydrocarbon Leakage and Dispersion Model

Fundamental Assumptions

Following hydrocarbon leaks in crude oil tank farms, the released vapors rapidly mix with ambient air to form flammable gas clouds. Given the significant density and viscosity variations among different hydrocarbon components, both the flow characteristics and physical properties of the mixture evolve dynamically within the dispersion zone. This section establishes a mathematical model describing this complex flow process by analyzing the mass transfer mechanisms, momentum exchange patterns, and energy transformation characteristics among the components of the mixed gas during hydrocarbon leakage. The model incorporates three key assumptions:(1)The hydrocarbon-air mixture undergoes unsteady three-dimensional flow, with all flow parameters treated as continuously differentiable functions in both spatial and temporal domains.(2)Within any differential control volume, hydrocarbon components and air share identical velocity vectors, neglecting slippage velocity effects caused by property differences between components.(3)While accounting for gas compressibility effects, all gaseous components are treated as ideal gases, thereby excluding intermolecular force influences on macroscopic flow behavior.

Numerical Solution Methodology

The finite volume method is employed in this study to achieve transient computation of both physical properties and flow characteristics for the gas mixture within the solution domain. The computational approach begins with discretizing the solution domain into multiple control volumes, where the flow properties at grid cell centers are used to characterize the evolution of the entire domain.

Discretization of the Continuity Equation

The continuity eq , containing unknown variables ρ and V, can be solved once the density and velocity fields at time t 0 are determined. The numerical implementation first involves discretizing the equation in both spatial and temporal domains, with an explicit scheme adopted for the temporal term.

In far-field regions from the leakage source, where flow velocities remain well below the local speed of sound, central differencing schemes are applied for the convection terms. This approach effectively represents the interfacial fluxes while minimizing numerical dissipation caused by property discontinuities between adjacent cells.

For near-field regions close to leakage sources, such as during valve breathing emissions where internal tank pressure exceeds the relief valve setting (typically 500–2000 Pa), the resulting jet velocities often exceed 100 m/s while remaining subsonic. These high-speed flows, confined to small regions near the source with rapid velocity decay downstream, present unique numerical challenges. Direct application of central differencing schemes in these areas tends to generate small-scale numerical oscillations. While these oscillations may not immediately cause solution divergence, they can propagate to far-field regions and contaminate the entire computational domain with errors.

To address this, a TVD (Total Variation Diminishing) scheme is implemented to suppress numerical oscillations in the near-field convection terms. Given that the jet velocities remain subsonic without physical discontinuities, specialized shock-capturing algorithms are unnecessary. The TVD formulation adopts a semi-implicit approach, where transport velocities are based on the previous time step’s distribution while fluxes are evaluated using current–time-step quantities.

Turbulence Characteristics and Vapor Concentration Distribution under Different Reynolds Numbers

As described in Section , the governing equations for vapor diffusion include both convective and diffusive terms, indicating that the wind field dominates the convective transport of vapors. Therefore, accurate simulation of the wind field is a prerequisite for precise prediction of vapor concentrations. This section examines the influence of wind field Reynolds number and liquid level height on the wind field distribution within the tank farm. It explores the relationship between the Reynolds number at the control region height, leakage rate, and vapor concentration during the diffusion process from leakage sources to the control space above the floating roof at different liquid levels. The distribution patterns of local wind fields and vapor concentrations under crosswind conditions are elucidated.

Vapor Concentration Distribution Patterns

Figure demonstrates the vapor concentration distribution patterns under different liquid level conditions. When the floating roof is at low liquid levels, vapor accumulates throughout the space above the roof. At high liquid levels (80%), vapor concentrates near the leakage source, showing greater downwind diffusion concentration compared to low liquid level conditions. This phenomenon occurs because higher floating roof positions bring the internal diffusion space closer to the external wind field, where enhanced airflow dilution and horizontal transport reduce vapor hazards within the tank. Conversely, lower liquid levels increase the distance between the floating roof and tank top, making internal vapor distribution less affected by wind. In this scenario, vapor ejected from pressure/vacuum vents first deposits on the roof surface due to gravity, then disperses throughout the entire tank space through buoyancy effects and reverse flows caused by wall obstructions.

Conclusions

The infiltration of hydrocarbon components induces progressive modifications to the air composition within the initial flow field, which reciprocally influences the evolutionary dynamics of the ambient flow. This study advances existing CFD methodologies by incorporating: (1) bidirectional coupling between mixture characteristics and flow field evolution, and (2) concentration gradients and buoyancy-driven flows arising from the low-density/low-viscosity properties of hydrocarbon components. A multiscale numerical framework with corresponding solution algorithms has been developed to characterize vapor leakage dispersion and cloud formation dynamics, enabling precise prediction of mixed flow field evolution.

Numerical investigations of vapor leakage were performed for an operational crude oil storage facility, accounting for critical parameters including wind field Reynolds number and liquid level height. The research establishes quantitative relationships between: control zone Reynolds number (at characteristic heights), leakage flux magnitude and resultant vapor concentration distributions.

Key findings elucidate the distribution patterns of local wind fields and vapor concentrations under crosswind conditions, particularly during vapor transport from leakage sources to the floating roof control zone across varying liquid levels. These results provide a theoretical foundation for quantitative risk assessment of vapor leakage in tank farms and evidence-based design of emergency response protocols.
