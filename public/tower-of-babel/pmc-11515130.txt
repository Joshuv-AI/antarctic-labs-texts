Validity of specific CPT indices in differentiating school-aged children previously diagnosed with attention deficit/hyperactivity disorder from school-aged children with non-attention deficit/hyperactivity disorder in general education classrooms: a case control study
Authors: Jeng-Dau Tsai, Han-Yin Sun, Hui-Ying Kuo, Si-Yu Chu, Yu-Wen Lee, Hsin-Hui Lu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundContinuous performance tests (CPTs) are a popular tool for evaluating the symptoms of attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). Performance measurements are typically linked to the biological features and cognitive functions of individuals. To determine the validity of specific CPT indices in differentiating between school-aged children with ADHD from with non-ADHD, each student’s sex, chronological age, and cognitive abilities should be considered.MethodsIn this prospective case–control study, a total of 30 non-ADHD students and 26 with ADHD who were aged 6 to 12 years were from general education classrooms. All students completed the Continuous Performance Tests (CPTs) and the Peabody Picture Vocabulary Test-revised (Mandarin-Chinese version). Demographic data were collected from the students’ parents.ResultsDetectability, Omissions, Commissions, and Hit Reaction Time Standard Deviation (HRT SD) yielded higher T-scores in children with ADHD than those without. Compared with non-ADHD students, those with ADHD had higher classification scores for Detectability, Omissions, Perseverations, and HRT SD. For each CPT index, after individual factors were controlled for, logistic regression revealed that only students with positive scores for Detectability, Omission, and HRT SD (adjusted odds ratios = 4.627, 9.977, and 3.908, Ps < 0.05) were likely to receive a diagnosis of ADHD. Furthermore, after individual characteristics were controlled for, Logistic regression also revealed that the cumulative positive scores of the Detectability, Omission, or and HRT SD remained associated with an increased risk of ADHD (adjusted odds ratio = 3.116, P < 0.01).ConclusionsCompared with school-aged children with non-ADHD in general education classrooms, those with ADHD exhibited significantly lower performance in inattention-related CPT indices. To reach an accurate diagnosis through CPTs, clinicians should pay attention to Detectability, Omission, and HRT SD. Compared with other CPT indices, Detectability, Omission, and HRT SD may function as more suitable indicators for distinguishing between school-aged children with and non-ADHD in general education classrooms. These indicators are robust and unobscured by individual characteristics.

Background

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is the most common neurodevelopmental disorder and the most prevalent chronic health condition affecting school-aged children [1]. Children with ADHD typically experience substantial learning difficulties, academic challenges, and problems in interpersonal relationships with family members and peers, and they exhibit low levels of self-esteem [2]. ADHD is often associated with classroom behavioural disturbances, which require teaching support in general education classrooms [3]. In elementary schools, teachers in general education classrooms tend to refer children with such distractive or overactive behaviours for psychological assessment and diagnosis. Early recognition, assessment, and management of ADHD can enhance the implementation of educational and psychosocial programs in general education classrooms for school-aged children with ADHD and their teachers [4].

Early assessment of ADHD minimises the adverse emotional and social effects associated with this condition and restores the cognitive functioning of children. Additionally, some individuals tend to mimic the symptoms of ADHD to justify their lack of success at school or work [5]. Currently, the diagnostic criteria of ADHD are based on clinical evidence. According to the American Academy of Pediatrics, the evaluation of ADHD involves obtaining the patients’ family and social history and reviewing the rating scale scores from teachers and parents or primary caretakers [6]. Notably, these questionnaires and scales have a certain degree of inaccuracy. For instance, some scales may have objective biases [7]. Some discrepancies have also been observed between parent and teacher ratings of problematic behaviours related to ADHD [8]. In addition, the implementation of medication management should not be solely based on parents’ reports of their children’s behaviours [9].

To address the aforementioned weaknesses, multiple ADHD assessment instruments have been developed. Currently, the Continuous Performance Tests (CPTs) are regarded as one of the most commonly used performance-based measures for evaluating the symptoms of ADHD, such as sustained attention and impulsivity [10]. During CPTs, children are asked to observe certain stimuli and press the spacebar on a keyboard as fast as possible after any letter is displayed, except for the letter X, to which they are instructed not to respond. The outcome measures examined are errors of omission (failing to respond to a target), errors of commission (responding to a nontarget, i.e., X), and response time. CPT indices such as the Omission, Hit Reaction Time (HRT), HRT Standard Deviation (HRT SD), Variability, and HRT Block Change are used as indicators of inattentiveness [11, 12]. Commission and Perseveration rates are used as indicators of impulsivity, and the Hit Reaction Time Inter-Stimulus Intervals Change (HRT ISI Change) is used as an indicator of vigilance. Despite the success of CPTs, some researchers have questioned their utility as a differential diagnosis mechanism for ADHD [13].

CPT performance is linked to individual characteristics. According to a meta-analytical review, sex is as a major moderating factor in the assessment of core ADHD symptoms by using CPTs [14]. Another meta-analysis indicated that the intelligence quotient (IQ) of paediatric populations with ADHD [15] and adult populations with ADHD [16] is lower than that of their healthy counterparts. Moreover, CPT performance improves with age, particularly between the ages of 6 and 12 years [17]. Notably, few studies have examined whether specific CPT indices are more valid than others in differentiating between school-aged children with and non-ADHD in terms of individual sex, chronological age, and cognitive abilities. Therefore, to examine the relationship between CPT performance and ADHD diagnosis in school-aged children, the variables of sex, chronological age, and cognitive abilities should be controlled for.

Despite the aforementioned concerns, CPTs have been successfully used to provide objective ADHD assessments supporting clinical diagnoses. In this study, we examined the sensitivity of each CPT index to differentiate between children with ADHD and those with non-ADHD in general education classrooms. After sex, chronological age, and cognitive abilities were controlled for, the CPT indices that differentiated between children with ADHD and those with non-ADHD were measured and summarised to evaluate the precision of CPTs in diagnosing ADHD in school-aged children in general education classrooms in elementary schools.

Methods

Participants

A total of 30 students with ADHD and 30 with non-ADHD were enrolled from general education classrooms. Participants with non-ADHD were recruited using social media platforms, whereas those with ADHD were recruited from the paediatric department of a medical centre. Four students with ADHD were excluded from the study because their cognitive abilities were insufficient (IQ < 80) for them to understand how to respond to a CPT. All students were school-aged children (6–12 years) from central Taiwan. Students with ADHD had received their diagnoses from child psychiatrists in accordance with the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) criteria, and they were excluded if they had received a diagnosis of other neurological (e.g., epilepsy) or neurodevelopmental (e.g., autism spectrum disorder) disorders. Similarly, students with non-ADHD were excluded if they had received a diagnosis of any neurological or neurodevelopmental disorder.

Measures

Assessment of cognitive ability

In this study, the Mandarin Chinese version of the Peabody Picture Vocabulary Test-Revised (PPVT-R) [18] was used to evaluate the cognitive abilities of the students. Generally, the Mandarin Chinese version of the PPVT-R is an individually administered, norm-referenced test of cognitive abilities [19]. It is designed for children aged 3–12 who have vision and hearing in the normal range. Each test item comprises four simple black-and-white illustrations. After the examiner says a word, the examinee is tasked with selecting the corresponding image that accurately represents the meaning of that word.

Assessment of ADHD symptoms

In this study, the Mandarin Chinese version of the Swanson, Nolan, and Pelham Teacher and Parent Rating Scale Version IV (SNAP-IV) [20] was used to evaluate the severity of ADHD symptoms and their severity. The SNAP-IV is a 26-item questionnaire that is completed by both parents and teachers. These 26 items are divided into 18 items evaluating the symptoms of ADHD (9 for attention deficit (AD) symptoms and 9 for hyperactivity deficit (HD) symptoms) and 8 items evaluating the symptoms of oppositional defiant disorder, as defined in the DSM-5. Each item is scored on a 4-point Likert scale with endpoints ranging from 0 (not at all) to 3 (very much). The SNAP-IV comprises inattention, hyperactivity or impulsivity, and oppositional subscales. Satisfactory levels of reliability and concurrent validity have been demonstrated for its Mandarin Chinese version [20].

CPTs

CPTs are neuropsychological tests conducted to evaluate the level of sustained attention. A common characteristic of all CPTs is that these tests involve the sequential presentation of stimuli, letters, or objects over an extended period. CPTs require the participants to pay attention and correctly respond to target stimuli while simultaneously ignoring stimuli that serve as nontarget distractors [21, 22]. The Conners Kiddie Continuous Performance Test Second Edition (K-CPT 2) evaluates sustained attention in children aged 4 to 7 years [12]. In this test, children are presented with a series of easily recognisable pictures (e.g., a house, soccer ball, and boat) on a computer screen. They are asked to press the spacebar on the keyboard every time a picture other than the soccer ball appears. The K-CPT 2 is set to run for 7.5 min, and it consists of five blocks, each containing 30 target and 10 nontarget stimuli as well as a 20-trial subblock with 1.5-s inter-stimulus intervals (ISIs) and a 20-trial subblock with 3-s ISIs, for a total of 200 trials. The stimulus presentation time is 500 ms. The Conners Continuous Performance Test Third Edition (CPT-3) evaluates sustained attention in children aged 8 years and above [11]. It consists of six blocks, each divided into three 20-trial subblocks. A total of 360 letters are presented one at a time for approximately 250 ms, with ISIs of 1, 2, or 4 s, which are counterbalanced and vary depending on the block. Participants are allowed to complete the CPT-3 in 14 min, including a brief trial to introduce the steps.

Regarding the indices of CPTs, the Response Style Index is a signal detection statistic that measures an individual’s natural response style in tasks involving a speed-versus-accuracy trade-off. Sustained attention is associated with multiple dependent measures, such as Detectability (d′), Omissions, Commissions, Commissions, Response Time (i.e., Hit Reaction Time (HRT), Hit Reaction Time Block Change (HRT Block Change), and Hit Reaction Time Inter-Stimulus Intervals Change (HRT ISI Change)), and Variability of Standard Error (i.e., Hit Reaction Time Standard Deviation (HRT SD) and Variability). Table 1 presents the details of each CPT index. In this study, the CPT performance indices were transformed into T-scores (mean ± SD = 50 ± 10, range = 20–80) and classified in accordance with the recommended guidelines. The classification of HRT was scored as 1 = atypically fast (T-scores < 40), 2 = a little fast (T-scores = 40–44), 3 = average (T-scores = 45–54), 4 = a little slow (T-scores = 55–59), 5 = slow (T-scores = 60–69), and 6 = atypically slow (T-scores ≥ 70). The classification of the other indices was scored as 1 = low (T-scores < 45), 2 = average (T-scores = 45–54), 3 = high average (T-scores = 55–59), 4 = elevated (T-scores = 60–69), and 5 = highly elevated (T-scores ≥ 70). The classification scores of HRT were dichotomised into 1 = elevated (i.e., classification scores = 1, 2, 5, and 6) and 0 = none (i.e., classification scores = 3 and 4). Similarly, the classification scores of the other indices were dichotomised into 1 = positive (i.e., classification scores = 4 and 5) and 0 = none (i.e., classification scores = 1, 2, and 3).

Procedure

After the parents of the students were informed of the research procedures, their informed consent was obtained. All parents completed the SNAP-IV and provided their demographic information. Before undergoing any CPTs, all participants completed the Mandarin Chinese version of the PPVT-R. Those administering the tests were blinded to which children had ADHD. Because high levels of attention and activity control are not necessary during this period, methylphenidate, as a medication for ADHD, was paused for a minimum of 2 weeks before the tests were administered. All tests were administered in a quiet room for a duration of 1 h. On the same day, the remaining assessments were conducted, with a total testing duration of approximately 2–3 h.

Statistical analysis

Continuous demographic variables were evaluated using t tests, and categorical demographic variables were compared using the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test. The sensitivity of each index was examined as an appropriate indicator of ADHD. T-scores and classification scores were evaluated using t tests to determine group differences. Effect sizes were calculated using partial eta squared (ηp2) and were directly translated into the percentage of variance explained. A larger effect size indicated the requirement of a smaller sample size, whereas a smaller effect size indicated the requirement of a larger sample size [23]. Cohen [24] provided a basic framework for interpreting these effects as small (ηp2 = 0.01), moderate (ηp2 = 0.06), or large (ηp2 = 0.15).

Logistic regression analysis was conducted to determine whether the ADHD group was more likely than the non-ADHD group to exhibit elevated CPT performance after sex, chronological age, and cognitive abilities were controlled for. Logistic regression analysis was also conducted to determine whether the total elevation in CPT indices increased the accuracy of CPT classification. A P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Because of the small sample size in this study, P values less than or equal to 0.10 were considered to indicate trends. Post hoc statistical power (SP) was calculated to determine the significance of the results of logistic regression analysis involving a sufficient number of participants. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics for Windows (version 25.0; IBM, Armonk, NY, USA), and all SP calculations were performed using G*Power (USA).

Results

All participations were age-matched (P = 0.496) and gender-matched (P = 0.176) between the ADHD (20 boys and 6 girls) and non-ADHD (18 boys and 12 girls) groups. The two groups were also matched for cognitive abilities (P = 0.139). In the SNAP-IV, statistically significant subscale scores were observed for AD (P < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.70), HD (P < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.65), and total ADHD (P < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.73) between the ADHD and non-ADHD groups. No significant differences were observed in response style (impulsive versus cautious) between the ADHD and non-ADHD groups (P = 0.472, Table 2).

In terms of T-scores, the scores for Detectability (P < 0.01, ηp2 = 0.12), Omissions (P < 0.01, ηp2 = 0.16), Commissions (P < 0.05, ηp2 = 0.08), and HRT SD (P < 0.05, ηp2 = 0.07) were higher in the ADHD group than in the non-ADHD group (Fig. 1). As shown in Table 3, the classification scores for Detectability (P < 0.05, ηp2 = 0.10), Omissions (P < 0.01, ηp2 = 0.15), Perseverations (P < 0.05, ηp2 = 0.11), and HRT SD (P < 0.05, ηp2 = 0.09) were higher in the ADHD group than in the non-ADHD group. In the non-ADHD group, HRT ISI Change (33.3%), HRT (50%), HRT SD (16.7%), and variability (13.3%) were associated with the most positive outcomes. By contrast, in the ADHD group, HRT (56.3%), HRT ISI Change (50%), HRT SD (38.5%), and Omission (30.7%) were associated with the most positive outcomes. The ADHD group exhibited higher percentages for HRT, HRT ISI, and HRT SD than the non-ADHD group. Among all indices, Omissions (P < 0.05) and HRT Block Change (P < 0.05) significantly differed between the two groups.

Simple logistic regression analysis was conducted to evaluate the indices of CPTs. The results indicated that participants with positive scores for Omission were more likely to receive a diagnosis of ADHD than those without (odds ratio [OR] = 6.222, 95% confidence interval [CI] = [1.185, 32.684], P < 0.05, SP = 0.76). With sex, chronological age, and cognitive abilities controlled for (Table 4), logistic regression revealed that participants with positive scores for Detectability (adjusted OR = 4.627, 95%CI = [0.802, 26.679], P < 0.10, SP = 0.79), Omissions (adjusted OR = 9.977, 95% CI = [1.715, 84.943], P < 0.05, SP = 0.99), and HRT SD (adjusted OR = 3.908, 95% CI = [1.024, 14.922], P < 0.05, SP = 0.70) were more likely to receive a diagnosis of ADHD than those without. In summary, compared with other CPT indices, Detectability, Omissions, and HRT SD may serve as more suitable indicators for diagnosing ADHD, because they are not obscured by individual characteristics.

The indices of Detectability, Omissions, and HRT SD for ADHD diagnosis were combined. Multiple logistic regression revealed that with sex, chronological age, and cognitive abilities controlled for, the cumulative scores of these three CPT indices remained associated with an increased risk of ADHD (adjusted OR = 3.116, 95% CI = [1.368, 7.098], P < 0.01, SP = 0.64).

Discussion

In this study, we examined the sensitivity of CPT indices for differentiating between children with ADHD and with non-ADHD in general education classrooms in elementary schools, with sex, chronological age, and cognitive abilities controlled for. We also used the cumulative scores of these CPT indices, which differentiate between children with ADHD and those with non-ADHD, to determine the accuracy of CPTs in diagnosing ADHD. The results revealed that school-aged children with ADHD exhibited lower scores for detectability, omissions, and HRT SD than children with non-ADHD. In addition, compared with the CPT indicators of impulsivity, the indicators of inattention were more accurate in differentiating between children with and with non-ADHD in general education classrooms in elementary schools.

One of the strengths of this study is that it involved DSM-5-based diagnostic interviews. It also involved a robust matched pair design for two groups of participants depending on their grade and educational arrangement. For comparing children with ADHD and those with non-ADHD, the effects of sex and chronological age were examined. No difference in cognitive abilities or impulsive or cautious response style in CPTs involving a speed-versus-accuracy trade-off was observed between the ADHD and non-ADHD groups, thus mitigating any confounding effects between the two groups. Unlike a previous large-scale study [13], this study involved a small sample size but moderate-to-large effect sizes and SP, which enhanced the validity of our findings.

Ord et al. [13] examined the embedded validity indicators of CPTs and reported that the Detectability (d′), Omissions, Commissions, HRT, HRT SD, and HRT ISI Change significantly predicted validity status. Çak et al. [25] examined CPT measures to identify the features (e.g., inattentiveness) of children with ADHD, and they reported that omission rate, HRT SD, variability, and HRT ISI Change served as indicators of inattentiveness. In this study, we found that the inattentiveness indices of CPTs exhibited adequate variability for differentiating between school-aged children with ADHD and those with non-ADHD, thereby confirming the aforementioned results. However, the impulsivity indices of CPTs exhibited low sensitivity for differentiating between children with ADHD and those with non-ADHD. These results can be explained by two hypotheses.

The first hypothesis is that CPTs are sensitive to only certain core deficits among school-aged children with ADHD, such as inattention, but not to other deficits such as impulsivity. In a previous study on CPTs [26], the index of commission errors was reported to have limited diagnostic capacity. In this study, we assumed that this limited diagnostic capacity was attributable to the high cognitive load of our CPT tasks. In contrast to other CPTs, our tasks included nontarget distractors that may have increased the complexity of the tests for the two groups. These high cognitive demands, which increased the rates of commission errors [27], may explain why this index was not sufficiently sensitive and specific to ADHD. Further research is required to determine how different cognitive demands (e.g., with and without distractors) affect the clinical utility of this index.

The second hypothesis is that impulsivity tends to decline with age [28]. During the period from preschool age to school age, impulsive symptoms are inadequate for differentiating between children with ADHD and those with non-ADHD [29]. Given that hyperactivity and impulsivity decline over time after peaking in the aforementioned period, further research is required to identify the mediators or moderators of impulsivity trajectories among children with ADHD. Identifying these mediators or moderators can aid in the identification of children who are at risk of ADHD and can provide clues for treatment development.

Performance validity testing (PVT) refers to the methodology used by neuropsychologists to determine whether the performance on a neuropsychological test is valid over the course of an assessment [30]. This methodology involves the use of either standalone tests designed exclusively for this purpose or specific indicators embedded within traditional neuropsychological measures with established utility. In this study, we found that Detectability, Omissions, and HRT SD exhibited clinical utility as embedded validity indicators for the diagnosis of ADHD. However, these indicators should not be used alone because of their low sensitivity. In a previous study [13], individual CPT variables significantly predicting PVT performance were combined in several ways to identify the most accurate embedded validity indicators. CPT scores may be useful as a component of a multivariate, multipoint continuous approach for PVT. According to our findings, enhancing index accumulation can increase the accuracy of the differential diagnosis of ADHD.

In contrast to the results of previous studies [14–17], our findings indicated that the performance of CPTs is related to individual characteristics (i.e., cognitive abilities). Further research into the demographic and cognitive characteristics of children with ADHD is required to gain further insights into the relationship between CPT performance and ADHD diagnosis. This study involved a comparative analysis of children aged between 6 and 12. There were 26 students in the ADHD group and 30 in the non-ADHD group. The age range of 6–12 years encompasses a broad developmental span. While subdividing this age range into 6–9 years (young school age) and 10–12 years (old school age) would be more appropriate, such division may limit the applicability of available statistical methods for a small sample size. This issue merits further research.

This study has several limitations pertaining to the characteristics of children and the research design. First, our study included a small sample size, which may have limited the SP of some logistic regression analyses, potentially impeding the detection of the significant effects of CPT indices for differentiating between school-aged children with ADHD and those with non-ADHD. Second, no participant subgrouping into AD alone or HD alone groups was performed to draw correlations between the CPT indices. Generally, the following characteristics of children with ADHD should be known: the duration of their condition, whether they had received specific behavioural therapy, and whether they had any other comorbidities (e.g., anxiety or oppositional behaviours) that may affect their performance on CPTs. In addition, the number of hours that children spend playing video games daily should be known [31]. Recent studies have revealed a direct correlation between increased video gaming time and improved performance on tests similar to CPTs [32]. Third, our participants completed other assessments on the same day of their tests, but they were unable to complete the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children Fifth Edition (WISC-V, Chinese version), which would have taken a long time to complete. Therefore, we used only the Mandarin Chinese version of the PPVT-R to evaluate individual differences in cognitive abilities. This approach differs from that used in clinical practice, in which obtaining an exact cognitive ability score is necessary. Future studies should attempt to utilise the Chinese version of the WISC-V to evaluate cognitive abilities whenever sufficient time is available.

Conclusion

Neuropsychologists often use CPTs as a supplementary tool for diagnosing ADHD. In this study, compared with children with non-ADHD, those with ADHD performed significantly lower on three CPT measures related to inattention, namely detectability, omission rate, and HRT SD. To appropriately conduct CPTs, clinicians should consider these three robust indices when distinguishing between school-aged children with ADHD and those with non-ADHD in general education classrooms after account for individual characteristics that may confound the evaluation results. In addition, CPTs should be viewed as a key component of comprehensive diagnostic evaluations. Integrating behavioural observation reports from parents and teachers in various contexts is also necessary to determine whether a child meets the diagnostic criteria for ADHD. This strategy can enhance the clinical validity of ADHD evaluations.
