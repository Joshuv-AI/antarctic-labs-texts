High order well-balanced and total-energy-conserving local discontinuous Galerkin methods for compressible self-gravitating Euler equations


Abstract
. In this paper, we develop a high order structure-preserving local discontinuous Galerkin (DG) scheme for the compressible self-gravitating Euler equations, which pose great challenges due to the presence of time-dependent gravitational potential. The designed scheme is well-balanced for general polytropic equilibrium state and total energy conserving for multiple spatial dimensions without an assumption of spherical symmetry. The well-balanced property is achieved by decomposing the gravitational potential into equilibrium and perturbation parts, employing a modified Harten–Lax–van Leer–contact flux and a modification of the discretization for the source term. Conservation of total energy is particularly challenging in the presence of self-gravity, especially when aiming for high order accuracy. To address this, we rewrite the energy equation into a conservative form, and carefully design an energy flux with the aid of weak formulation from the DG method to maintain conservation as well as high order accuracy. The resulting scheme can be extended to high order in time discretizations. Numerical examples for two and three dimensional problems are provided to verify the desired properties of our proposed scheme, including shock-capturing, high order accuracy, well balance, and total energy conservation.

1.
Introduction

Numerical simulations of self-gravitating (SG) gas dynamics have become a crucial tool for exploring a wide range of astrophysical phenomena. The SG effect is particularly important in modeling phenomena such as Jeans instability
[
44
,
27
,
40
]
, Sedov explosions
[
44
,
37
,
59
,
28
]
, and star formation
[
46
]
. Specifically, the compressible SG Euler equations in the
d-
dimensional case can be written as:

(1.1a)

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\begin{bmatrix}\rho\\
\rho\mathbf{u}\\
E\\
\end{bmatrix}+\nabla\cdot\begin{bmatrix}\rho\mathbf{u}\\
\rho\mathbf{u}\otimes{\mathbf{u}}+p{\mathbf{I}}_{d}\\
(E+p){\mathbf{u}}\end{bmatrix}=-\begin{bmatrix}0\\
\rho\nabla\phi\\
\rho{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\nabla\phi\end{bmatrix},

(1.1b)

\Delta\phi=4\pi G\rho.

Here
\rho
,
{\mathbf{u}}=(u_{1},\cdots,u_{d})^{T}
,
E
,
p
,
\phi
are the fluid mass density, velocity, total non-gravitational energy, gas pressure, and gravitational potential, respectively.
{\mathbf{I}}_{d}
denotes the
d-
dimensional identity matrix. The total non-gravitational energy is given by

(1.2)

E=\rho e+\frac{1}{2}\rho{\|{\mathbf{u}}\|}^{2},

where
e=\mathcal{E}(\rho,p)
is the specific internal energy related to
\rho,p
from equation of state (EOS), and
\|\cdot\|
denotes the typical Euclidean vector norm. For the ideal gas law, the pressure
p
is given by
p=(\gamma-1)\rho e
, where
\gamma
is the specific heat ratio.
G
in equation
(
1.1b
) is the gravitational constant.

The major difference between the SG Euler equations and the Euler equations with static gravitational potential is the time-dependent gravitational source term, i.e. the gravitational potential is modeled by the Poisson equation (
1.1b
), which complicates the formulation and numerical implementation. One approach is to rewrite the Poisson equation as a first-order hyperbolic system with a relaxation time, therefore the solver designed for Euler equations can also be applied to the Poisson equation
[
44
]
. This strategy reduces the computational effort compared to solving a large linear system from the Poisson equation, particularly by improving the efficiency on adaptive meshes. Another is to directly solve the Poisson equation. Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) algorithms
[
27
]
and multipole expansions
[
15
,
41
]
are widely used. However, this approach would impose strict constraints on boundary conditions. In this paper, we construct our scheme under the framework of local discontinuous Galerkin (LDG) methods, which are specifically designed to handle partial differential equations (PDEs) involving high order derivatives and are flexible for various types of boundary conditions in complicate geometries. The LDG methods have been widely used for problems involving the Poisson equation, such as Vlasov-Poisson equations
[
17
,
18
,
62
,
21
]
, Burgers-Poisson equations
[
34
]
, and Drift-Diffusion equations
[
36
,
35
]
, etc.

The compressible SG Euler equations belong to the family of hyperbolic conservation laws with source terms. In these applications, near-equilibrium flows often encounter, and conventional schemes may fail to capture small perturbations from the hydrostatic equilibrium states due to numerical discretization errors, unless a very refined mesh is applied, which leads to significant computational cost. In such cases, well-balanced (WB) schemes become important which satisfy the following properties: preserving the steady solutions exactly up to machine error; capturing small perturbations well even on relatively coarse meshes; preserving the accuracy of the proposed schemes. For WB schemes, it is crucial to consider a balance between the flux and source terms. In recent decades, WB schemes have drawn much attention. Many early works were proposed for shallow water equations (SWEs) over a non-flat bottom topology, and we refer the reader to
[
4
,
1
,
54
,
29
,
22
,
56
,
53
]
for different kinds of WB reconstruction. These approaches have also been extended to compressible Euler equations with a static gravitational source term, within the framework of finite difference (FD) methods
[
32
,
55
]
, finite volume (FV) methods
[
28
,
5
,
47
,
23
,
43
,
3
,
10
,
49
]
, discontinuous Galerkin (DG) methods
[
31
,
52
,
20
,
30
,
60
]
, etc. A study focusing on the comparison between WB schemes and non-WB schemes can be found in
[
24
]
. The main challenge of WB schemes for the time dependent source term arises from the reformulation of gravitational potential, where the balance between pressure gradient and the source term, i.e.
-\rho\nabla\phi=\nabla p
, cannot be directly used.

In addition to the WB property, TEC is also crucial for the simulation of the SG Euler equations. Since the gravitational force is self-generated by the gas, the gravitational potential energy can be regarded as part of the total energy. For SG Euler equations under a hydrostatic equilibrium, the gravitational energy may take negative values, so that the total energy could be much smaller than the internal energy. Numerical schemes without TEC often yield nonphysical results in certain cases
[
27
]
, particularly in the context of underresolved meshes or large time-step sizes.
TEC has also been observed to play a pivotal role in plasma models, including Vlasov–Ampère equations
[
11
,
57
,
12
,
33
]
, Vlasov–Poisson equations
[
17
,
18
,
62
,
21
]
, and Vlasov–Maxwell equations
[
13
,
38
,
58
]
. In these applications, designing TEC schemes for Poisson-type equations presents greater challenges as compared to Ampère-type equations, as there is no direct evolving equation for the external potential. In particular, the compressible SG Euler equations can be interpreted as the macroscopic limit of the gravitational Vlasov–Boltzmann–Poisson equations
[
6
,
16
,
19
]
in the regime of vanishing dimensionless Knudsen numbers. For SG Euler equations, one popular technique for designing TEC schemes is based on the total energy conservation form, but their implementations are limited to second-order accuracy
[
27
,
40
]
. Another one is to apply summation-by-parts and the mass conservation equation to discretize the source term in the energy equation
[
39
]
, which would require careful modifications when extending to high order in time discretizations
[
59
]
. In this paper, we adopt the first technique and will extend it to high order accuracy.

For compressible SG Euler equations, one recent work by
[
59
]
addressed the satisfaction of both WB and TEC properties through a hydrostatic reconstruction approach applied to the flux term. Their proposed scheme was developed within the framework of DG methods, to ensure the consistency between the Euler equations and the Poisson equation. However, their scheme was constrained to the case of spherical symmetry, where the Poisson equation can be solved analytically by direct integration. In contrast, in high-dimensional spatial domains without spherical symmetry the analytical solution is not available, which makes the generalization of WB property to multiple spatial dimensions being much more complicated
[
59
]
.

The main objective of this paper is to construct a high order DG scheme for compressible SG Euler equations, which can preserve the WB and TEC properties in high dimensional spaces. Specifically, we employ the following main techniques to achieve both WB and TEC properties as well as maintaining high order accuracy:

(1)

The WB property is obtained based on the idea of hydrostatic reconstruction. We decompose the gravitational potential into equilibrium and perturbation components. Modifications are applied to the Harten-Lax-van Leer-contact (HLLC) flux and source term for the equilibrium part in order to hold the WB property. A standard treatment of the perturbation part is enough to ensure high order accuracy. Preserving the WB property would perform better in near equilibrium state.

(2)

The TEC property is realized based on the conservative form for the total energy. To address the loss of accuracy due to a time derivative appeared in the energy flux, we apply summation-by-parts to restore the desired order of accuracy. The resulting scheme can be easily generalized to high order temporal accuracy with multi-stage Runge-Kutta time discretizations.

(3)

The high order accuracy in space is obtained by the DG framework. The DG methods can be flexibly designed with high order accuracy for general geometries in high dimensions. We also borrow the weak formulation of DG methods to restore the high order accuracy in the energy flux, which is not easy for a FV/FD method
[
27
,
40
]
. Besides, we adopt the oscillation eliminating (OE) technique introduced in
[
42
]
to control numerical oscillations and we reformulate the oscillation damping procedure without destroying the WB property.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we will briefly review the compressible SG Euler equations, the polytropic steady-states for the SG Euler equations, and the total-energy-conservation form. The multi-dimensional HLLC flux, the standard LDG scheme, and the structure-preserving LDG scheme will be described in detail in Section
3
, followed by an OE technique to control numerical oscillations. The WB and TEC properties are proven in Section
4
. Extensive two- and three-dimensional numerical experiments are performed to validate accuracy, robustness, shock capturing ability, WB property, and TEC property in Section
5
. A brief conclusion will be drawn in the last section.

2.
Mathmatical Model

In this section, we introduce the compressible SG Euler equations, along with a discussion of their steady-state solutions and the TEC property. For the sake of easy presentation, we introduce the following notations:

(2.1)

{\mathbf{U}}=\begin{bmatrix}\rho\\
\rho\mathbf{u}\\
E\\
\end{bmatrix},{\mathbf{F}}({\mathbf{U}})=\begin{bmatrix}\rho\mathbf{u}\\
\rho\mathbf{u}\otimes{\mathbf{u}}+p{\mathbf{I}}_{d}\\
(E+p){\mathbf{u}}\end{bmatrix},{\mathbf{S}}({\mathbf{U}},\nabla\phi)=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
-\rho\nabla\phi\\
-\rho{\bf u}\cdot\nabla\phi\end{bmatrix},

where
{\mathbf{F}}
and
{\mathbf{S}}
denote the flux term and source term respectively. Then the equations (
1.1a
) and (
1.1b
) can be rewritten as follows:

(2.2)

{\mathbf{U}}_{t}+\nabla\cdot{\mathbf{F}}({\mathbf{U}})={\mathbf{S}}({\mathbf{U}},\nabla\phi),\quad\Delta\phi=4\pi G\rho.

2.1.
Polytropic steady-states and Lane-Emden equation

The compressible SG Euler equations admit the hydrostatic stationary solution. The pressure gradient force is balanced by the gravitational force:

(2.3)

\rho=\rho({\mathbf{x}}),\;{\mathbf{u}}=0,\;\nabla p=-\rho\nabla\phi,

where
{\mathbf{x}}=(x,y,z)^{T}
denotes the spatial coordinates.
Moreover, an additional Poisson equation (
1.1b
) has to be satisfied. A general polytropic hydrostatic equilibrium is considered in such self-gravity model and some astrophysical applications, which is characterized by:

(2.4)

p=\kappa\rho^{\gamma},

where
\kappa
is a positive constant. By combining equations (
1.1b
),(
2.3
), and (
2.4
), we obtain

(2.5)

\nabla\cdot\left(\kappa\gamma\rho^{\gamma-2}\nabla\rho\right)=-4\pi G\rho.

We introduce the following transformations:

(2.6)

\rho=\lambda\theta^{n},\quad\gamma=\dfrac{n+1}{n},

where
\lambda
and
n
are both positive constants, and
\theta
is a function of
{\mathbf{x}}
. When
n\neq 0,1
, equation (
2.5
) becomes nonlinear and does not admit a simple analytical solution. Substituting (
2.6
) into (
2.5
), it can be simplified as:

(2.7)

\dfrac{\kappa(n+1)\lambda^{\frac{1-n}{n}}}{4\pi G}\Delta\theta=-\theta^{n}.

We apply the following scaling:

a=\sqrt{\dfrac{\kappa(n+1)\lambda^{\frac{1-n}{n}}}{4\pi G}},\qquad\xi=\dfrac{x}{a},\qquad\eta=\dfrac{y}{a},\qquad\mu=\dfrac{z}{a}.

Thus the equation (
2.7
) can be rewritten in the following non-dimensional form:

(2.8)

\Delta^{\prime}\theta:=\dfrac{\partial^{2}\theta}{\partial\xi^{2}}+\dfrac{\partial^{2}\theta}{\partial\eta^{2}}+\dfrac{\partial^{2}\theta}{\partial\mu^{2}}=a^{2}\left(\dfrac{\partial^{2}\theta}{\partial x^{2}}+\dfrac{\partial^{2}\theta}{\partial y^{2}}+\dfrac{\partial^{2}\theta}{\partial z^{2}}\right)=a^{2}\Delta\theta=-\theta^{n}.

In the spherically symmetric case, we introduce the radial coordinate
r=\sqrt{\xi^{2}+\eta^{2}+\mu^{2}}
, and the equation (
2.8
) reduces to the classical Lane-Emden equation
[
37
]
:

(2.9)

\dfrac{1}{r^{2}}\dfrac{\partial{}}{\partial{r}}\left(r^{2}\dfrac{\partial{\theta}}{\partial{r}}\right)=-\theta^{n}.

The Lane-Emden equation can be analytically solved
[
37
]
only for a few special integer values of the index
n
,

(2.10)

\begin{cases}\theta_{0}(r)=1-\dfrac{1}{6}r^{2},&n=0,i.e.\,\gamma=\infty,\\[8.0pt]
\theta_{1}(r)=\dfrac{\sin(r)}{r},&n=1,i.e.\,\gamma=2,\\[8.0pt]
\theta_{5}(r)=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{3}r^{2}}},&n=5,i.e.\,\gamma=\dfrac{6}{5}.\end{cases}

However, the polytropic hydrostatic equilibrium in two dimensional radial symmetry case is quite different. To avoid introducing too many new symbols, we reuse the notation
r=\sqrt{\xi^{2}+\eta^{2}}
, then we can obtain

(2.11)

\dfrac{1}{r}\dfrac{\partial{}}{\partial{r}}\left(r\dfrac{\partial{\theta}}{\partial{r}}\right)=\dfrac{\partial^{2}{\theta}}{\partial{r}^{2}}+\dfrac{1}{r}\dfrac{\partial{\theta}}{\partial{r}}=-\theta^{n}.

Actually, when
n=1
, the equation
(
2.11
)
is the
0th
Bessel ordinary equation. It has no explicit solution but a integral form:

\theta(r)=\dfrac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\cos(r\sin\alpha){\text{d}}{\alpha}.

In our numerical tests, it can be computed by a high order quadrature rule.

We use the superscript “
e
” to denote functions corresponding to the steady state. As in the case of the compressible Euler equations with a static gravitational potential, the equilibrium state is described by the density
\rho^{e}
, velocity
{\mathbf{u}}^{e}
, and pressure
p^{e}
. In contrast, the equilibrium state of the SG Euler equations must additionally involve the gravitational potential
\phi^{e}({\mathbf{x}})
. For the solution
\theta({\mathbf{x}})
of (
2.7
), the equilibrium state has following analytic form:

(2.12)

\rho^{e}=\lambda\left(\theta({\mathbf{x}})\right)^{n},\;{\mathbf{u}}^{e}=0,\;p^{e}=\kappa(\rho^{e})^{\gamma},\;\phi^{e}=-\dfrac{\kappa\gamma}{\gamma-1}(\rho^{e})^{\gamma-1}.

2.2.
Total-energy-conserving Property

The total energy
E_{tot}
is defined as the sum of the total non-gravitational energy
E
and the gravitational energy
\frac{1}{2}\rho\phi
. Thus we can rewrite the energy equation of (
1.1a
) in a conservative form:

(2.13)

\dfrac{\partial{}}{\partial{t}}\left(E+\dfrac{1}{2}\rho\phi\right)+\nabla\cdot\left((E+p){\mathbf{u}}+{\mathbf{F}}_{g}\right)=0,

with the energy flux
{\mathbf{F}}_{g}
:

(2.14)

\mathbf{F}_{g}=\dfrac{1}{8\pi G}\left(\phi\nabla\left(\dfrac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}\right)-\dfrac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}\nabla\phi\right)+\rho{\mathbf{u}}\phi.

The energy conservative form (
2.13
) can be obtained by several simple steps:

(2.15)

\begin{split}&\dfrac{\partial{}}{\partial{t}}\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\rho\phi\right)+\nabla\cdot{\mathbf{F}}_{g}-\rho{\mathbf{u}}\nabla\phi\\
=&\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{\partial{\rho}}{\partial{t}}\phi+\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{\partial{\phi}}{\partial{t}}\rho+\dfrac{1}{8\pi G}\left(\phi\Delta\left(\dfrac{\partial{\phi}}{\partial{t}}\right)-\dfrac{\partial{\phi}}{\partial{t}}\Delta\phi\right)+\nabla\cdot(\rho{\mathbf{u}})\phi\\
=&\dfrac{1}{8\pi G}\left(\phi\Delta\left(\dfrac{\partial{\phi}}{\partial{t}}\right)+\dfrac{\partial{\phi}}{\partial{t}}\Delta\phi\right)+\dfrac{1}{8\pi G}\left(\phi\Delta\left(\dfrac{\partial{\phi}}{\partial{t}}\right)-\dfrac{\partial{\phi}}{\partial{t}}\Delta\phi\right)-\dfrac{1}{4\pi G}\Delta\left(\dfrac{\partial{\phi}}{\partial{t}}\right)\phi\\
=&0.\end{split}

By adding the energy equation of (
1.1a
) and (
2.15
) we can get the conservative form (
2.13
).
Notice that there exists the time derivative term
\partial\phi/\partial t
in
{\mathbf{F}}_{g}
. Inspired by
[
27
]
, we introduce
\dot{\phi}
to take place of
\partial\phi/\partial t
, which satisfy another Poisson equation:

(2.16)

\Delta(\dot{\phi})=\Delta\left(\dfrac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}\right)=4\pi G\dfrac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}=-4\pi G\nabla\cdot(\rho{\mathbf{u}}).

Therefore, the gravity flux can be written into a compact form:

(2.17)

\mathbf{F}_{g}=\dfrac{1}{8\pi G}\left(\phi\nabla\dot{\phi}-\dot{\phi}\nabla\phi\right)+\rho{\mathbf{u}}\phi.

When periodic boundary condition or compact support boundary condition is applied on
\Omega
, the total energy conservation is derived:

(2.18)

\dfrac{{\text{d}}}{{\text{d}}{t}}\int_{\Omega}\left(E+\dfrac{1}{2}\rho\phi\right){\text{d}}{{\mathbf{x}}}=0.

3.
Numerical schemes

In this section, we will present the high order, WB and TEC LDG scheme for the compressible SG Euler equations. The standard LDG scheme will also be presented.

3.1.
Notations

We first introduce following notations for LDG methods. We assume that the computation domain
\Omega\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
d
denotes the space dimension, is partitioned into mesh
{\mathcal{T}}_{h}=\{K\}
with its boundary
\Gamma_{h}
. The mesh is regular, i.e. there exists a positive constant
\sigma
satisfying that

(3.1)

\max_{K\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}\dfrac{h_{K}}{\rho_{K}}\leq\sigma,

h_{K}=\text{diam}(K),\quad\rho_{K}=\max\{\text{diam}(S):S\text{ is a ball contained in }K\},

where
\text{diam}(K)=\sup\{||{\mathbf{y}}_{1}-{\mathbf{y}}_{2}||,\forall{\mathbf{y}}_{1},{\mathbf{y}}_{2}\in K\}
.

We define several discontinuous finite element space of
V_{h}^{k},\Sigma_{h}^{k}
as:

(3.2a)

V_{h}^{k}=\{v\in L^{2}(\Omega):\,v|_{K}\in\mathbb{P}^{k}(K),\;\forall K\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}\},

(3.2b)

\Sigma_{h}^{k}=\{{\bf v}\in(L^{2}(\Omega))^{d}:\,{\bf v}|_{K}\in(\mathbb{P}^{k}(K))^{d},\;\forall K\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}\},

where
\mathbb{P}^{k}(K)
can be either the space of polynomials of total degree up to
k
, i.e.
P^{k}
, or the space of polynomials of degree in each component up to
k
, i.e.
Q^{k}
. We denote by
{\mathcal{E}}_{I}
the set of all interior faces of the triangulation
{\mathcal{T}}_{h}
, while
{\mathcal{E}}_{B}
the set of the boundary of the triangulation
{\mathcal{T}}_{h}
. Let
K^{+}
and
K^{-}
be two adjacent elements of
{\mathcal{T}}_{h}
and
{\mathbf{x}}
is an arbitrary point on the
(d-1)
dimensional face of
{\mathcal{E}}=\partial K^{+}\cap\partial K^{-}\in{\mathcal{E}}_{I}
. The vector
{\mathbf{n}}^{+}
and
{\mathbf{n}}^{-}
are the corresponding outward unit normals for the points on the face
{\mathcal{E}}
. We also define the average
\{\!\!\{\cdot\}\!\!\}
and jump
[\![\cdot]\!]
of a function
u\in V_{h}^{k}
and
{\mathbf{q}}\in\Sigma_{h}^{k}
at
{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathcal{E}}_{I}
as follows:

\begin{split}\{\!\!\{u\}\!\!\}=\dfrac{1}{2}(u^{+}+u^{-}),&\qquad[\![u]\!]=u^{+}{\mathbf{n}}^{+}+u^{-}{\mathbf{n}}^{-},\\
\{\!\!\{{\mathbf{q}}\}\!\!\}=\dfrac{1}{2}({\mathbf{q}}^{+}+{\mathbf{q}}^{-}),&\qquad[\![{\mathbf{q}}]\!]={\mathbf{q}}^{+}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}^{+}+{\mathbf{q}}^{-}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}^{-}.\end{split}

Moreover, we define the operator
{\mathbf{P}}
to represent the
L^{2}
projection from
L^{2}(\Omega)
onto
V_{h}^{k}
, i.e. for a function
u\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, then
{\mathbf{P}}u\in V_{h}^{k}
. It is defined by:

(3.3)

\int_{K}u\,v{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{x}}}=\int_{K}{\mathbf{P}}u\,v{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{x}}},\qquad\forall v\in V_{h}^{k},\forall K\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}.

3.2.
The multi-dimensional HLLC flux

The HLLC flux is based on the approximate Riemann solver and consider the contact wave compared to the Harten–Lax–van Leer (HLL) flux. The HLLC flux is defined by:

(3.4)

{\mathbf{F}}^{\text{hllc}}({\mathbf{U}}_{L},{\mathbf{U}}_{R};{\mathbf{n}})=\begin{cases}{\mathbf{F}}({\mathbf{U}}_{L}),&\text{ if }0\leq S_{L},\\
{\mathbf{F}}_{*L},&\text{ if }S_{L}\leq 0\leq S_{*},\\
{\mathbf{F}}_{*R},&\text{ if }S_{*}\leq 0\leq S_{R},\\
{\mathbf{F}}({\mathbf{U}}_{R}),&\textbf{ if }0\geq S_{R},\end{cases}

where
S_{L},S_{R},S_{*}
are respectively the estimated left signal velocity, right signal velocity, and middle wave speed. The unit vector
{\mathbf{n}}
denotes the direction of Riemann problem. The state velocity
\widehat{u}_{L},\widehat{u}_{R}
along the direction
{\mathbf{n}}
is defined by
\widehat{u}={\mathbf{u}}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}
. With sound speed
c=\sqrt{\gamma p/\rho}
, they can be computed by:

(3.5)

S_{L}=\min\{\widehat{u}_{L}-c_{L},\widehat{u}_{R}-c_{R}\},\quad S_{R}=\max\{\widehat{u}_{L}+c_{L},\widehat{u}_{R}+c_{R}\},

S_{*}=\dfrac{p_{R}-p_{L}+\rho_{L}\widehat{u}_{L}(S_{L}-\widehat{u}_{L})-\rho_{R}\widehat{u}_{R}(S_{R}-\widehat{u}_{R})}{\rho_{L}(S_{L}-\widehat{u}_{L})-\rho_{R}(S_{R}-\widehat{u}_{R})}.

The fluxes
{\mathbf{F}}_{*L}
and
{\mathbf{F}}_{*R}
are given by:

(3.6)

{\mathbf{F}}_{*i}={\mathbf{F}}_{i}+S_{i}({\mathbf{U}}_{*i}-{\mathbf{U}}_{i}),\quad i=L,R,

{\mathbf{U}}_{*i}=\rho_{i}\left(\dfrac{S_{i}-\widehat{u}_{i}}{S_{i}-S_{*}}\right)\begin{pmatrix}1\\
{\mathbf{u}}_{i}\\
\frac{E_{i}}{\rho_{i}}+(S_{*}-\widehat{u}_{i})(S_{*}+\frac{p_{i}}{\rho_{i}(S_{i}-\widehat{u}_{i})})\end{pmatrix}.

Compared to the HLL flux, the HLLC flux considers the effect of contact and possesses the so-called contact property as outlined below:

Lemma 3.1
.

For any two states
{\mathbf{U}}_{L}=(\rho_{L},\mathbf{0},p/(\gamma-1))^{T}
and
{\mathbf{U}}_{R}=(\rho_{R},\mathbf{0},p/(\gamma-1))^{T}
, the multidimensional HLLC flux satisfies:

(3.7)

{\mathbf{F}}^{{\rm hllc}}({\mathbf{U}}_{L},{\mathbf{U}}_{R};{\mathbf{n}})=(0,p{\mathbf{n}}^{T},0)^{T}.

The proof of Lemma
3.1
is straightforward and is omitted here.
For more details of HLLC flux, we refer the readers to
[
2
,
48
]
.
The above contact property is very important in obtaining WB property.

To clearly demonstrate the importance of structure-preserving in the numerical section, we will describe two types of numerical schemes:

(1)

The standard scheme
: a baseline scheme that does not account for the WB and TEC properties;

(2)

The structure-preserving scheme
: our carefully designed scheme that enjoys both WB and TEC properties.

3.3.
The standard LDG scheme

We begin by briefly introducing the standard LDG scheme for SG Euler equations without consideration of WB or TEC properties. Following the idea of LDG scheme, we add the auxiliary term
{\mathbf{g}}=\nabla\phi
, representing the gravitational force. The compressible SG Euler equations (
1.1a
)-(
1.1b
) can be written as follows:

(3.8)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &{\mathbf{U}}_{t}+\nabla\cdot{\mathbf{F}}({\mathbf{U}})={\mathbf{S}}({\mathbf{U}},{\mathbf{g}}),\\
&{\mathbf{g}}=\nabla\phi,\\
&\nabla\cdot{\mathbf{g}}=4\pi G\rho.\end{aligned}\right.

The standard semi-discrete LDG scheme for (
3.8
) is given as follows: Find
{\mathbf{U}}_{h}\in[V_{h}^{k}]^{d+2}
,
\phi\in V_{h}^{k}
,
{\mathbf{g}}\in\Sigma_{h}^{k}
, such that for any test function
v\in V_{h}^{k}
,
{\mathbf{w}}\in\Sigma_{h}^{k}
,
\psi\in V_{h}^{k}
, the following holds:

(3.9a)

\displaystyle\int_{K}({\mathbf{U}}_{h})_{t}v{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{x}}}

of

(3.10b)

Remark 3.2
.

Following a similar approach in
[
8
,
7
]
, the stiffness matrix arising from the LDG methods exhibits the following block structure:

(3.12)

\begin{bmatrix}K&B\\
-B^{T}&S\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}{\mathbf{g}}_{h}\\
\phi_{h}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}g\\
f\end{bmatrix}.

Directly solving the linear system (
3.12
) is challenging due to the nonsymmetric saddle-point structure. A more practical approach is to apply the Schur complement, reducing the problem to solving a smaller linear system:

(3.13)

A\phi_{h}=(S+B^{T}K^{-1}B)\phi_{h}=f+B^{T}K^{-1}{\mathbf{g}}_{h}.

In that case, the stiffness matrix
A
in (
3.13
) is symmetric and positive definite and a suitable Krylov subspace method can be applied, such as Conjugate Gradient method (CG) and preconditioned Conjugate Gradient method (PCG). Since the sparse matrix remains unchanged at each time step, we apply the direct solver, i.e., Cholesky factorization at the beginning and reuse it throughout the computations. However, for the periodic boundary condition, the stiffness matrix exhibits another block structure:

\begin{bmatrix}K&B\\
-C^{T}&S\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}{\mathbf{g}}_{h}\\
\phi_{h}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}g\\
f\end{bmatrix}.

Similarly, we solve the smaller linear system:

(3.14)

A\phi_{h}=(S+C^{T}K^{-1}B)\phi_{h}=f+C^{T}K^{-1}{\mathbf{g}}_{h}.

Therefore, the stiffness matrix
A
in (
3.14
) will not be symmetric any more. We adopt the LU decomposition here.

3.4.
The structure-preserving LDG scheme

However, the scheme (
3.9
) does not inherently satisfy the WB or TEC properties. In this section, we introduce our structure-preserving LDG scheme. To obtain WB property, we split the source term into equilibrium state and perturbation state. To obtain the TEC property, we rewrite the energy equation in SG Euler equations (
1.1
) in the conservative form (
2.13
). Specifically, the SG Euler equations can be rewritten as:

(3.15a)

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial{\rho}}{\partial{t}}+\nabla\cdot(\rho{\mathbf{u}})=0,

(3.15b)

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial{\rho{\mathbf{u}}}}{\partial{t}}+\nabla\cdot(\rho{\mathbf{u}}\otimes{\mathbf{u}}+p\mathbf{I})=-\rho{\mathbf{g}}^{e}-\rho{\mathbf{g}}^{\delta},

(3.15c)

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(E_{tot}\right)+\nabla\cdot\left((E+p){\mathbf{u}}+{\mathbf{F}}_{g}\right)=0,

where the gravitational force
{\mathbf{g}}
is decomposed into an equilibrium
{\mathbf{g}}^{e}
and another perturbation
{\mathbf{g}}^{\delta}
part, which satisfy

(3.15d)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{g}}^{e}=\nabla\phi^{e},\;\nabla\cdot{\mathbf{g}}^{e}=4\pi G\rho^{e},

(3.15e)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{g}}^{\delta}=\nabla\phi^{\delta},\;\nabla\cdot{\mathbf{g}}^{\delta}=4\pi G\rho^{\delta},

where the gravitational potential
\phi
and the density
\rho
are correspondingly decomposed as

(3.15f)

\phi=\phi^{e}+\phi^{\delta},\;\rho=\rho^{e}+\rho^{\delta}.

Besides, the total energy
E_{tot}
and energy flux
{\mathbf{F}}_{g}
in (
3.15c
) are defined as:

(3.15g)

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}_{g}=\dfrac{1}{8\pi G}\left(\phi\dot{{\mathbf{g}}}-\dot{\phi}{\mathbf{g}}\right)+\rho{\mathbf{u}}\phi,

(3.15h)

\displaystyle E_{tot}=E+\dfrac{1}{2}\rho\phi,

where
\dot{\phi}:=\partial\phi/\partial t
satisfy another Poisson equation (
2.16
):

(3.15i)

\dot{{\mathbf{g}}}=\nabla\dot{\phi},\;\nabla\cdot\dot{{\mathbf{g}}}=-4\pi G\nabla\cdot(\rho{\mathbf{u}}).

We use the following notations for ease of presentation:

(3.16)

{\mathbf{U}}=\begin{bmatrix}\rho\\
\rho\mathbf{u}\\
E\\
\end{bmatrix},{\mathbf{W}}=\begin{bmatrix}\rho\\
\rho\mathbf{u}\\
E_{tot}\\
\end{bmatrix},{\mathbf{F}}({\mathbf{W}})=\begin{bmatrix}\rho\mathbf{u}\\
\rho\mathbf{u}\otimes{\mathbf{u}}+p{\mathbf{I}}\\
(E+p){\mathbf{u}}\end{bmatrix},{\mathbf{G}}({\mathbf{W}})=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}\\
{\mathbf{F}}_{g}\end{bmatrix},{\mathbf{S}}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
-\rho\nabla\phi\\
0\end{bmatrix}.

The semi-discrete structure-preserving LDG scheme for (
3.15
) can be written as follows: Find
{\mathbf{W}}_{h}\in[V_{h}^{k}]^{d+2}
,
\phi_{h}^{e}\in V_{h}^{k}
,
\phi_{h}^{\delta}\in V_{h}^{k}
,
\dot{\phi_{h}}\in V_{h}^{k}
,
{\mathbf{g}}_{h}^{e}\in\Sigma_{h}^{k}
,
{\mathbf{g}}_{h}^{\delta}\in\Sigma_{h}^{k}
, and
\dot{{\mathbf{g}}_{h}}\in\Sigma_{h}^{k}
, such that for any test function
v\in V_{h}^{k}
,
{\mathbf{w}}\in\Sigma_{h}^{k}
,
\psi\in V_{h}^{k}
, the following holds:

(3.17a)

\begin{split}\int_{K}({\mathbf{W}}_{h})_{t}v{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{x}}}&-\int_{K}{\mathbf{F}}({\mathbf{W}}_{h})\cdot\nabla v{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{x}}}+\sum_{{\mathcal{E}}\in\partial K}\int_{{\mathcal{E}}}\widehat{{\mathbf{F}}}_{{\mathbf{n}}_{{\mathcal{E}},K}}v^{\text{int}(K)}{\text{d}}{s}\\
&-\int_{K}{\mathbf{G}}({\mathbf{W}}_{h})\cdot\nabla v{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{x}}}+\sum_{{\mathcal{E}}\in\partial K}\int_{{\mathcal{E}}}\widehat{{\mathbf{G}}}_{{\mathbf{n}}_{{\mathcal{E}},K}}v^{\text{int}(K)}{\text{d}}{s}=\int_{K}{\mathbf{S}}v{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{x}}},\end{split}

where
\left({\mathbf{g}}^{e}_{h},\phi^{e}_{h}\right)
and
\left({\mathbf{g}}^{\delta}_{h},\phi^{\delta}_{h}\right)
satisfy the following Poisson equation:

(3.17b)

\begin{split}\int_{K}{\mathbf{g}}^{e}_{h}\cdot{\mathbf{w}}{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{x}}}&=\sum_{{\mathcal{E}}\in\partial K}\int_{{\mathcal{E}}}\widehat{\phi^{e}_{h}}{\mathbf{w}}^{\text{int}(K)}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}_{{\mathcal{E}},K}{\text{d}}{s}-\int_{K}\phi^{e}_{h}\nabla\cdot{\mathbf{w}}{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{x}}},\\
\int_{K}{\mathbf{g}}^{e}_{h}\cdot\nabla\psi{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{x}}}&=\sum_{{\mathcal{E}}\in\partial K}\int_{{\mathcal{E}}}\psi^{\text{int}(K)}\widehat{{\mathbf{g}}^{e}_{h}}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}_{{\mathcal{E}},K}{\text{d}}{s}-\int_{K}4\pi G\rho^{e}_{h}\psi{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{x}}},\end{split}

(3.17c)

\begin{split}\int_{K}{\mathbf{g}}^{\delta}_{h}\cdot{\mathbf{w}}{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{x}}}&=\sum_{{\mathcal{E}}\in\partial K}\int_{{\mathcal{E}}}\widehat{\phi^{\delta}_{h}}{\mathbf{w}}^{\text{int}(K)}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}_{{\mathcal{E}},K}{\text{d}}{s}-\int_{K}\phi^{\delta}_{h}\nabla\cdot{\mathbf{w}}{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{x}}},\\
\int_{K}{\mathbf{g}}^{\delta}_{h}\cdot\nabla\psi{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{x}}}&=\sum_{{\mathcal{E}}\in\partial K}\int_{{\mathcal{E}}}\psi^{\text{int}(K)}\widehat{{\mathbf{g}}^{\delta}_{h}}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}_{{\mathcal{E}},K}{\text{d}}{s}-\int_{K}4\pi G\rho^{\delta}_{h}\psi{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{x}}},\end{split}

(3.17d)

\phi_{h}=\phi_{h}^{\delta}+\phi_{h}^{e},\;{\mathbf{g}}_{h}={\mathbf{g}}_{h}^{\delta}+{\mathbf{g}}_{h}^{e},\;\rho_{h}=\rho_{h}^{\delta}+\rho_{h}^{e},

and let
\left(\dot{{\mathbf{g}}_{h}},\dot{\phi_{h}}\right)
be the solution of the following Poisson equation:

(3.17e)

\begin{split}\int_{K}\dot{{\mathbf{g}}_{h}}\cdot{\mathbf{w}}{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{x}}}&=\sum_{{\mathcal{E}}\in\partial K}\int_{{\mathcal{E}}}\widehat{\dot{\phi_{h}}}{\mathbf{w}}^{\text{int}(K)}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}_{{\mathcal{E}},K}{\text{d}}{s}-\int_{K}\dot{\phi_{h}}\nabla\cdot{\mathbf{w}}{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{x}}},\\
\int_{K}\dot{{\mathbf{g}}_{h}}\cdot\nabla\psi{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{x}}}&=\sum_{{\mathcal{E}}\in\partial K}\int_{{\mathcal{E}}}\psi^{\text{int}(K)}\widehat{\dot{{\mathbf{g}}_{h}}}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}_{{\mathcal{E}},K}{\text{d}}{s}-\int_{K}4\pi G\nabla\cdot(\rho{\mathbf{u}})_{h}\psi{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{x}}},\end{split}

where the flux
\widehat{\phi^{e}_{h}}
,
\widehat{{\mathbf{g}}^{e}_{h}}
,
\widehat{\phi^{\delta}_{h}}
,
\widehat{{\mathbf{g}}^{\delta}_{h}}
,
\widehat{\dot{\phi_{h}}}
, and
\widehat{\dot{{\mathbf{g}}_{h}}}
are the same as (
3.10
), i.e.,

(3.18a)

\displaystyle\widehat{{\mathbf{g}}^{e}_{h}}

\displaystyle=\{\!\!\{{\mathbf{g}}^{e}_{h}\}\!\!\}-C_{11}[\![\phi^{e}_{h}]\!]-{\mathbf{C}}_{12}[\![{\mathbf{g}}^{e}_{h}]\!],

(3.18b)

\displaystyle\widehat{\phi^{e}_{h}}

\displaystyle=\{\!\!\{\phi^{e}_{h}\}\!\!\}+{\mathbf{C}}_{12}\cdot[\![\phi^{e}_{h}]\!],

(3.18c)

\displaystyle\widehat{{\mathbf{g}}^{\delta}_{h}}

\displaystyle=\{\!\!\{{\mathbf{g}}^{\delta}_{h}\}\!\!\}-C_{11}[\![\phi^{\delta}_{h}]\!]-{\mathbf{C}}_{12}[\![{\mathbf{g}}^{\delta}_{h}]\!],

(3.18d)

\displaystyle\widehat{\phi^{\delta}_{h}}

\displaystyle=\{\!\!\{\phi^{\delta}_{h}\}\!\!\}+{\mathbf{C}}_{12}\cdot[\![\phi^{\delta}_{h}]\!],

(3.18e)

\displaystyle\widehat{\dot{{\mathbf{g}}_{h}}}

\displaystyle=\{\!\!\{\dot{{\mathbf{g}}_{h}}\}\!\!\}-C_{11}[\![\dot{\phi_{h}}]\!]-{\mathbf{C}}_{12}[\![\dot{{\mathbf{g}}_{h}}]\!],

(3.18f)

\displaystyle\widehat{\dot{\phi_{h}}}

\displaystyle=\{\!\!\{\dot{\phi_{h}}\}\!\!\}+{\mathbf{C}}_{12}\cdot[\![\dot{\phi_{h}}]\!].

For simplicity, let
{\mathcal{D}}_{1}
denote the discrete Poisson solver in (
3.17b
) and (
3.17c
), and let
{\mathcal{D}}_{2}
denote the discrete Poisson solver in (
3.17e
). In other words, we have:

(3.19)

\left({\mathbf{g}}^{e}_{h},\phi^{e}_{h}\right)={\mathcal{D}}_{1}\left(4\pi G\rho_{h}^{e}\right),\;\left({\mathbf{g}}^{\delta}_{h},\phi^{\delta}_{h}\right)={\mathcal{D}}_{1}\left(4\pi G\rho^{\delta}_{h}\right),\;\left(\dot{{\mathbf{g}}_{h}},\dot{\phi_{h}}\right)={\mathcal{D}}_{2}\left(4\pi G\nabla\cdot(\rho{\mathbf{u}})_{h}\right).

We note that the equilibrium state
\{\rho^{e}({\mathbf{x}}),{\mathbf{u}}^{e}({\mathbf{x}})=\mathbf{0},p^{e}({\mathbf{x}}),\phi^{e}({\mathbf{x}})\}
to be preserved is assumed to be stationary which is obtained from (
2.12
). Numerically the discrete equilibrium state variables
\rho_{h}^{e}
,
{\mathbf{u}}_{h}^{e}
and
p_{h}^{e}
are given by the projector in (
3.3
), i.e.
\rho_{h}^{e}={\mathbf{P}}\rho^{e}
,
{\mathbf{u}}_{h}^{e}={\mathbf{P}}{\mathbf{u}}^{e}=\mathbf{0}
, and
p_{h}^{e}={\mathbf{P}}p^{e}
. The discrete gravitational potential at equilibrium
\phi_{h}^{e}
is obtained from (
3.19
), namely the equilibrium state is initialized at the beginning and remain unchanged for later time. Therefore, only the perturbation part
\{\rho_{h}^{\delta},\phi_{h}^{\delta}\}
evolves over time.

For structure preserving, the WB property is obtained for the DG method with a modified HLLC flux and a revised source term approximation, which will be shown in Section
3.4.1
and Section

. The TEC property is ensured as the conservative form for the total energy

is used. However, directly solving the Poisson equation (
3.15i
) appeared in the energy flux with (

and numerically demonstrated in Section
5
.

3.4.1.
The modified HLLC numerical flux

Based on the contact property of HLLC flux, we follow the idea of the modifications in
[

:

(3.20)

\widehat{{\mathbf{F}}}_{{\mathbf{n}}_{\mathcal{E},K}}={\mathbf{F}}^{\text{hllc}}\left(\frac{p_{h}^{e,\star}}{p_{h}^{e,\text{int}(K)}}{\mathbf{U}}_{h}^{\text{int}(K)},\frac{p_{h}^{e,\star}}{p_{h}^{e,\text{ext}(K)}}{\mathbf{U}}_{h}^{\text{ext}(K)};{\mathbf{n}}_{{\mathcal{E}},K}\right)=\left(\widehat{f}^{[1]},\widehat{\bm{f}}^{[2],T},\widehat{f}^{[3]}\right)_{{\mathbf{n}}_{{\mathcal{E}},K}}^{T},

where

is chosen as

Based on the Lemma

, if

, then
\widehat{{\mathbf{F}}}_{{\mathbf{n}}_{\mathcal{E},K}}=\left(0,p_{h}^{e,*}{\mathbf{n}}^{T},0\right)^{T}
. With the

{\mathcal{E}}

\int_{{\mathcal{E}}}\widehat{{\mathbf{F}}}_{{\mathbf{n}}_{{\mathcal{E}},K}}v^{{\rm int}(K)}{\text{d}}{s}\approx\sum_{\mu=1}^{N}w_{{\mathcal{E}}}^{(\mu)}{\mathbf{F}}\left({\mathbf{U}}_{h}({\mathbf{x}}_{{\mathcal{E}}}^{(\mu)})\right)v^{{\rm int}(K)}({\mathbf{x}}_{{\mathcal{E}}}^{(\mu)}),

3.4.2.
Source term approximations

Let the source term
{\mathbf{S}}:=\left(0,{\mathbf{S}}^{[2],T},0\right)^{T}
with
{\mathbf{S}}^{[2]}=-\rho\nabla\phi^{e}-\rho\nabla\phi^{\delta}
. For the static gravitational potential, we need a balance between the flux gradient and source term. The treatment is similar to
[
52
,
20
]
. The gravitational potential at the equilibrium state
\phi^{e}
is considered as the static gravitational potential, while the perturbation part
\phi^{\delta}
is directly addressed.

(3.22)

\begin{split}\int_{K}\mathbf{S}^{[2]}v{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{x}}}&=\int_{K}-\rho\nabla\phi v{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{x}}}=\int_{K}-\rho\nabla(\phi^{e}+\phi^{\delta})v{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{x}}}=\int_{K}\rho\dfrac{\nabla p^{e}}{\rho^{e}}v{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{x}}}+\int_{K}-\rho\nabla\phi^{\delta}v{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{x}}}\\
&=\int_{K}\nabla p^{e}\left(\dfrac{\rho}{\rho^{e}}-\dfrac{\overline{\rho}}{\overline{\rho^{e}}}\right)v{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{x}}}+\int_{K}\dfrac{\overline{\rho}}{\overline{\rho^{e}}}p^{e}v{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{x}}}+\int_{K}-\rho\nabla\phi^{\delta}v{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{x}}}\\
&=\int_{K}\nabla p^{e}\left(\dfrac{\rho}{\rho^{e}}-\dfrac{\overline{\rho}}{\overline{\rho^{e}}}\right)v{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{x}}}+\dfrac{\overline{\rho}}{\overline{\rho^{e}}}\left(\sum_{{\mathcal{E}}\in\partial K}\int_{{\mathcal{E}}}p^{e}v{\mathbf{n}}_{{\mathcal{E}},K}{\text{d}}{s}-\int_{K}p^{e}\nabla v{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{x}}}\right)+\int_{K}-\rho\nabla\phi^{\delta}v{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{x}}}.\end{split}

With the
Q
points numerical quadrature in the
d
dimensional element
K
and the numerical quadrature in (
3.21
), the fully discrete version of (
3.22
) is given by:

(3.23)

\begin{split}\left<{\mathbf{S}}^{[2]},v\right>_{K}&:=\sum_{q=1}^{Q}w_{K}^{(q)}\left(\dfrac{\rho_{h}({\mathbf{x}}_{K}^{(q)})}{\rho^{e}_{h}({\mathbf{x}}_{K}^{(q)})}-\dfrac{\overline{(\rho_{h})_{K}}}{\overline{(\rho^{e}_{h})_{K}}}\right)\nabla p_{h}^{e}({\mathbf{x}}_{K}^{(q)})v({\mathbf{x}}_{K}^{(q)})\\
&\quad+\dfrac{\overline{(\rho_{h})_{K}}}{\overline{(\rho^{e}_{h})_{K}}}\left(\sum_{{\mathcal{E}}\in\partial K}\sum_{\mu=1}^{N}w_{{\mathcal{E}}}^{(\mu)}p^{e}_{h}({\mathbf{x}}_{{\mathcal{E}}}^{(\mu)})v^{\text{int}(K)}{\mathbf{n}}_{{\mathcal{E}},K}({\mathbf{x}}_{{\mathcal{E}}}^{(\mu)})-\sum_{q=1}^{Q}w_{K}^{(q)}p_{h}^{e}({\mathbf{x}}_{K}^{(q)})\nabla v({\mathbf{x}}_{K}^{(q)})\right)\\
&\quad+\sum_{q=1}^{Q}-w_{K}^{(q)}\rho_{h}(x_{K}^{(q)}){\mathbf{g}}^{\delta}_{h}({\mathbf{x}}_{K}^{(q)})v({\mathbf{x}}_{K}^{(q)}),\end{split}

where
\{{\mathbf{x}}_{K}^{(q)},w_{K}^{(q)}\}_{q=1}^{Q}
denote the quadrature points and weights in
K
.

3.4.3.
Energy flux
({\mathbf{F}}_{g})
approximations

As mentioned in (
2.17
), the energy flux has a very complex form. Notice that
\left({\mathbf{g}}_{h},\phi_{h}\right)
is also split into equilibrium state and perturbation state, thus it is obtained by:

\left({\mathbf{g}}_{h},\phi_{h}\right)=\left({\mathbf{g}}^{e}_{h},\phi^{e}_{h}\right)+\left({\mathbf{g}}^{\delta}_{h},\phi^{\delta}_{h}\right)=\left({\mathbf{g}}^{e}_{h},\phi^{e}_{h}\right)+{\mathcal{D}}_{1}(4\pi G(\rho_{h}-\rho_{h}^{e})).

However, the Poisson equation
{\mathcal{D}}_{2}
for the time derivative of gravitational potential needs a summation-by-parts approach to avoid an order deficiency. Specifically, (
3.17e
) will be modified as:

(3.24)

\begin{split}\int_{K}\dot{{\mathbf{g}}_{h}}\cdot{\mathbf{w}}{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{x}}}&=\sum_{{\mathcal{E}}\in\partial K}\int_{{\mathcal{E}}}\widehat{\dot{\phi_{h}}}{\mathbf{w}}^{{\rm int}(K)}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}_{{\mathcal{E}},K}{\text{d}}{s}-\int_{K}\dot{\phi_{h}}\nabla\cdot{\mathbf{w}}{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{x}}},\\
\int_{K}\dot{{\mathbf{g}}_{h}}\cdot\nabla\psi{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{x}}}&=\int_{{\mathcal{E}}}\psi^{{\rm int}(K)}\widehat{\dot{{\mathbf{g}}_{h}}}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}_{{\mathcal{E}},K}{\text{d}}{s}+\int_{K}4\pi G(\rho{\mathbf{u}})_{h}\cdot\nabla\psi{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{x}}}-\sum_{{\mathcal{E}}\in\partial K}\int_{{\mathcal{E}}}4\pi G\widehat{f}^{[1]}_{{\mathbf{n}}_{{\mathcal{E}},K}}\psi^{{\rm int}(K)}{\text{d}}{s}.\end{split}

Although the notation
{\mathcal{D}}_{2}
was previously defined, in the rest of this paper it will denote the modified Poisson solver (
3.24
), i.e.,

\left(\dot{{\mathbf{g}}_{h}},\dot{\phi_{h}}\right)={\mathcal{D}}_{2}\left(4\pi G\nabla\cdot(\rho{\mathbf{u}})_{h}\right).

Therefore, the numerical energy flux is given as follows:

(\widehat{{\mathbf{F}}_{g}})_{{\mathbf{n}}_{\mathcal{E},K}}=\dfrac{1}{8\pi G}\left(\widehat{\phi_{h}}\widehat{\dot{{\mathbf{g}}_{h}}}-\widehat{\dot{\phi_{h}}}\widehat{{\mathbf{g}}_{h}}\right)\cdot{\mathbf{n}}_{\mathcal{E},K}+\widehat{f}^{[1]}_{{\mathbf{n}}_{{\mathcal{E}},K}}\widehat{\phi_{h}},

where
(\widehat{{\mathbf{g}}_{h}},\widehat{\phi_{h}})=(\widehat{{\mathbf{g}}^{\delta}_{h}},\widehat{\phi^{\delta}_{h}})+(\widehat{{\mathbf{g}}^{e}_{h}},\widehat{\phi^{e}_{h}})
and
\left(\widehat{\dot{\phi_{h}}},\widehat{\dot{{\mathbf{g}}_{h}}}\right)
are defined in (
3.18
). The term
\widehat{f}^{[1]}_{{\mathbf{n}}_{{\mathcal{E}},K}}
denotes the first component of the modified HLLC flux introduced in (
3.20
). The modified HLLC flux is evaluated first, followed by the energy flux. Similar to (
3.21
), we omit the numerical quadrature on
{\mathcal{E}}
of
(\widehat{{\mathbf{F}}_{g}})_{{\mathbf{n}}_{\mathcal{E},K}}
here. In the internal of element
K
, the energy flux is given by:

{\mathbf{F}}_{g}=\dfrac{1}{8\pi G}\left(\phi_{h}\dot{{\mathbf{g}}_{h}}-\dot{\phi_{h}}{\mathbf{g}}_{h}\right)+(\rho{\mathbf{u}})_{h}\phi_{h}.

With the
Q
points numerical quadrature in the
d
dimensional element
K
similar in (
3.23
), we obtain the fully discrete version of
{\mathbf{G}}
. The numerical quadrature of the third term of
{\mathbf{G}}
in
K
is given by:

(3.25)

\int_{K}{\mathbf{F}}_{g}\cdot\nabla v{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{x}}}\approx\sum_{q=1}^{Q}w_{K}^{(q)}{\mathbf{F}}_{g}({\mathbf{x}}_{K}^{(q)})\cdot\nabla v({\mathbf{x}}_{K}^{(q)}),

where
\{{\mathbf{x}}_{K}^{(q)},w_{K}^{(q)}\}_{q=1}^{Q}
denote the quadrature points and weights in
K
.

3.4.4.
Flowchart for the structure-preserving LDG scheme

Suppose that the initial conditions for (
3.15
) are
\rho^{0}({\mathbf{x}})
,
(\rho{\mathbf{u}})^{0}({\mathbf{x}})
,
E^{0}({\mathbf{x}})
, and
\phi^{0}({\mathbf{x}})
. These variables are initialized by the projector (
3.3
):

(3.26)

\rho_{h}^{0}({\mathbf{x}})={\mathbf{P}}\rho^{0}({\mathbf{x}}),\quad(\rho{\mathbf{u}})_{h}^{0}({\mathbf{x}})={\mathbf{P}}(\rho{\mathbf{u}})^{0}({\mathbf{x}}),\quad E_{h}^{0}({\mathbf{x}})={\mathbf{P}}E^{0}({\mathbf{x}}).

The initial gravitational potential
\phi_{h}^{0}
and the total energy
(E_{tot})_{h}^{0}
are obtained by applying the Poisson solver
{\mathcal{D}}_{1}
, i.e.,

(3.27)

({\mathbf{g}}_{h}^{0},\phi_{h}^{0})={\mathcal{D}}_{1}(4\pi G\rho_{h}^{0}),\quad(E_{tot})^{0}_{h}={\mathbf{P}}\left(E_{h}^{0}+\tfrac{1}{2}\rho_{h}^{0}\phi_{h}^{0}\right):=E_{h}^{0}+{\mathbf{P}}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\rho_{h}^{0}\phi_{h}^{0}\right).

Based on the above approximations and modifications for the flux term and source term, the structure-preserving LDG scheme with Euler-forward method is implemented in the following steps:

•

Step 1.
Suppose that the variables
(\rho_{h}^{n},(\rho{\mathbf{u}})_{h}^{n},(E_{tot})^{n}_{h})
at time
t^{n}
remains known. The gravitational force and gravitational potential
({\mathbf{g}}_{h}^{n},\phi_{h}^{n})
is given by:

(3.28)

\left({\mathbf{g}}_{h}^{n},\phi^{n}_{h}\right)=\left({\mathbf{g}}_{h}^{e},\phi_{h}^{e}\right)+\left(({\mathbf{g}}_{h}^{\delta})^{n},(\phi_{h}^{\delta})^{n}\right),

where
\left(({\mathbf{g}}_{h}^{\delta})^{n},(\phi_{h}^{\delta})^{n}\right)
satisfy

(3.29)

\left(({\mathbf{g}}_{h}^{\delta})^{n},(\phi_{h}^{\delta})^{n}\right)={\mathcal{D}}_{1}\left(4\pi G(\rho_{h}^{\delta})^{n}\right)={\mathcal{D}}_{1}\left(4\pi G\left(\rho_{h}^{n}-\rho_{h}^{e}\right)\right).

The time derivative term
\left(\dot{{\mathbf{g}}_{h}^{n}},\dot{\phi^{n}_{h}}\right)
is given by the modified Poisson solver
{\mathcal{D}}_{2}
(
3.24
):

(3.30)

\left(\dot{{\mathbf{g}}_{h}^{n}},\dot{\phi_{h}^{n}}\right)={\mathcal{D}}_{2}\left(4\pi G\nabla\cdot(\rho{\mathbf{u}})^{n}_{h}\right).

The total non-gravitational energy
E_{h}^{n}
is given by:

(3.31)

E_{h}^{n}=(E_{tot})_{h}^{n}-{\mathbf{P}}\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\rho_{h}^{n}\phi_{h}^{n}\right).

•

Step 2.
Compute the modified HLLC flux. The modified HLLC flux is given by (
3.20
). We then get the HLLC flux at time
t^{n}
.

(3.32)

\widehat{{\mathbf{F}}}^{n}_{{\mathbf{n}}_{\mathcal{E},K}}={\mathbf{F}}^{\text{hllc}}\left(\frac{p_{h}^{e,\star}}{p_{h}^{e,\text{int}(K)}}{\mathbf{U}}_{h}^{n,\text{int}(K)},\frac{p_{h}^{e,\star}}{p_{h}^{e,\text{ext}(K)}}{\mathbf{U}}_{h}^{n,\text{ext}(K)};{\mathbf{n}}_{{\mathcal{E}},K}\right)=\left(\widehat{f}^{n,[1]},\widehat{\bm{f}}^{n,[2],T},\widehat{f}^{n,[3]}\right)_{{\mathbf{n}}_{{\mathcal{E}},K}}^{T}.

•

Step 3.
Compute the energy flux in the internal of an element
K
and on the face
{\mathcal{E}}
based on (
3.28
), (
3.30
) and (
3.32
). In the internal of element, the energy flux is given by:

(3.33)

{\mathbf{F}}^{n}_{g}=\dfrac{1}{8\pi G}\left(\phi^{n}_{h}\dot{{\mathbf{g}}^{n}_{h}}-\dot{\phi^{n}_{h}}{\mathbf{g}}^{n}_{h}\right)+(\rho{\mathbf{u}})^{n}_{h}\phi^{n}_{h}.

On the face of element, the energy flux is given by

(3.34)

(\widehat{{\mathbf{F}}_{g}})^{n}_{{\mathbf{n}}_{\mathcal{E},K}}=\dfrac{1}{8\pi G}\left(\widehat{\phi^{n}_{h}}\widehat{\dot{{\mathbf{g}}^{n}_{h}}}-\widehat{\dot{\phi^{n}_{h}}}\widehat{{\mathbf{g}}^{n}_{h}}\right)\cdot{\mathbf{n}}_{\mathcal{E},K}+f^{n,[1]}_{{\mathbf{n}}_{{\mathcal{E}},K}}\widehat{\phi^{n}_{h}}.

•

Step 4.
Evolve to obtain
(\rho_{h}^{n+1},(\rho{\mathbf{u}})_{h}^{n+1},(E_{tot})^{n+1}_{h})
. From the approximations of source term (
3.23
) and the semi-discrete form (
3.17a
), we have:

(3.35a)

\int_{K}\rho_{h}^{n+1}v{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{x}}}=\int_{K}\rho_{h}^{n}v{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{x}}}+\Delta t\left(\int_{K}{\mathbf{F}}^{n,[1]}_{h}\cdot\nabla v{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{x}}}-\sum_{{\mathcal{E}}\in\partial K}\int_{{\mathcal{E}}}\widehat{f}^{n,[1]}_{{\mathbf{n}}_{{\mathcal{E}},K}}v^{\text{int}(K)}{\text{d}}{s}\right),

(3.35b)

\int_{K}(\rho{\mathbf{u}})_{h}^{n+1}v{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{x}}}=\int_{K}(\rho{\mathbf{u}})_{h}^{n}v{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{x}}}+\Delta t\left(\int_{K}{\mathbf{F}}^{n,[2]}_{h}\cdot\nabla v{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{x}}}-\sum_{{\mathcal{E}}\in\partial K}\int_{{\mathcal{E}}}\widehat{\bm{f}}^{n,[2]}_{{\mathbf{n}}_{{\mathcal{E}},K}}v^{\text{int}(K)}{\text{d}}{s}+\left<{\mathbf{S}}^{[2],n},v\right>_{K}\right),

(3.35c)

\begin{split}\int_{K}(E_{tot})_{h}^{n+1}v{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{x}}}=\int_{K}(E_{tot})_{h}^{n}v{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{x}}}&+\Delta t\left(\int_{K}{\mathbf{F}}^{n,[3]}_{h}\cdot\nabla v{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{x}}}-\sum_{{\mathcal{E}}\in\partial K}\int_{{\mathcal{E}}}\widehat{f}^{n,[3]}_{{\mathbf{n}}_{{\mathcal{E}},K}}v^{\text{int}(K)}{\text{d}}{s}\right)\\
&+\Delta t\left(\int_{K}({\mathbf{F}}_{g})^{n}_{h}\cdot\nabla v{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{x}}}-\sum_{{\mathcal{E}}\in\partial K}\int_{{\mathcal{E}}}(\widehat{{\mathbf{F}}_{g}})^{n}_{{\mathbf{n}}_{{\mathcal{E}},K}}v^{\text{int}(K)}{\text{d}}{s}\right).\end{split}

Remark 3.3
.

The projection procedure in (
3.27
) and (
3.31
) is essential for preserving the WB property. Since the initial total energy
(E_{tot})_{h}^{0}
is projected onto the DG space
V_{h}^{k}
, the same projection procedure is required during the subsequent evolution to ensure consistency. This guarantees the pressure
p_{h}^{n}
remains invariant. As shown in Section
4
, omitting this procedure leads to the loss of the WB property.

3.5.
High order time discretizations

When first order Euler-forward method is considered, both the standard LDG scheme (
3.11
) and the structure-preserving LDG scheme (
3.35
) can be written in a compact form:

(3.36)

{\mathbf{U}}_{h}^{n+1}={\mathbf{U}}_{h}^{n}+\Delta t\mathcal{L}({\mathbf{U}}_{h}^{n},t^{n});\qquad{\mathbf{W}}_{h}^{n+1}={\mathbf{W}}_{h}^{n}+\Delta t\mathcal{H}({\mathbf{W}}_{h}^{n},t^{n}),

where
\mathcal{L}(\cdot,\cdot)
and
\mathcal{H}(\cdot,\cdot)
represent the spatial discretization operators associated with the respective schemes, and
\Delta t
is the time step size. When combined with high order strong-stability-preserving Runge-Kutta (SSP-RK) time discretizations
[
45
]
, both schemes are formulated as follows:

For the second order SSP-RK method:

\begin{split}{\mathbf{U}}_{h}^{(1)}&={\mathbf{U}}_{h}^{n}+\Delta t\mathcal{L}({\mathbf{U}}_{h}^{n},t^{n}),\\
{\mathbf{U}}_{h}^{n+1}&=\dfrac{1}{2}{\mathbf{U}}_{h}^{n}+\dfrac{1}{2}\left({\mathbf{U}}^{(1)}+\Delta t\mathcal{L}({\mathbf{U}}_{h}^{(1)},t^{n}+\Delta t)\right),\end{split}\qquad\begin{split}{\mathbf{W}}_{h}^{(1)}&={\mathbf{W}}_{h}^{n}+\Delta t\mathcal{H}({\mathbf{W}}_{h}^{n},t^{n}),\\
{\mathbf{W}}_{h}^{n+1}&=\dfrac{1}{2}{\mathbf{W}}_{h}^{n}+\dfrac{1}{2}\left({\mathbf{W}}^{(1)}+\Delta t\mathcal{H}({\mathbf{W}}_{h}^{(1)},t^{n}+\Delta t)\right).\end{split}

For the third order SSP-RK method:

\begin{split}{\mathbf{U}}_{h}^{(1)}&={\mathbf{U}}_{h}^{n}+\Delta t\mathcal{L}({\mathbf{U}}_{h}^{n},t^{n}),\\
{\mathbf{U}}_{h}^{(2)}&=\dfrac{3}{4}{\mathbf{U}}_{h}^{n}+\dfrac{1}{4}\bigg({\mathbf{U}}^{(1)}+\Delta t\mathcal{L}({\mathbf{U}}_{h}^{(1)},t^{n}+\Delta t)\bigg),\\
{\mathbf{U}}_{h}^{n+1}&=\dfrac{1}{3}{\mathbf{U}}_{h}^{n}+\dfrac{2}{3}\left({\mathbf{U}}^{(2)}+\Delta t\mathcal{L}({\mathbf{U}}_{h}^{(2)},t^{n}+\dfrac{1}{2}\Delta t)\right),\end{split}\quad\begin{split}{\mathbf{W}}_{h}^{(1)}&={\mathbf{W}}_{h}^{n}+\Delta t\mathcal{H}({\mathbf{W}}_{h}^{n},t^{n}),\\
{\mathbf{W}}_{h}^{(2)}&=\dfrac{3}{4}{\mathbf{W}}_{h}^{n}+\dfrac{1}{4}\left({\mathbf{W}}^{(1)}+\Delta t\mathcal{H}({\mathbf{W}}_{h}^{(1)},t^{n}+\Delta t)\right),\\
{\mathbf{W}}_{h}^{n+1}&=\dfrac{1}{3}{\mathbf{W}}_{h}^{n}+\dfrac{2}{3}\left({\mathbf{W}}^{(2)}+\Delta t\mathcal{H}({\mathbf{W}}_{h}^{(2)},t^{n}+\dfrac{1}{2}\Delta t)\right).\end{split}

3.6.
Oscillation-eliminating approach

We now address the issue of numerical oscillations that may arise when high order methods are applied to non-smooth problems. To control the numerical oscillations, we adopt the OE technique in
[
42
]
. Here, we give a brief introduction to the OE technique, for more details of this method we refer the readers to
[
42
]
.

The semi-discrete OEDG method can be expressed as an ordinary equation system with operator splitting:

\dfrac{{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{U}}}}{{\text{d}}{t}}+{\mathbf{L}}_{f}({\mathbf{U}})+\mathbf{\Sigma}({\mathbf{U}}){\mathbf{U}}=0\Longrightarrow\dfrac{{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{U}}}}{{\text{d}}{t}}=-{\mathbf{L}}_{f}({\mathbf{U}})\text{ and }\dfrac{{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{U}}}_{\sigma}}{{\text{d}}{t}}=-\mathbf{\Sigma}({\mathbf{U}}){\mathbf{U}}_{\sigma}.

Here
\mathbf{\Sigma}
is the damping term in the OE method and has scale-invariant and evolution-invariant properties. It is worth noting that the damping ODE is quai-linear while
\mathbf{\Sigma}({\mathbf{U}})
is fixed. The general high order RK or multi-step discretization can both combined with OE method to avoid certain numerical oscillation. We take the
s-
stage explicit RK method combined with the OE technique as an example:

\begin{split}\mathbf{U}^{n,0}_{h}&=\mathbf{U}^{n}_{\sigma}=\mathbf{U}^{n}_{h},\\
\mathbf{U}^{n,l+1}_{h}&=\sum_{0\leq m\leq l}\left(a_{lm}\mathbf{U}^{n,m}_{\sigma}-\Delta tb_{lm}{\mathbf{L}}_{f}\left({\mathbf{U}}^{n,m}_{\sigma}\right)\right),\quad l=0,1,\cdots,s-1,\\
\mathbf{U}^{n+1}_{h}&=\mathbf{U}^{n,s}_{\sigma},\end{split}

where
{\mathbf{U}}^{n,m}_{\sigma}
denote the function after the OE damping technique applied on
{\mathbf{U}}^{n,m}_{h}
, i.e.

\mathbf{U}^{n,l+1}_{\sigma}=\mathcal{F}_{\Delta t}\mathbf{U}^{n,l+1}_{h},\quad l=0,1,\cdots,s-1.

Here,
\Delta t
is the time-step size, and
\mathcal{F}_{\Delta t}
denotes the OE technique for notational convenience. Actually, we define
\mathcal{F}_{\Delta t}\left({\mathbf{U}}_{h}\right)({\mathbf{x}})=\mathbf{U}_{\sigma}({\mathbf{x}},\Delta t)
with
\mathbf{U}_{\sigma}({\mathbf{x}},\hat{t})
being the solution of the following initial value problem:

(3.37)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\dfrac{{\text{d}}}{{\text{d}}{\hat{t}}}\int_{K}{\mathbf{U}}_{\sigma}\cdot{\mathbf{v}}{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{x}}}+\sum_{m=0}^{k}\vartheta_{K}^{m}\left({\mathbf{U}}_{h}\right)\int_{K}\left({\mathbf{U}}_{\sigma}-{\mathbf{P}}^{m-1}{\mathbf{U}}_{\sigma}\right)\cdot{\mathbf{v}}{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{x}}}=0,\quad\forall{\mathbf{v}}\in[\mathbb{P}^{k}(K)]^{N},\\
&{\mathbf{U}}_{\sigma}({\mathbf{x}},0)={\mathbf{U}}_{h}({\mathbf{x}}).\end{aligned}\right.

The coefficient
\vartheta_{K}^{m}({\mathbf{U}}_{h})
is defined as:

\vartheta_{K}^{m}({\mathbf{U}}_{h})=\sum_{e\in\partial K}\beta_{e}\dfrac{\sigma^{m}_{e,K}({\mathbf{U}}_{h})}{h_{e,K}}.

\beta_{e}
is a suitable estimate of the local maximum wave speed in the direction
{\mathbf{n}}_{e}
on the interface
e
of the element
K
, which is set as the spectral radius of
\sum_{i=1}^{d}n_{e}^{(i)}\dfrac{\partial{{\mathbf{F}}_{i}}}{\partial{{\mathbf{u}}}}\Big|_{{\mathbf{u}}={\mathbf{u}}_{K}}
, where
n_{e}^{(i)}
is the
i
th component of
{\mathbf{n}}_{e}
.
h_{e,K}
,
\sigma^{m}_{e,K}({\mathbf{U}}_{h})
is defined as:

h_{e,K}=\sup_{{\mathbf{x}}\in K}\text{dist}({\mathbf{x}},e),\qquad\sigma^{m}_{e,K}({\mathbf{U}}_{h})=\max_{1\leq i\leq N}\sigma^{m}_{e,K}(u^{(i)}_{h}),

while
u^{(i)}_{h}
denoting the
i
th component of
{\mathbf{u}}_{h}
, and

\sigma^{m}_{e,K}(u^{(i)}_{h})=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }u^{(i)}_{h}\equiv\overline{u}^{(i)}_{h},\\
\dfrac{(2m+1)h_{e,K}^{m}}{2(2k-1)m!}\sum\limits_{|\alpha|=m}\dfrac{\frac{1}{|e|}\int_{e}\left|[\![\partial^{\alpha}u^{(i)}_{h}]\!]_{e}\right|{\text{d}}{S}}{||u^{(i)}_{h}-\text{avg}(u^{(i)}_{h})||_{L_{\infty}}(\Omega)},&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

The multi-index
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{d})
with
|\alpha|=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}
, and the derivative
\partial^{\alpha}u^{(i)}_{h}
is defined by:

\partial^{\alpha}u^{(i)}_{h}=\dfrac{\partial^{|\alpha|}}{\partial x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots x_{d}^{\alpha_{d}}}u^{(i)}_{h}.

The damping equation (
3.37
) can be explicitly implemented as:

\mathcal{F}_{\Delta t}{\mathbf{U}}^{n,l+1}_{h}={\mathbf{U}}^{(0)}_{K}\phi_{K}^{(0)}({\mathbf{x}})+\sum_{j=1}^{k}e^{-\Delta t\sum_{m=0}^{j}\vartheta_{K}^{m}({\mathbf{U}}^{n,l+1}_{h})}\sum_{|\alpha|=j}{\mathbf{U}}^{(\alpha)}_{K}\phi^{(\alpha)}_{K}({\mathbf{x}}),

where
{\mathbf{U}}^{(\alpha)}_{K}
denote the moment coefficient based on the orthogonal basis
\left\{\phi_{K}^{\alpha}\right\}_{|\alpha|\leq k}\subset\mathbb{P}^{k}(K)
. It is easy to find that
{\mathbf{U}}^{(0)}_{K}=\text{avg}({\mathbf{U}}^{n}_{K})
, thus the OE procedure will not influence the average value of the element
K
. To design OE method for WB scheme, take third order SSP-RK as example, the fully discrete scheme is outlined as follows:

\displaystyle\tilde{{\mathbf{W}}}_{h}^{(1)}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{W}}_{h}^{n}+\Delta t\mathcal{H}({\mathbf{W}}_{h}^{n},t^{n}),

\displaystyle{\mathbf{W}}_{h}^{(1)}={\mathbf{W}}_{h}^{e}+\mathcal{F}_{\Delta t}(\tilde{{\mathbf{W}}}_{h}^{(1)}-{\mathbf{W}}_{h}^{e}),

\displaystyle\tilde{{\mathbf{W}}}_{h}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{3}{4}{\mathbf{W}}_{h}^{n}+\dfrac{1}{4}\left({\mathbf{W}}^{(1)}+\Delta t\mathcal{H}({\mathbf{W}}_{h}^{(1)},t^{n}+\Delta t)\right),

\displaystyle{\mathbf{W}}_{h}^{(2)}={\mathbf{W}}_{h}^{e}+\mathcal{F}_{\Delta t}(\tilde{{\mathbf{W}}}_{h}^{(2)}-{\mathbf{W}}_{h}^{e}),

\displaystyle\tilde{{\mathbf{W}}}_{h}^{n+1}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{3}{\mathbf{W}}_{h}^{n}+\dfrac{2}{3}\left({\mathbf{W}}^{(2)}+\Delta t\mathcal{H}({\mathbf{W}}_{h}^{(2)},t^{n}+\dfrac{1}{2}\Delta t)\right),

\displaystyle{\mathbf{W}}_{h}^{n+1}={\mathbf{W}}_{h}^{e}+\mathcal{F}_{\Delta t}(\tilde{{\mathbf{W}}}_{h}^{n+1}-{\mathbf{W}}_{h}^{e}).

Specifically, suppose that
\tilde{{\mathbf{W}}}_{h}\big|_{K}\in V_{h}^{k}
and

\tilde{{\mathbf{W}}}_{h}\big|_{K}={\mathbf{W}}_{h}^{e}+{\mathbf{W}}_{h}^{\delta}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\sum_{|\alpha|=j}{\mathbf{W}}^{e,(\alpha)}_{K}\phi^{(\alpha)}_{K}({\mathbf{x}})+\sum_{j=0}^{k}\sum_{|\alpha|=j}{\mathbf{W}}^{\delta,(\alpha)}_{K}\phi^{(\alpha)}_{K}({\mathbf{x}}).

Then we apply the OE technique on the perturbation part

(3.38)

{\mathbf{W}}_{h}={\mathbf{W}}_{h}^{e}+\mathcal{F}_{\Delta t}({\mathbf{W}}_{h}^{\delta})=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\sum_{|\alpha|=j}{\mathbf{W}}^{e,(\alpha)}_{K}\phi^{(\alpha)}_{K}({\mathbf{x}})+{\mathbf{W}}^{\delta,(0)}_{K}\phi_{K}^{(0)}({\mathbf{x}})+\sum_{j=1}^{k}e^{-\Delta t\sum_{m=0}^{j}\vartheta_{K}^{m}(\tilde{{\mathbf{W}}}_{h})}\sum_{|\alpha|=j}{\mathbf{W}}^{\delta,(\alpha)}_{K}\phi^{(\alpha)}_{K}({\mathbf{x}}).

It holds that if the damping operator
\mathcal{F}_{\Delta t}
remains high order accuracy, then

||{\mathbf{W}}_{h}-\tilde{{\mathbf{W}}}_{h}||=||{\mathbf{W}}_{h}^{\delta}-\mathcal{F}_{\Delta t}({\mathbf{W}}_{h}^{\delta})||=O(\Delta x^{k+1}).

Thus the modified damping procedure (
3.38
) will not destroy the accuracy of our structure-preserving LDG scheme.

4.
Structure-preserving properties

Based on the flowchart of the structure-preserving LDG scheme, we now prove the WB and TEC properties of our proposed structure-preserving LDG scheme.

Theorem 4.1
.

The numerical method described in Section
3.4
together with the flowchart in Section
3.4.4
, preserves the explicitly known equilibrium state
{\mathbf{u}}^{0}=\mathbf{0}
,
p^{0}=-\rho^{0}\nabla\phi^{0}
,
\Delta\phi^{0}=4\pi G\rho^{0}
. Numerically, if the initial conditions are given by the steady states
\rho^{0}=\rho^{e}
,
{\mathbf{u}}^{0}={\mathbf{u}}^{e}=\mathbf{0}
,
p^{0}=p^{e}
, and
\phi^{0}=\phi^{e}
, then we have
\rho_{h}^{n}=\rho_{h}^{e}
,
{\mathbf{u}}_{h}^{e}=\mathbf{0}
,
p_{h}^{n}=p_{h}^{e}
, and
\phi_{h}^{n}=\phi_{h}^{e}({\mathbf{x}})
for all
n
, thereby confirming the well-balanced property of the scheme.

Proof.

The proof is provided in Appendix
A
.
∎

Theorem 4.2
.

The numerical method described in Section
3.4
together with the flowchart in Section
3.4.4
conserves the total energy:

(4.1)

\int_{\Omega}(E_{tot})_{h}^{n+1}{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{x}}}=\int_{\Omega}(E_{tot})_{h}^{n}{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{x}}},

under either compactly supported boundary conditions or periodic boundary conditions.

Proof.

The proof follows directly from (
3.35c
). Let the test function
v=1
and summation from the all elements, we have that

\begin{split}\sum_{K}\int_{K}(E_{tot})_{h}^{n+1}{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{x}}}&=\sum_{K}\int_{K}(E_{tot})_{h}^{n}{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{x}}}+\Delta t\left(\sum_{K}\sum_{{\mathcal{E}}\in\partial K}\int_{{\mathcal{E}}}\widehat{f}^{n,[3]}_{{\mathbf{n}}_{{\mathcal{E}},K}}{\text{d}}{s}+\sum_{K}\sum_{{\mathcal{E}}\in\partial K}\int_{{\mathcal{E}}}(\widehat{{\mathbf{F}}_{g}})^{n}_{{\mathbf{n}}_{{\mathcal{E}},K}}{\text{d}}{s}\right)\\
&=\sum_{K}\int_{K}(E_{tot})_{h}^{n}{\text{d}}{{\mathbf{x}}}+\Delta t\left(\sum_{{\mathcal{E}}\in\partial K\cap{\mathcal{E}}_{B}}\int_{{\mathcal{E}}}\widehat{f}^{n,[3]}_{{\mathbf{n}}_{{\mathcal{E}},K}}{\text{d}}{s}+\sum_{{\mathcal{E}}\in\partial K\cap{\mathcal{E}}_{B}}\int_{{\mathcal{E}}}(\widehat{{\mathbf{F}}_{g}})^{n}_{{\mathbf{n}}_{{\mathcal{E}},K}}{\text{d}}{s}\right)\end{split}

For the periodic boundary conditions, we have that

\sum_{{\mathcal{E}}\in\partial K\cap{\mathcal{E}}_{B}}\int_{{\mathcal{E}}}\widehat{f}^{n,[3]}_{{\mathbf{n}}_{{\mathcal{E}},K}}{\text{d}}{s}=\sum_{{\mathcal{E}}\in\partial K\cap{\mathcal{E}}_{B}}\int_{{\mathcal{E}}}(\widehat{{\mathbf{F}}_{g}})^{n}_{{\mathbf{n}}_{{\mathcal{E}},K}}{\text{d}}{s}=0.

And for the compactly supported boundary conditions, we have that

{\mathbf{u}}\big|_{{\mathcal{E}}_{B}}=0,

\widehat{f}^{n,[3]}_{{\mathbf{n}}_{{\mathcal{E}},K}}=(\widehat{{\mathbf{F}}_{g}})^{n}_{{\mathbf{n}}_{{\mathcal{E}},K}}=0,\quad\text{ on }{\mathcal{E}}_{B}.

We can finally obtain the TEC property.
∎

5.
Numerical tests

In this section, we will present our numerical schemes through a series of numerical examples. For comparison purposes, we also include the numerical results obtained by the standard LDG scheme. All simulations are implemented in C++ with double-precision. Unless otherwise specified, we use the PardisoLLT solver from Intel MKL, accessed through the Eigen interface, to solve the large sparse matrices arising from the discretized Poisson equations. The time step is

\Delta t={\rm CFL}\dfrac{h}{\max\limits_{K\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}\left(||\overline{{\mathbf{u}}}_{K}||_{\infty}+\overline{c}_{K}\right)},

where
\overline{c}_{K}=\sqrt{\gamma\overline{p}_{K}/\overline{\rho}_{K}}
,
h=\max_{K\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}h_{K}
and
||\cdot||_{\infty}
denotes the maximum component of average velocity. With
f\in L^{r}(\Omega)
and its numerical approximation
f_{h}\in V_{h}^{k}\subset L^{r}(\Omega)
,
the numerical
L^{r}
errors and convergence orders are defined as

L^{r}\text{ error }=||f-f_{h}||_{L^{r}(\Omega)},\qquad L^{r}\text{ order }=\log_{2}\left(\dfrac{||f-f_{h}||_{L^{r}(\Omega)}}{||f-f_{h/2}||_{L^{r}(\Omega)}}\right).

(
Jeans instability
) A classical example for an instability for compressible SG Euler equations is the so-called jeans instability problem, which was first described by Jeans
[
26
]
. As a fundamental problem in astrophysics, the Jeans instability problem serves as an excellent test case for verifying the TEC property of numerical schemes.
The domain is
\Omega=[0,1]^{2}
with periodic boundary conditions as well as the gravitational potential and
\gamma=5/3
.

The initial condition is similar in
[
44
,
27
,
40
]
. With the background constant density
\rho_{0}=1
and constant pressure
p_{0}=\rho_{0}/\gamma
, we apply a small perturbation on density and pressure.

(5.3)

\rho({\mathbf{x}},0)=\rho_{0}\left(1+\delta_{0}\sin({\mathbf{k}}\cdot{\mathbf{x}})\right),

p({\mathbf{x}},0)=p_{0}\left(1+\delta_{0}\sin({\mathbf{k}}\cdot{\mathbf{x}})\right),

{\mathbf{u}}({\mathbf{x}},0)=\mathbf{0},

and the gravitational potential
\phi
is given by:

\Delta\phi=4\pi G(\rho-\rho_{0}).

Here,
\delta_{0}=10^{-3}
denotes the wave amplitude, and
{\mathbf{k}}
is the wave vector. The total energy is defined as

E_{tot}=E+\dfrac{1}{2}(\rho-\rho_{0})\phi.

The amplitude of the perturbation changes with time as
e^{i\omega t}
, where
\omega
is determined by the Jeans dispersion relation in Fourier space
[
25
]
:

(5.4)

\omega^{2}=c_{0}^{2}|{\mathbf{k}}|^{2}-4\pi G\rho_{0},

with
c_{0}=\sqrt{\gamma p_{0}/\rho_{0}}
the sound speed. Hence we can identify the Jeans wave number
k_{J}
from (
5.4
)

k_{J}=\dfrac{\sqrt{4\pi G\rho_{0}}}{c_{0}}.

When
\omega^{2}<0,|{\mathbf{k}}|/k_{J}<1
, the wave perturbation becomes unstable and leads to gravitational collapse, whereas when
\omega^{2}>0,|{\mathbf{k}}|/k_{J}>1
, it undergoes stable periodic oscillations. For simplicity, we vary the gravitational constant
G
to investigate both unstable and stable cases.

This test adopts the OE technique and the PP limiter, following the approach used in Example
5.4
. We set
k_{1}=k_{2}=2\pi
. The numerical results of density on a
200^{2}
uniform mesh are shown in Figure
5.6
and Figure
5.8
. Due to the periodic boundary conditions, we implement with the PardisoLU solver from Intel MKL. In Figure
5.6
, we set
G=6.674
and observe the expected gravitational collapse near the point
(0.2375,0.2375)
. We illustrate the time history of the changes in total energy in Figure
5.7
. The maximum absolute value of the changes in total energy is
2.53137\times 10^{-13}
for the structure-preserving scheme, which is at the level of machine error, and is significantly lower than the
3.2845\times 10^{-6}
observed in the standard scheme. A sharp increase in total energy is observed during the gravitational collapse, and the instability renders the results of the standard LDG scheme unreliable. Additionally, Figure
5.8
shows the stable case with
G=0.6674
, where the density exhibits periodic variations.

6.
Conclusion

In this paper, a high-order well-balanced and total-energy-conserving local discontinuous Galerkin scheme has been developed for the compressible self-gravitating Euler equations. High order accuracy in time and in space is obtained by strong-stability-preserving Runge-Kutta methods and local discontinuous Galerkin framework, respectively. To address the difficulty from the time-dependent source term, we have split the gravitational potential to guarantee a correct preservation of steady states. With the energy conservative form, the total energy can be preserved and the scheme can easily extend to high order time discretizations. We have proven that our proposed scheme has well-balanced and total-energy-conserving properties. Extensive numerical results were provided to demonstrate the high order accuracy, shock-capturing ability, well-balanced property, and total-energy-conserving property.

For future work, we aim to extend the current framework to unstructured meshes, enabling the simulation of more complex astrophysical phenomena. In addition, we plan to investigate the positivity-preserving property of the scheme to further enhance its robustness in the presence of strong discontinuities.
