On Uniform Weighted Deep Polynomial approximation
Kingsley Yeon, Steven B. Damelin

Abstract
It is a classical result in rational approximation theory that certain non-smooth or singular functions, such as | x | |x| and x 1 / p x^{1/p} , can be efficiently approximated using rational functions with root-exponential convergence in terms of degrees of freedom [ 17 , 6 ] . In contrast, polynomial approximations admit only algebraic convergence by Jackson’s theorem [ 11 ] . Recent work shows that composite polynomial architectures can recover exponential approximation rates even without smoothness [ 19 ] . In this work, we introduce and analyze a class of weighted deep polynomial approximants tailored for functions with asymmetric behavior—growing unbounded on one side and decaying on the other. By multiplying a learnable deep polynomial with a one-sided weight, we capture both local non-smoothness and global growth. We show numerically that this framework outperforms Taylor, Chebyshev, and standard deep polynomial approximants, even when all use the same number of parameters. To optimize these approximants in practice, we propose a stable graph-based parameterization strategy building on [ 21 ] .

Introduction

Let
\{p_{\ell}\}_{\ell}^{L}
be a family of univariate polynomials on the real line where each
p_{\ell}
has degree
d_{\ell}
. Here
d_{\ell}\geq 1
. A
deep polynomial
of
L

P(x):=p_{L}(p_{L-1}(p_{L-2}(...(p_{1}(x)))).

The total (composite) degree of
P
is

D\;=\;d_{1}\,d_{2}\,\cdots\,d_{L}.

Typically, the number of layers
L
is small in comparison to the total degree
D
which can be taken to be quite large.
We note that the number of free parameters in the polynomial
P
is
d_{1}+...d_{L}-L+2
. It is widely believed that the layered structure of composite polynomials mimics in many ways layers of neurons in numerous deep learning networks and hence their approximation properties are natural problems to study
[
5
]
.

Functions with singularities are often difficult to approximate by polynomials due to poor behavior near the singularity, which is why classical theory relies on smoothness. Composite polynomials, however, often capture such structure more effectively. At its core, approximation theory is a story of compression: we seek low-dimensional parameterizations that accurately represent a function. Polynomials excel at compressing smooth functions, with classical orthogonal polynomials achieving exponential convergence for analytic functions. Similarly, Fourier series provide optimal representations for periodic inputs in
L^{2}
. For functions with discontinuities or sharp transitions, rational functions can be computed via the Remez algorithm.

In practice, many numerical algorithms use structured iterations such as Newton’s method. These can be written as composite rational or polynomial updates
f_{k+1}(x)=g(f_{k}(x),x)
, where
g\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
is a fixed-degree polynomial. Then
f_{k}
becomes a deep polynomial of degree
n^{k}
, defined by few tunable parameters. Such iterative structures appear naturally in algorithms for non-arithmetic operations. For example, the sign function can be written as
\text{sgn}(x)=x/|x|
, the comparison operator as
\text{comp}(u,v)=\tfrac{1}{2}(\text{sgn}(u-v)+1)
, and the max operation as
\max(u,v)=\tfrac{1}{2}(u+v+(u-v)\text{sgn}(u-v))
. Newton’s method is often employed to approximate
\text{sgn}(x)
, and has been shown to be optimal in the sense of requiring the fewest non-scalar multiplications
[
9
]
.

As a concrete example, to approximate
|x|
, consider

f_{k+1}(x)=\tfrac{1}{2}f_{k}(x)\left(3-x^{2}f_{k}(x)^{2}\right),\quad f_{0}(x)=1,

which converges to
1/|x|
for
x\neq 0
. Multiplying by
x^{2}
yields a sequence of deep polynomials converging exponentially to
|x|

[
19
]
. In many cases, such composite iterations surpass classical minimax approximants while using fewer degrees of freedom.

Our work in this paper is motivated by the following two papers,
[
19
]
and
[
6
]
. It is a classical result in univariate rational approximation, see
[
17
]

can be minmax approximated by type

, degree

. It is also known, see
[
6
]
, that for fixed

can be minmax approximated on all of

by an explicit
[\log_{p}d]+1

(d,d-1)

composite with double exponential rate with respect to number of degrees of freedom to express the rational function
r
. Here, the degree

is large enough.

For univariate polynomial approximation, Jackson’s classical result, see for example
[
11
]
, tells us that on all of
[-1,1]
and respectively
[0,1]
, the functions
|x|
and respectively

. It is also known, see
[

, that on all of
[-1,1]

|x|
and respectively

, can be well approximated with exponential rate of decay with respect to the number of degrees of freedom.

Motivated by the property that deep polynomials are well-suited for approximating non-regular functions (e.g., those that are only
C^{0}
), we ask whether they can be combined with a one-sided decaying weight to approximate functions that grow unbounded on one side and decay on the other. A simple example is the exponential function. Polynomials alone cannot approximate
e^{-x}
uniformly on
\mathbb{R}
, since they diverge at infinity. Rational functions do converge, but require divisions. Instead, we consider weighted polynomials of the form
q(x)e^{-Q(x)}
, where
Q(x)=1
for
x<0
and some external field
Q(x)
for
x\geq 0
. For example, if
Q(x)=x^{2}
, the resulting weight is a one-sided Gaussian. This form controls growth on the unbounded side while preserving decay elsewhere. The product becomes nonsmooth at the origin, making it a natural candidate for deep polynomials.

Compared to exponential sums
\sum_{n}a_{n}e^{x_{n}t}
, which also form a dense set, our approach is more stable and structured. Exponential sums are globally supported, ill-conditioned, and require nonlinear fitting of both amplitudes and exponents. In contrast, deep weighted polynomials are division-free, easily parameterized, and can be optimized with far fewer degrees of freedom. We show that this framework achieves uniform approximation for functions that grow on one side and decay on the other. Building on
[
21
]
, we propose a stable parametrization via graph-based optimization. This class of approximants is especially useful for approximating functions like
\log x
, which exhibit asymmetric behavior and arise frequently in applications such as computing
\log\det

[
20
]
.

2
Bernstein’s approximation problem

In 1924, the mathematician Sergei Bernstein
[
1
]
came up with a problem known as “Bernstein’s approximation problem”. In analogy to the Weierstrass approximation theorem which tells us that polynomials are dense in the space of real valued continuous functions on a compact interval, Bernstein asked if there are analogues for the real line? This question has ramifications which still continue to be studied. The first immediate observation is that we have to deal with the unboundedness of polynomials on the real line. Thus the formulation of Bernstein’s problem includes the combination
Sw
, where the function
w
decays fast at
\pm\infty
to counter the growth of the polynomial say
S
. We call the function
w
a weight. We require from
w
that

\lim_{|x|\to\infty}x^{n}w(x)=0,\,n\geq 0
. What can be approximated and in what sense?

A precise statement of Bernstein’s problem is the following. When is it true that for every continuous

f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
with
\lim_{|x|\to\infty}(fw)(x)=0
, there exists a sequence of
polynomials
\left\{S_{n}\right\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
with

\lim_{n\to\infty}||(f-S)w||_{\mathbb{R}}=0
? If true, then we say that Bernstein’s problem has a positive solution. The restriction that the combination
fw
has limit zero for large argument is essential. Indeed, if
x^{n}w(x)
is bounded on the real line for every non-negative integer
n
, then
x^{n}w(x)
has limit zero for large argument and so the same is true for every weighted polynomial
Sw
. So we could not hope to approximate, in the uniform norm a function
f
for which
fw
does not have limit
0
for large argument.

Bernstein’s problem has been studied, solved and extended by many, including for example N.I Akhiezer, K.I Bambenko, L.de Branges, L. Carleson, M.M Dzbasjan, T. Hall, S.Izumi, T. Kawata, S.N Mergelyan and H. Pollard and V.S Videnskii. See for example
[
8
]
and the many references cited therein.

One formulation of an answer to Bernstein’s problem is the following:

(1)

Let
w:\mathbb{R}\to(0,1]
be continuous with

\lim_{|x|\to\infty}x^{n}w(x)=0,\,n=0,1,2,...
. Then Bernstein’s problem has a positive answer iff (a) and (b) below hold:

(a)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\log(w^{-1}(t))}{1+t^{2}}dt=\infty.

(b)

There exists a sequence of polynomials
\{S_{n}\}
of degree at most
n\geq 1
such that
\lim_{|x|\to\infty}(S_{n}w)(x)=0
and with
{\rm sup}_{n\geq 1}||S_{n}w||_{\mathbb{R}}<\infty
.

In particular:

(2)

Let
w:\mathbb{R}\to(0,1]
be even, with
\log(w^{-1}(e^{x})
convex
on
\mathbb{R}

and with
\lim_{|x|\to\infty}x^{n}w(x)=0,\,n=0,1,2,...
. Then Bernstein’s problem has a positive answer iff
\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\log(w^{-1}(t))}{1+t^{2}}dt=\infty.

An immediate observation regarding the condition
\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\log(w^{-1}(t))}{1+t^{2}}dt=\infty
is that
it forces the weight
w
to decrease at least as fast as a polynomial for large argument.

Here is one example:

Let
\lambda\geq 1
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
and let
w_{\lambda}(x):=\exp(-|x|^{\lambda})
. In the literature, these weights (the special case
\lambda=2
is just the Hermite weight)
are examples of what are called Freud weights after the mathematician
Geza Freud
[
12
]
who first investigated them. Here,
w_{\lambda}
is of smooth polynomial decay for large argument and Freud weights are essentially characterized by the property that they are of smooth polynomial decay for large argument.

3.1
The classical case

The classical Jackson approximation theorem tells us for example that on a real interval
[a,b]
(
a,b
finite), uniformly for
n\geq 1
and functions
f:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}
,

E_{n}[f]={\rm inf}_{{deg}S\leq n}||f-S||_{[a,b]}\leq C\left(\frac{b-a}{n}\right)||f^{\prime}||_{[a,b]}

when the right hand side is finite. Here the
{\rm inf}
is taken over the
n\geq 1
dimensional space of algebraic polynomials, which exists from well known arguments in approximation theory. The rate is best possible amongst absolutely real valued continuous functions
f:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}
whose derivative is bounded. More generally, if
f
is continuous with modulus of continuity

w(f,\delta):={\rm sup}\left\{|f(x)-f(y)|:\,x,y\in[a,b]\,{\rm and}\,|x-y|\leq\delta\right\}

in variable
\delta>0
,
we have uniformly for
n\geq 1
and functions
f:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}
,

E_{n}[f]\leq C\left(w\left(f,\frac{b-a}{n}\right)\right)

for which the right hand side is finite.

If
f:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}
has a bounded
k
th (
k\geq 1
) derivative, the minmax rate of approximation as above is uniformly in
n,f,k
:
O\left(\frac{b-a}{n^{k}}\right)
.

Converse Bernstein results are also classical in the following sense. We find it convenient to formulate them for trigonometric polynomials so in this regard, set

E_{n}[g]={\rm inf}_{{deg}S\leq n}||g-S||_{[0,2\pi]}

which exists and the
{\rm inf}
is taken over all trigonometric polynomials
S
of degree at
n,\,n\geq 1
and
g:[0,2\pi]\to\mathbb{R}
is a periodic function.
The following holds: Let
0<\alpha<1
. Then uniformly in
g,n
,

E_{n}[g]=O(n^{-\alpha}),\,n\to\infty,\,{\rm iff}\,w(g,t)=O(t^{\alpha}),\,t\to 0^{+}

where
w(g,.)
is the modulus of continuity of
g
on
[0,2\pi]
.
Thus the error of of approximation of a
2\pi
periodic function
g
on
[0,2\pi]
by trigonometric polynomials of degree at most
n\geq 1
, decays with rate
O(n^{-\alpha})
iff the function
g
satisfies a Lipchitz condition of
0<\alpha<1
. More generally, if
k\geq 1
,

E_{n}[f]=O(n^{-k-\alpha})\,n\to\infty\,{\rm iff}\,w(g^{(k)}),t)=O(t^{\alpha})\,t\to 0^{+}.

3.2
Restricted range inequalities

The best way to motivate weighted Jackson and Bernstein theorems is to look carefully at the Freud weight,

w_{\lambda}(x):=\exp(-|x|^{\lambda})
for
\lambda\geq 1
. Calculations show that the weighted
polynomial
x^{n}w_{\lambda}(x)
achieves its maximum modulus at the so called Freud number

[

showed that there are positive constants

so that uniformly for every polynomial

Outside, the interval

decays quickly to zero for large argument so one cannot hope to approximate

Ditzian and Lubinsky constructed a weighted a modulus of continuity which has such a tail term.
[
7
]

See below.

Inequalities of the form

||Sw_{\lambda}||_{L_{p}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)}\leq C_{1}||Sw_{\lambda}||_{L_{p}\left(\left[-C_{2}n^{\frac{1}{\lambda}},C_{2}n^{\frac{1}{\lambda}}\right]\right)}.

are called restricted range inequalities. The crucial observation is that the right hand side of the estimate involves a sequence of intervals which expand with

as

Sharp forms of the restricted inequality above exist for all

for large classes of weights.
See for example,

Let
w=\exp(-\Phi):\mathbb{R}\to(0,\infty)
with
\Phi

w

Assume

is positive and increases on
(0,\infty)
with limits

and

and

\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{at\Phi^{\prime}(at)}{\sqrt{1-t^{2}}}dt=n,\,a>0

with degree at most

||p_{n}w||_{\mathbb{R}}=||p_{n}w||_{[-a,a]}

Consider again the even Freud weight

Then for every polynomial

of degree at most

||p_{n}w_{\lambda}||_{\mathbb{R}}=||p_{n}w_{\lambda}||_{[-a,a]}

with

\gamma_{\lambda}:=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma\left(\frac{\lambda}{2}\right)}{2\Gamma\left(\frac{\lambda+1}{2}\right)}

In particular:

||p_{n}\exp(-|x|^{2})||_{\mathbb{R}}=||p_{n}\exp(-|x|^{2})||_{[-\sqrt{n},\sqrt{n}]}

||p_{n}\exp(-|x|)||_{\mathbb{R}}=||p_{n}\exp(-|x|)||_{[-\frac{\pi n}{2},\frac{\pi n}{2}]}.

Forward and Converse Jackson and Bernstein: Freud weights

In formulating forward and converse Jackson and Bernstein theorems for Freud weights, see
[

For a real valued function
f:[-1,1]\to\mathbb{R}
, we recall for
h>0
and
k\geq 0
the
k

\Delta_{h}^{k}f(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{k}{i}(-1)^{k}f(x+\frac{kh}{2}-ih)
. (In our approximation, if any of the arguments of

lie outside
[-1,1]
, we take the difference as 0.)

We will use for our motivating example
w_{\lambda}(x):=\exp(-|x|^{\lambda})
with
\lambda>1
. See
[

]
. We restrict ourselves to
p=\infty
.

The first order modulus of continuity is defined for suitable
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
as:

w_{1,\infty}(f,w_{\lambda},t):={\rm sup}_{0<h\leq t}||w_{\lambda}\Delta_{h}(f)||_{[-h^{\frac{1}{1-\lambda}},h^{\frac{1}{1-\lambda}}]}+{\rm inf}_{C\in\mathbb{R}}||(f-C)w_{\lambda}||_{\mathbb{R}\setminus([-h^{\frac{1}{1-\lambda}},h^{\frac{1}{1-\lambda}}])}.

The inf in the tail term ensures that if

is constant, the modulus vanishes identically as one expects from a first order modulus. Notice that if we substitute
h:=n^{-1+\frac{1}{\lambda}}
then
[-h^{\frac{1}{1-\lambda}},h^{\frac{1}{1-\lambda}}]=[-C_{1}a_{n},C_{1}a_{n}]
.

More generally, if
r\geq 1
, the

w_{r,\infty}(f,w_{\lambda},t):={\rm sup}_{0<h\leq t}||w_{\lambda}\Delta_{h}^{r}(f)||_{[-h^{\frac{1}{1-\lambda}},h^{\frac{1}{1-\lambda}}]}+{\rm inf}_{{\rm deg}R\leq r-1}||(f-R)w_{\lambda}||_{\mathbb{R}\setminus([-h^{\frac{1}{1-\lambda}},h^{\frac{1}{1-\lambda}}])}.

For
0<\alpha<r
, the following holds uniformly for

First:
E_{n}[f,w_{\lambda}]\leq Cw_{r,\infty}(f,w_{\lambda}\left(\frac{a_{n}}{n}\right))\leq C_{1}\left(\frac{a_{n}}{n}\right)^{r}||f^{(r)}w_{\lambda}||_{\mathbb{R}}

4
Weighted Deep Polynomials

Moving forward, given a fixed weight
w
, a weighted deep polynomial is defined as

Q(x)\;=\;\bigl[w(x)\bigr]^{\alpha}\cdot P(x),

where the exponent
\alpha
is typically chosen to depend on
D
(for instance,
\alpha=D
). The rationale is that raising the weight to a high power localizes the effective region of approximation.
We note that as before, we will choose the weight
w
to balance the growth of
P
for large argument.

For different classes of real valued functions
f
and weights
w
, we now show that
numerically weighted deep polynomials capture
f
extremely well in the uniform norm in particular, for large argument.

We now study this problem in detail.

5
Existence and Uniqueness for Weighted Deep Polynomials

Theorem 5.1

(Existence and Uniqueness)
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}
be a compact set and let
w\colon K\to[0,\infty)
be a continuous weight function. Fix
D\in\mathbb{N}
and consider the space
\mathcal{Q}_{D}
of weighted deep polynomials of composite degree at most
D
. That is, each
Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{D}
has the form

Q(x)\;=\;[w(x)]^{\alpha}\;\Bigl(p_{1}\circ p_{2}\circ\dots\circ p_{L}\Bigr)(x),

where
\deg(p_{1})\,\deg(p_{2})\cdots\deg(p_{L})\,\leq D
and
\alpha
is a nonnegative exponent that may depend on
D
. Endow
\mathcal{Q}_{D}
with the weighted supremum norm

\|f\|_{\infty,w}\;=\;\max_{x\in K}\,\bigl|w(x)\,f(x)\bigr|.

Then, for any continuous function
f\colon K\to\mathbb{R}
and any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a
Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{D}
such that

\|\,f-Q\|_{\infty,w}\;<\;\varepsilon.

Moreover, there always exists a best approximant

\displaystyle\min_{Q\,\in\,\mathcal{Q}_{D}}\|f-Q\|_{\infty,w}
.
Uniqueness of this best approximant, however, may fail in the weighted supremum norm unless additional convexity or normalization conditions are imposed.

Proof.

Existence.
By standard results in classical approximation theory, ordinary (single-layer) polynomials of degree at most
D
are uniformly dense in the space of continuous real-valued functions on
K
when measured with a suitable weighted supremum norm. In particular, the same holds if one includes the weight factor
[w(x)]^{\alpha}
but sets the final outer layer
p_{L}(x)\equiv x
(i.e. the identity map). This construction recovers every single-layer polynomial of degree at most
D
as a special case of a deeper polynomial composition. Consequently, for each
\varepsilon>0
, one can approximate any
f\in C(K)
arbitrarily well by such a weighted deep polynomial.

Next, to show the existence of a best approximant, note that each weighted deep polynomial
Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{D}
is determined by finitely many coefficients (the polynomial coefficients within each layer, plus the exponent
\alpha
if it is fixed). Standard compactness arguments in finite-dimensional parameter spaces imply that one may choose bounds or normalizations on these coefficients (for instance, normalizing leading or trailing terms) so that the feasible set is compact
[
19
]
. Since the map
\bigl(\text{coefficients}\bigr)\mapsto\|f-Q\|_{\infty,w}
is continuous, it achieves its minimum on that compact set, thus guaranteeing a best approximant
Q^{*}
.

Uniqueness.
In strictly convex norms, best approximants in finite-dimensional subspaces are unique. However, under the weighted supremum norm
\|\cdot\|_{\infty,w}
, there may be multiple distinct functions in
\mathcal{Q}_{D}
that achieve the same maximum deviation from
f
. Indeed, classical Chebyshev (or sup-norm) approximation theory shows that even for single-layer polynomials, uniqueness can fail unless the polynomial space is constrained in a way that enforces strict convexity. The same reasoning extends directly to weighted deep polynomials. Namely, if the admissible compositions are large enough to achieve the same extremal error profile, multiple distinct composite polynomials may tie for best approximation. In settings where the norm or the deep polynomial structure is made strictly convex (by additional constraints or by selecting a strictly convex
L^{p}
-type norm on
K
), uniqueness follows immediately from the usual finite-dimensional convex-optimization arguments.

These observations establish both the universal approximation property (arbitrary closeness to
f
) and the existence of at least one minimizer in
\mathcal{Q}_{D}
, while clarifying the conditions under which that minimizer may or may not be unique.

6
Asymmetric weighted approximation

Consider approximating the function

f(x)=e^{-x},

which exhibits contrasting behavior on the two halves of
\mathbb{R}
: for
x<0
the function diverges (since
e^{-x}=e^{|x|}
) while for
x\geq 0
it decays. To obtain a finite approximation error over
\mathbb{R}
, the approximant must mimic this behavior.

We define the weight via a ReLU-based function:

w\bigl(\operatorname{ReLU}(x)\bigr)=\begin{cases}1,&x<0,\\[2.84526pt]
e^{-x^{2}},&x\geq 0.\end{cases}

Then, the weighted deep polynomial approximant is given by

Q_{n}(x)\;=\;\Bigl[w\bigl(\operatorname{ReLU}(x)\bigr)\Bigr]^{n}\,P(x),

where the exponent
n
is tied to the degree of the deep polynomial. For
x<0
the weight is constant so that
Q_{n}(x)=P(x)
can grow to match the divergence of
e^{-x}
; for
x\geq 0
the weight becomes

e^{-nx^{2}},

which decays rapidly as
n
increases. In effect, significant contributions for
x\geq 0
occur only when
n\,x^{2}
is small, i.e. for
x
of order
1/\sqrt{n}
.

A change of variables
y=\sqrt{n}\,x
(so that
dx=dy/\sqrt{n}
) transforms the error on
[0,\infty)
to

E_{n}^{+}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\Bigl|e^{-y/\sqrt{n}}-P\Bigl(\frac{y}{\sqrt{n}}\Bigr)e^{-y^{2}}\Bigr|^{2}\,dy.

Since
e^{-y/\sqrt{n}}
converges uniformly to 1 on any fixed interval as
n\to\infty
, the dominant contribution arises from a bounded region in
y
. Appropriately choosing
P(x)
(via rescaling) thus ensures that the error in approximating
f(x)
can be made arbitrarily small.

6.1
Effective Interval and Endpoint Localization

Due to the rapid decay of the weight for
x\geq 0
, the approximation is effectively localized to a small interval near the origin. After rescaling with
y=\sqrt{n}\,x
, the significant region is confined to an interval
[0,a]
in the
y
-variable. This endpoint
a
is determined by the condition

\int_{0}^{a}\frac{\Phi^{\prime}(t)}{\sqrt{a^{2}-t^{2}}}\,dt\;=\;\frac{\pi}{2},

(1)

where
\Phi(t)=t^{2}
plays the role of an external field. Changing variables via
t=au
yields

a\int_{0}^{1}\frac{2au}{a\sqrt{1-u^{2}}}\,du=\;2a\int_{0}^{1}\frac{u}{\sqrt{1-u^{2}}}\,du=\;2a.

Thus, condition (
1
) implies

2a\;=\;\frac{\pi}{2}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad a=\frac{\pi}{4}.

In the original
x
-variable, the effective interval is then approximately

\left[0,\frac{\pi}{4\sqrt{n}}\right].

This localization is key to ensuring that the weighted deep polynomial
Q_{n}(x)
converges to
f(x)
as
n\to\infty
.

7.1
Algorithm for Deep Weighted Polynomial Approximation

We seek to approximate a real-valued function
f(x)
by a
deep weighted polynomial
of the form

Q(x)\;=\;w(x)^{\gamma}\,h^{(L)}(x),

where the weight
w(x)\colon[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is typically chosen to enforce exponential decay or localization, and
\gamma\geq 0
is a tunable exponent. The function
h^{(L)}(x)
is the output of a scalar-valued
computational graph
of depth
L
, where each layer is constructed recursively via the polynomial update

h^{(\ell)}(x)\;=\;\sum_{i=0}^{w_{\ell}-1}a^{(\ell)}_{i}\left(h^{(\ell-1)}(x)\right)^{i},\quad h^{(0)}(x)=x,

and the parameter vector
\theta=(a^{(1)},a^{(2)},\dots,a^{(L)})\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{deep}}}
comprises all coefficients. This approach generalizes the framework introduced by Jarlebring et al.
[
21
]
, where the computational graph formalism is used to represent and differentiate matrix functions. Here, we specialize it to nonlinear polynomial approximants over one-dimensional input, optimizing over the coefficient vector
\theta
to minimize the weighted least-squares loss

L(\theta)\;=\;\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(Q(x_{i};\theta)-f(x_{i})\right)^{2}\Delta x,

where
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}\subset[a,b]
is a uniform sampling grid and
\Delta x=(b-a)/N
is the quadrature weight. The loss is minimized using gradient-based optimization, and gradients
\nabla_{\theta}L
are computed efficiently by automatic differentiation through the computational graph.

\{w_{\ell}\}
and total parameter count

are specified in advance. The graph consists only of multiplication and linear-combination operations, making it amenable to efficient symbolic or automatic differentiation. To avoid local minima, we perform
R
random restarts with Gaussian initialization of the coefficients. For each restart, we iteratively minimize the loss
L(\theta)
by backpropagating the residuals through the graph
[
21
]
and updating the parameter vector. The best set of coefficients
\theta^{*}
is retained. The weight
w(x)^{\gamma}
introduces asymmetry into the approximation, focusing accuracy in regions where
w(x)
is large—typically those with challenging function behavior—without increasing global polynomial complexity.

7.2
Exponential Function

Polynomials fail to approximate functions on unbounded domains because any non-constant polynomial
p(x)
diverges to
\pm\infty
as
x\to\pm\infty
. For example, only the constant polynomial can approximate
e^{x}
with finite error on
(-\infty,0]
; therefore, the minimax error is no less than
1/2
, since
e^{0}=1
while
e^{-\infty}=0
. This challenge highlights the limitations of classical approximation theory in settings where the target function exhibits extreme or asymmetric behavior at infinity.

As a first example, we consider a function that exhibits two distinct behaviors: it diverges exponentially as
x\to-\infty
, and decays rapidly as
x\to+\infty
. Such discontinuous structure, particularly in the derivatives, is well-suited to deep polynomial approximants.

In our approach, the weight function is defined asymmetrically as

w(x)=\begin{cases}1,&x<0,\\[2.84526pt]
e^{-x^{2}},&x\geq 0,\end{cases}

so that the overall approximant takes the form

Q(x)=w(x)\cdot q\bigl(p(x)\bigr).

Although
w(x)
is continuous, and its first derivative satisfies
w^{\prime}(0^{-})=w^{\prime}(0^{+})=0
, the second derivative is discontinuous at
x=0
. Explicitly, we have

w^{\prime\prime}(x)=\begin{cases}0,&x<0,\\
(4x^{2}-2)e^{-x^{2}},&x\geq 0,\end{cases}

so that
w^{\prime\prime}(0^{+})=-2
. This creates a challenge for smooth approximation, especially near
x=0
, which requires the composition
q(p(x))
to adapt across a non-smooth interface.

To ensure that the deep polynomial
q(p(x))
accurately tracks
f(x)=e^{-x}
across both regions, it must satisfy

Q(x)=\begin{cases}q(p(x))\approx e^{-x},&x<0,\\[2.84526pt]
e^{-x^{2}}\cdot q(p(x))\approx e^{-x},&x\geq 0,\end{cases}

which is equivalent to enforcing

q(p(x))\approx\frac{e^{-x}}{e^{-x^{2}}}=e^{-x+x^{2}}\qquad\text{for }x\geq 0.

Thus, the same deep polynomial
q(p(x))
must serve the dual role of approximating
e^{-x}
on the left and
e^{-x+x^{2}}
on the right. This type of behavior cannot be captured by a single unweighted polynomial of modest degree, given minmax approximation relies on equioscillation, but arises naturally from the compositional structure of our deep graph-based approximation. The ability to resolve such non-smoothness is a key strength of the deep polynomial approach
[
19
]
.

7.3
Airy Function

We consider the Airy function of the second kind, denoted
\mathrm{Bi}(x)
, which arises in a variety of physical applications. It satisfies the second-order linear differential equation

y^{\prime\prime}(x)-xy(x)=0,

and admits the integral representation

\mathrm{Bi}(x)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[\exp\left(\frac{1}{3}t^{3}+xt\right)+\sin\left(\frac{1}{3}t^{3}+xt\right)\right]\,dt.

The function
\mathrm{Bi}(x)
grows rapidly for large positive
x
, while exhibiting oscillatory behavior for negative
x
. This asymmetry and analytic complexity make it a compelling test case for structured approximants.

Airy functions appear prominently in semiclassical quantum mechanics, especially in potential well and tunneling problems, and also in wave propagation near turning points. Due to their special-function status, they are generally computed using library routines or special-purpose quadrature, making them computationally expensive to evaluate repeatedly in applications.

We construct approximants of the form

Q(x)=w(x)^{\gamma}\,q(p(x)),

where
p(x)
is a normalized inner polynomial,
q(\cdot)
is an outer polynomial, and
w(x)
is a weight selected to suppress growth or oscillation in regions of lesser importance. By adapting the composite structure to the underlying behavior of
\mathrm{Bi}(x)
, we find that deep weighted polynomials can match or exceed the performance of conventional polynomial approximants using far fewer degrees of freedom.

7.4
Generalized symmetric Freud weight on
\mathbb{R}
.

Consider the following generalization of the symmetric weight
w_{\lambda}(x):=\exp(-|x|^{\lambda})
for
\lambda>1
.
Let
w=\exp(-Q(x)):\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
where the external field
Q
is even and continuous and satisfies the following conditions:

(a)

Q^{\prime}
exists and is positive in
(0,\infty).

(b)

xQ^{\prime}(x)
is strictly increasing in
(0,\infty)

with
\lim_{|x|\to 0^{+}}xQ^{\prime}(x)=0.

(c)

The function
\frac{xQ^{\prime}(x)}{Q(x)}\sim 1
uniformly
for large enough argument.

Then

\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{atQ^{\prime}(at)}{\sqrt{1-t^{2}}}dt=n,\,a>0

has a unique solution
a=a_{n}
and for every polynomial
p_{n}
with degree at most
n
,
n\geq 1
and

||p_{n}w||_{\mathbb{R}}=||p_{n}w||_{[-a,a]}

Building on the classical Freud weight
w_{\lambda}(x)=\exp(-|x|^{\lambda})
, we extend this notion by optimizing over the two‐parameter family

Q(x)=c\,|x|^{n},\qquad c>0,\;n>1,

so that
w(x)=\exp(-Q(x))
. One checks that:

(a)

Q^{\prime}(x)=cn\,x^{\,n-1}>0
for
x>0
;

(b)

xQ^{\prime}(x)=cn\,x^{n}
is strictly increasing on
(0,\infty)
with
\lim_{x\to 0^{+}}xQ^{\prime}(x)=0
;

(c)

\displaystyle\frac{xQ^{\prime}(x)}{Q(x)}=\frac{cn\,x^{n}}{c\,x^{n}}=n
, hence asymptotically constant.

We then fit
(c,n)
by minimizing the discrete
L_{2}
-error of a deep weighted polynomial approximant to
e^{-x}
on
[-3,20]
. Figure
4
compares the resulting optimized field to the baseline Gaussian choice
(c,n)=(1,2)
. Even with a simpler choice like
(c,n)=(1,1)
, the approximation remains nontrivial—since the weight only acts on
x>0
, the growth for
x<0
must still be matched by the deep polynomial, which has exponential degree with respect to the number of layers. While we use
e^{-x}
as a controlled test case, in practice the growth and decay behavior may be unknown, as in the Airy function. Our goal is to approximate such functions using combinations of elementary functions.
