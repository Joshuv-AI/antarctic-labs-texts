Preliminary evaluation of a tailored difficulty index for long-distance family caregivers of older adults with dementia
Authors: Francesca B Falzarano, Verena R Cimarolli, Catherine Riffin, Sara J Czaja, Kathrin Boerner
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe bulk of research focused on family caregiving for persons with Alzheimer’s Disease and Related Dementias (ADRD) has been limited to those in close proximity to their care-recipient. The unique needs of the growing number of long-distance caregivers (LDCs) remain understudied and unaddressed. Existing measures of caregiver strain, which were primarily developed for and with geographically proximate caregivers, do not adequately capture the unique challenges faced by LDCs (e.g., difficulties with ensuring and monitoring care from afar). Thus, the current study aims to evaluate the suitability and psychometric properties of a caregiving difficulty index tailored for LDCs.MethodsData were from 40 participants enrolled in a pilot feasibility trial of a psycho-educational intervention for burdened LDCs of older adults with ADRD. Participants completed several measures assessing sociodemographic characteristics, caregiver burden, strain (i.e., family and work conflict, role captivity), and the 9-item difficulty index. An exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was conducted to examine the index’s underlying factor structure. Pearson’s correlations were used to assess the relationship among the final items and other validated measures of burden and strain. Cronbach’s alphas were calculated for the total index and each subscale to evaluate internal consistency.ResultsEFA findings revealed that retention of eight out of the original nine items yielded the best model fit, with items loading onto two distinct components capturing general caregiving and distance-specific challenges. Cronbach’s alphas were 0.79 for the total measure and between 0.67–0.80 for its subscales. Overall difficulty scores were also significantly positively related to caregiver burden (r = 0.42) and strain (rs = 0.31–0.44). Results provide initial evidence supporting the index’s reliability and validity.ConclusionFindings provide preliminary support for the potential suitability of the 8-item index for use with LDCs and underscore the importance of tailored assessments to adequately measure the unique challenges associated with the long-distance caregiving experience.Keywords: Long-Distance Caregiving, Family Caregivers, Burden, Strain

Family members and friends undertake roughly 83% of the care required for nearly seven million individuals living with Alzheimer’s Disease and Related Dementias (ADRD) in the United States [1]. The multidimensional responsibilities associated with ADRD family caregiving become increasingly diverse, intensive, and demanding over time as the disease progresses– the stress of which can result in adverse physical and mental health outcomes [10, 20, 28]. Specifically, ADRD caregiving-related stress has been linked to greater levels of burden, depression, and anxiety compared to non-ADRD caregivers of individuals with physical (e.g., stroke) and psychiatric (e.g., schizophrenia) illnesses [24, 30, 31]. This stress, in turn, can negatively affect the quality of care provided to and the quality of life of care recipients (CRs).

Changing demographic and societal trends—including declining birth rates, delayed child-rearing, smaller family sizes, increased workplace mobility, and population migration—have contributed to a growing number of children and aging parents living at a geographic distance from one another [7, 17, 36]. These shifts have increased the population of long-distance caregivers (LDCs) who manage, coordinate, and provide care from afar [7, 8]. While no standardized definition exists, LDCs generally reside at least one hour travel time away (one-way) from their CR [17]. Though estimates vary, recent data suggest that about 15% of ADRD family caregivers in the United States – about two million individuals – are LDCs who reside at least one to two hours away from their CR [2].

Although geographically removed from the direct care environment, the significant barriers imposed by physical distance yield challenges for LDCs distinct from those faced by geographically proximate caregivers [4]. Despite the geographic distance, LDCs engage in a wide range of caregiving activities—such as assisting with instrumental activities of daily living (IADLs; e.g., transportation, medication management), financial management, emotional support, and communicating with formal providers—all during visits to their CR and from afar, that closely mirror the responsibilities fulfilled by local caregivers [6, 9, 15, 22, 26]. Unique geographic distance-related factors, including travel time/duration, mode of transport (e.g., plane), costs, managing the strain of frequent travel, and maintaining emotional and physical connections with the CR, exacerbate care-related stress among LDCs [5, 6, 17]. Such challenges are further compounded when navigating the competing demands of caregiving with other obligations [15, 22]. Consequently, LDCs experience burden, stress, depression, anxiety, helplessness, guilt, and uncertainty [12, 15, 25] and report equal or greater emotional distress compared to their geographically proximate counterparts [11, 23, 35].

The challenges of caregiving are further magnified in the context of ADRD, which heightens the risk for severe burden compared to non-ADRD caregivers [29]. Managing the CR’s cognitive decline and dementia-related behavioral challenges (e.g., agitation, wandering, exit seeking, aggression), becomes increasingly complex when overseeing care from a physical distance. Other barriers, such as the lack of physical presence during healthcare interactions, often impede LDCs’ ability to engage with providers [16, 32, 33]. Although myriad validated instruments measuring caregiving-related burden and strain exist, they have primarily been designed for and psychometrically validated with caregivers who reside near or live with their CRs. Indeed, such measures are limited in capturing distance-related stressors (e.g., emotional impact of physical separation; logistical complexities in care coordination). While addressing gaps in support for LDCs requires tailored intervention strategies, a necessary first step is the development of targeted assessment tools that can capture the distinct caregiving challenges relevant to LDCs.

Study purpose

Given the limitations of existing measures in capturing aspects of distance-related caregiving challenges, this study focused on developing and evaluating the psychometric properties of a tailored, 9-item difficulty index for LDCs. In addition to measuring general caregiving challenges—such as financial, emotional, and physical difficulties—the index includes newly developed items to assess common distance-related challenges based on the most frequently mentioned issues identified by LDCs in our prior work [19]. Thus, the purpose of the current study was to provide preliminary evidence on the psychometric properties of a newly developed caregiving difficulty index designed for LDCs. Specifically, this study aimed to examine the index’s factor structure and provide initial evidence of its reliability and validity.

Method

The sample included 40 participants enrolled in a pilot feasibility trial of a psycho-educational intervention designed to support burdened LDCs of older adults with ADRD (PI: Cimarolli; R21AG069019). Participants completed interviews at pre-intervention (baseline) and post-intervention follow-up (eight weeks post-baseline). See Cimarolli et al. [13] for additional detail. Study activities were approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at the University of Massachusetts Boston (Protocol #: 3147). The present study utilizes data from participant baseline interviews due to the likelihood that the intervention influenced key variables of interest.

Participants

Participants were recruited using a multipronged approach, which included postings made via Alzheimer’s Association’s TrialMatch and Alzheimers.gov Clinical Trials websites, newsletters/listservs, community-based organizations, participant referrals, and professional networks. Prospective participants completed a virtual eligibility screening with the study’s Project Manager. Inclusion criteria required participants to: 1) identify as an LDC providing primary care to a CR with ADRD receiving homecare services; 2) ≥ 21 years-old; 3) reside at least two hours away (by usual means of transportation) from the CR; 4) exhibit significant caregiver burden as determined by a score of 13 + (total score = 48) on the Short Form Zarit Burden Interview (ZBI-12; [3]); 5) speak English fluently; and 6) not be enrolled or planning to enroll in a similar intervention study over the next three months.

Measures

Sociodemographic and caregiving characteristics

Study interviews were conducted via Zoom by the Project Manager. At baseline, participants were administered a series of single-item indicators assessing sociodemographic characteristics (e.g., age, gender, race/ethnicity, education). Additional single-item measures were administered to evaluate caregiving-related characteristics, including the caregiver’s kinship to CR, distance to CR in miles, travel time, and travel modality. The below-listed scales were also administered at baseline.

Long-distance caregiving difficulty index

Participants completed a 9-item index assessing the difficulty experienced with various aspects of the caregiving role. Three items were derived from the National Study on Caregiving (NSOC; [18, 21]); the remaining six items were developed based on the most frequently mentioned challenges identified via an open-ended question administered to 304 LDCs in our prior research [19]. The six long-distance caregiving-related items were reviewed for their clarity and relevance by an advisory committee of long-distance caregiving experts, including LDCs, and research and practice experts. The group was convened to support the development of the intervention and survey instruments for the current study. The final 9-item index thus measured difficulty across two hypothesized subscales: 1) general caregiving (3 items), which included challenges associated with the financial, emotional, and physical aspects of caregiving; and 2) long-distance (6 items). The long-distance items assessed difficulty with 1) accomplishing caregiving tasks from a distance, 2) monitoring care from a distance, 3) ensuring basic care from a distance, 4) dealing with inadequate care, 5) finding connection with the CR, and 6) traveling to the CR. Participants rated their perceived level of difficulty for each item on a Likert scale ranging from 0 (no difficulty) to 5 (very difficult). Items were summed to form subscale-specific and total scores. Higher scores indicated greater difficulty with caregiving (Cronbach’s alpha (α) = 0.78 for full scale).

Caregiver burden

The 12-item Zarit Burden Interview (ZBI-12) assesses perceived caregiver burden related to emotional and role strain [3]. Participants are asked to indicate how often they experience aspects of burden on a Likert scale ranging from 0 (never) to 4 (nearly always). Responses were summed, with total scores ranging from 0–48. Higher scores indicated higher levels of burden (α = 0.79).

Caregiver strain

Family conflict

A 4-item scale was used to measure the level of disagreement experienced with family members related to 1) the seriousness/safety of the CR’s illness, 2) family members’ attitudes toward the CR, and 3) family members’ attitudes toward the caregiver [27]. Response options ranged from 1 (no disagreement) to 4 (quite a bit of disagreement). Items were summed, with higher scores indicating higher family conflict (α = 0.83).

Job-caregiving conflict

Employment-related role conflicts were assessed via five items asking participants to indicate their level of agreement with statements such as “you have had less energy for your work” and “you’ve been dissatisfied with the quality of your work” [27]. Responses were rated on a Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), with higher scores reflective of greater family conflict (α = 0.67).

Role captivity

Three items were administered to assess the extent to which caregivers feel trapped in their role [27]. Participants rated the relevance of each statement using a four-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (not at all) to 4 (very). Sample items included “How much do you wish you were free to lead a life of your own?” and “How much do you feel trapped by your CR’s illness?” (α = 0.82).

Data analysis plan

Descriptive statistics (e.g., means, standard deviations, skewness, kurtosis) were calculated for all key study variables to screen for univariate outliers and ensure normality. Data were inspected via boxplots and calculated z-scores, with values > 3.29 used as a threshold to identify univariate outliers [34]. An exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was performed to evaluate the factor structure of the items, hypothesized to consist of two subscales: general caregiving and long-distance-specific difficulties. Principal axis factoring with Promax (oblique) rotation, which allows for correlated factors, was used to conduct the EFA. The number of factors retained was based on Eigenvalues greater than one and scree plot inspection. Several metrics were examined to ensure the minimum criteria were met to justify EFA as our analytic approach, including the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) statistic (while a minimum value of 0.50 is typically acceptable, a more conservative threshold of 0.60 was used to indicate sufficient sampling adequacy for factor analysis) [37]. Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity was also used to determine whether the variables are sufficiently interrelated to justify factor analysis (p < 0.05). Communalities for all items were examined, with values greater than 0.40 indicating adequate shared variance to contribute to a meaningful factor structure [14]. Factors with eigenvalues greater than 1.0 and items with pattern matrix loadings ≥ 0.40 were retained. Factor correlations were also examined to verify the appropriateness of oblique rotation. Items not meeting these criteria were removed, and the EFA was repeated to determine whether exclusion resulted in improved model fit.

The psychometric properties of the final set of items were subsequently explored. Pearson’s correlations between total difficulty scores and subscale-specific scores were conducted to evaluate the measure's structure and assess how well the subscales represent the overall construct of caregiving difficulty, providing initial evidence of construct validity. Reliability estimates were calculated via Cronbach’s alpha to examine internal consistency. To explore concurrent validity, Pearson’s correlations were also analyzed with other key variables of caregiver burden and strain (e.g., job-caregiving conflict). We also examined Kendall’s Tau (τb) correlation coefficiants to verify robustness. Missing data were handled using listwise deletion. Analyses were conducted using SPSS Version 29.0; A p-value of 0.05 was used for all analyses.

Results

Descriptive characteristics

Sample characteristics and descriptive statistics for key study variables are presented in Tables 1 and 2, respectively. All variables were normally distributed, and no outliers that warranted removal or transformation were identified; thus, all data were retained for analysis. Participants (N = 40) were between 28 and 77 years old (Mage = 51.3, SD = 11.6). Most (90%) were female and adult child caregivers (92.5%). Over half (67.5%) of participants reported White as their race/ethnicity, the remainder of participants identified as Hispanic/Latino (20%), Asian (7.5%), multi-racial (2.5%), or Black/African American (2.5%). LDCs reported living an average distance of 1093 miles from their CR. Half of the sample reported air travel as their primary mode of transportation, while the other half relied on a combination of plane, bus, or car travel to visit the CR.

Factor analysis

Using the NSOC and LDC-specific items as a composite indicator of caregiving difficulty, an EFA was conducted to assess the factorability of the 9-item index. PAF was used as the extraction method with Promax (oblique) rotation. EFA findings indicated that the nine items yielded an adequate model fit. The KMO test of sampling adequacy was 0.64, which is above the minimum recommended threshold of 0.60, and Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity (χ2 (36) = 94.03, p < 0.001) was significant. Communalities were above 0.40 (between 0.44 and 0.64), confirming adequate shared variance among the items [14]. Based on the above criteria, factor analysis was deemed suitable to evaluate the full 9-item index.

Two factors with eigenvalues greater than 1 were extracted, accounting for 65.51% of the total variance (ranging from 1.37 to 3.55). The two factors were moderately correlated (r = 0.39), supporting the use of oblique rotation. Table 3 displays communalities, factor loadings, and cross-loadings for the initial EFA conducted with the nine measurement items. One item (“Difficulty traveling to the CR”) did not significantly contribute to the factor structure and failed to meet the minimum criteria of one primary factor loading with no cross-loadings above 0.30 – with loadings of 0.31 for Factors 1 and 2, respectively. Based on these initial findings, the item was excluded from a subsequent factor analysis to determine whether their removal improved the model's fit.

The four long-distance caregiving difficulty items (accomplishing tasks, monitoring care, dealing with inadequate care, and ensuring basic care) all loaded onto the component reflecting long-distance caregiving (factor loadings range from 0.62 to 0.82), while the remaining items significantly loaded onto the component reflecting general caregiving challenges. The factor loadings of the three NSOC items ranged from 0.55 to 0.82. Although “difficulty finding connection with CR” was initially included as a long-distance challenge, it exhibited a primary factor loading of 0.67 on the general caregiving component. Overall, findings support two distinct factors underlying LDCs’ responses to the 8-item difficulty indicator. Although one of the nine originally proposed items was eliminated, the hypothesized two-factor structure was retained.

Psychometric properties of the 8-item long-distance caregiving difficulty index

Measure structure

Pearson’s correlations were conducted to explore the bivariate associations between total difficulty scores and subscale-specific scores to assess the measure's structure and evaluate how well the subscales represent the overall construct captured by the full index. Total difficulty scores were highly correlated with both the general caregiving (r = 0.81) and long-distance (r = 0.88) subscales (ps < 0.001). The two subscales were moderately correlated (r = 0.43, p < 0.006).

Internal consistency

Cronbach’s alphas were calculated for the 8-item measure to examine reliability, the results of which demonstrated to be acceptable (α = 0.79). Cronbach’s alphas for the two subscales assessing general caregiving (four items) and distance (four items) challenges were 0.67 and 0.80, respectively.

Concurrent validity

Correlations were examined among the 8-item difficulty index and several measures of caregiver burden (assessed via the ZBI-12) and strains (assessed using the Pearlin’s Family Conflict, Job-Caregiving Conflict, and Role Captivity scales) to evaluate concurrent validity (see Table 5). Total difficulty scores were moderately associated with greater burden (r = 0.42, p = 0.007). Further, total and subscale-specific difficulty scores demonstrated moderate relationships with family conflict (rs = 0.35–0.44) and job-caregiving conflict (r = 0.33–0.41). Additionally, the general caregiving subscale was significantly moderately associated with role captivity (r = 0.44) and burden (r = 0.52). As an additional robustness check, we also examined Kendall’s tau-b correlations, the results of which were consistent with Pearson’s correlations (Table 6). The overall pattern of results suggests that the total difficulty index and its two subscales are associated with well-established indices of caregiver burden and strain, providing initial evidence of concurrent validity. Discussion

As cases of ADRD continue to rise, shifting demographic trends portend an increasing number of individuals managing care from a distance. LDCs represent a growing segment of the family caregiver population and perform a broad array of care tasks, often as primary caregivers. Similar to their geographically proximate counterparts, LDCs experience significant burden, strain, and depression [15, 23, 38]. Managing the logistical, emotional, and relational aspects of providing care to an older adult with ADRD from a significant distance away can breed distinct forms of strain unique to the long-distance caregiving experience [23]. While several well-validated tools exist to assess burden and strain among proximate family caregivers, there are a lack of measures specifically designed to address the unique difficulities associated with long-distance caregiving. This gap impedes the ability to assess LDCs' specific needs and provide access to tailored support, ultimately increasing the risk of poor outcomes for this growing population of family caregivers.

To fill this gap, the current study provides initial insights into the factor structure, reliability, and validity of an 8-item index designed to assess caregiving difficulties among LDCs. EFA findings indicated that retaining eight of the proposed nine items across two components resulted in the most optimal model fit. Four items specific to long-distance caregiving (accomplishing tasks, dealing with inadequate care, monitoring care, and ensuring basic care) loaded strongly onto one component, while the remaining items represented general caregiving challenges. Interestingly, although "difficulty finding connection with the CR" was initially conceptualized as a long-distance challenge, it loaded more strongly onto the general caregiving factor. This suggests that difficulty connecting with one’s CR is a challenge that may be inherently tied to the ADRD caregiving role rather than being a result of living apart, underscoring that this challenge may be common across ADRD caregiving contexts, regardless of geographic proximity.

Secondly, the study provides preliminary evidence of the index’s reliability and validity. Analysis showed strong associations between the total and subscale-specific difficulty scores, suggesting that these items contribute meaningfully to the overall construct of caregiving difficulty in a sample of LDCs. Reliability estimates for the 8-item index indicated acceptable internal consistency for the full index and for both the general caregiving and long-distance difficulty subscales. Findings also showed that the 8-item index strongly correlated with validated caregiver burden and strain measures, supporting the tool’s concurrent validity. The general caregiving difficulty subscale was significantly associated with burden (ZBI-12) and three strain measures (job-caregiving conflict, family conflict, role captivity). Similarly, the total difficulty score was significantly related to burden, family conflict, and job-caregiving conflict. The long-distance subscale showed significant correlations with family and job-caregiving conflict, suggesting that long-distance caregiving may uniquely impact family dynamics and employment-related role conflicts. The finding that the long-distance subscale was not related to burden and role captivity is not entirely unexpected given that these existing measures were not designed to ascertain the specific challenges imposed by long-distance caregiving. Nonetheless, the significant relationships observed between the full index and validated measures of burden and strain highlight the distinct contributions of both subscales in assessing caregiving difficulties among LDCs. Thus, these findings provide preliminary evidence supporting the index’s reliability and validity in capturing caregiving difficulties among LDCs. Upon further testing of the index, it can be utilized in research and in practice settings to identify burdened ADRD LDCs who may qualify for intervention research and who may benefit from community-based caregiver interventions and supports.

Limitations and future directions

Study findings should be considered within the context of its limitations. The small sample size (N = 40) and small number of items per factor limit the generalizability of the findings and the factor analysis's robustness; thus, results should be interpreted with caution. Sample size limitations precluded conducting a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). Future research should include larger and more diverse samples and employ approaches such as CFA to confirm the tool’s two-component structure and evaluate additional dimensions of construct validity. Additionally, because the study was conducted in the context of an intervention, test–retest reliability could not be examined, limiting the evaluation of the index’s stability over time. While concurrent validity was established, future research should further investigate the index’s psychometric properties (e.g., predictive validity) and explore its applicability across diverse caregiving contexts, accounting for cultural and geographic characteristics, to ensure its applicability and suitability in capturing the unique difficulties associated with the long-distance caregiving experience. Similarly, because the inclusion criteria focused on burdened LDCs, future research should evaluate the index across caregivers with more varied burden levels. Lastly, future research should adopt user-centered design approaches by, for example, reviewing the measure with LDCs to gather their impressions to guide subsequent refinements and modifications. Such feedback can also provide valuable insights regarding the index’s face and content validity and highlight other potential aspects of the long-distance caregiving role that may not be captured by current items.

Conclusion

Overall, the results of the current study provide initial evidence for the potential of an 8-item difficulty index to adequately capture the unique challenges and difficulties faced by LDCs. While preliminary, the LDCare difficulty index offers a promising framework for assessing the multifaceted burdens experienced by the understudied but growing population of LDCs managing care from a distance. These findings mark an important first step toward developing a targeted assessment tool designed to capture the unique experience of long-distance caregiving. Such efforts are essential for enhancing support and well-being among the growing population of LDCs.
