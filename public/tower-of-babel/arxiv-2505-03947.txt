Frog Soup: Zero-Shot, In-Context, and Sample-Efficient Frogger Agents
Xiang Li, Yiyang Hao, Doug Fulop

Abstract
One of the primary aspirations in reinforcement learning research is developing general-purpose agents capable of rapidly adapting to and mastering novel tasks. While RL gaming agents have mastered many Atari games, they remain slow and costly to train for each game. In this work, we demonstrate that latest reasoning LLMs with out-of-domain RL post-training can play a challenging Atari game called Frogger under a zero-shot setting. We then investigate the effect of in-context learning and the amount of reasoning effort on LLM performance. Lastly, we demonstrate a way to bootstrap traditional RL method with LLM demonstrations, which significantly improves their performance and sample efficiency. Our implementation is open sourced at https://github.com/AlienKevin/frogger .



1
Introduction

Atari games have proven to be valuable testing environments for RL algorithms, leading to advancements such as Deep Q Networks
(
Mnih et al. 2013
)
. RL game playing agents are traditionally initialized with zero pre-existing knowledge about a specific game environment and learn to play the game through millions of interactions with the environment. Significant time and compute is often spent exploring states that will not be experienced during high scoring policies. Exploration is particularly challenging in environments that require long horizon action sequences and provide sparse rewards, such as the Atari games and real-world robotics challenges where the state space is too large to effectively sample through free-form exploration.

In this paper we will explore whether pretrained general RL agents like reasoning LLMs can play Atari games and investigate ways to leverage pretrained RL agents to reduce the training samples for training smaller agents from scratch. We first explore whether the contextual understanding and reasoning capabilities of multi-modal LLMs extend to successful zero-shot game play. Next, we analyze the performance of latest reasoning LLMs that are underwent RL post-training in domains such as math and code. We explore a variety of methods for leveraging LLMs with zero-shot and in-context RL and investigate whether LLMs can accelerate trainings of RL agents from scratch. We will evaluate whether our approach can reduce the training time required to reach equivalent performance thresholds to traditional DQN self-play. If this approach to prioritizing exploration is successful then it may accelerate the training of RL agents for other discrete game environments, and we can extend this research to explore opportunities in continuous state space game environments (e.g. 3D action games) as well as simulators for real-world robotics applications.

Background

2.1
RL for Atari Games

Existing RL methods that train from scratch often require massive number of training samples, which makes them resource intensive and limits their application to real-world domains where interaction with the environment is costly. For example, the original Nature DQN
(
Mnih et al. 2015
)
trained for 50 million frames to reach human-level performance.

2.2
Atari Game Selection

Traditional DQN has demonstrated strong performance in Atari games without any prior knowledge, even surpassing human players in some cases. However, certain games pose significant challenges for DQN when they include:

1. Sparse Rewards: Many games only provide rewards at the end of the episode, making it difficult for the agent to learn which actions contribute to success. Key actions often lack immediate feedback but are crucial for long-term success.

2. Exploration Challenges: Large state-action spaces make it inefficient for the agent to learn by random exploration, requiring significant time to discover meaningful trajectories.

3. Long Term Planning: Some games demand strategic foresight and an understanding of game mechanics across multiple phases, which is difficult for a DQN agent to learn without explicit memory or hierarchical decision-making.

While games like Montezuma’s Revenge and Pitfall are famous for their sparse rewards and exploration challenges, the pace of those games are significantly slower than games like Pong and Breakout. After some search, we identified Frogger as a great mix of planning and fast-paced game play. Frogger features a multi-phase gameplay where road-crossing and river-crossing follow different rules, as players must avoid objects while crossing the road but must land on objects while crossing the river. Under a strict 30 seconds time limit and faced with fast moving cars and logs at every turn, the player has to make rapid low-level decisions while planning their future routes maticulously to not be hit by a car or fall into water. While moving the frog up gives a 1 point reward, the most significant rewards are sparse and difficult to reach since the player has to move the frog all the way through 12 lanes to reach the home bays. Additionally, the river section requires sophisticated multi-step planning to land the frog in one of the unoccupied home bays.

2.3
Atari 2600 Frogger Game Mechanics

The objective of the Frogger game is to hop as many frogs to safety as the player can and to score the most points along the way. The frog needs to cross a road with five lanes to reach the riverbank. Then, it needs to cross a river with five lanes dotted with logs and turtles to reach one of the home bays. When all five home bays are filled with frogs, the player receives a hefty reward of 100 points and 2 points for the number of seconds remaining to encourage fast traversals. The player is given at most 30 seconds to move a frog into the home bay and the frog automatically dies on time out. Then, the player moves on to the next level with more and faster cars. Later levels also have alligators in the river and snakes on the riverbank and logs that can bite the player. There is no limit to the number of levels and the player can theoretically earn infinite points if they don’t make a mistake.

3
LLM Agents

Two research questions we have are: ”Can LLMs play Atari games out of the box?” and ”Can LLMs learn the rule of the game through a few trial and errors”. Recent research suggests that this may be possible. Atari-GPT
(
Waytowich et al. 2024
)
demonstrates that modern LLMs like GPT-4o play Atari games like Frogger. The paper highlights the LLMs’ ability to understand contextual game information and provide strategic guidance, something traditional RL agents lack. However, it was shown that the LLMs struggle with low-level controls and often performs worse than traditional RL algorithms like DQN. Digging into the cause of low performance, AtariGPT broke down game playing skills into visual, spatial, strategy, and identification subtasks. GPT-4o was shown to excel in all subtasks except spatial reasoning. In particular, Multimodal LLMs were found to have trouble accurately assessing the distance between game objects.

3.1
Spatial Reasoning of Multimodal LLM

To further investigate whether latest Multimodal LLMs have sufficient spatial reasoning skills for playing Frogger, we tested two latest vision LLMs: gpt-4o-2024-11-20 and Claude-3.7-sonnet. To make sure that the models can see the frames clearly, we tested two frame size configurations: the original 210 by 160 pixels and an upsampled 1024 by 1024 pixels version. The upsampled version not only shows the objects more clearly, but also gives the model more visual tokens and effectively increases the amount of available inference compute. We found that models can identify gaps between the car and the frog in the next lane but have difficulty assessing the size of the gap and ended up bumping into cars by choosing to move up. See Supplement section
5.1
for a detailed case study.

3.2
Object-Centric Atari

To unleash the true performance of LLMs unhindered by the vision encoders, we extract key objects from the frames using the Object-Centric Atari (OCAtari) framework
(
Delfosse et al. 2024
)
. OCAtari extracts a list of key objects along with their categories, x-y locations, and their widths and heights. Notably, even though the category, locations and sizes of the objects are much easier to access under the OCAtari framework, other useful information such as the general divide between the river and the road, the location of the five home bases at the top of the frame, lives left, the time bar, and the total scores are not available to the models. Additionally, OCAtari has shown that object-centric PPO agents perform on par with the pixel-based PPO agents on Freeway, a simplified version of Frogger
(
Delfosse et al. 2024
)
. Hence, we think OCAtari presents a fair environment to compare against pixel-based agents. See the middle of Figure
1
for an example object-centric representation of a single frame.

3.3
Zero-Shot LLM Agents

Since traditional LLMs like GPT-4o have difficulty reasoning through the coordinates, we only report results with the latest reasoning models. These reasoning models are pretrained on massive amount of world knowledge during their pretraining phase and then underwent additional RL post-training to solve challenging math and coding questions. We first test o3-mini on a zero-shot setting without showing it past rewards (rewards: hide). We test three configurations under this setting, one with only the current step’s state and action (past steps: 0), one that additionally includes three past steps’ states and actions (past steps: 3), and one that includes all past steps (past steps: all). The 3 past steps exactly mirrors the common 4 framestack configurations used in DQN works
(
Mnih et al. 2015
)
except past actions are also included in our case. For each of the configurations, we test under low, medium, or high reasoning efforts.
For the zero-shot setting, medium reasoning with 0 past steps achieves the highest episodic reward of 32, reaching as far as the 10th lanes out of the total 12 lanes in the game.

As shown in Figure
2
,
including more past steps counterintuitively leads to decreased performance across levels of reasoning efforts
, evident in the fact that 0 past steps performed the best among configurations with hidden rewards (blue lines are higher in the y axis compared to green and purple lines). Based on analysis of the LLM reasoning traces, we found one potential reason for this counterintuitive result is that the
richer context adds noise and biases the LLM during its reasoning
. We found that even model with high reasoning effort are susceptible to action frequency bias and would prefer taking more risky actions like going up because it was frequently taken in the past. See Supplement section
5.2
for an example prompt and response.

3.3.2
Zero-Shot Performance of Open-source LLM Agent

Recently, open-source reasoning models like QwQ-32B has demonstrated comparable performance to o1 on math, coding, and instruction-following benchmarks. We experiment with the latest QwQ-32B model to investigate the performance of open-source reasoning models on playing the challenging Frogger game. Under setting with 3 past steps and hidden rewards, which is the most common setup for Atari agents, we found that
QwQ-32B scored an episodic reward of 17 points, reaching as far as the 8th lane out of the total 12 lanes
. Given the same setup, o3-mini on low reasoning effort got 13 points, medium got 29, and high got 15. With a median completion token of 6315,
QwQ-32B outputs tokens between the range of o3-mini medium and high reasoning efforts. Its performance is also between these two o3-mini reasoning efforts.
Open-source models like QwQ-32B demonstrate strong game playing capabilities on Frogger and present exciting opportunities for RL fine-tuning, which we leave to future work. See Supplement section
5.6
for LLM outputs at the end of each life.

3.4
In-Context LLM Agents

Recent reasoning LLMs are known for their exceptional in-context learning and reasoning capabilities. To allow the LLM to better reflect on its past policies and improve its game playing strategy, we also test the three configurations above but showing rewards for each past action. We found that the usefulness of showing rewards depend heavily on level of reasoning efforts. On low reasoning effort, showing reward degrades the performance by biasing the model towards taking suboptimal and risky previous actions that earned a positive reward (like in the case of 0 past steps with low reasoning effort, the blue line is above the orange line, see Figure
2
). This bias is alleviated when the past context size increases from 0 to 3. On the other hand
with high reasoning effort, we notice showing past almost always help the model achieve better performance. In the case with 0 past steps, showing the rewards to the LLM in context doubled the episodic reward from 22 to 45
(See Supplement section
5.4
for LLM outputs at the end of each life). In the case with 3 past steps, showing the rewards in context more than doubled the episodic reward from 15 to 35. The only exception is when all past steps are included, where we see a slight decrease in episodic reward. We hypothesize this is due to including rewards for all past steps increased the amount of information the model has to process and are more likely to be biased towards frequent, positive reward actions. Hence, we have shown that reasoning LLMs can learn from past rewards and improve their policy.

3.4.1
Explorative LLM Agent

So far, we have prompted the LLM to choose the best action at every single step. To explore the tradeoff between exploration and exploitation, we alternatively prompt the LLM to explore the environment instead (see Supplement section
5.3
for an example prompt and output). We modify the configuration with high reasoning, all past steps and rewards shown to use a prompt that encourages exploration. This configuration is chosen because exploration only makes sense when the agent has a memory of past states and rewards. We found that
when asked to explore instead of picking the best action, the LLM express an intention to move up towards the river area where it is less explored. However, the overall episodic reward is only 1 point higher (21 vs 20) compared to the configuration with the best action prompt.
We think the main reason for the limited performance of both configurations is current reasoning model’s limitation in analyzing long action sequences. Due to this limitation, risky and suboptimal actions are repeatedly taken despite past exploration showing them as undesirable. See Supplement section
5.5
for LLM output at the end of each life. Full LLM trajectories and game play videos for all configurations are available on HuggingFace at
https://huggingface.co/datasets/AlienKevin/atari-frogger-traces
.

3.4.2
Reflective LLM Agent

Inspired by the Reflexion framework
(
Shinn et al. 2023
)
which allows LLM agents to verbally reflect on task feedback signals, store reflection in memory and improve decision-making in subsequent trials, we design a simplified Reflexion LLM agent for playing Frogger. We first let the model play a full episode of Frogger to completion. Then, the model is asked to reflect on the states, actions, and rewards collected from the past episode and provide a list of observations and advice to improve game play. Equipped with the feedback, the agent is tasked to play the game again. For the experiment, we first reflect on the trajectory sampled by the best configuration (high reasoning, 0 past steps, and rewards shown) and then append the feedback into the prompt to play the game again under the same configuration (see Supplement section
5.7
for the reflection prompt and LLM output). While the LLM feedback includes accurate observations and advice about the game,
the feedback is usually high-level and lacks key information such as the objects on the current lane become invisible and important details such as the exact speed of the cars.
Following the useful advice to align the frog relative to objects on the next lanes but not being told that the objects on the current lane become invisible, the agent repeatedly bumped into cars on the same lane and dropped into the river. Hence,
the performance drops from 45 to 21 despite the additional experience included in the prompt
. See Supplement section
5.8
for LLM outputs at the end of each life)

3.5
Depth-First Search Agent

Atari games run deterministically so an agent can technically memorize the game layout without learning a robust policy. To prevent this exploit, later versions of the Arcade Learning Environment implements sticky actions to inject stochasticity into the game
(
Machado et al. 2017
)
. Sticky actions allow the the environment to execute the agent’s previous action again, instead of the agent’s new action with some probability. To understand the complexity of the action sequence required to play a deterministic version of Frogger, we wrote a Depth First Search (DFS) algorithm that cloned the game environment at every frame to explore each of the 5 available actions. We later discovered that this technique has been applied to other Atari 2600 games under the name ”The Brute”
(
Machado et al. 2017
)
.

For our DFS algorithm, depending on the initial frame used to initiate the search (between 110 and 140), the algorithm could achieve a score of 8 (corresponding to successfully crossing the road then traveling 2 rows across the river) within a total of 35 simulated steps and 90 seconds of real world clock time on a mid-range desktop PC. Randomizing the action selection order did not meaningful impact required exploration steps. However, achieving a score of 9 (progressing one additional block in the water) required a minimum of 1,320 additional simulation steps and 23 minutes. The search to achieve a score of 10 exceeded memory limitations on our PC in our implementation without attempt at optimization. In a default Frogger environment where the Frog has 4 lives, this suggests
a naive policy could be trivially learned that achieves a baseline score between 32 and 36. However, as shown in
(
Machado et al. 2017
)
, ”the Brute” is very sensitive to small perturbations in the environment such as sticky actions and performance drastically decreases under more realistic game environments
with even small amount of randomness. Additionally, the DFS procedure necessitates the storage and reloading of intermediate states, which is not generally available for many realistic environments.

3.6
DQN Agent

Our traditional RL algorithm baseline is Deep Q-Network (DQN). In our implementation, the DQN agent processes the game’s visual observation using a three-layer convolutional neural network (CNN) and makes action decisions using a two-layer fully connected network.

To ensure efficient processing of game images, we apply the following standard preprocessing steps first used by
(
Mnih et al. 2015
)
. First, we convert RGB frames (210x160x3) to grayscale resized images (84x84x1) to reduce conputational cost while preserving essential information. Second, we stack four consecutive frames as input, enabling agent to infer motion dynamics.

Training DQN agents on Atari Games can be computationally expensive, especially for challenging games like Frogger. To establish a benchmark, we use simplified training setup. First, we limited Training Episodes: 5000 episodes to compare traditional DQN and LLM-guided DQN within a practical timeframe. Second, the replay buffer space is constrained to 8000 total time steps (states, action, rewards, next states pair) due to RAM limitation (50GB) for Google Colab.

Inspired by DQfD
(
Hester et al. 2017
)
,
we incorporate LLM-generated demonstrations using Prioritized Experience Replay (PER)
(
Schaul et al. 2016
)
. PER modifies the experience sampling probability by assigning higher priority to important transitions.
For the standard DQN, we setup an empty Priority Replay Buffer and calculate priority value for self-generated trajectories.

p_{i}=(|\delta_{i}|+\epsilon)^{\alpha}

where
\delta_{i}
is the TD-error,
\epsilon
is a small positive value ensuring that no priority is zero, and
\alpha
decides the amount of prioritization. See Figure
4
for a diagram of our DQN system enhanced with LLM demonstrations.

3.7
LLM Demonstrations Guided DQN

In the LLM-guided DQN, the first step is to collect LLM-generated gameplay demonstrations. Based on our previous experiments with LLM agents, we chose o3-mini as our model, and implement object coordinates as inputs to guide decision-making.

The image-to-text conversion process consists of several key steps:

•

Preprocessing the Game Observations. 1. Convert RGB game frames into grayscale 2D arrays of size 210×160 with uint8 pixel values. 2. Crop the image to focus only on the gameplay screen, removing irrelevant UI elements.

•

Binary Image Segmentation. 1. Convert the grayscale image into a binary representation (foreground objects vs. background). 2. Identify objects and apply filters to refine segmentation. Since some vehicles consist of multiple disconnected components (e.g., wheels separated from the main body), we filter out objects smaller than 5 pixels to remove noise.

•

Object Detection & Feature Extraction. For each detected object, compute its center position, width, and height. Apply a specialized frog detection algorithm, since the frog is unique in the game. It is identified as a 7×7 square with a grayscale value of 124.

•

Final Output: Object List & Frog Position. After processing, we obtain a structured list of objects with their bounding box coordinates and classification, along with the frog’s precise location.

we collect expert demonstrations from LLM-generated trajectories. Specifically, we use 10 LLM-played episodes, comprising a total of 1657 time steps
(s,a,r,s^{\prime},d)
, which accounts for
20.7\%
of the prioritized replay buffer. The episodic rewards range from 12 to 35.

With a prioritized replay buffer of size 8000, we initialize the buffer by preloading all expert transitions
(s,a,r,s^{\prime},d)
with initialized high priority value of 5.0, ensuring it is sampled frequently during the early training. Over the course of training, the priorities of these expert samples are dynamically updated based on TD-error as self-experience trajectories and gradually decreases as the network learns from these samples. Once the expert transitions’ priorities become sufficiently low, they are naturally replaced by newly generated agent experiences.

3.8
Experiment results of LLM guided DQN

Over the course of 5,000 episodes, we observed a clear upward trend in both agents’ average rewards, indicating progressive learning.

Initially, when playing randomly, the average reward was 8-10, meaning that the frog typically took only one or two steps forward before failing. Given the scoring mechanism (+1 point per step forward with 5 lives per game), this suggests that the frog struggled to make meaningful moves.

By the end of training, both agents demonstrated improved performance.
standard DQN shows average 15 points which means the frog consistently advanced average two steps or more, mostly remaining on the roadside, rarely frog is able to across road and reach the river. And LLM-guided DQN shows average rewards of 24, which means frog can manage to cross road 2-3 times per episode and died falling into the river. See Figure
6
for an example run of DQN vs LLM-Guided DQN.

Figure 6
:
Example of one episode by DQN vs LLM-Guided DQN

The LLM-guided DQN consistently outperformed the standard DQN in total rewards throughout training.
The performance gap emerged before 1,000 episodes and continued to widen as training progressed. While traditional DQN may eventually catch up given sufficient training time, our results demonstrate that LLM-based demonstrations accelerate learning, enabling the agent to achieve higher rewards within the same number of training episodes.
LLM-Guided DQN achieved 35.3% higher rewards after 5,000 episodes compared to standard DQN and demonstrated faster convergence.
This highlights the potential of leveraging LLM-generated demonstrations to improve the sample efficiency of RL agents. Especially at the beginning of training, when traditional RL agents struggled with random actions, LLM demonstration can show a good starting point.

3.9
Future Research Directions

3.10
RL fine-tuning of Reasoning LLMs

Since small open-source reasoning models like QwQ-32B have demonstrated strong potential on zero-shot game play, we want to explore their capabilities under more configurations, including rewards in context and as part of a reflective agent. We are most excited about the potential to fine-tune such reasoning models on the game and subsequently distill the fine-tuned model into much smaller student models for efficient inference.

3.10.1
LLM-Guided Monte Carlo Tree Search

As we demonstrated with our depth-first search baseline, over half of a level in Frogger can be completed through a recursive search of the state space. However, higher scoring game states remain challenging to achieve, especially when the environment is stochastic. As further research we would implement MCTS and use LLMs to strategically explore the space and prune branches in the tree, which we believe may enable an RL-trained agent to complete the first level for the first time. 4
Conclusion

In this work, we have demonstrated that reasoning LLMs can achieve reasonable performance on the challenging Frogger game when given only the previous states, actions, and rewards. We found that increased reasoning effort correlates with LLM performance but models are sensitive to the number of past steps included in the prompt. On high reasoning effort, having access to past rewards more than doubled model performance. Expanding beyond best next action prediction, we experiment with explorative and reflective LLM agents and found that further research is needed to alleviate bias and balance exploitation and exploration. Lastly, we show traditional RL algorithms like DQN can significantly benefit from LLM demonstrations, achieving 35.3% higher episodic rewards under similar compute budgets.
