Multiomic analyses on the contribution of transposable elements to the cis-regulatory landscape of different types of immune cells
Authors: Cui Du, Hairui Fan, Jiayao Jiang, Juan Yang, Shuai Chen, Nadezhda E Vorobyeva, Congrui Zhu, Liming Mao, Chenxi Li, Yanhua Li, Wenbin Bao, Ming-An Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTransposable elements (TEs) are mobile DNA elements abundant in eukaryote genomes. While recognized as key contributors to cis-regulatory elements (CREs), their association with the cell-specific regulatory landscape of various immune cells remain largely unclear. This study aims at depicting the association of TEs with the CREs in diverse types of human immune cells in peripheral blood, with special focus on monocytes which are crucial for innate immunity.ResultsUsing single-cell multiomic data of human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), we annotated the cell-specific CREs (csCREs) for different immune cell types, including sub-populations of B/T lymphocytes, monocytes, natural killer cells, and dendritic cells. A total of 63 TE families are significantly overrepresented in the annotated csCREs, including 32 primate-specific families belonging to endogenous retroviruses (ERVs). We observed a highly cell-specific TE enrichment profile in the csCREs of different immune cell types, with monocytes exhibiting the highest degree of TE enrichment. Focusing on monocytes, we found over 30% of their epigenetically-annotated enhancers are TE-derived and frequently bound by key myeloid regulators such as PU.1, ATF4 and CEBP proteins. Knockdown of SPI1 (the gene encoding PU.1) impaired the expression of hundreds of immune-related genes, including many associated with TE-derived enhancers. Interspecies comparison further showed that nearly half of human- and mouse-specific monocyte enhancers are created by TE insertions, and TE-derived enhancers correlate with the altered gene expression across species. By using luciferase reporter assay, we further validated the enhancer activity of the ERV elements adjacent to SDSL and TPK1, which are highly expressed in human relative to mouse monocytes.ConclusionsHere, we characterized the highly cell-specific profile of TE-derived CREs in different types of human blood immune cells. Integrative analysis further demonstrates the frequent contribution of TEs in creating monocyte enhancers including those specific for human or mouse, and indicates the potential involvement of TEs in mediating species-specific monocyte gene expression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12285-3.Keywords: Transposable element, Immune cell, Monocyte, Enhancer, Evolution, SPI1

Background

Transposable elements (TEs) are mobile DNA elements constituting approximately half of the human genome [1]. They form two major classes: retrotransposons (Class Ⅰ) replicate (i.e., copy-and-paste) through RNA intermediates to form new insertions, while DNA transposons (Class Ⅱ) usually move across genome through a “cut-and-paste” mechanism [2]. Retrotransposons are more abundant than DNA transposons, and they can be further classified as long terminal repeats (LTRs), long interspersed elements (LINEs), and short interspersed elements (SINEs). The sequences of many TEs – particularly the LTRs for endogenous retroviruses (ERVs) – are rich with transcription factor (TF) binding motifs, thus have an inherent capacity to create cis-regulatory elements (CREs) [3]. Further given their fast-evolving nature, TEs are well-regarded as a major source of genetic novelty [4–6]. To date, there are abundant data linking TEs to mammalian development and diseases [3, 7, 8]. However, how TEs shape the regulatory landscape of various types of immune cells – a highly heterogeneous and fast-evolving system – remains poorly understood.

The mammalian immune system mainly comprises of myeloid cells (e.g., monocytes, macrophages, and dendritic cells) and lymphocytes (e.g., B and T cell subtypes, and natural killer cells), which are differentiated hierarchically from hematopoietic stem cells under the drive of lineage-instructive TFs [9]. The immune system shows remarkable difference across species, human populations, or even individuals [10–13], likely due to persistent pathogen pressure [14]. Increasing evidences suggest that the divergence of CREs (particularly enhancers) – rather than protein-coding regions – is the predominant mediator of phenotypic evolution [15]. With the transcriptomic and epigenomic profiling of diverse immune cells including single-cell sequencing of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) [16–18], it provides the opportunity for the investigation of the regulatory landscape and evolution of different types of immune cells.

The regulatory roles of TEs have captured increasing attention in the past decades. Despite numerous studies linking TEs to immunity, most of them focused on the innate immune response [19]. For example, a seminar study discovered that primate-specific TEs create thousands of interferon (IFN)-stimulated enhancers to drive innate immunity evolution [20]. Later studies further uncovered TE-derived enhancers induced by various pathogens or stimuli in human, mouse, and other mammals [21–24]. Transcriptomic divergence across species is also evident for different immune cells [25–27]. One previous study found that dozens of TE families (particularly ERVs) are enriched in the enhancers of mouse CD8+ T cells, potentially regulating immune gene expression [28]. In addition, an L1M2a-derived enhancer was reported to regulate the expression of the interferon receptor IFNAR1 in human B cells [29]. A recent study also linked TEs to the tissue-adaptation of regulatory T cells [30]. Despite these advances, the contribution of TEs to the regulatory landscape of different human immune cells remains largely unclear.

Here, we characterized the cis-regulatory TE landscape in diverse human immune cells by using single-cell multiomic data, and then focused on monocytes to clarify the contribution of TEs in shaping their cis-regulatory landscape and influencing immune gene expression. The potential involvement of TEs on regulating the transcriptomic divergence of monocyte genes across species was also investigated.

Methods

Cell culture

THP-1 monocyte cell line was purchased from Procell (Wuhan, China). The culture medium consists of RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 0.05 mM β-mercaptoethanol, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S). K562 cells (human myeloid leukemia cells) are kindly provided by Prof. Shaobin Shang, and cultured using the medium consists of RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% P/S. Cells were grown in a constant-temperature incubator at 37 ℃ with 5% CO2.

Gene knockdown

We performed small interfering RNA (siRNA)-mediated knockdown to disrupt SPI1 expression. Human THP-1 cells were grown to 70–80% confluency in 6-well culture dishes. Human SPI1-specific siRNAs were mixed with JetPrime for 15 min. The scrambled RNA was used as vehicle control. The mixture was added to the cell medium to transfect THP-1 cells for 36 h. Finally, the cells were washed and then collected. The efficiency of the siRNA experiment was validated by qRT-PCR and Western blot. The sequence information of the siRNA for SPI1 was listed in Table S1.

Western blot

THP-1 cells were lysed with a RIPA buffer supplemented with phosphatase inhibitors for 20 min on ice, and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm at 4 ℃ for 15 min. After quantified using a BCA protein assay kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China), equal amounts (20 µg) of denatured proteins were loaded on 10% SDS-PAGE gel for electrophoresis, transferred to PVDF membranes (Immobilon, Darmstadt, Germany), and then blocked with 5% skim milk at room temperature for 1 h. PVDF membranes were incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with anti-PU.1 (1:1,000, sc-390405, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA), and then washed with 1×TBST (Solarbio, Beijing, China) before incubation with secondary antibodies (1:5,000). Protein bands were visualized using Enhanced Chemiluminescence (Abbike, BMU102, Wuhan, China) and then exposed with Tanon 4600 (Tianneng, Shanghai, China). HSP90 was used as the internal control.

Luciferase reporter assay

The sequences of selected TE elements (Table S1) were synthesized and inserted into pGL3-basic vectors (GeneCreate, Wuhan, China). K562 cells were co-transfected with pGL3-basic (containing TE sequence or blank control) and pRL-TK vectors. Luciferase activities were measured with the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Vazyme, DL101, Nanjing, China) 24 h after transfection. Data for firefly luciferase activity were normalized to Renilla luciferase activity. All experiments were performed at least three times independently.

scRNA-seq

The single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data for human PBMCs were downloaded from 10x Genomics website (Table S2). Cell Ranger (10x Genomics) was used for demultiplexing, followed by read alignment to human reference genome (GRCh38). Seurat v4.3.0 [31] was used for data filtering, normalization, dimensionality reduction, and clustering processes. Low-quality cells were filtered out based on the criteria: gene count >200, gene count < 20,000, mitochondrial content < 20%.

scATAC-seq

The single-cell assay for transposase-accessible chromatin using sequencing (scATAC-seq) data for human PBMCs were downloaded from 10x Genomics website (Table S2). Cell Ranger ATAC v2.1.0 (10x Genomics) was used for demultiplexing, followed by read alignment to human reference genome (GRCh38). Signac v2.1.0 [32] was used for subsequent analysis. Low-quality cells were removed based on the criteria: peak region fragments >3000, peak region fragments < 30,000, pct_reads_in_peaks >15%, blacklist ratio < 0.05, nucleosome signal < 4, transcription start site (TSS) enrichment >3. After filtering, a total of 110,202 peaks from 8,379 cells remained for latent semantic indexing (LSI) analysis [33]. Clustering and dimensionality reduction were then performed on the corrected LSI components. Finally, we obtained 16 clusters with resolution = 0.5 and dims = 2:30. The activity of each gene was quantified by assessing the local chromatin accessibility, including the gene body and the 2 kb region upstream of TSS. The accessible chromatin peaks for each cell type were identified by using MACS2 [34]. Differential chromatin accessibility analysis was performed by using the FindAllMarkers function, with the settings: Bonferroni-adjusted p-value < 0.05, log2foldchange >0.25. The genomic regions containing accessible chromatin peaks were annotated by ChIPSeeker v1.36.0 [35].

To better interpret the scATAC-seq data, we classified cells based on a scRNA-seq data for matched human PBMC samples. We utilized methods for cross-modality integration and label transfer. The shared correlation patterns between scATAC-seq gene activity and scRNA-seq gene expression were identified by the FindTransferAnchors function (reduction = cca.). Then the cell type label of each cell in scATAC-seq data was predicted by the TransferData function (weight reduction = lsi, dim = 2:30). A total of 7,161 cells were retained after filtering using a maximum prediction score ≥ 0.5.

RNA-seq

Total RNA was isolated from THP-1 cells using Trizol reagent (Qiagen). The mRNA was purified from total RNA using poly-T oligo-attached magnetic beads, and then the fragmented mRNA was used for the synthesis of cDNA via reverse transcription followed by purification. The PCR-amplified cDNA purification was conducted via an AMPure XP system (Beckman Coulter, Beverly, USA), and the obtained cDNA fragments were 250–300 bp long. The libraries were sequenced as 150 bp paired-end reads on BGISEQ-500 platform (BGI-Shenzhen, China).

Raw RNA-seq reads were trimmed with Trim Galore! v0.6.4. Transcript per million (TPM) values were calculated with RSEM v1.3.2 [36]. We aligned trimmed reads to the reference genome of human (GRCh38) or mouse (GRCm38) using STAR v2.7.3 [37] with default settings, and then obtained gene-level read counts by using the featureCount function of subread v2.0.0 [38]. At last, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified with DESeq2 v1.30.1 [39] with the cutoff: FDR < 0.05, |log2foldChange| >1.

ChIP-seq

Chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing (ChIP-seq) was performed in THP-1 cells following a previous study [40]. About 1 × 107 cells were used per reaction. First, the cells were fixed with 1% formaldehyde (252549, Sigma-Aldrich) at room temperature for 10 min. A final concentration of 0.2 M glycine was added to quench the fixation. Nuclear lysates were fragmented by Covaris S220 (Adaptive Focused Acoustics®). For each tissue, 3 µg of H3K27ac (ab177178, Abcam) antibodies were mixed with 25 µL Protein A/G Dynabeads (B23201, Selleck. cn), with rotation at 4 ℃ for 4 h. For per immunoprecipitation, about 50 µg of chromatin was incubated with antibody-bead complexes overnight at 4 ℃. ChIP-DNA was purified by MinElute PCR Purification kit (DC301, Vazyme). ChIP-seq libraries were constructed using the MGIEasy DNA library prep kit (1000006985, BGI-Shenzhen, China), and were sequenced as 100 bp paired-ended reads on BGISEQ-500 platform (BGI-Shenzhen, China).

Raw reads were trimmed with Trim Galore! v0.6.4 and then aligned to the reference genome (GRCh38 for human, GRCm38 for mouse) using Bowtie2 v2.3.5 [41] with default settings. PCR duplicates were removed using the rmdup function of SAMtools v1.13 [42]. After confirming the data reproducibility, reads from biological replicates were pooled together for further analysis. Peak calling was performed with MACS v2.2.6 [34] with settings: --fe-cutoff 2 -q 0.05. The parameters for the corresponding genomes of human (-g hs) and mouse (-g mm) were also specified.

The peaks were further cleaned by removing those that overlap ENCODE Blacklist V2 regions [43]. Differential binding analysis was performed using DiffBind v3.4.11 [44] with settings: minOverlap = 1, summits = 250, method = DBA_EDGER.

ATAC-seq

ATAC-seq data are collected from public studies (Table S2). ATAC-seq data were processed and analyzed following similar procedure as the ChIP-seq data. This included quality control and adapter trimming with Trim Galore!, alignment to the reference genome using Bowtie2, removal of PCR duplicates using the rmdup command from SAMtools, and peak calling using MACS2. Notably, peak calling for ATAC-seq data was performed using the parameters optimized for Tn5-based assay: --nomodel --shift 100 --extsize 200 -q 0.01.

Motif analysis

Single-cell TF motif activity was estimated for a set of 849 TFs from the CIS-BP database (human_pwms_v2) using the RunChromVAR function in Signac v2.1.0 [32, 45]. Differential TF activity between cell types was calculated by the FindMarkers function with threshold: |log2foldChange| >1, Bonferroni-adjusted P < 0.05.

Reference genome and annotation

Reference genome and gene annotation for human (GRCh38) and mouse (GRCm38) were downloaded from the Ensembl database (release 102) [46]. TE annotations were downloaded from the RepeatMasker website (http://www.repeatmasker.org/) on Oct 26, 2018. The clades for ERV families were obtained from the Dfam database [47]. Genome mappability along the reference genome was calculated by using the GEM-mappability program from the GEM suite [48].

Gene Ontology enrichment analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analyses for gene lists were performed by using Metascape [49]. GO enrichment analyses for genomic regions (e.g., peaks and enhancers) were performed with GREAT [50].

Epigenetic annotation of regulatory elements

Different types of regulatory elements are defined based on histone modifications and genomic distribution. Promoter-proximal CREs are defined as peaks that are within 500 bp from TSSs, distal-CREs as peaks that are more than 500 bp away from TSSs, and enhancers as H3K27ac peaks that are more than 500 bp away from TSSs.

Annotation of the genes adjacent to given genomic regions

The genes adjacent to given genomic regions (e.g., peaks, enhancers, ERV elements) were determined by comparing their TSSs against specified genomic regions using the window function of BEDTools v2.29.2 [51]. The TSS annotation file was retrieved from the Ensembl database (release 102) using BioMart [46].

TE enrichment analysis

TE enrichment analysis was performed using the TEENA web server [52]. To control for Family-Wise Error Rate, the calculated p-values were further adjusted with the Bonferroni method.

Interspecies differential expression analysis

Differential expression analysis between human and mouse was performed by using the ExprX package [40]. DEGs were determined by using the threshold: adjusted p-value < 0.05, |log2foldChange| >1.

Statistical analysis and data visualization

All statistical analyses were performed with R statistical programming language. Heatmaps for ChIP-seq and ATAC-seq data were generated using DeepTools v3.5.1 [53]. Heatmaps for RNA-seq data were generated using pheatmap. Representative tracks for RNA-seq, ChIP-seq, and ATAC-seq data were visualized using IGV v2.13.1 [54].

Results

Single-cell profiling uncovers the properties of cell-specific CREs in major human blood immune cell populations

Single-cell sequencing is particularly powerful for analyzing highly heterogeneous immune cell populations [55]. To identify the CREs in major immune cell populations, we analyzed public single-cell multiomic data for human PBMCs (Table S2). After pre-processing, we obtained 9,432 cells for scRNA-seq and 7,161 cells for scATAC-seq. We then annotated 12 major cell populations based on canonical markers, including subsets of T/B lymphocytes, monocytes, NK and pDC (Fig. 1A&B, S1). Subsequently, we annotated a total of 105,171 CREs (i.e., accessible chromatin regions) for these cell populations, with 33.7% (n = 35,443) of them locate in promoters and the remaining usually occur in intronic or intergenic regions (Fig. S2). We further defined cell-specific CREs (csCREs) as those showing significantly higher chromatin accessibility in each major cell population according to the scATAC-seq data. Overall, 28.8% (n = 30,297) of the CREs are annotated as csCREs, with their numbers ranging from 1,463 to 11,297 across different immune cell populations (Fig. 1C, Table S3).

Next, we determined the functional relevance of the annotated csCREs. As expected, the csCREs for various immune cell types are significantly associated with immune-related functions (Fig. 1D, Table S4). For instance, the csCREs for monocytes exhibit high enrichment for immune system processes and immune responses, and those for T cells are enriched for lymphocyte activation and leukocyte differentiation (Fig. 1D). Motif analysis was further performed to identify the regulators of these csCREs, with a list of putative upstream TFs uncovered – including many with well-recognized functions in the corresponding immune cell types (Fig. 1E, S3, Table S5). For example, we uncover the binding motifs of T-box (e.g., EOMES and TBX4/5/21) in the csCREs of NK and T cells [56, 57], and PU.1 (encoded by SPI1) and CEBP proteins in monocytes [9]. Notably, the high expression abundance of these TFs usually coincides with the increased accessibility of their binding motifs in the corresponding cell types (Fig. 1F, S4). Together, by leveraging the single-cell epigenomic data, we annotated and characterized the csCREs for major human immune cell populations.

The csCREs of various immune cell types exhibit distinct patterns of overrepresented TE families

Recent studies uncovered the regulatory roles of TEs in mouse T cells and human B cells [28, 29], yet a detailed comparison across diverse immune cell populations is still absent. Focusing on the immune csCREs annotated in this study (Table S4), we inspected the regulatory involvement of TEs in different human immune cell populations. Given that promoters differ remarkably from other CREs such as enhancers, promoter-proximal and distal csCREs are analyzed separately. We found that most csCREs are distal from promoters (Fig. 2A), and as expected, distal CREs show relatively low sequence conservation and high TE overlapping rate (Fig. S5, S6). Importantly, we demonstrate that high proportions of distal csCREs are derived from TEs (particularly LTRs/ERVs, LINEs, and SINEs) as annotated by the occurrence of TEs on their center, with their percentages ranging from 20.4% in naïve CD4+ T cells to 32.8% in CD16+ monocytes (Fig. 2B). Notably, the distal CREs for the two subsets of monocytes (CD14+ and CD16+ monocytes) both show higher overlap rate with TEs relative to other immune cell populations (Fig. 2B).

We further determined the specific TE families overrepresented in different groups of immune csCREs. In total, we identified 63 TE families significantly enriched in at least one group of distal csCREs, with the majority belonging to LTRs/ERVs (Fig. 2C&D, Table S6). Of note, 32 of the enriched ERV families are primate- or even hominoidea-specific (Table S7). In contrast, promoter-proximal csCREs rarely show TE enrichment, except for LTR71B which shows marginal enrichment in cDC cells. Hence, we focused subsequent analyses on distal csCREs. We observed the evident cell specificity of the TE enrichment signatures. For example, we observed the highest enrichment of LTR4, MER57B2, LTR26, LTR71A and L1MB2 in monocytes, LTR13, LTR2B, LTR77, LTR9 and MER5A in B cells, and LTR12, LTR21B and MER51A in T cells (Fig. 2D). Furthermore, the cell populations can be clearly clustered based on their TE enrichment profiles (Fig. 2E). GO analysis demonstrates that TE-derived csCREs are associated with immune-related functions (Fig. 2F), further supports the regulatory involvement of TEs in different immune cell types. Of note, 51.8% (32/63) of the significantly enriched TE families are primate-specific (Table S7), thus many of their derived csCREs are newly evolved in primates. Together, TEs form numerous immune csCREs with high cell-specificity, indicating their importance in shaping the cis-regulatory networks of different immune cell populations.

TE-derived monocyte CREs show enhancer-like epigenetic signature

Monocytes are myeloid-originated cells with crucial roles in innate immunity [58]. Their transcriptomes differ remarkably across species or even among human populations [25, 27, 59], implying the fast evolution of their transcriptional regulatory landscape. Further given that TE-derived CREs are poorly studied in monocytes relative to T and B lymphocytes, we focus on this specific cell type for in-depth investigation. To get a better resolution of CRE annotation, we integrated multiple sets of bulk ATAC-seq and epigenomic data of human monocytes [18, 60, 61]. According to the ChIP-seq data for multiple histone modifications, distal-CREs often bear high levels of H3K27ac which marks active enhancers and lack the two repressive histone marks H3K27me3 and H3K9me3 (Fig. 3A). Based on the H3K27ac mark, the majority (91.3%) of distal CREs identified in monocytes are annotated as activate enhancers (Fig. 3B).

Focusing on the epigenetically-annotated monocyte enhancers, we found that about one-third of them are derived from TEs (Fig. 3C), which are thereby denoted as TE-derived enhancers (abbreviated as TE-enhancers). In total, we identified 33 TE families significantly enriched in monocyte enhancers (Fig. 3D, S7, Table S8), with 84.8% (n = 28) matching those identified in the csCREs for CD14+ monocytes (Table S6). While most of these enriched TE families belong to LTRs/ERVs (e.g., LTR4 and MER57B2), we also noticed several families of LINEs (e.g., L1MB2), SINEs (e.g., MIR3) and DNA transposons (e.g., MER45B). As expected, monocyte TE-enhancers are also highly associated with myeloid and immune-related functions (Fig. 3E). Further inspection revealed the presence of TE-enhancers near multiple important immune genes, such as LYN, TRAF3, CGAS and MYD88 (Table S9). Of note, CGAS and MYD88 are key genes of the cGAS/STING pathway, which mediates the sensing of intracellular double-stranded DNA molecules [62]. These data suggest that TEs form numerous active enhancers in monocytes, which may potentially influence the expression of adjacent immune genes.

Computational screening uncovers putative regulators of TE-derived monocyte enhancers

After uncovering the enrichment of TEs in monocyte enhancers, we further screened for the TFs that may bind and regulate TE-derived monocyte enhancers. By motif analysis in monocyte TE-enhancers, we uncovered 30 candidate TFs (Table S10), which largely match the differential TF motifs identified by using chromVAR across different immune cell types (Fig. 1E). Multiple of these TFs are known to play critical roles in monocytes. For example, PU.1 is a key regulator for monocyte development and differentiation, and ATF4, CEBPA/B, IRF1, and STAT1/3 are also important for monocytes [63–65]. Importantly, several of these TFs (particularly PU.1 and CEBPA/B) have relatively high expression in monocytes compared to other immune cell types (Fig. S4). Therefore, these TFs are considered as putative upstream regulators of TE-enhancers in monocytes.

We then examined the binding of these TFs in human monocytes based on public ChIP-seq data, focusing on eight TFs including PU.1, CEBPA/B, ATF4, IRF1, SREBF2, and STAT1/3 (Table S2). Most of the binding peaks for these TFs reside outside of promoter regions and show strong H3K27ac mark, indicating they predominantly correspond to active enhancers (Fig. 4A&B, S8). We found that large proportions of their distal peaks overlap TEs (primarily LTRs, LINEs, and SINEs), ranging from 22.3% for STAT3 to 42.0% for CEBPA (Fig. 4C). Among the TEs bound by these TFs, LINE elements have the highest proportion. Specifically, the peaks of these TFs are significantly enriched with dozens of TE families, with the majority belonging to LTRs and LINEs (Table S11). Many of the 33 TE families significantly enriched in monocyte enhancers are also frequently bound by the analyzed TFs (e.g., LTR50, MER57B2, L2/L2b/L2c for PU.1) (Fig. 4D). In particular, 90.9% of the TE families enriched in monocyte enhancers also significantly overlap PU.1 peaks, which is the highest in these TFs (Fig. 4E) – implying the pivotal role of PU.1 in regulation monocyte TE-enhancers. Overall, we identified a set of TFs which are putative regulators of TE-enhancers in monocytes.

Regulation of TE-derived enhancers and immune genes by PU.1 in monocytes

PU.1 is a pioneer TF that has been extensively studied in monocyte and other myeloid lineages [63, 64, 66]. After revealing its prevalent binding on TE-derived monocyte enhancers, we further investigated its regulatory function by performing siRNA-mediated knockdown in THP-1 monocyte cells. The knockdown efficiency was confirmed by qRT-PCR and Western blotting (Fig. 5A&B). We first examined the transcriptomic alterations after SPI1 knockdown (SPI1KD) using RNA-seq (Fig. S9). In total, we identified 1,076 DEGs, with 510 up-regulated and 566 down-regulated after SPI1KD (Fig. 5C, Table S12). As expected, the down-regulated genes are tightly associated with immune-related functions (Fig. 5D), suggesting the critical role of PU.1 for regulating immune gene expression. We then performed H3K27ac ChIP-seq to compare the activation status of PU.1-bound loci before and after SPI1KD. A total of 4,154 genomic loci show altered H3K27ac level (Fig. 5E, Table S13), and 36.4% (n = 1,510) of them are bound by PU.1 (Fig. 5F). These loci are putative target loci under the direct regulation of PU.1. We further compared the loci with increased or decreased H3K27ac levels after SPI1KD and found that significantly higher percentage of H3K27ac-decreased loci are originally bound by PU.1 (Fig. 5G, S10). These data suggest that PU.1 prefers to activate its target loci.

We further investigated whether PU.1 regulates the expression of some immune genes in monocytes through TE-derived enhancers. Focusing on the 681 PU.1-bound loci showing decreased H3K27ac level after SPI1KD, we found that 33.9% (n = 231) of them are derived from TEs (Fig. 5H). Moreover, dozens of TE-derived loci are adjacent to genes down-regulated after SPI1KD (Table S12). For example, an L2b element locates about 2 kb upstream of IL1RN (Fig. 5I), which is a crucial immune gene expressed in monocytes [67]. The H3K27ac level of the PU.1-bound L2b element and the expression level of IL1RN

SPI1KD (Fig. 5I), indicating that this L2b element may mediate the expression of IL1RN under the control of PU.1. PU.1 also binds an L3-derived enhancer to control the expression of IL1B (Fig. 5J), which is a crucial monocyte-secreted cytokine known to be under PU.1-mediated transcriptional regulation [68]. Apart from these two genes, we also identified dozens of immune genes (e.g., CCR2, CYBB, and MSR1) likely under the control of PU.1-regulated TE-enhancers (Table S14). Taken together, these results indicate that PU.1 regulates the expression of many monocyte genes, with some associated with TE-derived enhancers.

Divergence of TE-derived monocyte enhancers between human and mouse

Since many TE families overrepresented in human monocyte enhancers are primate-specific (Table S6), we wonder how the TE enrichment pattern differ across species and if TE-enhancers contribute to the species-specific divergence of monocyte gene expression. For this purpose, we compared the epigenomic and transcriptomic data between the purified canonical monocytes for human (CD14+) and mouse (Ly6C+), respectively (Table S2). We used H3K27ac ChIP-seq data to annotate enhancers and found that high proportions of the epigenetically-annotated monocyte enhancers are not conserved across species (Fig. 6A). Impressively, TEs frequently overlap species-specific monocyte enhancers in both species, with 51.7% for human and 43.3% for mouse (Fig. 6B). Of note, LINEs show remarkably higher overlap rate to monocyte enhancers in human relative to mouse (Fig. 6B), despite that the global abundance of LINEs is comparable (21.7% vs. 20.0%) in both genomes. In contrast to human-mouse shared enhancers which only have 7 significantly enriched TE families (Fig. S11), a total of 69 TE families are significantly enriched in species-specific enhancers (Fig. 6C, Table S15). Many of the enriched TE families are species-specific, such as the primate-specific LTRs/ERVs (LTR4, LTR26, LTR8B, LTR36, MER72, HERV3-int), and rodent-specific SINEs (ID_B1, B3, B3A, B4A) and LTRs/ERVs (MTD, MTEa, ORR1D2, ORR1E). These data suggest the importance of TEs for mediating monocyte enhancer evolution in human and mouse.

We further determined the transcriptomic alteration between human and mouse monocytes. A total of 2,188 interspecies DEGs are identified, with 1,083 and 1,105 showing higher expression in human and mouse, respectively (Fig. 6D, S12A, Table S16). The DEGs with higher expression in either human or mouse monocytes are both significantly associated with defense response (Fig. 6E, S12B). Indeed, many of these DEGs are canonical immune genes, such as LYZ, S100A8/9, CD14, ITGB2, TNFSF13B in human and Ly6e, Slpi, Zbp1 in mouse (Table S16). To determine whether TEs mediate the interspecies expression divergence, we inspected the genes adjacent to species-specific enhancers, and found they indeed exhibit increased expression in human (Fig. 6F, S13). We identified hundreds of TE-enhancer adjacent genes showing higher expression in human monocytes (Table S17), including dozens known to be critical for innate immunity. For instance, a LTR84b-overlapped enhancer locates ~ 2.6 kb upstream of LYZ, which encodes the lysozyme enzyme critical for innate immunity [69]. Also, we observed a L1ME1-derived enhancer 4.2 kb upstream of NLRP3, which is a core gene for the NLRP3 inflammasome [70], and an AluSp-overlapped enhancer 7.7 kb upstream of the complement receptor C5AR1 (Table S17). Multiple other immune-related genes, such as PLD3 and IL1R1, are also adjacent to TE-overlapped enhancers. These data indicate the potential contribution of TEs in driving species-specific monocyte gene expression, which may further influence the immune properties of human monocytes.

Subsequently, we performed luciferase reporter assay in K562 myeloid cell line to assess the enhancer activity of three TE elements, which putatively form species-specific enhancers adjacent to SDSL, TPK1 and NOPCHAP1, respectively. These three genes exhibit significantly higher expression in human monocytes relative to mouse (Fig. 6G&H, S14), and SDSL shows myeloid-enriched expression (Fig. S15) according to Human Protein Atlas [71]. Our data confirmed the strong enhancer activity for two out of the three candidate TE elements (Fig. 6I). One is the LTR8B element upstream of SDSL, which is a metabolism-related gene associated with mitochondrial dysfunction and acute myeloid leukemia [72]. The other is the MER92C element upstream of TPK1, which is a protein kinase mediating the cell response to environmental stress [73, 74]. Surprisingly, the MLT1F2 element upstream of NOPCHAP1 shows repressive function (Fig. S14B), indicating the dependence on other DNA elements. Together, our data suggest that TEs create numerous species-specific monocyte enhancers in both human and mouse, which may contribute to the interspecies transcriptomic divergence of monocytes.

Discussion

In the past decade, the importance of TEs during mammalian development has been extensively studied, with special attention to embryonic stem cells (ESCs) and placentae [7, 40, 75, 76]. The importance of TEs for immunity has also long been recognized since the emergence of adaptive immunity in jawed vertebrates depends on the two TE-derived RAG1/2 transposases [77]. Nevertheless, the cis-regulatory roles of TEs in diverse types of immune cells are still poorly understood, likely due to the extreme cellular complexity of the immune system. Here, we characterized the regulatory roles of TEs in diverse human immune cells, first achieved a global picture by using single-cell sequencing data, and then focused on monocytes to elucidate their functions and underlying mechanisms.

Single-cell sequencing is particularly powerful for studying the highly heterogeneous immune system [55, 78]. By integrating the scATAC-seq data of human PBMCs, we annotated the CREs for major immune cell populations. Focused on the annotated immune csCREs, we confirmed their association with the corresponding lineage-instructive TFs, such as PU.1, IRF1, and CEBP in monocytes, GATA3, TBX21, TCF7 in T cells, and PU.1, PAX5 in B cells [9, 79]. A key finding is that dozens of TE families are enriched in these CREs with high cell-specificity. For example, we noticed many TE families exclusively enriched in the CREs for monocytes (e.g., LTR4, MER57B2, LTR71A), B cells (e.g., LTR9, LTR2, LTR43) or T cells (e.g., LTR12, MER51A). Further, the TE families enriched within immune CREs differ remarkably from those for human ESCs and placentae [40, 75, 76]. These data suggest that many TE families are selectively activated in distinct immune cell lineages. Accompanying recent studies linking TEs to B and/or T lymphocytes [28–30], our study provides a global profile of the TE regulatory landscape in various immune cell types and supports the widespread association of TEs to the regulatory landscape of different immune cells.

Among the studied immune cell populations, we performed an in-depth analysis of monocytes, which are crucial for host immunity and tissue homeostasis. By analyzing the CREs identified for various immune cell types by scATAC-seq, we found that many TE families are specifically enriched in the CREs for monocytes but not other immune cell types. It suggests that these TEs tend to be selectively activated in monocytes. In addition to LTRs/ERVs, it is surprising that other classes of TEs – particularly those belonging to LINEs (e.g., L1MB2/3, L2, L2b) – are also highly enriched in monocyte CREs. This observation is further confirmed by the analysis of epigenetically-annotated monocyte enhancers. LINEs constitute over 20% of the human genome, with LINE-1 (L1) the only type of TEs with autonomous transposition capacity [1, 7]. Unlike LTRs with inherited functions as CREs, the cis-regulatory roles of LINEs remain largely unexplored. A recent study provides the first experimental evidence that specific LINE1 elements (L1Md_Ts) can form enhancers in mouse ESCs [80], and other studies suggest that LINEs can also form promoters or insulators [81–83]. Our study demonstrates that LINEs can form numerous enhancers in human monocytes. Surprisingly, even though most of the LINE families enriched in human monocyte enhancers are also owned by mice, LINEs tend to create remarkably higher amounts of monocyte enhancers in human relative to mouse. These findings suggest that not only LTRs/ERVs, but also LINE elements, contribute to the regulatory landscape of human monocytes.

Unlike in ESCs and placentae where the TE regulators have been extensively studied [75, 76, 84], the TFs underlying TE activation in monocytes are unclear. Through computational screening, we identified multiple TFs that frequently bind TE-enhancers in monocytes, including several known to be important for monocytes or related myeloid lineages [85]. PU.1 was selected for in-depth analysis because it is a master pioneer TF that mediates the late specification of monocytes and derived macrophages [64, 66]. Indeed, the ectopic expression of SPI1 alone is sufficient to induce a myeloid gene program [86, 87]. We found that many immune genes are down-regulated after SPI1KD, suggesting the critical function of PU.1 in monocytes. Our data indicate that PU.1 mainly acts as a transcriptional activator, since a higher proportion of genomic loci with decreased H3K27ac after SPI1KD are originally bound by PU.1. Several other TFs (e.g., CEBPs and IRF1) frequently co-bind with PU.1 across the genome. This is probably because these TFs have combinatorial functions over some targeted loci, as recent evidence reveals that PU.1 and CEBPA collaborate to open closed chromatin regions [88]. Regarding the TE binding profiles, the screened TFs show both similarities and differences regarding the enriched families. Relative to other TFs, PU.1 shows higher degree of enrichment on several families of LTRs (LTR50, MER57B2, MLT1J, THE1D), LINEs (L2, L2b, L2c) and SINEs (MIR3). By integrating transcriptomic and epigenomic data, we found that PU.1 is likely to regulate many genes through its binding on adjacent TE-derived enhancers. Based on these results, we speculate that under the regulation of core TFs like PU.1, TEs participate in the monocyte regulatory network to regulate the expression of specific immune genes.

To understand the evolutionary divergence of monocytes, we performed transcriptomic and epigenomic comparisons between human and mouse. As expected, hundreds of genes show differential expression across species, and many of them have immune-related functions. We found that over 30% of monocyte enhancers are species-specific, with approximately half of these species-specific enhancers originating from TEs. This finding aligns with the well-recognized roles of TEs for driving the evolution of species-specific enhancers [4, 5, 7]. We identified dozens of TE families significantly overrepresented in non-conserved monocyte enhancers, which show several intriguing patterns: (1) Most enriched LTR families are primate- or rodent-specific, suggesting the role of young LTR families in creating novel monocyte enhancers; (2) Multiple ancient LINE families show a higher degree of enrichment in human, suggesting they create more primate-specific insertions to form monocyte enhancers; (3) Higher proportions of monocyte enhancers overlap SINEs in mouse relative to human, and more SINE families (most are rodent-specific) are significantly enriched in mouse-specific monocyte enhancers. Further analysis indicates that TE-derived enhancers are associated with the evolved expression of many adjacent genes, including dozens of immune-related genes (e.g., LYZ, NLRP3, C5AR1, PLD3, IL1R1) with higher expression in human relative to mouse monocytes. Luciferase reporter assay further confirmed the enhancer activity of representative TE elements associated with two genes showing higher expression in human relative to mouse. Overall, these data suggest that TEs have species-specific contributions in shaping the regulatory landscape of human and mouse monocytes, and further indicate their potential roles for the evolution of the immune properties of monocytes.

At last, we would like to note that this study also has several limitations. First, we focused on monocyte for in-depth investigation after uncovering a global picture about TE-derived CREs in different blood immune cell types. Therefore, the roles of TEs in other immune cell types, such as NK and DCs in bloods as well as the immune cells in different tissues, are less profiled and deserve further investigation. Second, while we systematically analyzed different immune cells in human PBMCs at single-cell resolution, matched single-cell investigation of mouse immune cells is lacking. We chose to focus on monocytes and used bulk sequencing data to compare between purified human and mouse monocytes. The strategy for interspecies comparison of purified immune cell populations has been widely adopted by previous studies [25, 89], yet a direct comparison of matched human/mouse immune cell types at single-cell resolution is anticipated to provide more precise results. Most conclusions from this study are based on genome-wide analysis, and further functional experiments would be beneficial to prove the regulatory roles of TEs on immune genes. For example, by using CRISPR experiments to delete distinct TEs from genome, it would provide further evidence about the regulatory function of TEs in immune cells such as monocytes. Third, we used THP-1 cell line as the in vitro model for major experiments, such as SPI1 knockdown. While THP-1 cells are widely used for studying human monocytes, it may not fully capture the properties of primary monocytes. Therefore, further studies with primary cells or in vivo models are anticipated.

Conclusions

In this study, we characterized the highly cell-specific profile of TE-derived cis-regulatory elements in different types of immune cells by using single-cell multiomic data of human PBMCs. Our integrative analysis further demonstrates the frequent contribution of TEs in creating monocyte enhancers, including those specific for human or mouse, and indicates the potential roles of TEs in mediating species-specific monocyte gene expression.
