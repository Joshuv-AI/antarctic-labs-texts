Efficacy of non-pharmacological interventions for childhood asthma: a systematic review and network meta-analysis
Authors: Jia Zhang, Zhen Ye, Fangyang Guo, Yumeng Ye, Xinyu Yu, Zhijian Song, Fenfen Zhang, Nan Gu, Meiying Ao, Qian Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChildhood asthma is a prevalent chronic respiratory disease. While inhaled corticosteroids and β-agonists remain cornerstone treatments, growing evidence highlights the complementary role of non-pharmacological interventions in improving asthma outcomes. This study aimed to systematically evaluate the efficacy of diverse non-pharmacological approaches through a network meta-analysis (NMA).MethodsSeven English and Chinese databases were searched from their inception to April 1, 2025, for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) related to non-pharmacological intervention in childhood asthma. The risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane risk of bias tool (ROB). Network meta-analysis was conducted using R 4.2.0 and Stata 14.0 software.ResultsA total of 41 studies with 3164 participants were included. Involved structured exercise programs, controlled breathing techniques, traditional moxibustion therapy, psychological interventions, and traditional therapeutic massage adjuvant treatment measures. Five asthma outcome indicators were focused on: FEV1, FVC, PEF, PAQLQ, and FeNO. The ROB was low in the included studies. The NMA of two-by-two comparisons showed that all non-pharmacological adjunctive interventions were able to improve asthma symptoms better, with exercise training improving FEV1 (MD = 3.67, 95%CI [1.39, 5.95]), PEF (MD = 6.07, 95%CI [1.07, 11.07]), and PAQLQ (MD = 0.93, 95%CI [0.33, 1.52]) with statistical significance (p < 0.05).ConclusionFive non-pharmacological interventions for childhood asthma demonstrated consistent efficacy across all modalities in alleviating asthma symptoms. Psychological interventions emerged as the optimal adjunctive therapy for improving FEV1, while exercise training exhibited the most potent therapeutic effect on FVC. Furthermore, massage therapy demonstrated superior efficacy in enhancing PEF, PAQLQ scores, and FeNO levels.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-025-06115-4.

Background

Asthma is a common chronic respiratory disease in children, but also a heterogeneous disease characterized by chronic airway inflammation and airway hyperresponsiveness [1]. The pathogenesis is very complex and has not yet been clarified. The theory of interaction of the mechanism between airway immune-inflammatory and neuromodulation is dominant [2]. Death rates from asthma in children globally range from 0.0 to 0.7 per 100,000 [3]. In recent years, with the deterioration of the environment, the number of incidences has dramatically increased and has become a severe public health problem globally [4, 5].

In Childhood Asthma management, pharmacotherapy remains the cornerstone of treatment, encompassing both modern pharmaceutical agents (e.g., glucocorticoids, β2-receptor agonists, and leukotriene receptor antagonists) and traditional natural medicines, Traditional medical systems employ distinct therapeutic approaches for asthma management: Traditional Chinese Medicine utilizes Ephedra sinica, Prunus armeniaca, acupuncture, and tuina; Ayurvedic medicine applies Ocimum tenuiflorum (Tulsi) and Nasya therapy; Unani-Tibb incorporates Nigella sativa monotherapy; while Ugandan traditional practice employs steam inhalation or decoctions of Albizia coriaria and Litsea cubeba [6]. However, current drug therapies present several clinical limitations: prolonged medication use may adversely affect child development, while chronic administration is often associated with localized or systemic adverse effects, increased drug resistance, and diminished treatment adherence [7]. These challenges have prompted growing interest in exploring safe, non-pharmacological adjunctive interventions as a promising research direction. In recent years, structured exercise programs (e.g., aerobic training, anaerobic training), controlled breathing techniques (e.g., inspiratory/expiratory muscle training), traditional moxibustion therapy (e.g., acupuncture point moxibustion), psychological interventions (e.g., psychological counseling), traditional therapeutic massage adjuvant treatment measures have gradually received attention [8, 9]. Non-pharmacological interventions offer distinct clinical advantages, including decreased reliance on medication, enhanced quality of life, attenuated inflammatory responses, and airway hyperreactivity [10–12].

Although non-pharmacological interventions for pediatric asthma have been widely studied, the absence of head-to-head comparative evaluations and meta-analytic integration limits evidence-based prioritization of therapeutic strategies. Network meta-analysis (NMA) is a robust statistical methodology that enables simultaneous comparison of multiple interventions through direct and indirect evidence [13]. This approach facilitates efficacy ranking across therapeutic options and identifies optimal treatment strategies by synthesizing all available comparative data. In this study, we employ NMA to systematically evaluate the relative effectiveness of diverse non-pharmacological adjunctive interventions for childhood asthma, with the ultimate goal of informing evidence-based clinical decision-making. This study will help clinicians and families of pediatric patients to better manage childhood asthma through non-invasive, non-pharmacological measures, thereby achieving prevention and treatment goals.

Methods

This study was reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis (PRISMA) for network meta analysis [14]. The International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) and the Cochrane Library were searched for no similar studies. The review protocol was registered in the PROSPERO under the registration number CRD420251023253. Available from https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/home.

Literature search strategy

Seven English and Chinese databases, including PubMed, the Cochrane Library, Embase, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Wanfang Database, Chongqing VIP Database (CQVIP), and Chinese Biomedical Literature Service System (SinoMed), were searched through April 1, 2025, for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) on non-pharmacological interventions in childhood asthma. We used subject terms and free words for joint searches, formulating and adapting the search strategy according to the characteristics of each database with English and Chinese language. Additionally, we used the references within relevant, retrieved articles to ensure potentially eligible studies. Inclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria of this study strictly follow the PICOS framework, including Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcomes and Study design.

Population: Children with asthma, including bronchial and variable asthma, aged 2–18 years. Intervention: Non-oral drug interventions, harmless auxiliary intervention measures such as respiratory training, exercise therapy, traditional Chinese exercise, environmental intervention, psychological intervention. Comparison: Routine care, placebo, or other pharmacological interventions. Outcome indicators: Primary outcomes: total effective rate, asthma attack frequency, pulmonary ventilation function (e.g., FEV1, FVC, PEF), ACT score, PAQLQ, etc.; additional outcome: inflammatory markers (e.g., FeNO, EOS, IgE), etc. Study design: Randomized controlled trials.

Exclusion criteria

The following studies were excluded: Asthma combined with or secondary to other major diseases. Repeated publication. Incorrect or incomplete data. The full texts are unavailable. Non-RCT designs, including observational studies and clinical trials, use incorrect randomization methods.

Study screening

EndNote X9 software was used to screen duplicate literature. Then, two investigators (ZJ and YXY) independently reviewed and checked article titles and abstracts. Subsequently, the full texts were further screened to exclude any literature that did not meet the inclusion criteria. In the event of a disagreement between the two reviewers, the opinion of a third reviewer (GN) was consulted.

Data extraction

Two investigators (ZJ and YXY) conducted data extraction independently using a structured template. In cases of disagreement, consensus was achieved through discussion with a third reviewer (GN). The extraction form comprised the following variables: Study characteristics, such as first author, corresponding author, year, language, the methods of random sequence generation, and allocation concealment. Participant demographics, including sample size, sex distribution, and age characteristics. Intervention protocols, including specific treatment modalities and duration of intervention. Outcome, mean, and standard deviation values before and after treatment. When the mean value was unavailable in the full-text article, we estimated the mean and standard deviation from the median and range, interquartile range, and/or 95% confidence interval range [15].

Risk of bias assessment

The risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool. Two blinded reviewers (ZJ and YXY) independently performed risk-of-bias assessments (high/low/unclear), with arbitration by a third researcher (GN) when needed. Study evaluation tools primarily included random sequence generation (selection bias), allocation concealment (selection bias), blinding of participants and personnel (performance bias), blinding of outcome assessment (detection bias), incomplete outcome data (attrition bias), selective reporting (reporting bias), and other sources of bias.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

Change-from-baseline values (calculated as post-treatment mean minus baseline mean) with their standard deviations served as inputs for network meta-analysis. We conducted a frequentist network meta-analysis with 10,000 iterations using R4.2.0 and Stata 14.0. Dichotomous outcomes were analyzed using odds ratios (ORs), while continuous outcomes were assessed via mean differences (MDs), both reported with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Treatment rankings were evaluated using the surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA), where higher values indicate superior efficacy or safety. Heterogeneity among studies was quantified using the I² index, with conventional thresholds applied: low (I² = 0–49%), moderate (I² = 50–74%), and high (I² = 75–100%) heterogeneity [17, 18].

The evidence network was visualized with nodes representing interventions (sized proportionally to their sample sizes) and edges denoting direct comparisons between interventions (with line thickness corresponding to the number of head-to-head randomized controlled trials). Publication bias was assessed using adjusted funnel plots for primary outcome measures. For NMA, potential publication bias was evaluated through qualitative examination of the evidence structure.

Study characteristics

The systematic review and network meta-analysis included 41 eligible RCTs, with a total study population of 3164 participants (intervention groups: 1,588; control groups: 1,556). The evidence base consisted of 26 Chinese-language studies and 15 English-language studies. Participants in the control groups were managed with conventional drug therapy alone, whereas those in the intervention groups received adjunctive non-pharmacological therapies in combination with conventional medications. Five non-pharmacological therapeutic modalities were evaluated: structured exercise programs, controlled breathing techniques, traditional moxibustion therapy, psychological interventions, and traditional therapeutic massage. Treatment durations varied among studies, ranged from 3 weeks to 6 months. The primary analysis focused on five clinically relevant endpoints: pulmonary function parameters, forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1), forced vital capacity (FVC), peak expiratory flow (PEF); Pediatric Asthma Quality of Life Questionnaire (PAQLQ); fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO). Risk of bias within studies

Methodological quality assessment was performed for 41 included studies utilizing the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (ROB), evaluating key domains of research design to determine the validity and reliability of the clinical evidence. Risk of bias assessment (see in Fig. 2) revealed generally favorable ratings, though methodological reporting was suboptimal. Critical limitations included insufficient documentation of randomization techniques (e.g., lack of sequence generation details) and allocation concealment, as well as unclear blinding implementation. However, low attrition rates and the use of objectively measured endpoints with robust statistical analyses strengthened the evidence base.Fig. 2Results of the risk of bias evaluation

Network meta-analysis

In this study, the network plot of RCTs evidence, the size of the network nodes is proportional to the total number of participants allocated to each intervention and the thickness of the lines is proportional to the number of studies evaluating each direct comparison. As the network structure contained no closed loops (and thus no potential for inconsistency), we applied a consistency model for all statistical analyses without requiring inconsistency assessment. The complete network Meta-analysis forest plot is shown in Supporting Information: Appendix 2.

FEV1

A total of 36 studies[19–21, 23–33, 35–45, 47–57] involving 2,835 patients reported forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1) outcomes across five non-pharmacological interventions. The network evidence plot is presented in Fig. 3. Analysis of FEV1 measurements revealed better outcomes across all five non-pharmacological adjunctive therapies relative to conventional pharmacological treatment separately. The network meta-analysis presented that exercise training (MD=3.67, 95%CI[1.39, 5.95]) was significantly better than conventional pharmacological treatment separately. Discussion

Childhood asthma substantially impairs overall health status in affected patients. The condition is pathologically characterized by persistent airway inflammation, which induces bronchial smooth muscle contraction, mucosal edema, and excessive mucus production[60]. These pathological alterations collectively result in airway obstruction, elevated airflow resistance, and consequent impairment of pulmonary ventilation efficiency[61]. Structured exercise programs, controlled breathing techniques, traditional moxibustion therapy, psychological interventions, and traditional therapeutic massage adjuvant treatment measures are demonstrates therapeutic enhancement potential while circumventing the adverse effects associated with pharmacological interventions, offering a safer complementary approach in clinical management[10, 12]. Pulmonary function testing encompasses the measurement of key spirometric parameters including FEV1, FVC, and PEF, which objective measures serve three critical functions: asthma severity classification, therapeutic strategy optimization and longitudinal assessment of treatment response[62]. PAQLQ serves as a validated multidimensional assessment tool in pediatric asthma management, quantifying disease impact across three core domains: physiological functioning, psychological well-being, and social adaptation[63]. FeNO is produced by airway cells, as a biomarker of airway inflammation, its determination is widely used in the diagnosis and monitoring of asthma and various respiratory diseases[64].

Summary of evidence

A network meta-analysis evaluated various rehabilitation interventions for asthma patients demonstrated that combined aerobic exercise and respiratory training significantly enhances pulmonary function parameters compared to monotherapies [65]. An NMA study by Jiang et al., concluded that different modalities of exercise rehabilitation were effective in improving the quality of survival in pediatric asthma pulmonary ventilation [9]. However, current evidence remains limited to exercise-based interventions, lacking comprehensive evaluation of diverse non-pharmacological approaches [66]. The comparative effectiveness across alternative non-drug therapies for asthma management remains poorly characterized in existing literature.

This systematic review and network meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials demonstrates that combined non-pharmacological and pharmacological interventions yield superior clinical outcomes for pediatric asthma symptom management compared to drug therapy alone. Exercise-based interventions represent the most extensively studied and clinically effective non-pharmacological approach, demonstrated statistically significant improvements in key outcomes including FEV1, PEF, and PAQLQ scores. SUCRA analysis revealed that psychological intervention emerged as the most effective adjunctive measure for enhancing FEV1. Conversely, exercise training was identified as the optimal supplementary strategy for improving FVC. Furthermore, massage therapy was determined to be the most beneficial auxiliary approach for augmenting PEF, PAQLQ scores, and FeNO levels. Although the investigation into exercise training documented instances of fatigue and cough, these symptoms remained within the anticipated range for normal physical activity and did not precipitate any adverse events.

Predictors of outcomes

Studies have shown that exercise can promote longitudinal growth of lung volume, improve the FEV1, FVC, and PEF indexes, and improve lung function in asthmatic patients. This results in less frequent emergency room visits, less frequent hospitalizations and fewer days in the hospital. It can also reduce the overall medication and economic burden of patients with asthma [7]. Regular aerobic exercise modulates immune function in asthmatic mice by upregulating anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-10 and TGF-β), which suppresses Th2-mediated inflammatory cascades. This mechanism concurrently attenuates eosinophilic airway infiltration, downregulates NF-κB signaling in pulmonary tissues, and reduces secretion of pro-inflammatory mediators [67, 68]. Long-term exercise upregulates β2-adrenergic receptor expression in airway smooth muscle, enhances bronchodilatation, and swimming promotes mucus clearance from small airways by increasing tidal volume and alveolar ventilation, reduces air retention, and delays the process of airway remodelling [69].

In asthmatic children, lung hyperinflation often leads to diaphragmatic hypoplasia and reduced contraction efficiency. Breathing exercises for pediatric patients can reduce dynamic pulmonary hyperinflation (DPH) due to shallow fast breathing, reduce endogenous PEEP, improve ventilation/blood flow ratios, and enhance lung compliance [70, 71].

Psychological factors such as anxiety, depression, and stress are among the triggers of asthma. Negative emotions induce bronchospasm by enhancing airway smooth muscle contraction and exacerbate airway inflammation by releasing cortisol and pro-inflammatory cytokines [72, 73]. Psychological interventions can indirectly improve symptom perception by modulating the neuro-immune-endocrine network, improving behavioural patterns and environmental adaptations, resulting in the rhythmic secretion of cortisol, suppressing neurogenic inflammation, and reducing anxiety and depression scores [74–76].

Moxibustion and Massage as traditional Chinese medical treatments regulate inflammatory immunity, neural reflexes, and pulmonary ventilation through multi-targets, activate TRPV1 channels, promote substance P release, and reduce the levels of Th2 type cytokines such as IL-4 and IL-5 [77, 78]. In addition, acupressure stimulation induces endogenous opioid peptide secretion, directly relaxes airway smooth muscle and enhances β2 receptor sensitivity [79, 80].

Strengths, limitations, and future directions

This study possesses several methodological strengths. First, the implementation of stringent inclusion/exclusion criteria enhanced the homogeneity across selected studies, facilitating valid comparative analyses. Second, our analytical approach incorporated pre-to-post treatment effect size calculations for all outcome measures, thereby improving the precision of network meta-analysis estimates. Third, comprehensive inconsistency assessments confirmed the absence of both global (network-wide) and local (loop-specific) inconsistencies, supporting the reliability of our findings.

However, our study does have limitations. We did not include countries such as Japan, South Korea, and Uganda that use traditional alternative therapies. There is a relative scarcity of high-quality randomized controlled trials in this field, leading to a risk of bias due to the absence of allocation concealment and blinding in most included studies. And some reports did not provide a clinical ethics statement. This review was able to integrate evidence that directly compared non-pharmacological adjunctive therapies with other treatment modalities, while also informing estimates using indirect evidence. The review did not, however, include all possible sources of indirect evidence.

Finally, for many modalities, the evidence is derived from small trials (e.g., the median number of exercise arms was 10). In addition to reducing risks from bias, primary research may benefit from deconstruction designs or from larger, head-to-head analyses of exercise modalities to better identify what works best for each candidate. Subsequent studies will conduct more in-depth analysis and research on specific pediatric asthma patients.

Conclusion

This systematic review and network meta-analysis of five non-pharmacological interventions for childhood asthma demonstrated consistent efficacy across all modalities in alleviating asthma symptoms. Notably, psychological interventions emerged as the optimal adjunctive therapy for improving FEV1, while exercise training exhibited the strongest therapeutic effect on FVC. Furthermore, massage therapy demonstrated superior efficacy in enhancing PEF, PAQLQ scores, and FeNO levels. However, due to the low level of evidence, more multicenter, large-sample, well-designed randomized studies are needed to verify these conclusions.
