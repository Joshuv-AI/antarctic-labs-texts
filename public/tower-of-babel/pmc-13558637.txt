Structure and mechanism of human sphingosine-1-phosphate transporter MFSD2B
Authors: Shahbaz Ahmed, Min Huang, Yaxin Dai, Xuebo Yang, Jiajun Dong, Xiang Yu, Chia-Hsueh Lee, Long N. Nguyen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) is an essential signaling lipid that maintains vascular integrity and regulates immune cell trafficking. The major facilitator superfamily domain–containing protein 2B (MFSD2B) serves as the main S1P exporter in red blood cells and platelets; however, its structure and transport mechanism are unclear. Here, we report the 3.0 Å cryo-EM structure of human MFSD2B bound to S1P. S1P is captured in a distinctive binding state, deeply buried within the C-domain, with its sphingoid tail accommodated by a hydrophobic pocket and its phosphate group coordinated by a cluster of polar residues within the transporter’s cavity. Mutagenesis and molecular dynamics simulations identify the TM2/TM11 lateral opening as the primary pathway for S1P translocation, with key charged residues acting as sequential anchors during transport. Furthermore, we demonstrate that MFSD2B functions as a uniporter, and that subtle rewiring of local charge networks can alter its coupling mechanism. Our work provides a molecular framework for understanding S1P transport mediated by MFSD2B in hematopoietic cells.

Introduction

Sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) is a potent bioactive sphingolipid that orchestrates a wide spectrum of physiological processes, including vascular integrity, cell survival, and lymphocyte trafficking1–5. In all eukaryotic cells, S1P is synthesized from sphingosine by sphingosine kinases 1 and 2 (SphK1/2)6–10. A specialized group of cell types, most notably vascular endothelial (both blood and lymphatic endothelia) and hematopoietic cells, can efficiently export S1P to the extracellular milieu, where it binds to albumin and high-density lipoproteins (apoM/HDL) for autocrine or paracrine signaling functions. However, due to its amphipathic nature, S1P cannot freely diffuse across lipid bilayers and requires dedicated transporters to mediate its translocation.

While several ATP-binding cassette transporters, including ABCA1, ABCC1, and ABCG2, have been implicated in S1P efflux11,12, only two transporters have been identified as bona fide S1P transporters: protein spinster homolog 2 (SPNS2) and major facilitator superfamily domain–containing 2B (MFSD2B)13–16. SPNS2 has been reported to be expressed in endothelial cells that mediate S1P secretion into lymph and is involved in the regulation of lymphocyte egress from lymph nodes17–19. In contrast, MFSD2B is highly expressed in hematopoietic cells, including red blood cells (RBCs) and platelets, where it mediates the release of S1P into the plasma16. Mouse studies indicate that SPNS2 and MFSD2B are important contributors to plasma S1P, although their relative contributions may vary across experimental systems14,20–22. Concomitant ablation of these sources of S1P caused hemorrhagic lethality in mouse embryos, underscoring their central physiological roles in maintaining systemic S1P homeostasis20.

We previously identified MFSD2B as the principal S1P exporter in RBCs and platelets using genetic knockout models. Lipidomic profiling demonstrated that deletion of MFSD2B causes S1P accumulation in these cells and reduces circulating S1P levels by ~54% relative to wild-type mice16. Among the known cell types where MFSD2B is expressed, RBCs have been demonstrated as the major source for circulating S1P, while platelets release a small amount of S1P upon activation23. Loss of MFSD2B disrupts RBC and platelet morphology and function, highlighting its essential role in blood cells’ physiology20,23. MFSD2B has also been implicated in cardiovascular function, although the mechanism is unclear24.

MFSD2B belongs to the major facilitator superfamily (MFS). Among MFS transporters, four members, MFSD2A, MFSD2B, SPNS1, and SPNS2, have been characterized as lysolipid transporters. MFSD2B and SPNS2 mediate S1P transport across the plasma membrane, whereas MFSD2A and SPNS1 translocate lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC) and lysophosphatidylethanolamine (LPE) via the plasma membrane and lysosomes, respectively25–27. Recent structural studies of MFSD2A, SPNS1, and SPNS2 have revealed how these transporters move amphiphilic substrates across cell membranes28–37: MFSD2A imports its substrates in a Na⁺-dependent manner, while SPNS1 exports lysosomal lysolipids utilizing a gradient of protons. In contrast, SPNS2 functions as a uniporter that drives S1P efflux down its concentration gradient. Despite these advances, the molecular mechanism by which MFSD2B recognizes and translocates S1P across the membrane remains unknown.

Here, we present the cryo-EM structure of human MFSD2B bound to S1P at 3.0 Å resolution. The structure captures a distinctive outward-facing conformation in which S1P binds deep within the transporter, coordinated by a hydrophobic pocket that accommodates its sphingoid tail and a hydrophilic cavity that recognizes its polar headgroup. Functional assays and molecular dynamics simulations delineate the lipid-binding and translocation pathway, revealing how key residues anchor the headgroup of S1P during transport. We also demonstrate that MFSD2B functions as a uniporter and identify residues that mediate the energy-coupling mechanism, uncovering the molecular basis of S1P release from hematopoietic cells.

Results

Structure determination of MFSD2B

MFSD2B is a relatively small membrane protein (~54 kDa), with most of its mass embedded within the membrane bilayer, making it a challenging target for cryo-EM analysis. To facilitate structural studies of MFSD2B, we adopted a fusion strategy that had previously enabled high-resolution structure determination of other difficult MFS transporters30,38–40. Specifically, a maltose-binding protein (MBP) was fused to the N-terminus from the amino acid position 41 and an MBP-specific binder (DARPin) to the C-terminus of MFSD2B (MFSD2BEM construct). Immunofluorescence analysis showed that MFSD2BEM is predominantly localized to the plasma membrane of human embryonic kidney (HEK293) cells, similar to the wild-type MFSD2B (MFSD2BWT) (Supplementary Fig. 1A, B). To ensure that MFSD2BEM remains capable of transporting S1P, we performed a cell-based export assay using HEK293 cells coexpressing human SphK2 and MFSD2B. The cells were supplied with [³H]-sphingosine, which was subsequently converted to [³H]-S1P by SphK2 and then exported to the medium by MFSD2B. Although the activity was slightly lower than that of MFSD2BWT, it is clear that MFSD2BEM retained efficiency to export S1P (Fig. 1A). Consistently, analysis of the [3H]-S1P levels in cell pellets indicated that expression of either MFSD2BWT or MFSD2BEM reduced intracellular S1P levels, further supporting the export of S1P from the cells. When cells were supplied with varying concentrations of sphingosine (1–10 μM) for S1P synthesis, we observed dose-dependent S1P export activity (Fig. 1B) and extracellular S1P levels increased progressively over time in both MFSD2BWT and MFSD2BEM (Fig. 1C). We determined the structure of MFSD2BEM at 3 Å resolution (Fig. 1D and Supplementary Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 1). The quality of the EM density is high, allowing reliable de novo model building for the majority of the transporter structure. MFSD2B adopts a canonical MFS fold comprising twelve transmembrane helices (TMs). TMs 1–6 form the N-domain, and TMs 7–12 form the C-domain (Fig. 1D, E). In this structure, the central cavity between the N- and C-domains was open to the extracellular side, indicating that the transporter is captured in an outward-facing conformation. The protein sample was incubated with exogenous S1P before grid preparation, and a well-defined density corresponding to S1P was observed within the transport cavity of MFSD2B (Fig. 1D, E; gray). In our experiments, n-dodecyl β-D-maltoside (DDM) was used for membrane extraction of MFSD2B before reconstituting into saposin nanoparticles. Detergents used for protein extraction could associate with lipid transporters, as previously reported for SPNS2, where DDM was found to occupy a site within the transporter33. Although extensive washing during reconstitution was performed to minimize potential residual DDM binding to MFSD2B, we sought to exclude the possibility that any remaining detergent accounted for the observed ligand density. Therefore, we extracted MFSD2B using digitonin instead of DDM and were able to obtain MFSD2B structure at a comparable resolution (3.1 Å and Supplementary Table 1). A similar elongated density was observed in the MFSD2B transport cavity (Supplementary Fig. 2H and Supplementary Table 1). The shape and size of this density are incompatible with a digitonin molecule, indicating that it corresponds to the added S1P.

S1P binding site and translocation pathway

In our structure, we found that the lipid tail of S1P was inserted deeply into the transporter, binding primarily within the C-domain, with its headgroup pointing toward the central cavity (Fig. 2A, B). In the previously reported structures of lysolipid transporters in the outward-facing conformation, the lysolipid substrate is either not observed, such as in the SPNS2 structure33, or it is located at the periphery of the transporter, such as in MFSD2A and SPNS1 structures31,37. The polar region of S1P is surrounded by a cluster of hydrophilic residues (K88, T192, Q298, Q301, and K423). K423 of MFSD2B is conserved in MFSD2A and has been reported to be critical for binding to the phosphate group of LPC in MFSD2A (Supplementary Fig. 3 and Supplementary table 2)35,37. To investigate the roles of these residues, we generated a series of mutants that are predicted to disrupt the interaction with S1P headgroup. Protein expression of the MFSD2B variants was largely unaffected (Supplementary Fig. 1C). Interestingly, mutations at K88, Q298, and K423 markedly decreased MFSD2B transport activity (Fig. 2C), indicating that these residues are critical for S1P transport by coordinating with the headgroup.

The sphingoid backbone of S1P resided in a hydrophobic pocket formed by TM7, TM8, and TM10, where it was aligned with hydrophobic conserved residues such as V325, L326, I382, and L386 (Supplementary Fig. 3). To assess the contribution of this region to fatty-tail recognition, we substituted these residues with alanine. These mutations significantly reduced transport activity without affecting protein expression (Fig. 2C and Supplementary Fig. 1C), underscoring the essential role of this hydrophobic pocket in S1P binding and accommodation.

In the extracellular half of MFSD2B, TM5/TM8 and TM2/TM11 form two lateral openings. Similar openings are also present in other lysolipid transporters and commonly serve as lipid translocation pathways. In MFSD2A, lysolipids may enter through either of these openings28,29,37, whereas in SPNS1, the TM5/TM8 pathway appears to be preferred and in SPNS2, the TM2/TM11 pathway is favored30–32. Because in the MFSD2B structure, S1P is located closer to the TM5/TM8 opening, we first examined the contribution of this gate to MFSD2B transport activity. Nevertheless, mutation of F63 on TM1, which sits directly in front of the TM5/TM8 opening, and mutations of Q319 and V322 on TM8 had negligible effects (Fig. 2D), suggesting that the TM5/TM8 opening may not be the translocation path for MFSD2B substrate.

To further investigate S1P translocation, we conducted molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. The protein was embedded in a mixed lipid bilayer containing key lipid compositions similar to erythrocyte lipid membranes (Supplementary Table 3)41,42, with the substrate S1P placed at the position that was observed in the cryo-EM structure, and three independent 500 ns simulations were performed. The stabilities of the membrane–protein system were evaluated. Bilayer thickness and Box-Z dimension fluctuated within approximately 3.80–3.88 nm and 10.47–10.51 nm, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 4A, B). The secondary structure (SS) was largely preserved during the simulations, and radius of gyration (Rg) values ranged from approximately 2.19 to 2.26 nm (Supplementary Fig. 4C, D). The spatial distribution of S1P across the three trajectories indicated distinct positional sampling within the MFSD2B cavity, with the hydrophobic tail primarily localized within the TM7/TM8/TM10 hydrophobic region and the headgroup showing a preferential orientation toward the TM2/TM11 opening (Fig. 3A). Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to characterize the dominant conformational variations sampled during the MD simulations. PCA of the combined three-replicate trajectory indicated that the independent simulations sampled partially distinct conformational regions within a common PC1–PC2 space (Supplementary Fig. 4E), and further analysis suggested that these conformational regions were associated primarily with localized structural differences among the sampled conformations (Supplementary Fig. 4F–I). Per-residue root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) analysis showed that most residues fluctuated below approximately 0.20 nm, whereas higher RMSF values were mainly observed in loop/terminal regions, with local peaks reaching approximately 0.4–0.5 nm (Supplementary Fig. 5D). Analysis of S1P-protein interactions showed that the S1P acyl tail formed persistent hydrophobic contacts with residues surrounding the TM7/TM8/TM10 hydrophobic pocket, including V325, L326, I382, and L386 (Supplementary Fig. 5E), consistent with the mutagenesis results (Fig. 2C). By comparison, hydrogen bond occupancies between the S1P headgroup and individual protein residues were generally below 25% throughout the simulations, suggestive of transient interactions (Supplementary Fig. 5F). In one representative trajectory (Rep1), we observed that the fatty tail stably inserted into the TM7/TM8/TM10 pocket, while its headgroup rotated from the central cavity to the lumen and TM2-TM11 opening (Fig. 3B–D, Supplementary Movie 1). During this process, the two basic residues K88 and K423 changed their positions to approach the headgroup of S1P (Fig. 3E). By the end of the 500 ns MD simulation, K88 moved closer to the phosphate group of S1P (Fig. 3E), and S1P began to orient toward the TM2/TM11 opening. Concomitantly, TM2 moved away from TM11, enlarging the lateral opening. The hydrophobic residue F85 on TM2 moved away, possibly creating space to facilitate S1P translocation through the TM2/TM11 gate (Fig. 3F). Importantly, we validated these observations by mutating F85 on TM2 and V419 and F420 residues both on TM11 to alanine. These changes substantially reduced S1P transport (Fig. 3G and Supplementary Fig. 1C). Potential conformational transitions during transport cycle

To gain insights into the structural transitions that occur during the transport cycle of MFSD2B, we next compared our outward-facing structure with its paralog, MFSD2A, which has been captured in outward-facing, outward-occluded, and inward-facing conformations28,29,34,37. Relative to the outward-facing conformation of MFSD2A, MFSD2B adopts a more closed configuration (Fig. 4A, top). This is primarily driven by a rotation of the C-domain toward the N-domain on the extracellular side (Fig. 4A, bottom). Individual domain alignments revealed minimal internal rearrangements (Supplementary Fig. 6A, RMSDs of 1.2 Å and 1.0 Å for the N- and C-domains, respectively), consistent with a predominantly rigid-body motion between the two domains. Consequently, transmembrane helices TM8 and TM11 shift closer to TM5 and TM2, respectively, narrowing the extracellular lateral cleft (Supplementary Fig. 6B). In MFSD2A, TM7 moves closer to TM1 during the transition from the outward-facing to the occluded state (11.2 Å to 9.0 Å; Fig. 4B). It is likely that MFSD2B also undergoes a similar movement, creating a narrow gate that restricts solvent access to the central cavity from the extracellular side (Fig. 4B). To further elucidate how MFSD2B may transition into the inward-facing conformation, we superimposed the outward-facing MFSD2B structure onto the inward-facing MFSD2A structure. This analysis revealed a pronounced rocker-switch-like motion of approximately 30° between the N- and C-domains (Fig. 4C, top). This motion induced reciprocal rearrangements of the transporter at the membrane interfaces. On the extracellular side, TM1 and TM7 constrict the vestibule, thereby closing the extracellular gate (Fig. 4C, bottom), while interactions between TM2–TM11 and TM5–TM8 strengthen to seal the lateral clefts that are thought to mediate lysolipid entry.

This rearrangement propagates through the protein to the intracellular interface, where it repositions key gating residues. A conserved tryptophan (W407 in MFSD2A; W390 in MFSD2B) in TM10 has been shown to act as a pivotal intracellular gate residue in MFSD2A (Fig. 4E, blue). In the outward-facing conformation, it engages the S170 residue (T164 in MFSD2B) on TM4 and the M191 (M185 in MFSD2B) and R192 (R184 in MFSD2B) residues on TM5, occluding access to the cytosol. The same interaction of these conserved residues in MFSD2B is also observed (Fig. 4E, orange). However, this interaction is disrupted in the inward-facing conformation (Fig. 4E, green), permitting gate opening.

The structural transition is accompanied by a remodeling of electrostatic contacts. Several inter-domain interactions, such as the R177–D398 salt bridge in MFSD2B and the R183–D415 salt bridge in MFSD2A, stabilize the outward-facing state and contribute to the closure of the intracellular vestibule (Fig. 4F, orange and blue), whereas the outward-to-inward transition breaks these interactions to permit cytoplasmic opening (Fig. 4F, green). Notably, the MFSD2B structural model predicted by AlphaFold2 adopts an inward-facing conformation. We therefore compared our structure with the AlphaFold2 model43 (Supplementary Fig. 6C). The conformational transition appeared similar to what has been observed when comparing MFSD2B with MFSD2A in different states: a rocker-switch like motion involving the opening of the intracellular gate, exemplified by the movement of W390 residue (Supplementary Fig. 6D), and the disruption of inter-domain hydrophilic interactions (Supplementary Fig. 6E). Although determining the structure of MFSD2B in additional conformational states will be essential to fully delineate its transport cycle, the present analysis provides a plausible framework for understanding its conformational transitions.

Furthermore, we have also compared MFSD2B with SPNS2, the other major plasma membrane S1P transporter. The overall conformation in the outward-facing state is similar between the two, and the distance between TM1 and TM7 is comparable, although TM8 in SPNS2 sits closer to TM5 (Fig. 4D). However, key residues involved in gating and inter-domain interactions are not conserved (Fig. 4E, F, purple), nor are the residues that coordinate the S1P headgroup (Supplementary Fig. 6F), consistent with the fact that the two transporters share no detectable sequence similarity. These observations suggest that, despite their similar overall architecture and shared substrate, MFSD2B and SPNS2 likely represent a case of convergent evolution toward S1P transport.

MFSD2B is an S1P uniporter

Unlike MFSD2A, which uses sodium to drive LPC/LPE import, MFSD2B transport function is independent of sodium6. Its activity, however, appears to be influenced by extracellular pH6. To examine whether MFSD2B-mediated S1P transport is dependent on a proton gradient, we measured [³H]-S1P release under different pH conditions. MFSD2B was capable of exporting S1P across a broad pH range, although its activity was slightly reduced at pH 5.5 (Fig. 5A). These results suggest that while MFSD2B activity may be affected by extracellular pH, S1P transport by MFSD2B does not require a transmembrane proton gradient. The independence from sodium and protons in MFSD2B is reminiscent of the transport properties of SPNS2, which functions as a uniporter30. If MFSD2B also operates as a uniporter, it should mediate S1P influx when the extracellular concentration exceeds that inside the cell. Indeed, under such experimental conditions, we observed S1P uptake mediated by MFSD2B (Fig. 5B), consistent with a previous study44. We also reconstituted purified MFSD2B into liposomes and performed an S1P uptake assay under conditions lacking any ion gradient across the membrane. Our results showed that MFSD2B remained active and was able to import [³H]-S1P and NBD-S1P (Fig. 5C, D and Supplementary Fig. 7A–C). In addition, because MFSD2B is highly expressed in RBCs and is responsible for S1P transport across the erythrocyte membrane, we performed ex vivo uptake assays for exogenous S1P (17:1) or [³H]-S1P using isolated RBCs. The results demonstrated that RBCs could take up extracellular S1P in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 5E, F). Further, increasing or decreasing the endogenous S1P levels in RBCs with Sph incubation and by SphK1 inhibition with PF543 inhibitor, respectively, influenced the ability of RBCs to take up extracellular S1P (Fig. 5E, F). The uniporter function of MFSD2B enables S1P transport bidirectionally. To characterize the translocation pathway of S1P in MFSD2B, we performed umbrella sampling simulations with potential of mean force (PMF) calculations in which S1P was initially positioned near the TM2/TM11 opening (Fig. 6 and Supplementary Table 3). Three independent replicates were carried out, each comprising approximately 20 windows with 30 ns sampling per window. The resulting PMF profiles revealed a continuous free-energy landscape connecting the TM2/TM11 opening to the central cavity, with no substantial energy barriers along the pathway (Fig. 6A), supporting the feasibility of substrate entry through this route. The umbrella windows showed sufficient overlap along the reaction coordinate, and convergence analyses indicated overall consistency of the PMF profiles across independent replicates (Supplementary Fig. 8A–C). Superposition of S1P conformations along the reaction coordinate from a representative trajectory (Rep1) showed a progressive movement of the substrate from the extracellular opening into the transporter cavity (Fig. 6B, C, Supplementary Movie 2). To further examine this process, four representative conformations corresponding to inflection points along the PMF profile were selected for structural analysis (Fig. 6D). At early stages of entry, the S1P headgroup engages residues lining the TM2/TM11 cleft, including K88, whereas at later stages, the S1P headgroup subsequently engages K423 near the central cavity during translocation. In addition, S1P formed interactions with other polar residues along the pathway, such as Q298, E188, Q160, and R184, further facilitating its movement toward the central cavity. Coupling mechanism in MFSD2B

Members of the MFS lysolipid transporter family employ diverse coupling strategies. Previous studies have shown that two aspartic acid residues of human MFSD2A (D93 and D97) are critical for Na⁺ binding37. Structural studies have revealed that Gallus gallus MFSD2A (GgMFSD2A) contains a cation-binding pocket formed by residues including D88, D92, T154 and K436 (corresponding to D93, D97, T159 and K436 in human MFSD2A), which may coordinate Na+ and couple ion binding to conformational transitions (Supplementary Fig. 9A)29. In the lysosomal lysolipid transporter SPNS1, it operates in a proton-dependent manner. Proton sensing in SPNS1 is mediated by a lumen-facing hydrophilic network composed of residues E87-D94-S97-R300-S442, in which the acidic residue D94 contributes to a pH-sensitive electrostatic switch that modulates transporter dynamics (Supplementary Fig. 9B)31. By comparison, SPNS2 functions as a facilitated-diffusion S1P exporter and is not thought to depend on either Na+ or proton gradients, consistent with the absence of a complete ion-coordinating network analogous to those observed in SPNS1 (Supplementary Fig. 9C)30.

How MFSD2B lost its cation coupling is unknown. The sodium binding sites D93 and D97 in MFSD2A correspond to G91 and D95 in MFSD2B (Supplementary Fig. 3). This divergence, where the first aspartate becomes a glycine in MFSD2B, likely reflects an evolutionary adaptation, allowing the transporter to operate independently of sodium or proton gradients to export S1P in hematopoietic cells. To test this hypothesis, we generated the G91D mutant and examined its activity in buffers at pH 5.5 and pH 7.5, with or without sodium (NaCl or choline chloride, respectively). In sharp contrast to MFSD2BWT, this mutant was active at pH 5.5 but inactive at pH 7.5 regardless of sodium or choline chloride presence (Fig. 7A and Supplementary Fig. 1C). Thus, although this single substitution does not confer sodium dependence, it converts MFSD2B into a proton-dependent transporter that possibly relies on proton influx to drive S1P export. To evaluate the role of these aspartic acids in proton dependence, we tested the G91D/D95A mutant, which has a single aspartate like MFSD2BWT. The results showed that the mutant remained active, especially at normal pH (Fig. 7B and Supplementary Fig. 1C). D95A, which lacks both acidic residues, was inactive in the same condition. Thus, the mutation of D to G at position 91 in MFSD2b likely abolishes or reduces the cation-dependent function of the transporter. In MFSD2A, T159 also participates in the Na⁺-binding network29. The corresponding residue in MFSD2B is T157 (Supplementary Fig. 3). We therefore performed additional mutagenesis to examine the importance of the side chain of D95 and T157. Substitution of either residue with different amino acids markedly reduced S1P export, with the exception of D95Y and T157S, which remained partially active (Fig. 7C and Supplementary Fig. 1C). These results suggest that both the side chain length and polar properties of D95 and T157 residues are required for MFSD2B function. Because D95Y preserved substantial activity, suggesting that the hydroxyl group of tyrosine can replace the carboxylic group of aspartic acid. Thus, we tested whether MFSD2BG91D/D95Y could function as a proton/S1P antiporter, similar to the MFSD2BG91D mutant. However, this mutant behaved similarly to the wild-type protein and was not inactive at high extracellular pH (Supplementary Fig. 9F). Thus, it appears that two aspartic residues are required for cation dependence in this family of transporters.

To understand how the G91D substitution enables proton-dependent S1P transport, we used AlphaFold2 to generate a structural model of MFSD2BG91D and predict the potential interactions of D91 with nearby residues. We found that the amine group of K423 formed two salt bridges with the carboxylic acids of D91 and D95, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 9E). These salt bridges are absent in the wild-type transporter, where the amine group of K423 instead points toward the central cavity (Supplementary Fig. 9D). Since K423 is a critical residue for S1P binding (Fig. 2C), we speculated that the loss of activity in G91D at pH 7.5 arises from the forming of these salt bridges that restrict K423 from engaging S1P. At low pH, protonation of D91 and D95 disrupts these interactions, allowing K423 to bind S1P, thus enabling the transport activity. Collectively, these findings identify the D91–K423–D95 triad as a key structural element that enables proton coordination and S1P transport in the G91D mutant. The G91D substitution unveils how subtle rearrangements in local charge networks can reprogram the transport mechanism of MFSD2B.

Discussion

Here we provide a structural and mechanistic basis for MFSD2B-mediated S1P transport. By combining cryo-EM, functional assays, and molecular dynamics simulations, we reveal how this transporter accommodates and translocates S1P. Our structural data revealed MFSD2B in an outward-facing conformation. We were unable to capture it in an inward-facing conformation, despite extensive efforts using varying substrates, detergents, lipid compositions, and nanodisc scaffolds. Nevertheless, comparison with its closest paralog MFSD2A suggests that MFSD2B also operates via a rocker-switch mechanism.

Our data show that the TM7/TM8/TM10 hydrophobic pocket forms a cleft with a crucial role in S1P fatty tail binding. Previous studies on MFSD2A suggest both TM5/TM8 and TM2/TM11 openings can potentially serve as gates for its lipophilic ligand’s binding and entry. In contrast, in our MD simulations of MFSD2B, S1P shows a higher propensity towards the TM2/TM11 gate, which widens substantially for S1P exit. Mutagenesis results of residues on TM2/TM11 but not TM5/TM8 highlight the importance of the former gate for the transport activity. In addition, proteoliposome-based and RBC-based S1P uptake assays suggest that MFSD2B functions as a uniporter, and umbrella sampling and PMF calculations demonstrate that S1P can enter the transporter through the TM2/TM11 opening via an energetically favorable pathway. This suggests that the gate between TM2 and TM11 may serve as a major route for both S1P entry and release in MFSD2B. Although the MD simulations provided structural insights regarding S1P binding and translocation, variability in conformational sampling was observed among replicates, highlighting the limitations of atomistic MD simulations in capturing the full conformational landscape of MFSD2B. Therefore, the proposed transport pathway should be interpreted together with the structural, mutagenesis, and transport data.

Two positively charged residues, K88 and K423, line the central cavity and engage the headgroup of S1P in a stepwise manner during translocation, akin to mechanisms observed in MFSD2A and SPNS2. The binding of S1P is sufficient to trigger conformational transitions in MFSD2B, enabling lipid translocation across the membrane. In order to import LPC/LPE, human MFSD2A requires co-transport of sodium, which binds to residues D93 and D97. In contrast, MFSD2BWT does not rely on a proton or Na+ gradient to transport S1P, and residue G91 (the position equivalent to MFSD2A D93) is likely responsible for this difference between the two related transporters. Indeed, mutating G91 to D converted MFSD2B into a proton-dependent transporter, with G91D and D95 (equivalent to MFSD2A D97) playing crucial roles in the proton-sensing mechanism, as they would coordinate with K423 to form salt bridges, thus interfering with S1P binding at physiological pH. At lower pHs, D91 and D95 would be protonated, disrupting salt bridges and releasing K423 to engage with the phosphate group of S1P. Thus, the appearance of G91 in MFSD2B has allowed it to adopt a facilitated diffusion mechanism to transport S1P. Erythrocytes continuously take up circulating sphingosine for S1P synthesis via SphK16. It is shown that the concentration of S1P in the cells is significantly higher than plasma S1P45. Thus, adopting uniporter function could provide a low-energy pathway by which MFSD2B releases and regulates S1P levels in the plasma for signaling functions.

Our structures provide insights into the molecular basis of substrate selectivity (S1P versus LPC) between MFSD2B and MFSD2A. LPC and S1P differ in both their headgroups and the polar neck linking the headgroup to the tail. LPC carries a phosphocholine on a glycerol backbone, while S1P carries a phosphate paired with a primary amine on a sphingoid backbone. The S1P head is therefore more compact, with the ammonium positioned adjacent to the phosphate, whereas in LPC the choline extends the polar region further from the phosphate. In MFSD2A, R89 (zebrafish R84) and K440 (zebrafish K433) form salt bridges with the LPC phosphate, while D93 (zebrafish D87) engages the choline moiety35. In MFSD2B, the equivalent residues are K88, K423, and G91, respectively. The D93-to-G91 substitution likely contributes to substrate selectivity. In addition, E316 in MFSD2A coordinates the LPC headgroup in a Na⁺-bridged manner during translocation28, whereas the equivalent position in MFSD2B is occupied by Q301. This difference may further contribute to the preference for S1P over LPC. Beyond these specific residues, we speculate that broader differences in the local pocket environment also shape selectivity, with features that recognize not only the headgroup but also the polar neck region. The more compact head–neck arrangement of S1P may fit the MFSD2B cavity better than the longer headgroup of LPC.

In summary, our work reveals the molecular basis by which MFSD2B operates to transport S1P in hematopoietic cells. Further work will be required to fully understand how S1P flips its orientation within the transporter during this transport cycle and how it is delivered to albumin or apoM/HDL, which are the major carriers of S1P in blood.

For MFSD2BEM, a maltose-binding protein (MBP) was inserted at the N-terminus, followed by a helical linker, truncated MFSD2B protein (residues 41–504), a DARPin (off7) at the C-terminus46, and a mVenus tag. Proteoliposome-based transport assay

Liposomes were generated by combining 80 mol% POPC and 20 mol% cholesterol in buffer A (20 mM HEPES, 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.5)32. The lipid mixture was hydrated and extruded to form unilamellar vesicles, which were subsequently supplemented with 0.1% (w/v) fatty acid–free bovine serum albumin (BSA). To reconstitute MFSD2B, 50 μg of purified protein was incorporated into 160 μL of preformed liposomes in the presence of 0.11% Triton X-100. The detergent was removed using SM-2 Bio-Beads (Bio-Rad) under gentle agitation at 4 °C for 2 hours. Following detergent removal, the resulting proteoliposomes were pelleted and washed twice with buffer A, then resuspended in 320 μL of the same buffer. Liposomes processed in parallel without protein insertion served as negative controls. For uptake assays, MFSD2B proteoliposomes or control liposomes were incubated with 2.5 μM [3H]-S1P or 2.5 μM NBD-S1P at 37 °C for 1 hour. Reactions were terminated by the addition of 1 mL cold buffer A, followed by three rounds of centrifugation and washing. [3H]-S1P uptake was quantified by Liquid Scintillation Counter. For fluorescent assays, NBD-labeled lipids were extracted from liposomes using methanol:chloroform (2:1, v/v), spotted onto silica gel TLC plates, and visualized using a fluorescence imaging system (ChemiDoc MP, Bio-Rad).

Cryo-EM sample preparation and data acquisition

Protein samples were concentrated to 10 mg/mL, and 0.4 mM fluorinated octyl maltoside and an MBP-specific nanobody (at a molar ratio of 1:1.2, MFSD2B:saposin) were added49. The samples were then centrifuged to remove insoluble S1P prior to grid preparation. Aliquots of 3.5 μl protein samples were applied to plasma-cleaned UltrAuFoil R1.2/1.3 300 mesh grids (Quantifoil). The grids were blotted for 1.5 s and plunged into liquid ethane using a Vitrobot Mark IV (FEI) operated at 10 °C and 100% humidity. The grids were loaded onto a 300 kV Titan Krios transmission electron microscope for data collection. Raw movie stacks were recorded with a K3 camera at a physical pixel size of 0.649 Å per pixel and a nominal defocus range of 1.1–2.1 μm. The exposure time for each micrograph was 1.8 s, dose-fractionated into 60 frames with a dose rate of ∼1.0 e–/Å2/s. The data collection parameters are summarized in Supplementary Table 1.

Cryo-EM data processing

The image stacks were gain-normalized and corrected for beam-induced motion using cryoSPARC450. Defocus parameters were estimated from motion-corrected micrographs in cryoSPARC4. Micrographs unsuitable for further analysis were excluded after manual inspection. Particle picking (blob, template, and TOPAZ pickers51) and two-dimensional classification were performed in cryoSPARC4. Selected particles from both pickers were merged, and duplicates were removed. Iterative three-dimensional classification was then carried out, followed by ab initio reconstruction and heterogeneous refinement to eliminate suboptimal particles. The selected particles were refined using non-uniform refinement52, followed by local refinement with soft masks encompassing MFSD2B to further improve map quality. Mask-corrected FSC curves were calculated in cryoSPARC4, and resolutions were reported based on the 0.143 FSC criterion.

Model building and refinement

An initial model of human MFSD2B was generated by AlphaFold2. This model was docked into the density map using Chimera53. The model was then refined iteratively; using Coot54, ISOLDE55, and Phenix56;. Structural model validation was performed using Phenix and MolProbity57. Figures were prepared using ChimeraX58.

Molecular dynamics simulation

All molecular dynamics simulations were performed using the GROMACS 2024.3 software59. Our structure was embedded in an erythrocyte-inspired asymmetric lipid bilayer by CHARMM-GUI41,42,60. The outer leaflet was composed of 40 mol% cholesterol, 27 mol% POPC, 26 mol% PSM, and 7 mol% POPE, while the inner leaflet contained 40 mol% cholesterol, 9 mol% POPC, 5 mol% PSM, 26 mol% POPE, 18 mol% POPS, 1 mol% POPI, and 1 mol% POPA. The system was solvated with TIP3P water and neutralized with 0.15 M NaCl. The CHARMM36m force field was used61. Each system was equilibrated according to the standard multi-step CHARMM-GUI protocol, during which positional restraints were gradually released before production simulations. Protonation states of titratable residues were assigned during CHARMM-GUI system preparation assuming physiological pH conditions, approximately pH 7.4. Standard protonation states were retained unless otherwise specified. For the S1P-bound MFSD2B system, three independent 500 ns production runs were performed. The membrane was oriented in the xy-plane, with the z-axis corresponding to the membrane normal. Semi-isotropic pressure coupling was applied at 1 bar using the C-rescale barostat with a coupling time of 5 ps62, allowing independent scaling of the membrane plane (xy) and the membrane normal (z). Temperature was maintained at 310.15 K using the v-rescale thermostat with a coupling time of 1 ps63. Periodic boundary conditions were applied in all directions. Bonds involving hydrogen atoms were constrained with the LINCS algorithm, allowing a 2 fs integration time step64. Long-range electrostatics were treated using PME with a real-space cutoff of 1.2 nm65. Lennard-Jones interactions were also truncated at 1.2 nm, with force-switching applied between 1.0 and 1.2 nm, in accordance with CHARMM force-field recommendations. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using protein Cα atoms after least-squares fitting. The trajectories from the three simulations were combined and projected onto a common PCA space to analyze conformational sampling. To define the sampled conformational ensemble, protein Cα RMSD-based GROMOS clustering66 was performed on the combined three-replicate State 2b trajectory using a 0.20 nm cutoff. The replicate contribution analysis showed the sampling time contributed by each independent simulation to the major structural clusters, and the displayed structures correspond to the representative central structures of Cluster 1 and Cluster 2, which accounted for 61.56% and 35.18% of the sampled frames, respectively (see Supplementary Fig. 5A–C).

For umbrella sampling calculations, S1P was initially positioned near the TM2/TM11 opening outside the central cavity. Umbrella sampling combined with the GROMACS weighted histogram analysis method (WHAM) was used to evaluate the PMF. Initial steered molecular dynamics simulations were performed to generate starting structures for the umbrella windows. Starting structures were extracted along the reaction coordinate and assigned to their corresponding umbrella positions. The reaction coordinate was defined as the center-of-mass distance between the S1P headgroup and a reference group formed by residues lining the S1P-binding cavity. During pulling, a harmonic restraint with a force constant of 1000 kJ mol−1 nm−2 was applied along the reaction coordinate, and the umbrella reference position was moved at a rate of −0.001 nm ps−1. Each umbrella window was equilibrated under restraint prior to a 30 ns production simulation. An initial set of 20 umbrella windows was generated for each replicate with approximately uniform spacing along the reaction coordinate. Additional windows were added at reaction coordinate positions where histogram overlap between neighboring windows was insufficient, resulting in a total umbrella sampling time of over 1.8 μs across the three replicates. The PMF profiles were reconstructed from the biased distributions using WHAM. Histogram overlap, cumulative time PMF convergence, and block analysis were performed to assess the reliability and temporal stability of the reconstructed PMF. For independent replicates, PMFs were aligned to a common reference region before averaging, and the final PMF was reported as the mean ± standard deviation among replicates.
