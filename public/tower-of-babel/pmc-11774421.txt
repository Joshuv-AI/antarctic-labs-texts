Effect of Millets Once a Day on Glycaemic Control among Women with Gestational Diabetes Mellitus in a Tertiary Care Setting – A Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Mahadevan Duraiswamy, Venkatachalam Jayaseelan, Jayalakshmy Ramakrishnan, Sasirekha Rengaraj, Yuvaraj Krishnamoorthy, Mohammed Kais, Murali Subbaiah
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) affects 14% of pregnancies globally, with a prevalence of 9–16% in India. Low-glycaemic index (GI) foods like millets may help control glycaemia in GDM. This study compared glycaemic control between GDM patients consuming millets once a day (MOD) and those receiving enhanced medical nutrition therapy (MNT) for 1 month.Methods:A parallel-arm randomized controlled trial was conducted among 224 GDM mothers at a tertiary centre from April 2022 to December 2023. Participants were randomized into two groups: The Enhanced MNT group received standard care with education materials, while the MOD group received 200 g of millets daily for 1 month and recipes. Follow-ups were at 2 weeks and 1 month. The primary outcome was the difference in glycaemic control between groups, analysed with a 95% confidence interval (CI) and P < 0.05 significance.Results:Of the 219 participants who completed the study (97.8%), adherence was 77.6% in the MOD group and 78.5% in the Enhanced MNT group. In an intention-to-treat analysis, an additional 11.6% (95% CI: -1.5% to 24.7%) in the MOD group achieved glycaemic control compared to the Enhanced MNT group, which was not statistically significant (P = 0.083). However, the MOD group had a significant mean post-prandial blood glucose reduction of -4.55 (95% CI: -8.55 to -0.56; P = 0.025).Conclusion:Both interventions effectively controlled glycaemic levels, with the MOD group showing slightly better post-prandial glucose control. Adherence to the protocol was high.Trial Registration:CTRI Registry CTRI/2022/04/042013.

INTRODUCTION

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is a widespread issue during pregnancy, impacting about 14% of all live births globally.[1] In India, the rates range from 7% to as high as 16% in West zones, with 13% in South zones like Tamil Nadu.[2] Women of South Asian descent, especially those from India, face a higher risk of developing GDM.[3] Despite better screening practices, not all cases are detected, which can lead to complications for both the mother and baby if not managed properly.[4] GDM management typically includes diet changes and lifestyle modifications, with medical nutrition therapy (MNT) playing a key role.[56] This often involves eating low-glycaemic index (GI) foods and physical activity.[7] However, about 30–40% of pregnant women with GDM need additional medical treatment to manage their blood sugar levels effectively.[8] A few studies have explored the effects of low-GI foods on perinatal outcomes[9101112] and nutritional counselling[13] in pregnant women with GDM. Despite millets’ traditional and nutritional importance, research on their impact on glycaemic control has been limited among individuals with diabetes mellitus in the general population.[141516] Some research suggests that millets might help improve insulin sensitivity,[15] but there are limited data on the effect on glycaemic control among pregnant women with GDM. To address the research gaps on millet and GDM, this study aims to determine whether eating millets at least once a day can improve glycaemic control more than other low-GI foods in GDM.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design

Open-label, parallel-arm, randomized controlled trial with a 1:1 allocation ratio.

Study setting

The study was conducted in the Obstetrics and Gynaecology Department at the Women and Child Hospital (WCH), a tertiary care hospital. It is a tertiary care facility with 300 beds and offers high-quality care to pregnant women. GDM mothers are examined by both residents and consultants, with diet counselling provided by a dietician. Universal screening for GDM is offered to all pregnant women at the first visit and repeated at 24–28 weeks of gestation, following the International Association of Diabetes in Pregnancy (IADPSG) criteria.[2] Suppose a pregnant woman is found to have abnormal blood glucose values in her OGTT, such as fasting blood glucose (FBG) ≥92 mg/dl, postprandial blood glucose (PPBG) ≥180 mg/dl at 1 hour, or PPBG ≥153 mg/dl at 2 hours. In that case, the patient was diagnosed with GDM, and she was advised to follow medical nutrition therapy, oral hypoglycaemic drugs, or insulin, depending on her blood glucose levels.

Study participants

All mothers who were diagnosed with GDM, who attended an obstetrics and gynaecology-outpatient department (OG-OPD) in a tertiary care hospital, Puducherry, between April 2022 and December 2023, were approached and enrolled by consecutive sampling in the study and were stratified into MNT only or MNT with OHA/insulin. Those with a previous history of recurrent miscarriage, pregnancy followed by assisted reproductive technology, or pre-existing medical conditions (chronic hypertension, chronic liver, or kidney disease) were excluded.

Sample size calculation

Assuming that the proportion of women achieving the glycaemic goal in the Enhanced MNT group is 81% and that in the millets once a day (MOD) group is 95%,[14] the ratio between the two groups is 1:1, the power is 80%, the confidence interval (CI) is 95%, and the sample size is estimated at 202 (101 in each arm) using OpenEpi software version 3.0. After adjusting for loss to follow-up of 10%, the final sample size was calculated to be 224 (112 in each arm).

Randomization and blinding

A random sequence was generated via Random Allocation software version 1.0 for 224 participants allocated into the MOD arm and the Enhanced MNT arm based on block randomization with varying block sizes of 2, 4, 6, and 8. The randomization sequence was generated for each stratum (MNT only or MNT with OHA/Insulin) and concealed from the researcher via sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes (SNOSEs). The researcher allocated pregnant women with GDM into the MOD arm and enhanced MNT arm under each stratum through sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes after completing the baseline assessment of the enrolled participants and when it was time to allocate the intervention. Because the intervention involved health education and the provision of a take-home ration, blinding the investigator and the participants was not possible.

Study procedure

Enrolment

All pregnant women diagnosed with GDM receiving care from OG-OPD were assessed according to the eligibility criteria. After informed consent was obtained, the patients were enrolled in the study. They were stratified as MNT only and MNT with OHA and/or insulin and allocated into either the enhanced MNT or MOD group on the basis of the randomization sequence for each stratum.

Baseline data collection

The baseline assessment of enrolled pregnant women took place in the OG-OPD, with 30 minutes allocated to each participant. A structured questionnaire, translated into Tamil and back-translated into English, was utilised to collect socio-demographic, clinical, and anthropometric details. The primary outcome parameters, FBG/PPBG, were obtained from recent hospital records. Baseline nutritional status was assessed via 24-hour dietary recall, and adherence was evaluated via the Perceived Dietary Adherence Questionnaire (PDAQ). Physical activity levels were gauged via the Pregnancy Physical Activity Questionnaire (PPAQ). Dietary recall data were analysed via DietCal software.

Intervention

Millets once a Day (MOD) arm: Participants in the MOD arm received nutritional counselling on millets and intermediate-GI foods through a booklet and a diet diary to record 1-month food intake. Counselling was provided individually to prevent contamination, and the booklet included information on GDM, millet recipes (12 locally practised traditional recipes), physical activity recommendations, and standard care. The participants received a 1-month supply of locally available millets, 30 packets of 200 g pre-weighed locally available millets [Finger Millet flour (Ragi), Pearl Millet flour (Kambu), Kodo Millet (Varagu), Little Millet (Saamai)], with the advice to consume once a day of 200 g of millets for 1 month and followed up at 2 weeks and 1 month. Quality millets were procured from the hospital’s diet department, ensuring variety to prevent monotony. Personalised counselling emphasised the importance of daily millet consumption and discouraged sharing to ensure proper nutrition for expectant mothers.

Enhanced MNT arm: The participants in the Enhanced MNT arm received standard care, including routine advice from a healthcare practitioner and dietician. The participants were recruited from the OPD, given nutritional counselling on low-GI foods through a booklet, and advised to perform physical activity for 30 minutes daily. Counselling was provided to one participant at a time to ensure that adequate time was spent and to prevent contamination. The booklet was equipped with illustrations of the GI categories of common food items (cereals, vegetables, fruits, meat and dairy products), with a focus on low-GI foods other than millets and a diet diary to record food intake for 1 month.

Endline assessment: At the end of 2 and 4 weeks (1 month), the biochemical parameters FBG and PPBG were collected. At 4 weeks, end-line assessment of the participants was performed in the OG-OPD within 1 week of buffer time. It included anthropometric details (weight), nutrition adherence with quantity and frequency of intake assessed via the 24-hour dietary recall method, a diet diary, the PDAQ, and the physical activity level assessed via the PPAQ.

Data collection tool

The PDAQ was used to assess dietary adherence based on questions regarding the food items consumed in the past 7 days. It has been used in pregnant women with GDM in the same setting. It has nine questions, and the score ranges from 0 to 63. A value equal to or above the mean indicates good adherence, and a value below the mean indicates poor adherence.[17]

Physical activity levels were assessed via the PPAQ. Physical activity was assessed by measuring the time spent in various activities per day per week and converting it into metabolic equivalents (METs) per hour. The results were categorised as sedentary (<1.5 METs), light (1.5–3.0 METs), moderate (3.0–6.0 METs), or vigorous (>6.0 METs). It is a semi-quantitative questionnaire and has 33 (31 multiple-choice questions and 2 open-ended questions) items. It includes household/caregiving, occupational, and sports/exercise activities.[18]

Primary outcome: Glycaemic goal

The glycaemic goal was defined as achieving either FBG <95 mg/dl or 2-hour PPBG <120 mg/dl[19] and categorised as the glycaemic goal achieved: yes or no.

Statistical analysis

The data were entered into Epicollect (open-access software) and analysed via Stata software (version 14). Continuous variables such as age were summarised as the mean (SD) or median inter-quartile range (IQR) depending upon the normality of distribution. Categorical variables such as education, family history of DM, and treatment history were summarised as proportions and compared via the Chi-square test. Intention to treat (ITT) was performed. Multiple imputations via the linear regression method for FBG and PPBG variables were performed on participants with missing outcomes. Cochran’s Q test was applied to test the absolute difference between the proportions of glycaemic control in both groups at different time points, with a 95% confidence interval (CI). A generalised estimating equation (GEE) was utilised to determine the change in glycaemic control between the MOD group and the enhanced MNT groups adjusted for the time and group–time interaction, with the Poisson family, log link, robust standard errors, and effect estimates reported as risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A P value of 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical aspects

The study was conducted according to the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki (2013), and all procedures involving human subjects/patients were approved by the JIPMER Institute Ethics Committee (IEC) vide letter JIP/IEC/2022/06 on 22nd February 2022. The study was prospectively registered with the Clinical Trials Registry – India with the ref. number CTRI/2022/04/042013. Written informed consent was obtained for participation in the study and use of the patient data for research and educational purposes.

There was an absolute difference of 11.6% (95% CI: -1.5% to 24.7%) for additional pregnant women in the MOD group who achieved the glycaemic goal, which was not significant (P value: 0.083). The unadjusted risk ratio (RR) for achieving glycaemic goals in the MOD group at the endline was 1.01 (95% CI: 0.93–1.09), which was also non-significant (P value: 0.768).

Adverse events

No serious adverse events were reported during the study period.

DISCUSSION

This study investigated the impact of incorporating once-a-day millet into the diets of pregnant women with GDM in India. This study compared the effectiveness of MOD to that of an enhanced MNT featuring low-GI foods other than millets. Both interventions were supplemented with health education and counselling. This study aimed to evaluate glycaemic control over 2 weeks and 1 month. The literature concerning the effects of millet on GDM is scarce, so studies on the effects of millet on diabetes mellitus and low-GI foods on GDM were used for comparison. Both intervention groups showed significant improvements in glycaemic control, with marginal differences. The MOD group yielded a slightly more significant reduction of -4.55 mg/dl in the mean PPBG. Adherence to prescribed diets was high in both groups, with an average of 78.1%, likely due to comprehensive nutritional counselling and booklets with millet recipes. This finding shows that glycaemic control is better in both interventions and millets have better effects on post-prandial blood glucose control when adherence is better.

Effects of millets on diabetes mellitus

Compared with studies on millet effects on diabetes mellitus, our study revealed significant reductions in FBG and PPBG. This surpassed the results of Jali et al.’s (2014)[14] study on type 2 diabetes patients, which used a foxtail-based diet. Our finding of a decrease in FBG also aligns with the findings of Geetha et al. (2020),[20] who studied pre-diabetic subjects, possibly because of the similar millet types used. However, Ren X et al.’s (2018)[15] study in China and Sobhana et al.’s (2020)[21] study in Hyderabad reported fewer reductions. These differences could be attributed to the different millets used, study designs, populations, and sample sizes. Interestingly, our PPBG reduction matched the meta-analysis on long-term millet consumption by Anitha S et al. (2021)[16] as the type of millet (finger, little, kodo, and pearl millet) we provided in our study was included in the meta-analysis and the long duration of 1 month in our study resulted in similar PPBG reduction.

Effects of a low-GI diet on GDM

Compared with relevant studies assessing low-GI diets in GDM, Grant et al. (2011)[9] reported a non-significant difference in glycaemic control between low-GI and control groups. Like our findings, Hu et al. (2014)[22] reported an 18.7% reduction in post-prandial blood glucose (PPBG) with low-GI intervention. Lv et al. (2019)[13] reported a 20% reduction in FBG and a 23.2% reduction in PPBG with glycaemic load intervention, echoing our results. Studies from Iran and Australia revealed reductions in FBG and PPBG with low-GI foods, consistent with our findings. Owing to their high fibre content and comparable GI, millets likely contributed to improved glycaemic control in our MOD group.

Adherence

In our study, adherence to MNT among pregnant women with GDM was high, with 78.1% following the protocol. Additionally, 94.5% of the patients in the MOD group and 85.71% in the Enhanced MNT group were classified as adherent based on the PDAQ scores. This contrasts with findings from Balas-Nakash et al. (2010)[23] in Mexico, where the average adherence was 55%, and only 50% achieved optimal glycaemic control. Mustafa et al. (2021)[24] in New Zealand reported moderate adherence to dietary recommendations among GDM women, with none fully adhering. However, Perichart-Perera et al. (2022)[25] reported high adherence rates (87%) regardless of metformin use, which is consistent with our study. Dempsey et al. (2023)[26] reported improved diet scores among pregnant women in an RCT, but adherence declined in late pregnancy. In contrast, our study revealed increased adherence to physical activity, possibly due to the high-risk nature of GDM and participant demographics. These findings highlight the importance of tailored dietary counselling and support for optimal adherence among pregnant women with GDM.

Strengths and limitations

The study has several strengths: a pioneering investigation of the glycaemic effect of millets in India amid rising millet advocacy, employment of a robust parallel-arm randomized control trial design, rigorous methodology ensuring allocation concealment and prevention of contamination, validated and translated assessment tools, comprehensive adherence evaluation methods, and high response rates with minimal loss to follow-up, indicative of strong participant engagement. Limitations include a small sample size affecting the results, exclusion criteria potentially restricting population eligibility, inability to blind due to the intervention nature, and ethical constraints that hinder the study of the isolated effect of millets.

Implications for public health and practice

It is important to educate pregnant women with GDM about the benefits of millets and low-GI foods and their impact on glycaemic control, particularly in regulating post-meal blood glucose levels. This information can be effectively communicated through various channels, including health education booklets. In addition, practical measures such as personalised counselling and dietary tracking can be employed to increase adherence among pregnant women with GDM. Our research aims to advocate for including millets as a prominent nutritional intervention in managing GDM, capitalising on initiatives such as the ‘International Year of Millets’ to promote greater awareness.

Recommendations for future research

Additional research involving biomarkers is needed to investigate the precise impact of consuming millets on post-meal blood sugar levels in pregnant women with gestational diabetes to understand the underlying mechanism, as well as its effects on pregnancy outcomes. Furthermore, there is a need for research to comprehensively understand the dynamics involved in developing, adopting, delivering, and ensuring the acceptability of interventions from a healthcare system perspective, particularly in primary care settings, to make them accessible to the broader community. This is important as millet consumption can potentially benefit the overall population.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, both interventions achieved commendable glycaemic control while incorporating MOD into the diet of pregnant women with GDM led to marginally better post-prandial blood glucose levels. The study also emphasised the importance of personalised dietary counselling and support in managing GDM effectively, which led to better adherence rates. Millets demonstrated a significant reduction in post-prandial blood glucose.
