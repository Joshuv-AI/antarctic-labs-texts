High-resolution cryo-EM structures of small protein–ligand complexes near the theoretical size limit
Authors: Kunwoong Park, Youngki Yoo, Hyunbum Jeon, Kiju Choi, Hanseong Kim, Eunju Kwon, Hyun-Ho Lim, Dong Young Kim, Kyoung Tai No
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) is a widely used technique for determining macromolecular structures at near-atomic resolution. The theoretical lower limit of particle sizes suitable for cryo-EM structural analysis is estimated to be 38 kDa; typical constraints involve factors such as image contrast and particle alignment accuracy. In this study, we present cryo-EM structures of two protein–ligand complexes near this lower size threshold. First, the structure of the maltose-binding protein complexed with maltose, with a structurally ordered mass of 40.8 kDa, was determined at a resolution of 2.4 Å; both the maltose and water molecules were clearly identified in this structure. The second structure was the kinase domain of human PLK1 complexed with onvansertib, with a structurally ordered mass of 31.6 kDa, below the theoretical 38 kDa limit; this domain was determined at a resolution of 3.4 Å using a gold-supported grid in the presence of β-octyl-glucoside. The density map clearly shows the backbone of PLK1 secondary structure, and the onvansertib. These results demonstrate that cryo-EM can be effectively employed to determine structures of small proteins or domains, and to perform structure-based drug screening for small proteins, without requiring structural fiducials for particle alignment.Subject terms: Cryoelectron microscopy, Cryoelectron microscopy, Proteins, Permeation and transport

Introduction

Cryo-electron microscopy (Cryo-EM) is an advanced technique used to determine the structures of biological macromolecules at near-atomic resolution. Moreover, cryo-EM enables the structural analysis of frozen macromolecules without requiring crystallization, which is a major barrier in protein crystallography. The methodological advantages of cryo-EM minimize structural deformation and reduce constraints on dynamic conformational changes that can occur during crystallization. These benefits are particularly valuable for structure-based drug screening, which requires dynamic structural information. However, the determination of cryo-EM structures has been limited by particle size. Accounting for factors such as radiation damage and signal-to-noise ratio, the theoretical lower limit of particle size for single-particle reconstruction is estimated to be 38 kDa1. Recent advances in detector sensitivity and data-processing algorithms have led to significant improvements in the size of macromolecules for cryo-EM structure determination2. These enhancements have enabled the determination of structures of small proteins (less than 100 kDa) using a conventional 200-keV transmission electron microscope (TEM)3. Recently, two cryo-EM structures of RNA-containing complexes approaching the size limit have been determined. The structure of the SAM-IV riboswitch RNA (40 kDa) was determined at a resolution of 3.7 Å using conventional single-particle reconstruction methods; meanwhile, the structure of an ACA2 dimer–RNA complex (40 kDa) was determined at a resolution of 2.6 Å using the deep-learning-based Blush regularization method4–6. While algorithmic advances, such as Blush, likely played a critical role, the successful determination of these structures also appears to have benefited from the intrinsic contrast advantage of RNA molecules, whose phosphate groups strongly scatter electrons, facilitating higher-resolution visualization compared to proteins7. The smallest protein structures determined using cryo-EM include NADPH oxidase (46 kDa)8, streptavidin (52 kDa)9, and SRP68/72 (58 kDa)10. Additionally, with respect to membrane proteins, cryo-EM structures of human GLUT4 (a structurally ordered mass of 47 kDa) and viral GPCR (39 kDa) embedded in detergent micelles and lipid nanodiscs have been determined11,12.

In this study, we present cryo-EM structures of two small protein–ligand complexes near the theoretical size limit for cryo-EM. The maltose-binding protein (MBP) complexed with maltose was determined with a structurally ordered mass of 40.8 kDa at a resolution of 2.4 Å, and the human polo-like kinase 1 kinase domain (hPLK1KD) complexed with the anti-cancer compound onvansertib was determined with a structurally ordered mass of 31.6 kDa at a resolution of 3.4 Å. Both structures were determined using conventional single-particle reconstruction methods without symmetry enforcement or structural fiducials. This study highlights the potential of traditional cryo-EM methods to achieve high-resolution structures of small protein–ligand complexes that exceed the theoretical size limit, without requiring structural fiducials, thereby opening avenues in drug discovery.

Results and discussion

Cryo-EM structure of MBP complexed with maltose

Escherichia coli MBP is a 40.7 kDa protein (residues 27–396, without the N-terminal signal sequence). MBP is commonly used as a fusion tag for recombinant protein expression, as it enhances the solubility of target proteins and simplifies purification through maltose-binding affinity chromatography13,14. In this study, we purified MBP and employed conventional single-particle reconstruction methods to determine its cryo-EM structure. Recombinant MBP was expressed in E. coli using an MBP-fusion vector system and purified through maltose-binding affinity chromatography followed by size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) (Supplementary Fig. 1a). MBP was initially applied to a Quantifoil grid. A total of 2713 micrographs were collected over a nominal defocus range of −0.8 to −2.4 μm, using a 300 kV TEM. After motion correction and contrast transfer function (CTF) estimation, 2341 micrographs were selected based on a CTF-fit resolution cutoff of 3.5 Å, with a median CTF-fit resolution of 2.95 Å (Supplementary Fig. 1). The average defocus values across micrographs ranged from 0.1 to 3.5 μm, with a median of 1.7 μm (Supplementary Fig. 2). The cryo-EM map of MBP obtained from the Quantifoil grid (MBPQuantifoil) was reconstructed at an overall resolution of 2.8 Å, using 429,425 particles after two-dimensional (2D) and three-dimensional (3D) classifications (Supplementary Fig. 1). The local resolution of the density map ranged from 2.5 to 2.9 Å (Supplementary Fig. 1b).

Next, we applied MBP to an UltrAuFoil grid to assess whether the resolution could be improved. Gold supports for cryo-EM grids have been shown to reduce beam-induced particle motion and to produce thinner, more uniform ice layers compared to carbon supports15,16. This improvement allows for lower defocus values, which can lead to a stronger CTF envelope at high spatial frequencies and to higher-resolution signals17,18. A total of 3328 micrographs were collected from this grid in a nominal defocus range of −0.4 to −1.8 μm (Supplementary Fig. 3), and 2947 micrographs were selected based on a CTF-fit resolution cutoff of 3.5 Å after motion correction and CTF estimation (Supplementary Fig. 4a). Compared to the micrographs collected from the Quantifoil grid, those from the UltrAuFoil grid showed narrower average defocus ranges (0.1–1.8 μm), lower median defocus values (1.1 μm), and improved median CTF-fit resolution (2.77 Å; Supplementary Fig. 2), with well-defined Thon rings and low median astigmatism (185 Å), supporting the suitability of these micrograph for high-resolution reconstruction (Supplementary Fig. 3). The cryo-EM density map of MBP from the UltrAuFoil grid (MBPUltrAuFoil) was reconstructed from 509,884 particles selected using 2D and 3D classifications at an overall resolution of 2.4 Å (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 4). Particles for MBPUltrAuFoil were significantly enriched in low average defocus bins of less than 1.0 μm, compared to those for MBPQuantifoil. Specifically, 152,097 of 509,884 particles (29.8%) for MBPUltrAuFoil had average defocus values below 1.0 μm, while only 28,569 of 435,429 particles (6.72%) for MBPQuantifoil were within this range (Supplementary Fig. 2e, f). The conical Fourier shell correlation (FSC) area ratio (cFAR) was 0.42, and the local resolution of the density map ranged from 2.0 to 2.4 Å (Supplementary Fig. 4b, d). The map quality was sufficient to build an atomic model of MBP (Fig. 1a–c and Supplementary Fig. 5a–c). The central holes of the maltose pyranose ring and residues W88 and W258 were observed in the density map (Fig. 1d–f). Moreover, the atomic model of MBP was fully constructed using the de novo model-building program ModelAngelo19 (Supplementary Fig. 5d). The final model contained no outliers in the Ramachandran plot, with a map correlation coefficient (CCmask) of 0.83 and a Q-score of 0.82 (Supplementary Table 1).

The cryo-EM structure of MBPUltrAuFoil includes one MBP monomer (residues 28–395), one maltose molecule, and 207 water molecules. This structurally ordered mass corresponds to approximately 40.8 kDa, excluding the water molecules. MBP exhibits a two-lobe conformation (Fig. 1g, h). Lobe-1 features an α/β fold characterized by a central β-sheet with strands arranged in the order β2, β1, β3, β13, β6, and β5/β14, with peripheral helices (α1–α5 and α11–α14). This lobe also includes strands β4 and β15, which interact with β13. Similarly, Lobe-2 forms an α/β fold with a central β-sheet composed of β8, β11, β7, and β12, along with peripheral helices (α6–α10 and α15–α16) and an additional two-stranded β-sheet formed by β9 and β10 (Fig. 1i, j). The cryo-EM structure of the MBP–maltose complex closely resembles the crystal structure of the MBP–maltose complex (PDB ID: 1ANF)20 (Fig. 1k; RMSD of 0.5 Å for 367 Cα atoms), whereas it differs from the crystal structure of apo-MBP (PDB ID: 1JW4)21 (Fig. 1l; RMSD of 4.0 Å for 367 Cα atoms). In the cryo-EM structure, the maltose molecule is bound within the cleft between the two lobes, forming hydrogen bonds with residues K41, W88, R92, E137, E179, and Y181 (Supplementary Fig. 5e). These interactions are consistent with those observed in the crystal structure of MBP (Supplementary Fig. 5f). This analysis suggests that the cryo-EM structure can elucidate the structural features of the MBP–maltose complex with accuracy comparable to that of the high-resolution MBP crystal structure.

Cryo-EM structure of hPLK1KD complexed with onvansertib

PLK1 is a serine/threonine protein kinase that activates the G2/M transition and regulates meiosis during the cell cycle22. As a proto-oncogene, PLK1 is frequently overexpressed in tumor cells, making it a key target for cancer drug development23. Onvansertib is a PLK1 inhibitor that competitively binds to the ATP-binding site of the kinase24. To investigate the potential of cryo-EM for analyzing small protein–ligand complexes, we performed a cryo-EM structural analysis of hPLK1KD (residues 13–345) in complex with onvansertib, with an approximate size of 37.4 kDa. hPLK1KD was mutated to prevent phosphorylation of the activation loop in the kinase (T120V mutation) and was expressed using a baculovirus–insect cell system, which aids in the folding of eukaryotic proteins. hPLK1KD was purified through immobilized metal affinity chromatography followed by SEC (Supplementary Fig. 6a). The purified protein was mixed with onvansertib and applied to an UltrAuFoil grid. A total of 4053 micrographs were acquired, with a nominal defocus range of −0.5 to −2.1 μm. Of these, 3016 micrographs were selected based on a CTF-fit resolution cutoff of 4.0 Å after motion correction and CTF estimation (Supplementary Fig. 6). The cryo-EM micrographs of hPLK1KD exhibited high particle contrast even at low defocus values (−0.5, −0.7, and −1.1 µm), with well-defined Thon rings and low median astigmatism (127 Å) (Supplementary Fig. 7). After 2D and 3D classifications, a cryo-EM density map of the hPLK1KD–onvansertib complex was reconstructed at an overall resolution of 3.1 Å with 134,412 particles (Supplementary Fig. 6). However, angular distribution analysis showed that the particles were strongly biased toward a single orientation (Supplementary Fig. 6e).

To mitigate particle orientation bias, we examined the effects of cryo-EM grids and detergent additives on particle orientation (Fig. 2). First, micrographs were collected using grids with additional supporting films composed of graphene oxide or 2-nm carbon (Fig. 2b, c). However, the particle quality obtained from these micrographs was inferior to that from the UltrAuFoil grid, and no improvement in particle orientation preference was observed (Fig. 2a–c). Following this, detergent effects were investigated by adding cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) or β-octyl-glucoside (β-OG) to the hPLK1KD–onvansertib complex (Fig. 2d, e). CTAB failed to improve particle quality or reduce orientation bias, whereas β-OG significantly reduced orientation bias. Particle classes with different orientations were observed in this 2D classification (Fig. 2e). From this grid, a total of 13,000 micrographs were acquired with a nominal defocus range of −0.6 to −2.0 μm. Of these 7260 micrographs were selected based on a CTF-fit resolution cutoff of 3.5 Å after motion collection and CTF estimation (Supplementary Fig. 8). These micrographs showed comparably high contrast and clear Thon rings with low astigmatism (median: 192 Å), comparable to those obtained without β-OG (Supplementary Figs. 7 and 9). A cryo-EM density map was reconstructed at an overall resolution of 3.4 Å from 200,803 particles (Fig. 3a–d and Supplementary Fig. 8). Although the overall density map resolution was slightly lower than that of the β-OG untreated hPLK1KD–onvansertib complex, significant improvements were observed in key indicators of overall map quality, including model–map correlation (from 0.62 to 0.78 in CCmask; Supplementary Table 1), Q-score (from 0.51 to 0.65; Supplementary Fig. 10a, b), and cFAR (from 0.25 to 0.54; Supplementary Figs. 6d and 8d). Moreover, the density maps of α-helices and β-sheets clearly showed the secondary structure without any loss of density (Fig. 3e–h and Supplementary Fig. 10c–f). The number of residues built during the construction of the atomic model of hPLK1KD using ModelAngelo19 increased from 14 to 202 out of 269 residues (Supplementary Fig. 10g, h).

The final atomic model included one hPLK1KD (residues 38–306) and one onvansertib molecule. This structurally ordered mass corresponded to approximately 31.6 kDa, which is smaller than the actual protein size of 37.9 kDa. In the cryo-EM structure, hPLK1KD exhibited a two-lobe conformation (Fig. 3i, j). The N-lobe featured a β-sandwich composed of two β-sheets interspersed with helices (α1 and α2). One β-sheet was composed of β1, β2, and β5, and the other comprised β3, β4, β6, β7, and β8. The C-lobe formed an α-helical fold with two short β-sheets (β9 and β12, and β10 and β11) (Fig. 3i, j). Superimposition of the cryo-EM structure of hPLK1KD onto the associated crystal structure (PDB ID: 2YAC)24 resulted in an RMSD of 0.9 Å for 269 Cα atoms (Fig. 3k). In the cryo-EM structure, onvansertib bound within the crevice between the two lobes and formed hydrogen bonds with residues K61, D69, C133, R136, and D194 of hPLK1KD (Supplementary Fig. 10i–k). The residues interacting with onvansertib were mostly conserved in the crystal structure of hPLK1KD (Supplementary Fig. 10l), suggesting that the cryo-EM structure can elucidate the structural features of the hPLK1KD–onvansertib complex as accurately as the high-resolution crystal structure.

Implications of cryo-EM determination of small protein–ligand complexes

Various strategies have been developed to overcome size limitations in cryo-EM structural analysis, including phase-plate imaging25,26, graphene supports27, particle symmetry, and engineered scaffolds28,29. Although these techniques enhance alignment accuracy and improve signal-to-noise ratios, they often introduce technical complexity or require extensive structural modifications. In this study, we present high-resolution cryo-EM structures of small protein–ligand complexes near the theoretical size limit for cryo-EM structural analysis, achieved without the use of additional structural fiducials. The cryo-EM density map of the MBP–maltose complex, with a mass of approximately 41.2 kDa and an ordered mass of 40.8 kDa, clearly shows densities for the protein, ligand, and water molecules. Similarly, the map of the hPLK1KD–onvansertib complex provides sufficient resolution for building a model of the protein–ligand complex. Notably, this represents the smallest cryo-EM structure reported to date, with a purified complex of approximately 37.9 kDa and an ordered mass of 31.6 kDa in the density map, to the best of our knowledge, surpassing the previously assumed theoretical size limit.

These results were consistently reproducible across multiple cryo-EM micrograph collections and density map analyses. This demonstrates that high-resolution cryo-EM structures of asymmetric proteins smaller than 40 kDa can be determined using conventional cryo-EM techniques, without relying on molecular symmetry, scaffolding, or specialized equipment. Thus, these findings highlight the potential of traditional cryo-EM methods for elucidating the structures of small proteins that bind chemical drugs. Furthermore, our findings suggest that structural analysis could be extended to macromolecules smaller than the currently accepted minimum size limit. Henderson’s theory1 estimated the size limit based on a total electron dose of 20 e−/Å2. Given that the currently used total electron doses range from 40 to 60 e−/Å2, proteins smaller than 38 kDa can be analyzed through cryo-EM. As image contrast and reconstruction algorithms continue to advance, high-resolution cryo-EM analysis of increasingly small and challenging targets is expected to become more common.

Methods

Protein expression and purification of MBP

MBP (UniProt ID: P0AEX9) was purified as a byproduct during the purification of zebrafish PLK1KD. DNA encoding the target protein, which included an N-terminal tobacco etch virus (TEV) protease cleavage sequence, was inserted between the NdeI and BamHI restriction sites of the pMal-c5x plasmid (Addgene ID: 66999; Addgene, Watertown, MA, USA). The plasmid was introduced into E. coli BL21 (DE3) cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Transformed cells were cultured in 1 L of Luria–Bertani medium (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) at 37 °C. When the optical density at 600 nm reached 0.8, the culture was transferred to a cold room and cooled for 1 h. Protein expression was induced by adding 1 mM isopropyl β-D-thiogalactopyranoside. After culturing at 16 °C for 18 h, cells were harvested by centrifugation at 3000×g for 10 min, resuspended in buffer A (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 400 mM NaCl, and 3 mM DTT), and lysed by sonication. The cell lysate was loaded onto dextrin-Sepharose resin (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA) and eluted with buffer B (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 400 mM NaCl, 3 mM DTT, and 10 mM maltose). The eluate was treated with TEV protease and incubated at 4 °C overnight to remove C-terminal flexible residues. TEV protease was subsequently removed by passing the solution through Ni–NTA resin (Qiagen, Venlo, Limburg, Netherlands). MBP was further purified by size-exclusion chromatography using a Superdex-200 Increase 10/300 GL column (Cytiva) equilibrated with buffer C (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, and 400 mM NaCl).

Protein expression and purification of PLK1KD

DNA encoding hPLK1KD (UniProt ID: P53350; residues 13–345, containing the T210V mutation) was cloned into the pFastBac1 vector with a 6×His tag and a TEV protease cleavage site. Recombinant baculovirus was generated using the Bac-to-Bac system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and transfected into Sf9 insect cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cells were cultured in Sf-900 SFM medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 27 °C for 72 h and then harvested by centrifugation at 3000×g for 10 min. The cell pellet was resuspended in buffer D (50 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 500 mM NaCl, 20 mM imidazole, 1 mM β-mercaptoethanol, 1% NP-40, and 1 mM PMSF) and lysed by sonication. The lysate was centrifuged at 20,000×g for 30 min, and the supernatant was loaded onto Ni–NTA resin (Qiagen). The resin was washed with buffer E (50 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 500 mM NaCl, 20 mM imidazole, and 1 mM β-mercaptoethanol), and the bound protein was eluted using buffer F (50 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 500 mM NaCl, 300 mM imidazole, and 1 mM β-mercaptoethanol). The eluted protein was incubated with TEV protease at 4 °C overnight to remove the 6×His tag. hPLK1KD was further purified through SEC using a HiLoad 26/600 Superdex 200 pg column (Cytiva) equilibrated with buffer G (50 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, and 5 mM tris(cyanoethyl)phosphine).

Cryo-EM data collection

Micrographs of MBP were obtained using Quantifoil R1.2/1.3 300-mesh copper grids and UltrAuFoil R1.2/1.3 300-mesh gold grids (SPI Supplies, West Chester, PA, USA). Quantifoil grids were subjected to glow discharge at 15 mA for 60 s using a PELCO easiGlow system (Ted Pella, Redding, CA, USA) under ambient air at 25 °C, and the UltrAuFoil grids were glow-discharged at 15 mA for 30 s. Subsequently, 3 µL of MBP (1.0 mg/mL) was applied to the grids. Grids were blotted for 5 s at a blot force of 5 using humidity-saturated Whatman No. 1 filter paper (Cytiva) and plunge-frozen in liquid ethane using a Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

To prepare grids containing the hPLK1KD–onvansertib complex, hPLK1KD (0.6 mg/mL) was mixed with onvansertib at a molar ratio of 1:4. Subsequently, 0.05% (w/v) β-OG or 0.25 mM CTAB was added to the hPLK1KD–onvansertib complex to assess the effects of detergent on micrograph quality. Each sample (3 µL) was applied to UltrAuFoil grids (SPI Supplies) and vitrified using the same procedures as those used for the MBP UltrAuFoil grid. To examine the effect of the support film on micrograph particle quality, hPLK1KD–onvansertib complex was applied to graphene oxide grids and thin carbon grids. A Quantifoil R2/2 GO 300-mesh copper grid (Agar Scientific, Stansted, Essex, UK), covered with a graphene oxide film, was not glow-discharged. Meanwhile, the Quantifoil R1.2/1.3 300-mesh copper grid with a 2 nm continuous carbon film (SPI Supplies) was glow-discharged using a PELCO easyGlow system (Ted Pella) at 10 mA for 10 s. The hPLK1KD–onvansertib complex was vitrified on grids with support films using the same procedures as those used for the other grids.

Micrograph data were collected at Baobab AiBIO Co. (Incheon, South Korea) using a 300 kV Titan Krios microscope equipped with a Falcon 4 direct electron detector, a Selectris-X energy filter (10 eV slit width), and a 100 μm objective aperture (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For the tilted micrographs, the stage alpha angle was set to 30°, and micrographs were collected in EPU accurate mode using UltrAuFoil grids containing the hPLK1KD–onvansertib complex treated with β-OG. Data collection parameters are summarized in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2.

Data processing and structure determination

Micrograph processing and map reconstruction were performed using the cryoSPARC software (v4.4.0 for hPLK1KD-onvansertib and v4.7.1 for MBP-maltose)30. To reconstruct the cryo-EM map of MBP from the UltrAuFoil grid (MBPUltrAuFoil), 3328 raw micrographs were motion-corrected using Patch Motion Correction. Defocus values for each micrograph were estimated using Patch CTF Estimation, and 2947 micrographs with a CTF-fit resolution higher than 3.5 Å were selected through manual curation. From these micrographs, a total of 4,347,245 particles were picked, within a diameter range of 50–70 Å using the Blob Picker and extracted with a box size of 300 × 300 pixels for 2D classification. After multiple rounds of 2D classification, 542,631 particles were selected. A separate set of particles was re-picked from the micrographs using the TOPAZ program31 in the cryoSPARC platform, yielding another set of 1,217,135 particles. The two particle sets were combined through additional rounds of 2D classification, yielding a final dataset of 1,063,552 particles. Following two rounds of ab initio 3D reconstruction and hetero-refinement, 509,884 particles were selected. The final MBPUltrAuFoil map was reconstructed after applying reference-based motion correction and non-uniform refinement, with an overall resolution of 2.35 Å, as determined by the gold-standard Fourier shell correlation (FSC) criterion at the 0.143 (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Micrographs of MBP from the Quantifoil grids (MBPQuantifoil) were processed using the same workflow as described for MBPUltAuFoil, resulting in a final map with an overall resolution of 2.80 Å (Supplementary Fig. 1a).

To reconstruct the cryo-EM map of the hPLK1KD–onvansertib complex, 4053 raw micrographs were motion-corrected, defocus values were estimated, and micrographs with a CTF-fit resolution higher than 3.5 Å were selected using the same procedure as that used for MBPUltrAuFoil. A total of 8,352,733 particles were selected from 3016 micrographs using the Template Picker, with templates obtained from an initial dataset. These particles were extracted for 2D classification using a box size of 350 × 350 pixels. After multiple rounds of 2D classification, a refined set of 577,458 particles was selected. Additionally, a separate set of particles was re-picked from the micrographs using the TOPAZ program31, yielding 2,118,292 particles. The two particle sets were combined through repeated rounds of 2D classification, resulting in a final selection of 1,476,515 particles. Ab initio maps were reconstructed from the combined set. The initially chosen 8,352,733 particles were then classified into ab initio maps through three rounds of heterogeneous refinement, resulting in a subset of 435,356 particles. The final map of the hPLK1KD–onvansertib complex was reconstructed from 134,422 particles after 3D variability analysis, reference-based motion correction, and non-uniform refinement, with an overall resolution of 3.04 Å (Supplementary Fig. 6a). Micrographs of β-OG-treated hPLK1KD–onvansertib were processed using the same workflow as that used for MBPUltAuFoil, resulting in a final map reconstructed from 200,803 particles at an overall resolution of 3.38 Å (Supplementary Fig. 8a).

The non-default parameters used in the cryoSPARC program during data processing were as follows: During 2D classification, the number of online-EM iterations and the batch size per class were set to 40 and 200, respectively. During ab initio 3D reconstruction, the maximum resolution, initial resolution, initial minibatch size, and final minibatch size were set to 6 Å, 12 Å, 300, and 1000, respectively. During hetero-refinement, the initial resolution was set to 10 Å. During non-uniform and local refinements, the number of extra final passes, the initial low-pass resolution, and the option to minimize over per-particle scale were set to 5, 10 Å, and enabled, respectively.

Atomic model building and structure analysis

Atomic models of MBP and hPLK1KD were built by fitting the associated crystal structures into the reconstructed cryo-EM maps. The crystal structures of MBP (PDB ID: 1FQC) and hPLK1KD (PDB ID: 2YAC) were fitted into the cryo-EM maps using ChimeraX32, rebuilt by manual tracing in COOT33, and refined using Phenix.refinement34,35. Water molecules were identified using Phenix.douse35 and manually selected. Figures were generated using ChimeraX32 and PyMOL36. The refinement statistics are summarized in Supplementary Table 1.
