Abstract
. We introduce a nonlinear and nonlocal model that describes the range expansion of a population resulting from growth and competition for space. This type of phenomenon underlies the expansion of colonies of immotile cells which motivated this work; Similar mechanisms are at play in urban sprawling which we briefly discuss as well. We rigorously derive a singular limit of this model corresponding to a regime where dispersal occurs only from saturated areas. The limiting model, which has the structure of an obstacle free boundary problem in time, provides an effective approach to the description of the range expansion of a population as a result of growth, saturation and dispersion. We then establish the main mathematical properties of this singular problem. In particular, we characterize the evolution of a free boundary that delimits the saturated area. We identify traveling wave solutions and characterize the asymptotic speed of spreading of compactly supported solutions.

Introduction

Reaction-diffusion equations, with local and non-local diffusion, provide the classical modeling framework for the spread of biological populations, capturing essential features such as growth, competition for resources and range expansion. Although these models are remarkably successful in various contexts, their derivation typically relies on assumptions of random motion of individuals in this population.
Brownian motion or jump processes type assumptions lead to linear diffusion
(as in the classical Fisher-KPP equation
[
26
,
18
]
),
while fluid-like behavior yields nonlinear diffusion models
(commonly used for tumor growth
[
11
,
9
,
10
]
).

However, in many instances, this type of assumption of random movement is not justified. Often, individuals do not move on their own, yet the growth process still results in shuffling or in long range expansion owing to congestion or competition for space.
This is the case for the range expansion of immotile cells
[
10
,
16
,
39
]
which is the main motivation for this work.
These cells, by definition, do not actively move around; instead, population expansion occurs through local proliferation (mitosis) and mechanical interactions between cells (pushing).

Moreover, reaction-diffusion equations do not agree with some characteristic features of the expansion of such colonies.
For example, solutions of reaction-diffusion equations are not typically monotone increasing in time (at least not initially),
but experimental observations show that immotile cells only move away from their position when they are being replaced by other cells, thereby suggesting that time monotonicity should be a feature of a mathematical model describing this phenomena.

The present paper is primarily motivated by a current work in collaboration with G. Steinbach
[
5
]
on the mathematical modeling of immotile cell colonies expansion.
Based on this work, we introduce a new nonlinear and nonlocal equation for growth with saturation effect on dispersion.
It describes the evolution of a population that grows locally until congestion (which corresponds to the lack of available space rather than diminishing resources) leads to the addition of individuals at a nearby non fully congested location thus expanding the spatial reach of the population.
While preserving many features of reaction-diffusion equations, this new equation also differs on some key aspects, for instance by exhibiting monotonicity in time at every point.

After deriving the model (see Section
1.1
below), we establish some of its basic mathematical properties in Section
1.2
.
We then investigate a singular limit that highlights the role of congestion in colony expansion.
This limit is analogous to the stiff pressure limit of the porous medium equation, a classical framework for congestion particularly in tumor growth (see for example
[
35
,
31
,
33
,
15
]
). The resulting asymptotic model takes the form of a free boundary problem (obstacle problem type):

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u=g(u)(1-K*{\mathbb{1}}_{\{u=1\}})+g(1)K*{\mathbb{1}}_{\{u=1\}}&\mbox{ when }u<1,\\
\partial_{t}u=0&\mbox{ when }u=1.\end{cases}

Crucially, this limit differs from the Hele-Shaw type models derived for example in
[
35
]
and often associated to the modeling of biological tissues growth; it is significantly more amenable to numerical simulation and has important features that are in qualitative agreement with experimental observations.
A central goal of this paper is to analyze in detail this singular equation. We establish a comparison principle, prove the existence of traveling fronts and study the asymptotic speed of spreading for compactly supported initial data.

Although this work was motivated by the study of immotile cell colonies, the type of mechanism described by this singular model is fairly general and is relevant for a large variety of frameworks. An example, which we discuss briefly in Section
2.4
, is the growth of cities
[
27
]
.

The paper is organized as follows.
In Section
1
, we introduce the new equation and we formally derive its singular limit.
The main results of this paper are then stated in section
2
: We rigorously justify the singular limit mentioned above and analyze the mathematical property of the resulting free boundary problem.
The proofs of these results take up the remaining sections.

1.
Derivation of the model

1.1.
A nonlinear nonlocal equation for range expansion

We introduce a model which describes the range expansion of a colony of immotile cells, resulting from growth and pressure mediated dispersal.
Denoting the cell population density by
u(t,x)
(at time
t>0
and spatial location
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
), this model writes:

(1.1)

\partial_{t}u=F[u]+\mathcal{L}[u],

with

(1.2)

F[u]=g(u)(1-K*p)

and

(1.3)

\mathcal{L}[u]:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K(x-y)\Big[g(u(y))(1-p(x))p(y)-g(u(x))(1-p(y))p(x)\Big]\,dy.

Here
p(t,x)=P(u(t,x))
is a pressure variable which satisfies

\mbox{ $u\mapsto P(u)$ is increasing with $P(0)=0$ and $P(1)=1$}.

The role of this pressure term is to represent the effect of congestion. The maximum pressure is normalized to be
1
when
u
reaches its saturation level,
u=1
, which can be interpreted as a maximum packing density.
The kernel
K=K(|x-y|)\geq 0
is a function of the distance and is normalized:

(1.4)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K(x)\,dx=1.

Let us now explain the meaning of the various terms in (
1.1
). The function
F[u]
describes the local production of new cells owing to the growth process (e.g. mitosis). This process is limited by congestion in a nonlocal way.
The growth function
g\geq 0
, typically given by
g(u)=ru
(or
g(u)=u(1-\frac{u}{M})
if depletion of resources is relevant), is the rate of growth in the absence of competition for space.
Similar production terms, typically with
g(u)=u
and
P(u)=u
, have been used extensively in nonlocal models of ecological invasion
such as the non-local Fischer-KPP equation
[
19
,
20
,
34
]

(1.5)

u_{t}=u(1-K*u)+\Delta u

studied for instance in
[
7
,
8
,
22
,
6
,
24
]
.

Unlike reaction-diffusion equations such as (
1.5
), where dispersion is described by the standard diffusion operator, Equation (
1.1
) includes a nonlocal operator
\mathcal{L}
that captures the cellular rearrangements induced by growth and competition for space.
This integral operator is conservative, that is:

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{L}[u](x)\,dx=0\qquad\mbox{ for any function }u(x).

Hence, it does not contribute to growth.
It can be split into gain and loss terms

\mathcal{L}[u]=\mathcal{L}_{+}[u]-\mathcal{L}_{-}[u]
. The loss term, given by

(1.6)

\mathcal{L}_{-}[u](x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K(x-y)g(u(x))(1-p(y))p(x)\,dy=F[u](x)p(x),

accounts for the fact that some fraction of the newly produced cells at position
x
do not contribute to the increase of
u(t,x)
.
Indeed, cell division may give rise to some mechanical displacement of pre-existing cells, leading to a local rearrangement of the cell population.
As a consequence, some cells are pushed to occupy new locations farther from the site of mitosis.
The form of the loss term (
1.6
) captures the dependence of this process on the pressure
p
: The closer
p
is to 1, the more new cells are shuffled away from the location
x
.

The gain term
\mathcal{L}_{+}
(obtained from
\mathcal{L}_{-}
by symmetry considerations) describes the transfer of new cells to
x
following production at nearby locations
y
.

We note that the interaction kernel
K(x-y)
, which appears in both
F
and
\mathcal{L}
describes a complex mechanism which involves the pre-mitosis growth of a cell and the mechanical forces exerted by the growing cell on its neighbors.
Because of friction and other viscous forces, these interactions cannot occur over long distances and we will assume that
K
is supported in a ball
B_{\ell}(0)
.

We refer the reader to our work with G. Steinbach
[
5
]
, in which we give a more detailed discussion of the biological grounds that justifies this model (
1.1
).

1.2.
Properties of (
1.1
)

Equation (
1.1
) introduces a novel phenomenological framework for modeling the evolution of population density when
growth and congestion are the main drivers of range expansion.
A remarkable property of this equation is that its solutions are
monotone increasing functions of time which satisfy
0\leq u(t,x)\leq 1
for all
t>0
, as long as similar bounds are satisfied initially.
To see this, we first observe that
after a simple reorganization of the various terms, Equation (
1.1
) can be rewritten as

(1.7)

\partial_{t}u=\Big[g(u)(1-K*p)+K*(g(u)p)\Big](1-p),\qquad p=P(u).

The assumption
P(1)=1
thus implies that the right-hand side of (
1.7
) vanishes when
u=1
. It follows that for any initial configuration such that
u(0,x)\leq 1
, the solution of (
1.1
) satisfies

u(t,x)\leq 1\mbox{ for all }t>0.

By contrast, we recall that solutions of the non-local reaction diffusion equation (
1.5
) might overshoot and take values greater than
1
, which in turn leads to non-trivial behaviors such as oscillations near the front.
The bound
u(t,x)\leq 1
is very natural in our framework and accounts for the natural incompressibility of the cells.

Next, we note that the normalization (
1.4
) implies that
K*p\leq 1
so that the right-hand side of (
1.7
) is non-negative (recall that
g\geq 0
):
any solution with
u(0,x)\in[0,1]
will be monotone increasing in time and satisfy
u(t,x)\in[0,1]
for all
t>0
.
This monotonicity is a fundamental feature of (
1.1
). It is an important aspect of the evolution of a population of
immotile
cells, which is not captured by classical reaction-diffusion equations such as the F-KPP equation for which solutions might, at least initially, be decreasing in time at some locations.

1.3.
Singular limit

Next, we derive a somewhat more explicit equation by considering the extreme case when movement only emanates from locations which are saturated, that is where
u=1
, while cells that are created at a location where
u<1
stay there.
This corresponds to the assumption that
p=0
whenever
u<1
, that is
p(1-u)=0
.
We will show here that in this case, the general model (
1.1
) reduces to the singular equation already mentioned in the introduction:

(1.8)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u=g(u)(1-K*{\mathbb{1}}_{\{u=1\}})+g(1)K*{\mathbb{1}}_{\{u=1\}}&\mbox{ when }u<1\\
\partial_{t}u=0&\mbox{ when }u=1.\end{cases}

This pressure law arises naturally as the singular
stiff
limit

\gamma\to\infty
of the power-law relation
P(u)=u^{\gamma}
.
Such limits have been extensively investigated across various mathematical models. For instance, it is well established that the limit
\gamma\to\infty
of the porous medium equation

\partial_{t}u-\mathrm{div}(u\nabla p)=uf(p),\qquad p=u^{\gamma}

leads to a free boundary problem of Hele-Shaw type (see for instance
[
3
,
21
]
for results when
f=0
and

[
35
,
31
,
33
,
23
]
for application to tumor growth).
A similar approach was developed to handle congestion in crowd motion
[
37
,
30
,
29
,
14
,
28
,
1
]
(in that case congestion is a consequence of advection rather than growth).

Equation (
1.8
) can also be regarded as a free boundary problem, but not of Hele-Shaw type. Instead, it has the structure of an obstacle problem: It is equivalent to

(1.9)

\max\{u-1,\partial_{t}u-[g(u)(1-K*{\mathbb{1}}_{\{u=1\}})+g(1)K*{\mathbb{1}}_{\{u=1\}}]\}=0.

This model provides a more geometrical description of the evolution of the saturated set
S(t)=\{u(t,\cdot)=1\}
and the free boundary
\partial S(t)
. We refer to our work with G. Steinbach
[
5
]
for a more detailed discussion of (
1.8
) and its relevance to the description of range expansion of immotile cells.

Formally, we obtain (
1.8
) from (
1.1
) by first observing that the condition
“
p=0
when
u<1
”
implies in particular that
g(u(t,x))p(t,x)=g(1)p(t,x)
so that (
1.1
) simplifies to (using the form (
1.7
)):

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u

\displaystyle=\Big[g(u)(1-K*p)+K*(g(1)p)\Big](1-p)

(1.10)

\displaystyle=\Big[g(u)(1-K*p)+g(1)K*p\Big](1-p).

We can now use the fact that
\partial_{t}u=0
a.e. in
\{u=1\}
(since
u\leq 1
and
\partial_{t}u\geq 0
), to infer from (
1.10
) that

0=g(1)(1-p)\mbox{ in }\{u=1\}.

and thus
p={\mathbb{1}}_{\{u=1\}}
.
Equation (
1.10
) then becomes

(1.11)

\partial_{t}u=\Big[g(u)(1-K*\mathbb{1}_{\{u=1\}})+g(1)K*\mathbb{1}_{\{u=1\}}\Big]\mathbb{1}_{\{u<1\}}

which corresponds to (
1.8
).

In this paper, we will rigorously justify the derivation of (
1.11
)
as the singular limit of (
1.1
) with the
\gamma
-pressure law
P(u)=u^{\gamma}
when
\gamma\to\infty
.
We will then study the mathematical properties of (
1.11
). These results are stated in Section
2
below.

To conclude this introduction, we stress the fact that this model provides a very effective approach to the description of the range expansion of a population as a result of growth, saturation (competition for space) and dispersion.
It is very simple to simulate numerically (see
[
5
]
) and
it can easily be adapted to other framework. To illustrate this, we include in Section
2.4
a brief discussion on the mathematical modeling of cities growth.

Although (
1.11
) appears to be new, we should note that there is a vast literature that represents dispersal phenomena with the aid of integral operators. But they all differ from the models (
1.1
) and (
1.11
) in significant ways.
First, the most natural nonlocal version of reaction-diffusion equations like
Fisher-KPP equation

(1.12)

\partial_{t}u=\Delta u+f(u)

is the following non-local dispersion equation:

(1.13)

\partial_{t}u=f(u)+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K(y-x)[u(y)-u(x)]\,dy.

It has been studied for instance in
[
12
,
4
,
13
]
.
Our nonlinear integral operator
\mathcal{L}[u]
describes a process that is very different from the linear dispersion of (
1.13
).
The fact that
\mathcal{L}[u]
involves
g(u)
(rather than
u
), indicates that in our model growth is required for dispersion to take place. Its dependence on the pressure
p
further highlights the role of competition for space in the dispersion mechanism.

Other models with nonlocal dispersion have been proposed that are closer to (
1.11
). Non-local models have been used in particular for biological invasions in spatial ecology and epidemiology, since the pioneering work of Kendall
[
25
]
.
The dispersal of plant seeds is another example of non-local dispersal. Thus, considering jump processes has led to the study of nonlocal equations.
This has motivated the mathematical studies of the prototype equation
[
32
,
36
]

\partial_{t}u=-u+\lambda(1-u)K*u.

We also mention the neural field equation
[
17
,
38
]
which takes the form:

\partial_{t}u=-u+K*S(u).

Even though these equations bear some similarities with the model we discuss here, there are notable differences. For example, these non-local equations, like the local equation (
1.12
), do not lead in general to monotone in time solutions. Note also that congestion is not taken into account in these models.

2.
Assumptions and main results

Our goal in this paper is twofold:
We will justify rigorously the singular limit leading to (
1.11
)
(from (
1.1
))
and we will establish several important properties of this model which correspond to key features of the
biological phenomena we aim to describe: existence of traveling wave solutions and spreading speed of propagation.
All these results are presented below.

2.1.
Assumptions and definition

Throughout the paper, we assume that
K
satisfies

(2.1)

K\geq 0,\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K(x)\,dx=1,\quad\|\nabla K\|_{TV}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla K|<+\infty.

While these assumptions are sufficient to derive (
1.11
) and prove its well-posedness,
the long time asymptotic properties will be established under the additional assumptions that
K
be bounded, radially symmetric and compactly supported:

(2.2)

K(x)=K(|x|)\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\quad\mathrm{Supp}\,K=B_{\ell}(0).

Concerning the growth rate
g
, we assume

(2.3)

g(0)=0,\quad g(u)\geq ru\quad\forall u\in(0,1]\mbox{ for some $r>0$},\quad L:=\sup_{u\in[0,1]}|g^{\prime}(u)|<\infty.

A simple example is
g(u)=r_{0}u
, but we can also consider nonlinear functions
g
that take into account the competition for resources (such as nutrients), such as
g(u)=r_{0}u(1-\frac{u}{M})
with
M>1
.
The sign of
g(1)-g(u)
plays an important role in the equation, and some properties of the model
(in particular the comparison principle) require the additional assumption

(2.4)

g(u)\leq g(1)\mbox{ for all }u\in[0,1].

This condition is typically satisfied when
g
is monotone increasing on
[0,1]
(if
g(u)=r_{0}u(1-\frac{u}{M})
, it holds as long as
M>2
).

Despite the apparent simplicity of (
1.11
), its well-posedness
is not immediate.
The non locality in
x
and the singular definition of the pressure
p={\mathbb{1}}_{u=1}
raise some significant challenges.
For our analysis, it will be convenient to view (
1.11
) as an infinite system of coupled ODEs parametrized by
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We will thus adopt the following definition of solutions:

Definition 2.1
.

The function
u(t,x)
is a solution of (
1.11
) if for all
t>0
,
u(t,\cdot)
is a function in
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfying
0\leq u(t,\cdot)\leq 1
and if for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the function
t\mapsto u(t,x)
is a Lipschitz function which solves (
1.11
) a.e
t>0
.

2.2.
Main results

While solutions of (
1.1
) and (
1.11
) are naturally Lipschitz in time, (
1.11
) has no obvious regularizing mechanisms with respect to the
x
variable (unlike reaction-diffusion equations).
However, we will show that the equation propagates some regularity of the initial data.
For simplicity, we restrict ourselves to Lipschitz initial data so that solutions will be Lipschitz in both
t
and
x
.
The approach that we develop here could easily be extended to initial data that are only (locally) Hölder continuous.
Throughout the paper, we thus assume that the initial data
u^{in}
is a Lipschitz function:

\|u^{in}\|_{\mathrm{Lip}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}:=\sup_{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d},x\neq y}\frac{|u^{in}(x)-u^{in}(y)|}{|x-y|}<\infty.

Singular limit.

Our first task is the rigorous derivation of (
1.11
) as the singular limit of (
1.1
).
We thus consider the equation (
1.1
) (or equivalently (
1.7
)) with
P(u)=u^{\gamma}
, which we rewrite here for convenience:

(2.5)

\partial_{t}u=\Big[g(u)(1-K*p)+K*(g(u)p)\Big](1-p),\qquad p=u^{\gamma}.

The first result is:

Theorem 2.2
.

Assume that
K
and
g
satisfy respectively (
2.1
) and (
2.3
).
For all
\gamma\geq 1
, Equation (
2.5
) has a unique solution
u_{\gamma}(t,x)
satisfying
u_{\gamma}(0,x)=u^{in}(x)
.
When
\gamma\to+\infty
,
u_{\gamma}
converges, up to a subsequence, locally uniformly in
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
to a function
u_{\infty}(t,x)
solution of (
1.11
) with
u_{\infty}(0,x)=u^{in}(x)
. It satisfies
0\leq u_{\infty}(t,x)\leq 1
and
\partial_{t}u_{\infty}(t,x)\geq 0
a.e. in
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Furthermore, for all
T>0
we have

u_{\infty}\in\mathrm{Lip}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}),\quad{\mathbb{1}}_{\{u_{\infty}=1\}}\in BV_{loc}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}).

This theorem, proved in Section
3
, provides in particular the existence of a solution to (
1.11
).
As noted above, we do not need to work with Lipschitz initial data to get such a result: Hölder continuous initial data will yield Hölder continuous solutions.
On the other hand, Lipschitz regularity is the highest regularity that we can propagate: even with smooth initial data, the solution of (
1.11
) cannot be
C^{1}
.

Comparison principle and uniqueness.

The uniqueness of the solution constructed in Theorem
2.2
(which would guarantee the convergence of the whole sequence
\{u_{\gamma}\}
in Theorem
2.2
) is not obvious: The singular definition of the pressure
{\mathbb{1}}_{\{u=1\}}
together with the non-locality of the convolution with
K

do not seem to accommodate classical arguments.
However, we will prove in Section
4
that (
1.11
) has a comparison principle when
g
satisfies (
2.4
), and uniqueness will follows:

Proposition 2.3

(Comparison principle)
.

Assume that
g
satisfies (
2.3
)-(
2.4
) and that
K
satisfies (
2.1
).
Let
u_{1}(t,x)
,
u_{2}(t,x)
be two solutions of (
1.11
) with Lipschitz initial data
u_{1}^{in}
and
u_{2}^{in}
satisfying

0\leq u_{2}^{in}(x)\leq u_{1}^{in}(x)\leq 1\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Then

0\leq u_{2}(t,x)\leq u_{1}(t,x)\leq 1\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\;\forall t>0.

It is interesting to note that this comparison principle does not hold without the condition (
2.4
) on
g
.
Indeed, if
there exists
u_{0}\in(0,1)
such that
g(u_{0})>g(1)
,
we can construct a simple counterexample as follows (here we are also assuming that
K>0
in
B_{\ell}(0)
):

Define
u_{2}^{in}\equiv u_{0}
and let
u_{1}^{in}
be a smooth function such that
u_{1}^{in}\geq u_{2}^{in}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
u_{1}^{in}(x)=1
for
x<0
and
u_{1}^{in}(\ell/2)=u_{0}
.
Then
\partial_{t}u_{2}(0,\ell/2)=g(u_{0})
while

\partial_{t}u_{1}(0,\ell/2)=g(u_{0})(1-K*{\mathbb{1}}_{x<0})+g(1)K*{\mathbb{1}}_{x<0}<g(u_{0})
(since
K*{\mathbb{1}}_{x<0}(\ell/2)>0
).
It follows that, at least for short time,
u_{1}(t,\ell/2)<u_{2}(t,\ell/2)
.

The comparison principle implies:

Corollary 2.4
.

Under the conditions of Proposition
2.3
,
let
u^{in}(x)\in[0,1]
be a Lipschitz function in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Equation (
1.11
) has a unique solution satisfying
u(0,x)=u^{in}(x)
.

Invasion.

It we further assume that
K>0
in
B_{\ell}(0)
, we can show that
the colony will eventually invade the whole space.
This invasion property follows trivially from the time monotonicity of the solutions and the fact that the only stationary solutions of (
1.11
) are the constant
u\equiv 0
and
u\equiv 1
(see Lemma
4.3
):

Proposition 2.5
.

Assume that
K
and
g
satisfy respectively (
2.1
) and (
2.3
) and that
K>0
in
B_{\ell}(0)
.
Any solution
u(t,x)
of (
1.11
) such that
u(0,\cdot)
is not identically zero satisfies

u(t,x)\nearrow 1\quad\mbox{ as }t\nearrow\infty,\;\mbox{ for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

It is also possible to quantify this invasion and show the following:

Lemma 2.6
.

Under the assumptions of Proposition
2.5
, and assuming that
u^{in}(x)>\eta
in
B_{\delta}
(for some positive
\eta
and
\delta
), then

(2.6)

\{u(t,\cdot)=1\}\supset B_{\underline{c}(t-t_{0})_{+}}(0)

for some
\underline{c}>0
and
t_{0}\geq 0
.

The rest of the paper is devoted to further characterizing the expansion of the saturated set (and the support) of the solution as
t\to\infty
.

Traveling waves.

One of the great success of reaction-diffusion equations is
their ability to precisely describe the spreading of initially compactly supported solutions (speed and profile) via traveling wave solutions.
The extensive theory developed in that framework needs to be extended to (
1.11
).
In this paper, we will prove the existence of planar traveling waves with speed
c\in[c^{*},+\infty)
and show that compactly supported initial data spread with speed
c^{*}
.

We recall that planar traveling waves are particular solutions of the equation that take the form
u(t,x)=\phi(x\cdot e-ct)
with
e\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
(the direction of propagation) and
c>0
(the speed of propagation) and where the profile
\phi:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
connects the two stationary states:

\lim_{s\to-\infty}\phi(s)=1,\qquad\lim_{s\to+\infty}\phi(s)=0.

The existence of such solutions is a fundamental feature of reaction-diffusion equations and the following theorem, proved in Section
5
, should be compared to classical results for the Fischer-KPP equation:

Theorem 2.7
.

Assume that
g
satisfies (
2.3
)-(
2.4
) and that
K
satisfies (
2.1
)-(
2.2
).
There exists a minimal speed
c^{*}>0
such that for all
c\geq c^{*}
, (
1.11
) has a unique (up to translation) traveling wave solution
\phi_{c}
with speed
c
while no traveling wave exists with speed
c<c^{*}
.

Up to translation, we can further assume that the saturated set satisfies
\{\phi_{c}=1\}=(-\infty,0]
and the followings hold:

(1)

if
c>c^{*}
, then
\phi_{c}(s)>0
for all
s\in\mathbb{R}

(2)

if
c=c^{*}
, then
\{\phi_{c^{*}}>0\}=(-\infty,\ell]
.

As usual, the existence of traveling wave solutions and the comparison principle imply a bound on the speed of spreading. Since
\phi_{c^{*}}
is semi-compactly supported, we actually get a bound on the speed of propagation of the support:

Corollary 2.8
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
2.7
,
let
u^{in}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,1]
be a Lipschitz initial data with
\mathrm{Supp}\,u^{in}\subset B_{R}(0).

Then the corresponding solution
u(t,x)
of (
1.11
) satisfies

\mathrm{Supp}(u(t,\cdot))\subset B_{R+\delta+c^{*}t}(0).

Interestingly, for compactly supported initial conditions,
the support of
u(t,\cdot)
and the congested set

S(t)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,;\,u(t,x)=1\}

remain close for all
t
:
The following result (proved in Section
6
) shows that the support of the solution is confined to a finite neighborhood of the congested set:

Proposition 2.9
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
2.2
, assume further that
\mathrm{Supp}\,K\subset B_{\ell}
.
Then any solution

of (

) satisfies

\mathrm{Supp}\,u(t,\cdot)\subset\mathrm{Supp}\,u(0,\cdot)\cup\big(S(t)+B_{\ell}\big),\qquad\mbox{for all $t>0$.}

In particular for

large enough so that
\mathrm{Supp}\,u(0,\cdot)\subset S(t)
(that is once the congested set overtakes the initial support of

), then

S(t)\subset\mathrm{Supp}\,u(t,\cdot)\subset S(t)+B_{\ell}.

The region
\{0<u(t,\cdot)<1\}
is thus constraint to an annulus of width

Speed of spreading.

Now that we proved that solutions cannot spread with speed faster than

, we want to show that

characterizes precisely the speed of propagation of any compactly supported initial data as follows:

Theorem 2.10
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
2.7
, assume further that
K
is given by

(2.7)

K=\frac{1}{|B_{\ell}|}\mathbb{1}_{B_{\ell}}.

Given

, a Lipschitz initial data, compactly supported and not identically zero, the solution

of (

in the sense that for all direction

there hold:

\lim_{t\to\infty}u(t,cte)=0\qquad\forall c>c^{*}

and

(2.9)

\lim_{t\to\infty}u(t,cte)=1\qquad\forall c\in[0,c^{*}).

The proof of this last theorem is given in Section

.
We state the result with

)
for simplicity (see (

)).
However, we expect this theorem to hold for more general kernel

.
The proof relies on the construction of a radially symmetric subsolution that propagates with speed close to

implies that for all

Further considerations.

These results demonstrate that (
1.11
) is a sound mathematical model that yields dynamics consistent with experimental observations.
It retains many of the key features typically associated with reaction-diffusion equations and we anticipate that additional properties not addressed here can also be extended to this framework. This includes the stability of traveling wave profiles or the refined characterizations of front propagation, such as results related to the precise front location (e.g. Bramson-type shifts).

Proving the existence of traveling waves for that model is actually more delicate: when working with the singular model (
1.11
), a crucial simplification occurs as the convolution
K*{\mathbb{1}}_{u=1}
depends on the position of the front but not on the function
u
.
In addition, it is not clear that this general model satisfies a comparison principle, so the existence of traveling wave solutions does not immediately imply the asymptotic speed of propagation property.

2.3.
Generalizations

The basic mechanisms modeled by (
1.11
) are fairly general and apply to other settings where a population extends its range only when congestion, interpreted as a lack of available space rather than resources, forces some individuals to relocate to nearby, uncongested areas.
We have already mentioned the mathematical modeling of urban sprawl which we further discuss in Section
2.4
below; other potential applications include ecological invasions (e.g. swarming of honeybees) or the early dispersal of modern humans across southern Asia and Europe (where competition for space is linked to the availability of cultivable land and habitable living space).

These examples lead us to the slightly more general equation:

(2.10)

\partial_{t}u=\Big[g(u)+h(u)K*{\mathbb{1}}_{\{u=1\}}\Big]{\mathbb{1}}_{\{u<1\}}.

The function
g(u)
represents the growth rate in the absence of competition for space (e.g.
g(u)=ru
if resources are abundant).
The term
h(u)K*{\mathbb{1}}_{\{u=1\}}
describes growth at position
x
due to the individuals who traveled there from a nearby saturate place.

It is not difficult to extend our analysis to (
2.10
).
The existence and uniqueness of a Lipschitz, monotone-in-time solution can be proved by proceeding as above as long as
g
and
h
are non-negative Lipschitz functions.
Spreading properties require the additional conditions

g(0)=0,\quad g(1)>0,\quad h(0)>0.

In particular, the condition
g(0)=0
ensures the finite speed of propagation of the support (and thus the existence of semicompactly supported traveling wave solutions).
The condition
g(1)>0
is needed to reach the saturation level
u=1
in finite time, which together with the condition
h(0)>0
is required to start the spatial expansion of the population (these last two conditions give a lower bound on the minimal speed of the traveling wave
c^{*}
).

2.4.
Urban Sprawl

Equation (
2.10
) provides a natural framework for the modeling of the long-term spatial expansion of a city (urban sprawl) and land-use transitions.
Indeed, the lack of space availability (congestion) plays a key role in
the development of new urban area in previously undeveloped land and nonlocal (but finite range) jumps are natural as developers and city planners look to expand the city’s footprint.

We refer to
[
27
]
and the many references therein for a detailed discussion of various mathematical models of urban growth.
A simple model
[
2
]
for land-transitions classifies land into three main categories: Vacant Land (rural or undeveloped land available for future constructions), New Development (area in transition) and Established Development (mature city centers where new constructions are no longer possible).
As shown in Proposition
2.9
, our model naturally describes
these three regions (respectively
\{u=0\}
,
\{0<u<1\}
and
\{u=1\}
), with in particular the saturated region where
u=1
that corresponds to the mature areas of the city.

We point out that in this framework, one should think of
t
as a parameter measuring the size of the population (rather than the actual time) and
u(t,x)
as the density of housing units at some location
x
(or the normalized number of people who can reside at location
x
).
As the population grows (that is as
t
increases), Equation (
2.10
) describes the addition of new constructions, according to various considerations, such as:

•

The total number of housing units that can be built at each location is limited by several considerations (e.g. zoning laws, environmental constraints etc.). We can fix this constraint as
u(t,x)\leq 1
for simplicity, but we can extend this model by taking
u(t,x)\leq H(x)
where
H(x)
is a given non-negative function (regions where
H(x)=0
correspond to non constructible land).

•

When
0<u(t,x)<1
, there is a natural rate of construction
g(u)
due to increased desirability: people moving to the neighborhood lead to improvement in the local infrastructure, school, commerce etc. which in turn incentivizes further local construction.

•

The saturated core where
u=1
is viewed here as the inner city - where all services are readily available (a model where that threshold is different from the constraint might be relevant too). Such neighborhood are extremely attractive, and since further local construction is limited, it leads to new developments in surrounding (including possibly currently empty) areas.

We leave it to further studies to explore more in depth the relevance of models of type (
2.10
)
to represent urban sprawling dynamics.

in the equation (

) to show that the limit
(u_{\infty},p_{\infty})
solves

\partial_{t}u_{\infty}=\big[g(u_{\infty})(1-K\ast p_{\infty})+K\ast(g(u_{\infty})p_{\infty})\big](1-p_{\infty})\quad\mbox{ a.e. in }\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Furthermore, it is straightforward that
p_{\gamma}(t,x)
converges to
0
pointwise in
\{u_{\infty}(t,x)<1\}
(in fact the convergence is locally uniform in that open set) and thus
p_{\infty}(t,x)=0
a.e. in
\{u_{\infty}(t,x)<1\}
.
In particular we have
g(u_{\infty})p_{\infty}=g(1)p_{\infty}
a.e. in
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which yields

(3.7)

\partial_{t}u_{\infty}=\big[g(u_{\infty})(1-K\ast p_{\infty})+g(1)K\ast p_{\infty}\big](1-p_{\infty})\quad\mbox{ a.e. in }\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Finally, using the fact that
\partial_{t}u_{\infty}=0
a.e. in
\{u_{\infty}=1\}
(since
u_{\infty}\in W^{1,\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
), Equation (
3.7
) yields

0=g(1)(1-p_{\infty})\quad\mbox{ a.e. in }\{u_{\infty}=1\}

hence
p_{\infty}={\mathbb{1}}_{\{u_{\infty}=1\}}
a.e. in
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

It remains to show that
t\mapsto u_{\infty}(t,x)
solves (
1.11
) for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
(see Definition
2.1
).
We thus fix
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Since
K\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we have
K\ast p(t,x_{0})=K\ast{\mathbb{1}}_{\{u_{\infty}=1\}}(t,x_{0})
a.e.
t>0
and the following convergence holds:

K\ast p_{\gamma}(t,x_{0})\to K\ast{\mathbb{1}}_{\{u_{\infty}=1\}}(t,x_{0})\quad\mbox{ a.e. }t>0

(the important thing is that this limit holds pointwise in
x_{0}
and not just a.e.).
Next, the monotonicity and continuity of
t\mapsto u_{\infty}(t,x_{0})
(also pointwise in
x_{0}
) implies the existence of
t_{0}(x_{0})\in[0,\infty]
such that
u_{\infty}(t,x_{0})<1
for
t<t_{0}(x_{0})
and
u_{\infty}(t,x_{0})=1
for
t\geq t_{0}(x_{0})
.
Since
p_{\gamma}(t,x_{0})
converges to
0
(locally uniformly) for
t\in(0,t_{0}(x_{0}))
, we can pass to the limit in (
2.5
) to get

\partial_{t}u_{\infty}(t,x_{0})=[g(u_{\infty})(1-K\ast{\mathbb{1}}_{\{u_{\infty}=1\}})+g(1)K\ast{\mathbb{1}}_{\{u_{\infty}=1\}}](t,x_{0})\qquad\mbox{ a.e. }t\in(0,t_{0}(x_{0})).

Finally, we obviously have
\partial_{t}u_{\infty}(t,x_{0})=0
for all
t>t_{0}(x_{0})
, and so

\partial_{t}u_{\infty}=[g(u_{\infty})(1-K\ast{\mathbb{1}}_{\{u_{\infty}=1\}})+g(1)K\ast{\mathbb{1}}_{\{u_{\infty}=1\}}](1-{\mathbb{1}}_{\{u_{\infty}=1\}})\qquad\mbox{ a.e. }t>0.

This equality holds for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, so
u_{\infty}(t,x)
is a solution of (
1.11
) in the sense of Definition
2.1
.

∎

4.
Comparison principle and uniqueness

In the previous section, we showed that given a Lipschitz initial data,
(
1.11
) has a solution that is Lipschitz in both
x
and
t
.
In order to prove uniqueness, we first prove that any solution must be
Lipschitz:

Lemma 4.1
.

Assume that
g
satisfies (
2.3
) and that
K
satisfies (
2.1
).
Let
u(t,x)
be a solution of (
1.11
) in the sense of Definition
2.1
with initial solution
u^{in}\in\mathrm{Lip}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then
u\in\mathrm{Lip}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
for all
T>0
. More precisely, the following pointwise estimate holds:

|u(t,x)-u(t,y)|\leq e^{Lt}\Big[|u^{in}(x)-u^{in}(y)|+\|\nabla K\|_{TV}|x-y|\Big].

Proof.

The proof follows closely that of Lemma
3.2
.
As a preliminary remark, we note that given
u(t,x)
a solution of (
1.11
) and
v(t,x)
any function satisfying
v(t,x)\leq 1
almost everywhere, we have:

\mathrm{sign}(u-v)\partial_{t}u\leq[g(u)(1-K*{\mathbb{1}}_{\{u=1\}})+g(1)K*{\mathbb{1}}_{\{u=1\}}]\mathrm{sign}(u-v).

This inequality follows from the fact that
[g(u)(1-K*{\mathbb{1}}_{\{u=1\}})+g(1)K*{\mathbb{1}}_{\{u=1\}}]\geq 0
and

\displaystyle(1-{\mathbb{1}}_{\{u=1\}})\mathrm{sign}(u-v)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0&\mbox{ if }u=1\\
\mathrm{sign}(u-v)&\mbox{ if }u<1\end{cases}

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{sign}(u-v)

since
\mathrm{sign}(u-v)\geq 0
when
u=1
.

Given
h\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we set
v(t,x)=u(t,x+h)
. Since
u
and
v
both solve (
1.11
) and satisfy
u,\;v\leq 1
, we can write the two inequalities:

\mathrm{sign}(u-v)\partial_{t}u\leq[g(u)(1-K*{\mathbb{1}}_{\{u=1\}})+g(1)K*{\mathbb{1}}_{\{u=1\}}]\mathrm{sign}(u-v),

and

\mathrm{sign}(v-u)\partial_{t}v\leq[g(v)(1-K*{\mathbb{1}}_{\{v=1\}})+g(1)K*{\mathbb{1}}_{\{v=1\}}]\mathrm{sign}(v-u).

Adding these inequalities, we deduce:

\displaystyle\mathrm{sign}(u-v)\partial_{t}(u-v)

\displaystyle\leq[(g(u)-g(v))(1-K*{\mathbb{1}}_{\{v=1\}})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+(g(1)-g(u))(K*{\mathbb{1}}_{\{u=1\}}-K*{\mathbb{1}}_{\{v=1\}})]\mathrm{sign}(u-v)

which implies

(4.1)

\partial_{t}|u-v|\leq L|u-v|+L|K*{\mathbb{1}}_{\{u=1\}}-K*{\mathbb{1}}_{\{v=1\}}|.

We now observe that
K*{\mathbb{1}}_{\{v=1\}}(x)=\int K(x-y){\mathbb{1}}_{u(t,\cdot+h)=1}(y)\,dy=K*{\mathbb{1}}_{\{u=1\}}(x+h)

and so (
3.4
) gives

\displaystyle|K*{\mathbb{1}}_{\{u=1\}}-K*{\mathbb{1}}_{\{v=1\}}|(x)

\displaystyle\leq|h|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla K|

Going back to (
4.1
), we now find

\partial_{t}|u-v|\leq L|u-v|+\|\nabla K\|_{TV}L|h|.

and we can conclude as in the proof of Lemma
3.2
.
∎

Next, we prove a non-degeneracy estimate on the speed of spreading of the saturated set:

Lemma 4.2
.

Under the conditions of Lemma
4.1
, and given

(t_{0},x_{0})
such that
u(t_{0},x_{0})=1
,
there exists constants
r_{1},c_{1}\in(0,+\infty)
depending on
t_{0}
such that

u(t,x)=1\qquad\mbox{ for all }|x-x_{0}|\leq r_{1}\mbox{ and }t\geq t_{0}+c_{1}|x-x_{0}|.

Proof.

Since
u(t_{0},x_{0})=1
, the Lipschitz regularity (Lemma
4.1
) implies

(4.2)

u(t_{0},x)\geq 1-C_{0}|x-x_{0}|

for some constant
C_{0}
depending on
t_{0}
. In particular,
u(t_{0},x)\geq\frac{1}{2}
if
|x-x_{0}|\leq r_{1}:=\frac{1}{2C_{0}}
.
It remains to see that this implies that
u(t,x)
reaches the value
1
after some finite time. Indeed, if
u(t_{0},x)<1
, then we denote by
t_{*}
the first time when
u(t_{*},x)=1
(with
t_{*}=\infty
if this never happens). Since we have
\partial_{t}u\geq g(u)
for
t\in(t_{0},t_{*})
we get

\int_{u(t_{0},x)}^{1}\frac{1}{g(s)}\,ds\geq t_{*}-t_{0}.

Assumption (
2.3
) implies
g(s)\geq\frac{r}{2}
for
s\in(1/2,1)
and so this inequality gives

t_{*}-t_{0}\leq\frac{2}{r}(1-u(t_{0},x))\leq\frac{2C_{0}}{r}|x-x_{0}|\qquad\mbox{ for all }|x-x_{0}|<r_{1}:=\frac{1}{2C_{0}}.

The result follows.
∎

We now have all the ingredients needed to prove that (
1.11
) satisfies a comparison principle:

Proof of Proposition
2.3
.

Given
u_{1}(t,x)
and
u_{2}(t,x)
two solutions of (
1.11
) with Lipschitz initial data satisfying
u_{1}^{in}(x)\geq u_{2}^{in}(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we consider the function

v_{1}(t,x)=u_{1}((1+\delta)t+\eta,x)

for small
\delta,\eta>0
(both destined to go to zero).
We are going to show that

v_{1}(t,x)\geq u_{2}(t,x)\qquad\mbox{for all }t>0,\;x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

The role of
\delta
is to make
v_{1}
a strict supersolution of the equation, while
\eta
ensures that the congested sets of
v_{1}
and
u_{2}
are strictly separated at time
t=0
: Lemma
4.2
(with
t_{0}=0
) implies that

(4.3)

v_{1}(0,x)=u_{1}(\eta,x)=1\mbox{ for all }x\in\{u_{1}^{in}=1\}+B_{\eta/{c_{1}}}

and the fact that
u_{2}^{in}(x)\leq u_{1}^{in}(x)\leq 1

then gives

\{u_{2}^{in}=1\}+B_{\eta/{c_{1}}}\subset\{v_{1}^{in}=1\}.

The comparison principle will be proved by looking at the first time
t_{*}
at which the free boundaries
\partial\{v_{1}(t)=1\}
and
\partial\{u_{2}(t)=1\}
meet at some point
x_{*}
. Indeed, for all
t<t_{*}
, the pressures
{\mathbb{1}}_{\{v_{1}=1\}}
and
{\mathbb{1}}_{\{u_{2}=1\}}
are then ordered, a fact which allows us to compare the nonlocal terms
K*{\mathbb{1}}_{\{u=1\}}
in the equations for
v_{1}
and
u_{2}
and derive a contradiction from the fact that
v_{1}(t_{*},x_{*})=u_{2}(t_{*},x_{*})=1
(since
v_{1}
is a strict supersolution of the equation).

To make this argument rigorous, we introduce the open set
\Omega_{1}(t)=\{v_{1}(t,\cdot)<1\}
and define

\Lambda(t)=\sup_{\overline{\Omega_{1}(t)}}u_{2}(t,x).

The strict separation property (
4.3
) implies that given
x_{0}\in\Omega_{1}(0)
, we have
u_{2}(0,x)\leq u_{1}(0,x)<1
for all
x\in B_{\eta}/{C_{1}}(x_{0})
and that

\Lambda(0)<1.

Next, we want to show that

(4.4)

\Lambda(t)<1\quad\mbox{ for all $t>0$}

by a contradiction argument.

Since
\Omega_{1}(t)
is decreasing and
t\mapsto u_{2}(t,x)
is a Lipschitz function, we have

\Lambda(t+s)=\sup_{\overline{\Omega_{1}(t+s)}}u_{2}(t+s,x)\leq\sup_{\overline{\Omega_{1}(t)}}u_{2}(t+s,x)\leq\Lambda(t)+Cs.

Assuming that (
4.4
) does not hold, this semi-continuity implies that there exists

t_{0}:=\inf\{t\,;\,\Lambda(t)=1\}
such that

t_{0}>0,\quad\Lambda(t_{0})=1,\quad\mbox{ and }\Lambda(t)<1\quad\forall t<t_{0}.

The fact that
\Lambda(t_{0})=1
and the continuity of
u_{2}
implies that there exists
x_{0}\in\overline{\Omega_{1}(t_{0})}
such that
u_{2}(t_{0},x_{0})=1
.
On the other hand, the fact that
\Lambda(t)<1
for
t<t_{0}
implies that
u_{2}(t,x)<1
whenever
v_{1}(t,x)<1
and so

(4.5)

{\mathbb{1}}_{\{u_{2}=1\}}(t,x)\leq{\mathbb{1}}_{\{v_{1}=1\}}(t,x)\qquad\mbox{ for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\;t\in[0,t_{0}).

In order to get a contradiction, we first notice that both
t\mapsto u_{2}(t,x_{0})
and
t\mapsto v_{1}(t,x_{0})
solve the differential equation for
t<t_{0}
.
Indeed, the definition of
t_{0}
and the fact that
x_{0}\in\overline{\Omega_{1}(t_{0})}\subset\overline{\Omega_{1}(t)}
for
t<t_{0}

implies that
u_{2}(t,x_{0})<1
for all
t<t_{0}
and therefore

\partial_{t}u_{2}(t,x_{0})=[g(u_{2})+(g(1)-g(u_{2}))K*{\mathbb{1}}_{\{u_{2}=1\}}](t,x_{0}),\qquad\forall t<t_{0}.

Furthermore the fact that
x_{0}\in\overline{\Omega_{1}(t_{0})}
together with Lemma
4.2
implies that
v_{1}(t,x_{0})<1
for all
t<t_{0}
and so (using the definition of
v_{1}
and (
4.5
)) we get

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v_{1}(t,x_{0})

\displaystyle=(1+\delta)[g(v_{1})+(g(1)-g(v_{1}))K*{\mathbb{1}}_{\{v_{1}=1\}}](t,x_{0})

\displaystyle\geq(1+\delta)[g(v_{1})+(g(1)-g(v_{1}))K*{\mathbb{1}}_{\{u_{2}=1\}}](t,x_{0})\qquad\forall t<t_{0}

(we stress out the fact that this is where we use the condition on
g
that
g(1)-g(u)\geq 0
).
Denoting
h_{2}:=K*{\mathbb{1}}_{\{u_{2}=1\}}
, we deduce that at
x=x_{0}
and for
t<t_{0}
we have:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(v_{1}-u_{2})

\displaystyle\geq(1-h_{2})[g(v_{1})-g(u_{2})]+\delta(1-h_{2})g(v_{1})+\delta g(1)h_{2}

\displaystyle\geq q(t)(v_{1}-u_{2})+\delta\Big[(1-h_{2})g(v_{1})+g(1)h_{2}\Big]

with
q(t)=(1-h_{2}(t,x_{0}))\frac{g(v_{1}(t,x_{0}))-g(u_{2}(t,x_{0}))}{v_{1}(t,x_{0})-u_{2}(t,x_{0})}
bounded function and
(1-h_{2})g(v_{1})+g(1)h_{2}\geq 0
.

Since
v_{1}(0,x_{0})-u_{2}(0,x_{0})\geq 0
and
v_{1}(t_{0},x_{0})-u_{2}(t_{0},x_{0})=v_{1}(t_{0},x_{0})-1\leq 0
, this differential inequality implies that
v_{1}(t_{0},x_{0})=1
and that

(1-h_{2})g(v_{1})+h_{2}g(1)=0\mbox{ for all $t<t_{0}$}

which is only possible if
g(v_{1}(t,x_{0}))=0
for all
t<t_{0}
, a contradiction since
v_{1}(t_{0},x_{0})=1
.

We have thus proved that (
4.4
) must hold. This implies that (
4.5
) holds for all
t>0
and so the nonlocal terms satisfy
K*{\mathbb{1}}_{\{v_{1}=1\}}(t,x)\geq K*{\mathbb{1}}_{\{u_{2}=1\}}(t,x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
t>0
.
We can then proceed as above to show that in the set
\{v_{1}(t,x)<1\}
, we must have
u_{2}(t,x)\leq v_{1}(t,x)
. Since this inequality clearly holds when
v_{1}=1
, we deduce

v_{1}(t,x)=u_{1}((1+\delta)t+\eta,x)\geq u_{2}(t,x)\quad\mbox{ for all $x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ and $t>0$}.

We can now pass to the limit
\delta\to 0
and
\eta\to 0
to get the result.
∎

We end this section with a proof of the invasion property:
Proposition
2.5
follows from the monotonicity in time and the following lemma:

Lemma 4.3
.

Assume that
g
satisfies (
2.3
) and that
K>0
in
B_{\ell}(0)
. Then
the only stationary solutions of (
1.11
) are the constant
u\equiv 0
and
u\equiv 1
.

Proof of Lemma
4.3
.

Stationary solutions must satisfy (for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
)

\mbox{either }u(x)=1\mbox{ or }g(u(x))(1-K\ast{\mathbb{1}}_{\{u=1\}}(x))+g(1)K\ast{\mathbb{1}}_{\{u=1\}}(x)=0.

Since
g(1)\neq 0
, the second condition is equivalent to

K\ast{\mathbb{1}}_{\{u=1\}}(x)=0\mbox{ and }g(u(x))=0,

which is the same as (using (
2.3
))

K\ast{\mathbb{1}}_{\{u=1\}}(x)=0\mbox{ and }u(x)=0.

In particular we see that a stationary solution
u(x)
can only take the values
0
or
1
and so
u={\mathbb{1}}_{E}
for some set
E
.

If
E\neq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then there exists
x_{0}\notin E
such that
|B_{\ell}(x_{0})\cap E|>0
.
This last condition implies that
K*{\mathbb{1}}_{\{u=1\}}(x_{0})=K\ast{\mathbb{1}}_{E}(x_{0})>0
. Since
u(x_{0})=0<1
, we get a contradiction

∎

Finally, we conclude this section with the proof of Lemma
2.6
:

Proof of Lemma
2.6
.

Equation (
1.11
) gives

\partial_{t}u\geq\min(g(u),g(1))\geq ru\quad\mbox{ as long as }u<1.

Given
x_{0}\in B_{\delta}(0)
, we have
u(t,x_{0})\geq u^{in}(x_{0})\geq\eta
(recall that
u
is monotone increasing in time).
It follows that as long as
u(t,x_{0})<1
, we have

\partial_{t}u(t,x_{0})\geq r\eta
.
We deduce

u(t,x)=1,\;p(t,x)=1,\quad\mbox{ for all }x\in B_{\delta}(0)\mbox{ and for }t\geq t_{0}

with
t_{0}=1/(r\eta)
.
In turn, this implies that there exists
\eta_{0}>0
(depending on
K
and
\delta
) such that
K*p(t,x)\geq\eta_{0}
for
t\geq t_{0}
and
x\in B_{\ell}(0)
. Going back to (
1.11
), we now get:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u(t,x)

\displaystyle=g(u(t,x))(1-K*\mathbb{1}_{\{u=1\}}(t,x))+g(1)K*\mathbb{1}_{\{u=1\}}(t,x)

\displaystyle\geq g(1)K*\mathbb{1}_{\{u=1\}}(t,x)

\displaystyle\geq g(1)\eta_{0}\geq r\eta_{0}

for
x\in B_{\ell}(0)
and

, as long as

.
We deduce that

u(t,x)=1,\;p(t,x)=1,\quad\mbox{ in }B_{\ell}(0),\mbox{ for }t\geq t_{0}+t_{1}

with

.
Iterating this argument, we find that

u(t,x)=1,\;p(t,x)=1,\quad\mbox{ in }B_{k\ell}(0),\mbox{ for }t\geq t_{0}+2kt_{1}

which yields the result.
∎

5.
Traveling waves: Proof of Theorem
2.7

In order to further characterize the convergence of
u(t,x)
to the stationary state
1
as
t\to\infty
, we now investigate the existence of (planar) traveling wave solutions of (
1.11
).
Traveling waves are solutions of the form
u(t,x)=\phi(x\cdot e-ct)
with
e\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
,
c\in\mathbb{R}
and

\lim_{s\to-\infty}\phi(s)=1,\qquad\lim_{s\to+\infty}\phi(s)=0.

Since all solutions of (
1.11
) are monotone increasing and Lipschitz continuous with respect to
t
, the profile function
s\mapsto\phi(s)
is monotone decreasing and Lipschitz continuous.
In particular, there exists
s_{0}
such that
\{\phi=1\}=(-\infty,s_{0}]
, and up to translation, we can always assume that
s_{0}=0
(which we will do from now on).
Equation (
1.11
) gives

-c\phi^{\prime}(x\cdot e)=g(\phi(x\cdot e))+(g(1)-g(\phi(x\cdot e)))K\ast{\mathbb{1}}_{\{x\cdot e\leq 0\}}(x)\quad\mbox{ for }x\cdot e>0

and
the existence of planar traveling wave solutions then hinges on the fact that there exists a function
h:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
such that

(5.1)

h(x\cdot e)=K\ast{\mathbb{1}}_{\{x\cdot e\leq 0\}}(x).

Indeed up to a rotation, we can assume that
e=e_{1}
and write

\displaystyle K\ast{\mathbb{1}}_{\{x_{1}<0\}}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\{y_{1}<0\}}K(x-y)\,dy

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}K(x_{1}-y_{1},x^{\prime}-y^{\prime})\,dy^{\prime}\,dy_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}K(x_{1}-y_{1},y^{\prime})\,dy^{\prime}\,dy_{1}

which is a function of
x_{1}
only.
Note that we are using the fact that
K
is radially symmetric. Without that assumption, the function
h
will depend on the direction
e
.
The function
h
only depends on
K
and can be computed explicitly for simple choices of the kernel
K
. It clearly satisfies

h:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]\mbox{ non-increasing with }h(0)=1/2,\mbox{ and }h(s)=0\mbox{ for all }s\geq\ell

(we also have
h(s)=1
for all
s\leq-\ell
, but this property won’t be useful here).
We thus have the following Lemma:

Lemma 5.1
.

Up to translation, we can assume that the profile
\phi
of a traveling wave with speed
c>0
, satisfies
\phi(s)=1
for all
s\leq 0
and solves the following initial value problem:

(5.2)

\phi(0)=1,\qquad-c\phi^{\prime}(s)=g(\phi(s))+(g(1)-g(\phi(s)))h(s)\quad s\in[0,\infty).

with
h
defined by (
5.1
).

We denote by
\phi_{c}
the unique solution of (
5.2
) (for
c>0
). We have to identify the values of
c
for which
\phi_{c}(s)
remains non-negative and converges to
0
at
+\infty
.
This last condition is actually easy to obtain:
Since
h(s)=0
in the interval
[\ell,+\infty)
, (
5.2
) gives

-c\phi_{c}^{\prime}(s)=g(\phi_{c}(s)),\qquad s\geq\ell.

Hence
\phi_{c}(s)
takes non-negative values in
[\ell,+\infty]
if and only if
\phi_{c}(\delta)\in[0,1]
and it satisfies
\lim_{s\to\infty}\phi_{c}(s)=0
in that case. In addition,
\phi_{c}(\ell)=0
implies
\phi_{c}(s)=0
for all
s\geq\ell
(which corresponds to the traveling wave with minimal speed).

Proving the existence of a traveling wave solution with speed
c
is thus reduced to making sure that the solution of (
5.2
) takes non-negative values in
[0,\ell]
.
We note that if there exists
s_{0}\in(0,\ell)
such that
\phi_{c}(s_{0})=0
, then
-c\phi_{c}^{\prime}(s_{0})=g(1)h(s_{0})>0
and thus
\phi_{c}^{\prime}(s_{0})<0
. This implies that for any
c>0
,
\phi_{c}
can vanish at most once in
(0,\ell)
and when it does we will have
\phi_{c}(\ell)<0
. So we only need to study the sign of
\phi_{c}(\ell)
as a function of
c
.

The discussion above shows that Theorem
2.7
is an immediate consequence of the following proposition which we prove below:

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
\phi_{c}
be the unique solution of (
5.2
).
There exists a unique

6.
Spreading of compactly supported solutions

6.1.
Spreading of the support and the saturated set

The existence of traveling wave solutions with finite speed, together with the comparison principle (Proposition
2.3
), imply that compactly supported solutions propagate with finite speed, as stated in Corollary
2.8
- see also the proof of Theorem
2.10
below.
In this section, we will prove that this spreading occurs with speed
c^{*}
.

We note that with general reaction-diffusion equation, it is important to distinguish the spreading of the support of the solution (which might take place instantly) and the spreading of the invasion front (characterized by traveling wave solutions).
For our problem, this distinction is not relevant. Indeed, we can show that the support of
u(t,\cdot)
and the saturated set remain close to each other for all time, as stated in Proposition
2.9
which we prove below:

Proof of Proposition
2.9
.

Equation (
1.11
) together with assumption (
2.3
) imply

\partial_{t}u\leq L(u+K*{\mathbb{1}}_{\{u=1\}}).

In particular we have

u(t,x)\leq u(0,x)e^{L}t+\int_{0}^{t}e^{L(t-s)}K*{\mathbb{1}}_{\{u=1\}}(s,x)\,ds.

This inequality implies that if
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is such that
u(0,x_{0})=0
and
u(t_{0},x_{0})>0
, then we must have

\int_{0}^{t_{0}}e^{L(t_{0}-s)}K*{\mathbb{1}}_{\{u=1\}}(s,x_{0})>0.

Since the function
s\to K*{\mathbb{1}}_{\{u=1\}}(s,x_{0})
is non-decreasing, this means that
K*{\mathbb{1}}_{\{u=1\}}(t_{0},x_{0})>0
, and the fact that
K
is supported in
B_{\ell}(0)
yields

|\mathrm{Supp}\,\,{\mathbb{1}}_{\{u=1\}}(t_{0},\cdot)\cap B_{\ell}(x_{0})|=|S(t_{0})\cap B_{\ell}(x_{0})|>0

which implies that
x_{0}\in S(t)+B_{\ell}
.

We just proved that given
(t_{0},x_{0})
such that
u(t_{0},x_{0})>0
, we must have either
u(0,x_{0})>0
or

x_{0}\in S(t_{0})+B_{\ell}(0)
. The result follows.
∎
