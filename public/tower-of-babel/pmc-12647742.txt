A multi-model deep learning framework for SEM-based defect detection in Perovskite thin films
Authors: Zulfikar Ali Ansari, Sahil Soni, Shahin Fatima, Shadab Siddiqui, P Venkata Hari Prasad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe urgent need to transition toward sustainable energy sources has positioned perovskite solar cells (PSCs) as a leading candidate for next-generation photovoltaics. Among them, formamidinium lead iodide () based devices have demonstrated power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) exceeding 25% with the potential for low-cost fabrication. However, structural defects such as pinholes, accumulation, and grain boundary irregularities significantly compromise their efficiency, stability, and long-term reliability. Conventional defect characterization using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) is labor-intensive, subjective, and unsuitable for large-scale quality control, underscoring the need for automated, high-precision detection strategies. In this study, we propose a multi-model deep learning framework for automated defect classification in mixed-dimensionality perovskite films. The framework targets five critical defect types: pure 3D perovskite, 3D perovskite with excess, 3D perovskite with pinholes, 3D–2D mixed perovskite, and 3D–2D mixed perovskite with pinholes. Three complementary architectures are benchmarked: ResNet50V2 and DenseNet169 for high-accuracy classification, and YOLOv9 for real-time detection with computational efficiency. Extensive data augmentation and transfer learning were employed to mitigate dataset scarcity, enabling robust feature extraction from a limited set of 2,380 SEM images. The results show that ResNet50V2 and DenseNet169 achieved a test accuracy of 96.7% and weighted F1-score of 0.966, while YOLOv9, though moderate in accuracy (45.0%), demonstrated exceptional computational efficiency with an 8-minute training time. The proposed framework not only enables precise identification of morphological defects but also supports scalable quality control in PSC manufacturing. Furthermore, the deployment of the trained models as an interactive Streamlit-based web application demonstrates its practical utility for real-time laboratory and industrial adoption. These findings highlight the potential of deep learning–driven defect analysis to accelerate the optimization and commercialization of perovskite solar cell technologies.Keywords: Perovskite solar cells, Deep learning, ResNet50V2, DenseNet169, YOLOv9, Data augmentationSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

The accelerating global demand for renewable energy, driven by the imperative to mitigate climate change and reduce reliance on fossil fuels, has placed solar photovoltaics (PV) at the forefront of sustainable energy technologies1. In 2024, PV accounted for more than 30% of new renewable capacity additions worldwide, according to the International Energy Agency2. Among emerging PV technologies, perovskite solar cells (PSCs), particularly those based on formamidinium lead iodide (), have attracted significant attention due to their high power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) exceeding 25%, low-cost fabrication, and favorable optoelectronic properties, including high absorption coefficients, low exciton binding energies, and superior charge carrier mobilities3. Despite this progress, the commercial deployment of based PSCs remains hindered by structural and morphological defects such as pinholes, accumulation, and grain boundary irregularities. These defects critically impact device efficiency, stability, and long-term reliability. Efforts to overcome these challenges through compositional engineering, such as mixed-cation formulations and 3D–2D hybrid structures, have improved moisture resistance and operational stability, yet defect detection and characterization remain major bottlenecks. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM), the conventional technique for morphological analysis, is labor-intensive, subjective, and unsuitable for large-scale quality control. Machine learning (ML) offers a powerful pathway to automate defect analysis in PSCs by enabling rapid and objective interpretation of complex imaging datasets. Prior studies have demonstrated the utility of convolutional neural networks (CNNs), transfer learning frameworks, and object detection models in predicting stability, classifying microstructures, and identifying surface defects. However, most approaches are constrained by the limited availability of large, annotated SEM datasets, restricting their robustness and applicability in real-world fabrication environments. To address these limitations, this study presents a multi-model deep learning framework for automated defect detection in mixed-dimensionality perovskite films. The framework classifies five critical defect categories: pure 3D perovskite, 3D perovskite with excess, 3D perovskite with pinholes, 3D–2D mixed perovskite, and 3D–2D mixed perovskite with pinholes using complementary architectures. Specifically, ResNet50V2 and DenseNet169 are employed for high-accuracy classification, while YOLOv9 is implemented for real-time detection with computational efficiency. Our work addresses these challenges by developing a multi-model deep learning framework for automated defect detection in mixed-dimensionality perovskite films, categorized into five defect type. In this work, the class ”pure 3D perovskite” is not treated as a defect but rather as a reference baseline category. This inclusion allows the deep learning models to establish clear discriminative boundaries between pristine films and those exhibiting morphological irregularities such as accumulation, pinholes, or mixed 3D–2D phases. Related work

Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have rapidly evolved as a next-generation photovoltaic technology, with research extending beyond compositional engineering to data-driven methods for accelerating material discovery and performance optimization. Recent literature demonstrates the increasing role of ML and DL in enabling predictive modeling, defect analysis, and process optimization. In this context, Hui et al. (2023), highlighted the broad potential of ML for PSC design, emphasizing predictive capabilities for bandgap tuning, stability estimation, and interface optimization4. Similarly, Liu et al. (2024) demonstrated ML as a robust characterization tool for analyzing perovskite structures, achieving improved predictive accuracy by incorporating fabrication parameters alongside device metrics5. Kusuma et al. (2025) applied ML to optimize device configurations, revealing enhanced efficiency and stability in novel architectures when trained on large-scale datasets6. Beyond efficiency predictions, DL has also been applied to morphology identification. Vakharia et al. (2023), adapted a multiscale-SinGAN architecture with feature selection strategies to identify perovskite thin-film morphologies, achieving robust performance but with high computational requirements7. Marchenko et al. (2025), advanced this direction by applying ML to recognize hybrid perovskite and perovskite-related structures from X-ray diffraction data, expanding applications beyond SEM-based defect detection8. Similarly, Massuyeau et al. (2022) demonstrated a deep learning approach for automatically identifying new hybrid perovskites from X-ray diffraction patterns, underscoring DL’s potential for material screening at the discovery stage9. These studies collectively reveal that while ML and DL methods have been widely employed for property prediction, bandgap estimation, and structural identification, their application in direct SEM-based defect classification of films remains limited. Moreover, most prior works have not addressed the challenge of deployable solutions that integrate high accuracy with real-time usability. The present work bridges this gap by introducing a multi-model deep learning framework combining ResNet50V2, DenseNet169, and YOLOv9 for automated classification and detection of perovskite thin-film defects. Unlike previous studies focusing primarily on predictive modeling or diffraction-based identification, this study targets SEM microstructural analysis and delivers a Streamlit-based web application, enabling real-time defect analysis for laboratory and industrial environments (Fig. 1).

Collection of the dataset

High-quality scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of thin films were collected and categorized into five defect classes: pure 3D perovskite, 3D perovskite with excess PbI2, 3D perovskite with pinholes, mixed 3D-2D perovskite and mixed 3D-2D perovskite with pinholes1011128. A total of 2,380 images (224224 pixels) were curated, with 452 images per class allocated for training, 12 for validation, and 12 for testing as illustrated sample of dataset in Fig. 1. Expert consensus labeling was employed to minimize subjectivity in class assignment.

The five categories were selected here because they represent the most frequent and performance-critical defect scenarios in thin films, as reported in previous experimental work. These classes (pure 3D, excess, pinholes, mixed 3D–2D, and mixed 3D–2D with pinholes) strongly influence device efficiency and stability. While this study focuses on these five classes, the same framework can be extended to include additional defects such as voids, cracks, or interfacial delamination, provided sufficient annotated SEM datasets are available.

Material used

For preparing the perovskite thin films, different high-purity chemicals and solvents were used. The solvents DMF (dimethylformamide, 99.8%), DMSO (dimethyl sulfoxide, 99. 8%), ethyl acetate (99. 5%), hexane (99. 5%), acetonitrile (99. 8%), ethanol (99%) and IPA (isopropyl alcohol, 99. 9%) were bought from Sigma Aldrich. These liquids act as the medium in which the perovskite precursor chemicals dissolve and spread on the glass substrate during film preparation. The main salt needed to form the perovskite structure, lead iodide (II) (, 99. 99%), was obtained from the Tokyo Chemical Industry. Several organic salts (called ammonium iodides and chlorides) were used to tune the structure and stability of the films. These included FAI (formamidinium iodide,>98%), MABr (methylammonium bromide,>98%), PEAI (phenethylammonium iodide,>99%), DDAI (n-dodecylammonium iodide,>99%), CMAI (cyclohexylmethylammonium iodide,>99%), TMAI (2-thiophenemethylammonium iodide,>99%), and MACl (methylammonium chloride, >98%). All of these were purchased from GreatCell Solar and used directly without further purification.

Fabrication of Perovskite films

Plain or ITO-coated glass substrates were cleaned by sequential sonication in acetone, ethanol, and 2-propanol, followed by 15-minute UV ozone treatment. High-purity powder was synthesized via a one-pot method according to the literature13, washed three times with ethyl acetate and acetonitrile, dried in a vacuum oven for more than 24 hours, and stored in a glovebox filled with . For pristine - films, a 1.8 M precursor 1139 mg - and 40 mg MACl in 1 mL DMF:DMSO (9:1 v/v). This solution was spin-coated on substrates in two steps: 1000 rpm for 10 s, then 5000 rpm for 30 s. During the second step, 100 L of ethyl acetate–hexane (7:3 v/v) antisolvent was applied at 10 s intervals (30–40% RH). Films were annealed at C for 20 min to form -. For 2D perovskite layers on 3D perovskite, 4 mM solutions of PEAI, TMAI, CMAI, or DDAI in IPA were sprayed at 3.0 mL/min using gas.

Film characterization

Crystallographic properties were analyzed using a Rigaku MiniFlex XRD with Cu K radiation (Å). Absorption spectra were measured with a Shimadzu UV-3600 Plus UV–vis–NIR spectrophotometer. Steady-state photoluminescence was recorded using an Edinburgh Instruments FS5 spectrofluorometer. Morphology was examined with a JEOL/JSM-F100 scanning electron microscope14.

Preprocessing and data augmentation

To standardize input, images were normalized and resized. Given the limited dataset size relative to the >50,000 samples typically required for deep learning, extensive data augmentation was performed using Keras’s ImageDataGenerator. Transformations included horizontal/vertical flips, rotations, ±10% zoom, random shearing, and brightness shifts. These augmentations simulated realistic imaging variations, improved model generalization, and reduced overfitting15. The data preprocessing and model validation process for this study began with the collection and categorization of SEM images, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Perovskite solar cell (PSC) films were distributed into five defect types: pure 3D perovskite, 3D perovskite with excess, 3D perovskite with pinholes, 3D–2D mixed perovskite, and 3D–2D mixed perovskite with pinholes, where models utilized 452 training, 12 validation, and 12 test images per class (totalling 2,380 images at 224224 pixels), reflecting a significant effort given the labour-intensive nature of sample synthesis, preparation, microscopy operation, and expert labelling16,18,18. This limitation constrained the dataset size compared to the 50,000+ images typically recommended for deep learning19. A balanced distribution between classes was maintained to prevent bias, with models having 452 training images per category20 for validation and testing, supported by a standardized image processing pipeline that included acquisition, normalization, resizing, expert labeling with consensus for ambiguous cases, and a normalization prediction workflow.

Deep learning architectures

A multi-model strategy was employed to leverage complementary strengths:

ResNet50V2: A residual network architecture utilizing skip connections to mitigate vanishing gradients and enable deep feature extraction, suited for fine-grained texture analysis21. It is a deep convolutional neural network that excels at extracting hierarchical features from complex datasets. With its residual learning mechanism and pre-activation layers, it ensures effective training of very deep networks, even with smaller datasets. In our work, it provides a highly accurate solution for classifying SEM images of perovskite thin films, making it a powerful tool for automated defect detection.

DenseNet169: A densely connected convolutional network that maximizes feature reuse and parameter efficiency, enhancing robustness under limited data conditions22. It is an efficient and high-performing deep neural network that excels at extracting detailed features from complex images. Its dense connectivity allows for better feature reuse and reduces overfitting, making it an excellent choice for defect detection in SEM images of perovskite thin films.

YOLOv9: An advanced real-time object detection model with GELAN backbone and PGI module, optimized for speed and efficiency23. Unlike the classification models, YOLOv9 was trained from scratch for defect localization in SEM micrographs. It is a state-of-the-art real-time object detection model that is exceptionally fast and efficient. It is well-suited for real-time defect detection in perovskite thin films, offering both classification and localization capabilities. For real-time object detection and localization of defects, we adopted the latest architecture in the YOLO series. Unlike the pretrained classification models, YOLOv9 was implemented as a detection network, designed for high speed and accuracy on dense and complex images such as SEM micrographs. It utilizes the GELAN backbone and PGI module for efficient feature learning and is trained from scratch for object-level annotation tasks, excelling in both computational efficiency and parameter utilization with hardware split between CPU (Intel i7-11800H) and GPU (NVIDIA RTX 3050 Ti), ensuring fair comparison.

Training and validation

Transfer learning was applied to ResNet50V2 and DenseNet169, initialized with ImageNet weights and fine-tuned on the perovskite dataset24. Models were trained using categorical cross-entropy loss and the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 1e-4. Training spanned 50 epochs with early stopping to prevent overfitting. Validation was performed using a stratified subset to ensure class balance. YOLOv9 was trained independently with bounding box annotations, emphasizing computational efficiency.

Model deployment

The trained models were integrated into a user-friendly Streamlit web application, enabling real-time defect analysis. Users can upload SEM images to receive predictions along with confidence scores and visual overlays. The interface incorporates visualization panels and a reference gallery, ensuring accessibility for researchers and technicians without machine learning expertise. Results

The performance of the proposed framework was comprehensively evaluated using the curated SEM dataset of perovskite films. Three architectures, ResNet50V2, DenseNet169, and YOLOv9, were benchmarked across five defect categories, with results assessed using accuracy, weighted F1-score, per-class metrics, and computational efficiency.

Model performance

DenseNet169 and ResNet50V2 achieved the highest classification performance, each with 96.7% test accuracy and a weighted F1-score of 0.966. Their confusion matrix exhibited near-diagonal dominance as illustrated in Fig. 7, 8 with only minor misclassifications between ”3D perovskite” and ”3D perovskite with excess.” DenseNet169 required longer training time (297 minutes) compared to ResNet50V2 (81 minutes), reflecting its deeper connectivity but also confirming its robustness for small datasets. In contrast, YOLOv9 obtained 45.0% accuracy and an F1-score of 0.411, but completed training in only 8 minutes, underscoring its suitability for rapid deployment despite limited accuracy as illustrated in Table 1.

Discussion

The results of this study demonstrate the effectiveness of a multi-model deep learning framework for automated defect detection in perovskite thin films. Both ResNet50V2 and DenseNet169 achieved high classification accuracy, highlighting their ability to capture subtle morphological variations in SEM images, including pinholes, accumulation, and grain boundary irregularities, while YOLOv9, although exhibiting moderate accuracy, provided exceptional computational efficiency with rapid training and real-time detection capabilities. In the context of the validation of our study, we have observed that ResNet50V2 and DenseNet169 provide nearly identical and very high accuracy, which is 98.33, making them well-suited for precise defect classification. YOLOv9, although less accurate than 61.66, offers advantages in speed and real-time deployment, suggesting a trade-off between accuracy and efficiency as detailed in Fig. 10.

Comparative bar chart summarizing classification performance of ResNet50V2, DenseNet169, and YOLOv9. The results illustrate the balance between accuracy and computational efficiency across models.

Additionally, in the final phase of our study, we deployed the defect classifier as a Streamlit-based web application, showcasing its practical utility and flexibility for real-time analysis of perovskite solar cell SEM images as showed in Fig. 11. This tool allows researchers to upload SEM images and receive immediate defect predictions, such as 3D perovskite with pinholes or 3D-2D mixed perovskite, alongside confidence scores and descriptive tooltips, enhancing transparency and encouraging critical evaluation of results. The interface, designed with usability in mind, includes visualization panels and a reference image gallery, making it accessible to laboratory technicians and researchers without deep learning expertise. Tested with our 1,250-image test set, the application consistently delivered accurate classifications despite dataset limitations, demonstrating its potential as a valuable tool for the research community. By providing an interactive platform to analyze defect types, this web application not only bridges the gap between academic research and practical applications but also offers a flexible, user-friendly solution that can be further refined and expanded, empowering researchers to explore and address material defects in perovskite solar cells effectively. This deployment underscores the adaptability of our approach and its readiness for broader adoption in both research and industrial settings.

While DenseNet169 and ResNet50V2 achieved superior accuracy, their longer training times highlight the classic trade-off between computational efficiency and predictive precision. In future work, this balance could be optimized by firstly, knowledge distillation, where lighter models inherit the representational power of deeper architectures, secondly, model pruning and quantization to reduce redundant parameters while retaining accuracy, and finally, semi-supervised or self-supervised approaches that influence large volumes of unlabelled SEM data. These strategies may allow for more efficient deployment without compromising classification robustness.

Comparative analysis

The comparative study highlights the evolution of machine learning approaches for perovskite solar cell (PSC) defect detection as outlined in Table 5. Early CNN-based models, such as those by Li et al. (2019), achieved moderate accuracies of 85–90% on limited SEM datasets, but lacked scalability and deployment readiness. Subsequent efforts, including Sun et al. (2022), improved performance (88%) but continued to face challenges in distinguishing fine morphological variations. Review studies, such as Bansal et al. (2023), emphasized the persistent bottleneck of dataset scarcity and inconsistent preprocessing, which hinder robust model generalization. More recent frameworks, like Zhou et al. (2025), demonstrated higher accuracies (93%) using larger datasets, though their applicability remained confined to research-level demonstrations. In contrast, the proposed framework in this work leverages transfer learning, extensive augmentation, and a multi-model strategy combining ResNet50V2, DenseNet169, and YOLOv9. This integration achieved superior classification accuracy of 96.7% (ResNet50V2/DenseNet169) while also incorporating YOLOv9 for computational efficiency (training time: 8 minutes). Notably, this study advances beyond prior work by delivering a practically deployable solution through a Streamlit-based web application, bridging the gap between laboratory research and industrial-scale quality control. Thus, the proposed framework establishes a new benchmark for accuracy, robustness, and usability in automated PSC defect detection.

Previous studies in PSC defect detection, such as Li et al. 2019 with 200 test images25, Sun et al. 2022 with 160 test images26, also faced data set limitations. In our 12 test instances, the results per class are consistent with earlier works in this domain; however, they are admittedly small. We highlight that this reflects the high cost and labor-intensive nature of SEM data acquisition. We emphasize the need for community-wide dataset sharing to strengthen statistical robustness.

Limitations and future work

However, the study also emphasizes ongoing challenges, particularly the limited availability of large and diverse SEM datasets. The persistent difficulty in distinguishing subtle morphological variations, such as between pure 3D perovskite and 3D perovskite with excess, highlights the need for improved imaging protocols, domain adaptation, and advanced feature extraction techniques. Future work should prioritize collaborative dataset expansion, semi-supervised or self-supervised learning approaches, and hybrid modeling strategies to further improve generalization across fabrication environments.

Another limitation arises from the scale of identifiable pinholes. In our dataset, pinholes typically appear at dimensions of 500 nm. Well-fabricated films often exhibit much smaller pinholes (<100 nm), which may not be reliably captured given the current SEM resolution and magnification. Thus, the framework may fail to detect nanoscale defects, and future efforts should prioritize high-resolution SEM imaging.

Practical implications

The proposed multi-model deep learning framework offers significant practical benefits for both research and industrial applications of perovskite thin films. By combining high-accuracy classifiers (ResNet50V2 and DenseNet169) with a fast detection model (YOLOv9), the framework enables automated, objective, and real-time defect identification from SEM images, reducing reliance on labor-intensive and subjective manual analysis. The deployment through a Streamlit web application ensures that researchers, laboratory technicians, and quality control personnel can easily upload SEM images and obtain immediate predictions with confidence scores and visual overlays, facilitating rapid decision-making and process optimization. In industrial settings, this approach supports scalable quality control, allowing manufacturers to monitor film morphology, identify defects early, and adjust fabrication parameters to enhance device stability and efficiency. Moreover, the framework’s ability to process small datasets effectively through transfer learning and data augmentation demonstrates its suitability for experimental environments with limited SEM data. Overall, this tool bridges the gap between computational research and practical application, accelerating the development, standardization, and commercialization of high-performance, defect-resilient perovskite solar cells.

Conclusion

This study presented a multi-model deep learning framework for automated defect detection in mixed-dimensionality perovskite films using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images. By integrating ResNet50V2, DenseNet169, and YOLOv9, the framework effectively classified five critical defect categories with high reliability. ResNet50V2 and DenseNet169 achieved superior performance, each attaining 96.7% test accuracy and a weighted F1-score of 0.966, underscoring the effectiveness of transfer learning and data augmentation in overcoming dataset scarcity. Although YOLOv9 achieved a moderate accuracy of 45.0%, it offered substantial computational efficiency, completing training in only eight minutes, highlighting its potential for rapid, real-time deployment. A key contribution of this work lies in bridging the gap between academic research and practical application. The deployment of the trained models within a Streamlit-based web application enables researchers and technicians to perform real-time defect analysis with confidence scores and visualization panels, without requiring advanced expertise in machine learning. This implementation demonstrates scalability for laboratory and industrial quality control, offering a practical pathway toward defect-resilient perovskite solar cell manufacturing.
