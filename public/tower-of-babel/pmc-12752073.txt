Cost-effectiveness analysis of datopotamab deruxtecan versus docetaxel for previously treated advanced or metastatic non-small cell lung cancer
Authors: Fanxu Zeng, Shuishi Li, Pengbo Deng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe advent of antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) has significantly reshaped therapeutic strategies for lung cancer. However, given their exceptionally high treatment costs, evaluating whether their clinical benefits justify these expenses is crucial, highlighting the need for robust economic assessments. This study was designed to clarify the relative cost-effectiveness of datopotamab deruxtecan (Dato-DXd) for individuals who had undergone treatment for advanced or metastatic non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) from the American payers’ perspective.MethodsUtilizing data from the phase III TROPION-Lung01 randomized trial, a Markov model with a three-state structure and horizon of 20 years was implemented to simulate economic and clinical outcomes of Dato-DXd versus docetaxel. Outcomes were assessed using incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICERs), and incremental net health benefits (INHBs). All results were evaluated against a $150,000 per quality-adjusted life year (QALY) willingness-to-pay (WTP) threshold. To assess model robustness, probabilistic and one-way sensitivity analyses were performed.ResultsIn the overall and nonsquamous populations, Dato-DXd incurred additional costs of $225,314 and $241,136, respectively, with corresponding QALY gains of 0.09 and 0.08. This yielded ICERs of $2,483,946/QALY and $3,025,602/QALY, far exceeding the WTP threshold. In contrast, among patients with squamous histology, Dato-DXd resulted in both higher costs ($118,390) and reduced effectiveness (−0.17 QALYs), rendering it a dominant strategy. Sensitivity analysis identified body weight and drug acquisition cost as key variables influencing cost-effectiveness outcomes.ConclusionsDato-DXd showed poor cost-effectiveness in individuals who had undergone treatment for advanced or metastatic NSCLC, particularly those with squamous cell carcinoma in the USA.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12962-025-00694-w.

Introduction

Lung cancer is the second-most common malignancy and the predominant contributor to cancer-associated death in the USA, with approximately 226,650 new diagnoses and 124,730 deaths anticipated in 2025 [1]. An estimated 85% of these are due to non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), which is separated into two major histologic subtypes: nonsquamous (~75%) and squamous (~25%) [2, 3]. M Alarmingly, over 70% of NSCLC cases are diagnosed when already advanced or metastatic, where the 5-year survival rate remains below 18% [4]. While the advent of targeted treatments and the use of immune checkpoint inhibitors has increased survival, many cases still experience disease progression [5–7]. For those who fail initial therapy, docetaxel-based regimens remain the standard of care; however, they are associated with modest clinical benefit [6, 8]. This has fueled efforts to identify more effective treatment strategies.

Antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) are an exciting new development in treatment that is gradually transforming the cancer treatment paradigm [9]. These agents offer advantages over conventional therapies, including improved tumor targeting, the ability to circumvent drug resistance mechanisms, and potential penetration of the blood-brain barrier [9, 10]. One such target, trophoblast cell-surface antigen 2 (TROP-2), a transmembrane glycoprotein highly expressed in NSCLC and associated with poor prognosis, has emerged as a key candidate for ADC development [10, 11]. Datopotamab deruxtecan (Dato-DXd) is a TROP-2-directed ADC, consisting of a humanized monoclonal IgG1 antibody linked via a cleavable tetrapeptide linker to a camptothecin-derived topoisomerase I inhibitor [12, 13]. Dato-DXd exerts its antitumor effects by releasing its cytotoxic payload inside target cells, inducing DNA damage and apoptosis, while also exhibiting bystander-killing effects [12, 13]. The phase III TROPION-Lung01 trial (NCT04656652) recently demonstrated the effectiveness of Dato-DXd in cases that had previously undergone treatment for advanced or metastatic NSCLC. In the overall trial population, Dato-DXd significantly improved progression-free survival (PFS) compared to docetaxel (median PFS: 4.4 vs. 3.7 months; HR: 0.75; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.62–0.91). For the nonsquamous subgroup, overall survival (OS) was also prolonged (median OS: 5.5 vs. 3.6 months; HR: 0.63; 95% CI: 0.51–0.79) [14]. These results suggest that Dato-DXd may become a valuable treatment option, particularly for squamous cell carcinoma who typically have poor prognoses and limited alternatives.

Despite its promising clinical activity, Dato-DXd, like other ADCs, is associated with substantial costs [15, 16]. This raises concerns about its cost-effectiveness and accessibility, particularly within publicly and privately funded healthcare systems. Evaluating the economic value of such innovative therapies is essential to guide decisions by clinicians, patients, payers, and policymakers. Here, the cost-effectiveness of Dato-DXd relative to docetaxel in NSCLC cases that had previously undergone treatment for advanced or metastatic disease was investigated using data from the USA.

Materials and methods

This evaluation of cost-effectiveness utilized data from the TROPION-Lung01 phase III randomized controlled trial, which enrolled patients from February 2021 to November 7, 2022. The study design adhered to the CHEERS 2022 statement issued by the International Society for Pharmacoeconomics and Outcomes Research [17] (Supplementary Table S1).

Population and intervention

The modeled patient population included individuals with stage IIIB/C or IV NSCLC with no actionable genetic mutations, who had previously undergone treatment with platinum-based chemotherapy and programmed death receptor 1/programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-1/PD-L1) inhibitors. Participants were randomly assigned to be given either Dato-DXd (n = 299; 6 mg/kg every 3 weeks) or docetaxel (n = 305; 75 mg/m2 every 3 weeks), in accordance with the clinical trial protocol [14, 18] (Supplementary Table S2). It was assumed that the simulated patient was a 64-year-old male, weighing 70 kilograms and with a body surface area of 1.84 square meters to calculate the dosage of the medication [19]. Tumor response was monitored every six weeks. If the disease progressed or if unacceptable toxic effects developed, post-progression treatments were administered based on trial data and National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines, with 52.2% of cases in the Dato-DXd arm and 55.4% in the docetaxel arm receiving subsequent therapy [8, 14] (Supplementary Table S2). Patients not eligible for further treatment received the best supportive care (BSC). Model establishment

A decision-analytic Markov model was constructed to provide a simulation of the clinical course of advanced or metastatic NSCLC following prior treatment. The model comprised three health statuses, namely, PFS, progressive disease (PD), and death (Fig. 1). All cases entered the model with PFS status and could transition to PD or death in subsequent cycles. After reaching PD status, cases could remain there or transition to death [22]. The model used a 6-week cycle length and a 20-year time horizon, by which time more than 99% of patients were assumed to have died. Model development and simulations were conducted using TreeAge Pro 2021 (https://www.treeage.com).Fig. 1Markov Model. Abbreviation: NSCLC, non-small cell lung cancer; M, Markov

Primary model outcomes in this study consisted of total costs, life-years (LYs), incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICERs), incremental net health benefits (INHBs), and quality-adjusted LYs (QALYs). Costs and health outcomes were both discounted at an annual 3% rate in line with economic evaluation standards [19]. The ICER was defined as the additional cost per additional QALY gained, with comparisons made to a WTP threshold of $150,000/QALY [19, 22]. Model survival and transition probabilities

Given the limited follow-up duration in clinical trials, extrapolation of survival data beyond the observed period is necessary to support long-term projections in health economic modeling. To this end, Kaplan-Meier (KM) curves of OS and PFS analyses were digitized using GetData Graph Digitizer (version 2.26, available at: http://www.getdata-graph-digitizer.com/index.php) [23, 30]. The reconstructed data were then fitted to a series of commonly applied parametric distributions, including Exponential, Log-logistic, Log-normal, Gompertz, and Weibull models, with selection guided by clinical plausibility, visual fit, and the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) (Supplementary Figure S1 and Table S3) [22]. Among these, the Weibull distribution demonstrated the best overall fit and was used to simulate individual patient data for further modeling. Cycle-specific, time-dependent probabilities of transition were obtained from the fitted Weibull model using the equation: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(1{\rm{ }} - {\rm{ }}exp{\rm{ }}\{ \lambda {\left( {t - u} \right)^\gamma }-\lambda {t^\gamma }\} )$$\end{document}, where u is the Markov cycle, t represents the current model cycle, λ and γ respectively denote the scale and shape parameters. Calculations were performed with R (version 4.4.2, available at: http://www.rproject.org) (Table 1) [22].

Utility values and cost inputs

Health state utility values ranged from 0 (death) to 1 (perfect health), in correspondence with health-related quality-of-life (HRQoL) values associated with each Markov state. As the TROPION-Lung01 trial did not report utility values, previously published literature sources were utilized to inform these estimates. The utility for patients in the PFS state was set at 0.650, whereas patients in the PD state were assigned a utility of 0.430 [20, 21]. Adjustments were applied to reflect the disutility associated with treatment-related adverse events (AEs) of grade 3 or above, with an incidence of ≥ 5% [19, 22–24].

Only direct medical costs were utilized when conducting the present analyses. These encompassed the primary treatment costs, management of key AEs (e.g., stomatitis, interstitial lung disease [ILD]/pneumonitis, neutropenia, and leukopenia), routine tumor imaging, follow-up visits (including imaging and laboratory tests), drug administration, best supportive care (BSC), and end-of-life care (Table 1). All unit costs were sourced from the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services and previously published literature [25–28]. Medical costs were adjusted to 2025 USD rates by Consumer Price Index [31].

Sensitivity and subgroup analyses

Multiple sensitivity analyses were performed to evaluate both model robustness and the effects of input uncertainty. One-way sensitivity analyses were undertaken by modulating all model parameters by ±20% of their base-case values, following common approaches from previous studies to assess the impact of input variation on ICERs [19, 22]. Probabilistic sensitivity analysis was conducted through 10,000 Monte Carlo simulations, with parameter values drawn from appropriate statistical distributions based on their nature [19, 22]. Results were visualized using tornado diagrams, cost-effectiveness acceptability curves, and scatter plots of cost-effectiveness.

Subgroup analyses were carried out for key patient subpopulations based on available forest plot data. These subgroups were stratified by age, sex, smoking history, presence of brain metastases at baseline, and actionable genomic alterations [14]. As detailed survival curves were unavailable for these subgroups, the assumption was made that their clinical characteristics were similar to the overall population, except for PFS, OS, and corresponding HRs [22].

Results

Baseline results

In the overall population, treatment with Dato-DXd produced a total of 1.03 QALYs and 2.04 LYs at an estimated cost of $294,055. In the non-squamous population, the outcomes were similar, yielding 1.01 QALYs and 1.98 LYs at a cost of $308,747. By contrast, docetaxel yielded 0.94 and 0.93 QALYs (1.92 LYs in both populations) at significantly lower costs of $68,742 and $67,611, respectively. These differences resulted in ICERs of $2,483,946 per QALY ($1,745,514 per LY) and $3,025,602 per QALY ($3,495,260 per LY) in the overall and non-squamous populations, respectively, both accompanied by negative INHBs of −1.41 and −1.53 QALYs (Table 2). Sensitivity analysis

The tornado diagrams from the one-way sensitivity analysis revealed that body weight had the greatest effect on the results. Varying body weight from 30 to 140 kg shifted the ICER from $955,387/QALY to $5,158,924/QALY. Other key drivers of uncertainty included the cost of Dato-DXd, which ranged from $35,154 to $52,732 (producing ICERs from $1,948,950/QALY to $3,018,941/QALY), and the utility value of the PFS state, which ranged from 0.520 to 0.780 (corresponding to ICERs of $2,079,655/QALY to $3,083,358/QALY) (Fig. 2). Subgroup analysis

Across all patient subgroups analyzed, Dato-DXd failed to demonstrate cost-effectiveness. The subgroup with actionable genomic alterations showed the most favorable ICER, at $870,383/QALY, though it still resulted in an INHB of −2.36 QALYs. Patients younger than 65 years and those who were current or former smokers exhibited ICERs of $1,717,112/QALY and $1,722,280/QALY, respectively. Discussion

The results of the prospective phase 3 TROPION-Lung01 trial establish Dato-DXd as a novel therapeutic option in NSCLC cases that have previously undergone treatment for advanced or metastatic disease. Importantly, its emergence challenges traditional classifications and treatment paradigms in NSCLC. Globally, lung cancer remains the most economically burdensome malignancy, with an estimated cost of $3,888 billion [32]. In the United States alone, the average expenditure for lung cancer care ranges between $20 billion and $30 billion annually and continues to rise [33, 34]. It is mainly used for advanced targeted therapy, ADCs and disease management, etc [35–38]. This underscores a substantial economic strain on both patients and healthcare payers. As the incidence of NSCLC continues to increase, it becomes essential to assess the value, defined as the balance between clinical benefit and financial cost, of emerging therapies. The rising demand for effective treatments in advanced or metastatic settings, coupled with a growing emphasis on cost-effectiveness in healthcare policy, has prompted the need to evaluate the economic viability of Dato-DXd in this clinical context.

This study represents the first evaluation of Dato-DXd cost-effectiveness relative to docetaxel in NSCLC cases that have undergone previous treatment for advanced or metastatic disease. Utilizing a Markov modeling approach, the present analysis found that while Dato-DXd offered modest improvements in life-years gained of 2.04 and 1.92 life-years (24.48 and 23.04 months of quality-adjusted survival), this benefit came at a considerably higher financial cost. The additional expenditure per patient was nearly $220,000. The ICER of Dato-DXd was $2,483,946 per QALY gained, which far exceeds the commonly accepted U.S. threshold of $150,000 per QALY. One-way sensitivity analysis highlighted patient body weight as a major cost driver. Intriguingly, the model suggested that Dato-DXd is only likely to be cost-effective in individuals below 10 kg in weight, which is an unrealistic scenario for adult patients, thereby raising significant ethical and practical concerns. This finding suggests that modifications to the current dosing regimen may be warranted. For example, a shift from weight-based to fixed-dose administration, similar to the dosing approach for immune checkpoint inhibitors, could improve cost efficiency. Presently, Dato-DXd is marketed in 100 mg vials, and dosage calculations based on body weight frequently result in partial vial usage, with the remainder discarded as medical waste. A more efficient drug distribution system could involve reallocating unused portions from underweight patients to those with higher body weights, potentially reducing overall waste and cost. Previous studies also support that such decisions can address the issues of ADCs bottle waste and low economic efficiency [39–41]. Drug pricing also emerged as a critical determinant of cost-effectiveness. Threshold analysis indicated that Dato-DXd would only be considered cost-effective if its price were reduced by approximately 87.5%, bringing the cost to $6.54 per mg. This aligns with prior observations that pharmaceutical companies often price new therapies well above the inflation-adjusted annual cost increases [42]. In the context of rare and life-threatening disorders where treatment options are limited, such pricing strategies significantly hinder access. To mitigate this, a shift toward patient- or cycle-based payment models rather than per-vial pricing is warranted. Additionally, federal and state-level policy interventions are needed to incorporate cost-effectiveness analyses into drug pricing negotiations, thereby facilitating equitable access while alleviating the financial burden on patients. Unaffordable treatment costs have been linked to lower treatment adherence, worse prognoses, increased recurrence rates, and decreased overall survival [43].

Notably, Dato-DXd was not found to be cost-effective in any histological subgroup, with particularly unfavorable results among patients with squamous cell carcinoma. In this cohort, Dato-DXd resulted in substantial costs with minimal therapeutic gain, making it an unattractive treatment option. Squamous cell carcinoma, a subtype of NSCLC, generally carries a more favorable prognosis than other subtypes, in part because it often presents as a localized disease at diagnosis. Several studies have reported that stage-matched outcomes for squamous cell carcinoma are better than those for adenocarcinoma or large cell carcinoma [44]. These findings suggest that in resource-constrained environments, Dato-DXd may offer limited survival benefits, particularly for non-squamous histologies, and should be reserved for select patient populations where greater therapeutic efficacy is anticipated. Furthermore, subgroup analyses revealed no survival advantage with Dato-DXd among never-smokers and patients with brain metastases. Prior research suggests that smokers may respond more favorably to immunotherapy due to a higher tumor mutation burden, which enhances immune recognition and tumor cell destruction [45]. Conversely, the brain’s immunosuppressive microenvironment is thought to inhibit anti-tumor immune responses, diminishing the effectiveness of therapies, including Dato-DXd, in patients with brain metastases [45]. However, the microenvironment of patients with brain metastases itself has an immunosuppressive effect, which can enhance tumorigenesis and prevent antitumor effects [46]. These observations reinforce the need for predictive indicators to identify populations that may derive benefits from targeted therapies like Dato-DXd. Clinical decision-making in NSCLC should incorporate a range of molecular and clinical factors, including TROP-2 expression, programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression, tumor mutation burden (TMB) status, and the potential for monotherapy versus combination therapy. Such a multifactorial approach will facilitate the development of more precise, cost-effective, and clinically beneficial treatment strategies.

This study has several limitations. First, the model was built on clinical trial data, which, like most modeling studies, introduces inherent uncertainties. Nevertheless, the survival data fitted well to Weibull distributions and was validated through sensitivity analysis. Long-term outcomes for Dato-DXd remain unclear and will require further updates from the TROPION-Lung01 trial to refine model predictions. Second, the analysis did not include costs linked to grade 1 or 2 AEs, which could lead to a slight underestimation of total treatment costs. However, sensitivity analyses showed that varying AE-related costs within plausible ranges did not significantly impact the overall model results. Third, the utility values were not obtained from the TROPION-Lung01 trial but from previously published literature on advanced or metastatic NSCLC. While utility inputs are critical in cost-effectiveness modeling, one-way sensitivity analysis indicated that variations in progression-free and progressive disease utility values had minimal influence on model outcomes.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this economic evaluation indicates that Dato-DXd is not a cost-effective treatment for NSCLC cases that have previously undergone treatment for advanced or metastatic disease within the U.S. healthcare system, particularly among patients with squamous cell carcinoma, never-smokers, and those with brain metastases. To improve the cost-effectiveness profile of this agent, strategies such as drug price reductions and modifications to dosing practices should be considered. These findings offer valuable insights for clinicians, patients, and healthcare policymakers seeking to balance clinical benefit with economic sustainability.
