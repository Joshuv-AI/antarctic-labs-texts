Type II Kounis Syndrome Secondary to Amoxicillin Allergy
Authors: Hrushikesh Reddy Pamreddy, Arihant Surana, Krishna Sagar Gajula, Usama Talib, Allen Filliberti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundKounis syndrome is an under-recognized cause of acute coronary syndrome precipitated by allergic or anaphylactic reactions. History of Presentation

A 64-year-old woman presented to the emergency department with acute onset of tongue swelling, lip swelling, facial and hand erythema, burning sensation in her extremities, and progressive dyspnea. Symptoms began approximately 30 minutes after taking an amoxicillin pill for a groin rash. Shortly after symptom onset, she developed lightheadedness and began experiencing chest discomfort while on the way to the hospital.

On arrival, vital signs revealed profound hypotension with blood pressure of 55/33 mm Hg, heart rate of 94 beats/min, respiratory rate of 21 breaths/min, and oxygen saturation of 96% on room air. Physical examination was notable for angioedema of the lips and tongue, diffuse cutaneous flushing, and mild respiratory distress.

She was immediately treated for anaphylaxis with intramuscular epinephrine and intravenous methylprednisolone and diphenhydramine, with subsequent hemodynamic improvement.

Past Medical History

The patient's medical history was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, plantar fasciitis, and acquired left hallux valgus. She reported a known allergy to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole. She denied use of any regular medications despite her chronic conditions. Social history was significant for a 50 pack-year smoking history. She denied alcohol or recreational drug use.

Of note, she had presented to the emergency department 6 months prior with chest pressure and mildly elevated troponin (20 ng/L), and she was advised to undergo further cardiac work-up but had left against medical advice. An outpatient echocardiogram performed 2 months later demonstrated normal left ventricular size and function, with an ejection fraction of 55% to 60% and no wall motion abnormalities, suggesting subclinical coronary disease.

Differential Diagnosis

Given the presenting symptoms, anaphylaxis was immediately recognized. However, the development of chest discomfort in this setting raised several considerations, such as type II Kounis syndrome (allergic mediator-induced plaque destabilization), demand ischemia secondary to hypotension, Takotsubo cardiomyopathy, and epinephrine-induced coronary vasospasm.

Investigations

Laboratory studies

Initial laboratory evaluation was notable for metabolic acidosis (bicarbonate: 16 mEq/L, anion gap: 19, lactic acid: 4.4 mmol/L), hyperglycemia (glucose: 213 mg/dL), and an atherogenic lipid profile (low-density lipoprotein cholesterol: 154 mg/dL, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol: 41 mg/dL). Complete blood count, renal function, and liver enzymes were within normal limits. Serum tryptase, obtained approximately 90 minutes after symptom onset, was 7.6 ng/mL (reference: <11.4 ng/mL). Detailed laboratory values are provided in Supplemental Table 1.

Electrocardiography

The initial electrocardiogram (ECG) demonstrated normal sinus rhythm without acute ischemic changes. A repeat ECG obtained after the patient reported worsening chest pain revealed ST-segment elevations in leads II, III, aVF, and V6, with reciprocal ST-segment depressions in leads I, aVL, V2, and V4, consistent with acute inferior ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) with posterior involvement (Figure 1).

Chest radiography

Chest x-ray showed diffuse increased interstitial markings with a possible trace left pleural effusion.

Echocardiography

Postprocedural transthoracic echocardiography showed low-normal left ventricular systolic function (ejection fraction: 55%) with mild to moderate hypokinesis of the basal and midinferior and inferolateral segments. Right ventricular function was normal with no significant valvular disease or pericardial effusion.

Management

Emergency department management

Upon recognition of anaphylaxis, the patient received immediate treatment with intramuscular epinephrine, intravenous methylprednisolone 125 mg, and intravenous diphenhydramine 50 mg. Intravenous fluid resuscitation was initiated for hypotension.

When repeat ECG demonstrated ST-segment elevations consistent with inferior STEMI, a Code STEMI was activated. Given her known contrast allergy (prior reaction to iodinated contrast), she was premedicated with hydrocortisone and diphenhydramine before cardiac catheterization.

Coronary angiography and intervention

Emergent coronary angiography was performed via right radial arterial access (Figure 2).

Successful percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) of the mid-RCA was performed with implantation of a 3.5 × 48 mm everolimus-eluting stent, postdilated with a 3.75-mm noncompliant balloon catheter (Figure 3, Figure 4, Figure 5). Final angiography demonstrated an excellent result, with TIMI flow grade 3 (Figure 6).

The patient experienced complete resolution of chest pain after intervention. Postprocedural ECG showed resolution of ST-segment elevations with development of T-wave inversions in the inferior leads, consistent with successful reperfusion.

Postprocedural medical therapy

The patient was initiated on guideline-directed medical therapy including aspirin 81 mg daily, ticagrelor 90 mg twice daily, atorvastatin 80 mg daily, valsartan 160 mg daily, and metoprolol succinate (dose titrated to heart rate). Postprocedural intravenous normal saline was administered at 100 mL/h for 4 hours for contrast-induced nephropathy prophylaxis.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient's postprocedural course was uncomplicated, with a hospital length of stay of 3 days. Her high anion gap metabolic acidosis and mild transaminitis, attributed to hypoperfusion during anaphylaxis, resolved with fluid resuscitation and restoration of coronary perfusion.

Cardiac biomarkers demonstrated appropriate rise and fall, with creatine kinase–MB trending down from 202 to 63 ng/mL by hospital day 2, consistent with successful reperfusion (Table 1). Echocardiography on postprocedure day 1 confirmed preserved ejection fraction at 55% with regional wall motion abnormalities corresponding to the RCA territory.

The patient completed inpatient cardiac rehabilitation and was referred for outpatient cardiac rehabilitation. Discussion

Kounis syndrome, first described by Nicholas Kounis in 1991, refers to the concurrence of acute coronary syndrome (ACS) with allergic or hypersensitivity reactions, including anaphylaxis.1 The syndrome encompasses a spectrum of presentations classified into 3 variants: Type I occurs in patients with normal coronary arteries, where allergic mediators induce coronary vasospasm; type II occurs in patients with pre-existing atheromatous disease, where mediator release precipitates plaque erosion or rupture; and type III involves stent thrombosis triggered by hypersensitivity reactions.2, 3, 4

A systematic review of amoxicillin-induced Kounis syndrome identified only 33 cases reported in the literature from 1972 to May 2022, with a striking male predominance (81.8% male).5 Given that type II Kounis syndrome represents approximately 48% of all cases, and considering the male predominance, our case represents 1 of only approximately 3 reported cases of type II Kounis syndrome in a female patient secondary to amoxicillin.2

The pathophysiology involves inflammatory mediators such as histamine, leukotrienes, platelet-activating factor, and cytokines, which exert direct effects on the coronary vasculature and atherosclerotic plaque.1 Histamine promotes coronary vasoconstriction via H1 receptors and activates platelets. Mast cell–derived proteases can degrade the fibrous cap of vulnerable plaques, precipitating rupture.1,6 In the present case, the angiographic finding of acute mid-RCA occlusion with nonobstructive disease in the remaining coronary system, in the setting of systemic anaphylaxis, strongly supported allergic mediator-induced plaque destabilization.

Serum tryptase in our patient was within normal limits (7.6 ng/mL); however, tryptase elevation is neither sensitive nor specific for Kounis syndrome, and the absence of a baseline level limits interpretation.7, 8, 9 An acute-to-baseline ratio exceeding 1.685 supports systemic mast cell activation.8,10 The clinical presentation and angiographic findings were diagnostic.

Management of Kounis syndrome presents unique challenges, as therapies for anaphylaxis and ACS may have conflicting effects.11,12 Epinephrine may exacerbate coronary vasospasm, while beta-blockers may worsen bronchospasm. Our patient received epinephrine before STEMI recognition, and emergent revascularization was prioritized once diagnosed.

Nicorandil, a coronary vasodilator with minimal hemodynamic effects, may be useful in Kounis syndrome with suspected vasospasm.11,13 In our case, complete thrombotic occlusion rather than isolated vasospasm made primary PCI the definitive treatment. The decision to proceed with catheterization despite a history of contrast allergy reflects the favorable risk-benefit ratio in the setting of ongoing STEMI.

Clinicians must maintain vigilance for cardiac complications during anaphylaxis, as chest pain may be attributed to the allergic reaction itself rather than recognized as a distinct cardiac event. ECG should be obtained promptly in any patient with chest discomfort during anaphylaxis.

Conclusions

This case illustrates type II Kounis syndrome presenting as inferior STEMI in a patient with anaphylaxis secondary to amoxicillin. Prompt recognition and emergent PCI resulted in successful revascularization and a favorable outcome. Clinicians should maintain high suspicion for ACS when chest symptoms develop during allergic reactions, even in patients without known coronary artery disease.
