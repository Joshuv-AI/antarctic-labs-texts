Unraveling the nexus between lung cancer and rheumatoid arthritis using integrative transcriptomics and genomics
Authors: Heng Li, Liping Ding, Nini Li, Xiaoping Hong, Dongzhou Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIndividuals with rheumatoid arthritis (RA) are at a significantly increased risk of developing lung cancer (LC), with a 30%-40% higher incidence compared to the general population. Although a link between the two conditions has been established, the molecular mechanisms and genetic factors remain not fully elucidated. This study aims to uncover the potential associations between LC and RA through an integrative approach of transcriptomics and genomics analysis.MethodsWe collected whole-genome expression data from patients with LC, RA, and healthy controls. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) was applied to analyze genomic variations in blood samples from RA patients, including those with comorbid LC and interstitial lung disease (ILD). Using bioinformatics tools, we identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and conducted an integrative analysis of pathways shared and specific to LC and RA.ResultsThe transcriptome analysis identified 1,051 DEGs common to both LC and RA, with distinct regulatory patterns. Among these, 441 genes were commonly upregulated, 345 were downregulated, and 265 exhibited opposite regulation between the two diseases. After integrating significant genomic mutation data, an additional 92 upregulated, 90 downregulated, and 41 oppositely regulated key genes were identified. Functional and enrichment analyses of these key genes revealed shared alterations in immune-related pathways, particularly the upregulation of viral response and immune signaling pathways, and the downregulation of T-cell receptor (TCR) signaling, T cell activation, and non-coding RNA metabolism. Furthermore, lymphocyte and leukocyte migration, as well as the positive regulation of programmed cell death, showed opposite regulation patterns between the two diseases. Laboratory tests also revealed changes in lymphocytes.ConclusionThis study delineates potential common mechanisms between LC and RA, suggesting that an enhanced viral response, attenuated TCR signaling, and reduced T cell activation in the peripheral system may represent shared pathological mechanisms, potentially contributing to the increased LC risk in RA patients. Conversely regulated DEGs may serve as novel biomarkers for pulmonary diseases. The changes in immune-related pathways and their potential association with lymphocyte activity provide new perspectives for understanding the relationship between LC and RA.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15046-3.Keywords: Rheumatoid arthritis, Lung cancer, Genomic variations, Immune response pathways

Introduction

Patients with Rheumatoid Arthritis (RA) have a significantly higher risk of developing lung cancer (LC) compared to the general population, with a standardized incidence ratio (SIR) of 1.35 [1.14; 1.60] [1]. This increased risk is partly attributed to underlying connective tissue disease, which contributes to poorer outcomes in LC patients, especially those with interstitial lung disease (ILD) or Qi deficiency syndrome [2]. RA is a systemic autoimmune disorder with a global prevalence of approximately 0.5-1.0% [3–5], characterized by chronic synovitis, systemic inflammation, and the presence of autoantibodies, leading to joint damage and multi-system complications [6, 7]. In addition, smoking RA patients are more likely to develop lung cancer than non-RA smokers [8]. In a 2016 study, Curtis et al. found that among 5671 RA patients treated with tropinib, lung cancer was the most frequently observed malignancy (0.42%) [9]. Similarly, a 2022 study by Chatzidionysiou et al. reported a higher incidence of lung cancer in RA patients (56 per 100,000) compared to the general population, with a hazard ratio (HR) of 1.76 (95% CI 1.60 to 1.93) even after adjusting for smoking [10]. While the precise mechanisms driving this association remain unclear, genetic factors are believed to play a significant role [11, 12]. Recent studies also suggest potential molecular overlaps between lung cancer and other chronic diseases [13].

Genetic predisposition accounts for approximately 60% of RA susceptibility [14, 15]. More than 100 genetic loci associated with RA have been identified, with 80% of risk variants located in non-coding regions that influence gene expression and mRNA stability [16]. The HLA-II gene contributes to 30% of RA susceptibility [17], with specific alleles such as HLA-DRB1 and IRX1 strongly implicated in RA pathogenesis [18, 19]. Polymorphisms in other HLA genes, including HLA-DPB1, HLA-B, and HLA-A, further increase susceptibility [20]. Additionally, mutations in the PTPN22 gene, particularly the R620W (rs2476601) variant, have been linked to approximately a two-fold increased risk of RA and shown to enhance neutrophil activation in affected individuals [21–23]. Further studies have identified additional RA susceptibility genes, such as MMEL1 and CTLA4 in the Han Chinese population [24, 25], and international collaborative efforts have uncovered 101 RA-associated loci, offering new perspectives for drug development [26]. These discoveries not only enhance our understanding of the genetic foundations of RA but also open up new avenues for creating targeted therapies. For instance, the CTLA4 gene is a known immune checkpoint, and its mutations are associated with RA development. Therefore, CTLA4-targeted drugs, such as Abatacept, which modulate immune responses, have already been utilized in RA treatment. Abatacept works by inhibiting T cell activation, reducing the immune system’s attack on joints, and has been shown to effectively alleviate RA symptoms [27]. Moreover, genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have revealed significant enrichment of RA-associated single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in the enhancer regions of T cells and NK cells [28], and identified multiple SNPs linked to RA susceptibility, including TRAF1-C5 and TAT4 [29–32]. These findings underscore the critical role of genetic variation in the pathogenesis of RA and provide valuable insights for the development of novel therapeutic strategies. For example, JAK inhibitors (like tofacitinib) and IL-6 receptor inhibitors (such as tocilizumab) regulate these immune signaling pathways to slow or stop the progression of RA [33, 34].

Recent studies have expanded beyond single gene mutations to examine broader genetic factors, which involve multiple genes and play a critical role in genetic diversity. Understanding these genetic factors at a genome-wide level in RA patients is essential for elucidating the complex genetic architecture underlying the disease. Given the established epidemiological and genetic links between RA and lung cancer, we hypothesize that the two diseases may share overlapping as well as distinct molecular mechanisms. To test this, we conducted an integrative multi-omics analysis, aiming to identify common and disease-specific genetic and transcriptomic features, including DEGs, between RA and lung cancer. The identification of DEGs common to both diseases, along with the analysis of their regulatory patterns, may reveal shared pathogenic pathways, novel biomarkers, and therapeutic targets relevant to both conditions.

Materials and methods

Patients and controls

Patients diagnosed with RA and healthy controls were recruited from the Department of Rheumatology at Shenzhen People’s Hospital, the Second Clinical Medical College of Jinan University, China, between January 2020 and March 2022. All participants were over 18 years of age and provided written informed consent. Given the higher prevalence of RA in females (approximately 80%), the study predominantly included female patients (90%). All RA patients met the 2010 ACR/EULAR classification criteria. Detailed medical histories and biochemical test results were retrieved from the hospital’s information system. Peripheral blood was collected from all participants and stored at −80 °C in a biobank for future analyses. The study received ethical approval from the Shenzhen People’s Hospital ethics committee (LL-KY-2022158-01) and was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data acquisition and preprocessing

Transcriptomic data related to RA and lung cancer, including both patient and control groups, were obtained from publicly accessible databases such as the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). The GEO accession numbers for the LC and RA datasets are listed in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2, respectively, and can be accessed directly from the GEO website. Rigorous preprocessing of the raw transcriptomic data was performed to ensure the reliability and accuracy of the analysis. This process included the removal of outliers, normalization of the data, and correction for batch effects to minimize technical variability. For microarray datasets, raw CEL files were processed in R (version 4.2.2) using the oligo or affy package, and expression values were derived using the Robust Multi-array Average (RMA) algorithm, which includes background correction, quantile normalization, and summarization at the probe level. Log2 transformation was applied where necessary, based on intensity distribution thresholds (e.g., 99th percentile > 100 or interquartile range > 50 with a positive 25th percentile). Between-array normalization was further performed using the normalizeBetweenArrays function from the limma package (version 3.54.0). For RNA-seq datasets, normalization was performed using either the variance stabilizing transformation (VST) from the DESeq2 package (version ≥ 1.36.0) or the trimmed mean of M-values (TMM) method from the edgeR package (version ≥ 3.38.0), depending on dataset compatibility. Outlier samples were identified and excluded based on metadata inspection, hierarchical clustering, or UMAP projection. To eliminate unwanted technical variation, batch effects were corrected using the ComBat algorithm from the sva package (version ≥ 3.44.0), which applies an empirical Bayes approach to adjust for known batch variables. All preprocessing procedures were performed using R and Bioconductor packages, including GEOquery (version 2.66.0), Biobase (version 2.58.0), limma (version 3.54.0), sva (version ≥ 3.44.0), and umap (version 0.2.10.0), ensuring consistent and reproducible data preparation across studies.

Data processing and normalization

Whole genome sequencing was performed by BGI with an average depth of 33.46×, genome coverage ≥ 1× at 99.18%, and ≥ 10× at 98.01%. All samples had Q30 scores > 91%. Reads were aligned to GRCh38 using BWA-MEM, and variants were called and filtered using GATK (v4.1.4) with VQSR based on HapMap, 1000G, and dbSNP. The workflow included MarkDuplicates and BQSR, ensuring standardization and batch effect control.

Functional annotation and pathway analysis

Functional annotation of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) identified in RA and lung cancer was conducted using DAVID Bioinformatics Resources 6.8 (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/). To further explore the biological significance of these DEGs, pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the KEGG, GO, and BioCarta databases. This analysis facilitated the identification of key signaling pathways potentially involved in the pathogenesis of RA and lung cancer.

Protein-Protein interaction (PPI) analysis

To elucidate the interactions among proteins implicated in RA and lung cancer, a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed using the STRING database (Version 11.0; https://string-db.org/). This analysis not only identified the interactions between proteins but also assessed the binding affinities and characterized the resultant protein complexes, providing deeper insights into the molecular interactions driving these diseases.

Clinical data collection

Clinical safety and efficacy assessments were conducted through a series of standardized laboratory tests. These included routine blood tests, liver and kidney function tests, and immunoglobulin level assessments. Specific serological markers relevant to RA activity and severity, such as rheumatoid factor (RF), C-reactive protein (CRP), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide (anti-CCP) antibodies, complement C4 (C4), neutrophil counts, and lymphocyte counts, were systematically evaluated to monitor the patients’ clinical status.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 17.0 software (SPSS, Chicago, USA). The corrplot package in R language was used to visualize the heat map of correlation coefficient values. Using Prism software, we performed linear regression and other relevant tests. Normally distributed data were compared by one-way ANOVA or t-test. If the normal distribution was not observed, nonparametric methods were used for analysis. Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). According to the number of samples. GraphPad Prism 5 was used to generate graphics. In the transcriptomic analysis, multiple hypothesis testing was addressed by applying the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to control the false discovery rate (FDR), with adjusted p-values < 0.05 considered significant for identifying differentially expressed genes.

Results

Transcriptomic and genomic data generation

In this study, we employed an integrative approach combining transcriptomic and genomic analyses to explore the relationship between rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and lung cancer (LC) (Fig. 1). Transcriptomic analysis was conducted on RA (n = 30) and LC (n = 16) patients, each compared with distinct healthy control (HC) groups (RA-HC: n = 30; LC-HC: n = 45), identifying differentially expressed genes (DEGs). The HC samples used for RA vs. HC and LC vs. HC comparisons were independent and non-overlapping. Genomic analysis was based on a separate cohort consisting of 9 individuals, divided equally among three groups: RA (n = 3), RA with lung cancer (RA LC), and RA with interstitial lung disease (RA ILD). This analysis aimed to identify unique mutations across the diseases and integrate these findings with transcriptomic data to highlight genes with both DNA-level mutations and RNA-level expression differences.

Overview of Integrative Transcriptomics and Genomics

Transcriptomic analysis reveals shared and distinct pathways in LC and RA

Following data preprocessing and standardization, we obtained normalized expression data for RA and healthy controls (Figure S1A) and for LC and healthy controls (Fig. 2B), ensuring the reliability of subsequent analyses. Differential gene expression analysis identified significant expression changes between RA and healthy controls and between LC and healthy controls, using a fold change threshold of > 1.2 (Fig. 2A-B). Differential expression analysis revealed that both RA and LC groups had extensive transcriptional alterations compared to healthy controls, with LC showing a slightly greater number of upregulated genes (Fig. 3A). Nonlinear dimensionality reduction using UMAP revealed clear separation in gene expression profiles among RA, LC, and healthy controls, highlighting significant transcriptomic differences (Fig. 2C-D). Intersection analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between LC and RA identified 441 commonly upregulated and 345 commonly downregulated genes (Fig. 3B). Additionally, 191 genes were upregulated in RA but downregulated in LC, and 74 genes were downregulated in RA but upregulated in LC.

Pathway and process enrichment analysis was conducted on four groups of DEGs: RA up/LC up, RA up/LC down, RA down/LC up, and RA down/LC down (Fig. 4A-D). This analysis revealed key biological processes and pathways for each group. In the RA up/LC up group, pathways related to neutrophil degranulation, innate immune response, and cytokine signaling were significantly enriched (Fig. 4A). The network of enriched terms highlighted connections between neutrophil degranulation and innate immune response via Toll-like Receptor Cascades (Fig. 4E). PPI analysis identified Molecular Complex Detection (MCODE) networks, underscoring the inflammatory and immune dysregulation common to both diseases (Fig. 4I). In the RA down/LC down group, pathways like lymphocyte activation and regulation of T cell activation had the highest significance and enrichment scores (Fig. 4B). PPI analysis revealed dense protein networks associated with these pathways (Fig. 4J). For the RA up/LC down group, pathways such as metabolism of RNA, protein modification, and translation were significantly enriched, indicating differences in cell cycle regulation and DNA damage response between LC and RA (Fig. 4C). Network analysis showed RNA methylation, RNA catabolic process, and regulation of RNA splicing as independent pathways, with MCODE analysis highlighting metabolism of RNA and translation as key components (Fig. 4K). In the RA down/LC up group, significant pathways included signal transduction by growth factor receptors, adherens junction, and RORA activates gene expression (Fig. 4D). PPI analysis demonstrated distinct networks, indicating RORA’s critical role in gene regulation and its contribution to the differing disease mechanisms in LC and RA (Fig. 4L).

Genomic analysis identifies key mutations in RA, RA-LC, and RA-ILD

The genomic analysis in this study focused on identifying key mutations across three patient groups: primary rheumatoid arthritis (RA), rheumatoid arthritis with lung cancer (RA-LC), and rheumatoid arthritis with interstitial lung disease (RA-ILD). The human reference genome (GRCh38/HG38) was used as a control to distinguish mutant genes among these groups. Shared mutations within each disease group were identified by intersecting individual mutation profiles to reduce intra-group variability. These mutations are missense variants that change amino acid sequences and can potentially affect gene function [35]. The number of mutated genes was 4,283 in RA, 5,399 in RA-LC, and 4,727 in RA-ILD. RA-LC presented a notably higher mutation count (Fig. 5A). Chromosomal distribution analysis revealed that chromosome 19 had the highest mutation density in all three diseases. RA had fewer mutations on chromosomes 2, 3, 13, and X. Compared to RA and RA-ILD, RA-LC exhibited a higher number of mutations on the X chromosome, while RA-ILD had fewer mutations on chromosomes 4, 5, and 18 (Fig. 5B-D). The widespread presence of these mutations suggests their potential roles in the development of these diseases.

Venn diagram analysis illustrated the unique and shared mutations across RA, RA-LC, and RA-ILD, providing crucial insights into the distinct and common molecular mechanisms underlying these conditions (Fig. 5E).

Functional enrichment analysis of the RA-specific mutated genes revealed significant enrichment in pathways related to negative regulation of dendritic cell differentiation, glycosylation, and extracellular matrix organization. Molecular Complex Detection (MCODE) analysis further demonstrated that pathways involving negative regulation of nuclear division and female gamete generation were closely linked, as well as pathways related to plasma lipoprotein assembly, remodeling, and clearance, and dendrite self-avoidance (Fig. 6A, E). In RA-LC, functional enrichment analysis showed significant enrichment in pathways related to sensory perception, chromosome segregation, microtubule cytoskeleton organization, and flavonoid glucuronidation. MCODE analysis revealed that these pathways were relatively independent, with protein dephosphorylation linking gamete generation and microtubule cytoskeleton organization (Fig. 6B, F). In RA-ILD, enrichment analysis indicated that the ECM glycoproteins pathway was the most significantly enriched, suggesting a central role in RA-ILD progression. Further protein interaction analysis showed that proteins related to ECM glycoproteins and acrosome reaction were grouped within the same protein complex, hinting at complex interactions in RA-ILD development (Fig. 6C, G). When analyzing the common mutated genes in RA, RA-LC, and RA-ILD, the “detection of stimulus” pathway showed the highest significance and enrichment, suggesting its crucial role in the pathophysiological mechanisms of these diseases. The “Extracellular matrix organization” pathway ranked second, highlighting the importance of ECM in disease development. Protein interaction analysis indicated that proteins related to “detection of stimulus” were relatively independent, while those associated with “Extracellular matrix organization” were connected to other pathways, such as “Diseases associated with O-glycosylation of proteins” and “NABA ECM REGULATORS,” suggesting that ECM organization may involve multiple interacting pathways in the progression of RA, RA-LC, and RA-ILD (Fig. 6D, H).

Integration of transcriptomic and genomic data reveals differential genes at DNA and RNA levels

The integration of genomic and transcriptomic data in this study identified 223 genes with concurrent alterations at both DNA and RNA levels in LC and RA (Fig. 7A). The number of genes with concordant regulation (RA up/LC up and RA down/LC down) was 4.4 times higher than those with discordant regulation (RA down/LC up and RA up/LC down). Some of these genes were involved in common pathways, while others were unique to each disease. Functional analysis revealed both shared and distinct pathways between LC and RA (Fig. 7B). For the RA up/LC up group, the top pathways were related to viral response and immune signaling. In contrast, the RA down/LC down group showed enrichment in TCR signaling, T cell activation, and non-coding RNA metabolism. The RA down/LC up group was associated with lymphocyte and leukocyte migration, while the RA up/LC down group was primarily linked to positive regulation of programmed cell death.

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) analysis highlighted that cell activation and immune signaling pathways were central, connecting to pathways like viral response and lymphocyte migration (Fig. 7C-D). The immune signaling and viral response pathways were prominent in the RA up/LC up group, while cell activation and lymphocyte migration were key in the RA down/LC down group (Fig. 7E). Transcription factor analysis revealed that the RA down/LC down group had the most associated factors, with ZNF362 being the most significant, indicating a role in immune regulation and autoimmune diseases. NR1I2 was the most significant in the RA up/LC up group, influencing drug metabolism. The RA down/LC up group was linked to MYBL1, involved in cell proliferation, while the RA up/LC down group was associated with CEBP, a regulator of immune and inflammatory responses (Fig. 7F).

The integrated analysis also showed cell-type-specific expression patterns. The RA up/LC up and RA down/LC up groups were mainly found in lung monocytes and bone marrow neutrophils, while the RA down/LC down group was specific to bone marrow naive T cells. The RA up/LC down group was linked to DESCARTES fetal adrenal and lung lymphoid cells, overlapping with the RA down/LC down group (Fig. 7G). Further analysis in PaGenBase confirmed the RA down/LC down group’s specificity for CD56 + NK cells, while the RA up/LC up and RA down/LC up groups were specific to blood (Fig. 7H).

Correlation of laboratory parameters with combined genomic and transcriptomic findings in RA, RA-LC, and RA-IL

The analysis of laboratory parameters across RA, RA-LC, and RA-ILD patients highlights significant clinical differences that correlate with the combined genomic and transcriptomic findings. Rheumatoid factor (RF) levels were highest in RA-LC patients compared to RA and RA-ILD (Fig. 8A), potentially reflecting the increased autoimmunity and inflammation in this group. C-reactive protein (CRP), a key inflammation marker, was markedly elevated in RA-ILD patients, indicating a stronger systemic inflammatory response (Fig. 8B). Similarly, the erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was significantly higher in RA-ILD, supporting the observation of more intense inflammation in this subgroup, while RA and RA-LC patients had moderate ESR values (Fig. 8C).

The levels of rheumatoid factor A, C-reactive protein B, erythrocyte sedimentation rate C, complement C4 D, neutrophils E, and lymphocytes F across RA, RA-ILD, and RA-LC groups. The green shaded area represents the reference range for each parameter

Complement C4 levels were fairly consistent across all groups, though slightly reduced in RA-LC patients, suggesting subtle differences in immune complex activity (Fig. 8D). Neutrophil counts were elevated in both RA-ILD and RA-LC, with RA-ILD showing the highest levels, which aligns with the previously identified pathways involving neutrophil degranulation and innate immune response (Fig. 8E). Conversely, lymphocyte counts were lower in RA-ILD and RA-LC compared to RA, possibly reflecting the downregulation of T cell activation pathways identified in the genomic and transcriptomic analysis (Fig. 8F).

These clinical findings are consistent with the combined genomic and transcriptomic results. The increased neutrophil counts correspond with the upregulation of neutrophil-related pathways in RA-ILD and RA-LC. The lower lymphocyte counts in RA-ILD and RA-LC may be linked to the downregulated immune response pathways in these groups. Together, these results suggest that the interplay between genomic, transcriptomic, and clinical data provides a comprehensive understanding of the distinct pathophysiological profiles in RA, RA-LC, and RA-ILD.

Discussion

This study provides an integrative analysis of molecular and clinical features distinguishing RA, RA-LC, and RA-ILD by combining transcriptomic, genomic, and clinical data. The results suggest both shared and disease-specific molecular pathways that may be involved in disease progression and pulmonary complications in RA.

Transcriptomic profiling revealed a notable overlap in immune-related pathways between RA and LC, such as neutrophil degranulation and cytokine signaling, suggesting that shared immune dysregulation could contribute to disease comorbidity. Conversely, the identification of genes with opposite expression patterns between the two diseases points to distinct mechanisms, particularly in RNA processing and cell cycle regulation.

Genomic analysis showed that RA-LC had the greatest mutational burden, with notable involvement of the X chromosome. Enrichment in chromosome segregation and microtubule-related pathways may reflect increased genomic instability in this subgroup. In RA-ILD, the enrichment of extracellular matrix glycoprotein pathways aligns with previous reports implicating ECM remodeling in lung fibrosis.

Integration of transcriptomic and genomic data identified 223 genes with concordant alterations in RA and LC. The directionality of expression in shared gene sets (RA up/LC up and RA down/LC down) implies coordinated regulation, possibly reflecting common pathogenic features. Transcription factors such as ZNF362 and NR1I2 may play regulatory roles, but further validation is needed.

Laboratory indicators supported these findings. Elevated RF and neutrophils in RA-LC and RA-ILD, and increased CRP and ESR in RA-ILD, highlight systemic inflammation. Slightly decreased C4 in RA-LC may suggest altered immune complex dynamics.

These findings may have potential clinical implications, such as identifying candidate biomarkers like ZNF362, NR1I2, and SMAD7. Similarly, SMAD7 has been identified as an independent prognostic biomarker in lung adenocarcinoma, supporting the relevance of transcriptomic markers in LC prognosis [36]. The consistent upregulation of neutrophil-related and ECM remodeling pathways in RA-LC and RA-ILD, together with their correlation with laboratory parameters such as neutrophil count and CRP, further suggests their potential utility in diagnosis or prognosis.

This study is limited by its retrospective design and relatively small sample size. Future studies using larger, independent cohorts and experimental validation are needed to confirm these findings and explore their translational relevance.

Conclusion

In summary, this study highlights the value of integrating multi-omics and clinical data to explore the complex mechanisms underlying RA and its pulmonary complications. While common inflammatory processes are observed across RA, RA-LC, and RA-ILD, distinct molecular signatures suggest divergent pathogenic pathways. These insights offer a foundation for future biomarker discovery and personalized therapeutic strategies, although further validation is essential.
