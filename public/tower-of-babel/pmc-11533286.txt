Assembly and comparative analysis of the multichromosomal mitochondrial genome of globally endangered seagrass Halophila beccarii
Authors: Siting Chen, Yuanfang Pan, Siting Qiu, Guanglong Qiu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background Halophila beccarii is one of the oldest two generations of seagrass plants and one of the 10 species of seagrass currently at risk of extinction worldwide. Therefore, how to effectively protect the H. beccarii resources from extinction is a huge challenge. Molecular biology research can provide a scientific basis for species conservation. So far, there has been no detailed analysis of the mitochondrial genome of the genus Halophila.ResultsThe mitochondrial genome of H. beccarii was assembled into 28 circular chromosomes, ranging in length from 41,738 bp to 104,744 bp, with a total length of 1,964,072 bp and a GC content of 46.71%. It contains 39 genes, including 26 protein coding genes, 10 tRNA genes, and 3 rRNA genes. Repeat sequence analysis and prediction of RNA editing sites revealed a total of 850 dispersed repeats, 1,205 simple repeats, 61 tandem repeats, and 120 RNA editing sites. Analysis of codon usage indicates that codons ending in A/U are preferred. Gene migration between the mitochondrial genome and the chloroplast genome was observed through homologous fragment detection. In addition, Ka/Ks analysis showed that most protein coding genes in the mitochondrial genome experienced negative selection, while only the nad3 gene experienced potential positive selection in most Alismatales. Nucleotide polymorphism analysis revealed variations in each gene, with rpl10 being the most significant. In addition, comparative analysis shows that the GC content is conserved, but there are significant differences in the size and structure of mitochondrial genomes among different species of Alismatales. The phylogenetic analysis based on the mitochondrial genome reflects the exact evolutionary and taxonomic status of H. beccarii.ConclusionIn this study, we sequenced and annotated the mitochondrial genome of H. beccarii, and compared it with the mitochondrial genomes of other plants in Alismatales. Our findings enrich the mitogenome database of seagrass plants and highlight the potential for mitochondrial genes to help decipher plant evolutionary history.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-024-05765-3.

Background

Halophila belongs to the family Hydrocharitaceae, with 17 species worldwide, all of which are marine submerged plants. Widely distributed along the coasts of the Red Sea to the Indian Ocean and the Western Pacific. There are only three species in China [1], and research mainly focuses on taxonomy [2, 3], morphology [4–7], and physiology [8, 9]. At present, the global distribution area of H. beccarii does not exceed 2 × 105 hm2, mainly distributed in Asian countries and regions such as Malaysia, Thailand, Singapore, Bangladesh, India, China, Myanmar, and the Philippines [10]; The area of H. beccarii recorded in China is also less than 200 hm2 [11], mainly distributed in Guangxi, Guangdong, Taiwan, Hong Kong, Fujian, Hainan and other regions [12]. In 2000, approximately 4 hm2 was discovered in Xia Bai Ni, Yuen Long District, Hong Kong [12]; About 9 hm2 was discovered on Donghai Island in Zhanjiang, Guangdong [13]. In addition, there are reports of the distribution of H. beccarii along the coast of the South China Sea [14, 15]. The important functions of seagrass meadows include carbon sequestration, water purification, mitigating wave erosion, stabilizing the seabed, and providing habitat and ecosystem service value for marine organisms. In China, H. beccarii is a dominant species of seagrass in tropical and subtropical waters, and it often grows in nearshore waters with a large amount of nutrient input and turbid water bodies [16]. Therefore, the H. beccarii seagrass meadows have been in an accelerated decline state and are currently listed as a vulnerable species on the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) Red List of Threatened Species [17]. Therefore, how to effectively protect the H. beccarii resources from extinction is a huge challenge. Studying the molecular diversity and evolution of this species will help establish more effective conservation strategies for the future. However, due to the lack of genomic resources and unclear genetic relationships, there has been little progress in molecular biology research on H. beccarii for a long time.

Mitochondria and chloroplasts are organelles in higher plant cells that possess a semiautonomous ge

netic system, carrying relevant genetic information [18]. The nuclear genome carries the vast majority of information, but chloroplast and mitochondrial genomes are also indispensable in eukaryotes [19–22]. Since the initial endosymbiotic events, the plant mitochondrial genome has undergone rapid and significant structural changes [23]. Therefore, the mitochondrial genome of plants is approximately 100–10,000 times larger than that of animals, with a more complex structure [24]. The mitochondrial genome of terrestrial plants exhibits significant changes in genome size, ranging from 66 kb in Viscum scurruloideum [25] to 11.7 Mb in Larix sibirica [26], which can be attributed to frequent recombination of repetitive sequences and the introduction of exogenous sequences through intracellular or horizontal transfer [19]. The number of genes in the mitochondrial genome of terrestrial plants varies greatly, usually between 32 and 67 [27, 28]; however, functional genes exhibit significant conservatism [23]. In addition, structural complexity is another important feature of the plant mitochondrial genome. Although the mutation rate of plant mitochondrial genomes is lower compared to plastid and nuclear genomes, the structure and gene sequence of plant mitochondrial genomes are highly variable [28–31].

Mitochondria are powerful tools for studying species origin, genetic diversity, and phylogenetics [24, 32]. However, due to their complex structure, it is difficult to purify [19] and assemble their genomes [27], which makes researching plant mitochondrial genomes relatively challenging. Only 8 species in Alismatales have been sequenced for mitochondrial genomes. There are significant differences in genome structure and content, nucleotide substitution rate, and repeat recombination level among plant mitochondrial genomes [29, 33]. These changes in mitochondrial genomes have been observed not only among plant species but also within the same species [24, 34]. Therefore, the mitochondrial genome is a valuable source of genetic information for studying plant phylogeny and basic cellular processes [18]. In addition, the mitochondrial genome is widely used for evolutionary analysis and interspecies differentiation research, especially for constructing ancient phylogenetic relationships and relationships between closely related species, as its genetic system is usually matrilineal and relatively independent of the nucleus [35–37]. So far, there has been no detailed analysis of the mitochondrial genome of the genus Halophila, which may greatly hinder a deeper understanding of the evolution of this large family of mitochondrial genomes. The complete chloroplast genome of H. beccarii has been assembled (GenBank accession number: NC_051970.1); However, there are currently no reports on the mitochondrial or nuclear genomes of this species. Therefore, in this study, for the first time, sequencing and annotation of the mitochondrial genome of H. beccarii were performed. In addition, we analysed the genomic characteristics, repetitive sequences, RNA editing, codon preference, and migration sequences and conducted comparative genomic analysis and phylogenetic analysis with other plants in Alismatales. These results will contribute to a better understanding of the structure and function of the mitochondrial genome of H. beccarii, and provide useful molecular markers for conservation biology, population genetics, and evolutionary research of this species.

Results

Mitochondrial genome sequencing and genomic characteristics of H. beccarii

The total DNA of H. beccarii was sequenced and raw data was prepared for assembly, resulting in 47.35 G Illumina sequencing data and 20.73 G Nanopore PromethION sequencing data, with an average read length of 14,020 bp (Table S1). Then, we assembled the complete mitochondrial genome of H. beccarii. The mitochondrial genome of H. beccarii was assembled into 28 circular chromosomes, ranging in length from 41,738 bp to 104,744 bp, with a total length of 1,964,072 bp (Fig. 1). The average GC content of the mitochondrial genome of H. beccarii is 46.71%, and the GC content between chromosomes ranges from 44.95% to 47.81% (Table 1). We obtained a sequencing depth above 50 × for the short reads and 10 × for the long reads for most of the chromosomes (Table S2). The mitochondrial genome of H. beccarii encodes 39 genes, including 26 protein coding genes, 10 tRNA genes, and 3 rRNA genes (rrn18, rrn26, and rrn5) (Table 2 and Table S3). Research has shown that the mitochondrial genome of most terrestrial plants contains three rRNA genes [38]. H. beccarii is a kind of seagrass and also has three rRNA genes in the mitochondrial genome. Here, three rRNA genes, namely rrn18 (1,458 bp), rrn26 (3,051 bp), and rrn5 (116 bp), from the mitochondrial genome of H. beccarii were annotated. In addition, 9 different tRNA genes (trnM-CAT has two copies) were identified in the mitochondrial genome of H. beccarii. The length of these tRNAs ranges from 71 to 84 bp, with a total length of 741 bp.

In addition, we identified 12 Group II introns, including 4 cis-spliced introns and 8 trans-spliced introns, located in 3 cis-spliced genes (ccmFc, nad4, and nad7) and 3 trans-spliced genes (nad1, nad2, and nad5), respectively (Fig. 1, Table 2 and S5). The exons of nad1, nad2, and nad5 are encoded on different chromosomes (Fig. 1).

The nucleotide composition of the whole mitogenome (Table 4) was A (26.59%), T (26.70%), G (23.34%) and C (23.37%). The GC content of the entire mitogenome, CDS, cis-spliced intron, tRNA, rRNA and non-coding region had a GC content of 46.71%, 43.06%, 54.62%, 52.77%, 52.17% and 46.71%, respectively. Repeat sequences in the mitogenome of H. beccarii

The mitochondrial genome of H. beccarii has 850 dispersed repeats (2 types), ranging from 29 bp to 1,908 bp, accounting for 75,634 bp (4%) of the genome. Most of these repeat sequences (531 out of 850, 62.5%) are medium-sized repeat sequences (50 to 500 bp) and 7 repeats are large repeats (> 500 bp) (Table S6, Fig. 2). The abundance of both types of repeats was the highest when repeats were in the range of 40–49 bp (Fig. 3).Fig. 2Distribution of repeats in the Halophila beccarii mitogenome. SSRs are DNA fragments with a length of 1–6 bp, widely used in species research [27]. As shown in Fig. 4 and Table 5, we identified 1,205 SSRs in the mitochondrial genome of H. beccarii, and detected SSR loci including monomer, dimer, trimer, tetramer, pentamer and hexamer repeat sequences. Tetramer repeat sequences are the most abundant SSR types, accounting for 47.55% of the total identified SSRs, followed by monomer and dimer repeats, accounting for 23.73% and 19.67% of the total SSRs, respectively; the number of trimer, pentamer and hexamer repeats was the lowest. Monomer repeat sequences composed of A/T bases account for 97.20% of monomer SSRs, while dimeric repeat sequences composed of AT/AT bases account for 70.89% of dimeric SSRs. Analysis of codon usage

We analysed the codon composition of the H. beccarii mitogenome (Table 8). The number of codons in all coding genes was 10,745, and the GC1, GC2 and GC3 content and the average GC content of 3 bases (all GC) were less than 50%, indicating that the codons of the H. beccarii mitogenome were biased because of the use of both A and T bases. The effective number of codons (ENC) was 52.78, which indicated that the codon preference of the H. beccarii mitogenome was weak [32]. As shown in Table 3, most protein-coding genes use ATG as the start codon, while nad1 uses ACG as the start codon, which may be the result of RNA editing changes [27]. The utilization rates of TAA, TGA, and TAG stop codons are 42.31%, 34.62%, and 23.08%, respectively. The usage rate of TAA stop codons is the highest. Substitution rates of protein-coding genes

To evaluate the selection pressure in the evolutionary dynamics of protein-coding genes in closely related species, non-synonymous (Ka) and synonymous (Ks) substitution rates (Ka/Ks) were calculated. In the case of neutral selection, Ks = Ka or Ka/Ks = 1. If the Ka value is higher than the Ks value, it indicates positive selection (Ka/Ks > 1), while if Ks > Ka or Ka/Ks < 1, it indicates negative selection [39, 40]. Compare the 26 protein-coding genes from the mitochondrial genome of H. beccarii with the mitochondrial genomes of 6 other plants in Alismatales using Ka/Ks calculations. As shown in Fig. S1, the range of Ka/Ks for gene specific substitution rate ranges from 0.044 for nad4L gene to 3.614 for nad3 gene. The nad3 gene showed the highest average Ka/Ks value (1.472), higher than 1, indicating that positive selection occurred during the evolutionary process. However, in most species, the Ka/Ks values of most genes are less than 1, indicating that they have undergone negative selection during the evolutionary process. The average Ka/Ks value of the atp9 gene is the smallest (0.085), which is less than 1.0 in all species, indicating strong purification selection and high conservatism in the evolutionary process of Alismatales.

Nucleotide diversity

Nucleotide diversity (Pi) can be used to evaluate the variation of nucleic acid sequences in different species, and regions with high variability can serve as potential molecular markers for population genetics [41]. The nucleotide diversity of 26 protein-coding genes and 3 rRNA genes in 7 species of Alismatales plants is shown in Fig. S2. The Pi values of 29 genes range from 0.032 to 0.142, with most Pi values below 0.1. In protein-coding genes, rpl10 (Pi = 0.142) showed the highest variability, while ccmFn (Pi = 0.127) and atp8 (Pi = 0.117) also showed high variability. On the contrary, the most conservative protein-coding gene is nad4L (Pi = 0.032).

Comparison of mitogenome sizes and GC contents with those of other Alismatales plants

The size and GC content of the mitochondrial genome of H. beccarii were compared with the size and GC content of the mitochondrial genomes of eight other published plants in Alismatales (Table S8). As shown in Fig. S3, the genome size of plants in Alismatales varies greatly, ranging from 178,929 bp (Phyllospadix iwatensis) to 1,964,072 bp (H. beccarii). The size of the mitochondrial genome of H. beccarii is the largest in plants of Alismatales (Fig. S3A). However, the difference in mitochondrial genome GC content is relatively small, about 46% (Fig. S3B).

Comparison of the genome structure with other mitochondrial genomes of plants in Alismateles

As shown in Fig. S4, the homology between H. beccarii and other plants in Alismatales is very low.

Synteny analysis

Two methods were used to perform collinearity analysis of H. beccarii, Spirodela polyrhiza, Butomus umbellatus, Stratiotes aloides, Zostera marina, Zostera japonica and Phyllospadix iwatensis (Fig. S50, S6). As shown in Fig. S5, the homology between H. beccarii and other plants in Alismatales is very low. There are many homologous collinear blocks between the mitochondrial genome of H. beccarii and six other species in Alismatales (Fig. S6). The results indicate that there has been a significant genome rearrangement between the mitochondrial genome of H. beccarii and plants of Alismatales. In addition, conservation analysis of protein-coding genes in seven Alismatales species showed significant differences in the types and quantities of protein-coding genes among different species. For example, Spirodela polyrhiza had the most protein-coding genes (37), while Zostera japonica had the fewest (24), mainly due to the significant loss of ribosomal protein-coding genes. In S. aloides, rps2, rps4, rps7, rps8, rps10, rps11, rps13, rps14, rps19, rpl2, rpl5, rpl6 and rpl16 were lost. Discussion

General features of the H. beccarii mitogenome

Mitochondria provide the energy required for the life processes of plant cells [19]. The plant mitochondrial genome is a fascinating molecule that has attracted great interest due to its variation in noncoding regions and low conservation between species [42]. However, due to the relatively complex nature of plant mitochondrial genomes, sequencing and analysis of plant mitochondrial genomes are difficult [29]. With the rapid development of high-throughput sequencing and assembly technology, plant mitochondrial genome projects and high-quality mitochondrial genome assembly have grown rapidly in the past few years [27]. This study introduces the main characteristics of the mitochondrial genome of H. beccarii. The mitochondrial genome length of H. beccarii is 1,964,072 bp, consisting of 28 circular chromosomes, ranging in size from 41,738 bp to 104,744 bp.

The mitochondrial genome sizes of other species with multichromosomal structures vary greatly, ranging from 66 kb in Viscum scurruloideum [25] to 11,318 kb in Silene conica [43]. Most of these angiosperm species have two to five contigs [44], except for Cynomorium [45], Fagopyrum esculentum [46], Gastrodia elata [47], Geranium brycei [48], Lophophytum mirabile [49], Ombrophytum subterraneum [50], Rhopalocnemis phalloides [51], Silene [43, 52], Paphiopedilum micranthum [53], Cymbidium ensifolium [54] and Punica granatum [55]. The shortest mitogenome of H. beccarii is 41,738 bp, encoding part of nad1.

RNA editing occurs during the post-transcriptional process of higher plant mitochondrial and chloroplast genomes, and can alter genetic information at the mRNA level [27]. The study of RNA editing sites helps to understand the expression of plant mitochondrial genes [38]. In this study, the number of predicted RNA editing sites (120 sites) in the mitochondrial genome of H. beccarii was lower than that of other angiosperms, such as Photinia serratifolia (488) [24], Ilex metabaptista (543) [19], Cymbidium ensifolium (530) [54], and Punica granatum (466) [55]. Consistent with previous studies, the most abundant transfer type in H. beccarii is TCA = > TTA [32, 56], and the selection of editing sites shows a strong bias, with all editing sites being C-T edited, which is the most common editing type in the plant mitochondrial genome [19]. In addition, the second position of the triplet codon is most prone to RNA editing events, and most amino acids are converted to leucine after RNA editing [36]. In addition, RNA editing may lead to premature termination of the coding process in the mitochondrial genome of H. beccarii, thereby altering the function of the gene [32].

Mitogenome comparison in Alismatales

With the rapid development of sequencing technology, more and more complete plant mitochondrial genomes have been assembled and reported recently, which helps to compare and analyze the mitochondrial genome characteristics of various plants [27, 32]. We compared the mitochondrial genomes of H. beccarii with those of other plants in Alismatales to learn more about its structure and organization. The mitochondrial genome has undergone extensive rearrangement, with extremely inconsistent structures in Alismatales, which may be the main reason for the evolution and diversity of plant mitochondrial genomes [35].

Ka/Ks analysis and comparison of genomic characteristics with other plant mitochondrial genomes should help to comprehensively understand the evolution of plant mitochondria [28]. Generally speaking, consistent with previous studies [32, 36], most protein-coding genes in H. beccarii exhibit negative selection during evolution, indicating that the protein-coding genes in the mitochondrial genome are relatively conserved. However, the nad3 gene is the only gene that undergoes positive selection during the evolutionary process. Other plant mitochondrial genomes also have protein-coding genes with a Ka/Ks ratio > 1, and high gene Ka/Ks ratios play an important role in further studying gene selection and evolution of species [57].

Mitochondrial genome size and GC content are the main factors for evaluating species [56]. We compared the mitochondrial genome size and GC content of H. beccarii with other plants in Alismatales. The mitochondrial genome sizes vary greatly, but their GC content is relatively consistent in Alismatales, which supports the conclusion that GC content is highly conserved in the evolutionary process of higher plants [36]. The size of the mitochondrial genome of H. beccarii is the largest in plants of Alismatales (Fig. S3).

Compared with the mitochondrial genomes of other plants in Alismatales, the mitochondrial genome of H. beccarii is conserved in terms of gene quantity and gene content, encoding 26 of the 43 core and variable genes of plant mitochondria, except for sdh3, sdh4, rps1, rps2, rps3, rps4, rps7, rps8, rps10, rps11, rps13, rps14, rps19, rpl2, rpl5, rpl6 and rpl16, which were lost from the mitochondrial genome of H. beccarii (Fig. S7). Sdh3 and sdh4 encode succinate dehydrogenase, which are repeatedly lost in the mitochondrial genome of angiosperms [58]. Although sdh3 and sdh4 are lost from the mitochondrial genome in many Alismatales plants, sdh4 remains in the mitochondrial genome of S. polyrhiza.

Patterns of codon use bias

Codons play a crucial role in the transformation of genetic information [19]. There are significant differences in the utilization rate of genomic codons among different species and organisms, which is believed to be the result of a relative balance gradually formed through long-term evolutionary selection within cells [59]. In the mitochondrial genome of H. beccarii, all protein-coding genes are typical ATG start codons. The results of codon position analysis indicate that the codon preference in the mitochondrial genome of H. beccarii is weak, with RSCU > 1 for 32 codons, most of which end with A/U bases. The results indicate that there is a strong A or U bias in the third position of the codon in the protein-coding genes of the mitochondrial genome of H. beccarii; This is usually observed in the mitochondrial genome of plants [32].

Plasmid origin sequences in the mitochondrial genome of H. beccarii

The evolution of mitochondrial genomes involves many structural rearrangements and gene transfer events [44]. An important feature of the evolution of plant mitochondrial genomes is gene transfer between mitochondrial and chloroplast genomes [27, 60, 61]. Therefore, tracking the inter genomic transfer between organelle genomes is crucial for understanding the evolution of plant mitochondrial genomes [23]. In the process of mitochondrial evolution, the length and sequence similarity of migration fragments vary in higher plants [62]. The origin sequence of plastids accounts for 0.1% to 10.3% of the mitochondrial genome [63]. For example, the plastid derived sequences in Hibiscus cannabinus [64] account for 1.98% (11,281 bp), kiwifruit [65] ranges from 1.16% (8,937 bp) to 4.05% (37,483 bp), Citrullus lanatus [66] accounts for 6% (23 kb), vitis [67] accounts for 8.8%, Vitex rotundifolia [68] accounts for 2.36%, Bupleurum chinense [32] accounts for 2.56% and Ipomoea batatas [37] accounts for 7.35%. In addition, the sequences obtained from plastids cover less than 5% of the mitochondrial genomes of most angiosperms [63]. 13 of the 28 chromosomes in the genome of H. beccarii contain plastid derived fragments, accounting for 0.71% (14,004 bp) of the mitochondrial genome of H. beccarii (Fig. 7, Table 9). The plastid derived sequences in Chr11 reached 6,982 bp (9% of Chr11) (Table 9). These characteristics indicate that plastid derived sequences originate from multiple transfer events, and after their intracellular transfer, these sequences undergo multiple rounds of recombination. In addition, the most common transfer of tRNA genes is from the chloroplast genome of angiosperms to the mitochondrial genome [36, 60]. Among the 28 chromosomes in the mitochondrial genome of H. beccarii, a total of 13 contain plastome-origin sequences, encoding 4 intact tRNAs (Tables 2 and 9).

Phylogenetic inference

Due to its many advantages, including maternal inheritance, rapid evolution, low recombination rate, and many available molecular markers, the mitochondrial genome has become a useful tool for studying taxonomy, phylogeny, evolution, population genetics, and comparative genomics [35, 37]. Alismatales are mostly aquatic or swampy plants and are an important representative group of aquatic monocotyledonous plants [69]. This order is one of the earliest differentiated lineages in the roots of existing monocotyledonous plant systems, representing the oldest lineage of monocotyledonous plants and of great value for studying the origin and early evolution of monocotyledonous plants [70]. Due to adaptation to the environment, various groups of Alismatales exhibit a large number of convergent phenomena in their adult morphology. The traditional method of analyzing the systematic evolution of Alismatales using a large number of common derivatives is more prone to biases caused by the occurrence of syntax and polyphyletic groups, leading to research results and viewpoints mainly based on comparative morphology. In early classification systems, there were significant differences in the definition and division of the category and coverage of this purpose, and the classification of taxonomic units at the genus level of each family under the order was also broad and narrow [70]. Subsequently, Alismatales was redefined based on molecular evidence [70]. The redefined Alismatales is a good monophyletic group, consisting of 14 families and 52 genera. Although molecular systematics research on Alismatales has been conducted for many years, the phylogenetic relationships between some important taxa have not yet been clarified. The mitochondrial genome helps to understand the evolutionary relationships between species in Alismatales. In this study, based on information obtained from the mitochondrial genome, a phylogenetic analysis was conducted on the mitochondrial genomes of H. beccarii and eight published plants of Alismatales. The evolutionary relationships between these species are consistent with the topological structure of the phylogenetic tree, indicating the consistency between traditional taxonomy and molecular taxonomy. This suggests the possibility of using information obtained from mitochondrial genomes in plant phylogenetic research. In addition, these results will lay the foundation for further determining the evolutionary relationship of Alismatales. However, due to the lack of sufficient representative mitochondrial genomes, it is necessary to sequence more mitochondrial genomes of Alismatales in order to better study the phylogenetic and evolutionary biology within this large family.

The size and structural changes of plant mitochondrial genomes are significant, but functional genes remain conserved [71]. Nucleotide diversity (pi) reveals changes in nucleotide sequences of different species (Fig. S2). Our data shows that the pi value of the rpl10 gene region is the highest among these regions.

Conclusions

In this study, we sequenced the mitochondrial genome of Halophila for the first time. Sequencing, assembly, and annotation were performed on the mitochondrial genome of H. beccarii, and a comprehensive analysis was conducted on the DNA and amino acid sequences of the annotated genes. The mitochondrial genome length of H. beccarii is 1,964,072 bp, consisting of 28 circular subgenomes, ranging in size from 41,738 bp to 104,744 bp. In addition, 39 genes were annotated in the mitochondrial genome, including 26 protein-coding genes, 10 tRNA genes, and 3 rRNA genes. Then, repeats, RNA editing sites, homologous fragments between mitochondria and chloroplasts, codon usage bias patterns, and selection pressures were analyzed. In addition, Ka/Ks analysis, nucleotide polymorphism analysis, and comparative analysis of genomic characteristics were also conducted to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the mitochondrial genome evolution of Alismatales. In addition, based on the mitochondrial genomes of this species and 8 other plants in Alismatales, the evolutionary status of H. beccarii was validated through phylogenetic analysis. This study provides a wealth of information about the mitochondrial genome of H. beccarii, which will contribute to future research on genetic variation, systematic evolution, and conservation of H. beccarii. Therefore, these results help us lay a solid foundation for the protection of this seagrass.

Methods

Plant materials, mitochondrial DNA isolation and genome sequencing

The samples were collected at Shatian, Beihai, Guangxi, China (21° 28′ 51.56′′ N, 109° 42′ 33.64′′ E) on March 21, 2024, in the intertidal zone, and identified by Dr. Guanglong Qiu from Guangxi Academy of Marine Sciences (Guangxi Mangrove Research Center). The voucher specimen is stored in the Plant Specimen Museum of Guangxi Academy of Marine Sciences (Guangxi Mangrove Research Center), with voucher number GMRCH0015. The collection of H. beccarii was permitted by the local government. The use of plants in this study complies with all local, national, or international guidelines and legislation related to plant research. The leaves were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and then stored at -80 °C for DNA extraction. Total genomic DNA for next-generation sequencing was extracted using a universal plant DNA kit (Genepioneer Biotechnologies, Nanjing, China). Total genomic DNA for long-read sequencing was extracted using the CTAB method. DNA purity was detected by 0.7% agarose gel. Then, the qualified library was sequenced and assembled by applying second- and third-generation sequencing platforms by Genepioneer Biotechnologies (Nanjing, China). Qualified libraries were sequenced using Illumina Novaseq6000 platform for next-generation sequencing. The Oxford Nanopore PromethION sequencer was used for real-time single-molecule sequencing. In order to obtain high-quality mitochondrial genomes of H. beccarii, fastp (version 0.23.4, https://github.com/OpenGene/fastp) software was used to filter raw data, remove sequencing connectors and primer sequences from reads, filter reads with an average quality value less than Q5, filter reads with a quantity (N) greater than 5, and obtain high-quality reads for next-generation sequencing. Filtlong (v0.2.1, https://link.zhihu.com/?target=https%3A//github.com/rrwick/Filtlong) software was used for filtering third-generation sequencing data and Perl scripts were used for statistics.

Assembly and annotation of the mitogenome

Plant mitochondrial genes are very conserved. Taking advantage of this feature, the third-generation alignment software Minimap2 (v2.1) [72] was used to compare the original third-generation data with the reference gene sequence (plant mitochondrial core gene), and sequences with a length greater than 50 bp were selected as candidate sequences for alignment. Sequences with more alignment genes (one sequencing sequence contains multiple core genes) and higher alignment quality (covering more complete core genes) were selected as seed sequences. The sequences with overlap greater than 1 kb and similarity greater than 70% were screened and obtained all third-generation sequencing data of mitochondrial genome. Then, the third-generation assembly software Canu [73] was used to calibrate the obtained third-generation data, and Bowtie2 (v2.3.5.1) [74] was used to align the second-generation data onto the corrected sequence. Then the default parameters of Unicycler (v0.4.8) were used to compare the second-generation data and the corrected third-generation data for concatenation. The corrected third-generation sequencing data was compared to the contig obtained in the second step of Unicycler using minimap2, and the branch direction was manually determined to obtain the final assembly result.

The following steps were used for mitochondrial genome annotation: BLAST was used to compare the encoded proteins and rRNAs with published plant mitochondrial sequences, and further manual adjustments were made based on nearby species. tRNAscanSE [75] (http://lowelab.ucsc.edu/tRNAscan-SE/) was used to annotate tRNAs. ORFs were annotated with OpenReading Frame Finder (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gorf/gorf.html). RNA editing sites were predicted using PmtREP (http://cloud.genepioneer.com:9929/#/tool/alltool/detail/336). OGDRAW (https://chlorobox.mpimp-golm.mpg.de/OGDraw.html) was used to create mitochondrial genome maps.

Analysis of repeats

Three types of repeats (simple sequence repeat, tandem and dispersed) were detected in the mitochondrial genome. MISA (v1.0) [76] was used for simple sequence repeat analysis. Tandem repeats were identified using Tandem repeats finder software (v4.09, parameters: 2 7 7 80 10 50 2000 -f -d -m) [77]. BLASTN (v2.10.1, parameter:—word_size 7, evaluate 1e-5, remove redundancy, remove tandem repeats) was used to detect dispersed repeats. Circos (v0.69–5) was used to visualize these repeats.

Codon usage analysis

We used a self-coding Perl script to analyze the codon composition of the mitochondrial genome of H. beccarii, in order to screen for unique CDS and determine the number of codons per gene, GC content (GC1, GC2, and GC3), average GC content of three bases (GC_all), effective number of codons (ENC), and RSCU of synonymous codons.

Homologous fragment analysis

The chloroplast genome sequence of H. beccarii (NC_051970.1) was downloaded from the NCBI genome resource database. Homologous fragments between the mitochondrial genome and the chloroplast genome were identified using BLAST software. Screening criteria were set as E-value ≤ 1e−5, default for other parameters. The results were visualized using Circos (v0.69–5).

Phylogenetic tree construction

In order to obtain the phylogenetic position of H. beccarii, 8 plant mitochondrial genomes (Table S9) were downloaded from the NCBI genome resource database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Among these species, the complete mitochondrial genome sequences of these species can not only be analyzed in NCBI but also have clear positions in taxonomy and are widely used. Shared CDSs from 9 species in Alismatales were compared using MAFFT (v7.427,—-automatic mode) software [78]. The aligned sequences were connected end-to-end and trimmed with trimAl (v1.4. rev15, parameter:—gt 0.7), and jmodeltest-2.1.10 software was used to predict the trimmed model and determine that it is a GTR type. Then RAxML v8.2.10 (https://cme.h-its.org/exelixis/software.html) software was used to select the GTRGAMMA model with bootstrap = 1000, to construct a maximum likelihood evolutionary tree.

Analysis of synonymous and nonsynonymous substitution ratio and pi

Six plants of Alismatales (Table S9) were used as references and the Ka/Ks of protein-coding genes in the mitochondrial genome of H. beccarii were analyzed. We grouped these species pairwise and extracted homologous gene pairs, and then used Mafft v7.427 (https://mafft.cbrc.jp/alignment/software/) to align homologous gene pairs. KaKs Calculator v2.0 (https://sourceforge.net/projects/kakscalculator2/) software [40] was used to calculate the Ka and Ks values for each gene pair, and MLWL calculation method was selected. Finally, the Ka/Ks values for each pair of genes were calculated and a box plot was drawn. Mafft software (v7.427, – auto mode) was used to perform global alignment of homologous gene sequences from different species, and the pi value of each gene was calculated using dnasp5 [79].

Comparative analysis of the mitogenome structure

The mitochondrial genome of H. beccarii was used as a reference and was compared with the mitochondrial genomes of six other plants in Alismatales (Table S9). CGView software (default parameters) [80] (http://stothard.afns.ualberta.ca/cgview_server/) was used to compare mitogenome structures for Alismatales. Nucmer (v4.0.0 beta2, – maxmatch parameter) software was used to compare the mitochondrial genomes of H. beccarii and other plants in Alismatales, and a dot-plot was generated.
