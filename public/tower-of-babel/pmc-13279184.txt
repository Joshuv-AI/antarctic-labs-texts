The MV Hondius Warning: Hantavirus Surveillance Blind Spots
Authors: Junwen Guo, Jan C. Semenza
Published: 2026

For years, hantavirus infections in Europe were regarded as a relatively minor public health concern, largely because the dominant strain circulating in Northern and Western Europe, the Puumala virus (PUUV), is not transmitted human-to human, and is typically associated with mild disease.1,2 This perception changed dramatically following the April 2026 outbreak aboard the cruise ship MV Hondius, which propelled hantaviruses into the global spotlight.3 The deaths of passengers infected with the South American Andes virus (ANDV)—one of the few hantaviruses known to spread between humans4,5—triggered alarm in a world still shaped by the collective memory of the COVID-19 pandemic. Despite their differing clinical severity, all hantaviruses share a critical epidemiological bottleneck: human infection requires an initial zoonotic spillover event. The MV Hondius outbreak almost certainly began with such rodent-to-human transmission.3 For this spillover to occur, a convergence of ecological, climatic, and behavioral conditions was necessary for passengers to be exposed to infected reservoirs or fomites. We have shown that such an initial spillover event remains the decisive stage in hantavirus emergence and is highly context dependent.10 Shifting climatic, ecological and biodiversity conditions, specifically those altering rodent population dynamics, viral prevalence, and human–rodent interfaces, determine spillover risk.9,10

Ecological dimensions of hantavirus emergence are often overlooked, yet climate extremes and shifting biodiversity are increasingly important determinants of transmission.9,10 Europe's longstanding reputation as a low-risk region for hantavirus infection has also contributed to substantial underreporting.11 Mild or atypical infections often remain undiagnosed because clinicians do not routinely suspect hantavirus disease outside recognized endemic areas. Our pan-European assessment, integrating climatic, environmental, biodiversity, and socioeconomic variables, revealed that regions at elevated risk for hantavirus transmission extend far beyond areas with confirmed human cases (Fig. 1B). This blind spot strongly suggests that current surveillance systems fail to detect a substantial proportion of infections.

The ecology of hantaviruses further underscores the importance of reservoir species in constraining viral spread. In Europe, PUUV is primarily carried by the bank vole (Clethrionomys glareolus), whereas ANDV circulates in South America in the long-tailed pygmy rice rat (Oligoryzomys longicaudatus).2 Hantaviruses are generally characterized by a high degree of host specificity, which historically has acted as a natural geographic barrier to viral expansion. Consequently, the long-term persistence of ANDV in Europe following imported cases linked to the MV Hondius outbreak is theoretically limited by the absence of its primary natural reservoir.

Nevertheless, an important and unresolved question is whether ANDV possesses the capacity to adapt to European rodent species through viral evolution, viral dispersion aided by population mobility, and host-switching. Despite the high degree of genomic stability and low mutation rate of this RNV virus, existing evidence suggests that ANDV can infect a broader range of hosts than previously assumed. In the Americas, ANDV has been detected in multiple wild rodent species, including Abrothrix longipilis, Loxodontomys micropus, Rattus rattus, the black rat or ship rat, a species already widespread across Europe, as well as in other mammals such as bats.12, 13, 14 Earlier laboratory infection study has demonstrated successful (at least temporary) cross-species infection in non-native rodents, including the North American deer mouse (Peromyscus maniculatus).15 While these findings do not demonstrate that European rodents can serve as competent reservoirs or amplifying hosts necessary for ANDV transmission, they indicate that host barriers may not be absolute. At present, there is no published experimental or ecologic evidence indicating that native European rodents can sustain ANDV transmission. However, this largely reflects another critical surveillance blind spot, as rodents are rarely monitored for allochthonous or non-native pathogens. Qualified risk assessments depend on such monitoring data.

Even so, the possibility — however remote — of ANDV adapting to new reservoir hosts in Europe should not be dismissed. The unprecedented media attention generated by the MV Hondius outbreak provides an opportunity to strengthen hantavirus surveillance with a broader One Health framework, with ecological and reservoir monitoring, for the detection of both endemic hantaviruses and newly emerging strains. More broadly, the outbreak serves as a reminder that zoonotic diseases cannot be understood solely through the lens of clinical medicine. They are fundamentally ecological diseases, shaped by climate change, biodiversity loss, environmental disruption, and human behavior. Only integrated surveillance of human cases with wildlife and environmental monitoring can shed light on these surveillance blind spots in Europe and beyond.
