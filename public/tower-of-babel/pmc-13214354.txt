An unusual case of diffuse glabrous skin dermatophytosis caused by Trichophyton tonsurans in a limited-resource setting: A case report
Authors: Isidore W. Yerbanga, Fagnima Traoré, Abdoulaye Sawadogo, André Nagalo, Djima Fahriane Dora Koulibary, Seydou Nakanabo Diallo, Felix Bongomin, Angélique Bonnet, Jean-Pierre Gangneux, Sanata Bamba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Trichophyton tonsurans is an anthropophilic dermatophyte increasingly reported in several regions worldwide, reflecting its expanding epidemiological distribution and growing public health importance. This fungal agent is associated with outbreaks that are difficult to control. We report here the first published case in Burkina Faso of diffuse glabrous skin dermatophytosis caused by T. tonsurans in a socioeconomically disadvantaged patient prescribed a treatment regimen consisting of miconazole cream and oral terbinafine, highlighting the challenges of post-treatment follow-up.

Introduction

Trichophyton tonsurans is an anthropophilic dermatophyte recognized as an etiological agent of dermatophytosis [1]. Recent data highlight a considerable increase in the frequency of dermatophytosis due to T. tonsurans worldwide [2]. In the United States of America, T. tonsurans has been reported as the leading cause of pediatric tinea capitis, accounting for 91.8% of cases [3]. This fungus has been associated with outbreaks in various countries, including the United Kingdom and Japan [4], [5]. The burden of T. tonsurans infections remains unclear but the available data indicate a marked increase in dermatophytosis caused by T. tonsurans in America, Europe, and Asia [2], [4], [5], [6], [7]. In Africa, the prevalence of tinea capitis caused by T. tonsurans varies considerably between countries [8], with some countries, such as Ivory Coast (0.68%) [9] and Gabon (4.1%) [10], reporting a low prevalence. By contrast, a moderate prevalence has been reported in Nigeria (13.2%) [11] and very high prevalences have been reported in Kenya (77.8%) [12] and Madagascar (88.2%) [13]. However, cases of glabrous skin dermatophytosis due to this fungus are rare. In Ethiopia and Nigeria, T. tonsurans accounted for 11.23% and 9.5% of tinea corporis cases, respectively [14], [15]. We describe here a case of diffuse glabrous skin dermatophytosis caused by T. tonsurans, focusing on diagnostic and therapeutic challenges.

Case

A 34-year-old male married, monogamous farmer, father of six children, residing in Seguenega (Yaadga Region), with no known relevant personal or family medical history, consulted at the “Centre Hospitalier Universitaire Régional de Ouahigouya” (CHUR-OHG) for multiple pruritic, erythematous-squamous, and centrifugal lesions. The patient said that there were no similar cases in his family. The lesions began in the groin area about four months before the consultation at CHUR-OHG, gradually spreading to the thighs and buttocks. The patient attempted self-medication with an unspecified treatment, which was unsuccessful. He also reported using traditional medicine on the spreading lesions, but the specific remedies could not be identified, and no improvement was observed. Given the diffuse spread of the lesions over a large area of glabrous skin, the patient decided to seek specialist care at CHUR-OHG.

The clinical examination on admission revealed a patient in good general condition. His temperature was 36.7°C, blood pressure 123/68 mmHg, heart rate 74 beats/minute, weight 73 kg, height 1.83 m, and body mass index (BMI) 21.8. Dermatological examination showed multiple erythematous-squamous lesions of various sizes, ranging from 5 cm to 15 cm in diameter, with well-defined, circumscribed borders. The lesions primarily affected the bilateral groin area, buttocks, inner thighs, and extended toward the perineal area. Other lesions were also identified in the left axillary area and right thoracoabdominal area (Fig. 1). The nails and hair were unremarkable. Laboratory results were as follows: red blood cells = 4.38 × 10⁶/mm³, hemoglobin = 11.4 g/dL, platelets = 232 × 10³/mm³, white blood cells = 6.75 × 10³/mm³, eosinophils = 0.65 × 10³/mm³. A serological test for HIV was negative. It was not possible to perform other laboratory tests due to a lack of funding. Direct microscopic examination of scales treated with 10% potassium hydroxide (KOH) revealed regular, hyaline, and septate hyphae. Culture on Sabouraud dextrose agar (LIOFILCHEM®, Italy) at 30°C for two weeks resulted in colonies with a yellow, powdery surface on the obverse side and yellow-brown pigmentation on the reverse side (Fig. 2). Microscopic observations were performed by gently placing a piece of transparent adhesive tape (Scotch®) on the colony and transferring it onto a drop of lactophenol blue on a glass slide. Fungal structures were observed under a light microscope, with the 10 × and 40 × objectives. Microscopic examination revealed septate, hyaline hyphae producing polymorphic microconidia and short conidiophores arising perpendicularly from the parent hyphae. The microconidia were teardrop-shaped, club-shaped, elongated, or, in some cases, rounded (balloon-shaped). Intercalary and terminal chlamydoconidia were occasionally observed. No macroconidia were seen (Fig. 3). The patient was diagnosed with diffuse glabrous skin dermatophytosis due to T. tonsurans. Miconazole (2% cream applied twice daily for 6 weeks) and terbinafine (250 mg once daily for 6 weeks) were prescribed and a follow-up appointment was scheduled for two weeks later. However, the patient did not attend the appointment and attempts to contact him were unsuccessful. The patient was, thus, lost to follow-up.

Discussion

Trichophyton tonsurans, which originates from South-East Asia and Australia, is currently recognized as an emerging cause of fungal infections [16]. We report here the first case in Burkina Faso of diffuse glabrous skin dermatophytosis caused by the anthropophilic fungus T. tonsurans. In Burkina Faso, glabrous skin dermatophytosis is usually diagnosed by clinical examination [17], [18], [19]. This approach is consistent with published reports [20] and has the advantage of being inexpensive, while allowing for the early initiation of patient care. However, the accurate identification of dermatophytes to species level is essential to determine the most appropriate antifungal treatment [21]. It also provides information about the probable source of infection (humans for anthropophilic species, animals for zoophilic species, and soil for telluric species), making it possible to educate patients on how to avoid reinfection and to improve outbreak management [21]. A knowledge of the profile of fungal agents responsible for dermatophytosis in a particular area makes it possible to implement empiric treatment protocol for these skin infections. In Burkina Faso, the only published study reporting a case of dermatophytosis due to T. tonsurans to date is that by Guiguemde et al. [22]. This situation may be explained by the clinical examination-based approach to dermatophytosis diagnosis in this country, but also by the presence of many asymptomatic carriers of T. tonsurans infection [16]. This high frequency of asymptomatic carriage highlights the need to conduct community investigations to detect and treat these cases, to break the chain of contamination. Indeed, dermatophytosis, although frequently benign, can promote bacterial superinfections and, in immunocompromised individuals, it can result in tissue invasions underlying disseminated dermatophytosis and invasive dermatitis [20]. The initial lesion reported by the patient occurred in the groin area. This location is consistent with published data reporting that intertriginous areas, including the groin, axillae and interweb spaces, are more susceptible to dermatophyte infections due to the greater sweating, maceration, and alkaline pH of these areas [23]. Some underlying conditions, such as lymphoma, leukemia, myelodysplastic syndrome and diabetes mellitus, may promote the spread of lesions [24]. However, given the financial constraints, with the exception of a serological test for HIV, which was negative, no tests were performed to identify underlying predisposing factors. Antifungal treatment was administered based on the results of the direct examination, which revealed hyaline septate hyphae. This examination, particularly in a limited-resource settings, is a key element for confirming the fungal origin of the lesions and ensuring the rapid initiation of antifungal treatment. Indeed, culture, although considered the gold standard for dermatophytosis diagnosis, is time-consuming, with the fungus identifiable only after several days [25]. However, direct examination cannot identify the species, which constitutes a limitation for adequate patient management. Following the antifungal drug prescription, a follow-up appointment was scheduled for 14 days after discharge. The patient failed to attend the scheduled follow-up visit and all attempts to contact him were unsuccessful. The current security constraints in the northern region of Burkina Faso, which limit the mobility of the population, together with the poor state of the health system and the absence of specific resources dedicated to the tracing of such cases, prevented the identification of alternative solutions for locating the patient and ensuring appropriate follow-up. In this context, as reported in previous studies, it was difficult to assess clinical cure or treatment failure [26]. As T. tonsurans is an anthropophilic fungus transmitted between humans, this patient may represent a potential source of spread in the community, highlighting the need for epidemiological investigations in the Seguenega area to identify and manage potential cases. Finally, due to its high transmissibility, an intensification of hygiene measures is crucial to prevent the contamination of healthcare and laboratory personnel.

One limitation of this study is the lack of use of new techniques, such as mass spectrometry and molecular biology methods, for species identification [27]. These methods are increasingly being integrated into routine medical mycology practice in high-income countries, but they remain largely inaccessible in many low-resource countries, including Burkina Faso.

Conclusion

This case report is the first to describe T. tonsurans as the causal agent of diffuse glabrous skin dermatophytosis in Burkina Faso. It highlights the challenges of managing such cases in a limited-resource setting and underscores the need for further epidemiological studies to determine the prevalence and possible circulation of this pathogen in the region.
