Clinical and functional outcomes of subacromial spacer implantation and superior capsular reconstruction for massive irreparable rotator cuff tears: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Naeemeh Kalali, Mohammad H. Ebrahimzadeh, Mohsen Dehghani, Ali Moradi, Mahdieh Sahebi, Nafiseh Jirofti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVarious surgical techniques are available for treating Massive and Irreparable Rotator Cuff Tears (MIRCTs). However, there is no agreement on which approach is the most effective. We have conducted a systematic review and analyzed the clinical outcomes of two advanced procedures: Subacromial Spacer Implantation (SSI) and Arthroscopic Superior Capsular Reconstruction (ASCR) for managing MIRCTs.MethodsA comprehensive search using MeSH and keywords was performed on Medline/ PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Scopus, and Cochrane Database up to August 2023 for observational and pre-post clinical trials regarding the study outcomes. American Shoulder and Elbow Score (ASES), Constant score (CS), Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), University of California-Los Angeles (UCLA), Simple Shoulder Test (SST), Subjective Shoulder Value (SSV), Japanese Orthopaedic Association (JOA), and Range of Motion (ROM) were considered as clinical and functional outcomes. The retrieved studies were assessed for methodological quality. Random-effects meta-analysis was used to estimate the pooled Weighted Mean Difference (WMD) with a 95% confidence interval (CI), considering the clinical and methodological heterogeneity across studies. We also performed subgroup meta-analysis and sensitivity analysis.ResultsFindings of the meta-analysis on 35 pre-post clinical trials, mainly fair to good quality, showed that clinical scores significantly improve after ASCR, ASES (WMD: 40.76; 95% CI: 35.9 to 45.6), CS (WMD: 33.34; 95% CI: 24.2 to 42.3), and VAS (WMD: -4.30; 95% CI: -4.8 to -3.7). The ROM reached 45.83 (95% CI: 33.6 to 58) for abduction, 42.8 (95% CI: 28.6 to 56.9) for flexion, 0.13 (95% CI: -1.5 to 1.8) for internal rotation, and 11.5 (95% CI: 7.5 to 15.4) for external rotation after surgery. The acromiohumeral distance (AHD) increased by 2.69 mm (95% CI: 1.9 to 3.4 mm). We found no publication bias, and the meta-analysis findings were robust in sensitivity analysis.ConclusionsTwo advanced procedures, SSI and ASCR, for patients with MIRCTs showed significantly improved clinical and functional outcomes. It is difficult to directly compare the effectiveness of these two procedures. More long-term, large-scale, and high-quality trials are needed to confirm their effectiveness.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-025-09384-3.

Background

Rotator cuff tear (RCT) is the most common shoulder injury that can lead to shoulder dysfunction, despite the technical improvement, the failure rate of Massive and Irreparable Rotator Cuff Tears (MIRCTs) was still demonstrated to be high [1]. Various surgical techniques are available to treat RCTs, depending on the size of the tear, the patient’s characteristics, and the surgeon’s experience. Massive rotator cuff tears are particularly challenging due to poor tissue quality, bone quality, and muscle atrophy, often leading to poor outcomes [2]. Surgical options for treating RCTs include arthroscopic debridement, partial repair, graft interposition, reverse shoulder arthroplasty, subacromial spacer implantation (SSI), and superior capsular reconstruction (SCR) [3]. SCR is an arthroscopic procedure developed for patients with "MIRCTs". This procedure can improve function and decrease pain in certain individuals with chronic tears who have not found relief through non-operative management. SCR is an advanced procedure that helps retain the static stability of the shoulder joint and prevents cuff tear arthropathy [4]. SSI is typically performed arthroscopically through the lateral shoulder. This technique avoids superior migration by placing biodegradable spacers beneath the acromion and is used when the rotator cuff cannot be repaired, allowing for improved biomechanics of the shoulder. The goal of balloon implantation is to provide a relatively quick, lower-risk procedure to relieve pain and restore the biomechanics of the glenohumeral joint [5]. Advanced treatment options, such as SCR and SSI, have been recommended to give a passive biological constraint to superior humeral head migration and improve functional and clinical outcomes [6]; however, the findings are controversial, and no consensus has been made [7, 8].

We conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to compare the outcomes of SCR and SSI, with a comprehensive assessment for patients with MIRCTs, along with the critical appraisal of the literature and identification of the possible sources of heterogeneity between studies. We analyzed shoulder scores such as American Shoulder and Elbow Score (ASES), Constant score (CS), Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), University of California-Los Angeles (UCLA), Simple Shoulder Test (SST), Subjective Shoulder Value (SSV), Japanese Orthopaedic Association (JOA), and Range of Motion (ROM) following the Arthroscopic Superior Capsular Reconstruction (ASCR) and SSI [9–22]. Previous systematic reviews examined the exclusive use of two surgical methods, SCR and SSI, which did not reach meta-analysis [23]. This systematic review and meta-analysis is the first study to evaluate comprehensively the clinical and functional outcomes of two advanced procedures, SCR and SSI. Our goal was to provide valuable information to help clinicians make informed decisions and optimize patient management.

Methods

The review protocol was registered on the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) database with ID CRD42023460060, and conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines and PRISMA checklist (see Supplementary Material 3) [24].

Search strategy

We searched articles in the following online databases: PubMed, Embase, Cochrane, Web of Science, and Scopus until August 2023 by two independent researchers (MS and NK) using the MeSH terms (“rotator cuff” OR “rotator cuff injuries” OR “shoulder injur*” OR “irreparable rotator cuff tear*” OR “massive rotator cuff tear*”) AND (“subacromial spacer” OR “balloon spacer” OR “subacromial balloon spacer” OR “subacromial spacer implantation” OR “subacromial balloon spacer implantation” OR “superior capsule reconstruction” OR “Superior capsule” OR “capsule reconstruction”). Conflicts were resolved by consensus. Pre-post clinical trials in English were collected, and gray literature sources, such as meeting abstracts, as well as the reference lists of publications, were reviewed for additional relevant studies. Duplicate publications were removed.

Eligibility criteria

This review focused on pre-post clinical trials published in English that met the PICOT (participant, intervention, comparison, outcome, time) requirements. The studies involved adult patients who had MIRCTs and underwent ASCR or SSI. For two-arm clinical trials in which one arm incorporated an ASCR or SSI procedures, we included only the data from that specific arm in our review. The observational and case-series studies that reported pre-post clinical and functional data, including ASES, CS, VAS, Q-DASH, UCLA, SST, SSV, JOA, and ROM, with various follow-up times were included. We excluded studies with incomplete data or irrelevant outcomes, reviews, letters to the editor, editorial commentary, teche snical notes, animal studies, and casereports. When multiple publications originated from the same population or patient cohort, only data from the most recent report with the largest sample size and long-term follow-up were included.

Data extraction

We carefully extracted data from all eligible studies using a pre-designed Excel form. The form includes information such as the first author, country, year, study design, sample size, demographic characteristics (mean age and sex), pre-post functional outcomes, details of the interventions, and follow-up durations (Tables 1 and 2). Studies had to report clinical outcomes as mean with standard deviation to be eligible for quantitative analysis. To ensure standardization and minimize errors, all patients in a group must complete the minimum follow-up period to be included in the analysis. The data were extracted independently by two reviewers (NK and NJ), and the observed differences were resolved by consensus of review team members.

Quality assessment

The present systematic review utilized the quality assessment tool to evaluate the methodological quality of included pre-post studies with no control group [60]. This tool was developed by the National Institutes of Health (NIH) and evaluates methodological quality and risk of bias through a 12-question scale. Responses to each question were categorized as yes (Y), no (N), not applicable (NA), or not reported [61]. Based on the results, each study’s quality was categorized as poor (score less than 5), fair (score of 5 to 8), or good (score of more than 8). Two reviewers, MS and NK, assessed the risk of bias in the studies, and the third reviewer, MD, verified their findings.

Statistical analysis

Our meta-analysis examined the clinical outcomes, including ASES, CS, VAS, Q-DASH, UCLA, SST, SSV, JOA scores, and ROM in patients with MIRCTs who undergo ASCR or SSI. We employed the reported mean, standard deviation, and sample size from each study and used forest plots to show heterogeneity and calculate the pooled WMD with corresponding 95% CI. Due to clinical and methodological heterogeneity, a random-effects meta-analysis was conducted to account for the heterogeneity of the study populations [62]. Pooled estimates with their corresponding 95% CIs were calculated using inverse-variance weights methods. Cochran’s Q statistic was also used to analyze the statistical significance of heterogeneity. The heterogeneity across studies was also assessed using the I2 statistic quantitatively [63]. To find out which study (if any) had the most impact on the heterogeneity and evaluate the robustness of summary findings, sensitivity analysis was performed by successively removing a particular study. Due to the considerable heterogeneity observed, subgroup analysis were conducted based on follow-up time, geographical area (continent), surgical techniques, graft type (Inspace, allograft, autograft, allograft + autograft, autograft + synthetic scaffold), sample size (< 30, and ≥ 30), and study quality (Poor, Fair, Good). Additionally, we rigorously examined potential publication bias through meticulous visual inspection of the funnel plots, and Begg’s and Egger’s statistical tests to ensure result credibility [64]. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and the significance level was less than 0.05 for all except the heterogeneity test. Stata version 17.0 (Stata Corp., College Station, TX) was used for all statistical analyses.

Results

Study characteristics

We conducted a comprehensive search and found a total of 2467 records. After removing duplicates (n = 1380), we screened 1087 records, of which 530 were excluded based on their titles and abstracts. We then screened the full text of 237 studies and identified 102 studies that met our inclusion criteria (excluding unrelated studies due to study outcomes and patients, inadequate design, analyses, and techniques). After evaluating the primary outcomes and data reported, we included 35 case-series, observational, and pre-post clinical trials in our systematic review and meta-analysis (Fig. 1).Fig. 1PRISMA flowchart for selection and screening of studies

PRISMA flowchart for selection and screening of studies

The studies included in this review were published between 2013 and 2023 and involved both male and female participants. They were conducted across three continents: Asia, Europe, and North America, which reflects a variety of clinical settings and patient populations. The sample sizes varied significantly, ranging from just 7 participants in small-scale case series to 188 in larger observational cohorts. This variability in study design, population size, and geographical distribution highlights the heterogeneity of the available evidence.

Quality assessment findings

Based on the quality assessment of 35 studies included in the meta-analysis, none of them adequately covered all the domains of quality assessment tools and received a total 12 scores in the systematic review. Six studies (17.2%) were deemed to have poor quality, 18 studies (51.4%) were rated as fair quality and 11 studies (31.4%) had good quality. Most studies faced challenges in four areas: the participants’ representativeness, blinding of outcome assessors, lost follow-up after baseline, and taking multiple measurements of the outcome before and after the intervention. Regarding the clarity of study objectives, it was noted that most studies were good-rated in terms of specifying eligibility/selection criteria for the study population, enrollment of eligible participants, and providing a clear and consistent description of the intervention across the study population. American shoulder and elbow surgeons (ASES)

Meta-analysis of 22 single-arm studies [27–31, 34, 35, 37, 39–44, 47, 48, 50–53, 55, 58] showed that the ASES score significantly increased by an average of 40.76 points (95% CI: 35.9 to 45.63) as illustrated in Fig. 2. Sensitivity analysis confirmed that the mean change in ASES score was consistent across a range of summary WMDs (40.08 to 42.63), demonstrating the robustness of the meta-analysis findings. The high I-square value of 95.6% indicated substantial heterogeneity among the reported data for ASES. However, meta-regression and subgroup analysis suggest that the patient’s age and follow-up duration are unlikely sources of this heterogeneity. Subgroup analyses based on follow-up time, geographic region (continent), graft type, sample size, and study quality are shown in Figs. 3, 4, 5, 6 and 7. Despite these stratifications, heterogeneity remained substantial, suggesting that unmeasured confounding factors such as variations in surgical techniques, patient selection criteria, tear chronicity, and extent of tendon retraction or fatty degeneration could have contributed to the observed variability. Moreover, no evidence of publication bias was found based on statistical tests (P = 0.40 for Egger’s regression asymmetry test and P = 0.54 for Begg’s test), as shown in Fig. 8.

This indicates that other unmeasured factors, like the criteria for patient selection, might have influenced the variability seen among the studies.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluate two surgical techniques for managing MIRCTs. The results showed that ASCR and SSI can significantly improve shoulder scores during follow-up. As there was no literature comparing the two procedures, it was challenging to assess the outcomes of SSI and SCR definitively, and direct comparison between the data was impossible due to differences in sample populations and quality across the studies.

The SCR technique has gained popularity in recent years. It was first introduced by Mihata et al., which aimed to transfer soft tissue between the superior glenoid and the humeral head, balance the forces around the shoulder joint, and it was developed to improve glenohumeral articulation function by preventing superior humeral head migration and recentering it [44, 65]. According to our meta-analysis, both ASES and VAS scores showed significant improvement after ASCR, which is in line with previous studies on patients with MRCTs undergoing ASCR [58, 66–68]. The ASES and VAS scores are essential tools for assessing functional outcomes. It is reliable, valid, and responsive after treating rotator cuff tears [13, 69]. In particular, CS and SSV scores showed significant improvement [25, 26, 29, 34, 38, 39, 43, 48, 49, 56]. Other shoulder scores, including Q-DASH, UCLA, SST, and JOA, showed improvement. In addition, the ROM indicates improvement in flexion, abduction, and external rotation movements; these results align with previous studies [70, 71]. Notably, internal rotation showed a minimal mean change of just 0.13°, which is clinically insignificant. This likely indicates that the reconstructive techniques used have limited potential for biomechanical recovery. While the overall ROM may improve, some specific movements may demonstrate only slight functional gains. This observation emphasizes the need to consider the nuances of recovery in rehabilitation [72].

The SSI has been recently proposed and has shown promising clinical efficacy [73]. It is a simple and minimally invasive surgical method that can center the humeral head during dynamic movements without reducing the resting supra position and forward movement of the humerus [74]. However, it is not considered a reliable approach for controlling anterosuperior migration of the humeral head [75]. The results of SSI should be interpreted with caution because the number of this procedure was limited. Nevertheless, the literature reviewed has shown positive clinical outcomes for patients with MIRCTs who underwent spacer implantation [76]. Results of our meta-analysis indicate that all functional scores significantly improved [45, 54, 57]. These results are acceptable and consistent compared to the previous systematic review [76].

Overall, all clinical and functional outcomes assessed demonstrated significant improvement following both ASCR and SSI procedures. We observed considerable heterogeneity among the studies, as indicated by high I-square values. To explore the sources of heterogeneity, we conducted a meta-regression analysis based on the length of follow-up and patient age for each outcome to identify the cause of this heterogeneity. Still, none of the variables we examined were found to be the source of the heterogeneity. Moreover, subgroup meta-analyses were conducted based on various follow-up times, graft types, sample sizes, and quality of the included studies. Only in a few cases, including external rotation, internal rotation, and AHD, variations in study quality, graft selection, and sample size may partly explain the observed high heterogeneity among the included studies. In this regard, studies classified as “poor” methodological quality often lack standardized protocols for patient selection, surgical technique, and outcome assessment, which can introduce significant bias and variability in reported results [77]. Similarly, studies using autografts often involve diverse donor sites, graft preparation methods, and biological properties that can influence healing and functional results, causing inconsistent findings across studies [78]. Moreover, studies with a small sample size (< 30 participants) are more susceptible to random error, unstable effect estimates, and overrepresentation of individual patient characteristics [79]. Among all the outcomes assessed, internal rotation showed the highest heterogeneity (I² = 99.2%). Unlike other ROM parameters, internal rotation is often measured using non-standardized or semi-quantitative methods, leading to significant measurement differences among studies. Furthermore, internal rotation largely depends on the integrity and functional recovery of the subscapularis muscle, which can be influenced differently by surgical technique, graft tension, or rehabilitation protocol. Additionally, this movement is highly susceptible to postoperative stiffness and capsular contracture, which vary considerably across patient groups and study environments [80–82].

The persistence of high heterogeneity despite subgroup analyses suggests that additional unaccounted factors may have influenced the pooled outcomes. Variability in surgical techniques, patient selection criteria, the extent of tendon retraction or the number of tendons involved, and fatty degeneration could have contributed to inconsistent functional and structural results across studies [80, 83, 84]. Furthermore, differences in rehabilitation protocols, postoperative evaluation methods such as imaging modalities and scoring systems, and surgeon experience may have further amplified inter-study variability. Notably, most studies did not provide detailed information on the surgeons’ experience, which limited the ability to perform formal subgroup or regression analyses. The anterior-posterior dimension of the graft, representing the superior–posterior tear size (supraspinatus, infraspinatus, and teres minor), has been shown to influence clinical outcomes following SCR, especially in cases involving subscapularis tears [58]. Notably, when the infraspinatus tendon is irreparable, SCR often fails to achieve satisfactory functional recovery, regardless of the graft type employed [85]. Therefore, caution is warranted when interpreting the outcomes of studies on MIRCTs. These findings highlight the inherent complexity of comparing outcomes across heterogeneous patient populations and underscore the need for conducting well-designed, adequately powered, and methodologically robust clinical trials with standardized definitions, procedural protocols, and long-term follow-up to generate more consistent and generalizable evidence, improve data comparability, and reduce statistical heterogeneity.

Our findings should be interpreted with caution in clinical decision-making, because no adjustment for multiplicity was performed. In addition, our study had some limitations. Firstly, the studies in both procedure groups used different methods to evaluate outcomes, differences in study design, and patient population, which may affect the accuracy of the results. Secondly, pre-post study designs without control groups are inherently susceptible to bias, and observed improvements may be influenced by natural recovery. Although methodological quality was assessed using the NIH tool and subgroup analyses were performed based on study quality (poor, fair, and good), variations in study rigor could still affect the overall pooled outcomes. Thirdly, it is essential to acknowledge that the weaknesses of each study, such as short-term follow-up or incomplete data, which may impact the overall conclusions of the present study. Fourthly, the limited number of studies evaluating SSI, especially for some clinical and functional outcomes, may lead to imprecision and wider confidence intervals in the pooled estimates. Finally, a key limitation of our study lies in the lack of direct two-arm or randomized controlled trials (RCTs) that compare SCR and SSI. Most available evidence comes from single-arm case series or cohort studies, which may introduce heterogeneity and limit the ability to draw firm conclusions.

In addition to the limitations mentioned above, our study also possesses several notable strengths, and implemented a rigorous and comprehensive search strategy across multiple major databases, resulting in a more inclusive collection of evidence compared to previous reviews. In previous systematic reviews, only one study specifically evaluated SCR and SSI, but it did not progress to a meta-analysis [23]. Two other reviews examined general surgical techniques for managing RCTs but did not provide a focused comparison between these two advanced procedures [61, 70]. In contrast, our systematic review and meta-analysis represent the first and most comprehensive effort of SCR and SSI procedures. By synthesizing high-quality clinical and functional outcome data, this study offers an evidence-based outcomes that can inform clinical decision-making and optimize surgical strategies. Furthermore, this work not only addresses a critical gap in the current body of literature but also sets a new benchmark for evaluating advanced shoulder reconstructive procedures. It provides valuable insights for both clinical practice and future research in rotator cuff repair.

In summary, our findings highlight a critical need for future research to provide reliable and robust evidence on the comparative effectiveness of SCR and SSI techniques. To achieve this, studies should be methodologically rigorous and well-powered, designed as two-arm randomized clinical trials with standardized intervention protocols, clearly defined eligibility criteria, and consistent outcome measures. It is essential to evaluate long-term efficacy, durability, and any potential late complications.

Conclusions

To summarize, ASCR and SSI procedures have led to significant improvements in clinical and functional outcomes. However, it is difficult to directly compare the effectiveness of the two methods due to differences in sample populations and the limited number of studies on SSI. Our findings should be interpreted with caution in clinical decision-making, because no adjustment for multiplicity was performed. To ensure their effectiveness and provide better guidance for clinical practice, long-term prospective trials with larger sample sizes and rigorous methodology are necessary.
