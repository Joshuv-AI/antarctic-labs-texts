Programmable Nucleic Acid Sensing in Human Cells Using Circularizable ssDNA
Authors: Ahmed Mahas, Raphael Ferreira, Lisa M. Riedmayr, George M. Church
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Programmable technologies that sense nucleic acid signatures in living cells and trigger cellular functions hold promise for biotechnology and medicine. Here, we develop SONAR (Sensing Of Nucleic acids using ASOs and Reverse-transcriptases), a platform that detects target DNA and RNA sequences and triggers controlled gene expression in human cells. SONAR operates through circularizable single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) sensors that, upon hybridization with complementary DNA or reverse-transcribed RNA, undergo target-dependent ligation via cellular ligases, subsequently driving expression of genetic payloads. For RNA sensing, we employ antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) to prime targeted reverse transcription, generating complementary DNA that promotes ssDNA circularization. We demonstrate SONAR’s ability to detect ssDNA, exogenous and endogenous RNA, couple sensing to programmable expression of diverse protein payloads, including reporters, recombinases, and genome editors, and enable enrichment and clonal recovery of target-positive cells from mixed populations. This platform establishes a versatile framework for targeted nucleic acid detection and inducible gene expression, with broad potential applications in diagnostics, therapeutics, and synthetic biology.

Introduction

Distinct RNA expression profiles and specific DNA mutations are hallmarks of diseases such as cancer, viral infections, and genetic disorders, influencing disease onset, progression, and treatment resistance1,2. Detecting these molecular signatures within living cells could greatly enhance precision diagnostics and targeted therapeutics. Biosensors capable of real-time detection would enable earlier disease identification, precise monitoring of treatment responses, and selective therapeutic activation, ultimately improving clinical outcomes. For instance, the detection of specific nucleic acid sequences could trigger programmable outputs such as amplified reporter signals for pathogen detection, the generation of synthetic biomarkers for non-invasive diagnostics, or precise genome editing3,4. More advanced approaches could further activate immune responses at tumor or infection sites or initiate apoptotic pathways as kill-switch mechanisms, thereby limiting pathogenic cell proliferation. Importantly, by preferentially activating therapeutic payloads in target cells, these engineered biosensors significantly enhance treatment specificity, thereby reducing off-target effects and associated toxicities.

Recent advancements in nucleic acid-responsive technologies, including eukaryotic toehold switches5, circular RNAs6, and ADAR-based biosensors7–9, represent significant milestones in the field, expanding the capabilities for nucleic acid sensing and controlled cellular responses. However, these approaches rely on intricate setups, complicated designs, are strictly confined to RNA detection, and/or are unable to detect potentially pathogenic single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), ultimately narrowing their potential therapeutic and diagnostic applications. Consequently, there is an unmet need for innovative platforms that overcome these limitations, enabling the detection and integration of diverse nucleic acid signals with high flexibility, sensitivity, and specificity, and thus enabling next-generation therapeutic and diagnostic interventions.

While plasmids have traditionally been the dominant platform for nucleic acid-based expression and sensing applications, alternative approaches are expanding the molecular toolkit. Notably, breakthroughs in synthesizing long, high-fidelity single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) molecules have unlocked new opportunities for gene expression10, genome editing11,12, and advanced synthetic biology applications13. Additionally, ssDNA exhibits lower immunogenicity than dsDNA, supporting improved cellular viability and making it more suitable for potential therapeutic applications11. Long ssDNA molecules also provide unique structural properties for innovative biosensor designs. Their ability to undergo sequence-specific circularization and ligation enables highly specific nucleic acid sensing within cells.

Here, we introduce SONAR (Sensing Of Nucleic acids using ASOs and Reverse-transcriptases), a programmable platform for detecting specific DNA and RNA sequences directly within living human cells. SONAR functions by recognizing a targeted complementary nucleic acid sequence, which triggers the circularization of a ssDNA sensor followed by a unique target-dependent ligation mechanism mediated by endogenous cellular ligases. To facilitate RNA detection, we employ antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) to act as primers within the cells, initiating a targeted reverse transcription using a Moloney murine leukemia virus reverse transcriptase (MMLV-RT). This process produces a complementary reverse-transcribed DNA (RT-DNA), which serves as a splint to enable the ligase-mediated circularization of the ssDNA sensor. Once circularized, the ssDNA sensor acts as an efficient, programmable vector, driving the precise expression of genetic payloads within the cell (Fig. 1a). Using SONAR, we link intracellular target recognition to programmable gene-expression outputs (reporters, recombinases, and genome editors) and enable single-nucleotide discrimination through engineered ASO priming. Results

Target-dependent circularization activates ssDNA sensors

First, we tested whether engineered ssDNA sequences can sense and specifically respond to complementary DNA sequences in human cells. To achieve this, we designed a linear ssDNA sensor consisting of a green fluorescent protein (GFP) reporter, a transcription termination sequence, and a cytomegalovirus (CMV) promoter, arranged in the specified order. In its linear form, the CMV promoter and GFP coding sequence remain physically separated, preventing GFP expression. However, the sensor is phosphorylated at its 5′ end, enabling ligation and circularization when hybridized with a complementary DNA splint. Upon recognition of such a splint, the ligation restores the correct arrangement of the expression cassette, allowing GFP transcription to be initiated. We observed GFP expression 24 h after co-transfection of the ssDNA sensor with its complementary DNA splint, demonstrating efficient target-dependent activation (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 1a). In contrast, co-transfection with a complementary RNA splint failed to induce GFP expression, confirming the DNA-specific nature of the ligation mechanism. We also found that SONAR activation produced sustained reporter expression for several days in cells, with more durable signal kinetics than GFP mRNA in the matched control experiment, supporting persistent downstream output following target-dependent sensor circularization (Supplementary Fig. 1b). To confirm successful sensor circularization in cells, we amplified the ligation site via PCR using junction-spanning primers. Specific amplification occurred exclusively in cells transfected with both the ssDNA sensor and a complementary DNA splint (Fig. 1c). Additionally, treatment of the ssDNA sensor with a phosphatase prior to transfection reduced GFP expression, supporting an endogenous ligase-mediated mechanism and highlighting the requirement for a 5′ phosphate for successful circularization (Supplementary Fig. 2a).

ASO-primed reverse transcription enables RNA sensing

Next, we examined the sensor’s capability to detect specific RNA sequences within living human cells. We hypothesized that targeted intracellular reverse transcription of RNA sequences into complementary DNA (RT-DNA) would enable interaction with the sensor, leading to its circularization and payload expression. Previous findings from prime editing studies have demonstrated that targeted reverse transcription in live cells is feasible14. To be able to initiate targeted reverse transcription, we designed chemically stabilized ASOs that hybridize to their RNA targets, creating DNA:RNA duplexes. These duplexes then recruit an exogenously supplied RT to mediate a targeted synthesis of complementary DNA (Fig. 1a). For our study, the ASOs were modified with locked nucleic acids (LNAs) throughout their backbone, leaving the terminal four nucleotides unmodified to function effectively as DNA primers for reverse transcription (Supplementary Fig. 2b).

To validate this approach, we designed ASOs targeting exons of two endogenous transcripts, GAPDH (glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase) and ACTB (β-actin). HEK293T cells were transfected with RT alone, ASOs alone, or a combination of both. To validate a successful ASO-guided reverse transcription and complementary RT-DNA synthesis, we designed quantitative PCR (qPCR) primers spanning exon-exon junctions. Notably, qPCR as well as gel electrophoresis confirmed precise RT-DNA synthesis only in cells co-transfected with both ASOs and RT, verifying the specificity and effectiveness of the ASO-guided reverse transcription process (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. 2c).

After demonstrating that DNA splints activate the ssDNA sensor and that targeted intracellular reverse transcription can generate complementary RT-DNA from RNA templates, we tested whether these two capabilities can be combined to effectively detect target RNA sequences within living cells. First, we tested whether the sensor could detect exogenous RNA expressed from a plasmid. To achieve this, we designed four distinct ASOs targeting different regions of the target RNA, alongside a control plasmid lacking the target RNA sequence. Cells were co-transfected with a plasmid expressing the MMLV-RT and either the plasmid encoding the RNA target or the control plasmid. After 24 h, ASOs were co-delivered with the ssDNA sensor encoding NanoLuc luciferase (Nluc15) as a reporter payload (Fig. 1e). All tested ASOs successfully activated the sensor, with the most effective ASO resulting in an approximately 52-fold higher signal than the no-target control (Fig. 1f and Supplementary Fig. 3a). The sensor’s activity increased proportionally with the concentration of the plasmid encoding the target RNA, and exhibited a strong linear correlation with RT-qPCR measurements across the tested range, demonstrating its ability to quantitatively sense RNA levels in cells (Fig. 1g and Supplementary Fig. 3b). Additionally, increasing ASO concentrations led to enhanced sensor activation, confirming that ASO abundance directly influences detection efficiency (Fig. 1h and Supplementary Fig. 3c).

We also evaluated the influence of nuclear localization signals (NLS) on the RT activity, comparing RT variants with and without NLS. RTs without NLS showed a significantly higher sensor activation compared to RTs with NLS, indicating that cytoplasmic reverse transcription is more efficient for this application (Supplementary Fig. 4a). Notably, the absence of an RT completely abolished sensor activation, confirming that reverse transcription is essential for RNA sensing (Fig. 1i and Supplementary Fig. 4a). In addition, we evaluated whether a truncated RT variant lacking the RNase H domain (RT ΔRH), previously shown to support efficient prime editing in cells16, could similarly activate the ssDNA sensor. Both full-length and truncated RT exhibited comparable performance (Supplementary Fig. 4b, c). Thus, the full-length RT without NLS was used in subsequent experiments, while noting that the truncated variant may provide an added margin of safety for applications where RNA preservation is critical.

We also explored additional design parameters to optimize our ssDNA sensor system. In our initial designs, we selected 60-nucleotide sensing sequences based on the hypothesis that DNA:DNA hybridization at this length would be inherently stable and efficient. To evaluate the impact of sensing region length, we next compared sequences ranging from 45 to 75 nucleotides. All variants demonstrated comparable levels of activation, indicating that sensing region length has a minimal impact on overall performance within this range (Supplementary Fig. 4d). We also compared alternative sensor configurations by placing the sensing sequence between the stop codon and polyA terminator instead of between the promoter and start codon. While this alternative design was effectively activated upon splint-mediated circularization, our original design demonstrated greater specificity and more robust activation (Supplementary Fig. 4e). Therefore, we chose the original sensor configuration and the 60-nt length in subsequent experiments to maintain consistency and maximize system performance.

Detection of endogenous transcripts by SONAR

Next, we evaluated the sensor’s ability to detect endogenous RNA transcripts. First, we tested whether SONAR could detect RNA expressed from a genomically integrated sequence introduced via the PiggyBac transposase system. For this, we generated a HEK293T cell line stably co-expressing the target RNA sequence and inducible MMLV-RT. qPCR confirmed that an efficient and specific RT-DNA synthesis occurred specifically in the presence of targeting ASOs (Supplementary Fig. 5a). Using the most effective ASO (Fig. 1f), we observed a significant 17.5-fold increase in SONAR activation for detecting the integrated target sequence compared to controls (Fig. 2a). Encouraged by these results, we further engineered sensors targeting different endogenous transcripts, including EEF1A1 (Eukaryotic translation elongation factor 1 alpha 1), GAPDH, and ACTB mRNAs. For all transcripts, we designed and evaluated several ASOs targeting distinct regions within the 3′ UTR. All tested ASOs produced varying yet significant sensor activation compared to the non-targeting sensor control (Supplementary Fig. 5b–d). Subsequent validation with the top-performing ASOs, compared to stringent negative controls (including non-targeting ASO and no-ASO conditions), consistently induced strong and specific sensor activation, confirming SONAR’s specificity and reliability in detecting endogenous transcripts (Fig. 2b–d). Minor variability in background signal among some non-targeting controls was also observed (Supplementary Fig. 5b, c). Some target sequences, e.g., for ACTB detection, inherently contain ATG codons, which could introduce unintended translation start sites within the sensing sequence, potentially disrupting proper SONAR-mediated payload expression. To address this challenge, we strategically introduced mismatches within the sensor sequences. Specifically, selected bases within ATG motifs were substituted, such as converting T to G or A to C. To verify that these intentional mismatches do not compromise sensor efficacy, we tested a sensor (ACTB-B) containing three such mismatches. Our results confirmed that this modified sensor maintained effective and highly specific detection of ACTB transcripts (Supplementary Fig. 5e, f). Notably, another sensor (ACTB-A), previously evaluated in Fig. 2d, also contained a single mismatch and similarly maintained high detection activity, demonstrating that targeted mismatch incorporation effectively mitigates unintended translation initiation without negatively impacting sensor performance.

SONAR compatibility across cellular contexts and formats

To broaden the scope of SONAR, we evaluated its performance across two human cell lines (HeLa, HCT116) and a mouse line (Hepa1–6), each tested with distinct target transcripts (ACTB, EEF1A1, and a plasmid-derived sequence, respectively). At 48 h post-transfection, SONAR showed strong, target-dependent activation (>20-fold in HeLa cells), demonstrating robust performance across diverse mammalian cell types and target contexts (Supplementary Fig. 6a).

A critical step toward advancing SONAR is the ability to deliver all of its components in a single, plasmid-free formulation. Such co-delivery is essential for enabling rapid, controlled, and translationally relevant applications of the platform. To this end, we developed formats in which RT is supplied as either in vitro-transcribed mRNA or as purified protein, co-formulated with the ssDNA sensor and ASO using lipid-based delivery methods (Fig. 2e). Using RT mRNA, SONAR robustly detected endogenous transcripts when all components were introduced simultaneously in a single formulation (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 6b). To evaluate whether purified RT protein could support SONAR functionality when co-delivered with its components, we first tested a range of RT protein concentrations to identify doses that maximize activity without increasing background (Supplementary Fig. 6c). Under these optimized conditions, co-delivery of RT protein with the ssDNA sensor and ASO produced robust, target-dependent activation (>8-fold) compared to controls (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Fig. 6d). When applied across multiple transcript targets (GAPDH, ACTB) and mammalian cell lines (U2OS, HCT116), this format consistently yielded activation of approximately 5–10-fold over background (Supplementary Fig. 6e, f). This protein-based modality provides a tightly controlled and transient system that minimizes potential off-target effects while simplifying deployment. These results establish that single-formulation delivery of SONAR components is feasible using either mRNA- or protein-based RT, underscoring the flexibility of the platform.

To determine whether SONAR perturbs the abundance or translation of its RNA targets, we quantified target transcript levels and protein output following SONAR activation. RT-qPCR showed no detectable change in target RNA abundance, indicating that SONAR does not substantially affect transcript stability (Supplementary Fig. 7a). To evaluate potential effects on translation, we utilized a GFP-targeting version of the system to assess its impact on GFP expression. We first optimized SONAR for the GFP target sequence and identified the most effective ASO (Supplementary Fig. 7b), and then tested it in a GFP reporter cell line containing one or two integrated GFP copies alongside a GFP-targeting siRNA (Supplementary Fig. 7c). While siRNA caused a marked decrease in GFP fluorescence, SONAR did not produce any detectable reduction in signal (Supplementary Fig. 7d).

Finally, to test the potential immunogenicity of the ssDNA sensor, we used hTLR9 reporter cells, which are designed to evaluate innate immune activation by exogenous DNA18. Delivery of the ssDNA did not elicit detectable immune activation, indicating that SONAR’s ssDNA component is well tolerated and unlikely to pose immunogenicity concerns (Supplementary Fig. 7e).

SONAR couples sensing to diverse payloads

As previously discussed, SONAR has the potential to function beyond a mere sensor, as it can trigger gene expression of any payload upon detecting target RNA. This capability expands its use to more advanced gene regulation applications, such as driving programmable genetic circuits in human cells. To test this, we designed a system in which SONAR mediates the expression of a Cre recombinase, which in turn enables the expression of a luciferase reporter. We employed a floxed-stop reporter plasmid in which three polyadenylation (polyA) signals, flanked by Cre loxP sites, were inserted between a CMV promoter and an Nluc luciferase coding sequence, preventing luciferase expression without a Cre-mediated excision of the polyA signals (Fig. 2h). Initially, we validated that the Cre recombinase could be expressed from a circularizable ssDNA sensor upon the addition of a DNA splint, and that this Cre expression mediated a robust activation of the Nluc reporter (Supplementary Fig. 8a). We then tested whether this Cre-expressing SONAR system can be activated via RNA targets. The Cre SONAR system successfully detected target RNA from both a genomically integrated sequence and a plasmid-expressed target, resulting in a more than 6-fold increase in luciferase activity compared to controls (Fig. 2h and Supplementary Fig. 8b). These findings highlight SONAR’s ability to function as a programmable genetic circuit, integrating RNA sensing with precise gene regulatory outputs.

To assess the capacity of the ssDNA sensor to express large genetic payloads while retaining its target-specific responsiveness, we engineered a sensor encoding Cas12a, a large genome-editing enzyme (>1300 amino acids)19. To validate Cas12a expression, we employed a reporter system consisting of an mCherry sequence followed by a stop codon and an out-of-frame Nluc sequence. Successful Cas12a expression and cleavage of the target sequence, i.e., the region containing the stop codon, can restore Nluc expression via a NHEJ repair-mediated frame shift (Supplementary Fig. 8c). The functionality of this system was confirmed by co-transfecting a Cas12a-expressing plasmid (Supplementary Fig. 8d). Upon co-delivering the Cas12a-expressing ssDNA sensor and the reporter plasmid, we observed robust Nluc activation exclusively in the presence of the specific splint DNA, demonstrating the sensor’s capability to efficiently express large proteins in response to specific nucleic acid sequences (Supplementary Fig. 8e).

SONAR discriminates single-nucleotide variants

Accurate discrimination of SNPs is critical for biosensors, particularly in applications where distinguishing pathogenic mutations from benign sequences is essential. To address this challenge, we explored whether introducing synthetic mismatches on ASOs could enhance SONAR’s sensitivity and ability to differentiate SNPs (Supplementary Fig. 9a). To test this, we designed a series of ASOs with strategic mismatches at different positions near the 3′ end and evaluated their impact on sensor activation. By comparing the sensor’s activation induced by each mismatched ASO to that of its perfect-match counterpart, we quantified SONAR’s sensitivity to single-base changes. Unmodified ASOs exhibited modest discrimination between matched and mismatched targets. However, when an LNA modification was introduced at the nucleotide immediately preceding the 3′ terminus, or LNA 3′–1, discrimination markedly improved (approximately 6-fold) (Supplementary Fig. 9b). Notably, such LNA modifications near the 3′ end also slightly reduced overall efficiency compared to ASOs without LNA modifications in these positions (Supplementary Fig. 9b).

To further evaluate SONAR’s SNP-resolving capacity, we systematically tested multiple SNP contexts across two representative target RNAs. In nearly all cases, SONAR accurately distinguished single-nucleotide variants with a clear preference for the perfectly matched base at the ASO 3′ end, consistent with efficient discrimination enabled by LNA placement near the terminus (Fig. 2i and Supplementary Fig. 9c, d). One target, however, exhibited a noncanonical pattern for a specific SNP (SNP T), where an ASO ending with a 3′ G outperformed the expected complementary 3′ A (Supplementary Fig. 9d). Such context-dependent deviations may arise from local sequence composition or secondary structure influencing primer recognition and extension, and are consistent with prior observations that M-MLV RT can tolerate certain mismatches at the 3′ end under favorable sequence contexts20. While SONAR effectively distinguishes SNPs within transcribed regions, it cannot detect non-transcribed or intergenic SNPs located in regulatory DNA elements, as these are not represented at the RNA level. Overall, these findings demonstrate SONAR’s ability to distinguish SNPs, providing a promising approach for applications involving mutation detection.

Target-guided enrichment from mixed cell populations

Finally, many biologically and clinically important cell states are defined by specific intracellular RNAs rather than surface markers, making it difficult to isolate, recover, and propagate live target-positive cells without engineered reporters. We therefore asked whether SONAR could directly link sequence identity to a sortable output for enrichment from heterogeneous mixtures. We engineered two fluorescently distinguishable cell lines (BFP and mCherry), introduced the target sequence into only one line, mixed the two populations, and then selected cells based on target-specific, SONAR-driven GFP activation. This strategy yielded highly specific enrichment of the target-containing population, with 94.4% BFP+ among GFP+ cells when the target was in the BFP line (<1% mCherry+), and 87.9% mCherry+ among GFP+ cells when the target was swapped to the mCherry line (Fig. 2j).

We next asked whether SONAR sequence-specific activation could be coupled to single-cell–derived clonal expansion. In an independent experiment, the target sequence was introduced into the mCherry cell line, and SONAR-driven GFP activation was used to sort single GFP⁺ cells into 96-well plates. After 2–3 weeks of single-cell expansion, plate-reader fluorescence (BFP, mCherry) was used to assign cell line identity (Supplementary Fig. 10a). Recovered clones were strongly enriched for the target-containing cell line (20 mCherry+ vs 7 BFP+; Supplementary Fig. 10b), supporting sequence-guided isolation of expandable clones. Thus, SONAR converts sequence identity into an actionable enrichment output, enabling recovery of target-defined cells from mixed populations.

Discussion

SONAR enables sequence-specific detection of both exogenous and endogenous RNA targets with minimal design optimization. Testing only a few ASO configurations was sufficient to identify active sensors, underscoring the system’s inherent robustness and modularity. Mechanistically, SONAR operates through target-dependent ssDNA circularization, a previously unexplored molecular strategy for intracellular biosensing that distinguishes it from RNA- or protein-based systems. This simplicity and predictability make SONAR not only a practical sensing tool but also a foundational platform that can inspire future generations of programmable biosensors and gene-control systems.

SONAR successfully detects RNA targets by reverse-transcribing them into DNA via an exogenously provided RT. Future work may explore whether endogenous RTs, such as LINE1-21, Polθ-22, or telomerase23-derived RTs, could be harnessed to support this process. In principle, these enzymes might be recruited via engineered ASOs or click chemistry, which would eliminate the dependency on exogenous enzymes. Beyond RNA detection, SONAR could, in principle, be extended to detecting viral DNA and RNA. As an exploratory test, we examined a plasmid-based AAV model that transiently generates ssDNA intermediates and observed modest activation (Supplementary Fig. 12). Future optimization may enable SONAR variants suited for direct viral-genome detection in such settings.

Having demonstrated SONAR activity across multiple human and murine cell types, a new direction will be translating this system for in vivo use. Effective delivery will be central to this effort, and our results showing that SONAR functions with RT supplied as mRNA or purified protein highlight its compatibility with non-viral delivery systems such as lipid nanoparticles (LNPs). Recent advances in LNP engineering have enabled multi-cargo delivery, including proteins, mRNAs, and even ssDNA, suggesting that existing LNP platforms could feasibly support SONAR’s multi-component architecture24–27. Given the rapid clinical progress of ASOs, RT-based therapeutics, and LNP delivery systems, advancing SONAR toward preclinical or in vivo validation represents a realistic step toward translational deployment28–30.

SONAR establishes a versatile framework for programmable nucleic-acid sensing in living cells, with potential applications in diagnostics, gene regulation, and synthetic biology. By linking molecular recognition to durable gene expression, SONAR enables live-cell analyses and prospective isolation of cell states defined by transcript signatures, converting intracellular RNA identity into an actionable enrichment and clonal recovery workflow. SONAR could further support condition-responsive therapeutic circuits that activate only in disease-specific contexts. Continued optimization is expected to further extend its utility across research and translational applications.

Methods

Single-stranded DNA generation

All ssDNA constructs were synthesized by Genscript (GenExact™ Single-Stranded DNA Synthesis Service) with 5′ end phosphorylation. Details of the ssDNA design and structure are provided in Supplementary Fig. 13. All ssDNA sensor sequences used in this study are provided in Supplementary Data 1.

Antisense oligonucleotides

All ASOs used in this study were obtained from Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT) as Affinity Plus antisense oligos. ASOs contained Locked Nucleic Acid (LNA) modifications at various nucleotides along the backbone, with the exception of the last four nucleotides at the 3′ end, which remained unmodified (Supplementary Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 13). Additionally, the first two or three nucleotides at the 5′ end of all ASOs were modified with phosphorothioate linkages. Other ASOs used for mutation detection had different patterns of modifications. All ASOs and modifications are provided in Supplementary Data 1.

Reverse transcriptase cloning

All plasmids were generated using standard molecular cloning methods, including Gibson assembly and restriction–ligation cloning. MMLV-RT plasmids were cloned by Gibson assembly of PCR products. Full-length MMLV-RT with nuclear localization signal (NLS) or lacking NLS was amplified from Addgene plasmid #190105 using Q5 Hot Start High-Fidelity 2× Master Mix (NEB). PCR products were purified using the Monarch Gel Extraction Kit (NEB) and then assembled under the control of the EF1α promoter into the backbone of Addgene plasmid #138272 (digested with SmaI and NotI) using the NEB HiFi DNA Assembly Master Mix.

To generate the RT ΔRH construct, the previously described MMLV-RT plasmid lacking NLS was PCR-amplified using primers designed to exclude the RNase H domain. The amplified PCR product was purified and then circularized by Gibson assembly with a short, double-stranded linker fragment, generated by annealing two complementary oligonucleotides (IDT).

Target sequences cloning

Exogenous RNA target sequences were expressed from a modified version of Addgene plasmid #191138. To introduce custom sequences, the 3′ UTR of the original construct was replaced with a synthetic sequence generated by annealing two complementary oligonucleotides (IDT). These oligos were first phosphorylated and annealed using T4 Polynucleotide Kinase (NEB), then ligated into the NheI- and ApaI-digested #191138 backbone using T4 DNA Ligase (NEB). For the no-target control plasmid, the 3′ UTR sequence of the above plasmid was replaced with part of the mCherry coding sequence. This mCherry fragment was PCR-amplified and then Gibson-assembled into the backbone, which had been digested with XhoI and ApaI. An expression plasmid encoding GFP target sequence was obtained from Addgene (Addgene #11153). Target constructs harboring single-nucleotide variants (SNPs) were generated by de novo gene synthesis and cloning by Twist Bioscience.

PiggyBac plasmids

To generate stable cell lines co-expressing a target sequence and MMLV-RT, two PiggyBac (PB) transposon plasmids were constructed: (1) Target sequence PB plasmid: The target sequence was PCR-amplified from the previously described plasmid and cloned into the pB-CAGGS-dCas9-KRAB-MeCP2 vector (Addgene #110824), which carries a blasticidin resistance marker. The vector was digested with NheI and PmeI, and the insert was assembled using Gibson assembly. (2) MMLV-RT PB plasmid: The MMLV-RT coding region was PCR-amplified from the cloned MMLV-RT plasmid and inserted into a modified version of PB-TRE-dCas9-VPR (Addgene #63800), in which the hygromycin resistance cassette was replaced with puromycin. The recipient plasmid was digested with NheI and PmeI, and assembly was performed using Gibson assembly. This construct places MMLV-RT under the control of a TRE3G inducible promoter.

To generate HEK293T cell lines stably expressing BFP or mCherry fluorescent proteins, the BFP coding sequence (VectorBuilder) and mCherry coding sequence (gBlock, IDT) were PCR-amplified and cloned downstream of a CAG promoter in PiggyBac transposon plasmid carrying a blasticidin resistance marker using Gibson assembly.

Reporter plasmids cloning

For the floxed luciferase reporter plasmid for evaluating SONAR activity with a Cre payload, we utilized the pcDNA3.1_Floxed-STOP_Fluc plasmid (Addgene #122962), which expresses firefly luciferase (Fluc) under the control of a CMV promoter. This construct contains three polyadenylation (polyA) sequences flanked by loxP sites positioned between the CMV promoter and the Fluc coding sequence, thereby blocking downstream expression until Cre-mediated excision. To replace Fluc with Nluc, the Nluc coding sequence was PCR-amplified and cloned into the same vector, digested with EcoRI and XhoI, generating the pcDNA3.1_Floxed-STOP_Nluc construct.

To construct Nluc reporter plasmids for evaluating SONAR activity with a Cas12 payload, synthetic gBlocks (IDT) encoding an mCherry sequence, a Cas12 target site, and an out-of-frame Nluc coding sequence were designed and cloned downstream of the CMV promoter into the plasmid backbone (Addgene #182540), which had been digested with NheI and XbaI, using the NEB HiFi DNA Assembly Master Mix. To construct Cas12 crRNA expression cassettes, synthetic gBlocks (Twist Bioscience) containing the U6 promoter and Cas12 crRNA sequences were cloned into Cas12 reporter plasmids linearized with MfeI using the NEB HiFi DNA Assembly Master Mix. Each U6-Cas12 crRNA cassette was integrated into its corresponding reporter plasmid, which harbored the Cas12 target site positioned between the mCherry and Nluc coding sequences. To evaluate the reporter functionality, enAsCas12a plasmid (Addgene #107941) was used.

mRNA production

The full-length MMLV-RT was PCR-amplified from the constructed plasmids described above and cloned into an in vitro transcription (IVT) backbone containing a CleanCap Reagent AG-compatible T7 promoter, 5′ and 3′ UTRs, and a 41-nt poly(A) tract. The resulting plasmids were linearized with XhoI and purified using the QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen). The RT ΔRH IVT plasmid was constructed by digesting the full-length RT IVT backbone described above with EcoRV and MscI to exclude the RNaseH domain, and then circularized by Gibson assembly with a short, double-stranded linker fragment, generated by annealing two complementary oligonucleotides (IDT). In vitro transcription was performed with the HiScribe™ T7 mRNA Kit with CleanCap Reagent AG (NEB, E2080), substituting UTP with N1-Methylpseudouridine-5′-Triphosphate (TriLink Biotechnologies, N1081), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The reaction was terminated after 4 h with a 15-min incubation with DNase I (NEB). The transcribed mRNAs were subsequently purified with the Monarch RNA Cleanup Kit (NEB, T2050).

RT protein production and purification

Codon-optimized MMLV RT-NLS was produced and purified by GenScript using their Customized Protein Production Service in a bacterial expression system.

Tissue culture

Cell lines, including HEK293T (CRL-3216), HeLa (CCL-2), HCT116 (CCL-247), U2OS (HTB-96), and Hepa1-6 (CRL-1830), were sourced from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, Virginia) and maintained at 37 °C and 5% CO₂. All cell lines were cultured in Gibco™ DMEM with high glucose and pyruvate (ThermoFisher cat. #11-995-073), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, ThermoFisher cat. #A5209501) and 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin (ThermoFisher catalog #15140122).

For experiments requiring GFP fluorescence monitoring (described below, such as the ssDNA stability assay and assessment of SONAR’s effect on target translation), HEK293T-GFP cell lines were cultured in FluoroBrite DMEM (ThermoFisher, Cat. #A1896701) supplemented with GlutaMAX™ (Fisher Scientific, Cat. #3505006), Sodium Pyruvate (Fisher Scientific, Cat. #11360070), 10% FBS, and 1% Penicillin–Streptomycin, at 37 °C in 5% CO₂ in black, tissue culture (TC)-treated 96-well plates with clear bottoms (Costar 3603, Corning).

Transient transfection

For all experiments in which RT was delivered in plasmid format, unless otherwise specified, approximately 2 × 10⁴ HEK293T, 1.5 × 10⁴ HeLa, 1.8 × 10⁴ HCT116, or 2.2 × 10⁴ Hepa1-6 cells were seeded per well in 96-well TC-treated plates and cultured to ~70% confluency prior to transfection. Transfections were performed using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, L3000015). For each well, nucleic acid–Lipofectamine complexes were prepared by combining the desired plasmids or oligonucleotides with 0.35 µL of Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (0.2 µL for HeLa cells) and 0.2 µL of P3000 enhancer in a total volume of 10 µL Opti-MEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Complexes were incubated for 20 min at room temperature before being added to the cells.

To assay for ssDNA sensor activation using splint sequences, cells were transfected with 25 ng of ssDNA sensor with GFP payload and 125 nM of specific DNA splint, non-specific DNA splint, or specific RNA splint. All oligos were purchased from IDT.

For qPCR analysis of targeted ASO-mediated reverse transcription of endogenous RNA, HEK293T cells were grown in 24-well TC-treated plates to ensure ample nucleic acid availability for subsequent analyses, and allowed to grow to approximately 70% confluency prior to transfection. The cells were first transfected with plasmids expressing reverse transcriptase. After 24 h, the media was changed, and the cells were transfected with ASOs at a final concentration of 100 nM.

For detection of RNA transcripts expressed from plasmids, cells were first transfected with 40 ng of RT-expressing plasmids and 40 ng of plasmid encoding the RNA target sequence, or a non-targeting control plasmid as a negative control. After 24 h, the media was changed, and cells were transfected with 25 ng of ssDNA sensor along with the corresponding ASOs. Unless otherwise specified, ASOs were used at a final concentration of 125–150 nM in all experiments involving plasmid-expressed RNA targets. For the detection of the target delivered into BFP and mCherry fluorescent cell lines, 15 ng of ssDNA was used.

For the detection of endogenous RNA transcripts, cells were first transfected with 50 ng (or 30 ng for HeLa) of RT-expressing plasmids. After 24 h, the media was changed, and the cells were transfected with 25 ng of ssDNA sensor mixed with 125 nM of the respective ASOs.

For RT-qPCR analysis of target transcript levels following SONAR transfection, cells were transfected with SONAR components as described above. In parallel, 100 nM of siRNAs targeting GAPDH (Horizon Discovery, Cat. #L-004253-00-0005) or a non-targeting control siRNA against GFP (Horizon Discovery) were delivered using Lipofectamine™ RNAiMAX Reagent (Invitrogen, Cat. #13778-100) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

For detection of a genomically integrated target, approximately 2.8 × 10⁴ HEK293T cells stably expressing the target sequence and an inducible MMLV-RT were seeded into 96-well TC-treated plates in media supplemented with 1.5 µg/mL doxycycline. After 24 h, once the cells reached approximately 70% confluency, they were transfected with 25 ng of ssDNA sensor and 125 nM of the corresponding ASOs.

For the floxed luciferase assay with Cre-SONAR, cells were first transfected with 40 ng of RT-expressing plasmids and 40 ng of plasmid encoding the RNA target sequence, or a non-targeting control plasmid as a negative control. After 24 h, the media was changed, and cells were transfected with 5 ng of pcDNA3.1_Floxed-STOP_Nluc reporter, 5 ng of the ssDNA sensor encoding Cre recombinase, and 200 nM of the corresponding ASO. On day 3, the cells were processed following the protocol described in the “Luciferase Assays” section below.

For the floxed luciferase assay with Cre-SONAR targeting a genomically integrated target, HEK293T cells expressing the target sequence with inducible RT were prepared as discussed above. When the cells reached approximately 70% confluency prior to transfection (24 h), they were transfected with 5 ng of pcDNA3.1_Floxed-STOP_Nluc reporter, 5 ng of the ssDNA sensor encoding Cre recombinase, and 200 nM of the corresponding ASO. After 24 h, the cells were processed following the protocol described in the Luciferase Assays section.

For Cas12 detection assays using plasmid-expressed enAsCas12a, cells were transfected with 10 ng of enAsCas12a plasmid and 20 ng of the Nluc Cas12 reporter plasmid, which served both as a surrogate reporter for Cas12 activity and as an expression cassette for the Cas12 crRNA. For the no-Cas12 control, 10 ng of a non-targeting plasmid was used in place of the Cas12 plasmid. After 48 hours, cells were processed according to the protocol outlined in the Luciferase Assays section.

For Cas12-SONAR detection assays, cells were transfected with 25 ng of Cas12 ssDNA sensor, 10 ng of Nluc Cas12 reporters, and either 125 nM or 0 nM of specific DNA splint. After 48 h, the cells were processed following the protocol described in the Luciferase Assays section. For SONAR assays using RT mRNA, HEK293T cells were prepared as described above. When cells reached approximately 70% confluency, 110 ng of full-length RT, RT ΔRH, or CleanCap M6 mCherry mRNA (TriLink Bio, L-8103), 20 ng of ssDNA sensor, and 120 nM of the corresponding ASO were co-transfected using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, Cat. #L3000015) as described above. After 48 hours, the cells were processed following the protocol described in the Luciferase Assays section.

For SONAR assays using purified RT proteins, HEK293T and HCT116 cells were prepared as described above. For U2OS cells, 2.2 × 10⁴ cells were seeded per well in 96-well TC–treated plates. When cells reached approximately 70% confluency, 1.5 µg (unless otherwise specified) of purified RT or dCas9 protein (Alt-R™ S.p. dCas9 Protein V3, IDT, Cat. #1081066), 20 ng of ssDNA sensor, and 120 nM of the corresponding ASO were co-transfected using Lipofectamine CRISPRMAX™ Cas9 Transfection Reagent (Invitrogen, CMAX0001). For each well, protein and nucleic acid mixtures were complexed with 0.5 µL of CRISPRMAX reagent and 0.5 µL of Cas9 Plus reagent in a total volume of 10 µL Opti-MEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and incubated for 10 min at room temperature before being added to cells. After 48 h, cells were processed as described in the Luciferase assays section.

For ssDNA stability assays, HEK293T cells seeded in black, 96-well plates were transfected as described above for ssDNA sensor activation using splint sequences. In parallel, 25 ng of GFP mRNA (TriLink Bio, L-7601) was transfected using Lipofectamine™ MessengerMAX™ (Invitrogen, LMRNA001) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

To assess the effect of SONAR on target translation, HEK293T-GFP cells were seeded at 2.2 × 10⁴ cells per well in black, 96-well plates and transfected with SONAR components targeting the GFP sequence or a non-targeting (NT) control, as described above. A final concentration of 120 nM ASOs was used for both GFP-targeting and NT control conditions. In parallel, siRNAs targeting GFP were transfected at 10 or 100 nM using Lipofectamine™ RNAiMAX Reagent (Invitrogen, Cat. #13778-100) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

For the detection of AAV genomic ssDNA using the sensor, HEK293T cells were seeded in 96-well tissue culture-treated plates. Once the cells reached approximately 50% confluency, they were transfected with AAV packaging plasmids, including 38 ng of a cis-plasmid containing an ITR-flanked BFP-encoding sequence with the sensor target site under the EF1A promoter (VectorBuilder), 38 ng of a Rep/Cap plasmid (Addgene #104963), and 45 ng of an AAV helper plasmid (Addgene #112867). After 34 h, the media was changed, and cells were transfected with 50 ng of ssDNA sensor. 24 h after sensor transfection, cells were processed according to the protocol described in the Luciferase Assays section.

Cell line generation

HEK293T cells were seeded in 6-well TC-treated plates and cultured until approximately 75% confluency before transfection. Cells were transfected with 2.5 µg of the cloned piggyBac plasmids (as described above) and 1 µg of Super piggyBac Transposase plasmid (System Biosciences, PB210PA-1) using Lipofectamine 3000. Forty-eight hours post-transfection, selection was initiated by adding 2 µg/mL puromycin and 5 µg/mL blasticidin to the culture medium, depending on the resistance markers encoded by the plasmids. Cells were maintained under these selective conditions for two weeks to ensure stable integration and selection of transfected cells.

For the generation of fluorescent HEK293T cell lines, the polyclonal selected populations were further purified by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) to isolate BFP+ or mCherry+ cells and remove non-fluorescent cells.

The HEK293T-GFP cell line was generated via homology-independent targeted integration (HITI31) as previously described32, with minor modifications. GFP donor templates were used to achieve targeted genomic integration. GFP-positive cells were isolated using a BD FACSAria™ cell sorter and maintained in Gibco™ DMEM (high glucose, with pyruvate) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin.

Luciferase assays

To measure luciferase activity, we used the NanoLuc (Nluc) luciferase reporter system (Promega). At 24 or 48 h post-transfection (as specified in the figure legends), cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and the Nano-Glo Luciferase Assay (Promega) was applied directly to the cells according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Lysates were transferred to black 96-well plates with clear bottoms (Costar 3603, Corning), and luminescence signals were measured using a BioTek Synergy H1 microplate reader and Gen5 v2.01.14 software.

Fluorescence microscopy

Fluorescent signals within the cells were detected and analyzed using the FLoid™ Cell Imaging Station (Life Technologies).

Fluorescence measurement of GFP expression

Intracellular GFP expression in the ssDNA stability and SONAR translation assays was measured in cells plated in black, 96-well plates with clear bottoms (Costar 3603, Corning) and cultured in FluoroBrite medium (as described above). Fluorescence was recorded using a BioTek Synergy H1 microplate reader and Gen5 software (v2.01.14) at an excitation wavelength of 485 nm and an emission wavelength of 535 nm.

Flow cytometry analysis and cell sorting

Forty-eight hours post SONAR transfection (described above), cells were harvested by removing the media, washed once with PBS, and then trypsinized using TrypLE Express (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 12604013) for 3 min. Resuspended cells were pelleted via centrifugation for 5 min at 500 g. After centrifugation, cell pellets were resuspended in PBS with 2% FBS and transferred into 5 ml round-bottom polystyrene flow tubes with a cell strainer (Corning Life Sciences, 352235). Cells were analyzed by flow cytometry (Symphony A1, BD Biosciences) and data were collected using BD FACSDiva software and processed with FlowJoTM v10 software.

For fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS), cells were prepared as described above. Cells were sorted based on the indicated fluorescence gates, and single cells were deposited directly into 96-well plates pre-filled with 100 µL of complete growth medium per well.

Plate-reader fluorescence quantification of BFP/mCherry cell line identity

Following 2–3 weeks of incubation after GFP⁺ single-cell sorting, the media was removed, and the cells were washed once with PBS and dissociated using TrypLE Express for 3min. Cells were resuspended in FluoroBrite medium and transferred to black, 96-well plates with clear bottoms. All wells were processed in parallel using the same handling and transfer procedure across the entire 96-well plate, without manual pre-selection of wells. Fluorescence was recorded using a BioTek Synergy H1 microplate reader and Gen5 software (v2.01.14) using a double-read acquisition, in which each well was measured sequentially in two channels corresponding to cell line markers: BFP (Ex/Em 402/456 nm) and mCherry (Ex/Em 587/620 nm). The paired BFP and mCherry measurements per well were used to assign cell line identity as described below.

Quantitative PCR analysis

Twelve hours after ASO transfection, both RNA and ssDNA were extracted using the RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (QIAGEN, 74134). Following extraction, samples were treated with RNase A (NEB, T3018) for 15 min, followed by ezDNase treatment (Invitrogen, 117660512) for 4 min. ssDNA was then purified and concentrated using Monarch® Spin PCR & DNA Cleanup Kit (NEB, T1130). In total, 2 µL of each sample was prepared for quantitative PCR (qPCR) using the Luna® Universal qPCR Master Mix (NEB, M3003). qPCR was conducted on a Roche LightCycler® 96 platform with the following thermal cycling protocol: an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 60 s, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s and extension at 60 °C for 30 s, and culminating with a melt curve analysis from 60 °C to 95 °C. Cycle quantification (Cq) values were automatically calculated using the Roche LightCycler® 96 software. All qPCR reactions were performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility and reliability of the results.

RT-qPCR analysis

For RNA quantification experiments, total RNA was extracted either 24 h after target plasmid transfection (for varying target concentration experiments) or 48 h after SONAR transfection, using the RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (QIAGEN, 74134). From each sample, 100 ng of total RNA was used for quantitative PCR with the Luna® Universal One-Step RT-qPCR Master Mix (NEB, E3005). RT-qPCR was performed on a Roche LightCycler® 96 platform following the same cycling conditions described above, except that samples were first subjected to ezDNase treatment at 37 °C for 2 min and cDNA synthesis at 55 °C for 10 min prior to amplification. Cq values were automatically calculated by the Roche LightCycler® 96 software.

For the SONAR–RT-qPCR correlation analysis, target abundance at each plasmid-input concentration was quantified as 2−ΔCt, where ΔCt = Cttarget − Ct ACTB, and ACTB served as the reference housekeeping gene. Mean 2−ΔCt values across biological replicates at each concentration were then correlated with the corresponding mean raw SONAR NanoLuc measurements obtained at the same input concentrations. Pearson correlation and linear regression were calculated across these paired concentration points.

PCR amplification of the splint-ligated ssDNA sensor

Cells were transfected with varying concentrations of the ssDNA-GFP sensor, with or without the specific splint DNA, as described above. Genomic DNA was collected 24 h post-transfection by removing the medium, washing the cells once with PBS, and resuspending them in 100 µL of QuickExtract™ DNA Extraction Solution (Biosearch Technologies, QE09050). The lysate was incubated at 66 °C for 6 min followed by 98 °C for 2 min to complete the extraction. Subsequently, 2.5 µL of the extracted genomic DNA was used as input in 50 µL PCR reactions using Q5 Hot Start High-Fidelity 2× Master Mix (NEB). Gel images were taken using the Gel Doc XR System and Image Lab Analysis Software (Bio-Rad).

TLR9 reporter assay

The HEK-Blue™ hTLR9 reporter assay (InvivoGen, hkb-htlr9) was used to assess the immunogenicity of SONAR ssDNA according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, HEK-Blue cells (HEK293T-derived cells expressing human TLR9 and a SEAP reporter gene) were seeded at 4 × 10⁴ cells per well in 96-well plates and grown to approximately 70% confluency. Cells were then transfected with varying concentrations of ssDNA, as indicated. As a positive control, cells were transfected with different concentrations of the TLR9 agonist ODN2006 (InvivoGen, tlrl-2006). After ~20 h, TLR9 activation was quantified using QUANTI-Blue™ SEAP Detection Reagent (InvivoGen, rep-qbs) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance at 650 nm was measured using a BioTek Synergy H1 microplate reader and Gen5 software (v2.01.14).

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (v10.6.0). Microsoft Excel (v16.89.1) was used for data organization and simple calculations. Quantitative data are presented as mean ± s.d., and n refers to biological replicates as defined in the figure legends. All statistical tests were two-tailed. Data were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and for variance equality using the F-test or Brown–Forsythe test, where appropriate. Statistical significance was determined using unpaired t-tests or one-way ANOVA followed by the indicated post-hoc multiple-comparisons tests, as specified in the figure legends. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. Sample sizes were chosen on the basis of standard practice for transient cell-based reporter, qPCR, fluorescence, and flow-cytometry assays, and to assess reproducibility across independent biological replicates. Most quantitative experiments were performed with n = 3 independent biological replicates, unless otherwise indicated in the figure legends, with selected supportive assays performed with n = 4 or n = 5 biological replicates. These sample sizes were sufficient to assess reproducibility and detect consistent differences between experimental and control conditions in the assays performed. No data were excluded from the analyses. The experiments were not randomized. The Investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment.
