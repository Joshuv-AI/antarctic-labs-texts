The Yang-Baxter equation, Leibniz algebras, racks and related algebraic structures
Nanyan Xu, Yunhe Sheng

Abstract
. The purpose of this paper is to clarify the relations between various constructions of solutions of the Yang-Baxter equation from Leibniz algebras, racks, 3-Leibniz algebras, 3-racks, linear racks, trilinear racks, and give new constructions of solutions of the Yang-Baxter equation. First we show that a 3-Leibniz algebra naturally gives rise to a 3-rack on the underlying vector space, which generalizes Kinyon’s construction of racks from Leibniz algebras. Then we show that a trilinear rack naturally gives rise to a linear rack. Combined with Lebed’s construction of solutions of the Yang-Baxter equation from linear racks, our results give an intrinsic explanation of Abramov and Zappala’s construction of solutions of the Yang-Baxter equation from trilinear racks. Next we show that a 3-Leibniz algebra gives rise to a trilinear rack, which generalizes Abramov and Zappala’s construction from 3-Lie algebras. Finally, we construct solutions of the Yang-Baxter equation using central extensions of 3-Leibniz algebras and Leibniz algebras. In particular, given a 3-Leibniz algebra, there are two different approaches to construct solutions of the Yang-Baxter equation, namely either consider the central extension of the Leibniz algebra on the fundamental objects, or consider the Leibniz algebra on the fundamental objects of the central extension of the 3-Leibniz algebra. We also show that there is a homomorphism between the corresponding solutions.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
The Yang-Baxter equation

In 1967, Yang discovered the Yang-Baxter equation as the consistency condition for the factorization in the quantum mechanical many-body problem
[
33
]
. At the same time, the Yang-Baxter equation also appeared in Baxter’s work of eight-vertex model
[
8
]
. A solution of the Yang-Baxter equation on a vector space
V
is an invertible linear map
R:V\otimes V\rightarrow V\otimes V
satisfying

(1)

\displaystyle(R\otimes{\rm{Id}}_{V})({\rm{Id}}_{V}\otimes R)(R\otimes{\rm{Id}}_{V})=({\rm{Id}}_{V}\otimes R)(R\otimes{\rm{Id}}_{V})({\rm{Id}}_{V}\otimes R).

The Yang-Baxter equation is widely used in various areas of mathematics and physics, such as integrable systems, knot theory, statistical mechanics, quantum field theory, quantum computing, braided categories, etc.
FRT-construction, introduced by Faddeev, Reshetikhin and Takhtajan, provided a way to obtain some bialgebras by means of
R
-matrices, where
R
-matrices are viewed as matrix solutions of the Yang-Baxter equation
[
16
]
.
Jones proposed “baxterization” which shows the connection between representations of the braid group and solutions of the Yang-Baxter equation
[
20
]
.

In order to simplify the study of the Yang-Baxter equation, Drinfel’d proposed to consider the Yang-Baxter equation at the level of sets
[
12
]
. There are a lot of research results on the set-theoretical solutions of the Yang-Baxter equation. Etingof-Schedler-Soloviev
[
15
]
and Lu-Yan-Zhu
[
28
]
paid attention to the non-degenerate involutive set-theoretical solutions and studied the structure group, which is used to classify set-theoretical solutions. In 2006, Rump introduced the notion of braces, which are generalization of radical rings and give rise to non-degenerate involutive set-theoretical solutions of the Yang-Baxter equation
[
30
]
. Later, Guarnieri and Vendramin extended braces to the noncommutative setting by defining skew braces in order to obtain non-degenerate non-involutive set-theoretical solutions
[
18
]
. Bardakov and Gubarev obtained non-degenerate set-theoretical solutions of the Yang-Baxter equation using the connection between Rota-Baxter groups
[
19
]
and skew-left braces
[
7
]
. In
[
5
]
, Bai, Guo, Sheng and Tang introduced the notion of post-groups, which are equivalent to skew left braces, and give rise to set-theoretical solutions of the Yang-Baxter equation.

1.2.
Racks, Leibniz algebras, 3-racks and 3-Leibniz algebras

Set-theoretical solutions of the Yang-Baxter equation are closely related to many algebraic structures as mentioned above. It is worth noting that there is another algebraic structure, named rack, which also plays an important role in the study of the Yang-Baxter equation. As an algebraic interpretation of Reidemeister moves, a
rack
is a set
X
with a binary operator
\lhd:X\times X\to X
satisfying

•

self-distributive law:
(x\lhd y)\lhd z=(x\lhd z)\lhd(y\lhd z),\,\,\,\forall x,y,z\in X
,

•

for all
x\in X
, the map
\cdot\lhd x:X\rightarrow X
sending
y\in X
to
y\lhd x
is a bijection.

If the binary operation
\lhd
only satisfies the self-distributive law, then
(X,\lhd)
is called a
shelf
.
Racks are connected with the theories of groups, group presentations and crossed modules, and open the practical possibility of finding a complete sequence of computable invariants for framed links and for 3-manifolds. Many examples of racks are listed in
[
17
]
. In
[
3
]
, Andruskiewitsch and Gra
{\rm\tilde{n}}
a proved that there is a one-to-one corresponding between racks and certain set-theoretical solutions of the Yang-Baxter equation (Theorem
2.21
). Biyogmam generalized racks to
n
-racks and gave some examples in
[
9
]
. In this paper, we consider the case
n=3
, i.e.
3
-racks. One can construct a
3
-rack
(X,T^{\lhd})
by a rack
(X,\lhd)

[
13
]

and one can also obtain a rack
(X\times X,\lhd^{T})
from a
3
-rack
(X,T)

[
14
]
.

Racks are closely related to Leibniz algebras, which were introduced by Loday in his study of algebraic K-theory
[
26
]
. Specially, a
Leibniz algebra
is a vector space
\mathcal{E}
together with a bilinear operation
[\cdot,\cdot]_{\mathcal{E}}
such that

\displaystyle[[x,y]_{\mathcal{E}},z]_{\mathcal{E}}=[[x,z]_{\mathcal{E}},y]_{\mathcal{E}}+[x,[y,z]_{\mathcal{E}}]_{\mathcal{E}},\,\,\,\forall x,y,z\in\mathcal{E}.

In
[
21
]
, Kinyon proved that a Leibniz algebra
(\mathcal{E},[\cdot,\cdot]_{\mathcal{E}})
gives rise to a rack structure
\lhd
on
\mathcal{E}
via
x\lhd y=\exp(\mathrm{ad}^{R}_{y})(x)
(Theorem
2.14
).

Furthermore, Casas, Loday and Pirashvili defined
n
-Leibniz algebras and proved that an
n
-Leibniz algebra gives rise to a Leibniz algebra structure on the set of fundamental objects
[
11
]
. Biyogmam proved that the tangent space of a Lie
n
-rack at the neutral element has an
n
-Leibniz algebra structure
[
9
]
. Since
3
-Leibniz algebras and
3
-racks are generalizations of Leibniz algebras and racks respectively, the following question naturally arises:

Q:

Is there a
3
-rack structure on a
3
-Leibniz algebra as a generalization of Kinyon’s result?

On the other hand, Lebed showed that a central Leibniz algebra
(\mathcal{E},[\cdot,\cdot]_{\mathcal{E}},{\bf 1})
gives rise to a solution of the Yang-Baxter equation via the following formula
[
24
]
:

\displaystyle R^{Lei}(x\otimes y)=y\otimes x+{\bf 1}\otimes[x,y]_{\mathcal{E}},\,\,\,\forall x,y\in\mathcal{E},

Due to the close relationship between Leibniz algebras and
3
-Leibniz algebras, it is natural to consider the following question:

Q:

Can
3
-Leibniz algebras give rise to solutions of the Yang-Baxter equation?

1.3.
Linear racks and trilinear racks

Kr
{\rm\ddot{a}}
hmer and Wagemann defined linear shelfs as a coalgebraic version of shelfs
[
22
]
. Similar definition has been proposed in
[
10
]
and
[
23
]
. In this paper, we use linear racks to express the coalgebraic version of racks so that the relationship between linear racks and linear shelfs is similar to the relationship between racks and shelfs.
Abramov and Zappala generalized linear racks to reversible TSD, which we call trilinear racks in this article. They showed that trilinear racks can be used to construct solutions of the Yang-Baxter equation
[
1
]
. On the other hand, Lebed showed that linear racks give rise to solutions of the Yang-Baxter equation in
[
23
]
. However, the relation between the above two approaches of constructing solutions of the Yang-Baxter equation is not clear. Since at the level of sets, there is a passage from
3
-racks to racks, so it is natural to consider the following question:

Q:

Is there a passage from trilinear racks to linear racks as a coalgebraic version of the passage from
3
-racks to racks? If so, is the construction of solutions of the Yang-Baxter equation from trilinear racks given in
[
1
]
is consistent with the one given in
[
23
]
?

In
[
1
]
, the authors also construct trilinear racks from
3
-Lie algebras. While Lebed show that one can construct a linear rack from a Leibniz algebra in
[
23
]
. Since
3
-Leibniz algebras are natural generalizations of Leibniz algebras, so we propose the following question:

Q:

Whether trilinear rack structures can be constructed using
3
-Leibniz algebras?

1.4.
Main results and outline of the paper

The purpose of this paper is to solve the questions proposed above, and clarify the relations between various constructions of solutions of the Yang-Baxter equation. First we show that a 3-Leibniz algebra naturally gives rise to a 3-rack structure on the underlying vector space, which generalizes Kinyon’s construction of racks from Leibniz algebras given in
[
21
]
. Therefore, there are two approaches to obtain a rack from a 3-Leibniz algebra, and we show that there is a rack morphism between those two racks, which is a map preserves rack structure. If we further consider the corresponding solutions of the Yang-Baxter equation, there is also a homomorphism between solutions. 2.
A passage from 3-Leibniz algebras to 3-racks

In this section, we construct a
3
-rack structure on a
3
-Leibniz algebra, which generalizes Kinyon’s result in
[
21
]
. Consequently, a 3-Leibniz algebra
\mathcal{L}
gives rise to two rack structures on
\mathcal{L}\times\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{L}\otimes\mathcal{L}
respectively. We also show that there is a homomorphism between the corresponding set-theoretical solutions of the Yang-Baxter equation on
\mathcal{L}\times\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{L}\otimes\mathcal{L}
.

Definition 2.1
.

[
11
]

A right
3
-Leibniz algebra
is a vector space
\mathcal{L}
with a linear map
[\cdot,\cdot,\cdot]_{\mathcal{L}}:\mathcal{L}\otimes\mathcal{L}\otimes\mathcal{L}\rightarrow\mathcal{L}
such that for any
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},y_{1},y_{2}\in\mathcal{L}
, the following identity holds:

(5)

\displaystyle[[x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}]_{\mathcal{L}},y_{1},y_{2}]_{\mathcal{L}}=[[x_{1},y_{1},y_{2}]_{\mathcal{L}},x_{2},x_{3}]_{\mathcal{L}}+[x_{1},[x_{2},y_{1},y_{2}]_{\mathcal{L}},x_{3}]_{\mathcal{L}}+[x_{1},x_{2},[x_{3},y_{1},y_{2}]_{\mathcal{L}}]_{\mathcal{L}}.

In addition, if
[\cdot,\cdot,\cdot]_{\mathcal{L}}
is skew-symmetric, then
(\mathcal{L},[\cdot,\cdot,\cdot]_{\mathcal{L}})
is a
3
-Lie algebra.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
(\mathcal{L}_{1},[\cdot,\cdot,\cdot]_{\mathcal{L}_{1}})
and
(\mathcal{L}_{2},[\cdot,\cdot,\cdot]_{\mathcal{L}_{2}})
be two
3
-Leibniz algebras. A linear map
f:\mathcal{L}_{1}\rightarrow\mathcal{L}_{2}
is a
3
-Leibniz algebra homomorphism
if

\displaystyle f[x,y,z]_{\mathcal{L}_{1}}=[f(x),f(y),f(z)]_{\mathcal{L}_{2}},\,\,\,\forall x,y,z\in\mathcal{L}_{1}.

Moreover,
f
is a
3
-Leibniz algebra isomorphism
if
f
is bijective.

Remark 2.3
.

It is obvious that, given a right
3
-Leibniz algebra
(\mathcal{L},[\cdot,\cdot,\cdot]_{\mathcal{L}})
, then
(\mathcal{L},[\cdot,\cdot,\cdot]^{\prime}_{\mathcal{L}})
is a left
3
-Leibniz algebra, where
[x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}]^{\prime}_{\mathcal{L}}=[x_{3},x_{2},x_{1}]_{\mathcal{L}}
for all
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\in\mathcal{L}
. In this paper, we only consider right

-Leibniz algebras.

Let

.

Definition 2.15

Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}[x,y,z]_{\mathcal{L}}=[\mathcal{D}x,y,z]_{\mathcal{L}}+[x,\mathcal{D}y,z]_{\mathcal{L}}+[x,y,\mathcal{D}z]_{\mathcal{L}},\,\,\,\forall x,y,z\in\mathcal{L}.

.

Let

\displaystyle\mathrm{ad}^{R}_{x_{1},x_{2}}(y)=[y,x_{1},x_{2}]_{\mathcal{L}},\,\,\,\forall y\in\mathcal{L}.

Lemma 2.17
.

Let
(\mathcal{L},[\cdot,\cdot,\cdot]_{\mathcal{L}})
be a
3
-Leibniz algebra and
\mathcal{D}:\mathcal{L}\rightarrow\mathcal{L}
a derivation. Then
\exp(\mathcal{D})=\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{k!}\mathcal{D}^{k}
is a
3
-Leibniz algebra isomorphism of
\mathcal{L}
.

Proof.

For any
x,y,z\in\mathcal{L},
the equality
\mathcal{D}[x,y,z]_{\mathcal{L}}=[\mathcal{D}x,y,z]_{\mathcal{L}}+[x,\mathcal{D}y,z]_{\mathcal{L}}+[x,y,\mathcal{D}z]_{\mathcal{L}}
leads by induction to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k!}\mathcal{D}^{k}[x,y,z]_{\mathcal{L}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1}+i_{2}+i_{3}=k\\
i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}\geqslant 0\end{subarray}}\Big[\frac{\mathcal{D}^{i_{1}}(x)}{i_{1}!},\frac{\mathcal{D}^{i_{2}}(y)}{i_{2}!},\frac{\mathcal{D}^{i_{3}}(z)}{i_{3}!}\Big]_{\mathcal{L}}.

Then we obtain

\displaystyle[\exp(\mathcal{D})(x),\exp(\mathcal{D})(y),\exp(\mathcal{D})(z)]_{\mathcal{L}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big[\sum_{i_{1}=0}^{+\infty}\frac{\mathcal{D}^{i_{1}}(x)}{i_{1}!},\sum_{i_{2}=0}^{+\infty}\frac{\mathcal{D}^{i_{2}}(y)}{i_{2}!},\sum_{i_{3}=0}^{+\infty}\frac{\mathcal{D}^{i_{3}}(z)}{i_{3}!}\Big]_{\mathcal{L}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1}+i_{2}+i_{3}=k\\
i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}\geqslant 0\end{subarray}}\Big[\frac{\mathcal{D}^{i_{1}}(x)}{i_{1}!},\frac{\mathcal{D}^{i_{2}}(y)}{i_{2}!},\frac{\mathcal{D}^{i_{3}}(z)}{i_{3}!}\Big]_{\mathcal{L}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{k!}\mathcal{D}^{k}[x,y,z]_{\mathcal{L}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp(\mathcal{D})[x,y,z]_{\mathcal{L}}.

Obviously,
\exp(-\mathcal{D})
is the inverse of
\exp(\mathcal{D})
. Thus,
\exp(\mathcal{D})
is an isomorphism.
∎

Lemma 2.18
.

Let
(\mathcal{L},[\cdot,\cdot,\cdot]_{\mathcal{L}})

\alpha:\mathcal{L}\rightarrow\mathcal{L}

Proof.

For any
z\in\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle(\alpha\circ\mathrm{ad}^{R}_{x,y}\circ\alpha^{-1})(z)=\alpha[\alpha^{-1}(z),x,y]_{\mathcal{L}}=[z,\alpha(x),\alpha(y)]_{\mathcal{L}}=\mathrm{ad}^{R}_{\alpha(x),\alpha(y)}(z).

Since
\mathcal{L}
is finite-dimensional, we have

\displaystyle\alpha\circ\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{(\mathrm{ad}^{R}_{x,y})^{k}}{k!}\circ\alpha^{-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{\alpha\circ(\mathrm{ad}^{R}_{x,y})^{k}\circ\alpha^{-1}}{k!}=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{(\alpha\circ\mathrm{ad}^{R}_{x,y}\circ\alpha^{-1})^{k}}{k!}=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{(\mathrm{ad}^{R}_{\alpha(x),\alpha(y)})^{k}}{k!}.

Notice that composition with
\alpha
is continuous, thus we have

\displaystyle\alpha\circ\exp(\mathrm{ad}^{R}_{x,y})\circ\alpha^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha\circ\sum_{k=0}^{{+\infty}}\frac{(\mathrm{ad}^{R}_{x,y})^{k}}{k!}\circ\alpha^{-1}=\alpha\circ\lim_{N\rightarrow{+\infty}}\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{(\mathrm{ad}^{R}_{x,y})^{k}}{k!}\circ\alpha^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{N\rightarrow{+\infty}}\alpha\circ\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{(\mathrm{ad}^{R}_{x,y})^{k}}{k!}\circ\alpha^{-1}=\lim_{N\rightarrow{+\infty}}\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{(\mathrm{ad}^{R}_{\alpha(x),\alpha(y)})^{k}}{k!}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\frac{(\mathrm{ad}^{R}_{\alpha(x),\alpha(y)})^{k}}{k!}=\exp(\mathrm{ad}^{R}_{\alpha(x),\alpha(y)}),

which finishes the proof.∎

5.
Central extensions of (3-)Leibniz algebras and the Yang-Baxter equation

Recall that a central Leibniz algebra gives rise to a solution of the Yang-Baxter equation (Theorem
3.9
). In this section, first we use central extensions of Leibniz algebras to construct solutions of the Yang-Baxter equation. Then we prove that a
3
-Leibniz algebra
\mathcal{L}
gives rise to two central Leibniz algebras, which are
(\mathbb{K}\oplus\mathcal{L})\otimes(\mathbb{K}\oplus\mathcal{L})
and
\mathbb{K}\oplus(\mathcal{L}\otimes\mathcal{L})
respectively. We show that there is a homomorphism between the corresponding solutions of the Yang-Baxter equation.

5.1.
Leibniz algebras and the Yang-Baxter equation

A central Leibniz algebra
(\mathcal{E},[\cdot,\cdot]_{\mathcal{E}},{\bf 1})
can be viewed as a central extension of Leibniz algebras. Recall that a central extension of a Leibniz algebra
(\mathcal{E},[\cdot,\cdot]_{\mathcal{E}})
is a short exact sequence of Leibniz algebras

0\longrightarrow\mathbb{K}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i}}{{\longrightarrow}}\hat{\mathcal{E}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{E}\longrightarrow 0,

such that
{i}(\mathbb{K})
is in the center of
\hat{\mathcal{E}}
. Then we can see that a central Leibniz algebra
(\mathcal{E},[\cdot,\cdot]_{\mathcal{E}},{\bf 1})
gives rise to a central extension of
\mathcal{E}/{\mathbb{K}{\bf 1}}
:

0\longrightarrow\mathbb{K}{\bf 1}\longrightarrow\mathcal{E}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{E}/{\mathbb{K}{\bf 1}}}\longrightarrow 0.

Two such extensions

\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{1}
and
\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{2}
of a Leibniz algebra
(\mathcal{E},[\cdot,\cdot]_{\mathcal{E}})
by
\mathbb{K}
are isomorphic if there exists a Leibniz algebra homomorphism
\theta
from
\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{1}
to
\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{2}
such that

\theta\circ i_{1}=i_{2},\,\,\,p_{2}\circ\theta=p_{1}.

It is well known that central extensions of a Leibniz algebra
(\mathcal{E},[\cdot,\cdot]_{\mathcal{E}})
by
\mathbb{K}
are classified by the second cohomology group
\mathcal{H}^{2}(\mathcal{E};\mathbb{K})
of the Leibniz algebra
(\mathcal{E},[\cdot,\cdot]_{\mathcal{E}})
with coefficients in the trivial representations
[
27
]
. In fact, suppose that
s:\mathcal{E}\rightarrow\hat{\mathcal{E}}
is a linear map satisfying
p\circ s={\rm{Id}}_{\mathcal{E}}
, then
\hat{\mathcal{E}}
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{K}\oplus\mathcal{E}
as vector space by
F(X)=(X-sp(X),p(X))
for all
X\in\hat{\mathcal{E}}
and
F^{-1}(a,x)=a+s(x)
for all
(a,x)\in\mathbb{K}\oplus\mathcal{E}
. Define a linear map
\omega:\mathcal{E}\otimes\mathcal{E}\rightarrow\mathbb{K}
as follows:

\displaystyle\omega(x,y)=[s(x),s(y)]_{\hat{\mathcal{E}}}-s[x,y]_{\mathcal{E}},\,\,\,\forall x,y\in\mathcal{E}.

Then we obtain a bilinear map
[\cdot,\cdot]_{\omega}
on
\mathbb{K}\oplus\mathcal{E}
by

(22)

\displaystyle[(a,x),(b,y)]_{\omega}=F[F^{-1}(a,x),F^{-1}(b,y)]_{\hat{\mathcal{E}}}=(\omega(x,y),[x,y]_{\mathcal{E}}).

(\mathbb{K}\oplus\mathcal{E},[\cdot,\cdot]_{\omega})
is a Leibniz algebra if and only if
\omega
is a 2-cocycle with coefficients in the trivial representation
\mathbb{K}
. Moreover,
[\omega]\in\mathcal{H}^{2}(\mathcal{E};\mathbb{K})
is independent of the choice of
s
.

Since any central extension of
\mathcal{E}
by
\mathbb{K}
is isomorphic to
(\mathbb{K}\oplus\mathcal{E},[\cdot,\cdot]_{\omega})
, then we only consider central extensions of the form
(\mathbb{K}\oplus\mathcal{E},[\cdot,\cdot]_{\omega})
in the sequel.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
(\mathcal{E},[\cdot,\cdot]_{\mathcal{E}})
be a Leibniz algebra and
(\mathbb{K}\oplus\mathcal{E},[\cdot,\cdot]_{\omega})
a central extension of
\mathcal{E}
by
\mathbb{K}
. Then the linear map

defined as follows is a solution of the Yang-Baxter equation:

(23)

\displaystyle R^{Lei}((a,x)\otimes(b,y))=(b,y)\otimes(a,x)+(1_{\mathbb{K}},0)\otimes(\omega(x,y),[x,y]_{\mathcal{E}}).

Moreover, solutions induced by isomorphic central extensions of
\mathcal{E}
are equivalent.

Proof.

It is obvious that
(1_{\mathbb{K}},0)
is a central element of the Leibniz algebra
\mathbb{K}\oplus\mathcal{E}
, then we obtain a solution of the Yang-Baxter equation
R^{Lei}:(\mathbb{K}\oplus\mathcal{E})\otimes(\mathbb{K}\oplus\mathcal{E})\rightarrow(\mathbb{K}\oplus\mathcal{E})\otimes(\mathbb{K}\oplus\mathcal{E})

given by (
15

\displaystyle R^{Lei}((a,x)\otimes(b,y))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(b,y)\otimes(a,x)+(1_{\mathbb{K}},0)\otimes[(a,x),(b,y)]_{\omega}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(b,y)\otimes(a,x)+(1_{\mathbb{K}},0)\otimes(\omega(x,y),[x,y]_{\mathcal{E}}).

Let

be the Leibniz algebra homomorphism such that two central extensions
(\mathbb{K}\oplus\mathcal{E},[\cdot,\cdot]_{\omega_{1}})
and

are isomorphic. Then we have
\theta(a,x)=(a-f(x),x)
, where

such that
\omega_{2}-\omega_{1}=\partial f
, where

\displaystyle(\partial f)(x_{1},\cdots,x_{k+1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq k+1}(-1)^{j+1}f(x_{1},\cdots,x_{i-1},[x_{i},x_{j}]_{\mathcal{E}},x_{i+1},\cdots,\hat{x_{j}},\cdots,x_{k+1}).

Denote by
R^{Lei}_{1}
and
R^{Lei}_{2}
the corresponding solutions of the Yang-Baxter equation induced by central Leibniz algebras
(\mathbb{K}\oplus\mathcal{E},[\cdot,\cdot]_{\omega_{1}})
and
(\mathbb{K}\oplus\mathcal{E},[\cdot,\cdot]_{\omega_{2}})
respectively. For all

, we have

\displaystyle(\theta\otimes\theta)R^{Lei}_{1}((a,x)\otimes(b,y))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\theta\otimes\theta)((b,y)\otimes(a,x)+(1_{\mathbb{K}},0)\otimes(\omega_{1}(x,y),[x,y]_{\mathcal{E}}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(b-f(y),y)\otimes(a-f(x),x)+(1_{\mathbb{K}},0)\otimes(\omega_{1}(x,y)-f[x,y]_{\mathcal{E}},[x,y]_{\mathcal{E}}),

\displaystyle R^{Lei}_{2}(\theta\otimes\theta)((a,x)\otimes(b,y))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R^{Lei}_{2}((a-f(x),x)\otimes(b-f(y),y))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(b-f(y),y)\otimes(a-f(x),x)+(1_{\mathbb{K}},0)\otimes(\omega_{2}(x,y),[x,y]_{\mathcal{E}}),

which implies that
(\theta\otimes\theta)R^{Lei}_{1}=R^{Lei}_{2}(\theta\otimes\theta)
, i.e.
R^{Lei}_{1}
and
R^{Lei}_{2}
are equivalent.
∎

Corollary 5.7
.

Let
(\mathcal{L},[\cdot,\cdot,\cdot]_{\mathcal{L}})
be a
3
-Leibniz algebra over
\mathbb{K}
.
Then we obtain a solution of the Yang-Baxter equation
R^{Lei}_{\mathds{K}\oplus(\mathcal{L}\otimes\mathcal{L})}
on
\mathds{K}\oplus(\mathcal{L}\otimes\mathcal{L})
given by

\displaystyle R^{Lei}_{\mathds{K}\oplus(\mathcal{L}\otimes\mathcal{L})}\Big((a,x_{1}\otimes x_{2})\otimes(b,y_{1}\otimes y_{2})\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(b,y_{1}\otimes y_{2})\otimes(a,x_{1}\otimes x_{2})+(1_{\mathds{K}},0)\otimes(0,[x_{1},y_{1},y_{2}]_{\mathcal{L}}\otimes x_{2}+x_{1}\otimes[x_{2},y_{1},y_{2}]_{\mathcal{L}}).

Proof.

By Proposition
2.6
,
(\mathcal{L}\otimes\mathcal{L},\{\cdot,\cdot\})
is a Leibniz algebra. Then
(\mathbb{K}\oplus(\mathcal{L}\otimes\mathcal{L}),[\cdot,\cdot]_{0},(1_{\mathbb{K}},0))
is a central extension of the Leibniz algebra
\mathcal{L}\otimes\mathcal{L}
, where
[\cdot,\cdot]_{0}
is given by (
22
) with
\omega=0
, that is, for any
(a,x_{1}\otimes x_{2})
,
(b,y_{1}\otimes y_{2})\in\mathds{K}\oplus(\mathcal{L}\otimes\mathcal{L})
,

\displaystyle[(a,x_{1}\otimes x_{2}),(b,y_{1}\otimes y_{2})]_{0}=(0,\{x_{1}\otimes x_{2},y_{1}\otimes y_{2}\})=(0,[x_{1},y_{1},y_{2}]_{\mathcal{L}}\otimes x_{2}+x_{1}\otimes[x_{2},y_{1},y_{2}]_{\mathcal{L}}).

Then by Theorem
3.9
, there is a solution of the Yang-Baxter equation as follows:

\displaystyle R^{Lei}_{\mathds{K}\oplus(\mathcal{L}\otimes\mathcal{L})}\Big((a,x_{1}\otimes x_{2})\otimes(b,y_{1}\otimes y_{2})\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(b,y_{1}\otimes y_{2})\otimes(a,x_{1}\otimes x_{2})+(1_{\mathbb{K}},0)\otimes[(a,x_{1}\otimes(b,y_{1}\otimes y_{2})),y]_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(b,y_{1}\otimes y_{2})\otimes(a,x_{1}\otimes x_{2})+(1_{\mathbb{K}},0)\otimes(0,[x_{1},y_{1},y_{2}]_{\mathcal{L}}\otimes x_{2}+x_{1}\otimes[x_{2},y_{1},y_{2}]_{\mathcal{L}}).\quad\quad\qed

Define an injective map

\mathfrak{s}:\mathds{K}\oplus(\mathcal{L}\otimes\mathcal{L})\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{L}}\otimes\overline{\mathcal{L}}
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{s}(a,x\otimes y)=a(1_{\mathds{K}},0)\otimes(1_{\mathds{K}},0)+(0,x)\otimes(0,y),\,\,\,\forall(a,x\otimes y)\in\mathds{K}\oplus(\mathcal{L}\otimes\mathcal{L}).

For any
(a,x_{1}\otimes x_{2})
,
(b,y_{1}\otimes y_{2})\in\mathds{K}\oplus(\mathcal{L}\otimes\mathcal{L})
, we have

\displaystyle\mathfrak{s}[(a,x_{1}\otimes x_{2}),(b,y_{1}\otimes y_{2})]_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathfrak{s}(0,[x_{1},y_{1},y_{2}]_{\mathcal{L}}\otimes x_{2}+x_{1}\otimes[x_{2},y_{1},y_{2}]_{\mathcal{L}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(0,[x_{1},y_{1},y_{2}]_{\mathcal{L}})\otimes(0,x_{2})+(0,x_{1})\otimes(0,[x_{2},y_{1},y_{2}]_{\mathcal{L}}),

\displaystyle\{\mathfrak{s}(a,x_{1}\otimes x_{2}),\mathfrak{s}(b,y_{1}\otimes y_{2})\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{a(1_{\mathds{K}},0)\otimes(1_{\mathds{K}},0)+(0,x_{1})\otimes(0,x_{2}),b(1_{\mathds{K}},0)\otimes(1_{\mathds{K}},0)+(0,y_{1})\otimes(0,y_{2})\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[(0,x_{1}),(0,y_{1}),(0,y_{2})]_{\overline{\mathcal{L}}}\otimes(0,x_{2})+(0,x_{1})\otimes[(0,x_{2}),(0,y_{1}),(0,y_{2})]_{\overline{\mathcal{L}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(0,[x_{1},y_{1},y_{2}]_{\mathcal{L}})\otimes(0,x_{2})+(0,x_{1})\otimes(0,[x_{2},y_{1},y_{2}]_{\mathcal{L}}),

which implies that
\mathfrak{s}[(a,x_{1}\otimes x_{2}),(b,y_{1}\otimes y_{2})]_{0}=\{\mathfrak{s}(a,x_{1}\otimes x_{2}),\mathfrak{s}(b,y_{1}\otimes y_{2})\}
. Therefore,
(\mathds{K}\oplus(\mathcal{L}\otimes\mathcal{L}),[\cdot,\cdot]_{0})
is a subalgebra of
(\overline{\mathcal{L}}\otimes\overline{\mathcal{L}},\{\cdot,\cdot\})
.
