Vascular closure devices in electrophysiology procedures: Systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Leonardo M. Knijnik, Birju R. Rao, Soroosh Kiani, David B. DeLurgio, Michael S. Lloyd
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVascular closure devices (VCDs) have been shown to reduce the time to hemostasis in single-point arterial access sites, but their benefit in electrophysiology (EP) procedures requiring multipoint venous access remains uncertain.ObjectiveThis study aimed to perform a systematic review and meta-analysis evaluating the efficacy and safety of VCDs compared with manual compression or figure-of-8 suture for venous hemostasis after EP procedures.MethodsA systematic search of PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar was conducted in June 2025 to identify randomized controlled trials comparing VCDs with manual or figure-of-8 closure in EP procedures.Results6 randomized controlled trials including 848 patients (460 treated with VCDs) were analyzed. VCDs significantly reduced hematoma formation (3.4% vs 7.7%; odds ratio, 0.51; 95% confidence interval, 0.25–0.92; P = .03) and a composite outcome of all vascular complications (10.4% vs 17.2%; odds ratio, 0.70; 95% confidence interval, 0.50–0.98; P = .04). Times to hemostasis, ambulation, and disposition were significantly shorter in the VCD group by 5.5, 129, and 84 minutes, respectively.ConclusionVCDs modestly reduce vascular complications and significantly shorten recovery time after EP procedures.

Key Findings ▪In our meta-analysis, vascular closure devices reduced the rate of hematoma and total vascular complications,▪Time to ambulation, discharge, and disposition were also lower in the vascular closure arm.▪The cost-effectiveness of vascular closure devices remains uncertain.▪Further head-to-head trials comparing different vascular closure devices are needed.

Introduction

Cardiac ablations for the treatment of arrhythmias are now a cornerstone of cardiology, with an estimated 1,115,550 cardiac ablations worldwide in 2019.1 The annual rate of ablations is more than 0.5% among Medicare and Medicaid beneficiaries.2 Hematoma remains an important complication associated with ablations. In the National Cardiovascular Data Registry, hematoma complicated 0.35% of atrial fibrillation (AF) ablations.3

Vascular closure devices (VCDs) can potentially provide a strategy to achieve faster hemostasis for multiple venous sites than manual pressure. Various methods have been studied, including a suture-based device and resorbable expandable sealants, either collagen-based or polyethylene glycol, which close the track between the venotomy and the skin. These closure devices have demonstrated superiority to manual compression (MC) in arterial access in preventing complications and decreasing bedrest time.4,5 Their use in ablation procedures varies widely by center.6

Multiple randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing VCDs with MC or figure-of-8 (FO8) skin suture have been performed. These trials were individually small and underpowered for important but still relatively rare complications such as hematoma formation. Thus, we aimed to perform a systematic review and meta-analysis assessing the efficacy of VCDs for the prevention of femoral vein bleeding complications after electrophysiological (EP) procedures.

Materials and methods

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion in this meta-analysis was restricted to studies that met all the following eligibility criteria: (1) RCTs, (2) comparing VCDs with FO8 suture or MC, (3) in patients undergoing venous access for EP procedures, and (4) those reporting any of the outcomes of interest. We excluded studies with (1) no control group or (2) routine arterial access.

Search strategy and data extraction

We systematically searched PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar in June 2025 using the following Medical Subject Headings terms: venous closure device, VCD, venotomy, ablation, and RCT. 2 authors (L.K. and B.R.) independently performed a literature search and extracted the data after predefined search criteria and quality assessment. Disagreements were resolved by consensus and with the senior author’s (M.L) input. This research adhered to Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines; all included primary studies were conducted with institutional review board approval and obtained appropriate informed consent.

End points and subgroup analyses

The primary outcomes of interest were death, hematoma formation, vascular complications, deep venous thrombosis, need for adjuvant device use for hemostasis, time to ambulation, time to hemostasis, and time to disposition.

Quality assessment

Quality assessment was performed using the Risk of Bias 2 tool by the Cochrane Collaboration for assessing bias in randomized trials.7 Studies are scored as unclear, high, or low risk of bias in 5 domains: selection, performance, detection, attrition, and reporting. A funnel plot was used to interrogate for publication bias.

Statistical analysis

The systematic review and meta-analysis were performed in accordance with recommendations from the Cochrane Collaboration and the Updated Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis statement.8 Pooled treatment effects for binary end points were compared using odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Heterogeneity was examined using the I2 statistic. The DerSimonian random-effects model was used. Review Manager 5.4.1 (Nordic Cochrane Centre, The Cochrane Collaboration, Copenhagen, Denmark) was used for statistical analysis. Subgroups were compared using the χ2 tests.

For continuous outcomes reported as medians with interquartile ranges, meta-analysis was performed using the median.difference package in R (version 4.3.2) based on the quantile estimation method developed by McGrath et al.9 This approach is appropriate for time-based outcomes, which are commonly right-skewed, and enables pooling of non-normally distributed data. A random-effects model was applied using restricted maximum likelihood estimation, and heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

The initial search yielded 477 results. After removal of duplicate records, 21 full articles were reviewed (Supplemental Figure 1). Of those, 15 were excluded owing to the wrong population, intervention, or trial design. 6 RCTs were included in the qualitative and quantitative review.10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16 The most common exclusion reasons were inadequate study design and study intervention. A total of 848 participants were included. VCDs were used in 460 patients (54%). Three studies used FO8 as the control group, 2 studies used MC, and 1 study used either of the 2 strategies.

The follow-up time was 30 days in all included studies. Clinical events

There were no deaths reported. There were no symptomatic deep vein thromboses (DVTs) reported. RELIASEAL performed a limited ultrasound substudy, and 3 asymptomatic DVTs were diagnosed. There were no emergency room visits reported. VCDs decreased the rate of hematoma (3.4% vs 7.7%; OR, 0.48; 95% CI, 0.25–0.92; P = .03; I2 = 0%) (Figure 1). The composite of all vascular complications of any severity was also lower in the VCD arm (10.4% vs 17.2%; OR, 0.64; 95% CI, 0.42–0.98; P = .04; I2 = 0%) (Figure 2). There was no difference in hematomas >6 cm (0.8% vs 1.1%; OR, 0.84; 95% CI, 0.22–3.24) (Supplemental Figure 2). Small hematomas (<6 cm) were less common in the VCD arm (3.0% vs 7.3%; OR, 0.47; 95% CI, 0.23–0.94; P = .03; I2 = 0%) (Supplemental Figure 3). Access site superficial bleeding was not different between groups, with elevated heterogeneity (4.8% vs 7.21%; OR, 0.72; 95% CI, 0.72–1.87; I2 = 64%) (Supplemental Figure 4). There were only 3 arteriovenous fistulas or pseudoaneurysms, with 1 in the VCD arm and 2 in the control arm. For hematomas, the RCT with the largest weight was STYLE-AF (48%).Figure 1Hematoma. Time to events

Four studies reported time-to-event data. The time to hemostasis was shorter in the VCD group (3.55 minutes vs 10.5 minutes; weighted pooled difference, 5.52 minutes less; 95% CI 3.15–7.89; P < .001; I2 = 72.23%) (Figure 3). The time to ambulation was also shorter in the VCD group (175 minutes vs 318 minutes; weighted pooled difference, 129 minutes; 95% CI, 177.46–80.57; P < .001; I2 = 74.4%) (Figure 4). The time to disposition was shorter in the VCD group (191.1 minutes vs 320.0 minutes; weighted pooled difference, 84.2 minutes less; 95% CI, 65.48–102.92; P < .0001; I2 = 0%) (Figure 5).Figure 3Time to hemostasis. Subgroup analysis

As part of an exploratory post hoc analysis, we analyzed all categorical outcomes, dividing studies according to their control arm (FO8 vs MC subgroups) and the type of device on the active arm (suture based vs sealant based). There were no statistically significant differences among those 4 subgroups on any outcome (Supplemental Figures 6–17).

Quality assessment

The RCT appraisal is presented in Supplemental Figure 18. All studies had adequate randomization and concealment of allocation, but all were open-label studies. The funnel plot analysis is presented in the Supplement. There was no apparent publication bias (Supplemental Figure 19).

Discussion

In this systematic review of 6 RCTs comparing VCDs with FO8 suture or MC, we found that VCDs reduced the rate of hematoma formation and reduced the times to hemostasis, ambulation, and disposition.

The observed absolute risk reduction was of 4.3%, translating into a number needed to treat of 24 patients to prevent 1 hematoma. Of note, there was no change in the rate of large (>6 cm) hematomas; thus, it is possible that mostly small hematomas were prevented. The decreased times to hemostasis, ambulation, and disposition of 5, 129, and 84 minutes, respectively, are clinically significant because longer bed rest times are associated with musculoskeletal and urinary discomfort, and decreased lengths of stay may increase EP laboratory throughput. This decision on throughput must be weighed against the costs of VCDs. Assuming that AF ablations would use 2 VCDs, a cost of $250 per VCD, and the observed number needed to treat of 24, there would roughly be a cost of $12,000 for every prevented hematoma and $5500 per hour for earlier time to ambulation. Given the lack of primary study-level cost data, this is to be considered illustrative. In addition, 1 of the included studies performed a downstream cost analysis and found no difference between groups.14

Mechanistically, VCDs may achieve hemostasis either directly (in a suture-based system) or indirectly (sealant based) by closing the venotomy and promoting quick thrombosis outside the vessel wall. With MC or FO8, hemostasis is achieved by the body’s natural thrombotic pathway; in that sense, they are similar to sealant-based VCDs, but likely take longer to form robust clots that are hemostatic.17 Further mechanistic studies are needed to better understand the precise microscopic mechanisms at the venotomy site.

Almost all of the included patients had venous sheath bore sizes of ≤15F. However, given the recent introduction of pulsed field ablation and larger venous sheaths, it is possible to speculate that the observed differences in venous bleeding complications and discharge times could be potentially larger with pulsed field ablation. Previous data have shown that FO8 sutures are potentially inadequate for larger bore sheaths.18 In addition, 2 studies (STYLE-AF and SAFE) had very few same-day discharges per protocol, so it is possible that the difference in time to discharge was underestimated compared with time to ambulation. Only 1 previous meta-analysis on this topic has been published. It did not include several RCTs available in our analysis; nonrandomized data were included, which limits the meta-analysis internal validity; and no time-to-event analysis was performed.19

In our study, there was an unclear rate of DVTs. Almost all patients in this study underwent procedures in which transseptal access was performed, and thus, anticoagulation was administered. The risk of symptomatic DVT seems to be zero or very low in this population given no reported cases of this complication. However, asymptomatic DVTs were not searched, and this may not be applicable to procedures in which heparin is not routinely administered and patients are not anticoagulated, such as supraventricular tachycardia ablation.

Our study has several limitations. First, multiple types of VCDs were included (suture, plug-based), so specific advantages of 1 type of VCD over another are not addressed in our analysis, and the control arms used both FO8 suture and MC. Second, all the included studies were unblinded, raising the possibility of ascertainment bias, which is particularly important in clinical decisions regarding ambulation times after bed rest. Third, there was some heterogeneity in the included populations in terms of index procedures, which varied by study. Fourth, this could have been translated into high heterogeneity in the outcomes of times to ambulation and hemostasis. However, the categorical outcomes had little to no measured heterogeneity, as did time to discharge. Fifth, the available data do not include information on emergency department readmissions, delayed discharges owing to access site complications, or other downstream resource utilization. As such, a pooled comprehensive cost-benefit analysis of VCDs was not feasible based on the included data. Finally, the statistical weights were uneven across studies, representing their differing event rates. For example, STYLE-AF had the highest weight despite not being the largest study. However, no heterogeneity was found across studies, suggesting that although some studies were more sensitive in detecting hematomas than others, the relative impact size comparing VCDs with the control group was constant across studies.

Conclusion

VCDs have a modest effect in reducing the rate of procedural hematoma and a composite outcome of all vascular complications. With these devices, there were reduced times to hemostasis, ambulation, and discharge, facilitating same-day discharge after EP procedures.
