Five Practical Considerations for the Management of Out-of-hospital Cardiac Arrest: Reflections from the British Cardiovascular Intervention Society Out-of-hospital Cardiac Arrest Focus Group
Authors: Guilherme Movio, Uzma Sajjad, Evan Ansell, Rupert Simpson, Marco Mion, Paul Rees, Thomas Johnson, Nick Curzen, Maria Rita Maccaroni, John R Davies, Nilesh Pareek, Thomas R Keeble
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) remains associated with poor survival and marked variation in post-resuscitation care. Drawing on discussion from the British Cardiovascular Intervention Society (BCIS) OHCA focus group, this article summarises five practical considerations for management following return of spontaneous circulation. First, selective conveyance to specialist cardiac arrest centres may be most effective when aligned with the BCIS algorithm, particularly for patients with features suggesting a reversible cardiac cause. Second, early risk stratification with MIRACLE2 may support decision-making when interpreted alongside specialist clinical judgement. Third, early multidisciplinary team review may improve coherence in a complex condition that often spans cardiology, intensive care, neurology and emergency medicine. Fourth, neuroprognostication is likely to be most robust when delayed appropriately, guided by European Resuscitation Council recommendations and delivered through a structured multimodal multidisciplinary team process. Finally, post-survival rehabilitation and family support should be regarded as core components of the OHCA pathway rather than optional afterthoughts.

Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) remains one of the most challenging conditions in acute cardiovascular and intensive care practice. Despite advances in pre-hospital resuscitation, coronary intervention and post-resuscitation intensive care, outcomes remain poor overall, with only 8% of patients surviving to hospital discharge within the UK.1–3 Substantial variation persists in triage, conveyance and treatment after return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC). The British Cardiovascular Intervention Society (BCIS) specialist cardiac arrest centre model offers one possible framework for a more consistent pathway of care.4

This article summarises five practical considerations for the management of OHCA from the point of ROSC in the community to longer-term care after discharge. These are not intended as prescriptive recommendations, but rather reflect specialist centre experience supported by evolving literature on selective conveyance, structured risk stratification, multidisciplinary review, neuroprognostication and post-discharge rehabilitation.4,5

1. Stratified Conveyance of Selected Subgroups to Cardiac Arrest Centres May Be Associated with Improved Outcome

Patient selection for direct conveyance to a specialist cardiac arrest centre remains one of the most important practical questions in OHCA care. Patients with an initial shockable rhythm, even those without overt ST-elevation MI (STEMI), are more likely to have an acute cardiac cause and are more likely to benefit from expedited conveyance to a cardiac arrest centre.1,4,5 This has been reinforced by the BCIS conveyance feasibility pilot study, which evaluated expedited conveyance of OHCA patients with STEMI (usual practice) and any patients with shockable rhythms (even in the absence of STEMI) to cardiac arrest centres.1 The study concluded that the algorithm was feasible to deliver in practice and demonstrated improved 30-day survival with favourable neurological recovery in patients with an initial shockable rhythm and among those with lower MIRACLE2 scores, although the results did not reach statistical significance.1,6 A subsequent 3.5-year regional evaluation from the east of England has suggested an association between implementation of the BCIS algorithm and improved 30-day mortality and favourable neurological outcome across a population of 2.8 million, using data from the BCIS OHCA registry.7

These data support a more personalised approach to patient selection rather than wholesale transfer. This suggests that fidelity to the BCIS conveyance algorithm may convey the most appropriate patients to specialist centres where they may have access to immediate coronary angiography, revascularisation, cardiac intensive care and post-arrest expertise.1,4 The BCIS algorithmic approach is also more cost-effective than standard care, reducing intensive care unit (ICU), ward, post-assessment and ambulance-related costs in a recent health economic analysis.8

2. Risk Stratification Using the MIRACLE2 Score May Enhance Clinical Decision-making

A second practical consideration is the assessment of the severity of the OHCA event and the subsequent risk of neurological injury, which is the cause of death in 70% of cases. Such decisions are rarely straightforward, particularly in the first few hours after ROSC when the clinical picture remains incomplete, and neuroprognostication is currently performed 72 hours after admission.9 For that reason, a structured risk stratification tool may be useful to guide treatment decisions, provided that it supports rather than replaces specialist clinical judgement.6,9,10

The MIRACLE2 score was developed as a practical bedside risk score for early prediction of poor neurological outcome after OHCA, to be calculated on arrival at a treating hospital (Figure 2).6 In the original derivation and validation study, MIRACLE2 showed good discriminatory performance for poor neurological outcome, supporting its use as an early classification tool in specialist post-arrest pathways.6 Subsequent validation work has also suggested that it remains practical and clinically useful at the point of hospital admission.11

In our experience, MIRACLE2 is most helpful when used to inform early multidisciplinary discussion rather than as a stand-alone determinant of care limitation. Premorbid function, arrest circumstances, haemodynamic instability, frailty, comorbidity and the presence or absence of a reversible cardiac cause all remain highly relevant to decisions around escalation, angiography, mechanical circulatory support or treatment limitation.6,11,12

A pre-hospital modified form of the MIRACLE2 score has recently been validated with a similar predictive capacity and does not require arterial blood gas measurement, therefore allowing the earliest possible neurological prediction immediately after ROSC, which may aid triage decisions.12 Prospective validation studies are required to understand its potential within the OHCA pathway.12

3. Early Admission Multidisciplinary Team Review May Help Bring Coherence to a Complex Presentation

OHCA is a complex condition, which rarely sits neatly within the boundaries of a single speciality. Even where a primary coronary event is suspected, the patient may also have co-existent cardiogenic shock, aspiration pneumonia, severe hypoxaemia, renal failure, metabolic disturbance or significant hypoxic-ischaemic brain injury. In this setting, one of the practical advantages of a specialist-centre model may be the opportunity for early multidisciplinary review involving emergency medicine, cardiology, critical care, nephrology and neurology.1,4,13.

Early multidisciplinary team review may help identify immediate concerns: appropriateness for immediate coronary angiography and further escalation, presence of potentially reversible shock state and whether early neurological concerns should shape the treatment plan.13,14 In our practice, it also helps reduce variability in decision-making, particularly when patients would otherwise be reviewed sequentially in isolation by multiple teams at different times and with potentially distinct clinical priorities.

Within the BCIS specialist-centre model, the advantage of direct transfer is therefore not confined to 24/7 catheter laboratory access alone; it also includes access to an environment in which early decisions are more likely to be made collaboratively and by teams familiar with the complexities of post-OHCA care.1,4

4. Neuroprognostication Is Likely to Be Most Robust When Based on European Resuscitation Council Guidance and a Dedicated Multidisciplinary Team Process

Neuroprognostication remains one of the most important and sensitive aspects of OHCA care.10,15 The 2025 European Resuscitation Council (ERC) and European Society of Intensive Care Medicine post-resuscitation care guidelines emphasise that prognostication should be delayed until major confounders have been excluded and should rely on a multimodal approach rather than any single sign, test or bedside impression.15 In patients who remain unconscious after 72 hours, the recommended framework incorporates clinical examination, electrophysiology, biomarkers, electroencephalography and neuroimaging interpreted together.15

This cautious approach is particularly important after OHCA, since sedation, haemodynamic instability, metabolic derangement, seizure activity and the broader effects of critical illness may all confound early neurological assessment. A dedicated neuroprognostication multidisciplinary team (MDT) may therefore be especially valuable in specialist centres, where serial review and multimodal interpretation are feasible.15

In our current virtual MDT service model, neuroprognostication is undertaken through a formalised multidisciplinary case review. Each case is presented by an ICU neuroprognostication nurse and an ICU physician using a standardised pro forma that captures the relevant clinical information, including arrest characteristics, post-resuscitation course, serial neurological examination findings, potential confounding factors and the results of relevant investigations, including neuroimaging and other ancillary tests. A neurologist, cardiologist, neurophysiologist and neuroradiologist are also in attendance to contribute specialist interpretation from their respective perspectives. This service-oriented model of neuroprognostication supports a systematic and reproducible assessment of prognosis based on the integrated interpretation of multimodal data, rather than a single contemporaneous bedside opinion.16

An important departure from current practice includes the increasing number of patients who are treated by extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation (ECPR); ECPR patients may take longer to regain consciousness than non-ECPR OHCA patients, and standard neuroprognostication systems may yield misleading results.17

5. Post-survival Care Deserves to Be Considered a Core Part of the Out-of-hospital Cardiac Arrest Pathway

A final consideration is the holistic care of survivors after the acute admission. This is often overlooked in pathways that tend to focus on emergency resuscitation, coronary reperfusion and ICU survival. However, the available literature suggests that many OHCA survivors and their families continue to experience substantial cognitive, psychological and social difficulties long after discharge.18–21 Related work by Mion et al. found that survivors frequently sought early follow-up and a holistic, multidimensional assessment of arrest sequelae, while family members commonly described on-going psychological difficulties and a need for dedicated support.19 These findings resonate strongly with our own experience in OHCA follow-up.

For a specialist centre, survival to discharge should be seen as an important milestone rather than the endpoint of care. The BCIS post-discharge rehabilitation consensus statement makes several recommendations to address the complex medical, physical and psycho-social needs that can significantly affect quality of life after discharge.18 Follow-up pathways should include review of cardiac status, secondary prevention, cognitive symptoms, mental health, family impact, driving and work advice, as well as referral into rehabilitation or survivor support services where available.18,19

In our centre, these needs are addressed through the CARE (Care After REsuscitation) clinic, which offers both telephonic and face-to-face holistic follow-up at 1, 3 and 12 months after discharge.19 This model extends beyond the survivor to include the co-survivor or primary caregiver, reflecting the broader psychological and practical consequences of OHCA. Patients and co-survivors are also signposted to peer support organisations such as Sudden Cardiac Arrest UK, the world’s largest OHCA patient support group.22

The wider impact of OHCA should also be recognised in relation to lay rescuers and bystanders, who may experience persistent distress after involvement in a resuscitation attempt; emerging support models, such as the RescQ initiative, suggest that rescuer support should also be considered within comprehensive OHCA care pathways. 23–26

Conclusion

Taken together, these five considerations (Table 1) from the BCIS OHCA focus group reflect a specialist-centre approach to OHCA that is structured but not dogmatic. Patient selection for cardiac arrest centre conveyance may be most effective when this prioritises those most likely to benefit from specialist cardiac care in accordance with the BCIS conveyance framework.1,4 Clinical assessment and appropriateness of individualised therapeutic approaches may be strengthened by combining the MIRACLE2 score with experienced clinical judgement.6,11,12 Early admission MDT review may improve coherent decision-making in a condition that routinely crosses speciality boundaries.3 Neuroprognostication is likely to be most robust when it is aligned with ERC guidelines, is multimodal and delivered through a structured and dedicated MDT process.15,16 Finally, post-survival care to address the complex recovery process deserves greater prominence as a core component of the pathway rather than as an afterthought.18,19,26

From the perspective of the BCIS OHCA focus group, these points are best understood not as fixed rules, but as practical reflections from specialist centre experiences. The growing literature suggests that a selective, multidisciplinary and longitudinal OHCA pathway may offer a useful model for future service development.
