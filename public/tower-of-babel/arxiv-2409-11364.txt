On the number of elements beyond the ones actually observed
by Eugenio Regazzini

Abstract
In this work, a variant of the birth and death chain with constant intensities, originally introduced by Bruno de Finetti way back in 1957, is revisited. This fact is also underlined by the choice of the title, which is clearly a literal translation of the original one. Characteristic of the variant is that it allows negative jumps of any magnitude. And this, as explained in the paper, might be useful in offering some insight into the issue, arising in numerous situations, of inferring the number of the undetected elements of a given population. One thinks, for example, of problems concerning abundance or richness of species. The author’s purpose is twofold: to align the original de Finetti’s construction with the modern, well-established theory of the continuous-time Markov chains with discrete state space and show how it could be used to make probabilistic previsions on the number of the unseen elements of a population. With the aim of enhancing the possible practical applications of the model, one discusses the statistical point estimation of the rates which characterize its infinitesimal description.

(9)

\begin{split}\hskip 30.00005ptR^{*}_{t}(\delta_{x},n)&=\frac{\vartheta^{n}}{(\vartheta+1)_{n}}+\\[5.0pt]
&+e^{-(\vartheta+n)\mu t}\sum_{\rho=0}^{n}\frac{(\vartheta(e^{\mu t}-1))^{\rho}}{\rho!}\biggl(\mathmybb{1}_{[n-\rho,+\infty)}(x)-\frac{\vartheta^{n-\rho}}{(\vartheta+1)_{n-\rho}}\biggr)\end{split}

(\vartheta)_{n}
being the Pochhammer symbol for the
rising factorial
, i.e.,
(\vartheta)_{n}\coloneq\vartheta(\vartheta+1)\ldots(\vartheta+n-1)
if
n\geq 1
, and
(\vartheta)_{0}\coloneq 1
. Then,

C
.

The transition function of the Markov chain, whose existence has been
established in
(
B
)
, is given by

p^{*}_{t}(x,y)\equiv R^{*}_{t}(\delta_{x},y)-R^{*}_{t}(\delta_{x},y+1)

for every
x,y
in
S
and
t\geq 0
, where
R^{*}_{t}
is defined by (
9
).

In the rest of the paper, given any probability
\tau
on
S
, the symbols
(\tau,\{\mathbf{P}^{x}:x\in S\})
and

\mathbf{P}^{(\tau)}
will designate the Markov chain
X
with initial distribution
\tau
and transition function

p^{*}_{t}(x,y)
, and the corresponding probability measure on
(\Omega,\mathscr{F})
, respectively. Then,

\displaystyle R^{*}_{t}(\tau,n)

\displaystyle\coloneq\mathbf{P}^{(\tau)}\{X(t)\geq n\}=\sum_{x\geq 0}\tau(x)R^{*}_{t}(\delta_{x},n)\qquad\text{($n\in S$)}

\displaystyle=I(n)+e^{-(\vartheta+n)\mu t}\sum_{\rho=0}^{n}\frac{(\vartheta(e^{\mu t}-1))^{\rho}}{\rho!}\Delta(n-\rho)

where

I(n)\coloneq\frac{\vartheta^{n}}{(\vartheta+1)_{n}}\qquad\text{($n\in S$)}

and

\Delta(n)\coloneq\tau([n,+\infty))-I(n)\qquad\text{($n\in S$)}.

An immediate consequence of these definitions is that

(10)

\pi^{*}(n)\coloneq I(n)-I(n+1)=\frac{\vartheta^{n}}{(\vartheta+1)_{n+1}}(n+1)\qquad\text{($n\in S$)}

is a probability density function w.r.t. the counting measure on
(S,\mathscr{P}(S))
. The corresponding
probability measure, denoted by the same symbol, has
finite moments of any order
. Indeed, for every

\rho>0
, one has

(11)

\sum_{n\geq 1}n^{\rho}\pi^{*}(n)=\rho\sum_{n\geq 1}\int_{n-1}^{n}x^{\rho-1}I(n)\,dx=\sum_{n\geq 1}I(n)\{n^{\rho}-(n-1)^{\rho}\}.

Since

0<I(n)\{n^{\rho}-(n-1)^{\rho}\}\leq n^{\rho}\,\frac{\vartheta^{n}}{n!}\qquad\text{($n\geq 1$)}

then the series has a finite sum.

Moreover, setting
z\coloneq\vartheta(e^{\mu t}-1)
, one gets

\sum_{n\geq 1}\{n^{\rho}-(n-1)^{\rho}\}\bigg\lvert e^{-(\vartheta+n)\mu t}\sum_{\rho=0}^{n}\frac{z^{\rho}}{\rho!}\Delta(n-\rho)\bigg\rvert\leq\\[5.0pt]
\leq\sum_{n\geq 1}\{n^{\rho}-(n-1)^{\rho}\}e^{-(\vartheta+n)\mu t+z}<+\infty

which, combined with the previous result, entails

(12)

\mathbf{P}^{(\tau)}(\lvert X(t)\rvert^{\rho})<+\infty

for every starting distribution
\tau
, every
\rho>0
and
t>0
. Of course, (
12
) extends to

t=0
if
\tau
has finite
\rho
-th moment.

Other properties of
(\tau,\{\mathbf{P}^{x}:x\in S\})
, in particular long-time properties, are described in the next
section.

4
Long-time properties of the process

It is plain to see that
\pi^{*}
is the equilibrium distribution, i.e., the limiting probability distribution
of
p^{*}_{t}(x,\cdot\,)
, as
t\rightarrow+\infty
, for all
x
. Some of its more interesting properties are described
in the following two propositions. The former concerns stationarity.

D
.

The limiting probability distribution
\pi^{*}
is invariant for the Markov chain with transition
function
p^{*}_{t}
, that is

\pi^{*}(y)=\sum_{x\geq 0}\pi^{*}(x)p^{*}_{t}(x,y)\qquad\textup{($y\in S$, $t\geq 0$)}.

Moreover,
(\pi^{*},\{\mathbf{P}^{x}:x\in S\})
is a stationary Markov chain, i.e., the joint distribution

\sum_{x\in S}\pi^{*}(x)\,\mathbf{P}^{x}\{X(t_{1}+s)\in A_{1},\ldots,X(t_{n}+s)\in A_{n}\}\qquad\textup{($A_{j}\subset S$, $j=1,\ldots,n$)}

is independent of
s
whenever
0\leq t_{1}+s<t_{2}+s<\ldots<t_{n}+s
.

The latter proposition pertains to the class structure of the chain and ergodicity.

E
.

Every state
x\in S
is positive recurrent, i.e.,

\mathbf{P}^{x}\bigl(\textup{$\{t\geq 0:X(t)=x\}$ is unbounded}\bigr)=1

and the expectation
\mathbf{P}^{x}
of the return time

T_{x}\coloneq\inf\{t>J_{1}:X(t)=x\}

obeys

\mathbf{P}^{x}(T_{x})=\frac{(\vartheta+1)_{x}}{\mu(\vartheta+x)(1+x)\vartheta^{x}}.

Moreover, the ergodic condition holds true, i.e.,

\mathbf{P}^{(\tau)}\biggl\{\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}f(X(s))\,ds\rightarrow\sum_{x\geq 0}\pi^{*}(x)f(x)\textup{ as $t\rightarrow+\infty$}\biggr\}=1

for any
\tau
and every bounded function
f\colon S\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.

Propositions (
D
) and (
E
) follow from plain combinations of (
A
) with some basic
results, for which the reader is referred to
Norris, 1997
: Theorems 3.5.1, 3.5.2, 3.5.3, 3.8.1,

Liggett, 2010
, Subsection 2.6.1.

4.1
Bounds on the error in approximating the equilibrium

In this subsection the speed of approach to equilibrium is considered from two different points of view:
convergence of the cumulative distribution of
X(t)
and convergence of its moments. The corresponding
results also provide expressive bounds on the error in approximating for fixed
t
.

The first statement is about the Kolmogorov
uniform distance
between
\Pi^{*}(x)\coloneq\sum_{n\leq x}\pi^{*}(n)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
, and the cumulative distribution function
F^{(\tau)}_{X(t)}
of
X(t)
, i.e.,

F^{(\tau)}_{X(t)}(x)\coloneq\mathbf{P}^{(\tau)}\{X(t)\leq x\}
.

F
.

If
T(x)\coloneq\sum_{n\leq x}\tau(n)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
, then

\sup\{\lvert\Pi^{*}(x)-F^{(\tau)}_{X(t)}(x)\rvert:x\in\mathbb{R}\}\\[5.0pt]
\leq\sup\{\lvert\Pi^{*}(x)-T(x)\rvert:x\in\mathbb{R}\}\exp\{-\mu t-\vartheta(e^{\mu t}+\mu t-1)\}.

Proof of
(
F
)
.

The uniform distance between
\Pi^{*}
and
F^{(\tau)}_{X(t)}
is equal to

\displaystyle\sup_{n\geq 1}\lvert R^{*}_{t}(\tau,n)-I(n)\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{n}\Delta(n)\cdot\sup_{n}e^{-(\vartheta+n)\mu t}e_{n-1}(\vartheta(e^{\mu t}-1))

(from the end of Section
3
, with the proviso that
e_{\nu}(x)\coloneq\sum_{k=0}^{\nu}x^{k}/k!
)

\displaystyle=\sup_{n}\Delta(n)\cdot\sup_{n}e^{-\vartheta\mu t}\sum_{\rho=0}^{n-1}e^{-\mu t(n-\rho)}\frac{(\vartheta(1-e^{-\mu t}))^{\rho}}{\rho!}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{n}\Delta(n)e^{-\vartheta\mu t-\mu t+\vartheta(1-e^{-\mu t})}.\@qedbox{}

The next result concerns the error of the
m
-th moment of
\Pi^{*}
in approximating the homologous moment of

F^{(\tau)}_{X(t)}
.

G
.

If the starting distribution
\tau
has finite
m
-th moment, for some integer
m\geq 1
, then

\bigg\lvert\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{m}\,d\bigl(F^{(\tau)}_{X(t)}(x)-\Pi^{*}(x)\bigr)\bigg\rvert\leq m\,\mathscr{K}_{h_{m}}(\tau,\pi^{*})\cdot\exp\{-\mu t-\vartheta(e^{-\mu t}+\mu t-1)\}\qquad\textup{($t\geq 0$)}

where
h_{m}
is a real-valued function and
\mathscr{K}_{h_{m}}(\tau,\pi^{*})
a real number defined by

h_{m}(x)\coloneq\sum_{\rho\geq 0}\frac{e^{-\vartheta}}{\rho!}\vartheta^{\rho}(x+\rho)^{m-1}\qquad\textup{($x\geq 0$)}

and

\mathscr{K}_{h_{m}}(\tau,\pi^{*})\coloneq\int_{0}^{+\infty}h_{m}(x)\lvert T(x)-\Pi^{*}(x)\rvert\,dx

respectively.

Remark
.

\mathscr{K}_{h_{m}}
is an example of
Kantorovich-Rubinstein functional
. It represents the value of the
minimal (total) translocation cost in the case of transits permitted (masses
q
on
S^{2}
such that

q(\{n\}\times S)-q(S\times\{n\})=\tau(n)-\pi^{*}(n)
, for every
n\in S
), when the unit cost
c
of
transportation from
x
to
y
is given by

c(x,y)=\lvert x-y\rvert\max(h_{m}(x),h_{m}(y)).

For a comprehensive account of these concepts, the reader is referred to
Rachev et al., 2013
, Chapter 5.

Proof of
(
G
)
.

Recalling that the
m
-th moment of a probability distribution
p

supported by some subset of
\mathbb{R}^{+}
,
with cumulative distribution function
F_{p}
, is presentable as

\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{m}\,p(dx)=m\int_{0}^{+\infty}x^{m-1}[1-F_{p}(x)]\,dx

then, by force of (
11
)-(
12
), the absolute difference between moments to be investigated
can be written and majorised as follows

\displaystyle\bigg\lvert\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{m}\,d(F^{(\tau)}_{X(t)}-\Pi^{*})\bigg\rvert=\bigg\lvert\sum_{k\geq 0}\bigl((k+1)^{m}-k^{m}\bigr)\big(R^{*}_{t}(\tau,k+1)-I(k+1)\big)\bigg\rvert

\displaystyle\leq f(t;\vartheta,\mu)\sum_{\rho\geq 0}\mathscr{P}_{g(t)}(\rho)\sum_{\nu\geq 1}[(\rho+\nu)^{m}-(\rho+\nu-1)^{m}]\cdot\lvert T(\nu-1)-\Pi^{*}(\nu-1)\rvert

(where
\mathscr{P}_{g(t)}
denotes the Poisson distribution with parameter
g(t)\coloneq\vartheta(1-e^{-\mu t})
,
f(t;\vartheta,\mu)\coloneq\exp\{g(t)-t(\lambda+\mu)\}
)

\displaystyle=f(t;\vartheta,\mu)\sum_{\rho\geq 0}\mathscr{P}_{g(t)}(\rho)\sum_{\mu\geq 1}m\int_{\nu-1}^{\nu}(\rho+x)^{m-1}\,dx\cdot\lvert T(\nu-1)-\Pi^{*}(\nu-1)\rvert

\displaystyle=f(t;\vartheta,\mu)mD_{m}(t)

where
D_{m}(t)\coloneq\int_{0}^{+\infty}\sum_{\rho\geq 0}\mathscr{P}_{g(t)}(\rho)(\rho+x)^{m-1}\cdot\lvert T(x)-\Pi^{*}(x)\rvert\,dx
. The last equality is a consequence of the Fubini theorem applied
to the integral of the positive function
(\rho,x)\mapstochar\rightarrow(\rho+x)^{m-1}\lvert T(x)-\Pi^{*}(x)\rvert
,
(\rho,x)\in S\times\mathbb{R}^{+}
, w.r.t. the measure
\mathscr{P}_{g(t)}\times\mathrm{Leb}
. To complete the proof it suffices
to check the validity of the following relations, in which
S(\cdot\,,\cdot\,)
denotes the generic Stirling
number of the second kind.

\displaystyle G(t,x)

\displaystyle\coloneq\sum_{\rho\geq 0}\mathscr{P}_{g(t)}(\rho)(\rho+x)^{m-1}\qquad\text{(finite for every $t\geq 0$, $x\geq 0$)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\binom{m-1}{j}x^{m-1-j}\sum_{k=0}^{j}S(j,k)g(t)^{k}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\binom{m-1}{j}x^{m-1-j}\sum_{k=0}^{j}S(j,k)\vartheta^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\rho\geq 0}\mathscr{P}_{\vartheta}(\rho)(\rho+x)^{m-1}\eqcolon h_{m}(x).

Then,

\displaystyle 0\leq D_{m}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{+\infty}G(t,x)\lvert T(x)-\Pi^{*}(x)\rvert\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{+\infty}h_{m}(x)\lvert T(x)-\Pi^{*}(x)\rvert\,dx=\mathscr{K}_{h_{m}}(\tau,\pi^{*})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\sum_{k=0}^{j}\binom{m-1}{j}S(j,k)\,\vartheta^{k}\int_{0}^{+\infty}x^{n-1-j}\lvert T(x)-\Pi^{*}(x)\rvert\,dx

\displaystyle<+\infty

since, by hypothesis,

\int_{0}^{+\infty}x^{m-1-j}\lvert T(x)-\Pi^{*}(x)\rvert\,dx<+\infty

for
j=0,\ldots,m-1
.

Remark
.

If
m=1
and
\mathbf{P}^{(\tau)}_{X(t)}
denotes the probability distribution (under
\mathbf{P}^{(\tau)}
) of
X(t)
,
then
\mathscr{K}_{h_{m}}(\mathbf{P}^{(\tau)}_{X(t)},\pi^{*})
reduces to the
Gini dissimilarity index

\mathscr{G}(\mathbf{P}^{(\tau)}_{X(t)},\pi^{*})=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\lvert F^{(\tau)}_{X(t)}(x)-\Pi^{*}(x)\rvert\,dx

that, historically, can be viewed as a prototype of Kantorovich functional. See, once again,

Rachev et al., 2013
. Then, the same argument as in the previous proof can be used to validate the inequality

\mathscr{G}(\mathbf{P}^{(\tau)}_{X(t)},\pi^{*})\leq\exp\{-\mu t-\vartheta(e^{-\mu t}+\mu t-1)\}\mathscr{G}(\tau,\pi^{*})

provided that
\tau
has finite expectation
.

5
Outline of potential practical uses of the model

Here is a description of how the Markov chain analysed in the last three sections might be used to tackle
some of the problems outlined in the Introduction. One notes, preliminarily, that the following treatment
rests on the realistic assumption that
times and magnitudes of negative jumps are the sole facts one
can actually observe
. Then – denoted by
\mathbf{t}^{(n)}
and
\mathbf{d}^{(n)}
the vectors
(t_{0},\ldots,t_{n})
and

(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})
, respectively, where
0=t_{0}<\ldots<t_{n}
,
d_{k}\in S\setminus\{0\}
for
k=1,\ldots,n
, and

n\geq 1
– one considers the events

\displaystyle H_{\mathbf{t}^{(n)}}

\displaystyle\coloneq\bigcap_{k=1}^{n}\{\text{a \emph{negative} jump occurs at $t_{k}$}\}

\displaystyle H_{\mathbf{t}^{(n)},\mathbf{d}^{(n)}}

\displaystyle\coloneq\bigcap_{k=1}^{n}\{\text{a \emph{negative} jump of magnitude
$d_{k}$ occurs at $t_{k}$}\}

It is clear that the accomplishment of the aim set at the beginning of this section largely depends on the
analysis of the (conditional) probabilities
\mathbf{P}^{(\tau)}(\bigcap_{j=1}^{n}\{X(t_{j})=x_{j}\mid H_{\mathbf{t}^{(n)},\mathbf{d}^{(n)}})
and
\mathbf{P}^{(\tau)}(\bigcap_{j=1}^{n}\{\lvert\Delta X(t_{j})\rvert=d_{j},X(t_{j})=x_{j}\}\mid H_{\mathbf{t}^{(n)}})
, where, assuming that
X(t-0)\neq X(t)
,
\Delta X(t)\coloneq X(t-0)-X(t)
and
\lvert\Delta X(t)\rvert
stand for size and magnitude, respectively, of the jump at
t>0
. Now, by simple reasoning, one can
prove

H
.

Let
x_{0}\coloneq x
and,
\forall n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}\setminus\{0\}
, let
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
belong to
S^{n}
,

\mathbf{d}^{(n)}\coloneq(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})
to
(S\setminus\{0\})^{n}
,
\mathbf{t}^{(n)}\coloneq(t_{0},\ldots,t_{n})
with

0=t_{0}<\ldots<t_{n}
, and
D^{(\tau)}_{n}(\mathbf{t}^{(n)},\mathbf{d}^{(n)})\coloneq\sum_{x\in S}\tau(x)\sum_{x_{1}\geq 0}\ldots\sum_{x_{n}\geq 0}p^{*}_{t_{1}}(x,x_{1}+d_{1})\ldots p^{*}_{t_{n}-t_{n-1}}(x_{n-1},x_{n}+d_{n})
. Then,

(13)

\mathbf{P}^{(\tau)}(\lvert\Delta X(t_{1})\rvert=d_{1},X(t_{1})=x_{1},\ldots,\lvert\Delta X(t_{n})\rvert=d_{n},X(t_{n})=x_{n}\mid H_{\mathbf{t}^{(n)}})=\\[5.0pt]
=\frac{1}{\sum_{d_{1}\geq 1}\ldots\sum_{d_{n}\geq 1}D^{(\tau)}_{n}(\mathbf{t}^{(n)},\mathbf{d}^{(n)})}\sum_{x\geq 0}\tau(x)p^{*}_{t_{1}}(x,x_{1}+d_{1})\cdot\ldots\\[5.0pt]
\ldots\cdot p^{*}_{t_{n}-t_{n-1}}(x_{n-1},x_{n}+d_{n});

(14)

\mathbf{P}^{(\tau)}(\lvert\Delta X(t_{1})\rvert=d_{1},\ldots,\lvert\Delta X(t_{n})\rvert=d_{n}\mid H_{\mathbf{t}^{(n)}})=\\[5.0pt]
=\frac{1}{\sum_{d_{1}\geq 1}\ldots\sum_{d_{n}\geq 1}D^{(\tau)}_{n}(\mathbf{t}^{(n)},\mathbf{d}^{(n)})}D^{(\tau)}(\mathbf{t}^{(n)},\mathbf{d}^{(n)});

(15)

\mathbf{P}^{(\tau)}(X(t_{1})=x_{1},\ldots,X(t_{n})=x_{n}\mid H_{\mathbf{t}^{(n)},\mathbf{d}^{(n)}})=\\[5.0pt]
=\frac{1}{D^{(\tau)}_{n}(\mathbf{t}^{(n)},\mathbf{d}^{(n)})}\sum_{x\geq 0}\tau(x)p^{*}_{t_{1}}(x,x_{1}+d_{1})\cdot\ldots\cdot p^{*}_{t_{n}-t_{n-1}}(x_{n-1},x_{n}+d_{n}).

Distribution (
14
) may play a role as a basis for statistical inference, e.g., estimation of the
generally unknown parameters
\vartheta
and
\mu
present in the expression of the transition function
p^{*}_{t}
.
Indeed, consistently with the basic initial assumption about what is actually observable, for each

\mathbf{d}^{(n)}
and
\mathbf{t}^{(n)}
, (
14
), considered as a function of
(\vartheta,\mu)
, represents the

likelihood function
, conditionally on the hypothesis that observations (negative jumps) occur at times

t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}
. However, the rather laborious development of such a statistical idea goes beyond the aim of
the present paper. A decidely more conventional likelihood function will be considered, along with some of
its consequences, in Section
6
, where one imagines situations in which jumping times are
unavailable data.

Distribution (
15
) can be used, e.g., to evaluate the probability that, immediately after the last
observation of new specimens of a given species, the number of unseen elements of that very same species is
not less than an arbitrarily fixed integer
\xi
, conditionally on the fact that those observations occurred
at times
t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}
and consisted in
d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}
new specimens, respectively:

(16)

\mathbf{P}^{(\tau)}(X(t_{n})\geq\xi\mid H_{\mathbf{t}^{(n)},\mathbf{d}^{(n)}})=\frac{1}{D^{(\tau)}(\mathbf{t}^{(n)},\mathbf{d}^{(n)})}\sum_{x\geq 0}\ldots\sum_{x_{n-1}\geq 0}\tau(x)p^{*}_{\Delta t_{1}}(x,x_{1}+d_{1})\ldots\\[5.0pt]
\ldots p^{*}_{\Delta t_{n-1}}(x_{n-2},x_{n-1}+d_{n-1})R^{*}_{\Delta t_{n}}(\delta_{x_{n-1}},d_{n}+\xi)\\[5.0pt]
=\sum_{x_{n-1}\geq 0}m_{n-1}(x^{(\tau)}_{n-1};\mathbf{d}^{(n)},\Delta\mathbf{t}^{(n)})\mathbf{P}^{x_{n-1}}(X(\Delta t_{n})\geq d_{n}+\xi\mid X(\Delta t_{n})\geq d_{n})

where
\Delta t_{j}\coloneq t_{j}-t_{j-1}
(
j=1,\ldots,n
),
\Delta\mathbf{t}^{(n)}=(\Delta t_{1},\ldots,\Delta t_{n})
,

m^{(\tau)}_{n-1}(x_{n-1};\mathbf{d}^{(n)},\Delta\mathbf{t}^{(n)})\coloneq\frac{1}{D^{(\tau)}(\mathbf{t}^{(n)},\mathbf{d}^{(n)})}\sum_{x\geq 0}\ldots\sum_{x_{n-2}\geq 0}\tau(x)\\[5.0pt]
\ldots p^{*}_{\Delta t_{n-1}}(x_{n-2},x_{n-1}+d_{n-1})R^{*}_{\Delta t_{n}}(\delta_{x_{n-1}},d_{n})\qquad\text{($n=2,1,\ldots$)}

m^{(\tau)}_{0}(x;d_{1},t_{1})\coloneq\frac{\tau(x)R^{*}_{t_{1}}(\delta_{x},d_{1})}{D^{(\tau)}(t_{1},d_{1})}

and, of course,

\mathbf{P}^{x_{n-1}}(X(\Delta t_{n})\geq d_{n}+\xi\mid X(\Delta t_{n})\geq d_{n})=\frac{R^{*}_{\Delta t_{n}}(\delta_{x_{n-1}},d_{n}+\xi)}{R^{*}_{\Delta t_{n}}(\delta_{x_{n-1}},d_{n})}\qquad\text{($n=1,2,\ldots$)}.

It is worth noticing that equality

\sum_{x\geq 0}m^{(\tau)}_{n-1}(x;\mathbf{d}^{(n)},\mathbf{t}^{(n)})=1

holds true for every
n=1,2,\ldots
, and
m^{(\tau)}_{n-1}(x;\mathbf{d}^{(n)},\mathbf{t}^{(n)})>0
for every
x,n
.
Further useful information is also provided by the
expectation

(17)

\mathbf{P}^{(\tau)}(X(t_{n})\mid H_{\mathbf{t}^{(n)},\mathbf{d}^{(n)}})=\sum_{s\geq 0}m^{(\tau)}_{n-1}(s;\mathbf{d}^{(n)},\Delta\mathbf{t}^{(n)})\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{R^{*}_{\Delta t_{n}}(\delta_{s},k\vee d_{n})}{R^{*}_{\Delta t_{n}}(\delta_{s},d_{n})}-d_{n}.

Reinterpretation of the previous probability evaluations would be perfectly justifiable from perspectives
quite different than the species abundance, like, for example, the number of unseen migrants along the
Mediterranean routes during a certain period. In any case, they represent probabilities of events

conditional
on either the event
H_{\mathbf{t}^{(n)}}
or the event
H_{\mathbf{t}^{(n)},\mathbf{d}^{(n)}}
, evaluated
for every
\mathbf{t}^{(n)}
(
(\mathbf{t}^{(n)},\mathbf{d}^{(n)})
, respectively) and for every
n
. One will alternately
confine oneself to considering that distribution which corresponds to the specific
\mathbf{t}^{(n)}

(
(\mathbf{t}^{(n)},\mathbf{d}^{(n)})
, respectively) which turns out to be true. But it is quite natural to think of
questions which require the evaluation of “absolute” probabilities like when, e.g., one is wondering about
the probability distribution of the first jump time, jointly with its magnitude and the number of unseen
elements immediately after its occurrence. As far as the calculus of these probabilities is concerned, the
jump chain/holding times description, recalled at the end of Section
2
, can provide proper tools.
In the same notation introduced therein, completed by
\bar{J}_{k}
to denote the
k
-th
negative
jump
time (
k=1,2,\ldots
), fix
\nu
in
\mathbb{Z}^{+}
and assume that
\bar{J}_{1}
corresponds to the
(\nu+1)
-th jump
of the chain. The
\mathbf{P}^{x}
-probability that such an event occurs jointly with
\{\lvert\Delta X(\bar{J}_{1})\rvert=d\}
is given by

(18)

\frac{\lambda^{\nu}\mu}{(\lambda+\mu x)\ldots(\lambda+\mu(x+\nu))}\mathmybb{1}\{1\leq d\leq x+\nu\}=\frac{\vartheta^{\nu}}{(\vartheta+x)_{\nu+1}}\mathmybb{1}\{1\leq d\leq x+\nu\}

which can also be reinterpreted as
\mathbf{P}^{x}\{X(\bar{J}_{1})=x+\nu-d,\lvert\Delta X(\bar{J}_{1})\rvert=d\}
. If

\bar{J}_{1}
corresponds to the
(\nu+1)
-th jump of the chain, then its probability distribution is the same
as that of the sum of
(\nu+1)
-independent exponentially distributed random numbers of parameter

\lambda+\mu(x+j)
,
j=0,\ldots,\nu
, respectively. Then, by the convolution theorem, the
Laplace
transform
of the density of that sum is

\displaystyle p\mapstochar\rightarrow

\displaystyle\prod_{j=0}^{\nu}\frac{\lambda+\mu(x+j)}{p+\lambda+\mu(x+j)}\qquad\text{($p>-\lambda$)}

\displaystyle=\Gamma\begin{bmatrix}\vartheta+x+\nu+1\\
\vartheta+x\end{bmatrix}\cdot\Gamma\begin{bmatrix}\dfrac{p}{\mu}+\vartheta+x\\[10.00002pt]
\dfrac{p}{\mu}+\vartheta+x+\nu+1\end{bmatrix}

whose determining function is given by

(19)

\mathbb{R}\ni t\mapstochar\rightarrow\frac{\mu}{B(\nu+1,\vartheta+x)}e^{-(\vartheta+x)\mu t}(1-e^{-\mu t})^{\nu}\mathmybb{1}\{t>0\}

where
B
stands for the
Euler Beta function
. Then, combination of (
19
) with (
18
)
gives

\mathbf{P}^{x}\{\bar{J}_{1}>t_{1},X(\bar{J}_{1})=x+\nu-d,\lvert\Delta X(\bar{J}_{1})\rvert=d\}=\\[5.0pt]
=\frac{\vartheta^{\nu}}{(\vartheta+x)_{\nu+1}}\int_{t_{1}\vee 0}^{+\infty}\frac{\mu}{B(\nu+1,\vartheta+x)}e^{-(\vartheta+x)\mu t}(1-e^{-\mu t})^{\nu}\,dt\cdot\mathmybb{1}\{1\leq d\leq x+\nu\}

for every
x\in S
,
\nu\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
,
d\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}\setminus\{0\}
and
t_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
, i.e,

I
.

Given any
x\in S
, the function
f
defined on
\mathbb{R}^{+}\times S\times(S\setminus\{0\})

Brief mention to the estimation of the rate of negative jumps

The conditions under which estimation has been tackled so far imply that

6.4
Final remarks from a Bayesian perspective

It should be noted that both the coefficient
(v/m)^{2}
in (
K
) and the variance of the limiting law
in (
L
) depend on
\vartheta_{0}
, that is a hypothetical value of an unknown parameter. This event, which
generally affects similar statistical statements of a frequentistic nature, compromises value and practical
usefulness of them. It stems from the prejudice against the inclusion in the theory of statistics of the
initial opinions – in the form of subjective probabilities – of the “user” of the theory. But “… no problem can be correctly stated in statistics without an evaluation of the initial probabilities… [even though]… probabilities must often be based on vague, uncertain, and fragmentary information”
(
Finetti, 1972
, Chapter 8). A situation which could arise also in connection with the problem of
estimating
\vartheta\coloneq\lambda/\mu
, because of the supposed little reliable information about
\lambda
.
In any case, in order to take due account of initial probabilities, one ought to modify and complete the
model
\mathscr{M}
according to the usual Bayes-Laplace paradigm. Thus, the space of the “observables” is
replaced with the product of the parameter space and the space of observables, viz,
(\mathbb{R}^{+}\times D^{\infty},\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{R}^{+})\otimes(\mathscr{P}(D))^{\infty})
. Its first coordinate, say
\tilde{\vartheta}
,
represents the random parameter, while the subsequent ones, denoted by
\tilde{d}_{1},\tilde{d}_{2},\ldots
,
have the same meaning as in
\mathscr{M}
. Denoting by
q
the initial distribution of
\tilde{\vartheta}
–
i.e., a probability measure on
(\mathbb{R}^{+},\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{R}^{+}))
– the assumption that the elements of

\mathscr{M}
form a conditional distribution for
(\tilde{d}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
, given
\tilde{\vartheta}
, is
tantamount to endowing the above product space with the probability measure
Q
presentable as

Q(d\vartheta\,d\mathbf{x})=q(d\vartheta)\varphi^{\infty}_{\vartheta}(d\mathbf{x})\qquad\text{($\vartheta\geq 0$,
$\mathbf{x}\in D^{\infty}$)}

with the proviso that
\varphi_{0}\coloneq\delta_{1}
. It is important to note that
\tilde{d}_{1},\tilde{d}_{2},\ldots
are
exchangeable
w.r.t.
Q
and their (joint) distribution is
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}\varphi^{\infty}_{0}\,dq
.

Based on these premises, one can now try to find a sort of Bayesian justification for the moment estimator
defined in Subsection
6.1
, and, then, discuss Bayesian counterparts of (
K
), (
L
),
which, of course, are free from the defect mentioned at the beginning of the present one. As far as the first
point is concerned, let
\hat{s}_{n}=\hat{s}_{n}(\tilde{d}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{d}_{n})
denote any potential estimator
of
\tilde{\vartheta}
, and suppose that the loss suffered by the “user” for deciding
\hat{s}_{n}
is

\lvert L(\hat{s}_{n})-\bar{d}^{(n)}\rvert
. This loss function, although rather odd, is not totally senseless. Since
the corresponding statistical risk
Q(\lvert L(\hat{s}_{n})-\bar{d}^{(n)}\rvert)
vanishes when

\hat{s}_{n}=\hat{\vartheta}_{n}
, then the moment estimator
\hat{\vartheta}_{n}
can be viewed as a Bayesian estimator
of
\tilde{\vartheta}
. In the new Bayesian context the study of the consistency of
\hat{\vartheta}_{n}
leads to
consider
Q(\bigcap_{k\geq m}\{\lvert\hat{\vartheta}_{k}-\tilde{\vartheta}\rvert<\varepsilon\})
which, by virtue of
(
K
), obeys

(24)

Q\Big(\bigcap\nolimits_{k\geq m}\{\lvert\hat{\vartheta}_{k}-\tilde{\vartheta}\rvert<\varepsilon\}\Big)\geq 1-\biggl(\frac{1}{m}+\sum_{k\geq m+1}\frac{1}{k^{2}}\biggr)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}\biggl(\frac{v(x)}{L(x+\varepsilon)-L(x)}\biggr)^{\!\!2}\,q(dx)

provided that the integral is finite
. Supporters of non-Bayesian views of statistics might object that
the Bayesian answer provided by (
24
), to the original critical issue about the indeterminacy of the
bound in (
K
), is simply illusory. Indeed, some say that the evaluation of
q
is impractical,
others say that prior distributions do not exist. But, in the present case one should note that the new bound
boils down to the expectation of

W(\tilde{\vartheta})=\biggl(\frac{v(\tilde{\vartheta})}{L(\tilde{\vartheta}+\varepsilon)-L(\tilde{\vartheta})}\biggr)^{\!\!2}=\frac{\tilde{\vartheta}+L(\tilde{\vartheta})(1-L(\tilde{\vartheta}))}{(\varepsilon\cdot L^{\prime}(\tilde{c}))^{2}}

where
\tilde{c}
is some suitable point of the interval
(\tilde{\vartheta},\tilde{\vartheta}+\varepsilon)
, and, to
evaluate it, complete knowledge of
q
is not required at all. In
A7
and
A8
there are
computations of the elements of the above expression for small values and, more importantly, for large values
of

, respectively. In fact, for the latter, in
A8

where

is a random number taking value in
(0,1)
. Then, with a view to practical
uses of (
24

two moments of

directly. From a subjectivistic viewpoint, the sole requirement such an opinion has to

Reconverting (

\int_{\mathscr{C}^{(n)}}\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{(n+1)_{k}}{k!}\lvert z\rvert^{k}(\mathscr{H}^{-1}_{n}(p))^{k}\,\mathscr{D}_{n}(dp)\\[5.0pt]
\leq\int_{\mathscr{C}^{(n)}}\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{(n+1)_{k}}{k!}\lvert z\rvert^{k}\,\mathscr{D}_{n}(dp)\eqcolon{}_{2}F_{1}(a,n+1;a;\lvert z\rvert)

a
is any positive, and, as usual,
{}_{2}F_{1}
denotes the
Gaussian
(or
ordinary
)

hypergeometric series
. Thus, one gets

\frac{1}{(z+1)_{n+1}}=\frac{1}{(n+1)!}\sum_{k\geq 0}(-1)^{k}\frac{(n+1)_{k}}{k!}\mu^{(n)}_{k}z^{k}\qquad\text{($\lvert z\rvert<1$)}

where
\mu^{(n)}_{k}\coloneq\int_{\mathscr{C}^{(n)}}(\mathscr{H}^{-1}_{n}(p))^{k}\,\mathscr{D}_{n}(dp)\in(0,1]
,
(
k=0,1,\ldots
), and

(A6.2)

D(z)=\frac{l(z)-1}{z}=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{z^{n}}{(n+1)!}\sum_{k\geq 0}(-1)^{k}\frac{(n+1)_{k}}{k!}\mu^{(n)}_{k}z^{k}.

The series of the absolute values may be majorised by

\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{\lvert z\rvert^{n}}{(n+1)!}\,{}_{2}F_{1}(a,n+1;a;\lvert z\rvert)

which, since
{}_{2}F_{1}(a,n+1;a;\lvert z\rvert)\sim e^{(n+1)\lvert z\rvert}
as
n\rightarrow+\infty
(see 2.3.2(13) in

Erdély et al., 1953
), converges. This allows one to interchange the order of summation in (
A6.2
) to
obtain

\frac{l(z)-1}{z}=\sum_{\nu\geq 0}c_{\nu}z^{\nu}\qquad\text{($\lvert z\rvert<1$)}

where

(A6.3)

c_{\nu}=\sum_{k=0}^{\nu}(-1)^{k}\,\frac{(\nu-k+1)_{k}}{(\nu-k+1)!}\,\frac{\mu_{k}^{(\nu-k)}}{k!}

for
\nu=0,1,\ldots
. To compute
\mu^{(n)}_{k}
one may equate the
k
-th derivatives, at
z=0
, of both
sides of (
A6.1
) (for the left side one might need the Faà di Bruno formula concerning the successive
derivatives of composite functions). Since
D(0)=1
, there is
r\in(0,1)
for which the following expansion
of
L
can be derived by means of the ordinary rules, i.e.

L(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\beta_{n}z^{n}\qquad\lvert z\rvert<r

with

\displaystyle\beta_{0}

\displaystyle=1/c_{0}=1

\displaystyle\beta_{n}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\nu=1}^{n}c_{\nu}\beta_{n-\nu}\qquad\text{($n=1,2,\ldots$)}

c_{0},c_{1},\ldots
being the same as in (
A6.3
).

For the sake of illustration, one determines
\beta_{1}
and
\beta_{2}
.

\displaystyle\beta_{1}=-c_{1}\beta_{0}=-c_{1}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{k=0}^{1}(-1)^{k}\,\frac{(2-k)_{k}}{(2-k)!}\,\frac{\mu_{k}^{(1-k)}}{k!}

\displaystyle=-\biggl(\frac{1}{2}-1\biggr)=\frac{1}{2}

entailing
L^{\prime}(0)=\beta_{1}=1/2
;

\displaystyle\beta_{2}=-\sum_{\nu=1}^{2}c_{\nu}\beta_{2-\nu}

\displaystyle=-\biggl(-\frac{1}{4}+c_{2}\biggr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}-\sum_{k=0}^{2}(-1)^{k}\,\frac{(3-k)_{k}}{(3-k)!}\,\frac{\mu^{(2-k)}_{k}}{k!}

\displaystyle=\mu^{(1)}_{1}-\frac{11}{12}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\biggl(1+\frac{1}{2}\biggr)-\frac{11}{12}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{6}

entailing
L^{\prime\prime}(0)=2\beta_{2}=-1/3
. Cf. Figures
1(b)
and
1(c)
.

The function
D
can also be seen as analytic continuation of a remarkable analytic special function defined
on
\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\RE z>0\}
. In fact, from a classical result (for this and next developments, cf.
HMF
,
Chapter 8),

\Phi(1,1+a,z)=az^{-a}e^{z}\gamma(a,z)\qquad\text{($\RE a>0$)}

where
z^{-a}
stands for the principal value of the power, and

\gamma(a,z)\coloneq\int_{0}^{z}x^{a-1}e^{-x}\,dx

is just the incomplete gamma function with the restriction that the integration path does not cross the
negative real axes. Then,

\displaystyle D(z)

\displaystyle=e^{z}\cdot z^{-(z+1)}\gamma(z+1,z)\qquad\text{($\RE z>0$)}

\displaystyle(=\Gamma(z+1)e^{z}z^{-(z+1)}P(z+1,z))

and, by virtue of a well-known recurrence relation,

z\cdot D(z)=z\Gamma(z)\biggl(\frac{e}{z}\biggr)^{\!\!z}P(z,z)-1\qquad\text{($\RE z>0$)}.

These remarks will come in handy in
A7
and
A8
, in which the problem of finding exact and
asymptotic evaluations of
L(\vartheta)
, and related functions, is dealt with.

A7
Evaluation of
L(\vartheta)
for small and moderate values of
\vartheta

With a view to computing
L(\vartheta)
it suffices to pay attention to
D(\vartheta)
which, according to the
final statements of
A6
, satisfies

\displaystyle\vartheta D(\vartheta)

\displaystyle=\vartheta\Gamma(\vartheta)\biggl(\frac{e}{\vartheta}\biggr)^{\!\!\vartheta}P(\vartheta,\vartheta)-1\qquad\text{($\vartheta>0$)}

\displaystyle=\vartheta\Gamma(\vartheta)e^{\vartheta}\gamma^{*}(\vartheta,\vartheta)-1

where
\gamma^{*}
is the well-known Tricomi variant of
\gamma
. If
\vartheta
is not large, then one may
compute
D
by resorting to certain numerical series deduced from 8.7.1 in
HMF
, to obtain

\displaystyle D(\vartheta)

\displaystyle=\Gamma(\vartheta)\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{\vartheta^{k}}{\Gamma(\vartheta+k+1)}

\displaystyle=e^{\vartheta}\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!(\vartheta+k)}\qquad\text{($\vartheta>0$)}.

There are also various tables of values of
\gamma,P,\ldots
. It is worth mentioning the classical work of

Pearson, 1922
in which the values of

I(u,p)\coloneq\frac{1}{\Gamma(p+1)}\int_{0}^{u\sqrt{p+1}}e^{-x}x^{p}\,dx

are tabled for
p>-1
and
u>0
,
I
being obviously connected with
P
through the relation

P(\vartheta,x)=I\biggl(\frac{x}{\sqrt{\vartheta}},\vartheta-1\biggr)\qquad\text{($\vartheta,x>0$)}.

Of course, there is also a great deal of software for computing the above functions.

A8
Asymptotic approximation of
L
(large
\vartheta
)

The representations of
D
displayed at the end of
A6
are likewise suitable for the study of the
asymptotic behaviour of
D
and related functions, as
z\rightarrow\infty
. Before dealing with the rather particular
cases of interest for the present paper, it is worth recalling that the assertion “the formal series

\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}a_{s}z^{-s}
is an
asymptotic
(or
Poincaré
) expansion of the function
f(z)

– in symbols,
f(z)\sim\sum_{s\geq 0}a_{s}z^{-s}
– as
z\rightarrow\infty
in the unbounded set

\mathscr{C}\subset\mathbb{C}
”, means that

(A8.1)

z^{n}\biggl\{f(z)-\sum_{s=0}^{n-1}\frac{a_{s}}{z^{s}}\biggr\}\rightarrow a_{n}

as
z\rightarrow\infty
in
\mathscr{C}
, uniformly w.r.t.
\arg z
, for each natural
n
.

In point of fact, it follows from
HMF
8.12.18–8.12.20 and
Paris, 2016
that

(A8.2)

\sqrt{\vartheta}D(\vartheta)\sim\sum_{s\geq 0}f_{s}\vartheta^{-s/2}\qquad\text{($\vartheta\rightarrow+\infty$)}

where

f_{0}=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}},\quad f_{1}=-\frac{2}{3},\quad f_{s}=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}A_{s}+B_{s}\quad\text{for $s=2,3,\ldots$}

and
A_{s}=A_{s}(0)
,
B_{s}=B_{s}(0)
are the same as in
Paris, 2016
, Table 2 with
x=0
: e.g.,
A_{0}=1
,

A_{2}=1/12
,
A_{4}=1/288
, … ,
A_{1}=A_{3}=A_{5}=\ldots=0=B_{0}=B_{2}=B_{4}=\ldots
,
B_{1}=1/3
,
B_{3}=4/135
,

B_{5}=-8/2835
, … ; hence
f_{0}\approx 1.25331
,
f_{1}\approx-0.66667
,
f_{2}\approx 0.10444
,
f_{3}\approx 0.02963
,
f_{4}\approx 0.00435
,
f_{5}\approx-0.00282
.

Consequently, one has

\frac{1}{\sqrt{\vartheta}D(\vartheta)}\sim\sum_{s\geq 0}\frac{k_{s}}{\vartheta^{s/2}}\qquad\text{($\vartheta\rightarrow+\infty$)}

where
k_{0}=1/f_{0}=\sqrt{2/\pi}\approx 0.79788
,
k_{1}=-f_{1}/f_{0}^{2}=4/(3\pi)\approx 0.42441
,
k_{2}=(f_{1}^{2}-f_{0}f_{2})/f_{0}^{3}=(2/\pi)^{3/2}(4/9-\pi/24)\approx 0.15926
,

k_{3}=(-f_{1}^{3}+2f_{0}f_{1}f_{2}-f_{0}^{2}f_{3})/f_{0}^{4}=-(2/\pi)^{2}\cdot(8/27+\pi(1/18+2/135))\approx-0.20968
, …

k_{s}=-\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\sum_{i=1}^{s}f_{i}k_{s-i}\qquad\text{($s=1,2,\ldots$)}

and, more importantly,

\displaystyle L(\vartheta)

\displaystyle\sim\sqrt{\vartheta}\sum_{s\geq 0}\frac{k_{s}}{\vartheta^{s/2}}\qquad\text{($\vartheta\rightarrow+\infty$)}

\displaystyle\approx\sqrt{\vartheta}\,\biggl\{0.79788+\frac{0.42441}{\sqrt{\vartheta}}+\frac{0.15926}{\vartheta}-\frac{0.20968}{\vartheta^{3/2}}+\ldots\biggr\}.

The above asymptotic expansion (
A8.2
) is valid for
\lvert z\rvert\rightarrow+\infty
in
\lvert\arg z\rvert<\pi/2
. This
implies that there are
\rho>0
and
\eta\in(0,z/2)
such that
zD(z)
has no zeros in the annular sector

S_{\eta,\rho}\coloneq\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\lvert z\rvert\geq\rho,\lvert\arg z\rvert<\pi/2-\eta\}
. Moreover, since one knows, from

A6
, that
D
is analytic on
\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0,-1,-2,\ldots\}
, then
L(z)=\dfrac{z}{zD(z)}
is
analytic on
S_{\eta,\rho}
. This fact allows differentiation of the asymptotic expansion of
L
any number
of times in the sector
S_{\eta,\rho}
. See
Henrici, 1977
, Theorem 11.3f. In particular, one obtains

L^{\prime}(\vartheta)\sim\frac{1}{2}\frac{k_{0}}{\vartheta^{1/2}}-\sum_{s\geq 3}\frac{s-2}{2}\frac{k_{s-1}}{\vartheta^{s/2}}\qquad\text{($\vartheta\rightarrow+\infty$)}

and

L^{\prime\prime}(\vartheta)\sim-\frac{1}{4}\frac{k_{0}}{\vartheta^{3/2}}+\sum_{s\geq 5}\frac{(s-2)(s-4)}{4}\frac{k_{s-3}}{\vartheta^{s/2}}\qquad\text{($\vartheta\rightarrow+\infty$)}.

v^{2}(\vartheta)=\vartheta+L(\vartheta)[1-L(\vartheta)]\sim\frac{\pi-2}{\pi}\vartheta+\alpha_{1}\sqrt{\vartheta}+\alpha_{2}+\sum_{s\geq 1}\frac{\alpha_{s+2}}{\vartheta^{s/2}}\qquad\text{($\vartheta\rightarrow+\infty$)}
