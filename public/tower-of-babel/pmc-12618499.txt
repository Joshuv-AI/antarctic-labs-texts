Exploratory analysis of seasonal variability and recovery potential of Al, Cu, Fe, and Zn in municipal solid waste incineration bottom ash
Authors: Monika Chuchro, Barbara Bielowicz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe growing demand for metals such as aluminum, copper, iron, and zinc has driven the search for alternative sources. Waste-to-Energy markets enable energy generation and recovery of valuable materials from municipal incinerator bottom ash. This study addresses the occurrence of seasonal variability in metal concentrations, its potential impact on recovery efficiency, and the comparability of incineration residues with natural deposits. Unlike previous research, our approach focused on identifying possible seasonal drivers, interpreting correlations between metals, and comparing bottom ash composition with residues from other plants and natural ores, thereby supporting the concept of sustainable resource management. We analyzed samples collected over 52 weeks to identify seasonal variations in metal concentrations. Seasonal analysis and correlation matrices revealed variability and weak dependencies between metal concentrations. A comparative analysis was performed across European and Asian waste-to-energy facilities. The mean value of aluminum in Poland is 32,481 ppm, while in other cities it ranges from 17,000 to 85,000 ppm. Copper concentrations range from 597 to 25,100 ppm (4567 ppm in Poland). Our results show that the concentrations of analyzed metals vary throughout the year, with colder seasons exhibiting higher levels than warmer periods of the year, highlighting the need for effective recycling strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23794-2.Keywords: Bottom ash, Metals, Municipal solid waste incineration, Exploratory data analysisSubject terms: Chemistry, Climate sciences, Environmental sciences

Introduction

In view of the growing demand for various metals, primarily aluminum, copper, iron, and zinc, which are utilized in diverse technologies, intense searches for their potential sources continue. These metals play crucial roles in strategic technologies impacting climate, environment, energy, and production chains across various industries. Municipal Solid Waste Incineration Plants (MSWI) known also as Waste-to-Energy (WtE) plants are a key component of contemporary waste management strategies. Beyond reducing waste volume, they enable energy generation and recovery of valuable materials, including the above-mentioned elements. These metals have widespread applications in industry, and their recycling fits into the concept of Circular Economy (CE). By reintroducing these metals into circulation, we can limit natural resource exploitation, reduce greenhouse gas emissions, and decrease the amount of waste deposited on landfills1,2.

Metal recovery from municipal waste incineration residues is implemented in various countries worldwide using technologies such as magnetic separation, density separation, flotation, and hydrometallurgical processes3. A key challenge is not only recovering elements in a form suitable for reuse but also minimizing the negative environmental impact of their processing. The high variability of municipal waste composition in different regions worldwide further complicates these processes, requiring adaptation to local conditions4,5.

During waste incineration, metals present in the flue gas stream undergo a series of physical and chemical processes6; metals can occur in three main forms, including solid residues in bottom ash, where the majority of heavy and non-ferrous metals settle in this fraction. A second form is fine fractions in fly ash, where metals with low boiling points, such as copper and aluminum, are present and can occur in the form of particles transported by flue gases. In the gaseous phase, certain elements, such as aluminum, can also be present.

The main goal of this research is to determine the stability of selected metals’ occurrence, which is essential for determining the direction of their processing and recovery from valuable materials. In the study, we analyzed the variability of waste generated over 1 year.

According to data from the Main Statistical Office, 13.4 million tons of municipal waste were collected in Poland in 2023, representing a 0.2% increase compared to 20227. On average, each resident generated 356.7 kg of waste, which is 1.9 kg more than in the previous year. The collected municipal waste was processed through various methods. Recovery processes accounted for 6,391,900 tons, or 47.5% of the total, and included recycling (2,120,200 tons or 15.8%), biological processing such as composting or fermentation (1,586,000 tons or 11.8%), and thermal conversion with energy recovery (2,685,600 tons or 20.0%). Disposal methods were used for 4,159,400 tons of waste, amounting to 30.9% of the total, which included thermal conversion without energy recovery (92,100 tons or 0.7%) and landfilling (4,067,300 tons or 30.2%).

In 2023, eight municipal waste incineration plants were operating across Poland, focusing on recovering energy—both electricity and heat—through high-efficiency cogeneration. Additionally, part of the municipal waste was co-incinerated in a combined heat and power plant in Zabrze and at cement plants. These methods accounted for 20% of the total municipal waste generated. Weight system data show that approximately 0.26 tons of secondary waste are generated per ton of incinerated municipal waste, and this amount is expected to increase in the future.

Studies conducted in Western Europe suggest that municipal waste incineration residues may serve as potential sources of essential elements for industrial use8. In Switzerland, nearly half of the operating incineration plants use FLUWA technology to recover valuable metals, such as zinc, from fly ash9.

Elements like aluminum, copper, iron, and zinc present in municipal waste originate from various sources, including everyday consumer products, technological components, and building materials. Aluminum, due to its light weight and corrosion resistance, is commonly found in waste as packaging material such as cans and foil, in building components like window and door frames, in electronic device casings, and in food packaging like Tetra Pak containers. During incineration, aluminum typically transforms into metallic aluminum due to its low melting point of 660 °C. It accumulates in the ash as small pellets or larger fragments and can be separated using wire mesh and eddy current separators. Oxidation during the process results in the formation of aluminum oxides like Al2O3, which are chemically stable and often embedded in the ash matrix. These oxides may also bind with other metal oxides and are harder to recover, although they may be reused in cement production or as raw materials in metallurgy. Some aluminum compounds, such as aluminum chlorides (e.g., AlCl3), may form in the presence of chlorine and sulfur during combustion. These compounds are highly hygroscopic, potentially mobile, and environmentally hazardous if not properly stabilized.

Copper, known for its excellent electrical conductivity, appears in waste in pure form or as part of alloys. It comes from sources such as electrical wiring, electronic devices, plumbing components, construction materials, and various household appliances. During incineration, copper can withstand high temperatures (its melting point is 1085 °C) and is often found as metallic copper in bottom ash. This metallic form is easily recoverable, though copper oxides such as CuO and Cu2O may also form, either coating the metallic surface or existing as individual particles. These oxides are more difficult to recover but are useful in hydrometallurgical processing. Sulfide and sulfate compounds of copper, including CuS and CuSO₄, may also be present, especially in fly ash. These compounds are environmentally mobile and require careful stabilization. In more complex forms, copper can also bind with aluminum oxides or be embedded in glassy or ceramic matrices, requiring chemical leaching for recovery.

Iron is the dominant metal found in secondary products from incineration. It is mainly introduced through packaging materials, construction waste, household tools, and appliances. In waste, iron appears as cast iron or stainless steel. Its magnetic properties allow for efficient recovery during recycling. Post-incineration, iron may remain in metallic form or transform into oxides such as hematite (Fe2O3) or magnetite (Fe3O4), which are less readily recoverable by mechanical means but are valuable inputs for steel production. The most challenging iron to recover is that bound within mineral matrices. The efficiency of iron recovery depends on the technology used and the composition of the ash. Countries like Germany and Japan utilize advanced magnetic separation systems capable of recovering up to 90% of iron from incineration residues.

Zinc is another important element found in municipal waste, originating from galvanized materials, used batteries, and anti-corrosion coatings. Incineration at temperatures between 800 and 1200 °C causes zinc to undergo various chemical transformations, resulting in residues across different ash types. These residues contain zinc in several chemical forms, each requiring specific recovery methods depending on their physical and chemical characteristics.

The most common form is zinc oxide (ZnO). Zinc oxide can be recovered through hydrometallurgical processes, allowing for selective precipitation and extraction of pure zinc. In fly ash, zinc chlorides are also observed, which form through reactions between zinc and hydrochloric acid (HCl) present in the flue gas10. The recovery of these compounds requires advanced neutralization and recombination methods, often preceded by separation from fly ash11.

In bottom ash, zinc occurs as stable silicates and alumino-silicates. It can form such mineral phases as willemite (Zn2SiO4) or other silicates, where it is chemically bound to silicon, oxygen, and aluminum12. These compounds are thermally and chemically stable, making them difficult to extract but potentially useful in construction as a corrosion-resistant material11.

Seasonal variability in the composition of municipal solid waste is a phenomenon observed in many regions worldwide, affecting both the total amount and the proportions of fractions (e.g., biodegradable matter, plastics, or construction debris), as confirmed by studies on seasonal waste stream characteristics13–15. Moreover, even with a constant presence of elements in the input stream, thermal processes and fractionation mechanisms (volatilization, condensation, incorporation into mineral phases) determine which metals, and in what forms, enter the bottom ash and ultimately affect their recoverability.

Despite extensive research on municipal solid waste incineration residues, the potential seasonal variability of metal concentrations remains insufficiently explored, even though such variability may significantly influence recovery efficiency and long-term management strategies. While seasonal fluctuations are well documented for biodegradable fractions16,17, their potential influence on metals has rarely been studied. Metals can be introduced in a seasonally variable manner through packaging (increased use during holiday periods), construction and demolition waste (more common in summer), or discarded electronic equipment (linked to consumer replacement cycles). Understanding these influences is essential for optimizing recovery technologies and benchmarking facilities2,18.

The main goal of this study was to identify and quantify potential seasonal patterns in aluminum, copper, iron, and zinc concentrations in municipal solid waste incineration bottom ash over a one-year period, and to evaluate how such patterns may affect the recovery potential of these metals. An additional objective was to compare the Polish results with data from other regions to contextualize possible regional differences in composition and recovery potential. Particular attention was given to identifying possible seasonal patterns and assessing statistical relationships between the studied metals. An important complementary objective was to compare the Polish results with data reported from other municipal solid waste incineration plants in Europe, Japan, and Korea, in order to evaluate regional differences and contextualize the recovery potential of these metals. Additionally, comparisons were made with ore deposits.

Materials and methods

Materials

From January to December 2021, weekly samples of incineration bottom ash were collected from the Thermal Waste Treatment Plant in southern Poland19. The plant can process up to 245,000 tons of municipal waste per year. The mixed municipal waste stream is directed towards incineration after being separated from residues and other residues generated through mechanical processing of municipal waste (following material recycling processes).

The residual flow is divided into hazardous wastes classified under EWC codes 19 01 07*—solid wastes from gas treatment and 19 01 13*—fly ash containing hazardous substances, as well as non-hazardous waste classified under EWC code 19 01 12—bottom ash and slag other than those mentioned in 19 01 11, recovered metal concentrates classified under codes EWC 19 01 02—ferrous materials removed from bottom ash, EWC 19 12 02—ferrous metal, and EWC 19 12 03—non-ferrous metal20.

The subject of the study is secondary waste with an EWC code of 19 01 12—- bottom ash and slag other than those mentioned in 19 01 11, which accounts for approximately 24% of the amount of incinerated waste processed by the plant. Immediately after the incineration process, oversized ferrous metal scrap (EWC 19 01 02) is recovered from bottom ash, where iron metals make up 73% and the content of contaminants in this residue is 27%. Furthermore, as a result of processing bottom ash, ferrous and non-ferrous metals are recovered.

Samples and data

Fifty-two samples of incineration bottom ash corresponding to each week of the 2021 year were subjected to analysis. Samples were only collected during regular working days, excluding weekends and holidays. The samples used for the study were collected from Incinerator Bottom Ash (IBA) that had undergone preliminary seasoning and valorization processes, and their collection was carried out in accordance with the guidelines specified in PN-EN 14,899:2006 “Characterization of waste—Sampling of materials—Structure preparation and application of a sampling plan”21 as well as in the publication: “List of methods—sampling and sample preparation and analysis of solid residues from thermal treatment of waste and products generated during their processing” 22. During the sampling day, eight original samples with a weight of approximately 3.5–4.0 kg each were collected using a wheel loader equipped with an automatic weighing system. The samples were taken from the end of the conveyor belt (from the falling stream of waste) at the point where it was discharged. The composite sample, consisting of eight original samples with a total maximum weight of approximately 32 kg, was then directed to a reduction process. From the original samples, a rectangular two-layer prism was formed, with each layer containing four samples. The reduction process involved dividing the prism into two equal parts by excavating it from the shorter side. The first reduced prism was sent to the next stage of reduction, while the second prism was discarded. In the final stage, two prisms with a weight of approximately 8 kg each were obtained. Sampling from these prisms was performed using a shovel, along their midsection. At this stage, approximately 300 kg of material was collected and directed to a sample splitter, resulting in laboratory samples weighing approximately 10 kg each. Prior to the preparation of analytical samples, the bottom ash samples were subjected to weighing and drying. Subsequently, to ensure that more than 70% of the sample passes through a 2 mm sieve, crushing was performed using a jaw crusher (Terminator Jaw Crusher TM Engineering). The crushed material with a mass of approximately 250 g was then ground and sieved through a 75 μm mesh.

Next, using an LM201 ESSA FLSmidth grinder, the samples were subjected to grinding. To digest the samples,

a modified royal water solution consisting of equal parts of concentrated HCl and HNO3 acids and distilled water was used. Digestion took place for one hour in a block heater. Finally, the sample was diluted with diluted HCl to its full volume.

The determination of elemental concentrations: aluminum (Al), copper (Cu), iron (Fe), and zinc (Zn) was performed using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) with an ELAN 9000 spectrometer. Quality control was performed using reference standards STD BVGEO01 and OREAS 262. Analysis of the chemical composition of bottom ash was carried out at the Bureau Veritas Commodities Canada Ltd. laboratory.

The granulometric composition and mineralogical structure (XRD) of the ash were not analyzed in this study, as the primary focus was on temporal variability of bulk metal concentrations. These analyses require dedicated experimental setups and will be addressed in future work to clarify the mineral binding forms and grain-size distribution of metals. These aspects are important for understanding the physical partitioning of metals and their mineral binding forms12. However, they require dedicated experimental setups and were beyond the scope of this article, which focused on the statistical seasonality of bulk metal concentrations. Future research will expand on this work by integrating granulometric distribution and XRD mineralogy to determine specific modes of occurrence of Al, Cu, Fe, and Zn.

For zinc, 3 out of 52 observations (5.8%) exceed the UDL. These values were replaced using the Tobit regression method23, and the substituted values are highlighted in bold in Table A1. Methods

In this paper, statistical methods and data mining techniques were employed at the stages of data preparation, data analysis, and modeling. To replace values above the upper limit of detectability, we used the Tobit regression model, which is recommended for replacing values below the lower limit of detectability (left-censored) and above the upper limit of detectability (right-censored) for environmental data24,25. Subsequent steps included exploratory data analysis (EDA), encompassing summary statistics, visualizations, and correlation analyses based on Pearson’s r and Spearman’s ρ26. Additionally, the data were analyzed to evaluate the presence of specific components in time series: trend and seasonality. To assess their presence, we created seasonal plots with box and whisker plots and column plot for trend analysis27. For comparison with selected waste incinerators plants, we used the Student’s t-test. The entire analysis was conducted in accordance with the CRISP-dm methodology, allowing each step of data analysis and results to be reproducible28.

The Pearson’s linear correlation analysis is used to assess the relationship between two numerical variables. The Pearson correlation coefficient (r) quantifies the strength and direction of the relationship between two variables, ranging from − 1 (perfect negative correlation) to 1 (perfect positive correlation), with 0 indicating no correlation. Pearson’s correlation is parametric, in the sense that it relies heavily on the “mean” parameter for the two objects. This means that Pearson’s correlation might have higher validity for a normal distribution, with a centered mean and no outliers, than for a distribution that is not normally distributed26,30. The statistical significance of the correlation is determined using a hypothesis test, which compares the observed r value with a critical value. The critical value depends on the degrees of freedom (n − 2) and the chosen significance level (α). The table of critical values for two-tailed tests provides the r values that are statistically significant at a given α level. For instance, with n = 50 and α = 0.05, the critical r value is approximately 0.279. If the calculated absolute r value exceeds this threshold, it can be concluded that the correlation is statistically significant at α = 0.05. Otherwise, if the r value is below the critical value, no significant relationship between the variables can be inferred.

The Spearman’s rank correlation is a second correlation used in this paper. It is a non-parametric statistical test that serves to determine the existence of a relationship between two variables. Unlike Pearson’s linear correlation, Spearman’s rank correlation does not require the assumption of normality of the data distribution. Spearman’s rank correlation is calculated based on the ranks of the data, rather than their absolute values. The interpretation of ρ is the same as for Pearson’s linear correlation26,30. Often, both correlations—Pearson’s linear and Spearman’s rank—are used together to determine the existence of a relationship between two variables. Pearson’s linear correlation is more sensitive to data that are normally distributed, whereas Spearman’s rank correlation is more robust to non-normally distributed data or data with outliers. By using both correlations together, a more detailed picture of the relationship between variables can be obtained. For example, if Pearson’s linear correlation shows significant correlation but Spearman’s rank correlation is lower or not significant, it may indicate that the relationship between variables is linear. Conversely, if Spearman’s rank correlation is higher and significant but Pearson’s linear correlation is lower or not significant, it may indicate that the relationship between variables is non-linear or that the data are non-normally distributed30,31.

One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) is one of the most frequently used statistical methods to determine the significance of differences between data subsets. Analysis of variance is used when there are at least three mutually independent groups with normal distribution and similar variance. ANOVA uses the F -for statistical significance. The F-test compares the variance in each group mean from the overall group variance. If the variance within the group is smaller than the variance between groups, the F-test obtains a higher F value, and thus a higher likelihood that the observed difference is significant. The null hypothesis is that there is no statistically significant difference between group means. The alternative hypothesis is that there are at least two groups that are statistically significantly different from each other32,33.

A one-sample t-test is a statistical test used to determine whether an unknown population mean is different from a specific value. This test should be performed for large samples (more than 30 observations). The t-test is highly sensitive to non-detects33,34.

Results

The integration of municipal solid waste incineration plants into contemporary waste management strategies has been shown to be highly effective in reducing waste volume and generating energy (WtE). However, this approach also poses significant challenges related to metal recovery from combustion residues. The high variability of municipal solid waste composition and the need for efficient and environmentally friendly processing technologies highlight the complexity of this issue.

Exploratory data analysis

A descriptive analysis was conducted for four time series, each consisting of 52 observations, representing the concentrations of elements identified in bottom ash from municipal waste incineration. The analysis began with exploratory data analysis (EDA), including descriptive statistics, column plots, and correlation matrices for the examined elements. Subsequent steps involved seasonal trend assessment and t-test calculations.

The analyzed elements are commonly found in bottom ash, but their concentrations and statistical characteristics highlight important differences in variability and potential recovery. Aluminum and iron dominate the composition, exceeding (aluminum) or close to (iron) 30,000 ppm on average although aluminum shows greater variability between seasons, while iron remains more stable, with distinct periods of increased concentration. Copper and zinc occur at much lower levels, in the range of several thousand ppm, and both exhibit strong skewness due to extreme values and the presence of censored data above the upper detection limit. This suggests a heterogeneous distribution of non-ferrous metals in the waste stream, likely linked to irregular inputs such as discarded electronics or galvanized materials (Fig. 1, Table 1, Appendix Table A1).

The time series patterns visible in column plots indicate potential seasonal signals, particularly for aluminum and zinc, which display recurring peaks and troughs. In the case of iron, four periods of variation are observed, which may reflect both changes in waste composition and technological aspects of the incineration process. The copper series is characterized by a mid-year increase shifted relative to aluminum, suggesting different sources or combustion dynamics for these two metals (Fig. 1, Table 1, Appendix Table A1). These observations emphasize that, although all four elements are consistently present in bottom ash, their variability over time differs markedly, which is important when assessing recovery strategies and comparing data across facilities.

The occurrence of correlations between elements was assessed using Pearson’s linear correlation coefficient and Spearman’s non-parametric rank correlation (Fig. 2). For 52 observations at a significance level of 0.05 (p-value), the absolute value of the correlation coefficients exceeded 0.275, indicating statistically significant relationships (Fig. 2). It is evident from Fig. 2 that only the correlation between copper and zinc exhibited statistically significant non-linearity, with Spearman’s rank correlation (0.44) being higher than Pearson’s linear correlation coefficient (0.32).

To determine the shape of the relationship, the correlation between copper and zinc was presented as a graphical representation in Fig. 3. The relationship is clearly non-linear, with copper content increasing at a faster rate than zinc content. In the case of low copper concentrations (up to approximately 3000 ppm), a higher number of outliers can be observed for zinc. Additionally, an interesting relationship occurs at approximately 5000 ppm copper concentrations, where these samples simultaneously exhibit low zinc content with one outlier. As copper concentration increases, zinc content rises but at a slower pace. The change in the shape and strength of the correlation between these elements is clearly linked to their concentration levels. Furthermore, some samples with low copper content also show significant zinc content (e.g., samples 9, 14, 46, and 51).

High zinc content accompanied by low copper content was observed during periods outside of the summer months (e.g., samples 9, 14, 46, and 51). Moreover, some samples exhibited very high copper concentrations that were beyond the measurement capabilities of the device (UDL). These samples were obtained during spring and autumn (Appendix Table A1), including samples 17, 36, and 44. The non-linearity suggests possible phenomena of competition or substitution between waste sources, or process-related effects (sintering, volatilization). Moreover, this non-linear and variable relationship between copper and zinc highlights the need for a detailed analysis of inter-element dependencies. Understanding such detailed, including functional, relationships between elements may improve the accuracy of recovery estimates.

Seasonal analysis of elements concentration

An examination of seasonal trends for aluminum revealed a significant increase in average concentration during summer (34,600 ppm) and winter (35,200 ppm), amounting to approximately 7000 ppm more than in spring (27,200 ppm) or autumn (27,300 ppm). Furthermore, the variability of observations is most pronounced during the summer period, with an IQR of 18,200 ppm, whereas other seasons exhibit significantly lower variability (less than 10,100 ppm). The data show a clear upward trend for aluminum concentration in both autumn and winter months (Fig. 4A). The interquartile range (IQR) for aluminum exhibits similar values across seasons, ranging from 3366 ppm in autumn to 3911 ppm in winter. There are no significant seasonal trends observed in the IQR. The F-statistic for one-way ANOVA were calculated as 2.66 with a p-value of 0.0587. The value of statistics lies on the threshold of statistical significance. In the data, there are few right-tailed outliers, observed in autumn.

The differences between the highest and lowest median values for copper concentration amount to 27%. The F-statistic for one-way ANOVA was calculated as 0.726 with a p-value of 0.541. This indicates that zinc in the waste incineration samples does not exhibit seasonal trends.

The difference in average iron concentration values across seasons amounts to approximately 10% and ranges from approximately 28,100 ppm for summer to 31,100 ppm for winter. The variability of iron concentrations can be observed, with a significant change in IQR. Spring is characterized by a low value of IQR at approximately 3900 ppm. Summer has an IQR value of 7700 ppm, which is close to the winter value of 6400 ppm. The highest IQR value occurs in autumn and amounts to 17,900 ppm, or 4.6 times higher than the spring IQR value. Right-tailed outliers are observed for two seasons: spring and summer. Additionally, left-tailed outliers are also present for spring and winter (Fig. 4C). The F-statistic for one-way ANOVA was calculated as 0.523 with a p-value of 0.669. This indicates that iron in the waste incineration samples does not exhibit seasonal trends.

The difference between the lowest and highest median values for zinc ranges from approximately 1989 ppm in fall to 2534 ppm in winter. Spring and summer have relatively similar median values, with 2193 ppm and 2186 ppm, respectively. Despite having the lowest median value in fall, the fall season exhibits the highest IQR value at 3939 ppm. The IQR values for the other seasons are as follows: spring- 1808 ppm, summer- 2196 ppm, and winter- 2357 ppm. The data show only right-tailed outliers are present in both spring and winter (Fig. 4D). One-way ANOVA analysis reveals that the F-statistic is 0.751 with a p-value of 0.527, indicating that zinc concentrations in the samples of waste incineration do not exhibit significant seasonal trends.

Comparison with other MSWI and deposits

The choice of incineration plants for comparison was guided by the availability of reliable and detailed published data on aluminum, copper, iron, and zinc concentrations. To ensure geographical representativeness, both European and Asian facilities were included. Plants with substantially different waste management systems (e.g., Japan, Denmark, Switzerland, and Korea) were selected in order to illustrate the variability in bottom ash composition under diverse technological and feedstock conditions. While this selection enables a broad contextualization of the Polish results, direct comparability remains limited by differences in waste feedstock composition, pre-sorting efficiency, and applied recovery technologies. In order to assess whether the average concentration of the selected elements in the population is the same, or greater or less than in other urban waste incineration plants in Europe and Asia, a one-sample t-test was used. The two-tailed t-test and one-tailed t-test were performed to compare the average value in the sample with the assumed mean population value33. For each element, individual incinerators were selected for comparison, searching for values lower than in the sample, similar values, and higher values (Table 2). The first test had a t-statistic of 11.224 and a p-value of 1.089e − 15, which allows us to reject the null hypothesis that means are equal and accept the alternative hypothesis that the actual mean is higher than 17,000 ppm. The second test had a t-statistic of − 18.502 and a p-value less than 2.2e − 16, indicating that we can reject the null hypothesis about different means and instead conclude that the population mean is lower than 58,000 ppm.

In order to assess comparability, a single t-test was conducted to examine whether the population’s average copper concentration matched the mean value of 4700 ppm observed in Denmark (Table 2, Fig. 5B). The t-statistic was − 0.24 and the p-value was 0.8114, indicating that we do not have sufficient grounds to reject the null hypothesis that the means are equal. Higher average values for copper were observed for Japan, exceeding three times the value measured in Poland (Japan Plant A—15,300 ppm) and nearly twice in another location (Japan C—9470 ppm)35,36.

Two t-tests were performed to assess whether the average iron value in the population is higher than the mean value of 29,400 ppm for Korea, and lower than the mean value of 32,000 ppm for Switzerland-Hinwil. The first test had a t-statistic of 0.374 with a p-value of 0.355, indicating that we do not have sufficient grounds to reject the null hypothesis that the means are equal. The second test had a t-statistic of − 1.533 with a p-value of 0.066, also indicating that we cannot reject the null hypothesis that the means are equal.

Three t-tests were performed to assess whether the average zinc value in the population is higher than the mean value of 1600 ppm for Switzerland-Hinwil, similar to the mean value of 3400 ppm for Denmark-Copenhagen, or lower than the mean value of 5140 ppm for Japan Plant A . The first test had a t-statistic of 4.740 with a p-value of 8.761e − 06, indicating that we can reject the null hypothesis of equal means and accept the alternative hypothesis that the population mean is higher than Switzerland-Hinwil. The second test had a t-statistic of − 0.287 with a p-value of 0.776, not allowing us to reject the null hypothesis of equal means. The third test had a t-statistic of − 5.146 with a p-value of 2.147e − 06, indicating that we can also reject the null hypothesis of equal means and accept the alternative hypothesis that the population mean is lower than Japan Plant A.

A significant difference in average metal values is visible between European and Asian waste incinerators. Notably, high average values occur in Japanese waste incinerators. The Polish waste incinerator exhibits average copper and zinc concentrations comparable to those of the Denmark–Copenhagen incinerator and higher than those observed in Switzerland. It also exhibits a higher average concentration of aluminum compared to Switzerland- Hinwil, and an average iron concentration similar to that observed in Switzerland- Hinwil and Korea.

Discussion

The exploratory data analysis showed that the highest average concentration was recorded for aluminum (32,481 ppm), followed by iron (29,910 ppm), copper (4567 ppm), and zinc (3297 ppm). Aluminum exhibited high variability, while copper displayed skewness due to extreme values. The relationship between copper and zinc was weak and non-linear. Samples containing around 5000 ppm copper consistently exhibited low zinc content, which may indicate substitution or competition effects in waste sources, or selective volatilization and condensation during combustion36. Below this threshold, zinc values were highly variable, reflecting multiple sources and heterogeneous input streams. Although intriguing, this relationship does not allow for predictive modeling.

Statistical tests showed that seasonal differences were weak. The results indicate that not all metals exhibit statistically significant seasonal trends. Aluminum showed an almost significant dependence, which may be related to seasonal fluctuations in packaging waste and construction materials37. By contrast, copper, iron, and zinc did not exhibit systematic seasonality, which is consistent with the findings of Singhal et al.16, who also observed weak seasonal signals in inorganic fractions. This suggests that although feedstock composition fluctuates, technological factors such as furnace operation, pre-sorting efficiency, and feed homogenization attenuate seasonal signals in bottom ash. These findings align with recent work on seasonal drivers of municipal waste composition13,15, but they differ from studies reporting stronger seasonal effects in biodegradable fractions16,17. This highlights the need to differentiate between organic and inorganic fractions when assessing seasonal impacts on recovery potential.

The high variability of municipal waste incineration plant residues is a significant challenge in metal recovery. To address this issue, various technologies such as magnetic separation, density separation, flotation, and hydrometallurgical processes have been implemented worldwide18. The challenge lies not only in recovering elements in a form suitable for reuse but also minimizing the negative environmental impacts associated with their processing.

The recycling of metals from municipal solid waste incinerator bottom ash can play an important role in supporting sustainable resource management. Conversely, the zinc content in coal combustion residues exhibits high variability due to the inherent presence of metal-bearing minerals within the coal’s structural framework, which are released during the combustion process. Notably, this variability limits the recovery potential of zinc from these residues.

The results obtained were compared to the contents of aluminum, copper, iron, and zinc in exploited deposits as well as their cut-off grade (see Table 2)39–42,48. However, it is essential to note that the values provided for the analyzed residues refer to raw material, not enriched or processed material. Therefore, one should expect higher concentrations of aluminum, copper, iron, and zinc in the produced concentrates. A comparison with other facilities showed that Polish bottom ash was similar to Danish ash in terms of copper and zinc concentrations, but significantly lower in iron and aluminum compared to Japanese plants36,37. Only copper concentrations approached the cut-off values typical for ore deposits.

The aluminum content in the incineration bottom ash in Poland amounts to 32,481 ppm, which is a significantly lower value than the range of values found in natural deposits, which ranges from 80,000 to 320,000 ppm, and below economic cut-off. In the case of bottom ash samples from incinerators in Poland, the average value exceeds the European facility’s values and is below those of Asian facilities, with a median value between the two (Table 2, Fig. 5A). The literature data indicate that the average aluminum content in municipal solid waste incineration residues ranges from 30,000 to 80,000 ppm37,49,50, and these results are broadly consistent with studies from other European plants. For comparison, the aluminum content in coal combustion residues is lower and ranges from 10,000 to 20,000 ppm51. This difference arises from the greater proportion of non-organic and mineral materials being burned in municipal waste, which contain significantly more aluminum compared to coal.

The analysis revealed seasonal trends for aluminum concentrations in the samples. The average concentration was found to be highest during summer and winter. This suggests that seasonal factors, such as changes in waste input or incineration conditions, may influence aluminum concentrations in municipal waste incineration plant residues. Notably, similar seasonality patterns have been observed in other studies on elemental concentrations in waste incineration products16,17,52. These findings suggest that seasonal trends in elemental concentrations may be a common phenomenon in municipal waste incineration plants, and further research could explore the underlying causes of these patterns.

Meanwhile, the copper content in the combustion residue from a municipal incineration plant in Poland is 4567 ppm, which falls within a range of values determined by the cut-off grade, ranging from 2000 to 5000 ppm. In contrast, the copper content in natural deposits is between 2000 and 10,000 ppm. The copper content in IBA from Poland is significantly higher than in coal ash, where copper concentrations range from 500 to 1500 ppm4,53. Similar results were obtained for other European waste incineration facilities (Table 2, Fig. 5B), where copper values fall within the range of 2000–5000 ppm4,37. The obtained results, however, are lower than those reported for Japanese plants, where much higher concentrations have been documented35,36. This underlines the influence of regional consumption and waste management practices on residue composition. These differences arise from the presence of non-ferrous metals in household waste, particularly electronic elements, wires, or other components containing copper that end up in incinerators. In contrast, coal ash is characterized by a significantly lower copper content, which limits the potential for copper recovery from such waste.

European and Korean waste incineration plants have similar average iron values4,37,38. Japan has significantly higher average values for iron, ranging from twice to approximately 2.5 times the values of European solid waste incineration plants35,36 (Table 2, Fig. 5C). In Poland, iron in waste occurs at an average concentration of 29,910 ppm, which is a significantly lower value than found in natural deposits (150,000–680,000 ppm) and below the cut-off grade of 150,000 ppm. Coal-burning ash contains much higher levels of iron, reaching ranges of 20–88%54. The higher iron content in coal-burning ash is due to the natural presence of iron-bearing minerals in coal structure, which remain in the residues after combustion. As a result, coal-burning ash has greater potential for iron recovery than municipal waste incinerated.

The zinc content in the samples is higher than in Korea and Switzerland-Hinwil plants, similar to the content in Denmark-Copenhagen, but lower than in Japanese incineration plants4,35–37. The content of zinc is approximately 28 times greater than in the Korean facility, twice as much as in Hinwil, but also about 1.5 times less than Japan Plant A and Japan C, and even 6 times less than Japan Plant B (Table 2, Fig. 5D)4,35,36,38. In the case of coal bottom ash, zinc content is slightly lower and ranges from 52 to 237 ppm55. The differences can be attributed to a larger amount of galvanized materials and zinc alloys present in municipal waste, which release zinc into the ash during combustion. Zinc content in Polish raw municipal waste is also below the threshold for economic recovery, as the cutoff grade for zinc is 10,000 ppm, while natural deposits typically contain between 10,000 and 40,000 ppm of zinc.

From an economic perspective, copper recovery from Polish bottom ash appears the most promising, while aluminum, iron, and zinc remain below economic thresholds. Nevertheless, their recovery may still be justified on environmental grounds, contributing to circular economy objectives. This aligns with global discussions on critical raw materials recovery from secondary sources36,38. Recent studies indicate that large-scale implementation of recycling can contribute to a reduction in greenhouse gas emissions and to a decrease in the amount of waste sent to landfills. In particular, the recovery of metals from bottom ash is associated with substantially lower greenhouse gas emissions compared to conventional mining and extraction processes5,56,57. However, as other authors have noted50 (e.g., Pan et al., 2008), the feasibility of large-scale recovery depends on integrating mineralogical analysis, advanced separation methods, and life-cycle assessments, which were outside the scope of the present study.

This study has several limitations. First, the analysis covered only one year of data, which restricts the ability to capture inter-annual variability or long-term trends. Second, only four metals (Al, Cu, Fe, Zn) were examined, while other potentially valuable or hazardous elements were not included. Third, comparisons with international plants relied on published data that may differ in sampling and analytical protocols, limiting direct comparability. Finally, granulometric and mineralogical analyses were not performed, which constrains interpretation of metal binding forms and recovery feasibility. Future research integrating multi-year datasets, broader elemental coverage, and combined geochemical–mineralogical approaches will help to address these gaps.

Conclusions

This study highlights that municipal solid waste incineration residues represent a secondary resource with variable recovery potential. Aluminum, copper, iron, and zinc were consistently present, but only copper concentrations approached economic cut-off levels. Seasonal variability was generally weak; however, aluminum exhibited moderate fluctuations that may be linked to changes in packaging and construction waste inputs.

By reintroducing these metals into circulation, we can limit natural resource exploitation, reduce greenhouse gas emissions, and decrease the amount of waste deposited on landfills. However, feasibility differs between elements, with copper recovery being most economically promising, while aluminum, iron, and zinc remain below economic thresholds.

The high variability in municipal waste composition across regions underscores the need for site-specific recovery strategies. Advanced separation and hydrometallurgical technologies offer opportunities for sustainable recovery, but further integration of mineralogical data and life-cycle analysis is required to assess fully evaluate feasibility.

In an era of growing urbanization and municipal waste production, incinerators play a key role in waste management. However, the combustion process generates large amounts of ash containing significant concentrations of heavy metals. Effective metal recovery from these ashes not only reduces the amount of waste deposited in landfills but also contributes to reducing the exploitation of natural resources. Metals such as aluminum, copper, iron, and zinc require substantial energy and environmental input for primary extraction, and recycling them can significantly reduce greenhouse gas emissions. Metal recovery from ash emits substantially fewer greenhouse gases than conventional extraction processes, while simultaneously limiting the disposal of potentially valuable metals. Specifically, municipal solid waste incineration residues contain higher concentrations of copper and zinc, indicating greater heavy metal pollution in this fraction. In contrast, coal combustion ash typically shows lower copper and zinc levels but higher amounts of aluminum and iron. . Both ash from waste incinerators and coal combustion require proper treatment, but they can be potentially economically viable for metal recovery.

Compared to other locations, such as Japan (Plant B: 2510 ppm) and Korea (597 ppm), copper concentrations in Polish IBA are significantly higher and suggesting potential economic viability for metal recovery36,38. Higher values may be associated with inaccurate separation of municipal waste by households, resulting in a greater proportion of metal-containing waste in the waste stream entering the incineration facility. Consequently, ash from the Polish waste incineration plant has high potential for metal recovery due to its relatively large concentrations of aluminum, copper, iron, and zinc. The implementation of metal enrichment technologies, such as magnetic separation, flotation, or hydrometallurgical methods, can significantly increase the recovery efficiency.

In conclusion, the high variability of municipal waste incineration plant residues presents a significant challenge for metal recovery. However, by implementing advanced recovery technologies and recycling metals from these residues, it is possible to limit natural resource exploitation, reduce greenhouse gas emissions, and decrease the amount of waste deposited in landfills.
