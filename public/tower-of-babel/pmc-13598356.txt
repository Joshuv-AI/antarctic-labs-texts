Concrete Behaviours Expected in Interdisciplinary Teams in Home Nursing Care Inspired by Buurtzorg: Qualitative Descriptive Study
Authors: Benjamin Olivares Bøgeskov, Gry Segoli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundInterdisciplinary teams in home care and home nursing are being implemented across Denmark, inspired by the Buurtzorg model. While structural aspects have been studied, less is known about the concrete, observable behaviours that make interdisciplinary teams function well in practice.AimTo identify and thematise observable behaviours in well‐functioning interdisciplinary teams' meetings in home care and home nursing care, thereby contributing to practice development and implementation.MethodThe study used a qualitative descriptive, practice‐oriented design to identify concrete behaviours rather than generate new theory. Data were collected during the implementation of interdisciplinary teams in a metropolitan municipality covering 10 districts and about 185 professionals. Material comprised 14 preliminary observations, 64 structured participatory observations of team meetings across all the municipality's 16 interdisciplinary teams, and two individual and three focus group interviews with staff and leaders. Data were analysed thematically, with two authors coding independently before refining themes iteratively through discussion.Ethical IssuesThe study complied with the Danish Code of Conduct for Research Integrity and national legislation. Informed consent was obtained, and GDPR requirements were observed.ResultsThirty‐six observable actions were identified and grouped into four sets of behaviours: (1) performing meeting goals (shared understanding, shared solving, shared deciding); (2) following the meeting's methodology (role clarity, documentation, participatory dialogue); (3) creating conditions (presence, psychological safety, adequate facilities, and leadership support); and (4) sharing perspectives, recognising others (disciplinary and holistic perspectives, including existential care). A distinctive marker of well‐functioning teams was the practice of open questioning, which fostered sensemaking and co‐creation.LimitationsFindings are drawn from one municipality, which limits generalisability.ConclusionsWell‐functioning interdisciplinary teams are characterised less by information exchange and coordination than by achieving shared understanding, collective problem‐solving, and integration of diverse perspectives. These behaviours reflect and produce a shift from efficiency‐driven management towards co‐creation and holistic care and can guide future implementation of interdisciplinary teams.Keywords: Buurtzorg, home care, home nursing, interdisciplinary teams, qualitative research, team meetings

Introduction

The growing complexity of healthcare needs among ageing populations presents major challenges to home nursing care [1, 2]. To deliver high‐quality, person‐centred care without increasing costs, several countries have sought inspiration in the Dutch Buurtzorg model [3, 4, 5]. Buurtzorg is based on small, self‐managed interdisciplinary teams (IDTs) that provide holistic care with high professional autonomy and minimal managerial oversight [4]. The model has been linked to improved patient and staff satisfaction, as well as cost reductions [3, 4].

In Denmark, authorities have supported large‐scale trials of Buurtzorg‐inspired IDTs, known locally as Faste Teams (Stable Teams). In 2021, the government invested 191 million DKK (30 million USD) to implement IDTs in 25 municipalities and has committed 371 million DKK (58 million USD) in 2024 to support a national rollout starting in autumn 2025 [6]. However, the Danish approach involves local adaptations, with varying team structures, degrees of self‐management, leadership roles, and involvement of civil society [7, 8]. This variation complicates both implementation and evaluation, raising questions about how—and to what extent—Buurtzorg principles can or should be translated into Danish home care [7, 8].

Background

The Danish experience points to promising effects of IDTs on work satisfaction, coordination, and responsiveness [7, 9] in line with international findings [5, 10]. However, successful implementation requires not only restructuring but also big cultural change and new competencies among health professionals [5, 7, 9].

Existing studies tend to focus on implementation evaluations or self‐reported outcomes [5, 7], with less attention paid to the competences and observable behaviours through which IDT care is enacted in practice—particularly during team meetings. More research is needed on the team competences required [11] and how key processes—such as communication, responsibility‐sharing, clinical decision‐making, and knowledge flow—are carried out in practice [12, 13]. Understanding the needed competences and expected behaviours is essential for guiding effective implementation and developing relevant training.

This study seeks to address this gap by identifying concrete, observable behaviours expected in an IDT meeting that enable or hinder well‐functioning IDTs in the context of home nursing care. Therefore, the research question is: What concrete, observable behaviours characterise well‐functioning IDT meetings in home care and home nursing care?

Method

This study follows the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) [14], including 21 checklist items, though not necessarily in the same order.

The implementation started during 2024. This work package consisted of observing these weekly meetings to identify concrete behavioural changes to be expected and promoted, and took place between August 2024 and January 2025.

After an initial six‐month implementation, once teams' meeting routines were well established, the research phase began. Two authors, supported by three educators, conducted participatory observations of IDT meetings in all teams to explore and describe practices that strengthen well‐functioning teams.

Study Design

The study is based on a qualitative descriptive practice‐oriented design [15] aimed at describing practice [16]. This design was chosen to identify concrete behaviours rather than to generate new theory. The methodology involved two main stages:

Stage 1. Preliminary observation and development of a context‐specific observation guide adapted to the Danish IDT structure and the participating municipality. The guide was based on 14 preliminary observations and refined through a workshop with 30 municipal leaders and staff to narrow the focus onto IDT's priority areas in their municipality and a first set of expected observable behaviours (see Figure 1).

Stage 2. Further observations and interviews guided by the observation guide. Sixty‐four additional participatory observations [17] and interviews—both focus groups and individual interviews [18]—were analysed using thematic analysis [19], as described in detail later.

Both individual and focus group interviews were conducted after the initial thematic analysis used to validate and expand emerging themes and support triangulation.

During meetings, observers used the guide to document detailed behaviours, clearly distinguishing between factual descriptions and interpretive reflections. After each meeting, participants were invited to comment on the process and reflect on successes or difficulties. This clarified the behaviours participants expected in a well‐functioning team. Observers shared key observations to prompt dialogue, requiring pedagogical sensitivity—especially in the case of negative findings—but also allowing findings to be discussed with participants, thereby strengthening triangulation. Observers could adapt to their notes by correcting possible misunderstandings and incorporating participants' own observations.

Interviews aimed to explore participants' experiences of behavioural changes following IDT implementation and their perceived contribution to well‐functioning teams.

Data Analysis

Observation notes and individual and focus group interviews (audio‐recorded and transcribed verbatim) were analysed using thematic analysis [17], with a focus on latent themes, attending to behaviours that either strengthen or weaken well‐functioning teams. Initially, the two authors independently coded observation data as it was generated, using an inductive approach to allow emerging themes to be discussed and explored, including in the focus group interviews. The focus groups further enabled participants' reflections on and assessment of the observed behaviours. Subsequently, both authors independently coded the full dataset in separate Word documents, after which codes and themes were compared, condensed, and refined collaboratively through an iterative process moving between data and themes.

Conceptual Background

Teams are defined as “a small number of people with complementary skills who are committed to a common purpose, performance goals, and approach for which they hold themselves mutually accountable” (Katzenbach & Smith, 1993, 45). A key feature is the production of a “collective work‐product”, which distinguishes teams from working groups [18]. In contrast, pseudo‐teams involve individuals who work side by side but “lack the characteristics that define a true team”, such as shared purpose, open communication, and mutual commitment [19].

Interdisciplinary collaboration involves reciprocal engagement across roles, based on cooperation, coordination, and equality [20]. However, there is growing recognition that team collaboration extends beyond efficient task coordination and must encompass shared sensemaking and co‐creation.

In this article, a well‐functioning IDT is not defined by some form of objective efficiency, but by its ability to embody the core characteristics of true team‐based collaboration as defined by the participants themselves.

Ethical Considerations

This study complies with Danish legislation, which does not require approval from an ethics committee for this type of qualitative research [21]. The study adheres to the Danish Code of Conduct for Research Integrity [22]. All participants were informed of the researchers' presence and the aims of the project. Written consent was obtained for both individual and focus group interviews. All requirements under the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) were strictly followed.

Performing the Meeting Goals

The first theme is performing meeting goals. These goals are achieved through three collective behaviours: Understanding, solving, and deciding. The processes were not linear but often unfold iteratively.

Shared Understanding

Although understanding was not immediately observable, many visible actions aimed to build a shared understanding.

As a first step, participants sought and shared existing information and experience. For example: “Two employees who together have a lot of experience with the citizen in question, who is new to this home care and previously lived alone with his or her relative, who is now in a nursing home. The citizen has an xx diagnosis, and there is a lot of relevant knowledge being shared about how the staff should approach the citizen.” Field note (FN).

They also raised awareness of what kind of information should be collected for future meetings. Here, for instance: “The team members are brainstorming about what they should observe for the next meeting. Possibly to gather information from relatives.” FN.

Creating a shared understanding went beyond reviewing information and summarising facts. It required special skills and collective effort. For example, a student in practice was asked to present a case, which proved to be a challenge; in fact: “[the student] finds it extremely difficult. A colleague contributes … and together they manage to describe the citizen.” FN. This collective effort led to better understanding, as “the analysis leads to a general discussion about the housing situation, self‐determination, and the citizen's wishes for the future.” FN.

Participants also challenged existing understanding and explored new hypotheses. For instance, a participant argued: “…there is no rehabilitation potential, and the citizen has difficulty finding motivation for training.” FN. In response, a nurse questioned the reason for this lack of motivation and asked: “. whether a citizen is unrealistic about her own abilities?” FN.

This opened space for re‐evaluating the situation and potentially uncovering new explanations for the perceived lack of progress.

Shared Solving

Shared Solving concerns reaching agreement on the problem and exploring possible responses. Without it, meetings risked becoming inconclusive, as reflected in the statement: “good to review three citizens, but no solution!” FN This pattern was also observed in later meetings, where discussions “end without a conclusion or action.” FN.

The process involved: (1) agreeing on the problem and (2) identifying solutions. In acute cases, problems were easily defined and managed by standard procedures. However, in complex cases—such as citizens with multiple illnesses, difficult social conditions, or those categorised as “green citizens”, meaning individuals triaged as clinically stable—problem definition was more challenging. Take the example of the complex situation:

“The helper reports that the vacuum cleaner is broken. The citizen states that he/she does not have money to buy a new one. It is mentioned that the son has withdrawn from the relationship due to disagreements concerning finances. There is a discussion in the team about what should be done – guardianship, one‐time assistance, or speaking with the citizen.” FN.

For “green citizens,” dialogue often lacked direction. As a result, “because there is no immediate issue to solve, the dialogue often remains vague, searching for a potential problem. If no clear problem emerges, the conclusion is often: “let us keep an eye on XX, and report if anything changes.”” FN.

Once a problem was defined, the team sought solutions and an action plan. Yet sometimes discussions stalled: “There is a discussion about guardianship and power of attorney, but without any follow‐up or a plan being established.” FN.

Problem definition and solution‐finding frequently overlap. Furthermore, issues perceived as beyond the team's mandate were dismissed:

“She is lonely. There is nothing we can do about it.” FN.

Sometimes proposed solutions reshape the definition of the problem, such as lack of motivation:

“The occupational therapist suggests possible welfare technologies, which are quickly dismissed, as the citizen is not motivated for this.” FN.

Creative, even counterintuitive, proposals may arise. For example:

“If we remove house cleaning – what happens? Another says: Nothing; she will be activated.” FN.

In well‐functioning teams, action plans extend beyond the group, drawing on external expertise and support.

Shared Deciding

After defining the problem and discussing solutions, the team had to decide on a course of action. By taking shared responsibility, they assigned clear responsibilities for each action. In well‐functioning teams, decisions were shared, clearly defined in steps, and delegated to the appropriate individuals.

In poorly functioning teams, decisions were often taken solely by the formal or informal leader, without consensus. By contrast, team leaders in well‐functioning teams allowed the group to reach shared decisions, using authority only when necessary. As a care assessor puts it: “It is not for me to say yes or no, but we must decide together.” FN.

Furthermore, decisions were expected to remain open to discussion: “The meeting leader asks questions about decisions and holds pauses, allowing time for additional perspectives.” FN.

Once a shared decision is made, both the plan and responsible individuals must be identifiable to all, ensuring acceptance and clarity about next steps. Normally, this task is handled by the meeting leader, not necessarily the team leader, as can be seen in this field note:

"The meeting leader creates a plan for follow‐up, [including] specialised professional efforts and contacts the hospital for KAD bags, as well as ensuring that the user is followed up on regarding heart evaluation and the need for compression stockings. When the contact person returns from leave, the meeting leader will ask the contact person to create a new daily rhythm plan. All of this is documented and updated in the electronic patient record system by the meeting leader." FN.

In well‐functioning teams, a sense of shared responsibility is evident. As much as possible, all participants were engaged. However, shared responsibility does not mean lack of clarity. On the contrary, it is characterised by clear delegation of tasks:

“It is agreed that the assistant will be the point of contact for the citizen and will work on building a relationship with the citizen to create a sense of security for the citizen.” FN.

Following the Meeting's Methodology

Team meetings pursued their goals by following a concrete methodology. While practice varied across teams, we observed a set of recurring actions essential to achieving the meetings' purpose: Role clarity, documentation, and participatory dialogue.

Knowing and Performing One's Role

Meetings functioned best when roles were assigned and enacted. Across teams, three roles consistently appeared: Facilitator, screen coordinator, and documentation coordinator. In established teams, roles were allocated easily, often agreed upon previously, while in newer teams the process could be unstructured, as illustrated in this observation: “There was no clear distribution of roles; even though it was asked at the beginning of the meeting who had which role, this was not answered. In general, it feels messy and unstructured. The leader has taken on most of the roles (meeting leader + moderator) without having defined this to the team members.” FN.

Although roles were intended to rotate, they were often taken by the same individuals, either due to expertise or confidence, as a team coordinator explains: “some professional groups do not feel ready to take on the responsibilities that come with the roles.” FN.

Rotation nevertheless supported skill development, reduced vulnerability to staff changes, and occasionally led to innovations in meeting structure.

The facilitator typically steered dialogue by following the agenda, encouraging perspectives, prompting reflection, guiding decision‐making, summarising agreements, and keeping time. However, where facilitation was weak, meetings became opaque:

“There is no written agenda or other material for common information, but it is conducted verbally by the chairperson. It is unknown how and who has selected the citizens, and on what issues. We will find this out later when the individual citizens are reviewed.” FN.

In some cases, district leaders acted as facilitators, which increased efficiency but sometimes narrowed dialogue and reduced participation.

The screen coordinator made the citizen's information visible to all, enhancing clarity and shared understanding, as noted by a care assessor: “when the screen is shared, something special actually happens.” FN.

The documentation coordinator updated records in real time, aligning spoken and written accounts and ensuring agreements were not lost.

Participating in the Dialogue: Talking, Listening, Asking

Three observable actions characterised high‐quality dialogue: Everyone speaks, everyone listens, and everyone asks.

Everyone speaks: Uneven participation hindered dialogue. Dialogue was poorer where there was a “tendency for only those who know the citizen to participate.” FN.

By contrast, well‐functioning teams, even those not directly involved with the citizen, contributed insights. For example, a “care assessor, who has known the institution [not the citizen] for many years, offers insights” FN.

Everyone listens: Selective listening undermined dialogue, while mutual attention enabled co‐created solutions.

“The meeting leader takes the floor too much. There are two nurses at another table who only contribute when it is relevant to them and sit and talk among themselves when it is not relevant to them.” FN.

Everyone asks: Participants posed open questions, exposing their own uncertainty and inviting reflection. In certain cases: “Where [team members] may have doubts, they asked curious questions of the professional group that has more knowledge in the area. Example: Social and healthcare helpers express: ‘How does it relate to guardianship and power of attorney matters?’” FN.

They also asked fundamental open questions, such as: “How do we best help him?” FN.

In well‐functioning teams, questioning was not confined to leaders but practised across all participants.

Updating Documentation: Read and Write in Real Time

Although dialogue dominated the meetings, documentation—both reading and writing—was integral. Participants compared oral discussion with written notes, correcting inconsistencies, as occasionally there were “doubts about whether the documentation reflected what was said verbally.” FN.

Real‐time documentation, particularly on a shared screen, created a common language and consensus on formulations, while also revealing platform limitations such as missing fields for team decisions.

Documentation often acted as an additional participant, extending dialogue beyond participants' memory. Reading aloud supported understanding by creating common knowledge, since “not everyone was aware of or has read about the citizen's overall problem,” FN and “other professional groups were unsure whether agreements/plans were documented.” FN.

Updating documentation collectively ensured accuracy and visibility of perspectives, while offering insight into professional languages.

“Example of [team becoming aware of] missing documentation. This made the group aware that the citizen's situation had changed, had become increasingly complex, and was not up to date.” FN.

Yet this thoroughness came at a cost, reducing the number of citizens reviewed:

“Participant: “It is nice to see what is being documented, but it delays the next citizen review.”” FN.

Creating Conditions

The third theme concerns actions that create the necessary conditions for IDTs to function.

Being Present—On Time, in the Right Place, in the Right Team

For meetings to succeed, they had to occur regularly, on time, and with the right participants. Observations showed variation: Some teams started punctually with all present, whereas others began late, participants left early, answered calls, or held side conversations. As occasionally noted, “several [participants] arrive a few minutes late.” FN.

Leadership played a key role in protecting time and space for meetings and ensuring facilities were suitable, as complaints about noise and lack of privacy were common. Team performance was also undermined when relevant professional groups were absent—whether nurses, planners, or assistants—or when the worker with a close relationship to the citizen was missing. In such cases, participants described the dialogue as pointless.

Leader's comment: “If xx and xx (groups) of professionals do not show, the others start wondering how important it is for them to attend.” Individual Interview.

Frequent staff redeployment also eroded relevance: Professionals sometimes cared for citizens outside their team, leaving them unable to contribute meaningfully. Occasional late arrivals with coffee suggested individual habits, but persistent absence reflected structural issues, such as weak leadership in building team culture or organisational failures in securing time and staffing for meetings.

Creating Psychological Safety—Focus on Solutions, Not Blame

Participants emphasised the need for psychological safety, noting that “It requires us to create a psychologically safe space, and in the long run, we hope to involve everyone.” FN.

When safety was present, dialogue was open, as reflected in this observation: “there was generally a positive atmosphere among the staff, which made everyone feel comfortable in their roles and able to contribute freely with experience and knowledge.” FN. But it also allowed them to address problems: “There is talk of a situation that was difficult to handle. This indicates that there is psychological safety in the group.” FN.

By contrast, some observations showed a “tendency for troubleshooting and assigning guilt. It is evident that some participants are defending themselves” (FN), revealing that focusing on mistakes undermined psychological safety.

Praise from colleagues—not just leaders—strengthened psychological safety:

“There were two truly dedicated and experienced employees who had a good relationship with one of the reviewed citizens and received praise from their colleagues; you could really feel the engagement in the room.” FN.

Actively inviting quiet participants further reinforced trust. A particular team “is focused on incorporating silent voices.” FN.

This openness allowed questions born of genuine uncertainty and welcomed creative ideas:

“The team asks questions and suggests what to do to prevent falls. For example, the discussion about non‐alcoholic beer.” FN.

Ensuring Adequate Physical and Technical Conditions

Physical and technical conditions shaped meeting quality. Cramped, noisy, or non‐private rooms disrupted dialogue; in some cases, “there is not enough space at the table for everyone, which is why they are sitting in two circles.” FN.

Similarly, malfunctioning screens or devices hindered participation. Addressing such shortcomings was usually taken up collectively, with leadership responsible for securing improvements.

Sharing Perspectives, Recognising Others

The final theme concerns the behaviours that enable participants to view citizens from multiple perspectives. We divided these into disciplinary perspectives and holistic perspectives, spanning clinical to existential issues.

The Citizen Is Seen From All Disciplinary Perspectives

A well‐functioning team enabled participants to see the citizen from all perspectives. The first step was that each participant not only possessed competence within their own domain but also shared it. In such teams, “there are many relevant professional inputs from social and healthcare assistants and social and healthcare helpers who visit the home.” FN. However, “lack of knowledge and competence in the individual [professional] can slow down the collaboration process.” FN.

A lack of competence may slow collaboration and compromise care. Researchers noted statements or inaction that suggested insufficient expertise regarding conditions such as dementia, for example: “The absence of a clear intervention is sometimes justified as a ‘natural’ progression of dementia.” FN.

Thus, strong team culture could not compensate for absent professional expertise. In well‐functioning teams, participants not only demonstrated expertise but actively contributed their perspectives. Distinct, articulated viewpoints ensured that citizens were seen from multiple angles:

“They thoroughly covered the perspectives of the assistants, helpers, nurses, therapists, and the assessor regarding all three citizens. However, it was striking that it was these citizens in need of reablement who were present. The rehabilitation therapist herself/himself states that she/he is probably one of those who are most attentive to involving citizens.” – FN.

This shows how individual competence enabled staff to identify issues and bring them forward, inviting interdisciplinary reflection. Contributing one's disciplinary perspective was not a defence of territory but enriched the collective work. The team was not a mere collection of individuals; each member brought their perspective into shared discussion.

Well‐functioning teams also recognised one another's competencies and actively involved colleagues, as presented by a participant: “[we are] involving the right professional. Everyone knows each other's roles.” FN.

In weaker teams, this involvement was missing, since “at times, relevant professionals who sit silently in the room and could contribute with relevant knowledge are forgotten or not involved.” FN. In contrast, “in well‐functioning teams, they learn from each other.” – Interview with leader.

It is not only that participants shared their perspective; they also learned to see from many perspectives.

The Citizen Is Seen From a Holistic Perspective

The interdisciplinary view was complemented by a holistic one, where clinical issues were considered alongside social and existential concerns.

A first aspect of this holistic approach was that perspectives extended beyond the citizen's immediate situation. Teams also considered biography and future, including proactive planning and preventive efforts. For example:

“The citizen has back pain and difficulty standing. She has to call herself when she needs help. One asks: ‘Is there no form of help we can offer more consistently?’ It is asked: ‘Is it her whose husband died?’ ‘If I remember correctly, she doesn't have a network. So, it would be nice if she could go to a day centre’; ‘I will contact the citizen!’” FN

Here, attention shifted from isolated symptoms to the citizen's broader life situation. Such teams integrated rehabilitation and prevention as part of their shared mindset rather than treating them as the responsibility of one profession. Not all teams did so, since occasionally it was “missing clear and explicit rehabilitation work.” FN.

Where present, rehabilitation and prevention were understood as collective responsibilities embedded in team culture. This sometimes meant that elements of clinical leadership, usually associated with nurses, were shared. Forming a holistic understanding, defining goals, and setting next steps were not expected solely from nurses but also undertaken by occupational therapists, care assessors, or facilitators. Skilled facilitators without nursing backgrounds were observed questioning clinical decisions, prompting re‐evaluation.

Teams that successfully integrated perspectives were also more capable of what may be termed existential care, directed towards citizens and relatives alike:

“Increased awareness of citizens and relatives (wife) who do not receive help. Conversations about how to build trust and awaken motivation in the citizen.” – FN.

Attention to trust, motivation, and relatives' needs reflected a capacity to move beyond immediate problem‐solving. Addressing existential concerns—trust, motivation, loneliness, meaning—was not the task of a single profession. Rather, existential care characterised teams that had moved beyond fragmented services towards coherent, interdisciplinary care with a holistic perspective.

Discussion

Several results' relevance extends beyond this implementation, particularly the identification of actions that constitute team meeting goals—most notably ‘shared understanding’. Existing literature seldom addresses understanding itself as a desirable team skill, despite focusing on related concepts—for example, critical reflection [23, 24], improved information exchange [25], or communication skills [13, 25, 26]. The dominant theoretical framing tends to view IDTs primarily as instances of “information sharing and care coordination across professional boundaries”(O'Donnell et al., 2025a, 30).

In our view, such a framing is limited and does not fully reflect what the observed teams were striving to achieve. More than sharing information, our data suggest that teams were making sense of information while learning to view issues from other disciplinary perspectives. This resonates with recent work positioning sensemaking as a central process in interprofessional communication, where shared meaning is constructed rather than information merely exchanged [27]. More than coordinating predefined tasks, they were seeking creative solutions to wicked problems. At the core of both processes was the pursuit of a shared understanding.

Well‐functioning teams frequently asked open questions, moving beyond task coordination into collective inquiry. Such questioning included reflection on their own roles and limitations. In this sense, the teams we observed illustrate what has been described as the “(new) collaborative turn” in older people's care [28], moving away from the efficiency‐oriented model of New Public Management—where teams simply coordinate tasks—towards co‐creation.

Our findings also provide insight into the role of professional disciplines within teams. Literature emphasises the importance of strong professional identity and role clarity for effective teamwork [12, 29]. IDTs can disrupt established identities and roles [9], particularly among nurses [30, 31], when long‐standing mono‐professional patterns are challenged or when professionals are asked to perform tasks perceived as beyond their scope [7]. Our data point to a need for clear meeting‐related roles—such as chairing or documentation—but were less conclusive on the need for sharply distinguished professional roles.

A team cannot compensate for lack of individual competence; professionals must be skilled in their fields. Yet as teams build trust and knowledge, some role fluidity emerged—for instance, coordinators occasionally assumed aspects of clinical leadership, usually expected from nurses. Whether this reflects a failure of nursing clinical leadership or a success of co‐creative teamwork remains ambiguous. As a general rule, role fluidity is concerning when it fills gaps left by a profession not fulfilling its role, but it can enhance the flow of team meetings when it arises from shared learning and a strengthened understanding of each other.

This suggests two challenges to professional roles in team structures: First, when professionals are required to engage in tasks or dialogue they consider irrelevant [31]; and second, when they must rethink how their perspectives contribute to shared understanding and shared decisions [12]. While our data offer limited insight into the first, they clearly show that collaboration is hindered when professionals restrict input to their own discipline. Well‐functioning teams, by contrast, require members to voice perspectives proactively—sometimes unprompted—while anticipating how others might complement or intersect.

Finally, our data highlight one behaviour that encapsulates several themes: The way IDTs engage with questions. As emphasised, everyone asks. The clearest sign of a well‐functioning team was the use of open, reflective questions—where assumptions were challenged, uncertainty expressed, and others' knowledge sought [32]. This ability to ask freely and constructively captures the essence of interdisciplinary teamwork.

Limitations

Although based on abundant empirical material, the study is limited to a single implementation project, making it highly context‐dependent. The extent to which themes—such as technology use, documentation practices, or meeting roles—can be generalised is uncertain. To balance specificity with transferability, theme descriptions are not exhaustive, and some emerged more clearly through observed failures than successes. Identifying what undermines well‐functioning teams does not necessarily indicate how to improve them. Finally, the qualitative findings do not offer objective criteria for defining well‐functioning teams, beyond those that demonstrate the expected behaviours. Nor do these findings allow conclusions to be drawn regarding the clinical outcomes associated with IDTs, which warrants further research.

Conclusions

Our analysis identifies four sets of behaviours that should be promoted in IDTs. Some reflect and foster a paradigm shift in how the goals of IDTs are understood. The new team structures and methods demand behaviours oriented towards developing shared understandings of complexity and generating creative, collective solutions. These stand in contrast to the New Public Management model, which emphasised efficiency through coordination and division of labour.

This shift challenges views of teams centred primarily on better communication and coordination, moving instead towards co‐creation and joint problem‐solving. For those seeking to replicate or scale up well‐functioning IDTs, particular attention should be given to how team members are both willing and able to formulate and respond constructively to fundamental questions—questions that signal vulnerability, acknowledge uncertainty, and invite colleagues' insights to make sense of complexity.
