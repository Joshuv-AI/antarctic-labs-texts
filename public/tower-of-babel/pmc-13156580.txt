Calcified cerebral emboli—A case series
Authors: Anmol Kakaria, Yannick Yangue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCalcified Cerebral Emboli (CCE) was first reported in 1981 and is an increasingly common yet under-reported cause of acute ischemic stroke (AIS). We present 2 rare cases of acute ischemic stroke due to calcified cerebral emboli. Calcified cerebral emboli are identified on CT scan of the brain with a follow-up MRI to show area of ischemia. Initially thought to be caused due to cardiac and/or endovascular procedures, prior research has found it can occur spontaneously as well. Treatment involves standard medical treatment for stroke, however neurosurgical procedures such as thrombectomy can be considered. We present this case series for clinicians to be aware of what is an increasingly common cause of acute ischemic stroke, especially in high-risk patients. We also applied the diagnostic criteria as proposed by Menounos et al.Keywords: Calcified, Cerebral, Emboli, Stroke

Introduction

Calcified cerebral emboli is defined as a calcified component, which usually originates either from a heart valve or vessel. It fragmentizes, travels to the brain, blocking blood flow and causing ischemia. First reported in 1981 [1], it was considered to be rare and remained under-reported, either due to it being considered as a sequela due to prior infection or being mistaken for unspecified calcification in the brain. Initially, it was assumed that it iatrogenic in nature (mainly due coronary procedures), it now can be considered to occur spontaneously as well. What was considered rare, has now been found to be increasingly common with the incidence rate at about 3-6% amongst the causes of acute ischemic stroke.

There are potentially multiple sources for CCE with the most common source being due to aortic valve calcifications (AVC) [[2], [3], [4], [5]]. Due to CCE being under-reported, this has led to missed diagnoses and delay in treatment, leading to clinical deterioration of the patient and higher mortality rates. In this case series, we present 2 such cases.

Case report one

A man in his early 60s presented to Accidents & Emergency department with right flank pain, recurrent diarrhea and feeling generally unwell. Patient has a history of metastatic prostate cancer, type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, hypercholesterolemia and chronic heart failure due to mitral valve disease. On examination, he had tenderness on the left side. Bloods showed elevated inflammatory markers. CT scan of the abdomen and pelvis showed no clear infective etiology. Patient was initially treated as pyelonephritis and started on antibiotics. Echocardiography of the mitral valve revealed small mobile mass on the posterior leaflet and severe mitral regurgitation (Fig. 1A), suspicious for infective endocarditis. Patient was treated with antibiotics empirically until blood cultures grew streptococcus mitis and streptococcus oralis.

After a month into his admission patient developed sudden onset of slurred speech, right facial droop and right arm weakness which associated with a fall. His speech improved and the patient underwent CT Head which showed a calcified focus (Hounsfield Units 116) within the left M1 and M2 segments of the middle cerebral artery (MCA) (Fig. 1B). Patient was then treated with high dose aspirin and started on Apixaban.

A follow-up MRI (Fig. 1C) of the head performed the next day revealed multiple areas of acute restricted diffusion, consistent with acute infarcts. These included lesions in the left basal ganglia and thalamus, a tiny focus in the left occipital cortex, a small curvilinear area in the right parietal lobe cortex, and a small focus in the left cerebellar hemisphere.

A subsequent follow-up CT head demonstrated calcification in the lateral portion of the left middle cerebral artery.

Patient had a repeat echocardiography done which showed flail posterior leaflet with a 4 × 9 cm attached mass at the leaflet of mitral valve (Fig. 1D). He was then transferred to the cardio-thoracic team at the nearest tertiary care center and underwent mitral valve repair.

We applied the revised CCE diagnostic criteria as proposed by Menounos et al. [5] and we found that the patient fulfilled all of the major criteria and 2 of the minor criteria (CCE localized to MCA and presence of embolic source).

Case report two

A patient in her 80s presented with 3 hours history of left sided weakness, facial droop and fixed right sided gaze. Upon her arrival in hospital, her weakness and gaze had some improvement but she still had a persistent droop. No seizures or loss of consciousness. She has a history of epilepsy for which she takes sodium valproate. On examination she was alert and vitally stable. She had 5/5 power on the right upper and lower limbs and 4/5 power in the left upper and lower limbs along with left sided facial droop and slow examination of left gaze. Her National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score was 6 and Clinical Frailty Score (CFS) of 5. Her blood results showed a hemoglobin of 129 g/L, white cell count of 8.6 109/L, C - reactive protein of 68 mg/L and estimated Glomerular Filtration Rate (GFR) of > 90 mL/min/1.732 m2.

An electrocardiogram was performed which showed new onset atrial fibrillation and started on apixaban and bisoprolol. She had a CT head showed focal calcification in proximal inferior M2 branch of right MCA (Fig. 2A) which was done at 4 hours and 21 outside of thrombolysis window and 300 mg of aspirin was given. A follow-up MRI head was done and revealed an acute ischemic infarct involving the right insula, right precentral gyrus, right frontal operculum, and right temporal lobe. Review of a prior CT scan demonstrated calcification within the distal right M1 segment, suggestive of a calcified embolus (Hounsfield Units 188). Additionally, a small aneurysm was identified in the distal M1 segment of the right middle cerebral artery, measuring approximately 7 mm (Fig. 2B).

Patient was shifted to the stroke ward treated as a presumed right sided lacunar stroke Infarct. Her weakness, speech and visual changes all improved while admitted and she received input from the occupational therapy, physiotherapy and speech and language therapy teams. A low dose statin (20 mg) was also started for secondary prevention. Patient was discharged with no further follow up.

Patient had a follow-up CT angiogram of the head as outpatient, which showed Solitary small dysplastic wide-necked right middle cerebral artery aneurysm and a tiny anterior right sylvian aneurysm. Her case was discussed in Neurovascular Multi-Disciplinary Team meeting and decided that there would be no further follow-up.

We applied the revised CCE diagnostic criteria as proposed by Menounos et al. [5] and we found that the patient fulfilled all of the major criteria and 1 of the minor criteria (CCE localized to MCA).

Discussion

CCE can either be picked up incidentally or patients may report symptoms due to acute ischemic stroke. The incidence rate has been reported to be between 3-6% amongst causes of all strokes. CT head remains the gold standard for picking up CCE which are usually depicted as a hyper-dense calcified focus. The distribution is also varied depending on the type of the arteries affected. Amongst the major cranial arteries, the MCA is usually the most common and is usually depicted as a solitary lesion sized 2-3 mm [6]. The pial vessels are also affected and are usually depicted as multiple smaller lesion leading to the term of ‘salted pretzel sign’ [7].

CCE are commonly misdiagnosed as other conditions such as infection sequelae or intra-cranial calcifications. Only 2 studies [7,5] have a proposed a diagnostic criteria to help radiologists diagnose CCE, with only one [5] being specifically for CCE only, while the other was looking at CCE dependent on a specific source. MRI Head is less helpful with diagnosing CCE, but is typically done to depict the area of ischemia. CT head is then looked at retrospectively and usually CCE has been found to be the cause. CT angiogram can be considered as well but whether it would be part of routine investigations for AIS remains to be seen and remains dependent on local guidelines.

In earlier reports of CCE, it was theorized that CCE were caused mainly due to iatrogenic causes (endovascular procedures and cardiac catheterization). However recent reports such that CCE tend to originate from calcified plaques along the cardiovascular vessel system. Aortic valve is the most common source (either as calcifications or due to aortic stenosis) followed by the mitral valve. Treatment follows standard medical treatment for acute ischemic stroke with some cases maybe requiring neuro-surgical intervention [9].

Early recognition is key as CCE is still under-reported which has led to delayed diagnoses, leading worsening of the clinical condition of the patient and increasing the risk of mortality, especially high-risk patients (with known co-morbidities of valvular disease, endocarditis, atrial fibrillation etc.)

After applying the new diagnostic criteria, we found that it is effective and has the potential to guide stroke physicians in scoring the likelihood of a diagnosis of CCE. However, considering the sample size being small, we recommend a more robust and detailed study to assess the long-term efficacy of the diagnostic criteria. The lack of an overall score predicting the likelihood of CCE needs further clarification.
