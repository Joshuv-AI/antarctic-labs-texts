Isolation and characterization of a novel methanogen Methanosarcina hadiensis sp. nov. from subsurface Boom Clay pore water
Authors: Francisco Javier Giménez, Eveline Peeters, Miroslav Honty, Natalie Leys, Kristel Mijnendonckx
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSafe geological disposal of radioactive waste requires a thorough understanding of geochemical conditions in the host formation. Boom Clay is a potential candidate in Belgium, where active methanogenesis has been detected in its deep subsurface, influencing the local geochemistry. However, the pathways driving this process and the characteristics of the methanogenic archaea involved remain unclear. We isolated a distinct archaeal strain from Boom Clay pore water and characterized it geno‐ and phenotypically. Isolate TD41E1‐1 belongs to a novel species of the Methanosarcina genus, for which the name Methanosarcina hadiensis sp. nov. is proposed. TD41E1‐1 cells are coccus‐shaped, irregularly sized cells enveloped by extracellular polymer substances. Growth and substrate utilization experiments and genomic analysis demonstrated that the strain prefers methylated compounds or hydrogen as substrates for methane production. Although it possesses a complete acetoclastic pathway, no growth was observed in the presence of acetate in the tested conditions. Based on its phylogenetic relation to other known Methanosarcina species and on the presence of c‐type cytochromes, it can be concluded that the strain likely occupies an intermediate position between type I and type II Methanosarcina species. These findings provide valuable insights for assessing Boom Clay's suitability for geological disposal of radioactive waste.

INTRODUCTION

Deep terrestrial subsurface sites can serve practical purposes, such as carbon dioxide capture and storage or the long‐term containment of radioactive waste. At these sites, it is of great importance to maintain stable CO2 concentrations and pH conditions over extended periods of time (Ali et al., 2022; International Atomic Energy Agency, 2003). Impermeable clays or claystones are typically regarded as effective geological barriers and thus, as suitable environments for such applications (Jia et al., 2023). For this purpose, the deep subsurface environment of Boom Clay is presently under consideration as a prospective host formation for the secure geological disposal of radioactive waste. The Boom Clay formation occurs in the Campine subcrop in the North‐East of Belgium at a depth between 161 and 263 m below sea level. Boom Clay mineralogy is mainly dominated by quartz and clay (Zeelmaekers et al., 2015). Boom Clay is studied extensively at the site in Mol within the confines of the High Activity Disposal Experimental Site underground research facility (HADES URF), located at a depth of 225 m (Li et al., 2023).

The subsurface sediment of Boom Clay contains 20 wt% of pore water (Honty et al., 2022). At present, the elemental composition of Boom Clay pore water is well‐known and a reference pore water composition was established. It exhibits a highly reducing redox potential and mainly consists of about 880 mg L−1 carbonate and 5–150 mg L−1 C dissolved organic carbon (Honty et al., 2022). This dissolved organic fraction has a wide molecular size spectrum and is rich in carboxylic acid, phenolic and ketonic groups (Bleyen et al., 2018; Bruggeman & De Craen, 2012). However, measurements of the pH, the pCO2 and other dissolved gases in Boom Clay are technically challenging; hence these are calculated via geochemical modelling based on an equilibrium among carbonates (Wang et al., 2023). The dissolved carbonate system has an important function (e.g., pH buffering) towards radionuclide migration and speciation, rendering knowledge of representative in situ pCO2 values and their controlling mechanism essential for geochemical modelling (Gaucher et al., 2009). Notably, a seven‐year in situ monitoring study on Boom Clay pore water revealed that measured pCO2 and pH values did not correspond to the calculated data (Mijnendonckx et al., 2019). An important reason was the production of CH4, which indicated that an active methanogenic community was present in the pore water (Mijnendonckx et al., 2019).

In general, three methanogenic pathways are being recognized (Liu & Whitman, 2008): (i) the hydrogenotrophic pathway employing CO2 reduction to CH4 using H2 as primary electron donor, (ii) the acetoclastic pathway, converting acetate to CH4, and, (iii) the methylotrophic pathway, converting methylated compounds such as methanol, methylamines or methyl sulphides to CH4. The methylotrophic pathway bifurcates into H2‐dependent methyl reduction and H2‐independent methyl dismutation. Despite their shared core metabolic features, methanogenic archaea exhibit remarkable diversity and inhabit various natural and anthropogenic environments (Liu & Whitman, 2008). Environments where methanogens thrive are typified by anoxic conditions and very low concentrations of more energetically favourable electron acceptors such as NO3 −, Mn4+, Fe3+, and SO4 2− (Liu & Whitman, 2008). While the hydrogenotrophic pathway is the most common and phylogenetically widespread methanogenic pathway, acetoclastic methanogens, only present in the Methanosarcinales order, are estimated to account for most biogenic CH4 production albeit dependent on temperature, organic matter degradation and other environmental conditions (Conrad, 2020). For example, among the different methanogenic pathways, acetoclastic methanogenesis is favoured during complete degradation of organic matter while during incomplete degradation, fermentation stoichiometry is less constrained, allowing for preferential H2 production followed by hydrogenotrophic methanogenesis (Conrad, 2020). Methylotrophic methanogens dominate in high‐sulphate environments, where competition with sulphate‐reducing bacteria for acetate and H2 impedes colonization by hydrogenotrophic and acetoclastic methanogens (Mitterer, 2010; Oremland et al., 1982; Xu et al., 2021; Zhuang et al., 2019). Additionally, elevated salt concentrations as encountered in marine, coastal, hypersaline lake and solar saltern settings, lead to an elevated production of osmoprotectants like glycine betaine by microorganisms thriving in these environments. Given that these substances serve as non‐competitive substrates for methylotrophic methanogens, these environments promote the dominance of such methanogenic organisms (Conrad, 2020; Mesquita et al., 2023). Recent investigations underscore the expanding importance of methylotrophic methanogens, indicating their broader distribution and significance (Mesquita et al., 2023; Narrowe et al., 2019; Schorn et al., 2022; Söllinger & Urich, 2019).

While a more detailed investigation of the bacterial community of Boom Clay pore water has been conducted before, a more thorough characterization of the prevalent methanogenic archaea in this habitat is still lacking. (Wouters et al., 2013). Previously, culture‐independent methodologies did indicate the presence of methanogenic archaea from at least two distinct phylogenetic groups: Methanobacteriales and Methanomassiliicoccales, typified by a distinct methanogenic metabolic pathway, namely the hydrogenotrophic and methylotrophic pathway, respectively (Mijnendonckx et al., 2019). This observation suggests that a diverse array of substrates might underlie methanogenesis within the Boom Clay habitat. The indication that methanogenesis might take place in the Boom Clay pore water was unexpected, particularly given the refractory nature of dissolved organic matter (Mijnendonckx et al., 2019). Remarkably, in some piezometers, rates of microbial methane production were such that they impacted the pCO2‐carbonate equilibrium. Moreover, increased CH4 concentration was observed in correlation with increasing pH levels (Mijnendonckx et al., 2019). The presence of methane can exert a substantial impact on pore water geochemistry, analogous to observations made in marine sediments (Guseva et al., 2021). These are mostly indirect effects due to the coupling of anoxic methane oxidation and sulphate reduction, leading to the secondary precipitation of carbonates and sulphides (Guseva et al., 2021). The empirical finding of the rising methane concentrations complicates the further development of geochemical models for Boom Clay pore water, thereby underscoring the need for a better understanding of contributing factors that influence pore water composition, including microbial activities such as methanogenesis. For example, the models could benefit from including microbial reactions, and therefore, knowledge on the ongoing reactions and reactions rates are needed.

In this study, a culture‐dependent methodology is employed to delve deeper into the functional characteristics of methanogenesis within the deep subsurface Boom Clay ecosystem. The successful isolation of a distinct methanogenic archaeal strain from the Boom Clay pore water is presented, followed by a comprehensive pheno‐ and genotypic characterization. The sampled piezometer filter had remained undisturbed since 13 years ensuring that no external perturbations had influenced the residing microbial community. Newly generated insights into growth and methanogenic properties of this strain will enhance understanding of the geochemistry of the Boom Clay habitat.

EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURES

Collection of Boom Clay pore water

The water sample used in this study was collected from the horizontal piezometer TD‐41E located at the Test Drift part of the HADES URF (Figure A1). The boreholes for the installation of the piezometer were drilled with a continuous nitrogen flux to avoid oxidation. The piezometer was manufactured out of stainless steel and installed in 2004. It is comprised of six porous filter screens, each measuring 0.1 m in length with an average pore diameter of 9 μm. Water was sampled from the outermost filter in the piezometer (TD‐41E‐01) at 8.8 m from the gallery lining using a dedicated sampling container. This container was emptied and distributed in several septum bottles in an anaerobic glove box with an Ar/H2 (99/1%) atmosphere. The chemical composition of the pore water was determined as in Honty et al. (2022) and is shown in Table A1.

Isolation of strain TD41E1‐1

Enrichment of methanogenic archaea was performed by supplementing Boom Clay pore water to DSMZ 141c medium with 120 mM methanol at a 10% v/v ratio (Koblitz et al., 2022). A mixture of 0.2 μm filtered H2/CO2 (80/20%) at 1 bar over ambient pressure was supplemented to the medium after inoculation. The final pH was measured and ranged between pH 7.7–7.8. The samples were incubated at 37°C and were transferred to fresh medium every two weeks (10% v/v). Vancomycin (100 μg mL−1) was added to the cultures after five enrichments.

After nine enrichments of two weeks each, 100 μL of the liquid sample was transferred to the same growth medium solidified with 1.5% agar (Oxoid, Belgium). Plates were incubated during three weeks at 37°C in an anaerobic jar (Axonlab, Belgium) supplemented with H2/CO2 (80/20%) at 1 bar over ambient pressure. Single colonies were suspended in liquid medium, incubated at 37°C during two weeks, thereby obtaining a pure strain named TD41E1‐1.

Optical microscopy

50 μL of a stationary phase culture of strain TD41E1‐1 was applied on a microscope slide and visualized with an Eclipse Ti automated inverted wide‐field epifluorescence microscope (Nikon, Brussel, Belgium) equipped with a 20× magnification (Plan Fluor, numerical aperture 0.5) dry 16 objective and a Prime BSI sCMOS camera controlled by the NIS Elements software. Autofluorescence was visualized in the DAPI channel (excitation 387/11 nm, emission 452/45 nm).

High resolution scanning electron microscopy

A 25 mL culture of an enrichment culture, strain TD41E1‐1, Methanolobus profundi MobM, Methanosarcina acetivorans C2A was incubated in liquid modified DSMZ 141c medium with 120 mM methanol and a 25 mL culture of Methanomassiliicoccus luminyensis B10 was incubated in liquid DSMZ 1637 medium. Cultures were grown until stationary phase and pelleted by centrifugation during 5 min at 8500 g. Afterwards, cells were prepared in the Centre for Scientific Instrumentation (CIC) (Granada, Spain) using the method described in Mazia et al. (1975). As a last step, cells were brought to the critical point with CO2 in a Critical Point Dryer CPD 7501 (Polaron, Watford, United Kingdom) and coated with carbon using the Emitech K975X Sputter Coater System (Emitech, France). High resolution scanning electron microscopy AURIGA (FIB‐FESEM) (Carl Zeiss SMT, Roßdorf, Germany) was performed in the CIC (Granada, Spain).

Transmission electron microscopy

A 25 mL culture of strain TD41E1‐1 was incubated in liquid‐modified DSMZ 141c medium with 120 mM methanol until stationary phase and was pelleted by centrifugation during 5 min at 8500 g. Cells were washed twice with PBS and fixated overnight at 4 C in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate trihydrate (Sigma‐Aldrich) with 2.5% glutaraldehyde (vol/vol, Sigma‐Aldrich) pH 7.2. Afterwards, cells were washed with 0.2 M sodium cacodylate trihydrate and post‐fixated in 1% OsO4 (vol/vol) at 4°C in the dark. Samples were washed three times 5 min with distilled water and dehydrated in graded concentrations of ethanol and polymerized in Embed 812. Ultra‐thin sections (50–70 nm) were mounted on slides and stained with 1% toluidine blue in a 1% Borax solution. Afterwards, slides were counterstained with 1% uranyl acetate in a 50% ethanol solution for 1 min. Finally, slides were stained for 5 min with lead citrate in a CO2‐free atmosphere (Reynolds, 1963). Slides were visualized on a transmission Electron Microscope LIBRA 120 PLUS (Carl Zess SMT, Roßdorf, Germany) in the CIC (University of Granada, Spain).

Growth and substrate utilization

To monitor growth at different temperatures, strain TD41E1‐1 was diluted 1/100 in fresh liquid‐modified DSMZ 141c medium with 120 mM methanol. A mixture of 0.2 μm filtered H2/CO2 (80/20%) at 1 bar over ambient pressure was supplemented to the medium after inoculation. Cultures were incubated at 4, 20, 30, 37, 42, 50, 55, and 72°C. Growth curves were constructed by measuring OD600 and CH4 production on regular times, followed by statistical analysis with the R Growthcurver package (version 0.3.1) (Sprouffske & Wagner, 2016) using R (version 4.3.0, 2023‐04‐21) (R Core Team, 2017).

To investigate if methanol could be consumed in the absence of hydrogen, cultures with 20 or 120 mM methanol were supplemented with 1 bar over ambient pressure Ar/CO2 (80/20%). Additionally, the range of substrates that could be used for methane production was tested by replacing methanol with 120 mM Na‐acetate, 120 mM K‐acetate, 120 mM Mg‐acetate, 20 mM TMA, 20 mM QMA, 20 mM betaine monohydrate, 20 mM Na‐formate, 10 mM DMS, 20 mM MT or 20 mM MePr. The cultures were supplemented with H2/CO2 (80/20%) or Ar/CO2 (80/20%) at 1 bar over ambient pressure. Hydrogenotrophic methanogenesis was investigated by omitting all substrates from the growth medium except H2/CO2 (80/20%) at 1 bar over ambient pressure. Samples were incubated at 37°C and growth was monitored by measuring methane production after 14 days and 1 month. A volume:headspace ratio of 30%:70% was used for these experiments. Acetate was measured with the Acetic Acid Assay Kit (Megazyme, Ireland) according to the manufacturer's protocol.

Gas chromatography–flame ionization detector

Gas chromatography in combination with a flame ionization dector (GC‐FID) was employed for the analysis of methane and methanol. Methane production was measured by sampling the headspace and injecting it into a Thermo Scientific TRACE GC Ultra GC equipped with an FID detector (Interscience, Belgium) and a capillary column Rxi®‐5Sil MS (60 m × 0.18 mm × 0.10 μm, Restek, France). The temperature of the FID was set at 250°C, the oven temperature was set at 50°C followed by an increase of 20°C min−1 until it reached 200°C where it was kept for 10 min. He was used as a carrier gas at a flow rate of 1 mL min−1 and a split ratio of 35:1. Sample concentrations were determined based on a calibration curve created by injecting triplicates of different volumes of CH4 gas. Methanol was analyzed according to Joseph et al. (2022) using potassium carbonate (K2CO3) as salt and ethyl acetate as organic solvent in the salting‐out assisted liquid–liquid extraction method. To this end, 1 mL samples were taken each time point and kept at −20°C until the end of each growth experiment.

DNA extraction and 16S rRNA gene sequence analysis

A 25 mL culture of strain TD41E1‐1 was incubated in modified DSMZ 141c medium with 120 mM methanol until stationary phase and was pelleted by centrifugation during 10 min at 5000 g. Cells were subsequently washed three times with PBS at room temperature. Total DNA was extracted using a combination of the lysis method described in Povedano‐Priego et al. (2021) followed by the Qiagen QIAamp DNA mini kit according to the manufacturers protocol (Qiagen, Belgium). The concentration of the DNA was analyzed with the Quantifluor dsDNA sample kit (Promega, the Netherlands) and integrity was analyzed with gel electrophoresis. Bacterial contamination was excluded by performing RT‐PCR on a Rotorgene Q (Qiagen, Belgium) using the KAPA SYBR® FAST qPCR Master Mix (Merck Life Science, Belgium) according to the manufacturer's protocol with primers 910F (5′‐AGCGGTGGATGATGTGGATTAA‐3′) and 1141R (5′‐TTGTCACCGGCAGTCTCTCTAG‐3).

The 16S rRNA gene amplicon was generated by performing a touchdown PCR reaction in which the reaction mixture contained 30 ng gDNA, primers 344F (5′‐ACGGGGCGCAGCAGGCGCGA‐3′) and 915R (5′‐GTGCTCCCCCGCCAATTCCT‐3′) and Phusion High‐Fidelity Polymerase (Thermofisher Scientific, Belgium). PCR conditions were the following: 2 min at 98°C followed by 15 cycles of 30 s at 98°C, 30 s at 72°C, lowered with 1°C every cycle, and 1 min at 72°C, followed by 20 cycles of 30 s at 98°C, 30 s at 64°C and 1 min at 72°C and a final extension of 5 min at 72°C. The obtained PCR product of 571 bp was subjected to Sanger Sequencing with the 344F primer (Eurofins Genomics, Germany). The obtained sequence was used as an input to screen the NCBI 16S rRNA database (O'Leary et al., 2015).

Whole‐genome sequencing

Genomic DNA was subjected to whole‐genome sequencing using a combination of short‐read and long‐read sequencing. A Short‐Insert DNA library was constructed followed by a 2 × 150 bp pair‐end sequencing approach using the DNA Nanoball Sequencing platform. After sequencing, the raw reads were filtered, including removal of adaptor sequences, contamination, and low‐quality reads from raw reads (BGI genomics, Bejing, China). An overview of the statistics of the clean data is presented in Table A2. Nanopore sequencing was performed with the Oxford Nanopore Technologies PromethION sequencing platform. Magnetic beads were initially employed to screen large DNA fragments. Subsequently, library construction was performed involving repairing and end‐prepping the DNA, purifying and recovering it using magnetic beads, quantifying the DNA with a Qubit fluorimeter, and adding barcodes to each sample. The barcode‐linked products were purified again, and pooling ratios were calculated for multiple barcode‐linked products based on data volume requirements. After connecting linkers to each DNA fragment, the connected DNA was purified once more using magnetic beads, and its quantity and recovery rate were measured. The kit type used was SQK‐LSK110 and as expansion kit EXP‐NBD196 was used. The prepared library was loaded onto a flow cell type FLO‐PRO002[R9.4.1]. Basecalling was performed with Guppy 6.3.9 (BGI genomics, Bejing, China). An overview of the statistics of the clean data is presented in Table A3.

Genome assembly of short‐read sequencing was performed with SPAdes (version 3.15.5) with k‐mer sizes 21,33,55,77 (Prjibelski et al., 2020). For ONT sequencing, genome assembly was performed with Trycycler (version 0.5.3) using the recommended parameters (Wick et al., 2021). A first QC of the reads was performed where reads shorter than 1000 bp and 5% of the reads with the lowest quality were discarded. Afterwards, reads were subsampled into 16 read sets, which were divided into four groups to perform the assembly. Canu (version 2.2) (Koren et al., 2017), flye (version 2.9.1) (Kolmogorov et al., 2019), minimap2 (version 2.24)–miniasm (version 0.3)–minipolish (version 0.1.3) (Wick & Holt, 2021) and raven (version 1.8.1) (Vaser & Šikić, 2021) were used as assemblers with the recommended parameters. Subsequently, assemblies were clustered in per‐replicon groups and spurious, incomplete, or badly misassembled contigs were excluded using the default settings. This resulted in one good cluster that was used for reconciliation of the contigs, followed by a multiple sequence alignment, a partitioning of the reads, and the generation of a consensus sequence (Wick et al., 2021). Afterwards, bwa‐mem2 (version 2.2.1) (Vasimuddin et al., 2019) was used for the alignment of paired‐end reads to the consensus sequence followed by a final polishing step using Polypolish (version 0.5.0) (Wick & Holt, 2022). CheckM v1.2.2 was used to check the quality of the assembled genome (Parks et al., 2015). Data were visualized with the Microbial Genome Circular plotting tool (MGCplotter version 1.2.0) (Shimoyama, 2022). Gapseq was used for pathway analysis and metabolic network reconstruction with standard parameters (Zimmermann et al., 2021). The assembled genome sequence generated and analyzed during the current study is available under accession number CP136241. The 16S rRNA gene sequences were submitted to GenBank under accession numbers PQ068816–PQ068818.

Phylogenetic analysis

We used the genome taxonomy database GTDB‐Tk version 2.3.2 and release R214 for taxonomic classification of the strain (Chaumeil et al., 2022). In addition, the whole genome sequence of strain TD41E1‐1 was uploaded to the Type Strain Genome Server (TYGS) for in silico‐based taxonomic analysis (Meier‐Kolthoff & Göker, 2019). Digital DNA–DNA hybridization (dDDH) was used to calculate genome‐to‐genome distances. To this end, all pairwise comparisons among the set of genomes were conducted using GBDP, and accurate intergenomic distances inferred under the algorithm ‘trimming’ and distance formula d 5 . 100 distance replicates were calculated each. Digital DDH values and confidence intervals were calculated using the recommended settings of the GGDC 4.0 (Meier‐Kolthoff & Göker, 2019). Finally, all representatives from the Methanosarcina genus were downloaded from NCBI using NCBI Datasets (n = 202) and genomic distances were calculated using PaSiT and orhoANI via GenDisCal with the standard parameters (Goussarov et al., 2020). From all Methanosarcinaceae genomes, reference genomes (including that of strain TD41E1‐1) were downloaded from NCBI (n = 44) and annotated with Prokka (version 1.14.6) (Seemann, 2014). This was used as input data for phylogenetic analysis with PhyloPhlAn 3.0 (version 3.0.67) based on the provided dataset of 400 universal marker genes (Asnicar et al., 2020).

Data description

The assembled genome sequence generated and analyzed during the current study is available under accession number CP136241. The 16S rRNA gene sequences were submitted to GenBank under accession numbers PQ068816–PQ068818.

Strain Methanosarcina hadiensis TD41E1‐1 is deposited in the DSMZ‐German Collection of Microorganisms and Cell Cultures GmbH under accession number DSM 117368.

RESULTS

Isolation of a strain from Boom Clay pore water belonging to Methanosarcina spp.

Initial attempts of obtaining microbial growth in liquid media optimized to grow M. luminyensis, previously identified in Boom Clay pore water using a culture‐independent approach (Mijnendonckx et al., 2019), were unsuccessful (data not shown). However, upon incubating the water sample in modified DSMZ 141c medium, microbial growth was observed and SEM enabled to visualize a heterogeneous microbial community consisting of cells with diverse morphologies including cocci, bacilli, and filaments (Figure A2). Further enrichments resulted in a clonally pure strain that belongs to the genus Methanosarcina. The isolate is named TD41E1‐1 (=DSM 117368), based on the name of the filter from which the original sample was taken.

Whole‐genome sequencing enabled us to map the complete genome of strain TD41E1‐1. CheckM indicated a completeness of 100% and contamination of 1.31%. It consists of a circular chromosome of 4,338,376 bp with a GC content of 41.3%, a coding density of 0.75%, a total of 3632 putative protein‐encoding genes, and 73 RNA features including three sets of ribosomal RNAs (5S, 16S, and 23S) and 64 tRNAs (Figure A3).

Based on the genome sequence, a more detailed phylogenetic analysis was performed. GTDB‐Tk classification indicated that strain TD41E1‐1 could not be classified as a known species but is most similar to Methanosarcina mazei S‐6 (84% ANI) isolated from an anaerobic sewage digester (Mah & Kuhn, 1984). To investigate genomic distances within the Methanosarcina genus more in detail, we compared ANI and PaSiT4 (Goussarov et al., 2020) (Figure 1). Overall, similar clusters were observed with both methods; however, more nuances are seen with PaSiT4. For example, all species indicated as type strains cluster separately according to ANI (except for Methanosarcina soligelidi that clusters with M. mazei), while less clusters were observed with PaSiT4. Importantly, both methods indicate that strain TD41E1‐1 does not cluster with any other species (Figure 1). Furthermore, in silico dDDH used to calculate genome‐to‐genome distances (Meier‐Kolthoff & Göker, 2019), showed a maximum dDDH (d 4) value of 27.8% with M. mazei C16 and M. soligelidi SMA‐21 and 27.7% with M. mazei S‐6. Altogether, our data indicate that we isolated a new species that we propose to name Methanosarcina hadiensis. These phylogenetic relationships are supported by a visual representation constructed for the Methanosarcinaceae reference genomes based on 400 universal markers (Figure 2).

Phylogenetic tree created with Phylophlan 3.0 based on the provided dataset of 400 universal marker genes (Asnicar et al., 2020) illustrating the relationship of strain TD41E1‐1 (marked in blue) and other Methanosarcinaceae species.

Cells of strain TD41E1‐1 form extensive surface appendages

Phase‐contrast microscopy, scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) were used to examine cellular morphology (Figure 3). Cells are coccus‐like and are present as single cells, pairs, or as aggregates. SEM analysis of TD41E1‐1 revealed irregularly sized cells enveloped by extracellular polymer substances (Figure 3). A large amount of these substances appear to be present and are also visualized with TEM. Upon zooming in, surface appendages, often possessing a substantial diameter of up to approximately 100 nm, were observable (Figures 3 and A2). These aggregates and the distinct surface appendages appear much larger compared to those observed in M. acetivorans C2A, M. profundi MobM, M. luminyensis B10 (Figure A4) or other archaea documented to produce surface appendages (Chaudhury et al., 2018; Jarrell et al., 2011; Jarrell et al., 2013) but might be similar to those observed in Methanosarcina subterranea HC‐2, isolated from a deep diatomaceous shale formation (Shimizu et al., 2015). Autofluorescence of TD41E1‐1 cells was confirmed with microscopy (Figure A5).

(A) Phase contrast microscopy picture; (B) SEM picture with examples of surface appendages indicated with a red arrow, and (C) TEM picture of a pure liquid culture of strain TD41E1–1.

To identify putative genetic factors involved in the observed aggregation and surface appendages, we screened the genome sequence of strain TD41E1‐1 and compared it with other Methanosarcina species with complete genomes. The genome of M. subterranea HC‐2 was not yet available so could not be included in our comparison. Strain TD41E1‐1 encodes a major S‐layer protein (CMNCKANO_0206) that is highly similar to that of other Methanosarcina species. In addition, its genome harbours multiple genes encoding glycosyltransferases for which no homologue is identified in other Methanosarcina species (Supplementary Table). Notably, a transposase is located upstream of a cluster of seven genes encoding glycosyltransferases (CMNCKANO_0472‐CMNCKANO_0479), putatively indicating that these genes were acquired via horizontal gene transfer. There is an additional gene cluster encoding several proteins involved in cell wall biogenesis (CMNCKANO_3977‐CMNCKANO_3983), which seems unique to strain TD41E1‐1. Furthermore, strain TD41E1‐1 harbours seven genes encoding a NitT/TauT family transport system permease protein, an ABC transporter involved in mineral and organic ion transport (Saier & Milton, 2000). Notably, six of these proteins are located in a large region (ca. 109 kb representing 125 genes) that is probably acquired via horizontal gene transfer and a large part of this gene cluster is not present in Methanosarcina barkeri str. Fusaro, M. acetivorans C2A, M. mazei Go1 or Methanosarcina horonobensis HB‐1 (Supplementary Table).

A complete archaellum cluster flaJIHFGD/EB followed by a chemotaxis cluster cheRDCABYFW is also present. A second flagellin cluster is separated by a transposase (CMNCKANO_2445‐CMNCKANO_2452). In addition, strain TD41E1‐1 comprises two gene clusters coding for PilA and separated by a transposase (Supplementary Table). The second cluster is followed by genes encoding FlaI and FlaJ. However, FlaJ is disrupted by a transposase thus probably not functional. Despite the presence of the complete gene clusters, TEM imaging did not show the presence of archaella in modified DSMZ 141c medium supplemented with 120 mM methanol (Figure 3).

Growth at suboptimal temperature results in a prolonged lag phase

To assess the potential growth range of strain TD41E1‐1, we monitored optical density at 600 nm (OD600) and methane production in modified DSMZ 141c medium supplemented with 120 mM methanol over 14 days at various temperatures: 4, 20, 30, 37, 42, 50, 55, and 72°C. No growth was observed at 4°C and temperatures above 50°C (data not shown). CH4 production was evident at 30, 37, and 42°C, while CH4 was much lower at 20°C (Figure 4).

Growth of strain TD41E1‐1 at different temperatures in function of time visualised as (A) optical density (OD600), (B) CH4 production in the head‐space, and (C) methanol consumption. 20°C is shown as blue circles, 30°C as red squares, 37°C as green triangles and 42°C as orange diamonds. Data represents average and standard deviation of three biological replicates.

The differences observed at 30, 37, and 42°C are primarily due to variations in the lag phase, as the maximum amount of CH4 produced and generation time were similar among these temperatures (Figures 4 and A6). At 30, 37, and 42°C, the maximum amount of CH4 produced in the headspace was 1.0 ± 0.15, 1.2 ± 0.01, and 1.1 ± 0.04 mmol, respectively, with a shortest generation time of 10.5 ± 2.0, 9.7 ± 1.7, and 11.2 ± 1.5 h, respectively. The shortest lag phase of 2 days was observed for 42°C. A noticeable increase in OD600 and a decrease in methanol coincided with the increase in CH4 production (Figure 4). Once all methanol was consumed, OD600 values rapidly declined to similar levels for 30, 37, and 42°C. Despite significantly lower CH4 production during the first 14 days, strain TD41E1‐1 demonstrated the capability to achieve similar levels of CH4 after 55 days at 20°C (max. level of 1.1 ± 0.1 mmol CH4) but with a much longer generation time of 4.5 ± 0.3 days (Figures A6 and A7).

Methanol emerged as a potential limiting factor as evidenced by considerably lower OD600 values in the presence of 20 mM methanol compared to 120 mM. Moreover, the stationary phase was reached after just 3 days in the presence of 20 mM methanol as all methanol was consumed (Figure A8). Furthermore, growth rapidly halted when limited headspace was available (16% vs. 70%) (Figure A9). Re‐injection with H2, CO2 or H2:CO2 indicated both H2 and CO2 could also be limiting factors for optimal growth (Figure A9).

Strain TD41E1‐1 prefers methylated compounds or hydrogen as methanogenic substrates

Gapseq was used for pathway analysis and indicated that strain TD41E1‐1 is capable of carrying out the four methanogenic pathways (Figure 5 and Supplementary Table). In addition, to verify the functionality of these pathways, we conducted growth experiments with strain TD41E1‐1 using various substrates, including methanol, trimethylamine hydrochloride (TMA), acetate, H2/CO2, tetramethylammonium chloride (QMA), betaine hydrate, formate, dimethylsulfide (DMS), sodium methanethiolate (MT), and methyl propionate (MePr) (Table 1).

Clear CH4 production was observed when utilizing methanol and TMA after 14 days (Table 1). Methane was generated both in the presence and absence of H2, with a higher amount in the presence of H2, as expected due to distinct reaction mechanisms (Liu & Whitman, 2008). Notably, when 120 mM methanol was provided in the absence of H2, only a negligible amount of CH4 was produced after 14 days compared to the case of 20 mM methanol without H2. It took up to 1 month to generate a similar amount of CH4 without H2 as was achieved within 14 days with 20 mM methanol. Our experimental observations are consistent with the genomic data as we identified multiple substrate‐specific methyltransferases and methylotrophic corrinoid proteins involved in the metabolism of methanol, mono‐, di‐ and trimethylamines. Additionally, we identified permeases for uptake and the gene ramA, encoding a methylamine methyltransferase corrinoid activation protein (Supplementary Table).

After 1 month, neither CH4 production nor acetate consumption occurred in the absence of H2 (Table 1 and Figure A10). Also in the presence of H2, no acetate consumption was observed and the CH4 production was similar to that observed under hydrogenotrophic conditions, suggesting that this metabolic pathway is likely the one being activated (Table 1 and Figure A10). This finding was unexpected considering the presence of a complete acetoclastic pathway (Figure 5). The cellular uptake of acetate likely occurs through diffusion or via SatP (CMNCKANO_3124), an acetate/succinate:H+ symporter, homologous to MM_0903 in M. mazei, which has been previously implicated in acetate uptake (Welte et al., 2014). Subsequently, acetate is enzymatically converted to acetyl‐CoA through the concerted action of acetate kinase Ack (CMNCKANO_2613) and phosphotransacetylase Pta (CMNCKANO_2614). Similar to other Methanosarcina species, strain TD41E1‐1 harbours two cdhABCDE gene clusters encoding acetyl‐CoA decarbonylase/synthase. However, only one of these clusters contains a full‐length cooC gene, which encodes a carbon‐monoxide dehydrogenase accessory protein (Supplementary Table). The Ack protein in strain TD41E1‐1 shares the highest protein sequence identity (93.4%) with that of M. horonobensis HB‐1, while its protein sequence identity is lower (ca. 77%) compared to Ack present in M. barkeri str. Fusaro, M. acetivorans C2A and M. mazei Go1. Remarkably, M. horonobensis HB‐1 possesses two adjacent copies of Ack, one with high protein sequence identity (93.4%) to that of strain TD41E1‐1 and the other with higher protein sequence identity (85.8%–95.1%) to those found in other Methanosarcina strains (Figure A11). However, both copies of Ack retain all conserved residues shown to be involved in kinase activity suggesting their functional capability (Buss et al., 2001; Singh‐Wissmann et al., 1998).

While most Methanosarcina species possess a complete hydrogenotrophic methanogenic pathway, some have lost their ability to produce methane through this pathway (Shimizu et al., 2011; Thauer et al., 2008). Nevertheless, strain TD41E1‐1 exhibited hydrogenotrophic methanogenesis, albeit with a longer lag phase compared to methylotrophic methanogenesis (Table 1). We identified all essential hydrogenases in the hydrogenotrophic pathway, each interacting with different electron carriers (Kulkarni et al., 2018; Mand & Metcalf, 2019; Thauer et al., 2008), namely the membrane‐associated EchA‐F, VhtGACD, and cytoplasmic FrhADGB (Supplementary Table). Furthermore, two additional copies of vhtGAC are present, but lacking vhtD, which encodes a hydrogenase maturation protease (Supplementary Table). However, it is possible that VhtD could activate all copies of Vht (Guss et al., 2009). Strain TD41E1‐1, similar to several other Methanosarcina species, has an alternative pathway to reduce methanophenazine with H2, involving the combination of Frh with a membrane‐associated, energy‐conserving F420H2‐dehydrogenase complex encoded by fpoABCDHIJKLMNO with fpoF located downstream of this cluster (Supplementary Table).

Irrespective of the substrate used, the last step of methanogenesis produces a heterodisulfide molecule that requires subsequent reduction. This reaction is catalyzed by the enzyme heterodisulfide reductase (Hdr). In strain TD41E1‐1, we identified multiple copies of the membrane bound HdrED and cytoplasmic HdrABC enzyme complex, similar to M. acetivorans, M. mazei and M. barkeri. However, only the function of HdrED and HdrA1B1C1 has been elucidated (Buan & Metcalf, 2010). Notably, an additional gene cluster annotated as fdhA–fdhB–mvhD–hdrA 2 BC is present in strain TD41E1‐1 (Supplementary Table). Nucleotide BLAST analysis revealed a similar cluster is only present in M. mazei C16, M. mazei strain zm‐15, and M. horonobensis HB‐1. However, the physiological role of this cluster remains unclear, although it is possible that the fdhAB operon, encoding formate dehydrogenases, is involved in formate oxidation to CO2 (Costa et al., 2010). However, CH4 production could not be confirmed with formate as methanogenic substrate (Table 1).

In addition to the conventional substrates, various compounds have been identified as alternative substrates for methanogenesis (Kurth et al., 2020). We tested QMA and betaine monohydrate as possible alternative substrates. However, no CH4 production was observed with QMA and betaine monohydrate after one month (Table 1). Although we identified a glycine betaine transport system and permease (CMNCKANO_0959‐CMNCKANO_0691), no specific corrinoid protein‐coenzyme M methyltransferase (MtgB) nor corrinoid protein (MtgC) was found (Mesquita et al., 2023). Also, no homologues of MtqB or MtgC, specific for QMA were found. On the other hand, Gapseq predicted a complete pathway for methanogenesis from dimethylsulfide, methanethiol and methyl propanoate but not for methylsulfides. Indeed, we found genes encoding the methyl‐sulphide methyltransferase MtsA and the corrinoid protein MtsB, involved in utilization of methylthiopropanoate, methanethiol and dimethylsulfide (Tallant et al., 2001) but no homologues of the methyltransferases MtsD, MtsF, and MtsH, specific to methylsulfides in M. acetivorans were detected (Oelgeschläger & Rother, 2009). Growth was observed with DMS, MT, and MePr, albeit with a shorter lag phase in the presence of MT (Table 1).

DISCUSSION

To improve our understanding on how the present microbial communities could impact the geochemistry of the Boom Clay pore water, we successfully isolated and characterized the first methanogenic archaeon of Boom Clay pore water at a depth of 225 m below sea level. As a first step, we characterized this strain in detail and the obtained knowledge could be used in future detailed experiments to explicate the ongoing processes. The isolated strain, TD41E1‐1, belongs to the Methanosarcina genus and is remarkably versatile, capable of utilizing a wide variety of substrates to produce CH4 but it is not able to perform acetoclastic methanogenesis in the tested conditions. This was rather unexpected as acetate is a key intermediate of microbial metabolism in the deep subsurface and acetoclastic methanogenesis would be the preferred route if acetate is abundant (Conrad, 2020; Miettinen et al., 2018). Moreover, Methanosarcina species are known to be able to utilize acetate as a methanogenic substrate, although there are some exceptions (Lyimo et al., 2000; Shimizu et al., 2015; Simankova et al., 2001). They are often dominant in anaerobic digesters with high acetate concentrations (De Vrieze et al., 2012). However, they have a low affinity for acetate so they are rapidly outcompeted by Methanothrix (Conklin et al., 2006; De Vrieze et al., 2012). This lower affinity for acetate and thus a limited intake could be explained by the presence of Ack/Pta as acetate activation system compared to acetyl‐CoA synthetase in Methanothrix (Jetten et al., 1990). Acetate is not routinely measured in Boom Clay pore water but concentrations are not expected to be in millimolar range but rather in micromolar range (Bleyen et al., 2018). This could explain why strain TD41E1‐1 does not harbour an active acetoclastic pathway. In addition, strain TD41E1‐1 could cluster in large aggregates enveloped by extracellular polymer substances, which could further lower its affinity for acetate (De Vrieze et al., 2012).

Recent studies have proposed dividing the Methanosarcina genus into type I and type II species based on their H2 metabolism and the presence of c‐type cytochromes (Zhou et al., 2021). Environmental conditions could be used to predict the dominant physiological type. Type I species can use H2 and thrive in environments with high rates of carbon flux. In contrast, type II species cannot use H2 and are typically found in low‐organic‐carbon environments (Zhou et al., 2021). It is hypothesized that in low‐energy environments, Methanosarcina species cannot compete with H2 specialists leading to the loss of their ability to utilize H2 (Thauer et al., 2008). The Boom Clay pore water used in this study contained 83 ± 30 mg L−1 DOC, an intermediate value compared to the data presented previously (Zhou et al., 2021). However, biodegradation of this organic matter is characterized by very slow kinetics (Bleyen et al., 2018), thus more similar to that in type II environments. Interestingly, strain TD41E1‐1 exhibits all physiological characteristics of type I species. Putatively, the cell aggregates and surface appendages observed in the SEM pictures may play a role in an efficient H2 cycling between cells (Chaudhury et al., 2018; Jarrell et al., 2011; Jarrell et al., 2013), potentially allowing the strain its ability to utilize H2. However, it is unclear if these large cell aggregates and surface appendages are produced in situ as the production of such structures generally depends on environmental conditions (Jarrell et al., 2013; Veiga et al., 1997). Despite its ability to utilize H2, TD41E1‐1 harbours only a short but possibly non‐functional fragment of the gene encoding a five‐heme c‐type cytochrome (CMNCKANO_2775), which is expected to be absent in type I species. These findings indicate that strain TD41E1‐1 could potentially be an intermediate species.

Strain TD41E1‐1 differs from the two methanogenic archaea from the Methanobacteriales and Methanomassiliicoccales orders, previously identified via a culture‐independent approach. However, the available data is limited, based only on a single DGGE analysis of a few samples (Mijnendonckx et al., 2019). As a result, the dominant methanogenic archaea in Boom Clay pore water remains unclear, and a more comprehensive analysis across multiple piezometers is needed to gauge the prevalence of the isolated taxon in the pore water. Nevertheless, current findings suggest a diverse methanogenic community with a preference for both methylotrophic and hydrogenotrophic methanogens. However, the source of the various electron donors driving methanogenic reactions in Boom Clay pore water remains unclear as its organic composition has not been fully characterized (Durce et al., 2015). Additionally, Boom Clay has small pore sizes (log‐normally distributed around 60 nm) and connecting pore throats (10–50 nm) and a low hydraulic conductivity, limiting the mobility of cells, nutrients, and dissolved organic matter (Durce et al., 2015). As a result, microbial activity is not anticipated in undisturbed Boom Clay. However, installation of a piezometer alters these conditions, providing space, water, and a continuous supply of organic matter leaching from the clay. The leachable fraction is controlled by the total TOC content, although kerogen might also contribute (Durce et al., 2015). Notably, Boom Clay kerogen contains a relatively high nitrogen content, likely in the form of amines, and a broad range of methylated groups have been detected within this kerogen fraction (Deniau et al., 2008). Furthermore, Boom Clay is deposited in a shallow marine environment (Honty et al., 2022), typically characterized by the presence of osmolytes that serve as precursors for methylamines (Conrad, 2020; Mesquita et al., 2023). This environment may thus promote the growth of methylotrophic methanogens. Hydrogen produced during fermentation reactions could be used by hydrogenotrophic methanogens or during hydrogen‐dependent methylotrophic reactions. Regardless of the ongoing reaction mechanism, the reaction kinetics are expected to be slow. This is not only due to the limitations in organic matter but also because the in situ temperature of 16°C is suboptimal for strain TD41E1‐1, as demonstrated by the prolonged lag phase observed under these conditions.

In conclusion, we isolated and characterized strain TD41E1‐1 from Boom Clay pore water that belongs to a novel species of the Methanosarcina genus, for which the name M. hadiensis sp. nov. is proposed. It exhibits some unique characteristics relative to previously characterized Methanosarcina species, such as the inability to perform acetoclastic methanogenesis and the presence of extensive surface appendages. The determined growth kinetics can serve as a foundation for implementing methanogenic activity in the current geochemical model, enhancing our understanding of contributing factors that influence pore water composition. Further isolates from Boom Clay pore water would provide additional knowledge on how these species thrive in environments with highly recalcitrant organic matter and can give further insights into the evolution of this genus.
