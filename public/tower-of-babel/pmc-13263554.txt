Considerations on the safety of topical hemostatic irrigation in “BLEED-LESS: a scoping review on blood loss elimination in endoscopic decompression”
Authors: Zhongqiang Wang, Xiaozhang Ying, Qi Zheng
Published: 2026

We read with great interest the recent scoping review by Ma et al. titled “BLEED-LESS: a scoping review on blood loss elimination in endoscopic decompression—lessons and literature on endoscopic spine surgery” (1). The authors have provided a comprehensive overview of hemostatic strategies in endoscopic spine surgery and innovatively proposed the FIBRE protocol. This framework offers invaluable systematic guidance, particularly for surgeons new to this field. We particularly appreciate the effort to integrate multimodal approaches and translate complex techniques into a clear clinical pathway. However, we wish to discuss the safety considerations of a specific hemostatic regimen proposed in the article, hoping to contribute to the refinement of this excellent protocol.

While topical application of tranexamic acid (TXA) has been shown to reduce bleeding in surgical procedures, its potential neurotoxicity should be emphasized, especially in the context of an incidental dural tear (IDT). Evidence indicates that high concentrations of TXA in direct contact with neural tissue or upon entry into the cerebrospinal fluid can induce serious adverse effects, including seizures and neuronal injury (2). IDT is not uncommon in endoscopic spine surgery, and the pressure from continuous irrigation may facilitate the entry of fluid into the subarachnoid space through an undetected minor dural defect. Consequently, adding TXA directly to the irrigation fluid, while increasing local concentration, may elevate the risk of intrathecal exposure. Therefore, we recommend strengthening the safety prerequisites and operational details for TXA use, which we believe would further enhance the safety and clinical applicability of the FIBRE protocol. This aligns with the review’s core principle of balancing hemostatic efficacy against complication risks.

We again thank Ma and colleagues for their outstanding work, which undoubtedly advances the standardization of hemostatic protocols in endoscopic spine surgery. We hope our comments can serve as a reference for the ongoing refinement of this protocol, helping to fortify the cornerstone of patient safety while pursuing effective hemostasis.
