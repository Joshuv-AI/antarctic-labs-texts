Strengthening research capacity in Cameroon: qualitative insights on the impact of mentorship, training and funding on receiving seed grants
Authors: Julie Marie Buser, Jovanny Fouogue, Ella August, Rachel Louise Gray, Demsash Hailu, Tamrat Endale, Yolanda R Smith, Jeanne Fouedjio
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionEnhancing research capacity in low-resource settings is crucial for informed policy-making and public health advancement. This study explores the experiences of sexual and reproductive health and rights (SRHR) researchers in Cameroon who received seed research grants, focusing on their motivations, challenges and anticipated impact.MethodsA qualitative study design using a phenomenological approach with thematic analysis was employed to understand and interpret the early lived experiences of the researchers. We conducted in-depth, semistructured interviews with physicians, obstetrics and gynaecology residents, and nurse midwives (n=16) involved in a 2-year research training programme who developed competitive proposals and received seed grant funding in April 2024. Interviews were conducted in September and October 2024 after receiving grant funding and before starting data collection.ResultsThe study identified three main themes: roles of mentorship and training as catalysts for anticipated research success, challenges faced and strategies used to overcome them, and impact of grant funding on research scope and professional development. Mentorship and the structured training programmes equipped researchers with essential skills for grant applications and anticipated project execution. Despite challenges such as tight deadlines, financial limitations and ethical clearance hurdles, researchers demonstrated resilience and employed adaptive strategies. Grant funding expanded their research scope, enabled exploration of new methodologies and facilitated the proposal of community-centred studies addressing public health issues.ConclusionsThese findings highlight that targeted investment in mentorship, training and grant support is important for strengthening research capacity and enabling researchers to address priority public health and SRHR issues in Cameroon. Strengthening these foundational elements may help drive sustainable improvements in public health research and policy in Cameroon and other low-resource settings.

Introduction

In recent years, research capacity in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) has been increasingly recognised as a pivotal force for informed policy making and the advancement of public health, particularly in sexual and reproductive health and rights (SRHR).1 2 The ability to conduct high-quality research is a crucial driver for formulating practical and culturally relevant health policies.3 Like other African nations, Cameroon faces unique challenges that underscore the need for local research initiatives to inform health strategies and interventions tailored to its sociocultural context.4

Historically, the research landscape in LMICs, specifically in Cameroon, has been constrained by limitations in funding, infrastructure and skilled human resources.5 To address these constraints, the Center for International Reproductive Health Training at the University of Michigan (CIRHT-UM) initiated a comprehensive 2-year research training programme that began with the distribution of competitive seed research grants in April 2024 to fuel research projects and bolster local researchers’ competencies and skills.6 The CIRHT-UM programme is designed to impart essential research methodology, ethically sound research practices and scientific publication skills, aiming to nurture a self-sustaining research culture.

This initiative is particularly relevant given the disparities in research output between francophone (French-speaking) and anglophone (English-speaking) African countries due to historical, linguistic and structural disparities.7 Anglophone countries, influenced by British colonial education systems, often have higher visibility in international research due to English being the dominant publication language.8 Researchers in these countries also tend to receive more international research funding and have stronger academic networks with English-speaking countries.9 Established research institutions and supportive government policies in some anglophone countries further boost their research outputs.5 Meanwhile, francophone countries may face challenges due to language barriers and less access to international funding and resources, although many are striving to improve their research infrastructures and global collaborations.10

Research capacity in Cameroon is growing but is uneven and faces challenges related to funding, resources, its unique bilingual context and governance.1113 Despite this, Cameroon’s universities and researchers are increasingly active in international collaborations and regional research networks.11 14 These complexities in research capacity intersect with pressing public health issues, such as SRHR. In Cameroon, SRHR is impacted by the country’s cultural diversity and infrastructural challenges, particularly in rural areas where service access is limited.15 Cultural taboos and stigma hinder comprehensive sexual education, particularly among youth, while inconsistencies in policy implementation persistently affect maternal health and family planning, exacerbated by high maternal and infant mortality rates.16 17 Additionally, Cameroon faces significant HIV/AIDS prevalence and gender inequality, complicating women’s ability to exercise their rights.18 Scientific research in Cameroon aims to tackle these challenges by identifying effective interventions and informing policy, despite being constrained by limited resources and funding.19 Nevertheless, the government and international partners continue striving for improved SRHR outcomes through initiatives focused on accessibility, education and equity, supported by ongoing research efforts.20 21

As the global health community endeavours to improve SRHR outcomes, understanding the experiences of Cameroonian researchers who have participated in such initiatives becomes paramount. The interplay between the provision of seed grants and structured research mentoring can offer insights into the practical bottlenecks and opportunities researchers encounter. Our study explores the experiences and outcomes of researchers in Cameroon who received an SRHR seed research grant. By understanding researchers’ motivations, professional growth and reflections on their early research training and their grant-funded projects, the study seeks to identify the factors contributing to their anticipated successes and challenges. The ultimate goal is to enhance support mechanisms and refine training initiatives to better meet the needs of researchers, thereby improving SRHR research outcomes and fostering interdisciplinary partnerships in Cameroon.

Methods

This study adheres to the Consolidated criteria for Reporting Qualitative research checklist to enhance the transparency and comprehensiveness of reporting our qualitative research study.22

Description of research training programme

In partnership with the University of Yaoundé I, CIRHT-UM developed an extensive research training initiative aimed at strengthening participants’ research skills and capacity for faculty and trainees at varying levels of research experience. Training is conducted using a mix of remote webinars, in-person sessions and interactive workshops. Prior to receiving a seed grant, the curriculum addressed core research competencies, including formulating research questions, conducting literature reviews, exploring research design and understanding statistical concepts. These foundational topics were introduced through focused remote webinars, each lasting about an hour. The programme’s in-person components provided more in-depth instruction on areas such as drafting research grant proposals and upholding research ethics. After receiving a seed grant, in-person trainings included fulfilling principal investigator responsibilities, mentoring and designing survey instruments, with sessions ranging from 8 to 16 hours. This was the point in the programme when participants were interviewed about their experience receiving a seed grant. The timeline for the entire programme is shown in figure 1.

Future training will be offered in both qualitative and quantitative data analysis techniques and their related software. To ensure sustainability and power asymmetries, these trainings are conducted in tandem with a US-based researcher and Rwanda-based researcher who had previously received a seed grant and completed the CIRHT-UM research training programme. Additional workshops will cover scientific writing, manuscript preparation, journal selection, implementation science and strategies for seeking external funding. Collectively, these modules will equip participants with the comprehensive knowledge and practical skills necessary to undertake rigorous, ethical and high-impact scientific research. To foster continued sustainability and peer-to-peer experience sharing, some individuals who complete this programme from the University of Yaoundé I will be trained as research trainers so they can continue to educate new researchers locally, as well as serve as co-trainers for research trainings conducted with future CIRHT-UM partner institutions in other sub-Saharan African countries. Additional institutional sustainability aspects include mentorship training of the selected local research mentors, training of the research coordinator and training in holding effective writing workshops/retreats.

Interviewer characteristics and research team reflexivity

The interviewer for the study is an individual with a background in public policy, sustainable development, veterinary medicine, and management, law and economics. The interviewer is male and has experience in field epidemiology. No prior relationship or familiarity existed between the interviewer and the participants nor with the CIRHT-UM programme. The interviewer approached the research with no specific assumptions related to the topic.

The research team comprised Cameroon-based and US-based individuals with diverse academic and professional backgrounds, including public health, clinical medicine and qualitative research. For those US-based researchers, we recognise that our positionalities, as both outsiders to the local context and collaborators with experience in global health, shaped our approach to data collection and analysis. We recognise that our perspectives, training and prior experiences influenced the development of interview guides, interpretation of participants’ narratives and thematic analysis. Throughout the study, we engaged in ongoing self-reflection and critical discussions to identify and mitigate potential biases. Regular debriefings with Cameroon-based research team members allowed us to remain mindful of our assumptions and prioritise the authentic voices and lived experiences of Cameroonian researchers. We strove to create a respectful and open research environment, acknowledging and valuing participants’ expertise, while continuously reflecting on the power dynamics inherent in conducting research across contexts.

Study design

We employed a qualitative study design using a combination of phenomenology and thematic analysis to understand and systematically interpret the early lived experiences of researchers applying for and initiating grant-funded projects, providing valuable insights for enhancing research capacity strengthening programmes. Interviews were conducted at the programme’s initial stage, after they received grant funding and before starting data collection.23 24 Interviews were conducted between 15 September and 20 October 2024, transcribed in December 2024 and translated in January 2025.

Participant selection

Purposive sampling was used to invite all 16 participants from the CIRHT-UM research training programme in Yaounde, Cameroon, who developed research proposals and received seed grants to participate in the interviews in person. 16 researchers agreed to participate, and no one withdrew during the study. These participants included obstetrics and gynaecology medical doctors, faculty, residents and midwives. Interviews were conducted before seed grant recipients initiated data collection.

Setting

Only the interviewer and the participant were present during the interviews. The study was conducted at academic institutions affiliated with CIRHT-UM research training programmes in Cameroon, primarily the Universities of Yaoundé I and Dschang. The University of Yaoundé I is a public university in Cameroon, located in the capital city, Yaoundé. It is a bilingual institution (French/English) with five faculties/schools, four specialised centres, two virtual universities and 65 laboratories of research.25 The University of Yaoundé I is the oldest university in Cameroon.25 The University of Dschang was founded more recently, in 1993, and is a non-profit public higher education institution located in the small city of Dschang in the west region, about 425 km northwest of Yaoundé.26

Data collection

In-depth, semistructured interviews were conducted face-to-face using open-ended questions to allow participants to describe their experience of receiving a research grant and participating in the research programme in their own words. Online supplemental appendix 1 includes the questions from the interview guide. The study authors developed questions in the interview guide based on sources relating to understanding grant opportunities, overcoming application challenges, forging collaborations and navigating funding limitations, among others.2730 Probing questions were included to elicit rich, detailed descriptions of participants’ thoughts, feelings and perceptions of the grant application process, mentorship, training, challenges and impacts. Participants chose whether the interview would be conducted in French or English.

Interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim by a professional translator to capture the participants’ exact words. We used the advanced machine translation service DeepL to translate the interview transcripts from French to English.31 Three bilingual researchers reviewed the translated transcripts to ensure accuracy and address any nuances or context-specific meanings the automated translation might have missed. Two of these bilingual researchers were linguistically and culturally competent with respect to the Cameroonian context.

Interviews lasted, on average, 34 min, ranging from 16 to 53 min. 14 interviews were conducted in French and 2 in English. Data saturation was reached after seven interviews; however, given the small sample size, all transcripts were included for comprehensive analysis. Summarised versions of some interviews were provided to participants for feedback or clarification. No repeat interviews were conducted.

Data analysis

Using phenomenology and inductive thematic analysis to deeply understand the participant experience, we began by bracketing to set aside preconceived notions, ensuring an unbiased approach to the data.32 Two authors trained in qualitative research independently coded the data using a detailed descriptive coding scheme (online supplemental appendix 2) to capture significant statements that reflect participants’ experiences. We followed the inductive tradition, where codes and themes were allowed to emerge from the participants’ narratives rather than being deduced from a pre-existing theory. Intercoder reliability was ensured by resolving any discrepancies through discussion. Microsoft Word was used to organise, code and analyse qualitative text data through systematic use of commenting, highlighting and manual extraction of codes and themes. Themes were derived by incorporating the four phases of theme development outlined by Vaismoradi et al: ‘initialization’, during which initial codes were created and meaningful segments are identified: ‘construction’, where these codes were examined and categorised into preliminary themes; ‘rectification’, involving the refinement and revision of themes for accuracy and coherence; and ‘finalization’, where final themes were clearly defined and labelled.33 Participant quotations are presented to vividly illustrate each theme, providing rich, detailed insights into their lived experiences.

Patient and public involvement

As the study participants were SRHR providers, there was no patient or public involvement in the development of the research question, study design, recruitment or dissemination.

Findings

Participant characteristics

The study included 16 participants aged between 27 and 58 years, with an average age of 41 years and 56.3% were male (table 1). All participants were healthcare providers in SRHR. Themes

We identified three themes, and each included three subthemes, that collectively capture the key elements of the experiences, challenges, opportunities and impacts associated with applying for and conducting research funded by initial grants: (1) the roles of mentorship and training as catalysts for anticipated research success, (2) the challenges faced and strategies used to overcome them and (3) the impact of grant funding on research scope and personal development. These themes, and associated subthemes, emphasise the critical roles of mentorship and training, the adaptive strategies employed to navigate challenges and the transformative impact of grant funding on research capabilities and professional growth.

Mentorship and training as catalysts for anticipated research success

Significant role of mentors

Across all interviews, local mentors were identified as important in informing researchers about grant opportunities and providing continuous support. Participants learnt about these opportunities through mentors or professors who encouraged them to apply. As one obstetrician-gynaecologist and assistant lecturer shared, “Well, it has to be said that there are 16 of us who have obtained this funding as part of the project you know so well, and we’re lucky or fortunate enough to have a mentor who herself decided to lend herself to this process. This makes the game, if I can call it that, easier, because subject to the same constraints, we’re obliged to help each other, we’re obliged to discuss. When a mentor and someone finds a solution, they share it with the others, so that everyone doesn’t end up stagnating in the same place.” A resident in obstetrics and gynaecology mentioned, “I’d like to say that I can only thank my teachers and mentors because they’ve been understanding, they’ve always been available to coach me, to help me build this research project, when the subject wasn’t right, they helped me improve and they always gave me their time to check if I was going in the right direction; So it was really their availability, the availability of the teachers and mentors that enabled me to meet the requirements, otherwise I wouldn’t have been able to.”

Comprehensive training

According to participants, training from the University of Michigan was transformative, covering essential aspects such as protocol writing, research methodologies and ethical considerations. Specific training sessions helped researchers adapt their projects, write effective grant proposals, set clear objectives and understand qualitative and quantitative studies. Participants consistently described these training sessions as extremely useful in preparing them for the grant application process. As noted by another obstetrician-gynaecologist and assistant lecturer, “Ah, the training was really excellent. There were specific training sessions, because even to write an introduction, we were trained on it, even for the objectives in relation to the subject we had chosen, we worked on them, everyone wrote their objectives, we came back to the plenary session to look at whether the objective was in line with the subject we had chosen. We really had training sessions on methodology, whether it was qualitative studies, quantitative studies, we had step-by-step training so it was, we were elaborating, we were being trained and as soon as we were trained that allowed us to move forward on our project…on the elaboration of our project, really step-by-step.” A different obstetrician-gynaecologist echoed this sentiment, “Well, the fast-track training was a reminder for me because it was comprehensive. It covered all fields, all types of research, but also went beyond the sectors, the areas of predilection in which every novice researcher sometimes finds themselves confined. There was already this broadening, this reminder of the breadth, the immensity of research portfolios in reproduction, alongside which there was a skills training focused on the application process for funding That was the biggest novelty for me, but also the most important aspect, because it had been a long time since I’d been thrown back into the swing of things. So it was really step-by-step very practical to give us the tips, tricks, principles and techniques of putting together a grant application.”

Practical guidance

The importance of grounded, step-by-step guidance was emphasised, illustrating how targeted training programmes equip researchers with the necessary skills to apply for and conduct research projects successfully. As a result, participants gained valuable insights and skills that enhanced their perceived ability to conduct impactful research projects. A resident in obstetrics and gynaecology explained, “The project team provides us with a great deal of guidance on the various strategies we need to adopt to achieve our objectives.” According to one obstetrician-gynaecologist and assistant lecturer, “…this training has already made us understand that there is such a thing as possible research funding and yes, there is a process for applying, it’s not anarchic, There’s an outline to follow, so it was really, we really discovered this outline because we were shown step by step what we had to produce, what were the strong points we had to give in our protocol for the application and so on, this whole guide was given to us. I didn’t know anything about that before as far as applying for funding was concerned, so I was able to understand these steps thanks to this training.”

Challenges in the research process and strategies for overcoming them

Common obstacles

Tight deadlines, financial limitations, navigating ethical clearance and balancing professional responsibilities were recurrent challenges articulated throughout several interviews. Adapting projects to meet specific criteria further complicated the process. Additionally, the importance and necessity of obtaining ethical clearance were consistently emphasised. An obstetrician-gynaecologist explained, “There were also requirements, especially for ethical clearance. The protocol for ethical clearance must be scrupulously respected. As soon as there’s a sentence missing, we reject your file. So, if you have to write up a research project and you have to ask for ethical clearance, in any case, research cannot be published if you don’t have ethical clearance. So you can rest assured that you’re working within the framework of the protocol issued by the national/regional ethics committee.” Participants described challenges and strategies related to managing time effectively while balancing multiple professional and personal responsibilities. They also mentioned the need to modify the research project to fit within budgetary limitations of the grant. As one assistant lecturer in the School of Medicine described, “The budgeting fell short, because I work in three communities. I work in the extreme North with areas that are even in conflict, because from the start it was in a “Boko-Haram” context, so you can see that it’s not easy, and you have to motivate the people who go out into the field, who take risks to collect the data; well, we’ll make do.”

Adaptive strategies and collaborative efforts

Researchers overcame these challenges using support from mentors, collaborative teamwork and personal resourcefulness. They adapted their projects to align with grant criteria, pre-financed some actions and used their networks to gather necessary resources. Participants detailed strategies to overcome application process challenges, noted the availability or lack of local support and highlighted the interactions, cohesion and cooperation among team members working on the research project. Per one assistant lecturer, “The main constraint we encountered during the application process was the time constraint, and to put an end to that we combined teamwork because it enabled us to move forward at the same level and expose our problems so that they could be resolved in a general way.” The negotiation process with stakeholders and collaborative efforts with local staff or other professionals were also significant in navigating challenges. A midwife explained, “Going into the community isn’t always easy. It’s not easy. And so we have to meet the district chief. As I said earlier, if I don’t meet the district chief, I really can’t get involved. Yes, because it’s the district chief who has the community health workers. And these community health agents know the locality.”

Persistence and resilience

The need for persistence and resilience was highlighted, as researchers often had to balance multiple responsibilities and navigate complex administrative processes while pursuing their research goals. Participants reflected on their intrinsic motivation and dedication to continue research work despite challenges or financial constraints and shared their overall experiences, thoughts and insights gained from the process of applying for and being awarded the grant. A midwifery instructor stated, “Already when you ask yourself questions like why do mothers die, why do children die, you have to look for it, you have to understand the elements that are responsible for this mortality, so you have to, that’s where the motivation and knowledge for this project comes from.” Another obstetrician-gynaecologist noted, “We’re Africans, we adapt. I’m going to do this research work because we teach you to love what you do, by explaining to people the benefits of what you do and you feel useful in what you do.”

Impact of grant funding on research scope and personal development

Enhanced research capabilities

Grant funding enabled researchers to undertake comprehensive and community-centred SRHR studies, addressing significant public health issues which would have been financially unfeasible otherwise. This grant funding allowed for the exploration of new methodologies and the expansion of research scope. As one obstetrician-gynaecologist said, “As far as methodologies are concerned, perhaps I’d have chosen simpler methodologies instead of doing mixed qualitative-quantitative studies, but on top of that it enabled me to reframe, to be a little more precise in my line of research, the methodology yes, because if it wasn’t for CIRHT-UM, I wasn’t even going to know about qualitative studies, there are still other types of methodology out there where there’s a lot of research to be done.” Participants discussed strategies for obtaining and using various resources, like ethical clearance and acquiring research inputs. A nurse and midwifery instructor noted, “Just recently we were trained in a few analysis tools for quantitative data. If it hadn’t been for this funding, I might not have considered this kind of methodology.”

Professional growth and career development

Participants reported that receiving the grant directly contributed to their career development, strengthening their confidence and ability to produce quality research, publish findings and contribute to public health policies. The experience of obtaining and managing grant-funded projects boosted their confidence and motivated them to pursue further research opportunities. According to one obstetrician-gynaecologist and assistant lecturer, “As a teacher-researcher, it’s true that getting a grant gives myself and others confidence; so when you come up with a bigger, more promising project, reviewers trust you because they know you’ve already managed a smaller grant. So for me as a researcher, it’s a considerable asset. The spin-offs aren’t immediate yet, but we know that in the future, given the research career path we’ve chosen, it’s a positive point.”

Participants shared their expectations about how receiving the grant might impact their career trajectory or research development, highlighting unforeseen advantages or opportunities that emerged from being awarded the grant. A resident in obstetrics and gynaecology remarked, “With this grant, it is a great pleasure for us to be able to benefit from the expertise of certain international researchers. For example, we had a doctor from the United States, and another from Rwanda, so it was really nice to be able to benefit from their experience, and to know that there are people like that who do research, who make it their daily life, and who are really at ease with it.”

Influence on research directions

Researchers highlighted how the funding allowed them to target specific public health issues, such as maternal mortality and family planning, and to collect data in challenging environments. Many participants anticipated that this experience would shape their future research directions and methodologies, expressing intentions to incorporate new approaches and pursue work in similar areas. One assistant lecturer shared, “There’s now this training that helps us understand what research really is, what the basics are, the fundamentals of research, even since the first course was applying for funding, we didn’t know there’d be even more interesting courses afterwards, It’s only afterwards that we start to receive courses in research design, qualitative research, for example, and now we can understand the world of research, because we don’t always have the opportunity to do this in our basic universities. Honestly, we don’t really have research courses, we only have science courses, public health courses, but the research course itself is rare. So it’s an opportunity for us to have this high-quality training to really understand what research is, what the research methods are, the international standards.” Participants also noted that the specific criteria or requirements of the grant influenced the design or execution of their current research projects. They also shared their concerns or uncertainties before starting their projects, gauged their enthusiasm and passion for conducting research in general and offered suggestions for improving the grant application process to better support researchers. An obstetrician-gynaecologist commented, “The project will also have an impact on the population, on the participants, because they will benefit from family planning counseling and then those who accept a method will receive their methods directly. So my project will reduce unmet needs in terms of family planning, and by extension, the project will have the effect of reducing maternal mortality. That’s for the population/community and for the researcher, it will enable me to publish, take part in conferences and make myself better known and seek out new partners. And from there, I can submit a new project to the partners, saying, ‘Here’s what I’ve already managed as a grant, I’ve already won a grant. That’s how I’ve managed it, and that can increase my self-confidence.’”

Discussion

The experiences of researchers in Cameroon who received seed research grants underscore the anticipated impact of mentorship, comprehensive training and targeted grant funding on their future research success. Across all interviews, participants emphasised the critical roles of mentors and structured training programmes in preparing them for the complexities of grant applications and project planning. Despite common challenges such as tight deadlines, financial limitations and ethical clearance hurdles, researchers described employing adaptive strategies and collaborative efforts that highlighted their resilience and dedication. The grant funding was perceived to broaden the scope of their research plans and promote professional growth, with many participants expressing intentions to address pressing public health issues and to contribute to the advancement of SRHR in Cameroon. The findings reflect an optimistic outlook on future collaborations and opportunities anticipated to arise from the current research. Collectively, these insights emphasise the perceived necessity of continued investment in mentorship, training and grant funding as foundational elements to cultivating a robust and self-sustaining research culture aimed at achieving positive outcomes in public health.

The experiences of Cameroonian researchers awarded seed grants reinforce the importance of mentorship, comprehensive training and targeted funding for future research success. While there is limited literature that directly mirrors the context or integrated approach of our study, our findings echo challenges cited by Van der Veken et al,34 including persistent limitations in time, resources and skills that can impede research. As in other studies,3537 our participants described mentorship and structured support as essential for navigating these barriers and building resilience, particularly for early-career researchers. However, while Kaba et al38 found that mentorship relationships formed during interventions were often sustained beyond the duration of funding, our study did not directly assess the persistence of such relationships. Instead, our findings reflect the high value participants placed on mentorship during the initial grant period.

Furthermore, Dossou et al and Ogundahunsi et al39 40 found that capacity-strengthening strategies targeting both individuals and institutions were especially impactful. Our intervention was designed to specifically support individual researchers with institutional sustainability elements embedded, primarily in the form of training trainers and mentors. We acknowledge that expanding future programmes to include more institutional components may further strengthen research capacity, as suggested by these authors.

Although our results align with broader recommendations for holistic capacity strengthening, such as those by Kasprowicz et al37 and Boum et al,41 there remains limited literature directly comparable to our context of integrated mentorship, training and targeted funding in Cameroon. Thus, our study provides new insights into how individually focused interventions can foster resilience, professional growth and research capacity at the early-career level, while indicating potential areas for further research on institutional-scale approaches.

Implications and future directions for research

The findings of this study have implications for the ongoing development of seed grant funding programmes and research training initiatives like those offered by CIRHT-UM. Understanding researchers’ motivations and early-stage experiences provides valuable insights into the initial steps of the research process and participation in these programmes. By exploring the obstacles encountered and the strategies employed to address them, this study identifies aspects of programme implementation that may benefit from further consideration or adaptation to better meet researchers’ needs. While direct links to improved SRHR health outcomes are beyond the scope of this analysis, these findings may inform efforts to strengthen research capacity and support in the future. This study offers critical information on better supporting and facilitating reproductive health research endeavours by exploring the obstacles encountered during the early research process and the strategies used to overcome them.

The next step in this programme is implementing the seed grant research, after which additional interviews will allow for a more comprehensive understanding of the researchers’ ongoing motivations, learning experiences and professional outcomes. Later work will also examine collaborative dynamics and dissemination strategies, which are critical to translating research into practice and promoting interdisciplinary partnerships in SRHR. In the future, intentional training on locally relevant issues and stakeholder engagement may contribute to advancing SRHR policy and practice in Cameroon.

While participants described many benefits of this international, high-resource country partnered programme, we acknowledge the need to critically reflect on the sustainability and power asymmetries inherent in such initiatives. Prior to initiating the partnership, we secured support from the institutional leadership and Ministry of Health. True research capacity strengthening in Cameroon, and globally, relies upon long-term local leadership, investment and the prioritisation of African-led research agendas. It is important to move toward locally owned and administered programmes, foster equitable authorship and leadership opportunities and support the broad ecosystem of African-led capacity-building efforts. Building sustainability requires shifting from externally driven, time-limited interventions to models that centre local knowledge, foster reciprocal learning and ensure ongoing funding and administrative autonomy for local researchers and their institutions.

Strengths and limitations

The study’s strengths include the use of in-depth, semistructured interviews, which provide rich qualitative data and a nuanced understanding of Cameroonian researchers’ experiences, challenges and motivations. The meaningful collaboration and richness for the local contextual understanding of the qualitative data are also a strength of the study.

While this study provides valuable insights into the experiences of researchers who received seed research grants, it is limited in scope to SRHR providers and two institutions. Additionally, the reliance on self-reported data introduces the potential for social desirability bias whereby knowing that authors are the funders of their grants, participants could have avoided mentioning weaknesses or barriers pertaining to the processes of grant allocation, conduct of mentoring and supportive monitoring. The translation from French to English may have led to some nuances being lost despite efforts to ensure accuracy.

Conclusion

Through understanding researchers’ motivations, professional growth and reflections on their grant-funded projects, the study underscores the critical roles of mentorship, tailored training and targeted funding in supporting researchers and strengthening SRHR research capacity in Cameroon. Addressing persistent barriers and leveraging local expertise are necessary to sustain progress. Continued investment in locally led research capacity-strengthening initiatives will be crucial for advancing public health outcomes in Cameroon and other low-resource settings.
