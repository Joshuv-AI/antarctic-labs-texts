Atrichia with papular lesions in Syrian siblings exposing global diagnostic challenges in genetic alopecia: A rare case report
Authors: Nabeha Haytham Alibrahim, Ayham Qatza, Abdullah Dukhan, Mohammad Almoustafa, Thaer Douri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAtrichia with papular lesions is a rare autosomal recessive genodermatosis characterized by complete, irreversible hair loss that typically begins in infancy and is often misdiagnosed due to phenotypic similarity to other forms of alopecia. A 4-year-old male patient presented with complete hair loss since infancy, with absent eyebrows and eyelashes and no evidence of regrowth. His younger sister exhibited identical features, consistent with a history of consanguinity in the family. Physical examination revealed smooth skin and multiple firm, painless, keratotic papules (2–3 mm) on the scalp and cheeks. A 4-mm punch biopsy demonstrated an absence of mature hair follicles, replaced by keratin-filled cysts without significant inflammation. These histopathological findings, together with the clinical presentation, supported a high-likelihood diagnosis of atrichia with papular lesions. Established diagnostic criteria supported this assessment even in the absence of human hairless gene testing, particularly in consanguineous families in resource-limited settings. The family received counseling regarding the irreversible nature of the condition and was offered psychological support. This case highlights the diagnostic challenges encountered in resource-limited settings and proposes a clinical framework that prioritizes heightened clinical awareness and targeted screening in high-risk populations, including consanguineous families with a history of atrichia with papular lesions or other autosomal recessive skin disorders.Keywords: Atrichia with papular lesions, atrichia congenita, hair loss, case report

Introduction

Atrichia with papular lesions (APL) is a rare autosomal recessive genodermatosis characterized by complete, irreversible hair loss that typically begins in infancy. 1 This condition lies within the spectrum of congenital alopecia, alongside Moynahan syndrome, progeria, hidrotic ectodermal dysplasia, and vitamin D–dependent rickets type II (VDDR II). 2 More than 30 mutations in the human hairless (HR) gene on chromosome 8p12 have been associated with APL, predominantly in consanguineous families; however, sporadic heterozygous mutations have also been reported. 3 Patients with APL are usually born with normal hair, followed by shedding within the first few months of life. 4 A distinctive feature is the development of keratin-filled cystic papules, which typically appear between 2 and 3 years of age and progressively increase in number. 5 This report describes APL in a consanguineous Syrian family with two affected siblings, contributing rare data from the Middle East and highlighting diagnostic challenges in low-resource settings. APL is frequently misdiagnosed as treatment-resistant alopecia universalis, which may lead to unnecessary corticosteroid use and associated adverse effects. Robust clinical and biopsy criteria may facilitate diagnosis without the need for advanced genetic testing in populations with high rates of consanguinity and in resource-limited settings, as demonstrated in our case.

Case presentation

Chief complaints

A 4-year-old male patient presented to the dermatology clinic with complete hair loss and no evidence of regrowth. He was born with normal hair; however, progressive shedding occurred during the first few months of life, resulting in complete hair loss by 6 months of age.

Personal and medical history

The child was born at term through spontaneous vaginal delivery, with a birth weight of 3.2 kg and no perinatal complications. Developmental milestones were achieved on schedule, and there was no notable medical history or known allergies. The parents reported a consanguineous marriage (first cousins). Although family history was initially unremarkable for similar conditions, the patient’s younger sister developed identical symptoms (Figure 1).

Phenotypic appearance of the affected sister at 2 months of age. (a) Sparse eyebrows and eyelashes; (b) patchy scalp hair loss without evident papular lesions.

Physical examinations

Physical examination revealed complete absence of scalp hair, eyebrows, and eyelashes over smooth, nonscarring skin. Multiple firm, painless, whitish keratotic papules (2–3 mm) were observed, predominantly on the scalp and cheeks (Figure 2). The skin, nails, teeth, and sweating were normal, with no additional integumentary abnormalities. Growth, development, and neurological status were consistent with the patient’s age. Differential diagnoses and laboratory investigations

An initial misdiagnosis of alopecia areata led to treatment with standard topical and systemic corticosteroids, without significant improvement. The differential diagnoses included alopecia universalis, VDDR II, ectodermal dysplasia, and APL. However, normal growth, neurological findings, and ectodermal structures excluded ectodermal dysplasia. Laboratory investigations showed normal complete blood count, liver and kidney function, thyroid function, and serum zinc levels. Serum calcium (9.8 mg/dL), phosphorus (4.2 mg/dL), and alkaline phosphatase (256 U/L) levels were within normal limits. A 25-hydroxyvitamin D level of 32 ng/mL excluded VDDR II.

Histopathological examination and diagnosis of APL

Following informed parental consent, a 4 mm punch biopsy was performed on the scalp crown under local anesthesia. Low-power examination (40×) demonstrated a thinned epidermis and absence of mature terminal hair follicles and sebaceous glands in the dermis (Figure 3(a)). Higher magnification (100×) revealed multiple keratin-filled follicular cysts in the upper and mid-dermis, lined by stratified squamous epithelium. No significant inflammatory infiltrate or dermal fibrosis was observed (Figure 3(b)). Genetic testing for HR gene mutations was recommended but was not performed because of financial and resource limitations. The characteristic clinical presentation, combined with histopathological findings and exclusion of differential diagnoses, supported a high‑likelihood diagnosis of APL. Established diagnostic criteria support this conclusion even in the absence of HR gene testing, particularly in consanguineous families in resource-limited settings.

Concomitant case

The 2-month-old younger sibling demonstrated a clinical progression that closely parallels that of the older sibling and manifests distinct features at this early stage. She was born with normal hair, followed by the recent onset of patchy hair shedding similar to the early infancy phase observed in her brother (Figure 1). No papular lesions were present, consistent with their typical onset between 2 and 3 years of age. Physical examination showed smooth, nonscarring skin without ectodermal abnormalities. This pattern supports a familial autosomal recessive inheritance of APL and underscores the importance of early clinical surveillance for the development of papular lesions.

Therapeutic intervention and follow-up

The family received counseling regarding the irreversible nature of the condition. Psychological support was offered to mitigate potential psychosocial effects. The patient is being followed up every 3 months to monitor the development of papular lesions and to provide symptomatic care. Genetic counseling was recommended to inform reproductive decision making and assess the risk for future pregnancies.

Discussion

Alopecia is a common presentation in primary care and arises from diverse etiologies. Pediatric alopecia presentations differ from those in adults and warrant careful evaluation for congenital and hereditary disorders, which may be associated with multisystem syndromes. 6 APL has been reported predominantly among Irish Travelers, an ethnic minority group in Ireland. 7 This report describes familial APL in Syrian siblings born to first-cousin parents, thereby expanding the known ethnic distribution and underscoring consanguinity as a key risk factor in susceptible populations.

Mutations in the HR gene, which encodes the hairless protein, have been identified as the underlying molecular cause of APL. 8 This protein functions as a nuclear corepressor, similar to those associated with retinoic acid and thyroid hormone receptors and regulates gene expression through histone deacetylation to modify chromatin structure and regulate gene expression.9–12 Through these mechanisms, the protein modulates epithelial stem cell differentiation within the outer root sheath and in the transition to the first adult hair cycle. 13 Deficiency of this protein leads to premature apoptosis of hair bulb cells during early catagen, ultimately resulting in complete hereditary alopecia. 4

APL represents a severe form of congenital, early-onset alopecia and is commonly associated with sparse or absent eyebrows, eyelashes, and body hair. 14 The presence of characteristic papular lesions is a hallmark feature of APL and arise from cystic formations resulting from progressive keratin retention within follicular structures. These lesions typically emerge around 2 years of age. 15 This clinical pattern is consistent with the manifestations observed in our patient, which included complete hair loss in the early postnatal period and the subsequent development of keratotic papules on the scalp and cheeks.

Alopecia universalis congenita (AUC) typically presents with complete hair loss at birth or in the immediate neonatal period and lacks the characteristic papular lesions and keratin-filled follicular cysts seen in APL. Histologically, AUC may demonstrate miniaturized or absent hair follicles; however, lower dermal follicular cysts and follicular structural disintegration are not observed.16,17 VDDR II often presents with alopecia that clinically and histologically resembles APL or AUC. However, VDDR II is associated with systemic features of rickets, including hypocalcemia, hypophosphatemia, rachitic bone changes, and seizures. These abnormalities reflect an underlying defect in the vitamin D receptor and can be excluded by normal serum calcium, phosphorus, and 25-hydroxyvitamin D levels. 18 In contrast, ectodermal dysplasia typically involves multiple ectodermal structures, including nails, teeth, sweat glands, and, in some cases, hair. It is commonly associated with specific dental abnormalities such as hypodontia and with hypohidrosis. By comparison, patients with APL have normal teeth, nails, sweating, and growth, with pathology largely confined to the hair follicles and no evidence of widespread ectodermal involvement. 15 APL is further distinguished by its autosomal recessive inheritance, a consanguineous family background, characteristic papular lesions, and histopathological replacement of mature hair follicles by keratin-filled follicular cysts. 19 These features are not observed in AUC, VDDR II, or most forms of ectodermal dysplasia.

Yip et al. 3 proposed updated diagnostic criteria for APL based on a review of the literature and clinical experience. The diagnostic framework includes five major and five minor criteria, with a diagnosis considered highly probable when at least four major criteria are met. The major criteria include the following: (a) complete and permanent absence of scalp hair within the first months of life; (b) the presence of smooth, whitish, or milia-like papules on the face, scalp, and other body sites from early life; (c) histopathological replacement of mature hair follicular structures by follicular cysts filled with cornified material in scalp biopsy specimens; (d) identification of pathogenic variants in the HR gene via genetic testing; and (e) clinical and/or molecular exclusion of vitamin D–dependent rickets. The minor criteria include the following: (a) a documented family history consistent with consanguinity; (b) absence or marked reduction of axillary, pubic, or body hair and/or sparse or absent eyebrows and eyelashes; (c) normal growth and development with healthy bones, teeth, nails, and normal sweating; (d) whitish hypopigmented streaks on the scalp; and (e) lack of response to standard treatment modalities. In clinical practice, the relative importance of histopathology‑based diagnosis versus molecular confirmation should be considered in context. Histopathology provides direct evidence of structural abnormalities and can reliably distinguish APL from other congenital alopecias when interpreted alongside clinical features and biochemical markers.18,20 However, in early-stage or atypical APL, histopathological findings may occasionally appear normal or inconclusive, underscoring the role of HR gene sequencing as the reference standard for definitive diagnosis.19,21 The histopathological findings in the present case closely resemble the characteristic changes described in genetically confirmed APL, in which similar follicular cysts and lower dermal follicular disintegration have been reported. 19 Although histopathology alone cannot identify the underlying HR gene mutation, it provides a robust morphological correlate of the disease and can support a highly probable clinical diagnosis when molecular testing is unavailable. Conversely, molecular confirmation provides definitive evidence of the underlying etiology, enables genotype–phenotype correlation, and is essential for genetic counseling and carrier testing in family members.3,22 The present case illustrates the diagnostic challenges encountered in resource-limited settings, where HR gene sequencing is often unavailable. In such contexts, clinical and histopathological findings serve as the primary criteria for diagnosis. This observation supports the clinical validity of the diagnostic frameworks when genetic testing is not feasible.

This approach also helps avoid the administration of unnecessary treatments, including systemic corticosteroids or disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs, which are ineffective in APL. 23 In the present case, application of established diagnostic criteria enabled early differentiation of APL from other conditions and prevented further exposure to systemic corticosteroids and disease-modifying agents. This strategy minimized the risk of iatrogenic complications and was consistent with recommendations for the diagnosis of APL in resource-limited settings. However, targeted screening and increased clinical awareness in high-risk populations, particularly consanguineous families with a history of congenital alopecia or other autosomal recessive skin disorders, may represent a more resource efficient and appropriate strategy. Such an approach may facilitate early recognition of APL, reduce unnecessary immunosuppressive therapy, and enable timely genetic counseling and informed family planning. Furthermore, this approach achieves these objectives without necessitating universal screening for a rare, untreatable genodermatosis.

A key limitation of this report is the absence of genetic confirmation of a pathogenic HR gene mutation. HR gene analysis is widely considered a major diagnostic criterion for APL and provides definitive molecular evidence of the disorder. In the present case, this analysis was not feasible because of financial constraints, limited access to specialized laboratories, and insufficient infrastructure for routine genetic testing. Consequently, the diagnosis was based on the integration of clinical, histopathological, and biochemical findings, consistent with the recommendations of Yip et al. 3 for cases in which genetic testing is not feasible.

Conclusion

This case highlights the importance of considering APL in the differential diagnosis of congenital or early-onset alopecia, particularly in consanguineous families. Early recognition may prevent unnecessary treatments and facilitate appropriate genetic counseling. Dermatologists should maintain a high index of suspicion for APL in patients with universal alopecia resistant to therapy, especially when papular lesions are present. Increasing awareness and understanding of APL among healthcare providers may improve diagnostic accuracy and help avoid potentially harmful interventions.
